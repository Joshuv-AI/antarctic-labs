Environmental and Genetic Determinants of Early‐Life Telomere Length in Tree Swallows
Authors: Claudie‐Anne Langlois, Fanie Pelletier, Carolyne Houle, Julien G. A. Martin, Dany Garant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEarly‐life environmental and genetic factors play key roles in shaping the fitness of individuals by influencing life‐history traits. The impact of early life conditions is hypothesized to be reflected at the cellular level on telomere length. In many animal species, telomeres shorten with age and their length early in life is often related to individual life expectancy. It is therefore essential to understand the relative importance of environmental and genetic factors influencing telomere length during this early developmental period. In this study we determine the relative influence of environmental and genetic components on telomere length in Tree swallows ( Tachycineta bicolor ) across various environments. To do so, we used data from 518 nestlings across 176 broods, collected over 2 years in a population breeding in southern Québec (Canada). We found no effect of environmental conditions on telomere length but found that nestlings from larger broods tended to have slightly longer telomeres. Parent‐offspring regressions revealed a significant and positive association between nestling rTL and that of their mothers. While the association was also positive for fathers‐offspring, it was slightly weaker and not statistically significant. Heritability of telomere length estimated using an animal model ranged between 0.34 and 0.55. Our results suggest that telomere length of Tree swallow nestlings is influenced by the proximal environment (i.e., within a brood) and by a genetic component. These findings suggest that broader‐scale environmental conditions might not directly affect nestlings at this stage.

Introduction

Adverse environmental conditions limit resource availability, thereby reducing the amount of energy individuals can allocate to their various biological functions (Stearns 1992). Energy limitation leads to trade‐offs in resource allocation, primarily between maintenance, growth and reproduction (Roff 1992; Stearns 1992). Trade‐offs may occur during early‐life development, a period characterized by rapid growth and high physiological demands (Lindström 1999; Metcalfe 2003; Monaghan 2008; Stearns 1992). Early‐life conditions are therefore crucial because they can shape morphological, behavioural and life‐history traits at later life stages (Lindström 1999; Monaghan 2008; Stearns 1992). For example, in several species of birds and mammals, early conditions influence adult survival and reproductive performance, and thus ultimately modulate fitness (Cooper and Kruuk 2018; Lindström 1999; Monaghan 2008). A meta‐analysis also revealed that favourable early environments were associated with delayed onset of reproductive senescence across species (Cooper and Kruuk 2018). However, the mechanisms underlying the persistent effects of early‐life conditions on adult traits and fitness remain poorly understood. One hypothesis proposed to explain this process is that early‐life adversity exerts long‐term effects at the cellular level, specifically by influencing telomere dynamics (Heidinger et al. 2012; Monaghan 2014; Monaghan and Haussmann 2006).

Telomeres are non‐coding DNA sequences that protect chromosome ends in eukaryotic organisms and are highly conserved across species (Blackburn 1991). Telomeres consist of tandemly repeated sequences that are synthesized by a riboprotein enzyme called telomerase (Blackburn 1991). They confer stability to chromosomes (Muller and Herskowitz 1954) and prevent terminal base pair losses of coding DNA by the semi‐conservative DNA replication process (Olovnikov 1996). Telomere length (TL) is a dynamic trait that shortens with each cell division and under oxidative stress (Von Zglinicki 2002) but can be partially restored by telomerase. When telomeres are too short, chromosomes undergo irreversible damage that can lead to cell senescence (Campisi 2005; Klapper 2001). In several species, a negative relationship is observed between age of individuals and their TL (Asghar et al. 2015; Bichet et al. 2020; Dugdale and Richardson 2018; Haussmann et al. 2003). Early‐life TL has been positively associated with survival and lifespan across multiple studies, including in humans (Bakaysa et al. 2007), mammals (Froy et al. 2021; Van Lieshout et al. 2019) and birds (Bichet et al. 2020; Heidinger et al. 2012). Moreover, TL measured early in life appears to be a stronger predictor of longevity than TL measured at older ages, likely due to selective disappearance of individuals (Haussmann and Marchetto 2010; Heidinger et al. 2012; Sheldon et al. 2022). A recent meta‐analysis confirmed this pattern by revealing a significant negative association between early‐life TL and mortality risk across bird and mammal species (Eastwood et al. 2023). Understanding the relationship between early‐life environmental conditions and telomere length (TL) is thus critical for uncovering the mechanisms that govern telomere dynamics and their lifelong consequences.

TL may be influenced by both genetic and environmental factors (Chik et al. 2022; Dugdale and Richardson 2018; Pepke et al. 2022). First, environmental factors, such as adverse conditions during early life may reduce the energy available for cellular repair processes and increase oxidative stress, both of which may accelerate telomere shortening (Epel et al. 2004). A meta‐analysis across different taxa observed that environmental factors, such as competition, nutritional constraints, pathogen‐related infections, predation and disturbance by human activities, appeared to negatively influence TL in animals (Chatelain et al. 2020). In birds, for instance, lower incubation temperature was shown to reduce embryonic growth rate and increase TL in nestlings, likely due to slower cell division rates and reduced oxidative stress (Hope et al. 2023; Stier et al. 2020; Vedder et al. 2018). Variation in food availability and nutrient access may also impact TL through its effect on telomerase activity (Paul 2011). An increased sibling competition for limited parental resources was also shown to accelerate telomere shortening, especially in large broods or for nestlings in poor condition (e.g., Criscuolo et al. 2023; Nettle et al. 2015).

Genetic factors may also influence TL and a recent meta‐analysis reported a mean heritability of TL across vertebrates of approximately 0.45 (Chik et al. 2022). Studies have also reported a positive correlation between early‐life TL and fitness‐related traits (Eastwood et al. 2019; Pauliny et al. 2006), suggesting that natural selection may favour individuals with longer telomeres. Previous studies across various bird species have reported a predominantly maternal transmission pattern of TL (Asghar et al. 2015; Belmaker et al. 2019; Horn et al. 2011; Reichert et al. 2015), although some evidence also points to paternal transmission (Bauch et al. 2019; Njajou et al. 2007). These contrasting results may stem from non‐genetic parental effects, such as variation in parental care, and epigenetic inheritance of TL via germ cells, with sex‐specific patterns potentially linked to heterogamy (De Meyer et al. 2014). Most of these studies, however, estimated TL heritability using parent‐offspring regressions (but see Asghar et al. 2015), which limit the ability to estimate additive genetic component of variation (De Villemereuil et al. 2018; Kruuk 2004).

In this study, we examined the environmental and genetic factors affecting early life TL under natural conditions, based on data collected over two breeding seasons from a population of Tree swallows ( Tachycineta bicolor ) breeding in southern Québec, Canada. More specifically, we investigated the influence of five environmental variables known to affect development and survival of nestlings in this species. First, we tested for an effect of land cover type on TL, as more intensive agricultural practices tend to reduce food availability (Bellavance et al. 2018; Garrett, Lamoureux, et al. 2022; Ghilain and Bélisle 2008). We also assessed the effect of a high density of conspecifics and of the presence of competitors (House sparrows, Passer domesticus ) on TL, as they respectively represent higher and lower habitat quality (Bourret et al. 2015; Robillard et al. 2013). Additionally, we tested for an effect of weather (cold snaps, temperature and precipitation) on TL, which affects food availability and energy demands (Garrett, Lamoureux, et al. 2022) and for an effect of ectoparasites on TL, which negatively affect nestling condition (Sigouin et al. 2021). We estimated the additive genetic component of variance in TL using an animal model to efficiently assess heritability (Kruuk 2004) and compared it to parent‐offspring regression estimate.

Tree swallows are aerial insectivores that typically produce a single brood of five nestlings on average and provide biparental care (Stutchbury and Robertson 1988). Although this species is socially monogamous, extra‐pair paternity is very frequent, resulting in most broods (> 80% of them) having mixed genetic parentage (Jones 2003; Lessard et al. 2014). In these broods, about half of offspring are sired by the social male (i.e., WPY: within‐pair young), while others are sired by extra‐pair males (i.e., EPY: extra‐pair young) (see Lessard et al. 2014 for example). Previous studies showed a negative correlation between TL and survival in this species (Haussmann et al. 2003, 2005). A study by Belmaker et al. (2019) also suggested a high heritable component of TL (0.81 ± 0.17) in Tree swallows using parent‐offspring regression, which appeared to be primarily driven by maternal inheritance. Studies have also investigated the impact of intra‐brood competition on telomere dynamics by experimentally manipulating brood size (Belmaker et al. 2019; Gómez et al. 2021). While Belmaker et al. (2019) found no effect of brood size on nestling TL, Gómez et al. (2021) reported a slightly negative effect of enlarged broods on TL. Here, we focused on natural variation in brood size and in ecological conditions, as these may provide a more realistic representation of female reproductive decisions. In addition to parent‐offspring regressions, we also use an animal model framework to estimate additive genetic variance and heritability. Our approach will thus help refining our understanding of the factors shaping early‐life TL in this species.

Materials and Methods

Study System and Data Collection

The study system was established in 2004 and is located in southern Québec (Canada). It is composed of 200 nest boxes spread across 20 farms (i.e., 10 per farm). The farms are distributed along an east–west gradient of agricultural intensification covering an area of approximately 10,200 km2 (detailed in Ghilain and Bélisle 2008). We visited the nest boxes every 2 days from May to July to monitor breeding activities, from nest building to fledging of nestlings, to collect data on laying, hatching, and fledging dates, fledging success, as well as clutch and brood sizes. We monitored the growth of nestlings by measuring their body mass (±0.01 g) at 2 days (D2) and 12 days (D12) of age with a digital scale. In this study, we used data collected over two reproductive seasons (2023 and 2024).

We ringed all breeding adults, captured in nest boxes, and 12‐day‐old nestlings with aluminium bands (US Fish and Wildlife Service) for individual identification. Prior to their banding, we individually marked the nestlings using a nail clipping code. We caught adult females during incubation and again at least 10 days later, during food provisioning (for the purpose of a different study—see Barbeau et al. submitted). Females were visually categorized into two age groups: second year (SY) for those with brown plumage, and after second year (ASY) for those with blue‐green plumage. We captured the males only once, during food provisioning. We collected blood samples from each individual for molecular analyses (see section below). All the research complies with applicable laws on sampling from natural populations and animal experimentation.

Environmental Factors Influencing Habitat Quality

We first determined habitat quality using the extent of agricultural intensification on farms. To do so, we characterized different land cover types within a 500 m radius around each nest box on each farm. We used this scale because a previous study in this system showed that females rearing nestlings spend ~80% of their daily time within 500 m of the nest (Garrett, Pelletier, et al. 2022c). Land cover was assessed annually in the field by visual identification of the cover type within each field polygon, delineated in advance using orthophotos (scale: 1:40,000) in QGIS (2021). Based on the protocol of Turcotte et al. (2018), land cover was categorized into five classes: intensive cultures (corn, soya and cereals), extensive cultures (pastures and perennial cultures, e.g., various grasses, alfalfa and clover), water bodies, forested areas and anthropic areas (buildings and roads). For each farm, we calculated the mean percentage of each category across the 500 m‐radius areas surrounding the nest boxes. We then used the mean percentage of intensively cultivated land for each farm as our measure of agricultural intensification, as it reflects habitat degradation and increased pesticide use, both of which reduce resource availability for insectivorous species (Ghilain and Bélisle 2008; Stanton et al. 2018).

To further assess habitat quality, we used the occupancy of nest boxes by Tree swallows (number of nest boxes occupied on a farm = local tree swallow density) and House sparrows (presence or absence) on each farm. Tree swallows are known to congregate where resources are more abundant (Bourret et al. 2015; Dunn and Winkler 1999), making their nest box occupancy a reliable indicator of habitat quality. The occupancy of nest boxes by Tree swallows was estimated by the number of nest boxes occupied by the species out of a total of 10, considering only the first broods (as in Labonté‐Dupras et al. 2024). A nest box was considered occupied when at least one egg was laid during the breeding period. The presence of House sparrows was used as an indicator of lower habitat quality, as they compete aggressively with Tree swallows and their presence results in lower nest box occupancy by Tree swallows (see details in Robillard et al. 2013). The presence of House sparrows (recorded as 0 or 1) was determined based on whether at least one nest box per farm was occupied by this species. This approach was used because House sparrows occurred on only two farms (out of 20) during the study period.

Temperature and precipitation were also measured, as they are crucial variables early in life for Tree swallows, affecting the metabolism of nestlings and the availability of insects (Mccarty and Winkler 1999). A Thermochron iButton device was fixed on the outside of one nest box on each farm to record hourly ambient temperature, while another was placed inside a nest box to measure hourly internal conditions (model DS1922L; Embedded Data Systems). Using the temperature data recorded inside nest boxes, we calculated the mean nest temperature experienced by each brood between hatching and day 12 post‐hatch, as the average of all 24‐h nest‐box temperature measurements collected during this period. We also quantified the number of cold snaps experienced by each brood (as defined by Garrett, Lamoureux, et al. 2022: a cold snap occurs when the daily maximum temperature falls below 18.3°C, a threshold associated with reduced insect availability). Consecutive days below this threshold were considered as separate cold snaps. Using temperature outside the nest boxes, we counted the number of cold snaps experienced by each brood between minimum hatching date and D12 post‐hatch. This variable is particularly relevant given that nestlings typically develop thermoregulatory capacity only around 9–10 days after hatching (Dunn 1979). Additionally, Garrett et al. (2022) reported reduced fledging success in broods exposed to several cold snaps. In addition, precipitation was measured using a rain gauge installed on each farm. Rainfall was measured every 2 days during nest monitoring visits, and total precipitation for each brood was calculated as the cumulative amount of rainfall between the maximum hatching date and D12 post‐hatch. Finally, the presence of ectoparasites, specifically mites, was assessed by visually inspecting the whole body of all nestlings in a brood as well as the handling bag and the nest box. If at least one mite was detected, then the entire brood was considered parasitized. As ectoparasites feed on blood of nestlings, they reduce the resources available for growth and induce physiological stress, and Tschirren et al. (2021) reported a negative effect of mites on TL in Great tits ( Parus major ).

Molecular Sexing and Paternity Analysis

For molecular sexing and paternity analyses, in 2023, we used blood collected from the brachial vein on P8‐grade filter papers (Fisher Scientific). Blood was collected from adults at first capture and nestlings were sampled at D12. In 2024, blood was collected in capillary samples that were used for sexing, paternity assignments and TL analyses (see the ‘Telomere length measurements’ section for details on extraction methods). DNA from filter papers was extracted using a salt‐based method, as described in Porlier et al. (2009). Nestling sex was determined and adult sex validated by amplifying chromo‐helicase‐DNA‐binding (CHD) genes using the P2/P8 primer pair. Parentage assignment was conducted using five microsatellite loci (IBI Ms5‐29, TBI 81, TBI 104, HrU7 and Hir 19), following the protocol detailed in Lessard et al. (2014). Maternity was established in the field, with the female captured in the nest box considered the mother of all nestlings, a status confirmed by genetic exclusion using CERVUS. Paternity was assigned for juveniles with a known mother using a likelihood‐based approach in CERVUS, with a confidence level set at 90%. See Lessard et al. (2014) for more details on molecular sexing and paternity analysis.

Telomere Length Measurements

For both years, for telomere length measurements, we used blood collected using 70 μL microhematocrit capillary tubes (Fisher Scientific). Microcapillary tubes were stored at −80°C until extraction. Blood was collected from females at each capture (incubation and food provisioning), once from males and at D12 for nestlings. DNA was extracted from blood sample using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen). DNA concentration and quality were measured using optical density spectrophotometry (NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer). TL was measured using the Real‐time PCR (qPCR) method developed by Cawthon (2002) and adapted for birds by Criscuolo et al. (2009). TL is measured as the ratio of telomeric sequence abundance (T) to that of a control single‐copy gene (S) (Pfaffl 2001). This ratio is standardized to a reference sample, providing a measure of relative telomere length (rTL). The single control gene used was the glaceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). The qPCRs were performed using Biorad CFX96 with the following primers: telg (5′‐ACA CTA AGG TTT GGG TTT GGG TTT GGG TTT GGG TTA GTG T‐3′) and telc (5′‐TGT TAG GTA TCC CTA TCC CTA TCC CTA TCC CTA TCC CTA ACA‐3′) for telomeric sequences, and GAPDH‐F (5′‐AAC CAG CAA AGT ACG ATG ACA T‐3′) and GAPDH‐R (5′‐CCA TCA GCA GCA GCC TTC A‐3′) for the GAPDH reference gene. Primers were diluted to final concentrations of 10 μM for both telomeric and GAPDH sequences. Each reaction contained 2 μL of DNA (for a total of 4 ng), 5 μL of iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio‐Rad), and nuclease‐free water to reach a final reaction volume of 10 μL.

The qPCR conditions for telomeric sequence amplification consisted of an initial denaturation step of 5 min at 95°C, followed by 39 cycles of 15 s at 95°C, 30 s at 51.5°C (55.5°C for control gene) and 15 s at 72°C. A final dissociation step was conducted, consisting of 5 s at 65°C and 95°C, to generate a melt curve and assess the presence of non‐specific amplification or primer‐dimer by‐products. Samples with a melt‐curve deviating by more than ±0.5 cycles were excluded from the plate.

The samples were analysed in a random order on 96‐well plates. Both the S and T genes were amplified on the same day, and DNA was diluted to the required concentration on the same day of analysis to avoid degradation. Each sample was run in triplicates. For each qPCR plate, we included standards consisting of three repeated samples to assess inter‐plate repeatability, a golden sample (GS) as a reference, a negative control consisting of sterile nuclease‐free water, and a serial dilution series run in duplicates. The serial dilution series consisted of five concentrations, starting at 12 ng and decreasing by half at each step to produce the following quantities: 12, 6, 3, 1.5 and 0.75 ng. This dilution series was used to calculate plate efficiencies, with an acceptable range of 100% ± 15% and R 2 > 0.98. Among the plates meeting these criteria, the average plate efficiency was 102.89 ± 8.46 and average R 2 was 0.989 ± 0.005. The three repeated samples were selected from the sample with high DNA concentration and quality (based on spectrophotometric ratios) and differed between 2023 and 2024. The GS consisted of a mix of DNA from 100 randomly selected individuals, standardized to 2 ng/μL, and served as a reference for calculating rTL. All standards were diluted once, then separated into aliquots and stored in the freezer at −80°C until use. Each aliquot of the standards was thawed and used for two plates, with a different aliquot used for every subsequent two plates to ensure consistency and avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles.

For each plate, we measured the number of PCR cycles (Ct) necessary to accumulate a sufficient fluorescent signal to cross a threshold for both reactions. Any sample with a Ct standard deviation greater than 0.2 was examined to determine whether the deviation was caused by a single outlier well. If the duplicate wells standard deviation still exceeded this threshold, the sample was removed from the analysis and reanalyzed. Based on sample acceptance criteria (i.e., intra‐sample Cq and melt‐curve differences), 95 samples were excluded from our analyses. We assessed the intra‐assay repeatability of Cq from the remaining samples by calculating the intra‐class correlation coefficient (ICC), using the rptR package (Eisenberg et al. 2015; Stoffel et al. 2017). We used a mixed‐effects model with sample identity as a random effect and performed 1000 bootstrap iterations. The repeatability estimation based on triplicates for the GAPDH gene was 0.951 ± 0.002 (CI = [0.946, 0.955]) and 0.966 ± 0.002 (CI = [0.962, 0.969]) for telomere.

rTL was calculated using the model presented by Pfaffl (2001) based on quantification cycle (Cq), see Equation (1): (1) rTL=ETelo∆CqTeloCqGS−CqEchEGAPDH∆CqGAPDHCqGS−CqEch

The rTL was based on the plate efficiency of both telomere (E Telo ) and the GAPDH gene (E GAPDH ). An ideal amplification efficiency of 100% corresponded to a doubling of the product at each cycle (E = 100% = 2). The Cq values were standardized by the deviation between the mean Cq of the GS (Cq GS ) and our unknown sample of interest (Cq Ech ). Inter‐assay repeatability, based on the repeated samples and the GS included on each qPCR plate (n = 85 in 2023, n = 64 in 2024), was estimated using the extrapolated repeatability (Re) approach adjusted for between‐plate variation (Nakagawa and Schielzeth 2010), resulting in values of 0.899 for 2023 and 0.874 for 2024.

Statistical Analyses

Environmental Determinants of rTL

We evaluated the influence of environmental conditions on nestlings rTL using a linear mixed‐effects model. To assess the effect of habitat quality, we included agricultural intensification, local Tree swallow density and presence of House sparrows as fixed effects in the model. To test for the effect of variation in ambient conditions during development, we included the number of cold snaps experienced between hatching date and D12, as well as the mean daily temperature and precipitations. The effect of intra‐brood competition was estimated by adding brood size (number of nestlings hatched) and the relative mass at D2 of each nestling (e.g., the difference between a nestling body mass and the mean mass of all nestlings from the same brood), and their interaction (to test for a possible differential effect of intra‐brood competition on relative mass). The effect of ectoparasites was assessed based on their presence or absence in the nest at D12. To examine whether rTL may reflect a potential trade‐off with early growth (see Ringsby et al. 2015 for example), we included mean daily growth rate, calculated as the difference in mass between D2 and D12 divided by 10 days. Maternal age (SY or ASY), year (2023 or 2024), and nestling sex (Female or Male) were also included as fixed effects in the model to account respectively for potential age‐related maternal effects, environmental variation between breeding seasons and sex‐related differences in rTL. Random effects included nest ID, to account for non‐independence among nestlings from the same brood, farm ID, to control for shared environmental conditions within farms, and qPCR plate ID, to account for potential technical variability related to qPCR processing. Prior to modelling, variance inflation factors (VIF) were used to assess multicollinearity. All variables had VIF < 3 and correlations coefficients < 0.5 (see Table S1), and were thus kept in the model (as suggested by Zuur et al. 2010). Model selection was performed using a backward stepwise approach based on p‐values (with α = 0.05), but we also provide the full model in Table S2. Significance was based on p‐value (i.e., p ≤ 0.05, confirmed by LRT) (Crawley 2007). Analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.2; R Core Team 2025). Linear mixed‐effect models were fitted using the lmer function from the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015). Models assumptions were confirmed using the DHARMa package (Hartig 2024). R 2 were obtained with the performance package (Lüdecke et al. 2021).

Heritability and Components of Variance in rTL

We first estimated the heritability of rTL using mother–offspring and father–offspring regression approaches for comparison with previous studies (e.g., Belmaker et al. 2019). Maternal rTL was calculated as the mean value of rTL measured during incubation and food provisioning when both were available (N = 100 clutches). If only one measure was available, it was used directly (N = 63 clutches). In cases where an individual had two measurements per stage, one was randomly selected to avoid pseudoreplication (N = 4). Paternal rTL was used from males who were both the social and genetic father of offspring in a nest (N = 44 clutches). Offspring rTL was calculated as the mean rTL of all sampled nestlings in a brood. Year was initially included as a factor to account for potential environmental differences between breeding seasons but was not significant in any model (not shown). Narrow‐sense heritability (h 2) was estimated as twice the slope of the regression between parental (either maternal or paternal, if statistically significant) and offspring rTL (Falconer and Mackay 1996).

For comparative purposes, we also conducted linear mixed model analysis using all available females, social fathers and genetic fathers as predictive variables of rTL of each genetically assigned offspring. In this model, we included nest ID and qPCR plate ID as random effects to account for non‐independence among offspring and technical variation. We also included an interaction between the paternity status of the offspring (i.e., WPY or EPY) and paternal rTL on offspring rTL, to assess whether the association between paternal rTL and offspring rTL differed depending on whether the offspring was sired by the social father (WPY) or by an extra‐pair male (EPY). Unfortunately, the power for this analysis was very low (26.1%; power estimated by comparing the full model to a completely reduced model using a likelihood ratio test with 1000 simulations in the R package simr; Green and Macleod, 2016), but we still report results in the Supporting Information (see Table S3).

We also estimated heritability and assessed genetic, maternal and residual components of variance using a univariate Bayesian animal model with the R package MCMCglmm (Hadfield 2010), incorporating a multigenerational pedigree. Although we initially included maternal identity as a random effect, this component could not be reliably estimated (poor mixing and convergence of the chains) and was therefore excluded from the final model. Our model included only random effects to avoid confounding genetic and environmental sources of variation. We used this approach since environmental variables that appear to influence rTL may themselves have a genetic basis and to consider possible covariance in environmental and genotypic effects (see Albecker et al. 2025), and since sources of variance in rTL are still poorly understood. Random intercepts were included for individual identity linked to the pedigree (VA) and qPCR plate identity (VQ). We used parameter expanded priors (V = 1, nu = 0.002, alpha.mu = 0, alpha. V = 1000) for all random effects and ran the model for 5,100,000 iterations, including a burn‐in of 10,000 iterations and a thinning interval of 5000. All autocorrelation values were below 0.02 and effective sample sizes were 1018. We estimated the narrow sense heritability as the proportion of total phenotypic variance explained by additive genetic variance h 2 = VA/VP, where VP is the sum of the variance components associated with random effects and the residual (VR) variance (VP = VA + VQ + VR). We then compared these results to estimates obtained when excluding VQ from the phenotypic variance, as suggested by De Villemereuil et al. (2018), as this component reflects methodological control and does not carry biological meaning in explaining phenotypic variation.

Results

Environmental Determinants of rTL

After model selection, only brood size was retained as a statistically significant fixed effect, with nest ID and plate ID kept as random effects (see Table 1; 518 nestlings originating from 176 broods and analysed across 69 plates were included in the final model). Our results show a positive relationship (slope ± SE: 0.045 ± 0.019; Table 1) between brood size and rTL, where nestlings from larger broods tend to have longer telomeres (Figure 1). The full model can be found in Table S2 and presents quantitatively similar results, with only brood size being significantly positively related to rTL (0.048 ± 0.021). We note that our final model also suggested a positive, yet only marginally non‐significant, relationship between nestling mean daily growth rate and their rTL (0.106 ± 0.059; Table 1). Both our final and full models had marginal R 2 explaining < 2% of the variance in rTL, while random effects explained > 55% of the variance. In the final model, nest identity alone explained approximately 18% (σ 2 Nest ID = 0.030; Table 1) of the total variance.

Heritability of Components of Variance in rTL

Using a mother–offspring regression, we found a positive relationship between maternal rTL and offspring rTL (β = 0.185 ± 0.070, p = 0.009, n = 163, Figure 2a), suggesting a heritability estimate of h 2 = 0.37. The relationship was also positive between father rTL and offspring rTL, but the estimated slope was slightly weaker and not significant, (β = 0.163 ± 0.127, p = 0.21, n = 44, Figure 2b). Animal model analyses estimated VA at 0.063 (95% highest posterior density interval (HPDI): 0.027–0.101) (see Table 2). Based on this model, h 2 was 0.34 (95% HPDI: 0.16–0.57) when VQ was included in the VP. Heritability increased to 0.55 (95% HDPI: 0.25–0.80) when VQ was excluded from the calculation.

Discussion

In this study, we assessed the factors shaping early‐life rTL in Tree swallow nestlings to better understand how natural environmental and genetic components influenced this trait. We found limited effects of habitat‐scale environmental factors, such as agricultural intensification, but a significant influence of brood‐scale environment, such as brood size, on rTL variation. We also found that both parent–offspring regressions and animal model analyses indicated a heritable genetic component underlying rTL variation. Taken together, these findings suggest that early‐life rTL is shaped by a combination of genetic inheritance and early developmental conditions within broods.

Environmental Determinants of rTL

Contrary to our expectations, most habitat‐scale environmental factors tested showed no detectable relationship with rTL. For instance, agricultural intensification is known to influence nestling development by altering habitat quality, including food availability, nesting sites and exposure to agrochemicals (Garrett, Lamoureux, et al. 2022; Garrett, Pelletier, et al. 2022a, 2022b; Ghilain and Bélisle 2008; Poisson et al. 2021; Sigouin et al. 2021). Previous studies in our system have shown that broods tend to be smaller and fledging success lower in more intensively managed landscapes (Bellavance et al. 2018; Garrett, Lamoureux, et al. 2022; Ghilain and Bélisle 2008). We therefore expected rTL to be negatively affected by increasing agricultural intensity, as also previously suggested by Gómez et al. (2021), but found no such effect.

Cold snaps during the nestling period also showed no relationship with rTL in our analyses, despite their known negative effects on survival. For example, Garrett et al. (2022) previously showed that broods exposed to four or more cold snap days within the first 12 days post‐hatching experienced a 36.2% reduction in fledging success, likely due to reduced insect availability. Within our 2 years study, most nestlings (465 out of 518) were exposed to less than four cold snap days, while only 53 experienced four or more. This limited variation in exposure may have constrained our ability to detect an effect of cold snaps on rTL. It is also possible that the effect of cold snaps on rTL could not be detected because these events cause high nestling mortality early in development, so before rTL were analysed (most nestlings were sampled at D12). In fact, early nestling mortality may have generally influenced the results reported in this study, not only regarding cold snaps. One other possible explanation for the lack of broader habitat‐scale effect is that they were not detected because rTL was measured very early in life. At this stage, nestlings were not yet very much exposed to environmental challenges that may influence telomeres.

At the brood‐scale, the only environmental factor we found to have a significant relationship with rTL was brood size, which was positively associated with rTL. This suggests that, contrary to our expectations, larger broods may not impose early‐life constraints on telomere dynamics. Our finding thus contrasts with the hypothesis that larger broods may intensify competition among siblings for resources (Roulin et al. 2012) and thereby accelerate telomere attrition. For example, Gómez et al. (2021) experimentally manipulated brood size in Tree swallows and reported a tendency for nestlings from enlarged broods to have shorter TL, especially in intensively managed agricultural habitats. The negative effect of increased competition on TL has been detected in other species, including European starlings ( Sturnus vulgaris ) (Nettle et al. 2013) and Barn swallows ( Hirundo rustica ) (Costanzo et al. 2017), particularly for the smallest nestlings within a brood. Under natural conditions, females may adjust clutch size according to their own body condition and/or depending on environmental resources available, leading to an optimal brood size relative to their provisioning ability. In Tree swallows, Nooker et al. (2005) found that brood size was larger when food availability was higher and that females that were experimentally manipulated to reduce their foraging capacity produced smaller broods. Females producing larger broods may also be those best equipped to sustain higher‐quality parental care despite poorer environmental conditions, thus potentially limiting sibling competition within their broods. In this species, brood size manipulation experiments have shown that females often increase their parental investment when raising larger broods, even when brood size is experimentally increased (Ardia 2007; Bortolotti et al. 2011). For instance, Belmaker et al. (2019) found only a limited effect of artificially enlarged brood size on TL in Tree swallows. However, it is important to note that the estimated effect of brood size is quite small and may have little biological impact despite being statistically significant.

The positive trend we detected between growth rate of nestlings and rTL was also surprising, as a trade‐off between rapid growth and telomere maintenance may have been expected. Previous studies in birds have shown that slower growth was associated with longer telomeres (Herborn et al. 2014; Vedder et al. 2018), possibly due to a slower rate of cellular division (Monaghan and Ozanne 2018). In contrast, other studies were more equivocal, with some reporting positive (Boonekamp et al. 2014) or no association (Vedder et al. 2017) between these two traits. Since growth rate appears to be a key determinant of post‐fledging survival in Tree swallows (McCarty 2001), this positive association, albeit non‐significant, may indicate that some individuals are advantaged, maybe by a greater energy income. Further investigation is needed to further interpret this result, for instance, by exploring growth patterns and the possible presence of compensatory growth (Metcalfe and Monaghan 2001).

Taken together, our results suggest that in good nesting environmental conditions, the usual trade‐offs between sibling competition, rapid growth and telomere maintenance (Monaghan and Ozanne 2018) may be reduced, allowing nestlings in larger broods to grow well and still maintain longer telomeres. Moreover, the relatively large proportion of variance explained by nest identity, which reflects resemblance among nestlings from the same brood, suggests a potentially important role of the shared nest environment.

Heritability and Components of Variance in rTL

Parent‐offspring regressions revealed a positive association between nestling rTL and that of their mothers. While the association was also positive for fathers‐offspring, it was slightly weaker and not statistically significant. These findings are somewhat consistent with results from a previous study in the same species (Belmaker et al. 2019) and support our results suggesting that mothers may influence their offspring rTL whether at the genetic level or through the care they provide. Parental care was shown to affect offspring TL in some species. For example, a study on king penguins ( Aptenodytes patagonicus ), where eggs were experimentally swapped between breeding pairs, found that young telomere length was more strongly related to foster mothers' TL than to genetic mothers' TL, suggesting a greater role of parental care rather than a direct genetic effect on nestlings' TL (Viblanc et al. 2020).

Heritability estimates obtained from our animal model analysis were largely similar to our estimate from the parent‐offspring regressions. Our estimates approached the average heritability of 0.45 estimated for TL in vertebrates by Chik et al. (2022) but were much lower than the previous estimate on Tree swallows reported by Belmaker et al. (2019) (0.81). Beside differences in methodology, the relative importance of environmental and genetic factors may vary among populations of a given species due, in part, to differences in local selective pressures acting on traits. In any case, this difference among studies highlights the need to explore potential factors (ex. resources fluctuations) explaining this variation. Unfortunately, we were unable to clearly separate the maternal effect from the additive genetic effect with our animal model analysis, likely due to having only 2 years of data and also having considerable overlap between maternal and genetic identity in our dataset, due to repeated pairings of the same males and females in both years (see also Table S4). Yet, the animal model likely provides a more accurate estimate of heritability than the parent offspring regression because it allows accounting for the complex relatedness structure among individuals, rather than relying solely on direct parent‐offspring relationships.

In conclusion, our study reveals that rTL in young Tree swallows is determined by both brood‐scale environmental conditions and genetic factors. Environment effects on early‐life rTL appears to occur primarily through the immediate brood environment rather than broader habitat‐scale factors like habitat quality or weather conditions. Together, our findings underline the influence that parents can exert on the brood‐scale environment, and thus in determining nestling rTL. It is possible that broader‐scale effects were not detected here because rTL was measured very early in life. Future studies could aim to examine the effects of environmental variation by following the same individuals throughout their lifetime to assess at which stage broader‐scale environmental effects may be more prevalent.
