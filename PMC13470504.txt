Photothermal immunomodulatory nanoagents synchronize immunogenic cell death and TAM reprogramming for colorectal cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Xiaodan Chen, Junrong Zhang, Jing Qin, Dechun Zheng, Hui Zheng, Mengxin Wang, Zhengyu Hu, Hailong Lin, Jingchao Li, Lin Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) continues a critical obstacle to effective colorectal cancer immunotherapy. A photothermal immunomodulatory nanoagent was developed to sequentially ignite immunogenic cell death (ICD) and reprogram tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) through stimuli-responsive cascade actions, thereby enhancing antitumor effect. The hybrid nanoagent (MIB@HA) containing hollow manganese dioxide (H-MnO2), indocyanine green (ICG), and thermosensitive nitric oxide (NO) donor (BNN6) is synthesized and subsequently camouflaged with hyaluronic acid (HA). Through HA mediated recognition, MIB@HA achieves efficient tumor enrichment and prolonged tumor retention. Upon laser irradiation, the ICG-mediated photothermal effect simultaneously amplifies MnO2-enabled chemodynamic therapy (CDT) to induce ICD and triggers on-demand NO release. The released NO promotes macrophage polarization toward an antitumor M1-like phenotype and synergizes with T-cell infiltration, thereby improving the conversion of local therapy into productive antitumor immunity. In the mouse model of colorectal cancer, this approach promotes the infiltration of antitumor macrophages and effector T cells into tumor sites for effective colorectal cancer immunotherapy. By synchronizing ICD induction with NO-driven TAM reprogramming under a single external trigger, this work provides a programmable strategy to enhance antitumor immunity.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is the third most prevalent malignant tumors worldwide and remains a major threat to human health [[1], [2], [3]]. As the disease progresses, the tumor microenvironment (TME), which consists of malignant cells, fibroblasts, immune cells, vascular endothelial cells, and diverse signaling molecules [[4], [5], [6]], plays an essential role in sustaining tumor expansion and metastatic dissemination [7,8]. The immunosuppressive TME limits effector T-cell activation and intratumoral infiltration while simultaneously dampening immune-amplifying processes such as antigen presentation [[9], [10], [11]]. Although immunogenic cell death (ICD) is induced to ignite antitumor immunity through the releases of tumor-associated antigens and danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [[12], [13], [14]], these cues often fail to propagate into durable T-cell responses [[15], [16], [17]], in part because tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) act as an immune gatekeeper that elevates the threshold for T-cell infiltration and dampens immune amplification [[18], [19], [20]]. Therefore, synchronization of ICD induction with TAM reprogramming are pivotal for enhancing treatment efficacy [21,22].

Nitric oxide (NO) is a biologically active signaling mediator involved in a wide range of physiological functions, including immune homeostasis maintenance and the regulation of vascular [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. Unlike traditional cytotoxic drugs, NO can act as a regulatory cue for reprogramming the immune microenvironment [27,28], controlled NO generation has been associated with the metabolic and transcriptional programs of pro-inflammatory macrophages, including inhibition of mitochondrial respiration [29], remodeling of tricarboxylic acid cycle metabolism, and activation of the iNOS-NO-HIF-1α signaling axis, thereby serving as a key to unlocking immunosuppressive barriers [30,31]. For instance, NO can promote macrophage polarization from the protumoral M2 phenotype toward the antitumor M1 phenotype [32,33], thereby fostering effector T-cell infiltration [34]. However, NO translation is hindered by limited tumor accumulation and insufficient control over release kinetics [35,36]. The current NO delivery systems lack a unified trigger to concurrently amplify ICD while relieving immunosuppression within the same therapeutic window [37,38]. Therefore, programmable delivery systems that integrate NO donors with precisely controllable triggering modules are urgently needed to enable spatiotemporally defined NO release at the tumor sites [39,40], to couple NO-mediated TAM reprogramming with ICD-driven immune priming within a shared therapeutic window [41,42], and also to maximize the efficiency of antitumor immune conversion [43,44].

To address these limitations of NO delivery, nanomaterials provide a tunable strategy for spatiotemporally controlled cargo release in tumors [45,46]. Engineered delivery systems can increase the accumulation and retention of therapeutic agents at tumor sites and improve the antitumor efficacy [[47], [48], [49]]. For example, hollow manganese dioxide (MnO2) nanomaterials with high loading capacity and structural programmability are versatile carriers for multimodal therapies [50,51]. In particular, stimuli-responsive nanoparticles enable on-demand release and cascade amplification under exogenous triggers, allowing temporal coupling of tumor immunotherapy with immune regulation [[52], [53], [54]]. Therefore, an integrated strategy that simultaneously ignites ICD and regulates TAM-mediated immunosuppression under the same external stimulus can improve the therapeutic outcomes [55,56].

We herein report an exogenous laser-induced photothermal immunomodulatory nanoagent for reprogram the immunosuppressive microenvironment and strengthen antitumor immunity in colorectal cancer. The nanoagent (MIB@HA) consists of hollow MnO2 co-encapsulating the photothermal agent indocyanine green (ICG) and the thermosensitive NO donor BNN6, followed by hyaluronic acid (HA) surface coating to enable the tumor targeting effect (Fig. 1a). The targeting mechanism mediated by the HA shell ensures efficient tumor accumulation. Upon laser irradiation, the ICG-mediated photothermal effect simultaneously amplifies MnO2-enabled chemodynamic therapy (CDT) to induce ICD and triggers on-demand NO release. The released NO promotes macrophage polarization toward an antitumor M1-like phenotype and synergizes with T-cell infiltration, thereby improving the conversion of local therapy into productive antitumor immunity. Thus, MIB@HA-based therapeutic strategy induces marked growth inhibitions of the bilateral tumors in colorectal cancer mouse models (Fig. 1b). Accordingly, this study reports a nanoagent that simultaneously achieves photothermal-enhanced CDT and on-demand release of NO under a single external stimulus to synchronize NO-induced TAM reprogramming with ICD-mediated immune activation within the shared treatment window, thereby providing a promising strategy for the management of aggressive and metastatic colorectal cancer.Fig. 1Design of photothermal immunomodulatory nanoagent (MIB@HA) for synchronized immune microenvironment reprogramming and enhanced antitumor immunity in colorectal cancer. Experimental section

Materials

Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), polyethyleneimine (PEI), anhydrous sodium carbonate (Na2CO3), potassium permanganate (KMnO4) and ammonium hydroxide were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent (Shanghai, China). ICG and BNN6 was sourced from Adamas (Shanghai, China). Glutathione (GSH) and hyaluronate (HA) was purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). All assay kits were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (China) and Proteintech (USA). All solvents were provided by Sinopharm (China).

Synthesis of H-MnO2

TEOS (11.8 mL) and ammonium hydroxide (4.8 mL) were dissolved in 100 mL of water/ethanol (1:9 v/v), and stirred for 6 h. Then, the solution was washed with ethanol to obtain SiO2 nanomaterials dispersed in water. SiO2 (6.0 mg/mL) and PEI (20 mg/mL) were dissolved in 20 mL water, and stirred for 1 h. Then, the product was centrifuged and washed with water, and the obtained product was mixed with 10 mL KMnO4 (20 mg/mL) in aqueous solution (20 mg/mL). After 1 h of the reaction, the product was washed with water to obtain SiO2@MnO2. SiO2@MnO2 (6.0 mg/mL) and Na2CO3 (20 mg/mL) were dissolved in 15 mL water, and stirred for 12 h under 60 °C. Then, the solution was washed with water to obtained H-MnO2.

Synthesis of MIB@HA

ICG (2 mg/mL), BNN6 (2 mg/mL) and H-MnO2 (2 mg/mL) were dissolved in 10 mL water/ethanol (1:4 v/v), and stirred for 24 h. Then, PEI (20 mg/mL, 10 mL) was added into the solution for stirring of 6 h. The product was washed with water/ethanol (1:4 v/v) to obtain MIB. Next, the MIB were dissolved in 10 mL of HA (20 mg/mL) solution and stirred for another 6 h. The product was then washed with water to obtain MIB@HA. Another control nanoagent (MI) was also synthesized by loading ICG into H-MnO2.

Photothermal performance of MIB@HA

Using a thermal infrared imager (FLIR C200, UK), the thermal images and temperature profiles of different concentrations of MIB@HA were monitored during exposure to 808 nm laser. The photothermal conversion efficiency of MIB@HA was determined.

The photothermal conversion efficiency was then calculated according to the following equation:η=mck(Tmax−Tsurr)−QDisI(1−10−Aλ)where c is the specific heat capacity of water (4.2 kJ/kg·°C); m is the mass of water; k is the slope of the curve fitting with time (s) as the ordinate and the temperature decrease gradient (−lnθ) as the abscissa; Qdis is the heat change of the solvent; and Aλ is the absorbance value of the solution at the corresponding wavelength.

Hydroxyl radical (·OH) generation assay

A 2 mL MB solution containing MIB@HA (25 μg/mL), H2O2 (100 μM) and GSH (100 μM) was prepared. For the laser-treated groups, the MIB@HA solutions were irradiated with an 808 nm laser (1.0 W/cm2, 6 min). The resulting changes in MB absorbance were then analyzed by UV-vis spectrophotometry.

BNN6 release assay

MIB@HA dispersions (25 μg/mL) were incubated with GSH (10 μM) at room temperature for 12 h under mild agitation. After incubation, the supernatant was collected, and the release of BNN6 was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).

NO release assay

MIB@HA dispersions (25 μg/mL) were incubated with GSH (10 μM) at room temperature for 12 h under mild agitation. To assess photothermally induced NO release, the samples were further irradiated with an 808 nm laser (1.0 W/cm2, 6 min). The resulting solutions were then processed by ultrafiltration, and NO levels were measured using a commercial NO assay kit (Beyotime, S0021S, China). The relative percentage of NO release was subsequently calculated.

Cell uptake assessment

CT26 cells were plated in confocal dishes at 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 12 h. The cells were then incubated with medium containing MI, MIB, or MIB@HA nanoagents at a concentration of 25 μg/mL. The cellular uptake was subsequently examined by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM).

Assessment of in vitro cytotoxicity

The cytotoxicity of MI, MIB, and MIB@HA was examined by a cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. CT26 cells were cultured in 96-well plates and exposed to different concentrations of the nanoagent (0-200 μg/mL) for 24 h. Next, the absorbance at 450 nm was recorded with a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed relative to that of the PBS-treated control group.

Assessment of in vitro therapeutic efficacy

To assess therapeutic efficacy, CT26 cells were incubated with MI, MIB, and MIB@HA and then subjected to 808 nm laser irradiation at 1.0 W/cm2 for 6 min. The cell viability was examined via CCK-8 assay. The cell survival and death were also visualized by Calcein-AM/propidium iodide (AM/PI) staining. The green fluorescence corresponded to viable cells, whereas red fluorescence indicated dead cells.

Intracellular ROS assessment

CT26 cells were plated in confocal dishes at 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 12 h. After incubation with MI, MIB, or MIB@HA (25 μg/mL) for 24 h, the cells were incubated with the ROS probe H2DCFDA for 45 min. The cells were then subjected to laser irradiation (808 nm, 1.0 W/cm2, 6 min). CLSM was used to analyze intracellular ROS generation.

Intracellular NO assessment

CT26 cells were seeded into confocal dishes at 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 12 h. The cells were then exposed to MI, MIB, or MIB@HA at 25 μg/mL for 24 h, followed by incubation with NO-sensitive probe for 20 min. Subsequently, the cells were irradiated with an 808 nm laser at 1.0 W/cm2 for 6 min, and intracellular NO generation was evaluated by CLSM.

Evaluation of ICD in vitro

CT26 cells were treated with MI, MIB or MIB@HA with or without 808 nm laser irradiation. After 4 h of incubation, the culture supernatants were harvested and centrifuged to remove cellular debris. The ATP content in the collected supernatants was then determined with a commercial ATP assay kit. The release of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) was further analyzed by ELISA kit. After various treatments, the cell culture supernatants were collected and the HMGB1 concentrations were quantified. Surface exposure of calreticulin (CRT) was examined by immunofluorescence staining. Briefly, after various treatments, CT26 cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde, blocked with bovine serum albumin (BSA), and sequentially incubated with an anti-CRT primary antibody and then the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibody. The cells were imaged by confocal laser scanning microscopy.

Macrophage polarization assay

RAW264.7 macrophages were treated with MI, MIB or MIB@HA with or without 808 nm laser irradiation, and then were collected for staining with anti-CD86 and anti-CD206 antibody. Flow cytometry was used to quantify their M2 phenotype (CD86−CD206+) and M1 phenotype (CD86+CD206-).

CT26 tumor mouse models

All the animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Donghua University (approval number DHUEC-STCSM-2022-05). Female Balb/c mice (Shanghai JieSiJie Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd) were used to establish the bilateral CT26 tumor mouse models.

In vivo fluorescence imaging and biodistribution analysis

Balb/c mice bearing CT26 tumors were intravenously injected with MI, MIB or MIB@HA (ICG equivalent dose: 5 mg/kg, 100 μL each) in PBS. For the competitive blocking experiment, an excess amount of free HA was injected before administration of MIB@HA. In vivo fluorescence imaging was performed at designated time points to monitor the nanoagent tumor accumulation in real time. At 24 h after injection, tumors and major organs were excised for ex vivo fluorescence evaluation.

Evaluation of in vivo photothermal property

At the same time point, the tumor sites were exposed to an 808 nm laser (1.0 W/cm2,6 min) and the changes in local tumor temperature were measured by an infrared thermal imager.

In vivo evaluation of antitumor effects

A bilateral CT26 tumor model was established, and treatment was initiated when the tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm3. The animals were randomly allocated to different treatment groups and intravenously administered with MI, MIB, or MIB@HA, followed by laser irradiation at the tumor sites. The body weights and tumor volumes were monitored at 2-day intervals throughout the study. After the treatment, the tumor inhibition rate was determined, and the tumors and major organs were collected for H&E staining.

In vivo immune response evaluation

For immune response analysis, tumor tissues were collected and dissociated into single-cell suspensions. The maturation of dendritic cells was assessed by staining with anti-CD80 and anti-CD86 antibodies. Tumor-infiltrating CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell populations were quantified after staining using antibodies against CD3, CD4, and CD8. In addition, the polarization status of TAMs was examined on the basis of CD86 and CD206 expression. All stained samples were subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± SD. Quantitative assessment of immunofluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ software. Statistical analyses were carried out with GraphPad Prism 8, and the differences among groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance is denoted as follows: * indicates p < 0.05, ** indicates p < 0.01, and *** indicates p < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Material synthesis and characterization

Hollow manganese dioxide (H-MnO2) was constructed and further utilized as a loading platform for both photothermal agent ICG and a thermal-responsive NO donor BNN6, forming a hybrid MnO2-ICG-BNN6 (MIB) nanomaterials. To further enhance the biocompatibility and improve the tumor-targeting capability, MIB nanomaterials were surface-engineered with HA, ultimately generating the MIB@HA nanoagents.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) characterization demonstrated that MI, MIB, and MIB@HA nanoagents were uniformly dispersed spherical structures, possessing an average particle size of approximately 100-110 nm (Fig. 2a). In addition, elemental mapping analysis demonstrated the homogeneous distribution of Mn, O, C, N, and S elements throughout the MIB@HA nanoagents (Fig. 2b). The distinct surface functionalization led to differences in hydrodynamic sizes, with MI, MIB, and MIB@HA showing average diameters of 106, 122, and 134 nm, respectively (Fig. 2c). Their zeta potentials also varied, which was measured to be −25 mV, −10 mV, −7 mV and −16 mV for H-MnO2, MI, MIB, and MIB@HA, respectively (Fig. 2d). Collectively, the stepwise shifts in zeta potentials collectively indicated successful ICG loading as well as effective HA surface modification. Moreover, the colloidal stability of the three nanoagent systems was further investigated. The results showed that MI, MIB, and MIB@HA exhibited no significant changes in hydrodynamic sizes over a 14-day period in water, indicating that all three formulations possessed excellent colloidal stability (Fig. 2e). MI, MIB, and MIB@HA with ICG loading exhibited nearly identical absorbance profiles from 300 to 900 nm (Fig. 2g). All the three nanoagents displayed similar fluorescence signals in the 775-850 nm region, providing favorable conditions for both in vitro and in vivo tracking of the nanoagent, attributed to the incorporation of ICG (Fig. 2h). By analyzing drug contents using UV-vis spectrophotometry and HPLC, the drug loading efficiency (LE) in MIB@HA was 32.6 ± 0.7% for ICG and 21.8 ± 0.5% for BNN6, and the loading capability (LC) was 4.2 ± 0.2% and 2.8 ± 0.1% for ICG and BNN6, respectively. The highly consistent optical properties across multiple batches confirmed successful ICG/BNN6 co-loading and demonstrated the reproducibility of MIB@HA fabrication (Fig. S1, Supporting information).

Property analysis of nanoagents

Upon GSH depletion, photothermal stimulation of nanoagents significantly promoted ·OH production. To confirm this, the absorbance of MIB@HA was recorded after incubation with GSH. An obvious increase in absorption was observed across the 780 nm region following the reaction (Fig. 3a). The observed increase in absorbance further confirmed the effective GSH depletion capability of MIB@HA. The photothermal performance was subsequently evaluated. Under identical concentrations, all the three nanoagents (MI, MIB and MIB@HA) showed comparable photothermal heating behaviors, with temperatures rising to about 51.3 °C after 6 min of laser irradiation (Fig. 3b). Photothermal analysis demonstrated a clear concentration-dependent heating behavior of the nanoagent (Fig. 3c). In particular, at a concentration of 50 μg/mL, MIB@HA induced a temperature elevation of up to 25.5 °C. Similar results were also obtained for different laser powers (Fig. 3d). Both MIB and MIB@HA exhibited reproducible heating and cooling profiles over five consecutive laser on/off cycles, with no appreciable attenuation in the maximum temperature, demonstrating their favorable photothermal stability under repeated laser irradiation (Fig. 3e and S2, Supporting information). The temperature profiles remained stable throughout five laser on/off cycles, demonstrating the superior photothermal stability of MIB and MIB@HA (Fig. 3e). Motivated by the photothermal capability of MIB@HA, we then investigated the effects on ·OH generation using MB degradation as an indicator and NO release. As shown in Fig. 3g, laser irradiation led to a more pronounced decrease in MB absorbance compared to GSH-treated MIB@HA alone, suggesting that the photothermal heating enhanced the ·OH production. BNN6 is a type of heat-sensitive NO donor. Negligible release of BNN6 was observed for MIB@HA without GSH, whereas BNN6 release became evident after incubation with GSH (Fig. 3h). Correspondingly, the NO concentration was limited to 6.3 μM without laser irradiation but increased markedly to approximately 114 μM after 6 min of irradiation (Fig. 3i). Taken together, the results indicated that MIB@HA nanoagents after GSH depletion, effectively promoted the ·OH and NO generation upon laser irradiation due to their favorable photothermal properties.

In vitro therapeutic and ICD assays

CT26 cells were selected to evaluate the cellular uptake and therapeutic performances. Benefiting from HA-mediated active targeting mechanism via receptor-mediated endocytosis, MIB@HA exhibited the strongest fluorescence intensity in CT26 cells under identical concentration and incubation conditions (Fig. 4a and S3, Supporting information). Flow cytometric analysis showed that the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) in the MIB@HA group was approximately 1.7 times that observed in the MI and MIB groups without HA coating (Figs. S4–5, Supporting information), demonstrating the effective targeting effect of HA modification. CT26 cells treated with MI, MIB, and MIB@HA (25-200 μg/mL) exhibited cell viabilities exceeding 80% (Fig. 4b), suggesting their favorable biocompatibility. To explore the enhancing efficacy of photothermal effect on the performance of CDT, the reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation was assessed in vitro by H2DCFDA detection method. Relative to the control group, enhanced intracellular ROS generation was confirmed by the increased fluorescence signals in MI, MIB, and MIB@HA treated cells, arising from MnO2-mediated Fenton-like reaction (Fig. 4d). This fluorescence was further intensified upon laser irradiation, suggesting a photothermal-induced enhancement of CDT-mediated ROS generation. Particularly, the MIB@HA + laser group exhibited a 1.65-fold increase in ROS intensity compared with the group without laser treatment (Fig. 4e). The elevated ROS production was likely due to the enhanced targeting efficiency and cellular uptake of MIB@HA. These results confirmed that laser irradiation effectively triggered and amplified ROS generation within the nanoagent-treated cells. The in vitro therapeutic effect was further confirmed by Calcein AM/PI staining, where the MIB@HA + laser group displayed substantially stronger red fluorescence intensity (dead cells) relative to the other treatment groups (Fig. 4f). These results indicate that PTT and CDT contribute to ICD through complementary mechanisms. ICG-mediated photothermal heating directly induces thermal stress and tumor cell damage while simultaneously enhancing MnO2-mediated Fenton-like reactions to amplify ROS generation and CDT. Thus, the combined thermal and oxidative stresses cooperatively intensify tumor cell damage and promote subsequent ICD.

After incubation with MI, MIB, and MIB@HA, followed by laser irradiation, biomarkers associated with ICD (ATP, HMGB1 and CRT) were examined. Compared with the PBS control, extracellular ATP and HMGB1 release in the MI + laser and MIB + laser groups increased by approximately 2.78-fold and 3.25-fold, respectively, whereas the MIB@HA + laser group showed more pronounced increases of approximately 4.31-fold (Fig. 4g and h). Incubation with MIB@HA alone did not significantly cause ATP or HMGB1 secretion. Immunofluorescence staining was conducted to assess CRT expression in CT26 cells. Minimal CRT expression was detected in the absence of laser irradiation, whereas the pronounced fluorescence signals were observed in the MI + laser, MIB + laser, and MIB@HA + laser groups (Fig. 4i). Notably, the CRT fluorescence intensity in the MIB@HA + laser group was 1.75-fold and 1.81-fold higher than that in the MI + laser and MIB + laser groups (Fig. S6, Supporting information). Overall, these findings demonstrated that laser-activated MIB@HA induced robust ROS production and markedly enhanced the ICD effects.

Intracellular NO production following different treatments was further evaluated using a NO-specific fluorescent probe. After combined treatment with MI, MIB, or MIB@HA and laser irradiation, strong green fluorescence signals were observed in the MIB + laser and MIB@HA + laser groups, whereas negligible fluorescence was detected in the remaining groups (Fig. 4j). Quantitative fluorescence analysis further demonstrated that the NO generation in the MIB@HA + laser group was 1.48-fold higher than that in the MIB + laser group (Fig. S7, Supporting information). These results collectively indicated that the photothermal activation enabled precise generation of NO. Local NO within the tumor microenvironment can exert immunostimulatory effects by modulating macrophage polarization and immune cell functions. To further investigate this immunomodulatory effect, flow cytometry was performed to analyze macrophage polarization dynamics. Following treatment with MI, MIB, or MIB@HA in combination with laser irradiation, a pronounced shift in macrophage phenotype was observed (Fig. 4k). Notably, in the MIB@HA + laser group, the proportion of M1-like macrophages (CD86+CD206-) reached 24.5%, which was significantly higher than that in the MI + laser and MIB + laser groups under the same irradiation conditions, as well as in the non-irradiated groups, where the maximum M1 polarization rate was only 12.8%. In parallel, the percent of M2-like macrophages (CD86−CD206+) was markedly diminished. The positive rates in the MIB + laser and MIB@HA + laser groups decreased to 18.7% and 10.5%, respectively (Fig. 4l). This phenotypic reprogramming was attributed to photothermally enhanced NO production, which drove pro-inflammatory signaling and facilitated the reprogramming of M2 macrophages into the M1 phenotype.

In vivo tumor gathering and photothermal performances

The tumor accumulation capability and photothermal performance of the nanoagent were evaluated in CT26 tumor mouse models. To further verify the targeting effect of MIB@HA, competitive inhibition was performed by pre-injection of free HA to block receptor-mediated binding. Compared with the MIB and HA + MIB@HA groups, the MIB@HA group exhibited the strongest fluorescence signal at the tumor sites and maintained the signals for the longest duration (Fig. 5a). Notably, after the pre-injection of HA to block the tumor targeting effect, the HA + MIB@HA group exhibited a markedly weakened fluorescence signal at the tumor regions. This result suggested that the superior tumor enrichment of MIB@HA was mainly associated with HA-mediated recognition of CD44 receptors expressed on tumor cells. At 24 h post-injection, tumor fluorescence intensity in the MIB@HA group was approximately 1.62-fold higher than that in the MIB group (Fig. 5b). Biodistribution analysis further revealed that the nanoagent predominantly accumulated in the tumor and liver, with minimal distribution in the heart, spleen, kidney, and lung (Fig. 5c). The in vivo photothermal performance of MIB and MIB@HA was assessed using infrared thermal imaging. In vivo tumor therapeutic efficacy evaluation

The therapeutic performance in vivo was assessed using a bilateral CT26 tumor-bearing mouse model after intravenous injection of MI, MIB, or MIB@HA, followed by laser irradiation applied to the primary tumor site (Fig. 6a). Compared with the control group, the MI + laser, MIB + laser, and MIB@HA + laser groups all exhibited pronounced inhibition of the primary tumor growths (Fig. 6b). Notably, consistent with this finding, the mean tumor weights at the study endpoint were significantly decreased in the MI + laser, MIB + laser, and MIB@HA + laser groups (Fig. 6c). Further quantitative analysis proved that the inhibition rates of primary tumor growth were 10.2% for the laser group, 13.4% for the MIB@HA group, 37.2% for the MI + laser group, 51.7% for the MIB + laser group, and 92.5% for the MIB@HA + laser group (Fig. 6d). Meanwhile, significant suppression of distant tumor growth was also observed following treatment of MI, MIB, and MIB@HA in combination with laser irradiation. After 18 days of treatments, both the MIB + laser and MIB@HA + laser groups demonstrated pronounced antitumor efficacy, as reflected by substantially smaller tumor volumes than those observed in the control group (Fig. 6e). In contrast, the MIB@HA and MI + laser groups exhibited only moderate tumor growth inhibition, likely due to its relatively limited therapeutic potency. The weights of distant tumors were lowest in the MIB@HA + laser group compared with other treatment groups (Fig. 6f). The MIB@HA, MI + laser, MIB + laser, and MIB@HA + laser groups displayed moderate inhibition rates of 21.7%, 39.2%, 77.8%, and 89.4%, respectively (Fig. 6g). These observations were further corroborated by the histological examination of tumor sections (Fig. S8, Supporting information). Extensive necrotic regions were detected in the MIB@HA, MI + laser, MIB + laser, and MIB@HA + laser groups, whereas tumors in the control group displayed only limited necrosis (Fig. 6h). In vivo anti-tumor immune response assessment

To further elucidate whether the enhanced therapeutic efficacy of MIB@HA combined with laser irradiation was associated with systemic immune activation, the intratumoral immune response was comprehensively evaluated in vivo (Fig. 7a). Dendritic cells (DCs) maturation was evaluated with CD80 and CD86 as the activation markers. The percentage of matured DCs (CD80+CD86+) was significantly elevated in the MIB@HA + laser group (35.7%) relative to the PBS group (13.5%), laser-only group (12.9%), MIB@HA-only group (16.9%), MI + laser group (19.8%), and MIB + laser group (21.4%) (Fig. 7b). Quantitative analysis confirmed that the MIB@HA + laser treatment significantly promoted DC maturation relative to all other groups (Fig. 7c). We next evaluated T-cell infiltration in both primary and distant tumors. In primary tumors from mice treated with MIB@HA alone, the proportions of CD3+CD4+ and CD3+CD8+ T cells reached 9.1% and 15.1%, respectively (Fig. 7d), suggesting a certain degree of immune activation relative to the blank control group. After additional laser irradiation, these proportions in the MIB@HA + laser group further increased to 31.3% for CD3+CD4+ T cells and 32.0% for CD3+CD8+ T cells (Fig. 7e). A comparable pattern was also observed in distant tumors. Compared with the MI + laser and MIB + laser groups, treatment with MIB@HA + laser led to a marked increase in T-cell infiltration (Fig. 7f). Specifically, the percentages of CD3+CD4+ and CD3+CD8+ T cells in the MIB@HA + laser group reached 35.9% and 29.6%, respectively, both showing clear upward trends (Fig. 7g). Collectively, the results suggested that local photothermally enhanced CDT not only stimulated the immune responses within the primary tumor but also elicited systemic antitumor immunity that contributed to the suppression of untreated distant tumors.

NO can influence the polarization tendency of macrophages. Accordingly, we further evaluated NO release from the nanoagent at the tumor site and characterized the phenotypes of TAMs. To verify NO generation at the tumor sites, tumor sections were imaged using a NO-sensitive fluorescent probe to visualize intratumoral NO levels. Strong green fluorescence was observed in the MIB@HA + laser group, whereas little to no such signal was detected in the group without laser irradiation (Fig. 7h). The macrophage polarization following different treatments was further evaluated by quantifying the proportions of M1-like and M2-like macrophages. The representative gating strategy for macrophages is shown in Fig. S12, Supporting information. As shown in Fig. 7i, the MIB@HA + laser group exhibited a pronounced increase in M1-like macrophages (CD86+) together with a marked decrease in M2-like macrophages (CD206+) relative to the control and other treatment groups. In primary tumors from mice treated with MIB@HA alone, the CD206+ macrophages percentage was 69.5%, whereas that of CD86+ macrophages was 18.6%. After subsequent laser irradiation, these values shifted to 54.6% for CD206+ cells and 40.1% for CD86+ cells in the MIB@HA + laser group. Quantitative density analysis further confirmed that MIB@HA + laser induced the most robust M1 polarization and the strongest suppression of M2 phenotype (Fig. 7j). Analysis of NO production levels together with flow cytometric evaluation of CD86 and CD206 expression demonstrated that laser-activated nanoagent treatment significantly increased intratumor NO accumulation and induced pronounced polarization of macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype. This phenotypic reprogramming promoted the inflammatory signaling cascades and reshaped the tumor immune microenvironment toward an immunostimulatory state.

Thus, the combination of MIB@HA and laser irradiation effectively promoted dendritic cell maturation, promoted T-cell infiltration in tumors, and induced macrophages to reprogram from the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype to the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. The synergistic activation of innate and adaptive immunity provided mechanistic support for the observed systemic antitumor efficacy and the distant inhibition effect.

Conclusion

A photothermal immunomodulatory nanoagent, MIB@HA, was developed to reprogram the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and enhance colorectal cancer immunotherapy through synchronized cascade activation. MIB@HA comprises hollow MnO2 loaded with indocyanine green and the thermosensitive NO donor BNN6, followed by HA coating to improve biocompatibility and tumor-targeting capability. Upon laser irradiation, photothermal heating enhanced MnO2-mediated chemodynamic therapy and simultaneously triggered on-demand NO release, thereby coupling immunogenic cell death (ICD) induction with tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) reprogramming within the same therapeutic window.

The therapeutic benefit of this strategy likely resulted not merely from the parallel activation of these two processes, but from their reciprocal reinforcement within the tumor immune microenvironment. Photothermal-enhanced CDT induced CRT exposure and the release of ATP, HMGB1, and tumor-associated antigens, thereby facilitating the recognition and phagocytosis of dying tumor cells, dendritic cell maturation, and tumor-specific T-cell activation. However, the conversion of ICD-derived immunogenic signals into productive antitumor immunity may be restricted by M2-like TAM-mediated immunosuppression. In the present system, photothermally triggered NO generation was accompanied by an increase in CD86+CD206- macrophages and a decrease in CD86−CD206+ macrophages, suggesting a shift toward a more pro-inflammatory phenotype. Such TAM reprogramming may create a more immunologically permissive microenvironment that supports the processing of ICD-derived tumor debris and promotes effector T-cell infiltration. Nevertheless, the biological effects of NO depend on its concentration, duration of exposure, cellular source, and local redox environment. Therefore, the localized and on-demand release of NO from MIB@HA may be important for achieving immunostimulatory activity while limiting nonspecific or counter-regulatory effects. Because the downstream metabolic and transcriptional pathways were not directly examined, the observed changes in CD86 and CD206 expression should be interpreted as being consistent with NO-associated macrophage reprogramming rather than as definitive evidence for a specific molecular pathway. Collectively, the coordinated induction of ICD and TAM reprogramming converted localized treatment into systemic antitumor immunity, as evidenced by enhanced DC maturation, M1-like TAM polarization, T-cell infiltration, and inhibition of primary and distant tumor growth. This single-trigger strategy provides a programmable framework for overcoming tumor microenvironment-associated immunosuppression and strengthening antitumor immunity.
