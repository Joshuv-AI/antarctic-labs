Three-scale singular limits with applications to rapidly rotating fluids and the hyperbolization of dispersive systems
Vincent Duchêne, Arnaud Duran, Khawla Msheik

Abstract
We consider singular problems for a general class of quasilinear hyperbolic systems that involve two a priori independent stiff parameters. We argue that such situations may lead to the rapid development of small-amplitude spatial oscillations of small wavelength starting from arbitrarily smooth initial data. Despite this phenomenon we provide sufficient conditions on initial data that secure the uniform control of solutions and show strong convergence in the singular limit of small stiff parameters. We apply our general theory to the rapidly rotating shallow-water system with bottom topography, and to hyperbolic systems stemming from a constraint-relaxation strategy applied to dispersive models for the propagation of water waves —specifically the Benjamin–Bona–Mahony, Boussinesq–Peregrine and Serre–Green–Naghdi equations.

1.1
Presentation

In this work we are interested in the behavior of solutions to general three-scale systems of hyperbolic quasilinear equations, of the form

{\sf S}_{0}(U)\partial_{t}U+\sum_{l=1}^{d}{\sf S}_{l}(U)\partial_{x_{l}}U+{\sf G}(U)U=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathscr{L}_{\delta}U.

Here,
U:(t,\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
(with
d,n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
) is the unknown and
{\sf S}_{l}
(
l\in\{0,\dots,d\})
(respectively
{\sf G}
) are given smooth functions into
n\times n
real-valued symmetric matrices (respectively real-valued matrices), while
\mathscr{L}_{\delta}
is a constant-coefficient differential or pseudo-differential operator, skew-adjoint for the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
inner-product. Precise assumptions of our framework are listed in
Section

1.3
below. A prototypical example for the considered stiff operators is

\mathscr{L}_{\delta}={\sf L}_{0}+\delta\sum_{l=1}^{d}{\sf L}_{l}\partial_{x_{l}}

where
{\sf L}_{0}
is a skew-symmetric matrix and for all
l\in\{1,\dots,d\}
,
{\sf L}_{l}
is a symmetric matrix. Importantly,
\delta
and
\varepsilon
are positive parameters that are in principle independent, and we are specifically interested in the asymptotic regime

0<\varepsilon\ll\delta\ll 1.

Systems of such type arise naturally for instance in geophysical models in the presence of rapid rotation and rapidly propagating waves (we specifically discuss the rotating shallow-water system in this work). In such situation the dimensionless parameter
\varepsilon
is the so-called Rossby number defined as the ratio of inertial force to Coriolis force, while
\varepsilon/\delta
is the square of the so-called Mach number defined as the ratio of the flow velocity to the wave celerity. Hence we are interested in a regime of low Rossby (
\varepsilon\ll 1
) and low Mach (
\varepsilon/\delta\ll 1
) numbers, where the effects of fast rotation are predominant (
\delta\ll 1
). We are also motivated by applications to the “hyperbolization” of weakly dispersive equations, such as the Benjamin–Bona–Mahony equation, Boussinesq–Peregrine or the Serre–Green–Naghdi systems. In such situation the dimensionless parameter
\delta
is (the square root of) the so-called shallowness parameter measuring the strength of dispersive effects while
\varepsilon^{-1}
is an artificial relaxation parameter which formally determines the accuracy of solutions to the system of quasilinear equations as approximations to the corresponding dispersive equations.

Of paramount importance to our framework with respect to the existing literature is the presence of the symmetrizer
{\sf S}_{0}(U)
depending non-trivially on the unknown. We argue that, in conjunction with the presence of the two independent parameters in our asymptotic regime, non-trivial symmetrizers allow for a mechanism of
development of a new spatial scale
which, to the best of our knowledge, has not yet been unveiled in the literature. Specifically, we shall argue that solutions to systems belonging to the class studied in this work emerging from smooth initial data
may gradually develop spatial oscillations with wavelength of size
\delta
, on a timescale of size
\varepsilon/\delta
.

Of course such rapid development small spatial scales is a strong obstacle to our aspiration for uniform (with respect to
0<\varepsilon\ll\delta\ll 1
) strong control of solutions and eventually strong convergence as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
and, possibly,
\delta\searrow 0
. In order to obtain such results, we will ensure that the rapid spatial oscillations are of small magnitude, securing in particular a uniform bound on the Lipschitz norm of solutions at each time. This will be enforced through an assumption of well-prepared initial data. However our aim is to provide the least possible restrictions on the initial data that secure the propagation in time on a relevant timescale of strong controls of solutions, uniformly with respect to
0<\varepsilon\ll\delta\ll 1
.

Standard two-scale singular limits

The class of systems we study, namely (

First and foremost, when

It is tempting to compare the case when

[

Three-scale singular limits

Let us now review some works in the literature that specifically consider three-scale singular limits. Let us now describe the mechanism of small-scale development that can be triggered by systems of the form (
1

\left\{\begin{aligned} e^{w}\partial_{t}u&=\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon}\partial_{x}u-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}v,\\[4.30554pt]
e^{w}\partial_{t}v&=\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon}\partial_{x}v+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}u,\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{t}w&=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(7)

It is obvious that system (
7
) is of the form (
1
) and satisfies
1
–
3

4
since
{\sf L}_{\delta}
the symbol associated with the operator

\mathscr{L}_{\delta}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\delta\partial_{x}&1&0\\
-1&\delta\partial_{x}&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}

has eigenvalues
\lambda_{0}(k)=0
and
\lambda_{\pm}(k)={\rm i}(\pm 1+\delta k)
which continuously evolves from size
\gtrsim 1
at
k=0
to size
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
for Fourier wavenumbers at distance
\epsilon

Denoting
w_{0}\coloneqq w(t=0,\cdot)
and

7

e^{w_{0}}\partial_{t}z=\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon}\partial_{x}z+\frac{{\rm i}}{\varepsilon}z.

Let us first notice that if
\delta=0

z(t,\cdot)=z_{0}(\cdot)\exp(t\,\frac{{\rm i}}{\varepsilon}e^{-w_{0}(\cdot)}),\quad z_{0}(\cdot)=z(t=0,\cdot),

\ell

\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon/\ell)
, as soon as
w_{0}
has spatial variations. This naturally leads to investigating the situation

Consider now the case
\delta>0
.
We introduce the flow map
\Phi(t,x)
defined by

\partial_{t}\Phi(t,\cdot)=-\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon}e^{-w_{0}(\Phi(t,\cdot))},\qquad\Phi(t=0,x)=x,

and one quickly checks that
Z(t,\cdot)=z(t,\Phi(t,\cdot))
satisfies the identity

Z(t,x)=Z(0,x)e^{-\frac{{\rm i}}{\delta}(\Phi(t,x)-x)}.

It is now apparent that a new scale is triggered (when
\delta\ll 1
) by variations of
\Phi(t,x)-x
that emerge on the timescale of size
\varepsilon/\delta
. Indeed, we have

\big\lvert(\partial_{x}u,\partial_{x}v)\big\rvert_{L^{\infty}}\approx\big\lvert\partial_{x}z\big\rvert_{L^{\infty}}=\big\lvert\tfrac{1}{\partial_{x}\Phi}(\partial_{x}Z)\circ\Phi^{-1}\big\rvert_{L^{\infty}}

and

|\partial_{x}Z|=|(\partial_{x}z_{0})\circ\Phi-\frac{{\rm i}}{\delta}(\partial_{x}\Phi-1)(z_{0}\circ\Phi)|.

Yet one has

\partial_{x}\Phi(t,\cdot)=e^{w_{0}(\cdot)-w_{0}(\Phi(t,\cdot))},

which shows that

\forall t,x\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad\exp(\inf(w_{0})-\sup(w_{0}))\leq\partial_{x}\Phi\leq\exp(\sup(w_{0})-\inf(w_{0})),

and we have
\Phi(t,x)\leq x-\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon}te^{-\sup(w_{0})}
, hence we see by a continuity argument that, given
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
w_{0}(x_{1})\neq w_{0}(x_{2})

such that

\inf|\partial_{x}\Phi(t,\cdot)-1|\geq|e^{w_{0}(x_{1})-w_{0}(x_{2})}-1|.

As a conclusion, we find that unless one imposes decay or smallness assumptions on the initial data, then one has

\sup_{t\in[0,\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}]}\big\lvert(\partial_{x}u,\partial_{x}v)\big\rvert_{L^{\infty}}\approx\delta^{-1}.

The same reasoning for higher-order derivatives (provided initial data are sufficiently regular) yields

\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\quad\sup_{t\in[0,\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}]}\big\lvert(\partial_{x}^{k}u,\partial_{x}^{k}v)\big\rvert_{L^{\infty}}\approx\delta^{-k}.

Such estimates are the signature of the emergence of oscillations with wavelength
\delta
on the timescale of size
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon/\delta)

4

(8)

6
Applications

In this section we apply our results to several systems arising in geophysics and oceanography,
selecting our cases of study so as to emphasize several key features of our results. We start with the shallow-water system with rapid rotation (small Rossby number) in conjunction with possibly small Mach number, in
Section

6.1
. We argue that in the case of flat bottom, our analysis is not necessary as the structural properties of the system allows to easily obtain the desired control of solutions by standard techniques. We then introduce a framework with mild topographical effects where such techniques fail, while our results apply. This may be the signature that spatially varying topography allows for the development of spatial oscillations with small wavelength when both the Rossby and Mach numbers are small, as described in
Section

1.5
.

Then we turn in
Section

6.2
to systems that recently arose as a strategy to approximate dispersive equations through stiff hyperbolic quasilinear systems. There the parameter
\varepsilon
is related to an artificial constraint-relaxation strategy, while the parameter
\delta
bears physical significance (in our case measuring the strength of dispersive effects) and is typically assumed to be small in the regime of validity of the underlying dispersive equations. We start in
Section

6.2.1
with the “hyperbolization” of the Benjamin–Bona–Mahony equation. As previously we observe that standard techniques apply in the standard flat-bottom case but that taking into account topography variations may generate undesirable spurious oscillations when implementing the constraint-relaxation strategy. We then move in
Section

6.2.2
to the case of the Boussinesq–Peregrine system, which can be interpreted as a bi-directional, multi-dimensional version of the Benjamin–Bona–Mahony equation with bottom topography. We show that the corresponding hyperbolized system still fits into our framework, but discuss additional restrictions associated with higher dimensions. Finally, we consider in
Section

6.2.3
the case of the Serre–Green–Naghdi system which is a fully nonlinear version of the Boussinesq–Peregrine system, and was the key target of the artificial constraint-relaxation technique.

6.2
Augmented hyperbolized systems

As previously mentioned, the “augmented hyperbolized systems” we consider in this section stem from a strategy of constraint-relaxation applied to dispersive equations. As a consequence, the parameter
\varepsilon
which is related to the constraint relaxation can be freely chosen and is expected to drive the accuracy of the approximation, while the parameter
\delta
is characterizing by the physical situation.
In the same spirit, some unknowns of the relaxed systems are “augmented unknowns” that may be directly related to the physical quantities of the underlying dispersive equation only in the asymptotic limit
\varepsilon\searrow 0
. Consequently a crucial discussion is the way these augmented unknowns are initially set, since it is expected to have a consequence on the accuracy of the approximation. From a technical viewpoint, our assumptions of well-prepared initial data may be secured only if these augmented variables are set “sufficiently well”. Since preparing the initial data in practice is typically cumbersome and may be numerically costly, it was the main incentive of this work to relax as much as possible the requirements on initial data to allow only
mildly well-prepared
initial data. We refer to
[
15
]
for further discussion on that matter, and to
[
14
, Chapter 9]
and
[
30
,
24
]
for further results and references on augmented hyperbolized systems.

6.2.1

Appendix A
Product, commutator and composition estimates

In this section we first recall standard product, commutator and composition estimates in Sobolev spaces; see for instance
[
35
, Appendix B]
for relevant references. Then we turn to a key product estimate which we crucially use to control energy functionals involving space and time regularity with different weights.

A.1
Standard estimates

Lemma A.1

(Continuous embedding)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
such that
s>d/2
. Then for any
f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one has
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\big\lvert f\big\rvert_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\big\lvert f\big\rvert_{H^{s}}.

Lemma A.2

(Products)
.

Let
d,\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
and
s,s_{0},\dots,s_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
\sum_{n=0}^{\ell}s_{n}\geq 0
,
\sum_{n=0}^{\ell}s_{n}>s+\ell d/2
and
s_{n}\geq s
for
n\in\{0,\dots,\ell\}
. Then for any
f_{n}\in H^{s_{n}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one has
\prod_{n=0}^{\ell}f_{n}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\big\lvert\prod_{n=0}^{\ell}f_{n}\big\rvert_{H^{s}}\lesssim\prod_{n=0}^{\ell}\big\lvert f_{n}\big\rvert_{H^{s_{n}}}.

In particular,
H^{s_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a Banach algebra as soon as
s_{0}>d/2
.

Lemma A.3

(Product)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
,
s_{0}>d/2
and
s\geq 0
. Then for any
f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{s_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
g\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one has
fg\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\big\lvert fg\big\rvert_{H^{s}}\lesssim\big\lvert f\big\rvert_{H^{s_{0}}}\big\lvert g\big\rvert_{H^{s}}+\Big\langle\big\lvert f\big\rvert_{H^{s}}\big\lvert g\big\rvert_{H^{s_{0}}}\Big\rangle_{s>s_{0}}.

Lemma A.4

(Commutator)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
,
s_{0}>d/2
and
s\geq-1
. Denote
\Lambda^{s}=(\Id-\Delta_{\bm{x}})^{s/2}
. Then for any
f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{s_{0}+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
g\in H^{s-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one has
[\Lambda^{s},f]g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\big\lvert[\Lambda^{s},f]g\big\rvert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\big\lvert f\big\rvert_{H^{s_{0}+1}}\big\lvert g\big\rvert_{H^{s-1}}+\Big\langle\big\lvert f\big\rvert_{H^{s}}\big\lvert g\big\rvert_{H^{s_{0}}}\Big\rangle_{s>s_{0}+1}.

Lemma A.5

(Composition)
.

Let
d,n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
,
s_{0}>d/2
and
s\geq 0
. Let
F\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
be such that
F(\bm{0})=0
, and
M\geq 0
. Then there exists
C_{M}>0
such that for any
g\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap H^{s_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\big\lvert g\big\rvert_{H^{s_{0}}}\leq M
, one has
F(g)\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\big\lvert F(g)\big\rvert_{H^{s}}\lesssim C_{M}\big\lvert g\big\rvert_{H^{s}}.

A.2
Revisited anisotropic space-time estimate

We now provide a key product estimate for functions depending on space and time. This estimate, together with our notion of admissible weights (
2.1
), is a key ingredient for the control of energy functionals involving non-homogeneous weights.

Lemma A.6

(Space-time products)
.

Let
d,\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
,
s_{0}>d/2
,
m_{k}\geq m_{j}\geq 0
. For
n\in\{0,\dots,\ell\}
, let
(j_{n},\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{1+d}
be such that
j_{n}\geq m_{j}
and
j_{n}+|\alpha_{n}|\geq m_{k}
. Let
i\geq 0
and denote
I=i+\sum_{n=0}^{\ell}|\alpha_{n}|
and
J=\sum_{n=0}^{\ell}j_{n}
. There exists
C>0
and
i_{n}\geq|\alpha_{n}|
(
n=0,\dots,\ell
) such that for any sufficiently regular functions
U_{n}
(
n=0,\dots,\ell
) one has

\big\lvert\prod_{n=0}^{\ell}(\partial_{t}^{j_{n}}\partial^{\alpha_{n}}U_{n})\big\rvert_{H^{i}}\leq C\sup_{(i_{0},\dots,i_{\ell})}\prod_{n=0}^{\ell}\big\lvert\partial_{t}^{j_{n}}U_{n}\big\rvert_{H^{i_{n}}}

where the supremum is taken over
(i_{0},\dots,i_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{1+\ell}
such that

\sum_{n=0}^{\ell}i_{n}=I+\ell s_{0}

and for all
n\in\{0,\dots,\ell\}
,
(j_{n},i_{n}+j_{n})\in[(m_{j},s_{0}+m_{k}),(J-\ell m_{j},I+J-\ell m_{k})]
where we write

(j_{n},i_{n}+j_{n})\in[(j_{\star},k_{\star}),(j^{\star},k^{\star})]
if and only if

\begin{cases}(j_{n},i_{n}+j_{n})\in[j_{\star},j^{\star}]\times[k_{\star},k^{\star}]&\text{ if }k_{\star}\leq k^{\star},\\
(j_{n},i_{n}+j_{n})=\theta_{n}(j_{\star},k_{\star})+(1-\theta_{n})(j^{\star},k^{\star}),\ \theta_{n}=\frac{(i_{n}+j_{n})-k^{\star}}{k_{\star}-k^{\star}}&\text{ if }k_{\star}>k^{\star}.\end{cases}

We represent in
Figure

5
the admissible values for
(j_{n},i_{n}+j_{n})
.

Case
i_{\star}+j_{\star}<i^{\star}+j^{\star}

(b)

Case
i_{\star}+j_{\star}>i^{\star}+j^{\star}

Proof.

Assume first that

. We use product estimates of
A.2
with well-chosen

such that
{|\alpha_{n}|+s_{n}=i_{n}}
, distinguishing between two cases.

•

If
s_{0}+m_{k}\leq I+J-\ell m_{k}
, we set
s_{n}=\theta_{n}i+(1-\theta_{n})s_{0}
with
\theta_{n}=\frac{j_{n}+|\alpha_{n}|-m_{k}}{I+J-(\ell+1)m_{k}-i}
, so that
j_{n}+|\alpha_{n}|=\theta_{n}(I+J-\ell m_{k}-i)+(1-\theta_{n})m_{k}
and hence

If

P(D)=P(\frac{1}{{\rm i}}\nabla_{\bm{x}})
.

Scalar Fourier multipliers commute:
F(D)G(D)=G(D)F(D)=(FG)(D)
.

Matrix Fourier multipliers associated with matrix symbols act on vector-values functions through scalar Fourier multiplier entries. The adjoint of such operators in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
is the Fourier multiplier associated with the Hermitian transpose of their symbol.

(Reformulation of Hypotheses
3
and
4
)
.

Using the above notations, Hypothesis
3
and
4
read equivalently:
the Fourier multiplier
\mathscr{L}_{\delta}
is skew-symmetric for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
inner-product and
there exists

such that for all

,

,
