Executive Summary
This report is part of the ongoing Solar Time-Based Residential Analytics and Cycle Time
Estimator (SolarTRACE) project, led by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL).
The SolarTRACE project uses project-level data provided by installer-partners to assess
timelines for residential and small commercial solar photovoltaic (PV) projects—and now
storage projects—of up to 20 kW that were installed in the United States between 2017 and
2023. This report provides an update to our previous 2022 report, which analyzed projects
installed between 2017 and 2021 (Cruce et al., 2022b).

Residential and small commercial solar PV and storage projects in the United States require
anywhere from several weeks to several months between contract signing and system operation
(Cruce et al., 2022b). As part of the installation and interconnection process, most projects
require review and approval by the local electrical utility and the authority having jurisdiction
(AHJ). These reviews typically include permits, one or more inspections, and the utility
interconnection agreement and permission to operate (PTO), collectively called the PI&I
process. The PI&I process helps ensure that installed systems meet building, electrical, and fire
codes and safety standards. However, PI&I processes, complexity, and timelines vary widely
across the tens of thousands of AHJs and 3,000 utilities in the United States (Stanfield et al.,
2012). For example, a typical (median or average) solar permit might take 1–2 weeks for review
and approval by the AHJ, but timelines can range from as short as same-day approval to as long
as 6 weeks or more (Cruce et al., 2022b; O’Shaughnessy et al., 2022). Other non-PI&I
interactions between the solar installer and customer—including contracting, site surveys and
system design, scheduling and completing the installation, and roof repairs or electrical upgrades
as needed—may also contribute to project timelines.

The SolarTRACE project continues to track timeline changes for residential projects in the
United States. The dataset now covers 22% of residential solar and 33% of residential storage
installations in the United States between 2017 and 2023. This report updates our previous
analysis from 2022 and includes:

 • Updated 2017–2023 timelines for rooftop solar PV projects up to 20 kW, which, after
 decreasing 2017–2019, have now increased again and averaged 4.5–6 months from
 contract signing to PTO in 2023

 • The first-ever reporting of timelines for residential and small commercial PV-plus-
 storage projects up to 20 kW, which are longer than those for residential solar and
 averaged 8–9 months from contract signing to PTO in 2023.

As shown in Figure ES-1, overall project timelines (contract signing to PTO) for residential and
small commercial PV were a month longer in 2023 than in 2019. This trend reverses the timeline
improvements found in our previous report (Cruce et al., 2022b) and shows that, after declining
2017–2019, overall PV-only project timelines in our data increased again starting in 2021.
Recent timeline increases were primarily due to longer design (contract signing to permit
submission) and installation (permit approval to installation complete) phase times than in earlier
years. These timeline increases affected most projects in the data, with increases of 3–4 weeks at
the first quartile, interquartile range, median, mean, and standard deviation between 2019–2020

1 Introduction
This report is part of the ongoing Solar Time-Based Residential Analytics and Cycle Time
Estimator (SolarTRACE) project, led by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL).
The SolarTRACE project uses project-level data provided by installer-partners to assess
timelines for residential and small commercial solar photovoltaic (PV) projects—and now
storage projects—of up to 20 kW that were installed in the United States between 2017 and
2023. This report provides an update to our previous 2022 report, which analyzed projects
installed between 2017 and 2021 (Cruce et al., 2022b).

Residential and small commercial solar PV and storage projects in the United States require
anywhere from several weeks to several months between contract signing and system operation
(Cruce et al., 2022b). As part of the installation and interconnection process, most projects
require review and approval by the local electrical utility and the authority having jurisdiction
(AHJ). These reviews typically include permits, one or more inspections, and the utility
interconnection agreement and permission to operate (PTO), collectively called the PI&I
process. The PI&I process helps ensure that installed systems meet building, electrical, and fire
codes and safety standards, along with any additional requirements set forth by states, local
AHJs, and/or utilities. However, PI&I processes, complexity, and timelines vary widely across
the tens of thousands of AHJs 1 and 3,000 utilities in the United States (Stanfield et al., 2012).
For example, a typical (median or average) solar permit might take 1–2 weeks for review and
approval by the AHJ, but timelines can range from as short as same-day approval to as long as 6
weeks or more (Cruce et al., 2022b; O’Shaughnessy et al., 2022).

Using best practices and streamlined PI&I processes—such as the Solar Automated Permit
Processing Plus (SolarAPP+™) online-instant permitting platform—can decrease project
timelines and costs, benefiting customers, installers, AHJs, and utilities (Cook et al., 2024;
O’Shaughnessy et al., 2022). The number of AHJs offering online-instant permitting platforms
has accelerated over the last 5 years, alongside a widespread switch to online/electronic
permitting options in general (Cruce et al., 2022b). Similarly, many utilities have worked to
streamline their interconnection processes, and some do not require an interconnection approvals
prior to system installation (Ardani and Margolis, 2015; Fekete et al., 2022). At the same time,
the U.S. residential solar market has also faced other changes and challenges, including a
significant increase in project volume across many markets (followed by a contraction in some
markets), a rapidly increasing market share of residential storage projects, supply chain
disruptions, staffing shortages, cost increases, new state net rate structures, and the closure of
many solar companies, including several large-volume installers (Agopian, 2024; Cruce et al.,
2022b; Davis et al., 2024; Feldman et al., 2024; Kennedy, 2023; SEIA, 2024). Whether these and
other challenges have offset ongoing improvements to solar adoption processes remains an open
question.

2 Background
The rooftop solar adoption process typically consists of multiple interactions between the
customer, installer, AHJ, and/or utility (Sinitskaya et al., 2019). These include PI&I processes,
which are typically coordinated by the installer with the AHJ and utility, and include permit and
interconnection (IX) paperwork development, submission, and inspections. Other non-PI&I
interactions between the solar installer and customer include marketing and customer acquisition,
contracting, site surveys and system design, scheduling and completing the installation, and roof
repairs or electrical upgrades as needed. Collectively, these PI&I and non-PI&I processes and
interactions across all stakeholders constitute the solar adoption process. Each process may
contribute to project timelines, costs, and customer experiences, positively or negatively.

As shown in Figure 1, the timelines tracked in our data and reported here typically start at
contract signing with the customer. Working with the customer, the installer then conducts any
necessary site surveys, finalizes the system design, and completes and submits the necessary
PI&I paperwork and applications to the AHJ and utility (the design phase). The AHJ and utility
then review these applications, request revisions as required, and issue the necessary permits and
approval to build (ATB) (the review phase). In many cases, these reviews occur concurrently,
though they may also happen sequentially (e.g., utility approval required prior to issuing an AHJ
permit). Alternatively, some utilities do not require an interconnection application/ATB prior to
system installation (Fekete et al., 2022), and an increasing number of AHJs offer online-instant
approval of systems that meet specific design criteria (Cook et al., 2024).

After all pre-installation permits and approvals are obtained, the installer works with the
customer to schedule and complete the system installation (the install phase). Although the
installation itself may only take a day or two of on-site labor to complete, installers often need to
schedule it days or even weeks ahead of time based on customer, crew, and equipment
availability. Most AHJs and some utilities then require one or more inspections of the system
before final approval (the inspection phase). Some AHJs require both rough-in inspections
during installation and final inspections when installation is complete. Afterward, the AHJ or

 3

installer submits the final inspection results to the utility, and the installer submits the final
interconnection/PTO request to the utility (the post-inspection phase). The utility then reviews
and grants/approves PTO (the final IX/PTO phase) (Cruce et al., 2022b).

2.1 Project Timelines for Rooftop Solar
Residential PV project timelines have been widely reported over the last 5 years, including from
previous iterations of the SolarTRACE dataset (Cruce et al., 2022b; Fekete et al., 2022;
O’Shaughnessy et al., 2022); installer surveys (Cook et al., 2021; Taylor, 2019); SolarAPP+
utilization data from AHJs (Cook et al., 2024, 2023, 2022; Williams et al., 2022); BuildZoom
permit data (O’Shaughnessy et al., 2020); and other installer-provided interconnection data
(Ardani et al., 2015). Some key trends emerge across this body of literature.

First, there is a wide range of project timelines for residential PV projects across the United
States, including for individual PI&I (and non-PI&I) phases and for overall project time (from
contract signing to PTO). For example, AHJ permit review and approval times can range from
same-day (i.e., over the counter or via an online-instant platform) to 6 weeks or more (Cook et
al., 2024; Cruce et al., 2022b). Furthermore, AHJ permit review times can differ significantly
even within the same AHJ; O’Shaughnessy et al. (2020) identified at least one AHJ where
installer-specific AHJ permit review times ranged from same-day to nearly 2 weeks.

The same trend holds true for total project timelines as well. According to installers surveyed in
2019, a typical project took 10 weeks from contract signing to PTO, although reported timelines
ranged from 6–13 weeks at the 25th and 75th percentiles, and larger installers typically reported
longer timelines than smaller installers (Cook et al., 2021). In the previous study using the
SolarTRACE dataset, we found somewhat longer timelines through 2021, ranging from 10–22
weeks at the quartiles (14–16 weeks at the median and mean). We also found that some projects
took as long as 40 weeks or more (Cruce et al., 2022b). O’Shaughnessy et al. (2020) found a
similar range when measuring from permit submission through passed inspection: 7 weeks at the
median but ranging from 4–14 weeks at the quartiles.

These results suggest that timelines span a wide range across AHJs, installers, and customers in
the U.S. residential and small commercial solar market. Furthermore, not only do total project
and by-phase timelines vary significantly between projects, but overall average timelines are also
skewed by projects that are slower and much slower than typical. In our previous study, we
found that average timelines were typically 1–2 weeks longer than medians for all project phases
as well as for total/overall timelines. Furthermore, at least 5% of projects took 4x–10x longer
than the median for any given phase, and at least 5% of projects took 2.5x–3x longer than the
median for their total timelines (see the appendix tables of Cruce et al., 2022b). Although we
lacked data to assess potential causes, these findings suggest that customers who experience
slower-than-typical timelines are not uncommon.

2.2 Residential Solar PI&I
PI&I processes, requirements, and complexity can affect project timelines, and other studies
have focused specifically on PI&I-related challenges and costs (Burkhardt et al., 2015; Dong and
Wiser, 2013), project cancellations (Cruce et al., 2022a; Liao, 2020; Moezzi et al., 2017), and
interactions between the customer, installer, AHJ, and utility (Sinitskaya et al., 2019).

 4

Many states set minimum PI&I requirements. For permitting, states often select a particular year
for building, electrical, and fire codes. Enforcement of these codes is delegated to AHJs, and
many AHJs set additional and/or more stringent local requirements (Stanfield et al., 2012). For
interconnection, state regulators may mandate specific PI&I process requirements for their
regulated utilities and may impose penalties on utilities that do not meet these requirements. For
example, many states have established a maximum timeline for residential interconnection
application review and response. Other utilities have set review timeline targets or other PI&I
process improvements on their own (Ardani et al., 2015; Fekete et al., 2022).

Installers must navigate these differing PI&I requirements and processes across the tens of
thousands of AHJs and 3,000 utilities in the country. These AHJs and utilities differ in their
levels of PI&I process streamlining or complexity and in their application review and approval
timelines. Complex or burdensome PI&I processes can increase project timelines
(O’Shaughnessy et al., 2022, 2020), soft costs (Burkhardt et al., 2015; Cruce et al., 2022a; Dong
and Wiser, 2013), and project cancellation rates (Cook et al., 2021). For example, surveyed
installers reported that permitting and interconnection challenges are primary drivers of project
delays and customer cancellations (Cook et al., 2021). Streamlining PI&I processes and using
best practices could significantly reduce project timelines and costs (O’Shaughnessy et al.,
2022). For example, online-instant permitting platforms automatically review residential systems
for code compliance and issue approvals with little or no AHJ staff review time required, saving
an average of 14.5 days (31%) across permitting, installation, and inspection timelines (Cook et
al., 2024). The number of AHJs now offering SolarAPP+, Symbium, or other automatic, online-
instant permitting review platforms has accelerated over the last 5 years, along with a widespread
switch to online/electronic permitting options in general (Cruce et al., 2022b).

2.3 Recent Changes in the Residential Solar Market
Other changes in the residential solar market may provide additional insights into year-over-year
changes in project timelines. For example, the benefits of PI&I process improvements can be
offset by large, rapid increases in permitting volume or other challenges—such as the COVID-19
pandemic and associated supply chain disruptions. Between 2010 and 2020, annual residential
PV installations increased by a factor of 10 and, despite a slight slowdown during the first few
months of the COVID-19 pandemic, doubled again through the end of 2023 (Davis et al., 2024,
2022; SEIA, 2020). The residential PV-plus-storage market has also expanded dramatically and
now accounts for 25% of residential solar installations (Davis et al., 2024). When surveyed in
late 2021, many AHJs reported struggling with both this record-high permitting volume and
ongoing staffing challenges (Cruce et al., 2022b). The approval of California’s new net rate
structure for residential solar customers at the end of 2022 led to a rush of projects qualifying
under the previous policy prior to the new policy’s April 15, 2023 effective date, which may
have temporarily delayed interconnection reviews and approvals (Feldman et al., 2024). Other
utilities have also experienced recent challenges and delays with interconnection (Kennedy,
2023). Although these challenges are often temporary as AHJs and utilities learn to adapt, hire
additional staff, and/or further streamline their PI&I (Ardani and Margolis, 2015; Cruce et al.,
2022b), they may offset ongoing improvements to the solar adoption process.

3 Datasets and Methods
This report uses an updated version of the SolarTRACE project-level timeline dataset, which was
originally described in previous publications (Cruce et al., 2022a, 2022b). 2 The dataset was
aggregated from time-stamped data provided by installer-partners covering projects they
installed from 2017–2023.

3.1 Project-Level Timeline Dataset
Since 2019, NREL has partnered with solar installers/developers who provide project-level and
PI&I process data for their residential and small commercial solar projects in the United States.
Since then, 16 unique installers have contributed project-level data, representing a range of small
(<100 installations per year), mid-scale (100–1,000 per year), large (>1,000 per year), and very
large (>10,000 per year) companies. 3 The installer-provided data includes dates for key
milestones of the residential solar adoption process (see Figure 1), namely contract signing,
permitting, utility ATB/interconnection application, installation, inspections, and final request
for PTO. Installers also provided project identifying information necessary for analysis,
including project size (in kilowatts), type (PV only, PV plus storage), AHJ responsible for
permitting, utility, and whether it was a retrofit project or was part of a new construction build.
The installer-provided data was then aggregated into the project-level timeline dataset. 4

The project-level timeline dataset currently encompasses approximately 900,000 PV and PV-
plus-storage projects of up to 20 kW installed from 2017–2023. Of these projects, approximately
875,000 were both retrofits (i.e., not part of new construction) and included at least one set of
dates from which timelines could be calculated (e.g., permit submission and permit approval
dates). Project counts by year, system size, and system type are shown in Table 1, along with
market shares based on annual installation counts from Wood Mackenzie (Davis et al., 2024).
Note that our analysis this year differs from our previous report (Cruce et al., 2022b): We now
include all 875,000 projects with at least one set of timeline dates in our analysis, rather than
only using the smaller subset of projects that include every date for all key milestones tracked.
This decision gives us greater market coverage than our previous analysis, thus potentially better
representing the range and variability in project timelines across installers and jurisdictions. 5 For
comparison, about half of projects (~400,000) include every key milestone date.

We analyzed project timelines by first assessing annual timelines and year-over-year changes
across all retrofit PV or PV-plus-storage projects nationwide in the project-level dataset. Then,
we compared state-by-state timelines for the top 10 states by installation volume (shown in
Figure 2 and Figure 3).

We calculated timelines for each PI&I and non-PI&I phase shown in Figure 1, along with pre-
installation and post-installation phase totals and total project timelines (contract signing to
PTO). Timelines for each phase were bucketed into their corresponding year via two methods:
The design and review phases were categorized by the date of permit submission, and the
installation phase and all post-installation phases were categorized by the date of installation.
Overall project timelines, including pre-installation and post-installation phase totals, were also
based on the installation date.

Finally, note that not all installer-partners provided data for all years in the dataset. Of our 16
installer-partners, five provided data covering 2019–2023, and a sixth provided data covering
2020–2023. Together, these six installers represent about 410,000 projects, referred to as the “six
installer subset.” Although we do not focus our analysis or directly report results on installer-by-
installer differences in the data, we do discuss where year-over-year changes in timeline results
may be affected by the changing number of installers who provided data in that year (for
example, installers with notably faster-than-average timelines in earlier years who were not able
to provide data in 2023).

3.2 Study Limitations
As with our previous work using SolarTRACE data, this study is limited by its data sources and
applicability of results. Our data covers a quarter of residential PV-only projects and a third of
storage projects in the United States in 2019–2023, but does not include every state, AHJ, or
utility that installed solar, nor does it cover all projects across jurisdictions that are included.
Because of the limited number of installer-partners, our results are weighted by the higher
installation volumes from our larger installers; previous studies have found timeline differences
between installers even within the same AHJ (O’Shaughnessy et al., 2020), so timelines reported
here, in our public data, and on our data viewer website might not be fully representative of
timelines seen by all projects in a given jurisdiction. Our choice to use the larger overall project-
level dataset, rather than the smaller subset of projects reporting all key milestone dates, means
that a differing number of installers and projects may be included in the timeline calculations for
each phase. Thus, because timelines for each PI&I or non-PI&I phase—and pre- and post-
installation and overall totals—are calculated individually, results for individual phases cannot
be added together directly to estimate composite timelines.

Finally, because California represents a large proportion of both the U.S. solar market and our
data (45% of projects), our national/overall dataset trends are also heavily weighted by trends in
California. Similarly, national and state-level results may also be weighted by trends in
jurisdictions with the largest installation volumes, both in California and in other states.

4 Results
We first report results for residential and small commercial solar PV timelines (up to 20 kW),
including for the overall project-level dataset and for the top 10 states by market volume. We
then report results for residential storage projects in the same categories.

4.1 PV-Only Timelines (up to 20 kW)
Here, we report timeline summary statistics for PV-only projects up to 20 kW. Timeline results
are first presented for the overall dataset of all installed projects, then by state for the top 10
markets by 2017–2023 volume. Year-over-year timelines from 2017–2023 are reported for each
category.

Note that our previous study (Cruce et al., 2022b) only reported residential PV-only timelines for
systems up to 10 kW. However, we now have sufficient data on systems sized 10–20 kW—
representing large residential and/or small commercial projects—to include in our analysis. We
initially separated the smaller (<10 kW) and larger (10–20 kW) systems into separate data
subsets for our timeline analysis. However, we found that total and most phase timelines for
these subsets have become increasingly similar since 2020: Medians for total project timelines
(contract signing to PTO) were within 6% in 2020 and ±1% since 2021; differences at the
quartiles averaged ±5% since 2020; and means differed by an average of 4%. We also found that
the subset of smaller projects had somewhat more distribution in the range of overall timelines
than the larger projects, both in terms of standard deviation (77 versus 65 days) and interquartile
range (75 versus 69 days). However, given that these timeline differences are relatively small—
and that year-over-year timeline changes are comparable—we have elected to combine both
subsets back into a single dataset consisting of all PV-only projects up to 20 kW for reporting
and analysis. Timelines for each subset (<10 kW, 10–20 kW) are reported in the SolarTRACE
public data file. 7

As shown in the figures, in 2022 and 2023, a median or average PV-only project in the United
States required approximately 100–115 business days between contract signing and PTO (20–23
weeks, or about 4.5–6 months). However, there is significant range across project timelines in
the data: A quarter of projects required 3 months or less (first quartile), while 10% of projects
required more than 9 months and 5% of projects took a year or more. These slower- and much-
slower-than-typical projects skew the average of both total and by-phase timelines upward,
resulting in mean timelines that are usually 5–15 days slower than the median.

The results show that after declining from 2017–2019, overall PV-only project timelines in our
data increased again starting in 2021 (Figure 5). A PV-only project in 2022/2023 took nearly a
month longer to complete than in 2019/2020 (20 business days longer at the average and
median)—a 20%–30% increase. This increase is also found at the quartiles (14 and 26 days
longer at the first and third quartile, respectively) and the 90th percentile, and both the
interquartile range and standard deviation increased by approximately 25 days.

As we identified in our previous report (Cruce et al., 2022b) and as seen in Figure 4, much of the
improvement in overall timelines from 2017–2019 came from faster post-installation timelines,
especially for the inspection and post-inspection (inspect-to-IX submit) phases. Although these
timelines also saw a small uptick during the COVID-19 pandemic (2021–2022), they have since
declined again. Note that the recent uptick in final interconnection/PTO timelines is primarily
due to a significant increase in 2023 timelines in California, which accounts for 45% of the
dataset (see the next section for state-by-state timelines).

 12

The recent longer project timelines in our data are primarily due to increased pre-installation
phase times. Pre-installation timelines have increased steadily since 2020, driven by increases in
both design (sign–submit) and installation (approve–install) times. For the design phase, project
timelines at the median and above nearly doubled between 2020 and 2023: from 2.5 to 4 weeks
at the median, from 3.5 to 7 weeks at the mean and 75th percentile, and from 7 to 13 weeks at the
90th percentile. 8 Installation phase timelines also increased by 1–3 weeks at the median and mean
in 2021/2022, though 2023 timelines have improved slightly. Notably, the longest 10% of
projects (above the 90th percentile) saw substantial increases in installation timelines, doubling
from 2–3 months to 4–6 months or longer. Altogether, total pre-installation phase timelines
(sign–install) increased for all statistical values, with larger increases for longer projects, i.e., an
increase of 1.5 weeks at the 25th percentile, 3.5 weeks at the median, 5 weeks at the mean, 6.5
weeks at the 75th percentile, and 9 weeks at the 90th percentile.

We also evaluated whether these increased timelines were present across all installer-partners
and whether the overall results were affected by the loss of 2023 data from previously faster-
than-average partners. In the six installer subset, five of the six installers saw timelines increase
from their respective lows in either 2019 or 2020. For two of these installers, timelines increased
through 2022 before decreasing again in 2023, while other installers saw continued increases
through 2023. However, timeline increases for each installer and across the entire subset were
generally similar to the overall dataset, suggesting that the year-over-year trends in the overall
timeline data are not primarily caused by the changing availability of partner data for each year. 9

Finally, although not a significant driver of overall timeline changes, we found a larger increase
in AHJ permitting timelines during the COVID-19 pandemic than in our earlier report.
Previously, we found that permit review times increased by about 1–2 days between 2019 and
2020/2021 (Cruce et al., 2022b) at the median and mean. However, our new analysis found a
somewhat larger increase across our larger dataset, of about 3–6 days at the median and mean.

4.1.2 PV-Only State-Level Timelines
Project timelines at the state level provide insights into (1) differences between regional markets
and (2) variability within a particular market. For example, permit and interconnection review
times are known to vary widely across AHJs and utilities, which in turn constitute their
respective states and regional markets. PI&I requirements, processes, and timelines are also often
set or regulated at the state level, such as via public utility commissions and state laws. However,
while stricter state-level requirements or targets alone do not necessarily correlate with faster
timelines (Fekete et al., 2022), states can also encourage or require the use of best practices (e.g.,
California Senate Bill 379). Thus, the question is: How do PI&I and total project timelines differ
both across and within states?

Permit review timelines for the top 10 residential solar states are shown in Figure 6. 10 As seen in
the figure, most states shown here saw permitting timeline increases during the COVID-19
pandemic, and most states saw subsequent permitting timeline improvements in 2023. However,
review times still show significant variability, ranging from same-day at the low end 11 to 15
business days (3 weeks) at the 75th percentile and 30 days (6 weeks) or more at the 90th
percentile (average timelines were between 10 and 15 days).

Our results suggest that total PV-plus-storage timelines (contract signing to PTO) initially
improved as the residential storage market developed—even as total annual installations
increased by a factor of 10 from 2017–2020 (Davis et al., 2024). In 2019/2020, a typical
residential PV-plus-storage project required approximately 100 (median) to 120 (mean) business
days from contract signing to PTO (20–24 weeks, or 4.5–6 months)—that is, only about 25
business days (5 weeks) longer than PV-only projects at the time. However, as with PV-only
projects, PV-plus-storage timelines also increased dramatically starting in 2021. By 2023, PV-
plus-storage timelines were 50%–60% longer than in 2019/2020: The median and mean both
increased by 60 business days, the 75th percentile by 70 days, and the 90th percentile by over 80
days. As a result, in 2023, a typical residential storage project required around 8–9 months from
contract to PTO—or 3 months (13 weeks) longer than for a typical residential PV-only project.

As with PV-only projects, most of the recent increase in PV-plus-storage timelines is from the
pre-installation phase, which doubled from a median of 55 days to 110 days and a mean of 70
days to 130 days between 2020 and 2023. Design phase (sign–submit) timelines accounted for
the majority of this increase (adding 25–30 days, or 5–6 weeks), but installation phase (approve–
install) times also increased (adding another 1.5–4 weeks). In contrast, post-installation phase
timelines have returned to 2020 levels after a moderate increase in 2021–2022.

In comparing review phase timelines, there are a few trends to note. First, total review phase
timelines have been somewhat longer for PV-plus-storage projects than for PV-only projects, by
about 2–9 days at the median and mean over the last several years. For AHJ permit reviews, the
difference is smaller: only up to 2 days at the median and 3–4 days at the mean through 2022,
with a larger difference of 5 days at the median in 2023 (PV-only permitting times declined more

in 2023 than did PV-plus-storage times). The larger difference is between utility pre-installation
interconnection/ATB timelines, where PV-only timelines appear to be much faster than PV-plus-

California, where several of the largest utilities do not require ATB prior to PV-only system
installation—resulting in a “same-day” review timeline for these projects. When California was

5 Conclusions
This study provides an update to previous work that uses the SolarTRACE dataset to evaluate
residential solar timelines (Cruce et al., 2022b, 2022a; Fekete et al., 2022; O’Shaughnessy et al.,
2022). Our project-level dataset now encompasses 22% of residential solar and 33% of
residential storage installations in the United States from 2017–2023. Key takeaways from this
study include the following.

 • After declining from 2017–2019, overall project timelines for residential solar have
 increased by nearly a month since 2019. A typical solar project in the United States now
 requires 4.5–6 months between contract signing and PTO. However, there is also
 significant variability in timelines: There is a range of 3.5 months between the first (3
 months) and third (6.5 months) quartiles, while 10% of projects require more than 9
 months to complete.

 • Recent timeline increases are primarily attributable to longer design (contract signing to
 permit submission) and installation (post-permit) timelines, which collectively increased
 by 1.5–9 weeks at these statistical values (including median, mean, quartiles, and 90th
 percentile).

 • Overall project timelines for residential PV-plus-storage projects are longer than for
 residential solar, and the median/average difference has increased from about 5 weeks in
 2019 to almost 13 weeks (3 months) in 2023. A residential storage project now requires
 8–9 months from contract signing to PTO.

Our results suggest that continued work to improve and streamline PI&I processes is valuable,
but that non-PI&I phases are also increasingly important contributors to project timelines. It
remains unclear what the exact cause(s) of recent increases in project timelines are, or whether a
combination of factors is at play—new data collected over the next year or two, following the
recent contraction of the residential solar market, might provide greater insights in the future.

Our analysis presents several avenues for future work. Such work may include:

 1. Continuing to track PI&I processes, changes, and differences between AHJs/utilities,
 including the use of best practices and online-instant permitting, requirements specific to
 residential storage, and trends in PI&I and overall project timelines

 2. Assessing whether our results are representative of timelines for all projects in these
 AHJs, through additional data sharing and analysis partnerships

 3. Determining the cause(s) of recent timeline increases, especially for the design and
 installation phases

 4. Analyzing the relationship between AHJ-level PI&I changes, AHJ installation volume,
 installer-specific timelines, and other factors on overall results.
