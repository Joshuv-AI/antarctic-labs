Protonated portlandite precursor unlocks enhanced nucleation and growth for cement hydration
Authors: Jingyi Zeng, Qiao Liu, Jin Yang, Zhongyong Zhang, Huangjie Zou, Ying Su, Yingbin Wang, Yubo Li, Neng Li, Xingyang He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCalcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) and calcium hydroxide (CH, portlandite) are the dominant hydration products of Portland cement. However, the anisotropic layered CH and uncoordinated nucleation of C-S-H and CH severely restrict the macroscopic performance of cementitious materials. Here, we report a hydrogen-bond-regulated proton transfer strategy to mechanochemically synthesize a positively charged, isotropic disordered CH precursor (CHP_H+). This precursor serves as a growth-accelerating nucleus to trigger dual heterogeneous nucleation of CH and C-S-H, in situ forming a CH@C-S-H composite. Free energy calculations reveal that hydroxyl vacancies on CH surfaces drive spontaneous water adsorption to generate ≡Ca−OH2+ sites, which stabilize silicate clusters and promote composite growth. The resulting cementitious material shows accelerated hydration kinetics, higher hydration heat release, and greatly improved elastic modulus and compressive strength, delivering a 5.4-fold improvement in 12 h strength over the control. This work provides an atomic-scale design principle for advanced cementitious materials by reprogramming the nucleation and growth pathways of hydration products via protonation regulation.Subject terms: Computational science, Nanoscale materials, Civil engineering, Atomistic models

Introduction

Calcium hydroxide (CH) is a significant inorganic basic compound with a wide range of applications, including construction materials, biomedicine, environmental remediation, capture of greenhouse gases, and cultural relic restoration1–7. CH has a layered crystal structure in which interlayer interactions are controlled by van der Waals (vdW) forces, resulting in low tensile and shear resistance8–10. Layered crystal structures exhibit significant advantages and inherent defects in both performance and application. Their anisotropy and interface effects could be crucial for high-performance design but could also be the source of material failure. A classic example is cement, one of the largest artificial materials, whose hydration products typically contain 15–25 wt.% CH11. The CH may form microcrystals within the C-S-H gel structure to enhance its mechanical properties12–15, or it may grow into crystal-oriented and large-sized particles, thereby impairing the mechanical and durability performance of cementitious materials16–19. Therefore, the critical barrier in the field is how to optimize the interlayer properties of layered materials (e.g., CH) and enhance their interfacial compatibility with the host matrix (e.g., C-S-H) to break through the bottlenecks restricting the performance and application of these materials.

This dilemma stems largely from the nucleation and growth behavior. According to classical nucleation theory, nucleation is the process by which atomic clusters overcome the nucleation energy barrier, formed by the competition between surface energy and volumetric free energy, leading to the formation of a thermodynamically stable phase20,21. During the growth of CH, the OH− densely packed layer forms two-dimensional nuclei preferentially, while the octahedral coordination of Ca2+ causes the nuclei to grow preferentially along the 2D plane22. This results in the anisotropic mechanical properties of CH crystals, i.e., the elastic modulus in the a and b directions exhibits superior performance dynamically, while that in the c direction is poor23–26. Therefore, the key to overcoming the above dilemma lies in how to alter the nucleation and growth behavior of the crystal.

It is reasonable to postulate that if specific nucleation seeds are introduced during the initial nucleation stage to reduce the nucleation energy barrier, they would guide the surrounding atoms or ions to attach to their surface and gradually grow. This allows the phase growth range to disperse across multiple nucleation sites, which can be expected to form refined crystal morphology. Crystal refinement is expected to suppress coarse grain growth in CH crystals, eliminate localized stress concentrations, and mitigate mechanical property defects caused by lattice anisotropy. Furthermore, the introduction of CH precursors with surface functionalization and lattice disorder as templates is expected to induce heterogeneous nucleation of C-S-H, thereby achieving synergistic enhancement of both the interface properties between CH and C-S-H and the microstructure of C-S-H. In fact, during the formation processes of natural biominerals such as shells and bones, organisms utilize organic molecules (such as collagen and silk fibroin) as templates to regulate the oriented crystallization of inorganic minerals (calcium carbonate and calcium phosphate), ultimately forming biomineral materials with high strength and high toughness27,28.

In this work, we proposed a hydrogen-bond-regulated proton transfer strategy to prepare protonated CH (CHP_H+) with disordered structure, and guided the heterogeneous nucleation and cooperative formation of CH and C-S-H, achieving enhanced nucleation of hydrates and breakthroughs in mechanical strength. Due to mechanochemical activation in water, CH was protonated and formed a surface with positive charge (i.e., ≡Ca−OH2⁺) and high adsorption energy. This greatly promoted the adsorption of [SiO4]4− and the nucleation and growth of C-S-H, forming the CH@C-S-H composite structure with high mechanical contribution. Furthermore, the structure-disordered CHP_H+ served as a molecular template to improve the morphology and distribution of CH. This strategy offers a practical pathway to regulate hydrate nucleation and growth for advanced cementitious materials.

Results

Microstructure of the CHP

We proposed a mechanochemical strategy based on water-medium ball grinding to induce protonation on the CH surface. To demonstrate the unique properties of protonated CHP (CHP_H+), raw CH before grinding (CH_Raw), chemically synthesized CHP (CHP_Perf.) with similar particle size, and dry-grinding CHP with hydroxyl-vacancy (CHP_OHV) were selected as control groups. CH_Raw and CHP_Perf. were commercial products manufactured via chemical synthesis (Fig. 1a), with average particle sizes (D50) of 7891.5 and 223.2 nm, respectively. CHP_OHV and CHP_H+ were both prepared from CH_Raw via mechanical grinding (Fig. 1b), with D50 of 260.4 and 247.9 nm, respectively. The particle size distribution curves for the 4 types of CH are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

The crystal structures of 4 types of CH were investigated using high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM), as shown in Fig. 1c–f and Supplementary Fig. 2. CH_Raw and CHP_Perf. possess relatively intact single-crystal structures, characterized by highly ordered atomic arrangements and a unique, consistent crystallographic orientation throughout the crystal (Fig. 1g). In contrast, CHP_OHV and CHP_H+ exhibit substantial crystalline distortions, dislocations, and vacancies (Fig. 1h). This phenomenon is commonly observed after mechanical grinding, as prolonged mechanical force disrupts the crystal lattice, interferes with the regular atomic packing, and reduces the long-range order of the crystal29. The appearance of diffraction rings in the FFT patterns, originating from randomly oriented grains, demonstrated the transition of the CH sample from a single-crystalline to a polycrystalline state after grinding. This evolution signified a diminished preferred orientation, resulting in macroscopically isotropic properties for both CHP_OHV and CHP_H+. Crystal orientation determines the arrangement of atoms on its surface and the nature of the grain boundaries, which affects the pattern of crystal growth and may lead to anisotropic growth habits of the crystal30. TEM observations yield two key conclusions: CHP exhibits crystal defects, and its crystalline orientation is complex and multidirectional rather than uniform. The alteration of the crystalline structure of CHP could prove instrumental in the growth of C-S-H and CH, which will be elaborated upon later.

Protonation mechanism of CHP surface

The protonation phenomenon of CH was first revealed through experimental observations, as shown in Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 3. The protonated CHP_H⁺ dispersed in water exhibited superior dispersibility and stability, with a static sedimentation rate of only 12% after 2 days. As shown in Fig. 2b, the increase in zeta potential indicates that the positive charge density on the surface of CHP_H+ was enhanced. The absence of significant variation in both OH− concentration (Fig. 2c) and conductivity (Supplementary Fig. 4) in the CHP_H+ dispersion suggested that the observed enhancement in zeta potential was not primarily governed by solution ion concentrations. Distinct shoulder peaks were observed at 533.6 eV in the XPS O 1s spectrum (Fig. 2d) and at 983.6 eV in the Auger electron O KL1 spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 5). We assigned this signal peak to the oxygen originating from the –OH2+ group (OP), which is characteristic of the protonated CH. Due to the overlap among the adsorbed water peak (OA), the oxygen vacancy (OV) peak, and the OP peak31,32, further verification was conducted using in-situ XPS in the subsequent section. As shown in Fig. 2e, the 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectrum of CHP_H+ exhibits a distinct shoulder peak at 6 ppm, which could be an overlapping peak formed by hydrogen from adsorbed water and hydrogen from –OH2+2,33. Notably, to eliminate interference from surface-adsorbed water on test results, all post-processing operations for samples were conducted within a N2-atmosphere glove box (Supplementary Fig. 6). The air exposure time (i.e., the interval between sample unpacking and testing) was kept consistent to ensure the adsorbed water content would remain at a similar, low baseline level. As shown in Fig. 2f, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra reveal that the defect concentration of CHP_H+ is markedly lower than that of CHP_OHV but higher than that of CH_Raw and CHP_Perf34. This observation implied that the oxygen vacancies generated during water-medium ball grinding were healed after protonation. Additionally, protonation did not result in a phase change, as no new signal peaks were observed in the Raman spectrum (Fig. 2g), X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectrum (Fig. 2h), or derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) spectrum (Fig. 2i) of CHP_H+. The evolution of the crystallographic parameters (Supplementary Fig. 7) in the XRD spectra and the shift of characteristic peaks toward lower temperatures in the DTG spectrum (Fig. 2i), as observed in the protonated CHP_H+ after grinding, can be primarily attributed to size reduction and lattice distortion. Overall, the protonation of CHP_H+ can be considered as a localized alteration of the chemical environment on the particle surface, involving the transformation of O–H related chemical states, rather than a long-range phase structural change or a variation in ion concentration in the solution.

The protonation of the CH surface follows two potential mechanisms, as in Eqs. (1) and (2) and Eqs. (3) and (4). The first is a classical protonation process, in which solvated H⁺ may be directly grafted onto the oxygen atoms of the perfect CH lattice surface under mechanical force (Fig. 3a). The second is a unique protonation pathway we propose for the aqueous mechanochemical environment, based on Lewis acid-base theory35. Mechanical force elicits the formation of hydroxyl vacancies on the CH surface; this induces local coordination unsaturation, which in turn exposes adjacent Ca2+ and forms discrete isolated Lewis acid sites. Water is a classic Lewis base whose oxygen contains a Lewis base site36. The oxygen in water can be adsorbed at vacancy sites and coordinate with calcium to form ≡Ca−OH2⁺ (Fig. 3b). This hypothesis was verified through computational simulations (Fig. 3) and experimental analyses (Fig. 4).

The thermodynamic behaviors of the two protonation pathways were explained based on density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Two models were constructed: one for perfect CH and another for CH with OHV, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 8. The density of states is presented in Fig. 3c. For the perfect CH model, the valence band maximum near the Fermi level is primarily contributed by the electronic states of O, which exhibits a highly occupied electronic state and an electron-donating tendency. This suggests that the O sites readily act as electron donors to form coordination bonds with H⁺, which is consistent with the electron transfer trend shown in Fig. 3d. For the CH with OHV model, the entire electronic structure shifts toward lower energy. The conduction band minimum, predominantly composed of Ca electronic states, moves closer to the Fermi level, indicating that Ca sites possess empty orbitals with a tendency to accept electrons. This electronic feature supports the role of Ca as an electron acceptor, enabling coordination with oxygen atoms in water molecules, which is consistent with the electron transfer trend shown in Fig. 3e. As shown in Fig. 3f, the adsorption energy of H+ adsorbed on the perfect CH surface is +1.93 eV, indicating that this process cannot occur spontaneously and needs an external environment to provide energy. The adsorption energy of H2O adsorbed on the hydroxyl vacancies of CH is −0.99 eV, indicating that the process is spontaneous.

The two types of protonation pathways were further interpreted by well-tempered metadynamics (WT-MetaD). WT-MetaD simulated the protonation pathways in the Perf.CH + H+ system and the CH with OHV + H2O system, which are represented by Path I (Fig. 3g) and Path II (Fig. 3h), respectively. Both reaction pathways were set up with two sets of collective variables (CVs). In path I, H in a single water molecule dissociates and adsorbs on the oxygen atom of –OH on the surface of the perfect CH, where d(HW-OW) and d(HW-OS) are set to CV1 and CV2, respectively. In Path II, individual water molecules are adsorbed on a hydroxyl vacancy on the CH surface, where d(OW-Ca1) and d(OW-Ca2) are set to CV1 and CV2, respectively. The protonation process in Path I is divided into two stages (Fig. 3i, j). In the initial stage (IS), a water molecule is confined above the surface by hydrogen bonding of three other water molecules and a surface oxygen. In the final stage (FS), a proton of this water molecule is transferred to the oxygen on the surface of perfect CH, which requires crossing a high energy barrier of 54.828 kJ mol−1. The free energy of the system at FS is much larger than that of IS, which suggests that the process is unlikely to occur on a perfect CH surface and that FS is unstable. The protonation process on reaction Path II is divided into three stages (Fig. 3k, l). In the IS, a water molecule above the defective CH is confined above the defect by the hydrogen bonding of three other water molecules. In the middle stage (MS), this water molecule is adsorbed by a ligand-unsaturated calcium ion around the defect on the CH surface after crossing an energy barrier of 8.749 kJ mol−1. In the FS, this water molecule crossing an energy barrier of 16.125 kJ mol−1 is filled to the O defect position and is immobilized by three calcium ions around the defect via Ca–O bonds. At this stage, the energy reaches the lowest point of the system, and the final state is stabilized. Overall, in Path I, the energy of FS is much higher than that of IS, which means the reaction is more unlikely to proceed. In contrast, in Path II, the energy of FS is lower than that of IS, and the reaction energy barrier is lower, which implies that the reaction has the potential to proceed spontaneously. This finding provides a feasible route for experimentally verifying protonation. Rather than tracking the protonation on the CH surface under dynamic grinding conditions, the Path II can be directly verified by statically observing the interaction between the CH with OHV (i.e., CHP_OHV) and water molecules. The experimental verification derived from this theoretical prediction is presented in Fig. 4.

The experimental validation strategy for protonation is shown in Fig. 4a. The isotope deuterium (D) is incorporated as a tracer to eliminate interference from water-medium ball grinding, atmospheric H2O, and –OH in CH, thus clarifying the characteristic signal of the protonated product (i.e., –OH2+). Specifically, deuterated water (D2O) serves as the proton source and contacts the surfaces of CHP_OHV and CHP_H+, thereby tracing the occurrence and pathways of protonation. Meanwhile, CHP_Perf. is employed as the control group to distinguish the exchange between OH⁻ and DO⁻ induced by the ionization of CH in D2O. Finally, the evolution of D, OD, and O after protonation was monitored via 2D NMR, in-situ Raman, and in-situ XPS, respectively.

D2O molecules were controllably adsorbed as a monolayer on the CHP surfaces guided by BET theory, and then 2D NMR testing was performed after desorption and drying. This experiment effectively eliminates signal interference caused by CH ionization in D2O, thereby providing crucial evidence to determine whether surface reactions occur. Firstly, based on the H2O adsorption-desorption isotherms (Supplementary Fig. 9), a pressure condition (420 Pa) was determined where the adsorbed capacities of the three samples were comparable and all exhibited monolayer adsorption. Secondly, D2O cyclic adsorption-desorption experiments were conducted at a programmed maximum pressure of 420 Pa (Fig. 4b–d). The results showed that the adsorbed capacity of CHP_OHV decreased by 22.7% in the second adsorption cycle compared to the first, while that of CHP_Perf. decreased by only 7.1%, indicating the presence of chemical adsorption on the surface of CHP_OHV. The slightly larger reduction rate for CHP_H+ relative to CHP_Perf. arises from incomplete protonation of OH vacancies on its surface during water-medium ball milling. Finally, 2D NMR test (Fig. 4e) and Raman test (Supplementary Fig. 10) were conducted on the samples after D2O adsorption-desorption. Both the 2D NMR spectrum and the Raman spectrum of CHP_OHV and CHP_H+ revealed signal peaks associated with D, whereas CHP_Perf. showed no distinct signal peaks. The results indicate that D2O undergoes chemical adsorption on the CHP_OHV surface and generates deuterated hydroxyl groups, thereby experimentally confirming the thermodynamic feasibility of protonation of CH with defects.

As shown in Fig. 4f–i, the proton transfer process was monitored by in-situ Raman testing in a D2O gas atmosphere. The A1g(OH) peaks of Ca(OH)2 and Ca(OD)2 are located at 3617 and 2664 cm−1, respectively37. CHP_Perf. exhibited its initial –OD peak at 25 min (Fig. 4f), attributed to proton exchange induced by CH ionization. In contrast, CHP_OHV (Fig. 4g) and CHP_H+ (Fig. 4h) exhibited –OD peaks at 7 and 10 min, respectively, attributed to proton transfer induced by protonation. Based on the deconvolution results of the in-situ Raman testing (Supplementary Fig. 11), the variation in –OD proportion is shown in Fig. 4i. Before 20 min, the Ca(OD)2 contents of CHP_OHV and CHP_H+ increased slowly, corresponding to proton exchange driven by protonation. After 20 min, the Ca(OD)2 content in all groups rose sharply, suggesting that CH ionization began to dominate the subsequent proton exchange process. The results further confirm the feasibility of the protonation process and enable preliminary quantification of the protonation degree.

As shown in Fig. 4j–m, in-situ XPS was used to investigate the evolution of the O 1s binding energy of the samples during H2O vapor adsorption and thermal desorption processes. The purpose of this test is to provide experimental evidence for the assignment of the protonated characteristic peak (OP) in Fig. 2d, and to exclude interference from the OA and OV peaks within the overlapping region regarding the assignment of the OP peak. The three peaks (OV, OA, and OP) near 532–533 eV were deconvoluted into a single composite peak to eliminate the systematic errors caused by subjective peak splitting fitting (Supplementary Fig. 12). After H2O vapor injection, the relative proportion of the peaks in the 532–533 eV range increased for both CHP_Perf. and CHP_OHV, and their main peaks shifted toward higher binding energy by 0.4 eV. After stopping H2O injection and heating for desorption, the O 1s spectra of both samples gradually recovered to their initial state. The peak shift is attributed to the variation in the intensity of the OA peak (ca. 532 eV). In contrast, due to the presence of a signal peak at 533.6 eV for CHP_H+, the total content of OV, OA, and OP at each time point is markedly higher than that of the other two groups (Fig. 4m), while its main peak shows a shift of only 0.2 eV (Fig. 4l). Further normalized comparison revealed that neither CH refinement nor physically adsorbed water induced significant shoulder formation at 533.6 eV (Supplementary Fig. 13), indicating that the characteristic signal at this position does not originate from defects or molecular water adsorption but should be attributed to the distinctive OP peak formed during protonation.

The protonation process disrupts electrostatic interactions, maintaining the CHP surface in a state of positive charge. It is expected that protonated CH will exhibit adsorption advantages toward anionic groups such as SiO44−, CO32−, SO32−, and CrO42−, demonstrating broad application prospects.

Enhanced nucleation effect of protonated CHP

The effect of protonated CHP on the nucleation and growth of C-S-H is a topic that merits discussion. During the IS of cement hydration, the clinker dissolves and releases Ca2+ and [SiO4]4−, which saturate the pore solution. At this stage, it is expected that positively charged protonated CHP will serve as a template for C-S-H gel growth, inducing heterogeneous nucleation of C-S-H gel by adsorbing [SiO4]4− groups, as shown in Fig. 5a. This would promote C-S-H growth on CHP particles that are distant from clinker grains, rather than direct deposition on the clinker surface. The above mechanistic hypothesis was verified through a combination of theoretical simulations and experimental tests.

Atomic models of [SiO4]4− adsorption on the surfaces of perfect CH and protonated CH were established. As shown in Fig. 5b, [SiO4]4− tends to form ionic bonds on the CH surface, while on the protonated CH surface, it tends to form covalent bonds that are more stable. As shown in Fig. 5c, the adsorption energy of [SiO4]4− on the protonated CH surface reaches −5.92 eV, which is 1.75 times that of [SiO4]4− on the CH surface (−3.39 eV). The results indicate that protonation enhances the affinity of CH towards [SiO4]4−, providing theoretical support for the enhanced nucleation effect.

A series of comparative experiments was conducted to further validate the adsorption advantage of protonated CH towards [SiO4]4−. As shown in Fig. 5d, three types of CHP were individually introduced into the extracted cement pore solution (CPS), and changes in Si content within both CPS and CHP were measured after 12 h standing. As shown in Fig. 5e, the addition of CHP_H+ reduced the Si concentration in CPS by 35.7%, which was much higher than the effects observed in CHP_Perf. (3.6%) and CHP_OHV (13.4%). As shown in Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 14, CHP_H+ induces greater C-S-H gel formation in CPS, with the thermogravimetry (TG) weight loss of the C-S-H gel reaching 6.81%, which was much higher than that of CHP_Perf. (4.72%) and CHP_OHV (5.74%). The microstructure and atomic content of CHP_Perf. (Fig. 5g), CHP_OHV (Fig. 5h), and CHP_H+ (Fig. 5i) after standing in CPS were investigated by TEM-EDS analysis. A substantial amount of gel adheres to the surface of CHP_H+, with Si content of 0.41%. In contrast, the gel content adhering to the surfaces of CHP_Perf. and CHP_OHV is lower, with Si contents of 0.25% and 0.29%, respectively. Furthermore, consistent with the trend observed in Fig. 2a, CHP_H+ maintained prolonged dispersion without rapid sedimentation in the CPS (Supplementary Fig. 15), which was favorable for the nucleation and growth of C-S-H gel on the CHP_H+ surface. The results indicate that protonated CHP can effectively capture [SiO4]4− and induce the formation of C-S-H gel, revealing its potential as an enhanced nucleation promoter.

The influence of CHP_Perf., CHP_OHV, and CHP_H+ at a 5 wt.% dosage on the properties of cement paste was investigated to further explore the enhanced nucleation effect of protonated CHP. The hydration heat release results are shown in Fig. 6a. CHP_H+ had an accelerating effect on the cement hydration, with the value of the main hydration peak increasing by 48.0%, 52.2%, and 46.9% compared to the group without CHP, the CHP_Perf. group, and the CHP_OHV group, respectively. The time of main hydration peak of the CHP_H+ group was the shortest, reduced by 31.5% compared to the group without CHP. The compressive strength results are shown in Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 16. CHP_H+ exhibits excellent performance in enhancing compressive strength, and its 12-h strength reaches 16.7 MPa, which is much higher than that of Without CHP (2.6 MPa), CHP_Perf. (6.3 MPa) and CHP_OHV (7.5 MPa). Based on the results of TG (Supplementary Fig. 17) and quantitative XRD (Supplementary Fig. 18), the phase composition of the samples at 12 h and 28 days was quantified, as shown in Fig. 6c. During the early hydration stage (12 h), CHP_H+ can accelerate the hydration reaction of C3S and induce the formation of more C-S-H gel. In the later hydration stages (28 days), the phase composition of the hydration products in all samples is similar except for CH content. The acceleration effect of CHP_H+ is comparable to that of C-S-H seed38–40, with no negative impact on later-stage strength, making it a promising cement hydration accelerator.

The pore structure of the samples at 28 days was explored as shown in Fig. 6d. The pore size can be divided into four ranges as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1941. All three types of CHP can effectively decrease the porosity of cement paste and refine its pore size distribution, an improvement that is primarily attributed to the nano-micron scale filling effect of the precursor particles. However, the most probable aperture of the CHP_H+ group is only 12.81 nm, much lower than that of CHP_Perf. (22.31 nm) and CHP_OHV (21.16 nm). This indicates that CHP_H+ not only acts as a physical filler but also induces the heterogeneous nucleation and dense growth of C-S-H gel on its surface via the enhanced nucleation effect, which aligns with the results in Fig. 5. To further characterize the effect of CHPs on the complex and heterogeneous pore structure, fractal geometry is used and the results are shown in Fig. 6e. The fractal dimension ranges from 2 to 3, with lower values indicating smoother and flatter pore surfaces, while higher values signify irregular and rough pore surfaces42. The pore structure shows two fractal regions with well-defined D values, separated by a transition region without clear fractal property. Research indicates that Region I, the macroscopic fractal region, represents the packing pattern of cement particles. Region II, the transition region, is more related to the random space left between C-S-H bundles and other larger hydrates, such as CH and AFt43. Region III, the microscopic fractal region, reflects the microstructure of C-S-H hydrates. Figure 6f shows the primary phases corresponding to different fractal regions. Overall, compared to CHP_Perf. and CHP_OHV, the Region II range of CHP_H+ extends toward lower pore sizes, which indicates an increased content of structurally complex CH and C-S-H bundles. This implies that the stacking patterns and microstructure of the hydration products have undergone alterations, which will be further verified in Fig. 7.

The 29Si NMR spectrum of the samples at 28 days obtained by fitting is shown in Fig. 6g. These peaks show different connectivity patterns of silicon44: the monomer of Q0 represents an isolated or unreacted silicon-oxygen (Si–O) tetrahedron; the terminal group of Q1 is a dimer bond of the tetrahedron; the middle group of Q2(Al) and Q2 represent the linear bonds of Si–O tetrahedron. By employing deconvoluted spectra, the relative peak intensities can be employed to determine the hydration degree (α) and the silicate polymerization state (Fig. 6h), which is characterized by the main chain length (MCL) and Al/Si ratios (Fig. 6i). The addition of all three types of CHP enhanced α and MCL compared to the group without CHP, which involve multiple mechanisms such as solubilization and nucleation. CHP efficiently supplies Ca2+ and forms bridging bonds during silicate polymerization, thereby promoting the formation of long-chain C-S-H gel15. Compared with other groups, the addition of CHP_H+ has no significant effect on α (2.60–5.68% increase), but it significantly affects Q2 (19.32–31.25% increase). Under similar α values, CHP_H+ induces a larger MCL compared to the group without CHP, increasing by 10.91%, which is much higher than that of CHP_Perf. (3.01%) and CHP_OHV (1.85%).

Growth habits of CH and C-S-H regulated by protonated CHP

The elastic modulus maps (Fig. 7a–d) and the corresponding experimental elastic modulus frequency and the theoretical probability density function (Supplementary Fig. 20) were plotted based on 32 × 32 elastic modulus maps. Deconvolution results of the theoretical frequency counts for all samples are presented in Fig. 7e–h. Previous studies45–47 have reported that the elastic modulus is classified into the following categories: pore (≈ 7 GPa), low density C-S-H (≈ 20 GPa), high density (HD) C-S-H (≈ 35 GPa), ultra-high density (UHD) C-S-H (≈ 45 GPa), CH (≈ 58 GPa), and cement clinker (≈ 120 GPa). The nanoindentation technique addresses the limitations of the test in Fig. 6c in distinguishing gel products and provides deeper insights into the effects of various CHPs on the microstructural mechanical properties of C-S-H gels. Supplementary Fig. 21 shows the proportion of each component and the reasons for discrepancies with quantitative XRD/TG results.

In this study, the water-cement ratio and curing age (28 days) were adjusted to achieve similar compressive strength and phase composition across all groups (Supplementary Fig. 22), facilitating the precise distinction between the nucleation and filling effects of CHP and the additional improvement effect resulting from protonation. Previous studies have indicated that the restricted hydration space allows nanoscale CH to be tightly embedded within the C-S-H gel, resulting in a CH@C-S-H composite structure that contributes to the enhanced mechanical performance of the gel phase13–15. The nano-micron scale of CHP_Perf. and CHP_OHV provided the initial conditions for the formation of this composite structure, leading to a notable increase in HD C‑S‑H phase content compared with the group without CHP. Furthermore, the enhanced nucleation effect induced by protonation modification further promoted the formation of UHD C-S-H, increasing its content in the CHP_H+ group from 3.8% in the CHP-free group to 17.4%. Typically, UHD C-S-H exhibits higher content in ultra-high performance concrete with low water-cement ratios48. Although the mechanical identification of UHD as CH remains feasible49, the UHD C-S-H content in the CHP_Perf. and CHP_OHV groups was only 8.6% and 9.3%, respectively, ruling out interference from exogenous CH addition. Two key mechanisms underpin this observed phenomenon. First, the CHP_H+ triggers the formation of CH@C-S-H composite structures via heterogeneous nucleation, with this process being directly driven by surface protonation. Second, as shown in Fig. 7i, j, polymorphic CHP induces the formation of irregular CH, which weakens the anisotropy of the CH crystal structure and results in a more uniform modulus distribution across the CH phase.

The effects of CHP on the size and morphology of CH in cement paste were observed by scanning electron microscope (SEM). As CH is difficult to observe directly due to encapsulation by C-S-H gel, CH in natural micropore regions was selected for characterization, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 23. Given the randomness in the size and distribution of natural micropores, which could cause observational deviations, we introduced an equal volume of fine N2 bubbles into fresh samples to create artificial micropores (Supplementary Fig. 24). These artificial micropores further mitigate the inhibitory effect of spatial confinement on CH growth50 and provide a stable observation window. In addition, SEM-EDS of ettringite was provided to distinguish it from CH (Supplementary Figs. 25 and 26). The CH in both the CHP_Perf. group and the group without CHP showed classic hexagonal plate morphology. As shown in Fig. 7k, CH is usually considered to precipitate as large crystals of tens of micrometers in capillary pores14. As shown in Fig. 7l, the uniformly distributed CHP provided extra nucleation sites for CH precipitation and growth, which increased the number of CH and reduced the particle size of CH. Moreover, the irregular CH with weakened anisotropy was identified in Fig. 7m, n. As expected, the complex multidirectional crystalline state of CHP_OHV and CHP_H+ tends to induce similarly complex multidirectional CH, i.e., the template effect.

Discussion

This study reveals the kinetic mechanism of protonation on the surface of CH, and further elucidates the enhanced nucleation effect of protonated CHP on the hydration process and microstructure evolution of Portland cement. The corresponding hydration behavior and structural evolution are shown in Fig. 8a–e. During the early hydration period (Fig. 8d), protonation endows the surface of CHP_H⁺ with ≡Ca−OH2+ active sites (Fig. 8a), enabling it to break through the limitation of conventional CH precursors that only induce homogeneous nucleation of themselves, thus exerting a dual nucleation effect (Fig. 8b). On the one hand, CHP_H⁺ provides sites for the oriented nucleation and growth of the CH phase. On the other hand, CHP_H⁺ adsorbs [SiO4]4− via electrostatic interaction and induces C-S-H nucleation (Fig. 5a–e), accelerates the nucleation and growth of C-S-H gel (Fig. 6a–c), and forms the CH@C-S-H composite hydration product (Fig. 5f, i). During the hydration process (Fig. 8e), CHP_H+ continuously exerts a dual regulatory effect (Fig. 8c). It effectively inhibits the coarse and anisotropic growth of the CH phase (Fig. 7k–n), while promoting the formation of UHD C-S-H (Fig. 7d, h, j) and resulting in a dense microstructure (Fig. 6d–i). In contrast, non-protonated CHP can only induce homogeneous growth of CH (Fig. 8h, i), refine the CH crystal structure, and form a conventional HD C-S-H (Fig. 8j). It is difficult to achieve the synergistic regulation and structural optimization of hydration products as seen with CHP_H+ (Fig. 8f, g). Protonation regulation fundamentally reprograms the growth pathways of cement hydration products at the atomic level, offering an approach to the theoretical design and performance optimization of advanced cementitious materials.

Methods

Materials

Ordinary Portland cement (P.O. 42.5 type) was produced from Hubei Huaxin Cement Co., Ltd. The XRD, phase composition, XRF, and particle size of the cement are shown in Supplementary Fig. 27. CH_Raw (CH content ≥ 99%) was purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. CHP_Perf. (CH content ≥ 99%) was purchased from Hebei Yuanying New Materials Co., Ltd. D2O (99.9%) was purchased from Jinan Manalab Technology Co., Ltd. Alumina (≥ 99.9%) was purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.

Preparation of CHP_OHV and CHP_H+

CHP_OHV and CHP_H+ were both prepared under the conditions shown in Supplementary Fig. 6. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 28, CHP_H+ was prepared by mixing CH_Raw (30 × g), deionised water (150 × g) and balls (400 × g), then grinding for 2 h. CHP_OHV was prepared by mixing CH_Raw (3 × g) and balls (500 × g), then grinding for 3 h. For tests of particle size, zeta potential, conductivity, and pH, all samples were first diluted (water-to-solid ratio = 300) and sonicated for 5 min. For tests of XPS, 1H NMR, EPR, Raman, XRD, and TG, sample preparation followed the methods outlined in Supplementary Fig. 6.

Sample preparation

The dosage of each type of CHP is 5 wt.% by mass of cement. The water-to-cement ratio was set to 0.35. All types of CHP were first mixed with the water (CHP/water = 1:7) and ultrasonically dispersed for 10 min. These mixtures are then added to the cement and stirred for 2 min. Then, the cement slurries were cast into square molds with internal dimensions of 40 × 40 × 40 mm3, and were cured in standard environment (>90% R.H. and 20 ± 1 °C) at the age of 8 h, 12 h, 1, 3, 7, and 28 days. For tests of XRD, TG and NMR, the cement pastes were ground into powder and passed through a 200-mesh sieve. For tests of Mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) and SEM, the cement pastes were split into small pieces, around 2 and 5 mm, respectively. For tests of indentation, the cement pastes were vacuum dried, epoxy impregnated and polished according to ref. 46.

Test methods

Characterization of CH

TEM images were obtained using an FEI Talos F200X G2 (USA). Samples were uniformly dispersed in absolute ethanol after being ultrasonically dispersed.

The particle size was determined by Brookhaven Instruments NanoBrook Dynamic Light Scattering. Deionized water was used as the dispersion medium, and particles were dispersed using ultrasonication.

The zeta potential was measured using NanoBrook Dynamic Light Scattering from Brookhaven Instruments.

The conductivity and pH value were measured by conductivity meter (Seven Compact S230) and pH meter (Seven Compact, Mettler Toledo).

The XPS spectrum and Auger electron spectrum were acquired using a Thermo Scientific ESCALAB Xi+ spectrometer (USA) with a monochromatic Al anode Kα radiation (1486.6 eV), and the charging effects were corrected by setting the C 1s binding energy of the adventitious carbon to 284.8 eV.

Solid-state 1H NMR spectra were acquired on a Bruker AVANCE NEO 400 MHz spectrometer with a 4 mm zirconia rotor at a spinning rate of 10 kHz.

An EPR spectrometer (Bruker EMXplus-6/1, Germany) was employed to analyze the oxygen vacancies in the specimens.

Raman measurements for the specimens were performed using a Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution (Japan) system. Spectra were acquired using 488 nm laser excitation, covering a wavenumber range of 50–4000 cm−1.

XRD measurements were performed using a Rigaku SmartLab SE diffractometer (Japan) to determine the mineral phase composition. The measurements were conducted over a 2θ range of 5°–80° with a scanning rate of 5° min−1 and a step size of 0.02°.

TG was generated using thermogravimetric calorimeter (German-resistant STA449F3) with a heating rate of 10 °C min−1, spanning from room temperature to 800 °C under the nitrogen atmosphere.

H2O/D2O adsorption isotherms were measured using a BSD-660 analyzer (Beishide, Beijing). Prior to measurement, the samples were degassed at 120 °C for 8 h. The analysis was performed with H2O/D2O vapor at a testing temperature of 60 °C.

2D NMR testing was performed using a Bruker Avance NEO 600 MHz spectrometer with a 4 mm zirconia rotor at a spinning rate of 10 kHz.

In-situ Raman spectra for the samples were recorded using a Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution (Japan) instrument with a 488 nm laser excitation. The sample was pretreated in the Raman reaction cell at 120 °C to remove adsorbed water. Then the temperature was lowered to 60 °C and D2O vapor was introduced at a rate of 1 mL min−1.

In-situ XPS spectra were acquired using a Thermo Fisher Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi system. The sample was pretreated in the XPS reaction cell at 120 °C to remove adsorbed water. Then, water vapor was introduced at a rate of 1 mL min−1 at 60 °C, with measurements taken every 10 min. After 50 min, water vapor injection was stopped and the temperature was raised to 120 °C.

Characterization of cement paste

The concentration of Si in the pore solution was determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Agilent 5110, USA). For the measurement, a 0.5 mL aliquot of the solution was digested with 2 wt.% HNO3 and subsequently diluted to a final volume of 5 mL with deionized water.

XRD patterns of the cement paste samples at different curing ages were recorded using a Rigaku SmartLab SE diffractometer (Japan) with Cu Kα radiation. The data were collected over a 2θ range of 5°–90° at a scanning speed of 2° min−1. Alumina was used as an internal standard, and its content accounted for 20% of the total mass.

The compressive testing machine (DYE-330A) was used to test the compressive strength, and the loading speed was 2.4 kN s−1. For each mixture, 6 samples were tested and the results are averages.

Isothermal calorimetry (TAM AIR, C80) was used to monitor the hydration heat. 4.5 × g of paste was poured into a sealed plastic ampoule with a constant temperature of 20 °C.

Thermogravimetric (TG) was generated using thermogravimetric calorimeter (German-resistant STA449F3) with heating rate of 10 °C min−1, spanning from room temperature to 900 °C under the nitrogen atmosphere.

SEM images and EDS elemental mapping image were obtained using a German Zeiss GeminiSEM 300 system.

The micro-mechanical property of cement paste (28 days) was measured by the NANOVER CB500 instrument. Samples were vacuum dried, epoxy impregnated and polished before the test. The test was performed on a pre-set 32 × 32 grid at 10 μm intervals, where irregular data were discarded and retested next to the grid until the number of qualified data reached 1024. The load linearly increased to the maximum load of 5 mN within 10 s, then the 5 mN load lasted for 5 s, and the final 10 s linearly decreased to 0 mN. Regions for nanoindentation were selected to avoid cracks, large pores and large cement clinker particles to ensure sufficient valid data points.

Computational details

The well-defined first-principles calculations based on DFT were implemented in the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP)55, using the frozen-core projector augmented-wave method to describe the interaction between the atomic cores and the valence electron density56. The exchange-correlation potential was approximated within the generalized gradient approximation using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional57. The VASPsol++ module was used to add an aqueous environment58. The dispersion corrected DFT-D3 schemes was employed to describe the vdW interactions59. Plane-wave cutoff energy was set to 500 eV. The conjugate gradient algorithm was used in ionic optimization, convergence threshold was set to 10−5 eV in electronic relaxation and 0.05 eV Å−1 in Hellmann–Feynman force on each atom. The Brillouin zone in reciprocal space was sampled by a Γ-centered Monkhorst-Pack scheme with 2 × 2 × 1 k-point grids for geometry optimization60. Atomic coordinates of the optimized DFT computational models are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

The WT-MetaD simulation was used as an enhanced sampling method to reveal the protonation mechanisms of different systems61. The WT-MetaD simulations were implemented by CP2K/Quickstep code62 and PLUMED code63. The protonation process of perfect CH and CH with O vacancy were among the study components that were taken into consideration when determining the WT-MetaD parameters. A history-dependent bias is introduced into the potential through sporadic additions of Gaussian functions in metadynamics simulations. The system is pushed out of kinetic traps and into uncharted areas of the energy landscape by the potential energy. In order to guarantee a smoother convergence with a bias factor, WT-MetaD scales the heights of Gaussian hills at each step64. A Gaussian hill with an initial height of 1 kJ mol–1, a width of 0.2 Å, and a bias factor of 10 for water dissociation was placed every 5 timesteps (0.5 fs) in this study. Supplementary Fig. 30 shows time-evolution free energy profiles, which demonstrate the convergence of the metadynamics simulations. The free energy profiles remained stable with further simulation time, indicating that the metadynamics calculations have converged. Initial and final configurations are provided in Supplementary Data 1.
