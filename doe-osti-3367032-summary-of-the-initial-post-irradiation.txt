 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Reference cladding systems for sodium fast reactors are based on the historical steel, HT-9. HT-9 is a
Fe12Cr ferritic/martensitic steel with additions of Mo, W, V, and other minor elements and demonstrates
low irradiation swelling and adequate mechanical properties. Extensive irradiation literature exists on the
use of HT-9 as cladding for metal fuel, primarily irradiation on the U-Zr/HT-9 system from the
Experimental Breeder Reactor-II (EBR-II) and Fast Flux Test Facility (FFTF) sodium fast reactor [1–3],
and as a structural material from experiments in the FFTF [4–6]. The large amount of historical data makes
the U-Zr/HT-9 system the reference fuel specification for many nuclear reactor vendors that seek to license
modern sodium-cooled fast reactors in the United States. However, it is yet unclear how variations in
impurity content within HT-9 fundamentally affect irradiation performance at various irradiation
temperatures. Recent work suggests that impurity content may noticeably alter the production of helium
through nuclear transmutation [7]. For these reasons, High-Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) irradiation of HT-9
steels with known variations in the impurity content is particularly timely to generate data to enable more
accurate refinement of the chemical specification for nuclear-grade HT-9 material. This report summarizes
the initial transmission electron microscopy characterization of HFIR-irradiated HT-9 steels following
mechanical property measurements by the Advanced Fuels Campaign (AFC) [8]. This report includes
qualitative results of the cavity, dislocation loop and cluster/precipitate microstructures as well as radiation-
induced segregation. Quantitative results are being shared with partner institutions and will be included in
more detail in a future report in FY2026.

Two versions of HT-9 steel subjected to a two-step heat treatment (1040 °C for 30 min (air-cooled) followed
by 760 °C for 1 hour (air-cooled)) were provided by AFC partnering institutions and irradiated in the HFIR
as part of a larger irradiation campaign on ferritic-martensitic steels and related alloys [9]. Compositions
for the high-nitrogen and low-nitrogen heats are shown in Table 1 and are provided through the vendor.
Details of the HFIR irradiation conditions are shown in Table 2. The temperatures reported are from SiC
thermometry analysis and more information is available in a previous report [8].

Through-focus bright-field (BF) TEM (using an objective aperture of 10 μm) was conducted to characterize
the size and density of cavities smaller than 5 nm in diameter. Underfocused and overfocused images were
collected with a defocus value between -1 μm to +1 μm to obtain precise size measurements [11]. Images
were taken at a field of view of 314 × 314 nm with a 4096 × 4096 resolution (pixel size of about 0.08 nm).
Representative TEM-BF micrographs are shown in Figure 1.

Figure 1: (a) Underfocused and (b) overfocused micrographs of sample HL12. Red arrows indicate small
 cavities.

High-angle annular dark field (HAADF) and BF images were collected to characterize cavities larger than
5 nm in diameter. Cavities appear dark in HAADF and bright in BF micrographs. Images had a field of

  Figure 2: (a) HAADF and (b) BF micrographs of sample HL12. Red arrows indicate several cavities.

Sessile a〈100〉 loops on the [100] habit planes and glissile a/2〈111〉 loops on the [111] habit planes
form under irradiation in ferritic-martensitic steels [12]. To image them, STEM-BF micrographs near the
[001] zone axis were collected. The a〈100〉 loops appear edge-on in the image, perpendicular with the
[200] directions while the a/2〈111〉 loops appear as elongated ellipses perpendicular with the [110]
directions. To differentiate potential dislocation loops from clusters/precipitates and possible FIB damage,
each suitable location was imaged via a tilt series to confirm the nature of observed defects. Images had a
field of view of 452 × 452 nm with a 2048 × 2048 resolution, leading to a pixel size of about 0.22 nm. A
representative, near on-zone STEM-BF micrograph is shown in Figure 3. The high density of other
microstructural features makes the characterization of dislocation loops challenging and detailed analysis
of the tilt series is ongoing to determine quantitative values.

To characterize precipitates and elemental radiation-induced segregation (RIS) at grain boundaries,
compositional maps were collected via STEM-EDS using the Super-X energy dispersive spectrometer
system. For RIS, the sample was tilted to place the boundary edge-on, and images had a field of view of
about 226 × 226 nm with a 1024 × 1024 resolution, leading to a pixel size of about 0.22 nm. Representative
elemental maps for RIS are shown in Figure 4.

To image precipitates such as Cr-rich M23C6 carbides, Ni-Si-Mn clusters (G-phase) or α’ precipitates,
images were taken with a field of view between 639 and 5040 nm with a 1024 × 1024 resolution, leading
to a pixel size of 0.62 and 4.92 nm, respectively. Representative elemental maps are presented in Figure 5
and Figure 6. Since the difference between the two heats studied is the nitrogen content, characterizing
nitrides is of particular importance. However, because nitrogen is a light element, STEM-EDS is not
adequate to study its partitioning in the microstructure. Elements known to bond with nitrogen to form
nitrides in HT-9 (V and Cr) will be considered instead to quantify the nitride microstructure, as shown in
Figure 6 with potential V,Cr-nitrides forming at grain boundaries.

In summary, cavities, dislocation loops and clusters/precipitates as well as radiation-induced segregation at
grain boundaries of two heats of HT-9 irradiated in HFIR have been characterized using TEM.
Quantification includes size distributions (cavities, loops and precipitates) and elemental
enrichment/depletion values at grain boundary. Results are being shared with Los Alamos National
Laboratory to support a future milestone for the Advanced Fuels Campaign.
