ABSTRACT
Key Points•HAPLO and MMUD transplants achieve post-transplant outcomes that are not different than MUD in a large, prospective study.•UCB transplantation was associated with inferior survival and treatment-related mortality compared to MUD.

Introduction

HLA-identical sibling donors (or matched related donors [MRD]) are considered optimal donors for allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT). However, only approximately one-third of patients will have an available MRD.1 Because the average age of transplant recipients continues to increase and younger donors are preferred, MRD use will likely decrease further. HLA-matched unrelated donors (MUD; 8/8), defined as donors that matched at the allele level at HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, and HLA-DRB1, are traditionally considered the best donors for patients lacking MRD.2, 3, 4, 5 Nevertheless, many patients, including disproportionately those from racial minorities and multiracial backgrounds, face barriers to HCT because of the unavailability of MUD.1,6,7

Recent advances in the safe use of HLA-mismatched donors, including haploidentical related donors (HAPLO; or mismatched related donors), mismatched unrelated donors (MMUD), and umbilical cord blood (UCB) donors, have greatly expanded access to HCT for patients lacking MRD and MUD.5 Retrospective comparisons of patients who received transplants from HLA-mismatched donors using regimens that effectively controlled graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) to those who received transplants from MUD show similar outcomes for many post-HCT end points.4,5,8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 However, prospective comparisons are lacking.

BMT CTN 1702 is a multicenter prospective biologic assignment clinical trial, in which patients likely to benefit from allogeneic HCT and lacking MRD had a donor search prognosis score calculated based on HLA and patient race/ethnicity. Patients who were very likely to find MUD were guided to proceed to MUD search and transplantation, whereas patients very unlikely to find an MUD group were guided to proceed directly to HCT using a HAPLO, MMUD, or UCB donor per institutional preference without further attempts to find MUD to avoid delayed HCT. Patients in the “less likely” category had no donor selection strategy specified and followed institutional preference. The primary analysis of BMT CTN 1702 compared outcomes for the 3 donor search prognosis categories (very likely, very unlikely, or less likely to have an 8/8 HLA-matched MUD donor), whether or not the patients received a transplant.14 This study only reports on outcomes in patients who received a transplant according to the actual donor used (MUD, HAPLO, MMUD, or UCB). It describes previously unreported individual comparisons of HAPLO, MMUD, and UCB to MUD and indirectly provides a comparison of the 3 types of alternative donors to each other. This study also describes a previously unreported subgroup comparison of outcomes of MUD vs HAPLO or MMUD transplants, where all patients received posttransplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy)–based GVHD prophylaxis.

Methods

Study design

The study design has been published previously.15,16 Briefly, this was a multicenter biologic assignment study that directed patients with a very low likelihood of finding an 8/8 HLA-A–, HLA-B–, HLA-C–, and HLA-DR–matched MUD to proceed to HCT expeditiously using the best available alternative donor and compared their outcomes to the group very likely to find an MUD. There were no requirements for age, disease status, conditioning regimen, or GVHD prophylaxis.

Other eligibility requirements included diagnoses of acute myeloid leukemia, acute lymphoblastic leukemia, myelodysplastic syndromes, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, Hodgkin lymphoma, acquired aplastic anemia, or sickle cell disease. Participants had to be considered as suitable HCT candidates per institutional standards, with the intent to proceed to HCT within 6 months of enrollment, and needed to commit to following the donor search strategy dictated by the search prognosis algorithm. Patients who had a prior allogeneic HCT or formal unrelated donor search were not eligible. The study was approved by the National Marrow Donor Program Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent. The trial was registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov as NCT03904134.

Biostatistical considerations

Sample size

Sample size requirements for this study, based on primary end point analyses, have been detailed previously16 and are thus not reproduced here.

Analysis plan

This prespecified secondary analysis focused on the subset of patients who received a transplant, analyzed according to the donor type received. Population characteristics were compared using the Pearson χ2 or Kruskal-Wallis tests for categorical or continuous variables, respectively. Outcomes were measured from the day of transplantation and were censored at last contact. Posttransplant overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) were summarized by donor type using the Kaplan-Meier estimate. Relapse, treatment-related mortality (TRM), grade 2 to 4 and 3 to 4 acute GVHD, and chronic GVHD were described by donor type using cumulative incidence, with relapse as a competing risk for TRM and death as a competing risk for the others. Hazard rates and cause-specific hazard rates (for competing events) were compared between donor types using the Cox proportional hazards model, adjusted for the following prespecified patient characteristics: recipient age at HCT, race/ethnicity, Karnofsky Performance Status at HCT, cytomegalovirus serostatus, disease risk index at HCT, comorbidity index at HCT, and conditioning regimen intensity. DFS, relapse, and TRM are not reported for patients with nonmalignant disease. There was no adjustment for donor age, graft type (bone marrow vs peripheral blood), and GVHD prophylaxis regimen because of the confounding effect of donor type. We also examined interactions between each covariate and donor type in the Cox models and assessed the effect of center on transplant outcomes using a score test. Adjusted survival and DFS after transplant were summarized for each donor type using a stratified Cox model after adjusting for the same variables, whereas adjusted cumulative incidence was described based on a stratified Fine-Gray model. A subgroup analysis comparing outcomes by donor in patients who received PTCy-based GVHD prophylaxis was also performed. All analyses were performed using statistical software SAS version 9.4 or R version 3.6.3.

Results

Patient characteristics

BMT CTN 1702 enrolled 1751 evaluable patients at 47 centers between June 2019 and May 2022. Results analyzed by search prognosis score categories have been previously reported.16 Of the evaluable patients, 1179 (67.4%) proceeded to HCT at 45 centers with a median of 3.7 months (range, 0.7-42.6) from enrollment: MUD (n = 771), HAPLO (n = 254), MMUD (n = 112), and UCB (n = 41). One patient who received a transplant had no follow-up forms submitted and was included in the demographic tables but not in any of the outcome analyses. Baseline characteristics of patients who received a transplant are shown in Table 1. UCB recipients were younger and more likely to receive myeloablative conditioning (MAC) than patients with other donor types. MUD recipients were more likely to be non-Hispanic White than patients who received transplants from alternative donor sources. Peripheral blood stem cells were more frequently used for MUD/MMUD transplants than for HAPLO. The use of PTCy as GVHD prophylaxis was significantly correlated with donor type: 23.2% for MUD, 83.9% for HAPLO, 65.2% for MMUD, and 0% for UCB. No patient who received a UCB transplant received an ex vivo expanded product. Median time from diagnosis to transplant was 6.2 months and did not differ significantly by donor type (P = .65). Median time from enrollment to HCT was 3.7 months for all patients and ranged from 3.4 to 3.9 months across the 4 donor groups. OS

The unadjusted Kaplan-Meier probability of 2-year survival from transplant was not significantly different between MUD (64.5%; 95% confidence interval [CI], 61-68) and the other donor types (HAPLO: 64.7%; 95% CI, 58.6-70.6; MMUD: 60.7%; 95% CI, 50.7-70.2; and UCB: 50.8%; 95% CI, 35.6-65.9; overall P = .337; Table 2). On multivariable analysis, when compared to MUD, survival was not significantly different for HAPLO or MMUD (hazard ratio [HR], 1.08 and 1.18, respectively; P = not significant) but was significantly inferior for UCB (HR, 2.68; 95% CI, 1.67-4.19; P < .001; Table 3). In an inverse propensity score-weighted analysis, results were consistent with the multivariable analysis: HR for UCB vs MUD was 2.95 (95% CI, 1.27-6.84; P = .012), whereas HR for HAPLO and MMUD were not significantly different from that of MUD. Adjusted probabilities of survival from transplant date by donor type are shown in Figure 1A). In a subgroup analysis restricted to patients aged ≤25 years who received MAC (MUD, 48; HAPLO, 14; MMUD, 14; UCB, 12), results were consistent with the overall group in terms of direction and magnitude but did not reach statistical significance for the MUD vs UCB comparison because of a smaller sample size (HAPLO vs MUD: HR, 1.1; P = .89; MMUD vs MUD: HR, 1.1; P = .88; and UCB vs MUD: HR, 2.47; P = .10). Other significant predictors for inferior survival in the Cox model were lower Karnofsky Performance Status, higher disease risk index, and higher HCT comorbidity index. Race or ethnicity and conditioning regimen intensity were not significantly associated with OS (Table 3).

Relapse, TRM, and DFS

These end points were assessed in patients who received a transplant for malignant diseases only. In univariate analysis, 2-year cumulative incidences of relapse were not different between the donor types (Table 2). The 2-year cumulative incidences of TRM were 15.4% (95% CI, 12.8-18.1), 18.6% (95% CI, 13.9-23.7), 16.9% (95% CI, 10.3-24.7), and 31.8% (95% CI, 18.4-47) after MUD, HAPLO, MMUD, and UCB HCTs, respectively (P = .132). The corresponding 2-year probabilities of DFS were 57.2% (95% CI, 53.5-60.80), 56% (95% CI, 49.6-62.2), 54% (95% CI, 44.1-63.6), and 42.2% (95% CI, 27-58.1), respectively (P = .32).

In multivariable analyses (Table 4), risk of relapse did not differ significantly with any donor type when compared to MUD (HRs, 1.17, 1.29, and 1.30 for HAPLO, MMUD, and UCB, respectively; P = .44). TRM and DFS were significantly inferior for UCB when compared to MUD (TRM: HR, 3.31; 95% CI, 1.78-6.13; P < .001; DFS: HR, 1.99; 95% CI, 1.27-3.10; P = .002). There were no significant differences between MUD, HAPLO, or MMUD with respect to TRM and DFS in the Cox model. Adjusted cumulative incidences of relapse, TRM, and DFS by donor type are shown in Figure 1B-D.

GVHD

In univariate analyses, the cumulative incidence of acute GVHD grades 2 to 4 and grades 3 to 4 did not differ significantly by donor type (Table 2). The 2-year cumulative incidences of any chronic GVHD were 39.5% (95% CI, 35.9-43.2), 29.6% (95% CI, 23.8-35.6), 34.6% (95% CI, 25.9-44), and 22.4% (95% CI, 10.8-36) for MUD, HAPLO, MMUD, and UCB, respectively.

In the multivariate Cox model (Table 3), rates of acute GVHD were significantly higher with UCB vs MUD (grades 2-4: HR, 2.17; P = .001; grades 3-4: HR, 2.92; P = .004) but were not significantly different with HAPLO or MMUD versus MUD. Rates of chronic GVHD were significantly lower with HAPLO than with MUD (HR, 0.71; P = .018) but were not significantly different with MMUD or UCB vs MUD. The adjusted cumulative incidences of acute GVHD grades 2 to 4 and grades 3 to 4 and any chronic GVHD are shown in Figure 1E-G.

Analysis of patients who received PTCy

An exploratory analysis was conducted in the subset of patients who received PTCy-based GVHD prophylaxis (MUD: n = 179; HAPLO: n = 213; MMUD: n = 72). Univariate outcomes are shown in supplemental Table 1. On multivariable analyses, there was no significant difference for HAPLO or MMUD recipients compared with MUD recipients with respect to survival (HAPLO: HR, 1.3; P = .159; MMUD: HR, 1.46; P = .131), relapse (HAPLO: HR, 1.16; P = .49; MMUD: HR, 0.92; P = .783), TRM (HAPLO: HR, 1.43; P = .20; MMUD: HR, 1.59; P = .19), or DFS (HAPLO: HR, 1.24; P = .19; MMUD: HR, 1.15; P = .54). Although grades 2 to 4 acute GVHD were not different among patients who received HAPLO, MMUD, or MUD transplantation (HAPLO vs MUD: HR, 1.18; P = .374; MMUD vs MUD: HR, 1.18; P = .51), grades 3 to 4 acute GVHD were higher in MMUD transplants than in MUD transplants (MMUD: HR, 2.39; P = .017; MUD: HR, 2.52; P = .038). Chronic GVHD (any grade) was not significantly different among the donor groups.

Discussion

We report prospectively evaluated HCT outcomes for MUD vs HLA-mismatched donors for patients enrolled in BMT CTN 1702, a large multicenter biologic assignment clinical trial for patients who lacked an MRD. We show in multivariable analyses adjusted for prespecified clinical covariates that OS was not significantly different between MUD and HAPLO and MMUD HCTs but was significantly inferior with UCB, although this was the smallest group, with 41 patients. Among patients who received a transplant for a malignant disease, relapse incidence did not differ by donor type. However, TRM and DFS were significantly inferior with UCB vs MUD but not significantly different with HAPLO and MMUD vs MUD. The risks of developing acute GVHD grades 2 to 4 and grades 3 to 4 were significantly higher with UCB than with MUD but were similar with HAPLO and MMUD vs MUD. The risk of developing chronic GVHD was significantly lower in HAPLO than in MUD but comparable for the other donor types vs MUD. It should be noted that almost all HAPLO HCTs (and 65% of MMUD) used PTCy, which is known to reduce the incidence of chronic GVHD.17 These results provide evidence from a large prospective clinical trial that HAPLO and MMUD transplant success is similar to that of MUD with the conditioning and GVHD prophylaxis used. Our findings are consistent with results from previous retrospective comparisons showing comparable outcomes of HAPLO/MMUD to MUD when PTCy-based GVHD prophylaxis is used for HCT using T-cell–replete grafts from HLA-mismatched donors and when conventional GVHD prophylaxis is used for MUD.5,8, 9, 10,12 Furthermore, they support the approach of rapidly proceeding to transplant using a HAPLO/MMUD if the donor search prognosis predicts finding an MUD as very unlikely.

Our study showed significantly inferior outcomes for UCB when compared to MUD with respect to survival, TRM, DFS, and acute GVHD, suggesting that a HAPLO or MMUD should be preferred over UCB. Prior retrospective and meta-analyses comparing UCB to adult donor sources provide conflicting results. Some suggest noninferior outcomes11,13,18,19; however, others suggest poorer outcomes with UCB.20, 21, 22, 23 A relatively large prospective, randomized comparison (BMT CTN 1101) of 2-unit UCB vs HAPLO using nonablative conditioning demonstrated superior OS with HAPLO transplants.24 The superiority of HAPLO over UCB was also observed in a large retrospective comparison using similar eligibility criteria.25 Nevertheless, caution is appropriate in interpreting the poorer outcomes of UCB seen in our study, given the relatively small numbers of UCB included and the differences in baseline characteristics between UCB and other donor types. To address this, we performed both a multivariable analysis and an inverse propensity score weighted comparison that showed consistent results. No center effect on survival could be demonstrated on formal testing (P = .88; data not shown). Furthermore, center experience did not have a major effect on UCB outcomes in BMT CTN 1101.26 Our study accrued predominantly adult patients, and the inferiority of UCB to MUD, specifically in pediatric patients, cannot be inferred from this analysis. In a subgroup analysis of patients aged ≤25 years with MAC, the differences between MUD and UCB were consistent in direction and magnitude with the overall analysis; however, the difference was not statistically significant given the smaller sample.

Strengths of this study include its prospective multicenter design and the large number of patients enrolled. The patients were enrolled some time before HCT, when evidence for the lack of an MRD became available. Furthermore, use of the prescribed donor search algorithm to enable timely HCT using either MUD or alternative donors based on search prognosis score resulted in clinically similar times from diagnosis to HCT (range, 5.0-6.2 months) and enrollment to HCT (range, 3.4-3.9 months) for the different donor types (Table 1). Although the trial was not randomized in a conventional sense, donors were assigned predominantly by search prognosis score, which was biologically determined; therefore, the trial was not simply observational. Another limitation of this study was the freedom that participating centers had to use preparative regimens, GVHD prophylaxis regimens, and graft source (marrow vs blood-derived cells) of their choice. This likely resulted in a closer reflection of clinical practice and also in baseline differences between the donor groups. A multivariate Cox model was used to adjust for differences in such covariates. Accrual of pediatric patients and patients with nonmalignant disease was limited; therefore, the results of this study are most applicable to an adult population undergoing HCT for malignant disease. A particular limitation of our study is the disparate use of PTCy-based GVHD prophylaxis between MUD (23.2%), HAPLO (83.9%), and MMUD (65.2%). PTCy was considered a standard approach to GVHD prophylaxis for T-cell–replete HCT from adult HLA-mismatched donors but not for MUD by most centers during the period of enrollment on BMT CTN 1702 (2019-2022) before the publication of BMT CTN 1703, showing benefits to PTCy in HLA-matched donor transplants.17 Furthermore, the extent of the disparity in application of PTCy and use of bone marrow made it impractical to adequately adjust for this variable in the Cox model. The overall results reported in this study largely represent a comparison of HAPLO and MMUD performed using PTCy-based regimens for GVHD prophylaxis vs MUD performed using calcineurin inhibitor plus methotrexate (CNI/MTX). The overall results may have limited applicability when comparing MUD vs HAPLO and MMUD when all transplants are performed using PTCy-based GVHD prophylaxis. To address this issue, we performed a comparison of donor-specific outcomes limited to patients who received PTCy-based prophylaxis. Although we acknowledge the limited power of this smaller subgroup analysis, we found that, compared to MUD, HAPLO and mismatched related donors were associated with a higher risk of grades 3 to 4 acute GVHD, whereas other outcomes were not significantly different. Recently published comparisons of PTCy vs CNI/MTX-based GVHD prophylaxis for MUD donors suggest better outcomes for PTCy12,17 and have led to an increase in use of PTCy-based regimens for MUD, particularly in conjunction with reduced-intensity conditioning regimens. Furthermore, some retrospective reports suggest that outcomes for MUD may be superior to those using HLA-mismatched donors when PTCy is used for all patients.27, 28, 29 However, our study represents the first comparison of these donors using PTCy in a prospective trial.

Consistent with previously reported retrospective comparisons of MUD performed with CNI/MTX vs HAPLO performed using PTCy-based regimens,8,9 the risk of chronic GVHD in our study was significantly lower in HAPLO than in MUD in the overall trial. However, this difference is abrogated when HAPLO is compared to MUD in patients using PTCy-based regimens. This finding is consistent with results from BMT CTN 1703, suggesting that chronic GVHD rates are substantially lower after MUD transplants using PTCy than CNI/MTX.17

In summary, the results of this large prospective comparison of donor-specific outcomes for predominantly adult patients undergoing HCT suggest that comparable outcomes can be achieved using HAPLO and MMUD to those achieved using MUD when proceeding to an alternative donor transplant promptly if the likelihood of finding an MUD is low.
