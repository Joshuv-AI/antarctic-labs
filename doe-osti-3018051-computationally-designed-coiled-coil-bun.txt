Title: Computationally designed coiled coil ‘bundlemers’ as model colloidal nanoparticles for
solution assembly and materials design

Executive Summary:
As a collaborative team at the University of Delaware and the University of Pennsylvania,
Kloxin, Pochan and Saven designed new biomimetic nanomaterials de novo, leveraging a
variety of complementary areas of expertise: computational design of biopolymers (Saven at the
University of Pennsylvania), and synthesis and characterization (Kloxin and Pochan at the
University of Delaware). Overall activities included: sequence-specific peptide synthesis;
covalent crosslinking; noncovalent assembly; site-specific functionalization; and nanostructural
characterization using electron microscopy and solution-phase (x-ray and neutron) scattering.
Using natural and non-natural amino acids, the team created modular, functional peptide
building blocks for elaboration of new nanostructured materials. Ultimately, the development of
robust peptide-based, building blocks provides tools for researchers to readily produce complex
nanomaterial structures in a wide range of applications. The project had three, interconnecting
goals in an effort to provide the broader scientific community with a new peptide-based
paradigm for materials design and characterization. First, we further developed the coiled-coil
bundle-based toolbox (otherwise known as the ‘bundlemer’ toolbox) via computational design
with experimental bundle assembly verification. Second, we developed new uses of covalent
interactions, in addition to desired physical (noncovalent) interactions, to assemble bundlemers
into 1-D polymer chains with targeted chain rigidity, length, and dispersity. Thirds, we used the
above designs to experimentally realize (physical or covalent) polymers to target the creation of
liquid crystals or to realize interparticle assembly into nanoporous lattices. The close integration
of the three groups was instrumental in success of the biomolecular materials design, formation,
and understanding for future designs.

Project Activities:
Underlying the success of the complete research effort was the design of new bundlemer
building blocks with desired net charge, charge display, and non-natural chemical handles for
physical and covalent assembly. The importance of specific charge display between different
bundlemer sequences is one of the primary take aways of the research with many directly
observable differences in solution assembly of bundlemer particles with the same overall charge
but different amino acid sequences of the constituent peptides.

Significant progress was made during the award in the formation of bundlemer 1-D polymer
chains via combination of physical and covalent interactions in both the formation of rods with a
defined, monodisperse length. In order to form monodisperse chains, we took advantage of
computationally designed parallel coiled coil designs for bundlemer formation. These are distinct
from our previously studied D2 symmetric (antiparallel) tetrahelical bundles. Specifically, with a
parallel design, one can have desired covalent ‘click’ chemical reactive groups all on one end of
the assembled bundlemer particle. Therefore, unlike antiparallel coiled coil forming sequences
that have both N and C termini of the constituent peptides on both ends of the formed coiled

coil, the parallel bundlemer designs provide for anisotropic display of the functional groups.
When two bundlemers are reacted together via a thiol-Michael click reaction, one bundlemer
with cysteines as the n-terminal amino acid and the other bundlemer with a maleimide
functionality placed at the n-terminus of the constitutent peptides, they form dimer bundlemer
rods. Small-angle x-ray scattering in aqueous solution clearly shows that there are ~10 nm long
rigid cylinders in solution, which is precisely the nanostructure that would form from the click
conjugation between the parallel bundlemer building blocks. The short rods (~10 nm in length)
resulted in nematic liquid crystal formation. Additionally, the solution behavior of bundlemer rod
chains made with covalent interactions were thoroughly characterized in the context of rigid
polyelectrolytes via small-angle neutron or X-ray scattering in addition to microscopy. These 1-d
rods provide an additional example of a model colloidal nanoparticle.

The solution assembly of properly designed bundlemer building blocks via physical interactions
was the other area of significant success during the award. Importantly, the results highlight the
significance of the precise display of charged and other functional groups from the periphery of
the respective bundlemer building blocks, something we can directly control through
computational design. Results show clearly how the bundlemers can be considered model
colloidal nanoparticles even as individual particles and when not first linked into a polymer
chain. The first example is bundle film formation where physical interactions first dominate in
the formation of a film with subsequent covalent interactions introduced to stabilize the films.
Alloc side chains (typically used as protecting groups for charged side chains during the solid
phase synthesis of bundle-forming peptides) that contain a carbon-carbon double bond are
being used on the periphery of bundlemer building blocks to serve as one functional group to
react with thiols in what is known as a thiol-ene click reaction. Cysteine thiols were included on
the same bundle as allocs as well as on separate bundles that can be mixed with alloc-
fucntionalized bundles to produce covalently crosslinked films made exclusively from peptide
bundles. The use of the thiol-ene click reaction is an example of the three click conjugation
reactions used to link bundlemers together (Thiol-Michael, CuAAc azide/alkyne, and Thiolene).
An important experimental discovery during this work was the formation of exact, crystalline-like,
interbundle, nanoporous lattices when casting films of bundles functionalized with allocs. The
hydrophobic alloc side chains provided for interbundle interactions that formed a very specific,
FCC lattice symmetry on assembly together in solution. Additional studies were explored with
other natural and non-natural hydrophobic side chains displayed from the periphery of
bundlemer building blocks. Different positions of hydrophobic groups produced nanoporous
lattices with different symmetry while different bundlemers with hydrophobic groups in the same
positions sometimes producing the same lattice while others produced different lattices. This is
key to the need for future, additional research to better understand the role of individual
funcational groups on interparticle interactions and lattice formation.

Another example of solution assembly of nanoporous latticles through physical interactions
utilized bundlemer designs that form coiled coil bundlemers but that have desired charge states.
Specifically, mixtures between oppositely charged bundlemers were created in order to use
electrostatic interactions to form nanostructure. Comparisons were made between mixtures of
bundlemers with mixed charge within the individual bundlemers (what we call “mixed charge”
bundlemers with a desired net charge) vs. mixtures using new bundlemer designs that only
have one type of charged residue within their peptide primary sequence (what we call “single-
charge bundlemers”). An example of the critical observations are as follows: two different
mixtures can be compared, both having +24 bundlemers mixed with -24 bundlemers. However,
in one case the +24 bundlemer is a mixed-charge particle with both acidic and basic amino acid
side chains while the other +24 bundlemer is a single-charge particle with only basic side
chains. The -24/+24 mixed charge/single charge assembly produces a clear, specific lattice on

the nanoscale. In stark comparison, the mixture with +24/-24 mixed charge/mixed charge
sequences also assembled in solution due to electrostatic interactions but with no specific lattice
structure and only amorphous aggregates formed. This concept was ubiquitous in our studies-
mixtures of oppositely charged bundlemers that have single charge character all formed
crystalline-like nanoporous lattices with the structure of the lattice controlled by the amount of
charges on each particle, the positions of the charges on the inherent bundlemers, and the type
of charged amino acid. The lattices have potential in the formation of functional membranes for
ion separations, biomolecular and/or chiral molecule separations, or catalytic membranes.

The final example of control in physical assembly of designed bundlemer particles is the robust
formation of liquid crystal phases when observing single charged bundlemers in solution.
Positively charged single charged bundlemers were designed and studies in different
concentrations in solution, with or without added salt. In all single charge particle systems, we
observed robust end-to-end particle physical stacking that led to liquid crystal formation
(nematic phases at lower concentration, hexagonal columnar and smectic phases at higher
concentrations). The interparticle stacking was studied with peptide primary structure changes
as well as molecular dynamics simulations, all providing clear evidence for hydrophobic
interactions, polar interactions, and hydrogen bonding all contributing to the end-to-end stacking
stability. The impact of charge, charge screening via the addition of exogenous salt, and
peptide concentration was observed. Overall, the work is just the beginning of the design of new
liquid crystal systems using single charged bundlemers. A final study performed included
adding oppositely charged amino acids back into the single charged particles in a defined
fashion to see when mixed charge character is introduced. The liquid crystalline bundlemers
have potential in the formation of advanced polymer or biomaterials as well as biocompatible
sensing materials.

Throughout the entire project, University of Delaware and University of Pennsylvania students
were trained in a collaborative, interdisciplinary environment with experimental and
computational students learning to work together and plan successful research.
