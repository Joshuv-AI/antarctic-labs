Alternaria alternata as a causal agent of rose black spot: resistance screening and structural defense mechanisms in Rosagermplasm
Authors: Rui Zhou, Hailan Wang, Hmmam Zarif, Cheng Zhong, Chunguo Fan, Guozhen Yuan, Yufei Chang, Hongchi Liu, Jun Lu, Jinyi Liu, Changquan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBlack spot is one of the most destructive foliar diseases of roses worldwide, causing severe defoliation and significant economic losses, particularly under humid conditions. Although Diplocarpon rosae is the primary causal agent globally, members of the Alternaria alternata species complex have emerged as important pathogens in Chinese rose production regions. This study aimed to evaluate the pathogenicity of these isolates, assess resistance among diverse rose germplasm, and examine the potential roles of cuticular wax and lignin in disease resistance.ResultsKoch’s postulates confirmed the isolates as causal agents of black spot-like symptoms. The pathogen was recovered from three rose cultivars, identified by morphology and ITS sequencing, and pathogenicity was verified via inoculation assays. Field evaluation of 48 germplasm accessions revealed clear variation in disease response: 8 resistant, 19 moderately susceptible, and 21 highly susceptible genotypes. Detached-leaf assays supported these findings, with R. laevigata var. laevigata and R. roxburghii f. normalis developing only minimal lesions. Resistant accessions generally exhibited higher cuticular wax and lignin contents than susceptible genotypes. Cuticular wax ranged from 13.00 to 37.90 mg g⁻¹ FW, and lignin from 6.98% to 10.95% dry weight across accessions.ConclusionsAlternaria isolates closely related to A. alternata were associated with black spot-like symptoms in rose germplasm. Wild species such as R. laevigata var. laevigata and R. roxburghii f. normalis may serve as valuable resources for breeding disease-resistant cultivars. However, ITS-based identification requires confirmation by multilocus phylogenetic analysis, and the roles of cuticular wax and lignin in resistance remain to be validated through further functional and multi-year field studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09440-7.Keywords: Rosa, Black spot, Alternaria alternata species complex, Diplocarpon rosae, Disease resistance, Cuticular wax, Lignin

Introduction

Roses (Rosa spp.), members of the Rosaceae family, are among the most economically and culturally significant ornamental plants worldwide. They are widely cultivated for their diverse flower colors, prolonged blooming periods, distinctive plant architecture, and rich floral fragrance [1, 2]. With over 30,000 cultivars and approximately 200 wild species, the genus Rosa exhibits remarkable adaptability to a wide range of climatic and cultivation conditions [3, 4]. As a cornerstone of the global floriculture industry, roses account for a significant share of the international cut-flower trade and serve as key raw materials for the perfume, pharmaceutical, cosmetic, and food-processing industries [5, 6]. However, despite their high economic and horticultural importance, sustainable rose production is severely constrained by foliar diseases, which greatly reduce plant health, ornamental quality, and commercial market value [7, 8]. Chief among these constraints is black spot disease, the most destructive and geographically widespread foliar disease affecting roses worldwide. While traditionally attributed to the host-specialized fungus Diplocarpon rosae (asexual stage Marssonina rosae), recent studies indicate a shifting etiological landscape. In major rose-producing regions—particularly in China—black spot-like symptoms are frequently associated with members of the A. alternata species complex, either as the sole associated pathogen or in mixed infections with Diplocarpon rosae [8–10]. This shift is particularly concerning because members of the A. alternata species complex are necrotrophic pathogens with a broad host range and strong environmental plasticity. Unlike Diplocarpon rosae, their pathogenic role, epidemiological characteristics, and specific impact on modern rose cultivation systems remain poorly characterized [11, 12].

The identification of Alternaria sp. closely related to A. alternata as a pathogen associated with rose black spot raises critical questions regarding its pathogenic stability across diverse rose germplasm and key host traits determining resistance and susceptibility. Current disease management strategies rely predominantly on frequent fungicide applications and the cultivation of resistant varieties. However, the development of fungicide resistance, increasing environmental concerns, and stricter pesticide regulatory policies [13–15] increasingly limit long-term chemical control. Thus, the creation and use of genetically resistant cultivars is the best long-term and cost-effective way to control black spot disease. Nevertheless, elite resistant germplasm and the key defense-related traits conferring resistance to Alternaria sp. remain largely uncharacterized within existing Rosa collections.

Structural defense barriers constitute the first line of plant protection against foliar pathogens. The leaf cuticle and cell wall act as fundamental physical barriers, reinforced by lignin deposition, cuticular wax accumulation, and callose formation to impede pathogen penetration, restrict hyphal expansion, and limit lesion development [16–19]. Given that such structural defenses are particularly crucial against necrotrophic pathogens like Alternaria sp., leveraging genetic sources that naturally enhance these traits is a promising strategy.

Wild Rosa species represent a valuable but underutilized reservoir of such resistance; previous studies, particularly those at Texas A&M University, have identified strong black spot resistance in diploid species including R. rugosa, R. roxburghii, R. laevigata, R. banksiae, and R. wichurana [20, 21], which have been successfully used in hybridization programs to generate resistant progeny [21]. However, these breeding efforts have focused almost exclusively on Diplocarpon rosae. Consequently, resistance to Alternaria sp. in both wild and cultivated germplasm—along with the specific structural basis of this resistance—remains undefined.

China, one of the world’s leading rose producers, exemplifies the urgency of bridging these gaps. Here, roses rank among the top ten traditional flowers, and Yunnan Province alone produces over 17.8 billion stems annually across 23,400 ha [22, 23]. Black spot poses a particularly severe threat in this region, where disease incidence frequently exceeds 90% under humid field conditions, resulting in yield losses of 15–40%, quality degradation, and significant postharvest decline [8]. These substantial economic impacts underscore the immediate need for systematic germplasm screening and resistance-focused breeding. To address this need, the present study employed three integrated approaches: (i) confirming the etiology of black spot through pathogen isolation, morphological and molecular characterization, and Koch’s postulates; (ii) evaluating resistance across 48 diverse Rosa accessions using complementary field assessments and detached-leaf inoculation assays; and (iii) elucidating the structural defense mechanisms underlying resistance. The resulting findings pinpoint elite resistant germplasm and provide a foundation for accelerating the development of durable, structurally reinforced black spot resistance in rose cultivars.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and sample collection

Forty-eight Rosa accessions were collected from the Baima Teaching and Research Base of Nanjing Agricultural University (Supplementary Table S1) and maintained under standard field management. Stem cuttings from each accession were rooted and cultivated in a greenhouse under controlled conditions of 24/16 ± 2 °C (day/night) and 70 ± 5% relative humidity. Fully expanded, healthy, disease-free leaves were collected from vigorously growing plants for subsequent detached leaf inoculation assays to assess resistance to black spot-like disease.

Symptomatic leaves exhibiting typical black spot lesions were collected separately from field-grown plants of R. chinensis ‘Old Blush,’ R. hybrida ‘Claude Monet’ and R. multiflora var. inermis at the same location. All samples were placed in sterile plastic bags and transported to the laboratory, where pathogen isolation was performed within 4 hours of collection.

Pathogen isolation and purification

Symptomatic leaves were surface-sterilized sequentially in 75% ethanol for 30 s and 4% sodium hypochlorite (NaClO) for 2 min, then rinsed three times with sterile distilled water (ddH₂O). Under aseptic conditions, 5 × 5 mm tissue segments were excised at the interface of healthy and necrotic tissue, blotted dry using sterile filter paper, and placed onto potato dextrose agar (PDA). Plates were sealed with Parafilm® and incubated at 25 °C in darkness.

Emerging fungal colonies were subcultured onto fresh PDA and incubated at 25 °C for 10 days. Pure cultures were obtained through three consecutive transfers of hyphal tips or conidia under a stereomicroscope.

Morphological and molecular identification of the pathogen

Morphological characterization

Three purified isolates (GL, XD and QW) were cultured on 90 mm PDA plates for macroscopic observation. After incubation at 25 °C in darkness for 10 days, colony traits were observed and photographed. We recorded colony diameter, surface texture, and zonation, as well as the colour of the obverse and reverse sides.

To prepare conidial suspensions, 10-day-old cultures were flooded with sterile distilled water containing 0.01% Tween 80. The colony surface was gently scraped to release conidia, and the suspension was filtered through four layers of sterile cheesecloth to remove mycelial fragments. The final concentration was adjusted to 1 × 10⁶ conidia mL⁻¹.

For microscopic examination, conidial suspension aliquots were stained with lactophenol cotton blue on glass slides and observed under an Olympus BX53 microscope at 400× magnification. Morphometric analysis of 50 conidia per isolate was performed using ImageJ v1.53. Measured parameters included conidial length, width, beak length, transverse and longitudinal septation, shape and pigmentation.

Molecular identification

Total genomic DNA was extracted from fungal mycelia of purified isolates after 10 days of cultivation on PDA medium. Genomic DNA extraction was performed using a modified cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method, according to the protocol described by [24]. For molecular species identification and taxonomic confirmation, the nuclear ribosomal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The standard universal fungal primer pair ITS1 (5′-TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGC-3′) and ITS4 (5′-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3′) were used for ITS amplification, as previously reported by [10, 25].

All PCR amplifications were carried out in a total reaction volume of 25 µL. The PCR reaction mixture consisted of 1× standard PCR buffer, 2.0 mM MgCl₂, 0.2 mM of each dNTP, 0.4 µM of each forward and reverse primer, and 1 U of recombinant Taq DNA polymerase, with template genomic DNA added appropriately to meet amplification requirements. The thermal cycling program was set as follows: initial pre-denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min; 35 consecutive cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, primer annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and DNA extension at 72 °C for 1 min; followed by a final terminal extension step at 72 °C for 10 min to complete fragment synthesis.

Following commercial sequencing of the amplified ITS fragments, the resulting nucleotide sequences were compared with the NCBI non-redundant database using BLASTn to determine taxonomic affiliation. Isolates that showed ≥ 99% sequence similarity and 100% query coverage with reference sequences of A. alternata were assigned to this taxon.

Pathogen inoculation

Ten-day-old cultures of the purified isolates were used to prepare a conidial suspension. Spores were dislodged from the colony surface using sterile potato dextrose water (PDW), filtered through three layers of gauze, and adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ conidia mL⁻¹ using a hemocytometer. For resistance screening, 32 detached leaves per accession were collected (16 for inoculation and 16 as controls). The leaves were surface sterilized as previously described, placed adaxially upward on moist filter paper in Petri dishes, and spot inoculated with 10 µL of the spore suspension per leaf. Control leaves received 10 µL of sterile PDW. The Petri dishes were initially incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 48 h to promote infection. After that, they were transferred to a growth chamber with a 12 h light/12 h dark photoperiod at 25 °C for an additional 4–5 days before disease symptoms were assessed.

Field evaluation

Field surveys of natural black spot incidence were conducted monthly from May to October 2021 at the Rose Resource Garden, Baima Teaching and Research Base, Nanjing Agricultural University. For each of the 48 Rosa accessions, ten plants were randomly selected and evaluated during each survey. Determination of lignin and cuticular wax content

A total of 12 accessions were selected for structural trait quantification, covering the complete disease resistance gradient from HR to HS. The selected materials included wild rose species, ancient cultivars and modern commercial varieties. This stratified sampling maximized phenotypic differences for correlation analysis while ensuring experimental practicality.

Fifteen biological replicates were set for each accession in both lignin and cuticular wax measurements. Each replicate was a single healthy, fully expanded leaf collected from an individual plant. All samples were harvested at the identical developmental stage and canopy position to reduce environmental interference. For lignin quantification, leaves were oven-dried at 25 °C to a constant weight, ground into fine powder and sieved through a 40-mesh screen. Lignin content was determined using a commercial assay kit (BC4205; Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) based on the acetyl bromide method. Briefly, 10 mg of dry powder was pre-extracted with 95% ethanol to eliminate pigments and soluble phenolics, followed by incubation with 25% (v/v) acetyl bromide in glacial acetic acid at 70 °C for 30 min. After adding sodium hydroxide and hydroxylamine hydrochloride, the absorbance was detected at 280 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (T-UV1810S; Shanghai Yoke Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Lignin content (% dry weight) was calculated against a standard curve generated with pure lignin standards.

For cuticular wax analysis, leaves from consistent canopy positions were rinsed, air-dried and weighed for fresh weight (FW). Epicuticular wax was extracted by submerging leaves in 50 mL chloroform for 4 min. After full solvent evaporation, the samples were reweighed. Wax content was calculated based on weight difference and expressed as mg g⁻¹ FW, following a previously described protocol [27].

Results

Isolation, purification, and identification of the pathogen

Three fungal isolates, designated GL, XD, and QW, were recovered from symptomatic leaves of R. chinensis ‘Old Blush’, R. hybrida ‘Claude Monet’, and R. multiflora var. inermis, respectively. The leaf samples were collected from field-grown plants at the Baima Teaching and Research Base. Following single-spore purification on PDA, all isolates formed circular colonies with initially white aerial mycelia that gradually turned yellow-brown as the cultures matured. The colonies were fluffy and cotton-like in texture. Isolate XD developed obvious concentric zonation on both the obverse and reverse surfaces of the colony, whereas GL and QW showed faint or no distinct ring structures.

Microscopic examination revealed light brown conidia in young cultures that darkened to deep brown at maturity. Conidia displayed diverse morphologies, including obclavate (inverted club-shaped), oval, nearly spherical, and obpyriform (inverted pear-shaped) forms, with 3–9 transverse septa and 0–6 longitudinal or oblique septa. Conidia ranged from 15.5 to 32.5 μm in length and 5.5–13.0 μm in width. Most conidia lacked a distinct beak, although some possessed a short cylindrical beak measuring 0.0–6.0 μm in length (Fig. 1B). These morphological features were consistent with the genus Alternaria.

To further characterize the three fungal isolates, the nuclear ribosomal ITS region was amplified and Sanger sequenced. The ITS sequences of isolates GL, XD and QW shared 100% nucleotide identity. BLASTn searches against the NCBI nr nucleotide database returned 100% sequence similarity to numerous reference accessions labeled A. alternata. Nevertheless, single-locus ITS data lacks sufficient discriminatory power to delimit species within the A. alternata species complex. Phylogenetic reconstruction grouped all three isolates within a robust, well-supported clade comprising published strains assigned to the A. alternata complex (Fig. 1C, D). Integrating morphological traits and ITS sequence data, we tentatively classify these isolates as members of the A. alternata species complex. Full, unambiguous species identification would require multilocus phylogenetic sequencing and analysis.

Pathogenicity assays were conducted using mycelial plugs harvested from 10-day-old pure cultures of each isolate. The plugs were inoculated onto detached leaves of R. ‘Old Blush’. Necrotic lesions emerged at 3 days post-inoculation (dpi). Lesions expanded rapidly across the leaf tissue, covering the majority of the leaf surface by 6–9 dpi. Full leaf necrosis, accompanied by visible white aerial mycelium, was observed at 12 dpi (Fig. 1E). Quantification of lesion diameters showed that isolate GL possessed the highest virulence, producing the most severe and widespread leaf symptoms.

Field evaluation of black spot resistance in Rosa accessions

Based on the disease severity index (DSI ± SE) and the resistance criteria in Table 1, Levene’s test (p = 0.0023) and Bartlett’s test (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table S2) indicated significant heterogeneity of variance among the 48 rose accessions. Therefore, a non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis’s test was performed (H = 180.63, df = 47, p = 1.44 × 10⁻¹⁷), followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test. Two accessions showed the highest resistance: R. laevigata Michx. var. laevigata (DSI ± SE = 1.71 ± 0.32) and R. roxburghii Tratt. f. normalis (DSI ± SE = 4.5 ± 0.54) (Table 3). These wild accessions exhibited the lowest disease severity under natural infection pressure. Overall, the 48 accessions were classified into four resistance groups: 2 h, 4 MR, 19 MS, and 23 HS. After Bonferroni correction, Dunn’s test identified significant differences (p < 0.05) in 38 of 1,128 pairwise comparisons (3.37%). These differences were predominantly observed between HR and HS accessions. For example, R. laevigata var. laevigata differed significantly from ten highly susceptible cultivars, including R. hybrida ‘Claude Monet’ and R. hybrida ‘Fragrance King Terrazza’.

DSI values and resistance grades of Rosa accessions under natural field infection by A. alternata

Subsequent one-way ANOVA revealed highly significant differences in DSI among the 48 accessions (p < 0.001), validating the prerequisite for Scott–Knott ESD analysis. Resistance clustering was therefore performed using the Scott–Knott ESD test. Combined with the standardized resistance grading criteria, the 48 accessions were ultimately divided into four resistance categories (Table 3). Among the tested rose germplasm, eight accessions showed strong disease resistance, including R. laevigata Michx. var. laevigata and R. roxburghii Tratt. f. normalis. Nineteen accessions were grouped into the moderately resistant tier, represented by R. lucieae Franch. var. lucieae and R. multiflora Thunb. var. multiflora. Sixteen accessions, such as R. chinensis ‘Si Mian Jing’ and R. hybrida ‘Fen Shan’, were classified as moderately susceptible. The most susceptible group contained five accessions, including R. hybrida ‘Gigi’ and R. hybrida ‘Joy Terrazza’. Furthermore, 95% confidence intervals were used to reassess the stability of the fixed-threshold resistance classification. The results showed that only R. laevigata var. laevigata consistently exhibited HR, whereas R. sterilis was stably classified as MR and R. chinensis ‘Mutabilis’ as MS. Eight accessions were consistently classified as HS, while the remaining accessions showed unstable resistance phenotypes across grading thresholds (Table 3). Specifically, R. roxburghii Tratt. f. normalis displayed an intermediate phenotype between HR and MR. Seven accessions, including R. laevigata ‘Red Flower’ and R. hybrida ‘Queen of Sweden’, showed intermediate resistance between MR and MS. Fifteen accessions, such as R. glomerata and R. fortuneana ‘Prickle Peduncle’, spanned three resistance grades (MR–MS–HS). The remaining 14 accessions, exemplified by R. chinensis ‘Xinghua Chunyu’ and R. chinensis ‘Si Mian Jing’, were graded as intermediate between MS and HS.

Detached leaf inoculation assay

To validate the field-based resistance rankings under controlled conditions, detached-leaf inoculation assays were performed using a conidial suspension of the confirmed A. alternata-like isolate at a concentration of 1 × 10⁵ conidia mL⁻¹. The DSI was calculated for each accession, and resistance phenotypes were classified into five grades consistent with field evaluations: I, HR, MR, MS, and HS (Fig. 2; Tables 2 and 4). Levene’s test (p < 0.05) and Bartlett’s test (p = 0.0054) confirmed heterogeneous variances in the DSI data from the detached-leaf inoculation assay. Therefore, the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis’s test was applied (H = 88.3656, df = 47, p = 0.000247), followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test. Three accessions were classified as MR: R. laevigata Michx. var. laevigata, R. roxburghii Tratt. f. normalis, and R. sterilis. Twenty-three accessions were rated as MS, including R. chinensis ‘Mutabilis’, R. chinensis ‘Jin’ou Fanlü’, and R. hybrida ‘Spirit of Freedom’. The remaining 22 accessions were HS, such as R. hybrida ‘Fen Shan’ and R. hybrida ‘Fragrance King Terrazza’. Notably, no HR accession was observed under controlled inoculation conditions, in contrast to the field evaluation.

The remaining accessions showed unstable resistance classifications across confidence intervals. Specifically, only R. laevigata var. laevigata exhibited an intermediate response between HR and MR. Two accessions, R. fortuneana ‘Prickle Peduncle’ and R. lucieae Franch. var. lucieae, varied across three resistance grades (MR–MS–HS). Notably, R. laevigata ‘Red Flower’ was the most unstable accession, exhibiting all four resistance grades (HR–MR–MS–HS). Resistant accessions were predominantly found among ancient R. chinensis types and R. multiflora lineages, whereas all tested modern R. hybrida cultivars were susceptible. These consistent patterns across both field surveys and controlled inoculation assays indicate that wild and ancient rose germplasm harbors more stable and effective resistance to black spot than modern hybrids. This difference likely reflects the historical breeding focus on ornamental traits at the expense of disease tolerance.

Discussion

Roses are economically important ornamental crops that are vulnerable to a range of fungal diseases. Black spot-like disease stands out as one of the most destructive, greatly impairing plant vigour and ornamental quality [14]. This disease prevails in field cultivation, with infection rates occasionally exceeding 90%; severe outbreaks can even cause plant mortality [30]. These challenges underscore the urgent need to breed modern rose cultivars with durable, structurally based disease resistance.

Pathogen isolation and identification are fundamental prerequisites for investigating disease resistance in roses. Multiple studies have confirmed that the pathogens responsible for rose black spot in both southern and northern China belong to A. alternata [8, 10]. In the present study, Alternaria isolates were recovered from three rose cultivars and characterized through morphological observations and ITS sequence analysis. To our knowledge, this is the first report confirming that black spot-like symptoms on rose germplasm at the Baima Teaching and Research Base, Nanjing Agricultural University, are associated with pathogens closely related to A. alternata. These findings are consistent with previous reports of Alternaria-associated black spot-like disease on roses in Henan and Shandong Provinces, suggesting that Alternaria spp. have emerged as dominant pathogens causing black spot-like disease in major rose-growing regions of China [8, 10]. In contrast, Diplocarpon rosae remains the primary causal agent of classical black spot disease in roses worldwide [14]. This discrepancy points to regional variation in pathogen populations and the potential for mixed infections involving Alternaria spp. and Marssonina rosae [31].

Collectively, our results provide a robust foundation for clarifying local pathogenic characteristics and offer valuable insights to guide future epidemiological studies and integrated disease management strategies in rose production.

Plant disease resistance is commonly evaluated through field surveys and in vitro inoculation assays. In a field survey by Bai et al. [32], 50 rose varieties from the Beijing area were classified into five resistance levels. In our experiment, we systematically evaluated the resistance of 48 rose accessions using both natural field disease surveys and in vitro leaf inoculation. Field-based assessments grouped accessions into four resistance levels, while in vitro inoculation classified them into three, with highly consistent resistance classification patterns observed between the two methods. These findings further verify that in vitro detached-leaf inoculation serves as a reliable and efficient technique for high-throughput screening of rose black spot disease resistance [21].

Resistance classification based on fixed DSI thresholds provides a feasible framework for large-scale germplasm screening but entails inherent statistical uncertainty that requires careful interpretation. In this study, we mitigated this limitation by calculating 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the DSI values of each accession. The results indicated that numerous accessions previously assigned to a single resistance category exhibited confidence intervals that spanned two or even three adjacent resistance grades. This phenomenon highlights a core limitation of threshold-based classification systems: such systems define discrete, absolute resistance boundaries, whereas disease severity data are inherently continuous and susceptible to temporal and environmental fluctuations.

Boundary accessions, including R. roxburghii Tratt. f. normalis (HR–MR intermediate) and R. multiflora Thunb. var. cathayensis (MR–MS intermediate), cannot be statistically and unequivocally categorized into a single resistance grade based on the current dataset. For accessions with broader confidence intervals covering grades from MR to HS, such as R. laevigata ‘Red Flower’ and R. lucieae Franch. var. lucieae, the classification uncertainty is further amplified. This variability may stem from intra-accession phenotypic differences, dynamic environmental responses across sampling periods, or potential genetic heterogeneity within the tested germplasm.

Accordingly, we propose that accessions with intermediate resistance phenotypes or cross-boundary 95% CI should be considered tentative classifications rather than definitive resistance assignments. Multi-year field investigations and multi-environment replicated trials are necessary to validate the phenotypic stability of these boundary accessions. Prior to such systematic validation, the resistance performance of these materials should be interpreted cautiously in breeding practices, and their resistance levels should not be overinterpreted without independent experimental corroboration. This rigorous interpretation is consistent with the correlative nature of our structural defense trait data and ensures that the germplasm screening results are applied scientifically and prudently in future rose resistance breeding.

The combined results demonstrated that ancient rose varieties (e.g., ‘Pocelina’) possess markedly stronger disease resistance than modern hybrid roses, which supports previous conclusions [20, 21] that wild and ancient rose germplasm carries abundant genetic resources for disease resistance. Ancient and wild rose accessions, such as R. laevigata Michx. var. laevigata and R. roxburghii Tratt. f. normalis, have evolved robust disease resistance via long-term natural selection [20]. Their genetically complex and highly heterozygous genomes retain a greater number of functional resistance (R) genes, enabling effective defense against fungal pathogens, including black spot and powdery mildew [14].

Under persistent pathogen pressure, these genotypes have evolved both constitutive and inducible defense mechanisms, conferring superior adaptability and ensuring sustainable survival and reproduction in pathogenic environments. In contrast, modern rose cultivars, which have undergone intensive artificial hybridization and directional selection, are primarily bred for superior ornamental attributes, including flower shape, color, and fragrance. Such breeding priorities have frequently led to the loss or functional attenuation of resistance genes. Genomic studies [1, 33] have demonstrated that artificial selection during modern rose domestication mainly targeted continuous flowering, floral organ development, and flower color formation, while numerous disease resistance loci were either eliminated or remained underutilized. Modern rose cultivars originate from merely eight to ten wild progenitor species, leading to a narrow genetic basis and a severe genetic bottleneck that restricts the introgression of novel resistance alleles [14]. Accordingly, most resistance-related loci in modern hybrids remain latent or recessive and cannot be fully fixed through continuous backcrossing, ultimately resulting in inadequate and unstable disease resistance in hybrid progenies [34].

These phenotypic differences are largely determined by intrinsic physiological and biochemical mechanisms. The plant cell wall is the primary physical barrier against pathogen invasion, and variation in its structure and composition is closely associated with disease resistance. [35] Genes involved in the biosynthesis of epicuticular wax and lignin play key roles in plant defense. Epicuticular wax not only reduces the surface water film required for fungal spore attachment and germination but may also contain bioactive compounds with antimicrobial activity. [36, 37] As a major cell wall polymer, lignin accumulates rapidly during pathogen infection, strengthening cell wall rigidity and forming a physical barrier that restricts pathogen penetration and hyphal proliferation. Together, leaf cuticular wax and lignin constitute an important first line of structural defense against fungal invasion in roses.

The observed resistance trend implies that these structural traits are closely associated with reduced black spot disease severity. Consistent with previous findings, the cuticular wax layer can hinder pathogen adhesion, while lignin deposition enhances cell wall rigidity, restricts fungal colonization, and suppresses the activity of pathogen-derived enzymes and toxins, thereby slowing lesion expansion [18, 38, 39]. Importantly, the current results only demonstrate a correlative relationship rather than definitive causality between these structural traits and disease resistance. Higher accumulation of wax and lignin in resistant accessions, including wild species R. laevigata Michx. var. laevigata and R. roxburghii Tratt. f. normalis, as well as the cultivars ‘Spirit of Freedom’ and ‘Creamy Eden’, cannot confirm that these traits directly confer black spot resistance. Potential confounding factors, such as genetic linkage and pleiotropic effects, cannot be ruled out. Therefore, although these accessions exhibit favorable structural defense characteristics, their utilization as donor materials for resistance gene mining and rose breeding remains preliminary. Further functional validation experiments are required to elucidate the precise regulatory roles of wax and lignin in rose black spot resistance.

Preliminary results from this study suggest that resistant rose accessions, including certain ancient roses and wild Rosa species, tend to possess higher levels of epicuticular wax and lignin in their leaves. These findings offer initial correlative insights into the structural basis of disease resistance but do not confirm a causal mechanism. Further investigations involving histochemical staining, targeted gene expression profiling, and genetic or chemical manipulation of wax and lignin biosynthesis pathways are essential to verify the direct defensive functions of these structural traits against A. alternata infection. Pending such systematic functional validation, all mechanistic inferences drawn from the present study should remain tentative.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrated that an Alternaria alternata-like pathogen is associated with black spot-like disease in rose germplasm at Nanjing Agricultural University and revealed substantial variation in resistance among 48 Rosa accessions. R. laevigata var. laevigata and R. roxburghii Tratt. f. normalis exhibited the highest and most stable resistance. This resistance was associated with increased cuticular wax and lignin deposition, suggesting a potential role of structural defenses in disease tolerance. However, these findings are correlative, and further histochemical characterization, molecular detection, and functional validation are required to elucidate the mechanistic roles of wax and lignin in pathogen defense.

Pathogen identification in this study relied on morphological characteristics and ITS sequence analysis; therefore, multi-locus phylogenetic approaches are necessary to achieve accurate species-level resolution. The resistant wild Rosa accessions identified here represent valuable genetic resources for interspecific hybridization and marker-assisted breeding, providing a foundation for the development of new rose cultivars with durable black spot resistance and improved ornamental traits.
