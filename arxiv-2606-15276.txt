Collapsibility in Multiparametric Models of Random Simplicial Complexes
Jon V. Kogan

Abstract
We study collapsibility in the multiparametric models of random simplicial complexes, namely the lower and upper models. In the upper model, we improve upon a result of Farber and Nowik, and assert that the homology is a.a.s concentrated in a single dimension by proving that the complex collapses to that dimension. In the lower model, we prove that the complex a.a.s collapses to the dimension with maximal non-trivial cohomology. We then compare this threshold to the ones derived previously for the special cases of the clique complex (by Kahle) and the Linial-Meshulam model.

Introduction

1.1
Background

Random simplicial complexes form a probabilistic framework for studying topology, extending the classical Erdős–Rényi random graph model to higher dimensions.
Foundational examples include the Linial–Meshulam complex
Y_{d}(n,p)
(with the 2-dimensional version first appearing in
[
11

, and in
[

]
for general

-skeleton, and the random clique complex

, obtained by taking the clique (also called flag) complex of
G(n,p)
(first appearing in
[
9

Since then, extensive work has been devoted to understanding how topological invariants such as connectivity, homology, and collapsibility depend on the underlying probability parameters; see, for example,
[

,

,

These classical models, as well as most others at the time, derive their randomness from faces of a single dimension. To capture interactions between simplexes of different dimensions, Costa and Farber introduced the multiparametric models of random simplicial complexes in
[
4
]
and
[

Consider a random hypergraph

We may then convert this distribution on hypergraphs to two distributions on simplicial complexes:

If, instead, we consider the distribution on

In this paper we wish to study the

A collapse is in particular a combinatorially tractable version of a homotopy equivalence, shrinking the size of the complex while preserving all homotopical invariants (such as homology).

Collapsibility has been extensively studied in the two classical models of random complexes mentioned above. In

The analogous result regarding the clique complex

[

]
.

In this paper, we wish to establish similar collapsibility results for the multiparametric models. Such a result already exists for the upper model, but is not tight.

[

1.2
Results

In the lower model, we prove:

Theorem 1.5
.

For
\underline{X}(n;p_{1},...)
where
p_{i}=n^{-\alpha_{i}},\alpha_{i}\geq 0
, let
d
be the smallest integer s.t
S_{2}^{d}(\left\{\alpha_{i}\right\}_{i})>d+2
. Then
\underline{X}
a.a.s collapses onto dimension
d-1
.

In other words, the lower model a.a.s collapses to the largest dimension which is known to have non-trivial cohomology (which
S_{2}^{d-1}(\left\{\alpha_{i}\right\}_{i})<d+1
would imply by
Lemma

3.6
and
Theorem

1.4
).
The case where
S_{2}^{d}(\left\{\alpha_{i}\right\}_{i})=d+2
is where the threshold lies for collapsibility of the Linial-Meshulam-Wallach model (see
Example

3.7
for more detail), so
Theorem

1.5
is the best possible for this level of asymptotic
“granularity”
and general parameters (see
Example

3.8
for an example where restricting the parameters to the ones of the clique complex yields a strictly better bound).

In order to prove a corresponding result in the upper model, we define in
Definition

4.4
the concept of
redundancy
. It is a measure of how vertices are shared among maximal faces, and in
Lemma

4.8
we show it is strongly related to the dimension to which a simplicial complex collapses.

Using this, formulated in the notation of
Definition

1.2
, we prove that simplexes of dimension greater than
l
cannot combine to form sufficiently complex structures, and as a result:

Theorem 1.6
.

Suppose
p_{i}=n^{-\alpha_{i}},\alpha_{i}\geq 0
, such that there exists
r\in\mathbb{N}
s.t
p_{j}=0,j>r
. Then
\overline{X}(n;p_{1},...)
a.a.s collapses onto dimension
l=\left\lfloor\max_{i}(i+1-\alpha_{i})\right\rfloor
.

In particular, all homology above dimension
l
is strictly
0
(over any coefficient group), improving the bounds and the collapsibility result from
Theorem

1.3
.

[
6
]
also prove that
\overline{X}(n;p_{1},...)
a.a.s has a complete (
l-1
)-skeleton, making it appear similar to the Linial-Meshulam-Wallach model after the collapse.
One should note, however, that collapsing procedures involve choices, as well as the fact that different
l
-faces in
\overline{X}
are independent only in the case
l=r
, but otherwise are positively correlated if they share at least

vertices (as the existence of one makes the existence of all faces containing it more likely).
From

1.6
we conclude in

that, provided

is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of spheres.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
D(n;\vec{p}(n))
be a family of distributions depending on a natural number
n
and perhaps other parameters.
D(n;\vec{p}(n))
satisfies a property

(asymptotically almost surely) if
\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}P(q)=1

Example 2.2
.

Let
G(n,p)
be a distribution on graphs with
n
vertices, where each edge appears independently with probability

.
A theorem by Erdős and Rényi states that
\frac{\ln(n)}{n}
is the threshold function for connectivity, meaning that for

the graph is a.a.s disconnected, and for

Definition 2.3
.

A simplicial complex is called

Definition 2.4

For a simplicial complex define a relation

-dimensional face.
This is obviously reflexive and symmetric. Complete this relation under transitivity and call it

-dimensional faces under this relation.

A pure-

-dimensional faces are in the same component. A simplicial complex where all maximal simplexes are of dimension greater or equal to

The importance of strong connectivity components is seen in the following:

(Lemma 2.7 in
[

]
)

Let

It turns out strongly connected complexes can be constructed one face at a time:

Definition 2.6
.

For a simplicial complex

Any tree has a filtration

Let

Lemma 2.13
.

A simplicial complex

collapses onto dimension

if and only if all strong connectivity components of

w.r.t dimension
d+1
collapse onto dimension

.

Proof.

A particular elementary collapse

is possible if and only if
\sigma
is free. This is determined only by faces containing
\sigma
, which are all above the dimension of
\sigma
.
If

From here both directions are clear: any sequence of elementary collapses collapsing

gives rise to a sequence collapsing any strong connectivity component (merely the subsequence of collapses happening in that component), and sequences of collapses for all components may be strung together to produce a collapse of
C
onto dimension
d
.

3
Lower Model

Proposition 3.1
.

The terms
budget
and
cost
are well defined for
\underline{X}(n;p_{1},...)
where
p_{i}=n^{-\alpha_{i}}
.
More concretely, assume that a simplicial complex
A
has
a_{0}
vertices,
a_{1}
edges and so on. Then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\log_{n}(E(A\subset\underline{X}))=a_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{\dim(A)}a_{i}\alpha_{i}
.

Proof.

For a particular choice of an injective map
f
from the vertices of
A
to
a_{0}
vertices of
\underline{X}
, the probability that this map extends to a map of complexes may be written as follows:
Define
B_{k}
to be the event that
f
sends all
k
-faces in
A
to
k
-faces in
\underline{X}
. Then we are interested in
P\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{\dim(A)}B_{i}\right)
.

P\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{\dim(A)}B_{i}\right)=P(B_{1})P(B_{2}\mid B_{1})P(B_{3}\mid B_{2}\cap B_{1})...=\prod_{i=1}^{\dim(A)}P\left(B_{i}\middle\lvert\bigcap_{j=1}^{i-1}B_{j}\right)

by definition of conditional probability. But in the lower model
P(B_{i}\mid\bigcap_{j=1}^{i-1}B_{j})=p_{i}^{a_{i}}=n^{-a_{i}\alpha_{i}}
. There are
\binom{n}{a_{0}}
subsets of
[n]
of size
a_{0}
, and for each such subset there are
a_{0}!
possible maps
f
(if
A
has symmetries these maps might induce the same map on complexes, but this does not affect the asymptotics).
Therefore,

\binom{n}{a_{0}}n^{-\sum_{i=1}^{\dim(A)}a_{i}\alpha_{i}}\leq E(A\subset\underline{X})\leq a_{0}!\binom{n}{a_{0}}n^{-\sum_{i=1}^{\dim(A)}a_{i}\alpha_{i}}

and so
\log_{n}(E(A\subset\underline{X}))\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}a_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{\dim(A)}a_{i}\alpha_{i}
.

This generalizes the calculation from
[
8
]
for the simplex, giving
E(\Delta^{d}\subset\underline{X})=\binom{n}{d+1}n^{-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\binom{d+1}{i+1}\alpha_{i}}
and a budget
\log_{n}(E(\Delta^{d}\subset\underline{X}))\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}d+1-\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\binom{d+1}{i+1}\alpha_{i}
.

As for expansion operations, since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\log_{n}(E(A\subset\underline{X}))
depends only on the number of faces of each dimension, an expansion operation
A\mapsto A\cup_{Q}\Delta^{d}
increases the number of
i
-faces by
\binom{d+1}{i+1}-q_{i}
, where
q_{i}
is the number of
i
-faces in
Q
. So the cost is well defined, and only depends on the type of expansion operation.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

It is at this point that we may give an interpretation to the expressions

This means that

In terms of

Meanwhile,

This is the smallest

Proof.

By the proof of

E(D)
is of the form
n^{f_{0}(D)-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f_{i}(D)\alpha_{i}}
(up to a constant factor), where
f_{i}(D)
denotes the number of
i
-dimensional faces of
D
.
If the operation added a vertex, this costs at least
S^{m-1}_{1}(\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i})-1
, and since
S^{m-1}_{1}(\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i})\geq S^{k}_{1}(\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i})>1
the result follows.
Otherwise, we know that
f_{0}(D^{\prime})=f_{0}(D)
,
f_{i}(D^{\prime})\geq f_{i}(D)
for all
i
and that
f_{l}(D^{\prime})>f_{l}(D)
, and so
n^{f_{0}(D^{\prime})-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f_{i}(D^{\prime})\alpha_{i}}=o(n^{f_{0}(D)-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f_{i}(D)\alpha_{i}})
as
\alpha_{l}>0
.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

For
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i}
where
S^{k}_{1}(\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i})>1
and
m>k
, only a finite number of isomorphism types of strongly connected components
A
have
E(A\subset\underline{X})\not\to 0
.

Proof.

By
Corollary

3.4
, all expansion operations decrease the asymptotics of
E(\bullet\subset\underline{X})
.
In particular, if we find a number
v_{0}
such that any
m
-dimensional component
A
with
|A|>v_{0}
has
E(A\subset\underline{X})\to 0
, the claim will follow.

Denote the budget by
b
, and the cost of a vertex adding operation by
c_{m}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\binom{m}{i}\alpha_{i}-1\geq S_{1}^{k}-1>0
. If
A
has
a
vertices,
E(A\subset\underline{X})\sim n^{b-(a-m-1)c_{m}}
. For
a
such that
c_{m}>\frac{b}{a-m-1}
, the exponent will be negative, so the expectation will go to 0.

Furthermore, the probability of any component to have more vertices than
\frac{b}{c_{m}}+m+1
is bounded by the sum of probabilities of the components with exactly
\left\lceil\frac{b}{c_{m}}+m+1\right\rceil
vertices (which is finite), and still tends to 0.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

S_{2}^{d}>d+2
entails
S_{1}^{d-1}>1
.

Proof.

For all
1\leq i\leq d
, the coefficients of
\alpha_{i}
in
S_{2}^{d}
and
S_{1}^{d-1}
are
\binom{d+2}{i+1},\binom{d}{i}
respectively (the coefficients are
0
outside of this range).

\binom{d+2}{i+1}/\binom{d}{i}=\frac{(d+2)!i!(d-i)!}{d!(i+1)!(d+1-i)!}=\frac{(d+2)(d+1)}{(i+1)(d+1-i)}<d+2

for all
1\leq i\leq d
, where the final inequality is true as
1\cdot(d+1)=(\frac{d+2}{2})^{2}-(\frac{d}{2})^{2}\leq(\frac{d+2}{2})^{2}-(\frac{d}{2}-i)^{2}=(i+1)(d+1-i)
.
All in all, coefficient-wise,
S_{1}^{d-1}\geq\frac{S_{2}^{d}}{d+2}
, so by assumption
S_{1}^{d-1}>1
.
∎

We can now proceed to prove
Theorem

1.5
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.5
.

By the proof of
Proposition

3.1
,
S_{2}^{d}
is the cost of
\partial(\Delta^{d+1})
, which has
d+2
vertices. By assumption
d+2-S_{2}^{d}<0
, so by the first moment method a.a.s no simplexes of dimensions
d+1
and above appear. So it suffices to look at
d
-dimensional strongly connected components.
Since
S_{2}^{d}>d+2
, combining
Lemma

3.6
and
Corollary

3.4
gives that all expansion operations of dimension
d
have a positive cost.

For
d
-dimensional strongly connected components to collapse to dimension
d-1
, it is enough for a growth process to exist, such that every expansion operation has a free face, as in that case we may simply collapse the faces from last to first.
Since a.a.s no faces above dimension
d
are attached, the only expansions that have no free faces are those attached along
\partial(\Delta^{d})
.

Assume
C
is a strongly connected subcomplex in
\underline{X}
with a growth process
\Delta^{d}=C_{0}\subset\dots\subset C_{m}=C
such that
C=C_{m-1}\cup_{\partial(\Delta^{d})}\Delta^{d}
. This incurs no loss of generality, since any growth process featuring such an attachment at an intermediate stage can simply be truncated at that step.
In
C_{m-1}
, the maximal faces of
\partial(\Delta^{d})
come from distinct
d
-faces. Denote those faces by
f_{0},...,f_{d}
, in order of addition in this growth process, and denote by
C_{t_{i}}
the first stage containing
f_{i}
.

By
Proposition

3.1
,
\lim_{n}\log_{n}(E(C_{t_{0}}))\leq d+1-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\binom{d+1}{i+1}\alpha_{i}
(which is just the budget).
Further,

\lim_{n}\log_{n}(E(C_{t_{1}}))\leq d+1-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\binom{d+1}{i+1}\alpha_{i}+1-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\binom{d}{i}\alpha_{i}

as at a certain point a vertex adding operation must add the vertex of
\partial(\Delta^{d})
not contained in
f_{0}
(note- this claim is not contingent on this vertex being absent in
C_{t_{0}}
).
Similarly,

\lim_{n}\log_{n}(E(C_{t_{k}}))\leq d+2-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\binom{d+1-j}{i+1-j}\alpha_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\binom{d+1-k}{i+1-k}\alpha_{i},

as we must add the
(j-1)
-face in
\partial(\Delta^{d})
not contained in
\bigcup^{j-1}_{i=0}f_{i}
.

All in all (under the convention that
\binom{b}{a}=0
if
a<0
or
a>b
):

\lim_{n}\log_{n}(E(C_{m-1}))\leq d+2-\sum_{k=0}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\binom{d+1-k}{i+1-k}\alpha_{i}=d+2-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{k=0}^{d}\binom{d+1-k}{i+1-k}\alpha_{i}.

For
i<d
,
\sum_{j=0}^{d}\binom{d+1-j}{i+1-j}=\binom{d+2}{i+1}
by the hockey-stick identity. For
i=d
,
\sum_{j=0}^{d}\binom{d+1-j}{i+1-j}=d+1
.
C
has one
d
-face more than
C_{m-1}
, and so

\lim_{n}\log_{n}(E(C))=-\alpha_{d}+\lim_{n}\log_{n}(E(C_{m-1}))\leq d+2-S_{2}^{d}<0.

We conclude
E(C)\to 0
, which finishes the proof by Markov’s inequality.
∎

4
Upper model

In this section we transition to thinking about the upper multiparametric model
\overline{X}(n;p_{1},...)
where
p_{i}=n^{-\alpha_{i}}
and
p_{j}=0
for
j>r
, some maximal dimension.
Unlike in the lower model, the components we wish to collapse may be of a significantly greater dimension than what they collapse to, so we need a slight change in definitions:

Definition 4.1
.

Let
C\subset C^{\prime}
be simplicial complexes.
C
is called an
honest subcomplex
if no two maximal faces of
C
are contained in the same maximal face of
C^{\prime}
.
If this is the case, we denote it by
C\subset_{h}C^{\prime}
. Clearly
E(C\subset_{h}\overline{X})\leq E(C\subset\overline{X})
. Replacing
\subset
by
\subset_{h}
in the definition of cost in
Definition

2.9
, we call this quantity the
honest cost
(The
“honest budget”
is identical to the budget as the honesty condition is trivial in the case of a single simplex).

Remark 4.2
.

Strong connectivity components w.r.t any dimension are honest subcomplexes. The reason for using this definition rather than the components themselves is for ease of calculation.

In the lower model, the non-honest cost decreases predictably with every expansion operation. This is no longer true in the upper model (as a complex with many maximal faces may be contained in a single simplex of a greater dimension).
In contrast, the honest cost does decrease predictably under honest containment, as we will see in
Lemma

4.3
.

Recall from
Definition

1.2
that
\beta_{i}:=i+1-\alpha_{i}
,
\beta:=\max\{\beta_{i}\}
,
l=\lfloor\beta\rfloor
. Recall as well that
\gamma_{k}:=\max_{i\geq k}\{\beta_{i}\}
and
e_{k}:=\gamma_{k}-k
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
Z
be a strongly connected complex w.r.t dimension
l+1
. In
\overline{X}(n;p_{1},...,p_{r})
,
p_{i}=n^{-\alpha_{i}}
we have the following:

1.

E(Z\subset\overline{X})=O(n^{\beta})
. In particular, the budget is less than
l+1
.

2.

Let
Z^{\prime}=Z\cup\tau,\tau\simeq\Delta^{m},m>l
be the result of an operation expanding
Z
(w.r.t dimension
l+1
) which increases the number of maximal faces. Then
E(Z^{\prime}\subset_{h}\overline{X})=O(E(Z\subset_{h}\overline{X})n^{e_{l+1}+v+l-m})
, where
v
is the number of new vertices added by the operation.
In other words, the honest cost of such an operation is at least
m-e_{l+1}-v-l
(note that
e_{l+1}<0
and that
m\geq v+l
).

Proof.

1.

Consider a particular injective mapping
g
of the vertices of
\sigma=\Delta^{d},d\geq l+1
to
[n]
.
g(\sigma)
is a face in
\overline{X}
if and only if
g(\sigma)
is contained in a face in
X
(the hypergraph).
Denote by
J_{i}
the event that
g(\sigma)
is contained in an
i
-dimensional face in
X
. Then

P(J_{i})=\binom{n-d-1}{i-d}n^{-\alpha_{i}}-\sum_{j=0}^{i-d-1}\binom{n-d-1}{2(i-d)-j}n^{-2\alpha_{i}}+\dots=\theta(n^{i-\alpha_{i}-d})

where we use inclusion-exclusion, and the fact that the appearance of different simplexes in
X
is independent. Since
\max(i+1-\alpha_{i})=\beta<l+1
, we conclude, assuming
d\geq l+1
, that
i-\alpha_{i}-d<-1
, and so the first term dominates asymptotically.
Further,
c\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{j}}=\theta(c)
, so the sum is of the claimed asymptotic class.
Using this we get

P\left(\bigcup_{i=d}^{r}J_{i}\right)=\theta\left(\sum_{i=d}^{r}n^{i-\alpha_{i}-d}-\sum_{i\geq j\geq d}^{r}n^{i+j-\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}-2d}+\sum_{i\geq j\geq k\geq d}\dots\right)

where we use the same arguments again, and conclude

P\left(\bigcup_{i=d}^{r}J_{i}\right)=\theta\left(\sum_{i=d}^{r}n^{i-\alpha_{i}-d}\right).

By the same argument as in
Proposition

3.1
, there are
\theta(n^{d+1})
possible
g
’s, and so:

E(\Delta^{d}\subset\overline{X})=\theta(n^{d+1}(n^{-\alpha_{d}}+n^{1-\alpha_{d+1}}+...+n^{r-d-\alpha_{r}}))=\theta(n^{\max_{i\geq d}\{i+1-\alpha_{i}\}})=\theta(n^{\gamma_{d}}).

(1)

But
\gamma_{d}\leq\beta<l+1
, giving the budget.

2.

Denote
E(Z\subset_{h}\overline{X})=\zeta
, and suppose
Z^{\prime}
has
v
vertices more than
Z
. Let
g
be an injective map from the vertices of
Z^{\prime}
to
[n]
.
g(Z^{\prime})
is an honest subcomplex of
\overline{X}
if and only if all the following are satisfied:

a)

g(Z)
is an honest subcomplex.

b)

g(\tau)
is a face in
\overline{X}
.

c)

No face of
\overline{X}
contains both
g(\tau)
and a maximal face of
g(Z)
.

Let
\left\{Z_{i}\right\}
be the maximal faces of
Z
. Then by the same arguments as above

P\left(g(Z^{\prime})\subset_{h}\overline{X}\mid g(Z)\subset_{h}\overline{X}\right)=

\sum_{i=m}^{r}\left(\binom{n-m-1}{i-m}-\sum_{Z_{j}}\binom{n-|Z_{j}\cup\tau|}{i-|Z_{j}\cup\tau|+1}\right)n^{-\alpha_{i}}+o(n^{i-m-\alpha_{i}})=\theta(n^{\gamma_{m}-m-1}).

The difference of binomial coefficients is to exclude maximal faces containing both
g(\tau)
and one of the
Z_{j}
(containing more than one is excluded by the condition that
g(Z)
is an honest subcomplex), and the asymptotic is achieved by the observation that
\sum_{Z_{j}}\binom{n-|Z_{j}\cup\tau|}{i-|Z_{j}\cup\tau|+1}=o\left(\binom{n-m-1}{i-m}\right)
as well.

P(g(Z^{\prime})\subset_{h}\overline{X})=P(g(Z^{\prime})\subset_{h}\overline{X}\mid g(Z)\subset_{h}\overline{X})\cdot P(g(Z)\subset_{h}\overline{X})=\theta\left(P(g(Z)\subset_{h}\overline{X})n^{\gamma_{m}-m-1}\right).

E(Z\subset_{h}\overline{X})=\sum_{g|_{Z}}P(g(Z)\subset_{h}\overline{X})
and similarly
E(Z^{\prime}\subset_{h}\overline{X})=\sum_{g}P(g(Z^{\prime})\subset_{h}\overline{X})
.
Therefore

E(Z^{\prime}\subset_{h}\overline{X})=\sum_{g}P(g(Z^{\prime})\subset_{h}\overline{X})=\theta\left(n^{\gamma_{m}-m-1}\sum_{g}P(g(Z)\subset_{h}\overline{X})\right)=

\theta\left(n^{\gamma_{m}-m-1+v}\sum_{g|_{Z}}P(g(Z)\subset_{h}\overline{X})\right)=\theta\left(n^{\gamma_{m}-m-1+v}E(Z\subset_{h}\overline{X})\right).

Recall from
Definition

1.2
that
e_{m}=\gamma_{m}-m
, and that
e_{m}\leq l+1-m+e_{l+1}
, and we get the desired result.

∎

Part 2 of the lemma should be understood in the following way- the new simplex must intersect with the old complex at least at
l+1
vertices in order to maintain strong connectivity.
If all other vertices are new- the operation costs
e_{l+1}
, and we pay
1
for any additional vertex of intersection. We formalize the concept in the next subsection.

4.1
Redundancy

Definition 4.4
.

Let
Z_{0}\subset Z_{1}\subset...\subset Z
be a growth process of a simplicial complex, strongly connected w.r.t dimension
k
. The expansion operation
Z_{i}\cup_{C}\Delta^{d}=Z_{i+1}
is said to have
redundancy

t_{i}
if
|Z_{i+1}|-|Z_{i}|=d+1-k-t_{i}
, i.e
t_{i}=d+1-(|Z_{i+1}|-|Z_{i}|)-k
, meaning
\Delta^{d}
is attached along
t_{i}+k

“old vertices”
- vertices in
Z_{i}
.
The redundancy of
Z
is said to be
R(Z):=\sum_{i}t_{i}
.

Remark 4.5
.

The redundancy of a complex w.r.t dimension
k
does not depend on the choice of the growth process. Indeed, denoting
Z_{i+1}=Z_{i}\cup_{C}\sigma_{i+1}
,

|Z|=|Z_{0}|+\sum_{i=1}\left(\dim(\sigma_{i})+1-k-t_{i}\right)=\sum_{\sigma\in M(Z)}(\dim(\sigma)+1)-(|M(Z)|-1)k-R(Z).

Therefore

R(Z)=\sum_{\sigma\in M(Z)}(\dim(\sigma)+1)-(|M(Z)|-1)k-|Z|

(2)

which is independent of the process. Note- the above formula also does not require
Z
to be strongly connected, and therefore can be used as the definition in this case.
Note: only faces of dimension greater or equal to
k-1
count as maximal for this purpose
.

We may now reinterpret
Lemma

4.3
as saying that an expansion operation
op
has a cost of
R(op)-e_{l+1}
.
The redundancy may be thought of as a measure of the complexity of a complex.
Redundancy
0
complexes are akin to
“trees”
, as any new face adds the maximal number of vertices keeping it strongly connected w.r.t dimension
k
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
C\subset Z
be an induced subcomplex. Then (w.r.t
k
)

.

Proof.

The general case will follow from the case where
C
and
Z
differ by a single vertex:
Suppose
C
is the induced subcomplex on
Z\setminus\left\{w\right\}
, for some vertex
w
.

We observe how the expressions in
Equation

2
differ between
C
and
Z
: Obviously
|C|=|Z|-1
.
If
|M(C)|=|M(Z)|
,
\sum(\dim(\sigma)+1)
decreases by at least
\deg_{M}(w)
- the number of maximal faces containing
w
. This is a non-empty set, and so in this case
R(C)\leq R(Z)
.

If
|M(C)|<|M(Z)|
(it cannot increase as different maximal faces in

come from different maximal faces in
Z
), suppose
\left\{\sigma_{j}\setminus w\right\}_{j\in J}
are no longer maximal.
Then

, but

\sum_{\sigma\in M(C)}(\dim(\sigma)+1)\leq\sum_{\sigma\in M(Z)}(\dim(\sigma)+1)-\sum_{j\in J}(\dim(\sigma_{j})+1)

(where the inequality is due to the possible reductions in dimensions).

\dim(\sigma_{j})+1\geq k
for all

, and comparing with
Equation

2
grants the result in this case as well.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
C^{\prime}=C\cup_{G}\sigma
, where
\sigma
is a simplex. If

has at most

For two faces

By

We proceed by induction on the number of maximal faces in

Assume we proved the claim for any

WLOG we assume all vertices in

are in

(any new vertex is free, and so may be gotten rid of by collapses). We proceed by induction on the number of vertices

in
G
. For

1.

If there exists a vertex

common to all maximal faces of

If each vertex has exactly 1 maximal face of

In the first case,

(b)

3.

Suppose there exists a vertex

.

has less vertices than

|C_{I}\cap\sigma_{i_{1}}|\geq k
by assumption), and therefore
R(Z_{I+1})\geq R(Z_{I})+\sum_{j}\delta_{j}
.

Let
\epsilon_{j}=R(Z_{i_{j}+1})-R(Z_{i_{j}})
denote the redundancy added at step
i_{j}
. If the expansion
Z_{i_{J}}\mapsto Z_{i_{J}+1}
does not add any new vertices to
C_{I}
(meaning
\delta_{J}=0
), then the intersection
\sigma_{i_{J}}\cap C_{I}\cap\left(\bigcup^{j<J}\sigma_{i_{j}}\right)
must consist of at least two maximal faces (otherwise the intersection is an existing face of
C_{I}\cap\left(\bigcup^{j<J}\sigma_{i_{j}}\right)
), therefore
R(Z_{i_{J}+1})-R(Z_{i_{J}})=\epsilon_{J}\geq 1
.

All in all,
R(Z)\geq R(Z_{I+1})\geq\sum_{j=2}(\delta_{j}+\epsilon_{j})\geq k
, contradicting our assumption.
Thus
C_{i}
has at most
k
maximal faces, as desired.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

The bound in
Lemma

4.8
is tight. This is easily seen by plugging
\partial(\Delta^{k+1})
into
Equation

2
.

4.2
Proof of
Theorem

1.6

We now have set the table to prove
Theorem

1.6
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

2.13
, collapsibility to a certain dimension is determined by strong connectivity components w.r.t the dimension above it. Since strong connectivity components are always honest subcomplexes, it will be enough to prove that all honest subcomplexes of
\overline{X}
strongly connected w.r.t dimension
l+1
collapse to dimension
l
.

In
Lemma

4.3
we saw that an expansion operation w.r.t dimension
l+1
has an honest cost of at least
-e_{l+1}
, and we start with a budget of less than
l+1
.
From this we can deduce by arguments identical to those of
Corollary

3.5
that only a finite number of isomorphism types of complexes strongly connected w.r.t dimension
l+1
may a.a.s appear as honest subcomplexes of
\overline{X}
, and those have less than
l+2+\frac{l+1}{|e_{l+1}|}
vertices.

By
Lemma

4.3
we may also exclude all strong connectivity components with redundancy greater or equal to
l+1
, as those would cost more than the budget.
But by
Lemma

4.8
, a component strongly connected w.r.t dimension
l+1
with redundancy of at most
l
collapses onto dimension
l
, finishing the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.10
.

Under the conditions of
Theorem

1.6
and assuming
\beta\not\in\mathbb{N}
, the integral homology of
\overline{X}
is concentrated in dimension
l
(by
Theorem

1.3
) and is free.
If
l>2
,
\overline{X}
is also simply connected, and consequently
is homotopic to a wedge of spheres of dimension
l
(this is a folklore result in algebraic topology, proven by combining the Hurewicz and Whitehead theorems).

5
Open Problems

•

As we mentioned after the statement of
Theorem

1.6
, the upper model, after the collapse, appears to be similar to the Linial-Meshulam model with intersecting faces being positively correlated.
It might be interesting to investigate in
\overline{X}
various phenomena prominent in the study of
Y_{d}(n,p)
, such as the shadow or expansion.

•

The point above also suggests an approach to an underexplored area in random complexes- complexes that are symmetric (such as all models mentioned in this paper), but are not built from independent parts.
This calls to mind things like the Ising model or its generalizations- the Random Cluster Model of random graphs (see
[
7
]
), or the Plaquette Random-Cluster Model in cubical complexes (see
[
3
]
). In these models, however, the probabilities are assigned based on topological properties of the entire complex, rather than individual correlations.

•

Corollary

4.10
does not apply when
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
. This is where the complex
“transitions”
between the regime where it has complete (
l-2
)-skeleton to one where it has complete
(l-1)
-skeleton, and so might have non-trivial homologies in two dimensions simultaneously.
This, as always, begs the question of whether it is a wedge of spheres in that critical case as well.

•

In the lower model we did not address the case where
S_{2}^{d}=d+2
for some
d
, which is also unaddressed in Fowler’s
Theorem

1.4
.
It is clear that the answer in this case will depend on lower order terms, since if
E(\partial(\Delta^{d+1}))\to\infty
and
p_{d+1}
is sufficiently small, there will be simplex boundaries generating homology and preventing the collapse, while
E(\partial(\Delta^{d+1}))\to 0
enables us to use the present proof as is.
The case where
E(\partial(\Delta^{d+1}))
tends to a positive constant would also depend on whether
p_{d+1}=1
or not, giving outcomes with probabilities not necessarily tending to
0
or
1
in the latter case.

•

Lemma

4.8
should be useful in other questions concerning collapsibility. The author also believes that these are not the only possible uses of redundancy. Certainly, a complex with almost maximal redundancy is close to the complete skeleton of a simplex, but what is the minimal redundancy for which a complex cannot be collapsible, or have non-trivial homology? If these are different, can we characterize the complexes
“in between”
, or construct a model generating them?
