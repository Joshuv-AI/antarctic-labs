Executive summary
The goal of this work was to develop a model to forecast the disturbance storm time index, Dst, 1–12 hours into
the future. We did not successfully accomplish this task. The main conclusion is that the forecasting limitation
appears to be driven more by information availability than by the specific form of the Dst forecasting model.
When future solar-wind and interplanetary magnetic-field drivers are supplied to a diagnostic “Cheating” model,
Dst forecasts improve substantially. However, those future drivers are not available in real time. Observing solar
activity drivers closer to the Sun might provide meaningful upstream visibility into impending storms near Earth.
A sudden-commencement analysis supports the same conclusion. Most storms are preceded by sudden commence-
ments, but most sudden commencements are not followed by storms. Thus, a sudden commencement alone is not a
reliable real-time storm-warning signal.

Bottom line: The main barrier to accurate 1–12 hour-ahead Dst forecasting is likely lead-time information about

Forecasting model comparison
We performed a forecasting study that compared deep-learning models that differed in what information was
available at forecast issue time. All learned models used the same forecasting architecture and forecasted Dst 1–12
hours ahead; the comparison isolates the value of the information supplied to the model.

• Persistence: assumes future Dst remains equal to current Dst.
• Dst-only: uses the previous 48 hours of Dst.
• Operational: uses the previous 48 hours of Dst and the previous 48 hours of observed drivers, including scalar
  magnetic-field strength, IMF Bz , solar-wind proton density, and solar-wind speed.
• Cheating: uses previous 48 hours of Dst and drivers, plus the next 12 hours of future drivers. This model is not
  deployable in real time (hence why it is called “Cheating”), but it is useful as a diagnostic upper bound.

The learned real-time models improved on the Persistence model, and the Operational model was usually slightly
better than the Dst-only model. However, the improvement from adding past driver information was modest (see
Figure 1). The much larger improvement came from the Cheating model, which had access to future driver values.
This indicates that the future driver trajectory contains substantial information about future Dst, especially around
storm onset and intensification.

Figure 1: Overall holdout comparison across model variants. For mean absolute error (MAE), root mean-squared error
(RMSE), relative MAE (which is the model’s MAE divided by the Persistence model’s MAE), relative RMSE (model’s RMSE
/ Persistence model’s RMSE), and interval width (the width of the 95% forecast interval in units of Dst), smaller is better; for
relative MAE and relative RMSE, values below 1 indicate improvement over Persistence. For 95% interval coverage, values
closer to 95% are better (coverage measures how often future Dst values fall within their 95% predictive intervals). The
real-time learned models improve on Persistence, but the diagnostic Cheating model is substantially more accurate (measured
in MAE, RMSE, relative MAE, and relative RMSE) and with less uncertainty (smaller mean interval width).

A concrete example occurred during the May 2024 storm (Figure 2). The Dst-only model sees a sudden jump in
Dst (i.e., a sudden commencement) but the model does not forecast an impending storm (more on this in the next
section). The Operational model sees the same sudden jump in Dst as the Dst-only model as well as a sudden jump
in solar wind speed (Vsw), Scalar B, proton density, and a minor, negative drop in Bz. The Operational model still
didn’t predict an impending storm. The Cheating model does predict an impending storm, but it had access to
the future drivers which included a sustained rise in Scalar B, proton density, and solar wind speed along with a
sustained negative drop in Bz . Those driver conditions are important predictors of Dst, but those future driver
values are not available in real-time. This example illustrates the central issue: the information needed to anticipate
the storm is present in the future drivers, but not in the past Dst or past driver values.

Why sudden commencements do not solve the problem
A natural question after looking at Figure 2 is whether sudden commencements can serve as a warning signal. A
sudden commencement is a short, sharp upward jump in Dst. Physically, it is commonly interpreted as the magnetic
response to a burst of solar-wind pressure that briefly compresses Earth’s magnetic field. This is different from the
main phase of a geomagnetic storm, when Dst usually drops rather than rises.
For this analysis, a storm was defined as starting when Dst first reaches ≤ −50 nT and ending when Dst later
recovers to ≥ −25 nT. Using this definition, there were 1,656 storms in the data set, which used hourly data from
January 1, 1964 through January 10, 2026.
Because there is not a standard Dst-only operational definition of sudden commencement analogous to the storm-
threshold definition, I used an operational proxy. A sudden commencement was defined as a one-hour positive Dst

jump that is large relative to recent background variability. Specifically, the jump had to be more than 2 times the
mean absolute one-hour Dst change over the previous 23 hours, provided Dst was not already inside a storm at the
end of that 23-hour baseline interval. By this definition, there were 30,916 sudden commencements in the data
set.

These two results are not contradictory. They answer different questions. Looking backward from storms, sudden
commencements are common. Looking forward from sudden commencements, storms are uncommon. For forecasting,
the forward probability is the relevant quantity, because a forecaster observes the sudden commencement first and
must decide whether a storm is likely to follow. This helps explain why the Dst-only model in Figure 2 did not
predict an impending storm even though it had just observed a sudden commencement: 9 out of 10 times a storm
does not follow a sudden commencement.

Sensitivity to the sudden-commencement definition
The sudden-commencement calculation depends on operational choices: how large a Dst jump must be to count
as a sudden commencement, and how long after that jump one should look for storm onset. To test whether the
main conclusion depended on these choices, we varied the jump threshold multiplier from 1 to 5 and the follow-up
window from 6 to 48 hours. See results in Figure 3.

Figure 3: Sensitivity of P(storm | SC) and P(SC | storm) to the operational SC definition. The x-axis varies the number of
hours after an SC checked for storm onset; the same value is also used as the backward storm-to-SC lookup window. The
y-axis varies the SC jump threshold multiplier. The black outline marks the current operational setting: a 24-hour follow-up
window and a 2x jump threshold.

The precise probabilities changed across this grid, but the qualitative conclusion remained the same. The forward
probability P(storm | SC) remained well below 1 across all definitions considered. Even using a strict SC definition
and a 48-hour follow-up window, the largest forward probability was 0.366. Under the current operational definition,
it was 0.094.

 4

The backward probability P(SC | storm) was much more sensitive to the definition, ranging from about 0.08 to 0.96.
If the SC definition is strict and the look-back window is short, fewer storms are classified as having a preceding
SC (only 8.5%). If the SC definition is loose and the look-back window is long, nearly all storms have at least one
detected positive Dst jump before onset (96.1%). These results suggest the time between sudden commencements
and storm onsets can be quite variable.
The important forecasting conclusion is therefore not the exact value of either probability under one definition.
The important conclusion is the asymmetry: sudden commencements are common before storms in
hindsight, but sudden commencements alone are weak forward-looking storm predictors.

Integrated interpretation
The model comparison and sudden-commencement analysis point to the same conclusion. Dst-only information
is often ambiguous before storm onset. These findings are consistent with those found by Chase and Larsen
(LA-UR-22-21012). A sudden upward Dst jump may be part of the canonical storm sequence, but in the historical
data most sudden-commencement-like events are not followed by threshold storms. This helps explain why the
Dst-only model should not necessarily forecast a storm-level drop from a sudden commencement alone.
The Operational model adds past and current driver information, but the improvement over the Dst-only model is
modest. The Cheating model, in contrast, improves substantially because it uses the future driver trajectory. This
suggests that the missing information is not primarily in the model architecture; it is in the inputs available at
forecast issue time.
If future solar-wind and IMF drivers were known, or if their Earth-directed evolution could be observed earlier, Dst
would likely be much more forecastable. Without that earlier driver information, a Dst-only or real-time driver-only
model has limited ability to distinguish sudden commencements that will be followed by storms from those that will
not. This points to the value of missions such as PUNCH1 , which is designed to observe the corona and young
solar wind as a connected Sun-to-Earth system and has already demonstrated, in an initial proof-of-concept test,
the ability to predict the near-Earth arrival of a solar eruption with high precision. PUNCH is not a direct Dst
forecasting system, but it is an example of the type of earlier upstream information about solar-wind and coronal
mass ejection evolution that could improve the driver inputs needed for operational Dst forecasting.

Conclusion
This project did not show that Dst forecasting is impossible. It showed that the main barrier to accurate 1–12 hour-
ahead Dst forecasting is likely lead-time information about the drivers. Future solar-wind and IMF conditions
are highly informative, but they are not available to a real-time model at issue time. Sudden commencements
alone do not solve this problem, because most sudden commencements are not followed by storms. Substantial
improvement in operational Dst forecasting will likely require better upstream driver measurements or reliable
forecasts of those drivers, rather than only a different Dst forecasting model.
