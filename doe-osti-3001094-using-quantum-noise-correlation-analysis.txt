I. Background/Introduction

 In an insightful and pioneering experiment, Hanbury Brown and Twiss (HBT) demonstrated that
quantum statistical noise correlations could be used to measure the size of the star Sirius [Hanbury Brown
1956]. This correlation effect relies on quantum interference between possible detection paths for two
indistinguishable photons. This technique, often called intensity interferometry, exploits the interference
of photons independently emitted from different regions of a non-coherent light source. The basis is the
measurement of intensity correlations as a function of time and detector spacing. No beam splitting or
preservation of phase information is required. The discovery of the HBT effect was a landmark in the
development of the field of quantum optics [Glauber 2006].
 The original HBT experiment exploited two-photon correlations manifested in a time-domain signal
to measure the size of a distant photon source (a star). We attempted to test the same principle of
quantum statistical noise correlation to measure the ion temperature in a laboratory plasma. If
eventually successful, the next step will be to build a compact, inexpensive, and robust ion temperature
diagnostic suitable for a burning plasma environment. This diagnostic would require only a small
optical view of the plasma through a port to record impurity line emission. It would be straightforward to
locate the instrumentation remote from the reactor behind the neutron and bio-shielding. This technique
relies solely on quantum correlations to measure ion temperature—there is no grating dispersion of a
wavelength spectrum or pulse-height analysis of photon energy. There is no need to inject a laser or
particle beam into the plasma to stimulate emission. The quantum noise correlation instrumentation
tested consisted of two single-photon detectors capable of picosecond resolution, a time-tagging unit, and
simple light collection optics with a filter monochromator to pass only photons from a bright spectral line
in the visible wavelength range.
 This project was an application of quantum sensing to enhance diagnostic capability for plasma and
fusion science. Quantum sensing is “use of a quantum system, quantum properties, or quantum
phenomena to perform a measurement of a physical quantity” [Degen 2017]. This project applied a
variation of the HBT technique to a plasma, and is an application of a quantum sensing approach to
improve plasma measurement. A significant challenge for a diagnostic based on the HBT effect is
maximization of the second-order correlation function. This requires careful characterization of
instrumental effects, collection of data ensembles, and development of appropriate analysis algorithms.

II. Research Methods

 Under typical fusion plasma conditions, electron impact excitation is the dominant process through
which ions radiate line emission. Doppler broadening typically dominates other line broadening
mechanisms, and is determined by the ion temperature. Specifically, for a spatially uniform quasi-
monochromatic thermal light source (a plasma), the second-order correlation function associated with the
HBT effect takes the form [Boiko 2009]:

 The spatial coherence term in Eq. 1 contains an argument similar to the dimensionless number in

measurement of g(2), particularly in the original stellar measurements made by Hanbury Brown and Twiss.
The HBT effect is apparent only if a pair of photons is emitted within a coherence time. The response
bandwidth of currently available detectors has improved substantially since the original HBT
measurement. For example, the hybrid photodetectors in our setup have an instrument response function

plasma) is unacceptably large with two single-photon detectors.
 The instrumentation assembled to measure the temporal correlation function of an emission line from
a plasma is illustrated in Fig. 1. The upper panel is a schematic illustration of the optical layout, the lower
panel is a photograph of the actual setup. Space constraints required the entire setup to be contained on 3
× 6 foot optical table. Light was transferred from a small collection telescope via a 600 µm core
solarization resistant fiber optic. The f = 100 mm compact monochromator selects the emission line to be
measured, with a range of 200 – 650 nm. The 600 µm fiber core is the entrance aperture of the
monochromator, and the 200 µm pinhole is the exit aperture, which provides a passband of several nm for
a Doppler broadened line. The distance between the 200 and 2000 µm pinholes is 2.7 m, which keeps the
spatial coherence term close to 1. The Rochon polarizer passes linearly polarized light of one orientation
(while diverting the orthogonal orientation) in order to eliminate decorrelation effects due to unpolarized
light. Although not shown in the figure, the light path contains multiple 6.3 mm diameter apertures at
various locations in order to eliminate stray light propagation through the system. The photon pulses
produced by the hybrid photodetectors are recorded by time-correlated single photon counting modules
with time bin width down to 813 fs. These modules are mounted in a PC sitting on a shelf mounted on
the optical table frame. Thus control and data acquisition moves with the instrumentation, and the entire
setup was portable.
 If ion temperature measurement is ultimately successful with this technique, the next step will be to
design a compact, inexpensive, and robust ion temperature diagnostic suitable for a burning plasma
environment. This diagnostic would require only a small optical view of the plasma through a port plug,
and would be straightforward to locate remote from the device behind the neutron and bio-shielding (Fig.
2). Suitable visible emission lines for the ion species of interest would need to be identified (even highly
charged tungsten produces visible emission, e.g. [Shinohara 2015]). The two detectors shown in the
figure would be arrays of SPADs in order to multiplex photon counting and reduce the time needed to
record an ion temperature with sufficient accuracy. The filter, beamsplitter, and detectors could be placed
in a purpose-built mount, and the electronics integrated into the package to produce a compact diagnostic
system occupying a fraction of a cubic meter. Testing could be done on a large-scale plasma experiment
since the port space requirement would be small. The technique may also be useful for measurements on
plasmas that are spatially small, such as in a Z-pinch device. Furthermore, while SPAD detectors are
most convenient for this measurement, there are hybrid and other detector technologies to extend
sensitivity into the ultraviolet and even x-ray.

 With this diagnostic technique, even a single view of the plasma could provide information on the
radial profile of the ion temperature. In any given plasma there are multiple ion species, and the
emissivity of each ion species peaks at a certain electron temperature. Using a separately measured
electron temperature profile (e.g., from Thomson scattering), the approximate location from which the
line radiation of each ion species was emitted can be determined. Correlating the measured ion
temperature of each species with the plasma minor radius enables production of an approximate ion
temperature profile.
 The main remaining question is how long the signal will have to be collected to make an ion
temperature measurement. There is a high probability the HBT quantum optical effect will behave as
expected. With a two-detector setup, it will be too long (>>1 sec) for a practical measurement on a fusion
plasma. This could be addressed with an array of detectors to multiplex measurement of photon
correlation. Megapixel SPAD arrays are available [Morimoto 2020], but readouts necessary for time-
correlated single photon counting of such large arrays are not yet available.
