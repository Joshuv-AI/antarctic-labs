Patterns of dialysis-related complications and their association with baseline characteristics in outpatient hemodialysis: a retrospective analysis of nursing documentation
Authors: Jie Zhang, Liu Sha Chen, Xi Lin Yang, Wen Yang, Yi Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo describe complication patterns documented in routine nursing records and explore their association with baseline demographic and diagnostic characteristics among outpatients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) on maintenance hemodialysis. The goal is to inform the development of nurse-led, documentation-informed observation protocols rather than to establish definitive predictive markers.MethodsA single-center, retrospective observational study was conducted using electronic nursing records from January 1, 2024, to April 30, 2025. Two reviewers independently extracted data. The analysis focused on patient-level occurrence (ever vs. never) of intradialytic hypotension (IDH) as the primary outcome. Session-level event counts are reported solely to contextualize nursing workload and do not represent incidence rates. Univariate screening (P < 0.1) preceded multivariable binary logistic regression.ResultsAt the patient level, increasing age showed a marginal inverse association with IDH occurrence. Diabetic nephropathy was also associated with lower recorded odds of IDH compared to the uremia reference group. Associations for other complications did not reach conventional significance and are interpreted as exploratory.ConclusionBaseline characteristics demonstrate context-dependent associations with complication patterns captured in routine outpatient hemodialysis nursing documentation. Given the absence of dialysis prescription variables and the inherent limitations of retrospective chart abstraction, these observations are hypothesis-generating. Future prospective studies integrating real-time hemodynamic monitoring and structured nursing assessments are needed to validate these patterns.

Introduction

Hemodialysis remains the most critical form of renal replacement therapy for patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD), significantly extending life expectancy [1]. Despite ongoing advances in dialysis technology, dialysis-related complications remain common. These include intradialytic hypotension (IDH), vascular access bleeding, infections, hematoma, and hypoglycemia [2]. Such complications not only disrupt the dialysis process and reduce treatment adherence but also increase the risk of treatment interruption, thereby adversely affecting patient outcomes and quality of life [3–5].

Epidemiological data indicate that the incidence of (IDH) ranges from 20% to 30%, while vascular access-related complications represent a major clinical threat among outpatients undergoing dialysis [6]. Compared with in-hospital dialysis settings, outpatient dialysis units face greater challenges in continuous clinical monitoring and emergency response capabilities [7]. Due to limited real-time surveillance and restricted access to immediate medical support, acute complications in outpatient hemodialysis patients are more likely to be detected late or inadequately managed, increasing patient safety risks [8]. Therefore, improving early identification of high-risk patients and implementing precise nursing interventions are essential for enhancing patient safety and the overall quality of care in outpatient dialysis settings.

Existing research on dialysis complications has primarily focused on technical parameters, such as dialysis modality, blood flow rate, ultrafiltration volume, and anticoagulation protocols [9]. Although the influence of these hemodialysis parameters—such as dialysis mode and ultrafiltration rate—on complication risk has been extensively studied, their optimization is typically led by physicians [10].

In contrast, nurses are the healthcare professionals most closely involved with patients during dialysis, responsible for real-time monitoring and implementation of clinical interventions. They are directly confronted with patient-specific, non-technical risk factors, including advanced age, cognitive function, illness perception, and self-management capacity [11, 12].

Therefore, from a nursing practice perspective, a systematic investigation of the association between these baseline patient characteristics and the risk of complications is essential. Such research would support the development of informative risk assessment models and individualized intervention strategies. However, evidence in this area remains limited [13].

Growing evidence suggests that these patient-level factors play a critical role in treatment adherence, complication occurrence, and overall clinical outcomes [14, 15].

However, conventional risk models often rely on static demographic or laboratory variables, overlooking the dynamic, time-dependent nature of intradialytic physiology. Emerging evidence underscores that complication risk is highly time-dependent, shaped by real-time hemodynamic fluctuations and intra-individual variability that baseline snapshots alone cannot fully capture [16, 17]. In outpatient settings, where continuous physician oversight is limited, nurses serve as the primary observers of acute clinical changes. Their documentation reflects not only patient physiology but also clinical vigilance, intervention thresholds, and workflow realities [18, 19]. This study systematically analyzed routine nursing records to describe complication patterns and explore their relationships with baseline demographic and diagnostic characteristics. By focusing on what is consistently captured in everyday clinical practice, we aim to provide a documentation-informed foundation for nurse-led monitoring. Importantly, given the retrospective design and unmeasured treatment-level variables, these associations should be interpreted as context-dependent patterns rather than independent risk factors.

Methods

Study design and data source

This was a single-center, retrospective observational study designed to examine the occurrence of complications in patients undergoing hemodialysis and to identify associated factors. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. Data were collected from electronic medical records generated during routine clinical care between January 1, 2024, and April 30, 2025.

The observation window spans 16 months to capture seasonal variations in fluid management practices, ensure adequate longitudinal follow-up for stable outpatients, and align with the hospital’s electronic record upgrade cycle. This duration reflects a pragmatic approach to retrospective chart review rather than a fixed calendar constraint.

All information was recorded in real time by nursing staff during dialysis sessions and subsequently exported from the hospital’s information management system into Excel files. Data extraction was performed by two trained nurses independently. Discrepancies in data extraction or complication coding were resolved through discussion with a senior researcher. Free-text nursing notes were coded into structured categories based on a predefined dictionary of symptoms and events. The dataset included patient demographic characteristics, primary diagnosis of kidney disease, and detailed records of complications. No experimental interventions were performed, and all data were derived from standard clinical practice.

The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Patients received hemodialysis care according to standardized nursing protocols. When complications occurred, nursing staff followed established standard operating procedures (SOPs) for management. The type and outcome of each intervention were systematically documented and used as outcome measures in the analysis [20].

Study population and inclusion/exclusion criteria

No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, as this study utilized consecutive eligible records from routine clinical practice during the observation period. Missing data were handled via complete-case analysis: patients with severely incomplete baseline or complication documentation were excluded. For multivariable modeling, listwise deletion was applied, consistent with standard practice in exploratory observational analyses. Because excluded records lacked sufficient information for reliable outcome ascertainment, no formal imputation was performed. The potential for selection bias related to incomplete documentation is acknowledged as a limitation.

Outcome measures

This study primarily operated at the patient level. For each patient, complication occurrence was coded as a binary variable (“1” if documented in any session; “0” otherwise). Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the occurrence of intradialytic hypotension (IDH). IDH was defined according to the National Kidney Foundation’s Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) guidelines as a reduction in systolic blood pressure of ≥ 20 mmHg or a decrease in mean arterial pressure of > 10 mmHg during dialysis, accompanied by clinical symptoms (e.g., dizziness, nausea, or syncope) [21]. In this retrospective analysis, IDH identification relied on routine nursing documentation, where blood pressure drops were confirmed by repeated measurements within 5 min and triggered standard clinical interventions (e.g., saline administration or ultrafiltration adjustment). Importantly, this represents a clinically recorded event subject to nurse-dependent detection thresholds and local documentation practices, rather than a standardized research-grade hemodynamic measurement. Potential variability in outcome ascertainment due to clinical judgment and workflow norms is acknowledged and explicitly addressed in the Limitations section.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes (infection, hypoglycemia, palpitation, premature termination, bleeding, and hematoma) were analyzed as exploratory variables due to low event frequencies. They were defined as binary variables based on nursing documentation as follows:

Infection

Any dialysis-related infectious event documented during the treatment period, including catheter-related bloodstream infections or arteriovenous fistula/graft infections. Diagnosis was based on clinical signs (e.g., erythema, discharge, fever), laboratory findings, and interventions such as antibiotic initiation or access site management [22].

Hypoglycemia

A documented episode of abnormally low blood glucose level (typically < 70 mg/dL or < 3.9 mmol/L) occurring during or immediately after dialysis, accompanied by clinical symptoms such as sweating, tremor, confusion, or drowsiness. Blood glucose measurements and symptom reports were recorded by nursing staff.

Palpitation

Patient-reported sensations of rapid, irregular, or pounding heartbeat, assessed and documented by nursing personnel during dialysis. Cases were included only if the symptom was explicitly recorded in the nursing notes and not attributable to pre-existing arrhythmias without acute change.

Premature termination of dialysis

Unplanned early discontinuation of a hemodialysis session due to clinical or technical reasons, including vascular access clotting, severe patient discomfort (e.g., persistent (IDH) or nausea), or equipment malfunction. Sessions terminated for non-medical reasons (e.g., patient refusal unrelated to symptoms) were also captured but analyzed separately when possible.

All secondary outcomes were extracted from structured and unstructured fields in the nursing records. While standardized criteria were used, the consistency of complication coding across different nurses could not be independently verified, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

Arteriovenous fistula bleeding

Bleeding at the arteriovenous fistula site occurring during or immediately after hemodialysis, typically due to inadequate cannulation, poor needle site hemostasis, or premature dislodgement. Events requiring intervention such as prolonged compression, additional dressing, or medical assistance were recorded. All instances were documented by nursing staff in the clinical notes.

Hematoma

Development of a clinically evident subcutaneous hematoma at the needle insertion site, characterized by localized swelling, discoloration, and pain. Severity was assessed based on size and symptoms, with documentation made at the time of occurrence by the attending nurse. Cases were included only if the hematoma was directly linked to the dialysis access procedure and required monitoring or intervention.

These complications were captured as part of the secondary outcome set to evaluate procedural safety and patient vulnerability related to vascular access management. Data were extracted from nursing records using standardized definitions to ensure reliability and consistency in event reporting.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were first used to summarize the characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed; categorical variables were expressed as frequency (n) and percentage (%).

To examine the association between baseline patient characteristics and the occurrence of complications, univariate analyses were conducted. For categorical variables, the chi-square test was used; Fisher’s exact test was applied when expected cell counts were small. For continuous variables, the independent samples t-test was employed for normally distributed data, while the Mann-Whitney U test was used for variables that deviated from normality, as assessed by visual inspection and the Shapiro-Wilk test.

Variables with P < 0.1 in univariate analyses were retained for multivariable modeling. This threshold is widely adopted in exploratory epidemiological frameworks to reduce omitted variable bias while acknowledging that strict P < 0.05 cutoffs may prematurely exclude clinically relevant covariates in moderate-sized cohorts. Primary diagnosis was entered as a single categorical predictor to preserve clinical interpretability, avoid artificial fragmentation of overlapping diagnostic concepts, and mitigate multicollinearity. Categories reflect distinct primary etiologies leading to ESKD as coded in hospital records.

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 334 patients who received outpatient hemodialysis at our center between January 1, 2024, and April 30, 2025, were included in the study. Of these, 173 (51.8%) were male and 161 (48.2%) were female. The age range was 18 to 89 years, with a mean age of 55.61 ± 13.65 years.

According to body mass index (BMI) classification, 66.47% of patients (n = 222) had a normal BMI (18.5–24.9), 21.26% (n = 71) were overweight (25.0–29.9), and 2.10% (n = 7) were obese (≥ 30.0). A total of 189 patients (56.59%) had a high school education level or lower, while 145 (43.41%) had attained high school or higher education level.

With regard to primary diagnosis, uremia was the most common condition, accounting for 49.70% (n = 166) of cases, followed by chronic renal failure (20.36%, n = 68), hypertensive nephropathy (10.18%, n = 34), and diabetic nephropathy (7.78%, n = 26). Other diagnoses, including polycystic kidney disease, accounted for 3.29% (n = 11). (Table 1).

Discussion

This retrospective chart review reveals that complication patterns documented in routine outpatient nursing records do not uniformly align with traditional risk paradigms. IDH emerged as the most frequently documented complication. Notably, older age and diabetic nephropathy showed marginal, exploratory associations with lower recorded IDH odds [23]. These findings should not be interpreted as biological protection. Instead, they likely reflect differential clinical management in routine practice: older and diabetic patients routinely receive more conservative ultrafiltration targets and heightened monitoring vigilance. Routine nursing documentation indirectly captures these adaptive practices but cannot disentangle them from underlying patient physiology [24]. This tension highlights a recurring theme in dialysis research: trial-derived risk factors often fail to translate directly to real-world chart data, where what emerges is a composite of physiological vulnerability, clinical judgment, and institutional workflow norms.

The analytical design intentionally aggregated session-level events into patient-level binary outcomes. While pragmatic, this approach obscures temporal dynamics, recurrent event clustering, and within-patient variability. Consequently, our findings reflect occurrence patterns rather than time-dependent incidence or session-level risk. Furthermore, outcome ascertainment relied on routine nursing documentation. Although IDH was defined using established criteria, its identification depended on individual clinical thresholds, repeated measurements, and intervention triggers. This introduces observer-dependent variability: what constitutes a “documented event” may reflect local nursing judgment as much as physiological severity. These limitations reinforce the need to interpret associations as context-dependent observations rather than isolated causal effects.

Recent literature underscores that intradialytic risk is highly time-dependent, shaped by real-time hemodynamic fluctuations and intra-individual variability that static baseline variables cannot fully capture [23, 25]. Complication profiles in chronic dialysis are increasingly recognized as emerging from the interplay of volume status shifts, medication adherence, autonomic responsiveness, and treatment adaptations. In outpatient settings, nursing-driven monitoring and early detection serve as critical safety nets. Integrating structured nursing assessments with continuous biosensor data could bridge the gap between retrospective documentation patterns and prospective intervention. Speculative mechanisms such as vascular compliance shifts or autonomic blunting in diabetes remain hypothesis-generating and require dedicated physiological studies [12–26].

Associations for age and diabetic nephropathy reached borderline statistical significance with modest effect sizes. These should be viewed strictly as exploratory signals requiring validation in prospective, multicenter cohorts. Secondary outcomes occurred too infrequently to support inferential conclusions and are reported solely for descriptive completeness. Ultimately, the value of nursing documentation lies not in its precision for risk modeling, but in its capacity to capture the lived clinical trajectory of outpatients—where risk is negotiated session by session, not predetermined at baseline.

Limitations

First, critical dialysis prescription variables were unavailable, leaving residual confounding unavoidable. The model cannot disentangle patient-level characteristics from treatment-level variability. Second, patient-level aggregation sacrifices temporal resolution. Third, outcome classification is inherently observer-dependent. Fourth, borderline associations carry substantial uncertainty and require prospective confirmation. Finally, low-frequency secondary outcomes limit statistical power.

Future directions

Future work should prioritize multicenter prospective designs that integrate wearable hemodynamic monitors, standardized nursing event coding, and session-level prescription data. Randomized evaluations of nurse-led, real-time ultrafiltration adjustment protocols are needed. Expanding ultrasound-guided vascular access training and exploring prognostic biomarkers may further refine outpatient safety frameworks. Any predictive model must acknowledge that dialysis risk is dynamic, emerging from the continuous negotiation between patient physiology, treatment parameters, and frontline clinical vigilance [27, 28].

Conclusion

Baseline characteristics demonstrate context-dependent associations with complication patterns captured in routine outpatient hemodialysis nursing documentation. Given the absence of dialysis prescription variables, the aggregation of session-level data, and the inherent subjectivity of clinical documentation, these observations are hypothesis-generating rather than predictive. Future prospective studies integrating real-time hemodynamic monitoring, standardized nursing assessments, and treatment-level variables are needed to validate these patterns and translate them into actionable, nurse-led observation protocols.
