Real-world effectiveness of pembrolizumab for first-line treatment of metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer: a decade of evidence and experience
Authors: Riddhi A. Babel, Stephen V. Liu, Tao Fan, Mayur M. Amonkar, Vamsidhar Velcheti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains a global burden, particularly due to late‑stage diagnosis. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) for mNSCLC, including first-line (1L) pembrolizumab regimens, significantly improved survival for these patients, establishing them as the current standard of care (SoC). Since the approvals of pembrolizumab, a large volume of real-world evidence (RWE) has been published across patient populations and with long-term follow-ups. This narrative review evaluates the available RWE on PubMed and Embase, assessing real-world clinical outcomes from 2016 to 2026 against pembrolizumab (KEYNOTE) clinical trials. Across geographies and practice settings, RWE for 1L pembrolizumab in mNSCLC is supportive of KEYNOTE findings, with survival outcomes comparable to the trials. Among heterogeneous populations, older age did not alter outcomes; however, cohorts with higher performance scores expectedly experienced poorer outcomes. Global treatment patterns highlighted pembrolizumab-containing regimens as the most administered ICI, affirming its role as SoC in 1L mNSCLC treatment. The volume of RWE studies, long-term follow-up data, and consistency in results observed across regions and diverse patient populations reinforce the use of pembrolizumab regimens as 1L SoC for patients with mNSCLC. Further analysis on the impact of heterogeneous subpopulations on the efficacy of pembrolizumab would be beneficial to support evidence-based decision-making.

Disease background

Disease overview and burden

Lung cancer is one of the most common cancers and the leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally, with approximately 2.5 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths in 2022 [1]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) comprises approximately 87% of lung cancer diagnoses, of which 40–50% are metastatic due to its high metastatic potency and nonspecific symptomology in early stages [2–5]. The global incidence of metastatic NSCLC (mNSCLC) varies by sex, race, and region. The age‑standardized incidence rate is estimated to be two times higher in men compared to women (32.1 versus 16.2 cases per 100,000), and cases are approximately 30% higher among Black men than White men [1,6–8]. Age at diagnosis does not differ by sex, and the median age of diagnosis is 70 years [7]. Geographically, significant differences are observed, with NSCLC incidence as high as 39.4 per 100,000 in Eastern Asia versus less than 4.0 per 10,000 in certain African areas, which may be a result of regional variations in diagnostics or reporting [1,9–12].

Despite a decline in incidence in recent years [6], NSCLC remains a global burden, with poor survival rates at later stages and considerable impact on the quality of life (QoL) of both patients and caregivers [6,13]. mNSCLC also carries a substantial economic burden, with inpatient, outpatient, and pharmacy services contributing to considerably high healthcare resource use and significantly impacting the work productivity of patients [14–16]. Poorer outcomes such as lower rates of progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) are associated with factors such as age, sex, comorbidities, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG-PS), and genomic alterations and biomarkers [17,18]. Genomic alterations and biomarkers such as PD-L1 expression, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) alterations, and Kirsten rat sarcoma virus (KRAS) mutations also alter disease prognosis and treatment pathways [19–21].

Evolving 1L treatment landscape in mNSCLC

The management of mNSCLC represents a prototype malignancy for precision oncology as treatment is biomarker-driven, guided by PD-L1 status and oncogenic driver mutations such as EGFR, anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) and ROS1 [22,23]. Treatment options include surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy [24–26]. The evolution of targeted therapy and biomarker-informed therapies has broadened the mNSCLC treatment landscape, with over 15 Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved targeted biomarker therapies improving outcomes for patients with mNSCLC [27–33]. For patients with metastatic disease, the choice of first-line (1L) systemic therapy influences subsequent treatment lines, early disease control, and OS and QoL. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed 1L treatment for mNSCLC, shifting practice from universal platinum-based chemotherapy to biomarker-driven and combination immunotherapy strategies. In patients with mNSCLC who lack actionable genomic alterations, ICIs—particularly PD-1 inhibitors—have significantly improved survival and are the standard of care (SoC) [17,24,26,34–37].

The first major shift came with pembrolizumab, a PD-1 inhibitor, for the treatment of mNSCLC with high PD-L1 expression (tumor proportion score [TPS] ≥50%) and no EGFR or ALK alterations [29]. The FDA-approved 1L pembrolizumab monotherapy in October 2016 based on the KEYNOTE-024 trial. KEYNOTE-042 further demonstrated significant improvements in survival outcomes with PD-L1 ≥1%, with the greatest benefit in the PD-L1 ≥50% cohort, reinforcing high PD-L1 expression as the optimal setting for ICI monotherapy [28]. Pembrolizumab monotherapy also received European Medicines Agency (EMA) approval for previously treated advanced or metastatic NSCLC in June 2016, and for untreated mNSCLC in December 2016, based on KEYNOTE-024 [29,38,39]. To extend benefit to patients with lower or absent PD-L1 expression, chemo-immunotherapy combinations were developed. Pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy was later approved by the EMA and FDA for 1L treatment of nonsquamous (EMA: July 2018, FDA: August 2018) and squamous mNSCLC (EMA: January 2019, FDA: October 2018) based on the KEYNOTE-189 and KEYNOTE-407 trials, respectively [33,39–42].

The NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology (NCCN Guidelines®) recommend ICI-based therapies as preferred 1L treatment options for patients with advanced or mNSCLC who lack actionable genomic alterations. Recommendations are either for single‑agent PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors or combined with chemotherapy, with the choice of treatment option determined by PD-L1 expression and clinical characteristics (category 1 level evidence). Pembrolizumab-containing regimens are included among the preferred National Comprehensive Cancer Network® (NCCN®) treatment options alongside atezolizumab- and cemiplimab-based regimens [26]. Alternative ICI treatment options (durvalumab, ipilimumab, and nivolumab) alone or in combination are listed as “Other Recommended” [17,26,34–37,43]. The European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines similarly recommend platinum-based chemotherapy plus PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors such as pembrolizumab for 1L treatment of advanced/mNSCLC in patients with ECOG-PS 0–1, regardless of PD-L1 TPS status. ICI pembrolizumab monotherapy is recommended by ESMO for patients with PD-L1 TPS ≥50% and ECOG-PS 0–1 alongside other ICIs such as atezolizumab and cemiplimab [24].

Importance of real-world evidence in oncology

Real-world evidence (RWE) is increasingly important in oncology drug development and lifecycle management, prior to and following regulatory approval [44,45]. During the pre-approval phase, RWE can quantify disease burden, unmet need, current treatment patterns, and outcomes in clinical practice, which support indication selection, justify accelerated or orphan pathways, and evidence the clinical and economic value of new therapies [44–46]. Following regulatory approval, the value of RWE broadens to evaluate clinical trial generalizability and provide insights into treatment patterns, resource use, and long-term outcomes [45]. While randomized controlled trials (RCT) are the gold standard for assessing effectiveness and safety, they are conducted under idealized conditions and may not reflect real-world populations and settings [47]. RWE can broaden RCT generalizability across trial-eligible and underrepresented patient populations, including the elderly, those with poor PS, comorbidities, and more diverse racial and socioeconomic groups, thereby increasing clinician confidence [48,49]. Additionally, frequent, regular imaging is performed during clinical trials to monitor progression, this is less frequent in RW studies due to standard practice, therefore RWE may provide median survival rates more representative of true patient outcomes. Overall, RWE informs guidelines, health-technology assessments, reimbursement and coverage decisions, and post‑approval safety monitoring, supporting treatment selection for mNSCLC across diverse patient groups [45].

Since the first FDA approval of ICIs for the 1L treatment of mNSCLC 10 years ago [38], pembrolizumab (monotherapy or combination therapy) has become a global SoC for mNSCLC and is reimbursed extensively [43,50]. Numerous RWE studies have since been published, including broader patient populations and with long-term efficacy and safety follow-up periods [35,51–54]. Given the large volume of global RWE for 1L pembrolizumab in mNSCLC, this narrative review aims to summarize the studies of multiple large cohorts from 2016 to 2026, across regions and practice settings. Additionally, this study aims to discuss pembrolizumab’s effectiveness in clinical practice, comparing observed outcomes to clinical trial evidence. Uniquely, this review aims to comprehensively collate heterogeneous RWE across a range of patient populations and regions, including more diverse patient populations underrepresented in clinical trials. By synthesizing this RWE alongside pivotal trial data, this review aims to contribute to the understanding of pembrolizumab-based regimens as a 1L treatment strategy in routine practice, offering insights into regimen selection across diverse patient populations.

Methods for narrative review

A narrative review of the current RWE pertaining to 1L treatment of mNSCLC was conducted, emphasizing the use of ICIs, primarily the PD-1 inhibitor pembrolizumab, and the corresponding RW clinical outcomes, comparing RWE to KEYNOTE clinical trials. Findings are presented as qualitative narrative review as methodological differences between RWE and clinical data prevented formal statistical comparison. Searches were conducted in PubMed and Embase in July 2025 for original research papers, systematic literature reviews, or conference abstracts published from 2016 to July 2025 to capture RWE publications since pembrolizumab’s 1L approval in mNSCLC. Keywords searched included “pembrolizumab,” “non-small cell lung cancer,” “first-line,” “long term outcome,” “overall survival” and “real world” (Table S1). Supplementary hand searches were performed using these search terms until February 2026 to ensure relevant articles were included. As this is a narrative, study selection was based on clinical relevance, and scientific significance as opposed to a systematic literature approach. RWE studies were included in this review if the study population consisted of adults, with any histology or PD-L1 expression, and were treated with pembrolizumab monotherapy or combination therapy; however, anti-PD-1/PD-L1 class studies were included for reporting of treatment patterns. Inclusion of RWE studies was closely aligned with criteria similar to that of the KEYNOTE clinical trials referenced throughout this review (KEYNOTE 024, 042, 189, and 407) [28–30,33,40]. Limited exclusion criteria were applied, including studies that were biomarker-specific, healthcare resource use-related, or focused on clinicopathologic risk factors. Subgroup data were included within the review when reported by papers; however, these were not explicitly searched for.

Real-world evidence for pembrolizumab in 1L mNSCLC

Since its approval, a substantial volume of RWE has been published for the use of 1L pembrolizumab in mNSCLC, both as monotherapy and in combination with chemotherapy, showing overall longer survival compared with historical real-world data utilizing other therapies [55,56]. In total, 44 US-based studies and 117 non-US-based studies have been published across pembrolizumab monotherapy and combination therapy, including countries such as Canada, France, Italy, Norway, Poland, Argentina, India, China, and Japan (Figure 1) [57–66]. Studies varied in design from retrospective cohort or database studies [35,51,60,67–70], with few ambispective studies, and data collection commenced as early as October 2016, generating a wealth of RWE for 1L pembrolizumab in mNSCLC [37,71–73]. The PFS results in these studies appeared to be comparable to those seen in the KEYNOTE-024 trial, where median PFS was 7.7 months, suggesting PFS may be comparable across RCT and RWE [29].

ToT was reported as 7.6 months by Gadgeel et al. (2024), within a cohort with ECOG-PS similar to the KEYNOTE trials (n = 309) [83]. Velcheti et al. (2019) and (2022) reported cohorts of patients with similar ECOG-PS (0–1) as in the KEYNOTE trials (n = 386 and 807) and identified ToT as 6.9 and 7.4 months [81,84]. A similar median ToT of 6.7 (n = 804) and 7.0 (n = 505) months was reported by Velcheti et al. (2024) and Gadgeel et al. (2024) [35,83]. ToT reported across these studies is comparable to treatment duration in the KEYNOTE-024 trial (7.9 months), suggesting efficacy of pembrolizumab in RCT and RWE situations may be comparable [29].

Outcomes based on histology

No US-based RWE studies reported OS and PFS by histology. However, Velcheti et al. (2022) reported ToT for patients with nonsquamous mNSCLC (n = 544) to be 7.6 months [81]. These results appeared to be consistent with the analogous endpoint in KEYNOTE-024 (median pembrolizumab duration, 7.9 months) [29].

Outcomes based on levels of PD-L1 TPS expression

Most US-based studies reported PD-L1 TPS of ≥50% for 1L pembrolizumab monotherapy. However, few studies reported outcomes for cohorts of patients with increased PD-L1 TPS (≥90%). OS for patients with PD-L1 TPS ≥90% treated with 1L pembrolizumab was reported by Ricciuti et al. (2024) and (2021), where, across a 3-year follow-up, median OS of 30.4 and 27.0 months were observed (n = 197; n = 69), respectively [77,79]. In these exploratory analyses, statistically significant increases compared with patients with a PD-L1 TPS 50–89% were reported (p < 0.01 and p = 0.03, respectively), though this small sample size reduces statistical power (Table 1) [77,79].

Similarly, Aguilar et al. (2018) reported OS for patients with PD-L1 TPS ≥90% (n = 73) as 33.6 months [76]. The OS reported across these RWE studies demonstrates a long-term survival benefit at a 3-year follow-up in patients with advanced NSCLC and a PD-L1 TPS ≥90%. The survival outcomes exceed the 20.0–26.3 month median OS reported for the KEYNOTE-024 and −042 trials for patients with PD-L1 TPS ≥50%, suggesting pembrolizumab may be efficacious in patients with PD-L1 TPS ≥90% [28,29]. Ricciuti et al. (2024) and (2021) also reported median PFS of 9.0 and 6.0 months for patients with PD-L1 TPS ≥90% (n = 197; n = 69) [77,79]. Identifying statistically significant PFS increases compared to the PD-L1 TPS 50–89% subgroup within these exploratory analyses (p < 0.001 and p = 0.02, respectively) [77,79]. Additionally, Aguilar er al., (2019) reported median PFS of 14.5 months for patients with PD-L1 TPS ≥90 (n = 80) [74]. The descriptive PFS results in these RWE studies exceed those seen in the KEYNOTE-024 trial for patients with PD-L1 TPS ≥50%, where median PFS was 7.7 months [29].

ToT was 6.3 months for patients with PD-L1 TPS ≥90% (n = 80) in a study by Aguilar et al. (2019) [74]. ToT reported in this study appears to be comparable to the median treatment duration in the KEYNOTE-024 trial (7.9 months) for patients with PD-L1 TPS ≥50%, suggesting ToT may be similar across patient TPS [29].

Outcomes based on length of follow-up

Many US-based RWE studies have included 2 or more years of follow-up data, ranging up to 5 years [35,81,85].

Multiple studies cover a 3-year follow-up of patients with mNSCLC treated with 1L pembrolizumab, ranging from 19.6 to 30.4 months depending on TPS [77,79,85]. Velcheti et al. (2022) reported a median OS for patients who initiated 1L pembrolizumab monotherapy of 19.6 months for patients in the advanced NSCLC electronic health record cohort (n = 566) and 21.1 months in the randomly selected spotlight cohort (n = 228) after 3 years [85]. Median OS after 3-year follow-up was also reported by Ricciuti et al. as 18.6–30.4 months depending on PD-L1 TPS expression (n = 516), and as 20.0 months across all PD-L1 TPS expressions (n = 150) by Ricciuti et al. (2021) [77,79]. Five-year real-world survival outcomes were presented by Velcheti et al. (2024) for advanced/mNSCLC treated with 1L pembrolizumab monotherapy and reported median OS of 19.2 months for the whole cohort (n = 804) and 17.5 months for a cohort of adults aged ≥75 years old (n = 310) [35]. The descriptive OS data from these 3‑ and 5-year follow-up studies appear to be consistent with the long-term median OS data observed in KEYNOTE-024 and −042 trials, where OS was 26.3 and 20.0 months, respectively, for patients ≥50% with PD-L1 TPS [86,87].

Three-year follow-up was also frequently reported for PFS, ranging from 4.8 to 9.0 months across studies depending on patient TPS [77,79,85]. Velcheti et al. (2022) reported a median PFS of 7.3 months in the randomly selected spotlight cohort of patients treated with 1L pembrolizumab (n = 228) [85]. A 3-year follow-up was also reported by Ricciuti et al. (2024), with PFS of 5.4–9.0 months depending of PD-L1 TPS expression (n = 516), and PSF of 4.8 months (n = 150) by Ricciuti et al. [77,79]. The descriptive PFS results in these RWE studies are similar to each other and appear to be comparable to PFS in the KEYNOTE-024 trial, where median PFS was reported as 7.7 months [29].

Three-year follow-up for ToT was reported by Velcheti et al. as 2.1–7.4 months depending on ECOG-PS score (n = 1,044) [81]. In a long-term 5-year study by Velcheti et al. (2024), ToT was 6.7 months (n = 804) [35]. ToT data in these studies are similar across follow-ups and appear to be comparable to treatment durations in the KEYNOTE-024 trial (7.9 months), although they may be slightly reduced in patients with poorer ECOG-PS [29].

Subpopulation analyses

Older patients are vastly underrepresented in clinical trials that set the standards for the efficacy and safety of cancer treatments, with patients enrolled in clinical trials tending to be younger, and thus healthier [88]. Velcheti et al. (2024) therefore explored long-term survival outcomes in the subgroup of patients aged ≥75 years. Across 310 patients who were ≥75 years, median OS was 17.5 months, and the survival rates at one and 3 years were 58.7% and 28.7%, respectively. These results appear to be consistent with the 12‑month OS rate reported for patients ≥75 years old in KEYNOTE-24 and KEYNOTE-042 (n = 49, 12-month OS: 60.7%) [28,29,35].

Velcheti et al. (2022) evaluated ToT with 1L pembrolizumab monotherapy over an expanded patient population treated at US oncology clinics, including those with an ECOG-PS of 2, who were ineligible to participate in KEYNOTE-024 and −042. In this subpopulation (n = 237), ToT was 2.1 months, compared with 7.4 months for patients with ECOG-PS 0–1 (n = 807), and 9.9% of patients remained on pembrolizumab at 24 months [81].

Treatment patterns

US RW treatment patterns for anti-PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy have been reported for patients with mNSCLC by histology, with Velcheti et al. (2019) reporting the proportion of squamous mNSCLC patients treated with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy as 10% in 2015, 17% in 2016, 28% in 2017, and 27% in 2018 [89]. A similar trend was observed in patients with nonsquamous mNSCLC with the proportion of patients treated with 1L anti-PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy increasing from 3% in 2015 to 12% in 2016, 29% in 2017, and then decreasing slightly to 23% in 2018, highlighting the increasing use of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 treatments [90]. Additionally, among patients prescribed 1L therapy, the percentages who received therapy were lowest among those who received anti-PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy (28–38% compared with 50–62% for chemotherapy and 56–62% for chemotherapy with vascular endothelial growth factor inhibitor [VEGF]) [90]. A large proportion (25%) of those initiating 1L anti-PD-L1 in 2017 were still on therapy at cutoff [90].

In an SLR by Machado et al. (2026) including 17,506 patients across 12 studies, mean OS was identified to be 21.0 months [56]. Where study populations were similar to those in the KEYNOTE trials, Descourt et al. (2023) and Decroisette et al. (2023) reported OS of 22.6 and 22.0 months, respectively (both n = 845) [60,97]. Brant et al. (2025) also identified a median OS of 21.0 months (n = 242) [98]. In Piedra et al. (2024) and Cortellini et al. (2025), cohorts with matched baseline characteristics (n = 329 and n = 709, respectively) to KEYNOTE-024 were reported to have a median OS of 22.3 and 27.5 months, respectively [91,99]. The OS reported across these RWE studies appears to be consistent with the 20.0–26.3 months reported in the KEYNOTE-024 and −042 trials, suggesting similar efficacy in a RW cohort [28,29].

Mean PFS was reported to be 8.7 months (n = 3,210) in the SLR by Machado et al. (2026) [56]. Descourt et al. (2023) reported PFS of 8.2 months (n = 845) [60]. Additionally, in a cohort of patients (n = 86) with similar ECOG-PS as the KEYNOTE trials by Pérol et al. (2021), PFS was 7.7 months [100]. Goto et al. (2022), Friedlander et al. (2020) and Piedra et al. (2024), also reported cohorts of patients with ECOG-PS (n = 240; n = 246; n = 329, respectively) in line with KEYNOTE trials and identified PFS of 10.0, 11.3, and 12.5 months, respectively [99,101,102]. Descriptive PFS in these studies appears to exceed those seen in the KEYNOTE-024 trial, where median PFS was 7.7 months, although cross-study differences in populations and methods limit direct comparison [29].

Median ToT was reported by Goto et al. (2022) as 5.6 months across patients [101]. Descourt et al. (2023) similarly reported ToT of 8.0 months (n = 845) [60]. The largest ToT was reported by Cvijić et al. (2023) and ranged from 10.1 to 10.3 months (n = 78) [103]. ToT reported across these studies appears to be comparable to treatment duration in the KEYNOTE-024 trial (7.9 months), suggesting the efficacy of pembrolizumab in RCT and RWE situations may be comparable [29].

Outcomes based on histology

Limited non-US monotherapy RWE studies reported outcomes by histology, with none reporting on pembrolizumab specifically. Two Swedish studies by Wagenius et al. (2024) and (2025) reported OS for patients with squamous and nonsquamous mNSCLC treated with PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy [104,105]. Wagenius et al. (2024) reported that the median OS for patients with nonsquamous mNSCLC treated with any 1L systemic treatment, including platinum-based and PD-1/PD-L1-based therapy, and was higher (18.6 months) than for patients with squamous mNSCLC (13.3 months) [104]. Wagenius et al. (2025) identified that patients treated with PD-(L)1 inhibitor regimens with nonsquamous mNSCLC had median OS of 19.8 and 5.0 months for ECOG-PS groups 0–1 and 2, respectively, and that patients with squamous mNSCLC had a median OS of 15.0 and 8.9 months for ECOG-PS groups 0–1 and 2, respectively [105].

Outcomes based on levels of PD-L1 TPS expression

Similarly to US-based studies, PD-L1 expression was most commonly ≥50% in global monotherapy RWE. Brant et al. (2025) was the only global publication in monotherapy to report survival outcomes in a wider PD-L1 TPS cohort, reporting patients with any PD-L1 TPS status (n = 242) [98]. This Norwegian study reported a median OS of 21.0 months, suggesting that a broader cohort may not result in reduced pembrolizumab efficacy when observation data are compared with the median OS of 20.0–26.3 months seen in the KEYNOTE-024 and −042 trials, although differences in methodology limit comparison [28,29,98]. Both PFS and ToT outcomes were not reported by PD-L1 TPS in RWE.

Outcomes based on length of follow-up

Follow-up time reported ranged across reports. Multiple studies report survival outcomes with a long follow-up, including a UK-based study (n = 82) with 3-year follow-up reported by Ansel et al. (2023) where a median OS of 13.6 was reported [106]. Additionally, some studies have substantially longer follow-up periods for 1 L pembrolizumab treatment: Tambo et al. (2025) reported a median OS of 19.1 months over a follow-up of 6 years (n = 95), further demonstrating the long-term data associated with pembrolizumab use [107]. The OS results in these RWE studies still appear to be comparable to those seen in the KEYNOTE-024 and −042 trials, where median OS was 20.0–26.3 [28,29,86,87].

For PFS, Ansel et al. (2023) reported a 3-year follow-up with a median PFS of 6.9 months (n = 82) [106]. Six-year follow-up was reported by Tambo et al. (2025), identifying a median PFS of 6.9 months (n = 95), further demonstrating the long-term data associated with pembrolizumab use [107]. The PFS results in these RWE studies appear to be comparable to those seen in the KEYNOTE-024 trial, where median PFS was 7.7 months [29].

The longest follow-up reported for ToT was just over 2 years, in a German multicenter study by Frost et al. (2021) where median ToT was 8.2 months (n = 153) [108]. This is higher than the 5.0 and 5.7 months reported in an Irish study by Chu et al. (2022) and by Goto et al. (2022), each with 1-year follow-up (n = 20) [101,109].

Subpopulation analyses

For the subpopulation of patients aged ≥75 years with mNSCLC and treated with 1L pembrolizumab, OS was reported by Goto et al. (2022) in a Japanese medical record review study [101]. A median OS for adults aged ≥75 years (n = 138) of 23.5 months was reported compared with an OS not reached for patients aged <75 years (n = 303) [101].

Goto et al. (2022) also reported outcomes by ECOG-PS, including an ECOG-PS of 2, which is a subpopulation deemed ineligible for KEYNOTE-024 and −042 trials. In this expanded cohort, median OS was reported as 11.8 months for patients with ECOG-PS 2 (n = 36) and was not reached for patients with an ECOG-PS 0–1 (n = 240) [101]. In a European multicenter study by Friedlaender et al. (2020), median OS was 7.8 months for patients with ECOG-PS 2 (n = 56) and was not reached for patients with ECOG-PS 0–1 (n = 246) [102]. Additionally, a global retrospective study by Cortellini et al. (2025) reported a5-year survival rate of 12.7% in patients with ECOG-PS ≥2 (n = 161) [91]. While OS for older adults reported by Goto et al. (2022) appears to be consistent with the KEYNOTE-024 and −042 trials, where OS was 26.3 and 20.0 months, respectively, OS was reduced in patients with ECOG-PS ≥2 [28,29].

For adults aged ≥75 years treated with 1L pembrolizumab (n = 138), PFS was also reported by Goto et al. (2022) as 9.5 months, compared with 10.1 months for adults <75 years (n = 303) [101]. Stratified by ECOG-PS, Goto et al. (2022) reported PFS of 3.7 months for patients with ECOG-PS 2 (n = 36) and 10.0 months for patients with PS 0–1 (n = 240) [101]. Friedlaender et al. (2020) also reported a median PFS for patients with ECOG-PS 2 of 2.6 months (n = 56), compared with 11.3 months for patients with PS 0–1 (n = 246) [102]. The PFS results in these RWE studies appear to show comparable PFS to the KEYNOTE-024 trial (7.7 months) across age, but lower PFS for patients with an increased ECOG-PS of 2 [29].

ToT was also reported by Goto et al. (2020). Across patients aged ≥75 years (n = 138), median ToT was 5.7 months, compared with 5.6 months for patients aged <75 years (n = 303) [101]. When categorized by ECOG-PS, patients with ECOG-PS 2 (n = 36) reported median ToT of 1.9 months, and patients with PS 0–1 reported ToT of 5.7 months [101]. While the ToT across ages did not significantly differ, patients with ECOG-PS 2 appear to demonstrate reduced descriptive ToT compared with the KEYNOTE-024 trial (7.9 months) [29].

Treatment patterns

Treatment patterns were reported less frequently than survival outcomes across RWE outside of the US. In the DARWIN study (2025), which characterized the treatment patterns for those with advanced/metastatic NSCLC who initiate 1L treatment with selected immunotherapies (pembrolizumab, atezolizumab, cemiplimab, nivolumab, durvalumab, ipilimumab) across France, Spain, and the Netherlands, pembrolizumab was identified as the most frequently prescribed ICI therapy as of February 2025 [110]. This is supported by an earlier study by Griesinger et al. (2022) which reported the uptake of pembrolizumab by year and histological type using French and German databases. Griesinger et al. (2022) reported that the uptake of pembrolizumab was quicker in Germany than France across histological type, with 16.0–21.6% of patients treated with pembrolizumab in Germany in 2017 compared with 2.9–3.1% in France in the same year [72]. The proportion of patients treated with pembrolizumab considerably increased in both countries, across histology, in 2018 (22.5–24.6% in Germany; 16.3–18.1% in France) [72].

Outcomes based on histology

Multiple US-based RWE that have evaluated pembrolizumab in combination with chemotherapy according to histology [116,117]. Across RWE, longer OS for patients with nonsquamous mNSCLC was reported compared with patients with squamous mNSCLC [116,117].

Velcheti et al. (2021) reported a median OS of 16.5 months among patients (n = 283) with nonsquamous mNSCLC [116]. Liu et al. (2022) similarly reported a median OS of 17.2 months in a retrospective database analysis (n = 337) [114]. In a longer, 5-year follow-up by Liu et al. (2025) a median OS of 15.0 months for patients with nonsquamous (n = 1,960) and 12.9 months with squamous mNSCLC (n = 433) was reported [51]. In patients with squamous mNSCLC, a retrospective observational study by Liu et al. (2023) reported OS of 15.3 months (n = 364) [117]. Results from these RWE studies appear to be consistent the OS reported for patients with nonsquamous mNSCLC in the KEYNOTE-189 trial (22.0 months) [30,40]. For patients with squamous mNSCLC, median OS in the KEYNOTE-407 trial (15.9 months) highlight the similarity between the trial outcomes and RWE outcomes, although methodologies differ, which limits comparison [33].

Similarly to OS, longer PFS for patients with nonsquamous mNSCLC compared with patients with squamous mNSCLC has also been reported [116,117]. Velcheti et al. (2021) reported a median PFS of 6.4 months among patients with nonsquamous mNSCLC (n = 283) [116]. Liu et al. (2022) similarly reported a median PFS of 6.2 months in a retrospective database analysis (n = 337) [114]. Results from these RWE studies appear to be consistent with reported PFS for patients with nonsquamous mNSCLC in the KEYNOTE-189 trial (9.0 months) [30,40]. For patients with squamous mNSCLC, median PFS in the KEYNOTE-407 trial (6.4 months) highlight the apparent similarity between the trial outcomes and RWE outcomes [33].

Velcheti et al. (2021) reported a median ToT of 5.6 months among patients with nonsquamous mNSCLC (n = 283) [116]. In patients with squamous mNSCLC, a retrospective observational study by Liu et al. (2023) reported a median ToT of 6.5 months (n = 364) [117].

Outcomes based on levels of PD-L1 TPS expression

Patient populations across US-based RWE for 1 L pembrolizumab in combination with chemotherapy included patients with a wide range of PD-L1 TPS.

Median OS demonstrated trends by TPS score in a study by Velcheti et al. (2021) where median OS was 20.6 months for patients with PD-L1 ≥50% (n = 79), 16.3 months for patients with PD-L1 1%–49% (n = 77) and 13.2 months for patients with PD-L1 <1% (n = 79) [116]. Liu et al. (2022) demonstrated similar results, reporting median OS as 20.6 months for patients with PD-L1 ≥50% (n = 105), 16.9 months for patients with PD-L1 1%–49% (n = 104) and 13.2 months for patients with PD-L1 <1% (n = 103) [114]. Liu et al. (2025) reported OS as 12.0 months for patients with PD-L1 <1% (n = 587) and as 17.7 months for PD-L1 ≥1% (n = 1,059) [51]. For squamous mNSCLC, Liu et al. (2023) and (2025) reported median OS of 17.2 and 12.9 months for patients with PD-L1 <1% (n = 94 and 120) and 16.2 and 14.9 months for patients with PD-L1 ≥1% (n = 172 and 197) [51,117]. These RW findings appear to be comparable to the KEYNOTE-189 and −407 trials that included patients with any TPS (189, 22.0 months; 407, 15.9 months) [30,33,40].

Median PFS also demonstrated trends by TPS score in Velcheti et al. (2021) [116]. Median PFS was 8.8 months for patients with PD-L1 ≥50%, 5.9 months for patients with PD-L1 1%–49% and 5.0 months for patients with PD-L1 <1% [116]. Liu et al. (2022) demonstrated similar results, reporting median PFS as 9.3 months for patients with PD-L1 ≥50% (n = 105), 5.7 months for patients with PD-L1 1%–49% (n = 104) and 5.0 months for patients with PD-L1 <1% (n = 103) [114]. These RW findings appear to be comparable to the KEYNOTE-189 and −407 trials that included patients with any PD-L1 TPS (189, 9.0 months; 407, 6.4 months) [30,33,40].

A similar trend was also identified for ToT in Liu et al. (2021), with patients that have PD-L1 ≥50% undergoing 10.0 months on treatment compared to 6.2 and 4.9 months reported by patients with PD-L1 1%-49% and ≤1% respectively [118]. Liu et al. (2023) also reported that median ToT was 6.9 months for patients with PD-L1 ≥1% (n = 172) and 5.8 months for patients with PD-L1 <1% (n = 94) [117]. Liu et al. (2025) further split results by histology and PD-L1 TPS, reporting ToT of 4.7 and 5.8 months for nonsquamous (n = 587) and squamous patients (n = 120) with PD-L1 <1, and ToT of 6.4 and 6.0 months for nonsquamous (n = 1,059) and squamous patients (n = 197) with PD-L1 ≥1% [51].

Outcomes based on length of follow-up

Several long-term follow-ups have been conducted for US-based pembrolizumab combination therapy. Liu et al. (2025) reported a 5-year follow-up of 1,960 patients with nonsquamous mNSCLC and 433 patients with squamous mNSCLC, where median OS was 15.0 and 12.9 months, respectively. Although lower than in the KEYNOTE-189 and −407 trials (22.0 and 17.2 months), the KEYNOTE trials had a shorter follow-up period [51]. However, the 5 ‑year OS rate across this study, 21.6% and 18.3% for nonsquamous and squamous patients, respectively, appeared to be comparable to, or higher than seen in the 5-year update of the KEYNOTE-189 (19.4%) and 407 (18.4%) clinical trials [31,32,51].

Median PFS was not reported across any studies with long-term follow-up.

Liu et al. (2025) also reported that the five-year follow-up for ToT in patients with nonsquamous (n = 1,960) and squamous (n = 433) mNSCLC was 5.6 and 5.8 months, respectively, and was consistent with RWE with two-year follow-ups by Liu et al, of 5.8 and 6.5 months (n = 377 and 364) [51,114,117].

Subpopulation analyses

Survival outcomes for pembrolizumab combination therapy have been evaluated in subpopulations of patients who are older than the patients typically included in clinical trials. Velchetti et al., (2020) reported OS for patients with nonsquamous mNSCLC aged ≥75 years and ECOG-PS of 0–2 and any PD-L1 expression (n = 99), identifying a median OS of 15.5 months [54]. This study also reported outcomes for patients aged ≥75 years by PD-L1 TPS, identifying median OS of 20.6 months in patients with PD-L1 ≥50 (n = 30), 18.8 months in patients with PD-L1 1%–49% (n = 26) and 8.9 months in patients with PD-L1 <1% (n = 29) [54].

Median PFS in Velchetti et al., (2020) for patients aged ≥75 years was 7.0 months [54]. For patients aged ≥75 years and according to PD-L1 TPS expression, median PFS was 10.9 months in patients with PD-L1 ≥50, 9.7 months in patients with PD-L1 1%–49% and 4.1 months in patients with PD-L1 <1% [54].

ToT was for patients aged ≥75 years as 4.9 months in Velchetti et al., (2020) [54].

Treatment patterns

US treatment patterns for pembrolizumab in combination therapy were reported less frequently than outcomes and were reported across PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor combinations. Velcheti et al. (2019) reported various therapy combinations and corresponding treatment patterns for patients with nonsquamous mNSCLC and identified 15% of patients in 2017 and 31% of patients in 2018 were treated with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor combinations, demonstrating their increased use in clinical practice [90]. Similarly, the proportion of patients with squamous mNSCLC treated with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor combinations was 1% in 2017 and 3% in 2018 [119]. In 2021–2022, a retrospective cohort study by Liu et al. (2023) of 2,754 patients with mNSCLC (1,769 nonsquamous and 985 squamous) highlighted that regardless of histology or PD-L1 status, most patients received a PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor-containing regimen, consistent with recommendations, among which the majority received pembrolizumab as monotherapy or with platinum-based chemotherapy (40.8% nonsquamous and 33.1% squamous received pembrolizumab with platinum-based chemotherapy) [43].

Outcomes based on histology

Polish RWE multicenter retrospective analysis of 206 patients with nonsquamous mNSCLC and 133 squamous patients, reported median OS of 17.3 and 16.5 months, respectively [66]. In the Netherlands, a large retrospective registry study by Verschueren et al. (2024) reported median OS by histology and PD-L1 expression (n = 663) [128]. In patients with nonsquamous mNSCLC, median OS for TPS <1% (n = 269) was 10.0 months, 19.9 months for TPS 1–49% (n = 158) and 26.2 months for TPS ≥50% (n = 59) [128]. In patients with squamous mNSCLC, median OS was 10.0 months for TPS <1% (n = 70) and 25.6 for TPS ≥1% (n = 47) [128]. Across these cohorts, this study identified no significant difference in the efficacy of pembrolizumab in combination with chemotherapy when comparing their findings to the KEYNOTE-189 and −407 trials, except for the cohort of nonsquamous mNSCLC with PD-L1 <1% where survival outcomes were shorter [30,33,40,128]. Likely due to the differences in the patient and treatment characteristics between the RW and trial cohorts, where patients with an ECOG-PS ≥2 were included in this study by Verschueren et al, a significant predictor of poorer survival outcomes [128].

Median PFS according to histology was reported in a retrospective Polish study by Knetki-Wróblewska et al. (2024), where median PFS was 13.0 and 12.1 months for patients with nonsquamous (n = 206) and squamous (n = 133) mNSCLC, respectively [66]. Across histology and PD-L1 TPS expression, median PFS for patients with nonsquamous mNSCLC was 6.0 months for PD-L1 <1% (269), 9.1 months for PD-L1 1–49% (n = 158) and 22.2 months for PD-L1 ≥50% (n = 59) [128]. In patients with squamous mNSCLC, median PFS was 7.1 months for PD-L1 <1% (n = 70) and 7.5 months for PD-L1 ≥1% (n = 47) [128]. These results appear to be comparable, or exceed, the median PFS reported in KEYNOTE-189 trial for nonsquamous mNSCLC (9.0 months) and KEYNOTE-407 trial for squamous mNSCLC (6.4 months) [30,33,40].

Median ToT was not reported by histology in RWE.

Outcomes based on levels of PD-L1 TPS expression

Knetki-Wróblewska et al. (2024) reported median OS of 16.7 months for patients with PD-L1 TPS <1% (n = 173) and 16.5 months for patients with TPS 1–49% (n = 166) [66]. Similarly, a multicenter Italian observational cohort study by Leonetti et al. (2024) of 1,068 patients reported a median OS of 12.2 months in patients with PD-L1 TPS ≥50% (n = 38), 16.6 months in TPS 1–49% (n = 543), and 15.5 in TPS <1% (n = 425) [73], similar to that of KEYNOTE-407 (15.9 months), which included patients across PD-L1 TPS [30,33,40].

Median PFS by PD-L1 TPS was reported by Knetki-Wróblewska et al. (2024), with median PFS of 12.0 months for patients with PD-L1 TPS <1% (n = 173) and 13.0 months for patients with TPS 1–49% (n = 166) [66]. Additionally, an Italian retrospective, multicenter study by Cafaro et al. (2024) reported median PFS of 5.8 months for patients with PD-L1 TPS <1% (n = 123) and 9.9 months for patients with TPS 1–49% (n = 156) [61]. These results appear to be similar to the KEYNOTE-189 and −407 trial, which included patients across PD-L1 TPS (189, 9.0 months; 407, 6.4 months) [30,33,40].

Median ToT by PD-L1 TPS was rarely reported, only present in one RWE study by Cafaro et al. (2024) who reported median ToT as 5.8 months (n = 279) [61].

Outcomes based on length of follow-up

Most global RWE for pembrolizumab combination therapy report follow-ups of 1 year or less. Zhang et al. (2021) report the longest follow-up of 2 years and a median OS of 24.2 months (n = 34) [69]. Follow-ups between 1 and 2 years were reported by Wasamoto et al. (2025) and Wallrabenstein et al. (2023) with median OS of 24.0 (n = 30) and 12.6 (n = 75) months, respectively [125,129].

The longest follow-up for PFS was also 2 years, 7.6 months (n = 34) reported by Zhang et al. (2021) [69]. Follow-ups between one and 2 years were reported by Cafaro et al., Wasamoto et al. (2025) and Wallrabenstein et al. (2023) with median PFS of 8.0 (n = 279), 7.5 (n = 30) and 7.0 (n = 75) months, respectively [61,125,129].

Median ToT was reported infrequently in studies with longer follow-ups. In Renaud et al. (2023) with an average follow-up of 4.5 months, ToT was 5.1 months, comparable with ToT in Cafaro et al. (2024) with the longest follow-up of 19.7 months [61,127].

Subpopulation analyses

Survival outcomes for pembrolizumab combination therapy have been evaluated in subpopulations of patients older than those typically included in clinical trials. A global retrospective cohort study by Barrichello et al. (2025) reported median OS by age, reporting that for adults aged ≥80 years (n = 111) and PD-L1 TPS ≥50%, median OS was 15.0 months compared to 24.8 months for patients aged <80 years (n = 558) [130]. OS outcomes in these age groups appear to be comparable to the median OS reported in the KEYNOTE-189 and −407 trials (15.9–22.0 months) [30,33,40].

Barrichello et al. (2025) also reported median PFS as 6.4 months for patients ≥80 years old (n = 111), compared to 7.3 months for patients aged <80 years old (n = 558) [130], which appeared to be comparable to the median PFS seen in the KEYNOTE-189 and −407 trials (6.4–9.0 months) [30,33,40].

Treatment patterns

Global treatment patterns for the use of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in combination with chemotherapy have been sparsely reported across RWE. Apter et al. (2024) described treatment utilization in a RW mNSCLC cohort in a 2.7-million-member state-mandated health provider across 2017–2020 [70]. In 2017–2018, 3.5% of mNSCLC patients with adenocarcinoma (n = 486) and 3.6% with squamous cell carcinoma (n = 125) were treated with 1L PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor and chemotherapy combination in this large Israeli healthcare study, with this proportion increasing significantly in 2019–2020 [70].

Discussion

This narrative review synthesized the real-world clinical outcomes for 1L pembrolizumab in patients with mNSCLC to assess how these data support pembrolizumab’s role, as 1L monotherapy and combination therapy for these patients in routine clinical practice. By synthesizing these RW data from a wide range of regions and patient populations alongside pivotal trial results, this review provides clinicians with evidence to support the use of pembrolizumab-based regimens as a 1L treatment strategy in routine practice and regimen selection across diverse patient populations.

RWE is necessary for understanding treatment effectiveness and outcomes in broader mNSCLC populations, as clinical trials often feature younger patients, and older, comorbid patients with poorer ECOG-PS, brain and liver metastases, and worse disease prognosis are ineligible and underrepresented [47,49]. RWE also provides insights into long-term outcomes and treatment patterns, supporting clinical and regulatory decision-making [131].

Across 160 RWE studies spanning varied patient populations and follow-up lengths, pembrolizumab has a substantial and growing body of evidence, which appears to be comparable to- and supportive of KEYNOTE clinical trial findings, reinforcing the role of pembrolizumab as the SoC for 1L mNSCLC therapy in routine practice. OS in RWE appeared to be consistent with clinical trials, and PFS often exceeded KEYNOTE trial results, though differences in study design, eligibility criteria, and the non-randomized nature of our cohort preclude formal comparative conclusions. Additionally, this may in part be a result of the impact of differing assessment intervals, which could result in inflated time to progression, the increased availability and impact of subsequent therapies, treatment optimization, or differing follow-up schedules. Despite this, PFS was continuously high in more diverse populations and varied geographies, highlighting the survival benefit of pembrolizumab across broad, RW patient populations. ToT also appeared to be consistent with that shown in KEYNOTE trials, demonstrating pembrolizumab tolerability across broad patient populations and long-term follow-up.

In the last 10 years following its approval, pembrolizumab has become SoC for the 1L treatment of mNSCLC and is the most frequently used ICI in clinical practice worldwide [50,57]. Consistent RWE with clinical trials, long-term follow-up, and large numbers of studies reinforce the role of pembrolizumab-based regimens as SoC 1L treatment for mNSCLC without actionable driver mutations within the ICI treatment landscape.

Globally, survival outcomes with pembrolizumab monotherapy appeared to be comparable to KEYNOTE-024 and KEYNOTE-042 trials and exceeded the results for chemotherapy alone [28,29]. OS appeared to be comparable across the majority of RWE studies, including a global SLR that pooled the mean OS across over 17,000 patients, representing one of the largest data syntheses of 1L pembrolizumab in PD-L1 ≥50% mNSCLC [56]. This SLR also included RWE recorded with up to 5 years of follow-up, broader patient populations than those recorded in clinical trials, including older patients and those with ECOG ≥2, and broad geographic coverage [56]. Despite the RW population differences, survival outcomes were aligned with KEYNOTE-024, reinforcing the external validity of this trial data [29,56].

PFS appeared to be frequently higher in RWE studies than reported in KEYNOTE trials (up to 22 months) despite the inclusion of broader patient populations, including patients with higher ECOG-PS and brain metastases, demonstrating the PFS benefit of pembrolizumab outside of clinical trial populations [72,86,91–93,95,96,132,133]. However, as previously discussed, this inflation could be in part due to methodological differences. OS appeared to be sustained across longer follow-ups of up to 5 years, demonstrating the durable, long-term efficacy of pembrolizumab in a broad patient population, consistent with clinical trial outcomes [28,35,81,85,86]. Furthermore, treatment patterns confirm pembrolizumab as the most frequently prescribed ICI across multiple countries, affirming its efficacy and establishing its position as the preferred 1L treatment for mNSCLC [72,110].

For pembrolizumab combination therapy, OS and PFS appeared to be consistent with KEYNOTE-189 and KEYNOTE-407, suggesting that pembrolizumab in combination may be similarly efficacious across clinical trial populations and RW populations. Across histology and PD-L1 TPS cohorts, RWE demonstrated descriptively consistent survival rates than in KEYNOTE-189 and −407, suggesting that survival benefit with pembrolizumab combination therapy in RWE may be sustained across histology and PD-L1 TPS populations [30–33,66,73,128]. Long-term efficacy was also comparable to KEYNOTE trials, with one in seven patients still receiving pembrolizumab combination therapy after 2 years of treatment [31,32,51]. Pembrolizumab combination therapy was the most commonly administered treatment option for patients with both squamous and nonsquamous mNSCLC, demonstrating its high usage as 1L treatment for mNSCLC regardless of histology [43].

Real-world patient populations were generally older, with more comorbidities, and more likely to be female than KEYNOTE trial populations [35,51,53,116,124]. Despite this, treatment effectiveness appeared to remain consistent across pembrolizumab-based regimens, with comparable findings to KEYNOTE seen across RWE populations. Other observational studies that included patients ≥75 years of age with PD-L1 expressing advanced NSCLC have also reported survival benefits of frontline pembrolizumab monotherapy for these patients [101,134,135]. These findings demonstrate that across more diverse patient populations, pembrolizumab-based regimens show survival outcomes that, while sometimes numerically lower than in pivotal trials due to the increased age and additional comorbidities, remained clinically meaningful, reinforcing their role as a cornerstone of 1L therapy in routine practice.

Improved survival outcomes were often reported in patients with higher PD-L1 TPS. The median OS of 30.4 months observed for patients with PD-L1 TPS ≥90% treated with 1 L pembrolizumab monotherapy was significantly higher (p < 0.01) compared to the PD-L1 TPS 50–89% subgroup in one exploratory analysis [79]. As previously mentioned, this inflation could be in part due to methodological differences between RCTs and RWE. However, survival rates for patients with lower PD-L1 TPS were still comparable to the respective TPS populations within the KEYNOTE trials, suggesting that additional population differences did not affect survival outcomes [31].

Compared with alternative ICIs, pembrolizumab monotherapy has shown improved outcomes across RWE (median pooled OS: 21.0 months), with shorter OS reported for nivolumab and atezolizumab monotherapy (4.2–17.0 months, and 6.4 months, respectively) [55,56]. Compared with the treatment duration of pembrolizumab monotherapy in this review, the median treatment duration of atezolizumab and cemiplimab monotherapy is less (5.3 and 6.3 months, respectively) [136,137]. However, the comparison of ICIs across different studies is limited due to methodological heterogeneity, including study timeframes, duration of treatments, patient populations, follow-up, and missing data, which may all affect outcomes. While a 2022 SLR reported the largest volume of evidence for nivolumab, likely due to its status of being the first approved ICI of NSCLC, pembrolizumab has longer follow-up data, is more commonly used in clinical practice, and shows substantial OS benefit, presenting pembrolizumab as a reliable, durable treatment option [28,53–55,79,86].

The evidence presented within this narrative review is validated in studies evaluating pembrolizumab in advanced diseases such as melanoma, cervical cancer, and head and neck cancer. In patients with advanced melanoma, the median OS across RWE for those receiving pembrolizumab ranges from 19.4–30.0 months, and one retrospective cohort study of 4,127 patients reported findings aligned with clinical trials [138,139]. RWE studies also report effectiveness of pembrolizumab in advanced cervical cancer and comparative efficacy to clinical trials in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, supporting the broader validity of pembrolizumab findings from this review in mNSCLC [140,141].

The RWE presented in this review validates the generalizability of RCT findings and strengthens the role of 1L pembrolizumab-based regimens as SoC in trial-eligible patients with mNSCLC and patients with mNSCLC excluded from trials. In older adult patients, what appeared to be OS, PFS, and ToT were identified across monotherapy studies [35,101], supporting pembrolizumab use in this subpopulation. In patients treated with pembrolizumab combinations, median OS as identified in Barrichello et al. (2025) was lower than KEYNOTE-189 and −407 [30,33,130]. However, the subgroup of patients ≥80 years old contained a larger proportion of patients with ECOG-PS ≥2 than included in the KEYNOTE trials and within the <80 ‑year‑old subgroup, thus leading to worse prognosis [130]. These findings are supported by Nebhan et al. (2021) who assessed the efficacy of ICIs within patients aged ≥80 years and identified similar findings and safety across age groups [142]. The RWE presented affirms pembrolizumab-based regimens as the SoC for older patients, showing apparent comparative efficacy to the KEYNOTE trials regardless of age.

In patients with ECOG-PS 2, a reduction in OS, PFS, and ToT was reported compared with patients with mNSCLC with an ECOG-PS 0–1, though small population size and lack of consistent documentation of ECOG-PS limit the ability to draw robust conclusions [91,101,102]. Poorer outcomes are expected in patients with ECOG-PS 2, as they are more likely to have comorbidities and experience adverse events, adverse event-related deaths, and treatment discontinuations [143]. Treatment of these patients with pembrolizumab results in improved outcomes when compared with other ICIs, with a median OS of 4.0 months reported for ECOG-PS 2 patients treated with nivolumab compared to 7.8–11.8 months for pembrolizumab [101,102,144]. While pembrolizumab-based-regimens may not result in the efficacy reported in the KEYNOTE trials, pembrolizumab may still offer the most effective treatment option for these patients.

The findings from broader patient subpopulations may provide reassurance to clinicians during treatment selection for patients underrepresented within clinical trials. Current literature highlights that clinician decision-making is multifaceted and takes a collaborative approach to balance clinical evidence and patient preference [145,146]. The optimal treatment pathway for patients with mNSCLC is decided on an individual basis and considers many factors such as histology; tumor genotype; PD-L1 expression; disease burden; patient factors, including patient preferences, ECOG-PS, and comorbidities; and system factors, including test and treatment wait times, access to genotyping and therapies, and reimbursement [146]. The RWE in this review suggests pembrolizumab is suitable as 1L monotherapy or with chemotherapy for use across a wider patient cohort. Clinicians also report difficulties in prescribing optimal treatment due to RW population differences in age, comorbidities, and ECOG scores, all of which can be a barrier to certain treatment options [120,147,148].

Patient adherence is a crucial component that impacts treatment effectiveness and clinical outcomes as well as being associated with patient satisfaction, QoL and treatment-related toxicity. A retrospective cohort study that assessed the adherence rate of NCCN recommendations and the impact of timing of guideline updates on adherence reported pembrolizumab adherence to be 86% (n = 201), therefore increasing the likelihood of physician choosing this treatment option [148,149]. Treatment with ICIs, namely, pembrolizumab, nivolumab, and atezolizumab, all show improved patient QoL compared to chemotherapy alternatives, which may subsequently encourage patient adherence and lead to improved outcomes [150]. Brahmer et al. (2017) demonstrated that fewer pembrolizumab‑treated patients reported deterioration, compared to those who underwent chemotherapy (31% versus 39%), which contributes to increased patient adherence [150]. Lower discontinuation rates and fewer toxicity events have also been observed in patients treated with pembrolizumab compared to nivolumab [151,152]. Over time, there has been increased use of immunotherapy, and use of chemotherapy alone is uncommon, as true contraindications to immunotherapy are uncommon.

This review is limited by its non-systematic nature, which may lead to potential selection bias. Differences in the capture of baseline ECOG-PS, the handling of missingness and censored cases, immortal time and ascertainment bias, follow-up duration, and the impact of treatment switching may have resulted in observed differences between RWE and RCTs, which limits comparability. Formal statistical comparison between RWE and KEYNOTE trials could not be conducted due to methodological differences between studies, which limit the non-statistical comparison and conclusions drawn. Other ICIs are discussed where relevant as guideline-listed alternatives or comparators; however, comparator data are infrequently reported in RWE and a comprehensive review of comparator RW data is beyond the scope of this narrative review, therefore no direct comparison can be made. Similarly, statistical comparison to current ICIs was not the focus of this narrative review, and therefore comparators included were based on relevance to the treated population within the KEYNOTE trials. Additionally, the majority of RWE was conducted in European or North American patient populations, with few publications from other regions, limiting extrapolation. RWE is vulnerable to bias, loss to follow-up, or the use of historical controls, which may skew results. Furthermore, no tool was used to assess risk of bias or data quality of the included RWE studies. Therefore, heterogeneity in data sources, including differences between EHR-based and registry-based studies introduces variable susceptibility to selection bias, outcome misclassification, and missing data that impact the comparability of findings and the strength of conclusions drawn from this narrative review. Quality of data and missing data may also impact results. Whilst other classes of therapies, such as antibody drug conjugates, continue to evolve the treatment pathway for NSCLC, this was outside of the aims of the current narrative review and therefore was not discussed. Further systematic analysis of these populations is required in order to draw comparative conclusions.

The available volume of RWE for pembrolizumab is substantial and encompasses diverse patient populations, histological subtypes, PD-L1 TPS ranges, biomarkers, and long follow-up periods. However, further analysis is needed to clarify how other prognostic factors such as comorbidities influence the effectiveness of pembrolizumab as monotherapy and in combination regimens. RWE for driver‑mutation‑positive versus negative patients treated with ICIs would be beneficial to identify response rates and survival outcomes in this population with limited treatment lines. Longer-term follow-up conducted outside of the US and Europe is required to confirm the global generalizability of current findings, and more granular subgroup analyses by ECOG-PS, age, and metastatic sites would improve understanding of treatment effectiveness in populations that are underrepresented in clinical trials and support more informed clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

Substantial burden is associated with mNSCLC for patients, their caregivers, and healthcare systems worldwide. The KEYNOTE clinical trials established 1L pembrolizumab as a highly efficacious ICI, demonstrating meaningful and significant improvements in OS and PFS as monotherapy and in combination with chemotherapy. A decade of RWE for 1L pembrolizumab demonstrates its effectiveness that broadly aligns with pivotal KEYNOTE trial outcomes. This consistency is observed across regions and diverse patient populations, including subpopulations underrepresented within KEYNOTE trials. Collectively, the available RWE broadly supports pembrolizumab-based regimens as central components of 1L SoC for mNSCLC without actionable driver alterations, with regimen choice guided by PD-L1 expression, disease burden, and patient tolerability. Further longer follow-up data and subgroup analyses would be beneficial to understand how treatment outcomes are impacted by specific patient characteristics such as driver‑negative versus positive populations particularly for populations underrepresented in clinical trials, to support evidence-based decision-making for these populations.

Future perspectives

Pembrolizumab is a frequently administered SoC treatment of mNSCLC. Further long-term RWE outcomes data and subgroup analyses will solidify the survival outcomes and QoL benefits associated with pembrolizumab treatment, confirming pembrolizumab’s position as SoC across patient populations and supporting evidence-based decision-making for populations with limited treatment options. Improved patient stratification driven by next-generation predictive biomarkers and screening will enhance response rates by precisely identifying those most likely to benefit from pembrolizumab regimens. Furthermore, pembrolizumab may be utilized for future combination regimens integrating novel checkpoint inhibitors, targeted therapies, or immunomodulatory agents, which could significantly expand its therapeutic potential in populations with historically limited treatment options. Over the next decade, developments in this field will enable novel combination sequencing, biomarker-adapted dosing strategies, and proactive management of immune-related adverse events, collectively maximizing both the clinical efficacy and the long-term safety profile for mNSCLC patients. Alongside this, RWE is expected to evolve through the use of increasingly integrated oncology datasets and advancing analytical tools linking electronic health records, patient-reported outcomes, and molecular profiling data. This will enable a more thorough assessment of long-term outcomes, strengthening the impact of RWE on clinical guidelines, reimbursement, and safety monitoring while further contextualizing the effectiveness and tolerability of pembrolizumab-based regimens in routine clinical practice. Ultimately, as this evidence base matures, pembrolizumab is expected to continue providing durable survival outcomes for patients with mNSCLC, with the potential to demonstrate sustained longer-term survival outcomes across real-world patient populations.
