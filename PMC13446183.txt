Critical illness-associated cerebral microbleeds (CIACM) after urosepsis: An under-recognized cause of persistent encephalopathy in the ICU
Authors: Rashid Al Sheheimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCritical illness–associated cerebral microbleeds (CIACM) are increasingly recognized as a form of structural brain injury in critically ill patients, particularly in the setting of sepsis, severe hypoxemia, and multiorgan failure. On susceptibility-weighted MRI, CIACM typically shows a characteristic distribution involving the corpus callosum, juxtacortical white matter, brainstem, and cerebellum, with relative sparing of the deep gray nuclei. However, deep gray nuclei involvement may be observed in more severe cases, supporting the concept of diffuse microvascular injury rather than a focal neurologic disorder. Although the exact mechanism remains not completely elucidated, endothelial dysfunction, blood–brain barrier disruption, and hypoxia-related microangiopathy are playing major roles. Clinically, CIACM carries important significances, including prolonged encephalopathy, uncertainty in neurologic prognostication, and challenges related to anticoagulation management. Despite growing recognition, the condition likely remains underdiagnosed because MRI is not routinely performed in critically ill patients with persistent altered mental status. We describe a patient with septic shock secondary to obstructive uropathy who developed persistent encephalopathy and was later found to have CIACM on MRI.Keywords: Critical illness-associated cerebral microbleeds, Septic shock, Susceptibility-weighted imaging, Cerebral microbleeds, Critical illness, Encephalopathy

Critical illness-associated cerebral microbleeds (CIACM) have recently been recognized as a distinct manifestation in patients with severe systemic illness, particularly those with sepsis and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). We present a case of septic shock secondary to obstructive uropathy complicated by CIACM.

Case presentation

A 23-year-old woman with recurrent nephrolithiasis, otherwise healthy, presented to the emergency department with a 4-day history of left flank pain radiating to the groin, fever, and lethargy. On arrival, she was drowsy with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 13/15 and was in septic shock, with a blood pressure of 75/38 mmHg and heart rate of 133 beats/min, requiring immediate resuscitation and 2 vasopressors. During initial assessment, she developed acute hypoxemic respiratory failure, with oxygen saturation in the 80s, requiring endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation.

Laboratory investigations showed marked inflammation, including a white blood cell count of 44,000/µL and C-reactive protein of 401 mg/L. Chest radiography demonstrated severe diffuse bilateral pulmonary consolidations. Abdominal computed tomography revealed a left ureteric stone with associated hydronephrosis, consistent with infected obstructive uropathy complicated by septic shock and sepsis-associated acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). She underwent emergency bilateral double-J ureteric stent insertion at the discretion of the urology team to ensure maximal urinary drainage and decompression in the setting of severe sepsis and hemodynamic instability. Us was drained during the procedure from left ureter.

Her respiratory status progressed to severe ARDS, with a PaO2/FiO2 ratio of 70, requiring lung-protective ventilation and 3 sessions of prone positioning. Her course was further complicated by multiorgan failure (MOF), including acute kidney injury, deranged liver enzymes, and thrombocytopenia. Peripheral blood film showed no evidence of hemolysis or schistocytes. Blood, respiratory, and urine cultures, viral respiratory screening as well as COVID 19 were negative. Over the following 5 days, her hemodynamics, oxygenation, and MOF gradually improved, allowing vasopressor and sedative medications to be reduced.

Despite cessation of sedation, she remained severely encephalopathic. Three days after sedation was stopped, she had absent cranial nerve reflexes, no motor response and Glasgow Coma Scale of 3. Brain computed tomography showed subtle cortical hyperdensity and punctate lesions in the corpus callosum raising concern for microhemorrhages (Fig. 1). Because of persistent coma and lack of neurological recovery, brain magnetic resonance imaging was performed. Susceptibility-weighted imaging revealed innumerable cerebral microhemorrhages involving the brainstem, cerebellum, corpus callosum, supratentorial white matter, and both basal ganglia, with an overall pattern most consistent with CIACM (Fig. 2).

Neurological recovery was gradual. She remained severely encephalopathic during the first 4 days after sedation cessation. On Day 5, brainstem reflexes started to return, followed by progressive improvement in arousal, eye opening, motor responses, and command following over Days 6-10. By Day 10, she had regained adequate consciousness and airway protective reflexes, allowing successful extubation. She later recovered full consciousness, with generalized weakness but no focal neurological deficit, and was transferred to the ward before discharge home in stable condition.

Discussion

CIACM is an increasingly recognized neuroimaging entity in critically ill patients, particularly in the context of sepsis, respiratory failure, and MOF [[1], [2], [3]]. This case demonstrates a classic yet underreported presentation of CIACM occurring in the setting of urosepsis secondary to obstructive pyelonephritis, complicated by septic shock and severe hypoxemic respiratory failure. We believe that the patient’s ARDS was not due to a primary pulmonary infection, but rather represented sepsis-associated ARDS secondary to septic shock. The primary source of sepsis was most likely infected obstructive uropathy.

A key feature of CIACM is its characteristic radiological distribution, most commonly involving the corpus callosum, juxtacortical and subcortical white matter, brainstem, and cerebellum. The classic CIACM pattern is usually described as relatively sparing the deep gray nuclei [[1], [2], [3], [4]]; however, less common involvement of the basal ganglia and thalami has been reported, indicating that limited deep gray nuclei involvement does not necessarily exclude CIACM [5]. In our patient, the dominant distribution of microbleeds involved the corpus callosum, subcortical/juxtacortical white matter, brainstem, and cerebellum, supporting CIACM, while the basal ganglia foci were interpreted as an atypical but previously described extension of this spectrum. This pattern remains useful in distinguishing CIACM from other intracerebral bleedings [4,6]. The associated small convexity subarachnoid hemorrhage may reflect microvascular fragility during severe systemic illness, although it is not specific for CIACM.

The pathophysiology of CIACM is likely to be multifactorial, representing a final common pathway of critical illness rather than a disease-specific pattern [1,2,7]. In this patient, several mechanisms likely combined to produce diffused microvascular injury. Sepsis-induced systemic inflammation promotes endothelial activation and capillary leak, leading to disruption of the blood–brain barrier and extravasation of blood products [2,7,8]. Concurrent severe hypoxemia, as seen in ARDS, is a central driver of microvascular injury, particularly in metabolically vulnerable white matter regions [3,9]. Additionally, sepsis-associated coagulopathy further predisposes to hemorrhagic transformation at the microvascular level. Renal dysfunction and metabolic derangements, commonly seen in urosepsis, may further exacerbate blood–brain barrier disruption and contribute to microbleed formation [10].

Although CIACM has been extensively reported in patients with primary pulmonary pathology, particularly ARDS and COVID-19, its occurrence in urosepsis remains less frequently reported [2,3]. This case highlights that CIACM should be viewed as a manifestation of systemic critical illness rather than organ-specific disease, emphasizing that severe sepsis—regardless of source can cause in diffuse neurovascular injury. The combination of septic shock, hypoxemia, and MOF in this patient reflects a “multiple-hit” model causing cerebral injury. From a clinical perspective, CIACM remains likely under-recognized, as neuroimaging is not routinely pursued in unstable ICU patients with encephalopathy [2,3]. The presence of CIACM introduces challenges in anticoagulation strategies, particularly in patients requiring extracorporeal support, where the balance between hemorrhagic and thrombotic risk becomes more complex [11]. Furthermore, CIACM has been associated with critical illness severity, prolonged ICU stay, and variable neurological outcomes, ranging from meaningful recovery to persistent cognitive impairment [2,3,9].

Management of CIACM remains supportive, with no targeted therapy available. Treatment is directed toward controlling the underlying sepsis, optimization of oxygenation, and stabilization of systemic hemodynamics [2,3]. CIACM occurs in very sick ICU patients with substantial mortality (∼20%-40% in reported series) and frequent cognitive impairment among those formally tested. About two-thirds of reported patients with outcome data are functionally independent at discharge, but follow-up is short and long-term prognostication is still not clear too [2].

In conclusion, CIACM demonstrates a radiological signature of systemic microvascular failure in severe sepsis and hypoxemia. Recognition of this entity is essential, as it reshapes neurological dysfunction in critically ill patients from purely functional encephalopathy to structural brain injury with potential prognostic and therapeutic considerations.
