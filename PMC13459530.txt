Size-Resolved Lipoprotein Fatty Acid Content as a Novel Nuclear Magnetic Resonance-Derived Trait Specifically Associates with Genetic Variants That Control Fatty Acid Metabolism
Authors: Aziz Belkadi, Gaurav Thareja, Nisha Stephan, Anna Halama, Karsten Suhre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Population-level nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)-based lipoprotein profiling is a key tool for investigating dysregulated lipoprotein metabolism and its role in cardiovascular disorders. However, associations with size-resolved lipoprotein composition readouts are difficult to dissect, as these traits are often highly correlated. Derived variables can therefore be more relevant to biological interpretation. Here, we show that the total fatty acid (FA) content of the lipoproteins derived from their lipid headgroup concentrations using the formula FA = 3TG + 2PL + CE strongly correlates (Spearman rho = 0.98) with the independently measured total fatty acid content. This observation is not self-evident since these variables are determined using different portions of the NMR spectrum. Using NMR data acquired on the Nightingale platform for 274,303 UK Biobank (UKB) participants and genetic associations as a readout, we then show that this relationship also holds at the size-resolved lipoprotein level. We identified eight gene loci where the proposed FA variables display a significantly stronger genetic association signal than that of the corresponding lipid headgroup variables. Five of these loci (LIPC, LIPG, PLB1, LPL, and APOC3) have a direct function in FA metabolism. Including computed size-resolved FA variables may therefore improve the biological interpretation of future studies based on Nightingale’s data.

Introduction

Identifying and understanding molecular biomarkers that are relevant to health and disease are major objectives of population-based biobank studies. Associations of metabolomic and proteomic readouts from a large number of blood samples with the corresponding health phenotypes of study participants can provide new insights into the underlying dysregulated biological pathways, suggest new biomarkers, and indicate potential therapeutic targets. , However, readouts from deep phenotyping platforms often correlate, and it is not always possible to identify those traits that are most relevant for the phenotype under investigation. In such situations, it is reasonable not only to prioritize traits that display the strongest associations with the end point but also to focus on traits that can be directly related to specific biological pathways. Relevant traits are often derived as sums or ratios of variables directly measured by a research platform.

The nuclear magnetic resonance platform (NMR) operated by Nightingale Health Plc is one of the most widely used lipoprotein profiling platforms in population-based studies, with over 1 million samples analyzed to date. The Nightingale platform reports size-resolved lipoprotein composition profiles at a population scale across 14 lipoprotein size classes. A total of 249 readouts is generally provided to the user to characterize lipoprotein composition, many of which correlate at different degrees. , Note that only 107 of these readouts are directly measured by the NMR platform, while 61 are derived variables representing the sum of two or more measured readouts and another 81 are ratios, that is, the relative content of specific lipids reported as percentages.

The Nightingale NMR platform assays specifically the concentrations (mmol/L) of TG, PL, CE, and free cholesterol (FC) across 14 lipoprotein size classes, covering six very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) classes, one class of intermediate-density lipoproteins, three classes of low-density lipoproteins (LDL), and four classes of high-density lipoproteins (HDL). The total cholesterol (TC) and the total lipid (TL) contents of every lipoprotein class are mathematically derived as the sum TC = CE + FC and TL = TG + TC + PL, respectively. Also computed is the aggregated content of each of these variables in all VLDL, LDL, and HDL particles and the total over all lipoprotein particles. The primary lipid variables (TG, PL, CE, and FC) are quantified based on the NMR signal of their headgroups. Additionally, the platform quantifies the total fatty acids in all lipoprotein particles based on the NMR signal from the fatty acyl chains. Finally, it also measures a number of small molecules (metabolites) and a few proteins [full list at https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/label.cgi?id=220].

Most studies using the Nightingale platform focus on the lipid headgroup readouts (lipid type X in size class Y). However, many biological processes are rate-limited by fatty acid metabolism and not the availability of lipid headgroups. Fatty acids (FA) have numerous physiological functions, ranging from structure building in cell membranes to energy supply and cell-to-cell signaling. Dysregulated fatty acid metabolism has been associated with many complex disorders, including cardiovascular disease, Alzheimer’s disease, type 2 diabetes, and cancer. FA are transported in the bloodstream to their target tissues by lipoproteins in the form of energy-rich triglycerides (TG), phospholipids (PL), and cholesteryl esters (CE), where TGs carry three fatty acyl side chains, PLs carry two, and CEs carry one. These fatty acyl groups can be cleaved off from their carrier molecules and converted via the beta oxidation pathway to provide energy-rich molecules and building blocks for the de novo synthesis of carbohydrates, amino acids, nucleotides, and other lipids. FA readouts are currently not provided by the Nightingale platform. It would therefore be of interest to include variables representing fatty acid metabolism directly into future analyses.

Noting that the numbers of fatty acyl side chains bound to each lipid headgroup are three for triacylglycerols (TGs), two for phospholipids (PLs), and one for cholesteryl esters (CEs), and we hypothesized that the side chain-weighted sum of the concentrations of the three fatty acyl chain carrying lipids could be an appropriate estimate for the total fatty acid content.

In the following, we show that the NMR-derived total FA content highly correlates with the FA content computed from the total lipid content using the formula CE + 2PL + 3TG. It is not obvious that this relationship should hold since the NMR signal for the FA side chains comes from another part of the spectrum compared to those for the three lipid head groups. We then conjecture that the same relationship holds within the 14 size-resolved lipoprotein classes. To verify this conjecture, we use NMR data from over 270,000 UK Biobank participants and conduct a genome-wide association study (GWAS) with the size-resolved FAs. We assume that genetic associations among correlated traits are strongest for the traits that are biologically most closely related to the associated gene’s biological function, as we previously showed in GWAS with other metabolomics platforms. Finally, we show that gene loci that are involved in fatty acid metabolism associate more strongly with the computed FA variables than with the original lipid headgroup-representing variables.

Methods

Data Sources

This research has been conducted using the UK Biobank Resource under application number 588633. UK Biobank has ethical approval from the North West Multicenter Research Ethics Committee (MREC) (approval number 11/NW/0382), and all participants provided written informed consent. All data were obtained through the UKB RAP system on the DNAnexus platform (data were dispensed on 23 April 2023).

Lipoprotein Profiling

A total of 249 metabolic biomarkers from EDTA plasma samples of 274,303 participants, generated by Nightingale Health for Phase 1 and Phase 2 of the study, were extracted from category 220. The Nightingale NMR platform assays the concentrations (mmol/L) of TGs, PLs, CEs, and FC across 14 lipoprotein size classes, covering six very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) classes [chylomicrons and extremely large (XXL-VLDL, with average particle diameter >75 nm), very large (XL-VLDL, 64 nm), large (L-VLDL, 53.6 nm), medium (M-VLDL, 44.5 nm), small (S-VLDL, 36.8 nm), and very small (XS-VLDL, 31.3 nm)], one class of intermediate-density lipoproteins (IDL, 28.6 nm), three classes of low-density lipoproteins (LDL) [large (L-LDL, 25.5 nm), medium (M-LDL, 23.0 nm), and small (S-LDL, 18.7 nm)], and four classes of high-density lipoproteins (HDL) [very large (XL-HDL, 14.3 nm), large (L-HDL, 12.1 nm), medium (M-HDL, 10.9 nm), and small (S-HDL, 8.7 nm)]. The total cholesterol (TC) and the total lipid (TL) contents of every lipoprotein class are mathematically derived as the sum TC = CE + FC and TL = TG + TC + PL, respectively. Also computed is the content of each of these variables in all VLDL, LDL, and HDL particles and as the total over all lipoprotein particles. The primary lipid variables (TG, PL, EC, and FC) are quantified based on the NMR signal of their headgroups. Additionally, the platform quantifies the total fatty acids in all lipoprotein particles based on the NMR signal from the fatty acyl chains.

Definition of the Fatty Acid Variables

Total fatty acid levels for UKB participants were computed using the following formula: FAt=3·TGt+2·PLt+1·CEt where TGt represents the total triglyceride (TG) levels, PLt represents the total phospholipid (PL) levels, and CEt represents the total cholesteryl ester (CE) levels. Similarly, the fatty acid levels in lipoprotein subclass x were computed as FAx=3·TGx+2·PLx+1·CEx where x represents one of the 14 lipoprotein subclasses (TG x denotes the TG levels, PL x denotes the PL levels, and CE x denotes the CE levels within the given lipoprotein subclass).

Genome-Wide Association of the Fatty Acid Variables

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) were conducted using a discovery replication design and conservative Bonferroni significance levels (P-value <0.05/number of tests). Samples for discovery were selected based on the following criteria: participants with "Ethnic background|Instance 0" recorded as "Irish”, "British”, or "any other white background,” resulting in NMR data for 256,884 UKB participants. Plink2 was used to extract 9,293,763 imputed genotypes from BGEN files (category 100319) that met the following criteria: --geno 0.1 --hwe 1e-15 --mac 100 --maf 0.01 --mind 0.1. Next, Plink2 was used to perform an association analysis between the imputed genotypes and fatty acid levels in 14 lipoprotein subclasses. Linear models were computed using Plink2, with inverse normal-scaled FA levels per lipoprotein subclass as the dependent variable. Age (category 21022), sex (category 22001), and the first 10 genotype principal components (category 22009) were included as covariates.

For replication, data from 14,326 UKB non-European participants were used, defined as those whose "Ethnic background|Instance 0" was not recorded as "Irish”, "British”, or "any other white background". Associations were considered replicated if P-value < 0.05/number of Bonferroni significant association in the discovery.

For all replicated signals for FA levels in lipoprotein subclasses, linear models were then calculated using Plink2 to determine their associations with TG, PL, CE, FC, and TL across all lipoprotein subclasses. The P-gain statistic was used to identify cases where using FA as a trait significantly increased the strength of the association compared to all other lipid head groups in the same size class. The P-gain for associations with FA in a given lipoprotein subclass was then computed as P−gain=min(P(TGx),P(PLx),P(CEx),P(FCx),P(TLx))/P(FAx) where P(FA x ) represents the P-value for the FA association in lipoprotein subclass x and P(TG x ), P(PL x ), P(CE x ), P(FC x ), and P(TL x ) represent the P-values for the association with TG, PL, CE, FC, and TL in the same lipoprotein subclass x, respectively.

Log10-scaled P-values and P-gains were used throughout to prevent numeric overflows and rounding of small P-values to zero. The significance of the P-gain was determined based on a Bonferroni correction threshold (P-gainBonf = 10 × number of tests, see ref ). Associations on correlating variants were lumped into trait-independent associations by merging all variants in high linkage disequilibrium (LD > 0.8) for a single trait and keeping the strongest association as sentinel.

Other Resources

Variants were annotated using OpenTarget API. LocusZoom was used to generate regional association plots with LD annotation based on the European population.

Results

Directly Measured and Computationally Computed Total FA Contents Highly Correlate

The measured total lipid content (TLt) and the measured total fatty acid content (mFAt) of the lipoproteins highly correlate at a level of Spearman’s rho = 0.91 (Figure A). Notably, the computed total FA content (cFAt), computed as 3 times the TGt content, 2 times PLt, and 1 time CEt, displays an even stronger correlation with mFAt at a level of Spearman rho = 0.98 (Figure B). These correlations are based on UKB data from 274,303 participants (see the Methods section).

Note that the intercept of the linear fit of TLt to mFAt is 10 times larger than those of the association of cFAt and mFAt (intercept = 1.39 vs −0.13), which suggests that some lipids are not accounted for by the mFAt signal. The slope of the linear fit between cFAt and mFAt is larger than one (slope = 1.1), suggesting an overestimation of total fatty acids by about 10% when these are computed based on the abundance of lipid headgroups. This is in agreement with the previous observation that some of the lipids detected based on their headgroups are not accounted for by the measurement of the FA side chains.

The Nightingale platform does not measure fatty acid levels on the size-resolved level. These variables are newly defined in this work, and their biological validity is addressed in the next section. Here, we discuss their correlation with the other size-resolved lipid traits. As expected, FA levels in the TG-rich lipoprotein subclasses (XXL-VLDL and XL-VLDL) strongly correlate with the TG levels in these lipoprotein subclasses, whereas the FA levels in the PL-rich lipoprotein subclasses (M-HDL and S-HDL) correlate with the PL levels in these lipoprotein subclasses (Figure C). FAs correlate within lipoprotein classes at the VLDL, LDL, and HDL level and anticorrelate across the VLDL and HDL classes (Figure D). The observed degree of correlation is expected and matches similar degrees of correlation observed between the lipid headgroups. These correlations reflect the effect of taking the sum of the three lipid headgroups (CE, PL, and TG) weighted by the number of attached FA side chains and their relative abundance.

GWAS on FA in Lipoprotein Subclasses Identifies 16 Independent Associations

We conducted a genome-wide association study (GWAS) on the computed FA levels in all lipoprotein subclasses using data from the European UKB participants (the discovery cohort). A total of 305,500 associations involving 73,526 variants reached a Bonferroni level of significance (P-value < 0.05/(9,293,763 × 14); Figure and Table S1). Among these associations, FA in XL-VLDL exhibited the highest number of associations (28,640), while FA in L-LDL had the lowest (13,357). A total of 2715 associations were replicated at a Bonferroni level of significance using data from the non-European UKB participants (P-value < 0.05/305,500, Table S2).

For each replicated association, we computed the P-gain (see the Methods section) to identify those associations that were dominated by an FA signal. A total of 332 associations reached a Bonferroni level of significance (P-gain >10 × 2715; Table S3). We then clumped highly correlated linked variants using an LD cutoff of r 2 > 0.8 and obtained 16 distinct genetic signals at eight gene loci (Table and Figure and Figure S1 and Table S4). We thus identified 16 independent genetic associations where the computed size-resolved FA levels exhibited a significant P-gain compared to the five matching lipid markers quantified by the NMR platform: TL, FC, CE, PL, and TG.

Functional Annotation of the FA Associations

Locus annotation using OpenTargets mapped these 16 associations to eight gene loci with candidate genes known to play key roles in lipid metabolism: APOB, PLB1, LPL, SQLE, APOC3, LIPC, LIPG, and PLTP. At the APOB locus, we observed significant associations across all lipid traits, particularly within IDL and LDL subclasses. For PLB1, LPL, SQLE, and APOC3, strong associations were detected in the very large VLDL subclasses (XXL-VLDL, XL-VLDL, and L-VLDL). Meanwhile, associations within the HDL subclasses were prominent at the LIPC, LIPG, and PLTP loci (Figure and the Supporting Information, Figure S1).

Five Gene Loci Are Directly Linked to FA Metabolism

Among the eight identified loci, five could be directly linked to FA composition in lipoproteins: LIPC, LIPG, LPL, PLB1, and APOC3 (Table S5). Of these, LIPC, LIPG, and LPL encode lipases that hydrolyze TG and/or PL to release free FAs. LIPC encodes the hepatic lipase, which hydrolyzes TG and PL in circulating lipoproteins such as HDL, IDL, and chylomicron remnants. Beyond its enzymatic role, the hepatic lipase also facilitates lipoprotein uptake by acting as a ligand for cell surface receptors or heparan sulfate proteoglycans. LIPG encodes the endothelial lipase and plays a central role in HDL metabolism. Its gene product primarily functions as a phospholipase, with minor TG lipase activity. LPL encodes lipoprotein lipase, which hydrolyzes TG in chylomicrons and VLDL, yielding two free FAs and one 2-monoacylglycerol molecule. APOC3 is believed to inhibit PLB1, a phospholipase that releases FAs from both the sn-1 and sn-2 positions of PL.

The remaining three genesAPOB, SQLE, and PLTPare also implicated in lipid metabolism, albeit not directly in FA turnover: APOB encodes the primary apolipoprotein for chylomicrons, VLDL, IDL, and LDL, serving as a carrier protein in lipid transport. SQLE encodes squalene epoxidase, which catalyzes the stereospecific conversion of squalene to 2,3­(S)-oxidosqualene, a key intermediate in cholesterol biosynthesis. PLTP is involved in the transfer of PL from VLDL to HDL, supporting HDL remodeling and function. Discussion

Using Nightingale NMR lipoprotein profiling data from 274,303 UK Biobank participants, we showed that the total fatty acid content of blood-borne lipoproteins can be computed as the sum of the three major lipid headgroups (CE, PL, and TG) weighted by their respective number of attached fatty acyl side chains. The hereby computed total FA content very strongly correlates (Spearman rho = 0.98) with the measured total FA content, reflecting the high internal consistency of the NMR measurements since the lipid content is derived from another part of the NMR spectrum compared to the fatty acid content.

A major strength of the Nightingale platform is its ability to not only provide readouts of total lipoprotein composition but also to assess the CE, PL, TG, and free cholesterol content at a size-resolved level in 14 size classes, covering the full range of chylomicrons, VLDL, LDL, IDL, and HDL lipoproteins. However, to the best of our knowledge, no measurements of the FA content at the lipid size class level are currently available (or even possible). Therefore, we introduced a new set of computed variables derived from the lipid content of the individual lipid size classes as the weighted sum of their TG, PL, and CE contents.

While we focus here on TG, PL, and CE, other lipid species present in plasmaincluding lysophospholipids (e.g., lyso-PC) and partial glycerides (e.g., monoacylglycerols and diacylglycerols)may contribute to the measured signals. These include both constitutively present species and intermediates generated by lipase activity

To test the validity and evaluate the biological interest of these 14 new FA variables, we conducted a GWAS and identified those loci at which the new FA variables lead to a significant increase in the strength of the association compared to the associations with the respective TG, PL, CE, and free cholesterol variables. We argue that stronger associations with fatty acid variables than with lipid headgroup variables indicate that fatty acyl side chain composition is biologically more relevant than lipid headgroup identity in these cases. This conclusion aligns with previous findings showing that fatty acyl chain length and degree of unsaturation contribute to the cellular function by modulating membrane biophysical properties, lipid signaling, and protein–lipid interactions, often beyond the effects of headgroup identity alone.

Lipid-lowering medications and fasting status systematically alter lipid levels and are typically treated as confounders in association studies. However, our interest here is in the correlation structure among lipid variables within the same individuals. Adjusting for these factors prior to computing correlations would distort the natural covariation pattern we aim to characterizefor instance, statins alter TG, LDL, and HDL in correlated ways that reflect real biology. We have therefore retained individuals regardless of medication use or fasting status and have not regressed out these variables.

We showed that this approach indeed worksamong several hundred independent genetic associations with other NMR variables (Figure , top panel), only eight gene loci emerge that display a substantial gain in the strength of association (Bonferroni significant P-gain, Figure , bottom panel) when using fatty acid rather than lipid headgroup variables. Our GWAS on computed FA levels identified 16 independent genetic variants for which the associations with FA levels in specific lipoprotein subclasses were stronger than those observed with traditional lipoprotein lipid measures by the NMR platform: TG, PL, CE, FC, and TL. Variant annotations linked these variants to eight genes: APOB, C2orf16, LPL, TRIB1, APOC3/APOC1/APOC4 gene cluster, LIPC, LIPG, and PLTP. Manual curation then suggested that some gene annotations reflect proximal regulatory effects rather than direct functional roles. Variants annotated to C2orf16 are likely influencing PLB1, given the gene’s close genomic proximity to C2orf16 and the well-established role of PLB1 in FA metabolism. Similarly, variants linked to TRIB1 may act through SQLE, a gene involved in cholesterol biosynthesis and lipid regulation. Within the APOC3/APOC1/APOC4 cluster, the variants are most likely functionally associated with APOC3, supported by its putative inhibitory role on PLB1 activity.

Among the identified loci, five genesPLB1, LPL, APOC3, LIPC, and LIPGwere directly linked to the FA composition in the lipoproteins. For APOB, SQLE, and PTLB, our data suggest that their primary effects may be more pronounced on the FA side chains than on the lipid head groups. In addition, four of these genes have known associations with metabolic disorders. Loss of function in LIPC is associated with hypercholesterolemia and hypertriglyceridemia, whereas gain of function mutations are linked to hypocholesterolemia. LIPG loss of function has been implicated in hyperalphalipoproteinemia, a condition characterized by elevated HDL cholesterol. APOB deficiency results in hypobetalipoproteinemia, marked by abnormally low levels of apoB-containing lipoproteins. LPL deficiency leads to hypertriglyceridemia due to impaired hydrolysis of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins. These findings underscore the physiological and pathological relevance of FA composition in lipoproteins and highlight the potential of the computed FA traits as biomarkers for lipid-related disorders.

Our study also had limitations. The Nightingale platform provides several highly correlated readouts, which raises the question of whether additional correlated readouts contribute to independent information. We argue that the derived variables contain biologically relevant information, are easily computed at no extra experimental costs, and have only a marginal increase in multiple testing burden. The latter can be controlled when using the P-gain statistic and only takes significant associations forward to interpretation.

To summarize, we define a new NMR-derived variable that represents the FA side chain content of the individual lipoprotein size classes. Using a GWAS approach, we show that this new variable specifically identifies associations that are biochemically related to FA-related pathways. While a significant portion of current NMR biomarkers (142 out of 249) are derived measures, our study supports the integration of derived FA levels within lipoprotein subclasses as a meaningful addition to the panel of NMR-based metabolic biomarkers.
