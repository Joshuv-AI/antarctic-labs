Bilateral Leydig Cell Tumor in a Capuchin Monkey (Sapajus apella): Case Report

Ewerton Lourenço Barbosa Favacho, Sandla Freitas da Silva, Jamerson Luiz Brito da Silva, Daniella Kaísa de Oliveira Bezerra, Valíria Duarte Cerqueira, Gabriela Riet Correa Rivero, Pedro Soares Bezerra Júnior

Abstract

Leydig cell tumors (LCTs) are uncommon in nonhuman primates (NHPs). A bilateral LCT was identified in a 20‐year‐old male capuchin monkey (
Sapajus apella
) maintained under human care. Ultrasonography revealed a right testicular nodule, while necropsy identified bilateral testicular masses. Histopathology confirmed bilateral LCT. This case documents bilateral LCT in 
S. apella
, a rare finding in NHPs.

Introduction

Testicular neoplasms in NHPs are uncommon and have been reported in only a few individuals and as isolated cases [, , , , , , ]. Reports of bilateral LCTs in NHPs are scarce [, ], with few cases documented in primates of the genus Sapajus [, ]. The present study describes the clinical and pathological features of an LCT in a capuchin monkey (
Sapajus apella
) maintained under human care, with the aim of contributing to knowledge of this type of tumor in NHPs.

Materials and Methods

Humane Care Guidelines

The animal was maintained under human care at the Escola Experimental de Primatas (EEP). The present study used exclusively material obtained after the animal's death and did not involve experimental procedures, interventions, or experimentation on live animals.

Case Description

A male capuchin monkey (
Sapajus apella
), approximately 20 years old, maintained under human care at the Escola Experimental de Primatas (EEP), had a history of enlargement of the right testicle since 2023. The animal underwent ultrasonographic examination, which revealed an enlarged right testicle (3.18 cm), with heterogeneous echotexture, mixed echogenicity, and an anechoic cystic structure measuring 0.98 × 0.57 cm, suggesting a degenerative or infiltrative process of possible neoplastic origin. The left testicle had preserved dimensions (2.3 cm) (Figure ). Cytological examination was performed, and the findings were consistent with a hemorrhagic cystic formation, with no evidence of neoplastic cells.

In 2024, the animal died as a result of neurological abnormalities and was submitted for necropsy at the Animal Pathology Laboratory of the Federal University of Pará (UFPA), Castanhal Campus. On external examination, both testes were located in their normal scrotal position and were enlarged. The right testicle contained a friable nodule measuring 3.5 × 2.0 cm, orange in color on cut surface, with cystic cavities filled with clear fluid. The left testicle showed a similar but smaller nodule, measuring 2.1 × 1.0 cm (Figure ). No significant gross lesions were observed in other organs.

Fragments of various organs, including the testes, were collected, fixed in 10% buffered formalin, routinely processed for histology, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm thickness, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), according to the protocol described by Prophet et al. [].

Histologically, the testicular masses were composed of pleomorphic cells arranged in a solid pattern and supported by scant stroma. The cells had abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm containing numerous vacuoles of variable sizes and occasional brownish pigment. The nuclei were round and homogeneous, with dense chromatin and inconspicuous nucleoli, and no mitotic figures were observed in 2.37 mm2. Cystic areas were also observed, containing hyaline, mildly eosinophilic material, with no cells identified within the cystic spaces. No associated hemorrhage or large accumulations of blood were observed. In the remaining testicular parenchyma, marked depletion of germ cells within the seminiferous tubules and mild interstitial fibrosis were observed. The epididymal tubules were empty (Figure ). Based on the microscopic findings, a histopathological diagnosis of bilateral Leydig cell tumor was established.

Discussion

The diagnosis of bilateral Leydig cell tumor in the present study was established based on the pathological findings, which were consistent with those previously described in the literature for NHPs [, , , ]. However, in this case, LCT was confirmed in both testes, differing from previous reports in NHPs, in which this tumor was identified in only one testicle. In those reports, although tumors were present in both testes, they originated from different cell types, with LCTs restricted to the left testicle [, , ]. In humans, these tumors show no preference for laterality, with bilateral occurrence reported in approximately 3% of cases [, ].

In NHPs, LCTs are poorly documented, and both their incidence and the factors involved in their development remain poorly established [, ]. Although cryptorchidism represents a predisposing factor in humans, this type of tumor is not associated with this condition in other species, and several possible causes related to its development have been described [, , , , ]. In this context, despite the phylogenetic proximity to humans, no association between cryptorchidism and tumor development was observed, and the etiopathogenic mechanisms involved in these species remain uncertain.

Endocrine manifestations associated with LCTs are poorly described in NHPs. Although alopecia has been reported as a sign of hyperestrogenism in other species [], in the present case it was not possible to determine whether this finding resulted from the neoplasm or senescence. Signs of feminization were not observed, differing from those described in humans [], possibly influenced by the animal's cohabitation with a neutered female, which made libido assessment difficult. Therefore, it was not possible to differentiate tumor‐associated hyperestrogenemia from effects related to senescence, and the presence of subclinical hormonal activity cannot be ruled out.

Scrotal ultrasonography was a rapid, noninvasive, and low‐cost method and was useful for evaluating the right testicular nodule. However, it had limitations in assessing the contralateral testicle, reinforcing the importance of correlating ultrasonographic findings with gross and histopathological findings. In human medicine, ultrasonography is widely used because of its availability, painless nature, and favorable cost‐effectiveness, and it is employed in the investigation of testicular disorders, including nodules, masses, and enlargement [, ]. Furthermore, intratesticular masses should be considered potentially malignant, making histopathological confirmation necessary [].

Although cytological examinations may serve as a diagnostic tool, their sensitivity for detecting LCTs is limited because these tumors generally grow slowly and rarely cause enlargement. In addition to cystic areas that may compromise sample adequacy, as probably occurred in the present study, most of these tumors are identified incidentally during necropsy [, , , , ].

It is important to highlight that the main differential diagnoses for LCT in NHPs include other primary testicular neoplasms, such as seminoma and Sertoli cell tumor,1–3,5,7 as well as non‐neoplastic conditions such as testicular hyperplasia []. However, the histological features observed in the present case are considered sufficient for definitive diagnosis in both humans [] and other animal species [].

LCTs in NHPs generally exhibit benign behavior and have a favorable prognosis, with orchiectomy being the treatment of choice [, ]. However, surgical intervention was not indicated in the present case because clinical management was directed toward treating the neurological condition presented by the animal. In addition, no evidence of metastasis was observed during necropsy, supporting the generally favorable biological behavior described for this neoplasm.
