 ABSTRACT
 This report serves as the deliverable for Milestone- M3FT-26AN080502017:
Summary of Accomplishments for Online and Offline Analytical Qualification of
Chloride Fuel Salts. The qualification of chloride fuel salts is a critical challenge
for the development and deployment of molten salt reactors (MSRs), requiring
precise control and verification of chemical and isotopic composition to ensure
predictable neutronics, manageable radiological behavior, and safe plant
operation. Currently, there is no universally accepted standard for fuel salt
qualification, particularly for chloride-based systems, which underscores the need
for robust, science-based analytical methodologies. This report presents a
comprehensive evaluation of both online and offline techniques for quantifying
chloride fuel salts, including multielectrode array voltammetry, differential
thermal analysis (DTA) probes, inert gas fusion (IGF) analyzers, and gamma
spectrometry using the Mirion NAIS-2x2 NaI(Tl) detector. The integration of these
methods enables real-time monitoring of actinide concentrations, redox state, and
thermophysical properties, as well as high-precision measurement of impurities
and isotopic composition. The acquisition of advanced instruments such as the
Bruker Leonardo G6 for IGF and the Mirion NaI detector enhances analytical
capabilities, supporting the establishment of operational envelopes and impurity
thresholds. These approaches provide essential data for neutronic impact
evaluation, feedstock documentation, and compliance with nuclear safety
standards. The methodologies developed and validated in this report lay the
groundwork for future standardized protocols, bridging the gap between laboratory
research and commercial reactor operation, and advancing the safe and efficient
deployment of chloride-fueled MSRs.

 1. Introduction
 The development and deployment of advanced molten salt reactors (MSRs) require the ability to
accurately characterize and control the properties of their fuel salts, particularly chloride-based systems.
Chloride fuel salts serve as both nuclear fuel and primary coolant, and their chemical and thermophysical
properties directly impact reactor safety, performance, and regulatory compliance. However, unlike
traditional solid fuels, the qualification of molten salt fuels presents unique challenges: the composition of
liquid fuel can be adjusted throughout its operational life, and its properties are highly sensitive to
impurities, redox state, and temperature.
 Despite the critical role of fuel salt qualification in ensuring predictable neutronic behavior, corrosion
control, and safe operation under both normal and accident conditions, there is currently no universally
accepted standard for the qualification of fuel salts. Existing guidance such as NUREG-2246, provides
frameworks for advanced reactor fuel qualification, but is largely tailored to solid fuels and do not fully
address the dynamic, adjustable nature of liquid fuel salts or the specific requirements for chloride systems
[1]. NUREG-7299 provides guidance on fuel salt qualification for MSRs and reports on salt properties
needed for supporting fundamental safety functions (FSF) under normal and design basis event operations,
as shown in Table 1 and Table 2. While NUREG-7299 does provide insights on methodology for obtaining
these properties, it also outlines the technical difficulties obtaining these properties and understanding the
fuel salt’s chemical and physical behaviors under normal and accident conditions. The absence of
standardized impurity limits, thermophysical property requirements, and monitoring protocols creates
uncertainty for both reactor vendors and regulators, and underscores the need for science-based, regulator-
ready methodologies [2][3].

 To address these gaps, Argonne National Laboratory has been working to develop online and offline
analytical techniques for qualifying chloride fuel salts. Online methods enable real-time, in-situ monitoring
of salt chemistry and properties, supporting rapid detection of impurities and redox imbalances. Offline
techniques provide high-precision measurements of elemental and isotopic composition, supporting
comprehensive characterization and quality assurance. By integrating these analytical approaches, Argonne
aims to establish a robust framework for fuel salt qualification, supporting the development of operational
envelopes, impurity thresholds, and performance standards necessary for the safe and efficient deployment
of chloride-fueled MSRs. For this AFC project, the current techniques under development are voltammetry,
differential thermal analysis, inert gas fusion analysis, and gamma spectroscopy. Other extant measurement
techniques can also provide valuable information regarding the fuel salt characteristics and will likely be a
part of a future qualification approaches. The assessment of alternate tools, however, is outside the scope
of this project.
 A central challenge in chloride fuel salt synthesis is the real-time, in-situ quantification of actinide and
impurity concentrations under high-temperature, corrosive conditions. Among the available analytical
techniques, voltammetry, particularly when implemented as a multielectrode array voltammetry sensor, has
emerged as a uniquely powerful tool. The voltammetry probe enables direct, online measurement of key
parameters such as uranium concentration, redox state, and the presence of corrosion products, all within
the operating reactor or salt processing environment [4]. Recent advances have demonstrated that
voltammetry can provide accurate, quantitative measurements of UCl₃ concentrations in chloride salts
across a wide range of compositions, including the high actinide loadings relevant to molten chloride fast
reactors. By applying advanced correction techniques for cell geometry and solution resistance,
voltammetry probes have achieved preliminary measurement errors as low as 2–4% even at uranium
concentrations exceeding 70 wt% UCl₃, well beyond the range of previous studies.
 Electroanalytical approaches are the only techniques currently capable of providing continuous, real-
time feedback on the chemical state of the salt during both synthesis and reactor operation, enabling rapid
detection of deviations from specification, redox imbalances, or the presence of unreacted precursors. This
capability is essential for ensuring that the salt maintains the required composition and redox environment
to support the FSFs of the reactor: limiting radionuclide release, removing heat, and controlling reactivity
[1][2][4]. Furthermore, the voltammetry probe supports quality assurance during salt production, facilitates
process control during actinide chlorination, and provides a direct link between laboratory-scale synthesis
and reactor-scale operation.
 Emerging techniques are also being developed to address gaps in online and in-situ analysis. The
Differential Thermal Analysis (DTA) probe, for example, is being adapted for use in molten salt
environments to provide real-time measurements of phase transitions and liquidus temperatures. [5] These
thermophysical properties are critical for verifying salt purity, detecting the presence of impurities or

 3

unreacted precursors, and ensuring the salt meets operational specifications. The DTA probe’s ability to
monitor these properties in-situ offers a promising complement to chemical analysis, supporting a more
comprehensive approach to fuel salt qualification [3].
 Offline analytical techniques remain essential for high-precision characterization and quality assurance.
The Inert Gas Fusion analyzer (IGF) is widely used to quantify light elements such as oxygen and nitrogen,
which even at very low levels, can have a profound impact on the chemical stability, corrosion behavior,
and overall performance of molten salt reactor systems. The inert gas fusion analyzer operates by heating
a sample in a graphite crucible under a flow of inert gas (typically helium or argon), causing the sample to
release light elements as gases. These gases are then quantitatively measured using thermal conductivity or
infrared detection. This technique offers several advantages for fuel salt qualification including high
sensitivity and direct quantification, allowing for quality assurance and corrosion and performance
monitoring [6].
 Finally, gamma spectroscopy capabilities for fuel salt qualification are also being pursued. The NaI
(sodium iodide) detector, a gamma spectroscopy tool, enables sensitive detection of radioactive isotopes
and fission products, providing critical information on salt composition and radiological safety. A NaI
detector consists of a sodium iodide crystal doped with thallium [NaI(Tl)], which emits light (scintillates)
when struck by gamma rays. The intensity and energy of the emitted light are proportional to the energy of
the incident gamma photons. This light is then converted into an electrical signal by a photomultiplier tube,
allowing for the construction of a gamma-ray energy spectrum. NaI gamma spectroscopy can detect trace
levels of radioactive contaminants, including activation products or residual radioisotopes from previous
operations. This is essential for verifying the radiological purity of fresh salt batches and for ensuring that
recycled or processed salts meet regulatory and operational standards. By tracking the gamma signatures
of key isotopes, operators can monitor the effectiveness of salt purification processes, detect leaks or cross-
contamination, and support nuclear material accounting and safeguards. Together, these offline methods
support comprehensive post-synthesis and post-operation analysis, validating the results of online
monitoring and informing the development of impurity thresholds and operational envelopes [7].
 Ultimately, the establishment of standardized qualification protocols for chloride fuel salts will require
coordinated research, validation, and regulatory engagement. The methodologies discussed herein represent
essential steps toward closing the most urgent gaps in fuel salt specification, enabling the transition from
laboratory-scale research to commercial reactor operation, and providing the foundation for future global
standards in molten salt fuel qualification.
 2. In-Situ Analysis of Chloride Fuel Salts
 In-situ monitoring of fuel salts is critical for ensuring that the fuel performs as described in the reactor
safety analysis. Salt composition dictates thermophysical and thermochemical properties which in turn
affect FSFs [2]. Key measurements for ensuring FSFs and acceptable reactor fuel lifetime include fissile
material inventory, neutron absorber concentrations, and thermophysical and thermochemical properties.
As part of a larger effort to develop multimodal salt properties measurement approaches, an electrochemical
sensor for dissolved actinides was applied to monitoring of the synthesis for complex high-concentration
uranium chloride salts. This experiment offered a unique environment for sensor evaluation due to the
dynamically changing composition and salt properties, and requirement that the final salt composition be
verified as part of the reactor safety basis.
 Measurements were conducted in UCl3 - (68% NaCl-32% KCl) salts with various concentrations of
UCl3. The UCl3 was synthesized in-situ using a ZnCl2 chlorination process previously described [4,8]. The
composition of the salts is summarized in Table 3. The density and molarity of each salt was calculated
based on the bulk density and the ideal mixing density model [9]. Figure 1 shows images of (a) the final
ternary eutectic salt with 76 wt% UCl3 and (b) samples of salt at various concentrations of UCl3
investigated. These salts were purple in color with increasingly darker shade with UCl3 concentration.

 One advantage of the ZnCl2 salt synthesis route and production of UCl3 directly in the salt mixture is
that the reactant and UCl3 product are electrochemically active and quantifiable with voltammetry. Figure
2 depicts the voltammograms of an NaCl-KCl salt spiked with 10 wt% ZnCl2 and the resulting 14.5 wt%
UCl3-NaCl-KCl salt after contacting the salt with deplete uranium. Before contacting, the voltammogram
showed a distinct set of redox peaks corresponding to the Zn/Zn(II) redox reaction. After contacting the
voltammogram, two sets of redox peaks corresponding to the U/U(III) and U(III)/U(IV) redox reactions
were present. When compared with respect to the approximate oxidation potential of chlorine gas, the
Zn/Zn(II) redox potential lied between that of the U/U(III) and U(III)/U(IV) redox potentials. This implies
that while it is energetically favorable for ZnCl2 to oxidize U to UCl3 it is not energetically favorable to
form undesirable UCl4.

 3. Emerging Techniques for On-line Fuel Analysis
 To provide additional key information regarding the salt composition, Argonne’s in-situ DTA probe
has begun to be optimized for fuel qualification purposes. DTA is an analytical technique that measures the
temperature differences between sample and reference materials that can be used to identify phase transition
under controlled conditions. From the data collected, the elemental composition, liquidus temperature, and
phase stability of the salt can be determined, which are three fuel salt properties addressed in NUREG/CR-
7299 necessary for a comprehensive understanding to qualify the fuel salt for deployment [2]. Typically,
DTA measurements require sample extraction and off-line analyses that involve several manual operations.
This severely limits the frequency at which these measurements can be performed and incurs additional
measurement uncertainty associated with the sample extraction procedures. The compact, in-situ DTA
probe instead automates all these procedures and enables rapid, online analyses.
 The probe was initially designed for a multimodal suite for material control and accounting purposes
in a molten salt medium [11], however, the DTA probe’s ability to monitor salt purity in real time based on
elemental composition, liquidus temperature, and phase stability make this probe optimal for fuel
qualification tool. The probe contains a small crucible to hold the salt sample of interest and a reference
material, shown in Figure 5. Thermocouples are welded to the bottom of the sample crucible and the
reference material to monitor temperature during the tests. Argon gas is used as the cooling gas flowing
through the probe and is automated and monitored through a LabVIEW program. During operation, gas is
flowed through the two Swagelok inlets to evenly and consistently cool the sample and reference at a
controlled rate.

 To optimize the probe, initial testing with non-radiological salts was completed to determine proper
procedures to obtain consistent results. The first phase of testing investigated the LiCl-KCl binary system,
which is extensively defined in the literature, and allowed for lower temperature requirements for operation.
Six different ratios of LiCl-KCl were tested, including the eutectic. Initial testing took place in a Kerr
furnace inside of an inert gas glovebox where oxygen levels were kept under 10 ppm. The set-up of the
system can be seen in Figure 5. Each composition had three samples taken and were cycled through three
times. To obtain a sample, the probe body was fully submerged in a crucible containing a minimum of 200g
of salt at the ratio being tested and left to sit for 30 minutes to ensure the salt sample being tested was with
fresh salt. Before any samples were taken, baseline empty crucible runs were completed to record and
remove any background noise before analyzing any data. Each run took approximately 15-30 minutes to
cool down, depending on temperature range required; it took approximately 15-20 minutes for the probe to
reheat to the starting temperature.
 The temperature differential between the sample and the reference for each sample, as they were cooled,
are shown in Figure 6, and the results are outlined in Table 4. All samples exhibited a eutectic temperature
between 345-355°C. For the LiCl-KCl eutectic composition (green), only one transition temperature was
seen, which was expected. Samples off the eutectic composition had two expected transition temperatures:
the eutectic and the liquidus. The liquidus transition temperature is specific to the LiCl-KCl ratio, while the
eutectic transition temperature is consistent for all compositions. The liquidus temperature is slightly lower
than the value found in other studies, which is most likely due to supercooling. Supercooling is a metastable
state in which a material will stay in a liquid phase when cooled below its melting temperature [13].

 The next phase involved testing with salts containing uranium. For this, we tested one sample of UCl3-
NaCl-KCl (42.9 mol% NaCl, 20.3 mol% KCl, and 36.8 mol% UCl3) that was previously assessed with the
in-situ voltammetry probe. This sample was exactly on the eutectic concentration, so only one transition
temperature was expected to be seen. Prior to testing, the sample crucible on the probe was cleaned out
several times using methanol to ensure no residual LiCl-KCl remained. Before any salt was added, baseline
cooling runs inside of an electro-melt furnace were completed again for further confirmation the probe was
empty, and no LiCl-KCl eutectic transition temperatures were seen. For this phase of testing, the sample
crucible on the probe was manually filled with NaCl-KCl-UCl3 powder, due to the size of the remaining
salt sample. Similar to the first phase of experiments, the probe was set up inside of an electro-melt furnace
in an inert-gas glovebox and heated above 500°C. Each run took approximately 22-27 minutes, with varying
cool down rates from 12-17 minutes and 10 minutes for reheat. The temperature differential was then
plotted versus temperature, as seen in Figure 9.
 . The eutectic transition temperature from literature has been determined to be 470°C [15]. Using the
DTA probe, the eutectic temperature was on average seen at 355°C. Similar to the first phase of the
experiments, the slightly lower measured eutectic temperature is most likely due to the supercooling
phenomena. The degree of supercooling is dependent on the rate of cooling, therefore, higher the cooling
rates increase the degree of supercooling. To determine the degree of supercooling, the cooling rate versus
transition temperature was plotted to determine the zero-rate transition, as shown in Figure 9. The zero-rate
transition temperature was found to be 468.01 °C, which closely matched literature values taken using off-
line measurement approaches.

 The next step with the DTA probe will be to develop a high temperature probe to further validate and

 4. Offline Fuel Analysis
 4.1 Sodium Iodide Detection
 The isotopic composition of fuel salt is a critical parameter for fuel qualification in molten salt reactors,
 as it directly impacts reactor neutronics, radiological behavior, and operational safety. [2,3] To support
 high-precision gamma spectrometric analysis, a Mirion NAIS-2x2 NaI(Tl) LED temperature-stabilized
 scintillation detector was procured and set up for future integration with fuel salt synthesis equipment. This
 advanced detector eliminates peak shift problems, enabling reliable operation even in non-air-conditioned
 environments, with a 7% energy resolution and gain stability within 2% across a wide temperature range (-
 20 to 50 °C). The system features generic efficiency characterization using CANBERRA’s
 ISOCS™/LabSOCS™ software, allowing for quantitative measurements without the need for physical
 calibration sources. Its all-metal housing and robust locking mechanism ensure durability and secure
 deployment. The detector is integrated with an OSPREY MCA, providing an all-in-one high-voltage power
 supply, preamplifier, and digital multichannel analyzer.
 Upon receipt of the Mirion Technologies NaIS-2x2 NaI(Tl) scintillation detector with LED temperature
 stabilization, we first proceeded to install the hardware and set up the software. The NAIS-2x2 detector
 head was plugged into the Osprey multi-channel analyzer, which receives data from the detector and
 operates the proprietary LED temperature stabilization. The NaI(Tl) detector itself would register
 unacceptably high counts per minute without additional shielding from background radiation, so we
 installed it in a lead shield assembly. The shield also serves to increase sensitivity to radiation coming from
 the salt samples. In particular, the Mirion 7419C shield and collimator are designed for the 2x2 size detector.

 14

This shield includes a collimator on the front end to reduce gamma shine from the periphery of the detector
and has 8 mm of lead shielding around the detector. Importantly, it also includes a 1 mm tin filter on the
front to attenuate fluorescence X-rays.
 Once the detector was installed in the shield and connected to the Osprey, we prepared the software
component of the detection system. The Genie 2000 software is the main software suite for operation of the
detector, along with data acquisition and analysis. We additionally connected it to the LabSOCS software
package, which enables the creation of geometry efficiency calibration curves without the need for physical
testing with a calibration source. Although this software eliminates the need for source-based corrections
for counting geometry, it does not eliminate the need for source-based energy calibration. For the initial
energy calibration of the detector to complete its set-up, we chose radium-226, as it has multiple gamma
peaks over a wide energy range. The Table 6 below shows the main peaks useful for calibration. The
calibration was successful, as evidenced by subsequent Ra-226 spectra showing each major peak at its
expected location.

 While the region of energy greater than approximately 400 keV did not show observable peaks, there
are three clearly defined peaks within the 0-250 keV range, at 84 keV, 102 keV, and 180 keV. This limited
selection of peaks is predictable based on the nature of the samples measured, as depleted uranium does not
have substantial gamma activity. Although the detector calibration needs slight readjustment in the 0-250
keV region, we assign the two lower-energy peaks to be X-ray lines of uranium and its thorium daughter
product, and the higher peak as the 185.7 keV U-235 gamma emission [16]. Based on peak intensity, we
chose the 84 keV and 180 keV peaks for further analysis.
 To test if the counting method chosen was able to quantify uranium in the salt samples, the areas under
the 84 keV and 180 keV peaks in each spectrum were determined separately, by summing a subset of
channels and subtracting the same sum from the blank (sample S02, no uranium) spectrum. These peaks
areas were plotted against the known mass of depleted uranium in each sample, as shown in Figure 12.

 17

 Figure 12. Peak areas plotted against uranium mass for X-ray and gamma emissions at 84 keV (orange)
 and 180 keV (blue).

 Figure 12 shows that the peak area for both the X-ray and U-235 gamma peaks are correlated with the
mass of uranium in the sample. This shows that the NaI(Tl) spectrometer can be successfully used to
quantify the amount of uranium in salt samples. By comparing peak area for uranium-specific peaks in a
given spectrum with a spectrum collected on a blank, the total amount of uranium in the sample can be
calculated. This initial test covers the total salt mass range of 75-240 mg and a per-sample uranium mass
range of 7.8-125 mg, demonstrating that the method works even on small samples.
 Based on these successful results, we are confident in moving forward with NaI(Tl) scintillation
detector technology for measuring the uranium content of salt samples. We have also identified several
improvements that will enhance sensor implementation. First, larger samples sizes would yield better
counting statistics and a higher precision in uranium content assay, for similar or shorter count times. This
is a straightforward improvement to adopt going forward. Longer count times would also yield the same
improvement, although this modification would result in slower analysis time. Related to detector
calibration, we identified that our detector calibration using Ra-226 was not as accurate as preferable. While
this does not negatively impact the results of the uranium quantification, we will recalibrate the instrument
using a source like europium-152 or similar. Finally, given the successful results obtained in this proof-of-
concept test, we believe that the NaI(Tl) scintillator will be useful for measuring the isotopic composition
of uranium in salt samples. Future experiments will require uranium samples of different enrichments and
will likely compare the intensities of the X-ray and U-235 gamma peaks.

 4.2 Inert Gas Fusion Analyzer
 Inert Gas Fusion Analysis is a highly reliable and precise technique for determining oxygen content in
molten chloride salts, making it an essential tool for fuel salt qualification. Oxygen contamination in
chloride salts can significantly affect corrosion susceptibility, redox balance, and thermophysical
properties, factors that are critical for the performance and safety of molten salt reactors. The method is a
rapid off-line technique that requires minimal sample preparation, typically involving grinding the salt into
powder and encapsulating it in an inert atmosphere glovebox to prevent contamination. IGF has been
successfully demonstrated for both fluoride and chloride salts, providing critical data for verifying salt
purity and mitigating corrosion risks. Integrating IGF into the fuel qualification workflow enhances the
accuracy of impurity analysis, supports the safe operation of molten chloride reactors, and ensures
compliance with stringent nuclear safety standards [3].
 The Analytical Chemistry Laboratory (ACL) at Argonne National Laboratory already has a LECO IGF
which can only analyze oxygen in fluoride salts. To further the laboratory’s capabilities to analyze chloride
salts, a Brucker G6 Leonardo was purchased this year, shown in Figure 13. The Bruker Leonardo G6 is
designed for high-sensitivity oxygen determination, capable of detecting oxygen concentrations down to
the low parts-per-million range, which is essential for meeting the stringent purity requirements of nuclear-
grade chloride and fluoride salts as it is compatible with both. The system has integrated data management
features supporting rigorous documentation and traceability, which are critical for regulatory compliance
and nuclear quality assurance. Integrating the Bruker Leonardo G6 into the fuel salt qualification process
enhances the accuracy and efficiency of oxygen impurity monitoring, directly supporting the safe operation
of molten chloride reactors and adherence to nuclear safety standards.

 Figure 13. Brucker IGF installed inside of a fume hood

 This year, the G6 Leonardo was purchased, received, and installed in a fume hood in a radiological
laboratory. Extensive work was performed to prepare the lab and the hood for installation. The fume hood
had beryllium remediation performed to ensure that the instrument could easily be installed in the hood
without the industrial hygiene risk. The power to the lab was upgraded to provide a new 60A, 2P, heavy-

 19

duty, NEMA 1 disconnect switch suitable for 480V service for the IGF. The chiller required a new 208 V
to 240 V buck boost transformer. After the instrument and chiller were installed in the hood, the system
and software were set up for analysis. At present, the system has passed leak checks, and blank crucible
and baseline tests have been completed. Preliminary tests with an oxygen and nitrogen sample were
conducted, and the peaks for both species were observed. Transitioning to full exercising and assessment
of the system will be initiated in FY27.

 5. Conclusions
 The successful completion of milestone M3FT-26AN080502017 marks a significant advancement in
the development and integration of online and offline analytical techniques for the quantification and
qualification of chloride fuel salts at Argonne National Laboratory. As molten salt reactors move closer to
deployment, the need for robust, science-based methodologies to characterize fuel salt properties, especially
in chloride systems, has become increasingly urgent. This report demonstrates that a combination of real-
time, in-situ monitoring and high-precision offline analysis is essential for ensuring that fuel salts meet the
stringent requirements for reactor safety, predictable neutronics, and regulatory compliance.
 Online techniques, such as multielectrode array voltammetry and emerging differential thermal analysis
probes, have proven invaluable for monitoring actinide concentrations, redox state, and phase transitions
during salt synthesis and operation. These tools provide rapid feedback, enabling process control and early
detection of deviations from specifications, which are critical for maintaining the fundamental safety
functions of the reactor. Offline methods, including inert gas fusion analysis and gamma spectrometry with
the Mirion NAIS-2x2 NaI(Tl) detector, offer high sensitivity and precision for quantifying impurities,
isotopic composition, and radiological contaminants. The acquisition and commissioning of advanced
instruments like the Bruker Leonardo G6 for IGF and the Mirion NaI detector have expanded Argonne’s
analytical capabilities, allowing for accurate oxygen and nitrogen determination in chloride salts and
reliable uranium quantification in salt samples.
 The integration of these analytical approaches supports the establishment of operational envelopes,
impurity thresholds, and performance standards necessary for the safe and efficient deployment of chloride-
fueled MSRs. Furthermore, the methodologies developed here provide a foundation for future standardized
qualification protocols, bridging the gap between laboratory-scale research and commercial reactor
operation. Continued research, validation, and regulatory engagement will be essential to refine these
techniques and ensure their adoption as global standards for molten salt fuel qualification.
 In summary, the work documented in this report provides a strong basis for the future fuel qualification
and analysis approaches that will be needed to support the next generation of advanced nuclear reactors.
The progress achieved here not only addresses current gaps in fuel salt specifications but also lays the
groundwork for a safer, more reliable, and more efficient nuclear energy future.
