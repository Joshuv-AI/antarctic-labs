Payment Structure Considerations

 Payment Structure Considerations
BESS systems have unique technical and operational characteristics that require
consideration when designing payment structures. Key topics include:

 ❑ Asset classification
 ❑ Compensation mechanism
 ❑ Value stacking
 ❑ Contract/agreement design
 ❑ System efficiency and degradation

                                                                             National Laboratory of the Rockies | 3

 BESS does not fit easily into existing power sector segments of because it can provide services
 across multiple power sector functions and perform dual operations of charging and discharging.

 • BESS is often treated as either power generation, network asset, or load
 • Double charging for injections and withdrawals can present a financial
 barrier for viable projects
 • Some jurisdictions create a new grid asset category with dynamics of
 charging and discharging

 Examples:
 ◦ Texas: amended regulations to allow transmission and distribution utilities to own BESS that sell
 energy or ancillary services without registering as a power generator
 ◦ UK: amended its regulations on power generation to include “energy storage” and “energy
 storage facility” (power generation)
 ◦ Ireland: BESS is now only charged for withdrawing electricity from the grid (charging)
 Source: (Dias Filho et al. 2023); (Department of the Environment, Climate and Communications 2022)

 Example of regulations that enable value stacking:
 The California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) developed categories of services
 BESS can provide and rules for utilities when procuring services including:
 • BESS projects can only provide services at the voltage level to which they are interconnected or
 higher, but not lower;
 • Reliability services are prioritized over non-reliability services and ensure storage cannot
 contract for additional services that would interfere with any obligation to provide reliability
 services;
 • BESS projects must comply with all performance and availability requirements for services it
 provides and that noncompliance penalties be communicated in advance;
 • BESS projects must inform the utility of any services it currently provides or intends to provide;
 and
 • Utilities take measures to prevent double compensation to BESS projects for services provided.

 What Round-trip efficiency is the ratio of the energy charged to the battery to the energy
 discharged from the battery
 • It is the measured as a percentage
 • It can represent the total DC-DC or AC-AC efficiency of the BESS*

 Why For BESS to be economically viable, the cost difference between buying and selling power
 must exceed efficiency losses and operating costs
 Example calculation for BESS round-trip efficiency
 Battery charging efficiency: 95% Round-trip efficiency:
 Battery discharging efficiency: 97% 95% x 97% = 92.2%

 ➢ The BESS must be able to sell power (discharge) for a price
 at least 7.8% more than it pays to charge

*AC-AC efficiency is typically more important to utilities, as they only see the battery’s charging and discharging from the point of
interconnection to the power system, which uses AC.

                                Degradation                                                                       National Laboratory of the Rockies | 10

 What BESS degradation is the natural, gradual loss of a battery’s ability to store and deliver energy
 over time
 • Systems can be expected to degrade 1%-3% of its nameplate capacity per year
 Why The more the system degrades, the less energy it can provide for grid services
 Reduced capacity and shortened operational life can impact the system’s economic viability

 Factors the influence degradation rates:

 ▪ temperature/ambient conditions ▪ current state of charge

 ▪ aging ▪ depth of charge

 ▪ frequency of charging and ▪ ancillary services provided
 discharging cycles ▪ operational life of the battery
