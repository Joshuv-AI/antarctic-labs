Ribonucleotide errors, RNase HII, and ribonucleotide excision repair
Authors: Julianna R Cresti, Lyle A Simmons
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReplicative DNA polymerases are responsible for the high-fidelity duplication of genomes in all organisms. The overall DNA replication process is highly accurate, with deoxyribonucleoside monophosphate (dNMP) errors occurring on the order of 1 in tens of millions of replicated bases. However, DNA polymerases are more prone to misincorporating ribonucleoside monophosphates (rNMPs) in place of their cognate dNMPs, with measurements on the order of 1 in a couple thousand base pairs synthesized. Owing in part to the high concentration of rNTPs relative to dNTPs in vivo, rNMPs may represent the most common nucleotide requiring removal from genomic DNA in organisms spanning all domains of life. Cells employ a set of pathways, including ribonucleotide excision repair (RER), to address abundant genomic rNMPs. Defects in ribonucleotide excision repair result in mutagenesis, neurological disorders, and embryonic lethality. In this review, we discuss the mechanisms for how ribonucleotides are misincorporated by DNA polymerases, how RNase H enzymes recognize and cleave RNA-DNA hybrids, and the process of ribonucleotide excision repair. Further, we review and discuss how ribonucleotide error correction occurs in eukarya, archaea, and bacteria, providing perspectives across a broad range of organisms and ribonucleotide resolution pathways.Keywords: RNase HII, Bacillus subtilis, DNA replication, ribonucleotides, mismatches

Overview of RNA-DNA hybrids

RNA-DNA hybrids can be covalently or non-covalently incorporated in the genome through several different routes. Transcription causes RNA-DNA hybrids to occur in the form of R-loops, non-covalent hybrid structures where the transcript remains base-paired with the template strand and the non-template strand is displaced as ssDNA (Drolet and Brochu 2019). Primers synthesized by primase (DnaG) account for estimates of 20,000–30,000 ribonucleotides covalently incorporated in genomic DNA during each round of replication (Schroeder et al. 2015; McLean et al. 2022). Primase-mediated RNA-DNA hybrids are abundant on the lagging strand and require processing, removal, and replacement with deoxyribonucleoside monophosphates (dNMPs) (Randall et al. 2019; Lowder and Simmons 2023).

The main focus of this review is on single ribonucleotide errors. DNA polymerases misincorporate ribonucleoside monophosphates (rNMPs) covalently during normal DNA replication, causing “sugar errors” (Williams et al. 2016; Zhou et al. 2021). Both replicative and translesion DNA polymerases make sugar errors (Nick McElhinny et al. 2010; Vaisman et al. 2013; Yao et al. 2013; Ordonez et al. 2014). rNMPs, which have C3′-endo sugars, cause DNA to adopt a conformation that closely resembles A-form in regions where they are embedded (Kornberg and Baker 1992; Fedoroff et al. 1993; Ratmeyer et al. 1994). RNA-DNA hybrids play a dichotomous role in cells; while they are essential for cell viability and may be tolerated in certain cases, persistent hybrids can lead to genome instability (Hamperl and Cimprich 2014). For example, RNA primers are necessary for transient priming during leading and lagging strand synthesis (Li et al. 1995; Randall et al. 2018; Cronan et al. 2019; Randall et al. 2019; Lowder and Simmons 2023). However, failure to remove ribonucleotides from DNA can cause DNA strand breaks and mutagenesis, both of which lead to genome instability (Nick McElhinny et al. 2010; Caldecott 2014; Chiu et al. 2014; Williams and Kunkel 2014; Schroeder et al. 2015; 2017; Kouzminova and Kuzminov 2021; McLean et al. 2022).

Misincorporation by DNA polymerases

One of the major avenues by which ribonucleotides become embedded in DNA is erroneous incorporation by DNA polymerases (Nick McElhinny et al. 2010; Clausen et al. 2013; Yao et al. 2013). Strikingly, the most frequent nucleotide error formed during DNA replication is the substitution of an rNMP for its corresponding dNMP. Of course, the primary role of a DNA polymerase is to faithfully synthesize nascent DNA from 5′ to 3′ during replication (Figure 1A) (Kornberg and Baker 1992). The structure of DNA polymerases, which resembles a partially closed right hand, is mostly conserved, in addition to its mechanism, that requires two divalent metal cations (Figure 1B) (Joyce 1997; Steitz 1999; Joyce and Benkovic 2004; Brown and Suo 2011). The fidelity of DNA polymerases and thus replication requires (1) faithful pairing of dNMPs and (2) successful rNTP exclusion from the polymerase active site (Brown and Suo 2011). The movement of the fingers, palm, and thumb subdomains are hypothesized to contribute to fidelity, as nucleotide substrates will perturb the active site conformation differently (Figure 1B) (Rothwell et al. 2005; Tsai and Johnson 2006; Beckman et al. 2008; Joyce et al. 2008; Xu et al. 2009; Brown and Suo 2011). There are six different families of DNA polymerases, with reverse transcriptases (RTs) sometimes treated as a seventh family, but the main replicative polymerases vary between organisms (Filée et al. 2002; Brown and Suo 2011). For example, the main replicases in eukaryotes are members of the B family, whereas the major replicative bacterial polymerases are from the C family—Pol III (DnaE) in Escherichia coli and other gram-negative bacteria and PolC in Bacillus subtilis and most gram-positive bacteria (Filée et al. 2002).

In vivo rNTPs outnumber dNTPs by 10- to 80-fold in organisms like E. coli, Pyrococcus abyssi, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae, although the magnitude varies at different stages of cell growth (Buckstein et al. 2008; Nick McElhinny et al. 2010; Lemor et al. 2018). As a result, both replicative and translesion DNA polymerases often mistakenly incorporate single or even short tracts of rNMPs, and this rate of incorporation corresponds to the role of the DNA polymerase in vivo (Traut 1994; Ferraro et al. 2010; Nick McElhinny et al. 2010; Lemor et al. 2018). Up until recently, accurate quantification of cellular nucleotide pools was limited, but in the past few years, advances in method development have allowed for better separation of nucleotides (Ranjbarian et al. 2022). As a consequence of high rNTP abundance, DNA polymerases evolved several mechanisms to distinguish between appropriate and incorrect substrates (Joyce 1997; Brown and Suo 2011). Still, both low- and high-fidelity DNA polymerases fall along a spectrum of sugar selectivity values, although generally, low-fidelity polymerases are less adept at distinguishing between rNTPs and dNTPs than high-fidelity polymerases due to a more open and flexible active site (Ling et al. 2001; DeLucia et al. 2006; Mizukami et al. 2006; Moon et al. 2007; McCulloch and Kunkel 2008; Brown and Suo 2011).

To reduce rNMP incorporation, most DNA polymerases use Mg2+ over Mn2+, and more importantly, employ a steric exclusion mechanism, often involving a bulky or aromatic amino acid that acts as a molecular sieve. This “steric gate” residue was originally thought to allow for near complete exclusion of ribonucleotides from DNA polymerase active sites (Figure 1B) (Van de Sande et al. 1972; Tabor and Richardson 1990; Ide et al. 1993; Sousa and Padilla 1995; Joyce 1997; Brown and Suo 2011). The amino acid side chain of the steric gate clashes with the C2′ –OH of a rNTP but not the C2′ –H on a dNTP (Joyce 1997; Brown and Suo 2011). In family A polymerases, like DNA polymerase I (Pol I), a conserved glutamate (Glu) residue typically serves this purpose, while family B polymerases use an aromatic residue, including tyrosine (Tyr) or phenylalanine (Phe); RTs, X-, and Y-family polymerases, also coined translesion polymerases for their role in synthesis across bulky lesions or adducts, also have Tyr or Phe residues for limiting rNTPs from their active sites (Silvian et al. 2001; Zhou et al. 2001; DeLucia et al. 2003; Evans et al. 2008; Brown and Suo 2011). In Geobacillus kaustophilus and Bacillus subtilis PolC (Figure 1B), a histidine residue (His) serves this purpose (His1275 and His1268, respectively) (Evans et al. 2008). Interestingly, some polymerases in the X-family have been shown to use a segment of the protein backbone to exclude rNTPs, rather than a single amino acid residue (Brown et al. 2010; Brown and Suo 2011). Alanine (Ala) mutagenesis of the steric gate residue in Sulfolobus sulfataricus DinB homolog (Dbh) bypass polymerase has been shown to diminish its dNTP/rNTP selectivity (DeLucia et al. 2003). Additionally, DNA polymerase μ (Pol μ), which lacks a bulky residue for rNTP exclusion, was shown to have increased the sugar selectivity when a tyrosine residue was introduced by mutagenesis (Ruiz et al. 2003).

Moreover, other nearby amino acid residues help to impose selectivity constraints toward dNTPs (Brown and Suo 2011). Within the Dbh active site, noncovalent interactions, like hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces, between the steric gate Phe12 and surrounding amino acid moieties aid in the correct positioning of the gate to recognize the C2′ –OH of an incoming rNTP for successful steric exclusion (Silvian et al. 2001). The backbone amido group of Phe12 can engage in hydrogen bonding with an incoming dNTP at C3′ –OH, which acts to stabilize binding of the preferred substrate; this is also a common interaction observed in X-family polymerases (Silvian et al. 2001). Similarly, data in E. coli using Pol I Klenow fragment (KF), which has a steric gate at Glu710, implicate additional proximal residues, including Phe762, in positioning the nucleotide C2′ and C3′ –OH groups, as well as facilitating exclusion of a rNTP from the active site through hydrogen bonding (Astatke et al. 1998). Additionally, most polymerase families have a conserved hydrophobic amino acid before the steric gate whose backbone amido group may hydrogen bond with a non-bridging oxygen of the β-phosphate of a nucleotide; other polymerases use a residue with a hydroxyl group side chain to form this interaction with the β-phosphate (Brown and Suo 2011).

Kinetics and enzyme dynamics play an important role in sugar discrimination (Brown and Suo 2011). As described above, rNTPs bind weakly to the polymerase active site, but pre-steady state kinetics show that the slowed rate of incorporation is more critical to their designation as poor polymerase substrates (Astatke et al. 1998; DeLucia et al. 2003; Gardner et al. 2004; Joyce et al. 2008; Brown et al. 2010). Possible explanations for slow incorporation are the lack of accessibility of the 3′–OH and/or distorted binding as a result of the substrate adopting an A-form conformation (Hall and McLaughlin 1991; Egli et al. 1993; Fedoroff et al. 1993; Ban et al. 1994; Wahl and Sundaralingam 2000). In addition, replicative DNA polymerase catalytic subunits, like those for PolC but not E. coli Pol III (DnaE), often have intrinsic 3′-5′ exonuclease or “proofreading” activity, which requires the primer terminus containing the rNMP to be moved or oriented to the exonuclease domain of the enzyme, albeit this activity has been shown to be weak and less efficient for sugar errors (Evans et al. 2008; Williams et al. 2012; 2016). Furthermore, rNTPs have been shown to slow the conformational change of the KF polymerase domain by roughly 100-fold (Joyce et al. 2008). Thus, DNA polymerases use several different mechanisms to try and limit ribonucleotide incorporation. Although these mechanisms are designed to exclude ribonucleotides, DNA polymerases incorporate rNMPs at high rates, which can threaten genome integrity (Nick McElhinny et al. 2010; Reijns et al. 2012; Clausen et al. 2013; Yao et al. 2013; Heider et al. 2017).

Rate of genomic ribonucleotide misincorporation events

In recent years, it has been shown that ribonucleotides are incorporated in genomes at a much higher frequency than initially hypothesized (Nick McElhinny et al. 2010; Yao et al. 2013). Previously, dNMP mismatches were hailed as the most frequent DNA polymerase error during replication in bacteria. In B. subtilis, mutation accumulation lines showed that one mismatch error was made every 15.5 rounds of replication in cells deficient for mismatch repair (MMR) (Schroeder et al. 2016). Strikingly, it has been estimated that E. coli Pol III holoenzyme incorporates up to 2000 rNMPs per one round of chromosomal replication in vitro (Figure 1A) (Yao et al. 2013). Further, in vivo incorporation events range from 200 to 2,200 or more rNMPs per round of chromosomal replication in various bacterial species (Kouzminova et al. 2017; Zatopek et al. 2022).

In vitro estimates for DNA polymerases α (Pol α), ε (Pol ε), and δ (Pol δ) in S. cerevisiae align with bacterial replicases for high levels of incorporation, finding 1 rNMP is stably incorporated by DNA polymerases for every 625, 1,250, or 5,000 dNMPs, respectively (Nick McElhinny et al. 2010). Similarly, Homo sapiens Pol δ was shown to incorporate 1 rNMP for every 2000 dNMPs, which can be extrapolated to millions per round of replication, given the size of the human genome (Clausen et al. 2013). In agreement with this prediction, Mus musculus embryos null for Ribonuclease H2 (see below) also contain millions of rNMPs nested in their genomic DNA (Reijns et al. 2012).

In the absence of Ribonuclease HII (see below), the Thermococcus kodakarensis genome showed 1 rNMP incorporated for every 1,500 base pairs or 1,000 rNMPs incorporated per each round of DNA replication, demonstrating that sugar errors do indeed occur in archaea at levels comparable to those in eukarya and bacteria (Zatopek et al. 2019). Concurrently, Thermococcus species 9°N (9°N) DNA polymerases B (polB, PolB) and polymerase D (polD, PolD) incorporated 1 rNMP for every 2,500 and 1,500 base pairs in vitro, respectively (Gardner and Jack 1999; Greenough et al. 2014; 2015; Heider et al. 2017). More recent studies using high-throughput sequencing for Haloferax volcanii and Thermococcus barophilus demonstrated that approximately 1 rNMP is incorporated in the genome for every 900 base pairs in the absence of Ribonuclease HII (see below); this rate is only slightly reduced in wild type cells (Moalic et al. 2025). Thus, such high levels of ribonucleotide incorporation cause rNMPs to be the most common nucleotide in need of repair in vivo (Nick McElhinny et al. 2010; Reijns et al. 2012; Yao et al. 2013; Williams and Kunkel 2014; Lemor et al. 2018). Given the high rate of ribonucleotide incorporation, there are different types of rNMP errors formed with several implications for the structure and stability of DNA.

Contributions of high-throughput sequencing (HTS) methods to in vivo mapping of ribonucleotide errors

Over the past decade, several high-throughput sequencing techniques have been developed to map and quantify rNMP incorporation in the genome with single-nucleotide resolution, providing a more accurate way to determine the effect of ribonucleotides in vivo. Ribose-seq was developed to detect single misincorporated rNMPs in the genome, determining both their identity and abundance, as well as mapping their location and upstream sequence context (Koh et al. 2015; Balachander et al. 2019; Gombolay et al. 2019). Results in S. cerevisiae deficient for RNase H2 showed increased global rNMP incorporation specifically rCMP and rGMP, with a preference for the leading strand (Koh et al. 2015). This method has been shown to be highly adaptable, as it has been repurposed to study rNMP incorporation in archaeal and algal genomes (El-Sayed et al. 2021; Moalic et al. 2025), as well as the effects of clinical mutations in Aicardi-Goutières syndrome (see below) (Kundnani et al. 2024). Another important method is hydrolytic end sequencing (HydEn-seq), which produces a DNA library via alkaline hydrolysis at the genomic coordinates of misincorporated rNMPs; this method was used to corroborate the proposed division of labor between DNA polymerases in S. cerevisiae based on the rNMP “footprints” produced by mutant polymerases (Clausen et al. 2015; Wanrooij et al. 2017). Polymerase usage sequencing (Pu-seq), which similarly maps rNMPs in the genome, allows investigators to glean more detailed information, including the direction of fork progression, location of replication origins, efficiency of replication origins, frequency of termination, and relative timing of replication (Daigaku et al. 2015). To improve on accuracy and reproducibility, investigators developed embedded ribonucleotide sequencing (emRibo-seq) (Ding et al. 2015; Reijns et al. 2015), in which library preparation is modified by using Ribonuclease HII (see below) treatment rather than alkaline hydrolysis. While the applications are more broad, rare damage and repair sequencing (RADAR-seq) can be used to sequence rNMPs using PacBio Single Molecule Real-Time (SMRT) sequencing to determine their location and frequency (Zatopek et al. 2019; Liman et al. 2026). Most recently, Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) nanopore sequencing has been adapted for detecting single ribonucleotides in the genome using their MinIon device (Grasso et al. 2024). Together, these high-throughput mapping techniques have helped studies quantify the burden imposed by different types of rNMP lesions in the cell and unveil any sequence or strand-specific effects on incorporation in the genome with single-nucleotide or near single-nucleotide accuracy.

Types of single ribonucleotide lesions

Like dNMP errors, rNMP errors vary broadly. While incorporation of a single rNMP alone is considered an error, rNMPs have the propensity to form mismatches and are likely to become damaged, resulting in ribonucleotide lesions (Loeb and Preston 1986; Cheng et al. 1992; Randerath et al. 1992; Malfatti et al. 2017). rNMP residues that participate in a Watson-Crick base pair are considered canonical errors, while mismatched and damaged rNMPs are grouped as noncanonical sugar errors. An aba- sic or AP site, which is missing a nitrogenous base and instead has a C1′ –OH, is an abundant intermediate of base excision repair (BER) and can occur via spontaneous depurination about 10,000 times per genome in mammalian cells each day but is likely to occur frequently with a ribose sugar as well (Lindahl and Nyberg 1972; Randerath et al. 1992; Küpfer and Leumann 2007; Moreira et al. 2008; Malfatti et al. 2017). Further, abasic ribonucleotides have been shown to be generated more slowly and be more stable than DNA AP sites (Küpfer and Leumann 2007). Reactive oxygen species (ROS) can lead to oxidation of all bases, with guanine being the most susceptible, leading to the formation of 7,8-dihydro-8-oxoguanine (8-oxoguanine) (Cooke et al. 2003; Lee and Kang 2019). Oxidatively-damaged lesions may also occur in RNA provided that rNMP incorporation numbers are high (Ordonez et al. 2014; Sastre-Moreno et al. 2014; Cilli et al. 2015). Therefore, misincorporation of these seemingly small lesions could pose challenges for the integrity of the genome if left unresolved.

Genomic consequences of single ribonucleotide lesions

Although relative stability estimates vary, it is clear that DNA is stable over longer periods of time than RNA (Bada et al. 1994; Hofreiter et al. 2001; Willerslev et al. 2004; Allentoft et al. 2012; Matange et al. 2021). Conversely, the C2′ –OH group renders RNA unstable; under alkaline conditions, deprotonation of the C2′ –OH generates a good nucleophile, which can attack the electrophilic phosphorous atom 5′ to the ribonucleotide, forming a 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate, as well as a 5′ –OH (Oivanen et al. 1998; Li and Breaker 1999; Caldecott 2014). The former product is recalcitrant to extension by DNA polymerases or ligation by DNA ligase, resulting in a single-stranded break or a nick in the DNA backbone (Oivanen et al. 1998). If left unresolved, these single-stranded discontinuities can cause both DNA and RNA polymerases to stall and lead to deleterious mutations, including double-stranded breaks (DSBs) and chromosomal rearrangements, upon subsequent rounds of DNA replication (Nick McElhinny et al. 2010; Reijns et al. 2012; Clausen et al. 2013; Cannan and Pederson 2016).

As discussed earlier, the subtle variation in structure, namely the presence or absence of the 2′–OH, between a rNTP and dNTP leads to major structural and functional differences of their respective polymers. The DNA helix must be dynamic, especially during initiation of DNA replication, when the two strands are separated to allow for entry of the replication machinery (Duderstadt et al. 2011). Deformation of the phosphodiester backbone modifies the flexibility of DNA (Privé et al. 1987). Moreover, it has been shown that long double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) is 10%–20% more elastic than double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) (Abels et al. 2005). Ribonucleotides, including single rNMPs, incorporated in dsDNA have been shown to cause B-form DNA to adopt the A-form conformation (Hall and McLaughlin 1991; Egli et al. 1993; Fedoroff et al. 1993; Evich et al. 2016). Data from atomic force microscopy-based single molecule force spectroscopy, molecular dynamics simulations, and nuclear magnetic resonance have shown that rGMP embedded in the genome altered the stretch modulus—but the change in elasticity was more nuanced in that the sequence context may have affected whether the dsDNA could be stretched less or more (Chiu et al. 2014). Thus, incorporated ribonucleotides may impart changes in the biophysical properties of the DNA that could interfere with biological processes that bend or open the DNA.

The accumulation of rNMPs in the genome can lead to replication stress and genome instability. For example, replicative DNA polymerases often stall after the incorporation of one or more rNMPs, but some replicative and translesion DNA polymerases can synthesize DNA with short tracts of sequential rNMPs (Gao et al. 1997; Astatke et al. 1998; 1998; Gardner and Jack 1999; Cases-Gonzalez et al. 2000; Göksenin et al. 2012). Additionally, DNA polymerases usually become slowed or stalled when attempting to replicate over rNMPs in the template strand (Nick McElhinny et al. 2010; Yao et al. 2013). Deletion of Ribonuclease HII (RNase HII) (see below) in B. subtilis results in a 2-fold increase in GC-to-AT transitions (Schroeder et al. 2017). S. cerevisiae defective for RNase H2 (see below) and containing a mutant Pol ε with an increased propensity to incorporate rNMPs experienced stunted growth and cell cycle defects (Nick McElhinny et al. 2010). The cell cycle defects, whichincluded the accumulation and activation of the S-phase checkpointwere demonstrated to be specific to rNMPs misincorporated during DNA replication (Nick McElhinny et al. 2010). In mice, deletion of RNase H2 (see below), increases rNMP abundance in the genome and leads to embryonic lethality (Hiller et al. 2012; Reijns et al. 2012). Similarly, mutations in all three subunits of human RNase H2 (see below) have been linked to Aicardi-Goutières syndrome (AGS), an autosomal recessive disease that results in symptoms that mimic congenital viral infections and ensuing infant mortality, suggesting that unresolved RNA-DNA hybrids pose an immense burden on the human genome (Crow et al. 2006; Rychlik et al. 2010; Kind et al. 2014). Further, high-throughput sequencing data in yeast using orthologous mutations to those observed clinically in AGS patient RNase H2A, H2B, and H2C genes demonstrated higher rNMP abundance in the genome, as well as strand-specific patterns of incorporation (Kundnani et al. 2024). Therefore, persistent rNMPs can result in mutagenesis, disease, and embryonic lethality, underscoring the importance of sugar error correction mechanisms.

Ribonuclease H family enzymes

The Ribonuclease H (RNase H) family is highly conserved and comprises three enzymes: Ribonuclease HI/H1 (RNase HI), Ribonuclease HII/H2 (RNase HII), and Ribonuclease HIII (RNase HIII), with each protein corresponding to the genes rnhA, rnhB, and rnhC in bacteria, respectively (Kanaya and Crouch 1983; Itaya 1990; Itaya et al. 1999; Ohtani et al. 1999).The nomenclature for RNase H proteins differs in each domain of life, with bacterial and archaeal orthologs named using Roman numerals (i.e., E. coli RNase HII) and eukaryotic orthologs using Arabic numerals (i.e., Homo sapiens RNase H2) (Ohtani et al. 1999; Cerritelli and Crouch 2009).As their names imply, each enzyme has endoribo-nuclease activity, allowing for the specific recognition and removal of ribonucleotides from RNA-DNA hybrids commonly formed in vivo (Keller and Crouch 1972). RNase H enzymes are found across all three domains of life, and they are present in other systems, including retroviruses (i.e. HIV) (Doolittle et al. 1989; Crouch 1990; Kohlstaedt et al. 1992; Champoux and Schultz 2009; Tadokoro and Kanaya 2009). Although it is unclear how RNase H genes were attained, it is hypothesized that the rnhA gene was acquired through horizontal gene transfer facilitated by a retrovirus between bacteria and eukaryotes (Ohtani et al. 1999).

Bacterial and eukaryotic genomes often encode two of the three RNase H family members (Figure 2A) (Itaya 1990; Ohtani et al. 1999; 1999). The E. coli genome contains both the rnhA and rnhB genes, while B. subtilis has rnhB and rnhC (Itaya 1990; Ohtani et al. 1999; 1999). Wild strains of B. subtilis encode a third RNase H named RnhP, an rnhA homolog encoded on the 84-kb plasmid pBS32 (Nye et al. 2021). Like E. coli, eukarya often have orthologs of rnhA and rnhB (Ohtani et al. 1999; Cerritelli and Crouch 2009). Interestingly, most archaea only encode rnhB, although exceptions like Halobacterium sp. NRC-1 and Sulfolobus tokodaii also contain rnhA, suggesting that a single RNase H is sufficient for resolution of RNA-DNA hybrids in many archaeal organisms (Ohtani et al. 1999; 2004; 2004; Tadokoro and Kanaya 2009). An early question in the literature probed whether the multiplicity of RNase H genes is important to a single organism, especially given that most archaea and some bacteria only have one, or if each individual gene is essential for cell growth and survival. Studies in bacteria that introduced mutations to each of the rnh genes demonstrated that indeed single deletions of rnhA, rnhB, and rnhC were tolerable, but a concomitant deletion of rnhB and rnhC in B. subtilis was synthetically lethal (Ogawa and Okazaki 1984; Itaya et al. 1999).

The RNase H family may be further subdivided based on sequence similarity and function into two classes: Type 1 and Type 2 RNase H proteins (Ohtani et al. 1999; 1999; Tadokoro and Kanaya 2009). Type 1 RNase H proteins include RNase HI, while the second class encapsulates RNase HII and RNase HIII (Ohtani et al. 1999). RNase HII and RNase HIII have a more recent common ancestor, archaeal RNase HII (Figure 2) (Ohtani et al. 1999). Some speculation suggests that Type 2 proteins emerged through an evolutionary process called neutral drift, which involves the accumulation of random mutations that lead to enzymatic differences but do not impact the overall function of the enzyme (Ohtani et al. 1999). Strikingly, biochemical studies show that RNase HI and RNase HIII are functionally similar, exhibiting the same metal preferences and contributing to Okazaki fragment maturation during lagging strand replication and resolution of R-loops during transcription (Figure 2A) (Nowotny and Yang 2006; Tadokoro and Kanaya 2009; Randall et al. 2018).

RNase H proteins have common structural elements, including a conserved catalytic core, called the RNase H-fold, and an active site composed of acidic residues (DEDD/DEDE); the β-sheet core is composed of five β-strands, three of which are antiparallel and two parallel, and a variable number of proximal alpha-helices (Katayanagi et al. 1990; Yang et al. 1990; Nowotny et al. 2005; Nowotny and Yang 2006; Nowotny et al. 2007; Majorek et al. 2014). Mechanistically, both classes of endoribonucleases use acidic residues to position and coordinate divalent metal ions, like Mg2+, to facilitate catalysis (Katayanagi et al. 1990; Yang et al. 1990; Steitz and Steitz 1993; Yang and Steitz 1995; Nowotny and Yang 2006). Specifically, they activate a water molecule to serve as the nucleophile and cleave the bond just upstream of the ribonucleotide that bridges the 3′ O of the previous nucleotide to the P of the 5′ phosphoryl group, also referred to as the P–O3′ bond (Haruki et al. 1998). In the absence of metals, these enzymes have been shown to be inactive, demonstrating that the metal ions are necessary cofactors (Davies et al. 1991; Katayanagi et al. 1993; Nowotny and Yang 2006; Randall et al. 2018). Additionally, active site mutations have been shown to reduce or completely abrogate activity, suggesting that they are required for enzyme function (Katayanagi et al. 1993; Randall et al. 2018).

Of all three family members, RNase HII is the only protein conserved in all domains of life, which suggests its unique importance to maintaining genome integrity. As mentioned earlier, the catalytic core and general mechanism are conserved across RNase H family members, but differences in the active site geometry dictate the substrate repertoire of each enzyme (see below).

Ribonuclease H mechanism

Although the preferred RNA-DNA hybrid substrate of each RNase H family member varies, both substrate binding/recognition and the catalytic mechanism are mostly conserved between enzyme types (Hyjek et al. 2019). Two divalent metal ions are required cofactors for the phosphoryl transfer reaction carried out by RNase H proteins, and there is a preference for Mg2+ ions (Yang et al. 2006). They each form coordinate covalent bonds, some with the residues of the DEDD/DEDE motif, that facilitate catalysis and release of the product (Yang et al. 1990; Ohtani et al. 1999; Tadokoro and Kanaya 2009). More specifically, the first Mg2+ ion positions the water molecule for attack of the electrophilic phosphorous atom of the scissile phosphate in an SN2-like mechanism (Yang et al. 1990; Ohtani et al. 1999; Tadokoro and Kanaya 2009). The second Mg2+ ion has been shown to stabilize the leaving group and simultaneously destabilize the enzyme-substrate complex to promote product release (Yang et al. 1990; Ohtani et al. 1999; Tadokoro and Kanaya 2009). In structures designed to mimic the reaction intermediate, the metal ions appear to move closer to one another, potentially bringing the nucleophile in close proximity to the scissile phosphate, but after incision, they end up farther apart than in their initial and intermediate positions (Yang et al. 1990; Ohtani et al. 1999; Tadokoro and Kanaya 2009). Under alkaline conditions, a water molecule can be deprotonated to form a hydroxide ion, which is coordinated by that first Mg2+, serving as the nucleophile (Yang et al. 1990; Ohtani et al. 1999; Tadokoro and Kanaya 2009). The nucleophilic attack generates a pentacovalent intermediate at the 5′ phosphate, which can be resolved by releasing a 3′ oxyanion as the reaction leaving group, thus cleaving the P–O3′ bond (Lahiri et al. 2003). Alternatively, Type 1 RNase H enzymes likely use a mechanism that employs general acid-base catalysis (Nakamura et al. 1991; Kanaya et al. 1996). In this one-metal mechanism, a conserved His residue, which is missing from Type 2 RNase H proteins, can deprotonate a water molecule, generating a nucleophile (Oda et al. 1993). If this mechanism were implemented by Type 2 enzymes, a nearby amino acid with a carboxyl side chain might act as a general base (Bastock et al. 2007).

RNase H proteins bind the minor groove of an RNA-DNA hybrid (Nowotny et al. 2007). The B-form DNA portion of the hybrid is recognized by one region of the enzyme, while another houses the active site and binds the RNA portion by interacting with the protruding 2′ –OH groups (Nowotny et al. 2005; 2007; Rychlik et al. 2010). The DNA-binding groove contains both a phosphate-binding pocket, which anchors the substrate, and a channel in a region called the basic protrusion, whose residues constrain RNA from entry (Tadokoro and Kanaya 2009; Rychlik et al. 2010). The basic protrusion consists of several conserved positively charged amino acids (Kanaya et al. 1991; Tadokoro and Kanaya 2009). Studies in E. coli RNase HI using alanine-scanning mutagenesis demonstrated the importance of these residues for substrate binding (Kanaya et al. 1991). The basic protrusion may be one of the first points of substrate contact with the enzyme to promote binding and conversion to the enzyme–substrate complex (Nowotny et al. 2005). In human and E. coli RNase HI, the RNA portion of the hybrid binds in a proximal groove, separated from the DNA-binding groove by a highly conserved ridge of three Asparagine (Asn) and/or Glutamine (Gln) residues (Nowotny et al. 2005). Additional contacts in this region between side chains and the protein backbone contribute to recognition of the 2′ –OH groups (Tadokoro and Kanaya 2009). The location of the binding domains is not conserved between Type 1 and Type 2 RNase H proteins, and Type 2 enzymes lack a basic protrusion (Tadokoro and Kanaya 2009). Structural data have shown that substrate binding in both Type 1 and Type 2 RNase H proteins has little to no effect on the conformation of the enzyme (Nowotny et al. 2005; Nowotny and Yang 2006; Nowotny et al. 2007; Rychlik et al. 2010). Despite utilizing similar biochemical mechanisms, each of the RNase H family members display different substrate preferences (Randall et al. 2018; 2019; Schroeder et al. 2023). RNase HII alone can repair single rNMPs misincorporated in duplex DNA, with the mechanism discussed below.

Ribonuclease HII/H2 and substrate recognition

RNase HII/H2 is the sole RNase H protein responsible for the repair of single rNMPs, which are incorporated in the genome when DNA polymerases make errors (Nick McElhinny et al. 2010; Yao et al. 2013). It is also the only RNase H protein conserved throughout all domains of life. In prokaryotes, RNase HII consists of a single polypeptide, while eukaryotic RNase H2 is heterotrimeric (Ohtani et al. 2000; Jeong et al. 2004; Crow et al. 2006; Rychlik et al. 2010; Figiel et al. 2011). The catalytic subunit, which is homologous to prokaryotic RNase HII (rnhB), is RNase H2A (RNASEH2A) (Jeong et al. 2004; Crow et al. 2006; Cerritelli and Crouch 2009; Figiel et al. 2011). It has been suggested that the auxiliary subunits RNase H2B (RNASEH2B), which has a proliferating cellular nuclear antigen (PCNA) binding site, and RNase H2C (RNASEH2C) aid in interaction with the replication and repair machinery (Chon et al. 2009).

Like other RNase H family members, the crystal structure of RNase HII from the gram-negative bacterium Thermotoga maritima reveals a predominantly positively charged region between the catalytic and C-terminal domains that binds the substrate in the minor groove, accommodating the mostly A-form rNMP-containing strand (Kanaya et al. 1991; Rychlik et al. 2010). The N-terminal catalytic domain contains the ubiquitous RNase H-fold, but differently than Type 1 RNase H enzymes, one or two κ-helices separate β-strands two and three (Rychlik et al. 2010). Type 2 proteins have an additional point of differentiation from Type 1 enzymes—a helix-loop-helix domain in the C-terminus (Rychlik et al. 2010). The DNA-only strand mostly contacts the C-terminal domain through noncovalent interactions whereas the rNMP-containing strand is primarily interacting with the acidic active site in the catalytic core (Rychlik et al. 2010). The protein-DNA interface comprises seven nucleotides of the hybrid substrate (Rychlik et al. 2010). Several highly conserved lysine residues (Lys47, Lys122, and Lys138) participate in electrostatic interactions with the phosphodiester backbone of the substrate (Rychlik et al. 2010). A space-filling model for the AlphaFold prediction of the structure of B. subtilis RNase HII with the same hybrid substrate and labeled amino acid residues is shown (Figure 2B). Several bacterial species, including B. subtilis and Bacillus stearothermophilus, do not have the same extended C-terminal region as T. maritima but instead have an elongated N-terminus (shown in cyan in Figure 2B) (Tadokoro and Kanaya 2009; Permanasari et al. 2013). Data have indicated a possible role for this region in substrate-binding, as partial truncation of this region in B. stearothermophilus RNase HII did not significantly change the overall structure but decreased RNase H activity, binding affinity, and thermal stability (Muroya et al. 2002; Permanasari et al. 2013).

Although RNase HII binds RNA-DNA hybrid substrates in the minor groove (Nowotny et al. 2005, 2007), several additional residues in T. maritima RNase HII contribute to a refined specificity or precision even, with which to recognize the junction between a single rNMP and its surrounding dNMPs. For example, a conserved arginine residue (Arg22) wedges itself into the substrate, so that its side chain is parallel with the minor groove (Rychlik et al. 2010). Arg22 is essential to the glycine-arginine-glycine (GRG) motif, also formed by Gly21 and Gly23, which bulges into the minor groove and additionally forms three hydrogen bonds with its backbone amido groups that aid in positioning the 2′ –OH of the rNMP (Figure 2B); the GRG motif also stabilizes a glutamate (Glu19) residue in the active site during catalysis (Rychlik et al. 2010). In addition, an invariant tyrosine (Tyr163) residue located in the helix-loop-helix motif of the C-terminal domain forms a hydrogen bond with the 2′ –OH of the embedded rNMP (Figure 2B) (Rychlik et al. 2010). Further, assuming the rNMP is at the +1 position, Tyr163 exhibits pi-pi stacking with the deoxyribose sugar of the +2 dNMP, but this stacking interaction is not as efficient as it would be in the absence of the 2′ –OH (Rychlik et al. 2010). Thus, this forces the dNMP upwards and distorts the backbone (Rychlik et al. 2010). Together, Tyr163 and the GRG motif form the junction-sensing module required for recognition and cleavage of single rNMPs in DNA (Figure 2B) (Ohtani et al. 1999; 2008; Rychlik et al. 2010). RNase HIII, the other Type 2 enzyme, lacks both an invariant Tyr residue as well as the central Arg residue of the GRG motif, perturbing its ability to precisely recognize the single rNMP-DNA junction (Rychlik et al. 2010). The corresponding residue numbers are labeled in the AlphaFold prediction of the structure of B. subtilis RNase HII in Figure 2B.

The observed catalytic mechanism is conserved with that of other RNase H proteins, but the coordination spheres of the required two divalent metal cations differ (Nowotny and Yang 2006; Rychlik et al. 2010). Specifically, in the T. maritima structure, metal ion A forms coordinate covalent bonds with the side chain of Asp18, the carbonyl of the backbone of Glu19, the pro-Sp nonbridging oxygen of the scissile phosphate, the pro-Rp nonbridging oxygen of the phosphate at +2 (made possible by the deformation induced at this position via Tyr163), and two water molecules—one of which serves as the nucleophile when brought into closer proximity to the phosphate group at the junction (Rychlik et al. 2010). On the other hand, metal ion B is coordinated by three of the DEDD motif side chains, the oxygen of the 3′ leaving group, and the pro-Sp nonbridging oxygen (Rychlik et al. 2010).

Ribonucleoside monophosphate specificity

Several groups have investigated the biochemical activity of RNase HII from a variety of organisms on rNMP errors in vitro. Work using E. coli RNase HII demonstrated endoribonuclease activity on rGMP paired with dCMP, as well as on an abasic site, depending on the reaction conditions, and an 8-oxoguanine lesion with a ribose sugar (Malfatti et al. 2019; Cresti and Simmons 2026). In comparison, RNase HII from the archaeon P. abyssi showed RNase H activity on the Watson-Crick base pair rG:dC and mismatched rNMPs but not damaged rNMP lesions in vitro (Woese et al. 1990; Malfatti et al. 2019; Reveil et al. 2023). Yeast and bacterial RNase HII have also been shown to be active on mismatched rNMP errors in vitro and in vivo (Shen et al. 2011; Malfatti et al. 2017; 2019; Cresti and Simmons 2026). Further, additional in vitro assays revealed that RNase HII from B. subtilis and RNase H2 from humans, S. cerevisiae, and M.musculus lack activity on damaged rNMP lesions, similar to P. abyssi (Shen et al. 2011; Malfatti et al. 2017; 2019; Cresti and Simmons 2026). Instead, in humans, AP endonuclease 1 (APE1) was shown to be able to incise ribonucleotide lesions both in vitro and in vivo by making an incision on the 5′ end of the oxidized or AP rNMP (Malfatti et al. 2017). In concert, these data suggest that the substrate scope of bacterial, archaeal, and eukaryotic RNase HII/H2 may differ due to loss of activity during evolution. Further, there are differences between the pathway that is initiated by RNase HII in different domains (see below).

Ribonucleotide excision repair

Ribonucleotide excision repair (RER) is the canonical repair pathway dedicated to the resolution of single rNMPs misincorporated in DNA (Williams et al. 2016). In S. cerevisiae, cell extracts from strains with a deletion of the catalytic subunit of RNase H2 (RNH201) displayed lower activity on rNMPs embedded in DNA; deletion of flap endonuclease 1 (RAD27), which had been previously implicated in Okazaki fragment maturation, prompted similar results (Rydberg and Game 2002). Together, these results were the first to identify two of the proteins involved in repair of single sugar errors. Additional studies probed the remainder of the pathway using in vitro reconstitution with yeast proteins (Sparks et al. 2012). RER has been studied in several organisms to date, and we present a comparison of this process between eukaryotes and prokaryotes below.

Eukarya

In yeast, mice, and humans alike, the same proteins, whose genes are listed in Table 2, are required to carry out RER (Sparks et al. 2012). RNase H2 makes an incision on the 5′ side of the rNMP, generating a nick with the rNMP downstream from the nick and a free 3′ –OH (Figure 3A) (Rydberg and Game 2002; Sparks et al. 2012; Williams and Kunkel 2014). PCNA is then recruited to the site of the lesion by RNase H2, which contains a PCNA-binding domain, also called the PIP-box motif (Kelman 1997; Bubeck et al. 2011). It has been shown in mammalian cells that this domain, which contains important residues F300 and F301, is essential for RNase H2 localization to the site of replication (Bubeck et al. 2011). Clamp loader Replication Factor C (RFC), is a heteropentamer with homology to AAA+ proteins, activates and loads PCNA onto the DNA through an ATP hydrolysis cycle (Yao and O’Donnell 2012). Pol δ, the main lagging strand DNA polymerase in eukaryotes, is recruited to the site of the lesion, through interaction with dsDNA facilitated by PCNA (Figure 3A) (Downey et al. 1990; Li et al. 1995; Sparks et al. 2012). It has been shown that PCNA helps to stimulate RER, but the absence of the PIP-box motif of RNase H2 did not impact RER completion in vitro (Sparks et al. 2012; Chon et al. 2013). Once Pol δ is loaded, it can extend from the 3′ –OH, displacing the downstream DNA (Figure 3A) (Williams and Kunkel 2014). This process, called strand displacement synthesis, creates a 5′ flap that contains the rNMP, which is then cleaved by a flap endonuclease (Williams and Kunkel 2014). Pol ε is also able to perform this step, albeit less efficiently (Garg et al. 2004; Sparks et al. 2012). In eukarya, FEN1 is the most efficient protein to cleave the ribonucleotide-containing flap internally, leaving behind the nick and releasing the ssDNA with the rNMP (Figure 3A) (Rydberg and Game 2002; Garg et al. 2004; Sparks et al. 2012). Exonuclease 1 (Exo1) can substitute here and allow for complete RER, but it is about 2–3 fold slower (Tran et al. 2002; Stith et al. 2008; Sparks et al. 2012). Further, Dna2, a nuclease that enacts repair on longer 5′ flaps during Okazaki fragment maturation, is unable to substitute for FEN1, as its product has been shown to require additional downstream processing by FEN1 (Burgers 2009; Sparks et al. 2012). Interestingly, Pol δ can engage in “idling,” or using its 3′-5′ exonuclease activity, when FEN1 activity is low or absent (Garg et al. 2004). The nick in the dsDNA backbone is repaired by DNA ligase I, whose PCNA-binding domain helps to localize ligase to the repair site, forming a new phosphodiester linkage (Figure 3A) (Montecucco et al. 1998; Vijayakumar et al. 2007; Sparks et al. 2012). PCNA binds both FEN1 and ligase to recruit them to the repair site, although it has only been shown to increase the efficiency for ligase (Li et al. 1995; Montecucco et al. 1998; Vijayakumar et al. 2007). In vivo data in S. cerevisiae suggest that the MMR pathway can repair single mismatched rNMPs in the genome (Shen et al. 2011). Moreover, S. cerevisiae, mouse, and human RNase H2 appear to be inactive on damaged rNMPs, specifically a ribose moiety with an abasic site (rOH) or 8-oxoguanine (r8oG), although human RNase H2 is still able to bind the lesions. Interestingly, human APE1 has demonstrated activity on incising damaged rNMPs in vitro and in vivo, suggesting the involvement of APE1 in an additional pathway dedicated to the repair of a subset of single rNMP lesions which differs from its role in canonical BER (Figure 3B) (Malfatti et al. 2017).

When canonical RER is impaired or absent, Topoisomerase 1 (Top1), whose canonical role is to relieve tension in the genome from supercoiling during transcription, has been shown to initiate a compensatory pathway for repair of ribonucleotides (Figure 3C) (Sekiguchi and Shuman 1997; Kim et al. 2011; Williams et al. 2013). The action of Top1 at rNMPs comes at the cost of error-prone removal, as deletion of RNase H2 in S. cerevisiae led to an observed increase in 2–5 bp deletions in short tandem repeats (STRs) (Nick McElhinny et al. 2010). Similarly, the same mutations were observed during periods of high transcription, which require Top1 (Lippert et al. 2011; Takahashi et al. 2011). Top1 cleaves 3′ to the rNMP-containing strand, forming a nick (Sekiguchi and Shuman 1997). Although the 3′-phosphotyrosyl linkage formed by Top1 is highly stable in the cell, an rNMP near the scissile phosphate provides a nearby nucleophile, the 2′ –OH, which can attack the Top1-DNA intermediate to produce a 5′ –OH and 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate (Petersen and Shuman 1997; Sekiguchi and Shuman 1997; Kim et al. 2011). These products causes Top1 to dissociate, and the ends must be processed for ligation (Kim et al. 2011). If Top1 initiates repair on an rNMP in a STR, the downstream end processing leads to short deletions, as mentioned earlier (Kim et al. 2011; Williams et al. 2013). In fact, the consequences caused by RNase H2 deficiency in yeast were alleviated by concomitant deletion of Top1 (Williams et al. 2013).

Archaea

Archaeal RNase HII consists of a single catalytic monomer with homology to the eukaryotic RNase H2 catalytic subunits (Lai et al. 2000; Muroya et al. 2001). Although the structure of RNase HII (rnhB) was characterized in the early 2000s, the RER pathway has only been explored more recently, over the past decade (Heider et al. 2017). Many of the critical findings have been made in the archaeon Thermococcus kodakarensis. Archaea employ the same conserved RER mechanism as eukaryotes, in which RNase HII (rnhB) first recognizes and nicks the dsDNA 5′ to the rNMP (Figure 4A) (Heider et al. 2017). Kinetic studies have shown that this process is fast for each of the rNMPs, but the rate of turnover is markedly reduced, suggesting that RNase HII might continue to bind the site of repair after cleavage in order to protect it or act as a recruitment platform for other RER proteins (Heider et al. 2017). Interestingly, from a kinetic standpoint, 9°N RNase HII displays a preference for pyrimidines (Heider et al. 2017). Next, PolB, which has been shown to be the most accurate of the two DNA polymerases, extends from the 3′ –OH of the nick by stranddisplacement synthesis and generates a 5′ flap (Figure 4A)(Greenough et al. 2014; 2015; Heider et al. 2017). PolD, which is unique to archaea and shown to be essential, is the major replicative polymerase for both leading and lagging strand DNA replication. For this polymerase DP1 is the proofreading subunit, whereas DP2 carries out catalysis (Cann et al. 1998; Ishino et al. 1998; Greenough et al. 2015; Greci and Bell 2020). However, PolD has weak strand displacement synthesis activity, but might be able to substitute for PolB (Figure 4B) (Greenough et al. 2014; 2015; Heider et al. 2017; Reveil et al. 2023). Fen1 (fen) cleaves the 5′ flap, which contains the rNMP, followed by DNA ligase (lig) to complete repair (Figure 4A) (Nakatani et al. 2000; Heider et al. 2017). Similar to eukaryotes, PCNA and RFC may be used in this pathway, although in vitro they were only required when salt concentrations exceeded 100 mM (Ladner et al. 2011; Heider et al. 2017). Similar to what has been observed in eukaryotes, archaeal RNase HII can bind PCNA in three different modes via the PIP-box motif (Bubeck et al. 2011). The motif inserts into a hydrophobic groove near the C-terminus of PCNA, stimulating activity of RNase HII on Okazaki fragments and single ribonucleotide errors (Bubeck et al. 2011). RNase HII was also shown to bind PCNA in P. abyssi, suggesting conservation among archaeal species (Meslet-Cladiere et al. 2007). In the absence of strand displacement synthesis, Fen1 has been shown to degrade the 5′ flap in vitro, after which PolB or PolD can complete repair synthesis (Figure 4B) (Reveil et al. 2023). Both the canonical and noncanonical P. abyssi RER pathways have been shown to repair rA:dC in vitro (Reveil et al. 2023). Notably, P. abyssi RNase HII has little to no activity on an rG:dA mismatch in vitro (Malfatti et al. 2019). In vitro assays demonstrate that P. abyssi RNase HII can bind damaged rNMP lesions, including rOH:dC and r8oxoG:dC but remains inactive for incision, like eukaryotic RNase H2 (Malfatti et al. 2017; 2019). Currently no redundant pathways have been described for repair of misincorporated rNMPs in archaea, unlike the backup pathways described in eukarya.

Bacteria

Bacterial RER was first explored in E. coli, when it was discovered that the Y11A mutation of the steric gate residue in the translesion polymerase DNA polymerase V (umuC, Pol V) led to increased spontaneous mutagenesis in vitro but not in vivo (McDonald et al. 2012; Vaisman et al. 2013; 2014). It was suspected that other proteins, like RNase HII (rnhB), could somehow repair the misincorporated rNMPs that persisted (Vaisman et al. 2014). In gram-negative E. coli and gram-positive B. subtilis, RNase HII introduces the same 5′ nick relative to an rNMP, initiating the repair pathway (Figure 5A) (Vaisman et al. 2014; Schroeder et al. 2017; Randall et al. 2018). During repair synthesis, Pol I (polA), whose processivity is aided by the β-clamp, extends from the 3′ –OH of the nick, displacing the downstream DNA containing the rNMP lesion and creating a flap (Figure 5A) (Vaisman et al. 2014; Schroeder et al. 2017). E. coli Pol I encodes its own FEN domain with 5′ flap endonuclease activity and thus does not require a separate FEN protein for the repair pathway; in contrast, the FEN domain of B. subtilis Pol I has weak 5′ flap endonuclease activity, so FEN (fenA) is likely required to process the 5′ flap generated via strand displacement synthesis (Figure 5A)(Lowder and Simmons 2023). After the completion of repair synthesis and flap removal, DNA ligase (ligA) seals the phosphodiester backbone (Figure 5A) (Vaisman et al. 2014; Schroeder et al. 2017).

RNase HII (dark purple) is recruited to the site of the error and makes an incision 5′ to the rNMP (red). The resulting nick in DNA can be sealed by LigA (light blue). (B) In bacteria, mispaired rNMPs could also be substrates for MMR, although efficiencies vary, and this process is initiated by MutS (blue) (Shen et al. 2011). (C) If RNase HII is mutated or deleted, NER can substitute (UvrC is shown in orange) but often leads to increased genomic instability because of mutagenic resynthesis by translesion polymerases (brown) (Vaisman et al. 2013; 2014; Schroeder et al. 2017).

Interestingly, in vitro and in vivo assays have revealed the ability of E. coli RNase HII to repair single mismatched rNMPs, namely rG:dA and rG:dT, suggesting a potential role of RER in the resolution of mismatches in the cell (Shen et al. 2011; Malfatti et al. 2019; Cresti and Simmons 2026). Further, in vivo data demonstrated that MutS, which is the first protein in the MMR pathway, can also enact repair on rG:dT lesions, although the canonical MMR machinery repairs mismatches incurred in duplex DNA (Shen et al. 2011). Alternatively, rC:dA was repaired in vivo by RNase HII but not efficiently by MMR (Shen et al. 2011). Moreover, MutS was unable to repair a mismatched rNMP in a tract of 5 rNMPs in vivo (Shen et al. 2011). Interestingly, B. subtilis RNase HII was inefficient at repairing both rG:dC and rG:dT in vitro, and we speculate that rGMP might be bound more weakly by the enzyme (Cresti and Simmons 2026). Together, these observations imply that bacteria might employ MMR as a secondary pathway in the absence of RER for the repair of a subset of single mismatched rNMPs (Figure 5B). Additionally, E. coli RNase HII has been shown to incise damaged rNMPs, including rOH and r8oG, in vitro, which contrasts with what has been shown for B. subtilis RNase HII, archaeal RNase HII, and eukaryotic RNase H2 (Malfatti et al. 2017; 2019; Cresti and Simmons 2026). Instead, human APE1, which typically participates in BER, repairs abasic and oxidized rNMPs through a separate pathway from both BER and RER, suggesting divergence in the substrates of RNase HII/H2 within the bacterial domain and more broadly between the domains Eukarya, Archaea, and Bacteria (Woese et al. 1990; Malfatti et al. 2017; 2019).

It has been shown in E. coli that the RER machinery interacts directly with RNA polymerase (RNAP), a surprising finding, especially considering conservation of the proteins in eukaryotes and the existing literature on eukaryotic RER proteins and their interactions with different components of the replisome (Hao et al. 2023). This work addressed the prevailing question of how RNase HII actually surveils the genome to find rNMP errors, suggesting that RNase HII uses RNAP as a vehicle to search for and locate ribonucleotide errors to repair them in real time via transcription-coupled RER (TC-RER) (Hao et al. 2023).

In the absence of RER, nucleotide excision repair (NER) may enact repair of single misincorporated rNMPs in bacteria, albeit leading to mutagenic resynthesis (Figure 5C) (Vaisman et al. 2013, 2014; Wozniak and Simmons 2022). UvrA, which is stimulated by binding ATP, searches and then recruits UvrB to the site of an error. Next, UvrC makes two incisions in the strand containing the error and UvrD releases the product, leaving a gap that is approximately 11 nucleotides long (Lin and Sancar 1990, 1992; Wozniak and Simmons 2022). A translesion DNA polymerase (i.e., DnaE in B. subtilis or a Y-family DNA polymerase in E. coli) can fill this gap, although due to its lower fidelity, it has the propensity to make errors increasing mutagenesis (Vaisman et al. 2013; 2014; Schroeder et al. 2017).

Summary

Due to the high abundance of rNTPs in cells, rNMPs are often misincorporated by DNA polymerases during DNA replication by evading conserved exclusion mechanisms. These single rNMPs are repaired by RNase HII/H2 in all organisms through the RER pathway. Although the RER pathway is mostly conserved across domains, there are notable differences in the types of rNMP lesions that are resolved, which is especially interesting given that all the substrates have a 2′ –OH. In addition, the compensatory pathways for repair of rNMPs in the absence of functional RER do not resemble one another but can remove rNMPs, generating different mutational signatures. These differences in the repair repertoire of various RNase HII/H2 enzymes may be explained by divergence in RNase HII/H2 and RER components between organisms across the three domains of life. Future work will be necessary to determine if there are other compensatory pathways used to detect and repair ribonucleotide errors in the genome or if ribonucleotide insertion has a clear biologically beneficial regulatory function.
