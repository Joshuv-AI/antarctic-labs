Temporal reorganization of maternal care during early life stress in mice
Authors: Hannah Jo, Eman Ali, Catherine Jensen Peña
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaternal separation is a widely used early-life stress (ELS) paradigm in rodents. Disrupted maternal care by separation of pups from the dam for several hours per day across neonatal and juvenile development has been found to alter hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis function, molecular and circuit-level brain development and function, and behaviors associated with anxiety, depression, and cognition. However, dams have been found to temporarily increase their care towards pups upon reunion — typically associated with positive outcome measures — calling into question whether this increase in care compensates for care lost during separation. Using a mouse model of ELS, we quantified maternal home cage behaviors from postnatal day P2-16 across three observation periods per day (prior to maternal separation, immediately following reunion, and several hours following reunion), with maternal separation beginning on P10. Four composite variables of home cage behavior frequency were created: total dam-pup contact, rough contact, no contact, and dam self-care. Consistent with previous reports, we found a transient increase in care upon reunion, along with an increase in rough contact and a decrease in dam self-care. We then calculated geometric area-under-curve across the day and found that the increase in total contact among ELS litters does not result in overall more care, as indicated in previous publications, but rather a temporal reorganization of care. These findings provide important nuance to understand how maternal separation disrupts offspring care.Keywords: Maternal separation, Early-life stress, Early life adversity, Maternal behavior

Introduction

The extent and type of care that offspring receive in early life can affect mental and physical health across the lifespan. In humans, adverse childhood experiences are associated with increased risk for mental health syndromes including depression, anxiety, and addiction, and physical health challenges including cardiovascular disease, metabolic disease, and even immune disorders and cancer (Felitti et al., 1998; Gilbert et al., 2015; Hughes et al., 2017). Rodent models of early-life stress (ELS) have been a useful tool for understanding the consequences of the early environment on offspring wellbeing and neurodevelopment. Maternal separation is a widely used ELS paradigm which involves separating pups from their dams for several hours per day (Plotsky and Meaney, 1993; Tractenberg et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2024; Pryce et al., 2005). This separation is thought to model aspects of deprivation (Murthy and Gould, 2018). Maternal separation paradigms vary across research groups as to timing or age of separation, whether pups or dams are separated from the home cage, and whether siblings are kept together during separation or housed individually (Tractenberg et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2024). Maternal separation has been found to increase corticosterone response to stressors, altering hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA)-axis regulation, emotional dysregulation, and anxiety-and-depression-like behaviors (Anisman et al., 1998; Fóscolo et al., 2022; Lippmann et al., 2007; Liu et al., 2000; Pihoker et al., 1993; Plotsky and Meaney, 1993; Vargas et al., 2016).

Interestingly, when separated pups are reunited with their mothers after both short or long periods of separation, dams have been observed to increase their care towards pups (de Sá Couto-Pereira et al., 2016; Granata et al., 2022; Macri et al., 2004; Millstein and Holmes, 2007; Pryce et al., 2001; Stamatakis et al., 2015; Zimmerberg et al., 2003; Baracz et al., 2020; Macrì et al., 2008). Dams, or indeed other caring alloparents, will naturally retrieve scattered pups to a nest (Beach and Jaynes, 1956; Fleming and Rosenblatt, 1974; Reisbick et al., 1975) and begin licking and grooming pups (Champagne et al., 2003; Stern and Johnson, 1989), which is associated with positive stress regulation and other positive developmental effects (Liu et al., 1997; Meaney, 2001; Weaver et al., 2004). Together, these observations have begged the question of whether maternal separation paradigms prompt an increase in care that compensates for the stress of separation (Orso et al., 2019). While many long-term negative consequences of maternal separation have been documented, these observations nevertheless call into question the effects of the paradigm. A few studies have made multiple observations across the day to determine how long increases in post-reunion care persist (de Sá Couto-Pereira et al., 2016; Granata et al., 2022; Macrì et al., 2008), though none have explicitly calculated effects relative to loss of care during separation. It is therefore not currently known whether these increases in care may actually make up for the care missed during the period of separation.

To help reconcile the literature, we sought to determine whether maternal separation increases maternal care transiently or throughout the day in C57BL/6 mice. We hypothesized that increased care following dam-pup reunion may be transient and insufficient to compensate for the lack of care experienced during the hours of separation. To test this, we observed home cage behaviors at three time points throughout the day from postnatal days P2-17. We used an ELS paradigm in which pups are separated from their dams for three-four hours per day during a late juvenile period from postnatal days P10-17, allowing us to observe how ELS alters maternal behavior both within and across groups (Peña et al., 2017). By including multiple periods of observation before the start of maternal separation, immediately following pup reunion, and several hours after reunion, we sought to provide a more nuanced profile of how maternal separation disrupts total contact and dynamic timing of maternal care.

Methods

Animals

C57BL/6J mice were maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 4 a.m.) with ad libitum access to food and water. All experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Princeton University. All behavioral testing occurred during the animals’ light cycle. Mating occurred in-house to prevent shipping stress during gestation. Two multiparous adult (6-8 months) C57BL/6J female mice were mated with one male. Males were removed after 5 days and females were separated into individual maternity cages 1–3 days prior to parturition. Litters were weighed and counted and cages cleaned on the day of birth (PND0) and minimally balanced when necessary and possible to have between 4 and 9 pups (average litter size 6.26 pups; average male/female ratio 0.8) but were otherwise left undisturbed. Litter size and ratio did not differ by group. Cages were cleaned with minimal disruption to the litter once/week. Standard-reared cages included EnviroDri nesting material and a cardboard hut as enrichment. Offspring were weaned at postnatal PND21.

Early life stress

All litters were randomly assigned to standard-rearing (n = 7) or ELS (n = 9) conditions. ELS was administered from postnatal day P10 to 17 as previously described (Balouek et al., 2023; Parel et al., 2023; Peña et al., 2017) and consisted of a combination of maternal separation for 3-4 h per day during the light cycle and limiting home cage resources. Enrichment huts were removed from ELS cages and nesting material was depleted to ½ of an EnviroDri nesting puck during the week of ELS. On each separation day, the entire litter of pups was removed to a clean cage with clean bedding material and returned to the home cage at the end of the separation period. Start times were varied 1-3 h across days to prevent habituation. Pups were given access to hydrogel in the separation cages, but none were observed to eat it. Nesting material was restored at the end of ELS. The impact of ELS on offspring from these litters was reported previously (Parel et al., 2023).

Observations of home cage dam behaviors

Home cage maternal behavior was scored as previously described (Champagne et al., 2003; Jensen Peña and Champagne, 2013; Peña et al., 2013). Maternal behavior was observed for three 60-min observation periods daily during P1-16 (Fig. 1A), such that all cages were observed before the start of ELS (P1-9) and for the first week of ELS (P10-16). Observations ended one day prior to the completion of ELS for unplanned reasons. Every day, three 60-min observations were conducted per cage: the first observation was 1 h prior to pup separation, the second was immediately post-reunion (i.e. the first hour following dam-pup reunion), and the third observation was later post-reunion (i.e. 2-3 h after reunion) (Fig. 1A). The time of maternal separation was purposefully varied each day to prevent habituation, and thus four post-reunion observations were done in the dark cycle under red light. Behavioral observations were made every 3 min during each observation period for a total of 20 observations per hour (Champagne et al., 2003). The frequency of each behavior was calculated as the number of observations of the behavior divided by the total number of observations, and then transformed as percent time. The following individual behaviors were scored: nursing, licking/grooming, licking/grooming and nursing (LGN), passive contact, “hut” (when dams and pups were together in an enrichment hut), pup retrieval, pup stepping, nest building, dam eating, dam drinking, dam self-grooming, and no-contact/other. Notably, standard-reared “Std” cages had enrichment huts preventing observation of individual behaviors beyond dam-pup contact. Thus, “hut” time is considered as contact with pups but prevents distinguishing between passive contact, licking/grooming, and nursing that was more readily observable in ELS cages without the hut. We then created three composite behaviors: total contact (i.e. time in hut, time in observed contact, licking/grooming, LGN, and nursing), rough contact (i.e. pup stepping, pup retrieval, and nest building during which pups are often disrupted and stepped on but that isn't the primary behavior of the dam), and dam self-care (i.e. dam eating, drinking, and self-grooming) (Fig. 1B).

Home-cage behavior was scored in three 1h sessions: 1 h before separation, 1 h after separation, and 2-3 h after reunion. B) Composite frequency scores were created for active contact, rough contact, and self-care behaviors.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed in R (Version 4.5) and GraphPad Prism (Version 10). Pairwise group comparisons were performed using an unpaired two-tailed student's t-test. Analysis of maternal behavior over time (days and hours) was conducted with repeated measures ANOVA, followed by Tukey's HSD post hoc tests when appropriate. Differences in proportion between groups were calculated using a chi-squared test for non-parametrical categorical data, with post hoc pairwise comparisons based on adjusted p-values. Total contact with pups was calculated as geometric area under curve (AUC) for each “bin” of time: during the 3 h period of separation (Bin 1), during the 3 h following reunion (Bin 2), and for the remainder of the day (Bin 3; 18 h). Calculations were made using average contact frequency after P10, by litter. Significance was set at the p < 0.05 level.

Results

Dams engage in a variety of pup-directed and self-directed home cage behaviors, the frequency of which changes across the postpartum period and circadian cycle (Champagne et al., 2003; Jensen Peña and Champagne, 2013). We first quantified twelve home cage behaviors and examined how the overall frequency distribution of mouse dam behaviors differed as a function of developmental phase and group. Proportion of time spent in each individual behavior was aggregated by dam across either P1-9 or 10-16, and then averaged by group (Fig. 2). Consistent with observations of maternal behavior in rats (Champagne et al., 2003), total time in contact with pups (licking, nursing, LGN, other contact, or hut-obscured pup contact) decreased somewhat from the early (P1-9) to later (P10-16) postpartum period among Std dams [mean 78.6 ± 6.7% to 62.1 ± 15.1% of time; paired t-test: t(1,6) = 2.95, p = 0.026]. This change was largely attributable to increases in eating and time not in contact with pups (Fig. 2). Decreased contact was mitigated somewhat among ELS-assigned dams (mean 74.1 ± 8.3%; t(1,8) = 1.86, p = 0.100). A chi-squared test for non-parametrical categorical data revealed that the distribution of behaviors differed significantly (χ2(24) = 194.88, p < 0.05). Post-hoc tests of the chi-square indicate that differences in dam self-care accounted for the largest share of the overall effect, suggesting that ELS dams allocated time away from pup-directed care and toward self-directed behaviors.

Stacked proportions of mean time spent in each category of observed maternal behavior for all cages from P1-9, and by standard-reared (STD) and ELS dams separately from P10-16, across the entire period indicated. Note, enrichment huts given to STD cages were removed during ELS enabling more detailed observations. “Hut” is therefore considered dam-pup contact but individual behaviors were obscured.

Next, we focused on the effect of ELS after P10. We generated composite variables for total contact time, rough contact, and self-care behavior, as described in methods. We first calculated the mean frequency of each composite behavior across all observations, regardless of postpartum day or time of day (Fig. 3A–D). ELS moderately increased total contact time [Fig. 3A; unpaired two-tailed t-test: t(1,8) = 1.885, p = 0.093] and rough contact with pups [Fig. 3C; t(1,8) = 2.951, p = 0.011], and decreased time not in contact with pups [Fig. 3B; t(1,8) = 2.81, p = 0.0140]. Total self-care was not substantially altered by ELS [Fig. 3D; t(1,8) = 1.67, p = 0.117]. These behavioral shifts among ELS dams were present across ELS days, and not limited to only the first days of the manipulation, for example (Supplemental Fig. 1).

We next sought to determine whether ELS altered home cage behaviors across the day, or whether changes in behavior were specific to the period following reunion. To do this, we restricted analyses to the post-P10 period to characterize how these group differences unfolded across the reunion window. For each dam, we aggregated total observed contact at - 1h (morning baseline), +1 h (immediately post-reunion), and +2-3h (late window). A two-way repeated measures ANOVA revealed a significant effect of time, F(1.55, 21.63) = 22.14, p < 0.001, with a significant group × time interaction [Fig. 4A; F(1.55, 21.63) = 8.55, p = 0.0034]. The main effect of the group showed a trend toward higher overall observed contact in ELS dams, [F(1,14) = 4.20, p = 0.0597]. Post-hoc comparisons using estimated marginal means with Tukey adjustment showed that ELS dams exhibited higher observed contact than Std dams at the +1h timepoint (ELS - Std = 6.233, Tukey-adjusted p = 0.0046), whereas groups did not differ at -1h or +2-3h timepoints.

No-contact behavior also varied by condition and time with significant main effects of group, [Fig. 4B; F(1,14) = 7.09, p = 0.0185, and time, F(1.59, 22.21) = 6.23, p = 0.0107]. In addition, there was a significant group × time interaction F(1.59, 22.21) = 5.54, p = 0.0159. Std dams exhibited more no-contact behavior than ELS dams at +1 h and +2-3h, but not at an hour prior to reunion. Rough contact showed a significant main effect of the group [Fig. 4C; F(1,14) = 7.88, p = 0.0140], with ELS dams exhibiting higher rough contact overall. However, there was no significant effect of time, F(1.90, 26.54) = 0.28, p = 0.747, and no group × time interaction, F(1.90, 26.54) = 2.19, p = 0.134. Thus, this increase is notably stable across the reunion window. Self-care varied across the reunion window (Fig. 4D), showing a significant effect of time, F(1.50, 20.99) = 24.84, p < 0.001, and a significant Group × Time interaction, F(1.50, 20.99) = 5.72, p = 0.0161, but no main effect of group, F(1,14) = 2.78, p = 0.117. Post-hoc comparisons show that Std dams showed higher self-care than ELS dams at the +1h timepoint (Std - ELS - 3.680, Tukey-adjusted p = 0.0095), whereas groups did not differ at -1h or +2-3h. Together, we found that ELS alters the temporal organization of maternal behavior after reunion, with ELS dams showing increased immediate post-reunion contact, reduced no-contact, elevated rough contact, and relatively decreased self-care.

To account for the fact that half of the third (+2-3h) observations were done in the dark phase, given the purposely varied time of maternal separation each day, we determined whether ELS impacted behavior across the phase transition. It is known that pup grooming, nursing, and contact are elevated in the light compared to dark phase, and self-care behaviors are elevated in the dark compared to light phases (Jensen Peña and Champagne, 2013). Our data replicates this, with a main effect of dark to decrease total contact [Supplemental Fig. 2; F(1,14) = 44.20, p < 0.0001] and increase self-care [F(1,14) = 47.14, p < 0.0001]. There was also an interaction between phase and ELS for both behaviors, such that ELS seems to blunt the effect of phase on behavior [total contact: F(1,14) = 6.578, p = 0.023; self-care: F(1,14) = 10.12, p = 0.007], consistent with an interpretation that ELS alters the temporal organization of care.

Finally, we asked whether the increase in post-reunion contact in ELS dams is sufficient to compensate for the contact that is necessarily lost during separation. We first plotted total group-mean contact among Std litters across the 3-h separation interval, 3-h immediate post-reunion period, and remainder of the day (Fig. 5A: Bins 1, 2, and 3, respectively), assuming linear rise in contact between the post-reunion and next pre-reunion measurement periods. We then calculated area-under-curve for each time bin, by litter. While there is an increase in care in the 3-h bin following reunion of ELS pups, this increase was insufficient to make up for the care lost during the 3 h of separation such that total contact across Bins 1 and 2 was still lower among ELS mice (Fig. 5B; Welch's two-tailed t-test for unequal distributions: t(1,8) = 3.729, p = 0.008). Total contact across the day was not significantly different between groups (Fig. 5B). These findings indicate that maternal separation induces temporal reorganization of care, rather than overall more or less care.

Discussion

In this study, we asked whether maternal separation produces a sustained increase in maternal care, or whether changes in dam behavior are transient and offset by reductions in contact during separation. By repeatedly sampling home-cage behavior across the day, we confirm previous reports that maternal separation increases dam contact with pups following reunion, but show that this compensation is transient, and is also accompanied by a small but sustained increase in rough contact. This provides a more nuanced account of how dams alter their behavior during this ELS paradigm, now showing that maternal separation does not upregulate caregiving beyond what was absent during separation as suggested by previous reports using single snapshots of behavior only following reunion (Millstein and Holmes, 2007; Baracz et al., 2020). Instead, changes in care during ELS are better interpreted as a reshaping of both the timing and quality of maternal care.

Brief compensation rather than enduring enhancement

ELS dams showed a clear peak in observed contact during the first hour after reunion, followed by a decline back to a pre-separation baseline within 3 h of reunion. This pattern mirrors previous reports that dams increase licking, grooming, and nursing immediately after pup reunion (de Sá Couto-Pereira et al., 2016; Granata et al., 2022; Macri et al., 2004; Millstein and Holmes, 2007; Pryce et al., 2001; Stamatakis et al., 2015; Zimmerberg et al., 2003; Baracz et al., 2020; Macrì et al., 2008), but our data provide nuance to this effect by showing that such an increase is temporally limited. When behavior is only observed following pup reunion, ELS dams appear more attentive than controls; however, this increase is not maintained even 3 h after reunion. These findings are consistent with previous work in rats that examined active nursing across the day in rats exposed to brief or prolonged maternal separation from P2-10 and similarly found that increases in nursing were most pronounced after reunion, and that self-maintenance behaviors were most depleted (Macri et al., 2004; Macrì et al., 2008).

When we calculate average pup care as a mean of observed care across multiple observation periods, as in (Macrì et al., 2008), we find that ELS modestly impacts total contact time. However, this approach fails to explicitly account for the period of lost contact during the separation window. Here, directly accounting for this lost care period for the first time by calculating geometric AUC, we no longer find evidence for increased care overall. Our results therefore challenge the notion that post-separation increases in maternal care fully offset the effects of ELS. Instead, they point to a dynamic relocation of care toward a short, intense reunion period. What emerges, then, is not an increase in care, but a more imbalanced maternal state—one that is marked by temporal compression, decreased self-maintenance, and hypervigilance.

Quality of care: increased rough contact under ELS

Beyond observed contact, we observed that rough interactions such as stepping on pups, dragging them while nest-building, or abrupt retrieval were increased in ELS dams. These behaviors take up a small percentage of overall observations. Nonetheless, they represent qualitatively different forms of contact. Prior work suggests that the benefits of maternal care depend not only on its amount, but also on its predictability, contingency, and quality (Baram et al., 2012; Champagne et al., 2003; Liu et al., 1997). In this context, an increase in rough handling may impair some of the regulatory advantages conferred by higher contact frequencies. The three composite metrics help illustrate this trade-off. Indeed, while ELS increased contact during observation periods, ELS also increased rough contact and altered patterns of self-care. This hints at a dysregulated caregiving pattern wherein dams intensify pup-focused behaviors during reunion at the cost of predictable interactions and fulfilling their own physiological needs.

Temporal redistribution and unpredictability of care as a disruption

Previous studies on increased care following maternal separation—considered positive—have always appeared paradoxical given the many known negative outcomes on physical and mental health such as weight gain, altered HPA-axis activity, neuroinflammation, altered neurocircuitry, cognitive behaviors, depression and anxiety-like behaviors, and altered gene regulation throughout the brain (Bath et al., 2016; Cao et al., 2014; Dutcher et al., 2020; Lippmann et al., 2007; Liu et al., 2008; Lukas et al., 2011; Matthews et al., 1996; Matthews and Robbins, 2003; Peña et al., 2019a, 2019b; Plotsky and Meaney, 1993; Romeo et al., 2003; Schmidt et al., 2006; Spyrka et al., 2020; Tractenberg et al., 2016; Vargas et al., 2016; Vazquez et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). These outcomes are better understood with an updated interpretation that there is a diurnal reorganization of care. A key consequence of maternal separation is therefore not a change in the overall amount of care, but a disruption of its temporal structure. That is, care is compressed into a brief, intense reunion bout and redistributed away from the rest of the day. Even if observed contact time is similar, such fragmentation exposes pups to long gaps without access to the dam punctuated by short bursts of care, mirroring prior work showing that unpredictable, disorganized maternal care is itself sufficient to program stress-sensitive circuits (Glynn and Baram, 2019; Granata et al., 2022; Molet et al., 2016; Rice et al., 2008).

The developmental consequences of early care depend as much on its quality, including its sensitivity and contingency to offspring state — coupled with its predictability over time — as on its total quantity (Aran et al., 2024; Glynn and Baram, 2019; Kangas et al., 2022; Noroña-Zhou et al., 2020). In this vein, temporal redistribution is not a benign rearrangement of caregiving but the very stressor. Rodent limited bedding and nesting ELS paradigms tend to fragment maternal behavior such that dams deliver care in more erratic bouts with frequent behavior changes (higher “entropy” of care) (Demaestri et al., 2022; Ivy et al., 2008). Although entropy within a given observation window may be greater with limited bedding and nesting than maternal separation paradigms, both paradigms increase nest sorties and decrease the amount of time dams spent on the nest, and maternal separation increased nest reorganizations (“fluffing”) (Demaestri et al., 2022). We add to this showing that maternal separation also alters the organization of care across the day. The apparent “compensation” observed after reunion, considered in isolation, could overstate the adequacy of care. Considering the broader temporal structure of care, maternal separation first deprives pups of care followed by intense contact within a brief post-reunion window, with dams showing blunted diurnal rhythms of behavior and elevated rough handling. Such disorganization of care, rather than any net reduction in its amount, predicts later offspring vulnerability to cognitive and emotional deficits (Glynn and Baram, 2019). This reframing may offer greater context for the apparent post-separation paradox. We emphasize, though, that we have quantified the broader temporal organization of care rather than its moment-to-moment contingency on specific pup cues, which would require continuous, granular behavioral tracking (Lapp et al., 2023).

There is important translational relevance of this framing. In observational cohorts, less predictable maternal sensory signals in infancy predict poorer executive function and cognitive performance (Davis et al., 2017). Other longitudinal work shows that greater unpredictability in maternal mood forecasts higher anhedonia and negative affectivity in childhood and adolescence (Molet et al., 2016). The effects persist into adulthood, with childhood unpredictability associated with higher rates of depression, anhedonia, and anxiety disorder symptoms in later life (Hunt et al., 2024, 2026).

Implications for modeling postpartum depression and ELS

The present data may have implications for how rodent maternal separation models are used to understand human postpartum depression (PPD). While most maternal separation and other ELS studies focus on effects within offspring, these paradigms also impact dams, but little is understood about the nature of such effects. PPD is most common after experiencing perinatal stress. Clinically, PPD is often characterized by impaired mother-infant interaction, including reduced maternal sensitivity, difficulty assuming the parenting role, and disrupted caregiving (Letourneau et al., 2017; Milgrom et al., 2011). In our model of ELS, this disruption appears as a dysregulated diurnal rhythm of caregiving, increased rough care, and a decrease in self-care immediately after reunion — all suggestive of a broader reorganization of maternal behavior and disrupted mother-offspring relationship. To the extent that self-care reflects maternal regulation and recovery, its reduction may also support use of maternal separation paradigms as a proxy for aspects of PPD. These patterns of behavior change also align with prior evidence that maternal separation can induce anxiety- and depression-like phenotypes in dams, even when maternal behavior does significantly decline (Boccia et al., 2007; Kalyani et al., 2017; Kosten and Kehoe, 2010). While we acknowledge the differences between any rodent model and the human experience, rodent and human maternal responses may diverge at the behavioral level while still converging on a neurobiological, hormonal basis (Mundorf et al., 2022).

In rodents, while brief separation may lead to temporary compensatory caregiving, chronic postpartum separation stress disrupts persistent maternal neuroadaptations altering dopamine dynamics and transcriptional plasticity in the dorsal hippocampus (Chan et al., 2025). Therefore, effects of separation are not limited to caregiving outcomes alone but extend to crucial neural processes that support maternal function. Thus the translational relevance of maternal separation may lie less in reproducing the exact behavioral presentation of PPD and more in modeling how postpartum stress destabilizes the mother-infant dyad and the neural adaptations that usually support maternal responsiveness.

Limitations and future directions

Several caveats should be considered when interpreting this study. First, our observations sampled three 60-min periods per day largely during the light cycle; dams may show different patterns of care during the dark phase or in unsampled intervals (Peña et al., 2013), as indeed we find (Supplemental Fig. 2). Continuous or video-based tracking would allow accurate characterization of and predictability in dam-pup interactions (Lapp et al., 2023). Second, our ELS paradigm begins at P10 based on previous findings that this postnatal window effectively produces offspring stress sensitivity in male and female mice (Peña, 2025; Peña et al., 2017, 2019a, 2019b) which is later than many maternal separation studies beginning shortly after birth. While it is possible that this difference in timing could impact generalizability of our findings given changes in maternal behavior itself across the postnatal period (Champagne et al., 2003), we replicate the post-reunion increase in maternal care previously reported, suggesting that dams react similarly to maternal separation regardless of pup age. Third, standard-reared cages included enrichment huts that obscured scoring of specific maternal behaviors such as nursing or grooming during hut time. Although our composite “observed contact” metric was made to remedy this issue, given that all dams were in contact with their litters while within their huts, future work could use alternative enrichment to equalize observability across groups. Fourth, ELS here combines maternal separation and depleted nesting material. As a result, we cannot fully separate the effects of physical resource limitation from those of pup removal. Finally, we focused on maternal behavior and did not directly assess pup outcomes in this cohort. However, these data intersect with and inform many other studies using maternal separation paradigms that focus on behavioral and mechanistic outcomes in offspring.

In sum, our findings indicate that maternal separation with limited nesting material in mouse dams produces a temporally restricted increase in maternal care immediately after reunion. These results help mediate conflicting results in the literature by showing that the ostensible compensation in maternal care is highly time-dependent, coupled with changes in care quality. Rather than alleviating the impact of maternal separation, the paradigm appears to disrupt both the quantity and organization of maternal care, supporting its continued use as a model of early-life stress while highlighting the need to carefully consider when and how we measure maternal caregiving.
