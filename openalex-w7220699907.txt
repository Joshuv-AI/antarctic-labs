Robot-assisted surgery for rectal cancer with persistent descending
mesocolon: analysis of vascular anatomical features and short-term
outcomes
Aya Narumi1 · Marie Hanaoka1 · Shunsaku Nakagawa1,2 · Mari Nakagawa1,3 · Shinichi Yamauchi1 ·
Hiroyasu Kagawa1 · Masanori Tokunaga1 · Yusuke Kinugasa1
Received: 18 February 2026 / Accepted: 11 July 2026

Abstract
Purpose Persistent descending mesocolon (PDM) is a rare congenital anomaly characterized by a medial shift of the descending colon and vascular variations that can complicate colorectal surgery. This study aimed to clarify the PDM-associated
vascular anatomy and describe the short-term outcomes of robot-assisted surgery.
Methods We retrospectively analyzed 541 patients with primary rectal cancer who underwent robot-assisted surgery (2017–
2024) and compared the short-term outcomes between the PDM and non-PDM groups. The vascular anatomy, including
inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) branching patterns and marginal artery defects, was assessed in 501 patients using preoperative enhanced computed tomography.
Results PDM was identified in 22 patients (4.1%) in this study. The short-term outcomes were comparable between the
groups. The PDM group had a significantly higher prevalence of radial IMA branching patterns and a higher frequency of
marginal artery defects than the non-PDM group (9% vs. 2%).
Conclusion PDM is associated with a higher prevalence of radial IMA branching patterns. Marginal artery defects were
observed more frequently among patients with PDM; however, this finding requires cautious interpretation because of the
small number of affected cases and potential verification bias. Appropriate identification and management of vascular variations may contribute to acceptable short-term outcomes of robot-assisted surgery in patients with PDM.
Keywords Persistent descending mesocolon · Robot-assisted surgery · Rectal cancer · Vascular anatomy · Marginal
artery

Introduction

to fuse with the parietal peritoneum during embryological

and the mesocolon of the descending colon tends to shorten.
These features are frequently associated with strong and

as the radial branching pattern of the inferior mesenteric

the marginal artery, which can lead to postoperative complications [3–5]. However, despite several case reports on
patients with PDM [6–11], comprehensive reports summarizing the vascular anatomy of PDM remain limited.
Recently, robot-assisted surgery, developed as an evolution of conventional laparoscopic techniques, has been
increasingly used in colorectal surgery, and numerous studies have demonstrated its clinical benefits [12–16]. In rectal
cancer, robotic approaches have shown favorable short- and

long-term outcomes compared with conventional laparoscopic surgery [15].
For cases with PDM, some reports have focused on the
short-term outcomes using the conventional laparoscopic
approach [3, 4, 17, 18]. Demanding techniques, such as
adhesiolysis and mobilization of the splenic flexure, which
are often required for PDM cases, were associated with a
prolonged operative time, increased blood loss, and higher
complication rates [4]. In contrast, robot-assisted surgery
achieved better short-term outcomes than conventional
laparoscopy, particularly with respect to operative time for
adhesiolysis [19] and splenic flexure mobilization [20], and
postoperative complications related to these procedures
[21]. Therefore, we focused on the perioperative characteristics of the robotic approach when performing surgery
in patients with PDM. However, no studies have focused
on robot-assisted surgery for rectal cancer in patients with
PDM.
This study aimed to investigate the anatomical characteristics of PDM, particularly the branching pattern of the
IMA and its relationship with marginal artery defects, and
describe the short-term outcomes of robot-assisted surgery
for primary rectal cancer in patients with PDM.

Methods
Patients
We retrospectively analyzed the data of patients with clinical Stage I-IV disease who underwent rectal cancer resection
for primary rectal cancer at the Institute of Science Tokyo
Hospital between October 2017 and December 2024. Since
the introduction of robot-assisted surgery at our institution
in October 2017, most rectal cancers have been treated using
the da Vinci Surgical System (Intuitive Surgical™). During the study period, 566 patients underwent rectal cancer
resection (Fig. 1). After excluding patients who underwent
open abdominal surgery, laparoscopic surgery, total proctocolectomy for ulcerative colitis, those with missing data,
and those with a history of colorectal surgery, 541 patients
were included in the analysis of short-term outcomes. These
patients were classified into PDM and non-PDM groups
based on operative findings, and short-term outcomes
were compared between the two groups. Furthermore, to
evaluate the vascular anatomy, 40 patients were excluded
because of a history of aortic surgery and the absence of
contrast-enhanced CT. In the remaining 501 patients, threedimensional vascular reconstruction was performed using
preoperative contrast-enhanced CT to assess the sensitivity
and specificity of preoperative assessment for PDM and the

Surgery Today

differences in vascular anatomy between the PDM and nonPDM groups.
Patient characteristics, including age, sex, body mass
index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status (ASA-PS), tumor location, clinical stage, surgical
procedure, and short-term outcomes, were retrospectively
obtained from the Institute of Science Tokyo Hospital database. Informed consent was obtained from all patients, and
the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics
Committee (approval number: M2000-831).

Intraoperative diagnosis of PDM: PDM was diagnosed
intraoperatively based on the medial shift of the descending colon and the absence of fusion between the descending
colon and parietal peritoneum.

Definition of PDM

Operative findings were assessed based on operative time,
intraoperative blood loss, presence of adhesions, and presence of PDM, which was defined intraoperatively. Perioperative outcomes were evaluated based on the prevalence
of postoperative complications occurring within 30 days
after surgery and the length of postoperative hospital stay.
Postoperative complications were classified according to
the Clavien-Dindo classification [22] and defined as grade
II or higher.

Preoperative assessment of PDM: As a fundamental preoperative practice, contrast-enhanced abdominopelvic CT and
barium enema examinations were routinely performed during the study period to assess PDM. The presence of PDM
was assessed preoperatively when both modalities demonstrated characteristic findings. On CT, PDM was assessed
as the location of the right margin of the descending colon,
medial to the right border of the left kidney [17]. On barium
enema, PDM is assessed as an abnormally medially shifted
descending colon.
CT images were first evaluated by a board-certified surgeon
who did not take part in this study and was blinded to the
clinical outcomes. The images were then reviewed by one of
the study investigators who was familiar with the colorectal
surgical anatomy. In cases of discrepant interpretations, the
findings were discussed with a third experienced physician,
and a final decision was made by consensus.

In this study, PDM was diagnosed based on intraoperative
findings, and the sensitivity and specificity of preoperative
PDM assessment were evaluated in 501 patients.

Short-term outcomes

Radiological criteria for evaluation of vascular
anatomy
The branching pattern of the IMA, the length between the
IMA/IMV and the descending colon, and the marginal
artery defects were assessed using three-dimensional computed tomography (3D-CT) reconstruction with the AWS
system (Canon Medical Corporation). The anatomical classification of the IMA branching pattern was based on previously reported classifications [23, 24], and each case was
categorized into one of four types (Fig. 2).

Type 2 (Common trunk type): The LCA and SA arise from

To assess the distance between the IMA/IMV and the
descending colon, the horizontal distances from the IMA/
IMV to the descending colon were measured at the level of
the IMA root (Fig. 3). To minimize the variability caused
by the physiological mobility of the descending colon in
patients with PDM, the IMV-to-descending colon distance
was measured on axial CT images at the level of the IMA
origin. The measurement was defined as the horizontal
distance from the right border of the IMV at its origin to
the right border of the descending colon, as identified on
the same axial slice. This anatomical level was selected
because the descending colon at this level is usually adjacent to the retroperitoneal plane and is considered relatively
less mobile than more distal segments [25]. However, the
measured distance may still be influenced by bowel distension, stool/gas content, patient positioning, adhesions, and
transient bowel movement at the time of CT acquisition.
Therefore, this parameter should be interpreted as a radiological surrogate rather than as a fixed anatomical distance.
Measurements were performed only when the descending
colon was clearly identified on the same slice in contact
with or immediately adjacent to the retroperitoneal plane,
which served as the CT-based reference level for improving
measurement consistency.

Type 3 (Radial type): The LCA and SA originate from the
IMA at the same point.
Type 4 (Absence type): LCA is absent.
The classification of IMA branching patterns was primarily based on axial CT images, whereas additional crosssectional views and three-dimensional reconstructions were
used when necessary to confirm the branching anatomy. The
arterial phase of contrast-enhanced CT was used for vascular assessment. The LCA was defined as the first branch
arising from the IMA that supplied the descending colon.
Type 4 (absence type) was determined when the first branch
from the IMA supplied the sigmoid colon or more distal
segments, indicating the absence of the typical LCA. When
ambiguous branching patterns or accessory branches were
observed, the classification was based on the main branch
arising directly from the IMA that supplied the descending
colon, and the accessory branches were not considered as
the LCA.

Radiological criteria for marginal artery defect
Marginal arterial defects were evaluated using contrastenhanced CT in the arterial phase with multiplanar and
3D reconstruction. They were defined as a clear interruption of the expected marginal arterial arcade between the
descending and sigmoid colon, with no continuous marginal
artery identifiable on either multiplanar CT images or threedimensional (3D) reconstruction. When available, intraoperative findings or examinations of the resected specimen
were reviewed to confirm whether the marginal artery was
absent or hypoplastic in the corresponding region.

Results
Patient characteristics and PDM frequency
A total of 541 patients who underwent robot-assisted rectal cancer surgery were analyzed to evaluate short-term
outcomes, of whom 22 (4.1%) were diagnosed with PDM
based on intraoperative findings (Table 1). Based on preoperative imaging, PDM was not confirmed intraoperatively in two patients. Conversely, five patients diagnosed
with PDM intraoperatively did not meet radiological criteria. Thus, the sensitivity and specificity of the preoperative
diagnosis of PDM were 77.2% and 99.6%, respectively. The
median patient age was 66 years in both groups. Although
not statistically significant, the proportion of male patients
was higher in the PDM group (86.3% vs. 66.2%). No other
significant differences in baseline characteristics, including BMI, ASA-PS, tumor location, and clinical stage, were
observed.

Discussion
In this study, we investigated the vascular branching patterns
of the IMA in patients with PDM and found a significantly
higher prevalence of the radial branching pattern (Type 3) in
the PDM group than that in the non-PDM group. Moreover,
marginal artery defects were identified in two patients with
PDM (9.1%), and both had a Type 3 IMA branching pattern.
Compared with previous reports that mainly described the
radiologic anatomy of the descending colon and its relationship with the IMA and IMV in PDM during laparoscopic
surgery [17], this study offers several additional insights.

Specifically, we focused on a cohort undergoing robotassisted rectal cancer surgery, systematically analyzed IMA
branching patterns using a detailed classification, and evaluated the presence of marginal artery defects.
The prevalence of PDM from operative findings in our
cohort was 4.4% among 501 patients and 4.1% among 541
patients, which is consistent with previous reports ranging
from 1.3% to 4.8% [3, 4, 17, 18]. Regarding the branching pattern of the IMA in the general population, previous reports have shown that Type 1 is the most common
(41–58%), followed by Type 2 (27–45%), Type 3 (8–21%),
and Type 4 (0–5%) [27]. These distributions were similar
to those observed in the non-PDM group. However, a significantly higher proportion of type 3 patterns (31.8%) was
observed in the PDM group. While previous studies have
described a tendency toward a radial branching pattern of
the IMA among patients with PDM [28], only a few studies have used this typology. Among the reports that used
this typology, Wang reported a Type 3 pattern in 11.1%
[3], whereas Nozawa et al. [18] found none, and Nitta et
al. [29] found 15.4% among PDM cases, all of which were
lower than our result of 31.8%. Given the limited number
of reports on PDM, further case studies are warranted to
determine the distribution of this type.
The distance from the IMA/IMV to the descending colon
was significantly shorter in PDM cases, reflecting mesenteric shortening and medial shift of the descending colon.
This is consistent with previous studies [5, 17]. These anatomical alterations, including the shorter distances between
the IMA/IMV and the descending colon, may increase the

technical difficulty of colorectal surgery and underscore the
importance of preoperative recognition and planning. Our
high sensitivity and specificity rates suggest that the preoperative diagnosis of PDM is clinically useful. However, these
CT-based findings should be interpreted cautiously, as they
may reflect the positional changes of the descending colon
at the time of CT acquisition, rather than a completely fixed
anatomical relationship. Marginal artery defects are also
important surgical considerations in this context. Tonder et
al. [30] reported marginal artery defects at 4.7% of cadaveric
Sudeck’s critical points, and Watanabe et al. [31] found a
5% defect rate intraoperatively, irrespective of PDM. In the
present study, the observed marginal artery defects did not
consistently correspond with the classical Sudeck’s point.
This observation suggests a potential vascular vulnerability associated with PDM rather than a typical anatomical
watershed area. Defects of the marginal artery, which were
assessed by preoperative CT, were found in two patients in
the PDM group (9.1%), and both were Type 3 IMA branching, which accounted for 29% of Type 3 (2 of 7). A previous
report also described a case of PDM with a radial branching
pattern of the IMA in which the LCA directly served as the
marginal artery [3], as in this study. Meanwhile, there were
eight patients with defects of the marginal artery (1.7%) in
the non-PDM group, and seven cases were Type 1, except
for one indeterminate case. The distribution of the IMA
branching types was entirely different from that observed in
the PDM group. The incidence of marginal artery defects in
non-PDM cases was relatively low in this cohort. Previous
studies assessed all cases, irrespective of PDM status, either

intraoperatively or in cadaveric examinations, with approximately 100 cases, whereas our analysis was based on the
preoperative CT evaluation of 479 non-PDM cases. These
methodological differences may explain this discrepancy.
The number of marginal artery defects observed in the PDM
group was small. In addition, intraoperative confirmation of
these defects was not uniformly available in all cases, which
may have introduced a verification bias. Moreover, the clear
visualization of marginal vessels on CT may be limited by
the imaging conditions; therefore, CT-based evaluation may
underestimate the presence of small or hypoplastic arteries.
Consequently, the rates of marginal artery defects in both
groups should be interpreted with caution. Larger studies
combining radiological and intraoperative assessments are
required to clarify the relationship between PDM and variations in the marginal artery.
Although the study was subject to bias, given that marginal artery defects were observed during surgery and the
close proximity of the IMA/IMV to the marginal artery in
some cases of PDM, intraoperative assessment of intestinal perfusion is essential to avoid ischemia at the anastomotic site. Hamada et al. [4] reported two cases of PDM
that required additional resection due to proximal ischemia.
In another report, a patient with PDM developed severe
colonic stenosis after sigmoidectomy with ligation of the
IMA root and required colostomy, which may have been due
to the absence of a marginal artery [7]. Despite these critical
anatomical variations in PDM cases, our analysis of robotassisted surgery demonstrated no significant differences in
the short-term outcomes between patients with and without
PDM. Additionally, no cases of bowel ischemia or anastomotic leakage attributable to vascular variations or marginal
artery defects were observed in patients with PDM.
Based on these findings, when PDM is suspected on
preoperative CT, our intraoperative strategy is as follows:
First, we aim to preserve the LCA as much as possible while
dividing the IMA. The IMV is usually preserved during the
intraperitoneal procedure, as we prefer to divide it during
the mini-laparotomy phase after direct assessment of the
marginal artery and bowel perfusion. Second, colonic perfusion was carefully assessed during the mini-laparotomy. If
the bowel color appeared suboptimal, the demarcation line
was evaluated using ICG fluorescence in Firefly mode of
the da Vinci Surgical System. When a demarcation line was
observed proximal to the intended transection line (Fig. 5a,
b), we temporarily returned to the intraperitoneal procedure
to perform additional adhesiolysis and/or splenic flexure
mobilization. Alternatively, adhesions between the sigmoid
segments can be released during the mini-laparotomy phase
to achieve tension-free anastomosis. Third, bowel perfusion
was reassessed using ICG fluorescence before completing
anastomosis.

A prolonged operative time has been reported in PDM
cases [3–5, 18]. In this study, all patients with PDM had
intra-abdominal adhesions, and mobilization of the splenic
flexure was required in one case for a safe anastomosis. Nevertheless, in the present cohort, no significant difference in
the operative time was observed between patients with and
without PDM, despite the technical complexity associated
with adhesiolysis and mobilization of the splenic flexure.
Most adhesions are typically located on the left side, involving the sigmoid colon, sigmoid mesocolon, small intestinal
mesentery, or occasionally the right colon in patients with
PDM. Therefore, adhesiolysis can often be performed using
standard port placement for robotic sigmoidectomy. In
rare cases where adhesions around the patient’s right side
require dissection, the standard robotic setup may provide
an insufficient range of motion, and laparoscopic adhesiolysis may be performed. Furthermore, when more extensive
upper abdominal adhesiolysis or splenic flexure mobilization is necessary, modifications in port placement, docking
position, and targeting may be required. Previous studies
have reported that robot-assisted surgery achieves shorter
operative time, less intraoperative blood loss, and lower
complication rates than conventional laparoscopic surgery
in procedures such as adhesiolysis and splenic flexure mobilization [19–21]. These findings suggest that robot-assisted
surgery may be considered an option for PDM cases requiring adhesiolysis. However, because the present study lacked
a direct comparison of surgical approaches, further comparative studies are needed to better define the role of robotassisted surgery in patients with PDM.
This study has several limitations. First, only 22
patients with PDM were included; therefore, the absence
of statistically significant differences in short-term outcomes should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence. Second, the short-term outcomes can be influenced
by the surgeon’s experience and patient-related factors,
including the level of adhesion, especially in patients
with PDM. Furthermore, the method of confirming
marginal artery defects differed between the PDM and
non-PDM groups, which may have introduced some
verification bias. Further multi-institutional studies with
larger cohorts are required to validate these findings.
Nevertheless, this is the first study to evaluate IMA
branching patterns in PDM, identify marginal artery defects
using preoperative imaging, and describe the short-term
outcomes of robot-assisted rectal surgery in this patient
population. These findings highlight the important anatomical and clinical considerations for the surgical management
of patients with rectal cancer and PDM.

Conclusion
Our findings suggest that PDM is associated with a high
prevalence of radial branching patterns in the IMA and that
marginal artery defects are observed more frequently in
patients with PDM, although the latter finding requires cautious interpretation because of the small number of affected
cases and potential verification bias. Appropriate identification and management of vascular variations may contribute
to acceptable short-term outcomes of robot-assisted surgery
in patients with PDM.
