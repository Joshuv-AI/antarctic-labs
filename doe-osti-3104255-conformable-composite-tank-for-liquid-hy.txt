1.0 Executive Summary
Raytheon Technologies Research Center (RTRC), with its partners, Argonne National Labs and University of Dayton
Research Institute proposed to design and manufacture a conformable composite tank prototype to store liquid
Hydrogen (LH2) for heavy-duty truck applications. A dual-wall tank concept was proposed where an outer shell
covers and protects an inner pressure vessel that stores liquid hydrogen. The inner pressure vessel is liner-less
and designed with a conformable concept, derived from prior RTRC ARPA-E program where multiple vessels are
coalesced into one, maximizing space utilization and reducing surface area for heat leak. The gap between the
dual wall is evacuated of air pressure, and filled with multi-layer insulation to minimize radiative heat transfer.
Fiber reinforced composites were proposed for light-weighting and conformable shape manufacture. To minimize
LH2 leakage, carbon nanotubes-infused composites were proposed, in addition to use of thin plies. The project
was divided into two 18-month budget periods (BP), BP1 and BP2, separated by a Go/No-Go gate review. The
high-level goal for BP1 was to demonstrate the feasibility of the overall approach, the tank design, material
selection, and manufacturing methodology, build and test a prototype tank; and the goal for BP2 was to design
and manufacture a conformal tank per DOE requirements (minimum 20 kg of LH2) and perform a successful test
of the tank. The program was terminated about two-thirds of the way through BP1. The outcome of this program
would have permitted long-haul trucks and construction vehicles to use liquid Hydrogen, reducing the emission
of green-house gases. This technology is applicable to other transportation sectors as well.

2.0 Background
The overarching goal of the program is to develop storage technology for a carbon-free cryogenic fuel (e.g.
Hydrogen) for heavy-duty ground transport. LH2 allows for high density storage of H2. One major issue is
efficient storage of hydrogen utilizing the full space envelope available on a vehicle. Tank placement envelopes
can change based on the type and size of the vehicle. Efficient use of storage space is key to maximizing the
range of vehicles. The goal of a conformal tank concept is to make efficient use of space. It can potentially
reduce the requirement from multiple tanks to one tank and therefore, significantly reduce the need for balance
of plant (BoP) equipment, making the technology more viable, affordable and adoptable by industry. The tank
needs to be light-weight and meet permeability, boil-off and pressure requirements. The major technological
aspects of this program are to develop a carbon-fiber composite, conformable tank for use in cryogenic
temperature regime, and adapt it for heavy duty vehicular conditions.

In the first 2 quarters of the program (May-Oct. 2024), the conceptual design of the conformal tank for the
truck, was done. In the next quarter (Nov-2025 through January 2025), the conceptual design of a tank to be
tested at the end of Phase 1, was done. This is a smaller tank, and of a cylindrical shape. Following this, the
detailed design of the Phase 1 test article had commenced. In Q2 2025, progress was made on several fronts:
the preliminary design of the Phase 1 tank was completed. The detailed design of the composite tanks was
almost done. Some aspects of the boss design and flange design remain to be completed. The manufacturer for
the tank (using AFP) was down-selected and preliminary quotes obtained. The testing lab for the Go/No Go test
(Task 7) was also identified and quotes obtained. University of Dayton Research Institute (UDRI) performed
static and fatigue tests at ambient and cryogenic temperatures. They also performed thermal fatigue tests on
the composite panels. In Q3, 2025, the design of the Phase 1 tank was almost completed. There was a revision
in the design of the sealing approach, moving to spring-energized seals- which provide more control over the
sealing process, and reduce compressive stresses induced in the composite tank from bolt tightening.

1. Phase 1 Tank Concept and Design:
 1.1. Full-scale Phase 2 Tank Conceptual Design
A conceptual design of a product-oriented conformable composite tank was completed at the start of the
program, providing a target configuration for the development of key enabling technologies during the first
phase. Figure 1 shows the concept, consisting of an inner tank capable of holding 0.5 cubic meters of liquid
hydrogen with an outer shell. An insulating vacuum space is between the two, with support structures
maintaining the gap and transmitting loads from the inner tank to the outer shell. The two-chamber geometry is
derived from previous RTRC experience with high-pressure conformable composite tanks designed to maximize
storage volume while reducing weight and space requirements.

 1.2. Subscale Phase 1 Tank Design
Figure 2 illustrates the subscale Phase 1 tank planned to be fabricated for thermal testing. The inner hydrogen

 1.2.1.Upper Port and Lower Boss
Figure 3 shows the boss design for the hydrogen tank. This boss serves to seal the polar hole at each end of the
composite tank, with additional provisions for hydrogen ingress and egress at the upper boss (to be discussed
below). A spring-energized C-seals acts against the inner tank to contain the liquid hydrogen. This is a proven
seal in a variety of applications, including liquid hydrogen, and consists of a C-shaped jacket of PTFE with a
metallic spring element inside. The PTFE jacket seals against the mating surfaces while the inner spring provides
a restoring force to the jacket to compensate for PTFE’s reduced elasticity at extreme cryogenic temperatures.
Metal components are targeted to be 316 stainless steel, a material that performs well at cryogenic conditions.

 Figure 3: Boss design with spring-energized seal

 Page 4 of 30

A retaining ring, which is spread and spiraled into the tank during assembly, contacts the inside of the tank and
rests against a lip on the inner anchor. The anchor piece threads to the seal bulkhead component to compress
the spring-energized seal. A hex cutout in the anchor allows for it to be turned with a wrench while holding the
bulkhead to bring the parts together, with a ring of PTFE between the bulkhead and composite tank serving as a
spacer to achieve the seal working height. Because the spring- energized seal operates in a controlled gap versus
a controlled load, a relatively low load of around 50 lbs. per inch of circumference is transferred to the
composite. The seal does require a fine surface finish for optimum performance, so it is planned to use a caul
plate during fabrication of the tank in the areas where the seal acts against the composite.

Figure 4 shows the working port design at the top of the tank that seals against the outer shell and provides for
charging and discharging the inner tank. The outer shell is split into two halves at its midline, allowing for
insertion of the seal cap with an O-ring that contacts the inner surface of the shell for sealing the vacuum space.
A bearing nut on the outside is threaded onto the cap to compress the seal against the shell. With this location
predicted to be significantly warmer than the inner tank and air being the working fluid, standard O-ring
material options may be expected to provide effective sealing.

Hydrogen ingress and egress to the tank is performed through a tube-within-a-tube arrangement. The inner
tube transitions from stainless steel to a PTFE section extending to the bottom of the tank, allowing for
hydrogen withdrawal with minimal heat conduction. The outer stainless steel tube passes through the inner
tank upper bulkhead via a Swagelok FK tube fitting (commonly used in hydrogen piping) welded to the bulkhead.
Just above the fitting this tube incorporates a flexible bellows section to compensate for small misalignments
and thermal motion while avoiding the transfer of loads from the port and exterior tubing to the inner tank,
potentially compromising the inner tank seal. Above the bellows section, the tube is welded to a larger flanged
cylindrical piece that is loaded against the upper inner surface of the cap to seal and support the external tubing.
A metal C-seal is targeted for this location, where the metal-to-metal interface is favorable to conventional seals
but at a temperature that is expected to be outside of the limits of common O-ring materials.

 1.2.2.Shell flange and inner tank support
The outer shell is split at its midline, with metal flanges bonded to each end and bolted around the inner tank at
assembly. An O-ring between the flanges seals the vacuum space. The support consists of a single ring at the
midsection of the tank, bonded to the inner tank and interfacing with the flanges of the outer shell halves. The
support may be metal or composite, with material selection a balance between structural and thermal
performance.

Locating the support at the tank midline offers a number of advantages versus placement at the top and
bottom. Attempting to hold the tank with top and bottom supports creates issues with proper alignment and
load balancing between the two end supports, and so would be unforgiving of geometrical variance and relative
thermal motion. The midline support has no such issues, with small variations accommodated by the bellows
tube at the port. Further, the midline support allows for full coverage of MLI around the inner tank and is
assembled with visibility to how the components and MLI are seated.

 1.3. Test fixture
As a risk-reduction step, a benchtop test fixture was designed (Figure 5) to allow for evaluating the seal
performance under a variety of representative tank operating conditions. The test fixture is built from two high-
vacuum blind flanges. These off-the-shelf parts are designed for use in vacuum piping, and have a sharp lip just
inside the bolt circle which bites into a copper gasket to seal when the flanges are bolted together. These
flanges are modified by machining a groove in the top flange for the spring-energized seal and removing
material to create a small chamber to hold a mating composite plate. The composite plate is 3.6 inches in
diameter and of similar thickness to the inner tank wall; it rests on a brass spacer piece that may be shimmed to
accommodate a range of plate thicknesses as well as investigate seal performance under small variations in
working height. The seal acts directly against the composite to create an inner plenum. Two tubes allow for
instrumentation of this plenum as well as the introduction of a variety of working fluids (air, liquid nitrogen,
liquid hydrogen, etc.) at pressure to evaluate the seal under a range of conditions. The seal creates a separate
annular plenum between it and the flange gasket, where a tube is connected to allow for pulling a vacuum. This
mimics the configuration of the tank boss where inside the seal is pressurized liquid hydrogen and outside the
seal is the inter-tank vacuum space. The fixture was fabricated in the RTRC machine shop.

 1.4. Conformal tank conceptual studies:
An XL-based tool was used to perform calculations for storage volume of conformal tanks. This tool was used to
run various parametric studies with number of lobes and lobe diameters as variables. The outer dimensions of
the tank can be estimated with this tool, and this was used to determine whether it fits in the storage envelope
in a truck, behind the cab. It was determined that using a 3-lobed configuration, with a tank length of 2.6m, and
a lobe diameter of 0.85m, it would yield a conformal tank of dimension 2.6m x 1.7m wide x 0.85 m, and has a
capacity of equal to or exceeding 130 kg of LH2 with an assumed ullage. Other combinations of parameters (e.g.
increasing the number of lobes) yield higher tank capacity, if needed. This tank size will fit into an envelope
behind the cab of a semi-truck.

2. Thermo-structural Analysis of Inner and Outer Tanks:

tank, to be manufactured and tested in Phase 2. This was to determine the feasibility and capability of the tank

atmospheric pressure. These are described in sections 2.1 and 2.2 below. Following this, a detailed structural
analysis of a cylindrical tank (to be designed and manufactured in Phase 1), was performed. This is described in
sections 2.3, 2.4 and 2.5.

 2.2. Conformal (Phase 2) tank Inner Tank Internal pressure analysis:
A finite element analysis of the conformal inner tank under internal pressure was performed. The inner tank has
the same thickness as the over-wrap. Composite material properties used here are the same as in the above
tank (AS4-8552). Quasi-isotropic layup [0/45/-45/90]s was assumed. Orthotropic, homogeneous properties of
the composite were computed using classical laminate theory (CLT). Symmetry boundary conditions were used
to reduce the model size to one lobe from the full model size of 2 lobes. Furthermore, half of one lobe was
modeled, with symmetry boundary conditions applied to the truncated face. An internal pressure of 13.5 bar
was applied. Stresses in most of the tank is well below 130 MPa. At the location of the corner, the stresses are
higher. This is well below the failure limit for this material (computed via an in-house classical laminate theory
(CLT) code to be at 428 MPa) for the quasi-isotropic layup. The corner location with the highest stress was
modeled without any structural material in the gap/cavity. Carbon-fiber based ‘noodle’ structural support will
be added to the model to reduce stresses. Once this is incorporated in the model, the highest stresses, which
are currently well below the allowable limit, are expected to come down further. This suggests that the
thickness of the tank can be comfortably reduced. The level of reduction is a topic for detailed design (to have
been done in Phase 2 of the program).

 2.3. Cylindrical (Phase 1) composite tank Outer Tank Buckling Analysis:
Having shown feasibility of the above design, a tank design with cylindrical shape and with hemispherical ends
was created for Phase 1 tasks. This was to incorporate some of the technical risks in Phase 1. The design of this
tank was described earlier. A detailed structural analysis of the outer and inner tanks was performed. A finite
element (FE) of the outer tank (Phase 1 design) was created in Abaqus to analyze the tank for buckling. Initial
design considerations pointed us to using a composite-foam sandwich structure. However, for ease of
manufacture, it was decided to pursue an all-composite outer tank for Phase 1.

First, an eigenvalue analysis of the outer tank was performed- to determine its capacity to buckle under
atmospheric pressure. The tank geometry is shown in Figure 7 (a). The material model used was that of a
homogenous laminate, with quasi-isotropic properties of a carbon-epoxy system. Ply level properties were taken
from literature [1] for a system similar to IMS65-Q183, since material properties for this material were not
available at this time. Laminate-level homogenized (quasi-isotropic) properties were computed using Classical
Laminate Theory. The eigen-buckling analysis of the outer tank gave the first eigenvalue as 11.7, under a loading
of 1.01e5 Pa (1 atm). This suggests that the load required to buckle the tank is 11.7 times the atmospheric
pressure. The mode of buckling is at the center of the tank (Figure 7 (b)), as expected. This is the cylindrical
portion and is easier to buckle than at the hemispherical ends.

This analysis was followed by a Riks analysis of the tank where the atmospheric load was applied explicitly, and
the buckling load was computed. The load is applied, step by step, until the structure buckles, the Riks method
can capture post-buckling response as well. A ‘pre-existing defect’, computed via the eigenvalue analysis, was
applied to the model to initiate buckling. The load proportionality factor (LPF) at the point of buckling, output
from the results when multiplied by the applied pressure, yielded a buckling load of 11.7 times that of
atmospheric pressure, confirming the result of the eigen-buckling analysis. This is deemed to be sufficient
structural margin. If there is a need for more margin, then thickness of the tank can be increased and/or the
thickness of the flange in the mid-section can be increased, stiffening the area of the tank most likely to buckle.
This model was to be re-run with more updated properties when available.

 2.4. Cylindrical (Phase 1) composite tank Inner Tank thermo-structural Analysis:
An FE model of the inner tank was set up- as seen in Figure 8. Composite material properties for the tank
(material: Teijin IMS65-Q183) are unavailable since the materials test program had just commenced. At the
termination of the program, only room temperature properties were tested, but not at cryogenic temperatures.
For a reasonably accurate analysis, cryogenic properties of the material are also required. Hence, properties of a
similar/equivalent composite i.e. a carbon -epoxy unidirectional tape (Toray T800-epoxy) were taken from
literature [2], where both room temperature and cryogenic temperatures were available. Li et. al. [2]. tested,
computed and validated composite properties for carbon fiber epoxy system at ambient and cryogenic
temperatures.

The FE model consists of the tank, the boss (i.e. flange, bolt, sealing, cushion and tubes etc.). A detailed boss
design was created, and used in this model. The loading in the FE model has 3 parts to it: (i) compression of the
seal and composite tank due to bolt loads, (ii) thermal stresses due to cool down, and (iii) stresses in the tank
due to internal pressure. It was expected that stresses in the area of the tank in contact with the boss will be
affected by bolt load and also by the cooldown- hence the need to model all the loads in a single model.
Temperature profiles (at equilibrium) were used to simulate the cooling process.

The model has contact enforced between the upper bulkhead and the seal, the seal and the composite tank, and
between the tank and the cushion below it. The composite tank is modeled at ply-level detail where individual
ply directions are defined. The two primary goals of this modeling effort are to determine the stress margin in
the tank, and to determine dynamics of the seal as it deforms under compression. This simulates the sealing
process, which is critical to maintaining vacuum and the thermal integrity of the tank.

Continuum-shell elements of type ‘SC8R’ were used to model the tank. This element type provides the flexibility
of allowing the user to easily change/apply different ply directions to determine an optimal ply orientation (as
with shells elements), and at the same time, be able to capture through-thickness stresses at the location of
flanges due to compressive loads- which shells either cannot capture effectively (thin shells) or with low
accuracy (thick shells). The mesh of the tank and boss parts are shown in Figure 9. The cool-down portion of the
analysis (the second load-case) was simulated isothermally, i.e. a temperature profile was applied to the tank in
4 zones of varying temperatures. A transient heat transfer analysis was not performed as a part of this analysis.
The model, as it stands, has several complexities including multi-body contact between the flanges, the anchor,
tank, seal and cushion, in addition to a ply-by-ply description of the tank. To keep the model complexity
tractable, a coupled thermal-structural approach was not taken here. Cooling was assumed to be iso-thermal, as
a preliminary step, with temperature profiles taken from a separate transient analysis, and simplified into 4
zones, where temperature in each zone is uniform. The model and results are described further below. Details
of the model, BCs and material properties are also mentioned in the conference presentation [3].

 2.5. Effect of Layup on thermal stresses:
Thermo-structural analyses on various layups were performed in an attempt to reduce transverse tensile
stresses in tank due to internal pressure. Earlier structural analyses showed plenty of margin for stresses along
the fiber direction, but in the transverse direction stresses were much closer to the allowable limit. To reduce
transverse stresses, an effort was made to determine optimal layups for wound cylinders. The following layup
from Farhood et. al. [4] i.e. [90/90/90/90/20/-20/……/-20/90/90/90/90] was chosen for an evaluation via
structural analysis. The 90 degree ply refers to ply along the hoop direction. This layup reduced the transverse
stresses in the tape relative to the layup analyzed in prior studies (i.e. +55/-55). This layup was further
studied/analyzed by varying the angle of the non-hoop ply to 10 degrees, 20 degrees and 30 degrees i.e.
[90/90/90/90/10/-10/……/10/-10/90/90/90/90] and [90/90/90/90/30/-30/……30/-30/90/90/90/90]. The
resulting transverse stresses are shown in Figure 14. The contour plots show highest stresses in all the plies
through the thickness. As can be seen, these stresses are far lower than the 42 MPa stress value seen in the
default layup used in the first design [55/-55/+55/-55]. The lowest transverse stresses are for a ply angle of 10
degrees. However, this angle may present difficulties during manufacture and such considerations may force a
change to higher tape angles e.g. 20 degrees or 30 degrees.

A literature survey was conducted to determine the ideal layups to reduce micro-cracking. As is known, micro-
cracking contributes to the permeability of gases through the composite, and one source of micro-cracking is
inter-laminar stresses. These are typically lower when the ply angles between adjacent plies are low.
Furthermore, to reduce the propagation of micro-cracks from one ply to another, Bechel et. al. [5] suggested
that having higher angles between adjacent plies is preferrable e.g. 0/90 rather than 0/45 is preferrable. This
requirement stands in conflict with the prior requirement where a lower difference in ply angles are preferred to
reduce thermal stresses. Hence further FE analyses are required to reconcile these requirements and optimize
layup to minimize permeability. Additionally, these layups have to be manufacturable, and this requires input
from the manufacturing entity. This was not completed due to termination of program. Transient thermo-
structural analysis of flat panels with different layups was initiated. Preliminary models were set up and run, to
test the models. This work was not completed.

3. Thermal/heat transfer and Slosh models:
Heat transfer and sloshing analyses were performed on the Phase 1 (cylindrical tank) to be tested at the end of
Phase 1, to determine the requirements for insulation, vacuum and assess boil-off rate. Additionally, a sloshing
analysis was performed for a preliminary assessment of the degree of slosh mitigation from baffles.

 3.1. Heat leak and Boil-off computations for Phase 1 tank: Heat transfer modeling focused on evaluating
 the thermal response of the Phase I prototype under cryogenic conditions. The goal was to estimate
 the boil-off rates for increasing quantities of LH2 in the tank. The analysis shows that the smaller first
 prototype will have a greater boil-off loss due to its smaller volume and greater surface area to volume
 ratio. Figure 15 shows the trend between thermal loss or boil-off and tank size for a cylindrical tank. In
 this analysis, heat leak into the tank is calculated based on tank surface area and insulation
 characteristics for typical MLI insulation. The heat leak is then translated into an evaporation mass flow
 rate based on the latent heat of vaporization for LH2. The figure also shows that for the Phase I tank,
 the boil-off will be around 2% per day due to its small size. Since heat leak (i.e., boil off mass) is
 proportional to surface area, for larger tanks (i.e. Phase II tank and a full-sized tank for a semi-truck),
 boil off as a percentage of tank capacity will be significantly lower and potentially below the 1% per day
 limit. While this simplified analysis captures only losses through the insulation, high-fidelity models
 capturing structural details have also been developed (Figure 16). Simulations show that inner tank
 structural supports can create significant conduction paths and require special care to minimize
 thermal losses.

3.2. Inter-tank supports: Inter-tank structural supports are components placed between the inner tank and
 the outer tank. They support the weight of the inner tank with liq. H2, through contact with the outside
 tank. These loads get reacted out of the outer tank and into the truck structure to which the tank is
 attached. However, these inter-tank supports also serve a path for heat ingress into the inner tank.
 Detailed simulations show that inter-tank structural supports can create significant conduction paths
 from the outer tank to the inner tank, and will require innovative solutions and optimization to
 minimize thermal losses while providing the required mechanical support. Figure 15 shows that with
 minimal boss and support losses, the boil-off targets for Phase 2 tank can be met. Figure 16 shows
 high-fidelity model of the tank with structural features (inter-tank supports), and it is seen that the
 supports serve as paths for heat leak. Several tank support topologies were being evaluated to reduce
 heat leak. The intent of these concepts is to minimize the support cross-sectional areas while
 increasing the length of the conduction path. The assembly and interference of the supports with the
 multi-layer insulation (MLI) also need to be considered. The connections to external pipes also need to
 be properly insulated to minimize thermal losses. Figure 17 shows the effect of boss and inter-tank
 supports as a path for heat leak, and the resulting increase in boil-off.

4. Manufacturing process:
Inner tank manufacturing:
With the final objective being to build a conformal composite tank, the tank design involves convex and concave
surface features. Two major existing automated composite processes, filament winding (FW) and automated
fiber placement (AFP) were considered for the inner tank manufacturing. In FW, as shown in Figure 20(a), the
wet fiber tow will bridge across a concave surface feature and forms air-gap defects in the composite, instead of
conforming to the concaved surface. However, in AFP, as shown in Figure 20(b), the roller are able to press
tacky prepreg tapes onto various surfaces and the tacky tapes adhere to the previously placed prepreg surface.

                                                Page 16 of 30

Another challenge of manufacturing the inner tank is with the mandrel. The mandrel will provide support for
the fiber placement and define the tank inside surface/shape. However, after manufacturing, the mandrel has
to be removed. With the small tank end openings, the mandrel removal will be challenging. The team is
considering water soluble mandrel option for the prototype. Collapsible or bladder mandrel are considered to
be more feasible options for tank production.

Figure 20: An example of (a) a Filament winding process, and (b) Automated fiber placement process.

Outer shell manufacturing:
The outer shell is a two half-shell design. In overall tank assembly, the outer shell opens up and the inner tank
and insulation materials such as multi-layer insulation (MLI) are placed in, and then the shell is closed. An
insulative vacuum is pulled in the space between the outer shell and inner tank. To prevent shell buckling under
vacuum, both composite laminate and sandwich (composite skin/core/composite skin) structures are
considered as candidates for the outer shell. Since the shell is not expected to have concave surface features,
AFP, FW, and even forming are considered potential manufacturing options.

Insulation:
Multi-Layer Insulation (MLI): The team worked with Aerospace Fabrication & Materials (AFM) on MLI materials
for LH2 tank prototype. The MLI grades of interest are the standard grade PolaWrap® and premium grade
PolaLoft™, as shown in Figure 21, to provide the balance of cost and performance. The MLIs are prefabricated
stack up of ultrathin surface-aluminized polyester film reflector layers to provide efficient thermal insulation
with minimal weight and stiffness. The premium grade has intentionally-designed crinkled layers to improve
insulation efficiency and reduce weight. The insulation has two sections. One is in tape form with customized
cut to wrap around the LH2 tank spherical domes smoothly. The other is in an MLI blanket form, cut to fit the
middle cylinder of LH2 tank. The design of the MLI tape and blanket was in the final stage at program
termination. Reference insulation performance is shown in Figure 22.

UDRI performed the fabrication and testing of coupons for mechanical and thermal response and for material
properties at room temperature and cryogenic temperature. Composite test coupons were fabricated without
and with carbon nanotubes (CNTs). The following sub-sections provide the details of the test set-up, testing and

 8.1. Introduction:
Material evaluation through laminate level and coupon testing of carbon/epoxy laminates under cryogenic
conditions was performed. A novel cryostat was designed for performing a variety of mechanical tests of
polymer matrix composites (PMCs). Shear and tensile testing of various control and nano-modified laminates
was conducted using the developed cryostat chamber. Room temperature and liquid nitrogen (LN2) test data on
mechanical properties, including an assessment of the microcracking initiation (via acoustic emission) and extent
of microcracking was evaluated using optical microscopy. Panels were fabricated as per manufacture’s
guidelines, and after fabrication, panels were subjected to flash thermography (FT) scans to check for defects
prior to testing.

Additionally, the use of Carbon Nanotubes (CNTs) was proposed to reduce microcracking- a critical consideration
in composite tanks. Carbon/Epoxy based cryogenic storage tanks can have microcracking (under repeated
thermal cycling) which may lead to reduced structural integrity and leakage/permeability concerns.

 8.2. Cryostat:
A novel cryostat (Figure 23) was designed for performing a variety of mechanical tests to include tension,
compression, shear and fracture toughness of PMC composites modified with carbon nanotubes. The outer
walls of the top section of the cryostat are double wall vacuum insulated to reduce boiling off nitrogen. This was
done to not only reduce the amount of nitrogen required during a test but also to allow the system to reach a
steady state sooner and prevent bubbles forming in the test chamber that would interfere with strain and
acoustic measurement equipment.

 Figure 23. Cryostat chamber set-up for material evaluation (UDRI)

It takes ~ 20 min to fill the chamber with ~ 25 liters of N2 and the specimen comes to temperature at the same
time filling is complete. To reduce losses, an outer wall has been added to the cylinder for vacuum insulation
including a Cu coil wrap for the connecting rod.

 8.4. Non-destructive evaluation and physical characterization:
After fabrication, the panel were subject to testing of various kinds to assess their suitability for testing. Flash
thermography was used to check the panels for defects such as delamination and porosity, to eliminate
defective panels from testing. Additionally, scanning electron microscopy and optical microscopy was applied to
panels to check for microscopic defects such as cracks, debonding at interfaces, porosity in matrix, and also the
placement and distribution of CNTs after consolidation. Physical characterization of the coupons was performed
to determine fiber volume fraction content, which was about 55.6% for tape (without CNTs) and 56.7% for tape
with CNTs. For fabric, the corresponding numbers are 51.% and 50.65%.

 8.5. Testing of coupons:
Short beam shear testing was performed following ASTM D2344/D2344M-22 using Wyoming Test Fixture WTF-
SB-422 flex fixture. The fixture was adjusted for a singe 0.25” diameter load pin, and two 0.125 in. diameter
support pins. The span settings were calculated at 4x the average thickness for each material.

Tensile test specimens were prepared in accordance with ASTM D3039/D3039M-17, with dimensions of 1 inch
in width and 10 inches in length. Machining was carried out using an Extec Labcut 5000 precision 2-axis CNC
diamond wet saw. G10 fiberglass tabs were bonded using the EA-9394 C2 epoxy adhesive system to facilitate
load introduction and reduce grip slippage; tab surfaces were lightly sanded to further enhance grip. Manually
operated grips were used for testing. The wedges were knurled to decrease slipping of the specimen. A single
face mounted Epsilon model 3542 clip on extensometer was used to record strain during testing. Tests were
controlled using a constant crosshead displacement rate of 0.05 in/min.

All specimens were preconditioned for at least 24 hours at laboratory ambient conditions prior to testing. All
specimens were visually inspected, and dimensions measured prior to being loaded into the test frame. Room
temperature tests were conducted under ambient lab conditions, while cryogenic tests were performed with
specimens fully submerged in liquid nitrogen.

 contractor due to IP issues contributing in part. Technically, the program proceeded well and was
 ahead of schedule for the first 3 quarters.

 3. One sub-contractor (Faurecia) did not join the program due their concern about US govt. restrictions on
 their international operations (in Europe and China).

 4. The scope of testing at cryogenic temperatures needed to be made more efficient as the program
 developed. The test matrix involved various combinations of 2 composite layups, 2 ply architectures,
 without and with CNTs, at room and cryogenic temperatures, for static and fatigue testing. The testing
 schedule was speeded up by UDRI to make up for the delayed start, and significant progress was
 achieved in this regard. The remainder of the test matrix was reduced and made more targeted to
 desired layups, based on learnings from tests done prior in the program. However, it was not completed
 due to program termination.
