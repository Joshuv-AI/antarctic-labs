Clinical and operational impact of single anesthesia events in the surgical management of early-stage lung cancer: a single-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jordan Dozier, Rachel Talton, Maya Hammonds, Eric Veytsman, Katrina J. Jiang, Christopher Zumwalt, Reginald Du, Susan Gunn, Derek Vonderhaar, Grey Mayhall, Mona Bansal, Mariella Gastañaduy, Brian Pettiford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer causes 160,000 annual deaths in the U.S. Delays of >8 weeks after radiologic detection worsen outcomes: increasing upstaging risk and reducing median survival. This study aimed to evaluate whether single anesthesia events (SAEs), which combine diagnosis (via robotic bronchoscopy) and treatment (via robotic surgery), reduce time to treatment interval (TTI) and cost compared to the standard of care in early-stage lung cancer patients.MethodsEarly-stage lung cancer patients who underwent biopsy and resection from October 2021 to August 2024 were identified and grouped by surgical event: SAE vs. traditional. SAE patients underwent robotic biopsy, pathologic evaluation, and robotic resection in the same anesthetic encounter. Traditional patients underwent robotic biopsy on a separate day before robotic resection. Patient characteristics and outcomes were retrospectively compared.ResultsTotal of 61 patients (42 traditional, 19 SAE) were included. Patient characteristics had no significant difference. Median pathological nodule size (cm) was lower in the SAE cohort (1.5 vs. 2.1; P=0.03). Median time from diagnostic computed tomography (CT) to resection was 84.5 days for traditional patients vs. 48.0 days for SAE patients (P<0.001). Median time from initial pulmonologist consultation to resection was 62.5 days for traditional patients vs. 38.0 days for SAE patients (P=0.002). There was no significant difference in cost between the two.ConclusionsCompared with the traditional pathway, the more streamlined SAE approach was associated with a significantly shorter TTI without increased direct costs. These findings suggest that SAE may represent an operationally efficient approach to the management of selected patients with early-stage lung cancer, although larger studies are needed to assess quality-of-life benefits and to confirm the clinical & operational implications.

Highlight box

Key findings

• A single anesthesia event (SAE), combining robotic bronchoscopy (RB) with rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE) and robotic surgical resection, was associated with significantly shorter time-to-treatment intervals compared with the traditional staged diagnostic and surgical pathway.

• Median time from imaging to surgery decreased from 84.5 to 48.0 days, and median time from pulmonology consultation to surgery decreased from 62.5 to 33.0 days.

What is known and what is new?

• Delays in treatment initiation for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer are associated with disease progression and worse oncologic outcomes. RB, rapid on-site cytologic evaluation, and robotic-assisted thoracic surgery have each improved the diagnosis and management of pulmonary nodules.

• This study demonstrates that an SAE pathway can substantially reduce time to treatment compared with a traditional staged approach in a contemporary cohort of patients undergoing RB and robotic resection.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• For appropriately selected patients at experienced, high-volume centers, an SAE pathway may improve care delivery by reducing treatment delays and consolidating diagnostic and therapeutic procedures into a single anesthetic event.

• Because this was a retrospective single-center study with a limited sample size, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating.

• Prospective multicenter studies are needed to determine whether reduced time to treatment translates into improvements in patient-reported outcomes, long-term oncologic outcomes, complication rates, and cost-effectiveness.

Introduction

Lung cancer has been the leading cause of cancer mortality in the United States since the late 20th century, responsible for approximately 350 deaths per day and surpassing the annual mortality of breast, prostate, and colorectal cancers combined (1,2). Timely diagnostic and therapeutic interventions are critical, as delays in care significantly impact patient outcomes (3). One multicenter study of patients with positive lung cancer screenings revealed that nearly half experienced delays in follow-up, a factor that has been previously shown to correlate with clinical upstaging (4). Furthermore, treatment delays of ≥8 weeks following radiologic detection of lung cancer are associated with worse outcomes, including reduced median survival, increased 30-day mortality, and an increased risk of upstaging (5).

Among currently available diagnostic modalities, robotic bronchoscopy (RB) has demonstrated accuracy in navigation, improved ability to sample peripheral and difficult-to-reach lesions, and a higher diagnostic yield. Additionally, RB is associated with a lower rate of complications including pneumothorax, bleeding, and the need for post-procedure chest tube placement (6-9). Similarly, among the current treatment modalities for curative intent, robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS) has been shown to improve 5-year survival, reduce intraoperative complications, and enhance postoperative rehabilitation (10,11). Recent efforts to expedite lung cancer time to treatment interval (TTI) have included utilizing rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE) during radial endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) or bronchoscopy (12). One study found that the TTI from EBUS/bronchoscopy to a multidisciplinary team treatment-decision meeting was 77.9% shorter when ROSE was used, compared to cases without ROSE (13).

By leveraging the synergistic benefits of these different treatment modalities, multidisciplinary teams have explored single anesthesia events (SAEs), wherein suspicious nodules are biopsied, diagnoses are established using ROSE, and definitive surgical resections are performed all within the same setting (Figure 1). While previous studies have demonstrated the benefits of SAEs for lung cancer, their findings have been undermined by factors like inadequate control groups. Two case series, involving two (14) and fifteen (15) patients, respectively, have demonstrated the successful use of RB and RATS for lung cancer within an SAE. However, neither study included a matched control cohort. A retrospective cohort study found that SAEs not only reduced the time from identification to treatment but also decreased complication rates and hospital length of stay (LOS) (16). Notably, the control group in this study underwent a variety of treatments beyond RATS, including radiation and chemotherapy. Additionally, an abstract describing another retrospective cohort study has endorsed the safety of SAEs; however, the full-text article was unavailable to evaluate the specifics of the reported benefits (17). One study has specifically evaluated the clinical impact of SAEs in patients undergoing RB and RATS with a matched control group. This study also examined the cost implications in the SAE setting; however, this study discussed the time from imaging to surgery but did not address the possible influence of external primary care providers on the timeline. Furthermore, this study addressed direct and total cost, but did not provide further economic evaluation, such as reimbursement or net revenue (18). Therefore, our study aimed to evaluate whether the use of SAEs influenced time to treatment, complications rate, and overall procedural costs within a high-volume tertiary-level hospital system. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1272/rc).

Methods

This study is a single-center retrospective cohort included patients with suspected early-stage lung cancer who underwent either SAEs or standard of care (traditional pathway) between October 2021 to August 2024. The exposure variable was diagnostic pathway type, categorized as SAE versus the traditional diagnostic pathway involving sequential pulmonary, interventional radiology, pathology, and thoracic surgery evaluation. The study size was determined by the institutional implementation of the SAE pathway in 2022 and the availability of all consecutive eligible patients treated between October 2021 and August 2024; no formal sample size calculation was performed. Consecutive eligible patients meeting prespecified inclusion criteria were included to minimize selection bias. Data was collected retrospectively and entered into an institutional patient registry database. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ochsner Institutional Review Board (IRB) on 9/25/2024 (DHHS: FWA00002050), and the requirement for consent was waved by the IRB.

The primary endpoints were TTI, defined as intervals from imaging-to-surgery and pulmonary consultation-to-surgery (days), and postoperative complications occurring within 30 days of resection. Secondary endpoints included hospital LOS (days), direct costs, net revenue (USD), and other adverse events. Baseline demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, preoperative pulmonary function (FEV1 and DLCO, % predicted), smoking history (pack years), and tumor size (cm). These were obtained from patient medical records and reported as continuous variables.

Statistical analysis

Means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages were used to describe the entire population in terms of demographic and clinical characteristics. Comparison of demographic and clinical characteristics, as well as primary outcomes between patients in the standard care and SAE groups were examined with student t-tests or Wilcoxon tests for continuous variables and chi-square or Exact tests for categorical variables. All analyses were performed using SAS 15.2.

Results

The analysis included 61 patients: 42 patients who underwent the traditional approach, and 19 who underwent the SAE approach. Baseline clinical characteristics (Table 1) were comparable between the two cohorts. No significant differences were observed in age (P=0.30), sex distribution (female, P=0.26), preoperative pulmonary function (FEV1, P=0.71; DLCO, P=0.92), or smoking history (P=0.95). Lesion-level data collected showed tumor size differed significantly between groups, with smaller nodules observed in the SAE cohort (1.5 vs. 2.1 cm, P=0.03). Adenocarcinoma was the most common histologic type in both cohorts, making up 67% (n=28/42) of diagnoses in the traditional cohort and 68% (n=13/19) of diagnoses in the SAE cohort.

Clinical and operational outcomes (Table 2) demonstrated significant improvements in the SAE cohort. Specifically, preoperative time intervals were markedly reduced compared to the traditional approach: imaging-to-surgery decreased from 84.5 to 48.0 days (P<0.001), and pulmonology consultation-to-surgery decreased from 62.5 to 38.0 days (P=0.002). Additionally, net revenue was higher in the SAE group (P=0.03), despite no significant differences in direct costs (P=0.35) or hospital LOS (P=0.35). There were no major complications in either group. And while there were no minor complications observed in the SAE cohort and 12 minor complications in the traditional cohort, the study was not designed or powered to determine whether SAE independently influences complication rates (Table 3).

Discussion

In our study, the SAE protocol achieved a median imaging-to-surgery time to treatment interval (ISTTI) interval of 48 days. Since this timeline can be influenced by external primary care providers, the pulmonary consultation-to-surgery time to treatment interval (PSTTI) interval better reflects the efficiency of the SAE approach. This interval was just 38 days. Both intervals align with the recommended 6–8-week treatment initiation window for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (5,19). Delays in treatment for NSCLC are associated with reduced survival in early-stage disease, as one study demonstrated improved cancer-specific survival when TTI <1 month, while TTI >3 months was linked to worse overall survival (20). Another study from the Cleveland Clinic identified 50 days (7 weeks) as an inflection point for both 1- and 5-year survival (21). Based on these findings, we suggest that the time from diagnosis to treatment for stage IA1 NSCLC patients should ideally be under one month and not exceed two months. Our SAE protocol reduced ISTTI by 36.5 days and PSTTI by 24.5 days compared to the traditional approach. While shorter TTI has been associated with improved oncologic outcomes in prior observational studies, our study did not evaluate survival, recurrence, or long-term oncologic outcomes. Therefore, whether the observed reduction in TTI translates into improved survival remains unknown.

Although patient-reported outcomes were not collected in our dataset, consolidating diagnostic and therapeutic procedures into a single anesthetic event may reduce the logistical burden associated with multiple procedures. It is also reasonable to assume that earlier diagnosis and treatment reduce anxiety. This assumption is supported by a study showing that delays in treatment for adult oncology patients were associated with increased anxiety and depression (22). Future prospective studies incorporating validated patient-reported outcome measures are needed to determine whether SAE improves quality of life, treatment-related anxiety, or patient satisfaction.

Regarding potential confounders to our study, differences in payor mix between cohorts represents an important limitation when interpreting the financial analyses. The SAE cohort had a slightly higher commercial and private insurance payor mix compared to the traditional cohort. This likely contributed to the SAE cohort’s average $688 more reimbursement per case than the traditional cohort, and thus higher median net revenue (P=0.04). Also of note, while not statistically significant, there was a trend towards lower direct costs in the SAE cohort, showing an average of $4,579 less in direct costs per case. This trend may be associated with increased LOS in the traditional cohort, and the associated higher nursing costs. Thus, despite the difference in payor mix, the SAE cohort demonstrates a higher average contribution margin, which trends towards statistical significance (P=0.06). Contribution margin is a measure of profitability defined as net revenue minus direct costs attributable to patient care, excluding indirect costs. Importantly, reimbursement, net revenue, and contribution margin reflect institutional financial performance rather than direct patient-centered benefit. Nonetheless, the reduced direct cost and increased profitability suggest that SAE may be financially favorable from a hospital operational perspective; however, given the modest sample size and potential differences in payor mix between groups, these results should be interpreted as exploratory and require validation in larger studies.

Despite demonstrating several advantages of the SAE approach in treating lung cancer, our study has potential limitations. There were two false positives in the SAE cohort where patients underwent resection for benign conditions: hamartoma and sclerosing pneumocytoma.

In the case of sclerosing pneumocytoma, the final pathology, as well as ROSE, results favored low grade epithelial neoplasm. Importantly, results from ROSE and final pathology were concordant and ultimately, resections were carried out due to atypia on ROSE and malignant features such as PET avidity or increased growth. The occurrence of two benign resections in the SAE cohort highlights an important tradeoff of the SAE approach. Because definitive resection is performed before permanent pathology is available, there is an inherent risk of resection for lesions ultimately found to be benign. ROSE should therefore be considered an adjunct to, rather than a replacement for, definitive histopathologic diagnosis. In cases of significant diagnostic uncertainty, a staged approach with review of final pathology prior to resection may be preferable. As such, patients should be counseled preoperatively regarding the possibility of benign resection.

Potential sources of bias included selection bias due to non-random assignment to diagnostic pathways and information bias associated with retrospective chart review. Specifically, the SAE cohort presented with an overall lower median nodule size on final pathology (0.6 cm smaller) compared to the traditional cohort. This may reflect selection bias during early implementation of the SAE pathway, as smaller lesions may have been perceived as favorable candidates for combined diagnostic and therapeutic management or may reflect the benefit of reduced TTI. Differences in lesion complexity may have influenced complication rates, operative decision-making, and overall outcomes. However, this does not negate the faster TTI observed.

Given the limited sample size, the study was not adequately powered to support robust multivariable adjustment. Accordingly, all reported comparisons are unadjusted and remain susceptible to residual confounding. Finally, the generalizability of this study may be limited as well as it was a small, single institution study, and larger prospective studies are needed to validate these findings.

Conclusions

Although our retrospective design, single-center setting, and relatively small sample size limit extrapolation, the SAE approach to the diagnosis and treatment of lung cancer for early-stage NSCLC in our population aligns with clinical guidelines for timely intervention. SAE may reduce TTI and may be operationally feasible in selected patients at high-volume centers, but its effects on patient-centered outcomes, complications, cost-effectiveness, and long-term oncological outcomes require prospective validation. SAE potentially provides a streamlined, patient-centered pathway from diagnosis to treatment, making it a promising avenue for further investigation.
