Optimizing Cooling for
AI Data Centers
The Cooling Operations Optimized for Leaps in Energy, Reliability,
and Carbon Hyperefficiency for Information Processing Systems
(COOLERCHIPS) project, funded by ARPA-E, progresses energy-efficient
data center design by rethinking cooling for increasingly power-dense
AI hardware. As AI chips push beyond traditional thermal limits, cooling
becomes a mission-critical bottleneck: essential for performance and
reliability, yet historically one of the most energy-intensive parts of a
data center. COOLERCHIPS uses HPC to scale two-phase flow thermal
simulations from chip level to full data center architecture, exploring
designs that could reduce cooling energy consumption to under 5%
of the total information technology load—an order-of-magnitude
improvement over today’s systems.

Leveraging Kestrel’s large-scale simulation capabilities, researchers model
diverse operating conditions, identify optimal working points for chip-
and server-level cooling loops, and generate rich datasets to train ML
models that accurately predict two-phase heat transfer behavior. These
ML models are integrated to create a digital twin of the data center rack,
enabling optimized heat transfer under dynamic load conditions while
beneficially minimizing power usage effectiveness (PUE). This digital twin
framework directly informs cooling system design and control strategies
for the next generation of AI chips, servers, and racks, enabling more
efficiently cooled data centers.

 Charting Energy Savings
 Across the U.S. Building
 Stock
 NLR’s BuildStock platform combines its commercial
 (ComStock™) and residential (ResStock™) building models
 to generate datasets of end-use savings profiles. The profiles
 show the difference in hourly energy consumption between
 baseline buildings and buildings with some "what-if"
 measure scenario applied that improves energy efficiency or
 demand flexibility. Each time series profile is disaggregated
 by end use and fuel type. BuildStock leverages Kestrel to run
 millions of building energy simulations that estimate energy
 usage across the building stock and for the intensive data
 processing required to produce the individual profiles and
 complete dataset.

 With funding from DOE’s CMEI, in FY 2025, BuildStock added
 three new ComStock datasets, 31 measure scenarios, and
 13 reports. NLR conducted a user engagement survey to

 Faster Discovery of Efficient
 Thermoelectric Materials with ML
 Thermoelectric materials convert heat into electricity and vice versa,
 enabling cooling and waste heat recovery—two key pathways to
 improving energy efficiency across industrial, transportation, and
 consumer technologies. Supported by the National Science Foundation,
 researchers use HPC and ML to accelerate the discovery and
 optimization of Zintl phase thermoelectric materials with improved
 stability and efficiency.

 Zintl phases are intermetallic compounds that rank among the best
 performing thermoelectric materials. Leveraging HPC, researchers
 previously identified more than 1,800 new Zintl phases, laying the
 groundwork for rapid materials screening. This work was published in the
 Journal of Materials Chemistry A.7 Building on this foundation, the team
 developed a motif-based design strategy that treats atomic substructure
 like Lego-style building blocks for assembling new compounds. GPU-
 accelerated ML workflows on Kestrel then automated the combination
 and evaluation of these motifs, rapidly identifying promising new
 thermoelectric materials. This approach complements detailed
 thermodynamic modeling demonstrated in recent work published
 in Advanced Functional Materials,8 where first-principles simulations
 helped explain large variations in electron concentrations and guided
 synthesis conditions that enhance performance. These research efforts
 show how HPC and ML can dramatically speed thermoelectric materials

 generation energy conversion materials.

 Fast Neuromorphic Computing Hardware
 for Next Gen AI
 Neuromorphic computing, which mimics the structure and function of the human brain, offers
 a potential pathway for implementing energy-efficient AI. A key challenge in designing ion-
 based neuromorphic hardware is understanding how atomic-scale interactions in materials
 affect conductivity, switching behavior, and long-term stability—properties that directly
 influence the speed and energy efficiency of neuromorphic devices. Neuromorphic architectures
 rely on massive numbers of low-power, densely interconnected memory elements, such as
 electrochemical random-access memories (ECRAMs), whose conductance is tuned by ions
 moving from an ion reservoir, across the electrolyte, and into and out of the channel.

 In this project, researchers investigate ECRAM architectures that incorporate atomically thin,
 two-dimensional channel materials, whose conductivity is highly sensitive to the local ionic
 environment at the electrolyte/channel interface. Even small improvements in ion conductivity
 or charge transport at these interfaces can translate into significant reductions in total energy
 consumption. By modeling the atomic and electronic structures of potential new materials,
 researchers aim to better understand these interactions and identify material combinations that
 will maximize conductivity response while minimizing energy losses across ECRAM devices used
 in neuromorphic computing.

 Funded by the Semiconductor Research Consortium’s Joint University Microelectronics Program
 2.0 and using Kestrel for extensive first-principles calculations, researchers provided new
 insights into the suitability of materials for these devices. Findings from this work, published in
 MRS Advances,14 show how specific chemical configurations and atomic interactions alter the
 electronic and structural stability of promising device materials. This detailed understanding is
 critical, because stable interfaces and predictable electronic behavior are key to optimal operation
 in neuromorphic devices and circuits. By illuminating how atomic-level structure governs
 macroscopic performance, the team helps inform the design of next-generation materials
 that can support faster, more efficient neuromorphic computing hardware—advancing the
 development of AI systems that operate with dramatically lower energy requirements.

Molecular Simulations Improve
Water Purification Membranes
Reverse osmosis membranes are widely used for desalination and water
purification, yet their frequent deformation under compaction reduces
water flow and limits the membranes’ filtering capacity. Supported by
the National Alliance for Water Innovation (NAWI) and funded by DOE’s
CMEI, researchers use molecular modeling to understand how ultrahigh
pressures change membrane structure and how improved designs can
prevent this deformation.

Large-scale molecular simulations enabled by Kestrel allowed the
team to visualize, at the atomic level, how pressure and water flow
can compress individual membrane layers. These insights revealed
how even small structural shifts can restrict water throughput, raising
energy costs for clean water production. Building on this knowledge,
researchers identified a robust membrane design that resists physical
compaction while maintaining over 99% salt rejection and limiting water
permeability loss to under 10% at pressures up to 200 bar. Published in
Nature Communications,17 this work demonstrates how molecular-level
understanding can lead to more durable, longer-lasting desalination
membranes. By maintaining performance under demanding conditions,
these new membranes could reduce operating costs, improve industrial
reliability, and expand access to clean water in regions facing increasing
water stress.
