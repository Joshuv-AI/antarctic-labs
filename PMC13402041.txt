A challenging diagnosis of pediatric (cyclic) Cushing disease: the decisive role of hair cortisol
Authors: Lizanne Berkenbosch, Saartje Straetemans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCushing disease is a rare and often difficult diagnosis. Therefore, a significant delay between the onset of symptoms and the final diagnosis frequently occurs. We present a previously healthy 12-year-old boy in whom hypercortisolism was suspected based on clinical symptoms: abrupt excessive weight gain despite adequate exercise and dietary interventions in combination with growth arrest. The results of conventional biochemical diagnostics for Cushing disease were contradicting and could not confirm the diagnosis. Because of a persistent clinical suspicion after a wait-and-see period, cortisol levels in the boy's 11 cm long hair were analyzed. The very high hair cortisol levels supported the clinically suspected Cushing disease. Magnetic resonance imaging of the pituitary showed evidence of an adenoma, which was confirmed postoperatively as an adrenocorticotropic hormone producing adenoma. This confirmed the diagnosis of Cushing disease, with most likely a cyclical course, 1.5 years after the initial presentation. This case adds to the scarce literature on pediatric cyclic Cushing disease and emphasizes the added value of hair cortisol.Keywords: Cushing disease, hair cortisol, child

Introduction

The overall incidence of endogenous Cushing syndrome (CS) is 0.7 to 5 per million per year. Of these, only approximately 5 to 10% occur in the pediatric population [1-3]. Due to its rarity in children, many pediatricians are unfamiliar with the clinical presentation of CS in this age group. Unlike adult patients—who often exhibit symptoms such as muscle atrophy and easy bruising—children may present with different or more subtle signs [4, 5]. The most characteristic feature in children is a combination of weight gain and linear growth retardation [4, 6]. As in adults, the initial step in diagnosing CS is to confirm the presence of hypercortisolism. Commonly used diagnostic tools include 24-hour urinary free cortisol measurement, late-night salivary cortisol sampling, and the overnight dexamethasone suppression test. A major disadvantage of these tests however is that they only measure cortisol at a certain point in time and therefore do not provide any certainty about the days or weeks preceding and in between the tests. While these tests are generally sufficient in cases of overt CS, it poses a challenge in cases of mild or cyclic CS, where cortisol levels may fluctuate. Consequently, there is often a substantial delay between the onset of symptoms and definitive diagnosis [3, 6, 7]. One proposed solution is to repeat these tests over several days. However, in cyclic CS, episodes of hypercortisolism can last from hours to months [2], making the condition difficult to capture with intermittent testing. In recent years, hair cortisol analysis has emerged as a promising diagnostic tool in such cases. Cortisol is incorporated into growing hair shafts via passive diffusion from surrounding blood capillaries. Given the average hair growth rate of approximately 1 cm per month, segmental analysis of scalp hair can provide a retrospective timeline of cortisol exposure over several months [2, 8]. This is particularly advantageous in patients with suspected cyclic CS. In the case presented here, measurement of hair cortisol proved decisive in establishing the diagnosis. Since delays in diagnosis and treatment have been associated with increased long-term morbidity [9], we aim to highlight the utility of hair cortisol analysis as a valuable adjunct in the diagnostic work-up of pediatric patients with suspected cyclic CS.

Case presentation

A previously healthy 12-year-old boy presented with unexplained weight gain since approximately 18 months (Fig. 1). In an effort to counter this, he began exercising intensively and restricted his caloric intake. Despite these measures, there was no improvement in his weight trajectory. Physical examination revealed no signs of bruising, acanthosis nigricans, skin thinning, acne, hypertension, or muscle weakness. Pubertal development was consistent with the following Tanner stage: axillary hair development 1, pubic hair development 1, genital development 2, with testicular volumes of 5 mL bilaterally. School performance was normal, no concentration difficulties, or fatigue. Mild emotional difficulties were noted, possibly related to weight concerns. His growth chart showed a decline in linear growth velocity alongside continued weight gain (Figs. 1 and 2).

Diagnostic assessment

The combination of rapid weight gain and impaired linear growth raised suspicion of an underlying disorder, prompting further investigation. Initial screening showed normal thyroid function and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) levels and included two 24-hour urinary free cortisol measurements, both of which were elevated. Subsequent diagnostic tests for CS, as recommended by the Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline, yielded conflicting results [10]. Two overnight dexamethasone suppression tests showed no suppression of cortisol, arguing in favor of CS, while 3 midnight salivary cortisol measurements remained within the normal range, arguing against CS. Determination of plasma adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) failed to yield valid results. It was considered that these inconsistencies might result from pseudo-Cushing syndrome and/or potential noncompliance with dexamethasone administration at home. The case was discussed at a multidisciplinary team meeting, where consensus was reached to perform an additional in-hospital 2-mg 48-hour dexamethasone suppression test (0.5 mg dexamethasone administered orally every 6 hours for 48 hours). This demonstrated adequate cortisol suppression, including after corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) stimulation, arguing against the presence of CS. These discrepant findings prevented a definitive diagnosis of CS. The growth retardation was interpreted as a possible prepubertal dip in the absence of overt pathology. The patient was referred to an obesity management program, and his height, weight, and pubertal development were closely monitored. Despite these interventions, growth charts over the following months continued to show stagnation of linear growth alongside ongoing weight gain (Fig. 1 and 2). Additionally, there was no progression in pubertal development and endocrine assessment demonstrated low levels of luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH). Given the persistent clinical suspicion of CS, further diagnostic testing was performed, including repeated 24-hour urinary free cortisol and midnight salivary cortisol measurements, both of which yielded inconclusive and conflicting results (Table 1). To explore the possibility of cyclic CS, hair cortisol analysis was performed. The patient had 11 cm of scalp hair available for testing, allowing retrospective assessment of cortisol levels over approximately 11 months. Hair cortisol concentrations ranged from 13.5 pg/mg to 26.1 pg/mg, substantially exceeding reference values (50th percentile: 2.51 pg/mg, 97.5th percentile: 14.9 pg/mg) [11], indicating prolonged hypercortisolism. Subsequent magnetic resonance imaging of the pituitary gland revealed an image consistent with a possible pituitary adenoma (Fig. 3). Based on the biochemical evidence of cortisol excess, the imaging findings, and the fluctuating pattern of hypercortisolism, a diagnosis of cyclic Cushing disease due to an ACTH producing pituitary adenoma was established. Genetic testing (AIP, CDC73, CDKN1A, CDKN1B, CDKN2B, CDKN2C and MEN1) did not find a hereditary cause of pediatric pituitary adenoma.

Treatment

The patient was referred to a neurosurgical center, and treatment with metyrapone was initiated to reduce cortisol levels in preparation for surgery. He subsequently underwent transsphenoidal resection of a confirmed ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma.

Outcome and follow-up

Postsurgical morning serum cortisol level was low, indicating remission. Following surgery, the patient experienced transient diabetes insipidus and central hypothyroidism, both attributed to the trans-sphenoidal procedure.

Two years after surgery, the patient remained in remission. However, full recovery of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis was delayed, necessitating ongoing hydrocortisone replacement therapy for approximately 2 years. At follow-up, pubertal progression was appropriate and growth velocity increased substantially. However, the patient continued to experience severe fatigue, his academic performance had declined, and although his body mass index (BMI) has started to improve, it remains elevated.

Discussion

We described a 12-year-old boy exhibiting conflicting results throughout the diagnostic work-up for CS, despite a persistent and strong clinical suspicion. Ultimately, hair cortisol analysis confirmed the presence of hypercortisolism. Although the patient achieved remission following surgical removal of the ACTH-producing pituitary adenoma, he continued to experience long-term consequences of prolonged cortisol excess. Patients with a history of CS are at risk of developing long-term adverse health outcomes. These may include pituitary hormone deficiencies, hypertension, obesity, insulin resistance, cardiovascular dysfunction, and absence of catch-up growth following remission. In addition, psychological disturbances, reduced cognitive and academic performance, and impaired physical functioning have been reported [1, 3, 4, 12-14].

Due to its rarity—particularly in the pediatric population—and the diagnostic complexity, there is often a significant delay between the onset of symptoms and a confirmed diagnosis [3, 6, 7]. A longer diagnostic delay has been associated with an increased risk of persistent or recurrent Cushing disease following surgical treatment [13, 14]. Moreover, patients with a normal weight at the final follow-up were found to have had a shorter duration of hypercortisolism, a lower BMI-z-score at diagnosis, and greater reductions in BMI z-score at 6 ± 2 and 12 ± 2 months post-treatment compared with those who remained overweight or obese [9]. These findings underscore the importance of early recognition and diagnosis of CS in children to minimize the risk of long-term morbidity.

The conflicting test results in our patient were most likely attributable to an intermittent disease course, known as cyclic CS. Cyclic CS has been reported in approximately 15% of adult patients with CS, although some authors suggest that signs of cyclicity may be present in up to 70% of cases [15-17]. In contrast, reports on cyclic CS in the pediatric population are scarce and largely limited to case reports, likely due to the rarity of CS in children overall. To date, only 6 pediatric cases have been described in the literature, comprising 2 males and 4 females, aged between 3 and 15 years [18-23]. In all but 1 case, the diagnosis was cyclic Cushing disease caused by pituitary corticotropin overproduction. The exception involved the youngest patient, whose symptoms began shortly after birth and who was ultimately diagnosed with micronodular adrenocortical hyperplasia at the age of 3 years [23].

Although there is no universally accepted definition of cyclic CS, it is proposed that diagnosis requires biochemical evidence of at least 3 peaks and 2 troughs in cortisol levels [15-17]. In adults, cyclic CS has been reported in association with all etiologies of CS, and treatment approach is generally the same as in noncyclic cases. The underlying pathophysiology of cyclic CS remains poorly understood [2]. Cyclic CS should be considered when routine testing yields inconclusive or normal results despite a strong clinical suspicion. In such cases, repeated testing over time is recommended. In recent years, the analysis of cortisol levels in hair has gained attention as a valuable diagnostic method for detecting (cyclic) Cushing syndrome [8, 24, 25]. Cortisol becomes integrated into the hair shaft during its formation through passive diffusion from the bloodstream, with each centimeter of hair representing approximately 1 month of cortisol exposure due to the average hair growth rate of 1 cm per month. It allows for month-by-month reconstruction of cortisol exposure when hair is segmented appropriately. This enables clinicians to detect cyclic patterns or identify the approximate onset and duration of hypercortisolism. In addition to offering a retrospective timeline, hair cortisol measurement has several advantages: it is noninvasive, painless, stable at room temperature for prolonged periods, and relatively simple to collect and transport [24, 25]. These practical benefits make it particularly suitable for use in pediatric populations and outpatient settings. A limitation of this method is that sufficient hair length is required to evaluate long-term cortisol exposure.

In 2019, de Kruijf et al [11] established reference intervals for hair cortisol concentrations in a cohort of 625 healthy children. Their study showed no association between hair cortisol levels and gender, BMI, smoking, excessive sweating, or hair care practices (eg, use of gel or hair coloring products). However, ethnicity and age did influence cortisol concentrations, possibly due to differences in hair growth rate and structure. Future research should focus on the standardization of hair cortisol analysis, the establishment of diagnostic thresholds across different age groups and clinical conditions, its integration into diagnostic guidelines for suspected cyclic or subclinical CS, and its potential role in monitoring treatment response and disease recurrence [26, 27].

In conclusion, hair cortisol analysis was decisive in establishing the diagnosis of hypercortisolism in this case. Its retrospective nature proved particularly valuable in identifying a fluctuating pattern of cortisol excess, which is often missed by conventional testing. Given the known association between diagnostic delay and long-term morbidity in pediatric CS, hair cortisol analysis may offer an important complementary tool—especially in cases where clinical suspicion persists despite inconclusive standard test results.
