Exact analytical solutions for the piston effect in supercritical fluids under post-acoustic approximation – Short-time asymptotics, thermal penetration depth and comparison with the Spacelab D-2 experiments
Mátyás Szücs

Abstract
Near the liquid-vapor critical point, fluids become highly compressible, giving rise to a special, strongly coupled thermo-mechanical process: the piston effect. In this phenomenon, a thin thermal boundary layer develops near a heated wall; owing to strong thermal expansion, this layer acts like a piston, compressing the bulk fluid adiabatically and resulting in a seemingly accelerated thermal response. Although the piston effect is a thermo-acoustic process, the characteristic time scale of the boundary perturbation is typically orders of magnitude larger than the acoustic time scale of the setup. Consequently, rapid acoustic propagation can be neglected, justifying a post-acoustic approximation with a spatially uniform but time-dependent bulk pressure. Within the linear regime, the temporal evolution of pressure can be directly connected to the heat flux entering through the boundaries. As a result, the problem reduces to a diffusion equation governed by a spatially homogeneous source term that depends explicitly on the boundary conditions. Exact, closed-form analytical solutions are derived for effectively one-dimensional problems in both Cartesian and spherical coordinates, considering boundary conditions of the first and second kinds. Short-time asymptotic behavior and thermal penetration depth are analyzed for all four cases. By incorporating the heat capacity of a container via a homogeneous model, an effective boundary condition coupling the wall heat flux and the time derivative of the wall temperature is derived, allowing for a direct comparison with experimental data from the Spacelab D-2 mission. The analytical predictions show good agreement with the experimental results without relying on any numerical simulations.



I
Introduction

The thermodynamic distinction between the liquid and vapor phases vanishes at the critical point, where the coexistence curve terminates and the latent heat of vaporization becomes zero. Above the critical point, the fluid exhibits both liquid-like and gas-like properties at the same time, combining liquid-magnitude densities with gas-like extremely high compressibility and thermal expansion
6
;
17
, the latter two being strongly correlated through the Imre ellipse
30
. The emerging high compressibility—supplemented by the strong state dependence of thermophysical properties—gives rise to strongly coupled thermo-mechanical phenomena
5
;
37
;
36
;
16
.

The efficiency of thermodynamic cycles operating above the critical point drastically increases
9
, a feature that is increasingly exploited by next-generation energy technologies, such as Supercritical Water-Cooled Reactors
24
;
34
and Enhanced Geothermal Systems
11
;
25
. By applying supercritical fluid technologies, the risk of a conventional, subcritical boiling crisis
32
;
31
is entirely eliminated. However, this engineering advantage introduces a further thermal challenge: the phenomenon of heat transfer deterioration
19
, where a localized, sudden drop in the heat transfer coefficient leads to hazardous wall-temperature spikes that threaten structural integrity. Ensuring safe system operation in these applications fundamentally requires a deep understanding of the underlying strongly coupled thermo-mechanical interactions.

To clearly understand how different physical parameters affect these processes, exact analytical solutions provide the most powerful tool, offering direct physical insights that are often obscured in numerical formulations. However, the derivation of exact analytical solutions is severely limited, particularly for partial differential equations governing complex thermal systems. In these cases, the mathematical constraints imposed by both the boundary conditions and the non-trivial source terms often make closed-form solutions impossible to obtain. Consequently, explicit analytical expressions are rarely found in the literature for highly coupled processes.

The anomalous heat conduction behavior observed in the vicinity of the liquid–vapor critical point can be explained via a specific thermo-mechanical coupling
21
;
2
;
35
. Near the critical point, thermal diffusivity tends to zero, meaning that thermal equilibration is expected to slow down—a phenomenon referred to in the literature as “critical slowing down.” In contrast, microgravity experiments, where buoyancy-driven convection is eliminated, have demonstrated unexpectedly fast temperature equilibration, termed “critical speeding up”
13
;
27
. This contradiction is resolved by thermal expansion. Within a closed tank filled with a supercritical fluid, a heated wall induces a thermal boundary layer that expands intensively due to the large value of the thermal expansion coefficient. This expanding boundary layer compresses the bulk fluid like a piston, thereby causing an adiabatic-like temperature increase throughout the whole volume. This phenomenon, known as the piston effect, was originally described by Onuki
et al.
21
through the mechanism of initial isentropic, acoustic-wave propagation, and its characteristic time-scale structure has been discussed in detail in subsequent studies
3
. The associated thermoacoustic-wave dynamics, including acoustic emission, reflection, and resonance near the critical point, have also been investigated in subsequent thermoacoustic formulations
22
;
26
.

Regarding the piston effect—a classical benchmark problem that has been investigated both theoretically and experimentally—analytical solutions have been reported in the literature
21
;
27
. However, most classical analytical treatments are commonly restricted to idealized constant-temperature boundary conditions and one-dimensional Cartesian geometries. Moreover, these solutions are approximate in the sense that the rising bulk temperature is typically incorporated as a parametric correction at the thermal boundary layer rather than being obtained as a fully coupled state variable of the transient problem
21
. As a consequence, they do not provide an explicit closed-form description of the mutual evolution of the bulk temperature and the transient thermal boundary layer throughout the entire process. To the best of our knowledge, a fully coupled exact closed-form solution accounting for such thermal interactions over the complete transient evolution has not yet been reported for the configuration considered here.

To bridge this gap, the objective of this paper is to derive exact, closed-form analytical solutions for the post-acoustic approximation of the piston effect for effectively one-dimensional problems. In Sec.
II
, the post-acoustic approximation is derived from the linearized hydrodynamic balance equations through a two-time-scale asymptotic expansion. This reduction transforms the highly coupled thermo-mechanical problem into a simplified diffusion equation governed by a spatially homogeneous dynamic heat source that originates explicitly from the boundary conditions. In Sec.
III
, the exact solutions of the post-acoustic governing equation are derived in both Cartesian and spherical coordinates, considering boundary conditions of the first kind (prescribed wall temperature) and the second kind (prescribed wall heat flux). For all four fundamental configurations, the short-time asymptotic behavior and the thermal penetration depth are systematically analyzed in Sec.
IV
. However, boundary conditions appearing in real-life problems, such as the experiments conducted during the Spacelab D-2 mission, are more complex than constant wall temperature or constant heat flux
28
. Treating the wall of the cell applied in those tests as a homogeneous body, a boundary condition coupling the wall heat flux directly to the time derivative of the wall temperature is derived in Sec.
V
. The analytical solution derived from this coupled boundary condition shows good agreement with the experimental data obtained during the Spacelab D-2 mission, without relying on any numerical simulations.

II.1
Asymptotic separation of acoustic and thermal phenomena

Asymptotic descriptions of near-critical thermoacoustic and boundary-layer processes have been developed in various previous studies
8
;
38
. In particular, such analyses have identified the coexistence of wave-like behavior in the bulk and diffusion-dominated dynamics in the boundary layer. However, the present objective is different: here, the multiple-scale analysis
1
is applied to systematically resolve the coupled thermo-acoustic phenomena. As will be demonstrated, the reciprocal of the Péclet number corresponds exactly to the ratio of the acoustic and thermal diffusion time scales, which serves as the small scale-separation parameter. By combining this two-scale asymptotic method with a systematic averaging over the fast acoustic processes, the post-acoustic approximation of the piston effect is obtained, which proves to be equivalent to the fundamental model proposed by Boukari
et al.
2

36
) the classical diffusion equation is obtained

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial t}=a\nabla^{2}\Theta+(\gamma-1)a\frac{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{V}}\left.\nabla\Theta\right|_{\partial\Omega}\cdot\mathbf{n},

(42)

where a homogeneous volumetric heat source emerges, originating from the boundary heating.

Let us observe that in (
42
), the ratio of the heated surface area to the fluid volume automatically introduces a characteristic geometric length scale, hence now we fix
\ell
as

\displaystyle\ell=f\frac{\mathcal{V}}{\mathcal{A}},

(43)

where
f
denotes a dimensionless geometric shape factor reflecting the dimensionality of the domain:

\displaystyle\text{plane wall: }f=1,\quad\text{cylinder: }f=2,\quad\text{sphere: }f=3.

(44)

Alongside the classical diffusion time scale
\tau_{\rm d}=\frac{\ell^{2}}{a}
, a further time scale characterizing the apparent bulk heating appears, defined respectively as

\displaystyle\tau_{\rm p}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau_{\rm d}}{f\left(\gamma-1\right)},

(45)

thus reducing the governing equation (
36
) to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial t}=a\nabla^{2}\Theta+\frac{\ell}{\tau_{\rm p}}\left.\nabla\Theta\right|_{\partial\Omega}\cdot\mathbf{n}.

(46)

Although the acoustically originated spatial pressure gradients have vanished, compressibility manifests itself in the energy balance through the specific heat ratio
\gamma
embedded within
\tau_{\rm p}
. Approaching the critical point, heat capacity ratio diverges, hence
\tau_{\rm p}\ll\tau_{\rm d}
, but in parallel,
\tau_{\rm a}\ll\tau_{\rm p}
, therefore,
\tau_{\rm p}
emerges as an intermediate time scale reflecting the permanent footprint of the suppressed fast time scale acoustic dynamics on the slow diffusion time scale.

To characterize how the localized conduction and the global compression interact, (
46
) can be rearranged as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial t}=\frac{\ell^{2}}{\tau_{\rm d}}\left(\nabla^{2}\Theta+\frac{1}{\ell}\frac{\tau_{\rm d}}{\tau_{\rm p}}\left.\nabla\Theta\right|_{\partial\Omega}\cdot\mathbf{n}\right).

(47)

The explicit appearance of the time-scale ratio
\frac{\tau_{\rm p}}{\tau_{\rm d}}\ll 1
in (
47
) provides a clear physical basis to establish distinct transport regimes. Introducing
\zeta
as the wall-normal coordinate measured directly from the heated boundary, the domain naturally splits into two asymptotic regions:

•

Near the boundary,
i.e.,
when
\zeta\ll\ell\frac{\tau_{\rm p}}{\tau_{\rm d}}
, local heat conduction dominates the process, establishing a steep thermal gradient, nevertheless modulated by the global compression.

•

Throughout the vast majority of the bulk fluid,
i.e.,
when
\zeta\gg\ell\frac{\tau_{\rm p}}{\tau_{\rm d}}
, the piston effect drives the dynamics. The rapid adiabatic compression entirely dictates the instantaneous temperature rise, mathematically explaining the famously observed critical speeding up of the system.

From a thermoacoustic perspective, this separation of regimes reflects the underlying coupling between fast acoustic propagation and slow thermal diffusion. In particular, the rapid adiabatic compression of the bulk—mediated by acoustic waves—appears in the post-acoustic limit as an effective volumetric heat source, while the thermal diffusion remains confined to a thin boundary layer. Therefore, the classical thermoacoustic description—typically formulated in terms of coupled wave and diffusion equations—reduces in the present asymptotic limit to a boundary-driven heat transfer problem, where the piston effect manifests as a heterogeneous source term in the diffusion equation.

III
Analytical determination of the temperature field for the piston effect near a heated wall

In this section, exact analytical solutions for the governing equation of the post-acoustic approximation (
46
) are derived. The transient thermal response to specific boundary perturbations is investigated, assuming an initially homogeneous equilibrium temperature
T_{0}
. We consider strictly one-dimensional heat propagation, presenting solutions in both planar geometry (
e.g.,
, for ”long” insulated pipes of length
L
and cross-sectional area
A
) and spherical geometry of radius
R
. The latter configuration more closely mimics realistic experimental setups, such as those detailed in
28
. Accordingly, the characteristic geometric length scale yields
\ell=L
for the planar case and
\ell=R
for the spherical case. Denoting the generalized spatial coordinate by
\xi
(where
\xi
represents the axial coordinate
x
in planar geometry and the radial coordinate
r
in spherical geometry), the temperature field is sought in the additive form

\displaystyle\Theta(t,\xi)=\Theta_{\rm b}(t)+\Theta_{\rm d}(t,\xi),

(48)

where
\Theta_{\rm b}(t)
denotes the spatially uniform bulk (hereafter also termed volumetric) temperature rise induced by the piston effect—recalling that the volumetric source term in (
46
) is homogeneous—and
\Theta_{\rm d}(t,\xi)
represents the localized contribution of thermal diffusion. As a reminder, these temperature contributions, as well as the corresponding boundary conditions, are defined as offsets relative to the initial equilibrium temperature
T_{0}
. Consequently, (
46
) can be reformulated using the generalized one-dimensional Laplace operator as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Theta_{\rm d}}{\partial t}=a\frac{1}{\xi^{f-1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\left(\xi^{f-1}\frac{\partial\Theta_{\rm d}}{\partial\xi}\right)+\left(\frac{\ell}{\tau_{\rm p}}\left.\frac{\partial\Theta_{\rm d}}{\partial\xi}\right|_{\xi\in\partial\Omega}\cdot\mathbf{n}-\frac{{\rm d}\Theta_{\rm b}}{{\rm d}t}\right),

(49)

where
f=1
corresponds to the planar geometry and
f=3
to the spherical configuration [cf. (
44
)]. Since the bulk heating is driven entirely by thermal expansion resulting from surface heat flux, (
49
) can be decoupled into an ordinary differential equation governing the bulk temperature rise,

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\Theta_{\rm b}}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell}{\tau_{\rm p}}\left.\frac{\partial\Theta_{\rm d}}{\partial\xi}\right|_{\xi\in\partial\Omega}\cdot\mathbf{n},

(50)

\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm b}(0)

\displaystyle=0,

(51)

and a classical, unforced diffusion equation for the localized thermal perturbation,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Theta_{\rm d}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=a\frac{1}{\xi^{f-1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\left(\xi^{f-1}\frac{\partial\Theta_{\rm d}}{\partial\xi}\right),

(52)

\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm d}(0,\xi)

\displaystyle=0.

(53)

The dynamic coupling between the bulk temperature rise (
50
) and the diffusive field (
52
) is established through the temperature gradient at the boundary.

Within both geometrical configurations, boundary conditions of the first (
i.e.,
Dirichlet) and second (
i.e.,
Neumann) kind are investigated. For the Dirichlet case, a constant wall temperature
T_{\rm w}
is prescribed at the boundary,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\Theta(t,\xi)\big|_{\xi\in\partial\Omega}=\Theta_{\rm w},

(54)

where
\Theta_{\rm w}=T_{\rm w}-T_{0}
, which yields the time-dependent boundary condition for the diffusive component:

\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm d}(t,\xi)\big|_{\xi\in\partial\Omega}=\Theta_{\rm w}-\Theta_{\rm b}(t).

(55)

For the Neumann case, a constant wall heat current density
\dot{q}_{\rm w}
is prescribed at the boundary,
i.e.,

\displaystyle-\dot{\mathbf{q}}(t,\xi)\big|_{\xi\in\partial\Omega}\cdot\mathbf{n}=\dot{q}_{\rm w},

(56)

which relates to the temperature gradient via Fourier’s law as

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial\Theta_{\rm d}}{\partial\xi}\right|_{\xi\in\partial\Omega}\cdot\mathbf{n}=\frac{\dot{q}_{\rm w}}{\lambda}.

(57)

By convention,
\dot{q}_{\rm w}>0
corresponds to a heated wall. Assuming that the diffusive penetration depth remains far from the core and opposite boundaries, semi-infinite solutions are sought subject to the asymptotic condition

\displaystyle\lim_{\xi\rightarrow\infty}\Theta_{\rm d}(t,\xi)=0.

(58)

Consequently, the validity of the solutions presented below is restricted to the short-time regime,
t\ll\tau_{\rm d}
. Table
1
summarizes the variables, parameters, and boundary conditions for both configurations.

Usually, analytical solutions are first presented for boundary conditions of the first kind, followed by those of the second kind. In the present case, however, due to the bulk heating effect, a constant wall temperature yields a time-dependent boundary condition of the first kind for the diffusive temperature contribution, which demands significantly more complex calculations. Therefore, for didactic reasons, we begin with the boundary condition of the second kind, thereby moving from the mathematically simpler case toward the more complex ones.

III.1
Constant heat flux on the wall

Since a constant heat flux is prescribed at the wall, the evolution of the bulk temperature rise can be integrated directly. For the planar configuration, this yields

\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm b}(t)

\displaystyle=L\frac{\dot{q}_{\rm w}}{\lambda}\frac{t}{\tau_{\rm p}},

(59)

whereas for the spherical configuration, it gives

\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm b}(t)

\displaystyle=R\frac{\dot{q}_{\rm w}}{\lambda}\frac{t}{\tau_{\rm p}}.

(60)

Consequently, in both geometries, the bulk temperature increases or decreases strictly linearly with time.

III.1.1
Planar geometry

The resulting subproblem corresponds to classical diffusion with a boundary condition of the second kind in a semi-infinite domain, the solution of which is expressed as

\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm d}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{q}_{\rm w}}{\lambda}\sqrt{4at}\ierfc\frac{x}{\sqrt{4at}},

(61)

where
\ierfc\eta=\frac{\exp\left(-\eta^{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}}-\eta\erfc\eta
denotes the first integral of the complementary error function, and
\erfc\eta
is the standard complementary error function
7
. To facilitate a direct comparison between the diffusive and bulk contributions, it is advantageous to rewrite the pre-factor of
\ierfc
in (
61
) into a form analogous to (
59
), yielding

\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm d}(t,x)

\displaystyle=L\frac{\dot{q}_{\rm w}}{\lambda}\sqrt{4\frac{t}{\tau_{\rm d}}}\ierfc\frac{x}{\sqrt{4at}}.

(62)

III.1.2
Spherical geometry

In spherical coordinates, the governing equation can be reduced to a planar form with modified boundary conditions by employing the standard transformation

\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm d}(t,r)=\frac{\vartheta(t,r)}{r}.

(63)

The corresponding spatial derivatives take the form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Theta_{\rm d}}{\partial r}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\vartheta+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\vartheta}{\partial r},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{2}\frac{\partial\Theta_{\rm d}}{\partial r}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial^{2}\vartheta}{\partial r^{2}}.

(64)

Furthermore, we introduce a localized spatial coordinate
\zeta
, measured from the outer surface of the sphere pointing inward along the negative radial direction, defined as

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=R-r,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial r}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\zeta^{2}}.

(65)

Substituting these transformations yields the following initial-boundary value problem for the auxiliary variable
\vartheta
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\vartheta}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=a\frac{\partial^{2}\vartheta}{\partial\zeta^{2}},

(66)

\displaystyle\vartheta(0,\zeta)

\displaystyle=0,

(67)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R^{2}}\vartheta(t,0)+\frac{1}{R}\left.\frac{\partial\vartheta}{\partial\zeta}\right|_{\zeta=0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\dot{q}_{\rm w}}{\lambda}.

(68)

This formulation represents a classical diffusion problem with a boundary condition of the third kind (Robin condition) in a semi-infinite region
7
. Solving this system and transforming the result back to the original variables yields the diffusive temperature field:

\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm d}(t,r)

\displaystyle=R\frac{\dot{q}_{\rm w}}{\lambda}\frac{R\exp\left(-\frac{\zeta^{2}}{4at}\right)}{R-\zeta}\left[\erfcx\left(\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{4at}}-\sqrt{\frac{t}{\tau_{\rm d}}}\right)-\erfcx\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{4at}}\right],

(69)

where
\erfcx\eta=\exp\left(\eta^{2}\right)\erfc\eta
denotes the scaled complementary error function.

III.2
Constant wall temperature

A constant non-zero temperature difference prescribed at the wall results in a time-dependent boundary condition of the first kind for the diffusion problem, which is treated here using the Laplace transform method. The inverse Laplace transforms of the resulting non-trivial expressions are summarized in App.
A
.

III.2.1
Planar geometry

Applying the Laplace transform with respect to the time coordinate to the governing equations (
50
), (
52
) and the boundary condition yields

\displaystyle s\hat{\Theta}_{\rm d}(s,x)

\displaystyle=a\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{\Theta}_{\rm d}}{\partial x^{2}},

(70)

\displaystyle s\hat{\Theta}_{\rm b}(s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{L}{\tau_{\rm p}}\left.\frac{\partial\hat{\Theta}_{\rm d}}{\partial x}\right|_{x=0},

(71)

\displaystyle\hat{\Theta}_{\rm d}(s,0)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Theta_{\rm w}}{s}-\hat{\Theta}_{\rm b}(s),

(72)

where
s
denotes the complex frequency (
i.e.,
the Laplace-domain variable). The negative sign on the left-hand side of (
71
) originates from the outward-pointing normal vector. Enforcing the physical requirement that the temperature field must remain bounded as
x\rightarrow\infty
, the solution of (
70
) is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\Theta}_{\rm d}(s,x)

\displaystyle=A(s)\exp\left(-\sqrt{\frac{s}{a}}x\right),

(73)

where
A(s)
represents the complex frequency-dependent amplitude. Correspondingly, the boundary values are
\hat{\Theta}_{\rm d}(s,0)=A(s)
and
\left.\frac{\partial\hat{\Theta}_{\rm d}}{\partial x}\right|_{x=0}=-\sqrt{\frac{s}{a}}A(s)
. Substituting these expressions into (
71
) and (
72
) leads to the system

\displaystyle s\hat{\Theta}_{\rm b}

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{\tau_{\rm p}}\sqrt{\frac{s}{a}}A(s)=\frac{\sqrt{\tau_{\rm d}}}{\tau_{\rm p}}\sqrt{s}A(s),

\displaystyle A(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Theta_{\rm w}}{s}-\hat{\Theta}_{\rm b}(s),

(74)

which via partial fraction decomposition yields

\displaystyle\hat{\Theta}_{\rm b}(s)=\Theta_{\rm w}\frac{\frac{\sqrt{\tau_{\rm d}}}{\tau_{\rm p}}}{s\left(\sqrt{s}+\frac{\sqrt{\tau_{\rm d}}}{\tau_{\rm p}}\right)}=\frac{\Theta_{\rm w}}{\frac{\sqrt{\tau_{\rm d}}}{\tau_{\rm p}}}\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}+\frac{\frac{\sqrt{\tau_{\rm d}}}{\tau_{\rm p}}}{s}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}+\frac{\sqrt{\tau_{\rm d}}}{\tau_{\rm p}}}\right),

(75)

and, in virtue of (
73
)

\displaystyle\hat{\Theta}_{\rm d}(s,x)=\Theta_{\rm w}\frac{\exp\left(-\sqrt{\frac{s}{a}}x\right)}{\sqrt{s}\left(\sqrt{s}+\frac{\sqrt{\tau_{\rm d}}}{\tau_{\rm p}}\right)}=\frac{\Theta_{\rm w}}{\frac{\sqrt{\tau_{\rm d}}}{\tau_{\rm p}}}\exp\left(-\sqrt{\frac{s}{a}}x\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}+\frac{\sqrt{\tau_{\rm d}}}{\tau_{\rm p}}}\right).

(76)

The time-domain temperature contributions are determined by performing the inverse Laplace transformation on (
75
), giving

\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm b}(t)

\displaystyle=\Theta_{\rm w}\left[1-\erfcx\left(\sqrt{\frac{\tau_{\rm d}}{\tau_{\rm p}}\frac{t}{\tau_{\rm p}}}\right)\right],

(77)

\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm d}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\Theta_{\rm w}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{4at}\right)\erfcx\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{4at}}+\sqrt{\frac{\tau_{\rm d}}{\tau_{\rm p}}\frac{t}{\tau_{\rm p}}}\right).

(78)

III.2.2
Spherical geometry

First, the governing equations (
50
), (
52
) and the associated boundary condition are converted from spherical coordinates to the localized Cartesian inward radial coordinate via (
63
) and (
65
). Then, performing the Laplace transform with respect to time yields

\displaystyle s\hat{\vartheta}(s,\zeta)

\displaystyle=a\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{\vartheta}}{\partial\zeta^{2}},

(79)

\displaystyle s\hat{\Theta}_{\rm b}(s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{R}{\tau_{\rm p}}\Bigg(\frac{1}{R^{2}}\hat{\vartheta}(s,0)+\frac{1}{R}\left.\frac{\partial\hat{\vartheta}}{\partial\zeta}\right|_{\zeta=0}\Bigg),

(80)

\displaystyle\hat{\vartheta}(s,0)

\displaystyle=R\left(\frac{\Theta_{\rm w}}{s}-\hat{\Theta}_{\rm b}(s)\right).

(81)

Enforcing the physical requirement of a bounded temperature field as
\zeta\to\infty
leads to the solution of (
79
) in the form

\displaystyle\hat{\vartheta}(s,\zeta)

\displaystyle=A(s)\exp\left(-\sqrt{\frac{s}{a}}\zeta\right).

(82)

Substituting (
82
) into (
80
) and (
81
) yields the transformed bulk temperature rise,

\displaystyle\hat{\Theta}_{\rm b}(s)=\Theta_{\rm w}\frac{\frac{\sqrt{\tau_{\rm d}}}{\tau_{\rm p}}\sqrt{s}-\frac{1}{\tau_{\rm p}}}{s\left(s+\frac{\sqrt{\tau_{\rm d}}}{\tau_{\rm p}}\sqrt{s}-\frac{1}{\tau_{\rm p}}\right)}=\Theta_{\rm w}\left[\frac{1}{s}+\frac{1}{\mu_{-}-\mu_{+}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}-\mu_{+}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}-\mu_{-}}\right)\right]

(83)

where the final expression is obtained via partial fraction decomposition using the roots
\mu_{\pm}
of the characteristic quadratic polynomial in
\sqrt{s}
found in the denominator:

\displaystyle\mu_{\pm}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sqrt{\tau_{\rm d}}}{\tau_{\rm p}}\left(1\pm\sqrt{1+4\frac{\tau_{\rm p}}{\tau_{\rm d}}}\right).

(84)

Accordingly, the transformed diffusive field is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\vartheta}(s,\zeta)=-\Theta_{\rm w}\frac{R\exp\left(-\sqrt{\frac{s}{a}}\zeta\right)}{\mu_{-}-\mu_{+}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}-\mu_{+}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}-\mu_{-}}\right).

(85)

Finally, the inverse Laplace transformation provides the analytical solution in the time domain:

\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm b}(t)

\displaystyle=\Theta_{\rm w}\left[1+\frac{\mu_{+}}{\mu_{-}-\mu_{+}}\erfcx\left(-\mu_{+}\sqrt{t}\right)-\frac{\mu_{-}}{\mu_{-}-\mu_{+}}\erfcx\left(-\mu_{-}\sqrt{t}\right)\right],

(86)

\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm d}(t,\zeta)

\displaystyle=-\Theta_{\rm w}\frac{R\exp\left(-\frac{\zeta^{2}}{4at}\right)}{R-\zeta}\Bigg[\frac{\mu_{+}}{\mu_{-}-\mu_{+}}\erfcx\left(\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{4at}}-\mu_{+}\sqrt{t}\right)-\frac{\mu_{-}}{\mu_{-}-\mu_{+}}\erfcx\left(\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{4at}}-\mu_{-}\sqrt{t}\right)\Bigg].

(87)

IV
Short-time asymptotics and thermal boundary layer thickness

In this section, the short-time asymptotics and the thermal boundary layer thickness are analyzed in non-dimensional form. This analysis is performed for both the planar and spherical configurations under Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions. Time is scaled by the characteristic piston time-scale
\tau_{\rm p}
, while spatial dimensions are normalized by the characteristic length scale
\ell
, yielding the dimensionless time and space variables

\displaystyle\check{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{t}{\tau_{\rm p}},

\displaystyle\check{\xi}

\displaystyle=\frac{\xi}{\ell}.

(88)

The ratio of the piston time-scale to the diffusion time-scale directly appears in all solutions and is denoted by
\varepsilon=\frac{\tau_{\rm p}}{\tau_{\rm d}}
. In the vicinity of the critical point,
\varepsilon\ll 1
, meaning that
\varepsilon
serves as the fundamental small parameter to distinguish between the short-time and long-time regimes. For the Dirichlet boundary condition, the temperature is non-dimensionalized using the prescribed wall temperature difference
\Theta_{\rm w}
. For the Neumann boundary condition, the natural temperature scale is given by the dimensional coefficient
\ell\frac{\dot{q}_{\rm w}}{\lambda}
. Consequently, the dimensionless temperature is defined as

\displaystyle\check{\Theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Theta}{\Theta_{\rm w}},

and

\displaystyle\check{\Theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Theta}{\ell\frac{\dot{q}_{\rm w}}{\lambda}},

(89)

respectively. Since the thermal boundary layer develops in the immediate vicinity of the heated wall, the solutions are analyzed in terms of the wall-normal coordinate
\zeta
, where
\zeta=x
in the planar geometry and
\zeta=R-r
in the spherical configuration. Finally, the thermal boundary layer thickness, representing the thermal penetration depth, is evaluated using the classical gradient-based definition applied strictly to the diffusive temperature contribution
\Theta_{\rm d}
,

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm th}(t)=\frac{\Theta_{\rm d}(t,\zeta=0)}{-\left.\frac{\partial\Theta_{\rm d}}{\partial\zeta}\right|_{\zeta=0}}.

(90)

Note that it is physically essential to isolate
\Theta_{\rm d}
and exclude the bulk temperature rise
\Theta_{\rm b}
from this evaluation, because the rapid adiabatic compression uniformly elevates the background fluid temperature. Evaluating the penetration depth using the total temperature field would obscure the true thickness of the conduction-dominated zone, as the local wall heating operates on top of a dynamically rising bulk baseline.

Throughout the subsequent parts of this section, the dimensionless spatial variables utilized for the planar and spherical configurations are explicitly defined as

\displaystyle\check{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{L},

\displaystyle\check{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\frac{R-r}{R}=\frac{\zeta}{R}.

(91)

For all numerical evaluations and plots presented in this section, the single free model parameter is fixed at
\varepsilon=0.01
.

IV.1
Constant heat flux on the wall

The dimensionless temperature field for the Neumann boundary condition in Cartesian coordinates can be formulated as

\displaystyle\check{\Theta}(\check{t},\check{x})=\check{t}+\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\varepsilon\check{t}}\exp\left(-\frac{\check{x}^{2}}{4\varepsilon\check{t}}\right)-\check{x}\erfc\frac{\check{x}}{\sqrt{4\varepsilon\check{t}}},

(92)

directly leading to the classical thermal penetration depth

\displaystyle\check{\delta}_{\rm th}(\check{t})=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\varepsilon\check{t}}.

(93)

Although the solution is not strictly self-similar due to the explicit appearance of the length scale
\ell
in (
46
), the similarity variable

\displaystyle\eta(\check{t},\check{x})=\frac{\check{x}}{\check{\delta}_{\rm th}(\check{t})}

(94)

proves highly effective. Thereby, (
92
) can be reformulated as

\displaystyle\check{\Theta}(\check{t},\check{x})=\check{t}+\check{\delta}_{\rm th}(\check{t})\left[\exp\left(-\frac{\eta^{2}(\check{t},\check{x})}{\pi}\right)-\eta(\check{t},\check{x})\erfc\frac{\eta(\check{t},\check{x})}{\sqrt{\pi}}\right].

(95)

Within the thermal boundary layer,
i.e.,
, for
\check{x}\ll\check{\delta}_{\rm th}(\check{t})
, corresponding to
\eta(\check{t},\check{x})\ll 1
, the dimensionless temperature field (
95
) can be approximated as

\displaystyle\check{\Theta}(\check{t},\check{x})\approx\check{t}+\check{\delta}_{\rm th}(\check{t})\left[\left(1-\frac{\eta^{2}(\check{t},\check{x})}{\pi}\right)-\eta(\check{t},\check{x})\left(1+\frac{2}{\pi}\eta(\check{t},\check{x})\right)\right]\approx\check{t}+\check{\delta}_{\rm th}(\check{t})\left[1-\eta(\check{t},\check{x})\right]=\check{t}-\check{x}+\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\varepsilon\check{t}}.

(96)

The resulting temperature distribution reveals a dynamically shifting linear profile, in which the superposition of three distinct physical phenomena can be recognized. The linear spatial dependence
-\check{x}
ensures flux consistency at the interface, establishing the steady Fourier gradient required to transport the prescribed heat flux into the medium. This time-independent spatial gradient is superimposed on the linearly growing bulk temperature
\check{t}
driven by the global adiabatic compression. Finally, the term
\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\varepsilon\check{t}}
represents the near-boundary temperature rise caused by local diffusion. This indicates that while the local temperature gradient remains fixed to satisfy the constant heat flux condition, this linear profile shifts upward as time progresses. Furthermore, as the thermal boundary layer grows, the spatial extent of this linear zone continuously expands deeper into the medium, meaning that its far end moves progressively further away from the heated wall. These are illustrated in Fig.
1
.

A straightforward scaling check of the similarity variable reveals an important physical constraint regarding the condition
\eta\ll 1
. While this inequality is naturally satisfied near the heated boundary, mathematically it can also be fulfilled if
\sqrt{\varepsilon\check{t}}\gg 1
. In dimensional variables, this secondary limit corresponds to
\sqrt{\frac{t}{\tau_{\rm d}}}\gg 1
, which represents the long-time diffusion-dominated regime. Since this contradicts our initial assumption that
t\ll\tau_{\rm d}
, no physical conclusions can be drawn from this secondary limit within the framework of the present model.

Dimensionless temperature distribution evaluated at equal time steps in the interval
\check{t}\in[0.01,1]
under a constant wall heat flux for the planar configuration, where time progresses from the black to the red lines. The solid lines represent the exact analytical solution, and the dashed lines denote the linear boundary asymptotics presented in (
96
).

Outside the thermal boundary layer,
i.e.,
, for
\check{x}\gg\check{\delta}_{\rm th}(\check{t})
, corresponding to
\eta(\check{t},\check{x})\gg 1
, the diffusive contributions decay, reducing (
95
) to the uniform bulk temperature

\displaystyle\check{\Theta}(\check{t},\check{x})\approx\check{t}+\check{\delta}_{\rm th}(\check{t})\left[\exp\left(-\frac{\eta^{2}(\check{t},\check{x})}{\pi}\right)-\eta\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{\eta^{2}(\check{t},\check{x})}{\pi}\right)}{\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\pi}}\right]=\check{t}.

(97)

This limit where
\eta\gg 1
carries profound physical meaning for the early-stage dynamics. At the very beginning of the process, the thermal boundary layer thickness approaches zero, implying that the condition
\eta\gg 1
holds true throughout virtually the entire fluid volume. In dimensional variables, this short-time regime directly corresponds to
\sqrt{\frac{t}{\tau_{\rm d}}}\ll 1
. Since this perfectly aligns with our initial assumption that
t\ll\tau_{\rm d}
, the physical validity of this limit is strictly consistent with the scope of our model. This reveals that initially the fluid undergoes a spatially homogeneous temperature rise driven by the hidden acoustic process manifesting in the adiabatic compression.

The dimensionless temperature field for the Neumann boundary condition in the spherical geometry is given by

\displaystyle\check{\Theta}(\check{t},\check{\zeta})=\check{t}+\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{\check{\zeta}^{2}}{4\varepsilon\check{t}}\right)}{1-\check{\zeta}}\left[\erfcx\left(\frac{\check{\zeta}}{\sqrt{4\varepsilon\check{t}}}-\sqrt{\varepsilon\check{t}}\right)-\erfcx\frac{\check{\zeta}}{\sqrt{4\varepsilon\check{t}}}\right],

(98)

which yields the thermal penetration depth

\displaystyle\check{\delta}_{\rm th}(\check{t})=\erfcx\left(-\sqrt{\varepsilon\check{t}}\right)-1=2\exp\left(\varepsilon\check{t}\right)-\erfcx\sqrt{\varepsilon\check{t}}-1.

(99)

When
\sqrt{\varepsilon\check{t}}\ll 1
, corresponding to the short-time regime
t\ll\tau_{\rm d}
, (
99
) can be approximated as

\displaystyle\check{\delta}_{\rm th}(\check{t})\approx 2\left(1+\varepsilon\check{t}\right)-\left(1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\varepsilon\check{t}}\right)-1=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\varepsilon\check{t}}\left(1+\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\varepsilon\check{t}}\right).

(100)

Constant wall temperature

For the Dirichlet boundary condition, the dimensionless temperature field in Cartesian coordinates can be written as

\displaystyle\check{\Theta}(\check{t},\check{x})=1-\erfcx\sqrt{\frac{\check{t}}{\varepsilon}}+\exp\left(-\frac{\check{x}^{2}}{4\varepsilon\check{t}}\right)\erfcx\left(\frac{\check{x}}{\sqrt{4\varepsilon\check{t}}}+\sqrt{\frac{\check{t}}{\varepsilon}}\right),

\displaystyle\check{\delta}_{\rm th}(\check{t})=\frac{\erfcx\sqrt{\frac{\check{t}}{\varepsilon}}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\varepsilon\check{t}}}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\erfcx\sqrt{\frac{\check{t}}{\varepsilon}}}.

(105)

To understand the initial transient behavior, we analyze the short-time regime under the condition
\sqrt{\frac{\check{t}}{\varepsilon}}\ll 1
(implying
\check{t}\ll\varepsilon\ll 1

\displaystyle\check{\delta}_{\rm th}(\check{t})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{\check{t}}{\varepsilon}}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\varepsilon\check{t}}}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{\check{t}}{\varepsilon}}\right)}=\varepsilon\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\check{t}}{\varepsilon}}\frac{1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{\check{t}}{\varepsilon}}}{1-\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\check{t}}{\varepsilon}}+2\frac{\check{t}}{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\approx\varepsilon\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\check{t}}{\varepsilon}}\left(1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{\check{t}}{\varepsilon}}\right)\left(1+\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\check{t}}{\varepsilon}}\right)\approx\sqrt{\pi\varepsilon\check{t}}\left(1+\frac{\pi-2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{\check{t}}{\varepsilon}}\right).

(106)

The leading-order term
\sqrt{\pi\varepsilon\check{t}}
corresponds to classical diffusion, while the first-order correction captures the distortion induced by the bulk heating.

Conversely, for longer times where
\sqrt{\frac{\check{t}}{\varepsilon}}\gg 1

\displaystyle\check{\delta}_{\rm th}(\check{t})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\check{t}}{\varepsilon}}}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\varepsilon\check{t}}}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\check{t}}{\varepsilon}}}}\rightarrow\infty,

(107)

confirming that as time progresses, the local diffusive perturbation expands in an unbounded domain and eventually fills the entire bulk domain, rendering the short-time semi-infinite assumption invalid.

V
Transient response considering the thermal inertia of the container wall

When modeling an experimental configuration, the idealization of an instantly prescribed constant wall temperature or a strictly constant heat flux may result in a predicted temperature response that differs strongly from the actually measured values.

Regarding the microgravity experiments performed during the Spacelab D-2 mission, the heat pulse experiments
3
3

3

In a heat pulse experiment, the duration of the boundary heating is orders of magnitude shorter than all other characteristic time scales, as numerically verified in subsection
V.3
were conducted in a thin-walled spherical tank made of copper and coated with a thin gold layer to minimize radiation heat losses. The heating wire was glued on the outer surface of this sphere along a single equator. Since copper is an excellent heat conductor, the wall temperature proved to be almost perfectly homogeneous during the experiments. The numerical solutions presented by Straub
et al.
applied the measured wall temperature directly as a boundary condition, thereby achieving excellent agreement with the experimental data
28
. However, when no experimentally measured boundary values are available, this procedure cannot be implemented. In such cases, a comprehensive model of the experimental setup can provide deeper insight. The heating wire warms up due to the electric current flowing through it, which heats the outer surface of the tank. The inner surface of the shell, heated by conduction through the wall, then transfers heat to the fluid layer in contact with it. A detailed analysis of the setup reveals that the heat capacity of the wire is negligible relative to that of the wall, but the latter is comparable to the heat capacity of the supercritical fluid filling. Furthermore, the thin-walled copper sphere shell can be treated as a lumped homogeneous body because its internal diffusion time scale is negligible even compared to the short duration of the boundary heating.

In what follows, we formulate the conjugate boundary condition
23
;
12
characterizing the energy storage capacity of the wall, which delays and dampens the thermal footprint of the heater. The post-acoustic temperature response in the fluid domain is then presented by applying this derived effective boundary condition. Finally, the obtained exact analytical solution is validated against the experimental data gathered during the Spacelab D-2 mission.

V.1
Formulation of the conjugate boundary problem and the effective boundary condition

As previously noted, the heat capacity of the heater wire is neglected, therefore the heating power
\dot{Q}_{\rm h}
emitted by the wire directly heats the sphere shell, which is treated as a lumped capacitance body. The schematic representation of the experimental setup is presented in Fig.
10
. Accordingly, the energy balance of the shell can be formulated as

\displaystyle m_{\rm w}c_{\rm w}\frac{{\rm d}\Theta_{\rm w}}{{\rm d}t}=\dot{Q}_{\rm h}-\dot{Q}_{\rm w},

(124)

where
m_{\rm w}
and
c_{\rm w}
represent the mass and the specific heat capacity of the wall, respectively, while
\dot{Q}_{\rm w}
denotes the heating power transferred from the wall to the fluid layer in contact with it.

Figure 10:
Schematic of the experimental setup used in the microgravity experiments during the Spacelab D2 mission.

Conjugate conditions at the solid–fluid interface provide continuity of the temperature and heat flux fields, expressed as

\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm w}(t)

\displaystyle=\Theta(t,R),

(125)

\displaystyle\lambda\left.\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial r}\right|_{r=R}

\displaystyle=\dot{q}_{\rm w},

(126)

where
R
denotes the inner radius of the spherical shell. For a thin-walled shell with wall thickness
\delta_{\rm w}
satisfying
\delta_{\rm w}\ll R
, the outer and inner surface areas can be approximated as equal. For simplicity, the inner radius is chosen as the only characteristic dimension of the system. Therefore, the mass of the shell can be approximated as
m_{\rm w}\approx\varrho_{\rm w}4\pi R^{2}\delta_{\rm w}
. Consequently, the solid–fluid heat flux can be derived from (
124
) as

\displaystyle\dot{q}_{\rm w}=\dot{q}_{\rm h}-\varrho_{\rm w}c_{\rm w}\delta_{\rm w}\frac{{\rm d}\Theta_{\rm w}}{{\rm d}t},

(127)

with the heater heat flux defined as
\dot{q}_{\rm h}=\frac{\dot{Q}_{\rm h}}{4\pi R^{2}}
and the solid–fluid heat flux as
\dot{q}_{\rm w}=\frac{\dot{Q}_{\rm w}}{4\pi R^{2}}
. Note that both heat current densities are evaluated relative to the inner surface of the shell. Substituting (
127
) into the conjugate boundary condition (
126
) and applying the temperature continuity constraint (
125
) yields the effective boundary condition

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial r}\right|_{r=R}=\frac{\dot{q}_{\rm h}}{\lambda}-\frac{\varrho_{\rm w}c_{\rm w}\delta_{\rm w}}{\lambda}\left.\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial t}\right|_{r=R},

(128)

acting directly on the fluid domain. This effective boundary condition can be recognized as a modified Neumann boundary condition where the ideal constant heat flux is dynamically dampened and delayed due to the heat capacity of the container. The coefficient of the time derivative of the temperature can be rearranged as

\displaystyle\frac{\varrho_{\rm w}c_{\rm w}\delta_{\rm w}}{\lambda}=\frac{\varrho_{\rm w}c_{\rm w}}{\varrho c_{p}}\frac{\delta_{\rm w}}{a}=\frac{\varrho_{\rm w}c_{\rm w}}{\varrho c_{p}}\sqrt{\frac{a_{\rm w}}{a}}\frac{\delta_{\rm w}}{\sqrt{a_{\rm w}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\rm w}\varrho_{\rm w}c_{\rm w}}{\lambda\varrho c_{p}}}\sqrt{\frac{\tau_{\rm w}}{a}}=\frac{\mathcal{B}\sqrt{\tau_{\rm w}}}{\sqrt{a}},

(129)

where
a_{\rm w}=\frac{\lambda_{\rm w}}{\varrho_{\rm w}c_{\rm w}}
is the thermal diffusivity of the solid wall and
\tau_{\rm w}=\frac{\delta_{\rm w}^{2}}{a_{\rm w}}
is the corresponding diffusion time scale of the shell, furthermore,
\mathcal{B}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\rm w}\varrho_{\rm w}c_{\rm w}}{\lambda\varrho c_{p}}}
denotes the involved thermal effusivity ratio. This representation highlights that the thermal contact and the corresponding energy buffering at the solid–fluid interface can be characterized by an emerging effective characteristic thermal velocity
\mathsf{v}_{\rm th}=\frac{\sqrt{a}}{\mathcal{B}\sqrt{\tau_{\rm w}}}=\frac{\sqrt{a_{\rm w}a}}{\mathcal{B}\delta_{\rm w}}
. Nevertheless, to simplify the subsequent calculations, we retain the original unified form of the coefficient presented in (
129
).

Substituting the temperature field decomposition (
48

While such an extension is mathematically possible, the present first-order formulation already fulfills the primary objective of this study by providing a closed-form analytical description that captures the bulk fluid thermalization and the essential time scales of the coupled transient response with sufficient accuracy.

VI
Conclusions

In this study, the post-acoustic approximation of the piston effect was analyzed. By applying the method of multiple scales, the fast acoustic transients and the slow thermal diffusion processes were systematically separated, providing a validation for the zero-flow thermalization model originally proposed by Boukari
et al.
2
. Furthermore, instead of using the classical integro-differential equation formulation, a boundary-coupled diffusion equation was derived.

The primary theoretical and practical contributions of this work are summarized through the following focal points:

•

Derive exact, closed-form analytical solutions to the post-acoustic approximation of the piston effect for both planar and spherical geometries under Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions.

•

Analyze the thermal penetration depth and short-time asymptotic behavior for all four configurations investigated above. Our analysis proved that in the short-time regime and near the heated boundary, the spherical and planar solutions coincide to leading order, making the planar approximation adequate. A comparison of our exact solutions with that proposed by Straub
et al.
27
shows that, although the latter correctly captures the initial temporal evolution, its use of a spatially decoupled formulation leads to an overprediction of the thermal response at longer times.

•

Incorporate a dynamic conjugate boundary condition to analyze the Spacelab D-2 microgravity experiments, where the exact analytical solution demonstrates good agreement with the experimental bulk temperature data.

The derived solutions are valid in the short-time regime, before the thermal disturbance reaches the opposite boundary or, in the spherical configuration, the center of the domain. At later times, the semi-infinite approximation breaks down and the finite-domain problem can be continued by a Fourier-type eigenfunction expansion, using the present short-time solution as a matched initial state. In that regime, however, the response becomes increasingly governed by global diffusion and finite-size equilibration rather than by a localized boundary-layer piston-effect mechanism alone. Therefore, the present solutions are intended to provide the closed-form short-time branch of the full transient response, where the piston-effect-driven boundary–bulk coupling is most clearly exposed.

The exact analytical solutions developed in this framework provide a powerful benchmark for validating complex numerical solvers, while offering direct physical insights, including a detailed parameter dependency analysis of the piston effect. Furthermore, expanding the presented asymptotic framework to higher-order terms of the small parameter remains an interesting future direction, specifically to investigate the onset and behavior of the secondary fluid flows that are expected to emerge over the slow diffusive time scale.
