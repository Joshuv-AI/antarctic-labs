CST2 promotes melanoma malignant phenotypes through the IL-6-STAT3-NF-κB signaling axis
Authors: Lei Zhao, Jinhua Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMelanoma is a highly aggressive malignancy with increasing incidence and mortality over recent decades. Cystatin SA (CST2) encodes a secreted cysteine protease inhibitor that is overexpressed in various cancers and promotes tumor progression; however, its role in melanoma remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the expression and clinical significance of CST2 in melanoma as well as its biological functions and underlying molecular mechanisms in melanoma progression.MethodsCST2 expression was analyzed in melanoma tissues using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset. The prognostic value of CST2 was assessed using Kaplan-Meier analysis. In vitro gain- and loss-of-function experiments were performed to evaluate the effect of CST2 on melanoma cell proliferation, colony formation, and migration. Mechanistic studies included protein-protein docking and co-immunoprecipitation to detect the interaction between CST2 and interleukin-6 (IL-6). Western blotting was used to examine the activation status of IL-6 downstream signaling pathways.ResultsCST2 was significantly upregulated in melanoma tissues and was correlated with poor patient prognosis. Overexpression of CST2 promoted melanoma cell proliferation, clonogenicity, and migration, whereas CST2 knockdown suppressed these malignant behaviors. CST2 interacted with IL-6, and its knockdown reduced the expression of IL-6, phospho-STAT3, and phospho-NF-κB. These suppressive effects were reversed by IL-6 overexpression, indicating that CST2 exerted its oncogenic effects through the IL-6-STAT3-NF-κB axis.ConclusionsCST2 promotes melanoma progression by activating the IL-6-STAT3-NF-κB signaling pathway, and may serve as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in melanoma.Keywords: Melanoma, Cystatin SA, CST2, IL-6-STAT3-NF-κB signaling axis

Highlight box

Key findings

• Cystatin SA (CST2) is significantly upregulated in melanoma tissues and correlates with poor patient prognosis.

• CST2 promotes melanoma cell proliferation, colony formation, and migration in vitro.

• Mechanistically, CST2 interacts with interleukin-6 (IL-6) and positively regulates the IL-6/STAT3/NF-κB signaling axis.

What is known and what is new?

• Melanoma is a highly aggressive skin malignancy with increasing global incidence, and novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets are urgently needed. Cystatins have been implicated in various cancers, but the specific role of CST2 in melanoma progression and its underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood.

• This study systematically characterizes CST2 expression and prognostic value in melanoma using TCGA data, combined with in vitro functional assays and mechanistic investigations. We demonstrate for the first time that CST2 physically interacts with IL-6 and activates the downstream STAT3/NF-κB signaling cascade, establishing a novel CST2/IL‑6 regulatory axis that drives melanoma progression.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Our findings position CST2 as a potential prognostic biomarker and highlight the CST2-IL‑6 axis as a candidate pathway for further mechanistic and translational exploration in melanoma. However, given the current limitations of in vitro evidence and the absence of in vivo and independent clinical validation, the therapeutic relevance of targeting CST2 remains preliminary. Future studies using animal models, larger clinical cohorts, and more detailed molecular dissection are warranted to assess the translational potential of this axis.

Introduction

Melanoma is a highly aggressive malignancy, with steadily increasing incidence and mortality rates in recent decades. Patients with metastatic disease have an extremely poor prognosis; metastasis to vital organs such as the lungs, liver, or brain triggers severe complications, drastically diminishes quality of life, and leads to life-threatening conditions, with a five-year survival rate of merely 15–20% (1,2). The management of advanced or metastatic melanoma is particularly challenging due to the high risk of recurrence and frequent development of resistance to available therapies (3,4). Consequently, the discovery of novel biomarkers for prognostic prediction and therapeutic intervention represents a critical and urgent need in melanoma research.

Cystatin SA (CST2) encodes a secreted cysteine protease inhibitor found in high concentrations in bodily fluids, including saliva, tears, and seminal plasma. Functioning as an oncogene, CST2 is overexpressed in a range of malignancies, such as breast, prostate, ovarian, colorectal, pancreatic, and gastric cancers, where its upregulation is associated with tumor prognosis and contributes to oncogenesis and progression (5-7). This evidence suggests that CST2 could serve as a biomarker for early cancer detection and prognostic assessment. Nevertheless, its precise role in melanoma pathogenesis remains unclear.

The interleukin-6 (IL-6)-STAT3-NF-κB signaling axis functions as a pivotal signaling hub in tumorigenesis, coordinating key oncogenic processes, such as cell proliferation, apoptosis evasion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), angiogenesis, immune escape, and the maintenance of cancer stemness (8-10). Although physiologically important, its frequent dysregulation in various malignancies underscores its pathogenic role. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms governing its activity and dysregulation remain unclear.

In this study, CST2 was significantly overexpressed in melanoma tissues and served as an independent factor for a poor prognosis. Functional experiments showed that CST2 enhances the malignant phenotype of melanoma cells. Mechanistically, CST2 acts as a ligand for IL-6 and exerts its function through the IL-6-STAT3-NF-κB signaling axis, highlighting its potential as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0795/rc).

Methods

Differential expression analysis

Gene expression profiles and corresponding clinical information for the Skin Cutaneous Melanoma (SKCM) project were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database to construct the TCGA-SKCM dataset. This dataset comprised 473 samples (472 tumor samples and 1 adjacent normal tissue sample). All 472 tumor samples were extracted and stratified into two groups for comparative analyses based on clinical information: (I) dead patients vs. surviving patients; and (II) patients with pathologic stage IV disease vs. patients with pathologic stage I–III disease. Differential expression analysis was performed on the raw counts matrix using the R package DESeq2, with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified at thresholds of |log2 fold change| >1 and adjusted P value (adj.P.Val) <0.05.

To more reliably identify genes aberrantly expressed in tumor tissues, we constructed an integrated TCGA_GTEx-SKCM dataset comprising a total of 1,282 samples, including 812 normal skin tissue samples from the GenotypeTissue Expression (GTEx) database, 1 adjacent normal tissue sample from TCGA-SKCM, and 469 tumor tissue samples from TCGA-SKCM. From this integrated dataset, we extracted all TCGA melanoma tumor samples (n=469) and GTEx normal skin samples (n=812), and performed differential expression analysis comparing tumor versus normal tissues using the R package limma. Genes with log2 fold change >1 and adj.P.Val <0.05 were defined as significantly upregulated, while genes with log2 fold change <−1 and adj.P.Val <0.05 were defined as significantly downregulated.

Using the expression data and overall survival (OS) information from tumor samples in the TCGA-SKCM dataset, we performed univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis to screen for genes significantly associated with prognosis (P<0.05). Subsequently, the DEGs identified from the comparisons of deceased vs. surviving patients and pathologic stage IV vs. stage I–III were respectively intersected with the prognosis-associated genes to identify candidate genes that simultaneously exhibited differential expression and prognostic significance. The intersection results were visualized using the R packages ggplot2 and VennDiagram. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Prognostic analysis of CST2

To further evaluated the prognostic significance of CST2 expression in patients with melanoma. Survival analysis was performed to assess the association between CST2-high and CST2-low expression groups based on the median CST2 expression level from the TCGA-SKCM dataset. To evaluate the prognostic value of CST2 in melanoma, a univariate Cox regression analysis was performed by incorporating clinical variables and CST2 expression. Variables demonstrating marginal significance (P<0.1) in the univariate analysis were subsequently included in a multivariate Cox regression model to adjust for potential confounding effects. The results are presented as forest plots to visualize hazard ratios and confidence intervals.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation analysis is a commonly used approach for large-scale functional enrichment analysis of genes, encompassing three categories: biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) is a widely used database that stores information on genomes, biological pathways, diseases, drugs, and related topics. GO functional annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed on the DEGs associated with melanoma using the R package clusterProfiler, with statistical significance defined as a P value <0.05. GO analysis encompassed three primary categories: BP, MF, and CC.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

To further characterize pathway-level alterations, we performed GSEA using the “c2.cp.all.v2022.1. Hs.symbols.gmt” gene set collection (containing 3,050 canonical pathways) from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). GSEA was implemented using the clusterProfiler package, with a nominal P value <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Cell culture and transfection

Human melanoma cell lines A375 and SK-MEL-28 were all purchased from Chinese Academy of Sciences Typical Culture Collection Cell Bank, Shanghai, China (catalog number: SCSP-533, SCSP-5223), and cultured in DMEM (1×) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and maintained at 37 ℃ in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For gene knockdown experiments, cells were transfected with either CST2-shRNA1 (TTTGTCATCAGCGAGTATAAC) or CST2-shRNA2 (TGAAT-TACTTCTTCGACATAG) using Lipofectamine 145 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, L3000001, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Non-targeting scrambled short hairpin RNA (shRNA) (CST2-NC) was used as a negative control. The full-length human CST2 coding sequence (NCBI Reference Sequence: NM_001322.3) was polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-amplified from a human complementary DNA (cDNA) library and subcloned into the lentiviral expression vector CD513B. The construct was verified by Sanger sequencing. For lentivirus production, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with the CST2 knockdown or overexpression plasmid and the packaging plasmids pMD2.G and psPAX2 (Addgene) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Viral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 h post-transfection. A375 and SK-MEL-28 cells were seeded at 2×105 cells/well in 6-well plates, cultured overnight to 50–60% confluence, and transduced with lentiviral particles at an multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 5 in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with medium containing 2 µg/mL puromycin for selection over 10–14 days (with medium changes every 3 days), and the surviving cells were expanded and maintained in 1 µg/mL puromycin. The construct was verified by Sanger sequencing. CST2 knockdown or overexpression was validated by Western blotting (WB).

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells/well and cultured for 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h post-transfection. At each time point, the medium was replaced with 100 µL of fresh medium containing 10 µL of the CCK-8 reagent, followed by incubation for 2 h at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2. The absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader.

Clonogenic formation assay

For the colony formation assay, cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 4,000 cells/well in 2 mL of complete DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The plates were gently agitated to ensure uniform cell distribution, and then incubated for 7–14 days under standard culture conditions to allow colony development. Following incubation, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde or pure methanol for 15 min at room temperature, and then rinsed thoroughly with distilled water. After air-drying, colonies were visualized and counted under an inverted optical microscope.

Transwell assay

Cell invasion capability was assessed using a Transwell chamber system (Corning, 3422, USA). Following transfection, the A375 cells were harvested and resuspended in serum-free DMEM. A total of 100 µL of the cell suspension (2×105 cells/mL) was seeded into the upper chamber of Transwell insert. The lower chamber was filled with 600 µL DMEM containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 48 h of incubation at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2, non-invading cells on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully removed using a cotton swab. Cells that had invaded through the Matrigel and adhered to the lower membrane surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 10 min. Following washing with PBS and air-drying, the stained cells were visualized and quantified under an inverted light microscope (Nikon, ECLIPSE Ts2R, Japan) in five random fields per insert.

WB analysis

Cells were harvested for total protein extraction. Proteins were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Scientific, 78442, USA). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 ℃ and the supernatants were collected. The protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (PierceTM, A65453, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of protein (20–30 µg per lane) were separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST (TBS with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature, and the putative interacting partner overnight at 4 ℃ with the following primary antibodies: CST2 (1:2,000, Cat No.19935-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), c-Myc (1:2,000, Cat No.10828-1-AP, Proteintech), cyclin D1 (1:2,000, Cat No.60186-1-Ig, Proteintech), Vimentin (1:2,000, Cat No.10366-1-AP, Proteintech), E-cadherin (1:2,000, Cat No.20874-1-AP, Proteintech), IL-6 (1:2,000, Cat No.66146-1-Ig, Proteintech), phospho-STAT3 (1:2,000, Cat No.9145S, CST), STAT3 (1:2,000, Cat No.9139S, CST), phospho-NF-κB (1:2,000, Cat No.3033S, CST), NF-κB (1:2,000, Cat No. 8242S, CST), and GAPDH (1:2,000, Cat No.A19056, ABclonal, Wuhan, China). After washing with TBST, the membrane was incubated with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and a chemiluminescence imaging system.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

The cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and harvested by scraping. Cell pellets were lysed on ice for 30 min using IP lysis buffer [25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1% NP-40, and 5% glycerol] supplemented with a complete protease inhibitor cocktail and phosphatase inhibitors.

The whole cells lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4 ℃. The supernatant (whole-cell extract) was collected, and the protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit. The pre-cleared lysate was divided into two aliquots: one for the specific antibody (IP) and the other for the control immunoglobulin G (IgG control) overnight at 4 ℃ with constant rotation. The following day, 30 µL of 50% slurry of protein A/G agarose beads was added to each sample to capture the antibody-antigen complexes. The incubation was continued for an additional 2–4 hours at 4 ℃ with rotation, and the immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted from the beads by adding 40 µL of 2× protein sample buffer and boiling at 95 ℃ for 10 min.

After boiling, the samples were centrifuged briefly at 12,000 ×g for 2 min to remove the beads. The supernatants were resolved by SDS-PAGE on 10% or 12% polyacrylamide gels (depending on the molecular weight of the target proteins) and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature. Following blocking, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 ℃ with gentle shaking. After three washes with TBST three times, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (diluted 1:5,000–1:10,000 in blocking buffer) for 1 h at room temperature. Following three additional washes with TBST, the protein bands were detected with a chemiluminescence imaging system.

Statistical analysis

All data calculations and statistical analyses were performed using R programming (version 4.0.2; https://www.r-project.org/). All experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated at least thrice independently. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance between groups was determined using two-tailed Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was set at *P<0.05.**P<0.01, ***P<0.001

Results

CST2 is highly expressed in melanoma and correlates with adverse clinical outcomes

Comprehensive differential expression analysis identified 543 genes that were significantly altered in deceased melanoma patients dead compared to alive, and 734 genes were differentially expressed in pathologic stage IV melanoma compared to stages I–III. Concurrently, Cox regression analysis revealed 1,992 genes associated with poor OS. The intersection of these gene sets through Venn analysis yielded 10 candidate genes: COL17A1, SLC9A2, NTS, PNLDC1, AQP5, PCSK9, CST2, TRIM71, AC005281.1, and AC093866.1 (Figure 1A, table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tcr-2026-0795-1.xlsx). Among these, NTS, CST2, TRIM71, and AC005281.1 exhibited significantly elevated expression in melanoma tissues compared to that in normal controls (Figure 1B). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis confirmed that high expression levels of these four genes were significantly correlated with an unfavorable prognosis (Figure 1C-1F), and high CST2 expression was significantly associated with poor prognosis in both GSE19234 and GSE65904 datasets (Figure S1). Given that CST2 showed the most pronounced upregulation, it was selected for the subsequent investigation. Both univariate (hazard ratio =1.322, 95% confidence interval: 1.009–1.733, P=0.043) and multivariate (P=0.051) Cox regression analyses established CST2 expression as a prognostic factor for melanoma (Figure 1G, Table 1).

Overexpression of CST2 promotes the malignant phenotype of melanoma

IHC results showed CST2 protein expression was significantly upregulated in melanoma tissues compared to normal controls (Figure 2A,2B). WB confirmed the generation of stable A375 and SK-MEL-28 cell lines overexpressing CST2, and a series of functional experiments were conducted (Figure 2C,2D). The results demonstrated that CST2 overexpression significantly enhanced the proliferative capacity, clonogenicity, and migratory ability, as determined by CCK-8, colony formation, and Transwell assays, respectively (Figure 2E-2I). WB results showed that overexpression of CST2 increased the expression of c-Myc, cyclin D1, and Vimentin, and decreased the expression of E-cadherin (Figure 2J,2K).

Knockdown of CST2 suppressed tumor proliferation, clonogenicity, and migration

Subsequently, WB confirmed that stable CST2 knockdown cell lines were established (Figure 3A,3B). CCK-8, colony formation, and Transwell assays confirmed that CST2 knockdown inhibited cell proliferation, clonogenic ability, and migration in both A375 and SK-MEL-28 cells (Figure 3C-3G). WB results showed that knockdown of CST2 resulted in decreased expression of c-Myc, cyclin D1, and Vimentin, while increased expression of E-cadherin was observed (Figure 3H,3I).

Functional enrichment analysis implicates CST2 in pro-tumorigenic pathways

Stratification of TCGA melanoma transcriptomic data based on CST2 expression levels defined the CST2-low and CST2-high cohorts. Differential expression analysis of these groups identified 936 significantly upregulated and 419 downregulated genes (Figure 4A). GSEA revealed significant enrichment of multiple oncogenic pathways in the CST2-high group, including REACTOME_FCERI_MEDIATED_MAPK_ACTIVATION, REACTOME_FCGR3A_MEDIATED_IL-6_SYNTHESIS, REACTOME_SIGNALING_BY_THE_B_CELL_RECEPTOR_BCR, and REACTOME_FCERI_ MEDIATED_NF_κB_ACTIVATION (Figure 4B), which are functionally associated with tumor progression and invasion. Additional functional enrichment analysis highlighted the involvement of the IL-17 signaling pathway and epidermal cell differentiation (Figure 4C,4D). WB results showed that CST2 knockdown reduced the expression of IL-6, phospho-STAT3, and phospho-NF-κB (Figure 4E,4F), indicating that CST2 affects the IL-6-STAT3-NF-κB axis signaling pathway.

CST2 interacts with IL-6 to regulate melanoma progression through the IL-6-STAT3-NF-κB axis signaling pathway

Protein-protein docking simulations identified potential interaction sites between CST2 and IL-6, a finding validated by Co-IP assays, which confirmed their endogenous interaction (Figure 5A,5B). Functional analyses, including CCK-8, colony formation, and Transwell assays, revealed that IL-6 overexpression rescued the impaired proliferation, colony formation, and migration resulting from CST2 knockdown. This is consistent with these phenotypic observations (Figure 5C-5G). WB analysis demonstrated that IL-6 overexpression restored the expression levels of IL-6, phospho-STAT3, and phospho-NF-κB, which were suppressed by CST2 knockdown (Figure 5H,5I). Taken together, these results indicate that CST2 interacts with IL-6 to regulate malignant phenotypes in melanoma via the IL-6-STAT3-NF-κB signaling axis.

Discussion

Melanoma, a malignant neoplasm originating from melanocytes, is one of the most aggressive cancers globally, and typically originates from melanocytes in the skin. This cancer is characterized by high malignancy, pronounced propensity for metastasis and recurrence, and is responsible for approximately 75% of all skin cancer-related mortalities (11,12). Despite recent advances in targeted therapy and immunotherapy, the prognosis for patients with advanced-stage melanoma remains poor, underscoring the urgent need for novel prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

The cystatin family of proteins, traditionally known as cysteine protease inhibitors, has been increasingly implicated in tumor progression across multiple cancer types (13,14). However, the specific role of CST2 in melanoma has remained largely unexplored. In the present study, we identified CST2 as a significantly upregulated gene in melanoma tissues that was associated with poor clinical outcomes. Through integrated bioinformatic screening, functional assays, and mechanistic investigations, we demonstrated that CST2 promoted melanoma cell proliferation, clonogenicity, and migration.

The IL-6-STAT3-NF-κB signaling axis represents a cornerstone in the molecular pathogenesis of multiple malignancies and serves as a critical bridge between chronic inflammation and tumor development (15,16). In this study, knockdown of CST2 suppressed the expression of IL-6, phospho-STAT3, and phospho-NF-κB. Furthermore, we demonstrated that CST2 interacted with IL-6 and regulated malignant cellular phenotypes through the IL-6-STAT3-NF-κB signaling cascade.

However, several important questions remain unanswered, given the current limitations of in vitro evidence and the absence of in vivo and independent clinical validation, the therapeutic relevance of targeting CST2 remains preliminary. Then, whether CST2 directly binds to IL-6 or acts through an intermediate adaptor protein requires further structural and biochemical validation. The precise molecular mechanism by which CST2 regulates IL-6 expression- whether through transcriptional activation, mRNA stabilization, or post-translational regulation-remains to be determined. It is unclear whether the CST2-IL-6 interaction is cell-autonomous or involves paracrine signaling within the tumor microenvironment. Addressing these questions will be critical for fully understanding the functional significance of this regulatory axis. Future studies using animal models, larger clinical cohorts, and more detailed molecular dissection are warranted to assess the translational potential of this axis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that CST2 was highly expressed in melanoma and correlates with poor prognosis. Mechanistically, CST2 interacted with IL-6 and activated the IL-6/STAT3/NF-κB signaling pathway to promote melanoma cell proliferation, colony formation, and migration. While these findings suggested that the CST2-IL-6 axis may represent a promising avenue for further investigation, the translational potential of targeting this pathway remains preliminary and requires substantiation through in vivo studies, independent clinical validation, and more detailed mechanistic dissection.
