 Chapter 1
 INTRODUCTION

This report presents the results of environmental activities and monitoring programs at the Princeton
Plasma Physics Laboratory (PPPL) for Calendar Year (CY) 2025. The report provides the Department of
Energy (DOE) and the public with information on the level of radioactive and nonradioactive pollutants, if
any, that are released into the environment resulting from PPPL operations. The report also summarizes
environmental initiatives, assessments, and programs undertaken in 2025. The objective of the Annual
Site Environmental Report (ASER) is to document PPPL’s efforts to protect the public’s health and the
environment through its environmental protection, safety, and health programs. To optimize production
efficiency and reader engagement, future ASERs will follow a five year base year-and-update model. The
2024 ASER serves as our anchor report and subsequent intermediate years, beginning with 2025, will be
published as abbreviated updates until the next comprehensive release in 2029. Base years may be
scheduled more frequently if significant updates are required.

1.1​Site Mission
PPPL is addressing critical national priorities through three major missions:

 ●​ Developing the scientific knowledge and advanced engineering to enable fusion to power the
 United States (U.S.) and the world;
 ●​ Advancing the fundamental science base of low-temperature plasma, plasma-material
 interactions, and new materials enabled by plasma for nanoscale fabrication and sustainable
 manufacturing ; and
 ●​ Furthering the development of the scientific understanding of the plasma universe, from the
 laboratory to astrophysical scales.

1.2​Primary Operations and Activities
1.2.1 National Spherical Torus Experiment-Upgrade (NSTX-U)

In 1999, PPPL began its flagship experiment, the National Spherical Torus Experiment (NSTX). With
support from the DOE and Princeton University, PPPL is working through the recovery project which will
result in an upgrade of NSTX-U as an international fusion research user facility. First plasma is scheduled
to start in Fiscal Year (FY) 2027.

1.2.2 Lithium Tokamak Experiment (LTX)
LTX continues to explore new paths for plasma energy efficiency and sustainability, after producing its
first plasma in 2008. The primary goal of LTX is to investigate the properties of a lithium liquid coating for
plasma surfaces or plasma-facing components (PFC). PPPL also coordinates a national program to unify
research on liquid metal for future tokamaks and has conducted liquid metal experiments on tokamaks
in Europe and Asia.

1.2.3 Magnetic Reconnection Experiment (MRX)
MRX investigates the explosive process of magnetic reconnection, giving rise to astrophysical events that
include auroras, solar flares, and geomagnetic storms. The process occurs when the magnetic field lines
in plasmas break and violently reconnect. Generating and studying reconnection under controlled
laboratory conditions can yield insights into solar outbursts and the formation of stars and greater
control of experimental fusion reactions.

1.2.4 Facility for Laboratory Reconnection Experiments (FLARE)
FLARE is a one-of-a-kind device designed to probe the physics behind magnetic reconnection, one of the
most fundamental yet still not fully understood phenomena in the universe. Offering capabilities found
nowhere else in the world, FLARE helps scientists study when magnetic field lines approach each other,
snap apart and then reattach in new configurations. This research will expand our understanding of solar
flares, the aurora borealis and atmospheric disturbances that could damage satellites and power grids,
as well as improve our understanding of fusion plasma behavior.

1.2.5 Quantum Diamond Laboratory (QDL)
QDL is a space devoted to studying and refining the processes involved in using plasma, the electrically
charged fourth state of matter, to create high-quality diamond material for quantum information science
applications. Scientists around the world are exploring quantum diamond as a way to harness the
properties of atoms and particles for a variety of tasks, including performing calculations far faster than
today’s computers can, enabling secure communications and measuring temperature and magnetic
fields with unparalleled precision.

1.2.6 International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER), International Collaboration, and
Other Plasma Physics Research
PPPL scientists and engineers collaborate with researchers from other fusion laboratories in the U.S. and
around the world. For more information on PPPL collaborations, refer to the Executive Summary of the
2024 Annual Site Environmental Report.

1.3​Campus Improvement Projects
1.3.1 Princeton Plasma Innovation Center (PPIC)

The construction of PPIC began in 2025. PPIC will be a 71,000 square foot facility, featuring adaptable,
state-of-the-art laboratories, collaboration and visualization spaces, and offices. It is the Lab’s first new
building in decades, serving as an international hub of fusion research and providing ample lab space for
PPPL’s expansion into microelectronics, quantum sensors and devices, electromanufacturing, and aerosol
science. The new building is scheduled for occupancy in 2027.

1.3.2 Critical Infrastructure Recovery and Renewal (CIRR)

The CIRR project will focus on modernizing the Laboratory’s aging electrical, mechanical, and
communications systems, which are essential infrastructure that powers scientific research and daily
operations. DOE approved the project’s advanced procurement phase in April 2025, allowing PPPL to
order long-lead time equipment while final designs and bid packages are completed. All changes or
updates to PPPL’s environmental permits have been incorporated into the planning process.

1.4​General Environmental Setting
The climate of central New Jersey is classified as a midlatitude, rainy climate with mild winters, hot
summers, and no dry season. In 2025, temperatures ranged from 2 degrees to 98 degrees Fahrenheit (°F)
(-16.7 degrees Celsius [°C] to 36.7°C) with an annual average temperature of 53.8°F (12.1°C) according to
National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) local climatological data.

The typical regional climate is moderately humid with a total average precipitation of 45.6 inches. In
2025, the total rainfall for the year was 34.6 inches (87.6 centimeters [cm]), or 11 inches (27.9 cm) below
the regional average [Hug26a].

2.1​Environmental Permits
2.1.1 Applicable Environmental Laws and Regulations

A. Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA)
RCRA regulates the generation, storage, treatment, and disposal of hazardous wastes, underground
storage tanks containing petroleum, hazardous substances, and universal waste. Recyclable used oil is
regulated under a NJ delegated RCRA program.In 2025, PPPL shipped 9.4 tons of combined hazardous,
universal, and Toxic Substance Control Act (TSCA) waste, of which 6.8 tons were recycled and 2.6 tons
were incinerated [San26].

B. National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA)
RCRA covers the potential impact of federal actions to the environment and an examination of
alternatives to those actions. In 2025, PPPL performed NEPA reviews of seven proposed activities [Str26].
All these activities were determined to be categorical exclusions (CXs) in accordance with DOE NEPA
Implementing Procedures [DOE26a].

C. Toxic Substance Control Act (TSCA)
TSCA governs the manufacture, use, and distribution of regulated chemicals listed. In 2025, PPPL shipped
859.8 pounds of polychlorinated biphenyls (PCB) TSCA Hazardous Substances, which consisted of
capacitors, ballast, and radio frequency (RF) filters. There were no asbestos shipments in 2025 [San26].

D. National Emission Standards for Hazardous Air Pollutants (NESHAPs)
NESHAPS is regulated by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) for tritium (Chapter 5) and boilers
(<10 million British Thermal Units [BTUs]). Greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions inventory tracking and
reporting are regulated by the EPA. The annual 2025 boiler adjustment results were completed as
required by the permit. Fuel consumption and sulfur content for the generators and boilers are recorded;

4.1 Nonradiological Water Programs: Environmental Monitoring
4.1.1 New Jersey Pollutant Discharge Elimination System (NJPDES) Program

A. Monthly Discharge Monitoring Reports (DMR)

In compliance with permit requirements of the NJPDES permit, NJ0023922, PPPL and the Department of
Energy Princeton Site Office (DOE-PSO) submitted to NJDEP monthly discharge monitoring reports
(DMRs) for permitted discharges DSN001, retention basin outfall, and DSN003, Delaware and Raritan
(D&R) Canal pump house filter backwash discharge. Data is presented in Appendix Tables 15 and 16
[Hug26c].

In 2025, PPPL managed its NJPDES discharge to surface water (DSW) permit, which is effective from April
1, 2025, through March 31, 2030 [NJDEP24]. The major updates in the 2025 permit from the 2019
permit involve adjusting specific effluent limits and operational requirements for the new permit term.
Most notably for outfall DSN001, NJDEP will introduce new limits for total recoverable copper, phased in
over 36 months. The new permit also streamlines monitoring by removing Waste Characterization
Report (WCR) requirements for manganese at DSN001 and chromium (hexavalent and trivalent) at
DSN003.

B. Chronic Whole Effluent Toxicity Study

Annual Chronic Whole Effluent Toxicity testing for DSN001 was completed on May 16, 2025. The
Ceriodaphnia dubia survival rate was inhibition concentration (IC25) >100% or no observable effect
concentration (NOEC) [NJDEP24, PPPL25a].

4.3.2 ​ Environmental Releases

During waste characterization sampling of excess soil for a future construction project, laboratory data
confirmed the presence of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAH) above the NJDEP Soil Remediation
Standards (SRS), specifically benzo(a)anthracene and benzo(a)pyrene. No specific source of discharge
was identified. Contamination was limited to a small area of shallow soil and the soil was removed from
PPPL with excess soil disposal [San25]. PPPL’s contracted Licensed Site Remediation Professional (LSRP)
formally stated on September 12, 2025 the remediation has been completed in compliance with N.J.A.C.
7:26C, Remediation of Contaminated Sites, that is protective of public health, safety, and the
environment. The remediation included the completion of a Remedial Investigation and Remedial Action
as defined pursuant to N.J.A.C. 7:26E, Technical Requirements for Site Remediation.

5.1​Radiological Emissions and Dose
5.1.1​ Maximally Exposed Individual (MEI)
The MEI dose represents the highest potential dose to a member of the public from all facility effluent
pathways. In 2025, the dose to the MEI was 3.83E-02 milli roentgen equivalent man (mrem) or 3.83E-04
milliSievert (mSv), as shown in Exhibit 5-1 [Hug26g]. The annual dose from natural sources is 3.10E+02
mrem [NCRP09]. The MEI was defined as a representative person at the nearest off-site business, located
351 meters from the stack, utilizing a 0.23 occupancy factor to reflect a 2,000-hour work year.
Atmospheric doses were modeled using a sector-averaged Gaussian plume, with HTO (tritium oxide)
results adjusted by a 2.86 multiplier to incorporate the 65% terrestrial food ingestion pathway
(vegetables, milk, and meat) as mandated by DOE-HDBK-1216-2015 [DOE15]. Liquid pathway
assessments followed ISCORS Technical Report 2005-02 [ISCORS05] for sanitary system worker exposure
and a standard 730 L/yr ingestion model for surface water. The groundwater ingestion dose is reported
as zero because site foundation sumps capture local groundwater and route it into the surface water
basins, ensuring all facility-contributed activity is consolidated and accounted for within the surface
water pathway. All assessments utilized updated ICRP 103 dose conversion factors per
DOE-STD-1196-2022.

5.1.2​ Committed Effective Dose Equivalent (CEDE)
The CEDE to the population within 80 km (about 50 miles) of the facility was 2.62E-02 person-rem or
2.62E-01 person-mSv (Exhibit 5-1). This was calculated using a population-weighted grid approach. The
total 2025 collective dose represents a fraction of the estimated 5.4 million person-rem received
annually by the same population from natural background radiation, confirming that facility operations
have no significant radiological impact on the surrounding environment or public.

Demographic data was obtained from the SecPop 4.4.1 code, based on the 2020 U.S. Census, which
distributed about 17.55 million people into a polar grid consisting of 16 compass sectors and 8
equidistant 10 km rings. Atmospheric collective doses were calculated using a sector-averaged Gaussian
plume model evaluated at the radial midpoint of each ring (e.g., 5 km, 15 km). In accordance with
DOE-HDBK-1216-2015, the HTO dose was adjusted by a multiplier of 2.86 to ensure that the terrestrial
food ingestion pathway (vegetables, milk, and meat) was fully accounted for in the high-density
metropolitan areas. Collective doses for liquid pathways were similarly evaluated, focusing on the

specific populations at risk. Following the methodology in ISCORS Technical Report 2005-02, the sanitary
system collective dose was applied to the wastewater treatment plant staff, while the surface water
collective dose was based on an estimated 1,000,000 downstream residents utilizing the regional water
system. [Hug26g].

5.4 Environmental Radiological Monitoring
5.4.1​ Waterborne Radioactivity

A. Surface Water
The concentration for all surface water samples were below the lower limit of detection (LLD) (Appendix
Table 5) [Fre26b].

B. Ground Water
The highest activity concentration of tritium in ground water was collected in the Airshaft at 1.35E+02
pCi/L on December 10, 2025 [Fre26b]. This concentration is well below the state and federal Drinking
Water Standard of 2E+04 pCi/L (Appendix Table 4)[Fre26b]. Groundwater monitoring at PPPL is
conducted primarily to track legacy impacts and monitor the efficacy of facility controls. Realistically,
there is no completed dose pathway to the public via groundwater. The PPPL building foundation sump
system (specifically at D-Site) is designed to intercept local groundwater, which is then pumped into the
site’s surface water storm sewer. As a result, any facility-contributed radioactivity in groundwater is
accounted for in the surface water pathway analysis.

Chapter 5 – Environmental Radiological Program Information​ Page 17

C. Drinking (Potable) Water
In 2025, tritium concentration in the potable water samples was below the LLD (Appendix Table 5).

D. Biota

The radiological impact on the environment was evaluated using the Graded Approach established in
DOE-STD-1153-2019 [DOE19]. Compliance was demonstrated by comparing maximum measured tritium
concentrations in surface water against the Biota Concentration Guide (BCG)—which are the
concentration levels that would result in the maximum allowed dose to plants and animals.

A sum of fractions (SOF) was calculated by dividing each site-specific measurement by the corresponding
BCG screening limit. Since the total resulting SOF of 3.00E-06 is well below the threshold of 1.0, the
facility is in compliance with the environmental protection requirements of DOE O 458.1 [DOE25b]. This
confirms that facility operations do not exceed the established dose limits for aquatic animals (1.0
rad/day) or terrestrial plants and animals (0.1 rad/day).
