Amebic liver abscess: A case report
Authors: Xiaoying Xu, Qingling lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Amebic liver abscess (ALA), caused by Entamoeba histolytica, is the most common extraintestinal manifestation of amebiasis. However, in contemporary clinical practice, diagnosis increasingly relies on serology and polymerase chain reaction (PCR), whereas direct microscopic identification of motile trophozoites in aspirated specimens has become rarely documented. Furthermore, optimal management necessitates complete anti-amebic therapy covering both invasive tissue stages and luminal intestinal colonization to prevent relapse. Here, we report a case of a 25-year-old male with a history of splenectomy and pulmonary tuberculosis, presenting with fever, right upper quadrant pain, and cough. Imaging revealed a right hepatic lobe abscess. While abscess and blood cultures remained sterile, sputum culture grew E. coli. Fresh microscopic examination of the aspirate readily demonstrated motile, erythrophagocytic trophozoites, subsequently confirmed as E. histolytica by sequencing. Although the patient showed initial clinical improvement with metronidazole, he later developed severe bleeding and ulceration of the ascending colon, ultimately requiring partial colectomy. This case underscores the enduring diagnostic value of conventional wet-mount microscopy in the molecular era, offering a rapid and cost-effective tool. Critically, it also highlights a key therapeutic principle ALA treated with a tissue-active agent must be followed by a luminal agent to eradicate intestinal carriage. Failure to do so risks persistent colonic disease, severe complications, or treatment failure, as observed in this patient.

Introduction

Amebiasis is the second leading cause of mortality among parasitic protozoan diseases and is distributed worldwide. Globally, approximately 50 million people are infected with Entamoeba histolytica, with annual deaths ranging from 40,000 to 100,000 [1]. In China, the average infection rate of intestinal amebiasis is 0.949%, corresponding to an estimated 10.69 million infected individuals nationwide [2]. Asymptomatic and mild intestinal infections are more common, whereas severe intestinal infection and hepatic abscess are less frequent. Ameboma and infections at other extra intestinal sites are rarely observed [3]. Herein, we report the case of a 25-year-old man who developed an amebic liver abscess.

Case report

A 25-year-old man presented with a 3-day history of cough, sputum, weakness, myalgia, and right upper quadrant pain. On admission, he had fever (39.5°C) and right upper quadrant tenderness with hepatomegaly.

Laboratory findings revealed elevated leukocytes (17.9 ×10⁹/L, 93.8% neutrophils), interleukin−6 (385 pg/mL), procalcitonin (19.3 ng/mL), and liver enzymes (AST 227 U/L, ALT 77 U/L, total bilirubin 100.4 µmol/L, direct bilirubin 65.5 µmol/L, ALP 262.6 U/L); other tests and HIV/hepatitis serology were normal/negative.

Chest CT showed left lower lobe bronchiectasis with consolidation; abdominal CT revealed a 14.9 × 14.8 cm complex lesion in the right liver lobe suggestive of abscess (Fig. 1A). Sputum culture grew sensitive E. coli, but blood cultures and subsequently drained abscess fluid (200 mL dark brown) were culture-negative. Despite intravenous ceftriaxone, the patient worsened. Ultrasound-guided percutaneous drainage was performed, and microscopic examination of the fluid revealed numerous motile trophozoites with ingested erythrocytes. Under 40 × magnification without staining, trophozoites with extending pseudopodia exhibiting directed movement were observed, and ingested red blood cells were visible within the trophozoites (Fig. 2). Based on these findings, the patient was started on metronidazole 750 mg three times daily for 14 days. His symptoms resolved rapidly, and the liver abscess decreased by half in size of treatment (Fig. 1B). During this period, however, he developed ascending colon ulceration with gastrointestinal bleeding, necessitating ascending colectomy with intestinal anastomosis and abdominal drainage under general anesthesia. At three weeks postoperatively, inflammatory markers and liver function tests had returned to normal.

Discussion

Entamoeba histolytica is a protozoan parasite that may cause asymptomatic colonization, amebic colitis, or extraintestinal abscesses [4]. Although ALA classically affects adult men [5] and is associated with travel to endemic regions, our patient developed the disease without such exposure, possibly facilitated by an immunocompromised state secondary to prior splenectomy and pulmonary tuberculosis. Laboratory parameters, including leukocytosis, neutrophilia, and deranged liver function tests were consistent with an acute hepatic necrotizing process.

Microscopic detection of motile trophozoites in abscess aspirate remains the definitive diagnostic standard, while real-time PCR and serology have become widely adopted in routine practice [6]. Nevertheless, its reported sensitivity is often below 10% [7], largely because viable trophozoites are often sequestered in the peripheral viable tissue rim rather than within the central liquefied pus. Otherwise, degenerated trophozoites are difficult to distinguish from host inflammatory debris. In this case, fresh pus aspirated near the abscess wall appeared chocolate-colored and viscous. A wet mount prepared after incubation at 37°C for 10 min revealed motile trophozoites with extending pseudopodia and directional movement. This finding affirms that despite the advent of molecular diagnostics, a properly performed fresh microscopic examination remains a rapid and highly instructive tool, particularly in resource-limited settings or when PCR results are pending. Serological testing, though widely accessible, carries inherent limitation. In endemic regions, persistent IgG titers may reflect past exposure rather than current invasive disease [8], hampering specificity for acute ALA. Conversely, molecular techniques offer high sensitivity, with recent evidence suggesting that urine and saliva may serve as non-invasive alternative specimens, achieving up to 97% detection rates in ALA patients [9]. However, neither serology nor PCR provides the real-time morphological cues, such as erythrophagocytosis and directional motility, that unequivocally confirm tissue invasion. It suggested that microscopy was not merely a historical procedure but a complementary diagnostic cornerstone.

The critical therapeutic corollary is powerfully underscored by the subsequent clinical course of our patient. Although metronidazole, a tissue-active nitroimidazole, effectively controlled the hepatic abscess, intestinal colonization was not concurrently addressed. This oversight is particularly hazardous because ALA almost invariably originates from antecedent intestinal infection. Yet, in patients with ALA, stool microscopy is notoriously insensitive [10]. The stool positivity rate is only 12%–18% [11]. The absence of overt dysentery often masks persistent colonic involvement. In our case, untreated intestinal amebiasis progressed to ascending colon ulceration with life-threatening hemorrhage, which proved refractory to interventional embolization and ultimately necessitated surgical colectomy. This complication illustrates that incomplete anti-amebic therapy exposes patients to a substantial risk of recrudescence, severe intestinal sequelae, or secondary extraintestinal relapse.

Accordingly, for any confirmed invasive amebiasis, a dual-phase regimen covering both invasive trophozoites and intraluminal cysts is mandatory to achieve complete parasitological clearance and prevent delayed complications. While percutaneous aspiration served predominantly as a diagnostic maneuver in our patient, it may also have contributed to source control, and future preventive strategies. Oral vaccination represents a potential preventive strategy for amebiasis in high-risk populations [12].

Conclusion

This case describes that conventional microscopy of fresh abscess aspirate remains a rapid, cost-effective, and diagnostically decisive tool in the molecular era. It provides real-time morphological evidence of invasive trophozoites that neither serology nor PCR can replace. More importantly, it highlights treatment of invasive amebiasis must include not only a tissue-active nitroimidazole but also a subsequent luminal agent to eradicate intestinal colonization. Failure to do so risks delayed colonic ulceration, hemorrhage, or relapse. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for ALA even in immunocompromised patients without endemic travel history. Although percutaneous drainage may be beneficial in selected cases, complete parasitological clearance hinges on appropriate dual-phase anti-amebic chemotherapy.
