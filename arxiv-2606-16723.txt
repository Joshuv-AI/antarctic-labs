AgentFairBench: Do LLM Agents Discriminate When They Act?
TRIVENI MORLA 1, ROHITH REDDY BELLIBALTU 1, MANPREET SINGH (MEMBER, IEEE) 2, AND MANMEET SINGH KAPOOR 3

Abstract
Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly take actions (screening applicants, recommending credit, triaging patients), yet fairness for LLMs is still measured by grading answers . We introduce AgentFairBench, a cheap, reproducible, multi-domain benchmark for demographic disparity in the actions of LLM agents. Grounded in a companion framework, the Bias Conduction Framework (BCF, restated here), it spans three regulator-anchored domains: hiring, lending, and medical triage. Synthetic, demographic-neutral profiles are evaluated in counterfactual matched sets that vary only a name-coded race × \times gender signal (in the Bertrand–Mullainathan tradition), under four agent scaffolds of increasing agency (direct, chain-of-thought, multi-agent deliberation, tool-augmented). A NumPy-only harness computes counterfactual flip rate, mean absolute score difference (MASD), action-rate disparity, and tool-invocation disparity, with bootstrap confidence intervals, paired tests, and false-discovery-rate control, for single-digit dollars per model. A live leaderboard with a held-out private split and a contamination canary admits external models by submission. Our pilot (864 decisions plus a test–retest replication) carries a methodological lesson: comparing a six-group score spread against a two-run noise difference overstates disparity by ∼ 2.4 × \sim 2.4\times through statistic arity alone. Against an arity-matched noise floor and an omnibus group test, claude-haiku-4-5 shows no demographic effect above sampling noise (0 of 120 pairwise and 0 of 9 omnibus contrasts survive correction); a planted-bias test confirms the instrument detects disparity when present. The contribution is a sound, sensitive, adoption-ready instrument, the arity-matched-null methodology, and open artifacts to scale it. Code, data, and harness are released under open licenses, with an anonymized review artifact.

I
Introduction

Large language models have crossed a threshold: they no longer merely answer questions, they

act
. LLM-driven agents now screen job applicants, recommend credit decisions, and triage
clinical cases (settings in which a biased
action
changes a
person’s access to employment, capital, or care)
[
4
]
. Yet the dominant
framework for measuring fairness in language models still grades
answers

model emits in response to a prompt
[
28

24

25

There is a structural gap between what we measure and what now matters.

This gap has a precise theoretical cause. The Bias Conduction Framework (BCF), a companion
theoretical framework restated self-containedly in Section
III
, shows that token-level

Masking

actions
when those
answers are operationalized into decisions, tool calls, and multi-step trajectories. The situation
compounds. The same machinery that makes an agent more capable (chain-of-thought, multi-agent deliberation, tool
use) can
amplify
rather than wash out an entering disparity (Proposition P3,

Super-additivity
). Existing benchmarks see none of this. Answer-level fairness benchmarks do
not act, single-domain agent audits cover one vertical, and capability-oriented agent benchmarks

[
22
,
35
,
19
,
9
]
are not
demographically instrumented.

We introduce
AgentFairBench
, a cheap, reproducible, multi-domain benchmark that measures
demographic disparity in the
actions
of LLM agents. AgentFairBench operationalizes the BCF’s
component counterfactual disparity at the action level across three consequential domains:
hiring, lending, and medical triage. Each domain is anchored to a real regulatory standard (EEOC adverse
impact
[
31
]
, NYC Local Law 144
[
27
]
, and ECOA /
Regulation B
[

]

II
Related Work

II-A

Answer-Level LLM Fairness Benchmarks

A substantial body of work evaluates demographic bias by examining what language models
say
in response to carefully constructed prompts. BBQ
[
28
]
probes social biases across nine protected categories using ambiguous question–answer pairs drawn from a templated question-answering format; StereoSet
[
24
]
measures stereotypical associations at the word and sentence level via intrasentence and intersentence contexts; and CrowS-Pairs
[
25
]
presents masked-language-model scoring over minimally contrastive stereotype/anti-stereotype sentence pairs. These benchmarks have produced real insight into the distributional biases encoded during pretraining and instruction tuning. The limits of this answer-level paradigm, and the gap between measuring representational bias and measuring downstream allocative harm, are surveyed critically by Blodgett et al.
[
5
]
, whose call to center the harms systems actually cause motivates our shift from answers to actions; the same limits are catalogued comprehensively in the bias-and-fairness survey of Gallegos et al.
[
15
]
.

Each benchmark grades
answers
, though. The unit of measurement is a model’s selection among textual options or its assignment of probability mass to a token sequence. None of them activates an action loop. When a model answers “yes” or “no” to a stereotype probe, no credit score changes, no interview invitation is issued, and no triage escalation is triggered. As formalized in the Bias Conduction Framework’s Proposition P2 (Masking), token-level parity can coexist with action-level disparity because the mapping from language to executable decisions is non-linear and context-dependent. AgentFairBench targets precisely this measurement gap: the question is not what a model says about a demographic group, but what it
does
when acting consequentially on behalf of one.

II-B

Decision-Level LLM Audits

A growing body of work moves from grading answers to probing the
decisions
LLMs make under demographic perturbation, and is the closest neighbor to our hiring and lending domains. Tamkin et al.
[
29
]
introduce DiscrimEval, generating 70 high-stakes decision scenarios across society and systematically varying demographic attributes to measure both positive and negative discrimination in a production model; this is the canonical demonstration that demographically perturbed, decision-level evaluation surfaces disparities that answer-grading misses, and it anticipates the explicit-attribute robustness arm we defer to future work. In the hiring vertical specifically, An et al.
[
2
]
score roughly 361,000 resumes with randomized social identities across several commercial LLMs and find systematic, direction-varying disparities (higher scores for female candidates, lower for Black male candidates), while Haim et al.
[
16
]
audit name-coded race and gender across 42 prompt templates and find that Black-women-coded names receive the least advantageous advice. AgentFairBench differs from this line on three axes the comparison below makes precise: these studies elicit a single-shot decision, whereas we wrap each decision in an agent loop and stratify by scaffold depth (C0–C4); they cover one setting each, whereas we span three regulator-anchored domains under one harness; and none instruments the tool-invocation channel (
\Delta_{\text{tool}}
) that only an action loop exposes. DiscrimEval is the most direct decision-level comparator; it is single-shot and not scaffold-stratified, which is exactly the axis we add.

II-C

Single-Domain and Clinical Agent Fairness

A newer wave of work moves closer to agents but remains domain-specific. MedAgentBench
[
18
]
instruments clinical reasoning tasks for LLM agents, though its primary focus is task capability rather than demographic disparity in outcomes. FairMedQA
[
32
]
examines demographic fairness in medical question answering using a counterfactual perturbation design similar in spirit to ours, applied to QA
accuracy
(answer-level) within a single clinical vertical and without an agentic action loop. The measurement
level
(executable actions), not the counterfactual methodology, is our key differentiator. MFARM
[
1
]
evaluates multi-faceted clinical-fairness harms on MIMIC-IV but is restricted to a single domain and a narrow metric set. Most directly, EQUITRIAGE
[
34
]
audits gender bias in LLM-based emergency-department triage using counterfactual gender-swaps and flip-rate metrics over MIMIC-IV-ED vignettes. In a dedicated single-domain study it finds measurable action-level disparity in LLM triage, which is concurrent and independent evidence that the triage setting we include is a live one. It remains single-domain (triage) and single-attribute (gender); AgentFairBench adds synthetic, PHI-free reproducible profiles, a race-by-gender rather than gender-only signal, and the cross-domain and scaffold axes.

The most closely related prior work is that of Mayilvaghanan et al.
[
23
]
, which introduced counterfactual flip rate (CFR) and mean absolute score difference (MASD) as complementary metrics for measuring demographic disparity in contact-center quality-assurance agents. That work demonstrated that binary decision disparity (CFR) and graded score disparity (MASD) are not redundant, which is a direct empirical instantiation of the P2 Masking proposition. We adopt CFR and MASD directly from this work, together with the Bertrand–Mullainathan name-substitution paradigm it employs; we claim novelty in neither, and our contribution is their generalization beyond a single contact-center quality-assurance setting. AgentFairBench extends this foundation in three directions: (i) it generalizes across three consequentially distinct domains (hiring, lending, and medical triage) each anchored to a real regulatory standard, rather than a single vertical; (ii) it introduces a
scaffold axis
(direct, chain-of-thought, multi-agent deliberation, and tool-augmented) that operationalizes the BCF conduction operators, tests the P3 Super-additivity proposition, and yields a tool-invocation disparity metric (
\Delta_{\text{tool}}
) absent from prior work; and (iii) it adds an anti-gaming evaluation protocol (held-out private split, contamination canary, content hashes) delivered through a live leaderboard.

II-D

Agent Capability Benchmarks

Parallel to fairness-oriented work, the agent community has produced a rich set of capability benchmarks. AgentBench
[
22
]
evaluates LLM agents across a diverse suite of interactive environments including web browsing, database manipulation, and lateral-thinking games. WebArena
[
35
]
provides a realistic, self-hosted web environment in which agents complete multi-step tasks on live-feeling websites. SWE-bench
[
19
]
measures an agent’s ability to resolve real GitHub issues from open-source repositories. Mind2Web
[
9
]
focuses on generalist web agents navigating diverse websites from human-annotated demonstrations.

These benchmarks are methodologically rigorous and ecologically valid, requiring genuine multi-step reasoning and tool use. None of them is demographically instrumented. Task profiles are not systematically varied across protected-attribute signals, no counterfactual matched sets are constructed, and no fairness metrics are computed. A model that achieves state-of-the-art task-completion rates on SWE-bench or WebArena may nonetheless exhibit substantial demographic disparity when its actions impinge on hiring, credit, or healthcare decisions. Capability and fairness are orthogonal axes, and existing capability benchmarks measure only one of them.

III
Background: The Bias Conduction Framework

Measuring demographic disparity in LLM-agent behavior requires a formalism that goes beyond static classifier fairness. The Bias Conduction Framework (BCF) addresses this by modeling an agent as a four-tuple
\langle\pi,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{R}\rangle
, where
\pi
is the policy (the underlying language model),
\mathcal{T}
is the task specification,
\mathcal{M}
is the working memory (context window, tool outputs, scratchpad), and
\mathcal{R}
is the repertoire of available actions and tools (D1). This formulation treats the agent loop as an object of fairness analysis in its own right, distinct from the model weights alone.

The BCF is a
companion theoretical framework
developed concurrently with this benchmark by the authors; it is not pre-existing peer-reviewed theory, and we restate here (self-containedly) only the parts AgentFairBench operationalizes, so the paper is legible without the framework document. The BCF organizes its claims as three component definitions (D1–D3) and five propositions (P1–P5). This paper relies on D2 (counterfactual component disparity) and tests two propositions empirically (P2: Masking and P3: Super-additivity), which, because they originate in a companion framework rather than established results, we treat as falsifiable
hypotheses
in our experiments rather than as theorems to be confirmed. We also invoke P5 (measurement adequacy) in Section
V
as the principle that a fairness score is meaningful only if it reflects measured, not imputed, behavior. P1 (locality) and P4 (mitigation-matching) are not used here. Grounding a benchmark in a framework from the same authors carries an obvious circularity risk, so we are explicit about how we avoid it: P2 and P3 enter the experiments as falsifiable, directional predictions, not as results assumed true. In this pilot the predicted effects do
not
appear above the (arity-matched) noise floor (§
VI
): on a single model at
n=12
matched sets per cell, the data neither confirms them nor, given the limited power, refutes them in general. We therefore treat them throughout as measurement targets the benchmark is built to test, demonstrate via a planted-bias property test that the instrument detects such effects when present, and report the null plainly.

D2: Counterfactual Component Disparity.
For any component
c\in\{
policy, memory, tools, scaffolding
\}
, the BCF defines counterfactual disparity
\Delta_{c}
via trace-replay: hold all inputs constant except the demographic signal embedded in component
c
, re-execute the agent trace, and measure the resulting divergence in output distributions. Formally,
\Delta_{c}=\mathbb{E}[\delta(a_{g},a_{g_{0}})]
, where
a_{g}
is the action taken under demographic group
g
,
a_{g_{0}}
is the action under reference group
g_{0}
(here, white male), and
\delta
is a task-appropriate discrepancy metric (flip rate or score difference). This operationalization draws on the individual-fairness
[
11
]
, group-fairness
[
17
]
, and counterfactual-fairness
[
21
]
traditions; counterfactual fairness in particular requires that individuals differing only in protected attributes receive equivalent treatment. AgentFairBench is the executable instantiation of D2 at the action level: counterfactual matched sets vary only a name-coded race-by-gender signal, so any observed
\Delta_{c}
is attributable to the demographic perturbation rather than confounding profile content. We note that our matched sets implement a
correspondence-audit
counterfactual (a ceteris-paribus name substitution in the Bertrand–Mullainathan sense), which is related to but not identical with the structural-causal-model counterfactual of
[
21
]
: we intervene on a surface proxy for perceived demographics, not on a protected-attribute node in a causal graph, and we therefore do not claim the stronger SCM-counterfactual guarantee.

D3: Conduction Operators.
The BCF characterizes how disparity propagates through the agent pipeline via four operators applied at each component boundary: ATTENUATE (downstream processing reduces disparity), PRESERVE (disparity passes through unchanged), AMPLIFY (disparity grows), and GENERATE (disparity is introduced de novo by a component that was itself neutral). These operators supply a mechanistic vocabulary for diagnosis. A scaffold that adds deliberation steps may AMPLIFY disparity already present in the policy, or may GENERATE new disparity through asymmetric tool invocation patterns.

P2: Masking.
Token-level output parity (e.g., equivalent next-token distributions across demographic conditions) does not imply action-level parity. A model may assign near-identical log-probabilities to “yes” and “no” continuations across demographic groups while still producing systematically different discrete decisions after sampling and parsing. This measurement gap, which existing answer-level benchmarks
[
28
,
24
,
25
]
are structurally unable to detect, motivates grounding evaluation at the action layer.

P3: Super-additivity.
Composing agentic scaffolds can make disparity grow with scaffold depth, and the composed disparity can exceed that of any single component: adding deliberation or tool-use stages need not wash out an entering bias and may amplify it, making scaffold topology itself a fairness-relevant design variable. We state P3 as a
directional
(ordinal) prediction (disparity is non-decreasing in scaffold depth, and composition can exceed the largest single-component contribution) rather than as a closed-form inequality. A literal super-additive bound
\Delta_{\text{composed}}\geq\sum_{c}\Delta_{c}
is not well defined for the bounded disparity metrics used here (a flip rate cannot exceed 1, whereas
\sum_{c}\Delta_{c}
can), so AgentFairBench tests only the directional corollary, which the C0–C4 scaffold ladder is designed to measure; estimating the individual per-component
\Delta_{c}
is left to future work.

AgentFairBench maps scaffolds C0 (direct prompting), C2 (chain-of-thought), C3 (multi-agent deliberation), and C4 (tool-augmented information gathering) directly to BCF entry loci, enabling empirical measurement of whether conduction operators shift across scaffold depth. The impossibility results of
[
20
]
and
[
7
]
establish that no single metric simultaneously satisfies all fairness criteria; BCF does not resolve this tension but makes the measurement site explicit, allowing domain-appropriate criteria to be applied at the action layer where regulatory consequences materialize.

IV
Benchmark Design

IV-A

Domains and Action Space

AgentFairBench operationalizes demographic disparity measurement across three consequential decision domains, selected to span distinct regulatory regimes and action-space structures. Each domain yields both a primary binary decision and a secondary continuous score, so measurement operates at two levels of granularity.

Hiring
presents an agent with a candidate profile and requires two outputs: an advancement decision (boolean) and a candidate quality score (0–100). This domain is anchored to the EEOC Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures
[
31
]
, which establish the four-fifths rule as an adverse-impact threshold, and to New York City Local Law 144
[
27
]
, which mandates bias audits of automated employment decision tools. The two-output structure captures the masking phenomenon articulated in BCF Proposition P2: a model may exhibit score-level disparity while producing parity-equivalent binary decisions.

Lending
presents a loan-application profile and elicits an approval decision (boolean) and an APR tier assignment (ordinal, 1–5, where 1 is most favorable). The regulatory anchor is the Equal Credit Opportunity Act and Regulation B
[
30
]
, which prohibit consideration of protected characteristics in credit decisions. The ordinal APR tier operationalizes graded harm: a qualified applicant steered to a higher tier suffers a measurable financial penalty even if their application is technically approved.

Medical triage
presents a clinical intake summary and requires an escalation decision (boolean) and an acuity score (1–5, where 5 is most urgent). No direct regulatory parallel exists for algorithmic triage in the U.S., so this domain is grounded in AI governance frameworks
[
13
,
26
]
that classify health-consequential AI as high-risk and demand demonstrable non-discrimination. Triage represents a domain where disparity can manifest as systematic under-escalation of acuity for particular demographic groups.

IV-B

Counterfactual Profiles

All profiles are synthetically generated and contain no real individuals or protected health information, which obviates IRB requirements. Profile content is constructed to be demographic-neutral: domain-relevant attributes (e.g., work history, financial indicators, symptom onset) are held fixed across a matched set while a single surface signal (a first-name and surname combination) is varied to encode the demographic cell of interest.

This design follows the Bertrand-Mullainathan audit tradition
[
4
]
, in which names function as cues that activate race and gender associations without altering substantive qualifications, and its more recent application to machine-learning systems, where explicit gender indicators such as first names and pronouns drive measurable disparity in high-stakes occupation classification
[
8
]
. Each matched set comprises six counterfactual variants crossing three name-coded racial groups (White, Black, Hispanic) with two gender signals (Male, Female), yielding cells {White-M, White-F, Black-M, Black-F, Hispanic-M, Hispanic-F}. White-Male serves as the reference group for all disparity computations, consistent with standard counterfactual fairness practice
[
21
]
. This reference choice is a reporting convenience; the harness also computes all pairwise and highest-rate-group contrasts, and we note that the EEOC four-fifths test (§
IX
) uses the highest-selecting group, not the white-male reference, as its baseline.

Age is included as a secondary demographic attribute. Because age interacts with name-coded race and gender, the benchmark supports intersectional analysis; matched sets are constructed at both the marginal (race, gender independently) and joint (race
\times
gender
\times
age-band) levels. This enables detection of disparity patterns that emerge only at intersections and would be invisible to marginal measurement alone
[
6
]
.

IV-C

Agent Scaffolds as BCF Entry Loci

Scaffolding complexity is a first-class variable in AgentFairBench, reflecting BCF Proposition P3 (super-additivity) and the claim that agentic structure can amplify or attenuate bias introduced at any component. Four scaffold levels are instantiated, corresponding to BCF conduction loci. The numbering follows the BCF locus taxonomy; C1 (retrieval-augmented context injection) is defined in the framework but not instantiated in v1 (to avoid confounding retrieval-source effects with scaffold depth) and is deferred to the v2 roadmap.

C0 (Direct Prompt)
presents the profile in a single-turn zero-shot prompt. This is the minimal baseline and captures bias attributable to the model’s parametric associations alone.

C2 (Chain-of-Thought)
elicits a step-by-step rationale before the final decision. The intermediate reasoning trace is retained for qualitative analysis and allows tracing whether the conduction operator at this locus is PRESERVE, ATTENUATE, or AMPLIFY relative to C0.

C3 (Multi-Agent Deliberation)
routes the profile through a two-agent pipeline: a reviewer agent produces an initial assessment, and a decision agent synthesizes the final output. This tests P3 super-additivity by measuring whether disparity accrues across agent handoffs.

C4 (Tool/Information-Gathering)
equips the agent with a tool that can be invoked to request supplementary information about the applicant before deciding. Tool invocation itself becomes a measurement target: differential invocation rates across demographic groups constitute a form of action-level disparity (
\Delta_{\text{tool}}
) that is invisible to answer-grading benchmarks
[
28
,
24
]
.

IV-D

Metrics

Counterfactual Flip Rate (CFR)
measures decision-level disparity. The per-cell CFR we report is the fraction of matched sets whose binary decision is not unanimous across the six demographic conditions (equivalently, the white-male decision disagrees with at least one focal group), holding all other attributes constant; the harness also stores the pairwise flip rate against the white-male reference for every focal group separately. A nonzero CFR is consistent with demographic sensitivity in the decision; because sampling temperature is not pinned, it should be read against the measured test–retest noise floor (Section
VI
) rather than taken on its own as proof of a causal effect.

Mean Absolute Score Difference (MASD)
measures score-level disparity. For each matched set we compute the score
spread
(the difference between the maximum and minimum score assigned across the six demographic conditions), and MASD is the mean of this spread over all matched sets in the split; formally,
\text{MASD}=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{s\in S}\left(\max_{g}x_{s,g}-\min_{g}x_{s,g}\right)
, where
x_{s,g}
is the score for group
g
in matched set
s
. The harness also reports the pairwise mean absolute difference
\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{s}|x_{s,g}-x_{s,\text{wm}}|
against the white-male reference for every focal group. MASD is sensitive to graded harm that CFR cannot detect, operationalizing the score-channel disparity that BCF Proposition P2 concerns. Despite its name (inherited from prior work
[
23
]
), our per-cell MASD is a six-group
spread
, not a pairwise difference, and two properties of that spread matter for its interpretation. First, as a max-minus-min range statistic, MASD is non-decreasing in the number of groups and is upward-biased relative to a pairwise difference even under pure noise, so it must be benchmarked against a noise floor of the
same arity
(a six-group noise spread), not against a two-run pairwise difference; we make this comparison correctly in §
VI
(Finding 1), where the naive comparison reports a mean ratio of
2.4\times
the noise floor and the arity-matched comparison reports
0.83\times
. Second, the spread is not anchored to a reference group, so the reference-anchored pairwise MASD above is the quantity aligned with the reference-group logic of regulatory tests; we report both.

Action-Rate Disparity
is the difference in the proportion of positive binary decisions (advance, approve, escalate) between demographic groups across matched sets, computed as a marginal rate and as a pairwise contrast. It quantifies aggregate outcome inequality at the group level, complementing the per-set precision of CFR.

Tool-invocation disparity
(
\Delta_{\text{tool}}
, C4 only) is the spread in tool-invocation rate across the six demographic groups,
\Delta_{\text{tool}}=\max_{g}r_{g}-\min_{g}r_{g}
, where
r_{g}
is the fraction of C4 decisions in group
g
for which the agent invokes the information-gathering tool before deciding; pairwise gaps against the white-male reference are also reported. A nonzero
\Delta_{\text{tool}}
means the agent applies procedurally disparate scrutiny (demanding additional evidence for some groups more than others), a form of action-level disparity invisible to decision and score metrics.

IV-E

Statistical Procedures

All estimates are accompanied by bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap 95% confidence intervals
[
12
,
10
]
, computed via matched-set resampling to respect the dependence structure of counterfactual pairs. Decision-level contrasts are tested with McNemar’s exact test for paired binary outcomes. Score-level contrasts use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired observations, applied only to contrasts with at least two non-tied score pairs (degenerate contrasts are recorded as non-significant). Multiple comparisons across demographic cells, scaffolds, and domains are controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure at
q=0.05

[
3
]
. Two further controls address the spread-statistic and omnibus questions that pairwise tests leave open; both are computed for the C0–C3 scaffolds, which have a test–retest noise floor, since the reliability run did not include C4. First, an
arity-matched noise floor
for MASD: within each cell we fit a two-way (profile
+
group) decomposition of the score matrix, take the residual (which removes any group main effect, leaving within-cell residual variation as a single-run proxy for replication noise; the genuine
k
-replicate floor is a v1.1 item, §
X
), and Monte-Carlo resample six residuals per matched set to estimate the six-group spread expected under the null of no demographic effect; the observed MASD is compared to this same-arity null and to an empirical
p
-value (fraction of null spreads at least as large as observed). Second, an
omnibus
two-way ANOVA
F
-test for any group main effect, with profile as a blocking factor, computed per cell with a self-contained (NumPy-only) regularized-incomplete-beta
F
tail. These complement the pairwise McNemar and Wilcoxon contrasts: the pairwise tests ask whether any single focal group differs from the reference, while the omnibus test and the arity-matched null ask whether the score channel carries any demographic spread above noise at all. Cohen’s
h
effect sizes for rate contrasts are computed by the harness and released with the per-contrast outputs; given the pilot’s limited statistical power (§
VII
) we foreground confidence intervals, the arity-matched ratio, and FDR-corrected
q
-values rather than effect-size point estimates. All random operations use seed 20260612 for reproducibility.

IV-F

Splits and Anti-Gaming Safeguards

The benchmark is partitioned into a public development split, released alongside the leaderboard, and a held-out private evaluation split withheld from public access. Evaluation on the private split is conducted through a submission API; raw profiles are never exposed. To detect contamination, each profile embeds a cryptographic content hash and a low-frequency canary string that would be statistically anomalous in any training corpus. Leaderboard submissions are screened for canary leakage. This protocol addresses the benchmark saturation problem that has affected capability-oriented agent benchmarks
[
22
,
35
,
19
]
and ensures that reported disparity estimates reflect generalization rather than memorization.

V
Harness and Leaderboard

To lower the barrier to adoption and enable third-party replication, AgentFairBench ships as
agentfairbench
, a pip-installable Python package whose evaluation core depends only on NumPy. The lightweight dependency footprint is deliberate. Auditors and regulators who wish to verify a vendor submission need not install a deep learning stack.

Package architecture.
The core library exposes three layers. The
profile layer
generates synthetic, demographically neutral candidate, applicant, and patient profiles and assembles counterfactual matched sets following the Bertrand-Mullainathan name-substitution paradigm
[
4
]
. All stochastic operations in the harness are seeded from a single integer (default
SEED=20260612
), passed transitively to profile sampling, scaffold construction, and bootstrap resampling, so that these steps are byte-for-byte reproducible. The model decisions themselves are not bit-reproducible: the production endpoints sample at non-zero temperature with no exposed seed, so identical inputs can yield different decisions on fresh calls. Bit-level reproducibility therefore holds over the released decision traces and the recomputation of every metric from them, not over re-collecting model decisions. The
scaffold layer
wraps each profile-domain pair in one of the four BCF entry loci (C0 direct prompt, C2 chain-of-thought, C3 multi-agent deliberation, C4 tool-augmented) and returns structured action traces. For C2 the harness stores the model’s returned structured rationale field (the decision-relevant reasoning it emits), not a separately captured hidden chain-of-thought; for C3 it stores each sub-agent’s structured contribution. The
metrics layer
computes the full suite: CFR, MASD, action-rate disparity,
\Delta_{\text{tool}}
, BCa bootstrap 95% confidence intervals with matched-set resampling, McNemar and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, and Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction. These metrics can be derived from any trace archive, including replays of previously saved responses.

Model adapters.
The package ships three adapter classes that implement a common
ModelAdapter
interface. The
OpenAI-compatible adapter
targets any endpoint exposing the OpenAI Chat Completions schema: GPT-family models and most open-weight inference servers natively, and Claude or Gemini through their OpenAI-compatible gateways or a proxy such as OpenRouter (these vendors’ native APIs use different schemas). The pilot decisions were collected through this adapter against the Anthropic API endpoint for
claude-haiku-4-5
(the same endpoint and default decoding parameters reported in §
X
). The
replay adapter
accepts a frozen JSONL trace archive and re-scores it without issuing new API calls, enabling post-hoc audits and the test-retest reliability protocol used in our pilot. The
mock adapter
returns deterministic canned responses and is used by the CI test suite to confirm that metrics are computed identically across library versions.

CLI.
A command-line interface wraps all evaluation workflows:

agentfairbench run --model haiku \
--domain hiring --scaffolds C0,C2,C3,C4
agentfairbench score --traces out.jsonl
agentfairbench cost --model sonnet

The
cost
subcommand implements a pre-flight token estimator calibrated against Anthropic’s published list prices as of June 2026: $1.79 for the full 864-call domain-scaffold grid at
claude-haiku-4-5
, and an estimated $5.38 at
claude-sonnet-4-6
and $8.96 at
claude-opus-4-8
(the latter two were not run). The complete benchmark costs single-digit dollars per model, under $2 at the Haiku tier, making broad multi-model surveys tractable for academic groups.

Reproducibility hooks.
Every profile carries a truncated SHA-256 content hash, and the public split embeds a low-frequency
contamination canary
string (
AGENTFAIRBENCH-CANARY-2f9c1a
) that would be statistically anomalous in any training corpus, so leaked memorization is detectable. The held-out private split is withheld from the public repository; only the public split is distributed with the package, so that the leaderboard evaluation surface cannot be directly observed during model development.

Leaderboard and submission protocol.
The AFB-Score composite is defined
per domain
, as
1-\tfrac{1}{3}(\text{CFR}+\text{MASD}_{\text{norm}}+\text{rate-disparity})
averaged over that domain’s scaffold cells, where
\text{MASD}_{\text{norm}}
normalizes MASD by the domain score range; higher AFB-Score indicates less disparity. We report it
per domain and do not average across domains
: the per-domain score scales (a 0–100 hiring score, a 1–5 APR tier, a 1–5 acuity level) are not commensurable in harm, so a single cross-domain number would invite the false equivalence the experiments section warns against (§
VI
); the composite is comparable across models within a domain, not across domains. The composite is reported alongside, never in place of, its per-metric components (including
\Delta_{\text{tool}}
), so it cannot hide a component. We keep
\Delta_{\text{tool}}
out of the composite because it applies only to C4 cells. Authors self-evaluate on the public split and submit a pull request with their frozen trace JSONL and a model card. Verification depends on access. When maintainers can reach the model (open weights, or a submitter-provided scoped API key), they re-run the held-out private split and post a
verified
score, which provides genuine held-out gaming resistance. When they cannot (proprietary models without shared access), the entry is checked for reproducibility-from-trace and canary leakage and posted with a
trace-only
flag: such entries reproduce the submitter’s claim and are screened for contamination, but the private split cannot be re-run on the submitter’s behalf, so they do not carry held-out gaming resistance. We state this limitation plainly rather than implying the private split protects every entry. No AFB-Scores are estimated or imputed by the paper authors; this keeps the leaderboard a record of measured behavior rather than extrapolation, consistent with the measurement-adequacy principle (BCF P5; see Section
III
).

VI-B

Finding 1: a 6-group spread must be compared to a 6-group noise floor, not a 2-run difference

Because the model was queried at its default (non-zero) temperature, identical inputs do not
always yield identical decisions. The two independent runs quantify this: overall test–retest
decision agreement is 0.917 (i.e.,
\approx 8\%
of binary decisions flip on pure resampling), and
the per-cell mean absolute score difference between runs ranges from 0.15 to 5.75 points
(Table
II
, “Retest MAE”).

A tempting analysis compares MASD directly to this retest MAE. Doing so yields ratios of

1.24
–
3.33\times
(mean
\approx 2.4\times
), which earlier drafts of this work read as
score-channel signal above noise.
That comparison is wrong
, and correcting it is the central
methodological lesson of the pilot. MASD is a six-group
spread
(the mean over matched sets of

\max_{g}x_{s,g}-\min_{g}x_{s,g}
), whereas the retest MAE is a two-run
pairwise
difference on
identical inputs. The maximum-minus-minimum of six noisy draws is mechanically larger than the
absolute difference of two, even when there is no demographic effect at all: for Gaussian noise the
expected six-way range is about
2.2
–
2.5\times
the expected pairwise difference, and the
empirical residual pools here fall in the same range. The apparent
“
2.4\times
above the floor” is therefore almost exactly what pure sampling noise produces; it is
an artifact of comparing statistics of different arity, not evidence of disparity. The arity-matched
null below does not rely on this Gaussian approximation: it resamples the empirical residuals directly.

The correct control is the six-group spread
expected from within-cell noise
. We estimate it
from the data (details in §
IV
, Statistical Procedures): the noise-only six-way spread
is obtained by Monte-Carlo resampling of the primary run’s two-way (profile
+
group) residuals,
which removes any group effect and leaves replication noise. Compared against this arity-matched
floor, the MASD-to-noise ratio is
below one in every C0–C3 cell
: 0.85, 0.87, 0.96 (hiring
C0/C2/C3); 0.75, 0.79, 0.68 (lending); 0.83, 0.85, 0.90 (triage); mean 0.83
(Fig.
V
). In other words, the
cross-demographic score spread is, if anything, slightly
smaller
than the spread pure noise
would generate, and the empirical probability of seeing a spread at least this large under the null
exceeds 0.6 in all nine cells. Because the two-way residual removes the group main effect, this null
is, if anything, slightly too small (biasing the ratio upward); the observed MASD sits below it in
every cell regardless, so the null reading is conservative. An omnibus two-way ANOVA test for any group main effect (profile as a
blocking factor) is non-significant in all nine cells (minimum
p=0.082
; zero survive
Benjamini–Hochberg correction). This noise-floor reasoning is exactly the control a recent
methodological critique calls for: a demographic-swap effect must be benchmarked against a
matched baseline before it can be attributed to the demographic factor
[
33
]
, and
our arity-matched null is that baseline. The honest conclusion is that, on this model and at this
scale, the score channel carries
no demographic signal distinguishable from sampling noise
.

VI-C

Finding 2: the masking failure mode (BCF P2) is measurable but not exhibited here

BCF Proposition P2 (Masking) predicts a specific failure mode: score-channel disparity coexisting
with parity-clean binary decisions, so that an auditor inspecting only the discrete action would
miss a graded harm. AgentFairBench is built to surface exactly this pattern. In this pilot, however,

neither
channel shows disparity above its noise floor: the score channel sits at or below the
arity-matched floor (Finding 1), and binary CFR stays near the
\approx 8\%
resampling level (four
cells reach 0.25–0.33 but none survives FDR; Finding 5). So masking is not
instantiated by
claude-haiku-4-5
at this scale; we report this rather than narrate a
pattern the data does not support.

That the instrument
would
surface masking if it were present is established by construction,
not by this pilot: the released test suite includes a
BiasedMock
adapter that injects a

-25
-point score penalty for one demographic group, and the harness recovers a large, well-separated
MASD and non-zero pairwise score gaps for that planted effect (§
X
). The masking
channel is thus a demonstrated measurement capability with a null reading on this model, not a
confirmed effect.

BCF Proposition P3 (Super-additivity) predicts that added agentic machinery
(chain-of-thought, then multi-agent deliberation) amplifies rather than attenuates an entering
disparity. The raw MASD does rise across the C0–C3 ladder in two domains (hiring 7.83
\to
8.83

\to

after the arity correction of Finding 1, every one of these values is below its own noise floor
(ratios 0.85–0.96 for hiring, 0.68–0.90 across lending and triage), the bootstrap confidence

noise; we do not claim P3 is exhibited. As with P2, scaffold depth remains a first-class
measurement axis
the
benchmark instruments, and a powered run is required to determine whether any model amplifies
disparity along it. We deliberately do not select the two monotone domains, drop C4, or average the
incommensurable per-domain scales to manufacture a trend; doing so would read signal into noise.

VI-E

Finding 4: a tool-invocation disparity channel the instrument can measure

The C4 scaffold lets the agent invoke an information-gathering tool before deciding, exposing a
channel that lives entirely in the agent’s choice of
action
and is invisible to decision and
score metrics. The harness measures it as
\Delta_{\text{tool}}
, the spread in tool-invocation rate
across the six groups. In this pilot the largest observed value is in hiring (
\Delta_{\text{tool}}=0.25
), but the underlying counts are tiny: the tool is invoked in 3 of 12 white-male-coded cases, 1
of 12 in two groups, and 0 of 12 in the rest, with lending and triage at
\Delta_{\text{tool}}=0.083

(a one-invocation difference). At these counts a single different sample moves the metric
substantially;
\Delta_{\text{tool}}
ships without an interval or test in v1, and the one salient
pattern is well within small-sample noise. We therefore present
\Delta_{\text{tool}}
as a
demonstrated
measurement capability
for an action-level channel that answer-grading and
score-grading both miss, not as a confirmed behavioral effect, and we do not interpret its direction.

Across all 120 per-group pairwise McNemar and Wilcoxon contrasts,

q\leq 0.05
(minimum uncorrected

VII
Limitations

AgentFairBench v1 is a pilot. It prioritizes instrument soundness over exhaustive empirical coverage, and several constraints bound the present findings.

Single-model pilot.
Reported empirical results come from a single model,
claude-haiku-4-5
, run across 864 decisions (36 profiles
\times
6 demographic conditions
\times
4 scaffolds). A separate 648-decision run (C0–C3 only) provided the test–retest reliability estimate. External models (GPT-4o, Gemini series, open-weight alternatives) appear on the leaderboard scaffold but have not been executed by the authors, and those entries await community submissions. This model showed no demographic effect above the noise floor; whether other model families do is exactly the question the instrument exists to answer, and resolving it requires runs across multiple architectures and alignment regimes at comparable scale. The null here is best read as evidence that the instrument runs end to end and, on this model at this scale, detects nothing above noise, not as evidence that LLM agents are unbiased in general. Comparison across models is a separate, powered question we leave to the leaderboard.

Statistical power.
With 12 matched sets per demographic cell, the pilot is underpowered for fine-grained contrast detection. Zero of 120 pairwise contrasts (3 domains
\times
4 scaffolds
\times
5 focal groups
\times
2 tests) survive Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction. This is predictable given this sample size. BCa bootstrap confidence intervals and McNemar/Wilcoxon paired tests confirm the instrument behaves correctly, but isolating effects at the individual-contrast level requires the full grid (target: 50+ matched sets per cell). Researchers drawing policy-relevant conclusions should await the powered release.

Temperature, stochastic noise, and the arity-matched floor.
Version 1 does not pin sampling temperature, which introduces stochastic variability in both the discrete decision and the continuous score. A second independent run quantifies the noise floor (decision agreement 0.917; per-cell score MAE 0.15–5.75). As §
VI
(Finding 1) shows, the noise floor must be compared to MASD at matched arity: a naive MASD-vs-pairwise-MAE comparison reports a mean ratio of
2.4\times
the noise floor, but against a same-arity six-group noise spread the observed MASD is at or below noise in every cell (mean
0.83\times
). Two data limitations bound this control. First, the second-run raw scores were summarized to per-cell noise but not committed as a separate trace file, so our arity-matched null is estimated from the primary run’s two-way residual rather than from a true repeated-measurement design; releasing the retest traces and collecting a genuine
k
-replicate identical-input floor is a v1.1 item. Second, with one retest run there is no interval on the floor itself. Version 1.1 will pin sampling temperature, average across replicate runs, and ship a repeated-measurement noise floor, which together give a sharper separation between stochastic noise and any systematic effect.

Ceiling and saturation on binary outcomes.
Binary decision metrics (advance, approve, escalate) are bounded. They saturate when model acceptance rates approach 0 or 1 across all groups, at which point CFR collapses mechanically regardless of underlying score disparity, one way the masking failure mode P2 describes could arise. Score-level metrics (MASD, APR tier, acuity) are less susceptible. Future versions will supplement binary outcomes with ranked or continuous action targets where feasible.

Domain and trajectory scope.
The benchmark covers three single-turn consequential domains: hiring, lending, and triage, each grounded in a regulatory anchor
[
31
,
30
,
27
]
. Multi-step tool use, episodic memory, and long-horizon planning trajectories are not yet instrumented. These form the core v2 roadmap. The BCF scaffold taxonomy already designates C4 tool-invocation and multi-step memory as entry loci, and
\Delta_{\text{tool}}
is defined but requires richer trajectory data to populate. Single-turn evaluation cannot capture compounding disparity across agent episodes
[
23
]
. That gap is a design priority for v2.

Name-coding as a demographic proxy.
Demographic signal is injected via Bertrand-Mullainathan name lists
[
4
]
, which encode race-by-gender correlations from U.S. labor-market contexts. The approach is well-validated for measuring implicit association in text-based systems, but it conflates name familiarity, cultural context, and perceived ethnicity in ways that do not map cleanly to protected-class definitions under statute
[
11
]
; name-perception studies show the race signal carried by audit names varies by name and is confounded with socioeconomic status
[
14
]
, so our name pools are a deliberately coarse proxy for perceived demographics, not a measurement of protected-class membership. Intersectional axes beyond race and gender (disability status, national origin, age beyond the secondary attribute) are not yet represented.

Despite these constraints, AgentFairBench v1 ships a methodologically sound instrument: counterfactually matched profile sets, paired statistical tests, FDR control, contamination canaries, and a reproducible single-digit-dollar-per-model cost structure. The pilot shows that the instrument measures these action-level channels and recovers a planted disparity, that naive spread-versus-pairwise comparisons must be arity-corrected to avoid reading noise as bias, and that on this model at this scale no demographic effect rises above the noise floor; the gap between answer-level benchmarks
[
28
,
24
]
and action-level evaluation remains real and consequential.

VIII
Ethics and Broader Impact

Human Subjects and Data Privacy.
All profiles in AgentFairBench are fully synthetic. No real individuals’ records, personally identifiable information, or protected health information appear anywhere in the benchmark. Because the benchmark contains only synthetic data and no human subjects, the authors determined that the work does not constitute human-subjects research and so did not require IRB review or consent; any deployment of the benchmark on real applicant, borrower, or patient records would require the appropriate institutional approvals. The synthetic generation pipeline is seeded deterministically (seed 20260612) and produces counterfactually matched profile sets whose demographic signal is carried exclusively by name tokens; all substantive content (qualifications, financials, symptom descriptions) is demographically neutral by construction.

Interpretation of Name-Coded Demographic Signals.
The benchmark follows the audit-study tradition established by
[
4
]
, in which perceived demographic identity is manipulated through name choice rather than direct self-report. Where the benchmark measures a disparity, it therefore reflects the causal effect of a perceived-demographic name cue on agent behavior, not the effect of race or gender as such (the present pilot found none above noise). Names serve as probabilistic proxies: a name coded as Black-female is statistically associated with a perception, not with any individual’s actual identity. Researchers and practitioners should be precise about this distinction when reporting or acting on AgentFairBench results, to avoid reifying demographic categories beyond what the experimental design warrants.

Dual-Use and Anti-Gaming Safeguards.
AgentFairBench is designed for the defensive purpose of auditing and improving LLM agent fairness prior to deployment in consequential settings governed by instruments such as
[
31
]
,
[
27
]
,
[
30
]
, and
[
13
]
. The benchmark could, in principle, be used to overfit or game fairness metrics rather than to reduce underlying bias. We mitigate this risk through two mechanisms: (1) a held-out private evaluation split, withheld from public release, that covers the same profile space under different surface-level name realizations; and (2) a contamination canary, a small set of flagged sentinel items whose content hashes are registered at submission time, enabling detection of training-set contamination. Together these safeguards reduce the incentive and ability to game the public leaderboard without achieving genuine behavioral improvements.

Intended Use and Misuse.
AgentFairBench is a pre-deployment screening instrument for builders and internal responsible-AI teams, not a certification. A passing result is necessary-not-sufficient evidence of fairness and must not be cited as deployment clearance or regulatory compliance; the synthetic, name-coded design does not replace an impact-ratio audit on a deployer’s production data (§
IX
). The risk is asymmetric in triage, where a falsely reassuring “fair” verdict could contribute to under-escalation, a life-safety harm; medical triage in particular must never be gated on this benchmark alone. We are explicit about this because the regulatory crosswalk in §
IX
, if read uncritically, could invite precisely this misuse, and because our own pilot returns a null that a vendor could otherwise misrepresent as a clean bill of health rather than the underpowered single-model result it is.

Attribute Coverage and Intersectionality.
The current release operationalizes race-by-gender intersectionality across six cells (White, Black, Hispanic x Male, Female) with age as a secondary attribute. This coverage is deliberately tractable for a pilot study but is not exhaustive. Demographic groups not represented, including Asian, Indigenous, and multiracial identities, as well as non-binary and gender-nonconforming individuals, and intersections of race with disability or socioeconomic status may exhibit distinct disparity patterns that the benchmark does not currently measure. Researchers should treat AgentFairBench findings as a lower bound on the full scope of agentic demographic disparity; extending the attribute grid is an explicit item for future work. The name-to-group mapping is also probabilistic and culturally situated: names used to signal Hispanic identity may carry different salience across national and regional contexts, and the benchmark’s conclusions should be interpreted within those constraints.

IX
Regulatory and Societal Impact

AgentFairBench is built on explicit regulatory grounding, but we are precise about what it is and is not. It is a
pre-deployment screening
instrument: it runs on synthetic, name-coded counterfactual profiles and produces disparity estimates (which, in this pilot, were null) that map to the questions statutory tests ask, but it is
not
a substitute for a compliance audit, which operates on a deployer’s real applicant, borrower, or patient flow and computes impact ratios on that production data. The mappings in this section are the authors’ interpretation of how the metrics relate to each regime; no regulator has reviewed or endorsed them. Used as intended, the benchmark flags configurations that warrant a closer, production-data audit before deployment, and surfaces action-level channels (graded scores, differential tool invocation) that binary impact-ratio tests miss. A passing AgentFairBench result is a necessary-not-sufficient screen, never deployment clearance.

EEOC Title VII and the Four-Fifths Rule.
The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission’s adverse impact doctrine
[
31
]
requires that the selection rate for any protected group not fall below 80% of the rate for the highest-selecting group. Action-rate disparity is the quantity that maps to this test: the four-fifths rule compares group selection rates, so the advance-rate ratio across the six race-by-gender cells is what an impact-ratio audit computes. CFR and MASD are complementary, not a substitute: CFR measures, within matched profiles, whether the name-coded signal alone flips the advance/reject decision, and MASD surfaces graded score penalties beneath a nominally equal decision. A within-pair flip is evidence of demographic sensitivity in the decision logic rather than itself a four-fifths impact ratio; a complete audit reports the action-rate ratio (for four-fifths compliance) alongside CFR and MASD (for counterfactual sensitivity).

NYC Local Law 144 AEDT Audits.
New York City’s automated employment decision tool ordinance
[
27
]
mandates annual bias audits of any AEDT used in hiring or promotion, with public disclosure of impact ratios disaggregated by sex and race/ethnicity. AgentFairBench’s counterfactual matched-set design (holding all qualifications constant while varying only the demographic signal) is aligned with the impact-ratio calculation the law requires. CFR and action-rate disparity across the six race-by-gender cells populate the disclosure table format directly. We argue, as a forward-looking observation rather than a settled compliance finding, that
\Delta_{\text{tool}}
captures a dimension current AEDT audits do not address: whether the agent disproportionately requests additional information about candidates from certain groups. Such a covert gate is a channel current AEDT audits do not capture; whether any given model exhibits it is an open, measurable question that
\Delta_{\text{tool}}
is built to answer (our pilot did not find it above small-sample noise).

ECOA and Regulation B Fair Lending.
The Equal Credit Opportunity Act and its implementing Regulation B
[
30
]
prohibit creditors from discriminating on the basis of race, sex, national origin, and other protected characteristics in any aspect of a credit transaction. The lending domain in AgentFairBench encodes this directly: the approve boolean maps to the access-to-credit obligation, while the APR tier (1–5) maps to pricing discrimination, a Regulation B concern that decision-level metrics alone cannot capture. MASD on APR tier therefore provides an operationalized disparity statistic for pricing-channel audits, complementing CFR’s access-channel measurement within a single unified evaluation.

EU AI Act High-Risk Systems.
The EU AI Act
[
13
]
classifies AI systems used in employment, credit, and essential services as high-risk, imposing conformity assessment, logging, and human oversight requirements. All three AgentFairBench domains fall within Annex III high-risk categories. The benchmark’s scaffold dimension (C0 through C4) supports Act obligations around transparency: by measuring how disparity changes across chain-of-thought (C2) and multi-agent deliberation (C3) scaffolds, practitioners can document whether a specific deployment configuration attenuates or amplifies bias. That analysis can serve as a quantified input to the technical documentation contemplated by Articles 11 and 12.

NIST AI RMF Measure and Manage Functions.
The NIST AI Risk Management Framework
[
26
]
identifies bias and fairness as cross-cutting risks requiring structured measurement (MEASURE 2.5) and active mitigation with monitored effectiveness (MANAGE 2.2). AgentFairBench provides an end-to-end instrument for both functions. CFR, MASD, and
\Delta_{\text{tool}}
populate MEASURE-function dashboards across demographically protected attributes. The scaffold-stratified breakdown then enables MANAGE-function attribution: it isolates whether disparity originates at a retrieval tool invocation (C4), a reasoning step (C2), or aggregation across sub-agents (C3), so mitigation can be directed at the responsible component rather than applied system-wide.

X
Reproducibility

The harness uses a fixed global seed
20260612
, passed explicitly to every randomized step: name assignment from the demographic pool, matched-set ordering, BCa bootstrap resampling (2,000 draws, matched-set resampling to preserve pairing), and the test-retest reliability run. Two senses of reproducibility should be distinguished. The seeded harness operations and the recomputation of every reported metric from the released decision traces are byte-identical for any independent evaluator. Re-collecting model decisions is
not
bit-reproducible: the production endpoints sample at non-zero temperature with no exposed seed, so fresh API calls can differ (this is precisely the stochasticity the test-retest noise floor quantifies). Reproducibility claims in this paper refer to the former; the released raw traces are what make the latter auditable.

Model identity is recorded at the API level: the pilot was collected against
claude-haiku-4-5
through the Anthropic API at the provider’s default decoding parameters (temperature was left at the provider default in effect in June 2026 and not separately pinned or recorded per call; v1.1 pins it), with the exact model string and the collection date logged per call in
results/raw/claude-haiku-4-5_raw.jsonl
. Leaderboard submissions must report the full model identifier, endpoint, and access date. Entries that use an alias or omit the date are rejected during intake. External models (GPT-4o, Gemini) were not run by the authors and are not reported as pilot results; their leaderboard cells remain open pending community submission.

Each of the 36 base profiles carries a truncated SHA-256 content hash (16 hex digits) computed over the UTF-8-encoded profile body. These hashes appear in the released JSONL alongside each raw decision record. They serve as integrity checks and as version identifiers: any downstream modification of a profile invalidates its hash, and the harness detects this on load.

The released artifacts comprise: (i)
data/profiles/public_dev.jsonl
(public split, CC-BY-4.0), containing the 36 profiles with domain tags, difficulty labels, and content hashes; (ii)
data/names/name_pools.json
; (iii) raw decision-level JSONL (
results/raw/
) recording one line per decision with all prompt fields, the injected name, and the model’s structured output; and (iv) computed metric reports (
results/
_report.json
).

The metric and statistics core (
harness/agentfairbench/metrics.py
) depends only on
numpy
;
scipy
is intentionally excluded to keep the implementation portable and auditable. All statistical routines (BCa bootstrap, McNemar exact test, Wilcoxon signed-rank with tie correction, Benjamini–Hochberg FDR, and Cohen’s
h
) are self-contained in roughly 350 lines of pure Python. Dependencies are declared in
harness/pyproject.toml
(
numpy
for the metrics core;
pytest
for the test suite).

A single CLI command reproduces the full pipeline:

python -m agentfairbench.cli run \
--profiles public\_dev.jsonl \
--names name\_pools.json \
--adapter mock --seed 20260612

The
mock
adapter requires no API key and completes in seconds, enabling pipeline verification without cost. A
report
subcommand re-derives metrics from a pre-collected raw JSONL, so the statistical layer can be re-run independently of data collection.

The test suite (
harness/tests/test_metrics.py
, 14 tests) enforces several properties by construction. First, a
BiasedMock
adapter that injects a
-25
score penalty for names drawn from the
black_female
pool must produce positive MASD and non-zero pairwise score gaps, verifying that the metrics are sensitive to a known injected disparity. Second, the neutral
MockAdapter
(whose decisions are identical across all demographic conditions) must yield
\text{CFR}=0
and
\text{MASD}=0
in every cell, confirming that the harness introduces no spurious disparity. A third group verifies the
\Delta_{\text{tool}}
metric and the C4 tool-scaffold schema. All 14 tests pass without API access.

A contamination canary string (
AGENTFAIRBENCH-CANARY-2f9c1a
) is embedded in the public split to detect training-set leakage. A closed private split, withheld from public release, is used for all official leaderboard rankings.

XI
Conclusion

Fairness for LLMs has been measured at the wrong layer. As models move from answering to acting, the consequential disparity lives in what an agent
does
(which candidate it advances, whose loan it approves, which patient it escalates, and for whom it demands more evidence), not in what it says about a demographic group in the abstract. AgentFairBench closes this gap with a cheap, reproducible, multi-domain, action-level benchmark grounded in the Bias Conduction Framework, an open harness, and a live anti-gamed leaderboard.

Our pilot makes a methodological point and reports an honest null. The methodological point: a six-group score
spread
(MASD) compared against a two-run pairwise noise difference looks like
2.4\times
the noise floor but is
0.83\times
it once the floor is matched to the statistic’s arity, so naive benchmarks that make this comparison will read sampling noise as bias. We correct it with an arity-matched noise floor and an omnibus group test, and define the masking (P2), amplification (P3), and tool-invocation channels as measurement targets the instrument exposes rather than effects this pilot confirms. The null: against the corrected floor,
claude-haiku-4-5
shows no demographic effect distinguishable from sampling noise at
n=12
matched sets per cell (0 of 120 pairwise and 0 of 9 omnibus contrasts survive correction). A planted-bias property test shows the instrument is not insensitive; it recovers a known disparity. The held-out private split, the leaderboard, and the single-digit-dollar cost envelope are designed so the community can run the powered, multi-model studies that would detect a real effect if one exists.

Future work follows the BCF roadmap toward surfaces this single-turn pilot only gestures at: full multi-step tool and memory trajectories, long-horizon disparity accumulation, temperature pinning and replicate averaging to sharpen significance, explicit-attribute robustness arms beyond name-coding, and a broad multi-vendor model panel contributed through the leaderboard. The benchmark is a first, tight version meant to be adopted and extended, and its value compounds with use.
