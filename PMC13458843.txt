Intraoperative three-dimensional imaging-guided vertebral body stenting for cleft-associated very severe osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures: a surgical technique case series
Authors: Hiroki Eguchi, Yosuke Nakayama, Eitaro Okumura, Ryo Hashimoto, Motoo Kubota, Shigekuni Tachibana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVery severe osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (vsOVCF) associated with intravertebral clefts represent a technically challenging subgroup of vertebral augmentation because of severe vertebral collapse, distorted intravertebral anatomy, and increased risk of cement leakage. Conventional fluoroscopy alone may be insufficient for accurate device positioning in these cases. We describe the surgical technique and technical utility of intraoperative three-dimensional (3D) imaging during vertebral body stenting (VBS) in three consecutive patients with cleft-associated vsOVCF without neurological deficits. All patients demonstrated intravertebral clefts on preoperative imaging. The procedure was performed using a bilateral transpedicular approach under biplanar fluoroscopy with adjunctive intraoperative 3D rotational imaging. Intraoperative 3D imaging enabled confirmation of balloon and stent positioning within residual cancellous bone rather than within the cleft cavity. Vertebral height restoration ranged from 9% to 18%, and all patients experienced marked pain relief, with mean Numerical Rating Scale scores improving from 8.0 preoperatively to 1.0 postoperatively. Cement leakage into the adjacent disc occurred in two cases, although no spinal canal leakage or symptomatic complications were observed. Postoperative computed tomography at 3 and 6 months demonstrated maintained vertebral height without recollapse, osteonecrosis, or implant-related complications. Intraoperative 3D imaging may serve as a useful technical adjunct for safer and more controlled VBS in anatomically complex fractures.Keywords: Osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture (OVCF), vertebral body stenting (VBS), intravertebral cleft, three-dimensional imaging (3D imaging), spinal augmentation

Introduction

Osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs) are a common cause of pain, disability, and reduced quality of life in elderly patients (1). Vertebral augmentation procedures, including vertebroplasty and techniques using vertebral expansion devices such as balloon kyphoplasty (BKP), are widely used for pain relief and mechanical stabilization (2). However, treatment of very severe osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (vsOVCF), generally defined as vertebral collapse to less than one-third of the original vertebral height, remains technically challenging (3,4).

In particular, vsOVCF associated with an intravertebral cleft represents a distinct pathological condition characterized by pseudarthrosis and dynamic instability (5,6). These fractures frequently demonstrate severe vertebral collapse, disrupted trabecular architecture, endplate defects, and proximity to the posterior vertebral wall, all of which increase the technical difficulty and risk of cement leakage during vertebral augmentation procedures.

Conventional biplanar fluoroscopy may be insufficient to accurately assess device position in these anatomically distorted vertebrae. In particular, cleft cavities may be misinterpreted as residual cancellous bone, potentially resulting in inaccurate placement of vertebral expansion devices. Intraoperative three-dimensional (3D) imaging may provide additional spatial information that improves procedural accuracy in such cases.

Vertebral body stenting (VBS) was developed to improve vertebral height restoration and maintain reduction using an expandable metallic stent prior to cement augmentation (7). Unlike BKP, the deployed stent helps prevent loss of reduction after balloon deflation. Previous studies have demonstrated favorable radiographic and clinical outcomes with VBS (7,8); however, evidence regarding its application in cleft-associated vsOVCF remains limited.

This report describes our surgical technique and the technical utility of intraoperative 3D imaging during VBS for cleft-associated vsOVCF. We present this article in accordance with the SUPER reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0124/rc).

Preoperative preparations and requirements

We retrospectively reviewed three consecutive patients with symptomatic cleft-associated vsOVCF treated at Kameda Medical Center (Kamogawa, Chiba, Japan). Indications for this surgical technique were defined as follows: (I) vertebral collapse to less than one-third of the original vertebral height, (II) persistent severe pain despite conservative treatment, (III) absence of neurological deficits, and (IV) presence of an intravertebral cleft on preoperative imaging.

Failure of conservative treatment was defined as persistent severe back pain despite analgesic medication, inability to achieve satisfactory functional recovery, and/or progressive vertebral collapse on serial radiographs. In all three cases, vertebral fractures were diagnosed immediately after symptom onset using plain radiographs at outside institutions. Surgical intervention was considered only after prolonged conservative management failed. The interval from symptom onset to surgery ranged from 3 to 9 months.

All patients were referred to Kameda Medical Center for surgical evaluation. Preoperative computed tomography (CT) was routinely performed during the initial evaluation to assess fracture morphology, intravertebral cleft configuration, vertebral body collapse, and posterior wall integrity (Figure 1). In all three cases, surgery was performed within approximately 1 month after CT acquisition to minimize interval changes in fracture morphology.

Step-by-step description

Step 1: Patient positioning and fluoroscopic setup

The patient is positioned prone on a radiolucent operating table. Careful padding is applied to prevent pressure-related complications. Biplanar fluoroscopy is arranged to obtain stable anteroposterior and lateral views throughout the procedure. Proper alignment of the vertebral body is confirmed before instrumentation.

Step 2: Transpedicular access

A bilateral transpedicular approach is established under fluoroscopic guidance. Using a low lateral trajectory, the pedicle is cannulated while avoiding cortical breach. Particular caution is required in severely collapsed vertebrae because the residual cancellous corridor may be markedly reduced.

The drill is advanced to approximately three-quarters of the anterior vertebral body while maintaining continuous fluoroscopic confirmation (Figure 2A,2B). Severe collapse and altered vertebral morphology may obscure anatomical landmarks; therefore, trajectory adjustment should be performed carefully.

Step 3: Balloon catheter insertion

After creating the working channel, balloon catheters are inserted bilaterally into the vertebral body (Figure 2C). Balloon position is initially assessed using conventional biplanar fluoroscopy.

At this stage, a major technical pitfall is misinterpreting the intravertebral cleft cavity as residual cancellous bone. Placement within the cleft may result in ineffective expansion and poor mechanical support.

Step 4: Intraoperative 3D imaging confirmation

Intraoperative 3D rotational imaging is performed before balloon inflation (Figure 2D-2F). This step is the key technical feature of our procedure.

This additional spatial information allows repositioning when balloon placement is suboptimal. In our series, 3D imaging confirmed that balloon catheters were located within residual cancellous bone rather than within the cleft cavity.

Step 5: Balloon expansion and stent deployment

Balloon expansion is performed gradually and symmetrically to reduce the collapsed vertebral body and create an internal cavity. Excessive expansion should be avoided to prevent endplate disruption or posterior migration.

After satisfactory expansion, the vertebral body stents are deployed (Figure 2G). A key advantage of VBS over BKP is that the metallic stent preserves the restored cavity and reduces loss of reduction after balloon deflation.

Step 6: Cement injection

High-viscosity PMMA cement is injected using a staged pause-and-inject technique to reduce cement leakage risk (Figure 2H,2I).

Continuous fluoroscopic monitoring is essential during cement injection. Injection should be stopped immediately if leakage toward the posterior wall, venous channels, or intervertebral disc is observed.

In our series, cement leakage into the adjacent disc occurred in two cases, but no spinal canal leakage occurred.

Postoperative considerations and tasks

Patients were monitored postoperatively for neurological deterioration, worsening back pain, and procedure-related complications including cement leakage, pulmonary embolism, and adjacent vertebral fracture.

Early postoperative mobilization was encouraged as tolerated. Pain relief was evaluated using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS). All procedures were completed successfully without intraoperative complications. All patients experienced marked pain relief, with mean NRS improving from 8.0 preoperatively to 1.0 postoperatively.

Radiographic evaluation included assessment of vertebral height restoration, angular correction, and cement leakage. Vertebral height restoration ranged from 9% to 18%.

Cement leakage into the adjacent intervertebral disc occurred in two of three cases. However, no spinal canal leakage, pulmonary embolism, or symptomatic complications were observed.

CT was routinely performed at 3 and 6 months postoperatively. No recollapse or adjacent vertebral fractures were observed during follow-up. CT performed at both 3 and 6 months demonstrated maintained vertebral height without evidence of recollapse, osteonecrosis, or implant-related complications in all cases (Figure 3C,3D).

Discussion

Management of vsOVCF remains one of the most technically demanding and controversial areas in spinal augmentation. In particular, when vsOVCF is associated with an intravertebral cleft, the procedure becomes substantially more complex due to the combination of severe structural collapse, disrupted trabecular architecture, and increased risk of cement leakage. Previous studies have reported high rates of cement leakage in severely collapsed vertebrae, especially in the presence of posterior wall defects or endplate disruption, leading many authors to consider such cases a relative contraindication for vertebral augmentation (7,8). These concerns are clinically relevant, as complications such as spinal canal leakage or pulmonary embolism, although uncommon, may be catastrophic.

Despite these concerns, it is important to recognize that not all vsOVCFs are biologically or mechanically equivalent. In particular, the presence of an intravertebral cleft represents a distinct pathological entity characterized by pseudarthrosis and dynamic instability. Intravertebral clefts have been shown to exhibit mobility on dynamic imaging and are associated with delayed vertebral collapse and progressive kyphotic deformity over time (9,10). This instability is widely considered a major contributor to persistent pain. Therefore, while severe vertebral collapse alone may increase procedural risk, the coexistence of a cleft may identify a subgroup of patients who are more likely to benefit from stabilization procedures.

From a technical perspective, cleft-associated vsOVCF presents unique challenges. The internal architecture of the vertebral body is often severely disrupted, with irregular cavities, endplate compromise, and proximity to the posterior wall. Under these conditions, conventional biplanar fluoroscopy may be insufficient to reliably assess the spatial relationship between instruments, residual cancellous bone, and cortical boundaries. Conventional fluoroscopy alone may misinterpret cleft cavities as cancellous bone, potentially leading to device malposition. Such misinterpretation may result in ineffective expansion, asymmetric stent deployment, or increased risk of cement leakage.

In this context, intraoperative 3D imaging offers a potential solution by providing additional spatial information that cannot be obtained from standard fluoroscopy. In the present series, 3D imaging enabled confirmation that the balloon and stent were positioned within the residual cancellous bone rather than within the cleft cavity or in proximity to the posterior wall. This distinction is critical, as placement within the cleft cavity may lead to insufficient mechanical support, whereas proximity to the posterior wall increases the risk of neurological complications. Although this study does not include a control group, these intraoperative findings suggest that 3D imaging may enhance procedural accuracy in anatomically complex cases.

Another important consideration is the mechanism of pain relief in cleft-associated vsOVCF. Traditionally, vertebral augmentation has been evaluated in terms of vertebral height restoration and kyphotic correction. However, the results of the present study suggest that these parameters may not be the primary determinants of clinical outcome in this specific subgroup. Despite only modest vertebral height restoration (9–18%), all patients experienced marked pain relief. This observation supports the concept that stabilization of pseudarthrosis, rather than anatomical reduction, is the key therapeutic mechanism in cleft-associated fractures.

This distinction has important implications for the choice of augmentation technique. BKP has been widely used for OVCF, but one of its limitations is the partial loss of restored vertebral height following balloon deflation prior to cement injection. VBS was developed to address this limitation by deploying an expandable metallic stent that maintains the created cavity and provides internal structural support (11). Biomechanically, this may be particularly advantageous in severely collapsed vertebrae, where the residual trabecular framework is insufficient to maintain reduction. Clinical studies have supported the effectiveness of VBS in achieving vertebral height restoration and kyphotic correction. A multicenter study by Diel et al. demonstrated favorable radiological and safety outcomes with VBS (12), and more recent prospective data have further supported its effectiveness and safety (13). Although most previous studies have focused on fractures with moderate collapse, the present findings extend these observations to a more severe subset of cleft-associated vsOVCF.

Cement leakage remains a major concern in vertebral augmentation, particularly in severely collapsed vertebrae. In the present series, leakage into the intervertebral disc was observed in two of three cases. Although these events were asymptomatic, discal leakage should not be considered benign. Previous studies have demonstrated that cement leakage into the disc is associated with an increased risk of adjacent vertebral fractures, likely due to altered load distribution and increased stress on neighboring segments (14). Importantly, the occurrence of leakage despite the use of intraoperative 3D imaging highlights that advanced imaging cannot eliminate this risk. Cement leakage is influenced by multiple factors including cement viscosity, fracture morphology, and injection technique, as previously reported (15,16).

The present study has several limitations. First, the sample size is extremely small, and the findings should be interpreted as preliminary. Second, the retrospective design, extremely small sample size, and lack of a control group preclude statistical comparison with other vertebral augmentation techniques, such as BKP or other vertebral expansion devices. Therefore, definitive conclusions regarding the superiority of VBS or the additional value of intraoperative 3D imaging cannot be drawn from this case series. Third, although follow-up CT evaluations were available at both 3 and 6 months in all cases, longer-term radiological assessment beyond 6 months was unavailable, limiting evaluation of delayed osteonecrosis or late recollapse. Furthermore, all cases were carefully selected and involved cleft-associated fractures without neurological deficits, which may limit the generalizability of the results.

Despite these limitations, this study provides important technical insights. Rather than suggesting that vertebral augmentation should be broadly applied to all vsOVCF cases, our findings emphasize the importance of patient selection and procedural strategy. In particular, cleft-associated vsOVCF represents a unique subgroup in which stabilization may provide significant clinical benefit despite the high procedural risk. In such cases, intraoperative 3D imaging may serve as a valuable adjunct, enabling more accurate device placement and supporting safer execution of VBS.

In conclusion, cleft-associated vsOVCF represents a technically challenging but clinically meaningful indication for vertebral augmentation. Intraoperative 3D imaging provides additional spatial information beyond conventional fluoroscopy and may improve procedural accuracy in this complex setting. While this technique does not eliminate complications such as cement leakage, it may contribute to safer and more controlled VBS in carefully selected patients. Further studies with larger cohorts and comparative designs are warranted to clarify its clinical impact.

Conclusions

Intraoperative 3D imaging may facilitate more accurate balloon and stent positioning during VBS for cleft-associated vsOVCF. Although further comparative studies are warranted, this technique appears to be a useful adjunct for improving procedural accuracy in anatomically complex cases.
