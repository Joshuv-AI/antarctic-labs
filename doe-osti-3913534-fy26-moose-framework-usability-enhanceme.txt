 Abstract

This report presents a comprehensive overview of user-oriented enhancements delivered to the
Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE) framework in Fiscal Year 2026,
with a focus on advancing mesh generation, Constructive Solid Geometry (CSG) support, and
control algorithm development for nuclear reactor modeling and simulation. The improvements
address longstanding challenges in usability, accuracy, and interoperability, enabling more efficient
workflows for both new and experienced users.

 Key developments include the introduction of specialized meshing tools for fast reactor restraint
systems, such as the GapLineMeshGenerator, which automates the creation of conformal gap meshes
critical for modeling structural rings and load paths in sodium-cooled fast reactors. Advanced 2D
and 3D meshing algorithms now support boundary layer refinement, economical hole conversion,
and quadratic element generation, allowing for high-fidelity discretization of complex geometries
while minimizing computational overhead.

 A major milestone is the implementation of a general-purpose single time-dependent parameter
inverse solution functionality in Optimization module. This implementation established the initial
control algorithm within MOOSE, enabling inverse simulation workflows where users can recover
control histories to achieve prescribed output targets. This feature streamlines transient analysis
tasks such as startup, shutdown, and load-following scenarios, reducing the need for custom scripting
and manual intervention.

 The report also details the continued development of a robust CSG framework, which automates
the conversion of mesh-based geometry inputs into generic Monte Carlo (MC) CSG definitions.
New features include support for lattices and “engineering units” in the CSG framework and the
complete implementation of the CSG generation process for the Reactor Geometry Mesh Builder
(RGMB) mesh generators. Demonstrations using the Advanced Burner Test Reactor (ABTR)
geometry confirm the consistency and accuracy of CSG and finite element mesh representations,
with material-wise volumes agreeing within statistical uncertainties. This advancement bridges the
gap between deterministic and stochastic simulation tools, facilitating seamless integration of MC
codes like Shift into multiphysics workflows.

 Collectively, these enhancements position MOOSE as a leading platform for integrated, high-
fidelity nuclear reactor analysis, reducing user burden, improving reliability, and enabling broader
adoption across the reactor physics and multiphysics simulation communities.

1 Introduction

The Department of Energy Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (DOE-NEAMS)

program continues to drive innovation in computational tools for advanced reactor analysis, with

a focus on integrating high-fidelity physics, usability, and workflow efficiency. At the core of this

effort is the MOOSE [4] framework, which serves as the foundation for a suite of reactor physics

applications and multiphysics modeling tools. Over the past year, the NEAMS program has

prioritized enhancements that address both technical challenges and user experience, responding to

stakeholder feedback and evolving research needs across national laboratories, regulatory agencies,

and academic collaborators.

 This FY26 report documents a new phase of user-oriented improvements to the MOOSE

framework, emphasizing expanded meshing capabilities, Constructive Solid Geometry (CSG) support,

and advanced control algorithm development. These enhancements are designed to streamline

the simulation setup process, improve geometric fidelity, and enable more sophisticated analysis

workflows for nuclear reactor systems.

 High-fidelity modeling of reactor components increasingly demands flexible and accurate mesh

generation tools. Traditional finite element mesh (FEM) construction for complex nuclear geometries

has often required specialized software, steep learning curves, and manual intervention—barriers

that can limit adoption and introduce errors. Building on the success of the MOOSE Reactor

Module, which was developed to provide accessible, open-source meshing solutions tailored to

reactor applications, this year’s work delivers new features that directly address gaps identified by

users. Notably, the introduction of the GapLineMeshGenerator enables automated meshing of fast

reactor core restraint systems, a critical capability for modeling structural rings and load paths in

sodium-cooled fast reactors. Additional improvements include boundary layer meshing, economical

hole conversion in three dimensions, and extended support for quadratic elements, allowing users to

discretize challenging geometries with greater accuracy and efficiency.

 As simulation workflows grow in complexity, the need for robust interoperability between

 1

deterministic and stochastic analysis tools has become increasingly important. Monte Carlo (MC)

codes, which rely on CSG representations rather than mesh-based geometries, are essential for

reference neutronics calculations, cross section generation, and multiphysics coupling. Historically,

users have been required to manually construct equivalent geometry inputs for each code, a process

that is both time-consuming and error-prone. This report details the new developments for the

generalized CSG framework within MOOSE, enabling automated conversion of mesh generator

inputs into generic MC CSG definitions. This advancement supports seamless integration of MC

codes such as Shift, reducing user burden and improving workflow reliability.

 Another major focus of this year’s work is the implementation of a general-purpose control

algorithm for inverse simulation problems. Many reactor analysis tasks—such as startup, shutdown,

and transient load-following—require the ability to recover control histories that achieve prescribed

output targets. Until now, such workflows have relied on custom scripting and manual iteration. The

new control algorithm, developed within the MOOSE optimization module, provides a standardized,

user-friendly approach to inverse simulation, broadening the scope of analyses that can be performed

and improving reproducibility.

 These improvements were guided by direct engagement with NEAMS stakeholders and external

collaborators, including Argonne National Laboratory, Idaho National Laboratory, the Nuclear

Regulatory Commission, and university partners. Demonstrations and validation exercises confirm

that the new capabilities produce consistent and accurate results across a range of reactor geometries

and analysis scenarios.

 This report builds upon prior years’ user-oriented enhancements to the MOOSE framework

[5, 6, 7, 2], continuing the commitment to advancing multiphysics simulation, improving usability,

and supporting the evolving needs of the nuclear science and engineering community.

2 2D and 3D Meshing Improvements

The FY26 work took on three limitations of the native meshing tools in the MOOSE framework

and its Reactor Module. Some reactor geometries could not be described by any existing mesh

generator at all. Element size in unstructured meshes could be controlled only through a target

area, which is a blunt instrument wherever the solution varies rapidly across a thin near-wall region.

And the tetrahedral meshing path was restricted to first-order elements. In each case the underlying

machinery had already been built up between FY22 and FY25, so most of the work reported

below consists of lifting the restrictions that kept that machinery from reaching realistic reactor

components.

 This chapter contains the following key improvements:

 • Conformal gap meshing: The new GapLineMeshGenerator produces a polyline that follows

 the external boundary of an arbitrary 2D mesh at a uniform offset. This is the geometric

 primitive needed to mesh fast reactor core restraint systems.

 • 2D boundary layers: XYDelaunayGenerator can now generate biased boundary layers along

 the outer boundary and along each hole boundary, so that near-wall gradients are resolved

 without refining the entire domain.

 • Standalone conformal layer meshing: The new XYTriangleBoundaryLayerGenerator

 applies the same layering algorithm outward or inward from any 2D boundary, covering coating,

 cladding, and gap-filling geometries.

 • Economical hole conversion in 3D: Hole meshes stitched into a tetrahedralization no

 longer need to be converted to TET4 in their entirety. Only the elements touching the stitched

 surface are converted, and the interior of the hole mesh is preserved.

 • Quadratic tetrahedralization: XYZDelaunayGenerator can now emit TET10 and TET14

 elements, and curvature carried by a second-order input surface is inherited by the generated

 mesh.

2.1 New Capabilities to Mesh Fast Reactor Core Restraint Systems

In a sodium-cooled fast reactor, the core restraint system surrounds the assembly bundle with

structural rings separated from the outermost assemblies by a gap of nominally uniform thickness.

That gap sets the load path during core radial expansion, and resolving it is much of the reason a

restraint model is built at all. Meshing it calls for a curve that follows the external boundary of an

already-meshed, and generally non-convex, assembly bundle at a fixed normal offset. No existing

MOOSE mesh generator could produce such a curve, so users constructed the offset polygon by

hand for every core layout.

 A new Reactor Module generator, GapLineMeshGenerator, supplies that curve. It takes

a 2D mesh in the XY plane and returns a polyline mesh of the same kind produced by

PolyLineMeshGenerator, tracing the input boundary at a user-specified offset. The boundary of the

input mesh is extracted first, using the same libMesh utility that XYDelaunayGenerator relies on. If

no boundary is named through boundary ids the external boundary is found automatically, which

assumes a connected input mesh with a single outer boundary manifold; otherwise only the named

boundaries are used. Each boundary vertex is then displaced by the requested thickness along the

average of the normals of the edges meeting at that vertex. Averaging the normals keeps the offset

uniform around corners, not just along the straight runs between them. The displaced vertices are

reconnected into a new polyline through the meshing utilities of PolyLineMeshGenerator. Setting

gap direction takes the offset outward or inward with respect to the volume enclosed by the

boundary, so one generator serves both a restraint ring outside a bundle and a liner inside a vessel.

 By default the generator emits one EDGE2 element per side of the input boundary polygon.

That is the minimum description of the offset curve, and it leaves the mesh density to be chosen

later by the triangulator. Setting max elem size instead inserts additional nodes along each side.

Node spacing on the polyline is rarely an end in itself: the polyline is normally handed on to

XYDelaunayGenerator as a hole, and spacing along a hole boundary constrains the element size of

the surrounding triangulation.

 We made supporting changes to PolyLineMeshGenerator in the same contribution. Its point-

insertion logic moved into the shared utility MooseMeshUtils::buildPolyLineMesh(), so that both

meshes containing multiple closed loops. Multiple loops arise whenever a gap is generated around

more than one disconnected component. PeripheralTriangleMeshGenerator was updated to use

the shared utilities as well.

2.2 New 2D Triangulation Capabilities

XYDelaunayGenerator produces a conforming triangulation of a domain defined by an outer

boundary curve and an arbitrary number of holes. It has become the tool of first resort for 2D

reactor geometries that do not lend themselves to structured meshing. Until this year its only

control over element size was a target area, applied either uniformly or through a spatial function.

Target areas work well for geometry-driven refinement. They serve physics-driven refinement near

walls poorly, because what is wanted there is a thin, strongly graded layer of elements over an

otherwise coarse interior. Raising the target area everywhere to resolve a near-wall gradient is

expensive, and an area function tuned to imitate a boundary layer is awkward to write and fragile

under geometry changes.

 Boundary layer meshing was therefore added to XYDelaunayGenerator in MOOSE. A layer

along the outer boundary is requested with outer boundary layer thickness, outer boundary -

layer num, and outer boundary layer bias, which set the total thickness of the layered region,

the number of layers within it, and the growth factor applied to successive layer thicknesses. Hole

boundaries take the same three parameters in vector form (holes boundary layer thickness,

holes boundary layer num, and holes boundary layer bias) with one entry per hole, so differ-

ent obstructions in the same domain can carry different near-wall resolutions. The layers are

built by offsetting the relevant curve inward using the normal-averaging algorithm developed for

GapLineMeshGenerator, and the region between consecutive offset curves is triangulated in isolation.

The remaining interior is then triangulated as before under the prescribed target area.

 Figure 2.2 compares the two cases for a circular domain containing three meshed obstructions.

Without boundary layers the triangulation runs straight up to the walls. Because the obstruction

boundaries are discretized much more finely than the target area implies, the result is the fan of

stretched, poor-quality triangles visible around each obstruction in Figure 2.2(a). With boundary

layers active, Figure 2.2(b), the near-wall elements are graded and well shaped and the transition to

the interior is smooth, while the interior element size is untouched. Listing 2.2 gives the input; the

boundary layer request amounts to six additional parameters on the existing XYDelaunayGenerator

2.3 New 3D Tetrahedralization Capabilities

XYZDelaunayGenerator tetrahedralizes a 3D domain defined by a boundary surface and, optionally,

a set of hole meshes. Holes can either be left empty or stitched into the result so that a single

conforming mesh is produced. Stitching imposes a compatibility requirement: the faces of the hole

mesh that meet the generated tetrahedra must be TRI3. The original implementation met that

requirement by converting the entire hole mesh to TET4 whenever its external surface contained

non-TRI3 faces. The result is correct but wasteful. A structured hexahedral hole mesh becomes six

tetrahedra per hexahedron throughout its volume, when the only elements that actually need to

change are those touching the stitched surface.

 The 3D transition layer capability delivered in FY25 offers the alternative, and

XYZDelaunayGenerator now puts it to use. The conversion method parameter now selects

between ALL, which reproduces the previous full conversion, and SURFACE, which invokes the

BoundaryElementConversionGenerator machinery to convert only the elements adjacent to the

external surface. The converted layer consists of TET4 elements presenting TRI3 faces to the tetra-

hedral mesh and PYRAMID5 elements presenting QUAD4 faces to the unconverted interior, so the hole

mesh stays conforming throughout. Figure 2.4 shows a cutaway of an 8 × 8 × 8 hexahedral hole

under both strategies. Full conversion turns 512 hexahedra into 3072 tetrahedra; surface conversion

retains the 216 interior hexahedra and adds 768 tetrahedra and 1392 pyramids, for 2376 elements in

total. The advantage grows with hole refinement, since the converted region scales with the surface

area rather than the volume. For the same geometry at 16 × 16 × 16, full conversion produces 24576

tetrahedra while surface conversion produces 12392 elements, half as many, of which 2744 are the

original hexahedra. Listing 2.4 shows the input; the two strategies differ by one parameter.

2.4 Extended Quadratic Element Support

Quadratic elements are attractive for exactly the geometries that motivate tetrahedral meshing in

the first place. First-order tetrahedra represent curved fuel, cladding, and structural surfaces only

as a faceted approximation, and the faceting error is independent of how well the physics is resolved:

it can be reduced only by refining the mesh. Second-order tetrahedra place a node at the midpoint

of each edge, and if those nodes lie on the true surface the geometric error drops by an order in

the element size at no cost in element count. 2D triangulation in MOOSE has supported TRI6 and

TRI7 output for some time. The Netgen-based tetrahedralization path did not.

 On the libMesh side, NetGenMeshInterface was extended to produce quadratic tetrahedra by

the approach already used for 2D triangulation: the domain is tetrahedralized at first order and the

resulting elements are then promoted, with mid-edge and face-center nodes added afterward. MOOSE

exposes the capability through the new tet element type parameter of XYZDelaunayGenerator,

which accepts TET4, TET10, and TET14.

 Curvature comes from the input surface; the generator does not manufacture any of it. If the

boundary surface, or a hole mesh, is itself a second-order (TRI6 or TRI7) surface whose mid-edge

nodes lie on the true geometry, those positions are transferred onto the corresponding faces of the

generated tetrahedra. A first-order input surface produces straight-edged elements with mid-edge

Much of the meshing work described above began as a request from a NEAMS user or an external

stakeholder who depends on the Reactor Module for production analysis. The exchanges summarized

here motivated several of the capabilities already reported, and they left behind a backlog of requests

2.5.1 Fast Reactor Assembly and Restraint Modeling

The fast reactor applications team at Argonne National Laboratory (ANL) builds duct-scale

thermomechanical models of SFR assemblies in which neighboring ducts interact through

their load pads. Their original seven-assembly input carried seven nearly identical copies of

PolygonConcentricCircleMeshGenerator and stitched the above-core and top load pads in as

separate meshes to place them at the correct elevations. We reorganized it jointly with the analysis

team. The batch mesh generation action now instantiates all seven assemblies from a single speci-

fication, and the subdomain swaps mechanism of AdvancedExtruderGenerator assigns the load

pad subdomains as the extrusion proceeds, which retires the stitching entirely (Fig. 2.6). Repeating

a duct bowing calculation reproduced the original to within 1% near the assembly top, where the

load pad contact of interest occurs. The team then asked for each load pad face as an indepen-

dent sideset, so that a contact pair can be isolated when a calculation fails to converge; driving

SideSetsFromNormalsGenerator through the same batch action supplied them. The restraint ring

raised in the same discussion became the GapLineMeshGenerator work of Section 2.1.

2.6 Future Work

Two FY27 tasks extend the meshing work reported here. The larger, funded to support the NEAMS

3 Initial Development of a New Control Algorithm in MOOSE

A recurring need in advanced reactor analysis is the inverse of the usual simulation question. Instead

of prescribing a control history and computing the resulting power, the analyst prescribes the power

history and asks what control history would produce it. Startup and shutdown schedules, load-follow

duty cycles, and reactivity-worth estimates for transient test facilities are all posed this way. The

FY26 effort under the Generalized Reactor Control for Multiphysics Simulations task (Year 1 of 3)

delivered the first general-purpose machinery in MOOSE for this class of problem. A time-dependent

scalar control parameter is now recovered on the fly, so that a selected simulation output follows a

 23

user-specified target throughout a transient.

 Until now, users who needed this behavior wrote it themselves, typically as a bespoke combina-

tion of a driver input, hand-written transfers, and a convergence check tailored to one physics code.

The new capability replaces that pattern with a small set of framework objects in the MOOSE

optimization module, together with a syntax block that assembles the whole workflow from a handful

of inputs.

3.1 Problem Statement and Solution Strategy

Let y = F (p; t) denote a forward transient model that advances one time step given a scalar control

parameter p, and let ytarget (t) be the prescribed history the model output must follow. At each time

step the task is to find the root of

                                  R(p) = F (p; t) − ytarget (t) = 0,                               (1)

where every evaluation of R costs one full solve of the forward model over the current time step.

The parameter is not held fixed across the transient; Eq. (1) is solved anew at every step, so the

converged sequence p(t) is the recovered control history.

 The design adopted here places the root find inside the fixed-point (Picard) loop that MOOSE

already provides for coupled applications. The forward model runs as a sub-application; each

fixed-point iteration of the parent application triggers one sub-application solve of the current time

step and therefore one evaluation of R. A Control object on the parent application observes the

returned output, updates the parameter estimate, and pushes the new value back down before the

next iteration. Convergence of the fixed-point loop is redefined to mean convergence of the root

find. Reusing the fixed-point machinery this way introduced no new execution model. The forward

model needs no modification either, beyond exposing the parameter as a Receiver postprocessor

and the tracked quantity as an ordinary postprocessor.

3.4 Status and Next Steps

The objects and syntax described above, together with their documentation and regression tests,

were delivered to the MOOSE framework, and the reactivity-search demonstration is reproducible

from the Griffin point kinetics input distributed with the capability. What is in place handles a

single scalar parameter tracking a single scalar output, which covers reactivity search but not the

full scope of the three-year task. The FY27 activity turns to a single-mechanism control strategy:

matching a power ramping schedule with the several components of one control mechanism, a

set of control drums or control rods, while holding every operating parameter inside its regulated

range. It will again be built in the MOOSE optimization module, reusing the classes and methods

already available there. The first step retains a single independent parameter, which is sufficient

wherever the individual drum angles can be correlated through a prescribed relation. Releasing the

components to move independently is the harder case. Total power alone no longer fixes a unique

solution, so an additional output such as the peaking factor has to be tracked alongside it, and the

 29

scalar update has to give way to a small dense Jacobian. Whether the fixed-point formulation scales

to production use will be settled on a coupled three-dimensional neutronics and thermal hydraulics

model, where each forward evaluation is expensive and the iteration count per time step starts to

matter.

4 Constructive Solid Geometry Support

The ultimate goal of this work in MOOSE is to create a generalized and automated process for

creating generic Monte Carlo (MC) Constructive Solid Geometry (CSG) definitions from MOOSE

geometry input to support the reactor analysis needs in the reactor physics community. As high-

fidelity multiphysics simulations have become central to nuclear reactor analysis, MOOSE has

advanced significantly in its ability to generate finite element meshes for complex reactor geometries.

However, the growing use of MC codes in these workflows introduces a workflow challenge because

MC codes rely on CSG definitions rather than mesh-based representations used by MOOSE. This

often requires users to manually create multiple equivalent geometry descriptions across codes,

increasing effort and the likelihood of error. To address this gap, this CSG framework development

in MOOSE supports the automated conversion of mesh-based geometry input into generic MC CSG

definitions, with the aim of streamlining coupled workflows, reducing user burden, and enabling

broader integration of MC codes into reactor multiphysics analysis.

 In FY24, initial work was done to investigate the feasibility of adding generalized support for

CSG model generation in MOOSE and subsequently create a multi-year implementation plan [7].

The first phase of that implementation plan was carried out in FY25, which was to develop the

initial CSG base framework in MOOSE that can be used by any mesh generator to implement the

method(s) required to create a generic CSG representation for that mesh generator [2]. The first

phase of development included support for defining surfaces, cells, and universes in a MOOSE CSG

model and a command-line option to trigger the CSG generation process.

 In FY26, substantial strides were made to implement all remaining components in the CSG base

 30

framework, including support for lattices, transformations, and “engineering units” (all described in

more detail in Section 4.4). Engineering units, which are described in detail in Section 4.4.6, are an

analogous CSG-based definition to that used by the Titan front-end interface to Shift [10]. The

added support for engineering units in the CSG framework and development of a few specific initial

engineering units was a crucial step in developing the direct connection of the Shift MC code [11]

and other MOOSE-based applications such as Griffin [9].

 Following the completion of the additional components in the CSG framework, the CSG

generation method for the Reactor Geometry Mesh Builder (RGMB) mesh generators [12] was

completed this FY. The implementation, which uses engineering units, is described in detail in

Section 4.5 and demonstrated using the ABTR [13] in Section 4.6.

4.1 Motivation

Having tools and support for easily creating accurate MC models is a common request among the

reactor physics analysis community due to the complex nature of constructing CSG representations.

For users of MOOSE-based nuclear reactor analysis software, MC CSG models must be manually

developed in order to generate group cross sections (such as for deterministic neutronics analysis),

create reference neutronics results, perform multiphysics analysis with multiple analysis tools, and

do code-to-code comparisons. Manually creating these MC CSG models is a complex and tedious

task that requires users to be familiar with the syntax and complexities of a specific MC code

and to understand how the CSG models are created. Additionally, creating multiple geometry

input representations (i.e., MOOSE and MC) for the same model manually, especially for large

complex models, is unnecessarily time consuming and can lead to inconsistencies in the geometry

definitions due to human-introduced errors that are difficult to identify. For users who already use

MOOSE-based workflows, the information required to generate any geometry representation of a

model is generally already available in the MOOSE input and can therefore be used to create any

other type of geometry representation given the right workflows and tools in place. For example,

MOOSE uses reporting IDs to represent collections of finite elements that define different regions,

 31

such as material zones, pin type, axial zones, etc.. These IDs that are already available in MOOSE

can be leveraged automatically in the CSG model generation to assign CSG materials or other

domain types (such as tally regions or depletion zones) to the different CSG spatial domains (also

known as cells). Implementing an automated process for converting MOOSE-based geometries to

MC CSG geometries will improve the workflow efficiency and reduce user error in neutronics and

multiphysics analysis.

4.2 Constructive Solid Geometry Overview

CSG is commonly used in MC neutronics because it represents geometry with analytically defined

surfaces rather than a discretized finite element mesh. In a finite element mesh, curved boundaries

such as fuel pin cylinders must be approximated by many mesh elements, so geometric accuracy

depends on the mesh resolution and element shape. In contrast, CSG preserves these boundaries

exactly by defining regions from mathematical surfaces and combining them with Boolean operations.

For this reason, MC calculations are often used as reference solutions, since they avoid spatial

discretization error in the geometric representation.

 A CSG model is built from surfaces, cells, and universes. Surfaces are defined by equations, such

as planes or spheres, and each surface separates space into positive and negative half-spaces. Cells

are then formed by combining these half-spaces through Boolean operations such as intersection,

union, and complement to create closed spatial regions. Each cell has both a region and a fill, where

the fill may be a material, void, universe, or lattice. Universes are collections of cells that allow

hierarchical and repeated geometry definitions, with all geometry ultimately belonging to a root

universe. A lattice is a repeated patterned array of universes, such as a pin lattice or assembly

lattice.

 To preserve geometric fidelity, the CSG model is generated directly from the user-specified

mesh generator input rather than from the finite element mesh produced by MOOSE. This ensures

that the CSG representation remains consistent with the intended geometry regardless of mesh

 32

refinement. Although it is theoretically possible to derive a CSG model from a finite element mesh,

doing so would require representing many discretized boundary facets as separate CSG surfaces,

making the model unnecessarily complex, inefficient, and less practical for reliable MC simulation.

4.3 The CSGBase Framework

Built around the CSGBase class and integrated with the existing MeshGenerator system, the

framework allows developers to implement generateCSG() methods that construct surfaces, regions,

cells, universes, lattices, and engineering units, optionally with applied transformations, for use

in MC codes. For users, CSG generation is invoked with the --csg-only command-line option,

which mirrors the --mesh-only option but produces a JSON file containing the generic CSG

geometry description instead of a finite element mesh (see [14] for a detailed description of this

JSON output). Internally, the CSGOnlyAction traverses the mesh generator dependency tree and

calls generateCSG() for each mesh generator in the input. By leveraging the existing MOOSE

mesh generator infrastructure and data-driven workflow, the framework can bypass finite element

mesh construction entirely when only a CSG model is needed, improving efficiency while preserving

consistency with the user-specified geometry.

4.4 CSGBase Components

The framework for CSG-based geometry definition is implemented through the new CSGBase class,

which is the main class developers will interact with when implementing the generateCSG() method

for any mesh generator. The generateCSG() method returns a unique pointer to the CSGBase object

that was created or modified for a particular mesh generator using the CSGBase class methods. This

class acts as a container and driver for all methods necessary for creating a CSG representation such

as generating surfaces, cells, universes, lattices, and engineering units of the mesh generator under

consideration. Lattices, engineering units, and transformations are new components implemented

in FY26 and are described in detail here. The other components and the details of initializing a

 33

CSGBase object, which were implemented in FY25, are briefly described here for completeness, but

detailed documentation for these components can be found in [2] and in the MOOSE documentation

[15].

4.4.1 Surfaces

The CSGSurface class represents an analytical surface used to define the spatial extent of the region

of a CSGCell (Section 4.4.3). The CSGBase class natively supports the definition of basic surface

types such as planes, spheres, and axis-aligned cylinders. In addition, it provides the ability to

define custom surfaces based on user needs (e.g. a torus for fusion applications), so that applications

may create a specific surface type in their MeshGenerator that is not currently supported within

MOOSE’s main CSGBase framework. CSGSurface objects are defined by a name and map of surface

coefficients to their values that define the surface equation (e.g. the values for coefficients A, B, C,

and D in the equation of the plane Ax + By + Cz = D).

4.4.2   Regions

The CSGRegion class represents a spatial domain or region defined by Boolean operations applied

to surfaces and other regions. These Boolean operations include half-spaces, unions, intersections,

and complements. The resulting region is used to defined the domain of a CSGCell (Section 4.4.3).

CSGRegion objects are stored internally using postfix notation (also known as Reverse Polish

Notation) [16], which is more useful for converting to downstream MC codes as this representation

provides an unambiguous precedence ruleset for Boolean operators. During the CSG output step that

produces a JSON file, the region of each cell is written in both postfix and infix (human-readable)

format. An example of both these representations can be seen in Listing 4.8 where planes are used

to construct a triangular prism region.

 34

4.4.3   Cells

The CSGCell class represents a cell defined by a region and a spatial fill property. Four types of cell

fills are supported: void, material, universe (Section 4.4.4), and lattice (Section 4.4.5). Currently,

material fills are not connected to any MOOSE material definitions, and are represented as a

placeholder name (see Section 4.7). It is up to the end user to specify the compositions that

correspond to each material name, which is a standard separation of input specifications for most

MC codes.

4.4.4 Universes

The CSGUniverse class represents a universe which is a collection of cells that can be used as a fill

for other cells or used in a repeated manner, such as in a lattice (Section 4.4.5). Each CSGBase

instance contains a root universe, which represents the topmost-level of the hierarchy of connected

cells and universes in the CSG representation.

4.4.5 Lattices

The CSGLattice class represents a lattice, or a structured arrangement of repeated universes used

to model regular patterns in geometry. As noted in Section 4.4.3, a CSGLattice is meant to be used

as a fill for a CSGCell. In CSGBase, 2D Cartesian and x-oriented hexagonal lattices are supported

natively through the CSGCartesianLattice and CSGHexagonalLattice classes, respectively, while

custom 2D lattice types can also be defined through the same framework (similar to custom

CSGSurface types). A y-oriented hexagonal lattice can also be constructed by defining an x-oriented

CSGHexagonalLattice and then applying a rotation transformation (see Section 4.4.7).

 Because the universes assigned to individual lattice elements do not always occupy the full spatial

extent of each element, a CSGLattice object also stores an “outer” fill to define the remaining space.

This outer region can be represented as void, a material placeholder name, or another CSGUniverse,

 35

ensuring that the full lattice domain is completely and consistently defined.

 The framework currently supports only direct creation of 2D lattices. However, a “3D” lattice-

like structure can still be represented by filling multiple 3D cells with 2D lattices and arranging

those cells in layers.

 The framework uses a row-column (i, j) indexing scheme for both CSGCartesianLattice

and CSGHexagonalLattice types, where the 0th row is the top-most row and the 0th column is

the left-most position. This convention is consistent with how users define lattice layouts in the

MOOSE mesh generator input. For hexagonal lattices, however, many MC codes instead use a

ring-based indexing scheme when accessing or defining lattice elements. To support downstream

code interoperability, the CSGHexagonalLattice class provides methods for converting between the

row-column and ring-based indexing conventions.

4.4.6 Engineering Units

So far, the underlying geometry representation described within the MOOSE CSG framework

assumes that the geometry is comprised of rudimentary CSG components such as surfaces, cells, and

universes. However, some MC codes, such as Shift via the Titan interface, simplify the geometry

representation by describing geometric constructs in terms of “engineering units” that derive from

reactor design terminology (e.g., pin, pebble, etc.) instead of standard CSG terminology [10].

 An engineering unit, defined through the abstract CSGEngUnit class, is a user-friendly, domain-

specific CSG object that can be used in place of basic CSG components within a CSGBase instance.

Each engineering unit is defined to mimic the behavior of a CSGSurface, CSGCell, or CSGUniverse

class, such that it can be used in a “plug-and-play” manner for a CSGBase instance, but the unit only

stores the necessary data to describe the engineering-scale domain, and not the underlying surfaces,

cells, and universes. Its primary purpose is to serve as a shortcut for a collection of underlying CSG

components, allowing a geometry to be defined through a small set of intuitive, human-readable

attributes, rather than by specifying each component by hand. These unit definitions are designed

 36

to connect to Titan’s interface with a 1-to-1 correspondence between a MOOSE CSGEngUnit and a

Titan unit.

 In order to facilitate conversion between an engineering unit-based representation and a CSG-

based one, each custom engineering unit class implements the expandUnit() method. This method

rebuild the engineering unit using basic CSG components (CSGSurfaces, CSGCells, CSGUniverses,

and CSGLattices) from the engineering unit’s attributes. When an engineering unit is expanded

via this method, it directly replaces itself in the CSGBase instance with the collection of basic

components that were built during expansion. This way, the end user can specify whether they are

interested in an engineering unit-based or CSG-based representation, and the CSGBase framework

will store the necessary engineering units or CSG objects accordingly.

 A specific example of one of these engineering units is the CSGNPolygonUnit which defines a

regular N -sided polygon that behaves as a surface-type of engineering unit. The polygon is 2D,

or infinite along the z-axis, and is assumed to have the right-most edge parallel to the y-axis, as

shown in Fig. 4.1. This orientation can be adjusted by applying a CSG rotation transformation

(Section 4.4.7).

4.4.7 Transformations

Three types of object transformations are supported in the CSGBase class: rotation, translation,

and scaling. These transformations can be applied to any CSGBase object (CSGSurface, CSGRegion,

CSGCell, CSGUniverse, CSGLattice, and CSGEngUnit). When a transformation is applied, existing

attributes for the object (such as surface coefficients) are not directly altered. Instead, the series of

transformations applied to the object are stored directly on the object. It is up to the downstream

connected code to process the transformations in a way that makes most sense for that code.

4.5 Implementation of generateCSG() methods for RGMB Mesh Generators

As mentioned in Section 4.3, each mesh generator has an optional generateCSG() method, which

if defined, tells MOOSE how to define the CSG representation of the mesh generator. If all mesh

generators within the MOOSE input file have a corresponding generateCSG() method defined, then

calling --csg-only on the input file will generate the CSG-equivalent representation of the output

finite element mesh that is described by the input file. In FY25, generateCSG() was implemented

for PinMeshGenerator within the Reactor Geometry Mesh Builder (RGMB) mesh generators [2], a

 In this format, it is difficult to retrieve engineering-level parameters such as the pin pitch, ring

 radii, etc., which are quantities that MC codes like Titan use to define the underlying geometry.

 In order to support an alternative geometry representation that is more tailored to Titan-based

 44

 geometry representations, the generateCSG() methods in RGMB were updated to handle engineering

 unit-based representations as well.

 Two engineering units were added to the MOOSE Reactor module to facilitate this process:

 DuctedPinEngUnit and PinUniverseEngUnit. DuctedPinEngUnit is invoked by PinMeshGenerator

 in its generateCSG() method, and it represents a 2D/infinite or 3D cylindrical pin structures sur-

 rounded by one or more hexagonal or square ducts. This is done by specifying the apothems (center-

 to-flat distances) of the ducts, the ring radii, the radial and axial region names of each pin region,

 and the geometry type (“Hex” or “Square”) of the pin cell. A PinUniverseEngUnit is constructed

 in the expandUnit() implementation of DuctedPinEngUnit and represents a 2D/infinite cylindrical

 pin structure, i.e. a series of concentric infinite cylinders surrounded by a background region with no

 ducts. PinUniverseEngUnit is defined by specifying the ring radii and radial region names of each

 incremental region. Axially, DuctedPinEngUnit defines a separate PinUniverseEngUnit instance

 for each axial layer in order to separately assign region ID’s to the cylindrical and background

 regions.

 At the AssemblyMeshGenerator level, the generateCSG() method is responsible for taking

 the CSGBase objects created by the input PinMeshGenerator instances, and placing them into their

 appropriate lattice positions within the assembly lattice. The outer region of the lattice is set to a

 material fill that matches the background region ID’s of the mesh for each axial layer, and then

 this lattice is surrounded by a series of CSGNPolygonUnit objects to represent the assembly ducts

 and assembly outer boundary. For hexagonal lattices, the pin-level CSGBase objects need to be

 rotated by 90 degrees before placing them into the assembly lattice, and this operation is represented

 within the CSGBase object of the AssemblyMeshGenerator as a CSG transformation (described in

 46

Section 4.4.7).

 Finally, at the CoreMeshGenerator level, the lattice generation process is repeated one more

time, this time placing the CSGBase objects of the input AssemblyMeshGenerator instances into

their correct positions within the core lattice. The key distinction with the core lattice is that

dummy positions can also be specified, which is done by filling the lattice position with a CSG

universe that contains only a void cell. If a core peripheral ring is present, the core lattice is

surrounded by an additional cylinder that is filled with the region ID representing the peripheral

ring region. When a core peripheral ring is not present, the core lattice is still surrounded by a

cylinder that is filled with a void region. This cylinder is defined to be large enough to contain

the entire core lattice, and this additional step is carried out to define the outer boundary of the

entire CSG domain in a simpler manner, instead of as a jagged or non-uniform boundary that would

result for a hexagonal lattice or a lattice with dummy assembly positions. Representing the outer

boundary this way simplifies how boundary conditions are applied in the downstream MC input.

4.6 Demonstration and Verification for ABTR geometry

As the generateCSG() method was defined for each mesh generator in RGMB, an existing RGMB-

based mesh input file for the full-core ABTR [13] can now be used to generate the equivalent

CSG representation for the output mesh. The mesh representation is an extruded hexagonal core

with a mixture of heterogeneous (pin-resolved) and homogeneous subassembly regions. Running

--csg-only on the MOOSE geometry input file produces a JSON output file that contains the CSG

definition of the problem. A custom script was then used to convert the JSON output file of this

geometry to OpenMC syntax [17] and plotted within OpenMC to confirm correctness of the CSG

representation. These plots are shown in Figure 4.2. It should be noted here that the conversion

step to OpenMC did not use an engineering unit-based approach and instead relied on the expanded

representation that uses the basic CSG surfaces, cells, universes, and lattices only.

4.7 Future Work and Stakeholder Engagement

The full implementation of CSG support in MOOSE will require additional effort and an ex-

panded group of developers. The implementation plan originally developed in FY24 and the

status of each task is shown in Table 4.2 [7]. The addition of lattices, engineering units, and

transformations to the CSGBase framework (Sections 4.4.5 to 4.4.7) completes Task 1. The im-

plementation of the generateCSG() method for the PinMeshGenerator, AssemblyMeshGenerator,

and CoreMeshGenerator using engineering units completes Task 2 for the RGMB mesh generators

(Section 4.5). In Section 4.6, the prototyping goal defined in Task 6 was completed with the

generation of the generic CSG definition for the ABTR model.

 The next phase of development for this workflow is focused on the connection to specific MC

codes (Task 4 in Table 4.2). This effort will be led by the Reactor Physics Technical Area in

NEAMS, with the Multiphysics Technical Area providing advisory support and revising the CSGBase

framework as necessary.

 One component that is still missing from the MOOSE CSG framework that will be necessary

for the complete connection to physics codes is handling of material compositions and other physical

domain characteristics. As noted in Section 4.4.3, the material fill type for a CSGCell is simply a

placeholder string ID, and does not directly connect to a material composition, whether that is

defined in MOOSE or through the MC code. The initial work in FY27 will focus on defining the

material definition workflow in order to support the complete connection between MOOSE and

Titan. Related, analysts are often interested in defining or computing additional domains in phase

space, such as defining depletion zones or tally regions to support cross section generation. Shift, the

underlying MC code for the Titan interface, has support for “overlay” geometries, which is a second

CSG model that represents the same physical space but has different regions specifically designed

for the definition of these additional domains like tally regions [11, 18]. The CSGBase framework

 50

will need to add support for defining additional phase space domains for these overlay geometries,

as well as determining any additional components that need to be added to the framework. Close

engagement with relevant MOOSE, Titan/Shift, and Griffin developers will be necessary to determine

exactly where and how this connection to material definitions and other domain characteristics

should be defined.

 Additional work in FY27 will be to make significant progress in defining the generateCSG()

methods for the remaining mesh generators in MOOSE’s Reactor Module (Task 2 and 3 in Table 4.2).

The order in which these mesh generators will be supported will depend on user and downstream

developer needs. Therefore, discussions with expected users will be necessary to understand which

mesh generators to prioritize first.

5 Conclusions & Future Work

The meshing work delivered in FY26 extended the MOOSE Reactor Module along the three

directions identified at the start of the year, and stakeholder engagement over the same period

pointed to where that work should go next.

 Fast reactor core restraint systems had previously required either a hand-built mesh or a geome-

try idealization; they can now be meshed natively through GapLineMeshGenerator and the conformal

gap construction of Section 2.1. Two-dimensional triangulation gained boundary layer support, both

inside XYDelaunayGenerator and as the standalone XYTriangleBoundaryLayerGenerator, so thin

coatings and near-wall regions can be resolved without giving up unstructured meshing. Surface-only

hole conversion made three-dimensional tetrahedralization cheaper, and quadratic element support

now runs the whole path from surface definition to tetrahedral output. FY27 carries this forward

on two fronts (Section 2.6): a Fuels Technical Area task that extends XYZDelaunayGenerator and

the BISON generators inherited from it toward biased mesh density, mesh metadata, and defect

and design-variation studies for TRISO fuel and compact meshing, and a broader effort to expose

the geometric metadata a MOOSE mesh already carries so that multiphysics workflows, including

 The control algorithm task delivered the infrastructure for time-dependent reactivity search in

MOOSE. On the Griffin point kinetics model of Section 3, the inverse solve formulation recovers the

reactivity history needed to follow a prescribed power schedule, and its structure does not depend

on the physics application it drives, so the next phase of the task can attach the same control loop

to a full multiphysics reactor model. That next phase, year 2 of the three-year task, turns to a

single-mechanism control strategy that coordinates several components of one control mechanism,

such as a set of control drums, to match a power schedule without any operating parameter leaving

its regulated range, and it is a step toward a longer-term goal of automated transitions between

operating modes and control logic general enough to extend beyond a single reactor type.

 The implementation of a robust CSG framework (Section 4) and automated conversion from

mesh-based inputs to MC geometry definitions significantly streamlines multiphysics workflows and

reduces the potential for user error. Specifically this FY, the addition of lattices, transformations, and

engineering units complete the necessary framework components needed to support the connection

to other MC codes like Shift. The CSG generation methods were also fully implemented for the

RGMB mesh generators in the Reactor Module. Demonstrations and verification exercises on

the ABTR reactor confirm that these new capabilities produce consistent and accurate geometry

representations across different codes and analysis tools. Looking ahead, continued development will

focus on expanding CSG support to additional mesh generators, integrating material composition

workflows, and focus on the connection to the MC code Shift via the Titan interface. Ongoing

stakeholder engagement will guide prioritization and ensure that future enhancements address the

evolving needs of the reactor physics and multiphysics simulation communities.

 Collectively, these improvements position MOOSE as a leading platform for integrated, high-

fidelity nuclear reactor analysis.
