Delirium modifies the prognostic performance of SAPS-II
Authors: Ignazio De Trizio, Maria Angeliki Komninou, Reto Schüpbach, Giovanna Brandi, Jan Bartussek
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

The Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS)-II is widely used for mortality prediction and ICU benchmarking [1]. However, its calibration may deteriorate when case mix and clinical practice differ from the development setting [2, 3 ]. Delirium and related assessment states are common in critically ill patients and may identify clinically distinct populations in whom the relation between baseline severity and mortality differs [4]. We, therefore, evaluated whether SAPS-II calibration varied across delirium-related clinical states in a large single-center ICU cohort.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of 10,378 adult ICU patients treated between February 1, 2019 and December 31, 2024. Mortality was predicted using the original SAPS-II equation and the routinely recorded SAPS-II value based on the first 24 h of ICU treatment. The outcome was all-cause mortality during the index hospitalization. Delirium was regularly assessed using the Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist (ICDSC). The primary analysis used a post-baseline stratification based on the maximum ICDSC value recorded during the entire ICU stay, complemented by ICD-10 F05 coding: (1) ICDSC unavailable (no valid maximum ICDSC score available), (2) no delirium (MaxICDSC = 0 with complete assessment), (3) subsyndromal delirium (MaxICDSC 1–3 with complete assessment), (4) clinical delirium (MaxICDSC > 3 without F05), and (5) coded delirium (MaxICDSC > 3 with F05). Patients with incomplete assessment and MaxICDSC 0–3 were excluded from the primary analysis and separated into MaxICDSC 0 and MaxICDSC 1–3 groups in a sensitivity analysis.

Calibration was summarized using expected/observed (E/O) mortality ratios and LOWESS-smoothed observed-minus-predicted mortality curves. Statistical effect modification was assessed using maximum-likelihood logistic regression with logit-transformed SAPS-II-predicted mortality, delirium-related clinical states, and their interaction; ICDSC unavailable was the reference group. The global interaction was tested by likelihood-ratio comparison. Prespecified robustness analyses used ICDSC classifications restricted to 24, 48, and 72 h, and landmark restrictions requiring an ICU stay of at least 3 days or survival for at least 3 days. Additional calibration and discrimination metrics, risk-band estimates, group characteristics, assessment-opportunity analyses, and an admission-category-adjusted model are reported in the Supplementary Material.

Results

Overall, SAPS-II overpredicted mortality (E/O = 2.55; mean predicted risk 37.2% versus observed mortality 14.6%). Miscalibration differed across delirium-related states and predicted-risk strata (Fig. 1).

At predicted risk < 0.25, mortality was lowest in patients without delirium (3/388, 0.8%), followed by subsyndromal and coded delirium (2.7% each), with marked overprediction (E/O = 13.65, 4.21, and 5.10, respectively). Clinical delirium had higher observed mortality (5.8%) and less overprediction (E/O = 2.14). ICDSC-unavailable patients remained close to calibration in this band (E/O = 0.94).

Among patients with predicted risk ≥ 0.75, the point estimate approached calibration in patients without delirium (13 deaths among 18 patients; observed mortality 72.2%; E/O = 1.21, 95% CI 0.71–2.28), whereas persistent overprediction was observed in subsyndromal, clinical, and coded delirium (E/O 2.71–4.82). Because the high-risk no-delirium stratum was small and phenotype assignment depended on subsequent assessment and clinical course, this contrast should not be interpreted as a protective effect of delirium. The interaction between phenotype and logit-transformed predicted risk was significant (likelihood-ratio χ2 = 62.9, 4 df, p = 7.1 × 10⁻13). Compared with the ICDSC-unavailable reference group, the risk–mortality association was flatter in subsyndromal, clinical, and coded delirium and steeper in patients without delirium (Table 1).

Calibration slopes ranged from 1.34 in patients without delirium to 0.38 in coded delirium, while AUC ranged from 0.90 to 0.68, respectively (Supplementary Table S1). The interaction remained detectable with 24-, 48-, and 72-h definitions and in both 3-day landmark analyses; adjustment for admission category did not materially alter it (χ2 = 69.9, 4 df, p = 2.4 × 10⁻14).

Separating the 2,028 incompletely assessed patients into MaxICDSC 0 (n = 850) and MaxICDSC 1–3 (n = 1178) retained strong evidence of interaction (χ2 = 68.3, 6 df, p = 9.0 × 10⁻13). Incomplete ICDSC 0 was not equivalent to complete no-delirium assessment: mortality was 43.6%, E/O = 1.21, and calibration slope = 0.47, whereas mortality was 10.8% in the group ICDSC 1–3 (E/O = 3.51 and slope = 0.48), (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Tables S9–S11) (Table 2).

Discussion and conclusions

SAPS-II calibration was not uniform across groups defined by delirium assessment and documentation. This is relevant because SAPS-II-derived mortality ratios are widely used for ICU benchmarking, quality assessment, and risk interpretation. If the distribution of delirium-related states differs between units or over time, apparent differences in standardized mortality may partly reflect subgroup composition and assessment practice rather than true differences in quality of care. The same limitation applies at the individual level: a SAPS-II risk estimate may have substantially different meaning depending on whether a patient remains assessable, develops delirium, or never receives a valid ICDSC assessment. The observed differences indicate that the relationship between SAPS-II-predicted and observed mortality varies materially across delirium-related states. This constitutes statistical effect modification and differential subgroup calibration but does not by itself establish a causal effect of delirium. Because the primary delirium-related clinical states were determined using information collected after the first 24 h, the resulting patterns combine baseline severity with subsequent survival, assessability, sedation, documentation, and case mix.

The high-risk pattern is particularly susceptible to selection. Patients must survive and be sufficiently assessable to accumulate repeated ICDSC observations, whereas early deterioration, deep sedation, severe neurological impairment, or treatment limitations may preclude valid assessment. Differences in surgical, neurological, and other admission categories may also contribute. The persistence of the interaction after adjustment for admission category and across time-restricted and landmark analyses supports the robustness of the descriptive finding but does not establish a causal mechanism. The ICDSC-unavailable group should therefore be interpreted as a group defined by assessment availability and documentation patterns rather than as a biological control group. These patients had high severity of illness, 93.7% received mechanical ventilation, with a median SAPS-II of 53, mortality of 51.5%. The proportion with unavailable ICDSC decreased from 13.0% among ICU stays of 24– < 48 h to 2.6% among stays of at least 7 days, supporting an important assessment-opportunity component. A similar pattern was observed in patients with an incomplete maximum ICDSC score of 0, who had high mortality and comparatively good global SAPS-II calibration. This group likely included patients with very limited assessment opportunity, in whom an observed score of 0 cannot be considered equivalent to a complete no-delirium assessment.

Our findings have two practical implications. First, SAPS-II-based benchmarking should not assume that calibration is transportable across ICUs with different delirium prevalence, sedation practices, patient case mix, or documentation intensity. Second, subgroup-specific calibration checks may be necessary before SAPS-II-derived mortality ratios are used for comparisons between units or for local performance monitoring.

Limitations include the retrospective single-center design, exclusions related to objections, short ICU stays, and data quality, post-baseline subgroup assignment, incomplete assessments, and potential residual confounding. The results are therefore hypothesis-generating. They suggest that SAPS-II-derived mortality ratios may be sensitive to both the distribution and the ascertainment of delirium-related states. External validation in independent cohorts with prospectively defined assessment windows is required before these findings can inform adjustments to benchmarking practice.
