The impact of functional architecture dynamics in bipolar disorder staging
Authors: Andrea Caporali, Francesca Martella, Salvatore Saluzzi, Nicolaja Girone, Beatrice Feliciani, Clara Cavallotto, Mauro Pettorruso, Simonetta Gerevini, Emi Bondi, Cristiana Perrotta, Mauro Gianni Perrucci, Giovanni Martinotti, Bernardo Dell'Osso, Annabella Di Giorgio, Claudio D’Addario, Francesco de Pasquale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBipolar disorder (BD) follows a neuro-progressive trajectory, yet current clinical staging models lack robust biological markers. Based on recent evidence, we hypothesized that BD stages are associated with alterations in the functional architecture of the Triple Network Model. We analyzed resting-state fMRI data from 123 individuals spanning five clinically defined BD stages. Using a hierarchical approach, we investigated how functional connectivity features characterize these stages, transitioning from coarse- to fine-grained analyses in both clinical classification (binary early-versus-late grouping to five-stage discrimination) and connectivity metrics (mean and standard deviation of connectivity to topological dynamic features). First, low-level functional connectivity features, combined with gene expression data, discriminated early- from late-stage illness, reaching higher performance than unimodal approaches. Next, evaluating the full staging spectrum revealed a non-linear trajectory in whole-brain connection density, which was lower at each successive stage from 0 to 3 but unexpectedly higher at stage 4. Network topology analysis contextualized this finding, revealing a reorganization of functional hubs in the final stage. Specifically, three hubs within the Salience Network exhibited decreased centrality despite the overall increase in connection density. This shift corresponded to a loss of temporal synchronization between these hubs and default-mode hubs, an axis of communication mediated by the salience network that is known to be important in healthy subjects and disrupted in psychiatric disorders. Overall, our findings suggest that late-stage BD is characterized by a subtle but significant reorganization of brain architecture, accompanied by alterations in the dynamics of the salience network which loses its coordinating role.Keywords: Bipolar disorder staging, Resting-state fMRI, Connectivity dynamics, Functional hubs, Machine learning, Gene expression

Introduction

Bipolar disorder (BD) is characterized by recurrent mood episodes contributing to a progressive accumulation of cognitive burden (Oliva et al., 2025). Within this neuro-progressive framework (Kapczinski et al., 2014), clinical staging has been proposed as a promising tool to support prognosis and treatment planning (Berk, 2009). Crucially, staging models describe a continuum that begins before clinical onset, progressing from familial risk through prodromal symptoms to chronic illness. Recent studies validated these models in BD patients, showing that they may transition across stages and that illness progression is associated with episode burden and functional impairment (Cremaschi et al., 2024; de la Fuente-Tomas et al., 2020; Macellaro et al., 2023; van der Markt et al., 2019). Nevertheless, the application of these staging models remains largely retrospective and primarily based on clinical indicators (Berk et al., 2007; Dell'Osso et al., 2024; Duffy, 2014; Kapczinski et al., 2009; Kupka and Hillegers, 2012), lacking the standardization required for clinical practice.

The initial phase of this continuum is driven by the strong genetic basis of BD (Smoller and Finn, 2003). Identifying the relationship between genetic risk and BD is complex, since it frequently involves shared heritable vulnerabilities with other psychiatric disorders. However, it is well established that first-degree relatives have a seven- to ten-fold higher lifetime risk of developing BD compared to the general population (Rowland and Marwaha, 2018). Thus, considering these high-risk relatives provides a critical window into the preclinical phase of the staging trajectory, prior to symptomatic onset.

Identifying biological markers for such a trajectory is difficult (Grewal et al., 2023). Available markers are confined to structural phenotypes, such as ventricular enlargement and gray matter volume loss (Duarte et al., 2018; Strakowski et al., 2002), and inflammatory alterations, such as increased Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNFα) (Kauer-Sant'Anna et al., 2009; Stertz et al., 2013). In the context of functional neuroimaging, biomarkers of illness staging remain scarce (Favre et al., 2014; Macoveanu et al., 2024) and typically relate to separate phases of the BD spectrum, rather than addressing the complete staging. At its initial phase, functional alterations preceding clinical onset, and thus reflecting inherited vulnerability (Cattarinussi et al., 2024), have been reported in the default-mode network (DMN), cortico-limbic, fronto-thalamic-striatal, and fronto-occipital circuits (e.g., reduced prefrontal-limbic and fronto-striatal coupling). Further, in terms of connectivity dynamics, offspring of BD patients show an intermediate profile between patients and controls, characterized by a reduction in the dynamics of hippocampal-somatomotor network as opposed to an increase in the hippocampal-limbic one (Saccaro et al., 2025). At the final BD stages (well-established illness), most studies (Martella et al., 2025) report an altered communication at rest mainly centered on the DMN, frontoparietal (FPN), and salience (SAL) networks (Fortea et al., 2023; Syan et al., 2018; Yoon et al., 2021), collectively described as the Triple-Network Model (TNM) (Menon, 2011). Within TNM, where SAL regulates the switching between DMN (internally oriented) and FPN (externally oriented), four main findings have been linked to BD. First, a reduced connectivity within these resting-state networks (RSNs) was reported (Baker et al., 2014; Favre et al., 2014; Gong et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2019; Yoon et al., 2021). Second, altered interactions among them were observed (Fortea et al., 2023; Syan et al., 2018), including decreased FPN-SAL (Ellard et al., 2018; Yoon et al., 2021) and DMN-SAL coupling (Lopez-Larson et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2024), and reduced DMN-FPN anticorrelation (Favre et al., 2014; Yoon et al., 2021). Third, altered RSN dynamics were described, suggesting reduced network flexibility (Cattarinussi et al., 2023; Long et al., 2023). Within the DMN, decreased switching rates (Han et al., 2020) and reduced temporal variability were observed (Liang et al., 2020; Luo et al., 2021; Nguyen et al., 2017). In contrast, the FPN showed increased dynamic connectivity in both euthymic and depressed BD patients (Liu et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2022). In the SAL, variability increased in interactions with externally oriented systems while decreasing with internally oriented ones, such as the DMN (Pang et al., 2018). Fourth, BD also seems to affect the functional brain topology (Perry et al., 2019). Unlike schizophrenia, where widespread topological alterations are consistently reported (Doucet et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2017), topological changes in BD are more regionally confined: altered segregation or integration in specific functional subsystems involving DMN, sensorimotor, and mid-cingulate areas (Pan et al., 2024; Platania et al., 2023; Roberts et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2017). These findings seem to suggest that fMRI-based features represent promising candidate biomarkers for BD staging.

In parallel, molecular studies identified several potential biomarkers for BD (Martella et al., 2025), including DNA methylation (D'Addario et al., 2012; D'Addario et al., 2018; Fries et al., 2016; Ludwig and Dwivedi, 2016), microRNAs dysregulation (Ceylan et al., 2020; Martins et al., 2022; Rong et al., 2011; Tekin et al., 2022), and histone modifications (Abe et al., 2011; Gavin et al., 2009; Hobara et al., 2010). Based on these considerations, our experimental hypothesis is that the integration of these complementary domains characterizes the BD stages. To this aim, we developed a Machine Learning (ML) model integrating TNM and molecular features. First, we tested whether low-level functional connectivity features (specifically the amplitude and temporal variability of RSN connections) together with gene expression markers could discriminate early vs late BD phases. We then investigated the functional architecture underlying the full five stage model. We focused on dynamic network properties of the final stages (3 and 4) where we observed a major reorganization of functional hubs in stage 4. This corresponded to a loss of temporal synchronization between SAL and DMN, an axis of communication mediated by salience network that is known to be important in healthy subjects and disrupted in psychiatric disorders.

Materials and methods

This study, registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (Id: NCT07343739), is part of the BOARDING-PASS project, aimed at identifying biological and neuroimaging correlates of BD and developing predictive models for individualized staging. Its rationale, design, and overall analytic strategy were reported in a published study protocol (Girone et al., 2025). Within this framework, we investigated whether functional connectivity features could characterize the distinct BD stages. To achieve this, we organized our analyses hierarchically from coarser to finer resolutions for both clinical classification and connectivity features. Specifically, classification ranged from a binary early-vs-late BD distinction to a full five-stage model, while connectivity features progressed from simple mean and standard deviation metrics to more complex topological and dynamic properties. We first explored whether low-level features of dynamic connectivity could discriminate early from late BD stages and then classify all five stages individually. Moving beyond these classification tasks, we then characterized the functional architecture underlying each specific stage across the entire clinical spectrum. We compared connection density across stages. To interpret the non-linear trajectory observed in this metric, we shifted to a higher level of the analytical hierarchy by evaluating the underlying topological and dynamic hub properties.

Our analysis pipeline can be summarized as follows (see Fig. 1):

•Data acquisition and preprocessing: resting-state fMRI were used to compute dynamic connectivity matrices related to DMN, FPN, and SAL interactions; in parallel, blood samples were processed to isolate peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and quantify gene-expression levels for neurotrophic and inflammatory markers.

Participants

A total of 128 individuals were enrolled in the study, recruited from three psychiatric centers in Italy: ASST Fatebenefratelli-Sacco in Milan; ASST Papa Giovanni XXIII in Bergamo; and the ASL 2 Lanciano-Vasto-Chieti in Chieti (Girone et al., 2025). Inclusion criteria comprised age between 18 and 70 years and the ability to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria included major neurological or systemic medical conditions, intellectual disability, current or recent substance use disorder, and contraindications to MRI (e.g., metallic implants, claustrophobia, pregnancy). All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Milan, Area 1 (approval n. 0008265/2023). Following data acquisition, two participants were excluded from analysis because, although they did not meet any exclusion criteria, they were unable to complete the MRI protocol. In addition, three participants were excluded during MRI quality-check procedures. Therefore, the final sample comprised 123 subjects (M = 60, F = 63; Mean age: 44.9 ± 13.1 years). In addition to the patient sample, 29 healthy controls (HC) were acquired at the two MRI sites (M = 16, F = 13; Mean age: 40.0 ± 12.6 years). These participants were used to estimate scanner-specific effects and to inform the harmonization procedure, so that inter-site variability could be removed while preserving illness-related variance in the patient data (see Neuroimaging data).

Stage assignment, sociodemographic and clinical variables

Participants were clinically assigned to one of the five stages of the Kupka and Hillegers staging model (Kupka and Hillegers, 2012), which describes BD illness progression. Clinical staging was conducted by trained psychiatrists and psychologists who had previously received harmonized clinical training on the assessment procedures across recruiting centers. Ambiguous cases were discussed in consensus meetings with senior clinical investigators. Stage assignment was based exclusively on available clinical data, including lifetime history, course of illness, and current symptom profile. Importantly, at the time of the assignment, raters were blinded to all biomarker data. Although formal inter-rater reliability was not assessed, the model relies on objective criteria that limit rater disagreement compared with categorical diagnoses or symptom ratings. According to the Kupka and Hillegers framework, five stages (0-4) were defined, reflecting both clinical severity and functional impairment. Briefly, stage 0 denotes individuals at familial risk without symptoms; stage 1 includes prodromal or subthreshold presentations; stage 2 corresponds to the first full syndromic episode; stage 3 reflects recurrent mood episodes with inter-episode recovery; and stage 4 captures chronic illness, characterized by persistent symptoms or rapid cycling. The final sample had the highest representation in stage 3 (n = 58) followed by stage 1 (n = 19), stage 2 (n = 18), stage 4 (n = 16), and stage 0 (n = 12). Medication exposure varied across stages and is reported in Supplementary Information (Table S1). Stage 0 individuals were unmedicated, consistent with their at-risk status. From stage 1 onward, participants may have received medications, including lithium, antiepileptics, antipsychotics (first and second generation) and long-acting injectable antipsychotics, often in combination. We note that prior reviews (Hafeman et al., 2012) indicate that medications are a minimal confound for BD neuroimaging and, when effects occur, tend to normalize connectivity toward healthy values. This, together with the comparable medication exposure between stage 3 and stage 4 (non-significant difference), suggests that the pharmacological impact on our results is limited (see SI for more details).

In addition, socio-demographic and clinical measures were collected to characterize the sample and test for potential confounds across stages. All assessments were conducted before the first MRI scan by trained psychiatrists using standardized clinical interviews and psychometric instruments. Collected measures included demographic variables (age, sex, handedness, premorbid IQ, and educational status), illness-related variables (duration of illness, duration of untreated illness, and symptom severity), as well as lifetime psychiatric and medical comorbidities, substance- and alcohol-use screening, and smoking. Statistical analyses testing for group differences across the five clinical stages are reported in the SI (see Table S2). Briefly, no differences were found on demographic variables, comorbidities, substance or alcohol use, or smoking. Regarding illness-related variables, significant differences, as expected, were observed between the extremes of the staging spectrum, i.e. asymptomatic at-risk subjects vs chronic patients.

Molecular data

Peripheral gene expression markers were used as proxies of biological processes, reflecting the current transcriptional activity of selected inflammatory and neurotrophic genes. Peripheral venous blood samples were collected for gene expression analyses. Blood was drawn into two 5 ml vacutainer tubes containing sodium citrate. PBMCs were isolated by density-gradient centrifugation and stored at -80 °C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted from unsorted PBMCs, as widely adopted for blood-based biomarker studies (Alexovic et al., 2024; Goossens et al., 2021). All biological samples collected at the three recruiting sites were transferred under standardized conditions to the central laboratory in the University of Teramo for standardized processing and analysis. RNA purity was assessed by NanoDrop (A260/280). After reverse transcription, the relative abundance of mRNA species in PBMCs was assessed by real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction in duplicate, using GAPDH and β-actin as endogenous reference genes. Cycle threshold (CT) values > 33 were considered below the reliable detection limit. Relative expression was calculated by the comparative ΔΔCT method (2−ΔΔCT). No explicit batch-effect correction was applied. Samples were anonymized and coded prior to processing. The analysis focused on a panel of 9 candidate genes previously implicated in neuroplasticity, stress regulation, and immune-inflammatory response: brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), a neurotrophin whose peripheral expression has been established as a biomarker for psychiatric disorders (Cattaneo et al., 2016); a set of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, i.e., the interleukins (ILs) IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, and IL-8, TNFα, and interferon-gamma (IFNγ). These have been found consistently altered in peripheral blood across neuropsychiatric conditions (Gedek et al., 2025; Halstead et al., 2023). Finally, we included IL-19, a member of the IL-10 family with emerging evidence of involvement in neuroimmune dysregulation (Horiuchi et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2025).

Gene expression values were normalized and expressed as relative fold changes. These molecular features were subsequently matched to neuroimaging-derived measures and used as input variables in the ML analyses. Notably, due to technical issues during blood sample processing, gene expression data were available for 118 subjects out of the 123 included in the neuroimaging dataset. The remaining five cases were excluded from all ML analyses (complete-case analysis) to keep the different models directly comparable.

Neuroimaging data

Resting-state fMRI data were acquired using 3T MRI scanners at two participating sites, i.e. ASST Papa Giovanni XXIII in Bergamo and ITAB Center at the University G. D'Annunzio Chieti-Pescara. Functional images were obtained with a gradient-echo EPI sequence (36 axial slices; TE = 30 ms; TR = 2000 ms; voxel size = 3×3×4 mm3; matrix = 64×64; FOV = 192×192 mm2) acquired in interleaved order. Each resting-state session consisted of 400 volumes collected over approximately 13 minutes, during which participants were instructed to rest with eyes open and fixate on a crosshair.

Preprocessing was performed using a combination of FSL (Jenkinson et al., 2012) and custom MATLAB scripts, following standard pipelines for motion correction and denoising. Steps included: reorientation to standard space; motion correction (MCFLIRT); temporal high-pass filtering; spatial smoothing (FWHM = 5 mm); brain extraction and nonlinear registration to the MNI152 template (FSL-FNIRT). Motion outlier volumes were identified using a subject-specific threshold based on the boxplot criterion (defined as 75th percentile plus 1.5 times the interquartile range of each subject's framewise displacement distribution), as implemented in fsl_motion_outliers, and replaced via spline interpolation to preserve signal continuity for sliding-window connectivity estimation. Nuisance regression, global signal regression and white matter/CSF signal removal were not applied. The extent of residual motion across stages was assessed via ANOVA on framewise displacement distributions, showing no significant differences between clinical stages (see Supplementary Information, Fig. S1). The residual motion seems to have a limited impact on the observed stage-related connectivity differences.

Static and dynamic functional connectomes were then estimated by computing z-transformed Pearson correlation coefficients between all pairs of brain regions defined by a parcellation scheme comprising 184 nodes belonging to nine RSNs, already adopted in previous studies (Baldassarre et al., 2014; de Pasquale et al., 2021). Dynamic connectivity was calculated using a sliding-window approach. Window length was set to 30 TRs (60 s), falling within the range identified as adequate for capturing temporal fluctuations in resting-state fMRI (Preti et al., 2017). This choice is also consistent with common practice in dynamic connectivity studies of psychiatric disorders, where window lengths between 20 and 50 TRs are most frequently employed (Cattarinussi et al., 2023). Step size was set to 2 TRs (4s) as a compromise between maximizing temporal resolution and limiting computational cost (Shakil et al., 2016).

To account for scanner site effects in the multisite design, ComBat harmonization was applied to the dynamic functional connectivity data (Johnson et al., 2007; Yu et al., 2018). Following the recommendation of (Jodoin et al., 2025), ComBat parameters were estimated on healthy control static connectomes acquired from the two acquisition sites (Bergamo and Chieti). The estimated parameters were then applied to the dynamic connectivity matrices of all participants, patients and HC alike. The effect of harmonization on the group-averaged connectivity matrices is illustrated in SI, see Fig. S2. All subsequent analyses of this study were conducted on the harmonized connectivity matrices.

Machine learning procedure

To explore stage-related biological patterns, we implemented a supervised classification pipeline integrating resting-state fMRI connectivity and peripheral gene expression data. Functional features were derived from dynamic connectivity matrices, considering the RSNs of the TNM (DMN, FPN and SAL) previously introduced. Mean and standard deviation (Std) over time of within- and between-network connectivity were extracted as neurofunctional features. For each subject, these 12 features, together with the corresponding nine molecular ones, were entered into the ML classification procedure. To keep the three models directly comparable (fMRI, gene expression, and multimodal), all classification analyses were restricted to the 118 participants with complete data.

Classification was addressed at two levels of granularity. First, participants were grouped into early (stages 0-2) and late (stages 3-4) illness stages according to the Kupka and Hillegers staging model. This grouping reflected the clinically relevant distinction from early or episodic phases to chronic illness trajectories explored in previous studies (Kauer-Sant'Anna et al., 2009), while also ensuring a larger sample size per class for ML training. Classification was performed using a Support Vector Machine (SVM) with a polynomial kernel (degree = 2). This choice was motivated by previous literature applying it to fMRI- and genetic-based classification (Ahmadi et al., 2020; Cox and Savoy, 2003; Yang et al., 2022). All data were standardized prior to training, and the kernel scale parameter was estimated heuristically. The same pipeline was then applied to discriminate all five stages individually.

To identify the optimal subset of features, we implemented an exhaustive feature selection procedure within a Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) framework (see Fig. 1B). At each iteration, the model was trained on N – 1 participants and tested on the remaining one, iterating across all possible feature combinations of increasing size. This procedure ensured that every configuration of features was evaluated. To mitigate class imbalance, training observations were weighted. Each weight consisted of the inverse of their class frequency in each class and fold, so that the two classes contributed equally to the loss. No data resampling was applied, and the held-out participant never entered weight estimation. For each model, we computed four performance metrics: (i) accuracy (ratio of correct predictions to total observations); (ii) sensitivity (true positive rate); (iii) precision (positive predictive value); (iv) F1-score (the harmonic mean of precision and sensitivity). To account for class imbalance, sensitivity, precision, and F1-score were macro-averaged, i.e., computed independently for each class and then averaged with equal weight, in both the binary and the five-stage analyses. The model achieving the highest macro-averaged F1-score was selected as the optimal classifier.

To evaluate the statistical significance of the optimal model performance, we applied a permutation test (1000 permutations), as in (Ojala and Garriga, 2010): at each iteration, stage labels were randomly permuted across all participants and the entire LOOCV pipeline was repeated on the permuted labels. The observed performance (accuracy and F1-score) was compared against this null distribution, and the p-value was computed as p=C+1M+1, where C is the number of permutations producing a statistic greater than or equal to the observed one and M the number of permutations. With M = 1000, the smallest achievable p-value is 1/1001 ≈ 0.001; permutation p-values reaching this bound therefore indicate that the observed statistic exceeded the entire null distribution. Moreover, we quantified confidence intervals (CI) by resampling with replacement participants (2000 resamples) from the pooled cross-validated predictions. Accuracy, macro-F1, sensitivity, and specificity were recomputed at each resample to derive 95% percentile confidence intervals. To verify that performance was not driven by the class imbalance, we additionally compared the observed accuracy against the No-Information Rate, i.e. the accuracy attainable by always predicting the majority class, using a one-sided binomial test (Kuhn, 2008). The macro-F1-score was likewise compared to that of a majority-class predictor. We also compared performance between our three optimal models (trained on fMRI, gene expression, and combined features) on the same 118 complete cases using McNemar's exact test on their paired classifications.

Further, discrimination between classes was characterized through ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) and precision-recall curves based on the SVM decision scores for the late-stage class. Since the decision threshold was not optimized, the operating point of the reported confusion matrices corresponds to the default SVM decision boundary. Finally, we assessed the robustness of the classifier across acquisition sites. To this aim, predictions were stratified by scanner (gene-expression measures were processed at a single central laboratory), and performance metrics were computed separately for each site. The differences in misclassification rate between scanners were tested with Fisher's exact test. Macro-averaged performance metrics, confidence intervals and results of the permutation test are reported here, while the remaining analyses are reported in the SI.

Topological analysis

To characterize changes in the integrative architecture of the functional connectome across stages, we computed betweenness centrality (BC) and overall connection density on individual binarized static connectivity matrices (Rubinov and Sporns, 2010; Zuo et al., 2012). As in previous studies (de Pasquale et al., 2021), each subject’s weighted connectivity matrix was first transformed into a binary graph using a percolation-based thresholding procedure. This method identifies, for each subject, the highest connection threshold that preserves full graph connectedness (i.e., one single connected component). The resulting adjacency matrices thus represent the most informative backbone of the network while ensuring topological comparability across individuals.

To quantify global changes in network connectivity across stages, we computed connection density for each subject, defined as the proportion of existing edges relative to all possible edges (Rubinov and Sporns, 2010). HC were included as a reference. Differences in connection density between HC and each stage were assessed using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, given the non-normal distribution of residuals. We applied Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) to correct for multiple comparisons. Moreover, BC was computed using the Brain Connectivity Toolbox (Rubinov and Sporns, 2010). BC quantifies the extent to which a node lies on the shortest paths between all other node pairs, thus indexing its centrality in routing global communication through the brain. Nodes with high BC act as integrative hubs, facilitating communication across distinct network communities, whereas nodes with low BC mainly participate in local processing. To control for inter-individual variability in network density, BC values were normalized within each subject by dividing by the sum of all nodal BCs. Normalized BC values were then averaged within each clinical stage. Finally, we ranked nodes by their mean BC at each stage and computed the rank difference between stage 3 and stage 4 to identify hubs showing the strongest relative decrease in centrality. Nodes whose standardized rank decrease exceeded 2 standard deviations were considered to show a significant loss in centrality across stages. This choice corresponds to a defined statistical hypothesis: since the rank-change distribution was a standard Gaussian, a 2-standard-deviation cutoff isolates the tail of the distribution (one-tailed probability P ≈ 2.3%). Nodes below this threshold can therefore be regarded as showing a decrease significantly larger than the rest of the set (see SI for a detailed discussion). We also verified the robustness of the ranking procedure by repeating the analysis with a subject-by-subject ranking approach. We obtained that the key nodes of interest were identified by both ranking methods, see SI for further details.

Dynamic connectivity clustering

To examine coordinated changes in the connectivity profiles of the identified hubs, for each of them we computed the temporal dynamics of their connections with TNM nodes, i.e. DMN, FPN, and SAL. To identify patterns of connectivity co-fluctuations, we computed a correlation matrix from these dynamic connections. This step was computed at the subject level, and the resulting matrices were then averaged within each stage to obtain a stage-specific representation of the hub dynamics. To characterize the structure of these patterns, we applied hierarchical clustering (average linkage). Average linkage was preferred to single and complete linkage since it is more robust to residual noise, as reported in the context of resting-state fMRI clustering, see for example (Wang and Li, 2013; Wang et al., 2016). In this analysis, each dendrogram leaf represents one connection of the hub, and the linkage height (y-axis) reflects the dissimilarity between connection profiles. The dissimilarity was computed using Euclidean distance, since Hamming or Jaccard distance are not applicable, as the input is a continuous correlation matrix rather than binary data. Correlation-based distances were not preferred because they would discard amplitude information, which in this analysis is of functional interest. The resulting dendrograms therefore illustrate, for each stage, how the hub connections cluster according to the similarity of their temporal dynamics.

To estimate the optimal number of clusters, we maximized the inconsistency coefficient, as in (Wang and Li, 2013). This identifies natural cut-off points by comparing the height of a specific link to the average heights of other links in the same level of the hierarchy. A high inconsistency coefficient indicates a natural cut level, where two relatively dissimilar clusters are being merged. We found that three clusters (k=3) optimized the mean inconsistency coefficient at stage 3. Therefore, three clusters were extracted from each dendrogram and the network composition of each cluster (i.e., number of DMN, FPN, and SAL nodes) was quantified. To check whether the choice of three clusters was robust to the sample-size imbalance between stage 3 (n = 58) and stage 4 (n = 16), we repeatedly subsampled stage 3 to the size of stage 4 (1000 resamples) and recomputed the inconsistency curve. This confirmed that the k = 3 solution is stable under resampling (see SI). To assess whether the distribution of cluster-network memberships differed between stage 3 and stage 4, we constructed, for each hub, a contingency table summarizing the number of DMN, FPN, and SAL connections within each cluster at each stage. Results

Classifying early vs late BD stages

To study the impact of the TNM architecture on BD staging, we initially considered two broad BD groups: early (combining stages 0, 1, and 2 subjects) and late (stages 3 and 4 patients). At this phase, we focused on low-level features of within- and between-network interactions, namely their mean and standard deviation. This allowed us to capture both the amplitude and the temporal variability of functional interactions. These features were considered alone and combined with molecular data (see Materials and Methods) to develop ML models for stage classification. The analysis pipeline is reported in Fig. 1. Of note, fMRI data were harmonized through the ComBat approach, see Materials and Methods and Fig. S2.

First, we tested a unimodal ML driven by fMRI features. We obtained an optimal model consisting of four features: the mean SAL connectivity, mean FPN-SAL connectivity, the standard deviation within SAL, and the standard deviation between DMN and SAL. This model achieved an F1-score of 65.9% (95% CI = [56.8; 74.4]) and an accuracy of 66.1% (95% CI = [57.6; 74.6]), both statistically significant (permutation test, p = 0.004 for both metrics) and larger than the majority-class baseline (macro-F1 of a majority-class predictor = 0.38), see Fig. 2A. These results are encouraging, suggesting that a low-dimensional model (four features) encompassing both amplitude and dynamics of connections within TNM can discriminate early vs late BD stages. Next, we considered gene expression data. To assess this contribution in our dataset, we initially tested a model driven exclusively by molecular features. We obtained a performance comparable to the fMRI-based model (70.9% F1-score, 95% CI = [62.7; 79.3] and 72.0% accuracy, 95% CI = [63.6; 80.5]; see Fig. 2B), with both metrics statistically significant (permutation test, p = 0.001). The optimal gene expression-based model comprised BDNF, IL-19, IL-2, IL-4, and IL-8 mRNA levels.

Finally, we tested an integrated ML model combining both fMRI and gene expression features. This multimodal approach reached the highest F1-score (76.6%, 95% CI = [68.2; 84.0]) and accuracy (77.1%, 95% CI = [69.5; 84.7]), both statistically significant (permutation test, p = 0.001 for both metrics) and above the majority-class baseline (majority-class macro-F1 = 0.38), see Fig. 2C. Sensitivity increased to 77.4%, and precision reached 76.5%. Moreover, despite being entirely data-driven and without any prior assumption on the optimal feature set, the feature selection converged toward a low-dimensional model (only 4 out of 21 features), with a balanced contribution from fMRI (2 features: Average SAL and Std DMN-SAL) and gene expression markers (2 features: BDNF and TNFα). Statistically, difference in performance between this model and the fMRI-based one showed a trend towards significance (McNemar exact test, p = 0.08), whereas it did not differ significantly from the gene expression-based model. We note that to reach significance is inherently difficult in a sample of this size, where the paired tests rest on a small number of discordant classifications.

Notably, all three binary models exceeded the majority-class baseline (binomial test against the No-Information Rate; see SI), with an Area Under the Curve increasing from 0.68 (fMRI) and 0.74 (gene expression) to 0.82 for the multimodal model. ROC and precision-recall curves (see Fig. S3), bootstrap confidence intervals, and site-stratified validation, which showed comparable performance across the two scanners, are also reported in SI.

Five-stage BD model

Next, we considered the complete five BD stages of the Kupka and Hillegers model. In this case, the ML procedure identified an optimal model comprising nine features, four fMRI- and five mRNA-based (average connectivity within SAL, Std between DMN-FPN, Std between DMN-SAL, Std between FPN-SAL, BDNF, IL-4, IL-6, IL-19, and TNFα). Notably, in this multi-class setting, performance remained above chance level (20%), with a macro F1-score of 45.9% (95% CI = [34.4; 55.0]) and an overall accuracy of 55.1% (95% CI = [45.1; 62.9]), see Fig. 2D. Both metrics passed the permutation test (p = 0.001 in both cases). Notably, given the uneven stage distribution, the majority-class baseline reaches an accuracy of 48.3% (stage 3) and an F1-score of 13.0%. In this case, the model is very close to the majority-class in terms of accuracy, but it reaches a much higher F1-score, as shown by its 95% confidence interval. Nevertheless, we note that the classification accuracy varied substantially across stages. Stage 3 showed the highest discriminability (F1 = 0.68), confirming a relatively distinguishable profile. In contrast, stage 4 (F1 = 0.40) was frequently confused with stage 3, indicating a partial continuity between recurrent and chronic phases.

Thus, broad connectivity and molecular features separate illness stages above chance but do not resolve the finer distinction between adjacent late stages. These results, although affected by the limited sample size of the classes involved, seem to suggest that these low-level features cannot capture potential subtle inter-stage differences. We note, however, that the strong dimensionality reduction adopted here (from the 184-node connectome to 12 TNM summary features), although motivated by previous literature and necessary to limit overfitting, may also have contributed to this loss of sensitivity.

Next, to characterize how the functional architecture varies across illness stages, we examined the connection density across the five stages and HC, reported in Fig. 3. We found the highest density for HC. At-risk individuals (stage 0) showed an intermediate lower density corresponding to a reduction trend as compared to controls (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, FDR-corrected p = 0.08). From the prodromal phase onward (i.e., stages 1-3), the density was significantly reduced relative to HC (stage 1, p = 0.012; stage 2, p = 0.009; stage 3, p < 0.001; all FDR-corrected). Thus, at-risk and prodromal individuals occupied an intermediate location between healthy controls and the fully syndromic stages, with density lower at each successive stage from controls up to stage 3 patients. On the contrary, stage 4 patients showed a significant density increase relative to stage 3 (p = 0.037), converging toward values comparable to stage 0. Notably, this increase reversed the gradual reduction observed up to stage 3. These findings suggest that, even in euthymic patients, chronic BD is associated with a substantial reshaping of the functional connectome, characterized by a non-linear pattern of connections, that diminish from HC to at-risk individuals, and continue to diminish up to stage 3, while at stage 4 there is an increase in connection density.

Such a pattern might explain the limited performance of the five-stage classification. In fact, low-level features such as the average amplitude and temporal variability of connections fail to capture the inversion occurring between stage 3 and 4. However, we expect this change to affect the architecture of communication at a finer level, particularly in the topological role played by the involved functional hubs.

Topological reorganization between stages 3 and 4

Based on previous findings, the observed increase in connectivity from stage 3 to stage 4 does not seem to reflect clinical recovery: in fact, despite this increased connection density, patients exhibit greater clinical severity. To characterize such reorganization, we investigated the underlying topological architecture. In fact, we hypothesized that such an increase in connections would correspond to a loss of centrality, and thus functional integration, in connector hubs. To test this hypothesis, we extracted BC-based hubs, ranked them at each stage, and identified those showing the strongest BC decrease between stage 3 and 4 (see Materials and Methods). The idea was to identify hubs that were highly central (high rank) at stage 3 but decreased their influence (low rank) at stage 4. Hubs were considered to show a loss of BC when their rank change exceeded two standard deviations below the mean (red bars in Fig. 4A). Since the distribution of BC difference did not deviate significantly from a standard Gaussian distribution, this criterion selects nodes significantly different from the rest of the sample (see SI for further details). Five hubs showed a significant reduction: the left rostral prefrontal cortex (LRPFC, z = −2.12, P = 1.70%), left (LAI, z = −2.44, P = 0.73%) and right (RAI, z = −2.28, P = 1.13%) anterior insula, left middle temporal area (LMT, z = −2.31, P = 1.04%) and left parieto-occipital sulcus (LPOS, z = −2.28, P = 1.13%). Notably, three of these hubs (LRPFC and L/RAI) belong to the SAL. As illustrated in Fig. 4B, BC in these hubs largely decreased (87 ± 4 rank change, on average) from stage 3 (blue line) to 4 (red line), indicating a marked reduction of centrality. We note that this approach, based on the average stage-rank, has the advantage of being robust against noise fluctuations within each stage, but it does not account for within-stage variability. Thus, as a control, we also performed a subject-specific analysis based on the individual BC rank. Hubs were extracted from the distribution of the individual rank changes (see details in SI). Notably, this analysis returned the same three SAL nodes as before, plus four additional regions: the right posterior superior temporal gyrus (RIST, z = -2.96), the right cerebellum (Rcbl, z = -2.24), the right posterior intraparietal sulcus (Rpips, z = -2.10), and the right posterior cingulate cortex/precuneus (RPCPreCun, z = -2.02). The convergence of two distinct ranking strategies on the SAL nodes supports the robustness of our hub identification.

Positive values indicate an increase in BC rank from Stage 3 to Stage 4; negative values indicate a decrease. Red bars indicate nodes whose BC rank dropped by more than two standard deviations below the mean change. B) Corresponding betweenness centrality curves for Stage 3 and Stage 4, with vertical lines marking the five hubs that exhibit the strongest reduction in rank.

Besides these five hubs (L/RAI, LRPFC, LMT, and LPOS), it is noteworthy that the vast majority of nodes, including important integrative DMN regions such as the posterior cingulate cortex and medial PFC, did not exhibit substantial BC changes. The BC rank distribution displayed a short negative tail, indicating that the topological reorganization from stage 3 to stage 4 is locally confined rather than widespread. This pattern indicates that late-stage BD involves “subtle” topological reorganization, particularly in SAL-related nodes, while the overall global architecture of the functional connectome remains largely preserved.

Dynamic reorganization between stages 3 and 4

Next, we investigated the observed reorganization in terms of their connectivity dynamics. For each hub, we extracted the dynamics of its connectivity with nodes of the TNM (i.e., part of the DMN, FPN, and SAL; see Materials and Methods). To quantify their synchronization, i.e., how similarly each node fluctuated over time with the considered hub, we correlated these time-resolved connectivity patterns across all nodes and subjects. Then, we applied hierarchical clustering at each stage to identify coherent communities of nodes that co-fluctuate with the hub. If network organization is preserved, we expect these clusters to roughly correspond to DMN, FPN, and SAL, indicating that each hub maintains preferential temporal communication with separate large-scale systems (Ponce-Alvarez et al., 2015). To validate this hypothesis, first we estimated the optimal number of clusters to be adopted. To this aim, we computed the inconsistency coefficient of the dendrogram as a function of the number of clusters (see Fig. 5). At stage 3, the mean inconsistency coefficient showed a clear peak at k = 3, i.e. three clusters. This choice was confirmed to be stable by subsampling control (see SI and grey dashed line in Fig. 5). Interestingly, at stage 4 the absolute maximum of the coefficient was k = 2, without local peaks. The coefficient showed an essentially monotonic decreasing trend. This two-cluster solution would suggest that these hubs at stage 4 partition their temporal communication into two subsystems rather than three, reflecting a coarser temporal organization. To investigate this reorganization, we fixed the number of clusters at 3 and we compared how the hubs reorganize their temporal communication between stages.

For stage 3, both the full sample (black, solid) and the mean across 1000 subsamples matched to the stage-4 size (grey, dashed) are shown; both peak at k = 3 (red dashed line). This suggests three clusters as the most natural partition of the dendrogram and that this choice is stable under subsampling. At stage 4, the coefficient is highest at k = 2 and decreases monotonically.

In Fig. 6 (left side), we report the hierarchical clustering at stage 3 (see Fig. S4 for clustering results for LPOS). Across all five hubs, particularly the RAI and LRPFC, the clustering revealed three well-differentiated communities: one predominantly composed of DMN nodes (97% DMN, the proportion was computed as the average across the LRPFC, RAI, and LAI; gray lines in Fig. 6); one mainly consisting of SAL regions (86% SAL; dotted lines in Fig. 6), and one corresponding to a DMN-FPN mix (67% DMN and 33% FPN; black lines in Fig. 6). This suggests that, during this BD phase, the temporal structure of hub communication is functionally specific with respect to DMN and SAL. A similar specialization was also observed in the L/RAI and LPOS, while the LMT exhibited a more mixed but still discernible modular structure (see Fig. 6).

As expected from the inconsistency coefficient analysis, at stage 4 the dendrogram changed markedly. If we cut the dendrogram at three clusters to be compared to stage 3, in this case the distinction between networks is less evident: clusters are no longer dominated by nodes from a single RSN. For RAI, two clusters became composed of DMN nodes alone, while the full FPN and SAL nodes were in the last cluster with the remaining DMN nodes. Similarly, LRPFC exhibited a large heterogeneous cluster incorporating DMN, FPN, and SAL nodes. Next, we statistically tested changes in cluster composition between stage 3 and 4 using a Monte Carlo permutation test with FDR correction (see Materials and Methods). This analysis confirmed a significant node redistribution in both the RAI and LRPFC (FDR-corrected, p = 0.005 and 0.049, respectively). For LAI, we observed a similar trend that did not reach statistical significance. We speculate that this finding may indicate a more prominent involvement of the right insula in chronic BD as compared to the left insula. On the contrary, clusters remained very similar for the LMT and LPOS (see Fig. S4 in SI). Of note, in line with these findings, none of the four additional hubs identified by the subject-specific ranking (RIST, Rcbl, Rpips, RPCPreCun), not belonging to SAL, showed a significant change in cluster composition between stage 3 and stage 4 (all FDR-corrected p > 0.05, see SI). This absence of temporal reorganization in these hubs reinforces the specificity of the effect to the SAL-related hubs.

These findings suggest that these hubs do not maintain separate axes of communication, represented by temporal synchronization, with DMN and SAL. Since synchronization clusters become more mixed at stage 4, the difference between stage 3 and stage 4 seems to involve a loss of temporal organization and functional specificity in TNM connector hubs.

Discussion

In this study, we characterized the impact of the Triple Network Model functional architecture on BD staging. We showed that combining TNM connectivity features with gene expression markers in a ML classification model discriminates early (stages 0-2) from late (stages 3-4) BD. Beyond this binary classification, we mapped the specific functional architecture underlying each individual stage across the clinical spectrum. This revealed a non-linear trajectory of connectivity across stages: connection density was lower at each successive stage from healthy controls to stage 3, with at-risk relatives occupying an intermediate level, before exhibiting as significantly higher at stage 4 relative to stage 3. This late-stage network reconfiguration was accompanied by reduced centrality and diminished temporal synchronization within SAL hubs. We linked these topological shifts to an altered balance of RSN roles within the TNM, driven specifically by a loss in the coordinating role of the SAL. Collectively, these findings suggest that the difference between recurrent and chronic BD is marked by subtle topological reconfigurations driven by altered communication dynamics within the TNM.

Early vs. late stage multimodal classification

Initially, we considered two stages of BD (early vs. late). This choice, apart from considerations related to our sample size, can be supported by clinical evidence. In fact, early BD is associated with better outcomes and greater treatment responsiveness (Berk et al., 2010; Schuepbach et al., 2008), whereas long-standing illness is linked to higher burden, poorer prognosis, and reduced therapeutic response (Gelenberg et al., 1989; Swann et al., 1999). Thus, the early-late split reflects a clinically meaningful distinction that has been previously explored (Kauer-Sant'Anna et al., 2009; Rosa et al., 2012). In this data, the multimodal ML classification reached the highest performance across all metrics compared with models trained on either fMRI or molecular data alone (see Fig. 2). This difference did not reach statistical significance, which likely requires considerably larger cohorts. However, large multimodal BD staging datasets are currently not available to our knowledge. Nonetheless, the performance of the multimodal model points to a benefit of integrating functional and molecular information. This is in line with prior work showing that multimodal ML models, applied to discriminate BD from HC or different psychiatric patients, tend to outperform unimodal approaches (Colombo et al., 2022; Jan et al., 2021; Montazeri et al., 2023). Notably, as in our study, these models typically combine neuroimaging with peripheral molecular measures. Our results extend this pattern in the context of staging: our multimodal classifier comprised a compact and balanced feature set (2 neurofunctional + 2 gene expression markers, out of 21 variables). This low dimensionality seems in contrast with the only previous ML study attempting BD staging (Mwangi et al., 2016), based on a large number of structural MRI features to distinguish BD patients from healthy controls, and evaluated staging only a posteriori. Differently, our findings suggest that BD staging involves few coordinated changes in the dynamics of functional interactions. These findings are consistent with previously reported TNM alterations in BD, where illness progression was not taken into account (Cattarinussi et al., 2023; Fortea et al., 2023; Han et al., 2020; Syan et al., 2018). Moreover, both in the fMRI-only model and in the multimodal one, half of our neurofunctional features relate to dynamics of interactions. This is in line with previous works where functional dynamics emerged as powerful BD predictor (Rashid et al., 2016). The remaining features of our model are molecular: the expression level of BDNF and TNFα, i.e., a pro-inflammatory cytokine and a neurotrophic factor. Their co-selection is consistent with a model of BD neuro-progression driven by reduced neurotrophic support and sustained inflammatory activity (Rosenblat and McIntyre, 2016), which may contribute to the underlying neurotoxic processes (Barbosa et al., 2014; Mansur et al., 2013).

The ML performance decreased when the model was extended to the five stages. This could be ascribed to several methodological factors, e.g., the limited and uneven sample across stages and the feature reduction performed on the low-level connectivity features, as well as the definition of the clinical boundaries of the staging model itself. In fact, BD stages are defined by illness course rather than by biological mechanisms. Thus, the diagnostic boundaries both within and between BD and other psychiatric disorders are not clear-cut, as overlapping clinical phenotypes frequently confound distinct categorization (Phillips and Kupfer, 2013). However, our results show that even a clinical distinction as broad as early versus late illness has a biological counterpart that a ML model can detect. This indicates that biological information differs across stages and could, in principle, inform how stages are defined. To take classification beyond categorical definitions is the aim of approaches such as the nomothetic network psychiatry, which builds diagnosis and staging from biomarker and phenome data rather than from DSM categories (Maes and Stoyanov, 2022). In these approaches, unsupervised methods could cluster patients directly from connectivity and molecular features, letting biologically coherent subgroups emerge (not necessarily matching current stage boundaries). This is beyond the scope of this study, but our findings can be considered a preliminary step in that direction.

Topological reorganization at stage 4

Analysis of the functional connections across the BD stages revealed a non-linear trajectory. Specifically, when healthy controls were included as a baseline reference, connection density peaked in this group and lower at each successive stage from 0 to 3. Within this context, stage 0 individuals, i.e. at familial risk but not yet clinically affected, exhibited an intermediate density, positioning them between HCs and the recurrent phase (stage 3). This intermediate profile aligns with a vulnerability gradient previously reported in at-risk populations (Saccaro et al., 2025; Whittaker et al., 2018). Notably, this gradient in connection density reversed at stage 4, where density was significantly higher than stage 3 (see Fig. 3). This increase brought connection density back toward the level observed in at-risk individuals, without fully restoring it to that of healthy controls. Since this hyperconnectivity corresponds to more severe clinical staging, this seems to suggest that adding connections is not beneficial per se. Indeed, brain networks operate under a cost-efficiency trade-off: increasing connection density raises metabolic demand, particularly on hubs, and may therefore reduce the communication efficiency rather than improving it (Hillary and Grafman, 2017). Such phenomena are not unique to BD, but they have been documented both in computational models and across various clinical populations (Cabral et al., 2014; Fornito et al., 2015; Li et al., 2024). In the BD context, several fMRI studies have interpreted altered connectivity between the SAL and DMN as a compensatory recruitment mechanism aimed at maintaining functional performance during euthymia (Wessa et al., 2014; Yoon et al., 2021). Because all patients in our sample were evaluated during a euthymic phase, we speculate that the prolonged illness burden characteristic of stage 4 may have sculpted a trace in the brain at rest, detectable even in the absence of evident symptoms.

Further, as efficient large-scale communication at rest is closely tied to the integrative role of functional hubs (de Pasquale et al., 2016; Kabbara et al., 2017; van den Heuvel and Sporns, 2013), we examined the topological properties of this architecture by identifying BC-based hubs. A loss of centralization and integration has already been seen in BD, see for example (van Dellen et al., 2020). Here, we observed a significant decrease in BC from stage 3 to stage 4 in five hubs: LRPFC, L/RAI, LMT, and LPOS. Crucially, although our analysis was entirely data-driven and not restricted a priori, three of these emergent hubs (LRPFC, LAI, and RAI) belong to the SAL. Given the coordinating role of the SAL network and its well-established disruption in BD, this result is noteworthy.

The observed BC changes may be clinically relevant when considering the functional role of these hubs. In healthy individuals, the left rostral prefrontal cortex supports goal management and the regulation of internally versus externally focused cognition (Burgess et al., 2007; Rowe et al., 2007; Volle et al., 2011), fundamental requirements for maintaining coherence between self-referential and executive processes. Because bipolar patients demonstrate attenuated prefrontal engagement and reduced structural integrity even outside acute episodes (Abe et al., 2023; Blumberg et al., 2003), the drop in LRPFC centrality at stage 4 may reflect a diminished integrative control over the balance between internal mentation and goal-directed behavior. A parallel interpretation applies to the bilateral anterior insula hubs (L/RAI). The left anterior insula is central to affective processing and interoceptive integration (Lotze, 2024; Simmons et al., 2013), whereas the right insula supports exteroceptive attention, salience detection, and network switching (Cai et al., 2014; Simmons et al., 2013; Ulrich et al., 2022). In line with our findings, these regions exhibit disrupted resting-state connectivity with frontoparietal networks in BD (Ellard et al., 2018; Pastrnak et al., 2021; Qiu et al., 2020). Consequently, their loss of centrality in advanced stages may reflect a weakening in the brain's ability to mediate interoceptive-exteroceptive integration and govern cognitive network switching as the illness becomes chronic. This pattern of late-stage network disruption is further compounded by the left middle temporal area and the left parieto-occipital sulcus. The LMT serves as a hub of the empathy network, contributing to socio-emotional processing while its connectivity tracks the success of emotion regulation (Jauniaux et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2024). Altered functioning of this region has been linked to BD (Ping et al., 2022): its loss of centrality at later stages is therefore consistent with the weakening of social-affective integration in chronic illness. Meanwhile, the LPOS supports visuospatial analysis and the control of goal-directed actions (Tosoni et al., 2015). A reduction in its centrality suggests a reduced integrative role in coordinating these processes in advanced stages of BD.

In summary, the involvement of prefrontal and parietal hubs aligns with large-scale meta-analyses reporting alterations in these regions in BD (Schumer et al., 2023). Our findings extend this literature by suggesting that, beyond connectivity alterations, late-stage BD may involve a reduction in the integrative role of these hubs within RSN architecture. Notably, these regions are implicated beyond BD. For instance, altered self-inhibition of the anterior insula represents a shared feature across both mood disorders and schizophrenia (Kandilarova et al., 2021). This convergence on shared SAL nodes, alongside disorder-specific differences (Kandilarova et al., 2023), likely reflects the broader clinical heterogeneity of the affective spectrum and the unclear boundaries between major psychiatric conditions. Ultimately, these topological reconfigurations provide a potential neurobiological basis for the attentional and cognitive flexibility deficits commonly observed in BD during euthymic phases (Bora et al., 2009; Martinez-Aran et al., 2004), carrying relevant implications for the patient's long-term occupational functioning and psychosocial recovery (Martinez-Aran et al., 2004).

Dynamic reorganization at stage 4

Recent works show that hub interactions are inherently dynamic: hubs fluctuate over time to coordinate network states (de Pasquale et al., 2012; de Pasquale et al., 2016; de Pasquale et al., 2021; Fransson and Thompson, 2020). These dynamics are crucial for adaptive cognition, as they allow rapid reconfiguration of functional hierarchies to meet task demands (Bassett and Sporns, 2017; Shine et al., 2016). For this reason, to investigate the mechanism underlying the loss of centrality of the identified hubs from stage 3 to stage 4, we focused on the temporal dynamics of their connectivity. This allowed us to unravel possible modes of synchronization within the TNM (de Pasquale et al., 2021). The identified synchronization-based clusters suggest a marked shift in hub communication between stages. Stage 3 exhibited a clear pattern of functional specialization (see Fig. 6, left) based on three clusters, as suggested by the inconsistency analyses: one cluster was predominantly composed of DMN nodes, one cluster mainly composed of SAL nodes, while the third cluster reflected a mixed subsystem including DMN and FPN nodes. The presence of the two homogeneous clusters suggests that hub communication at this stage remains organized into temporally coordinated and functionally specialized modules. This separation between DMN- and SAL-dominated synchronization patterns indicates that SAL hubs communicate with their own network and with part of the DMN at different time points rather than simultaneously (otherwise they would be grouped in the same cluster). This can be linked to previous work showing that SAL may exert a causal influence on DMN activity (Das and Menon, 2020), representing a temporal gating system for DMN engagement.

At stage 4, this temporal architecture was no longer evident: distinct DMN- and SAL-dominated clusters could not be identified. These channels of communication were replaced by a mixed synchronization with all TNM networks (see Fig. 6, right). This suggests a reduced temporal alternation between communication modes, consistent with a weakening of the mechanism supporting dynamic routing of communication among distributed brain systems (DMN and SAL in our case) (Cocchi et al., 2013; Fries, 2005; Shine et al., 2016). This may contribute to a diminished ability of hub regions to regulate transitions between internally and externally oriented cognitive modes in later illness stages. This loss of temporal coordination was already seen in BD studies, and it has been linked to reduced network switching rate and RSN flexibility (Han et al., 2020; Nguyen et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2020). As a control, we note that LMT and LPOS, the identified hubs not belonging to SAL, as well as the other nodes identified by both adopted methodologies did not show the same pattern discussed above (see SI). This supports the specificity of the identified synchronization mechanism for SAL-related hubs.

In summary, this altered mechanism in SAL hubs may be linked to their functional impairment and the associated cognitive dysfunction that contributes to disability and loss of autonomy in chronic BD patients. Given the clinical need for stage-informed assessment and management (Kapczinski et al., 2009; Kapczinski et al., 2014), these findings suggest that functional neuroimaging markers may, if validated in longitudinal and independent samples, contribute to BD staging frameworks.

Finally, we acknowledge that several potential clinical and methodological confounders (e.g., sample size, medication exposure, residual symptoms) could influence the reported findings. While we sought to carefully evaluate and mitigate these factors through a series of control analyses, disentangling their individual contributions remains a complex challenge, as discussed in the next section.

Methodological limitations and future perspectives

Certain methodological limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample was unbalanced across the five clinical stages, reflecting the natural distribution of subjects along the BD spectrum, with a higher representation of stage 3 patients. Furthermore, the size of the minority classes was small (e.g., n = 12), and this particularly affects the five-stage classification. We mitigated this through macro-F1 model selection, inverse-frequency class weighting, permutation testing, and comparison against baseline classifiers. However, such small class sizes inevitably limit the performance of the five-stage model. Future studies should include larger and more evenly distributed samples to improve model performance and generalizability.

Second, despite being a fundamental step, external validation was not implemented. This was due to the fact that, currently, there are no independent datasets on BD staging comprising fMRI and genetic data. This should be addressed in the future to validate the reported findings.

Third, the interpretation of topological alterations and hub reorganization could be strengthened by collecting concurrent cognitive, behavioral, or sleep measures, which were not acquired here. Further limitations concern residual confounders. Regarding residual symptom severity, this did not significantly differ between stages 3 and 4, but residual clinical variation within euthymia cannot be entirely excluded. Medication exposure naturally increased across stages, which is intrinsic to a framework where treatment typically begins at stage 2. However, this, again, did not differ significantly between stages 3 and 4 (see SI). While the literature suggests that pharmacological effects on fMRI data in BD are generally subtle and tend to normalize connectivity rather than introduce spurious alterations, the possibility of minor confounding medication effects persists. Other variables, including head motion, lifetime psychiatric and medical comorbidities, substance/alcohol use, and smoking, showed no statistically significant differences across stages (see SI). Finally, several factors may influence peripheral gene expression. Circadian timing, fasting status, and body mass index were not recorded, and recent or subclinical infection was not applied as a formal exclusion criterion. These variables are rarely accounted for in this type of study, but they may have affected inflammatory and transcriptional measures. Differential leukocyte counts were likewise unavailable and cell-type deconvolution was not performed, so a contribution of PBMC subpopulation composition to the observed transcriptional differences cannot be excluded. As for medication, the frequent use of multiple compounds per patient precluded a reliable estimation of drug-specific effects on the molecular markers, which future studies should address. Despite our multiple controls, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out.

Fourth, we note that the developed approach in the future could be clinically useful since the required data is rather accessible. Analyzing peripheral gene expression markers is relatively accessible and cost-effective; the neuroimaging features are obtained from a resting-state acquisition, which is easier to tolerate for psychiatric patients than task-based paradigms. Moreover, as structural MRI is often already part of the diagnostic tools to exclude organic causes, adding a few minutes of resting-state acquisition requires a limited marginal cost. Most importantly, the value of these biomarkers would lie both in distinguishing clinical stages and, if validated longitudinally, in flagging the risk of progression before it becomes clinically evident. Realizing this prognostic potential requires moving beyond the present cross-sectional design to the stage transitions characterization. Since longitudinal data are being collected within the same cohort (Girone et al., 2025), future analyses will aim to track within-subject changes to validate the temporal dynamics inferred here.

Fifth, our findings rely on the established clinical staging framework rather than deriving novel stage classes directly from biomarkers. Future work should adopt a data-driven approach to group patients based on biological features alone, testing whether these biotypes align with or cut across current clinical stages (Maes and Stoyanov, 2022).
