0. SUMMARY

This study demonstrates that placing an acoustic source at depth significantly enhances
the detection of tunnels. By adapting the methodology of vertical seismic profiling to
acoustic-range frequencies, we introduce a novel approach for tunnel detection. To our
knowledge, this report documents the first application of this technique.

The field study was conducted on a cleared site at the Oak Ridge National Laboratory;
the subsurface was clay. A series of vertical shafts were bored immediately before and
after the placement of a 40 ft long, 3 ft diameter steel pipe located 10 ft below the
surface. Acoustic signatures were produced at various depths up to 30 ft and an array
of surface geophones was used to collect the resulting signals.

The innovation lies in using subsurface acoustic signatures to better enable the
collection of wide-angle diffracted waves by surface geophones. These diffracted waves
appear as subharmonic frequencies of the source signal and are recorded by a surface
geophone array. While the concept draws from vertical seismic profiling used in oil and
gas exploration, a key distinguishing feature is the use of acoustic frequencies, which
are necessary for detecting small subsurface features.

The findings have important implications. Critical information pertaining to diffracted
source signals are not measured by current state-of-the-art methods. Although this
study confirms enhanced detectability, accurate localization and imaging will require
additional research. Future work should focus on leveraging diffracted-wave strength
and time delays to enable full imaging and characterization.

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

1.1.1 Military and Intelligence Applications

Tunnels and underground cavities have been used for concealment and clandestine
activity since antiquity. As a breaching tactic for besieged walled and defended cities,
tunneling has been used by ancient peoples to gain undetected access and overpower
the cities’ defenses from within. The earliest recorded evidence of employing tunnels in
battle is depicted in Assyrian carvings dating back 4000 years [1]. In fact, tunnel
warfare to breach city defenses was an established tactic of the Romans in their many
conquests. More recently, tunnel warfare was used by Union troops in the American
Civil War, by the Japanese during World War II, and by the North Vietnamese during
the Vietnam war [2]. Today, tunnels continue to facilitate covert troop and militant
movement along borders such as the Demilitarized Zone between North and South
Korea, Gaza Strip, and the Syria–Israel border. With the advent of modern explosives,
tunnels have been used to covertly place explosives to gain military tactical advantages.

 3

Beyond military applications, tunnels serve other illicit purposes, including information
gathering and shielding. Small-diameter boring operations can be used to clandestinely
breach secure perimeters and covertly place eavesdropping devices. Underground
facilities offer protection from attack, the most recent example of which is the
underground nuclear material and development activities of Iran. Tunnels are also
frequently employed as escape routes.

As an established tactic of offensive warfare, countermeasures have been developed to
detect the vibrations accompanying digging and boring operations. Historically, tunnel
activity was assessed by picking up these vibrations using bowls of water or drums [1].
All tunneling operations produce vibratory waves, which are difficult, if not impossible to
mask. Signal detection of boring/tunneling operations using geophones, microphones,
or fiber optics, are used to passively detect tunneling/boring activity. However, passive
measurement requires that the receiver be in proximity to the source, depending on the
boring operation and subsurface geology.

1.1.2 Narcotics Smuggling

Illicit materials (e.g. narcotics) are routinely transported across the southern border of
the United States via tunnels [3–7]. These tunnels range in diameter from 20–100 cm
(8–39 in). Approximately 170 tunnels (sized for human movement) have been
discovered along the U.S. border with Mexico in the past two decades (the first one was
detected in 1998), and their numbers are increasing [3-7]. Notably, none of these
tunnels were found using modern sensing technologies (seismic, ground penetrating
radar, electrical resistivity imaging, etc.), but rather through informants or accidental
discoveries during construction [8]. Tunnel construction varies from simple bored-out
holes to more sophisticated, wall-reinforced designs, sometimes including active
ventilation and artificial lighting. Some cross-border tunnels extend up to a mile or more
in length. While these tunnels seem like an obvious means of transporting people, they
are not often used as such. The cost and effort to build these tunnels is not
insignificant, therefore they are not routinely used for human transport, since the risk of
discovery via an informant is too high [9].

Increased border security (checkpoints, surface interrogation, etc.) have caused drug
cartels to direct even more of their efforts towards tunneling to avoid detection. The
U.S. Government does not have reliable estimates of the total quantity of illicit drugs
smuggled through these tunnels [4–6]; however, 10 to 40 tons are seized every month
at an estimated societal cost of $70 billion [10].

becomes much more difficult. The absence of detectable vibrations due to
boring/digging operations is further complicated by the following:

1.2 TUNNEL CONSTRUCTION

Many tunnels are dug manually, using hand tools such as picks and shovels, though
more advanced equipment such as jackhammers is also employed. Tunnel structure
can be variable. Small tunnels may have no added support, but most include
reinforcements – such as wood or metal beams, concrete, or metal piping - to maintain
structural integrity, particularly in softer soils. Even tunnels excavated in solid rock
sometimes incorporate support structures, although some do exist with no modification.
A key issue is water penetration and its impact on tunnel walls and floors. Large
tunnels often include concrete flooring or piping to prevent erosion and ensure stability.
Piping is relatively easy to install and many drilling techniques add pipe as drilling
advances into the soil as part of the process. These structural elements offer
opportunities for detection since their higher densities create acoustic impedance
contrast with the surrounding soil, producing signal reflections that can be monitored.

With the advent of commercially available, highly portable horizontal directional drilling
(HDD) and advanced microboring systems, tunnels can now be bored rapidly and
accurately up to 2.4 km (1.5 miles) in length and as deep as 30 m (100 ft). The more
advanced systems are not only highly portable (see Figure 2) but also allow the
operator to know the precise location of the drill during the boring process [14]. These
systems further advance smuggling operations, since they do not require as many
personnel and can facilitate small tunnel diameters (down to 10 cm [4 inches]). These
small tunnels are extremely difficult to detect since they require higher frequencies in
the acoustic range, which are also highly attenuated. In urban environments, these
small tunnels are further obscured, often blending with utility infrastructure such as
water, fuel, or sewage pipes.

1.3 STATE OF THE ART

The vast majority of tunnel-detection methods rely on active measurement, with The
primary means are seismic sensing, GPR, and electrical resistivity being the primary
techniques. Of these, seismic and GPR are the most widely used. It is important to
distinguish between detection and localization. Detection confirms the presence of an
object within the measurement range, whereas localization identifies its spatial
coordinates.

To facilitate localization, it is standard practice to deploy a surface array of sensors in a
grid and record signal frequency, strength, and time correlations. Reflected signals are
particularly valuable, as the time delay between the source and the reflection allows
estimation of distance to the object. However, soil inhomogeneity and complexity make
reflected signals noisy and challenging to interpret, even under the most ideal
conditions. Detectability, localization, and imaging are improved by the collection and
analysis of large data sets, especially regarding signal patterns and the attenuation
characteristics of the signals with the subsurface physical geology.

1.3.1 Ground Penetrating Radar
Ground penetrating radar is an imaging method in which pulsed radio-frequency waves
are sent into a substrate surface. In a perfectly homogenous medium, these waves

 7

travel unaltered and are not reflected back to the source. However, changes in the
electrical permittivity or susceptibility cause the waves to reflect, scatter, or diffract,
allowing sensors – typically positioned near the transmitter and rastered across an area
– to record the resulting signals. These changes arise from differences in density,
composition and structure. Knowledge of the measured signal strengths, sensor
location, and detector fidelity can be used to image and identify buried features
including tunnels or cavities. In reality, soil substrates are not perfectly uniform and
contain discontinuities—such as rock, water, organic matter, and voids—which add to
the complexity of interpretation. Signal attenuation also limits the operational depth of
most GPR-based systems to less than 30 meters (100 feet), with groundwater further
accelerating attenuation and reducing penetration depth.

1.3.2 Seismic
Seismic-based methods are among the most widely used for subsurface exploration
due to the ability of seismic waves to propagate over long distances. In these systems,
seismic energy is generated at the surface, either through high-pressure impulses (e.g.,
explosives) or mechanical vibrators (thumpers). Similar to GPR, the returned signal
reflects changes in the propagation media; however, in this instance, reflections,
diffractions, and scattering are driven by contrasts in acoustic impedance.

A notable advantage of seismic methods is their sensitivity to frequency shifts caused
by variations in density and structural features. This capability has made seismic
methods are widely used for oil and gas exploration, where long wavelengths enable
interrogation over long distances and are ideal for detecting extensive, deeply buried
hydrocarbon reservoirs.
 A key adaptation of this approach is vertical seismic profiling (VSP), whereby a seismic
transceiver is deployed down a vertical borehole to both generate source signals and
detect reflections, along with an array of surface sensors [15]. This embodiment
provides depth-constrained source information and enhances data quality, enabling
improved resolution, feature identification, and imaging.

However, a limitation of conventional seismic methods is their reliance on low
frequencies (<1Hz) with long wavelengths. These wavelengths restrict detection to
subsurface features larger than 40–50 meters (~130–165 feet) in diameter. The
majority of man-made tunnels are well below this size range and, as a result, not
detectable using standard seismic means.

1.3.3 Electrical Resistance
This method is based on sending an electrical direct current between two buried
electrodes. The resistivity between the probes is measured and can be mapped by
repositioning the probes on a grid and repeating the measurements [16]. Because this
approach is limited to bulk resistance measurements, the information collected is
featureless and requires multiple measurements to discern features and properly
characterize. This method is also limited to near-surface features. A secondary effect
achieved by this approach is Induced Polarization, whereby components of the soil
(especially fluid–solid interfaces) become polarized by the applied current [17]. When

 8

the electrical current is removed, the charged sites discharge electricity to return to their
normal “nonpolarized” state. This discharge current produces a measurable decaying
voltage signal that is characteristic of the material interface.

1.3.4 Other technologies
Other technologies that have been explored for tunnel detection include infrared
measurements to identify potential thermal anomalies associated with tunnels and
electromagnetic (EM) fields, whereby a secondary EM field is induced into the ground
and measured [16]. Gravimetry, via the direct measurement of variations in the
gravitational fields, is also being explored as a means of detecting subsurface objects
and structures.

Current approaches often combine two or more detection methods, with multi-method
data fusion significantly improving detection fidelity. Artificial intelligence (AI), machine
learning (ML), and predictive mapping are increasingly being explored to enhance
performance. These computational tools, however, require large, well-structured
datasets to train reliable models and to accurately characterize the subsurface. One of
the most advanced systems was developed by the U.S. Army Engineer Research and
Development Center (ERDC) and utilizes both sonic and electromagnetic methods in a
system known as Rapid Reaction Tunnel Detection (R2TD) [18]. This system relies
heavily on sophisticated mathematical modelling to extract information from low signal-
to-noise environments.

Studies exploring the attenuation of soils with acoustic frequencies of 1 to 10 kHz are
limited to surface-based systems measuring reflected signatures. These systems are
not strongly coupled with the subsurface structure, resulting in poor signal transmission
(and detection). In this study, we prototyped a subsurface-based imaging system using
both through-transmission and surface-generated (pulse–echo) signatures to improve
detection by improving coupling efficiency and providing additional signature information
for detection.

2. PROJECT DESCRIPTION

The experimental portion of this effort was conducted on an approved site at Oak Ridge
National Laboratory. A professional drilling and boring contractor was used to bore the
vertical shafts and place the tunnel. Upon completion of the field work, the contractor
removed the piping and filled in all of the excavated soil. A description of these
activities is described further in the report.

2.1 HYPOTHESIS AND RATIONALE

To overcome the challenges with the current state-of-the-art approaches for tunnel
detection, a concept was devloped to place an acoustic source transmitter at a depth
that is expected to lie below the target tunnel. A surface array of geophones was used
collect the signals, which were combined with the advanced signal analysis for
improved detection. A site on the ORNL reservation was utilized and experiments were

 9

conducted to test the effectiveness of the proposed approach. This configuration allows
for measurement of three signal features that are not typically captured when both the
source and receivers are located at the surface:

 1. attenuation of a through-transmission signal,
 2. wide-angle scatter, and
 3. wide-angle diffraction.

An example of through transmission is highlighted in Figure 3. In the traditional pulse–
echo configuration (left), the source and receiver are collocated at the surface, and only
reflected energy is captured. In contrast, when the source is buried below the target, the
transmitted signal passes through the object or void and it is detected by surface-based
sensors (right). A key advantage of through transmission is better resolution on object
morphology and size, while the reflected (pulse–echo) approach allows for ranging by
measuring the time delay (ranging with through transmission is possible with multiple
receivers).

Diffraction occurs when the direction of the propagating source signal is deviated from a
straight-line projection of the original direction. For acoustic and seismic wave
propagation, this deviation occurs with density gradients and is typically not far removed
from the source direction as shown in shown in Figure 5. As a result, the detection of
diffracted surface-generated signals with surface-mounted sensors is unlikely, but the
probability of detection with a subsurface source and surface array of detectors is
higher.

Figure 5. Diagram highlighting the increased probability of detecting refracted waves using a
subsurface source signal.

 11

It is important to note that surface-based methods can be adapted for wide-angle
detection by placing the source at a far distance from the receivers. While this
approach is suitable for seismic range frequencies which do not attenuate rapidly, it is
less feasible for acoustic frequencies where the attenuation is high.

Seismic methods are used extensively in the oil industry to identify large hydrocarbon
reservoirs. The long wavelengths (low frequencies) of seismic waves can propagate
over great distances and are used to detect large geological features. Our approach
shares similarities with vertical seismic profiling (VSP), in which a downhole transceiver
generates signals that are recorded primarily by surface-based sensors [15]. However,
unlike VSP, our approach employs higher-frequency source signals optimized to detect
small features like manway-sized tunnels. Although similar downhole-surface
configurations have been proposed for monitoring volcanic or seismic activity, those
methods, as with VSP, use wavelengths too long to resolve small tunnel-sized
anomalies [11]. The two key distinctions of our approach (compared to VSP) are the
use of acoustic-range frequencies and the use of both surface and subsurface source
signals resulting in a higher level of signature information to improve detection fidelity.

A potential risk is that the rapid attenuation associated with higher frequencies may
result in signals too weak to be measured by conventional detectors. This risk is most
pronounced for surface-generated signals. However, the propagation characteristics of
subsurface-generated signals (particularly those moving from higher to lower density
media) are not as well studied. A key benefit of this approach is that a greater fraction
of the generated energy will be coupled to the soil to improve signal strength over a
surface-based approach.

2.3 APPROACH

The initial step was to deploy an array of geophones on the site surface several days
before performing the vertical boring and acoustic-source testing to establish the
ambient noise background. The geophones collected data continuously over a 10-day
period (2024 August 19–29) at 2000 Hz. During this interval, approximately 0.1 in of
rain fell on August 20, with an additional ~1.5 in having occurred on August 16–17;
otherwise, the site remained dry throughout the test period. Vertical boring was
achieved using a rotary blast hole drill rig operating nominally at 200 rpm, producing a
borehole 15.2 cm (~6 inches) in diameter. The acoustic source was a single electronic
trumpet/snail horn with a nominal output frequency of 510 Hz and sound levels between
105–118 dB. A surface array of 25 geophones was placed on the mound in varied
locations. The acoustic source and a representative geophone are shown in Figure 8.
Baseline measurements were collected by placing the trumpet horn at borehole depths
of 1, 5, 10, 15, 20, and 30 ft (0.3–10 m) as shown in Figure 9. Starting at a depth of 1 ft,
the horn was pulsed at 1 second intervals for up to 10 seconds. Afterwards the horn
was removed, the bore depth increased to the next setting, and the procedure repeated.
This continued up to a final depth of 30 ft. In addition to the pulsed horn signal, data
were collected continuously, encompassing boring, horn sounding, drilling, and latent
intervals. It should be noted that in both boreholes, no rocks, concrete, or other non-soil
content was encountered.

A key technique for interpreting seismic and acoustic signals is the Fast Fourier
Transform (FFT), which decomposes a time-domain signal into its constituent frequency
components. A single FFT may be calculated with a finite window of time-series data,
enough to obtain a statistical average, based on the assumption that a complicated
time-series signal is the linear superposition of sine waves across a range of
frequencies. The resulting detected frequencies range from near 0 to the Nyquist
frequency (here, 1000 Hz), which is half of the time-series sampling rate (here, 2000
Hz). Because the FFT assumes perfect sinusoidal content at each frequency, at least 2
sample points per cycle are needed to characterize each sinusoid. A common way to
visualize the FFT is through the power spectral density (PSD), obtained by squaring the
FFT amplitudes. Thus, the PSD is a functional array of power versus frequency indices.

For signals which vary with time—as in this study, which includes boring, drilling, horn
sounding, and quiescence— it is common practice to show the temporal evolution of the
PSD in a spectrogram (also referred to as a “waterfall” plot). In this study’s
spectrograms, time series are windowed into 30 second chunks, with no overlap, to
produce each PSD. Each PSD used a 4096-point windowing function. Thus, the
spectrograms have 30-sec time and ~0.5 Hz frequency resolution.

Across all source depths, the boring activity is clearly seen by the broad vertical bands
up to 1000 Hz, indicating that the boring activity produced a wideband acoustic
signature. The source signal is also shown as a green band between each of the boring
measurements. The actual measured frequency of the source signal was closer to 490
Hz. The measured source signal is consistent for all of the baseline measurements.
This consistency also occurs when the tunnel is present. However, when the source
transmitter is placed at 15 ft (~5 m) depth, there is a double frequency peak measured
for geophones at sites 1–4. This double-peak feature is clearly visible and consists of
the baseline signal and a slightly higher band closer to 500 Hz. The reason for this
frequency split is unclear. It may be due to misorientation of the source horn or be
indicative of a frequency shift because at 15 ft (~5 m), the source is placed at the
bottom of the mound where it sits on the original surface. A change in soil composition
at this location may be responsible; however, this phenomenon was not observed in the
baseline measurements.

The sensor geometries help explain variations in the observed responses. Geophones
1, 2, and 4 were located across from the source with pipe length roughly perpendicular
to the source–geophone transect. Geophone 3 lay across the pipe at an oblique angle.
Geophone 5’s transect also crosses the tunnel, albeit obliquely, and extended beyond
the tunnel’s end ( Figure 18). Geophone 6 differed from all others by being on the same
side of the tunnel as the source and located significantly behind it; as a result, it will not
be expected to exhibit features associated with a through transmission path.

Comparison of baseline and tunnel-present measurements reveals a key feature: the
emergence of a subharmonic at approximately half the source frequency. This feature
appeared in all sensors (including those not shown) but only when the source was
placed at depths of 15, 20, or 30 ft - well below the tunnel depth. The subharmonic
cannot be attributed to reverberation within the steel pipe, as it is absent when the
source lies shallower than the buried pipe. For the same reason, it cannot be signal
scatter. The only plausible explanation is that the subharmonic results from diffraction
of the original source signal. This is further evidenced by the fact that the subharmonic
frequency is half the emitted frequency. This result strongly suggests that a wide-angle
scatter effect had taken place as described in Figure 4. Because of its wide-angle
nature, it was not detectable by the receivers for the shallower depth source signals.
This diffraction-induced subharmonic provides a new and promising approach for tunnel
detection.

4. CONCLUSIONS

This study showed that key information for detecting buried tunnels is enhanced by
deep placement of an acoustic source. The approach represents a novel application of
vertical seismic profiling using acoustic-range frequencies for tunnel detection and this
report describes its first implementation. Notable findings include:

  Acoustic pulses (470 Hz and 100 dB) were highly detectable over long distances
 (over 20 meters) in a highly attenuating medium.
  VSP successfully detected a subharmonic acoustic signature associated with the
 tunnel.
  The subharmonic was detectable at all sensor locations, including those not
 adjacent to the tunnel.
  This unique signal was only detectable when the acoustic source was placed
 below the tunnel, confirming our hypothesis and strongly suggesting that
 measurement of wide-angle refraction of the source signal was responsible.
 Because the subharmonic was only observed for source signal placement below
 the tunnel, this method can be used to assess the tunnel depth.
  Proof of concept data suggest that deeper source placement improves
 detectability, further supporting the hypothesis.

The research team established a new methodology to identify tunnels and subterranean
cavities. Further analysis is needed to explore measured signal intensity as a function
distance, arrangement, and placement from the source location and tunnel location and
orientation. Better controlled testing will be necessary to verify observations and build
an empirical model to develop a predictive methodology.

Further investigations with different soil types and subsurface geologies are proposed to
confirm repeatability and improve the methodology. These include time-delay analyses
and correlation of signal strength to enable imaging and localization.
