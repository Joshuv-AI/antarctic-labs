Decongested 2D IR Spectra
Reveal N‐Terminal
Helices in Amyloid Oligomers Associated with Type 2 Diabetes

Shivani T Shivani, Josée Maurais, Brynn E LeMasters, Harrison J Esterly, Martin T Zanni

Abstract

Oligomeric peptides are implicated in amyloidosis, yet
only a few
atomic structures of amyloid oligomers have been identified. Recently,
we reported an atomic structural model of human islet amylin polypeptide
(hIAPP) oligomer [

ShivaniS. T.,

et al. 
Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. U.S.A.
2026, 123, e2528103123
]. The model was generated by trapping the oligomers with
three alanine substitutions in a sequence termed 3A-hIAPP. These substitutions
neither impact the physiologically critical β-sheet region of
hIAPP nor the cytotoxicity and oligomer size measured through biochemical
assays. However, their effect on the structure of the N-terminal helices
could not be assessed. By modifying our 2D IR spectrometer, we interwove
the acquisition of multiple pulse sequences, enabling waiting-time-dependent
spectra of hIAPP oligomers to be measured during protein aggregation.
Spectra acquired at a waiting time of 1.2 ps are decongested of disordered
structures, revealing features originating from the oligomeric N-terminal
α-helix and the adjacent kink and disulfide-loop regions. The
spectra of hIAPP oligomers closely match those of 3A-hIAPP, strengthening
the structural relevance of our atomic model for hIAPP oligomers.
These findings also establish a workflow for testing and generating
atomic structures of hIAPP oligomers associated with early-onset type
2 diabetes and for oligomers from other amyloid diseases.

Introduction

Intrinsically disordered or partially
denatured proteins form transient
oligomers prior to amyloid fibril formation, which constitute the
disease-associated plaques (see A for schematic). In many cases, the oligomers are
thought to be a source of cytotoxicity, and they are notoriously difficult
to structurally characterize due to their transient nature, low concentrations,
small size, and heterogeneous size distributions.
−

 As a result, only a few atomic structures of amyloid
oligomers have been reported.
,
 One approach to structural
characterization has been to either stabilize the oligomers or destabilize
the fibrils, thereby populating the intermediate oligomeric state
by slowing the aggregation. This strategy has been implemented using
antibodies, ligand binding, small-molecule inhibitors, cross-linking,
and sequence truncations, followed by structural analysis using high-resolution
techniques such as X-ray, cryoEM, and NMR spectroscopy.
−

 Although these methods have provided some of the earlier insights
into the atomic structure of amyloid oligomers, it remains unclear
whether the resulting oligomers represent native intermediate structures
or are structurally altered.

Human islet amylin polypeptide (hIAPP) is a 37-residue
polypeptide
(B) that forms
transient oligomeric intermediates and ultimately aggregates into
amyloid plaques implicated in type 2 diabetes. These transient oligomers
are cytotoxic and thought to permeabilize the cell membrane, cause
inflammation, and interfere with receptor-mediated processes.
,,,−

 We recently reported an atomic structural model of
oligomers formed by a version of hIAPP that has three alanine substitutions
at Leu-12, Phe-15, and Asn-31, that we call 3A-hIAPP. These substitutions slowed aggregation from hours to days,
enabling structural characterization by high-resolution 2D/3D NMR spectroscopy (sequence and substitutions are shown in ). Extensive control
experiments were performed to assess the validity of this model. These
alanine substitutions did not perturb cytotoxicity, oligomer size
distribution as measured by cross-linking, or the morphology according
to TEM. Moreover, a β-sheet previously
identified using isotope labeling and two-dimensional infrared (2D
IR) spectroscopy located in the famous Phe-23 to Leu-27 region of
the sequence, was recapitulated in the alanine-substituted model.
,
 The resulting atomic structure of the dimer created from the NMR
data is shown in C.

The derived structure consists of 4 structural features:
a loop
constrained by a disulfide bond between Cys-2 and Cys-7, N-terminal
α-helices that span from Ala-5 to Val-17 for one strand and
extending to Asn-21 in the other (C), followed by a kink that ends at Phe-23,
and a parallel β-sheet spanning Phe-23 to Ser-28 (“FGAILS”
region). This oligomeric model is notable for structural and biophysical
reasons. The FGAILS β-sheet correlates with the likelihood of
a species to develop amyloid plaques and type 2 diabetes; it is only
observed in species that develop type 2 diabetes. The N-terminal sequence that spans the helices are conserved
across species and is likely the recognition sequence of IAPP.
,
 The helices are amphipathic, which promotes helical bundles and
plays a role in membrane binding and aggregation. The kink that lies in the middle of the helix and the β-sheet
is the site of a serine-to-glycine (at Ser-20) hereditary mutation
that is associated with the early onset of type 2 diabetes. Our experimentally derived structural model
also has similarities with earlier computationally derived structures,
,
 such as that by Ding and co-workers who also predicted a helix-kink-sheet
structure of the oligomers, albeit with a more compact tertiary fold. Overall, the features of the structural model
in C are consistent
with prior biophysical studies and physiological observations.

The atomic model developed from 3A-hIAPP is physiologically relevant
only to the extent that its structure and biochemical properties resemble
those of hIAPP. In our original study, we presented spectroscopic
evidence that the FGAILS β-sheet in our model has a comparable
structure as hIAPP. Here, we test the N-terminal helices and surrounding
disulfide-loop and kink regions. The helix is of relevance since the
alanine substitutions typically promote helix formation,
,
 and so Ala-12 and Ala-15 in 3A-hIAPP could artificially extend the
length of the helices. To study these regions, we modified our 2D
IR spectrometer so that it could collect waiting-time-dependent 2D
IR spectra of hIAPP and 3A-hIAPP during aggregation. The waiting time
(defined as t
2) is the second delay in
a typical 2D IR pulse sequence (D). During this time, spectral features decay because
of vibrational relaxation thus, modes with shorter lifetimes gets
suppressed at longer t
2, which leads to
higher-resolution spectra that can resolve hidden spectral features.
Previously, our group and others have found that 2D IR spectra collected
at longer t
2 reduces contributions from
random coil features in spectra of hIAPP fibrils.
,
 At t
2 = 1 ps, spectra were shown to
be less congested, which allowed improved resolution of β-sheet
features. These experiments were performed
on fully aggregated hIAPP fibrils that were no longer kinetically
evolving, so signals could be averaged without time constraints. However,
for oligomers, spectra must be collected while aggregation is occurring,
making rapid data collection imperative. At concentrations typically
used in our 2D IR experiments (0.1–1 mM), monomers assemble
to oligomers within minutes, followed by aggregation into amyloid
fibrils within hours (A).
,
 During this process, the relative
concentrations of these 3 species (monomer, oligomer, and fibril)
evolve continuously, as do the resulting 2D IR spectra as shown in A. Thus, the speed
at which the spectra can be measured determines the accuracy with
which the structural kinetics can be monitored. Previous studies on
this subject relied on mechanical stages that increment the waiting-time
delay by physically moving two retroreflector mirrors, which is a
slow process (typically, tens to hundreds of milliseconds per step).
,
 By generating the waiting time electronically using a pulse shaper
rather than a mechanical stage, we could update the pulse sequence
shot-to-shot, allowing us to interweave 2D IR pulse sequences so that
multiple-waiting-time spectra could be continuously measured throughout
aggregation. Similar pulse shaping strategies have been previously
implemented in other nonlinear spectroscopy.
,
 The resulting spectra are much less congested and reveal features
created by the N-terminal α-helix and the neighboring loop and
kink regions. The spectra are nearly identical between hIAPP and 3A-hIAPP,
which strongly supports the relevance of our oligomeric structural
model and establishes a rigorous workflow that could be applied to
generate and test atomic structural models of oligomers associated
with early-onset type 2 diabetes, IAPP oligomers from other species,
and oligomers from other amyloid diseases.

Methods

Preparation of Protein Sample

hIAPP samples were prepared
using FMOC microwave-assisted solid-phase peptide synthesis following
previously published procedures, briefly
summarized here. For the synthesis, we use polystyrene resin (PALPEG-PS)
and FMOC-protected amino acids. Synthesized polypeptides were cleaved
using an acidic cleavage cocktail containing trifluoroacetic acid:thioanisole:ethanediol:anisol
in a 90:5:4:1 (v:v:v:v) at 37 °C for 30 min in microwave-assisted
cleavage system (CEM). After cleavage, peptide is precipitated and
washed in cold ether. To form the Cys-2–Cys-7 disulfide bond,
the polypeptide is dissolved in DMSO for 24 h followed by a 50% dilution
with 20% acetic acid for 12 h. Separate syntheses were performed for
each isotope labeled samples. We labeled different residues in hIAPP
with 13C18O. Labeled amino acids were prepared
using 18O exchange on 13C labeled FMOC-protected
amino acids (purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories) using 18O water and anhydrous 1, 4 dioxanes containing 4 M HCl. Two
separate labeling experiments are discussed. One, with labeled Phe-23
to Ala-25, and other with labeled Val-32, Gly-33. hIAPP is purified
using two rounds of reversed-phase HPLC system that uses a C18 column
(XSelect CSH, Waters). We use a gradient of two buffersA:
0.045% (v/v%) HCl in water and B: 80% acetonitrile, 20% water and
0.045% HCl (v/v%). HPLC is run at 1% per minute until 45% Buffer B
was reached. After the first round of purification, peptides were
dissolved in HFIP and diluted with 4x water and repurified using same
gradient. MALDI-TOF (Bruker Ultraflex) was used to characterize and
assess the purity of the hIAPP samples. Samples were quantified using
Nano Drop (Thermo Fischer) using 280 nm absorbance with an extinction
coefficient of 1490 M–1 cm–1 and
FTIR (Nicolet iS10, Thermo Scientific). In addition to unlabeled and
labeled hIAPP, we synthesized a version of hIAPP, referred to as 3A-hIAPP,
that has alanine substitutions at Leu-12, Phe-15, and Asn-31 (B). These three alanine
substitutions extend the aggregation time of the hIAPP oligomers while
keeping the structure intact, enabling us to obtain a high-resolution
structure of 3A-hIAPP using NMR spectroscopy. All samples were prepared
by reconstituting the lyophilized peptide to 500 μM in 50 mM
tris buffer prepared in D2O. To minimize extent of aggregation,
we did not perform any stirring. The sample was quickly placed between
two CaF2 windows using 75 μm spacer such that the
dead time between reconstitution and data acquisition remained less
than 2 min.

2D IR Spectroscopy and Pulse Shaping

2.5 μJ mid-infrared
pulses centered at 6200 nm having a pulse width of 75 fs at 10 kHz
repetition rate were generated using a commercial Yb:KGW laser system
and OPA (Carbide and Orpheus, from Light conversion). A 75/25 ZnSe
beam splitter is used to create two mid-IR laser beams, with the higher
intensity beam sent through a pulse shaper that is set in a 4f-geometry
to generate the pump pulses. An arbitrary waveform generator (AWG,
from Signatec) created the optical masks in the pulse shaper and was
used to shape the spectrum and phase of the pump pulse to create 2
pulses and scan the coherence time delay (t
1) and waiting-time delay (t
2). Waiting-time
delays were created by applying a linear phase to the waveform. The
maximum double pulse delay that can be generated at high fidelity
with our pulse shaper when configured with 75 g/mm gratings is 5 ps
at 5.5 μm. This delay must include both the t
1 and t
2 delays. The maximum
coherence delay was set to t
1 = 2.5 ps
so that a waiting times up to 2.5 ps could be measured; we report
spectra collected at t
2 = 0, 0.6, and
1.2 ps, which is within the relevant t
2 range for proteins.
,,
 All spectra were collected using a four-frame phase cycling scheme,
and t
1 was incremented in 40 fs steps,
thereby requiring 256 combinations of pulses for each 2D IR spectrum.
The pulse sequence for each waiting time was interwoven so that each t
1 delay sequence has its own t
2 delay. We made three such sequence for acquiring three t
2 delays corresponding to 0, 0.6, and 1.2 ps,
one after the other before data collection for the next loop starts.
Spectra were measured at 3 min interval and representative 2D IR spectra
shown were averaged over 9–12 min window. We note that the
diffraction efficiency of the pulse sequence depends on the t
2 delay due to the finite resolution of the
shaper, which can be corrected (see Figure S1). As noted above, many more than 3 waiting times can be collected
as the AWG card holds up to 10× more memory than what we utilized
in this study. Pump and probe beams are focused on the sample cell
using 90° parabola to create a spot size of ∼60 μm.
The probe beam is dispersed onto a 64-channel mercury cadmium telluride
(MCT) array detector using 75 g/mm gratings, giving a probe frequency
resolution to 4 cm–1. Each MCT element is read shot-to-shot
(JackHammer, from PhaseTech spectroscopy). 2D IR spectra are generated
by a mathematical Fourier transform along t
1 and an optical Fourier transform of the probe pulse to give the
frequency axes ωpump and ωprobe,
respectively.

Modeling of 2D IR Spectra

2D IR simulations of the
model oligomeric structure were performed using a MATLAB-based simulation
package, Coupled OScillator MOdel Spectrum Simulator (COSMOSS), which
is based on vibrational exciton theory. The local mode frequency was set to 1650 cm–1 for
the amide I transition, which primarily consists of the carbonyl stretch
and some C–N stretch and N–H bend. A combination of
the transition dipole coupling (TDC) model and Jansen coupling map
was used to calculate coupling constants. TDC was used for long-range
interactions, and for estimating short-range coupling, we used the
Jansen map. Diagonal disorders were neglected
in presented calculations. In this work, we show the representative
2D IR spectra measured from the most stable conformation obtained
from 2D/3D NMR and MD simulations.

Results

Waiting-Time-Resolved 2D IR Spectra Uncovers the N-Terminus
Helices of hIAPP Oligomers and New Cross-Peaks

The aggregation
of hIAPP was monitored continuously over 7 h (T
agg). The kinetics of hIAPP aggregation as monitored via the
presence of amyloid fibrils using the β-sheet feature (peak
I, described below) is shown in A. The kinetics are sigmoidal, typical of amyloid nucleation,
with a half-rise time of T
agg = 3.5 h,
and lag phase of T
agg = 2 h. Aggregation
is mostly complete by T
agg = 5 h. During
the lag phase, there exists an equilibrium between monomers and oligomers.

Shown in are representative spectra, at T
agg =
0, 2, and 6 h, for early lag phase, toward the end of lag phase, and
fibril spectra, respectively. B–D contains the 3 spectra corresponding to t
2 = 0, 0.6, and 1.2 ps at T
agg = 0 h. The spectrum at t
2 = 0 ps is broad, centered at 1645 cm–1 along the
ωpump axis (peaks A and B, in B) (Unless noted otherwise, peaks will be
referenced by their ωpump frequency and label. Table S1 lists the pump and probe frequencies
for each peak) and has no other clearly defined features. This spectrum
is typical of a congested spectrum that is dominated by random coil
structures. In contrast, the spectrum at t
2 = 1.2 ps, where signal from random coil mostly decayed due to faster
vibrational relaxation, is much better
defined (d),
with at least 4 clearly resolved and sharp features (labeled C, D,
E, and G).

Peaks C and D are consistent with the α-helix
of the oligomer
structure in C. We present the following evidence in support of this conclusion:
First, peaks C and D have a frequency which is very typical for a
helix. Second, with the increasing t
2, we see a decrease in intensity due to vibrational
relaxation, and a frequency shift (overlaid spectra and diagonal slices
are provided in Figure S2). The center
frequency of peak C is slightly blue-shifted (∼3 cm–1) compared to peak A (Figure S2). Similarly,
at the end of aggregation, peak K corresponding to the delocalized
mode in fibril as observed in previous studies, is red-shifted by ∼4 cm–1 compared
to the peak C (see Figure S3). Thus, peak
C neither represents random coil nor any fibril mode. Third, the narrow
line shape of peak C and D is consistent with an excitonic vibration,
i.e., a vibration delocalized over many coupled oscillators.
,
 Fourth, the feature arises from oligomeric helices, not monomeric,
which we conclude from a comparison to 3A-hIAPP given below. The narrow
line shapes suggest a homogeneous oligomer structure, although neither
the 2D IR nor the NMR data used to generate the oligomer structure
is size-selected. Additionally, the size
distribution does not appear to change during the lag phase, consistent
with a stable oligomer structure followed by a nucleating event. Thus, based on these observations, we conclude
that introducing a waiting time of 1.2 ps has decreased the spectral
congestion, leaving the spectral signature of a delocalized mode,
consistent with the N-terminus α-helices of the oligomer shown
in C.

The t
2 = 1.2 ps spectra also resolve
a second diagonal peak (peak E) and an off-diagonal peak (peak G)
both at ωpump = 1667 cm–1. Peaks
E and G are separated by 36 cm–1 along ωprobe. The off-diagonal peak (peak G) is not an overtone transition,
but a cross peak. 2D IR pulse sequences generate pairs of peaks with
opposite phases formed by fundamental and overtone transitions. Peak
pairs are separated by diagonal anharmonicity that creates an anharmonic
shift of about 16 cm–1 for amide I vibrations, which is far too small to explain the 36 cm–1 frequency difference between peaks E and G. Instead,
we conclude that the peak separation arises from the destructive interference
occurring at the position marked F*. The position of F* occurs where
a cross peak would be expected between peaks C and E, which we highlight
with the dashed box in D. Coupling between C and E-mode would produce a positive
cross peak that overlaps with the negative overtone of peak E, causing
destructive interference. Indeed, simulations using a 2 × 2 coupled
matrix shown in Figure S4 illustrate how
this type of interference can occur. Additionally, the corresponding
cross peak (peak H) below the diagonal, at ωpump =
1647 cm–1 shows aggregation kinetics similar to
peak E (see Figure S5). Thus, we conclude
that the α-helix mode at 1647 cm–1 is coupled
to the mode at 1667 cm–1. In a later section, we
use simulations and additional experiments to assign the 1667 cm–1 feature to the kink regions of the oligomers with
contributions from the disulfide region.

As aggregation proceeds
and fibrils form, as shown in E–G (T
agg = 2 h)
and H–J
(T
agg = 6 h), peaks A and B give way to
a peak pair at ωpump = 1620 cm–1 (peaks I and J). These peaks are created
by the parallel β-sheets of the amyloid fibrils and were used
to follow the aggregation kinetics in A. By 5 h, aggregation is complete, and all
features can be assigned to fibrils. While aggregation proceeds, the
helical peaks C and D disappear as the oligomer disappears. Peak E
becomes less intense, and its cross peak G to the helix is replaced
by the cross peak O with the β-sheet mode. Peak K appears at
ωpump = 1643 cm–1, which is assigned
to a delocalized mode of the fibril β-sheet as previously observed. The kinetics of peaks N and O are very similar
to the fibril kinetics (peak I), confirming that these features arise
from the fibril as well (Figure S5).

Thus, introducing a waiting time into the 2D IR pulse sequence
decreases spectral congestion, revealing the α-helical mode
of the oligomer (peaks C and D) and a high-frequency mode (peak E)
that is coupled to the helix (peaks G and H).

2D IR Spectra of 3A-hIAPP Are Nearly Identical to hIAPP

Shown in are
the results from experiments performed on 3A-hIAPP analogous to those
on hIAPP shown in . The alanine substitutions placed into hIAPP to create 3A-hIAPP
slow fibrillization so that it takes >30 h for 3A-hIAPP to fully
aggregate
(A). Considering
the extended aggregation time, we show representative 2D IR spectra
at T
agg values of 0, 18, and 35 h. These
points lie at the very beginning, at the end of the lag phase, and
after the fibrils have fully formed, respectively, analogous to the
time points shown for hIAPP but on a slower time scale.

The spectra of 3A-hIAPP are remarkably similar
to hIAPP. At T
agg = 0 h and t
2 = 1.2 ps, when 3A-hIAPP is in the oligomeric form, peaks
C, D, E,
and G are clearly observed. The relative intensities and line widths
are also nearly identical (Figure S6).
Thus, the 3A-hIAPP oligomer has the same secondary structure as that
of hIAPP to within the resolution of our experiments. More details
are given below, but that means that the N-terminal α-helix
and high-frequency mode at ωpump = 1667 cm–1, which we later assign to the kink and loop regions, as given in , are structurally
correct. Indeed, it was previously established that 3A-hIAPP exists
during the lag phase almost entirely in the oligomer structure and
so the strong similarity to hIAPP indicates that the t
2 = 1.2 ps waiting-time spectrum eliminates the monomeric
features from the spectra, consistent with their largely disordered
structure.

We are not particularly
interested in the structure of the fibrils
because the alanine substitutions were chosen to hinder aggregation,
and thus, they are expected to form nonphysiological fibrils. Alanine
substitutions replaced bulky side chains to reduce the hydrophobic
contacts and alter the steric zippers that are thought to stabilize
fibrils. Even a single-point mutation
in hIAPP has been shown to produce alternate fibril polymorphs. Nonetheless, we include the 2D IR spectra of
the fibrils to help highlight the uniqueness of the oligomer features,
the effectiveness of sharpening the spectra using waiting-time delays,
and how the spectra are utilized to monitor fibrillization.

Kink Contributes to the High-Frequency Features of the Oligomers

In this section, we systematically investigate and discuss each
of these possible assignments for peaks E and G. These two features
lie at ωpump = 1667 cm–1, which
is at the high end of typical values observed for amide I band of
proteins. Possible origins for features in this range include (a)
side-chain absorbances, (b) the FGAILS β-sheet, (c) the E-mode
of the α-helices, and (d) regions of the oligomer with nontypical
secondary structures that have large couplings. We note that peak
E is strongly coupled to the α-helix when in the oligomeric
form, as evidence by the F* region in D and D, as discussed
above. In what follows, we walk through our reasoning supported by
experiments and simulations that conclude that peaks E and G are created
by nontypical secondary structures in the kink and disulfide-loop
regions.

We reached this conclusion from the following arguments:

- (a)There are no side-chain absorbances
in this high-frequency region in a deuterated solvent. In H2O buffer, asparagine, arginine, and glutamine side chains absorb
near 1667 cm–1, but those side chains absorb at
about 1620 cm–1 in D2O, the solvent used
in these experiments.
,,
 Even when these side chains are coupled as occurs, for example,
when side-chain “ladders” are formed in amyloid fibrils
like in polyQ, they only reach 1645 cm–1. Acidic side chains, aspartic acid and glutamic acid,
are not present in hIAPP or 3A-hIAPP (sequence shown in A).
- (b)Small β-sheets are known to
absorb in this frequency range. Oligomers
have a small β-sheet that spans residue FGAILS. Thus, to test
if the FGAILS β-sheet is the source of these features, we synthesized
hIAPP with 13C18O labels at residues FGA and
performed analogous aggregation experiments. The data are shown in Figure S7. The signal from the labeled FGA residues
is shifted to 1570 cm–1 (peaks P and Q), but peaks
E and G are unchanged and thus not created by the β-sheet. We
also note that peaks C and D are also unchanged, consistent with the
assignment of these two peaks to the helices.
- (c)The E-mode of α-helices are
much weaker in intensity and lie higher in frequency compared to the
A-mode discussed above.
,
 The frequency difference
between the two modes can vary anywhere from a few cm–1 up to 10 cm–1 in ideal helices, oftentimes these
two modes are indistinguishable from each other.
,
 In any case, observed frequency is 15 cm–1 higher
than the A-mode with comparable intensity. Thus, we rule out this
possible assignment.
- (d)That leaves noncanonical secondary
structures as the remaining assignment. To that end, we systematically
simulated portions of the oligomer structure looking for high-energy
features (simulation results from individual segments are shown in Figure S8). We identified three regions that
could explain the high frequency. First, across the entire C-terminus,
from residues Phe-23 to Tyr-37, the only location that created a higher
frequency feature was Val-32 and Gly-33 (Figure S8f). To experimentally test this region, we synthesized and
measured 13C18O-labeled hIAPP at Val-32 and
Gly-33, which lie in the middle of the C-terminus which would disrupt
the expected nearest neighbor coupling. Those labels did not alter
peaks E and G (Figure S9). Moreover, these
residues are not coupled to the helices and so do not explain the
cross-peaks G and H. Thus, the C-terminal region is not responsible
for the 1667 cm–1 peaks.

Shown in are the simulations of the two other regions that produce
high-frequency
features, namely, the kink and disulfide-loop regions. Unlike Val-32
and Gly-33, these spectra exhibit cross-peaks to the α-helix,
explained Peak E is created by dihedral angles that produce large
positive coupling constants. Indeed, it is well established that kinks
and turns have high-frequency modes around 1670 cm–1.

To experimentally tested the kink region, we measured
spectra of
S20G hIAPP. Glycine mutations are common in regions that connect secondary
structure elements because glycine is more flexible, so it is known
to stabilizes kinks or turns. S20G is
a hereditary mutation in hIAPP that is associated with early-onset
type 2 diabetes. The 2D IR spectrum of
S20G hIAPP exhibits the same helix peaks C and D and the same high-frequency
peaks E and G. However, the intensity of peaks E and G are much larger,
eclipsing the α-helix peak (B), whereas in hIAPP and 3A-hIAPP, peaks
E and G were half as intense as the α-helix peak (Figure S6). Thus, peaks E and G at 1667 cm–1 are definitively assigned to the kink region.

The disulfide-loop region could not be directly
tested. Ala-5 is
the only residue in that region that is straightforward to isotope-label,
but labeling a single isotope is usually insufficient to disrupt secondary
structure couplings. We also synthesized C2S and C7S hIAPP to eliminate
the disulfide bond, but aggregation occurred within a few minutes,
similar to other reported studies.
,
 Therefore,
we could not observe the oligomeric state. Thus, we can definitively
assign peaks E and G at 1667 cm–1 to the kink region
but cannot eliminate the possibility that residues within the disulfide
bond also contribute.

Discussion

This paper reports data collected with a
modified 2D IR spectrometer
that incremented waiting-time delays optically rather than mechanically.
This technical development allowed us to intertwine several pulse
sequences so that multiple 2D IR spectra can be collected nearly simultaneously.
Rapid data collection made it possible to collect 2D IR spectra with
several waiting time while hIAPP aggregates, thereby measuring amyloid
oligomers before they fibrillize. Spectra with a waiting time of t
2 = 1.2 ps resolved the α-helix in the
oligomers and uncovered a high-frequency feature that we assigned
to the kink region connecting the α-helix to the FGAILS β-sheet.
The spectra of hIAPP and 3A-hIAPP are nearly identical. These results
have important implications for the structural model of hIAPP oligomers
and for the physics of waiting-time experiments.

This study
strengthens the argument that 3A-hIAPP is a good structural
model for the hIAPP oligomers. The 2D IR spectra of 3A-hIAPP and hIAPP
during the lag time are nearly identical for all waiting times. The
helix frequencies lie within 3 cm–1 of each other
(see Figure S10 for overlap of hIAPP and
3A-hIAPP 2D IR spectra), indicating that the length of the 3A-helix
is very similar or identical to that of hIAPP. Simulations shortening
the helix by just 2 residues create a 3 cm–1 frequency
shift that is outside the experimental difference (Figure S11). That is important information, because alanine
mutations are known to stabilize helices and the alanine substitutions
at residues Leu-12 and Phe-15 in 3A-hIAPP reside in the helix. Thus,
our data are evidence that those two alanine substitutions do not
artificially elongate the helix.

The 2D IR spectra of 3A-hIAPP
and hIAPP also exhibit the same high-frequency
feature at 1667 cm–1. We established that the oligomeric
kink residues contribute to this feature. The oligomeric kink region
is adjacent to the helix and spans part of the critical 20–29
residue, which is a critical determinant of type 2 diabetes susceptibility.
,
 A hereditary mutation having Gly-20 in place of Ser-20 shows faster
aggregation and higher cytotoxicity. Thus,
knowing the structure of this region is critical. Our data support
the structural model of hIAPP generated from 3A that is shown in C as being a rigorous
construct for the N-terminal helices and adjacent turn and possibly
loop regions.

It is also interesting to find that the waiting-time
experiments
resolve the helical eigenstates of the oligomers. Previously, we reported
that amyloid fibrils of hIAPP exhibited sharp β-sheet features
at t
2 = 1.0 ps which is reproduced here. We attributed that observation to longer lifetimes
of amyloid β-sheets than disordered protein regions. Now seeing
a similar observation but for helices, means that 2D IR spectra with t
2 > 1.2 ps will be a useful tool to study
decongesting
spectra. There are two factors that can help explain the physics behind
longer lifetimes of different hIAPP features. One factor is that the
lifetimes of random coil regions of proteins are shorter than helices
and β-sheets because they are more strongly impacted by solvation
dynamics. Usually, disordered regions
of proteins have more solvent exposure, which changes the vibrational
dynamics as observed in experiments and simulations.
,
 Lifetimes are more difficult to calculate than line shapes, but
it would reason that frequency fluctuations would also enable faster
intra- and intermolecular vibrational relaxation. That being said,
the oligomer is small enough that all residues will be solvent exposed
to some degree, which may be why the FGAILS β-sheet, which is
small, is not enhanced in the waiting-time experiments here. The other
factor is that excitonically delocalized vibrations might have intrinsically
longer lifetimes than localized modes. Delocalization over many eigenstates might decrease overlap with
any single state and cause the lifetimes to be longer. Hence, a fast
lifetime of any single local mode would collapse the delocalized wave
function. We do not believe that there is a compilation of experimental
data in the literature that is sufficient to address these questions
for proteins nor do we believe that there is a theoretical study that
addresses this question directly. It would be useful to have a systematic
understanding of the origins. Nonetheless, it is clear from our results
that waiting time delays is a very useful tool for decreasing congestion
and studying hidden structural features during hIAPP kinetics.

Conclusions

The decongested 2D IR spectra of the hIAPP
reported here are identical
to an alanine-substituted version called 3A-hIAPP. Two of the alanine
substitutions were made at the N-terminus, so as not to perturb potential
helical structure, but it was unknown whether the alanine substitutions
altered the helicity of the N-terminus. By generating the waiting
time in our 2D IR pulse sequences with a pulse shaper, we were able
to measure back-to-back waiting-time 2D IR spectra during aggregation.
At waiting-time delays of >1.2 ps, the spectra become much better
resolved because the random coil features that caused spectral overlap
and congestion in the 2D IR spectra have decayed, leaving the excitonic
features created by well-defined secondary and tertiary structures.
The spectra resolve the helix and a high-frequency feature created
by the disulfide-loop and the kink residues. These features are identical
in hIAPP and 3A-hIAPP, supporting the physiological relevance of the
atomic structure generated from 3A-hIAPP. With these experiments,
we now know that both the C- and N-terminal regions are well represented
by 3A-hIAPP. That is important because 3A-hIAPP is based on a structural
basis for understanding the long-known correlation between the sequence
and disease.

Besides validating our current structural model
of hIAPP, the experiments
presented here complete a procedure to generate new structural models
of hIAPP. The workflow is as follows. A promising variant of hIAPP
is identified from ThT or other kinetics measurements that has a lag
time of at least 15 h at hundreds of micromolar concentrations, necessary
for NMR spectroscopy and represent the physiological concentrations
of hIAPP when it is secreted from the pancreatic islets.
,
 2D IR spectroscopy is performed to test the similarity of the variant
oligomer to the wild-type oligomer in as many locations as practically
possible. For example, we previously tested for the critical FGAILS
β-sheet and here, we tested for
the N-terminal helices, and the kink and possibly the disulfide-loop
residues. Biochemical assays should also be performed, including cytotoxicity.
If all of those pass the test, then 2D/3D NMR experiments
along with constrained molecular dynamics can be used to produce the
atomic structure. With this procedure now in place, it can be applied
to obtain models for other amyloid oligomers. Similarly, IAPP oligomers
in other diabetic mammals, or of the hereditary mutant S20G hIAPP
in humans. Solving a series of IAPP structures could enable new insights
into the atomic features that ultimately impact β-cell loss
and insulin loss in type 2 diabetes.
