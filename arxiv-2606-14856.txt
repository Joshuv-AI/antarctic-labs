Fully nonlinear oblique transmission problems: Well-posedness
Iñigo U. Erneta

Abstract
. We introduce elliptic transmission problems involving fully nonlinear equations in both the interior bulk equations and the interface transmission condition. In contrast to all previous works, our transmission condition depends on both the normal and tangential derivatives of the solution from each side of the interface. Our main result establishes the existence and uniqueness of viscosity solutions for constant-coefficient oblique transmission problems with flat interfaces.



1.
Introduction

Transmission problems provide a general mathematical framework to describe phenomena across heterogeneous domains.
Specific examples from the applied sciences include
the modeling of composite materials in Physics, the description of diffusion across cell membranes in Biology, or regime-switching models in Economics.
In each of these problems, the configuration space is made up of several components with different properties.
While a variational “divergence-form” theory for such problems is by now classical, an equally satisfactory non-variational approach has yet to be established.
The present paper develops a methodology aimed at filling this longstanding gap.

1.1.
Framework and motivation

Given smooth bounded open connected sets
\Omega^{+}\subsetneq\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define
\Omega^{-}:=\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega^{+}}
as the complement of
\overline{\Omega^{+}}
inside
\Omega
.
The two
phases

\Omega^{\pm}
are separated by the
interface

\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{+}\cap\Omega
, which is a smooth hypersurface, and we have the partition
\Omega=\Omega^{+}\cup\Gamma\cup\Omega^{-}
.
For “global” functions
u\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, we use the superscript notation

u^{\pm}:=u\chi_{\Omega^{\pm}\cup\Gamma}

to denote the restrictions to each phase, up to the interface.

We are interested in the transmission problem

(1.1)

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u,x)=f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }\Omega^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\nabla u^{-},x)=g(x)&\text{ on }\Gamma,\\
u^{+}=u^{-}&\text{ on }\Gamma,\\
u=\phi&\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
F^{\pm}
and
G
are (nonlinear) operators,
f^{\pm}
and
g
are functions in
\Omega^{\pm}
and
\Gamma
, respectively, and
\phi
is a boundary datum on
\partial\Omega
.
The second order equations in
\Omega^{\pm}
are called
bulk equations
, while the first order equation on
\Gamma
is known in the literature as a
transmission condition
.
The second equation on
\Gamma
simply expresses the continuity of
u
across the interface and will be assumed implicitly throughout the text.

Problem (
1.1
) arises naturally when studying stochastic processes across multiple regimes.
As a guiding example, consider a diffusion process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
in the two-phase domain
\Omega
.
Suppose that, upon reaching the interface
\Gamma
, the process can undergo either of the following behaviors:

•

reflect back into the phase from which it came;

•

transmit across the interface into the adjacent phase;

•

propagate along the interface in a prescribed tangential direction.

Let
\nu
denote the unit normal vector to the interface
\Gamma
, pointing into
\Omega^{+}
.
Assume that a payoff
\phi
is received when the process
X_{t}
exits the domain through
\partial\Omega
, at which point the process is stopped.
Then, at least heuristically, the expected payoff
u(x)
associated with a process starting at
x\in\overline{\Omega}
should solve (
1.1
), together with a linear transmission condition

(1.2)

\gamma_{\nu}^{+}\partial_{\nu}u^{+}-\gamma_{\nu}^{-}\partial_{\nu}u^{-}+\gamma_{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}u=0\quad\text{ on }\Gamma,

where the coefficients can be interpreted as follows:

•

\gamma_{\nu}^{+}>0
is the probability of moving into
\Omega^{+}
in the normal direction
\nu
;

•

\gamma_{\nu}^{-}>0
is the probability of moving into
\Omega^{-}
in the normal direction
-\nu
;

•

\gamma_{\Gamma}
is a tangent vector to the hypersurface
\Gamma
and
|\gamma_{\Gamma}|=1-\gamma_{\nu}^{+}-\gamma_{\nu}^{-}
is the probability of sliding along
\Gamma
in the tangential direction
\gamma_{\Gamma}
.

Note here that since
u
is continuous across the interface, assuming
u^{+}
and
u^{-}
to be differentiable up to
\Gamma
, the tangential gradient
\nabla_{\Gamma}u:=\nabla[u|_{\Gamma}]
is well defined and independent of the restriction.

It is more convenient to instead write the linear transmission (
1.2
) in the form

(1.3)

\gamma^{+}\cdot\nabla u^{+}-\gamma^{-}\cdot\nabla u^{-}=0\quad\text{ on }\Gamma,

where
\gamma^{\pm}=\gamma^{\pm}(x)
are vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with

(1.4)

\gamma^{\pm}\cdot\nu>0\quad\text{ on }\Gamma.

Thus, the transmission condition (
1.3
) can be interpreted as a coupling of oblique derivatives from each side of
\Gamma
.
Assumption (
1.4
) is a natural ellipticity condition which prevents the process from degenerating to a lower dimensional motion on the interface.

Our model (
1.1
) encompasses most problems of interest, such as:

•

Expected payoff
.
Assuming a linear anisotropic diffusion on each phase, the expected payoff satisfies the linear transmission problem

\begin{cases}\operatorname{Tr}(A^{\pm}(x)D^{2}u)=f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }\Omega^{\pm},\\
\gamma^{+}(x)\cdot\nabla u^{+}-\gamma^{-}(x)\cdot\nabla u^{-}=g(x)&\text{ on }\Gamma,\end{cases}

where
f^{\pm}
is the running cost of remaining in
\Omega^{\pm}
, while
g
is the “toll” for crossing
\Gamma
.

•

Stochastic control
.
For a one-parameter family of stochastic processes
\{X_{t}^{\alpha}\}
, the maximum expected gain when choosing a control for
\alpha
will satisfy the linear programming or Bellman-type equations

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha}\,\Big[\operatorname{Tr}(A_{\alpha}^{\pm}(x)D^{2}u)-f_{\alpha}^{\pm}(x)\Big]=0&\text{ in }\Omega^{\pm},\\
\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha}\,\Big[\gamma^{+}_{\alpha}(x)\cdot\nabla u^{+}-\gamma^{-}_{\alpha}(x)\cdot\nabla u^{-}-g_{\alpha}(x)\Big]=0&\text{ on }\Gamma.\end{cases}

•

Differential games
.
For two-player games with strategies described by two-parameter processes
\{X^{\alpha\beta}_{t}\}
, the optimal payoff for a player satisfies the Isaacs-type equations

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\inf_{\beta}\sup_{\alpha}\,\left[\operatorname{Tr}(A_{\alpha\beta}^{\pm}(x)D^{2}u)-f_{\alpha\beta}(x)\right]=0&\text{ in }\Omega^{\pm},\\
\displaystyle\inf_{\beta}\sup_{\alpha}\,\left[\gamma^{+}_{\alpha\beta}(x)\cdot\nabla u^{+}-\gamma^{-}_{\alpha\beta}(x)\cdot\nabla u^{-}-g_{\alpha\beta}(x)\right]=0&\text{ on }\Gamma.\end{cases}

All linear operators featured in the nonlinear problems above should satisfy appropriate ellipticity assumptions.
These conditions involve three natural ellipticity constants:

0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
and
\mu\geq 0
.
Namely, the symmetric matrices
A^{\pm}(x)
are assumed to be uniformly elliptic with

\lambda I\leq A^{\pm}(x)\leq\Lambda I\qquad\text{ for }x\in\Omega^{\pm},

while the transmission coefficient vectors
\gamma^{\pm}(x)
are
uniformly oblique
, i.e., they have uniformly elliptic normal components

\lambda\leq\gamma^{\pm}_{\nu}(x):=\gamma^{\pm}(x)\cdot\nu(x)\leq\Lambda\qquad\text{ for }x\in\Gamma,

and bounded tangential ones

|\gamma^{\pm}(x)-\gamma^{\pm}_{\nu}(x)\nu(x)|\leq\mu\qquad\text{ for }x\in\Gamma.

Our nonlinear operators
G
satisfy analogous ellipticity conditions, as explained in Section
2
.

From the point of view of applications, it is also interesting to allow obliqueness to degenerate on one side of the interface.
Namely, we will consider nonlinear transmission operators of the form

G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-},x)

where
0\leq\theta\leq 1
is a constant
interpolation parameter
.
Note that the transmission condition on
u^{-}
seems to disappear when
\theta
vanishes.
Somewhat surprisingly, our results and estimates below are all independent of this parameter.
We give the intuition behind this phenomenon in Section
2
(see Proposition
2.17
and Remark
2.18
).

1.2.
State of the art

Our present work is concerned with the well-posedness of the above models, a fundamental question dating back to the seminal work of Picone
[
14
]
.

The mathematical analysis of transmission problems has a rich history, particularly within the framework of linear divergence-form operators.
The classical variational theory was initiated by J.-L. Lions
[
12
]
, where solutions are understood in the weak sense via energy formulations.
In variational configurations, the transmission condition across the interface involves matching the conormal derivatives

A^{+}(x)\nabla u^{+}\cdot\nu-A^{-}(x)\nabla u^{-}\cdot\nu=g(x)\quad\text{on }\Gamma,

which arises naturally from integration by parts.
For further details on variational problems, we refer the reader to the monograph of Borsuk
[
1
]
and the references therein.

In the non-variational setting, the study of boundary value problems with oblique derivatives has its modern roots in the viscosity theory for the Neumann problem.
The well-posedness for non-divergence form equations with oblique boundary conditions was established by Lieberman
[
10
]
by adapting Perron’s method; see also
[
9
]
and compare with the work of Li-Zhang
[
11
]
on fully nonlinear equations.

The application of viscosity solutions to transmission problems, however, is a more recent development.
To the best of our knowledge, there are only three previous works dealing with this subject.
Motivated by free boundary problems, De Silva-Ferrari-Salsa
[
5
,
6
]
pioneered this direction by establishing the viscosity framework.
Subsequently, Soria Carro-Stinga
[
15
]
developed an existence and uniqueness theory of viscosity solutions.
All these works share a fundamental limitation: the transmission operator
G
is assumed to be linear and independent of the tangential derivatives of the solution.

The results of our paper constitute the first treatment of transmission problems with genuinely oblique interface conditions, where the transmission operator depends nonlinearly on both the normal and tangential components of the gradient on each side of the interface.
This setting introduces significant geometric and analytical challenges, as the interface condition can no longer be simplified or decoupled from the bulk equations through standard barrier arguments.

1.3.
Main result

In this first paper, we focus on the case when
\Gamma
is flat, while curved interfaces will be treated in a future work.
It is convenient to introduce a special notation for the flat case.
Below, we write
B_{R}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon|x|<R\}
for the ball of radius
R>0
centered at the origin.
The half-balls are denoted by

B_{R}^{+}:=B_{R}\cap\{x_{n}>0\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad B_{R}^{-}:=B_{R}\cap\{x_{n}<0\},

while the associated flat interface is written

T_{R}:=B_{R}\cap\{x_{n}=0\}.

Our main result establishes the well-posedness of the Dirichlet problem for constant-coefficient transmission problems.
For the exact definition of ellipticity and viscosity solution, see Section
2
:

Theorem 1.1

(Existence and uniqueness)
.

Let
\{F^{\pm},G\}
be constant-coefficient uniformly elliptic operators.
Let
f^{\pm}\in C(\overline{B}_{1}^{\pm})
,
g\in C(\overline{T}_{1})
, and
\phi\in C(\partial B_{1})
.
Let
0\leq\theta\leq 1
be a constant.

Then there exists a unique viscosity solution
u\in C(\overline{B}_{1})
to the transmission problem

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u)=f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-})=g(x)&\text{ on }T_{1},\\
u=\phi&\text{ on }\partial B_{1}.\end{cases}

We defer regularity questions to our companion paper
[
7
]
, where we obtain optimal piecewise
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity of viscosity solutions.
The above existence and uniqueness result is crucial for the regularity theory of variable-coefficient problems.
There, we use Theorem
1.1
to construct barriers satisfying suitable constant-coefficient inhomogeneous transmission problems, which play a key role in the perturbation theory from
[
7
]
.

We obtain the unique viscosity solution by Perron’s method, adapting the approach of Soria Carro-Stinga
[
15
, Section 4]
.
This technique requires three basic ingredients: an Alexandrov-Bakelman-Pucci (ABP) maximum principle, a comparison principle for semicontinuous functions, and the construction of appropriate barriers.

The classical ABP estimate follows by a well-known geometric argument involving linear polynomials that touch the solutions from one side.
For non-oblique transmission problems, the proof of the corresponding estimate can be reduced to the classical one by subtracting a simple explicit barrier.
By contrast, our proof of the ABP for genuinely oblique transmission problems is more involved and requires a new argument based on piecewise linear polynomials (see Section
3
).

A comparison principle for non-oblique transmission problems had already appeared, first for continuous functions in
[
5
,
6
]
, and then for semicontinuous ones in
[
15
]
.
Our proof for oblique problems follows the same strategy, using a delicate regularization procedure combined with the ABP estimate and the punctual boundary regularity for classical fully nonlinear equations in
[
8
]
.
We point out that some additional care is required at one technical point of the original argument (see Remark
4.4
).
Once the comparison principle is established, uniqueness follows.

We need barriers to show that the boundary condition is attained.
For this, we seek inspiration in classical barriers for the oblique derivative problem, which can be understood as a transmission problem with
\theta=0
(see Proposition
2.17
).
The most delicate point corresponds to those boundary points in the closure of the interface.
Here, we construct piecewise smooth barriers that extend Lieberman’s
[
9
]
barrier based on earlier work of Miller
[
13
]
.
The easier case away from the interface can be treated by modifying a simple barrier of Li-Zhang
[
11
]
.

To conclude, we remark that we do not yet have an existence and uniqueness result analogous to Theorem
1.1
for variable-coefficient transmission problems.
The only missing ingredient is a comparison principle, as our regularization procedure does not seem to extend to variable coefficients.
Such a result might perhaps be obtained by adapting the method of doubling variables described in the notes of Crandall-Ishii-Lions
[
4
]
; however, we were unable to carry this out.
We refer the reader to
[
4
]
and the references therein for further background.

1.4.
Notation

In general, given an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote its bulk phases by

\Omega^{+}=\Omega\cap\{x_{n}>0\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\Omega^{-}=\Omega\cap\{x_{n}<0\},

and its flat interface by

T=\Omega\cap\{x_{n}=0\}=\partial\Omega^{\pm}\cap\Omega.

For
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
R>0
, we denote the open balls in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

B_{R}(x_{0})=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon|x-x_{0}|<R\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad B_{R}=B_{R}(0).

When
x_{0}\in T
, we also define the open balls on the interface as

T_{R}(x_{0})=B_{R}(x_{0})\cap\{x_{n}=0\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad T_{R}=T_{R}(0).

For a continuous function
u
in
\Omega
, we define its restrictions to each phase, up to the interface, by

u^{+}:=u\chi_{\overline{\Omega}^{+}\cap\Omega}=u\chi_{\Omega^{+}\cup T}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad u^{-}:=u\chi_{\overline{\Omega}^{-}\cap\Omega}=u\chi_{\Omega^{-}\cup T},

where
\chi_{E}
is the characteristic function of the set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The positive and negative parts of
u
are

u_{+}:=\max\{u,0\}\qquad\text{and }\qquad u_{-}:=\max\{-u,0\}.

Note our use of superscripts for restrictions, while subscripts denote the positive and negative parts.

We write vectors in terms of tangential and normal components as
x=(x^{\prime},x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
.

Derivatives are written as subscripts, e.g.,
u_{x_{i}}
,
u_{x_{i}x_{j}}
, and so on.
We also use the gradient vector
\nabla u=(u_{x_{1}},\ldots,u_{x_{n}})

and the tangential gradient
\nabla^{\prime}u=(u_{x_{1}},\ldots,u_{x_{n-1}},0)
.

The space of symmetric
n\times n
matrices is denoted
\mathcal{S}_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
The set of symmetric matrices
A\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
satisfying
\lambda I\leq A\leq\Lambda I
is written
\mathcal{A}_{\lambda,\Lambda}
.
For matrices
N\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we use the operator norm

\|N\|=\sup_{x\in B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}}|Nx|.

The set of upper semicontinuous (u.s.c.) functions
u\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is denoted by
USC(\Omega)
,
and the set of lower semicontinuous (l.s.c.) ones by
LSC(\Omega)
.

A constant is
universal
if it depends only on
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
, and
\mu
.
We note in particular that a universal constant does not depend on the interpolation parameter
0\leq\theta\leq 1
.

In Section

Section

In Section

2.
Viscosity solutions to transmission problems

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set.
For a constant
0\leq\theta\leq 1
, we consider the transmission problem

(2.1)

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u,x)=f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }\Omega^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-},x)=g(x)&\text{ on }T,\end{cases}

where the operators
F^{\pm}
and
G
are
uniformly elliptic
, in the sense that there are constants
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
and
\mu\geq 0
such that:

•

For any symmetric matrices
M
and
N
, with
N
nonnegative definite, we have that

\lambda\|N\|\leq F^{\pm}(M+N,x)-F^{\pm}(M,x)\leq\Lambda\|N\|;

•

For any vectors
\xi^{\pm}
and
\eta^{\pm}=(\eta^{\prime\pm},\eta_{n}^{\pm})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
, with
\eta_{n}^{\pm}\geq 0
, we have that

\lambda\eta^{+}_{n}-\Lambda\eta^{-}_{n}-\mu(|\eta^{\prime+}|+|\eta^{\prime-}|)\leq G(\xi^{+}+\eta^{+},\xi^{-}+\eta^{-},x)-G(\xi^{+},\xi^{-},x)\leq\Lambda\eta^{+}_{n}-\lambda\eta^{-}_{n}+\mu(|\eta^{\prime+}|+|\eta^{\prime-}|).

Remark 2.1
.

Note that the function
G(\xi^{+},\xi^{-},x)
is increasing in
\xi_{n}^{+}
and decreasing in
\xi_{n}^{-}
.

Throughout the paper, we always assume that
F^{\pm}(M,\cdot)
and
f^{\pm}
are continuous up to the interface, on
\Omega^{\pm}\cup T=\overline{\Omega}^{\pm}\cap\Omega
.
We also assume that
G(\xi^{+},\xi^{-},\cdot)
and
g
are continuous on
T
.

In Section
5
, we construct solutions to (
2.1
) via Perron’s method.
This approach naturally leads to solutions in the viscosity sense, which are only continuous a priori.
When applying this strategy, it is also necessary to consider viscosity sub- and supersolutions to (
2.1
) defined for merely semicontinuous functions.

A continuous function
\varphi

touches

u\in USC(\Omega)
from above
(resp.
u\in LSC(\Omega)

from below
) at a point
x_{0}\in\Omega
if
\varphi(x_{0})=u(x_{0})
and

\varphi(x)\geq u(x)\qquad\text{(resp. }\varphi(x)\leq u(x))

for all
x
in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
.
It touches
strictly
if the inequalities are strict for
x\neq x_{0}
.

Definition 2.2

(Admissible function)
.

We say that
\varphi\in C(\Omega)
is
admissible
if its restrictions
\varphi^{\pm}
are
C^{2}
in the interior and
C^{1,1}
up to the interface, i.e., if

\varphi^{\pm}\in C^{2}(\Omega^{\pm})\cap C^{1,1}(\Omega^{\pm}\cup T).

Note in particular that the tangential gradients match on the interface, i.e.,
\nabla^{\prime}\varphi^{+}=\nabla^{\prime}\varphi^{-}
on
T
.

This class of piecewise
C^{1,1}
test functions yields the following notion of viscosity solution:

Definition 2.3

(Viscosity solutions)
.

We say that
u\in USC(\Omega)
(resp.
u\in LSC(\Omega)
) is a
viscosity subsolution
(resp.
supersolution
) to (
2.1
) if whenever an admissible function
\varphi
touches
u
from above (resp. below) at
x_{0}\in\Omega
and:

•

x_{0}\in\Omega^{\pm}
, then

F^{\pm}(D^{2}\varphi(x_{0}),x_{0})\geq f^{\pm}(x_{0})\qquad(\text{resp. }F^{\pm}(D^{2}\varphi(x_{0}),x_{0})\leq f^{\pm}(x_{0}));

•

x_{0}\in T
, then

G(\nabla\varphi^{+}(x_{0}),\theta\nabla\varphi^{-}(x_{0}),x_{0})\geq g(x_{0})\qquad\text{(resp. }G(\nabla\varphi^{+}(x_{0}),\theta\nabla\varphi^{-}(x_{0}),x_{0})\leq g(x_{0})).

We say that
u\in C(\Omega)
is a
viscosity solution
to (
2.1
) if it is both a viscosity subsolution and supersolution.

Remark 2.4
.

In the definition above, we may replace admissible functions by
piecewise quadratic polynomials
, i.e., continuous functions
P
such that each restriction
P^{\pm}
is a second order polynomial.
Indeed, suppose that an admissible function
\varphi
touches
u
from (say) above at
x_{0}\in T
.
For
x\in\Omega^{\pm}
close to
x_{0}
, we have that

\begin{split}\varphi(x)&=\varphi(x_{0})+\nabla\varphi^{\pm}(x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})+\int_{0}^{1}\big[\nabla\varphi^{\pm}((1-t)x_{0}+tx)-\nabla\varphi^{\pm}(x_{0})\big]\cdot(x-x_{0})dt\\
&\qquad\leq\varphi(x_{0})+\nabla\varphi^{\pm}(x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})+C|x-x_{0}|^{2}=:P(x),\end{split}

where
C>0
depends on the Lipschitz seminorms of
\nabla\varphi^{\pm}
.
The piecewise quadratic polynomial
P
satisfies
\nabla P^{\pm}(x_{0})=\nabla\varphi^{\pm}(x_{0})
and touches
u
from above at
x_{0}
, whence the equivalence follows.

Remark 2.5
.

In the subsolution (resp. supersolution) condition, instead of touching
u
from above (resp. below), it is equivalent to impose that the inequality holds whenever
u-\varphi
has a local maximum (resp. minimum) at
x_{0}\in\Omega
.

The advantage of considering test functions with bounded second derivatives lies in the following technical tool, used throughout the paper:

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
In the subsolution (resp. supersolution) condition at the interface, we may assume that
the piecewise quadratic polynomial
P
touches
u
strictly and additionally satisfies

F^{\pm}(D^{2}P,x_{0})<-\varepsilon^{-1}\qquad\text{(resp. }F^{\pm}(D^{2}P,x_{0})>\varepsilon^{-1}\text{)}.

Proof.

We only check the subsolution property, the supersolution one being analogous.
Let
P
be a piecewise quadratic polynomial touching
u
from above at
x_{0}\in T
.
By translation, we may assume that
x_{0}=0
.

For each small
\delta>0
, fix
C=C(\delta)>0
large to be chosen below.
Consider the modified piecewise quadratic polynomial

\overline{P}(x):=P(x)+\delta|x_{n}|-\frac{1}{2}Cx_{n}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\delta|x^{\prime}|^{2},

and note that
\overline{P}
touches
u
strictly from above at
0
, in a smaller neighborhood.

A computation at
0
shows that for all
i,j<n
, we have

\overline{P}^{\pm}_{x_{i}}(0)=P^{\pm}_{x_{i}}(0),\qquad\overline{P}_{x_{n}}^{\pm}(0)=P^{\pm}_{x_{n}}(0)\pm\delta,

\overline{P}_{x_{i}x_{j}}=P_{x_{i}x_{j}}+\delta\cdot\delta_{ij},\qquad\overline{P}^{\pm}_{x_{i}x_{n}}=P^{\pm}_{x_{i}x_{n}},\qquad\overline{P}^{\pm}_{x_{n}x_{n}}=P^{\pm}_{x_{n}x_{n}}-C,

whence by uniform ellipticity

F^{\pm}(D^{2}\overline{P}^{\pm},0)\leq F^{\pm}(D^{2}P^{\pm},0)-\lambda C+\Lambda\delta<-\varepsilon^{-1}

by choosing
C>0
sufficiently large depending on
\varepsilon
,
\delta
, and
F^{\pm}(D^{2}P^{\pm},0)
.

If
\overline{P}
satisfies the transmission condition at
0
, then by uniform ellipticity of
G
we deduce

g(0)\leq G(\nabla\overline{P}^{+}(0),\theta\nabla\overline{P}^{-}(0),0)\leq G(\nabla P^{+}(0),\theta\nabla P^{-}(0),0)+(1+\theta)\delta\Lambda,

and letting
\delta\downarrow 0
we recover the condition on
P
.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

An argument similar to the above shows that the transmission condition is equivalent to the alternative:
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-},x)=g(x)
or
F^{\pm}(D^{2}u,x)=f^{\pm}(x)
on
T
.

Our viscosity solutions satisfy the usual closedness property:

Proposition 2.8

(Closedness)
.

Let
\{F_{k}^{\pm}
,
G_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
be continuous operators on
\Omega^{\pm}\cup T
and
T
, respectively, with ellipticity constants
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
and
\mu\geq 0
.
Let
\{u_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}\subset USC(\Omega)
be viscosity subsolutions (resp.
\{u_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}\subset LSC(\Omega)
be viscosity supersolutions) to

\begin{cases}F_{k}^{\pm}(D^{2}u_{k},x)=f_{k}^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }\Omega^{\pm},\\
G_{k}(\nabla u^{+}_{k},\theta_{k}\nabla u^{-}_{k},x)=g_{k}^{\pm}(x)&\text{ on }T,\end{cases}

for some
\{f_{k}^{\pm}\}\subset C(\Omega^{\pm}\cup T)
,
\{g_{k}\}\subset C(T)
, and
\{\theta_{k}\}\subset[0,1]
.
Assume that
\theta_{k}\to\theta

Then the “half-relaxed limit”

\textstyle\limsup^{\star}u_{k}(x):=\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup\left\{u_{l}(y)\colon l\geq k,\,y\in B_{1/k}(x)\right\}\in USC(\Omega)

\text{(resp. }\textstyle\liminf_{\star}u_{k}(x)\displaystyle:=\lim_{k\to\infty}\inf\left\{u_{l}(y)\colon l\geq k,\,y\in B_{1/k}(x)\right\}\in LSC(\Omega)\text{)}

Remark 2.9
.

The half-relaxed limits above are well-known in the viscosity literature and guarantee that the resulting limit function is semicontinuous; for instance, see Section 6 in

In particular, if a sequence

of viscosity solutions to the above problems converges uniformly on compacts in

Proof.

Let
P
be a piecewise quadratic polynomial touching

from above at
x_{0}\in\Omega
.
The case
x_{0}\in\Omega^{\pm}
is already known, hence we assume that
x_{0}\in T
.
By Lemma

, we may assume that

(2.2)

F^{\pm}(D^{2}P^{\pm},x_{0})<f^{\pm}(x_{0}).

Let

and

For

Instead of studying the fully nonlinear problem (
2.1
) directly, it is more convenient to consider envelopes of linear problems.
The most celebrated ones are the Pucci extremal operators, defined on symmetric matrices
M\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
by

\mathcal{M}^{+}(M;\lambda,\Lambda)=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{\lambda,\Lambda}}\operatorname{Tr}(AM)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathcal{M}^{-}(M;\lambda,\Lambda)=\inf_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{\lambda,\Lambda}}\operatorname{Tr}(AM).

Thus, the operators
\mathcal{M}^{\pm}(D^{2}u;\lambda,\Lambda)
can be interpreted as the envelopes of all linear uniformly elliptic operators in non-divergence form

\operatorname{Tr}(A(x)D^{2}u)=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}(x)u_{x_{i}x_{j}},

with coefficient matrices
A(x)
in
\mathcal{A}_{\lambda,\Lambda}
.

We can similarly define the envelopes of linear boundary conditions for the gradient.
Let
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda,\Lambda,\mu}
be the set of
oblique coefficients
given by

\mathcal{O}_{\lambda,\Lambda,\mu}=\left\{\gamma=(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}\colon\quad\lambda\leq\gamma_{n}\leq\Lambda,\quad|\gamma^{\prime}|\leq\mu\right\}.

For vectors
\xi=(\xi^{\prime},\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
, we define the upper operator
\mathcal{N}^{+}
by

(2.3)

\mathcal{N}^{+}(\xi;\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)=\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{O}_{\lambda,\Lambda,\mu}}\gamma\cdot\xi=\left(\Lambda\chi_{\{\xi_{n}>0\}}+\lambda\chi_{\{\xi_{n}<0\}}\right)\xi_{n}+\mu|\xi^{\prime}|

and the lower operator
\mathcal{N}^{-}
by

(2.4)

\mathcal{N}^{-}(\xi;\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)=\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{O}_{\lambda,\Lambda,\mu}}\gamma\cdot\xi=\left(\lambda\chi_{\{\xi_{n}>0\}}+\Lambda\chi_{\{\xi_{n}<0\}}\right)\xi_{n}-\mu|\xi^{\prime}|.

Note here that
\mathcal{N}^{\pm}(\nabla u;\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)
are the envelopes of all oblique derivative conditions

\gamma(x)\cdot\nabla u=\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}(x)u_{x_{i}},

with coefficients
\gamma(x)
in
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda,\Lambda,\mu}
.

It is natural to extend this last notion to transmission conditions:

Definition 2.10

(Extremal transmission operators)
.

For vectors

\xi^{\pm}=(\xi^{\pm}_{T},\xi_{n}^{\pm})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
,
we define the operators
\mathcal{T}^{\pm}
by

\mathcal{T}^{+}(\xi^{+},\xi^{-})=\mathcal{T}^{+}(\xi^{+},\xi^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu):=\sup_{\gamma^{\pm}\in\mathcal{O}_{\lambda,\Lambda,\mu}}\left[\gamma^{+}\cdot\xi^{+}-\gamma^{-}\cdot\xi^{-}\right],

\mathcal{T}^{-}(\xi^{+},\xi^{-})=\mathcal{T}^{-}(\xi^{+},\xi^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu):=\inf_{\gamma^{\pm}\in\mathcal{O}_{\lambda,\Lambda,\mu}}\left[\gamma^{+}\cdot\xi^{+}-\gamma^{-}\cdot\xi^{-}\right].

Note by definition that

\mathcal{T}^{\pm}(\xi^{+},\xi^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)=\mathcal{N}^{\pm}(\xi^{+};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)-\mathcal{N}^{\mp}(\xi^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu),

hence
\mathcal{T}^{\pm}
have explicit expressions given in terms of (
2.3
) and (
2.4
).
As above, we can view the operators
\mathcal{T}^{\pm}(\nabla u^{+},\nabla u^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)
as envelopes of linear oblique derivative transmission conditions

\gamma^{+}(x)\nabla u^{+}-\gamma^{-}(x)\cdot\nabla u^{-}=\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}^{+}(x)u_{x_{i}}^{+}-\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}^{-}(x)u_{x_{j}}^{-},

with coefficients
\gamma^{\pm}(x)
in
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda,\Lambda,\mu}
.

We note the invariance property

\mathcal{T}^{\pm}(-\xi^{+},-\xi^{-})=-\mathcal{T}^{\mp}(\xi^{+},\xi^{-}).

In the special case when the vectors satisfy
\xi_{n}^{+}\geq 0
,
\xi_{n}^{-}\geq 0
, and
\xi^{\prime+}=\xi^{\prime-}=\xi^{\prime}
, we have that

(2.5)

\mathcal{T}^{-}(\xi^{+},\xi^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)=\lambda\xi_{n}^{+}-\Lambda\xi_{n}^{-}-2\mu|\xi^{\prime}|,

and considering the interpolation parameter
0\leq\theta\leq 1
, we see that

\mathcal{T}^{-}(\xi^{+},\theta\xi^{-})\geq\mathcal{T}^{-}(\xi^{+},\xi^{-})

in this case.
Universal bounds of this type will yield estimates that hold uniformly in
0\leq\theta\leq 1
.

Remark 2.11
.

If
A=(a_{ij})\in\mathcal{A}_{\lambda,\Lambda}
, then the vector
\gamma=Ae_{n}
belongs to
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda,\Lambda,(\Lambda-\lambda)/2}
.

Note that since the set

In particular, our extremal operators control the envelopes of all conormal derivatives and their associated transmission conditions.
These are the natural Neumann-type boundary conditions arising in classical variational problems, for which transmission problems are well understood.

The variational theory is based on the equivalence between the divergence-form equation

(2.6)

\operatorname{div}(A(x)\nabla u)=0\qquad\text{ in }B_{1},

with (say) piecewise constant coefficients

(2.7)

\begin{cases}\operatorname{Tr}(A^{\pm}D^{2}u)=0&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\
A^{+}\nabla u^{+}\cdot e_{n}=\theta A^{-}\nabla u^{-}\cdot e_{n}&\text{ on }T_{1}.\\
\end{cases}

An easy way to see this is by formally integrating (

More rigorously,

The extremal operators above lead to universal classes of viscosity subsolutions and supersolutions to transmission problems.
We introduce the following:

Definition 2.12
.

We say that

u\in\overline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g)
in
\Omega

u\in LSC(\Omega)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{M}^{-}(D^{2}u;\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)\leq f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }\Omega^{\pm},\\
\mathcal{T}^{-}(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)\leq g(x)&\text{ on }T.\end{cases}

We also define the solution class

S(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g)=\underline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g)\cap\overline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g).

.

Remark 2.14

Our solutions classes are natural extensions of the classical solution classes for Pucci operators.

,

.

Solution classes behave well under the addition of admissible barriers.

We conclude this section by giving a simple characterization of the degenerate problem.
Namely, when
\theta=0
, our transmission problem decouples into an oblique derivative problem above and a Dirichlet problem below, with boundary data on the interface.
More precisely, we have:

Proposition 2.17

The following are equivalent:

(i)

The function

is a viscosity solution to the degenerate transmission problem

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u,x)=f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }\Omega^{\pm},\\

(ii)

The functions

\end{cases}

The boundary regularity for the problems in (ii) is well-known: for smooth coefficients and sources, the functions

Proof.

That (ii) implies (i), with

3.
The ABP maximum principle

The classical ABP maximum principle provides a quantitative lower bound for viscosity supersolutions to second order PDEs.
In this section, we prove a variant of this result for oblique transmission problems.
For this, we touch the supersolution from below by appropriate piecewise linear polynomials.
Choosing the coefficients on each side to be comparable in size, the usual ABP technique will allow us to control the original function by the volume of its gradient image.

In the next statement, recall our subindex notation for the positive and negative parts

u_{+}=\max\{u,0\},\qquad u_{-}=\max\{-u,0\},

and compare it with the superindex notation for the restrictions

u^{+}=u\chi_{\{x_{n}\geq 0\}},\qquad u^{-}=u\chi_{\{x_{n}\leq 0\}}.

Theorem 3.1

(ABP estimate)
.

Let
u\in\overline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},g)
in
B_{R}
, with
u\in LSC(\overline{B_{R}})
.

There is a universal constant
C>0
such that

\sup_{B_{R}}u_{-}\leq\sup_{\partial B_{R}}u_{-}+CR\left(\|f^{+}_{+}\|_{L^{n}(\{u=\Gamma_{u}\}\cap B_{R}^{+})}+\|f^{-}_{+}\|_{L^{n}(\{u=\Gamma_{u}\}\cap B_{R}^{-})}+\|g_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{R})}\right),

where
\Gamma_{u}
denotes the convex envelope of
-u_{-}\chi_{B_{R}}
in the ball
B_{2R}
.

Proof.

Consider the piecewise linear barrier

(3.1)

\varphi(x):=\frac{1}{\lambda}\|g_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{R})}(x_{n})_{+}.

Since
\varphi_{x_{n}}^{+}\geq 0
and
\varphi_{x_{n}}^{-}=0
, from (
2.5
), we have that

\mathcal{T}^{-}(\nabla\varphi^{+},\theta\nabla\varphi^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)=\lambda\varphi_{x_{n}}^{+}=\|g_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{R})},

and hence by Lemma
2.15

(3.2)

u-\varphi\in\overline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+},f^{-},0)\quad\text{ in }B_{R}.

For
\xi=(\xi^{\prime},\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times(0,\infty)
, let
\ell_{\xi}
be the continuous piecewise linear polynomial

(3.3)

\ell_{\xi}=\xi^{\prime}\cdot x^{\prime}+\xi_{n}\Big\{2\frac{\Lambda}{\lambda}(x_{n})_{+}-(x_{n})_{-}\Big\}.

Since
(\ell_{\xi})_{x_{n}}^{+}=2\frac{\Lambda}{\lambda}\xi_{n}>0
and
(\ell_{\xi})_{x_{n}}^{-}=\xi_{n}>0
, recalling
0\leq\theta\leq 1
, from (
2.5
) we have that

\mathcal{T}^{-}(\nabla\ell_{\xi}^{+},\theta\nabla\ell_{\xi}^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)=\lambda(\ell_{\xi})_{x_{n}}^{+}-\Lambda(\ell_{\xi})_{x_{n}}^{-}-\mu(1+\theta)|\nabla^{\prime}\ell_{\xi}|\geq\Lambda\xi_{n}-2\mu|\xi^{\prime}|.

Therefore, if a vertical translation of
\ell_{\xi}
touches
u-\varphi
from below somewhere at
T_{R}
, then

(3.4)

\Lambda\xi_{n}-2\mu|\xi^{\prime}|\leq 0

by definition of viscosity supersolution.

Let
v
be the auxiliary function

(3.5)

v:=u-\varphi+\sup_{\partial B_{R}}(u-\varphi)_{-}

and observe that
v\geq 0
on
\partial B_{R}
.
We assume that its negative part is nontrivial, with

(3.6)

M:=\sup_{B_{R}}v_{-}>0.

Let
c_{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}
be such that the vertical translation
\widetilde{\ell}_{\xi}:=\ell_{\xi}+c_{\xi}
touches
-v_{-}\chi_{B_{R}}
from below on

.
Finally, recall that

is the convex envelope of

We note the following properties of

(i)

from below in

.

Motivated by conditions (i) and (ii), we define the coefficient set

By construction, each

yields a piecewise linear polynomial

below

in
\overline{B}_{2R}

-v_{-}

.
Therefore, recalling definition (

), we have the following alternative depending on whether such a tangency point lies in

\nabla\widetilde{\ell}^{-}_{\xi}=\xi\in\nabla\Gamma_{v}(B_{R}\setminus T_{R})\qquad\text{ or }\qquad\nabla\widetilde{\ell}^{+}_{\xi}=A(\xi)\in\nabla\Gamma_{v}(B_{R}\setminus T_{R}),

where

A(\xi^{\prime},\xi_{n})=(\xi^{\prime},\textstyle\frac{2\Lambda}{\lambda}\xi_{n})\qquad\text{ for }(\xi^{\prime},\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}.

We partition the rescaled coefficients by

, where

X_{1}:=\textstyle\frac{M}{R}\Xi\cap\nabla\Gamma(B_{R}\setminus T_{R})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad X_{2}:=\textstyle\frac{M}{R}\Xi\setminus X_{1}.

From the dichotomy above, it follows that

X_{1}\cup A(X_{2})\subset\nabla\Gamma_{v}(B_{R}\setminus T_{R})

and taking the Lebesgue measure on both sides, we obtain the lower bound

(3.7)

\Gamma_{v}
is

away from the interface and recalling (
3.2
) and (
3.5
), by the classical argument we deduce an upper bound

|\nabla\Gamma_{v}(B_{R}\setminus T_{R})|\leq\int_{\{v=\Gamma_{v}\}\cap(B_{R}\setminus T_{R})}D^{2}\Gamma_{v}\leq C\left(\int_{\{u=\Gamma_{u}\}\cap B_{R}^{+}}(f_{+}^{+})^{n}+\int_{\{u=\Gamma_{u}\}\cap B_{R}^{-}}(f_{+}^{-})^{n}\right),

, a fact which follows by convexity of

.
Since

) yield

M\leq CR\left(\|f_{+}^{+}\|_{L^{n}(\{u=\Gamma_{u}\}\cap B_{R}^{+})}+\|f_{+}^{-}\|_{L^{n}(\{u=\Gamma_{u}\}\cap B_{R}^{-})}\right).

\begin{split}\sup_{B_{R}}u_{-}&\leq\sup_{\partial B_{R}}(u-\varphi)_{-}+\sup_{B_{R}}v_{-}\\

The ABP maximum principle admits an extension to bounded domains:

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset B_{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded open set and let

in

\sup_{\Omega}u_{-}\leq\sup_{\partial\Omega}u_{-}+CR\left(\|f_{+}^{+}\|_{L^{n}(\{u=\Gamma_{u}\}\cap\Omega^{+})}+\|f_{+}^{-}\|_{L^{n}(\{u=\Gamma_{u}\}\cap\Omega^{-})}+\|g_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(T)}\right),

where
\Gamma_{u}
denotes the convex envelope of

in the ball

Proof.

It is not difficult to see that

in

.
Using the piecewise linear barrier

and following the proof of Theorem

, we see that the contact points in

belong to

The result follows.
∎

4.
Comparison principle and applications

Here, we consider the constant-coefficient inhomogeneous transmission problem in cylinders

(4.1)

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u)=f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }Q_{R}^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-})=g(x)&\text{ on }T_{R},\end{cases}

where
Q_{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the standard cylinder

Q_{R}=B_{R}^{\prime}\times(-R,R)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}

and in particular

Q_{R}^{+}=B_{R}^{\prime}\times(0,R),\qquad Q_{R}^{-}=B_{R}^{\prime}\times(-R,0),\qquad T_{R}=B_{R}^{\prime}\times\{0\}.

Our main goal is to establish a comparison principle for viscosity solutions to (
4.1
).
Such a result is fundamental to both the existence and uniqueness theory for the associated Dirichlet problem, as well as the optimal piecewise
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity in
[
7
]
.
Our approach is adapted from
[
5
,
6
, Section 3]
and
[

, Section 4]
, where the authors studied linear isotropic transmission conditions of the form
G=\xi^{+}_{n}-\xi^{-}_{n}
.
The proof in the nonlinear oblique case (
4.1
) follows the same strategy, with some modifications (see especially Remark
4.4
after the proof of Theorem
4.3
).

Let
\rho<R
and

.
For

in the horizontal direction by

u^{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime},x_{n})=\sup_{y^{\prime}\in B_{\rho}^{\prime}}\left[u(y^{\prime},x_{n})-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}|y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}|^{2}\right]\qquad\text{ for }x=(x^{\prime},x_{n})\in Q_{\rho}.

This is the classical sup-convolution of Jensen applied to the trace
u(\cdot,x_{n})
, for each

.
Such a regularization of

has nice properties:

Lemma 4.1

(c.f. Lemma 3.1 in
[
6
]
and Proposition 4.5 in
[
15
]
)
.

The following properties hold:

(i)

u^{\varepsilon}\in USC(\overline{Q}_{\rho})
,

pointwise in

as

, and hence for any sequence

u(x)=\textstyle\limsup^{\star}u^{\varepsilon_{k}}(x)\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{B_{1/k}(x)}u^{\varepsilon_{k}}\quad\text{ for }x\in Q_{\rho};

(ii)

For each

, the trace
u^{\varepsilon}(\cdot,x_{n})
is Lipschitz continuous on
\overline{B}_{\rho}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
and pointwise second order differentiable a.e. in

;
3
3

3

That is, for each

and almost every

, there is a quadratic polynomial

.

(iii)

If

is a viscosity subsolution to (

) in

, then for

, with

, the function
u^{\varepsilon}
is a viscosity subsolution to

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u^{\varepsilon})=f^{\pm}(x)-\omega_{f^{\pm}}(r_{\varepsilon})&\text{ in }Q_{r}^{\pm},\\

where

Remark 4.2

An analogous statement holds for

and its lower envelopes

The proof of Lemma
4.1
turns out to be essentially the same as in the linear non-oblique case:

Proof.

The regularity properties (i) and (ii) are classical.
We only check the subsolution property (iii) for the transmission condition, the one for the bulk equations being analogous.

Let
P
be a piecewise quadratic polynomial touching
u^{\varepsilon}
from above at
x_{0}\in T_{r}
.
Since
u^{\varepsilon}(x_{0})=u(x_{\varepsilon})-\varepsilon^{-1}|x^{\prime}-x^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}
for some
x_{\varepsilon}\in\overline{T}_{\rho}
with
|x_{\varepsilon}-x_{0}|\leq r_{\varepsilon}
, it follows that the translated piecewise quadratic polynomial
Q(y):=P(y+x_{0}-x_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon^{-1}|x^{\prime}-x^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}
touches

at
x_{\varepsilon}
.
By the transmission condition and uniform ellipticity, we then have that

\begin{split}G(\nabla P^{+}(x_{0}),\theta\nabla P^{-}(x_{0}))=G(\nabla Q^{+}(x_{\varepsilon}),\theta\nabla Q^{-}(x_{\varepsilon}))\geq g(x_{\varepsilon})\geq g(x_{0})-\omega_{g}(r_{\varepsilon}),\end{split}

whence the claim follows.
∎

The comparison principle states that the difference of viscosity sub- and supersolutions to (
4.1
) belongs to the subsolution class
\underline{S}
.
More precisely, we have the following:

Theorem 4.3

(c.f. Theorem 4.7 in
[
15
]
)
.

Let
\{F^{\pm},G\}
be constant-coefficient operators with ellipticity constants
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
and
\mu\geq 0
.
Let
\{f^{\pm},\widetilde{f}^{\pm}\}
be functions in
C(Q^{\pm}_{R}\cup T_{R})
, let
\{g,\widetilde{g}\}
be functions in

, and let

If

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u)\geq f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }Q_{R}^{\pm},\\

respectively, in the viscosity sense, then

u-v\in\underline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;f^{+}-\widetilde{f}^{+},f^{-}-\widetilde{f}^{-},g-\widetilde{g})\quad\text{ in }Q_{R}.

Proof.

where

and
\widetilde{c}_{\varepsilon}=\max\{\omega_{\widetilde{f}^{+}}(\widetilde{r}_{\varepsilon}),\omega_{\widetilde{f}^{-}}(\widetilde{r}_{\varepsilon}),\omega_{g}(\widetilde{r}_{\varepsilon})\}

Let
P

from above at

.
The case

.
By Lemma

\textstyle\mathcal{M}^{+}(D^{2}P^{\pm};\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)<-\|f^{\pm}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{\rho}^{\pm})}-c_{\varepsilon}-\|\widetilde{f}^{\pm}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{\rho}^{\pm})}-\widetilde{c}_{\varepsilon}.

By the ABP method, the set of points in

Hence, by (ii) in Lemma

, for each small

First we regularize the boundary datum

,

(4.15)

\overline{P}_{x_{i}}(y_{0})=(L_{\overline{u}})_{x_{i}}-(L_{\overline{v}})_{x_{i}},\qquad\overline{P}_{x_{n}}^{+}(y_{0})\geq(L_{\overline{u}})_{x_{n}}^{+}-(L_{\overline{v}})_{x_{n}}^{+},\qquad\overline{P}_{x_{n}}^{-}(y_{0})\leq(L_{\overline{u}})_{x_{n}}^{-}-(L_{\overline{v}})_{x_{n}}^{-}.

By (
4.10
), (
4.14
), (
4.15
), and uniform ellipticity, we conclude

\begin{split}g(y_{0})-c_{\varepsilon}&\leq G(\nabla L_{\overline{u}}^{+},\theta\nabla L_{\overline{u}}^{-})\\
&\quad\leq G(\nabla L_{\overline{v}}^{+}+\nabla\overline{P}^{+}(y_{0}),\theta\nabla L_{\overline{v}}^{-}+\theta\nabla\overline{P}^{-}(y_{0}))\\
&\qquad\leq G(\nabla L_{\overline{v}}^{+},\theta\nabla L_{\overline{v}}^{-})+\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla\overline{P}^{+}(y_{0}),\theta\nabla\overline{P}^{-}(y_{0}))\\
&\qquad\quad\leq\widetilde{g}(y_{0})+\widetilde{c}_{\varepsilon}+\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla\overline{P}^{+}(y_{0}),\theta\nabla\overline{P}^{-}(y_{0});\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)\end{split}

whence we see that

(4.16)

g(y_{0})-c_{\varepsilon}-(\widetilde{g}(y_{0})+\widetilde{c}_{\varepsilon})\leq\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla\overline{P}^{+}(y_{0}),\theta\nabla\overline{P}^{-}(y_{0});\lambda,\Lambda,\mu).

Using that

y_{0}\in T_{\delta}(x_{0})
,
by continuity of
g
and
\widetilde{g}
, the left-hand side can be bounded below by

(4.17)

g(y_{0})-c_{\varepsilon}-(\widetilde{g}(y_{0})+\widetilde{c}_{\varepsilon})\geq g(x_{0})-c_{\varepsilon}-(\widetilde{g}(x_{0})+\widetilde{c}_{\varepsilon})-\omega_{g}(\delta)-\omega_{\widetilde{g}}(\delta).

Recalling that
\nabla\overline{P}^{\pm}=\nabla P^{\pm}-(a^{\prime}_{\vartheta},0)
, with
|a_{\vartheta}^{\prime}|<\vartheta
, by uniform ellipticity the right-hand side of (
4.16
) is controlled by

(4.18)

\begin{split}&\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla\overline{P}^{+}(y_{0}),\theta\nabla\overline{P}^{-}(y_{0}))\\
&\quad\leq\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla P^{+}(y_{0}),\theta\nabla P^{-}(y_{0}))+2\mu\vartheta\\
&\qquad\leq\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla P^{+}(x_{0}),\theta\nabla P^{-}(x_{0}))+(\Lambda+\mu)\left(\|D^{2}P^{+}\|+\|D^{2}P^{-}\|\right)|y_{0}-x_{0}|+2\mu\vartheta\\
&\qquad\quad\leq\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla P^{+}(x_{0}),\theta\nabla P^{-}(x_{0}))+C\delta+2\mu\vartheta.\end{split}

From (
4.16
), combining (
4.17
) and (
4.18
) and letting
\vartheta\downarrow 0
and
\delta\downarrow 0
, we deduce the claim.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

For linear non-oblique transmission conditions, an analogous result to Theorem
4.3
was established first for continuous sub- and supersolutions in
[
5
,
6
]
, and subsequently extended to the semicontinuous setting in
[
15
]
, by the same methods.
The proof in
[
15
]
uses two auxiliary functions
\overline{u}^{\varepsilon}
and
\overline{v}_{\varepsilon}
(c.f.
\overline{u}
and
\overline{v}
above) defined as solutions to certain Dirichlet problems with merely semicontinuous boundary data.
In particular, it is not clear whether the boundary datum is achieved, or in what sense, a point which is crucial to the whole argument.
In our proof above, we avoid this issue by further regularizing the boundary datum
u_{k}
, then taking replacements
\overline{u}_{k}
, and finally passing to the limit as
k\to\infty
.

As an immediate corollary of Theorem
4.3
, we obtain the uniqueness of viscosity solutions to the Dirichlet problem in (say) balls:

Corollary 4.5

(Uniqueness of viscosity solutions)
.

Let
\{F^{\pm},G\}
be constant-coefficient operators.
Let
f^{\pm}\in C(B_{R}^{\pm}\cup T_{R})
,
g\in C(T_{R})
, and
\phi\in C(\partial B_{R})
.
Let
0\leq\theta\leq 1
.

The Dirichlet problem

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u,x)=f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }B^{\pm}_{R},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-},x)=g(x)&\text{ on }T_{R},\\
u=\phi&\text{ on }\partial B_{R},\end{cases}

has at most one viscosity solution
u\in C(\overline{B_{R}})
.

Proof.

If
u
and
v
are viscosity solutions, then applying Theorem
4.3
to their differences in cubes centered on the interface, we see that
u-v\in S(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;0,0,0)
in
B_{R}
.
Since
u=v
on
\partial B_{R}
, the ABP maximum principle now yields
u=v
in
B_{R}
.
∎

As another corollary, we see that horizontal difference quotients belong to the solution class, a key result for the regularity theory in our work
[
7
]
:

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
u\in C(Q_{R})
be a viscosity solution to

(4.19)

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u)=0&\text{ in }Q^{\pm}_{R},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-})=0&\text{ on }T_{R}.\end{cases}

Then, for all
e=(e^{\prime},0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
, we have that

u(x+e)-u(x)\in S(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;0,0,0)\quad\text{ in }Q_{R-|e|}.

Proof.

Since (
4.19
) is invariant under horizontal translations, it follows that
u(x)
and its translation
u(x+e)
are viscosity solutions in the smaller cube
Q_{R-|e|}
.
Applying Theorem
4.3
to their differences now yields the claim.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

We can extend some of the above results to convex equations.
Assuming convexity of the operators
F^{\pm}
and
G
, arguing similarly to the proof of Theorem
4.3
, we can show that the set of viscosity supersolutions to (
4.1
) is closed under convex combinations.
As a corollary, we deduce that horizontal second order incremental quotients are in the supersolution class.
More precisely, if
u
is a viscosity solution to (
4.19
), then for all
e=(e^{\prime},0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
we have that

u(x+e)+u(x-e)-2u(x)\in\overline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;0,0,0)\quad\text{ in }Q_{R-|e|}.

This result suggests that horizontal second derivatives are bounded below.
However, we do not know whether a two-sided bound exists, or if the full Hessian is bounded up to the interface.
If true, such a result would then have important implications for the regularity theory of (non-convex) variable-coefficient problems; see Section 7 in
[
7
]
for details.

5.
Existence of viscosity solutions via Perron’s method

Let
F^{\pm}
and
G
be constant-coefficient operators with ellipticity constants
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
and
\mu\geq 0
.
Let
f^{\pm}\in C(B_{1}^{\pm}\cup T_{1})\cap L^{\infty}(B_{1}^{\pm})
and
g\in C(T_{1})\cap L^{\infty}(T_{1})
.
Given
\phi\in C(\partial B_{1})
, we apply Perron’s method to construct a viscosity solution
u\in C(\overline{B}_{1})
to the inhomogeneous transmission problem

(5.1)

\begin{cases}F^{\pm}(D^{2}u)=f^{\pm}(x)&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\
G(\nabla u^{+},\theta\nabla u^{-})=g(x)&\text{ on }T_{1},\end{cases}

satisfying the Dirichlet boundary condition

(5.2)

u=\phi\quad\text{ on }\partial B_{1}.

Our solution is defined as the largest viscosity subsolution, namely,
we will prove the following:

Theorem 5.1

(Existence)
.

Let
u
be the function given by

u(x)=\sup\left\{v(x)\colon v\in\mathcal{U}\right\}\qquad\text{ for }x\in\overline{B}_{1},

where
\mathcal{U}
is the set

\mathcal{U}=\left\{v\in USC(\overline{B}_{1})\text{ viscosity subsolution to~\eqref{eq:lul}},\text{ with }v\leq\phi\text{ on }\partial B_{1}\right\}.

Then
u\in C(\overline{B}_{1})
is a viscosity solution to (
5.1
) and satisfies the boundary condition (
5.2
).

Theorem
5.1
will follow from the discussion and three Lemmata
5.2
,
5.3
, and
5.4
below.

Since the function
u
need not be continuous (nor semicontinuous) a priori, we must take its l.s.c. and u.s.c. envelopes
u_{\star}
and
u^{\star}
, respectively, given by

u_{\star}(x)=\lim_{\rho\downarrow 0}\inf_{B_{\rho}(x)\cap\overline{B}_{1}}u\qquad\text{ and }\qquad u^{\star}(x)=\lim_{\rho\downarrow 0}\sup_{B_{\rho}(x)\cap\overline{B}_{1}}u\qquad\text{ for }x\in\overline{B}_{1}.

Note in particular that

u_{\star}\leq u\leq u^{\star}\quad\text{ on }\overline{B}_{1}.

To show that the boundary condition (
5.2
) is attained, we first construct barriers vanishing at given points on the sphere
\partial B_{1}
.
As explained in the Introduction, the most delicate case corresponds to when such a point meets the interface.
Here, we use a piecewise smooth barrier inspired by Lieberman’s
[
9
]
barrier for the oblique derivative problem based on earlier work of Miller
[
13
]
.
For points away from the boundary, we modify a simple barrier of Li-Zhang
[
11
]
for the oblique derivative problem.

Lemma 5.2
.

For each
x_{0}\in\partial B_{1}
, there is a viscosity supersolution
h\in C(\overline{B}_{1})
to (
5.1
) satisfying

h(x_{0})=0\qquad\text{ and }\qquad h(x)>0\quad\text{ for }x\in\overline{B}_{1}\setminus\{x_{0}\}.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}=(x_{0}^{\prime},(x_{0})_{n})\in\partial B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
.
We distinguish two cases according to the relative position of
x_{0}
with respect to the interface:

Case 1.

|(x_{0})_{n}|=\kappa>0
.

Let
v
and
w
be the quadratic and piecewise quadratic functions given by

v(x)=1-(x_{0}\cdot x)^{2}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad w(x)=3-(x_{n})_{+}-x_{n}^{2},

respectively.
Note that

v(x_{0})=0,\qquad\qquad v>0\,\text{ on }\overline{B}_{1}\setminus\{x_{0}\},\qquad\qquad v\geq\kappa^{2}\,\text{ on }T_{1},

w\geq 1\,\text{ on }\overline{B}_{1},\qquad\qquad w\geq 3\,\text{ on }T_{1},

as well as

\begin{cases}\textstyle\mathcal{M}^{+}(D^{2}v;\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)=\mathcal{M}^{+}(D^{2}w;\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)=-2\lambda n^{-1}&\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm},\\
\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla w^{+},\theta\nabla w^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)=-\lambda&\text{ on }T_{1}.\end{cases}

Letting

h:=\varepsilon^{-1}\min\big\{4\kappa^{-2}v,w\big\}

and choosing
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, we see from the above that

(5.3)

\textstyle\mathcal{M}^{+}(D^{2}h;\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)<-F^{\pm}(0)-\|f^{\pm}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1}^{\pm})}\qquad\text{ in }B_{1}^{\pm}

in the viscosity sense, and

(5.4)

\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla h^{+},\theta\nabla h^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)<-G(0,0)-\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(T_{1})}\qquad\text{ on }T_{1}.

It follows that

h
is a viscosity supersolution to (
5.1
) and satisfies the claim.

Case 2.

(x_{0})_{n}=0
.

For simplicity, throughout the proof, we write
T
to denote
the “global” interface

T=\{x_{n}=0\}.

By translation and rotation, we may assume that
B_{1}=B_{1}(e_{1})
,
T_{1}=T_{1}(e_{1})=T\cap B_{1}(e_{1})
, and
x_{0}=0\in\partial B_{1}(e_{1})
.
Note here that
e_{1}=(1,0,\ldots,0)
.

We introduce polar coordinates in the
(x_{1},x_{n})
-plane:

x_{1}=r\cos\phi,\qquad x_{n}=r\sin\phi,\qquad\text{ with }\qquad-\pi/2\leq\phi\leq\pi/2.

Note that the tangent plane to the convex set
B_{1}(e_{1})
at
x_{0}=0\in\partial B_{1}(e_{1})
is

\{x_{1}=0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
hence we always have
x_{1}\neq 0
and therefore

r>0\qquad\text{ in }B_{1}(e_{1}).

We also note that

T=\{x_{n}=0\}=\{\phi=0\}.

Let
w=w(x_{1},x_{n})
be the function of two variables given in polar coordinates by

w=r^{\alpha}\zeta(\phi),

where
\zeta
is the piecewise smooth function

\zeta(\phi)=2-e^{\beta(|\phi|-\frac{\pi}{2})}

and
\alpha
,
\beta
are universal constants given by

\beta=2\frac{\Lambda}{\lambda}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\alpha=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\beta}{2e^{\beta\frac{\pi}{2}}-1}\min\left\{\frac{\lambda}{2\mu},\beta\right\}.

We point out that
0<\alpha<1
as well as
1\leq\zeta(\phi)\leq 2
for
-\frac{\pi}{2}\leq\phi\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
, whence it follows that

r^{\alpha}\leq w\leq 2r^{\alpha}\quad\text{ on }\overline{B}_{1}(e_{1}).

Observe also that
w\in C(\overline{B}_{1}(e_{1}))
is piecewise
C^{2}
, i.e., its restrictions are
C^{2}
up to the interface

w^{\pm}\in C^{2}(B_{1}^{\pm}(e_{1})\cup T_{1}(e_{1})),

and a fortiori an admissible function.

We claim that
w
is a strict supersolution, in the sense that

(5.5)

\textstyle\mathcal{M}^{+}(D^{2}w;\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)\leq-c_{0}r^{\alpha-2}\quad\text{ in }B_{1}(e_{1})^{\pm},

(5.6)

\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla w^{+},\theta\nabla w^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)\leq-c_{0}r^{\alpha-1}\quad\text{ on }T_{1}(e_{1}),

where
c_{0}>0
is the universal constant

(5.7)

c_{0}=\frac{\lambda\beta}{2e^{\beta\frac{\pi}{2}}}.

Let us check (
5.5
) first.
By Miller’s polar representation
[
13
]
, every non-divergence operator

Lw=\operatorname{Tr}(A(x)D^{2}w)

acting on
w(x_{1},x_{n})
, with
A(x)\in\mathcal{A}_{\lambda,\Lambda}
, can be written as

Lw=r^{\alpha-2}\left[a(x)\alpha(\alpha-1)\zeta+2b(x)(\alpha-1)\zeta^{\prime}+c(x)(\zeta^{\prime\prime}+\alpha\zeta)\right],

for some
2\times 2
elliptic matrix

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a(x)&b(x)\\
c(x)&d(x)\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathcal{A}_{\lambda,\Lambda}
.
Observing that

0<\alpha<1
,
\zeta^{\prime}<0
, and
\zeta^{\prime\prime}+\alpha\zeta<0
,
a computation shows that

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}^{+}(D^{2}w)&=r^{\alpha-2}\left[-\lambda\alpha(1-\alpha)\zeta-(\Lambda-\lambda)(1-\alpha)\zeta^{\prime}+\lambda(\zeta^{\prime\prime}+\alpha\zeta)\right]\\
&=r^{\alpha-2}e^{\beta(|\phi|-\frac{\pi}{2})}\left[\lambda\big(-\beta^{2}+\alpha^{2}(2e^{\beta(\frac{\pi}{2}-|\phi|)}-1)\big)+\beta(\Lambda-\lambda)(1-\alpha)\right]\\
&\qquad\leq r^{\alpha-2}\lambda e^{\beta(|\phi|-\frac{\pi}{2})}\left[-\beta^{2}+\alpha^{2}(2e^{\beta\frac{\pi}{2}}-1)+\beta\frac{\Lambda-\lambda}{\lambda}\right]\\
&\qquad\qquad\leq r^{\alpha-2}\lambda\beta e^{\beta(|\phi|-\frac{\pi}{2})}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\beta+\frac{\Lambda}{\lambda}-1\right]\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq-r^{\alpha-2}\lambda\beta e^{-\beta\frac{\pi}{2}}\leq-c_{0}r^{\alpha-2},\end{split}

where in the last line we have used the definition of
\beta
and
c_{0}
in (
5.7
).

Next, we verify (
5.6
).
A direct computation shows that the gradient of
w
away from the interface
T=\{\phi=0\}
is given by

\nabla w=r^{\alpha-1}\Big[\left(\alpha\zeta(\phi)\cos\phi-\zeta^{\prime}(\phi)\sin\phi\right)e_{1}+\left(\alpha\zeta(\phi)\sin\phi+\zeta^{\prime}(\phi)\cos\phi\right)e_{n}\Big],

and hence, restricted to the interface
T_{1}(e_{1})
from each side

\nabla w^{\pm}=r^{\alpha-1}\Big[\alpha\zeta(0)e_{1}+[\zeta^{\pm}]^{\prime}(0)e_{n}\Big]\quad\text{ on }T_{1}(e_{1}).

Since
[\zeta^{+}]^{\prime}(0)<0
and
[\zeta^{-}]^{\prime}(0)>0
, we see that
w_{x_{n}}^{+}<0
and
w_{x_{n}}^{-}>0
on
T
, and hence by (
2.5
)

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla w^{+},\theta\nabla w^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)&=\lambda(w_{x_{n}}^{+}-\theta w_{x_{n}}^{-})+\mu(1+\theta)|w_{x_{1}}|\\
&\qquad\leq\lambda w_{x_{n}}^{+}+2\mu|w_{x_{1}}|=r^{\alpha-1}\left(\lambda[\zeta^{+}]^{\prime}(0)+2\mu\alpha\zeta(0)\right)\end{split}

whence, recalling (
5.7
), we deduce

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla w^{+},\theta\nabla w^{-})&\leq r^{\alpha-1}\left(-\lambda\beta e^{-\beta\frac{\pi}{2}}+2\mu\alpha(2-e^{-\beta\frac{\pi}{2}})\right)\leq-c_{0}r^{\alpha-1}\quad\text{ on }T_{1}(e_{1}),\end{split}

which was the claim.

Finally, we extend
w
in the remaining variables
x=(x_{1},x^{\prime\prime},x_{n})\in B_{1}(e_{1})\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-2}\times\mathbb{R}
by

(5.8)

h(x):=w(x_{1},x_{n})+\frac{1}{2}c_{1}|x^{\prime\prime}|^{2},

where
c_{1}>0
is the universal constant

c_{1}:=2^{\alpha-2}\frac{c_{0}}{2}\min\left\{\frac{1}{\Lambda n},\frac{1}{\mu}\right\}.

Since
r\leq 2
in
B_{1}(e_{1})
, from (
5.5
) we obtain

\begin{split}\textstyle\mathcal{M}^{+}(D^{2}h;\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)&\leq\mathcal{M}^{+}(D^{2}w)+c_{1}\textstyle\mathcal{M}^{+}(D^{2}\frac{1}{2}|x^{\prime\prime}|^{2})\\
&\quad\leq-2^{\alpha-2}c_{0}+\Lambda(n-2)c_{1}\\
&\qquad\leq-2^{\alpha-3}c_{0}\qquad\text{ in }B_{1}(e_{1})^{\pm},\end{split}

and from (
5.6
) we deduce

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla h^{+},\theta\nabla h^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)&\leq\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla w^{+},\theta\nabla w^{-})+c_{1}\textstyle\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla\frac{1}{2}|x^{\prime\prime}|^{2},\theta\nabla\frac{1}{2}|x^{\prime\prime}|^{2})\\
&\quad\leq-2^{\alpha-1}c_{0}+2\mu c_{1}\\
&\qquad\leq-2^{\alpha-2}c_{0}\qquad\text{ on }T_{1}(e_{1}),\end{split}

In particular, the last two inequalities show that the function
h
defined in (
5.8
) satisfies

(5.9)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{M}^{+}(D^{2}h;\frac{\lambda}{n},\Lambda)\leq-c_{2}&\text{ in }B_{1}(e_{1})^{\pm},\\
\mathcal{T}^{+}(\nabla h^{+},\theta\nabla h^{-};\lambda,\Lambda,\mu)\leq-c_{2}&\text{ on }T_{1}(e_{1}),\\
\end{cases}

with
c_{2}=2^{\alpha-2}c_{0}>0
.
Replacing
h
by
\varepsilon^{-1}h
, from (
5.9
) we see that we may take
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small such that (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) hold, whence the claim follows.
∎

For
x_{0}\in\partial B_{1}
and
\varepsilon>0
, we define the lower barrier

\underline{\varphi}(x)=\phi(x_{0})-\varepsilon-C_{\varepsilon}h(x),

where
h
is given by Lemma
5.2
and
C_{\varepsilon}\geq 1
is chosen large such that
\underline{\varphi}\leq\phi
on
\partial B_{1}
.
Since
\underline{\varphi}\in\mathcal{U}
is a continuous function, by definition of
u
and l.s.c. envelope, we have that

\underline{\varphi}\leq u_{\star}\leq u\leq u^{\star}\quad\text{ on }\overline{B}_{1}.

In particular, we see that
\phi(x_{0})\leq u_{\star}(x_{0})+\varepsilon
for all
x_{0}\in\partial B_{1}
and
\varepsilon>0
, whence it follows that

(5.10)

\phi\leq u_{\star}\leq u\qquad\text{ on }\partial B_{1}.

Similarly, the upper barrier

\overline{\varphi}(x)=\phi(x_{0})+\varepsilon+C_{\varepsilon}h(x)

is a continuous supersolution satisfying
\overline{\varphi}\geq\phi
on
\partial B_{1}
.
By comparison principle (Theorem
4.3
), for all
v\in\mathcal{U}
, we have that
v-\overline{\varphi}\in\underline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;0,0,0)
in
B_{1}
and hence the ABP yields
v\leq\overline{\varphi}
in
B_{1}
.
Recalling the definition of
u
and u.s.c. envelope, we deduce
u\leq u^{\star}\leq\overline{\varphi}
in
B_{1}
and arguing as above, we conclude

(5.11)

u\leq u^{\star}\leq\phi\qquad\text{ on }\partial B_{1}.

Combining (
5.10
) and (
5.11
), we see that the boundary condition (
5.2
) is attained with

(5.12)

u_{\star}=u=u^{\star}=\phi\qquad\text{ on }\partial B_{1}.

Next, we show that
u^{\star}
is a subsolution:

Lemma 5.3
.

The u.s.c. envelope
u^{\star}
is a viscosity subsolution to (
5.1
)

Proof.

We only check the transmission condition.
Let
P
be a piecewise quadratic polynomial touching
u^{\star}
from above at
x_{0}\in T_{1}
.
By Lemma
2.6
, we may assume that

(5.13)

F^{\pm}(D^{2}P^{\pm})<-\|f^{\pm}\|_{L^{\infty}}(B^{\pm}_{r_{0}}(x_{0})),

for a small
r_{0}>0
.

Fix
\delta>0
small.
By definition of
u^{\star}
and
u
, a vertical translation of
P_{\delta}(x):=P(x)+\frac{1}{2}\delta|x-x_{0}|^{2}
touches an u.s.c. subsolution
v_{k}
from above at
x_{k}\in B_{r_{k}}(x_{0})
, with
r_{k}\downarrow 0
.
Taking
\delta>0
sufficiently small, since
v_{k}
are subsolutions, by (
5.13
) we have that
x_{k}\in T_{r_{0}}(x_{0})
for infinitely many
k
and at those points

g(x_{k})\leq G(\nabla P_{\delta}^{+}(x_{k}),\theta\nabla P_{\delta}^{-}(x_{k}))\leq G(\nabla P^{+}(x_{k}),\theta\nabla P^{-}(x_{k}))+2\delta\mu r_{k}.

Since
x_{k}\to x_{0}
and
r_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
, passing to the limit yields the claim.
∎

Combining (
5.12
) and Lemma
5.3
, we see that
u^{\star}\in\mathcal{U}
, and by definition of
u
we conclude

(5.14)

u=u^{\star}\quad\text{ on }\overline{B}_{1}.

To finish the argument, it remains to show that
u_{\star}
is a supersolution:

Lemma 5.4
.

The l.s.c. envelope
u_{\star}
is a viscosity supersolution to (
5.1
).

Proof.

Otherwise, there is a piecewise quadratic polynomial
P
touching
u_{\star}
from below at
x_{0}\in T_{1}
(the case
x_{0}\notin T_{1}
is known) and violating the transmission condition at that point, whence by continuity we obtain

(5.15)

G(\nabla P^{+}(x),\theta\nabla P^{-}(x))-g(x)\geq\eta>0\qquad\text{ for all }x\in T_{r}(x_{0}),

for some small
r>0
and
\eta>0
.
Arguing as in the proof of Lemma
2.6
we may assume that

(5.16)

F^{\pm}(D^{2}P^{\pm})>\|f^{\pm}\|_{L^{\infty}(B^{\pm}_{r}(x_{0}))}.

For
\delta>0
, we modify
P
by
P_{\delta}(x):=P(x)-\frac{1}{2}\delta|x-x_{0}|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\delta r^{2}
.
By (
5.15
), (
5.16
), and uniform ellipticity, we can choose
\delta>0
sufficiently small such that

G(\nabla P_{\delta}^{+}(x),\theta\nabla P_{\delta}^{-}(x))\geq G(\nabla P^{+}(x),\theta\nabla P^{-}(x))-2\mu\delta r\geq g(x)+\eta-2\mu\delta r>g(x)

for all
x\in T_{r}(x_{0})
, and

F^{\pm}(D^{2}P^{\pm}_{\delta})\geq F^{\pm}(D^{2}P^{\pm})-\Lambda\delta>\|f^{\pm}\|_{L^{\infty}(B^{\pm}_{r}(x_{0}))}.

Therefore,
P_{\delta}
is a strict subsolution to (
5.1
) in
B_{r}(x_{0})
.

Since
P_{\delta}<u_{\star}\leq u
on
\partial B_{r}(x_{0})
, it follows that the function

v=\begin{cases}\max\{u,P_{\delta}\}&\text{ in }B_{r}(x_{0})\\
u&\text{ on }\overline{B}_{1}\setminus B_{r}(x_{0})\end{cases}

is in the set
\mathcal{U}
.
However, since
P_{\delta}(x_{0})>u_{\star}(x_{0})=\lim_{\rho\downarrow 0}\inf_{B_{\rho}(x_{0})}u
, we can find a point
y\in B_{r}(x_{0})
where
u(y)<P_{\delta}(y)=v(y)
, contradicting the definition of
u
.
We conclude that no such piecewise quadratic polynomial
P
can exist, hence
u_{\star}
is a subsolution.
∎

Thanks to (
5.14
) and Lemma
5.4
, by comparison principle
u-u_{\star}\in\underline{S}(\lambda,\Lambda,\mu,\theta;0,0,0)
in
B_{1}
, whence by (
5.12
) and the ABP we deduce that
u\leq u_{\star}
on
\overline{B}_{1}
.
Therefore,
u=u^{\star}=u_{\star}\in C(\overline{B}_{1})
is a viscosity solution to (
5.1
).
