Vitamin B3 (Nicotinamide) Supplementation in Primary and Scleroderma‐Related Raynaud Phenomenon
Authors: Mogeshni Govender, Natalie Tomson, Johnny Fang, Mohamed Bakry, Raymond Liu, Kathleen Morrisroe, Ross Penglase, Kokum Dissanayake, Ted Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to explore if vitamin B3 (nicotinamide) supplementation is a safe and effective adjunctive therapy for patients with primary Raynaud phenomenon (RP) and scleroderma‐related RP. The effect of nicotinamide supplementation in human patients with RP has not been studied before.MethodsPrimary endpoints were to assess adherence to vitamin B3, vitamin B3‐related adverse events, and changes in the following: (1) RP frequency and severity (via the Raynaud Condition Score diary), (2) RP‐related quality of life (via the Short‐Form Assessment of Systemic Sclerosis‐Associated Raynaud's Phenomenon questionnaire), and (3) nailfold videocapillaroscopy (NVC) patterns and parameters. NVC incorporated artificial intelligence using Capillary.io software based on the validated CAPI‐Detect algorithm. We conducted a six‐week prospective longitudinal, within‐participant crossover intervention study using a three‐phase study design: A1 (baseline), B (vitamin B3 500 mg twice daily orally), and A2 (vitamin B3 washout); each phase was two weeks long. A total of 38 participants completed the study (20 with primary RP and 18 with scleroderma‐related RP).ResultsVitamin B3 showed excellent adherence (98.7%) with few mild adverse events. NVC patterns improved in 45% postsupplementation (P < 0.001; total cohort), with increased normal and reduced abnormal capillaries. RP‐related quality of life and severity improvements were significant only in primary RP, persisting after washout.ConclusionVitamin B3 was well tolerated and improved NVC patterns and parameters in both groups, with benefits persisting postwashout. Participants with primary RP had significant improvements in patient‐related outcome measures, which were not found in the scleroderma‐related RP group. Scleroderma microvasculature may be more resistant to vasodilation, possibly due to structural remodeling. Improvements during the washout phase suggest that vitamin B3 may induce functional microvascular effects beyond its pharmacological half‐life.

INTRODUCTION

Raynaud phenomenon (RP) is characterized by episodic vasoconstriction of digital capillaries in response to cold exposure and/or emotional stress, resulting in pallor, cyanosis, and reactive erythema. 1 RP affects approximately 3% to 5% of the general population and is more common in female patients. 1 It is classified as primary (idiopathic) or secondary, the latter most commonly being associated with systemic autoimmune diseases (SADs), which account for approximately 20% of cases. 1 Among SADs, RP is particularly prevalent and severe in scleroderma, in which it may lead to complications such as digital ulceration and autoamputation. Even in the absence of tissue injury, RP can significantly impair quality of life. 2

Current pharmacological therapies for RP have limited efficacy and tolerability. Randomized trials and meta‐analyses demonstrate only modest benefits of calcium channel blockers, with uncertain clinical significance. 3 These agents are frequently poorly tolerated or contraindicated in patients with gastrointestinal dysmotility, cardiovascular disease, and/or hypotension—conditions commonly comorbid in scleroderma. 3 Phosphodiesterase inhibitors have shown inconsistent efficacy and share similar adverse effect profiles, as do alpha‐adrenergic antagonists, topical nitrates, intravenous prostacyclin, and endothelin receptor antagonists. 3

Nicotinamide (vitamin B3) is an indirect vasodilator that promotes NAD+ synthesis and exhibits anti‐inflammatory and DNA repair properties. 4 Because excess nicotinamide is not stored in the body, it must be obtained through dietary intake or supplementation. 4 Low nicotinamide levels have been associated with scleroderma‐related fibrosis, with up‐regulation of CD38—an enzyme responsible for NAD+ degradation—being observed in patients with diffuse cutaneous systemic sclerosis. 5 In murine models, NAD+ supplementation attenuated skin and lung fibrosis 5 ; however, the effect of nicotinamide supplementation in humans with scleroderma, including scleroderma‐associated RP, has not been studied.

Compared with standard RP therapies, vitamin B3 is inexpensive and generally safe at doses below 3 g/day. 6 Although nicotinamide is generally well tolerated, reported adverse effects at 500 mg twice daily (BD) include gastrointestinal symptoms, headache, and flushing and, at higher doses (>3 g/day), potential hepatotoxicity, with thrombocytopenia being rare. 6 In Australia, it is available over the counter and has a long history of use in dermatological conditions and pellagra. At a dose of 500 mg BD, no dose adjustment is required for renal or hepatic impairment, and there are no known clinically significant drug interactions. 7 Nicotinamide has a half‐life of approximately 4 hours, with around 70% excreted as metabolites within 96 hours, primarily via the kidneys. 7

In a phase III randomized, placebo‐controlled trial by Chen et al, 8 oral nicotinamide administered at 500 mg BD for 12 months was safe and effective in reducing the incidence of nonmelanoma skin cancer and actinic keratoses in individuals who were at high risk. Treatment adherence was high, with 94% of participants still receiving therapy, and no significant differences in adverse events were observed between the nicotinamide and placebo groups. 8 Nicotinamide has a unique mechanism of action, relative cost‐effectiveness, and a favorable safety profile compared with standard treatments for RP. We therefore aimed to explore if vitamin B3 supplementation could be a safe and effective adjunctive treatment for primary and/or scleroderma‐related RP when taken orally at 500 mg BD dosing. PATIENTS AND METHODS

Recruitment and consent

Participants were recruited from the Canberra Hospital Rheumatology outpatient clinic and private Rheumatology practices within the Australian Capital Territory (ACT) in Australia. Additional community recruitment occurred through Arthritis ACT and the ACT Scleroderma Support Group. Potential participants were screened by the principal and/or associate investigators. Written informed consent was obtained in person from all participants.

Ethics approval was granted by the ACT Human Research Ethics Committee (2025.ETH.00108). The study was conducted in accordance with the National Health and Medical Research Council National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research (2007) and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical trial registration was not required as per ethics approval.

Study design

We conducted a six‐week prospective longitudinal, within‐participant crossover intervention study using a three‐phase study design: A1 (baseline), B (vitamin B3 500 mg BD orally), and A2 (vitamin B3 washout), with each phase being two weeks long (Figure 1). Participants were observed during the winter months (May to September 2025), when mean daily temperatures in this region are typically ≤10°C, to maximize detection of cold‐induced RP symptoms. The rationale for 14 days of vitamin B3 administration was due to its short half‐life and use of similar study timeframes with other RP interventions. 11

At visit 1, potential participants visited the study site for a one‐on‐one interview with the primary investigator (to check eligibility) and, if eligible, moved onto data collection (demographic and RP‐related information collected; BP, NVC, and the SF‐ASRAP questionnaire completed). At the subsequent two visits to the clinic, BP, NVC, and SF‐ASRAP were completed. Participant race and ethnicity were self‐reported, with individuals answering an open‐ended question.

Over the six weeks, participants completed daily paper diaries recording RCS parameters (number of RP episodes per day, average duration of each episode [in minutes], and severity RCS), maximum and minimum daily ambient temperatures, and any medication changes. During the vitamin B3 phase, diaries were kept for potential adverse events and vitamin B3 doses that were missed. Smokers also kept a daily diary of cigarettes consumed throughout the six weeks.

Outcome measures

PROMs

SF‐ASRAP

The SF‐ASRAP (Supplementary Figure 1) is a validated PROM assessing the impact of RP on quality of life over the preceding seven days in patients with systemic sclerosis. 12 It comprises a 10‐item, five‐point Likert scale (rating RP impacts from “not at all” to “very much”). It is designed to measure both the severity and impact of RP by assessing three core domains of (1) physical sensations of RP (pain, numbness, tingling, color change, coldness), (2) functional difficulties with hand use during RP attacks, and (3) emotional impact from RP episodes and daily functional limitations.

The instrument generates a composite score that reflects the overall burden of RP, ranging from 20 to 80, with higher scores indicating worse symptoms and greater functional impairment. A Patient‐Acceptable Symptom State (PASS) threshold of 45.77 has been established, in which patients feel their RP symptoms are manageable at scores below 45.77, and scores above this indicate that their RP‐related quality of life was not acceptable. 12 , 13 A minimally important clinical difference (MICD) cutoff of 3.76 has also been validated, in which a minimum delta of 3.76 or more is required between two SF‐ASRAP scores in time for a meaningful change in RP symptoms between time points. 12 , 13 Therefore, the lower the PASS, the better the symptom state with relation to RP‐related quality of life, and the higher the MICD, the greater the clinical difference in RP‐related quality of life between visits.

The SF‐ASRAP was used in this study as a symptom‐based PROM for primary RP as well because it is likely also useful in assessing primary RP. In particular, the pain domain is of relevance in both primary and secondary RP because digital ischemic episodes commonly manifest with pain, paresthesia, and cold‐induced discomfort irrespective of underlying etiology. Given the lack of validated PROMs specific to primary RP, SF‐ASRAP was selected as an exploratory tool to capture symptom burden.

RCS

The RCS (Supplementary Figure 2) has been widely used and validated in RP clinical trials. It is recognized within the Outcome Measures in Rheumatology framework. 14 The RCS uses a 0 to 10 visual analog scale assessing daily RP severity, accounting for both episode frequency (number of episodes per day) and mean episode duration (in minutes). Higher scores indicate worse RP severity. The MICD is 1.5 points and above, and the PASS is <3.4 of 10. 15

NVC

NVC is a safe, noninvasive gold‐standard technique for assessing microcirculation and distinguishing primary from secondary RP, with established utility in systemic sclerosis. The EULAR Fast Track Algorithm defines five capillaroscopic patterns for patients with scleroderma to improve diagnostic accuracy and interobserver reliability. 16 These patterns are normal, nonspecific, early scleroderma, active scleroderma, and late scleroderma. 16 It is important to note that a diagnosis of scleroderma does not necessarily require an abnormal NVC, as indicated by the 2013 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for systemic sclerosis. 10

The NVC used in this study incorporated artificial intelligence (AI) based on the validated CAPI‐Detect algorithm developed by Capillary.io using machine learning. 17 It recognizes the same five capillaroscopic patterns as the Fast Track Algorithm and has demonstrated superior accuracy to the EULAR Fast Track Algorithm. 17 Its AI algorithm generates automated pattern classification alongside quantitative measures of capillary density, morphology, limb diameters, and abnormal features. 17

In this study, NVC was performed by the principal investigator using a 250× USB capillaroscope with Capillary.io AI software. Microscope oil was applied to the nailfolds of the index to fifth digit of both hands, and 4 images per finger (32 NVC images per patient, per visit) were captured, as per EULAR recommendations. 16 AI outputs were reviewed by the principal investigator for quality control. All assessments were conducted in a temperature‐controlled clinic room.

In this study, participants with primary RP had no clinical or serological features of an underlying connective tissue disease at baseline. Patients with primary RP can indeed have abnormal NVCs, as demonstrated by a study looking at the prognostic value of abnormal NVC patterns in patients with primary RP, who had no clinical or serological features of a connective tissue disease at baseline. 18 They observed 3,029 patients with primary RP at six‐month intervals for about 4.8 years and found that scleroderma NVC patterns were significantly associated with developing scleroderma, dermatomyositis, or mixed connective tissue disease. 18 For participants with primary RP, some did have abnormal NVC patterns. This subgroup may represent primary RP patients who are at risk of developing a connective tissue disorder in the future, but who do not currently have clinical or serological features of a connective tissue disease at current.

To assess changes in NVC pattern between visits, we assigned numbers to each of the five patterns as follows: normal (0), nonspecific (1), early scleroderma (2), active scleroderma (3), and late scleroderma (4). Directional categorical change in nailfold capillary pattern was scored from −3 to +3, in which negative values indicate improvement in pattern and positive values indicate progression in pattern, with 0 indicating no change (Supplementary Table 1). Level of improvement or progression (deterioration) in pattern was defined by comparing pattern changes between visits. For example, if the pattern was active scleroderma at visit 1 and early scleroderma at visit 2, the improvement would be 2 minus 3, equaling −1 (mild categorial improvement).

Statistical analysis

STATA 18.5 was used for all statistical analyses. Power calculations indicated that a total sample size of 17 participants per group (including an anticipated 10% withdrawal rate) would provide adequate statistical power (95%) for repeated measure analysis, hypothesizing a small effect size of vitamin B3 (f = 0.2), a two‐sided alpha of 0.05, and three repeated measures: (1) frequency of RP episodes, (2) severity of RP episodes (RCS), and (3) quality‐of‐life impact assessed using the SF‐ASRAP. A high correlation among repeated measures (P = 0.9) was assumed. Accordingly, a total sample size of fewer than 40 participants was considered sufficient to detect small effect size differences.

Descriptive statistics of demographics and disease characteristics were stratified by primary versus scleroderma‐related RP groups. Continuous variables were compared using Student's t‐tests, whereas categorical variables were compared using Pearson χ2 tests. Longitudinal changes in NVC patterns and parameters were assessed using Student's t‐tests and Pearson χ2, as appropriate. Analysis for SF‐ASRAP and RCS outcomes were conducted using the MICD and PASS cutoffs.

For both SF‐ASRAP scores and the cohort's RCS diary outcomes, multivariate regression models were employed to adjust for participants’ age and minimum to maximum ambient temperatures, given the potential influence of temperature on subjective RP experiences. To estimate the marginal effect of vitamin B3 at the intervention visit (visit 2) and to compare responses between primary RP and scleroderma‐related RP groups, three independent variables were included in the model: primary (indicator variable equals 1 if participant belongs to the primary group), visit 2 (indicator variable equal to 1 for visit 2), and the interaction term between the primary RP group and visit 2 (visit 2 multiplied by primary).

RESULTS

Demographics

A total of 43 participants were recruited. Two withdrew due to inability to comply with the study schedule (one with primary RP and one with scleroderma‐related RP). One additional participant with scleroderma‐related RP was excluded because of insufficient capillary visualization on NVC due to darker skin pigmentation. Two participants with primary RP were excluded due to changes in vasoactive medications during the study period (one participant stopped methylphenidate during the vitamin B3 phase of the study; the other took tadalafil orally and applied rectogesic ointment to the fingers for RP management on two occasions during the vitamin B3 phase).

Consequently, 38 participants completed the study (20 with primary RP and 18 with scleroderma‐related RP). Among participants with scleroderma, 12 had limited systemic sclerosis and 6 had diffuse systemic sclerosis. Three participants in the primary RP group had chilblains. None of the participants had digital ulcers.

Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics of the study cohort. The mean age of RP onset was approximately 33 years. The cohort comprised 30 female patients and 8 male patients, with the majority identifying as White (84.2%, N = 32 of 38). One participant smoked, and the number of cigarettes smoked a day for this participant did not significantly change throughout the study. Participants with scleroderma‐related RP were significantly older than those with primary RP (mean age 57.4 vs 45.4 years, P = 0.01). The remaining demographic variables and pre‐existing pharmacological and nonpharmacological RP management were similar between groups.

RCS diary adherence and medication changes

In total, there were six weeks of daily RCS diaries for each of the 38 participants, equating to 1,596 diaries. There was excellent adherence to the RCS paper diaries, with 99.5% of diary entries (N = 1,588 of 1,596) completed for the total cohort. None of the study participants had changes to vasoactive medications during the study period.

Safety and tolerability of vitamin B3

In total, each participant was expected to take 28 vitamin B3 tablets, corresponding to a total of 1,064 tablets for the entire cohort. There was excellent adherence to vitamin B3, with only 1.3% of doses (N = 14 of 1,064) missed. Reports of potential adverse events were minimal at 13.2% (N = 5 of 38). Specifically, four participants with primary RP reported mild headache, including one with concomitant light‐headedness, with none of these events leading to vitamin B3 discontinuation. One male participant with scleroderma (calcinosis, RP, esophageal dysmotility, sclerodactyly, telangiectasias [CREST] syndrome variant) and extensive skin telangiectasia discontinued vitamin B3 because of melena. There were no significant systolic BP changes in either group throughout the study. Of the 38 participants, 34 (~89%) completed an optional end‐of‐study survey assessing poststudy completion intentions to continue vitamin B3 supplementation. Among the 34 respondents, 70.6% reported that they would continue supplementation (24 of 34; 13 with primary RP and 11 with scleroderma‐related RP).

Number of NVC images across the three visits

Although 32 images per NVC were taken, the AI‐NVC software excluded suboptimal images (eg, blurry images or too much light reflection from oil) to increase the accuracy of the NVC parameters reported. As per Supplementary Table 2, there were no significant changes in the average number of NVC images analyzed by the AI software for each group at each visit.

NVC pattern changes

With regard to the primary RP group, five participants at baseline had a nonspecific pattern, five had an early scleroderma pattern, and the rest (N = 10 of 33) had a normal pattern. At baseline, for the scleroderma RP group, one participant had a normal pattern, two had nonspecific patterns, three had an early scleroderma pattern, nine had an active scleroderma pattern, and three had a late scleroderma pattern. NVC pattern improved in 44.7% of participants (N = 17 of 38) post–vitamin B3 (ie, comparing visit 2 to 1 in Table 2; P < 0.001), in which 55.2% (N = 21 of 38) had no change in NVC pattern post–vitamin B3 (Table 2). Specifically, for the primary and scleroderma groups, NVC pattern improvements were statistically significant post–vitamin B3 (P < 0.008 and P < 0.014, respectively; Table 2). After the washout phase (ie, visit 3 vs visit 2), 23.6% of participants (N = 9 of 38) had NVC pattern deterioration; the majority of which were a mild categorical progression.

NVC parameter changes

For the total cohort, there was a significant increase in the mean percentage of normal capillaries, along with significant reductions in the mean percentage of abnormal shapes and giant capillaries after 14 days of vitamin B3 supplementation compared with baseline (all P < 0.05; Figure 2A). These percentages were calculated relative to the total capillary count on NVC for each participant. For the total cohort, there was also an increase in the mean percentage of normal capillaries postwashout compared with the post–vitamin B3 phase (P < 0.05; Figure 2A). There was an increased percentage of tortuosities postwashout compared to baseline (P < 0.05). The increase in normal capillaries and the reduction in giant capillaries following vitamin B3 supplementation were driven predominantly by patients with scleroderma‐related RP (Figure 2C).

In this study, a reduction in total capillary number (which includes normal and abnormal capillaries) was observed in the primary RP group following vitamin B3 (Supplementary Table 3; P < 0.01), although the absolute magnitude of change was small and should be interpreted with caution. The proportion of morphologically normal capillaries did increase post–vitamin B3 in this group, although not significantly (Figure 2B). In the scleroderma group, the total number of capillaries did not significantly change post–vitamin B3 (Supplementary Table 3), but the total percentage of normal capillaries did significantly increase post–vitamin B3 in this group (P < 0.05; Figure 2C). There was a significant decrease in hemorrhages post–vitamin B3 in the primary RP group (P = 0.05) but no significant change in the scleroderma group (Supplementary Table 3).

Physiologically, venous limb width exceeds arterial limb width in nailfold capillaries. As expected, the primary RP group exhibited smaller mean capillary limb widths at baseline compared with the scleroderma‐related RP group (means for total cohort: arterial limb width, 12 vs 18.3 μm; venous limb width, 17 vs 26.1 μm; apical limb width, 20.2 vs 30.3 μm). However, the differences in mean limb widths across phases for both groups were not statistically significant.

SF‐ASRAP (RP‐related quality of life)

In the primary RP group, the proportion of participants achieving the PASS increased from 45% at baseline to 75% post–vitamin B3 and remained 75% after the washout period (Table 3). In this group, the mean SF‐ASRAP score postwashout was significantly lower than the 45.77 PASS threshold (P = 0.03; Table 3), and the corresponding change from baseline (ie, visit 3 vs visit 1) exceeded the MICD (P = 0.03; Supplementary Table 4). In contrast, among participants with scleroderma‐related RP, the proportion achieving the PASS was 27.8% at baseline, 33.3% post–vitamin B3, and 27.8% postwashout (Table 3). Neither the mean SF‐ASRAP scores nor their changes from baseline were significantly below the PASS threshold or exceeded the MICD (Supplementary Table 4) at either post–vitamin B3 or postwashout assessments. The above conclusions remain unchanged after multivariate regression to adjust for age and ambient temperature (Supplementary Table 5). We found that the improvement at both visits 2 and 3 were only in the primary RP group because the interaction term (visit 2 multiplied by primary) was statistically significant with a P value of 0.01.

DISCUSSION

Short‐term vitamin B3 supplementation for RP demonstrated excellent adherence and low adverse event profile in this study, with no significant difference in systolic BP. One male participant with scleroderma (CREST syndrome variant) and extensive skin telangiectasia discontinued vitamin B3 due to melena. Significant comorbidities included systemic and pulmonary hypertension, ischemic heart disease, chronic kidney disease, and prior decompensated heart failure. He had a documented history of chronic gastrointestinal blood loss before study enrollment. Approximately one month before commencing vitamin B3, he was admitted to the hospital with symptomatic anemia (hemoglobin 70 g/L), requiring transfusion. He was having six to seven weeks of rectal bleeding, and gastroscopy identified a single gastric angiodysplastic lesion. The working diagnosis was gastrointestinal bleeding secondary to telangiectasia in the context of scleroderma. Hemoglobin improved to 99 g/L on discharge, with resolution of melena. A follow‐up colonoscopy was planned but had not yet occurred at study entry.

During the study period, the participant developed melena. Concomitant medications included sildenafil, low‐dose prednisolone (2.5 mg daily), and intermittent laxatives. He was not taking nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), antiplatelet agents, or anticoagulants. Platelet counts during the study ranged from 130 × 109 to 150 × 109/L. He used tranexamic acid orally as needed for the melena but ultimately had to stop taking vitamin B3. Although vitamin B3 is not typically associated with clinically significant gastrointestinal bleeding, its vasodilatory effects raise a theoretical possibility of exacerbating bleeding in patients with pre‐existing vascular lesions. However, causality cannot be established in this case.

Scleroderma independently increases gastrointestinal bleeding risk (hazard ratio 2.98, 95% confidence interval 2.21–4.02). 19 Future studies should exclude patients with a history of melena, gastric antral vascular ectasia, or known gastrointestinal telangiectasia or those using NSAIDs, antiplatelet agents, or anticoagulants. In future studies, when discussing vitamin B3 side effects with participants, melena should be reported as a potential adverse effect, especially in the scleroderma group.

Following vitamin B3 supplementation, participants with primary RP demonstrated improvements in quality of life that persisted into the washout phase. The primary RP group also demonstrated a significant reduction in the severity of RP post–vitamin B3 and into the washout phase. These significant results in the primary RP group were robust even after adjustment for participant age and ambient temperature.

Despite the scleroderma‐related RP group having no significant improvements in subjective PROMs, both the primary and scleroderma groups had significant improvements in objective NVC patterns post–vitamin B3. Both groups also had improvements in certain objective NVC parameters as well. NVC pattern improvements persisted into the washout phase in both groups, particularly in the primary RP group. These ongoing improvements in the washout phase for the primary RP group for both the NVC pattern analysis and PROMs could suggest that vitamin B3 may induce functional microvascular effects beyond its pharmacological half‐life.

The total capillaries post–vitamin B3 for the scleroderma group did not significantly change. The relatively smaller NVC pattern improvements in the scleroderma group compared to the primary group, alongside limited PROM gains, support the notion that scleroderma microvasculature may be more resistant to vasodilation, likely due to structural remodeling. Other possibilities for the disparity between the subjective and objective results in the scleroderma cohort may reflect heightened symptom perception in scleroderma or the broader impact of systemic disease on SF‐ASRAP quality‐of‐life scores.

Capillary density remains a key capillaroscopic parameter, with loss linked to adverse outcomes. In this study, capillary number decreased slightly in primary RP (minimal change), whereas normal capillaries increased nonsignificantly. In scleroderma, capillary number was unchanged, but normal capillaries increased significantly post–vitamin B3. Overall, this suggests altered capillary morphology or visibility rather than true loss, possibly reflecting improved perfusion or recruitment.

Although there were relatively quick changes in the NVC patterns and parameters, it has been found in another study that acute systemic hypoxia in healthy participants resulted in a statistically significant increase in nailfold capillary apical, arterial and venous diameter and was still responsive postocclusive reactive hyperemia and venous congestion. 20 Notably, one participant with primary RP with chilblains experienced complete resolution after two weeks of vitamin B3, with recurrence during washout (Supplementary Figure 5), suggesting potential real‐world efficacy. AI‐NVC analysis via the CAPI‐Detect algorithm has improved accuracy and reproducibility compared to manual scoring approaches and screening algorithms such as the EULAR fast‐track method. 16

These relatively quick improvements in NVC pattern and parameters could be explained by early, quantitatively detected changes in microvascular features, which may precede—but do not confirm—structural vascular remodeling. This study was adequately powered, with high vitamin B3 adherence and daily RCS diary completion. The study, which was conducted between May and September (winter) to reduce temperature‐related variability in RP severity, was supported by daily ambient temperature diaries. Temperature was included as a covariate in the regression model, and the observed associations remained statistically significant after adjustment for temperature.

This study adopted a pragmatic, real‐world approach to participant inclusion rather than applying extensive exclusion of potential secondary contributors such as medication‐induced vasospasm or conditions that may mimic RP. Although this enhances external validity and reflects clinical heterogeneity encountered in routine practice, it may also introduce diagnostic overlap and confounding effects. Future studies may benefit from stratified designs or more stringent exclusion criteria to reduce heterogeneity and further delineate treatment effects.

Although no placebo group was included, the within‐participant crossover intervention in the A1–B–A2 design allowed participants to serve as their own controls. However, causal inference regarding the effects of nicotinamide is limited given the absence of a placebo‐controlled group. The two groups were comparable in demographics and RP management, minimizing confounding. However, the predominantly White, nonsmoking study population may limit generalizability.

NVC imaging may be limited in individuals with increased skin pigmentation due to reduced capillary visualization. This reflects a recognized limitation of conventional white‐light videocapillaroscopy. Emerging technologies, including green‐light and multispectral videocapillaroscopy, have shown to improve capillary visualization in darker skin tones. This limitation may therefore be mitigated in future studies with the use of alternative imaging modalities, improving inclusivity and generalizability of microvascular assessments.

PROMs are inherently susceptible to placebo response and expectation effects, and this should be considered when interpreting the study findings. The short treatment duration and follow‐up may not capture longer‐term structural microvascular changes on NVC, particularly in scleroderma, in which vascular remodeling occurs over extended timeframes. NVC assessments were performed by a single, nonblinded capillaroscopist, which may introduce observer bias; however, this was mitigated by the use of AI‐assisted analysis. Prior work using laser Doppler flowmetry has demonstrated that short‐term vasodilator therapy can improve peripheral blood perfusion without corresponding changes in NVC morphology, suggesting a dissociation between functional and structural vascular responses. 21 Incorporation of complementary functional measures in future studies could help distinguish early perfusion‐related effects of vitamin B3 from longer‐term structural microvascular remodeling.

In conclusion, oral nicotinamide 500 mg BD for 14 days was largely safe and well tolerated in both primary and scleroderma‐related RP groups. Participants with primary RP demonstrated significant improvements in PROMs, whereas subjective improvements in scleroderma‐related RP were not significant. However, AI‐assisted NVC demonstrated significant improvements in quantitative capillary parameters in both groups. Given the short treatment duration, these NVC changes may not represent true structural vascular remodeling and may reflect functional microvascular effects, including improved capillary perfusion and recruitment. Apparent shifts in NVC pattern classification should therefore be interpreted with caution because they may reflect threshold effects applied to continuous, AI‐derived variables rather than definitive reversal of established microangiopathy.

The more limited subjective response in scleroderma‐related RP may reflect reduced vasodilatory responsiveness in the context of established structural vascular disease. Improvements observed during the washout phase suggest that nicotinamide may exert sustained functional microvascular effects beyond its pharmacokinetic half‐life. Larger randomized, placebo‐controlled trials with longer duration and/or higher dosing—particularly in scleroderma‐related RP—are required to determine whether these early functional changes translate into meaningful and sustained clinical or structural vascular outcomes.
