The contact temperature of arbitrary quantum states
Alain Joye, Marco Merkli

Abstract
An intuitive scheme to assign a temperature to an arbitrary state of a quantum system is to investigate the heat flow resulting from the coupling to a thermometer. We introduce a simple model of a universal thermometer with the following property. When it is prepared in a Gibbs equilibrium state at inverse temperature β ∈ ℝ \beta\in\mathbb{R} and brought into thermal contact with a system in any state, the heat flow between the system and thermometer vanishes for a unique value of β \beta . We call this value the contact temperature β c ∈ ℝ \beta_{\rm c}\in\mathbb{R} of the system state. The thermometer is universal in that it yields a unique contact temperature for arbitrary states of finite dimensional quantum systems.



1
Introduction

The equilibrium state of a quantum system whose dynamics is generated by a Hamiltonian
H
is given by the Gibbs density matrix

\gamma_{\beta}=\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{{\rm Tr}\,e^{-\beta H}}\,,

where
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
is the (inverse) equilibrium temperature. The Gibbs density matrix is obtained as the maximizer of the entropy under the constraint of a fixed mean energy. Different approaches have been proposed to extend the notion of temperature to general quantum states
\rho
, see the review
[
4
]
. Among them is the effective temperature
\beta^{*}
of
\rho
defined as the Gibbs equilibrium temperature resulting in the same average energy, determined by
{\rm Tr}(H\rho)={\rm Tr}(H\gamma_{\beta^{*}})
. The effective
\beta^{*}
is the unique minimizer of the function
\beta\mapsto S(\rho|\gamma_{\beta})
, the von Neumann relative entropy of
\rho
with respect to the Gibbs state. An approach parallel to standard (equilibrium) thermodynamics is developed in
[
1
]
, where a non-equilibrium temperature is constructed as the partial derivative of the von Neumann entropy with respect to an internal energy. In the current work we follow a more concrete, operational strategy for defining the temperature, in line with some previous work
[
17
]
. There a contact temperature is defined for classical thermodynamical systems. The idea is to couple the system in a non equilibrium state to a thermostat in equilibrium and to analyze for which thermostat temperature the total heat exchange through the contact boundary vanishes. In the current paper we develop this idea for quantum systems. We assign a temperature to arbitrary states
\rho
of any (finite dimensional) quantum system by coupling it to a thermometer or thermostat, an (infinite dimensional) quantum system in thermal equilibrium. The contact with the thermometer is mediated by an interaction producing equal transition probabilities between all system+thermometer states at a fixed total energy. We show that this results in a single value of the thermometer temperature before the coupling, for which the heat flow vanishes. This value is the effective contact temperature of the system. The contact temperature can be positive or negative, as is the case for virtual temperatures in thermal machines
[
2
]
and effective temperatures of a related approach
[
14
]
, where the authors introduce two temperatures
\beta_{c}
and
\beta_{h}
(cold and hot) associated to a quantum state
\rho
, defined by the ability of the system in the state
\rho
to either furnish or extract energy when coupled to reservoirs.

1.1
Outline of approach and of main results

We now explain our main results in a somewhat informal way and we present the rigorous aspects in the coming sections. The system, called
{\rm A}
, is a
d
-level quantum system with a Hamiltonian
H_{\rm A}
having non-degenerate energies
\varepsilon_{1}<\cdots<\varepsilon_{d}
. The thermometer, or system
{\rm B}
, has Hamiltonian
H_{\rm B}
with eigenvalues
\eta_{k}=k/N
,
k=0,1,\ldots
and
N
large.
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}
are coupled by an energy exchange interaction described by a
thermal process

U
, a unitary acting on the total complex
{\rm A}+{\rm B}
, having the commutation property

[U,H_{\rm A}+H_{\rm B}]=0.

As a consequence of the commutation property, a change in the energy of
{\rm A}
is entirely due to a change in the energy of
{\rm B}
and vice versa. Given a state (density matrix)
\rho
of
{\rm A}
, our main quantity of interest is the change in the energy of
{\rm A}
due to the contact with
{\rm B}
in the Gibbs state

\tau_{\beta}=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{\rm B}}}{{\rm Tr}e^{-\beta H_{\rm B}}},

which is given by

Q_{\rm A}(\beta)=\mathrm{Tr}_{{\rm A}{\rm B}}{\big(U(\rho\otimes\tau_{\beta})U^{*}H_{\rm A}\big)}-\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm A}{(\rho H_{\rm A})}.

The state
\rho\otimes\tau_{\beta}
of A+B before the contact is factorized, and so the energy change may be interpreted as heat. We call
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
the energy (or heat) flow resulting from the contact with the thermometer. The thermometer Gibbs state
\tau_{\beta}
is defined only for
\beta>0
as
{\rm Tr}e^{-\beta H_{\rm B}}=\infty
for
\beta\leq 0
, because
{\rm B}
has infinitely many energy levels. Thus in the above definition of
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
we consider
\beta>0
. As expected, the flow vanishes if
{\rm A}
is in the Gibbs state at the same temperature as
{\rm B}
: for
\rho=\gamma_{\beta}
we have
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)=0
, which follows from the commutation property
[U,H_{\rm A}+H_{\rm B}]=0
.

In order to guarantee a good thermal contact between
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}
, the two systems should be able to exchange energy. In the theory of open quantum systems, particularly in weak coupling regimes, well-coupledness is often stated as a ‘Fermi Golden Rule’ condition. While the interaction in the present work is not scaled by any coupling constant, our well-coupledness condition shares the same two central physical aspects with the Fermi Golden Rule. Namely, the system and thermometer (reservoir) should have compatible Bohr energies (energy differences) and the interaction should allow energy transitions in tandem, within the system and the reservoir. In the current setup the total energy is conserved by
U
, so that energetic transitions in
{\rm A}
must be accompanied by equal and opposite energetic transitions in
{\rm B}
, that is, the Bohr energies of
{\rm A}
should also be Bohr energies of
{\rm B}
. Given that the thermometer spectrum is
k/N
,
k=0,1,\ldots
this leads us to the following assumption.

(i) For all
\ell,m
, the difference
|\varepsilon_{\ell}-\varepsilon_{m}|
must be a multiple of
1/N
.

In order to allow for energy exchange transitions, the unitary
U
must have non-vanishing matrix elements between energy eigenstates of
H_{\rm A}+H_{\rm B}
. We consider a thermal contact for which all equal energy transitions are
equiprobable
. Namely, we assume that

(ii) The transition probabilities induced by
U
, between any two equal energy eigenstates of the uncoupled Hamiltonian
H_{\rm A}+H_{\rm B}
, are the same.

The equal probability property (ii) is an expression of
randomness
, which we think is natural for complex or ‘generic’ systems. Namely, we show in Section
2
that for the purpose of the heat flow, the property (ii) is equivalent to taking the unitary
U
as a random operator, drawn according to the unique, bi-invariant probability measure on the unitary group (the Haar measure).
Besides its spectrum, the manner how the thermometer couples to systems
{\rm S}
is an equally important part of its definition. In our model this manner is encoded in the operator
U
which depends on the spectrum of
{\rm S}
due to the condition (ii). Part of the definition of the thermometer, therefore, is that it will couple to any system in a fashion which produces equal probability energy transitions, irrespective of the detailed nature of
{\rm S}
.

Even a small change in the system energies can alter the Bohr resonance condition (i) and simultaneously the structure of
U
. This may lead to a decreased value of the mathematical expression of the heat flow. For example, take energies
\varepsilon_{\ell}
such that (i) is satisfied with a resulting
Q_{\rm A}>0
. An infinitesimal change of the energies into new ones with the property that for all
\ell\neq m
, the difference
|\varepsilon^{\prime}_{\ell}-\varepsilon^{\prime}_{m}|
is not an exact multiple of
1/N
, implies that
U^{\prime}
satisfying condition (ii) with the new energies, reduces to (a direct sum of) pure phases and consequently
Q^{\prime}_{\rm A}=0
. The flow is thus not continuous in the system energies, when one only considers (ii) without (i). Physically, however, a drastic change in the heat flow under infinitesimal alteration of the energy spectrum of
{\rm S}
is unrealistic. The conditions (i) and (ii) work together on a physical level. Together they eliminate this unnatural instability. Still, given an arbitrary energy spectrum
\{\varepsilon_{\ell}\}
of
{\rm S}
, the rationality condition (i) will not be verified in general. However, for
N
large enough, the condition is satisfied to an arbitrary degree of precision. It turns out, as we show below, that the heat flow
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)

does not depend on
N
. Therefore the condition (i) is not a physically relevant restriction on
{\rm S}
.

In conlcusion, jointly the assumptions (i) and (ii) give a universal thermometer model, yielding a unique contact temperature associated to any state
\rho
of
{\rm S}
, regardless of the physical nature of
{\rm S}
.

Thermodynamic results.
Our first result is Theorem
1
, in which we obtain the following explicit expression for the heat flow,

Q_{\rm A}(\beta)=\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq d}\frac{e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{j})}}{k(k-1)}p_{j}\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}(\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{l})-\sum_{k=2}^{d}\frac{p_{k}}{k}\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}(\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{l}),

where the
p_{j}
are the (
H_{\rm A}
energy) populations of the state
\rho
. The explicit form of the heat flow allows for a direct discussion of its properties (see Section
2
). Our main question now is whether there is a unique root (zero)
\beta_{\rm c}
of the function
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
. We show that (unless
\rho
is the ground state or the top excited state of
H_{\rm A}
),
\beta\mapsto Q(\beta)
is continuous and strictly decreasing in
\beta
(for all
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
) and that
Q(\beta)<0
for
\beta\rightarrow\infty
while

Q_{\rm A}(0):=\lim_{\beta\rightarrow 0_{+}}Q_{\rm A}(\beta)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\varepsilon_{j}(\frac{1}{d}-p_{j}).

Consequently, if
Q_{\rm A}(0)\geq 0
, then there exists a unique
\beta_{\rm c}\geq 0
which makes the flow vanish. The root
\beta_{\rm c}
is called the contact temperature of
\rho
. The existence and uniqueness of
\beta_{\rm c}\geq 0
is the content of our Theorem
2
.

As is readily visible from the explicit expression above,
Q_{\rm A}(0)
is negative for very legitimate states
\rho
(populations
p_{j}
). In this case the monotonicity implies that the function
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
does not have any non-negative root. How should we assign a temperature to states for which
Q_{\rm A}(0)<0
? The physical meaning of this inequality is that the system
{\rm A}
in the state
\rho
will transfer heat to
{\rm B}
even if
{\rm B}
is infinitely hot,
\beta=0_{+}
(measured according to the Gibbs equilibrium temperature
T=1/\beta
). In order to define the contact temperature in the case when
Q_{\rm A}(0)<0
, the first impulse is to use the above explicit formula for
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
for all values of
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
— even though it was derived for
\beta>0
. The monotonicity and the boundary values
\lim_{\beta\rightarrow-\infty}Q_{\rm A}(\beta)=\infty
and
\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\-\infty}Q_{\rm A}(\beta)<0
would then guarantee the existence of a single root in
(-\infty,0)
for
Q_{\rm A}(0)<0
. However, this extension of
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
to negative
\beta
is not physical. Indeed,
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
is supposed to represent the energy difference of a finite system before and after the contact with the thermometer; so
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
must be bounded above by
\varepsilon_{d}-\varepsilon_{1}
and cannot have the above stated boundary condition at
\beta=-\infty
. The origin of this inconsistency is that the Gibbs state
\tau_{\beta}
is ill defined for
\beta\leq 0
, and therefore so is the defining expression of
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
.

Figure 1:
The energy flow
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
(see also the definition (
6
)), is a strictly decreasing, convex function of
\beta>0
and has a well defined limit value
Q_{\rm A}(0)
as
\beta\rightarrow 0_{+}
. If
Q_{\rm A}(0)>0
then there is a unique root
\beta_{\rm c}>0
(blue curve). If
Q_{\rm A}(0)=0
then the unique root is
\beta_{\rm c}=0
(green curve). If
Q_{\rm A}(0)<0
then there is no root in
[0,\infty)
(red curve). However, by extending the function
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
to a function
\bar{Q}(\beta)
also defined for
\beta<0
, we have a unique root
\beta_{\rm c}<0
of the extended function
\bar{Q}(\beta)
. This is the (negative) contact temperature associated to the system state. The question mark indicates that one has to decide what the extension should be. We argue it should be the flow resulting from the coupling to a thermometer having finitely many levels, which is in the Gibbs equilibrium state with
\beta<0
, when the number of levels is taken to infinity.

The physically correct approach is to consider first a finite-level thermometer for which the Gibbs state is defined for all
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, also for
\beta<0
, and then take the limit of infinitely many levels. The
finite-level
thermometer is defined by the Hamiltonian
H_{{\rm B},K}
acting on
\mathbb{C}^{K+1}
with eigenvalues
\eta_{k}=k/N
,
k=0,1,\ldots,K
. The state
\tau_{\beta,K}=e^{-\beta H_{{\rm B},K}}/{\mathrm{Tr}}e^{-\beta H_{{\rm B},K}}
is a well defined density matrix for all
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and determines the heat flow

Q_{{\rm A},K}(\beta)=\mathrm{Tr}{\big(U(\rho\otimes\tau_{\beta,K})U^{*}H_{\rm A}\big)}-\mathrm{Tr}{(\rho H_{\rm A})},

where the trace is over
\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{K+1}
(system plus thermometer Hilbert space) and
U
depends on
K
, as it implements equiprobable energy transitions of
H_{\rm A}+H_{{\rm B},K}
. We show in Theorem
4
that the
\infty
-level limit

\bar{Q}_{\rm A}(\beta)=\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}Q_{{\rm A},K}(\beta)

exists and is given by

\bar{Q}_{\rm A}(\beta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Q^{+}_{\rm A}(\beta):=\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq d}\frac{e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{j})}p_{j}-p_{k}}{k(k-1)}\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}(\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{l}),&\quad\beta>0,\\[15.0pt]
Q^{-}_{\rm A}(\beta):=\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq k<j\leq d}\frac{e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{j})}p_{j}-p_{k}}{(d-k+1)(d-k)}\sum_{l=k+1}^{d}(\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{l}),&\quad\beta\leq 0.\end{array}\right.

This is a continuous extension of
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
, originally defined for
\beta>0
, now extended to
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. The extended function is strictly decreasing (unless
\rho
is the ground or top excited state of
H_{\rm A}
) and satisfies
|\bar{Q}_{\rm A}(\beta)|\leq\varepsilon_{d}-\varepsilon_{1}
for all
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. It has a unique root
\beta_{\rm c}\in\mathbb{R}
which coincides with the previous non-negative root of
Q_{\rm A}
in case
Q_{\rm A}(0)\geq 0
, and it also yields a unique negative root which is defined to be the contact temperature of states
\rho
for which
Q_{\rm A}(0)<0
.

Structural results.
The heat flow
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
is determined by the reduction to the system part alone, of a thermal process (
U
) acting on the system+thermometer complex. We study this structure in a more general setting in Sections
4
and
5
. In the latter section, we consider a bipartite system with Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm R}
(‘system plus reservoir’) with
\dim{\mathcal{H}}=d<\infty
,
\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm R}\leq\infty
, having a non-interacting Hamiltonian
H+H_{\rm R}
. We take a unitary map
V
on
{\mathcal{H}}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm R}
such that
[H+H_{\rm R},V]=0
(thermal process). We define the completely positive and trace preserving (CPTP) map acting on operators
X\in\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}})
on the system, by

\Phi_{\beta}(X)={\rm Tr}_{\rm R}\big(V(X\otimes\tau_{\beta})V^{*}\big),

where
\tau_{\beta}\propto e^{-\beta H_{\rm R}}
is the Gibbs state of
{\rm R}
(and
\beta>0
). Suppose that the eigenvalues
\varepsilon_{j}
of
H
are simple. Due to the conservation of total energy by
V
, the thermal channel
\Phi_{\beta}
leaves the subspace
\mathcal{D}
of diagonal operators (in the eigenbasis of
H
) invariant, and it leaves the off-diagonal operators
\mathcal{O}
invariant as well (see Lemma
1
).
\Phi_{\beta}
has thus the block decomposition

\Phi_{\beta}=\Phi_{\beta}|_{\mathcal{D}}\oplus\Phi_{\beta}|_{\mathcal{O}}.

Denote by
\psi_{j}
the eigenbasis of the system Hamiltonian
H
. The probability of transition
\psi_{j^{\prime}}\rightarrow\psi_{j}
in the system, due to the contact with
{\rm R}
(mediated by
V
), is given by

G_{jj^{\prime}}(\beta)={\rm Tr}\Big(|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|\Phi_{\beta}\big(|\psi_{j^{\prime}}\rangle\langle\psi_{j^{\prime}}|\big)\Big).

This defines a
d\times d
matrix
G(\beta)=(G_{jj^{\prime}}(\beta))
with
G_{jj^{\prime}}(\beta)\geq 0
and
\sum_{j=1}^{d}G_{jj^{\prime}}(\beta)=1
for all
j^{\prime}
, which represents the restriction
\Phi_{\beta}|_{\mathcal{D}}
. So
G(\beta)
is a stochastic matrix, called the stochastic matrix associated to the quantum channel
\Phi_{\beta}
. The vector
v_{\beta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with components
\propto e^{-\beta\varepsilon_{j}}
,
j=1,\ldots,d
represents the system equilibrium (Gibbs) density matrix
\propto e^{-\beta H}
. It is a fixed point of the stochastic matrix,
G(\beta)v_{\beta}=v_{\beta}
. It follows from a Perron-Frobenius argument (see Corollary
2
) that if
G_{jj^{\prime}}(\beta)>0
for all
j,j^{\prime}
and if all the Bohr energies
\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{k}
of
H
are distinct (
j\neq k
), then the eigenvalue
1
of
\Phi_{\beta}
is simple (eigenvector
v_{\beta}
) and all other eigenvalues have modulus strictly less than
1
. These general properties hold in particular for the specific model of the thermometer coupled to the system.

We show in Proposition
1
that for the system-thermometer model, the heat flow and the stochastic matrix are related for
\beta>0
as,

Q_{\rm A}(\beta)={\vec{\varepsilon}}\cdot G(\beta){\vec{p}}-{\vec{\varepsilon}}\cdot{\vec{p}},

where
{\vec{\varepsilon}}=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{d})^{T}
and
{\vec{p}}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})^{T}
are the column vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose entries are the energies of
H_{\rm A}
and populations of
\rho
, and where
G(\beta)
is the
d\times d
stochastic matrix associated to
\Phi_{\beta}
. Moreover, the matrix elements of
G(\beta)
are given by

G_{jj^{\prime}}(\beta)=\sum_{s=\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}}^{d}\frac{1}{s}\big(e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_{s}-\varepsilon_{j^{\prime}})}-e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_{s+1}-\varepsilon_{j^{\prime}})}\big)\ \ \ \text{with \ }\ e^{-\beta\varepsilon_{d+1}}=0,

from which we see directly that
G_{jj^{\prime}}(\beta)>0
, and the eigenvalues of
G(\beta)
are strictly positive. The general theory of reduced thermal channels, as outlined above, then implies the following result (Theorem
5
): If all nonzero Bohr energies
\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{k}
of
H_{\rm A}
are distinct, then
\Phi_{\beta}|_{\mathcal{D}}
has a simple eigenvalue
1
with associated spectral projection
|v_{\beta}\rangle\langle v_{0}|
(
v_{0}
is the vector in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with elements all equal to
1
) and all other eigenvalues of
\Phi_{\beta}|_{\mathcal{D}}
, as well as all eigenvalues of
\Phi_{\beta}|_{\mathcal{O}}
, have modulus strictly less than
1
. The said spectral property implies that the repeated contact of the system with fresh thermometers at temperature
\beta
, resulting in the repeated application of
\Phi_{\beta}
to any initial state
\rho
, will drive the system to the Gibbs state
\gamma_{\beta}=e^{-\beta H}/{\rm Tr}e^{-\beta H}
at the temperature of the thermometers. This in turn means that the contact temperature
\beta_{\rm c}^{(k)}
of
\rho^{(k)}=\Phi_{\beta}^{k}(\rho)
satisfies
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\beta_{\rm c}^{(k)}=\beta
. We discuss the details of this dynamical process of approach to equilibrium in Section
5.1
.

2
Energy flow between system and thermometer

Let
{\rm A}
be a quantum system defined on the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm A}=\mathbb{C}^{d}
with Hamiltonian

H_{\rm A}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\varepsilon_{j}|\psi_{j}^{\rm A}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}^{\rm A}|,

(1)

where the
\varepsilon_{j}
and
\psi_{j}^{\rm A}
are the energies and energy eigenstates. We assume

(ND)

The energies are ordered,
\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}<\cdots<\varepsilon_{d}
and non-degenerate.

States of the system
{\rm A}
are density matrices
\rho
on the system Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm A}
(non-negative operators of unit trace).
The equilibrium state at temperature
T=1/\beta>0
is given by the Gibbs density matrix

\gamma_{\beta}=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{\rm A}}}{Z_{{\rm A}}},

(2)

where
Z_{\rm A}={\rm Tr}e^{-\beta H_{\rm A}}
is the partition function. We call this
\beta
(or
T=1/\beta
) the
equilibrium temperature
.

The Hamiltonian of the bath
{\rm B}
, also called the thermometer, is given by

H_{\rm B}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{m_{\rm B}}\eta_{k}|\psi^{\rm B}_{k,m}\rangle\langle\psi^{\rm B}_{k,m}|=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\eta_{k}P_{k}^{\rm B},

(3)

where the
\eta_{k}\geq 0
are the eigenvalues, all having degeneracy
1\leq m_{\rm B}<\infty
and where
P_{k}^{\rm B}
is the (rank
m_{\rm B}
) spectral projection associated to
\eta_{k}
. The vectors
\{\psi_{k,m}^{\rm B}\}
form an orthonormal basis of the (infinite-dimensional) bath Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm B}
, and the domain of
H_{\rm B}
is the subspace
{\rm dom}(H_{\rm B}):=\{\Psi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm B}\,:\,\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{m_{\rm B}}|\langle\psi_{k,m}^{\rm B}|\Psi\rangle|^{2}\eta_{k}^{2}<\infty\}
. We take the spectrum of the bath to be equally spaced, with small gaps,

(S)

\eta_{k}=\frac{k}{N}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
for an integer
N
. Moreover, the system energies satisfy
\varepsilon_{s}=\varepsilon_{1}+\frac{l_{s}}{N}
for some integers
l_{s}
,
s=1,\ldots,d
, with
0=l_{1}<l_{2}<\cdots<l_{d}
.

As
N(\varepsilon_{s}-\varepsilon_{1})=l_{s}\geq s-1
we must have
N\geq\max_{2\leq s\leq d}\frac{s-1}{\varepsilon_{s}-\varepsilon_{1}}
. In fact, we are interested in taking
N
very large (
\rightarrow\infty
), so that
\rm B
has the characteristics of a macroscopic (reservoir) system. The part of condition (S) saying that the Bohr energies
\varepsilon_{s}-\varepsilon_{s^{\prime}}
of
{\rm A}
are multiples of the basic Bohr energy
1/N
of the thermometer
{\rm B}
guarantees that
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}
are able to exchange energy which causes an energy flow. The thermal equilibrium state of
{\rm B}
is given by

\tau_{\beta}=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{\rm B}}}{Z_{{\rm B}}},\ \ \beta>0,

(4)

where
Z_{{\rm B}}=\mathrm{Tr}(e^{-\beta H_{\rm B}})
and
\beta
is the reservoir equilibrium temperature. The joint system
{\rm A}+{\rm B}
is described by the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm A}{\rm B}}={\mathcal{H}}_{\rm A}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm B}
. Its uncoupled Hamiltonian (dropping
\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}
and
\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}\otimes
from the notation) is

H_{{\rm A}{\rm B}}=H_{\rm A}+H_{\rm B},

with domain
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm A}\otimes{\rm dom}(H_{\rm B})
.
The interaction between
{\rm A}
and
{\rm B}
is given by a thermal process (thermal quantum operation)
U
, which is a unitary operator on
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm A}{\rm B}}
satisfying

[U,H_{{\rm A}{\rm B}}]=0.

(5)

The energy flow
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
is defined as the difference of the energy of
{\rm A}
after and before the interaction with
{\rm B}
,

Q_{\rm A}(\beta)=\mathrm{Tr}{\big(U(\rho\otimes\tau_{\beta})U^{*}H_{\rm A}\big)}-\mathrm{Tr}{(\rho H_{\rm A})}.

(6)

The first trace is over
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm A}{\rm B}}
, the second one over
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm A}
. The flow (
6
) is invariant under translations of
H_{\rm A}
by
\alpha\mathbb{I}
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Due to (
5
) and the invariance of the trace under unitary conjugation, we have
\mathrm{Tr}(U(\rho\otimes\tau_{\beta})U^{*}(H_{\rm A}+H_{\rm B}))=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho H_{\rm A})+\mathrm{Tr}(\tau_{\beta}H_{\rm B})
. This shows that
U(\rho\otimes\tau_{\beta})U^{*}H_{\rm B}
has finite trace and that we have
Q_{A}(\beta)+Q_{B}(\beta)=0
, where
Q_{B}(\beta)=\mathrm{Tr}{(U(\rho\otimes\tau_{\beta})U^{*}H_{\rm B})}-\mathrm{Tr}{(\tau_{\beta}H_{\rm B})}
. If
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)>0
then
{\rm A}
receives energy due to the interaction with
{\rm B}
. In the opposite case
{\rm A}
gives energy to
{\rm B}
.

We introduce a genericity assumption on the thermal process
U
which can be formulated in two equivalent ways: either in terms of uniformity or in terms of randomness. We show in Theorem
1
that they are equivalent, for the purpose of the energy flow. The genericity assumption in terms of uniformity is:

(G)

The transition probability induced by
U
, between any two equal energy eigenstates of the uncoupled Hamiltonian
H_{{\rm A}{\rm B}}
, are the same.

Denoting the distinct eigenvalues of
H_{{\rm A}{\rm B}}=H_{\rm A}+H_{\rm B}
by
E_{\ell}
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
we have the spectral representation

H_{{\rm A}{\rm B}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}E_{\ell}P_{\ell}.

(7)

The
P_{\ell}
are the eigenprojections with
\dim P_{\ell}=g_{\ell}\geq 1
, where
g_{\ell}
is the (finite) degeneracy of the eigenvalues
E_{\ell}
. The Hilbert space is partitioned into spectral subspaces

{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm A}{\rm B}}=\bigoplus_{\ell=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm A}{\rm B}}^{(\ell)},\qquad{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm A}{\rm B}}^{(\ell)}={\rm Ran}P_{\ell}.

Due to (
5
) we have

U=\bigoplus_{\ell=0}^{\infty}U^{(\ell)}
for unitaries
U^{(\ell)}
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm A}{\rm B}}^{(\ell)}
.
The genericity assumption in terms of randomness is the following:

(R)

The
U^{(\ell)}
are independent random unitaries for different
\ell
. For any fixed
\ell
,
U^{(\ell)}
is distributed according to the Haar measure on the group of unitaries acting on
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm A}{\rm B}}^{(\ell)}
.

The Haar measure is the unique probability measure on the unitary group that is invariant under left and right multiplication by any unitary matrix.

Under the assumption
(R)
the energy flow (
6
) is a random quantity
Q_{A}(\beta)=Q_{\rm A}(\beta,\omega)
, where
\omega
indicates the randomness parameter. The next result gives an explicit form of the flow and shows that the average (expectation),
\mathbb{E}[Q_{\rm A}(\beta,\omega)]
is
the same
as the
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
, (
6
) obtained under the genericity assumption
(G)
.

Theorem 1

(Explicit form of the energy flow)
.

Assume that either
(G)
or
(R)
hold. Let
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
denote the flow (
6
) in the former case, and let
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
denote the expectation of the flow (
6
) in the latter case. Then we have for any
\beta>0
,

\displaystyle Q_{\rm A}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq d}\frac{e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{j})}}{k(k-1)}p_{j}\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}(\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{l})-\sum_{k=2}^{d}\frac{p_{k}}{k}\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}(\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{l}).

(8)

Moreover,

Q_{\rm A}(0):=\lim_{\beta\rightarrow 0_{+}}Q_{\rm A}(\beta)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\varepsilon_{j}(\frac{1}{d}-p_{j}).

(9)

We prove Theorem
1
in Section
A.1
.

Discussion and remarks.
(i) It is manifest from (
8
) that the flow
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
is independent of the spectral gap
1/N
of
{\rm B}
as well as the bath multiplicity
m_{\rm B}
(see (
3
)). It can be written equivalently as

Q_{\rm A}(\beta)=\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq d}\frac{e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{j})}p_{j}-p_{k}}{k(k-1)}\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}(\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{l}).

(10)

(ii) As the
\varepsilon_{k}
are ordered, (
8
) is the difference of two non-negative terms, one depending on
\beta
, the other not. The sign of
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
is thus determined by the relative size of these two terms, and
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)=0
when the two are equal.

(iii) The first term in (
8
) does not depend on
p_{d}
, the population of the most excited state. Is is strictly positive unless
p_{d}=1
, in which case it vanishes. The second term in (
8
) which is subtracted from the first one, does not depend on the ground state population
p_{1}
. It is strictly positive unless
p_{1}=1
, in which case it vanishes.

(iv) The expression (
10
) shows that
Q_{A}(\beta)>0
if
e^{\beta\varepsilon_{k}}p_{k}
is decreasing in
k
and
Q_{A}(\beta)<0
if
e^{\beta\varepsilon_{k}}p_{k}
is increasing in
k
. For
e^{\beta\varepsilon_{k}}p_{k}
constant in
k
,
i.e.

p_{k}=e^{-\beta\varepsilon_{k}}/Z_{\rm A}
(Gibbs state) we have
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)=0
.

3
The contact temperature

Suppose the flow
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
, given by (
6
) for
\beta>0
, has a unique root
\beta_{\rm c}>0
. Then this root is the contact temperature of the state
\rho
of
{\rm A}
. The function
\beta\mapsto Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
has the limit (
9
) as
\beta\rightarrow 0_{+}
. Physically,
Q_{\rm A}(0)
is the energy flow of the system
{\rm A}
(positive for an inflow of energy) caused by the contact with a thermometer
{\rm B}
which has infinite equilibrium temperature
T=1/\beta=\infty
. The second law of thermodynamics says that energy will flow from a hotter body to a colder body — if both bodies are in (or close to) thermal equilibrium. In this situation we must have
Q_{\rm A}(0)>0
. However, if the state
\rho
of
{\rm A}
is
not
close to equilibrium, then there is no reason to expect
Q_{\rm A}(0)>0
and indeed, (
9
) characterizes all
\rho
with
Q_{\rm A}(0)<0
. Such
\rho
are ‘far from equilibrium’ and they violate the second law of equilibrium thermodynamics — the system
{\rm A}
in such a state will give energy when coupled to a thermometer in equilibrium at arbitrarily high temperature. This is counterintuitive only if we follow the erroneous instinct to apply the second law of thermodynamics to systems far from equilibrium.

Two extreme cases: ground and most excited states.
The energy flow associated to the ground state
\rho=|\psi^{\rm A}_{1}\rangle\langle\psi^{\rm A}_{1}|
is

Q_{\rm A}(\beta)=\sum_{k=2}^{d}\frac{e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{1})}}{k(k-1)}\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}(\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{l})>0,

which is strictly positive for all
\beta>0
and satisfies
\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}Q_{\rm A}(\beta)=0
. So it makes sense to associate
\beta_{\rm c}=\infty
to the ground state of
{\rm A}
. For the most excited state
\rho=|\psi^{\rm A}_{d}\rangle\langle\psi^{\rm A}_{d}|
, (
8
) gives

Q_{\rm A}(\beta)=-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{l=1}^{d-1}(\varepsilon_{d}-\varepsilon_{l})<0.

(11)

This is a strictly negative constant, independent of
\beta
and it has no root. We will see in Theorem
3
that it is natural to assign
\beta_{\rm c}=-\infty
to the most excited state.

We show in the next two sections that there is a finite
\beta_{\rm c}\in\mathbb{R}
for
any
state
\rho
other than the ground and top excited states. Moreover,
\beta_{\rm c}>0
for
Q_{\rm A}(0)>0
and
\beta_{\rm c}<0
for
Q_{\rm A}(0)<0
, while
Q_{\rm A}(0)=0
corresponds to
\beta_{\rm c}=0
.

3.1
Positive contact temperature

As discussed above,
Q_{\rm A}(0)\geq 0
is a consequence of the second law of thermodynamics if the state
\rho
is close to equilibrium. Here we assign a
\beta_{\rm c}\geq 0
to any state
\rho
with
Q_{\rm A}(0)\geq 0
.

Theorem 2

(Existence and regularity of non-negative contact temperature)
.

Let
\rho
be any state of
{\rm A}
other than the ground state or the most excited state of
H_{\rm A}
.

(i)

If
Q_{\rm A}(0)>0
then there exists a unique
\beta_{\rm c}>0
such that
Q_{\rm A}(\beta_{\rm c})=0
.

Moreover,
\beta_{\rm c}
is a real analytic function of the populations of
\rho
, that is of the probability vector
\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d}
, on the open simplex
{\mathcal{S}}=\{(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{d})\in(\mathbb{R}_{+}\backslash\{0\})^{d}\ |\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}=1\}
.

(ii)

If
Q_{\rm A}(0)=0
then
\beta_{\rm c}=0
is the unique root of
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
in
[0,\infty)
.

Passive and active states.
Consider a Hamiltonian
H={\rm diag}(E_{1},\ldots,E_{d})
, with possibly degenerate energies. A state
\rho
is called passive if and only if
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho H)\leq\mathrm{Tr}(V\rho\,V^{*}H)
for all unitaries
V
. If
\rho
is not a passive state, then it is called an active state. One can show that
\rho
is passive if and only if
\rho={\rm diag}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})
is diagonal in the
H
basis and
E_{j}\leq E_{k}\Rightarrow p_{j}\geq p_{k}

[
9
]
. For our model, it follows from (
9
) and the fact that the eigenvalues
\varepsilon_{j}
of
H_{\rm A}
are ordered (see the condition (ND)), that
Q_{\rm A}(0)\geq 0
for all passive states, see the Remark (iv) below Theorem
1
for
\beta=0
.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\rho
be a passive state other than the ground state of
H_{\rm A}
. Then
\beta_{\rm c}>0
. There are active states for which
\beta_{\rm c}>0
.

Example.
One may rewrite (
9
) as

Q_{\rm A}(0)=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\Big(p_{j}-\frac{1}{d}\Big)(\varepsilon_{d}-\varepsilon_{j}).

For a qubit (
d=2
) we have
Q_{\rm A}(0)\geq 0\Leftrightarrow p_{1}\geq p_{2}
, with equivalence of the equalities. So
\beta_{\rm c}\geq 0
is equivalent with a diagonal
\rho
being a passive state. For
d=3
,
Q_{\rm A}(0)=(p_{1}-\frac{1}{3})(\varepsilon_{3}-\varepsilon_{1})+(p_{2}-\frac{1}{3})(\varepsilon_{3}-\varepsilon_{2})
. By choosing
p_{2}>p_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}<\varepsilon_{3}
such that
Q_{\rm A}(0)>0
we obtain an active state
\rho={\rm diag}(p_{1},p_{2},1-p_{1}-p_{2})
for which
\beta_{\rm c}>0
. In particular, any
p_{1},p_{2}
with
\frac{1}{3}<p_{1}<p_{2}
will give such an active state.

Proof of Theorem
2
.
The proof of Theorem
2
is simple. Viewing
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
, (
8
) as a function of
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, we have for

,

\lim_{\beta\rightarrow-\infty}Q_{\rm A}(\beta)=\infty,\qquad\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}Q_{\rm A}(\beta)=-\sum_{k=2}^{d}\frac{p_{k}}{k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}(\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{\ell})<0.

Consequently as the function
\beta\mapsto Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
is continuous it must have at least one root in
\mathbb{R}
. This root is actually unique, because the function is strictly decreasing. In fact, it follows directly from (

) that for
n=1,2,\ldots

(and
p_{1}\neq 1
,
p_{d}\neq 1
),

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}\partial_{\beta}^{n}Q_{\rm A}(\beta)>0.

(12)

We see directly that the unique root satisfies
\beta_{\rm c}>0\Leftrightarrow Q_{\rm A}(0)>0
— see also Fig. ​
1

Let us turn to the regularity of
\beta_{\rm c}
in the probability vector
\mathbf{p}:=\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d}
for
Q_{\rm A}(0)>0
. We write

when we consider
\mathbf{p}
as a variable. The expression (

) gives the extension of

to

and being linear in

. By the argument above, for any
\mathbf{p}\in]0,1[^{d}
, there exists a unique
\beta(\mathbf{p})>0
such that

. The analytic implicit function theorem (see

[
5
]
, Chap. X, §2, (10.2.1) and (10.2.4)) implies that the map
]0,1[^{d}\ni\mathbf{p}\mapsto\beta(\mathbf{p})
is real analytic. Restricting this function
\beta
to
\mathcal{S}\subset]0,1[^{d}
by expressing one

as a function of the others, we get a real analytic function which coincides with
\beta_{\rm c}(\mathbf{p})
.
∎

As seen in point (iv) after Theorem

we have

3.2
Negative contact temperature

We now consider the situation
Q_{\rm A}(0)<0
which is complementary to that treated in Theorem
2
.
To derive the existence of
\beta_{\rm c}\geq 0
in Theorem
2
we argued that we may consider
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
as a function of
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
— simply extending the expression (
8
) of Theorem
1
to all
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. For
\beta>0
the quantity
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
has a physical meaning (difference of energy of
{\rm A}
before and after the effect of the thermometer). But for
\beta<0
it does not. Even though the expression (
8

Consider then a truncated version of the thermometer Hamiltonian given in (
3

H_{{\rm B},K}=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\eta_{k}|\psi^{\rm B}_{k}\rangle\langle\psi_{k}^{\rm B}|,\qquad\eta_{k}=\frac{k}{N},

(13)

where

). The thermometer Gibbs state can now be defined for any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
as in (

\tau_{\beta,K}=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{{\rm B},K}}}{Z_{{\rm B},K}},\qquad\text{with}\qquad Z_{{\rm B},K}=\frac{1-e^{-\frac{\beta}{N}(K+1)}}{1-e^{-\frac{\beta}{N}}}.

(14)

(For
\beta=0

{\rm A}
is defined as in (

Q_{{\rm A},K}=\mathrm{Tr}{\big(U(\rho\otimes\tau_{\beta,K})U^{*}H_{\rm A}\big)}-\mathrm{Tr}{(\rho H_{\rm A})},

(15)

where

satisfies (
5

H_{{\rm A}{\rm B}}=H_{\rm A}+H_{{\rm B},K}
, and thus depends on

\bar{Q}_{\rm A}(\beta)=\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}Q_{{\rm A},K}(\beta).

(16)

Then we have the following result

Theorem 3
.

(1)

Suppose
\rho
is neither the ground nor the most excited state of
H_{\rm A}
. Then there exists a unique
\beta_{\rm c}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\bar{Q}_{\rm A}(\beta_{\rm c})=0
. Moreover,
\beta_{\rm c}
has the same sign as
\bar{Q}_{\rm A}(0)
and
\beta_{\rm c}=0
if and only if
\bar{Q}_{\rm A}(0)=0
.

For
\bar{Q}_{\rm A}(0)\neq 0
,
\beta_{\rm c}
is a real analytic function of the probability vector
\mathbf{p}=\{p_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d}
(the popluations of
\rho
) on the open simplex
{\mathcal{S}}=\{(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{d})\in(\mathbb{R}_{+}\backslash\{0\})^{d}\ |\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}=1\}
.

(2)

If
\rho
is the ground state, then
\bar{Q}_{\rm A}(\beta)>0
for all
\beta\in\mathbb{R},
and
\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\bar{Q}_{\rm A}(\beta)=0
. If
\rho
is the most excited state, then
\bar{Q}_{\rm A}(\beta)<0
for all
\beta\in\mathbb{R},
and
\lim_{\beta\rightarrow-\infty}\bar{Q}_{\rm A}(\beta)=0
.

In accordance with statement (2) of Theorem
3
we assign
\beta_{\rm c}=\infty
to the ground state and
\beta_{\rm c}=-\infty
to the most excited state.

Theorem

.

Theorem 4
.

Let

. Then we have

\bar{Q}_{\rm A}(\beta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Q^{+}_{\rm A}(\beta):=\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq d}\frac{e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{j})}p_{j}-p_{k}}{k(k-1)}\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}(\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{l}),&\quad\beta>0,\\[15.0pt]

Both

are defined for

(are in fact analytic entire in

(18)

which also coincides with the limit of

as

The
\bar{Q}_{\rm A}(\beta)
,

4
Energy flow, thermal channel, stochastic matrix

For
\beta>0
, we define the quantum channel
\Phi_{\beta}:\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}}_{A})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}}_{A})
by

\Phi_{\beta}(X)={\rm Tr}_{B}\big(U(X\otimes\tau_{\beta})U^{*}\big),\qquad X\in\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}}_{A}).

(32)

The CPTP map
\Phi_{\beta}
is a particular example of a reduced thermal quantum channel. Due to (
5
) the system Gibbs state (
2
) is invariant under this channel,

\displaystyle\Phi_{\beta}(\gamma_{\beta})=\gamma_{\beta}.

(33)

We derive general properties of those channels in Section
5
. We associate to
\Phi_{\beta}
a
d\times d

G_{jj^{\prime}}=\big\langle\psi^{\rm A}_{j}\big|\Phi_{\beta}\big(|\psi^{\rm A}_{j^{\prime}}\rangle\langle\psi^{\rm A}_{j^{\prime}}|\big)\big|\psi^{\rm A}_{j}\big\rangle.

(34)

The matrix elements have the following interpretation. Given that the system
A
starts in the energy eigenstate
\psi^{A}_{j^{\prime}}
before interacting with the thermometer
B
, the probability of finding the system (upon a quantum measurement of the observable
H_{A}
) in the eigenstate
\psi^{A}_{j}
after the interaction is just
G_{jj^{\prime}}
. Therefore we have
G_{jj^{\prime}}\geq 0
and
\sum_{j=1}^{d}G_{jj^{\prime}}=1
for all
j^{\prime}
, which means that
G
is a stochastic matrix, which we call the stochastic matrix associated to the quantum channel
\Phi_{\beta}
. The energy flow
Q_{A}
is expressed via the stochastic matrix

as follows.

Proposition 1
.

With the notation of Theorem
1
we have for
\beta>0

Q_{\rm A}={\vec{\varepsilon}}\cdot G{\vec{p}}-{\vec{\varepsilon}}\cdot{\vec{p}},

(35)

where
{\vec{\varepsilon}}=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{d})^{T}
and
{\vec{p}}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})^{T}
are the column vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose entries are the energies of
H_{\rm A}
and populations of
\rho
, and where
G
is the
d\times d
stochastic matrix associated to
\Phi_{\beta}
, (
34
). Moreover, the matrix elements of
G
have the following expression,

G_{jj^{\prime}}=\sum_{s=\max\{j,j^{\prime}\}}^{d}\frac{1}{s}\big(e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_{s}-\varepsilon_{j^{\prime}})}-e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_{s+1}-\varepsilon_{j^{\prime}})}\big)\ \ \ \text{with \ }\ e^{-\beta\varepsilon_{d+1}}=0,

(36)

and the eigenvalues of
G
belong to
(0,1]
,
1
being a simple eigenvalue.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Using the orthonormal basis
\psi^{\rm A}_{j}\otimes\psi^{\rm B}_{km}
of
\mathcal{H}_{\rm A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\rm B}
and writing the corresponding matrix elements of
U
as
U_{jkm,j^{\prime}k^{\prime}m^{\prime}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}

\displaystyle\big(U(\rho\otimes\tau_{\beta})U^{*}H_{\rm A}\big)=\sum_{jkm}\,\varepsilon_{j}\big\langle\psi_{j}^{\rm A}\otimes\psi^{\rm B}_{km}|U(\rho\otimes\tau_{\beta})U^{*}|\psi_{j}^{\rm A}\otimes\psi^{\rm B}_{km}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{jkm}\sum_{j^{\prime}i^{\prime}}\sum_{k^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\varepsilon_{j}\langle\psi_{j^{\prime}}^{\rm A}|\rho|\psi^{\rm A}_{i^{\prime}}\rangle\frac{e^{-\beta\eta_{k^{\prime}}}}{Z_{\rm B}}U_{jkm,j^{\prime}k^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\overline{U_{jkm,i^{\prime}k^{\prime}m^{\prime}}}.

(37)

Due to (
5
) the product of the matrix elements vanishes unless
\varepsilon_{j}+\eta_{k}=\varepsilon_{j^{\prime}}+\eta_{k^{\prime}}=\varepsilon_{i^{\prime}}+\eta_{k^{\prime}}
. The simplicity of the eigenvalues
\varepsilon_{j}
forces
j^{\prime}=i^{\prime}
. The expression thus only depends on the diagonal density matrix elements
p_{j^{\prime}}=\langle\psi_{j^{\prime}}^{\rm A}|\rho|\psi_{j^{\prime}}^{\rm A}\rangle
,

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\big(U(\rho\otimes\tau_{\beta})U^{*}

\displaystyle H_{\rm A}\big)=\sum_{jj^{\prime}}\sum_{kk^{\prime}mm^{\prime}}\varepsilon_{j}p_{j^{\prime}}\frac{e^{-\beta\eta_{k^{\prime}}}}{Z_{\rm B}}|U_{jkm,j^{\prime}k^{\prime}m^{\prime}}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\sum_{jj^{\prime}kk^{\prime}mm^{\prime}}\chi\big(\varepsilon_{j}+\eta_{k}=\varepsilon_{j^{\prime}}+\eta_{k^{\prime}}=E_{\ell}\big)\varepsilon_{j}p_{j^{\prime}}\frac{e^{-\beta\eta_{k^{\prime}}}}{Z_{\rm B}}|U^{(\ell)}_{jkm,j^{\prime}k^{\prime}m^{\prime}}|^{2}.

(38)

Next we calculate the modulus square of the density matrix elements. Consider first the assumption
(G)
. For each
\ell
, the transition probabilities between any eigenstates
\psi^{\rm A}_{j}\otimes\psi^{\rm B}_{km}
and
\psi^{\rm A}_{j^{\prime}}\otimes\psi^{\rm B}_{k^{\prime}m^{\prime}}

\big|U^{(\ell)}_{jkm,j^{\prime}k^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\big|^{2}\equiv\big|\langle\psi^{\rm A}_{j}\otimes\psi^{\rm B}_{km},U^{(\ell)}\psi^{\rm A}_{j^{\prime}}\otimes\psi^{{\rm B}}_{k^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\rangle\big|^{2}=1/g_{\ell},

(39)

where

is the size of the matrix. The value of the constant

is determined by the condition that

is unitary on

. Indeed, the column

must be a vector of norm one, so
\sum_{j^{\prime}k^{\prime}m^{\prime}}|U^{(\ell)}_{jkm,j^{\prime}k^{\prime}m^{\prime}}|^{2}=1
. For constant

, so

.

Consider now the assumption

distributed according to the Haar measure, one has

\mathbb{E}[|U^{(\ell)}_{jkm,j^{\prime}k^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\big|^{2}]=1/g_{\ell}
.

In either way — understanding that we take the average in the random case — we have from (
4

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\big(U(\rho\otimes\tau_{\beta})U^{*}

\displaystyle H_{\rm A}\big)=\sum_{\ell}\frac{1}{g_{\ell}}\sum_{jj^{\prime}kk^{\prime}}\chi\big(\varepsilon_{j}+\eta_{k}=\varepsilon_{j^{\prime}}+\eta_{k^{\prime}}=E_{\ell}\big)\varepsilon_{j}p_{j^{\prime}}\frac{m^{2}_{\rm B}e^{-\beta\eta_{k^{\prime}}}}{Z_{\rm B}}=\vec{\varepsilon}\cdot G\vec{p},

(40)

where the factor
m^{2}_{\rm B}
is due to the sums over
m,m^{\prime}
and where
G
is the
d\times d
matrix with elements

{G}_{jj^{\prime}}=\sum_{\ell}\frac{1}{g_{\ell}}\sum_{kk^{\prime}}\chi\big(\varepsilon_{j}+\eta_{k}=\varepsilon_{j^{\prime}}+\eta_{k^{\prime}}=E_{\ell}\big)\frac{m_{\rm B}^{2}e^{-\beta\eta_{k^{\prime}}}}{Z_{{\rm B}}}.

(41)

Here,

is the characteristic function taking the value

if the condition in the argument is satisfied and

else. This shows (

as given in (

If in the above argument instead of taking the trace over

Our next task is to see that the expressions (

Introducing the integers

with

Now

\sum_{\ell_{s}\leq\ell<\ell_{s+1}}e^{-\beta E_{\ell}}=e^{-\beta\varepsilon_{1}}\sum_{\ell_{s}\leq\ell<\ell_{s+1}}e^{-\beta\ell/N}=e^{-\beta\varepsilon_{1}}\frac{e^{-\beta\ell_{s}/N}-e^{-\beta\ell_{s+1}/N}}{1-e^{-\beta/N}}=\frac{e^{-\beta\varepsilon_{s}}-e^{-\beta\varepsilon_{s+1}}}{1-e^{-\beta/N}}.

We check on (

and where

5
Reduced thermal quantum channels

Consider a general bipartite system with Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm tot}={\mathcal{H}}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm R}
where
\dim{\mathcal{H}}=d<\infty
and
\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm R}\leq\infty
. Let
H
and
H_{\rm R}
be self-adjoint, bounded below Hamiltonians on
{\mathcal{H}}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm R}
, given by

H=\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|,\qquad H_{\rm R}=\sum_{k}\eta_{k}|\chi_{k}\rangle\langle\chi_{k}|,

(49)

with finitely degenerate eigenvalues, and where the eigenvectors are orthonormal bases of the respective spaces. The equilibrium states at inverse temperature
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
are

\gamma_{\beta}=\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{Z_{\beta}},\qquad\tau_{\beta}=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{\rm R}}}{Z_{\beta,{\rm R}}}.

In the infinite dimensional case we assume that
e^{-\beta H_{\rm R}}
has a finite trace, which necessitates
\beta>0
since
H_{\rm R}
is bounded below. Set

H_{\rm tot}=H+H_{\rm R}

where we omit trivial tensor factors in the notation (and don’t fret about the simple domain considerations). Let
V
be a unitary on
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm tot}
satisfying

\displaystyle[H_{\rm tot},V]=0.

(50)

We define the reduced thermal quantum channel
\Phi:\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}})
by

\Phi(X)=\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm R}\big(V(X\otimes\tau_{\beta})V^{*}\big),

(51)

where
\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm R}
is the partial trace over
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm R}
. The map
\Phi
is CPTP (completely positive, trace preserving)
[
7
,
6
,
10
,
19
,
15
]
and has
\gamma_{\beta}
as an invariant vector. Complete positivity implies directly that for all
X\in\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}})
,

\Phi(X^{*})=\big[\Phi(X)\big]^{*}.

(52)

The spectrum of the map
\Phi
is thus invariant under complex conjugation: the eigenvalues of
\Phi
are either real or they appear in complex conjugate pairs.
Moreover, again by virtue of
\Phi
being CPTP, its spectrum
\sigma(\Phi)
satisfies

\displaystyle\sigma(\Phi)\subset\{z\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ |z|\leq 1\}\quad\text{and}\quad 1\in\sigma(\Phi).

(53)

Denote the sets of operators on
{\mathcal{H}}
which are diagonal and off diagonal relative to the eigenbasis
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d}
of
H
, by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle=\big\{X\in\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}})\ \text{s.t. $\langle\psi_{j}|X\psi_{k}\rangle=0$ for $j\neq k$}\big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}

\displaystyle=\big\{X\in\mathcal{B}({\cal H})\ \text{s.t. $\langle\psi_{j}|X\psi_{j}\rangle=0$ for all $j$}\big\}.

(54)

These two sets are orthogonal complements of each other when considering
\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}})
as a Hilbert space with inner product
\langle X|Y\rangle_{\mathcal{B}}=\mathrm{Tr}(X^{*}Y)
, and we have the orthogonal decomposition

\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}})=\mathcal{D}\oplus\mathcal{O}.

(55)

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that all eigenvalues
\varepsilon_{j}
of
H
are simple. Then

(a)

\Phi
leaves both
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{O}
invariant, that is,
\Phi
is reduced by (block-diagonal in) the decomposition (
55
).

(b)

If all nonzero Bohr energies
\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{k}
of
H
are distinct, then
|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{k}|
is an eigenvector of
\Phi
whenever
j\neq k
, with eigenvalue
z_{j,k}
such that
|z_{j,k}|\leq 1
. Moreover,
z_{j,k}=\overline{z_{k,j}}
.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

We start the proof by noticing that the map
\,{\cal U}_{t}:\rm{\cal B}({\cal H})\rightarrow{\cal B}({\cal H})
given by
\,{\cal U}_{t}(X)=e^{-itH}Xe^{itH}
satisfies
\Phi\circ{\cal U}_{t}={\cal U}_{t}\circ\Phi
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. We provide the argument for the convenience of the reader (see also the review article
[
12
]
). For any
X\in{\cal B}({\cal H})
we have

\displaystyle\Phi({\cal U}_{t}(X))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm R}(V(e^{-itH}Xe^{itH}\otimes\tau_{\beta})V^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm R}(V(e^{-itH}Xe^{itH}\otimes e^{-itH_{\rm R}}\tau_{\beta}e^{itH_{\rm R}})V^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm R}(Ve^{-itH_{\rm tot}}(X\otimes\tau_{\beta})e^{itH_{\rm tot}}V^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm R}(e^{-itH_{\rm tot}}V(X\otimes\tau_{\beta})V^{*}e^{itH_{\rm tot}})

\displaystyle=e^{-itH}\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm R}(e^{-itH_{\rm R}}V(X\otimes\tau_{\beta})V^{*}e^{itH_{\rm R}})e^{itH}

\displaystyle=e^{-itH}\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm R}(V(X\otimes\tau_{\beta})V^{*})e^{itH}

\displaystyle={\cal U}_{t}(\Phi(X)).

(56)

\mathcal{U}_{t}
is a unitary group on
\mathcal{B}
and its selfadjoint generator
\mathcal{L}
is the commutator
\mathcal{L}=[H,\cdot\,]
, that is,
\mathcal{U}_{t}=e^{-it\mathcal{L}}
. The eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}
are the Bohr energies
\Delta=\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}
. They have multiplicity
m(\Delta)=|\{(i,j)\ :\ \varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}=\Delta\}|
. For every
(i,j)
the element
X_{ij}:=|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|\in\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}}_{\rm A})
is a normalized eigenvector of
\mathcal{L}
with eigenvalue
\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}
. The set
\{X_{ij}\ :\ \varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}=\Delta\}
is an orthonormal basis for the eigenspace of
\mathcal{L}
associated to the eigenvalue
\Delta
. Therefore the eigenprojection of
\mathcal{L}
associated to
\Delta
is given by

\Pi_{\Delta}=\sum_{i,j\,:\,\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}=\Delta}|X_{ij}\rangle_{\mathcal{B}}\langle X_{ij}|,

where for
X,Y\in\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}})
we define
|X\rangle_{\mathcal{B}}\langle Y|:\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}})
by the action
|X\rangle_{\mathcal{B}}\langle Y|(Z)=X\,\mathrm{Tr}(Y^{*}Z)
.
The spectral decomposition of
\mathcal{L}
is then,

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{\Delta}\Delta\,\Pi_{\Delta},

where the sum runs over all (distinct) Bohr energies. By (
56
),
\Phi
and
\mathcal{L}
commute, which is equivalent to saying that
\Phi
and
\Pi_{\Delta}
commute for all
\Delta
. In other words,
\Phi

Now suppose that the nonzero eigenvalues

This completes the proof of the Lemma

The restriction of the channel

to diagonal operators has the following physical meaning. If the system starts out in the eigenstate
\psi_{j^{\prime}}
before interacting with
{\rm R}
, then the probability of finding the system (upon a quantum measurement of the observable

) in the eigenstate

after the interaction with
{\rm R}
is

G_{jj^{\prime}}=\big[\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm R}(V(\ket{\psi_{j^{\prime}}}\bra{\psi_{j^{\prime}}}\otimes\tau_{\beta})V^{*})\big]_{jj}=\big[\Phi(\ket{\psi_{j^{\prime}}}\bra{\psi_{j^{\prime}}})\big]_{jj},

(57)

where we use the notation
[\cdots]_{kl}=\langle\psi_{k}|\cdots|\psi_{l}\rangle

[

By linearity of (

\displaystyle\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{d}G_{jj^{\prime}}\frac{e^{-\beta\varepsilon_{j^{\prime}}}}{Z_{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}Z_{{\rm R},\beta}}\big[\mathrm{Tr}_{\rm R}(e^{-\beta(H+H_{\rm R})})\big]_{jj}=\frac{e^{-\beta\varepsilon_{j}}}{Z_{\beta}},

where

\Phi^{*}(X)=e^{\beta H}\Psi\big(e^{-\beta H}X\big),\qquad\forall X\in\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}}).

Then, relations (

.

Suppose that all eigenvalues
\varepsilon_{j}
of

for all

for all

(respectively

.
(a) was proven before the lemma. To prove (b) we note that by Lemma

Lemma 3
.

(a)

Suppose that
\Phi
is a homogeneous channel with associated stochastic matrix

.

(b)

The channel
\Phi
is homogeneous if

only allows energy conserving transitions, and with equal probability at any fixed energy, that is if

|V_{jr,j^{\prime}s}|^{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}p_{E}&\text{if $\varepsilon_{j}+\eta_{r}=\varepsilon_{j^{\prime}}+\eta_{s}\equiv E$}\\

.

Proof of Lemma

.

(a) For a homogeneous channel we have

, so (

, or detailed balance condition, see

. This implies that

is selfadjoint as an operator on the Hilbert space

, were the new inner product is

\langle\varphi|\psi\rangle_{D}:=\langle\varphi|D^{-1}\psi\rangle.

\displaystyle\langle\varphi|G\psi\rangle_{D}=\langle\varphi|D^{-1}G\psi\rangle=\langle\varphi|G^{*}D^{-1}\psi\rangle=\langle G\varphi|D^{-1}\psi\rangle=\langle G\varphi|\psi\rangle_{D}.

.

(b) Using the definition of

where

. ∎

We close this Section with another general structural property of
\Phi
, the monotony with respect to the free energy functional. For
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, let
{\cal F}_{\beta}
be the real valued continuous map defined on the compact set of density matrices on
\cal H
by

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{\beta}(\rho)=\beta\mathrm{Tr}(\rho H)-S(\rho),

(72)

where
S(\rho)=-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\ln\rho)
.
The functional
{\cal F}_{\beta}
is the free energy of
\rho
multiplied by
\beta
. The relative entropy of
\rho
with respect to
\sigma
, two density matrices s.t.
\sigma>0
, is given by
S(\rho|\sigma)=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\ln\rho-\rho\ln\sigma)
. By Klein’s inequality,
S(\rho|\sigma)\geq 0
with equality if and only if
\rho=\sigma

[
3
]
.

Lemma 4
.

Under the assumption (
50
) and for
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\Phi
given in (
51
) is positive, we have for any density matrix
\rho
,

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{\beta}(\Phi(\rho))\leq{\cal F}_{\beta}(\rho).

(73)

Proof.

By the data processing inequality, valid for positive trace-preserving
\Phi
,
[
11
,
20
,
16
]
, and using that
\gamma_{\beta}
is invariant under
\Phi
we have,

\displaystyle S(\Phi(\rho)|\Phi(\gamma_{\beta}))=S(\Phi(\rho)|\gamma_{\beta})\leq S(\rho|\gamma_{\beta}).

(74)

Next,
S(\rho|\gamma_{\beta})=-S(\rho)+\beta\mathrm{Tr}(\rho H)+\ln(Z_{\beta})={\cal F}_{\beta}(\rho)+\ln(Z_{\beta})
and the same holds for
\rho
replaced by
\Phi(\rho)
, namely
S(\Phi(\rho)|\gamma_{\beta})={\cal F}_{\beta}(\Phi(\rho))+\ln(Z_{\beta})
. Then (
74
) implies (
73
).
∎

Lemma
4
provides a link between the energy the entropy variations under the action
\Phi
, known as Landauer’s bound:

\displaystyle\beta\big[\mathrm{Tr}(\Phi(\rho)H)-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho H)\big]\leq S(\Phi(\rho))-S(\rho).

(75)

Coming back to our thermometer model of Section
2
we recognize the left side of the inequality (
75
) as the flow
Q_{\rm A}(\beta)
(see (
6
)) times
\beta
, that is we have,

\beta Q_{\rm A}(\beta)\leq S(\Phi_{\beta}(\rho))-S(\rho).

(76)

Suppose
\rho
is such that
Q_{\rm A}(0)>0
. Then
\beta_{\rm c}>0
. We know that
\Phi_{\beta}
is CPTP for
\beta>0
, so (
76

, and it shows that (recall that

is monotone decreasing)

\displaystyle S(\Phi_{\beta}(\rho))\geq S(\rho)\ \ \text{for all}\ \ 0<\beta\leq\beta_{\rm c}.

(77)

We conclude that the von Neumann entropy can only increase under the application of the thermal channel
\Phi_{\beta}
, in the given range of parameters.

For finite-dimensional

5.1
Application: Approach of equilibrium

The energy flow of the thermometer model of Section
2
is given by (
6

, defined in (

, from which we see explicitly that

for all

(b) shows that

is a homogeneous channel, because

only allows for energy conserving transitions with equal probability at fixed energy — this is the assumption (G). As a consequence of Lemmas
1
,
2
and Corollary
2
we obtain the following result.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\Phi_{\beta}
, (
32
), be the reduced thermal quantum channel of the thermometer model of Section
2
. Then

(a)

\Phi_{\beta}
leaves the space of diagonal matrices (w.r.t.

) invariant, and it leaves the space of off-diagonal matrices invariant:
\Phi_{\beta}=\Phi_{\beta}|_{\mathcal{D}}\oplus\Phi_{\beta}|_{\mathcal{O}}
.

(b)

If all nonzero Bohr energies
\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{k}
of

are distinct, then
\Phi_{\beta}|_{\mathcal{D}}
has a simple eigenvalue
1
with associated spectral projection
|v_{\beta}\rangle\langle v_{0}|
(with
\mathcal{D}
identified with
\mathbb{C}^{d}
— see also (
59

, as well as all eigenvalues of

, have modulus strictly less than

.

We may let the system in an initial state
\rho
interact with the thermometer, then decouple it, resulting in the state
\Phi_{\beta}(\rho)
, and letting it subsequently interact with a fresh thermometer (at the same temperature as the previous one). Repeating this process
k
times gives a discrete dynamics on the system:

\rho\mapsto\rho^{(k)}\equiv\Phi_{\beta}^{k}(\rho),\qquad k=1,2,\ldots

(79)

(apply

\Phi_{\beta}
to

). If the nonzero Bohr energies are distinct, then according to Theorem

(b), we have for any density matrix

where the speed of convergence is exponentially fast in

. For

is not the ground nor the most excited state of

,

(81)

where

is the vector of the populations of
\rho^{(k)}
, with
p^{(k)}_{j}=\langle\psi^{\rm A}_{j}|\rho^{(k)}\psi^{\rm A}_{j}\rangle
. Of course,
G
and the
\vec{p}^{\,(k)}
on the right side of (

and the solution of the equation gives

. By (

), we have

as

.

Consider now the flow
Q_{\rm A}
as a function of two variables,
\beta>0
and

(the matrix elements of an operator acting on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
). An explicit formula is given in (

), for example. Suppose we have

and some density matrix

(viewed as an element in

). Since

), the implicit function theorem tells us that there is a unique map

, defined and differentiable in a neighbourhood of

, with

, such that

. Therefore by uniqueness,
\beta(\rho^{(k)})=\beta_{\rm c}^{(k)}

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\beta_{\rm c}^{(k)}=\beta.
