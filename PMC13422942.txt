Identifying pharmacy medicine-dispensing patterns associated with high risk of asthma and COPD exacerbation: an Australian retrospective cohort study
Authors: Rachel House, Biljana Cvetkovski, Katherine Borg, Mike da Gama, Nicholas Biggs, Sergio Pintaldi, Marius Mather, Sinthia Bosnic-Anticevich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo determine whether patterns of asthma and/or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) medicine dispensed in pharmacy dispense records (PDRs) associated with high risk of asthma and COPD exacerbation can be identified in long-term PDRs.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingNostraData between 2011 and 2019.ParticipantsDeidentified PDRs containing respiratory medicines, with a minimum of three dispensing events across at least 5 years.Main outcome measuresThe primary outcome measures were to describe the frequency, duration and dosage of medicines such as short-acting beta-agonist (SABA), oral corticosteroids (OCS) and inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), dispensed within PDRs containing respiratory medicines.ResultsOf 26 055 926 PDRs explored, 14.7% were eligible for inclusion (n=3 837 950) and represented people with a median age of 48 years who visited an average of five different pharmacies over a range of 5–8 years. The most common medicine dispensed was SABA (72.7%), and 35.2% were dispensed ≥3 in a year. Almost half the PDRs dispensed with OCS (49.2%) were dispensed ≥1 g cumulatively. Asthma and asthma/COPD PDRs were most commonly dispensed Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) Step 4 medications (36%), and only 7.5% of PDRs demonstrated ≥60% adherence to preventer/controller inhalers.ConclusionsPatterns of medication dispensing associated with high risk of asthma and COPD exacerbation, such as the overuse of SABA and OCS and inadequate adherence to preventer/controller inhalers, can be identified in long-term PDRs. This real-time information in PDRs should be further researched to support identification of people at risk of exacerbation in primary care, to optimise management in primary care and deliver targeted intervention.

Introduction

Asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are respiratory conditions that affect 11% and 2.5% of Australians, respectively.1 When these conditions are poorly controlled, they present a substantial individual and socioeconomic burden and respiratory-related mortality.2–5 These events are compounded in circumstances that are preventable with good disease control and optimal medical management of the respiratory condition.3 6 7

It has been identified that people with respiratory conditions who are at high risk of having an exacerbation have a history of poor adherence to preventer/controller inhalers.3 8–11 In an asthma population presenting to community pharmacy, up to 72% of people with poorly controlled asthma were not adherent to or not using an inhaled corticosteroid (ICS).12 In a study exploring adherence in people with COPD, it was found that moderately and highly nonadherent cohorts had higher exacerbation rates than the adherent patients, and adherent patients had significantly lower healthcare costs than all the nonadherent groups.13 Adherence to respiratory medicines may be suboptimal due to factors including cost or patient beliefs about preventer medicine; therefore, identification of poor adherence in a clinical setting is essential to provide patient support, optimise disease control and minimise exacerbations.14 15

Over-use of short-acting beta-agonist (SABA) is also an indicator of poor disease control and increased risk of exacerbation in people with asthma.16 17 SABA over-use, defined as the prescription or collection of ≥3 SABA per year,16 has been observed in people with asthma by the SABA use IN Asthma (SABINA) studies around the world.16 18–23 Analysis of electronic medical records (EMRs) in Australia also demonstrated that >50% of people with asthma were prescribed ≥3 SABA per year but only 28% self-reported it.24 In Australia, SABA is available over the counter (OTC) without a prescription, and more than 70% of people used SABA more than two times per week.25 These significant rates of SABA over-use and indication of frequent exacerbation paint a grim picture for the status of respiratory care and the impending burden of uncontrolled respiratory disease.

Oral corticosteroids (OCS), while being a mainstay treatment for acute asthma/COPD exacerbations, providing rapid symptom relief and inflammation reduction,26–31 can result in adverse effects when overused. Hancock et al32 also explored EMRs in Australia retrospectively and demonstrated that people who are repeatedly exposed to OCS had an increased risk of osteoporosis (OR 1.95 (95% CI 1.43 to 2.66)) and sleep apnoea (OR 1.78 (95% CI 1.30 to 2.46)), while Hew et al33 explored the PBS data retrospectively and showed that people with asthma using OCS were also obtaining medicines to treat comorbidities developed by the use of OCS, for example, diabetes and osteoporosis. Further to this, the EMR and PBS data also showed that OCS were prescribed at a dose that exceeds the lifetime cumulative safe dose (ie, 1000 mg prednisolone‐equivalent or more) in 32%32 and 25%33 of the cohort, respectively. Whereas this prescription pattern was shown in 26% of people with COPD within just 12 months.30 Many people receive OCS without clear action plans or adequate education on their use, which may contribute to the inappropriate and excessive prescription of OCS.34

Pharmacy dispense records (PDRs) contain information about medicines that patients have had dispensed by their pharmacist at a pharmacy with a doctor’s prescription. Serhal et al have demonstrated that there is a mismatch between the data in EMRs and PDRs,35 as people do not always get their doctor-prescribed medicines dispensed, for several reasons including financial and beliefs about the medication.36 37 Thus far, no research has explored PDRs with respiratory medicines over the long term, which potentially reflects the patterns and adherence of medicine use most accurately.35 38

PDRs have not been used previously for identifying patients at high risk of asthma and COPD exacerbations. Early detection enables timely healthcare interventions to reduce risk and optimise disease management.39 Current pharmacy-based interventions rely on patient presentation, limiting their reach.40 A data-driven approach using real-world PDRs offers a scalable alternative for proactive identification.41 Unlike traditional screening methods that require significant time and training, large-scale datasets can be analysed digitally and, in the future, through artificial intelligence.41 Understanding the potential of these PDRs is essential for advancing predictive and preventative respiratory care.

This study aims to determine whether patterns of asthma and COPD medicine dispensed in PDRs associated with high risk of asthma and COPD exacerbation can be identified in long-term PDRs in Australia.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study took the form of a retrospective, observational, secondary data analysis study, using electronic PDRs dating from 2011 to 2019 and is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist (online supplemental appendix A) for cohort studies. This study commenced in 2021 and did not include PDRs after 2019 to prevent analysing data potentially skewed by COVID.

Data source and data extraction

PDRs from the NostraData (Kew, Victoria, Australia) database were used in this study. NostraData is an analytics company (https://www.nostradata.com.au/public/) that supplies the infrastructure that stores all pharmacy data relating to dispense and medicines purchases; that is, NostraData provides the backend storage for all data collected through pharmacy software. For over a decade, NostraData has captured deidentified patients’ information. It comprises dispensing data from more than 4500 pharmacies across Australia, representing 70%–80% of total prescription volume of the Australian Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme system across all states in Australia, building and pilot-testing algorithms, integrating with pharmacy systems and supporting the use of pharmacy data to feed back into pharmacy. NostraData provided a demographically representative dataset of anonymous PDRs on medication or asthma and COPD on a longitudinal basis, which made it possible to track medications dispensed at different pharmacies and/or on different occasions.42 While each PDR represents data from an individual, the dataset captures only dispensing events recorded by participating pharmacies and contains no patient identifiers. It may not necessarily reflect an individual’s complete medication history. Therefore, for the purposes of this study, the dataset was depersonified and referred to as ‘PDRs’ instead of ‘individuals’ or ‘patients’.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

A PDR was included in this study if it contained at least one respiratory medicine indicated for asthma and/or COPD (online supplemental appendix B) (either an inhaled medication, a tablet or a biologic) across at least 5 years of the dispensing medication record (the data does not have to be continuous) with a minimum of 3 dispensing events. PDRs were excluded if they (1) contained only OCS with no respiratory medications dispensed, and/or (2) had inconsistent gender information (suggestive of an administrative error or shared record).

Data management

Respiratory condition

The deidentified data in the PDR does not contain information relating to diagnosis; a decision tree (figure 1) was developed to categorise the PDRs into ‘asthma’, ‘COPD’, ‘asthma/COPD’ medication profiles, and SABA±OCS and unknown PDRs. This decision tree was based on national guidelines for asthma (Global Initiative for Asthma; GINA) and COPD (COPD-X).43 44 The decision tree operates as a sequential elimination, so all included PDRs contain an ICS or mast cell stabiliser (MCS), and the combinations are classified by the presence of:

Long-acting muscarinic agonist (LAMA): ICS+LAMA (without long-acting beta2-agonist (LABA)) is categorised as COPD.

LAMA and LABA: ICS+LAMA+ ABA is categorised as asthma/COPD.

LAMA and LABA: ICS+LAMA+ ABA is categorised as asthma/COPD.

All other ICS-containing combinations (including ICS+LABA) are categorised as asthma.

COPD, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; ICS, inhaled corticosteroids; LABA, long-acting beta2-agonist; LAMA, long-acting muscarinic agonist; MCS, mast cell stabiliser; OCS, oral corticosteroids; SABA, short-acting beta-agonist; SAMA, short-acting muscarinic agonist; Theo, theophylline.

These categorisations took into consideration the changes in the GINA guidelines between 2011 and 2019, especially with the addition of LAMA to ICS/LABA for people in Steps 4 and 5 from the 2015 guidelines onwards.45 The PDR was determined as an ‘unknown’ medication profile if the medication pattern was not aligned with the national guidelines.

Medication dispensed

The number and proportion of each respiratory medicine dispensed within each PDR were calculated.

SABA over-use

For asthma and asthma/COPD PDRs with a SABA dispensed, the number of SABA dispensed in a calendar year was also calculated. For the PDRs dispensed, ≥3 SABA in a calendar year, they were labelled ‘SABA over-use’. This definition was based on the evidence that use of three or more SABA canisters per calendar year is associated with being at high risk of asthma exacerbation.16

High OCS dose

For asthma, asthma/COPD, COPD and SABA±COPD PDRs in which OCS was dispensed, the cumulative dose of OCS dispensed was also calculated and categorised into three categories: >0 g to <0.5 g, 0.5 g to <1 g and ≥1 g. For any PDR in which a cumulative dose of ≥1 g was dispensed, OCS use was defined as ‘high OCS dose’.

Asthma status

For the PDRs categorised as ‘asthma’ and ‘asthma/COPD’, in accordance with the GINA guidelines, the asthma status was determined based on the dose of the ICS dispensed.43 46 To evaluate the change in the asthma status, two time points of the PDRs were mapped, the first and last medication dispensed. The asthma status was determined using online supplemental appendix C.

Adherence to preventer/controller inhaler

The adherence to preventer/controller inhaler was determined using the number of puffs and dose for each inhaler prescribed per day and the date of medication dispensed.44 47 The adherence was calculated using the medication possession ratio (MPR), which refers to the number of days with medication supply in a time period.48 The adherence analysis assumes ideal use in accordance with the respiratory guidelines.

MPR is reported as a dichotomised measure, as based on published literature, the clinical cut-off for adherence to preventer/controller inhaler is ≥60%. PDRs with an MPR above the threshold of 60% are considered to have moderate-good adherence.49–51

Data analysis

The data extracted were analysed using descriptive and associative analysis using Python V.3.11.0 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) and Microsoft Excel. The descriptive analysis for categorical data was described in frequencies and percentages. Pearson’s χ2 test with a two-sided significance threshold of α=0.05 was used to explore the statistical associations between asthma and asthma/COPD PDRs with excessive SABA dispensed. Continuous data were described using the sample mean (± sample SD) and median.

Patient and public involvement

This study was carried out with members of NostraData and the University of Sydney. Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct or dissemination plan of this study. The deidentified nature of the NostraData dataset precluded direct patient involvement; future implementation studies arising from this may incorporate patient involvement in the study design.

Results

SABA over-use

More than half of the asthma and asthma/COPD PDRs with SABA dispensed (51.0%, n=7 59 171) had ≥3 SABA dispensed in a year, and there was a statistically significant association between the PDRs (asthma, asthma/COPD) and SABA dispensed (χ²=1 53 549, df=1, p<0.001). Comparing the PDRs, the proportion with ≥3 SABA dispensed in the asthma/COPD PDRs (62.4%, n=1 87 642) was higher than that in the asthma PDRs (26.6%, n=5 71 529).

Discussion

This is the first study to determine the dispensing patterns of asthma/COPD medicine in PDRs and identify patterns associated with a high risk of exacerbation in patients with asthma/COPD. This included the identification of SABA overuse, high OCS doses dispensed cumulatively and poor adherence to preventer/controlled inhalers. This creates an opportunity for large-scale screening of real-world data for targeted early intervention and risk minimisation strategies by health care professionals (HCPs).

Leveraging PDRs as a screening tool offers an opportunity to enable proactive care, allowing pharmacists to identify patients at high risk before clinical presentation and implement timely interventions to reduce exacerbation risk and optimise disease management. Identification of individuals with poorly controlled respiratory disease has traditionally relied on face-to-face clinical encounters, often occurring during exacerbations and necessitating reactive rather than proactive care.25 52 53 Although pharmacy-based screening tools are generally well accepted by pharmacists and patients, their implementation is hindered by time constraints, resource limitations and variable patient engagement.54 Using dispensing data could overcome these barriers by reducing the burden on both HCPs and patients.

Given the scale of dispensing data, such an approach could facilitate population-level screening with substantially reduced time and resource demands compared with traditional methods. Furthermore, the availability of large, longitudinal datasets raises the question of whether predictive modelling can be developed to anticipate exacerbations.55 Recent work demonstrates this potential: a machine-learning model incorporating data from digital inhalers successfully predicted impending exacerbations, highlighting the feasibility of integrating predictive analytics into preventative respiratory care.56

SABA continues to be the most prominent medicine used in respiratory disease, with 72.7% of PDRs in this study containing SABA. This study identified that 35.2% of the PDRs had ≥3 SABA dispensed, which is similar to the 38% prescribed ≥3 SABA in a year identified in Bateman et al (SABINA).20 However, the finding in this study is more concerning in an Australian setting as it only represents a fraction of the total SABA dispensed, because the SABA dispensed recorded in the PDRs are via a prescription and patients are also able to access SABA OTC without a prescription.25 Azzi et al surveyed people with asthma purchasing SABA without a prescription and found that 70% use their SABA more than two times per week in 4 weeks and were more likely at risk of using OCS and less likely to be using a preventer daily.25 With widespread SABA overuse identified at the pharmacy, pharmacists are at the forefront of identifying and referring patients at high risk of asthma/COPD exacerbation to their doctors. Pharmacy-based respiratory interventions have been successfully trialled in the literature, but failure to implement in practice meant that SABA overuse and subsequent exacerbations, burden on individuals and healthcare resources will continue to be problematic.40 57–61

This study also identified that OCS were dispensed cumulatively at rates associated with suboptimal management of respiratory disease, high risk of exacerbation and linked to adverse effects. More than a third of PDRs contained OCS, with almost half dispensed ≥1 g of OCS cumulatively over 5–8 years, and this finding is much higher than the 25% cohort exposed to ≥1 g of OCS over a 5-year period reported in Hew et al.62 High OCS dose exposure could indicate OCS overuse, and this is of high concern in the respiratory community both to people with respiratory conditions and their HCP.63 To combat this issue, OCS stewardship initiatives are recommended, focusing on raising awareness of OCS risks and appropriate indications.29 64 65 Initiatives include: promoting alternative therapies (eg, biologics, macrolides) and optimising inhaler technique, adherence, and asthma education; implementing systematic assessment of OCS exposure and toxicity, and establishing thresholds for specialist referral and encouraging multidisciplinary review and individualised action plans.29 64 65 While pharmacists are commonly recognised for their role in optimising inhaler technique and adherence, there is room to increase their presence among the other initiatives of OCS stewardship given they have a front-row seat to evidence of OCS overuse as shown in this study. Monitoring OCS use is not only useful in terms of avoiding adverse effects but also as a red flag, as using OCS to manage COPD is a significant indicator of being at high risk of future exacerbation.47 66 67 Pharmacists can make significant improvements to respiratory clinical outcomes in optimising medicine use, and this study demonstrated they have the means to monitor, identify and refer patients at high risk of future exacerbation.12 35 40 58

Respiratory burden in this population is high, with evidence demonstrating almost three-quarters of the asthma and asthma/COPD PDRs in this study were dispensed GINA Step 3–5 medications, suggesting patients were experiencing asthma symptoms at a moderate-severe level. This finding was higher than the 49% cohort with moderate-severe asthma in general practice, as shown in the EMR data in Price et al.68 While this study does not have diagnostic information in the PDR and therefore is not immediately comparable to Price et al, the high dispensing rates of GINA Step 3–5 medications are still of concern. This population presenting to their doctors with respiratory symptoms, requiring these medicines, may need to be further evaluated by their prescribers for diagnostic and medication management review. With evidence demonstrating that EMRs do not necessarily contain diagnostic information or rates that are not consistent with national records,69 it is evident that patients are exposed to both under- and over-treatment.32

This study’s finding further highlights the need for people with respiratory conditions to be reviewed to ensure optimal management and minimal burden to individuals and healthcare resources. With patients visiting their pharmacy to receive their medications, it is an opportunity for them to speak to a qualified HCP on multiple occasions throughout the year. This interaction should be maximised to ensure people with respiratory conditions do not continue to be exposed to suboptimal medicine use and are identified for review and referral to their doctor.

The next step is to translate these insights into actionable tools for clinical practice. Machine-learning approaches, already validated in digital inhaler studies, could be adapted to PDR datasets to create automated risk stratification systems. These systems could be embedded into pharmacy software or shared electronic health records, enabling pharmacists and prescribers to receive real-time alerts for patients exhibiting high-risk patterns. Additionally, implementation studies are needed to evaluate the feasibility, cost-effectiveness and clinical impact of PDR-driven interventions compared with traditional screening methods. Ultimately, leveraging PDRs for predictive analytics could shift respiratory care from reactive management to proactive prevention, reducing exacerbation rates and improving long-term outcomes for people with asthma and COPD.

Strengths and limitations

It is important to be cautious when interpreting the data in PDRs and be considerate of the limitations in this study. First, the limitations of the NostraData dataset include that it does not represent data from all Australian pharmacies and there is potential for data entry errors. While most dispensing information is electronically linked to a prescription, there are instances that require human data entry. This is apparent in gender and age information, which in some cases were not consistent (explained by manual data entry error), so the reported age distribution might not be representative, and the record of preventer/controller inhalers dispensed that appeared as negative and fractional numbers in PDRs (and therefore were excluded to ensure the accuracy of the data analysed). Second, categorisation of respiratory condition in this study was based on medication dispensed in the PDR and not diagnostic information, and therefore care must be taken in extrapolating that information. Third, adherence measure to medication is only indicative in accordance with the medication dispensed in the PDRs at participating pharmacies that partner with Nostradata, and this may not necessarily represent the complete dispensing history, and/or the patients taking them. Fourth, the changes in the GINA guidelines from 2011 to 2019 are also a limitation when considering the asthma status of the patients’ GINA steps. Finally, the report of SABA use in this study may not provide the complete picture of the individuals’ SABA use, which may potentially be higher, as it can be purchased OTC without being recorded on their PDR. Given these limitations were not anticipated until after data analysis, and therefore further research will be required to carry out sensitivity analyses to strengthen the robustness of the findings.

Furthermore, we must exercise caution in interpreting the value of these results in current practice. At this point in time, not all HCPs have access to PDRs reflective of dispensing patterns across multiple pharmacies. People who used a shared electronic health record and permit their HCP to access it may be privy to this information; however, most HCPs can only have access to records that have been recorded in their practice, therefore limiting the information available to them. Nevertheless, future practice should consider existing and evolving datasets for the feasibility of linking PDRs and developing a monitoring tool for use in clinical practice.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that long-term PDRs can be used to identify high-risk medicine-use behaviours—such as SABA overuse, exposure to high OCS dose and poor adherence to preventer/controller inhalers—at scale. Medication patterns in PDRs have not been used extensively in clinical practice thus far, and awareness of patterns of dispensing associated with high risk of exacerbation can potentially alert pharmacists to investigate further and deliver targeted interventions where risk is identified. Therefore, future research should focus on developing predictive models that integrate this real-time information on dispensing patterns with other real-world data sources, such as EMRs and patient-reported outcomes, to anticipate risk of exacerbations before they occur and thereby reduce the frequency and severity of exacerbation in the future through improved management in primary care.
