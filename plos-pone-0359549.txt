Perceived factors associated with self-reported AI-assisted academic cheating among undergraduate students: Evidence from ethical ambiguity, technological affordances, institutional gaps, academic pressure, and peer comparison

Hanh Van Nguyen, Mai Thi-Thuy Duong

ABSTRACT
The rapid development of generative artificial intelligence (AI) has made academic cheating in higher education increasingly complex and difficult to regulate. Using a cross-sectional self-report survey of 863 undergraduate students at a university of science and technology in Vietnam, this study examined the latent structure of students’ perceptions of factors associated with AI-assisted academic cheating and the associations of the resulting constructs with self-reported cheating behaviors. Data were analyzed using exploratory factor analysis, confirmatory factor analysis, and covariance-based structural equation modeling. The results supported a three-factor structure consisting of AI-Assisted Academic Cheating Behaviors (AICB), Ethical Ambiguity, Technological Affordances, and Institutional Gaps (ETIG), and Academic Pressure and Peer Comparison (APPC). In the structural model, ETIG was positively associated with AICB (β = 0.228, p < 0.001), whereas APPC showed a small negative association (β = −0.148, p = 0.022). Gender was also positively associated with AICB (β = 0.142, p < 0.001), with male students tending to report higher AICB scores than female students. Student seniority was not significantly associated with AICB (β = 0.018, p = 0.622). Field of study was positively associated with AICB (β = 0.104, p = 0.008), with non-STEM students tending to report higher AICB scores than STEM students. The model accounted for approximately 5% of the variance in AICB, indicating modest explanatory power. Overall, the findings suggest that unclear boundaries around acceptable AI use, ease of access to AI tools and difficulty detecting AI-generated work, gaps in institutional guidance, and academic pressure and peer comparison may warrant further examination in relation to AI-assisted academic cheating.

1. Introduction Academic cheating has long been a persistent concern in higher education [ 1 ], because it undermines the core values of academic work, including honesty, fairness, responsibility, and respect for intellectual effort [ 2 ]. Behaviors such as plagiarism, unauthorized use of materials during examinations, and fabrication of research data not only damage educational quality but also weaken trust in the authenticity of learning outcomes and the value of academic degrees [ 3 , 4 ]. Studies have shown that academic cheating is prevalent across many higher education systems and is often associated with institutional contexts, peer norms, and cultures of integrity [ 5 , 6 ]. Furthermore, the ways students understand and practice academic integrity are strongly shaped by academic culture, pedagogical practices, and broader social values, meaning that the definition of cheating is not always consistent across contexts [ 7 ]. However, the emergence of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) has not alleviated this longstanding problem. Instead, GenAI has reshaped academic cheating in ways that are more complex and difficult to regulate in higher education [ 8 ]. The concept of AI-assisted academic cheating refers to the unethical use of AI technologies to produce academic work without genuine student effort [ 9 ], undermining fairness and devaluing educational credentials [ 10 , 11 ]. AI systems based on large language models such as ChatGPT are capable of generating coherent academic text, summarizing documents, supporting argumentation, and addressing a wide range of learning tasks [ 12 – 16 ]. These tools offer substantial opportunities for personalized learning and improved student learning efficiency [ 15 , 17 ]. AI tools such as ChatGPT have also demonstrated impressive performance by passing standardized tests and professional examinations in various fields such as law [ 18 ] or medicine [ 19 , 20 ]. At the same time, AI's powerful content generation capacity also increases the risk of misuse in academic assessment contexts [ 4 ]. When students can use AI to generate complete academic products within a very short time, the boundary between using AI as a legitimate learning support tool and using it to replace personal learning effort becomes increasingly blurred [ 3 , 4 ]. Moreover, AI-generated content is often difficult to detect through conventional plagiarism detection tools, making the control of academic cheating more complicated in contemporary higher education [ 21 ]. Unlike traditional forms of academic cheating, which are often easier to identify, AI-assisted cheating exists along a broad spectrum and frequently falls within gray areas between integrity and fraud [ 22 ]. Students may use AI to complete entire assignments, generate fake data or research results, paraphrase content to avoid plagiarism detection, or use AI in situations where instructors have explicitly prohibited it [ 22 ]. Therefore, AI-assisted cheating should not be understood as a single behavior, but as a spectrum of academic practices that may include full delegation of academic tasks to AI, submission of AI-generated work without intellectual contribution, lack of transparency in acknowledging AI assistance, and use of AI in violation of ethical or instructional guidelines [ 22 ]. This classification suggests that AI-related academic cheating exists across a wide spectrum, blurring the lines between authentic learning and outsourced academic work [ 8 ]. Beyond identifying forms of cheating behavior, previous research has also examined academic, ethical, technological, and institutional conditions associated with AI-assisted academic cheating. Academic pressure has frequently been associated with dishonest academic practices [ 23 ]. Heavy workloads, pressure to achieve high academic performance, and intense competition may be relevant to students’ inappropriate use of AI in academic work [ 23 ]. Ethical ambiguity may also be relevant when students are uncertain about the boundary between legitimate AI assistance and academic misconduct [ 4 ]. Technological characteristics such as ease of use and difficulty of detection may also be relevant to students’ perceptions of opportunities for inappropriate AI use [ 15 , 21 ]. Institutional policies, instructor guidance, and peer norms have also been identified as relevant contextual conditions [ 4 , 23 ]. When educational institutions do not provide clear policies on AI use in learning, or when instructors fail to offer specific guidance on how to use AI responsibly [ 4 ], students may interpret and apply academic norms in different ways [ 24 ]. A recent qualitative study identified four categories of factors frequently mentioned by students regarding AI-assisted academic dishonesty: academic pressure, ethical ambiguity, strong AI capabilities, and gaps in institutional academic policy [ 22 ]. Taken together, this literature suggests that AI-assisted academic cheating should be examined in relation to the broader academic, ethical, technological, and institutional context [ 22 ]. While a growing body of research has emerged on AI-assisted academic cheating, the majority of empirical studies focus on describing the prevalence of this behavior [ 8 ], measuring cheating intentions [ 25 ], or examining students’ perceptions of whether AI use constitutes academic cheating [ 24 ]. Studies have shown that students may conceal cheating behavior when asked directly [ 23 , 26 ], while others have explained cheating intention using behavioral models such as the Theory of Planned Behavior [ 25 ]. However, these approaches focus primarily on whether students cheat, with less attention to students’ perceptions of academic pressure, ethical ambiguity, AI capabilities, and institutional policy gaps in relation to inappropriate AI use. This leads to the first research question: Research question 1 (RQ1): How are the dimensions identified from students’ perceptions of academic pressure, ethical ambiguity, AI capabilities, and institutional policy gaps associated with their self-reported AI-assisted academic cheating behaviors? This study also examines the roles of gender and student seniority as relevant variables that may further clarify differences in academic cheating tendencies. In this regard, a study conducted in Vietnam found differences by gender and student seniority in tendencies toward AI-assisted academic cheating [ 23 ]. In related work, one study found that female students reported witnessing more cheating behaviors at school than male students, whereas male students may hold more tolerant views toward academically dishonest behavior than female students [ 27 ]. Another report indicated that male students were more likely than female students to engage in academic cheating in both face-to-face and online educational settings [ 28 ]. However, one study found no significant difference between males and females in academic cheating behavior, suggesting that the influence of gender may depend on the research context and the type of cheating behavior considered [ 29 ]. Similarly, student seniority has also been considered a factor that may influence academic cheating behavior. Female students in higher years tended to cheat more, whereas male students showed similar cheating rates across seniority levels [ 23 ]. Research question 2 (RQ2): How are gender and student seniority associated with students’ self-reported AI-assisted academic cheating behaviors? 2. Methods 2.1. Research design This study employed a cross-sectional self-report survey design to examine the latent structure of items assessing students’ perceptions of factors associated with AI-assisted academic cheating and the associations of the resulting constructs with self-reported AI-assisted academic cheating behaviors (AICB). The analysis proceeded in two stages. First, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was used to identify the underlying structure of the perception items without imposing predetermined dimensions. This exploratory approach was appropriate because the items were developed from prior qualitative findings rather than from an established measurement model. Second, covariance-based structural equation modeling (CB-SEM) was used to evaluate the measurement model of the constructs identified through EFA and to estimate their structural associations with AICB. Gender, student seniority, and field of study were also included as observed background variables in the structural model. The questionnaire was administered anonymously online to reduce social pressure and encourage more honest responses to sensitive questions about academic cheating [ 26 ]. Before participation, students were informed about the purpose of the study, the anonymity of their responses, their voluntary participation, and their right to withdraw. Completion of the questionnaire was considered informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines and was approved by the Scientific Board of the Faculty of Education, Hanoi University of Science and Technology, Vietnam (FED-SRB-2025-010, date: October 1, 2025). 2.2. Participants The participants were undergraduate students enrolled at a university of science and technology in Vietnam. Data were collected through an anonymous online survey distributed to students from a range of academic programs within the university. Convenience sampling was used because it was feasible to access student groups from different disciplines. The survey was conducted between October 25 and November 22, 2025. A total of 881 responses were received. After incomplete or invalid questionnaires had been removed, 863 responses were retained for subsequent statistical analyses. Survey instrument Drawing on findings from a previous qualitative study of AI-assisted academic cheating in higher education [ 22 ], the survey was designed to assess students’ self-reported cheating behaviors, their perceptions of factors associated with such behaviors, and selected background characteristics. The AICB section initially comprised eleven items assessing students’ self-reported engagement in specific forms of inappropriate AI use in academic work. One conceptually overlapping item was removed before the final analyses, leaving ten items for EFA, CFA, and structural modeling. Participants indicated how often they had engaged in each behavior on a five-point frequency scale ranging from 1 (Never) to 5 (Very often). A further eleven items assessed students’ perceptions of factors potentially associated with AI-assisted academic cheating. These items were not assigned a priori to predefined subscales; instead, their empirical structure was examined using EFA. Responses were recorded on a five-point agreement scale ranging from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 5 (Strongly agree). The questionnaire also collected information on gender, student seniority, and field of study. Programs in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics were classified as STEM, whereas programs outside these disciplinary areas were classified as non-STEM. Gender was coded as 0 = female and 1 = male. Student seniority was coded as 1 = first year, 2 = second year, 3 = third year, and 4 = fourth year or above. Field of study was coded as 0 = STEM and 1 = non-STEM. 2.4. Data analysis The data were analyzed using SPSS version 26 and AMOS version 24. The significance level was set at 5%. Data analysis followed a sequential procedure including descriptive statistics, exploratory factor analysis, confirmatory factor analysis, and structural model testing. First, descriptive statistics were calculated for item responses. The normality of observed variables was examined using skewness and kurtosis. Skewness values within ±2 and kurtosis values within ±4 were considered acceptable [ 34 ]. Second, EFA was conducted on the 21 items retained for analysis to examine their underlying factor structure. The suitability of the data for EFA was evaluated using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. A KMO value of 0.70 or higher was considered acceptable, and a significant Bartlett’s test at p < 0.05 indicated that the correlation matrix was appropriate for factor analysis [ 35 ]. Factors were extracted using principal axis factoring because the purpose was to identify latent constructs. Because the factors were expected to be conceptually related, oblique rotation was used. Items were retained when they had a primary factor loading of at least 0.50, with 0.70 or higher considered preferable [ 35 ]. Items were also examined for cross-loadings, and a difference of at least 0.20 between the primary loading and any cross-loading was considered acceptable [ 36 ]. Third, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to validate the measurement model derived from EFA. Standardized factor loadings of 0.70 or higher were considered preferable, loadings from 0.60 to 0.69 were considered acceptable, and loadings of 0.50 to 0.59 were considered conditionally acceptable when theoretically justified [ 36 ]. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability (CR). Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.70 or higher were considered preferable, while values between 0.60 and 0.70 were considered acceptable [ 36 , 37 ]. CR values of 0.70 or higher indicated adequate reliability, whereas values above 0.95 could suggest item redundancy [ 35 ]. Convergent validity was evaluated using average variance extracted (AVE), with values of 0.50 or higher indicating adequate convergence [ 35 ]. When the AVE was slightly below 0.50 (0.40 or higher), the construct was interpreted cautiously and retained only when CR was adequate and the construct was theoretically coherent, following the logic that adequate composite reliability may support convergent validity when AVE is marginal [ 38 ]. Discriminant validity was assessed using the Fornell–Larcker criterion and the heterotrait–monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT). For the Fornell–Larcker criterion, discriminant validity was supported when the square root of AVE for each construct was greater than its correlations with other constructs [ 36 ]. For HTMT, values below 0.85 indicated strict discriminant validity, while values below 0.90 were considered acceptable for conceptually related constructs [ 35 ]. Finally, CB-SEM was used to examine associations between the identified latent constructs and self-reported AICB. Gender, student seniority, and field of study were entered as observed background variables. Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices. The chi-square/degrees of freedom ratio (χ²/df) below 3 indicated good fit, while values below 5 were considered acceptable [ 39 ]. CFI and TLI values of 0.90 or higher indicated acceptable fit, and values of 0.95 or higher indicated good fit [ 40 , 41 ]. RMSEA values of 0.08 or lower indicated acceptable fit, and values of 0.06 or lower indicated good fit [ 40 , 41 ]. SRMR values of 0.08 or lower were considered acceptable [ 40 , 41 ]. Structural paths were interpreted as statistically significant when p < 0.05. The explained variance of AI-assisted academic cheating behaviors was reported using R 2 . 3. Results 3.1. Discussion and Implications 4.1. Discussion This study examined how students’ perceptions of academic pressure, ethical ambiguity, AI capabilities, and institutional policy gaps were empirically organized and how the resulting dimensions were associated with self-reported AICB. The analyses identified two dimensions, ETIG and APPC, which showed different patterns of association with AICB. The structural model also indicated associations involving gender and field of study, whereas student seniority was not significantly associated with AICB. Given the modest proportion of variance explained by the model, these findings should be interpreted as evidence of association rather than as strong explanatory or causal evidence. In relation to RQ1, ETIG was positively associated with students’ self-reported AICB (β = 0.228, p < 0.001). Higher ETIG scores, reflecting unclear ethical boundaries, easy access to AI tools, difficulty detecting AI-assisted work, and gaps in institutional guidance, were associated with higher self-reported AICB. This pattern is consistent with previous research highlighting the difficulty of distinguishing legitimate AI assistance from academic misconduct and the challenges that generative AI creates for academic integrity policies and practices [ 3 , 4 , 21 ]. Importantly, the EFA showed that items concerning ethical ambiguity, technological affordances, and institutional policy gaps clustered within a single dimension rather than remaining separate. This finding suggests that students may perceive these issues as closely interconnected. For example, uncertainty about acceptable AI use may coexist with perceptions that AI-generated work is difficult to detect or that institutional and instructor guidance is insufficient. Therefore, the present findings support considering these ethical, technological, and institutional issues together when examining students’ self-reported AI-assisted academic cheating [ 22 ]. APPC showed a small negative association with AICB in the structural model (β = −0.148, p = 0.022), but this result requires particularly cautious interpretation. At the latent correlation level, APPC was essentially unrelated to AICB (r = 0.014), while APPC and ETIG were substantially correlated (r = 0.716). The negative structural coefficient represents the association between APPC and AICB after accounting for ETIG and the other variables in the model, rather than a simple inverse relationship between academic pressure and cheating. Therefore, it should not be interpreted as evidence that academic pressure or peer comparison reduces AI-assisted academic cheating. Academic pressure has been discussed in previous research as relevant to dishonest academic practices [ 23 ], and students in the present study also reported relatively high agreement with items concerning academic performance pressure and overloaded schedules. Taken together, these results suggest that the relationship between academic pressure, peer comparison, and AI-assisted cheating may be more complex than a straightforward positive or negative association and warrants further investigation. In relation to RQ2, gender was positively associated with self-reported AICB (β = 0.142, p < 0.001), with male students tending to report higher AICB scores than female students. This pattern is broadly consistent with studies reporting higher levels of academic cheating or greater tolerance of dishonest academic behavior among male students [ 27 , 28 ], although previous findings have not been consistent across contexts [ 29 ]. The association observed in the present study was modest and should be interpreted cautiously because the sample was drawn from a single university and was unevenly distributed across gender, field of study, and student seniority. Although field of study was included as a control variable in the structural model, statistical adjustment cannot fully address these sample imbalances. Field of study was also positively associated with AICB (β = 0.104, p = 0.008), with non-STEM students tending to report higher AICB scores than STEM students. Because field of study was introduced primarily as a background control variable rather than as a focal variable in the research questions, this finding should be treated as supplementary. It should also be interpreted cautiously because STEM students constituted 81.3% of the sample and the distribution of field of study varied substantially across gender and student seniority groups. Further research with more balanced disciplinary samples is needed before drawing broader conclusions about differences between STEM and non-STEM students. Student seniority was not significantly associated with AICB (β = 0.018, p = 0.622). This differs from previous findings in the Vietnamese context indicating variation in AI-assisted academic cheating across year levels [ 23 ]. The present result suggests that such differences may not be consistent across samples or institutional settings. Future studies could examine whether year-level differences depend on factors such as assessment practices, AI experience, or exposure to institutional guidance. Overall, the structural model accounted for approximately 5% of the variance in students’ self-reported AICB, indicating that the observed associations represent only a limited part of a more complex phenomenon. Other potentially relevant variables, including perceived risk of detection, attitudes toward AI use, AI literacy, assessment design, moral disengagement, peer norms, and trust in institutional policies, were not examined in the present model. Future research should investigate these variables using broader samples and longitudinal or experimental designs that can provide stronger evidence about temporal and potentially causal relationships. 4.2. Practical implications The findings point to several areas that higher education institutions may consider when developing guidance for student use of generative AI. The positive association between ETIG and self-reported AICB highlights the relevance of clear boundaries around acceptable AI use, explicit course-level expectations, and consistent institutional guidance. Students may benefit from concrete explanations of which uses of AI are permitted, which require disclosure, and which may constitute academic misconduct. The small negative association between APPC and AICB should not be interpreted as evidence that academic pressure protects against cheating. Instead, the result indicates that academic pressure and peer comparison have a more complex relationship with AICB when considered alongside ETIG and the background variables. Therefore, institutions should avoid drawing intervention conclusions from this coefficient alone. Gender and field of study were also associated with self-reported AICB, with male students tending to report higher scores than female students and non-STEM students tending to report higher scores than STEM students. However, both associations were modest and were observed in an unevenly distributed sample from a single institution. These findings may not provide a basis for targeting particular gender or disciplinary groups. Guidelines on AI use and academic integrity education should be provided across programs and academic years. 4.3. Limitations and future research directions This study has several limitations that also point to directions for future research. First, the data were collected through convenience sampling from undergraduate students at a single university of science and technology in Vietnam. The sample was also unevenly distributed across gender, field of study, and student seniority. Therefore, future studies should examine the factor structure and structural associations in more diverse, multi-institutional samples and across different disciplinary contexts. Second, the study relied on self-reported data about academically sensitive behaviors. Although participation was anonymous, underreporting remains possible. Several AICB items also showed low mean scores, which may have reduced the magnitude of the observed associations. Future research could use complementary approaches such as indirect questioning, list experiments, behavioral indicators, or mixed-methods designs to reduce reliance on direct self-report. Third, the cross-sectional design does not establish temporal ordering or causal relationships among the variables. Longitudinal studies could clarify how students’ perceptions and self-reported AI-assisted cheating behaviors change over time, while appropriately designed experimental studies could provide stronger evidence regarding potential causal mechanisms. Finally, the structural model accounted for only approximately 5% of the variance in self-reported AICB, indicating that substantial variation remains unexplained. Future studies should examine additional variables such as perceived risk of detection, attitudes toward AI use, AI literacy, assessment design, moral disengagement, peer norms, and trust in institutional policies.
