Executive Summary
This report describes some examples of the operation of two grid-forming (GFM) inverter-based
photovoltaic-battery hybrid power plants connected to the electric system of the island of Kauai
in Hawaii. Both plants are photovoltaic power plants with battery energy storage and are
connected to the networked transmission system operated by Kauai Island Utility Cooperative
(KIUC), along with several other power plants including an oil-fired plant, diesel generators,
small hydroelectric plants, a biofuel plant, and several other photovoltaic plants.

While many studies have examined the potential benefits of GFM operation to enhance grid
stability in regions with high levels of inverter-based resources (IBRs), nearly all grid-connected
IBRs have historically been operated using grid-following (GFL) controls. At the time of the
events described in this report, the two GFM plants in Kauai were the only known IBR plants in
the U.S. that operated in grid-forming mode while in parallel with a transmission system. A
primary goal of this report is to demonstrate the feasibility, potential benefits, and challenges of
operating IBR plants in GFM mode. In particular, this report describes two major grid stability
events that, along with related studies by the authors and others, indicate significant benefits of
GFM operation.

This report also compares the performance of the two GFM plants to a specification for GFM
IBRs developed by the UNIFI Consortium.

The benefits described here are enabled in part because the two plants in question include both
PV and battery energy storage (BESS). For two of the PV-BESS hybrid facilities described in
this report, the solar inverters operated in GFL mode, while the battery inverters operated in
GFM mode, known as AC-coupling. The other hybrid facilities use DC-coupled inverters that
deliver both PV and BESS energy to the grid through the same power electronic device. Battery
inverters that operate in GFM mode while grid-connected are now available from several
vendors for utility-scale plants. In addition, some vendors offer GFM inverters that accept DC-
coupled PV and battery inputs simultaneously. However, while GFM operation of PV inverters
is feasible, GFM-capable inverters for utility-scale PV-only applications are not widely available
currently.

1 Introduction and Background
1.1 Overview of Grid-Forming Functionality for Inverter-Based
 Resources
For many years, electric system operators and others have connected photovoltaic (PV) and wind
generation to power systems. PV and wind generators are typically coupled to the power system
via power electronic inverters that convert DC energy into AC energy, so they are sometimes
referred to as inverter-based resources (IBRs). Grid-connected IBR inverters have historically
been designed to inject a controlled current waveform into the power system, and the magnitude
and phase of the current are controlled to inject the energy produced by the PV panels or wind
turbine. This is referred to as grid-following (GFL) operation because the inverter follows the
frequency and phase angle of the grid voltage waveform at its point of connection. This has
historically worked well, as evidenced by the high levels of inverter-based generation that have
been reached by many power systems around the world [1]. Conventionally, synchronous
generators “form” the grid, and IBRs follow it; hence their controls can be referred to as grid-
following. However, as grid operators draw on increasing amounts of IBRs, a power grid can
reach a point where its voltage starts to become unstable because the remaining voltage sources
(conventional rotating generators) no longer provide a strong enough voltage reference for all the
IBRs to synchronize to [2], [3]. One solution to this is to operate some of the IBRs differently,
such that instead of following the grid voltage, they provide their own internal voltage reference;
in other words, they become grid-forming (GFM) [4], [5].

Inverters that operate in grid-forming mode in off-grid applications have existed for decades. For
grid-connected operation, GFM inverter controls must allow the inverter’s voltage output
waveform to shift over time to synchronize with other voltages sources on the system, just as a
synchronous generator connected to a grid with other generators does. Because PV and wind
energy are weather dependent, GFM inverters available today generally have battery energy
storage systems (BESS) as a DC input. They may also allow for other input sources such as PV
in addition to the BESS. (The feasibility of GFM PV and GFM wind has also been demonstrated
at a large scale in laboratory settings [6], but GFM PV inverters and GFM wind turbine
generators are not yet commercially available at the time of this writing.)

Many studies have shown that operation of some of the IBRs on a power system in GFM mode
can not only allow the GFM IBRs to be stable in weak grid conditions, but also can help provide
a stable voltage reference for nearby GFL inverters [7]. However, only limited large-scale
examples exist of GFM IBRs connected to a transmission system. This report presents an
example of two GFM PV-battery plants operating on the same transmission system along with
other resources on the Hawaiian island of Kauai.

1.3 Kauai’s First GFM PV-Battery Plant
In March 2017, a 13 MW AC PV system with a 13 MW, 52 MWh BESS was connected (IBR 4).
This PV-BESS hybrid power plant is AC-coupled, meaning that the PV portion of the plant and
the BESS portion of the plant use separate inverters. The BESS portion of the plant was
converted from GFL to GFM operation in March 2019 via an inverter control upgrade. The GFM
BESS inverters use a control approach that includes a virtual synchronous machine (VSM). The
change was made by the plant owner-operator in cooperation with KIUC to improve system
frequency response and stability. This plant has continued to operate in GFM mode since 2019.
This is one of the first examples of a transmission-connected GFM IBR plant in the world [9].
KIUC later would convert an additional existing PV-BESS plant to GFM, as discussed in Section
3.

2 November 21, 2021, Loss-of-Generation Event
On November 21, 2021, the oil-fired plant designated Plant A tripped offline due to an internal
failure while generating 26.1 MW. The total system load at the time was 43.1 MW, so the trip
caused a sudden generation loss of 60.6% of the total load. The trip occurred at 5:30 am, so no
solar generation was online, but the BESSes at IBR1, IBR2, IBR3, and IBR4 and IBR4 were
online. The four IBRs ramped up generation within about 50 ms, keeping the power system
online; Figure 3 shows how the generation mix transitioned from 87% synchronous and 13%
inverter-based immediately before the trip to 27% synchronous and 73% inverter-based
immediately after the event as the four IBRs quickly increased their output. This event and some
of the analysis of it has been described in [10], [11].

This is a notable demonstration of the ability of the four IBRs to rapidly increase active power
and keep the system loads online in response to a sudden loss of generation. While IBR4 was in
GFM model (virtual synchronous machine), the remaining three IBRs were in GFL mode. They
had been designed to provide very fast frequency response per KIUC’s requirement to respond in
less than 50 ms [12]. However, as the four IBRs began responding, the frequency and active
power of the four plants began to oscillate with a frequency of 18 to 20 Hz at a high amplitude,
as shown in Figure 4 and Figure 5. The oscillation was measured throughout the island by digital
fault recorders (DFRs) and was visible in frequency, voltage, active power, and reactive power
measurements. This system-wide subsynchronous oscillation had not previously been observed.

The oscillation was sustained for about one minute before dampening out as the frequency
returned to the normal operating range, as seen in Figure 6. The oscillation frequency ranged
over the course of the event between 18 and 20 Hz. A small amount of load was shed (1.7 MW)
due to the operation of an underfrequency load shedding (UFLS) relay. KIUC attributed this to a
UFLS relay misoperation, as the frequency never remained below the UFLS setting of 59.2 Hz
for more than a few milliseconds at a time.

It is remarkable that the power system survived this event given that more than 60% of the
generation tripped. This would not be possible in a system of this size without IBRs because the

 6

power frequency droop response of a synchronous generator typically takes 500 ms or longer to
start responding and several seconds to reach full output, so the system frequency would drop too
low to be recovered, likely causing all generation to trip and blacking out the entire power
system. Therefore the fact that the power system recovered and only a small number of
customers lost power can be seen as a successful and novel outcome for such a severe loss of
generation.

However, the high-amplitude system-wide subsynchronous oscillation that immediately followed
the IBRs’ rapid response is clearly problematic.

2.1 Oscillation Investigation and Source Identification
KIUC, the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR, formerly NREL), and the owner of IBR1
and IBR2 initiated an investigation of the event with a goal of understanding its cause and
mitigating it. NLR’s work on this was funded by a DOE Integrated Energy Systems Office
project for which KIUC and the plant owner were partners at the time of the event. Several
findings from the oscillation analysis are used as examples in a recently published guide from the
Energy Systems Integration Group titled Diagnosis and Mitigation of Observed Oscillations in
IBR-Dominant Power Systems [13].

When Plant A tripped, in addition to causing a sudden loss of 60% of the system’s instantaneous
generation, it also resulted in a sudden loss of inertia. The total synchronous inertia of the
generators online dropped from 121 MVA-seconds (an inertia constant, H, of 0.9 seconds, which
is already low by conventional standards) to 58 MVA-seconds (H = 0.7 seconds). This loss of
inertia contributed to a maximum rate of change of frequency (ROCOF) of about -2.7 Hz/s,
neglecting the 18-20 Hz component, as seen in Figure 7a. Note that if the initial downward spike
in frequency is considered, a much higher ROCOF of around -53 Hz/s or more can be estimated,
for example as shown in Figure 7b, where 60 Hz DFR frequency data at multiple locations is
shown, and the magnitude of this ROCOF can vary significantly depending on measurement
method [14], [15], [16]. However, these measurements during and shortly after the initial
transient event are heavily influenced by the distortion of the voltage waveform caused by the
fault and the tripping transient, so frequency and ROCOF measurements during these initial few
milliseconds should not be considered reliable. ROCOF calculation is further complicated by the
18-20 Hz oscillation that occurred during the frequency event. For this reason, we focus on the
ROCOF approximation made when ignoring the initial fault-induced transient and removing the
18-20 Hz oscillatory component.

NLR also applied dissipating energy flow (DEF) analysis [18], [19] to the IBR data from the
event. Briefly, DEF analysis identifies sources of power system oscillations by computing the
energy injected at the oscillatory frequency at any source or line,

 𝑊𝑊 = � Δ𝑃𝑃dΔ𝜃𝜃 + Δ𝑄𝑄d(ln Δ𝑉𝑉) (1).

Here, P, Q, V, and θ are the active power, reactive power, voltage magnitude, and voltage phase
angle of the source under analysis. The Δ operator denotes the filtered component of each
variable at the oscillatory frequency. If the integral (1) is positive, it indicates that the plant is a
net source of oscillatory energy, and if (1) is negative, it indicates that the plant is a net sink of
oscillatory energy. In addition, the two terms in the integral can be analyzed separately to learn
the effects of the plant’s active power and reactive power injections on the oscillation. It is also
possible to visually estimate the result of the DEF integral when looking at a plot of active power
and voltage phase angle over time: if active power and frequency generally oscillate in phase
with each other, the first term of the integral will be positive, and the active power of the source
in question is likely driving the oscillation. Conversely, if active power and frequency oscillate
out of phase with each other, the source is sinking oscillatory energy (i.e. damping the
oscillation). The same logic can be applied to the reactive power-voltage term of the integral.

DEF analysis of the DFR data from the 18-20 Hz event showed that IBR1 and IBR2 sourced
oscillatory energy, while IBR 3 and IBR4 had negligible impact on oscillatory energy, as seen in
Figure 9. Thus, the team suspected that IBR1 and IBR 2 were responsible for the oscillation.

 Figure 9. DEF analysis of data from the four IBRs online during the event.

At the time when the team first applied DEF to the 18-20 Hz event, DEF analysis was not proven
to provide trustworthy results for faster IBR-driven oscillations, but it was trusted for slower
electromechanical oscillations and was in use by at least one system operator. A recent paper
proposes a theoretical basis for DEF analysis of oscillations involving IBRs under certain
assumptions [10]. That paper also provides more detail on the application of DEF to this
oscillation event.

power flow analysis indicated that IBR1 and IBR2 provided negative damping (i.e. were
oscillation sources). IBR4 was neither a source nor a sink of the oscillation according to this

Fortunately, the KIUC system has fewer than 80 buses and only 7 synchronous generators were
online at the time of the event. This relatively small size made it feasible to develop an
electromagnetic transient (EMT) model of the entire system. Because an EMT model can capture
the full details of the IBR controls, we expected it would be possible to recreate the event in
simulation. The team developed an EMT model in PSCAD software and included vendor-
supplied black-box models of IBR1 and IBR2. The PSCAD model was derived from KIUC’s
PSS/E model. Note that based on the pre-event power flow recording in KIUC’s supervisory
control and data acquisition (SCADA) software, we revised the settings of the breakers,
generation units, loads, and switched shunts in KIUC’s PSS/E model before the conversion. In
the KIUC EMT model, generic EMT models of IBR3 and IBR4 were included, with control
parameters tuned so that their output closely matched the recorded DFR data when replaying the
recorded DFR data at the plant terminals in simulation. Tuning of the vendor-supplied PSCAD
models of IBR1 and IBR2 included matching power setpoints to SCADA data and matching
some control parameters for which it was not possible to determine the actual values in use in the
field during the event. The tuned IBR models were incorporated into the full system PSCAD
model. The tuned EMT model was able to replicate the 18-20 Hz oscillations as well as the
important characteristics of the IBR responses. For example, Figure 12 compares the PSCAD
simulated time-domain response of IBR1 to the measured response. The team also performed
mode shape analysis and DEF analysis on the PSCAD simulation traces. Figure 13 compares the
DEF and mode shape analyses of the simulation data to the same analyses of the measured event
data. The match is not perfect, but it is good enough to capture the salient characteristics of the

While a validated EMT model provides the ability to replay the event and experiment with
oscillation mitigation techniques using trial and error, it does not provide fundamental insight
into the causes of the oscillation. Therefore the team collaborated with Purdue University to
develop state-space small-signal model of the entire KIUC system using the techniques described
in [21]. Because the internal details of the controls of the IBRs were not available, the state-space
model used selected generic models that were tuned to emulate the responses of IBR1-IBR4.
Additional information on the state-space model can be found in [10]. The small signal analysis

 12

indicated that the phase-locked loops (PLLs) and active power controllers of IBR1 and IBR2 are
highly related to modes near 20 Hz. The eigenvalue analysis indicated that reducing the PLL
gains or increasing the power-frequency droop constants of IBR1 and IBR2 can dampen modes
near 20 Hz [10].

As a final step in the oscillation root cause analysis, we developed a single-inverter infinite-bus
model that captures the 18-20 Hz mode. Because IBR1 and IBR2 oscillated together and not
against each other, and they have similar controls and settings, a single-inverter model was found
to be able to capture the important characteristics of the oscillatory mode. To investigate the
effect of internal control details that are not externally visible in the black-box model, a generic
GFL inverter controller was again used in this step. The model is shown in Figure 14. More
details of this model can also be found in [10].

Using this simplified model and performing a frequency scanning study as visualized in Figure
15, we were able to identify four key parameters that influence the 18-20 Hz mode:

 1. The frequency measurement delay, shown in red in Figure 14. Higher delays contribute
 to instability.
 2. The power-frequency droop constant. Lower droop constants (i.e. more aggressive
 droops) contribute to instability
 3. The PLL gain. Higher PLL proportional gains (i.e. faster PLLs) contribute to instability.
 4. The grid impedance. Higher grid impedances (i.e. lower SCRs, weaker grid conditions)
 contribute to instability.

 Figure 15. Nyquist plots of the open-loop transfer function 𝑯𝑯(𝒔𝒔)𝒆𝒆−𝒔𝒔𝒔𝒔 of the infinite-bus system in
Figure 14. The GFL becomes stable if (a) reducing the frequency measurement delay, (b) adopting
 a less aggressive P/f droop constant, (c) reducing the PLL gain, or (d) increasing the SCR.

When all four of the above parameters are in the range that contributes to instability, the 18-20
Hz oscillation mode has zero or negative damping, as was the case on November 21, 2021.
Changing any one of the four key parameters beyond a certain threshold can cause the oscillation
mode to have positive damping. Of course, the desired level of damping of system-wide
oscillatory modes is typically significantly greater than zero, so it may be difficult to achieve a
desirable stability status by adjusting just one parameter. In addition, adjusting any of the above
parameters would have other implications. For example, increasing the grid strength (SCR ratio)
implies keeping more synchronous machines online or decreasing line or transformer impedance,
both of which have cost implications. Reducing the PLL speed would have implications for
control system performance, especially fast frequency response, which is a requirement for IBRs
on Kauai. Reducing the delay in the frequency-power control loop may require control hardware
changes or may affect how the system responds to transient events that affect the ability to
measure frequency accurately. In addition, changes to PLL gains or control system delays
generally are not possible without significant work by the inverter manufacturer. And using a
less aggressive droop response reduces the amount of power the IBRs inject for a given change
in frequency, which reduces the system’s ability to control frequency.

In practice, shortly after the event, KIUC did temporarily increase the droop constant of the IBRs
from 3% to 4%, moving from a fairly aggressive droop response to a more moderate one. This is
a change that KIUC was able to make easily via SCADA. (It is believed to be uncommon for
other utilities to have direct control over IBR droop gains today.) KIUC deemed the resulting
reduction in system frequency response to be worth compromising to achieve improved
oscillatory stability. This change was made shortly after the event and without the benefit of the

 14

analysis described above, based on the knowledge that aggressive droop gains often lead to
stability issues [22]. The PSCAD model of the system had not yet been developed at that point,
and the PSS/E model did not capture the oscillation, so it was not possible to simulate the
change, but it was expected to increase damping.

It is also worth noting that two of the four contributing factors to the oscillatory event are related
to frequency measurement, which is a requirement of GFL control and is not typically a
requirement of GFM control. 2 In addition, instability in weak (low-SCR) grids is a known
challenge for GFL IBRs. This observation points towards another potential mitigation that is
examined below: converting one or more IBRs to GFM mode.

Finally, note that because of the high proportion of IBRs on their system, KIUC had required an
extremely fast frequency response time from their IBRs of less than 50 ms [12]. While this helps
with system frequency response, it creates a control design challenge for IBR manufacturers. It is
not easy to design a grid-following IBR controller that can measure a frequency change, take
control action, and increase power output within just three line cycles. While designing such a
controller may not be impossible, it may lead to design compromises that sacrifice oscillatory
stability. In contrast, GFM IBR controls naturally inject active power in much less than 50 ms
following frequency changes simply by following their short timescale control objective, which
is to maintain a constant or nearly constant voltage magnitude and phase angle in the first few
line cycles following a grid disturbance [23]. Because frequency is the time derivative of phase
angle, maintaining a constant phase angle inherently requires injecting active power to resist
frequency changes.

Based on the oscillation investigation described above, it was concluded that IBR1 and IBR2,
which were both in GFL mode, were the primary drivers of the oscillation. Both use inverters
from the same manufacturer. The combination of reduced grid strength due to the tripping of
Plant A, aggressive droop response, and a fast PLL with a significant delay in its control path led
to the 18-20 Hz oscillation.

2.2 Oscillation Mitigations
Based on the oscillation source analysis summarized above, the team examined three potential
oscillation mitigation methods:

 1. Method 1: Increasing the droop constant of IBR1 and IBR2 from 3% to 4% (as already
 implemented by KIUC)
 2. Method 2: Reducing the PLL proportional gains of IBR1 and IBR2
 3. Method 3: Converting one or more IBRs to GFM mode.

2
 While typical GFM IBR controls do not depend on frequency measurement for fast, low-level controls or
synchronization with other resources, they may measure frequency for other purposes.

 15

Two other potential mitigations were not examined in detail because they were not considered
practical. Reducing the delay in the frequency measurement path of IBR1 and IBR2 would
require different control hardware in the already-installed IBRs, so it was not considered
feasible. Similarly, maintaining SCR above a certain value (for example, 3) at IBR1 and IBR2 at
all times was not considered feasible because it would cause KIUC to incur significant operating
costs (to maintain synchronous machines online and/or keep IBRs offline, for example) or capital
costs (to reduce line or transformer impedances, for example). Therefore, the three mitigation
methods considered were related to the droop constant (which is easily adjustable), the PLL gain
(which may be adjustable via a software change with the help of the inverter manufacturer), and
the conversion to GFM, which was on the development roadmap of the plant owner and inverter
manufacturer of IBR1 and IBR2.

Regarding method 3, note that while converting a GFL IBR to GFM does not increase the
conventional SCR calculation at nearby generators (because a weakness of the conventional SCR
metric is that it does not distinguish between GFL and GFM IBRs), it does increase the grid
strength by reducing the sensitivity of voltage to current changes, so, for other IBRs near the
converted IBR, this mitigation option can be considered a way of addressing the impact of grid
strength on the oscillation.

The initial method of evaluating the three mitigation methods was by using the newly developed
PSCAD model to simulate the event with and without the proposed methods implemented. For
method 1, droop constants are user-accessible in the black-box PSCAD models of IBR1 and
IBR2, so the team tested that change in simulation. The results are shown in Figure 16. As
expected, this causes the oscillation to have positive damping, though it is still visible in the
response to the disturbance.

Simulating method 2 required collaboration with the manufacturer of the inverters in IBR1 and
IBR2 because PLL parameters are not accessible to the user of the black-box model, as is often
the case. The plant owner facilitated this collaboration, and the team was able to simulate method
2, as shown in Figure 17. As with method 1, method 2 also causes the oscillation to have positive
damping, though it is again still visible.

It was also investigated via simulation whether implementing method 2 would cause the IBRs to
no longer meet the fast frequency response time requirement of 50 ms. It was found that IBR1
and IBR2 could still meet the requirement with the slower PLL.

There are multiple possible ways of implementing method 3, conversion of a second IBR plant to
GFM mode, because there are three different IBRs that could be converted, and there are many
ways of implementing GFM controls. The two common implementations of GFM control are
droop-based GFM control and virtual synchronous machine (VSM) GFM control [4]. Many
variations of those two methods exist, and other methods exist as well. And of course, inverter
manufacturers generally do not share the details of their GFM implementations (or GFL
implementations), which are typically considered intellectual property. To keep the number of
simulations reasonable, the team chose to use representative generic versions of droop-based
GFM control [24] and VSM-based GFM control [25]. We simulated each of these two GFM
implementations at the locations of IBR1, IBR2, and IBR3. The stability of each case is
summarized in Table 4, and case 2 (IBR1 converted to droop-based GFM control) is pictured in
Figure 18. In summary, converting any IBR to either form of GFM control stabilized the 18-20
Hz mode. Table 4 also shows that converting all the large PV-BESS plants on Kauai to either
form of GFM control also stabilizes the oscillation.

Note that even though IBR3 was found not to be a driver of the oscillation, converting IBR3 to

other nearby GFL IBRs [5], [7], [26].

simulation. Comparing Figure 16, Figure 17, and Figure 18, note that method 3 (GFM
conversion) provides better damping of the 18-20 Hz mode than the other two methods.

3 Transition of IBR1 to GFM Operation
The PV-BESS plant identified as IBR1 in Figure 5 had previously been scheduled to be
converted to GFM mode to allow it to power a microgrid consisting of local loads at times when
the main grid is not available. In part to allow for microgrid loads to remain online during
planned and unplanned island transitions, the conversion to GFM mode was planned to be
permanent, such that the plant would operate in GFM mode both when the microgrid is
connected to KIUC’s system and when it is islanded. This also allows the GFM IBR plant to
contribute to the grid strength of the KIUC power system.

To prepare for the conversion to GFM operation, the owner of IBR1 had collaborated with NLR
to test one duplicate inverter and the power plant controller at NLR’s Flatirons test site [29]. This
occurred prior to the PHIL tests of the three mitigation methods described in the previous
section.

From March of 2022 till August of 2022, KIUC and the plant owner-operator of IBR1
collaborated to convert the plant to GFM operation. Inverters were converted to GFM mode one
inverter at a time and tested to verify reliable operation.

IBR1 is rated at 14 MW AC and consists of twelve 1.4-MW DC-coupled PV-BESS inverters.
When in GFM mode, the inverters use a droop-based GFM control algorithm.

At the time of its conversion to GFM mode, this was the only known transmission-connected
microgrid in the U.S. (and maybe the world) where an IBR always operated in GFM mode, even

5 GFM Plant Performance During Transmission-Level
  Intentional Island Event
A second event occurred on July 15, 2024, that also highlighted the value of operating IBR1 in
GFM mode. A wind-driven brush fire (not caused by the utility) spread quickly and was moving
toward the highway where two transmission circuits connect the west portion of Kauai to the rest
of the system, as shown in Figure 30. Critical loads in the west portion of the grid included a
hospital and a shelter for evacuees from the brush fire affected area. Before the brush fire
reached the highway and transmission circuits, KIUC engineers proactively disconnected both
ends of the two transmission lines, de-energizing the lines and creating an intentional island
(microgrid) including three transmission buses as shown in Figure 31. Microgrid operation had
not been planned for in advance.

The intentional island included two large generation sources: a customer-owned hydroelectric
generator and the PV-BESS plant, IBR1. All loads remained online during the island transition.
An unknown amount of small distribution-connected PV and PV-BESS generation, primarily
rooftop PV, was also present in the microgrid. The distribution-connected PV was uncontrolled,
invisible to the utility, and used primarily conventional grid-following inverters. The
distribution-connected PV-BESS was primarily configured to serve local loads. The microgrid
load included a hospital and an evacuation center for people who had evacuated due to the fire.

6 Comparison of GFM Plant Performance to UNIFI
  GFM Specifications
This section compares the performance of the two GFM IBRs on Kauai, IBR1 and IBR4, to the
UNIFI Specifications for Grid-Forming Inverter-Based Resources, Version 2 [30]. (At the time
of this writing, the UNIFI Consortium expects Version 3 of their GFM specifications to be
published in early 2026.) Each subsection below focuses on one requirement from the UNIFI
specification and compares the GFM IBRs’ performance to the requirement. Note that the UNIFI
specification was developed after IBR1 and IBR4 were already installed and therefore should not
be considered a requirement for those IBRs. Nevertheless, it is interesting to compare the two
GFM IBRs’ performance to the specification.

Each subsection below briefly paraphrases the applicable UNIFI requirement; for the full
requirement, see [30]. Most UNIFI requirements note that the IBR is only required to exhibit the
specified behavior to the extent possible within equipment limits; this report does not repeat that
caveat for each requirement. Note that version 2 of the UNIFI requirement is largely qualitative
and does not currently contain quantitative pass-fail criteria. Nevertheless, for each requirement
we state whether the IBR responses appear to comply. In addition, while some requirements in
the UNIFI specification are specific to GFM IBRs (i.e., one would not expect a GFL IBR to meet
those requirements), other requirements in the UNIFI specification apply to all IBRs, both GFM
and GFL.

6.1 Autonomously Support the Grid
The UNIFI specification requires GFM IBRs to autonomously respond to changes in the grid
based on local measurements over the subtransient and transient time frames. This is vague-
sounding requirement that is intended simply to state that GFM IBRs should not rely on external
control signals to provide grid support on short timescales, though they may also respond to
communicated commands (such as automatic generation control signals of SCADA commands)
over longer timescales.

The autonomous plant responses to the April 2, 2023, trip event shown in Section 4 demonstrate
the IBR1 and IBR4 do respond autonomously to changes in voltage and frequency. There may be
room to improve the autonomous responses through control tuning. Nevertheless, it is the
autonomous responses of the plants that kept the grid stable and arrested the fall of frequency
during this very severe generation trip event.

6.2 Dispatchability of Power Output
The UNIFI specification requires GFM IBRs to have dispatchable steady-state power output.
Like the other dispatchable generation sources on Kauai, IBR1 and IBR4 are dispatched at one-
second intervals through KIUC’s SCADA commands, which provide active power setpoints to
each plant. The active power commands reflect a combination of secondary frequency regulation

 33

through automatic generation control (AGC) and scheduling dispatch commands. The state of
energy (SOE) of each plant’s BESS is fed back to KIUC’s control center for use in dispatch.

6.3 Positive Damping of Voltage and Frequency Oscillations
The UNIFI specification requires GFM IBRs to provide non-negative damping, noting that this
may be achieved simply by being in GFM control mode.

Contrasting the system responses during the two trip events, as shown in Sections 2 and 4,
illustrates the improvement in damping of the 18-20 Hz mode when IBR1 was converted to
GFM mode. The mode had nearly zero damping (or negative damping, depending on the
moment) during the 2021 event with one GFM IBR, and very high damping during the 2023
event. This, along with the analysis presented in [10], indicates that changing IBR1 from GFL to
GFM greatly improved the damping of this mode.

6.4 Active and Reactive Power Sharing Across Generation Resources
The UNIFI specification states that GFM IBRs are expected to autonomously share power with
other resources via a power-frequency droop characteristic, like power sharing between
conventional resources.

The two GFM plants on Kauai (as well as the GFL plants) have power-frequency droop
characteristics that autonomously adjust power output in response to frequency changes. For the
GFM plants, this is implemented in the plant controllers and occurs on a timescale that is slower
than the inverter-level GFM response and faster than the AGC timescale. At any given time, the
output of each GFM inverter is the sum of its GFM power response to any disturbance and the
power command from the plant controller, which in turn is the sum of the plant-level droop
response plus the AGC signal from the utility. Figure 27 and Figure 29 show the droop responses
of IBR1 and IBR4 to a frequency change caused by a major loss of generation. Note that the
response of the GFL plant, IBR2, as shown in Figure 28, is quite similar to that of IBR1. This is
as expected: the droop responses of GFL and GFM IBRs are not necessarily distinguishable
externally, though their implementations inside the inverter controls are different. The difference
between GFM and GFL controls is on a shorter timescale than droop response.

Note that while IBR1 employs a droop-based GFM control structure and IBR4 uses a virtual
synchronous machine control structure, they are able to synchronize and share power with each
other and with the other resources on Kauai, as expected and as required by the UNIFI
specification.

6.5 Operation in Grids With Low System Strength
The UNIFI specification requires GFM IBRs to operate stably in weak grid conditions. It also
notes that GFM IBRs can improve system strength.

 34

Following the trip of the largest generator in the 2021 and 2023 events described above, the
KIUC grid strength was greatly reduced. As noted in Section 2, one cause of the oscillation
during the 2021 event was the response of two GFL IBRs (IBR1 and IBR2) to the weakened
system strength. During the 2023 event, the conversion of IBR1 for GFM mode not only
prevented it from exciting the 18-20 Hz mode, but also improved the system strength enough that
IBR2 did not excite the 18-20 Hz mode either. Qualitatively, IBR1 and IBR4 meet the
requirement to operate in low system strength.

The ability of IBR1 to operate in a grid that conventionally would be considered weak can also
be observed in its ability to operate in an electrical island without any synchronous machines.
Conventionally, the SCR seen by IBR1 when islanded would be zero, since there is no
synchronous resource providing fault current. However, because IBR1 is operating as a strong
voltage source, the island grid is actually not weak in the sense that the voltage is not highly
sensitive to changes in current. This illustrates a weakness in traditional grid strength metrics that
do not account for GFM IBR contributions, a point also noted by the UNIFI specifications
document.

current for small levels of voltage unbalance, as often occur in normal operations, and should aid
in reducing the voltage unbalance factor (VUF).

occurred during the unplanned island event mentioned in Section 5. The level of voltage

Thus IBR1 appears to comply with this requirement.

6.8 Response to Symmetrical Faults
The UNIFI specification requires GFM IBRs to inject current in response to symmetrical faults
to resist the voltage drop, up to the IBR’s short-term rated current.

A fault event on January 4, 2024, provided a somewhat rare opportunity to observe the GFM
IBR responses to a three-phase fault, though the event is somewhat complex and also involved
other fault types. The fault started as a phase-to-phase fault on a generator step-up (GSU)
transformer at the other oil-fired generation station (not Plant A) but was not able to be cleared
due to a protection setting error that has since been fixed. The phase-to-phase fault evolved into a
three-phase fault after 4.86 seconds and was eventually cleared when all the generation on the
island tripped several seconds later. Figure 37 shows the voltage on each phase at the 57 kV
substation where IBR1 is connected. Because the fault was on the Y side of a Y-D step-up
transformer, which is the low-voltage side, the high-voltage side saw a significant drop only in
phase A for the first portion of the fault. From 4.86 seconds until 7.90 seconds the fault became
nearly symmetrical (three-phase) and the two GFM IBRs injected current up to their ratings to
resist the voltage drop, as required by the UNIFI specification. Figure 38 shows the current from
IBR1, which remained around 1.1 p.u. on all phases during the three-phase portion of the event
until IBR1 tripped at about 8 seconds shortly after the voltage dropped suddenly due to the trip
of the largest synchronous generator. Figure 39 shows the current from IBR4, which remained
around 1.2 p.u. for the three-phase portion of the event. IBR4 demonstrated robust ride through,
staying online for 5 seconds after the voltage dropped to about 0.2 p.u., as required by the
voltage ride-through characteristic in Table 10.

The UNIFI specification requires GFM IBRs to maintain balanced voltage to the extent possible

(GFL), even though IBR1 is farther from the fault location. The negative sequence current
response (red trace in lower left plot of each figure) of IBR1 (GFM) also starts slightly sooner
and has greater magnitude than that of IBR2 (GFL). Note that both IBRs also appear to source
zero-sequence current; this current is not sourced by the inverters themselves but by their

6.10 Response to Abnormal Frequency
The UNIFI specification requires that GFM IBRs modulate active power to aid in frequency
event stability and recovery, that they share power with other resources via a droop-like
characteristics, and that the power injected in the subtransient time frame (the first few cycles
following a change in the grid) resist frequency change.

Section 4 demonstrates the ability of IBR1 and IBR4 to respond in the subtransient time frame to
a severe frequency change by injecting large amounts of active power almost instantaneously
following the loss of the large synchronous generator. It also demonstrates the ability of both
IBRs to share power with other resources over a longer time frame.

NLR, KIUC, AES, and their partners recently completed a field experiment on Kauai where
active power pulses were injected from IBR2 to estimate the inertia of the KIUC system and its
components. The experiment measured significant “effective inertia” values for both IBR1 and
IBR4, indicating that they do resist frequency changes on the inertial timescale (though of course
they do not have physical rotating masses electromagnetically coupled to the power system like
synchronous machines do). This work will be described in detail in future publications. Initial
preparations for the field tests include simulations and lab testing are described in [35], [36].

6.11 Response to Phase Jump and Voltage Steps
The UNIFI specification requires GFM IBRs to absorb or inject active or reactive power in a
way that resists changes in positive sequence voltage phase angle and magnitude. No major
positive sequence phase angle jumps have been examined on KIUC.

The complex fault event examined in Figure 37 through Figure 39 contained two significant
positive sequence voltage magnitude steps: one at 4.86 seconds and one at 7.90 seconds. Both
IBR1 and IBR4 significantly increase their reactive power output almost immediately following
the drop in voltage magnitude at 4.86 seconds, as shown in Figure 42 and Figure 43,
respectively. 4 Following the subsequent voltage drop at 7.9 seconds, both GFM IBRs again
increase their reactive power injection, but IBR1 trips a few cycles later, possibly self-protecting
due to overcurrent. While it is important for an IBR to protect itself from damage due to
overcurrent, from a system stability perspective, it would be preferable for IBR1 to limit its
current and remain connected. Both IBRs do comply with the requirement to resist voltage drops.

6.12 Intentional Islanding
The UNIFI specification notes that not all GFM IBRs are required to have the capability to form
an intentional island, which generally requires that the IBR be specifically designed for that
purpose. For example, IBR4 is a GFM IBR, but it is not designed to form an intentional island
containing any loads besides its own internal auxiliary loads. For IBRs that are designed for
intentional islanding, the UNIFI specification requires the ability to maintain a stable voltage and
frequency within the island.

As highlighted in section 5 above, IBR1 has demonstrated the capability to form an intentional
island and maintain stable voltage and frequency in the island. This is beyond what IBR1 was
designed for because the island contained two additional transmission buses, and it was designed
to form a microgrid only with the loads at its substation. In addition to the example provided in
section 5, where IBR1 supported an intentional island containing multiple transmission buses,
IBR1 has also demonstrated the ability to form an intentional island consisting only of the loads
on its own bus.

6.13 Black Start and System Restoration
The UNIFI specification notes that not all GFM IBRs are required to provide black start service,
but for those that are designed for black start, it specifies that they should be able establish a

 45

voltage without external support, supply inrush current, and serve as a voltage reference to which
other resources can synchronize. IBR1 is designed to black start its local microgrid loads. IBR 4
is not designed for black start of loads beyond its own local plant auxiliary loads.

To date we have not evaluated examples of IBR1 providing black start.

6.14 Regulating Voltage Harmonics
The UNIFI specification states that GFM IBRs should comply with grid operator requirements,
and that they may also reduce voltage harmonics caused by other nearby components. We have
not examined the ability of IBR1 or IBR4 to comply with voltage harmonic requirements or to
reduce voltage harmonics present on the system.

6.15 Communications Between System Operator and IBR Plant
The UNIFI specification leaves details of communications between an IBR plant the system
operator up to them, but it does specify that communications should be cyber-secure and that the
GFM IBR should continue to function if communications are interrupted.

The UNIFI team has not examined in detail the security of communications to and from IBR1
and IBR4. Both IBRs do communicate via SCADA with KIUC and are designed to continue
operating at their last received setpoints upon a loss of communications. Communicated
information from KIUC to the plants includes not just power setpoint but many other plant
control parameters and data.

6.16 Secondary Voltage and Frequency Response
The UNIFI specification does not require GFM plants to participate in secondary voltage or
frequency control, but for those that do, it simply specifies that the plant should move to the new
setpoint within the time specified by the system operator.

KIUC’s SCADA system sends AGC commands in combination with dispatch commands as a
single signal. The signals are sent once per second. Both IBR1 and IBR4 respond to their
respective AGC signals. More information on the GFM IBRs’ response to active and reactive
power commands will be included in a future report.

6.17 Summary and Recommendations
While IBR1 and IBR4 were built before UNIFI’s specification for GFM IBRs was published,
they are largely compliant with its requirements. One area for improvement is with the ability to
ride through faults, particularly for IBR1. This is known to be a challenge for many IBRs,
including conventional grid-following ones, but it may be particularly challenging for GFM
IBRs because their voltage source-like characteristic makes limiting current more difficult.
Nevertheless, inverter manufacturers are improving their ride-through capabilities and are
expected to overcome this hurdle as they gain more experience. The owners of the IBR plants on

 46

Kauai continue to work together regularly with KIUC to monitor and improve plant
performance. Key aspects include improving ride-through performance and improved tracking of
firmware revisions.

Comparing the Kauai IBR plants’ performance to the UNIFI specification primarily involved
qualitative comparisons, for example comparing a field data trace to a qualitative description of
the desired plant behavior. It would help industry to establish quantitative criteria for GFM
performance, because these could be used to decide whether a GFM inverter or plant meets
minimum performance criteria without relying on subjective judgement by experts. However,
that does not mean it would necessarily be appropriate to make each of UNIFI’s criteria
quantitative; indeed, some do not lend themselves to quantitative pass-fail metrics. Other UNIFI
requirements that could be made quantitative perhaps should not because a quantitative metric
would unduly restrict innovation, or because the metric is system-specific and not easily
generalizable.

An additional challenge with dialing in a quantitative GFM performance specification comes in
deciding whether it applies only to the commercially available technology of GFM BESS or to
all GFM technologies, including GFM PV, wind, STATCOMs, and HVDC, which have different
capabilities. To date, the UNIFI specification has remained technology agnostic, which
complicates selection of pass-fail criteria because the capabilities of GFM IBRs have limitations
related to the primary energy source.

As an alternative to – or perhaps in addition to – identification of pass-fail metrics for selected
criteria in the UNIFI specification, GFM specifications can define test-based pass-fail metrics.
For example, various grid operators starting with Hawaiian Electric have applied simulation-
based tests to determine whether an IBR plant is or is not GFM [37]. Part of HECO’s test, which
is targeted at GFM BESS, was incorporated into NERC’s GFM specification [9], versions of
which are now in use or proposed by AEMO, ERCOT, MISO, and others. The core
HECO/NERC GFM test takes the relatively simple approach of connecting the inverter (or plant)
to a simulated voltage source with a load and then tripping the voltage source; if the inverter
maintains the load voltage during and after the trip of the voltage source, it is considered GFM. 5
Other tests have been proposed that quantify the inertia of the GFM source, test its ability to
operate in very weak grid conditions, test its response to frequency and phase angle changes, or
perform a scan of the inverter’s impedance at a range of frequencies [38]. Some of these tests
correspond to specific requirements in the UNIFI GFM specification and hence could inform a
future UNIFI specification.

An additional consideration for UNIFI to clarify is whether the GFM requirements apply at the
individual inverter level (i.e., the IBR unit) or at the IBR plant level. This should be made clear
in the UNIFI specification.

The tests recommended in Table 11 are intended to be run in EMT simulation. It is possible but
not easy to run similar tests on MW-scale hardware inverters, and very difficult to run such tests
on full IBR plants. Currently most GFM specifications use EMT simulation as the primary
means of GFM performance verification. It is recommended that the UNIFI specification take a
similar approach, and also state that the EMT model of the IBR unit should be validated against
hardware tests, for example as specified in IEEE P2800.2, the draft conformity assessment
document for IEEE 2800.

7 Current Status of KIUC System and Lessons
  Learned
The two GFM IBRs on Kauai form a vital part of KIUC’s daily generation mix. They and the
other resources on the island allow KIUC to operate with up to 100% of energy coming from
renewable sources for several hours at a time.

KIUC has considered converting IBR2 to GFM mode but would like to see improved ride-
through performance before making that change. The UNIFI consortium is also investigating a
low-magnitude oscillation that occurred briefly between IBR1 and IBR4. This event does not
appear to raise system stability concerns at this point, and KIUC plans to keep both IBR1 and
IBR4 in GFM mode.

KIUC currently plans to add two more GFM BESSes with 35 MW and 43 MW power capacity
and four hours of energy storage capacity, pending final approvals. They also plan to convert an
inefficient steam turbine generator to a dedicated synchronous condenser of 12.5 MVA. The
smaller synchronous condenser will be operated continuously, with Plant A’s larger synchronous
condenser capability acting as a back-up.

DFR data has proven extremely valuable for understanding IBR performance and diagnosing and
troubleshooting issues. Both the 60 Hz “slow” data, which is recorded continuously, and the
waveform-level data, which is recorded for brief intervals when triggered by certain events, are
of high value. However, the team has found various issues with data accuracy, scaling,
synchronization, and calculation of derived quantities like frequency. Some events that were first
believed to be examples of IBR misoperation turned out to be simply bad data; this situation can
be quite difficult to diagnose and requires collaboration with the DFR hardware or software
vendor to troubleshoot.

In summary, this report describes several significant events and milestones, including:

 • The first transmission-connected GFM IBR in the U.S., and one of the first in the world
 • The first known example of two GFM IBRs operating on the same transmission system,
 at least in the U.S. and possibly in the world. Notably, the two GFM plants use inverters
 from different manufacturers with very different control schemes.
 • The first known example of a GFM IBR in the U.S. supporting a microgrid containing
 multiple transmission nodes
 • An example of a mix of GFM and GFL IBRs sustaining a power system following the
 sudden loss of 60% of the generation
 • A GFL IBR-driven subsynchronous oscillation encompassing an entire power system
 • The application of a GFM IBR to mitigate the oscillation mentioned above.
Of course, Kauai is a relatively small power system (around 82 MW peak load), with a
transmission voltage of 57 kV (meshed), which is quite low compared to major continental

 50

power systems. Nevertheless, the lessons learned there, combined with the lessons being learned
in the other Hawaiian Islands, Australia, Great Britain, and elsewhere indicate that as GFM IBRs
designed for operation in parallel with bulk power systems overcome the challenges that
inevitably face new technology, they are likely to become key components in power grids around
the world. Indeed, we now see several mainland U.S. grid operators moving towards deploying
GFM IBRs as a way of future proofing the stability of their systems against changing resource
mixes. We hope that this report proves useful to those considering deploying GFM IBRs.
