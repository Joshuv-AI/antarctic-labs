Resilience Hub?

What does it mean to be a resilience hub?

Why are communities interested in these hubs?

 Definitions: Resilience

NLR defines resilience as a system’s ability to anticipate, prepare for, and adapt to changing conditions and
withstand, respond to, and recover rapidly from disruptions through adaptable and holistic planning and
technical solutions.
Many definitions of resilience exist, a sample of which is shown below—but most focus on ability to respond to a disaster
or hazard. A community should select the definition that applies to their specific culture, location, and needs.
• “The ability to withstand disasters better, respond and recover more quickly, and excel under new conditions” (source:
 National Governors Association).
• The ability of people and their communities to anticipate, accommodate, and positively adapt to or thrive amidst
 changing conditions and hazard events (source: Urban Sustainability Directors Network [USDN]).
• “The ability of systems to withstand and continue to perform after damage or loss of infrastructure” (source: Federal
 Emergency Management Agency [FEMA]).

 Definitions: Microgrid

Microgrid: “A group of interconnected loads and
distributed energy resources within clearly defined
electrical boundaries that acts as a single
controllable entity with respect to the grid. A
microgrid can connect and disconnect from the grid
to enable it to operate in both grid-connected or
island mode.”

(Electrical) Islanding: “Islanding consists in
splitting the electricity network into fragments that
are able to self-sustain their internal power
demand. It should preserve sensible loads from
outages and guarantee the continuity in the
electricity supply, when a high amount of distributed
generation occurs.”

 Definitions: Resilience Hub

 Definition #1: NLR
 “Resilience hubs are a collection of services and resources
 that support communities before, during, or after a disaster or
 disruptive event. They improve resilience by providing
 critical resources which may include electricity, shelter,
 fresh water, food, programming, medical services, a hub
 for emergency responders, and other supplies needed in
 an emergency and afterward during the recovery process.”

Photo by Paul Norton, NLR 11999

 The Why: “Resilience Hubs can help reduce stress on systems and infrastructure such as public safety,
 hospitals, and transportation while increasing community adaptive capacity. Resilience Hubs can become
 community cornerstones where neighbors come together to better understand one another, cooperate
 toward common goals, and bolster the health of their shared community.”
Source: USDN (n.d.); Kramer, Belding, and Coney (2023)

Operation and Development

For resilience hubs focused on adding
backup power, what are the phases of project
development?

Resilience Hub Energy Project Development

 Phase Phase Phase Phase Phase
 1 2 3 4 5

 Developing a resilience hub involves five major phases. Although the steps may overlap or cycle back, this
 framework outlines a general path from ideas and goal-setting to operation. We will focus on Phases 1 and 2.

Note: These phases were developed from the unpublished work of Ryan Burg and Chrissy Scarpitti within the DOE’s Energy to Communities (E2C) program.

                                                                                                                                                       National Laboratory of the Rockies   |   38

Resilience Hub Energy Project Development

 1. Build a team. Bring together local leaders, city and county emergency management, utility contacts,
 and community partners. You can’t do it alone.

 2. Understand risks and needs. What disasters affect your area (e.g., storms, heat waves)? What
 services are most critical during those times (e.g., heat, charging phones, storing medicine)? What
 services can you reasonably expect to provide?

 3. Select a site. Choose a location that’s central, trusted, and accessible (e.g., school, recreation center,
 library). Consider the primary and secondary uses of a site during both blue and black sky conditions.

 4. Inspect the building. Check the roof, electric panel, and heating, ventilation, and air conditioning
 (HVAC) systems—are they in good condition and ready to support any new energy systems under
 consideration?

 5. Gather cost ideas. You can start by getting informal quotes or talking to groups who’ve done similar
 projects to understand approximate costs. Begin to consider long-term maintenance and operations
 costs.

 Phase 1 goals: Align vision, understand needs, and assess site readiness.

                                                                                                  National Laboratory of the Rockies   |   39

Resilience Hub Energy Project Development

 1. Collect information. Determine the hub’s capabilities and services (see Slides 35-37). Assess the
 building’s load considering any additions required by the resilience hub’s new functions (e.g., additional
 occupancy, cooking). Consider whether power generation will be needed to support planned services.

 2. Size the system. Experts will estimate the power requirements of the backup energy system to keep the
 hub running during an outage.

 3. Understand the cost. This includes the upfront equipment, installation, and long-term maintenance as
 well as how much could be offset by energy savings.

 4. Ownership and operations. Who will own the equipment? Who will maintain it? Local government, a
 nonprofit, or a third-party provider? For which scenarios will the hub be activated, and for how long?

 5. Engage the utility. Utility interconnection information and procedures are crucial to project success.

 6. Start paperwork. Begin gathering letters of support and early agreements if you’re applying for funding or
 forming partnerships.

 Phase 2 goals: Determine if the project is technically and financially realistic.
