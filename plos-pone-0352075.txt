Harmonizing the National Lung Screening Trial: Kernel resolution and field of view compensation

Aravind R Krishnan, Michael E Kim, Chenyu Gao, Gaurav Rudravaram, Trent Schwartz, Andre T S Hucke, Lucas W Remedios, Thomas Z Li

ABSTRACT
Low-dose chest computed tomography scans acquired in multi-centre studies exhibit heterogeneity in the form of varying spatial resolution, reconstruction kernel and semantic field-of-view that hinder the reproducibility of quantitative measures and generalizability of downstream analyses. The National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) provides a large, open collection of low-dose chest CT scans characterized by heterogeneous spatial resolution, reconstruction kernels and field-of-view configurations. This diversity presents an opportunity to standardize the data, enabling reproducible biomarker estimation and broader reuse of the NLST as a homogeneous quantitative imaging resource. In this work, we present a standardization pipeline that was applied to 105,191 scans from 23,669 participants in the NLST. The pipeline addresses the following elements: (1) Resampling through-axial-plane (z) spatial resolution to 1.0 mm (2) Harmonization of reconstruction kernels across intra-vendor (paired) and inter-vendor (unpaired) settings using an anatomy guided multipath cycleGAN where scans were mapped to a reference Siemens B30f (soft) and Siemens B50f (hard) kernel (3) Semantic field-of-view extension across all scans. Quantitative consistency across percent emphysema and body composition assessment (skeletal muscle and subcutaneous adipose tissue volumes) was evaluated in paired, cross-sectional and longitudinal settings. For paired data, harmonization improved agreement of quantitative measures relative to the soft kernel reference. Cross-sectional analyses demonstrated reduced inter-kernel variability, with small to medium effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 0.2 to 0.5) for percent emphysema and improved correlations with anthropometric measures post field-of-view extension. Longitudinal analysis using linear mixed effect models, showed reduced kernel bias and improved longitudinal consistency of biomarkers reflected by the increase in intraclass correlation coefficients. This work provides a harmonized NLST imaging resource that enables consistent and reliable quantitative biomarker estimation for lung cancer screening, with open source code released at https://github.com/MASILab/Harmonized_NLST_dataset and the harmonized dataset to be made publicly available once data return to the NLST is completed.

1 Introduction Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality and low-dose chest computed tomography (LDCT) has emerged as an effective screening tool for high-risk populations by enabling early detection of lung cancer nodules [ 1 , 2 ]. The National Lung Screening Trial (NLST), a landmark randomized controlled trial of 53,454 participants, assigned individuals to undergo three annual screenings with either LDCT or chest radiography, demonstrating a 20% decrease in lung cancer mortality in the LDCT group as compared to radiography [ 3 , 4 ]. Following its completion, the NLST imaging dataset was made publicly available [ 5 ], providing a large-scale longitudinal LDCT cohort that has substantially advanced lung cancer risk modelling and quantitative imaging research. Researchers have leveraged the NLST to develop prediction models for future lung cancer risk from a single timepoint CT scan [ 6 ], longitudinal chest CTs [ 7 ] and a combination of imaging and clinical records [ 8 , 9 ]. Furthermore, the NLST has enabled quantitative imaging studies to study associations between lung cancer risk and imaging biomarkers that include emphysema [ 10 ], coronary artery calcification [ 11 ] and body composition [ 12 ]. Despite its transformative impact and widespread adoption in quantitative imaging research, the full potential of the NLST dataset has yet to be realized. A major but often overlooked challenge is the substantial heterogeneity in image acquisition protocols across the cohort. The NLST comprises LDCT scans acquired at 33 institutions using 23 distinct scanner models and 41,019 unique acquisition parameter combinations, as encoded by the DICOM Series Description. Consequently, the dataset exhibits considerable variability in through-plane spatial resolution (slice thickness), reconstruction kernels, and field of view (FOV) ( Fig 1 ). Such acquisition variability directly affects quantitative imaging measurements and limits reproducibility across studies. Differences in slice thickness introduce partial volume effects, with thicker slices showing increased spatial averaging [ 13 ]. These effects have been shown to impact CT-derived radiomic features of lung nodules [ 14 , 15 ] and attenuation based measures that include percent emphysema [ 16 ]. Similarly, reconstruction kernels influence image texture, noise characteristics and edge definition [ 17 ], affecting emphysema [ 18 ] and body composition measures [ 19 ]. 10.1371/journal.pone.0352075.g001 Fig 1 Acquisition heterogeneity in scans obtained from different vendors in the NLST. The National Lung Screening Trial houses heterogeneous low-dose chest CT scans acquired from multiple institutions and vendors. Image variability arises from differences in through-plane spatial resolution, reconstruction kernels and semantic field-of-view. Reconstruction kernels influence the pixel noise and image texture in the underlying anatomy, creating differences in quantitative biomarker measures. In addition, scanner specific field-of-view configurations may truncate anatomy, affecting body composition assessment. These sources of heterogeneity motivate the need for a standardized dataset that enables consistent and reproducible quantitative image measures. Lung cancer screening protocols often restrict the field of view, resulting in scanner dependent truncation patterns [ 20 ] that obscure skeletal muscle and subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT). Such truncation leads to incomplete body composition quantification and limits the value of LDCTs in opportunistic comprehensive assessment [ 20 ]. Prior studies have demonstrated that harmonization strategies can improve the robustness and reproducibility of quantitative imaging biomarkers across heterogeneous CT acquisitions [ 21 ], while FOV standardization or extension [ 20 ] approaches can recover anatomically consistent representations for downstream analysis. Together, these observations highlight the need for systematic harmonization of acquisition-related variability to ensure consistent and reproducible quantitative biomarkers across the NLST. While variability in slice thickness, reconstruction kernels and FOV can be addressed individually through resampling, kernel harmonization [ 21 – 23 ] and semantic FOV extension [ 20 ] respectively, comprehensive standardization of the NLST requires their integration into a unified framework. To address this need, we propose a multi-stage standardization pipeline that performs through-plane resampling, multi-domain reconstruction kernel harmonization and semantic FOV extension across all scans and timepoints in the NLST. Through plane resampling standardizes images to a slice thickness of 1 mm, mitigating slice thickness variability across scans, which otherwise introduces variability in quantitative measures. Following resampling, nine representative kernels obtained from three distinct scanners in the NLST are harmonized using an anatomically guided multipath cycleGAN model [ 23 ], addressing intra-scanner and inter-scanner harmonization. For intra-scanner pairs with a one-to-one correspondence, hard kernels are mapped to their corresponding soft kernel, while inter-scanner harmonization translates all kernels to reference styles defined by Siemens B30f (soft kernel) and Siemens B50f (hard kernel). We choose the Siemens kernels as references for inter scanner harmonization due to their impact on emphysema quantification in lung CT [ 16 , 24 ]. Harmonized images subsequently undergo semantic FOV extension [ 25 ] to restore truncated skeletal muscle and SAT, enabling complete body composition assessment. We evaluate the effectiveness of standardization using percent emphysema and body composition measures, derived from anatomical labels generated by TotalSegmentator [ 26 ]. Performance is assessed at three levels: a) intra-scanner paired analysis b) cross-sectional comparisons at each timepoint and c) longitudinal evaluation of biomarker stability within subjects. The resulting standardized NLST dataset enables consistent and reproducible quantitative image analysis across multiple scanners and timepoints, thereby expanding its utility for the research community. 2 Materials and methods 2.1 NLST population The NLST is a prospective randomized control screening trial where participants were randomized to obtain a low-dose chest CT (LDCT) or a chest radiograph [ 3 ]. Participants included in this study were between the ages of 55 and 74 years, with a smoking history of 30 packs years (current smokers) or had quit within the last 15 years (former smokers). Participants were screened at three different timepoints (T0, T1 and T2) at one year intervals with the first screening performed at timepoint T0, immediately after patient randomization. Subjects diagnosed with lung cancer were not offered follow up scans. The primary endpoint of the NLST was lung cancer mortality. 2.2 Data curation for standardization We standardize the CT arm of the NLST (dataset version: 2011.02.03/04.07.22, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT00047385 ) comprising of LDCT scans reconstructed with different acquisition parameters from multiple vendors. We obtained all data upon request with a data use agreement ( https://cdas.cancer.gov/learn/nlst/images/ ). All DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) scans are converted to NIfTI (Neuroimaging Informatics Technology Initiative) using the dcm2niix tool (version 1.0.2) [ 27 ], leading to 25206 participants with 195373 scans. The dataset consists of scans with variable slice thickness, slice spacing and 39 distinct reconstruction kernels from four different vendors. We apply an exclusion criteria as a first round of filtering ( Fig 2 ) that excludes images with missing kernel information, scans from the Toshiba vendor, non-standard image orientations, excessive slice thickness (≥ 4 mm) or slice spacing (≥ 4 mm) in the axial plane, four dimensional CT volumes and underrepresented kernels at baseline. Scans reconstructed from the Toshiba vendor exhibit a different noise texture when compared to Siemens, GE and Philips [ 28 ]. This makes it particularly challenging to harmonize Toshiba kernels to a common reference [ 29 ], resulting in their exclusion. We defined a reconstruction kernel as underrepresented if the number of scans reconstructed with said kernel is less than one hundred at baseline, as baseline contained the greatest diversity of kernels. We provide a list of excluded kernels across all timepoints in the supplementary material ( Supplementary table ST1 in S1 File ). After applying the filtering criteria, 105,191 scans from 23669 participants remained. These scans were reconstructed using the following nine representative kernels from three vendors: Siemens (B50f, B30f, B80f), GE (BONE, LUNG, STANDARD) and Philips (B,C,D). 10.1371/journal.pone.0352075.g002 Fig 2 Inclusion and exclusion criteria of images in the NLST. The inclusion criteria for the NLST dataset standardization begins with 25206 subjects having 195373 scans. Scans were excluded based on metadata information, reconstruction characteristics, image orientation, spatial resolution, slice thickness, dimensionality and manual quality assurance. A total of 23669 subjects with 105,191 scans were available for analysis across three different timepoints, reconstructed using nine representative kernels from Siemens, GE and Philips vendors. We conducted a second round of quality assurance through manual visual assessment to ensure that scans represented a chest CT. We used the quality control tool developed by Kim et al.[ 30 ] to review triplanar views of each scan, classifying them as “yes”, “no” or “maybe” based on image quality. 1803 scans that were labelled as no or maybe were excluded. Common reasons for exclusion included incomplete CT volumes with missing slices, incomplete lung coverage in the display field of view (missing apical or basal lung regions), motion artifacts, truncation artifacts producing blank or corrupted slices, shadow artifacts in dual phase acquisitions, stair step artifacts and reconstruction failures. Rare cases of exclusion involved cupping artifacts, irregular lung anatomy, metal artifacts and image distortions (ruptured breast implants) ( S1 Fig ). 2.3 NLST standardization pipeline To reduce acquisition related heterogeneity in the NLST, we implemented a multi-stage standardization pipeline comprising of spatial resampling in the through-plane, reconstruction kernel harmonization using an anatomy guided multipath cycleGAN and semantic field of view extension to impute missing skeletal muscle and SAT ( Fig 3 ). These stages address variability in through-axial-plane resolution, reconstruction kernels and truncated FOVs that impact quantitative measurements. We evaluate the efficacy of standardization using biomarker measurements derived from the images that include percent emphysema and body composition measures derived from skeletal muscle and SAT. 10.1371/journal.pone.0352075.g003 Fig 3 Proposed standardization pipeline to address acquisition heterogeneity. Heterogeneous NLST scans are resampled to a through-plane resolution of 1.0 mm while preserving the in-plane resolution. Reconstruction kernels are harmonized using a multipath cycleGAN with a shared latent space “L”, enabling multi-domain harmonization within and across scanners. The harmonized images undergo semantic field of view extension, where muscle and subcutaneous adipose tissue are out painted beyond truncation. Anatomical masks are subsequently derived using TotalSegmentator, which are used for downstream analysis to obtain percent emphysema and body composition measures. 2.3.1 Spatial resampling and image preprocessing. The through-plane spatial resolution of the NLST scans varied across images and can introduce partial volume effects that impact downstream analysis. Therefore, we resampled all images to have a through-axial-plane resolution of 1.0 mm using cubic spline interpolation implemented in nibabel (version 5.3.2). The native in-plane resolution was preserved across all images. The in-plane display field-of-view (DFOV) differed across images obtained from Siemens, GE and Philips vendors. The mismatch in DFOV during cross vendor harmonization can result in artifacts beyond the FOV extent in images with circular DFOVs [ 31 ]. Therefore, we standardized the DFOV to be circular across all the scans before training the harmonization model [ 21 ]. All images were clipped to a range of [−1024, 3072] Hounsfield units (HU) to ensure consistent intensities [ 22 ]. 2.3.2 Training data for kernel harmonization. Within a given vendor in the NLST, subjects can have an image reconstructed with a hard kernel and a corresponding soft kernel, with a one-to-one correspondence existing between them. Such image pairs are “paired” kernels, obtained from the same projection data. We obtain kernel pairs by matching scanner acquisition parameters that include peak kilovoltage, reconstruction diameter, exposure time, x-ray tube current, exposure and spiral pitch factor. All pairs with mismatched meta data were excluded. Although scans were reconstructed from the same projection data, scan pairs exhibit subtle positional differences within the FOV. Therefore, we conducted a two-step quality control process involving: 1) manual inspection of the difference image to ensure alignment and 2) quantitative evaluation using root mean squared error (RMSE). We obtain a total of six different reconstruction kernel pairs at each timepoint: Siemens (B30f, B50f), Siemens (B30f, B80f), GE (BONE, STANDARD), GE (LUNG, STANDARD), Philips (B, D), and Philips (C, D). A detailed list of the number of pairs at each timepoint is provided in Table 1 . While kernels from the same vendor enabled paired kernel harmonization, all other possible combinations of kernels across vendors served as data for cross vendor harmonization. We only considered the pairs at timepoint T0 to train our kernel harmonization model, where the data was split into 70% for training, 15% for validation and 15% for testing provided (see Table 2 for sample size splits). Recent advances in multipath cycleGAN architectures enable multi-domain kernel harmonization within a unified framework where a shared latent representation captures anatomy and style [ 21 ]. Unlike conventional cycleGAN models that require independent networks for each kernel combination, a multipath cycleGAN architecture scales efficiently in a multi-vendor lung screening dataset like the NLST, consisting of nine reconstruction kernels from three different vendors. We adapted an anatomically guided multipath cycleGAN [ 23 ] for multi-vendor kernel harmonization across paired and unpaired reconstruction kernels in the NLST. The model consists of domain specific encoders and decoders connected through a shared latent space, enabling translation between any source and target kernel. We incorporate anatomical guidance using segmentation masks derived from TotalSegmentator [ 26 ] (version 2.8.0) that capture all available regions of interest in the image. This entails a tissue statistic loss that penalizes differences in the mean Hounsfield units between the source image and harmonized image to prevent inversion of tissue intensity in any given path. Previously, the anatomy guided multipath cycleGAN was trained on four reconstruction kernels from the NLST [ 23 ]. We retrained this model to include all nine representative kernels by adopting a two-stage training approach to facilitate multi-vendor kernel harmonization. In stage one, Siemens and GE kernels were trained jointly using domain specific encoder-decoders and discriminators. In stage two, Philips kernels were introduced by freezing stage one weights and training additional networks to prevent catastrophic forgetting, allowing for domain adaptation ( S2 Fig ). We implemented the model in PyTorch (version 2.5.1) with CUDA 12.4. We train our model on axial slices of size 512 × 512 pixels that are normalized to [−1,1]. Stage one was trained for 200 epochs on an NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada Generation GPU (48GB RAM) using a batch size of one, Adam optimizer [ 32 ] and a learning rate of 2 × 10 −4 that remains constant for 100 epochs before linearly decaying by a rate of 1.98 × 10 −6 . We trained stage one using 5000 randomly sampled slices from each domain, owing to the large dataset across multiple domains. In stage two, training across all datasets is computationally expensive owing to the number of image slices across all domains in addition to the encoder-decoder and discriminator architectures. Therefore, stage two was trained using the same configuration with 2000 randomly sampled slices from each domain. Each path in the network was governed by a least squares GAN (LSGAN) [ 33 ] loss function, a cycle consistency loss (mean absolute error (MAE) weighted by a factor of 10) and the tissue statistic loss (mean squared error (MSE) weighted by a factor of 1). In each path, forward translation maps the source kernel to the target kernel while backward translation maps the target kernel to the source kernel. During inference, paired kernels were harmonized to the subject matched soft kernel within each vendor. At each cross sectional timepoint, all kernels were harmonized to the style of Siemens B30f (soft kernel) and Siemens B50f (hard kernel), yielding two standardized outputs per scan. All implementation details about model training, evaluation and analysis are provided at https://github.com/MASILab/Harmonized_NLST_dataset . 2.3.4 Semantic field of view extension. Thoracic CT scans in the NLST enable opportunistic body composition assessment of skeletal muscle and SAT. However, variable truncation patterns limit the FOV leading to underestimation of body composition measurements. Therefore, we perform semantic field of view extension using the algorithm developed by Xu et al.[ 20 ]. The algorithm follows a two-stage framework: stage one focuses on predicting a bounding box that estimates the extent of the complete body while stage two imputes the truncated anatomical regions using the truncated input image and the corresponding FOV mask, treating semantic FOV extension as an out painting task. We apply FOV extension slice-wise to all the images post kernel harmonization and stack all slices to obtain the semantically extended volume. 2.3.5 Downstream analysis. Using TotalSegmentator (version 2.8.0), we derive segmentation labels on the semantically extended images. All default labels were obtained, followed by skeletal muscle and SAT segmentations using the tissue types model. We compute percent emphysema using the lung segmentation masks from TotalSegmentator. Emphysema can be characterized by regions in the lung that have a radio density range from −900 to −1024 HU [ 34 ]. Therefore, emphysema was quantified as the percentage of voxels with attenuation less than −950 HU. Skeletal muscle and SAT volumes were computed by multiplying the voxel counts with the voxel volume obtained from the image header. 2.4 Evaluation and statistical analysis We evaluate the performance of our standardization pipeline across three different settings: a) paired reconstruction kernels at each timepoint b) cross sectional cohorts across all kernels at each timepoint and c) longitudinal scans acquired from the same subject over time. For paired reconstruction kernels, we assess agreement in percent emphysema, skeletal muscle and body composition assessment before and after harmonization. We report the root mean square error (RMSE) that quantifies agreement along with the 95% confidence intervals while Bland Altman [ 35 ] style plots are used to visualize the agreement between measures. Differences in measurements were computed as hard kernel minus soft kernel for all pairs. 95% confidence intervals were computed through bootstrapping with replacement using 1000 resamples. Body composition assessment was performed post FOV extension. At the image level, we evaluate image similarity metrics using RMSE, peak signal to noise ratio (PSNR) and structural similarity index (SSIM) for intensity windows corresponding to lung ([−1000,0] HU), muscle ([−29, 150] HU) and SAT ([−190, −30]). Two-sample, two-sided, Wilcoxon signed ranked tests were used to determine significant changes in distributions of measures before and after harmonization. To account for multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction ( p < 0.05). For cross sectional analysis, at each timepoint, we matched cohorts on age, sex and smoking status using exact matching to reduce demographic confounding. To ensure balanced comparison across kernels, we used the kernel with the lowest sample size for matching at a given timepoint. Using the matched cohorts, we quantify the effect size between the emphysema distributions of the reference kernel and each source kernel before and after harmonization using Cohen’s d [ 36 ]. Effect sizes are small when d = 0.2, medium when d = 0.5 and large when d ≥ 0.8. A positive d indicates that group one had a higher mean compared to group two while a negative d indicates the opposite. The reference kernels for harmonization were Siemens B50f and B30f. For body composition analysis, we use the data from all kernels across all timepoints, computing the SAT ratio before and after FOV extension as the ratio of SAT volume to the combined skeletal muscle and SAT volume. The agreement between truncated and FOV extended measurements were evaluated using Bland-Altman analysis of SAT ratios to quantify the systematic increases associated with the recovery of missing tissue. Furthermore, we evaluate body composition assessment by examining the association between SAT and skeletal muscle indices (cm 3 /m 2 ) and their corresponding anthropometric measures (fat mass for SAT and fat free mass for skeletal muscle) following Xu et al [ 20 ]. We reported the spearman rank correlation and 95% confidence intervals computed through bootstrapping with 1000 resamples (with replacement). At timepoints T1 and T2, we used the approximate anthropometric measures obtained at baseline for correlation analysis. However, we see a difference in texture between the imputed and existing regions. 4 Discussion and conclusion In this work, we deployed and validated a multi-stage standardization pipeline for low-dose lung cancer screening scans from the NLST. The curated dataset consists of scans reconstructed from nine reconstruction kernels across three vendors, exhibiting variability in through-plane resolution and field of view. Our pipeline standardizes through plane resolution followed by multi-domain kernel harmonization using an anatomically guided multipath cycleGAN, enabling intra-vendor and inter-vendor kernel harmonization within a unified model. Subsequently, harmonized images undergo semantic FOV extension, enabling comprehensive body composition assessment of skeletal muscle and SAT. We evaluated harmonization and FOV extension at paired, cross sectional and longitudinal levels using percent emphysema and body composition measures. The proposed pipeline reduced variability in emphysema and body composition measures at the intra-vendor and inter-vendor levels. Longitudinally, emphysema and body composition measures showed stability as evidenced by improved ICC driven by a reduction in residual variance and increase in between subject variability. We investigated the efficacy of our proposed standardization pipeline across intra-vendor, inter-vendor and longitudinal data settings in the NLST. At the intra-vendor setting, subjects have scans reconstructed with a hard kernel and corresponding soft kernel, where a one-to-one pixel correspondence exists. However, hard kernels amplify the voxel noise relative to soft kernels. This is observed from the difference in the texture of the underlying anatomy, resulting in discrepancies in image similarity metrics within the lung, skeletal muscle and SAT regions. Harmonization to the reference soft kernel enforces consistent textures in the underlying anatomy, resulting in consistent improvements in RMSE, PSNR and SSIM. The performance of our proposed approach was compared to a histogram matching baseline to address the model complexity. Histogram matching corrects intensity differences by directly mapping one image intensity distribution onto the other. On the same kernel pairs from Siemens and GE vendors, our approach outperforms the baseline owing to the shared latent space that can learn a common representation across images followed by the adversarial nature of training that pushes the synthetic images close to the target than a distribution level correction. On the Philips pairs, histogram matching does better on RMSE and PSNR compared to our approach. This could be attributed to the complexity of the two-stage approach that focuses on adapting additional kernels to existing ones. However, our approach preserves structural detail as evidenced from the SSIM measures for lung and SAT regions. Additionally, differences in textures have implications on quantitative image measures. Hard kernels are known to overestimate emphysema compared to soft kernels [ 39 ] as a result of the amplified voxel noise. Harmonization mitigates these differences, enabling consistent and reproducible measures across all kernel pairs and timepoints. For body composition measures, differences in skeletal muscle and SAT volumes suggest inconsistencies attributed to kernel, with notable underestimation of SAT volumes. Harmonization mitigates discrepancies in body composition measures, ensuring consistency across all pairs. Overall, these findings are consistent with prior studies that investigate paired kernel harmonization through emphysema and body composition measures [ 19 , 24 , 40 ], demonstrating the ability of our unified standardization pipeline to produce consistent and reproducible measures at the intra-vendor level. While paired intra-vendor analysis presents a reference soft kernel for each subject that verifies harmonization, cross sectional settings acquire scans across multiple subjects, vendors and reconstruction settings. Scans exhibit variability in spatial resolution, field of view and reconstruction kernel across vendors, leading to a lack of one-to-one correspondence between scans. The variability in reconstruction kernels impact percent emphysema quantification while truncation patterns hinder the ability to attain whole body composition measures. We therefore evaluated the proposed standardization pipeline across all cross sectional timepoints in the NLST, assessing the ability to obtain consistent percent emphysema measures and body composition measures for the whole extent of the body. Emphysema distributions vary across kernels with hard kernels showing overestimation compared to soft kernels. Harmonization to the reference soft and hard kernels mitigated bias in measures as evidenced from the small to medium effect sizes, suggesting improved consistency in measures. Field of view extension led to increased SAT ratios as evidenced from the positive mean difference on the Bland Altman plots, suggesting imputation of peripheral SAT. Furthermore, the improved correlations of muscle and SAT indices with anthropometric measures suggest that body composition measures are more consistent post FOV extension. The improvements in SAT and muscle correlations can be attributed to FOV extension rather than kernel harmonization, that has already been validated on a subset of images from the NLST by Xu et.al [ 20 ]. Particularly, the interpretations are consistent with larger gains observed for GE images, which exhibited more pronounced FOV truncation in unharmonized images. Overall our results result are consistent with previous works [ 20 ] with FOV extension having also added value to lung cancer diagnosis [ 12 ]. Across longitudinal timepoints, subjects may acquire scans with different reconstruction kernels from different vendors that can obscure the true biological progression due to acquisition related variability. We assessed the impact of our proposed pipeline in the longitudinal setting across all scans and investigated the stability of biomarkers and preservation of within subject variability. On the unharmonized images, kernel bias dominated emphysema measures with hard kernels showing higher coefficients compared to the reference soft kernel with soft kernels underestimating these measures. The increase in magnitude in the timepoint beta coefficients suggested changes over time when compared to the baseline T0. Post harmonization to the reference B30f soft kernel, kernel coefficients reduced, suggesting the mitigation of bias in emphysema measures. Additionally, an increase in the between subject variance and decrease in the within subject variance lead to a higher ICC, suggesting that emphysema measures were more stable across time. While harmonization to the B50f kernel mitigated kernel bias and showed improvements in the between subject variance, the within subject variance increased. This can be attributed to the overestimation of emphysema when harmonized to a hard kernel. Therefore, it is advisable to choose a soft kernel reference for longitudinal analysis. In the case of body composition measures, SAT indices were underestimated due to truncation across longitudinal timepoints which is reflected in the kernel coefficients. Post harmonization to any reference, kernel coefficients are minimized followed by improvements in the ICC. These results suggest that SAT indices are dependent on the truncation pattern of the corresponding vendor and semantic extension promotes whole body composition assessment. We note that residual kernel associated differences remained after harmonization, particularly for Siemens B80f and GE LUNG. Their clinical significance is unknown since outcome specific thresholds for meaningful change over comparable longitudinal intervals were not evaluated. Furthermore, when reconstruction kernels differ across visits, residual measurement differences may affect estimates of longitudinal progression despite statistical adjustment for kernel. Further validation is needed to determine the influence of these residual effects on longitudinal analyses. Our work is not without its limitations. The NLST images were reconstructed using filtered back projection (FBP) which was the standard reconstruction method available during the trial. In contrast, modern LDCT increasingly uses iterative and deep learning reconstruction techniques, that can produce non-linear spatially varying noise characteristics that differ from those of FBP [ 41 , 42 ]. Since our harmonization framework learns transformations between the noise textures of kernels using FBP, the trained pipeline is specific to FBP reconstructed images, requiring additional fine tuning or retraining before application to images reconstructed with modern techniques. Extending the framework to accommodate these reconstruction techniques represents an important direction for future work. Although the final harmonized cohort remained broadly representative of the full NLST cohort, differences in the racial distribution of participants with Toshiba or underrepresented-kernel scans (Supplementary Table ST2 in S1 File ) may limit the generalizability of our findings to demographic groups and acquisition sites disproportionately associated with these excluded imaging conditions. For the images reconstructed from the Philips vendors, we occasionally notice hallucinations in the peripheral SAT regions for images harmonized to the reference Siemens B30f kernel ( S5 Fig ). This could be attributed to the instability in the stage two training of the multi-domain kernel harmonization model where the goal is to adapt reconstruction kernels from a new vendor to existing vendors. Similarly, performance for the (B, D) and (C, D) pairs from the Philips vendor fell short in comparison to the other vendors. The reduced sampling exposure, combined with the difficulty of aligning the Philips kernels to the existing representation may have contributed to the weaker performance. These limitations could be addressed through improved training strategies that involve the utilization of a unique kernel from each vendor during stage one training. We observed minor improvements in quantitative image measures at longitudinal timepoints for certain kernels as opposed to the baseline timepoint due to our training data consisting of images obtained at timepoint T0. Incorporating longitudinal data during training could benefit quantitative imaging at the cross sectional and longitudinal levels. We occasionally observed artifacts in the GE images harmonized to the Siemens B30f kernel in the peripheral muscle and SAT regions. However, these artifacts did not hamper the downstream body composition analysis. Furthermore, the NLST accounts for the weight of the subject during initial screening only. The correlations of the muscle and SAT indices at longitudinal timepoints were obtained using the baseline weight measurements serving as a proxy, which may not indicate the true biological correlation at timepoints T1 and T2. In conclusion, we developed a unified standardization pipeline to address heterogeneity in reconstruction kernels, axial spatial resolution and field of view in low-dose chest CT scans acquired in the NLST. By integrating through plane resampling, multi-domain kernel harmonization and semantic field of view extension, our approach mitigates acquisition variability, providing a consistent representation across diverse image settings. We demonstrate that the proposed pipeline reduces kernel and field of view heterogeneity in intra-vendor, cross sectional and longitudinal settings, enabling consistent and reproducible quantitative imaging measures. We aim to publicly release the harmonized NLST dataset following data return to the NLST, highlighting its broader impact for the lung cancer research community.
