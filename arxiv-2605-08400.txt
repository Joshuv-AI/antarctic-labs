On Observation Time for Recovering Latent Hawkes Networks
Jonas Linkerhägner, Michele Bortolasi, Lorenzo Baldassari, Maarten V. de Hoop, Ivan Dokmanić

Abstract
Dynamics of interacting systems in engineering, society, and nature often evolve over latent networks that govern which entities can interact. We study the problem of inferring these networks from event-based observations, which arise naturally in finance, seismology, and neuroscience. While there is substantial algorithmic work addressing this important problem, theoretical results are scarce. In this paper we ask the following fundamental question: what is the minimum time that one must observe the dynamics in order to exactly recover the underlying network, as a function of the number d d of interacting entities? For a class of stationary Hawkes processes with sparse, weak interactions, we prove that an observation time of order log ⁡ d \log d is sufficient and necessary. For the upper bound we construct a two-stage estimator that uses clipped and binned event data for screening, followed by a least-squares refinement, and apply concentration bounds derived from the Poisson cluster representation. For the lower bound we combine Fano’s inequality with Jacod’s Girsanov formula for point processes on a suitable subclass of networks.

Introduction

Reconstructing the latent interaction network is a central problem in the statistical analysis of point processes.
In this paper, we ask:

How must the observation time scale with the number of nodes
d
in order to recover the directed interaction network exactly?

We give an answer for Hawkes processes
achab2018uncovering
;
bacry2020sparse
;
embrechts2018hawkes
, when excitations of events occur over a sparse, weakly coupled interaction network.
Such processes model a variety of phenomena, including aftershock propagation after earthquakes
ogata1988statistical
;
zhuang2002stochastic
;
marsan2008extending
, high-frequency trading
bowsher2007modelling
;
large2007measuring
;
bacry2012non
, neuronal spike trains
truccolo2005point
;
pillow2008spatio
, social activity and information cascades
zhou2013learning
;
he2015hawkestopic
, and epidemic spreading
chiang2022hawkes
.

Suppose, for example, that we record earthquake occurrences in a seismic region.
The nodes of the underlying network could be subsurface elements such as fault segments or spatial cells; the edges may describe direct triggering influence, mediated, for instance, by stress transfer: that is, how a seismic event in one element modulates the probability that events will occur in other elements.
Recovering this directed network helps understand how seismic activity is coordinated and how triggering effects propagate
nandan2022are
;
kaya2026deep
.

Such interacting systems are high-dimensional but sparse: a seismic region may contain many fault segments, but each segment directly interacts only with a small number of nearby segments as dictated by the fault geometry.
This motivates studying how the observation time required for network reconstruction scales with the number of nodes
d
, when each node is directly influenced by fixed
k\ll d
others, and these direct influences are individually moderate.

Here we provide a theoretical analysis of the network recovery problem.
We prove that the observation time required for exact network recovery is of order
\log d
.
For the upper bound, we construct a two-stage estimator in Section
3
that uses clipped and binned event data for screening, followed by local least-squares regression and thresholding. Combined with concentration bounds derived from the Poisson cluster representation

, it shows that exact support recovery is possible with high probability when

T\gtrsim\log d.

In Section
4
, we prove a matching information-theoretic lower bound by applying Fano’s inequality

to an explicit subclass of interaction networks. This reduces exact recovery to controlling the mutual information between the latent network and the observations, which we bound via the corresponding Kullback–Leibler divergences using Jacod’s Girsanov formula for point processes
jacod1975multivariate
.
As a consequence, for all
d
large enough, any estimator fails to achieve exact support recovery with non-vanishing probability when the observation time is
T\lesssim\log d
.

, causal graph recovery

2
Model, assumptions, and main result

In this section, we introduce the Hawkes model under study, formulate the exact support-recovery problem, and specify the sparse, weak interaction setting in which our analysis takes place.

2.1
Exponential Hawkes process

We consider a
d
-dimensional exponential Hawkes process observed over a stationary time window
[0,T]
. Writing
N:=(N_{1},\dots,N_{d})
for the counting process, and using the exponential kernel with decay rate
\beta>0
, let

X_{j}(t):=\int_{(-\infty,t]}e^{-\beta(t-s)}\,dN_{j}(s),

denote the exponential shot-noise process associated with node
j
. Each coordinate evolves according to the SDE

dX_{j}(t)=-\beta X_{j}(t)\,dt+dN_{j}(t).

Thus, with the exponential kernel, the Hawkes process admits a Markov representation. The state variable used throughout the proofs is

X(t)=(X_{1}(t),\dots,X_{d}(t)).

The conditional intensity of node
i
is

\lambda_{i}(t)=\mu_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\theta_{ij}X_{j}(t-),\qquad i\in[d].

Here,
\mu_{i}>0
is the background intensity of node
i
and
[d]=\{1,\dots,d\}
.
X_{j}(t-)
denotes the left limit of the state just before time
t
, ensuring that the intensity is predictable. The matrix

\Theta:=(\theta_{ij})_{i,j\in[d]}

is the interaction matrix. We consider the excitatory case, so for all
i,j\in[d]
we have
\theta_{ij}\geq 0
.
This model is a standard choice for multivariate event data with self- and cross-excitation, where
\theta_{ij}>0
means that the activity at node
j
directly increases the event rate at node
i
through the current state
X_{j}(t-)
.
\theta_{ij}=0
means that there is no edge from
j
to
i
.
We do not consider inhibitory Hawkes processes in this work, since this would require additional constraints, or a nonlinear intensity function to ensure that the intensities remain nonnegative.

To focus narrowly on the task of interaction recovery, we treat the decay rate
\beta>0
as known throughout the paper. Also we take the observed data to be

Y_{T}=(X(0),N_{[0,T]}),

where
N_{[0,T]}
is the counting process restricted to the window
[0,T]
. For exponential Hawkes with known
\beta
,
X(0)
is the Markov state summarizing the past. The fact that
X(0)
is treated as observable is an initialization assumption rather than additional information. In practice,
X(0)
is available in stationary simulations after burn-in, and can be approximated from a warm-up window of pre-
0
event data.

2.2
Exact support-recovery

Our goal is exact network recovery from the observed data
Y_{T}
.
In practice this corresponds, for example, to inferring regions with a higher aftershock risk from observing earthquakes, identifying assets that transmit volatility shocks to other assets, or identifying epidemic spreading routes from observing infections.
Since
\theta_{ij}>0
corresponds to a directed edge from node
j
to node
i
, network recovery corresponds to support recovery of the interaction matrix
\Theta
.
For each row
i
, let

S_{i}:=\{j\in[d]:\theta_{ij}>0\}

denote the parent set of node
i
, that is, the set of source nodes that directly excite node
i
. We ask how the required observation time
T
must scale with the ambient dimension
d
to recover the full support pattern
(S_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
exactly with high probability.

2.3
Sparse weak-interaction model class

We now formalize the sparse, weak-interaction setting in which exact support recovery will be studied. We fix constants

\beta>0,\qquad 0<\mu_{-}\leq\mu_{+}<\infty,\qquad 0<w_{-}\leq w_{+}<\infty,

an integer
k\geq 1
, and a coupling level
\alpha>0
.

Sparse network structure.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{d,k}
denote the class of nonnegative matrices
B=(b_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d\times d}
such that

•

each row of
B
has at most
k
nonzero entries:

\|b_{i\cdot}\|_{0}\leq k,\qquad i\in[d];

•

every nonzero entry of
B
lies in
[w_{-},w_{+}]
:

b_{ij}\neq 0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad b_{ij}\in[w_{-},w_{+}].

\mathcal{B}_{d,k}
specifies the structural part of the model class: each node has at most
k
direct parents, and every nonzero edge weight is bounded above and below.

Hawkes parameters.

We then consider Hawkes models whose interaction matrix is of the form

\Theta=\alpha B,\qquad B\in\mathcal{B}_{d,k},

and whose background intensities satisfy

\mu_{i}\in[\mu_{-},\mu_{+}],\qquad i\in[d].

In addition, we define

\theta_{-}:=\alpha w_{-},\qquad\theta_{+}:=\alpha w_{+},\qquad\gamma:=\frac{k\theta_{+}}{\beta},

where
\gamma
is a uniform upper bound on the total excitation that
one row can receive, measured relative to the exponential decay rate
\beta
.
Indeed, since each row has at most
k
nonzero entries and each interaction weight is at most
\theta_{+}
,

\frac{1}{\beta}\rho(\Theta)\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\|\Theta\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{k\theta_{+}}{\beta}=\gamma.

We impose the subcriticality condition
\gamma<1
, a sufficient form of the standard Hawkes stability condition
hawkes1971spectra
. It guarantees existence of a unique stationary law and excludes explosive excitation cascades.
Together, this yields the class of Hawkes parameters studied in the paper:

\mathcal{G}_{d,k}:=\Bigl\{(\mu,\Theta):\Theta=\alpha B,\ B\in\mathcal{B}_{d,k},\ \mu_{i}\in[\mu_{-},\mu_{+}]\ \forall i,\ \gamma<1\Bigr\}.

The weak-interaction setting captures large sparse systems where individual interactions are weak, such as neuronal networks, social activity cascades, and epidemic propagation. Since excitation is sufficiently moderate in these cases, the contribution of a true parent remains detectable, while higher-order cascade effects remain controlled. This is precisely the regime in which one can hope to prove sample complexity bounds by combining support separation with concentration uniformly in
d
.

Finally, the smallest possible nonzero interaction strength in this class has to fulfill
\theta_{-}>0
.
This is the minimum-signal condition needed when
d
grows: without a positive lower bound on nonzero edges, true interactions could become arbitrarily weak, and exact support recovery becomes impossible.

2.4
Main result

The family
\mathcal{G}_{d,k}
models large sparse systems of weakly interacting event processes.
In this regime, our main result identifies the correct scaling of the observation time with dimension
d
for exact network recovery: for fixed
k
and fixed coupling level
\alpha
, recovery is possible if and only if

T\asymp\log d.

To prove this, we combine an achievability result in Theorem
3.1
with an impossibility result in Theorem
4.1
, provided in Sections
3
and
4
, respectively.

3
Upper bound on the observation time

In this section, we prove the upper bound by constructing an explicit estimator.
Figure
2
summarizes the procedure.
Since exact support recovery is equivalent to identifying, for each node
i
, the set of nodes with a direct influence on it, the estimator is built node by node. Instead of attempting to recover the full parent set in one step, the estimator proceeds in two stages.

In the screening stage, the estimator asks which nodes are active shortly before node
i
has an event.
This is measured by the empirical covariance score between the current activity of a candidate node
j
and the occurrence of a future event of node
i
.
The activity is reconstructed from the observed event counts, sampled at time resolution
h
, and clipped at level
R
.
The
m
nodes with the largest scores form a candidate list of possible parents.
In the weak-interaction regime, these scores are shown to separate true parents from non-parents, ensuring that the true support is contained in the candidate set (sure screening).

In the second stage, the estimator reevaluates this reduced set so as to separate genuine direct influences from multi-hop correlations.
It fits a local ordinary least-squares regression on the selected candidates and thresholds the estimated coefficients.
Row sparsity keeps this low-dimensional, while clipping and binning allow the relevant empirical quantities to concentrate.
This strategy turns the high-dimensional recovery problem into a collection of smaller, local problems.

We now make the construction precise.

Definition of the estimator.

It is defined row by row. Fix a target node
i\in[d]
.
We choose a bin size
h>0
, a clipping level
R>0
, and a candidate set size
m\geq k
.
Let
n=\lfloor T/h\rfloor
,
so that we use the discrete time points
0,h,\dots,(n-1)h
.
For each bin
r=0,\dots,n-1
, we record two quantities.
First, we sample the state on a grid defining

U_{j,r}:=X_{j}(rh),\qquad U_{r}=(U_{1,r},\dots,U_{d,r}),

and the clipped and sampled state of node
j
as

\psi_{R}(x):=\min\{x,R\},\qquad Z_{j,r}^{(R)}:=\psi_{R}(U_{j,r}).

Second, we record the binned event indicator for each node
i
in

Y_{i,r}^{(h)}:=\mathbf{1}\{N_{i}((rh,(r+1)h])\geq 1\}.

Z_{j,r}^{(R)}
is the clipped and sampled candidate state, measuring the past activity of node
j
, while
Y_{i,r}^{(h)}
measures whether node
i
has an event shortly afterwards.

The first stage screens candidate parents using the empirical covariance between these two quantities:

\widehat{F}_{ij}^{(h,R)}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}Z_{j,r}^{(R)}Y_{i,r}^{(h)}-\Bigl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}Z_{j,r}^{(R)}\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}Y_{i,r}^{(h)}\Bigr).

(1)

For the target node
i
, let
\widehat{C}_{i}
be the set of indices corresponding to the
m
largest values of
\{\widehat{F}_{ij}^{(h,R)}:j\in[d]\}
.
The set
\widehat{C}_{i}
is the screening set: it contains the nodes whose clipped and sampled activity is most correlated with future events of node
i
.

Figure 2
:
a
We observe event streams from a Hawkes process with an underlying latent network. To recover the network, our two-stage estimator works node by node.
b
For each node
i
, it converts the observed event times into a binned activity trace
Y_{i}
. Also, for all candidates
j\in[d]
, it computes the state
X_{j}(t)
and its sampled and clipped variant
Z_{j}
.
c
The first stage screens for nodes that are active shortly before an event at node
i
, retaining the nodes with the highest scores as a candidate set
C
of possible parents (grey).
d
The second stage centers
Y_{i}
and
Z_{j}
, performs a local regression on this reduced candidate set, and thresholds the fitted coefficients to recover the correct support (green).

The second stage refines this candidate set by a local least-squares regression.
We first center the clipped and sampled state and the binned event indicator,

\widetilde{Z}_{j,r}:=Z_{j,r}^{(R)}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}Z_{j,r}^{(R)},\qquad\widetilde{Y}_{i,r}:=Y_{i,r}^{(h)}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}Y_{i,r}^{(h)}.

For any candidate set
C\subset[d]
, we write

\widetilde{Z}_{C,r}:=(\widetilde{Z}_{j,r})_{j\in C}.

We then define the empirical covariance matrix and the empirical cross-covariance:

\widehat{\Sigma}_{C}^{(R)}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\widetilde{Z}_{C,r}\widetilde{Z}_{C,r}^{\top},\qquad\widehat{g}_{i,C}^{(h,R)}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\widetilde{Z}_{C,r}\widetilde{Y}_{i,r}.

(2)

On the screened set
\widehat{C}_{i}
, if
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\widehat{C}_{i}}^{(R)}
is invertible, we define the local least-squares coefficient vector by

\widehat{y}_{i,\widehat{C}_{i}}^{(h,R)}:=\bigl(\widehat{\Sigma}_{\widehat{C}_{i}}^{(R)}\bigr)^{-1}\widehat{g}_{i,\widehat{C}_{i}}^{(h,R)}.

(3)

The estimated parent set of node
i
is obtained by thresholding the local least-squares coefficients. We choose the threshold
\tau_{\alpha,h}:=\alpha w_{-}h/2
and define

\widehat{S}_{i}:=\{j\in\widehat{C}_{i}:\widehat{y}_{ij}^{(h,R)}\geq\tau_{\alpha,h}\}.

(4)

Now we can state the exact support recovery by bounded blocks result.

Theorem 3.1
.

Fix
m\geq k
. There exists
\alpha_{0}>0
such that for every fixed
0<\alpha\leq\alpha_{0}
there exist constants
C_{*},c_{*}>0
only depending on
(\beta,\mu_{\pm},w_{\pm},k,m,\alpha)
, and
d_{0}\geq m
such that for every
d>d_{0}
, every
B\in\mathcal{B}_{d,k}
, and every observation time satisfying

T\geq C_{*}\log d,

for every row
i
, the empirical covariance matrix in (
2
) is invertible, and the bounded-block estimator defined in (
1
), (
3
) and (
4
) satisfies

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\widehat{S}_{i}=S_{i},\ \forall i\bigr)\geq 1-d^{-c_{*}}.

Sketch of proof.

The proof has three ingredients; the full details are in Appendix
A
. First, for fixed
(h,R)
, the theoretical screening scores

F^{(h,R)}_{ij}:=\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(Z^{(R)}_{j,0},Y^{(h)}_{i,0}\bigr)

admit the first-order expansion

F^{(h,R)}_{ij}=hH^{(R)}_{ij}+O(Rh^{2}),\qquad H^{(R)}_{ij}\approx\frac{\mu_{j}\theta_{ij}}{2\beta},

uniformly over the weak-interaction class. More precisely,
for suitable fixed choices

R=R(\alpha)\asymp\alpha^{-1}
and

h=h(\alpha)\asymp\alpha^{2}
,

\min_{j\in S_{i}}F_{ij}^{(h,R)}-\max_{j\notin S_{i}}F_{ij}^{(h,R)}\geq c_{\rm scr}\,\alpha w_{-}h,

uniformly over
\mathcal{G}_{d,k}
; see Proposition
A.5
. Thus, every true parent has a strictly
larger theoretical screening score than every non-parent.

Second, the bounded block statistics concentrate uniformly. Since

Z_{j,r}^{(R)}\leq R
and
Y_{i,r}^{(h)}\in\{0,1\}
, the Poisson cluster
representation gives a Bernstein-type inequality for the dependent block
averages. Consequently, with probability at least
1-d^{-c}
, simultaneously
for all rows, all coordinates, and all candidate sets
C
with
|C|\leq m
,

\max_{i,j}|\widehat{F}_{ij}^{(h,R)}-F_{ij}^{(h,R)}|+\max_{i,C}\|\widehat{g}_{i,C}^{(h,R)}-g_{i,C}^{(h,R)}\|_{\infty}+\max_{C}\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{C}^{(R)}-\Sigma_{C}^{(R)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C\delta_{T},

where

\delta_{T}:=\sqrt{\frac{(h^{-1}+R^{2}h+R^{2}/h)\log d}{n}},\qquad n=\lfloor T/h\rfloor;

see Lemma
A.12
. If
C\delta_{T}\leq c_{\rm scr}\alpha w_{-}h/2
, the empirical screening scores
preserve the screening gap, and hence
S_{i}\subseteq\widehat{C}_{i}
for every
row
i
; see Proposition
A.9
.

Third, on this same high-probability event, the local least-squares step is
stable; see Lemma
A.4
. The theoretical Gram matrices on all sets
C\supseteq S_{i}
,

|C|\leq m
, have eigenvalues bounded away from zero, and their theoretical
regression coefficients satisfy the separation

\min_{j\in S_{i}}y^{(h,R)}_{ij,C}\geq\frac{3}{4}\alpha w_{-}h,\qquad\max_{j\in C\setminus S_{i}}|y^{(h,R)}_{ij,C}|\leq\frac{1}{4}\alpha w_{-}h;

see Proposition
A.6
. The preceding concentration bounds and a standard matrix-inversion perturbation
argument imply

\|\widehat{y}^{(h,R)}_{i,\widehat{C}_{i}}-y^{(h,R)}_{i,\widehat{C}_{i}}\|_{\infty}\leq C^{\prime}\delta_{T}.

Taking
C^{\prime}\delta_{T}\leq\alpha w_{-}h/8
, thresholding at

\tau_{\alpha,h}=\alpha w_{-}h/2
includes every parent and excludes every
non-parent; see Proposition
A.10
. Since
h(\alpha)
and
R(\alpha)
are fixed once
\alpha
is
fixed, the required small-deviation conditions are implied by

T\geq C\log d
. This proves the theorem.
∎

4
Information-theoretic lower bound

Theorem
3.1
shows that there exists a procedure that achieves exact network recovery once the observation time
T
is of order
\log d
. We now check whether this is the intrinsic scaling of the problem, or whether it comes from how we constructed the procedure. In this section, we prove that the logarithmic scaling of the observation time in
d
is optimal for the problem by establishing an information-theoretic lower bound. It holds uniformly over all estimators based on the observed process, regardless of their construction or computational cost.
Rather than treating the full model class of Section
2
in complete generality, we restrict our attention to a simple subclass. This is sufficient for proving optimality: since Theorem
3.1
already gives an upper bound of order
\log d
over the full class, it remains only to find a subclass with matching scaling in
d
. Any such lower bound then applies
a fortiori
to the full class.

The subclass we consider consists of interaction matrices with all rows equal to zero except for one target row
i_{\star}
. Fix
i_{\star}\in[d]
and background intensities
\bar{\mu},\bar{\mu}_{\star}\in[\mu_{-},\mu_{+}]
. The only unknown part of the interaction matrix is the support of size
k
of the
i_{\star}
-th row. We denote the set of admissible supports by

\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star}):=\{S\subset[d]\setminus\{i_{\star}\}:|S|=k\}.

Thus, each
S\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})
specifies which
k
nodes can influence node
i_{\star}
. For a given support
S
, we define the corresponding interaction matrix
\Theta^{(S)}=(\theta^{(S)}_{ij})
by

\theta^{(S)}_{i_{\star}j}=\begin{cases}\theta_{-},&j\in S,\\
0,&j\notin S,\end{cases}\qquad\theta^{(S)}_{ij}=0\quad\text{for }i\neq i_{\star}.

Thus, in this subclass, exact support recovery amounts to identifying the unknown set
S
from the observation
Y_{T}=(X(0),N_{[0,T]})
.

We now place a uniform prior on
S
. The initial uncertainty about the correct support is then

\log|\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})|=\log{\binom{d-1}{k}}
nats (using natural logarithmic convention). Therefore, an estimator can succeed only if the observation
Y_{T}
carries a comparable amount of information about
S
. This information is measured by the mutual information
I(S;Y_{T})
. Fano’s inequality turns this intuition into a lower bound on the error probability: if
I(S;Y_{T})
is much smaller than
\log|\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})|
, then many supports remain statistically indistinguishable, and every estimator must fail with non-negligible probability. For more details, see Appendix
B
.

Since
\bar{\mu},\bar{\mu}_{\star}\in[\mu_{-},\mu_{+}]
and every active edge has the minimum allowed signal
\theta_{-}=\alpha w_{-}
, every
\Theta^{(S)}
, with
S\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})
belongs to
\mathcal{G}_{d,k}
. Thus, any impossibility result proved for this subclass applies
a fortiori
to the full class
\mathcal{G}_{d,k}
. This is made precise by the following theorem, whose complete proof is given in Appendix
C
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For fixed
k\geq 1
, there exist constants
c>0
and

\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, depending only on

(\beta,\bar{\mu},\bar{\mu}_{\star},\theta_{-},k)
, such that for all sufficiently
large
d
, if
T\leq c\log d
, then

\inf_{\widehat{S}}\sup_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})}\mathbb{P}_{S}(\widehat{S}\neq S)\geq\varepsilon.

Consequently,

\inf_{\widehat{G}}\sup_{(\mu,\Theta)\in\mathcal{G}_{d,k}}\mathbb{P}_{\mu,\Theta}\bigl(\widehat{G}(Y_{T})\neq\operatorname{supp}(\Theta)\bigr)\geq\varepsilon.

In particular, along any sequence
T_{d}=o(\log d)
, the worst-case
exact-recovery error over the full class is bounded away from zero.
Combined with Theorem
3.1
, this identifies the optimal dimension dependence as logarithmic for fixed
k
and fixed coupling level.

Sketch of Proof.

Let
S^{\star}
be uniformly distributed on

\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})
, and let
Y_{T}
be the stationary observation generated under the interaction matrix
\Theta^{(S^{\star})}
and the fixed background intensities
\bar{\mu},\bar{\mu}_{\star}
.
Fano’s inequality gives, for any estimator
\widehat{S}=\widehat{S}(Y_{T})
of
S^{\star}
,

\sup_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})}\mathbb{P}_{S}(\widehat{S}\neq S)\geq 1-\frac{I(S^{\star};Y_{T})+\log 2}{\log|\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})|}.

Since
\log|\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})|=\log{\binom{d-1}{k}}
, it remains to bound the mutual information
I(S^{\star};Y_{T})
.
Let
P_{S}^{T}
be the law of the observation
Y_{T}
when the parent set is
S
. Let
P_{0}^{T}
be the reference law in which all interaction coefficients are set to zero, while the background intensities
\bar{\mu},\bar{\mu}_{\star}
are kept unchanged. The mutual information
I(S^{\star};Y_{T})
measures how much information the observation contains about the unknown support
S^{\star}
. We do not bound this mutual information directly. Instead, we compare every possible law
P_{S}^{T}
with the same law
P_{0}^{T}
. Since
P_{0}^{T}
does not depend on

, Lemma

gives

I(S^{\star};Y_{T})\leq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})|}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})}\operatorname{KL}(P_{S}^{T}\|P_{0}^{T}).

(5)

It is therefore enough to obtain a bound on
\operatorname{KL}(P_{S}^{T}\|P_{0}^{T})
that holds uniformly over
S\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})
.
For each
S
, the KL divergence splits into two terms: one coming from the stationary law of
X(0)
, and one coming from the conditional law of the counting process
N_{[0,T]}
given
X(0)
; see Lemma
C.1
. The stationary-law contribution is bounded by a constant
C_{\rm init}
, independent of both
d
and
T
. The reason is that, after a permutation of the nodes, the stationary laws differ only on the fixed
(k+1)
-dimensional block formed by node
i_{\star}
and its
k
parents; see Proposition
C.1
.

For the conditional-law contribution, the laws
P_{S}^{T}
and
P_{0}^{T}
differ only through the intensity of the counting process
N_{i_{\star}}
:

\lambda_{i_{\star}}^{(S)}(t)-\lambda_{i_{\star}}^{(0)}(t)=\theta_{-}\sum_{j\in S}X_{j}(t-).

Under
P_{0}^{T}
, all interaction coefficients are zero, so the intensity of

is equal to its background intensity:
\lambda_{i_{\star}}^{(0)}(t)\equiv\bar{\mu}_{\star}
for all

.
Jacod’s point-process Girsanov formula
jacod1975multivariate
therefore yields

\operatorname{KL}(P_{S}^{T}\|P_{0}^{T})\leq C_{\mathrm{init}}+\frac{\theta_{-}^{2}}{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{S}\Bigl[\Bigl(\sum_{j\in S}X_{j}(t-)\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr]\,dt;

see Proposition
C.2
. Now the advantage of the subclass becomes clear: for
j\neq i_{\star}
, the processes
X_{j}
are independent stationary Poisson shot-noise processes with background intensity
\bar{\mu}
. Hence,

\mathbb{E}_{S}\Bigl[\Bigl(\sum_{j\in S}X_{j}(t-)\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr]=\frac{k^{2}\bar{\mu}^{2}}{\beta^{2}}+\frac{k\bar{\mu}}{2\beta}=:C_{\mathrm{path}},

uniformly in
S
,
d
, and
t
; see Lemma
C.5
. Therefore,

\operatorname{KL}(P_{S}^{T}\|P_{0}^{T})\leq C_{\mathrm{init}}+\frac{\theta_{-}^{2}}{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}C_{\mathrm{path}}\,T.

Substituting this bound into Fano’s inequality gives

\inf_{\widehat{S}}\sup_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})}\mathbb{P}_{S}(\widehat{S}\neq S)\geq 1-\frac{C_{\mathrm{init}}+\frac{\theta_{-}^{2}}{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}C_{\mathrm{path}}T+\log 2}{\log\binom{d-1}{k}};

(6)

see Proposition
C.3
. Since
k
is fixed,

\log\binom{d-1}{k}\asymp\log d
, so the right-hand side is bounded away from zero whenever
T\leq c\log d
for
c>0
small enough.

Finally, any estimator of the full support matrix over
\mathcal{G}_{d,k}
induces, on this subclass, an estimator of the unknown support
S
by restricting the estimated support to the
i_{\star}
-th row. Hence the lower bound obtained on the subclass also applies to exact support recovery over the full class
\mathcal{G}_{d,k}

∎

Based on Theorem
4.1
, we can make a few remarks:

•

The term
C_{\mathrm{init}}
is the bound on the information contained in stationary initial state
X(0)
. It does
not
depend on
d
and does not grow with
T
. Therefore, in the dimension-asymptotic regime with all other parameters fixed, it contributes only an additive
O(1)
term. The part of the KL bound that grows with the observation time is

\frac{\theta_{-}^{2}}{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}\,C_{\mathrm{path}}\,T,\qquad C_{\mathrm{path}}=\frac{k^{2}\bar{\mu}^{2}}{\beta^{2}}+\frac{k\bar{\mu}}{2\beta}.

Thus the lower-bound argument identifies the necessary information scale

T\gtrsim\frac{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}{\theta_{-}^{2}\,C_{\mathrm{path}}}\log\binom{d-1}{k},

up to dimension-independent additive constants depending on

(\beta,\bar{\mu},\bar{\mu}_{\star},\theta_{-},k)
.

•

The logarithmic dependence on
d
is the combinatorial part of the required observation time. The constant prefactor reflects the amount of information generated per unit time. For
d
sufficiently large, it can be read informally as follows: a larger minimum edge strength
\theta_{-}
makes recovery easier, since distinguishability scales quadratically in
\theta_{-}
; a larger background intensity at node
i_{\star}
makes recovery harder, because it increases the background activity against which parental influence must be detected; a larger background activity
\bar{\mu}
of the parent process makes recovery easier, as it increases the aggregated signal power
C_{\mathrm{path}}
of the parents; and a smaller decay rate
\beta
makes recovery easier, since longer memory strengthens the cumulative effect of parent events.

5
Conclusion and future work

We proved that the observation time required for exact network recovery in Hawkes processes scales logarithmically with the network size
d
.
This puts the success of empirical work on a theoretical basis: a large network size does not make exact recovery prohibitive, because
d
only enters through the combinatorial cost of identifying a sparse parent set.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result of its kind.
We also identify the key factors that make our proof possible.
The known exponential kernel provides a Markov state representation, the intensity depends linearly on this state, and stationarity removes burn-in effects while providing uniform distributional control over the observation window.
Weak interactions play an equally important role.
Indirect excitation cascades may create correlations between non-adjacent nodes and thereby mimic direct causal influence.
The weak-interaction condition ensures that such effects remain smaller than the contribution of a true parent.
This separation is the central statistical mechanism behind both the screening gap and the stability of the local regression step.

A natural direction for future work is to sharpen the dependence on other parameters, especially the coupling strength.
This is particularly important for applications where the available observation window is usually constrained.
Other extensions include signed or inhibitory interactions, unknown or coordinate-dependent decay rates, marked Hawkes processes, and spatially structured networks.
Signed interactions are challenging because cancellations may destroy separation arguments and the intensity may approach zero.
Marked and spatial models would bring the theory closer to applications.

One speculative but promising application area is earthquake dynamics. Earthquake catalogs are naturally event data, and classical aftershock models already use Hawkes processes to describe triggering.
It would be interesting to develop a multiscale (marked and spatial) extension of the theory and connect it to emerging network-based forecasting approaches.

Appendix A
Upper bound proof details

Appendix
A
contains the proof of the upper bound.
We organize it into five sections.
We begin with an intuitive description and a short overview before turning to the technical details. The key starting point is that the estimator does not work directly with the raw, unbounded process
X(t)
. Instead, it clips the covariates, that is, the states, individual variables, or specific combinations of them, at a finite level
R
, and then discretizes time into small bins of size
h
. The first step is therefore to show that this preprocessing preserves the signal: the covariance used to distinguish true parents of events (trigger nodes) from non-parents must survive clipping and binning, and the resulting regression problem must remain well posed. Once this foundation is established, the next challenge is statistical: the empirical averages computed from the data must be shown to concentrate around their true values. This is nontrivial because the binned observations are not independent, as events in a Hawkes process can trigger later events. The Poisson cluster representation, however, isolates the independent cluster roots, making it possible to control dependence across bins and derive explicit exponential tail bounds. Finally, we combine the theoretical separation between parents and non-parents with these concentration bounds to show that the two-stage estimator succeeds with high probability. The contents of the sections are outlined in the following list:

•

Section A1
contains some lemmas resulting from the choice of the framework and the assumptions made; these results are not specific to the estimator but rather reflect fundamental properties of stable multivariate Hawkes processes, and they serve as the basic toolkit invoked repeatedly throughout the subsequent sections

•

Section A2
introduces clipping and binning and analyzes the effect on covariances and, consequently, on screening gaps;

•

Section A3
shows that the least-squares regression on clipped covariates, run on any candidate set containing the true parents, produces slope estimates that are meaningful and that the associated Gram matrix remains well-conditioned uniformly;

•

Section A4
derives concentration inequalities for the empirical averages of the bounded observables used by the estimator, by decomposing the Hawkes process into clusters with independent roots, bounding the impact of each cluster on the statistics of interest, and combining these bounds into explicit tail probabilities that decay exponentially in the sample size;

•

Section A5
combines the theoretical screening gap and regression separation with the empirical concentration to show that, with probability tending to one, the two-stage estimator first includes all true parents in the screened superset and then recovers them exactly by thresholding the bounded least-squares coefficients.

First of all we prove the uniform bounds for any fixed order moments of

Proposition A.1
.

For every

\sup_{d\geq 1}\sup_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{d,k}}\sup_{1\leq j\leq d}\mathbb{E}[X_{j}(0)^{p}]<\infty,\qquad p=1,\dots,P.

Proof.

Fix

and apply Dynkin’s formula in stationarity with the coherent infinitesimal generator of the process.
Then letting the smoothing parameter tend to zero leads to

\beta p\,\mathbb{E}\!\Big[X_{j}\min\{X_{j},M\}^{p-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{j}<M\}}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\lambda_{j}\bigl(X(0)\bigr)\bigl(\min\{X_{j}+1,M\}^{p}-\min\{X_{j},M\}^{p}\bigl)\Big].

Both sides are nonnegative and increase to their untruncated counterparts as

\beta p\,\mathbb{E}[X_{j}^{p}]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\lambda_{j}\bigl(X(0)\bigr)\big((X_{j}+1)^{p}-X_{j}^{p}\big)\Big].

Now, since
(x+1)^{p}-x^{p}\leq c_{p}(1+x^{p-1})

\lambda_{j}\bigl(X(0)\bigr)\leq\mu_{+}+\theta_{+}\sum_{\ell\in S_{j},\,|S_{j}|\leq k}X_{\ell}(0)

\beta p\,\mathbb{E}[X_{j}^{p}]\leq c_{p}\mu_{+}\bigl(1+\mathbb{E}[X_{j}^{p-1}]\bigr)+c_{p}\theta_{+}\sum_{\ell\in S_{j},\,|S_{j}|\leq k}\mathbb{E}\bigl[X_{\ell}(1+X_{j}^{p-1})\bigr].

(7)

Using Young’s inequality for the mixed term gives

\mathbb{E}\bigl[X_{\ell}(1+X_{j}^{p-1})\bigr]\leq\mathbb{E}[X_{\ell}]+\mathbb{E}[X_{\ell}^{p}]+\mathbb{E}[X_{j}^{p}]\leq M_{1}+M_{p}+M_{p},

where, with the convention

,
M_{p}:=\sup_{d,B,j}\mathbb{E}[X_{j}(0)^{p}]
.
Therefore taking the supremum over

\beta p\,M_{p}\leq C_{p}\mu_{+}\bigl(1+M_{p-1}\bigr)+C_{p}\beta\gamma\bigl(M_{1}+M_{p}\bigr),

\beta(p-C_{p}\gamma)\,M_{p}\leq C_{p}\bigl(\mu_{+}+\beta\gamma M_{1}+\mu_{+}M_{p-1}\bigr).

For

and for

\mathbf{1}\{N_{i}((0,t))\geq 1\}\leq N_{i}((0,t])

\mathbb{E}\bigl[N_{i}^{(h)}-Y_{i,0}^{(h)}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{h}\mathbf{1}\{N_{i}((0,t))\geq 1\}\lambda_{i}(t)\,dt\leq\int_{0}^{h}\mathbb{E}\bigl[N_{i}((0,t])\lambda_{i}(t)\bigr]dt.

(23)

Now, for
t\leq 1
, using
X_{\ell}(t)\leq X_{\ell}(0)+N_{\ell}((0,t])

\lambda_{i}(t)\leq\mu_{+}+\theta_{+}\sum_{\ell\in S_{i}}X_{\ell}(t)\leq C_{1}\left(1+\sum_{\ell\in S_{i}}X_{\ell}(0)+\sum_{\ell\in S_{i}}N_{\ell}((0,t])\right),

Hence the expectation in the previous integral satisfies

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\bigl[N_{i}((0,t])\lambda_{i}(t)\bigr]\leq{}&C_{1}\mathbb{E}[N_{i}((0,t])]+C_{1}\sum_{\ell\in S_{i}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[N_{i}((0,t])X_{\ell}(0)\bigr]\\
&+C_{1}\sum_{\ell\in S_{i}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[N_{i}((0,t])N_{\ell}((0,t])\bigr].\end{split}

(24)

The first term satisfies
\mathbb{E}[N_{i}((0,t])]\leq C_{2}t
by compensator identity and Proposition
A.1
. For the mixed term, using the compensator identity multiple times,
\lambda_{i}(s)\leq C_{3}(1+\sum_{m\in S_{i}}X_{m}(s))
, Cauchy Schwarz, stationarity, and Proposition
A.1
with
P=2
, it holds
\mathbb{E}[N_{i}((0,t])X_{\ell}(0)]\leq C_{3}t
. For the last term, we use the martingale decomposition for
N_{q}((0,t])
(for any
q
), Itô isometry, and stationarity to state that
M_{q}
is a square-integrable martingale, and then for
t\leq 1
it holds

\mathbb{E}\bigl[N_{q}((0,t])^{2}\bigr]\leq 2\mathbb{E}[M_{q}(t)^{2}]+2\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{q}(s)\,ds\right)^{2}\right]\leq C_{4}t\qquad\text{uniformly in }(q,d,B),

where again the elementary inequality
(x+y)^{2}\leq C(x^{2}+y^{2})
, the Itô isometry, Cauchy Schwarz for integrals, and Proposition
A.1
with
P=2
have been used. Then it is easy to bound the term
\mathbb{E}\bigl[N_{i}((0,t])N_{\ell}((0,t])\bigr]
by Cauchy Schwarz. Hence, using
|S_{i}|\leq k
, combining all the bounds obtained above for the right-hand terms in (
24
) and defining
C_{bin,1}
as a convenient combination of the constants in (
24
) lead to
\mathbb{E}\bigl[N_{i}((0,t])\lambda_{i}(t)\bigr]\leq C_{bin,1}t
, and substituting this bound in (
23
) gives:

\mathbb{E}\bigl[N_{i}^{(h)}-Y_{i,0}^{(h)}\bigr]\leq\int_{0}^{h}\mathbb{E}\bigl[N_{i}((0,t])\lambda_{i}(t)\bigr]dt\leq\frac{C_{bin,1}}{2}h^{2}

Exploiting the facts that
0\leq Z\leq R
and that
\operatorname{Cov}(Z,N_{i}^{(h)})=\operatorname{Cov}(Z,\Lambda_{i}^{(h)})
(this follows from substituting the martingale decomposition in the expectations, because
Z
is
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable and
N_{i}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{i}(s)ds
is an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-martingale), then it is possible to show the following:

\lvert\operatorname{Cov}(Z,Y_{i,0}^{(h)})-\operatorname{Cov}(Z,N_{i}^{(h)})\rvert\leq 2\|Z\|_{\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl[N_{i}^{(h)}-Y_{i,0}^{(h)}\bigr]\leq C_{bin,1}Rh^{2}.

(25)

\operatorname{Cov}(Z,N_{i}^{(h)})-h\operatorname{Cov}(Z,\lambda_{i}(0))=\operatorname{Cov}(Z,\Lambda_{i}^{(h)})-h\operatorname{Cov}(Z,\lambda_{i}(0))=\int_{0}^{h}\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(Z,\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{i}(0)\bigr)dt.

As before, it is easy to show that
\lvert\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(Z,\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{i}(0)\bigr)\rvert\leq 2R\,\mathbb{E}\lvert\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{i}(0)\rvert
. Now:

\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{i}(0)=\sum_{\ell\in S_{i}}\theta_{i\ell}(X_{\ell}(t)-X_{\ell}(0)),

Writing down the integral form of SDE for
X_{\ell}(t)
, taking the absolute value, and using stationarity and Lemma
A.1
, we get

\mathbb{E}\lvert X_{\ell}(t)-X_{\ell}(0)\rvert\leq\beta\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[X_{\ell}(s)]ds+\mathbb{E}[N_{\ell}(t)-N_{\ell}(0)]\leq C_{5}t.

Therefore, we can define conveniently
C_{bin,2}
such that
\mathbb{E}|\lambda_{i}(t)-\lambda_{i}(0)|\leq C_{bin,2}t
uniformly in
(i,d,B)
, and thus

\lvert\operatorname{Cov}(Z,\Lambda_{i}^{(h)})-hH_{ij}^{(R)}\rvert\leq 2R\int_{0}^{h}C_{bin,2}t\,dt\leq C_{bin,2}Rh^{2}.

Combining this with (
25
) and defining
C_{bin}:=C_{bin,1}+C_{bin,2}
proves (
22
).
∎

Lemma
A.3
, together with the screening gap already established for the clipped covariances, yields Proposition

below: the separation between true parents and non-parents is preserved by the observable quantities used by the estimator, provided

is sufficiently small relative to

Proposition A.5
.

There exist
\alpha_{2}>0
, a function

while

for all

whenever

.

Proof.

If

X(0)

.

A.3
Bounded local regression

Fix
R\geq 1
and a candidate set
C\subset\{1,\dots,d\}
with
|C|\leq m
. Define:

\Sigma_{C}^{(R)}:=\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(Z_{C}^{(R)}(0),Z_{C}^{(R)}(0)\bigr),\qquad Z_{C}^{(R)}(0):=(Z_{j}^{(R)})_{j\in C},\qquad g_{i,C}^{(h,R)}:=\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(Z_{C}^{(R)}(0),Y_{i,0}^{(h)}\bigr).

Before stating the uniform nondegeneracy of the clipped Gram matrix
\Sigma_{C}^{(R)}
, we define, whenever
\Sigma_{C}^{(R)}
is invertible, the centered theoretical least-squares slope as

y_{i,C}^{(h,R)}:=\bigl(\Sigma_{C}^{(R)}\bigr)^{-1}g_{i,C}^{(h,R)}

(27)

Lemma A.4
.

There exist
\alpha_{3}>0
,
R_{3}:(0,\alpha_{3}]\to[1,\infty)
, and
\kappa>0
, depending only on
(\beta,\mu_{-},\mu_{+},w_{+},k,m)
, such that for every fixed
0<\alpha\leq\alpha_{3}
, every
R\geq R_{3}(\alpha)
, every
d
, every
B\in\mathcal{B}_{d,k}
, and every
C\subset\{1,\dots,d\}
with
|C|\leq m
, we have
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{C}^{(R)})\geq\kappa
.

Proof.

Under
\alpha=0
the coordinates are independent shot-noise processes. Hence, for any fixed
C
:

\Sigma_{C,0}^{(R)}=\text{diag}\Bigl(\operatorname{Var}_{0}\bigl(\psi_{R}(X_{j}(0))\bigr)\Bigr)_{j\in C}.

Now
\operatorname{Var}_{0}(X_{j}(0))=\mu_{j}/(2\beta)\geq\mu_{-}/(2\beta)
, then Lemma
A.2
with
i=j
and
\ell=j
and choosing
R
large enough gives

\operatorname{Var}_{0}\bigl(\psi_{R}(X_{j}(0))\bigr)\geq\mu_{-}/4\beta
for all
j
. Thus
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{C,0}^{(R)})\geq\mu_{-}/4\beta
for all
|C|\leq m
.
Next compare
\Sigma_{C}^{(R)}
to
\Sigma_{C,0}^{(R)}
. Fix
j,\ell\in C
. By adding and subtracting the unclipped covariances at couplings
\alpha
and
0
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}_{\alpha}\bigl(Z_{j}^{(R)},Z_{\ell}^{(R)}\bigr)-\operatorname{Cov}_{0}\bigl(Z_{j}^{(R)},Z_{\ell}^{(R)}\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad=\Bigl[\operatorname{Cov}_{\alpha}\bigl(Z_{j}^{(R)},Z_{\ell}^{(R)}\bigr)-\operatorname{Cov}_{\alpha}(X_{j},X_{\ell})\Bigr]+\Bigl[\operatorname{Cov}_{\alpha}(X_{j},X_{\ell})-\operatorname{Cov}_{0}(X_{j},X_{\ell})\Bigr]

\displaystyle\qquad\phantom{=}+\Bigl[\operatorname{Cov}_{0}(X_{j},X_{\ell})-\operatorname{Cov}_{0}\bigl(Z_{j}^{(R)},Z_{\ell}^{(R)}\bigr)\Bigr].

The first and third brackets are
O(R^{-1})
uniformly by Lemma
A.2
. For the middle bracket, if
j=\ell
then Proposition
A.2
gives

\lvert\operatorname{Var}_{\alpha}(X_{j})-\operatorname{Var}_{0}(X_{j})\rvert\leq C\alpha,

while if
j\neq\ell
then independence at
\alpha=0
gives
\operatorname{Cov}_{0}(X_{j},X_{\ell})=0
and Proposition
A.2
yields

\lvert\operatorname{Cov}_{\alpha}(X_{j},X_{\ell})-\operatorname{Cov}_{0}(X_{j},X_{\ell})\rvert=\lvert\operatorname{Cov}_{\alpha}(X_{j},X_{\ell})\rvert\leq C\alpha.

Hence every entry of
\Sigma_{C}^{(R)}-\Sigma_{C,0}^{(R)}
is
O(\alpha)+O(R^{-1})
, uniformly over all
(d,B,C)
. Recalling the bound for operator norm given by the Frobenius norm, since the matrix dimension is at most
m
, it holds that

\sup_{|C|\leq m}\lVert\Sigma_{C}^{(R)}-\Sigma_{C,0}^{(R)}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C_{m}\bigl(\alpha+R^{-1}\bigr).

We take
\alpha_{3}>0
small and then
R_{3}(\alpha)
large enough so that the right-hand side is at most
\mu_{-}/(8\beta)
. Weyl’s inequality and bound
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{C,0}^{(R)})\geq\mu_{-}/4\beta
yield the desired claim with
\kappa:=\mu_{-}/8\beta

∎

The next proposition shows how the theoretical OLS slope computed on any candidate set
C\supset S_{i}
of bounded size is close to the rescaled true coefficient vector
\theta_{i,C}
up to small errors from clipping and binning and that this approximation is tight enough to correctly separate true parents from non-parents:

Proposition A.6
.

There exist
\alpha_{4}>0
, a function
R_{4}:(0,\alpha_{4}]\to[1,\infty)
, and a constant
C_{y}>0
depending only on
(\beta,\mu_{\pm},w_{\pm},k,m)
such that for every fixed
0<\alpha\leq\alpha_{4}
, every
R\geq R_{4}(\alpha)
, and every

0<h\leq\min\left\{\frac{w_{-}}{8C_{y}}\frac{\alpha}{R},1\right\},

one has, for every row
i
and every candidate set
C\supset S_{i}
with
|C|\leq m
,

\lVert y_{i,C}^{(h,R)}-h\theta_{i,C}\rVert_{\infty}\leq C_{y}\left(\frac{\alpha h}{R}+Rh^{2}\right).

(28)

In particular,

\min_{j\in S_{i}}y_{ij,C}^{(h,R)}\geq\frac{3}{4}\theta_{-}h,\qquad\max_{j\in C\setminus S_{i}}\lvert y_{ij,C}^{(h,R)}\rvert\leq\frac{1}{4}\theta_{-}h.

(29)

Proof.

Fix
(i,C)
. Recalling the definition of
g_{i,C}^{(h,R)}
, Lemma
A.3
applied coordinatewise gives

g_{i,C}^{(h,R)}=h\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(Z_{C}^{(R)}(0),\lambda_{i}(0)\bigr)+r_{i,C}^{(h,R)},\qquad\lVert r_{i,C}^{(h,R)}\rVert_{\infty}\leq CRh^{2}.

(30)

Now, because
C\supset S_{i}
, using the definition for
\lambda_{i}(0)
:

\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(Z_{C}^{(R)},\lambda_{i}\bigr)=\sum_{\ell\in C}\theta_{i\ell}\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(Z_{C}^{(R)},X_{\ell}\bigr).

Writing
X_{\ell}=Z_{\ell}^{(R)}+\delta_{\ell}^{(R)}
with
\delta_{\ell}^{(R)}=(X_{\ell}-R)_{+}
gives
\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(Z_{C}^{(R)},\lambda_{i}\bigr)=\Sigma_{C}^{(R)}\theta_{i,C}+q_{i,C}^{(R)}
, where
q_{i,C}^{(R)}:=\sum_{\ell\in C}\theta_{i\ell}\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(Z_{C}^{(R)},\delta_{\ell}^{(R)}\bigr)
, then

g_{i,C}^{(h,R)}=h\Sigma_{C}^{(R)}\theta_{i,C}+hq_{i,C}^{(R)}+r_{i,C}^{(h,R)}.

(31)

To bound
q_{i,C}^{(R)}
, fix
j,\ell\in C
. Since
0\leq Z_{j}^{(R)}\leq X_{j}
and
\delta_{\ell}^{(R)}=(X_{\ell}-R)_{+}
,

\lvert\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(Z_{j}^{(R)},\delta_{\ell}^{(R)}\bigr)\rvert\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[Z_{j}^{(R)}\delta_{\ell}^{(R)}\bigr]+\mathbb{E}[Z_{j}^{(R)}]\mathbb{E}[\delta_{\ell}^{(R)}]\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[X_{j}\delta_{\ell}^{(R)}\bigr]+\mathbb{E}[X_{j}]\mathbb{E}[\delta_{\ell}^{(R)}].

By Cauchy–Schwarz, Proposition
A.1
with
P=4
and (
18
),

\mathbb{E}\bigl[X_{j}\delta_{\ell}^{(R)}\bigr]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X_{j}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[(\delta_{\ell}^{(R)})^{2}]}\leq\frac{C}{R},\qquad\mathbb{E}[X_{j}]\mathbb{E}[\delta_{\ell}^{(R)}]\leq\frac{C}{R}.

Hence each coordinate of
\operatorname{Cov}(Z_{C}^{(R)},\delta_{\ell}^{(R)})
is
O(R^{-1})
, uniformly in
(d,B,i,C,\ell)
. Since
|C|\leq m
and
\theta_{i\ell}\leq\alpha w_{+}
,

\lVert q_{i,C}^{(R)}\rVert_{\infty}\leq C\alpha/R.

Now, rewriting (
27
) using (
31
), gives

y_{i,C}^{(h,R)}-h\theta_{i,C}=\bigl(\Sigma_{C}^{(R)}\bigr)^{-1}\Bigl(hq_{i,C}^{(R)}+r_{i,C}^{(h,R)}\Bigr).

Therefore, using Lemma
A.4
implication
\lVert(\Sigma_{C}^{(R)})^{-1}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\kappa^{-1}
and the obtained bound for
\lVert q_{i,C}^{(R)}\rVert_{\infty}
gives

\lVert y_{i,C}^{(h,R)}-h\theta_{i,C}\rVert_{\infty}\leq C_{y}\left(\frac{\alpha h}{R}+Rh^{2}\right)

for a constant
C_{y}
depending only on the model parameters. Choose
\alpha_{4}\leq\alpha_{3}
and then choose
R_{4}(\alpha)\geq\max\left\{R_{3}(\alpha),8C_{y}/w_{-}\right\}
and
0<h\leq\min\{1,\theta_{-}/(8C_{y}R)\}
. If
R\geq R_{4}(\alpha)
then

C_{y}\frac{\alpha h}{R}\leq\frac{1}{8}\theta_{-}h,\qquad C_{y}Rh^{2}\leq\frac{1}{8}\theta_{-}h,

so the right-hand side of (
28
) is at most
h\theta_{-}/4
. Since
\theta_{ij}\geq\theta_{-}
on
S_{i}
and
\theta_{ij}=0
on
C\setminus S_{i}
, (
29
) follows.
∎

A.4
Bounded-block concentration via Poisson clusters

First of all, set
0<h\leq h_{0}:=1
and recall
\gamma<1
. The observables we are focusing on are the following four:

Z_{j,r}^{(R)},\qquad Y_{i,r}^{(h)},\qquad Z_{j,r}^{(R)}Y_{i,r}^{(h)},\qquad Z_{a,r}^{(R)}Z_{b,r}^{(R)}

(32)

Now, we introduce the terminology used in the context of the cluster representation of Hawkes processes:

•

Root
: these are events that arrive according to a independent Poisson process with background intensity
\mu_{v}
. They are “exogenous” because they are not caused by previous events;

•

Type-
j
: since the process
X
has
d
components indexed by
j=1,\dots,d
, each event in the system carries a label
j
indicating which component it belongs to. This label is called its “type”;

•

Offspring
: each root can trigger direct offspring, which in turn can trigger their own offspring (called multiple generations). Thus, each cluster
\mathcal{C}
has an independent root and each event of type-
j
produces type-
i
children according to an independent Poisson point process on
(0,\infty)
with intensity
\theta_{ij}\exp\{-\beta t\}\,dt
. Therefore the mean offspring matrix is
K:=\Theta/\beta
and consequently
\|K\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma<1
;

•

Progeny
: while offspring refers only to the events that are immediately triggered by one specific root, the progeny of one root includes every single event in the cluster, across all generations;

•

Age
: in the cluster representation, each cluster has a root event that is “born” at some time
t
, namely the time at which the root arrives. The age of any event in the cluster is the time that passed since the root was born. Thus if an event occurs at time
s
, its age is
s-t
. Hence, “age” is not absolute time, but rather time measured relative to the birth time of the root of the cluster to which the event belongs.

Now, set
a>1
s.t.
a\gamma<1
, fix a finite coordinate set
J\subset[d]
with
|J|\leq 2
and denote the type-
j
events at time
t
in a cluster
\mathcal{C}
as
(j,t)\in\mathcal{C}
. Define the type weights
q_{v}^{(a)}
as

q_{v}^{(a)}:=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}a^{\ell}\sum_{j\in J}(K^{\ell})_{jv},\qquad v=1,\dots,d.

(33)

Note that
q_{v}^{(a)}=\mathbf{1}\{v\in J\}+a\sum_{i=1}^{d}K_{iv}q_{i}^{(a)}
, thus
q_{v}^{(a)}\geq 1
for every
v\in J
. Define also:

a_{h}(t):=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta(rh-t)}\textbf{1}\{t\leq rh\}\leq C_{h}\times\begin{cases}e^{\beta t},&t<0,\\
1,&t\geq 0,\end{cases}\qquad\quad\quad C_{h}:=\frac{e^{\beta h}}{1-e^{-\beta h}},

(34)

where the inequality follows from a simple calculation. Lastly, the weighted local cluster size is:

H_{J}(\mathcal{C}):=\sum_{(j,t)\in\mathcal{C},\,j\in J}a_{h}(t)+\sum_{(j,t)\in\mathcal{C},\,j\in J}\textbf{1}\{t\geq 0\}.

(35)

Because
q_{j}^{(a)}\geq 1
on
J
and
a_{h}(t)\leq C_{h}
, defining
W_{J}^{(a)}(\mathcal{C}):=\sum_{(j,t)\in\mathcal{C}}q_{j}^{(a)}
, it holds

H_{J}(\mathcal{C})\leq(C_{h}+1)\sum_{(j,t)\in\mathcal{C},\,j\in J}1\leq(C_{h}+1)W_{J}^{(a)}(\mathcal{C}).

(36)

The following lemma shows that the exponential moment of the weighted cluster size is uniformly bounded, regardless of the dimension
d
and the network configuration.

Lemma A.5
.

There exist constants
\eta_{A}>0
and
C_{A}>0
, depending only on
(h,R,\beta,\gamma)
, such that for every
d
, every
B\in\mathcal{B}_{d,k}
, every
J\subset[d]
with
|J|\leq 2
, and every root type
v
,

\mathbb{E}_{v}\big[e^{\eta_{A}H_{J}(\mathcal{C})}\big]\leq C_{A}.

(37)

Proof.

Choose
a\in(1,\gamma^{-1})
and abbreviate
q_{v}:=q_{v}^{(a)}
. By (
36
), it suffices to prove the Lemma for
W_{J}^{(a)}(\mathcal{C})
. First of all note that:

0\leq q_{v}^{(a)}\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}a^{\ell}\sum_{j\in J}\|K\|_{\infty}^{\ell}\leq|J|\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(a\gamma)^{\ell}=\frac{|J|}{1-a\gamma}\leq\frac{2}{1-a\gamma}=:Q.

(38)

Let
W_{J,v}^{(a)}
denote the weighted total progeny when the root has type
v
, and let
W_{J,v}^{(a,m)}
be the same quantity truncated at generation depth at most
m\geq 0
. Similarly, if root
v
has
M_{i}
type-
i
offspring (note that
M_{i}
is Poisson with parameter
K_{iv}
), denote
W_{J,i,k}^{(a,m)}
the truncated weighted total progeny generated by type-
i
offspring. Then defining
F_{v}^{(m)}(\eta):=\mathbb{E}_{v}\big[e^{\eta W_{J,v}^{(a,m)}}\big]
,

\displaystyle F_{v}^{(m+1)}(\eta)={}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[e^{\eta\left(q_{v}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{i}}W_{J,i,k}^{(a,m)}\right)}\right]=e^{\eta q_{v}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{M_{i}}e^{\eta W_{J,i,k}^{(a,m)}}\biggl|M_{i}=m_{i}\right]\right]=

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle e^{\eta q_{v}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\left(F^{(m)}_{i}(\eta)\right)^{M_{i}}\right]=e^{\eta q_{v}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}e^{-K_{iv}}\sum_{m_{i}=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(F_{i}^{(m)}K_{iv}\right)^{m_{i}}}{m_{i}!}=

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\exp\left(\eta q_{v}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}K_{iv}\bigl(F_{i}^{(m)}(\eta)-1\bigr)\right),

where
F_{v}^{(0)}(\eta)=e^{\eta q_{v}}
. Set
A:=2/(1-a^{-1})>1
, then
A(1-a^{-1})>1
and
\exists x_{0}>0
depending only on
a
, such that

e^{x}-1\leq A\frac{x}{1+A/a}\qquad\text{for every }0\leq x\leq x_{0}.

(39)

Now choose
\eta_{A}>0
such that
\eta_{A}Q(1+A/a)\leq x_{0}
. This choice, for
m=0
and (
38
), implies
\eta_{A}q_{v}\leq x_{0}
and therefore
F_{v}^{(0)}(\eta_{A})-1=e^{\eta_{A}q_{v}}-1\leq A\eta_{A}q_{v}
. Assume that the same inequality holds for a fixed
m>0
and
\forall v
as inductive step; using the obtained expression for
F_{v}^{(m+1)}(\eta)
,
\sum_{i=1}^{d}K_{iv}q_{i}\leq q_{v}/a
and the same reasoning used for the zero-step, it holds

F_{v}^{(m+1)}(\eta_{A})\leq\exp\!\left(\eta_{A}q_{v}+A\eta_{A}\sum_{i=1}^{d}K_{iv}q_{i}\right)\leq\exp\!\left(\eta_{A}q_{v}\Bigl(1+\frac{A}{a}\Bigr)\right)\leq 1+A\eta_{A}q_{v},

concluding the induction. Since
W_{J,v}^{(a,m)}\uparrow W_{J,v}^{(a)}
almost surely as
m\to\infty
, monotone convergence yields
\mathbb{E}_{v}\big[e^{\eta_{A}W_{J}^{(a)}(\mathcal{C})}\big]\leq 1+A\eta_{A}Q=:C_{A}^{\prime}<\infty
,
uniformly in
(d,B,J,v)
. Combining with (
36
) and shrinking
\eta_{A}
by the factor
(C_{h}+1)
and renaming it proves (
37
).
∎

The proof of Lemma
A.5
gives a little more than the uniform bound stated there: monotone convergence in previous proof’s inductive step yields

\mathbb{E}_{v}\big[e^{\eta_{A}W_{J,v}^{(a)}}-1\big]\leq A\eta_{A}q_{v}^{(a)}.

(40)

Moreover, by the row-sum bound,

\sum_{v=1}^{d}\mu_{v}q_{v}^{(a)}\leq\mu_{+}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}a^{\ell}\sum_{j\in J}\sum_{v=1}^{d}(K^{\ell})_{jv}\leq\mu_{+}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}a^{\ell}\sum_{j\in J}\|K\|_{\infty}^{\ell}\leq 2\mu_{+}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(a\gamma)^{\ell}\leq C_{q}.

(41)

Consequently, for every integer

there exists

such that

\sum_{v=1}^{d}\mu_{v}\,\mathbb{E}_{v}\big[(W_{J,v}^{(a)})^{m}e^{\eta_{m}W_{J,v}^{(a)}}\big]\leq C_{m},

(42)

where
C_{m}>0
depends only on
(m,\beta,\mu_{+},k,w_{+},\gamma)
.
Indeed, choose
\eta_{m}>0
such that
2\eta_{m}\leq\eta_{A}
and use the pointwise bound

, valid for every

, together with (
40
) and (
41
). For

one simply uses (
37
) directly. We now use this result to prove the following lemma, which controls how long a cluster keeps producing events of the types we are interested in (for example
J
). Specifically, it shows that the probability of a cluster still having type-
J

, uniformly over all dimensions and all network configurations.

Lemma A.6
.

Fix
J\subset[d]
with
|J|\leq 2
. For one cluster started from a root of type
v
at time
0
, let

with the convention

. Thus the root itself is counted at age
0
when
v\in J

c_{L}>0
, and
C_{L}>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{v}(L_{J}\geq u)

\displaystyle\leq q_{v}^{(a)}e^{-c_{L}u},

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{d}\mu_{v}\mathbb{P}_{v}(L_{J}\geq u)

\displaystyle\leq C_{L}e^{-c_{L}u}.

(44)

Proof.

Set
c_{L}:=\beta(1-a^{-1})>0
. Let
N_{J}([u,\infty))
denote the number of type-
J
events in the full cluster whose ages lie in
[u,\infty)
, again counting the root when applicable. Then

\mathbb{P}_{v}(L_{J}\geq u)=\mathbb{E}_{v}[\textbf{1}\{L_{J}\geq u\}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{v}\big[N_{J}([u,\infty))\big].

For
u=0
, the root contributes exactly when
v\in J
, so

\mathbb{E}_{v}\big[N_{J}([0,\infty))\big]=\mathbf{1}\{v\in J\}+\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\sum_{j\in J}(K^{\ell})_{jv}\leq q_{v}^{(a)},

because
a^{\ell}\geq 1
and
q_{v}^{(a)}
contains the
\ell=0
root term explicitly. For

, the root does not contribute, and a type-

has age distributed as

with

and expected multiplicity

. Hence

\mathbb{E}_{v}\big[N_{J}([u,\infty))\big]=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\sum_{j\in J}(K^{\ell})_{jv}\mathbb{P}(S_{\ell}\geq u).

By Chernoff’s bound,

\mathbb{P}(S_{\ell}\geq u)\leq e^{-c_{L}u}\Bigl(\frac{\beta}{\beta-c_{L}}\Bigr)^{\ell}=e^{-c_{L}u}a^{\ell}.

Therefore, for

Together with the

and using (

Now we show an auxiliary lemma useful for proving the next one about conditional mgf of one block increment:

Lemma A.9
.

Let
(E,\mathcal{E})
be a standard Borel space, let
\eta
be a Poisson point process on
E
with intensity measure
\nu
, and let
F=F(\eta)
be a measurable functional with
\mathbb{E}[F]=0
. Assume there exists a deterministic measurable function
g:E\to[0,\infty)
such that for every configuration
\eta
and every point
z\in E
,

|D_{z}F(\eta)|:=|F(\eta+\delta_{z})-F(\eta)|\leq g(z).

Assume moreover that for some
\eta_{*}>0
,

\int_{E}g(z)^{2}e^{\eta_{*}g(z)}\,\nu(dz)<\infty.

Then there exist a constant
\lambda_{\mathrm{P}}>0
, depending only on
\eta_{*}
, such that

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\lambda F}\big]\leq\exp\!\left(\lambda^{2}\int_{E}g(z)^{2}e^{\eta_{*}g(z)}\,\nu(dz)\right)\qquad(|\lambda|\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{P}}).

(50)

Proof.

For
K\geq 1
, let
F^{(K)}:=(-K)\vee(F\wedge K)-\mathbb{E}[(-K)\vee(F\wedge K)]
.
The clipping map is
1
-Lipschitz, so
|D_{z}F^{(K)}|\leq g(z)
. It therefore suffices to prove the claim for bounded centered functionals and then let
K\uparrow\infty
.

Assume for the moment that
F
is bounded. Take
B\subseteq[0,1]
with
card(B)=card(E)
and s.t.
B\in\mathcal{B}([0,1])
. Since
([0,1],\mathcal{B}([0,1]))
is standard Borel, then Corollary (13.4) in
[
33
]
holds and
(B,\mathcal{B}([0,1])|_{B})
is standard Borel. By assumption
(E,\mathcal{E})
is standard Borel, then by Theorem (15.6) in
[
33
]
there exists an isomorphism
\phi:E\to B\subseteq[0,1]
such that
\phi
and
\phi^{-1}
are Borel measurable. Being
\eta
a Poisson point process with intensity
\nu
and being
\phi
Borel measurable, by Theorem (5.1) in
[
37
]
,
\phi_{\#}\eta
is again Poisson with intensity
\phi_{\#}\nu
and distributional properties are preserved.
The induced functional
\widetilde{F}(\phi_{\#}\eta):=F(\eta)
is well defined because
\phi
is a bijection and
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{F}(\phi_{\#}\eta)]=\mathbb{E}[F(\eta)]=0
. Moreover, for any
\lambda
it holds
\mathbb{E}[e^{\tilde{F}(\phi_{\#}\eta)}]=\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda F(\eta)}]
, thus any mgf bound for
\tilde{F}(\phi_{\#}\eta)
is exactly equivalent for
F(\eta)
. From the definition of pushforward:

|D_{w}\tilde{F}|=|\tilde{F}(\phi_{\#}\eta+\delta_{w})-\tilde{F}(\phi_{\#}\eta)|=|F(\eta+\delta_{z})-F(\eta)|\leq g(z)=(g\,\circ\,\phi^{-1})(w)=\tilde{g}(w)

so the add-one bound is preserved by redefining
g
. Lastly note that:

\int_{B}\tilde{g}(w)^{2}e^{\eta_{*}\tilde{g}(w)}(\phi_{\#}\nu)(dw)=\int_{E}\tilde{g}(\phi(z))^{2}e^{\eta_{*}\tilde{g}(\phi(z))}\nu(dz)=\int_{E}g(z)^{2}e^{\eta_{*}g(z)}\nu(dz)<\infty.

So it is possible to prove Lemma
A.9
over the transported system
(B,\mathcal{B}([0,1])|_{B},\phi_{\#}\eta,\tilde{F},\tilde{g},\phi_{\#}\nu)
, renaming it as the original one
(E,\mathcal{E},\eta,F,g,\nu)
.

Now, realize
\eta
as the time-
1
section of a marked Poisson process
\bar{\eta}
on
E\times[0,1]
with intensity
\nu(dz)\,ds
. Write

\eta_{s}:=\bar{\eta}(\cdot\times[0,s]),\qquad\eta^{s}:=\bar{\eta}(\cdot\times(s,1]),\qquad\mathcal{G}_{s}:=\sigma\!\bigl(\bar{\eta}|_{E\times[0,s]}\bigr),

and define the bounded Doob martingale
M_{s}:=\mathbb{E}[F(\eta_{s}+\eta^{s})\mid\mathcal{G}_{s}]=\mathbb{E}[F(\eta)\mid\mathcal{G}_{s}]
, then
M_{0}=\mathbb{E}[F]=0
and
M_{1}=F
. If a marked point
(z,s)
appears at time
s
, the jump of
M
is

J_{s}(z):=\mathbb{E}\Big[F(\eta_{s-}+\delta_{z}+\eta^{s})-F(\eta_{s-}+\eta^{s})\,\Big|\,\mathcal{G}_{s-},z\Big],

hence
|J_{s}(z)|\leq g(z)
and, because
F
is bounded, the martingale
M
is square-integrable. For the completed natural filtration
(\mathcal{G}_{s})
of the Poisson random measure
N(ds,dz):=\bar{\eta}(ds,dz)
, the Theorem (6.7) II in
[
28
]
yields a predictable integrand
H\in L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,1]\times E,d\mathbb{P}\,ds\,\nu(dz))
such that

M_{t}=\int_{(0,t]\times E}H_{s}(z)\,\widetilde{N}(ds,dz),\qquad\widetilde{N}(ds,dz):=N(ds,dz)-\nu(dz)\,ds.

At a jump time
s
of
N
with mark
z
, from Doob martingale definition

\Delta M_{s}=\mathbb{E}\Big[F(\eta_{s-}+\delta_{z}+\eta^{s})-(\eta_{s-}+\eta^{s})\,\Big|\,\mathcal{G}_{s-},z\Big]=J_{s}(z).

At same time, the stochastic integral representation has jump size
H_{s}(z)
. By uniqueness of the predictable representation,
H_{s}(z)=J_{s}(z)
for
d\mathbb{P}\,ds\,\nu(dz)
-a.e.
(\omega,s,z)
, and therefore

M_{t}=\int_{(0,t]\times E}J_{s}(z)\,\widetilde{N}(ds,dz).

Apply Itô formula to

(Proposition 8.19
[
19
]
), take expectations and use

:

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda M_{t}}]\leq 1+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda M_{s-}}]\left(\int_{E}\bigl(e^{|\lambda|g(z)}-1-|\lambda|g(z)\bigr)\,\nu(dz)\right)ds.

Note that for

,

0\leq e^{|\lambda|g(z)}-1-|\lambda|g(z)\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}g(z)^{2}e^{|\lambda|g(z)}\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}g(z)^{2}e^{\eta_{*}g(z)},

so the compensator term is finite by the integrability assumption

. Since

is bounded, also

almost surely for every

. Thus the function

is finite on

, and Gronwall’s inequality applies to give

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda F}]\leq\exp\!\left(\int_{E}\bigl(e^{|\lambda|g(z)}-1-|\lambda|g(z)\bigr)\,\nu(dz)\right)\qquad(\lambda\in\mathbb{R}).

Now choose
\lambda_{\mathrm{P}}:=\eta_{*}/2
. For

, again
e^{y}-1-y\leq y^{2}e^{y}
, so for
|\lambda|\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{P}}
,

e^{|\lambda|g(z)}-1-|\lambda|g(z)\leq\lambda^{2}g(z)^{2}e^{|\lambda|g(z)}\leq\lambda^{2}g(z)^{2}e^{\eta_{*}g(z)}.

Substituting this into the previous display gives (
50
) for bounded centered
F
. Finally apply the bounded case to
F^{(K)}
. Because

almost surely and in

, Fatou’s lemma for

yields

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda F}]\leq\liminf_{K\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda F^{(K)}}]\leq\exp\!\left(\lambda^{2}\int_{E}g(z)^{2}e^{\eta_{*}g(z)}\,\nu(dz)\right).

\int g(z)^{2}e^{\eta_{1}g(z)}\,\nu_{q}(dz)=\int_{I_{q}}\sum_{v=1}^{d}\mu_{v}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\Gamma_{\xi}(v,t)^{2}e^{\eta_{1}\Gamma_{\xi}(v,t)}\Big]dt=V_{q}(\xi).

Since Lemma
A.9
gives
\lambda_{\mathrm{P}}=\eta_{1}(h,R)/2=c_{\eta}h/(2R)
, (
51
) holds with
c_{\lambda}:=c_{\eta}/2
, i.e. the admissible range is
|\lambda|\leq c_{\lambda}h/R
.
∎

We now bound the aggregated second-order exponential moments of the one-cluster envelopes
\Gamma_{\xi}
:

Lemma A.11
.

Assume
0<h\leq h_{0}
, and
R\geq 1
. Then there exist constants
c_{\eta}>0
,
c_{\Gamma}>0
, and
C_{\Gamma}>0
, depending only on
(\beta,\mu_{+},k,w_{+},\gamma,h_{0})
, such that with
\eta_{1}(h,R):=c_{\eta}h/R
,
one has, uniformly over all
d\geq 1
, all
B\in\mathcal{B}_{d,k}
, all indices, and all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{d}\mu_{v}b_{Z_{j}}(v,t)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Gamma}h^{-2}e^{-c_{\Gamma}(-t)_{+}},

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{d}\mu_{v}b_{Y_{i}}(v,t)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Gamma}e^{-c_{\Gamma}(-t)_{+}},

(52)

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{d}\mu_{v}b_{Z_{j}Y_{i}}(v,t)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Gamma}\bigl(h^{-2}+R^{2}\bigr)e^{-c_{\Gamma}(-t)_{+}},

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{d}\mu_{v}b_{Z_{a}Z_{b}}(v,t)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Gamma}R^{2}h^{-2}e^{-c_{\Gamma}(-t)_{+}}.

(53)

Proof.

For
Y_{i}
, if the root is born at time
t
, then every contributing type-
i
event must occur at age at least
u:=(-t)_{+}
. Because
N_{\{i\},u}^{\rm tail}
counts events in the full cluster, including the root when
v=i
and
u=0
, this bound also covers the case
t\geq 0
in which the only contribution may come from the root itself. Thus
\Gamma_{Y_{i}}(x)\leq N_{\{i\},u}^{\rm tail}
. Applying (
45
) gives the first in (
52
).

For
Z_{j}
, first suppose
t\geq 0
. Then, recall the first definition in (
48
), then every type-
j
event contributes at most
C_{h}\leq C/h
to the sampled sum (see definition (
35
)), so considering every type-
j
event
\Gamma_{Z_{j}}(x)\leq C_{h}N_{\{j\},0}^{\rm tail}
. Here again
N_{\{j\},0}^{\rm tail}
counts the root itself when
v=j
, so the bound remains valid even if the cluster has no offspring at all. Then (
45
) yields the bound
\sum_{v}\mu_{v}b_{Z_{j}}(v,t)\leq Ch^{-2}
. Now suppose
t<0
and write
u:=-t>0
. Split the type-
j
events of the cluster according to their ages: events with age at least
u
satisfy
t+s\geq 0
, so we only use
a_{h}(t+s)\leq C_{h}
. For the remaining events, ages in the strip

[u-m-1,u-m)\cap[0,\infty),\qquad m\geq 0,

satisfy
t+s\in[-m-1,-m)
and therefore
a_{h}(t+s)\leq C_{h}e^{-\beta m}
. This includes the first partial window
[0,u)
when
0<u<1
, namely the case
m=0
. Hence

\Gamma_{Z_{j}}(x)\leq C_{h}N_{\{j\},u}^{\rm tail}+C_{h}\sum_{m\geq 0}e^{-\beta m}N_{\{j\},u-m-1}^{\rm win}=:\sum_{r\geq 0}w_{r}A_{r}.

(54)

where in the last definition we set

A_{0}:=N_{\{j\},u}^{\rm tail},\qquad A_{m+1}:=N_{\{j\},u-m-1}^{\rm win},\quad w_{0}:=C_{h},\qquad w_{m+1}:=C_{h}e^{-\beta m}\quad(m\geq 0).

Then let

W_{h}:=\sum_{r\geq 0}w_{r}=C_{h}\Bigl(1+\sum_{m\geq 0}e^{-\beta m}\Bigr)\leq C_{\beta}h^{-1}.

For
\phi_{\eta,W}(y):=y^{2}e^{\eta Wy}
, convexity of
\phi_{\eta,W}
on
[0,\infty)
, and Jensen’s inequality for the probability weights

give

\Bigl(\sum_{r\geq 0}w_{r}A_{r}\Bigr)^{2}e^{\eta\sum_{r\geq 0}w_{r}A_{r}}=W_{h}^{2}\,\phi_{\eta,W_{h}}\!\Bigl(\sum_{r\geq 0}p_{r}A_{r}\Bigr)\leq W_{h}^{2}\sum_{r\geq 0}p_{r}\phi_{\eta,W_{h}}(A_{r})=W_{h}\sum_{r\geq 0}w_{r}A_{r}^{2}e^{\eta W_{h}A_{r}}.

Applying this with
\eta=\eta_{1}
and using
W_{h}\leq C_{\beta}h^{-1}
yields

\Gamma_{Z_{j}}(x)^{2}e^{\eta_{1}\Gamma_{Z_{j}}(x)}\leq Ch^{-2}\left((N_{\{j\},u}^{\rm tail})^{2}e^{c_{h}\eta_{1}N_{\{j\},u}^{\rm tail}}+\sum_{m\geq 0}e^{-\beta m}(N_{\{j\},u-m-1}^{\rm win})^{2}e^{c_{h}\eta_{1}N_{\{j\},u-m-1}^{\rm win}}\right),

for some
c_{h}\asymp W_{h}\lesssim h^{-1}
. Let
\eta_{\rm C}>0
be the exponential-moment parameter from Lemma
A.7
. In order to apply Lemma
A.7
, we need
c_{h}\eta_{1}
sufficiently small. With
\eta_{1}(h,R):=c_{\eta}h/R
and
W_{h}\leq C_{\beta}h^{-1}
, one has
c_{h}\eta_{1}(h,R)\leq C_{\beta}\eta_{1}(h,R)/h=C_{\beta}c_{\eta}/R\leq C_{\beta}c_{\eta}
uniformly in
R\geq 1
and
h\in(0,h_{0}]
. It therefore suffices to choose

c_{\eta}\leq\min\!\left\{\frac{\eta_{\rm C}}{4C_{\beta}},\,\frac{\eta_{\rm C}}{2h_{0}}\right\}
, which depends only on
(\beta,\mu_{+},k,w_{+},\gamma,h_{0})
. With this choice, summing over
v
, using Lemma
A.7
and decreasing
c_{\Gamma}>0
if necessary lead to

\sum_{v=1}^{d}\mu_{v}b_{Z_{j}}(v,t)\leq Ch^{-2}\left(e^{-c_{\Gamma}u}+\sum_{m\geq 0}e^{-\beta m}e^{-c_{\Gamma}(u-m-1)_{+}}\right)\leq Ch^{-2}e^{-c_{\Gamma}u}

For the mixed
Z_{j}Y_{i}
, write
a:=2\Gamma_{Z_{j}}
and
b:=R\Gamma_{Y_{i}}
and apply
(a+b)^{2}e^{\eta_{1}(a+b)}\leq C(a^{2}e^{2\eta_{1}a}+b^{2}e^{2\eta_{1}b})
. The first term
4\Gamma_{Z_{j}}^{2}e^{4\eta_{1}\Gamma_{Z_{j}}}
is handled exactly as in the
Z_{j}
case, since the effective parameter
4c_{h}\eta_{1}=4C_{\beta}c_{\eta}/R\leq\eta_{\rm C}
. The second term
R^{2}\Gamma_{Y_{i}}^{2}e^{2R\eta_{1}\Gamma_{Y_{i}}}
has exponent
2R\eta_{1}=2R\cdot c_{\eta}h/R=2c_{\eta}h\leq\eta_{\rm C}
; since
\Gamma_{Y_{i}}\leq N_{\{i\},u}^{\rm tail}
, Lemma
A.7
applies and
R^{2}
gives the bound
C_{\Gamma}(h^{-2}+R^{2})e^{-c_{\Gamma}(-t)_{+}}
in (
53
). For
Z_{a}Z_{b}
, set
a:=2R\Gamma_{Z_{a}}
and
b:=2R\Gamma_{Z_{b}}
; the effective parameter for each
\Gamma_{Z_{a}}
term is
4Rc_{h}\eta_{1}=4R\cdot(C_{\beta}/h)\cdot(c_{\eta}h/R)=4C_{\beta}c_{\eta}\leq\eta_{\rm C}
, so Lemma
A.7
applies and yields the bound
C_{\Gamma}R^{2}h^{-2}e^{-c_{\Gamma}(-t)_{+}}
in (
53
).
∎

We now sum the profile bounds of Lemma
A.11
over all root blocks to obtain the total variance proxy
\sum_{q}V_{q}(\xi)
needed by the next bounded-block concentration lemma.

Proposition A.7
.

Assume
0<h\leq h_{0}
,
R\geq 1
, and
T\geq 1
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
, depending only on
(h,R,\beta,\mu_{+},k,w_{+},\rho,h_{0})
, such that uniformly over all
d\geq 1
, all
B\in\mathcal{B}_{d,k}
, and all indices,

\displaystyle\sum_{q=-\infty}^{n-1}V_{q}(Z_{j})

\displaystyle\leq C\,\frac{n}{h},

\displaystyle\sum_{q=-\infty}^{n-1}V_{q}(Y_{i})

\displaystyle\leq C\,nh,

(55)

\displaystyle\sum_{q=-\infty}^{n-1}V_{q}(Z_{j}Y_{i})

\displaystyle\leq C\,n\Bigl(\frac{1}{h}+R^{2}h\Bigr),

\displaystyle\sum_{q=-\infty}^{n-1}V_{q}(Z_{a}Z_{b})

\displaystyle\leq C\,n\,\frac{R^{2}}{h}

(56)

Proof.

Recall the definition for
V_{q}(\xi)
given in Lemma
A.10
. For
q=0,\dots,n-1
, by Lemma
A.11
:

V_{q}(Z_{j})\leq Ch^{-1},\qquad V_{q}(Y_{i})\leq Ch,\qquad V_{q}(Z_{j}Y_{i})\leq C(h^{-1}+R^{2}h),\qquad V_{q}(Z_{a}Z_{b})\leq CR^{2}h^{-1}.

For
q<0
, write
t\in[qh,(q+1)h)
so that
(-t)_{+}\asymp|q|h
and therefore

\displaystyle V_{q}(Z_{j})

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{-1}e^{-c|q|h},\quad

\displaystyle V_{q}(Y_{i})

\displaystyle\leq Che^{-c|q|h},

\displaystyle V_{q}(Z_{j}Y_{i})

\displaystyle\leq C(h^{-1}+R^{2}h)e^{-c|q|h},\quad

\displaystyle V_{q}(Z_{a}Z_{b})

\displaystyle\leq CR^{2}h^{-1}e^{-c|q|h}.

Summing the geometric tails and using
T\geq 1
, hence
n=\lfloor T/h\rfloor\gtrsim h^{-1}
, gives

\displaystyle\sum_{q=-\infty}^{n-1}V_{q}(Z_{j})

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{n}{h}+\sum_{m\geq 1}h^{-1}e^{-cmh}\right)\leq C\left(\frac{n}{h}+\frac{1}{h^{2}}\right)\leq C\frac{n}{h},

\displaystyle\sum_{q=-\infty}^{n-1}V_{q}(Y_{i})

\displaystyle\leq C\left(nh+\sum_{m\geq 1}he^{-cmh}\right)\leq C(nh+1)\leq Cnh,

and the two product bounds follow in the same way.
∎

Now, we are ready to state the central result for this section: the lemmas above show that each individual cluster has a limited impact on the empirical averages (Lemma
A.8
and Lemma
A.10
), and that this impact decays as the cluster ages (Lemma
A.6
and Lemma
A.11
). Combining these bounds across all clusters and all time blocks, the following proposition shows that every empirical average used by the estimator concentrates around its true value, with a tail probability that decays exponentially in
n
. This is the key statistical guarantee that will allow the estimator to succeed with high probability once
n
is large enough.

Proposition A.8
.

Assume
0<h\leq h_{0}
,
R\geq 1
, and
T\geq 1
. Then there exist constants
c,u_{0}>0
, depending on
(h,R,\beta,\mu_{+},k,w_{+},\gamma,h_{0})
, such that uniformly over all
d\geq 1
, all
B\in\mathcal{B}_{d,k}
, and all choices of
(\xi_{r})
from the family (
32
), the following holds for every
u
with
0<u\leq u_{0}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}(Z_{j,r}^{(R)}-\mathbb{E}Z_{j,0}^{(R)})\right|>u\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-cnhu^{2}\right),

(57)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}(Y_{i,r}^{(h)}-\mathbb{E}Y_{i,0}^{(h)})\right|>u\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-cn\frac{u^{2}}{h}\right),

(58)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}(Z_{j,r}^{(R)}Y_{i,r}^{(h)}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{j,0}^{(R)}Y_{i,0}^{(h)}])\right|>u\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-cn\frac{u^{2}}{h^{-1}+R^{2}h}\right),

(59)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}(Z_{a,r}^{(R)}Z_{b,r}^{(R)}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{a,0}^{(R)}Z_{b,0}^{(R)}])\right|>u\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-cn\frac{u^{2}}{R^{2}h^{-1}}\right).

(60)

Proof.

Let
S_{n}
as in (
47
) and
D_{q}
as in Lemma
A.10
with
q\leq n-1
. For
M\geq 1
, set

S_{n}^{(M)}:=\sum_{q=-M}^{n-1}D_{q}=\mathbb{E}[S_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}]-\mathbb{E}[S_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{-M-1}].

By Lemma
A.10
,
\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda D_{q}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{q-1}]\leq\exp\big(\lambda^{2}V_{q}(\xi)\big)
with
|\lambda|\leq c_{\lambda}h/R
. Iterating for
q=-M,\dots,n-1
gives

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda S_{n}^{(M)}}]\leq\exp\Big(\lambda^{2}\sum_{q=-M}^{n-1}V_{q}(\xi)\Big).

By Proposition
A.7
,
\sum_{q=-\infty}^{n-1}V_{q}(\xi)<\infty
. Moreover, differentiating (
51
) at
\lambda=0
shows
\mathbb{E}[D_{q}^{2}]\leq V_{q}(\xi)
, so
\sum_{q=-\infty}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[D_{q}^{2}]<\infty
. In particular
(S_{n}^{(M)})_{M\geq 1}
is Cauchy in
L^{2}
. To identify its limit, note that
S_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
-measurable and
\mathbb{E}[S_{n}]=0
, hence

S_{n}^{(M)}=\mathbb{E}[S_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}]-\mathbb{E}[S_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{-M-1}]=S_{n}-\mathbb{E}[S_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{-M-1}].

Note that
(\mathcal{F}_{-M-1})_{M\geq 1}
decrease to the tail sigma-field of the independent root blocks
(U_{q})_{q\in\mathbb{Z}}

\mathcal{F}_{-\infty}:=\bigcap_{M\geq 1}\mathcal{F}_{-M-1},

By the reverse martingale convergence theorem,

\mathbb{E}[S_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{-M-1}]\to\mathbb{E}[S_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{-\infty}]
in
L^{2}
and by Kolmogorov’s
0
-
1
law the tail sigma-field
\mathcal{F}_{-\infty}
is trivial, so
\mathbb{E}[S_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{-\infty}]=\mathbb{E}[S_{n}]=0
. Taking
|2\lambda|\leq c_{\lambda}h/R
, the same mgf bound applied with the sign-correct parameter
2\lambda
gives

\sup_{M}\mathbb{E}[e^{2\lambda S_{n}^{(M)}}]\leq\exp\Big(4\lambda^{2}\sum_{q=-\infty}^{n-1}V_{q}(\xi)\Big)<\infty.

where, for simplicity on exposition, we choose to introduce a suitable positive constant
C
instead of a numerical factor.
Thus, the family
\{e^{\lambda S_{n}^{(M)}}\}_{M}
is bounded in
L^{2}
and therefore uniformly integrable. Therefore

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda S_{n}}]=\lim_{M\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda S_{n}^{(M)}}]\leq\exp\Big(\lambda^{2}\sum_{q=-\infty}^{n-1}V_{q}(\xi)\Big).

In order to have
\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda S_{n}}]\leq e^{K_{\xi}\lambda^{2}}
with
|\lambda|\leq c_{\lambda}h/(2R)
, write

K_{\xi}:=\sum_{q=-\infty}^{n-1}V_{q}(\xi).

For any
\lambda\in[0,c_{\lambda}h/(2R)]
, Chernoff’s bound gives
\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{n}/n>u\right)\leq\exp\!\bigl(-\lambda nu+K_{\xi}\lambda^{2}\bigr)
. Choose
\lambda=nu/2K_{\xi}
(we can do this whenever
u\leq u_{0}
, as we will see in a moment), then

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{S_{n}}{n}>u\right)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{n^{2}u^{2}}{4K_{\xi}}\right),

and the same bound holds for the lower tail. Proposition
A.7
gives

K_{Z_{j}}\lesssim\frac{n}{h},\qquad K_{Y_{i}}\lesssim nh,\qquad K_{Z_{j}Y_{i}}\lesssim n(h^{-1}+R^{2}h),\qquad K_{Z_{a}Z_{b}}\lesssim\frac{nR^{2}}{h},

with constants depending only on
(h,R,\beta,\mu_{+},k,w_{+},\rho,h_{0})
. Thus the four displayed tail bounds (
57
)–(
60
) follow once

u\leq u_{0}:=\frac{c_{\lambda}h}{2R}\min\!\left\{\frac{1}{h},\ h,\ h^{-1}+R^{2}h,\ \frac{R^{2}}{h}\right\},

after absorbing fixed factors into the constant
c
.
∎

Next lemma translates Proposition
A.8
into simultaneous high-probability control over all empirical screening statistics
\widehat{F}_{ij}^{(h,R)}
, Gram matrices
\widehat{\Sigma}_{C}^{(R)}
, and score vectors
\widehat{g}_{i,C}^{(h,R)}
across all
d^{2}
index pairs and all candidate sets of size at most
m
, via a union bound costing a
\log d
factor.

Lemma A.12
.

Fix
0<h\leq 1
,
R\geq 1
, and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. There exist constants
C_{\rm prob},c_{\rm prob},C_{0}>0
, depending only on
(m,h,R,\beta,\mu_{+},k,w_{+},\gamma,h_{0})
, such that for every
d
, every
B\in\mathcal{B}_{d,k}
, and every
T\geq 1
satisfying

n\geq C_{0}\max\!\left\{\frac{h^{-1}+R^{2}h}{u_{0}(h,R)^{2}},\ \frac{R^{2}}{h\,u_{0}(h,R)^{2}}\right\}\log d,

(61)

with probability at least
1-d^{-c_{\rm prob}}
the following hold simultaneously:

(i)

for all rows
i
and all coordinates
j
,

\lvert\widehat{F}_{ij}^{(h,R)}-F_{ij}^{(h,R)}\rvert\leq C_{\rm prob}\sqrt{\frac{(h^{-1}+R^{2}h)\log d}{n}};

(62)

(ii)

for all candidate sets
C
with
|C|\leq m
,

\lVert\widehat{\Sigma}_{C}^{(R)}-\Sigma_{C}^{(R)}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C_{\rm prob}R\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{nh}};

(63)

(iii)

for all rows
i
and all candidate sets
C
with
|C|\leq m
,

\lVert\widehat{g}_{i,C}^{(h,R)}-g_{i,C}^{(h,R)}\rVert_{\infty}\leq C_{\rm prob}\sqrt{\frac{(h^{-1}+R^{2}h)\log d}{n}}.

(64)

Proof.

(i) Under (
61
), the screening/score deviation level
\sqrt{(h^{-1}+R^{2}h)(\log d)/n}
and the Gram deviation level
R\sqrt{(\log d)/(nh)}
are both at most
u_{0}(h,R)
after enlarging
C_{0}
if necessary. Hence Proposition
A.8
is applicable in each instance. For screening, write

\hat{m}_{ZY}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}Z_{j,r}^{(R)}Y_{i,r}^{(h)},\qquad\hat{m}_{Z}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}Z_{j,r}^{(R)},\qquad\hat{m}_{Y}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}Y_{i,r}^{(h)},

and similarly
m_{ZY},m_{Z},m_{Y}
for the expectations. Then by the definition at the beginning of Appendix
A.2
,
\widehat{F}_{ij}^{(h,R)}-F_{ij}^{(h,R)}=(\hat{m}_{ZY}-m_{ZY})-(\hat{m}_{Z}\hat{m}_{Y}-m_{Z}m_{Y})
. By Proposition
A.8
,

|\hat{m}_{ZY}-m_{ZY}|\leq C\sqrt{\frac{(h^{-1}+R^{2}h)\log d}{n}},\qquad|\hat{m}_{Z}-m_{Z}|\leq C\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{nh}},\qquad|\hat{m}_{Y}-m_{Y}|\leq C\sqrt{\frac{h\log d}{n}}.

Also
m_{Y}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,0}^{(h)}]\leq Ch
and
|\hat{m}_{Z}|\leq R
. Hence summing and subtracting
\hat{m}_{Z}m_{Y}
in
|\hat{m}_{Z}\hat{m}_{Y}-m_{Z}m_{Y}|
, using the bounds just obtained and the fact that

).

(ii) Now for the Gram matrix, write

\hat{M}_{ab}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}Z_{a,r}^{(R)}Z_{b,r}^{(R)},\qquad M_{ab}:=\mathbb{E}[Z_{a,0}^{(R)}Z_{b,0}^{(R)}],

and let
\hat{m}_{a},m_{a}
be the empirical and theoretical means of

. Then as before,
(\widehat{\Sigma}_{C}^{(R)}-\Sigma_{C}^{(R)})_{ab}=(\hat{M}_{ab}-M_{ab})-(\hat{m}_{a}\hat{m}_{b}-m_{a}m_{b})
. Now Proposition
A.8
yields

|\hat{M}_{ab}-M_{ab}|\leq CR\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{nh}},\qquad|\hat{m}_{a}-m_{a}|\leq C\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{nh}}.

Therefore

|(\widehat{\Sigma}_{C}^{(R)}-\Sigma_{C}^{(R)})_{ab}|\leq CR\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{nh}}+2R\cdot C\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{nh}}\leq CR\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{nh}}.

Since
|C|\leq m
is fixed, (
63
) follows.

(iii) For the score vector, write

\hat{s}_{a}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}Z_{a,r}^{(R)}Y_{i,r}^{(h)},\qquad s_{a}:=\mathbb{E}[Z_{a,0}^{(R)}Y_{i,0}^{(h)}].

Then

(\widehat{g}_{i,C}^{(h,R)}-g_{i,C}^{(h,R)})_{a}=(\hat{s}_{a}-s_{a})-(\hat{m}_{a}\hat{m}_{Y}-m_{a}m_{Y}),

so the same bounds as in the screening case give (

). Finally, there are at most
d^{2}
screening pairs
(i,j)
, at most
\sum_{r=0}^{m}\binom{d}{r}\leq C_{m}d^{m}
candidate sets
C
, and at most
d\sum_{r=0}^{m}\binom{d}{r}\leq C_{m}d^{m+1}
row-model pairs
(i,C)
. A union bound completes the proof.
∎

A.5
Sure screening and exact recovery

Proposition A.9
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
A.5
and Lemma
A.12
, there exists a constant
C_{\mathrm{scr}}>0
such that if

n\geq C_{\mathrm{scr}}\frac{h^{-1}+R^{2}h}{\alpha^{2}h^{2}}\log d,

(65)

then with probability at least
1-d^{-c}
one has
S_{i}\subset\widehat{C}_{i}
for every
i=1,\dots,d
.

Proof.

On the event (
62
), adding and subtracting
\min_{j\in S_{i}}F_{ij}
and
\max_{j\notin S_{i}}F_{ij}
and using conveniently the elementary inequality
|a_{j}-b_{j}|\geq a_{j}-b_{j}
, we get

\min_{j\in S_{i}}\widehat{F}_{ij}^{(h,R)}-\max_{j\notin S_{i}}\widehat{F}_{ij}^{(h,R)}\geq\min_{j\in S_{i}}F_{ij}^{(h,R)}-\max_{j\notin S_{i}}F_{ij}^{(h,R)}-2C_{\rm prob}\sqrt{\frac{(h^{-1}+R^{2}h)\log d}{n}}.

By Proposition
A.5
, the theoretical screening gap is at least
c_{\mathrm{scr}}\alpha h
. Thus if

2C_{\rm prob}\sqrt{\frac{(h^{-1}+R^{2}h)\log d}{n}}\leq\frac{1}{2}c_{\mathrm{scr}}\alpha h,

then every support index outranks every non-support index. Since
m\geq k\geq|S_{i}|
, the top-
m
empirical screening set contains
S_{i}
. This is implied by (
65
) after enlarging the constant.
∎

Proposition A.10
.

Under the assumptions in Lemma
A.4
, Proposition
A.6
, and Lemma
A.12
, there exists a constant
C_{ols}>0
such that if

n\geq C_{ols}\max\!\left\{\frac{R^{2}}{h},\frac{h^{-1}+R^{2}h}{\alpha^{2}h^{2}}\right\}\log d,

(66)

then with probability at least

the following holds simultaneously for all rows
i
and all candidate sets
C\supset S_{i}
with
|C|\leq m
:

\|\widehat{y}_{i,C}^{(h,R)}-y_{i,C}^{(h,R)}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{ols}\sqrt{\frac{(h^{-1}+R^{2}h)\log d}{n}}.

(67)

In particular, on the sure-screening event, the same bound holds for
C=\widehat{C}_{i}
for every

Appendix B
An information-theoretic view of support recovery

We briefly introduce the information-theoretic perspective of support recovery that is used in the lower bound.
We do not derive any new statements, but instead aim to explain why entropy, mutual information, KL divergence, and Fano’s inequality naturally appear in exact support recovery.

Information theory studies how much can be learned about an unknown object from noisy observations. Let
V
be a discrete random variable taking values in a finite set
\mathcal{V}
, and suppose that, conditional on
V=v
, the observation
Y
has law
P_{v}
. In communication language,
V
is the message, the stochastic mechanism
v\mapsto P_{v}
is a noisy channel, and an estimator
\widehat{V}=\widehat{V}(Y)
is a decoder. In a statistical problem,
the same viewpoint applies when
V
indexes a finite collection of hypotheses or models.

Support recovery fits naturally into this framework because the target is discrete. In the hard subclass used below, the unknown object is the parent set

S\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star}),\qquad|\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})|=\binom{d-1}{k}.

Thus
S
can be viewed as a message chosen from
\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})
. The Hawkes process acts as the noisy channel: conditional on
S
, it produces the observation

Y_{T}=(X(0),N_{[0,T]})

with law
P_{S}^{T}
. A support estimator
\widehat{S}=\widehat{S}(Y_{T})
is a decoder trying to infer which message was sent.

The entropy of a discrete random variable
V
is

H(V):=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{P}(V=v)\log\mathbb{P}(V=v),

where logarithms are natural, so information is measured in nats.
Entropy measures the uncertainty in
V
before observing any data.
If
V
is uniform on
\mathcal{V}
, then
H(V)=\log|\mathcal{V}|
.
Hence choosing one object among
M
equally likely alternatives requires
\log M
nats of information.
In our support-recovery problem, a uniformly chosen parent set satisfies

H(S)=\log|\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})|=\log\binom{d-1}{k}.

For fixed
k
, this quantity is of order
\log d
. This is the basic combinatorial uncertainty that the data must resolve.

After observing
Y
, the remaining uncertainty about
V
is measured by the conditional entropy
H(V\mid Y)
. The reduction in uncertainty caused by the observation is the mutual information

I(V;Y):=H(V)-H(V\mid Y).

If
Y
is independent of
V
, then
I(V;Y)=0
. If
Y
determines
V
exactly, then
I(V;Y)=H(V)
. Thus mutual information measures how much of the initial uncertainty about the message is actually transmitted through the noisy channel.

Mutual information can also be expressed using KL divergence. For probability measures
P
and
Q
,

\operatorname{KL}(P\|Q):=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\log\frac{dP}{dQ}\right],

whenever
P
is absolutely continuous with respect to
Q
. KL divergence is not a metric, but it measures statistical distinguishability: small KL divergence means that observations from
P
are hard to distinguish from observations from
Q
. If
V
has prior distribution
\pi
, and

\bar{P}:=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{v}P_{v}

is the mixture law of
Y
, then

I(V;Y)=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{v}\operatorname{KL}(P_{v}\|\bar{P}).

Thus the information carried by
Y
about
V
is the average distinguishability between the law generated by a fixed message and the overall mixture law.
To get a lower-bound, it is sufficeint to compare against a convenient reference law
Q
:

I(V;Y)\leq\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{v}\operatorname{KL}(P_{v}\|Q).

Fano’s inequality is the step that turns an information bound into an error lower bound.
The idea is the following.
Suppose
V
is uniform on a finite set
\mathcal{V}
with
M=|\mathcal{V}|
, and let
\widehat{V}=\widehat{V}(Y)
be any estimator.
If the estimator is usually correct, then after seeing
Y
there cannot be much uncertainty left about
V
: the value
\widehat{V}(Y)
almost identifies the true message.
The only substantial uncertainty comes from the event
\{\widehat{V}\neq V\}
.
On the event of no error, the message is known from the decoder’s output. On the error event, the true message may still be one of many alternatives.
Therefore a small error probability forces the conditional
entropy
H(V\mid Y)
to be small.

Fano’s inequality formalizes the converse implication. If the observation leaves large conditional entropy, then no estimator can have small error probability.
Equivalently, since

H(V\mid Y)=H(V)-I(V;Y),

if the mutual information
I(V;Y)
is much smaller than the initial uncertainty
H(V)=\log M
, then the observation has not conveyed enough information to identify the message. The finite-hypothesis form used here is

\mathbb{P}(\widehat{V}\neq V)\geq 1-\frac{I(V;Y)+\log 2}{\log M}.

The additive
\log 2
term accounts for the binary uncertainty of whether an error occurred, and is negligible when
M
is large.
The main message of the inequality is that reliable identification among
M
nearly hidden possibilities requires nearly
\log M
nats of mutual information.

This average-error statement immediately gives a minimax lower bound. For any prior
\pi
,

\sup_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{P}_{v}(\widehat{V}\neq v)\geq\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{v}\mathbb{P}_{v}(\widehat{V}\neq v).

Hence, if Fano’s inequality shows that the prior-average error is bounded away from zero, then every estimator must fail with non-negligible probability for at least one hypothesis in the class.

In our Hawkes setting, the message set is

\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star}),\qquad M=\binom{d-1}{k}

and for fixed
k
,
\log M\asymp\log d
. We show that the observation up to time
T
carries at most

C_{\mathrm{init}}+C_{\mathrm{path}}T

nats of information about this support, with constants independent of
d
and
T
. Fano’s inequality then compares the information available from the data to the information needed to identify the support.
If
T=o(\log d)
, the data contains too little information to distinguish among the possible parent sets, and the probability of an error is bounded away from zero.

The proof is structured in four parts. In Section

C.1
Preparatory lemmas

First, we prove the chain-rule for the Kullback–Leibler divergence.

Lemma C.1
.

For every
S\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})
,

\operatorname{KL}(P_{S}^{T}\|P_{0}^{T})=\operatorname{KL}(\pi_{S}\|\pi_{0})+\int\operatorname{KL}\!\bigl(K_{S}^{T}(x,\cdot)\|K_{0}^{T}(x,\cdot)\bigr)\,\pi_{S}(dx),

with equality in
[0,\infty]
.

Proof.

This is the standard chain rule for relative entropy of probability kernels. When
P_{S}^{T}\ll P_{0}^{T}
, write

P_{S}^{T}(dx,d\eta)=\pi_{S}(dx)K_{S}^{T}(x,d\eta),\qquad P_{0}^{T}(dx,d\eta)=\pi_{0}(dx)K_{0}^{T}(x,d\eta),

and expand the Radon–Nikodym derivative. The extended-real case follows by the usual approximation argument for relative entropy of
kernels.
∎

The next lemma bounds the mutual information by the average KL to a baseline.

Lemma C.2
.

Let
V
be uniform on a finite set
\mathcal{V}
, and let

be an

I(V;Y)\leq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\operatorname{KL}(P_{v}\|Q).

Proof.

Let
\bar{P}:=|\mathcal{V}|^{-1}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}P_{v}
be the mixture law.
Then

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{v}\operatorname{KL}(P_{v}\|Q)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{v}\operatorname{KL}(P_{v}\|\bar{P})+\operatorname{KL}(\bar{P}\|Q)=I(V;Y)+\operatorname{KL}(\bar{P}\|Q),

follows by writing

\log(dP_{v}/dQ)=\log(dP_{v}/d\bar{P})+\log(d\bar{P}/dQ)
,
integrating under

, and averaging over

.
∎

The next result collects several properties of the density of the stationary law of
one-dimensional Poisson shot noise. It is later used to show that translated stationary densities have finite KL cost.

For
r>0
, let
\pi_{r}^{\mathrm{P}}
be the stationary law of

, where

is Poisson with rate

Equivalently,

U_{r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\int_{(-\infty,0]}e^{\beta s}\,dM_{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta t}\,dM_{t}

and its characteristic function is

\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{itU_{r}}\bigr]=\exp\!\left(\frac{r}{\beta}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{e^{itu}-1}{u}\,du\right),\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(68)

Thus
U_{r}
is infinitely divisible with Lévy density
\nu_{r}(du)=(r/\beta)\mathbf{1}_{(0,1)}(u)u^{-1}\,du
.

Lemma C.3
.

Fix
r>0
and set
\alpha=r/\beta
. The law
\pi_{r}^{\mathrm{P}}
has a density

q_{r}
on
(0,\infty)
such that:

(i)

q_{r}
is strictly positive on
(0,\infty)
and unimodal.

(ii)

There exists a constant
c_{r}>0
such that

q_{r}(x)=c_{r}x^{\alpha-1}\quad\text{for Lebesgue-a.e. }x\in(0,1).

(iii)

There exists a finite constant
C_{r}
such that for Lebesgue-a.e.

-\log q_{r}(x)\leq C_{r}\bigl(1+x\log(1+x)+|\log x|\bigr).

(69)

(iv)

U_{r}
has exponential moments of every order,

\mathbb{E}[e^{\eta U_{r}}]=\exp\!\left(\frac{r}{\beta}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{e^{\eta u}-1}{u}\,du\right)<\infty,\qquad\eta>0,

(70)

and hence

\mathbb{E}[U_{r}\log(1+U_{r})]<\infty
,
\mathbb{E}[|\log U_{r}|]<\infty
, and

\int q_{r}|\log q_{r}|<\infty
.

Proof.

The Lévy measure has density
k(u)u^{-1}\,du
with

k(u)=\alpha\mathbf{1}_{(0,1)}(u)
nonincreasing. Sato

[
55
, Theorem 15.10 and Corollary 15.11]
therefore implies
that
U_{r}
is self-decomposable. Absolute continuity, strict positivity on

(0,\infty)
, and unimodality then follow from Sato

[
55
, Theorem 27.13, Lemma 53.1, and Theorem 53.1]
. This proves
(i).

For (ii), stationarity gives, for every

\int_{0}^{\infty}\{-\beta xf^{\prime}(x)+r(f(x+1)-f(x))\}q_{r}(x)\,dx=0.

Taking

removes the jump-in term and yields

\beta(xq_{r})^{\prime}-rq_{r}=0

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}((0,1))

(x^{1-\alpha}q_{r})^{\prime}=0

After redefining

on a null set, we may take this identity to hold

(0,1)

For (iii), use a unimodal version of

such

is nonincreasing on

A_{n}:=\{M([0,\delta/n])=n\},\quad B_{n}:=\{M((\delta/n,1])=0\},\quad C:=\{U_{r}^{(3)}\in[\beta\delta,1]\},

Lemma C.4

be the coordinate permutation that puts

i_{\star}
first, then the elements of
S
, and then all other coordinates. KL is
invariant under this bijection.

Under
\Theta^{(S)}
, all coordinates outside
\{i_{\star}\}\cup S
are independent
Poisson shot-noise coordinates with stationary law
\pi_{\bar{\mu}}^{\mathrm{P}}
,
and they are independent of the target/source block. The nontrivial block

(Z_{t},\mathbf{Y}_{t}):=(X_{i_{\star}}(t),(X_{s_{1}}(t),\dots,X_{s_{k}}(t)))
satisfies

\displaystyle dY_{t}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=-\beta Y_{t}^{(m)}\,dt+dN_{t}^{(m)},\qquad m=1,\dots,k,

\displaystyle dZ_{t}

\displaystyle=-\beta Z_{t}\,dt+dM_{t},

where
N^{(1)},\dots,N^{(k)}
are independent Poisson processes of rate

\bar{\mu}
and
M
has predictable intensity

\bar{\mu}_{\star}+\theta_{-}\sum_{m=1}^{k}Y_{t-}^{(m)}
. If
\bar{\pi}_{k}
denotes the
stationary law of this block, then

R_{S}\#\pi_{S}=\bar{\pi}_{k}\otimes(\pi_{\bar{\mu}}^{\mathrm{P}})^{\otimes(d-k-1)}.

Under the baseline model,

R_{S}\#\pi_{0}=\pi_{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}^{\mathrm{P}}\otimes(\pi_{\bar{\mu}}^{\mathrm{P}})^{\otimes k}\otimes(\pi_{\bar{\mu}}^{\mathrm{P}})^{\otimes(d-k-1)}.

Product additivity of relative entropy gives

\operatorname{KL}(\pi_{S}\|\pi_{0})=\operatorname{KL}\!\bigl(\bar{\pi}_{k}\|\pi_{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}^{\mathrm{P}}\otimes(\pi_{\bar{\mu}}^{\mathrm{P}})^{\otimes k}\bigr),

and the right-hand side is independent of the particular set
S
by
exchangeability of the source coordinates.

It remains only to prove finiteness. Let

\mathcal{H}=\sigma(Y_{s}^{(m)}:s\leq 0,1\leq m\leq k)
be the stationary source
history. Conditional on
\mathcal{H}
, the target process on
(-\infty,0]
is an
inhomogeneous Poisson process with intensity

\bar{\mu}_{\star}+\theta_{-}\sum_{m}Y_{t-}^{(m)}
. Decompose it as
M=M^{(0)}+M^{(1)}
,
where, conditional on
\mathcal{H}
,
M^{(0)}
is homogeneous Poisson of rate

\bar{\mu}_{\star}
,
M^{(1)}
is Poisson with intensity

\theta_{-}\sum_{m}Y_{t-}^{(m)}\,dt
, and the two are independent. Then

Z_{0}=U+W,\qquad U:=\int_{(-\infty,0]}e^{\beta s}\,dM_{s}^{(0)},\qquad W:=\int_{(-\infty,0]}e^{\beta s}\,dM_{s}^{(1)}.

Here
U\sim\pi_{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}^{\mathrm{P}}
and
U
is independent of

(\mathcal{H},W)
.

The
\mathbf{Y}_{0}
-marginal of
\bar{\pi}_{k}
is

(\pi_{\bar{\mu}}^{\mathrm{P}})^{\otimes k}
. Hence the entropy chain rule and
conditional Jensen’s inequality give

\operatorname{KL}\!\bigl(\bar{\pi}_{k}\|\pi_{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}^{\mathrm{P}}\otimes(\pi_{\bar{\mu}}^{\mathrm{P}})^{\otimes k}\bigr)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{KL}(P_{Z_{0}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{0}}\|\pi_{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}^{\mathrm{P}})\bigr]\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{KL}(P_{Z_{0}\mid\mathcal{H}}\|\pi_{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}^{\mathrm{P}})\bigr].

Indeed, since
\mathbf{Y}_{0}
is
\mathcal{H}
-measurable,

P_{Z_{0}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{0}}
is obtained by averaging

P_{Z_{0}\mid\mathcal{H}}
over the conditional law of
\mathcal{H}
given

\mathbf{Y}_{0}
, and relative entropy is convex in its first argument.
Given
\mathcal{H}
, the conditional law of
Z_{0}
is the mixture of translates

q_{0,w}(z)\,dz
with mixing law
P_{W\mid\mathcal{H}}
. Convexity of KL and
Lemma
C.4
therefore yield

\operatorname{KL}(P_{Z_{0}\mid\mathcal{H}}\|\pi_{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}^{\mathrm{P}})\leq\mathbb{E}[g(W)\mid\mathcal{H}]\leq C\,\mathbb{E}[1+W\log(1+W)\mid\mathcal{H}].

Thus finiteness follows once
\mathbb{E}[W\log(1+W)]<\infty
.

In fact
W
has exponential moments of all orders. For
\eta>0
, the conditional
Laplace functional gives

\mathbb{E}[e^{\eta W}\mid\mathcal{H}]=\exp\!\left(\theta_{-}\int_{-\infty}^{0}(e^{\eta e^{\beta s}}-1)\sum_{m=1}^{k}Y_{s-}^{(m)}\,ds\right)\leq\exp\!\left(\theta_{-}(e^{\eta}-1)V\right),

where

V:=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{\beta s}\sum_{m=1}^{k}Y_{s-}^{(m)}\,ds.

Using the stationary shot-noise representation of the sources and Tonelli’s
theorem,

V=\sum_{m=1}^{k}\int_{(-\infty,0]}(-u)e^{\beta u}\,dN_{u}^{(m)}=\int_{(-\infty,0]}(-u)e^{\beta u}\,d\widetilde{N}_{u},

where
\widetilde{N}
is homogeneous Poisson with rate
k\bar{\mu}
. The kernel

u\mapsto(-u)e^{\beta u}\mathbf{1}_{\{u\leq 0\}}
is bounded and integrable, so
Campbell’s formula implies
\mathbb{E}[e^{cV}]<\infty
for every
c>0
. Consequently

\mathbb{E}[e^{\eta W}]<\infty
for every
\eta>0
, and in particular

\mathbb{E}[W\log(1+W)]<\infty
. This proves
C_{\mathrm{init}}<\infty
and completes
the proof.
∎

C.3
Dynamic KL bound

It remains to control the entropy accumulated over the observation window.
We start by computing the stationary second moment of the aggregated source block.

Lemma C.5
.

For
S\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})
, set

\Sigma_{S}(t):=\sum_{j\in S}X_{j}(t)
. Under
\Theta^{(S)}
,
\Sigma_{S}
is a
stationary Poisson shot-noise OU process driven by a homogeneous Poisson process
of rate
k\bar{\mu}
, and

\mathbb{E}[\Sigma_{S}(t)^{2}]=\frac{k^{2}\bar{\mu}^{2}}{\beta^{2}}+\frac{k\bar{\mu}}{2\beta}=:C_{\mathrm{path}},\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof.

The
k
parent coordinates are independent Poisson shot-noise OU processes with
rate
\bar{\mu}
, so their sum is driven by the superposed Poisson process of rate

k\bar{\mu}
. Campbell’s formula gives

\mathbb{E}[\Sigma_{S}]=k\bar{\mu}/\beta
and

\operatorname{Var}(\Sigma_{S})=k\bar{\mu}/(2\beta)
, which proves the statement for

\mathbb{E}[\Sigma_{S}^{2}]
.
∎

In the following proposition, we derive the bound on the dynamic KL term.

Proposition C.2
.

For every
S\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})
,

\int\operatorname{KL}\!\bigl(K_{S}^{T}(x,\cdot)\|K_{0}^{T}(x,\cdot)\bigr)\,\pi_{S}(dx)\leq\frac{\theta_{-}^{2}}{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}\,C_{\mathrm{path}}\,T.

Consequently,

\operatorname{KL}(P_{S}^{T}\|P_{0}^{T})\leq C_{\mathrm{init}}+\frac{\theta_{-}^{2}}{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}\,C_{\mathrm{path}}\,T.

Proof.

Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Under model
S
, the intensity is

\lambda_{i_{\star}}^{(S)}(t)=\bar{\mu}_{\star}+\theta_{-}\sum_{j\in S}X_{j}(t-),\qquad\lambda_{i}^{(S)}(t)\equiv\bar{\mu}\quad(i\neq i_{\star}),

whereas under the baseline model

\lambda_{i_{\star}}^{(0)}(t)\equiv\bar{\mu}_{\star},\qquad\lambda_{i}^{(0)}(t)\equiv\bar{\mu}\quad(i\neq i_{\star}).

For
\pi_{S}
-a.e. initial state
x
, both point-process systems are
nonexplosive on
[0,T]
, and the only differing intensity is the

i_{\star}
-coordinate. Since both target intensities are bounded below by

\bar{\mu}_{\star}>0
, the conditional laws satisfy

K_{S}^{T}(x,\cdot)\ll K_{0}^{T}(x,\cdot)
.
Hence, we can apply Jacod’s
[
29
]
Girsanov formula for multivariate point processes (
[
11
, Chapter VI]
and
[
31
]
for more details) to the single differing coordinate
i_{\star}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}\!\bigl(K_{S}^{T}(x,\cdot)\|K_{0}^{T}(x,\cdot)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}\!\left(\lambda_{i_{\star}}^{(S)}(t)\log\frac{\lambda_{i_{\star}}^{(S)}(t)}{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}-\lambda_{i_{\star}}^{(S)}(t)+\bar{\mu}_{\star}\right)\!dt\,\middle|\,X(0)=x\right].

(71)

For
a\geq 0
and
b>0
,
a\log(a/b)-a+b\leq(a-b)^{2}/b
and the resulting entropy term is finite after averaging over
\pi_{S}
with
b=\bar{\mu}_{\star}
and
Lemma
C.5
. Applying this with
a=\bar{\mu}_{\star}+\theta_{-}\sum_{j\in S}X_{j}(t-)

and
b=\bar{\mu}_{\star}
gives

\operatorname{KL}\!\bigl(K_{S}^{T}(x,\cdot)\|K_{0}^{T}(x,\cdot)\bigr)\leq\frac{\theta_{-}^{2}}{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{S}\!\Bigl[\Bigl(\sum_{j\in S}X_{j}(t)\Bigr)^{2}\Bigm|X(0)=x\Bigr]\,dt.

Integrating over
\pi_{S}(dx)
and applying stationarity yields

\int\operatorname{KL}\!\bigl(K_{S}^{T}(x,\cdot)\|K_{0}^{T}(x,\cdot)\bigr)\,\pi_{S}(dx)\leq\frac{\theta_{-}^{2}}{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}\int_{0}^{T}\!\mathbb{E}_{S}[\Sigma_{S}(t)^{2}]\,dt=\frac{\theta_{-}^{2}}{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}\,C_{\mathrm{path}}\,T.

The last claim follows from Lemma
C.1
and Proposition
C.1
.
∎

C.4
Proof of Theorem
4.1

We now turn the KL bound into a lower bound over the hard subclass using Fano’s inequality
[
23
]
.
Let

M:=|\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})|=\binom{d-1}{k},

and let
S^{\star}
be uniform on
\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})
. The following
finite-
d
bound is the quantitative form used in the main text.

Proposition C.3
.

For every
d\geq k+2
and every
T>0
,

\inf_{\widehat{S}}\sup_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})}\mathbb{P}_{S}(\widehat{S}(Y_{T})\neq S)\geq 1-\frac{C_{\mathrm{init}}+\frac{\theta_{-}^{2}}{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}C_{\mathrm{path}}T+\log 2}{\log\binom{d-1}{k}}.

Proof.

For any estimator
\widehat{S}=\widehat{S}(Y_{T})
, Fano’s inequality gives

\sup_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})}\mathbb{P}_{S}(\widehat{S}\neq S)\geq 1-\frac{I(S^{\star};Y_{T})+\log 2}{\log M}.

The baseline mutual-information inequality in Lemma
C.2
,
with
Q=P_{0}^{T}
, yields

I(S^{\star};Y_{T})\leq\frac{1}{M}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})}\operatorname{KL}(P_{S}^{T}\|P_{0}^{T}).

By Lemma
C.1
, Proposition
C.1
, and
Proposition
C.2
, each summand satisfies

\operatorname{KL}(P_{S}^{T}\|P_{0}^{T})\leq C_{\mathrm{init}}+\frac{\theta_{-}^{2}}{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}C_{\mathrm{path}}T.

Substituting this into Fano’s inequality proves the claim.

∎

We now prove the information-theoretic lower bound.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Let
C_{\mathrm{path}}
be the constant from Lemma
C.5
, and let

C_{\mathrm{init}}
be the constant from Proposition
C.1
.
Since
k
is fixed,

\log\binom{d-1}{k}=k\log d+O(1),\qquad d\to\infty.

Hence there are constants
a_{k}>0
and
d_{1}\geq k+2
, depending only on
k
, such
that

\log\binom{d-1}{k}\geq a_{k}\log d,\qquad d\geq d_{1}.

Choose
d_{0}\geq d_{1}
large enough that

\frac{C_{\mathrm{init}}+\log 2}{\log\binom{d-1}{k}}\leq\frac{1}{4},\qquad d\geq d_{0},

and set

c:=\frac{a_{k}\bar{\mu}_{\star}}{4\theta_{-}^{2}C_{\mathrm{path}}},\qquad\varepsilon:=\frac{1}{2}.

If
d\geq d_{0}
and
T\leq c\log d
, then

\frac{\theta_{-}^{2}}{\bar{\mu}_{\star}}C_{\mathrm{path}}T\leq\frac{1}{4}\log\binom{d-1}{k}.

The finite bound in Proposition
C.3
therefore gives

\inf_{\widehat{S}}\sup_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})}\mathbb{P}_{S}(\widehat{S}(Y_{T})\neq S)\geq\frac{1}{2}.

This proves the theorem with the above constants.
The same argument also gives the
corresponding full-class impossibility statement, since
\mathcal{G}_{d,k}^{\mathrm{sub}}(i_{\star})\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{d,k}
. Indeed, any full support estimator
\widehat{G}(Y_{T})
induces
a row estimator
\widehat{S}(Y_{T})
by taking its estimated support in row

i_{\star}
. On the subclass, the event
\{\widehat{S}(Y_{T})\neq S\}
is contained
in the event that the full support is estimated incorrectly, and therefore

\inf_{\widehat{G}}\sup_{(\mu,\Theta)\in\mathcal{G}_{d,k}}\mathbb{P}_{\mu,\Theta}\!\left(\widehat{G}(Y_{T})\neq\operatorname{supp}(\Theta)\right)\geq\inf_{\widehat{S}}\sup_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{d,k}(i_{\star})}\mathbb{P}_{S}\!\left(\widehat{S}(Y_{T})\neq S\right).

∎
