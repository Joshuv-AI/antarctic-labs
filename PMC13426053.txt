An rGO-modified all-solid-state tetracaine-selective microsensor with enhanced interfacial stability for rapid pharmaceutical analysis
Authors: Ayman H. Kamel, Huda M. Al-Radhi, Hisham S. M. Abd-Rabboh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An all-solid-state tetracaine-selective microsensor with enhanced interfacial stability was developed for rapid potentiometric determination of tetracaine in ophthalmic formulations. The sensor was fabricated by depositing a reduced graphene oxide (rGO) ion-to-electron transducing layer onto a glassy carbon substrate, followed by coating with a poly(vinyl chloride)-based ion-selective membrane incorporating tetracaine–tetraphenylborate (TC/TPB) as the electroactive ion-association complex. A corresponding unmodified sensor was fabricated for comparative evaluation. The optimized rGO-modified microsensor exhibited a near-Nernstian slope of 59.1 ± 0.7 mV per decade over the concentration range of 1.0 × 10−6–1.0 × 10−1 mol L−1, with a detection limit of 5.49 × 10−7 mol L−1 and rapid response characteristics. Compared with the unmodified electrode, the rGO-modified sensor demonstrated superior potential stability, improved electrode-to-electrode reproducibility, and significantly enhanced interfacial charge-transfer behavior. Water-layer and chronopotentiometric studies confirmed the beneficial role of rGO in suppressing water-layer formation and improving ion-to-electron transduction at the membrane/substrate interface. The microsensor also exhibited excellent selectivity toward tetracaine over common inorganic ions, structurally related compounds, and pharmaceutical excipients, with stable operation across the pH range 4.0–7.0. Application to commercial ophthalmic formulations afforded recoveries between 98.6 and 101.6%, in close agreement with a reference UV-vis spectrophotometric method. The developed microsensor provides a simple, rapid, and cost-effective analytical platform for tetracaine determination and demonstrates the effectiveness of graphene-based solid-contact transducers in improving the performance of pharmaceutical potentiometric microsensors.

Introduction

Tetracaine hydrochloride (TC·HCl) is a potent ester-type local anaesthetic widely employed in ophthalmic formulations and minor surgical procedures due to its rapid onset and relatively long duration of action.1,2 However, overdosing or accidental misuse may cause serious cardiovascular and central nervous system toxicity, so accurate assessment of its content in pharmaceutical products and biological matrices is essential for both quality control and patient safety.2,3 Pharmacopoeial monographs therefore specify strict assay limits and require validated analytical methods for tetracaine-containing dosage forms.4

A wide range of physicochemical techniques has been reported for the determination of tetracaine. Several high-performance liquid chromatographic (HPLC) methods have been developed for its quantification either alone or in combination with related substances or degradation products in pharmaceutical formulations and biological fluids.1,5–7 These include liquid-phase and liquid–liquid–liquid microextraction coupled to HPLC for simultaneous determination of local anaesthetics in urine and plasma,1,6 as well as stability-indicating HPLC assays for tetracaine and its degradation product p-n-butylaminobenzoic acid.5 Gas chromatography and GC-MS procedures have also been proposed for the determination of tetracaine and other ester-type local anaesthetics in human serum, plasma and urine, particularly in forensic and toxicological investigations.8,9

In addition to chromatographic techniques, several spectroscopic and luminescence methods have been described. Classical spectrophotometric procedures have been reported for the assay of tetracaine in pharmaceutical preparations.10 More sensitive strategies involve fluorescence quenching using aromatic amino acids or erythrosin as probes,11,12 variable-angle synchronous fluorimetry,13 resonance Rayleigh scattering (RRS) and non-linear scattering techniques.12,14 Chemiluminescence approaches based on permanganate-induced emission or related systems have been coupled with flow or sequential-injection analysis for tetracaine and other local anaesthetics,15,16 and a sequential-injection spectrophotometric method has also been introduced.17 Electrochemical determination using voltammetric techniques at modified carbon electrodes was likewise reported.18 Although these methods generally offer excellent sensitivity and selectivity, they often require sophisticated and expensive instrumentation, multi-step sample pretreatment, relatively long analysis times and skilled operators, which may limit their routine application in many quality-control laboratories.

Potentiometric ion-selective electrodes (ISEs) provide an attractive alternative for routine pharmaceutical analysis. They combine simple design, low cost, rapid response and the ability to analyze colored or turbid samples without extensive pretreatment.19–21 ISE performance can be optimized by appropriate selection of the ion-association complex, membrane composition and plasticizer, as outlined in the IUPAC recommendations and subsequent reviews on modern ISEs.22,23 Previously reported tetracaine-selective electrodes have employed ion-association complexes based on phosphotungstate, phosphomolybdate, and tetraphenylborate counterions.24 Among these systems, tetracaine–tetraphenylborate offers favorable lipophilicity, high membrane compatibility, and efficient ion-exchange characteristics, making it particularly suitable for incorporation into all-solid-state microsensor architectures. However, these classical designs rely on conventional macro-electrodes with internal filling solutions and do not fully exploit recent advances in solid contact microsensors, which can offer improved mechanical robustness, miniaturization potential and enhanced long-term stability.

Despite previous reports on tetracaine-selective PVC membrane electrodes, most published systems rely on conventional liquid-contact architectures or bulky coated-wire designs, which suffer from limited miniaturization capability, poor potential stability, and susceptibility to interfacial water-layer formation. Furthermore, the interfacial charge-transfer behavior and long-term stability of tetracaine-selective all-solid-state potentiometric microsensors have not been systematically investigated. Accordingly, the present study introduces, for the first time, a reduced graphene oxide-modified all-solid-state tetracaine-selective microsensor. Although graphene-derived materials have been explored in other ion-selective electrode systems, no rGO-based tetracaine-selective microsensor has previously been reported. The developed platform combines the analytical advantages of nanostructured solid-contact materials with the selectivity of tetracaine–tetraphenylborate recognition chemistry while systematically investigating interfacial stability and ion-to-electron transduction performance.

Experimental

Reagents and solutions

Poly(vinyl chloride) (PVC, high molecular weight), o-nitrophenyl octyl ether (NPOE), dioctyl sebacate (DOS), dibutyl sebacate (DBS), sodium tetraphenylborate (NaTPB), tetrahydrofuran (THF), reduced graphene oxide (rGO), and tetracaine hydrochloride were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and used without further purification. All inorganic salts and interfering compounds employed in selectivity studies were of analytical reagent grade. Deionized water (resistivity ≥ 18.2 MΩ cm) was used throughout all experiments.

A standard tetracaine stock solution (1.0 × 10−2 mol L−1) was freshly prepared by dissolving an appropriate amount of tetracaine hydrochloride in deionized water and stored in amber glass bottles at 4 °C. Working solutions in the concentration range of 1.0 × 10−7–1.0 × 10−1 mol L−1 were prepared daily by serial dilution with deionized water. Britton–Robinson buffer solutions were used for pH adjustment where required.

Instrumentation and apparatus

Potentiometric measurements were carried out using a high-input impedance multichannel potentiometric measurement system coupled with a computer-controlled data acquisition interface for continuous electromotive force (EMF) recording. A conventional Ag/AgCl double-junction reference electrode was employed as the external reference electrode in all potentiometric measurements. Chronopotentiometric and electrochemical stability measurements were performed using an electrochemical workstation (Model 1010E, Gamry Instruments, USA) operated under computer control. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded using an FTIR spectrophotometer (SP3-300, Pye-Unicam, UK) over the spectral range of 4000–400 cm−1 for characterization of the synthesized tetracaine–tetraphenylborate ion-association complex. UV-visible absorbance measurements for the reference spectrophotometric analysis were carried out using a UV/vis spectrophotometer (GENESYS™ 10S, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) employing 1 cm quartz cuvettes at λmax = 312 nm.

Solution pH values were monitored using a calibrated digital pH meter. An ultrasonic bath was used for solution homogenization during membrane preparation. All weighing procedures were performed using an analytical balance with readability of ±0.1 mg. All experiments were conducted at ambient laboratory temperature (25 ± 1 °C).

Synthesis of tetracaine–tetraphenylborate (TC/TPB) ion-pair

The tetracaine–tetraphenylborate (TC/TPB) ion-association complex was prepared by ion-pair precipitation. Briefly, 20 mL of 1.0 × 10−2 mol per L tetracaine hydrochloride solution was transferred into a beaker under magnetic stirring, followed by dropwise addition of 25 mL of 1.0 × 10−2 mol per L sodium tetraphenylborate solution. The resulting precipitate was allowed to form under continuous stirring for 30 min at room temperature. The precipitated ion-pair was collected by vacuum filtration, thoroughly washed with deionized water to remove excess ions and unreacted species, and dried at room temperature for 24 h in the dark. The dried precipitate was gently ground to obtain a homogeneous fine powder and stored in an amber glass vial for subsequent membrane preparation. The obtained TC/TPB ion-pair was characterized by FTIR spectroscopy to confirm successful complex formation and assess its structural integrity prior to membrane incorporation.

Fabrication of the all-solid-state tetracaine-selective electrode

Two tetracaine-selective all-solid-state microsensor configurations were fabricated for comparative evaluation: a control electrode without transducer modification and an rGO-modified solid-contact electrode incorporating reduced graphene oxide (rGO) as an ion-to-electron transducing layer. Glassy carbon (GC) disk electrodes (3 mm diameter) were used as the conductive substrates. Prior to modification, the GC surfaces were mechanically polished using alumina slurry (0.3 and 0.05 µm sequentially), thoroughly rinsed with deionized water, and ultrasonicated in deionized water and methanol for 5 min each to remove residual contaminants.

For fabrication of the rGO-modified electrode, an aliquot of rGO suspension (1 mg mL−1 in THF solvent) was drop-cast onto the cleaned GC surface and allowed to dry at room temperature to form a uniform conductive transducer layer. The ion-selective membrane cocktail was prepared by dissolving 32 wt% poly (vinyl chloride) (PVC), 64 wt% o-nitrophenyl octyl ether (o,NPOE), 3 wt% tetracaine–tetraphenylborate (TC/TPB) ion-pair, and 1 wt% potassium tetrakis(4-chlorophenyl) borate (KTpClPB) in 3 mL tetrahydrofuran (THF). The mixture was vortexed and sonicated until a clear homogeneous solution was obtained.

An aliquot (100 µL) of the membrane cocktail was subsequently drop-cast onto either the bare GC electrode surface (control sensor) or the rGO-modified GC electrode surface (rGO/GC sensor), followed by drying at room temperature for 2 h to allow complete solvent evaporation and formation of a uniform membrane layer. Sensor conditioning and preconditioning

Prior to potentiometric measurements, the fabricated microsensors were conditioned by soaking in 1.0 × 10−3 mol per L tetracaine hydrochloride solution for 24 h at room temperature to establish equilibrium at the membrane/solution interface and ensure stable phase-boundary potential development. To improve low-concentration response and membrane equilibration, the conditioned sensors were subsequently immersed in 1.0 × 10−5 mol per L tetracaine solution for an additional 24 h.

When not in use, the microsensors were stored in 1.0 × 10−4 mol per L tetracaine solution at room temperature and rinsed thoroughly with deionized water prior to each measurement.

Potentiometric measurements and calibration protocol

All potentiometric measurements were performed at ambient laboratory temperature (25 ± 1 °C) in a 25 mL double-walled glass electrochemical cell under continuous magnetic stirring. The measurement cell consisted of the fabricated tetracaine-selective microsensor as the working electrode and an external Ag/AgCl double-junction reference electrode. Calibration curves were constructed by recording the electromotive force (EMF) response of the microsensors in tetracaine hydrochloride standard solutions over the concentration range of 1.0 × 10−7–1.0 × 10−1 mol L−1 at controlled pH conditions (typically pH 5.0 adjusted using Britton–Robinson buffer). The concentration of Britton–Robinson buffer was maintained constant at 50 mM for all calibration standards to ensure consistent ionic strength throughout the calibration procedure and to minimize potential variations arising from changes in solution composition. Measurements were performed by transferring the electrodes sequentially between individual standard solutions in ascending concentration order. For each concentration, the potential was monitored until a stable reading was obtained, defined as a potential variation of less than ± 0.2 mV min−1. The equilibrium potential values were then plotted against the logarithm of tetracaine concentration to generate the calibration curves.

The analytical performance parameters including slope, linear range, detection limit, and response time were determined according to IUPAC recommendations.22 The detection limit was estimated from the intersection of the extrapolated linear portion of the calibration curve with the baseline potential at low analyte concentrations.

Water-layer test and interfacial stability assessment

The interfacial stability of the fabricated microsensors was evaluated using a water-layer test to assess the possible formation of an aqueous film between the solid-contact substrate and the ion-selective membrane. The experiment was carried out under identical conditions for both the rGO-modified and unmodified tetracaine-selective microsensors. Each sensor was first immersed in Britton–Robinson (BR) buffer solution (50 mM, pH 5.0), and the potential was recorded every 30 s over a period of 5 min to establish the initial baseline response. The sensor was then transferred to 1.0 × 10−4 mol per L tetracaine hydrochloride solution, where the potential was again recorded every 30 s for 5 min. Finally, the sensor was re-immersed in the same BR buffer solution, and the potential was monitored under the same conditions for an additional 5 min.

The magnitude of potential drift and the reversibility of the potential response during sequential transfer between buffer and tetracaine solution were used to evaluate interfacial stability. A lower potential drift upon returning to the buffer solution was taken as evidence of suppressed water-layer formation and improved ion-to-electron transduction at the membrane/solid-contact interface.

Selectivity and pH studies

Selectivity coefficients (log KpotTC,X) with respect to common inorganic cations and typical pharmaceutical excipients (e.g., lactose, glucose, fructose, ascorbic acid, urea, creatinine) can be determined by the separate solution method (SSM).25,26 In this procedure, calibration curves are recorded in solutions of tetracaine and the potential responses in equimolar solutions of potential interferents (10−3 or 10−2 mol L−1) are compared using the Nicolsky–Eisenman equation.

The influence of pH on the electrode response is investigated by measuring the potential of two tetracaine solutions (e.g., 1.0 × 10−4 and 1.0 × 10−2 mol L−1) while gradually adjusting the pH between 3.0 and 11.0 with small additions of dilute HCl or NaOH. The pH range where the potential remains essentially constant (variation < 1 mV) is considered the working pH window of the sensor.

Pharmaceutical sample preparation and analysis

To assess the practical utility of the developed TC-TPB microsensor, the method was applied to the determination of tetracaine in commercially available pharmaceutical formulations, including ophthalmic solutions, single-dose eye drops, and topical anesthetic preparations. Each dosage form was handled according to its physical characteristics to ensure accurate extraction of tetracaine. For liquid formulations (0.5–1.0% w/v), an accurately measured volume corresponding to a known mass of tetracaine was transferred into a 25-mL volumetric flask. The sample was diluted with deionized water and mixed thoroughly, followed by brief sonication when necessary to ensure complete homogenization. For semi-solid preparations or samples containing suspended excipients, gentle centrifugation or membrane filtration was carried out to obtain a clear solution suitable for potentiometric measurement. Aliquots of the clarified sample solution were introduced into the potentiometric cell, and the pH was adjusted to the optimal working range (pH 4.0–7.0), typically around pH 5, to ensure stable sensor response. Ionic strength was standardized using a small volume of supporting electrolyte. Quantification of tetracaine was performed using the previously established calibration graph of the TC/TPB microsensor or, when appropriate, by applying the standard additions method to minimize matrix effects.

The potentiometric results obtained for each commercial product were subsequently compared with those generated by a validated UV-vis spectrophotometric assay at 312 nm. The excellent agreement between the two methods confirmed the accuracy and reliability of the microsensor for routine determination of tetracaine in pharmaceutical formulations.

Optimization of membrane composition and potentiometric calibration

The potentiometric performance of tetracaine-selective microsensors prepared using different membrane plasticizers was systematically investigated to optimize membrane composition and maximize analytical sensitivity. Four membrane formulations were fabricated using dioctyl sebacate (DOS), dibutyl sebacate (DBS), a mixed DOS/NPOE system, and o-nitrophenyl octyl ether (NPOE), respectively. The calibration plots obtained for the different membrane compositions over the tetracaine concentration range of 1.0 × 10−7–1.0 × 10−1 mol L−1 are presented in Fig. 3, while the corresponding analytical performance parameters are summarized in Table 2. All membrane formulations additionally contained 1 wt% KTpClPB as a lipophilic ionic additive to improve membrane perm-selectivity and reduce membrane resistance. As illustrated in Fig. 3, all fabricated microsensors exhibited the expected cationic potentiometric response, characterized by progressive increases in electrode potential with increasing tetracaine concentration. However, significant differences in sensitivity and linear response range were observed depending on the membrane plasticizer employed. Among the tested formulations, the NPOE-plasticized membrane (sensor IV) demonstrated the highest sensitivity and broadest linear dynamic range, affording a near-Nernstian slope of 59.1 ± 0.7 mV per decade over the concentration range 1.0 × 10−6–1.0 × 10−1 mol L−1, with a detection limit of 5.49 × 10−7 mol L−1, as summarized in Table 2. The detection limit (LOD) was estimated according to IUPAC recommendations from the intersection of the extrapolated linear segment of the calibration plot with the baseline potential response at low analyte concentrations. Consequently, the calculated LOD (5.49 × 10−7 mol L−1) lies slightly below the experimentally validated lower limit of the linear response range (1.0 × 10−6 mol L−1). The near-Nernstian slope was therefore calculated exclusively within the concentration interval of 1.0 × 10−6–1.0 × 10−1 mol L−1. The superior potentiometric performance of the NPOE-based membrane can be attributed to the relatively high dielectric constant and enhanced solvating ability of NPOE compared with the other investigated plasticizers. The elevated dielectric constant of NPOE facilitates efficient partitioning of the protonated tetracaine species into the membrane phase and enhances ion mobility within the polymeric matrix, thereby promoting more effective ion-exchange equilibrium and reducing membrane resistance. Consequently, improved charge transport within the membrane phase results in enhanced potentiometric sensitivity and broader linear response behavior.

In contrast, membranes plasticized with DOS and DBS (sensors I and II) exhibited lower slopes of 50.2 and 47.8 mV per decade, respectively, together with narrower linear ranges and higher detection limits (Table 2). This behavior may be attributed to the lower dielectric constants and weaker solvating capabilities of these plasticizers, which reduce membrane conductivity and limit analyte transport within the membrane phase. The mixed DOS/NPOE membrane (sensor III) displayed intermediate analytical behavior, yielding a slope of 55.7 mV per decade, confirming the beneficial contribution of NPOE even when partially incorporated into the membrane matrix.

Based on the obtained calibration characteristics and overall analytical performance summarized in Table 2, the NPOE-plasticized membrane composition was selected as the optimum formulation for fabrication of the subsequent tetracaine-selective all-solid-state microsensors. The achieved detection limit of 5.49 × 10−7 mol L−1 compares favorably with values reported for previously described potentiometric tetracaine sensors, highlighting the beneficial contribution of the rGO-based solid-contact architecture. The optimized microsensor exhibited a rapid response time of approximately 5 s following concentration changes, indicating efficient establishment of membrane/solution equilibrium and fast ion-to-electron transduction.

Repeatability and operational lifetime of the tetracaine-selective microsensor

Repeatability studies

The repeatability of the optimized tetracaine-selective microsensor was evaluated by performing repeated start–stop potentiometric measurements over the tetracaine concentration range of 1.0 × 10−6–1.0 × 10−1 mol L−1 at pH 6.0. During the experiment, the microsensor was sequentially immersed in tetracaine standard solutions of increasing concentration, and the corresponding equilibrium potentials were recorded after stabilization. The resulting potential–time response profile is presented in Fig. 4. Each concentration level was measured in triplicate (n = 3) using the same microsensor under identical experimental conditions. The reported values correspond to short-term repeatability measurements and are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. As shown in Fig. 4, the microsensor exhibited highly reproducible and stable potentiometric responses throughout the investigated concentration range, with negligible signal fluctuation between successive measurements. The average measured potentials at tetracaine concentrations of 1.0 × 10−6, 1.0 × 10−5, 1.0 × 10−4, 1.0 × 10−3, 1.0 × 10−2, and 1.0 × 10−1 mol L−1 were found to be 56.3 ± 0.4, 109.2 ± 0.6, 168.9 ± 0.4, 228.3 ± 0.6, 289.1 ± 0.8, and 350.1 ± 0.7 mV, respectively. The low standard deviation values obtained for all tested concentrations (all below 1 mV) indicate excellent repeatability and demonstrate the high reproducibility of the membrane potential generated at the membrane/solution interface.

Potential–time repeatability profile of the optimized tetracaine-selective microsensor during repeated start–stop measurements at increasing tetracaine concentrations.

The observed repeatability reflects the homogeneous distribution and stable retention of the TC/TPB ion-association complex within the optimized membrane matrix, together with efficient ion-exchange equilibrium and reproducible phase-boundary potential establishment during repeated measurements. Such reproducibility is particularly important for routine analytical applications requiring frequent recalibration and repeated sample analysis. The slightly smaller potential increment observed between 10−6 and 10−5 mol L−1 compared with the higher concentration intervals is attributed to the proximity of this concentration region to the practical detection limit of the sensor, where minor deviations from ideal Nernstian behavior are commonly observed. Nevertheless, the overall response remains consistent with the validated linear working range of 1.0 × 10−6–1.0 × 10−1 mol L−1.

Lifetime evaluation

The operational lifetime of the developed microsensor was assessed by periodically monitoring its potentiometric slope over a period of 60 days, using calibration measurements performed at regular time intervals. The variation in sensor slope as a function of time is illustrated in Fig. 5. As shown in Fig. 5, the microsensor exhibited an initial slope of 56.5 mV per decade and maintained near-Nernstian behavior throughout the first 50 days of operation, with slope values remaining above 55.5 mV per decade. The slightly lower initial slope recorded in this separate lifetime experiment compared with the optimized calibration slope (59.1 ± 0.7 mV per decade) is attributed to minor variations associated with membrane equilibration and repeated conditioning before initiation of long-term monitoring and does not indicate a significant loss of sensor sensitivity. After day 50, a gradual decline in sensitivity was observed, reaching approximately 49.5 mV per decade by day 60. For practical evaluation, the operational lifetime was defined as the period during which the slope remained within ±5% of its initial value. Based on this criterion, the useful operational lifetime of the sensor was estimated to be approximately 50 days.

Slope–time profile of the tetracaine–tetraphenylborate ion-selective microsensor monitored over 60 days.

The gradual decrease in potentiometric sensitivity over extended storage and use may arise from several membrane-aging phenomena commonly reported for PVC-based ion-selective membranes, including partial ion-pair leaching, membrane hydration, plasticizer redistribution, and structural rearrangement within the polymer matrix. Based on the point at which the slope deviated significantly from its initial near-Nernstian value, the practical operational lifetime of the fabricated tetracaine-selective microsensor was estimated to be approximately 50 days. This operational stability compares favorably with previously reported solid-contact pharmaceutical ion-selective electrodes, confirming the practical robustness and acceptable long-term applicability of the developed microsensor for routine pharmaceutical analysis. Although the present study did not include dedicated post-aging characterization experiments, these mechanisms are proposed only as possible contributors and should not be interpreted as definitive degradation pathways.

Selectivity of the microsensor

The selectivity of the developed tetracaine-selective microsensor toward the target analyte relative to potentially interfering species was evaluated using the separate solution method (SSM), and the obtained logarithmic potentiometric selectivity coefficients (log KpotTC,X) are summarized in Table 3. The investigated interfering species included common inorganic cations, biologically relevant compounds, pharmaceutical excipients, and structurally related drug molecules that may potentially coexist in pharmaceutical or biological matrices. As presented in Table 3, the fabricated microsensor exhibited excellent selectivity toward tetracaine over all tested interferents, with logarithmic selectivity coefficient values ranging from −2.18 to −4.15, indicating negligible interference from the examined species under the experimental conditions. In particular, the sensor displayed very low sensitivity toward common monovalent and divalent inorganic cations such as Na+, K+, Ca2+, and Mg2+, confirming that the membrane response is highly specific toward protonated tetracaine rather than general cationic species.

The observed selectivity behavior may be attributed primarily to the preferential lipophilic partitioning of protonated tetracaine into the membrane phase and its favorable ion-exchange equilibrium between protonated tetracaine and the lipophilic membrane-bound ion exchanger. In addition, the relatively large molecular size, hydrophobicity, and structural compatibility of tetracaine enhance its preferential extraction into the plasticized membrane compared with smaller inorganic ions and neutral interfering molecules. These factors collectively contribute to the strong selective recognition of tetracaine by the membrane system. Among the tested interferents, creatinine and lidocaine exhibited the highest selectivity coefficient values, with log Kpot values of −2.18 and −2.60, respectively (Table 3). Nevertheless, these values remain sufficiently low to confirm that neither compound significantly interferes with tetracaine determination under the experimental conditions employed. Overall, the selectivity data obtained demonstrates that the developed microsensor possesses high analytical specificity and is suitable for reliable tetracaine determination in complex pharmaceutical formulations without significant interference from common coexisting species.

pH effect

The influence of solution pH on the potentiometric response of the developed tetracaine-selective microsensor was investigated over the pH range 3.0–11.0 using tetracaine standard solutions of 1.0 × 10−4 and 1.0 × 10−2 mol L−1. The corresponding potential–pH profiles are presented in Fig. 6.

Potentiometric responses of the TC–TPB microsensor recorded over the pH range 3–11 for tetracaine concentrations of 10−2 and 10−4 mol L−1.

As shown in Fig. 6, the microsensor exhibited stable and nearly constant potential responses within the pH range 4.0–7.0 for both investigated tetracaine concentrations, indicating that the membrane response remains unaffected by pH variation within this interval. This behavior suggests that tetracaine remains predominantly in its protonated cationic form and that the membrane ion-exchange equilibrium is maintained under these conditions. Accordingly, this pH interval was identified as the optimum operational pH range for reliable potentiometric measurements. This behavior is consistent with the basic nature of tetracaine, which contains protonatable amino functionality essential for cationic potentiometric recognition.

At pH values below 4.0, a gradual increase in electrode potential was observed for both concentration levels (Fig. 6). This behavior may be attributed to interference from excess hydronium ions, which can partially contribute to the membrane potential and compete with protonated tetracaine during the ion-exchange process, thereby causing elevated emf readings.27 Conversely, at pH values above 7.0, the measured potential decreased progressively with increasing pH. This decline is likely associated with gradual deprotonation of tetracaine molecules at alkaline pH, leading to reduced concentration of the electroactive protonated tetracaine species available for membrane extraction. Additionally, hydroxide ions may disturb the membrane/solution interfacial equilibrium at higher pH values, further contributing to signal deterioration.28,29 Based on these observations, the developed tetracaine-selective microsensor exhibits optimum analytical performance and reliable potentiometric stability within the pH range 4.0–7.0, making it well suited for pharmaceutical analysis under near-neutral working conditions.

The observed pH dependence is consistent with the acid–base properties of tetracaine, which exists predominantly in its protonated cationic form within the pH range 4.0–7.0. Under these conditions, efficient ion exchange between protonated tetracaine and the membrane-bound ion exchanger is maintained, resulting in stable potentiometric responses. At higher pH values, progressive deprotonation reduces the concentration of the electroactive species, leading to diminished sensor response.

Interfacial stability evaluation

Water-layer test

The interfacial stability of the fabricated tetracaine-selective microsensors was evaluated using the water-layer test, and the resulting potential–time profiles are presented in Fig. 7. The rGO-modified microsensor (Fig. 7a) exhibited a highly stable and reversible potentiometric response during sequential transfer between Britton–Robinson (BR) buffer and 1.0 × 10−4 mol per L tetracaine solution, with negligible baseline drift observed upon re-immersion in the buffer medium. In contrast, the unmodified microsensor (Fig. 7b) displayed noticeable potential drift and incomplete recovery of the initial baseline after exposure to the tetracaine solution.

Water-layer test responses of tetracaine-selective all-solid-state microsensors recorded during sequential immersion in BR buffer (0–300 s), 1.0 × 10−4 mol per L tetracaine solution (300–600 s), and BR buffer again (600–900 s): (a) rGO-modified microsensor and (b) unmodified microsensor.

The pronounced drift observed for the unmodified electrode indicates the probable formation of an undesirable aqueous layer at the interface between the conductive substrate and the ion-selective membrane. Such water-layer formation is a well-recognized limitation in solid-contact ion-selective electrodes, as it may facilitate uncontrolled ion diffusion and accumulation at the buried interface, thereby leading to potential instability and diminished long-term reproducibility.30,31 By contrast, the superior anti-water-layer performance of the rGO-modified microsensor can be attributed to the intrinsic hydrophobicity of reduced graphene oxide together with its large effective surface area, both of which minimize water uptake and inhibit formation of a trapped aqueous film between the conductive substrate and the sensing membrane.

Additionally, the incorporation of the rGO intermediate layer enhances the interfacial contact between the membrane and the electrode substrate, thereby promoting more efficient ion-to-electron transduction and improving charge-transfer stability across the interface.30 The minimal potential drift and excellent reversibility observed for the rGO-modified microsensor therefore confirm the beneficial role of the rGO transducing layer in improving interfacial stability and suppressing water-layer formation, ultimately contributing to enhanced long-term potentiometric performance and operational reliability of the developed tetracaine-selective microsensor.

Chronopotentiometric analysis

To further evaluate the electrochemical stability and charge-transfer characteristics of the fabricated tetracaine-selective microsensors, chronopotentiometric measurements were performed under galvanostatic polarization by applying a constant current of ±1 nA, and the resulting potential/time profiles are shown in Fig. 8. The potential drift of the sensors under constant-current conditions was used to assess the short-term potential stability of the membrane/solid-contact interface.32 As illustrated in Fig. 8, the rGO-modified microsensor (Fig. 8a) exhibited significantly lower potential drift compared with the unmodified sensor (Fig. 8b), demonstrating markedly improved electrochemical stability after incorporation of the rGO intermediate layer. Reduced graphene oxide functions as an intermediate ion-to-electron transducer by providing a highly conductive pathway between the ion-selective membrane and the underlying electronic conductor. The large effective surface area and excellent electrical conductivity of rGO facilitate efficient charge accumulation and promote stable conversion of ionic membrane signals into electronic signals. The potential drift values (ΔE/Δt), calculated from the slopes of the chronopotentiometric curves, were determined to be 35 µV s−1 for the modified microsensor and 180 µV s−1 for the unmodified counterpart.

Chronopotentiometric responses of tetracaine-selective all-solid-state microsensors under galvanostatic polarization (±1 nA): (a) rGO-modified microsensor and (b) unmodified microsensor. The corresponding potential drift values were 35 and 180 µV s−1, respectively.

Enhanced capacitance improves charge-storage capability and reduces susceptibility to potential fluctuations at the membrane/solid-contact interface, thereby contributing to improved potential stability and measurement reproducibility. The increased capacitance effectively buffers potential fluctuations and suppresses signal drift during polarization, thereby improving overall sensor stability. These findings confirm that incorporation of the rGO transducing layer significantly enhances the interfacial electrochemical properties of the tetracaine-selective microsensor and contributes substantially to its improved potentiometric stability and operational reliability.

Analytical applications/pharmaceutical sample analysis

The practical applicability of the developed tetracaine-selective microsensor was evaluated through determination of tetracaine in commercially available ophthalmic formulations. Three representative commercial eye-drop preparations containing tetracaine hydrochloride at labeled concentrations of 0.5–1.0% (w/v) were selected for analysis. The investigated formulations included Minims® Tetracaine Hydrochloride, TRODEZ Tetracaine Eye Drops BP, and Tetracaine Hydrochloride Ophthalmic Solution USP.

Prior to analysis, appropriate aliquots of each formulation were accurately diluted with deionized water to bring the tetracaine concentration within the linear response range of the developed microsensor. The diluted samples were then measured potentiometrically under the optimized experimental conditions at pH 5.0, and tetracaine concentrations were determined using the previously established calibration plot. To verify the analytical accuracy of the proposed potentiometric method, the same pharmaceutical samples were analyzed independently using a reference UV-vis spectrophotometric method at 312 nm, and the obtained results were compared. The analytical data obtained from both methods are summarized in Table 4. As presented in Table 4, the developed microsensor provided excellent analytical recoveries ranging from 98.6 to 101.6%, indicating high accuracy for tetracaine quantification in the pharmaceutical formulations investigated. Furthermore, the determined tetracaine concentrations showed very close agreement with those obtained by the reference UV/vis spectrophotometric method, confirming the reliability and analytical validity of the proposed potentiometric approach. The high recoveries and excellent agreement with the reference method demonstrate that common formulation excipients present in ophthalmic preparations do not significantly interfere with sensor performance, which is consistent with the favorable selectivity characteristics discussed previously. These findings confirm the suitability of the developed tetracaine-selective microsensor for routine pharmaceutical quality-control analysis.

Conclusion

An all-solid-state tetracaine-selective microsensor based on a tetracaine–tetraphenylborate (TC/TPB) ion-association complex was successfully developed and optimized for rapid potentiometric determination of tetracaine in pharmaceutical formulations. Incorporation of reduced graphene oxide (rGO) as an intermediate solid-contact transducing layer significantly enhanced the electrochemical performance of the fabricated microsensor by improving interfacial ion-to-electron transduction and suppressing interfacial instability. The optimized rGO-modified microsensor exhibited near-Nernstian response behavior over a wide concentration range, low detection limit, excellent repeatability, favorable operational lifetime, and high selectivity toward tetracaine over common interfering species. Water-layer and chronopotentiometric studies further confirmed the superior interfacial stability of the modified microsensor, demonstrating substantially reduced potential drift and enhanced capacitance compared with the unmodified sensor. The developed microsensor was successfully applied to the determination of tetracaine in commercial ophthalmic formulations, providing excellent analytical recoveries and results in close agreement with those obtained using a reference UV-vis spectrophotometric method. Owing to its simplicity, rapid response, low cost, and robust analytical performance, the proposed sensing platform offers a promising alternative for routine tetracaine determination in pharmaceutical quality-control applications. Furthermore, the present work highlights the effectiveness of graphene-based solid-contact materials in improving the stability and analytical reliability of modern pharmaceutical potentiometric microsensors. Compared with conventional liquid-contact tetracaine-selective electrodes, the proposed microsensor eliminates the need for an internal filling solution while providing improved mechanical robustness, enhanced potential stability, and greater suitability for miniaturized analytical devices. Future studies may investigate alternative solid-contact materials such as carbon nanotubes, MXenes, conducting polymers, and hybrid nanocomposites to further optimize capacitance, potential stability, and long-term durability.
