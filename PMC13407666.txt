Moving in Tandem: Epilepsy-Dyskinesia Syndromes

ABSTRACT

Brain. 2026;149(2):563-578. doi: 10.1093/brain/awaf297. PMID: 40811633.Epilepsy-dyskinesia syndromes (EDSs) are a complex group of neurogenetic disorders characterized by the co-occurrence of epilepsy and movement disorders (MDs). Despite their increasing clinical recognition, the molecular and clinical spectrum of EDS remains poorly understood. While numerous genetic etiologies have been implicated, systematic characterization across diverse populations is lacking. This study aimed to delineate the molecular and clinical landscape of EDS in a large, multinational cohort, focusing on MD phenomenologies, genotype-phenotype correlations, and treatment responses. We conducted a multicenter, cross-sectional study involving 609 patients with childhood-onset MDs associated with pathogenic variants in 105 predefined genes. Clinical data were collected from over 30 centers across 25 countries using a standardized survey, capturing MD phenomenologies, seizure types, developmental trajectories, motor function and treatment outcomes. We classified EDS-associated genes into biologically meaningful groups by performing unsupervised clustering, which integrated protein-protein interactions and functional data. Genotype-phenotype correlations were assessed using a one-versus-remainder approach to quantify differential enrichment of clinical manifestations and treatment responses. Pathogenic variants were identified in 74 of the 105 predefined genes, with 12 genes accounting for two-thirds of cases. The most frequently reported genes were MECP2, ATP1A3, and GNAO1. Data-driven gene cluster analysis identified 12 functional groups, mapping EDS to relevant biological pathways and informing genotype-phenotype analyses. Dystonia (34.2%), stereotypies (24.6%) and ataxia (16.2%) were the most prevalent MDs, with gene- and pathway-specific MD signatures extending beyond previously known associations. Notably, most patients exhibited mixed MDs, highlighting the phenotypic complexity of EDS. Epilepsy was diagnosed in only 66.8% of cases, suggesting that some EDS primarily manifest as MDs. Developmental trajectories varied by genetic etiology. Pharmacological responses demonstrated gene- and pathway-specific treatment effects, confirming established therapeutic associations (eg, PRRT2 variants responding to carbamazepine) and identifying previously unrecognized effects, such as exacerbation of motor symptoms with levodopa/carbidopa in GNAO1 and MECP2 variants. This study provides a detailed characterization of EDS, identifying distinct genetic, phenotypic and therapeutic patterns. The findings underscore the need for early recognition of MDs within epilepsy cohorts, offer immediate insights to improve anticipatory guidance and clinical management of EDS, and advocate for personalized treatment strategies. By laying the groundwork for longitudinal studies to refine genotype–phenotype correlations and establish a natural history, this work paves the way for interventional clinical trials and precision medicine approaches.

Commentary

The analysis and interpretation of paroxysmal movements are common to both movement disorders (MD) and epilepsy. Yet too often both disciplines practice independently despite increasingly recognized conditions where both epilepsy and MD exist together, the so-called epilepsy dyskinesia syndromes (EDS).

Here we highlight a paper from the MD program at Boston Childrens with clear interest for epilepsy specialists involved in the care of people with EDS. 1

The authors present a large multicenter cross-sectional cohort of genetic EDS to illustrate the phenotype, molecular mechanisms and natural history of these disorders, essential for future advances, clinical trials and potential precision therapies.

One-hundred and five genes associated with EDS were predefined by Boston Childrens and the MD Society pediatric special interest group. Surveys were sent to and completed by 30 centers in 25 countries in almost every continent (except Africa) and supplemented by medical records and videos in some cases. Patients harboring a confirmed pathogenic predefined gene variant and a childhood onset MD were included.

The survey included information on demographics, movement and seizure semiology, clinical features, development, imaging and mortality. Response to treatment for the MD or epilepsy was available for many patients, including 47 different medications.

Based on the survey responses, 74 of the 105 pathogenic gene variants were represented in the total cohort of 609 patients. Twelve genes accounted for a majority of the cases. The most frequent genes were MECP2 (14.8%), ATP1A3 (7.4%), GNAO1 (6.7%), PRRT2 (6.4%) and SLC2A1 (5.7%). Other genes included CACNA1A, CDKL5, FOXG1, STXBP1, SCN1A/8A. While the numbers for some gene variants were small, this also reflects their real-world incidence even in tertiary centers.

The median age at genetic diagnosis was 4.4 years. A majority (46.5%) of genetic diagnosis were obtained through whole-exome sequencing, 30.6% from a gene panel and 11% on single gene testing. Only 9.4% of cases had previously been described in the literature. At time of reporting 13 of the 609 (2.1%) had died, a majority of whom harbored GNAO1 mutations (n = 4). Deaths due to early life drug resistant epilepsy or status dystonicus have been reported in GNAO1, but the natural history of this and other causes of EDS is limited. 2 Most patients will transition into adult clinics requiring adult epileptologists to be familiar with EDS.

Twelve distinct gene clusters with shared biological functions were identified using network-based clustering. These clusters highlighted the pathogenic drivers of EDS such as variants responsible for ion channels, GMP signaling, and others mediating neuronal transcription factors and synaptic function. Bioinformatics computational methods such as comparative phenotype enrichment analysis were used to enhance and build the phenotype for each genetic variant.

A majority of patients exhibited a hyperkinetic MD (76%), most commonly dystonia (34%), stereotypies (25%) and ataxia (16%). About half of all patients had a mixed often complex MD. MDs that commonly co-occurred were dystonia with chorea, dystonia with spasticity and ataxia with tremor. Significant associations were observed between MDs and genetic variants. For example, stereotypies were most common in patients with MECP2, and dystonia was most frequent in patients with GNAO1 and PRRT2. Ataxia was commonly observed in patients with CACNA1A, SCN1A or SLC2A1. Chorea was common in patients with FOXG1 and PRRT2 variants. A history of status dystonicus was reported in 7.5% (15 of 200) patients with dystonia. The association of paroxysmal tonic up gaze and CACNA1-related disease was highlighted. Generalized dystonia seen in MECP2-Rett syndrome. Motor stereotypies and chorea were seen in FOXG1-related disorders.

While all patients had a MD, epilepsy was present in only 67% of cases, likely due to variability or timing of expression given the cross-sectional nature of the study. The presence of epilepsy was associated with more severe disability and motor impairment. Most seizures began before the MD but this varied across genetic etiology.

37.2% of epilepsy diagnosis met criteria for DEE. Generalized motor (53%), focal seizures (21%) and generalized nonmotor (17%) were the most common seizure types. As expected, infantile spasms and generalized motor seizures were associated with CDKL5 3 and generalized nonmotor seizures with SLC2A1 4 or CACNA1A. 5

The article also examined how the MD and epilepsy related to functional gene clusters. Stereotypies were frequently seen in genes involved in cortical development. Tremor was seen in variants affecting the GABA A receptor. Chorea dominated gene clusters involved in cGMP-mediated signaling. Other novel associations included paroxysmal nocturnal dyskinesias in PRRT2, chorea in CDKL5, stereotypies in GNAO1, and dystonia in GRIN2B. Insights from these associations may drive further investigation into more disease specific therapies.

Of course, medication response data from a subjective cross-sectional survey of experts is limited in scientific validity but highlighted important associations. Similar information might be gleaned for example from opinions of experts at scientific meetings, so remain of value, particularly in rare disorders where large clinical trials are unlikely to occur. Complete control of seizures was seen in 42%, partial control in 43% and no control in 14% of patients. Some expected associations were seen such as medication-resistance epilepsy with CDKL5, 3 and medication responsiveness in PRRT2. 6 Less expected was that some antiseizure medications could worsen the MD in EDS such as perampanel in patients with ARX.

Eighty-seven percent of patients had developmental delay evenly divided among mild, moderate and severe in the under 5-year-olds and a prevalence of intellectual disability in 80% of the over 5 year old cohort. A degree of motor impairment was seen in 78% with 44% requiring a walking aid or wheelchair at a median age of 16 years, depending on the genetic etiology.

There is so much to unpack here, so reading of the paper and its visual and supplemental materials is strongly recommended.

The study adds important phenotypic information to previous datasets which have focused on either the MD or epilepsy in single gene disorders.

Limitations of the study include the phenomenological classification of MD and seizures from surveys rather than video-based assessment which can cause recall or reporting bias and inaccuracies in classification. As the study is cross-sectional there is no longitudinal data, although an analysis across the age spectrum was informative, for example, showing that hyperkinetic MD occurred earlier and hypokinetic MD later, or that the phenotype may change over time such as emergence of parkinsonism and spasticity in late childhood or even adults with FOXG1, SLC2A1 or WDR45 variants.

The numbers for many of the predefined variants were low or not even reported by the survey highlighting the ultra-rare nature of many genetic causes of EDS, and need for cross-collaboration among centers to study them in a longitudinal manner in sufficient numbers to determine the true natural history. Genetic testing is still not easily accessible in many regions; detailed phenotypes may allow us to recognize these clinico-genetic syndromes earlier or reclassify known variants. We should not forget about acquired causes of EDS such as post-hypoxic-ischemic, infectious, or autoimmune etiologies for which little is known of the phenotype and natural history.

The study highlights the need for early recognition of MD in epilepsy cohorts and close collaboration with our MD colleagues. Decisions on specialized medication or neuromodulation may need the input of both specialties and strongly supports joint clinics for EDS. Recognition of the underlying shared pathophysiology and natural history may lead to personalized or precision treatment approaches, prerequisites for much needed downstream therapeutic clinical trials.
