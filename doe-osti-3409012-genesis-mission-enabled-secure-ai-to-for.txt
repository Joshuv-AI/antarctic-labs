Summary

Argonne National Laboratory is supporting the U.S. Department of Transportation’s (USDOT’s)
Bureau of Transportation Statistics (BTS) with collaborative research on development and
application of privacy preserving AI frameworks that leverage unmatched AI expertise and
secure computing resources made available through the U.S. Genesis Mission 1. This research
advances U.S. energy security goals by supporting a safe offshore energy industry with secure,
domain-specific AI tools to analyze confidential industry datasets collected by BTS to rapidly
improve identification of hazards, precursors, and systemic safety risks in high-risk operational
environments.

The staged, security-first approach begins with development and testing of Argonne’s Genesis
Mission-enabled Secure AI to Fortify Energy Process Safety (Genesis-SAFE) framework within
Argonne’s accredited secure computing enclave (ABLE) leveraging Argonne’s AI scientific
assistant substrate (AISAC). Methods to build synthetic datasets were developed together with
BTS for use in preparing synthetic datasets that can be used to validate data containment,
governance, and security controls in the ABLE environment. Future research directions would
focus on applying the Genesis-SAFE framework to CIPSEA-protected datasets entirely within
ABLE to support confidentiality-preserving analysis of safety risks, trends, and contributing
factors.

1. Background and Partnership

BTS is the USDOT’s principal statistical agency, responsible for supplying statistically sound
baseline, contextual, and trend data used to shape transportation policy, investments, and
research across the United States and abroad. BTS data collections include such topics as the
national infrastructure and performance, transportation economics, commercial aviation, and
transportation safety. BTS is also the home of the National Transportation Library, which serves
as a repository for all DOT research, as well as a portal to information on the transportation
system and its consequences. As a leading source of timely, accurate, and reliable information
on the U.S. transportation systems used for moving people and goods, BTS seeks to
continuously improve its products and services and meet information needs by leveraging 21st
century methods and technologies.

BTS’ precursor safety data program includes several statistical data collections across
transportation modes and domains, including transit safety, maritime safety, and offshore
energy safety. Since 2017, Argonne has provided technical and analytical assistance in support
of BTS’ SafeOCS program, a confidential safety data reporting program for offshore energy,
sponsored by the U.S. Department of the Interior and independently administered by BTS.
Building on this foundation, Argonne and BTS initiated an effort in 2025 to explore whether
recent advances in AI can be applied safely and responsibly to confidential data to enhance
process safety insights—while preserving statutory confidentiality protections—to develop
broader insights for enhancing process safety in high-risk industries including the offshore
energy and maritime sectors.

2. Project Overview

As federal agencies and industry are exploring how to better use data to improve safety in high-
risk industries, there is a growing demand for domain-specific AI-enabled safety enhancement
frameworks that address the associated risks, process complexity, and data security
requirements. To meet this need, Argonne is leveraging the laboratory’s advanced computing
facilities and core capabilities in computational science, systems engineering, and safety risk
assessment to develop domain-specific, confidentiality-preserving AI tools to strengthen
process safety in high reliability organizations.

High reliability organizations (HROs) are known for their ability to remain stable and consistent
while navigating high-risk and complex situations, such as nuclear power plants, the VA health
system, oil & gas operations, and aircraft carrier operations:
 • Nuclear Power Plants: Manage a high-hazard technology and complex systems to
 prevent catastrophic failure.
 • The U.S. Veterans Health Administration (VHA): A large, integrated healthcare system
 that has formally adopted HRO principles to improve patient safety.
 • Oil and gas offshore operations are also considered high-reliability organizations (HROs)
 because they are high-risk environments that require systems and a culture focused on
 consistently avoiding catastrophic failure, despite operating in complex and hazardous
 conditions.
 • Air Traffic Control: Managing a complex, dynamic system with little room for error.

Energy process safety can be strengthened in HROs by:
  Leverage Argonne's advanced computing facilities, AI capabilities and expertise in safety
 risk assessment
  Develop and apply domain-specific, privacy-preserving, AI-enabled safety enhancement
 frameworks
Argonne, as a federally funded research and development center, is uniquely positioned to
provide science-based independent analyses, safety assessment methods, high-fidelity data,
and decision support tools to enhance the national capacity to build and operate safe, resilient,
and secure energy systems.

This technical report describes AI for Energy Process Safety (AI-EPS) research at Argonne to
develop and apply an LLM-driven multi-agent foundational model—embedded with knowledge
in oil and gas safety—that provides a data secure and transparent AI-EPS tool for use with
diverse stakeholders to better analyze root causes of near-miss safety incidents, predict

potential hazards, and suggest preventative actions to mitigate safety risks and achieve higher
levels of process safety.

Large Language Models (LLMs) provide a new method of analyzing textual data, as well as
enhanced reasoning power through development of multi-agent frameworks where each agent
is an LLM-driven module specialized in performing a complex task like data ingestion, analysis,
and evaluation. As part of this collaboration an LLM-driven multi-agent foundational model was
deployed, embed with knowledge in oil and gas safety, that provides unique and insightful
statistical analyses in a data secure user-friendly manner. This leverages the AI Scientific
Assistant Core (AISAC) 2 software developed at Argonne for knowledge grounded, provenance-
first, and data-secure research workflows. The AI-EPS tool will enable diverse stakeholders to
better understand major safety risks, identify underlying causes of near-miss safety incidents,
achieve high levels of safety, and avoid catastrophic failures while preserving confidentiality.

3. Argonne secure enclave ABLE

Data security is a key factor limiting the ability of safety data programs to tap into the powerful
capabilities of AI. Argonne’s internal generative AI resources, deployed within an isolated
enclave, represent a potential pathway for BTS to leverage these capabilities to advance
analysis of its confidential safety data.

It is essential to maintain security of participant data collected and analyzed under BTS
precursor safety data programs. The BTS confidentiality statute (49 U.S.C. 6307(b)) and
CIPSEA protect the information BTS collects for its programs. These laws require BTS to
ensure that any identifying, sensitive, or proprietary information collected is not released to
unauthorized persons or organizations.

A central element of the project is the Argonne Biomedical Learning Environment (ABLE), a
secure computing enclave designed to support analysis of sensitive data under controlled
conditions. To assure data privacy, the AI-EPS framework was built and deployed locally on
ABLE, with Argonne leveraging the Argo API which provides data-secure access to multiple
models by OpenAI, Anthropic and Google Gemini. In the local deployment, necessary security
measures and practices were set in place to allow Argonne to handle the secure data from and
communicate analytical insights to BTS. In the future, this will enable analysis of confidential
CIPSEA-protected industry datasets collected by BTS.

With ABLE, Argonne has established the necessary policies, procedures, controls and systems
to support analysis of sensitive data. ABLE is Argonne’s moderate security enclave that
implements NIST 800-53 controls with separation of duties between administration and
research activities. The enclave has the necessary privacy controls implemented into all
processes providing compliance with HIPAA and FISMA requirements for handling protected
health information and personally identifiable information. ABLE has received a 3-year
authorization to operate (ATO) from Department of Veterans Affairs after a thorough review of
capabilities and practices.

The enclave’s compliance to enable secure handling of sensitive data is reviewed and certified
by a third-party entity, called Avertium LLC, semi-annually. This Risk Assessment evaluates the

4. Project Accomplishments

4.1 Deployment of the AI-EPS multi-agent AI framework using AISAC

Under this Genesis-SAFE research project, an AI-EPS decision-support framework was
implemented using AISAC, which served as the underlying multi-agent software infrastructure
for this work. AISAC was initially developed under an Argonne Laboratory Directed Research
and Development (LDRD) effort and was subsequently further advanced under this project.

Within this study, AISAC provided the core infrastructure required to realize a functional multi-
agent system, including agent orchestration, tool integration, execution control, and connectivity
to both local and external computational and information resources. This software layer enabled
the coordinated operation of specialized agents responsible for distinct tasks such as
information retrieval, document interpretation, structured extraction, and analytical support. In
this role, AISAC supported the full multi-agent ecosystem underlying AI-EPS, allowing the
framework to move beyond a single-model chatbot paradigm toward a modular and extensible
architecture capable of executing more complex safety-analysis workflows.

Using this infrastructure, the AI-EPS framework was designed with an LLM functioning as a
central reasoning component while interacting with modular agents and tools to complete task-
specific operations. These operations include retrieving and interpreting safety-related
documentation, organizing extracted content into structured forms, and assisting with the
analysis of operational and incident-related information. The framework was informed by
Argonne’s domain expertise in offshore oil and gas safety as well as by the analytical
requirements of BTS safety-data programs, and it was developed to support both structured and
unstructured information sources, including near-miss narratives, equipment failure records,
standards and guidance documents, and publicly available reference materials. The architecture
also supports human-in-the-loop review, enabling subject-matter experts to introduce validation
and oversight where appropriate.

An important feature of AISAC in the present context is its largely LLM-agnostic design.
Because the framework is not tightly coupled to a single model provider or deployment pathway,
it is compatible with both commercial hosted models, and Argonne native LLM endpoints such
as Argo or ALCF Inference endpoints, as well as locally deployed models. This was particularly
advantageous for operation on ABLE, where the framework could be run with self-hosted
models, including local Ollama deployments, with minimal porting effort.

As a result, the same multi-agent infrastructure—including orchestration, tools, and resource
connectivity—could be retained while adapting the underlying model layer to the computing and
security requirements of the target environment.

The completed work established the core agentic architecture and demonstrated the feasibility
of using a domain-specific AI framework for process safety analysis. The final deployment is a
user-friendly multi-agent framework on ABLE for use in analyzing available safety data to draw
unique insight, in an autonomous manner.

4.2 Deployment and testing on ABLE

The project established the processes needed to bring code, data, and model assets into ABLE
and demonstrated basic agentic workflow functionality using a locally hosted LLM approach.
This reduced one of the project’s highest technical risks: whether the “reasoning engine” of an
agentic framework could operate entirely within the enclave without transmitting data or prompts
outside the secure boundary. Testing showed that the framework could function in ABLE using
self-hosted model infrastructure, Ollama, and providing a viable path for secure, enclave-based
AI analysis.

Realizing this locally-hosted approach required adapting AISAC, the underlying multi-agent
software on which AI-EPS is built, since it had been designed around external cloud LLM
endpoints rather than self-hosted models. This involved adding a new model-serving pathway
into AISAC’s core routing logic, along with a mechanism to automatically locate the GPU node
running the model, since ABLE’s job scheduler assigns compute nodes dynamically, and a fixed
connection point would otherwise break with each new job submission. The project also worked
through the practical steps of moving code and model assets from ABLE’s internet-connected
staging area into the fully isolated secure side, including resolving environment configuration
that does not transfer cleanly between the two. These steps were documented in a reusable
form, so the deployment process can be repeated or extended without re-solving the same
problems.

In successfully deploying and testing AISAC, the software underpinning the AI-EPS framework
components within ABLE, the project also clarified what is required to make such a framework
operable in a secure enclave rather than in a conventional development environment. This was
a significant accomplishment because secure enclaves impose constraints that can prevent
standard agentic AI implementations from functioning as designed, especially when those
implementations rely on external LLM APIs or cloud-based model access. This work establishes
feasibility of agentic AI for safety analysis within a controlled environment such as ABLE.

4.3 Methods for synthetic data preparation

To support secure AI development without exposing protected records, the project identified and
evaluated methods that could be used in future work to create synthetic datasets that mimic the
analytical structure and utility of the underlying confidential data while avoiding disclosure of
protected information. This was an important component of the overall effort because synthetic
data can serve several purposes in secure AI development: validating workflow design,
exercising data ingestion and analysis pipelines, and testing governance and containment
procedures. Synthetic data generation has become essential for addressing data scarcity,
privacy preservation, and testing in machine learning workflows.

The CIPSEA datasets contain a mixture of structured (numerical), unstructured (labeled), and
narrative (descriptions) data types. Because these data types present different challenges for
synthetic generation, the team evaluated candidate approaches for mixed tabular data and
considered future extensions for narrative content. Two methods were identified as particularly
relevant: copula-based statistical synthesis and Conditional Tabular Generative Adversarial
Networks (CTGAN).

Copula-based methods provide a transparent and computationally efficient way to model the
marginal distributions of individual variables and the dependence structure among them. These
methods are well suited for rapid prototyping and for datasets where interpretability and

statistical traceability are important. CTGAN methods, by contrast, are better suited to capturing
more complex nonlinear relationships, rare categories, and higher-order interactions in mixed
tabular data, though they generally require greater computational effort and tuning.

Initial experimentation with both approaches was carried out using public datasets selected to
resemble key structural features of the BTS data, including mixed numerical and categorical
fields. This work produced prototype Python/Jupyter workflows for evaluating accuracy, speed,
and implementation feasibility in a secure-AI development context. The tests did not yet include
narrative text fields, which remain a topic for future work. Synthetic treatment of narrative
content will likely require additional methods, such as text embeddings, controlled topic
grouping, or other language-model-assisted techniques, to preserve analytical value without
reproducing protected content.

Overall, this work established a practical methodological foundation for future synthetic-data
generation in support of GENESIS-SAFE. In the near term, copula-based methods appear well
suited for transparent baseline testing and workflow validation, while CTGAN offers a potential
path for higher-fidelity synthesis when more complex dependencies must be represented.
These methods can support future validation of security controls, enclave workflows, and AI
analysis pipelines prior to any approved use of confidential BTS datasets.

Additional technical detail on the synthetic-data methods evaluated in this project is provided in
Appendix A.

5. Significance of the Work and Future Directions

This project demonstrated that a domain-specific, agentic AI framework for energy process
safety can be designed with secure deployment as a primary requirement rather than an
afterthought. It also showed that ABLE can support the core technical components needed for
enclave-based AI workflows, including local model hosting and controlled execution of agentic
processes. The workflows can add immense value to the safety, security, and prosperity of
American energy supply. These accomplishments position Argonne and BTS for future phases
of work involving confidential data analysis under approved protections. They also provide a
strong foundation for extending AI-EPS to additional safety domains, including maritime
transportation and other high-risk operational settings where confidential safety data could be
used to identify hazards, precursor trends, contributing factors, and opportunities for prevention.

Appendix A
Synthetic Data Methods for Secure AI Workflow Development

Synthetic data generation was investigated as a supporting capability for secure AI development
within the GENESIS-SAFE effort. The primary objective is to create datasets that preserve
important analytical properties of the original data without disclosing protected information. In
this context, synthetic data may be used to:
 • validate data-ingestion, transformation, and analysis workflows;
 • test agentic AI pipelines within the ABLE enclave;
 • support evaluation of governance, containment, and security procedures;
 • enable method development before any future work with confidential datasets; and
 • provide a lower-risk environment for experimentation, debugging, and performance
 assessment.
For this project, synthetic data are intended to mimic the structure and statistical behavior of
confidential BTS safety data rather than replicate original records. The BTS datasets relevant to
this work contain multiple data types, including:
 • structured numerical data, such as counts, continuous values, or measured attributes;
 • categorical or labeled data, including classes, operating conditions, equipment types,
 or event categories; and
 • narrative text data, including descriptions of events, actions, perceptions, and
 contributing causes.
Narrative fields add an additional layer of complexity. In particular, useful synthetic narrative
data should ideally preserve some representation of the meaning of words and phrases; the
way events, causes, and effects are described; and the relationships among technical concepts
in incident descriptions.

Because of this, narrative-field synthesis may require additional approaches beyond
conventional tabular synthesis. One possible future direction is to use language-model-based
semantic grouping, embedding methods, or structured abstraction techniques so that narrative
content can be represented analytically without reproducing protected text.

Two candidate methods were identified as especially relevant for future synthetic-data
development with mixed tabular datasets:
 1. Copula-based statistical synthesis
 2. Conditional Tabular Generative Adversarial Networks (CTGAN)
These methods were selected because each offers complementary strengths for structured and
categorical data, and each can serve a different role in a staged synthetic-data workflow.

A.1 Copula-Based Method
A.1.1 Overview
Copula-based methods provide a statistically grounded approach for generating synthetic
tabular data by modeling:
 • the marginal distribution of each variable separately; and
 • the dependence structure among variables jointly.
This separation is useful because it allows the method to preserve the observed shape of each
feature while also capturing how features vary together. The resulting synthetic records are
newly sampled from the learned joint distribution rather than copied from the source data.
For project purposes, this makes copulas attractive as a “transparent baseline” method for
producing synthetic datasets that resemble the original data in distribution and correlation
structure while reducing the likelihood of row-level replication.

A.1.2 Conceptual Basis
The copula framework is based on Sklar’s theorem, which shows that a multivariate
distribution can be decomposed into:
 • its marginal cumulative distribution functions; and
 • a copula that captures dependence among variables.
In simplified form, if a multivariate distribution is written as:

                                                             𝐹𝐹(𝑥𝑥1 , 𝑥𝑥2 , … , 𝑥𝑥𝑑𝑑 ),

with marginals 𝐹𝐹1 , 𝐹𝐹2 , … , 𝐹𝐹𝑑𝑑 , then it can be represented as
                                  𝐹𝐹(𝑥𝑥1 , 𝑥𝑥2 , … , 𝑥𝑥𝑑𝑑 ) = 𝐶𝐶(𝐹𝐹1 (𝑥𝑥1 ), 𝐹𝐹2 (𝑥𝑥2 ), … , 𝐹𝐹𝑑𝑑 (𝑥𝑥𝑑𝑑 )),

where 𝐶𝐶 is the copula function.

This allows each variable to be transformed into a common uniform space on [0, 1], where the
dependency structure can be modeled independently of the original feature scales.
A commonly used option is the Gaussian copula, which models dependence through a
multivariate normal correlation matrix. In that case,
                          𝐶𝐶(𝑢𝑢; 𝑅𝑅) = Φ𝑅𝑅 (Φ−1 (𝑢𝑢1 ), Φ−1 (𝑢𝑢2 ), … , Φ−1 (𝑢𝑢𝑑𝑑 )),

where:
Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function,
Φ𝑅𝑅 is the multivariate normal cumulative distribution with correlation matrix 𝑅𝑅.

A.1.3 General Synthesis Procedure
A typical copula-based synthetic-data workflow proceeds as follows:
 1. Fit marginal distributions for each column
 o Numerical variables may use empirical or parametric distributions.
 o Categorical variables may use empirical frequencies.
 o Ordinal variables may be represented with cumulative probabilities or latent
 continuous variables.
 2. Transform the observed data into copula space
 o Each variable is mapped to a uniform scale using its fitted cumulative distribution.
 3. Estimate the dependence structure
 o For a Gaussian copula, the transformed variables are converted to normal scores
 and used to estimate a correlation matrix.
 4. Generate new samples in copula space
 o New vectors are drawn from the fitted multivariate dependence model.
 5. Map synthetic samples back to the original feature scales
 o Inverse marginal transformations are applied to recover numerical and
 categorical variables in their original representation.
 6. Post-process the synthetic dataset
 o Domain constraints, logical rules, type consistency, and formatting are checked
 and enforced.

A.1.4 Strengths and Limitations
Strengths
 • high interpretability;
 • fast training and sampling;
 • strong preservation of individual column distributions;
 • useful for baseline testing and explainable workflow development;

 • relatively low computational burden.
Limitations
 • limited ability to capture highly nonlinear dependencies;
 • weaker handling of complex interactions and tail dependence;
 • may be less effective for high-cardinality categorical variables;
 • narrative fields require additional treatment outside the standard copula framework.
For these reasons, copulas are a strong candidate for initial prototyping, workflow validation,
and transparent synthetic-data baselines.

A.2 CTGAN Method
A.2.1 Overview
Conditional Tabular Generative Adversarial Networks (CTGAN) are deep generative models
designed specifically for mixed tabular data. CTGAN extends the GAN paradigm to tabular
synthesis by learning to generate realistic synthetic rows that reflect the statistical properties
and dependencies of the source dataset.
Compared with copula-based methods, CTGAN is generally better able to represent:
 • nonlinear relationships,
 • multimodal numerical features,
 • imbalanced categorical variables, and
 • higher-order interactions among features.
This makes CTGAN attractive when the synthetic data must capture more complex structure
than can be represented by simpler probabilistic models.

A.2.2 Core Features
CTGAN incorporates several mechanisms designed for tabular data:
 • mode-specific normalization for numerical variables, allowing better handling of
 skewed or multimodal features;
 • conditional generation, which helps the model learn dependencies tied to specific
 categories; and
 • training-by-sampling, which improves representation of rare classes and imbalanced
 categorical variables.
In this framework:
 • the generator produces candidate synthetic rows from random noise and conditioning
 information; and
 • the discriminator attempts to distinguish synthetic rows from real rows.
Through adversarial training, the generator improves its ability to create synthetic samples that
resemble the original data distribution.

A.2.3 General Workflow
A CTGAN synthetic-data workflow typically includes:
 1. Preprocessing
 o identify variable types;
 o normalize numerical fields;
 o encode categorical fields.
 2. Training
 o train generator and discriminator adversarially over multiple epochs;
 o use conditional sampling to improve coverage of rare categories.
 3. Generation
 o sample noise vectors and conditions;
 o generate synthetic rows from the trained generator.
 4. Post-processing

 o decode variables into original formats;
 o enforce ranges, logical constraints, and domain-specific validity checks.

A.2.4 Strengths and Limitations
Strengths
 • captures complex nonlinear dependencies;
 • strong performance on mixed categorical and numerical data;
 • handles rare categories better than many simpler methods;
 • can preserve higher-order interactions;
 • supports targeted conditional generation.
Limitations
 • lower interpretability;
 • slower training and higher computational cost;
 • may require substantial hyperparameter tuning;
 • can experience instability or mode collapse;
 • still does not directly solve the problem of narrative-text synthesis.
For these reasons, CTGAN is often better suited to higher-fidelity production synthesis once
baseline workflows and evaluation criteria are established.

A.4 Initial Public-Dataset Testing
To explore implementation feasibility, both methods were tested on publicly available datasets
with mixed numerical and categorical structure. Prototype Python/Jupyter notebook workflows
were developed and executed to compare:
 • implementation complexity,
 • computational speed,
 • synthetic-data quality, and
 • suitability for later secure-enclave use.
The public datasets used in these early tests were chosen because they contain structured and

labeled tabular features broadly similar to those expected in the BTS context, although they do
not contain narrative text fields.
A.4.1 Synthetic Traffic Accident Risk Dataset
This dataset contains approximately 500,000 records and 10 columns, with mixed categorical
and numerical variables representing traffic scenarios such as weather, lighting, road type, time
of day, vehicle and driver information, and a continuous accident-risk target variable.
A.4.2 Heart Disease Prediction Dataset
This dataset contains mixed medical and demographic variables commonly used in
cardiovascular risk prediction. It provides a heterogeneous tabular structure useful for testing
synthetic-data methods, although it does not contain narrative fields.
These datasets were used for preliminary experimentation only. They served as stand-ins for
method development and testing and did not replace the need for future evaluation against the
structure and analytical needs of the BTS data.

A.5 Conclusion
Research into Synthetic Data Methods for Secure AI Workflow Development under this
Genesis-SAFEproject identified two promising synthetic-data approaches for future secure-AI
workflow development:
 • copula-based synthesis, offering speed, transparency, and strong baseline utility; and
 • CTGAN, offering improved fidelity for complex mixed-tabular relationships.
Together, these methods provide a practical technical foundation for future development of
synthetic datasets that can support testing of AI-EPS workflows, enclave deployment
procedures, and security controls before any approved use of confidential BTS data.
