Ancient DNA as a temporal lens: reconstructing evolution, migration, and disease dynamics
Authors: Abang Ulrich Akwo, Julius Luvanga, Ketonze Valademy Ketu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAncient DNA (aDNA) has transformed evolutionary biology and anthropology by providing direct, chronologically validated genetic evidence over millennia. This review synthesizes significant findings from the paleogenomic era (2010–2025), demonstrating how ancient DNA has resolved persistent debates across four interconnected themes: (i) human migration and admixture, revealing complex population transitions from archaic hominins to Holocene expansions; (ii) adaptation, tracking allele frequency changes during domestication and selection; (iii) pathogen history, clarifying the origins of pandemics and the evolution of microbiomes; and (iv) ecosystem dynamics, identifying extinction causes through sedimentary DNA and conservation genomics. We contend that scientific rigor and ethical stewardship are crucial for accurate conclusions, given ancient DNA study requires the destructive collection of culturally significant remains. This review argues that continued advancement will depend on the integration of genomic data with archaeological, isotopic, and proteomic evidence, and highlights the necessity for equitable involvement with descendant communities. By conceptualizing the past as a continuum of dynamic processes rather than static events, ancient DNA provides a revised historical narrative and insights relevant to contemporary concerns in conservation, health, and social justice.Keywords: Evolution, Paleogenomic, Genomic, Population

Introduction: rewriting history with molecules

Ancient DNA (aDNA) has transformed the study of evolution and human history from a method dependent on retrospective inference based mostly on contemporary genomes to a discipline grounded in direct, chronological genetic evidence. Traditional population and evolutionary genetics aimed to reconstruct history from a singular “static snapshot” of current diversity; however, ancient DNA (aDNA) provides sequential samples over time, converting that snapshot into a temporal lens as a functional “fourth dimension” in biology that enables the observation of genetic change as it occurs over centuries and millennia.1, 2, 3 Researchers routinely integrate ancient genomes into the genetic variation atlas by extracting DNA from archaeological and paleontological specimens, thus tracking changes in ancestries, population sizes, and interactions over time and across regions.1, 3 This temporal dimension has recontextualized many longstanding fundamental questions in evolution and history as matters amenable to precise hypothesis testing. In the exploration of human ancestry, ancient DNA (aDNA) has revealed previously unknown lineages, such as the Denisovans, and documented numerous occurrences of gene flow among Neanderthals, Denisovans, and modern humans, thereby contesting simplistic, tree-like representations of our species' history.2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 Research in population history indicates that extensive genomic time series reveal the settlement of regions like Europe and the Americas is marked by multiple migration events, admixture, and partial replacement, rather than uninterrupted local development, thereby requiring a reevaluation of conclusions based solely on contemporary DNA.2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 Adaptation, formerly inferred from current allele frequency patterns, can now be analyzed by tracking the temporal trajectories of functional variants, clarifying examples of selection on traits such as diet, pigmentation, and immunity, as well as adaptive introgression from archaic hominins.2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 Besides humans, ancient DNA time series from fauna, flora, and pathogens facilitate the correlation of demographic bottlenecks, domestication, and extinctions with climatic and cultural transformations at an unprecedented temporal resolution.1, 3, 9, 10 The use of ancient DNA as a historical tool depends entirely on methodological accuracy. Ancient molecules are ephemeral, degraded, and vulnerable to modern contamination; therefore, specialized extraction, library preparation, authentication, and analytical methodologies are essential prerequisites for reliable inference.3, 11, 12, 13 Innovative methodological research, including preliminary validations of authentic ancient sequences and contamination prevention measures for genomic-scale protocols concerning Neanderthal DNA, has established the criteria for assessing the reliability and analysis of aDNA data.11, 12, 13, 14 Contemporary primers and reviews synthesize these developments, outlining best practices for sampling, laboratory methodologies, damage modelling, and statistical analysis that substantiate all credible claims concerning historical migrations, adaptations, or extinctions.1, 3, 12, 13 The capacity of aDNA to convert theoretical narratives into measurable evolutionary “films” depends on the technological and analytical frameworks detailed in this methodological review.

Methodology

This review synthesizes the groundbreaking findings of the paleogenomic era (about 2010–2025) through a thematic narrative approach. A comprehensive literature search was conducted across Web of Science, PubMed, and Scopus to encompass this rapidly evolving field, employing targeted keywords such as “ancient DNA,” “archaeogenetics,” “ancient pathogens,” and “sedimentary ancient DNA,” limited to English-language publications. The reference lists of key reviews were examined to ensure comprehensive coverage. Studies were prioritized for in-depth discussion based on a hierarchy of criteria: first, adherence to stringent ancient DNA authentication protocols; second, the generation of genome-scale data enabling high-resolution inference; and third, the study's impact on resolving or fundamentally transforming a significant debate in anthropology, evolutionary biology, or palaeoecology. The synthesis is structured into four interconnected thematic divisions that represent the primary directions of investigation in the field: human migration and demographic history; adaptation and phenotypic evolution; disease and host-pathogen interactions; and ecology and environmental transformation. This notion seeks to highlight the difference between pre-genomic understanding and the empirically validated, temporally defined narratives enabled by ancient DNA. This qualitative narrative study does not employ traditional meta-analytic statistics but aims to provide a critical and coherent account of how paleogenomics has transformed our understanding of the distant past.

Rewriting human history: genomic reconstructions of migration and admixture

Archaic hominin introgression and modern human origins

Ancient DNA has significantly transformed our understanding of modern human origins by revealing that Homo sapiens interbred with extinct archaic hominins following their migration from Africa. Genome-wide analyses indicate that all non-African populations possess approximately 1–4% Neanderthal ancestry. Populations in Melanesia and Papua New Guinea possess an extra 2–5% Denisovan heritage, the highest globally, obtained from Neanderthal DNA.15, 16 The extended average fragment length of Denisovan ancestry in Oceanian populations indicates a more recent mixing event relative to that of Neanderthals.17 Numerous analyses demonstrate at least two distinct Denisovan gene flow events influencing East/South Asians and Papuans, with various Denisovan lineages contributing to contemporary Papuan genomes.18, 19 African populations exhibit relatively minimal identifiable archaic sequences, potentially indicating restricted back-migration from Eurasia or the existence of unsampled archaic lineages inside Africa.20 This genomic heritage comprises a combination of adaptive advantages and detrimental incompatibilities. A functionally important fraction of introrse archaic variations increased in frequency due to positive selection, offering adaptive benefits in novel contexts. Genome-wide scans have identified more than 100 high-frequency archaic haplotypes enriched for genes associated with immunology, pigmentation, and metabolism, including OAS1/2/3, TLR1/6/10, and OCA2.21, 22 The most evident instance is the Denisovan-derived haplotype at the EPAS1 gene in Tibetans, which facilitates high-altitude adaption and exhibits clear indicators of positive selection.23 Likewise, unique Neanderthal-like and Denisovan-like haplotypes in the innate immunity gene cluster TLR6–TLR1–TLR10 are present at abnormally high frequencies and have been associated with altered immunological responses.21 This adaptive legacy is, however, opposed by substantial evidence of purifying selection against incompatible archaic DNA. Genomic maps indicate a systematic reduction in Neanderthal and Denisovan ancestry adjacent to genes, particularly on the X chromosome and in regions with high testicular expression, a pattern that aligns with diminished hybrid fertility.17 Extensive “archaic deserts,” megabase-scale areas nearly wholly without introgression, are significant in the genome and likely contain sites where archaic alleles were fundamentally incompatible with contemporary human biology.17 The ancient genetic contribution represents a twofold legacy: a minor proportion of polymorphisms that facilitated adaption to new circumstances, and a more substantial proportion that was eliminated due to harmful effects, resulting in a lasting signature of selection across the human genome.

Holocene population transformations in Eurasia

The Neolithic transition and ensuing Bronze Age migrations in Eurasia signify two of the most significant demographic changes in human prehistory, with ancient DNA playing a crucial role in elucidating their genetic effects. Examinations of genome-wide data from numerous ancient individuals indicate that Europe's genetic composition over the past 12,000 years has been influenced by at least two significant, overlapping demographic waves, resulting in a three major ancestry components in contemporary Europeans, originating from indigenous hunter-gatherers, Anatolian Neolithic farmers, and Steppe pastoralists.24, 25 The Neolithic transition, commencing around 6400 BCE, though regional variation existed across the continent was characterized by a significant north-westward proliferation of Anatolian farmer-related ancestry throughout continental Europe. Early Neolithic populations in central and western Europe constitute a cohesive genetic cluster closely associated with Neolithic groups from Anatolia, distinctly separated from indigenous Mesolithic hunter-gatherers, indicating that the dissemination of agriculture entailed considerable demographic diffusion.24, 26 In numerous areas, especially in western and central Europe, early agriculturalists exhibit a near-complete genetic replacement of indigenous hunter-gatherer populations.27 Nonetheless, this process was not consistent. Genetic data from locations like Iberia, the Lower Danube basin, and the Baltic indicate centuries of coexistence and admixture between entering farmers and indigenous hunter-gatherers, with varying levels of hunter-gatherer ancestry remaining.27, 28 A significant revival of hunter-gatherer ancestry is evident in Middle Neolithic populations over most of Europe, suggesting intricate, bidirectional connections that obscure the clear distinction between cultural and demic diffusion theories.24 A significant genetic shift occurred between 3000 and 2500 BCE, marked by the swift westward proliferation of Steppe pastoralist ancestry associated with the Yamnaya herders of the Pontic-Caspian steppe. Late Neolithic Corded Ware populations in central and northern Europe possess over approximately 70–75% of their ancestry from a Yamnaya-related origin, indicating a significant and swift migration episode.24 The Steppe ancestry disseminated throughout much of Europe within a millennium through related archaeological complexes, frequently leading to near-total local population replacements, as evidenced in Denmark, and ultimately contributing 10–60% of the ancestry in contemporary Europeans.27, 29 These genetic discoveries have clarified enduring archaeological and linguistic controversies by illustrating that the dissemination of Steppe cultural packages and probable Indo-European languages into Europe entailed extensive population migrations, overlaying the pre-existing genetic mosaic established during the Neolithic era.

Case study: the peopling of the Americas

The early colonization of the Americas exemplifies how ancient DNA can transform conjectural migratory theories into a quantitatively defined demographic history. Through the analysis of genomes from ancient individuals in the Americas and Northeast Asia, researchers have clarified enduring questions regarding time, migration routes, and population composition. Genetic evidence robustly corroborates the Beringian Standstill model, indicating that the progenitors of all Native Americans separated from East Asian populations approximately 36–23 thousand years ago (ka) and remained genetically sequestered in Beringia for several millennia during the Last Glacial Maximum.30, 31 Whole-genome data evidence indicates that a major founding population, likely representing the primary ancestral source for Native Americans, emerged no earlier than around 23,000 years ago, though the possibility of additional minor contributing populations cannot be excluded. This population then diversified across the continent, dividing into Northern Native American (NNA) and Southern Native American (SNA) branches approximately 17.5–14.6 ka.30 Ancient genomes indicate that SNA-related groups swiftly migrated from north to south around 14,000 years ago, resulting in the emergence of various regionally structured populations across the continents.32 This image of an initial founding pulse was subsequently confounded by supplementary, more localized gene flow from Asia. For example, Eskimo–Aleut speakers possess approximately 50 % of their ancestry from a more recent Siberian origin, while Na-Dene speakers exhibit a smaller yet distinct contribution from a Paleo-Eskimo-related group, indicating that the settlement of the Americas entailed both an initial colonization and later Holocene migrations.33, 34 Ancient DNA has uncovered unforeseen and profound population organization within the ancestral lineage. The whole genome of the approximately 11,500-year-old Upward Sun River child (USR1) from Alaska unveiled a previously unidentified “Ancient Beringian” population that constitutes a basal branch to all other ancient and contemporary Native Americans.30 This population seems to have been predominantly supplanted or assimilated by subsequent northward migrations of NNA groups, rendering USR1 an ancient lineage without known direct descendants in modern populations, thereby underscoring the intricate demographic dynamics of early America.30 Genomic studies have elucidated the unique origins and trajectories of Arctic populations, indicating that Paleo-Eskimo groups (e.g., Saqqaq, Dorset) constitute a distinct lineage that settled in the Arctic approximately 5000 years ago and was predominantly supplanted by the forebears of contemporary Neo-Eskimos (Inuit and Yupik), exhibiting limited genetic continuity.33 These findings collectively demonstrate the capability of ancient DNA to refine the chronology of divergence and migration events, elucidate dispersal pathways, and uncover intricate population patterns and replacements that were not apparent through archaeological and contemporary genetic data alone.

The engine of adaptation: natural selection revealed through time

Direct observation of selective sweeps in human populations

Ancient DNA (aDNA) significantly transforms evolutionary inferences by enabling researchers to directly witness trajectories, facilitating the tracking of allele frequency variations over millennia. This time resolution has significantly enhanced the understanding of quintessential instances of human adaptation. The development of lactase persistence (LP) in Europe exemplifies a robust, recent selective sweep evidenced by ancient DNA. The pivotal lactase persistence (LP) regulatory variant (rs4988235-A) was nonexistent in Mesolithic hunter-gatherers and early Neolithic agriculturists, with the initial confirmed carriers emerging approximately 6000 years before present (BP) in Ukraine.35, 36 Its prevalence remained notably low (<1%) across Europe during the Bronze Age (∼4000–3200 BP), indicating that the proliferation of dairying and the significant Steppe pastoralist migrations were unable to facilitate its increase.35 aDNA indicates a pronounced sigmoidal increase commencing in the late Holocene, reaching frequencies approximating present levels (∼72%) by the mediaeval era.37 This trajectory signifies robust positive selection (selection coefficient s ≈ 0.05–0.06 per generation) over the past 3000–4000 years, contesting simplistic models that correlate selection intensity directly with milk consumption and implying that factors such as famine or pathogen exposure may have served as significant amplifiers.35, 38 Likewise, ancient DNA has elucidated the intricate history of skin pigmentation in West Eurasia. Mesolithic Europeans, exemplified by the individual from La Braña, have ancestral genes associated with darker pigmentation characteristics.39 The evolution of lighter skin pigmentation in European populations was not a singular historic event but rather a protracted and incremental process. Light-skin alleles, such as those at the SLC24A5 locus, were introduced by Neolithic farmers and subsequently increased in frequency over the past 5000 to 10,000 years due to a combination of admixture and subsequent selection, clarifying the interplay between population movement and local adaptation.40, 41, 42 Direct Observation of Selective Sweeps in Human Populations is seen in Table 1 below.

The temporal perspective of ancient DNA is equally effective for examining selection caused by pathogens. Genome-wide analyses reveal that the majority of adaptive changes in human immune genes occurred following the onset of the Bronze Age (less than 4500 years BP).41 Detailed analysis has documented natural selection during historical pandemics; investigations of individuals from before, during, and after the Black Death in London and Denmark revealed significant frequency shifts in variants at loci such as ERAP2 throughout the 14th century, indicating Yersinia pestis as a potent selective force.44 Moreover, ancient DNA elucidates the historical framework for adaptive introgression, indicating that prevalent Neanderthal and Denisovan haplotypes at innate immunity genes like as the TLR6-TLR1-TLR10 cluster have been enduring targets of selection.21 This method also establishes the chronology of notable adaptations, shown by the Denisovan-derived EPAS1 haplotype in Tibetans, illustrating that selection has enhanced this introgressed variant within the past few millennia.23 Collectively, these case studies redefine our understanding of adaptation from a collection of static images into dynamic “evolutionary trajectories,” whereby the intensity, timing, and ecological context of selection can be quantified directly.

Domestication as a real-time evolutionary experiment

Ancient DNA has transformed our understanding of domestication, demonstrating it not as a discrete “event” but as a prolonged, interconnected process involving various populations, recurrent gene flow, and evolving selection pressures over extensive durations. The domestication of canines illustrates this intricacy. Genomic data suggest a Late Pleistocene separation from wolves (11–16 thousand years ago), occurring before the advent of agriculture, with no singular current wolf population recognized as the exclusive ancestor.45 By around 11,000 years ago, a minimum of five distinct dog lineages had already emerged, signifying a profound pre-agricultural population structure.46 Moreover, dogs originate from various, separate wolf populations throughout Eurasia, and regional transitions like the nearly total replacement of Neolithic European dogs underscore a history of significant dog-wolf gene exchange and intricate demographic changes.46, 47 Conversely, horse domestication seems to be a subsequent and more pronounced phase. The contemporary domestic horse lineage (DOM2), representing the primary genetic cluster from which all modern domestic horses descend emerged in the lower Volga-Don steppe between 2200 and 2000 BCE, had a significant demographic bottleneck, and subsequently proliferated throughout Eurasia, supplanting nearly all indigenous wild horse lineages.48 Ancient genomes verify that early domesticated species, such as the Botai horses, are related to the ancestors of modern Przewalski's horses rather than contemporary domesticates, highlighting this trend of replacement.49 Narratives of plant domestication have also been substantially refined. The evolution of maize from its wild progenitor, teosinte, was driven by the selection of existing genetic variation rather than novel mutations. The essential tga1 allele, which accounts for the “naked kernel” phenotype, was present in wild teosinte populations thousands of years prior to domestication. Across these varied species, ancient DNA indicates convergent evolutionary trajectories that underlie the “domestication syndrome,” characterized by a set of associated features such as tameness and morphological alterations. Comparative genomics indicates that selection consistently focused on genes associated with neural crest cell formation and neurotransmitter signaling in dogs, horses, sheep, goats, and chickens, despite variations in the exact genes implicated.52, 53 This illustrates that domestication, regardless of its diverse timeframes and demographic histories, frequently modifies common developmental and brain gene networks to yield analogous phenotypic results.

Calibration of evolutionary clocks utilizing ancient genomes

A primary difficulty in evolutionary genetics is the “time-dependency” of molecular clocks: mutation rates derived from recent pedigrees can exceed substitution rates predicted from ancient fossil calibrations, resulting in contradictory date estimations.54 Ancient DNA offers a robust approach by providing directly dated genomes that act as calibration points within evolutionary lineages. By positioning radiocarbon-dated ancient mitochondrial and nuclear genomes at the tips of phylogenetic trees, researchers can estimate substitution rates over known intervals spanning tens of thousands of years. This tip-calibration approach improves rate estimation and, critically, allows for the modelling of temporal rate variation, a phenomenon where substitution rates are not constant over evolutionary time.55, 56 A significant discovery from these investigations is that tip-calibrated rates frequently exhibit faster rates than those obtained from conventional node-based techniques reflecting the time-dependent decay of apparent substitution rates over deeper evolutionary timescales. Utilizing these empirically validated, temporally calibrated rates allows for more accurate estimation of divergence dates, with shifts occurring in either direction depending on the specific dataset and calibration model employed. This improved accuracy has helped reconcile genetic timelines with archaeological and fossil evidence, resolving longstanding discrepancies between molecular and paleontological estimates for key events such as the emergence and spread of anatomically modern humans.57 This calibration power encompasses demographic models. Techniques such as the Pairwise Sequentially Markovian Coalescent (PSMC), which deduce historical population estimates from genomic sequences, necessitate an estimated mutation rate to translate coalescent units into years. Estimates derived from ancient DNA offer a vital empirical foundation, diminishing uncertainty and facilitating more precise reconstructions of demographic history for humans and other animals.57 The ancient genomes convert the molecular clock from simple rate-based approximations into a precise instrument, producing more dependable and accurate timelines for evolutionary occurrences.

Invisible companions: the deep history of pathogens and microbiomes

Reconstructing pandemic origins from ancient pathogen genomes

Ancient DNA has revolutionized our understanding of pandemic history by transforming historical outbreaks into precisely dated evolutionary occurrences. This methodology has uncovered profound origins, unforeseen transmissions, and the evolving characteristics of disease populations across millennia. The history of plague (Yersinia pestis) illustrates this change and is previously regarded as a predominantly mediaeval affliction, ancient DNA has revealed that Y. pestis infected humans in Eurasia as far back as 5000 years ago, with Late Neolithic and Bronze Age genomes constituting an extinct clade that possibly disseminated alongside Steppe population expansions.58 Ancient DNA has offered conclusive etiological and phylogenetic clarity for historically known pandemics. The extraction of Y. pestis DNA from 6th-century skeletons verified the bacterium as the etiological agent of the Justinianic Plague (541–750 CE) and positioned the strain within an ancestral phylogenetic branch, suggesting an Asian origin.59 Subsequent genomes from the First Pandemic indicate it was not a static strain but a divergent lineage with significant microdiversity.60 Research on the Black Death reveals a uniform genetic introduction into Europe, subsequently leading to centuries of diversification into various clades that established local reservoirs and subsequent outbreaks, linking the mediaeval pandemic to both earlier and contemporary strains.61, 62 Ancient DNA has elucidated a persistent enigma regarding tuberculosis in the Americas. Skeletal evidence indicated pre-contact tuberculosis; however, genomic analysis of 1000-year-old Peruvian people demonstrated that the causal agent was not a human-adapted Mycobacterium tuberculosis but rather a strain most closely related to M. pinnipedii, a pathogen affecting seals and sea lions.63 This discovery demonstrated a zoonotic transmission from marine mammals and contested the belief that New World tuberculosis originated exclusively from European arrivals post-1492. Recent ancient genomes from inland South America indicate that this marine lineage disseminated through human-to-human transmission, and molecular dating of the M. tuberculosis complex, employing ancient calibrations, corroborates a Holocene emergence associated with expanding, sedentary populations.64, 65 Ancient hepatitis B virus (HBV) genomes indicate a long-standing human-virus relationship extending into the Holocene, with evidence of infection in hunter-gatherers dating back over 10,500 years.66 These chronologically marked genomes disclose a narrative of lineage replacement and extinction. Neolithic HBV strains from Europe constitute profoundly different clades that exhibit closer phylogenetic affinity to non-human monkey HBV than to any contemporary human genotype, signifying extinct viral lineages.67 Extensive sampling indicates that the HBV strains present in prehistoric Eurasia saw significant substitutions, with Mesolithic viral lineages ultimately being replaced by a lineage linked with agriculture, which subsequently vanished.66, 68 Ancient pathogen genomics collectively recontextualises pandemic history, indicating that present pathogen diversity comprises merely the survivors of a far bigger, dynamic, and fluctuating Holocene environment.

Tracking viral evolution and host-jumps across centuries

In addition to elucidating origins, ancient viral genomes offer distinct perspectives on evolutionary rates, host-switching dynamics, and the constraints of models reliant exclusively on contemporary diversity. The examination of parvoviruses in carnivores demonstrates how paleovirology affects the analysis of viral sequences incorporated into host genomes (endogenous viral elements) and archaic viral DNA uncovers enduring patterns of interspecies transmission. Phylogenetic analyses indicate that parvoviruses have repeatedly transferred between host species such as dogs, wolves, and cats over millennia, a process driven more by ecological opportunity and receptor compatibility than by the accumulation of significant new mutations.69, 70 This suggests that host jumps are not infrequent, unusual occurrences but a persistent characteristic of viral evolution across extensive periods.

Ancient HBV sequences provide as a compelling case study in viral evolutionary dynamics. These genomes exhibit a “time-dependent” evolutionary rate phenomena, characterized by a high substitution rate at brief, recent timelines that significantly diminishes over extended, millennial-scale durations.71 This elucidates the seeming paradox wherein a 16th-century Italian HBV genome phylogenetically aligns with contemporary genotype D, exhibiting minimal temporal structure over centuries. The pattern results from influences such as purifying selection, recombination, and site saturation, indicating that a singular, uniform “molecular clock” is insufficient for dating ancient viral divergence events. Moreover, ancient HBV genomes demonstrate a strong correlation between viral lineage distributions and significant human demographic events, such as the Neolithic expansion, hence endorsing a hypothesis of prolonged host-pathogen co-dispersal.66 Collectively, these findings emphasize that viral evolution is contingent upon context, oscillating between swift adaptation during host transitions or epidemics and extended durations of relative stability within well-suited habitats.

Reconstructing ancestoral microbiomes and their evolution

The extraction of microbial DNA from archaeological materials has provided fresh insights into the evolutionary history of the human microbiome, correlating community alterations with significant food and lifestyle shifts. Dental calculus, also known as mineralized dental plaque, has demonstrated remarkable potential as a repository for ancient oral microbiomes. The in vivo mineralization encapsulates a microbial community that is safeguarded from post-mortem destruction and is highly indicative of oral taxa.72 Studies of calculus from individuals ranging from the Mesolithic period to the contemporary age have recorded significant ecological transformations. Hunter-gatherer oral communities exhibited notable diversity and had a reduced number of taxa linked to caries and periodontal disease.73 The Neolithic shift to agriculture was accompanied by a notable decrease in diversity and a switch to a disease-associated community profile, characterized by an abundance of cariogenic and periodontopathic bacteria, consistent with archaeological findings of heightened dental diseases.73 The most significant change transpired later, as the Industrial Revolution resulted in a notable decline in microbial diversity and the prevalence of highly acidogenic bacteria such as Streptococcus mutans, associated with the extensive intake of refined sugars and flour.72 Table 3 below shows the strengths and challenges of primary substrates for Ancient Microbiome Research.

Coprolites (ancient faeces) provide direct insight into the gut flora, although they pose considerable interpretation difficulties. Their DNA is frequently predominated by ambient bacteria from the adjacent soil, with less than half usually originating from anticipated gut taxa, complicating reliable authentication.74 Thus, dental calculus is presently regarded as a more dependable medium for examining temporal alterations in a preserved, host-associated microbial habitat. These ancient microbiome research possess significant implications for contemporary health. They illustrate that a “healthy” microbiome is not a fixed, universal condition but has co-evolved alongside human subsistence practices. The noted reduction in microbial variety from the Neolithic period to the industrial age indicates that present-day, low-diversity oral and gut microbiomes may represent what some researchers interpret as dysbiotic remnants of recent dietary alterations, perhaps exacerbating current illness prevalence.73 This historical viewpoint highlights the importance of utilizing suitable pre-industrial or ancestral baselines, rather than contemporary industrialized populations, when delineating healthy microbial states and formulating microbiome-based therapeutics.

Ecology and environmental change: ancient DNA as an ecosystem archive

Unraveling extinction causality in quaternary megafauna

The Late Quaternary extinctions of megafauna such as mammoths, woolly rhinoceroses, and gigantic ground sloths exemplify one of paleontology's most persistent controversies, typically divided between climatic change and human overkill explanations. Ancient DNA (aDNA) has transformed this discourse by uncovering the extended demographic and genetic pressures that preceded extinction, transcending simplistic endpoint narratives. Genomic investigations of woolly mammoths offer a comprehensive account of “extinction in progress.” Comparisons between a high-coverage genome from a ∼ 44,800-year-old mainland individual and one from a ∼ 4300-year-old mammoth from Wrangel Island indicate a population that had a prolonged bottleneck followed by a significant recent drop.75 The Wrangel individual exhibits roughly 20% reduced heterozygosity and a 28-fold escalation in homozygosity runs, evident genetic indicators of a diminutive, inbred population.75 Subsequent investigations revealed that this isolated relict population harbored an abundance of harmful mutations, signifying genomic deterioration due to strong genetic drift.76 These genomic findings reframe extinction as a gradual process of demographic decline, fragmentation, and reduced fitness that occurred over millennia, rather than a sudden occurrence. Importantly, sedimentary ancient DNA (sedaDNA) has demonstrated that diminutive, concealed populations persisted in refugia long after their apparent extinction from the macrofossil record. Permafrost cores from Alaska and Siberia indicate that mammoth and horse DNA remained in the environment until around 10,500 and 3900 years ago, respectively thousands of years later than the most recent bones found in those areas.77, 78 These ‘ghost ranges’ illustrate that megafauna coexisted with people for prolonged durations, challenging simplistic models of exclusively swift, human-induced extinction events and suggesting that extinction processes were more complex and protracted than previously assumed. The resulting image is not indicative of a singular cause, but rather of interrelated stressors. Climate-induced habitat alteration particularly the fragmentation and degradation of the mammoth steppe ecosystem initially diminished and segregated populations. The subsequent hunting pressure exerted by humans on these already weakened and genetically impaired populations likely contributed to their ultimate decline in numerous areas, though aDNA evidence alone cannot definitively establish causation or exclude the role of other interacting factors such as climate change and habitat fragmentation. This multi-stage model, elucidated by the historical genetic record, reconciles the traditional dichotomy between climatic and anthropogenic factors, illustrating that extinctions stemmed from the synergistic impacts of environmental change and human-induced pressures on species with pre-existing decreased resistance.

Ecosystem paleogenomics via sedimentary DNA

Sedimentary ancient DNA (sedaDNA) has transformed palaeoecology by offering a direct, comprehensive, and taxonomically extensive account of historical ecosystems derived from environmental materials such as permafrost, lake sediments, and cave deposits. The efficacy of sedaDNA resides in its capacity to obtain a comprehensive community snapshot from minimal quantities of genetic material released by various organisms into their surroundings.79 In contrast to conventional proxies such as pollen or macrofossils, which exhibit taxonomic bias, metagenomic sequencing of sedaDNA can concurrently identify plants, animals, birds, insects, fungi, and bacteria from a single sample.80 This methodology has been employed to reproduce the renowned “mammoth steppe,” the vast cold-adapted grassland ecosystem of the Pleistocene epoch. Comprehensive sedaDNA data from the Arctic indicate that this ecosystem sustained a distinctive assemblage of grasses, forbs, mammoths, woolly rhinoceroses, horses, and bison throughout the Last Glacial Maximum.78 The transition to the Holocene is characterized by a simultaneous reduction in steppe vegetation and its corresponding megafauna, supplanted by the proliferation of woody shrubs and a new assemblage of boreal forest fauna.78, 81

A primary advantage of sedaDNA is its heightened sensitivity. It can identify species that are uncommon, possess low fossilization potential, or are completely missing from the macrofossil record at a location. This has disclosed the initial occurrence of plant taxa absent in pollen records and, importantly, the prolonged existence of megafauna in refugial areas.81, 82 The approach has detected hominin DNA, including that of Neanderthals and Denisovans, in cave sediments in the absence of skeletal remains, showcasing its efficacy in uncovering elusive inhabitants.83 SedaDNA enhances our understanding of ecosystem responses to climate changes and human presence by offering a more comprehensive and intricate depiction of historical biotic groups and their transitions.

Conservation paleogenomics: informing management with deep-time data

Conservation paleogenomics employs ancient and historical DNA (defined here as DNA from specimens less than approximately 500 years old, often from museum collections) to determine historical baselines, identify decline reasons, and inform contemporary restoration techniques, surpassing the constraints of current genetic data.84, 85, 86 The situation of the woolly mammoth's last populations on Wrangel and St. Paul Islands is exemplary. Genomic investigations indicate that these isolated communities had significant reductions in genetic diversity and accumulated deleterious mutations, a phenomenon referred to as mutational meltdown.76 On St. Paul Island, multiproxy evidence correlated the exact period of extinction with freshwater scarcity due to the island's reduction in size, rather than human hunting.87 This accurate diagnosis genomic loss exacerbated by a particular environmental resource constraint offers a comprehensive framework for comprehending the complex vulnerabilities of small, isolated communities in contemporary times.85 The application of Ancient DNA in Conservation Paleogenomics is seen in Table 4 below.

Temporal genetic data are indispensable for existing vulnerable species. Comparisons between historical genomes (e.g., from museum specimens) and contemporary genomes can measure the precise loss of genetic diversity and effective population size, establishing significant recovery objectives.85 Moreover, ancient DNA can elucidate whether genetic variations across surviving groups stem from recent anthropogenic fragmentation (genetic drift) or signify profound evolutionary adaptations. This distinction is essential for conservation decisions: if variations are attributable to genetic drift, facilitated gene flow across populations can enhance genetic vitality; if they signify local adaptation, such admixture may be detrimental.86 Conservation paleogenomics offers an evidence-based, long-term view by reconstructing historical population connections, adaptive variation, and ecosystem composition. It recontextualizes conservation genetics from the management of the diminished remnants observed now to the restoration of the robust and interconnected evolutionary lineages and ecosystems that existed before significant human influence.

Ethical dimensions and responsible stewardship in ancient DNA research

Navigating core ethical tensions: destruction, colonial legacies, and community harm

Research on ancient DNA (aDNA) exists at a pivotal junction of scientific progress and significant ethical obligation. The discipline's reliance on destructive sampling of human remains generates an intrinsic conflict between the quest for knowledge and the conservation of limited, culturally relevant artefacts.3 The alteration or consumption of ancestral remains requires stringent justification based on potential scientific and societal benefits, adhering to ethical standards that prioritize minimal harm, comprehensive documentation, and the preservation of materials for future generations.88 In addition to physical devastation, the historical framework of ancient DNA study is laden with colonial legacies. Human remains in global museum collections frequently result from colonial extraction, and genetic research can inflict more harm by regarding ancestors as simply scientific specimens rather than familial connections, a technique criticized as ‘vampire science’.89, 90 Prominent conflicts, exemplified by the sequencing of Ancestral Puebloan DNA from Chaco Canyon without tribe input, underscore the significant societal damage that can occur when research is conducted unilaterally.90, 91 Such activities can compromise cultural sovereignty, jeopardize legal rights under legislation such as the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA), and exacerbate historical trauma by misrepresenting or oversimplifying intricate communal identities92 In reaction to these issues, fundamental ethical principles have been instituted. Alpaslan-Roodenberg et al. (2021) delineate five worldwide principles in the “Ethics of DNA Research on Human Remains”: compliance with rules, formulation of comprehensive strategies, mitigation of harm, assurance of data accessibility, and stakeholder engagement.88 This paradigm establishes an essential foundation; nevertheless, Indigenous researchers and allies contend that it falls short, as it may continue to prioritize scientific interests and the principle of open data over community governance.93 The Core Ethical Tensions in Ancient DNA Research and Evolving Frameworks is seen in Table 5 below.

Equitable partnerships: indigenous data sovereignty and collaborative models

The ethical critique of top-down research has sparked a significant movement towards Indigenous Data Sovereignty (IDS) and collaborative partnership frameworks. The Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics (CARE) provide a framework that reconceptualises genetic data as a relational obligation rather than an open-access resource.93, 95 This paradigm shifts states that Indigenous communities possess the right to govern data collection, management, interpretation, and distribution, ensuring that research yields concrete collective benefits and adheres to community values. This notion is progressively being implemented through innovative, community-oriented initiatives. Collaborations with Pueblo nations and other Indigenous people illustrate the potential for co-designing research from the beginning, incorporating oral histories alongside genetic data with community supervision of sampling, processing, and storytelling.91, 95 Researchers in the Global South promote “sustainable paleogenomics,” emphasizing long-term local capacity development, opposing “helicopter science,” and ensuring that research addresses enquiries pertinent to source communities.98, 99 These collaborations are additionally reinforced by developing legislative frameworks, like provincial legislation in Patagonia that confers statutory decision-making authority to Indigenous groups on ancestral remains and genetic data.99 Authentic ethical practice transcends mere consultation to achieve true co-governance. It encompasses collective authoring, community-driven interpretation, benefit-sharing agreements, and joint decisions on data access acknowledging that equitable partnership is not a hindrance to research but a fundamental requirement for its responsible and sustainable execution.

Responsible science communication in a politicized landscape

The capacity of aDNA to elucidate human origins and lineage entails a significant obligation for precise and considerate communication. Research results are frequently rapidly integrated into political discourses concerning identity, belonging, and sovereignty, potentially causing considerable social damage when oversimplified or misinterpreted.100 A key difficulty is the pervasive confusion of genetic lineage with cultural or political identity. Genetic data elucidate patterns of biological kinship and population history that is complex, continuous, and reliant on models rather than the distinct, essentialized identity categories constructed by cultures.101 The invocation of Philistine genetics to substantiate contemporary territorial claims, or the misrepresentation of direct-to-consumer DNA tests as evidence of tribal affiliation, illustrates the ease with which genetic “facts” can be weaponized, frequently opposing Indigenous definitions of sovereignty based on community and political continuity.100

Future syntheses: advancing an integrated history via paleo-omics

The trajectory of ancient DNA research indicates a significant transition from a specialized genomic discipline to an essential element of an integrative “paleo-omics” framework. The future depends not solely on sequencing existing samples, but on integrating genetic data with information from archaeology, chemistry, languages, and climate science to create comprehensive, predictive narratives of historical processes that can inform predictive models for future scenarios.

Integration of genetic, archaeological, and biogeochemical archives

The explanatory power of ancient DNA is significantly enhanced when integrated with supplementary evidence. Transdisciplinary studies are currently illustrating that the integration of genomic ancestry, artefact typology, stable isotope analysis (δ13C, δ15N, 87Sr/86Sr), and historical records can elucidate intricate social phenomena such as state-sponsored resettlement initiatives with a precision that no singular method can achieve.103 This integrated methodology enables researchers to progress from merely identifying individuals to comprehending their lifestyles: stable isotopes can elucidate personal life histories regarding diet and mobility, while ancient DNA can clarify kinship and community structure, collectively reconstructing the social and subsistence organization of historical populations.3, 104This reasoning applies to connecting biological and cultural evolution. Innovative frameworks in “paleoarchaeogenomics” promote the simultaneous examination of genetic migration signals with datasets from historical linguistics and epigraphy, evaluating theories for the concurrent dispersal of populations, languages, and technology.105 By seeing genes, artefacts, words, and isotopes as interconnected archives, future research can revolutionize our understanding of human history from a collection of discrete occurrences into a dynamic, process-oriented narrative.

The paleo-omics revolution: expanding from DNA to proteins and metabolites

A crucial advancement is the transition from paleogenomics to comprehensive “paleo-omics” the concurrent extraction and examination of DNA, proteins, metabolites, and other biomolecules from ancient materials. Paleoproteomics, the examination of ancient proteins, is especially revolutionary since proteins exhibit better durability than DNA in non-permafrost settings106 Recent advancements demonstrate this capability: species identification of fragmented bones via high-throughput protein fingerprinting (e.g., SPIN) enhances taxonomic analysis when morphology and DNA are inadequate.107 The examination of proteins and encapsulated compounds in dental calculus offers unparalleled insight into historical diets, disclosing particular plant and animal intake, oral health conditions, and potential medicinal applications.108 Enamel paleoproteomics has retrieved protein sequences exceeding one million years in age, extending molecular phylogenetics beyond the existing boundaries of DNA preservation and providing fresh insights into the Early Pleistocene.109 The primary objective is to obtain a comprehensive biochemical profile of historical living forms. Through the co-analysis of DNA (for ancestry and pathogens), proteins (for diet, tissue function, and phylogeny), stable isotopes (for provenance and trophic level), and organic residues on artefacts (for technology and usage), researchers can develop intricately contextualized biographies of individuals and sophisticated representations of their ecosystems.110 The optimization of information derived from each unique sample constitutes both an ethical and a scientific necessity.

Transitioning from historical data to predicting future alterations

The most significant future application of ancient genomics may be its ability to enhance predictions on biological responses to current anthropogenic climate change. Time-stratified genetic and sedimentary DNA (sedaDNA) records offer direct, empirical evidence on species and ecosystem responses to historical climatic upheavals. Conclusion: the past influencing the present and future

Ancient DNA has significantly reshaped our understanding of life's past, converting evolutionary biology and archaeology from fields based on inference into firsthand observers of dynamic processes over extensive periods. Paleogenomics has revolutionized static, tree-like models by offering a serial, time-stamped account of genetic evolution, uncovering a history marked by episodes of admixture, recurrent population replacements, and intricate interactions between genomes and their environments.2, 3 This review has delineated how this “fourth dimension” in biology has addressed enduring controversies from the colonization of continents and the processes of domestication to the origins of pandemics and the factors contributing to megafaunal extinction while consistently uncovering a narrative of complexity rather than simplicity. The field's notable advancement is a dual narrative of technical and ethical progression. Advancements in high-throughput sequencing, targeted capture, and analytics have facilitated the regular acquisition of genome-scale data from an expanding variety of specimens and sedimentary archives.116, 117 Simultaneously, the field has seen a requisite ethical evolution. Current frameworks advocate for the recognition of ancestral remains not merely as data sources but as finite, culturally significant resources, necessitating minimally invasive techniques, equitable collaborations with communities, and adherence to Indigenous data sovereignty.96, 118 The accountable management of both molecules and meanings has become fundamental to stringent science. The future of ancient DNA research indicates further integration and significance. Expanding sampling into under-represented regions and taxa is essential; however, the most significant advancements will arise from integrating genetic data with additional evidence such as isotopes, archaeology, paleoproteomics, and historical records to create comprehensive “paleo-omics” biographies of former lives and ecosystems.105, 106 This deep-time record is widely seen as an essential instrument for the future. Time-series data on genetic and ecological changes offer empirical evidence to calibrate models predicting species responses to climate change, so directly guiding conservation strategies.105, 106 The significance of ancient DNA will ultimately be determined not by the age of the sequences obtained, but by the insight with which they are analyzed and utilized. The discipline is at a pivotal juncture: it may either perpetuate extractive paradigms or innovate a more collaborative and equitable form of knowledge production that prioritizes the communities associated with its research. By adopting this latter approach, advancing integrative science through ethical collaboration, paleogenomics can realize its utmost potential. It can transcend mere historical revisionism to actively shape a more equitable and sustainable future, providing evidence-based insights for concerns including biodiversity conservation, public health, and the profound origins of human variation and resilience. This guarantees that the narratives encoded in ancient DNA contribute to both the scientific corpus and the global society.
