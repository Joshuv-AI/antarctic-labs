Abstract
Plastic use is ubiquitous in the modern world, and polyethylene terephthalate (PET) is one of
the most abundantly produced plastics (and the most highly produced polyester), with ~65
million metric tons manufactured annually. To the consumer, PET is likely most recognizable as
the plastic used to make beverage bottles. Like many plastics, traditional mechanical or
chemical means of PET deconstruction and upcycling are costly and inefficient. Because of
these challenges, recycled plastic is generally of lower quality and is more expensive to produce
than virgin plastic derived from petroleum. Ultimately, this results in most plastic ending up as
waste. We view plastic waste as an underutilized resource which, with the development of more
efficient and high-quality recycling processes, could (1) generate significant economic value
while (2) decreasing petroleum usage and greenhouse gas emissions, as well as (3) minimizing
its negative environmental and health impacts.

Biocatalytic recycling, or biomanufacturing the basic building blocks of new plastic from plastic
waste, is a promising approach to plastic reuse that complements existing recycling
technologies. Recently, biological enzymes capable of breaking down PET have garnered
significant attention as an attractive means of dealing with the plastic problem. These enzymes
are currently undergoing pilot studies for implementation in industrial-scale enzyme-based
recycling. However, there are significant limitations to current enzymes, including the need to
perform costly pre-processing of the plastic waste before the enzymes are able to work. Further

optimization of these enzymes is necessary to make these technologies competitive, and
ultimately incentivise industry-wide adoption of this biology-based green recycling technology.

In this work we demonstrate a means to design and generate performant biological enzymes,
capable of efficiently deconstructing plastic waste. Specifically, we applied recent advances in
artificial intelligence, machine learning, and statistical analysis to design new versions and
discover natural enzymes capable of breaking down PET. We focused on optimizing key
properties that are important for industrial-scale enzymatic recycling such as pH and
thermotolerance. Normal testing of enzymatic plastic-deconstruction is extremely labor intensive
and so through this work we also developed a robotic-assisted experimental pipeline capable of
characterizing thousands of candidate enzymes. The results of this iterative, AI-guided,
multi-discipline approach have led to increases in enzymatic breakdown of over 150X over
starting enzymes. This work supports the rapidly developing and transformative field of
biocatalytic solutions to environmental problems beyond the discovery and predictive
understanding of enzymes for polymer recycling, and has wide implications for tackling
numerous energy problems such as carbon capture and fixation (e.g., engineering carbon
monoxide dehydrogenase and the rubisco-pathway), biomining (e.g., design of
lanthanide-binding proteins) and biomanufacturing (e.g., lignin-deconstruction enzymes).

Objectives and Major Discoveries
The overarching goal of our project was to create new and optimized PET-depolymerizing
enzymes (PETases) useful for industrial application. Despite some challenges (departure of a
co-investigator), we were successful in nearly all of the goals we set out to achieve. We have
generated / discovered over 500 new PET-depolymerizing enzymes—equal to or more than
have been described collectively in the literature—and many of these new enzymes are highly
performant and rival the top enzymes in the literature. Three manuscripts have been accepted,
we are in various stages of the publication process for four additional writeups, and there will
likely be additional manuscripts as we work through the large data that were produced under
these efforts. We anticipate this work to have a large impact on the emerging field of AI-driven
protein design as well as wide implications for tackling numerous energy problems beyond
plastic depolymerization and upcycling.

Objective 1: We proposed to design and characterize approximately 1200 novel PETases that
are significantly different (25-65+ mutations) from all known PET-depolymerizing enzymes and
which contain unique properties useful for performant enzymatic PET recycling and upcycling.
The introduction of many simultaneous mutations, while still maintaining PETase function, will
enable us to more efficiently search for altered properties that depend on primary amino acid
sequence. This objective was executed almost exactly as proposed, and once the protocols
were up and running we further extended our efforts to the PETase-assisting enzyme MHETase,
which enhances the final monomer product by hydrolyzing one of the major PETase products
MHET.

To execute this objective we developed two brand new technologies. (1) Evolution-informed
computational design. In short, this methodology starts by deriving a quantitative model of
intra-protein residue-residue constraints from homologous sequences of a target protein (e.g.,
PETase). Using this quantitative model, we are able to predict the fitness of novel “design”
proteins, and through sampling protocols that allow us to explore diverse sequence spaces,
putative-enzymes are generated. We design these sequences to contain a large number of
mutations, and experimentally we have found that most have enhanced activity and property
profiles. Although we began developing the method prior to this funding mechanism, we were
able to solidify and generalize the framework during Y1 of BER funding (see Fram et al., 2024).
In the final years, the methodology was further extended to learn from the experimental results
in a “systems biology” style loop of design--test-learn-design. (2) System for
robotic-purification and enzymatic testing. Accurate testing and comparison of PETase
activity is labor intensive. In short, it requires synthesizing variant oligos, cloning it into an
expression vector, transformation into competent bacteria, expression and purification of the
variant protein, measuring and normalizing protein concentration, and then assaying activity
across conditions of interest. In order to test all of the computational variants, we developed a
platform that automates most of these steps, utilizing low cost (OT-2) open source robots. This
platform enables us to test hundreds of variants in a single assay, and greatly decreases errors
associated with manual experimental protocols (see Norton-Baker et al., 2024).

In total we generated and tested over 1200 PETase variants. The majority of these variants
(~800) were derived using a newly-developed evolution-informed computational method that
enables us to introduce ~10-20% sequence differences (30-60 mutations) in a single-shot of
design (Fram et al., 2004). In addition, we used AI to computationally mine natural sequence

databases for PETases likely to be pH- and thermo-tolerant (n~= 400, Norton-Baker and Komp,
manuscript in preparation). Although we did characterize ~200 natural sequences, the most
exciting variants were those generated by evolution-informed design. Of the ~800 sequences,
about 500 maintained activity even with high mutational burdens (average of 40 mutations
relative to the natural enzyme). Nearly all of these sequences were more performant from the
wild type sequence in which they were derived, and many of them (>50) had performance
characteristics that rivaled the top literature-derived PETase (LCC-ICCG). We also generated
around 120 MHETase variants. Although the hit rate and performance characteristics were not
as exciting as those with PETase, we were able to increase wild type activity approximately 5
fold in a single-shot of design (Murphy et al., in preparation).

Objective 2: We proposed to optimize PETases found in nature, described in the literature, or
discovered during these studies by testing mutagenized variants using directed evolution. This
objective was the most challenging scientifically, and we ended up pivoting from microfluidics
method development (creating a new directed evolution platform) to executing directed
evolution experiments with one of the alternate approaches, i.e., using a more established
microwell-based assay to test variants.

The proposal described the development of a novel ultra-high throughput microfluidics-based
assay for testing PETase activity in order to dramatically increase variant testing throughput.
Initially our microfluidic method development showed many promising results, but ultimately
each approach had significant scientific challenges that we were unable to overcome. (1)
Bacterial growth assay: were able to link bacterial growth rate to PETase products EG and TPA,
and were able to generate PET-nanoparticles. Unfortunately, the bacteria grew too slowly in
droplets to be detectable and nanoparticle generation was erratic (unreliable precipitation.
Further, this approach would have allowed us to only test PETases under physiological
conditions that allowed for bacterial growth, i.e., we wouldn’t be able to evolve under interesting
conditions like low pH or high temperature. (2) We successfully developed a fluorescent labeling
scheme that enabled us to dye PET nanoparticles and monitor their depolymerization as a
function of fluorescent signal. Nanoparticles were extensively characterized for their size,
shape, and dye release, and were successfully encapsulated in droplets. Unfortunately, when
we tried to add bacteria and lyse them to the droplets, the droplets aggregated, which causes
many issues with detection and reproducibility. Ultimately, we were unable to significantly
reduce the aggregation through buffer composition, pH, ionic strength, nanoparticle
concentration, lysis reagents and droplet generation techniques. We then explored additional
methods for cell lysis, but were consistently plagued by the instability of the dispersion and its
implication on fluorescent sorting. (3) We developed a new approach in which fluorescent signal
was decoupled from the aggregation-prone PET nanoparticles using two enzymes (a
ketoreductase and diaphorase) to convert the weakly fluorescent phenoxazine dye, resazurin, to
the strongly fluorescent dye resorufin in the presence of the PET monomer EG. Unfortunately,
the signal to noise ratio was very low even after an extensive optimization campaign. (4) We
tried to replace the second half of the reaction with a redox biosensor (Peredox) that would
detect the ratio of NAD+ to NADH. The results of this study indicated that we could only
marginally increase dynamic range (less than 2 fold) and, even after optimization, sensitivity
was very low. (4) Lastly, we embarked on a method for selectively encapsulating a specific
concentration of PETase variants derived from in vitro transcription and translation. The theory
was to use hydrogel beads to do enzyme selection, incubate the beads with substrate and sort
them on chip. We tried multiple substrates and enzyme variants and, although we were able to

detect activity, we were unable to recapitulate the known activity profiles of our controls. Some
of these assays are still under active development, but ultimately we decided to apply lower
throughput directed evolution using microwell plates.

Due to the issues we ran into with microfluidics assay development and since there is local
expertise at NREL for applying directed evolution on PETase (HotPETase author Dr. Elizabeth
Bell is now in Gregg Beckham’s group), we applied a more established approach using
microwell plates. In short, the assay involves pooled transformation of variants at a particular
position, isolated selection and growth of 96 transformants, lysis and incubation in microwell
format, and finally followed by HPLC detection of product formation. We applied this protocol to
nine positions in MHETase, with the positions chosen to be closest to the active site. There were
eight mutations across three positions that had increased activity, and we have followed up
testing all possible combinations of these mutations in a new natural enzyme as well as an
evolution informed design.

Objective 3: In the original application, we proposed to characterize key performance metrics of
new and optimized PETases in detail including solvent tolerance, stability, catalytic rate, and
substrate promiscuity. The proposal further described testing the top enzymes under a large
number of relevant conditions, varying pH, temperature, substrate etc.

We took several approaches to executing this objecting. (1) Thanks to the robotic platform we
developed for high throughput experimental purification and testing of PETases, we were able to
rapidly and characterize all of the enzymes that expressed. Measurements included expression
(bradford protein assay), thermostability (Differential Scanning Fluorimetry), and up to 16 activity
measurements (4 pHs, 2 temperatures, and 2 substrates) in duplicate. (2) In addition to high
throughput measurements, we have tested the top variants in small bioreactors to gauge
performance in a more industrially-relevant setting. (3) We have begun to follow up performance
testing on a battery of new substrates (e.g., PLA, PHB, ARMID, PBT and PBAT).

Conclusion
The objectives that we proposed were executed almost exactly as originally described. There
are several reasons that this work is both conceptually and practically impactful. (1) Several
groundbreaking computational and experimental technologies were developed and
disseminated to the community. Both the protein design method and the low-cost high
throughput robotic platform have general implications beyond plastic depolymerization, including
many real-world problems in medicine (e.g., protein biologics), energy (e.g., carbon capture and
fixation, biomining, biomanufacturing), and of course basic biology (e.g., understanding and
quantifying evolution). (2) We developed and characterized hundreds of new PETases and
MHETases, many of which are highly performant and rival the best enzymes described in the
literature. We anticipate these enzymes will be both a treasure trove for new machine learning
methods and will also be useful industrially for both PET depolymerization as well as
depolymerization of other synthetic polymers.
