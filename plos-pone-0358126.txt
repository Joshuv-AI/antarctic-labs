Evaluation of replication success: A statistical perspective and extended analysis of the SCORE data

Robbie C M van Aert, Marcel ALM van Assen, Rubén López-Nicolás

ABSTRACT
Replications are more and more conducted in multiple research fields. A recent large-scale replication project, Systematizing Confidence in Open Research and Evidence (SCORE) project, replicated 274 effects from research on business, economics, education, political science, psychology, and sociology. An open question remains how replication success can best be assessed. Thirteen methods for assessing replication success were applied in the SCORE project. The first goal of this paper is studying the statistical properties of replication success methods by a numerical study tailored to the characteristics of the SCORE data. The results of the numerical study show that replication success methods are unable to reliably draw a conclusion about replication success for typical sample sizes of the original study and replication. Hence, there is often insufficient information in one original study and one replication for drawing definite conclusions. The second goal of this paper is taking a novel perspective on the SCORE data by estimating the effect size of an original study and replication. Estimators based on meta-analysis methods that correct for potential bias in the original study were close to unbiased when synthesizing the original study and replication. The reanalysis of the SCORE data revealed that meta-analytic estimates were substantially closer to the effect size of the replication compared to the original study. The null hypothesis of no effect was rejected by the meta-analysis methods in 59.2% to 81.3% of the pairs of an original study and replication. We recommend researchers to make sure that methods for analyzing replicability have favorable statistical properties for the data at hand before applying these methods.

Introduction More and more replications are conducted in psychological research [ 1 ]. The main goal of a replication is usually to examine the replicability of a study, here defined as whether the same result is obtained as in the original study if the original study is executed again [ 2 ]. A single replication of an original study is sometimes conducted as well as large-scale replicability projects where the focus is on studying the replicability of multiple original studies of one or more research fields [ 3 – 6 ]. Other replicability projects focused on replicating a single original study in multiple labs to study the generalizability of multiple effects [i.e., Many Labs projects, [ 7 – 10 ]. The overall conclusion of the replicability projects in psychology, economics, and cancer biology is that the replicability of studies is low [ 3 – 6 ]. However, the assessment of the replicability of studies (i.e., is the original study successfully replicated?) is difficult. Currently, there is no consensus on how to best assess replicability. Common approaches used when studying replicability are whether the effect size of the original study and replication are in the same direction, the original study and replication are both statistically significant and in the same direction, or the effect size of the original study is in the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the replication. There are more approaches to study replicability based on, for example, meta-analysis methods [ 11 – 15 ], the prediction interval (PI; [ 16 ]), statistical power analysis [ 17 ], combining null hypothesis significance testing and equivalence testing [ 18 ], Bayes factors [ 19 ], and reverse Bayes analysis [ 20 ]. The statistical properties of a selection of the approaches to assess replicability have already been studied in a series of papers by Schauer, Hedges, and colleagues [ 12 , 21 – 26 ] and in Muradchanian et al. [ 27 ], Freuli et al. [ 28 ], and Kondzic et al. [ 29 ]. The overarching conclusion based on the studies by Schauer, Hedges, and colleagues is that the common approaches used in these replicability projects have unfavorable statistical properties such as a high Type-1 error rate. They also conclude that one original study and one replication is often not enough to have high statistical power when testing for replicability. In a simulation study, Muradchanian et al. [ 27 ] examined the statistical properties of multiple approaches to study replicability when the original study is affected by publication bias. Their results show that Bayesian methods and meta-analysis methods had the best properties when looking at the true and false positives. Freuli et al. [ 28 ] extended this research by also including different types of questionable research practices in the original study. Especially the questionable research practices selectively reporting outcomes and subgroup analyses yielded larger false positive rates of all included methods to assess replicability. Kondzic et al. [ 29 ] also conducted a simulation study but focused, in contrast to Muradchanian et al. [ 27 ] and Freuli et al. [ 28 ], on a prospective replication design. In a prospective replication design, the original study and replication are planned simultaneously and the design of the replication does not depend on the results of the original study. Kondzic et al. [ 29 ] concluded that there is also no single best replication success method for prospective replication designs and that Type-1 error rates can be inflated. The difficulties of assessing replicability based on only one original study and one replication have also motivated to focus on answering the two questions “what is the best estimate when meta-analyzing the original study and replication” and “is the meta-analytic effect different from zero?” These two questions have also been answered in the large-scale replicability projects by fitting a fixed-effect (FE) meta-analysis model [ 30 ]. In this FE model (also known as common-effect or equal-effect model), it is assumed that the true effect size of the original study and replication is the same. Answering these two questions about the meta-analytic effect size is arguably also more of substantial interest for the applied researcher. Van Aert et al. [ 13 , 14 ] developed methods for answering these two questions that take into account that the original study is selected; for instance, because replications usually only set out to replicate statistically significant original studies and/or because of publication bias [ 31 ]. Their results show that not taking this selection bias into account yield overestimated estimates in the FE model and inaccurate statistical inference while the proposed methods outperform the FE model. However, the results also show, in line with those of Schauer, Hedges, and colleagues, that only one original study and one replication is a challenging condition for the proposed methods that leads to, for example, low statistical power for testing the null hypothesis of no effect and imprecise estimates. The focus in this paper is on the replicability component of the Systematizing Confidence in Open Research and Evidence (SCORE) project [ 32 ]. In this project initiated by the Center for Open Science, the replicability [ 32 ], reproducibility [ 33 ], and analytical robustness [ 34 ] of the literature in business, economics, education, political science, psychology, and sociology were studied. Papers under scrutiny were selected from a sample of 62 journals in these six research fields and within the timeframe of 2009–2018 (see [ 35 ] for more details about the sampling). Replicability was studied by matching the selected papers with research teams that had sufficient expertise to replicate it. 274 effects from 164 papers were replicated by either collecting new data or using already existing data that was not incorporated in the original study. This paper makes both a methodological and empirical contribution to the literature. The methodological contribution is that we assess the performance (i.e., statistical properties) of methods to assess replication success of studies. The assessment of these replicability methods is tailored to data characteristics of the SCORE data such that it is directly applicable to these data. It is currently unknown whether the characteristics of the SCORE data (i.e., one replication study and with sample sizes that are common in these data) allow for drawing conclusions about replication success. The empirical contribution of our paper is that we reanalyze the SCORE data by taking a different perspective than the analyses in Tyner et al. [ 32 ]. They examined the replication success of the studies in SCORE, whereas we also take the perspective by focusing on estimating the meta-analytic effect size when combining the original studies and replications. This will yield relevant insights about, for example, the differences between meta-analytic effect sizes when there is and is not corrected for potential bias in the original studies. Moreover, our analysis also allows for drawing conclusions about differences in meta-analytic effect sizes between research fields. We also first study the statistical properties of these methods before applying the methods to the data of SCORE. Our paper differs from previous papers on methods to assess replicability and meta-analyzing original studies and replications in three ways. First, the main focus of our paper is to study the statistical properties of methods tailored to the characteristics of the data in SCORE. Previous papers studied the statistical properties by, for example, simulating data according to a particular research field and then evaluate the statistical properties of replication success and meta-analysis methods. However, these simulated data, and consequently also the conclusions of these papers, may not be readily applicable to the data of SCORE. We systematically examine the effect of selection bias, as observed in the SCORE data, on methods for assessing replication success and effect size estimation. The second difference between our paper and previous research is that we also evaluate the statistical properties of methods that aim to estimate the true effect size underlying both the original study and replication, and also in conditions of the SCORE data. Finally, we evaluate the statistical properties of a larger number of methods than what has been done before. Methods to assess replicability We focus on three aspects of replicability that are usually assessed in replicability projects: (1) is the original study successfully replicated, (2) what is the effect size estimate when combining the original study and replication and (3) is this effect different from zero. The included methods in our paper were all developed for analyzing one original study and one replication. There are also methods developed for Many Labs projects [e.g., 11], but these are not considered in this paper since the majority of original studies in the SCORE project were replicated once (259 out of 274, 94.5%). Assessing successful replication Table 1 provides an overview of methods to assess whether a replication was successful and when a replication success is concluded in the analyses that are described later on in this paper. We have grouped the methods into two sets depending on the approach that is taken to determine successful replication. The first set of methods is based on assessing the consistency between the conclusions of the original study and replication . Within this set, the method “agreement in sign and statistical significance” is based on testing the null hypothesis of no effect in the original study and replication. Replication success is concluded with this method if there is simultaneous agreement in (i) statistical significance of the original study and replication, and (ii) the direction of the effect size estimate in both studies [e.g., 32 , 36 ]. This method is frequently applied and included in replicability projects [ 3 – 5 ]. The “sceptical p -value” [ 20 , 37 ] uses the data of the original study to create a prior such that the lower bound of the credible interval of the posterior distribution equals zero. Subsequently, there is replication success if this prior distribution is in conflict with the data of the replication based on a frequentist hypothesis test. The “replication Bayes factor” [ 19 , 38 ] examines whether θ R , which is the true effect size of the replication, is more likely under a model with a true effect of zero compared with a model where θ R follows the posterior distribution of the original study using the Bayes factor. More evidence in favor of the model that equals the posterior distribution of the original study is indicative for replication success. The methods in the second set of Table 1 assess the similarity of both studies’ true effect sizes θ O and θ R . The first three methods are testing H 0 : θ O = θ R by evaluating whether θ ^ O is in the 95% CI for θ R and θ ^ R is in the 95% PI for θ O [ 16 ]. The hypothesis H 0 : θ O = θ R can also be tested with the Cochran’s Q -test, which is frequently used in meta-analysis to test for homogeneity in true effect size [ 39 ]. The Q -test can be used for testing exact and approximate replication [ 25 ]. Exact replication implies testing H 0 : θ O = θ R . Approximate replication allows for small differences between θ O and θ R that the researcher can define (for guidance on defining a small difference see Hedges et al. [ 25 ]). Schauer et al. [ 23 ] noted that the method based on the 95% PI and the Q -test for exact replication are equivalent in case of one original study and one replication. Moreover, the 95% PI and the Q -test are the uniformly most powerful unbiased tests [ 21 ] implying that the 95% PI for θ O and the Q -test always have higher statistical power than other unbiased tests of the same hypothesis if the model assumptions are met. Another method for assessing similarity between θ O and θ R is the correspondence test [ 18 ]. This test combines the results of two hypothesis tests: a difference and equivalence test. In the difference test, the null hypothesis of equal true effect size in the original study and replication (i.e., H 0 : θ O = θ R ) is tested. In the equivalence test, two null hypotheses are tested on whether the difference between the true effects is larger or smaller than an equivalence threshold (i.e., H 0 : θ O − θ R ≥ δ E and H 0 : θ O − θ R ≤ − δ E where δ E is the equivalence threshold). Equivalence is concluded if both tests are statistically significant, because this implies that the difference between θ O and θ R is smaller than δ E and larger than − δ E (i.e., | θ O − θ R | < δ E ). The correspondence test yields four different conclusions depending on statistical significance of the difference and equivalence test (i.e., equivalent, trivially different, different, and indeterminate). In SCORE [ 32 ], equivalence was considered to be a replication success regardless of the result of the difference test. In SCORE [ 32 ], replication success was concluded if equivalence was established based on the equivalence test alone (i.e., regardless of the outcome of the difference test), meaning that it was concluded that | θ O − θ R | < δ E . Hence, we include the equivalence test instead of the correspondence test in the analyses of this paper. In SCORE [ 32 ], a replication success was also concluded if θ ^ R was in the 95% CI for θ O . We excluded this method because the replication typically had larger sample size than the original study, leading to a smaller standard error and hence a narrower CI and more powerful hypothesis test. The small telescopes method [ 17 ] was also included in SCORE [ 32 ] and tests whether θ R is equal to or larger than an effect size with 33% statistical power based on the sample size of the original study. We did not include this method in our paper, because it is not about the (in)consistency between the conclusions of the original study and replication or the (dis)similarity of θ O and θ R . Estimating effect size and testing for no effect We also included seven meta-analysis methods to estimate the meta-analytic effect size and/or test the null hypothesis of no meta-analytic effect when combining an original study and a replication. All included methods assume that the same true effect size is estimated in the original study and replication. The included methods are summarized in Table 2 . The first four methods listed in Table 2 were also implemented in SCORE [ 32 ], that is, frequentist and Bayesian FE model, Edgington’s method, and a Bayes factor using only the data of the replication. In contrast to SCORE, we focus on hypothesis testing of the meta-analytic effect size and effect size estimation. The first two methods are the FE model [ 30 ] implemented in a frequentist as well as in a Bayesian framework. In these models, the meta-analytic estimate is computed under the assumption that estimates of θ O and θ R are unbiased. The third method in Table 2 , Edgington’s method [ 15 ], is a method that does not yield a meta-analytic estimate. The sum of the p -values is computed in this method for testing the null hypothesis of no effect in the original study and replication. The Bayes factor replication method is a Bayesian hypothesis test based on only the data of the replication that tests a two-sided hypothesis on whether the effect is equal to zero. In SCORE [ 32 ], a Bayes factor using nonlocal alternative densities was implemented [ 40 ]. The noncentrality parameter of the prior distribution is repeatedly changed in this testing procedure, which results in a range of Bayes factors. The reported Bayes factor in SCORE is the largest Bayes factor in this range. The last three methods listed in Table 2 , hybrid method of meta-analysis, extended hybrid method of meta-analysis, and snapshot hybrid, were not included in SCORE. The key characteristic of these methods is that only the effect size estimator of θ R is assumed to be unbiased. By taking into account that the original effect size is statistically significant (i.e., conditioning on statistical significance), the methods correct for potential bias in θ O . The hybrid method of meta-analysis (abbreviated as hybrid [ 13 ],) estimates the meta-analytic effect size and tests the null hypothesis of no effect. Hybrid and Edgington’s method both use the p -values for testing the null hypothesis of no effect, but hybrid corrects for potential selection bias in the original study. The extended hybrid method of meta-analysis (abbreviated as HYEMA [ 41 ],) is a generalization of hybrid that allows for the inclusion of more than one original study and one replication and allows for estimating the between-study variance in true effect size. HYEMA can, however, also be applied to the case with only one original study and one replication and when the between-study variance is assumed to be zero (i.e., FE model). Another difference between hybrid and HYEMA is that hybrid uses the sum of p -values for effect size estimation and statistical inference and HYEMA is implemented in a maximum likelihood framework. The last method in Table 2 , the snapshot hybrid method [ 14 ] applies a Bayesian framework to compute posterior model probabilities (PMP) that quantify the evidence in favor of a zero, small, medium, and large effect size, using Cohen’s rules of thumb [ 42 ]. Hence, the method quantifies how likely it is that the effect size has a particular magnitude rather than providing a point estimate. In the next section of the paper, we study the statistical properties of the methods by means of a numerical study. This numerical study is tailored to the data observed in SCORE. Based on these statistical properties, methods will be selected that are applied to the SCORE data later in the paper. Evaluation of the statistical properties of the methods Methods We evaluated the statistical properties of the included methods by employing a similar procedure as in van Aert and van Assen (2017, 2018). We tailored the evaluation of the statistical methods to the 225 original studies and replications in the SCORE project for which effect sizes were transformed to correlation coefficients. We did not analyze correlation coefficients directly but first applied the Fisher’s z transformation to make sure that the sampling variance is independent of the correlation [ 43 ] and because the sampling variance of the transformed correlations more closely follows the normal distribution. This is a desirable property given that many of the included methods assume a normally distributed effect size measure. A large portion of the original studies were reported as statistically significant in the SCORE project when using a two-tailed test with α = 0.05 (216 out of 225, 96.0%). Hence, we assumed a statistically significant original study by conditioning on the statistical significance of the effect size of this study. Without loss of generality, we assumed the original effect was positive. The conditional probability density function of the original study was obtained by first computing the statistical power π of observing a statistically significant effect size when testing a right-tailed hypothesis with α = 0.025 (which is equivalent to two-tailed testing with α = 0.05 but focusing on one direction) given the true effect size θ O and variance σ O 2 . The probability π was computed using the cumulative distribution function of the normal distribution. Subsequently, 100 equally distributed cumulative probabilities P i O were created using P i O = 1 − π + i × π 101 where i = 1 , \hdots , 100 . The P i O were transformed into Fisher’s z correlations of the original study using the quantile function of the normal distribution with mean θ O and variance σ O 2 in order to approximate the marginal probability density function. Effect sizes of the replication were generated in an analogous way as for the original study. The difference between generating data for the two types of studies was that there was no requirement for the replication to be statistically significant. That is, the marginal probability density function of the replication was obtained by creating 100 equally distributed cumulative probabilities P i R using P i R = i 101 . Corresponding Fisher’s z transformed correlations were computed using the quantile function of the normal distribution with mean θ R and variance σ R 2 . The joint distribution of the statistically significant original study and replication was approximated by combining all possible Fisher’s z transformed correlations of the original study and replication. This resulted in a grid of 100 × 100 = 10,000 combinations to which the methods evaluated in this study were applied. This procedure yields numerical approximations of the expectations of the outcome measures described below. Conditions for θ O , θ R , σ O 2 , and σ R 2 were selected in such a way to closely match the characteristics of the data in the SCORE project. The following Pearson correlation coefficients of the original study ( ρ O ) and replication ( ρ R ) were selected: 0, 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5. These values resembled a zero, small, medium, and large effect according to the rules-of-thumb by Cohen [ 42 ]. These Pearson correlation coefficients implied the following Fisher’s z transformed correlations of the original study ( θ O ) and replication ( θ R ): 0, 0.100, 0.310, and 0.549. The zero, small, and medium effects were also close to the observed Fisher’s z transformed correlations observed in the replications of the SCORE project (first quartile 0.049, median 0.134, and third quartile 0.252). We included conditions were θ O and θ R were equal to each other to examine whether the methods could detect replication success. We also included conditions where θ O was larger than θ R in order to study replication failure. In these conditions, θ O was always one magnitude of the rules-of-thumb by Cohen [ 42 ] larger than θ R . That is, θ O = 0.100 was combined with θ R = 0 , θ O = 0.310 was combined with θ R = 0.100 , and θ O = 0.549 was combined with θ R = 0.310 . Conditions for σ O 2 and σ R 2 were computed with the estimation equation of the variance of Fisher’s z correlations, which is 1 / ( N − 3 ) where N is the sample size of the original study or replication. This sample size was set equal to the first quartile, median, and third quartile of the sample sizes observed in the original studies (median N = 218, first quartile = 97 and third quartile = 2,236) and replications (median N = 556, first quartile = 215 and third quartile = 2,231) in the SCORE project. We also added a sample size of 10,000 for the replication to study the large sample statistical properties of the methods. This yielded the following conditions for σ O 2 = (0.0106, 0.0047, 0.00045) and for σ R 2 = (0.0047, 0.0018, 0.00045, 0.00010). The methods listed in Table 1 were included to study replication success. The last column of Table 1 details the criterion of each method for concluding replication success. These criteria were adopted from the SCORE project. The outcome of these methods was the replication success probability that was computed by dividing the cases were replication success was concluded by 10,000. The methods listed in Table 2 were included for testing the null hypothesis of no effect and effect size estimation. The outcomes for hypothesis testing were Type-1 error rate and statistical power. That is, how often the null hypothesis was rejected in the absence (i.e., Type-1 error rate) and presence of an effect (i.e., statistical power). For effect size estimation, the outcomes were bias and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE). The median of the posterior distribution was used as effect size estimate for the Bayesian FE model. Fisher’s z transformed correlations were analyzed with all included methods. Estimates of the Fisher’s z transformed correlations were (back-)transformed to correlation coefficients (using equation (11.39) in [ 44 ]) to facilitate interpretation of bias and RMSE of the effect size estimators. Note that we assume selection of originally statistically significant findings, hence all reported outcome measures are conditional on the original study being statistically significant by design of the numerical study. We used α = 0.05 for methods that tested hypotheses in a frequentist framework except for the sceptical p -value where we used the default “golden” implementation implying α = 0.123 [ 37 ]. For the methods based on Bayes factors, we implemented multiple thresholds, because different thresholds were used in the SCORE project. A successful replication was concluded in the SCORE project for a Bayes factors larger than 1 for the replication Bayes factor, larger than 3 for the Bayesian FE model, and larger than 10 for the Bayes factor based on the replication study. We implemented these thresholds as well as 20 and 150 for the methods based on Bayes factors, because these are commonly used thresholds in practice [ 45 ]. For the snapshot hybrid method, we examined how often the PMP was largest for the true effect size and how often it exceeded 0.75, indicating cases where there was more than three times as much evidence for the true model as for the alternatives. A PMP larger than 0.75 is in line with the Bayes factor threshold 3, because it indicates that there was three times more evidence for one model compared to another model. We also studied as outcome the coverage probability of the frequentist FE model, PI, hybrid, and HYEMA. This analysis was programmed in R [ 46 , Version 4.4.3]. We used the R packages “BFF” [ 47 , Version 4.5.0], “hypergeo” [ 48 , Version 1.2.14], “metaBMA” [ 49 , Version 0.6.9], “puniform” [ 50 , Version 0.2.9], and “ReplicationSuccess” [20, Version 1.3.3]. R code of our analyses is available at https://osf.io/acbd5 . Results We report the statistical properties separately for the methods that focus on assessing successful replication and estimating the effect size and testing the null hypothesis of no effect. For all Bayesian methods, we report the results for concluding successful replication or rejecting the null hypothesis of no effect if the Bayes factor in favor of this hypothesis was larger than 3. Results of Bayes factor methods based on a different threshold than 3, testing for approximate replication with the Q -test, and the coverage probability are not reported in this section and are available in the supplemental materials ( https://osf.io/ktazu ). Considering first the methods that assess consistency of both studies’ conclusions (colored green in the figure), optimal performance is as follows. If ρ O =0.1/ ρ R =0, ideally the probability of replication success equals 0 for the replication Bayes factor, 0.123 for the sceptical p -value, and 0.025 (i.e., half of α ) for agreement in sign and statistical significance. If both true effect sizes are different but larger than 0, the optimal probability of replication success is 1. To summarize the results of these methods, we first turn to the conditions with N R = 10,000. The replication Bayes factor performed optimal, whereas the sceptical p -value showed suboptimal performance for ρ O =0.1/ ρ R =0 (probability 0 for N O = 97 and 218). For ρ O =0.1/ ρ R =0, the probability of replication success was close to ideal with on average 0.020 for agreement in sign and statistical significance. Turning to conditions with N R < 10,000, we focus first on the condition ρ O =0.1/ ρ R =0. Here, the replication Bayes factor and agreement in sign and statistical significance performed well with high probabilities (> 0.95; i.e., 1 – replication success probability) that both studies’ conclusions are different. The sceptical p -value performed worse but reasonably well with probabilities close to 0.8 (the worst performance is for N R = 215, N O = 2,236, namely 0.780). The results for the other conditions ( ρ O =0.3/ ρ R =0.1 and ρ O =0.5/ ρ R =0.3) were mostly determined by the sample size of the replication. This means that the results within one column of subfigures with the same N R were largely similar. If ρ R =0.3 all three methods provided correct conclusions with probability larger than 0.8 for N R ≥ 215, with close to optimal performance for N R ≥ 556, reflecting the larger sample size and thereby larger statistical power for ρ R =0.3. Focusing on ρ R =0.1, a larger sample size of the replication was required to have high replication success probabilities, with good performance of agreement in sign and statistical significance and the sceptical p -value for N R = 2,231. The replication Bayes factor did not perform well in case of ρ R = 0.1 if the original sample size was large ( N O = 2,236), because the replication with ρ R =0.1 was more in line with the model of a null effect than with the model where the effect size equaled the original study’s observed effect size. For a smaller sample size of the replication (i.e., N R = 556), only the sceptical p -value showed reasonable to good performance (probability ≥ 0.782) when ρ R =0.1. Also generally, the sceptical p -value performed best of the three methods in conditions where the true effect sizes were different between original study and replication. Considering the methods that assess similarity of ρ O and ρ R (colored blue), optimal performance is a probability of replication success equal to 0 as the two studies’ true effect sizes are unequal, and a probability of 1 for the equivalence test if ρ O =0.1/ ρ R =0, as the difference between the two effect sizes is smaller than the equivalence threshold (0.2). Only if both studies’ sample sizes were large ( N O = 2,236, N R ≥ 2,231) all three methods (Hedges’ Q /PI, CI, equivalence test) performed well. Lower original study or replication sample sizes negatively affected performance. For Hedges’ Q /PI and the equivalence test, one large study’s sample size cannot compensate for the other study’s small sample size. For instance, performance for Hedges’ Q /PI was 0.4 or lower if N O = 97 and N R = 10,000. The equivalence test did not detect ‘equivalence’ of ρ O =0.1/ ρ R =0 well for ( N O = 2,236, N R = 556). This is because the standard error used in both tests are a function of both studies’ sampling error. Hence, we recommend not using these methods when evaluating replication success when one of the study sample sizes is not very large. On the other hand, the performance of the CI was mostly dependent on the replication sample size. The CI showed good performance (probability ≤ 0.001) for N R ≥ 556, independent of the original study’s sample size, except when both true effect sizes were close together ( ρ O =0.1/ ρ R =0). For a true effect size equal to zero, we expect replication success probabilities that are close to zero. Optimal performance equals 1 for the nonzero true effect sizes. Turning to the results, as statistical power of the test of the null hypothesis increases in true effect size and sample sizes, the three methods’ probabilities of replication success increased as a function of these parameters. For effect sizes 0.3 and 0.5 performance was good (probabilities equaled 1), irrespective of both studies’ sample sizes. As the original effect size was statistically significant, both studies’ conclusions were only consistent if the sample size of the replication was large enough. To summarize, assessing successful replication was challenging if the true effect size was zero or small and sample sizes of the original study and replication were not very large. The performance of all methods improved when the sample size of the original study increased, because the larger sample size diminished the bias in the original study’s effect size estimator. These results show that there was often not enough information in case of one original study and one replication to reliably assess replication success. For this reason, we do not recommend applying methods to assess consistency between the conclusion of the original study and replication as these showed especially unfavorable properties in case of small sample sizes. For methods assessing similarity of ρ O and ρ R , we showed that methods perform well when (i) both studies’ sample sizes were large, and (ii) the true effect was nonzero. Condition (i) often does not apply in practice, and (ii) is practically very relevant. As the equivalence test with a small threshold (i.e., 0.2) also performed well for a zero true effect size, we only recommend this test when both study sample sizes are large. In the next section, we reanalyzed the SCORE data to examine the replicability of the included original studies. The numerical study indicated several problems with methods to assess replication success for common sample sizes of the original study and replication in the SCORE data. Hence, we did not apply these methods to reanalyze the SCORE data. Instead, we focused on applying the methods for estimating the effect size and testing the null hypothesis of no effect. Applying methods to the SCORE data The reanalysis of the SCORE data consisted of four steps: (1) extracting the correlation estimates and sample sizes of the original studies and replications from the SCORE data, (2) transforming correlation estimates and computing their sampling variances, (3) applying the meta-analytic methods to estimate the effect size and testing the null hypothesis of no effect to the data of the previous step, and (4) summarizing the results of applying the methods. SCORE included in total 274 effects that were replicated. These studies used a wide variety of designs and consequently different effect size measures were reported. SCORE transformed the effect sizes to correlation coefficients primarily by using the R package “effectsize” [ 51 ] whenever this was possible (see p. 152 of Tyner et al. [ 32 ] and their supplement at https://osf.io/uqegb/ ). Correlation coefficients could be computed for 225 out of 274 (82.1%) pairs of the original study and replication. In the first step of the reanalysis, we extracted the correlation coefficients and sample sizes of the 225 pairs of an original study and replication from the publicly available data ( https://osf.io/g5sny/files/5xfdy ). In the second step, we first multiplied the correlations of the original study and replication by −1 if the correlation of the original study was negative. This was done to ensure that all correlations of the original study were in the same direction. Although SCORE analyzed the correlation coefficients directly, we decided to apply Fisher’s z transformation and computed their sampling variance [ 43 ]. The reason for applying this transformation is that all methods for estimating the meta-analytic effect sizes and testing the null hypothesis of no effect (except for the Bayes factor based on only the replication and Edgington’s method) assume that the effect size measure follows a normal distribution. However, the correlations in the SCORE data also included other types of correlations than Pearson correlation coefficients, such as point-biserial correlations, for which the usual distributional assumptions underlying Fisher’s z transformation do not generally hold. Consequently, how well 1 / ( N − 3 ) approximated the true sampling variance depends on the type of correlation. We return to this issue in the discussion section. The third step was applying the methods listed in Table 2 to the Fisher’s z transformed correlations. These methods were applied in the same way as described in the Methods section of the numerical study. The hybrid and snapshot method could not be applied to 14 pairs (6.2%), because the correlation of the original study was not significantly different from zero based on a two-tailed test with α = 0.05 , which is a requirement for these methods. In the last step, we created density distributions of the effect size estimates of the different methods and computed their mean and standard deviation. These results are presented per research field (business, economics, education, political science, psychology, and sociology) as well as averaged across all research fields. We report aggregated results in the remainder of this section. The results of the individual pairs of original study and replication are available in the supplementary materials ( https://osf.io/ktazu ). R code of the analyses are available at https://osf.io/gqnwk . Discussion Replications are important to build a cumulative body of evidence [e.g.[ 52 ],. However, there is no considered best way of evaluating the replicability of research. We studied the statistical properties of the majority of replication success methods included in the SCORE project [ 32 ]. We also adopted another perspective by meta-analyzing the original study and its replication and interpreting the resulting effect size estimation and statistical inference. This informs the researcher about the magnitude of an effect, and also whether it is significantly different from zero, which are arguably more relevant for theoretical advancement than whether a replication attempt was successful or not. By focusing on effect size estimation in the SCORE data, we also analyzed these data from a new angle that had not yet been considered. We conducted a numerical study tailored to the characteristics of the SCORE data to evaluate the statistical properties of the different methods. In general, the methods to assess replication success did not perform well, particularly in conditions when performance matters most, that is, when the true effect size is zero or close to zero. Hence, we do not recommend applying these methods. The only exception was the equivalence test that was able to correctly determine replication success if both study sample sizes were large (> 2,000). There was often not enough information in the data (i.e., sample sizes were too small) to draw a reliable conclusion about replication success. This is surprising and telling, because the sample sizes of the replications in the SCORE project were large; the median statistical power to detect the original effect size in the replications was 99.6%. However, for methods assessing replication success to perform well, the sample size of the original study needs to be large as well, which does not frequently occur in practice. Hence, we recommend researchers to be reluctant with applying replication success methods if the true effect size is expected to be zero or small in combination with sample sizes that are not large. In our numerical study, we also studied the statistical properties of methods for meta-analyzing the original study and replication. The effect size estimators of the traditional frequentist and Bayesian FE model were biased and corresponding hypothesis tests had inflated Type-1 error rates. Dedicated methods to correct for selection bias in the original study, hybrid, HYEMA, and snapshot hybrid, showed low bias and the ability to correctly quantify the magnitude of the effect size. The results also showed that HYEMA was an improvement over hybrid, because it had larger statistical power when testing the null hypothesis of no effect in all conditions. However, it also turned out that the uncertainty in the results of the methods to correct for selection bias was larger than of methods that did not correct for bias, so correcting for bias comes at the cost of less precise estimation. Based on all results, we recommend applying HYEMA to estimate the effect size. FE meta-analysis performed slightly better than HYEMA if statistical power for the original study was high, but this depends on the true effect size that is unknown in practice. In the reanalysis of the SCORE data, we focused on methods to estimate the effect size and test the null hypothesis of no effect given the unfavorable statistical properties of the replication success methods. When estimating the effect size in the SCORE data, the estimates based on the combination of an original study and replication were closer to zero than those of the original study and in between estimates of the original study and replication. There were only small differences between methods that corrected and did not correct for bias. The estimates averaged over all pairs of original study and replication were close to 0.2 and the modes of the estimates were approximately 0.07, signaling many true effect sizes that were of zero or small magnitude. The lack of correction for bias was likely caused by the generally substantially larger sample size of the replication versus the original study, resulting in more weight of the unbiased replication in a meta-analysis. However, we do not recommend to only apply methods that do not correct for bias, because our numerical study showed that the Type-1 error rate of these methods could be severely inflated. Hence, not surprisingly, these methods had a higher percentage of statistically significant findings than the methods that aim to correct for bias in the reanalysis of the SCORE data. We also presented the results of the reanalysis of the SCORE data separately for each research field. Most important to mention is that results within each field were diverse; with the exception of business and sociology where no large effects were found. The snapshot hybrid method found evidence for zero, small, medium, and large effects in study-pairs in the fields of economics, education, political science, and psychology. Hence, one should always be careful with general statements on the magnitude of the studies’ true effect size in a field. Evidence for zero or small effect sizes (i.e., a correlation of 0.1 or smaller) was predominant (> 50%) in all research fields except for economics, and with strongest evidence for zero and small effects in business. Because the distinction between a zero and small true effect size is most important for both theoretical and practical reasons and these true effects are most challenging for statistical methods to perform well, designing (original and replication) studies with sufficiently large sample sizes is of utmost importance when a zero or small true effect size is expected. Larger sample sizes then also result in less biased effect size estimators of traditional statistical methods, which we observed for original studies in economics that often use small sample sizes compared to other fields. As using large sample sizes for medium or large true effect sizes may entail a waste of resources, we support [ 53 ] and others to recommend conducting power analyses for study sample size justification. An important conclusion based on our findings is that there may not be sufficient information in a single original study and a single replication to draw definite conclusions. This observation is in line with previous work [e.g., 12,21–23,25]. At least part of the solution to this is conducting multiple replication studies. This can be set up as a registered replication report [ 54 ], in which an original study is replicated in multiple labs using the same experimental procedures. A registered replication report has the additional advantage that it allows for studying whether characteristics of the labs influence the replicability. Another solution is not to consider the original study, particularly when it has a small sample size, as it yields a biased effect size estimator due to being selected for statistical significance either by the researchers who replicate a study and/or publication bias [ 13 ]. This solution is backed up by our analysis of the SCORE data that generally showed similar estimates of the replication and meta-analysis methods correcting for statistical significance in the original study. However, note that that the precision is usually higher of these meta-analysis methods because of using more information than only the replication. We followed the implementation of the methods used in SCORE for both the numerical study and the reanalysis of the SCORE data. The only exception was that we used different Bayes factor thresholds. In SCORE [ 32 ], different Bayes factor thresholds were used for different methods. This made comparisons between methods challenging, so we implemented a Bayes factor of three for all methods such that three times more evidence for one hypothesis was considered replication success. A Bayes factor threshold of three is a commonly used threshold reflecting moderate evidence in favor of a hypothesis [ 45 ]. The numerical study was tailored to characteristics of the SCORE data. An advantage of this is that the results of the numerical study are directly applicable to the SCORE data. A disadvantage of this approach is that the generalizability of the findings of the numerical study might be limited. Future research might want to conduct an analysis with a more general set of conditions, although we believe that the methods assessing consistency remain flawed in case of selection of a statistically significant original study. Another limitation is that our calculation of the percentages of replication success and of the average effect size did not consider that some studies (32.0%) yielded multiple effects. These results may be biased if original studies yielding multiple effects systematically obtain higher (or lower) replication success or effect size than unique original studies. Tyner et al. [ 32 ] reported results both at the level of individual effects and at the level of the paper, and found only small differences between the two; median Pearson correlations of 0.25 (paper level) and 0.24 (effect level) for original studies, and 0.10 (paper level) and 0.13 (effect level) for replications. Hence, the violation of the independence assumption in our analyses is expected to affect our results only to a small extent and is unlikely to alter our conclusions. A final limitation of this study is that we reanalyzed the shared data by SCORE. By reanalyzing the shared data by SCORE, we also adopted their effect size transformations to correlation coefficients. We subsequently applied Fisher’s z transformation to the data in order to work with an approximately normally distributed effect size measure in our analyses. These effect size transformations often hinge on strong assumptions and yielding effect size estimates with unknown distributional properties [ 55 ]. Applying the transformations may have introduced bias in the results but also helped summarizing the results as most effect sizes were on a common scale. To conclude, we have shown that not all replication success methods had good statistical properties for the SCORE data. This warrants for not routinely applying replication success methods, but only applying methods that are expected to have good properties for the analyzed data. The decision on what methods to apply can be based on our numerical study or on available literature that has examined the properties of the methods [e.g., 22,27,28]. We hope for a continued attention for replications and the development of replication success methods that overcome the deficiencies of the existing methods.
