ABSTRACT
This LDRD project sought to evaluate molecular cages and related scaffolds as effective CO2
capture and catalytic processing agents. CO2(g) is an abundant carbon source that is prevalent in flue
gas. Molecular cages are promising scaffolds to selectively extract and efficiently chemically process
CO2 into valorized products. We computationally screened molecular cages for CO2 binding and
catalysis, synthesized several promising cage, half-cage, and macrocycles, spectroscopically
characterized their structures, and tested them for catalytic activity. We used mass spectrometry-
based laser spectroscopy and computational predictions to determine the structures of several
unprecedented ion-cage complexes. We discovered a set of macrocycles that selectively capture CO2
from air by forming a carbamate anion and modified the macrocycle to fine-tune the processes of
CO2 uptake and release. We improved by 8-fold the catalytic efficiency of a metal-pincer catalyst for
CO2 reduction to formate anion (HCO2-) by immobilizing in a polymer matrix.

1. INTRODUCTION
This report summarizes the progress we have made during LDRD 233046 on the over-arching goal
of this LDRD project; namely, to develop and evaluate cryptands and other cage-like compounds as
effective CO2 capture and catalytic processing agents. We formed a team composed of experts in
molecular synthesis (Harrison Root, Kristin Bowman-James), computational screening (Jessica
Rimsza), spectroscopic characterization (Timothy Zwier, PI) and computational spectroscopy
(Daniel Tabor,SUPN), and catalytic processing (Vitalie Stavila).

 X

Figure 1.1: Workflow and Timeline

Figure 1.1 shows the workflow and timeline we chose to follow, involving work on macrocycles,
multicycle cryptand molecular cages, cages and half-cages that mimic carbonic anhydrase (nature’s
tool for capturing and chemical processing CO2), and tethered cryptands. The chapters that follow
are divided approximately by the major tasks that addressed these various molecular scaffolds for
CO2 capture and chemical processing; namely, synthesis, computational screening, spectroscopic
characterization, and studies of the uptake/release of CO2 and the catalytic properties of the
compounds towards chemical processing.
Our efforts also divided naturally along studies of molecular scaffolds for capturing catalytically
active metal cations, and molecular scaffolds for selective capture and chemical processing of CO2 to
form carbamate adducts of the cages, from which they can be later released.

2. SYNTHESIS: CAGES AND HALF-CAGES FOR METAL ION CATALYSIS
This chapter describes the synthetic strategies, procedures, and outcomes led by Harrison Root, who
focused attention on the synthesis of molecular cages and half-cages that bind catalytically active
metal cations. A major focus of the work was on synthesis of cages containing tripodal nitrogen-
containing ligands that are known or suspected to bind Zn2+, the cation used by carbonic anhydrase
to capture CO2 in plants.

2.1. Synthesis of Imidazole Cages
Initial synthetic efforts in year one focused around development of an imidazole containing cage
(figure 1), which was hypothesized to have distinct binding pockets wherein a metal could
coordinate and interact with adsorbed gas molecules, in a mechanism reminiscent of carbonic
anhydrase.

 Figure 2.1. Synthetic scheme of imidazole containing cage, figure reproduced from Bete et. al.4

Under the conditions reported by Bete and coworkers4, we were unable to obtain the desired
product. Slight modifications to the protocols were made and ultimately the desired cage was
obtained, however the yield was low and irreproducible, causing our team to develop other
complexing cage geometries which better optimized synthetic protocols. The synthesis and
characterization of imidazole cage is detailed below.

 Scheme 1. Synthetic procedure to dibromo-imidazole precursor.

 13

To a round bottom flask charged with THF (500 mL), 1,3-Dibromo-2-(bromomethyl)benzene (
7.38g, 22.5 mmol), imidazole (12.76g, 188 mmol), and potassium carbonate (35.4g, 256 mmol) were
added. The reaction mixture was degassed and allowed to stir at reflux for 20 hours. The reaction
mixture was filtered and the resultant solution was dried via a rotary evaporator. The resulting solid
was dissolved in DCM (50 mL), and the solution was washed three times with saturated sodium
carbonate solution (30 mL). The organic layer was separated and dried with magnesium sulfate
before being filtered and dried again via rotary evaporator. According to Bete and coworkers, the
crude product could be purified by recrystallization in dichloromethane, this procedure only yielded
unreacted imidazole in our hands. However; recrystallization with ethyl acetate did result in the
desired dibromo-imidazole product. Which matched spectroscopically to the cited literature.

Figure 2.2. 1H NMR spectra of dibromo-imidazole precursor.

After successful synthesis of the dibromo-imidazole precursor, Suzuki coupling was used to
synthesize the desired dialdehyde-imidazole material (scheme 2).

Scheme 2. Synthesis of dialdehyde-imidazole precursor.

 14

Toluene (50 mL), ethanol (25 mL), and water (25 mL) were added to a 250 mL round bottom flask
and the resulting mixture was degassed with nitrogen. After degassing, dibromo-imidazole precursor
(1 g, 3 mmol), tetrakis(triphenylphosphine) palladium (0) (0.5 g, 0.4 mmol), sodium carbonate (9.9g,
93 mmol), and 4-formylphenylboronic acid (1.43 g, 10 mmol) were added to the reaction mixture.
The reaction was allowed to stir at 85 �C for 24 hours under an inert atmosphere. After 24 hours
the reaction was allowed to cool and water (80 mL) and ethyl acetate (45 mL) were added. The
organic layer was separated and the aqueous layer was further extracted with ethyl acetate (2 x 45
mL). The resulting organic layers were washed with water (3 x 45 mL) and then dried over
magnesium sulfate. The suspension was filtered and the organic layer was dried via rotary
evaporator. The resulting solid was dissolved in dichloromethane (45 mL) and then filtered twice
through activated charcoal. The solution was then concentrated via rotary evaporator and then
dissolved in a minimal volume of dichlormethane. Crystals were grown by slow diffusion of pentane
into the concentrated dichloromethane solution to yield the purified product (figure 3).

Figure 2.3. 1H NMR spectra of dialdehyde-imidazole precursor.

Finally, synthesis of the final cage was attempted (scheme 3) using commercially available 2,4,6-
Triethyl-1,3,5-beneznetrimethanamine, which is sold as a hydrochloride salt.

 15

Scheme 3. Attempted synthesis of imidazole containing cage.

2,4,6-Triethyl-1,3,5-beneznetrimethanamine trihydrochloride and dialdehyde-imidazole were placed
in a 50 mL round bottom flask and placed under an inert atmosphere. Afterwhich, a 3:1 mixture of
dichloromethane:methanol (15 mL) was added to the flask and the reaction mixture was allowed to
stir at room temperature for 48 hours. After 48 hours, sodium borohydride (52 mg, xx mmol) was
added in small portions and then reaction was allowed to stir three hours more. The mixture was
diluted with dichlorometane (10 mL) and then washed with water (4 x 10 mL). The resulting
suspension was filtered and concentrated via rotary evaporator and then purified by column
chromatography using 2% methanol in dichloromethane with an added 1% of ammonia in methanol
(1M) as an eluent. The resulting white solid was not the desired product, but was instead the reduced
dialdehyde-imidazole precursor (figure 4).

 Figure 2.4. Product resulting from attempted imidazole cage synthesis.

We hypothesize that this reaction failed because the hydrochloride salt of 2,4,6-Triethyl-1,3,5-
beneznetrimethanamine acidifies the solution enough to disfavor imine formation. We attempted to
remedy this problem by first washing the trihydrochloride starting material in 40 KOH and
extracting into chloroform, then using this activated material in the synthesis. Following the same
procedure outlined above (with the substitution of the freebase amine, and no sodium borohydride),
we were able to obtain the Schiff base version of the desired cage (Figure 5, 6) in a 10 mg yield.

 Figure 2.5. Synthesized Schiff-base imidazole cage.

 Figure 2.6. 1H NMR of synthesized Schiff-base imidazole cage

Unfortunately, due to the low yield (tens of milligrams) and irreproducibility of the synthesis, we
decided to pivot our synthetic efforts to more readily accessible cage structures which might have
proven to be effective catalysts for CO2.

2.2 Synthesis and Characterization of a tris-Biphenyl Cage5
The search for an easy-to-synthesize cage which can still bind metals led us to a class of cryptand
cages bearing amine and imine linkages to bis(2-aminoethyl)ethane-1,2-diamine caps. These cages
precipitate during synthesis and can be synthesized in one synthetic step from commercially available
precursors.

 17

 Figure 2.7. Synthetic scheme of tris-Biphenyl cage.

To synthesize this compound, diphenyl bicarbaldehyde (1 g, 4.8 mmol) was dissolved in acetonitrile
(100 mL). While stirring, a solution of bis(2-aminoethyl)ethane-1,2-diamine (TREN) (468 mg, 4.8
mmol) in acetonitrile (100 mL) was added dropwise. The reaction was left stirring at room
temperature overnight and an off-white precipitate was formed. The solution was filtered and then
washed three times with acetonitrile (10 mL) to yield the Schiff base biphenyl cage. Reduction of the
imine bonds to form the amine cage was achieved using sodium borohydride. Schiff base cage (1 g,
1.2 mmol) was dissolved in methanol (150 mL) in a 500 mL round bottom flask. The suspension
was degassed and sodium borohydride (2.72 g, 72 mmol) was added portion wise over one hour.
The reaction was allowed to stir at room temperature for three hours. The resulting solution was
evaporated to dryness via rotary evaporator and then redissolved in dichloromethane (20 mL) and
then washed with water (2 x 20 mL). The organic layers were dried with sodium sulfate, filtered, and
then evaporated to dryness resulting in the amine cage as a white powder (0.8g).

Figure 2.8. 1H NMR of tris-biphenyl cage.

The resulting tris-biphenyl cage was able to be complexed with Zinc, which we hypothesized would
allow for gas binding and catalytic centers for CO2 conversion reactions. Tris-biphenyl cage (10 mg,

0.012 mmol) and zinc acetate (5.3 mg, 0.024 mmol) were dissolved in 10 mL of 1:3
methanol:dichloromethane. The reaction was allowed to stir overnight and the precipitate was
filtered and then washed with methanol:dichloromethane to yield a tan powder of zinc complex.
The material was dissolved in acetonitrile and allowed to slowly evaporate to yield crystals of the
zinc complex (figure 9).

Figure 2.9. Single crystal structure of Zn – tris-biphenyl cage. Hydrogens and counterbalancing

Peak broadening and shifting in the NMR spectra was also observed which corroborated the single
crystal data proving successful metal complexation (figure 10).

Figure 2.10. 1H NMR spectra comparison between tris-biphenyl cage (top) and Zn complexed tris-
biphenyl cage (bottom).

Catalytic CO2 hydrogenation reactions were assembled in an inert N2 glovebag. For screening
experiments, catalyst (10 mg) was added to a reaction vessel along with triethylamine (6.00 mmol),
deionized water (3.0 mL), and a magnetic stir bar. The reaction vessel was sealed in a stainless-steel
100 mL Parr autoclave equipped with magnetic stirring and connected to a Parr reactor system. The
reactor was then charged to a total pressure of 70 bar with a 3:1 H2:CO2 gas mixture. The reaction
was heated to 120 °C and stirred for 2 h while pressure and temperature were monitored
throughout.

 19

After completion, the reactor was cooled to room temperature and depressurized. DMF (155 µL,
2.00 mmol) was added directly to the reaction mixture as an internal standard, followed by D2O (1.0
mL) to rinse the reactor, and an aliquot (70.0 µL) was removed and diluted with additional D2O (1.0
mL). The diluted aliquot was filtered through a 0.2 µm syringe filter and submitted for 1H NMR
analysis to quantify formate relative to the internal standard.

3. SYNTHESIS: MIXED AMINE/AMIDE MACROCYCLES FOR CO2
 CAPTURE AND RELEASE

 3.1 Introduction
The role of the Bowman-James group was to synthesize a series of macrocyclic and/or cryptand hosts
to either reversibly bind CO2, e.g., capture and release, or to bind CO2 for catalytic manipulation with
the goal of conversion to value-added chemical products. Amines are well known for their tendency
to form carbamates by reacting with CO2. Earlier work in terms of carbamate formation and
stabilization, García-España and coworkers effectively demonstrated carbamate formation in a
terpyridinophane-based aza macrocycle, where the carbamate is stabilized through interaction with a
bound Cu(II) ion,8, 9 while Gale’s group has shown that alkylcarbamate anions formed upon CO2
binding can be stabilized by neutral urea-based anion receptors.10, 11 Leigh’s group has reported an
interesting binding phenomenon of CO2 with a similar pincer-like tetra-amide macrocycle that does
not contain amine groups.12, 13 However, issues have surrounded the secondary process of either
catalytic conversion to more usable organic products; or release, the latter frequently requiring
significant energy input (e.g., heating).
This part of the LDRD project builds off a previous study during which amide/amine-based
macrocycles were found to capture CO2 forming carbamate complexes.14 This early structure was
stabilized through salt bridge formation between the carbamate and protonated secondary amine
(Figure 3.1). Complete carbamate formation was found to occur within a minute by bubbling CO2
through a solution of the macrocycle. However, while carbamate formation occurred readily, CO2
release required heating at 100o while bubbling Ar through the solution for one hour (R = H) and 10
minutes for R = methyl. Hence, considering the high temperatures required for CO2 release, the
overall catch and release process is energetically not favorable. Hence in the current LDRD project
we decided to explore other mixed amide/amine macrocycles and cryptands, with expanded work
on the prototype macrocycle, 1.

structure in (b).

We initially proposed three frameworks for the LDRD proposal: (1) simple macrocycles based on

through-put CO2 capture, potentially followed by catalytic conversion to other valued products; and

During preliminary studies, the amide cryptand and Mn, Zn-porphyrin aza cryptand were found to
be cost and labor prohibitive so most of the focus was on the prototype macrocycle.

3.3 Kinetic Studies of Carbamate Release

3.3.1 Experimental Section

The kinetics of CO₂ release from the 24-member carbamate adducts 1·CO₂/1·2CO2 and the 27-
membered 2·CO₂ were investigated using 1H NMR at different temperatures. Spectra were acquired
at 90, 100, 110, 120, and 130 °C for 1 and 70, 80, 90, 100, and 110 °C for 2 in DMSO-d6 under a
continuous nitrogen atmosphere. To ensure reproducibility, all kinetic experiments were performed
under identical conditions. A fresh 40 mmol solution of CO2-loaded macrocycle was used at each
temperature, while the same NMR tube, nitrogen-bubbling setup, and constant nitrogen flow was
employed throughout the study. At each temperature, the CO₂-loaded macrocycle solution was
exposed to a constant nitrogen flow for successive predetermined time intervals. A 1H NMR
spectrum was acquired after each interval, and the nitrogen bubbling was subsequently resumed
under identical conditions. The sequence was repeated until complete disappearance of the
carbamate resonances was observed, indicating complete CO₂ desorption. The aromatic proton
signals, which remained unchanged during the experiment, were used as an internal reference for
normalization of the NMR integrations. The normalized carbamate concentration at each time point
was fitted using a first-order kinetic model. Each kinetic experiment was carried out in triplicate,
from which an average rate constant (kavg(T)) and error was calculated.

3.3.2 Kinetic analysis of CO2 release

Table 3.1 contains the results of the kinetic analysis of the NMR data. The temperature dependence
of the rate constant for 1·CO2 and 1·2CO2 was calculated using the Arrhenius equation and resulted
in an activation energy (Ea) of 88.1 kJ mol-1. This relatively high activation energy barrier indicates
the robust nature of the 1 carbamate adduct at ambient temperature. The higher activation energy
than the reported aqueous amines and amine-functionalized porous sorbents (50-60 KJ mol-1)15 is

 3.4 Conclusions

In this work, amine/amide-based macrocycles were established as a potential candidate for an efficient
and repeated direct air capture of CO2 despite its low atmospheric concentration. Indeed, the 24-
membered macrocycle 1 was even able to bind CO2 in the solid state. These carbamates are stabilized
through multiple intramolecular hydrogen bonds as well as electrostatic interaction with the second,
intramolecular protonated amine site. This internal zwitterion accounts for energy intensive
regeneration of free macrocycle. Additionally, kinetic investigations of 1 and 2 offered quantitative
insight into their decarboxylation behavior. 1H-NMR experiments at variable temperatures enabled
determination of activation parameters, revealing the impact of subtle structural modifications on the
stability and release kinetics of the corresponding carbamates. The dicarbamate of the 24-membered
1 displayed a more complex CO2 release time-profile at high temperatures as compared to the larger
28-membered macrocycle with only one carbamate as indicated by the lower activation energy for 2
compared to 1, (58.31 vs 88.1 kJ mol-1, respectively). These findings contribute to a deeper
understanding of structure-property dynamics in macrocyclic CO₂ capture systems. The longer,
tetramine macrocycle 3 appears to impart structural flexibility to the macrocyclic skeleton leading to
spontaneous capture of atmospheric CO2 with a lower activation energy for release of the CO2,
providing facile and reversible CO2 uptake. In contrast, the smaller macrocycles provide more rigid
cavities, that better stabilize the carbamate once formed but also hinder its energy-efficient release.

4. COMPUTATIONAL SCREENING OF METAL CATION-CRYPTANDS

 4.1 Introduction
Molecular scale simulations were used to evaluate and identify structure-property relationships
within benzocryptand structures and the potential impact on gas separation properties, represented
by interactions with CO2. DFT study of benzocryptand (BzCrypt) complexes with ten metal cations,
starting from the K-BzCrypt structure, each cation was substituted and optimized to evaluate
stability, coordination geometry, and binding properties. Stability correlates with ionic radius and
coordination environment, with a cutoff near 0.8 Å below which cations (Li+, Zn2+) become
unstable. CO2 binding energies range from -16 to -37 kJ/mol, primarily governed by metal-oxygen
distances and coordination geometry. The study validates computational methods against literature,
identifies potential new stable complexes to be evaluated experimentally, and highlights potential for
catalytic applications and incorporation of larger metal clusters or anions in BzCrypt.

 4.2 Structure Optimization
Gas-phase molecular structure optimizations were carried out for BzCrypt complexes with a set of
ten metal cations (Na+, Mg2+, K+, Ca2+, Sr2+, Cd2+, Ba2+, Cs+, Li+, Zn2+) using the Fritz Haber
Institute ab initio molecular simulations (FHI-AIMS)16-19 code, chosen due to its computational
efficiency for large molecules. Each molecule was optimized with B3LYP generalized gradient
approximation (GGA)20 without constraints. Energy and forces were converged to 1×10–4 eV and
5×10–3 eV/Å, respectively. All calculations included scalar relativistic corrections and exchange-hole
dipole moment (XDM) dispersion correction21-23. The starting structures for all these optimizations
are based on the reported K+-BzCrypt structure from Foley et al.24, 25, with the internal cation
replaced. The BzCrypt structures were relaxed with increasing basis set size from ‘light’ to
‘reallytight’ based on standard procedure for calculating structures using the FHI-AIMs code.

 4.4 Conclusions
Our computational evaluation of a series of BzCrypt structures for CO2 binding identified that large
metallic cations, such as Ba2+ can be introduced into the structure to maximize interactions with
CO2. Interestingly, no upper bound was identified for cation incorporation into the BzCrypt. Other

 37

cryptand structures, such as the polyoxometalate cryptand34 have exhibited size selective for
encapsulation of the metal ion, indicating that the ability to adsorb large cations is not a universal
feature of cryptands. Since there is sufficient flexibly in the BzCrypt structure to accommodate large
ions, small di- or trinuclear metal clusters could instead be considered for incorporation or
encapsulation, providing a free metal site for catalysis. Note that binuclear cryptands are known,
such as the family of bimetallic hexaanionic carboxamide cryptands reported by Alliger at al. with
Mn2+, Fe2+/3+, Co2+, Ni2+, and Zn2+ ions35 and hexacarboxamide cryptands with Co2+ metals36 among
others37-39, but to the best of our knowledge bimetallic BzCrypt have yet to be reported. Another
interesting avenue would be the incorporation of anion species, which are larger than cations. For
example, favorable binding between Ba2+ and acetate within BzCrypt structures has been reported
by Zwier40. While the acetate was not incorporated into the cage, it stabilized via interaction with the
Ba2+ ion, effectively generating an overall +1-charged Ba-Acetate-BzCrypt structure with potential
pathways for synthesis of novel M-BzCrypt structures. Anion binding in cryptands and macrocycles
other than BzCrypt have been extensively reviewed41, further supporting the potential stability of
anion adsorption in these systems. While outside the scope of this study, a pathway for use of
BzCrypt as a scaffold for single atom or diatomic sites for catalysis or incorporation of anion species
could be explored.

5. ADAPTIVE HALIDE BINDING WITH ANION SIZE: TRIGGERING
 CHIRAL D3 TO TRIGONAL PRISM CHANGES IN A MOLECULAR
 CAGE42

 5.1 Abstract
We report the IR spectroscopy and structural analysis of the halide ions F-, Cl-, and Br- bound inside
a bicyclic amide-based cryptand cage (1) under cryo-cooled conditions in the gas phase. We record
infrared spectra of the cryo-cooled gas phase ions in the hydride stretch and fingerprint regions
using IR-UV double resonance methods. The IR spectra in the NH and CH stretch regions are
remarkably simple, reflecting highly symmetric structures for all three 1.X- complexes. We compare
the experimental spectra with calculated candidate structures using first-principles predictions of the
infrared spectra in the NH, CH, and fingerprint regions. We assign the 1.F- complex to a 9-
coordinate, D3 symmetry structure that contains six NH···F- and three aromatic CH···F- H-bonds,
much as it does in the crystalline solid, with a trigonal twist angle a=35o. While the gas-phase and X-
ray structures of 1.F- are similar, the crystal structure is compressed by its surroundings, with the
distance between the two bridgehead tertiary amines that is 0.58 Å shorter than our assigned gas-
phase structure. In contrast, the 1·Cl- and 1·Br- complexes are uniquely assigned based on their
infrared spectra to C3h symmetry structures in which the six amide N’s form a 6-coordinate trigonal
prism (a=0o) binding pocket, while the two tertiary N’s form caps on the prism’s triangular faces. To
accommodate these larger halide ions, the three aromatic rings rotate so that their closest CH groups
are nearly tangential to the spherical halide ion at the cage’s center.

 5.2 Introduction
 The field of supramolecular chemistry began out of the desire to capture ions selectively from
solution by pre-organizing the size, shape, and flexibility of molecules to match the ion’s binding
pocket, the lock and key effect.43-47 While supramolecular hosts come in many shapes and sizes, the
cryptand cage, first coined by Lehn, is among the most well-studied.41, 43, 46, 48 Bicyclic cryptands
encapsulate ions and molecules inside a 3-dimensional cage, leading to tighter binding than in simple
monocycles. While most studies of supramolecular structures and binding have been in solution and
solid state, very few reports of gas phase study of supramolecular interactions in the absence of
competing influences have appeared.25, 49-57 By their very nature, the isolated ion complexes
experience no structural distortions from their surroundings, providing a direct comparison with
calculations and an assessment of the inherent structural preferences that are at play.
 Recently we have been exploring the gas phase structural interactions of a polyether cryptand,
[2.2.2]-benzocryptand, and its binding with alkali metal ions (Figure 5.1a).25, 54 Here we expand our
study of cryptand binding from alkali metal cations to the halide anions, following the recent work
of Terry et al. on halide ion binding in calix-4-pyrrole complexes.56, 57 Our work combines both
experimental and computational investigation of the series of amidocryptand complexes 1·X- with X
= F, Cl, and Br (Figure 5.1b).41, 58-63 We recorded the infrared and ultraviolet spectra of the
sequestered ions under cryo-cooled conditions in the gas phase, leading to spectra that report
directly on the structure of the cage and its interaction with the halide anions bound inside it,
without interference from solvent or counterions or packing effects.

 39

Figure 5.1: (a) Chemical structures of [2.2.2]-Benzocryptand, and (b) the bicyclic phenyl-
amidocryptand (1), a selective halide anion receptor. Our work focuses on 1·X- complexes with

 In order to achieve selective binding of anions one must overcome numerous chemical
challenges compared to cations. First, anions are larger in size than their cation counterparts (e.g.,
Cl- vs K+) due to their more diffuse negative charges. Second, beyond the halides, molecular anions
come in multiple shapes and sizes with their negative charges resonantly stabilized throughout the
molecular structure. Finally, anions have high solvation energies that must be overcome for efficient
binding of the anion in the receptor site.
 We chose for our study the amidocryptand 1 originally reported by the Bowman-James group41,
58-63
 , focusing on binding of 1 with a series of halide ions in the gas phase to examine the
supramolecular interactions once encapsulated. Halide ions, although the simplest of the anions by
virtue of their spherical nature, are nonetheless highly important in Nature’s vast aqueous resources,
playing key roles in many biological processes and finding widespread use in industrial applications.
 Amidocryptand 1 has the fundamental architecture of a bicyclic three-dimensional cage, but its
interior walls are decorated with six amide NH groups that can engage in H-bonding to the halide
anions. The cavity is well-shaped to accommodate these spherical anions. Furthermore, the cavity
size is small enough to provide significant size-selectivity in its binding, with binding constants in
DMSO that fall off sharply with increasing halide ion size.63 Finally, the original studies included the
X-ray crystal structure of the fluoride ion complex, 1·F-, with which we can directly compare our
gas-phase results.63 In the crystal, binding is formally 9-coordinate because the aromatic CH groups
of the isophthaloyl moieties provide three CH···X- interactions in addition to the six NH···X- H-
bonds, a result confirmed by the 19F NMR coupling pattern in solution. Recently, Flood and co-
workers took this CH binding motif to its ultimate limit in creating a receptor with remarkable
selectivity for chloride ion, but made up entirely of CH···Cl- interactions from triazole groups.48
 The gas phase studies add qualitative new insight to these cages and their binding. In 1·F-,
determination of the gas phase structure completes its study in all three phases, as 19F NMR data was
consistent with a similar 9-coordinate binding for the complex in DMSO-d6 solution. Furthermore,
there is no X-ray crystal structure of neutral cage 1 with Cl- or Br-. In these cases, the structural

 40

deductions we make in the gas phase provide the only structural data available and directly report on
the inherent capacity of the amidocryptand cage to adsorb Br- in its interior.
 In our mass spectrometry-based studies, we cool the ionic complexes to vibrational temperatures
of about T=10 K in a cryo-cooled ion trap prior to recording their infrared spectra, thereby
removing congestion and broadening due to hot bands from the spectra. We present evidence that a
single conformation of each ion-cage complex is present in the ion trap, with all three halide ions
embedded inside the amidocryptand cage.
 To make structural assignments, we compare the vibrational spectra with calculated spectra of an
ensemble of possible structures generated from a newly developed conformational search algorithm.
As we shall see, based on the NH stretch, C=O stretch (amide I) and NH bend (amide II) infrared
transitions of the amide groups, and confirmed by the alkyl CH stretch fits, we can uniquely assign
the structure of the 1·F- complex to a nine-coordinate D3 symmetry structure similar to that from
the crystalline solid. By contrast, both the 1·Cl- and 1·Br- cage complexes embed the halide ion at the
center of a C3h symmetry cage structure, with the aromatic rings rotated so that they lie tangential to
the imbedded halide ions rather than pointing a CH group toward the ion. This C3h geometry is an
unusual bicapped trigonal prismatic structure in which the tren groups (tren=tris(aminoethyl)amine)
form caps on the triangular faces of the trigonal prism. We will discuss these structures, their
spectroscopic signatures, and the reasons for the change in structure that accompanies changing the
size of the halide ion. As we shall see, our gas-phase results clarify that cage 1 can adapt itself
binding pocket to the larger Cl- and Br- anions, still forming strongly bound complexes in which all
six amide NH groups are involved in equivalent H-bonds to the central halide ion. This points to
other factors being responsible for the drop-off in association constants observed in solution.

 5.3 Experimental and Computational Methods
 5.3.1 Sandia Cryo-Ion Experiments
 The experimental work was carried out in an apparatus that has been described previously.64, 65
The amidocryptand cage 1 was synthesized as described previously.62 The 1·X- complexes were
prepared by dissolving 1 at 100 �M concentration in a 50:50 H2O:CH3OH mixture. NaF, NaCl, and
NaBr salts were dissolved separately and added to the mixture at 8 times the concentration of 1.
 Ions from the electrospray source were stored in a linear quadrupole ion trap (QIT) before mass
selection of the parent ion using RF/DC (apex) isolation.66 The mass selected ions were transported
to an octupole ion trap cooled by a closed cycle refrigerator to 5 K. Helium pulsed into the trap
serves as trapping gas and a mechanism for cooling the internal degrees of freedom of the ions.
 UV photofragment spectra were recorded using the doubled output of a Nd:YAG pumped dye
laser (Coumarin 540A). UV excitation leads to photo-fragmentation of a few percent of the parent
ions in the octupole trap. The ion mixture is released from the trap to an analysis linear QIT that
operates in one of two modes. When recording a UV spectrum, resonant ejection is used to remove
parent ions from the trap selectively prior to collection of all remaining UV photofragment ions
independent of their mass. Alternatively, the mass spectrum of the photofragment ions can be
recorded at a fixed UV wavelength. We carried out the UV scans in sections of 200 cm-1 with UV
power monitored by a linear photodiode and kept flat over the scanned wavelength range.
 IR-UV double resonance was used to record ground state infrared spectra. To that end, a tuned
IR source (LaserVision OPO/OPA) was spatially overlapped with the UV laser in the cold ion trap
but temporally preceding the UV by 100 ns. Due to the breadth of the UV spectra, it was not

 41

possible to take conformer specific spectra using IR-UV depletion. However, by proper choice of
UV wavelength, we could record IR-UV gain spectra by detecting an IR-induced gain in the UV
photofragment ion signal. While these spectra are not inherently conformer-specific, the reported IR
gain spectra can be fully and uniquely accounted for by a single conformation in all three 1·X-
complexes. IR spectra in the 2600-3600 cm-1 region were recorded using the unfocused idler output
from the OPO/OPA at powers of about 10 mJ/pulse. Extension into the mid-IR required
difference frequency mixing of the signal and idler from the KTA crystals of the OPO/OPA in a
AgGaSe2 crystal, producing powers in the 0.25-2 mJ/pulse range in the 1000-1700 cm-1 region.

5.3.2 Calculations
Conformational Searches
 Initial conformational searches for each of the three ions interacting with the cryptand cages
were conducted with the CREST 3.0 package, using the GFN2-xTB functional to evaluate the
energies of the conformers.67 Searches were initialized from several different sets of Cartesian
coordinates, including highly symmetric conformers. The conformer searching was conducted both
with and without the non-covalent interactions setting included in the CREST package, returning
conformers found within 20 kcal/mol of the minimum energy structure. From here, all conformers
were optimized (and harmonic frequencies were calculated) first at the lower-cost r2SCAN-3c level
of theory developed by Grimme and co-workers.68 A select set of the low energy conformers were
optimized at the DFT B3LYP-D3/6-311+G(d,p)69-71 and wB97x-D3/6-311+G(d,p)72 levels of
theory. On the assigned structures, energy decomposition analysis was carried out using the ALMO-
EDA2 method73 at the B3LYP-D3BJ/6-311++G(d,p) level of theory using the Q-chem 6 package.74

Model Local Mode Hamiltonian
 The IR-UV double resonance spectra we acquire are in the 2600-3600 cm-1 region where
contributions due to the alkyl CH stretch, aromatic CH stretch, and amide NH stretch fundamentals
are present. Due to the number of degrees of freedom in the system, the vibrational spectroscopy
was initially evaluated using the r2SCAN-3c method. The scaling factors employed on the local mode
Hamiltonian matrix elements were parameterized based on prior work with alkylbenzenes.75
Following this, higher-level calculations were conducted at the B3LYP-D3BJ/6-311++G(d,p) and
wB97X-D3/6-311++G(d,p) levels of theory, applying the same scaling factors for the CH2 modes
as previously validated. All calculations were performed using the ORCA quantum chemistry
package.76
 The vibrational spectra were modeled using a reduced-dimensional local mode model
Hamiltonian framework. Local mode CH2 stretch vectors were obtained by rediagonalizing the
mass-weighted Cartesian force constant matrix, with other atoms (except the XH stretch of interest)
multiplied by a large number (25). When rediagonalized, the highest frequency mode corresponded
to the localized stretch of the desired XH oscillator. An orthogonal transformation was then applied
between the original normal mode coordinates and the local mode vectors. A similar procedure was
used to obtain the CH2 scissor mode vectors.77, 78
 The overall Hamiltonian (and linear dipole operator) was transformed to the local mode
representation at the harmonic level of theory. Coupling between the CH2 scissor modes and CH2
stretches was treated empirically, guided using the same scaling factors found in prior work (22 cm-
 ).

 5.3 Results and Analysis
 The amidocryptand cage 1 incorporates an isophthalamide group into each of the three linker
chains that make up the bicyclic cage. These serve as UV chromophores for UV spectroscopy and

of each of the three 1.X- complexes over the 34,400-37,000 cm-1 region (270-290 nm, Figure S5.1),
recorded under conditions in which photofragment ions of all masses are detected. In all three
complexes, a broad, nearly featureless UV absorption results, rising with excitation energy.
Photofragment mass spectra are shown in Figure S5.2.
 Figures 5.2a-c show IR-UV gain spectra of the three 1.X- cage complexes over the 2600-3600
cm-1 region that covers the NH and CH stretch fundamentals of the amidocryptand cage (Figure 1).
These gain spectra were recorded with the UV laser fixed at 36,800 cm-1. The three spectra are
strikingly similar to one another, indicating that the three share a close structural resemblance.
Despite the presence of 6 amide NH groups, 12 alkyl CH2 groups, and 12 aromatic CH groups, the
spectra are also notably uncongested. There are no free amide NH stretch fundamentals (3450-3500
cm-1),82 indicating that all six NH stretch groups in each cage complex are engaged in H-bonding.
Furthermore, the H-bonded amide NH stretch region has a single resolved band in each of the
complexes, pointing to a highly symmetric structure. The alkyl CH stretch regions of all three
spectra are also dominated by a sharp band near 2800 cm-1 that is likely due to symmetric stretch
transitions of the CH2 groups, while the (nominally) antisymmetric stretch transitions produce a
closely spaced set of transitions in the 2930-2980 cm-1 region. Finally, the aromatic CH stretch
region also has a single, dominant CH stretch transition at 3072 cm-1 whose intensity is enhanced in
1.F- relative to the Cl- and Br- analogs.
 Figures 5.2d-f present the corresponding IR-UV gain spectra of the 1.X- series in the 1450-1800
cm-1 region that covers the amide I (C=O stretch) and amide II (NH bend) regions. There are no
further significant absorptions identified in spectra that extend down to 1000 cm-1 (Figure S5.3).
Once again, a single band is resolved in both regions, consistent with high-symmetry structures in
which all six amide groups are in similar environments. We have recorded both IR-UV dip and IR-
UV gain spectra of the 1.Cl- complex (Figure S5.3), using UV monitor wavelengths at the red edge
of the spectrum (35160 cm-1) or blue edge (36800 cm-1) of the UV range studied here, respectively.
The depletion and gain spectra are identical to one another, indicating the presence of a single,
dominant structure responsible for the IR spectrum. In the 1.Cl-, and 1.Br- spectra, we see weak gain

complex, indicating the presence of a small leak-through of parent ion signal from one complex into
the other during the parent ion isolation step.

 5.4 Discussion
 5.4.1 Adaptive Binding by the Amidocryptand Cage
 Table 5.1 compares the key structural features of selected structures in the 1·X- series,
including the assigned global minima, low-lying energy isomers, and the X-ray crystal structure of
1·F-. A more complete structural comparison of the twelve structures from Figure 5.5 is given in
supplementary material (Table S5.1). The assigned, most stable structures of the isolated 1·X- cage
complexes, shown from two viewpoints in Figure 5.6a-c, possess certain structural elements in
common. For all three halide ions, the three isophthaloyl groups serve as bidentate ligands, using
both of their amide NH groups to H-bond to the halide ions, thereby forming a six-coordinate
binding pocket for the central halide ions. Second, as anticipated, all three halide ions also reside at
or very near the center of the ion cage, despite their significantly different six-coordinate ionic radii
(133 pm for F-, 181 pm for Cl-, and 196 pm for Br-). Third, the cage cavity is elliptical with its long
axis formed by the line joining the two tertiary amine N’s (labeled N(t) in Table 1, top view in
Figure 5.6). This long axis is significantly larger than the halide ion itself, minimizing destabilization
of the halide anion by the lone pairs of the tertiary amines. Fourth, the six N(H)···X- distances in

 Despite sharing certain structural features along the entire halide series, the cage itself does
reconfigure significantly with halide ion size. The assigned structure for the isolated 1·F- complex has
D3 symmetry, with one C3 symmetry axis (the N(t)···X-···N(t) axis) and three C2 axes perpendicular
to the C3 axis (Figure 5.6a), which bisect the phenyl rings of the three isophthaloyl groups. In the
1·F- complex, the cage adapts its shape to the small fluoride anion in part by twisting the linking
groups so that the aromatic rings are significantly tilted (top view), with forward facing lobes
pointing either clockwise (not shown) or counterclockwise (shown), thereby existing in two chiral
forms. It possesses a calculated trigonal twist angle of a=35o. This produces a propeller like
geometry with an end-to-end distance between the two bridgehead nitrogens of 7.97 Å and six
N(H)···F- distances near 3.04 Å.

 The D3 symmetry structure we observe in the isolate gas-phase 1·F- complex is the same 9-
coordinate structure found in the crystal structure of 1·F- from Kang et al., which was grown from a
solution of 1 in non-polar solvent with [n-Bu4]F as fluoride source (Table 1).62 Interestingly, the
crystal cage is compressed along its long axis (the bridgehead N axis) by 0.58 Å from the computed
gas-phase value, and also has shorter N(H)···F- distances by almost 0.2 Å, suggesting that crystal
packing effects may be significant. In the X-ray crystal structure, the trigonal twist angle is a’=+23o,
roughly similar to that in the gas phase (a’=+35o).
 While the D3 symmetry of 1·F- is quite unique as an anion receptor, a number of metal cation
trischelate complexes ML3 have this symmetry when the ligands ‘L’ are bidentate.84 In that case, the
ligands are free to assemble about the central metal cation driven by ligand field effects, and they can
adjust their positions to maximize binding. By contrast, the amidocryptand 1 binds to fluoride anion
through three bidentate isophthalamide ligands incorporated into the walls of the molecular cage,
with only electrostatic forces involved. Nevertheless, the symmetry of the cage’s chemical structure
is reflected in its stereochemical preference for D3 symmetry.
 In contrast, the assigned structures for both 1·Cl- and 1·Br- possess C3h symmetry, retaining 3-
fold symmetry about the N(t)···X-···N(t) axis while tilting the aromatic rings nearly tangential to the
spherical halide ion, with CH···X- angles of 102±2o (Figure 6b,c, side view). As a result when the
structures for 1·Cl- and 1·Br- are viewed down the tertiary amine axis (top view), they look strikingly
different from that of 1·F-.
 The trigonal twist angle in both 1·Cl- and 1·Br- is a=0o, with the six amide nitrogens forming a
trigonal bipyramid, as the triangular overlays in Figure 5.6 show. Furthermore, the plane of each
isophthaloyl group is now configured nearly parallel to the C3 axis. This extends the distance

 50

between the bridgehead nitrogens, whose calculated N(t)···N(t) distance expands from 7.98 Å in
1·F- to 8.83 Å in 1·Cl- and 1·Br- (Table 5.1). The amide NH groups still form reasonably strong,
nearly identical NH···X- H-bonds with N(H)···X- distances that are about 0.50 Å longer than in 1·F-
, an amount very close to the difference in six-coordinate ionic radii between F-(VI) and Cl-(VI)
(DR=0.48 Å). The amide groups adjust their binding to X- by twisting out of plane of the phenyl
ring by 28-30o.
 The primary reason for the structural distortion from D3 to C3h symmetry is that the larger sizes
of the halide ions Cl- and Br- destabilize geometries in which the aromatic rings form additional
CH···X- H-bonds, dropping the formal coordination number from nine to six. This is in apparent
contradiction to the norm that larger ion size should lead to larger coordination number, not
smaller. This unusual circumstance is a consequence of the constrained geometry of the cage, which
must distort to accommodate changes in the size of the ion that is imbedded inside it.
 Given the change in structural preference with halide ion size, we wondered whether there were
D3, C3h, and C3 symmetry minima on the conformational potential energy surface for all three 1·X-
complexes, and what their relative energies would be. This analysis, detailed in supplementary
material, shows that there is no stable D3 minimum on the 1·Cl- and 1·Br- potential energy surfaces.
Furthermore, when F- was substituted for Cl- in its C3h geometry, a C3 minimum was found that was
more than 60 kJ/mol less stable than the D3 minimum, because its structure is far too large to bind
well to F-, and shifts the F- away from the center of the cage.
Indeed, for none of the other low-energy minima for 1·F- does the F- ion lie at the center of the
cage, with all six NH···F- H-bonds of similar strength. Thus, based on our calculations, it appears
that, in 1·F-, only the D3 cage structure, with its nine-coordinate binding, can accommodate the
fluoride ion at the center of the cage.
 The complete energy level diagram (Figure S5.5) for the 1·X- cages shows a striking
dependence on the number of low-energy conformations with halide ion size. The 1·F- and 1·Br-
complexes both have a strong preference for a single structure. In 1·F-, the D3 symmetry structure is
more than 20 kJ/mol more stable than all other structures, while in 3·Br-, there are three other
structures between 12 and 17 kJ/mol, but no others below 40 kJ/mol of the global minimum. By
contrast, in 1·Cl-, there are three other structures with energies within 10 kJ/mol of the C3h global
minimum, and more than 17 structures within 20 kJ/mol. We deduce on this basis that the
amidocryptand cage 1 binds chloride ion well from a variety of cage structures. By contrast, strong
binding to both fluoride ion and bromide ion is much more structurally restrictive.

 5.4.2 Spectral signatures of the D3 to C3h structural change
 Given the structural change from 9-coordinate binding in the D3 symmetry cage of 1·F- to 6-
coordinate binding in the C3h symmetry cages of 1·Cl- and 1·Br-, it is worth returning to the spectra
of Figure 5.4 to consider how this structural change is reflected in the spectra.
 First, the intensity of the aromatic CH stretch transitions at 3070 cm-1 in the spectrum of 1·F-
(D3) is significantly enhanced, while the corresponding transitions in the other two complexes are so
weak as to be barely discernible. The DFT vibrational frequency calculations confirm that it is the
three closest CH groups on the phenyl rings that are responsible for the large IR intensity when the
aromatic ring is oriented to point these CH bonds directly at the anion, serving as a marker for the
presence of phenyl CH···F- interactions. In 1·Cl- and 1·Br-, the C3h symmetry structure is achieved by
rotating these aromatic CH groups nearly perpendicular to the halide ion, removing this intensity
enhancement.
 Second, we might have anticipated that the center frequencies of the NH stretch bands would
(i) shift significantly with changing halide ion due to changes in the strength of the NH···X- H-

 51

bonds, and (ii) would shift monotonically to lower frequency with increasing strength of binding (𝜈
NH(1·F ) < 𝜈NH(1·Cl ) < 𝜈NH(1·Br )). However, only small shifts are observed down the series, and
 - - -

the shifts that do occur are not monotonic down the halide ion series. Instead, 𝜈NH(1·Br-)=3309 cm-1
< 𝜈NH(1·F-)=3329 cm-1 < 𝜈NH(1·Cl-)=3348 cm-1. We ascribe this unusual pattern to the D3 to C3h
structural change that occurs between 1·F- and 1·Cl-, so that not only the halide ion but also the
molecular cavity changes down the series.
 Finally, the spectrum of 1·F-(D3) differs from those of the two C3h structures of 1·Cl- and 1·Br- in
that the H-bonded NH stretch band in the 1·F-(D3) spectrum is much broader (Δ𝜈 ~50 cm-1) than it
is in the other two (Δ𝜈 =16-18 cm-1). Since our spectra are recorded on the isolated complex cooled
to Tvib ~10 K, these differences in breadth are inherent to the complex in this single conformation.
The additional breadth in 1·F-(D3) is likely due in part to stronger coupling of the NH(v=1) levels
between each other and with background vibrational states, both of which are reduced in the C3h
structures of 1·Cl- and 1·Br-. The sensitivity of the NH stretch transitions to small changes in
geometry (Figure S5.6) suggests that even zero-point vibrational motion may contribute to the
breadth of these transitions in the experimental spectrum.

 5.4.3 Energetic Contributions to the Binding
 Table 5.2 summarizes the results of an energy decomposition analysis of the contributions to
the total binding energy from electrostatics, Pauli exclusion, dispersion, polarization, and charge
transfer.85 Several deductions can be made from this analysis. First, as anticipated, the total
interaction energies decrease with increasing size of the halide ion inside the cage, with 1·F-
significantly stronger than 1·Cl- and 1·Br-. Second, amongst the low-energy structures of 1·F-, the D3
structure has a net interaction energy more than 30 kJ/mol larger than all others, due to its nine-
coordinate binding. Third, the contribution from polarization is much larger in 1·F- (D3) than in any
of the other structures. In comparing these terms in the 1·F-(D3) to the C3h minima of 1·Cl- and
1·Br-, we see that 1·F-(D3) has a contribution from polarization almost twice (DEpol = -204 kJ/mol)
that of either 1·Cl-(C3h) or 1·Br-(C3h) (DEpol≈-110 kJ/mol). This is a likely consequence of the
orientations of the phenyl rings with their polarizable p clouds. Fourth, while the 1·Cl-(C3h) and 1·Br-
(C3h) terms are generally similar to one another, the Pauli exclusion term in 1·Br-(C3h) is significantly
larger (DEPauli = +497 kJ/mol) than in 1·Cl-(C3h), (DEPauli = +409 kJ/mol). This large destabilizing
term compensates for the slightly larger attractive terms present in 1·Br-(C3h) and is the most
obvious consequence of fitting the larger Br- ion inside the C3h cage. Finally, we have added to these
energy contributions a measure of the strain that the cage itself experiences in maximizing its
interaction with the halide ions. To that end, we calculated the fragment energy of cage 1 in ALMO-
EDA calculations at the optimized geometry for each 1·X-structure in Table 2 with the ion
removed. Clearly, the 9-coordinate binding in 1·F-(D3) comes with some strain in the cage (DEstrain =
+28.0 kJ/mol) that is relieved in the 1·F-(C3) structure. The corresponding , 1·Cl-(C3h) and 1·Br-(C3h)
structures are some 20-30 kJ/mol more stable than 1·F-(D3) in the absence of their anions.

Overall, favorable binding energies were observed in all cases. For the phenyl cages, binding energies

the coordination of the cryptand cages limits anion-CO2 interactions and cryptand-CO2 interactions
dominate the contribution to the predicted CO2 binding energies. Larger halides might present an
opportunity to provide similar cryptand-anion coordination, while increasing the accessible volume
in the. However, a sufficiently large (or small) anion size would require a shift in the cage geometry
from the pseudo-trifold to a more expanded geometry as discussed in the main text. This could lead
to a decrease in anion binding and an increase in cryptand cage heterogeneity. Therefore, when
considering cryptand cage complexes that protect the anion from interacting with gases such as CO2,
direct interactions with the cryptand cage dominate and can be further modified to modulate the
overall binding affinity of the cryptand to the gas molecule.

coordinates tightly around the anion and the slight increase in CO2 binding energies and is facilitated
through direct interactions with the cryptand cage and not with the anion. The inclusion of the
pyridine moieties within the cryptand cage directs more electron density towards the interior of the
cage. This increase in electron density destabilizes the anion slightly, leading to larger interior cavities

 5.5 Conclusions
 The amidocryptands are versatile anion receptors, sequestering a range of atomic and
polyatomic ions inside their three-dimensional, bimacrocyclic cages.41 Much of the structural insight
into this binding comes from X-ray crystal structures, which have exquisite detail, but rely on the
growth of suitable crystals. Even once formed, the cages themselves take up structures that
accommodate the anion but are also affected by the presence of counter-ions and solvent molecules.

 57

By studying the isolated anion-cage complex in the gas phase, we are afforded a complementary look
at the ion-cage complex in the absence of such effects, making possible a direct and rigorous
comparison with calculations. Mass selection assures the purity of the sample, while cryo-cooling
enables infrared spectra out of the zero-point vibrational level of the gas phase complex.
 We observe a single structure for the isolated 1·X- cages whose infrared spectra can be uniquely
assigned to a D3 symmetry structure for 1·F- and a C3h structure for both 1·Cl- and 1·Br-. 1·F-(D3) is
a twisted, bicapped trigonal prism with a trigonal twist angle of a = +35o, similar to the X-ray
structure found for 1·F- in crystalline form.63 The cage binds to fluoride ion by a nine-coordinate
binding with all six amide NH groups as principal binding sites and three aromatic CH···F-
interactions playing a secondary role. Interestingly, despite retaining this same 9-coordinate binding,
the amidocryptand cage in the solid has bridgehead-to-bridgehead tertiary amine distance and radial
distances that are significantly shorter than in the gas phase (Table 1), pointing to significant crystal
packing effects. In DMSO-d6 solution, there is NMR evidence that the same 9-coordinate binding is
also present in 1·F-, with an association constant of K=3.3X104 M-1.59
 The structural data we have obtained for 1·Cl- and 1·Br- is the only structural data on the
binding of 1 in its neutral form to these anions. Furthermore, the association constants for 1 with
Cl- (K=820 M-1) and Br- (K<5 M-1) are much smaller than those of F-, making it ambiguous whether
association in DMSO solution even involves these halide ions sequestered inside the cage. In the
absence of crystal structures, the present data on 1·Cl- and 1·Br- are especially helpful to our
understanding of binding inside these cages. Our results prove that both Cl- and Br- fit inside the
same C3h cage in the gas phase, with calculated binding energies that are quite similar. Thus, the
inherent size of the C3h binding pocket is adequate to accommodate both Cl- and Br- ions inside,
pointing the way for future studies in solution that determine the role played by cage deformation
and solvent effects on binding in solution.
 The C3h symmetry of the 1·Cl- and 1·Br- cages enables six-coordinate binding of the amide NH
groups, but re-orients the aromatic rings so that their planes are nearly tangential to the radius from
the central halide ion. The trigonal twist angle is now a=0o, so that the binding pocket is properly
termed a bicapped trigonal prism. In forming the trigonal prism binding pocket, the distance to the
bridgehead N’s is increased by more than 1 Å from that in the D3 symmetry counterpart, another
consequence of the larger size of the chloride and bromide anions.
In addition to providing predictions of the infrared spectra that led to assignments of these
structures, calculations at the r2-SCAN-3c level of theory have provided an overview of the
conformational landscapes of the 1·X- series at greatly reduced computational effort. These show
that 1·F- and 1·Br- are conformationally highly restricted, with no rivals to the D3 and C3h minima
within 22 and 12 kJ/mol in energy. By contrast, 1·Cl- has multiple C3 and C2-symmetry structures
that are within 10 kJ/mol of the C3h global minimum, suggesting an entropic contribution to the
association constants that favor the 1·Cl- complexes at ambient temperatures.59 Based on this
foundation of gas-phase data on the 1·X- series, it should be possible to study the inherent structural
preferences of a much wider range of ion-molecular cage complexes, expanding the number and
types of structures where experimental structural determinations are available.

6. STABILIZING A HIGHLY REACTIVE INTERMEDIATE INSIDE A
 MOLECULAR CAGE: DEPROTONATED AMIDOCRYPTAND

 6.1 Abstract
We report the infrared spectroscopy and structural assignment of the deprotonated form of a
bicyclic amide-based cryptand cage under cryo-cooled conditions in the gas phase. Electrospray
ionization of the amidocryptand cage (AmCryptand) in the presence of fluoride yields the
deprotonated anion [AmCrypt–H]− at m/z 681.43 as the dominant ion, formed by proton
transfer from an amide NH group to the encapsulated halide and subsequent loss of HF. Working
in the gas phase removes the solvent, counterion, and packing effects that shape solution and
crystalline measurements, so the spectra report directly on how the cage accommodates a negative
charge carried by its own framework. We record IR–UV double resonance spectra of the cryo-
cooled ions in the hydride stretch region. Sharp, well-resolved alkyl CH stretches appear, while the
amide NH stretches give rise to a broad, congested absorption spanning 3200 to 3400 cm¹
together with a sharp feature at its high-frequency edge. Following a conformational search of the
deprotonated cage, we compare the experimental spectrum with first-principles predictions and
with a reduced-dimensional local mode model that couples the NH and CH stretch fundamentals
to low-frequency framework motions of the cage. We assign the spectrum to a structure in which
the carbonyl adjacent to the deprotonated amide rotates inward, its oxygen accepting H-bonds
from the five remaining NH groups within the cavity.

 6.2 Introduction
Molecular recognition in supramolecular chemistry relies on the ability of a host framework to
organize noncovalent interactions around a guest. Cryptands, synthetic, three-dimensional cage-like
molecules, are among the most important examples of this principle because their three-dimensional
cavities can be designed to encapsulate ions and small molecules with high selectivity.43, 44, 98 Early
cryptand chemistry focused largely on cation binding, where preorganized heteroatom donors define
the size, shape, and electrostatic environment of the binding pocket. This design strategy has since
been extended to anion receptors, in which hydrogen-bond donors, electropositive C–H groups, and
other directional interactions are arranged to stabilize negatively charged guests inside molecular
cages. The flexibility of the host framework and its ability to reorganize cooperatively around charge
often plays a central role in determining the final structure.
Amidocryptands provide a useful platform for examining this interplay between preorganization,
flexibility, and charge stabilization. In these receptors, amide N–H groups act as hydrogen-bond
donors, while carbonyl groups provide polar functionality that can influence both the conformation
and electrostatic character of the cage.61 The amidocryptand studied here contains three
isophthalamide linkers arranged within a bicyclic framework, producing a cavity lined with multiple
N–H and C=O groups. Previous studies of halide-bound amidocryptands have shown that these
cages can reorganize substantially to accommodate anions of different size. In the gas phase,
cryogenic infrared spectra of the fluoride-, chloride-, and bromide-bound complexes revealed highly
structured binding motifs in which the amide N–H groups form an organized hydrogen-bonding
network around the encapsulated halide ion.
Deprotonation introduces a different type of perturbation. In halide-bound complexes, the negative
charge is carried by an external guest located within the cavity. In a deprotonated amidocryptand,

 78

the charge is generated directly on the host framework. Removal of an amide proton therefore
changes both the local electronic structure of one amide unit and the broader hydrogen-bonding
environment of the cage. The resulting amide anion can be stabilized through resonance
delocalization between the deprotonated nitrogen and the adjacent carbonyl group, and the cage
may respond structurally by reorienting carbonyl and N–H groups to form new intramolecular
hydrogen bonds. Deprotonation may therefore act as a trigger for reorganization of the entire cage.
Recent gas-phase work from Han et al.99 on deprotonated isophthalamide and benzamide establishes
a molecular-scale precedent for this behavior. In their cryogenic vibrational spectroscopic study of
deprotonated benzamide and isophthalamide complexes with CO2, the deprotonated
isophthalamide system exhibited a cooperative binding motif in which an intact amide N–H group
forms an intramolecular N–H⋯O hydrogen bond to the carbamate oxygen.99 Even relatively small
deprotonated amide frameworks can stabilize anionic charge through cooperative hydrogen bonding
with nearby amide groups. Cryogenic infrared spectroscopy combined with electronic-structure
calculations can identify these motifs by reducing thermal congestion and enabling direct
comparison with calculated structures.
The amidocryptand investigated here extends the question to a larger, three-dimensional receptor in
which several amide and carbonyl groups can reorganize around a deprotonated site, and that added
flexibility raises the demands on the vibrational modeling. Scaled harmonic calculations are often
insufficient for flexible hydrogen-bonded ions, because high-frequency X–H stretches can couple to
bends, combination bands, and low-frequency framework motions. Local-mode and reduced-
dimensional anharmonic models capture these effects while retaining a chemically transparent
description of individual N–H and C–H oscillators, linking spectral signatures to specific hydrogen-
bonding motifs in large supramolecular ions.
The structure of a deprotonated amidocryptand anion has not been established under such isolated
conditions. Although amide N–H groups are not typically acidic, [AmCrypt–H]― is formed readily
by electrospray ionization in the presence of fluoride, motivating our interest in this unusual anion.
A cryogenic gas-phase measurement therefore provides a direct route to determining how the cage
responds when negative charge is generated within its own framework and how this response differs
from its organization around an encapsulated guest.
Here, we report the cryogenic gas-phase infrared spectrum and structural assignment of the
deprotonated amidocryptand anion, [AmCrypt–H]― or [1-H]-. The ion is generated by electrospray
ionization and interrogated using IR–UV double-resonance spectroscopy. Anharmonic local-mode
modeling of a computed conformer ensemble assigns the spectrum. Comparison with the previously
characterized halide-bound amidocryptands allows us to distinguish a cage-centered deprotonation
motif from the guest-centered hydrogen-bonding motifs observed for encapsulated halides. Proton
loss is accompanied by substantial structural reorganization: the deprotonated amide site is stabilized
by resonance delocalization, the adjacent carbonyl group flips inward to serve as an internal
hydrogen-bond acceptor, and the surrounding amides adopt a distribution of hydrogen-bonding
environments that produces a distinct vibrational signature.

 6.3 Experimental and Computational Methods
The experimental work was carried out in an apparatus that has been described previously.25, 64The
phenyl amidocryptand cage 1 was synthesized as described previously.62 The deprotonated AmCrypt
cage was prepared by dissolving 1 at a concentration of 100 𝜇M in a 50:50 H2O:CH3OH mixture.
NaF salt was dissolved separately and added to the mixture at eight times the concentration of 1.

 79

Under typical nano-electrospray conditions (tip voltage ∼ 1000 V, electrospray current ∼ 10 nA,
drying tube temperature 120 ∘C), the deprotonated amidocryptand cage, [M ― H]―, with a mass of
681.4 Da, dominated the anion mass spectrum.
Candidate structures of the deprotonated amidocryptand were generated using the CREST 3.0
conformer-search package,67 with conformer energies evaluated using the GFN2-xTB semiempirical
method. Searches were performed using the non-covalent interaction option in CREST, and
structures lying within 40 kJ mol―1 of the lowest-energy conformer were retained for further
refinement. The resulting conformers were subsequently optimized, and harmonic vibrational
frequency calculations were performed, using the computationally efficient r2SCAN-3c composite
method developed by Grimme and co-workers.68
To assess the dependence of the predicted structures and spectra on the electronic structure
method, a representative subset of conformers spanning the relative energy range from about 0 to
17 kcal mol―1 was selected for higher-level calculations. These structures were reoptimized using
both B3LYP-D3BJ/6-311++G(d,p)69-71and 𝜔B97X-D3/6-311++G(d,p)100 levels of theory. CM5
partial atomic charges101were obtained from Gaussian 16 calculations, using Hirshfeld population
analysis as the underlying charge-partitioning scheme, to characterize the distribution of charge
within the receptor and assess how amide deprotonation alters the electrostatic environment of the
cage. All geometry optimizations, frequency calculations, and spectral simulations were otherwise
carried out with the ORCA version 5.0 quantum chemistry package.76
Conformational searches, optimization procedures and the model local mode Hamiltonian used in
predicting conformation-specific infrared spectra have also been described in previous chapters
and in the halide-ion amidocryptand paper.42

populated by reorganized structures in which the carbonyl adjacent to the deprotonated amide
rotates inward, setting the expectation for the structural assignment that follows.

The cryogenic IR spectrum of the deprotonated amidocryptand in the hydride stretch region is

absorption arises from the more strongly hydrogen-bonded N–H groups. These bands map onto the
graded set of N–H environments in the assigned structure: several amides remain only weakly

The calculated spectra of the two low-energy conformers are nearly identical. The predicted alkyl C–

features. The hydrogen-bonded N–H transitions are distributed across 3240–3400 cm―1, spanning
the width of the experimentally broadened contour. For each conformer, the calculated spectrum
contains two free or nearly free N–H stretching fundamentals separated by approximately 4 cm―1.
We therefore propose that both conformers contribute to the experimental spectrum, giving rise to
the observed set of four transitions in the free N–H stretching region.
Scaled harmonic frequencies alone are inadequate for this assignment. The hydride stretch region of
a flexible anion is sensitive to anharmonic coupling, to local hydrogen-bond strength, and to
coupling with low-frequency cage motions, and the harmonic picture treats each N–H oscillator
independently. Local-mode modeling was therefore used to support the assignment, providing a
reduced-dimensional description of the high-frequency N–H and C–H stretches while retaining the
influence of the soft framework modes that redistribute intensity and broaden the spectral features.
The broad absorption near 3266 cm―1 makes the need for this treatment clear. Its width and
asymmetry reflect coupling between localized N–H stretching motion and the rocking, twisting, and
breathing distortions of the cage, all of which contribute to the internal hydrogen-bonding network.
Within the model, cubic couplings mix the bright N–H fundamentals with stretch–low-frequency
combination bands, spreading a single local-mode oscillator strength over a range of frequencies.
The observed contour therefore reports on the softness of the reorganized framework as well as on
the static hydrogen-bond geometry, and the same treatment accounts for the comparatively broad,
less symmetric C–H stretch features discussed below.

 6.4.5 Comparison with Halide-bound Amidocryptand Infrared Spectra

The spectrum of the deprotonated amidocryptand is compared with those of the fluoride-, chloride-,
and bromide-bound AmCrypt complexes in Figure 6.6. The halide-bound spectra show relatively
sharp and consolidated N–H stretching features, reflecting hydrogen bonding from the amide N–H
groups to a centrally bound anion. In those complexes, the halide ion acts as an external template
that organizes the N–H donors into a more symmetric binding environment, and the local-mode
treatment correspondingly recovers a narrow cluster of similar N–H oscillators coupled to a
comparatively rigid cage.

The deprotonated cage produces a different pattern. A distinct high-frequency N–H feature appears
near 3450 cm―1, and the broad absorption near 3300 cm―1 indicates a wide distribution of
hydrogen-bond strengths, which is incompatible with a halide-like motif in which all amide N–H
groups are similarly engaged with a central guest. Applying the same local-mode framework to the
two cases separates the origins of these contours. The halide complexes require only weak coupling
to framework modes to reproduce their sharp features, whereas the deprotonated anion requires
substantial cubic coupling between the N–H fundamentals and low-frequency cage motions. The
added breadth is a direct consequence of a lower-symmetry, internally hydrogen-bonded structure
built on a more compliant framework.
The C–H stretching region also distinguishes the deprotonated amidocryptand from the halide-
bound complexes. In the deprotonated spectrum, the alkyl C–H stretching intensity extends across
approximately 2800–3000 cm―1, with additional aromatic C–H features near 3100 cm―1. Compared
with the more sharply resolved and consolidated C–H features of the halide-bound complexes,
particularly the fluoride complex, the deprotonated spectrum contains broader features with less
clearly defined maxima. These differences indicate that the C–H oscillators experience a wider range
of local environments in the deprotonated structure than in the more symmetric halide-bound
complexes.

 6.5 Conclusions

Cryogenic IR spectroscopy, conformer searching, and local-mode vibrational modeling together
establish the first structural characterization of the isolated deprotonated amidocryptand anion. Loss
of an amide proton generates an anionic site that is stabilized by resonance delocalization across the
amidate N–C–O unit, and the cage answers structurally. The neighboring carbonyl flips inward to
act as a hydrogen-bond acceptor within the cavity, and the remaining amide N–H groups settle into
a nonuniform internal hydrogen-bonding network. The conformer ensemble confirms that this
response is thermodynamically driven, with relative energies tracking the extent of the internal N–H
⋯O network rather than the strength of any single contact. The hydride stretch spectrum registers
the outcome as sharp weakly bound N–H features near 3450–3475 cm―1 alongside a broad,
framework-coupled absorption near 3266 cm―1, a fingerprint clearly separable from the
consolidated bands of the halide-bound complexes.
More broadly, these results characterize deprotonation as a cooperative supramolecular event that
reshapes the hydrogen-bonding scaffold, reorients a carbonyl, redistributes charge through
resonance, and leaves a distinct vibrational signature. The internal N–H⋯O motif identified here
closely parallels the cooperative stabilization observed in deprotonated isophthalamide ⋅ CO2,(Han
et al. 2025) where an intact amide N–H anchors the carbamate oxygen. Extending these cryogenic
measurements to [AmCrypt–H]― ⋅ CO2 and to related fluoride-derived adducts is a natural next
step, and the local-mode framework applied here provides the vibrational diagnostics needed to
assign such complexes.

7. CAPTURING THE CARBAMATE ANION INSIDE A MIXED
 AMINE/AMIDE MACROCYCLIC CAGE

 7.1 Abstract
We report the IR spectroscopy and structural analysis of the carbamate anion formed by CO2
addition to the mixed diamine/amide macrocycle (1) under cryo-cooled conditions in the gas phase.
Macrocycle 1 has two secondary amine sites at which carbamate formation could occur, separated by
ethyl groups that link two isophthaloyl rings, forming a symmetric, 24-membered macrocycle. We
seek to understand how the macrocycle structure accommodates the presence of the carbamate
anion at one of the amine sites in the absence of solvent, counterions, or packing effects. We record
infrared spectra of the cryo-cooled gas phase ions in the hydride stretch and fingerprint regions
using IR-UV double resonance methods. The IR spectrum of 1·CO2- in the NH stretch region is
composed of a set of five NH stretch fundamentals due to the secondary amine NH and four amide
NH groups, all of which are involved in H-bonding to the carbamate anion. Their absorptions are
spread over the 3100-3350 cm-1 region and are surprisingly broad. Transitions in the mid-IR are
much sharper and are diagnostic of the unique structure taken up by the carbamate and amide
groups. Based on an exhaustive search of conformation space, screened by efficient calculations at
the r2-SCAN-3c level of theory, we identify a set of three nearly isoenergetic structures that share the
same compact, folded architecture, differing in ethyl group dihedrals. The two aromatic rings are π-
stacked, with the CO2- group perpendicular to these planes at the base of a U-shaped trough. The
four amide NH groups are thereby positioned to H-bond to the CO2- anion, while the opposite
secondary amine NH forms a fifth H-bond. We compare this structure with the corresponding X-
ray crystal structure of its zwitterion form, and of a close analog in which one of the amine sites is
methylated to form a tertiary amine.

 7.2 Introduction
Most current schemes for CO2 capture from air or flue gas involve the use of amines as capture
agents, which selectively react with CO2 to form carbamates, R1R2-NH-CO2-.102, 103 This chemistry
occurs readily for primary or secondary amines, because formation of the carbamate anion requires
deprotonation of the amine. The proton that is released is taken up by other basic sites, typically by
other amine molecules in the solution. If capture is to be followed by release of CO2 in purified
form, one must reverse the reaction, an endothermic process.103, 104 Much effort has gone into fine-
tuning the binding energy of the carbamate to decrease the energy requirements for CO2 release
without compromising the selectivity associated with carbamate formation.

One such strategy is to incorporate two amine groups in the same molecule, where proton release
and uptake are joined in a single molecule. In earlier work, the Bowman-James group found that a
mixed amine/amide macrocycle that incorporates one secondary and one tertiary amine (Figure
7.1a, 1Me) was efficient at capturing CO2 from air to form a carbamate.13 The secondary amine site
served as the position of carbamate formation, while the tertiary amine accepted the proton to form
a zwitterion. Upon crystallization, the macrocycle structure incorporated a CO2-··· +HNR2 salt
bridge in a folded, π-stacked macrocycle framework.

As a part of this project, we returned to this interesting result by studying the structures and kinetics
of CO2 release from the unmethylated analog of the earlier macrocycle (Figure 7.1b), which thereby
possesses two secondary amines, both of which can act as sites for carbamate formation. They

 91

compared this macrocycle, whose functional groups are linked by ethyl spacers (thereby called ‘E-
mac’) to its larger, more flexible counterpart with propyl spacers (‘P-mac’), finding significant
differences in their modes of binding CO2 and the kinetics of their release.

In the present work, we study the spectroscopy and structural preferences of the anion form of the
E-mac carbamate macrocycle (Figure 7.1c), [1-H+CO2]- or 1·CO2- in shortened form, under cryo-
cooled conditions in the gas phase. Under these conditions, we probe the inherent structural
preferences, unperturbed by solvent, packing effects, or counter-ions.42 This facilitates a direct
comparison with calculations, enabling a full exploration of the conformational landscape of this
ion. Our structural tool is infrared spectroscopy, where structures are inferred by comparison with
calculation, lacking the full atomic detail of an X-ray structure, but gaining unperturbed infrared
spectra out of the vibrational zero-point level(s) of the low-lying conformations of the ion.

While the main focus of our work is on the carbamate anion, we also recorded the infrared spectrum
of the deprotonated form of 1, [1-H]-. This deprotonated form of the macrocycle is formally the
precursor to carbamate formation, which necessarily involved deprotonation of the amine site:

 7.4.1 Macrocycle carbamate 1·CO2-
Macrocycle 1 incorporates two isophthaloyl groups into its structure in symmetric positions,
separated from the two secondary amines by ethyl groups. The isophthaloyl groups serve as UV

spectra. Based on our previous work on the amidocryptand cages, which also contain isophthaloyl
groups, we took survey UV photofragment scans over the 34,400-37,000 cm-1 region (270-290 nm),
observing a broad, nearly featureless UV absorption that rises with excitation energy.

shown in Figure 7.2b. Both these gain spectra were recorded with the UV laser fixed at 36,800 cm-1.
Note that, due to the lack of sharp UV transitions due to a single conformer of the macrocycle, the
gain spectra we record are not conformation-specific but arise from shifts in the UV spectrum
induced by IR absorption. We interpret them as a weighted sum of spectra due to conformers with
significant population trapped in them during the collisional cooling in the cold trap.

In order to make structural assignments, we compare the experimental IR spectra with those
predicted by the low-energy conformers from our conformational search. To that end, we turn our
attention to the results of that search. Figure 7.3 presents an energy level diagram of the macrocycle
conformations found within 20 kJ/mol of the global minimum at the r2-SCAN-3c level of theory.

The eleven structures found within this energy window fall into two types, which we label as U-
shaped or Z-shaped. The more compact, U-shaped structures have the two aromatic rings π-stacked
with the CO2- forming the base of the ‘U’, while the Z-shaped structures are unfolded, with the two
aromatic rings on parallel, displaced planes bridged by the CO2- step. The lowest energy Z-shaped
structure K is nearly 10 kJ/mol above the global minimum U-shaped structure A. The macrocycle is
well-suited to stabilize the carbamate anion, as all eleven structures utilize the full set of four amide
NH groups to H-bond to the CO2- group. The lowest energy structures also maintain a strong amine
NH H-bond, forming a total of five H-bonds.

As a part of our survey of possible structures, we also carried out a conformational search of the
structural isomer in which the carbamate was formed at the amide rather than the amine sites. The
lowest energy of these structures is over 60 kJ/mol higher in energy than the global minimum,
structure A. This is a striking result, because the amine H in a secondary amine is much less acidic
(pKa~35-40) than the amide NH group (pKa~15-18).

 7.5 Discussion and Conclusions

 7.5.1 Comparing the gas-phase and X-ray crystal structures of 1·CO2-
Figure 7.10 compares the global minimum structure A of 1·CO2- to the X-ray crystal structure of
the N-methyl derivative of 1 from Wang et al.13 The two structures are nearly identical to one
another; this, despite the fact that the crystal is zwitterionic, while our gas-phase structure is an
anion. In the crystal, the tertiary amine formed by methylation accepts the proton removed from the
other amine, to form a CO2-···+HNR2 salt bridge. The gas phase anion has a neutral secondary
amine that forms an intramolecular H-bond of type CO2-···HNR2. Nevertheless, the secondary
amine orients its NH group towards the CO2-, much as the protonated amine does in the crystal, in
part because this points the N lone pair away from the anionic site.

In Chapter 3, we described the X-ray crystal structure of the single and double carbamate forms of 1
(E-mac), which were grown under different conditions. Interestingly, both the single carbamate and
double carbamate X-ray crystal structures incorporate Z-shaped structures for the carbamate
macrocycle, rather than the U-shaped structures that are more stable in the gas phase.

In contrast, the N-methyl derivative of 1 crystallizes into a U-shaped structure that corresponds
closely to the gas phase structure A assigned here. This is evidence for the robust nature of this
structure, which brings the secondary amine NH group(s) into close proximity with the CO2- group
of the carbamate. In the carbamate anion, this is a relatively minor part of the stabilization, while in
the X-ray crystal structure, the NH2+ forms a zwitterion which adds significantly to the stabilization
provided by the four amide NH H-bonds that are also formed to the CO2- group.

 100

In the double carbamate structure, 1.(2CO2-), carbamate formation occurs at both of the secondary
amine sites. One carbamate group is pointing interior to the macrocycle where it is stabilized by H-
bonds from all four amide NH groups to CO2-. The second carbamate group has points outside the
macrocycle cavity and has C-O/C=O bonds that indicate that it is a COOH group rather than CO2-.
The COOH group forms a H-bond with one of the amide C=O groups of the adjacent macrocycle.
In order to facilitate this intermolecular H-bonding, the macrocycle exists in a Z-shaped structure
very similar to structure K. Based on the kinetics experiments from chapter 3, this exterior group
releases CO2 more readily than the interior carbamate group.

8. BRIDGING HOMOGENEOUS AND HETEROGENEOUS CATALYSIS
 THROUGH IMMOBILIZING A RU-ACRIDINE COMPLEX FOR CO2
 HYDROGENATION

 8.1 Abstract
Hydrogenation of CO2 to formate enables efficient reversible hydrogen storage in a stable water-
soluble carrier while also generating a versatile chemical feedstock for downstream synthesis and
energy applications. Here we compare the performance of molecular Ru-9 catalyst with its
polymerized analog, Ru-9-Poly-TPM, for the hydrogenation of CO2 and carbonate/bicarbonate to
formate. These studies demonstrate that at equal Ru molar loading, Ru-9-Poly-TPM is significantly
more reactive than molecular Ru-9 in a basic aqueous media. These results show that
Ru-9-Poly-TPM is a highly efficient catalyst for CO2 hydrogenation to formate, delivering a high
turnover number (TON) of 2110 in 2 hours and a turnover frequency (TOF) of 1060 h−1 at 120 °C
and 70 bar under a 3:1 H2:CO2 mixture. Under these conditions, its activity is eight times higher than
that of the analogous molecular Ru-9 catalyst. Furthermore, Ru-9-Poly-TPM is an effective catalyst
for bicarbonate/carbonate hydrogenation to formate. In the hydrogenation of NaHCO3, Ru-9-
Poly-TPM achieves 88% conversion (TON 1570; TOF 787 h−1), whereas the homogeneous Ru-9
catalyst gives only a 2.6% conversion under the same conditions. In piperidine-mediated
CO2 hydrogenation, Ru-9-Poly-TPM also enables N-formylation of piperdine to form N-
formylpiperdine (89% yield; TON 2670). Recyclability tests for CO2 hydrogenation to formate show
an initial activity drop after the first cycle followed by stable performance in subsequent cycles.
Together, these results demonstrate that porous polymer immobilization can deliver a recoverable
Ru catalyst that enables high activity in water, exceeding that of the parent homogeneous complex at
matched metal loading.

 8.2 Introduction
The global economy depends on the scalable and cost-effective production of essential chemicals,
fuels, and materials, driving a critical need to energy- and atom-efficient processes. Hydrogen is
central to several industries, functioning as a reagent in refineries, ammonia generation105, and
petrochemical manufacturing106. To optimize industrial processes and develop more cost-effective,
high-value products, the activation of hydrogen and its transfer to small molecules are critical to
produce specialty chemicals and lightweight fuels. Of these potential products, formic acid/formate
formation from CO2/HCO3- hydrogenation has garnered significant attention for its potential as
hydrogen storage materials107 and utility in the chemical industries.108

The hydrogenation process of CO2/HCO3- to formic acid/formate has been extensively studied in
the literature and has demonstrated that it can be achieved through both homogeneous and
heterogeneous catalytic pathways.109-112 Research in homogeneous catalysis has demonstrated high
selectively and activity with various metals (e.g. Ru, Ir, Mn, Fe) for the conversion of CO2 to
formate.111 Most homogeneous catalysts in this field have demonstrated efficacy in non-aqueous
solutions.111 However, the growing demand for more efficient and scalable industrial processes has
prompted interest in both aqueous-based systems and heterogeneous catalysts, which offer
advantages such as simplified separation and catalyst recyclability. Top performing homogeneous
catalysts for aqueous based systems typically have Ru and Ir metal centers. Prior studies on such
homogeneous systems have demonstrated high activity and good selectivity in the direct

 102

hydrogenation of CO2 to formate, achieving high turnover frequencies under mild conditions.111, 113,
114
 In contrast, heterogeneous catalysts, such as those based on copper, palladium, and nickel, are
slightly less active in facilitating the conversion of CO₂ to formate/formic acid.109, 115, 116 However,
they offer significant advantages in ease of separation and reusability, making them attractive for
industrial applications. However, challenges remain in achieving high selectivity and activity,
particularly under aqueous conditions.117

PNP (Phosphorous-Nitrogen-Phosphorous) chelating ligands demonstrate outstanding performance
in hydrogenation catalysis in aqueous conditions.118 Notably, Ru-MACHO and its heterogenized
analogs effectively catalyze hydrogenation of NaHCO3119 and CO2120, 121 to produce formate under
mild conditions, while also facilitating the formation of formamide and methanol through amine-
assisted catalysis.122 Other PNP-based complexes containing an acridine backbone have proven
effective for the hydrogenation of CO2 to formate and formamide.123, 124 Therefore, to combine the
exceptional selectivity of high performance molecular based catalysts with the durability and
recyclability of heterogeneous systems, an Ru-acridine catalyst with an analogous structure to those
with known efficacy for CO2 hydrogenation123, Ru-9, was immobilized into a porous polymeric
framework to yield Ru-9-Poly-TPM. In aqueous media, polymer-immobilized Ru-9-Poly-TPM
exhibits markedly enhanced CO2/HCO3- hydrogenation activity relative to the homogeneous Ru-9
benchmark, delivering substantially higher turnover metrics. Ru-9-Poly-TPM also enables efficient
N-formylation of CO2 to formamides with high yield, highlighting the broader synthetic utility of the
immobilized platform. These experimental trends are further rationalized through modeling studies,
which provide mechanistic insight into how these catalysts activate CO2.

 8.3 Experimental and Computational Methods
The material used, detailed characterization methods, and catalyst preparation methods are given in
the Supporting Information.

8.3.1 General Procedure for Hydrogenation of CO2 / HCO3- / CO32- to formate
Catalytic hydrogenation reactions were assembled in an inert N2 glovebag. To screen reaction
conditions 3.34 µmol Ru (2.0 mg of Ru-9; 25.0 mg of Ru-9-Poly-TPM based on ICP-OES
measurements from Galbraith which determined the Ru wt% = 1.36) was used. In addition to the
catalyst, 6.00 mmol base, 3.0 mL deionized water (2.0 mL for NEt3) and a stir bar were added to the
reaction vessel. Catalytic solutions were sealed in a stainless steel 100 mL Parr autoclave with a
magnetic stir bar and attached to a Parr reactor system, in which the reaction mixture was charged to
30-70 bar 3:1 H2 to CO2 and then subsequently heated to 22-120 °C for 2 hours (Scheme 2, Tables
1-3). The pressure and temperature were monitored throughout.

After cooling to room temperature, DMF (155 μL, 2.00 mmol) was added directly to the solution as
an internal standard along with 1.0 ml of D2O. This was mixed and then 70.0 µL of the solution was
removed. 1.0 mL of D2O was added to the aliquot and the resulting solution was filtered through a
0.2 μm filter. This was submitted for NMR analysis.

8.3.2 General Procedure for CO2 Hydrogenation to formate cycling studies
For cycling studies, catalytic hydrogenation reactions were assembled in an inert N2 glovebag. The
reaction scale was increased, therefore, 5.00 µmol Ru (37.5 mg of Ru-9-Poly-TPM based on ICP-

 103

OES measurements from Galbraith which determined the Ru wt% = 1.36) was used. In addition to
the catalyst, 9.00 mmol (953.9 mg) Na3PO4, 4.5 mL deionized water, and a stir bar were added to the
reaction vessel. Catalytic solutions were sealed in a stainless steel 100 mL Parr autoclave with a
magnetic stir bar and attached to a Parr reactor system, in which the reaction mixture was charged to
70 bar 3:1 H2 to CO2 and then subsequently heated to 120 °C for 2 hours (Figure 8.2). The pressure
and temperature were monitored throughout.

After cooling to room temperature, DMF (155 μL, 2.00 mmol) was added directly to the solution
along with 1.0 ml of D2O. The solution was filtered to recover the catalyst. 70.0 µL of the filtrate
was removed, charged with 1.0 mL of D2O and the resulting solution was filtered through a 0.2 μm
filter. This was submitted for NMR analysis. The rest of the filtrate was submitted for ICP-OES
analysis at Galbraith Instruments. Subsequently, the catalyst was washed with water (5 x 2 mL) and
methanol (5 x 2 mL) and then dried under vacuum and subsequently brought into an N2 glovebox.
The catalyst was then reused for the next cycle, this process for up to 5 cycles.

8.3.3 General Procedure for N-formylation of CO2 to N-formylpiperidine
For the catalytic N-formylation of CO2 to N-Formylpiperidine a high pressure parr vessel was
assembled in an inert N2 glovebag. 3.34 µmol Ru (25.0 mg of Ru-9-Poly-TPM based on ICP-OES
measurements from Galbraith which determined the Ru wt% = 1.36) was used. In addition to the
catalyst, 10 mmol (987 µL) of piperdine and a stir bar were added to the reaction vessel. Catalytic
solutions were sealed in a stainless steel 100 mL Parr autoclave with a magnetic stir bar and attached
to a Parr reactor system, in which the reaction mixture was charged to 70 bar 3:1 H2 to CO2 and
then subsequently heated to 150 °C for 24 hours (Scheme 3). The pressure and temperature were
monitored throughout. After cooling to room temperature, mesitylene (139 μL, 1.00 mmol) was
added directly to the solution as an internal standard along with 1.0 ml of CDCl3. This was mixed
and then 70.0 µL of the solution was removed. 1.0 mL of CDCl3 was added to the aliquot and the
resulting solution was filtered through a 0.2 μm filter. This was submitted for NMR analysis.

8.3.4 Computational Methods
Density functional theory simulations of the reaction pathway with the Ru-catalyst was performed
following the methodology described by Kar et al.125 Beginning with the proposed reaction pathway,
all geometries were optimized using Gaussian 16. Reb. B.01126 with the hybrid B3LYP functional
((Becke's three parameter hybrid exchange functional with Lee-Yan-Parr correlation function).90, 127-
129
 Extensive basis set testing was performed (see Supporting Information) and the 6-311+G** basis
set was used along with Grimmes D3 dispersion correction90, which best replicates the previous
works. All molecules were singlet state, and no constraints were placed on the system during
relaxation. Of the seven structures that were reported along the reaction pathway by Kar et al.125,
two of them are transition states. In this case the STQN method for locating transition structure was
used130, 131, using the optimized product and reactant state. Transition states were confirmed through
identification of one imaginary frequency for the final optimized transition state. Following
optimization for each reaction coordinate, thermal and solvent effects were included. Water
solvation effects were incorporated through the polarizable continuum model (PCM) model using
the formalism variant (IEFPCM).132-134 The authors note that the use of the PCM model does not
incorporate explicit solvation effects, such as proton transfer, though previous studies125 indicated
that such effects are not a primary reaction method for formation of formic acid with a similar

 104

catalyst. Thermal effects were varied to be consistent with experimental values between 25oC to
120oC and are noted with the results.

 8.4 Results and Discussion

 8.4.1 Synthesis of Heterogeneous Catalysts
To create a heterogenized analog of Ru-9, we utilized Lewis acid-catalyzed Friedel-Crafts alkylation
of the acridine backbone using triphenylmethane as a comonomer and formaldehyde dimethyl acetal
(FDA) as the crosslinker, resulting in the polymerized analog Ru-9-Poly-TPM as depicted in
Scheme 1.122, 135 P-XRD demonstrates a shift from the crystalline molecular catalyst, Ru-9, to an
amorphous structure indicative of the formation of Ru-9-Poly-TPM. (Figure 8.1A) Fourier
transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was used to characterize the material further. As shown in
Figure 8.1B, Ru-9 displays a distinctive CO stretches at 1887 cm-1, while Ru-9-Poly-TPM, has a
peak at 1950 cm-1, indicating that the structural integrity of the molecular catalyst is preserved
following polymerization.136 Ru-9-Poly-TPM undergoes a blue shift with self-polymerization, in
agreement with the literature.136, 137 This is due to the replacement of the hydride with an additional
Cl atom, increasing the electronegativity on the Ru center. Porosimetry measurements of Ru-9-
Poly-TPM yielded a Branauer-Emmet-Teller (BET) surface area of 793 m2 g-1, an accessible pore
volume of 0.382 cm3 g-1, and average pore size of 39.4 Å. (Figure 8.1C) These results indicate that
Ru-9-Poly-TPM has an increased surface area, porosity, and pore volume than previously reported
values for an analogous Ru-9 polymer (Ru-9-Poly).136 This enhancement in porosity may be
attributed to the incorporation of triphenylmethane which facilitates the formation of rigid porous
polymeric frameworks.122 Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of the sufficiently dried solid sample of
Ru-9-Poly-TPM showed a minimal mass loss of 1.5 wt% up to 250 ˚C (Figure 1D), demonstrating
high thermal stability. ICP-OES measurements from Galbraith Instruments of Ru-9-Poly-TPM
reveal a Ru content of 1.36 wt% comparable to analogous Ru based system.122, 135 Additionally,
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with EDS images confirm the heterogenization of Ru-9 to
form Ru-9-Poly-TPM and that the catalyst is uniformly dispersed on the polymer. (Figure 8.1E).
Elemental maps indicate that Ru, P, Cl, and N are dispersed throughout the catalyst particles. There
is no evidence that Ru-containing species accumulate in specific locations or that catalyst
decomposition leads to the formation of multiple species that are physically mixed with the catalyst
particles.

Recyclability of Ru-9-Poly-TPM for CO2 hydrogenation to formate was evaluated over four
consecutive batch runs. In this reaction, Ru-9-Poly-TPM (0.005 μmol Ru atoms), Na3PO4 (9
mmol), water (4.5 mL) and a stir bar were added to a stainless-steel reactor, charged with 70 bar 3:1
H2:CO2 and heated to 150 °C for 2 h. Following each hydrogenation cycle, the catalyst was removed
from the reaction mixture by filtration and rinsed with water. Subsequently, the recovered catalyst,
along with fresh Na3PO4 and water, were added to the stainless-steel reactor and heated to 150 °C
for 2 h for each subsequent cycle. In the first cycle, Ru-9-Poly-TPM produced 10.58 mmol formate
(Figure S8.25). Upon recovery and reuse of the solid catalyst, the formate yield decreased to 3.34
mmol in cycle 2, followed by 2.92 mmol and 2.84 mmol in cycles 3 and 4, respectively (Figures
S8.26-29). Notably, after the initial decrease, catalytic performance stabilized, with cycles 2–4
averaging 3.03 ± 0.27 mmol, indicating that formate production was relatively consistent upon
subsequent reuse. After each cycle, the supernatant was recovered and submitted for inductively
coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) analysis, which demonstrated minimal Ru leaching
(<0.11%) (Table S8.1). Furthermore, IR spectrum of Ru-9-Poly-TPM after 4 consecutive cycles
showed retention of the CO stretch at 1950 cm-1, with a modest decrease in intensity, consistent
with preservation of the molecular Ru-9 catalyst within the porous polymeric framework during
catalysis (Figure S8.30). Quantitative EDS analysis of Ru-9-Poly-TPM was also conducted,
revealing a uniform distribution of the Ru atoms within the framework and further confirming their
retention (Figure S8.31). Overall, this indicates that the sharp activity loss in cycle 2 is not driven by
Ru leaching into solution but instead reflects potential catalyst deactivation pathways or changes in
site accessibility within the porous network as leading reasons for activity loss.

 8.4.2 Catalysis Mechanism and Structural Factors
Density functional theory (DFT) studies were carried out to evaluate CO2 hydrogenation to formate
with Ru-9 in water to with the full reaction pathway and energies included in Figure 8.2. Evaluating
Ru-9 allows mechanistic interrogation without complications from solubility and aggregation that
arise for the molecular catalyst in water. Notably, these solution-phase effects may also explain the
improved performance of Ru-9-Poly-TPM in basic aqueous media, where immobilization limits
aggregation and enhances active-site accessibility.. Here, the polymeric analog, Ru-9-Poly-TPM was
not modeled, as it is not expected to appreciably change the metal-center coordination environment
and therefore is unlikely to significantly affect the calculated reaction barrier. The first step in the
reaction is the transition of the Ru-9 complex from the meridional formato to the facial
coordination structure, associated with an increase in energy of 5.8 kcal/mol, a sufficiently low

 109

energy barrier to be able to be accessed under room temperature conditions and consistent with
previous DFT analysis of similar catalysts.125 Following transformation to the facial coordination
structure, binding of the CO2 with the Ru is favorable by 2.7 kcal/mol, followed by CO2 insertion
into the Ru-H bond, forming a pentacoordinated Ru via binding with the formate (-15.6 kcal/mol)
with a modest energy barrier (4.7 kcal/mol). Diffusion of the formate away from the Ru does not
exhibit an energy barrier (see Figure S8.32) and results in a deprotonated Ru-9-Poly-TPM
complex.

The introduction of an H2 molecule results in reformation of the pentacoordinated Ru via formation
of a dihydride species (-20.4 kcal/mol). This bound dihydride splits upon reaction with hydroxide,
resulting in a reformation of Ru-9 and release of a water molecule (-15.4 kcal/mol). Among the
reaction steps, the highest energy transition state is present for the splitting of the bound dihydride
via proton transfer to an available hydroxide to form water (26.7 kcal/mol). Activation energies of
comparable magnitude have been reported for proton transfer between a Ru dihydride structure and
an aqueous phase hydroxide with a reported energy barrier of 20.1 kcal/mol or 20.5 kcal/mol in a
Ru(II)-pincer complex140 and a aliphatic PNP pincer Ru complex141 respectively, where it served as
the rate limiting step. Notably, the high energy of this transition state is exacerbated using an implicit
solvation model, which does not account for low-energy transition states enabled by proton hopping
or similar mechanisms.142 In the case that such mechanisms occur, the transition state may be
decreased presuming that the reaction site is accessible for proton transfers to occur. In comparison
higher transition state energies, such as 37-43 kcal/mol were reported in cases where the Ru was
bound to chloride, necessitating direct CO2 insertion in the Ru-H bond.143 These findings
underscore the importance of aqueous hydroxide availability in the reaction mechanism and suggest
that Ru-9, and by extension its polymeric analog, can effectively catalyze CO₂ hydrogenation under
mild conditions.

 8.5 Conclusions
Ru-9-Poly-TPM, a porous polymeric analog of the Ru-9 complex, synthesized here for the first
time via Friedel–Crafts alkylation, translates Ru-9-type motifs into a recoverable heterogeneous
catalyst that is substantially more active than Ru-9 at equal Ru loadings. Ru-9-Poly-TPM efficiently
catalyzed the hydrogenation of CO2 and carbonate/bicarbonate to formate, delivering high
TONs/TOFs and consistently outperformed molecular Ru-9 catalyst at matched Ru loadings. The
enhancement is most striking for bicarbonate hydrogenation, where Ru-9-Poly-TPM hydrogenates
NaHCO3 to formate in 88% yield (TON 1570; TOF 787 h−1) while Ru-9 is largely inactive under
identical conditions (2.6% yield). High productivity is also observed for piperidine-mediated
CO2 hydrogenation to formamides. Recycling studies for CO2 hydrogenation to formate in an
alkaline aqueous media show a first cycle decrease followed by stable activity in subsequent runs;
importantly, ICP–MS indicates no detectable Ru leaching, pointing to non-leaching causes such as
catalyst deactivation pathways or changes in site accessibility within the porous network.

To identify the mechanisms behind the experimental trends, DFT calculations were used to provide
mechanistic insight into how Ru-9 activates CO2. These calculations indicate that Ru-9-mediated
CO2 hydrogenation in water proceeds through facile CO2 binding and insertion, with catalyst
regeneration limited by a modest barrier involving hydroxide-mediated proton-transfer step. The
lower activity for Ru-9 is therefore attributed to not due to intrinsic mechanistic limitations but
instead potentially due to limited catalyst accessibility under aqueous conditions arising from poor
solubility and aggregation/phase separation. In contrast, the higher activity of Ru-9-Poly-TPM
relative to Ru-9 may be consistent with polymer immobilization creating a high-surface area, water-
accessible environment that increases active-site exposure and may further promote base/proton-
transfer events required for catalyst regeneration, thereby accelerating turnover in aqueous media.
Overall, these results demonstrate that porous polymer immobilization can be a strategy that
can enhance catalytic performance at constant metal loading while retaining the operational
advantages of a molecular catalyst. Future work will focus on elucidating the origin of the rate
enhancement (including microenvironment, speciation, and transport effects), clarify the cause of
the first-cycle change, and focus on polymer tunability (e.g., pore architecture and functionality) to
improve stability and expand aqueous CO2/carbonate upgrading chemistry.

Materials and Methods
Milstein acridine catalyst (Ru-9) was purchased from Strem Chemicals and used without further
purification. NEt3, KOH, NaOH, Na3PO4, K3PO4, NaHCO3, piperdine, pentaethylenehexamine,
anhydrous dichloroethane (DCE) was purchased from Sigma Aldrich and used without further
purification. All chemicals were stored under inert N2 atmosphere in a glove box. Hydrogen gas was
obtained from Matheson Tri-gas and has a purity of 99.999%. 25% CO2 in H2 was purchased from
Matheson Tri-gas. All syntheses and catalytic reactions were assembled and performed under inert
N2 or argon atmosphere using either Schlenk line or glove box techniques.

Synthesis of Ru-9-Poly-TPM (Scheme 1)
Ru-9-Poly-TPM was synthesized analogous to literature protocols.122, 135 Ru-9 (0.3338 mmol, 202.0
mg) and triphenylmethane (3.005 mmol, 734.1 mg) was dissolved in 14 mL DCE. This solution was
then transferred under inert conditions to a 100 mL Schlenk round bottom flask containing
anhydrous FeCl3 (13.35 mmol, 2166 mg). Following that, 8.0 mmol of formaldehyde dimethyl acetal
(FDA), diluted with 3 mL of DCE, was added to the reaction mixture while stirring it at room
temperature. The whole reaction mixture was then agitated for 12 hours at 25 ˚C and then for 12
hours at 40 ˚C. After that, the temperature was raised to 80 ˚C, where the reaction was allowed to
continue for 48 hours. After cooling, G4 was used to filter the reaction. The resulting solid
combination was repeatedly rinsed with methanol and 4(M) HCl in water. Further methanol was
used as a solvent during the Soxhlet extraction process to wash the dark solid product for 48 hours.
The solid powder was adequately dried under vacuum, resulting in 946 mg of Ru-9-Poly-TPM
material. [ICP-OES results from Galbraith Laboratories determined Ru wt% = 1.36]
