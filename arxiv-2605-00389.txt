Play and Learn: Gamified Feedback for Ultrasound-Guided Catheter Insertion Training in Virtual Reality PubID: pubid: 979-8-3315-8342-2/26/$31.00 ©2026 IEEE
Mohammad Raihanul Bashar 1 , Alejandro Olivares Hernandez 2 , Yahia Zine 2 , Anil Ufuk Batmaz 1

Abstract
Virtual reality (VR) is widely used for procedural medical training, yet most simulators emphasize realism while providing limited formative feedback. We examine how gamification affects performance, workload, and experiential quality in VR training for ultrasound-guided peripheral intravenous catheter insertion. We developed a gamified simulator with semantically aligned visual and auditory feedback (e.g., progress indicators, alignment guidance, rewards) while preserving procedural fidelity. Two studies were conducted with novices (N=24) and clinicians (N=12). Results showed that gamification reduced task time, improved usability, and lowered workload across expertise levels. Qualitative findings indicate improved goal clarity and confidence for novices and better pacing for experts. Overall, gamification can function as an effective formative feedback in VR medical training.



I
Introduction

Peripheral intravenous catheter (PIVC) insertion is a common procedure for establishing rapid intravenous access, with over two billion catheters used annually worldwide
[
1
]
. Despite its prevalence, first-attempt failure rates can reach 39%, and overall catheter failure affects approximately 30–50% of patients due to complications such as infiltration, phlebitis, occlusion, dislodgement, and hematoma
[
2
,
3
]
. Traditional landmark-based techniques rely heavily on visual inspection and tactile estimation of superficial veins
[
4
]
, which has driven the growing adoption of ultrasound guidance. While ultrasound improves cannulation success, especially in patients with difficult intravenous access, and reduces complications
[
5
]
, it introduces substantial perceptual–motor demands. Trainees must coordinate both hands, maintain the needle within a narrow imaging plane, and interpret dynamic 2D images to make accurate 3D spatial judgments
[
6
]
. Novices often struggle with probe alignment, depth perception, and consistent needle visualization, and traditional training methods (e.g., mannequins and instructor-led sessions) provide limited feedback and insight into subsurface anatomy
[
7
,
8
]
.

Virtual reality (VR) enables procedural training by simulating complex environments, visualizing hidden anatomy, and allowing unlimited, risk-free practice
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
. It provides controlled anatomy, real-time feedback, and standardized conditions—key for ultrasound-guided PIVC, which requires integrated spatial, motor, and visual skills. However, many VR simulators prioritize workflow replication over learning progression, motivation, and error correction, risking disengagement and reinforcement of poor technique.

We present a gamified VR training system for ultrasound-guided PIVC insertion that supports key sub-skills, including probe–needle coordination, venipuncture, and spatial alignment, through real-time simulated ultrasound visualization. Gamified elements are designed as task-aligned feedback, including alignment guidance, progress indicators, and performance summaries, to support engagement and learning without compromising procedural realism. We evaluate the system through controlled user studies with novices and clinical professionals, assessing its impact on performance, workload, usability, and user experience. Results indicate that gamified feedback improves procedural efficiency, reduces workload, and enhances user experience across expertise levels. Our contributions are: (1) a VR training system integrating real-time ultrasound simulation with task-aligned gamified feedback for perceptual–motor learning, and (2) empirical evidence demonstrating its effectiveness across novice and expert users.

III
Virtual Reality Simulation of Ultrasound-Guided Peripheral Intravenous Catheter Insertion

To translate the perceptual–motor demands of ultrasound-guided PIVC insertion into VR, we designed a simulation emphasizing real-time needle visualization, probe–needle coordination, and spatial alignment. Because continuous needle-tip visibility is critical for safe cannulation, the system focuses on the
In-Plane (Longitudinal)
technique, which provides a full view of the vessel and advancing needle (see
Figure 1
). In the In-Plane approach, the probe is aligned parallel to the vein, enabling continuous tracking of the needle for precise control. In contrast, the Out-of-Plane method provides limited visibility and feedback, making it less suitable for novices; thus, our simulation focuses on the In-Plane technique.

The system renders ultrasound images in real time based on probe pose, allowing users to interpret anatomy and adjust needle trajectory. It targets core skills, probe stability, in-plane alignment, and response to dynamic feedback, within a controlled, repeatable environment.

:
Technical setup of the VR ultrasound-guided PIVC insertion simulation.

III-A

Technical Setup

The simulation was built in
Unity 2022.3
with the
PlusToolkit Ultrasound Simulator
and ran on a high-performance PC, ensuring stable real-time rendering. A Meta Quest 3 headset (connected via Quest Link) enabled immersive 6DoF interaction with low latency. Ultrasound images were generated in real time through OpenIGTLink streaming, and the anatomically accurate forearm model ran at a steady 90 Hz for smooth interaction.

A virtual medical classroom was created with a full-body mannequin on a hospital bed and an anatomically detailed forearm to enhance realism and ergonomics. Participants used controllers to operate the probe (non-dominant hand) and needle (dominant hand), with visible virtual hands supporting spatial awareness. The scene included an examination setup and real-time ultrasound display, while controller input enabled precise, consistent interaction. While the simulation provides real-time visual and auditory feedback for needle advancement, it does not currently model haptic resistance (e.g., tissue penetration forces during needle insertion), as standard VR controllers do not support force feedback.

III-A
1
Simulated Catheter Insertion Process

The simulation reproduced the key stages of ultrasound-guided PIVC insertion: vein localization, probe alignment, needle advancement, and confirmation of successful cannulation. Participants first identified a target vein using the virtual ultrasound probe, with the image updating dynamically based on probe pose relative to the mannequin arm. The system supported both Out-of-Plane (cross-sectional) and In-Plane (longitudinal) views to realistically depict subcutaneous anatomy.

Users initially located the vein in the Out-of-Plane view, where it appeared as a circular cross-section, then rotated the probe into the In-Plane configuration to align the beam parallel to the vessel and maintain continuous needle visualization during insertion. Visual and auditory feedback marked key milestones: successful vein entry triggered realistic ultrasound deformation and a brief confirmation sound. After each successful insertion, probe and needle interactions were temporarily disabled for five seconds and automatically reset, allowing brief reflection while maintaining consistent trial pacing.

III-A
2
Catheter Insertion Settings

To ensure consistency with real-world applications, the virtual environment allowed participants to align the simulated setup with their physical workspace. The scene depicted a hospital bed with a mannequin, which participants aligned to their real table surface to match reach distance and ergonomics. Using the
left controller’s index trigger
, participants grabbed the ultrasound probe, positioned their hand on the physical table, and adjusted bed height via three virtual buttons (raise, lower, auto-align) to calibrate the interaction plane with the virtual arm.

After alignment, participants scanned the forearm in the Out-of-Plane view to locate a vessel, then rotated the probe to the In-Plane orientation for longitudinal visualization. For insertion, they used the
right controller’s index trigger
to grasp and advance the needle, which remained active only while held to preserve realistic control. The system provided two ultrasound displays—a fixed large monitor and a movable tablet viewer that could be repositioned with the right controller for flexible viewing. Throughout each session, the system logged total attempts, success events, probe alignment, and insertion time; however, this study reports insertion time as the primary outcome because it directly reflects procedural efficiency, while other metrics supported validation and feedback.

III-B

Designing the Gamified Simulation

The gamified version extended the base simulation with motivational feedback and structured progression while preserving procedural realism. The goal was to enhance engagement and learning through goal-oriented feedback and adaptive guidance without compromising medical fidelity. Gamification was integrated at both the visual and interaction levels.

Fig. 2
:
Gamified VR interface showing real-time ultrasound imaging, star-based feedback, needle and probe positions, and task progress indicators.

A
cumulative progress-based feedback system
displayed five hollow stars at the start of each level; each successful insertion permanently filled one of five progress stars, which briefly turned gold (for 5 s) to provide immediate visual reinforcement before remaining filled to indicate cumulative progress (Figure
2
). Stars accumulated across attempts are not time-dependent, meaning users are not required to achieve consecutive successes within a time window. To support motor precision, we implemented a
landing window
guidance mechanism
[
60
]
: as the catheter approached the target, a semi-transparent circular guide appeared and changed color—green for optimal alignment and red for misalignment—then faded as alignment improved (Figure
3
).

Fig. 3
:
The landing-window alignment mechanism. (a) The triangular landing window appears when guidance is enabled, providing a target zone for correct needle approach. (b) As the needle advances through the window along the correct trajectory, the guide gradually disappears.

After each level, participants viewed a
performance summary
showing attempts, successful insertions, and completion time, along with a tiered digital badge (e.g.,
Apprentice
to
Grandmaster
) to support reflection and sustained motivation. Subtle success tones complemented the visuals. All metrics were logged using the same architecture as the base simulation to ensure direct comparability between conditions while maintaining medical authenticity.

IV
Motivation and Hypothesis

Effective VR medical training requires not only procedural realism but also engagement, structured feedback, and motivational support. Although VR enables safe, repeatable practice for ultrasound-guided catheter insertion, learners may struggle to track progress or maintain focus. Gamification addresses these gaps through goal structures and feedback cues; therefore, we compared a standard (non-gamified) simulation (
section III
) with a gamified variant.

Using a between-subjects design with one independent variable
Condition
(
Standard
vs.
Gamified

task performance
,
usability perception
, and

through two research questions.

RQ1. Does gamification enhance procedural performance efficiency during ultrasound-guided catheter insertion in VR?
Prior work indicates gamification can accelerate learning and improve motor coordination through immediate feedback and structured progression
[
61

62
]
. We hypothesize:
H1a:
Gamification improves task performance;
H1b:
Gamification improves learning progression across levels.

RQ2. How does gamification influence subjective experience (usability, workload, engagement)?
Prior research shows that well-designed rewards and feedback can increase usability, reduce cognitive workload, and foster intrinsic motivation
[
77
,
75
,
65
]
. Accordingly, we hypothesize:
H2a:
VR catheter gamification increases usability and
H2b:
reduces perceived workload.

V-C

Procedure

Participants completed a 45-minute session in either a
Standard
or
Gamified
condition. After consent, they received a briefing and watched a recorded VR catheter insertion demo, followed by a brief training phase to familiarize them with the system and reduce novelty effects. They then completed a demographic questionnaire and proceeded to their assigned condition, with group allocation balanced via a Latin Square (Figure
4
). For all tasks, participants aimed to perform insertions as quickly and accurately as possible; pre- and post-insertion tasks required a successful attempt, with retries allowed until success. This study was approved by the institutional research ethics board.

Standard Condition.

Participants performed ultrasound-guided insertions without gamified elements, e.g., no scores, rewards, or visual guidance, across five stages: a
pre-insertion
baseline (one insertion), three practice levels, and a
post-insertion
task identical to the baseline.
Level 1
required five successful insertions at a natural pace;
Level 2
required five insertions emphasizing both speed and accuracy; and
Level 3
allowed five minutes to complete as many successful insertions as possible.

Gamified Condition.

The task sequence matched the
Standard
condition but included gamification elements such as star rewards, auditory cues, landing-window guidance, performance summaries, and tiered badges. The
pre-insertion

Level 1

Levels 2
and
3

post-insertion
level. Across levels, multimodal feedback (visual, auditory, symbolic) aimed to enhance engagement and learning while preserving medical realism.

V-E

Discussion

Study 1 demonstrates that gamification benefits novice users across performance, usability, workload, and experiential quality. Participants in the gamified condition completed tasks faster, reported higher usability, experienced lower workload on several dimensions, and showed stronger engagement and goal clarity. These findings suggest that gamification effectively supports early skill acquisition in ultrasound-guided catheter insertion. To examine whether these benefits extend to trained clinicians, we conducted a second study with experienced medical professionals.

VII
Discussion

This work examined how gamification through visual cues, auditory reinforcement, and level-based progression affects performance, workload, and experiential quality in VR ultrasound-guided PIVC training. Across studies with novices (Study 1) and experts (Study 2), results show that well-designed gamification improves procedural efficiency, reduces perceived workload, and enhances usability and experience, though via different mechanisms. VII-B

Effects on Expert Performance and Skill Reinforcement

Study 2 demonstrated that gamification also benefited experts. The
Gamified
group showed faster completion times, particularly in later levels requiring sustained precision. Here, gamification did not teach procedural knowledge but streamlined execution by reducing self-monitoring demands and stabilizing pacing. Experts rated the gamified system as more usable and less cumbersome, with significantly lower mental, physical, temporal demand, and frustration. PXI gains in clarity of goals, challenge, enjoyment, feedback, and appeal indicate that even experienced users value structured progress cues, especially during repetitive tasks.

Experts in the
Standard
condition showed longer insertion times than both gamified experts and novices, likely due to lower familiarity with VR rather than lack of expertise. Unlike novices, who reported greater exposure to gaming and interactive systems, experts often had minimal VR experience and may have relied on real-world habits that did not transfer well to VR, increasing interaction overhead. In contrast, gamified feedback offered clear guidance and progress cues, reducing interpretation effort and improving efficiency. The consistent gains in the
Gamified
condition across both groups indicate that its benefits extend beyond VR familiarity, supporting both learning and performance.

VII-C

Cross-Study Synthesis

The studies reveal that VR gamification improves outcomes for both novices and experts, but through distinct pathways. For novices, it functions as
instructional scaffolding
, making alignment and success criteria explicit and supporting confidence formation. For experts, it acts as
cognitive optimization
, reducing workload and sustaining focus during repeated performance. Importantly, these benefits did not compromise clinical authenticity, a common concern in medical gamification

Overall, the findings demonstrate that perceptually salient, real-time feedback can support users across expertise levels, improving both early skill acquisition and expert performance consistency in VR medical training.

Future work should explore alternative gamification strategies, incorporate anatomical variability and dynamic scenarios, and involve larger, more diverse cohorts in longitudinal studies. The use of handheld VR controllers may also limit motor fidelity, as fine-grained hand movements and tactile precision required in real needle manipulation are not fully replicated, potentially affecting skill transfer.

Another limitation is the potential for negative transfer, as gamified cues (e.g., alignment guidance and progress indicators) are absent in real clinical settings and may foster reliance. However, these elements serve as formative scaffolding for early skill acquisition. IX
Conclusion

We empirically evaluated a gamified ultrasound-guided catheter insertion simulator with novices and clinicians. Results show that goal-aligned visual and auditory feedback improved procedural efficiency, reduced workload, and enhanced usability and experience without compromising realism. Rather than serving as superficial motivation, gamification acted as formative feedback, supporting novice learning and improving expert fluency. Overall, these findings can be used to improve the training systems for catheter insertion to increase performance and user experience.
