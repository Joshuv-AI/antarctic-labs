3. Executive Summary:

  Urban areas, including Detroit and Pittsburgh, have suffered significant dual outages
  of the electrical and water infrastructure in the past decade due, in part, to the
  increasing number of extreme weather events. With increasing temperatures and
  rainfall intensity, these regions need to prepare for increasing extreme events through
  community-based energy and water resilience analysis, planning, and enhancement.
  This project developed a suite of open-source, open-access, community-centered,
  data-driven assessment and distributed energy resource (DER) and planning tools for
  energy and water resilience enhancement in urban areas. Through establishing a
  multi-level community awareness and engagement mechanism and a comprehensive
  collection of power outage and flooding data, an innovative group of community
  energy and water resilience assessment and planning tools have been developed for
  a wide range of users with differing and variable sets of data available to them.
 The developed tools include (1) DOE EAGLE-I data-driven, deep-learning assisted
  resilience assessment and DER planning tools at the county level with socioeconomic
  factors incorporated; (2) Utility annual power outage data-driven tools for long term
  resilience assessment and DER planning and 15-min power outage data-driven tools
  for short term resilience assessment and planning; (3) Detailed engineering tools for
  energy and water systems resilience assessment and planning when the system
  topology and component fragility curves are available; (4) Alternative Resiliency Metric
  Calculation that extracts and separates outage and restoration processes; and (5) Co-
  optimization tools that evaluate the resilience of the power and sewage system and
  allow users to conduct joint planning with energy and wastewater systems.
 The developed tools provide planners, decision-makers, and stakeholders with
  powerful capabilities to systematically evaluate system/community resilience and
  optimal and actionable guidance for enhancing resilience while prioritizing DER
  investments. The tools have been used and validated in Detroit and Pittsburgh and
  can be used in other areas of the nation. In addition, this project will (1) advance the
  knowledge and applications of machine-learning methods in analyzing and fusing
  different layers of information and generating meaningful data points such as
  generating rare weather events; (2) significantly improve the energy and water
  resilience of the identified communities in Detroit and Pittsburgh and prepare for more
  frequent and severe weather conditions; (3) help communities assess extreme
  weather event impacts and address short-term and long-term resilience-related issues
 The developed tools have been made public via GitHub and demonstrated to
  community stakeholders and utility companies via the two annual workshops and
  numerous community engagement meetings. The project outcomes are also
  disseminated through publications in various journals and conference proceedings,
  and presentations at top conferences.

5. Background
5.1 Energy Resilience Assessment

An effective evaluation of resilience and its associated metrics is a critical aspect of
resilience-based planning of engineering systems. The assessment of power system
resilience can be broadly classified into two primary approaches: 1) Data-driven or macro-
level (system-level) approaches, which are also called statistics-based methods, and 2)
Detailed engineering or micro-level approaches, which are often simulation-based
methods.

5.1.1 Data-Driven Resilience Assessment

This approach focuses on evaluating the power system’s resilience from the system level,
taking into account various elements that contribute to its strength and ability to endure
disruptions. The performance of the system can be defined in terms of its physical
capabilities or its ability to maintain operational service. The qualities that would
guarantee a power system’s operational strength, such as the capability to guarantee the
availability of generation capacity or a continuous supply of electricity to consumers in the
event of a disaster, are referred to as operational resilience. On the other hand,
infrastructure resilience focuses on the power system’s physical robustness,
minimizing component failures or disruptions. This attribute allows the resilience curves
to effectively address various threats, irrespective of their characteristics [1], [2].
 1) Resilience Triangle: The resilience triangle theory was developed by Bruneau et
al. and served as the foundation for resilience assessment [3]. This approach primarily
examines how a system’s performance deteriorates throughout an event. The resilience
triangle method (Figure 1) provides a quantitative assessment of resilience through a
time-dependent metric. This metric evaluates the system’s functionality on a scale from
0% (representing a complete loss of service) to 100% (indicating no disruption in service)

 The Environment for Analysis of Geo-Located Energy Information (EAGLE-I) dataset
provided comprehensive power outage records across counties in the United States from
2014 to 2022 [5]. Based on EAGLE-I data, the studies in [6] and [7] proposed several
metrics to measure the performance aspects of the power grid during extreme outage
events, such as event duration, impact duration, and impact level. A probability

utility outage data are available, more specific resilience assessments can be done.

emulate the physical systems and test them under synthetic attacks, such as extreme
weather and cyber attacks. This technique emphasizes analyzing the specific fragility
curves of individual components within a system, offering a granular perspective on their
vulnerability to extreme events. A fragility curve represents the probability of failure of a
system or one of its components under a specific event or load condition. Dunn et al.
describe the fragility curve as a lognormal distribution [9],

5.1.3 Resilience Assessment with Overlapped Stages
 Resilience curves are typically categorized into (a) distinct, and (b) overlapping
phases. In previous studies, reference [12] has utilized resilience curves to model the
dynamics of outage occurrences and recovery processes in electric distribution systems
during disruptive events. These curves were systematically divided into two segments:
the outage process and the restoration process, which often overlap in time. By analyzing
extensive real-world utility data, they characterized the statistical behavior of these
segments and developed formulas to quantify key resilience metrics such as restoration
time, unserved customer hours, and the rates of outages and restorations [12], [13]. The
derived formulas established relationships between the expected values of these metrics
and the total number of outages during an event, offering a practical framework for
evaluation. Additionally, a formula for the variability in restoration times was proposed,
enabling the prediction of maximum restoration durations with 95% confidence [14]. The
reference [15] outlines a disruption phase marked by system failures, followed by a

                                        Page 7 of 117

recovery phase. Similarly, references [2], [16] introduce a resilience trapezoid model that
encompasses progressive disturbance, a degraded state, and a recovery phase. In
contrast, [17] represents restoration as an exponential curve rather than the straight-line
recovery typically seen in resilience triangles. [18] identifies phases such as unreliability,
disruption, and recovery, depicting them using triangles, trapezoids, or other geometric
shapes. [19] highlights the lowest point of resilience curves to distinguish between outage
and recovery phases, a method reflected in numerous resilience modeling approaches.
 Papers [20], [21], and [22] extract and separate the outage and restore processes
from distribution utility outage data in a new way, estimate the statistics of times between
successive restores or outages, and then show how standard resilience metrics can be
derived from these statistics. The overall effect is to compute some useful resilience
metrics from practical utility data. Reference [23] introduces Poisson process models in
terms of their rates for outage and restore resilience processes and performance curves.
The model parameters can be calculated from standard utility outage data [24].
 Moreover, our method utilizes sigma-algebra techniques to extract and separate
outage and restoration processes from distribution utility outage data. It computes
resilience values between consecutive outages and restoration events, enabling the
derivation of standard resilience metrics. Ultimately, this approach effectively generates
practical and insightful resilience metrics from real-world utility data.

5.2 Energy Resilience Planning
Resilience assessment provides quantitative methodologies to identify the vulnerabilities
and strengths of power systems, offering strategic directions to improve their resilience.
The literature has identified different types of resilience enhancing and planning
strategies.
 Allocation of distributed energy resources (DERs) is a strategic planning approach
that involves deploying distributed generation (DG) units, mobile power sources, and
other DERs at key nodes within the power system to bolster its resilience. Given the
inherent unpredictability of renewable energy systems during extreme weather
conditions, it is essential to include this uncertainty in the design and simulation of system
response. Doing so not only improves grid stability but also ensures reliability under both
standard and adverse conditions. Properly positioned DERs can serve as a preemptive
grid-hardening measure before adverse events occur. For instance, two-stage stochastic
mixed-integer programming models have been developed to optimize the location and
sizing of DGs in the first stage, while the second stage focuses on minimizing the
operating costs of DGs and load shedding. The determination of the optimal size for a
DG is driven by the objective of maintaining resilience above a specified threshold or
minimizing the cost while incorporating the resilience requirements into constraints.
 Several methods to enhance power system resilience have been tested in prior
research. For instance, line-hardening strategies were used in [25], [26]. Additionally,
mobile emergency generators are considered in [27] to be positioned before the disaster
to hasten the likely post-disturbance operations. In [28], a mobile energy resource
allocation strategy is developed to restore critical loads by determining the most reliable
restoration path against potential earthquakes. Besides the dynamic scheduling of mobile
resources, static renewable resources, such as permanent solar or wind installations, are
also considered in various literature. In [29], a cooperative game theory-based approach

                                        Page 8 of 117

is proposed to solve the sizing and siting problem of DERs. In [30], an algorithmic
formulation for optimal photovoltaic (PV) hosting and placement of distributed energy
resources for network resiliency enhancement is developed using a Genetic Algorithm
(GA) and tested on a 34-bus system. Similar to the works for resilience estimation, most
of the work in resilience enhancement demonstrated their work in small-scale systems
[27], [28], [29], [30].
 Depending on the data availability and the underlying resilience assessment method,
the resilience planning optimization models are formulated correspondingly, and the
planning tools are developed accordingly. We have taken the following approaches to
resilience planning tool development based on:
 • The data-driven resilience evaluation method using DOE EAGLE-I data
 • The data-driven resilience evaluation method using the Detroit Edison
 Energy (DTE) power outage data
 • The engineering approach (simulation based method)
 • The alternative Resiliency Metric Calculation that extracts and separates
 outage and restoration processes

5.3 Resilience Analysis and Co-optimization of Energy and Wastewater Systems
Power outages can have devastating impacts on wastewater infrastructure, leading to
sewage system failures and flooding that threaten public health and safety. Wastewater
collection and treatment systems rely on electricity for pumping stations, treatment
processes, and control systems. When the power grid goes down during storms or other
disasters, sewage pumps and treatment facilities may stop functioning, causing untreated
sewage to back up into streets and buildings or overflow into waterways [31]. During
Hurricane Ian, an assisted living community in Orlando, FL, experienced flooding due to
the failure of a nearby lift station. This water intrusion from the lift station failure
contributed to the flooding of the facility and caused the evacuation of all 122 residents
[32]. Authors of [33] found that rolling electrical blackouts led to frequent wastewater
treatment outages, contaminating rivers with raw sewage. Based on these Our
engineering-based method can simulate the sewage backup scenarios.
 In the United States, densely populated cities such as Detroit, MI face a confluence of
factors that exacerbate flooding impacts including low elevation, inadequate housing,
aging infrastructure, and rising water levels in receiving waters [34], [35]. Catastrophic
flooding and extreme precipitation have documented economic, social and health-related
impacts [36], [37], [38], and [39], power outages can also put vulnerable populations at
risk. Ice storms and freezing rain have been shown to be associated with power outages
in a number of contexts [40], [41]. Power restoration due to flood related service
interruptions often takes a considerable amount of time, depending on the extent of
damage [42]. Costs due to weather-related power outages are costly and difficult to
contain due to the complexity of power provision in the United States [43]. Extreme
weather events can cause power outages and restoration of power following hurricanes
and tropical cyclones have been shown to have disparities between urban and rural socio-
economic groups [44]. In some contexts, wind speed, precipitation, and previous outages
generally were more important in determining an outage even than simply storm surges
[45].

                                       Page 9 of 117

 Recent studies have explored diverse methodologies for optimizing urban stormwater
systems, addressing critical objectives such as flood risk mitigation, environmental
sustainability, and cost-effectiveness. A notable study introduced a multi-objective
optimization model using the Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II),
effectively balancing flood risk mitigation, facility efficiency, and economic considerations
across various rainfall scenarios [46]. Another comprehensive framework integrated
simulation and optimization techniques, emphasizing the balanced achievement of
multiple objectives, including environmental impacts, economic efficiency, and overall
system performance [47].
 Research by other researchers has emphasized sustainability and resilience through
innovative optimization approaches. One significant approach incorporated a
sustainability index alongside Low Impact Development (LID) and Best Management
Practices (BMPs), targeting enhanced resilience and environmental sustainability in
stormwater drainage systems [48]. Further, a bibliometric analysis spanning nearly two
decades (2004–2023) identified evolving trends and emphasized a growing focus on
integrated and sustainable stormwater management strategies [49]. Additionally, real-
time operational optimization strategies have gained attention, with predictive real-time
control methodologies emerging to dynamically manage stormwater systems. Such
methods leverage forecasted weather conditions to optimize system performance
proactively, substantially reducing flood risks and enhancing operational efficiency [50].
 Co-optimization of energy and wastewater systems has also garnered significant
attention due to its potential to enhance operational efficiency, reduce costs, and promote
environmental sustainability. Xie et al. developed a mixed-integer nonlinear programming
(MINLP) model for optimizing wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) and smart grids,
demonstrating substantial energy savings and improved effluent quality [51].
Decentralized micro water-energy nexus systems have been proposed, enabling
independent yet coordinated management of water and energy subsystems, effectively
achieving near-optimal outcomes [52].
 A comprehensive review highlighted the importance of integrating energy optimization
tools within wastewater facilities, emphasizing energy demand reduction, renewable
energy integration, and greenhouse gas emission reductions [53]. Real-time strategies
for energy management in WWTPs, including adaptive combined heat and power (CHP)
units, have demonstrated significant cost savings and efficiency improvements [54].
Research on co-digestion and fuel cells indicated potential for WWTPs to become net
energy producers by enhancing biogas yield and energy conversion efficiency [55].
Moreover, integrated design and optimization methods for urban districts have
successfully optimized wastewater treatment alongside energy systems, improving both
economic and environmental outcomes [56].
 Collectively, these studies underline the importance and effectiveness of integrated,
sustainable, and adaptive approaches for urban stormwater and energy-wastewater
management optimization. Our approach focuses on minimizing the risk of stormwater
backflow into households by employing Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) optimization.
This method simultaneously accounts for the specifications of both stormwater network
systems and electrical systems within power distribution networks, ensuring integrated
and resilient infrastructure management.

5.4 Visualization
The PHOENIX Prevalence Profiler (https://phoenix-data.wayne.edu/map/) visualizes
geospatial information and makes the aggregated data publicly available online using
open-access technology [57]. The tool is consistent with a growing array of interactive
web applications that include but are not limited to the Opportunity Atlas, City Health
Dashboard, Air Pollution in World, HRSA Map Tool, County Health Rankings and
Roadmaps, EnviroAtlas, and most importantly the recently updated FEMA RAPT
interactive map.
 The Prevalence Profiler differs from other interactive web applications in several key
aspects. First, local clinical data, investigators generated estimates, and a broad array of
social vulnerability indicators are woven together, whereas other tools tend to focus on
narrower sets of metrics or multi-component indexes. Second, the Prevalence Profiler is
internally modifiable, whereas other web applications tend to show a fixed set of metrics
or implement fewer than two-to-three updates over their lifespans. By contrast, iterative
updates are far more feasible with our web application because the PHOENIX Prevalence
Profiler is internally managed. Third, the prevalence profiler offers an array of capabilities
that together optimize end users to address multiple use cases: a) metric by metric scatter
plots are integrated with the geospatial visualization, b) filters for population size, but also
dozens of other metrics are available, c) custom image exports, d) deep links make
sharing easier, e) time period comparisons are available as data allow, f) users can add
their own points of interest data overlays using embedded geocoding support, and g) user
groups are available to focus on diverse use cases including energy resilience. While
some applications may have some of these features, we have yet to find an analogous
tool with the breadth of capabilities offered by the PHOENIX prevalence profiler.

6. Project Goal, Objectives, and Impact:
This project is to develop an open-source, community-centered, data-driven distributed
energy resource (DER) planning tool for energy and water resilience enhancement in
urban areas. Through establishing a multi-level community awareness and engagement
mechanism, we propose to develop a suite of community energy and water resilience
assessment and planning tool via analyzing multiple layers of information and interaction
between water and energy systems, and co-optimizing planning and operation by
considering mobile energy storage and network reconfiguration. The developed tool,
Data-driven Community-centered Resilient Assessment and Planning Toolkit for Nexus
of Energy and Water (DCRAPT-NEW), produced metrics that are integrated and
visualized by the WSU PHOENIX platform. By combining energy and water resilience
indices and other important community stress/vulnerability indicators, the tool can provide
community planners with a comprehensive view of energy and community resilience to
help them make informed decisions.
Impact
This project has produced significant impacts, including:
• Demonstrate the benefits of a multi-level, multi-channel community engagement
 mechanism for engaging communities in solving engineering and social issues.
• Provide an open-access and open-source assessment and planning tool as well as
 a platform for various community stakeholders to engage, view, access, and plan for
 energy-water resilience. The methods, tools, and developed procedure have been
 implemented and verified in Detroit and Pittsburgh and made publicly available.
• Help communities assess extreme weather event impacts and address short-term
 and long-term resilience-related issues such as displacement, economic security,
 housing insecurity, and mental and physical illnesses.
• Develop tools to significantly improve the energy and water resilience of the
 identified communities in Detroit and Pittsburgh, with sufficient documentation to
 allow translation to other urban centers experiencing more frequent and severe
 weather conditions.
• Advance the knowledge and applications of machine-learning methods in analyzing
 and fusing different layers of information, including incomplete and noisy data, and
 approaches for addressing data scarcity in energy resilience analysis.
• Mapping resilience characteristics of the study regions to allow future analysis of the
 key variables of significance.
 The team’s work has a direct impact on interdisciplinary research and training. This
DOE project has helped to ignite a collaborative arrangement focused on flooding and
water hazards between the three major research universities in Michigan, i.e., the
University of Michigan, Michigan State University, and WSU, through an entity called the
Michigan Center for Freshwater Innovation. This entity has already received funding from
the State of Michigan to look at solutions to flooding in the Detroit area; in addition, funds
were awarded from The Erb Family Foundation. An extension of that work has resulted
in an award from the National Fish and Wildlife Federation to develop “nature-based
solutions” (NBS) to the flooding issues. The results of this project also helped develop a
proposal titled “Solar Enhanced Community Resilience (SECURE) – Wayne Solar Pilot”
to the Michigan Solar for All Pilot Program in 2025.

7. Project Results and Discussion

7.1 Community Survey Results and Discussion

7.1.1. Detroit Surveys
We deployed two rounds of surveys to residents living in 28 of the 31 zip codes in Detroit.
We administered the first round of the survey from September to December of 2023. The
second survey round was administered roughly one year later, from August to October
2024. The two survey questionnaires can be found in Appendix A1. We collected a total
of 665 unique records, 260 from the first survey round, and 405 from the second survey
round. Among the 665 residents who responded, 16.24% were from the Jefferson-
Chalmers area.

Figure 3. Survey results of adverse weather impact on Detroit residents.

 Figure 3 illustrates the impact adverse weather had on Detroit residents. The vast
majority of Detroit residents reported being affected either by floods, windstorms, or ice
storms, often all three. Many reported being affected severely. A higher percentage of
Detroit residents who live outside of the Jefferson-Chalmers area reported being
personally affected by both ice storms and windstorms than other Detroit residents.
Meanwhile, the vast majority of Jefferson-Chalmers residents reported being personally
affected by floods, which is congruent with the FEMA National Risk Index. Specifically,
we found that residents living in areas with higher FEMA Risk Scores were more likely to
report personal experiences with flooding.
 About 90% of residents reported “experiencing power outages” during a storm (as
shown in Figure 4). About two thirds of residents reported not having power for more than
a day. Meanwhile, power outages unrelated to storms seemed less common among

Detroit residents (about 55%, as shown in Figure 5). Additionally, these power outages
seemed to be resolved within a day for most (about 60%) of residents.

 One of the most critical findings from this study is that residents who reported more
flooding experiences were more likely to engage in flood prevention, even after controlling
flood-prone locations and multiple other demographic characteristics. Particularly, a
higher extent of flooding was related to a higher degree of engagement in at-home
precautionary measures against flooding and a higher level of support for flood prevention
policies. Consistent with other research in the literature, the study found that renters were
less engaged in flood prevention compared to homeowners. Our findings also highlighted
that higher income was associated with higher flood prevention. Compared to other
residents, respondents who identified as Black/African American were less likely to
engage in flood prevention.
 Given residents’ differential engagement in flood prevention, targeted communication
strategies are necessary to improve public sentiment regarding the risks and
consequences of flooding. Urban flooding is likely to get worse as the extreme weather
events become more frequent and the infrastructure continues to degrade, especially in
an older city like Detroit. Interventions to promote private flood precautionary measures
and policy support for flood prevention need to be framed differently for those who are
not as severely impacted by flooding. Policies to mitigate and/or prevent flooding need to
be explained clearly to gain residents’ trust in the efficacy of such policies. Furthermore,

7.1.2. Pittsburgh Surveys
Two rounds of surveys were also carried out in Pittsburgh; locations are presented in
Figure 9. Among different types of natural hazards (floods, wind storms, ice storms, heat
waves, etc.) reported in the surveys, floods are most significant, shown in Figure 10.
Flooding in Pittsburgh is concentrated in low-lying neighborhoods such as Homewood,
Larimer, and Four Mile Run, where valley topography and aging combined-sewer lines
allow heavy rain to overwhelm the system, sending water into streets and basements.

structural upgrades and often lack access to insurance payouts or public recovery grants,
widening the resilience gap between tenure groups.
 The financial burden of flood-proofing falls hardest on lower-income households,
particularly in underserved communities that already contend with older, under-
maintained housing stock. These neighborhoods report not only greater physical damage
but also slower recovery after storms, reflecting decades of disinvestment. Recurrent
dampness fosters mold growth, aggravating respiratory illnesses such as asthma, while
the stress of repeated flooding compounds mental-health challenges, underscoring that
flood risk in Pittsburgh is as much a public-health and socioeconomic factor issue as it is
an infrastructure concern.
 In Pittsburgh, major floods have displaced residents, but the citywide percentage of
households forced to evacuate due to flooding is relatively low, likely only on the order of
a few percent. Severe storm events have caused localized evacuations; for example,
Hurricane Ivan’s torrential rains in 2004 “caused severe flooding and damage”, with
many Pittsburgh-area residents “forced to leave their homes” (some for months). More
recently, flash floods in April 2024 led to “numerous rescues and evacuations” in parts of
Allegheny County (e.g., Oakdale and Etna). These incidents, however, tend to be
confined to flood-prone neighborhoods. Overall, only a small fraction of Pittsburgh
households have ever had to temporarily relocate due to flooding, absent a citywide
inundation event. More Pittsburgh survey results can be found in Appendix A2.

7.3.2. Utility-data-driven Resilience Assessment Tool
We took a data-driven resilience trapezoid framework to characterize various states of
the power system during extreme events. Illustrated in Figure 2, a conceptual resilience
trapezoid delineates the states of a power system in response to an event, alongside the
temporal progression of these states over time. The system’s performance is a critical
factor in assessing resilience. Resilience analysis necessitates detailed modeling of
emergency responses to evaluate and compare the effectiveness of various
improvements across differing scenarios. Based on the power outage data availability
(i.e., annual data or 15-min real time power outage data), we developed long-term
assessment and event-based assessment methods (see Appendix A4 for the details of
the utility-data-driven methods development).

outages over an extended time horizon, such as annually, biennially, or over multiple
years. This study concentrates on evaluating the resilience index by utilizing annual

outages within the time frame.

7.3.3 Engineering (Simulation) Based Resilience Assessment Tool
We have developed a simulation-based engineering method for assessing the resilience
of both power and flood within local communities. The method requires power system
topology, weather model, and fragility model as input. The power system topology
includes substations, distribution lines, poles, and customers. Considering power
distribution networks are usually proprietary, we proposed a synthetic topology generation
method, and the synthetic network generated for Detroit is shown in Figure 11. The
topology is modeled as a graph, where each substation/pole/customer is modeled as a
node in the graph, and the power lines are modeled as edges. Each customer is assigned
to a single substation. The method can also simulate sewage backup flooding scenarios
given sewage system topologies (pumps and sewage lines).
 To estimate the power system resilience, we generate numerous hazard scenarios
using the proposed extreme weather scenario generation engine. The method supports
generating wind scenarios in thunderstorms and snow scenarios in snowstorms. A
snapshot of a generated wind speed field is shown below (Figure 19). When a hazard is
applied to the network, some power system components fail based on predefined fragility
models. For wind scenarios, our framework supports two fragility models, such as wind
fragility (Figure 20) and wind-tree-coupled fragility for distribution lines (Figure 21). The
former describes the likelihood of line failure due to strong wind, while the latter accounts
for scenarios where wind breaks tree branches, subsequently triggering line failures that
require tree coverage data for the area, as shown in Figure 22. After the thunderstorm
subsides, repair crews are dispatched to the failed component locations based on
predetermined repair strategies. Each simulation round concludes once the system
returns to its original state, and then a resilience value is calculated based on the
trapezoid method. The final resilience value for each region is calculated as a weighted
sum of episodic resilience values.

                                       Page 29 of 117

7.4.3. Simulation-Based Resilience Planning Tool
For the developed simulation-based (engineering approach) planning tool, the system
resilience is measured using the Monte Carlo method. For each simulation episode, there
are two stages, i.e., hazard and recovery. In the hazard stage, a generated weather
scenario is sampled from the weather scenario generator and applied to the given system.
In the recovery stage, repair crews are dispatched to outage locations to repair the
damaged power lines. The customer loses power when its connection to the substation
is lost. Solar panels and batteries are planned in this case to enhance the system’s
resilience. Taking thunderstorms as an example weather scenario, solar panels only work
in the recovery stage, while batteries work in both stages. The constraints of the
formulated problem include the site and size limitations of solar panels and batteries. The
problem can be formulated as follows:

 The solution is found through a customized genetic algorithm in the outer loop and the
resilience evaluation in the inner loop. The outer loop proposes new solution candidates,

while the inner loop evaluates the solution candidates. The genetic algorithms run in a
generation-by-generation manner. In each generation, a population of individuals
(candidates) is generated. Each individual is composed of the sites and sizes of solar
panels and batteries. After receiving the evaluation results from the inner loop, the genetic
algorithm generates the next generation through crossover and mutation. The iteration
stops when the maximum generation is reached, or the target resilience is reached.
 We proposed a customized Genetic Algorithm (GA), which incorporates a proximity
rejection mechanism, a global tournament selection process, and a weighted location
sampling design.
 • Global tournament selection: In the selection stage, if the best individuals are
 selected from the current generation only, the algorithm is not convergent. To
 improve convergence, the best individuals are selected from the current generation
 and all previous generations.
 • Proximity rejection: When generating new individuals, it is imperative to select
 spatially diverse locations. Locations are selected one at a time. When a new
 location is selected, the shortest distance to the nearest location among all the
 selected locations is denoted as K (hops). Then, the newly selected location is
 rejected with a probability of 1/K. It is also applied in the crossover step.
 • Weighted location sampling: The number of episodes in which the node loses
 power in the estimation phase is counted, then the numbers are scaled by the node
 degree. Finally, the numbers are normalized to produce a probability distribution.
 The score progression of the search process is shown in Figure 34 to demonstrate
the efficacy of the proposed customized GA. We also investigated a Bayesian
Optimization method, but it resulted in inferior results compared to the customized GA.

7.5. Resilience Correlation Analysis and Planning for Energy and Water Nexus

7.5.1. Resilience Correlation Analysis via Assessing Risks for Power Outages
from Extreme Precipitation and Flooding

Extreme weather in the form of heavy winds, rainfall, ice, and flooding is one of the main
causes of electrical power failure events. Increased frequency of extreme weather events
is resulting in increased incidence of urban flooding, such as in Detroit, MI. Detroit
struggles under aging electricity and water diversion infrastructure, dual vulnerabilities
that might result in significant economic and household risks. However, the links between
flooding and local and city-wide electricity outages are not well understood.
 We obtained yearly data on the incidence of electrical outages within substation
service areas from DTE Energy, the largest utility in Michigan, which serves the Detroit
Metro Area. These data include the total number of outage minutes and the number of
homes affected within each service area. We also obtained data on flooding, including
addresses and dates of times of flood-related calls to a hotline managed by the Detroit
Water and Sewage Department (DWSD). Flood calls were aggregated by year and DTE
substation service area. All measures were normalized by the number of homes within
each DTE service area using data from public tax records. Associations of flooding and
household electrical power failure were tested using bivariate and multiple linear
regression models, including US Census-based measures of income and ethnic
composition as possible confounding factors.
 Flood risk was weakly associated with the number of outage-impacted homes. Outage
resilience was positively associated with flooding at low levels of risk but not associated
in service areas that had homes with a more than 10% chance of being flooded during a
major precipitation event. Increased service area level affluence and median income were
positively associated with power outages, while social disadvantage and percent Black
residents were negatively associated with outages. Population and ethnic immigrant
populations were not associated with outages, in either the number of homes or total
minutes without electricity.
 We found that flood risk was weakly associated with the number of homes that
experienced outages within a power service area during the study period. We also found
that outage resilience was positively associated with flooding at low levels of risk, but not
associated in service areas that had homes had a more than 10% chance of being flooded
during a major precipitation event. Increased service area level affluence and median
income were positively associated with power outages, while social disadvantage,
percent Black residents were negatively associated with outages. Population and ethnic
immigrant populations were not associated with outages, in both the number of homes
and the total minutes without electricity.

 Scenario 1: Normal Operation: Under normal operational conditions, the network

effective power distribution throughout the region.
 Scenario 2: Outage due to Flooding: In the event of significant flooding, the network

units (yellow nodes) gradually resume production as weather conditions improve.

isolated buses and restoring essential services swiftly. This scenario underscores the co-
optimization strategy's effectiveness, balancing energy supply, demand management,
and stormwater pumping, thereby accelerating infrastructure recovery.

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions

Significant Accomplishments
a) Survey of communities in Detroit and Pittsburgh
 The comprehensive community surveys finished in Detroit and Pittsburgh highlight the
need for targeted support for residents in the two cities, many of whom are substantially
impacted by adverse weather events and have insufficient financial and/or social capital
for future disaster prevention. 82.7% of the responses in the Detroit Survey indicate the
loss of power in the past 5 years, and a large majority of Pittsburgh residents (83%) have
experienced at least one storm-induced power outage in the past 5 years. The surveys
also provide baseline data on local demographics, socioeconomic conditions, and
infrastructure conditions. The survey results also significantly enhance the accuracy and
relevance of the co-optimization model for energy and stormwater resilience planning via
the developed data-driven and engineering-based assessment and planning tools, which
facilitate targeted infrastructure investments and informed community engagement
strategies, directly contributing to improved resilience against energy outages and
flooding.
b) Data collection
 Data is critical for any data-driven and/or machine learning based methods. We
collected resilience related data (e.g., 15-min and annual power outage data) from energy
and water utility companies (DTE, GLWA, etc.), DOE power outage data (e.g., EAGLE-I
Dataset), FEMA resilience data, and conducted a comprehensive literature review to
retrieve useful data (such as local and regionally proximate storm data) that can be used
for community resilience analysis and planning. To address the extreme weather event
data scarcity and confidentiality of critical infrastructure, algorithms have been developed
to produce synthetic power grid networks and extreme weather event data. By
overcoming data scarcity and confidentiality barriers, synthetic power grid data and
extreme weather data generation enable researchers to openly test, validate, and
compare algorithms for resilience assessment and planning without exposing sensitive
details of real-world infrastructure, and allow systematic exploration of a wide range of
plausible scenarios that may not yet be fully captured or available in historical records.
c) Energy and water resilience assessment tools
 A suite of resilience assessment tools, including data-driven (macro-level) and
detailed engineering (micro-level) methods, has been developed for a variety of data
availability scenarios and a wide spectrum of users. Particularly, a robust suite of energy
and water resilience analysis tools was successfully developed, enabling integrated,
scenario-based planning to simultaneously address energy outages and stormwater
flooding risks. This accomplishment is critical because it provides planners, decision-
makers, and stakeholders with powerful capabilities to systematically evaluate
system/community resilience and effectively mitigate vulnerabilities.
d) Resilience assessment of the identified communities
 The completion of the resilience assessment for the identified communities in Detroit
and Pittsburgh is a significant milestone, as it provides comprehensive resilience maps
of the communities, highlights localized vulnerabilities (e.g., with a resilience index value

                                       Page 57 of 117

<0.9) and infrastructure needs, and enhances future preparedness and targeted
response strategies in the face of extreme events.
e) DER resilience planning tools
 Based on the resilience assessment methods developed, a suite of DER resilience
planning tools has been developed, which enhances the capability to strategically place
and size DER assets to maximize grid resilience and reduce community vulnerability to
extreme events. The integration of socioeconomic factors into DER planning ensures that
investments are not only technically optimal but also equitably distributed, addressing the
needs of vulnerable populations. This comprehensive approach helps to build a more
resilient energy system that can withstand and recover from disruptions. This
achievement is significant because it provides decision-makers with practical, actionable
guidance for enhancing resilience while prioritizing DER investments effectively.
Moreover, the planning tools meet the needs of different users with different data
availability.
f) Evaluation of the Resilience Planning Tools
 We conducted DER planning studies in the identified communities under different
extreme scenarios and compared the impact of different recovery strategies on the
system resilience using the developed tools. The results have been communicated to the
utility companies and community leaders to continuously improve the tools and to further
help the communities enhance their power resilience.
g) Co-optimization of energy and wastewater systems
 The co-optimization tools evaluate the resilience of the power and sewage
systems and allow users to conduct joint planning with energy and wastewater
systems. This facilitates further studies of the interaction of these two systems and allows
users to extend the framework to even larger areas. The co-optimization tools create an
integrated decision-making framework to concurrently manage flooding risks, power
outages, and community-specific vulnerabilities—a comprehensive approach previously
unaddressed in unified optimization modeling.
h) Community engagement workshops
 Two annual community engagement workshops were successfully held, which
brought together community residents and leaders, utility representatives, and academic
researchers. The workshops functioned as an effective platform for different community
stakeholders to work together to address the critical challenges in improving community
energy and water resilience. The project team successfully demonstrated the resilience
assessment and planning tools developed, and community stakeholders provided their
valuable feedback to validate and improve the tools. The community engagement
platform established by the annual workshops will have a lasting impact and continue to
serve the community beyond the project period.
Discussions and Conclusions
 Statistics-based methods focus on retrospective review followed by prediction
(extrapolative) of future scenarios based on real-world data; however, different studied
areas experienced different weather scenarios, which makes it difficult to compare the
resilience of these systems. Simulation-based methods focus on building a complete
model of the physical world, simulate the behavior of the components in the model, and

                                       Page 58 of 117

measure the resilience of the system in such synthetic/simulation scenarios. Simulation-
based methods are good at answering “what-if” questions, e.g., what is the resilience of
this system if such scenarios happen, but require more detailed data compared to the
statistics-based methods.
 Our deep-learning based resilience assessment method is not only based on
real-world data, but also capable of answering the “what-if” questions, providing a
powerful tool to conduct predictive studies without the detailed parameters of the physical
world. Our engineering-based methods try to incorporate real-world weather data to build
high-fidelity extreme weather scenarios, and apply them to the built model. Most of the
previous research focused on small-scale systems or transmission systems, while our
engineering-based method can support large-scale distribution system that is built
with high fidelity upon the real-world data. With detailed parameters of the physical
world, the method can provide detailed system behavior, providing a foundation for
resilience planning studies.
 As a summary, the developed tools can effectively characterize the resilience
baselines of the communities in Detroit and Pittsburgh. The community-specific
vulnerabilities have been identified for the communities in the region. The developed data-
driven and simulation/engineering-based tools have been cross-validated with each other
and validated against the survey results. The developed resilience assessment and
planning tools and the analysis results have been shared with the community
stakeholders, utility teams, and city planners, via various community engagement
activities, including two annual community resilience workshops and demonstration of the
developed tools. The results of this project have made the communities more informed
and enabled them to be better prepared for future extreme weather events. The project
results have also been disseminated via presentations at various professional
conferences, journal publications, and other outreach activities, including dedicated
demonstrations of the tools to utility companies in other regions for broader impacts.

9. Path Forward
The project team met with WSU’s Office for Technology Commercialization and the
Division of Research & Innovation to identify potential patentable and/or copyrightable
intellectual property, as well as to explore initial target markets for the energy-water
system-focused Resilience Assessment and Planning open-source tools developed.
 One immediate opportunity identified is the NSF Pathways to Enable Open-Source
Ecosystems (POSE) program, which supports the formation of self-sustaining open-
source ecosystems (OSEs). The POSE program offers two types of proposal tracks:
• Phase I: OSE Scoping and Planning Proposals
 Phase I is intended for open-source products with a small community of external
 users. The primary objectives are to: 1) Enable scoping activities that support the
 transition of existing open-source research products into sustainable and impactful
 OSEs. 2) Provide training for project teams to effectively build and maintain these
 ecosystems. Phase I awards are up to $300k with a maximum duration of one year.
• Phase II: Establishment and Expansion Proposals
 Phase II targets open-source products that already have a small but active community
 of external users and contributors. The objective is to support the full transition of
 these products into sustainable and robust ecosystems. Eligible teams must have

                                      Page 59 of 117

 completed the necessary scoping activities and developed a detailed plan for
 community-driven, distributed development and deployment.
 Phase II awards are up to $1,500,000 with a duration of up to two years.
 If we pursue the formation of an OSE, WSU will serve as the lead organization,
overseeing governance and the management of distributed development processes and
infrastructure. The University of Pittsburgh would be responsible for establishing a
decentralized, open network of contributors and developers committed to the OSE’s
vision. These contributors would bring their expertise and time to help develop and
maintain the core software product. To broaden the user base and foster community
engagement, we plan to promote the open-source software through various channels,
such as PowerGlobe, to attract users whose contributions can guide future development.
 To support long-term sustainability, a tiered business model is also being considered.
The base version of the software could remain free and publicly accessible, while a
premium version—offering advanced energy resilience analysis and optimization
capabilities—could be offered for a fee. We will continue to improve the energy and water
resilience assessment and planning tools developed through this project. In addition to
working with the utility companies in Detroit and Pittsburgh, such as DTE Energy, GLWA,
Detroit Water and Sewerage Department (DWSD), and Duquesne Light Company (DLC),
we are in touch with Eversource (in Connecticut) and Texas New Mexico Power (TNMP)
to get their feedback on the developed tools in the future.
 We will consider extending our research to real time control of power systems to
enhance system resilience. We have started to extend our research to co-design of
control and planning for power systems with high penetration of inverter based resources
(IBR), robust PID control against nonlinear uncertainties in modern power systems,
resilient distributed frequency regulation of renewable generators under communication
interruptions, and stochastic adaptive droop control in frequency regulation of power
systems with renewable generators.

A2. Survey results on flooding and Flood Related Damage Costs in
 Pittsburgh

A2.1 Flood-Related Damage Costs in Pittsburgh
Household Flood Damage Costs: While comprehensive Pittsburgh-specific survey data
is lacking, flood damage expenses for residents span a broad range, similar to other cities.
Minor basement floods (a few inches of water) might cost under $2,000 to clean up,
whereas typical remediation for a more substantial basement flood is around $3,000–
$10,000. Severe flooding that impacts first-floor living spaces or causes structural
damage can easily run into tens of thousands. (For instance, a homeowner in
Philadelphia faced a $23,000+ cleanup bill after a major sewer backup, illustrating the
high end of potential costs.)
 For Pittsburgh, we can reasonably estimate the distribution of out-of-pocket flood
costs (among those affected) as follows:
 • Minimal (< $500): Only ~5% of affected households – minor incidents (dampness
 or limited seepage) often handled with simple cleanup.
 • Moderate ($500–$5,000): The largest share (~40%). Many basement floods fall
 in this range, covering water pumping, new carpeting, drywall repairs, etc.
 • Significant ($5,000–$20,000): Roughly 30% of cases. These include deeper
 basement flooding or appliance damage, requiring professional restoration and
 some replacements.
 • Major (> $20,000): Perhaps 10% or slightly more. Catastrophic events (feet of
 water, structural repairs) can push costs well above $20k (even >$50k in extreme
 cases).

These brackets are inferred from typical flood remediation costs and regional anecdotes.

A2.2. Frequency of Yard Flooding (Past 5 Years)
Yard Flooding Incidence: Thanks to Pittsburgh’s hilly terrain, a majority of homes do not
experience frequent yard flooding. Upland and slope neighborhoods shed water quickly,
while low-lying “hollows” (such as the Four Mile Run area pictured above, nestled under
a Greenfield bridge) see repeated inundation. Heavy downpours can overwhelm storm
sewers, causing flash floods in valley neighborhoods – “severe flash floods” hit Four
Mile Run whenever the combined sewer pipe overflows. Nonetheless, many residents on

A3. Resiliency Metric Calculation Based on Decomposed Outage and
Restoration Curves

A3.1 Modeling the Outage 𝑶𝑶(𝒕𝒕) and Restoration 𝑹𝑹(𝒕𝒕) Curves.

The framework treats each disturbance as two cumulative, staircase‐shaped processes.

Outage curve 𝑶𝑶(𝒕𝒕)
Each time a grid component fails at a specific moment, the outage curve experiences a
sudden increase, reflecting the severity of the event 𝑂𝑂(𝑡𝑡) . The size of each jump
corresponds to the impact of the failure—such as the number of affected customers or
the amount of power lost in megawatts. The overall outage profile is built by accumulating
these discrete events over time, capturing the evolution of system disruption throughout
the event sequence.

Restoration curve 𝑹𝑹(𝒕𝒕)
Repairs typically begin after a delay that accounts for crew mobilization, travel time, safety
inspections, and delivery of necessary parts 𝑅𝑅(𝑡𝑡). As a result, each restoration event
occurs some time after the corresponding failure. These restorations are represented as
upward jumps on the recovery curve, with each jump reflecting the scale of the repair
completed. The restoration and outage curves align only after the system is fully restored.
Notably, the sizes of the failure and repair steps often differ, highlighting that the pace or
extent of restoration does not always directly match the nature of the initial failures.

A3.2 Closed-Form Residual Function 𝑹𝑹𝑹𝑹(𝒕𝒕)

The instantaneous mismatch between cumulative outages and restorations is
                        𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅(𝑡𝑡) = 𝑂𝑂(𝑡𝑡) − 𝑅𝑅(𝑡𝑡).                                      (A1)

The electrical subsystem optimization aims to minimize:

 • Full AC power flow validation at the distribution level,
 • Solar adoption likelihood and CAB-identified communities for DER installation,

A5.7 Stormwater Network Optimization Model
This section introduces a robust stormwater network optimization model tailored for
minimizing flooding and backflow risks while controlling operational costs in urban
environments. The model utilizes a graph-based representation comprising nodes and

optimization is conducted under multiple flooding scenarios using a Conditional Risk
approach, enhancing resilience against extreme weather events. The framework ensures
mass balance at each node, adheres to hydraulic and operational constraints, and
enforces logical conditions for pump activation based on inflow thresholds. Furthermore,
the document provides detailed guidelines for creating realistic inflow scenarios derived
from synthetic rainfall patterns, enabling precise and practical application. This
comprehensive optimization formulation forms a critical component in integrated
infrastructure planning, aligning stormwater management objectives with broader urban
resilience strategies.

Objective: The model minimizes total system cost by combining:

 • Expected water shortfall under uncertainty (using conditional risk to capture risk),
 • Pumping energy costs,
 • Capital costs for installing pumps on selected pipelines.

 time periods.
 • Pipe Capacity: Water flow must remain within pipe-specific capacity limits.

We developed a framework for enhancing the resiliency of power distribution networks

 Binary variables appear in both stages: in the first, to represent DG installation

and other network operational configurations. Due to the presence of integer variables in
the recourse stage, traditional decomposition techniques such as Benders or L-shaped
methods are inapplicable. Thus, an extensive form of SMIP has been employed.
 One of the central innovations of this work lies in the enforcement of radiality and
connectivity constraints for network reconfiguration. Given that the formation and topology
of post-damage islands are endogenous to the optimization process, a novel SuperGraph
methodology is introduced. By appending a virtual node and linking it via fictitious lines to
all physical nodes, radiality and connectivity constraints can be uniformly applied from
this virtual root, ensuring structural coherence across all emergent sub-networks.
 Complementing the operational and planning framework, this research also
introduced two novel, purely graph-theoretic resiliency indices to quantitatively assess
network robustness. The first index evaluates resiliency by comparing the number of
spanning trees in the network to that of a complete graph of equivalent size, providing a
discrete measure particularly suited for radial topologies. Recognizing the limitation of this
index to radial networks, a second index is proposed based on spectral graph theory: it
normalizes the Fiedler value—the second smallest eigenvalue of the Laplacian matrix—
by the number of nodes, offering a continuous metric of connectivity and resilience
applicable to more general network topologies. Together, these indices offer
complementary insights into network resiliency from a topological perspective,
independent of operational or flow considerations.
 The power flow modeling further distinguishes this work: acknowledging the
inapplicability of DC power flow in low-voltage systems due to high resistance-to-
reactance (R/X) ratios, a linearized AC power flow model is adopted to preserve realism
while maintaining computational tractability.
 Outage scenario generation is grounded in physical and statistical realism.
Recognizing wind as the primary driver of distribution-level outages, the methodology
                                       Page 106 of 117

incorporates fragility curves and wind-speed sampling from a Weibull probability
distribution function (PDF) to probabilistically assess line failures. For each scenario, line
outages are synthetically realized by comparing fragility-derived failure probabilities
against uniform random samples. Scenario probabilities are computed via the product of
individual line failure or survival likelihoods, under an independence assumption. To
manage the scenario space, Hamming distance is employed for scenario refinement and
reduction, retaining representative diversity while enhancing tractability.
Collectively, this research contributes a physically informed framework for resilient
distribution system planning, with strong implications for future grid-hardening strategies
in the face of escalating climate-induced threats.
