1 INTRODUCTION/OVERVIEW
Vast amounts of research have gone into understanding both fracture mechanics and thermal-
hydraulic-mechanical-chemical (THMC) coupling in many fields across science and engineering
(e.g. National Academies Report, 2020; Pyrak-Nolte and DePaolo, 2015). The physical properties
related to fracture, fluid flow, and mass and heat transport cause strong THMC coupling triggered
by natural processes and human-engineering activities in the subsurface (e.g., Tsang et al., 1988;
Berre et al., 2019; Berkowitz, 2002; Babadagli, 2020; Seales, 2020; National Academies Report,
2020). In natural settings spanning a broad range of length scales, feedbacks in thermal cracking
and fluid flow are likely to be important processes due to strong thermal-mechanical gradients, for
example at mid-ocean ridges (e.g., Olive and Crone, 2018) and subduction zones, with potential
first-order effects for fluid cycling in plate tectonics (e.g., Korenaga, 2007). This project broadly
aims to advance two main basic research fronts: (1) understanding the physics of THM processes
in geologic materials (adding the “C” in future work), particularly at high temperature and pres-
sure conditions of the deep crust, and (2) developing novel strongly coupled constitutive models,
relevant to complex mechanical behavior of deforming solids in non-equilibrium open systems.
 We consider this to be fundamental, basic research, with clear directions towards applications.
If we can understand the physics, and sense enough of the character of the network and processes
in action that are changing that network, we can develop methods to control the evolution of such
transport networks. These capabilities would be essential in developing capabilities for deep-
crustal minerals/metals/hydrogen mining through open-system reservoir reactors, as well as deep
geothermal heat mining.

geothermal industry is pushing to reach the conditions for supercritical aqueous fluids (> 22 MPa,
>375°C), (e.g., Reinsch et al., 2017; Asanuma et al., 2018; Watanabe et al., 2019). Mining heat
by fluid flow at supercritical conditions has the great advantage that the heat capacity is far higher
than water or steam, but creating fracture networks at these conditions is a fundamental challenge.
For many rock types, supercritical conditions fall in the broad regime at which grain-scale dissipa-
tive mechanisms (e.g., dislocation motion, lattice- and grain boundary diffusion) can be activated
alongside brittle deformation mechanisms, a.k.a the brittle-ductile transition.
 The primary forcings that lead to the evolution of fracture networks can be viewed as a map (
Fig. 2,left), with broadly three vertexes: far-field differential (or shear) stresses (e.g. from back-
ground tectonic forces), hydraulic stresses (e.g. due to fluid pumping), and thermal stresses (due
to thermal disequilibrium between fluid and rock). With increasing temperature, these couplings
become less well understood, as they are harder to study in the field (less direct observation pos-
sible) and the lab (more limited length and time scales for allowing processes to play out than in
natural/field settings). A conceptual illustration of THMC in a geothermal reservoir is shown in
Fig. 1b: injection of cold (chemically unequilibrated) fluid into a hot rock will lead to thermal and
hydraulic effects that are completely coupled to each other, as they both can generate stresses and
cause fracture, which then changes the network properties and effective permeability, heat trans-
fer, chemical reaction rates, mechanical properties, and thus the next increment of fracture and
flow. Those processes depend on the full thermodynamic conditions, including the structure and
chemistry of the rocks at any given state. The ensemble behavior can be described by a “consti-
tutive model,” a set of functions that captures approximate and effective mechanical, thermal and
transport properties of a representative elementary volume (REV) of rock, and how those prop-
erties evolve in time. The constitutive model often determines the limitations and strengths any
large scale continuum model. Whether any existing constitutive model could capture sufficient and
realistic variations in these possible paths is an open question that is central to this project.

printed acrylic fracture network and an emphasis on detailed acoustic monitoring of the fracture

 The project reported on here (and its continuation) focuses on the coupling of thermal and far-

networks at the grain scale, in the absence of fluid, with an emphasis on development of constitu-
tive models that are scalable to real-earth conditions. The two projects emphasize very different
physics, but will eventually converge on a broad new family of strongly coupled constitutive mod-
els.

1.2 Overview of the Project
This project (seed and recently funded continuation1 ) has a strong emphasis on novel laboratory
experiments and data-analysis methods integrating multiple sources (blue boxes in Fig. 2). These
acoustic and microstructural data feed into thermo-mechanical modeling (yellow boxes) comprised
of fracture network models and constitutive models developed in a framework of non-linear ther-
modynamics of irreversible processes (TIP), discussed below in Section 3.3.1. The lab data will
be integrated into our TIP-informed neural network (TIP-INN) architecture for constitutive model
learning, which is being developed primarily in the Earthshot grant.
  1.2.1 Over-arching Hypothesis
The hypothesis we are building is this:
• In the Earth, fluid flow and heat and mass transfer characteristics are governed by fracture net-
  works that emerge from the microscale. Thermo-elastic stresses driving small scale fracture
  (thermal cracking) is a fundamental process governing the emergence of larger scale patterns;
• With increasing temperature, the grain scale becomes a fundamental length scale in charac-
  terizing the mechanical system, because the physical character of grain boundaries is a strong
  function of temperature. At low temperatures, grain boundaries have less of an effect on fracture
  propagation; with increasing temperature, diffusive processes (as well as dislocations) can cause
  significant stress relaxation and damping of crack propagation, so crack tip interactions remain
  closer to the grain scale, and are sensitive to the rock’s “fabric” or the spatial distribution of the
  rock’s mineral phases and their physical properties.
To refine and test this hypothesis, we are integrating novel experimental studies with thermodynamics-

  1.2.2 Seed grant-specific hypotheses:
The original proposal, that was awarded at the seed level (about 30% of the requested funds), had
three intertwined parts: (1) Experiments, (2) Constitutive model development, and (3) Machine
learning methods for integrating mechanical data and acoustic fingerprints into thermodynamically
constrained constitutive model. The main reduction of the original scope of work was to focus only
on thermal cracking, and defer the coupling to hydraulic fracture processes. The proposed work
was built upon these hypotheses:
1. Hydraulic cracking and thermal cracking produce subtle differences in the spectral features of
 their acoustic emissions; when they are part of the frictional failure process of a fault rupture
 event, they will affect the spectral-temporal characteristics of the emitted seismic energy.
2. Coupling among thermal and hydraulic cracking will lead to feedbacks such that the cracking
 rates in the presence of hydraulic and thermal stresses may not be a linear combination of
 the end-member rates; similarly, the acoustic emission spectra may not be linear mixtures of
 end-member signatures.
The implication of these two hypotheses is that we can use unsupervised machine learning to
extract diagnostic spectral features from the acoustic signals, and identify which, if any, of those

2 SUMMARY (Abstract)
This project, entitled “Towards machine-learning a fully-coupled constitutive model for thermal-
hydraulic fracture in geothermal systems: phase I,” addresses challenges in understanding and
controlling subsurface fracture networks, which are crucial for applications like deep geothermal
heat mining and deep-crustal minerals/metals/hydrogen extraction. The research focuses on ad-
vancing the understanding of coupled thermal-hydro-mechanical-chemical (THMC) processes in
geologic materials, particularly under the high temperature and pressure conditions found in the
deep crust. This seed grant focused specifically on thermal cracking and the development of new
constitutive models. Significant progress was made in both experimental and theoretical domains.

 To study micro-scale fracture formation, the project demonstrated the ability to create thermal
cracking under stress in granite samples using a Paterson Gas-medium Deformation Apparatus. A
primary accomplishment was the application of novel high-temperature, near-field “needle” sen-
sors made to record acoustic emissions (AEs) during these experiments. A high-resolution event
detection methodology based on matched filtering was applied to continuous acoustic recordings,
over 270,000 acoustic events in a single experiment, providing new resolution into the internal de-
formation processes. Thermal cracking tends to produce random, unclustered acoustic emissions,
that, as stress increases, become more clustered as the sample approaches rupture. The results
suggest a competition between increasing temperature and stress influences the patterns of en-
ergy dissipation as the samples enter the brittle-ductile transition. Unsupervised machine learning
methods were also successfully applied to extract diagnostic spectral features from these acoustic
signals, which aims to characterize different cracking regimes and material states.

 The project also made significant progress in developing novel constitutive models that satisfy
the laws of thermodynamics, in the context of reversible (elastic) and irreversible (viscous/plastic)
processes. The core concept for a novel neural network architecture to learn these constitutive
models from data was validated. This physics-informed machine learning approach is designed
to discover the underlying thermodynamic potentials that describe reversible and irreversible pro-
cesses in deforming solids.

 Overall, the seed grant successfully validated the proposed integration of novel laboratory ex-
periments, advanced acoustic analysis, and thermodynamics-based machine learning (TIP-INN)
to characterize fracture network evolution. The key outcome of this ongoing research will be the
development of mechanical laws describing the effective properties of evolving micro-fracture net-
works, that can be directly incorporated into large-scale reservoir models used for deep heat mining
and resource extraction.

In this section, we provide detailed background and results on the experimental and theoretical
aspects of this project, starting with the experiments and proceeding to the theory and modeling.

3.1 Background: Thermal Cracking and Fracture Networks
Thermal cracking is understood to arise from (1) stresses caused by local changes in temperature
and thermal gradients and (2) internal, local stress concentrations at the grain scale due to thermal
expansion anisotropy and thermal expansion mismatch in rocks (e.g., Fredrich and Wong, 1986).
In experimental studies, cracking is usually observed indirectly via the recording of acoustic emis-
sions (AEs), that is, nano-seismic events. Laboratory observations have shown that higher heating
rates cause higher thermal gradients inducing greater thermal stresses and, consequently, greater
AE rates (Yong and Wang, 1980). The critical temperature at the onset of AE activity increases
with confining pressure Pc (Wang et al., 1989). This temperature threshold reflects a critical stress

σth (Pc ) for thermal cracking (e.g., Meredith et al., 2001), which is not a simple function. Experi-
ments reveal a “Kaiser effect” in the AE rate (e.g. Wang et al., 1989): AE activity starts only after
reaching the peak stress from the previous wave of cracking. That apparent history dependence
is caused by the reduction of elastic modulus M and thermal expansion coefficient Λth with in-
creasing crack density dc (e.g., Schubnel et al., 2006; Nasseri et al., 2009). As cracking progresses,

damaged rock reaches the critical stress σth  at a higher temperature than undamaged rock.
 Experiments have taught us that the thermal cracking of a rock is highly influenced by its micro-
structure. While coarse grained granitic rocks with complex, interlocking grain boundaries exhibit
the above-mentioned Kaiser and stress threshold effects, fine grained basaltic rocks tend, instead,
to crack during thermal contraction, when temperature drops (Daoud et al., 2020). It indicates
that grain boundaries and their morphology play an important role in accommodating thermally-
driven local grain-scale stresses. Furthermore, strong rock fabrics (i.e. crystallographic preferred
orientations rock fabrics) inherited from high temperature, large strain creep can influence the
orientation of thermal crack networks (Boudier et al., 2005). Far-field stress can similarly influence
the morphology of micro-crack networks.
 The density of thermally-induced cracks affects most of the mechanical properties (e.g., John-
ston and Toksöz, 1980; Fredrich and Wong, 1986; Faoro et al., 2013; Nasseri et al., 2007, 2009),
heat transport properties (e.g., Wong and Brace, 1979), and fluid transport properties (e.g., Sid-
diqi and Evans, 2015; Liu et al., 2018; Jones et al., 1997). These properties are coupled and vary
significantly if crack porosity is filled with gas, vapor or fluid. Thus, nonlinear effects become
significantly more complex in the presence of fluids. If there is thermal disequilibrium between
the fluid and the rock, thermal and hydraulic fracturing will be closely coupled (e.g., Zimmerman,
2000; McTigue, 1986; Ghassemi et al., 2008). In the conditions of “superhot” geothermal reser-
voirs, crustal rocks may be approaching or within the brittle-ductile transition, depending on their
bulk composition, microstructure, permeability structure, fluid composition and many other fac-
tors (Watanabe et al., 2019; Beeler et al., 2016). Thermal cracking in hot rock experiencing cold
thermal shock may be particularly effective at creating permeability (e.g., Watanabe et al., 2019),
but the roles of various thermally activated processes, such as viscous relaxation of crack tips, and
even the solid framework, that may work to both close and open porosity, locally, remain poorly
understood. Furthermore, recent permeability measurements in slowly deforming granite samples
at a broad range of temperatures (Meyer et al., 2024) showed that the fracture networks at high

• Initial detection: We use the short-term average/Long-term average (STA/LTA) technique to
  detect events above noise level and build an initial database of AEs (removing electrical noise
  pulses if necessary).
• High resolution detection: We densify the initial catalog with matched filtering (MF), that is,

 correlation-based technique detects new, potentially very weak events that are similar to their
 template event. MF is a computationally intensive task for which we have developed GPU-
 accelerated software (Beaucé et al., 2018).
As shown in Fig. 7c, for e1, this method detected about 271,744 events at a MF detection threshold
of 5x the RMS of the correlation coefficient (CC) time series (about 2000 times more events than
triggered catalog). This detection threshold is low but stacking 100 of the strongest (highest CCs)
or of the weakest (lowest CCs) detections produces a waveform closely similar to the template
(Fig. 7b), thus demonstrating that weak detections are small acoustic emissions. The number of
detected events is highly dependent on the detection threshold. For example, raising the threshold
to 6.5x the RMS gives a much smaller number of 213 events (Fig. 7c).
Temporal clustering statistics: The temporal statistics of events encode the physics of their in-
teractions: weakly clustered or random (e.g., Poissonian) occurrences indicate that when events
nucleate in response to some external forcing, they do not significantly interact with other events,
whereas strongly clustered occurrences indicate cascading caused by subsequent perturbations of
the stress field due to the events themselves (e.g., Marsan and Lengline, 2008; Beaucé et al.,
2022). Strong clustering may be promoted by the properties of the fracture network or by the
stress mediating properties of the rock, that is, its rheology. Here, we systematically characterize
the statistics of the AE occurrences by fitting a gamma law to the distribution of inter-event times
τ : p(τ ; γ, β) = Cτ γ−1 e−τ /β (e.g., Hainzl et al., 2006; Marsan et al., 2013), with results shown in
Fig. 7d,e. The model parameter, γ, varies between 1 (Poissonian, unclustered occurrences) and 0
(fully clustered occurrences). Our goal is to discover relationships between the event statistics and
the physical state and properties of the sample throughout time. We found that weak detections
(mostly small events) tend to behave randomly whereas strong detections (mostly large events)
exhibit much more clustering (Fig. 7d,e). This result suggests that small AEs are driven by the
pervasive microcracking due to thermal stressing while large AEs are part of cascading brittle
failure, and opens many new questions which we develop in Section 3.2.5.
  3.2.4 Unsupervised Pattern Discovery
Methodology (SpecUFEx+Hierarchical clustering) The aim of our unsupervised learning ap-
proach applied here is to extract subtle variations in the frequency content through time of all the
signals (Fig. 8a). The method used here, called SpecUFEx, operates on spectrograms (though
other time-frequency representations could be used). Further details are found in Holtzman et al.
(2018b), applied to microseismicity from The Geysers, and Sawi et al. (2022), applied to ice-
quakes in the Gorner Glacier. Briefly, this method involves two dimensionality-reducing, feature-
extracting steps to transform each spectrogram into a fingerprint. First, non-negative matrix fac-
torization (NMF) is applied to spectrograms of the AE waveforms to split each spectrogram into a
dictionary of frequency components (common to all events) and a matrix of activation coefficients
(unique for each event). Hidden Markov models (HMMs) are then learned on the NMF activation
matrices to further reduce dimensionality and highlight temporal patterns; each NMF matrix is rep-
resented as a sequence of hidden states in time, where states are patterns of frequency components.
The fingerprints are generated by counting transitions between states in time.

  3.2.5 Discussion of results and further motivation
We have demonstrated that placing a near-field sensor on a deforming sample and applying high-
resolution event detection methods provides a novel view into the processes occurring inside the

 17

 [FINAL REPORT: DOE BES Grant DE-SC-0024678]

sample and opens many questions. Unfortunately, due to a variety of noise sources (that we have
now isolated), we were only able to build a high resolution catalog for one experiment (e1). Thus,
we are limited in how much we can speculate on the meaning of the detected patterns. However,
interesting questions have arisen from the results of one experiment.
 The first-order pattern from the temporal clustering analysis is that the very numerous small
magnitude events detected below the noise level are close to random (γ → 1); these probably
reflect local internal stress mismatches (e.g., Fredrich and Wong, 1986) popping off randomly as
the sample heats up. In contrast, the larger events (strong detections) are strongly clustered in time,
suggesting cascading, interacting events. A general model for large-scale brittle failure predicts
the progressive coalescence of brittle deformation onto a highly localized 2D structure (e.g. Girard
et al., 2010; Renard et al., 2017); the temporal clustering may indicate the likelihood of large-scale
brittle failure as in e3,6. In e1, thermal stresses did not lead up to the macroscopic failure of the
sample, because we deliberately relaxed the stress between each heating step. In e3,6, we locked
the piston and they failed. Therefore, we would expect temporal clustering to increase over time
in the latter two. Further experiments in which the samples are stressed to failure will help to test
this conjecture.
 The second-order pattern in the strong detections (>6.5xRMS in Fig. 6e) is that as tempera-
ture increases, temporal clustering in the strong detections is less and less prominent; that is, the
gamma value generally increases with each stress step. In this sequence, those steps are increasing
both in temperature and stress (but lower stress than if we had locked the piston). We therefore
hypothesize that the decrease in clustering is due to the effects of increasing temperature, for ex-
ample, by causing a decrease in the ability of cracks to interact because of local relaxation of their
stress concentrations, or, a tendency for cracks to remain corralled along grain boundaries and thus
not form larger, through-going cracks. If the stress were higher, the potential for cracks to over-
come these local relaxations would be greater. We can cast these different paths as a competition
between temperature and stress in the patterns of dissipation of free energy, as the samples enter
the brittle-ductile transition, to be articulated in the GSM framework discussed in Section 3.3.2.
 This result is reminiscent of the behavior of synthetic earthquakes in a damage rheology model
(Ben-Zion and Lyakhovsky, 2006): at low temperatures, the system dissipates energy through
brittle failure cascading (i.e., AE clustering) whereas at higher temperatures ductile processes take
over. Monitoring AE clustering throughout time gives us insights into the propensity of a system
for brittle failure cascading along a certain temperature/stress path. How do these patterns depend
on heating rate, on present damage state, and on other aspects of a rock’s structure?
 Our unsupervised feature extraction method aims to find patterns in complex signals that we
can relate to the mechanical behavior of the system at given temperature and stress conditions.
While the CRAM patterns in e1 are fairly subtle, we expect that they will be quite different from
those in an experiment that approached and reached failure, just as the fracture networks shown
in Fig. 5 are very different between the sample that failed and the one that did not. One of our
broader aims is to move from unsupervised pattern discovery to the determination of labels that
are characteristic of the state of a system and its near-future evolution, building on the pioneering
work of Rouet-Leduc et al. (2017).

  3.3.1 Background: Thermodynamics for constitutive model development
Open, deforming systems (such as fracturing solids with heat transfer and fluid flow) require con-
stitutive models that obey the laws of thermodynamics, for example, by not allowing energy dissi-
pation greater than the local available free energy (the mechanical equivalent of allowing heat ex-
traction greater than the available thermal energy). The appropriateness of any formulation relies
upon identifying the length- and time-scales over which one can define useful “quasi-static” ap-
proximations for average properties. Thermodynamics of Irreversible Processes (TIP) formalisms
have been developed in two broad classes, linear and non-linear. In the linear TIP (e.g., Prigogine,
1965), irreversible processes (such as fluid flow, heat flow and chemical diffusion) are described
by a flux, which is the product of a thermodynamic force and a coefficient relating the two, such
as viscosity, thermal and chemical diffusivities, permeability, etc. For deforming solids, the lin-
ear TIP is generally insufficient because of common relationships between forces and fluxes, such
as a stress-dependent viscosity. The Generalized Standard Materials (GSM) formalism provides
a framework for non-linear TIP specifically developed for solid mechanics (e.g., Halphen and
Nguyen, 1975; Germain et al., 1983). It has long been applied to analysis of creep and fatigue
experiments on metals, plastics and other engineering materials by A. Chrysochoos’ group at the
Université de Montpellier, France. Thermoelastic problems are a classic application of the GSM;

 19

 [FINAL REPORT: DOE BES Grant DE-SC-0024678]

for example, Chrysochoos (2012) demonstrated that thermal dissipation alone can be confused for
inelastic mechanical dissipation. Larger strain problems with fracture, damage and plasticity are
also common applications (e.g., Daridon et al., 2011).
 As an illustration, with state variables vs as temperature, total strain, viscous strain and internal
microstructural variables, vs = [T, ε, εv , αi ], one would construct a Helmholtz free energy (HFE)
potential Ψ(vs ) that governs thermal-mechanical energy along with that in the internal variables,
and a dissipation pseudo-potential Φ(v̇s ) that governs all means of energy dissipation, such as
heat flow and irreversible deformation. “State laws” and “evolution laws” are derived from the
potentials, with certain constraints on the dissipation potential (positive, convex, and zero-valued
at equilibrium). REV constitutive laws derived in this framework will satisfy the second law of
thermodynamics when solved in a continuum model.
 In collaboration with A. Chrysochoos and L. Daridon, PI Holtzman applied this framework for
analysis of grain size evolution (Holtzman et al., 2018a) in high temperature conditions of mantle
convection. In this project, we are applying the GSM to thermo-elastic coupling with damage to
help understand the processes occurring in our experiments. As discussed in Section 3.1, there
is strong coupling through effects of fracture (networks) on the elastic moduli, thermal expansion
coefficient (and to a lesser extent heat capacity). Since the aim of this project is to extend THMC
into the brittle-ductile regime, we will also include brittle and viscous creep, building upon our
initial efforts in the seed award and as discussed further below.
  3.3.2 GSM-REV model of thermal cracking with damage
The Generalized Standard Materials (GSM) formalism is very well-suited for building a strongly
coupled constitutive model for deforming rocks in the brittle-ductile transition. In this framework,
we start by defining the Helmholtz free energy potential:
                                     Ψ(T, ε, αi ) = Ψth−el + Ψαi                                  (1)
where the form of the linear thermo-elastic term, Ψth−el is well known, shown in Fig. 9c; the free
energy associated with the internal variables αi will be developed. The failure point on the surface
will be dependent on the path through this space, and is governed by the microstructural state
described by the internal variables. Defining these internal variables are the art of this approach
(not too few, not too many); we will be exploring the extent to which they can be related to the
fracture network properties. The free energy describes the ability of the system to drive dissipative
processes: by definition, the stress is the gradient of the potential surface with respect to strain,
illustrated in Fig. 9c.
 Next, we construct a dissipation pseudo-potential that describes all available disspative, irre-
versible processes. The dissipation relevant to our experiments, and brittle-ductile deformation
should minimally account for brittle (e.g. brittle creep), viscous, and plastic strain rates. as well
as dissipation associated with evolution of the internal variables. We also add a radiative term for
elastic/seismic wave energy that can be estimated in experiments with acoustic sensors:
                                  Φ(q, ε̇j , α̇i ) = Φε̇j + Φα̇i + ΦR .                           (2)
The radiative component is separated because it should not be contributing to internal entropy
generation or heat production, and also can be estimated relatively directly. As we will discuss
and illustrate above, the characteristics of the seismo-acoustic wavefield contain a lot of untapped
information that could inform us as to variations in the mechanism of fracture network evolution.
Using existing work on GSM Damage models, the forms of these two potentials can be derived,
with parameters estimated from fitting to data. They can also be machine-learned entirely from
data, the aim of the TIP-INN. The Clausius-Duhem (C-D) inequality, a combination of the first and

 20

 [FINAL REPORT: DOE BES Grant DE-SC-0024678]

second laws, defines the energy dissipation, further explained in (Germain et al., 1983; Holtzman
et al., 2018a). In the GSM, the C-D inequality is derived from both potentials to ensure that the
balance of stored and dissipated energy is always maintained, regardless of the couplings in every
quasi-static time-step.
GSM-potential-driven ODE solver In the seed grant, we developed a novel approach to solving
non-linear ordinary differential equations derived in the GSM (Mittal et al., prep) using the poten-
tials directly. The key benefit of this solver is that it easily allows us to test the system evolution
for a range of thermo-visco-elastic (with damage and multiple internal variables) rheological mod-
els with minimal code modifications. The solver, which uses auto-differentiation, (Nobel, 2020)
works as follows:
1. Define state variables (vs = Temp, total strain, viscous strain, temperature, and internal vari-
 ables), the Helmholtz free energy (HFE) potential (function of state variables, vs ), and dissipa-
 tion pseudo-potentials (DSP), which are functions of the rates of change (“fluxes”) of vs , and
 the dual DSP (dDSP), which are functions of the stress (“thermodynamic forces”).
2. The auto-differentiation (AD) process calculates the symbolic derivative functions of all the
 potentials with respect to their state variables, and same for the dissipation potentials to generate
 the AD objects. This leads to accurate evolution even for complex forcings.
3. Integration (e.g. strain control, with temperature forcing): The AD(HFE) object are evaluated at
 each time step to calculate the stresses or more generally, thermodynamic forces driving change
 (the gradients of the HFE with respect to its state variables at the time step). The AD(dDSP) are
 evaluated as a function of stress and vs to obtain the fluxes of the state variables, which are then
 updated for the next time step. Dissipation terms (products of forces and fluxes) are calculated
 using the C-D inequality to determine the temperature evolution (due to heat flow and internal
 entropy production), while accounting for any couplings (e.g., thermo-elastic) permitted in the
 constitutive model, obeying the 2nd law.

  3.3.3 Physics-informed, machine-learned constitutive models: The TIP-INN
One of the rapidly advancing forefronts of ML in science is aimed at data-driven discovery of math-
ematical models that describe complex physical processes (e.g., Karniadakis et al., 2021; Schmidt
and Lipson, 2009; Brunton et al., 2016b,a) A subset of neural network (NN)-based approaches
are focused specifically on learning/discovering constitutive or mechanical models of deforming
materials directly from data (e.g., Masi et al., 2021, 2020; Sun et al., 2021; Vlassis and Sun, 2021;
Karapiperis et al., 2021). Our seed grant supported PI Holtzman to work with T. Mittal (unfunded
collaborator) on the design for a NN architecture that would integrate the GSM to learn under-
lying free energy and dissipation potentials from data. We discovered a remarkable alignment in
the work of several groups, (e.g., Masi et al., 2020, 2021; Masi and Stefanou, 2023; Vlassis and
Sun, 2021; González et al., 2019; Marino et al., 2023; Flaschel et al., 2023), who are extending
the PINNs to incorporate a thermo-mechanical framework closely related to the GSM formalism
described above, referred to as “thermodynamics-informed artificial neural networks” (TANNs).
Another group (in biophysics) is focused on non-linear or hyper-elasticity (e.g., Linka and Kuhl,
2023). While they do not incorporate dissipation, they developed the use of convex activation func-
tions in the NNs to reflect directly the requirements that the thermodynamic potentials be convex.
 We call our new architecture the TIP-INN, or Thermodynamics of Irreversible Processes-
Informed Neural Network.The TIP-INN, by construction, will satisfy energy conservation prin-
ciples while accounting for history-dependent nonlinear behavior common in deforming materials,
by discovering the underlying potentials from data. However, our aims extend to developing a
learning framework that integrates macroscopic measurements (stress, strain) with supplemental
information on the internal state of the material encoded in the spectral-temporal patterns in the
elastic wave-field (in this case, acoustic emissions in the ultrasonic range). We use signal pro-
cessing and unsupervised learning methods to extract “features” of the wavefield, to find the most
meaningful information in a reduced form. The integration of acoustic information directly into
the learning process for constitutive models is novel, and has high potential to lead to major im-
provements in our understanding of deforming solids with a vast array of potential applications.
 The main TIP-INN architecture development along with the unsupervised feature extraction
methods are both primarily funded now by the Earthshot grant, in which the first application will
be to viscoelastic materials. With partial support from the seed grant (along with support from
Earthshot project), we have produced a “proto-TIP-INN” that successfully discovers the underlying
Helmholtz free energy (HFE) potential (Li et al., prep), constrained by using convex activation
functions (for HFE) and the Clausius-Duhem inequality in the loss function.
 As a basis for comparison, we also modify an existing, related approach called NICE (Neural
Integrator for Constitutive Equations) developed by Masi and Einav (2024). Instead of learning
a dissipation potential, they uses a neural ODE to learn the dissipative aspects of the constitutive
model (from the data). We modified their architecture to incorporate convexity, which we call
NICE-M (Fig. 11a). Our architecture builds upon NICE-M by adding another network that learns
a dissipation potential (Fig. 11b), which significantly improves the prediction of the free energy
(Fig. 11c). In the continuation project, we are developing and testing the full thermo-visco-elastic
TIP-INN. In the context of the proposed work, we will apply the TIP-INN to the experimental data,
to complement the forward approach above.

stress gradients. These experiments are valuable for understanding how to extrapolate results from
the very small samples (20 mm long) in the Paterson experiments to field conditions; understand-
ing how micro and meso-scale gradients form and migrate is essential for predicting macroscopic
effects of fracture network evolution. Even simple laboratory demonstrations of fracture network
propagation (not individual cracks) are rare, and to have a well constrained experiment to model
would be valuable. The aim of these experiments is to observe the dynamics of thermal crack prop-
agation in a sample, to see if any non-linear effects (couplings) emerge from the thermal cracking
process alone, in the absence of fluids and hydraulic forces.
 Under the grant, we did manage to develop an apparatus and methodology, illustrated in Fig.
13. The design evolved far from the original proposal, which involved heating the sample at one
end, and imaging the cracking front with acoustic sensors. The new design involves quenching
the sample, a negative thermal shock, rather than heating. The sample sits in a bracket that hold
high-temperature piezoelectric sensors on the sample face, and also a larger, more sensitive, low-
temperature, far-field sensor at the end of a ceramic wave-guide, shown in 13a. The procedure
will involve slowly heating the sample to minimize thermal cracking in a vertical furnace, and then
dropping the sample into a bucket of cold water. The thermal shock will create a tensional stress
gradient on crack tips. Continuous acoustic recordings from the multiple high-T sensors will be
used to image the cracking front, in the submerged part of the sample.
 At present, the power supply and furnace are operational, built at LDEO, Columbia University.
The brass plates of the sample assembly bracket have been machined, but the sensors are not yet
produced. We will continue the development of this experiment.
