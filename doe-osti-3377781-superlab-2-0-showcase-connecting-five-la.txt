Executive Summary
SuperLab 2.0 (a five-lab demonstration) is a collaborative, national-scale experiment showcasing
the coordination of geographically distributed energy assets in real time. The demonstration
integrates 25 physical and digital assets, spanning wind, photovoltaics, batteries, electrolyzers,
DC fast chargers, microgrid controllers, building automation systems, small modular reactors,
control centers, and gas turbines, across five U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) national
laboratories: National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR), Idaho National Laboratory, National
Energy Technology Laboratory, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, and Sandia National
Laboratories. These assets are unified using Energy Sciences Network (ESnet), a low-latency,
high-performance DOE network, and are controlled via a centralized energy controller hosted at
NLR's Advanced Research on Integrated Energy Systems facility. The demonstration validates
the ability to stress-test hybrid energy systems under dynamic scenarios to de-risk advanced
control strategies for greater resilience and flexibility.

SuperLab 2.0 showcased a major advancement in federated national laboratory collaboration,
enabling real-time, cross-laboratory experimentation to coordinate geographically dispersed
distributed energy resources using various communication protocols and networks. SuperLab 2.0
built on previous demonstrations conducted between NLR and Pacific Northwest National
Laboratory and between NLR and Idaho National Laboratory to connect diverse assets such as
distant protection devices, a small modular reactor simulator, and a high-temperature
electrolyzer. Previous demonstrations were based on a single connection between two labs with
minimal coordination challenges. The five-lab demonstration—which included a centralized
controller, distributed test beds across different geographical locations, and use of protocols-
based communication—represents a scenario closer to real-world grid operations that coordinate
resources across a region to meet system needs. This experiment studied how local distributed
energy resource controllers interact with a centralized energy controller during normal and
abnormal events to maintain reliability.

The SuperLab team across the five labs implemented a notional power system model equivalent
to transmission and distribution lines, represented by the data networks interconnecting the labs.
Each lab continuously exchanged local parameters (such as real and reactive power) from its
controller hardware-in-the-loop (CHIL) and power hardware-in-the-loop (PHIL) assets through a
centralized energy controller at NLR, enabling real-time interaction and coordination across
sites. By leveraging ESnet as the communication backbone, the team successfully operated the
distributed assets as a unified power system, with each bus represented by a different laboratory.
This setup mirrors how assets interact in real-world power systems across dispersed locations
with various protocols and latencies. At each lab site, assets were operated using their own local
controllers, which were coordinated through an overarching operation and control layer of
centralized energy controller, equivalent to how an energy management system orchestrates
assets across a regional or national grid.

SuperLab's federated connectivity utilized a digital real-time simulator type of gateway to
connect CHIL and PHIL assets between labs. To enable this federated connection through ESnet,
a deterministic network was established where latency variations were consistent. This

 v

consistency allowed the development of digital filters for the power system assets across CHIL
and PHIL interfaces to avoid unstable and unreliable grid conditions.

This report provides an overview of the cross-laboratory configuration and offers insights into
interconnecting geographically distributed research assets to test them as if they were co-located.
The experiment represents a step toward linking nine DOE national laboratories, enabling
nation-wide simulations that can address utility-driven challenges with grid resilience, flexibility,
and modernization.

1 Introduction
SuperLab began in 2017 with eight participating laboratories from national labs and universities across
two continents, leveraging digital real-time simulator (DRTS) systems connected over the internet for
pioneering energy system demonstrations. While the early demonstration proved successful in asset
integration via virtual private networks, it also revealed significant latency challenges caused by
geographic distances and variable delays. To overcome this, the National Laboratory of the Rockies
(NLR) established connections using the U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) Energy Sciences
Network (ESnet), drastically reducing latency and enhancing the reliability of simulations. In December
2021 NLR researchers, Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) researchers, and ESnet staff
collaborated to demonstrate this enhanced capability during a live microgrid experiment in Cordova,
Alaska. Building on this success, NLR expanded efforts to demonstrate hybrid energy systems that
combined diverse energy resources, including variable generation and nuclear energy, that can be
optimized in response to grid conditions to support reliability. These milestones led to the formalization
of SuperLab 2.0, which connected five national laboratories—NLR, Idaho National Laboratory (INL),
Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL), Sandia National Laboratories (Sandia), and National
Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL)—through ESnet to explore solutions to enhance grid reliability.
SuperLab 2.0 primarily focused on coordinating the operation of geographically dispersed distributed
energy resources (DERs) and loads such as electric vehicle (EV) loads, thermal loads, and storage
systems.

The primary goal of SuperLab 2.0 was to develop a control platform that emulates the basic
functionality of a grid control center. This platform was designed to enable real-time communication,
control, and optimal coordination of emerging grid scenarios (e.g., mixed energy resources and loads). It
facilitated the coordination of diverse energy resources and loads, including microgrids, small modular
reactors (SMRs), building loads, DCFC, and hybrid energy plants, particularly in response to system
disturbances.

SuperLab 2.0 showcased a complex simulation of diverse assets responding to grid conditions,
leveraging a notional power system model and integrating geographically distributed assets across five
labs. These assets were real generation sources and loads, equipped with local energy controllers and
power conversion systems, that interacted with DRTS models, were digitally scaled in a DRTS
environment, and communicated with centralized energy controllers operating a notional power system
at NLR.

A communication architecture was established to enable seamless data exchange and coordinated
operation across all participating laboratories. The system incorporated a combination of legacy
communication protocols like TCP/IP, UDP, Modbus, and DNP3 to ensure data exchange among
diverse assets and platforms. Each laboratory operated its DRTS at a time step of 50 microseconds,
allowing high-fidelity simulation of transient dynamics. Local energy controllers at each site
continuously exchanged equivalent P–Q (real and reactive power) information every few milliseconds,
maintaining synchronization across the distributed network.

These local controllers interacted with a centralized energy controller hosted at NLR, which dispatched
operational commands to the emulated grid platform. The centralized energy controller coordinated the
distributed assets in real time, leveraging ESnet to continuously transmit energy status updates. This
configuration enabled accurate execution of setpoints and stable system response under both normal and

 1

abnormal grid conditions, effectively demonstrating the coordinated operation of geographically
dispersed assets as a unified, high-fidelity power system.

2 Resource Coordination
To support the SuperLab 2.0 five-lab demonstration project, NLR implemented a centralized energy
controller hosted at the Advanced Research on Integrated Energy Systems (ARIES) facility. Logical
decisions were made as predefined control actions in response to the grid conditions explored in the
demonstration.

 Figure 1. A notional power system network representing five-lab connections.
The centralized controller functioned as the operational hub for distributed assets across five national
laboratories integrated into a unified energy system connected to a notional 500-MW power grid network.
Leveraging the DRTS platform, the NLR team developed a digital twin that emulated realistic grid
conditions, enabling the virtual coordination of DERs, thermal generators, hydrogen systems, microgrids,
and diverse load profiles as if they were connected on a single regional network, as shown in Figure 1.
Importantly, the simulation platform maintained a fixed, synchronized communication rate among all
assets, closely mirroring the deterministic data exchange patterns observed in actual transmission and
distribution networks.

The ARIES DRTS platform was equipped with outfacing network interface cards, such as GTNETx2
simulator network interface cards from RTDS Technologies, which supports various protocols such as
SKT (TCP/UDP), Modbus, DNP, SV, and GOOSE, to interface with other national laboratory assets. This
enabled seamless, real-time communication with the assets hosted at each national laboratory.
Standardized communication protocols, including Modbus and TCP/UDP, were implemented over
secured, deterministic IP channels established through ESnet. This network architecture ensured secure,
low-latency, and reliable data transfer between geographically dispersed sites and NLR, enabling local
controllers at each laboratory to interact dynamically with the centralized energy controller. The next
section details 25 assets integrated from each participating laboratory, representing a diverse set of energy
resources that can reflect the complexities of a modern electric grid.

3 Laboratory Assets
Each lab’s assets and capabilities in SuperLab 2.0 were selected based on the lab’s domain expertise,
available physical and emulated infrastructure, and their ability to interface with the centralized energy
controller hosted at NLR. The NLR has one of the largest DRTS environments in the DOE national
laboratory network. The facility includes 27 DRTS racks (82 RTDS cores, 31 Opal-RT cores, and 7
Typhoon hardware-in-the-loop platforms) to enable regional grid-scale electromagnetic transient
simulation. The lab’s hardware also includes over 5,000 Internet of Things CHIL controllers, 44 real-
time CHIL Kria boards, 12 linear amplifiers (15 kW each), and 200 next-generation advanced metering
infrastructure meters, enabling hardware-level experimentation with AC/DC distributed grid automation
and controls, protection schemes, communication architectures, AI-driven model training, testing, and
other grid applications.

Moreover, NLR leveraged its ARIES assets for SuperLab 2.0, including a high-fidelity test bed that
integrates a 1.5-MW wind turbine, two photovoltaics (PV) systems totaling 3 MW, a 1-MWh lithium-
ion battery energy storage system (BESS), a 0.8-MW DCFC, and a 1-MW low-temperature electrolyzer
(LTE) operating in a PHIL and CHIL configuration. All assets are interconnected via a 7-MVA
controllable grid interface, enabling real-time multi-node control and synchronized interlaboratory
operations. These assets were chosen to represent DERs and grid-edge resources such as inverter-based
resources, storage, and hydrogen production systems. They were integrated into the power system
network in the RTDS/RSCAD simulation to support fast control and dispatch via GTNET interfaces.
Within the overarching control architecture, NLR hosts the central energy controller at its ARIES
facility, which communicates with all labs over ESnet using deterministic low-latency channels.
Locally, NLR assets operate under CHIL and PHIL configurations and respond to setpoints in real time.
Through digital scaling, NLR’s assets represent 61 MW of wind, 79 MW of PV, 20 MW of LTE, 7 MW
of DC fast charging, and a 50-MWh battery system. These scaling values were selected to emulate the
behavior of a large DER cluster, with power levels reflecting real-world scenarios such as feeder-scale
solar, depot-scale EV charging, and utility-scale battery dispatch.

At INL’s Human System Simulation Laboratory, the experiment integrates an SMR with high-
temperature electrolyzer (HTE) models to explore flexible nuclear/hydrogen hybrid operations. The
SMR, modeled for up to 300 MW of output, can ramp down to 50% capacity in 10 minutes with a 1.05-
MW/s ramp rate. Paired with a 25-kW HTE model for efficient “pink hydrogen1” production, the system
is digitally scaled to 220 MW for the SMR and 100 MW for the HTE, enabling real-time coordination
between nuclear power generation and hydrogen production. These assets were chosen for their
relevance in modeling next-generation hybrid nuclear/hydrogen systems, offering dispatchable baseload
power and scalable hydrogen output. INL's capabilities were tied together using UDP, allowing seamless
integration with the centralized energy controller at NLR. Within the overarching control scheme, INL
operates under a CHIL configuration, receiving dispatch instructions from NLR and responding with
real-time simulation data. The power scaling, 220 MW for SMR and 100 MW for HTE, was chosen by
anticipated commercial deployment targets and the need to realistically simulate regional-scale flexible
generation and hydrogen co-production strategies.

1
  pink hydrogen refers to hydrogen produced through water electrolysis powered by nuclear-generated electricity
(and, where applicable, nuclear heat), resulting in low-carbon and high-efficiency hydrogen production.

NETL’s assets combine a stand-alone 120-kW gas turbine with a solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC)–gas
turbine (GT) hybrid system, connected to a 120-kVA load bank for dynamic stability analysis. The
SOFC-GT hybrid achieves 70% fuel-to-electricity efficiency with a 50% load turn-down capability in
under 10 seconds, operating on natural gas or hydrogen. A solid oxide electrolyzer cell (SOEC)-GT
hybrid system also serves as a controllable load optimized for high-efficiency hydrogen production.
These assets were selected to study high-efficiency fuel-hydrogen-electricity integration and were tied
together through a co-simulation environment in Opal-RT where NETL assets interface directly with the
centralized controller at NLR using TCP/IP communication protocols. The lab operates within the
SuperLab 2.0 architecture under a PHIL configuration, receiving setpoints and sending telemetry in real
time. Power values were scaled to a 30-MW SOFC-GT, a 30-MW SOEC hydrogen production system,
and a 30-MW dynamic load, representing modular distributed power systems capable of flexible
peaking support or grid-following operations. These values reflect commercially relevant scales and
allow accurate modeling of start-up dynamics, load shifts, and hydrogen response curves.

At LBNL, SuperLab 2.0 connected building-level assets, including controllable heating, ventilating, and
air-conditioning systems; lighting; plug loads; and building envelope controls. Occupancy sensor data
informs dynamic load-shedding strategies, while behind-the-meter DERs such as three Tesla
Powerwalls, a bidirectional Nissan Leaf EV, and three SolarEdge inverters support managed charging
and discharging. These capabilities were chosen to emulate smart building-to-grid interactions and were
tied together through local automation controllers that communicated with NLR’s central energy
controller via Modbus TCP. Within the overarching architecture, LBNL functions as a demand
flexibility node, dynamically shifting loads and DER behavior based on real-time system needs. The
total digital scale—1 MW of PV, 2.5 MW of BESS and EVs, and 3 MW of building loads—was
upscaled to a 30-MW combined generation and 30-MW building load system, simulating a large
commercial facility or campus-scale net load center. This scaling allows LBNL to demonstrate how
building DERs can contribute to grid stability and load balancing under coordinated control.

At Sandia’s Distributed Energy Technologies Laboratory, SuperLab 2.0 integrated variable generation
and non-variable generation assets into a microgrid managed by an advanced grid controller. The lab
features a 0.9-MW wind turbine scaled to 1 MW, a 0.075-MW BESS scaled to 28 MW, and a 2-MW
diesel generator scaled to 1 MW for resilient backup. Additionally, a modeled 0.6-MW load (i.e., mixed
load representing residential and industrial customers) is scaled to 30 MW. These assets were selected to
represent a black-start-capable microgrid with diverse generation types and fast-responding storage.
They are tied together via Sandia’s microgrid controller and integrated into the distributed energy
system through Modbus-based communication and real-time data exchange over ESnet. Within the
control architecture, Sandia operates as an autonomous microgrid node capable of both grid-tied and
islanded operation modes. The digital scaling of assets—28-MW BESS, 30-MW load, and 9-MW
wind—was intended to mimic critical microgrid infrastructure and stress-test high-penetration inverter-
based systems under grid events such as frequency dips or voltage instability.

Together, these asset integrations across five national laboratories, enabled through low-latency ESnet
communication, real-time CHIL/PHIL interfaces, and centralized energy control at NLR, represent an
advancement in modeling and simulating distributed and hybrid energy systems under various grid
scenarios. The deliberate scaling of assets ensures each lab meaningfully contributes to realistic
simulations of 500 MW of distributed generation and 468 MW of dynamic load, allowing the system to
respond to grid disruptions and evaluate future grid strategies with high fidelity. The detailed breakdown
of assets by lab is shown in Table 1.

4 Connectivity via DOE Office of Science’s ESnet
The initial SuperLab connection successfully linked national laboratories across vast distances,
pioneering a new era of collaborative energy research. However, this early achievement came with a
significant challenge: latency. In the first SuperLab experiment, while asset integration across
geographically separated labs was technically possible, the unpredictable and variable latency in data
transfer over standard internet connections introduced jittering effects. These latency issues made
coordinated asset control difficult and, at times, unrealistic compared to actual energy system operations.
This problem was particularly critical in dynamic grid environments, where generation and load
conditions fluctuate rapidly, requiring precise and timely control within a few cycles on the order of
100–500 ms, which can capture the behavior of fault scenarios, loss-of-load events, and loss-of-
generation events. The issue was clearly demonstrated in an early NLR-INL SuperLab 1.0
demonstration with latency measurements of maximum 369 ms, minimum 20 ms, average 27.15 ms, and
with a variance of 11.5 ms, as shown in Figure 2. Such wide-ranging and unpredictable latencies
severely impacted the accuracy and reliability of experiments, making it impossible to truly replicate
real-world grid behavior.

 Figure 2. Latency measurements in SuperLab 1.0 demonstration between NLR and INL.
Recognizing the need for a robust solution, the team set out to find a way to correct these latency
challenges and enable more realistic, synchronized experiments. Their search led to the DOE’s ESnet—
a high-performance, dedicated scientific network. ESnet, renowned for supporting critical global
scientific endeavors such as the Large Hadron Collider, offered the stability and speed necessary for
SuperLab’s goals. When ESnet was paired with the On-Demand Secure Circuits and Advance
Reservation System (OSCARS)—a tool for reserving high-speed network resources—the team could
ensure consistent, low-latency communication during experiments.

This approach led NLR to reestablish SuperLab connections among geographically distributed labs via
ESnet (Figure 3). Consistent, deterministic communication with predictable latency, enhanced further by

 7

perfSONAR (performance Service-Oriented Network Monitoring Architecture), which is a network
measurement toolkit designed to provide federated coverage of paths and help to establish end-to-end
usage expectations and monitoring system at each site. This led the researchers to fully understand
network performance at each lab site and track down delays and packet drops behind each site’s
network. Each lab’s IT support was able to resolve issues with help from the ESnet team.

  Figure 3. SuperLab 2.0 showcase demonstrating five national laboratories working as one integrated
 energy system.
ESnet integration enabled the connection between PNNL and NLR to dramatically improve in 2023 with
latency parameters dropping to a maximum of 24.2 ms, a minimum of 23.6 ms, an average of 23.90 ms,
and a low variance of 0.02 ms. Results are shown in Figure 4. This reliable, stable communication
across national laboratories was critical for enabling accurate, synchronized experiments over large
distances.

Variance in latency was mitigated by establishing local filters within the notional power system running
in a DRTS environment at NLR and compensated parameters utilized in dispatching commands from the
centralized energy controller.

The improvements in network performance reduced variability in the connections to make it more
deterministic for real-time grid emulations within DRTS, enabling high-fidelity simulations at fixed time
steps as low as 50 µs for dynamic, real-time experimentation locally at each lab site. This setup allowed
simulation of complex grid scenarios involving mixed energy resources, including load and generation
fluctuations, across regional grids. It also facilitated the evaluation of how well centralized grid
controllers can coordinate with distributed assets to meet dynamic operational needs during unexpected
grid events. Furthermore, SuperLab 2.0 supports the analysis of fast transients such as inverter responses
and frequency excursions with local event resolution on the order of microseconds and end-to-end
control coordination within 25–50 ms.

One of the key challenges encountered during the five-lab demonstration was the integration of multiple
DRTS platforms (RTDS, OPAL-RT, and Typhoon) in use across the participating laboratories. Each of
these platform’s interfaces differently with CHIL and PHIL assets, creating interoperability and
synchronization challenges. To address this, the team used legacy communication protocols to establish
standardized interfaces between each lab’s local controllers and the centralized controller at NLR. This
approach ensured consistent data exchange and fixed communication intervals across all DRTS
platforms, as detailed in Section 5.

5 Demonstration of Use Cases
After establishing the unified simulation environment through ESnet with the centralized energy
controller at NLR's ARIES facility, as shown in Figure 5, the project team planned a SuperLab 2.0
demonstration to showcase the value and potential of an integrated energy system under realistic
operating conditions. This demonstration aimed to highlight the system’s ability to address key
challenges faced by utilities, including real-time coordination of DERs, dynamic load balancing, and
grid resilience during extreme events. It modeled three scenarios that reflect critical situations ranging
from abrupt spikes in electricity demand to significant losses in electrical load that have historically
challenged utility operators and grid stability.

The key challenge was replicating a real-world utility environment while leveraging geographically
distributed national laboratory grid assets. The team developed a notional power system model based on
the standard IEEE 14-bus system, which was modified to integrate each lab’s physical and simulated
assets at designated bus locations. Each lab operated its assets through local controllers connected to
their respective DRTS platforms. These platforms communicated in real time with a centralized DRTS
environment hosted at NLR, where all buses were combined into a single, unified power system model.
The centralized energy controller at NLR dispatched setpoints to each local controller through the DRTS
interfaces via dedicated ESnet connections, enabling coordinated operation across all sites. This
architecture allowed geographically distributed assets—spanning multiple national laboratories—to
interact dynamically as part of a single, integrated power system, effectively demonstrating large-scale
coordination across diverse hardware and simulation environments. It also addressed challenges such as
integrating heterogeneous assets across multiple laboratory sites; co-optimizing electricity, thermal
generation/load, and hydrogen systems; and ensuring interoperability across various protocols and
control platforms during different scenarios such as sudden load surges, generation intermittency, and
demand-side flexibility. SuperLab 2.0 demonstrated how the electric utility industry can leverage a
scalable, low-latency, multi-lab capability to study grid stability, flexibility, and operational reliability in
increasingly complex conditions with centralized energy controllers.

5.1 Scenario 1: Blue Sky Scenario With Inverter-Based Resources
This scenario represented a steady-state operating condition under high penetration of DERs. It is
designed to emulate a typical day (i.e., “blue sky” operation) in a power system where inverter-based
resources and flexible assets must be managed in a coordinated and balanced manner. The goal was to
baseline performance and system coordination under normal conditions to establish a reference point to
evaluate against more extreme or disruptive scenarios.

The response actions across all participating labs in this scenario were pre-scripted within the centralized
energy controller at NLR to ensure a smooth demonstration within the limited time window. Although
the controller executed the sequence in real time, this pre-scripted approach was used only for this
demonstration and can be replaced with fully dynamic, real-time operation for future control center
applications. While the overarching control logic and setpoints were executed centrally in real time, each
lab’s local controllers operated accordingly to carry out asset-level responses, such as dispatching gas
turbines, SMRs, and BESS or curtailing loads based on received commands. This approach mimics real-
world energy management system interactions with distributed control systems. The run-time window of
SuperLab 2.0 driven by the hybrid energy controller is shown in Figure 6 in Section 5.4.

In the blue sky scenario, each lab operated under normal, steady-state conditions while maintaining

5.2 Scenario 2: Sudden Load Increase (Inspired by 2021 Texas Winter
 Event)
This scenario reflected real-world emergency conditions, such as the February 2021 Texas power crisis,
simulating a sharp and sudden rise in power demand. To maintain equilibrium, generation must
quickly ramp up when the load increases suddenly. The SuperLab 2.0 project demonstrated how a
diverse mix of assets, such as batteries, SMRs, thermal generation, and EV charging infrastructure, can
coordinate through a centralized controller to close the demand gap while maintaining voltage and
frequency stability. Like the Blue Sky scenario, the control actions were programmed into the
centralized energy controller but executed in real time. The control system sent synchronized setpoints
to each lab, with local control loops managing device-level interactions and responses. At NLR, regular
loads increased to 100% capacity, with an additional 125 MW of central load. To quickly compensate
for this surge in demand, the centralized energy controller sent a signal to BESS to dispatch power
rapidly, which was reflected in a visible rise in BESS output. Meanwhile, DCFC EV loads were
curtailed by 50%, reducing EV charging demand to approximately 4.55 MW (Figure 7, Texas Winter
Event). The central energy controller sent commands to INL to turn OFF HTE to prioritize essential
loads, while the SMR ramped up its output to meet the increased power requirements (Figure 8, Texas
Winter Event). LBNL decreased building loads power consumption and ramped up BESS power to
provide additional support to the grid as directed by the central controller (Figure 9, Texas Winter
Event). The centralized energy controller sent signals to the microgrid at Sandia to increase generation
by 80%, allowing the BESS to push significantly more power, ramping from 3 MW to 24 MW, into the
nominal power grid to stabilize the system (Figure 10, Texas Winter Event). In the Sandia microgrid,
wind generation and the diesel generator were not available during this time. NETL ramped up its gas
turbine, increasing generation from the SOFC, and placed its gas generator on active standby to meet
backup needs per commands received from centralized controller. Additionally, the SOEC reduced its
load dramatically from 15.5 MW (50%) to just 2 MW (7%), freeing up further capacity for critical grid
operations (Figure 11, Texas Winter Event).

5.3 Scenario 3: Sudden Load Drop (Inspired by 2003 East Coast Blackout)
This type of scenario simulated a cascading failure similar to the 2003 historic East Coast blackout by
simulating a sudden and unexpected drop in electricity demand. If such occurrences are not dealt with
right away, they may cause instability throughout the system, potentially leading to a regional blackout.
The SuperLab 2.0 project showcased how fast-responding assets, like batteries, fuel cells, electrolyzers,

 12

and controllable loads, can absorb excess energy and stop frequency overshoots, preventing grid
collapse. The response actions across all participating labs in this scenario were programmed within the
centralized energy controller at NLR. While the overarching control logic and setpoints were executed
centrally, each lab’s local controllers operated accordingly to carry out asset-level responses.

At NLR, the centralized load was reduced by 50%, corresponding to a 125-MW load drop. To balance
the surplus of available generation, the centralized energy controller sent a signal to LTE to ramp up to
100% operation, producing up to 20 MW of green hydrogen, while DCFC EV charging was
simultaneously increased to 100%, bringing the DCFC to full-rated capacity (7 MW) to absorb excess
power (Figure 7, East Coast Blackout). The centralized energy controller sent a signal to INL to respond
by turning on its HTE while the SMR remained active but gradually ramped down, effectively shifting
some of the reactor’s output into hydrogen production (pink hydrogen) for storage (Figure 8, East Coast
Blackout). The centralized controller sent signals to LBNL to increase the building loads to 100% by
boosting HVAC system usage and increasing charging rates to the site's BESS, thus raising electricity
consumption to match the available supply (Figure 9, East Coast Blackout). Sandia decreased the
microgrid's export power to just 10%, and the BESS was shifted into a charging mode, helping to absorb
excess generation within the microgrid and stored energy for future peak demand periods as commanded
by the central controller (Figure 10, East Coast Blackout). Finally, the centralized energy controller
directed NETL through command signals to increase its SOEC load to 100%, further utilizing surplus
grid power for hydrogen production and maintaining system balance during the load drop event (Figure
11, East Coast Blackout).

6 Key Findings From Five-Lab Demonstration
The SuperLab 2.0 five-lab demonstration highlighted advancements in connecting geographically
distributed energy assets into a cohesive, real-time, unified energy system. A major finding was the
critical importance of consistent and deterministic communication between laboratories to enable
both local and centralized filter compensation, which are essential for nullifying parameter offsets and
maintaining time-synchronized asset control across each lab and the centralized energy controller at
NLR. In the initial SuperLab 1.0 experiment, significant latency and jitter up to 369 ms introduced
inaccuracies and made synchronized control of dynamic energy resources nearly impossible to induce
fault-driven response. These delays compromised the realistic integration of distributed assets and
stability of the experiments, especially during scenarios simulating fluctuating grid conditions in real
time. The new information learned from the five-lab demonstration includes the interaction of various
timescale devices such as the gas turbine at NETL, SMR command and control at INL, hydrogen
electrolyzer and BESS at NLR, DC microgrid at Sandia, and building management at LBNL as if they
were in a real-world setting, facilitated by ESnet. ESnet served as the network equivalent to those
connections, with all real-time interactions occurring at the local labs while the ESnet connection
mimicked the transmission/distribution lines. This setup is as close to a real-world environment as
possible, capturing the CHIL and PHIL physics interactions. Although it does not capture real-world
physics entirely, it is much more realistic than simple simulations.

The integration of ESnet and the OSCARS circuit reservation system proved transformative by
transitioning away from standard internet connections to high-speed, dedicated scientific networks. This
brought real-world fidelity to the experiments, allowing distributed laboratories to function as if they
were side-by-side in a real grid environment, a critical advancement in modeling transmission and
distribution operations under dynamic conditions. With ESnet, we were able to achieve more
deterministic connections, and utilizing ESnet's Science Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) approach ensured
that the connection latency remained consistent. This determinism allowed researchers to predict
computational steps ahead or behind specific points, leading to the development of more sophisticated
computational filters.

A key administrative best practice identified during the project was the standardization of
Interconnection Security Agreements (ISAs). By making ISAs generic for all future power and energy
asset remote connectivity standards, we have simplified the process and accelerated agreement
approvals. This approach is crucial for any laboratories looking to connect seamlessly and effectively in
the future. However, there is room for improvement. The ESnet team and the SuperLab team are
currently working to make the ISAs not only generic but also multiyear, ensuring they are sustainable
and more efficient for future projects. More work is needed to fully accomplish this goal, but the
ongoing efforts are a significant step toward streamlining the administrative process and fostering
smoother collaborations for remote connectivity.

Another key finding was that, in comparison to traditional modeling and simulation, leveraging
synchronized, high-fidelity asset control connections using CHIL and PHIL with actual assets enables
more realistic and actionable insights on grid asset interactions. This approach provides a higher level of
fidelity and realism, making the findings more applicable to real-world scenarios and allowing
researchers to better understand and optimize the dynamics of grid asset behavior. Assets from five
national labs (NLR, INL, LBNL, Sandia, and NETL) were scaled digitally and coordinated across
ESnet, simulating integrated energy systems that included wind, solar PV, BESS, SMRs, HTE, gas

 16

turbines, fuel cells, microgrids, EV chargers, and building-level controllable loads. This created the
ability to study complex behaviors such as flexible nuclear/hydrogen operations, electrical-thermal
coordination, building-to-grid interactions, and grid-forming inverter control under grid event scenarios.

The SuperLab 2.0 demonstration was positively received by utility stakeholders, with several
participants inquiring about how utilities can actively engage in addressing challenges specific to their
day-to-day operations. Utility feedback was collected during and following the demonstration,
highlighting the significant interest in SuperLab 2.0’s potential to de-risk the deployment of emerging
grid technologies. Industry representatives emphasized this benefit during the demonstration. Future
utility engagement will be pursued with the approval of DOE program managers to ensure coordinated
and effective collaboration.

By creating a virtual yet highly realistic environment through SuperLab 2.0, researchers, industry
partners, and utilities can address modern grid challenges without duplicating infrastructure investments.
SuperLab 2.0 fosters collaborative innovation, validates new operational strategies, and enables
scenario-based stress-testing of integrated energy systems.

In essence, the SuperLab 2.0 project validated that geographically distributed yet synchronized
assets and a virtual grid can serve as a platform to address evolving energy challenges without the
limitations of physical proximity.

7 Conclusion
The SuperLab 2.0 project validated a scalable and replicable framework for de-risking new grid
technologies and distributed energy systems without the need for massive physical infrastructure
investments. It demonstrated that geographically distributed laboratories could operate as a cohesive
integrated energy system under real-world grid dynamics, enabling complex system-level interactions
that can support future energy resilience strategies.

SuperLab 2.0 expanded on the initial SuperLab demonstration to integrate diverse energy resources from
five national laboratories. This demonstration presented a model for operating geographically
dispersed assets under a unified grid condition, replicating complex interdependencies of real-world
systems.

By leveraging DRTS and communication networks that operate at the same speed as those used in
CHIL/PHIL setups, we were able to closely replicate real-world environments. This includes
interactions between connected grid assets and real communication protocols. The result is a more
realistic emulation, capturing power system dynamics at 50-µs resolution and communication delays
that closely mirror real-world conditions.

By achieving consistent and deterministic communication alongside synchronized asset control,
SuperLab 2.0 opens new avenues for collaboration among national laboratories, utilities, and industry
stakeholders. This platform can accelerate technology validation, facilitate the integration of diverse and
distributed energy resources, and foster robust solutions for extreme weather events and grid
contingencies.

To further broaden its impact, SuperLab 2.0 plans to integrate additional national laboratories
specializing in high-performance computing, next-generation power conversion technologies, and
innovative grid-edge applications. Future development will also prioritize strong utility collaboration
frameworks, deploy targeted grid validation campaigns, and build a national repository of validated
control strategies for modern grid operations.
