Limited detection of Gulf War illness-like behavioral phenotypes in Charles River male Sprague-Dawley rats across multiple chemical and stress exposure paradigms

Rashelle Lashley, Joshua Brian Foster, Xueqin Wang, Albert John Muhleman, Samuel Weaver, Edison Lin, Liching Lai, Chien-liang Glenn Lin

ABSTRACT
Gulf War illness (GWI) is characterized by a range of neurological and neuropsychiatric symptoms, including chronic pain, fatigue, mood problems, and cognitive impairment. The pathophysiology is associated with war-related environmental chemical exposure and psychological stress, which are known to disrupt glutamatergic synaptic structure and function. The original objective of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of LH-001, an investigational compound designed to enhance tripartite glutamatergic synapses, in a rat model of GWI. We initially attempted to induce GWI-like behavioral phenotypes in Charles River male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats using a previously published protocol employed for male SD rats from Harlan involving exposure to Gulf War-related chemical agents and mild stress. Because robust behavioral phenotypes were not detected, we subsequently modified the induction protocol by increasing chemical exposure intensity and stress levels. Despite these adjustments, GWI-like behavioral phenotypes were not detected under the experimental conditions evaluated. To better interpret these findings, we compared our protocol and outcomes with previously published GWI rat studies. This comparison revealed substantial variability across studies in exposure paradigms, animal sources, timing of behavioral assessments, and reported outcomes. Collectively, our findings suggest that detection of GWI-like behavioral phenotypes in rats may be sensitive to experimental conditions and highlight the importance of continued efforts to validate, optimize, and standardize animal models to improve translational rigor and support preclinical therapeutic development.

Introduction Gulf War illness (GWI) is a chronic, multi-symptomatic condition affecting approximately one-third of veterans—around 250,000 people—who served in the 1990–1991 Gulf War [ 1 ]. GWI presents a wide range of symptoms that include fatigue, gastrointestinal disorders, and widespread pain, among others [ 2 ]. Neurological problems, including headache, sleep difficulty, anxiety, depression, memory deficit, and difficulty in concentration, are commonly reported symptoms [ 3 – 6 ]. Although the exact etiology of the disease remains unclear, it is widely believed that these clinical symptoms stem from a combination of war-related stress and exposure to a range of toxic chemical agents [ 7 – 10 ]. These chemical agents include pyridostigmine bromide (PB, administered as a prophylactic treatment against organophosphate nerve agents); pesticides (such as permethrin (PER) and N,N-diethyl-meta-toluamide (DEET)); chemical warfare agents (such as sarin); burn pits; byproducts of oil well fires; and depleted uranium. Current treatments for the complex disorder of GWI focus on managing symptoms; however, understanding its mechanisms is critical for developing more effective therapies. Growing evidence indicates that exposure to GW-related chemical agents can impair the structure and function of glutamatergic synapses in the central nervous system [ 11 ]. PB and certain Gulf War-related neurotoxic exposures, including organophosphate nerve agents such as sarin, have been associated with inhibition of acetylcholinesterase activity [ 12 ]. Under stress conditions, these exposures have been proposed to alter central neurotransmission and contribute to dysregulation of glutamatergic signaling, excitotoxicity, and synaptic dysfunction [ 13 , 14 ]. Air pollutants associated with burn pit exposure have also been proposed to contribute to neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, excitotoxicity, and disruption of glutamatergic signaling following systemic exposure [ 15 , 16 ]. Depleted uranium exposure has been associated with altered glutamate uptake, changes in glutamate receptor expression, neurochemical imbalance, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation [ 17 ]. Moreover, mounting evidence indicates that stress can cause dysregulation of glutamate transmission and losses of dendrites and their synapses [ 18 , 19 ]. Taken together, military-related toxic exposures may impair glutamatergic synapses, resulting in long-term structural and functional alterations in the brain and leading to neurological problems. Because synaptic dysfunction has been implicated in proposed mechanisms underlying GWI, we previously evaluated LDN/OSU-215111, a compound designed to enhance the structure and function of tripartite glutamatergic synapses, in a mouse model of GWI induced by Gulf War-related chemicals and stress. In that study, LDN/OSU-215111 improved behavioral deficits and normalized associated hippocampal abnormalities [ 20 ]. Subsequent studies identified LH-001, a metabolite of LDN/OSU-215111 with improved preclinical pharmacological properties. The original objective of the present study was to evaluate the effects of LH-001 in a rat model of GWI and determine whether enhancing tripartite glutamatergic synaptic function could influence GWI-related outcomes. To establish the model, we first applied a previously published rat exposure protocol developed for male Harlan-SD rats involving Gulf War-related chemicals and restraint stress, using Charles River SD rats [ 21 ]. Because expected GWI-like behavioral phenotypes were not observed, additional exposure paradigms incorporating modified chemical and stress conditions were subsequently evaluated. However, behavioral phenotypes consistent with GWI were still not detected under the experimental conditions used. Therefore, conclusions regarding the therapeutic efficacy of LH-001 cannot be drawn from the present study. Potential factors that may contribute to variability in GWI phenotype detection across experimental conditions and prior studies are discussed. Materials and methods Animals Male Sprague-Dawley rats were obtained from Charles River Laboratories (Raleigh, NC, USA) and used in all cohorts. Rat starting weights ranged from 225–275 g, at an estimated age of 7–8 weeks. Rats were left to habituate in the vivarium for one week before beginning the stress and chemical exposure protocol. Vivarium conditions were kept at a steady temperature (approximately 70°F) with a 12-hour room light/dark cycle. Rats were housed in groups or pairs depending on size and provided ad libitum access to food and water, except on days that required food or water deprivation. All procedures were done between 8 am and 6 pm. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of The Ohio State University following the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Experimental design In the original study design, we planned to use 24 rats of each sex per group, based on an assumed large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.8), a significance level (α) of 0.05, and 80% statistical power, following procedures adapted from Parihar et al. [ 21 ]. The study was designed to include four experimental cohorts, each consisting of 12 male or female rats per group. We initiated the study with Cohort 1, which included 48 male rats (12 rats per group). Animals were randomly assigned to one of four groups: control/vehicle (no GW exposure, vehicle treatment), control/LH-001 (no GW exposure, LH-001 treatment), GWI/vehicle (GW exposure, vehicle treatment), and GWI/LH-001 (GW exposure, LH-001 treatment). Rats in the GWI groups were exposed to GW-related chemicals (1.3 mg/kg PB, 0.13 mg/kg PER, and 40 mg/kg DEET) followed by 5-minute restraint stress on weekdays for five weeks (hereinafter referred to as the “low-dose/low-stress” protocol). Body weight was monitored weekly during the first six weeks. Beginning 4–5 weeks after completion of GW exposure, rats received either LH-001 or vehicle throughout the behavioral assessment period. Behavioral testing was initiated 8–9 weeks after exposure and included evaluation of anxiety-like behaviors (open field, light–dark transition, elevated plus maze, and novelty-suppressed feeding) and cognition (novel object location, novel object recognition, and Barnes maze). Investigators performing behavioral assessments were blinded to group allocation. Following completion of Cohort 1, expected GWI-like behavioral phenotypes were not detected ( Fig 1 ). Therefore, rather than proceeding directly with the remaining planned cohorts, we conducted additional exploratory studies to evaluate whether modifying exposure conditions would influence phenotype detection. First, we increased chemical exposure (13 mg/kg PB, 2.6 mg/kg PER, and 215 mg/kg DEET) while maintaining 5-minute restraint stress (“high-dose/low-stress” protocol) [ 22 , 23 ]. All behavior testing was performed between 10 a.m. and 6 p.m. Prior to each experiment, animals were habituated to the testing room ambient conditions for at least 30 minutes. The rats were then single caged for 10 minutes immediately before behavioral testing. After testing, each animal was returned to its home cage. The apparatus was cleaned with 70% ethanol between each animal to disrupt scent cues, and all apparatuses were cleaned with 10% bleach at the end of each day. Data were captured and analyzed using SMART software (Version 3.0.05, Panlab Harvard Apparatus®). All behavioral tests were based on our previous study, with modifications [ 20 ]. The details of these modifications are described in each test section below. Open field. Rats were placed in the center of a 100 cm x 100 cm x 40 cm open field apparatus. Open field trials ran for 10 minutes under low light (approximately 30 Lux) and were recorded. The percentage of time spent in the center and total distance traveled were measured and analyzed. Dark and light. Dark and light testing was done using a 100 cm x 100 cm x 40 cm open field apparatus. A divider and lid were placed to create a 100 cm x 70 cm light field and a 100 cm x 30 cm dark field within the apparatus. The divider contained a small hole for rats to pass between the two fields. The light field was set to approximately 350 Lux. Rats were placed in the light field, away from the entrance to the dark field, and allowed to explore for 10 minutes. The percentage of time in the light field and latency time to the dark field were measured and analyzed. Elevated plus maze. This apparatus consisted of a plus shaped platform measuring 122 cm x 122 cm and stood 1 m above the ground. Two arms were closed while two arms were open. General lighting was set with the open arms being brighter (approximately 80 Lux) than the closed arms (approximately 30 Lux). Rats were placed on the end of an open arm and allowed to explore for 10 minutes. The percentage of time spent in the open arms was measured and analyzed. Novelty-suppressed feeding. Novelty-suppressed feeding was done using Kellogg’s brand Froot Loops cereal. Rats were introduced to the cereal in their home cages two days prior to testing. Rats were food deprived overnight right before the test trial. These tests were done in a 100 cm x 100 cm x 40 cm open field under bright light conditions, approximately 80–90 Lux. A small dish of cereal was placed in the center of the field. Rats were allowed to freely explore the open field and interact with the cereal for 10 minutes. At the end of the 10 minutes, rats were returned to their home cages, and the dish of remaining food was weighed. The latency to first interaction with the cereal and the total percentage of cereal eaten were measured and analyzed. Novel object location. Rats were first habituated to the empty 100 cm x 100 cm x 40 cm arena for 10 minutes per day over two days. On day three, two identical objects were placed into the upper right and upper left regions of the arena. The rats were given 10 minutes to freely interact with the objects. On day four, the upper right object was moved to the lower right of the arena. Rats were again given 10 minutes to interact with the objects. Time spent with each object was measured. A discrimination index [(novel % time – familiar % time) / (novel % time + familiar % time)] was calculated to determine the relative interaction preference. Positive values indicate more interaction with the novel location object, negative values indicate more relative time spent interacting with the unmoved object, and zero represents equal interaction with both objects. Memory deficits are indicated by a decreased discrimination index relative to controls. To determine whether a rat was interacting with the objects, a 12 cm × 12 cm zone was defined around the novel object and its location. The rat’s head and upper body had to be within this target area and remain detectable by the camera and tracking software used for quantification. Novel object recognition. This test was an extension of the novel object location test. After the familiar object was moved on day four, the unmoved familiar object was replaced with a similar, novel object on day five. Animals were again allowed to freely explore the two objects in the arena for 10 minutes. Memory was then analyzed by generating a discrimination index, as described above, for the novel versus familiar object, with cognitive deficits defined as discrimination index scores significantly lower than controls. Barnes maze. The Barnes Maze apparatus was a 48-inch diameter round table (~1 m above the ground) with twenty 8 cm diameter holes equidistantly cut into the perimeter. One of these holes was chosen to have an escape box affixed below it that the animals were trained to find to escape the bright, open arena. Eight visual cues were placed around the table. Lighting was set to 80–90 Lux to induce stress. During the training phase, rats were placed in the center of the Barnes maze apparatus under an opaque cylinder. Once the cylinder was removed, rats were left to freely explore the maze for up to 5 minutes or until the target hole was found and entered the escape box. The rats were left in the escape box for 1 minute before being returned to the home cage. In the event the rat did not find the escape hole by the 5-minute mark, it was guided to the hole with a clear beaker and allowed to stay in the beaker, over the hole, for up to 2 minutes. If the rat still did not find the hole in the allotted time, it was gently nudged with the beaker for up to 1 minute. If the rat still did not enter the hole, it was placed in the hole. Once in the escape hole, the rat was left to rest in the escape box for 1 minute. After this minute was up, the rat was returned to the home cage, and the table was cleaned with 70% ethanol. The table was rotated a quarter turn clockwise in preparation for testing the next rat. This was repeated once daily for 4 days (Day 1–4). Probe trials were conducted on Day 8 (3 days after the last training trial). During the probe trial, the escape box was removed, and animals freely explored the maze for 2 minutes. The latency to find the target hole and the time spent in the quadrant containing the target hole were measured and analyzed. After the completion of Probe trial 1, the rats were trained to a new target hole with the same procedure, from Day 9–12. Probe trial 2 was conducted on Day 16, and the data were analyzed the same as Probe trial 1. Immunohistochemical analysis Immunohistochemistry was performed as previously described [ 20 ]. Statistical analysis We used GraphPad Prism (v5.01) to perform all data analysis and make all graphs. All n-values are shown in the figure legends. All body weight data were analyzed using a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with GW exposure (GW-exposed vs. control) as the between-subjects factor and time as the within-subjects factor, followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests where appropriate. Behavioral data from the low-dose/low-stress and low-dose/high-stress studies were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA with GW exposure (control vs. GWI) and LH-001 treatment (vehicle vs. LH-001) as independent factors, followed by Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons where appropriate. This analysis allowed assessment of the main effects of GW exposure and LH-001 treatment, as well as their interaction. If no significant main effects or interactions involving treatment were detected, data were subsequently collapsed across treatment groups to increase statistical power for testing the GW main effect. Comparisons between the combined “Control” and “GWI” groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed t-test. Behavioral data from the high-dose/low-stress study were analyzed using a one-way ANOVA, as this experiment included three groups (control/vehicle, GWI/vehicle, and GWI/LH-001). When the ANOVA was significant and assumptions of homogeneity of variance were met (assessed using Bartlett’s test), Tukey’s post hoc test was performed. If unequal variances were detected, a Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test was used. Data from predefined non-responders were excluded from behavioral datasets. In the low-dose/low-stress study, one control/vehicle rat, two control/LH-001 rats, one GWI/vehicle rat, and one GWI/LH-001 rat were classified as non-responders in the novel suppressed feeding test because they remained immobile at the edge of the testing box for the entire duration of the test. In the low-dose/high-stress study, one GWI/vehicle rat and one GWI/LH-001 rat were classified as non-responders in the novel object location test because they remained immobile at the edge of the testing box throughout the test. We examined the influence of these exclusions on the overall conclusions and found that the overall interpretation of the behavioral findings was unchanged. Immunohistochemical data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA, as described above. For these analyses, we used hippocampal sections from: 4 animals for DCX immunostaining (≥ 5 sections per animal); 4 animals for PV (≥ 4 sections per animal); and 3 animals for GFAP (≥ 3 sections per animal). Western blot data were analyzed by converting the signal to fold-change relative to the control vehicle. A one-sample t-test was used to compare these values to the theoretical value of 1.0. All data are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) with p-values less than 0.05 considered significant. [ 21 ]. Fig 1 shows the results from Cohort 1 (12 male rats per group; estimated power = 0.77 to detect a main effect with Cohen’s f = 0.4 at α = 0.05). Body weight data are shown in Fig 1A . Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA of body weight indicated no significant differences between GW-exposed and non-GW-exposed rats at any measured time point ( Fig 1A ). Anxiety-like behavioral data are shown in Fig 1B – 1H . GW-exposed rats did not exhibit anxiety-like behavior in the open-field test, showing no reduction in time spent in the center ( Fig 1B ) or total distance traveled ( Fig 1C ). Consistent results were observed in the elevated plus maze, where GW-exposed and control rats spent comparable time in the open arms ( Fig 1D ). Similarly, in the light–dark transition test, GW-exposed rats showed no decrease in latency to enter the dark compartment ( Fig 1E ) or time spent in the light compartment ( Fig 1F ). In the novelty-suppressed feeding test, GW-exposed rats did not differ from controls in food consumption ( Fig 1G ) or latency to feed ( Fig 1H ). Two-way ANOVA revealed no significant main effect of GW exposure across all anxiety-related behavioral tests. Cognitive assessment data are shown in Fig 1I – 1O . GW-exposed rats showed no deficits in recognition memory, discriminating effectively between objects in novel and familiar locations ( Fig 1I ) and between novel and familiar objects ( Fig 1J ) 24 h after initial exposure. In the Barnes maze, GW-exposed rats also exhibited normal spatial learning and memory, with no increase in latency to locate the target hole ( Fig 1K ) or decrease in time spent in the target quadrant during the probe test conducted one week after training ( Fig 1L ). GW-exposed rats also required similar time as controls to locate a newly trained target hole ( Fig 1M ) and spent comparable time exploring the new ( Fig 1N ) and previous target quadrants ( Fig 1O ). Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of LH-001 treatment in the novel object location test (F(1,44) = 4.088, p = 0.0493, ηp 2 = 0.085; Fig 1I ), suggesting that LH-001 treatment may enhance recognition of changes in object location regardless of GW exposure. However, post hoc Bonferroni tests did not reveal significant pairwise differences between groups. Two-way ANOVA also indicated a significant interaction between GW exposure and LH-001 treatment for time spent in the target quadrant in the Barnes maze (F(1,44) = 4.279, p = 0.0445, ηp 2 = 0.089; Fig 1L ), although Bonferroni post hoc comparisons were not significant. Control/LH-001 and GWI/vehicle groups showed a trend toward better spatial memory retention, whereas the GWI/LH-001 group showed performance similar to the control/vehicle group. Overall, these results indicate that low-dose/low-stress exposure did not produce detectable cognitive deficits, and LH-001 treatment showed a modest trend toward improving recognition or spatial memory. Above behavioral outcomes were analyzed using two-way ANOVA with GW exposure and LH-001 treatment as independent factors. Because neither a significant main effect of LH-001 treatment nor a significant interaction between GW exposure and LH-001 treatment was detected, pooled analyses combining the control/vehicle and control/LH-001 groups as “Control” and the GWI/vehicle and GWI/LH-001 groups as “GWI” were performed as exploratory analyses to facilitate visualization of overall GW exposure effects. These pooled analyses were not used for primary statistical inference. Consistent with the factorial analyses, pooled comparisons did not detect statistically significant differences between the Control and GWI groups across behavioral tests ( S5 File ; p > 0.05). The pooled sample size (n = 24 per group) provided high power to detect large effect sizes (estimated power = 0.94 for Cohen’s d = 0.8 at α = 0.05), but smaller effects cannot be excluded. High-dose/low-stress exposure study. In the absence of a clear phenotype in the initial GWI model, it is difficult to evaluate whether LH-001 has the potential to provide therapeutic efficacy for GWI. Therefore, we increased the doses of PB (13 mg/kg), PER (2.6 mg/kg), and DEET (215 mg/kg) [ 22 , 23 ] while maintaining the same five-minute restraint stress protocol (the “high-dose/low-stress exposure” protocol; Table 1 ). In this experiment, three groups were included: control/vehicle, GWI/vehicle, and GWI/LH-001, with the aim of determining whether higher chemical doses could induce measurable disease phenotypes. The same procedures and behavioral assessments described above were performed. Several explanations are possible. First, low-dose/high-stress exposure may sensitize neural circuits such as the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis or specific neurotransmitter systems (e.g., glutamatergic signaling), and LH-001 may act to normalize these stress-sensitized pathways. Third, LH-001 and the low-dose/high-stress exposure may act on the same biological system in opposite directions, such that the treatment effect becomes detectable only when stress elevates the system above a behavioral or neurochemical threshold. Discussion This study originally aimed to test the efficacy of compound LH-001, a novel synapse enhancer, in rat models of GWI. Unexpectedly, our initial GWI induction protocol in rats did not produce robust GWI-like behavioral phenotypes reported in previous studies [ 21 , 29 ]. After adjusting the induction protocol twice—once to a more potent chemical exposure and once to a higher level of stress we still did not detect robust GWI-like behavioral phenotypes under the experimental conditions used. These findings highlight the challenges of reproducing GWI-related phenotypes across experimental settings and underscore the importance of continued efforts to validate and standardize GWI animal models. These unexpected results prompted a closer examination of published GWI induction protocols. A comparison of our study with previous studies reporting behavioral outcomes is summarized in Table 3 . Several observations emerged from this comparison. First, considerable heterogeneity exists among published GWI protocols, including differences in chemical exposure regimens, stress paradigms, timing of behavioral assessments, animal sources, and other experimental conditions. Among the eleven published studies (Studies 2–12) summarized in Table 3 , only Studies 6 [ 30 ], 10 [ 31 ], and 12 [ 32 ] presented individual animal data, and Study 6 additionally provided raw behavioral data in the supplementary materials. Presentation of individual animal data alongside group summary statistics can improve transparency and facilitate interpretation of variability, response distributions, and potential outliers. Such reporting may also support cross-study comparisons and aid efforts to evaluate reproducibility across experimental settings. Third, an important observation from this study was that we did not detect robust GWI-like behavioral phenotypes in rats despite modifying the induction protocol to increase either chemical exposure intensity (Study 1b) or stress exposure (Study 1c). These findings suggest that, under the experimental conditions evaluated here, increasing chemical dose or stress level alone was insufficient to reproduce previously reported behavioral outcomes. However, because these variables were not systematically isolated and directly compared, we cannot exclude the possibility that they contributed to differences across studies. The timing of behavioral assessment is also unlikely to fully explain the observed findings. In Study 1c (low-dose/high-stress), behavioral testing was repeated at 6–8 months post-exposure following the initial assessment at approximately 3 months post-exposure, and robust GWI-like behavioral phenotypes were still not detected. The strain and source of rats are important considerations when interpreting variability across GWI studies. Of the 12 studies summarized in Table 3 , 10 used Sprague–Dawley rats and two used Wistar rats. Among the Sprague–Dawley studies, rat sources included Charles River (n = 2), Harlan/Envigo (n = 5), Taconic Farms (n = 1), and two studies that did not report the source. Although Sprague–Dawley rats are widely used in preclinical research, increasing evidence indicates that animals obtained from different vendors represent genetically and physiologically distinct populations rather than interchangeable stocks. Charles River and Harlan/Envigo Sprague–Dawley rats have undergone separate breeding histories and exhibit measurable differences in genetic background, physiology, metabolism, and responses to experimental challenges [ 33 ]. Such vendor-dependent differences have been reported to influence behavioral phenotypes, pharmacological responses, susceptibility to neurological injury, and other biological outcomes [ 34 ]. In the present study, rats were obtained from Charles River Laboratories, whereas several previous studies reporting GWI-like behavioral phenotypes used Sprague–Dawley rats obtained from Harlan/Envigo. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that vendor-related differences contributed to differences in observed outcomes across studies. However, because our study did not directly compare rat vendors under identical experimental conditions, our data do not support concluding that Charles River Sprague–Dawley rats are inherently unsuitable for GWI modeling or that vendor source alone explains the observed outcomes. Additional factors—including housing conditions, animal handling procedures, environmental conditions, exposure paradigms, timing of behavioral testing, and other laboratory-specific variables—may also influence phenotype expression. Our study employed a 5-day-per-week exposure schedule rather than continuous daily exposure protocols used in several previous studies. This schedule was selected to maintain consistency in animal handling, chemical administration, monitoring, and husbandry procedures throughout a prolonged study period. Nevertheless, intermittent exposure may differ from continuous exposure in cumulative exposure burden, pharmacokinetic profiles, recovery between dosing periods, and temporal development of behavioral and biological responses. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that this procedural difference contributed to variability in phenotype expression. Another procedural consideration is the source of chemicals used for exposure. Although PB, DEET, and permethrin were obtained from research-grade commercial suppliers, we did not independently verify purity, stability, or potency. Therefore, lot-to-lot or vendor-related variation cannot be excluded as a contributor to differences in effective exposure across studies. Finally, post-exposure housing conditions following topical chemical administration may also influence effective exposure. In the present study, animals were individually transferred to the procedure room under ambient environmental conditions for chemical administration and subsequently returned to their home cages and allowed to remain undisturbed for 5 minutes to permit initial dermal absorption. No additional airflow or procedural intervention was applied during this post-exposure period. Nevertheless, because dermal retention and systemic exposure were not directly measured, we cannot exclude the possibility that post-exposure environmental or husbandry factors contributed to variability across studies. It is important to note that the power analyses presented in the Results section were based on detecting large effect sizes and were informed by our previous findings in mice [ 20 ]. Accordingly, although the present study was adequately powered to detect large effects, it was underpowered to detect medium-sized effects. Therefore, the absence of statistically significant behavioral differences should not be interpreted as evidence that smaller effects were absent. In addition, power calculations were not reported in most of the studies summarized in Table 3 , which may make direct comparison of statistical sensitivity across studies difficult. Our pathological analyses showed trends toward reduced neurogenesis in the DG and increased GFAP immunoreactivity in the CA1 and CA3 regions of the hippocampus following high-stress/low-dose exposure; however, these changes did not reach statistical significance ( Fig 4 ). Therefore, the relationship between these neurobiological changes and GWI-like phenotype expression remains unclear. Although neurogenesis impairment and astrogliosis have been proposed in experimental models of GWI, evidence linking these changes directly to clinical GWI pathology remains limited. It is possible that the observed changes did not reach a sufficient threshold to produce measurable behavioral alterations under the conditions tested. Neurobiological alterations may precede the development of detectable behavioral deficits or persist in the absence of overt behavioral abnormalities, and the behavioral assays employed in the present study may not have been sufficiently sensitive to detect subtle functional impairments. These interpretations remain speculative and warrant further investigation in future longitudinal studies. In addition, high-stress/low-dose exposure increased VGLUT1 and EAAT2 expression, suggesting concurrent changes in glutamatergic signaling and glutamate clearance capacity. One possible interpretation is that increased EAAT2 expression reflects a compensatory response that helps maintain glutamate homeostasis and limits downstream excitotoxic effects. Such adaptive mechanisms may have attenuated the emergence of detectable behavioral phenotypes in the present study. Alternatively, subtle functional deficits may have been present but remained undetected. In our previous mouse study, we observed significant GWI-like phenotypes [ 20 ]; however, these phenotypes were not observed in rats, despite using the same exposure paradigm. This discrepancy may reflect species-specific differences in stress responsivity, pharmacokinetics, and behavioral sensitivity. In conclusion, our findings should not be interpreted as contradicting or invalidating previously published GWI studies. Rather, under the experimental conditions used in the present study—including animal source and strain background, exposure paradigm, and laboratory environment—we did not detect GWI-like behavioral phenotypes across multiple chemical and stress exposure conditions. Differences in vendor background, exposure schedules, housing conditions, and other laboratory-specific variables may contribute to variability across studies and should be evaluated directly in future validation efforts. Because in vivo testing in animal models remains an essential preclinical step and a regulatory requirement prior to human clinical trials, continued efforts to validate, optimize, and standardize reproducible GWI animal models will be important for strengthening translational rigor and supporting therapeutic development.
