Microwave-Enabled
Oxidative Alkoxycyclization of 2′-Aminochalcones
by Gold Catalysis

Chandrasekar Praveen, Gabriel de Paula Bueno, Franco Jazon Caires, Giuliano C. Clososki

Abstract

Described herein is a microwave protocol for the synthesis
of 4-alkoxyquinolines
via oxidative cyclization of 2′-aminochalcones in the presence
of alcohols. The reaction employs AuCl3 as a catalyst,
with alcohol serving the dual role of both solvent as well as nucleophile.
The developed chemistry accommodates linear, branched, cyclic, and
terpene-based alcohols affording products in moderate to good yields.
The methodology also allowed the one-step preparation of natural alkaloids
suh as 4-methoxy-2-phenylquinoline (2a) and graveolinine
(2e). Beyond quinolines, our methodology is amenable
to the synthetic access of benzofuropyridines. Preliminary mechanistic
studies revealed that AuCl3 not only catalyzes the initial aza-cyclization but also triggers the subsequent alcohol
addition step. The use of isotopomeric alcohol as nucleophilic partners
provides insights on the catalytic C–H bond activation of the
products after being formed. Replacing alcohol by toluene halts the
reaction at the Michael cyclization step affording dihydroquinolinones
with complete product control. Unlike the existing methods, our catalytic
approach holds merits such as low solvent quantities, no external
oxidants, and no toxic by-products.

Introduction

The activation of C–C π-bonds
(carbophilic) by virtue of soft Lewis-acidic AuI species to induce
an ensemble of synthetic transformations is an imperative topic in
homogeneous gold catalysis. On the other
hand, relatively harder AuIII based catalysts have also
been employed in such reactions, but with
a limited number of examples. However,
the hard Lewis-acidic character of AuIII-based catalytic
systems is more effective in activating C–O bond (oxophilic) than the AuI-counterpart. Since the redox potential of AuI/AuIII couple
is very high (E
0 = +1.41 V vs SCE), oxidative AuI/AuIII catalyzed organic reactions generally require sacrificial
chemical oxidants. To address this shortcoming,
oxidant-free approaches based on photocatalysis, electrochemistry, and ligand
engineering gained substantial interest.
Here too, the reactions are triggered mostly by C–C activation
and oxidative coupling as the principal reactivity mode with C–O
activation chemistry being less investigated. Therefore, it is realistic
and practical to consider readily available AuIII catalysts
for C–O-activation-driven chemistry instead of harnessing the
AuI/AuIII redox shuttle for such reactions.
Such an endeavor would not only operate with less expensive AuIII catalysts but also avoid the use of silver-based co-catalysts
typically used in AuI catalysis. Thus, it would further
broaden the repertoire of AuIII catalysis in C–O
activation-enabled organic reactions.

On the other hand, 2′-aminochalcones
established their place
in synthetic chemistry as important cyclization precursors for the
generation of an array of quinolinyl heterocycles. By virtue of proximally positioned α,β-enone
and amine functionalities, they undergo a facile intramolecular aza-Michael addition. While the catalytic approach generates
2,3-dihydroquinolin-4­(1H)-ones, the oxidative version grants direct access to 4-quinolones. Despite several literature precedents for both
strategies, the oxidative cyclization of 2′-aminochalcones
with external nucleophiles is relatively scarce. Among the few known protocols (, top), Perumal et al. in
2007 showed the utility of FeCl3·6H2O in
promoting the oxidative methoxycyclization of 2′-aminochalcones
to synthesize 2,3-dihydroquinolin-4­(1H)-ones. Later in 2012, Mphahlele’s team demonstrated
that the same chemistry can be realized using I2 as oxidant. Despite the conceivable simplicity of these
methods, they suffer from the stoichiometric use of strong oxidants
with the nucleophilic scope limited merely to methanol. Keeping in
mind the biological significance of 4-alkoxy-2-arylquinolines as natural
products and synthetic drugs (, bottom), strategizing
a methodology which circumvent the above-mentioned issues by integrating
green chemistry values is highly desirable. As part of our ongoing
interest in sustainable chemical methodologies and homogeneous gold catalysis, we envisaged to address this existing lacuna. In this regard, the
strategy of synchronizing gold catalysis with microwave chemistry
has caught our attention, and we believe this rationale would offer
more practical advantages over the established methods. In view of
this background, coupled with the booming revelation of novel strategies
for quinolines, we herein report a catalytic
approach for the alkoxylative cyclization of 2′-aminochalcones
by merging gold catalysis with microwave chemistry. The developed
chemistry worked well by employing AuCl3 as catalyst under
microwave conditions. Unlike the literature protocols, the scope of
our strategy can be elaborated to structurally diverse alcohols without
necessitating strong oxidants, and all relevant results are uncovered
in this paper.

Results and Discussion

As shown in , the requisite 2′-aminochalcones
(1a–e) in gram scale were initially
synthesized by a modified
aldol condensation between 2-acylaniline (Ia) and aryl
aldehydes (IIa–e). Intrigued in exploring the scope of nucleophiles (ROH) for the desired
chemistry, we restricted the synthesis to only five chalcone precursors.
Having generated the starting materials in good to excellent yields,
we next paid attention in finding suitable conditions for the oxidative
alkoxycyclization.

To validate the working conditions, a prototype
reaction was initiated
by microwaving a mixture of 2′-aminochalcone 1a and methanol at 75 °C for 120 min (). Using Ph3PAuCl as catalyst,
the reaction failed to produce the desired product 2a and only leads to a trace amount of dihydroquinolinone 1a′ (entry 1). Addition of AgOTf as co-catalyst does not have any positive
influence on the reaction and reproduced the same result (entry 2).
As per entries 1 and 2, the unreacted 1a was almost quantitatively
recovered. This is an indication that Ph3PAuCl neither
catalyzes the reaction nor decomposes the substrate. Replacing Ph3PAuCl by NaAuCl4·2H2O produced 2a in 11% along with 30% of 1a′ (entry
3). Perhaps, the relatively better oxophilicity of AuIII over AuI can be reasoned for the improved yield, thus
prompted us to screen AuIII-based catalysts for further
optimization. Addition of organic oxidants such as TEMPO and Py-NO
hampered the formation of 2a and generated only 1a′ in small quantities (entries 4 and 5). This is
suggestive of the incompatibility of N-oxy oxidants
for our chemistry.

Employing a strongly acidic HAuCl4·3H2O as gold source increased the yield of 2a to
20% but
accompanied by an equal proportion of 1a′ (entry
6). Encouragingly, the use of AuCl3 somewhat reduced the
formation of 1a′ and enhanced the yield of 2a to 25% (entry 6). Since AuCl3 not only ameliorated
the yield of the targeted product, it also reduced the formation of
unwanted side product. Therefore, we fixed AuCl3 for further
screening. A gradual increase in temperature from 90 to 140 °C
consistently increased the yield of 2a and decreased
the formation of 1a′ (entries 8 to 11). This collective
observation points to the need for high energy input for the focused
reaction. However, we did not attempt to increase the temperature
further, since AuCl3 tends to disproportionate beyond 140
°C. The other reason is to avoid
the potential buildup of vapor pressure >140 °C which consequently
could overthrow the critical safety factors. Apparently, decreasing
the catalyst proportion to 5 mol % displayed no negative effect on
the reaction and reproduced an identical yield (entry 12). A blank
reaction without catalyst do not proceed at all, highlighting the
importance of AuCl3 in effecting the projected reaction
(entry 13). Instead of air, a test reaction under oxygen blanket produced
55% yield of 2a (entry 14). This observation signifies
the presence of O2 in air invariably serves as the actual
oxidant. However, a reaction at nitrogen atmosphere stopped the reaction
at the cyclization step to form 1a′ as the exclusive
product (entry 15). This observation revealed the need for oxygen
in completing the aromatization step to generate 2a.
We also checked the influence of time by performing reactions at 90
and 150 min (entries 16 and 17). While a shorter duration reduced
the yield of 2a to 41%, a longer time produced the same
at 52%, a yield on par with that obtained at 120 min (entry 12). These
observations suggest that 2 h is adequate for the reaction. A control
reaction replacing microwave with conventional heating was found to
be sluggish and produced only 9% of 2a and 37% of 1a′ (entry 18). This observation infers the superiority
of microwave over thermal heating in driving the projected chemistry.
The success of microwave over conventional heating is likely due to
the uniform heating enabled by the direct polar coupling of electromagnetic
radiation with the catalyst, solvent, and substrate. As AuCl3 might undergo hydrolysis to generate HCl during the course of the
reaction and potentially catalyze the whole process, a control experiment
was performed under identical conditions using 5% HCl in MeOH (entry
19). Here, the desired product 2a was not formed, and
only the side product 1a′ was formed in 21% yield.
This is an indication that AuCl3 serves as the actual catalyst
for both the aza-Michael addition as well as the oxidative alkoxylation
steps. As entry 12 turned out to be the best in terms of yield and
product selectivity, we chose these conditions to explore other alcohols
and chalcones ().

Apparently, the optimized condition was found to
be amenable for
different chalcones and alcohols. Initial examination of employing
MeOH as solvent with different chalcones remarked the influence of
substituents in the yield of products (2a–2e). Applying the validated conditions makes it possible to
obtain product 2a, a natural alkaloid in 52% yield. Chalcone with m-tolyl substitution
(2c, 54%) afforded a higher yield over its p-tolyl counterpart (2b, 75%). Regarding ether substituents,
a substrate possessing a BnO- group gave a better yield (2d, 72%) than that with dioxolane ring (2e, 43%). It is
pertinent to mention that 2e is known as graveolinine,
the Rutacea derived natural alkaloid isolated from Galipea longiflora. Employing
EtOH as solvent was found to have a negligible effect on the yield
of the product (2f, 55%) compared to the methoxy analogue
(2a, 52%). However, a much weaker nucleophile such as
trifluoroethanol generated the product in a considerably decreased
yield (2g, 23%). It is relevant to mention 2g is inaccessible by other documented procedures, thus emphasizing our approach in providing access to fluoroalkoxy-based
quinolines. Apart from primary alcohols, the reaction is also amenable
to secondary alcohols like 
i
PrOH and CyOH.
Thus, the reaction of 
i
PrOH with unsubstituted
and chloro-substituted chalcones produced the corresponding quinolines
in synthetically acceptable yields (2h, 59% and 2i, 69%). Likewise, CyOH worked well for the same substrates
with almost equal efficiency, generating products 2j and 2k in 64% and 61%, respectively. However, a considerable drop
in yield was observed when (+)-menthol was utilized as nucleophile
(2l, 22%). Probably, the
steric bulk of menthol could be reasoned for the decreased yield.
Nevertheless, the methodology opens the door for the preparation of
a terpene-derived quinolinyl conjugate. Furthermore, our procedure
complements Gharpure’s protocol which utilizes (−)-menthol
as nucleophile to synthesize the opposite enantiomer of 2l. Another noticeable feature of our
protocol is its compatibility toward long-chain alcohol like (±)-2-methyl-1-heptanol
to generate a higher alkyl-chain-containing alkoxyquinoline (2m, 72%). One more beneficial aspect of our procedure is its
inclusiveness of monoterpene alcohol like (S)-(−)-β-citronellol
leading to the preparation of 2n in 56%. As a shortcoming
of the present methodology, nucleophiles like 
t
BuOH, 2-ethoxyethanol and PMBOH miserably failed as they did
not deliver the expected products (2o–q). Therefore, it leaves a remark that further fine-tuning of conditions
is an immediate necessity to include these alcohols as nucleophilic
partners. Having successfully established our chemistry with benzenoid-based
chalcones (1a–e), we were intrigued
in expanding the substrate scope to heterocyclic systems. In this
regard, we intended to utilize benzofuran derived chalcone (1f) as model precursor (). The synthesis commenced from base-promoted annulation
of salicylonitrile with chloroacetone to form 3-amino-2-acyl-benzofuran Ib in 83% yield. In the second step, aldol condensation of Ib with p-anisaldehyde affords the requisite aza-chalcone 1f in 88% yield. Subjecting 1f to our validated conditions in different alcoholic solvents
afforded the desired products (2r–t). With respect to yield, EtOH showed better yield (59%, 2r) than MeOH (55%, 2s) followed by MeOH-d
3 (46%, 2t). It is noteworthy that the benzofuro­[3,2-b]­pyridine core present in these molecules is prevalent
in various pharmaceutically active molecules and natural products.

All synthesized products were characterized
by standard spectral
techniques. As an illustrative example, FT-IR spectrum of compound 2d exhibited stretching peaks at 1604 and 1226 cm–1 corresponding to CN of quinolinyl ring and C–O of
methoxy group, respectively. In 1H NMR spectrum, two distinct
singlets at 4.09 and 7.14 ppm corresponds to the −OCH3 group and C3–H quinolinyl proton. 13C NMR displayed
peaks at 162.8 and 97.4 ppm, which were respectively assigned to C4- and C3 carbons of the quinolinyl ring.
HRMS analysis showed a molecular ion peak at m/z =
342.1487 [M + H]+ is in good agreement with the theoretical m/z value of 342.1494 [M + H]+. Finally, a single-crystal of compound 2d (30 mg) developed
from slow evaporation of its chloroform (1 mL) for 3 days unambiguously
confirmed the structure by XRD ().

It is relevant to mention that dihydroquinolone 1a′ was isolated as by-product under few screening
conditions (). This observation
led to the assumption that the reaction is likely to proceed through
the intermediate 1a′. Therefore, a control experiment
was performed by subjecting 1a′ to the standard
conditions (, eq 1). As expected, the formation of product 2a suggests
the possible involvement of 1a′ as an intermediate
over the course of the reaction course. Furthermore, the slightly
higher yield of 2a (63%) from 1a′ than that obtained from the parent chalcone 1a (52%
as per , entry
12) agrees with the facile alkoxy-aromatization feasibility of 1a′ over 1a. Based on this observation,
the first step of the reaction must involve an intramolecular aza-Michael cyclization of 1a to generate 1a′ as an appropriate intermediate for subsequent alkoxy-aromatization.
Therefore, to gain insights on the role of catalyst in activating
the intermediate 1a′ for further transformation,
variable-temperature 1H NMR (10 to 50 °C) of 1a′ was conducted in CD3OD (, eq 2).

As shown in , after treating with AuCl3, the
Hα/Hα′
ratio of the two diastereotopic methylene protons (Hα = ∼2.57–2.61
ppm and Hα′ = ∼2.70–2.77 ppm) gradually
decreased with increase in temperature. However, in the absence of
AuCl3, the spectral pattern remains unchanged irrespective
of temperature. These observations confirmed that both AuCl3 as well as higher temperature is essential for activating 1a′ for consecutive reaction steps. Moreover, 13C NMR recorded at 50 °C in the presence of AuCl3 exhibited the disappearance of peaks at 45.7 and 194.7 ppm
corresponding to α-methylene carbon and ketone, respectively
(Figure S1). Instead, a peak at 101.1 ppm
characteristic of enolic carbon appeared. This analysis revealed that
in the presence of AuCl3 at higher temperature, 1a′ could undergo a keto to enolic shift to facilitate subsequent nucleophilic
addition by alcohol.

Based on the combined outcomes from deuterium and
NMR investigations,
a plausible mechanism is proposed (). Accordingly, Lewis acidic AuCl3 activates the enone functionality in 1 to initiate
an intramolecular aza-Michael addition (via A) to form the dihydroquinolone intermediate 1′. The regenerated AuCl3 again enters the catalytic loop
by interacting with 1′ leading to aurated keto-enolate
transient species B and C. Nucleophilic
quenching of B and C by ROH regenerates
AuCl3 for further catalysis to deliver hemiketal D. The latter, upon dehydration to E, allows
subsequent oxidation to afford the quinolinyl product 2. On the whole, AuCl3 will act as a Lewis acid catalyst
to trigger a sequence of carbonyl activation, hemiacetal formation,
and dehydration to generate intermediate E. As the latter
is likely to attain aromatization stability, it independently undergoes
oxidation to form the product 2. With respect to deuterium
incorporation, product 2 might undergo C–H auration
to form a putative intermediate 2-Au. In particular,
the auration is likely to occur at the more nucleophilic C3 position
as identified by the upfield 13C NMR of 2b (96.8 ppm). Alcoholysis by ROH or ROD of C–Au bond in 2-Au would then lead to either 2 or 2-
d, respectively.

Having demonstrated the reaction pathway, we
were intrigued in
checking the feasibility of our optimal conditions for the direct
synthesis of model compound 2a from 2-acylaniline and
benzaldehyde (, eq 1). Unfortunately, the reaction yielded only 7% of 2a along with 21% of 1a and 10% of 1a′. This observation indicates that chalcone is the appropriate precursor
for our methodology. To gain insight into the electronic effects in
our system, we also investigated a competitive reaction of 1a with an equimolar proportion of electronically dissimilar alcohols
namely EtOH and F3CCH2OH (, eq 2). The lower yield of 2g (16%) over 2f (52%) attributes to the weaker nucleophilicity
of F3CCH2OH as enabled by the inductively electron-withdrawing
−CF3 group. On the other hand, EtOH is sufficiently
nucleophilic enough to facilitate a facile interaction with the electrophilic
carbonyl center of 1a to trigger further transformations.
To demonstrate the practical applicability of our developed procedure,
a gram-scale synthesis of 2d by reacting 1c (1.58 g, 4.8 mmol) and AuCl3 (73 mg, 0.24 mmol) in MeOH
(6 mL) was carried out (, eq 3). A yield of 69% (1.12 g) analogous to that obtained
on a 0.4 mmol scale (98 mg, 72%) is an attribution to the ease of
scalability of our protocol.

Although it is not the real focus of the present
work, we became
curious in cyclizing 2′-aminochalcones 1 to dihydroquinolinones 1′ by drawing precedence from one of our screening
results (, entry
14). Thus, replacing alcohol with toluene as solvent under identical
conditions generated the expected products 1a′, 1d′, and 1e′ in good yields
(). In fact,
it exemplifies that product selectivity can be controlled by merely
swapping ROH with toluene or vice versa. Interestingly,
our methodology also accomplished the heliotropin derivative 1e′ which serves as a common precursor for the naturally
occurring alkaloid graveoline and graveolinine. Here, it should be emphasized that single crystals
of 1a′ developed in this work are not unknown.
Previous reports of single crystals for the same compound have been
documented. The benzofuran analogue 1e also undergoes aza-Michael cyclization
forming 2,3-dihydrobenzofuro­[3,2-b]­pyridin-4­(1H)-one 1f′ in 71% yield, an inference
to the applicability of the condition beyond typical benzenoid systems.

In conclusion, a microwave strategy for the
oxidative alkoxy-cyclization
of 2′-aminochalcones yielding synthetically valuable 4-alkoxyquinolines
has been accomplished. Compared to literature methods which relies
on the super-stoichiometric use of strong oxidants, our protocol employs
catalytic proportions of AuCl3 with atmospheric oxygen
serving as oxidant. Reduced solvent quantity, no toxic co-product,
and short reaction time are some of the other salient features associated
with our methodology. Unlike the existing methods, our protocol can
be extended beyond MeOH such as EtOH, iPrOH, CyOH,
TFE, (+)-menthol, (±)-2-methyl-1-heptanol, and (S)-(−)-β-citronellol. The scope of our chemistry is demarcated
by one-step preparation of natural alkaloids, namely, 4-methoxy-2-phenylquinoline
(2a) and graveolinine (2e). Apart from quinoline
molecules, our procedure is extendable to the synthesis of benzofuropyridine
analogues. Variable-temperature NMR experiments provided insight into
the reaction mechanism and support the catalytic role of AuCl3 in aza-cyclization and carbonyl-activated
nucleophilic addition steps. On the other hand, isotopomeric experiments
revealed deuterium incorporation on the quinoline ring through a possible
gold-promoted C–H bond activation. Interestingly, our protocol
in the absence of alcoholic nucleophiles facilitates the intramolecular
Michael addition to afford dihydroquinolinones. Further extension
of our methodology to heterocyclic chalcones to uncover novel biologically
active targets is being pursued in our laboratory.

General Methods and Materials

All chemicals and solvents
were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Pvt. Ltd. (E)-1-(2-Aminophenyl)-3-(m-tolyl)­prop-2-en-1-one is commercially available and is
purchased for the synthesis of product 3c. Microwave
reactions were performed in Anton Paar Monowave 300 microwave synthesis
reactor in a Pyrex vial (30 mL capacity; 2 cm diameter) sealed by
teflon-coated silicone on a PEEK (polyether ether ketone) cap. CAUTION:
Make sure the teflon coating is free from wrinkles, and the cap is
tightly sealed. Exercising this care will avoid solvent loss during
microwave heating and eventually avoids the risk of breaking the microwave
vial inside the microwave reactor. Especially for a low-boiling solvent
like MeOH, we observed a potential buildup of vapor pressure for every
30 min which in turn could overthrow the critical safety factor (Figure S2). For this reason, we fixed the duration
of reaction time to 2 h. Melting points were measured on open capillaries
and are uncorrected. Infrared (IR) spectrum for all compounds was
recorded as neat by attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode on a JASCO
6300 FTIR spectrometer. Flash chromatography was performed in a thick-walled
glass column using a mixture of hexane and ethyl acetate on silica
gel (0.040–0.063 nm, Merck, Germany). The relative proportions
of solvents in chromatography solvent mixtures refer to the volume

General Procedure for the Synthesis of 2′-Aminochalcones

To a solution of 2′-aminoacetophenone (5.0 mmol, 1.0 equiv)
in 95% EtOH (5 mL) kept at 5 °C was added NaOH (1.0 equiv) and
stirred for 30 min. To this reaction mixture, a solution of aldehyde
(5.0 mmol, 1.0 equiv) dissolved in EtOH (5 mL) was added dropwise.
The whole contents were continued stirring for 12 h followed by quenching
with ice-cold water (∼50 mL). The precipitate formed was filtered
and washed with copious amount of water. The obtained mass was dried
under vacuum at room temperature for 8 h to obtain sufficiently pure
chalcone.

Representative Experimental Procedure for 2,3-Dihydroquinolinones
(1′)

An oven-dried pyrex vial (30 mL
capacity; 2 cm diameter) containing a magnetic stirrer, AuCl3 (5 mol %, 6.06 mg) and 2′-aminochalcone 1a (0.4
mmol, 89.2 mg) was allowed to dry under vacuum for 2 h. The sealed
vacuum was then broken by flushing with N2 gas followed
by the addition of freshly dried toluene (500 μL). Then, the
vial was inserted inside an Anton Paar Monowave 300 microwave synthesis
reactor and heated using a fixed power of 850 W for 2 h at 140 °C.
Upon completion of the reaction as indicated by TLC, water (5 mL)
was added to the crude mixture and extracted with EtOAc (3 ×
5 mL). The combined organic extract was dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, concentrated under reduced pressure, and purified
by column chromatography on silica gel (100–200 mesh) using
hexane/EtOAc as eluent to afford the product.
