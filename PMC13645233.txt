Sph-mediated mtDNA release and autophagy-dependent ferroptosis contribute to Staphylococcus aureus-induced mastitis

Yunfei Wei, Xinyu Zhao, Ziyi Yin, Yanmin Zhang, Yaxin Zhai, Baihe Zhan, Yeling Wang, Jiakang He, Jianfeng Wang, Xuming Deng, Lei Xu, Haihua Feng, Lakshmi Rajagopal

Abstract

Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), a major pathogen of mastitis, employs a diverse arsenal of virulence factors to invade and disrupt mammary tissues, thereby eliciting a complex and dysregulated inflammatory response. Sphingomyelin phosphodiesterase (Sph) has emerged as a key virulence determinant of S. aureus; however, its potential role and underlying mechanisms in mastitis remain elusive. In this study, we demonstrate that Sph deficiency significantly suppresses S. aureus-induced ferroptosis. Mechanistically, Sph disrupts mitochondrial cristae through interaction with Mic60, a core component of the mitochondrial contact site and cristae-organizing system (MICOS). Sph-mediated cytosolic mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) released is sensed by cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS), leading to activation of the STING signaling pathway, which further triggers the recruitment of GPX4 into the autophagy-lysosome pathway for degradation. Collectively, our study unveils a novel pathogenic mechanism whereby the S. aureus virulence factor Sph perturbs mitochondrial homeostasis, and engages the cGAS-STING axis to promote GPX4 degradation and ferroptosis, highlighting Sph as a potential therapeutic target for S. aureus-associated mastitis.
S. aureus is a major etiological agent of mastitis, a prevalent inflammatory disease of the mammary gland that adversely affects animal health and milk production. During infection, S. aureus deploys a diverse repertoire of virulence factors to damage host cells and promote disease progression. Among these factors, sphingomyelin phosphodiesterase (Sph) has been implicated in host cell injury; however, its contribution to mastitis pathogenesis remains poorly understood. Here, we show that Sph perturbs mitochondrial homeostasis by interacting with Mic60 and disrupting mitochondrial cristae architecture, thereby triggering activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway. Activated STING subsequently promotes the autophagic degradation of GPX4, a key antioxidant enzyme that protects cells against lipid peroxidation, ultimately driving ferroptotic cell death. Collectively, our findings uncover a previously unrecognized mechanism, and identify Sph as a potential therapeutic target for S. aureus-associated mastitis.

Introduction

Bovine mastitis, a major cause of economic loss in the global dairy industry, is primarily caused by Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), a ubiquitous Gram-positive pathogen with significant implications for public health []. Alarmingly, the clinical spectrum of S. aureus infection in humans ranges from localized abscesses to severe systemic diseases such as pneumonia, bacteremia, endocarditis, and osteomyelitis []. In the livestock and poultry industry, S. aureus is a major cause of mastitis, which can progress to severe suppurative inflammation and substantially compromise the structural and functional integrity of the mammary gland, thereby causing considerable economic losses to the dairy industry [,]. Therefore, elucidating the underlying mechanisms involved in S. aureus-induced host infections and developing novel therapeutic strategies to reduce bacterial pathogenicity or bolster host defense are pivotal for addressing S. aureus infections.

The genome of S. aureus encodes various virulence factors, including toxins, enzymes, adhesins, and other surface-associated proteins, which coordinate the pathogenic process by facilitating bacterial adhesion, invasion, dissemination, and immune evasion []. In contrast to receptor-dependent pore-forming toxins such as α-hemolysin, it was reported that β-hemolysin, also known as sphingomyelin phosphodiesterase (Sph), exerts its hemolytic activity primarily through the enzymatic hydrolysis of sphingomyelin in host cell membranes. Notably, Sph exhibits a characteristic “hot-cold” hemolytic phenotype, with moderate hemolysis at 37°C followed by markedly enhanced lysis after subsequent incubation at 4°C [,]. Moreover, Sph functions as a multifunctional effector that subverts host defenses through leukotoxicity and modulation of innate immune responses, while also inducing diverse forms of cell necrosis or apoptosis. The recent identification demonstrated that Sph provides a common structural framework belonging to the DNase I superfamily, consistent with its well-documented DNA-binding activity [,]. Furthermore, functional domain mapping of Sph identified His150 and His289 as critical residues for sphingomyelinase activity [], whereas Thr149 and His162 contribute to DNA binding [], indicating functionally distinct active sites. The sphingomyelinase and DNA-binding activities of Sph are crucial for promoting the formation of vegetations in infectious endocarditis, underscoring its potential role in disease progression. Recent evidence has emerged indicating that Sph knockout or antibody neutralization mitigates S. aureus-induced pneumonia in mice, implicating Sph as a key determinant of S. aureus pathogenicity []. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms by which Sph modulates host responses and contributes to S. aureus pathogenesis may provide new insights into the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.

The pathogenesis and progression of numerous diseases, including mastitis of infectious origin, involve multiple forms of regulated cell death (RCD), such as apoptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis []. Of particular note, ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by excessive accumulation of phospholipid peroxides, ultimately resulting in lethal membrane damage. Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) acts as the key regulator by the elimination of phospholipid hydroperoxides, thereby preventing ferroptosis []. Ferroptosis is closely linked to mitochondrial iron metabolism, and is accompanied by distinct morphological alterations of mitochondria during ferroptosis, including disintegration of mitochondrial cristae and rupture of the outer mitochondrial membrane []. Importantly, the integrity of mitochondrial cristae is critical for maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and limiting the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), thereby restraining aberrant inflammatory responses []. Growing evidence indicates that the bacterial virulence factor can manipulate host mitochondrial dynamics to promote infection. For example, listeriolysin O (LLO) produced by Listeria monocytogenes induces mitochondrial fission, highlighting a strategy by which intracellular bacteria subvert mitochondrial function to enhance pathogenicity []. Furthermore, bacterial toxin was found to trigger mitochondrial dysfunction and perturb redox homeostasis, thereby facilitating the release of damaged mtDNA into the cytosol and activating the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway and downstream type I interferon responses []. In addition to canonical inflammatory signaling, STING can initiate autophagy independently of IRF3/NF-κB activation, and STING trafficking-induced autophagy has been proposed to represent a primordial function of the cGAS-STING pathway []. Recent studies have further demonstrated that drug-induced mtDNA release can activate STING-dependent, autophagy-associated ferroptosis, which occurs independently of type I interferon responses []. Mechanistically, STING signaling has been implicated in promoting ferroptosis by facilitating the autophagic degradation of GPX4, thereby disrupting cellular redox homeostasis. This regulatory mechanism, observed in diverse pathological contexts ranging from neuroinflammation to cardiomyocyte injury, supports the existence of a functional STING-GPX4 axis linking innate immune signaling to ferroptotic cell death []. The functional importance of this STING-GPX4 regulatory axis is further supported by mutagenesis studies showing that disruption of the STING-GPX4 interaction through mutations at key residues, including T267 in STING and N146 in GPX4, attenuates GPX4 degradation and ferroptotic cell death []. Collectively, these findings prompted us to investigate whether and how Sph contributes to S. aureus-induced ferroptosis and to define the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Although S. aureus has been reported to induce diverse forms of regulated cell death, the molecular mechanisms by which it triggers mitochondria-associated ferroptosis remain incompletely understood. Herein, we report that S. aureus virulence factor Sph acts as a pivotal mitochondrial disruptor by directly interacting with Mic60, a core component of the mitochondrial contact site and cristae organizing system (MICOS), leading to cristae disorganization and subsequent mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) release. The liberated mtDNA subsequently activates STING signaling, which promotes the autophagic degradation of GPX4, a key suppressor of ferroptosis, thereby driving ferroptotic cell death. Collectively, our study elucidates a novel pathogenic mechanism through which Sph governs autophagy-dependent ferroptosis, thereby laying the theoretical groundwork for future inhibitor development and therapeutic intervention.

Results

S. aureus-induced mammary epithelial cell death is associated with ferroptosis

To investigate the correlation between S. aureus-induced mastitis and ferroptosis, we established an in vitro S. aureus-infection model using bovine mammary epithelial MAC-T cells infected with S. aureus strain ATCC25923. Infection at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 200 for 6 h resulted in approximately 40% cell death (), whereas no significant cell death was observed during the initial 3 h of infection under the same conditions (). Consistently, S. aureus triggered MAC-T cell death in an MOI-dependent manner. As a positive control, treatment with the ferroptosis inducer erastin similarly induced substantial cell death in MAC-T cells (, ). Furthermore, to assess the effect of ferroptosis in S. aureus-induced MAC-T cell death, we employed five different types of ferroptosis inhibitors, including the lipid peroxidation inhibitor ferrostatin-1, the lipoxygenase inhibitor zileuton, the nitroxide radical TEMPO (a selective mitochondrial ROS scavenger), the ACSL4 inhibitor troglitazone, and the iron chelator deferiprone (). As expected, these inhibitors effectively mitigated S. aureus-induced MAC-T cell death, with TEMPO and deferiprone providing the most pronounced protection (-). Moreover, TEMPO and deferiprone reduced S. aureus-induced cell death in a concentration-dependent manner at concentrations of up to 30 μM (, ), further supporting the involvement of oxidative stress and iron-dependent ferroptotic processes in S. aureus-induced cytotoxicity. In contrast, troglitazone showed diminished protective effects at concentrations ≥20 μM and tended to increase cell death, potentially owing to cytotoxicity at higher concentrations ().

Moreover, to investigate whether S. aureus infection induces ferroptosis, we monitored the key hallmarks of ferroptosis in MAC-T cells using specific fluorescent probes. As shown in and , both S. aureus infection and erastin treatment markedly increased the green fluorescence intensity of the redox-sensitive probe DCFH-DA compared with the negative control (NC), indicating enhanced intracellular ROS accumulation, which was subsequently reversed by deferiprone treatment. Likewise, staining with an Fe2+ probe revealed a pronounced elevation in intracellular fluorescence in S. aureus-infected and erastin-treated cells, indicating expansion of the labile Fe2+ pool, a critical catalyst for ferroptosis (, ). Moreover, the C11 BODIPY 581/591 probe, which exhibits a fluorescence emission shift from red to green upon oxidation, showed a marked increase in green fluorescence in S. aureus-infected and erastin-treated cells, which directly visualizes extensive lipid peroxidation within the cellular membranes (, ). Consistently, a robust loss of mitochondrial membrane potential following either S. aureus infection or erastin treatment (, ). Critically, deferiprone effectively attenuated ferroptosis, as demonstrated by the reduced intracellular ROS and labile Fe2+ levels, suppressed lipid peroxidation, and restored mitochondrial membrane potential (-). Together, these findings demonstrate that S. aureus infection triggers ferroptosis in mammary epithelial cells and that iron chelation with deferiprone protects against S. aureus-induced cellular injury.

Deletion of sph significantly attenuates S. aureus-induced ferroptosis in mammary epithelial cells

To elucidate the function of the sphingomyelin phosphodiesterase (Sph), we generated an sph deletion mutant using a CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene-editing strategy. In brief, an sph-specific sgRNA directed Cas9 to introduce a double-strand break at the chromosomal sph locus, followed by homologous recombination with a donor DNA fragment designed to delete the target gene, thereby generating an isogenic Δsph mutant (). To confirm that the resulting phenotypes were specifically attributable to sph deletion, we further constructed a complemented strain by introducing a pLI50-derived plasmid carrying an intact copy of sph into the Δsph mutant, thereby generating the Δsph::sph complemented strain ().

We further assessed the effects of sph deletion on fundamental bacterial physiology and virulence-associated phenotypes. Growth curve analysis revealed that deletion of sph had no detectable effect on the growth kinetics of S. aureus ATCC25923 (), indicating that this gene is dispensable for basic bacterial proliferation. In contrast, the Δsph mutant exhibited a markedly reduced capacity for biofilm formation (), consistent with previous reports implicating Sph in S. aureus biofilm development [,]. We next examined the contribution of Sph to S. aureus-induced host cell injury using an LDH release assay. Infection with the wild-type (WT) strain induced substantial cytotoxicity in MAC-T cells, whereas deletion of sph significantly reduced LDH release; this phenotype was restored in the complemented Δsph::sph strain (). Consistently, larvae infected with the Δsph mutant exhibited significantly improved survival compared with those infected with the WT or Δsph::sph strains in a Galleria mellonella infection model (). Notably, infection with either WT S. aureus or the complemented Δsph::sph strain markedly reduced GPX4 protein levels in MAC-T cells, whereas GPX4 abundance was largely preserved following infection with the Δsph mutant (, ). Consistent with these findings, deletion of sph markedly attenuated multiple ferroptosis-associated alterations induced by S. aureus, including intracellular ROS accumulation (, ), a diminished labile Fe2+ pool (, ), attenuated lipid peroxidation (, ), and loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (, ). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Sph contributes to S. aureus virulence and promotes ferroptosis-associated injury in mammary epithelial cells.

Sph induces ferroptosis in mammary epithelial cells

To functionally characterize the purified Sph protein, we first assessed its sphingomyelinase-associated hemolytic activity using an erythrocyte hemolysis assay. Treatment of erythrocytes with Sph (0.4 μg/mL) at 37°C for 1 h followed by incubation at 4°C for an additional 1 h induced pronounced hemolysis, confirming the biological activity of the purified protein. Critically, Sph-induced hemolysis was markedly suppressed by GW4869, supporting the involvement of sphingomyelinase activity in this process (). We next evaluated the cytotoxic potential of the purified Sph protein on MAC-T cells. As expected, treatment with Sph (0.5–32 μg/mL) exerted concentration- and time-dependent cytotoxicity (). Consistent with the characteristic “hot-cold” hemolytic activity of Sph, subsequent incubation at 4°C further enhanced Sph-induced cytotoxicity (). These findings indicate that the sphingomyelinase activity of Sph contributes substantially to membrane damage and cytotoxicity. Moreover, Sph elicited considerable cytotoxicity even in the absence of cold incubation, with this effect being further potentiated by subsequent cold exposure. This observation suggests that, in addition to its classical hot-cold hemolytic activity, Sph may engage additional mechanisms to promote mammary epithelial cell injury.

The established link between S. aureus infection and ferroptosis prompted us to interrogate a potential role for Sph in this form of cell death. Then, we examined the protein expression levels of the SLC7A11 and GPX4. Notably, Sph treatment markedly reduced the expression levels of both SLC7A11 and GPX4 in MAC-T cells (), suggesting disruption of the cellular antioxidant defense against ferroptosis. We next evaluated the cytoprotective effects of five pharmacological inhibitors targeting distinct ferroptosis-associated processes in Sph-treated cells (). Notably, the lipid peroxidation scavenger TEMPO and the iron chelator deferiprone effectively afforded pronounced protection against Sph-induced cytotoxicity, whereas ferrostatin-1, zileuton, and troglitazone exhibited comparatively weaker protective effects (-). These findings further support the involvement of ferroptotic mechanisms in Sph-induced mammary epithelial cell injury. Notably, at higher concentrations, troglitazone tended to exacerbate Sph-induced cytotoxicity, consistent with the concentration-dependent effects observed in .

Furthermore, morphological assessment provided robust evidence to support these findings. Untreated MAC-T cells exhibited the characteristic polygonal, cobblestone morphology and formed confluent monolayers. In contrast, Sph stimulation triggered pronounced cellular elongation, loss of intercellular contacts, and extensive cell detachment, whereas co-treatment with deferiprone substantially alleviated these morphological alterations (). As expected, Sph treatment recapitulated the hallmark signatures of ferroptosis that were previously observed during S. aureus infection. Specifically, the significant accumulation of intracellular ROS (, ), an increase in labile Fe2+ pool (, ), elevated lipid peroxidation (, ), and a loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (, ) induced by Sph treatment were observed. However, a concerted reversal of all these perturbations was markedly attenuated with deferiprone treatment (-). In summary, our data establish that Sph acts as an inducer of ferroptosis in mammary epithelial cells.

S. aureus Sph induces mtDNA release from mammary epithelial cells and activates cGAS-STING

Ferroptosis is characterized by profound mitochondrial alterations, including membrane densification with virtual disappearance of cristae []. To determine whether Sph contributes to these mitochondrial abnormalities, we first examined the ultrastructure of MAC-T cells by transmission electron microscopy. Compared with cells infected with the S. aureus Δsph mutant, cells infected with WT S. aureus or the complemented Δsph::sph strain exhibited pronounced mitochondrial fragmentation and vacuolization, increased electron density, and extensive disruption of cristae architecture, whereas the nuclear envelope remained largely intact (). Consistent with these observations, treatment with purified Sph induced similar mitochondrial fragmentation and cristae disruption (). Together, these findings support a critical role for Sph in S. aureus-induced mitochondrial structural damage. Given that severe mitochondrial structural disruption can compromise mitochondrial membrane integrity and facilitate the release of mitochondrial contents, we next investigated whether Sph-mediated mitochondrial injury could lead to the release of mtDNA. As predicted, infection with WT S. aureus or the Δsph::sph strains, but not the Δsph mutant, induced substantial mtDNA release, confirming the role of Sph in this process (). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining with an anti-DNA antibody revealed a pronounced accumulation of cytosolic DNA in Sph-treated cells (). The functional consequence of this structural instability was further evidenced by a sharp depletion of cellular ATP upon Sph challenge, indicating a critical collapse of mitochondrial function ().

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying Sph-induced mitochondrial fragmentation, we analyzed the expression of key regulators of mitochondrial fusion and fission. Sph treatment reduced the mRNA levels of the fusion-associated genes Mfn1 and Mfn2, while concomitantly increasing those of the fission-associated genes Drp1 and Fis1 (), thereby shifting the dynamic equilibrium toward mitochondrial fragmentation. The expression level of POLG serves as a critical indicator of the mtDNA replication and repair capacity, and indirectly reflects the functional status of the TFAM-dependent regulatory axis []. Strikingly, Sph treatment induced a pronounced downregulation of POLG protein levels (). Notably, this loss of POLG could not be rescued by co-treatment with TMP (2,3,5,6-Tetramethylpyrazine), a known inhibitor of TFAM degradation, or by the proteasome inhibitor bortezomib (). Given that cytosolic mtDNA can activate cGAS and subsequently engage STING-dependent innate immune signaling [], we next examined this signaling axis and found that Sph stimulation robustly activated the cGAS-STING signaling (). Collectively, these findings support a model in which Sph perturbs mitochondrial dynamics and mtDNA homeostasis, promotes cytosolic mtDNA accumulation, and consequently activates cGAS-STING signaling in mammary epithelial cells.

Sph targets Mic60 to disrupt the MICOS complex

Mitochondrial cristae disruption has been implicated as an important trigger of cGAS-STING pathway activation. Notably, Mic60 (IMMT, MICOS60), a core component of the mitochondrial contact site and cristae organizing system (MICOS), serves as a critical hub for maintaining cristae structure and mediating substance exchange between the mitochondrial membranes []. Furthermore, to investigate the interaction between Sph and the host MICOS complex, Co-IP assays were employed and demonstrated a robust interaction between Mic60 and both wild-type Sph and its enzymatically inactive mutants, T149A (defective in DNA-binding) and H150N (devoid of sphingomyelinase activity) (-). This interaction was functionally corroborated by the mitochondrial co-localization of transfected eGFP-Sph in NIH/3T3 and HEK-293T cells (). To further define the Mic60 region responsible for Sph binding, we generated a series of truncated His-Mic60 variants () and examined their interaction with Sph by Co-IP. Notably, all Mic60 variants except the Mic60201–327 (comprising amino acids 201-327) were detected in the immunoprecipitated complexes (-).

To further test the hypothesis that Sph targets Mic60 to disrupt mitochondrial homeostasis, we overexpressed the full-length human Flag-tagged Mic60 (1–757 aa) protein in NIH/3T3 cells, followed by treatment with recombinant Sph protein. Overexpression of Mic60 alone did not activate cGAS (), induce obvious alterations in mitochondrial morphology, or promote mtDNA release (, ). Notably, Mic60 overexpression markedly attenuated Sph-induced mitochondrial damage, reduced the cytosolic release of mtDNA, and diminished cGAS activation (). These findings support a critical role for Mic60 in mediating Sph-induced mitochondrial dysfunction.

The mitochondrial cristae architecture is critically maintained by the MICOS complex. The core MICOS subunits, including the scaffold protein Mic60, the adapter protein Mic19, and the membrane-shaping protein Mic10, form a stable assembly that is essential for inner membrane integrity. At crista junctions, Mic60 serves as a central organizing hub that recruits Mic19, which in turn facilitates the association of Mic10 with the complex. This hierarchical organization is critical for MICOS stability and function []. To investigate whether Sph disrupts mitochondrial integrity by targeting this complex, we first examined its interaction with Mic60. Co-IP assays in HC11 cells revealed that treatment with Sph markedly reduced the recovery of Mic60 when immunoprecipitated with a Mic60-specific antibody (). This finding suggests that Sph binding may alter Mic60 conformation or accessibility to the antibody, although additional mechanisms cannot be excluded. We next examined whether Sph affects the association of Mic60 with other core MICOS components. Notably, following Sph treatment, Co-IP using antibodies against either Mic19 or Mic10 revealed a marked reduction in their association with Mic60 (, ). These results indicate that Sph disrupts the interaction of Mic60 with key MICOS components, thereby destabilizing the organization of the core MICOS complex.

Consistent with MICOS disruption, Sph treatment induced aberrant processing of the dynamin-like GTPase OPA1, characterized by a shift from long OPA1 to short OPA1 isoforms, a molecular event associated with impaired cristae architecture and mitochondrial stress (). In summary, these findings reveal that Sph triggers the loss of mitochondrial cristae and disruption of mitochondrial dynamics through interaction with Mic60, an essential constituent of the MICOS. Specifically, the regions Mic601–200 and Mic60328–590 in Mic60 contribute critically to Sph binding. This interaction may therefore represent an important molecular event underlying Sph-induced mitochondrial structural damage and its pathogenic effects.

S. aureus Sph activates STING to promote GPX4 degradation and ferroptosis

The degradation of GPX4 protein, a key suppressor of phospholipid peroxidation, constitutes a pivotal event precipitating ferroptotic cell death. Our investigation revealed that the STING inhibitor C-176 [] effectively antagonized GPX4 depletion induced by Sph, prompting us to interrogate the mechanistic link between STING signaling and GPX4 protein stability (). We therefore hypothesized that STING signaling may promote GPX4 degradation by engaging the cellular autophagy-lysosomal machinery. Strikingly, we observed that Sph treatment markedly increased LC3B II accumulation (), indicating altered autophagic activity. We next examined whether lysosomal degradation contributes to Sph-induced GPX4 loss. Critically, co-treatment with either NH4Cl or chloroquine, both of which impair lysosomal function by disrupting lysosomal acidification, substantially restored GPX4 protein levels following Sph treatment (). These findings implicate the lysosomal degradation pathway in Sph-induced GPX4 depletion.

To further investigate the involvement of autophagic processing, we employed pharmacological inhibitors targeting distinct stages of the autophagy pathway. Bafilomycin A1, which inhibits vacuolar H+-ATPase activity and thereby disrupts lysosomal acidification and autophagosome-lysosome fusion, effectively prevented Sph-induced GPX4 loss. In contrast, 3-methyladenine (3-MA), an inhibitor of class III PI3K commonly used to suppress early autophagosome formation, failed to restore GPX4 protein levels (). Together, these findings suggest that Sph-induced STING activation promotes GPX4 degradation through a lysosome-dependent process involving the autophagy machinery, with the late degradative stage playing a particularly important role. Loss of GPX4 would consequently compromise cellular defenses against phospholipid peroxidation and thereby facilitate ferroptotic cell death.

Given that STING can induce autophagy independently of the type I interferon (IFN-I) response [,,], we explored the mechanism by which Sph triggers STING-dependent GPX4 degradation. Upon Sph stimulation, STING exhibited markedly increased colocalization with GPX4, a phenotype similarly observed following treatment with the canonical STING agonist cGAMP. Critically, this induced proximity between STING and GPX4 was effectively attenuated by the STING inhibitor C-176 (), suggesting that STING activation promotes the spatial association between STING and GPX4. Given the established role of activated STING in engaging LC3-associated autophagic machinery [] and previous evidence supporting the formation of a STING/GPX4/LC3B complex [], we hypothesized that activated STING may facilitate the recruitment of GPX4 to the autophagic machinery. Supporting this model, both Sph and cGAMP stimulation robustly enhanced the colocalization of GPX4 with LC3B, whereas C-176 substantially attenuated this effect (). Collectively, these results support that STING serves as a critical intermediary linking GPX4 to the LC3B-associated autophagic machinery, thereby facilitating the autophagic degradation of GPX4.

We next sought to trace the ultimate fate of GPX4 following its recruitment to LC3B-associated autophagic machinery. During autophagosome maturation, lipidated LC3B-II associates with autophagosomal membranes, and subsequent fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes results in the formation of autolysosomes. Notably, Sph treatment markedly increased the colocalization of GPX4 with LAMP2B, a lysosomal membrane-associated protein (), supporting the lysosomal trafficking of GPX4 following Sph stimulation.

To biochemically validate the physical interaction between STING and GPX4, we performed a series of Co-IP assays. We first sought to establish a direct interaction in a controlled overexpression system. In NIH/3T3 cells co-expressing Myc-tagged STING and Flag-tagged GPX4, reciprocal Co-IP experiments confirmed a robust association: immunoprecipitation of Myc-STING efficiently co-precipitated Flag-GPX4, whereas reciprocal immunoprecipitation of Flag-GPX4 co-precipitated Myc-STING (, ). This bidirectional validation unequivocally demonstrates that STING and GPX4 can form a molecular complex in a heterologous expression context.

STING-mediated non-canonical autophagy has been linked to its proton channel activity during trafficking from the endoplasmic reticulum to the ERGIC and Golgi compartments. Following activation and translocation, STING can alter proton homeostasis within these membranes, resulting in an increase in luminal pH. This perturbation in organelle acidity is sensed by the vacuolar H+-ATPase (V-ATPase), which undergoes increased assembly of its V1 and V0 sectors and subsequently promotes recruitment of ATG16L1, thereby facilitating LC3B lipidation [,]. To determine whether Sph induces a similar alteration in Golgi pH, we transfected NIH/3T3 cells with MGAT2-RpHLuorin2, a genetically encoded ratiometric probe that enables dynamic monitoring of pH within the cis- and medial-Golgi compartments. An increased 405/488-nm fluorescence ratio reflects a higher luminal pH []. Notably, Sph stimulation induced marked Golgi deacidification, comparable to that observed following treatment with cGAMP or bafilomycin A1, a V-ATPase inhibitor that increases organellar luminal pH ().

To investigate the role of STING in Sph-induced autophagic degradation of GPX4, we depleted STING in NIH/3T3 cells using siRNA (). STING knockdown significantly attenuated Sph-induced LDH release () and markedly preserved GPX4 protein levels following Sph treatment (, ). Concurrently, we examined ferroptosis-related phenotypes in STING-depleted 3T3 cells. STING knockdown substantially blunted the Sph-induced increases in intracellular ROS levels (, ), Fe2+ accumulation (, ), and lipid peroxidation (, ).

Furthermore, to determine the functional role of GPX4 in Sph-induced ferroptotic cell injury, we overexpressed GPX4 in NIH/3T3 cells (, ). Upon Sph stimulation, GPX4 overexpression significantly attenuated Sph-induced LDH release (). More importantly, GPX4 overexpression alleviated the Sph-induced increases in intracellular ROS levels (, ), Fe2+ levels accumulation (, ), and lipid peroxidation (, ) in 3T3 cells.

Collectively, these data delineate a coherent spatiotemporal pathway in which Sph-induced STING activation promotes the association of GPX4 with STING and LC3B-associated autophagic machinery, thereby facilitating the recruitment of GPX4 into the autophagy-lysosomal degradation pathway. Together, these results establish a mechanistic link between Sph-induced STING activation and autophagy-lysosome-dependent GPX4 degradation, thereby providing a molecular basis for the ensuing ferroptotic response.

Deficiency of Sph significantly attenuates S. aureus-induced mastitis in mice

To investigate the pathophysiological significance of Sph-induced ferroptosis in vivo, we established a murine mastitis model and administered the iron chelator deferiprone to assess its protective effect. Macroscopic examination and H&E staining of the mammary glands revealed that infection with wild-type (WT) S. aureus and the complemented Δsph::sph resulted in pronounced swelling and extensive architectural disruption of the mammary tissue, whereas the Δsph mutant elicited markedly attenuated pathological damage (, ). Concordantly, deferiprone conferred effective protection against infections caused by both the S. aureus WT and S. aureus Δsph::sph strains. Additionally, bacterial burden in the mammary glands was significantly elevated in mice infected with the WT and Δsph::sph strains, an effect that was reversed by the Δsph mutant (), indicating that Sph promotes bacterial persistence or colonization within mammary tissue. Furthermore, the levels of TNF-α and IL-6 were substantially increased in the WT and Δsph::sph infection groups (, ), underscoring a more robust systemic inflammatory response.

To establish a causal link between these pathologies and ferroptosis, we examined several biochemical and molecular markers associated with ferroptosis. Compared with infection by WT S. aureus or the complemented Δsph::sph strain, infection with the Sph-deficient Δsph mutant markedly attenuated GSH depletion and reduced the accumulation of the lipid peroxidation product malondialdehyde (MDA) (, ). Myeloperoxidase (MPO) was also markedly reduced in the S. aureus Δsph group compared to the WT and Δsph::sph infection groups (). Critically, immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis demonstrated reduced expression levels of GPX4 in mammary tissues infected with either WT S. aureus or Δsph::sph strain; whereas GPX4 expression was largely preserved in tissues infected with the Δsph mutant (). Collectively, our data delineate a critical role for Sph in driving ferroptosis within the host mammary gland, as indicated by the depleted GPX4 levels and elevated lipid peroxidation. These findings support an important contribution of Sph-induced ferroptotic injury to the pathogenesis of S. aureus mastitis.

Discussion

Investigating the dissemination pathways of pathogens is as critical as studying their intracellular survival for a complete understanding of microbial pathogenesis. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by excessive lipid peroxidation, has increasingly been implicated in tissue injury and, in certain infectious contexts, may facilitate pathogen dissemination [–]. However, the specific microbial effectors that manipulate host ferroptotic pathways and the molecular mechanisms by which they contribute to tissue damage and pathogen spread remain poorly understood. Emerging evidence also implicates ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of mastitis, particularly during S. aureus infection. Upon S. aureus invasion, bacterial virulence factors and the ensuing inflammatory response can promote excessive ROS production, perturb iron homeostasis, and impair cellular antioxidant defenses. Concurrent depletion of GSH and dysfunction of the GPX4-dependent antioxidant system favor the accumulation of phospholipid peroxides, thereby promoting ferroptotic death of mammary epithelial cells and aggravating tissue injury [,]. Here, we identify the S. aureus toxin Sph as a previously unrecognized effector that promotes ferroptotic injury during mastitis. Previous study revealed that Sph promotes S. aureus biofilm formation through its DNA-binding activity, while its enzymatic activities as a sphingomyelinase and phosphocholine-specific phosphodiesterase contribute to pathogenesis by modulating host inflammation and increasing infection severity [,]. In the present study, we reveal a novel mechanism that S. aureus toxin Sph functions as a mitochondrial disruptor by targeting the mitochondrial cristae-organizing protein Mic60, and promotes mtDNA release into the cytosol. This liberated mtDNA activates the cGAS-STING pathway, leading to STING-dependent autophagy-lysosomal degradation of GPX4, a key suppressor of ferroptosis, and ultimately promoting ferroptotic cell death.

Mitochondria are important regulators of ferroptosis, particularly under conditions in which mitochondrial metabolism and redox homeostasis are actively engaged, such as erastin-induced ferroptosis []. Recent research highlights that mitochondrial dysfunction, triggered by cellular stress, initiates the release of mtDNA, and cytosolic mtDNA serves as a potent ligand for the cGAS-STING pathway []. Beyond its canonical role in inducing type I interferon and inflammatory responses, cGAS-STING signaling is increasingly recognized as an important regulator of cellular homeostasis, including autophagy [,]. Accumulating evidence indicates that bacterial toxins can exploit mitochondrial vulnerability through distinct mechanisms. For example, bacterial phospholipase C has been reported to impair mtDNA repair and alter mtDNA methylation, thereby driving mitochondrial energy metabolism dysfunction []. Recent study showed that Salmonella genotoxin CdtB can enter the mitochondrial matrix and interact with mtDNA, and then disrupt mitochondrial DNA integrity and activates STING, thereby promoting a proinflammatory senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) []. Furthermore, Helicobacter hepaticus CdtB translocates to the mitochondrial inner membrane via Hsp90 and interacts with ATP5A1, thereby impairing mitochondrial function and promotes the release of mtDNA [], identifying a toxin‐driven mtDNA release pathway independent of direct mtDNA interaction. Together, these studies illustrate that bacterial toxins can trigger mtDNA release either by directly damaging the mitochondrial genome or by disrupting mitochondrial structure and function.

S. aureus Sph exhibits both sphingomyelinase and phosphodiesterase activities. Although Sph belongs to the DNase I superfamily and exhibits DNA-binding capacity, it lacks appreciable nuclease activity toward double-stranded DNA []. We therefore investigated whether Sph disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis by targeting the MICOS complex. This concept is supported by recent evidence showing that the Listeria monocytogenes pore-forming toxin listeriolysin O (LLO) interacts with Mic60 and promotes mitochondrial fragmentation, highlighting Mic60 as a host target exploited by bacterial pathogens to perturb mitochondrial dynamics []. Intriguingly, our results identify Mic60 as a host target of Sph and further suggest that regions outside Mic60201–327 contribute to Sph binding. Functionally, Sph impaired the association of Mic60 with Mic19 and Mic10, indicating destabilization of MICOS organization. Because Mic19 participates in coupling Mic60 to the Mic10-containing membrane-shaping module, disruption of Mic60-Mic19 association is expected to compromise Mic10 incorporation and thereby impair MICOS-dependent cristae organization [,,]. Consistent with this model, Sph induced pronounced cristae disruption accompanied by mtDNA release into the cytosol. This signifies that the architectural integrity of the cristae is not only critical for energy conversion but also acts as a vital barrier sequestering mtDNA. The liberated mtDNA is subsequently sensed by the cytosolic cGAS-STING pathway, thereby igniting a potent innate immune response []. Our work thus positions Sph-induced MICOS disruption as an upstream pathogenic event that directly bridges mitochondrial structural catastrophe to the initiation of STING-dependent inflammation, a mechanism with broad implications for infection and sterile inflammatory diseases. Gram-positive bacterial infections can activate both canonical autophagy and STING-induced type I-IFN responses []. Subsequent studies have revealed an evolutionarily conserved role of cGAS-STING signaling in autophagy that is independent of type I interferon production. Upon activation, STING traffics from the endoplasmic reticulum to the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC) and Golgi, where STING-containing membranes can support LC3 lipidation and autophagy-related membrane remodeling []. GPX4, a central suppressor of ferroptosis, is subject to multiple modes of protein turnover, including proteasomal degradation and lysosome-dependent degradation under specific stress conditions [,]. In our study, Sph-induced STING activation was accompanied by GPX4 loss and increased LC3-II accumulation. Notably, pharmacological inhibition of lysosomal acidification or late autophagic processing with bafilomycin A1, NH4Cl, or chloroquine markedly preserved GPX4 levels, whereas 3-MA failed to provide comparable protection. These findings support a model in which Sph-activated STING promotes GPX4 degradation through a lysosome-dependent autophagic pathway, with the late degradative stage playing a particularly important role.

The functional interplay between STING and GPX4 has recently garnered significant attention. Initial studies established that GPX4-maintained redox homeostasis facilitates STING activation [], while subsequent work revealed that activated STING can promote autophagy-dependent GPX4 degradation and thereby facilitate ferroptosis under mitochondrial stress and in neuronal models [,]. A seminal study by Wang et al. provided mechanistic insight into this process, showing that mitochondrial stress promotes STING-GPX4 association and trafficking through ERGIC-associated vesicular compartments toward LC3-positive autophagic structures, ultimately resulting in lysosomal GPX4 degradation []. In this study, we observed that Sph-induced STING activation promoted LC3-dependent trafficking of GPX4 to lysosomes for degradation. We therefore propose a model in which Sph-mediated disruption of the Mic60/MICOS complex induces mitochondrial cristae damage and mtDNA release, leading to activation of the cGAS-STING pathway. Activated STING subsequently facilitates the engagement of GPX4 with LC3-associated autophagic machinery and its delivery to lysosomal compartments for degradation. Together, these findings uncover a previously unrecognized virulence mechanism by which S. aureus couples mitochondrial structural damage to STING-dependent GPX4 degradation and ferroptotic tissue injury.

Conclusions

In summary, during S. aureus-induced mastitis, Sph targets Mic60 to disrupt the MICOS complex stability, leading to mitochondrial cristae disorganization and impaired mitochondrial dynamics. The ensuing release of mtDNA activates the cGAS-STING pathway, which promotes autophagy-lysosome-dependent degradation of GPX4. Loss of GPX4 compromises cellular defenses against phospholipid peroxidation, thereby driving lipid oxidative damage and ferroptotic death of mammary epithelial cells. This pathogenic cascade contributes to S. aureus-induced mammary tissue injury (). Collectively, these findings define a previously unrecognized Sph-driven pathogenic mechanism and identify Sph as a potential therapeutic target for disease intervention.

Cell culture

MAC-T and HC11 cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; CRL-10274 and CRL-3062, respectively). The cells were cultured in F-12 medium (Gibco, CN) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Umedium, Hefei, CN), 1% insulin-transferrin-selenium (ITS; M&C Gene Technology, Shanghai, CN) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (NEST Biotechnology, Wuxi, CN). NIH/3T3 (ATCC; CRL-1658) and HEK-293T (ATCC; CRL-3216) cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

All bacterial strains used in this study are listed in Table A in . For routine cultivation, S. aureus ATCC25923 (S. aureus WT strain) was grown in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB; Hopebio HB4114–19).

The S. aureus Δsph mutant was constructed using a CRISPR/Cas9-based gene-editing system as previously described []. For genetic complementation, the native sph gene was cloned into the shuttle vector pLI50 and introduced into the Δsph mutant by electroporation to generate the complemented strain, Δsph::sph. Appropriate antibiotics, including ampicillin, kanamycin, and chloramphenicol, were used as required for plasmid selection and maintenance.

In addition, E. coli strains DH5α and BL21(DE3) were employed for plasmid propagation and recombinant Sph protein expression, respectively. For recombinant Sph production, the sph coding sequence was cloned into the pET-28a expression vector and expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) as previously described []. All primers used in this study are presented in Table B in .

LDH assay

MAC-T cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and allowed to adhere before treatment. Cells were then infected with S. aureus WT, Δsph, or Δsph::sph strains at the indicated multiplicities of infection (MOIs), or treated with recombinant Sph protein for the indicated durations. Additionally, to assess the protective effects of ferroptosis inhibitors, cells were treated with the indicated inhibitors in the presence of S. aureus or recombinant Sph for 6 h. Positive and negative controls were included in parallel. Cytotoxicity was quantified by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release using a commercial LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime; Shanghai CN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Assessment of ferroptosis-associated cellular changes

After infection with S. aureus strains or stimulation with recombinant Sph protein at 37°C for 6 h, cells were incubated with fluorescent probes to assess ferroptosis-associated oxidative stress, intracellular Fe2+ accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and mitochondrial membrane potential. Intracellular ROS were detected using DCFH-DA (1 μM; TargetMol), Fe2+ levels were assessed using a live-cell fluorescent probe FerroOrange (1:1000; Meilunbio), lipid peroxidation was evaluated using C11 BODIPY 581/591 (10 μM; Maokangbio), and mitochondrial membrane potential was monitored using JC-10 (1:1000; Meilunbio). All probes were diluted in serum-free medium and applied according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope under identical imaging conditions for all experimental groups.

Hemolytic activity assay

Fresh erythrocytes (2.5% v/v) were treated with various concentrations of recombinant Sph protein at 37°C for 1 h, followed by incubation at 4°C for an additional 1 h. Double-distilled water (ddH2O) and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. After incubation, samples were centrifuged to remove intact cells and cellular debris, and the absorbance of the supernatants was measured at the appropriate wavelength. Hemolytic activity was calculated as: Hemolysis (%) = [(Asample - Anegative)/(Apositive - Anegative)] × 100. Asample, Anegative, and Apositive represent the absorbance values of the Sph-treated sample, PBS control, and ddH2O control, respectively.

Western blot analysis

Protein extraction was performed with RIPA Lysis Buffer containing phosphatase inhibitor cocktail and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). After centrifugation, the supernatant protein concentration was quantified using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (20 μg) were separated by 10% or 12.5% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore; IPVH00010/ISEQ00010). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST and incubated with the indicated primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing with TBST, membranes were probed with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein signals were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and captured with a Tanon 4200 imaging system. Band intensities were quantified by densitometric analysis and normalized to the corresponding loading control.

Growth curve analysis

Overnight cultures of S. aureus WT, Δsph, and Δsph::sph strains were diluted into fresh tryptic soy broth (TSB) and incubated at 37°C with shaking. Then, bacterial growth was monitored at 600 nm at 1-h intervals using a spectrophotometer (UNIC UV-2000).

Biofilm formation assay

The bacterial suspension (1 × 106 CFU per well) was incubated for 24 h. After incubation for 24 h at 37°C under static conditions, non-adherent cells were removed and the wells were gently washed with PBS. The attached biofilms were stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 1 h. The retained crystal violet was then solubilized in ethanol, and biofilm biomass was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 600 nm.

Galleria mellonella survival assay

Overnight cultures of S. aureus WT, Δsph, and Δsph::sph strains were harvested by centrifugation, washed, and resuspended in sterile PBS. The bacterial suspensions were adjusted to the same concentration, and 10 μL containing 1 × 106 CFU was injected into the last left proleg of each Galleria mellonella larva using a microsyringe. Subsequently, the larvae were placed at 37°C to monitor and record the survival of larvae every 12 h.

ATP levels determination

Intracellular ATP levels were quantified using a commercial ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime; Shanghai, CN). Briefly, following treatment with the recombinant Sph protein, MAC-T cells were lysed on ice with ATP lysis buffer, and the clarified supernatants were then harvested for ATP determination. The luminescence (RLU) of different samples was measured using a luminometer (Biotek Synergy LX).

Quantitative reverse transcription-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the M5 HiPer First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Mei5bio, CN). Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using 2 × M5 HiPer Real-Time PCR Mix (Mei5bio, China). Relative mRNA expression levels were normalized to β-actin and calculated using the 2^(–ΔΔCt) method. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table B in .

Transmission electron microscopy

MAC-T cells were collected and fixed with 4% glutaraldehyde for 12 h. Following fixation, samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, infiltrated, and embedded in resin. Ultrathin sections (50 nm in thickness) were prepared and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate to enhance ultrastructural contrast. The ultrastructural features of mitochondria were examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Hitachi High-Tech HT7800).

Immunofluorescence staining

For immunofluorescence staining, cells grown on coverslips or in confocal dishes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100. After blocking non-specific binding sites with 5% goat serum, samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1000; Abbkine) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Where indicated, mitochondria were pre-labeled with MitoTracker (Beyotime; Shanghai, CN) before fixation and immunostaining. After washing with PBS, samples were mounted in antifade medium and imaged using a laser scanning confocal microscope.

Mitochondrial isolation

Mitochondria were isolated from treated cells using a Cell Mitochondria Isolation Kit (Beyotime; Shanghai, CN). In short, cells were harvested and homogenized in the provided mitochondrial isolation buffer. The homogenates were first subjected to low-speed centrifugation to remove nuclei, unbroken cells, and cellular debris. The resulting supernatants were subsequently centrifuged at higher speed to pellet the mitochondrial fraction. The mitochondrial pellets were collected and resuspended in the appropriate buffer for further analyses.

Cell transfection

The NIH/3T3 or HEK-293T cells (1 × 106 cells per well) were used for transfection at the appropriate confluence using Lipofectamine 3000 together with P3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Shanghai, CN), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For GPX4 expression, the transfection medium was supplemented with ITS (containing selenium; M&C Gene Technology; Shanghai, CN). After 6 h, the medium was replaced with DMEM to further culture for 24 h.

CO-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) Assay

Co-immunoprecipitation assays were performed using a commercial Co-IP kit (Beyotime; Shanghai, CN). Briefly, treated cells were lysed in the recommended immunoprecipitation buffer and clarified by centrifugation. An aliquot of each lysate was retained as the input control, while the remaining lysate was incubated with the indicated primary antibody, followed by Protein A/G magnetic beads to capture the immune complexes. After extensive washing to remove nonspecifically bound proteins, the bead-bound complexes were eluted by boiling in SDS sample buffer and analyzed by Western blotting. To ensure blotting specificity, species-mismatched and/or Fc-specific secondary antibodies were employed.

Animal experiments

Female BALB/c mice aged 8–10 weeks were obtained from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology (Liaoning, CN) and housed under SPF conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle, with free access to standard chow and water. Following a one-week acclimatization period, the mice were randomly assigned into experimental groups (n = 6 per group).

For establishment of the lactating murine mastitis model, female mice were housed with males for mating and provided with a breeding diet. Pregnant females were subsequently housed individually, and lactating dams approximately 7 days postpartum were used for infection experiments. Mice were anesthetized, and the distal teat region was gently exposed. Bacterial suspension containing 5 × 106 CFU of S. aureus was slowly inoculated into the designated mammary gland using a microsyringe. Deferiprone was administered by oral gavage at 50 mg/kg every 8 h.

At 48 h post-infection, mice were anesthetized and subsequently euthanized. Mammary tissues were collected. Portions of each tissue sample were fixed for histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses, processed for biochemical assays, or snap-frozen and stored at −80°C for subsequent analyses.

H&E staining and immunohistochemical staining

Mammary tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 8 μm. For histological analysis, tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) according to standard procedures. For immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis, paraffin sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval in citrate buffer and blocking of nonspecific binding. Sections were then incubated with the indicated primary antibodies, followed by appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogenic substrate, and then examined using a light microscope.

Bacterial colonization determination

Freshly excised mammary tissues were weighed and homogenized in sterile buffer. After diluting the homogenate sample in a series of steps, it was inoculated onto TSB medium. After 24 h of incubation at 37°C, viable bacteria were enumerated by colony counting and expressed as colony-forming units (CFU) per gram of tissue.

Measurement of tissue GSH, MDA and MPO levels

Mammary tissues were weighed and homogenized in the appropriate assay buffers. According to the manufacturers’ instructions, the levels of glutathione (GSH; Solarbio; Beijing, CN), malondialdehyde (MDA; Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute; Nanjing, CN), and myeloperoxidase (MPO; Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute; Nanjing, CN) were measured using respective commercial assay kits.

Measurement of TNF-α and IL-6 levels

Inflammatory cytokines in mouse mammary tissue homogenates were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (Biolegend, Beijing, CN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader, and cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves.

Data and statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. For datasets with two independent variables, two-way ANOVA was performed followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant (* or #) at P < 0.05 and highly significant (** or ##) at P < 0.01.
