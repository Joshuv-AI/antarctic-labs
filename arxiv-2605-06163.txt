The normal subgroup theorem for lattices on two-dimensional Euclidean buildings Thanks: J.L. was supported by ANR-24-CE40-3137 project PLAGE Thanks: S.W. was supported through the DFG Heisenberg project WI 4079/6.
Jean Lécureux, Stefan Witzel

Abstract
. We prove the normal subgroup property for every group that acts properly and cocompactly on a two-dimensional Euclidean building: every normal subgroup has finite index or is contained in the finite kernel of the action. As a consequence, the non-residually finite lattices constructed by Titz Mite and the second author are virtually simple. They are the first known simple lattices on irreducible Euclidean buildings.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 22E40; Secondary 11F06, 20E07, 20F65, 43A85, 51E24

Margulis’s celebrated Normal Subgroup Theorem
[
Mar91
, Theorem 4]
asserts that an irreducible lattice
\Gamma
in a higher rank, semisimple algebraic group
G
over a local field has all normal subgroups either finite central or of finite index. Each factor of
G
is either a Lie group acting on a symmetric space of non-compact type (by Lie theory) or is an algebraic group over a local field acting on a Euclidean building (by Bruhat–Tits theory). Thus
\Gamma
acts as a lattice on a product of symmetric spaces and Euclidean buildings. Conversely, it follows from the classification of Euclidean buildings by Tits and Weiss
[
Tit86
,
Wei09
]
together with Margulis arithmeticity
[
Mar91
, Theorem 1]
that most lattices on such products are arithmetic, see
[
BCL19
]
for a discussion. As an exception there are infinitely many two-dimensional buildings that are not
Bruhat–Tits
, i.e. do not arise from algebraic groups, but are
exotic
and also admit lattices. Such lattices cannot be arithmetic.

The Normal Subgroup Theorem has been extended in various directions (see for example
[
BS06
]
,
[
SZ94
]
,
[
BH21
]
among many others). Our contribution is to extended it to all uniform lattices on two-dimensional buildings:

Theorem A

(Normal Subgroup Theorem)
.

Let
X
be an irreducible two-dimensional Euclidean building and let
\Gamma
be a group acting properly and cocompactly on
X
. If
N\lhd\Gamma
is a normal subgroup then either
N
is finite and lies in the kernel of the action on
X
or it has finite index in
\Gamma
.

The case where the building
X
is of type
\tilde{A}_{2}
was recently proven by Bader, Furman, and the first author
[
BFL
]
and we will discuss the relationship below. Extending the Normal Subgroup Theorem to exotic lattices can be seen as a result unifying arithmetic and exotic lattices. Besides that an important motivation is an expected contrast between arithmetic and exotic lattices. While arithmetic lattices are linear and consequently residually finite, this is not expected for non-arithmetic lattices. In fact, the following was conjectured in
[
BCL19
]
in the
\tilde{A}_{2}
case:

Conjecture B
.

Let
X
be an irreducible Euclidean building of dimension
\geq 2
.
Let
\Gamma<\operatorname{Aut}(X)
act properly and cocompactly on
X
. If
\Gamma
is not arithmetic then it is virtually simple.

Our main theorem reduces the conjecture for lattices on exotic buildings to proving that they are not residually finite, see Section
1
. Although the conjecture predicts simplicity of infinitely many groups, many of which are understood rather explicitly, not a single one was known to satisfy the conjecture until now. However, recently examples of lattices on buildings of type
\tilde{C}_{2}
were constructed by Titz Mite and the second author that are
not
residually finite
[
MW25
]
. In combination with our main theorem this yields:

Corollary C
.

There are simple groups acting properly and cocompactly on buildings of type
\tilde{C}_{2}
.

Specifically, the groups
\Gamma^{2}_{1}=\check{\Gamma}^{2}_{1}
,
\check{\Gamma}^{3}_{1}
,
\check{\Gamma}^{3}_{2}
,
\check{\Gamma}^{3}_{3}
,
\check{\Gamma}^{3}_{4}
of
[
MW25
]
are simple. An important feature of these simple groups is that they are
\textsc{cat}({0})
-groups since the building that they act on properly and cocompactly is a
\textsc{cat}({0})
-space. In particular, they are very explicitly understood geometrically. This is in contrast to other examples of finitely presented simple groups, such as Thompson groups and Kac–Moody groups: they also act properly on
\textsc{cat}({0})
-spaces but the action is not cocompact and therefore provides much less geometric information on the group. The simple lattices on products of trees found by Burger an Mozes
[
BM00b
]
are
\textsc{cat}({0})
but they form a single quasi-isometry class and are less rigid. Indeed, an important feature of a simple lattice
\Gamma
on a two-dimensional irreducible building
X
is that it has strong rigidity properties. For instance
\Gamma
inherits quasi-isometric rigidity from
X
in a very strong sense, see
[
MW25
, Proposition 4.15]
. The lattice
\Gamma
is also known or expected (depending on the type of
X
) to have the same rigidity properties as arithmetic lattices of higher rank with respect to representations and actions on other spaces
[
LdlSW24
,
Der25
]
.

The general strategy to prove Theorem
A
is still the one devised by Margulis in the case of arithmetic groups. Taking the action of
\Gamma
to be faithful, the plan is to prove that any of its proper quotients has property (T) and is amenable. Since
\Gamma
has property (T) by
[
Opp25
]
which passes to quotients we are concerned exclusively with the amenability part. It is a consequence of the following Factor Theorem. Its statement involves the building at infinity of
X
which is a bipartite graph with sets of vertices
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
and set of edges
\Delta
. The asymptotic structure of
X
induces a topology and, in fact, a measure class on all three sets. The obvious maps
\pi_{i}\colon\Delta\to\Delta_{i}
are
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
-equivariant.

Theorem D

(Factor Theorem)
.

Let
X
be an irreducible two-dimensional building with spaces of chambers
\Delta
and vertices at infinity
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
, and let
\Gamma
act on
X
properly and cocompactly. The only
\Gamma
-invariant, weak-* closed subgalgebras of
L^{\infty}(\Delta)
are
L^{\infty}(\Delta_{1})
,
L^{\infty}(\Delta_{2})
and
\mathbb{C}
.

The Normal Subgroup Theorem
A
follows from the Factor Theorem
D
as explained in Section
3
. The article is therefore devoted to proving Theorem
D
.

For buildings of type
\tilde{A}_{2}
both theorems were proven in
[
BFL
]
making use of results from
[
BCL19
]
. We will now discuss ingredients to the proof of Theorem
D
with an eye on how they relate to results in
[
BFL
]
and
[
BCL19
]
. Roughly speaking, given a subalgebra
A<L^{\infty}(\Delta)
the plan is to show that since it is
\Gamma
-invariant and closed, it is invariant under (almost all)
projectivities
. To do so, we first produce a sequence of elements of
\Gamma
that converges to the needed projectivity in the compact-open topology (Theorem
8.1
). This crucially uses ergodicity of the
singular Cartan flow
(Theorem
7.5
). Combining convergence in the compact-open topology with a martingale convergence result resembling Lebesgue differentiation (Theorem
9.1
) we show that the elements of
\Gamma
converge to the projectivity in the weak-*-topology (Theorem
10.1
). Since projectivities act highly transitively, this is enough to deduce Theorem
D
.

The proof involves various measure spaces and showing that maps between them are measure preserving or measure-class preserving. The best-known among them are the
harmonic
measures on
\Delta
which are indexed by vertices of
X
and lie in a common measure class; they appear in
[
Par06
]
or more recently
[
RT21
]
. Another important example is the measure on the regular and the singular
Cartan flow
, that was the main object of study in type
\tilde{A}_{2}
in
[
BCL19
]
(the regular Cartan flow is sometimes called
Weyl chamber flow
in more algebraic settings). Let us also mention the Haar measure on the group of projectivities and the measure on the space of pairs of opposite chambers. In order to compare measures and to show disintegration results
prouniform measures
were introduced in
[
BFL
]
as limit measures of finite spaces.

One of our main contributions is to develop the theory of prouniform measures further and to exploit it systematically. Specifically, in Appendix
A
we study how an inverse system of constant-to-one maps and a choice of a counting measure on one object leads to a measure on the limit, and how these measures can be compared and disintegrated. This method is used in Section
6
to construct measures on spaces of embeddings of complexes. The generality of the framework allows us to put constraints on the embeddings we consider (technically implemented by appropriate categories). This allows to subsume Haar measures on tdlc groups in the construction and make the comparison of the singular Cartan flow with the Haar measure of the projectivity group (Proposition
6.27
) seamless, which it was not in
[
BCL19
]
. Another illustration of how useful the approach is is when constructing the measure on the space of pairs of opposite chambers, whose existence is well-known (
[
Par06
,
RT21
]
): we can use that the effects of a choice of basepoint cancel out without having to explicitly compute these effects (Lemma
6.23
).

Another main difference with the proof of
[
BFL
]
lies in the proof of
weak-*
-convergence mentioned above. In
[
BFL
]
it used an intricate argument applying the Martingale Convergence Theorem to what was called the detecting flow, constructed for that purpose in an
ad hoc
fashion. We give a more streamlined argument, not using the detecting flow at all, only relying on some version of the Martingale Convergence Theorem which plays a role very analogous to the Lebesgue Differentiation Theorem.

All differences so far are improvements rendering arguments clearer and more natural, but are unrelated to the generalization from type
\tilde{A}_{2}
to arbitrary two-dimensional buildings. However, there is a new profound technicality as well. In order to apply the prouniform measure machinery in Section
6
, the building needs to satisfy a certain condition, called
symmetry
with respect to a family of complexes in
[
BFL
]
. The family is subcomplexes of an apartment for many measures, such as the regular Cartan flow, but consists of subcomplexes of
wall-spaces
, spaces that decompose as a tree times a line, for measures related to the singular Cartan flow. The corresponding analysis in type
\tilde{A}_{2}
in
[
BFL
]
could be carried out in a rather elementary combinatorial fashion, thanks to the fact that
\tilde{A}_{2}
has only three parallel classes of walls. The analogous analysis in our case in Section
5
is more involved.

Extending one of the main results of
[
BCL19
]
, we are able to prove what would be a special case of a Theorem of Howe–Moore in the algebraic setting:

Theorem E
.

Let
X
be a two-dimensional Euclidean building and let
\Gamma
be a group acting properly and cocompactly on
X
. The singular Cartan flows
\mathscr{Y}_{1},\mathscr{Y}_{2}
and the regular Cartan flow
\mathscr{F}
are ergodic.

More precisely, every non-trivial translation of
F
acts on
\mathscr{F}/\Gamma
ergodically.

While we do reprove the main technical core of
[
BCL19
]
, we cannot generalize its main result. This is because a result due to Schleiermacher
[
Sch70
]
about projectivity groups of projective planes is not known to hold for generalized quadrangles or hexagons. Instead, we get the following weaker conclusion:

Theorem F
.

Let
X
be a two-dimensional Euclidean building and let
\Gamma
be a group acting properly and cocompactly on
X
. Assume that for some
i\in\{1,2\}
no cocompact subgroup of the closure of the projectivity group
\overline{P}_{i}
admits a faithful representation in
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(K)
with
K
a local field. Then any linear representation of
\Gamma
has finite image.

1.
Just infiniteness and residual finiteness

In this brief section we recall the relationship between the normal subgroup property and residual finiteness. In particular, we show how Theorem
A
reduces Conjecture
B
to showing that the lattices are not residually finite, and how Corollary
C
follows.

We first recall the relevant definitions. The
finite residual

\Gamma^{(\infty)}
of a group
\Gamma
is the intersection of all its finite index subgroups (and one may equivalently restrict to normal ones). It is the subgroup of all elements of
\Gamma
that map trivially under any homomorphism to a finite group. The group
\Gamma
is
residually finite
if
\Gamma^{(\infty)}=\{1\}
. A group is
just-infinite
if every proper quotient is finite or, equivalently, every non-trivial normal subgroup is of finite index. It is
hereditarily just-infinite
if every finite index subgroup is just-infinite.

Note that another way to phrase the normal subgroup property in Theorem
A
is to say that the image
\bar{\Gamma}
of
\Gamma\to\operatorname{Aut}(X)
(which has finite kernel) is just-infinite. Note also that a finite index subgroup of a lattice on
X
is itself a lattice on
X
hence Theorem
A
asserts that in fact
\bar{\Gamma}
is hereditarily just-infinite.

There is the following dichotomy.

Proposition 1.1
.

If
\Gamma
is hereditarily just-infinite then
\Gamma^{(\infty)}
is either trivial or simple of finite index.

In particular,
\Gamma
is either residually finite or virtually simple.

In the theory of just-infinite groups
[
Wil71
]
, this is part of a trichotomy, namely a just-infinite group that is not hereditarily just-infinite is a
branch group
, see
[
Gri00
, Theorem 3(a)]
.

Proof.

If
\Gamma^{(\infty)}\neq\{1\}
then it has finite index since
\Gamma
is just-infinite. Then if
\{1\}\neq N\lhd\Gamma^{(\infty)}
is non-trivial normal, then
N
has to have finite index in
\Gamma^{(\infty)}
because
\Gamma^{(\infty)}
is just-infinite being finite index in the hereditarily just-infinite group
\Gamma
. Then
N
has finite index in
\Gamma
and it follows from the definition of
\Gamma^{(\infty)}
that
N=\Gamma^{(\infty)}
.
∎

Combining with Theorem
A
we see:

Corollary 1.2
.

If
\Gamma<\operatorname{Aut}(X)
is discrete and acts cocompactly on
X
then it is either residually finite or virtually simple. More precisely if
\Gamma=\Gamma^{(\infty)}
then it is simple.

In particular, Corollary
C
follows together with the Main Theorem of
[
MW25
]
.

2.3.

Let

Before moving on we make a general remark about structures at infinity. Let

be two geodesic rays. There are two extremal asymptotic equivalence relations on rays. The most restrictive one is that of eventually coinciding (or having a common parametrized subray):
\rho|_{[R,\infty)}=\rho^{\prime}|_{[R,\infty)}
for some

. The most inclusive on is that of having bounded distance

, which means that they define the same point at infinity. Between these two there are two intermediate relations only quotienting in the parallel respectively transverse direction: the rays can eventually coincide up to translation (have a common setwise subray):
\rho(t)=\rho^{\prime}(T+t)
for some

and all

\rho_{[R,\infty)}
and
\rho^{\prime}_{[R,\infty)}
bound a flat strip so that
\rho(t)
and
\rho^{\prime}(t)
are each others mutual projection for

, this is equivalent to
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
defining the same Busemann function. Considering rays that define the same point at infinity up to the relation of eventually coinciding up to translation leads to a refined structure that turns out to be a tree and will interest us next. See
[
Cap09
]
for the general
\textsc{cat}({0})
case. We note that for embeddings of sectors

we could do a similar construction, but there is no transverse direction so there are only two main asymptotic equivalence relations: that of having a common parametrized subsector and that of having a common setwise subsector.

The previous discussion leads to a more refined structure than

associated to

Let

3.
Amenable groups, amenable actions, and factors

3.1.
Amenable actions

The main part of the proof of Theorem
A
is to prove that a proper quotient of
\Gamma
is amenable.

There are many equivalent definitions of amenable groups. The one we will use here is the following: a locally compact group
G
is amenable if and only if each time it acts by affine maps on a weak-* compact subspace of a dual Banach space, it has a fixed point.

Amenable actions are supposed to generalize the notion of amenable groups, in a way that a group is amenable if and only if its action on a point is. However, it turns out that the various generalizations we could define from these various conditions are not equivalent.

One of these definitions is the following. An action of a locally compact group
G
on a locally compact space
Z
is
topologically amenable
if there exists a sequence of continuous maps
\mu_{n}\colon Z\to\operatorname{Prob}(G)
such that
\lVert\mu_{n}(\gamma x)-\gamma\mu_{n}(x)\rVert\to 0
uniformly for
(\gamma,x)
in every compact subspace of
G\times Z
. Here
\operatorname{Prob}(G)
can be regarded as lying in the dual of the space of compactly supported continuous functions on
G
and the norm is the dual norm:
\lVert\mu\rVert=\sup_{\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1}\int fd\mu
, and continuity of
\mu_{n}
is understood with respect to the weak-* topology.

The following follows from the main theorem of
[
Lé10
]
(see also
[
Kai05
]
or
[
RS96
]
for previous results).

Theorem 3.1
.

The action of
\Gamma
on
\Delta
is topologically amenable.∎

The most commonly used notion of an amenable action is amenability in the sense of Zimmer
[
Zim84
]
. The relationship between the various notions of amenable actions is clarified in the book
[
AR01
]
. One instance of such a relation is the following implication. A self-contained proof can be found in
[
GHL22
, Proposition 2.5]
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
G
be a group with a topologically amenable action on compact topological space
Z
. Let
G
act affinely on a convex, weak-
*
-compact subset
K
of a dual Banach space. Then there exists a
G
-equivariant measurable map
Z\to K
. ∎

In particular, if
Z
is equipped with a
G
-quasi-invariant probability measure, then pushing the measure by the map
Z\to K
gives rise to a measurable quotient. In that case this is a slightly weaker version of Zimmer’s amenability.

3.2.
From the Factor Theorem to amenability

In this paragraph we work in the category of measure spaces , which is the category whose objects are measure spaces, and morphisms are measurable, measure-class preserving maps, which are identified when the agree almost everywhere.

By a

By passing to a finite index subgroup we may assume that the action of

5.
Convex geometry on trees

In this section we study convex subcomplexes of complexes isometric to products of a line and a tree.
Understanding inclusions of such complexes will be crucial in the next section, as it will allow us to finally construct the natural measure on
\mathscr{Y}_{i}
. The restriction to a product of a line and a tree might seem artificial to the reader, but it turns out that this is in some sense optimal,
as illustrated in Example
6.9
.

5.1.
Convex functions on trees

Let
T
be a (possibly finite) metric tree, more specifically, a simplicial tree equipped with a complete geodesic metric that gives every edge a length that is bounded away from
0
(we will only need trees with finitely many edge lengths).

A function
f\colon T\to\mathbb{R}
is
convex
if for every geodesic
\gamma\colon[0,\ell]\to T
the composition
f\circ\gamma
is convex. It will be formally convenient to also allow the constant function
f\colon T\to\{\infty\}
to be convex.

Our interest in convex functions stems from the following elementary observation:

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
f,g\colon T\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be convex functions with
f+g\geq 0
. Then

C_{T,f,g}=\{(x,y)\in T\times\mathbb{R}\mid f(x)\leq y\leq-g(x)\}\subseteq T\times\mathbb{R}

is closed convex. Conversely if
C\subseteq T\times\mathbb{R}
is closed convex then
S=p_{T}(C)
is a (possibly not simplicial) subtree and

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=-\max\{y\in p_{T}^{-1}(x)\}

and

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\min\{y\in p_{T}^{-1}(x)\}

define convex functions
f,g\colon S\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
such that
C=C_{S,f,g}
.

In particular
C_{T,f,g}
is convex if and only if
C_{T,f,\infty}
and
C_{T,\infty,g}
are convex.

We say that
f\colon T\to\mathbb{R}
is
affine
if it its restriction to every (closed) edge is affine. Thus an affine function is determined by the values it takes on the set
V
of vertices.

For an affine function
f
we let

f_{vw}=\frac{f(w)-f(v)}{d(v,w)}

denote the slope of
f
on the edge from
v
to
w
. Note that
f_{vw}=-f_{wv}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

An affine function
f\colon T\to\mathbb{R}
is convex if and only if for every
v\in V
and every
w,w^{\prime}\in\operatorname{lk}(v)
the inequality

f_{vw}+f_{vw^{\prime}}\geq 0

(5.1)

is satisfied.

Proof.

Let
\gamma\colon[0,\ell]\to T
be a geodesic. The function
f\circ\gamma
is convex if and only if it is locally convex, so it suffices for
f
to be convex on sets of the form
\gamma(U)
where
U
are arbitrarily small open. Such
\gamma(U)
are contained in the union of two edges when
U
is small enough. Since
f
is affine on edges it is certainly convex in interior points of edges. In the vertex
v
the given condition is visibly necessary and sufficient.
∎

Write
\inf(f)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\inf\{f(x):x\in T\}
and put
M(f)=\{x\in T\mid f(x)=\inf(f)\}
if
\inf(f)>-\infty
and
M(f)=\emptyset
otherwise.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
f\colon T\to\mathbb{R}
be affine and convex. At every vertex
v\in V
there is at most one neighbor
w\in\operatorname{lk}(v)
with
f(w)<f(v)
and the set
M(f)
is either a simplicial subtree of
T
or is empty.

Proof.

The first claim is immediate from (
5.1
). That
M
is convex is just Lemma
5.1
applied with

constant to
\inf(f)
. But since
f
is affine and convex if it is constant on a neighborhood of an interior point of an edge, it is constant on the whole edge. Thus
M(f)
is a subcomplex.
∎

We put
f_{v}=\min\{f_{vw}:w\in\operatorname{lk}(v)\}
. The previous lemma tells us that if
f_{v}<0
then this minimum is attained in a unique neighbor.

Remark
.

Note that there are three (non-disjoint) cases in which

has dimension
<2
(empty interior): if

is a single vertex, if

is a linear graph and

, or if

and

are constant with

and a vertex

then

The rest of the section is technical but is entirely a consequence of understanding the example: If some vertex contains an upward slope that is steeper than the negative of its downward slope then any elementary extension first needs to aim at bringing some such upward slope down before eventually an elementary extension can move properly below the vertex. In order to bring the upward slope down it may be necessary to apply a similar process to a vertex further up. Some subtlety is added by the possibilities that there is no downward slope or that the downward slope is actually flat.

We embark on an investigation of elementary extensions of

along edges of the form

, and the path of edges

If

6.
Pro-uniform measures

Our goal in this section is to introduce pro-uniform measures. The general ideas for these constructions were developped in
[
BFL
]
, but we will need them in a much greater generality, which is why we develop a different framework. The general idea of this construction is to define a notion of a uniform measure on the set of embeddings of a given simplicial complex in another. However, in our more general setup we will need to put additional restrictions on these sets of embeddings.

To do so, it will be convenient to employ basic categorical language, especially (inverse) limits and (directed) colimits. The general constructions in a categorical framework are developped in Appendix
A
below, to which we will refer. Recall that a
directed set
is a partially ordered set
I
in which any two elements
i,j
have a common upper bound
k\geq i,j
; it may be thought of as a category with objects
I
and a single morphism
i\to j
whenever
i\leq j
. A
directed
(respectively
inverse
) system in a category
C
consists of an object
Z_{i}
for every
i\in I
and a morphism
Z_{i}\to Z_{j}
(respectively
Z_{i}\leftarrow Z_{j})
for every
i\leq j
such that the triangles associated to
i\leq j\leq k
commute; thus it is a covariant respectively contravariant functor from
I
to
C
. A
cocone
of a directed system in
C
consists of an object
Z
and morphisms
Z_{i}\to Z
such that the triangles associated to
i\leq j
commute; similarly a
cone
of an inverse system has morphisms
Z_{i}\leftarrow Z
. A
colimit
of a directed system
(Z_{i})_{i}
is a cocone
Z
that is universal in the sense that if
Z^{\prime}
is any other cocone then there is a unique morphism
Z\to Z^{\prime}
through which every
Z_{i}\to Z^{\prime}
factors; similarly a
limit
of an inverse system
(Z_{i})_{i}
is a universal cone. Limits or colimits may or may not exist but if they exist they are unique up to unique isomorphism. This justifies to denote them by
\lim_{i}Z_{i}
respectively
\operatorname{colim}_{i}Z_{i}
. A basic fact
[
Rie16
, Theorem 3.4.11]
is that for an object
X
of
C
the functor
\operatorname{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(-,X)
preserves colimits, that is, if
(Z_{i})_{i}
is a directed system whose colimit exists then

\operatorname{Hom}_{{\textbf{C}}}(\operatorname{colim}_{i}Z_{i},X)=\lim_{i}\operatorname{Hom}_{{\textbf{C}}}(Z_{i},X).

As usual all our categories will be locally small, meaning that the collection
\operatorname{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(Z,Z^{\prime})
of morphisms from
Z
to
Z^{\prime}
is a set. We will usually equip
\lim_{i}\operatorname{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(Z_{i},X)
with the limit topology obtained by giving each
\operatorname{Hom}_{{\textbf{C}}}(Z_{i},X)
the discrete topology. The point of what follows is to also equip it with a limit measure.

To motivate our degree of generality we give two examples that illustrate some complementary aspects.

Example 6.1

Let

For each subcomplex

. We can form a category

which consists of

is the set of embeddings just considered. We want to equip the

6.1.
Counting extensions

The general challenge we are considering in this section is the following. We are given two complexes

and
Z_{2}
and an embedding

and we want to say that the number of extensions
\kappa
of
\iota
along

in the commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\kappa}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}

(6.1)

does not depend on

in the sense that there is an

such that

for every

, and

are always type preserving embeddings, as in Example
6.1
, but sometimes are required to satisfy additional constraints, as in Example

. These constraints are implemented by requiring that
\alpha
be a morphism in a category

and
\iota
and
\kappa
are morphisms in larger category
{\textbf{C}}^{X}
that contains

and the object

. Then

and

and our goal will generally be to show that for certain

is constant-to-one. In the language of
[

this roughly corresponds to saying that

is

might not be all (type-preserving) embeddings.

We therefore say that (the object of

)

is

-symmetric
if for every morphism

Letters are generally chosen compatibly so that if

consists of embeddings

is satisfied.

Throughout the section fix a type

.
Concretely we consider the following. Let

be the category whose objects are non-empty convex subcomplexes of

and whose morphisms are type-preserving embeddings. Let

be the category whose objects are convex subcomplexes of

and

and whose morphisms are type-preserving embeddings. Similarly, let
\hat{{\textbf{Y}}}_{i}
be the category whose objects are non-empty convex subcomplexes of

and whose morphisms are type-preserving embeddings. Let

be the category that additionally has the object

and all type-preserving embeddings.

As in the construction of the singular flow, we are actually interested in a more restricted category. We define the category

as follows. Its objects are, as before, non-empty convex subcomplexes of

. However, its morphisms are given by elements of

: if

,

is a convex subcomplex and

is a subcomplex containing

then

is a morphism of

as well as

. For

as the set of

to

is either a map

We will not always want to restrict to finite complexes and therefore make the following definition. A map

Lemma 6.3

Lemma 6.5
.

If

is a directed system in

then its colimit exists in

and is a colimit in

Proof.

For each

there is by definition an

such that

is

is another subcomplex with morphisms

6.5.
Locally proportional measures

We will want to specify basic (compact) open subsets of the various spaces just described. The general pattern is as follows: we have a space
\mathscr{Z}
(which can be
\mathcal{S}_{o}
,
\mathcal{H}_{\zeta,o},\mathcal{L}_{\zeta,o}
) of embeddings of
Z\subseteq F
(respectively
S,H_{i},\ell_{i}(\mathbb{R})
) to
X
that take
0
to a base vertex
o\in X
. The space
\mathscr{Z}
can be identified with a space
\Omega
(resp.
\Delta,\operatorname{Opp}(\zeta),\operatorname{Opp}_{i}(\zeta)
) of boundary objects of
X
. A basic open neighborhood of
\iota\in\mathscr{Z}
is the set of embeddings that coincide with
\iota
on a compact subset
C\subseteq Z
which then corresponds to an open neighborhood in
\Omega
. Thus for
C\subseteq Z
we define

U_{C}(\iota)=\{\iota^{\prime}\in\mathscr{Z}\mid\iota|_{C}=\iota^{\prime}|_{C}\}\subseteq\mathscr{Z}

which corresponds to the neighborhood

U_{C}^{o}(\omega)=\{\iota^{\prime}(Z(\infty))\mid\iota|_{C}=\iota^{\prime}|_{C}\}\subseteq\Omega

where
\iota\in\mathscr{Z}
is uniquely determined by
\iota(0)=o
and
\iota(Z(\infty))=\omega
. If
C=\{p\}
is just a single point we just write
U_{p}^{o}
for
U_{C}^{o}
. In the case
\Omega=\Delta
by far the most common set
C
to consider will be the chamber
c_{0}
at the tip of
S
(the one containing
0
); for that reason we introduce the additional notation

U^{o}(\sigma)=U^{o}_{c_{0}}(\sigma)=\{\sigma^{\prime}\in\Delta\mid\operatorname{proj}_{o}\sigma^{\prime}=\operatorname{proj}_{o}\sigma\}

(6.4)

in that case.

A useful observation using this notation is that while the inclusion

(\operatorname{Opp}(\zeta),\mu_{\operatorname{Opp}(\zeta)}^{o})\to(\Delta,\mu_{\Delta}^{o})

is not measure-preserving, its restriction to
U_{0}^{o}
is measure-preserving up to a constant factor. We want to generalize this observation by showing that various measures that we define on
\Delta
not only define the same measure class but are in fact locally proportional.

One way to think about this is in terms of expectations: If
(Z,\mu)
is a measure space,

and
U\subset Z
is of finite, positive measure, we denote by
\fint_{U}f(x)d\mu(x)
the average (or expectation) of
f
over
U
, that is,

\fint_{U}f(x)d\mu(x)=\frac{1}{\mu(U)}\int_{U}f(x)d\mu(x).

We want to say that for sufficiently small
U
the expectation does not depend on
U
. This corresponds to the fact that the Radon-Nikodym derivative in
[
RT21
, Proposition 6.1]
is locally constant.

\displaystyle\iota

to be the measure that coincides with
\mu_{\Delta^{\text{op}}}^{o}

Proof.

Let
\iota\in\mathcal{F}
be such that
\iota(0)=o
and
\iota(0^{\prime})=o^{\prime}
for some vertex

of type

to the intersection is the pushforward from

Let

\iota^{\prime}(0)=\iota(a0)=\iota(0^{\prime})=o^{\prime}

\displaystyle U_{0}(\iota^{\prime})

7.
Ergodicity of Cartan flows

The main result of the section is that the singular Cartan flow is ergodic, Theorem
7.5
. Before we prove that the regular Cartan flow is ergodic, Theorem

. Both proofs use the Hopf argument, which was conveniently packaged in
[
BCL19
, Theorem B.1]
. Two sequences

and

in a compact space

Theorem 7.1

(Hopf argument)
.

Let

be a locally compact space with commuting proper actions

,
\Lambda\curvearrowright Z
. Denote the quotients and quotient maps by

and
\psi\colon Z\to Y\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=T\backslash Z
. Let
(\pi_{i}\colon Y\to W_{i})_{i\in I}
be a family of
\Lambda
-equivariant maps:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W_{i}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W_{j}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{i}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{j}}

(2)

7.1.
Ergodicity of the Cartan flow

In preparation of the section’s main goal, ergodicity of the singular Cartan flow, we first prove ergodicity of the regular Cartan flow:

.

The proof is basically the same as in

Next we want to extend the this ergodicity in a way that does not enter the proof of Theorem

Taking away the measure-theoretic subtleness ergodicity of the Cartan flow rests on two basic facts: the first is that any two elements of

[

]

[

for

, then

is a continuous function on

, and it is non-constant. By Proposition

, we deduce that

. Since

is weak-* dense in

we conclude that

, which concludes the proof.
∎

12.
Non-linearity

In this section we explain how to derive Theorem

. Unlike to the rest of the article we do not give a self-contained treatment but explain how to adapt
[
BCL19
, Section 8]
.

Proof of Theorem
F
.

Since
\Gamma
has property (T), by
[
BCL19
, Corollary A]
, it suffices to prove that any finite index subgroup of
\Gamma_{0}<\Gamma
has no representation
\rho:\Gamma\to\mathbf{G}(K)
, where
K
is a local field,
\mathbf{G}
is a connected, adjoint
K
-simple
K
-group and the image of the representation is Zariski dense and unbounded. Since
\Gamma_{0}
is still a lattice of
X
, we can assume
\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}
. By contraposition, we assume that such a representation exist, and aim to prove that
\overline{P}_{i}
has a representation into some
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(K)
.

To do so, the main tool we will use is Bader–Furman’s theory of algebraic representations
[
BF
]
. Let
Z
be a
\Gamma
-ergodic standard measure space. An
algebraic representation
of
Z
is a
\rho
-equivariant map
\phi:Z\to\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H}(K)
, where
\mathbf{H}<\mathbf{G}
is an algebraic subgroup. Among the algebraic representations there is an intial one
[
BF20
, Theorem 4.3]
)
\phi_{0}\colon Z\to\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H}_{Z}(K)
. This means that for every representation
\phi
there is a unique
\mathbf{G}
-equivariant map
\psi\colon\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H}_{Z}\to\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H}
defined over
K
such that
\phi=\psi(K)\circ\phi_{0}
. We call
\mathbf{H}_{Z}
(which is unique up to isomorphism of
\mathbf{G}
) the
gate
of
Z
.

The universal property of the gate implies that if
M
is a group acting on
Z
, and such that this action commutes with that of
\Gamma
, there exists a homomorphism from
M
to the automorphism group of the gate, which is (in the category we consider) the quotient
N(\mathbf{H}_{Z})/\mathbf{H}_{Z}(K)
. Furthermore, if
M
is a Polish group and the action is Borel, then this map is continuous
[
BCL19
, Proposition 8.5]
.

Now we consider ergodic actions of
\Gamma
on
\Delta
, on
\Delta^{\text{op}}
, and on
\mathscr{Y}_{i}/A_{i}
respectively (with their respective measure classes), and let
\mathbf{H}_{\Delta}
,
\mathbf{H}_{\Delta^{\text{op}}}
,
\mathbf{H}_{\mathscr{Y}_{i}/A_{i}}
be their respective gates. Composing an algebraic representation of
\Delta^{\text{op}}
with the map
\mathscr{Y}_{i}/A_{i}\to\Delta^{\text{op}}
gives a representation of
\mathscr{Y}_{i}/A_{i}
, so that we have inclusions
\mathbf{H}_{\Delta}>\mathbf{H}_{\Delta^{\text{op}}}>\mathbf{H}_{\mathscr{Y}_{i}/A_{i}}
.

The proof follows closely
[
BCL19
, p. 541]
, and we divide the proof into three steps as well (the fourth step is applying Schleiermacher’s Theorem, which we cannot). Step (1) is claiming that
\mathbf{H}_{\mathscr{Y}_{i}/A_{i}}
is a proper subgroup of
\mathbf{G}
, or equivalently, that there exists an algebraic representation of
\mathscr{Y}_{i}/A_{i}
which is not reduced to a point. By the previous remark, it suffices to prove the same for
\mathbf{H}_{\Delta}
. Now, as discussed in
3.1
and Proposition
3.2
, the action of
\Gamma
on
\Delta
is amenable, so using
[
BDL17
, Theorem 6.1]
(together with the ergodicity of the action of
\Gamma
on
\Delta\times\Delta\simeq\Delta^{\text{op}}
from Theorem
7.2
) we get the existence of a non-trivial representation of
\Delta
.

From the above discussion, since the group
M_{i}
acts on
\mathscr{Y}_{i}/A_{i}
, commuting with the action of
\Gamma
, we get a homomorphism
M_{i}\to N_{G}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathscr{Y}_{i}/A_{i}})/\mathbf{H}_{\mathscr{Y}_{i}/A_{i}}
. More down-to-earth, the group
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}=\{1,\tau\}
acts on
\Delta^{\text{op}}
by
\tau(c,d)=(d,c)
, and similarly we get a homomorphism
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\to N_{G}(\mathbf{H}_{\Delta^{\text{op}}})/\mathbf{H}_{\Delta^{\text{op}}}(K)
.

Step (2) of the proof claims that this morphism is not trivial. If it were, the two maps
\Delta^{\text{op}}\to\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H}_{\Delta}(K)
given by composing the first and second projection with the gate representation of
\Delta
would coincide. But since
\Delta^{\text{op}}\simeq\Delta\times\Delta
this would mean that this gate representation of
\Delta
is essentially constant, which, as we discussed, is not the case.

Before moving to step (3), let us discuss the projectivity group
G_{i}=\overline{P}_{i}
. This group acts 2-transitively on the boundary of
T_{i}
, so we can apply
[
BCL19
, Theorem 3.7]
(compiling some results from Burger and Mozes
[
BM00a
]
) to conclude that it has a cocompact closed subgroup
G^{+}_{i}
that is topologically simple.

In step (3) we show that the action of
G^{+}_{i}
is non trivial. Indeed, since
G_{i}^{+}
acts 2-transitively on
\partial_{\infty}T_{i}
, there exists a
m\in G_{i}^{+}
such that the map
d:\mathscr{Y}_{i}/A_{i}\to\Delta^{\text{op}}
is such that
d(m\iota)=\tau d(\iota)
for every
\iota
. The group
G_{i}^{+}
acts on
\mathscr{Y}_{i}/A_{i}
, commuting with the action of
\Gamma
, so we get a continuous homomorphism
G_{i}^{+}\to N_{G}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathscr{Y}_{i}/A_{i}})/\mathbf{H}_{\mathscr{Y}_{i}/A_{i}}
. Using all the previous equivariant maps, we see that the action of

on that space cannot be trivial. Since

is topologically simple (and the action is continuous), it means that the map
G_{i}^{+}\to N_{G}(\mathbf{H}_{\mathscr{Y}_{i}/A_{i}})/\mathbf{H}_{\mathscr{Y}_{i}/A_{i}}
is injective. Hence the group

is linear over a local field.
∎

Appendix A
Limit measures

A.1.
Constant-to-one maps and measures

Let
\mathbf{Meas}
be the category whose objects are
\sigma
-finite measure spaces and whose morphisms are surjective measure-preserving maps. Since the morphisms are maps rather than maps up to measure
0
, this is not the category one might usually want to consider for measure theory but it suits our purposes. Note that this choice means in particular, that there is a forgetful functor to the category
\mathbf{Mble}
of measurable spaces and measurable maps, and one further to
\mathbf{Set}
.

Definition A.1
.

A map
f\colon X\to Y
is
n
-to-one
if
\lvert f^{-1}(y)\rvert=n
for all
y\in Y
. It is
constant-to-one
if it is
n
-to-one for some
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
. We call
n
the
fiber size
of an
n
-to-one map.

Note that being constant-to-one is more restrictive than being finite-to-one. Further note that constant-to-one maps are surjective. The composite of an
m
-to-one and an
n
-to-one map is an
(mn)
-to-one map, so sets and constant-to-one maps form a category which we denote by
\mathbf{Set}_{cto}
.

We now consider constant-to-one maps in the context of measure spaces.

Definition A.2
.

Let
Y
be a countable set and let
\kappa_{Y}
denote the counting measure on
Y
(with respect to the discrete
\sigma
-algebra). For
r>0
the measure
r\cdot\kappa_{Y}
is a
rescaled counting measure
.
Let
f\colon X\to Y
be an
n
-to-one map and let
Y
be equipped with a rescaled counting measure
\nu
. The
uniform measure relative to
\nu
is the measure
\mu
on
X
with respect to the discrete
\sigma
-algebra given by
\mu(\{x\})=\frac{1}{n}\nu(\{f(x)\})
. Thus if
\nu=r\cdot\kappa_{Y}
then
\mu=\frac{r}{n}\cdot\kappa_{X}
. In that case we also say that
f
is
uniformly measure-preserving
.

The relatively uniform measure satisfies

\int_{X}g(x)d\mu(x)=\int_{y\in Y}\frac{1}{n}\bigg(\sum_{x\in g^{-1}(y)}g(x)\bigg)d\nu(y).

(A.1)

and
f_{*}(\mu)=\nu
.

If
\lambda
is the uniform measure relative to the counting measure on
X
then (
A.1
) can be written as

\int_{X}g(x)d\mu(x)=\int_{y\in Y}\int_{x\in g^{-1}(y)}g(x)d\lambda(x)d\nu(y).

This is the kind of expression that we are after, with the (relatively) uniform measures replaced by (relatively) pro-uniform ones, i.e. by limits.

Recall that a diagram
F\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}
is just a functor from a small (index) category to
\mathcal{C}
. For our purposes
\mathcal{D}
will always be a directed set so
(F(d))_{d\in\mathcal{D}}
is a directed (
F
covariant) or inverse (
F
contravariant) system. Also recall that
\mathcal{D}
is connected if any two objects are connected by a sequence of morphisms which can be traversed in forward or backward direction.

Proposition A.3
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathbf{Set}_{cto}
be a diagram of constant-to-one maps and let
d_{0}\in\mathcal{D}
. Assume
\mathcal{D}
to be small and connected and
F(d_{0})
to be countable (possibly finite). Then there is a unique diagram
G\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathbf{Meas}
such that
F
and
G
induce the same diagram in
\mathbf{Set}
(composing with the respective forgetful functors),
G(d_{0})
carries the counting measure and every morphism
G(d\to e)
is uniformly measure-preserving.

If
F(d_{0})
is countable then in fact all
F(d)
are countable.

Proof.

As a measurable space we take
G(d)
to be
F(d)
with the discrete
\sigma
-algebra. We equip
G(d_{0})
with the counting measure. If
G(d)
has been equipped with a measure and
d\to e
is a morphism in
\mathcal{D}
, we push the measure along
G(d\to e)
. If
G(e)
has been equipped with a measure and
d\to e
is a morphism we equip
G(d)
with the relatively uniform measure with respect to
G(d\to e)
. Since
\mathcal{D}
is connected, this defines (at least) a measure on every
G(d)
. For well-definedness it remains to observe that pushforward of measures and taking relatively uniform measures are functorial and mutually inverse for countable spaces with rescaled counting measures and constant-to-one maps.
∎

Remark
.

(1)

Note that replacing
d_{0}
by
d
where
F(d\to d_{0})
is
n
-to-one has the effect of scaling all measures by
n
. Replacing it by
d
by
d_{0}
where
F(d_{0}\to d)
is
n
-to-one has the effect of scaling all measures by
1/n
. It follows that all measures obtained by varying the choice of
d_{0}
are rationally proportional.

(2)

One could equip
G(d_{0})
with an arbitrarily chosen discrete measure at the expense of
G
being defined only on the subdiagram of
d
that map to
d_{0}
in order to avoid pushforwards. Since we will eventually be interested in inverse systems this would not be an issue since the resulting system is cofinal. On the other hand we do not have use for the added generality.

A.2.
Limit measures

The following theorem should be compared to Kolmogorov’s Consistency Theorem
[
Coh13
, Theorem 10.6.2]
which asserts that the limit of an inverse system consisting of finite subproducts of an infinite product exists provided the spaces are totally finite and inner regular. More general limit theorems are proven in
[
Cho58
]
.

Proposition A.4
.

If
I
is a directed set and
((X_{i})_{i},(f_{ij})_{ij})
is an inverse system in
\mathbf{Meas}
of countable discrete spaces with rescaled counting measures and uniformly measure-preserving maps then
\lim_{i}X_{i}
exists.

Proof.

We denote the
\sigma
-algebra on
X_{i}
by
\mathcal{S}_{i}
and the measure by
\mu_{i}
. Note that the category
\mathbf{Mble}
of measurable spaces and measurable maps has all limits. The limit we produce has as underlying measurable space the limit in
\mathbf{Mble}
. So let
X=\lim_{i}X_{i}
be the set theoretic limit with maps
f_{i}\colon X\to X_{i}
. Let
\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{f_{i}^{-1}(A):i\in I,A\in\mathcal{S}_{i}\}
so that the limit
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{S}
is the one generated by
\mathcal{A}
.

Since
I
is a directed system,
\mathcal{A}
is an algebra: if
A=f_{i}^{-1}(A_{i})
and
B=f_{j}^{-1}(B_{j})
are two arbitrary elements (with
A_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{i}
and
B_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{j}
), there is a
k\geq i,j
so that
A_{k}=f_{ik}^{-1}(A_{i})
and
B_{k}=f_{jk}^{-1}(B_{j})
satisfy
A=f_{k}^{-1}(A_{k})
,
B=f_{k}^{-1}(B_{k})
with
A_{k},B_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
. It follows that
A\cup B=f_{k}^{-1}(A_{k}\cup B_{k})\in\mathcal{A}
and
A\cap B=f_{k}^{1}(A_{k}\cap B_{k})\in\mathcal{A}
. If
A=f_{i}^{-1}(A_{i})
then
X\smallsetminus A=f_{i}^{-1}(X_{i}\smallsetminus A_{i})
since we took the
f_{ij}
(and hence the
f_{i}
) to be surjective.

For
A=f_{i}^{-1}(A_{i})
define
\bar{\mu}(A)=\mu_{i}(A_{i})
which is well-defined by commutativity of the system. We claim that it is a premeasure. Before proving the claim we make the following reduction: if
A^{j},j\in\mathbb{N}
form a partition of
X
consisting of sets in
\mathcal{A}
with
\bar{\mu}(A^{j})
finite (which exist by
\sigma
-finiteness of the
X_{i}
) then extending
\bar{\mu}
to a measure
\mu
on
X
is tantamount to extending
\bar{\mu}|_{A^{j}}
to a measure
\mu_{j}
on
A^{j}
: clearly
\mu_{j}=\mu|_{A^{j}}
while
\mu=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{j}
. Thus there is no loss in assuming that
\bar{\mu}(A)
is finite which we do from now on. (This may be surprising in comparison to Kolmogorov’s theorem where finiteness is a crucial assumption but if in the context of Kolmogorov’s theorem one wanted that the measures on finite products are
\sigma
-finite one would still need that the measures on the individual factors are finite for all but finitely many.)

To prove the claim let
(A_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of disjoint sets in
\mathcal{A}
such that the union
A=\bigcup_{m}A_{m}
is again in
\mathcal{A}
. As in the reasoning above, for finitely many of
A
and the
A_{m}
we can arrange to be preimages of measurable sets in some fixed
X_{i}
, so
\bar{\mu}
is monotonous and finitely additive. Thus
\bar{\mu}(A)\geq\bar{\mu}(\bigcup_{m=0}^{n}A_{m})=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\bar{\mu}(A_{m})
for every
n
. It follows that
\bar{\mu}(A)\geq\sum_{m}\bar{\mu}(A_{m})
.

In order for
\bar{\mu}
to be a premeasure it remains to see that

\bar{\mu}(A)\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\bar{\mu}(A_{m})

(A.2)

when the right hand side converges. Let
F_{n}=A\smallsetminus\bigcup_{m=0}^{n}A_{m}
which is a descending sequence of elements of
\mathcal{A}
. Then

\bar{\mu}(F_{n})=\bar{\mu}(A)-\sum_{m=0}^{n}\bar{\mu}(A_{m})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\bar{\mu}(A)-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\bar{\mu}(A_{m})

so we want to see that
\lim_{n}\bar{\mu}(F_{n})=0
knowing that
\bigcap_{n}F_{n}=\emptyset
. In general this is the core of the problem. In our case we just note that an element of finite measure in
\mathcal{A}
is compact in the limit topology on
X
. Thus if
\bigcap_{n}F_{n}=\emptyset
for
F_{n}
of finite measure then already
F_{n}=\emptyset
for some
n
by compactness.

By Carathéodory’s extension theorem,
[
Fol99
, Theorem1.14]
there exists a measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{S}
extending
\bar{\mu}
. Since the
X_{i}
are
\sigma
-finite, so is
X
. So Carathéodory’s theorem implies that
\mu
is the unique measure extending
\bar{\mu}
. We claim that
X
equipped with
\mathcal{S}
and
\mu
is the desired limit.

So let
(Y,\mathcal{T},\nu)
be a measure space with measure-preserving maps
g_{i}\colon Y\to X_{i}
commuting with the
f_{ij}
and let
g\colon Y\to X
be the set-theoretic limit. Then for
A=f_{i}^{-1}(A_{i})\in\mathcal{A}
we have
g^{-1}(A)=(f_{i}\circ g)^{-1}(A_{i})=g_{i}^{-1}(A_{i})
and
\nu(g^{-1}(A))=\mu_{i}(A_{i})=\mu(A)
. So the pushforward measure
g_{*}(\nu)
coincides with
\mu
on
\mathcal{A}
. Thus the uniqueness statement of Carathéodory’s theorem implies that they coincide on
\mathcal{S}
meaning that

is measure-preserving.
∎

Besides Carathéodory’s theorem, the technical core of the proof is the compactness argument to verify (
A.2
). In order to extend the proposition to more general cofiltered limits one could work with inner regular Borel spaces or more generally with compact measure spaces in the sense of

, Definition 342a(c)]
. The subtlety lies in choosing the right morphisms (roughly perfect maps).

Remark
.

If

is a an inverse system of countable discrete spaces and constant-to-one maps, then the measure defined in Proposition

A.3.

We are approaching the main goal of the appendix, namely to prove a disintegration result for pro-uniform measures. In the first step the base space is is countable counting space while the fibers carry pro-uniform measures.

\mu(A)=\mu^{j}(A^{j})\\
=\int_{y\in A_{o}^{j}}\int_{x\in A_{j}\cap(f_{o}^{j,j})^{-1}(y)}1d\lambda^{y,j}(x)d\nu_{o}^{j}(y)=\int_{y\in A_{o}^{j}}\int_{x\in A\cap(f_{o}^{j})^{-1}(y)}1d\lambda^{y}(x)d\nu_{o}^{j}(y)\\
=\int_{y\in A_{o}}\int_{x\in A\cap(f_{o})^{-1}(y)}1d\lambda^{y}(x)d\nu_{o}(y)

as needed.
∎

A.5.
Embeddings

In our applications we consider a category
\mathcal{C}
of cell complexes and injective cellular maps and the functor
F
is of the form
\operatorname{Hom}(-,X)
for some object
X
of
\mathcal{C}
. The limits arise from colimits

\lim_{i}\operatorname{Hom}(Y_{i},X)=\operatorname{Hom}(\operatorname{colim}_{i}Y_{i},X)

in
\mathcal{C}
. Fixing
o\in I
, equipping
\operatorname{Hom}(Y_{o},X)
with counting measure
\nu_{o}
and every
\operatorname{Hom}(Y_{i},X)
with the relatively uniform measure
\nu_{i}
, gives rise to a measure on the space
\operatorname{Hom}(Y,X)
of embeddings of
Y=\operatorname{colim}Y_{i}
.

Proposition A.10
.

The limit measure on
\operatorname{Hom}(Y,X)
is invariant under
\beta\in\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}}(X)
and under
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}}(Y)
for which
\alpha^{*}\colon\operatorname{Hom}(\alpha(Y_{o}),X)\to\operatorname{Hom}(Y_{o},X)
is measure preserving.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}}(Y)
,
\beta\in\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}}(X)
, let
A\subseteq\operatorname{Hom}(Y,X)
. We need to show that
A
and

A^{\prime}=\beta\circ A\circ\alpha=\{\beta\circ\gamma\circ\alpha:\gamma\in A\}

have the same measure. Let
i\in I
and write
\iota\colon Y_{i}\to Y
for the inclusion. It suffices to show that
A_{i}=\iota^{-1}(A)
and
A_{i}^{\prime}=\iota^{-1}(A^{\prime})
have the same measure. The map
\gamma\circ\iota\mapsto\beta\circ\gamma\circ\alpha\circ\iota
is a bijection
A_{i}\to A_{i}^{\prime}
so the measures of both sets are proportional since the measures are rescaled counting measures. The proportionality constant is the one scaling the uniform measure on
\alpha(Y_{o})
relative to the counting measure on
Y_{o}
to the counting measure on
\alpha(Y_{o})
. If
\alpha
is as in the assumption, this constant is one.
∎

The following example illustrates that the measure is not invariant under all of
\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}}(Y)
in general.

.
