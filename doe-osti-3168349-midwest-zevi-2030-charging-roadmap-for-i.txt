Executive Summary
Overview
Zero-emission Medium Duty and Heavy Duty vehicles (MD-HD ZEVs) hold great promise for
reducing operational costs through use of more energy e icient propulsion technology while
reducing reliance on fossil fuels. Consistent with the Department of Energy’s focus on energy
innovation, ZEVs and the necessary charging and refueling infrastructure to support them represent
an important area of focus for technological advancement. Widespread adoption of MD-HD ZEVs
can also provide beneﬁts by improving air quality, and lowering carbon emissions in the
transportation industry. Powertrain technologies are in production today to reduce tank-to-wheel
and well-to-wheel emissions of commercial vehicles to zero or near-zero emissions, but signiﬁcant
development remains to bring down the cost and address the operational limitations of these
technologies, particularly for larger vehicles. ZEV technologies have an opportunity to be an
important part of a ﬂeet’s mix of powertrains as capital and operational costs come down.
However, the greatest challenge preventing widespread adoption of ZEVs may be the lack of
adequate charging and hydrogen (H₂) fueling infrastructure.
In addition to the availability of charging infrastructure, reliability and operational predictability of
public charging stations are critical prerequisites for medium and heavy-duty ﬂeet electriﬁcation.
Unlike passenger vehicles, commercial freight operations depend on predictable dwell times and
high station availability to meet delivery schedules and comply with hours-of-service requirements.
Recent assessments of public DC fast charging networks indicate that reported charger uptime
often overstates real world usability, with vehicle–charger communication failures and equipment
faults disproportionately a ecting high power charging applications. For freight ﬂeets, charging
unreliability imposes direct operational and economic costs and undermines conﬁdence in
electriﬁcation. As a result, this study emphasizes conservative utilization assumptions,
appropriately sized sites, and phased deployment strategies that account for reliability constraints
during early infrastructure rollout.
The purpose of this project is to identify the anticipated public infrastructure needs for commercial
ZEVs primarily along the I-80 corridor through Indiana, Illinois, and Ohio, with additional
consideration for the Indiana highway system. This network of highways is critical to the U.S. freight
industry due to its proximity to U.S. manufacturing hubs and Great Lakes ports. Each year, 724
million tons of freight move through Indiana, making it the ﬁfth busiest state for commercial freight
tra ic (INDOT, 2025). Indiana, Illinois, and Ohio all rank in the top ten for U.S. truck freight volume
(Patterson, 2025). Along I-80 alone, average annual daily truck tra ic (AADTT) can reach 2,000 to
5,000 trucks per day, with up to 25,000 trucks per day near major cities like Chicago.
Providing adequate public charging and hydrogen refueling infrastructure across this corridor will
be crucial for the adoption of ZEV technology. This report will outline the ﬁrst phase of a public
infrastructure plan, focusing on the needs of regional and local electric trucks expected to use the
network in the 2025–2030 timeframe.

  For most locations in this study, 350kW charging technology is not likely to require

passenger car charging stations to serve medium-duty trucks can help meet near to medium-term
needs while waiting for wider adoption of heavy-duty BEV/ZEV and readiness for MCS.

Introduction
Background on the need for public EV charging infrastructure for medium and
heavy-duty trucks.
Public charging for medium and heavy-duty (MD-HD) trucks is essential to support the growing
adoption of electric vehicles in this sector. Heavy-duty trucks moving freight long distances will
need high-power charging stations, typically above 150kW per port, along highways to complete

Midwest ZEVI – Charging Roadmap for I-80 Corridor

their routes without signiﬁcant downtime (NLR). Medium-duty trucks, such as delivery vans and
service vehicles operating within cities, will require accessible public charging stations to maintain
their schedules, especially if they lack private charging infrastructure. Electric trucks carry larger
batteries than passenger car EVs, requiring more energy and power to charge within a reasonable
time. This is particularly true with public charging, as it is typically performed enroute, where the
time to charge impacts vehicle productivity and is expected by users to be comparable to
conventional refueling times. Public charging stations will also provide a crucial backup for trucks
needing to recharge unexpectedly due to extended routes or unforeseen delays (NLR). Large ﬂeets
of electric trucks will need public charging options to supplement depot charging, particularly
during peak usage times or when trucks are away from their base. As the reliability of charging
stations builds, truck owners will likely rely on public charging to support continuity of operations
when depot chargers are down or occupied.

MD-HD vehicle infrastructure will include a mix of private or semi-private depot-based charging and
refueling, as well as public charging or refueling stations. A signiﬁcant part of this study is
dedicated to understanding this relationship and forecasting the amount of public infrastructure
needed to support a growing EV truck ﬂeet in this region, with the purpose of creating a stable
roadmap for future MD/HD ZEV infrastructure within the corridor on which users, providers,
utilities, and policy makers can plan

In addition to the shortage of charging infrastructure to support an electriﬁed MD-HD truck ﬂeet,
other obstacles exist toward the development and deployment of that infrastructure. Large-scale
deployment of high-power DC charging infrastructure requires strong coordination across multiple
stakeholders, including federal, local, and state governments, electric utilities, ﬂeet owners and
operators, electric vehicle and component manufacturers, as well as manufacturers and operators
of charging equipment, and various NGOs promoting clean energy initiatives. This project brings
together a diverse range of partners and stakeholders to identify and articulate the wide range of
constraints and challenges with the goal of driving consensus on solutions. This collaboration
could serve as a model for bringing MD-HD ZEV infrastructure to fruition at a large scale.

The work described in this report is funded by the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) with matching
funds from Cummins, Inc. The funding is one of seven DOE grants to ZEV infrastructure projects
across the country, totaling over $7M. The DOE funding opportunity announcement (FOA)
underpinning this work, DE-FOA-002611, Area of Interest 10, describes that “the objective of this
area of interest is to develop regional plans for medium-duty and heavy-duty commercial vehicle
charging infrastructure and hydrogen infrastructure for essential freight corridors, including ports
(inland and ocean) and large freight depot locations that these freight corridors connect.”

This project applies innovative tools and detailed stakeholder outreach to develop a regional EV
charging and hydrogen fueling plan for MD-HD vehicles in the Indiana road network, focusing on the
I-80 corridor in Ohio, Indiana, and Illinois. This report satisﬁes the ﬁrst of two main deliverables for
the program: a near-term (2025-2030) deployment plan for an MD-HD EV charging network utilizing
readily available EV charging technology, with no hydrogen refueling requirements, and focused on
early adoption vehicle applications such as local and regional trucks. A second report, intended to
describe a medium-term (2030-2035) deployment plan that includes more advanced charging
technologies and hydrogen refueling, was not completed due to time and technical constraints and
is left for future work.

To address the nationwide scale of the MD-HD ZEV infrastructure challenge, the tools developed in
this project could be used to model other geographic areas as adoption increases and a nationwide
network unfolds. The tools also provide a method to predict and prepare for uncertain MD-HD
adoption scenarios, allowing for adjustments to plans by changing input variables according to the
latest data and making real-time changes to the roadmap. These models can allow for near real-
time changes in proposed charging sites as adoption projections change, avoiding over-investment
in areas where charging might be underutilized.

Any infrastructure plan is heavily reliant on the input assumptions at the time of creation. A key
initial assumption of this project is that 6% of medium and heavy-duty vehicles would be electriﬁed
by 2030, ramping up to 30% by 2035. These assumptions were based upon several factors at the
time of project conception, including ambitious corporate sustainability goals, pending greenhouse
gas emissions regulations, and an accelerated pace of technology development in battery-electric
and hydrogen powered trucks. Changes in U.S. policy and incentives toward hydrogen and electric
vehicle infrastructure, along with the resulting slow-down in technology rollout, has led the team to
revise the assumptions. While the project was initially scoped assuming 6% MD-HD BEV adoption
by 2030, updated market, policy, and cost signals led to revised penetration assumptions averaging
approximately 4.3% across vehicle classes, as reflected in the modeling results.
Numerous papers have been published describing the tradeo s between the adoption of zero-
emission vehicles and the buildout of infrastructure to support them. The availability of adequate
charging infrastructure is clearly essential for buyers to consider moving to electric or other
alternative-fueled vehicles. This study aims to demonstrate a ﬂexible approach to identifying key
locations for public charging that can be adapted to changes in market forces, technology, and
policy priorities.

Intended Use of Public EV Charging Infrastructure for MD-HD Trucks
Today, truck ﬂeets typically operate under highly reﬁned models that prioritize e iciency, cost
management, and reliability. Fleet managers focus on optimizing routes, managing fuel
consumption, scheduling maintenance, and ensuring timely deliveries. This industry is dominated
by trucks with diesel engines due to their high torque output, fuel e iciency, and long-range
capabilities, making them ideal for long-haul transportation (LHT). Fleet operations often involve
complex logistics, including warehousing, distribution, and coordination with third-party logistics
providers. However, with the shift to electric trucks, these operational models are poised for
signiﬁcant changes. Electric trucks o er the promise of lower fuel and maintenance costs. This
savings is heavily dependent upon the ability to charge the batteries at charging costs low enough
not only to be competitive with diesel but also o set the higher upfront costs of electric trucks vs.
their diesel counterparts. For many ﬂeets, this means depot or shared charging hubs, where
availability and reliability are assured, and overnight charging allows for the management of
electricity costs. The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) estimates that 75%–90% of
medium- and heavy-duty vehicle chargers will be located at depots by 2030, due to their cost-
e ectiveness and operational convenience. (NREL) This requires ﬂeet operators to invest in
charging infrastructure and adjust route planning to accommodate charging times and vehicle
range.

It is unlikely that any ﬂeet can satisfy 100% of its charging needs with depot charging. Some
smaller ﬂeets or single truck organizations may not be able to a ord the cost of charging
infrastructure, or the costs of installing it at a particular location may be cost prohibitive due to the
cost of bringing su icient electrical capacity to the depot. In the case of long-haul (LHT) or over-
the-road (OTR) trucking, making it to a depot each day for recharging may simply not be an option.
These represent scenarios where public charging stations may be required for ﬂeets to run electric
trucks in a particular region. Several operational cases for public charging stations were highlighted
in the introduction and are worth discussing in more detail.

The need for publicly available charging for long-haul trucks is apparent, given the multi-day trips
and infrequent return to base location. The adoption of long-haul electric trucks is expected to be
minimal in the near term due to the high cost of equipment and the tradeo between battery range
and cargo capacity, impacting operational e iciency. This is particularly true in the Midwest, where
incentives and regulations are not signiﬁcantly inﬂuencing adoption as they are in California and
other areas, and infrastructure for large trucks is low. These are the primary reasons that 2030 is
focused on regional and local trucks where return-to-base is ubiquitous, and most trips can be
completed by an electriﬁed truck without the need for recharging.

As previously cited from the NLR study, public charging stations will also provide a crucial backup
for trucks needing to recharge unexpectedly due to extended routes or unforeseen delays (NLR).
Large ﬂeets of electric trucks will need public charging options to supplement depot charging,
particularly during peak usage times or when trucks are away from their base. As the reliability of
charging stations builds, truck owners will likely rely on public charging to support continuity of
operations when depot chargers are down or occupied.

Feasibility of Truck Electriﬁcation and the Impact of Fleet Characteristics

The electrification of trucks is increasingly feasible due to advancements in battery technology,
declining vehicle costs, and supportive policy frameworks. However, the adoption of electric
trucks is significantly influenced by fleet size, vocation, and power take-off (PTO) requirements.
According to a study commissioned by the Clean Freight Coalition and written by Roland Berger,
larger fleets are generally better positioned to absorb the high upfront costs—battery-electric
Class 8 trucks can exceed $400,000 compared to $180,000 for diesel models—and to invest in the
necessary charging infrastructure (Roland Berger, 2024). In contrast, smaller fleets may face
financial and logistical barriers that slow adoption. Vocation also plays a critical role; urban and
regional haul operations are more conducive to electrification due to predictable routes and lower
daily mileage, whereas long-haul trucking faces challenges related to range limitations and sparse
high-power charging networks (Roland Berger, 2024). Additionally, trucks with high PTO demands,
such as those used in construction or utility services, require specialized electric solutions that are
still under development, further complicating their transition to electric platforms. These factors
underscore the need for targeted incentives and infrastructure planning to support a diverse range
of fleet operations in the shift toward electrification.

The impact of electrification on the operational model of the fleet is a key driver behind the need for
public enroute charging. The trip length of a particular vehicle in a fleet can vary such that when
electrified, some trips will exceed the usable range of the vehicle. While the Roland Berger study
shows that 97% of Class 3-6 vehicles stay within the usable range of an equivalent electric vehicle
on average, this still leaves 3% of all vehicles requiring on-route charging to complete their mission
(Roland Berger, 2024). Longer trips cannot easily be accommodated by simply having conventional
vehicles fulfill those trips, as those vehicles may be underutilized. Most fleet operations today
depend on route flexibility of the entire fleet to meet their needs. This is particularly true for Class
7-8 Heavy Duty vehicles, where the range of trips varies more widely. In this case, as much as 31%
of trips would exceed the usable range of an electric truck in this class, requiring fast on-route
charging to ensure uninterrupted operations and manage charge times with driver’s hours of
service requirements (Roland Berger, 2024).

Most modern EVs now o er at least 250 miles of usable real-world range, with high-end models
pushing beyond 400 miles under ideal conditions. In contrast, Class 3-5 Commercial EVs modeled
in this study have a usable range of only about 200 miles, with Class 6 trucks achieving closer to
150 miles, and long-haul EV trucks stretching to about 350 miles. This evidence suggests that the
ratio of chargers to vehicles should be greater for commercial vehicles than for passenger cars, with
su icient power and number of ports to accommodate reasonable wait time and dwell time.
Usable range assumptions in this study reflect conservative operational derates for payload,
climate, terrain, reserve SOC, and charging behavior. As a result, modeled usable ranges are
intentionally lower than battery-capacity-derived maxima, particularly for Class 8 applications.

Fleet Interviews

As a follow-up to the surveys, fleet representatives participated in interviews to provide deeper
insight into their future plans and decision-making. These discussions focused on how the trucking
industry expects to evolve in response to decarbonization efforts, including anticipated technology
pathways, reliability concerns, and comparisons across available alternatives.

While fleets acknowledge growing interest in reducing emissions, interviews revealed persistent
concerns about the reliability and maturity of emerging technologies. As a result, progress toward
cleaner fuels is expected to be driven more by operational feasibility and economic value than by
regulatory pressure alone. Beyond emissions reductions, clean technologies must demonstrate
competitive advantages such as reduced uncertainty, strong performance, resilience to fuel price
volatility, and clear total cost benefits.

Interview feedback emphasized that vehicle technology alone will not drive adoption.
Infrastructure readiness and operational alignment are viewed as equally critical in shaping
outcomes. Electrification is considered most viable where duty cycles are fixed and facilities can
support charging—particularly for last-mile delivery and shorter routes. In these cases, depot
charging is widely viewed as the preferred solution, as vehicles typically return to base daily and
can charge overnight. Under current conditions, many fleets are in a testing and learning phase,
working through challenges related to grid coordination, site constraints for high-power loads,
facility ownership models, demand variability, and permitting timelines.

For long-haul freight, megawatt-scale charging is expected to be a key enabler of battery-electric
truck deployment. Public charging located along major freight corridors and aligned with standard
hauling patterns could support longer-distance operations, provided stations are reliable,
compatible with typical trailer configurations, and integrated with driver hours-of-service
requirements.

Despite these developments, fleet operators remain cautious about rapid investment in
decarbonization solutions. The pace of technological change, evolving industry standards, and
uncertainty around supplier viability contribute to hesitation—particularly where infrastructure
investments risk becoming obsolete before reaching full value.

Interviewees also highlighted that decarbonization strategies are likely to vary by fuel type,
geography, and operational context, rather than following a uniform approach. Options such as
renewable diesel, biodiesel, and battery-electric vehicles may be viable in speciﬁc regions
depending on local fuel prices, infrastructure availability, facility access, and route characteristics.
Low-carbon fuels are seen as attractive near-term solutions due to their ability to reduce emissions
without requiring ﬂeet replacement, while battery-electric vehicles o er advantages where
charging can be seamlessly incorporated into daily operations.

Views on hydrogen di ered notably from other pathways. Most interviewees did not view hydrogen
as a near-term, scalable solution for trucking due to high fuel costs, infrastructure complexity,
e iciency losses, and strong competition from other technologies. Hydrogen was generally
characterized as a longer-term or niche option rather than an imminent, widespread choice.

Overall, the transition to zero-emission trucking is being shaped not only by technological progress
but also by the economic realities of freight operations. Thin margins, soft freight markets, limited
public funding, and reluctance to absorb higher transportation costs are constraining capital
investment. Emerging concepts such as dynamic wireless power transfer and electriﬁed roadways
were discussed in similar terms: potentially beneﬁcial in easing operational constraints, but only if
costs can be reduced, interoperability challenges resolved, and vehicle platforms evolve to support
their use.

Interoperability
To ensure that EV charging stations are interoperable, the federal government supports the
development and adoption of common charging standards. This includes backing the use of the
North American Charging Standard (NACS), originally developed by Tesla, which aims to ensure
seamless connections between EVs and charging stations across different networks, and SAE
J1772 / CharIN Combined Charging Standard (CCS), which is the predominant standard in the U.S.
for DCFC. Both the Combined Charging System (CCS) and the North American Charging Standard
(NACS) support AC charging and DC fast charging using a single vehicle inlet, enabling
interoperability across depot and public charging environments. CCS combines the SAE J1772 AC
interface with dedicated DC pins to support AC Level 1/2 charging at depots and DC fast
charging—typically up to ~350 kW—on public corridors. NACS similarly supports both AC and DC
charging within a compact connector, with increasing deployment at high-power DC fast-charging
stations while also enabling higher-voltage AC charging configurations that can reduce depot
infrastructure cost. As a result, fleets adopting either CCS or NACS can utilize AC charging for
long-dwell depot operations and DC fast charging for en-route needs, with differences between the

Other requirements

In addition to charging power and interoperability, optimizing a charging station for commercial
vehicles (like trucks, buses, and delivery vans) versus passenger cars involves several key
differences due to the unique operational, physical, and logistical needs of commercial fleets.
Other important features include:

 Physical Layout and Accessibility: Commercial vehicles need larger turning radii, drive-
 through bays, and higher clearance for large vehicles, whereas compact layouts with
 standard parking spaces are sufficient for passenger cars.

 Energy Management and Grid Integration: To manage the high energy loads, commercial
 vehicle stations may very often require energy storage systems, load balancing, and
 demand response capabilities.

 Fleet Management Integration: Integration with fleet telematics, scheduling software, and
 predictive maintenance tools is crucial for fleet vehicles that are centrally managed. This is
 also true for payment and access control, as fleet vehicles often use centralized billing,
 RFID, or fleet accounts for refueling traditional vehicles.

 Durability and Reliability: Chargers for MD/HD trucks must be rugged, weather-resistant,
 and capable of 24/7 operation.

 Location and Deployment Strategy: Commercial vehicle charging will need to be located
 near logistics hubs, depots, industrial zones, or highways where the vehicles operate the
 most, as opposed to residential, shopping, and public parking areas where passenger
 vehicles operate.

Of the unique requirements for commercial vehicle charging listed above, grid integration may be
the most challenging. It is imperative that utilities be engaged early and often in the process of
rolling out commercial vehicle infrastructure. Fleets that are installing depot charging at a
significant scale are already learning that the charging infrastructure is one of the most important
considerations when moving to electrification of vehicles. The same priorities apply to public
charging. The earlier that utilities are engaged in potential charging station implementation, the
more flexibility the utility will have in meeting the electricity needs of the station, while ensuring
reliability, efficiency, and resilience of the grid. Early warning allows utilities to address potential
grid constraints earlier and reduce lead times to implementation. Significant upgrades can take 18
months to multiple years to fully implement (Indiana Department of Transportation, 2023)

One generalization was to try to keep interconnections below 5 megawatts (MW) where possible.
This is roughly the threshold for ‘grid-scale’ upgrades where transmission level upgrades or new
substations might be required. This could discourage investment in public infrastructure and is
likely not required for the early adoption phase of public commercial vehicle charging, at least in
most areas. While upgrades of >1MW to 5MW could drive substation upgrades, these can be
limited, and many situations may only require transformer upgrades and some reconductoring.
Proximity to substations, or 12kV or 34kV distribution lines, are key to keeping down
interconnection costs. For the purposes of the modeling, the number of recommended ports at
any one location was limited to 10 ports rated at 350kW. This limits the peak power demand to 3.5
megawatts when all ports are running at peak power, allowing some room for other power
demands of the station to stay under the 5MW limit with a reasonable power factor. In practice,
multiple ports may be unused or running at significantly lower power, keeping the typical demand
well below this limit.

Charge point reliability
Electric vehicle (EV) charging infrastructure reliability is emerging as a critical factor in the
successful transition of freight ﬂeets to battery electric vehicles (BEVs). While public and
commercial EV charger networks have expanded rapidly, uptime and reliability remain inconsistent
across regions and operators. A recent study by ChargerHelp revealed that “true uptime”, deﬁned
as the ability of a driver to successfully initiate a charge, is often signiﬁcantly lower than the
reported uptime by charging networks. On an assessment of 4,800 charge points across the
country, ChargerHelp technicians found that while 15.4% of chargers reported as down, 26.3% of
ports tested failed. A Paren, Inc. dataset of 9,700 DCFC charging stations capturing over 12.8
million charging sessions strongly corroborated the discrepancy, which is largely due to software
overestimations and communication failures between vehicles and chargers (ChargerHelp, 2024).

Reliability of public charging infrastructure is a critical enabler for medium- and heavy-duty fleet
electrification. Unlike light-duty passenger vehicles, freight operations depend on predictable
dwell times and high station availability to maintain delivery schedules and comply with

Midwest ZEVI – Charging Roadmap for I-80 Corridor

hours-of-service constraints. As a result, charging failures impose direct operational and economic
costs on fleet operators.

Recent assessments indicate that reported charger uptime often overstates real-world usability. A
nationwide evaluation by ChargerHelp found that while 15.4% of DC fast-charging ports were
reported as offline, 26.3% of ports tested failed to initiate a successful charging session, reflecting
issues not captured by network self-reporting metrics (ChargerHelp, 2024). Analysis of more than
12.8 million charging sessions by Paren, Inc. corroborates this discrepancy, identifying software
and communication failures as dominant contributors to unsuccessful charging attempts.
Similarly, Kempower analysis of over 13 million sessions indicates that approximately 91% of
failures are attributable to technical faults such as vehicle–charger communication issues and
connector locking errors, rather than user behavior (Veikkolainen, 2024).

For freight applications, the consequences of unreliability are magnified. NACFE emphasizes that
public charging used by medium- and heavy-duty fleets must be tightly integrated with duty cycles,
site power availability, and utility coordination to ensure dependable operations (NACFE, 2023).
Unplanned downtime increases operating costs, reduces asset utilization, and erodes fleet
confidence in electrification, reinforcing the importance of reliability in infrastructure planning.

To address these challenges, charging network operators and fleet owners are increasingly
deploying real-time monitoring, remote diagnostics, and predictive maintenance tools to improve
fault detection and resolution times. Load balancing and dynamic power management are also
being implemented to reduce stress on high-power DC fast-charging equipment, particularly at
multi-port commercial installations.

In the context of this study, reliability considerations reinforce the need for adequately sized sites,
conservative utilization assumptions during early deployment, and coordination with utilities to
ensure robust power delivery. These factors are reﬂected in the modeling constraints and validation
steps described in the simulation and site selection sections of the report, and they directly
inﬂuence the utilization trajectories and operating cost assumptions evaluated in the ﬁnancial
analysis.

Simulation Methodology and Site Selection Criteria
The analysis performed in this project couples two national-laboratory-developed tools: POLARIS,
an agent-based transportation network microsimulation model developed by Argonne National
Laboratory, and EVI-Pro HD, a charging infrastructure siting and sizing tool developed by the
National Laboratory of the Rockies. Together, these tools simulate freight vehicle movements and
energy consumption across the transportation network and identify public charging locations and
capacities required to support projected battery-electric vehicle (BEV) operations.

POLARIS simulations generate vehicle trajectories and energy demand based on traffic forecasts
and BEV adoption assumptions. These outputs are used as inputs to EVI-Pro HD, which determines
charging station locations, port counts, and power requirements. The resulting charging network is
then evaluated to verify that charging availability supports trip completion without excessive
detours, congestion, or unmet demand. Validation results are used to refine siting and sizing
assumptions as necessary. Outputs from this process—including station utilization, peak power

Midwest ZEVI – Charging Roadmap for I-80 Corridor

requirements, and delivered energy—form the quantitative basis for the economic and financial
evaluations presented later in this report.

Transportation Network Simulations
In optimally siting EV charging locations and capacities to support freight electriﬁcation, detailed
understanding of tra ic trajectories is required to ensure that the site plan has maximum utilization
and consequently minimum total cost of ownership. To this end, an elaborate tra ic model is
developed in POLARIS to generate trajectories of passenger and freight trips on a typical day in
2030. This tool creates a virtual world populated by individual “agents,” such as travelers and
vehicles, each with its own distinct behaviors and decision-making capabilities. By modeling the
dynamic interactions of these agents with each other and the transportation network, POLARIS is
used to analyze and predict the system-wide impacts of new technologies, policies, and
infrastructure changes on tra ic ﬂow and energy consumption.

The simulations incorporate various aspects of transportation systems into their computations,
including travel demand, network supply, and ﬂeet operations. They model a typical day of 24-hour
simulation time at 1-second resolution. Model “agents” establish intended trajectories which are
reevaluated at each time step for rerouting based on charging requirements and interactions within
the network. Numerous simulations are run until convergence to reﬁne the ﬁnal tra ic output.

To inform the agent-based modeling of freight trip generation, the Freight Analysis Framework
Version 5 (FAF5) dataset is used, providing comprehensive estimates of the weight and value of
freight moving throughout the region. INRIX ﬂeet data is also used for calibration. Although light
duty tra ic is not the focus of this study, for complete modelling of the on-road transportation
network to include congestion, light-duty vehicle tra ic is also modeled using census zone data,
population information, and transit and mode choice models.

AVMT Forecasting

forecasts are developed using historical class-speciﬁc data from the Indiana, Illinois, and Ohio
Departments of Transportation. Historical anomalies, such as reductions due to the COVID-19

Charging Infrastructure Siting and Sizing
The trajectory and energy consumption outputs from the POLARIS simulations are provided to
EVI-Pro HD to determine optimal public charging station locations and capacities. Considering
factors such as vehicle types, operational patterns, and battery technology, EVI-Pro HD provides
customized recommendations for the optimal required charging power levels and strategic
placement of charging infrastructure. The optimization explicitly considers energy needs based on
vehicle class, vocation, required range, and operational parameters to ensure that each sited
location meets projected demand e iciently. Through coordinated use of POLARIS and EVI-Pro HD,
the siting e ort converges to an optimal siting plan which speciﬁes where charging stations would
be required to meet projected freight charging demand and the charging capacities needed at these
locations to cater to the power requirements of the tra ic network.

A ﬁltered set of approximately 500 prospective locations, corresponding to key network nodes, is

Cost and Budget Analysis for Public Charging Stations
Key Operational Decisions for Fleet Electriﬁcation and Public Charging
Buildout
Fleet owners evaluating the transition to zero-emission vehicles (ZEVs) must carefully balance
internal infrastructure investment with operational cost control. Critical decisions need to be made
around charging strategy optimization, route and dwell time analysis, infrastructure investment
planning, and total cost of ownership assessment. With corridor fast charging averaging $0.58/kWh
today, ﬂeets must assess how much energy can be shifted to lower-cost depot or private charging.
Depot charging typically o ers signiﬁcantly reduced rates, especially when paired with managed
charging and favorable utility tari s. Electriﬁcation success hinges on aligning routes and
schedules with charging availability. Fleets must identify which vehicles can reliably return to base
for overnight charging versus those requiring enroute fast charging. Determining the right scale of
private infrastructure involves forecasting energy demand, vehicle growth, and site constraints.
Overbuilding leads to underutilized assets; underbuilding risks reliance on expensive public
charging. As has been said previously, early coordination with utilities is essential to secure
capacity, minimize demand charges, and explore incentives. Smart charging systems and energy
storage can further reduce peak loads and costs.

Another key aspect of MD-HD infrastructure readiness is on the investment in publicly available
charging stations. In the Midwest, the capital and operational economics of commercial vehicle

charging diverge meaningfully between public truck-stop sites and private ﬂeet depots, largely due
to di erences in utilization patterns, electrical design requirements, and ownership objectives.
Truck-stop charging sites, typically located along interstate freight corridors, face higher upfront
capital requirements driven by multi-port, high-power DC fast charging installations intended to
serve diverse ﬂeets with limited dwell time. Capital costs at these sites include chargers and
dispensers, medium-voltage service extensions, large transformers, switchgear, and substantial
civil construction to accommodate heavy vehicles, circulation, and queuing. While many Midwest
truck stops already control land and have favorable zoning, grid interconnection and make-ready
costs frequently dominate capex due to the step-change in electrical demand relative to traditional
fuel and retail loads. In contrast, depot charging sites—often serving a single ﬂeet or small number
of vehicles—can be designed around predictable duty cycles and lower simultaneous peak
demand, enabling more modest electrical upgrades and simpler site construction, particularly
where land and access are already secured.

Operationally, Midwest truck-stop charging economics are highly sensitive to electricity tari s and
early-stage utilization. Public sites are typically exposed to commercial or industrial utility rates
with demand charges that reﬂect peak power draw, making low utilization periods particularly
punitive in breakeven analyses. As a result, demand charges must be explicitly allocated across
delivered energy rather than treated as ﬁxed overhead, with throughput growth over time being the
primary mechanism for margin improvement. Additional operating costs at truck stops include
higher maintenance and uptime requirements, transaction processing and payment fees, network
and software subscriptions, site operations labor, and customer service, all of which scale with
public access expectations. Depot charging sites, by comparison, often beneﬁt from greater
control over charging schedules, enabling managed or overnight charging that reduces peak
demand exposure and lowers e ective electricity costs. Depot operational expense (opex) is
typically dominated by energy charges and routine maintenance, with minimal transaction
management costs and lower expectations for 24/7 public uptime.

From a breakeven perspective, Midwest truck-stop charging stations are best understood as
throughput-driven infrastructure assets, where ﬁnancial viability depends on sustained growth in
delivered energy to absorb high ﬁxed electrical and capital costs. These sites incur greater risk
during early deployment but provide corridor-level resilience, interoperability, and ﬂexibility that
depots cannot. Depot charging, by contrast, functions as a cost-minimization strategy for ﬂeet
operators, prioritizing predictable operations and lowest possible $/kWh rather than revenue
maximization. A complete ﬁnancial assessment should therefore evaluate truck stops and depots
under distinct success metrics, explicitly accounting for di erences in capex structure, tari
exposure, utilization ramp-up, maintenance expectations, and transaction complexity, while
recognizing that both play complementary roles in supporting medium- and heavy-duty
electriﬁcation across Midwest freight networks.

What “Allocating Demand Charges Across Energy” Means
Demand charges are ﬁxed costs determined by peak power (kW), not by total energy sold (kWh). To
evaluate charging economics on a per-kWh basis, the annual demand charge is divided by the
annual energy delivered. This converts demand charges into an e ective $/kWh cost that can be
directly compared with retail charging prices. As utilization increases, the same ﬁxed demand
charge is spread across more energy, sharply reducing its per-kWh impact.

Key Findings for Policymakers and Investors
1. Demand charges are the dominant cost driver at low utilization. Early-stage stations appear
 uneconomic if demand charges are not recovered through pricing or utilization growth.
2. Allocating demand charges across energy reveals true cost per kWh. This step is essential for
 comparing electricity costs to retail charging prices and avoiding misleading conclusions.
3. Utilization rapidly improves economics. Once utilization exceeds approximately 10–15%,
 demand charges become a much smaller fraction of total cost.
4. Public DC fast charging can meet private-capital payback thresholds. At retail prices of $0.40–
 $0.60 per kWh, even modest utilization supports payback well within 8 years. Retail DC fast
 charging prices of $0.40–$0.60/kWh reﬂect current observed pricing for high-power public truck
 charging in select corridors. Actual prices will vary based on competition, regulatory oversight,
 and utility tari s and may decline as infrastructure matures.
5. Policy levers matter most during early deployment. Demand-charge reform, managed charging,
 and utilization guarantees can signiﬁcantly reduce early-stage risk and accelerate corridor
 build-out.

Several other sensitivities were excluded for simplicity. Those include transformer replacement
risk, higher maintenance in early years, software/network fees escalation, and demand charge
ratchets or coincident peak penalties.
Conclusion
For high-power truck charging, the economic question is not whether demand charges exist,
but how quickly energy throughput grows to absorb them. When demand charges are
properly allocated across delivered energy, four-port 350 kW truck-stop installations show strong
financial performance at realistic utilization and pricing levels. This supports both public-interest
infrastructure goals and private investment participation in medium- and heavy-duty charging
corridors.

Implementation Challenges, Recommendations and Timeline
This report focuses on planning of ZEV infrastructure for MD/HD vehicles, and not deployment of
stations and equipment. The successful deployment of this phased roadmap of electric charging
across the network will be dependent on overcoming several key challenges. Some of these
challenges are specific to the region, but others are more universal across the U.S. and even in
other regions of the world.

A good case study for examining the key elements to successfully building a MD-HD EV charging
station can be seen in construction of the EV charging station by Voltera Power in Lynwood,
California. Several factors were cited as critical to moving this site from concept to reality in 18
months.

 o Location: “Proximity to freight corridors is critical to minimizing downtime and
 maximizing operational efficiency (Colbert, 2025).” The Lynwood station sits
 strategically located along the I-710 corridor serving trucks that deliver freight from the
 Port of Los Angeles and Port of Long Beach to distribution centers further inland.
 o Scalability: Designing a charging station for expansion as EV adoption increases can
 save time and cost of expanding infrastructure in the future.
 o Planning for Utility Power: Grid power upgrades can often take years, while freight
 operators need charging infrastructure now to enable adding EVs to their fleets. Early
 utility planning and phased implementation of charging capacity helped get the
 Lynwood site operational in 18 months.

Deployment schedules can be significantly affected by zoning approvals, capital fundraising, labor
and EVSE availability, including chargers, but also transformers, switchgear, cable and
construction hardware. This plan is intended to help stakeholders throughout the implementation
value chain to understand the potential timing, quantity, and size of stations needed along the

As previously noted, implementation of the DCFC base plan was originally targeted for the 2025–
2030-time frame to accommodate 6% BEV adoption. The rollback of technology-forcing
legislation, such as the repeal of the Phase III Greenhouse Gas rule and the repeal of the CARB
waiver request, have put downward pressure on the initial assumptions, leading the team to
consider a market-driven approach to EV charging for trucks. The analysis of the charging station
locations uses the adoption calculations based on the interpolation of multiple market scenarios
for both the overall commercial electric vehicle adoption as well as the breakdown of electric
vehicles by class. The roadmap should evolve as new information becomes available and EV
purchasing scenarios advance. The model-based approach used in this project is designed to
make these adjustments with some convenience.

Like in other regions, EV infrastructure can be deployed across the network in phases. Areas of
higher predicted utilization are likely to come first, serving areas where local freight deliveries are
already moving toward electrification. For regional freight, clusters of fleets that deliver
predictable runs across the same highway corridors are good cases for electrification and could
drive simultaneous development of supporting public or shared charging stations. Investment in
charging for long-haul or over-the-road trucking in the Midwest is likely to ramp up later, as EV truck
technology advances to meet the needs of the application, and the forecasted adoption increases.

Shared depot charging has been highlighted as an alternative to the traditional ‘gas station’ model
for public electric vehicle charging stations for commercial vehicles. A recent whitepaper
published by CALSTART suggests that this traditional model may work well for passenger cars and
has been the model for government-supported programs to date but is ill-suited for the needs of
freight-moving vehicles. Shared depot sites allow for the configuration of sites that are tailored to
the needs of large trucks, giving fleets a secure, reliable charging option without the burdens of
leasing or ownership (Joseph, 2024). This said, the public charging station will likely still have a role
with commercial vehicles, such as charging for smaller vocational and delivery vehicles, serving
fleets operating away from or blocked out of shared depot facilities, and in areas where space and
traffic congestion are of less concern.

Ways to address the challenges of implementing a network of charging stations are not very
dissimilar to those of passenger car vehicles but do carry some nuance. Some should be
considered shorter term priorities, while others can be considered longer term priorities. Short
term focus areas can include increasing charger availability in areas of high demand, standardizing
charging protocols to ensure interoperability, improving reliability of chargers, and implementing
smart charging solutions to manage electricity demand and cost. Longer term priorities are
building out into lower demand areas to build a national network, integrating renewable energy,
developing charging technology for faster and more efficient charging, and firming up policies and
regulations to be supportive of infrastructure growth.

Conclusions and Recommendations
Summary of key recommendations for successful implementation of the charging plan.

This project was originally planned to include several sensitivity studies to determine potential
stress on the public charging network to ensure proper sizing. Time constraints prevented the
modeling of peak traffic days, alternative state of charge assumptions, and other factors that
would identify potential bottlenecks and areas of concern for electrical and traffic loading. It is
advised that such studies be performed as part of any future implementation phase.

This project also anticipated a second phase of infrastructure siting which was to incorporate
advanced ZEV technologies such as dynamic in-road wireless power transfer (DWPT), hydrogen
refueling for vehicles powered by fuel cells or clean-burning hydrogen engines, and smart-charging
technologies for electrical load management across stations and networks. Time and technical
constraints prohibited the completion of this phase of work within the project period. However,
significant information was gathered on these technologies through project working groups that is
accessible and could prove valuable for future efforts.

High-power charging represents a critical bridge between today’s light-duty networks and
tomorrow’s cost-effective electrified freight system. Aligning public funding, private investment,
and grid planning around scalable, corridor-based infrastructure will be essential to unlocking
reliable local and regional zero-emission transport.
