Ultrasound Combined With Arthroscopy‐Assisted Localization and Reconstruction of the Medial Patellofemoral Ligament
Authors: Zirong Huang, Jiansen Xu, Jiayi Zhu, Junhui Li, Yankan Ou, Weimin Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction is an effective treatment for recurrent patellar dislocation. This technical note describes an ultrasound‐assisted femoral localization strategy for medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction, aiming to improve femoral tunnel accuracy, enhance isometry, and reduce soft‐tissue dissection. Preoperatively, ultrasound is used to identify and mark the intersection of the horizontal line through the femoral footprint of the medial collateral ligament and the course of the adductor tendon as the target region; intraoperatively, localization and reconstruction are performed at a similar knee flexion angle, with patellofemoral alignment and tracking confirmed arthroscopically. Initial observations indicate high concordance between ultrasound marking and arthroscopic anatomic localization, enabling reliable femoral‐side fixation with smaller incisions and potentially fewer complications, which may facilitate functional recovery. This strategy may shorten the learning curve and promote a more minimally invasive treatment pathway; however, its effectiveness depends on operator experience and equipment resolution and may be limited in cases of severe obesity.

Arthroscopic medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) reconstruction has become increasingly prevalent. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 However, precise arthroscopic localization of the femoral tunnel remains a technical challenge. For beginners, extensive subdeep fascial dissection on the medial femur is often required to identify key anatomical landmarks, such as the adductor tendon and the medial collateral ligament. This step is time‐consuming and significantly increases the risk of postoperative subcutaneous hematoma and even skin necrosis. 5 To shorten the learning curve and reduce complications, we describe preoperative ultrasound prelocalization to establish an initial target range for femoral tunnel placement, thereby minimizing intraoperative soft‐tissue dissection and shortening operative time. Furthermore, numerous cases have shown high concordance between ultrasound‐based preoperative localization and arthroscopic intraoperative findings. On this basis, and without compromising accuracy, we propose that femoral tunnel localization can be accomplished using ultrasound alone without arthroscopic verification, thereby advancing a minimally invasive approach to the treatment of recurrent patellar dislocation.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Step 1: Preoperative Ultrasound Localization

Perform prelocalization in collaboration with an experienced musculoskeletal ultrasonographer (EPIQ CVx, Philips). With the patient supine, flex the operative knee to approximately 30°; this angle can be adjusted to match the intraoperative trajectory of the femoral guiding pin. Palpate the medial femoral epicondyle; then, sweep the ultrasound probe proximally and distally to identify the adductor tendon and the medial collateral ligament (MCL). Alternate transverse and longitudinal scans to confirm the intersection of the “horizontal line through the femoral attachment of the MCL” and the “extended line along the course of the adductor tendon.” Mark a cross on the skin; the center denotes the preliminary entry point for the femoral tunnel (Figure 1).

(B) Long‐axis ultrasound image of the medial knee. The yellow arrowheads indicates the adductor tendon, and the red arrow indicates the medial collateral ligament. The asterisk “*” denotes the preoperative target region for femoral tunnel placement.

Step 2: Intraoperative Positioning and Surface Marking

With an assistant's support, flex the knee to approximately 30° (consistent with the preoperative position). Mark the proximal one‐third to one‐half of the patella lengthwise as the intended region for patellar‐side anchor placement (Figure 2A).

The figure shows the patient's left knee. (A) The knee is flexed to approximately 30° and fixed, consistent with the preoperative localization posture. The patellar anchor insertion site is marked, and the femoral‐side “cross” region indicates the prelocalized target. (B) A 4.5‐mm medial‐row anchor is inserted on the patellar side. (C) A 2.0‐mm Kirschner wire (K‐wire) is drilled parallel into the center of the prelocalized region as an arthroscopic “marker.” (D) The tail sutures of the patellar‐side medial‐row anchor are used to simulate the reconstructed MPFL, looping around the K‐wire while performing knee flexion‐extension to assess isometry. (MPFL, medial patellofemoral ligament.)

Step 3: Initial Patellar Fixation and Establishment of the Femoral “Cue” Pin

Stabilize the patella with 1 hand. Make a 1 cm incision at the patellar mark. Insert a 2.0‐mm Kirschner wire parallel to the patellar surface as a guide and then create a 5‐mm‐deep socket with a 5.0‐mm cannulated drill. After removing the Kirschner wire, insert the appropriate tap/driver and seat a suture anchor (FastLock, Beijing Tianxing Medical) with trailing sutures (Figure 2B). Gentle traction on the sutures should mobilize the patella in the direction of pull without anchor backout, indicating secure fixation. Next, at the center of the preoperative femoral cross mark, advance a parallel 2.0‐mm Kirschner wire to serve as an arthroscopic “cue” (Figure 2C). If arthroscopic verification is satisfactory, this point is accepted as the femoral tunnel target (Video 1).

The patient first undergoes an ultrasound in the examination room, lying supine with the left knee fixed at approximately 30° of flexion. With assistance from a musculoskeletal ultrasound specialist and centering on the medial femoral condyle, the intersection region between the adductor tendon extension line and the horizontal line through the femoral attachment of the medial collateral ligament is identified and marked. Intraoperatively, the knee is likewise maintained at approximately 30° of flexion. A 4.5 mm all‐suture anchor is first placed on the patellar side. On the femoral side, a 2.0 mm Kirschner wire is inserted as a guide pin at the premarked point. The anchor tail sutures are looped around the guide pin, and with the knee in full extension, a turning point is marked to simulate the medial patellofemoral ligament. Minimal anterior‐posterior movement of the tail sutures during flexion‐extension indicates good isometry. Under arthroscopy, soft tissues are dissected along the guide pin to confirm that it lies at the intersection of the “adductor tendon extension line” and the “superior horizontal line of the medial collateral ligament femoral footprint,” after which a 5.0 mm femoral tunnel is created. Either a synthetic ligament or an autograft is selected. It is first fixed to the patellar‐side anchor, and the midpoint of the graft is drawn into the patellar trough to establish a secure connection. The graft is then shuttled into the femoral tunnel via a passing suture. Under direct arthroscopic visualization, the 2 free limbs are progressively and evenly tensioned to achieve optimal patellar reduction. Final femoral‐side fixation and tensioning are completed with an interference screw. A lateral retinacular release is subsequently performed to optimize patellar tracking and balance lateral tension. Final verification confirms clear anatomic relationships among the adductor apparatus, the medial collateral ligament, the reconstructed medial patellofemoral ligament, and the femoral‐side reconstruction site, with the target located precisely at the intersection of the adductor tendon extension line and the aforementioned horizontal line. Step 4: “Simulated Ligament” Isometry Assessment

Tie the patellar anchor sutures to the femoral guide pin to simulate the MPFL. In full extension, mark the baseline distance between the patella and the guide pin with a marker. Cycle the knee through full range of motion and observe the length change of the “simulated ligament”. Absence of discernible migration of the mark indicates good isometry, preliminarily confirming the femoral tunnel position (Figure 2D).

Step 5: Arthroscopic Portals and Subcutaneous Tunnel Creation

Create a 5‐mm medial portal at the slight lateral depression between the medial femoral condyle and the medial tibial plateau. Introduce a straight clamp toward the femoral guide pin to create a deep subcutaneous tunnel. Similarly, from the patellar portal, develop a subcutaneous tunnel directed toward the femoral guide pin.

Step 6: Femoral‐Side Key Anatomy Exposure and Tunnel Preparation

Insert a 70° arthroscope via the medial portal and a shaver via the patellar portal. Centered on the femoral guide pin at the medial femoral condyle, progressively release the deep fascia, alternating with radiofrequency coagulation for hemostasis. Expose the deep surface of the vastus medialis to facilitate identification of the adductor apparatus. Trace the adductor tendon to its femoral insertion at the adductor tubercle and expose the MCL femoral attachment. Define the femoral MPFL attachment as the intersection of the “horizontal line through the MCL femoral footprint” and the “extended line of the adductor tendon course.” Once arthroscopy confirms that the prelocalized guide pin is accurately positioned at this point, no adjustment is required. Over the guide pin, drill a 5.0‐mm diameter, 5‐cm deep femoral tunnel. Pass a folded shuttle suture through and leave it within the tunnel to facilitate graft passage.

Step 7: Patellar‐Side Graft Fixation and Shuttle

Suture the 2 limbs of the patellar anchor sutures to the midpoint of the graft (LARS, movmedix). Apply traction to slide the graft gently into the patellar socket (socket technique) and secure with knots under appropriate tension; repeat for the second suture limb. Through the patellar portal, retrieve the femoral shuttle suture with a curved clamp, place the graft trailing sutures into the shuttle loop, and deliver the graft through the subcutaneous and femoral bone tunnels to the lateral femur. Anchor suture tails may be shuttled concurrently to augment the MPFL construct if desired.

Step 8: Patellar Reduction and Femoral‐Side Fixation

Introduce the scope into the joint via the medial portal. Tension the graft by pulling the graft limbs under arthroscopic visualization, monitoring patellar tracking throughout flexion‐extension to restore patellofemoral congruence. Then, exit the joint, re‐enter the medial subcutaneous tunnel, place a guide pin within the femoral tunnel, and secure the graft with an interference screw (Beijing Tianxing Medical) for femoral fixation.

Step 9: Lateral Retinacular Release and Hemostasis

Establish a lateral portal at the lateral parapatellar region (junction of the lateral femoral condyle and patellar tendon) to maximize visualization and working space. Under arthroscopic guidance, perform an adequate lateral retinacular release using radiofrequency. Coagulate potential bleeding points within the joint and subfascial layers.

Step 10: Intraoperative Arthroscopic Confirmation of Anatomic Relationships

Arthroscopy should clearly delineate the adductor tendon, MCL, reconstructed MPFL, and femoral fixation screw and their spatial relationships. The key arthroscopic landmark for femoral tunnel localization is the intersection of the “horizontal line through the MCL femoral footprint” and the “extended line along the adductor tendon course” (Figure 3).

Postoperative Rehabilitation

Beginning on postoperative day 2, the brace is locked at 0° with weight‐bearing as tolerated, and passive to active‐assisted knee flexion is initiated, progressing to 90° by 2 weeks. At 1 month, the brace is discontinued, allowing unbraced activities of daily living and the introduction of progressive resistance exercises. By 3 months, patients may resume low‐impact aerobic activities such as jogging, swimming, and cycling. At 6 months, return to cutting/contact or competitive sports is permitted, provided quadriceps strength and proprioception are symmetric and there is no pain or instability.

DISCUSSION

Precise femoral tunnel placement is pivotal to postoperative stability, biomechanical restoration, and clinical outcomes following MPFL reconstruction. Malposition on the femoral side can lead to nonisometry, imbalanced tension across knee flexion‐extension, aberrant patellofemoral tracking, and early graft failure, potentially resulting in redislocation and pain. 6 , 7 Although surface palpation and fluoroscopic guidance are commonly used in clinical practice and can be effective, they are susceptible to localization errors due to patient habitus, surgeon experience, knee flexion angle, and radiographic projection variability. Even with the classic Schöttle point, studies indicate high technique sensitivity and strong dependence on positioning and image quality. 8 , 9 Consequently, an anatomy‐based arthroscopic femoral localization strategy has been increasingly recommended, using recognition of the adductor tubercle and the femoral footprint of the MCL to approximate the native attachment, thereby improving isometry and tracking. 5 However, for novices, identifying deep soft‐tissue anatomy arthroscopically remains challenging, the learning curve is steep, and broader deep fascial release is often required—factors that increase operative time and the risk of postoperative subcutaneous hematoma and cutaneous complications.

To shorten the learning curve and reduce complications, some authors have proposed preoperative metal marker localization combined with computed tomography, allowing 3‐dimensional assessment and adjustment of the skin marker's deviation from the ideal femoral target. 4 Although this approach can enhance the accuracy of preoperative planning to some extent, in patients with obesity, greater subcutaneous fat thickness introduces systematic bias between the skin marker and the bony landmark, impairing intraoperative reproducibility. Moreover, the preoperative 3‐dimensional imaging workflow increases time and cost and entails radiation exposure.

Ultrasound offers real‐time imaging, superior soft‐tissue discrimination, and bedside accessibility in the musculoskeletal system and has increasingly been applied to localization and interventions around the knee. In our technique, an experienced musculoskeletal ultrasonographer is integrated into the perioperative team. With the knee flexed to approximately 30° preoperatively, the intersection of the “horizontal line through the MCL femoral footprint” and the “extended line along the adductor tendon course” is used as the ultrasound‐guided target for femoral tunnel prelocalization, followed by arthroscopic verification. Our clinical practice shows a high concordance between the prelocalized point and the arthroscopic anatomic target, suggesting that an anatomically reliable femoral tunnel entry can be achieved with reduced deep dissection. For surgeons proficient in musculoskeletal ultrasound, intraoperative femoral localization can be performed directly under ultrasound, reducing or even obviating arthroscopic re‐verification. This shift—from limited dissection to near no dissection—shortens operative time and lowers the risk of subcutaneous hematoma and wound‐related complications.

Biomechanically, the femoral attachment of the MPFL lies near the adductor tubercle and the MCL femoral footprint; deviations from this region significantly alter graft length change and tension distribution through flexion‐extension, affecting patellar stress and tracking. 10 The 2 reproducible sonographic landmarks we employ (the MCL femoral footprint and the adductor tendon course) are superficial and present with clear echogenic features. Alternating transverse and longitudinal scanning facilitates reliable reproduction of the intersection across diverse body habitus. Compared with palpation alone or fluoroscopy‐based localization, ultrasound simultaneously visualizes soft‐tissue trajectories and bony landmarks, offering greater intuitiveness and procedural control. Furthermore, preoperative ultrasound localization and skin marking can be completed in the clinic, promoting workflow standardization, team coordination, and interoperator consistency.

Despite its feasibility and potential advantages, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the accuracy of ultrasound localization depends on operator experience and equipment resolution, with an inherent learning curve. Second, in cases of severe obesity or limited deep acoustic windows, landmark identification may still be affected. Third, current evidence primarily reflects our center's technical feasibility and arthroscopic concordance; prospective comparative studies are needed to evaluate differences between ultrasound‐based and fluoroscopy/arthroscopy‐based anatomic localization in isometry, clinical outcomes, complications, and cost‐effectiveness. In addition, multicenter reproducibility and generalizability across surgeons with varying training backgrounds remain to be validated. We therefore propose future research directions that include randomized or matched studies assessing quantitative femoral tunnel deviation, operative time, extent of soft‐tissue dissection, perioperative complication rates, and mid‐ to long‐term outcomes. Postoperative computed tomography or 3‐dimensional template registration may further validate tunnel accuracy, and standardized ultrasound training and competency assessments should be developed to enhance cross‐center consistency.

In summary, an ultrasound‐plus‐arthroscopy strategy for femoral localization during MPFL reconstruction provides a reproducible, minimally invasive pathway for novice surgeons, with the potential to shorten the learning curve, reduce wound‐related complications, and lay the groundwork for a fully ultrasound‐guided approach. In line with existing evidence regarding anatomic attachment, isometry, and the limitations of fluoroscopic localization, our technique adheres to the core principles of anatomic fidelity, isometric restoration, and minimized dissection and merits further validation and optimization in higher‐level studies.
