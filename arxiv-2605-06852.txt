Fluctuation-driven chiral ferromagnetism
Rokas Veitas, Ahmed Khalifa, Francisco Machado, Shubhayu Chatterjee

Abstract
In general, quantum fluctuations are suppressed in ferromagnetic materials because they admit a simple unfrustrated ground state, greatly limiting the scope of phenomena that can be observed in these materials. In this work, we show how magnetization-non-conserving couplings fundamentally alter this paradigm by demonstrating the existence of a chiral ferromagnet that is stabilized by quantum fluctuations. More specifically, we show how these spin-orbit interactions modify the classical phase diagram; whereas a classical analysis predicts only collinear states, we observe fluctuation-stabilized phases, including a ferromagnet with large orbital chirality and a chiral stripe regime. We elucidate how such couplings spontaneously generate a scalar orbital chirality, in contrast to conventional mechanisms which rely upon a field-induced canting of vector chiral order. The resultant chiral states exhibit distinct transport signatures, namely an enhanced thermal Hall effect, and are of direct relevance to moiré heterostructures, Rydberg-atom arrays, and solid-state materials featuring non-Kramers spins.



Quantum fluctuations are instrumental to generate and stabilize new correlated electronic phenomena, whether in the context of charge, spin, or orbital degrees of freedom
37
.
However, such fluctuations are strongly suppressed when a system admits a simple, classical ground state configuration
3
.
Consequently, diagnosing regimes where large quantum fluctuations can emerge within classically ordered phases constitutes a key challenge in quantum many-body physics as it serves as a precursor to qualitatively distinct physical phenomena.

One paradigmatic setting of particular interest are ferromagnets.
Their lack of frustration admits simple classical ground states which have largely limited explorations of quantum fluctuation driven phenomena.
This contrasts sharply with antiferromagnets (AFM), where even nearest-neighbor interactions on bipartite lattices induce quantum fluctuations, further enhanced when the system exhibits geometric frustration or longer-ranged interactions
34
;
21
;
9
.
Such quantum fluctuations enable fascinating correlated phenomena in AFMs, ranging from entangled spin liquids
38
;
48
;
7
;
11
to deconfined quantum criticality
40
;
43
;
39
.
In ferromagnets (FM), modifying lattice geometry or interaction range leaves the classical ground state intact, rendering such routes ineffective.
Establishing mechanisms that can induce and control strong quantum fluctuations in FM systems therefore holds promise to design and realize unexplored quantum phases.

Motivated by current advances in quantum simulators across both condensed matter and ultracold atomic platforms, we demonstrate how ubiquitous spin-orbit interactions can induce strong quantum fluctuations in conventional ferromagnets, leading to novel non-perturbative effects.
Specifically, symmetry-constrained spin-orbit coupling terms promote the spontaneous generation of scalar orbital chirality, even when the underlying classical ordering patterns are strictly collinear [Fig.
1
].
Remarkably, the resulting scalar chirality does not originate from the conventional classical mechanism of combining vector chirality and field-induced magnetization, but rather from quantum fluctuations generated by spin non-conserving terms.
The resultant state exhibits a non-zero thermal Hall current.
Finally, we argue that such quantum fluctuation stabilized phases are broadly accessible, by outlining concrete implementations in moiré heterostructures, neutral-atom arrays and solid-state materials hosting non-Kramers spins.

Model and Symmetries.—

To illustrate our proposal in the simplest context, let us consider a nearest-neighbor (NN) ferromagnetic XXZ Hamiltonian on a triangular lattice, where the pseudospin-up and -down states are encoded in orbital angular momentum degrees of freedom (we denote the local pseudospin operator with
\sigma^{z}
).
The proposed orbital encoding has two important effects.
First, while
\sigma^{z}
is flipped under time-reversal (i.e. is odd), the in-plane projection of the pseudospin
(\sigma^{x},\sigma^{y})
transforms trivially (i.e. is even) under the same symmetry action.
As a result, out-of-plane ordering corresponds to ferromagnetism, while in-plane ordering corresponds to ferroelectricity, enabling novel multiferroic phases.
Second, such orbital degrees of freedom often arise from on-site potentials with a smaller threefold symmetry
18
, naturally reducing symmetry of in-plane
\bm{\sigma}
-rotations to
C_{3v}
.
The resulting NN Hamiltonian takes the form

\begin{split}H&=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{z}\sigma^{z}_{i}\sigma^{z}_{j}+\left(\frac{J}{2}\sigma^{+}_{i}\sigma^{-}_{j}+\mbox{h.c.}\right)\\
&\quad-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{+}(\gamma_{ij}\,\sigma^{+}_{i}\sigma^{+}_{j}+\mbox{h.c.})\end{split}

(1)

where the first two terms correspond to a simple ferromagnetic Hamiltonian with easy-axis and easy-plane anisotropy, respectively.
Henceforth, we set
J=1
.

In this work, we focus on the third term
J_{+}
which naturally dresses the easy-axis ferromagnet with quantum fluctuations.
Notably, it is the only bond-dependent coupling allowed by the
C_{3v}
point group; this symmetry also fixes the phase
\gamma_{ij}=e^{-i2\phi_{ij}}
, where
\phi_{ij}
is the angle made by the bond
\langle ij\rangle
with the
+\hat{x}
-coordinate axis.
This sign structure implies that flipping the sign of
J_{+}
is equivalent to a pseudospin rotation of
\pi/2
about
\hat{z}
; for this reason we restrict our analysis to
J_{+}\geq 0
.

By contrast, interaction terms that mix between in- and out-of-plane components (e.g.
\sigma^{+}\sigma^{z}
), often studied in the context of spin Hamiltonians with spin-orbit coupling
23
;
26
;
25
;
14
;
30
, are strictly prohibited by time-reversal.
We note that, while the lack of inversion symmetry of
C_{3v}
permits a Dzyalozinskii-Moriya (DM) interaction, we have found that a weak DM term does not significantly affect the quantum phase diagram so we focus on the regime where
\imaginary[J]=0
.
This condition is enforced if the system preserves
C_{6}
symmetry.

Collinear classical ordering.—
We begin by considering the classical zero-temperature phase diagram of
H
.
When
J_{+}
is absent, the Hamiltonian
H
has no frustration and the ground state becomes a simple translation-invariant state: a ferromagnet (FM) for
J_{z}>J
, and an in-plane ferroelectric (FE) with a non-zero value of the electrical polarization
\mathbf{P}=\hat{z}\times\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle
for
J_{z}<J
.
On the other hand, at large
J_{+}
, we expect a configuration that breaks both translation and rotation (
C_{3z}
) symmetries: the phase structure of the bond-dependent term favors different ordering directions around each site, preferring an antiferroelectric (AFE) stripe order with a two-site unit cell.

To put this intuition on a firmer footing, we carry out a Luttinger-Tisza (LT) analysis of
H

28
;
29
;
16
.
The preferred ordering patterns are determined by the minimum-energy eigenvector of the Fourier-transformed
3\times 3
interaction matrix
J_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}({\bm{q}})
in pseudospin space.
Within the weak LT approximation, the pseudospins are only constrained to have unit length on average.
Once such an energy minimum is found, it corresponds to a classical configuration only if it survives the imposition of the strong LT constraint, which dictates that each individual pseudospin must have unit length.

The results from this LT computation are consistent with the expectations outlined previously.
For small
J_{+}
, the minimum of
J_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}({\bm{q}})
lies at the
\Gamma
point (
{\bm{q}}=0
), indicating uniform polarization either in-plane (ferrolectricity) or out-of-plane (ferromagnetism).
However, beyond a critical value of
J_{+}
, the minimum of the interaction matrix moves to the
M
point, indicating the stabilization of an antiferroelectric stripe phase.
Classically, therefore, the phase diagram of
H
consists of three simple collinear phases — a result further confirmed by a numerical optimization of energy over classical configurations in larger unit cells.

The LT analysis already offers some insight into the importance of quantum fluctuations in the phase diagram
20
.
Notably, at the boundary between the FE and stripe phases (
J_{+}=J
), the energy minima have two distinct features.
First, they are not unique: solutions to the weak LT conditions are satisfied along the lines that connect the
\Gamma
and the
M
points, corresponding to collinear incommensurate stripes that interpolate smoothly between the FE and the AFE stripe
1
.
This suggests a large degree of degeneracy.
Second, these solutions cannot satisfy the strong LT condition, indicating that they do not admit a simple classical spin configuration.

Quantum phase diagram.—
To find the quantum ground state of
H
, we employ large-scale tensor network computations with VUMPS on cylinders of width 4–8

42

We find that quantum fluctuations significantly modify the classical phase diagram over large swathes of parameter space, namely by restricting the orientation of the FE state and inducing a chiral ferromagnet.

For the FE phase, while the classical LT analysis allows for

to point in any direction in the plane, VUMPS ground state optimization finds that the ordering is locked perpendicular to the direction of the lattice vectors.
This may be intuitively understood via the order by disorder mechanism

36
;
4

C_{3v}
symmetric

The most remarkable effect of quantum fluctuations, however, shows up at the phase boundary between the FE and the stripe: the entire vicinity of the classical phase boundary is replaced by the FM, even as
J_{z}\to 0
[Fig.

However, this FM state is very distinct from the collinear FM at large

By contrast, the FM state at small

More significantly, its scalar orbital chirality,

To understand the origin of this chirality, let us start with the fully polarized FM:
\ket{\psi}=\prod_{i}\otimes\ket{\downarrow}
.
In addition to permuting sites, the action of the
C_{3z}
rotation attaches a phase
\omega
to up-spins and
\omega^{2}
to down-spins.
Assuming the number of sites is a multiple of
3
, then
\ket{\psi}
is an eigenstate of
C_{3z}
with eigenvalue of 1.
We may then imagine evolving the state with our Hamiltonian
H
in imaginary time to reach the actual ground state.
At each step, the
J_{+}
term creates two spin flips, so the spin contribution to the
C_{3z}
eigenvalue is
\omega^{2}
.
To remain a trivial representation of
C_{3z}
, the spatial part of the wavefunction needs to acquire a phase
\omega
under rotation, which is imparted by the associated phase factors
\gamma_{ij}
.
An explicit computation demonstrates that this imparts chirality into the cluster.
The term
J_{+}
thus serves to smoothly generate chirality at the expense of
z
-magnetization, changing the character of the ferromagnet while preserving its symmetry properties.

Notably this mechanism for generating scalar chirality is distinct from previous studies focusing on chiral antiferromagnets
12
;
33
;
13
;
19
.
There, either an explicit DM term in the Hamiltonian or spin-spin interactions drive the formation of a non-zero expectation of the vector chirality
\bm{D}_{jk}=\langle\bm{\sigma}_{j}\times\bm{\sigma}_{k}\rangle
, which combined with a non-zero magnetization
{\mathbf{m}}_{i}
induces a finite, three-body scalar chirality
\chi_{ijk}\approx{\mathbf{m}}_{i}\cdot\bm{D}_{jk}
.
In our calculation,
|\langle\bm{D}_{jk}\rangle|\lesssim 10^{-7}
, highlighting that the observed scalar chirality arises from correlated quantum fluctuations of different in- and out-of-plane pseudo-spin components.

Phase transitions and the intermediate chiral stripe.—
We now characterize the phase transitions present in the
J_{+}
-
J_{z}
phase diagram.
The transition between the FE and the FM is discontinuous (i.e. first-order) except at the special
\mathrm{SU}(2)
-symmetric point
J_{z}=J
and
J_{+}=0
.
We see this with VUMPS as a non-differentiable kink in the energy density and a finite region of “metastability” around the transition where VUMPS can converge ground states from the opposite phase.
This is expected; the FE and FM phases break different symmetries (
C_{3z}
and
\cal{T}
respectively) and a continuous phase transition is thus Landau-forbidden unless fine-tuned.
By the same argument, the transition between the FM (which breaks
\cal{T}
and
M_{y}
) and the stripe antiferroelectric (which breaks translation and
C_{3z}
) should also be first order.
However, the obtained energies vary smoothly and the transfer matrix correlation length appears to diverge [Fig.
3
(a)], suggesting that the transition is continuous, or at least, weakly first-order.

One possible reason for these observations is that the system exhibits not a single direct transition, but rather two closely spaced continuous phase transitions: first from the FM to a chiral stripe and then to the collinear stripe phase.
However, the chiral stripe phase might be difficult to distinguish in VUMPS.
The infinite cylinder geometry explicitly breaks
C_{3z}
, allowing the system to smoothly connect between the FM and the stripe phase.
Indeed, already within the FM region, we observe an asymmetry in the structure factor for the three
C_{3z}
-related
M
points which grows as one approaches the stripe phase [Fig.
3
(b)].

Given these limitations, we perform an exact diagonalization (ED) study
44
near the phase boundary on clusters that explicitly preserve all the symmetries.
Between the FM and stripe phase, the system displays two degenerate ground states of opposite chirality, featuring out-of-plane uniform ferromagnetic correlations (which break
\mathcal{T}
) as well as in-plane ferroelectric correlations at the three
M
points (suggesting translation symmetry breaking)—this indicates the presence of an intermediate stripe phase with finite scalar orbital chirality [Fig.
3
(c)].

We now complement this finite-sized analysis with a thermodynamic mean-field theory of the chiral stripe phase.
We construct a Landau free-energy functional that relaxes the unit-length constraint, enabling us to incorporate fluctuations while being able to accurately describe the vicinity of the continuous phase transition.
The free energy is described by a real soft field
m_{0}
and three complex soft fields

(2)

In the SM

and

Anomalous thermal Hall effect.—

A key experimental feature of states with spontaneous orbital chirality is an anomalous Hall signal.
Accordingly, the magnons in the chiral ferromagnet experience the Berry curvature of their Bloch bands, and can therefore exhibit a thermal Hall effect
17
.
To explicitly demonstrate this transport signature, we compute the thermal Hall conductivity
\kappa_{xy}
for the ferromagnet.

Starting with the fully polarized FM, we compute the magnon spectra
\varepsilon({\bm{k}})
and wavefunctions using the Holstein-Primakoff transformation
3
.
The complex phases in the quadratic magnon Hamiltonian, detailed in the SM
1
, break both time-reversal and reflection
M_{y}
, inducing Berry curvature
\Omega({\bm{k}})
for the magnon band [Fig.
4
(a)].
Accordingly, the 2D magnon thermal Hall conductivity takes the form,

\kappa_{xy}=-\frac{k_{B}^{2}T}{\hbar}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\left[c_{2}[n_{B}({\bm{k}})]-\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}\right]\Omega({\bm{k}}),

(3)

where
n_{B}({\bm{k}})=[\exp(\varepsilon(\k)/k_B T)-1]^{-1}
and
c_{2}[x]=\int_{0}^{x}dt\ln^{2}\left(\frac{1+t}{t}\right)
.
As is evident from Fig.
4
(b), the increased magnon thermal Hall conductance from decreasing
J/J_{+}
signals significant enhancement of the orbital chirality at smaller
J
.
We carefully note, however, that spin-wave theory can only partially capture such an effect, because it does not incorporate the entangled nature of the chiral orbital FM.
A complete and accurate characterization of the thermal Hall signal in this entangled chiral ferromagnet is an interesting direction for a future study.

Experimental realizations.—

Having discussed the wealth of quantum phases observable in a ferromagnetic XXZ Hamiltonian supplemented with a single, symmetry-constrained, magnetization non-conserving coupling, we now turn to discuss settings where our proposed Hamiltonian can be naturally realized.
We propose three distinct platforms.

One setting of particular interest is a lattice of moiré Wigner molecules in twisted
\Gamma
-valley TMDs
35
;
45
;
27
, observed recently in twisted homo-bilayer WS
2

22
.
Here, each molecule is localized at a triangular moiré superlattice site and possesses a four-dimensional low-energy state space composed of orbital angular momentum
\sigma^{z}=\pm 1
and spin
\pm 1/2

18
.
Spin-polarizing with an in-plane field preserves the time-reversal symmetry of the orbital pseudospin, resulting in the Hamiltonian found in Eq. (
1
).
For WS
2
, this can be achieved with a moiré lattice scale of 4 nm and a dielectric coefficient of 11
18
.

A second setting of contemporary interest is a triangular Rydberg array, where the pseudospins are encoded in two Rydberg states
2
;
8
;
5
.
The dipolar coupling between the Rydberg states naturally includes a spin non-conserving term with the necessary phase structure
24
.
While such terms are typically neglected due to level’s large energy difference
41
, bringing them to be resonant immediately unlocks the form of interactions considered in Eq. (
1
)
47
.
Exploring the ferromagnetic phase diagram using the native antiferromagnetic interactions can be done by initializing the system in the highest excited state, and adiabatically preparing the highest excited state of
-H
, or equivalently, the ground state of
H

.
We note that, in such platforms, one can explore how long-range interactions help to suppress or enhance the system’s quantum fluctuations.

Finally, materials featuring non-Kramers spins (e.g., rare earth compounds on a triangular lattice), naturally exhibit the interactions studied in this work

;
14
;
30
.
Whether the signs of the couplings are ferro- or anti-ferromagnetic depends on the microscopic exchange pathways and requires additional exploration.

Conclusions and outlook.—
In this paper, we demonstrated that quantum fluctuations aided by spin-orbit coupling can spontaneously induce scalar orbital chirality in a ferromagnet.
The orbital chirality arises spontaneously via strong quantum fluctuations near the phase boundary between classically collinear phases, in contrast to previous works
19
;
13
where it arises from a non-zero expectation value of time-reversal symmetric vector chirality in combination with an external field-induced magnetization.
This opens up a new category of quantum correlated ferromagnets to explore.

By showing that ferromagnetic Hamiltonians can exhibit strong quantum fluctuations when perturbed with bond-dependent couplings, our work naturally motivates several future directions.
First, we studied the spontaneous emergence of chirality in the
T=0
phase diagram.
An important question is the effect of thermal fluctuations, which have been proposed to enhance such chirality within the framework of spin-wave theory in chiral antiferromagnets
12
.
Second, the chiral orbital ferromagnet has low-energy magnon excitations, but their quantum wavefunction is expected to be very distinct from a conventional spin-wave excitation in a product state FM.
It would be interesting to study how orbital chirality ‘dresses’ the magnon wavefunction and impacts the magnon spectrum and dynamics.
Finally, such phase-constrained anisotropic perturbations to an XXZ Hamiltonian are not specific to the triangular lattice: they will realistically be present in any lattice with local pseudospin degrees of freedom encoded in degenerate chiral/anti-chiral orbitals.
We leave a detailed construction of such models and a study of their quantum phase diagrams to future work.

II
Classical Energy (Luttinger-Tisza)

In this section, we provide the details of the Luttinger-Tizsa analysis that is used in the main text.
the method represents quantum spins as classical two-dimensional vectors with unit length, with the Hamiltonian with anisotropic spin interactions taking the general form,

H=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}J_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\sigma_{i}^{\alpha}\sigma_{j}^{\beta},

(S2.1)

on any Bravais lattice.
We proceed by writing the Hamiltonian in momentum space by taking

\bm{\sigma}_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\bm{k}}\tilde{\bm{\sigma}}_{\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}_{i}}

(S2.2)

The Hamiltonian can be written as

H=\sum_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{J}^{\alpha\beta}(\bm{k})\tilde{\bm{\sigma}}_{-\bm{k}}^{\alpha}\tilde{\bm{\sigma}}_{\bm{k}}^{\beta},

(S2.3)

where
\mathcal{J}^{\alpha\beta}(\bm{k})
is the Fourier-transformed interaction matrix.
This becomes a quadratic form for which one needs to find the spin configuration that minimizes the energy, subject to the unit length constraint on each spin (strong constraint) that usually makes the problem analytically intractable.
The Luttinger-Tisza method relaxes this constraint to the weak constraint as a necessary (but not sufficient) condition that the strong constraint is followed,

\sum_{i}|\bm{\sigma}_{i}|^{2}=N,

(S2.4)

or, equivalently in momentum space:

\sum_{\bm{k}}|\bm{\sigma}_{\bm{k}}|^{2}=N,

(S2.5)

where
N
is the number of unit cells.
The problem of minimization simplifies into an eigenvalue problem, and if it happens that the strong constraint is satisfied then a valid classical ground state is found.

It is useful to write
H
(equation (1) in the main text) in Cartesian form
14

H=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\left[J_{z}\sigma_{i}^{z}\sigma_{j}^{z}+J_{xx}(\sigma_{i}^{x}\sigma_{j}^{x}+\sigma_{i}^{y}\sigma_{j}^{y})+4J_{+}(\bm{\sigma}_{i}\cdot\hat{e}_{ij})(\bm{\sigma}_{j}\cdot\hat{e}_{ij})\right],

(S2.6)

where
J_{xx}=J-2J_{+}
and
\hat{e}_{ij}
is the unit vector pointing from site
i
to site
j
.

Then the Fourier transform of the interaction matrix of our Hamiltonian takes the following 3 by 3 matrix form

\mathcal{J}({\bm{k}})=\sum_{\alpha}[\mathbf{J}(\hat{e}_{\alpha})\cos(\k\cdot\bm{a}_\alpha)],

(S2.7)

\mathbf{J}(\hat{e})=-\begin{pmatrix}J_{xx}+4J_{+}e_{x}^{2}&4J_{+}e_{x}e_{y}&0\\
4J_{+}e_{x}e_{y}&J_{xx}+4J_{+}e_{y}^{2}&0\\
0&0&J_{z}\end{pmatrix}

(S2.8)

\bm{a}_{1,2,3}
. (b) The BZ along with reciprocal lattice vectors. The high symmetry points are also shown.

We already know that there are the in-plane FE solution, stripe solution and the FM so we can see where they occur in parameter space.

FE
: we set
{\bm{k}}=0
in equation
S2.7
to get

\mathcal{J}(0)=-\begin{pmatrix}3J_{xx}+6J_{+}&0&0\\
0&3J_{xx}+6J_{+}&0\\
0&0&3J_{z}\end{pmatrix}=-\begin{pmatrix}3J&0&0\\
0&3J&0\\
0&0&3J_{z}\end{pmatrix}

(S2.9)

Note that we used the fact that
J_{xx}=J-2J_{+}
. We see that this matrix has two degenerate minimum eigenvalues at
\lambda=-3J
when
J>J_{z}
. There are two degenerate eigenvectors that can point either in the
x
or the
y
direction thus the state can point anywhere in the plane with the same energy. The classical state has accidental U(1) symmetry which we will see later gets broken by quantum fluctuations which can be captured at the level of linear spin wave theory (LSWT). Note this also gives us the
FM
when
J_{z}>J
.

Stripe
: now we choose one of the M points,
{\bm{k}}_{M}=(0,\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{3}a})
.

\mathcal{J}({\bm{k}}_{M})=\begin{pmatrix}J_{xx}-2J_{+}&0&0\\
0&6J_{+}+J_{xx}&0\\
0&0&J_{z}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}J-4J_{+}&0&0\\
0&J+4J_{+}&0\\
0&0&J_{z}\end{pmatrix}

(S2.10)

The minimum eigenvalue corresponding to the stripe is found with
\lambda=J-4J_{+}
with the eigenvector being completely in the
x
direction. A general solution is
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{r})=Re[\bm{v}e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{r}}]
which in this case is simply
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{r})=\cos(\k_M \cdot\bm{r})\hat{x}
. For the
M
point, the cosine factor is either
1
or
-1
at any point on the lattice thus this is a constant length spin vector (strong constraint is satisfied) that is alternating in a stripe fashion. In contrast with the FE there is no U(1) degeneracy as the stripes are locked to the lattice. Furthermore, We can determine that the transition between the FE and the stripe happens at
J_{+}=J
.

III
Spin Wave Excitations

III.1
Ferroelectric

We use the vacuum predicted classically where the spins order in the
xy
plane where we take the general case of pins pointing at an angle
\phi
in the xy plane. so we rotate the spin operators.

We define a local rotating coordinate system
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})
such that the new
z^{\prime}
-axis aligns with the classical spin direction
\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle
. The basis vectors are:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(\cos\phi,\sin\phi,0),

(S3.1)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(\sin^{2}\phi,-\sin\phi\cos\phi,-\cos\phi),

(S3.2)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(-\sin\phi\cos\phi,\cos^{2}\phi,-\sin\phi).

(S3.3)

The spin operators in the original laboratory frame
(x,y,z)
can be expressed in terms of the local frame operators
(\sigma^{x^{\prime}},\sigma^{y^{\prime}},\sigma^{z^{\prime}})
as:

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{z}

\displaystyle=-\cos\phi~\sigma_{i}^{x^{\prime}}-\sin\phi~\sigma_{i}^{y^{\prime}},

(S3.4)

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{x}

\displaystyle=\sin^{2}\phi~\sigma_{i}^{x^{\prime}}-\sin\phi\cos\phi~\sigma_{i}^{y^{\prime}}+\cos\phi~\sigma_{i}^{z^{\prime}},

(S3.5)

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{y}

\displaystyle=-\sin\phi\cos\phi~\sigma_{i}^{x^{\prime}}+\cos^{2}\phi~\sigma_{i}^{y^{\prime}}+\sin\phi~\sigma_{i}^{z^{\prime}}.

(S3.6)

We introduce bosonic creation and annihilation operators
a_{i}^{\dagger},a_{i}
via the Holstein-Primakoff transformation:

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{z^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=2a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}-1,

(S3.7)

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{x^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(a_{i}^{\dagger}+a_{i}),

(S3.8)

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{y^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=i(a_{i}-a_{i}^{\dagger}).

(S3.9)

Substituting these into the spin operators,

\sigma_{i}^{z}=-(e^{-i\phi}a_{i}^{\dagger}+e^{i\phi}a_{i}).

(S3.10)

Substituting the expressions for
\sigma^{x^{\prime}}
and
\sigma^{y^{\prime}}
into
\sigma^{\pm}
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{+}

\displaystyle=e^{i\phi}\left[2a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}-1+\left(e^{-i\phi}a_{i}^{\dagger}-e^{i\phi}a_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{-}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\phi}\left[2a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}-1-\left(e^{-i\phi}a_{i}^{\dagger}-e^{i\phi}a_{i}\right)\right].

Figure S2:
Representative plots for the magnon spectrum of the three classical states. (a) The FE magnon band (
J_{z}=0.5,~J=1.0,~J_{+}=0.5
). It is gapless at the gamma point. (b) The stripe magnon band for (
J_{z}=0.5,~J=1.0,~J_{+}=1.5
). (c) The FM magnon band for (
J_{z}=1.5,~J=1.0,~J_{+}=0.5
). Both the stripe and the FM bands are gapped.

We substitute the bosonic forms into the Hamiltonian,

-J_{z}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\sigma_{i}^{z}\sigma_{j}^{z}=-J_{z}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\left[e^{2i\phi}a_{i}a_{j}+a_{i}a_{j}^{\dagger}+a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+e^{-2i\phi}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}^{\dagger}\right].

(S3.11)

We need to expand terms like
\sigma_{i}^{+}\sigma_{j}^{-}
.

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{+}\sigma_{j}^{-}

\displaystyle=\left[2a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}-1+\left(e^{-i\phi}a_{i}^{\dagger}-e^{i\phi}a_{i}\right)\right]\left[2a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}-1-\left(e^{-i\phi}a_{j}^{\dagger}-e^{i\phi}a_{j}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-2(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}+a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j})-\left(e^{-i\phi}a_{i}^{\dagger}-e^{i\phi}a_{i}\right)\left(e^{-i\phi}a_{j}^{\dagger}-e^{i\phi}a_{j}\right)+\mathcal{O}(a^{4})

(S3.12)

note that linear and cubic terms sum to zero.

We expand
\sigma_{i}^{+}\sigma_{j}^{+}
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{+}\sigma_{j}^{+}

\displaystyle=e^{2i\phi}\left[2a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}-1+\left(e^{-i\phi}a_{i}^{\dagger}-e^{i\phi}a_{i}\right)\right]\left[2a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}-1+\left(e^{-i\phi}a_{j}^{\dagger}-e^{i\phi}a_{j}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=e^{2i\phi}\left(e^{-i\phi}a_{i}^{\dagger}-e^{i\phi}a_{i}\right)\left(e^{-i\phi}a_{j}^{\dagger}-e^{i\phi}a_{j}\right)+\mathcal{O}(a^{3})

(S3.13)

Note that other chemical potential, and linear terms sum up to zero when including the bond-dependent phases. We also make a unitary transformation
a_{i}\rightarrow e^{-i\phi}a_{i}
which cancels the phase in the spin conserving terms as it should but keeps the phase factor outside of
J_{+}
term.

With this we write the quadratic part of the Hamiltonian.

H^{(2)}_{\mathrm{SW}}=3J_{\pm}\sum_{i}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}+\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\left[\Delta_{ij}a_{i}a_{j}+t_{ij}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{i}+t_{ij}^{\ast}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+\Delta_{ij}^{\ast}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}^{\dagger}\right],

(S3.14)

where
t_{ij}=-J_{z}-2J+\tilde{\gamma}_{ij}J_{+}
and
\Delta_{ij}=-J_{z}+2J-\tilde{\gamma}_{ij}J_{+}
. Here
\tilde{\gamma}_{ij}=2\cos(2\phi+2n\pi/3)
with
n=0,~1,~2
for bonds along
\bm{a}_{1},~\bm{a}_{2},~\bm{a}_{3}
. By going into momentum space we get the following,

H=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{k}}\Psi_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}h(\bm{k})\Psi_{\bm{k}},

(S3.15)

where
\Psi_{\bm{k}}=(a_{\bm{k}}~a^{\dagger}_{-\bm{k}})^{T}
, and
h(\bm{k})
is given by:

h(\bm{k})=\begin{pmatrix}A(\bm{k})&B(\bm{k})\\
B(\bm{k})&A(\bm{k})\end{pmatrix},

(S3.16)

where
A(\bm{k})=3J+2(t_{1}\cos(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_1)+t_{2}\cos(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_2)+t_{3}\cos(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_3))
and
B(\bm{k})=2(\Delta_{1}\cos(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_1)+\Delta_{2}\cos(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_2)+\Delta_{3}\cos(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_3))
.

III.2
Stripe

We make the choice of a stripe order
\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle=\cos(\bm{k}_M \cdot\bm{r})\hat{x}
with
\bm{k}_{M}=(0,\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{3}})
. Similar to the FE we need to rotate our spin operators but here we need to do a site dependent rotation that makes the calculations follow in a way similar to the FE where we write the final result of the Hamiltonian:

H=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}\Psi_{{\bm{k}}}^{\dagger}h({\bm{k}})\Psi_{{\bm{k}}},

(S3.17)

where
\Psi_{{\bm{k}}}=(a_{{\bm{k}}}~a_{-{\bm{k}}}^{\dagger})^{T}
, and
h({\bm{k}})
is

h({\bm{k}})=\begin{pmatrix}A({\bm{k}})&B({\bm{k}})\\
B({\bm{k}})&A({\bm{k}})\end{pmatrix},

(S3.18)

where
A({\bm{k}})=-16J_{+}-4J-(4J_{+}+2J+2J_{z})\cos(\k\cdot\bm{a}_1)+(2J_{+}-2J+2J_{z})(\cos(\k\cdot\bm{a}_2)+\cos(\k\cdot\bm{a}_3))
and
B({\bm{k}})=(-4J_{+}-4J+2J_{z})\cos(\k\cdot\bm{a}_1)+(2J_{+}-4J-2J_{z})(\cos(\k\cdot\bm{a}_2)+\cos(\k\cdot\bm{a}_3))
.

III.3
Ferromagnet

For the ferromagnet, we do not need to rotate the spin operators. Thus, we get the following Hamiltonian

H=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}\Psi_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}h(\bm{k})\Psi_{\bm{k}},

(S3.19)

where
\Psi_{\bm{k}}=(a_{\bm{k}}~a_{-\bm{k}}^{\dagger})^{T}
and

h(\bm{k})=\begin{pmatrix}A(\bm{k})&B(\bm{k})\\
B(\bm{k})^{\ast}&A(\bm{k})\end{pmatrix},

(S3.20)

where
A(\bm{k})=-4J(\cos(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_1)+\cos(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_2)+\cos(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_3))+12J_{z}
and
B(\bm{k})=-8J_{+}(\cos(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_1)+\omega\cos(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_2)+\omega^{2}\cos(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_3))
. Representative plots for the magnon dispersion in the three classical states are presented in Fig.
S2
.

IV
Order by disorder

In the ferroelectric there is an accidental classical
U(1)
degeneracy where the moment can point anywhere in the plane which is why the magnon dispersion in this state is gapless. However, the model symmetry is reduced by the
J_{+}
term so we do not expect this accidental classical degeneracy to survive. One way to see that from LSWT is to compute the energy corrections from quantum fluctuations

\delta E=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}[\varepsilon({\bm{k}})-A({\bm{k}})]

(S4.1)

shown in Fig.
S3
. It turns out that spins that point perpendicular to the lattice principal directions have lower energy thus the
U(1)
accidental symmetry is reduced to
Z_{6}
.

Another form of order by disorder occurs along the classical degeneracy line between the FE and the stripe. we find that at small
J_{z}
the energy of the FE is smaller than the stripe and at a critical
J_{z}\approx 0.2
the stripe wins till the point where the three phases meet classically. Interestingly, at the classical tri-critical point, the FM wins the competition. Furthermore, since this is a point of phase transition, the magnon dispersion in all phases become gapless. For both the FE and the stripe this occurs at the
\Gamma
point. On the other hand, the FM magnon dispersion vanishes along the lines connecting the
\Gamma
to the
M
points. This is the same degeneracy lines occurring in LT.

U(1)
symmetry of the FE is broken in linear order in spin wave theory. States that point along the lines
\pi/3,~\pi/2
measured from the
x
axis have lower energy than the rest. (b) Along the line of the classical degeneracy between the FE and the stripe (
J=J_{+}
and
J_{z}<1.0
) for small
J_{z}
the FE has lower energy, however, above a critical
J_{z}
there is a transition and the stripe has the lower energy.

As derived previously in section
III
C, the
2\times 2
Hamiltonian
h({\bm{k}})
in Nambu space is given by

h(\bm{k})=\begin{pmatrix}A(\bm{k})&B(\bm{k})\\
B(\bm{k})^{\ast}&A(\bm{k})\end{pmatrix},

(S5.1)

where
A(\bm{k})=-4J(\cos(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_1)+\cos(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_2)+\cos(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_3))+12J_{z}
and

\mathcal{A}^{(n)}_{\mu}({\bm{k}})=i[\sigma U^{\dagger}({\bm{k}})\sigma\partial_{\mu}U({\bm{k}})]_{nn},

and

\Omega^{(n)}({\bm{k}})=\partial_{k_{x}}\mathcal{A}^{(n)}_{y}({\bm{k}})-\partial_{k_{y}}\mathcal{A}^{(n)}_{x}({\bm{k}}).

VI
Landau theory for the chiral stripe phase

In this section, we provide a detailed derivation of the Landau theory for the phase transition from the chiral orbital ferromagnet to the collinear stripe, arguing that an intermediate chiral stripe must intervene if the phase transitions are continuous.
Our ED calculations show a ground state that (i) carries non-zero angular momentum under
C_{3z}
(both chiral and anti-chiral states are seen, slightly split by an out-of-plane magnetic field), (ii) is at the
\Gamma
-point, i.e., preserves translation symmetry (ii) in-plane antiferromagnetic correlations with all nearest neighbors with peaks at the three
M
points, (iii) out-of-plane ferromagnetic correlations, and (iv) preserved
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry.
The goal of this section is to find out whether there is a classical ground state predicted by Landau theory (in the thermodynamic limit) which reproduces the properties of the quantum state by taking a superposition.
Specifically, we are looking for a classical state with a four-site unit cell, predominantly AFM nearest-neighbor correlations in-plane and ferromagnetic correlations out-of-plane.

To construct the Landau free energy functional
F
, we consider only the soft fields near the
M
points and the
\Gamma
point.
The coordinates of the three
M
points are set by
{\bm{M}}_{1}={\bm{b}}_{2}/2
,
{\bm{M}}_{2}=-{\bm{b}}_{1}/2
and
{\bm{M}}_{3}=({\bm{b}}_{1}-{\bm{b}}_{2})/2
, such that
{\bm{M}}_{1}+{\bm{M}}_{2}+{\bm{M}}_{3}=0
.
We note that under the point group generators, the
M
points transform as
C_{3z}{\bm{M}}_{1,2,3}={\bm{M}}_{2,3,1}
while
M_{y}{\bm{M}}_{1,2,3}={\bm{M}}_{1,3,2}
.
With these conventions, we can define and work out the transformation of the soft-fields using the symmetry actions defined in the Table I.

\sigma^{+}_{{\bm{r}}_{i}}=\sum_{n=1}^{3}\psi_{n}\,e^{i{\bm{M}}_{n}\cdot{\bm{r}}_{i}},\;\sigma^{z}_{{\bm{r}}_{i}}=\psi_{0}

(S6.1)

Note that
\psi_{n}
is a complex field which may be thought of as the local orientation of the component of the spin-vector at momentum
{\bm{M}}_{n}
is the complex plane, while
\psi_{0}
is a real-field.
Using Table I, we have

\sigma^{+}_{{\bm{r}}_{i}}\xrightarrow{C_{3z}}\omega^{2}\sigma^{+}_{C_{3z}({\bm{r}}_{i})}=\omega^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{3}\psi_{n}e^{i{\bm{M}}_{n}\cdot C_{3z}({\bm{r}}_{i})}=\omega^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{3}\psi_{n}e^{iC_{3z}^{-1}({\bm{M}}_{n})\cdot{\bm{r}}_{i}}=\omega^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{3}\psi_{n+1}e^{i{\bm{M}}_{n}\cdot{\bm{r}}_{i}}\equiv\sum_{n=1}^{3}\psi_{n}^{\prime}e^{i{\bm{M}}_{n}\cdot{\bm{r}}_{i}}

(S6.2)

where we have used that
C_{3z}^{-1}({\bm{M}}_{n})={\bm{M}}_{n-1}
with
{\bm{M}}_{0}\equiv{\bm{M}}_{3}
.
This implies that under
C_{3z}
,
\psi_{n}\to\psi^{\prime}_{n}=\omega^{2}\psi_{n+1}
, or in matrix notation:

\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}\\
\psi_{2}\\
\psi_{3}\end{pmatrix}\xrightarrow{C_{3z}}\omega^{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
1&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}\\
\psi_{2}\\
\psi_{3}\end{pmatrix}

(S6.3)

Similarly, under
M_{y}
, we have

\sigma^{+}_{{\bm{r}}_{i}}\xrightarrow{M_{y}}\sigma^{-}_{M_{y}({\bm{r}}_{i})}=\sum_{n=1}^{3}\bar{\psi}_{n}e^{i{\bm{M}}_{n}\cdot M_{y}({\bm{r}}_{i})}=\bar{\psi}_{1}e^{i{\bm{M}}_{1}\cdot{\bm{r}}_{i}}+\bar{\psi}_{3}e^{i{\bm{M}}_{2}\cdot{\bm{r}}_{i}}+\bar{\psi}_{2}e^{i{\bm{M}}_{3}\cdot{\bm{r}}_{i}}\equiv\sum_{n=1}^{3}\psi_{n}^{\prime}e^{i{\bm{M}}_{n}\cdot{\bm{r}}_{i}}

(S6.4)

where we have used that
{\bm{M}}_{1}
is invariant under
M_{y}
, while
{\bm{M}}_{2}
and
{\bm{M}}_{3}
are swapped by
M_{y}
.
This implies that under
M_{y}
,
(\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\psi_{3})\to(\bar{\psi}_{1},\bar{\psi}_{3},\bar{\psi}_{2})
, where
\bar{\psi}_{n}
denotes the complex conjugate of
\psi_{n}
.
Finally, under time-reversal and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, which are both on-site symmetries, we simply have
\psi_{n}\to\bar{\psi}_{n}
and
\psi_{n}\to-\psi_{n}
respectively.

For
\psi_{0}
, the transformations are much simpler,
C_{3z}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
map
\psi_{0}\to\psi_{0}
, while under
M_{y}
and
\mathcal{T}
,
\psi_{0}\to-\psi_{0}
.

The symmetry-constrained Landau free energy functional is given by

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=F^{(2)}+F^{(4)},

\displaystyle F^{(2)}

\displaystyle=r_{0}\psi_{0}^{2}+r\left(\sum_{n=1}^{3}|\psi_{n}|^{2}\right)+\lambda\,\text{Re}[\psi_{1}^{2}+\omega^{4}\psi_{2}^{2}+\omega^{2}\psi_{3}^{2}]

\displaystyle F^{(4)}

\displaystyle=u_{0}\psi_{0}^{4}+u_{1}\left(\sum_{n}|\psi_{n}|^{4}\right)+u_{2}\left(\sum_{n<n^{\prime}}|\psi_{n}|^{2}|\psi_{n^{\prime}}^{2}|\right)+u_{3}\sum_{n<n^{\prime}}\left(\text{Re}[\bar{\psi}_{n}\psi_{n^{\prime}}]\right)^{2}+w\,\psi_{0}^{2}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{3}|\psi_{n}|^{2}\right)+\ldots

(S6.5)

where we have written down only the quartic terms which have
O(2)
symmetry, and
\ldots
denote additional terms which break
O(2)
to the physical
C_{3z}
symmetry.
An example of such a term is
\psi_{0}^{2}\,\text{Re}[\psi_{1}^{2}+\omega^{4}\psi_{2}^{2}+\omega^{2}\psi_{3}^{2}]
.

First, we will analyze the theory without the ferromagnetic field at the
\Gamma
point, i.e., we will set
\psi_{0}=0
.
Later, we will argue that having
\psi_{0}\neq 0
does not change our main conclusions.

It is convenient to move to a representation of the order parameter
\psi_{n}
in terms of amplitude and phase, i.e,.
\psi_{n}=\rho_{n}e^{i\phi_{n}}
.
This leads to the following expression for the free energy

\displaystyle F=

\displaystyle r\sum_{n}|\psi_{n}|^{2}+\lambda\,\left[\rho_{1}^{2}\cos\left(2\phi_{1}\right)+\rho_{2}^{2}\cos\left(2\phi_{2}-\frac{4\pi}{3}\right)+\rho_{3}^{2}\cos\left(2\phi_{3}+\frac{4\pi}{3}\right)\right]+u_{1}\sum_{n}\rho_{n}^{4}+u_{2}\sum_{n<n^{\prime}}\rho_{n}^{2}\rho_{n^{\prime}}^{2}

\displaystyle+u_{3}\sum_{n<n^{\prime}}\rho_{n}^{2}\rho_{n^{\prime}}^{2}\cos^{2}\left(\phi_{n}-\phi_{n^{\prime}}\right)

(S6.6)

We note that at the quadratic level, the terms controlling the phases of
\psi_{n}
simply prefer to lock to
\phi_{n}=2(n-1)\pi/3
if
\lambda<0
, and to angles shifted by
\pi/2
if
\lambda>0
.
This can be interpreted as the spin direction locking to the lattice.
At the quartic level, however, provided
u_{3}
is positive, one prefers a state where the in-plane spin-vectors on the 4 sites of the unit cell are orthogonal, i.e.,
|\phi_{n}-\phi_{n}^{\prime}|=\pi/2
.
For a continuous phase transition, we expect
\rho_{n}
to be small near the critical point, so the first scenario is more appropriate.
However if the phase transition is a fluctuation driven first order transition, then
\rho_{n}
can immediately pick up a (relatively) large non-zero value upon ordering, and the
u_{3}
term can dominate.
We will argue that a continuous transition prefers a
1M
or a
3M
state, while a
2M
state can be preferred in the first order scenario.

In the scenario where the phases of
\psi_{n}
are locked by the quadratic term as discussed above, the free energy takes a simpler form in terms of a vector
\bm{\rho}=(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho_{3})
:

F=(r-|\lambda|)\bm{\rho}^{2}+u_{1}(\bm{\rho}^{2})^{2}+\left(u_{2}+\frac{u_{3}}{4}-2u_{1}\right)\sum_{n<n^{\prime}}\rho_{n}^{2}\rho_{n^{\prime}}^{2}

(S6.7)

When
r-|\lambda|<0
,
\bm{\rho}
prefers to acquire a non-zero expectation value, and by finding the minima w.r.t. each component
\rho_{n}
(or rather
\rho_{n}^{2}
), we find the following three equations:

\displaystyle-|r-|\lambda||+2u_{1}\rho_{1}^{2}+\left(u_{2}+\frac{u_{3}}{4}\right)(\rho_{2}^{2}+\rho_{3}^{2})=0,\,\text{ and cyclic permutations of this equation.}

\displaystyle\implies-3|r-|\lambda||+\left[2u_{1}+2\left(u_{2}+\frac{u_{3}}{4}\right)\right]\bm{\rho}^{2}=0\,\implies\bm{\rho}^{2}=\frac{3|r-|\lambda||}{2\left[u_{1}+u_{2}+\frac{u_{3}}{4}\right]}

(S6.8)

where in the second step we have added the three equations.
This clearly indicates that the magnitude of
\bm{\rho}
is set to a value
\rho_{0}
by the Landau parameters, but the relative components are not determined.
To determine this, we have to look at the full free energy.
We consider the
1M
state with
\bm{\rho}=\rho_{0}(1,0,0)
, the
2M
state with
\bm{\rho}=\rho_{0}(1,1,0)/\sqrt{2}
, and the
3M
state with
\bm{\rho}=\rho_{0}(1,1,1)/\sqrt{3}
, and compare their free energies (of course, other states are possible but unlikely for reasons we will see below).
The only term that distinguishes between these three cases is the last term in Eq. (
S6.7
)

\displaystyle F_{1M}

\displaystyle=(r-|\lambda|)\rho_{0}^{2}+u_{1}\rho_{0}^{4}

\displaystyle F_{2M}

\displaystyle=(r-|\lambda|)\rho_{0}^{2}+u_{1}\rho_{0}^{4}+\left(u_{2}+\frac{u_{3}}{4}-2u_{1}\right)\frac{\rho_{0}^{4}}{2}

\displaystyle F_{3M}

\displaystyle=(r-|\lambda|)\rho_{0}^{2}+u_{1}\rho_{0}^{4}+\left(u_{2}+\frac{u_{3}}{4}-2u_{1}\right)\rho_{0}^{4}

(S6.9)

Hence, if
u_{2}+u_{3}/4>2u_{1}
, i.e., the mutual repulsion between the different fields is stronger, then we prefer the
1M
state.
By contrast, if
u_{2}+u_{3}/4<2u_{1}
, i.e., the self repulsion of a field is stronger, then we prefer the
3M
state.
Within this analysis, the
2M
state is never preferred.

We can consider the classical spin texture of these states.
The
1M
state is of course the two-sublattice stripe.
Both the
2M
and the
3M

While the
2M

One can try to do spin-wave theory with the two configurations to see if one is preferred by the zero point energy.

We now consider the Landau theory which includes the soft field
\psi_{0}
corresponding to uniform
S^{z}
ordering.
If the coupling
w
is negative (positive), there is attractive (repulsive) interaction between the fields.
Since the ED observation indicates some possible coexistence, it is perhaps reasonable to assume that
w<0
.
The minimization of the free energy gives the following equations:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial(|\psi_{n}|^{2})}

\displaystyle=r+\lambda\cos\left(2\phi_{n}-\frac{2(n-1)\pi}{3}\right)+2u_{1}|\psi_{n}|^{2}+\sum_{n^{\prime}\neq n}\left[u_{2}|\psi_{n^{\prime}}|^{2}+u_{3}|\psi_{n^{\prime}}|^{2}\cos^{2}\left(\phi_{n}-\phi_{n^{\prime}}\right)\right]+w\psi_{0}^{2}=0

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial(\psi_{0}^{2})}

\displaystyle=r_{0}+2u_{0}\psi_{0}^{2}+w\sum_{n}|\psi_{n}|^{2}=0

(S6.10)

If we substitute the value of
\psi_{0}^{2}=-r_{0}-w\sum_{n}|\psi_{n}|^{2}
from second equation into the first one, we find a set of equations for
\psi_{n}
that resembles the equations of motion for
\psi_{n}
, but with renormalized coefficients for
r
,
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
.

\left(r-\frac{wr_{0}}{2u_{0}}\right)+\lambda\cos\left(2\phi_{n}-\frac{2(n-1)\pi}{3}\right)+2\left(u_{1}-\frac{w^{2}}{4u_{0}}\right)|\psi_{n}|^{2}+\sum_{n^{\prime}\neq n}\left[\left(u_{2}-\frac{w^{2}}{4u_{0}}\right)|\psi_{n^{\prime}}|^{2}+u_{3}|\psi_{n^{\prime}}|^{2}\cos^{2}\left(\phi_{n}-\phi_{n^{\prime}}\right)\right]=0

(S6.11)

Therefore, at this level of analysis, the
1M
(
3M

u_{2}+u_{3}/4>2u_{1}
(
u_{2}+u_{3}/4<2u_{1})
, which are identical conditions to what we found previously.
We note that if
w
is sufficiently large, then the quartic part of the free energy becomes unbounded from below, and higher order terms will be required to stabilize the system.
Finally, we also note that a quartic term of the form
w_{1}\psi_{0}^{2}\,\text{Re}[\psi_{1}^{2}+\omega^{4}\psi_{2}^{2}+\omega^{2}\psi_{3}^{2}]
will renormalize
\lambda\to\lambda-w_{1}/2u_{0}

\psi_{n}

\lambda=w_{1}/2u_{0}
