 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
A magnesium oxysulfate (MOS) grout formulation has been proposed to solidify the liquid effluent from
the Savannah River Plutonium Processing Facility (SRPPF) Aqueous Recovery System (ARS). This
formulation uses a combination of light-burnt MgO, anhydrous MgSO4, and dead-burnt MgO resulting in
a grout with good mixability and acceptable density. The setting time for the grout is within the operational
limits of the SRPPF facility and the leachate pH is ~9.4, which is within the assumed pH range of the brine
from the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP). When first tested at the 1-gallon small-scale and 55-gallon
full-scale, there was a significant foam layer that raised concerns of a nonhomogeneous final form. A
nonhomogeneous mixture could have variable density throughout the waste form and therefore allow for
unequal shielding. To reduce the potential for unequal shielding in the final form, a series of foam reduction
tests were carried out and a final formulation of 28 wt% light burnt MgO, 10 wt% MgSO4, and 62 wt%
dead burnt MgO was used to remove the foam in under two hours while still maintaining a mix that has an
acceptable density, mix time, peak temperature, and set time.

A series of full-scale tests were performed to prove that no unreacted liquid was present after the mix fully
cured. These tests, through destructive analysis, confirmed that no unreacted liquid material was present.
These same initial tests, however, included areas of unreacted dry materials, indicating incomplete mixing
and a need to optimize the mixing parameters at the full-scale. Tests were performed to evaluate changes
in mixing blade design, raw material addition methodology, liquid effluent feed rate, and time of mixing.
Optimizing these variables resulted in a grout with no foam and no unreacted liquid or solid material while
maintaining an acceptably short set time. The peak temperature remained below 100°C for all mixes, below
the boiling point of the effluent solution. The formulation’s repeatability and consistency proved the grout
to be robust.

1.0 Introduction

A novel magnesium oxysulfate (MOS) grout formulation was proposed to solidify the expected Savannah
River Plutonium Processing Facility (SRPPF) Aqueous Recovery System (ARS) liquid effluent. The initial
formula of 25 wt% light burnt MgO (LBMgO), 9 wt% MgSO4, and 66 wt% dead burnt MgO (DBMgO)
was developed in previous testing [1-4]. The proposed solidification process would have the dry materials
preloaded into 55-gallon drums with the liquid effluent added to the solids and mixed by a lost-paddle
design. The liquid effluent for ARS, containing primarily nitrate salts, is expected to contain radioactive
material (Pu, Am) at levels requiring it to be managed as transuranic (TRU) waste for future disposal at the
Waste Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP). WIPP requires that no observable liquid be present in the set mixture
and that the dose be less than 200 mrem/hr at the surface [2, 4, 5]. These two requirements necessitate a
well-mixed waste form to avoid unsolidified liquid or hot spots in entrained radioactivity [2, 4, 6]. To ensure
proper shielding, the density should be equal to or greater than the minimum target of 1.8 g/cm3.WIPP has
also indicated the leachate pH needs to be in the range assumed in the WIPP performance assessment (pH
8-10) [2, 4].
Prior full scale testing has demonstrated that the bulk material of the grout met WIPP requirements [7]. A
layer of foam, however, was observed at the top of the grout form. This layer was present after the mix had
set and, although the foam hardened, this raised concerns about the homogeneity of the mixture. Previous
testing has shown that mixing for longer than 4 hours or implementing a more reactive formula can
eliminate the presence of foam on the surface of the sample [4]. Once foam reduction methods were
established at the small-scale, the methodology was translated to the full-scale testing as detailed herein.
Overall, a slightly more reactive formulation (28 wt% LBMgO, 10 wt% MgSO4, 62 wt% DBMgO) was
used, in concurrence with longer mixing time, a slightly modified mixing blade, and a disciplined addition
of the liquid effluent, to improve the homogeneity of the mix as well as ensure the removal of the foam.
The pH of this formula was 9.47 ± 0.02 at the small-scale [7]. Foam dissipation should take less than one
hour, and the mix should take no more than 2 hours to set.
Mixes were evaluated by destructive analysis and sampled at various locations. A well-mixed,
homogeneous test would have no unreacted solid or liquid material.

2.0 Experimental Procedure

2.1 Full-scale Setup and Mixing Ingredients
The system developed for the full-scale tests included a 480 VAC Bauer 15 HP motor with 16.96:1 gear
box and a variable frequency drive (VFD), running at a maximum speed of 105 RPM. The gearbox was
purchased from INDCO and had a hollow bore keyway drive. The motor and gearbox were mounted on a
1” thick steel plate attached to two H-beams installed across a trench in SRNL’s 786-A Engineering
Development Laboratory (EDL). The VFD driving the mixing motor was controlled through a LabVIEW
Data Acquisition System (DAS). The DAS recorded data for each full-scale test. The dataset included post-
mix grout temperature captured via at least one inserted thermocouple [6], rotational shaft speed recorded
with a laser tachometer, and motor characteristic data, such as power, frequency, and derived torque from
the VFD. Figure 2-1 shows the mixing rig setup, prior to a full-scale test.

 Figure 2-1: Full-scale, 55-gallon drum and mixing rig setup in EDL

To properly interface with the mixing rig, a 55-gallon carbon steel drum was modified by welding a 1/8”
thick carbon steel plate to the bottom. The plate had a hole in each corner that aligned with stud anchors in
the EDL trench. The drum and plate assembly were anchored in the trench to mitigate any hazards to
personnel related to the drum moving or twisting during mixing. A keyed 2.375” drive shaft with a flange
connection was inserted into the gearbox to drive the mixing paddle. The paddle was mated with the same
flange, and the two were fastened together with bolted connections.

Two slightly different mixing paddle designs were used: one with equidistant blades, and another with each
pair of blades differing in height and length. The latter paddle also had one of the inner blades flipped to
break up potential clumps during mixing. A visual comparison of the paddles can be seen in Figure 2-2.
The modified paddle design, drawing number R-R3-F-00063, was a result of developmental testing, and
can be found in Appendix B.

 Figure 2-2: Comparison of paddle designs. Left: Equidistant paddle; Right: Modified Design.

For the standard formulation, the light burnt MgO (LBMgO) used was from Premier Magnesia, the dead
burnt MgO (DBMgO) used was from TIMAB Magnesia, and the anhydrous MgSO4 used was from either
Wintersun Chemical or Sigma Aldrich. MgSO4 brands were used interchangeably. The sodium nitrate was
from DudaDiesel Biodiesel Supply, and the nitric acid, lanthanum (III) nitrate hexahydrate, and erbium
nitrate pentahydrate were from Fisher Scientific.

The primary salt in the ARS effluent will be sodium nitrate [1]. This salt represents the largest possible
hindrance to solidification when compared to other projected components in the ARS. Prior testing
of sorbents for solidification was performed with 4M NaNO3 in deionized (DI) water as the surrogate
effluent [2, 4], but subsequent work added free HNO3 to decrease the pH of the effluent to a calculated
pH of 2.3 [4, 8]. The surrogate liquid effluent pH was chosen since it was deemed optimal when
considering processing constraints and the chemistry of the MOS grout. [4]. Based on availability,
erbium nitrate pentahydrate or lanthanum (III) nitrate hexahydrate was added to the simulant solution
as a surrogate for the TRU components, Am and Pu, and dissolved into the 4M NaNO3 solution adjusted
to pH 2.3. Either Er or La was used to evaluate surrogate distribution throughout the solidified material,
if needed. Prior work established the method to accurately analyze the material for the surrogates’
concentrations via Inductively Coupled Plasma Atomic Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-AES) [3, 4].
Consistent Er or La concentrations throughout the full-scale tests can help to confirm homogeneity and
adequate mixing [4].

2.2 Foam Reduction Testing
Based on previous testing at the small-scale, it was observed that foam production is due to the air that is
trapped within the dry solids before mixing, as well as the air that gets mixed in during the mixing process
but before the grout has set [7]. The foam is an active foam and does not dissipate. However, once the mix
begins to set, the foam is no longer active, and the foam begins to dissipate. Figure 2-4 gives an example

 4

of how foam dissipates from the surface throughout mixing. The increased viscosity of the grout also
ensures no additional air can be trapped. A more reactive formulation can help a mix set faster, increasing
foam dissipation rates. By contrast, more foam can be caused by the shape of the blade as it may introduce
more air, which in turn produces more foam [7]. An example of foam generation is shown in Figure 2-3.
Paddle design, mixing time, liquid addition, and reactivity of the mix all play a role in foam production and
dissipation. These factors were tested to reduce the amount of time it took to dissipate the foam.
Repeatability tests were performed to confirm reproducibility in creating a homogenous mix with no foam
and no unreacted solid material.

2.3 Destructive Analysis Methods
Destructive analysis was used to determine homogeneity of the mix as measured by the presence of either
unreacted solid or liquid material. This also provides samples for analytical methods such as density.
Density was analyzed using the same methodology as for the small-scale samples [4].

Full-Scale Test #1 used a coring drill at different sections of the mix to gather samples to determine the
mix’s homogeneity. The full-scale test was sampled four months after mixing. The grouted drum was
lowered into a trench and anchored. A coring drill was positioned at ground level above the drum and at an
angle that ensured that the core bit would not encounter one of the mixing paddle blades. A specialized
coring bit was attached to the head of the corer and manually lowered into the drum to collect each sample.
Two locations were selected for coring, and one location was selected for manual excavation. Figure 2-5
shows the configuration used to take core samples using this method.

 5

 Figure 2-5 Coring rig suspended above 55-gallon sample drum.
Coring was done in stages because the hollow drill bit would fill with material and needed to be emptied
periodically. The first core reached a depth of approximately 5.5 inches and the exterior of the core was
very dark in color and slightly soft like a very dense clay. At first, this seemed to indicate that the mix may
not have been fully cured. The center of each core, however, was not soft or clay-like at all but rather hard
and difficult to break apart without using large amounts of force. Based on this observation, it is believed
that the drilling action created a clay-like substance on the surface of each core sample, where it met the
surface of the drill bit (Figure 2-6). It can be rationalized that the heat generated from friction of the high-
speed drill bit as it drilled into the sample was enough to alter the consistency of this material.

 Figure 2-6 Smooth exterior walls of two coring holes made in Full-Scale Test #1.

 6

Starting at the depth just above where the mixing paddle was located, the mix became increasingly difficult
to core. Two different bits were used to core the deeper layers. The steel bit used for most of the coring was
serrated with many small, sharp teeth. At around 16 inches in depth below the surface of the mix, this coring
bit could no longer penetrate the lower layers of the mix. The bit with serrated teeth was swapped with
another steel corer consisting of square shaped teeth to see if a different coring bit could penetrate deeper.
Similarly to the serrated teeth bit, the square teeth corer failed to make much progress on the harder layers.
To mitigate the alteration of the mix by compressive forces or heat by friction, the electric coring drill was
substituted for a hammer and chisel. Roughly 16 inches deep was manually excavated on the side of the
drum opposite to the coring locations.

Due to coring and manual excavation challenges, it was determined that future full-scale destructive
analyses would include removing the exterior metal drum and utilizing a method of feathering and wedging
to shear off a clean profile of the mix. A smaller masonry drill bit and handheld electric drill were used to
create guidance holes for wedges or chisels to be placed into the grout. This method was used starting with
Full-Scale Test #4. Shearing the full-scale test removed risk to the integrity of the sample and gave a clearer
picture of the entirety of the mix by providing a larger cross-section of the grout to determine visual
homogeneity of the mix. While cure time was not investigated as part of this report, destructive analysis
was performed no less than 6 days after mixing to ensure the grout had fully set. Figure 2-7 shows the
implementation of the destructive analysis method.

3.1 Foam Reduction and Mix Homogeneity
Full-Scale Tests #1 and #2 were performed in FY24 and served as the impetus for foam reduction studies
as both mixes had substantial clumping found throughout and significant foam on the surface [6]. Testing
was first performed on representative small-scale tests to help identify methods to better mix the sample
and reduce the foam on the surface within two hours. Using a slightly more reactive mix or mixing the
sample until all foam dissipated were the two most effective techniques at foam removal [7]. The following
results are from full-scale testing in EDL that were guided by the small-scale results.

3.2 Full-Scale Test #3: Testing New Recipe
The first indication of a reduction in foam at a larger scale was achieved during Full-Scale Test #3, which
was allowed to mix for 2.5 hours, using the equidistant paddle design. No foam was present post mixing.
During destructive analysis, there was an apparent difference in grout uniformity indicating inadequate
mixing and that clumping around the paddle had occurred (Figure 3-1). The density of the lighter colored
grout, which made up the bulk of the material, was found to be 1.685 ± 0.009 g/cm3 and the darker grout
was found to be 2.101 ± 0.042 g/cm3. To ensure proper shielding, the density should be equal to or greater
than the minimum target of 1.8 g/cm3. Although this method had proven useful in getting rid of the foam
layer at the surface, additional work was needed to ensure that the materials are thoroughly mixed and that
no clumping occurs on or around the mixing blade.

3.3 Full-Scale Test #4: Liquid Loading During Mixing
During Full-Scale Test #4, the liquid addition method was changed. Instead of loading liquid prior to startup
of the mixer, liquid was introduced to the dry material while the mixer was rotating at 75 RPM and
eventually slowed down to 50 RPM. This resulted in foam dissipation after 40 minutes, faster than
previously observed in Test #3. While these process changes seemed promising, there were still clear
indicators of clumping. While the mixing paddle was stirring, a visible ‘wobbling’ was seen on the surface
of the grout. This was also observed during Test #3. After this test, the paddle design was updated to
mitigate clumping, see Figure 2-2.

3.4 Full-Scale Test #5: Modified Paddle Design
Full-Scale Test #5 was the first mix that used the modified paddle design and the more reactive grout
formula: 28 wt% LBMgO, 10 wt% MgSO4, 62 wt% DBMgO. The dry materials were loaded into the drum
as individual components in three stages, mixed at 50 RPM after each stage addition, and mixed again for
25 minutes at 50 RPM after all dry material had been added. The dry materials were mixed for a final 5
minutes at 50 RPM prior to the liquid effluent addition. The liquid effluent was added while the mixer was
running at 90 RPM. The liquid addition took a total of 6 minutes and 30 seconds, after which the mixer was
slowed to 70 RPM for 25 minutes. Then, the mixing was increased to 80 RPM for 6 minutes and the mixing
speed was further increased to 100 RPM for 4 minutes when the foam had fully dissipated. Destructive
analysis showed no clumping or unreacted dry material. The new paddle design resulted in a thoroughly
mixed grout and decreased foam dissipation time. Surface level samples were found to have a density of
1.805 ± 0.003 g/cm . Additionally, density measurements were taken for samples retrieved from the bottom
of the drum, 2.213 ± 0.014 g/cm3, and from the edge of the drum (within the bottom 10 inches), 2.011 ±
0.037 g/cm3. All density readings were at or above the target value of 1.8 g/cm3.

3.5 Full-Scale Test #6: Liquid Loading Prior to Mixing
Full-Scale Test #6 used the modified paddle design and performed liquid loading prior to mixing. During
this mix, a calibrated drum scale was utilized to observe the mass change of the liquid being pumped out

 9

of the solution drum. After 80 kg of liquid solution was added (approximately two thirds of the total
solution), the mixer was started, and the remainder of the liquid solution was added. The flow rate of liquid
addition was determined to be 8 kg/min. The mixer ran at 90 RPM for 24 minutes and then at 100 RPM
for 16 minutes. No foam was present, and a metal rod was inserted into the mix prior to setting as a
rudimentary test to check for the presence of clumping. If clumps were present in the mix, the rod would
encounter significant resistance. No indications of clumps were found during this mixing or with the metal
rod. After curing, destructive analysis further confirmed there was no observable clumping in this test.
Density measurements taken were inside the 95% confidence interval range for target density throughout
the entire form. Surface level samples were found to have a density value averaging 1.838 ± 0.005 g/cm .
Additionally, density readings were taken from samples at the bottom of the drum and edge of the drum
were found to be on average 2.137 ± 0.046 g/cm3 and 2.040 ± 0.008 g/cm3 respectively. All density readings
were above the target value of 1.8 g/cm3. Full-Scale Test #6 was a homogenous mix and reduced mixing
time for complete foam dissipation to 52 minutes. Full-Scale Test #6 was the first test for which all foam
was removed, and no clumping was observed after destructive analysis.

3.6 Full-Scale Test #7: Increased Liquid Loading Rate
Full-Scale Test #7 tested faster liquid addition to the drum. Target liquid addition was twice the rate (16
kg/min) of Full-Scale Test #6, while maintaining all other variables constant. The drum scale was used to
target two thirds liquid addition prior to mixer start up, but the drum was visibly full at ~50% liquid addition.
The mixer was started at 90 RPM and the remaining liquid effluent continued to be added into the mix.
There was noticeable increased turbulence during mixing. After 30 minutes, a rod was inserted to check for
clumping and a large mass was found on the paddle in the center of the mix. The mixer was restarted and
set to 100 RPM for 10 minutes. This assisted in breaking up some of the clumping and the surface of the
mix became more placid, but the follow up destructive analysis revealed a large amount of dry unreacted
material. No free liquid was observed. This liquid addition rate was too high for the effluent to soak into
the dry material and explains the initial ability to only add 50% of the liquid solution before starting the
mixer. This rapid liquid addition allowed less liquid to penetrate the dry material creating clumping, seen
in Figure 3-2,which in turn created more turbulence. Surface level samples were had an average density of
1.872 ± 0.007 g/cm . Density measurements taken from samples at the bottom of the drum and edge of the
drum were found to be on average 1.866 ± 0.008 g/cm3 and 2.026 ± 0.042 g/cm3 respectively. All density
readings were above the target value of 1.8 g/cm3

3.7 Full-Scale Test #8 & 9: Repeatability of Homogeneous Mix
Full-Scale Test #8 controlled mix reactivity by resuming previous rates of liquid addition. The targeted
flow rate for liquid addition was 8 kg/min and two thirds of the total liquid was successfully added prior to
mixing. Guidance in the form of R&D directions for Full-Scale Test #8 and #9 was generated to improve
repeatability of the tests by providing more detailed controls over the mixing parameters. Mixing with a
constant speed of 90 RPM was utilized throughout the test for 47 minutes post liquid addition when all
foam had dissipated. There was noticeably less turbulence during mixing than Test #7. A rod was inserted
downwards into the mix to confirm the lack of any noticeable clumping. The lack of resistance on the metal
rod was a good indicator that this mix contained no large clumps of unreacted dry solids. Figure 3-3 shows
Full-Scale Test #8, after the drum was removed and depicts a homogeneous waste form throughout. Surface
level samples were found to have a density value averaging 1.885 ± 0.007 g/cm . Density measurements
taken from samples at the bottom of the drum and edge of the drum were found to be on average 2.037 ±
0.036 g/cm3 and 1.946 ± 0.018 g/cm3 respectively.

Full-Scale Test #9 followed the same methodology as Test #8 and helped to confirm repeatability for the
mixing process. Surface level samples were found to have a density value averaging 1.909 ± 0.008 g/cm .
Density measurements from samples at the bottom of the drum and edge of the drum were found to be on
average 2.081 ± 0.035 g/cm3 and 1.895 ± 0.002 g/cm3 respectively. All density measurements for Tests #8
and #9 were above the target value of 1.8 g/cm3. Both Full-Scale Tests #8 and #9 were sampled for pH. The
average pH for Full-Scale Test #8 measured 9.25 ± 0.02 and the average pH for Full Scale Test #9 measured
9.20 ± 0.03.

3.8 Temperature Discussion
Temperature was measured as part of the full-scale mixes from the time grout mixing ceased until the
temperature returned to ambient conditions. Two points were specifically tracked for each test: set
temperature and maximum temperature. The temperatures at which the various mixes set varied
considerably with changes in process conditions spanning a total range of 48°C (28°C to 76°C). However,
under the mostly consistent conditions of Tests #6, #8, and #9, the set temperature was consistent with a
total range inside the calibration accuracy of the thermocouple of 2.2°C (41°C to 43°C). The low set
temperature minimizes the chances of the liquid effluent volatilizing during mixing. Maximum
temperatures varied due to several factors such as mix reactivity, amount of clumping, and mixer speed.
No mix exceeded 100°C and under the conditions set by Tests #6, #8 and #9 had a range of 7°C, compared
to all full-scale mixes with a range of 33°C. All maximum temperatures were realized at around 5 hours
after mixing stopped across all tests. Consistency of test conditions to reduce clumping and control
reactivity with the liquid effluent are critical to control expected mix temperatures across the production of
multiple drums.

4.0 Conclusions
MOS grout is a promising candidate to solidify the ARS liquid for disposal at WIPP. The previous
formulation was effective at solidifying the liquid effluent at an acceptable temperature while maintaining
an acceptable pH and density. The foam removal testing confirmed that a slightly more reactive formulation
(28 wt% LBMgO, 10 wt% MgSO4, 62 wt% DBMgO) is an acceptable alternative to remove the foam in a
timely manner, while still fulfilling the criteria for temperature, pH, and density. While investigating
processes to reduce foam, homogeneity of the mix was increasingly improved between Full-Scale Test #1
through #9 by controlling the rate of liquid addition, impeller speed, and mixing time. Two full-scale
repeatability experiments were performed to validate the successful mix. The operational parameters
required to produce a homogenous material were identified as follows:

 Mix the dry solids loaded into a 55-gallon drum for 30 minutes at 90 RPM immediately prior to
 mixing,
 Load liquid effluent continuously at a target rate of 8 kg/min until two thirds of the liquid has been
 added while the mixer is off
 Immediately start mixing at 90 RPM while adding the remainder of the liquid. Mix for a minimum
 of 45 minutes.

Following this procedure resulted in a consistent, homogenous mixture with no foam, no unreacted solid,
and no liquid material present.

5.0 Path Forward
Continued work to evaluate process parameters at the full-scale is recommended. The liquid effluent
supplied from ARS may be at temperatures above ambient and should be investigated to see the effects of
temperature on mix reactivity and set time. Further development of the SRNL set up to mimic the SRPPF

process set up would also add additional capabilities to mimic real world process conditions and allow for
tuning of the system to optimize processing times and workflows. Additional work to understand storage
constraints of premixed drums, as well as mixing of the dry materials prior to use in the ARS, is also
recommended.
