Haptic illusion during finger-held disk rotation: revisiting a deceptively simple illusion
Authors: Akira Imai, Patrice Revol, Yves Rossetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe “disk-rotating” haptic illusion reported by Cormack (1973), in which a disk or coin held in one hand and turned end over end between thumb and forefinger of the other hand is perceived as longer by the turning hand. This illusion may occur because of the interplay of the rotating and holding hands. To separate the contributions of each hand, two custom-made devices were used to allow independent rotation and holding of the disk. Twenty participants handled disks (30 and 39 mm in diameter), and rotated them for one minute under the following conditions: (a) ordinary use of both hands, (b) using both hands but turning and holding the disk using the two custom-made devices simultaneously, and (c) using only one hand for turning or holding. The magnitude of illusion increased to a level comparable to that reported by Cormack (1973) under conditions (a) and (b). However, under condition (c), the illusion for the rotating hand grew about 25% of those obtained under (a) and (b), with almost no illusion for the holding hand. Nevertheless, reconstruction of the estimated illusion using each magnitude of illusion obtained for the “one-hand” conditions of (c), this estimation showed a moderate illusion growing gradually. Rotation by the turning hand may mainly contribute to the illusion, while holding hand is involved with this illusion interactively. The results are discussed along with adaptation, aftereffect, and contrast effect, which may be involved in processing of bimanual signals underlying this illusion.Keywords: Haptic illusion, Disk rotation illusion, Bimanual interplay, Adaptation, Aftereffect, Contrast effect

Introduction

The haptic system is probably the most complex sensory modality as it requires integrating tactile, proprioceptive, and motor information and their respective dynamics. Therefore, it has been investigated less extensively than other sensory systems at the neurophysiological, behavioral, and psychological levels. Nevertheless, the haptic and visual systems are known to interact closely, and cross-modal interactions between them have been widely studied (Chen et al. 2017; Gori et al. 2013; Holmes et al. 2024; Kappers and Bergmann Tiest 2013; Lederman and Jones 2011; Mayes et al. 2024; Miller et al. 2018; Sann and Streri 2007; Tal and Amedi 2009; Tortelli et al. 2023; Wahn and König 2015). Particularly, the fact that visual illusions are similarly or sometimes paradoxically observed in the haptic domain suggests that there are common and distinct mechanisms underlying the formation of illusions in visual and haptic perception (Dijkerman 2017; Grünwald 2008; Hatwell et al. 2003; Kappers and Bergmann Tiest 2013, 2015; Lederman and Jones 2011). Indeed, research shows that a tactile stimulus consisting of three-dimensional figures that are isomorphic to visual geometric illusions induce a positive (and sometimes negative) haptic illusion in numerous studies for the Müller-Lyer illusion (Casla et al. 1999; Frisby and Davies 1971; Gentaz and Hatwell 2004; Gentaz et al. 2004; Heller et al. 2005; Mancini et al. 2010; Millar and Al-Attar 2002; Rudel and Teuber 1963; Suzuki and Arashida 1992; Watson and French 1966; Wong 1975), Titchener’s ⊥ or horizontal-vertical illusion (Casla et al. 1999; Gallace et al. 2007; Gentaz and Hatwell 2004; Landwehr 2014, 2015; Suzuki and Arashida 1992; Wahn and König 2015; Wong 1977), and others (Ballesteros et al. 2012; Casla et al. 1999; Day 1990; Gentaz and Hatwell 2004; Jones et al. 1974; Suzuki and Arashida 1992). These findings suggest that the illusions arise from unique factors within each modality, or sometimes through interplay between each modality. Perhaps the interactive processing might occur at the higher cognitive levels of the brain (Kappers and Bergmann Tiest 2013, 2015). Similar complex interactions have been described between proprioceptive and visual sources of information (e.g. Rossetti et al. 1995).

A particularly intriguing haptic illusion, occurring during the rotation of a simple coin with one’s fingers, was described in an overlooked report by Cormack (1973). Although interesting and relevant to the interaction between vision and haptic perceptions, it has received very little attention. According to Cormack (1973), holding the diameter axis of a disk with one hand and continuously rotating the disk perpendicular to the vertical axis of the disk with the other hand produces a haptic illusion in which the diameter of the disk is perceived as elongated along the axis of rotation. However, this curious “disk rotation illusion,” in which a simple rotational motion by the fingers to a familiar stimulus produces an illusory percept, has attracted little scientific attention. Only a few studies have referred to this illusion (Hayward 2008; Jones et al. 1974; Lederman and Jones 2011; Watanabe 1980, 1995, 1998), including a preliminary series of studies conducted by the present authors (Imai et al. 2017, 2018, 2019).

Cormack (1973) stated that the disk rotation illusion does not occur when the observer is looking at the hand in which the disk is being spun. Preliminary observations conducted before the present study similarly revealed no evidence of the disk rotation illusion when participants visually observed their own hand when the disk was rotating. This suggests that the disk rotation illusion disappears when vision overrides haptics. Actually, reports demonstrating visual dominance over tactile or touch sensation have been around for quite some time (Gibson 1933; Rock and Harris 1967; Rock and Victor 1964), and it would be consistent with recent findings that vision is dominant over audition in stimulus localization tasks (Bertelson and Aschersleben 1998), dominant over touch in shape recognition (Spence 2008) and rapid discrimination tasks (Hartcher-O’Brien et al. 2008), and dominant over proprioception in determining the spatial position of limbs (Botvinick and Cohen 1998; Ehrsson et al. 2004; Gallace and Spence 2005; Pavani et al. 2000) and in judging the apparent speed of movement of a visual target with or without proprioceptive cues (Revol et al. 2009). These findings support a notion that vision could prevent occurrence of the disk rotation illusion, by some underlying mechanisms such as proprioceptive adaptation observed in illusionary perception by upper arm stimulation, which was strongly modulated by vision (Seizova-Cajic and Azzi 2011).

In addition to what has been mentioned above, the necessity of using both hands for the disk rotation illusion implies that the signals from both body sides when experiencing the illusion are processed in an integrated manner (Tamè et al. 2015, 2016) with higher-order information processing (Eickhoff et al. 2010; Miyazaki et al. 2015; Song and Rees 2018). Such processes may play a key role in creating the illusionary disk image. For example, Miyazaki et al. (2015) examined inter-hemispheric desynchronization of visually induced motion sickness (VIMS) by using illusion of self-motion (vection) and/or by inappropriate optokinetic nystagmus (OKN). On recording fMRI signals, they showed that the inter-hemispheric correlation in the middle temporal area, but not in V1, V2, V3, V3A, or V7 was reduced during the viewing of the VIMS-inducing movie compared to the control, suggesting that a kind of visual illusion such as the vection could produce VIMS by evoking inter-hemispherically desynchronized activations in higher brain areas. It was also shown that the Müller-Lyer illusion occurs both in vision and in touch, and transfers cross-modally from vision to haptics (Mancini et al. 2010). Thus, if the disk rotation illusion does not require the integration of bimanual signals from both hands, then this illusion should be reproducible by executing it one hand at a time. Conversely, if the integration of information from both hands is necessary, then trying it one hand at a time will not reproduce the ordinary magnitude of illusion. Therefore, we assume that the disk rotation illusion could be a good tool to investigate the integration of information between vision and haptic perception and bimanual interplay. We tested this issue by using two custom-designed devices, which allow participants to hold and rotate a disk individually by one hand.

Cormack (1973) introduced the illusion, demonstrated its generality, and proposed possible explanatory mechanisms as follows: The magnitude of the illusion rapidly increased during the first 30 s of rotation and did not reach its peak point within 60 s; the magnitude of illusion was enhanced with increasing disk size and rotation speed, and changing the pressure of the supporting hand did not affect the illusion. In addition, a spherical edge reduced the magnitude of the illusion. He proposed two hypotheses to comprehend his results by the adaptive effects of holding and rotating hands, based on the similarity of the developmental aspect of this illusion to the decay process of the geometric figural (Hammer 1949) and motor-sensory aftereffects (Singer and Day 1965): (a) The thumb and index finger holding the disk, which serves as the axis of rotation, are always pressed against the edge of the disk, resulting in adaptation to the motion, so that the curved edge of the disk gradually feel flatter. However, the rotating finger spends less time touching the edge of the disk and does not adapt as much, and consequently, the length and width of the disk are felt as elongated in the direction of turning axis. (b) The rotating fingers move with an open-and-shut motion, in which the fingers spread slightly wider than the length of the diameter axis of the disk, and then the spread of the fingers shrinks to the width of thickness of the disk as the disk rotates. As a result of these repetitive movements, the rotating fingers adapt to about half the length of the disk diameter on average, while the holding fingers are always adapted only to the length of the disk diameter. Consequently, differences in the effect of adaptation for the fingers of each hand, and therefore, the perceived diameter of the disk changes. The first hypothesis, (a), could be called the holding-hand-primary hypothesis, and the second, (b), interplay of the holding-rotating hand hypothesis.

Despite these important findings, Cormack (1973) was unable to distinguish between the two hypotheses. In fact, he did not conduct any experiments to separately examine the individual effects of the two hands used to create the haptic illusion. Therefore, it is still unknown which hand mainly contributes to the illusion. This issue of using one hand or two hands for the disk rotation could be resolved with the use of a device that allows an individual to rotate the disk with one hand, and another device that enables the disk to rotate autonomously and to be held by one hand. We can then examine the magnitude of the disk rotation illusion to evaluate the effects of each hand individually. This is the key point of the present study: If the magnitude of the illusion in each one-hand trial could be measured separately, it would be compared to that in the two-hand trial. Furthermore, using these magnitudes of illusion from each one-hand trial, it becomes possible to calculate and estimate a magnitude of illusion assumed to be equivalent to that of the two-hand trial (we could name this ‘simulated magnitude of illusion: SMI’). The significance of this study lies in the fact that by examining whether the SMI is equivalent to or differs from that of the two-hand trial, we can obtain insights into the mechanisms underlying this illusion. In addition, if the SMI would not produce ordinary magnitude of illusion as that obtained by using two hands normally, some bimanual processes may be necessarily involved in this illusion.

The present study reports definitive findings on the disk rotation illusion using two novel devices: the first one holds a disk and is rotated by one hand, and second one rotates a disk autonomously using a mechanical motor. We have already examined the disk rotation illusion with the classic set-up and with these two devices in a series of preliminary studies (Imai et al. 2017, 2018, 2019), and obtained the following important findings: On the replication of Cormack’s (1973) classic method, the illusionary disk elongation was obtained at the same level as that of Cormack, which suggested the illusion is a robust phenomenon (Imai et al. 2017, n = 8); the magnitude of the illusion obtained by the rotating hand alone was not as large as that by two hands and the illusion did not appear clearly (Imai et al. 2018, n = 6); and the magnitude of the illusion obtained by the holding hand alone was much less than that by two hands, suggesting little contribution of the holding hand to the illusion (Imai et al. 2019, n = 4). While these findings are all suggestive, our findings seem to be vulnerable drawing firm conclusions due to the small number of participants in the preliminary study and to the fact that some participants already had prior knowledge of this illusion. Since perception and illusionary phenomena are influenced by the prior experience, not only for visual (Annan and Gilchrist 2004; Poom 2020; Vidal and Gilchrist 2004) but also for tactile (Chan 1996; Hirsiger et al. 2012; Martel et al. 2022) domain, we should replicate our preliminary studies with all naïve and substantial numbers of participants.

Building on these results, the present study used two custom-designed devices: a rotating hand device that allows one hand to rotate the disk, and a holding hand device that automatically rotates the disk when it is held. Using these two devices, we examined the effects of using one hand and two hands on the magnitude of disk rotation illusion to reach a definitive conclusion, with the aims of gaining a better comprehension of haptic perception and the availability of this illusion as a tool for examining the interaction of vision and haptic perception, and hemispheric interplay. The study’s significance lies in elucidating the interplay between the visual and haptic systems through the study of illusory phenomena.

Methods

Participants

Twenty Japanese university students (10 men; mean age = 21.3 years, SD = 1.5 years) with normal or corrected-to-normal vision, participated in the experiment. All participants were right-handed according to their self-assessments. None of the participants had prior knowledge of the illusion, and this was the first time they had participated in this type of experiment. The research protocol was approved by the institutional ethics board of the first author’s university (No. 22035) and was conducted in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Stimuli and apparatuses

Gray plastic disks measuring 30 mm and 39 mm in diameter, and 3 mm in thickness were used as stimuli. For the rotating hand condition, an apparatus enabling disk rotation with a single hand was constructed, referred to as the “self-rotation device” (Imai et al. 2018). The device was shaped as a piece of plastic 98 mm wide, 75 mm high, and 30 mm deep, with a concave shape cut out, leaving a 12 mm thick frame; the inner width after cutting was 74 mm (Fig. 1). It was designed to support both sides of the disk using two pointy pins that entered slightly into both edges of the disk aligned with its diameter axis. For the holding hand condition, a second apparatus was constructed to rotate the disk mechanically. This apparatus, called the “auto-rotation device” (Imai et al. 2019), was designed to rotate two tops of a quarter-cut disk using a stepping motor (Plexmotion, SSA-PR-4202) attached to a 150 mm copper arm; at its end, there were two thin pins at both the left and right sides, to be inserted into a quarter-cut disk on each side. The direction of rotation was converted by 90° using a conversion gear so that the disk rotated along a horizontal axis. Two quarter-cut disks were rotated by the stepping motor controlled by a function generator (GW Instek, AFG-2005) as in Fig. 1 and 2. As all the participants voluntarily rotated the disk with the diameter axis of the disk in the horizontal direction at a demonstration and a practice trial, the axis of rotation of these devices was set in the same way, as in Fig. 2 and 3.

A two-Euro coin (25.75 mm in diameter and 2.2 mm thick) was used as a stimulus for a practice trial. A one-Euro coin (23.25 mm in diameter and 2.33 mm thick) was also used as a stimulus for the first demonstration of this illusion. None of the participants had ever used these two coins before.

Measurement of the illusion

Two types of stimulus-matching sheets were prepared to evaluate the magnitude of the illusions. The rating sheet-A was almost the same as the evaluation form used in Cormack (1973) and Imai et al. (2017) (Fig. 3). It was a grading form on A4 size paper sheet with a 30 mm perfect circle and 10 oblong figures of which the diameter increased oblongly in a 3-mm-step, drawn in sequence and assigned the number “0” for the perfect circle and “11” for the most elongated one. These numbers were multiplied by 10 to indicate the percent magnitude of the illusion for the data analysis. The rating sheet-B was a grading form on A4 size paper with perfect circles of various sizes, numbered “1” through “15,” and was the same as the evaluation sheet used in Imai et al. (2018, 2019) to measure the magnitude of the illusion by one hand. The diameter of the first perfect circle on this sheet-B was 18 mm, and the diameter grew larger by a 2 mm-step until No. 7 (diameter 30 mm), larger by a 3-mm-step from No. 8 (diameter 33 mm) until No. 12 (diameter 45 mm), and larger by a 5 mm-step from No. 13 (diameter 50 mm) until No. 15 (diameter 60 mm). Before using sheet-B, participants were allowed to handle the stimulus disks and required to indicate the number on sheet-B that felt similar to the subjective size of the disk handled. The number they indicated was regarded as their “reference figure” for each disk and was used as the denominator to calculate the ratio of the magnitude of the illusion on their stimulus series.

Procedure

Besides the demonstration and practice trials, there were two “ordinary,” one “two-hand,” and two “one-hand” condition trials. Participants received explanations about the haptic illusion perceived by rotating the disk using both hands, and they experienced the illusion as the demonstration trial using the one-euro coin for a rotation time of 30 s. After this, they performed one practice trial using the two-euro coin and one each ordinary condition trial using 30 mm and 39 mm disks for a rotation time of 60 s. The order of using the 30 and 39 mm disk was counterbalanced across the participants. During the practice and ordinary condition trials, participants were instructed to hold the disk with their non-preferred hand and rotate it with their preferred hand upon the experimenter’s cue. Participants placed their hands under the desk so that they could not see their hands while rotating. While rotating the disk at their own pace, participants verbally responded to a digital metronome (SEIKO DM70) ticking every five seconds by selecting which of the figures on sheet-A was closest to the ratio of size of the diameter of the disk that they could feel with their right and left hand, and by answering with the number of the figure. Thus, the participant’s responses were collected 12 times every five seconds during the 60-s rotation time period, and the experimenter simultaneously recorded the responses using a video camera (Panasonic HX-A500). The number of rotations for each trial was measured offline using the video records. A two-minute rest period was provided between trials to allow the fingers time to recover from adaptation, and the same was adopted below as well.

Following the ordinary condition trials, the participants received instructions regarding the subsequent “two-hand” and “one-hand” conditions under which the holding hand and the rotating hand were each used individually. Under the two-hand condition, the participants simultaneously used the rotating hand with the self-rotation device and the holding hand with the auto-rotation device so that the condition simulated the ordinary condition. The participant was required to rotate the disk in their right hand in sync with the rotation speed of the disk in the holding hand, while holding the disk in the left device. Under the one-hand condition, the participant only used either the left or right device, and there were two trials; (1) participants only used the rotating hand with the self-rotation device; (2) participants only used the holding hand with the auto-rotation device. Under these one-hand conditions, participants evaluated the magnitude of illusion felt only for the one hand used. Sheet-A was used under the two-hand condition for the measurement of the magnitude of the illusion, and sheet-B under the one-hand condition. The two-hand trial was always administered first, and the order of other one-hand trials was counterbalanced across participants. Only the 39 mm disk was used for the one- and two-hand conditions because of the size restriction of the devices. The rotation speed (c/min) of the auto-rotation device was uniquely set for each participant to the speed obtained when they tried the 39 mm disk at their own pace under the ordinary condition. Participants were also required to rotate the disk of the self-rotation device so that its rotation speed matched as much as possible to that rotated under the ordinary condition. These two devices were placed under the desk in front of the participants so that they could not see them during each trial.

Statistical power and effect size

Each participant provided 60 responses, with 12 responses across the five conditions. A priori calculation using G*Power 3 (Faul et al. 2007) for the main effect of the condition indicated that an actual power of 0.807 with an effect size of 0.25 was shown by a total sample size of 110. Since the experiment employed a within-subjects design, the sample size of 110 was divided by the five levels of the disk rotation condition and the required number of participants was recalculated, which turned out to be 22. When the effect size was set to 0.3, it showed a total sample size of 80, following the same way as a necessity of 16 participants. Considering the robustness of the illusion in our preliminary study (Imai et al. 2017, Cohen’s f = 0.85), an effect size of about 0.25–0.4 or even higher could be expected, and we assumed that about 20 participants would be sufficient.

The significant interaction revealed many substantial differences among conditions over time in subsequent multiple comparison analysis. Among them, because our primary purpose was to compare the magnitudes of the illusion produced by the one-hand condition and those by the other conditions, special attention was focused on the differences between the one-hand condition and the others. The multiple comparison method by Bonferroni indicated the following results: The significant differences in the growth of the illusion appeared just after the first five seconds between the two one-hand conditions and the others (p < 0.05), but the difference between one hand-conditions was not significant. These differences between the one-hand conditions and others continued until the end of the trial (p < 0.05). After 15 s, the differences among the one hand-conditions were significant until the end of the trial (p < 0.05), suggesting that rotating the disk emerges a piece of evidence for the illusion but holding the disk does not (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Bimanual contributions for the disk rotation illusion

In this study, we aimed to understand how simple two-hand interactions can produce a haptic illusion in which motor, proprioceptive, and tactile information dynamically interact. Deceptively simple disk rotation illusion, first reported by Cormack (1973), was revisited. Cormack conducted a series of experiments and concluded that the adaptation mechanisms of the holding and rotating hands were responsible for this illusion. However, he did not examine the precise mechanism, that is, the respective contributions of the holding versus the rotating hands and their dynamic interactions. Two custom devices were designed so that participants could hold a rotating disk with one hand and rotate another disk with the other hand. Using these devices, it was possible to reveal the mechanisms underlying this illusion. Our results shed light on the complex interactions during bimanual object manipulation.

Given that Cormack’s (1973) study has attracted little interest, it was first necessary to confirm the robustness of the phenomenon in a series of preliminary studies (Imai et al. 2017, 2018, 2019). In the present study, it was confirmed that the optimal magnitude of the illusion by disk rotation was obtained using 30 mm and 39 mm disks under the ordinary condition. Furthermore, using two unique custom devices, a similar magnitude of the illusion was maintained under the two-hand condition as that observed in ordinary condition. These results indicate that the disk rotation illusion appears to be a robust and replicable phenomenon. Notably, the magnitude of the illusion obtained under the two-hand condition produced almost the same amount of illusion as that obtained under the ordinary condition, suggesting that our custom devices are a valid and reliable way to evaluate this illusion, comparable to the original method.

Under the one hand condition, the growth in the magnitude of the illusion under the one-hand condition using the rotating hand did not reach the same level as the ordinary and two-hand conditions. On the other hand, the magnitude of the illusion under the one-hand condition using the rotating hand was significantly greater than that obtained using the holding hand. These results indicate that the magnitude of the illusion increased with the rotating hand. Conversely, the magnitude of the illusion under the one-hand condition using the holding hand did not change significantly in either the increasing or decreasing direction, suggesting that the holding hand contributes little to this illusion independently but may do so interactively. These findings suggest that the rotating hand mainly contributes to the disk rotation illusion. This conclusion clearly rejects Cormack’s (1973) hypothesis (a), which mainly relies on the holding hand for the illusory effects, and supports hypothesis (b), which focuses on the effects of differential adaptation of both hands. In addition, the SMI calculated from the illusion magnitudes obtained in the one-hand conditions, which could be regarded as an index of bimanual integration, did not reach the magnitude of illusion by two-hand and ordinary conditions.

What does the SMI imply?

It was assumed if the SMI was equivalent to the magnitude of illusion in two-hand trial, bimanual signals from both hands could be accumulated additively, but if not, be integrated interactively. The present findings suggest that the SMI reflects interactive integration of bimanual signals related to differential adaptation in both hands. Van der Horst et al. (2008a, b) found that the curvature aftereffect in haptic shape perception of 3D objects occurred in both static and dynamic touch, and the aftereffect was much larger for dynamic than static touch. They also indicated that the dynamic aftereffect could be transferred between both hands at an almost comparable amount to that obtained for the same hand, but this was not the case for static touch. Inter-manual transfer of adaptive aftereffect occurred in tactile perception between distance and continuous size (Tamè et al. 2026). Therefore, bimanual interplay could occur under the task of judging the disk size using both hands dynamically, but not when only one hand is used. Thus, the interactive adaptation of aftereffect may appear only under the two-hand condition and make the disk image seem much larger than the real thing.

Still, it remains unclear why bimanual interplay makes the disk image larger, even when information from both hands is available. An additional contribution of the contrast effect (Gibson 1933; Wijntjes and Kappers 2009) may contribute to the SMI during integration of signals from both hands. This contrast effect likely occurs when signals from both hands are integrated, causing the image to appear larger than the actual disk size. The contrast effect has been demonstrated in the Ebbinghaus (Titchener) figure known as size-contrast illusion, in which a center circle surrounded by smaller circles (inducers) is overestimated compared to another center circle surrounded by larger circles. The size-contrast effect of the Ebbinghaus configuration is obtained not only for visual stimulation (Coren and Enns 1993; Coren and Girgus 1978) but also in the haptic domain (Franz et al. 2000, 2001). The contrast effect has also been seen in the size-weight illusion known as Charpentier’s effect: Two objects of different but identical weight produces the illusory perception that the larger object is lighter when lifted (Buckingham 2014, 2019; Charpentier 1891). Like the disk rotation illusion, the size-weight illusion is strongly influenced by visual guidance, as the illusion weakens when visual information is altered or unavailable.

Limitations

Before drawing conclusions, the following limitations should be considered. First, regarding the incongruence between the SMI and the magnitude of illusion to the two-hand condition, it could be argued that the one-hand conditions are not equivalent to the two-hand conditions minus one hand because of the alteration to the somatosensory and proprioceptive experience in ways that go beyond simply removing one hand from the task. During the one-hand condition, participants were not required to move the unused hand, thereby minimizing proprioceptive input from the contralateral hand. Then, the differences between the two-hand trial with movement of both hands and one-hand trial without movement of the other hand could be a cause of the differences between SMI and two-hand condition. It has been reported that bilateral activation of somatosensory areas after unilateral stimulation (Hansson and Brismar 1999; Korvenoja et al. 1999) would be mediated by interhemispheric connections (Fabri et al. 1999; 2001). However, it is also known that despite somatosensory interhemispheric communication as such, we do not experience bilateral “percepts” when we are stimulated on one side of the body, suggesting that there seems to be suppression mechanisms for ipsilateral neural activity (Hlushchuk and Hari 2006; Lipton et al. 2006). It has been reported that bilateral transfer in motor skill as a physiological phenomenon has occurred as a change in the activation pattern both in the cerebellum and in the cerebral cortex, with activations in cortical areas involved in somatosensory integration, areas involved in procedural memory (Uggetti et al. 2016). Taken together, these findings suggest that our disk rotation task, which is closely involved in haptic perception and motor control, would be related to a higher level of processing (Kappers and Bergmann Tiest 2013, 2015), and not so heavily to the somatosensory activation. Thus, the differences in perceptual integration and memory for visualizing the disk images may produce the incongruences between the SMI and two-hand condition. Because few haptic illusions specifically require the simultaneous use of both hands, firm conclusions regarding this issue cannot yet be drawn. This question should therefore be addressed in future studies.

Secondly, the estimation of the disk size was based on matching it to a sample, as used by Cormack (1973). This method seems to rely heavily on the visual imagery of the disk constructed in the primary visual cortex and subsequent stages during the processing of signals from both hands. Cormack required his participants to estimate elongation of the disk by choosing and calling out one of the visual matching stimuli, oblong, as “corresponding most closely to the felt shape” (p. 591) of the disk. This procedure implies that participants should reconstruct imagery of felt diameter of both the rotating and holding hands, respond to the “ratio” of those images, and finally choose a corresponding oblong stimulus. All these signals are transmitted to a primary area of tactile perception and related higher areas including the visual system, and combined to respond properly, in this case, transforming the combined signals to a visual imagery, selecting and responding with a suitable number of one of the matching stimuli (Coren and Enns 1993; Coren and Girgus 1978; Massaro and Anderson 1971). Therefore, the findings of the present study, which could not be reproduced by the illusion measured individually for the rotating and holding hands, suggest that the disk rotation illusion may be established by an imagery of the visual object which is connected to the aftereffect of adaptation, bimanual integration, and contrast effect. In the early visual cortex, imagery events exhibit lower levels of neural activation compared to perceptual events (Ganis et al. 2004; Ishai et al. 2000), and to hold weaker stimulus-specific representations (Lee et al. 2013). Additionally, visual perception could be dominant over the haptic representation (Bertelson and Aschersleben 1998; Botvinick and Cohen 1998; Ehrsson et al. 2004; Gallace and Spence 2005; Hartcher-O’Brien et al. 2008; Pavani et al. 2000; Revol et al. 2009; Seizova-Cajic and Azzi 2011; Spence 2008; Stokes and Biggs 2015), and perception evokes a stronger sensory experience of vision than imagery (Dijkstra and Fleming 2023). The disappearance of the disk rotation illusion during visual observation suggests that visual guidance may suppress haptic illusions when direct visual input overrides imagery-based representations. However, this mechanism was not directly examined in the present study and should be investigated further.

Thirdly, the magnitude of the illusion under the two-hand condition was estimated using Cormack’s (1973) original method, whereas the magnitude of the illusion under the one-hand condition was evaluated using our method, which uses different matching stimuli than Cormack’s. Our matching figures for the one-hand condition were all drawn, not as oblongs but as perfect circles differing in size. Prior to conducting one of our preliminary studies (Imai et al. 2018), it was investigated whether Cormak's original method using Sheet-A would be effective under the one-hand condition and whether it could induce the illusion. However, we found that four participants were unable to perform this task and that no evidence of illusion was obtained. Thus, we could not apply the original procedure to the one-hand condition, since the participants examined a single disk using one hand, and there was no reference trace from the other hand for judging a ratio of the disk elongation. Moreover, we assumed that “changes” in the shape felt do not necessarily occur only along the “elongating” direction but along the “shrinking” one, under the one-hand condition. There were indeed data of negative values by 11 participants under the one-hand condition using the holding hand, suggesting that the size of disk diameter by the holding hand was perceived as shrinking. On the other hand, there was only one participant who perceived shrinking under the one-hand condition using the rotating hand. Incongruence between the SMI and the magnitude of illusion of the two-hand condition, therefore, would come from a kind of constant error of estimation bias. Methods allowing independent evaluation of each hand may therefore yield smaller illusion magnitudes than those originally reported by Cormack. This suggests that our devices may have offered a better estimation method for the disk rotation illusion than the original procedure, but further investigation should be necessary to elucidate this issue.

Lastly, only 39 mm disks could be used because of restrictions of the auto-rotation device, so the generality of our results may be debatable. As Cormack (1973) suggested and we confirmed in our preliminary studies (Imai et al 2017, 2019), the larger the disk is, the smaller the magnitude of illusion. Smaller disks could not be examined in the present study and should be investigated in future research.

Conclusion

We conclude that the disk rotation illusion is mainly produced by the rotating hand. The magnitude of the illusion may be larger when using two hands that provide an ensemble of contrast effects between two different disk imageries constituted by signals from both hands with adaptation and aftereffects. For the one-hand condition, however, no ensemble occurs, as the disk imagery is only constructed by one hand and no contrast effect can occur. This causes a decrease of magnitude of the illusion under the one-hand condition, and the SIM is not as large as that of the ordinary condition in which both images of hands contribute to forming the illusion. Hence, the findings of the present study demonstrate that the disk rotation illusion is not merely an intriguing phenomenon in itself, but by examining and comparing it to other illusory phenomena such as size-contrast or size-weight illusions using both hands, we could not only further explore the interaction between vision and touch, but also gain insights into bimanual interactions and cross-modal phenomena.
