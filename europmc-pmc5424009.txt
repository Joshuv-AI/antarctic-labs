LOOSE BODIES IN THE PERITONEUM; WITH
NOTES OF A CASE IN WHICH A LOOSE
OVARY (?) WAS FOUND IN THE POUCH OF
DOUGLAS*
By Professor R. W. JOHNSTONE, C.B.E., M.D., F.R.C.S.E.
Some three months ago there was recommended to me at
Ward 34, Royal Infirmary, by Dr Rossie of Bathgate, a Mrs E.,
aged 32, who had given birth to one child eighteen months
before, the delivery being accomplished by forceps. Subsequent
to this the patient developed a feeling as if something were
coming down and some frequency of micturition, as well as
an occasional sharp pain on the right side. On examination
I found that she had a very slight degree of cystocele, some
enlargement of the cervix, and a very definite tenderness in
the right flank close to the crest of the ilium and about three
inches behind the anterior superior spine. This tenderness
appeared to be associated with a small movable mass. I
arranged to admit her, and about a fortnight later she came into
my ward. The tenderness over the right iliac crest had by
that time disappeared, but I thought it advisable to have an
X-ray photograph of her pelvis as the symptom had been
so very marked when I had last seen her. The photograph
showed nothing in the right iliac fossa but an oval opaque
mass in the right side of the true pelvis, with apparently some
calcareous deposits in it. The radiologists were sufficiently
intrigued by this finding to ask for an opportunity of photo-
graphing her a second time, and the second film, which was
taken with the patient lying prone, showed that this mass had
shifted its position and was now lying towards the left side
behind the uterus. It appeared to be an oval mass about the
size of a plum. Chloroform examination on the 5th of
December enabled one to feel this mass posteriorly but not
to explain its apparent mobility. A few days later I opened
the abdomen. No free fluid was found in the peritoneal cavity.
The right ovary was cystic and near the hilum there were little
gritty patches suggesting a commencing calcareous change.

There was no left ovary and the left Fallopian tube, which was
about half an inch long, terminated in a tapering point which was
apparently completely sealed at the outer end. Lying perfectly
free in the Pouch of Douglas was a hard calcareous mass about
the size and shape of a large Brazil nut, and resembling an
enlarged ovary very closely in shape and conformation. This
was lifted out, the cystic portion of the right ovary was removed
by resection and the abdomen closed. The patient subsequently
made an uninterrupted recovery and left the hospital quite free
of symptoms. On examination the loose body was found to
be dark greenish in colour and to have an outer coating of a
calcareous nature. It felt as if the interior were not calcareous
but rather softer, and on attempting to cut into it one found
that the interior consisted of dried-up tissue which crumbled
to dust on the least attempt to cut it with a knife. The
whole appearance with the slightly convoluted surface strongly
suggested an ovary and this supposition seemed to be confirmed
by the absence of the ovary on the left side. A portion of the
mass was decalcified and a microscopic section made. The
interior was absolutely structureless and no tissue could be
recognised, but here and there on the surface there were little
patches of quite clearly recognisable low cuboidal epithelium,
closely resembling the germinal epithelium of the ovary.
This rather remarkable case has led me to look into the
question of loose bodies in the peritoneal cavity. Dr John
Sturrock has kindly helped me in looking into the literature of
the subject and I very gladly take this opportunity of expressing
my indebtedness to him.
The loose bodies which have been described from time to
time have been either neoplasms, particularly fibroids of the
uterus, or smaller objects, most of which have been assumed to
have originated as, or in, appendices epiploicae. The literature
in regard to these latter has been recently summed up by Hunt
in an article upon Torsion of the Appendix Epiploicaas a cause
of acute abdominal symptoms. He refers to 11 cases where
either during the dissection of a body in the anatomical rooms
or clinically at operation an object of this nature was found.
A considerable number of these bodies were present in hernial
sacs, but the explanation of this is probably that these sacs
frequently form the lowest and most dependent pouch of the
peritoneal cavity and any loose body would naturally tend to
gravitate into them. Furthermore, it is probable that the

presence of a loose body in a hernial sac might well favour the
occurrence of strangulation and so determine the performance
of an operation leading to its discovery. Hunt, however, omits
a number of interesting cases recorded in the Transactions of
the Pathological Society of London. In all, nine specimens of
loose body from the peritoneal cavity were exhibited to this
Society between the years 1854 and i860. The first, which
was exhibited by Mr Shaw, was from a hernial sac and illustrates
the point I have just mentioned. The patient, who had been
wearing a truss, got out of bed without his truss to pass
water when, without any straining, he felt a round hard object
suddenly descend into the upper part of the scrotum, escaping,
as he said, " like a ball shot out from his belly." Finding that
he could not reduce his hernia he came to hospital the following
day and was operated upon twenty-four hours later. The loose
body, more or less round and about ih inches in diameter,
was described as " looking like a hard-boiled egg with the shell
removed." Section of it showed that what may be called the
cortex was as dense as fibro-cartilage, while the centre was
cystic and contained fat. Mr Shaw suggested that a detached
appendix epiploica had become coated with successive layers
of fibrin from the peritoneal serum and that this had formed
the dense outer covering which he described. Subsequent
discussion of this and one or two of the other specimens before
the Pathological Society suggests that these loose bodies which
originate in appendices epiploicae, are not due to a separation
of an appendix epiploica as a result of extreme torsion but
that they are developed in an appendix epiploica and undergo
the calcareous change so often described while in continuity
with the intestine and with a broad pedicle. Subsequent torsion
or attenuation of the pedicle leads to separation. This is the
view of Wilks, who found such a loose specimen and found in
the same body an appendix epiploica in which this series of
changes was apparently in process. One of the nine specimens
was exhibited by Dr Hughes Bennett and is described as being
as large as a billiard ball. Its nature corresponded to Shaw's
specimen. The two last specimens were exhibited by Mr
(subsequently Sir) Henry Thomson and by Dr Murchison, and
were both regarded as similar in nature to Shaw's original
specimen. One of the nine, however, which was found lying
on the upper surface of the liver in a cadaver, presented
appearances which led to its being diagnosed as originating in

an echinococcal cyst. On the whole, however, it would appear
that appendices epiploicae, whether detached by torsion or
giving rise to portions which present calcareous and other
degenerative changes prior to separation, provide the commonest
source of these loose bodies.
Another source to which reference is made by writers is
subperitoneal uterine fibroids. Bland Sutton makes a brief
reference to these, and Halliday Croom, in a paper read before
this Society in 1896, refers to Sir James Simpson as having
described instances discovered post-mortem. In the discussion
of Halliday Croom's paper Haultain suggested that these
tumours must have originated in the most superficial part of
the uterine muscle, and after extrusion have been attached by
attenuated peritoneal stalks which ultimately gave way probably
as a result of torsion. Croom also referred to Simpson as
having found a calcareous fibroid free in the Pouch of Douglas,
and to Depaul, who found a fibroma entirely free in the same
situation.
In the same article Croom records two striking cases of
his own. The first was a simple ovarian cyst occupying the
Pouch of Douglas, which he was able to lift out " entire without
securing or ligaturing anything whatever." The surface of the
tumour was smooth, save at one point where there was a little
roughness, and its removal was followed by absolutely no
haemorrhage. No trace of any pedicle could be felt or seen.
Croom's second case was that of a hard tumour about the
size of a billiard ball which was found lying in the Pouch of
Douglas. This also was found to be free of any attachment
and was merely lifted out. The contents of the tumour were
found to be old blood clot. Haultain, however, suggested during
the discussion that in this case, in which he had assisted Croom
at the operation, the loose body was really a tubal abortion
in which the blood clot had become coated with fibrin.
This rather meagre literature on the subject throws little,
if any, light on the specimen which I show you to-night, and
I venture to adhere to the belief that it is a unique specimen
of an ovary which has become detached. I base this firstly
upon the shape and size of the loose body; secondly, upon the
fact that the ovary on the left side was missing and that the
tube showed appearances strongly suggestive of a spontaneous
amputation ; thirdly, the finding of some low cuboidal epithelium
on the surface is, so far as it goes, rather confirmatory.

The feeling of gritty particles at the hilum of the right ovary-
suggests that such a degenerative change might have been
more advanced in the left ovary and might have favoured
torsion and subsequent detachment, much as it would appear
to do in the calcareous portions of the appendices epiploicae.
I regret that at the operation I did not explore the region of
the left kidney, because it appears that in all instances in which
an ovary is congenitally absent the kidney on the same side
is also absent. This omission could, of course, be rectified by
doing a pyelographic examination of the patient, but it is
doubtful whether she would be willing to put herself to so
much trouble in order to settle what is, after all, a somewhat
academic scientific point which would make no special appeal
to her.
If it is not an ovary, the question remains, what is it? It
does not correspond to the description of the bodies supposed
to originate in appendices epiploicje, and although I have not
cut right through the centre of the specimen, because to do so
would be to ruin it owing to its friability, yet in so far as I have
opened into it there has been no trace whatsoever of anything
like fat. If any Fellow of the Society can throw out any
further suggestion as to the possible origin of this curious body
I shall be exceedingly interested and glad to give him every
facility for investigation.
