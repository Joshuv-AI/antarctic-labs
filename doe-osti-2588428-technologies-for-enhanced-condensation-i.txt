ABSTRACT
The direct-fired supercritical carbon dioxide (sCO2) power cycle has higher efficiency, a compact
machinery footprint, and is resilient under transient operation. It has been shown that improving
the cycle’s main cooler significantly improves performance, including increased cycle efficiency,
decreased cost of electricity, and decreased cooler outlet temperatures. One way to improve this
process is through enhanced condensation techniques and management of condensation fluid. This
manuscript provides an overview of active and passive enhanced condensation technologies, with
a focus on passive approaches. Compared to passive approaches, active approaches can have
higher heat transfer enhancement, but the additional power and infrastructure can be problematic.
The passive approaches identified include microfins (helical and herringbone), corrugated tubing,
dimpled tubing, and pin fins. After comparison, it was found that the microfin geometry has the
best performance characteristics followed by the additively manufactured (AM) pin fins. It is noted
that current literature regarding AM enhancements is lacking, and investigation of conventional as
well as novel geometries fabricated in this manner may be worth further investigation. Benchmark
correlations for these geometries are identified and their limitations are outlined in this report. For
further investigation, a matrix of test articles is presented for possible experimentation.

1. INTRODUCTION
The direct-fired supercritical carbon dioxide (sCO2) power cycle, also called the Allam or Allam-
Fetvedt cycle, is an advanced power cycle which combusts a hydrocarbon fuel in an oxy-
combustion environment (Allam et al., 2013). The high-pressure combustor exit stream is directed
to a turbine, which extracts work as the fluid pressure decreases to the low pressure of the cycle.
Then the combustion products are cooled in a recuperator, water is removed, and the working fluid
is compressed to the high pressure of the cycle. Following compression, the fluid is cooled and
pumped through the recuperator before returning to the combustor. The cycle is considered a sCO2
power cycle since the primary constituent of the working fluid is carbon dioxide (CO2). An
example of this system is displayed in Figure 1. The cycle requires significant recuperation, up to
three times the combustor thermal input, to achieve high-efficiency operation.
Benefits of the direct-fired cycle include a high-pressure CO2 stream for carbon sequestration,
higher efficiency, lower water use, compact turbomachinery, and resilience under transient
operation (Brun et al., 2017; Moore et al., 2023).

System studies have identified that improving the effectiveness of the cycle main cooler can
significantly improve system performance. One study showed that the cycle efficiency increases
by 1.4 percentage points and the cost of electricity (COE) decreases by 3.8% as the cooler outlet
temperature decreases by 6 °C (Pidaparti et al., 2022).
Further, trans-critical operation is an option to minimize the levelized cost of electricity (LCOE)
and maximize power generation in sCO2 cycles. This is demonstrated in studies by Pidaparti et al.
(2022, 2023). In Figure 2, net plant efficiency and LCOE are plotted as a function of cooler
temperature (°C). The net plant efficiency is observed to increase monotonically with decreasing
cooler temperature. However, the LCOE decreases reaching a minimum near 20 °C before
increasing at lower cooler temperatures.
Cooling and compression state points are shown in Figure 3 on a pressure and enthalpy diagram.

2. REVIEW OF RELATED PRIOR WORK
Active and passive enhancement techniques for condensation have received significant attention
for multiple decades (Hughes et al., 2024b; Dalkilic and Wongwises, 2009). Passive technologies
are approaches that introduce heat transfer enhancement features or surface treatments to enhance
the heat transfer. The extended surface features enhance the heat transfer, are durable (when
manufactured at millimeter scale), and are accompanied by an increase in flow pressure drop.
Surface treatment approaches modify surface wettability to enhance heat transfer but typically
suffer from poor durability. For passive technologies, the conventional state-of-the-art
technologies are microfins which include helical and herringbone shapes. More recently there has
been interest in exploring the impact of additive manufacturing (AM) on condensation heat transfer
(Ho et al., 2019). Geometries of interest have focused on designs that are difficult to manufacture
by conventional means, including pin fins.
Enhanced technologies are an active area of inquiry. These approaches utilize mechanisms to
enhance heat transfer while consuming power from a source external to the fluid flow.
Considerable effort has focused on electrohydrodynamics (EHD) techniques (Sadek et al., 2006).
More recently, fluid acoustics have received attention (Hughes et al., 2023). Active enhancements
have also been combined with passive techniques (Hughes et al., 2024a).
To make a case for further exploration of passive extended surfaces (enabled by AM), enhanced
condensation of CO2 have been reviewed. Heat transfer enhancement and pressure drop penalty
have been tabulated and compared. The practical limitations of the active techniques were
considered, and passive extended surface fins were identified for additional study. Literature
specific to passive enhanced surfaces was reviewed for both conventional (Dalkilic and
Wongwises, 2009) and AM technologies (Ho et al., 2019). This report identifies research gaps
where high-performing conventional passive technologies have not been considered in AM parts
and potential opportunities for AM-enabled, high-performance heat transfer enhancements.

3.2 FOCUS ON PASSIVE TECHNOLOGIES
Though performance of passive technologies as represented by HE, PP, and TPF may not
outperform active systems like EHD, they tend to be mechanically simpler and do not require any
additional power to implement in a system. A range of passive enhanced condensation
technologies exist and have been studied. A majority of these technologies focus on different
geometries that can be implemented on the heat transfer tube resulting in better condensation
behavior. A range of these geometries, their performance characteristics and sources are shown in
Table 3.
These geometries include helical microfins, herringbone microfins, corrugated tubing, dimpled
tubing, and pin fins. Except for the pin fins, all of these geometries can be conventionally
manufactured. This means they are constructed by imprinting a desired pattern onto flat sheeting,
then rolling and welding this sheeting to create the tube, taking stock tube and twisting it to form

 as a die. Alternatively pin fins can be created using AM such as selective laser melting (SLM).

When comparing these geometries to one another it immediately becomes apparent that microfins
(both helical and herringbone) show the best heat transfer enhancement of all the conventionally
fabricated designs. The maximum HE value displayed for the corrugated tubing and dimpled
tubing was 1.5 and 1.84, respectively, whereas the range for the helical microfin HE was from 1.8
to 2.4, and for the herringbone microfin was from 1.7 to 4. The higher maximum HE values for
the microfin technologies have the drawback of higher pressure drop, particularly for the
herringbone microfins. However, the TPF listed in Table 3 suggests that there is considerable
potential for the microfin and AM pin-fin arrays to achieve better heat transfer for the same amount
of pumping power.
The microfin designs are believed to out-perform other technologies due to several factors. For the
helical microfins as compared to the smooth tube, there is an increase in surface area, increased
fluid mixing due to the fins, and increased surface tension on condensate drainage. This also results
in the liquid layer being distributed around the circumference, resulting in the flow being annular
rather than intermittent or stratified (Han and Lee, 2005). These same behaviors are generally true
of the herringbone microfin geometry as well, though there is an increase in HE and PP, as
compared to the helical microfins. For example, there is a 27% increase in pressure gradients as
compared to the helical microfins (Olivier et al., 2004).
The pin fin geometry is more novel, and as such there are fewer sources which have studied it. It
is the only passive technology included in Table 3 that is AM. Images of this design are shown in
Figure 5.

 Figure 5: Conical-shaped pins AM example panel (a), isometric view of conical pin design (b).

It becomes clear that of the passive technologies studied, the microfin geometries are the best by
performance as the TPF ranges are well above those shown for the other geometries. However, the
conventionally manufactured designs have been more extensively studied. Since there is less
literature regarding the AM design, and it is unclear if there may be potential benefits (such as
inherent surface roughness) posed by creating conventional designs utilizing AM processes,
further study of AM heat transfer tubes utilizing novel geometries as well as optimized
conventional geometries may be warranted to fill this gap in literature.

5. SUMMARY, CONCEPT, AND RECOMMENDATIONS
This study reviewed state-of-the-art condensation management technology in the form of helical
and herringbone microfins, and compared them to the performance of the smooth tube as the
baseline. The impact of targeted condensation management technology on system performance
was clear. A novel geometry can be designed for this specific use case and made possible due to
the adoption of AM, and may result in increased condensate management and system performance.
The design of this potential novel geometry is outside the bounds of this current review but may
warrant further investigation in the future.
A test article matrix is proposed for the experimental work in this area (Table 5). Further research
into this area is recommended with the fabrication of eight test articles, three being conventionally
manufactured (a smooth tube, helical microfins, and herringbone microfins), and five being AM
fabricated (these being a smooth tube, helical microfin, herringbone microfin, conical microfin,
and the novel condensate management specific AM geometry).

 Table 5: Test Article Matrix (Proposed test articles identified by green shaded cells.)

The conventionally manufactured test articles can be used to verify results against existing
literature, as well as provide a benchmark for the comparison of potential performance variations
against the same geometries created using the AM process. Whereas the novel condensate
management-specific AM geometry can be utilized to expand the literature on AM fabricated
designs.
This would seek to answer what impact the AM fabrication process has on enhanced condensation
tube performance as compared to conventionally manufactured tubes, as well as allow for the
expansion of literature on novel AM-fabricated tube performance with the condensate
management specific geometry and conical microfin. Finally, together these test sets allow for
multifaceted study on passive geometries for enhanced condensation technologies. However, these
questions could be investigated separately allowing for a smaller sub-population of test articles to
be fabricated.

6. CONCLUSIONS
A literature review and screening analysis was performed to identify enhanced condensation
technologies for sCO2 power cycles. Both active and passive technologies were considered. Active
technologies screened included EHD, active surface modification, and fluid vibration. Passive
technologies included heat transfer enhancement features and surface wettability modification.
The following are key findings from the study.
 • Of the active heat transfer enhancements considered, EHD has the highest thermal
 performance factor (ranging from 2.1 to 2) and some of the highest heat transfer
 augmentations (ranging from 3 to 8.3). Fluid vibration, including acoustic actuation, had
 only incremental enhancement for condensation flows.
 • The comparison between active and passive technologies for use in sCO2 applications can
 be guided both by thermal performance and practical considerations. EHD has the highest
 thermal performance factor and can be combined with microfins for improved
 performance. However, the high voltages and dedicated electrode installation in each tube,
 limit practical applications in power cycles. While surface wettability modification can
 improve performance, technologies using surface coatings have challenges with durability.
 Rigid heat transfer enhancement features are preferred after review of performance and
 practical considerations.
 • For conventional passive features, both herringbone and helical microfin tubes had high
 thermal performance factors, with herringbone microfins exceeding the performance of
 helical microfins.
 • Interest in AM has identified a path toward internally patterned pin fins. One paper
 presented results for helically patterned conical pin fins (Ho et al., 2019), that are
 competitive with the thermal performance of conventional helical microfin tubes.
 • Baseline correlations were identified for smooth tube condensation which will be needed
 to benchmark the condensation performance. Additionally, limited correlations for helical
 microfin and conical pin fin tubes were identified.
