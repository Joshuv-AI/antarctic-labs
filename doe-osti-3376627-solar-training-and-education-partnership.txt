Executive Summary

Solar Landscape’s STEP-UP program provided high quality solar installation training in
partnership with community-based organizations (CBOs) in various regions within the
U.S. Solar Landscape leveraged internal subject matter experts (SME’s) industry
guidance and regional training assessments to provide customized training designed to
support the growing solar and broader energy sector. Upon completion of the program,
the team assisted trainees and nonprofit partners with connections to Solar Landscape
contractors as well as local and National residential solar installation companies to
facilitate placement into careers and apprenticeships.

STEP-UP GOALS

  Provide skills focused jobs training with direct pathways to employment.
  Leverage existing technical training facilities (Job Corps, etc.) through multi-
 stakeholder high road partnerships to ensure that students are aware of
 opportunities in the solar industry.
  Increase access to career pathways into the solar workforce by offering industry
 led hands-on training
  Gain support from community solar host sites to provide sustainable funding for
 the program.

STEP-UP demonstrated success through training, job placement, and the development
of new partnerships. Partnership development was central to the success of the
program. Nonprofit partnerships were forged based upon each organizations’ ability to
recruit potential employees from the community and aid with wraparound services for
trainees. Nonprofit partners provided critical resources such as transportation and
childcare, thereby ensuring trainees would have support needed to access high paying
solar jobs.

Throughout the duration of the project, Solar Landscape developed multiple high road
partnerships with nonprofits and employers. One of the largest new partnerships
developed because of this project is the collaboration between Solar Landscape and

 4

YouthBuild Global which is the nonprofit organization that supports hundreds of
YouthBuild programs across the Country.

Solar Landscape also developed new partnerships with other solar companies, bringing
industry leaders together to support the design and delivery of high-quality solar
training. Companies including leading National residential solar installers as well as
major manufacturers joined the project as employer partners, guest speakers, and
supporters. Their commitment to workforce development is evident through their work
sharing job opportunities and interviewing trainees and providing materials to support
hands-on training.

Throughout the duration of the project, Solar Landscape reached 170 individuals with
training, performed training in four States including New Jersey, Massachusetts, Illinois,
and Texas. At the end of the project, 48% of trainees were employed or enrolled in
continuing education or an apprenticeship.

Background

The Interstate Renewable Energy Council (IREC) Census published during 2023
reported that 44% of solar industry employers said it was ‘very difficult’ to find qualified
applicants. Overall, 90% of all solar companies and 97% of solar installation companies
reported it was either ‘very difficult’ or ‘somewhat difficult’ to find qualified workers. This
survey demonstrates that the need for a trained workforce is widespread, and many
solar companies are experiencing hiring challenges, especially for installation roles. As
a leading National solar EPC, Solar Landscape can attest to the challenges with hiring a
qualified solar installation workforce.

According to An Updated Review of the Solar PV Installation Workforce Literature
(NREL, 2023) there are gaps in studies capturing what kinds of skills employers in the
solar industry are looking for. Given the lack of reliable data and metrics, existing
training programs may not align with what employers need. As a vertically integrated
engineering, procurement and construction (EPC) company, Solar Landscape ensures
that training is designed to meet the needs of solar installation and construction teams.
Further, the STEP-UP team was able to conduct informal regional needs assessments
to better understand hiring needs at the local level. The combination of internal subject
matter expertise, high-quality partnerships, the regional needs assessments, and the
Solar Landscape Technical Advisory Committee (TAC) provided Solar Landscape with
a strong foundation to build an industry-driven program.

Project Objectives

The objectives of STEP-UP were to train 500 people, perform training in 7 states, place

60% of trainees into employment and place 10% in an apprenticeship or pre-
apprenticeship program.

The project STEP-UP concluded early. The program was anticipated to run from
3/1/2024-2/28/2027 but ended in March of 2025. Because the project concluded
significantly earlier than anticipated, only a portion of the objectives and goals were met.
While the team was unable to meet all objectives, there are positive outcomes and

launching a Technical Advisory Committee (TAC), conducting a needs assessment,

Commercial Solar, and Residential Solar) with incorporated feedback from more than 5
members of the TAC. We established 6 new training sites exceeding our goal of 3, and
we reached over 1000 social media impressions, exceeding our goal of 100.

Most of the go/no-go decision points were also met. We exceeded our goal of enrolling
125 participants in training, reaching 170 trainees by the end of BP 1. Solar Landscape
hosted eleven training courses in BP 1, far exceeding the target of three trainings. On

end of BP 1, 48% of trainees were employed or enrolled in an apprenticeship,
continuing education, or a pre-apprenticeship.

Subtask 1.3: Assess New Markets
Milestone 1.2.1: Conduct a needs assessment

An informal regional needs assessment was conducted to assess new markets for solar
job demand. Completing a comprehensive assessment of local solar installation job
opportunities was challenging due to the lack of accurate State level data on solar jobs.
Solar Landscape assessed new markets based on favorable solar programs and
policies at the state and municipal level, data available from the Solar Energy Industry
Association (SEIA) as well as data collected from the Interstate Renewable Energy
Council (IREC) Annual jobs report.

Regional Solar Installation Job Assessment Highlights

NEW JERSEY: Incentives including potential SREC earnings of more than $200, retail
rate net metering, property and sales tax exemptions, a robust community solar
program and a low-income solar program all contribute to a strong market for solar and
a high projected growth of solar jobs. According to IREC, New Jersey had 6,628 solar
jobs in 2023. Although employers are optimistic, 55% continue to report overall hiring
difficulty. The combination of strong solar policies, high job growth, and employer
difficulty with hiring makes NJ uniquely positioned to have a successful solar job training
program.

ILLINOIS: Trailblazing policies and programs including The Climate and Equitable Jobs
Act (CEJA), Illinois Shines, Community Driven Community Solar (CDCA), and the
Illinois Future Energy Jobs Act (FEJA) are driving a projected 12% growth in solar jobs
in the State. The Minimum Equity Standard (MES) applies to the project workforce of
entities participating in certain Illinois Power Agency (IPA) programs and procurements,
including: The IPA's Illinois Shines Program, The Self-direct Program, and Competitive

 7

renewable energy procurements. The MES is part of the Equity Accountability System
(EAS), which was created by the Climate and Equitable Jobs Act (CEJA).

There is a State sponsored database that facilitates connections between individuals
interested in solar careers with solar employers. For STEP-UP, one of our key partners-
HACIA- is one of the FEJA approved programs. Once the HACIA students complete the
STEP-UP training they work with HACIA to earn NABCEP and to connect with
employers seeking to hire qualified workers.

NEW YORK: Since 2018 New York has shown a 24.5% job growth increase. Although
New York is ranked in the top 5 for Solar and Clean Energy jobs, it was still reported in
2024 that clean energy employers found it was very difficult to hire talent. Another
bright spot in NYS is the robust on-the-job (OTJ) training funding available through the
New York State Energy Research and Development Authority (NYSERDA). Other
states typically administer OTJ funds at the County level which can require a
cumbersome amount of paperwork. With the NYSERDA focused program there are
opportunities to connect employer partners to NYSERDA to apply for the PON and
offset the OTJ training expenses and incentivize companies to hire more trainees from
workforce programs.

MASSACHUSETTES: Massachusetts continues to be a top ten solar State. The
Massachusetts Clean Energy Center (MASS CEC) has ambitious programs aimed at
increasing access to solar and supporting workforce development opportunities.
Massachusetts has some of the highest electricity costs in the Country, which fuels the
continued growth of residential solar in the State. One challenge for programs in
Massachusetts is the strict journeyman ratio requirements. All MA solar projects require
a 1-1 ratio of journeyman to apprentice.

Subtask 1.4: Develop Training Materials
Milestone 1.3.1 Develop at least 2 new courses reviewed by a minimum of 3 SME’s

New curriculum resources were developed, shared with the entire Technical Advisory
Committee, and reviewed by internal Solar Landscape Subject Matter Experts (SME’s)
in addition to members of the TAC. Solar Landscape developed the curriculum based
on experience in the industry, data from SEIA and IREC, as well as input from subject
matter experts. In total more than 5 members of the TAC along with 3 internal Solar
Landscape SME’s provided feedback for the final review. The new and updated courses
cover commercial solar installation and residential solar installation and Solar
Operations & Maintenance (O&M). The residential and commercial curriculum covers
hands-on installation with flat roof and pitched roof systems, wire management, basic
electrical, safety, inverter installation, pipe bending, crimping, and more. Components of
the curriculum include a resource library of PowerPoint presentations, homework

 8

assignments, hands-on assessments, state by state solar policy overviews, materials
lists, and supplemental resources including a guide for building small scale off grid
systems, flash cards, and a video library. Curriculum resources are available to all
STEP-UP partner organizations.

2024. Prior to the training, YouthBuild Lake County instructors and students designed

practices, roofing, and carpentry. The hands-on STEP-UP training took place outdoors
on the mock pitched roof as well as a nearby flat roof which enabled participants to gain
solar experience under real world work conditions. Another highlight of working with
YouthBuild Lake County was the engagement with local contractors and employer
partners. There were presentations by Arch Electrical and a local IBEW Utility Scale
solar contractor. Employers provided the participants with opportunities to learn more
about jobs in the area. In addition to employers, a training representative from UNIRAC

Highlights, Challenges, and Takeaways

Challenges

 Training Locations: Some training partners were better equipped to host hands-on
 training than others. Some sites could not provide access to hand-tools and
 classroom spaces that are designed for construction training. This was particularly
 challenging for partnerships out of state where we had to travel with heavy
 equipment or purchase additional tools. It is more efficient to offer training out of
 state when partner organizations can provide a classroom space set up for
 construction training and access to tools.
 Recruitment & Outreach: During training with one of the first cohorts there were
 some participants who expressed having a fear of heights and were very hesitant to
 climb a ladder. For all following programs, the STEP-UP team worked closely with
 each organization to ensure that everyone participating in the training could confirm
 that they were able to climb a ladder and comfortable with being on a roof.
 Group Size: Another challenge was working with larger groups. To facilitate an
 effective hands-on training and ensure each participant has sufficient time to
 practice with different racking systems and participate in the assessment it is
 beneficial to have groups of 10-15 participants. When there are 20 or more students
 there is more waiting time to share tools, less space on the mock roof, and less
 hands-on experience for each trainee.
 Industry Barriers: One significant barrier to working in solar is having access to a
 personal vehicle and having an active driver’s license. Solar installation jobs require
 travel, and a large majority of jobs required workers to have personal transportation
 to get to and from the warehouse and potentially to and from the actual jobsites. This
 is a significant barrier for many participants in the training. Some partner
 organizations can offer trainees access to drivers ed and can help with the process
 to get a license, but it continues to be a barrier.

 12

What Worked Well

 Tri-Sector Partnerships: Leveraging partnerships with nonprofit organizations,
 industry, and government agencies was incredibly important to the success of the
 training and is the catalyst for the continuation of the training beyond the life of the
 award. State programs like Illinois Shines continue to support the development of
 solar workforce training programs.
 Baseline Skills: The STEP-UP training is a one-week crash course designed to
 prepare people for solar careers, but it is not a full-scale construction training. Many
 nonprofit partners engaged in this project already provide basic construction skills
 training. For example, participants in the training at YouthBuild Lake County and
 Just A Start had OSHA certifications and had already completed National Center for
 Construction Education and Research (NCCER) or the Home Builders Institute Pre-
 Apprenticeship Certificate Training (HBI PACT). These students were already
 equipped with critical construction skills. Having these skills ensured the STEP-UP
 team was able to go more in-depth with training and spend less time on basic
 construction skills.
 Wrap-Around Support: The STEP-UP team worked with partner organizations to
 ensure all training participants had access to additional wrap-around support
 services during and after the training. Participants were provided with stipends,
 career support services, soft skills training, resume preparation, and interview
 preparation support when applicable. Providing wrap-around support services is a
 team effort and proved to be effective based on anecdotal evidence from
 participants who often shared that the stipends made participation in the program
 more accessible.
 Program Sustainability: With program sustainability as a key goal of the project,
 STEP-UP also provided curriculum resources, on-going technical assistance, and
 non-consumable solar installation materials for all program sites. Supplies included
 residential racking systems, commercial flat-roof racking systems, 8 PV modules,
 recycled inverters, electrical conduit, and PV wire.
 Recruitment Efforts: As a technical training provider, we have relied on training
 partners in each location to lead all recruitment efforts. This approach ensured that
 we were able to focus on providing high-quality training while local organizations
 hosted training and recruited students. When requested, we provide schedules,
 photos, fliers, and other materials to support outreach and recruitment. In some
 instances, we have hosted web-based info sessions, and/or met with partner teams
 to review Q&A.

Subtask 4.4: Track Employment

Solar Landscape takes an innovative and worker-centric approach to its workforce
development strategy by not only supporting training but taking an active role in
connecting trainees to job opportunities. Following an NJRC training in 2024, Solar
Landscape hired two NJRC program graduates to join the construction team. NJRC is a
nonprofit agency that provides critically needed services to individuals returning from
state prison, county jail, addiction treatment centers, and veterans.

As part of the training a training with YouthBuild Lake County in October of 2024, Solar
Landscape invited recruiters from Arch Electric to present to students about job
opportunities with Arch. Juan, 20, of Zion, is one of ten people who completed the solar
training. Following graduation, he landed a full-time position with Arch Electric and
reflected in an interview with the Chicago Tribune that the rewards are more than
financial. “I know solar energy is important for the world, in a positive way.” Juan said.
“Solar is a big step that will help the world in the years to come.” Juan is still working
with Arch, earning over $80 an hour, and enrolled in the electrical apprenticeship
program.

Another highlight is that we have 27 trainees enrolled in registered apprenticeships in
Electrical, Building Maintenance & Repair, and Laborers. Other trainees from the
program are working with National and local solar companies.

One major challenge has been capturing up-to-date and accurate data on job retention
following training. Given the broad reach of the program, we rely heavily on our
nonprofit partners to share job opportunities with participants and follow up, and most of
our nonprofit partners have shared with us that once students are secure full time
employment they are less likely to respond to requests for updates and general
outreach.

Based on the latest data, 56% of trainees from the first half of BP 1 secured
employment or an apprenticeship, and 48% of the 170 total people trained are enrolled
in continuing education, an apprenticeship, or are employed in energy or a related
sector. Moving forward, Solar Landscape will continue to explore ways to support
workforce partners with reporting and follow up after a training and for the participants
trained during the course of this award, Solar Landscape will continue to reach out to
past trainees and partner organizations to collect data on job placement, and to share
job opportunities and additional resources.

Solar Industry Regional Jobs Analysis

Clean and Renewable energy is one of the top ways to help the United States build a more
equitable economy.

The year 2023 was a banner year for solar installation, with 40.3 GW added in new capacity,
representing a 76% increase compared to 2022. Solar energy has become the nation’s
fastest-growing source of electricity. This expansion in solar projects has resulted in
thousands of new jobs in roles in solar installation, project management, working in IT or
sales, and other related functions. In 2023, solar accounted for 55% of newly added
electricity-generating capacity, which was the highest proportion recorded since data
collection began.

Strong job growth was reported in both the solar and wind sectors, with increases of 5.3%
and 4.5%, respectively. Moving forward, projections by the United States Department of
Energy (DOE) suggest that the Inﬂation Reduction Act will double the share of electricity
generated from clean sources by 2030, primarily driven by growth in solar and wind. To
support this expansion, construction has intensiﬁed in many locations around the U.S. The
energy construction sector added nearly 90,000 jobs, growing by 4.5%, almost double the
economy-wide construction employment growth of 2.3%.

In coming years, a major barrier to growth in the solar and storage industry is anticipated to
be the lack of available workers. Recruitment and hiring of qualiﬁed employees have
remained long-standing challenges for this industry. According to the 2023 Solar Jobs
Census from the International Renewable Energy Council (IREC), hiring difficulty has
lessened somewhat, but it remains a critical concern for solar ﬁrms. As effective
recruitment strategies are developed and shared, employers will beneﬁt from tapping into
diverse candidate pools. Equally important will be partnerships with training providers to
ensure that courses and curricula align with industry needs.

Additionally, as the U.S. workforce becomes more diverse, it is essential that policymakers
and employers consider the full pool of potential workers, including underrepresented
groups historically not employed in particular sectors. New laws necessitate the proactive
implementation of job access and workforce development strategies to prepare workers of
all backgrounds for careers in construction, manufacturing, and other energy and
infrastructure-related ﬁelds. Investments from the Inﬂation Reduction Act particularly
emphasize the need for targeted hiring practices, pre-apprenticeship programs, and other

tools to increase access for underrepresented groups, especially women and Black
workers, who are consistently underrepresented in the solar industry.

Workforce development initiatives will be crucial for training millions of workers to ﬁll
positions created by investments from the Inﬂation Reduction Act. Sectors like
construction, transportation, manufacturing, and utilities are expected to see high job
growth, and these sectors would greatly beneﬁt from focused workforce development
efforts. Many of these growing sectors currently employ a high percentage of workers
without a bachelor’s degree, providing clearer insight into the workforce gaps that must be
addressed.

As we examine different states and their current and projected clean energy jobs, data will
be presented to highlight the progress made and identify areas for improvement. This
information will underscore the critical role that workforce development plays in
supporting solar and clean energy jobs across the nation.

Overview: In California, green jobs have shown stability in recent years. In 2022, there was

California Solar Policy Overview:

California's Renewables Portfolio Standard (RPS) is one of the most ambitious
renewable energy standards in the country. The RPS program requires all electric load
serving entities to procure 60% of its electricity portfolio from eligible renewable energy
resources by 2030.

The California Energy Commission: is leading the state to a 100 percent clean energy
future for all. As the state's primary energy policy and planning agency, the Energy
Commission is committed to reducing energy costs and environmental impacts of energy
use while ensuring a safe, resilient, and reliable supply of energy.

In December 2022, California approved its net energy metering (NEM) 3.0 policies, which
cut the level of compensation by 75% for customers selling their excess solar power back
to the grid.5 By the end of 2023, this had led to a dramatic slowdown in the residential solar
market. The immediate impact, however, was to stimulate installation in the early part of
the year as property owners sought to start projects before the policy became law in April.

As a result, residential installations in California initially surged but tapered off as projects
were completed, resulting in 13% growth from 2022 overall. Residential deployment
dropped 25% in the ﬁrst quarter of 2024 compared to Q1 2023, and analysts project a 40%
drop for 2024. Given the relative size of the California market, this slowdown is expected to
reverberate in the residential solar market nationwide, resulting in a 14% overall
decline.6 Since this Solar Jobs Census reports jobs as of 2023, it does not yet reﬂect the full
impact of the NEM 3.0 decision.

California Future Outlook:

While California has made signiﬁcant strides in harnessing solar power, challenges persist.
The intermittency of solar energy production, the need for continued technological
advancements, and the ongoing debate about land use for utility-scale solar installations
are areas that demand attention.

Furthermore, the state must navigate regulatory complexities and policy frameworks to
ensure a smooth transition to a renewable energy future. Continued collaboration between
government, industry stakeholders, and the public is essential to overcome these
challenges and build a sustainable energy ecosystem.

Looking ahead, the future of solar power in California appears promising. Advances in solar
technology, coupled with ongoing research and development, hold the potential to
enhance efficiency and reduce costs further. The state’s commitment to innovation,
coupled with the growing awareness of the environmental and economic beneﬁts of solar
energy, positions California as a frontrunner in the global transition to renewable power.

With solar jumping from 2,609 GWh to 48,950 GWh in the past decade, and coal nearly
completely phased out, data from the California Energy Commission shows that the state
is on the path to achieving its goal of being carbon neutral by 2045.

Incentive Program Description

Net Yes California's net energy metering (NEM 3.0) program allows solar

 back to the grid. These credits can be used to offset energy costs
 on a customer's monthly bill. Unlike traditional net metering which
 provides full retain value for exported solar, NEM 3.0 incorporates a
 Net Billing Tariff (NBT) which reduces the value to homeowners.

Tax Yes California has a property tax exemption for active solar energy
Exemptions systems. This exemption is in the form of a new construction
 exclusion, which means that the value of the solar system is not
 added to the property's assessed value.

Tax Credits No California does not have a state-specific solar tax credit, however,
 they do have a TREC (Tradable Renewable Energy Credits) and
 California residents can claim the Federal Residential Clean Energy
 Credit (formerly known as the Investment Tax Credit, or ITC). This
 credit allows homeowners to deduct up to 30% of the cost of
 installing solar panels from their federal income taxes.
 Unfortunately, recent federal changes to the ITC mean
 homeowners only have until the end of 2025 to take advantage of
 the ITC.

Community Yes Since 2007, California has dedicated over $1 billion towards low-
Solar income and community renewable energy programs. The state’s
 low-income rooftop solar, low-income multifamily solar, and
 community solar programs have resulted in over 13,365 approved
 projects and 430 MW of solar capacity online or in process.
 However, the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC)has
 been criticized for not providing enough financial incentives for
 developers to make community solar projects economically viable.
