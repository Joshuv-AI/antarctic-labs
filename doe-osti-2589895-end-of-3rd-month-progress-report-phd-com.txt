Preliminary Long-term Cycling:

Figure 3: Subsequent Voltage vs. Time analysis of fabricated (half-cell) coin cells, currently
cycling (between 0-1.0 V window) long-term.

 ●​ Although cells are still currently cycling, a notable phenomenon is observed for the cells
 containing Si (Figure 3). In which the voltage profile does not exceed below 0V, however
 the voltage is stabilized enough to facilitate adequate alloying of Si with Li
 (demonstrated by several of these cells having reached upwards of (550+ hrs) of
 continuous cycling. However, further characterization would be needed to support this.

 -​ According to the literature, this is known as a 'Memory Effect,' silicon and its
 derivatives tend to exhibit hysteresis.[1] In which the Li-ions become 'trapped' as it
 transitions from amorphous Si to a fully lithiated crystalline structure, thus
 causing partial cycling. It's a well-known phenomenon observed in Si-anodes

 (including commercially as well). That can also be reversed upon a slow, deep
 discharge.
 -​ Further XRD characterization is needed to confirm both the amorphous nature of
 the material (before cell testing) and after to verify the crystallinity.

Current Challenges:

 ●​ Increased Si wt% causing significant brittleness, preventing from reaching established
 target wt%.
 ●​ For the (3D) structured half cells, measurements are still pending. Current work has been
 trying to improve the flow of the paste, as the old recipe currently in use is no longer
 sufficient (with increased Si/ change in more complex architecture).

Proposed Solutions:
 ●​ To effectively surpass 10 wt% Si, modification to our procedure is being explored:
 ➢​ After tape-casting the material, press the disks further (prior to thermal treatment)
 → by manually applying deformation, the goal is to increase the density of
 dislocations → further inducing slip dislocation (during heat treatment).

 -​ Tape-casted samples containing 15wt% Si, remained intact only from
 tensile compression (from measurements in Figure 1) to make cells.

 ●​ Impregnation of Si nanoparticles (to pre-printed commercial based Cu ink) → to simplify
 the printing process while increasing the Si loading more effectively.[7]

 2)​ Synthesizing New Si-Cu paste, tuning rheology - for comparison with
 Commercial-based Cu paste

 ●​ The following Figures (5-8) details the different approaches taken, to modify the
 rheology re: a new formulation.[3-5]
 ●​ Previous work conducted also utilized the standard PVDF-NMP combination, however
 the material did not remain homogenous[2]. Thus, DMSO was then used instead.
 ●​ (Figure 5- left) Although, there seems to be relatively good flow - the initial problem (at
 first) was that the paste was too thin. As the print dried, it could not be removed from the
 glass it was printed on.
 ●​ (Figure 5-right) Thus, in an attempt to ‘thicken’ the paste - the DMSO solvent used was
 limited in conjunction with Triton X-100 to help stabilize but also enhance the solubility
 of the paste. Although improvements was observed, the printed sample still could not be
 removed from the glass.

Figure 5: Details the initial formulation attempts, to thicken paste: (left) utilizes PVDF-DMSO
vs. on the (right) PVDF-DMSO-Triton.

 ●​ (Figure 6) The PVDF-DMSO-Triton formulation was then tape-casted at both 20 µm &
 200 µm and it would not delaminate from the Cu foil.
 -​ Thermal treatment was still conducted to get an idea re: any possible
 deformations. In which, there was notable deformation to the material as
 observed, disks no longer flat.

 ●​ In addition, with minimal movement of the foil, the material began flaking off the foil
 due to insufficient binding.

Figure 6: Exhibits the PVDF-DMSO-Triton formulation: after thermal treatment (left) and then
tape-casted (right).

 ●​ (Figure 7- left) demonstrates the utilization of cellulose fibers as a binder in conjunction
 with DMSO. Which did improve the flow, however once dried the material began
 cracking as it was removed from the glass.
 ●​ (Figure 7- right) Thus, DMSO was then switched out with a DI water: Triton: glycerol to
 enhance extrusion. As observed, the material was then tape-casted and began flaking
 even more.

Figure 7: Details the modifications to the recipe, to improve the binding with cellulose fibers.

 ●​ (Figure 8) The binder was then switched again to polyacrylic acid (PAA), although the
 paste was adequately’ thickened,’ insufficient binding was still observed.

Figure 8: Demonstrates the substitution of binder to polyacrylic acid (PAA).

Current Challenges:

 ●​ Insufficient binding

 -​ Difference in color was observed for commercial-based Cu paste (orange) vs. Cu
 nanopowder (dark brown) used in the new formulation.
 -​ Upon contacting the company, it was determined a thin CuO layer was applied for
 shipping. Thus, it is strongly suspected this is the direct cause of the material not
 binding effectively.

 ↓

Proposed Solutions:

 ●​ (Figure 9) Lim et al. was able to bind the dark brown CuO nanopowder by simply heating
 the material with Pluronic f127/ and DI water to create a printable paste. Once printed, it
 was then debinded in air, followed by further reduction using H2/Ar to remove the CuO
 and effectively generate the Cu nanoparticles (orange).
