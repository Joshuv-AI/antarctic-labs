Scalar-Flatness for Critical Metrics of the L 2 L^{2} -Scalar Curvature Functional in Dimensions 5 ≤ n ≤ 9 5\leq n\leq 9
Heng Zhang

Abstract
. Let ( M n , g ) (M^{n},g) be a complete Riemannian manifold of dimension n ≥ 5 n\geq 5 endowed with a critical metric of the quadratic scalar-curvature functional 𝒮 2 ​ ( g ) = ∫ M R g 2 ​ d ​ V g . \mathcal{S}^{2}(g)=\int_{M}R_{g}^{2}\,dV_{g}. For n ≥ 10 n\geq 10 , Catino, Mastrolia and Monticelli [J. Math. 1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

The variational object of this paper is the squared scalar-curvature functional

(1.1)

\mathcal{S}^{2}(g)=\int_{M}R_{g}^{2}\,dV_{g}.

We work on smooth manifolds without boundary. On a noncompact manifold, a critical metric means a critical point of (
1.1
) with respect to compactly supported variations; equivalently, it is a smooth solution of the Euler–Lagrange system displayed below. If
M
has more than one connected component, all arguments are understood componentwise.

Quadratic curvature energies have long served as a testing ground for rigidity and compactness phenomena in Riemannian geometry. For
n\geq 4
, the usual decomposition of the curvature tensor relates the
L^{2}
-norm of the full curvature tensor to the three basic quantities

\int_{M}|W_{g}|^{2}\,dV_{g},\qquad\int_{M}|\Ric_{g}|^{2}\,dV_{g},\qquad\int_{M}R_{g}^{2}\,dV_{g},

where
W_{g}
,
\Ric_{g}
and
R_{g}
denote the Weyl, Ricci and scalar curvatures. The broader variational theory of such functionals includes, for example, the work of Anderson, Catino, and Gursky–Viaclovsky; see
[
1
,
2
,
7
,
8
,
15
,
16
]
and the references therein. The functional (
1.1
) is distinguished by the fact that its critical equation closes on the scalar curvature and the Ricci tensor alone.

More precisely,
using the notation

\nabla_{g}^{2}R:=\operatorname{Hess}_{g}R,\qquad\Delta_{g}R:=\operatorname{tr}_{g}(\nabla_{g}^{2}R).

a critical metric of
\mathcal{S}^{2}
satisfies

(1.2)

2R\Ric-2\nabla^{2}R+2\Delta R\,g=\frac{1}{2}R^{2}g.

Equivalently,

(1.3)

\displaystyle R\Ric-\nabla^{2}R

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{4(n-1)}R^{2}g,

(1.4)

\displaystyle\Delta R

\displaystyle=\frac{n-4}{4(n-1)}R^{2}.

The second equation is the trace of the first; see
[
5
, Proposition 4.66]
. We use the normalization of
[
11
, (1.1)–(1.2)]
. In particular, when
n\geq 5
, the scalar curvature of a solution is subharmonic. This elementary trace feature is one of the main reasons why finite-energy assumptions are effective in the present problem.

Let us recall the rigidity picture for
\mathcal{S}^{2}
which motivates the question considered here. Catino–Mastrolia–Monticelli proved in
[
12
]
that, in low dimensions and under suitable
L^{q}
hypotheses in the noncompact setting, critical metrics of
\mathcal{S}^{2}
are forced into the scalar-flat class; dimension four is exceptional because quadratic curvature integrals are scale invariant there, and the critical equation only gives harmonicity of
R
; the corresponding noncompact Liouville result is due to Yau
[
22
]
. In dimensions
n\geq 5
, their earlier theorem gives scalar-flatness under an
L^{q}
condition with
q
below an explicit threshold
q_{*}>2
, together with a lower bound for the scalar curvature. The finite-energy problem asks whether this lower-bound assumption can be eliminated at the endpoint most natural for the functional itself, namely

R_{g}\in L^{2}(M,g).

For complete noncompact critical metrics, Catino–Mastrolia–Monticelli answered this question affirmatively in dimensions
n\geq 10
and conjectured the same conclusion for every
n\geq 5

[
11
, Theorem 1.1 and Conjecture 1.2]
.

Conjecture 1.1

(Catino–Mastrolia–Monticelli)
.

Let
(M^{n},g)
be complete and noncompact, with
n\geq 5
. If
g
is a critical metric of
\mathcal{S}^{2}
and
R_{g}\in L^{2}(M,g)
, then
R_{g}\equiv 0
. Consequently
g
is a global minimizer of
\mathcal{S}^{2}
.

The aim of this paper is to settle precisely the dimensional range not covered by the high-dimensional conformal argument of
[
11
]
.

1.2.
Main result

Our result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(M^{n},g)
,
5\leq n\leq 9
, be a complete critical metric of
\mathcal{S}^{2}
satisfying

\int_{M}R_{g}^{2}\,dV_{g}<\infty.

Then
R_{g}\equiv 0
. In particular,
(M,g)
is a global minimum of
\mathcal{S}^{2}
.

The statement includes the compact case. If
M
is compact, integrating (
1.4
) gives the conclusion immediately because
n\neq 4
. Thus all work is in the complete noncompact case.

Together with
[
11
, Theorem 1.1]
, Theorem
1.1
gives the conjectured finite-energy scalar-flatness theorem in all dimensions in which it was proposed.

Corollary 1.2
.

Conjecture
1.1
holds true.

1.3.
Idea of the proof

We describe the proof in some detail, since the point of the paper is the change of conformal normalization in dimensions
5\leq n\leq 9
. The sign reduction in
[
12
, Lemma 5.1]
, combined with the strong maximum principle applied to (
1.4
), says that a complete noncompact critical metric with
R\in L^{q}
,
1<q<\infty
, has, on each connected component, either
R\equiv 0
or
R<0
; the same reduction is recalled in
[
11
, Section 2]
. Hence it remains only to exclude the second alternative. We set

u=-R>0.

Then (
1.3
)–(
1.4
) become

(1.5)

\displaystyle\Ric_{g}

\displaystyle=\frac{\nabla^{2}_{g}u}{u}-\frac{3}{4(n-1)}ug,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}u

\displaystyle=-\frac{n-4}{4(n-1)}u^{2}.

The proof of
[
11
]
in dimensions
n\geq 10
uses the conformal metric

\widetilde{g}=u^{6/(n-4)}g.

That metric leads to a steady quasi-Einstein equation, and the sign of the resulting coefficient is compatible with known scalar-curvature nonnegativity results for steady Ricci solitons and steady quasi-Einstein metrics
[
9
,
20
]
. This provides a gradient estimate for
u
after proving completeness of
\widetilde{g}
. Conformal changes of this type also occur in the analysis of stationary vacuum metrics and in rigidity problems for stable minimal submanifolds
[
3
,
13
,
10
,
18
]
. In the dimensions considered here, however, this same conformal metric does not have the sign structure needed for that argument.

Our replacement is the simpler metric

(1.7)

h=ug.

It corresponds to the choice
k=1/2
in the conformal identity of
[
11
, Proposition 2.1]
. With

f=\frac{n-4}{2}\log u,\qquad m=\frac{(n-4)^{2}}{10-n},\qquad\lambda=\frac{n-10}{8(n-1)},

one obtains

(1.8)

\Ric_{h}+\nabla^{2}_{h}f-\frac{1}{m}df\otimes df=\lambda h.

For
5\leq n\leq 9
, the parameter
m
is positive and finite, while
\lambda<0
. Thus (
1.8
) is an expanding finite-dimensional Bakry–Emery, or quasi-Einstein, equation. This places the problem in the framework of weighted comparison geometry
[
4
,
6
,
17
,
19
,
21
]
, rather than in the scalar-curvature argument used in the high-dimensional case.

The nontrivial preliminary step is to show that
h
is complete. Assuming incompleteness, a Hopf–Rinow limiting argument produces an
h
-unit-speed geodesic

\sigma:[0,T)\to M,\qquad T<\infty,

which is minimizing on compact subintervals and has infinite length with respect to
g
. If

v=u^{-\frac{10-n}{2(n-4)}},\qquad\alpha=\frac{n-4}{10-n},

then
g=v^{2\alpha}h
, and the infinite
g
-length reads

\int_{0}^{T}v(\sigma(t))^{\alpha}\,dt=\infty.

The second variation inequality along
\sigma
, together with the quasi-Einstein equation written in the form

\Ric_{h}-mv^{-1}\nabla^{2}_{h}v=\lambda h,

yields a one-dimensional Hardy inequality for
v\circ\sigma
. We prove a Logarithmic growth lemma showing that such an inequality prevents
v\circ\sigma
from growing fast enough near
T
to make the above integral diverge. The dimension restriction enters through the elementary estimate

\alpha p_{\rho}<1,\qquad\rho=\frac{m}{n-1},

where
p_{\rho}
is determined by
\rho p_{\rho}(p_{\rho}+1)=1/4
. This inequality is valid for
5\leq n\leq 9
, and it forces completeness of
h
.

Once completeness is available, we use the weighted Laplacian

\Delta_{f}^{h}=\Delta_{h}-\langle\nabla_{h}f,\nabla_{h}\cdot\rangle_{h}

on the complete weighted manifold
(M,h,e^{-f}dV_{h})
. For

w=-\frac{10-n}{2(n-4)}\log u,

one has

\Delta_{f}^{h}w=-\lambda,\qquad\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{f}^{h}|\nabla_{h}w|_{h}^{2}=|\nabla^{2}_{h}w|_{h}^{2}+|\nabla_{h}w|_{h}^{2}\bigl(m|\nabla_{h}w|_{h}^{2}+\lambda\bigr).

A cutoff maximum principle based on finite-dimensional Bakry–Emery Laplacian comparison gives

|\nabla_{h}w|_{h}^{2}\leq-\frac{\lambda}{m}.

In the original metric this is exactly

(1.9)

|\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2(n-1)}u^{3}.

Thus
u^{-1/2}
grows at most linearly in the
g
-distance. This gives a quadratic lower bound for
u
, and combining that bound with (
1.6
) and the finite-energy assumption
u\in L^{2}(M,g)
in a standard cutoff argument forces
u\equiv 0
, contradicting the assumption
u>0
.

1.4.
Organization

Section
2
derives the expanding quasi-Einstein structure associated with
h=ug
. Section
3
proves the one-dimensional Logarithmic growth lemma. Section
4
establishes completeness of the conformal metric
h
. Section
5
proves the weighted maximum principle and the resulting gradient estimate (
1.9
). Section
6

1.1
by the final
L^{2}

2.
Reduction to an expanding quasi-Einstein structure with positive parameter

The compact case of Theorem
1.1
is immediate: integrating (
1.4
) over
M
gives

0=\frac{n-4}{4(n-1)}\int_{M}R^{2}\,dV_{g},

and hence
R\equiv 0
. Henceforth
M
is assumed noncompact. Since all arguments are componentwise, we work on one connected component at a time.

We first record the sign reduction used in the sequel.

Lemma 2.1

(Sign alternative)
.

Let
(M^{n},g)
,
n\geq 5
, be a complete noncompact critical metric of
\mathcal{S}^{2}
with
R\in L^{q}(M)
for some
1<q<\infty
. Then
R\leq 0
. Consequently, on each connected component, either
R\equiv 0
or
R<0
.

Proof.

The nonpositivity is
[
12
, Lemma 5.1]
. Since
n\geq 5
, equation (
1.4
) gives
\Delta R\geq 0
wherever
R
is evaluated as a smooth function satisfying the critical equation. Once
R\leq 0
, the strong maximum principle applied to (
1.4
) implies that either
R\equiv 0
or
R<0
on each connected component. This same reduction is recalled at
[
11
, Section 2]
.
∎

By Lemma
2.1
, either
R\equiv 0
or
R<0
on the connected component under consideration. To prove the theorem, assume for contradiction that

R<0,

and set

u=-R>0.

Then (
1.3
)–(
1.4
) become

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Ric_{g}

\displaystyle=\frac{\nabla^{2}_{g}u}{u}-\frac{3}{4(n-1)}ug,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}u

\displaystyle=-\frac{n-4}{4(n-1)}u^{2}.

Define the conformal metric

(2.3)

h=ug.

Let

(2.4)

f=\frac{n-4}{2}\log u,\qquad m=\frac{(n-4)^{2}}{10-n},\qquad\lambda=\frac{n-10}{8(n-1)}.

For
5\leq n\leq 9
one has
m>0
and
\lambda<0
.

Proposition 2.2
.

The metric
h=ug
satisfies

(2.5)

\Ric_{h}+\nabla^{2}_{h}f-\frac{1}{m}df\otimes df=\lambda h.

Equivalently, if

(2.6)

v=u^{-\frac{10-n}{2(n-4)}},

then

(2.7)

\Ric_{h}-mv^{-1}\nabla^{2}_{h}v=\lambda h.

Moreover,

(2.8)

e^{-f}\,dV_{h}=u^{2}\,dV_{g}.

Proof.

We use the conformal formula
[
11
, Proposition 2.1]
. In the notation of that proposition, for any
k\neq 0
with
(n-2)k\neq 1
, the conformal metric

\widetilde{g}=u^{2k}g,\qquad F=[(n-2)k-1]\log u,

satisfies

\displaystyle\Ric_{\widetilde{g}}+\nabla^{2}_{\widetilde{g}}F

\displaystyle-\frac{1+2k-(n-2)k^{2}}{[(n-2)k-1]^{2}}\,dF\otimes dF

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-4)k-3}{4(n-1)}\exp\!\left(\frac{1-2k}{(n-2)k-1}F\right)\widetilde{g}.

Taking
k=1/2
gives
\widetilde{g}=h
,
F=f
, and the exponential factor is equal to
1
. Moreover,

\frac{1+2k-(n-2)k^{2}}{[(n-2)k-1]^{2}}=\frac{10-n}{(n-4)^{2}}=\frac{1}{m},

while

\frac{(n-4)k-3}{4(n-1)}=\frac{n-10}{8(n-1)}=\lambda.

This proves (
2.5
). Since
f=-m\log v
, we have

\nabla^{2}_{h}f-\frac{1}{m}df\otimes df=-m\left(\nabla^{2}_{h}\log v+d\log v\otimes d\log v\right)=-mv^{-1}\nabla^{2}_{h}v,

which gives (
2.7
). Finally, because
h=ug
,

dV_{h}=u^{n/2}dV_{g},

and hence

e^{-f}dV_{h}=u^{-(n-4)/2}u^{n/2}dV_{g}=u^{2}dV_{g}.

∎

It will be useful to introduce also

(2.9)

w=\log v=-\frac{10-n}{2(n-4)}\log u.

Then
f=-mw
, and the weighted Laplacian associated with
(h,e^{-f}dV_{h})
is

(2.10)

\Delta_{f}^{h}\psi=\Delta_{h}\psi-\left\langle\nabla_{h}f,\nabla_{h}\psi\right\rangle_{h}=\Delta_{h}\psi+m\left\langle\nabla_{h}w,\nabla_{h}\psi\right\rangle_{h}.

Lemma 2.3
.

The function
w
satisfies

(2.11)

\Delta_{f}^{h}w=-\lambda.

Consequently, if

G=\left|\nabla_{h}w\right|_{h}^{2},

then

(2.12)

\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{f}^{h}G=\left|\nabla^{2}_{h}w\right|_{h}^{2}+G(mG+\lambda).

Proof.

Let

a_{n}=\frac{n-4}{4(n-1)},\qquad\beta=\frac{10-n}{2(n-4)},

so that
w=-\beta\log u
and
\Delta_{g}u=-a_{n}u^{2}
. Since
h=ug=e^{2\varphi}g
with
\varphi=\frac{1}{2}\log u
, the Laplacian transforms as

\Delta_{h}\psi=u^{-1}\left(\Delta_{g}\psi+\frac{n-2}{2}\left\langle\nabla_{g}\log u,\nabla_{g}\psi\right\rangle_{g}\right).

Applying this to
w=-\beta\log u
gives

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}w

\displaystyle=-\beta u^{-1}\left(\Delta_{g}\log u+\frac{n-2}{2}\left|\nabla_{g}\log u\right|_{g}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\beta a_{n}-\frac{10-n}{4}u^{-1}\left|\nabla_{g}\log u\right|_{g}^{2}.

On the other hand,

m\left|\nabla_{h}w\right|_{h}^{2}=m\beta^{2}u^{-1}\left|\nabla_{g}\log u\right|_{g}^{2}=\frac{10-n}{4}u^{-1}\left|\nabla_{g}\log u\right|_{g}^{2}.

Therefore

\Delta_{f}^{h}w=\Delta_{h}w+m\left|\nabla_{h}w\right|_{h}^{2}=\beta a_{n}=\frac{10-n}{8(n-1)}=-\lambda.

For (
2.12
), use the weighted Bochner formula

\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{f}^{h}G=\left|\nabla^{2}_{h}w\right|_{h}^{2}+\bigl(\Ric_{h}+\nabla^{2}_{h}f\bigr)(\nabla_{h}w,\nabla_{h}w)+\left\langle\nabla_{h}w,\nabla_{h}\Delta_{f}^{h}w\right\rangle_{h}.

Since (
2.11
) is constant, the last term vanishes. From (
2.5
) and
df=-m\,dw
,

\Ric_{h}+\nabla^{2}_{h}f=\lambda h+\frac{1}{m}df\otimes df=\lambda h+m\,dw\otimes dw.

Thus

\bigl(\Ric_{h}+\nabla^{2}_{h}f\bigr)(\nabla_{h}w,\nabla_{h}w)=\lambda G+mG^{2},

which proves (
2.12
).
∎

3.
A Logarithmic growth lemma

The next elementary lemma is useful in the completeness argument.

Lemma 3.1

(Logarithmic growth lemma)
.

Let
v>0
be a
C^{2}
function on
(0,T)
, where
0<T<\infty
. Assume that there exist constants
\rho>0
and
b\geq 0
such that

(3.1)

\rho\int_{0}^{T}\frac{v^{\prime\prime}}{v}\phi^{2}\,dt\leq\int_{0}^{T}(\phi^{\prime})^{2}\,dt+b\int_{0}^{T}\phi^{2}\,dt

for every
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,T)
. Set

(3.2)

p_{\rho}=\frac{\sqrt{1+\rho^{-1}}-1}{2},

so that
\rho p_{\rho}(p_{\rho}+1)=1/4
. Then

(3.3)

\limsup_{t\to T^{-}}\frac{\log v(t)}{\log\frac{1}{T-t}}\leq p_{\rho}.

Consequently, for every
a>0
such that
ap_{\rho}<1
, one has

(3.4)

\int^{T}v(t)^{a}\,dt<\infty.

Proof.

Put
x=T-t
and
V(x)=v(T-x)
. Let

y=(\log V)^{\prime}.

Then
v^{\prime\prime}(t)/v(t)=V^{\prime\prime}(x)/V(x)=y^{\prime}+y^{2}
. Rewriting (
3.1
) in the
x
variable gives

\rho\int_{0}^{T}(y^{\prime}+y^{2})\phi^{2}\,dx\leq\int_{0}^{T}(\phi^{\prime})^{2}\,dx+b\int_{0}^{T}\phi^{2}\,dx.

For compactly supported smooth
\phi
,

(3.5)

\int(y^{\prime}+y^{2})\phi^{2}=\int(y\phi-\phi^{\prime})^{2}-\int(\phi^{\prime})^{2}.

Hence

(3.6)

\rho\int_{0}^{T}(y\phi-\phi^{\prime})^{2}\,dx\leq(1+\rho)\int_{0}^{T}(\phi^{\prime})^{2}\,dx+b\int_{0}^{T}\phi^{2}\,dx.

Fix
R\in(0,T)
sufficiently small. Given
0<r<R/8
, choose a smooth cutoff
\chi_{r,R}
such that

\begin{array}[]{ll}\chi_{r,R}=1&\text{on }[2r,R/2],\\
\chi_{r,R}=0&\text{near }0\text{ and near }R,\end{array}

with

|\chi_{r,R}^{\prime}|\leq C/r\quad\text{on }[r,2r],\qquad|\chi_{r,R}^{\prime}|\leq C/R\quad\text{on }[R/2,R],

and analogous second-derivative bounds. The function

\phi(x)=x^{1/2}\chi_{r,R}(x)

is smooth and compactly supported in
(0,T)
, because
\chi_{r,R}
is identically zero near
x=0
. Equivalently, one may start from the ideal piecewise-smooth cutoff and approximate it in
H_{0}^{1}(0,T)
. Substitution in (
3.6
) yields

\int_{0}^{T}(\phi^{\prime})^{2}\,dx=\frac{1}{4}\log\frac{R}{r}+O(1),\qquad\int_{0}^{T}\phi^{2}\,dx=O(R^{2}),

where
R
is fixed and the constants in
O(1)
are independent of
r
. Since
R
is fixed, the term
bO(R^{2})
is also independent of
r
and disappears after division by
\log(1/r)
and passage to the limit
r\to 0^{+}
. On
[2r,R/2]
,

y\phi-\phi^{\prime}=x^{1/2}\left(y-\frac{1}{2x}\right).

Therefore

(3.7)

\int_{2r}^{R/2}x\left(y-\frac{1}{2x}\right)^{2}\,dx\leq\frac{1+\rho}{4\rho}\log\frac{R}{r}+O(1).

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\displaystyle\log V(2r)-\log V(R/2)+\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{R/2}{2r}

\displaystyle\qquad=-\int_{2r}^{R/2}\left(y-\frac{1}{2x}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left|\int_{2r}^{R/2}\left(y-\frac{1}{2x}\right)\,dx\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left(\int_{2r}^{R/2}x\left(y-\frac{1}{2x}\right)^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{2r}^{R/2}\frac{dx}{x}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sqrt{\frac{1+\rho}{4\rho}}\log\frac{R/2}{2r}+o\!\left(\log\frac{1}{r}\right)

as
r\to 0^{+}
. Dividing by
\log(1/r)
and letting
r\to 0^{+}
first gives

\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\log V(2r)}{\log(1/r)}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1+\rho}{4\rho}}-\frac{1}{2}=p_{\rho}.

Replacing
2r
by
s
does not change the limsup, since

\log(1/r)=\log(1/s)+\log 2
and hence

\log(1/r)/\log(1/s)\to 1
as
s\to 0^{+}
. Therefore

\limsup_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{\log V(s)}{\log(1/s)}\leq p_{\rho}.

This is (
3.3
). If
a>0
and
ap_{\rho}<1
, choose
\varepsilon>0
with
a(p_{\rho}+\varepsilon)<1
. From (
3.3
), after decreasing the terminal interval if necessary, one has

v(t)\leq C_{\varepsilon}(T-t)^{-p_{\rho}-\varepsilon}\qquad\text{for }t\text{ sufficiently close to }T.

Thus

\int^{T}v(t)^{a}\,dt\leq C\int^{T}(T-t)^{-a(p_{\rho}+\varepsilon)}\,dt<\infty.

∎

4.
Completeness of the conformal metric
h=ug

We now prove that the conformal metric
h=ug
is complete. We first record a standard limiting-geodesic lemma in a form adapted to the present situation.

Lemma 4.1

(Finite conformal geodesic with infinite background length)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a connected complete Riemannian manifold, let
a\in C^{\infty}(M)
be positive, and set
h=ag
. If
h
is incomplete, then there exist
0<T<\infty
and an
h
-unit-speed geodesic

\sigma:[0,T)\to M

such that:

(i)

every compact subinterval of
[0,T)
is minimizing for
h
;

(ii)

the
g
-length of
\sigma
is infinite, namely

\int_{0}^{T}|\dot{\sigma}(t)|_{g}\,dt=\infty.

Consequently, for every
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,T)
,

(4.1)

\int_{0}^{T}\left[(n-1)(\phi^{\prime})^{2}-\Ric_{h}(\dot{\sigma},\dot{\sigma})\phi^{2}\right]dt\geq 0.

Proof.

Since
h
is incomplete, the Hopf–Rinow theorem (equivalently the equivalence between metric completeness and geodesic completeness in finite-dimensional Riemannian geometry), gives a maximal
h
-unit-speed geodesic
\zeta:[0,T_{*})\to M
with
T_{*}<\infty
. If
\zeta
were contained in a compact subset of
M
, then
(\zeta,\dot{\zeta})
would remain in a compact subset of
TM
, because
|\dot{\zeta}|_{h}\equiv 1
; the smooth geodesic equation for
h
would then extend it past
T_{*}
by the standard ODE extension theorem, a contradiction. Thus
\zeta
leaves every compact subset of
M
. Since
M
is connected, after joining the initial point of
\zeta
to a fixed reference point
o\in M
, there is a finite
h
-length curve starting at
o
and leaving every compact subset of
M
.

Let
B_{r}=B_{r}^{g}(o)
. Since
(M,g)
is complete, Hopf–Rinow implies that the closed
g
-balls
\overline{B_{r}}
, and hence the distance spheres
\partial B_{r}
, are compact. Set

D(r)=d_{h}(o,\partial B_{r}).

The preceding escaping curve has finite
h
-length, say at most
L_{0}
. Since its
g
-distance from
o
is unbounded, for every sufficiently large
r
it meets
\partial B_{r}
by continuity of
x\mapsto d_{g}(o,x)
. Hence

(4.2)

D(r)\leq L_{0}

for all sufficiently large
r
. Choose a sequence
r_{i}\to\infty
for which (
4.2
) holds.

For each
i
, choose
\eta_{i}\in C^{\infty}(M)
satisfying

\eta_{i}\geq 0,\qquad\eta_{i}\equiv 0\ \text{on }\overline{B_{r_{i}}},\qquad\eta_{i}\equiv 1\ \text{on }M\setminus B_{r_{i}+1}.

Such a function exists by the smooth Urysohn lemma, since the closed sets
\overline{B_{r_{i}}}
and
M\setminus B_{r_{i}+1}
are disjoint. Put

h_{i}=(a+\eta_{i})g.

The metric
h_{i}
is complete. Indeed, on the compact set
\overline{B_{r_{i}+1}}
the positive function
a+\eta_{i}
has a positive minimum
c_{i}>0
, while on
M\setminus B_{r_{i}+1}
one has
a+\eta_{i}\geq 1
. Thus
h_{i}\geq c_{i}^{\prime}g
on all of
M
, where
c_{i}^{\prime}=\min\{c_{i},1\}>0
. Since
g
is complete, this lower uniform comparison implies completeness of
h_{i}
.

We first compare the
h_{i}
- and
h
-distances from
o
to
\partial B_{r_{i}}
. Any curve from
o
to
\partial B_{r_{i}}
has an initial segment, ending at its first intersection with
\partial B_{r_{i}}
, contained in
\overline{B_{r_{i}}}
, where
h_{i}=h
. It follows that

(4.3)

d_{h_{i}}(o,\partial B_{r_{i}})=d_{h}(o,\partial B_{r_{i}})=D(r_{i}).

Indeed, the first-hit argument gives
d_{h_{i}}(o,\partial B_{r_{i}})\geq D(r_{i})
; the reverse inequality follows by approximating
D(r_{i})
by curves whose first boundary-hit segment is contained in
\overline{B_{r_{i}}}
.

Since
h_{i}
is complete and
\partial B_{r_{i}}
is compact, the distance from
o
to
\partial B_{r_{i}}
is attained; another application of Hopf–Rinow gives a minimizing
h_{i}
-geodesic. Hence there exist a point
x_{i}\in\partial B_{r_{i}}
and an
h_{i}
-minimizing geodesic
\gamma_{i}
from
o
to
x_{i}
such that

L_{h_{i}}(\gamma_{i})=d_{h_{i}}(o,\partial B_{r_{i}})=D(r_{i})\leq L_{0}.

The curve
\gamma_{i}
is contained in
\overline{B_{r_{i}}}
. Otherwise, if
y
were the first point at which
\gamma_{i}
hits
\partial B_{r_{i}}
before the endpoint
x_{i}
, then the initial segment from
o
to
y
would have
h_{i}
-length strictly smaller than
d_{h_{i}}(o,\partial B_{r_{i}})
, contradicting the definition of the latter. Hence
h_{i}=h
along
\gamma_{i}
.

We next prove that each initial segment of
\gamma_{i}
is minimizing for
h
. Let
p
be a point of
\gamma_{i}
. Since
\gamma_{i}
is globally minimizing for
h_{i}
, the initial segment
\gamma_{i}|_{o,p}
is also globally minimizing for
h_{i}
; otherwise a shorter
h_{i}
-curve from
o
to
p
would shorten the whole curve from
o
to
x_{i}
. Suppose that there were an
h
-curve
\alpha
from
o
to
p
with

L_{h}(\alpha)<L_{h}(\gamma_{i}|_{o,p}).

If
\alpha
remains in
\overline{B_{r_{i}}}
, then
L_{h_{i}}(\alpha)=L_{h}(\alpha)
, contradicting the
h_{i}
-minimality of
\gamma_{i}|_{o,p}
. If
\alpha
leaves
B_{r_{i}}
before reaching
p
, let
y
be its first intersection with
\partial B_{r_{i}}
. Since
\alpha|_{o,y}
lies in
\overline{B_{r_{i}}}
,

D(r_{i})\leq L_{h}(\alpha|_{o,y})<L_{h}(\alpha)<L_{h}(\gamma_{i}|_{o,p})\leq D(r_{i}),

again a contradiction. The remaining possibility, when the first intersection with
\partial B_{r_{i}}
is exactly the endpoint
p=x_{i}
, reduces to the first case because the curve up to its endpoint lies in
\overline{B_{r_{i}}}
. Therefore every initial segment of
\gamma_{i}
is
h
-minimizing. Consequently every subsegment of
\gamma_{i}
is also
h
-minimizing, because a shorter replacement of a subsegment would shorten a minimizing initial segment.

Parametrize
\gamma_{i}
by
g
-arclength
s\in[0,S_{i}]
. Since
x_{i}\in\partial B_{r_{i}}
, one has
S_{i}\geq r_{i}\to\infty
. For every fixed
S>0
, the images
\gamma_{i}([0,S])
lie in the compact
g
-ball
\overline{B_{S}}
, and for
i
sufficiently large this compact ball is contained in
B_{r_{i}}
; hence on
[0,S]
the curves are unparametrized
h
-geodesics.

Let
\varphi=(1/2)\log a
, so that
h=e^{2\varphi}g
. An unparametrized
h
-geodesic written in
g
-unit-speed parameter satisfies the smooth pregeodesic equation

(4.4)

\nabla^{g}_{s}\dot{\gamma}_{i}=\nabla^{g}\varphi-\left\langle\nabla^{g}\varphi,\dot{\gamma}_{i}\right\rangle_{g}\dot{\gamma}_{i}.

On each compact ball
\overline{B_{S}}
the right-hand side has uniformly bounded smooth coefficients. Passing to a subsequence of the initial velocities and using ODE compactness, then diagonalizing in
S
, we obtain smooth convergence on compact
s
-intervals to a
g
-unit-speed curve

\gamma:[0,\infty)\to M.

Equation (
4.4
) passes to the limit, so
\gamma
is an unparametrized
h
-geodesic.

The limit is locally minimizing for
h
. Indeed, suppose that a limit subsegment
\gamma|_{[s_{1},s_{2}]}
admitted an
h
-curve
\alpha
from
\gamma(s_{1})
to
\gamma(s_{2})
such that

L_{h}(\alpha)\leq L_{h}(\gamma|_{[s_{1},s_{2}]})-\delta

for some
\delta>0
. By the smooth convergence
\gamma_{i}(s_{j})\to\gamma(s_{j})
,
j=1,2
, we may join
\gamma_{i}(s_{1})
to
\gamma(s_{1})
and
\gamma(s_{2})
to
\gamma_{i}(s_{2})
inside small common
h
-normal balls by local
h
-geodesics with total length less than
\delta/3
. For
i
large, also

\left|L_{h}(\gamma_{i}|_{[s_{1},s_{2}]})-L_{h}(\gamma|_{[s_{1},s_{2}]})\right|<\delta/3.

Concatenating the two short endpoint connectors with
\alpha
gives an
h
-curve from
\gamma_{i}(s_{1})
to
\gamma_{i}(s_{2})
shorter than
\gamma_{i}|_{[s_{1},s_{2}]}
, contradicting the
h
-minimality of that subsegment.

Moreover, for every
S>0
,

L_{h}(\gamma|_{[0,S]})=\lim_{i\to\infty}L_{h}(\gamma_{i}|_{[0,S]})\leq\limsup_{i\to\infty}L_{h}(\gamma_{i})\leq L_{0}.

Thus
\gamma
has finite total
h
-length. Define

t(s)=L_{h}(\gamma|_{[0,s]})=\int_{0}^{s}\sqrt{a(\gamma(\tau))}\,d\tau,\qquad T=\lim_{s\to\infty}t(s)<\infty.

Since
a>0
, the function
t(s)
is strictly increasing. Reparametrize
\gamma
by
h
-arclength and denote the resulting curve by
\sigma:[0,T)\to M
. Then
\sigma
is an
h
-unit-speed geodesic and every compact subinterval is
h
-minimizing. Since
t(s)\to T
as
s\to\infty
, approaching the terminal time
T
in the
h
-arclength parameter is equivalent to letting the original
g
-arclength parameter
s
tend to infinity. Hence the
g
-length of
\sigma
is infinite.

The index inequality (
4.1
) follows from the second variation formula on compact minimizing subsegments of
\sigma
. More precisely, for a test function
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,T)
, choose a compact interval containing its support. On this interval the segment is minimizing; applying the index form to the
n-1
variations
\phi E_{j}
, where
E_{j}
are
h
-parallel orthonormal fields perpendicular to
\dot{\sigma}
, and summing in
j
gives (
4.1
).
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
5\leq n\leq 9
and let
u>0
solve (
2.1
)–(
2.2
) on a complete manifold
(M,g)
. Then the conformal metric

h=ug

is complete.

Proof.

It suffices to argue on a connected component, since completeness is a componentwise property. Assume by contradiction that
h
is incomplete on such a component. Apply Lemma
4.1
with
a=u
. We obtain an
h
-unit-speed locally minimizing geodesic

\sigma:[0,T)\to M,\qquad T<\infty,

whose
g
-length is infinite.

Let
v
be as in (
2.6
), and set

(4.5)

\alpha=\frac{n-4}{10-n}>0.

Since
h=ug
and
v=u^{-(10-n)/(2(n-4))}
,

g=u^{-1}h=v^{2\alpha}h.

Because
\sigma
has unit
h
-speed,

(4.6)

\infty=L_{g}(\sigma)=\int_{0}^{T}v(\sigma(t))^{\alpha}\,dt.

On the other hand, equation (
2.7
) gives, along
\sigma
,

\Ric_{h}(\dot{\sigma},\dot{\sigma})=m\frac{(v\circ\sigma)^{\prime\prime}}{v\circ\sigma}+\lambda.

Substituting this into the index inequality (
4.1
) yields

(4.7)

\frac{m}{n-1}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{(v\circ\sigma)^{\prime\prime}}{v\circ\sigma}\phi^{2}\,dt\leq\int_{0}^{T}(\phi^{\prime})^{2}\,dt-\frac{\lambda}{n-1}\int_{0}^{T}\phi^{2}\,dt

for every
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,T)
. Thus Lemma
3.1
applies with

(4.8)

\rho=\frac{m}{n-1}=\frac{(n-4)^{2}}{(10-n)(n-1)},\qquad b=-\frac{\lambda}{n-1}\geq 0.

Let
p_{\rho}
be given by (
3.2
). We claim that

(4.9)

\alpha p_{\rho}<1.

Indeed, since
p_{\rho}
is the positive root of
\rho p(p+1)=1/4
, it is enough to check that

\rho\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}+1\right)>\frac{1}{4}.

Using (
4.5
) and (
4.8
),

\rho\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}+1\right)=\frac{(n-4)^{2}}{(10-n)(n-1)}\cdot\frac{10-n}{n-4}\cdot\frac{6}{n-4}=\frac{6}{n-1}>\frac{1}{4}

for every
5\leq n\leq 9
. Hence (
4.9
) holds. Lemma
3.1
gives integrability on a terminal interval: for some
\varepsilon>0
,

\int_{T-\varepsilon}^{T}v(\sigma(t))^{\alpha}\,dt<\infty.

On the remaining compact interval
[0,T-\varepsilon]
, the function
v\circ\sigma
is smooth and positive, hence bounded above. Therefore

\int_{0}^{T}v(\sigma(t))^{\alpha}\,dt<\infty,

contradicting (
4.6
). Therefore
h
is complete.
∎

5.
A weighted maximum principle and the gradient estimate

We next use the complete quasi-Einstein structure to prove a gradient estimate for
u
.

Lemma 5.1

(Weighted cutoff maximum principle)
.

Let
(N^{n},h)
be complete, let
f\in C^{\infty}(N)
, and suppose that, for some
m>0
and
\lambda<0
,

(5.1)

\Ric_{h}+\nabla^{2}_{h}f-\frac{1}{m}df\otimes df=\lambda h.

Let
G\geq 0
be smooth and satisfy

(5.2)

\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{f}G\geq G(mG+\lambda),\qquad\Delta_{f}=\Delta_{h}-\left\langle\nabla_{h}f,\nabla_{h}\cdot\right\rangle_{h}.

Then

(5.3)

G\leq-\frac{\lambda}{m}\quad\text{on }N.

Proof.

Equation (
5.1
) says that the
m
-Bakry–Emery Ricci tensor satisfies

\Ric_{f}^{m}:=\Ric_{h}+\nabla^{2}_{h}f-\frac{1}{m}df\otimes df=\lambda h.

Put
N_{0}=n+m
. Since
m>0
, this is a finite synthetic dimension with
N_{0}>n
. The finite-dimensional Bakry–Emery Laplacian comparison theorem for
\Ric_{f}^{m}
with synthetic dimension
N_{0}
gives, away from the cut locus of a fixed point
o
,

(5.4)

\Delta_{f}r\leq(N_{0}-1)c\coth(cr),\qquad c=\sqrt{\frac{-\lambda}{N_{0}-1}},

where
r=d_{h}(o,\cdot)
. This finite-dimensional comparison goes back to Qian
[
19
]
; the formulation used here is, for example, the
N
-Bakry–Emery mean-curvature comparison stated in
[
21
, Theorem A.1]
. In particular,
\Delta_{f}r\leq C_{0}
on
\{r\geq 1\}
in the barrier sense.

Choose a nonincreasing smooth function
\eta:[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
such that

\eta\equiv 1\text{ on }[0,1],\qquad\eta\equiv 0\text{ on }[2,\infty),

with

|\eta^{\prime}|^{2}\leq C\eta,\qquad|\eta^{\prime\prime}|\leq C.

For
R\geq 2
, set

\eta_{R}(x)=\eta(r(x)/R),\qquad H_{R}=\eta_{R}G.

Since
(N,h)
is complete, the closed ball
\overline{B_{2R}(o)}
is compact by Hopf–Rinow. The function
H_{R}
is supported in this ball, hence it attains a maximum at some point
x_{R}
. If
H_{R}(x_{R})=0
, then
G=0
on
B_{R}(o)
and there is nothing to prove. Otherwise
x_{R}
lies where
G>0
and
\eta_{R}>0
.

The standard Calabi trick handles the possible cut-locus issue. More explicitly, if
x_{R}
lies in the cut locus of
o
, one replaces
r
near
x_{R}
by the smooth upper barrier

r_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon+d_{h}(\gamma(\varepsilon),x),

where
\gamma
is a minimizing geodesic from
o
to
x_{R}
. Then
r\leq r_{\varepsilon}
near
x_{R}
and equality holds at
x_{R}
. Since
\eta
is nonincreasing,
\eta(r_{\varepsilon}/R)G\leq\eta(r/R)G
near
x_{R}
, with equality at
x_{R}
; hence
x_{R}
is still a local maximum for the barrier version. Applying the following argument to
\eta(r_{\varepsilon}/R)G
and letting
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, we may compute as if
r
were smooth at
x_{R}
. At
x_{R}
,

\nabla H_{R}=0,\qquad\Delta_{f}H_{R}\leq 0.

Using (
5.2
),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq\Delta_{f}(\eta_{R}G)

\displaystyle=\eta_{R}\Delta_{f}G+G\Delta_{f}\eta_{R}+2\left\langle\nabla\eta_{R},\nabla G\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq 2\eta_{R}G(mG+\lambda)+G\Delta_{f}\eta_{R}-2G\frac{\left|\nabla\eta_{R}\right|^{2}}{\eta_{R}}.

Dividing by
G>0
and writing
H_{R}=\eta_{R}G
at
x_{R}
, we obtain

(5.5)

2mH_{R}\leq-2\lambda\eta_{R}-\Delta_{f}\eta_{R}+2\frac{\left|\nabla\eta_{R}\right|^{2}}{\eta_{R}}.

If
x_{R}\in B_{R}(o)
, then
\eta_{R}\equiv 1
in a neighbourhood of
x_{R}
and the two cutoff-error terms in (
5.5
) vanish; hence

H_{R}(x_{R})\leq-\frac{\lambda}{m}.

If
x_{R}
lies in the transition region
R\leq r(x_{R})\leq 2R
, then (
5.4
) and
\eta^{\prime}\leq 0
give

-\Delta_{f}\eta_{R}=-\frac{\eta^{\prime}(r/R)}{R}\Delta_{f}r-\frac{\eta^{\prime\prime}(r/R)}{R^{2}}\leq\frac{C}{R}+\frac{C}{R^{2}},\qquad\frac{\left|\nabla\eta_{R}\right|^{2}}{\eta_{R}}\leq\frac{C}{R^{2}}.

Therefore, in all cases,

H_{R}(x_{R})\leq-\frac{\lambda}{m}+O(R^{-1}).

Since
\eta_{R}\equiv 1
on
B_{R}(o)
,

\sup_{B_{R}(o)}G\leq\sup_{N}H_{R}\leq-\frac{\lambda}{m}+O(R^{-1}).

Letting
R\to\infty
proves (
5.3
).
∎

Proposition 5.2
.

For
5\leq n\leq 9
, the function
u=-R>0
satisfies

(5.6)

\left|\nabla_{g}u\right|_{g}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2(n-1)}u^{3}.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.2
,
h=ug
is complete. Apply Lemma
5.1
to the complete weighted manifold
(M,h,e^{-f}dV_{h})
and to

G=\left|\nabla_{h}w\right|_{h}^{2}.

The required differential inequality follows from (
2.12
). Hence

G\leq-\frac{\lambda}{m}=\frac{(10-n)^{2}}{8(n-1)(n-4)^{2}}.

Since

w=-\frac{10-n}{2(n-4)}\log u

and
h=ug
,

G=\left(\frac{10-n}{2(n-4)}\right)^{2}\frac{\left|\nabla_{g}u\right|_{g}^{2}}{u^{3}}.

Dividing by the prefactor gives (
5.6
).
∎

6.
Completion of the proof of the main theorem

We now finish the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Recall that we are arguing by contradiction under the assumption
u=-R>0
on the connected component under consideration.

From (
5.6
),

(6.1)

\left|\nabla_{g}(u^{-1/2})\right|_{g}=\frac{1}{2}u^{-3/2}\left|\nabla_{g}u\right|_{g}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2(n-1)}}.

Fix
O\in M
. Integrating (
6.1
) along minimizing
g
-geodesics gives constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

(6.2)

u(x)\geq\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}+d_{g}(x,O)^{2}}\qquad\text{for all }x\in M.

Let
\eta_{s}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
be a standard cutoff satisfying

0\leq\eta_{s}\leq 1,\qquad\eta_{s}\equiv 1\text{ on }B_{s}^{g}(O),\qquad\eta_{s}\equiv 0\text{ on }M\setminus B_{2s}^{g}(O),

and

\left|\nabla_{g}\eta_{s}\right|_{g}\leq\frac{C}{s}.

Such cutoffs may be obtained by taking the Lipschitz distance cutoff and smoothing it with only a harmless change in the gradient bound; see, for example, Greene–Wu
[
14
]
. Alternatively, the following integration by parts can be justified directly in the weak sense with the Lipschitz cutoff. Using (
2.2
), integration by parts gives

\displaystyle\frac{n-4}{4(n-1)}\int_{M}u^{2}\eta_{s}^{2}\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle=-\int_{M}(\Delta_{g}u)\eta_{s}^{2}\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle=2\int_{M}\eta_{s}\left\langle\nabla_{g}u,\nabla_{g}\eta_{s}\right\rangle_{g}\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{M}\eta_{s}\left|\nabla_{g}u\right|_{g}\left|\nabla_{g}\eta_{s}\right|_{g}\,dV_{g}.

Therefore, by (
5.6
),

(6.3)

\frac{n-4}{4(n-1)}\int_{M}u^{2}\eta_{s}^{2}\,dV_{g}\leq\frac{C}{s}\int_{B_{2s}(O)\setminus B_{s}(O)}u^{3/2}\,dV_{g}.

On the annulus
B_{2s}(O)\setminus B_{s}(O)
, the lower bound (
6.2
) implies

u^{-1/2}\leq C(1+s).

Hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}\int_{B_{2s}\setminus B_{s}}u^{3/2}\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\int_{B_{2s}\setminus B_{s}}u^{2}u^{-1/2}\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{B_{2s}\setminus B_{s}}u^{2}\,dV_{g}.

Since
u\in L^{2}(M,g)
, the right-hand side tends to zero as
s\to\infty
. Therefore

(6.4)

\int_{M}u^{2}\eta_{s}^{2}\,dV_{g}\to 0.

For any fixed
R>0
, taking
s>R
gives

\int_{B_{R}(O)}u^{2}\,dV_{g}\leq\int_{M}u^{2}\eta_{s}^{2}\,dV_{g}.

Letting
s\to\infty
and using (
6.4
), we obtain

\int_{B_{R}(O)}u^{2}\,dV_{g}=0.

Since the radius
R>0
is arbitrary,
u\equiv 0
on the component, contradicting
u>0
. Thus the negative scalar-curvature alternative is impossible on every connected component. By Lemma
2.1
, the only remaining possibility is

R\equiv 0.

This proves Theorem
1.1
.

Remark 6.1
.

The proof uses finite energy only through the sign alternative and the final
L^{2}
tail estimate. The conformal metric
h=ug
is tailored to the low-dimensional range: for
5\leq n\leq 9
, it produces a positive finite Bakry–Emery parameter
m
in the expanding quasi-Einstein equation. This is opposite to the conformal metric
u^{6/(n-4)}g
used in the high-dimensional argument of
[
11
]
, which is effective for
n\geq 10
by
[
11
, Theorem 1.1]
but does not have the same sign features in dimensions
5\leq n\leq 9
.
