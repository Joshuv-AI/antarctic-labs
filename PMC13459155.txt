Attenuation of Tau Hyperphosphorylation by Chronic Toxoplasma gondii Infection in a Mouse Model of Alzheimer's Disease
Authors: Seung‐Hwan Seo, Ji‐Eun Lee, Eun‐Ji Cho, Eun‐Hee Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlzheimer's disease (AD) is characterized by amyloid‐β (Aβ) peptide accumulation and tau protein‐mediated neurodegeneration, and neuroinflammation is increasingly recognized as a major process associated with tau pathology. Chronic Toxoplasma gondii infection reduces amyloid accumulation in AD models through immune modulation, but its stage‐specific associations with tau‐related pathology remain unclear. Here, we investigated how chronic T. gondii infection is associated with tau‐related molecular and neuropathological changes in the 5XFAD AD mouse model. At 40 weeks post‐infection, we profiled transcriptomic changes and evaluated Aβ‐associated (21 genes), Aβ/tau‐shared (22 genes), and tau‐associated (21 genes) pathological programs together with p‐tau immunoreactivity in brain sections. Chronic infection was associated with selective modulation of molecular networks linked to neuroimmune signalling and tau‐related pathways rather than broad suppression of AD‐related gene expression. Among tau‐related pathological stages, the neuroinflammation‐related amplification module linking Aβ and tau exhibited the most prominent transcriptomic change in T. gondii‐infected AD mice, whereas upstream kinase‐related initiation pathways and downstream toxin conversion or accumulation‐related processes changed relatively minimally. These transcriptomic changes were accompanied by a marked reduction in phosphorylated tau load at the tissue level. Collectively, these findings suggest that chronic T. gondii infection is associated with selective modulation of tau‐related pathogenic programs and reduced p‐tau burden in the 5XFAD brain.Keywords: 5XFAD mouse model, Alzheimer's disease, immune modulation, neuroinflammation, tau pathology, Toxoplasma gondii, transcriptomics

Introduction

AD is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the extracellular accumulation of amyloid‐β (Aβ) plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau proteins. While Aβ pathology is widely recognized as an early event in AD, tau pathology is more closely associated with synaptic dysfunction, neuronal loss, and clinical disease progression, highlighting the importance of mechanisms linking amyloid accumulation and tau‐mediated neurodegeneration [1, 2, 3].

Accumulating evidence indicates neuroinflammation as a key mechanistic link between Aβ aggregation and tau pathology. Aβ accretion induces microglial activation and phagocytosis, leading to the production of pro‐inflammatory mediators that promote tau phosphorylation, aggregation, and propagation [4, 5, 6]. Consistent with this perspective, experimental and clinical studies have demonstrated that microglial activation powerfully influences the severity and spread of tau pathology, rather than amyloid burden alone [7, 8].

Toxoplasma gondii is an intracellular protozoan parasite that can cause a lifelong chronic infection of the central nervous system and has emerged as a powerful regulator of host immune responses. In experimental AD models, chronic T. gondii infection has been associated with reduced Aβ plaque burden, altered microglial distribution, and increased plaque‐associated phagocytic activity [9, 10, 11, 12]. These observations have been reported across multiple studies and mouse models, including Tg2576, 5xFAD, and additional hAPP‐based models, supporting the view that the relationship between chronic T. gondii infection and reduced amyloid pathology is reproducible across different experimental settings [10, 11, 12, 13].

The 5XFAD model is widely used to study amyloid‐driven AD pathology and is most robust in recapitulating Aβ accumulation and plaque‐associated changes rather than primary tauopathy [14]. Nevertheless, phosphorylated tau has been reported in aged 5XFAD brains, supporting limited evaluation of p‐tau‐associated changes in this background [15, 16]. Accordingly, the present study was designed to examine infection‐associated tau‐related changes within an amyloid‐dominant AD model, rather than to provide a definitive assessment in a dedicated tauopathy model such as PS19 [17].

Despite accumulating evidence associating T. gondii infection with reduced amyloid pathology, the effects of infection‐induced immune modulation, particularly tau pathology‐stage‐specific impacts, remain poorly understood. Most studies have focused only on broad changes in Aβ deposition or overall tau phosphorylation, without systematically analysing the stages through which tau pathology initiates, amplifies, and propagates within the lesion brain.

This study investigated the processes by which chronic T. gondii infection modulates tau‐related molecules and neuropathological processes in an AD mouse model. By integrating transcriptomic profiling, stepwise analysis of tau‐related pathology programs, and validation of neuropathological changes during tau hyperphosphorylation, we aimed to determine plausible stage‐specific sensitivity of tau pathology to infection‐induced immune regulation. This approach provides a framework for understanding the pathways by which neuroimmune modulation shapes tau pathology beyond amyloid‐centric mechanisms.

Materials and Methods

Animals and Experimental Groups

Wild‐type C57BL/6 and 5XFAD transgenic C57BL/6 mice were obtained from an established chronic T. gondii ME49 strain infection cohort, described in our previous study [10]. We established four experimental groups: WT, TOXO (WT mice infected with T. gondii), AD (5XFAD mice), and AD + TOXO (5XFAD mice infected with T. gondii). WT and 5XFAD mice (7 and 8 weeks old, respectively) were orally inoculated with ten cysts of the T. gondii ME49 strain under specific pathogen‐free conditions and maintained until sacrifice at 40 weeks post‐infection. All brain tissues and histological sections analysed in this study were derived from the previously reported 40‐week post‐infection cohort [10]. The present study newly evaluated pathological tau phosphorylation by AT8 immunofluorescence and reanalyzed the pooled microarray dataset using a revised tau‐focused curated gene panel.

Ethics Statement

All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Seoul National University, Seoul, Republic of Korea (approval number: SNU‐110315‐5). The animals used were established in our prior study [10], and no other animals were experimented on in the present study. Animals were housed and handled in compliance with the Animal Protection Act and the Laboratory Animal Act of the Republic of Korea, following Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International standards (SNUIBC‐R110302‐1‐1), with efforts made to minimize discomfort and distress.

Histological Confirmation of T. gondii Infection

Archival paraffin‐embedded brain sections from the previously reported 40‐week post‐infection cohort [10] were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using a commercial staining kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) to assess brain parenchyma and visualize T. gondii cysts. The presence of cysts was examined in WT (n = 3), TOXO (n = 2), AD (n = 3), and AD + TOXO (n = 2) mice.

Microarray and Transcriptomic Analysis

Pooled microarray data from brain tissue samples collected at 40 weeks post‐infection from the previously reported chronic T. gondii infection cohort were analysed in the present study [10]. In the original experiment, total RNA from three mice per group was pooled prior to hybridization and applied to the Illumina MouseRef‐8 v2 Expression BeadChip (Illumina Inc., CA, USA) at Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, Republic of Korea). The resulting microarray dataset therefore represents group‐level pooled expression profiles. For the present study, the previously generated pooled microarray dataset was reexamined using a revised curated gene panel focused on amyloid‐, neuroinflammation‐, and tau‐related pathways while excluding genes reported previously from the same 40‐week dataset.

Data processing and normalization were performed using Illumina GenomeStudio v2011.1 (Gene Expression Module v1.9.0), with downstream analyses conducted in R v2.15.1, as previously described [10]. Hierarchical clustering was conducted utilizing PermuteMatrix, and heatmaps were generated employing conditional formatting in Microsoft Excel.

A revised curated panel of 64 AD‐related genes was assembled in a literature‐guided manner to represent Aβ‐associated, Aβ/tau‐shared, and tau‐associated pathways relevant to AD pathology. Genes were assigned according to their predominant relevance in the AD literature rather than strict functional exclusivity. Caspase‐related genes were included because caspase activation has been implicated in both Aβ‐associated neuronal stress and tau cleavage/toxic conversion in AD‐related contexts [18, 19, 20]. The genes were classified into Aβ‐associated (21 genes), Aβ/tau‐shared (22 genes), and tau‐associated (21 genes) groups and expression patterns were compared descriptively after normalization to the WT group. Because RNA samples were pooled prior to hybridization, the transcriptomic data shown in Figures 2 and 3 were used for descriptive profiling of curated gene‐expression patterns. WT‐normalized and AD‐normalized comparisons were calculated independently from the underlying group‐level normalized signals using WT and AD as the respective reference groups. This focused reanalysis was intended for descriptive evaluation of literature‐guided AD‐related gene sets rather than unbiased whole‐transcriptome pathway inference.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Paraffin‐embedded brain sections from 40‐week post‐infection samples were analysed by immunofluorescence. Following deparaffinization, rehydration, and heat‐induced antigen retrieval, the sections were incubated with an anti‐phospho‐tau (AT8) antibody (Invitrogen, CA, USA), followed by an Alexa Fluor 594‐conjugated donkey anti‐mouse IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (Invitrogen). The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI.

Fluorescent images were acquired with a DMI6000B inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) under identical exposure settings from anatomically comparable hippocampal regions across groups. One section per mouse was analysed, with three mice included in each group. p‐tau immunoreactivity was quantitatively assessed using ImageJ by measuring the mean fluorescence intensity (arbitrary units, a.u.) over the full image field of each section.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed utilizing GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). Quantitative data from p‐tau immunofluorescence are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Group variations were assessed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple‐comparison test. Statistical significance was defined as an adjusted p < 0.05, with an asterisk (*) indicating WT versus AD and a dagger (†) indicating AD versus AD + TOXO. Transcriptomic heatmaps were used for descriptive comparison of curated gene‐expression patterns derived from the pooled microarray dataset.

Results

Chronic T. gondii Infection Is Established in the AD Mouse Brain and Maintained

H&E‐stained coronal brain sections including the hippocampal region, collected at 40 weeks after oral infection with T. gondii ME49 cysts, were examined to confirm the presence of T. gondii cysts (Figure 1). Cysts were not detected in the WT or AD groups (Figure 1A,C). In contrast, distinct cyst structures were observed in both TOXO and AD + TOXO mice (Figure 1B,D), demonstrating that chronic T. gondii infection is maintained in the brain even in the context of AD‐associated pathology in the 5XFAD model.

Representative H&E‐stained coronal brain sections including the hippocampal region from WT (A), TOXO (B), AD (C), and AD + TOXO (D) mice at 40 weeks post‐infection are shown. T. gondii cysts were not observed in WT or AD mice, whereas cyst structures were observed in the brains of TOXO and AD + TOXO mice (yellow arrows). Scale bar, 50 μm.

Functional Organization of the Curated Gene Panel and Aβ‐Associated Molecular Programs

To organize the transcriptomic analysis, the revised curated panel of 64 AD‐related genes was classified into three functional categories: Aβ‐associated (21 genes), Aβ/tau‐shared (22 genes), and tau‐associated (21 genes) (Figure 2A). The corresponding overview heatmaps are provided in the Supporting Information. Within the Aβ‐associated subset, the genes were further reorganized into three functional modules representing Aβ generation and processing (Figure 2B), myeloid/microglial recognition and functional programming (Figure 2C), and endosomal and vesicular trafficking associated with Aβ homeostasis (Figure 2D).

Within the Aβ generation and processing module, AD mice exhibited transcriptional changes in specific genes rather than uniform pathway activation. The expression of non‐amyloidogenic APP‐processing genes (Ace, Adam10) was slightly elevated, whereas that of amyloidogenic components (Bace1, Bace2) was reduced (Figure 2B‐1). In AD‐normalized comparisons, AD + TOXO mice showed coordinated reductions in Ace, Adam10, and Aph1a, whereas Bace1/2 remained largely unchanged (Figure 2B‐2), indicating that chronic infection reshapes APP‐processing balance without directly targeting amyloidogenic enzymes.

Genes associated with myeloid/microglial recognition and activation were selectively elevated in AD mice, with prominent increases of Cd68 and Cd14 (Figure 2C‐1). These markers were attenuated in AD + TOXO mice, whereas other transcripts were relatively preserved (Figure 2C‐2), suggesting a qualitative reprogramming of myeloid‐associated responses rather than broad immune suppression.

Within the endosomal and vesicular trafficking module, AD mice showed an increased expression of APP‐handling regulator‐encoding genes, including Ctse, Abca7, and Sorl1, which were coordinately reduced in AD + TOXO mice, while that of Picalm remained stabilized (Figure 2D). Together, these descriptive transcriptomic patterns suggest that chronic T. gondii infection was associated with selective changes in Aβ‐associated molecular programs within the curated panel, which may be relevant to subsequent neuroinflammatory amplification and tau‐related pathology.

Chronic T. gondii Infection Selectively Modulates Stage‐Specific Tau‐Related Pathogenic Programs

Based on transcriptomic changes within Aβ‐ and tau‐associated networks, we examined whether chronic T. gondii infection modulates tau pathology across distinct pathogenic stages, including initiation, amplification, toxin conversion, neuronal dysfunction, and accumulation (Figure 3).

Genes related to tau phosphorylation and kinase‐associated signalling showed only minor expression differences among the experimental groups. In WT‐normalized comparisons, Gsk3a and Gsk3b were slightly increased in AD mice, whereas Cdk5 and Abl1 were slightly reduced, with similarly small differences observed for the other kinase‐related genes (Figure 3A‐1). In AD‐normalized comparisons, Gsk3a, Gsk3b, and Abl1 showed small directional changes opposite to those observed in AD mice, whereas Cdk5 and Mapkap1 remained largely unchanged (Figure 3A‐2). Overall, these findings indicate only limited variation in kinase‐associated tau signalling within the pooled transcriptomic dataset.

In contrast, genes driving neuroinflammation‐mediated amplification of tau pathology displayed the most pronounced alterations. AD mice showed a robust upregulation of inflammatory and microglial mediator genes, including Ccl3, Ccl4, Gfap, and Ch25h, which were coordinately reduced in AD + TOXO mice (Figure 3B), identifying this module as the most infection‐sensitive tau‐related pathway.

Genes associated with toxic tau conversion exhibited a comparatively modest modulation. AD‐normalized analysis revealed reductions in Cops8, Casp6, and Casp9, with minimal changes in Casp3 and Foxo3 (Figure 3C), suggesting a partial attenuation of proteolytic tau toxicity.

Neuronal and synaptic genes were broadly dysregulated in AD mice, whereas AD + TOXO mice showed a selective remodelling rather than uniform suppression, with stabilization of Bdnf and Reln and a modest increase in Stmn4 and Syt4 (Figure 3D). Finally, selected tau accumulation‐ and trafficking‐related genes, including Rin3, were induced in AD mice but selectively reduced in AD + TOXO mice (Figure 3E).

Collectively, these descriptive transcriptomic patterns suggest that chronic T. gondii infection was associated with the most prominent changes in the neuroinflammation‐related amplification module of tau pathology, whereas upstream kinase signaling and downstream toxicity‐ or accumulation‐related processes showed more limited and selective alterations.

Modulation of Tau‐Related Pathogenic Programs Is Accompanied by Reduced Tau Phosphorylation

To determine whether the transcriptomic modulation of tau‐associated pathogenic programs observed in Figure 3 translates into measurable neuropathological outcomes, we next evaluated pathological tau phosphorylation by immunofluorescence in comparable hippocampal regions across groups (Figure 4).

Consistent with progressive tau pathology, AD mice exhibited a marked enhancement in phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) immunoreactivity compared with WT controls. Quantitative image analysis revealed a significant elevation of p‐tau intensity in AD brains when normalized to WT levels. In contrast, p‐tau signals were reduced in AD + TOXO mice relative to AD mice, indicating an attenuation of pathological tau accumulation.

Crucially, this decline was consistently observed across the comparable hippocampal fields analysed and was confirmed by quantitative fluorescence intensity measurements normalized to WT controls. Together, these findings indicate that reduced p‐tau immunoreactivity in AD + TOXO mice was accompanied by infection‐associated transcriptomic changes in tau‐related pathogenic programs.

Discussion

This study investigated how chronic T. gondii infection modulates AD‐related pathology, with a particular focus on tau‐related molecular and neuropathological outcomes. Chronic T. gondii infection alleviates AD‐like pathology by reducing Aβ plaque burden, reconfiguring the microglial state, and enhancing plaque‐associated phagocytic activity [10, 21]. These findings have positioned immune modulation by infection as a potential regulatory factor in AD pathology.

Consistent with this view, T. gondii infection has been associated with diverse neuroimmune and host‐cellular changes, while parasite‐derived effectors have been reported to modulate inflammatory, metabolic, and tau‐related pathways across multiple experimental contexts [22, 23, 24, 25].

The relative contributions of resident microglia and recruited monocyte‐lineage cells should also be considered when interpreting the present findings. Prior studies have suggested that both cell populations may contribute to the neuroimmune changes associated with chronic T. gondii infection [10, 11]. In this context, our previous work in the same experimental system emphasized increased homeostatic microglia and plaque‐associated microglial responses [10], whereas Möhle et al. highlighted a contribution of recruited monocytes to amyloid clearance [11]. Because the present study was based on pooled transcriptomic reanalysis and tissue‐level p‐tau immunofluorescence, it does not provide sufficient cellular resolution to distinguish the relative contributions of microglia and monocyte‐derived cells.

Given the close mechanistic link between Aβ‐induced neuroinflammation and downstream tau pathology, we hypothesize that immune modulation by infection may also influence the tau‐related pathological cascade [26, 27, 28]. AD progression is increasingly recognized as a transition from Aβ accumulation to tau‐mediated neurodegeneration. Tau pathology is more proximally associated with synaptic dysfunction, neuronal loss, and clinical deterioration than Aβ accumulation alone, suggesting the involvement of neuroinflammatory processes in this transition [26, 29, 30].

Despite evidence linking T. gondii infection to reduced amyloid pathology, the mechanisms by which infection‐induced immune modulation affects tau pathology, particularly in a stage‐specific manner, remain largely unexplored [10, 21]. This study aims to address this research gap by systematically analysing the tau‐related pathological program during AD progression from amyloid pathology to tauopathy.

Descriptive transcriptomic profiling of the literature‐guided curated panel suggested that chronic T. gondii infection was associated with selective changes in molecular networks linked to neuroimmune signalling and tau‐related neural pathways rather than with uniform suppression of AD‐related gene expression. Notably, within this focused panel, the neuroinflammation‐related module linking Aβ and tau showed the most prominent coordinated transcriptomic change. In parallel, p‐tau immunoreactivity was reduced in AD + TOXO mice. These findings support an association between infection‐associated immune modulation and reduced tau‐related pathology. Additional genome‐wide transcriptomic approaches and mechanistic studies will be required to determine whether infection‐associated neuroimmune changes directly contribute to the reduction in p‐tau [26, 27, 31].

A stepwise analysis of the tau‐related gene program revealed that the amplification phase driven by neuroinflammation was most prominently and consistently regulated by chronic infection. In contrast, alterations in upstream kinase‐related initiation pathways and downstream processes associated with toxic conversion or accumulation were relatively minor and heterogeneous among individual genes. These patterns are consistent with the possibility that neuroinflammation contributes to the amplification and propagation of tau‐related pathology [26, 32, 33].

The key takeaway of this study is that a transcriptional reprogramming of tau‐related pathways was accompanied by a corresponding reduction in tissue‐level pathological tau hyperphosphorylation. Immunofluorescence confirmed a marked decrease in p‐tau load in AD + TOXO brains, providing neuropathological validation for the molecular changes identified upstream. In summary, these findings suggest that modulation of immune system–tau interactions may be relevant to tau‐related pathology beyond direct targeting of tau‐regulating enzymes.

Nevertheless, the limitations of the mouse model used in the present study should also be considered. Because 5XFAD mice primarily model amyloid‐predominant pathology, the present findings are best interpreted as evidence of infection‐associated tau‐related changes within an amyloid‐dominant AD background [14]. In this regard, the reduced p‐tau signal observed here supports the relevance of this model for exploratory analysis [15, 16], while further validation in dedicated tauopathy models such as PS19 will be important to extend these observations [17]. In addition, because the present analyses were performed using archival tissue from a previously established cohort, the possibility of survival‐related selection bias cannot be formally excluded. Prospective studies with predefined survival monitoring will be required to determine whether chronic T. gondii infection differentially affects survival according to AD disease burden.

Conclusion

Collectively, our findings suggest that chronic T. gondii infection provides a useful experimental framework for examining associations between neuroimmune modulation and tau‐related pathology, highlighting stage‐specific immune‐ and tau‐related changes beyond traditional amyloid‐centric approaches.
