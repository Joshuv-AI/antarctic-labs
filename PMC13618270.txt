Spatially Coupled Cl2 Confinement and Activation at Curved Ni Single‐Atom Sites for Highly Reversible Li–Cl2 Batteries
Authors: Minggang Xie, Keren Jiang, Xuehai Tan, Xuesong Xie, Zheng Cheng, Xianbin Meng, Yuxuan Xue, Yi Guan, Zhixuan Chen, Yifan Li, Renfei Feng, Zhi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLeveraging the high‐potential Cl2/LiCl redox couple (∼3.6 V), rechargeable Li‐Cl2 batteries hold compelling promise for next‐generation energy storage. However, their practical viability is severely impeded by poor Cl2 retention and sluggish conversion kinetics, particularly under deep‐cycling and high‐rate regimes. Here, we present a spatially coupled Cl2‐management cathode that integrates Cl2 confinement and short‐range transport within a micropore‐rich matrix with spontaneous activation at dense pore wall‐anchored, curved Ni single‐atom sites. Via this closed‐loop pathway from Cl2 storage to LiCl deposition, the Li‐Cl2 battery achieves a remarkable 1.21 V polarization reduction at 1500 mAh g−1 cut‐off capacity at 2000 mA g−1. Consequently, an ultrahigh cumulative capacity exceeding 1.0 million mAh g−1, which doubles the lifespan of state‐of‐the‐art Li‐Cl2 systems, is achieved through the integrated approach incorporating this pathway, the robust, partially graphitized support, and an optimized discharge‐cutoff protocol. Furthermore, the exceptional all‐climate robustness (–40°C to +70°C), coupled with a high reversible areal capacity of 7.65 mAh cm−2 in pouch cells, establishes a new paradigm for practical, high‐energy storage systems.Keywords: Cl2 Management, curvature‐modulation, lithium–chlorine batteries, single‐atom catalysis, wide‐temperature operation

Introduction

The global shift toward high‐density, low‐carbon, and sustainable energy systems has imposed unprecedented demands on advanced energy storage technologies. Among the diverse energy storage systems, the primary Li–SOCl2 battery is distinguished by its ultra‐high energy density up to 710 Wh kg−1 [1], although its application has been confined to one‐time use for over three decades [2, 3]. Excitingly, in 2021, Dai et al. pioneered a coupled strategy that integrated TFSI−/FSI− additives with highly microporous carbon cathodes, enabling reversible Cl2/Cl− redox reactions, thereby opening the door to secondary Li/Na‐Cl2 batteries in Li/Na–SOCl2 systems [4]. Since this breakthrough, an increasing number of studies have broadened the scope of this emerging chemistry, uncovering important advances such as the additional redox pathways involving SCl2/S2Cl2 [5], the development of high‐energy‐efficiency SO2/SO2Cl2 batteries based on synergistic S‐Cl chemistry [6], the elucidation of the catalytic role of fluorinated electrolyte additives [7], the regulation of discharge‐product deposition behavior [8], iodine‐induced self‐depassivation [9], the design of wide‐temperature, noncorrosive organic electrolytes [10], and reversible Si‐anode redox [11], all of which highlight the great potential of Li/Na‐Cl2 batteries. Similar to other anion‐based redox systems, the Cl2/Cl− redox at cathodes makes cathodes the primary determinant of overall performance in Li–Cl2 batteries. Encouragingly, to date, a growing variety of cathode materials have been explored, including crystalline porous frameworks such as MOFs [12] and COFs [13], porous polymers [14], and ICl3 cathodes [15].

Owing to their abundance, low cost, and lightweight nature, carbonaceous materials remain the most extensively investigated, featuring diverse structural designs such as defect engineering [16], bicontinuous structures [17], porosity enhancement [18], and blocking pore design [19], among others. Pioneering studies have established that porous carbon architectures, particularly those enriched with micropores are indispensable for Li‐Cl2 batteries due to their exceptional capability to physically confine Cl2 species. However, as the redox host, pristine carbon materials often lack intrinsic active sites, resulting in sluggish Cl2/Cl− conversion kinetics. A typical observation is that discharge kinetics lag behind charging kinetics, thereby compromising Li‐Cl2 battery power density [4]. This imbalance originates from sluggish Cl2 to Cl− conversion on conventional porous carbon cathodes with limited catalytic sites, leading to Cl2 escape and parasitic reactions at the Li anode that forms an insulating, irreversible LiCl layer and severely compromises cycling stability [19]. Single‐atom catalysts (SACs) with their unique electronic structures and maximum atom efficiency are a promising solution to address these kinetic limitations and suppress active species crossover [20, 21, 22]. A recent study on Co SACs for Li–Cl2 batteries showed that CoN4 centers lower the kinetic barrier for LiCl oxidation, underscoring the potential of SACs for chlorine redox [23]. Nevertheless, the high volatility of Cl2 requires effective local retention, whereas the sluggish LiCl/Cl2 conversion demands rapid interfacial kinetics to mitigate electrode passivation and Cl2 shuttling [24]. Thus, an integrated Cl2‐management architecture that spatially couples Cl2 confinement and LiCl deposition with efficient catalytic conversion sites is highly desirable but remains insufficiently developed. Furthermore, the Cl0‐rich oxidizing environment might exacerbate the oxidation of defect‐rich carbon hosts; such degradation, reflecting the known challenges in acidic oxygen electrocatalysis [25, 26], likely leads to compromised cycling durability.

In this research, we report a sustainable Ni‐mediated upcycling strategy for constructing a hierarchical porous cathode with anchored NiN4 sites, tailored for high‐performance Li‐Cl2 batteries. This configuration establishes a spatially coupled Cl2‐management architecture, in which abundant micropores (micropore volume fraction ∼ 50.1%) serve as local Cl2 reservoirs and enable short‐range redistribution, while densely distributed NiN4 sites anchored on the pore walls facilitate efficient LiCl/Cl2 conversion within the adjacent larger pores. Driven by this spatially coupled mechanism, the developed Li‐Cl2 battery demonstrates a remarkable polarization reduction under deep‐discharge conditions of 1500 mAh g−1 cut‐off capacity, exhibiting a 1.21 V decrease at 2000 mA g−1. Theoretical calculations also reveal that this curvature engineering stabilizes the NiN4 coordination while intrinsically facilitating reversible conversion between Cl2 and LiCl. Under stringent testing (1500 mA g−1 and 1500 mAh g−1 cut‐off capacity), the battery maintains stable cycling for 670 cycles, yielding a cumulative capacity exceeding 1.0 million mAh g−1, a twofold enhancement over state‐of‐the‐art Li‐Cl2 systems. This performance is further validated by the stable operation across a wide temperature window (–40 to +70°C) and the reversible areal capacity of 7.65 mAh cm−2 in pouch cells, underscoring its all‐climate and high‐energy applications.

To elucidate the intrinsic challenges in Li–Cl2 batteries, we first employed a typical hierarchically porous cathode (denoted as APC), derived from asphaltene via Na2CO3 activation (synthesis and characterization detailed later). In line with the established protocol [9], after the initial discharge to 2.0 V (LiCl deposition, Equation (1)), the cell underwent galvanostatic cycling where it was charged to a specific capacity and then discharged to 2.0 V. These capacity limits were increased stepwise from 500 to 2000 mAh g−1 (10 cycles each) at 500 mA g−1 to determine the maximum reversible capacity (Figure 1b). Using this approach, Li–Cl2@APC achieved a maximum reversible capacity of 980 mAh g−1 at a 1000 mAh g−1 charge limit. Upon extending the charge to 1250 mAh g−1, the characteristic Cl2 evolution plateau was sustained at ∼4.0 V (Figure 1c), yet this deep charging failed to translate into a commensurate discharge capacity. Even worse, increasing the charging capacity to 2000 mAh g−1 caused severe cell failure, manifested as a negligible discharge capacity of only 55 mAh g−1 (11% CE) in the subsequent cycle. This irreversible degradation was primarily driven by weak Cl2 binding and sluggish kinetics on the APC cathode, leading to the diffusion of unbonded Cl2 across the separator to attack the Li anode [5]. This parasitic reaction continuously depleted active Li, leading to the formation of insulating bulk LiCl cubes with edge lengths exceeding 5 µm (Figure 1d), an observation further corroborated by the intense LiCl reflections and suppressed Li peaks in the XRD pattern (Figure 1e). Meanwhile, the substantial irreversible LiCl accumulation observed on the APC cathode (Figure 1e) was associated with the lack of catalytic sites, leading to increased polarization and further hindering the sluggish Cl2/LiCl redox [23]. Such degradation mechanisms underpin the broader challenge in the field, where the cycling durability of Li–Cl2 batteries was frequently hampered, often falling short of 500 cycles, particularly when subjected to high current densities and deep cycling. Consequently, the development of functional and robust scaffolds capable of both effective Cl2 retention and accelerated redox kinetics remains the key to unlocking durable Li‐Cl2 batteries.

By strategically leveraging the dual role of Ni species, which initially served as graphitization catalysts during synthesis [27, 28] and subsequently persisted as robust single‐atom sites for Cl2/LiCl redox conversion, we engineered a hierarchical porous carbon matrix to function as a highly durable and active cathode. As illustrated in Figure 2a, a precursor mixture of asphaltene, NiPc, and Na2CO3 was subjected to ball‐milling, annealing, and acid washing. The resulting product, denoted as NiN4‐cPC, featured atomically dispersed Ni embedded in a curved, porous graphitic carbon framework. Scanning and transmission electron microscopy (SEM/TEM) revealed that NiN4‐cPC possessed a sponge‐like morphology composed of assembled nanosheets, forming a 3D hierarchical network (Figure 2b,c). This multiscale architecture was characterized by a homogeneous distribution of C, N, and Ni throughout the matrix (Figure 2d), without discernible metal‐containing nanoparticles. Further investigation via high‐resolution TEM (Figure 2e) revealed a sophisticated hierarchy, with pronounced cylindrical‐like micropores intricately embedded within the larger macropore walls. This multiscale organization is expected to facilitate electrolyte infiltration, provide space for LiCl deposition, promote local Cl2 confinement and shorten diffusion pathways, thereby suppressing the shuttle effect and improving Cl2 utilization.

Aberration‐corrected scanning TEM (AC‐STEM) was employed to resolve the atomic structure, revealing a carbon matrix that exhibited well‐defined locally curved turbostratic lattice fringes [29]. These fringes possessed an interlayer spacing of ∼ 0.34 nm, which approached the characteristic (002) plane of graphitic carbon (Figure 2f). Within this framework, atomic‐scale bright spots were identified anchored across the curved graphitic layers, confirming the presence of isolated Ni single‐atom sites. By adjusting the defocus, Ni atoms residing at various depths within the curved carbon shells were resolved (Figure S1). This depth‐profiling observation confirmed that the Ni SACs were anchored across a multi‐layered, locally curved graphitic framework, spanning from the exterior surfaces to the internal voids. Such a pervasive distribution coupled the intrinsic catalytic potency of Ni SACs with the spatial confinement of the surrounding micropores, thereby ensuring the efficient and localized conversion of Cl2/LiCl. The Ni loading was quantified as 1.07 wt% by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP‐OES), with other non‐metal elements quantified via CHNO analysis (Table S1). TEM imaging and XRD analysis (Figure S2) confirmed the formation of curved, bark‐like wrinkled graphitic domains, a morphology likely resulting from the Ni‐catalyzed transformation of asphaltenes during pyrolysis [30, 31]. The resulting curvature induced σ–π rehybridization, which could modulate the electronic structure of the anchored single‐atom catalysts [32]. Notably, the use of abundant asphaltene feedstock together with a facile ball‐milling process enabled a sustainable and scalable synthesis (Figure S3).

To elucidate the origin of this unique morphology, we conducted a control experiment by impregnating NiPc onto the APC host, followed by a secondary annealing at 800°C. Despite achieving a comparable Ni single‐atom mass loading of 1.14 wt%, the resulting NiN4‐APC retained the original amorphous morphology of APC with negligible curved graphitic features (Figure S4). The porous structure of NiN4‐cPC was further characterized by N2 adsorption/desorption (Figures 2g and S5). Among the fitting pore models, the mixed slit/cylindrical QSDFT model yielded the lowest fitting error of 0.103% (Table S2), consistent with the pore features observed. Quantitative analysis further confirmed this micropore‐dominated texture, yielding a micropore volume of 0.42 cm3 g−1, which accounted for 50.1% of the total pore volume, much higher than 0.29 cm3 g−1 of APC and 0.15 cm3 g−1 of NiN4‐APC (Table S1). X‐ray Diffraction (XRD) patterns of APC, NiN4‐APC, and NiN4‐cPC (Figure S6a) revealed no detectable reflections from crystalline Ni species. Notably, compared to the weak signal observed in APC, NiN4‐cPC exhibited a significantly more pronounced and sharper peak at 2θ = 26.4° (indexed to the graphitic 002 plane), indicating that Ni catalyzed the formation of locally ordered graphitic domains. This conclusion was further supported by Raman spectroscopy (Figure S6b): the ID/IG decreased to 0.72 for NiN4‐cPC, which was lower than that of both NiN4‐APC (0.81) and APC (0.87), in line with the Ni/Fe/Co‐mediated graphitization effect [33, 34, 35].

To definitively establish the valence state and local coordination environment of the Ni active sites, XANES and extended EXAFS analyses were performed. The Ni K‐edge XANES spectra revealed that the absorption edges of both NiN4‐cPC and NiN4‐APC were located at lower energies than those of NiO and close to the reference NiPc (Figure 3a), suggesting that the Ni possessed an oxidation state marginally lower than +2. The k3‐weighted fourier transform (FT) EXAFS spectra exhibited a single dominant peak arising from the first‐shell Ni–N coordination (Figure 3b), without detectable Ni–Ni scattering signals. The Ni–N peak of NiN4‐cPC was shifted towards a slightly lower radial distance of 1.89 Å compared to 1.91 Å of NiN4‐APC, which could be attributed to the turbostratic architecture of the carbon host [36, 37]. Quantitative EXAFS fitting yielded a Ni–N coordination number of approximately 4 for both NiN4‐cPC (Figure 3c) and NiN4‐APC (Figure S7a and Table S3), consistent with a typical NiN4 configuration. This proposed structural model was further supported by the good agreement between experimental XANES spectra (Figures 3d,e, and S7b) and simulations by FDMNES.

Li–Cl2 batteries were constructed utilizing the synthesized samples as cathodes and lithium metal as anode. At a current density of 1000 mA g−1, the NiN4‐cPC and NiN4‐APC cathodes delivered remarkable initial deep discharge capacities of 9423 and 7721 mAh g−1 (Figure 4a), respectively, with both significantly surpassing the 7163 mAh g−1 of the pristine APC. The enhanced initial capacity indicates more extensive LiCl deposition, thereby establishing a substantial initial inventory that served as a vital reservoir for subsequent reversible conversion from LiCl to Cl2. The maximum reversible capacity was subsequently quantified employing the same stepwise galvanostatic protocol as shown in Figure 1b. Upon incrementally increasing the charge cutoff capacity from 500 to 2000 mAh g−1 at 1000 mA g−1, the NiN4‐cPC electrode achieved a remarkable reversible capacity of 1822 mAh g−1 (Figure 4b). This performance represented an ∼86% enhancement over 980 mAh g−1 of pristine APC and substantially outperformed the 1163 mAh g−1 of NiN4‐APC. Furthermore, while maintaining exceptional cycling stability at a deep discharge capacity of 1500 mAh g−1, the Li‐Cl2@NiN4‐cPC cathode exhibited significantly reduced polarization (Figure S8a). Specifically, it delivered a narrow charge‐discharge voltage gap of 0.53 V, which was markedly lower than that of NiN4‐APC (0.64 V) and the pristine APC (0.72 V), accompanied by stable charge‐discharge plateaus. Given the gaseous nature and high diffusivity of the Cl2 intermediate, effective confinement of the active species was paramount to mitigating the shuttle effect and ensuring long‐term rechargeability. To rigorously evaluate Cl2 retention, the Li–Cl2@NiN4‐cPC cell was charged to 1500 mAh g−1 and subjected to rest periods of varying durations. Remarkably, the subsequent discharge capacities remained ∼1500 mAh g−1 after 1, 3, and 12 h of resting, confirming its excellent Cl2 retention ability (Figure 4c). Even after a prolonged 42 h rest, the capacity was fully retained and exhibited an apparent increase, despite a slight decrease in the discharge voltage plateau. The voltage evolution was associated with the formation of SO2Cl2 during retention [5, 6], whereas the increased capacity may be attributed to partial LiCl dissolution, which released occupied pore space and regenerated SOCl2 (Figure S9) [4]. Importantly, these resting protocols exerted minimal impact on the overall cycling stability; the rapid recovery of voltage profiles and high‐capacity retention validated the robust Cl2 confinement.

Notably, all the batteries discussed above were evaluated using the established protocol involving deep discharge to 2.0 V in every cycle. However, such deep discharge often induced severe electrode passivation, as evidenced by the evolution of the charge‐transfer resistance R ct extracted from EIS fitting (Figure S10) and prior reports [4, 9]. Analogous to observations in Li–O2 and Li–CO2 systems [38, 39], this extensive discharge led to the cumulative buildup of insoluble products that clog pores and escalate polarization [9], shortening cell life. To mitigate this irreversibility, we adapted a capacity‐limited cycling strategy from Li‐gas batteries, optimizing it to accommodate the unique features of the Li‐Cl2 system. The protocol initiated with a deep first discharge to 2.0 V to establish a sufficient LiCl inventory, followed by cycling with strict capacity limits applied to both charge and discharge. This optimized protocol effectively reduced polarization, decreasing the voltage gap of Li‐Cl2@NiN4‐cPC from 0.53 to 0.42 V, under the same cut‐off capacity of 1500 mAh g−1 and same current density of 1000 mA g−1 (Figure S8b). Synergistically, by coupling this protocol with efficient NiN4‐cPC catalysis, a remarkable 41.7% polarization reduction from the 0.72 V (catalyst‐free APC) was successfully achieved. During the subsequent long‐term cycling, this approach effectively suppressed polarization growth, enabling the Li‐Cl2@NiN4‐cPC to maintain significantly lower polarization and superior stability (Figure 4d,e).

The rate capability was further evaluated under a fixed capacity cutoff of 1500 mAh g−1 (Figure 4f). Even when the current density was increased to 5000 mA g−1, the Li–Cl2@NiN4‐cPC still exhibited exceptional resilience, retaining a capacity of 1388 mAh g−1 (92.5% retention) and high energy efficiency (Figure S11). In stark contrast, the capacities of the control samples, NiN4‐APC and APC, declined precipitously to 1140 and 249 mAh g−1, respectively. For instance, at 2000 mA g−1 and a cutoff capacity of 1500 mAh g−1, NiN4‐cPC delivered a remarkably low overpotential of 0.62 V, substantially outperforming NiN4‐APC and APC, which exhibited overpotentials of 0.71 and 1.83 V, respectively, corresponding to a 1.21 V polarization reduction relative to APC. These results underscored the superior high‐rate tolerance of NiN4‐cPC, which was attributed to the sustained acceleration of Cl2/LiCl redox kinetics even under rigorous electrochemical loads. The robustness of the cathode was further evaluated via long‐term cycling at 1500 mA g−1 with a cut‐off capacity of 1500 mAh g−1, translating to an areal capacity of 2.19 mAh cm−2. Li–Cl2@NiN4‐cPC demonstrated extraordinary stability (Figure 4g), sustaining operation for over 670 cycles before the discharge capacity faded to the 98% retention threshold (1470 mAh g−1). This translated to a monumental cumulative capacity exceeding 1.0 × 106 mAh g−1 (Figure 4h and Table S4), which represented a two‐fold increase over the current state‐of‐the‐art records for Li–Cl2 systems [4, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 19, 23, 40, 41]. Throughout this extended operational lifespan, the terminal charge potential remained highly stable (e.g., ∼4.12 V at the 600th cycle), indicating minimal polarization growth. The origin of the long‐term stability of Li‐Cl2@NiN4‐cPC was first investigated by post‐cycling EXAFS and ICP‐OES. NiN4‐cPC retained its characteristic Ni–N coordination feature after cycling (Figure S13), while its Ni content remained comparable before and after cycling (1.07 wt% and 1.21 wt%, respectively). Moreover, the fresh and cycled electrolytes exhibited similarly low Ni concentrations of 0.012 and 0.010 ppm, respectively, indicating the stability of the anchored NiN4 sites as well. APC, NiN4‐APC, and NiN4‐cPC were evaluated under the same capacity‐limited protocol, with both charge and discharge capacities fixed at 1500 mAh g−1 at 1 C (Figure S12). Although the capacity limitation helped delay excessive LiCl accumulation and electrode passivation, APC and NiN4‐APC still exhibited progressively increasing polarization and gradual deterioration of their voltage profiles. In contrast, NiN4‐cPC maintained lower polarization and more stable charge and discharge plateaus, indicating that its superior long‐term cycling stability arises from the combined contributions of the spatially coupled cathode architecture and the capacity‐limited cycling protocol.

To evaluate how cathode‐mediated Cl2 retention mitigated Li‐anode degradation, the cycled Li anodes paired with NiN4‐cPC and APC cathodes were examined. After cycling, the Li anode paired with NiN4‐cPC retained a thicker metallic Li layer of approximately 359 µm from the initial 400 µm Li foil, whereas only approximately 310 µm of metallic Li remained in the APC‐paired anode (Figure S14a,b). XPS analysis revealed LiCl‐ and Al–Cl‐related species on both cycled Li surfaces, demonstrating the formation of LiCl‐based interphases (Figure S14c). Consistently, XRD analysis showed weaker LiCl diffraction signals for the NiN4‐cPC‐paired anode, indicating reduced LiCl accumulation compared with the APC‐paired anode (Figure S14d). Collectively, this unprecedented combination of ultralong cycle life, deep capacity utilization, and voltage stability attested to the triple synergy of efficient catalysis enabled by active NiN4 sites, effective Cl2 confinement via the micropore‐dominated architecture, and the structural robustness of the cathode.

Ni K‐edge XANES spectra were collected at representative electrochemical states, including the first discharge to 2 V, charge to 1500 mAh g−1, re‐discharge to 1500 mAh g−1, and subsequent cycling steps, to investigate the electron transfer process of Ni sites in Li‐Cl2@NiN4‐cPC. As shown in Figure 5a, the Ni K‐edge absorption edge shifted to markedly lower energy relative to the OCV state during the first discharge process and then shifted back to higher energy upon charging to 1500 mAh g−1, although it remained lower than that at OCV, consistent with residual LiCl coverage on the cathode. A similar energy evolution was observed in the second cycle, suggesting an obvious reversible variation in the electronic state of Ni during the Li‐Cl2 electrochemical process, which reflected interactions between chlorine‐related species and the Ni sites. In addition, in situ Raman spectroscopy was applied to track the real‐time evolution of reaction intermediates. During the initial discharge (Figure 5b), the characteristic bands of SOCl2 at 193, 285, 344, and 490 cm−1 diminished or vanished entirely, which was accompanied by the emergence of new signals at 151, 218, and 461 cm−1 corresponding to S. This transition was consistent with the cathodic reduction of SOCl2 (4Li + 2SOCl2 → S + SO2 + 4LiCl) [42]. During the subsequent charging step to the cut‐off capacity of 1500 mAh g−1, S–Cl intermediates became distinct before being consumed during the subsequent discharge; specifically, the features at 205 cm−1 (SCl2/S2Cl2) and 444 cm−1 (S2Cl2) also appeared during charging and subsequently faded during discharging. This behavior pointed to the reaction sequence of S + Cl2 → SCl2, 2SCl2 ↔ S2Cl2 + Cl2 [5], indicating the involvement of electrochemically generated Cl2 from LiCl oxidation in the conversion between S and S–Cl species.

Cryo‐HRTEM was performed to examine the NiN4‐cPC cathode collected in the discharged state after cycling. As shown in Figure 5c, mesopores and macropores within the carbon framework were clearly observed. To distinguish the LiCl lattice from the carbon framework, fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis was conducted on the enlarged region. The resulting FFT pattern exhibited distinct diffraction spots attributable to crystalline LiCl, together with diffraction rings associated with the carbon framework. Notably, a localized LiCl nanocrystallite with a characteristic size below 10 nm was observed near the pore wall, providing spatial evidence for nanoscale LiCl deposition within the porous cathode structure (Figure 5d). While cryo‐HRTEM provides direct local evidence of LiCl deposition, it probed only a limited region of the cathode. In future studies, small‐angle x‐ray scattering (SAXS) may serve as a complementary technique to examine pore‐structure evolution statistically across broader length scales [43]. Mass spectrometry was employed to probe the release and retention of Cl2 from NiN4‐cPC cathodes at different electrochemical states (Figures 5e and S15). The fresh electrolyte exhibited an (m/z) 70‐to‐(m/z) 118 signal‐intensity ratio of 1.65, which was taken as the SOCl2‐derived background [16]. At room temperature, both the charged and discharged cathodes exhibited ratios close to this baseline value, indicating that the detected signals were dominated by residual SOCl2 electrolyte within the cathodes. In contrast, after the charged cathode was heated at 80°C for 30 min, the (m/z) 70‐to‐(m/z) 118 signal‐intensity ratio increased markedly to 3.96, indicating the release of Cl2 retained in the charged cathode. After the charged cell was rested at open‐circuit conditions for 12 h, the corresponding ratio decreased only slightly to 3.77 under identical heating conditions. The persistence of this elevated Cl2‐related signal supported the effective retention of electrochemically generated Cl2 within NiN4‐cPC.

Leveraging the recognized durability of Li–SOCl2 chemistry across broad temperatures and the strong ambient performance of Li–Cl2@NiN4‐cPC, we evaluated its behavior over a wide temperature window. The Li–Cl2@NiN4‐cPC cell was subjected to a stepwise thermal modulation protocol, cycling from 20°C (room temperature) to –40°C and back to +80°C in 10°C increments (Figure 6a). As the temperature decreased to –40°C, we observed a progressive increase in polarization, evidenced by the divergence of terminal charge and discharge voltages (Figure 6b). This phenomenon was attributed to typical kinetic sluggishness, including increased electrolyte viscosity and retarded ion diffusion [18, 23]. Despite the stringent requirements for regulating LiCl deposition and retaining Cl2 under such kinetically inhibited conditions, the Li–Cl2@NiN4‐cPC demonstrated remarkable resilience, sustaining stable cycling for 200 cycles at 1500 mA g−1 with a high cutoff capacity of 1500 mAh g−1 (Figure 6c). Conversely, elevated temperatures (up to 80°C) surmounted kinetic barriers, resulting in reduced polarization and well‐defined voltage plateaus due to enhanced Li+ transport (Figure 6d). However, operating at 80°C triggered a sharp performance decline, which was likely due to the thermal instability of the plastic O‐ring used to seal the coin cell (Figure S16). Demonstrating practical viability, the fabricated pouch cell with a high mass loading of ∼5.1 mg cm−2 successfully exhibited a stable open‐circuit voltage of 3.598 V while maintaining stable cycling at a high areal capacity ∼ 7.65 mAh cm−2. Optimizing Cl2 binding strength is essential, as insufficient adsorption not only impedes redox conversion but also permits the escape of weakly bound Cl2, causing active‐material loss and aggravating the shuttle effect. The efficacy of Cl2 confinement was further corroborated by the reported KBr/methyl orange (MO) oxidation assay [19]. To isolate the contribution of entrapped Cl2, charged electrodes underwent a washing step to eliminate loosely adsorbed surface residues. Notably, the charged NiN4‐cPC electrode induced a more pronounced bleaching effect than the APC and NiN4‐APC electrodes (Figure 7a). These results indicated that the dense microporosity of NiN4‐cPC effectively suppressed Cl2 effusion. A further control sample, NiN4‐APC‐KOH, was prepared by KOH activation and possessed a micropore volume comparable to that of NiN4‐cPC, together with atomically dispersed Ni–N sites and a relatively low‐curvature carbon framework (Figure S17 and Table S1).

Electrochemical evaluation of the charged cells by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) revealed that Li–Cl2@NiN4‐cPC exhibited a more positive reduction potential (Figure 7b) and the lowest Tafel slope among the tested samples (Figure 7c), collectively demonstrating the enhanced catalytic activity and favorable reaction kinetics of the curvature‐modulated NiN4 sites toward Cl2/LiCl‐centered conversion chemistry involving associated redox‐active S–Cl/S–Cl–O species. Li–Cl2@NiN4‐cPC also delivered the largest cathodic current response, suggesting that the superior Cl0‐to‐Cl− conversion kinetics arose from the synergy between the curvature‐modulated reactivity of the NiN4 sites and their close spatial proximity to the Cl2‐retaining pores. Galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) measurements were further performed to evaluate the apparent kinetic polarization (Figure S18). Among the four samples, NiN4‐cPC exhibited the smallest IR drop of 0.203 V and generally higher apparent Li+ diffusion coefficients (DLi+), indicating reduced polarization and improved kinetics.

First‐principles DFT calculations were performed to elucidate the combined impact of NiN4 catalysis and substrate curvature. A curved NiN4 (NiN4‐curved) and curved graphene model was constructed alongside a planar counterpart (NiN4‐planar and graphene). Thermodynamic evaluations confirmed that both structures satisfied essential stability criteria (E f < 0, U diss > 0); notably, NiN4‐curved exhibited superior robustness (E f = −2.66 eV, U diss = 1.08 V) compared to the planar model (−1.70 eV, 0.29 V), suggesting enhanced thermodynamic stability under harsh redox conditions (Figure 7d). Regarding the adsorption mechanism, Cl2 exhibited low adsorption energies of −0.23 eV on graphene and −0.22 eV on curved graphene (Figures 7e and S19–S22), suggesting negligible binding strength. In stark contrast, the introduction of NiN4 single‐atom could provide partially occupied Ni 3d states that effectively hybridized with the σ* antibonding orbital of Cl2. This interaction significantly strengthened chemisorption, increasing the adsorption energy to −0.58 eV for NiN4‐planar, which was further enhanced by curvature engineering to reach −0.64 eV. Furthermore, differential charge density analysis revealed that curvature amplified electron transfer from Ni to Cl2, activating the Cl2 molecule to promote rapid downstream redox reactions (Figure 7f).

To further unravel the thermodynamic origins of the enhanced performance, discharge and charge reaction pathways were simulated (Figure 7g,h). The results revealed that the prohibitive Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) on pristine graphene imposed an intrinsic barrier by the adsorption of Cl2, inherently limiting conventional carbon cathodes to primary Li–SOCl2 systems. In stark contrast, the introduction of Ni sites fundamentally altered this pathway, significantly reducing ΔG to −0.11 eV, enabling a spontaneous adsorption regime. Notably, the curved geometry further optimized this thermodynamic landscape, lowering ΔG to −0.17 eV. Underpinned by significant electron transfer from the Ni center, the NiN4‐curved site effectively activated Cl2, thereby expediting its conversion and favoring subsequent conversion. Further calculations involving the chlorine‐containing species SCl2, S2Cl2, and SO2Cl2 consistently demonstrated that the NiN4 reduced the thermodynamic difficulty associated with their conversion (Figures S23–27), while curvature modulation further strengthened this effect. During the subsequent charging process, both NiN4 motifs exhibited much stronger affinity for LiCl than graphene. Specifically, the NiN4‐curved configuration achieved the highest binding energy of −0.79 eV (versus −0.74 eV for NiN4‐planar), resulting in the lowest ΔG of −0.21 eV. This characteristic promoted site‐specific LiCl adsorption to ensure intimate cathode contact, which was critical for minimizing oxidation barriers and expediting the decomposition of insulating discharge products [23].

Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrated that the key to unlocking the potential of high‐energy Li–Cl2 chemistry lay in a spatially coupled architecture. By anchoring NiN4 single‐atom sites onto the walls of hierarchical, interconnected pores, we successfully bridged Cl2 storage, transfer and conversion within the hierarchical matrix. Crucially, this unique curvature within the partially graphitized scaffold intrinsically enhanced the catalytic activity of NiN4 towards Cl2/LiCl redox kinetics, delivering a remarkable 1.21 V polarization reduction under deep‐discharge conditions of 1500 mAh g−1 cut‐off capacity at 2000 mA g−1. Concurrently, the robust, partially graphitized support ensured structural integrity, while an optimized capacity‐limited cycling protocol preserved electrode stability. Empowered by these features, the Li–Cl2 battery effectively mitigated long‐term cycling degradation, delivering a record‐breaking cumulative capacity exceeding 1.0 million mAh g−1 even under stringent high‐rate (1500 mA g−1) and deep‐discharge (1500 mAh g−1) conditions. Furthermore, its exceptional all‐climate robustness (–40°C to +70°C) and the realization of a 7.65 mAh cm−2 areal capacity in pouch cells collectively established a new paradigm for practical, high‐energy storage systems. These results confirmed the exceptional promise of Li–Cl2 chemistry and laid the foundation for next‐generation applications.
