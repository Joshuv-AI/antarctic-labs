Beyond Negative Rollouts: Positive-Only Policy Optimization with Implicit Negative Gradients
Mingwei Xu, Hao Fang

Abstract
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), due to the deterministic verification, becomes a dominant paradigm for enhancing the reasoning ability of large language models (LLMs). The community witnesses the rapid change from the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) to Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), in which GRPO reduces the complicated advantage estimation with simple estimation over grouped positive and negative rollouts. However, we note that negative rollouts may admit no gradation of failure severity, and the combinatorial vastness makes penalizing a few sampled negatives unlikely to cover a meaningful reward signal under sparse binary rewards. In this work, we propose Positive-Only Policy Optimization (POPO), a novel RLVR framework in which learning can occur exclusively via online positive rollouts. Specifically, POPO utilizes bounded importance sampling over the positive rollout set. Thus, no disjoint negative rollouts are used for the gradient guidance during the policy post-training. We show that implicit negative gradients can emerge naturally through reinforcing the positive probability via rollouts redistribution. Next, POPO stabilizes the policy optimization through two mechanisms. First, it applies a siamese policy network with a momentum-based adaptation law for stabilized policy evolution. Second, we replace the KL-divergence with a bounded similarity penalty term in the siamese representation space. We conduct extensive experiments using publicly available, well-established text-LLM models, e.g., the Qwen family, across all-level mathematical benchmarks (MATH-500, AMC23, AIME 2024/2025, and Olympiad). Our experiment demonstrates that POPO achieves performance comparable to, or even superior to GRPO. Notably, we show that POPO can achieve 36.67 % \% in AIME 2025 with Qwen-Math-7B, outperforming GRPO 30.00 % \% . Our ablation and sweep studies further illustrate the necessity and robustness of POPO components. Our results suggest a new RLVR direction without negative rollouts.

Introduction

The emergence of reasoning capabilities in large language models (LLMs) through reinforcement learning (RL) makes a significant advance in the capability enhancement and human feedback (RLHF) alignment
(
36
)
. Well-known work, such as DeepSeek-R1
(
15
)
and OpenAI’s o1 series
(
22
)
, demonstrates that LLMs can surge intelligent chain-of-thought (COT)
(
48
)
reasoning ability through RL with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)
(
24
)
. In general, RLVR works by giving a sampled question
x
and the corresponding LLM-generated response
y
containing reasoning steps and a final answer, a verifier provides deterministic feedback such as
R(x,y)=\mathbf{1}[\texttt{verify}(y)=\texttt{True}]

(
7
)
. The simplicity of the verification mechanism, together with various RL algorithms, has achieved substantial improvements in mathematical problem-solving and coding tasks
(
25
)
, where LLMs show a strong ability of self-reflection and generating sophisticated reasoning chains.

Despite these successes, current RLVR approaches face a fundamental challenge: the treatment of incorrect responses. Take the mathematical reasoning task, for example. At the cold-start stage, the vast majority of sampled responses are incorrect, e.g., typical pass@k rates are usually below 50% for the AMC 23 dataset, which happens due to diverse situations such as computational errors, problem misinterpretations, and inappropriate solution strategies (see figure
1
). Thus, we begin to reconsider
do incorrect responses provide meaningful learning signal under the sparse reward signal setting?
and thus ask the question:
can a policy improve by reinforcing only from its successes?
This seems counterintuitive at first glance, given the conventions in RL, which reinforce the policy with the good reward while penalizing it for the bad rewards. However, a few insightful observations motivate it. First, like GRPO
(
40
)
, all correct responses share the same advantage over all incorrect ones. Thus, there is no gradation of “how good or how wrong” a response is. Second, the space of incorrect responses is combinatorially vast
(
54
)
for mathematical tasks, making it unlikely that penalizing a few sampled failures meaningfully covers the failure modes. Third, past work in contrastive self-supervised learning (SSL) demonstrates that effective representations are feasible using only positive samples, works such as ”Bootstrap your own latent”(BYOL)
(
14
)
and ”Exploring simple siamese representation learning” (SimSiam)
(
5
)
, achieving powerful latent representations.

Inspired by the above, we propose
Positive-Only Policy Optimization (POPO)
, a new on-policy optimization approach for RLVR that learns exclusively on positive reward responses. Specifically, POPO has the following contributions:

•

POPO introduces bounded importance sampling weights, which normalize over the positive rollout set, to preferentially reinforce positive responses, while introducing implicit negative penalties.

•

POPO uses an exponential moving average (EMA) to update the siamese policy network as an adaptive policy anchor, combining the benefits from SSL insights.

•

POPO replaces KL divergence with representation-space similarity, providing a soft bounded constraint that preserves semantic structure in the predictor space.

To evaluate the POPO algorithm, we use the DeepScaleR-Preview-Dataset for policy training. Then, we conduct extensive test experiments using publicly available, well-established LLM models, including the Qwen2.5 Math family, DeepSeek-R1 distilled models, and general text-LLM models, across all-level mathematical reasoning benchmarks (MATH-500, AMC23, AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and Olympiad). Our experiment demonstrates that POPO still achieves comparable or even superior performance compared with GRPO and its variants. Especially, in AIME 2025, we show that POPO can achieve 36.67
\%
using Qwen-Math-7B, outperforming GRPO 30.00
\%
. Last, our ablation and sweep studies illustrate the necessity and robustness of POPO components and hyperparameters. Our results have implications for future sparse RLVR beyond negative rollouts.

Related Work

RLHF and RLVR.
RLHF addresses a critical limitation that the next-token prediction objective optimized during pretraining and supervised finetuning (SFT) phases does not directly capture the intentions of human users. RLHF aligns LLM behavior to be close to human intent by training a reward model on human preference and optimizing the policy via RL
(
6
;
43
)
. However, learning reward modeling is an imperfect proxy for human intent and thus may be sensitive to the reward hacking issue
(
12
)
. Offline preference learning, which removes the reward modeling by purely using the contrastive preference, also has widespread usage. For example, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
37
)
has been applied for aligning the chatbot output to user intent
(
44
)
. On the other hand, RLVR replaces the learnable reward modeling process with deterministic verification pipelines, where correctness can be easily assessed in domains such as mathematical reasoning and code generation
(
7
;
56
)
. Recent technical reports
(
22
;
15
)
demonstrate that simple RLVR elicits sophisticated COT.

Policy Optimization Methods for RLVR.
Following the well-established adoption in GRPO
(
40
)
, it has become a widely used approach by removing the value network and estimating advantages through group-level reward normalization. A series of GRPO extensions have been proposed
(
45
;
58
;
57
;
55
;
2
;
29
;
49
;
20
;
35
;
9
;
51
)
. Among them, Dr.GRPO
(
32
)
removes per-response length and standard-deviation normalization to correct an optimization bias that artificially inflates advantage estimation. Dynamic sAmpling Policy Optimization (DAPO)
(
52
)
introduces decoupled clipping, dynamic sampling that filters zero-advantage groups, and token-level policy gradients. Soft Adaptive Policy Optimization (SAPO)
(
11
)
replaces hard clipping with a smooth temperature-controlled gate to preserve near-on-policy learning signals while attenuating off-policy outliers. However, all methods remain to learn from positive and negative rollouts.

Learning without Negative Samples.
Learning without negative samples (or exclusively from positive examples) undergoes a long debate in the area of contrastive representation learning
(
31
)
. The early work in self-supervised learning, such as SimCLR
(
4
)
and MoCo
(
18
)
, needs to rely on explicit negative samples. BYOL
(
14
)
changed this paradigm by demonstrating that representations can be learned with only positive samples through an asymmetric online-target architecture. Meanwhile, SimSiam
(
5
)
also illustrated that a Siamese network with only a stop-gradient operation can enable meaningful latent representation learning. Recently, in the post-training of LLM, SFT works by training the alignment with giving prompts and expected solutions. Thus, it can be treated as a naive way of learning without negative samples. However, SFT-based approaches only optimize a fixed cross-entropy objective, which clearly suffers from the limited exploration abilities. Meanwhile, SFT can also lead to distribution mismatch as the policy may evolve unboundedly, causing the strong over-fitting on the post-training data
(
30
)
. Recent work in Online SFT
(
28
)
, Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning
(
53
)
, Self-Taught Reasoner
(
56
)
, and ReST
EM

(
41
)
shows that iterative on-policy SFT supervision progressively improves reasoning capabilities. To this end, our POPO closes the gap by combining the insights from above.

3
Methods

3.1
Problem Setting and Preliminary

We consider improving an LLM’s mathematical reasoning ability through RLVR. Let
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},a_{i}^{*})\}_{i=1}^{N}
denote a set of mathematical problems, where
x_{i}
is a problem statement (prompt) and
a_{i}^{*}
is the given ground-truth answer. The policy
\pi_{\theta}
stands for an LLM that generates a sequence of tokens (response)
y=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{T})
given a prompt
x
:
\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})
, where
y_{<t}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{t-1})
denotes the response prefix. The reward function provides binary feedback based on answer correctness:

R(x,y)=\mathbf{1}[\texttt{extract}(y)=a^{*}]

(
7
)
, where
\texttt{extract}(\cdot)
parses the final answer from the response. Here, we consider the binary reward is
sparse
(provided only at sequence completion) and
deterministic
. For each prompt
x
, we partition generated response rollouts into two disjoint sets

\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)=\{y:R(x,y)=1\},\quad\mathcal{S}^{-}(x)=\{y:R(x,y)=0\}.

(1)

To maximize the expected reward, a general policy gradient optimization follows:

\max_{\theta}\;J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[R(x,y)\right],

(2)

\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[A^{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y)\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\right],

(3)

where
A^{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y)
is the estimated advantage function measuring how much better response
y
is compared to the average responses. equation (
3
) increases the probability of responses with positive advantage and decreases the negative ones. Thus, it can be viewed as
\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\sum_{i:A_{i}>0}A_{i}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid x)+\sum_{i:A_{i}<0}A_{i}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid x)

(
59
)
.

Unified View of Policy Optimization.

General policy optimization methods follow:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\mathrm{anchor}}}\left[w(\theta)\cdot f(A)\cdot\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\right]+\lambda\cdot\mathcal{R}(\theta),

(4)

where
w(\theta)
is an importance sampling weight,
f(A)
transforms the advantage, and
\mathcal{R}(\theta)
is a regularizer term to anchor the policy. Different methods instantiate these components differently: PPO uses clipped importance ratios with learned values for advantage estimations, whereas GRPO uses group-normalized rewards with KL divergence regularization.

3.2
Positive-Only Policy Optimization

Figure 2:
Overview of the POPO components. The policy network (upper part) follows the gradient backpropagation, while the Siamese network (lower part) uses momentum adaptation. Loss is in the grey box. Algorithm
1
shows forward and backward processes.

POPO optimizes the policy using only correct responses while maintaining stability through the following mechanisms: self-normalized weight, adaptive anchor, representation-space alignment, and entropy penalty (see figure
2
). The overall POPO loss function is:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{POPO}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\sum_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)}w_{\theta}(y\mid x)\cdot\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\right]+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\text{sim}}(\theta,\phi,\xi)+\beta\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}}(\theta),

(5)

where
w_{\theta}(y\mid x)
is an importance weight over the positive set;
\mathcal{L}_{\text{sim}}(\theta,\phi,\xi)
provides representation-space regularization, and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}}(\theta)
is an entropy bonus encouraging exploration. The coefficients
\alpha,\beta>0
control regularization strength. The key distinction from standard policy gradient methods is that the expectation is taken
only
over responses in
\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)
(rejected sampling as hard advantages). Although negative responses receive no explicit gradient signal, we show that the implicit negative gradient exists in section
3.3
.

Self-Normalized Weight over Positive Set.

POPO uses bounded and re-normalized weights over the positive set. Intuitively, this scheme creates a
self-competition
situation, where responses assigned a high probability receive larger weights. Thus, we reinforce the “confident correct answers” more strongly while maintaining diversity through normalization. It balances between uniform weighting (when all positives are equal) and concentrated weighting (when one positive dominates).

w_{\theta}(y\mid x)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{Z^{+}(x)},\quad\text{where}\quad Z^{+}(x)=\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)}\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x).

(6)

Adaptive Anchor.

Methods like BYOL
(
14
)
and SimSiam
(
5
)
achieve positive examples only through contrastive learning. POPO adopts this innovative design in principle. Basically, we maintain two copies of the policy: the policy network
\pi_{\theta}
with parameters
\theta
that we optimize, and a siamese policy
\pi_{\xi}
with parameters
\xi
that provides a stable anchor. Siamese policy parameters are updated using an exponential moving average (EMA) of the policy network parameter:

\xi\leftarrow\tau\cdot\xi+(1-\tau)\cdot\theta,

(7)

where
\tau\in(0,1)
is the momentum coefficient, e.g.,
\tau=0.999
.

Representation-Space Alignment.

With the above Siamese network architecture, we next define how the policy network should align with the Siamese network. Conventional
KL
divergence in probability space suffers from high variance, token-level sensitivity, and rigid point-wise constraints
(
21
)
. Thus, our POPO aligns the hidden representation within the semantic embedding space.

\mathcal{L}_{\text{sim}}(\theta,\phi,\xi)=-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\sum_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)}w_{\theta}(y\mid x)\cdot\mathrm{cos}\bigl(h_{\phi}(f_{\theta}(x,y)),\;\mathrm{sg}(f_{\xi}(x,y)+\epsilon)\bigr)\right],

(8)

where
f_{\theta}(x,y)
and
f_{\xi}(x,y)
are representations extracted from the both networks, respectively;
h_{\phi}
is a predictor head (implemented as MLPs),
\mathrm{sg}(\cdot)
denotes stop-gradient, and
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I)
is Gaussian noise. Specifically,
Predictor head.
Predictor
h_{\phi}
on the policy network is an asymmetrical design to ensure that the policy network must do “extra work” to match the Siamese network, preventing the collapse where both networks converge to outputs;
Stop-gradient.
The
\mathrm{sg}(\cdot)
operator ensures gradients flow only through the policy network. The Siamese network is updated solely via EMA;
Gaussian noise.
Adding small noise
\epsilon
provides robustness against trivial embeddings.

Entropy Regularization.

Like the recent approach
(
38
)
, our POPO also includes the entropy loss to ensure diversity:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[H(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x))\right].\ \text{where}\ H(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x))=-\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x).

(9)

3.3
Learning via Implicit Negative Reward

We answer the central question:
why POPO is effective without explicit penalties on the negatives?
via the weight probability redistribution, bounded divergence, and asymmetric learning.

Implicit Negative Gradients via Weight Probability Redistribution.

The fundamental constraint
\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)=1
implies the intuition that when POPO reinforces
\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)
for
y\in\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)
, softmax normalization implicitly forces
\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)
to decrease for
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}(x)
. Thus, we analyze the
gradient
of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{POPO}}
at the output logit level. Let
z_{y}
denote the logit (pre-softmax score) for response
y
, i.e.,
\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)=\exp(z_{y})/\sum_{y^{\prime\prime}}\exp(z_{y^{\prime\prime}})
.

Theorem 3.1

(Implicit Negative Gradient)
.

For any incorrect response
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}(x)
, the gradient of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{POPO}}
(
5
) with respect to the logit
z_{y^{\prime}}
satisfies

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{POPO}}}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}\;=\;\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\Big[1+\beta\Big(\!\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)+H\!\left(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\right)\!\Big)\Big].

(10)

This gradient is strictly positive when
\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\;>\;\exp\!\Big({-\tfrac{1}{\beta}-H(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x))}\Big)
. Simply speaking, the gradient decomposes into two reinforcing forces: a uniform “probability tax” from the NLL loss on positives (via the softmax partition function), and a probability-dependent penalty from entropy regularization that is strongest on the most probable incorrect responses. The full proof is given in Appendix
B.1
.

Stability through Bounded Gap and Asymmetric Architecture.

Having established the theorem of implicit negative gradients, we next show a lemma that stabilizes the asymmetric policy training. The EMA anchoring and similarity regularization jointly prevent unbounded policy drift, avoiding the pathological gradient explosions.

Lemma 3.2

(EMA-Bounded Parameter gap)
.

Under POPO updates with EMA momentum
\tau\in(0,1)
, learning rate
\eta>0
, and gradient norm bound
\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{POPO}}\|\leq G_{\max}
, the parameter divergence between the policy network
\theta_{t}
and the Simease network
\xi_{t}
satisfies at steady state:

\|\theta_{t}-\xi_{t}\|\;\leq\;\frac{\tau\,\eta\,G_{\max}}{1-\tau}.

(11)

This lemma guarantees the bounded gap even though we do not have the conventional
KL
divergence (see proof in Appendix
B.2
). Further, the asymmetric network architecture ensures that the policy network must actively align with a slowly evolving average rather than collapsing to a degenerate solution. Indeed, in symmetric architectures, training only on positive risks may cause self-distill SFT collapse
(
28
)
. Our POPO breaks symmetry through the above mechanisms.

Discussion and Conclusion

We propose the POPO framework that enables effective post-training RL via only positive rollouts. In recent RLVR research, we also note that POPO shares a similar spirit with recent works. For example,
(
9
)
uses a contrastive head to align reasoning paths within the semantic space.
(
51
)
applies the teacher-student architecture for reward extrapolation in on-policy distillation. However, POPO is unique in combining innovative components, including bounded importance sampling, adaptive EMA anchoring, and representation-space alignment. Further, we also provide mathematical proofs beyond the heuristic intuition. We test POPO using well-established open-source base LLM models on public mathematical reasoning benchmarks. Our results illustrate the potential implications of POPO in future RLVR. However, our work has a few limitations. First, we design POPO for sparse binary rewards, yet many practical scenarios are directly involved with dense reward signals
(
39
;
8
)
. We will extend our POPO by introducing an adaptive rollout partition mechanism for better utilizing negative rollouts. Second, POPO is validated only on text-only LLMs with purely mathematical reasoning benchmarks. We will extend POPO to other domains such as code generation tasks
(
25
)
, agentic RL
(
10
)
, and multimodal reasoning
(
33
)
. Third, we only test POPO up to 7B models. We will scale it up to 32B or 70B
(
50
)
in the future.

Appendix B
Proofs of Theoretical Results

B.1
Proof of Theorem
3.1
: Implicit Negative Gradient

Proof.

We analyze the gradient of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{POPO}}
with respect to the logit
z_{y^{\prime}}
for an incorrect response
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}(x)
. Recall the POPO loss in the main text equation (
5
):

\mathcal{L}_{\text{POPO}}(\theta)=\underbrace{-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)}w_{\theta}(y\mid x)\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}}+\alpha\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{sim}}(\theta,\phi,\xi)+\beta\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}}(\theta).

(1)

We compute the gradient contribution of each component separately. Throughout, we work with a single prompt
x
and drop the conditioning for brevity.

Step 1: Softmax preliminary.

Under the softmax parameterization
\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)=\exp(z_{y})/\sum_{y^{\prime\prime}}\exp(z_{y^{\prime\prime}})
, the standard softmax Jacobian gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\bigl(\delta_{y,y^{\prime}}-\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\bigr),

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{y,y^{\prime}}-\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x),

(3)

where
\delta_{y,y^{\prime}}
is the Kronecker delta. Since
y\in\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)
and
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}(x)
are in disjoint sets, we have
\delta_{y,y^{\prime}}=0
, and thus

\frac{\partial\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}=-\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\quad\text{for all }y\in\mathcal{S}^{+}(x).

(4)

Step 2: Weight invariance for negative logits.

The bounded importance weights:
w_{\theta}(y\mid x)=\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)/Z^{+}(x)
where
Z^{+}(x)=\sum_{y^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)}\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime\prime}\mid x)
. We show that these weights are invariant to perturbations of negative logits. For
y\in\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)
and
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}(x)
, by the quotient rule:

\frac{\partial w_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}=\frac{1}{Z^{+}}\frac{\partial\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}-\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{(Z^{+})^{2}}\frac{\partial Z^{+}}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}.

(5)

From (
2
) with
y\neq y^{\prime}
:

\frac{\partial\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}=-\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x).

(6)

Summing over the positive set:

\frac{\partial Z^{+}}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}=\sum_{y^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)}\frac{\partial\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime\prime}\mid x)}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}=-\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\sum_{y^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)}\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime\prime}\mid x)=-\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\,Z^{+}.

(7)

Substituting back into (
5
):

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)}{Z^{+}}-\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{(Z^{+})^{2}}\bigl(-\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\,Z^{+}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{-\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)}{Z^{+}}+\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)}{Z^{+}}\;=\;0.

(8)

This invariance has a clear interpretation: rescaling all probabilities by the same factor (as the softmax does when a single logit changes) leaves the
ratio

\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)/Z^{+}
unchanged. Crucially, this means the weight normalization over
\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)
does not attenuate the implicit gradient on negatives.

Step 3: NLL gradient on negative logits.

Applying the product rule and using (
8
):

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)}\left[\frac{\partial w_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}\cdot\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)+w_{\theta}(y\mid x)\cdot\frac{\partial\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}\right]

\displaystyle=-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)}w_{\theta}(y\mid x)\cdot\bigl(-\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\sum_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)}w_{\theta}(y\mid x)\;=\;\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x),

(9)

where the last equality uses
\sum_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)}w_{\theta}(y\mid x)=1
. This term is unconditionally positive: gradient descent on the NLL loss alone already decreases the probability of every incorrect response.

Step 4: Entropy gradient on negative logits.

The entropy loss is
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}}=-H(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x))=\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)
. Its gradient with respect to
z_{y^{\prime}}
is:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}}}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}\frac{\partial\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}\bigl(1+\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\bigl(\delta_{y,y^{\prime}}-\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\bigr)\bigl(1+\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\Bigl[\bigl(1+\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\bigr)-\underbrace{\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\bigl(1+\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\bigr)}_{=\;1-H(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x))}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\bigl[\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)+H(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x))\bigr].

(10)

Step 5: Similarity loss does not contribute at the logit level.

The similarity loss in the main text equation (
8
) depends on the negative logit
z_{y^{\prime}}
only through the weights
w_{\theta}(y\mid x)
. By Step 2,
\partial w_{\theta}(y\mid x)/\partial z_{y^{\prime}}=0
, and the cosine similarity term involves hidden representations
f_{\theta}(x,y)
for
y\in\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)
, which are computed from intermediate layers and do not depend on the output logit
z_{y^{\prime}}
. Therefore,
\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{sim}}/\partial z_{y^{\prime}}=0
.

Step 6: Combining and sign analysis.

Summing the contributions from Steps 3–5:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{POPO}}}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}+\beta\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}}}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}+\alpha\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{sim}}}{\partial z_{y^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)+\beta\,\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\bigl[\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)+H(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x))\bigr]+0

\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\Big[1+\beta\bigl(\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)+H(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x))\bigr)\Big].

(11)

Since
\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)>0
, the sign is determined by the bracketed factor. Setting it to zero:

1+\beta\bigl(\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)+H\bigr)=0\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)=\exp\!\Big({-\tfrac{1}{\beta}-H}\Big).

(12)

The gradient is strictly positive whenever
\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)
exceeds this threshold. In practice, this condition is typically satisfied (as
\exp\!\Big({-\tfrac{1}{\beta}-H}\Big)
is close to
0
) for any response with non-negligible probability, which completes the proof.
∎

The proof reveals two reinforcing forces that drive the implicit negative gradient. The first force originates from the weighted NLL loss on positives: through the softmax partition function, reinforcing correct responses imposes a uniform “probability tax” of
\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)
on every incorrect logit. Notably, the bounded importance weights
w_{\theta}(y\mid x)
do not attenuate this force (as shown in Step 2,
\partial w_{\theta}(y\mid x)/\partial z_{y^{\prime}}=0
for all
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}(x)
), so the weight normalization over
\mathcal{S}^{+}(x)
passes through without affecting the implicit gradient on negatives. The second force arises from the entropy regularization equation (
10
), which adds the strongest suppression (as the penalty) on the most probable incorrect responses.

B.2
Proof of Lemma
3.2
(EMA-Bounded Parameter gap)

Proof.

We define the parameter gap
\delta_{t}=\theta_{t}-\xi_{t}
, which helps to build a recurrence relation using the POPO update rules.

Step 1: Parameter gap recurrence.

At iteration
t
, the online parameters are updated via gradient descent:

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta\,g_{t},\quad\text{where }g_{t}=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{POPO}}(\theta_{t})\text{ with }\|g_{t}\|\leq G_{\max}.

(13)

The target parameters are updated via EMA (
7
):

\xi_{t+1}=\tau\,\xi_{t}+(1-\tau)\,\theta_{t+1}.

(14)

Subtracting (
14
) from (
13
):

\displaystyle\delta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\theta_{t+1}-\xi_{t+1}=\theta_{t+1}-\tau\,\xi_{t}-(1-\tau)\,\theta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\tau\,\theta_{t+1}-\tau\,\xi_{t}=\tau\bigl(\theta_{t+1}-\xi_{t}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\tau\bigl(\theta_{t}-\eta\,g_{t}-\xi_{t}\bigr)=\tau\,\delta_{t}-\tau\,\eta\,g_{t}.

(15)

Step 2: Norm bound via contraction.

Taking norms in (
15
) and applying the triangle inequality:

\|\delta_{t+1}\|\leq\tau\,\|\delta_{t}\|+\tau\,\eta\,G_{\max}.

(16)

This is a contractive recurrence with rate
\tau<1
and constant driving term
\tau\,\eta\,G_{\max}
.

Step 3: Steady-state bound.

Starting from
\delta_{0}=\theta_{0}-\xi_{0}=0
(since
\xi_{0}=\theta_{0}
), we unroll (
16
):

\|\delta_{t}\|\leq\tau\,\eta\,G_{\max}\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\tau^{k}=\tau\,\eta\,G_{\max}\cdot\frac{1-\tau^{t}}{1-\tau}.

(17)

Taking
t\to\infty
:

\|\delta_{t}\|\leq\frac{\tau\,\eta\,G_{\max}}{1-\tau},

(18)

which establishes the claimed bound (
11
). As a concrete example, for
\tau=0.999
,
\eta=1\times 10^{-6}
, and
G_{\max}=1
, the bound gives
\|\delta\|<10^{-3}
, confirming typical bounded gap throughout training.
∎

Lemma
3.2
bounds the parameter gap. In practice, the similarity loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{sim}}
also provides a second restoring force: its gradient component
\alpha\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{sim}}
pushes
\theta
back toward
\xi
in representation space, further reducing the effective divergence. The two mechanisms are complementary. When
\tau
is close to
1
(strong momentum anchoring), the EMA alone provides tight control and
\alpha
can be set small. Conversely, when
\tau
is smaller (faster target adaptation), a larger
\alpha
compensates by strengthening the representation-space tether.
