Introduction and main results

It is well known that the algebraic structure of Abelian integrals and the associated Picard-Fuchs equations
play an important role in the analysis of limit cycle bifurcations in (smooth) polynomial near-Hamiltonian systems.
The algebraic structure of Abelian integrals provides an efficient way to simplify Abelian integrals
as linear combinations of a finite number of generating functions.
By the Picard-Fuchs equations of generating functions we can study the qualitative property of Abelian integrals
to estimate the number of zeros of some Melnikov functions, see for example
[
1
,
12
,
13
,
19
]
and references therein.

Recently, the method of Picard-Fuchs equations has been applied in the study of limit cycles
for some piecewise smooth near-Hamiltonian systems. For example see
[
15
,
18
]
,
where homogeneous Picard-Fuchs equations are obtained for a separation line fixed.
Then a question arises naturally: How far can the method of Picard-Fuchs equations be extended
in piecewise smooth near-Hamiltonian systems?

1.1
Inhomogeneous Picard-Fuchs equations

In this paper, we shall firstly derive Picard-Fuchs equations for
piecewise smooth polynomial perturbations of planar Hamiltonian systems
with Hamiltonians
H(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}y^{2}+F(x)
, where
F(x)
is a polynomial of degree
n
.
Take the straight line
L(x,y,\theta)=0
as the separation line, where

L(x,y,\theta)=\sin(\theta)(x-c)-\cos(\theta)y,\quad 0<\theta\leq\pi,

and
c
is a constant. Then the perturbed Hamiltonian system can be written as

\begin{split}(\dot{x},\dot{y})=\left\{\begin{aligned} (H_{y}(x,y)+\varepsilon P^{+}(x,y),\,\,&-H_{x}(x,y)+\varepsilon Q^{+}(x,y)),\quad(x,y)\in\Sigma^{+},\\[4.30554pt]
(H_{y}(x,y)+\varepsilon P^{-}(x,y),\,\,&-H_{x}(x,y)+\varepsilon Q^{-}(x,y)),\quad(x,y)\in\Sigma^{-},\end{aligned}\right.\end{split}

(1)

where
|\varepsilon|\ll 1
,
\Sigma^{\pm}=\{(x,y)|\pm L(x,y,\theta)>0\}
,

P^{\pm}(x,y)
and
Q^{\pm}(x,y)
are polynomials in
(x,y)
.
Suppose that the unperturbed system (
1
)
|_{\varepsilon=0}
has a continuous period annulus

\mathcal{A}=\{\Gamma_{h}|h\in(\alpha,\beta)\}
, where
\Gamma_{h}\subset H^{-1}(h)
is a periodic orbit.
Further, we suppose that the separation line of (
1
) splits
\Gamma_{h}
into two nonempty connected parts

\Gamma_{h}^{\pm}=\Gamma_{h}\cap\Sigma^{\pm}
.

By
[
10

the number of limit cycles produced from

in system (

can be estimated by the number of isolated zeros of Melnikov function

\widetilde{M}(h)=\,\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{-}}Q^{-}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x-P^{-}(x,y)\mathrm{d}y+\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{+}}Q^{+}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x-P^{+}(x,y)\mathrm{d}y

As we can see from (

Before we present the Picard-Fuchs equations for these integrals, we should give a group of generating functions for

Theorem 1.1
.

Let

Theorem

,
I_{i,1}^{+}(h)

\widetilde{K}_{i}(h)
,

.
By

1.2
Asymptotic expansion of Melnikov functions

Next, we will discuss how to apply Picard-Fuchs equations (
5
)
to study bifurcations of limit cycles in system (
1

For Poincaré bifurcation of the period annulus
\mathcal{A}

the explicit expressions of
x_{i}(h)
and
y_{i}(h)

L(x_{i},y_{i},\theta)=0,\quad H(x_{i},y_{i})=h,\quad h\in(\alpha,\beta),

(7)

by equations (

Note that if the

y_{1}(h)=y_{2}(h)\equiv 0

\mbox{\boldmath$K$}(h)=\mbox{\boldmath$J$}(h)\equiv 0

Then Picard-Fuchs equations (
5

and can be used for Poincaré bifurcation just like for smooth polynomial perturbations,

It is more feasible to derive the asymptotic expansions of

and

at

from (

), where
\alpha\leq h_{0}\leq\beta

which makes it possible for us to derive the asymptotic expansion of

Therefore, we can study isolated zeros of

to determine limit cycles bifurcating near

in system (
1
),
where
\Gamma_{h_{0}}
could be a center or a homoclinic/heteroclinic loop.

For homoclinic or heteroclinic bifurcation of limit cycles in piecewise smooth near-Hamiltonian systems,
there are some papers about the compuation of the corresponding asymptotic expansions of Melnikov functions
with a fixed separation line (see
[
8
,
9
,
11
,
16
,
17
]
for example),
where formulas are given only for the first few coefficients.

Assume that
\Gamma_{\beta}
represents a homoclinic loop for

We can apply Theorems
1.1
and
1.2

to recursively compute the coefficients of the asymptotic expansion of

near

.
The method is illustrated in Section

It is worthy to mention that this method can be extented to the cases
where
\Gamma_{h_{0}}
is a center or a heteroclinic loop of (

)
|_{\varepsilon=0}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let (
14

Proof.

(ii) Because

On the other hand, by Green’s Theorem we have

(17)

Then substituting (

(iii) Because

Then eliminating

Taking

∎

3
Asymptotic expansion of Abelian integrals

In this section, we suppose that
\Gamma_{\beta}
is a homoclinic loop of the unperturbed system (
1
)
|_{\varepsilon=0}
.
We shall discuss the method of applying Picard-Fuchs equations
to compute the asymptotic expansion of Melnikov function

\widetilde{M}(h)
near
\Gamma_{\beta}
for the piecewise smooth system (
1
)
and how to determine the maximum number of isolated zeros of
\widetilde{M}(h)
for
0<\beta-h\ll 1
.
As an illustration, we study the asymptotic expansion of Melnikov function near a homoclinic hoop
for system (
12
).

3.1
Methodology

In order to compute the asymptotic expansion of
\widetilde{M}(h)

near
\Gamma_{\beta}
for system (
1
),
by (
4
) we only need to study the asymptotic expansions of integrals
I_{i,1}(h)
,
I_{i,1}^{+}(h)

and
\widetilde{K}_{i}(h)
for
0\leq\beta-h\ll 1
,
i=0,1,\ldots
,
n-2
.

By (
3
) we can see that the asymptotic expansion of
\widetilde{K}_{i}(h)

requires the corresponding asymptotic expansions of
x_{j}(h)
and
y_{j}(h)
for
0\leq\beta-h\ll 1
,
where points
(x_{j}(h),y_{j}(h))
,
j=1,2
, satisfy
H(x_{j}(h),y_{j}(h))=h

and
L(x_{j}(h),y_{j}(h),\theta)=0
.
Using the implicit function theorem, we can see that
x_{j}(h)
and
y_{j}(h)

are analytic functions in
|h-\beta|^{\frac{1}{p}}
,
where
p
is a positive integer depending on
H
and the value of
\theta
.
Therefore, the variation of the value of
\theta
for the separation line
may lead to changes in the form of the asymptotic expansion of
\widetilde{M}(h)
near
\Gamma_{\beta}

through integrals
\widetilde{K}_{i}(h)
.
See the next section for example.

For intergrals
I_{i,1}(h)
, we can use Picard-Fuchs equations (
20
) to compute their asymptotic expansions near
h=\beta
.
Note that (
20
) can be rewritten as

P(h)\mbox{\boldmath$X$}^{\prime}(h)=\mbox{\boldmath$T$}(h)\mbox{\boldmath$X$}(h),\quad h\in(\alpha,\beta),

(38)

where
P(h)={\rm det}\,(\mbox{\boldmath$T$}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$T$}_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$T$}_{4}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$T$}_{3})

is a polynomial in
h
of degree at most
n-1

satisfying
P(\beta)=0
,
and
\mbox{\boldmath$T$}(h)
is a matrix whose entries are polynomials in
h
of degree at most
n-2
.
Note system (
38
) can be well-defined at
h=\beta
.
Then we can use (
38
) to compute the asymptotic expansion of
\mbox{\boldmath$X$}(h)
near
h=\beta
with
undetermined coefficients. For the form of the asymptotic expansion of
\mbox{\boldmath$X$}(h)
, see
[
6
]

for a homoclinic loop passing through a hyperbolic saddle, a cusp or a nilpotent saddle.

For the asymptotic expansions of integrals
I_{i,1}^{+}(h)
,
we can rewrite (
5
) into the following form

P(h)\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{\prime}(h)=\mbox{\boldmath$T$}(h)\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}(h)+P(h)\mbox{\boldmath$J$}(h)-\mbox{\boldmath$S$}(h)\mbox{\boldmath$K$}(h),

(39)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$S$}(h)
is also a matrix polynomial in
h
of degree at most
n-2
.
The asymptotic expansions of
J
(h)
and
K
(h)
for
0<\beta-h\ll 1
can be obtained by using the corresponding asymptotic expansions of
x_{j}(h)
and

y_{j}(h)
,
j=1,2
. Then we can use (
39
) to compute the asymptotic expansion of
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}(h)
for
0\leq\beta-h\ll 1
.

It follows that
2\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}(h)={\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}(h)
for
\theta=\pi

because of the symmetry of
\Gamma_{h}
with respect to the
x
-axis.
Apparently, Picard-Fuchs equations (
39
) become into (
38
) in this case.

Without loss of generality, for
0<\theta<\pi

we assume that
\Gamma_{\beta}^{+}
does not
contain a singular point of the unperturbed system of (

Then
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}(h)
is analytic at

for

\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}(h)=\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$c$}}_{0}+\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$c$}}_{1}(h-\beta)+\cdots+\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$c$}}_{j}(h-\beta)^{j}+\cdots,\quad 0\leq\beta-h\ll 1,

(40)

where

The remaining coefficients can be obtained by substituting (

Remark 3.1
.

The method above is applicable to the case of
\Gamma_{\beta}
as a heteroclinic loop.
In this case
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}(h)
may not be analytic near

even for

could probably

In order to compute the asymptotic expansion of
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}(h)
near

when we vary the value of

At the end of this subsection, we shall present a result on determining the maximum number of isolated zeros of
\widetilde{M}(h)

for
0<\beta-h\ll 1
by using the coefficients of its corresponding asymptotic expansion. Because the asymptotic expansion of
\widetilde{M}(h)

near the homoclinic loop

(for example see Subsection

),
it is necessary for us to consider the asymptotic expansion of Melnikov functions in a general form.

Suppose that Melnikov function

can be expanded into the following form

M(h,\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$})=c_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$})+\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{l=1}^{p+1}c_{pj+j+l}(\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$})g_{l}(h)h^{j}

for

\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

g_{l}(h)\in\Big\{|h|^{\frac{q}{p}},\,|h|^{\frac{q+1}{p}},\,\ldots,\,|h|^{\frac{q+p-1}{p}},\,h\ln|h|\Big\},\quad 1\leq q\leq p

for some

and

To determine the maximum number of isolated zeros of

for
0<-h\ll 1

Theorem 3.7
.

Let (
41
) and (
42
) hold.
Let each coefficient

in (

\delta
.
Suppose that there exist
k

, such that for any coefficient
c_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$})

if
j\not\in\{i_{1},\,i_{2},\,\ldots,\,i_{k}\}\triangleq S_{k}
, we have

c_{j}=O(|c_{i_{1}},\,c_{i_{2}},\,\ldots,\,c_{i_{j}^{\ast}}|),

(43)

where
i_{j}^{\ast}=\max\{i_{s}\in S_{k}|i_{s}<j\}
, and particularly if
1\leq j<i_{1}
, then
c_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$})\equiv 0
for all
\delta
.
If further

\mathrm{rank}\,\frac{\partial(c_{i_{1}},\,c_{i_{2}},\,\ldots,\,c_{i_{k}})}{\partial(\delta_{1},\,\delta_{2},\,\ldots,\,\delta_{m})}=k,

(44)

then for any compact set

, when

there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that
M(h,\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$})
has at most

isolated zeros (counting multiplicity)
in
0<-h<\varepsilon_{0}
for
\delta

\in D
. Furthermore,
M(h,\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$})
can
have
k-1
isolated simple zeros in an arbitrary neighborhood of

for
h<0
and some
\delta
.

Remark 3.2
.

By
[
6
]
, for analytic smooth near-Hamiltonian systems near a homoclinic or heteroclinic loop passing through hyperbolic saddles, cusps or nilpotent saddles,
Melnikov function can be expanded into the form in (

3.7
can be also applied for the cases mentioned above.

Using the idea presented in the proof of Theorem 2.3.2 in
[
6
]
, we can prove the above theorem.
For convenience of the reader, we present the proof below.

Proof.

By (
43
) and (
44
) for any
j\not\in S_{k}
,
c_{j}
should be a linear combination of
c_{i_{1}}
,

c_{i_{2}}
,
\ldots
,
c_{i^{\ast}_{j}}
, and we can take
\Lambda
=(c_{i_{1}},\,c_{i_{2}},\,\ldots,\,c_{i_{k}})

as a free parameter vector. Then
M(h,\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$})
can be written as

M(h,\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$})\triangleq\widehat{M}(h,\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$})
, which is linear in
\Lambda
.
Then
\widehat{M}(h,\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$})
can be expanded as

\widehat{M}(h,\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$})=c_{i_{1}}f_{1}(h)+c_{i_{2}}f_{2}(h)+\cdots+c_{i_{k}}f_{k}(h),

(45)

where
f_{s}(h)\in C^{\infty}
,
1\leq s\leq k
. Comparing (
45
) with (

for
i_{1}=0
and

f_{s}(h)\sim h^{j_{s}}g_{l_{s}}(h)

1\leq l_{s}=i_{s}-Nj_{s}\leq N
. Then there exists a small interval
(\beta,0)
such that

,
h\in(\beta,0)
for any
1\leq s\leq k
.

We rewrite
\widehat{M}(h,\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$})
as
\widehat{M}(h,\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$})=f_{1}(h)M_{1}(h)
, where

M_{1}(h)=c_{i_{1}}+c_{i_{2}}f_{2,1}(h)+\cdots+c_{i_{k}}f_{k,1}(h),

with
f_{s,1}(h)=f_{s}(h)/f_{1}(h)\in C^{\infty}

f_{s,1}(h)=o(f_{s-1,1}(h))
.
Then
\widehat{M}(h,\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$})
has at most

zeros (counting multiplicity) for

has at most
k-1
zeros (counting multiplicity) for
0<-h\ll 1
. It suffices to prove that

Note that

can be written as

\frac{\mathrm{d}M_{1}(h)}{\mathrm{d}h}=f^{\prime}_{2,1}(h)M_{2}(h)

M_{2}(h)=c_{i_{2}}+c_{i_{3}}f_{3,2}(h)+\cdots+c_{i_{k}}f_{k,2}(h),

where

and
f_{s,2}(h)=o(f_{s-1,2}(h))
.
Because

for

negative and sufficiently close to

\frac{\mathrm{d}M_{1}(h)}{\mathrm{d}h}

has at most

zeros (counting multiplicity)

has at most
k-2
zeros (counting multiplicity) for
0<-h\ll 1

Since the functions
M_{1}(h)
and
M_{2}(h)
have the same form,
using mathematical induction on

\widehat{M}(h,\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$})
has most
k-1
zeros (counting multiplicity)
in
0<-h\ll 1
for
\delta

\in D
