Clinicopathological Features and Prognostic Impact of Colorectal Signet-Ring Cell Carcinoma: The Prognostic Impact of Metastatic Lymph Node Histology
Authors: Gen Tsujio, Yusuke Tanaka, Akio Shiomi, Shoichi Manabe, Shunsuke Kasai, Tadahiro Kojima, Takahiro Igaki, Yukihiro Mori, Takuma Oishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives: Colorectal signet-ring cell carcinoma (SRCC) is a rare histological subtype, accounting for only 0.2-0.9% of all colorectal cancers, and is often diagnosed at an advanced stage with a poor prognosis. However, the clinicopathological and prognostic characteristics of colorectal cancers with SRCC components remain poorly defined. Methods: We retrospectively analyzed 35 patients who underwent surgical resection for primary colorectal cancer containing SRCC components between September 2002 and December 2022 at the Shizuoka Cancer Center. The patients were divided into two groups according to the predominant histological subtype: SRCC (n = 7) and adenocarcinoma with a signet-ring cell component (AC with SRC; n = 28). Clinicopathological features and survival outcomes were compared between the groups. Among the patients with lymph node metastasis, survival was compared according to the presence of SRCC in the metastatic lymph nodes (LNM SRCC+, n = 13; LNM SRCC-, n = 13). Results: Of the 35 patients, 7 were classified as having SRCC and 28 as having AC with SRC. Most cases were T3 or deeper tumors (85.7%), with frequent lymphatic invasion (82.9%) and lymph node metastasis (74.3%). The prognosis did not differ between SRCC and AC with SRC, whereas the presence of SRCC in metastatic lymph nodes was associated with significantly poorer overall and relapse-free survival (P = 0.048 and 0.012, respectively). Conclusions: Colorectal cancers with SRCC components are typically advanced and aggressive. The presence of SRCC components confers a poor prognosis, and SRCC in metastatic lymph nodes represents an important adverse prognostic indicator. Keywords: colorectal cancer, signet-ring cell carcinoma, lymph node metastasis

Introduction

Most colorectal cancers are well or moderately differentiated adenocarcinomas, whereas other histological subtypes are rare; colorectal signet-ring cell carcinoma (SRCC) accounts for only 0.2-0.9% of cases, making it an extremely uncommon entity[1-3]. Histologically, SRCC is characterized by intracytoplasmic mucin that displaces the nucleus, although its morphology is heterogeneous, and extracellular mucin production may also be observed[4,5]. SRCC is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage and is associated with an extremely poor prognosis[3,6]. Nevertheless, because of its rarity, comprehensive reports are limited, and the clinicopathological features and prognostic implications of colorectal SRCC remain poorly defined.

The diagnosis of colorectal SRCC generally requires that signet-ring cells account for approximately half or more of the tumor; however, even when the proportion of signet-ring cells is below this threshold, tumors containing an SRCC component have been reported to share similar clinicopathological characteristics and a poor prognosis with SRCC[4,7-9].

Furthermore, recent studies have suggested that the histological subtype of metastatic lymph nodes may be associated with patient outcomes[10-13]. Given these observations, we considered that the presence of SRCC in metastatic lymph nodes, compared with those without SRCC, might be particularly associated with an unfavorable prognosis.

Several studies have also reported that, similar to mucinous adenocarcinoma, SRCC exhibits a relatively high frequency of microsatellite instability-high (MSI-high) status[14-17]. Patients with MSI-high/deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) colorectal cancer generally have a favorable prognosis; however, it remains unclear whether this favorable trend also applies to SRCC[18-22].

Therefore, this study aimed to clarify the clinicopathological characteristics of colorectal cancer containing a component of SRCC and to investigate the prognostic impact of the predominant histological subtype, the presence of SRCC in metastatic lymph nodes, and MMR/MSI status.

Methods

Patients and study design

Between September 2002 and December 2022, a total of 6967 patients underwent surgical resection for primary colorectal cancer at the Shizuoka Cancer Center. Of these, 35 patients (0.5%) whose tumors contained a component of SRCC were retrospectively included in this study. Patients were excluded if they (1) had multiple colorectal cancers or (2) had synchronous malignancies. Patients with distant metastases or those who received preoperative therapy were not excluded from the study.

The following clinical and surgical data were collected from electronic medical records: age, sex, tumor location, blood test results, preoperative therapy, postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy, tumor size, macroscopic type, and pathological findings. The histological diagnosis was based on World Health Organization criteria. Pathological staging was performed according to the 8th edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) classification. The cecum, ascending colon, and transverse colon were classified as right-sided. The descending and sigmoid colons were classified as the left-sided colons. Residual tumor (R) classification was defined as follows: R0, no residual tumor; R1, microscopic residual tumor; and R2, macroscopic residual tumor[23]. Genetic analyses, including MMR/MSI status and KRAS, NRAS, and BRAF mutation statuses, were performed as part of routine clinical practice at the discretion of the treating physician.

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Shizuoka Cancer Center (approval no. J2025-25-2025-1-3) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective study design, and all data were anonymized prior to analysis.

Pathological diagnosis for SRCC in primary tumors and lymph node metastases

Colorectal SRCC was diagnosed when all the following criteria were fulfilled: (1) the presence of tumor cells showing typical signet-ring cell morphology; (2) signet-ring cells account for approximately ≥ 50% of all tumors; (3) poorly defined glandular formation; (4) mucinous areas accounting for ≤ 50% of the tumor; and (5) exclusion of metastasis from SRCC originating in other organs, particularly gastric cancer[4,7](Figure 1). Cases that contained, but did not predominantly consist of, signet-ring cell components were classified as colorectal adenocarcinomas with a signet-ring cell component (AC with SRC) (Figure 1).

Patients with lymph node metastasis were divided into two groups: lymph node metastasis with SRCC (LNM SRCC+) and without SRCC (LNM SRCC-). A metastatic lymph node was regarded as SRCC+ if at least one metastatic lymph node fulfilled all of the following criteria: (1) the presence of tumor cells showing typical signet-ring cell morphology; (2) signet-ring cells accounting for approximately ≥ 50% of the tumor cells within the lymph node metastasis; and (3) mucinous areas accounting for ≤ 50% of the metastatic tumor (Figure 1). If none of the metastatic lymph nodes met these criteria, the case was classified as LNM SRCC- (Figure 1).

All histopathological specimens were re-reviewed by a board-certified pathologist from the Japanese Society of Pathology, who was blinded to clinical information and to the original pathology reports.

Surgical procedures

All surgeries were performed by surgeons certified by the Japanese Society of Gastrointestinal Surgery. Minimally invasive surgery (MIS), including laparoscopic and robot-assisted surgery, was performed by surgeons certified by the Japan Society of Endoscopic Surgery. The surgical procedures were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Japanese Society for Cancer of the Colon and Rectum[24].

When combined resection of adjacent organs or reconstruction of the biliary or urinary tract was required, an open surgical approach was selected, whereas MIS was chosen for all other cases. MIS included both laparoscopic and robot-assisted surgeries. Robot-assisted surgery was introduced for rectal cancer in December 2011 and for colon cancer in November 2019.

Patient follow-up

Patients were followed up according to the guidelines of the Japanese Society for Cancer of the Colon and Rectum[24]. Each follow-up visit included a physical examination, routine blood tests, and measurement of tumor markers, including serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen (CA) 19-9. Enhanced CT and colonoscopy were routinely performed during follow-up. Recurrence was diagnosed based on findings from the scheduled examination.

Outcome measurements

Overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) were compared between the SRCC and AC with SRC groups. In addition, survival outcomes were compared between the LNM SRCC+ and LNM SRCC- groups, as well as between the dMMR/MSI-high and pMMR/MSS groups. OS was defined as the time from surgery to death, and RFS was defined as the time from surgery to recurrence or death.

Statistical analysis

OS and RFS were calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and survival curves were compared using the log-rank test. Survival analyses were limited to patients who underwent R0 or R1 resection, whereas those who underwent R2 resection were excluded because of macroscopic residual disease.

Continuous variables were compared using the unpaired Student's t-test, while categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

All statistical analyses were performed using JMP 13 software (SAS Institute Japan, Tokyo, Japan).

Results

Patient characteristics

Among the 35 patients with tumors containing SRCC components, 7 (20.0%) were classified as having SRCC, and 28 (80.0%) were classified as having AC with SRC. The clinicopathological characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. The median age of the patients was 67 years; 22 (62.9%) were male, and 13 (37.1%) were female. The tumor was located in the right-sided colon in 17 cases (48.6%), the left-sided colon in 4 cases (11.4%), and the rectum in 14 cases (40.0%). Surgical curability was classified as R0 in 29 patients (82.9%), R1 in 2 (5.7%), and R2 in 4 (11.4%). Among the AC with SRC cases, mucinous adenocarcinoma was the most common subtype, observed in 15 cases (53.6%). Lymphatic invasion was identified in 29 patients (82.9%). Lymph node metastasis was present in 26 cases (74.3%), including 13 cases (50.0%) with LNM SRCC+ and 13 cases (50.0%) with LNM SRCC-. Among patients with LNM SRCC+, SRCC was identified in multiple metastatic lymph nodes in all cases. The median number of SRCC-positive lymph nodes was 5 (interquartile range [IQR], 4-9.5), and the median proportion of SRCC-positive lymph nodes among all metastatic lymph nodes was 67% (IQR, 63-100%). Among the 12 patients whose tumors were tested for MMR or MSI status, dMMR or MSI-high status was detected in 6 patients (50.0%), all of whom had right-sided colon cancer. Among the 14 patients tested, RAS mutations were detected in 2 patients (14.3%), and among the 8 patients tested, BRAF mutations were detected in 2 patients (25.0%). Distant metastases were identified in 4 patients (11.4%), of whom 3 had peritoneal dissemination.

Survival outcomes

The median follow-up period of all eligible patients was 36 months. Figure 2 shows the OS and RFS rates of the 31 patients who underwent radical resection for colorectal cancer with SRCC. The 3-year OS and RFS rates after curative resection were 66.4% (95% confidence interval [CI], 46.4-80.3%) and 50.6% (95% CI, 31.9-66.6%), respectively.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the clinicopathological characteristics and prognostic factors of colorectal cancer with an SRCC component. Overall, a high proportion of patients presented with advanced disease, with more than half classified as T4. Moreover, the rate of lymphatic invasion was remarkably high, and lymph node metastases were frequently observed. Most patients with distant metastases presented with peritoneal dissemination. These findings are consistent with previous reports of colorectal SRCC, which also demonstrated a tendency toward advanced T stage, high lymphatic invasion, frequent nodal metastasis, and a propensity for peritoneal spread[3,25-29]. Regarding the relationship between clinicopathological characteristics and predominant histological subtype, although not statistically significant, SRCC tended to occur in the rectum, whereas AC with SRC was more frequently observed in the right-sided colon. No other significant associations were identified between the predominant histological subtypes and clinicopathological factors.

In this cohort, the 3-year OS and 3-year RFS were 66.4% and 50.6%, respectively, indicating a generally poor prognosis compared with typical colorectal adenocarcinoma. This finding reflects the aggressive biological behavior of colorectal cancers containing SRCC components. We further classified colorectal cancers containing SRCC components into two groups, SRCC and AC with SRC; however, no significant association was observed between these groups and patient prognosis. Previous studies have reported that the presence of SRCC components is associated with poor prognosis. Our results are consistent with these observations[8,9]. Taken together, our data indicate that the presence of SRCC components is associated with a markedly poor prognosis, whereas the predominant histological subtype may not play a major role in determining the outcomes of colorectal cancers containing SRCC components.

In our analysis, we divided lymph node-positive cases into two groups according to the presence or absence of SRCC in metastatic lymph nodes. Patients with LNM SRCC+ had significantly poorer survival rates than those with LNM SRCC-. Previous studies have demonstrated that the histological features of metastatic lymph nodes are associated with patient outcomes. In stage III colorectal cancer, poorly differentiated carcinoma in the metastatic lymph nodes is associated with poorer outcomes[10]. In node-positive stage IV colorectal cancer, cases in which all metastatic lymph nodes showed tubular-type adenocarcinoma represented a favorable prognostic factor, whereas the presence of any poorly differentiated carcinoma within the metastatic lymph nodes was an unfavorable factor for cancer-specific survival[13]. These findings highlight the prognostic importance of histological features of metastatic lymph nodes. Although no prior studies have specifically addressed SRCC in this context, our results suggest that, similar to poorly differentiated carcinoma, the presence of SRCC in metastatic lymph nodes may represent a critical adverse prognostic factor in colorectal cancer. These findings raise the possibility that patients with LNM SRCC+ may represent a particularly high-risk subgroup. Such patients may benefit from more intensive therapeutic strategies, such as more intensive adjuvant chemotherapy. However, prospective studies are required to confirm these findings and clarify their clinical relevance.

SRCC, like mucinous adenocarcinoma, has been reported to exhibit a relatively high frequency of MSI-high status (approximately 30-40%) in previous studies[14,15,17]. In the present study, we also observed a high rate of dMMR/MSI-high tumors (50.0%), particularly in patients with AC with SRC. Of these, 50% had a mucinous adenocarcinoma component. Patients with dMMR/MSI-high tumors demonstrated significantly better outcomes than those with pMMR/MSS tumors. It is well established that patients with dMMR/MSI-high colorectal cancer generally have a more favorable prognosis than those with pMMR/MSS tumors[18-22]. Our findings suggest that this favorable trend may also apply to colorectal cancers containing SRCC components.

Regarding tumor location, both the right-sided colon and rectum were frequently involved. Although no statistically significant differences were observed, SRCC tended to occur in the rectum, whereas AC with SRC tended to occur in the right-sided colon. In addition, all six cases in which dMMR/MSI-high was detected were located in the right-sided colon, five of which were AC with SRC. Previous reports have suggested that colorectal SRCC can be categorized into two subtypes: one arising in the right-sided colon, associated with MSI-high CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP) positivity and a BRAF mutation, and another arising in the rectum, associated with inflammation-related carcinogenesis[29,30]. Our findings suggest that AC with SRC may correspond to the former subtype, whereas SRCC may correspond to the latter.

This study had several limitations. First, it was a retrospective, single-center study of Japanese patients with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, molecular testing was not performed uniformly in all cases. Third, given the small sample size inherent to the rarity of colorectal SRCC, particularly in node-positive and MSI/MMR-tested subgroups, the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive, and validation in larger multi-institutional cohorts is required. Fourth, because of the limited number of patients, a multivariate analysis to identify independent prognostic factors could not be performed.

In conclusion, colorectal SRCC is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage with lymph node metastasis, and the presence of SRC components is associated with a markedly poor prognosis. Among these cases, those with dMMR/MSI-high tumors may have a better prognosis. However, the presence of SRCC in metastatic lymph nodes appears to be particularly associated with poor outcomes and may serve as an important adverse prognostic factor in colorectal cancer.
