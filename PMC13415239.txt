Hematological Inflammation Indices Are Associated With Active Uveitis in Ankylosing Spondylitis
Authors: Mesude Seda Aydoğdu, Zarife Ekici Gök
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the relationship between active uveitis and hematological inflammation indices, specifically the HALP and CALLY scores, in patients with ankylosing spondylitis.MethodsThis retrospective cross‐sectional study included 140 patients with AS. The patients were divided into two groups based on the presence of uveitis. Laboratory data obtained during active uveitis episodes were used for the uveitis group, while routine follow‐up data were used for the control group. Hematological inflammation indices were calculated and appropriate statistical analyses were performed.ResultsIn patients with uveitis, NLR, PLR, SII, and AISI values were found to be significantly higher, while HALP and CALLY scores were significantly lower (all remaining significant after Benjamini‐Hochberg FDR correction). SIRI and MLR did not retain significance after FDR correction. In the ROC analysis, the HALP and CALLY indices demonstrated moderate discriminatory performance.ConclusionAlthough hematological inflammation indices are associated with the presence of uveitis, they have limited value for diagnostic purposes alone. In clinical practice, however, they may serve as complementary markers for assessing inflammatory burden in patients with AS and may provide supportive information when evaluating active uveitis.Keywords: ankylosing spondylitis, CALLY index, HALP score, inflammatory biomarkers, uveitis

Introduction

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is a chronic immune‐mediated inflammatory disease within the spectrum of radiographic axial spondyloarthritis. It primarily affects the axial skeleton, but is also associated with various extra‐articular manifestations. One of the most common of these is acute anterior uveitis, which affects approximately 20%–30% of patients during the course of the disease and significantly impacts morbidity and quality of life [1, 2].

The pathogenesis of AS is characterised by complex immune dysregulation and persistent systemic inflammation. Therefore, accurately assessing the inflammatory burden is essential for monitoring the disease. Traditional markers such as C‐reactive protein (CRP) and the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) are widely used in clinical practice. However, they may be inadequate because they are not sensitive enough at an individual patient level and do not fully reflect the multidimensional nature of immune activation. This highlights the need for more comprehensive biomarkers that can reflect the presence of inflammation and immune‐metabolic interactions.

In this context, composite inflammation indices derived from routine hematological parameters have received increasing attention in recent years. Markers such as the neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), the platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and the monocyte‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (MLR) have been found to be associated with inflammatory activity in various rheumatological diseases. These markers serve as indicators of the balance between innate and adaptive immune responses [3, 4, 5]. Recently, more comprehensive approaches have been proposed, such as the systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII), the systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), and the aggregate inflammation index (AII). These aim to provide a more comprehensive assessment of systemic inflammatory burden [6, 7]. However, these indices exhibit heterogeneous clinical performance and may fail to adequately reflect the multidimensional nature of systemic inflammation.

The HALP score, which is calculated using hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte and platelet levels, and the CALLY score, which is composed of C‐reactive protein (CRP), albumin and lymphocyte levels, were initially defined as prognostic markers in oncological diseases. In recent years, however, they have emerged as more comprehensive biomarkers that reflect systemic inflammation, immune status and nutritional status [8, 9]. As chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation are key mechanisms in rheumatological diseases, the HALP and CALLY scores could be used as simple, accessible biomarkers to assess disease activity and inflammatory burden in these conditions.

Interest in hematological inflammation indices within the spondyloarthritis spectrum is growing. However, the clinical value of the HALP and CALLY scores in the context of AS, particularly in relation to extra‐articular manifestations such as uveitis, has not been systematically evaluated in the literature. This creates a significant knowledge gap. Identifying simple, cost‐effective biomarkers associated with uveitis could improve our understanding of the systemic inflammatory burden of the disease and aid in clinical risk stratification.

The aim of this study was therefore to evaluate the clinical significance of hematological inflammation indices, particularly the HALP and CALLY scores, in patients with active uveitis and clinically stable patients without uveitis. The objective was to demonstrate the potential clinical value of these composite indices in more accurately reflecting the systemic inflammatory burden associated with uveitis.

Materials and Methods

This study was designed as a retrospective, cross‐sectional study. It was approved by the Health Sciences Research Ethics Committee at Malatya Turgut Özal University Faculty of Medicine (approval no. 2026/54, dated 29 January 2026). It was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients with AS were divided into two groups based on whether or not they had uveitis:

1. Patients with active uveitis.

2. Patients without a history of uveitis and clinically stable.

The study included patients aged ≥ 18 years who were regularly followed up and had been diagnosed with AS according to the Modified New York Criteria, and who presented to the Rheumatology or Ophthalmology outpatient clinic between January 2023 and January 2026. The study included patients with complete baseline clinical and laboratory data used in the primary analyses. Missing data for certain secondary variables, such as HLA‐B27, were noted in the relevant analyses. The patients' demographic characteristics and laboratory findings were recorded.

Patients with active infections, cancer, chronic liver disease, hematological disorders, other autoimmune or inflammatory diseases, or who were taking medications that affect hematological parameters were excluded.

All ophthalmological evaluations were conducted by an experienced ophthalmologist in accordance with the same clinical protocol. Active anterior uveitis was assessed through a biomicroscopic examination of the anterior segment. The presence and severity of anterior chamber cells were graded according to the Standar disation of Uveitis Nomenclature (SUN) criteria. Patients were evaluated for uveitis‐related complications, such as posterior synechiae. A detailed posterior segment examination was also performed. Those in the control group showed no signs of active uveitis, with biomicroscopic and fundus examinations indicating normal inflammation levels.

As this study was retrospective, laboratory data were obtained from the hospital's electronic medical records system. For patients in the uveitis group, blood parameters were obtained during active uveitis episodes. The majority of patients exhibited severe signs during these episodes. For the non‐uveitis group, the most recent measurements obtained during routine clinical follow‐up were used. All blood samples were collected via peripheral venous access, in accordance with standard clinical practice, and transferred to EDTA tubes. Hematological analyses were performed using automated hematology analysers, and biochemical parameters, including albumin and CRP, were measured in the same laboratory using standardised methods. The categorical variables included in the study were summarised using counts and percentages. The normality of the quantitative data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Variables that did not follow a normal distribution were reported as the median (minimum–maximum), while those that did follow a normal distribution were reported as the mean ± standard deviation.

An independent samples t‐test was used to compare two independent groups (with and without uveitis) based on the presence of uveitis for variables that met the normality assumption. For variables that did not meet the normality assumption, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. The Pearson chi‐square test and Yates' corrected chi‐square test were applied to compare categorical variables between groups.

Cohen's d was used for independent samples t‐test results and the rank‐biserial correlation coefficient (r) for Mann–Whitney U test results in effect size calculations. In interpreting Cohen's d, values < 0.20 indicate a negligible effect, 0.20–0.49 a small effect, 0.50–0.79 a moderate effect, and ≥ 0.80 a large effect; for the rank‐biserial r, the criteria of < 0.10 for no effect, 0.10–0.29 for small, 0.30–0.49 for moderate, and ≥ 0.50 for large effects were adopted.

Spearman's rho correlation coefficient was used to examine the direction and strength of the relationship between quantitative variables. The interpretation of the correlation coefficient was based on Alpar's (2012) criteria: 0.00–0.19: no or negligible relationship; 0.20–0.39: weak relationship; 0.40–0.69: moderate relationship; 0.70–0.89: strong relationship; 0.90–1.00: very strong relationship (Table 1).

Results

A total of 140 patients with AS were included in the study. Analysis of the patients' demographic characteristics revealed that 67 (47.9%) were male and 73 (52.1%) were female. The mean age was 44.12 ± 12.48 years. Uveitis was present in 60 patients (42.9%) and absent in 80 (57.1%).

All patients in the active group (100%) included in the study exhibited severe signs of anterior uveitis, including a marked cellular reaction in the anterior chamber and posterior synechiae. Biomicroscopic evaluation revealed a significant inflammatory response in the anterior chamber in all cases, with posterior synechiae present in all patients. Hypopyon was present in 5 patients. During posterior segment examination, no significant posterior segment involvement was observed in most patients, although mild vitreous findings were noted in a few cases. No advanced posterior segment pathologies, such as macular or optic disc oedema, were observed. Ophthalmological evaluations of patients in the control group revealed no signs of active anterior uveitis. The anterior segment examination in this group was normal, and no posterior synechiae were detected. Fundus examinations were evaluated as normal. Visual acuity was found to be lower in the active uveitis group than in the control group due to inflammation (mean 20/40, or 0.30 logMAR). This finding was particularly associated with the presence of severe anterior chamber reactions and posterior synechiae.

When disease activity was assessed, the mean Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI) was 4.73 ± 1.54, and the mean Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Score (ASDAS‐CRP) was 2.77 ± 0.89. In the uveitis group, the mean BASDAI score was 6.5 and the mean ASDAS‐CRP score was 3.8. In the group without uveitis, the respective scores were 3.4 and 2. Table 1 presents the patients' demographic characteristics, laboratory findings, disease activity scores and hematological inflammation indices.

The presence of uveitis was associated with a clearly defined proinflammatory profile. While NLR, PLR, SII, and AISI levels increased alongside elevated platelet counts, significantly lower lymphocyte, HALP and CALLY values were found (all p < 0.05; remaining significant after FDR correction). After FDR correction, SIRI (q = 0.054) and neutrophil counts (q = 0.066) did not retain statistical significance. CRP and ESR levels were also significantly higher in the uveitis group (p = 0.001 and p < 0.001; q = 0.002 and q < 0.001, respectively). The other parameters were similar. A comparison of the groups based on the presence of uveitis is presented in Table 2.

When the groups were compared in terms of categorical variables, similar distributions of gender and HLA‐B27 were observed (p > 0.05). However, a significant difference in medication use was found between the groups (p < 0.001). The distribution of treatments was as follows: anti‐TNF (52.1%), anti‐IL‐17 (7.9%), JAK inhibitor (5.0%) and csDMARD (35.0%). To minimise the effect of systemic inflammation on hematological indices, the non‐uveitis group primarily comprised patients receiving biologic therapy, reflecting a relatively well‐controlled inflammatory profile (Table 3). To address the potential confounding effect of treatment distribution, a sensitivity analysis was performed restricted to patients receiving anti‐TNF therapy, in which medication type was held constant (n = 73; uveitis absent n = 64, uveitis present n = 9). In this subgroup, NLR, PLR, SII, SIRI, AISI, CRP, and ESR remained significantly higher in the uveitis group, while HALP and CALLY scores remained significantly lower (all p < 0.05), see Table 2. These findings suggest that the associations between hematological inflammation indices and uveitis are not solely attributable to differences in treatment distribution.

ROC analysis revealed that the HALP and CALLY indices demonstrated moderate discriminatory performance for distinguishing patients with and without active uveitis. The area under the curve (AUC) for the HALP index was 0.675 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.590–0.751; p < 0.001) and for the CALLY index, it was 0.674 (95% CI: 0.590–0.751; p < 0.001, Figures 1 and 2).

Of the blood count‐based inflammation indices, the PLR showed the highest discriminatory performance (AUC = 0.693; p < 0.001). Although SII demonstrated high sensitivity (100%), its specificity was low. In contrast, NLR showed high sensitivity but limited specificity. The diagnostic performance of SIRI and AISI was lower compared to other indices.

Of the biochemical parameters, ESR provided the most balanced diagnostic profile, whereas CRP was characterised by high specificity but low sensitivity. Hematological parameters were statistically significant but had limited discriminatory power (Table 4). Overall, these findings suggest that hematological inflammation indices are moderately effective in identifying the presence of uveitis.

Spearman's correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationships between inflammation indices in patients with uveitis in more detail. Strong negative correlations were found between the HALP score and both the PLR and the SII. Significant inverse relationships were also observed with the NLR and the AISI. The CALLY index was also found to be negatively correlated with inflammation indices. Among these indices, very strong positive correlations were observed between SII and NLR, SIRI and AISI, and MLR and SIRI (Table 5). These findings suggest that inflammation indices form a strong biological network of relationships and can be considered as a unified whole that reflects different aspects of systemic inflammation.

Discussion

AS is a systemic disease characterised by chronic inflammation. In addition to axial skeletal involvement, it can present with various extra‐articular manifestations. One of the most common of these is anterior uveitis, which is considered an important clinical indicator of the systemic inflammatory burden of the disease [1].

Composite hematological indices are being studied increasingly in rheumatological diseases because they can reflect different components of the immune response. More broadly, serum and hematological biomarkers have been investigated in several rheumatological diseases, including familial Mediterranean fever and psoriatic arthritis, where they have been associated with disease activity, organ involvement, and disease‐related complications. These observations support the growing interest in easily accessible biomarkers as indicators of inflammatory burden across different rheumatic diseases [10, 11].

It has been suggested that the HALP score could serve as a more comprehensive biomarker, capable of reflecting both inflammation and nutritional status simultaneously [8]. Various clinical studies have reported an association between low CALLY scores and a higher inflammatory burden and impaired nutritional status [12]. Although indices such as NLR and PLR have previously been shown to be associated with inflammatory activity [3], evidence regarding the clinical utility of CALLY and HALP scores in rheumatological diseases remains limited. Furthermore, Atik and Atik reported significant associations between NLR, SII, and uric acid‐to‐HDL cholesterol ratio and disease activity in patients with AS, supporting the concept that composite inflammatory biomarkers reflect systemic inflammatory burden in this disease [13]. Although that study focused on overall disease activity rather than uveitis, its findings are consistent with our observation that hematological inflammation indices may provide valuable information regarding inflammatory status in AS. Nevertheless, only a small number of studies have specifically evaluated HALP and CALLY scores in patients with spondyloarthritis, psoriatic arthritis, and AS [14, 15]. The limited number of studies examining extra‐articular manifestations of AS highlights a significant knowledge gap in this field. Against this backdrop, our study adds to the existing literature by demonstrating an association between HALP and CALLY indices and active uveitis.

The main finding of our study is that there is a significant association between uveitis and hematological inflammation indices. The elevated NLR, PLR, SII, and AISI values found in patients with uveitis indicate a markedly activated acute inflammatory response; these associations remained statistically significant after Benjamini‐Hochberg FDR correction. SIRI showed a nominally significant difference but did not retain significance after correction for multiple comparisons. MLR values did not show significant differences between groups. This suggests that not all hematological indices reflect the presence of uveitis to the same extent, and that there is biological heterogeneity among these parameters. Conversely, HALP and CALLY scores were significantly lower in patients with uveitis, suggesting that these indices decrease as inflammatory burden increases and are inversely associated with systemic inflammation. Taken together, these findings suggest that hematological inflammation indices may serve as supportive biomarkers capable of reflecting the presence of uveitis.

The findings of our study suggest that inflammatory indices reflect distinct biological processes. Elevated NLR and PLR values have previously been associated with inflammatory activity in rheumatological diseases and are considered markers of the acute inflammatory response [16]. In contrast, lower HALP and CALLY scores may reflect not only inflammation but also alterations in nutritional and metabolic status. Taken together, these findings indicate that different hematological indices may capture complementary aspects of the inflammatory process rather than a single biological pathway.

However, the findings from the ROC analysis suggest that these indices should be used clinically with caution. The AUC values obtained for HALP and CALLY were approximately 0.67, indicating only modest discriminatory performance for distinguishing patients with and without active uveitis. Similarly, the fact that other inflammation indices also have limited discriminatory power suggests that these parameters are not suitable for use as diagnostic biomarkers on their own. Therefore, hematological indices should be considered in conjunction with clinical evaluation and traditional markers of inflammation. These findings support the view that these indices are not standalone diagnostic tools, but rather complementary markers that should be considered in the context of a clinical assessment.

Our study has certain limitations. The retrospective and cross‐sectional design means that causal relationships cannot be established. Laboratory data in the uveitis group were collected during active attacks, while those in the control group were obtained at routine follow‐up visits, which may have introduced a systematic measurement bias. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting differences between active uveitis and clinically stable disease states. Treatment distribution differed considerably between groups, with anti‐TNF agents used predominantly in the non‐uveitis group (80%) and csDMARDs almost exclusively in the uveitis group (82%). Although a multivariable analysis adjusting for medication type was not feasible due to the complete segregation of treatment categories between groups, a sensitivity analysis restricted to anti‐TNF users (n = 73) yielded consistent results, suggesting that the findings are not solely attributable to differences in treatment. The relatively small sample size and single‐centre recruitment further limit the generalisability of these findings.

Furthermore, gaps in certain clinical data may have acted as potential confounding factors. Despite these limitations, the study benefits from the use of real‐world data and the simultaneous evaluation of multiple hematological inflammation indices.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study found that inflammation indices such as NLR, PLR, SII, and AISI were significantly higher in patients with uveitis, whereas HALP and CALLY scores were lower, and these associations remained robust after Benjamini‐Hochberg FDR correction. SIRI and MLR did not retain significance after correction for multiple comparisons. These findings suggest that hematological inflammation indices are associated with active uveitis in patients with AS. However, their discriminatory performance was modest, and they should not be considered standalone diagnostic biomarkers. Rather, they may serve as supportive tools that complement clinical assessment and conventional inflammatory markers when evaluating inflammatory burden and active uveitis. Future prospective, multicenter studies are needed to further clarify the clinical and prognostic value of these indices.
