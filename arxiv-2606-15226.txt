Spreading speeds for nonlocal Fisher–KPP equations with time-dependent asymmetric kernels
Zhucheng Jin, Tao Zhou, [0.4em] School of Mathematical Sciences, University of Science and Technology of China, Hefei, Anhui 230026, P.R. China

Abstract
This paper investigates spreading speeds for nonautonomous Fisher–KPP equations with nonlocal diffusion. The dispersal kernel depends on time and may be asymmetric in space. We assume exponential boundedness of the kernel and the existence of uniform mean values. Under these assumptions, the rightward and leftward spreading speeds are proved to be given by the linearly determined formula. The proof is based on comparison arguments, a refined maximum principle and proper sub- and super-solutions. The result includes, in particular, periodic, almost periodic, and uniquely ergodic time dependence. And it allows kernels whose support does not contain the neighborhood of the origin. Key words . spreading speed; time-dependent kernel; asymmetric kernel; uniform mean value. 2020 Mathematical Subject Classification . 35K57; 35B40; 35B65; 92D25.

Introduction

In this work, we study the Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,x-y)[u(t,y)-u(t,x)]\mathrm{d}y+u(t,x)f(t,u(t,x)),&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R},\\
\displaystyle u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

(1.1)

Here
u(t,x)
is the density of a population. The initial function
0\leq u_{0}\leq 1
is continuous, compactly supported, and not identically zero. The reaction term is of Fisher–KPP type. The kernel
K(t,x-y)
describes the probability of the species jump from location
y
to location
x
at time
t
. Here, we do not require
K
to be symmetric in spatial variable. This equation can arise
in various contexts, such as phase transitions and combustion. In particular, it plays an important role
in ecological and biological modeling. For instance, (
1.1
) can describe the spatio-temporal evolution of an invading population into some empty environments.

Propagation phenomena in local diffusion equations have been studied extensively in the literature.
For the classical local Fisher–KPP equation

\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\partial_{xx}u(t,x)+u(t,x)(1-u(t,x)),\qquad t>0,\ x\in\mathbb{R},

(1.2)

the propagation dynamics are well understood. The works of Fisher
[
13
]
, Kolmogorov–Petrovskii–Piskunov
[
16
]
, and Aronson–Weinberger
[
2
]
show that the solution with compactly supported data invades the unstable state
u=0
with a definite speed.
Moreover, this speed coincides with the minimal speed of traveling fronts.
Later works developed this relation in a more abstract form, see, for example,
[
31
,
32
,
18
]
.

Nonlocal diffusion is used when dispersal is not well described by Brownian motion. One replaces
\partial_{xx}
by

\phi\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(x-y)[\phi(y)-\phi(x)]\mathrm{d}y.

(1.3)

This describes the long distance disperse. It is suitable for situations where seeds, pollen, individuals, or pathogens may move a long distance in a short time. From the analytical perspective, this replacement is not harmless. The nonlocal diffusion equation does not usually have the smoothing effect as parabolic equations, and the semiflow is not compact in the usual spaces. For this reason, proofs for local diffusion equations cannot be copied directly. Traveling waves and spreading speeds for nonlocal Fisher–KPP equations have been studied in
[

,

,
7
,
8
,

,

,

]
. In particular, asymmetric kernels and the difference between the two directions were discussed in
[
33
]
. Very recently, Du
et al.

[
10
]
provided a new approach to study this problem, which based on the asymptotic limit of the principal eigenvalue. This methodology may applicable to our problem if one has the property of principal eigenvalue of the nonautonomous operator.

In applications, dispersal and growth may change with season, resource level, temperature, or other environmental factors.
To make the model more realistic, the heterogeneous media are taken into consideration.

Now we give a rough introduction of our main results.
Due to the KPP reaction term, the spreading speed may be linearly determined.
Since the kernel itself depends on time, the linear selected speed should not be determined by the fixed exponential moment. Ansatz
e^{-\lambda(x-\int_{0}^{t}c(s)\mathrm{d}s)}
into the linearized equation.
The following quantity appears,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,y)(e^{\lambda y}-1)\mathrm{d}y+r(t),\qquad r(t):=f(t,0).

We assume that this quantity has a uniform mean value in time. This assumption is weaker than periodicity and still gives enough information for studying the spreading behaviors of solutions. It includes the usual periodic case and also covers almost periodic and uniquely ergodic examples.
Because
K(t,y)
may be nonsymmetric, the invasion speed to the right and to the left may be different. We therefore use two speeds, denoted by
c^{*}_{+}
and
c^{*}_{-}
. Roughly speaking, our result says that these speeds are given by

c^{*}_{+}=\inf_{\lambda>0}\frac{\left\langle\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(\cdot,y)(e^{\lambda y}-1)\mathrm{d}y+r(\cdot)\right\rangle}{\lambda},\qquad c^{*}_{-}=\inf_{\lambda<0}\frac{\left\langle\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(\cdot,y)(e^{\lambda y}-1)\mathrm{d}y+r(\cdot)\right\rangle}{-\lambda},

where the precise admissible range of
\lambda
is given below. This is the same linear determinacy principle as in the standard KPP case, but the exponential moment is replaced by its time average.

We state more explicitly what is allowed here. The function
K(t,y)
is only measurable in
t
. It is not assumed to be symmetric in
y
.
We also do not assume that the support of
K(t,\cdot)
contains a neighborhood of the origin. This last point is important in the proof, since the usual strong maximum principle is no longer available. To overcome this difficulty, we prove a refined maximum principle adapted to such kernels.
The upper estimate follows from exponential super-solutions and the uniform mean value formula.
The lower estimate is based on localized exponential sub-solutions whose centers move with suitable average speeds.
Some ideas are close to those in
[
12
,
19
,
33
]
, but the time-dependent and nonsymmetric kernel requires several modifications.

We first define the rightward and leftward spreading speeds.

Definition 1.1

Two constants
c^{*}_{+}
and
c^{*}_{-}
are called the spreading speeds of (
1.1
) in the positive and negative directions respectively provided the following two statements hold:

(i) For any nonnegative initial function
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\cap C(\mathbb{R})
with a compact support,

\displaystyle{\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}}\sup\limits_{x\geq(c^{*}_{+}+\epsilon)t}u(t,x)=\displaystyle{\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}}\sup\limits_{x\leq(-c^{*}_{-}-\epsilon)t}u(t,x)=0,~~\text{ for all small }\epsilon>0;

(ii) There is some
L>0
such that for any nonnegative initial function
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\cap C(\mathbb{R})
, if
u_{0}(x)>0
on an interval longer than
L
, then

\displaystyle{\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}}\sup\limits_{(-c^{*}_{-}+\epsilon)t\leq x\leq(c^{*}_{+}-\epsilon)t}|u(t,x)-1|=0,~~\text{ for all small }\epsilon>0.

(1.4)

Remark 1.2

The above definition implies that
c^{*}_{+}+c^{*}_{-}\geq 0
. When equality holds, the interval in (
1.4
) is empty for every sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
; in this degenerate case we say that the spreading speed is zero.

Before stating some assumptions, we first introduce the concept of
uniform mean value
which plays an important role in overcoming the difficulties induced by time heterogeneities, see
[
23
,
26
,
27
]
. We emphasize that most time heterogeneities admit a mean value, for instance periodic, almost periodic and uniquely ergodic media. Now we give the definition of uniform mean value.

Definition 1.3

A function
h\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty)
is said to have a uniform mean value if the following limit exists,

\langle h\rangle:=\lim\limits_{T\to+\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}h(t+s)\mathrm{d}t,\text{ uniformly for }s\geq 0.

Herein, the quantity
\langle h\rangle
is called the uniform mean value of
h
.

There are some equivalent and useful characterizations for the function
h
possessing a uniform mean value. As proved in
[
23
]
, the uniform mean value
\langle h\rangle
has the following alternative representations:

\langle h\rangle=\sup_{a\in W^{1,\infty}(0,\infty)}\essinf_{t\in(0,\infty)}\left(a^{\prime}+h\right)(t)=\inf_{a\in W^{1,\infty}(0,\infty)}\esssup_{t\in(0,\infty)}\left(a^{\prime}+h\right)(t).

(1.5)

Assumption 1.4
(KPP conditions)

The function
f:[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies:

(f1)

The function
f(t,\cdot)
is nonincreasing and Lipschitz continuous uniformly with respect to
t\geq 0
, and
f(\cdot,u)\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty)
for each given
u\in\mathbb{R}
;

(f2)

f(t,1)\equiv 0
for all
t\geq 0
and

h(u):=\inf_{t\geq 0}f(t,u)>0,\forall u\in[0,1).
Moreover, the function
r(t):=f(t,0)
has a uniform mean value, denoted by
\langle r\rangle
.

It is easy to see that
\inf_{t\geq 0}r(t)=h(0)>0
. A typical example of Assumption
1.4
is given by
f(t,u)=r(t)(1-u)
.

Next we state assumptions of kernel function
K
.

Assumption 1.5

The kernel function
K(t,y):[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty)
satisfies:

(K1)

The function
K
is Lebesgue measurable and
K(\cdot,y)\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty)
for a.e.
y\in\mathbb{R}
;

(K2)

The mean value
\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle
exists for a.e.
y\in\mathbb{R}
;

(K3)

Denote

\mathcal{I}:=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}:\;\;\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|K(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty)}e^{\lambda y}\mathrm{d}y<+\infty\right\}.

(1.6)

There exists
\eta>0
such that
(-\eta,\eta)\subset\mathcal{I}
. Moreover, for each
\lambda\notin\mathcal{I}
, we impose

\liminf_{T\to+\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,y)e^{\lambda y}\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t=+\infty.

(1.7)

Remark 1.6

Note that the kernel is integrable uniformly in time since
0\in\mathcal{I}
. The assumption (K3) implies that the kernel is exponentially bounded uniformly in time. It should be noted that, under certain situations, the endpoints of the interval
\mathcal{I}
,
\sup\mathcal{I}
and
\inf\mathcal{I}
, may be elements of
\mathcal{I}
. In the sequel, we denote

i_{+}:=\sup\mathcal{I}\text{ and }i_{-}:=\inf\mathcal{I}.

(1.8)

Condition (
1.7
) is imposed only at points outside the set
\mathcal{I}
. It excludes the possibility that the averaged exponential moment remains finite at a boundary point not belonging to
\mathcal{I}
, and it is used to handle the endpoint case in the variational formula of the spreading speed.

Define a map
H(\lambda):(i_{-},i_{+})\to\mathbb{R}
as

H(\lambda):=\left\langle\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(\cdot,y)[e^{\lambda y}-1]\mathrm{d}y+r(\cdot)\right\rangle,

(1.9)

and two constants

c^{*}_{+}:=\inf_{\lambda\in(0,i_{+})}\frac{H(\lambda)}{\lambda}\;\;\text{ and }\;\;c^{*}_{-}:=\inf_{\lambda\in(i_{-},0)}\frac{H(\lambda)}{-\lambda}.

(1.10)

Remark 1.7

Recalling Definition
1.3
and Assumption
1.5
, the Fubini-Tonelli theorem and the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem imply that for each
\lambda\in(i_{-},i_{+})
,

H(\lambda)=\left\langle\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(\cdot,y)[e^{\lambda y}-1]\mathrm{d}y\right\rangle+\left\langle r(\cdot)\right\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\langle K(\cdot,y)\right\rangle[e^{\lambda y}-1]\mathrm{d}y+\left\langle r(\cdot)\right\rangle.

(1.11)

Note that

\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{\pm}}\frac{H(\lambda)}{\pm\lambda}=+\infty.

Thus,
c^{*}_{\pm}
are well defined. The properties of
H(\lambda)
and
c^{*}_{\pm}
will be shown in Section
2.2
.

Now we state the spreading speed result of (
1.1
).

Theorem 1.8

Let Assumptions
1.4
and
1.5
be satisfied. Then the spreading speeds of (
1.1
) are given by
c^{*}_{+}
and
c^{*}_{-}
, which are defined in (
1.10
). Moreover,
\text{sgn}(c^{*}_{-}+c^{*}_{+})=\text{sgn}\left\langle\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(\cdot,y)\mathrm{d}y\right\rangle
.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we establish comparison principles, well-posedness, and elementary properties of the speed functions. Section 3 proves the upper and lower spreading estimates and hence Theorem
1.8
. Section 4 records several examples and special cases illustrating the role of asymmetric kernels.

Preliminaries

2.1
The well-posedness of (
1.1
) and the maximum principle

Throughout this subsection we only assume
\overline{K}_{0}(\cdot):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(\cdot,y)\mathrm{d}y\in L^{\infty}_{loc}([0,+\infty))
. We prove well-posedness by an iteration argument. This avoids any continuity assumption on
K(t,y)
in the time variable.

Proposition 2.1

There exists a unique solution to (
1.1
) satisfying
u(t,x)\in[0,1]
on
(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
. Moreover,
u(t,\cdot)
is continuous for all
t>0
.

Proof.
We use the integral formulation.
Let
L_{f}
be the Lipschitz constant of
f
and
\tau\leq 1
be small to be chosen later. For any
t\in[0,\tau]
, set

u_{1}(t,x)=u_{0}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(s,y)(u_{0}(x-y)-u_{0}(x))dyds+\int_{0}^{t}u_{0}(x)f(s,u_{0}(x))ds

and for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

u_{n+1}(t,x)=u_{0}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(s,y)\big(u_{n}(s,x-y)-u_{n}(s,x)\big)dyds+\int_{0}^{t}u_{n}(s,x)f(s,u_{n}(s,x))ds.

(2.1)

Recall that
u_{0}
is continuous, compactly supported, and satisfies
0\leq u_{0}\leq 1
. For any
(t,x)\in Q:=[0,\tau]\times\mathbb{R},

|u_{1}(t,x)-u_{0}(x)|\leq 2\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(s,y)dyds+\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{0}(x)f(s,u_{0}(x))ds\leq C\tau,

where
C=\big(2\|\overline{K}_{0}(\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}+L_{f}\big)
and

\begin{split}&\quad|u_{n+1}(t,x)-u_{n}(t,x)|\\
&=\Big|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(s,y)\big((u_{n}-u_{n-1})(s,x-y)-(u_{n}-u_{n-1})(s,x)\big)dyds+\int_{0}^{t}u_{n}f(s,u_{n})-u_{n-1}f(s,u_{n-1})ds\Big|\\
&\leq\Big(2\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(s,y)dyds+L_{f}\tau\Big)\sup\limits_{(t,x)\in Q}|u_{n}(t,x)-u_{n-1}(t,x)|,\end{split}

This gives

\sup\limits_{(t,x)\in Q}|u_{n+1}(t,x)-u_{n}(t,x)|\leq C\tau\sup\limits_{(t,x)\in Q}|u_{n}(t,x)-u_{n-1}(t,x)|\leq(C\tau)^{n+1}

(2.2)

Taking
\tau<\min\{1/C,1\}
, we deduce that the sequence
u_{n}(t,x)=u_{0}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(u_{i}(t,x)-u_{i-1}(t,x)\big)
converges uniformly on
Q
to some limit function
u(t,x)
. Letting
n\to+\infty
in (
2.1
), we have

u(t,x)=u_{0}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(s,y)\big(u(s,x-y)-u(s,x)\big)dyds+\int_{0}^{t}u(s,x)f(s,u(s,x))ds.

Hence
u(t,x)
satisfies (
1.1
) on
Q
. Similarly, we can find the solution
\tilde{u}
of (
1.1
) on
[\tau/2,3\tau/2]\times\mathbb{R}
with initial value
\tilde{u}(\tau/2,x)=u(\tau/2,x)
. We claim that
\tilde{u}(t,x)=u(t,x)
on
Q_{1}=[\tau/2,\tau]\times\mathbb{R}
. From the integral equation we obtain, for any
(t,x)\in Q_{1}
,

\begin{split}|u(t,x)-v(t,x)|&=\Big|\int_{\tau/2}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(s,y)\big((u-v)(s,x-y)-(u-v)(s,x)\big)dyds+\int_{\tau/2}^{t}uf(s,u)-vf(s,v)ds\Big|\\
&\leq C\tau/2\sup\limits_{(t,x)\in Q_{1}}|u(t,x)-v(t,x)|.\end{split}

Hence
\tilde{u}(t,x)=u(t,x)
on
Q_{1}=[\tau/2,\tau]\times\mathbb{R}
. Now we obtain a unique bounded solution to (
1.1
) on
[0,3\tau/2]\times\mathbb{R}
. An induction argument yields a unique bounded solution to (
1.1
) on
[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}
and thus on
[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
.

It remains to prove that the solution
u(t,x)
is continuous. Let
\omega_{0}(\cdot)
be the modulus of continuity of
u_{0}
. For any
x,\xi\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in[0,\tau]
, we have

\begin{split}&\quad|u_{1}(t,x)-u_{1}(t,\xi)|\\
&\leq|u_{0}(x)-u_{0}(\xi)|+\Big|\int_{0}^{t}u_{0}(x)f(s,u_{0}(x))-u_{0}(\xi)f(s,u_{0}(\xi))ds\Big|\\
&\quad+\Big|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(s,y)\big(u_{0}(x-y)-u_{0}(\xi-y)+u_{0}(\xi)-u_{0}(x)\big)dyds\Big|\\
&\leq(1+C\tau)\omega_{0}(|x-\xi|).\end{split}

This shows that
u_{1}(t,\cdot)
is uniformly continuous on
\mathbb{R}
uniformly with respect to
t\in[0,\tau]
and we denote its modulus of continuity by
\omega_{1}(\cdot)
. The same argument gives
\omega_{n+1}(|x-\xi|)\leq\omega_{0}(|x-\xi|)+C\tau\omega_{n}(|x-\xi|)
. Since
u_{n}\to u
uniformly on
Q
,
u(t,\cdot)
is continuous.

Next we prove maximum principles.

Lemma 2.2
(Maximum Principle)

Assume that
g(t,x,\eta)/\eta
is bounded if
\eta
is bounded and that
v(t,x)
is bounded and satisfies

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\partial_{t}v(t,x)\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,x-y)[v(t,y)-v(t,x)]\mathrm{d}y+g(t,x,v(t,x)),&x\in\mathbb{R},0<t<T,\\
\displaystyle v(0,x)\geq 0,&x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

Then
v(t,x)\geq 0
for
(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.
Setting
V(t,x)=v(t,x)p(t,x)
with
p(t,x):=\text{exp}{(\int^{t}_{0}\overline{K}_{0}(s)-g(s,x,v(s,x))/v(s,x)ds)}
, we have

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\partial_{t}V\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,y)\frac{p(t,x)}{p(t,x-y)}V(t,x-y)dy,&(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R},\\
V(0,x)\geq 0,&x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{array}\right.

(2.3)

For any
\phi(t,x)
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, we introduce the following notation:

[R_{k}\phi]:=\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\cdots\int_{0}^{t_{k-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}\left[K(t_{j},y_{j})\frac{p(t_{j},x-\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}y_{i})}{p(t_{j},x-\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{j}y_{i})}\right]\phi(t_{k},x-\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i})dy_{k}\cdots dy_{1}dt_{k}\cdots dt_{1},

with convention that
t_{0}=t
and
\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}y_{i}=0
if
j=1
.
We have
|[R_{k}V](t,x)|\leq\frac{(tP_{0})^{k}}{k!}\sup\limits_{s\in[0,T],x\in\mathbb{R}}|V(s,x)|
, where
P_{0}:=\sup_{s\in[0,T],x,y\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{p(s,x)}{p(s,x-y)}\,\|\overline{K}_{0}(\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T])}
.
Integrating (
2.3
) from 0 to
t
, we have

V(t,x)\geq V(0,x)+[R_{1}V](t,x),\ \forall(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R},

(2.4)

This gives, for any
(t_{1},x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle V(t_{1},x-y_{1})

\displaystyle\geq V(0,x-y_{1})

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t_{2},y_{2})\frac{p(t_{2},x-y_{1})}{p(t_{2},x-\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}y_{i})}V(t_{2},x-\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}y_{i})dy_{2}dt_{2}.

(2.5)

Substituting this into (
2.4
), we have

\displaystyle V(t,x)

\displaystyle\geq V(0,x)+[R_{1}V(0,\cdot)](t,x)+[R_{2}V](t,x),

Replacing
t_{1},y_{1}
and
y_{2}
by
t_{2}
,
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}y_{i}
and
y_{3}
, respectively, in (
2.1
), and then plugging it into
[R_{2}V](t,x)
, we have

\displaystyle V(t,x)

\displaystyle\geq V(0,x)+[R_{1}V(0,\cdot)](t,x)+[R_{2}V(0,\cdot)](t,x)+[R_{3}V](t,x).

By induction, we have, for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,

V(t,x)\geq V(0,x)+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}[R_{i}V(0,\cdot)](t,x)+[R_{k+1}V](t,x).

Note that

and

as
k\to\infty
. Hence
V(t,x)\geq 0
for all
(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}
, and thus

.

(Maximum principle on a domain)

Assume that
X_{1}(t)
and

are two continuous functions defined in

and
X_{1}(t)<X_{2}(t)
for all

. If

satisfies

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(t,x)\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,x-y)\left[u(t,y)-u(t,x)\right]\mathrm{d}y+a(t,x)u(t,x),\quad\forall t\in(0,T],\forall x\in\left(X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t)\right),\\
u(0,x)\geq 0,\hskip 220.00034pt\forall x\in[X_{1}(0),X_{2}(0)],\\

t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\left[X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t)\right]
.

Proof.
Denote

\Omega_{T}:=\{(t,x):0<t\leq T,\ X_{1}(t)<x<X_{2}(t)\}.

Choose
\mu>0
so large that

b(t,x):=\mu-\overline{K}_{0}(t)+a(t,x)\geq b_{0}>0,\qquad(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},

where
\overline{K}_{0}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,y)\mathrm{d}y
. Set
v(t,x)=e^{\mu t}u(t,x)
. Then

v_{t}(t,x)\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,x-y)v(t,y)\mathrm{d}y+b(t,x)v(t,x),\qquad(t,x)\in\Omega_{T}.

For
\delta>0
and
\alpha>0
, let

v_{\delta}(t,x):=v(t,x)+\delta e^{\alpha t}.

Choose
\alpha>\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\overline{K}_{0}(t)+\|b\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})}
. Then, one has

(v_{\delta})_{t}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,y)v_{\delta}(t,x-y)\mathrm{d}y-b(t,x)v_{\delta}(t,x)\geq\delta e^{\alpha t}\big(\alpha-\overline{K}(t)-b(t,x)\big)>0,~~(t,x)\in\Omega_{T}.

Moreover,
v_{\delta}>0
at
t=0
and on the parabolic boundary
x\notin(X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t))
. If
v_{\delta}
became nonpositive somewhere in
\Omega_{T}
, let
t_{0}
be the first time at which this happens and let
x_{0}\in[X_{1}(t_{0}),X_{2}(t_{0})]
be a contact point. Since the boundary values are positive,
x_{0}
is an interior point. At
(t_{0},x_{0})
, we have
v_{\delta}=0
,
v_{\delta}(t_{0},\cdot)\geq 0
, and
(v_{\delta})_{t}(t_{0},x_{0})\leq 0
. The strict differential inequality above gives instead

(v_{\delta})_{t}(t_{0},x_{0})>\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t_{0},y)v_{\delta}(t_{0},x_{0}-y)\mathrm{d}y\geq 0,

which is impossible. Hence
v_{\delta}\geq 0
in
\Omega_{T}
. Letting
\delta\to 0^{+}
yields
v\geq 0
, and therefore
u\geq 0
, in
\Omega_{T}
.

Lemma 2.4
(Refined maximum principle)

Assume that the hypotheses of Lemma
2.2
hold. Suppose that there exist an interval
[a,b]\subset(0,\infty)
and a number
T_{0}>0
such that

\int_{0}^{T_{0}}\int_{a}^{b}K(t,y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t>0.

Then there exist
R\in\mathbb{R}
,
L>0
, and
T>0
such that, if
v_{0}>0
on an interval of length larger than
L
, then
v(t,x)>0
for all
t\geq T
and
x\geq R
. If instead
[a,b]\subset(-\infty,0)
and the same integral positivity holds, then the analogous conclusion holds on a left half-line.

Proof.
We give the proof for
[a,b]\subset(0,\infty)
. The proof for the negative direction is identical after mapping
x\mapsto-x
. By Lemma
2.2
,
v\geq 0
. Choose
M>0
so large that
U(t,x):=e^{Mt}v(t,x)
satisfies

U(t,x)\geq U(0,x)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(s,y)U(s,x-y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s,\qquad t\geq 0.

Assume, after a translation, that
U(0,x)>0
on
[R,R+l]
, where
l
is larger than
a
and
b-a
. Let

A(t):=\int_{a}^{b}K(t,y)\mathrm{d}y.

By assumption,
\int_{0}^{T_{0}}A(t)\mathrm{d}t>0
. Iterating the integral inequality gives the usual Duhamel series lower bound. In particular, for each
n\geq 1
, the
n
-fold term is strictly positive at time
T_{0}
for every

x\in[R+na,\ R+l+nb],

because one may restrict all jumps to
[a,b]
and the time integrals to the set on which
A(t)>0
. Since
l
is chosen so that the intervals
[R+na,R+l+nb]
overlap and cover a right half-line, there is
R_{1}
such that
U(T_{0},x)>0
for all
x\geq R_{1}
. The first part of the proof of Lemma
2.2
then implies
v(t,x)>0
for all
t\geq T_{0}
and
x\geq R_{1}
.

Remark 2.5

The previous lemma only uses positivity of the kernel on a set with positive measure in time and space. If this condition holds in both spatial directions, then the same argument gives positivity on the whole line after some time. In particular, if the support of the kernel contains a neighborhood of the origin, the usual hair-trigger property follows.

2.2

and

We show some elementary properties of the variational formula of linearly determined speed. We start with the speed in the positive direction.
Define

(i) If

For the negative direction we use the change of variables

Proposition 2.8

We have
\text{sgn}(c^{*}_{-}+c^{*}_{+})=\text{sgn}\langle\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(\cdot,y)\mathrm{d}y\rangle=\text{sgn}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle\mathrm{d}y.

Proof.
First,
\langle\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(\cdot,y)\mathrm{d}y\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle\mathrm{d}y
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle dy\geq 0

If
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle dy=0
, then
\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle=0
almost everywhere. Hence
H(\lambda)\equiv\langle r\rangle
yield

c^{*}_{-}=c^{*}_{+}=0
.

We only prove the case
\mathcal{I}=(-\infty,+\infty)

Suppose now
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle dy>0
. Then either
\int_{0}^{+\infty}\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle dy>0

\int_{-\infty}^{0}\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle dy>0.

c^{*}_{-}+c^{*}_{+}>0
under the condition

\begin{split}\frac{H(\lambda)}{\lambda}&=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle(e^{\lambda y}-1)\mathrm{d}y+\langle r\rangle}{\lambda}\\

&\geq\frac{\int_{y_{0}}^{+\infty}\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle e^{\lambda{y_{0}}}\mathrm{d}y-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle\mathrm{d}y+\langle r\rangle}{\lambda}\to+\infty\\
\end{split}

as

&\geq\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{n}}{-\lambda_{+}\lambda_{n}}\langle r\rangle>0.\\
\end{split}

The first inequality follows from the convexity of

.

The proof of

Proposition 2.9

If

(resp.

and

(resp.

are positive, then there is

such that if

on an interval longer than

on

.

Proof.
It remains only to verify the assumptions of Lemma
2.4
.

Claim
: If
c^{*}_{+}>0
(resp.
c^{*}_{-}>0
), then there exist
T_{0}>0
and an interval
[a,b]\in(0,+\infty)
(resp.
(-\infty,0)

\mu\left\{t\in[0,T_{0}]:\;\int_{a}^{b}K(t,y)\mathrm{d}y>0\right\}>0,

where

is the Lebesgue measure. Set
\mathcal{T}:=\left\{t\in[0,T_{0}]:\;\int_{a}^{b}K(t,y)\mathrm{d}y>0\right\}

Suppose not. Then for any
[a,b]\subset(0,\infty)
and

, we have

\mu\left\{t\in[0,T]:\;\int_{a}^{b}K(t,y)\mathrm{d}y>0\right\}=0.

This implies that for each given

\int_{a}^{b}K(t,y)\mathrm{d}y=0,\text{ for a.e. }t\in[0,T].

Since
K\geq 0
, we see that

K(t,y)=0
for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
and
y\in[a,b]
. Since
a,b>0
, we have
i_{+}=+\infty
and
\int_{a}^{b}K(t,y)e^{\lambda y}\mathrm{d}y=0
for all

\lambda>0
and
\epsilon>0
. Choose

sufficiently small and

sufficiently large such that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}K(t,y)\mathrm{d}y\leq\int_{0}^{a}\|K(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}e^{\lambda y}\mathrm{d}y\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2},

c^{\prime}(\lambda)<0

[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]
. In particular,

\langle G_{\lambda_{2}}\rangle<0.

Choose
\theta_{0}>0
so small that

\langle G_{\lambda_{2}}\rangle<-3\theta_{0}.

By the reform formula of the uniform mean value (
1.5
), there exists

a\in W^{1,\infty}(0,\infty)
such that

\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{t\geq 0}\big(a^{\prime}(t)+G_{\lambda_{2}}(t)\big)<-2\theta_{0}.

Moreover, for
\lambda>0
,

\partial_{\lambda}G_{\lambda}(t)=\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,y)e^{\lambda y}y^{2}\mathrm{d}y\geq 0.

Hence
G_{\lambda}(t)\leq G_{\lambda_{2}}(t)
for all

\lambda\in[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]
and a.e.
t\geq 0
. Consequently,

\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{t\geq 0}\big(a^{\prime}(t)+G_{\lambda}(t)\big)<-2\theta_{0},\qquad\lambda\in[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}].

Thus the same function
a(t)
works for all

\lambda\in[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]
.

From (
1.6
), the definition of
\mathcal{I}
, we have

\begin{split}\lim_{l\to+\infty}\lim_{B\to+\infty}\frac{2l}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{B}(t,y)e^{\lambda y}\sin\frac{\pi y}{2l}\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,y)e^{\lambda y}y\,\mathrm{d}y,~~&\forall\lambda\in[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]\\
\lim_{l\to+\infty}\lim_{B\to+\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{B}(t,y)e^{\lambda y}\cos\frac{\pi y}{2l}\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,y)e^{\lambda y}\mathrm{d}y,~~&\forall\lambda\in[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}].\end{split}

By Assumption
1.5
, the above convergence is uniform in

t\geq 0
and
\lambda\in[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]
after first taking
B
large
and then taking
l
sufficiently large. Hence there exist
B_{0}>0
and,
for each
B>B_{0}
, a number
l_{0}(B)>B
such that, for all

l>l_{0}(B)
and
\lambda\in[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]
,

a^{\prime}(t)+\lambda c_{l,B}(\lambda)(t)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{B}(t,y)e^{\lambda y}\cos\frac{\pi y}{2l}\mathrm{d}y+\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,y)\mathrm{d}y-r(t)<-\theta_{0},\quad\forall t>0.

(3.4)

By the similar idea in
[
9
,
11
]
, we observe that

\begin{split}\int_{-l}^{l}K(t,x-y)e^{a(t)}e^{-\lambda y}\cos\frac{\pi y}{2l}\mathrm{d}y&\geq\int_{-l}^{l}K_{B}(t,x-y)e^{a(t)}e^{-\lambda y}\cos\frac{\pi y}{2l}\mathrm{d}y\\
&\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{B}(t,x-y)e^{a(t)}e^{-\lambda y}\cos\frac{\pi y}{2l}\mathrm{d}y\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{B}(t,y)e^{a(t)}e^{-\lambda(x-y)}\cos\frac{\pi(x-y)}{2l}\mathrm{d}y.\end{split}

The last inequality can be derived from
\cos\frac{\pi y}{2l}<0
for
l<|y|<2l
and
K_{B}(t,x-y)=0
for
|y|>2l
and
x\in[-l,l]
. By direct computation, we have for
x\in[-l,l]
and
t>0
,

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}\phi(t,x)-c_{l,B}(\lambda)(t)\partial_{x}\phi(t,x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,x-y)[\phi(t,y)-\phi(t,x)]\mathrm{d}y-r(t)\phi(t,x)\\
&\leq e^{a(t)}e^{-\lambda x}\cos\frac{\pi x}{2l}\bigg\{a^{\prime}(t)+\lambda c_{l,B}(\lambda)(t)-\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{B}(t,y)e^{\lambda y}\cos\frac{\pi y}{2l}\mathrm{d}y+\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,y)\mathrm{d}y-r(t)\bigg\}\\
&\quad+e^{a(t)}e^{-\lambda x}\sin\frac{\pi x}{2l}\bigg\{\frac{\pi}{2l}c_{l,B}(\lambda)(t)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{B}(t,y)e^{\lambda y}\sin\frac{\pi y}{2l}\mathrm{d}y\bigg\}\end{split}

Recall the definition of
c_{l,B}(\lambda)(t)
in (
3.1
) and the inequality (
3.4
). We see that
\phi(t,x)
satisfies for
\theta\in(0,\theta_{0})
,
x\in[-l,l]
and
t>0
,

\partial_{t}\phi(t,x)-c_{R,B}(\lambda)(t)\partial_{x}\phi(t,x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,x-y)[\phi(t,y)-\phi(t,x)]\mathrm{d}y-(r(t)-\theta)\phi(t,x)<0.

Next since
u\mapsto f(\cdot,u)
is Lipschitz continuous, there exists a constant
M>0
such that
f(t,u)\geq r(t)-Mu
. It suffices to choose
\eta>0
small enough such that
M\underline{u}(t,x)\leq\theta_{0}
for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, that is

\eta M\sup_{\lambda\in[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]}e^{\|a\|_{\infty}}e^{\lambda l}\leq\theta_{0}.

We obtain that
\underline{u}
is a subsolution of (
1.1
). This completes the proof.

If
c^{*}_{+}=c^{*}_{-}=0
, Theorem
3.1
gives zero spreading speed. We now assume
c^{*}_{+}+c^{*}_{-}>0
. The following lemma gives a positive lower bound in intervals with speeds close to
c^{*}_{+}
.

Lemma 3.3

Assume that
c^{*}_{+}+c^{*}_{-}>0
.
For all small
\delta>0
, for all
c^{*}_{+}-\delta<c_{1}^{+}<c_{2}^{+}<c^{*}_{+}
, we have

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\inf_{x\in[c_{1}^{+}t,c_{2}^{+}t]}u(t,x)>0.

(3.5)

Proof.

Step 1:

If
\lambda^{*}_{+}=+\infty
, the speed
c^{*}_{+}=0
. This is the trivial case.

Now we consider the case
\lambda_{+}^{*}\in(0,i_{+})
.
We first show there exist
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in(0,\lambda^{*}_{+})
such that
[c_{1}^{+}t,c_{2}^{+}t]\subset[X(\lambda_{1})(t),X(\lambda_{2})(t)]
.
Recalling (
3.2
), we have
\lambda\mapsto X(\lambda)(\cdot)
is continuous. For all
l>B
, we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}X(\lambda)(t)}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}=\frac{2l}{\pi}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{-B}^{B}K(s,y)e^{\lambda y}y\sin\frac{\pi y}{2l}\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s>0,\;\;\forall t>0,\lambda\in(0,i_{+}).

Hence, the function
\lambda\mapsto X(\lambda)
is increasing.
From (
3.1
), we see that
\lambda\mapsto\langle c_{l,B}(\lambda)\rangle
is continuous and increasing with
\lambda\in(0,\lambda^{*}_{+})
. Since
c^{\prime}(\lambda^{*}_{+})=0
when
\lambda^{*}_{+}<i_{+}
, we have

c^{*}_{+}=c(\lambda^{*}_{+})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle e^{\lambda^{*}_{+}y}y\mathrm{d}y.

We also have
\langle c_{l,B}(\lambda^{*}_{+})\rangle<c^{*}_{+}
and

\lim_{l\to+\infty}\lim_{B\to+\infty}\langle c_{l,B}(\lambda^{*}_{+})\rangle=c^{*}_{+}.

So there exists
0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\lambda^{*}_{+}
such
that
\langle c_{l,B}(\lambda_{1})\rangle<c_{1}^{+}<c_{2}^{+}<\langle c_{l,B}(\lambda_{2})\rangle
.

By the definition of the uniform mean value, for
T_{0}>0
large enough, we have

X(\lambda_{1})(t)<c_{1}^{+}t<c_{2}^{+}t<X(\lambda_{2})(t),\quad\forall t\geq T_{0}.

Assume that
u_{0}(x)>0
on an interval longer than
L
. If
c^{*}_{+}>0
, then Proposition
2.9
implies that
u(t,x)>0
on
[T_{1},+\infty)\times[R,+\infty)
for suitable
T_{1}>0
and
R>0
. Note that the shifted function
\phi(t,x-x_{0})
with
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is still a subsolution of (
1.1
). Choose
\eta>0
small enough and
x_{0}=2R
with
R>l
such that

\underline{u}(0,x-2R)=\eta\phi(0,x-2R)\leq u(T_{1},x),\;\;\forall x\in\mathbb{R}.

For the case of
c^{*}_{-}>0
, it suffices to choose proper
x_{0}
such that
\underline{u}(0,x-x_{0})\leq u(T_{1},x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

We next compare the solution
u(t+T_{1},x)
with a subsolution constructed
for the time-shifted equation. Set

K^{T_{1}}(t,y):=K(t+T_{1},y),\qquad f^{T_{1}}(t,u):=f(t+T_{1},u),\qquad r^{T_{1}}(t):=f^{T_{1}}(t,0).

Since the mean value in Definition 1.3 is uniform with respect to the time
shift, we have

\langle K^{T_{1}}(\cdot,y)\rangle=\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle,\qquad\langle r^{T_{1}}\rangle=\langle r\rangle.

Therefore the quantities
H(\lambda)
,
c^{*}_{+}
and
\lambda^{*}_{+}
are unchanged
for the shifted equation. Applying Lemma 3.2 to the coefficients

K^{T_{1}}
and
f^{T_{1}}
, we obtain, for
\lambda\in[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]
,
a function

\phi^{T_{1}}_{\lambda}(t,x)=\begin{cases}e^{a_{T_{1}}(t)}e^{-\lambda x}\cos\frac{\pi x}{2l},&|x|\leq l,\\
0,&|x|>l,\end{cases}

and

X^{T_{1}}(\lambda)(t):=\int_{0}^{t}c^{T_{1}}_{l,B}(\lambda)(s)\,ds,

where
a_{T_{1}}\in W^{1,\infty}(0,\infty)
and

c^{T_{1}}_{l,B}(\lambda)(s)=\frac{2l}{\pi}\int_{-B}^{B}K(s+T_{1},y)e^{\lambda y}\sin\frac{\pi y}{2l}\mathrm{d}y.

Then

\underline{u}_{\lambda}(t,x):=\eta\phi^{T_{1}}_{\lambda}\bigl(t,x-x_{0}-X^{T_{1}}(\lambda)(t)\bigr)

is a subsolution of

v_{t}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t+T_{1},y)\big[v(t,x-y)-v(t,x)\big]\mathrm{d}y+vf(t+T_{1},v).

On the other hand,
v(t,x):=u(t+T_{1},x)
is a solution of this shifted
equation. Choosing
\eta>0
sufficiently small and
x_{0}
properly, we may
assume

\underline{u}_{\lambda}(0,x)\leq v(0,x)=u(T_{1},x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},\quad\lambda\in[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}].

By the maximum principle,

\underline{u}_{\lambda}(t,x)\leq u(t+T_{1},x),\qquad t\geq 0,\ x\in\mathbb{R},\quad\lambda\in[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}].

Due to the continuity of
\lambda\mapsto X^{T_{1}}(\lambda)(t)
, for each
\hat{t}>T_{0}
and
x-x_{0}\in[c_{1}^{+}\hat{t},c_{2}^{+}\hat{t}]
,
there exists
\hat{\lambda}\in[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]
such that

x-x_{0}=X^{T_{1}}(\hat{\lambda})(\hat{t})
and

u(\hat{t}+T_{1},x)\geq\underline{u}_{\hat{\lambda}}(\hat{t},x)=\eta\phi^{T_{1}}_{\hat{\lambda}}\bigl(\hat{t},x-x_{0}-X^{T_{1}}(\hat{\lambda})(\hat{t})\bigr)=\eta e^{a_{T_{1}}(\hat{t})}\geq\eta e^{-\|a_{T_{1}}\|_{\infty}}>0.

Hence, we have

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\inf_{x\in[c_{1}^{+}t+x_{0},\;c_{2}^{+}t+x_{0}]}u(t+T_{1},x)>\eta e^{-\|a\|_{\infty}}>0.

Since
T_{1}
and
x_{0}
are fixed, they do not affect the limit as
t\to+\infty
. We obtain (
3.5
).

Step 2:
We use the approximation method to prove the situation
\lambda^{*}_{+}=i_{+}<+\infty
. The proof is inspired by
[
10
,
19
]
.

In this case, we see that

c^{*}_{+}=\lim_{\lambda\to i_{+}-}\frac{H(\lambda)}{\lambda}=\inf_{\lambda\in(0,i_{+})}c(\lambda)=c(i_{+})\text{ and }\lim_{\lambda\to i_{+}}c^{\prime}(\lambda)\leq 0.

The assumption (
1.7
) implies that
\lambda^{*}_{+}=i_{+}
is the unique minimum point of
c(\lambda)
.
For
n>0
, set

K_{n}(t,y):=K(t,y)\chi_{(-\infty,n]}(y)

and define

H_{n}(\lambda):=\left\langle\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{n}(\cdot,y)e^{\lambda y}\mathrm{d}y-\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(\cdot,y)\mathrm{d}y+r(\cdot)\right\rangle,\qquad\lambda>0,

and

c^{*}_{n,+}:=\inf_{\lambda>0}\frac{H_{n}(\lambda)}{\lambda}.

Equivalently,

H_{n}(\lambda)=\int_{-\infty}^{n}\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle e^{\lambda y}\mathrm{d}y-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle\mathrm{d}y+\langle r\rangle.

For each fixed
\lambda\in(0,i_{+})
, the dominated convergence theorem gives

H_{n}(\lambda)\to H(\lambda)\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Moreover, for
n
large enough,
H_{n}(0)>0
.
Since
K_{n}
has bounded support on the right for large
n
, we have

\frac{H_{n}(\lambda)}{\lambda}\to+\infty\quad\text{as }\lambda\to 0^{+}\quad\text{and as }\lambda\to+\infty.

Hence
c^{*}_{n,+}
is attained at some
\lambda^{*}_{n,+}\in(0,+\infty)
.

We first prove

\lim_{n\to\infty}c^{*}_{n,+}=c^{*}_{+}.

For every fixed
\lambda\in(0,i_{+})
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}c^{*}_{n,+}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{H_{n}(\lambda)}{\lambda}=\frac{H(\lambda)}{\lambda}.

Letting
\lambda\to(i_{+})^{-}
, we obtain

\limsup_{n\to\infty}c^{*}_{n,+}\leq c^{*}_{+}.

Then we prove the opposite inequality. Let
\lambda^{*}_{n,+}
be a minimizer of
H_{n}(\lambda)/\lambda
.
The previously upper bound implies that
\{c^{*}_{n,+}\}
is bounded above. We claim that

\{\lambda^{*}_{n,+}\}
is bounded and bounded away from zero. Indeed, since
H_{n}(0)\to\langle r\rangle>0
,
we see that
H_{n}(\lambda)/\lambda
is uniformly large as
\lambda\to 0^{+}
. On the other hand, since
i_{+}<+\infty
,
there exists an interval
[a,b]\subset(0,\infty)
such that

\int_{a}^{b}\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle\mathrm{d}y>0.

For
n>b
,

H_{n}(\lambda)\geq e^{\lambda a}\int_{a}^{b}\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle\mathrm{d}y-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle\mathrm{d}y+\langle r\rangle,

and therefore
H_{n}(\lambda)/\lambda\to+\infty
as
\lambda\to+\infty
, uniformly for large
n
.
Thus, up to a subsequence,

\lambda^{*}_{n,+}\to\lambda_{0}

for some
\lambda_{0}\in(0,+\infty)
.

We next show that
\lambda_{0}=i_{+}
. If
\lambda_{0}>i_{+}
, choose
\mu
such that

i_{+}<\mu<\lambda_{0}
. Then
\lambda^{*}_{n,+}>\mu
for large
n
. Note that

H^{\prime}_{n}(\lambda^{*}_{n,+})=c^{*}_{n,+}.

Thus we have

c^{*}_{n,+}=H^{\prime}_{n}(\lambda^{*}_{n,+})\geq H^{\prime}_{n}(\mu).

But
\mu\notin\mathcal{I}
, hence

H^{\prime}_{n}(\mu)=\int_{-\infty}^{n}\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle e^{\mu y}y\mathrm{d}y\to+\infty,\text{ as }n\to+\infty,

which contradicts the boundedness of
c^{*}_{n,+}
. Therefore
\lambda_{0}\leq i_{+}
.

If
\lambda_{0}<i_{+}
, then by dominated convergence,

\lim_{n\to\infty}c^{*}_{n,+}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{H_{n}(\lambda^{*}_{n,+})}{\lambda^{*}_{n,+}}=\frac{H(\lambda_{0})}{\lambda_{0}}.

Since in the present endpoint case the minimum of
H(\lambda)/\lambda
is attained only at
\lambda=i_{+}
,
we have

\frac{H(\lambda_{0})}{\lambda_{0}}>c^{*}_{+},

which contradicts the already proved inequality

\limsup_{n\to\infty}c^{*}_{n,+}\leq c^{*}_{+}.

Hence
\lambda_{0}=i_{+}
.

Finally, using
K_{n}\leq K
and Fatou’s lemma, we get

\liminf_{n\to\infty}H_{n}(\lambda^{*}_{n,+})\geq H(i_{+}).

Consequently,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}c^{*}_{n,+}=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{H_{n}(\lambda^{*}_{n,+})}{\lambda^{*}_{n,+}}\geq\frac{H(i_{+})}{i_{+}}=c^{*}_{+}.

Together with the upper bound, this proves

\lim_{n\to\infty}c^{*}_{n,+}=c^{*}_{+}.

Now let
u^{n}
be the solution of

\partial_{t}u^{n}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{n}(t,y)u^{n}(t,x-y)\mathrm{d}y-\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,y)\mathrm{d}y\,u^{n}(t,x)+u^{n}f(t,u^{n}),\qquad u^{n}(0,x)=u_{0}(x).

Since
K_{n}\leq K
, the comparison principle gives

u^{n}(t,x)\leq u(t,x),\qquad t\geq 0,\ x\in\mathbb{R}.

For the approximate equation, the right endpoint of the interval
\mathcal{I}
is
+\infty
. Meanwhile,

c^{*}_{n,+}
is attained at the interior point
\lambda^{*}_{n,+}
. Hence Step 1 applies to
u^{n}
.
Let
c^{*}_{+}-\delta<c^{+}_{1}<c^{+}_{2}<c^{*}_{+}
. Since
c^{*}_{n,+}\to c^{*}_{+}
, for all large
n
we have

c^{+}_{2}<c^{*}_{n,+}

and
c^{+}_{1},c^{+}_{2}
are still in the admissible interval in Step 1 for the approximate equation. Therefore,

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\inf_{x\in[c^{+}_{1}t,c^{+}_{2}t]}u^{n}(t,x)>0.

Using
u^{n}\leq u
, we conclude that

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\inf_{x\in[c^{+}_{1}t,c^{+}_{2}t]}u(t,x)>0.

This proves the endpoint case. The proof is complete.

Remark 3.4

In the endpoint case
\lambda_{+}^{*}=i_{+}<+\infty
, the direct construction
used in Step 1 may not work.
The reason is that the subsolution moves with speed, in the average sense, close to
H^{\prime}(\lambda)
, while the linearly determined speed is
H(\lambda)/\lambda
. When the minimum is attained at an interior point, these two quantities coincide at
\lambda=\lambda_{+}^{*}
because

c^{\prime}(\lambda_{+}^{*})=0
.
At the endpoint case, this identity is no longer available.
Thus
H^{\prime}(\lambda)
may fail to approach
c_{+}^{*}
as
\lambda\to i_{+}-
.
So we truncate the kernel function, which turns the endpoint
minimum into an interior minimum for the approximating problems.

By the symmetric argument, we can obtain the similar lemma.

Lemma 3.5

Assume that
c^{*}_{+}+c^{*}_{-}>0
.
For all small
\delta>0
, for all
-c^{*}_{-}<-c_{2}^{-}<-c_{1}^{-}<-c^{*}_{-}+\delta
, we have

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\inf_{x\in[-c_{2}^{-}t,\;-c_{1}^{-}t]}u(t,x)>0.

(3.6)

Next, we will adopt the idea in
[
19
]
to prove the inner spreading speed of our problem. We first use (
3.5
) and (
3.5
) to show the following lemma.

Lemma 3.6

Assume
c^{*}_{+}+c^{*}_{-}>0
.
Then we have

\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\inf_{x\in[(-c_{-}^{*}+\epsilon)t,(c_{+}^{*}-\epsilon)t]}u(t,x)>0,\quad\forall\epsilon>0.

Proof.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Choose
c_{l}
and
c_{r}
such that

-c^{*}_{-}<c_{l}<-c^{*}_{-}+\varepsilon,\qquad c^{*}_{+}-\varepsilon<c_{r}<c^{*}_{+}.

Let
\theta>0
be small such that
c_{l}+\theta<c_{r}-\theta
. By Lemmas
3.3
and
3.5
, there exist
\delta>0
and
T_{0}>0
such that

u(t,x)\geq\delta

for all
t\geq T_{0}
and

x\in[c_{l}t,(c_{l}+\theta)t]\cup[(c_{r}-\theta)t,c_{r}t].

We claim that

\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\inf_{x\in[c_{l}t,c_{r}t]}u(t,x)>0.

Suppose not. Define

m(t):=\min_{x\in[c_{l}t,c_{r}t]}u(t,x).

Then
\liminf_{t\to\infty}m(t)=0
. We choose a sequence
t_{n}\to\infty
such that

m(t_{n})=\min_{s\in[T_{0},t_{n}]}m(s),\qquad m(t_{n})\to 0.

Let
x_{n}\in[c_{l}t_{n},c_{r}t_{n}]
satisfy

u(t_{n},x_{n})=m(t_{n}).

Since
m(t_{n})<\delta
for large
n
, we have

x_{n}\in[(c_{l}+\theta)t_{n},(c_{r}-\theta)t_{n}].

Hence

[x_{n}-\theta t_{n},x_{n}+\theta t_{n}]\subset[c_{l}t_{n},c_{r}t_{n}].

Therefore

u(t_{n},y)\geq u(t_{n},x_{n}),\qquad y\in[x_{n}-\theta t_{n},x_{n}+\theta t_{n}].

Moreover, since
x_{n}
stays away from the moving
boundaries, for all sufficiently small
h>0
and large
n
, we have

x_{n}\in[c_{l}(t_{n}-h),c_{r}(t_{n}-h)].

Thus

u(t_{n}-h,x_{n})\geq m(t_{n}-h)\geq m(t_{n})=u(t_{n},x_{n}).

Consequently the left time derivative at
(t_{n},x_{n})
is nonpositive, in the
sense of the lower left Dini derivative. At the differentiable point
(t_{n},x_{n})
, we obtain

0\geq\partial_{t}u(t_{n},x_{n}).

On the other hand, by the KPP assumption,

f(t,u)\geq r(t)-Mu,\qquad 0\leq u\leq 1.

Using the spatial minimality of
u(t_{n},x_{n})
on
[c_{l}t_{n},c_{r}t_{n}]
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t_{n},x_{n}-y)u(t_{n},y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geq\int_{x_{n}-\theta t_{n}}^{x_{n}+\theta t_{n}}K(t_{n},x_{n}-y)u(t_{n},y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geq u(t_{n},x_{n})\int_{-\theta t_{n}}^{\theta t_{n}}K(t_{n},y)\mathrm{d}y.

Therefore

0\geq\partial_{t}u(t_{n},x_{n})\geq u(t_{n},x_{n})\left\{\int_{-\theta t_{n}}^{\theta t_{n}}K(t_{n},y)\mathrm{d}y-\overline{K}_{0}(t_{n})+r(t_{n})-Mu(t_{n},x_{n})\right\}.

(3.7)

Here
\overline{K}_{0}(t_{n})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t_{n},y)\mathrm{d}y
. Since
0\in\mathcal{I}
, namely

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|K(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty)}\mathrm{d}y<\infty,

we see that

0\leq\overline{K}_{0}(t_{n})-\int_{-\theta t_{n}}^{\theta t_{n}}K(t_{n},y)\mathrm{d}y\leq\int_{|y|>\theta t_{n}}\|K(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty)}\mathrm{d}y\to 0.

Since
u(t_{n},x_{n})=m(t_{n})\to 0
and
\inf_{t\geq 0}r(t)>0
, we obtain

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left\{\int_{-\theta t_{n}}^{\theta t_{n}}K(t_{n},y)\mathrm{d}y-K_{0}(t_{n})+r(t_{n})-Mu(t_{n},x_{n})\right\}\geq\inf_{t\geq 0}r(t)>0.

Since
u(t_{n},x_{n})>0
, this
contradicts the inequality (
3.7
). Hence

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\inf_{x\in[c_{l}t,c_{r}t]}u(t,x)>0.

Due to the choice of
c_{l}
and
c_{r}
, the proof is complete.

We next prove convergence to
1
in the inner spreading region. We first need two elementary estimates for the following nonautonomous equation.

Lemma 3.7

Let
\tau>0
and
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a<b
. Let
v_{i}(t,x)
,
i=1,2
, be solutions of the following equation

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v_{i}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,x-y)v_{i}(t,y)\mathrm{d}y-\overline{K}(t)v_{i}(t,x)+v_{i}(t,x)f(t,v_{i}(t,x)),&t\geq\tau,x\in\mathbb{R},\\
v_{i}(\tau,x)=v_{0}^{i}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

(3.8)

where
v_{0}^{i}
,
i=1,2
, are continuous functions with
0\leq v_{0}^{i}\leq 1
. If
v_{0}^{1}(x)=v_{0}^{2}(x)
for
x\in[a\tau,b\tau]
, then

\big|v_{1}(\tau+t,z)-v_{2}(\tau+t,z)\big|\leq ce^{ct}e^{-\eta\theta\tau/2},\ \forall t\geq 0,\ z\in[(a+\theta)\tau,(b-\theta)\tau],

where
\theta\in(0,({b-a})/{2})
,
\eta
is given in Assumption
1.5
and
c
is a constant depending on
K\text{ and }f
. In particular, fixing
T>0
,
for any
\sigma>0
, there is
M_{0}>0
such that if
v_{0}^{1}(x)=v_{0}^{2}(x)
for
x\in[a\tau,b\tau]
, then

\big|v_{1}(\tau+T,z)-v_{2}(\tau+T,z)\big|\leq\sigma,\ \forall\tau>M_{0},\ z\in[(a+\theta)\tau,(b-\theta)\tau].

Proof.
For a fixed
z\in\mathbb{R}
, introduce the space

X_{z}:=\left\{\phi\in C(\mathbb{R});\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}e^{-\eta|x-z|/2}|\phi(x)|<\infty\right\}

(3.9)

equipped with norm
\|\phi\|_{z}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}e^{-\eta|x-z|/2}|\phi(x)|
.

Now we denote
w(t,x)=v_{1}(t,x)-v_{2}(t,x)
. Note that
w
satisfies

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}w(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,x-y)w(t,y)\mathrm{d}y+B(t,x)w(t,x),&t\geq\tau,x\in\mathbb{R},\\
w(\tau,x)=v_{0}^{1}(x)-v_{0}^{2}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

(3.10)

where
B(t,x):=\frac{v_{1}f(t,v_{1})-v_{2}f(t,v_{2})}{v_{1}-v_{2}}-\overline{K}(t)
if
v_{1}\neq v_{2}
and
-\overline{K}(t)
if
v_{1}=v_{2}
. From (
3.10
), we have

w(t,x)=w(\tau,x)+\int_{\tau}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(s,y)w(s,x-y)\mathrm{d}yds+\int_{\tau}^{t}B(s,x)w(s,x)ds.

Therefore,

\begin{split}&\quad e^{-\eta|x-z|/2}w(t,x)\\
&\leq\|w(\tau,\cdot)\|_{z}+\int_{\tau}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(s,x-y)e^{-\eta|x-z|/2}\big|w(s,y)\big|\mathrm{d}yds+\int_{\tau}^{t}\big|B(s,x)e^{-\eta|x-z|/2}w(s,x)\big|ds\\
&\leq\|w(\tau,\cdot)\|_{z}+\int_{\tau}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(s,x-y)e^{\eta|y-z|/2-\eta|x-z|/2}\|w(s,\cdot)\|_{z}\mathrm{d}yds+\int_{\tau}^{t}\big|B(s,x)|\|w(s,\cdot)\|_{z}ds\\
&\leq\|w(\tau,\cdot)\|_{z}+\int_{\tau}^{t}\big(\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(s,y)e^{\eta|y|/2}dy+|B(s,x)|\big)\|w(s,\cdot)\|_{z}ds.\end{split}

(3.11)

By Assumption
1.5
,
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|K(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty)}e^{\eta|y|/2}\mathrm{d}y<\infty
. Hence (
3.11
) gives

\|w(t,\cdot)\|_{z}\leq\|w(\tau,\cdot)\|_{z}+M\int_{\tau}^{t}\|w(s,\cdot)\|_{z}ds,\quad\forall t>\tau,\ z\in\mathbb{R},

where
M
is a constant depending only on
K
and
f
.
The Gronwall’s inequality implies that

\|w(t,\cdot)\|_{z}\leq\|w(\tau,\cdot)\|_{z}e^{M(t-\tau)}\leq\|w(\tau,\cdot)\|_{z}e^{MT},\forall t\in[\tau,\tau+T],z\in\mathbb{R}.

That means

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\big(e^{-\eta|x-z|/2}|v_{1}(t,x)-v_{2}(t,x)|\big)\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\big(e^{-\eta|x-z|/2}|v_{0}^{1}(x)-v_{0}^{2}(x)|\big)e^{MT},\ \forall t>\tau,\ z\in\mathbb{R}.

Particularly, since
v_{0}^{1}(x)=v_{0}^{2}(x)
for all
x\in[a\tau,b\tau]
, we have

\begin{split}|v_{1}(\tau+T,z)-v_{2}(\tau+T,z)|&\leq e^{MT}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\big(e^{-\eta|x-z|/2}|v_{0}^{1}(x)-v_{0}^{2}(x)|\big)\\
&=e^{MT}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[a\tau,b\tau]}\big(e^{-\eta|x-z|/2}|v_{0}^{1}(x)-v_{0}^{2}(x)|\big).\end{split}

Due to
0\leq v_{0}^{1},v_{0}^{2}\leq 1
, we see that for all
z\in[(a+\theta)\tau,(b-\theta)\tau]

|v_{1}(\tau+T,z)-v_{2}(\tau+T,z)|\leq 2e^{MT}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[a\tau,b\tau]}e^{-\eta|x-z|/2}\leq 2e^{MT}e^{-\eta\theta\tau/2}.

This completes the proof.

Next we state that a solution starting from a positive constant function will converge to
1
.

Lemma 3.8

For all constant
\alpha\in(0,1)
, let
\tau\geq 0
and
v
be a solution of

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(t,x-y)v(t,y)\mathrm{d}y-\overline{K}(t)v(t,x)+v(t,x)f(t,v(t,x)),&t\geq\tau,x\in\mathbb{R},\\
v(\tau,x)=\alpha,&x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

(3.12)

Then we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}v(t,x)=1\quad\text{ uniformly in }x\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof.
From Assumption
1.4
, we have
h(v):=\inf_{t\geq 0}f(t,v)>0
for all
v\in[0,1)
and
h(1)=0
.
Let
\underline{v}
be the solution of

\underline{v}^{\prime}(t)=\underline{v}(t)h(\underline{v}(t)),\;\;\forall t\geq s,\;\;\text{ and }\;\underline{v}(\tau)=\alpha.

By comparison principle, we have

\underline{v}(t)\leq v(t,x)\leq 1,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R},t\geq\tau.

Note that
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\underline{v}(t)=1
. This completes the proof.

The next lemma completes the proof of Theorem
1.8
. Its proof follows the argument in
[
19
]
.

Lemma 3.9

For all
-c^{*}_{-}<c_{l}<c_{r}<c^{*}_{+}
, we have

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\inf_{x\in[c_{l}t,c_{r}t]}u(t,x)=1.

Proof.
Since
0\leq u\leq 1
, it is enough to prove that for any small
\theta>0
, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
T_{0}>0
large enough such that

u(t,x)\geq 1-\varepsilon,\forall t>T_{0},\;x\in[(c_{l}+2\theta)t,(c_{r}-2\theta)t].

Set
\alpha:=\frac{1}{2}\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\inf_{x\in[c_{l}t,c_{r}t]}u(t,x)\in(0,1)
.
Let
w:=w(t,x;\tau,\alpha)
be a solution of (
3.12
). The notation means that the initial time is
\tau
. Lemma
3.8
gives

\lim_{s\to+\infty}w(s+\tau,x;\tau,\alpha)=1,\text{ uniformly in }x\in\mathbb{R}.

Hence, for each
\sigma>0
, there is
T>0
such that

w(s+\tau,x;\tau,\alpha)\geq 1-\sigma,\forall s\geq T.

We define

u_{0}^{\tau}(\cdot):=\min\{\alpha,u(\tau,\cdot;0,u_{0})\}.

Here
u(\tau,\cdot;0,u_{0})
denotes the solution of (
1.1
) at time
\tau
.
Let
\underline{u}^{\tau}(t,\cdot;\tau,u_{0}^{\tau}(\cdot))
be the solution of (
3.8
) equipped with initial data
\underline{u}^{\tau}(\tau,\cdot)=u_{0}^{\tau}(\cdot)
.
From the definition of
\alpha
, note that
u^{\tau}_{0}(x)=\alpha
for all
x\in[(c_{l}+\theta)\tau,(c_{r}-\theta)\tau]
and
\tau>M
large enough.
Applying Lemma
3.7
to
\underline{u}^{\tau}
and
w
, and increasing
M
if necessary, we have for all
\tau>M

|w(\tau+T,x;\tau,\alpha)-\underline{u}^{\tau}(\tau+T,x;\tau,u_{0}^{\tau}(x))|\leq\sigma,\quad\forall x\in[(c_{l}+\theta)\tau,(c_{r}-\theta)\tau].

(3.13)

Hence,
\underline{u}^{\tau}(\tau+T,x;\tau,u_{0}^{\tau}(\cdot))\geq w(\tau+T,x;\tau,\alpha)-\sigma\geq 1-2\sigma
for all
x\in[(c_{l}+\theta)\tau,(c_{r}-\theta)\tau]
. Increasing
M
once more, we may assume that for all
t\geq M+T
,

[(c_{l}+2\theta)t,(c_{r}-2\theta)t]\subset[(c_{l}+\theta)(t-T),(c_{r}-\theta)(t-T)].

Set
\tau=t-T
. For all
t\geq T+M
large enough, we have

\underline{u}^{t-T}(t,x;t-T,u_{0}^{t-T}(\cdot))\geq 1-2\sigma,\quad\forall x\in[(c_{l}+2\theta)t,(c_{r}-2\theta)t].

Note that
u(t-T,x;0,u_{0})\geq u_{0}^{t-T}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. The comparison principle ensures that

u(t,x;0,u_{0})=u(t,x;t-T,u(t-T,x;0,u_{0}))\geq\underline{u}^{t-T}(t,x;t-T,u_{0}^{t-T}).

Therefore, we obtain

u(t,x;0,u_{0})\geq 1-2\sigma,\quad\forall t\geq T+M,x\in[(c_{l}+2\theta)t,(c_{r}-2\theta)t].

This completes the proof.

4
Special cases

We finish with a few simple cases covered by the assumptions above.

Example 4.1
(Separated time dependence)

Let
K(t,y)=d(t)J(y)
, where
d\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty)
is nonnegative and has a uniform mean value, and where
J\geq 0
satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}}J(y)e^{\lambda y}\mathrm{d}y<\infty
for
\lambda
in a neighborhood of the origin. Then

H(\lambda)=\langle d\rangle\int_{\mathbb{R}}J(y)(e^{\lambda y}-1)\mathrm{d}y+\langle r\rangle,

and the rightward and leftward speeds are obtained from (
1.10
). If
J
is not symmetric, the two speeds are generally different.

Example 4.2
(One-sided kernels)

If
\langle K(\cdot,y)\rangle=0
for a.e.
y>0
, then the averaged kernel allows no positive jumps. In this case
c^{*}_{+}=0
, whereas the leftward speed may be positive. The opposite conclusion holds when the averaged kernel is supported in
[0,\infty)
.
