Determinants of seasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccine uptake in high-risk individuals in northern Sweden
Authors: Lucas Grape, Wilma Persson, Adam Sergejev, Simon Vihtari Hansson, Lina Gyllencreutz, Henrik Togstad, Johan Normark, Urban Johansson Kostenniemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSeasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccination in high-risk individuals prevent disease, disability, and death. Nevertheless, many high-income countries continue to experience suboptimal vaccine coverage. We therefore aimed to identify the remaining vaccination barriers in these settings. We recruited patients seeking emergency department care at a hospital in northern Sweden. Using semi-structured interviews and medical records, we collected high-resolution individual-level data, and linked community-level determinants from national registers. Logistic regression was used to calculate odds ratios (OR) and adjusted odds ratios (aOR) for determinants associated with influenza and COVID-19 vaccination. We included 600 patients of whom 450 were eligible for both vaccines. Coverage among eligible individuals was 52.0% for influenza and 54.2% for COVID-19. Co-administration of both vaccines was high (OR 55.33, 95% CI: 30.86–99.22). Individual-level determinants associated with lower vaccine uptake included male biological sex (aOR: 0.57, 95% CI: 0.37–0.88, younger age (aOR: 0.50, 95% CI: 0.41–0.61), smoking (aOR: 0.43, 95% CI: 0.18–1.02), reduced independence (aOR: 0.59, 95% CI: 0.31–1.10), and age-only eligibility (aOR 0.41, 95% CI: 0.19–0.86). In multimorbid individuals, meeting more eligibility criteria showed no significant association with higher uptake (aOR: 1.14, 95% CI: 0.93–1.39). Finally, we noted only limited influence of community-level determinants. In conclusion, vaccine uptake for influenza and COVID-19 was primarily associated with individual-level determinants, whereas community-level showed little impact. Targeted communication, such as risk-stacking to improve risk-awareness, harmonized eligibility criteria, and co-administration, may help overcome remaining vaccination barriers in high-risk individuals.KEYWORDS: Vaccination; influenza vaccines; COVID-19 vaccines; patient acceptance of health care; health knowledge, attitudes, practice; risk factors

Introduction

Adults at increased risk of severe outcomes from respiratory viral infections include older individuals and those with chronic cardiopulmonary disease, diabetes, renal or hepatic failure, immunocompromise, or reduced functional capacity. In these groups, influenza and Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) remain major drivers of hospitalization and mortality despite effectiveness of available vaccines. Although Sweden provides seasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccination free of charge to high-risk individuals, coverage remains below optimal levels, indicating persistent barriers beyond cost. This raises a key question; which barriers still exist among high‑risk individuals in high-income countries?

Severe viral respiratory infections primarily include influenza and COVID-19, as these viral pathogens frequently cause seasonal outbreaks that may escalate into large-scale epidemics. High-risk individuals, such as elderly or persons with pre-existing conditions are particularly vulnerable. Among high-risk individuals who contract influenza, approximately 16.3% will require hospital admission and 1.0% will die. The corresponding proportions for COVID-19 are 16.2% and 0.7%.1,2 Annual community mortality among adults ≥65 y is estimated at 42.9 and 88.5 per 100,000 for influenza and COVID‑19, respectively.3 Taken together, these figures highlight the substantial disease burden that severe viral respiratory infections pose to elderly in high-income countries.3

Vaccinations drastically reduce risk of death in the elderly; by 50% for influenza4 and 34–95% for COVID-19 depending on number of dosages and timing.5 Similar effects have been reported in other risk groups. This highlights the importance of achieving high vaccine uptake in high-risk individuals.4–6

To increase coverage, Sweden has offers free seasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccination. Eligibility criteria include advanced age and the presence of specific chronic conditions (Table 1).7–13 Unlike the near‑100% coverage achieved in Sweden’s childhood immunization programme, vaccine uptake among high‑risk adults remains suboptimal. During the 2024/2025 season, coverage among individuals aged 65 y or older was 69.0% for influenza and 70.4% for COVID-19.14,15

Barriers to high vaccination coverage are extensively studied and operate at both individual and community levels. At the individual level, commonly reported determinants of lower vaccine uptake include vaccine hesitancy, socio-demographic characteristics such as male sex, lower educational attainment, minority status, and financial constraints.16–18 At the community level, factors such as poverty, rurality, and access to healthcare influence both vaccination opportunities and population attitudes. Systematic reviews consistently show socioeconomic inequalities in vaccine uptake, though associations vary across contexts, particularly between low- and middle-income and high-income settings.19,20

Two important knowledge gaps still exist; First, most studies focus on low-income or middle-income settings where vaccines are not universally free, emphasizing financial and access barriers. Less is known about high-income settings with publicly funded vaccination programmes, where behavioral and contextual factors may play a larger role.21 Second, although both individual- and community-level determinants are described individually, few studies examine them simultaneously.22,23 As a result, we still lack a clear understanding of how individual-level and community-level determinants interact.

To address these knowledge gaps, we examined the association of individual‑ and community‑level determinants with uptake of seasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccines in a high-income setting where vaccines are provided free of charge. Ultimately, our objective was to identify actionable targets for future interventions designed to increase vaccine uptake.

Material and methods

Study population

We prospectively recruited patients at the University Hospital of Umeå during two consecutive years. Adults aged 18–100 y requiring emergency care were eligible.

Study period and vaccination policy

Patients were recruited between October 1st, 2023 and April 30th 2025, thereby capturing two consecutive Swedish seasonal vaccination campaigns (2023/2024 and 2024/2025), which are delivered starting in October or November. Eligibility followed national recommendations from the Public Health Agency of Sweden (Table 1) with no region-specific eligibility criteria; vaccines are provided free of charge to eligible risk groups and co-administration of influenza and COVID-19 vaccines is encouraged in routine delivery. Vaccines used during the study period were Efluelda Tetra® (Sanofi Pasteur), Vaxigrip Tetra® (Sanofi Pasteur), and Fluenz Tetra® (AstraZeneca AB) for influenza, and Comirnaty® (BioNTech Manufacturing GmbH) for COVID-19.24

Collection of individual-level data

We collected data on vaccination status for influenza and COVID-19, previously known infections, pre-existing conditions, age, weight and length, and symptoms at the emergency department through semi-structured patient interviews during the care episode. Full postal address including postal code and postal town was also registered during the interview. Electronic medical record data were retrieved from three region-wide sources: (i) the emergency department patient register (AWERIA) for clinical findings at the emergency department, (ii) the electronic health record for clinical findings, pre-existing conditions and registered International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnoses at emergency department discharge for non-admitted patients and at hospital discharge for admitted patients, and (iii) the centralized laboratory information system for laboratory results including information regarding Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR) results. If routine clinical care did not include RT‑PCR testing for SARS‑CoV‑2 or influenza A/B, research staff collected a standardized nasopharyngeal RT‑PCR sample. Sampling was performed on the day of the emergency department assessment or within the subsequent two weeks to evaluate ongoing viral respiratory infection. Charlson Comorbidity Index was computed using validated conversion protocols.25 All data were collected and stored in REDCap Electronic Data Capture tools hosted at Umeå University.

Acquisition of community-level data

The patients home addresses were used to extract postal code, postal town, and municipality of residency.

We used the Postal Code Database (PDB), a nation-wide register containing information for all postal codes in Sweden to extract postal codes.26 The PDB compiles data from authoritative sources including the postal company PostNord for postal codes and localities, the Swedish Mapping, Cadastral and Land Registration Authority for geospatial information, Statistics Sweden (SCB) for demographic and income statistics, as well as supplementary data from municipalities and other public registers. This database contains demographic and income data updated through 2024 and provided mean annual income for each postal code and, at the postal‑town level, median annual income, earnings distribution, and population density (inhabitants/km2). If data on postal-code level was missing, we used income data on postal-town level as a placeholder. Based on earnings distribution data, we calculated an income ratio by dividing percentage of low-income category households by percentage of high-income category households for each study participant’s postal town. We calculated income ratio divergence by computing numerical difference between income ratio and a state of perfect balance between low-income and high-income category households (Income ratio = 1.0). Additionally, we obtained mean annual income, the GINI-coefficient for income distribution, and population density (inhabitants/km2) from SCB at municipal level, and unemployment, poverty, election participation, educational attainment, and the proportion of foreign-born individuals at both the postal-town level (using Regionala statistikområden, RegSO) and the municipal level. Finally, we also added each municipality’s three-level rural – urban classification from the Swedish Agency for Economic and Regional Growth.

Variable categorization

Categorized individual-level variables included body mass index (BMI), smoking habits, level of education, level of dependence, and living situation. BMI was calculated from height and weight and categorized using validated cut-offs27 as “underweight” (BMI < 18.5),” “normal weight” (BMI 18.5–24.9), “overweight” (BMI 25.0–29.9) and “obesity” (BMI ≥ 30). Smoking habits were categorized as “non-smoker” (no history of active smoking), “previous smoker” (previous history of active smoking but currently not engaged in active smoking), and “current smoker” (currently being an active smoker). Educational level was categorized according to the Swedish education system28 as “lower education level” (Swedish compulsory primary level school of 9 y), “medium education level” (2–3 y secondary upper secondary level education), and “higher education level” (having attended tertiary level education, i.e. college or university studies). Level of dependence was categorized as “no assistance needed” (fully independent), “non-medical home care” (needing home care for at least one activity of daily life but residing at-home), and “long-term care facility” (living in any type of assistant living facility). Living situation was categorized as “lives alone” or “share household,” with the latter defined as having at least one other person residing in the same household.

For community-level variables, population density at postal town and municipal levels was used to classify degree of urbanization (DEGURBA) according to the Eurostat 2021 “Degree of Urbanization” guideline29 into “mostly uninhabited area” (<10 inhabitants/km2), “very low density rural cluster cell” (10–50 inhabitants/km2), “low density rural cluster cell” (50–300 inhabitants/km2), “semi-dense urban cluster cell” (300–1,500 inhabitants/km2) and “dense urban cluster cell” (≥1,500 inhabitants/km2).

All other variables were analyzed and presented without categorization or grouping.

Grouping of study participants according to national vaccination criteria

We assessed each study participant according to the national recommendations issued by the Public Health Agency of Sweden for vaccinations of adults against influenza and COVID-19.7–13 For each vaccine, participants received a score corresponding to the number of eligibility criteria fulfilled (Table 1). Based on this score, individuals were classified as either recommended for vaccination or not meeting eligibility criteria.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome was an aggregated measure of seasonal vaccine uptake: the total number of seasonal vaccinations across influenza and COVID-19, by at least one dose, received in the past 12 months, treated as a three-level ordinal outcome. Secondary outcomes were vaccine-specific uptake measures defined as receipt of at least one dose within the past 12 months for: (i) influenza vaccination and for (ii) COVID-19 vaccination as a binary outcome.

Statistical analysis

We performed all statistical analyses using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 30 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive proportions were presented with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) according to Jeffreys, means were presented with standard deviation (SD) and medians with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s χ2 test and ordinal variables using the Kruskal–Wallis test. For continuous variables, normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Non-normal data were analyzed with the Mann-Whitney U test. For normally distributed variables, variance homogeneity was evaluated with Levene’s test followed by the independent-samples t-test when variances were equal and Welch’s t-test when they were not. A two-sided p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To assess the association between potential determinants and vaccine uptake, we first conducted univariate analyses, estimating crude odds ratios (OR) for each determinant. We used Pearson’s χ2 test for categorical determinants, trend tests based on the Mantel–Haenszel linear-by-linear association for ordinal determinants, and simple logistic regression for continuous determinants.

A regression model was developed using a stepwise approach; First, all individual-level and community-level determinants were included in a preliminary regression model. Second, multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and determinants with a VIF above the set threshold of 5.0 were removed to ensure model stability and reduce collinearity. This step was repeated until all remaining determinants were below the VIF threshold, resulting in a final model comprising of following individual-level determinants: age, biological sex, level of education, level of independence, living situation, smoking habits, previous illnesses, BMI, fulfillment of vaccine eligibility criteria, and previous infections with influenza and COVID-19, and the following community-level determinants: earnings at postal-code and postal-town level, at-risk-of-poverty at postal-town level, income ratio divergence at postal-town level, foreign-born at postal-town and municipal level, unemployment rate at postal-town level, national election participation at municipal level, population density at municipal level and degree of urbanization at postal-town level. Lastly, the final model was assessed using goodness-of-fit measurements with the model showing a statistically significant improvement over the crude model (p < .001), and Nagelkerke’s pseudo R2 at 0.348 indicated moderate explanatory power.

Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) were calculated using binary logistic regression for binary outcomes and ordinal logistic regression for ordinal outcomes.

Finally, to assess potential clustering and the need for multilevel modeling, generalized linear mixed models with a cumulative logit link were initially fitted with random intercepts specified separately at the postal code, postal town, and municipal levels. Clustering was evaluated by examining variance components and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). As these analyses indicated negligible between-cluster variation (ICC ≈ 0 across all levels), multilevel modeling was not considered warranted, and subsequent analyses were conducted using a standard regression framework.

Post-hoc power analysis and Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE)

To assess the study’s statistical power to detect meaningful associations, a minimum detectable effect (MDE) analysis was conducted. The MDE was estimated using standard approximations for logistic regression models, based on the observed sample size (n = 450), outcome prevalence (59.8%), a significance level of 0.05, and 80% statistical power.30 This analysis indicated that the minimum detectable odds ratio was approximately 1.31 and 0.76, representing the smallest effect size that could be reliably detected given the study design.

Ethics

Our study has been approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority. Dnr: 2023–04422–0. Informed, signed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

Study cohort

A total of 600 unique patients requiring emergency care were included in the study. Proportion of patients hospitalized in the cohort was 42.2% (95% CI: 38.3–46.1) resulting in 253 hospitalized patients and 347 patients returning to home after emergency department assessment.

There was an even sex distribution, median age was 66 y (IQR: 43–78), with 52.2% (95% CI: 48.2–56.1) being aged 65 y or above (Table 2). In the full cohort, 75.0% (95% CI: 71.4–78.3) had at least one known pre-existing condition. The most common was hypertension present in 43.2% (95% CI: 39.2–47.2) followed by other cardiovascular disease in 29.5% (95% CI: 26.0–33.2) (Table 3). The prevalence of pre-existing conditions increased with age reaching 94.2% (95% CI: 91.3–96.4) in participants aged 65 y or older. Participants were distributed on 183 postal codes, 41 postal towns, and 16 municipalities (Table 4).

According to Swedish national recommendations, 75.0% (95% CI: 71.4–78.3) of participants were eligible for influenza vaccination and 77.5% (95% CI: 74.0–80.7) for COVID-19. The eligibility criteria for the two vaccines were nearly identical; the only substantive difference was that eligibility for COVID-19 vaccination also included hypertension (Table 1). This resulted in a substantial overlap: of the 600 participants, 450 were eligible for both influenza and COVID-19 vaccination, 15 for COVID-19 only, and none for influenza only.

Vaccination coverage was similar for influenza and COVID-19. The share of study participants having received at least one dose in the past 12 months was 42.2% (95% CI: 38.3–46.1) and 42.7% (95% CI: 38.8–46.7), respectively.

Vaccine uptake outside of national recommendations was low (Table 6), making fulfillment of eligibility criteria a strong determinant of vaccination. Among participants eligible for influenza vaccination, 52.0% had been vaccinated in the past 12 months, compared with 12.7% among those not meeting eligibility criteria (OR: 7.47, 95% CI: 4.46–12.51, p < .001). Corresponding numbers for COVID-19 vaccination were 54.2% and 3.0% (OR: 38.75, 95% CI: 14.09–106.53, p < .001).

Among study participants eligible for vaccination, those aged less than 65 y had the lowest vaccine uptake in the past 12 months; 20.4% for influenza and 19.0 for COVID-19. Corresponding numbers among individuals aged 65 y or above were 68.6 and 73.5% in those with no risk conditions, and 64.5 and 70.1% in those with at least one risk condition, respectively (Table 1).

Eligible study participants who in the past 12 months received one vaccine often also received the other: Receiving influenza vaccination in the past 12 months was strongly associated with COVID-19 vaccination in the same period (OR 55.33, 95% CI: 30.86–99.22, p < .001). Among 450 eligible participants, 214 received both vaccines, while only 55 received just one. Co-administration was common, and in 83.6% (95% CI: 78.3–88.1), both vaccines were administered on the exact same date. Univariate and multivariate analyses showed minimal differences between results for influenza (Tables S1–S2) and COVID-19 vaccination (Tables S3–S4).

Given the extensive overlap, subsequent analyses use the aggregated primary outcome only: the total number of seasonal vaccinations against influenza and COVID-19 received in the past 12 months. All analyses are restricted to the 450 study participants eligible for both influenza and COVID-19 vaccinations.

Male biological sex, younger age, and smoking were all strong negative determinants of vaccine uptake. In contrast to male biological sex, female sex was associated with a higher likelihood of being vaccinated, with an aOR of 1.75 (95% CI: 1.13–2.70). Age showed a strong association with vaccine uptake; each 10‑year increase corresponded to a markedly higher likelihood of vaccination, with an aOR of 2.00 (95% CI: 1.65–2.43). Finally, current smoking was associated with reduced vaccine uptake with an OR of 0.43 (95% CI: 0.20–0.92) and aOR of 0.43 (95% CI: 0.18–1.02).

Pre-existing conditions had comparatively limited impact on vaccine uptake, whether measured using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (aOR: 1.02, 95% CI: 0.90–1.15) or by number of vaccine eligibility criteria fulfilled (aOR: 1.14, 95% CI: 0.93–1.39). Notably, individuals without any pre-existing condition eligible for vaccination solely by virtue of being aged 65 y or older showed markedly reduced vaccine uptake (aOR: 0.41, 95% CI: 0.19–0.86). No individual disease category showed significant association with vaccine uptake in the multivariate model, although there was a tendency toward higher uptake among participants with hypertension, other cardiovascular disease, chronic renal failure, or immunocompromising disease.

Lower levels of functional independence initially showed a strong but non-significant trend toward higher vaccine uptake (OR: 1.42, 95% CI: 0.94–2.16). However, in the multivariate model, this relationship shifted direction: reduced independence was instead associated with lower likelihood of vaccination (aOR: 0.59, 95% CI: 0.31–1.10). This indicates that confounding factors accounted for the apparent positive association in the unadjusted analysis and that reduced independence is a negative determinant.

Prior infection with influenza and COVID-19 demonstrated divergent associations with subsequent vaccination behavior. Previous COVID-19 infection was associated with higher vaccine uptake (aOR 1.80, 95% CI: 1.12–2.89) whereas prior influenza infection was associated with lower uptake (aOR: 0.31, 95% CI: 0.15–0.67).

Median income at postal-code, postal-town and municipal level showed no association with vaccine uptake (Table 8). No significant associations were observed between social and civic context variables and the outcome. Neither unemployment rate, higher educational attainment, the proportion of foreign-born individuals, nor national election participation showed any significant associations at either postal-town or municipal levels (Table 8).

Population density and the degree of urbanization showed no measurable association with vaccine uptake in this study. All determinants related to these areas had odds ratios at almost exactly 1.0, both at postal-town level and municipal level, indicating no effect.

Discussion

In this study, several individual-level determinants showed significant associations with uptake of seasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccines among vaccine-eligible high-risk individuals. In contrast, community-level determinants had minimal influence in this high-income rural setting. Despite national recommendations, free vaccination programs, targeted campaigns and substantial public-health efforts, nearly half of all high-risk individuals in our study remained unvaccinated against influenza and COVID-19, markedly below the national coverage among individuals aged 65 y or older at 69.0% for influenza and 70.4% for COVID-19.14,15 This indicates that important barriers persist at the individual level, and that structural or community characteristics alone are insufficient to explain suboptimal uptake.

Barriers to vaccination, as outlined in the World Health Organization (WHO) Europe’s Tailoring Immunization Programmes, arise at multiple levels.1 In the following section, we use this framework to interpret individual- and community-level determinants relevant to our setting and to outline possible strategies for improving vaccine uptake.

Individual-level determinants

At the individual level, key barriers described by the WHO include gaps in knowledge, motivation and risk-awareness.1 We noted a negative association between vaccination uptake and male biological sex, younger age and smoking in vaccine-eligible individuals. These findings align with previous studies reporting similar patterns, likely reflecting lower motivation and higher vaccine hesitancy in these groups.31,33

One group showed markedly lower vaccine uptake than others: individuals aged ≥65 y without any pre-existing conditions. This pattern likely reflects a perceived sense of resilience and low risk-awareness.16 Such individuals may not view themselves as old or at-risk, which reduces motivation and contributes to low uptake of seasonal vaccination. Although they are not the most vulnerable group, the risk of severe outcomes begins to rise notably from around 60–70 y of age,1–6,34 and low uptake in this population is a missed preventive opportunity.

Although several studies have reported higher vaccine uptake among individuals with pre-existing conditions compared with others,34 we found no significant associations between specific diseases and vaccine uptake. More notably, only weak positive association were observed for the number of eligibility criteria met and for the Charlson Comorbidity Index, both indicators of multimorbidity. According to the principle of risk-stacking, individuals with multiple pre-existing conditions should experience an additive increase in risk and therefore be even more strongly encouraged to vaccinate compared with individuals meeting eligibility on the basis of a single condition.35,36 However, national recommendations in Sweden do not explicitly emphasize risk-stacking; instead, the strategy focuses on promoting higher coverage across all designated risk groups.7–15 Our findings illustrate a potential drawback of this approach: individuals at particularly high risk may not recognize their elevated vulnerability, leading some to forgo seasonal vaccination.

Equally worrisome was the lower vaccine uptake among individuals with reduced independence. This group is especially vulnerable due to severely impaired health, and faces elevated risks of disability, hospitalization, and death following infectious diseases.37 Consequently, low vaccine coverage in this group is troubling. Reduced mobility outside of the home and dependence on others likely create substantial barriers to receiving vaccination.38

Addressing individual-level barriers will require improved risk communication. Risk-awareness needs to be enhanced among relatively healthy older adults who may not perceive themselves as vulnerable.16 At the same time, individuals at particularly high risk due to multimorbidity need clearer messaging that conveys the cumulative impact of multiple risk factors including the concept of risk-stacking35,36 to highlight their substantial potential benefit from vaccination. Beyond enhanced communication, logistical support for individuals with reduced independence, such as through at-home vaccination services, may help overcome barriers related to diminished functional capacity and thereby improve vaccine uptake in this population.38

Community-level determinants

At the contextual level, WHO stresses that barriers to vaccination often arise from structural constraints, including restricted access to vaccination services, limited opportunities shaped by health policies, and practical obstacles related to service provision, cost, or logistics.1 Low socioeconomic status, inequality and rural residency have repeatedly been reported as negative determinants of vaccine uptake across different income settings, and for multiple vaccines.31,39–41 Rural residency has similarly been linked to lower vaccine uptake, often attributed to increased barriers such as longer travel to vaccination sites.42

Given the heterogeneity of our study setting – ranging from urban centers to sparsely populated rural areas and spanning different income levels – we expected vaccination uptake to vary by area of residence, as reported previously;31,39–42 In contrast, community-level measures showed limited associations with vaccine uptake in our cohort. Given that in recent years, substantial efforts have been made in Sweden to reduce community-level barriers including compensatory measures targeting specific population groups and geographical areas43 and the financial incentives for regions that meet predefined vaccination coverage targets,44 one possible explanation is that in a high-income setting with free vaccination, national recommendations, and coordinated seasonal delivery, broad area-level differences may be less pronounced. However, this should be interpreted cautiously, as our design does not allow causal inference regarding the mechanisms behind these findings. Another possibility is that remaining inequalities operate more strongly within areas than between them, making them less detectable using area-level averages. This interpretation is consistent with Swedish population-based data showing substantial variation in vaccine uptake both between and within sociodemographic groups.

Taken together, these findings suggest that in this setting, remaining barriers to vaccination may operate more strongly at the individual level within clinically vulnerable groups than at the community level.

Co-administration of multiple seasonal vaccines

We observed a strong association between influenza and COVID‑19 vaccine uptake, with no meaningful differences in individual‑ or community‑level determinants across the two vaccines, suggesting that the main challenge may not be vaccine-specific acceptance but rather engagement in seasonal vaccination overall. In this context, co-administration and coordinated delivery of multiple seasonal vaccines may be particularly useful. This strategy is further supported by our findings, with co-administration occurring in 83.6% of study participants who received both vaccines in the last 12 months. These conclusions are consistent with previous studies also showcasing feasibility and effectiveness of same-day administration of influenza and COVID-19 vaccines.45 Furthermore, the “single-visit” strategy also mirrors the structure of childhood immunization programmes, where it is a well-established driver of high coverage.46 Taken together, these findings highlight the potential value of a broader multipathogen approach to seasonal vaccination in high-risk groups, in which harmonized eligibility criteria, and “single-visit” co-administration may improve uptake.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several important strengths. By including individuals seeking care at an emergency department, we captured a population with a higher burden of morbidity and among those at greatest risk of severe outcomes from viral respiratory infections. The use of high-resolution clinical data enabled precise characterization of individual-level determinants. Furthermore, the combined assessment of individual-level and community-level determinants provides a unique analytical approach, offering a more comprehensive understanding of influences on vaccine uptake. Conducting the study in a high-income setting with free vaccination minimized the impact of financial barriers, helping to isolate other determinants.

The study focused on a specific sub-section of individuals eligible for seasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccinations; those presenting to emergency departments. This group was selected because it represents a clinically distinct segment of the high-risk population, with higher levels of multimorbidity and healthcare utilization. It also tends to have worse outcomes, including higher mortality. For example, frequent emergency department users have been reported to have about 2.2-fold higher mortality than non-users,47 and multimorbidity in this setting has been linked to higher 30-d mortality (8.2% vs 1.2%).48 As vaccination uptake in this group was lower than at both national and regional levels, it further highlights its vulnerability and importance. While this indicates that the study captures a particularly vulnerable subgroup with lower vaccine uptake and greater healthcare needs, it may limit generalizability to the broader high-risk population. While this indicates that the study captures a particularly vulnerable subgroup with lower vaccine uptake and greater healthcare needs, it may limit the representativeness to the general high-risk population.

There are also additional limitations to consider. Because recommendations for influenza and COVID-19 vaccinations substantially overlapped, uptake patterns for the two vaccines could not be analyzed separately In addition, while respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is an important cause of severe illness in older adults,6 its recent introduction and low uptake in our cohort (0.3%) prevented meaningful analysis. Accordingly, determinant analyses were restricted to influenza and COVID-19 vaccination, and findings should not be extrapolated to RSV vaccine uptake.

Although the use of high-quality national register data with the possibility to link information across multiple levels strengthens the analyses, certain relevant measures were unavailable, potentially limiting their comprehensiveness. Therefore, we were unable to measure motivational factors (e.g., vaccine hesitancy, health literacy) or explicit access barriers to vaccination. While educational level and income may partly relate to these constructs,32,49 they cannot fully capture their complexity. Likewise, pre-existing conditions, level of dependence, and population density may indirectly relate to access,16 but without direct measures of perceived or practical access barriers we cannot determine whether reduced uptake primarily reflects access “friction” or other factors. Taken together, these limitations restrict our ability to assess motivational and access-related dimensions of vaccine uptake and, consequently, limit mechanistic interpretation of the observed associations. In addition, the study design does not allow us to determine why community-level associations were limited, and these findings should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that contextual inequalities have been eliminated.

Finally, the study sample was relatively small, thereby impacting the interpretation of negative findings. As the post-hoc power analysis indicated an MDE of ≥1.31 for odds ratios, smaller effects may have gone undetected and should therefore be considered when interpreting non-significant results.

Conclusion

Uptake of seasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccination was primarily associated with individual-level determinants: male biological sex, younger age, smoking and reduced independence were all associated with lower uptake. In contrast, community-level determinants showed only limited association in our cohort.

This pattern suggests that, in a high-income setting with free vaccination, remaining barriers may operate more strongly at the individual level within clinically vulnerable groups than at the community level. To reduce remaining barriers, targeted communication including risk-stacking may improve risk awareness among relatively healthy older adults and individuals with multimorbidity, while practical support such as at-home vaccination and co-administration of seasonal vaccines may help improve uptake further in high-risk individuals.
