Project Objectives
The University of Texas at Dallas (UTD) has a substantial history of US federal investment in wind energy.
The purpose was to establish the physical infrastructure for a center focused on wind energy research,
education, technology development and community outreach. The Center is located on the Richardson
campus of UTD within the same building as the Boundary Layer and Subsonic Wind Tunnel (BLAST) to
enable stronger team science in wind energy systems by locating the faculty, students and staff with the
wind tunnel. The project provided for the acquisition of vital equipment for scale and field testing to
further research objectives, education and outreach.

The Center for Wind Energy brings together university, civic and industry partners to create and test
new technologies to realize the potential of wind to support the energy needs of the nation. The Center
conducts fundamental and applied research primarily in high‐fidelity simulation of wind power systems
and components, measurement and analysis of wind fields for diagnostics and model validation, wind
tunnel testing, control system design for wind turbines and wind farms, large rotor design, grid
integration and energy storage. The Center for Wind Energy provides solutions to the wind power
industry and work on novel turbine and system‐level designs. This project provided essential resources
to renovate and equip space adjacent to the campus’ state‐of‐the‐art wind tunnel to create laboratory
and critical office space for modeling, measurements and control of wind energy systems.

The creation of the Intelligent Energy Systems Lab provides an area for students and faculty to design,
build and test intelligent systems for wind turbines and wind farms, which enhances the learning
experiences and capabilities. The proximity of this new group of students with the fluid mechanics
faculty and students located on the second floor of the same building enhances collaboration at the
intersection of fluid mechanics, aeromechanics and intelligent systems. As an added benefit, the
adjacency to the wind tunnel located in the same building allows easier transportation of experiments
constructed in the lab for testing in the tunnel.

The lab supports the development of mechatronics and electronics subsystems for research and
education in wind turbines and wind farms. This capability is synergistic with other facilities in the
building, which currently support fluid mechanics aspects of wind energy research and education such
as the BLAST wind tunnel. Wind turbine technology is progressively incorporating more and more
electronics and intelligence in a way that resembles the evolution of the aerospace industry in the late
20th century and the automotive industry in the early 21st century. A dedicated lab to these modern
technologies is necessary to grow our research and educational programs in wind energy.

The renovated facility houses a modern conference room with appropriate A/V capability. The
conference room is used to support the recruiting efforts of new members to WindSTAR, a National
Science Foundation (NSF) supported Industry/University Collaborative Research Center at UTD, with
both in‐person and virtual meetings using modern technology (e.g., monitor, camera, mic, speakers).
The conference room also supports participation in formal meetings with the European Academy of
Wind Energy, North American Wind Energy Academy and similar events. The Center for Wind Energy at
UT Dallas, in partnership with Sandia National Laboratories, is the host of the NAWEA/WindTech 2025
Conference in Dallas and the facilities and equipment procured by this project will be featured to
conference participants.

The renovated facility provides the potential for research and educational expansion into other energy
sources, storm predictions, and/or support research in aeronautical engineering. This project delivered

critical equipment and physical infrastructure to enable the Center for Wind Energy to achieve
excellence by creating a place where academia works in partnership with students, industry and civic
leaders to advance existing and future applications of wind engineering.

Scope of Work
The designated space for the Center for Wind Energy is located in a building on campus called the
“Waterview Science and Technology Center” (WSTC). This building houses our Boundary Layer and
Subsonic wind Tunnel. For the renovations, we have converted existing but unused lab and office space
in WSTC to have the following:

 • Intelligent Energy Systems Lab, which hosts an “electronics shop” plus student space with
 computer workstations. In this lab staff and students can perform prototyping and testing for
 diagnostics and control of wind turbine components. The lab also hosts workstations that run
 software for numerical simulation and design of wind energy systems such as codes developed
 by NREL (e.g., WISDEM, OpenFAST, FLORIS, FAST.Farm, WindSE and others) as well as
 Matlab/Simulink, machine learning codes and productivity software. Cubicles for students and
 postdocs were designed and placed in this lab space.
 • Space renovation required for new wind/wave tunnel. UTD acquired (with its own funds) a
 brand-new wind/water-wave tunnel (WWT) to expand the center's research, educational and
 outreach infrastructure. However, there was no space that can fit the WWT facility (which has a
 test section of approximately 9 m long, 1 m wide, and 2 m high). We therefore used the project
 funds to renovate two rooms to accommodate the new WWT.
 • Conference room for meetings with existing industry partners, recruiting new partners and the
 relevant staff. Offices for the Center leadership, faculty, staff members and visiting
 scientists/engineers.
 • Reception lobby to Center that serves as the entrance and reception area for visitors, clients,
 and employees. It plays a crucial role in creating a positive first impression and setting the tone
 for the entire office environment.

For equipment, the project called for the acquisition and integration of:

 • Circuit, transducer and actuator testing equipment (e.g., power supplies, oscilloscopes, multi‐
 channel data acquisition systems and standard tools, etc.) and benches to support workstations
 in the “electronics shop”.
 • Wind tunnel metrology/sensors and data acquisition systems for testing the effects of air
 moving past fixed and rotating solid objects.
 • Dedicated data storage to handle terabytes of data generated from computer simulations and
 experiments from the wind tunnel and other relevant labs conducting research in energy.

This project aimed to renovate an existing facility to create a modern, functional and collaborative
environment that supports the Center’s needs and enhances the productivity and efficiency of the staff
and students. The project involved comprehensive upgrades to the infrastructure, equipment and
aesthetics of the spaces while adhering to industry standards and safety regulations.

The renovation project encompassed a set of technical tasks to transform the existing areas into modern
and functional environments. The scope of work included the reconfiguration of approximately 6,300

sqft. of laboratory space, student workstations and storage office space, a reception lobby, a break
room, and a conference room. The project also entailed the integration of technology infrastructure,
such as high‐speed internet connectivity and audio‐visual systems for teleconferencing.

For the lab space, the scope involved expanding and reconfiguring the areas to accommodate new
equipment and facilitate efficient workflow. This included creating workstations for the development
and testing of prototypes equipment along with cubicle space for student work. Upgrades to the
mechanical, electrical and plumbing systems were carried out to ensure compliance with industry
standards and safety regulations.

The installation of state‐of‐the‐art equipment, instrumentation and machinery supports advanced wind
energy research and development activities. Safety features were enhanced, including ventilation
systems, emergency response mechanisms and appropriate storage solutions for hazardous materials.
Dedicated spaces for collaboration, experimentation and prototyping were created to foster innovation
and knowledge exchange among students and faculty. Energy‐efficient design principles were
incorporated throughout the project to improve sustainability.

Tasks, Milestones, and Deliverables
Task 1: Demolition and Site Preparation

The demolition phase is a critical component of the renovation project, involving systematically
dismantling and removing existing structures, fixtures and materials. During this phase, demolition
teams will carefully execute the removal process, following safety protocols and adhering to
environmental regulations. The demolition phase will follow the construction plans, including plumbing,
flooring, ceiling, HVAC, electrical and selective walls at blocking areas. The task serves as a foundation
for the subsequent construction and renovation activities.

 Milestone 1.1: Final Demolition

 Demolition and site preparation, where the cleared site is prepared for the subsequent
 construction phase.

 Deliverable 1.1: Final Demolition

 The start of the project was delayed. Time was spent determining the most suitable
 process for Facilities Management to access funds. Second, the University closed during
 winter break and personnel were given set holidays as paid leave. Construction and
 renovation were conducted by a General Contractor. University policy required three
 official bid submissions. The three bids were received in Q1, and the General contractor
 selected started the work in Q2. Demolition was completed during Q2, and the project
 followed a revised schedule submitted at the end of Q1.

Task 2: MEP (Mechanical, Electrical, Plumbing) and HVAC (Heating, Ventilation and Air Conditioning)

installed in the space, and the air duct was routed through the roof to connect to the new condensing
unit on the rooftop creating a new replacement split system. New data outlets were installed in the
partition walls, ensuring convenient access to data connections throughout the renovated space.

Issues, Risks, and Mitigation
At the end of the first quarter, funding was released to UTD in November 2023. Through the University
financial process, a cost center was generated to deposit the funds for usage throughout the project.
This cost center was established in December. With the funds in place, our Facilities Management team
solicited three official bids from General Contractors. The University went on winter break which caused
delay. By the end of January 2024, a General Contractor was selected, and work was scheduled to begin
at the start of Q2. The General Contractor generated a schedule to mitigate time loss to reach the
planned tasks and milestones by the end of Q4.

The project proceeded according to the revised schedule and experienced a few delays in Q4. As a
result, a 6-month co-cost extension was requested and granted for the following reasons:

 • Fiscal year rollover (FY 24 to FY 25) caused delay in purchasing
 o UTD FY24 ended August 31st with final close of purchasing on August 23rd
 o UTD eProcurement was offline August 28th – September 5th
 • Construction delay in support for wind/wave tunnel
 o Contractors (included in current contractual support) delayed finishing renovation until
 after wind/wave tunnel was installed. The installation took several weeks past the initial
 projected deadline due to several factors such as delays from the manufacturer to ship
 the pieces of the equipment, equipment failure during calibration and testing, and
 plumbing redesign based on the failure during testing.

Changes to Scope, Schedule, Budget, or Team
The scope of work and team remained unchanged throughout the project.

The schedule was adjusted to complete tasks 1-4 during Q2 and Q3 and the General Contractor agreed
to work within our scheduled parameters. Tasks 1-3 were completed by Q3, and Task 4 was completed
shortly after the start of Q4 after the installation of access controls. The schedule was then re-adjusted
as a result of the no-cost extension granted by DOE. Task 5 was completed by the end of Q6 and receipt
of all remaining equipment occurred in the weeks following the close of Q6.

Quotes for construction and equipment purchases were updated from the initial submission to reflect
the market prices at that time, which led to some increases and some decreases from the submitted
budget line items. These adjustments did not affect the total project budget, and final costs were
reported after invoices were processed through the vendors and the University. A balance was left for
the contingency of unplanned expenses, and a plan was discussed in the quarterly meetings on August
21, 2024, and November 13, 2024, to reallocate $160,000 (10%) of the project budget from construction
to equipment. This reallocation was approved, and a list of additional equipment purchases was
communicated and approved in April 2025.

Lessons Learned
The University supported preliminary architectural and engineering design work that took place before
the project was funded. This helped to speed up and inform some of the early processes. For example,
by having rough designs in place we were able to get estimates from a General Contractor that were
included in the initial proposal. Once the award was granted, we were able to finalize those designs
fairly quickly then get formal bids to start construction after the end of the first quarter. Additionally,
having these designs made writing the Statement of Project Objectives easier with very clear scope and
tasks.

The changing dynamics of pricing affected purchasing equipment. Though these policy changes were not
anticipated at the start of the project, their effects were felt nonetheless. In response, our team
prioritized critical equipment purchases and generated purchase orders as quickly as possible while also
seeking domestics sources. Vendors and suppliers were eager to meet with our team to work out
solutions, however disruptions in the supply chain resulted in several delays in receiving equipment.

Identifying equipment custodians, amongst faculty affiliated with the center, proved useful to procure
the equipment. It is expected that these custodians will also maintain the equipment as needed.
