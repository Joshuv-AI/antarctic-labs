Reply to “Real-world quality of life and healthcare resource utilization in patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis – Circular definition of CVRM comorbidities undermines the validity of core subgroup comparisons”
Authors: Jens Marquardt, Riku Ota, Giada Sebastiani, Diego Moreno Baca, Hayley Wallinger, Emily Quinones, Lena Amari, Elisabetta Bugianesi
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We thank Zhuang and Shen for their letter,1 in response to our recently published article in JHEP Reports reporting on the real-world quality of life and healthcare resource utilization in patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH).2 We thank the authors for their interest in our study and their considered thoughts, and welcome the opportunity to clarify aspects of our methodological approach, in particular our definition of ’additional comorbidities’ and how these were interpreted in our study.

There are limited data investigating patients with MASH and additional cardiovascular, renal and metabolic (CVRM) comorbidities, despite previous research suggesting these patients face worse clinical outcomes.3,4 Accordingly, our original study aimed to investigate the impact of CVRM comorbidities and fibrosis stage on outcomes in patients with MASH presenting in a real-world setting.

As noted, for the purpose of analysis patients were stratified based on fibrosis stage and the presence of additional CVRM comorbidities: early fibrosis-MASH without additional CVRM comorbidities (EFnoC); early fibrosis-MASH with additional CVRM comorbidities (EFwC); and advanced fibrosis-MASH with additional CVRM comorbidities (AFwC). All patients were required to have at least one of the five cardiometabolic risk factor criteria required to diagnose MASH, as stated in disease management guidelines.5 In addition, patients were deemed to have ‘additional CVRM comorbidities’ (classified as EFwC or AFwC) if they had been diagnosed with any of the following comorbidities: cerebrovascular disease, congestive heart failure, dyslipidemia, hypertension, metabolic syndrome, myocardial infarction, obesity (physician-reported presence or a BMI ≥30 kg/m2), peripheral artery disease, renal disease, stable angina, stroke, transient ischemic attack, type 2 diabetes mellitus, unstable angina, or one or more major cardiovascular events.

Our study used a cross-sectional approach, and thus patients were at different points in their disease journey in terms of diagnosis and treatment history. In our sample, just over half of patients had been diagnosed with MASH for ≥1 year at the time of data collection. Accordingly, when stratifying patients for analysis, the presence or absence of comorbidities present at the point of data collection (and not diagnosis) was used as our reference point, in order that the direct impact of these comorbidities on patient quality of life and healthcare resource utilization at the time of survey could be investigated. Therefore, any temporal impact that the presence or severity of comorbidities over time may have had on our findings was eliminated.

The presence of CVRM comorbidities is an important consideration in the management of MASH. We note that one of the concerns raised by Zhuang and Shen was our classification of ‘additional CVRM comorbidities’. Patients in the EFnoC group were only required to have one of the five qualifying cardiometabolic risk factors for MASH, whereas patients in the EFwC and AFwC groups could have possessed two or more qualifying cardiometabolic risk factors, alongside other CVRM comorbidities. Irrespective of the specific classification of CVRM comorbidities, the principal finding of our study remains unchanged: a greater burden of CVRM comorbidities was associated with worse symptomatic burden, poorer quality of life, and increased healthcare resource utilization. These findings reinforce the importance of comprehensive management strategies that address both liver disease and its associated cardiometabolic complications. Until the approvals of resmetirom and semaglutide for the management of MASH, treatment options in this setting were limited, and predominantly focused on treating the symptoms or related comorbidities of MASH rather than the underlying causes themselves. Accordingly, effective management of comorbid conditions and risk factors are still important considerations in improving outcomes in patients with MASH.

We also note the comments surrounding the limitations of sample size for the EFnoC group (n=170 patients) in comparison to the EFwC (1,575 patients) and AFwC (n = 478 patients) groups. While we note this potential imbalance between groups as a limitation, this reflects the realities for patients with MASH in that diagnosis can often be delayed, with patients typically presenting with more advanced disease. As stated, our study sampled consecutively consulting patients, and thus patients who visited their physician more frequently and may be more severely affected had a higher likelihood of being included. In addition, the inclusion of specialist physicians may have led to the inclusion of patients with more severe disease. Nonetheless, this was reflective of a real-world population of patients being managed for MASH.

We hope this response alleviates any concerns about the study, which as the authors state comprises one of the largest real-world multi-national datasets on patient-reported outcomes and resource utilization in MASH. These results highlight the need for improved diagnosis and awareness of MASH and associated comorbidities, in order that patients are diagnosed and treated earlier in their disease course. In addition, this demonstrates the need for holistic management strategies that address both MASH fibrosis and CVRM comorbidities.
