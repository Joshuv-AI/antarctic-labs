Occupational resilience: Scoping review of conceptualisations and applications in contexts of adversity
Authors: Gema Puig‐Esteve, Rodolfo Morrison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOccupational resilience has emerged as a construct that links the capacity to face adversity with the role of meaningful occupations in the adaptation and recovery of individuals, groups, and communities. However, its definition, theoretical frameworks, and applications remain heterogeneous and partial. This lack of systematisation hinders its integration into occupational therapy and occupational science.ObjectiveThe objective of this study is to map and synthesise the scientific literature on occupational resilience, identifying how it is conceptualised and operationalised.MethodsUsing established scoping review frameworks, publications from 2000 to 2025 were searched in databases and through manual searching, including empirical studies and conceptual literature that addressed occupational resilience and its relationship with occupational participation in contexts of adversity.Consumer and Community InvolvementThere was no direct participation of consumers or community representatives in the design, conduct, or interpretation of the findings of this study.ResultsThirty‐seven studies were included that show diverse uses of the term occupational resilience and related concepts, tending to conceptualise it as a trait, an individual capacity, a dynamic process, and an adaptive outcome linked to occupational performance. The findings highlight the role of meaningful occupations in modulating suffering and emotional activation and in rebuilding purpose, agency, roles, routines, and identities after adversity. They also reveal marked differences in definitions, measures, and designs, with a predominance of approaches focussed on individual resilience, mainly in work‐related contexts, and limited attention to structural dimensions and occupational justice.ConclusionThis review offers a structured view of occupational resilience and of the place that meaningful occupations occupy in resilience processes, proposing a process‐oriented definition that articulates personal, contextual, and occupational dimensions. The results underscore the need to define integrated theoretical frameworks and occupational therapy interventions explicitly oriented towards the occupational effects of trauma, which validate pain, support the restoration and reinvention of occupational participation, and question the social conditions that make sustained resilience necessary.

INTRODUCTION

In recent years, resilience has become a key concept for understanding the human capacity to adapt and maintain wellbeing and health in the face of adverse events, life changes, and crises of different magnitudes (Bonanno, 2004; Denckla et al., 2017). Contributions from neuroscience and research on the neural circuitry of resilience to trauma (Roeckner et al., 2021), advances in understanding the effects of trauma from a systemic perspective (Contreras, 2025), and studies on shared mechanisms between physical and social trauma (Neuner, 2023), among other developments, show that resilience emerges from the interaction between individual vulnerabilities, relational resources, and contextual conditions and that it can be observed both in trajectories of diverse emotional symptoms and in the preservation or recovery of social and occupational functioning (Denckla et al., 2017; Folke, 2016). Thus, it has been described that, after potentially traumatic experiences, some people not only regain their previous level of functioning but also experience positive changes in their relationships, priorities, and ways of inhabiting everyday life. As suggested in previous work on resilience in educational and community contexts, this post‐traumatic growth (PTG) can be understood as part of a new developmental process through which the person reorganises life, relationships, and life projects, incorporating new forms of meaningful participation that give meaning to the traumatic experience (Puig Esteve & Rubio Rabal, 2011). Even so, a large part of the scientific literature continues to use definitions centred on relatively stable individual characteristics, rather than on dynamic, relational, and contextualised processes (Luthar & Cicchetti, 2000), which generates tensions and ambiguities in the understanding of resilience.

In its psychological dimension, resilience is described as a relatively stable adaptation to adversity, observable in functioning trajectories that avoid psychopathology and, in some cases, involve positive transformation after difficult experiences (Bonanno, 2004; Tranter et al., 2021). Important debates persist regarding its definition, mechanisms, and the best ways to measure it, especially when the construct is translated into specific domains of occupational performance or participation (e.g., particular professions, caregiving roles, or specific everyday activities) (Masten, 2021).

An example of this controversy can be found in organisational psychology and occupational health, where interest in resilience in the workplace has become established, understood as the capacity of employees to adapt positively to intense demands, organisational change, and chronic stress, mitigating the impact of burnout and turnover (Vanhove et al., 2016; Youssef & Luthans, 2007). In contrast, the integrated framework proposed by Kossek and Perrigino (2016) conceptualises occupational resilience as a synthesis of individual traits, coping capacities, and adaptation processes in response to risks and triggers defined in the work and organisational domain.

Nevertheless, despite these contributions, in much of this specialised literature, the adjective ‘occupational’ is treated as synonymous with ‘work‐related’, as it stems from other disciplinary fields, with limited attention paid to the specificity of human occupations and their active role in adaptation processes as conceived in occupational therapy and occupational science (Vanhove et al., 2016).

Given that exposure to adversity (including violence, disasters, serious illness, forced displacement, or structural precarity) is a common human experience that directly affects occupational performance and participation (Berkes & Ross, 2013; Tranter et al., 2021), there has understandably been growing interest in how individuals, groups, and communities use meaningful occupations as mechanisms for coping, identity reconstruction, and resilience following traumatic experiences (Alonso‐Ferreira et al., 2022). These studies suggest that occupation is not only an outcome of ‘being resilient’ but also the means through which resources are mobilised, meaning is reconstructed, and new ways of living are negotiated after adversity (Gómez‐Antilef et al., 2020).

This perspective aligns with the conception of occupation as the set of meaningful daily activities of self‐care, productivity, and leisure that structure life, support identity, and mediate people's relationships with their contexts (American Occupational Therapy Association, 2020; Wilcock & Hocking, 2024). This view has given rise to critical theoretical proposals that emphasise the situated, social, and political nature of occupation and its potential to reproduce or challenge inequities and forms of occupational injustice, while also defending occupation as a right (Alonso‐Ferreira et al., 2022; Cirineu & Morrison, 2025; Farias & Laliberte Rudman, 2016). However, the link between resilience and occupation, that is, how people use meaningful occupations to sustain or rebuild life in the face of adversity, has only been explored in a fragmented manner and, to date, without systematisation focussed on occupational resilience as a construct (van Veenendaal, 2024; Yusuf et al., 2022).

Although some studies highlight engagement in meaningful, everyday activities as an opportunity to provide meaning, identity, and structure to daily life after a traumatic experience (Palma‐Candia et al., 2019), this fragmented view of resilience, together with the diversity of definitions and theoretical frameworks used to analyse it, makes it difficult to study and consolidate knowledge about how occupational participation contributes to adaptation and recovery after trauma.

In recent literature, several reviews have been published that explore resilience in the field of occupational science and therapy, addressing its relationship with clinical practice, education, or particular groups, including an integrative review on synergies between resilience and participation following occupational disruption (Van Niekerk, 2024) and a scoping review on how occupational therapy and occupational science researchers conceptualise resilience (Turner et al., 2025). These contributions have helped to make visible the relevance of resilience for health and participation, but they tend to treat it in a broad way, without focussing specifically on the term ‘occupational resilience’ or on the implications of its use in different fields. The review presented here is distinguished precisely by focussing on how occupational resilience has been conceptualised and applied in the literature and by analysing the tensions and opportunities this generates for the future development of occupational resilience frameworks in dialogue with these previous contributions.

In this context, the present scoping review aims to map how occupational resilience has been defined and operationalised in the scientific literature, to describe types of adversity and trauma and the occupational contexts in which it has been studied, and to analyse the role attributed to meaningful occupations in the resilience processes described, understood in the broad sense adopted in occupational therapy (Yusuf et al., 2022). In doing so, it seeks to provide a solid conceptual basis for occupational therapy practice and research and to advance towards an integrative framework of occupational resilience that is coherent with the specificity of the profession.

METHODS

Positionality statement

The first author is an occupational therapist and psychologist with extensive professional and academic experience working with populations in situations of vulnerability. Her research trajectory has focussed on the study of resilience from different theoretical frameworks, especially psychology and social education, which has shaped an integrative and sensitive approach to processes of adaptation, trauma, and reconstruction of everyday life. The second author is an occupational therapist and occupational science researcher. His approach to the study of resilience is situated mainly within critical theoretical frameworks in occupational science, with an emphasis on the relationship between occupation, social context, and occupational justice, which guides a process‐oriented and contextualised reading of the construct.

Study design

This study was designed as a scoping review, following the five‐stage framework proposed by Arksey and O'Malley (2005), which is appropriate for mapping emerging and heterogeneous areas of knowledge. In addition, the methodological recommendations of the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) for scoping reviews (Peters et al., 2020) and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) Statement guidelines for transparent reporting of results (Tricco et al., 2018) were taken into account. The protocol developed for this scoping review was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF; https://osf.io/bzre7/).

Step 1: Identification of the question and Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework

The guiding question was as follows: ‘How has occupational resilience been conceptualised and applied in the scientific literature published between 2000 and 2025 in populations exposed to adversity or trauma?’ This question was operationalised using the PCC framework, recommended for scoping reviews (Peters et al., 2020). The population included individuals, groups, or communities who had experienced or were experiencing situations of adversity or traumatic experiences. The concept focussed on occupational resilience, understood as the relationship between resilience and occupational participation. The context encompassed clinical, community, educational, social, family, and work‐related settings in which this link was described.

Step 2: Identification of relevant studies

Between August and November 2025, a systematic and comprehensive search was conducted in the PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection, ProQuest, and EBSCOhost databases, as well as in specialised and complementary resources ([removed for peer review]). The combination of these sources made it possible to integrate biomedical, social science, and occupational studies literature, as well as academic grey literature (especially theses) and publications in English, Spanish, and French on resilience and occupation, yielding a total of 642 articles.

In PubMed, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free‐text terms keywords related to resilience and occupation (e.g., ‘occupational resilience’, ‘Resilience, Psychological’, ‘resilience AND occupation’, and ‘occupational adaptation’) were combined with terms referring to adversity or trauma (‘adversity’, ‘social vulnerability’, ‘trauma and stressor related disorders’, and ‘psychological trauma’), applying filters for language (English, Spanish, and French), availability of abstract and full text, and publication period 2000–2025. In Scopus and Web of Science, equivalent search equations were used in title, abstract, and keywords; limited by year (2000–2025) and language; and excluded subject areas distant from the occupational perspective (e.g., veterinary science, engineering, mathematics, and oncology), as well as certain non‐relevant document types, in order to maintain the psycho‐social and occupational relevance of the results.

In ProQuest, the search was restricted to psychology, education, social work, and health databases, excluding press, correspondence, and non‐scientific documents and specifically including doctoral theses and other grey literature relevant to occupational resilience. In EBSCOhost, academic publications in psychology, nursing, and applied social sciences were retrieved, with filters for language (English, Spanish, and French), studies with humans, and the 2000–2025 period. ALCORZE‐WorldCat was used to cross‐check and extend results to international library catalogues, and Psicodoc, an international bibliographic database specialising in psychology and related disciplines, was used as an alternative to PsycINFO to retrieve Ibero‐American literature on resilience and occupation.

Full search strategies for each database, including fields and filters applied, are provided in Data S1.

Step 3: Study selection

The eligibility criteria were defined in accordance with the PCC framework. Scientific documents (original articles; systematic, narrative, or scoping reviews; doctoral or master's theses; and technical reports) were included if they simultaneously met the following criteria: (a) referring to individuals, groups, or human communities that had experienced or were experiencing adversity or traumatic experiences (e.g., serious illness, pandemic, violence, and forced displacement), because the aim of the review was to understand processes of occupational resilience precisely in contexts where routines, roles, and opportunities for participation are significantly threatened. Given the conception of resilience adopted in the article, in the absence of adversity or trauma, the construct of resilience loses its central meaning. In such cases, the relationship between occupation and adaptation cannot be interpreted as a resilience process; (b) conceptualised, named, or analysed occupational resilience explicitly or implicitly or explored resilience in close relation to occupational participation, occupations, or occupational adaptation; and (c) were published between 2000 and 2025, in English, Spanish, or French, with full text available, and were situated in clinical, community, educational, social, family, or work‐related contexts. The search was restricted to this period because, from the early 2000s onwards, contemporary conceptualisations of resilience in health and social sciences became consolidated, with influential syntheses critically reviewing the construct and reframing it as a dynamic, systems‐based process supported by emerging measurement tools (Luthar et al., 2000; Masten, 2021). This time restriction made it possible to focus the review on studies with comparable definitions and conceptual frameworks that are relevant for current practice.

Non‐scientific documents (emails, news items, and database entries without analytical content), studies focussed exclusively on psychological resilience with no reference to the occupational dimension, works published before 2000, publications in languages other than those predefined, research not conducted with humans, and articles that used the term ‘trauma’ solely as a synonym for physical injury without relation to adaptation or resilience processes were excluded. It was not required that the studies belong specifically to the field of occupational therapy or occupational science, in order to avoid an endogamic bias and to map broader uses of occupational resilience.

All identified references were exported to a reference manager (Mendeley) and subsequently to the Rayyan platform (Ouzzani et al., 2016), where duplicates were removed automatically and manually.

In total, 700 references were imported into Rayyan (642 from databases and 58 identified through manual searching). Of these, 29 duplicates were automatically removed by the software (100% match), and 6 additional duplicates were identified and excluded manually (4 during the initial screening and 2 at the full‐text stage), leaving 671 records for title and abstract screening. Of the 671 records assessed at title and abstract screening, 627 were excluded because they did not address resilience from an occupational perspective (C2), and 4 were excluded as duplicates; the remaining reasons for exclusion were infrequent or overlapped with C2.

Next, 40 articles that had passed this phase were reviewed in full text, applying at both stages the previously agreed inclusion and exclusion criteria (Table 1). The discrepancies were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, by consulting a third researcher. Finally, a total of 37 articles were included for further analysis.

The full selection process and reasons for exclusion at each stage were documented in a diagram adapted from PRISMA‐ScR (Figure 1), which shows the number of records identified in each source, those removed as duplicates, and the total number of studies finally included (n = 37).

Step 4: Data extraction (charting)

For the 37 included studies, a data extraction template was developed in Excel, informed by several articles, and refined iteratively (Peters et al., 2020; Tricco et al., 2018). This template, transferred to Rayyan artificial intelligence (AI), captured bibliographic data (authorship, year, and country), journal, language, type of publication (research/non‐research/review), and methodological design, as well as contextual information (population and sample size, occupational context [work‐related, community, educational, clinical, or family], and type of adversity or trauma).

Additionally, content‐related information was extracted: definitions of general resilience and occupational resilience, conceptual frameworks and models used, characteristics of the occupations involved, described occupational experiences, coping mechanisms linked to occupational engagement, findings on the role of occupation in resilience processes, challenges for participation, and recommendations or implications for occupational therapy practice. This distinction between contextual and content data made it possible, in line with other scoping reviews in the occupational field, to combine a quantitative descriptive analysis of the characteristics of the scientific production with a qualitative thematic analysis of the meanings and uses of the construct. Information on the items used for data extraction following full‐text reading of the 37 selected articles is provided in Data S3.

Step 5: Data analysis: Context and content

The analysis was organised into two complementary levels. The complete set of data extracted after analysis of each article, entered manually into the table available in Rayyan, can be consulted in Data S4.

Content analysis

Second, an inductive thematic analysis was conducted of the definitions and uses of the construct of occupational resilience, as well as of descriptions of the role of occupation in adaptation and recovery processes. Thematic analysis followed the phases proposed by Braun and Clarke (2006) (familiarisation with the data, generation of initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, and defining and naming themes). Based on the extracts relating to the role and potential of occupation in resilience processes (Data S4), initial codes were generated and then grouped into subthemes and, subsequently, into themes. The final themes (e.g., occupation as adaptation, as a protective and meaningful experience, or as a source of adversity) are integrated into the conceptual map in Figure 3.

To improve the traceability of the analytic process, Data S5 illustrates the coding pathway from extracts through to codes, subthemes, and themes.

The synthesis and reporting of this scoping review were guided by the PRISMA‐ScR statement; the completed checklist is provided as Data S6.

RESULTS

Conceptualisations of occupational resilience

The studies included in the analysis, originating from different disciplines, offer a predominantly work‐based interpretation of occupational resilience, focussing on how certain professions respond to adversity, trauma, and stress related to work across a wide range of contexts. This focus on the domain of paid employment reflects the origins of the term in occupational health and organisational psychology and contrasts with a broader understanding of occupation as a set of meaningful activities within everyday life. Consequently, the findings of the review highlight a significant bias in the literature towards work contexts, as well as the need to develop studies that explore occupational resilience in relation to other meaningful occupations and in situations of adversity not exclusively linked to work.

As shown in Table 3, occupational resilience is conceptualised in a heterogeneous way, oscillating between individually focussed approaches and perspectives that conceive it as a relational process situated within occupational participation. This diversity is reflected in explicit definitions, the terms used, and the theoretical frameworks that underpin them.

Occupational resilience as a work‐related trait or individual capacity

One group of studies conceives occupational resilience as an individual characteristic or capacity to withstand and manage stress arising from paid work and organisational demands. Studies conducted with police officers (Gümüştekin, 2022), lawyers (Switzer, 2025), nurses and other health professionals (Heritage et al., 2019), and early childhood teachers (Puspa Damayanti & Kurniawan, 2023) adopt this perspective, defining occupational resilience as the belief in people's capacity to adapt to the difficult conditions of their profession, confront obstacles, and maintain commitment to their work role or as the capacity to return to an acceptable level of functioning after periods of intense stress (LaGree et al., 2023).

In these studies, occupational resilience is operationalised as a relatively stable individual difference (trait or capacity) that protects against burnout, intention to leave, or reduced job satisfaction and commitment and is measured using general resilience scales, such as the Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (CD‐RISC), or instruments specifically focussed on work‐related resilience and occupational wellbeing. Likewise, some studies understand resilience as a hardy personality or an individual resource that regulates the effects of stressful events and enables people to maintain psycho‐social balance in the face of adversity (Blázquez Manzano et al., 2023).

Taken together, these studies place emphasis on resilience as a psychological resource that enables workers to sustain performance in high‐demand work settings, so that the ‘occupational’ dimension is understood mainly in labour and organisational terms: Occupation appears more as a site of risk and source of stress than as a meaningful process through which new ways of living are constructed following adversity.

Occupational resilience as a dynamic, relational process linked to occupational participation

A second group of publications describes occupational resilience as a dynamic process of coping and adaptation to adversity, inferred from the functioning trajectory of a person after facing traumatic stressors (Yusuf et al., 2022). Some authors emphasise that it is not a static trait but a changing response to adversity that emerges from the ongoing interaction between the person, their occupations, and the physical, social, and cultural environments in which these take place (Jacobs‐Nzuzi Khuabi et al., 2022; Van Veenendaal, 2024). In this vein, other perspectives hold that occupation is itself the mechanism of resilience, that is, the process through which people access resources, reconfigure their identity, and generate new ways of living after adversity (Isenberg et al., 2025).

Similarly, in some studies, occupational resilience is used as a synonym for work‐related resilience, indicating that it is a coping strategy based on learning a conscious, calm, and balanced response to challenging work situations, integrating self‐care, social support, and mindfulness strategies (Puspa Damayanti & Kurniawan, 2023). Its development, according to these authors, enables workers to resolve the stress inherent in their jobs in an adaptive way, recovering from conflicts, difficulties, and failures and responding to, confronting, and reducing disruptions through flexible changes in daily occupations (Brown, 2021) in a continuous cycle of adjustment of routines and roles in changing contexts that emphasises the idea of process.

Occupational resilience as an outcome or property of systems

Across the set of 37 studies selected for full‐text review, it was identified that resilience is manifested in how people modify, negotiate, or reconstruct their everyday occupational participation when faced with drastic changes, trauma, or contexts of occupational injustice. Brown (2021) highlights that occupational resilience involves a person's capacity to navigate and negotiate stressors successfully and creatively, whereas Freeman et al. (2024) and Wegner et al. (2022) show how people remake their occupations in the face of adversity. In line with this literature, several of the included studies describe positive changes in meaning, identities, and relationships that resemble what has been termed PTG, even when this label is not always used explicitly.

In this group of studies, occupational resilience is described less as an individual trait and more as an emergent property of person–occupation–environment systems of relationship. Participation in meaningful occupations acts as a vehicle for reorganising routines, roles, and identity narratives, so that ‘doing differently’ also enables ‘being differently’ following adversity. Some studies illustrate how everyday, community, or culturally relevant occupations become the main means through which continuity of life is sustained, meaning is reconstructed, and PTG is fostered, particularly in contexts of disability, social exclusion, or collective trauma.

In this sense, definitions are proposed that understand occupational resilience as the capacity to persist in, or transform, performance in certain occupations despite environmental barriers, traumatic events, or prolonged strain (Muriithi & Muriithi, 2020). Finally, in other cases, it is conceived as an observable outcome, inferred from better‐than‐expected social and occupational functioning under stressful circumstances (Denckla et al., 2017; Petri‐Romão et al., 2025; Srivastava & Shaw, 2015).

Contexts, adversities, and occupational settings

In the reviewed literature, terms such as work‐related stress, organisational uncertainty, work overload, or traumatic experiences are used, which are often grouped under the broad heading of ‘adversity’ (Figure 2). Consequences are described such as job insecurity, role conflicts, impostor syndrome, uncertainty regarding transitions, fatigue, or burnout, which affect work engagement and continuity and, when prolonged, wellbeing and health (Heritage et al., 2019; Isenberg et al., 2025).

Types of adversity considered in the analysed studies (n = 37).

Beyond employment, and in line with a broad understanding of occupation as participation in meaningful activities, adversity and exposure to potentially traumatic events also impact other areas of everyday life. In this sense, situations are identified that limit opportunities for participation or challenge the continuity of desired occupations, together with experiences of occupational deprivation in which, due to a lack of spaces, time, supports, or other resources or due to physical, organisational, or contextual barriers, it becomes unfeasible to carry out meaningful occupations (Muriithi & Gore, 2023; Muriithi & Muriithi, 2020; Van Niekerk, 2024; Van Veenendaal, 2024).

Likewise, contexts are described in which conditions or health risks (health crises, lockdowns, and food insecurity) alter routines and participation, forcing people to modify or replace occupations (Ahmed et al., 2024; Brown, 2021; Wegner et al., 2022; Yusuf et al., 2022), as well as settings in which adversity is linked to structural causes such as social injustice, poverty, insecurity, stigma, or lack of basic resources (Freeman et al., 2024; Molakeng et al., 2023; Srivastava & Shaw, 2015).

Only a small number of studies indicate the presence of critical events, positioning trauma as a qualitatively distinct experience associated with threats to life, exposure to potentially lethal violence, natural disasters, terrorist attacks, and experiences of war or extreme violence and explicitly linking it with profound disturbances of occupational performance, as these are considered crises that disorganise everyday life (Denckla et al., 2017; Henderson & Greenblatt, 2012; Lopez, 2011). In these cases, there is reference to severe childhood trauma, post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), shared (collective) trauma, or vicarious trauma, and it is described how these events force individuals, groups, and communities to reconfigure identity, roles, and participation in activities of daily living (Wegner et al., 2022).

Role of meaningful occupations in resilience

Across the included studies, occupation is linked in various ways with processes of resilience. When it is conceptualised explicitly, it appears mainly as an active mechanism, and not only as an outcome, for sustaining or rebuilding wellbeing in the face of adversity (Bowden et al., 2018). Meaningful occupations as support for adaptation and a source of wellbeing

Occupational therapy practices described in contexts of forced displacement, violence, serious illness, or structural marginalisation highlight the importance of addressing personal and structural barriers to occupational participation in an integrated way, including poverty, stigma, insecurity, and social or digital exclusion (Ahmed et al., 2024; Denckla et al., 2017). Qualitative studies with children, adults in crisis situations, and communities in socially disadvantaged contexts describe how participation in activities such as play, cultural practices, everyday care, community participation, or creative projects acts as a protective factor against psychological distress under conditions of prolonged stress (such as overload in demanding work contexts or lockdown during COVID‐19), offering structure, meaning, social connection, and a sense of efficacy and enabling identity readjustment and reinterpretation of adversity (Bowden et al., 2018; Henderson & Greenblatt, 2012).

In the Spanish adult population during the COVID‐19 lockdown, Rodríguez‐Bailón et al. (2023) describe how participation in meaningful activities acted as a key factor that positively contributed to mental wellbeing and enabled the adaptation of routines to maintain a certain level of occupational participation despite the restrictions. On the other hand, in young adults in South Africa, Wegner et al. (2022) showed that the reconfiguration of leisure occupations during the pandemic (e.g., adapting activities to the domestic environment or exploring new forms of leisure) helped to mitigate the impact of disruption and to sustain resilience processes through new meaningful routines.

Building on the notion of ‘occupational gifts’ proposed by Thibeault (2011), Brown (2021) uses this term to refer to meaningful occupations that can promote optimism, spirituality, resilience, and a sense of belonging in demanding and adverse contexts.

In Fort Albany First Nation, a remote Indigenous community in northern Ontario (Canada), traditional occupations such as hunting, fishing, or community gardening sustain cultural continuity, mental wellbeing, and social cohesion, becoming a core of community resilience in the face of food insecurity and geographic isolation (Ahmed et al., 2024).

In relation to childhood, Bowden et al. (2018) show that everyday activities such as play, music, social media use, and creative practices (e.g., reading, writing, or drawing) form part of the subjective experience of resilience, insofar as they facilitate emotional expression, provide moments of relief and relaxation, and allow children to gain distance from difficulties, helping them to perceive themselves as being in better shape after adverse situations (Özcan, 2024).

Similarly, among students and health professionals, engagement in occupations linked to meaningful work and the development of professional identity is related to greater resilience, higher satisfaction, and less burnout, suggesting that the sense of purpose provided by certain occupations buffers the impact of academic and work‐related stress (Isenberg et al., 2025; Magrin et al., 2017; Rees et al., 2018).

Specifically, in the case of doctoral occupational therapy students at a university in the United States, Isenberg et al. (2025) describe how participation in a structured ecological belonging programme (which combines guided academic activities reflecting on students' training trajectories with group spaces for social exchange and support) facilitated experiences of catharsis, improved wellbeing and confidence, and strengthened social connection, helping to prevent isolation.

Among young adults, occupation contributed to giving life meaning through leisure experiences during the pandemic (Wegner et al., 2022), whereas the incorporation of flexibility into everyday participation was linked to health, wellbeing, and social connection, especially when it involved a paradigm shift in how people live, work, learn, socialise, and enjoy life (Brown, 2021). Likewise, activities such as listening to music or using social media as a source of information helped redirect attention and promote relaxation (Bowden et al., 2018).

Occupation as a mechanism for coping and change

Several studies explicitly describe occupation as a mechanism for coping and change, illustrating how, through action, people regain a sense of function and control. In the case of mental health consultants working in early childhood programmes after the 9/11 attacks, Henderson and Greenblatt (2012) argue, from a theoretical perspective, that active participation in their professional role became a key adaptive strategy for managing their own responses to trauma. In this sense, some authors highlight doing as the central element of resilience, to the point that recovery from adversity depends on the extent to which these functions have become routine within a person's life (Van Niekerk, 2024). This idea supports the findings reported by Lopez (2011), who notes that people affected by forced displacement, violence, or illness report ‘doing things’ that matter to them (continuing to care for others, taking part in community activities, resuming work roles, or initiating new life projects) as a fundamental mechanism for reducing suffering, regaining control, and reconfiguring identity after traumatic experiences, such that recovery involves once again overcoming adversity and becoming a different person, better prepared for future challenges (Bowden et al., 2018). From this perspective, resilience is not only an internal quality but also something that occurs in and through occupations in particular contexts. In a way, as Muriithi et al. (2022) argue, a person ‘does’ resilience when they adapt, negotiate, or transform their occupational participation in the face of adversity.

In school contexts, the effectiveness of resilience‐promoting tasks is also highlighted, as their performance improves students' capacity to adapt to occupational challenges and participate meaningfully at school, thereby increasing confidence in their personal capacity and supporting the reconstruction of a sense of identity based on acceptance of limitations and adjustment of expectations (Jacobs‐Nzuzi Khuabi et al., 2022). In families of children with disabilities during COVID‐19, routines, enjoyable family activities, and meditation practices were conceptualised as social, educational, and behavioural supports with sufficient potential to enhance caregiver empowerment (Yusuf et al., 2022). Leisure literature during the pandemic also describes a transition from disruption and loneliness to positive adaptation through the creation or adaptation of occupations (home‐based exercise, video games, sewing, reading, and cooking), illustrating how occupational adaptation is a key mechanism of resilience (Wegner et al., 2022).

Occupation as a setting and source of adversity

In other studies, occupation appears as the setting in which adversity becomes visible, making communication and collaboration, as well as the application of problem‐solving skills, fundamental in that context (Gümüştekin, 2022). Mostly linked to the study of stress management in health‐care settings, some cases identify occupation itself (work performance or the context of clinical placements for students) as a source of stress that requires external intervention (Brough & Boase, 2019; Raper et al., 2023). Strategies such as preparing professionals to face complexity and uncertainty, incorporating AI technologies that reduce workload and mitigate risks (Adamopoulos et al., 2025), fostering professional vocation as a way of transforming intrinsic motivation into occupational and resilience skills (Xie et al., 2024), or promoting gradual return to the workplace after sick leave as a way of supporting adaptation (Hassanin et al., 2025; Heritage et al., 2019) can mitigate the negative impact of such stressful environments.

Occupation as the axis of the resilience process

The most recent theoretical approaches position occupation as the central construct of occupational resilience and as the axis for guiding intervention. Muriithi et al. (2022) conceptualise occupational resilience as the capacity to persist in performing a specific occupation, observable in the duration and intensity of participation over time, situated on a continuum that encompasses occupations that are beneficial and harmful to health. Van Niekerk (2024) proposes a bidirectional relationship between ‘doing differently’ and ‘being differently’. For this author, participation in adapted occupations after work disruption makes it possible to rebuild identities, foster future‐focussed attitudes, and alleviate situations of occupational injustice if it is accompanied by contextual changes and policies that expand opportunities for participation.

Furthermore, some studies explore how people negotiate their resilience through continuity and transformation of their occupations. In contexts of violence and poverty, they describe the presence of a receptive and supportive ecology in which self‐care and empowerment are prioritised through commitment to a personally meaningful purpose, enabling people, despite difficulties, to see the results of their efforts (Molakeng et al., 2023). Other studies show that occupations are maintained even in contexts of adversity, with persistence in occupation being decisive for its impact on health (Muriithi & Muriithi, 2020). From a more conceptual standpoint, a changing relationship between the person and the specific occupation is noted, introducing the concept of an occupational resilience optimisation process to emphasise that recovery involves once again overcoming whatever adversity arises and becoming a different person who is better prepared for the next challenge (Bowden et al., 2018; Muriithi & Muriithi, 2020).

Finally, Van Veenendaal (2024), drawing on a critical review, argues that it is preferable to avoid individualistic interventions focussed on teaching decontextualised resilience skills, instead proposing the promotion of interventions that facilitate occupation in its various forms, recognising its complexity, and acknowledging that it is precisely through doing in meaningful and just contexts that resilience is built.

DISCUSSION

As evidenced by the growing number of articles on occupational resilience published over the past 25 years, the interest in operationalising, measuring, and enhancing the mechanisms underpinning this human response to adversity has led to advances and the wider dissemination of its study across diverse settings, populations, and realities, with resilience often being categorised according to the context under examination. In much of the reviewed literature, whose specialised terminology is linked to disciplines such as organisational psychology, occupational health, or the work sciences, the term ‘occupational’ has been used almost exclusively as a synonym for ‘work‐related’, associated with the capacity to sustain one's professional role in high‐demand contexts.

From our perspective as occupational therapists, this normalisation of resilience has helped advance understanding of human responses to adversity; however, framing the study of resilience within the work context, often labelled ‘occupational’, generates confusion and a certain reductionism that needs to be acknowledged. In this work, occupation is understood as a network of meaningful activities of self‐care, caring for others, productivity, play, and community participation that sustain everyday life, in line with authors who describe occupation as the doing of work, play, and activities of daily living within specific temporal, physical, and sociocultural contexts (Kielhofner, 2004) and as everything people do to look after themselves, enjoy life, and contribute to the social and economic fabric of their communities (Townsend & Wikcock, 2024). Therefore, reducing occupational resilience to performance in paid work not only blurs the meaning of the concept but also creates tension with these conceptions and with the findings of this scoping review, which show how resilient responses are also configured through participation in meaningful occupations situated in the home, family, and community, including the maintenance of occupational balance, the preservation of valued routines and roles, the use of occupations as coping strategies, and the construction of wellbeing, sense of belonging, and personal growth in contexts of adversity.

Tensions and overlaps between approaches

The findings of this study highlight a conceptual tension between the dominant psychologising reading, centred on individual differences, and a dynamic understanding that conceives occupational resilience as a relational and transactional process. In this regard, the study of psychological and emotional responses to trauma has been enriched by analyses of the influence of the environment on recovery (Masten, 2021; Ungar, 2021). Incorporating a systemic and constructivist perspective into the study of occupational resilience invites consideration of factors such as social connection, respectful communication, perceived social support, and social belonging as foundations for examining a phenomenon that, as shown here, is not confined solely to the work domain.

At the same time, some more integrative frameworks articulate points of convergence between both perspectives, recognising that personal traits and resources (such as self‐efficacy, perseverance, or motivation) are constructed and updated through concrete experiences of occupational participation and that organisational, community, and family contexts can either facilitate or constrain the possibilities of deploying resilient responses (Heritage et al., 2019; Molakeng et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2024). Although the diversity of approaches (those that conceive it either as a trait or capacity or as a process situated in the occupations of daily life) provides considerable theoretical richness, it also makes it difficult to move towards more precise and operational definitions of occupational resilience that are coherent with the understanding of human occupation. Therefore, and in line with proposals that have underscored that resilience processes are constructed in relation to other people and to everyday contexts, especially in educational and community settings (Puig Esteve & Rubio Rabal, 2011), this study adopts a perspective on resilience in occupational terms, focussed on the interactions between people, environments, and occupations.

Thus, although the conception of occupational resilience as an individual attribute remains present, the literature review reveals a reiteration of similar proposals and arguments, compounded by the reliance on measurement instruments that perpetuate decontextualised interpretations disconnected from the specific occupational and sociocultural contexts in which people live and work. Advances in the fields of epigenetics and neuroscience (León‐Rodríguez & Cárdenas, 2020) support the need to incorporate environmental aspects into the study of responses to adversity, with particular attention to the presence of trusting relationships (Grotberg, 1999; Richardson et al., 2022).

From this perspective, we propose occupational resilience as a framework that organises intervention, placing the emphasis on resources, personal strengths, and support networks rather than on deficits and highlighting the role of the environment as a source of assets (supports and opportunities) to sustain the continuity and reorganisation of everyday life when facing and moving beyond adversity.

Consequently, it becomes imperative to reconceptualise occupational resilience not merely as an individual trait for enduring work‐related stress but as a socio‐ecological and multidimensional process that is manifested and constructed through participation in meaningful occupations. Within this framework, in which resilience emerges from the ongoing interaction between the person, their occupations, and their ecology, occupations (encompassing work, self‐care, leisure, and social participation) are configured as active mechanisms through which individuals and collectives negotiate adversity, access resources, and transform their circumstances and identities, making possible the recovery and re‐signification of trauma. In the same vein, Puig Esteve and Rubio Rabal (2015) propose the notion of ‘tutors of resilience’ to describe how certain people or activities can function as stable supports that offer safety, validate distress, and accompany processes of rebuilding life after traumatic experiences, fostering the re‐establishment of routines, basic trust, and continuity in everyday life. This scoping review reinforces that proposal by showing how, in different contexts, certain meaningful occupations and key support figures become anchoring points that sustain occupational participation and facilitate movement from positions of greater dependence towards higher levels of autonomy and engagement with daily life.

Reclaiming the place of occupation

Despite the theoretical centrality of occupation in occupational therapy and occupational science, most empirical studies on occupational resilience designed from occupational perspectives still focus on psychological variables (self‐efficacy, traits, and coping) and work‐related outcomes (burnout and intention to leave), with little or no attention paid to the specific occupations involved in occupational resilience processes. Even when populations whose lives are strongly shaped by occupation are studied (agricultural workers, teachers, trauma consultants, and carers), detailed descriptions of what they do, how they transform their routines, and which concrete activities sustain their resilience often remain in the background.

It is therefore necessary to incorporate the value of everyday life as a source of recovery, given its transformative potential. As Brown (2021) argues, it is in daily life that traumatic events have their impact, affecting those aspects that provide structure, organisation, and support. Reclaiming the potentially transformative role of everyday occupations (Cirineu & Morrison, 2025) entails recognising the importance of adapting to daily occupational challenges through flexible participation in everyday occupations that promote health, wellbeing, and social connection.

Contributions from trauma‐informed literature in occupational therapy

Across the 37 studies included in the review, many of the populations examined are exposed to high levels of stress linked to contexts characterised by lack of control and unpredictability. However, in many cases, these situations can be seen as normative when the personal, temporal, or cultural context in which they occur is considered, as they are often associated with educational or career transitions, service pressures, or bureaucratic demands. Labelling them as ‘severe adversity’ or ‘potentially traumatic events’, without differentiating levels of severity, duration, or the subjective impact of the event or challenge, creates some confusion when attempting to advance understanding of resilience as a human response.

This generalisation and equation of distress with trauma also tends to normalise high levels of stress and to treat as equivalent situations that differ markedly in severity and in their consequences for health and participation. Only a subset of the studies reviewed explicitly distinguishes trauma as a qualitatively different experience, associated with threats to life, severe violence, or collective disasters, and links it to profound disruptions in identity, roles, and occupational performance (Denckla et al., 2017; Freeman et al., 2024).

The specific literature on trauma and occupation offers a novel contribution (Lopez, 2011; Muriithi et al., 2022; Van Niekerk, 2024) by clarifying the impact of trauma in contrast with situations that, while challenging, do not necessarily disorganise everyday life or significantly interrupt occupational participation. From this perspective, trauma has been defined as the result of events or contexts experienced as harmful or potentially life‐threatening, which overwhelm usual coping capacities and produce enduring changes in one's sense of safety, in the body, and in identity (Denckla et al., 2017; Freeman et al., 2024).

In these studies, occupation appears as a central mechanism of post‐traumatic resilience, whereby people draw on everyday activities, productive roles, self‐care, and collective practices to reduce suffering, rebuild identity, and regain agency after traumatic experiences. For example, among women facing armed conflict, the transformation of caring occupations and community participation is linked to the reconstruction of life projects (Van Niekerk, 2024). Among people with work‐related PTSD or schizophrenia, the gradual resumption of work and the expansion of the occupational repertoire are associated with functional and symbolic recovery (Freeman et al., 2024). In communities that have experienced collective trauma, community and peace‐building occupations are described as pathways to collective resilience (Henderson & Greenblatt, 2012; Lopez, 2011).

Trauma‐sensitive interventions to move beyond a work‐stress‐centred view of occupational resilience

Based on the results, it is suggested that, for populations exposed to trauma, occupation‐based interventions need to be explicitly trauma‐informed, rather than limited to generic stress‐management programmes or work‐resilience training (Lopez, 2011; Muriithi et al., 2022; Van Niekerk, 2024). Occupational therapy evidence shows that, when traumatic experiences are present, occupational resilience involves processes of identity reconstruction and profound reconfiguration of doing, which require trauma‐sensitive interventions and more complex socio‐ecological frameworks.

Therefore, explicit recognition of trauma and its manifestations (avoidance, hypervigilance, dissociation, and disruptions of identity) is crucial in the planning and delivery of occupational therapy interventions, in order to avoid practices that may re‐activate traumatic memories in an uncontained way during the performance of certain occupations (Henderson & Greenblatt, 2012; Lopez, 2011). In addition, a gradual focus on the occupational repertoire is proposed, supporting the recovery or exploration of occupations that provide safety, meaning, and connection, which may be individual (self‐care and creative leisure) or collective (community activities, peace‐building, and mutual care) (Ahmed et al., 2024; Henderson & Greenblatt, 2012; Lopez, 2011; Van Niekerk, 2024).

Ultimately, an integrative approach reconciles the tension between individual and contextual perspectives by positioning occupation (doing) as the bridge that connects them. From this vantage point, knowledge generated by research on individual factors such as self‐efficacy, self‐regulation, or tolerance of ambiguity (Dudley‐Javoroski et al., 2024; Heritage et al., 2019) is strengthened by acknowledging that these factors are built and reinforced through successful participation in challenging occupations. Similarly, contextual factors such as organisational support (Xie et al., 2024) are expressed in practice through concrete ways of doing at work, such as engaging in collaborative, respectful work occupations that are consistent with people's values.

At the same time, the reconceptualisation of occupational resilience entails broadening the focus beyond work settings, recognising that resilience is nurtured and expressed across all life domains, from family relationships (Molakeng et al., 2023) to leisure activities (Bowden et al., 2018). Adopting this framework therefore has practical and theoretical implications that make it necessary, as Denckla et al. (2017) argue, to definitively move beyond the hegemony of individual resilience.

The risk of invulnerability

In the studies included in this review, moving beyond the conception of resilience as an exceptional, invulnerable response, adaptation is shown to unfold through ordinary efforts to reorganise everyday occupations after trauma or adversity, with these processes coexisting alongside ongoing distress.

From 2020 onwards, a gradual emergence of interest in the study of PTG can be observed, with a clear upturn that may be due to the size of the population affected by the COVID‐19 pandemic and the need to document people's capacity to find meaning and to develop despite adversity (Tranter et al., 2021; Vanhove et al., 2016).

This shift in focus in the study of responses to trauma, as evidenced in the global analysis of the articles, is consistent with the evolution of resilience research from deficit‐ and damage‐centred models towards perspectives that incorporate healing, PTG, and the human capacity to transcend adversity (Bonanno, 2004).

Although this emphasis on transformative potential is clinically and ethically relevant, it also entails the risk of interpretations that place the responsibility for ‘resisting’ and ‘emerging stronger’ on individuals, overlooking the obligation of governments and societies to address the conditions that generate and perpetuate vulnerability and violence (Kirmayer et al., 2011; Liebenberg & Ungar, 2009). It is no coincidence that a significant proportion of the studies analysed are oriented towards explaining and making sense of what has occurred in crisis contexts, which highlights both the need for comprehensive narratives and the danger of normalising ongoing exposure to traumatic contexts without demanding structural change.

In recent years, the study of resilience has become a fundamental element in understanding the human condition, making it possible to reinterpret human responses to trauma through a review of the characteristics present in individuals, groups, communities, and environments that have enabled them to move beyond harm. These findings resonate with trauma‐aware treatment approaches that stress the need to validate pain and to recognise the impact of shock, terror, and toxic stress before promoting resilience narratives (Contreras, 2025).

Translated into occupational therapy, this points to the relevance of developing interventions focussed on the occupational effects of trauma, with occupational therapists positioned to accompany individuals, groups, and communities in understanding how traumatic experience alters their occupational lives (occupational rupture, deprivation, alienation, or disruption, etc.) and what this implies for their occupational balance, performance, and occupational identity (Dür et al., 2022; Townsend & Wilcock, 2004; Whiteford, 2000). Recognising these impacts makes it possible to design interventions that do not focus on what the person ‘cannot do’ but rather on how to restore and recreate roles, participation, routines, and senses of self through safe, meaningful, and adapted occupations within climates of safety, trust, and respect.

Occupational resilience in the DNA of occupational therapy and occupational science

As noted throughout this review, some authors have advanced the conceptualisation of this construct from a holistic perspective grounded in the value of occupation. Although still scarce, there are several recent publications (Brown, 2021) whose contribution is to broaden understanding of the concept from an explicitly occupational standpoint. For these authors, optimal occupational resilience is that which enables people to persist in healthy and culturally meaningful occupations, to modify or abandon harmful occupations, and to participate in collective processes that expand opportunities for doing and being, especially for populations exposed to trauma, inequality, and occupational injustice (Gümüştekin, 2022; Muriithi et al., 2022; Rodríguez‐Bailón et al., 2023; Switzer, 2025).

In contrast with literature that uses ‘occupational’ as a synonym for work‐related and focuses on workers' capacity to sustain performance in high‐demand contexts, these contributions place occupational resilience at the core of the historical concerns of occupational therapy and occupational science: occupational justice, access to meaningful occupations, and the possibility of rebuilding everyday life following adversity. From this perspective, occupational resilience is not limited to enduring stress or maintaining a professional role; rather, it involves expanding opportunities for doing and being, renegotiating roles and relationships, and transforming the environments that perpetuate inequality and occupational injustice. In this way, occupational resilience becomes a bridging concept linking the clinical and sociopolitical dimensions of occupational therapy practice, offering a framework for designing interventions that move beyond individual ‘resilience skills’ training and instead focus on the transformative potential of occupations. In this sense, occupational resilience, within occupational therapy and occupational science, ceases to be merely an individual resource for enduring work‐related stress and becomes a framework for understanding how individuals and collectives rebuild everyday life through occupation.

Building on the synthesis undertaken in this scoping review and with the aim of moving towards a more process‐oriented conception, decoupled from the exclusively work‐ and productivity‐focussed view recurrent in the literature, occupational resilience is proposed here as the process through which individuals, groups, and communities, after experiencing adversity or trauma, use and transform their meaningful occupations (including everyday ones) to reduce suffering, reconstruct a sense of purpose and agency, and restore (or reinvent) patterns of participation, roles, routines, and occupational identities in contexts that may remain uncertain or threatening. This definition highlights the role of occupation throughout the resilience process and explicitly integrates personal, contextual, and occupational dimensions, extending previous proposals on occupational resilience that were centred on work settings and making it necessary to recontextualise occupational resilience as a construct.

Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations. In line with scoping review methodology, no formal appraisal of risk of bias or methodological quality of the included studies was conducted, given the need to broaden the evidence base even at the risk of including studies with unclear or incomplete designs. Furthermore, the searches were limited to a specific set of databases, languages, and descriptors, acknowledging the high likelihood that many relevant results would originate from fields such as psychology, public health, or education. This means that the concept does not emerge exclusively from or for occupational therapy and occupational science, but rather in dialogue with theoretical frameworks and practices from other disciplines. Consequently, it is possible that not all relevant works in the grey literature or in other geographical contexts were identified and that some nuances specific to occupational therapy and occupational science were not considered in broader approaches to resilience. In addition, the synthesis focussed on describing trends, thematic emphases, and gaps, reflecting the state of knowledge up to the date of the final search; therefore, subsequent studies will need to refine or expand the search patterns.

Finally, the absence of consumer and community involvement is acknowledged as a limitation of the study, and its inclusion in future research is recommended to strengthen the relevance and applicability of the findings.

CONCLUSIONS

Moving beyond the prevailing view of resilience as a stable internal trait related to work contexts, this scoping review presents a reconceptualisation of occupational resilience as a socio‐ecological and dynamic process aimed at rebuilding everyday life, sense of purpose, and agency, constructed at the intersection between people, specific occupations, and contexts, and expressed both in the capacity to reconfigure doing and in the real possibility of continuing to participate in meaningful occupations despite adversity.

The evidence indicates that engaging in meaningful occupations makes it possible to modulate suffering and emotional activation, reorganise the occupational repertoire, and protect and even create new forms of occupational participation that help rebuild or regain a sense of biographical continuity after the impact of trauma. Therefore, this scoping review confirms that occupational resilience is a particularly relevant construct at present, in a context marked by health, social, and ecological crises that increase the exposure of individuals, groups, and communities to experiences of adversity and trauma.

Finally, significant conceptual and methodological limitations in the available evidence are highlighted. The variability of definitions, theoretical frameworks, and measures used makes comparison of results difficult and limits the possibility of drawing robust conclusions for evidence‐based practice. It is therefore necessary to move towards greater uniformity in study designs, preferably longitudinal and multilevel; a more critical use of measurement tools; and the development of conceptual models that contribute to a better understanding of occupational resilience processes and support interventions that not only recognise people's capacity to adapt but also challenge the social conditions that make such adaptive effort necessary and sometimes unsustainable.
