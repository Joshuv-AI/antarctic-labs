Sex and socioeconomic inequalities in global asthma burden attributable to major risk factors, 1990–2021
Authors: Zongxuan Gao, Hao Zhu, Maoyu Zhang, Yi Dai, Tongheng Wu, Yingjie Han, Yonghuai Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAsthma remains a major global health issue, and modelled estimates suggest that its burden attributable to modifiable risk factors is unevenly distributed by sex and socioeconomic context. We aimed to assess long-term sex and socioeconomic inequalities in asthma burden attributable to major risk factors from 1990 to 2021.MethodsUsing Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 modelled estimates, we analysed asthma-specific disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), years of life lost (YLLs), deaths, and average years of life lost (AYLL; derived as YLLs divided by deaths) attributable to “All risk factors” as a GBD-defined aggregate category, and to four individual risk factors examined separately: high body-mass index (BMI), smoking, occupational asthmagens, and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) pollution. Temporal trends were evaluated using Joinpoint regression. Changes in DALYs were decomposed into the effects of population growth, population ageing, and epidemiological change using Das Gupta decomposition. Socioeconomic inequalities across levels of the Socio-demographic Index (SDI) were assessed using the slope index of inequality (SII) and concentration index (CI).ResultsFrom 1990 to 2021, global asthma DALYs attributable to All risk factors, as defined in the GBD risk-attribution framework, increased by 8.6%, mainly reflecting population growth and ageing, whereas the age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR) declined, with an estimated annual percentage change (APC) of −1.8% per year. Male DALYs decreased by 2.2%, whereas female DALYs increased by 24.6%. Among the four specific risk factors assessed individually, high BMI accounted for the largest DALY burden, while occupational asthmagens accounted for the greatest AYLL. Point-estimate inequality trends suggested narrowing absolute and relative inequalities for most risk factors, but occupational asthmagen-attributable burden showed persistent or widening inequalities, particularly in lower-SDI settings.ConclusionsBased on modelled GBD estimates, these findings suggest that equity-oriented strategies targeting modifiable risk exposures may help reduce asthma burden and promote global respiratory health equity.

Highlight box

Key findings

• From 1990 to 2021, global asthma disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) attributable to All risk factors increased despite a decline in the age-standardized DALY rate, suggesting that population growth and ageing contributed to the rising absolute burden.

• Among the individual risk factors assessed, high body-mass index accounted for the largest attributable DALY burden, whereas occupational asthmagens accounted for the greatest average years of life lost (AYLL).

• Point-estimate inequality analyses suggested persistent or widening socioeconomic inequalities in occupational asthmagen-attributable asthma burden, particularly in lower-Socio-demographic Index (SDI) settings.

What is known and what is new?

• Previous Global Burden of Disease (GBD)-based studies have described the global, regional, and national burden of asthma and selected attributable risk factors.

• This study integrates risk-specific burden estimates, sex-stratified analyses, AYLL, Joinpoint regression, Das Gupta decomposition, and absolute and relative socioeconomic inequality metrics within a unified GBD 2021 framework.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings suggest that equity-oriented asthma prevention strategies should address obesity, smoking, air pollution, occupational exposures, and access to asthma care, with particular attention to lower-SDI settings and sex-specific patterns of risk-attributable burden.

Introduction

Asthma is a common chronic inflammatory airway disease and remains an important contributor to respiratory morbidity and mortality worldwide (1). Although asthma-related mortality has declined in many regions, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) attributable to asthma remain substantial, reflecting the persistent effects of long-term symptoms, recurrent exacerbations, impaired quality of life, and premature mortality (2). The global distribution of asthma burden is highly heterogeneous, suggesting that demographic structure, socioeconomic development, health-system capacity, and exposure to preventable risk factors may jointly shape its population-level impact.

Several modifiable risk factors, including high body-mass index (BMI), smoking, occupational asthmagens, and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) pollution, contribute to asthma-related disease burden within the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) comparative risk assessment framework (2). High BMI may contribute to asthma through systemic inflammation, altered lung mechanics, and metabolic dysregulation (3), while tobacco smoke may promote chronic airway inflammation and oxidative stress (4). NO2 pollution has been associated with increased airway susceptibility and asthma risk, particularly during critical developmental periods in childhood (5), and exposure to occupational asthmagens remains an important contributor to work-related asthma in many settings (6). However, exposure to these risk factors is not evenly distributed across populations. Sex-related biological differences, smoking behaviours, occupational patterns, urbanization, environmental regulation, and unequal access to prevention and medical care may all contribute to sex and socioeconomic disparities in asthma burden (2,7). Nevertheless, the long-term global patterns of these disparities, particularly for risk factor-attributable asthma burden, remain insufficiently characterized.

Although previous GBD-based studies have described the global, regional, and national burden of asthma, many have focused on overall asthma burden, selected age groups, specific regions, or individual risk factors rather than providing an integrated assessment of risk-specific, sex-specific, and socioeconomic inequalities (8). Several recent analyses have also examined asthma burden and attributable risk factors using GBD data, but the combined assessment of multiple major risk factors, sex disparities, demographic drivers, and socioeconomic inequalities has not been fully evaluated within a unified analytical framework (9,10). In particular, few studies have jointly quantified absolute and relative socioeconomic inequalities using established inequality metrics, assessed average years of life lost (AYLL) as an indicator of premature mortality, and decomposed temporal changes in DALYs into contributions from population growth, population ageing, and epidemiological change (11). Taken together, the main contribution of this study is the integration of risk-specific burden estimates, sex-stratified analyses, AYLL, demographic decomposition, and socioeconomic inequality metrics within a single GBD 2021 analytical framework.

Therefore, using GBD 2021 modelled estimates, we examined temporal trends in asthma DALYs and AYLL attributable to major risk factors from 1990 to 2021, with particular attention to sex differences. We further quantified the contributions of population growth, population ageing, and epidemiological change to changes in DALYs using Das Gupta decomposition, and assessed absolute and relative socioeconomic inequalities across levels of the Socio-demographic Index (SDI) using the slope index of inequality (SII) and concentration index (CI). This study provides a comprehensive description of how modifiable risk exposures, sex, and socioeconomic context are associated with global asthma burden. We present this article in accordance with the GATHER reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0863/rc).

Methods

Data source

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.Data were obtained from the GBD 2021 study, which provides modelled estimates for diseases, injuries, and risk factors across 204 countries/regions and territories from 1990 to 2021 (11,12). We extracted data on DALYs, years of life lost (YLLs), deaths, and age-standardized DALY rates (ASDRs) due to asthma attributable to All risk factors, high BMI, smoking, occupational asthmagens, and NO2 pollution using the GBD Results Tool. Population estimates used in the decomposition analysis were also derived from the GBD 2021 study (13).

GBD risk-attributable estimates are generated within the comparative risk assessment framework. In this study, “All risk factors” refers to the GBD-defined aggregate estimate of asthma burden attributable to All risk factors included in the asthma comparative risk assessment framework and was not calculated by summing the four individual risk factors examined. Because these risk exposures may overlap, risk-specific attributable burdens were interpreted separately, and no joint effects or interactions were estimated.

We used GBD point estimates and corresponding 95% uncertainty intervals. Derived indicators, including AYLL, percentage change, decomposition components, the SII, and the CI, were calculated using point estimates. Full uncertainty propagation using GBD posterior draws was not performed; therefore, these derived metrics were interpreted descriptively rather than as estimates with fully propagated uncertainty. Negative lower bounds of uncertainty intervals for low-magnitude attributable estimates were retained as reported by GBD and were not interpreted as indicating negative disease burden.

Outcome measures

Asthma burden was assessed using DALYs, ASDRs, YLLs, deaths, and AYLL. AYLL was calculated as YLLs divided by deaths and was used as a complementary mortality indicator reflecting the average premature mortality burden per asthma death. Unlike total YLLs or YLL rates, which are influenced by population size and the number of deaths, AYLL provides information on the AYLL among individuals who died from asthma. AYLL was interpreted together with DALYs, ASDRs, YLLs, and deaths rather than as a replacement for these indicators. Sex differences were assessed using male-to-female ratios for ASDR and AYLL, with values greater than 1 indicating a higher burden among males and values less than 1 indicating a higher burden among females.

SDI

The SDI is a composite indicator reflecting a country’s level of socioeconomic development, based on fertility rate among females younger than 25 years, mean educational attainment among those aged ≥15 years, and lag-distributed income (LDI) per capita. SDI ranges from 0 (lowest development) to 1 (highest development), allowing comparison across low-, middle-, and high-development settings. Countries and territories were also grouped into five SDI quintiles according to GBD classifications (11).

Trend analysis

Temporal trends in ASDR, AYLL, and their male-to-female ratios were evaluated using Joinpoint regression analysis (Joinpoint, version 5.3.0). This approach identifies points (“joinpoints”) where significant changes in trends occur and estimates the annual percentage change (APC) for each segment using weighted least squares regression (8,14).

Decomposition analysis

To distinguish the contributions of demographic and epidemiological factors to changes in asthma DALYs, we applied Das Gupta decomposition. Total changes in DALYs between 1990 and 2021 were partitioned into three components: population growth, population ageing, and epidemiological change, defined as changes in age-specific DALY rates. Positive values indicated contributions to increased DALYs, whereas negative values indicated contributions to reduced DALYs. The decomposition was conducted using point estimates and was interpreted descriptively because uncertainty was not fully propagated (12).

Inequality assessment

Following the World Health Organization (WHO)/GBD framework, we evaluated absolute inequality using the SII and relative inequality using the CI. For each year, countries/regions were ranked by SDI from lowest to highest, and population-weighted relative ranks were assigned according to the cumulative population distribution, with each country positioned at the midpoint of its cumulative population share. The SII was estimated using weighted regression of asthma DALYs on the population-weighted SDI rank, representing the predicted absolute difference in burden between the highest and lowest ends of the SDI distribution. Positive SII values indicate a higher absolute burden among higher-SDI countries/regions, whereas negative values indicate a higher absolute burden among lower-SDI countries/regions. The CI was calculated by numerical integration of the concentration curve, which plots the cumulative proportion of asthma DALYs against the cumulative proportion of the population ranked by SDI. Positive CI values indicate concentration of burden among higher-SDI countries/regions, whereas negative CI values indicate concentration among lower-SDI countries/regions. Because uncertainty was not fully propagated for SII and CI, these inequality metrics were interpreted as point-estimate trends (15).

Statistical analysis

All analyses and visualizations were performed using R software (version 4.4.1). A two-sided P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for Joinpoint trend analyses. Decomposition and inequality analyses were based on point estimates and were interpreted descriptively.

Results

Global temporal trends in DALYs and ASDR

Globally, asthma DALYs attributable to All risk factors reached 5.88 million in 2021, representing an 8.6% increase compared with 1990. As defined in the Methods, this estimate refers to the GBD aggregate estimate and was not calculated by summing the four individual risk factors examined. However, the ASDR declined over the same period, suggesting that demographic changes contributed to the increase in total DALYs despite reductions in age-standardized burden. Sex-specific patterns diverged substantially: male DALYs decreased by 2.2% to 3.17 million, whereas female DALYs increased by 24.6% to 2.72 million. Among the four individual risk factors examined, high BMI accounted for the largest share of DALYs and showed the greatest increase from 1990 to 2021 (+45.7%), with a consistently higher burden among females (Table 1). In contrast, smoking- and occupational asthmagen-attributable DALYs declined in high-SDI and high-income regions, particularly among males. Regionally, DALYs declined in high-SDI and high-income regions, whereas Sub-Saharan Africa, low-SDI regions, and South Asia experienced substantial increases, largely driven by high BMI-related burden (Table 1 and Table S1).

Sex differences by risk factor

Sex differences varied markedly by risk factor, indicator, and socioeconomic setting. For DALY numbers, the increase in high BMI-attributable burden was more pronounced among females than males, particularly in South Asia, where high BMI-related DALYs increased by 202.0% in females and 140.6% in males. Smoking-attributable DALYs generally declined, especially among males in high-SDI and high-income regions, whereas female smoking-related burden remained relatively stable or increased in selected settings, including high-SDI regions and some lower-SDI regions. ASDR-based sex differences also varied substantially across risk factors and regions. Occupational asthmagens contributed more prominently to the male ASDR burden in higher-SDI populations, although sex differences for this risk factor narrowed over time (Table 1 and Table S1). For NO2 pollution, ASDR sex gaps varied by SDI level and region, with widening male-to-female differences in some settings by 2021. Analyses across countries/regions further showed heterogeneous ASDR and AYLL sex-difference patterns. Male predominance in AYLL and smoking-attributable ASDR was observed in several East Asian countries/regions, including the Republic of Korea and Japan, whereas the United States showed female predominance in ASDR attributable to All risk factors, high BMI, and smoking (Figure 1 and Table S2). For AYLL overall, males generally had higher values than females. In 2021, AYLL attributable to All risk factors was 27.2 years, representing a 6.3% decrease compared with 1990. Among the individual risk factors, occupational asthmagens accounted for the greatest AYLL, whereas smoking-related AYLL showed the most pronounced decline (Table 2).

Age-specific patterns

Age-specific analyses showed distinct patterns in DALY rates across risk factors. High BMI-related age-specific DALY rates increased with age and peaked in the oldest age groups, whereas occupational asthmagen-related age-specific DALY rates peaked earlier, consistent with the distribution of occupational exposure across adulthood (Figure 2 and Figures S1,S2). Smoking-related age-specific DALY rates showed an early peak among females aged 55–59 years in high-SDI regions (Figure S3). In childhood and adolescence, males had higher age-specific DALY rates attributable to high BMI and NO2 pollution in several age groups (Figures S3-S5). In adulthood, smoking contributed substantially to sex disparities in age-specific DALY rates in middle-SDI regions, whereas occupational asthmagen-related DALY rates were more prominent in older age groups in high-SDI regions (Figures S3,S5). For AYLL, the dominant contributor shifted from high BMI in younger populations to occupational asthmagens in later life. High BMI remained an important contributor to sex disparities in AYLL across most age groups (Figure 2).

Temporal trends by risk factor and region

Joinpoint analysis showed divergent temporal patterns by risk factor and SDI level. The global high BMI-related ASDR increased in recent years for both females and males, particularly in low-middle- and low-SDI regions (Figure 3 and Figures S6,S7). In contrast, smoking-related ASDR declined consistently across regions. A temporary increase in NO2 pollution-attributable ASDR was observed during 2005–2010 in middle-, low-middle-, and low-SDI regions (Figures S6-S8). By 2021, sex gaps in NO2 pollution- and high BMI-attributable ASDR had widened in several settings. Globally, AYLL attributable to All risk factors generally declined, whereas occupational asthmagen-related AYLL increased in both high- and low-SDI regions (Figures S7,S9). Furthermore, in low-middle-SDI regions, female smoking-related AYLL also increased over the past decade (Figures S6). Overall, male-to-female ratios showed divergent patterns across ASDR and AYLL. The ASDR ratio narrowed for occupational asthmagens but widened for the other risk factors, whereas the AYLL ratio increased for all risk factors, high BMI, and smoking but remained below 1 for occupational asthmagens (Figure 3).

Demographic decomposition of DALY changes

Das Gupta decomposition showed that population growth was the principal contributor to the increase in total asthma DALYs from 1990 to 2021, with population ageing also contributing in many regions. Epidemiological change generally contributed to reductions in DALYs, particularly for smoking-attributable burden. High BMI was the only individual risk factor with a net adverse epidemiological contribution globally in both males and females. Although NO2 pollution-attributable DALYs decreased at the global level, its DALY burden increased in low-SDI countries/regions, mainly reflecting population growth. These findings suggest that demographic expansion can offset improvements in age-specific rates and contribute to increasing absolute asthma burden, particularly in lower-SDI settings (Figure 4).

Socioeconomic inequalities across SDI levels

Point-estimate inequality analyses suggested that the socioeconomic distribution of risk-attributable asthma DALYs changed over time. For All risk factors, the SII shifted from positive to negative around 2008, suggesting a transition in absolute inequality from higher-SDI to lower-SDI concentration. The CI became negative earlier, after 1996, suggesting a relative concentration of burden among lower-SDI countries/regions. These temporal shifts should be interpreted cautiously because uncertainty was not fully propagated for SII and CI. In sex- and risk-specific analyses, absolute inequality declined for most risk factors, but occupational asthmagen-attributable burden was an exception, showing persistent or increasing inequality, particularly among females, with a disproportionate burden concentrated in lower-SDI countries/regions. For males, relative inequality decreased for NO2 pollution and high BMI, whereas smoking and occupational asthmagens showed renewed increases in relative inequality after 2010. Among females, SII declined for All risk factors, but occupational asthmagens showed intensified absolute and relative inequalities, with a disproportionate burden concentrated in lower-SDI countries/regions (Figure 5).

Discussion

Using GBD 2021 modelled estimates, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of long-term global trends, sex differences, demographic drivers, and socioeconomic inequalities in asthma burden attributable to major risk factors from 1990 to 2021. Several findings are noteworthy. First, although the ASDR declined, total DALYs increased, suggesting that demographic changes contributed to the increase in absolute burden. Second, high BMI accounted for the largest DALY burden and showed the greatest increase, with a consistently higher burden among females. Third, occupational asthmagens accounted for the greatest AYLL, suggesting a relatively high average premature mortality burden among asthma deaths attributable to occupational exposures. Finally, point-estimate inequality analyses suggested persistent or widening inequalities for occupational asthmagen-attributable burden, particularly in lower-SDI settings.

The increasing high BMI-attributable asthma burden is an important finding of this study. The heavier burden among females may partly reflect sex-specific differences in obesity prevalence, physical activity opportunities, metabolic factors, and access to preventive care, although these mechanisms were not directly examined in this study (3,16,17). These findings are consistent with evidence linking excess body weight to asthma through systemic inflammation, altered lung mechanics, and metabolic dysregulation (3,18). From a public health perspective, obesity prevention and weight management may be important components of asthma burden reduction, particularly in populations where high BMI is increasing rapidly and where females experience a disproportionate burden. Decomposition analysis further showed that population growth and population ageing contributed to the overall increase in asthma DALYs, suggesting that demographic shifts may pose increasing challenges for future disease prevention and health system planning.

NO2 pollution-attributable burden showed distinct age and socioeconomic patterns. The greater burden among children may be consistent with their higher ventilation rates, developing airways, and greater vulnerability to traffic-related air pollution (5,19). The temporary increase in NO2 pollution-attributable ASDR during 2005–2010 in middle-, low-middle-, and low-SDI regions could be consistent with rapid urbanization and changing traffic-related emissions, although this ecological analysis cannot directly attribute the trend to urbanization or examine individual life-course respiratory trajectories (20). In contrast, declines in higher-SDI settings may partly relate to air quality regulations, cleaner technologies, and lower demographic growth (21,22). These findings support the importance of targeted air quality interventions, especially in rapidly urbanizing areas and among children.

Occupational asthmagens accounted for the greatest AYLL among the individual risk factors examined, suggesting that asthma deaths attributable to occupational exposures may involve a relatively high average premature mortality burden. This finding is consistent with previous evidence that work-related asthma can be associated with persistent exposure, recurrent exacerbations, greater disease severity, and poorer prognosis when exposure continues (6,23). The higher or persistent burden in lower-SDI settings, particularly among females, may reflect weaker occupational health protections, limited workplace monitoring, and reduced access to early diagnosis and exposure avoidance (6,23-25). Because AYLL may be unstable in subgroups with small numbers of deaths, especially at the country, sex-specific, or risk-specific level, these findings should be interpreted together with DALYs, deaths, and YLLs rather than in isolation. These results support the importance of early identification of occupational asthma and strengthened workplace safety regulations to reduce premature mortality.

Smoking-attributable asthma burden generally declined over the study period, particularly among males and in higher-SDI settings. This pattern is consistent with the broader implementation of tobacco control measures, including taxation, public education, smoke-free policies, and smoking cessation interventions (4,26). However, female smoking-related DALYs increased in certain regions, most notably among females in high-SDI countries/regions. In parallel, smoking-related AYLL was higher among females in high-SDI settings than in low-SDI countries/regions, which may partly relate to differences in exposure patterns and age-specific smoking-related risks (4,27). These findings indicate the continued need for sex-sensitive tobacco control strategies, particularly in settings where female smoking prevalence, second-hand smoke exposure, or access to cessation support remains insufficiently addressed (26).

Pronounced sex and socioeconomic disparities were observed across major risk factors, but their magnitude varied substantially by region and risk factor. In higher-SDI settings, reductions in smoking- and occupational asthmagen-attributable burden may be consistent with long-term tobacco control efforts and improvements in occupational health protection (4,26). In contrast, increasing DALYs in lower-SDI settings may partly reflect population growth, rising high BMI-attributable burden, urbanization, and limited healthcare capacity (28-30). Country-level heterogeneity was also observed in analyses, including patterns in East Asian and other high-income settings, but these findings were used primarily to illustrate variation across settings rather than to establish country-specific causal explanations. Such patterns should be interpreted cautiously because country-level estimates may be influenced by differences in data availability, diagnostic practices, exposure assessment, healthcare access, and modelled uncertainty (11,12). Overall, these findings suggest that asthma burden is shaped by both risk exposure and broader social and health-system contexts.

Overall, inequality analyses demonstrated that both relative and absolute inequalities varied by risk factor, sex, and level of socioeconomic development. The most consistent inequality signal was observed for occupational asthmagens, for which both absolute and relative inequalities persisted or increased, particularly among females, with a disproportionate burden concentrated in lower-SDI countries/regions. This pattern may reflect unequal occupational protections, differences in exposure intensity, and disparities in access to diagnosis and treatment (6,7,31). Point-estimate trends suggested that absolute inequality for all risk factors shifted direction around 2008, while relative inequality shifted earlier. However, because uncertainty was not fully propagated for SII and CI, these turning points should be interpreted cautiously and should not be regarded as statistically definitive thresholds.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all estimates were derived from the GBD 2021 modelling framework rather than primary individual-level data. Therefore, the results depend on the availability, quality, and comparability of underlying data sources, which vary across countries/regions and over time. Differences in asthma diagnosis, coding practices, surveillance systems, and healthcare access may have affected the accuracy of asthma burden estimates, particularly in low-resource settings where underdiagnosis or misclassification may be more common (7,11,12). Second, risk-attributable burden estimates were generated within the GBD comparative risk assessment framework and depend on assumptions regarding exposure distributions, relative risks, and theoretical minimum-risk exposure levels (11). These estimates should not be interpreted as direct causal effects from this secondary descriptive analysis. Third, the risk factors examined in this study are not mutually exclusive, and possible interactions or joint effects, such as those between high BMI and air pollution, were not modelled. Fourth, full uncertainty propagation was not performed for derived indicators, including decomposition components, SII, CI, and AYLL. Therefore, temporal changes and turning points in inequality metrics should be interpreted cautiously. Fifth, SDI-based inequality analyses were conducted across countries/regions and therefore reflect ecological socioeconomic inequalities rather than individual-level socioeconomic disparities (15). Sixth, sex-specific estimates in GBD are based on binary sex categories, which limits assessment of gender diversity and gender-related social mechanisms (11,17). Finally, AYLL may be unstable when the number of asthma deaths is small, particularly in country-level, sex-specific, or risk-specific subgroups; therefore, extreme AYLL values and country-level rankings should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

This study suggests that asthma burden attributable to major risk factors remains unevenly distributed across sex and socioeconomic contexts. High BMI has become an increasingly important contributor to DALYs, occupational asthmagens are associated with the greatest AYLL, and demographic growth continues to increase absolute burden in lower-SDI settings. Although several risk-attributable burdens have declined in age-standardized terms, persistent sex and socioeconomic inequalities support the need for integrated, equity-oriented prevention strategies targeting obesity, smoking, air pollution, occupational exposures, and access to asthma care.
