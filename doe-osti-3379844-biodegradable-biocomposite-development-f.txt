ABSTRACT

Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) worked with Mitsubishi Chemical America, Inc. to develop a
highly biodegradable ~100% biobased feedstock for large format additive manufacturing (AM). Together,
they developed an AM feedstock based on the biobased polyester, polybutylene succinate (PBS), that is
typically used in agriculture and packaging projects. The motivation for this work was to leverage the high
degradability of this polymer in AM to create a new avenue for end-of-life disposal of the product. A
composite feedstock was developed from PBS, polylactic acid (PLA), and wood flour (WF). While WF
improves printability, reduces feedstock cost, and can improve stiffness, it also often results in a loss in
tensile strength which can inhibit/limit applications of the material. To recover the loss in tensile strength
suffered by including large quantities, up to 30 wt.% WF in the composite, a chain extender was used to
compatibilize the different composite constituents with each other. The feedstock was used to 3D-print a
demonstration mold for precast concrete catch basins and was tested by casting 750 lbs of concrete. The
mold survived the demonstration. Aerobic degradation testing of the composite feedstock showed that it
was highly degradable, 40% biodegradation after 90 days, compared to other incumbent biobased
feedstocks.

1. BIODEGRADABLE BIOCOMPOSITE DEVELOPMENT FOR LARGE SCALE ADDITIVE
 MANUFACTURING

 This phase 1/2 technical collaboration project (MDF-TC-2020-188) began on February 20, 2023 and
concluded on June 30, 2026. The collaboration partner Mitsubishi Chemical America, Inc (MCA) is a
large business. The project developed a biodegradable, biobased feedstock for large format additive
manufacturing and demonstrated its use in precast concrete tooling for high volume, low precision casting
operations.

1.1 BACKGROUND

 Mitsubishi Chemical Americas Performance Polymers Division (MCA-PPD) is in the
polymers division of MCA. MCA-PPD America has plants operating in Ohio, South Carolina, and
Brazil. They have a wide range of products including bio compounds, PVC, TPV, TPS, TPE, slush,
and tie resins. These products are used as solutions in various application areas such as building
and construction, medical, automotive, and packaging.
 BioPBS is produced by PTTMCC, a joint venture between PTT (Thai National Oil
company) and MCC. PTTMCC is headquartered in Thailand where the dedicated BioPBS plant
came online in 2016 with an annual capacity of 20,000 metric tons. The eco-friendly properties of
BioPBS can help bring a solution to the growing need for improved waste management and landfill
control globally.
 On the manufacturing technology side, AM is becoming a manufacturing method rather
than only a prototyping technique with recent advancements. This requires the feedstock materials
to meet some processing and mechanical requirements depending on the part and the application.
Composite tooling is one of the key areas in which large scale AM can make an impact. Composite
molds are typically manufactured out of metals via highly costly processes and with significantly
long lead times. Large scale AM process can offer rapid manufacturing of composite tooling and

 5

molds in freeform as a fast alternative to conventional approaches, especially for custom models.
However, large scale AM mainly uses carbon fiber reinforced petroleum-based polymer
feedstocks that are high cost, have high embodied energy and carbon footprint, and create landfill
waste. ORNL has been working on the development of bio-based alternative feedstocks suitable
for large scale AM. Most of these feedstocks were based on corn starch-based polylactic acid
(PLA) as a matrix system. Although PLA offers good mechanical properties such as high tensile
strength and stiffness, it has some downsides such as brittleness and low heat deflection
temperature that limit its use. Mitsubishi’s bio-based PBS grades can offer many advantages over
PLA as an alternative feedstock for large scale AM.
 Mitsubishi Chemical produces PBS grades from bio-sources and it has some key advantages
in terms of processing, performance, and end of life. First, its low melting point (115°C) enables
its processability at lower temperatures making it more energy efficient. High heat seal strength of
PBS compared to many other polymer resins can improve interlayer adhesion during the 3D-
printing process leading to improved z-strength relative to XY. Furthermore, PBS is more
compatible with bio-based fibers than PLA due to the presence of ester groups, and this can lead
to better adhesion between matrix and fibers, which can result in improved rheological behavior
and mechanical performance. Some other advantages of PBS are its high impact strength and
dramatically high ductility. Its high ductility can also enable production of parts with high
toughness. Moreover, PBS is highly compatible with PLA, and based on the desired property sets,
blends of PBS and PLA can be prepared and used. In terms of application, one key advantage of
PBS is its relatively high heat deflection temperature (HDT) (63 to 95°C at 0.45 MPa) compared
to PLA (~50 °C at 0.45 MPa), although its melting point is lower. Successful application of PBS
and its biocomposites in large scale AM can enable application of 3D-printed components up to
95°C. Lastly, from end-of-life aspect, one key feature of bioPBS is its biodegradability rate when
compared to PLA. When in contact with soil, PBS can decompose at a more favorable rate than
PLA at ambient conditions if certain environmental conditions are considered (like soil
composition, plastic surface area, filler content and type, among others). Other biopolymers,
including PLA, require composting conditions. In other words, once the use of a PBS mold/part is
complete, the mold can simply be buried in backyard soil, and it will decompose naturally in a few
years. The compost rate is currently unknown for thick-wall geometric shapes. One of the goals
of this project is to examine composting rate or degradation rate as this is a function of the final
part’s wall thickness and geometry as well as other features in the component such as fill type and
loading.

1.2 TECHNICAL RESULTS

1.2.1 Approach to Research

 One of the arching objectives of this work is the demonstration of PBS in a new application for the
material, specifically in LFAM. To achieve this goal, phase 1 investigated preliminary formulations using
PBS and selected renewable fillers and their effects on the thermal and mechanical properties as well as the
printability of the resin. Phase 2 focused on the demonstration of the material in a specific application. To
determine the application, business and engineering input was gathered from team members to identify
markets and products that would benefit from both LFAM functionality and the material itself, either in
terms of properties or by creating a new end-of-life disposal method such as aerobic degradation. Many
potential applications were evaluated including custom packaging for protection during shipping of high
value or irregularly shaped products, precast concrete anchors for offshore windmills, erosion prevention
and tress transport, degradable furniture, and tooling for precast concrete in construction applications. Each

 6

potential application is a significant departure from the material’s typical applications in consumables like
utensils or single use packaging, agricultural film, etc. Market size and material suitability are key factors
in down selection of the appropriate application area and ultimately, precast concrete tooling for
construction was chosen. Precast concrete tooling is a widespread business with multiple material families
and a range of applications from niche building facades and specialty concrete structures to high volume
products like curbs, manholes, catch basins and more. Very large precast objects require high strength
materials like steel and might not be suitable for PBS based feedstocks, but smaller objects like parking
curbs, catch basins, etc. are an opportunity. These applications would typically use a wooden mold that
would also be low cost, reusable up to 10-20 times, and the objects all have low surface finish and
geometrical tolerance requirements. The subsequent sections detail the outcomes of Phase 1/Phase 2 with
regards to developing a material that is 3D-printable and it’s use in large scale additive manufacturing.

1.2.2 Material Selection and Compounding

 Polybutylene succinate (PBS) was used as the basis for the material formulation aspect of this work
because it is a highly biodegradable material. Preliminary testing of PBS grades was performed first at the
small scale (50 g) and then at a slightly larger scale (6 Kg) for select formulations consisting of cellulose-
based reinforcement and a reactive compatibilizer. Three grades of PBS, with different melt flow indices
(MFI), were initially investigated for formulation with organic fillers: 92PBS-4, 72PBS-22, and 72PBS-50.
The MFI of polymer grades were very low MFI at 4 (higher viscosity) with the polymer grade 92PBS-4
(MFI indicated at the end of the grade name) and then two MFIs were surveyed for the 72PBS grade, MFI
of 22 and MFI of 50 (which 50 expected to have the lowest viscosity). Two organic reinforcements
investigated were wood flour (WF) and bleached soft kraft (BSK) pulp. An SEM image showing
reinforcement morphology is provided in Figure 1. WF is a low-cost biproduct of lumber manufacturing
that has not been pretreated to remove lignin or hemicellulose content. These particles are irregularly shaped
and fibrous with a large particle size distribution (~150 μm). Alternatively, BSK was also investigated.
BSK is cellulose pulp that is typically used to make paper products. It is highly refined, with little to no
remaining lignin or hemicellulose content and BSK fibers are high aspect ratio particles with thicknesses
of 3-5 μm, widths of 20-30 μm, and lengths up to 750 μm. These materials were chosen to provide a
comparison in different fiber types and in the composite can serve several roles: 1) as a rheology modifier
to improve printability, 2) decrease the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) which will oppose thermal
shrinkage 3) to stiffen and strength the final composite. Additionally, a block copolymer chain extender
known as Joncryl ADR 4468 (JON) was purchased from BASF and investigated as a potential
compatibilizer to improve interfacial adhesion between the polyester and polylactic acid. JON has an
epoxide pendant group which can react through hydroxyl (OH-) and carboxyl groups available on the chain
ends of the polymers in the blend as well as the OH- groups available on the surface of the lignocellulosic
materials initially investigated (BSK or WF).

1.2.3 Rheology of Composite Materials

 WF composite formulations were produced using both polymer resin grades for comparison and
the rheology of the composites and their respective ‘print’ temperature is reported (Figure 2). In brief,
samples were compression molded into 25 mm disks with 1.25 mm thickness for rheological testing and
isothermal frequency sweeps were conducted with the linear viscoelastic regime. An Ares G2 small
amplitude oscillatory shear (SAOS) rheometer was used to perform amplitude and frequency sweeps. The
storage modulus, loss modulus, and complex viscosity of each sample was recorded at a strain amplitude
within its linear viscoelastic envelope at frequencies ranging from 0.1 to 628 rad/s.
 A strongly non-Newtonian, shear thinning behavior is desirable in thermoplastics for additive
manufacturing feedstock, as such materials can flow easily at high shear rates but are stable at low shear
rates or at rest. In SAOS measurements, non-Newtonian behavior is indicated by a linear (in log-log space),
negatively sloping trend between complex viscosity and angular frequency. A curve or plateau in the
complex viscosity data at low frequencies indicates Newtonian behavior in which the viscosity of the
material would lose it dependence on shear rate at very low shear (or angular frequency). It was theorized
that addition of fillers or reinforcing fibers could disrupt the Newtonian behavior of the resins at low
frequencies, and a strong nonnewtonian shear thinning behavior could be achieved with the use of
compatibilizers between the fibers/fillers and resin. Figure 2 provides the complex viscosity of various
formulations at relevant processing temperatures (i.e. print temperatures) that demonstrate this type of
behavior. In Figure 2a, the effect of increasing WF concentration on neat PBS at 135°C is provided.
Generally, increasing WF content results in a significant increase in the low frequency complex viscosity
as well as greater shear thinning (indicated by increasing slope). This low frequency shift is what enables
superior shape retention in the material during printing and the complex viscosity of the 40 wt.%WF/PBS
is significantly higher than established print (i.e. 20 wt.%WF/PLA or 20 wt.%CF/ABS) materials at their
respective print temperatures. In Figure 2b, the melt viscosity for the PBS/PLA blend and PBS/WF blends
are provided. Due to the low melting point, the PBS/WF can be printed as a much lower temperature while
the inclusion of higher melting point PLA necessitated a print temperature of at least 200°C. Just like the
PBS composites, addition of WF to the PBS/PLA blend increases viscosity and shear thinning over the
frequency range of interest, however, the PBS/PLA blends have lower viscosity at high shear frequency
comparatively. Lastly, in Figure 2c, the effect of the JON chain extender is shown. A chain extender can
chemically bond different species together during reactive extrusion resulting in potentially higher
molecular weight or surface modified WF that is a more attractive surface to the polymers. As such, an
increase in viscosity is observed when JON is added to the PBS/PLA blend and when it is incorporated
with the WF.

  Viscosity vs frequency for printing materials at their respective print temperature and c) the effect of compatibilizer on melt

1.2.4 Mechanical Characterization

 Each composite sample compounded on a large scale was injection molded for dogbone samples
for tensile testing (ASTM D638 Type I). The mechanical properties of the samples were tested on an
MTS servomechanical frame with a 2500 lb-f load cell. Each sample was pulled in tension at 50 mm/min,
and at least 5 samples of each material formulation were tested. The yield strength, elastic modulus, and
strain at break each material after tensile testing is shown in Figure 3 for the preliminary data set focused
on down-selection of filler type and polymer grade for PBS and in Figure 3 for the polymer blends based
on 25 wt% PLA and 75 wt% PBS.

 Figure 3. Mechanical properties of composites: a) Yield strength b) Elastic Modulus and c) Strain to break.

 The addition of WF to each polymer grade resulted in a slight decrease in its yield strength, and the
addition of compatibilizer (samples labeled “1j” to abbreviation JON) to the WF did not significantly affect
its strength at the low WF concentrations. The addition of BSK to the 92PB4 resin maintained the same
yield strength as exhibited by the neat resin, indicating that BSK is likely a better reinforcement than WF,
most likely due to its high aspect ratio. The lack of change in strength upon addition of compatibilizer to
the WF is also not surprising, as the very low aspect ratio of WF does not allow it to carry a significant
tensile load regardless of any improved stress transfer between the matrix and particles. The addition of
compatibilizer to BSK in a composite, however, could result in an increased yield strength and can be
further studied if desired. Both resins showed similar trends in elastic modulus upon addition of fibers or
fillers, with the modulus of both approximately with addition of 10 wt.% WF. Again, the use of
compatibilizer did not significantly impact the tensile modulus of the WF composites. The WF increased
the modulus of the 92PB4 resin more than the addition of BSK, which could be attributable to the high
aspect ratio and flexibility of the BSK fibers in comparison to the stiff WF particles. Finally, the addition
of fillers and fibers to each resin reduced its strain to break and overall toughness. It should be noted that
the machine used only allowed measurement of strains up to 230%, and the neat samples did not exhibit
fracture during the entirety of their tensile tests. Interestingly, the 92PB4 sample with BSK was still able to
extend to over 200% strain before fracture, while the WF samples exhibited fracture strains around 150%.
Again, this behavior could be attributable to the length and flexibility of the BSK. Each of the 92PB4
composites exhibited necking and attained high fracture strains during tensile testing. However, addition of
WF to the 72PB22 resin resulted in brittle fracture at relatively low strain. The severe embrittlement of the
72PB22 and not 92PB4 upon addition of WF is interesting when considering the similar trends in the tensile
modulus and yield strength. The 92PB4 neat material and composites as well as the 72PB22 neat material
were all observed to yield at around 16% strain, while the 72PB22 composites fractured at around 10%
strain with a reduced yield strength. This indicates that the WF-containing 72PB22 samples were unable to
withstand the stress necessary to reach the yield strain of the resin itself, most likely due to unfavorable
interactions between the 72PB22 and WF. The high strains reached by the 92PB4 composites indicate
92PB4’s interactions with WF and BSK are much more favorable than that of 72PB22.

 9

 The 92PB4 grade was ultimately selected as the resin for the basis of subsequent formulations using
polymer blends and higher quantities of WF. Initial review of the preliminary data confirmed that while 10
wt.% WF was sufficient to improve printability (discussed in section 1.2.5) of the neat resin, the property
improvement was not sufficient to support the demonstration. PLA was chosen to create a polymer blend
as a 100% renewably sourced polymer which is commonly used with organic fillers. It also has higher
tensile strength (e.g. 50-60 MPa) and stiffness (e.g. 2-3 GPa) relative to PBS. Zhao et al. showed a roughly
linear relationship between PBS:PLA composition in polymer blends for stiffness and strength [1]. By
extrapolating the relationship presented By Zhao et al. to lower concentrations of PLA, 25 wt.% PLA in
PBS was predicted to produce at least a 1000 MPa elastic modulus and 50 MPa tensile strength (assuming
a rule of mixtures would hold). Figure 4 shows the results of several compounding experiments based on
this hypothesis. The PLA/PBS blend fell short of prediction, which may be because the PBS used herein
has lower mechanical properties than those used by Zhao et al. While PLA is a stronger polymer with
regards to its mechanical performance and incorporating additional PLA could potentially have improved
the mechanical performance, the project aimed to keep the PLA content as low as possible since it is
industrially biocompostable and not aerobically biodegradable. For that reason, the PLA content was
capped at 25 wt.% and instead, additional WF content was pursued to improve stiffness. Up to 30 wt. %WF
could be compounded into the mixture before the material lost too much melt strength and could not be
compounded. Adding 30 wt.% WF to the PBS/PLA blend produced a significant improvement in tensile
modulus (~1.7 GPa) and adding in 1 wt.% JON, the tensile strength loss, which typically accompanies
wood plastic composites, was recovered. While use of JON in low concentrations of WF did not show
significant improvement, in the polymer blends, it can react to compatibilize all three constituents, including
compatibilizing neighboring PLA and PBS chains which are not miscible with each other. With multiple
chemical bonds available to improve connectivity between the constituents, an improvement in tensile
strength was observed, however still underpredicted at only 20 MPa. However, properties can be partially
compensated through design of the structure to be printed (i.e. thicker walls) and that was ultimately the
route pursued for the demonstration.

1.2.5 Large Scale Printing & Demonstration

 Preliminary large-scale printing of the PBS composite feedstock was performed on the 3DP Work
Center to print multiple resin formulations in two initial test geometries: curl bars and hexagons. To
conserve limited material, short curl bars were produced which did not produce large amplitude distortion
during cooling. Printing of hexagons under the same conditions did show differences in printability.
Generally, the neat polymer resin would flow during deposition of layers and exhibited poor dimension
stability (as indicated by the increase in width of individual sides of the hexagon) (Figure 5A). Addition of
a filler improved the printability of the resin under the same conditions, Figure 5A-B, due to an

 10

improvement in viscosity. Between the two polymer grades, 72PB22 did have a generally lower viscosity
compared to 92PB4 and between two formulations with 10 wt.% WF, the 92PB4 with10 wt.%WF had
slightly between shape retention (Figure 5B-C). Generally, all formulations had excellent tack with the print
bed as well, however, as commented on in section 1.2.3, the mechanical properties of the low concentration
formulations were not sufficient for the application thus the materials were reformulated.

 Figure 5. Examples of hexagon prints for three compositions: a) Neat 72PB b) 72PB-10WF and c) 92PB-10WF.

 Finite element analysis (FEA) was conducted to assess the design-property relationship between the
proposed material for LFAM and the product, precast catch basin tooling (Figure 6). The design was a
roughly 2 ft3 volume cube with 4 walls and insert to create the cavity of the catch basin. This design was
modeled with the experimental properties measured from the highest performing grade identified (25 wt.
%PLA-75 wt.% PBS blend with 30 wt.%WF and 1 wt.% JON). The stresses and displacements calculated
by the FEA analysis are within the acceptable tolerance for this type of product and it was predicted that
the material would not undergo any significant deformation during loading. The maximum stress
experienced in the design is located near the connections (at bolts) joining the walls to the floor of the mold
and that stress was 9.4 MPa. The displacement under this condition is a maximum of 1.5 mm, however, this
is expected to be an overprediction as the actual stress concentration will be distributed over a wider area
due to the inclusion of a washer in the bolt design. Even with this artificial stress concentration in the
analysis, the factor of safety is significant (greater than 2) for the material selected. The overall tolerance
of the precast catch basin made using this tooling is within 1/8” of the intended structure’s dimensions.

 Figure 7 provides an overview of the manufacturing process used to create a demonstration piece and its use in
precast concrete tooling. In brief, the CAD design was 3D-printed in pieces on the Juggerbot Tradesman as individual
pieces in a heated chamber. Instead of machining the tool faces, which would add time and cost to get a surface finish
not required for this application, dry wall compound was used to create smooth surfaces. All components could have
dry wall compound applied in 30 min and then sanded down with a hand sander the next day to eliminate surface
roughness of the 3D-printed tool without significantly changing the internal geometry. Mold release film and spray
was applied to the interior faces prior to casting 785 lbs of concrete into the structure. One of the challenges not
anticipated with this mold design was the difficulty in demolding the insert for the cavity. While the walls demolded
easily, the insert was stuck, most likely due to the shrinkage of the cured concrete over time. Despite the inclusion of
mold release and plastic film, the insert could not be removed and had to be drilled out.

1.2.6 Aerobic Degradation of 3D-printing Materials

 Aerobic degradation studies were conducted on the material developed herein as well as cellulose
(reference/control) and 20 wt.% WF/PLA, which is a common biobased material that many report as biodegradable,
but which is industrially bio-compostable (Figure 8). In brief, aerobic degradation testing was conducted per ISO
17556 standard. Granulated feedstocks are buried in soil in a closed systems chamber and the evolving carbon dioxide
(CO2) due to the decomposition of materials within the closed chamber is monitored over time. Figure 8 shows that
the granulated PBS/PLA formulation was significantly more biodegradable than the incumbent biobased PLA
feedstock, which did not degrade at all. After 90 days the PBS/PLA feedstock had degraded to ~40%, initially
degrading slowly before the rate of degradation increased significantly above 40 days. The cellulose reference also
shows multiple degradation rates, initially degrading quickly before slowing down. This material is highly degradable
and after 90 days biodegradation was ~80% for cellulose control. Both feedstocks are polyesters that are susceptible
to hydrolysis, and both contain WF, a lignocellulosic material which is biodegradable. Hydrolysis rate depends on
time, temperature and then chemical factors like molecular weight so it is unsurprising that the PBS and PLA
feedstocks would exhibit different biodegradation rates. However, the PLA feedstock exhibited no significant
degradation despite the inclusion of WF. This suggests that the WF is not highly available on the materials surface to
act as initiation points for even a small amount of decomposition. After 90 days, both PBS and PLA are still present
in the blend, but the material has broken down significantly. With the inclusion of PBS, it exposes the other
constituents to enzymes and bacteria that will break down the materials, resulting in accelerated decomposition
compared to the homopolymer blend.

1.3 IMPACTS

 The goal of this project is the development of a new product line for BioPBS that will open
opportunities in markets there previously has not been a solution for. In phase 1 of this project, the goal
was to investigate some preliminary formulations to improve printability of the material and demonstrate
its utility in LFAM. Mitsubishi Chemical America and ORNL investigated cellulose-based reinforcements
in two polymers grades for the development of a LFAM feedstock material. A part of the motivation for
doing so was to create a feedstock that would provide alternative disposal opportunities, i.e.
biodegradability, for large scale tooling and other applications. The use of cellulose can provide tangible
environment and economic benefits through low cost; cellulose materials typically have a lower
environmental footprint compared to others (like glass fiber) and these materials can degrade in the natural
environment.

1.5 CONCLUSIONS

 The goal of this project was to create a LFAM feedstock from biobased polyesters and organic,
lignocellulosic fillers that could provide an alternative route for dispose – soil degradation. A key interest
in the study was determining if compatibility of the cellulose fillers with PBS would negatively affect
mechanical properties and it was observed that it would. The approach to improving the mechanical
properties was twofold: 1) the inclusion of a stronger/stiffer polymer like PLA and 2) the compatibilization
of the constituents during processing in the twin screw extruder. In this case, a chain extender, JONCRYL
AD R4468, which has an epoxide group that can react with OH- and carboxyl groups present in the polymer
chains as well as on the filler surface was used to improve chemical compatibility. A summary of the key
findings from the study is provided:

 13

 • For both polymer grades (72PBS and 92PBS), WF composites showed an increase in tensile
 modulus and the best balance in reduction of yield strength and strain at break for the two
 polymer grades and between the two filler types (BSK and WF)

 • WF and BSK formulations both printed well compared to neat polymers (which had poor shape
 retention and started yielding during printing). However, print conditions were not optimized for
 each formulation. WF was ultimately selected as the filler of choice due to the easier processing
 of this material into the compounder.

 • Use of 1wt.% of the chain extender resulted in a 75% improvement in the tensile strength of the
 PBS/PLA blend with 30 wt.% WF compared to the non-compatibilized blend. Practically, this
 meant that the tensile strength of the unfilled blend was recovered and the stiffness increased.

 • 750 lbs concrete catch basin was cast into the precast concrete mold and allowed to cure for 20
 days. The mold survives initial loading but the insert for the catch basin cavity was stuck and had
 to be drilled out.

 • The PBS/PLA blend with 30 wt.%WF was highly degradable compared with the neat
 PLA/20wt.%WF blend used for LFAM. It degraded up to 40% over 90 days.
