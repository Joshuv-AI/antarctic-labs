Variance Deltas for Visualizing and Explaining Posterior Uncertainty
Collin Cademartori

Abstract
In observational settings, where the data generating process and possibly the sample size are not controlled, it is typically impossible to guarantee a priori that quantities of interest will be estimated with sufficient precision. However, even when the data do not determine the quantities of interest, they may still allow determination of what is missing – unobserved information which, if observed, would meaningfully reduce uncertainty. We propose an interactive visualization system, termed variance deltas, to enable the discovery of such missing information from a Bayesian posterior distribution. This system, which we provide as a software package, represents missing information as subsets of unobserved model quantities, organized into a tree based on how well each subset explains uncertainty about the quantity of interest. This system both automates the construction of candidate subsets from minimal user input and implements interactive operations for the division and combination of subsets, allowing the efficient discovery of interesting and useful explanations. We demonstrate this system by using it to discover nonobvious explanations of uncertainty for (1) a treatment effect parameter in a simulated causal inference problem and (2) a population proportion in a forecasting model of real polling data with many sources of bias.



Keywords:
Uncertainty Quantification, Graphical Models, Bayesian Statistics, Sensitivity Analysis

1
Introduction

When modeling observational data where the data generating process is both uncontrolled and complex, statistical uncertainty about quantities of interest may be too great to permit any substantive inference
(
11
,
10
)
. Common causes of this symptom include having insufficient amounts of data, having data of the wrong type, and having a nonidentified nuisance parameter on which a quantity of interest depends. Remedies may include collecting more and/or different data, bringing prior information to bear, or rethinking the substantive goals of the analysis.

Finding the correct explanation for such uncertainty is often necessary to properly mitigate or accommodate it. But discovering such explanations typically requires manual analysis of the model and substantial guesswork. Furthermore, existing summarization and visualization tools often do not contain the information that we need. In Bayesian inference,
9
point out that the usual histogram- and scatter plot-based posterior visualizations “do not fully capture the multiple levels of variation and uncertainty in our inferences.”

We propose an interactive visualization system to structure and partially automate the search for such explanations in the Bayesian setting. The user of this system only needs to specify a small set of initial potential explanations, from which further explanations are automatically generated and organized into a tree structure. The branches of these trees may then be interactively divided, extended, and merged to produce new explanations. In these trees, we think of information as flowing from the root to the leaf explanations, with each successive explanation weaker than the last. Consequently, we term these visualizations variance deltas, in analogy with the tree-like flow of a river delta. We implement this system in a software package,
variance-deltas
, which we use to produce all figures.

1.1
Quantifying Explanations

To visualize uncertainty explanations, we must first measure explanatory power. The literature on explainable and interpretable machine learning has proposed many measures for explaining model predictions
(
13
,
19
)
. The popular SHAP method, for instance, essentially performs a sensitivity analysis of predictions with respect to the predictive features
(
20
,
23
)
. We follow a similar approach for explaining uncertainty.

We begin with a posterior density
p(\psi\mid y)
for a vector of unknowns
\psi\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
given observed data
y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Let
I^{*}=\{1,2,\ldots D\}
. For any
I=\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\}\subseteq I^{*}
, define the vector

\psi_{I}=\left(\psi_{i_{1}},\psi_{i_{2}},\ldots,\psi_{i_{k}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}.

The total index set
I^{*}
is partitioned into two disjoint subsets:

I^{*}=I_{\pi}\cup I_{\widetilde{y}},\quad I_{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{P},\;I_{\widetilde{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M},

where
P+M=D
. The unknowns
\psi_{I_{\pi}}
are the model parameters, and the unknowns
\psi_{I_{\widetilde{y}}}
are unobserved data which could hypothetically be observed. For notational simplicity, we will write
\pi=\psi_{I_{\pi}}
and
\widetilde{y}=\psi_{I_{\widetilde{y}}}
. We also isolate a distinguished parameter index
i_{\theta}\in I_{\pi}
which corresponds to a scalar quantity of interest
\theta
, i.e.
\theta=\psi_{i_{\theta}}
. (If there are multiple scalar quantities of interest, we assume that they are taken one at a time.)

In practice, we often begin with a posterior
p(\pi\mid y)
over just the parameters. When
\dim\left(\widetilde{y}\right)>0
, the probability model must then be extended over this additional data, as in Bayesian models of missing data (e.g. for imputation) or replicated data (e.g. for posterior predictive model checking).
In some cases, this extension may require additional parameters as well, such as observation-level random effects, in which case these are also included in
\pi
.

Potential explanations of our uncertainty about quantity of interest
\theta
are represented by vectors
\psi_{I}
with
I\subseteq I^{*}\setminus\{i_{\theta}\}
.
The degree to which
\psi_{I}
explains uncertainty about
\theta
– the explanatory power of
\psi_{I}
– can be quantified by the expected posterior variance of
\theta
given
\psi_{I}
, as a fraction of the overall posterior variance:

U^{2}_{I}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Var}\left(\theta\mid y,\psi_{I}\right)\mid y\right]}{\mathrm{Var}\left(\theta\mid y\right)}.

(1)

We refer to
U^{2}_{I}
as the uncertainty index of
\psi_{I}
. By the law of total variance,
0\leq U^{2}_{I}\leq 1
, and so
\psi_{I}
is a powerful explanation if
U^{2}_{I}\ll 1
. We can also write the uncertainty index as

U^{2}_{I}=1-\frac{\mathrm{Var}\left[\mathbb{E}\left(\theta\mid y,\psi_{I}\right)\mid y\right]}{\mathrm{Var}\left(\theta\mid y\right)}.

(2)

The rightmost term is a form of the Sobol’ index
(
26
)
, which measures the sensitivity of the conditional mean
\mathbb{E}\left(\theta\mid y,\psi_{I}\right)
to
\psi_{I}
. Thus, in our framework, locating powerful explanations
\psi_{I}
involves analyzing the sensitivity of
\theta
to the components of
\psi
.

Related Work

This work follows a growing literature on Bayesian workflow, which addresses all stages of the statistical modeling pipeline in an integrated manner.
Other applications of static or interactive graphics to problems in Bayesian workflow include the shinystan and bayesplot packages
(
8
,
7
)
– tools for plotting MCMC outputs and diagnostics. Outside the Bayesian setting, graph-based visualizations have been used to reveal model structure and variable importance
(
3
,
14
)
.

Previous work has also applied tools from sensitivity analysis to Bayesian workflow problems. Much of this work studies the sensitivity of inference to non-random aspects of the analysis, such as the prior specification
(
25
,
18
)
and the data sample
(
22
)
. Decision-theoretic applications, on the other hand, analyze sensitivity with respect to random, modeled quantities, as we do here. For instance, the expected value of partial perfect information is defined as the reduction in expected loss when a decision is made with and without conditioning on some unknown quantity
(
5
)
. When the loss is quadratic, this is closely related to our uncertainty index (
1
)
(
16
)
. Whereas such value of information analyses generally assume that we know which quantities we want to condition on in advance, this work is concerned with discovering interesting sets of unknowns to analyze.

The structure of our proposed visualizations is also inspired by John Tukey’s early work on interactive statistical graphics
(
6
)
. In moving beyond direct visualization of posterior samples, we are influenced by Tukey’s suggestion that, to invent useful new forms of visualization, we should “find new phenomena of potential interest, and then learn how to make displays that will be likely to reveal them”
(
28
)
.

2
Variance Deltas

Variance deltas are constructed from two components: the underlying tree which encodes conditional independence relationships in the model and a mapping from tree nodes to estimates of the corresponding uncertainty indices. The formal presentation of variance deltas is divded into four parts:
(i) the definition of the
distributary trees
underlying them, (ii) a method for estimating the uncertainty indices needed to plot them, (iii) an algorithm for semi-automatically constructing them, and (iv) the interactive operations that transform and extend them.

2.1
Distributary Trees

A variance delta is a visualization of a tree rooted at the quantity of interest
\theta
where the non-root nodes represent possible explanations
\psi_{I}
with
I\subseteq I^{*}\setminus\{i_{\theta}\}
. Maintaining our hydrological analogy, we term these distributary trees
1
1

1

In a river delta, a distributary is an outflowing branch of the river which does not rejoin the main stream of the river.
. The paths in a distributary tree are Markov chains (in a sense which we define below), and thus represent a diminishing flow of information from the start to the end node.

Definition 1

(Distributary Trees)
.

Let
T=\left(\mathcal{N},P\right)
be a tree, where

•

\mathcal{N}
is the set of tree nodes. Each node
\nu\in\mathcal{N}
is associated with a subset
I(\nu)\subseteq I^{*}
. To simplify notation, we write
\psi(\nu)
for
\psi_{I(\nu)}
, the unknowns associated to
\nu
.

•

P:\mathcal{N}\setminus\{\nu_{0}\}\to\mathcal{N}
maps each non-root node to its unique parent, where
\nu_{0}
is the root. The undirected graph with vertices
\mathcal{N}
and edges
\{\{P({\nu}),\nu\}\}_{\nu\in\mathcal{N}\setminus\{\nu_{0}\}}
must be acyclic and connected, so that
P
yields a well-defined tree.

Then
T
is a distributary tree for quantity of interest
\theta
if it satisfies the following constraints.

1.

(
Rooted at
\boldsymbol{\theta}
) We must have
I(\nu_{0})=\{i_{\theta}\}
.

2.

(
Markov Paths
) A path is a sequence
(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{k})
with
\nu_{i}=P(\nu_{i+1})
for
1\leq i\leq k-1
. Every path with
k\geq 2
must be a Markov chain in the sense that

\psi(\nu_{\leq i})\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\!\>\perp}\psi(\nu_{>i})\mid\psi(\nu_{i})\quad\text{for all}\quad 1\leq i\leq k-1,

(5)

where
\psi(\nu_{\leq i})=\psi_{I_{\leq i}}
with
I_{\leq i}=\cup_{j=1}^{i}I(\nu_{j})
and
\psi_{j>i}=\psi_{I_{>i}}
with
I_{>i}=\cup_{j={i+1}}^{k}I(\nu_{j})
.

3.

(
Unique Children
) For any
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
, if
P(\nu)=P(\nu^{\prime})
, then
I(\nu)\neq I(\nu^{\prime})
.

As we will see momentarily, constraints 1 and 2 above are the basic properties that make distributary trees useful. Constraint 3 implies that distinct rooted paths in
T
correspond to distinct Markov chains. Note also that, subject to constraint 3,
I(\cdot):\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{P}(I^{*})
need not be injective, so the same model quantities
\psi_{I}
can occur at different nodes of the same distributary tree.

2.2
Visualizing and Estimating Uncertainty

When we plot distributary trees in a variance delta, the horizontal position of each node
\nu
is determined by an estimate of its root uncertainty index
U_{I(\nu)}
, as in Figure
1
. It follows from Definition
1
that
U_{I(\nu)}
decreases from child to parent nodes.

Lemma 1
.

Let
T
be a distributary tree . Then
U^{2}_{I(\nu)}
decreases from child to parent nodes in
T
, and
U^{2}_{I(\nu_{0})}=0
, where
\nu_{0}
is the root of
T
.

Proof.

It follows directly from the definition (
1
) that
U^{2}_{I(\nu_{0})}=U^{2}_{\{i_{\theta}\}}=0
. Next, suppose
\nu=P(\nu^{\prime})
. Then by passing successively from child to parent nodes, we can construct a path beginning at
\nu_{0}
and terminating at
\nu^{\prime}
which contains
\nu
. Constraint 2 then implies that
\theta\to\psi(\nu)\to\psi(\nu^{\prime})
is a Markov chain, and the law of total variance gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Var}\left(\theta\mid\psi(\nu^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Var}\left(\theta\mid\psi(\nu),\psi(\nu^{\prime})\right)+\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Var}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid\psi(\nu),\psi(\nu^{\prime})\right]\mid\psi(\nu^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Var}\left(\theta\mid\psi(\nu),\psi(\nu^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Var}\left(\theta\mid\psi(\nu)\right),

where the final equality follows by the conditional independence of
\psi(\nu^{\prime})
and
\theta
given
\psi(\nu)
. Plugging the first and last terms above into (
1
) immediately yields
U^{2}_{I(\nu^{\prime})}\geq U^{2}_{I(\nu)}
.
∎

When working with approximate posterior samples
\mathcal{S}=\{\psi^{(s)}\}_{s=1}^{S}
(as produced by any MCMC algorithm), we estimate the denominator of (
1
) by the sample variance
\mathrm{var}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)=\frac{1}{S-1}\sum_{s=1}^{S}(\theta^{(s)}-\bar{\theta})^{2}
. To estimate the numerator of (
1
), we use a data splitting strategy. Assuming
S
is even, we use the first
S/2
samples
\mathcal{S}_{E}
to construct an estimator
\hat{E}_{I}\left(\mathcal{S}_{E},\cdot\right)

of the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid\psi_{I}=\cdot,y\right]
. Then, using the last
S/2
samples
\mathcal{S}_{V}
, we estimate the expected conditional variance
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Var}\left(\theta\mid y,\psi_{I}\right)\right]
as

\mathrm{cvar}_{I,\hat{E}}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)=\frac{2}{S}\sum_{\psi^{(s)}\in\mathcal{S}_{V}}\left(\theta^{(s)}-\hat{E}_{I}\left(\mathcal{S}_{E},\psi^{(s)}_{I}\right)\right)^{2}.

(6)

With these, our estimate of the uncertainty index (
1
) is then just

\hat{U}^{2}_{I}=\frac{\mathrm{cvar}_{I,\hat{E}}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)}{\mathrm{var}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)}.

(7)

Splitting the data in this way eliminates overfitting bias in the conditional variance estimate (
6
). In particular, if the expected mean squared error (MSE) of
\hat{E}
vanishes asymptotically, then (
6
) is asymptotically unbiased for the conditional variance (proof in supplementary materials). In most situations, an
\hat{E}
with vanishing MSE will not be available, and (
6
) will be biased upward by the expected MSE. However, we find in our experiments that, as long as
S
is large enough that the variance of
\hat{E}
small, the performance of (
7
) is sufficient.

The construction (
7
) still requires us to choose an estimator
\hat{E}
. Throughout our examples, we take

\hat{E}_{I}\left(\mathcal{S}_{E},\cdot\right)
to be a random forest regressor, chosen primarily for its ability to handle nonlinearities and interactions without explicit modeling. However, we emphasize that (
7
) can be used with any choice of
\hat{E}
.

2.3
Constructing Distributary Trees

Constructing useful distributary trees faces two difficulties, which we address in this section.

1.

Constructing distributary trees manually requires the user to know both (i) which unknowns
\psi_{I}
should be selected for the nodes of the tree and (ii) how to connect the nodes so that the Markov condition (
5
) is satisfied for every path in the tree. We resolve this problem by defining an algorithm to construct distributary trees from a specification of just the root node (the quantity of interest) and the leaf nodes.

2.

For any given model, the root and leaf nodes alone usually do not determine a unique valid distributary tree. This is an instance of a more general problem that arises in sensitivity analysis: the need to constrain the subsets of quantities with respect to which the analysis will be performed
(
4
,
17
,
15
)
. Therefore, we need an additional criterion that allows us to choose between multiple options for constructing the tree at any given stage of the algorithm. For this, we introduce a measure of explanatory specificity.

To resolve these difficulties, we need the concepts of factorizations and factor graphs.

Definition 2

(Factorization)
.

Let
h(\psi)
be a nonnegative function. Then
h
factorizes over
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(I^{*})
(equivalently,
\mathcal{F}
is a factorization of
h
), if, for functions
f_{I}:\mathbb{R}^{|I|}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

h(\psi)=\prod_{I\in\mathcal{F}}f_{I}(\psi_{I}).

(8)

Suppose
h(\psi)=p(\psi\mid y)
, the posterior density for a joint model
p(\psi,y)
, and that
p
factorizes over
\mathcal{F}
. Then
\mathcal{F}
is a Bayesian factorization if
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{L}\cup\mathcal{F}_{P}
with
\mathcal{F}_{L}\cap\mathcal{F}_{P}=\emptyset
, and where:

1.

The prior
p(\pi)
factorizes over
\mathcal{F}_{P}
.

2.

The likelihood (for observed and hypothetical data)
p(y,\widetilde{y}\mid\pi)
factorizes over
\mathcal{F}_{L}
.

A factorization
\mathcal{F}
can be conveniently represented using an undirected graph.

Definition 3

(Undirected Graph)
.

An undirected graph
M
is a pair
(V_{M},E_{M})
, where
V_{M}
is a set of vertices and
E_{M}
is a set of undirected edges. Specifically:

E_{M}\subseteq\left\{\{x,y\}\;|\;x,y\in V_{M},x\neq y\right\}.

An undirected graph is bipartite if the vertices can be partitioned as
V_{M}=V_{1}\cup V_{2}
, where
V_{1}\cap V_{1}=\emptyset
, and

E_{M}\subseteq\left\{\{x,y\}\;|\;x\in V_{1},y\in V_{2}\right\},

i.e. all edges connect a vertex in
V_{1}
with a vertex in
V_{2}
.

Definition 4

(Factor Graph)
.

The factor graph
G_{\mathcal{F}}
corresponding to a factorization
\mathcal{F}
is defined as

V_{G_{\mathcal{F}}}=I^{*}\cup\mathcal{F},\quad E_{G_{\mathcal{F}}}=\left\{\left\{i,I\right\}\;\Big|\;I\in\mathcal{F},i\in I\right\}.

In words,
G_{\mathcal{F}}
is a bipartite graph with a vertex for every factor
I\in\mathcal{F}
and index
i\in I^{*}
, with an edge between
i
and
I
if and only if
i\in I
.

2.3.1
Measuring the Specificity of an Explanation

If we want to minimize
U^{2}_{I}
, the best we can do is to observe all unknown quantities other than
\theta
. In other words, we have

I^{*}\setminus\{i_{\theta}\}\in\underset{I\subseteq I^{*}\setminus\{i_{\theta}\}}{\mathrm{arg}\min}\;U^{2}_{I}.

(9)

Despite its maximal explanatory power,
\psi_{I^{*}\setminus\{\theta\}}
cannot tell us which quantities
specifically
explain our uncertainty about
\theta
and is thus not a useful explanation. To generate trees containing potentially useful explanations, we need to exclude explanations that are overly non-specific. This also provides a useful criterion to resolve the second difficulty above: When faced with multiple choices for the next node in a tree, we can always choose the node
\nu
with more specific explanation
\psi(\nu)
. We quantify specificity in terms of the distance between the unknowns
\psi(\nu)
and likelihood factors in the posterior factor graph.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a Bayesian factorization of the posterior density
p(\psi\mid y)
with
G_{\mathcal{F}}
the corresponding factor graph.
We start with a few desiderata:

1.

If
I^{\prime}\subseteq I
, then
\psi_{I^{\prime}}
must be at least as specific as
\psi_{I}
.

2.

An explanation
\psi_{I}
is maximally specific if the quantities in
\psi_{I}
are involved in exactly one likelihood factor, i.e. if there is exactly one
I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{L}
with
I\subseteq I^{\prime}
. These explanations point to a particular quantity of (hypothetically) observable information which is insufficient or missing.

3.

Conversely,
\psi_{I}
is minimally specific if
\psi_{I}
is equally involved in modeling every data point, as with a global mean or scale parameter. Such explanations do not suggest particular remedies, since
any
additional data could be relevant to learning
\psi_{I}
.

Our specificity measure interpolates these extremes by measuring the distance between explanations
\psi_{I}
and likelihood factors in
G_{\mathcal{F}}
.
A path in
G_{\mathcal{F}}
is any sequence of (distinct) vertices
\overline{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k})
where
(v_{j},v_{j+1})
are joined by an edge for all
1\leq j\leq k-1
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{k}(G_{\mathcal{F}})
be the set of all paths in
G_{\mathcal{F}}
of length
k
. For each
i\in I^{*}
, the depth
d_{\mathcal{F}}(i)
of
i
, is defined as the length of the shortest path from
i
to a likelihood factor. Symbolically:

d(i)=\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{L}}d(i,f),\text{ where }d(i,f)=\min\left\{k\;\Big|\;\exists\overline{v}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(G_{\mathcal{F}})\;s.t.\;\overline{v}_{1}=i,\overline{v}_{k}=f\right\}.

(10)

Similarly, the set of most relevant likelihood factors for
i
is defined as

\mathcal{F}_{L}(i)=\left\{f\in\mathcal{F}_{L}\mid d(i,f)=d(i)\right\}.

(11)

Finally, for any index set
I\subseteq I^{*}
, the likelihood specificity is defined as

S_{\mathrm{lik}}(I)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}_{L}|}\left|\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{F}_{L}(i)\right|,

(12)

In words, sets
I
with low
S_{\mathrm{lik}}(I)
index unknowns
\psi_{I}
which are most relevant to a relatively small set of likelihood factors. Furthermore, it is easy to see that likelihood specificity satisfies the above three desiderata. Note in particular that
S_{\mathrm{lik}}(I)
is minimized when
\psi_{I}
occurs in exactly one likelihood factor, and is maximized when some
\psi_{i}
with
i\in I
is a global quantity, in the sense that
d(f,i)=d(i)
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}_{L}
.

2.3.2
Generating Distributary Trees From Leaves

Given our measure of specificity
S_{\mathrm{lik}}
, we may now define a procedure for generating distributary trees. To do this, we need to leverage information about the dependencies between model quantities, which can be derived from the factor graph
G_{\mathcal{F}}
. Specifically, for a factor graph
G_{\mathcal{F}}

M_{\mathcal{F}}
with vertices
V_{M_{\mathcal{F}}}=I^{*}
and edges

E_{M_{\mathcal{F}}}=\left\{\{i_{1},i_{2}\}\;\Big|\;\exists I\in\mathcal{F}\text{ s.t. }i_{1},i_{2}\in I\right\}.

In other words, we connect two indices in
M_{\mathcal{F}}
if they belong to a common factor in
\mathcal{F}

G_{\mathcal{F}}
is connected, which implies also that the Markov graph
M_{\mathcal{F}}
is connected. If

A subset

2.4
Operations on Distributary Trees

Given an initial distributary tree , we may want to add nodes to find the better explanatory sets. To this end, we develop a suite of operations which modify distributary trees.

Branching from a node.

Given a node
\nu
in
T

Definition 6

(15)

where
\nu^{\prime}

3
Example: Causal Inference in Simulated Panel Data

Our goal in this example is inference for a treatment effect parameter in a simulated causal inference problem based on panel data. We simulate data
y_{it}
for
1\leq i\leq N=6
units over
1\leq t\leq T=10
time points, with unit 1 exposed to a treatment after time
t_{\delta}=6
. Figure
3
plots the simulated data, with vertical lines showing the treatment time for unit 1.

Figure 3
:
The simulated panel data. Units 1-3 are all identical in the pre-treatment period, but divergent in the post-treatment period. Only unit 1 is treated.

Estimating treatment effects relies on inferring correlations between units in the pre-treatment period which can be extrapolated into the post-treatment period. In particular, we rely on the post-treatment behavior of the untreated units to learn how the treated unit was likely to behave in the absence of treatment. This strategy and the corresponding inference for the treatment effect can be undermined in multiple ways.

A lack of relevant data is the most straightforward threat to inference. For instance, in Figure
3
, units 4 – 6 are a poor match to unit 1 in the pre-treatment period, and thus provide little reliable information about unit 1 in the post-treatment period.
On the other hand, units 2 and 3 closely match unit 1 in the pre-treatment period, but create a different threat to inference: Despite matching unit 1 equally well before treatment, units 2 and 3 diverge and give incompatible predictions in the post-treatment period. In this case, we have too much potentially relevant information to infer the treatment effect with confidence.

3.1
Parameters and Model

We fit the data in Figure
3
using a latent factor model augmented with a treatment effect parameter. Our model closely resembles Bayesian approaches to synthetic control, a causal inference methodology for observational panel data
(
1

24
)
. Specifically, the matrix of observations

(18)

4
Polling Model Example

Our second example applies variance deltas to characterize uncertainty in a complex model of polls from the 2016 U.S. presidential election. In particular, we analyze the model of
12
, which includes components for temporal trends, interstate correlation, and several sources of potential bias/measurement error. We use variance deltas to isolate two model components which together explain most of the posterior uncertainty and which allow us to determine the extent to which this uncertainty could be reduced.

The data are a subset of polls from the 2016 U.S. presidential election at the state and national level. Each poll reports the number of respondents and the number supporting the Democratic candidate, Hilary Clinton. Our goal is to forecast the proportion of voters who will support the Democratic candidate on election day in each state.

4.1

Let
1\leq t\leq T

(19)

m^{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{51}
and
\mathbf{S}^{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{51\times 51}
are hyperparameters governing the prior on the election day outcome.

Results of state and national polls are given a binomial model combining
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{b}
with terms for various types of polling bias. Letting
i=1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{state}}
index state polls and
y_{i}
denote the number of respondents supporting the Democratic candidate out of
n_{i}
respondents in poll
i
, we have

y_{i}\sim\mathrm{binomial}\left(\mathrm{logit}^{-1}\left(\mu^{b}_{s_{i},t_{i}}+\beta_{i}\right),n_{i}\right),

(20)

where
s_{i}
,
t_{i}
denote the state and day for poll
i
, and
\beta_{i}
models various sources of bias. National polls are modeled similarly, except that state-level terms including
\mu_{st}
are averaged with weights accounting for each state’s share of the national vote in the previous election.

The bias terms
\beta_{i}
in (
20
), also modeled on the logistic scale, are decomposed into several further terms which are designed to capture the following sources of polling bias:

•

A pollster-specific “house” effect
\mu^{c}
, modeling the nonzero, temporally stable bias that each pollster exhibits toward one party or the other.

•

A poll mode effect
\mu^{m}
, reflecting the phenomenon that the averages of phone- and internet-based polls tend to differ.

•

A population effect
\mu^{r}
, modeling differences between registered and likely voter polls.

•

A time-varying partisan nonresponse effect
\epsilon_{t}
, modeling differential rates of response between members of the two parties. The
\epsilon_{t}
are assigned an AR(1) prior.

•

State-level measurement error
\xi
accounting for aggregate differences in the accuracy of polls in different states. This is assigned a multivariate normal prior with covariance determined by state correlations in previous elections and demographics.

•

Poll-level measurement error
\zeta
, a catch-all term representing additional variance in the polls’ sampling frames unaccounted for by the above terms. These terms are assigned different prior scales at the state and national levels.

The complete model specification is given in
12
. Code and 2016 polling data for the model can be accessed at
https://github.com/TheEconomist/us-potus-model
.
We fit this model to available 2016 data using Stan
(
27
)
.

Uncertain inference for the election-day Democratic support parameter
\mu^{*}_{s}=\mu_{sT}
is particularly troublesome in states
s
with
\mathbb{E}\left[\mu^{*}_{s}\mid y\right]\approx 0.5
, i.e. those believed to be swing states. As an example, we analyze the state of Wisconsin (indexed by
s^{*}=49
), taking
\mu^{*}_{s^{*}}
as our quantity of interest. Transforming to the proportion scale, the
95\%
credible interval for
\mathrm{logit}^{-1}\left(\mu^{*}_{s^{*}}\right)
is
[.478,0.564]
, indicating failure to forecast the winner of Wisconsin. A summary of analyses for other swing states is given in the supplementary material.

5
Conclusion

We introduce variance deltas, an interactive visualization method for explaining posterior uncertainty about scalar quantities of interest in Bayesian models, and implement this system in a software package
variance-deltas
. When the Bayesian model has a factorization
\mathcal{F}
, we show that the corresponding Markov graph
M_{\mathcal{F}}
can be utilized to semi-automatically construct and transform variance deltas, enabling the efficient generation and exploration of potential explanations.

Our two examples demonstrate that variance deltas are capable of rapidly eliminating poor explanations of uncertainty and focusing our attention on specific, interpretable subsets of model quantities which can explain a large fraction of our overall uncertainty. In the simulated panel data example, we are able to identify small combinations of units which would be useful to observe over a longer period, while determining that other units are of vanishing small value. In the polling model example, we are able to isolate a pair of parameters that explain most of our uncertainty, directing us towards particular types of information which are needed to remove bottlenecks from our analysis.

The usefulness and general applicability of variance deltas is limited, however, by the structure of the graph
M_{\mathcal{F}}
. In particular, our ability to discover useful and specific explanations depends on
M_{\mathcal{F}}
being not too dense. When
M_{\mathcal{F}}
is
almost
sparse – in that a few vertices have many neighbors – we can recover a sparse graph by conditioning on these vertices at every step. (Details of this adjustment are given in the supplementary materials.) But when the graph
M_{\mathcal{F}}
is very far from sparse, as can occur for instance in large regressions, it may be impossible to construct useful variance deltas, or the explanatory sets
I
may be too large to reasonably estimate the uncertainty index
U^{2}_{I}
.

6.2
Separating Set Construction

First, we review some basic terminology from graph theory and establish notation. An undirected graph
M=(V_{M},E_{M})
consists of set of vertices
V_{M}
and edges

E_{M}\subseteq\left\{\{i,j\}\mid i,j\in V_{M}\right\}.

(21)

A path in
M
is any sequence of vertices
(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{m})\in V_{M}^{m}
such that
\{i_{k},i_{k+1}\}\in E_{M}
for all
1\leq k\leq m-1
and any
m\geq 2
. For any set of vertices
F\subseteq V_{M}
, we define the following:

1.

N_{M}(F)
is the set of neighbors of
F
in the graph
M
, given as

N_{M}(F)=\left\{j\in V_{M}\setminus F\;\Big|\;\{i,j\}\in E_{M}\text{ for some }i\in F\right\}

(22)

If
i\in V_{M}
is a single vertex, then we will write
N_{M}(i)
for
N_{M}(\{i\})
.

2.

C_{M}(F)
is the connected component of
F
in
M
, i.e. the set of all
j\in V_{M}
reachable by a path starting at some
i\in F
. This may be given explicitly as

C_{M}(F)=\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}N^{k}_{M}(F),

(23)

where
N^{k}_{M}(F)=\underbrace{N_{M}\circ N_{M}\circ\cdots\circ N_{M}}_{k\text{ times}}(F)
for
k\geq 1
, and
N^{0}_{M}(F)=F
.

3.

M-F
is the graph with vertex set
V_{M-F}=V_{M}\setminus F
and edge set

E_{M-F}=\left\{\{i,j\}\in E_{M}\;\Big|\;i,j\in V_{M}\setminus F\right\}.

(24)

Recall that a density
p(x)
which factorizes over
\mathcal{F}
has the useful property that conditional independence relationships between components of
x
can be described by separating sets.

Lemma 3
.

Let
p(x)
with
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
factorize over
\mathcal{F}
, and let
M_{\mathcal{F}}
be the corresponding Markov graph. Let
F_{1}
,
F_{2}
, and
S
be subsets of
\{1,\ldots,D\}
. Suppose that every path from a vertex
i\in F_{1}
to a vertex
j\in F_{2}
includes a vertex in
S
. Then we have that

x_{F_{1}}\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\!\>\perp}x_{F_{2}}\mid x_{S}.

(25)

In this case, the set
S
is called a
separating set
for
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. If there exists any separating set for
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
, then these sets are said to be
separable
.

A separating set
S
for vertex sets
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
is called a minimal separating set if no proper subset of
S
is a separating set. For separable
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
, there may be many minimal separating sets. The following lemma gives one method to find a minimal separator.

Lemma 4
.

Let
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
be separable vertex sets in a connected, undirected graph
M
. Then the following are minimal separators of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
:

S_{M}(F_{1};F_{2})=N_{M}(G_{1})\cap N_{M}(F_{1}),\quad S_{M}(F_{2};F_{1})=N_{M}(G_{2})\cap N_{M}(F_{2}),

(26)

where
G_{1}=C_{M-N_{M}(F_{1})}(F_{2})
, and
G_{2}=C_{M-N_{M}(F_{2})}(F_{1})
.

If
M
is connected, then Lemma
4
implies a simple algorithm for computing
S_{M}(F_{1};F_{2})
and
S_{M}(F_{2};F_{1})
. To calculate
G_{1}
, for instance, we can perform a depth-first search starting at any node in
F_{2}
, collecting all discovered vertices while ignoring all edges with a vertex in
N_{M}(F_{1})
. Then calculating
S_{M}(F_{1};F_{2})
from
G_{1}
reduces to trivial set operations, and
S_{M}(F_{2};F_{1})
may be computed similarly.

Proof.

We prove that
S_{M}(F_{1};F_{2})
is a minimal separator of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. The proof that
S_{M}(F_{2};F_{1})
is a minimal separator is entirely symmetric.

We begin by showing that
S_{M}(F_{1};F_{2})
is a separator for
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. Let
p
be any path of length
m
from a vertex in
F_{1}
to a vertex in
F_{2}
.
First, we select the last vertex in
p
which lies in
N_{M}(F_{1})
Specifically, define

\begin{split}I_{p}&=\left\{1\leq k\leq m\;\Big|\;p_{k}\in N_{M}(F_{1})\right\},\\
i_{*}&=\max I_{p}.\end{split}

(27)

We now claim that
I_{p}\neq\emptyset
and
i_{*}<m
. To show
I_{p}\neq\emptyset
, we also define
J_{p}=\{1\leq k\leq m\mid p_{k}\notin F_{1}\}
and
j_{*}=\min J_{p}
. Clearly
J_{p}\neq\emptyset
since
p_{m}\in F_{2}
by definition, and
F_{1}\cap F_{2}=\emptyset
since
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are assumed separable. Furthermore,
j_{*}>1
since
p_{1}\in F_{1}
by definition. Thus, we must have
p_{j_{*}-1}\in F_{1}
. Since
\{p_{j_{*}-1},P_{j_{*}}\}\in E_{M}
, it follows that
p_{j_{*}}\in N_{M}(F_{1})
, and thus
j_{*}\in I_{p}
, so
I_{p}\neq\emptyset
.

Furthermore, if
m\in Ip
, then there would exist some
i^{\prime}\in F_{1}
with
(i^{\prime},p_{m})\in E_{M}
. But then
(i^{\prime},p_{m})
would be a path from
F_{1}
to
F_{2}
, contradicting the assumption that
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are separable. Therefore,
i^{*}<m
, as claimed.

Now consider the truncated path
p^{\prime}=(p_{i_{*}+1},\ldots,p_{m})
. Note that
p_{m}\in F_{2}
, and that
p^{\prime}_{k}\notin N_{M}(F_{1})
for all
1\leq k\leq|p^{\prime}|
. If this were not true, there would exist a
k_{*}>i_{*}
such that
p_{k_{*}}\in N_{M}(F_{1})
, which contradicts the definition of
i_{*}
. In other words,
p^{\prime}_{1}
is connected to
F_{2}
by a path which does not pass through
N_{M}(F_{1})
, and thus
p^{\prime}_{1}\in C_{M-N_{M}(F_{1})}(F_{2})
. Since
p^{\prime}_{1}=p_{i_{*}+1}
, this implies that
p_{i_{*}}\in N_{M}(C_{M-N_{M}(F_{1})}(F_{2}))
.

Putting these pieces together, we conclude that

p_{i_{*}}\in N_{M}(F_{1})\cap N_{M}(C_{M-N_{M}(F_{1})}(F_{2}))=N_{M}(F_{1})\cap N_{M}(G_{1}),

(28)

and therefore, since
p
was an arbitrary path from
F_{1}
to
F_{2}
,
N_{M}(F_{1})\cap N_{M}(G_{1})
must separate
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
.

To show that this set is also a minimal separator, let
H\subset N_{M}(F_{1})\cap N_{M}(G_{1})
. Then let
j^{\prime}\in(N_{M}(F_{1})\cap N_{M}(G_{1}))\setminus H
. Because
j^{\prime}\in N_{M}(F_{1})
, there exists
i^{\prime}\in F_{1}
such that
\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}\in E_{M}
. Note also that
i^{\prime}\notin H
since
i^{\prime}\notin N_{M}(F_{1})
. Furthermore, since
j^{\prime}\in N_{M}(C_{M-N_{M}(F_{1})}(F_{2}))
, there exists a path
p^{\prime}
(of length
m^{\prime}
) such that
\{j^{\prime},p^{\prime}_{1}\}\in E_{M}
,
p^{\prime}_{m}\in F_{2}
, and
p^{\prime}_{k}\notin N_{M}(F_{1})
for all
1\leq k\leq m^{\prime}
. The latter property implies in particular that
p^{\prime}_{k}\notin H
for all
1\leq k\leq m
. Putting this all together,
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime},p^{\prime}_{1},\ldots p^{\prime}_{m})
is a path from
F_{1}
to
F_{2}
which does not include any vertex in
H
, showing that
H
is not a separator for
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. Since
H
was an arbitrary subset of
N_{M}(F_{1})\cap N_{M}(G_{1})
, we conclude that this set is in fact a minimal separator of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
, as claimed.
∎

Finally, the separating set function
S^{*}_{M}(F_{1};F_{2})
used in Algorithm 2 is produced from
S_{M}(F_{1};F_{2})
as

S^{*}_{M}(F_{1};F_{2})=\begin{cases}S_{M}(F_{1};F_{2}),&F_{1},F_{2}\text{ separable},\\
\emptyset,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and similarly for
S^{*}_{M}(F_{2};F_{1})
.

Definition 9

6.6.2
Asymptotic Unbiasedness of Conditional Variance Estimator

In this section, all expectations, variances, and distributions are implicitly conditional on
y
. We suppress the explicit dependence on
y
for notational clarity. Recall that for
I\subseteq I^{*}
and
\psi^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, we use
\hat{E}_{I}\left(\mathcal{S},\psi^{\prime}_{I}\right)
to denote an estimator of the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid\psi_{I}=\psi_{I}^{\prime}\right]
based on a sample
\mathcal{S}=\{\psi^{(s)}\}_{s=1}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
, where
\theta=\psi_{i_{\theta}}
. Given such an estimator, we then construct the data-splitting expected conditional variance estimator
\mathrm{cvar}_{I,\hat{E}}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)
as follows. First, we partition the sample as
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{E}\cup\mathcal{S}_{V}
, where
|\mathcal{S}_{E}|=|\mathcal{S}_{V}|
and
\mathcal{S}_{E}\cap\mathcal{S}_{V}=\emptyset
(assuming
|\mathcal{S}|
even). Then our estimator is defined as

\mathrm{cvar}_{I,\hat{E}}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)=\frac{2}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{\psi^{(s)}\in\mathcal{S}_{V}}\left(\theta^{(s)}-\hat{E}_{I}\left(\mathcal{S}_{E},\psi^{(s)}_{I}\right)\right)^{2}.

(33)

We now establish a sufficient condition for the estimator (
33
) to be asymptotically unbiased.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be an i.i.d. sample from
p(\psi)
. Suppose that, for
I\subseteq I^{*}
, the average mean squared error (MSE) of our conditional expectation estimator
\hat{E}_{I}\left(\mathcal{S}_{E},\psi_{I}\right)
vanishes in the sense that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\hat{E}_{I}\left(\mathcal{S}_{E},\psi_{I}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid\psi_{I}\right]\right)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\hat{E}_{I}\left(\mathcal{S}_{E},\psi_{I}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid\psi_{I}\right]\right)^{2}\;\Big|\;\psi_{I}\right]\right]\to 0\text{ as }|\mathcal{S}|\to\infty,

where the inner expectation above is the point-wise MSE of
\hat{E}_{I}\left(\mathcal{S}_{E},\psi_{I}\right)
and the outer expectation averages over inputs
\psi
, with
\psi\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\!\>\perp}\mathcal{S}
.
Then the data-splitting expected variance estimator (
33
) is asymptotically unbiased for the true expected variance
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Var}\left(\theta\mid\psi_{I}\right)\right]
.

Proof.

We take
I\subseteq I^{*}
as fixed. We start by expanding each term in the conditional variance estimator:

\displaystyle\left(\theta^{(s)}-\hat{E}_{I}\left(\mathcal{S}_{E},\psi^{(s)}_{I}\right)\right)^{2}=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left(\theta^{(s)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid\psi_{I}=\psi_{I}^{(s)}\right]\right)^{2}}_{A}+\underbrace{\left(\hat{E}_{I}\left(\mathcal{S}_{E},\psi^{(s)}_{I}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid\psi_{I}=\psi_{I}^{(s)}\right]\right)^{2}}_{B}

\displaystyle\hskip 21.68121pt-2\underbrace{\left(\theta^{(s)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid\psi_{I}=\psi_{I}^{(s)}\right]\right)\left(\hat{E}_{I}\left(\mathcal{S}_{E},\psi^{(s)}_{I}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid\psi_{I}=\psi_{I}^{(s)}\right]\right)}_{C}.

The expected value of term
A
is exactly the estimand. Specifically:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[A\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[A\mid\psi_{I}=\psi_{I}^{(s)}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\theta^{(s)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid\psi_{I}=\psi_{I}^{(s)}\right]\right)^{2}\;\Big|\;\psi_{I}=\psi_{I}^{(s)}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Var}\left(\theta\mid\psi_{I}\right)\right].

Next, we observe that term
C
vanishes in expectation. Specifically, since
\mathcal{S}_{E}
is independent of
\psi^{(s)}\in\mathcal{S}_{V}
, it follows that

\left(\theta^{(s)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid\psi_{I}=\psi_{I}^{(s)}\right]\right)\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\!\!\>\perp}\left(\hat{E}_{I}\left(\mathcal{S}_{E},\psi^{(s)}_{I}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid\psi_{I}=\psi_{I}^{(s)}\right]\right)\;\Big|\;\psi_{I}^{(s)}.

Therefore, iterating expectations and applying the above conditional independence, we can write the expected value of
C
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\theta^{(s)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid\psi_{I}=\psi_{I}^{(s)}\right]\right)\left(\hat{E}_{I}\left(\mathcal{S}_{E},\psi^{(s)}_{I}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid\psi_{I}=\psi_{I}^{(s)}\right]\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\theta^{(s)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid\psi_{I}=\psi_{I}^{(s)}\right]\right)\;\Big|\;\psi_{I}=\psi_{I}^{(s)}\right]}_{C^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\hat{E}_{I}\left(\mathcal{S}_{E},\psi^{(s)}_{I}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid\psi_{I}=\psi_{I}^{(s)}\right]\right)\;\Big|\;\psi_{I}=\psi_{I}^{(s)}\right]\right].

Now the factor
C^{\prime}
is just

C^{\prime}=\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid\psi_{I}=\psi_{I}^{(s)}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid\psi_{I}=\psi_{I}^{(s)}\right]=0,

so in fact
\mathbb{E}\left[C\right]=0
.

Finally,
\mathbb{E}\left[B\right]
is exactly the average mean squared error of the estimator
\hat{E}_{I}\left(\mathcal{S}_{E},\psi^{(s)}_{I}\right)
over inputs
\psi^{(s)}
. If this average MSE vanishes as
|\mathcal{S}|\to\infty
, then the above shows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{cvar}_{I,\hat{E}}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{\psi^{(s)}\in\mathcal{S}_{V}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\theta^{(s)}-\hat{E}_{I}\left(\mathcal{S}_{E},\psi^{(s)}_{I}\right)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Var}\left(\theta\mid\psi_{I}\right)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[B\right]

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Var}\left(\theta\mid\psi_{I}\right)\right],

as claimed.
∎

In practice, most practical estimators
\hat{E}
will
not
have asymptotically vanishing average MSE, and Lemma
9
will not apply exactly. Instead, any residual bias in the estimator
\hat{E}
will bias our estimate of the expected variance
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Var}(\theta\mid\psi_{I})\right]
, and of the uncertainty index, upwards. In this sense, the estimator (
33
) is conservative: It may yield “false negatives” by underestimating the uncertainty reduction associated with a set
\psi_{I}
, but it is unlikely to yield “false positives”.

6.7
Causal Latent Factor Model Details

This section provides all details of the model specification for the latent factor model used in the example of Section 3, as well as a description of how the posterior predictive data were generated for a new unit and at new time points.

6.7.1
Model Specification

The latent factor model in Section
3
is a model of panel data
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times T}
. Recall that the factor model decomposes this data as

\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}\boldsymbol{\phi}+\boldsymbol{\overline{\delta}}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon},

(34)

where
\boldsymbol{\phi}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
are the latent factors and corresponding loadings,
\boldsymbol{\overline{\delta}}
is the matrix of treatment effects (zero for all untreated units), and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
the matrix of independent, idiosyncratic normal errors. We now give detailed structural and distributional assumptions for these parameters.

Most importantly, the factor loadings
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}
are assumed to be lower triangular with nonnegative diagonal. This assumption is made to avoid nonidentification of the parameters
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and
\boldsymbol{\phi}
, which otherwise occurs due to the fact that
\boldsymbol{y}
depends on these parameters only through their product
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\boldsymbol{\phi}

(
2
)
. Critically, this identifying assumption does
not
constrain the product
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\boldsymbol{\phi}
.

We now state our parametric and distributional assumptions.
For each unit
1\leq i\leq N
, the factor loadings
\lambda_{i\cdot}
control both the scale of
y_{i\cdot}
(through the norm
\|\lambda_{i\cdot}\|
), and the relative importance of each latent factor to
y_{i\cdot}
(through the relative square magnitudes of the components
\lambda_{ij}^{2}/\|\lambda_{i\cdot}\|^{2}
). We parametrize
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
to allow specifying priors for these two components separately. Specifically, we decompose
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
as

\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{R},

where:

•

\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is a diagonal matrix with
S_{ii}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sigma^{\prime}_{i}>0
controlling the scale of unit
y_{i\cdot}
.

•

\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}
is the Cholesky factor of a correlation matrix, i.e. a lower diagonal matrix with positive diagonal elements (i.e.
R_{kk}>0
for
1\leq k\leq K
) and unit vector rows (i.e.
\|R_{i\cdot}\|=1
for
1\leq i\leq N
). Note that the squared components of each row sum to
1
:
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left[R_{ik}\right]^{2}
. Thus, these squared components express the (standardized) unit
y_{i\cdot}
as a weighted average of the latent factors.

For the scale parameters
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
, we assign priors

\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{InverseGamma}\left(11,10s\right),

where
s
is a fixed scale hyperparameter which we set to the mean standard deviation of the units
y_{i}
. This prior has mean
s
and standard deviation
s/3
.

Subject to the lower triangular and positive diagonal constraints, we take each row of the matrix
\mathbf{R}
to have a uniform prior. Since the rows must be unit vectors, each row is constrained to a compact space, and thus a uniform distribution is a proper prior.

The
K
latent factors are then assigned independent AR(1) process priors with autocorrelations
\rho_{k}
and stationary variances fixed to be
1
:

\displaystyle\phi_{k1}\sim\mathrm{normal}\left(0,\sqrt{1-\rho_{k}^{2}}\right)\text{ for }1\leq k\leq K,

\displaystyle\phi_{kt}\sim\mathrm{normal}\left(\rho_{k}\phi_{k(t-1)},\sqrt{1-\rho_{k}^{2}}\right)\text{ for }1\leq k\leq K\text{ and }2\leq t\leq T

\displaystyle\rho_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{uniform}(0,1).

Finally, the nonzero treatment effects
\delta_{t}
and idiosyncratic errors
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
receive centered normal priors:

\displaystyle\delta_{t}\sim\mathrm{normal}\left(0,\sigma_{\delta}\right)\text{ for }1\leq t\leq T-t_{\delta}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{it}\sim\mathrm{normal}\left(0,f\times\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\right)\text{ for }1\leq i\leq N\text{ and }1\leq t\leq T,

where
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
is the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
diagonal element of the scale matrix
S
in our decomposition of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, and
f
is a fixed scaling hyperparameter. This parameterization implies that
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
control the
overall
scale of
y_{i\cdot}(0)
(combining the latent and idiosyncratic components), and
f
controls the relative size of the idiosyncratic component. We set
f
to
0.025
to express the prior belief that the idiosyncratic component should typically be no more than
5\%
of the scale of the latent component. Choosing a small
f
is beneficial for identification, since either the latent or idiosyncratic component can account for a majority of the variation in the data absent some constraint.

6.7.2
Posterior Predictive Sampling

In order to generate hypothetical data at earlier time points and for an additional unit, we proceed as follows. First, we sample each of the latent factors at
T^{*}
earlier time points. Because the AR(1) prior for the latent factors
\boldsymbol{\phi}
is stationary and Gaussian, it is time-reversible. Therefore, we can sample the latent factors at earlier times by simply iteratively applying the autoregressive equations in reverse. Specifically, let
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\phi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times T^{*}}
represent the latent factors at the earlier times in reverse temporal order (i.e. with
\widetilde{\phi}_{\cdot 1}
immediately preceding
\phi_{\cdot 1}
in time). Then, for each
1\leq k\leq K
, we simulate
\widetilde{\phi}_{k\cdot}
as:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}_{k1}=\rho_{k}\phi_{k1}+\xi_{k1},

(35)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}_{kt}=\rho_{k}\widetilde{\phi}_{k(t-1)}+\xi_{kt}\text{ for }2\leq t\leq T^{*},

(36)

\displaystyle\xi_{kt}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{normal}\left(0,\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\rho_{k}^{2}}}\right),

(37)

\displaystyle(\phi_{k1},\rho_{k})\sim p(\phi_{k1},\rho_{k}\mid\boldsymbol{y}).

(38)

6.9
Almost-Sparse Markov Graphs

The examples presented in this paper all involve models with a Markov graph
M_{\mathcal{F}}
that is sufficiently sparse to have useful local structure, which can then be visualized using our proposed trees. Many commonly encountered models lack this pleasant property, which limits the usefulness of our visualization technique as defined in the main text. In this section, we describe this problem in greater detail and introduce a simple extension of our method which can be fruitfully applied to “almost” sparse graphs (to be adequately defined below).

For our purposes, a Markov graph is sparse if every vertex (corresponding to a single, scalar unknown quantity
\psi_{i}
) is connected to only a few other vertices. In a non-sparse graph, there will be at least one vertex that is connected to a large fraction of the other vertices – potentially all other vertices. The existence of such highly-connected vertices tends to shorten the shortest paths between any two vertices.

It follows from our minimal separator construction
26
that the length of a branch in a distributary tree generated by Algorithm 2 is upper bounded by the length of any shortest path connecting the root vertex to a vertex in the leaf node. Consequently, when applied to non-sparse Markov graphs, Algorithm 2 will tend to generate shallow trees with fewer nodes, limiting our ability to discern relationships among the quantity of interest and other unknown quantities.

In some cases, this problem is unavoidable. However, in many cases, a Markov graph may be “almost” sparse in the sense that a majority of the vertices have few neighbors, while a few vertices have many neighbors. We refer to the former as local vertices and the latter as global vertices. In this case, the global vertices may block our ability to discern useful local connections among the local vertices. A natural solution in cases like this is to condition away the global vertices.

Specifically, suppose that we have divided all unknowns into local parameters
\psi^{L}=\psi_{I_{L}}
and global parameters
\psi^{G}=\psi_{I_{G}}
, with

I_{L}\cup I_{G}=I^{*},\;I_{L}\cap I_{G}=\emptyset,\;\text{and}\;|I_{G}|\ll|I^{*}|.

Then our posterior distribution is
p(\psi^{L},\psi^{G})
(suppressing dependence on the data for notational simplicity). If
p(\psi^{L},\psi^{G})
factorizes over
\mathcal{F}
, then it is easy to see that
p(\psi^{L}\mid\psi^{G})
factorizes over

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\left\{f\cap I_{L}\;\Big|\;f\in\mathcal{F}\right\},

and that the corresponding Markov graph is
M_{\mathcal{F^{\prime}}}=M_{\mathcal{F}}-I_{G}
(i.e. the graph obtained from
M
by deleting all vertices in
I_{G}
). Consequently, if
I_{G}
corresponds to the global vertices in
M_{\mathcal{F}}
, then
p(\psi^{L}\mid\psi^{G})
will have a sparse Markov graph
M_{\mathcal{F}}-I_{G}
, to which we can then fruitfully apply Algorithm 2.

This strategy leads to a simple extension of Algorithm 2. Let
\mathrm{PostTree}\left(M,\theta,\{\check{\psi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{l},\gamma\right)
denote the procedure defined in Algorithm 2, which outputs a distributary tree
T
. Let
\nu^{\mathrm{int}}(T)
denote the internal nodes of
T
, i.e. all non-root and non-leaf nodes. Algorithm
3
gives the modified procedure. In words, we simply apply Algorithm 2 to
M-I_{G}
, and then modify the resulting distributary tree by inserting the global indices
I_{G}
at each internal node in
\nu^{\mathrm{int}}(T)
. It is easy to see that the resulting tree
T
is still a separating tree with respect to the original Markov graph
M
.

Distributary Tree Construction with Global Parameters

Algorithm
3
provides a theoretical solution to the problem of finding useful distributary trees in non-sparse models, but two practical problems remain. The first is the problem of how to specify the global parameters
\psi^{G}
. As the statement of Algorithm
3
indicates, this is entirely user specifiable. While the concept of a global parameter was not given a precise definition, in most cases, we expect there to be a fairly clear delineation between the parameters with “few” neighbors and the parameters with “many”. In such cases, simple heuristics can identify the candidate global parameters automatically from the graph
M
.

The second problem arises when
I_{G}
is large. For any internal node
\nu
in
T
, we will have
I_{G}\subset I(\nu)
, so the size of
I_{G}
lower bounds the size of the node index sets
I(\nu)
. In this case, the size of
I_{G}
can make the difference between
\hat{U}_{I(\nu)}
being tractable and intractable to compute. By constructing minimal separating sets at each internal node, Algorithm
2
was designed to suppress the size of the node index sets
I(\nu)
. But by injecting
I_{G}
in each internal node, Algorithm
3
lacks this virtue, and may thus reasonably be seen as only a partial solution to the problem.

When
I_{G}
is large, another possible solution is to re-run the original posterior inference algorithm (e.g. Markov chain Monte Carlo, as used in our examples) conditioning on a particular value of
\psi^{G}
. In this case, we obtain samples directly from
p(\psi^{L}\mid\psi^{G})
, and can thus drop
I_{G}
from the computation of the
\hat{U}_{I(\nu)}
. The result, however, is only an approximation to the output of Algorithm
3
since, in this case, we only condition on a single value of
\psi^{G}
(rather than taking an expectation over the posterior).
