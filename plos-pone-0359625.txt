Strengthening alcohol consumption screening in primary care for culturally diverse populations in Latin America: Adaptation and content and face validity of the AUDIT in Guaviare, Colombia

Andrea López-González, Adriana Buitrago-López, Nelcy Rodríguez Malagón, Nicolás Martínez Ramos, Jhon Sebastián Patiño Rueda, Deyanira Sindy Moya Chaves, Dora Yonda Suns, Carlos Gómez-Restrepo

ABSTRACT
Background: The World Health Organization developed the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test, widely used to detect alcohol consumption and guide interventions in primary care. Despite its broad use, evidence of its validity in culturally diverse settings remains limited. Although Spanish versions exist, none have been culturally adapted for Guaviare, a Colombian Amazon region with multicultural communities and socioeconomic and healthcare inequities. Such inequities may intensify alcohol consumption and related consequences. Without cultural adaptation, AUDIT screening may underestimate consumption and hinder effective primary-care interventions. Objective: This study aimed to culturally adapt and assess the content and face validity of the AUDIT in both interview and self-administered formats for alcohol consumption screening among adults in Guaviare, Colombia. Methods: This study followed a descriptive qualitative approach to guide the cultural adaptation and validation of a scale. Phase 1: cultural adaptation was conducted through consensus among researchers and a panel of community experts (n = 11), using a nominal group technique to prioritize adjustments from the community’s perspective. Phase 2: Field testing involved cognitive interviews (n = 18) to assess content and face validity, as well as semantic and technical equivalence. Additional cultural refinements were made based on participants’ feedback. Results: Cultural adaptations were made to the instructions, questions, and response options. Most adjustments focused on improving comprehension of the scale and guiding respondents to answer each item accurately. In addition, modifications were implemented to make the scale more recognizable and culturally appropriate for the community. Conclusion: This study shows that the culturally adapted AUDIT for adults in Guaviare demonstrates adequate content and face validity, representing the first validated version of the scale for this population. The findings underscore the importance of context-sensitive adaptations to ensure accurate identification of alcohol consumption and to strengthen primary care interventions.

Introduction Alcohol consumption is increasing and represents a global public health problem due to its high morbidity and mortality, particularly in low- and middle-income countries such as Colombia [ 1 – 3 ]. This condition can lead to both physical and social consequences in the short and medium term [ 1 , 4 ]. Since 1987, the World Health Organization (WHO) has developed and validated the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) to screen for hazardous alcohol consumption and to support interventions in primary care [ 5 ]. This scale currently has two validated Spanish versions for Latin American contexts, one from Ecuador and another from Mexico [ 5 , 6 ]. However, these versions have not been culturally adapted for diverse populations or for groups experiencing social and economic disadvantages in Latin America, nor do they incorporate locally relevant cultural alcohol consumption practices. Applying the scale without cultural adaptation may lead to comprehension difficulties and operational challenges during administration, ultimately compromising measurement validity [ 2 ]. The distinction between translation and cultural adaptation is critical to understanding the gap described above. Translation refers to the linguistic conversion of an instrument’s content from one language to another, aiming for lexical and grammatical equivalence. Cultural adaptation, by contrast, is a broader process that extends beyond word-for-word translation to ensure content, technical, and semantic equivalence between the original instrument and the target population, particularly when the target context differs substantially from the one in which the instrument was originally developed [ 7 , 8 ]. Cultural adaptation therefore involves modifying instructions, item wording, response options, and administration procedures to reflect idiomatic expressions, regionally relevant drinking practices, and the local cultural context, while ensuring that the underlying construct retains its original meaning [ 7 , 8 ]. This process reflects not only a methodological necessity, but also a recognition of communities’ right to actively participate in validating their own knowledge and practices, according to their own needs and context [ 9 ]. Internationally recognized guidelines, such as those proposed by the ISPOR Task Force for Translation and Cultural Adaptation [ 10 ], formalize this process into a series of systematic steps—typically involving translation, expert and community review, and cognitive assessment of respondent understanding through techniques such as cognitive interviewing [ 11 ]. Beyond linguistic differences, terminology and expressions related to alcohol consumption vary substantially across Spanish-speaking regions, shaped by the sociocultural characteristics of each area [ 12 ]. Consequently, experts have emphasized the importance of culturally adapting measurement instruments when the target population differs from that for which the scale was originally developed. This recommendation is particularly relevant in culturally diverse settings, such as Guaviare, even when a Spanish version is already available [ 13 ]. Guaviare is a culturally and ethnically diverse region located in the Colombian Amazon, home to multiple Indigenous peoples and internal migrants [ 14 , 15 ]. The region also faces intersecting social, environmental, and territorial challenges, including poverty, armed conflict, and limited access to healthcare and basic services [ 16 – 18 ]. These factors contribute to reduced quality of life and increased alcohol consumption relative to national levels [ 17 ]. The high levels of alcohol consumption in the region have likely contributed to increases in homicides, theft, domestic violence, traffic accidents, and physical injuries [ 19 ]. Primary healthcare plays a crucial role in the early detection and management of alcohol consumption in Guaviare due to the limited availability of mental health specialists. The AUDIT is one of the most strongly recommended instruments for this purpose by the WHO [ 5 , 20 ]. However, there is no Spanish version of the AUDIT that has been culturally adapted for the diverse population of Guaviare. Cultural adaptation plays a crucial role in addressing this gap, as it enables a clearer understanding of the instructions, questions, and response options, thereby improving the accuracy and validity of responses and facilitating the appropriate implementation of the interventions recommended by the scale [ 13 , 21 ]. Additionally, the cultural adaptation and validation of this scale could demonstrate a methodological approach that can be learned and replicated by researchers working in other contexts. This transferable framework creates opportunities to generate culturally appropriate measurement tools and advance knowledge in diverse communities worldwide. This study aimed to culturally adapt and assess the content and face validity of the AUDIT in both interview and self-administered formats for alcohol consumption screening among adults in Guaviare, Colombia. Content and face validity are two related but distinct properties commonly assessed during instrument adaptation. Content validity refers to whether the items of an adapted scale adequately represent the construct they are intended to measure, while face validity refers to whether the instrument appears, at first glance, to measure what it claims to measure from the perspective of those who administer or respond to it [ 22 ]. Together, both properties are essential when culturally adapting a scale such as the AUDIT for a new population. Materials and methods Settings and study sites This study was conducted in two locations in Guaviare, Colombia: San José del Guaviare, the capital of the region (urban area), and the township of Charras-Boquerón (rural area). This area is home to Indigenous, Afro-descendant, and farming communities, as well as victims of the armed conflict, migrants, and others [ 14 , 15 ]. There are few health centers in the region due to its remote geographical location and the limited availability of financial resources and healthcare personnel. In San José del Guaviare, there is a public hospital that provides primary care and specialized medical services; however, it does not have any psychiatrists available. In contrast, in the township of Charras-Boquerón, located approximately two hours from San José del Guaviare, there is only primary care provided by a general practitioner and nursing staff. Study design This was a qualitative descriptive study conducted to support the cultural adaptation and the assessment of content and face validity of the AUDIT [ 23 , 24 ]. The theoretical framework used in this study followed the recommendations of the International Test Commission (ITC) and the guidelines outlined in the book Measurement and the Measurement of Change proposed by Polit & Yang [ 8 , 22 ]. The study was divided into two consecutive phases in which qualitative data collection methods were implemented, including structured observation, the nominal group technique, and cognitive interviewing ( Fig 1 ). The cultural adaptation was conducted on the Spanish version of the AUDIT recommended by the WHO since 2001 for use in Latin American populations. This adaptation was carried out for both the interviewer-administered and the self-administered formats ( S1 Appendix ) [ 5 ]. 10.1371/journal.pone.0359625.g001 Fig 1 General description of the phases of the AUDIT adaptation. The cultural adaptation was divided into two consecutive phases. The first phase comprised the steps leading to the first adapted version of the AUDIT. The second phase included additional refinements that resulted in the final adapted version of the AUDIT. Participants A purposive, criterion-based, non-probability sampling approach was employed to capture the sociocultural diversity of the population in Guaviare, Colombia. Participants were selected considering characteristics such as social group (settlers, peace agreement signatories, rural farmers, and Indigenous or Afro-descendant people), educational level, sex, age, and place of residence, to ensure heterogeneous representation. In the context of Guaviare, these groups reflect distinct historical and cultural backgrounds related to settlement, rural livelihoods, and ethnic identity. Individuals aged 18–65 years who were Spanish speakers with adequate comprehension and communication skills, and who were either native residents or migrants with occupational or educational integration in the department, were eligible for inclusion, provided they gave voluntary consent. Those unavailable during the data collection period were excluded. The recruitment strategy involved gatekeeping and door-to-door approaches. Recruitment continued until all predefined sociodemographic characteristics were represented in the sample. Eleven participants were included as community experts (Phase 1), and 18 participants were selected for field testing (Phase 2). Recruitment took place from April 2, 2024, to August 5, 2024. The community experts were divided into two groups of five and six individuals, respectively—one from a rural area and the other from an urban area of Guaviare, Colombia. They participated in collaborative activities with the research team to review, discuss, and propose cultural adjustments to the AUDIT scale. Adaptation team The cultural adaptation of the instrument was undertaken by an interdisciplinary team of researchers, comprising professionals with expertise in clinical epidemiology, biostatistics, alcohol consumption, public health, primary care, linguistics, and scale adaptation and validation (ALG, NMR, JSPR, DSMC, and DYS). The team included both technical personnel based in the region and experts with extensive experience working with populations in complex socio-economic contexts. Community experts (Phase 1) participated as co-creators in the adaptation process, contributing local knowledge, cultural practices, and communication styles specific to the communities of Guaviare. Adapted scale framework The AUDIT is available in two formats, interview and self-administered. It was developed by the WHO for screening alcohol consumption and suggesting appropriate interventions based on the obtained score [ 5 ]. The AUDIT is freely available for research and publication use ( S1 Appendix ). It consists of ten items grouped into three dimensions of hazardous alcohol consumption: alcohol consumption (items 1–3), symptoms of dependence (items 4–6), and harmful alcohol consumption (items 7–10) [ 5 , 25 ]. Each question offers three to five ordinal response options, assigned discrete scores ranging from zero to four, allowing for the calculation of a total score between zero and 40 points. A score equal to or greater than eight indicates hazardous alcohol consumption, with a sensitivity of 90% and a specificity of 80% [ 5 ]. The WHO currently recommends the use of the Spanish version validated in Mexico for Spanish-speaking populations in Latin America [ 26 ]. Data collection Phase 1. An initial exploration of the context of alcohol consumption was conducted through on-site structured observation ( S2 Appendix ) in San José del Guaviare and the township of Charras-Boquerón (Department of Guaviare, Colombia). The researcher participated in community meetings and celebrations, visited alcohol-selling establishments, and observed households where alcoholic beverages were consumed. The findings derived from these observations were shared with the research team to inform culturally appropriate adjustments aligned with the local context. The research team met virtually to carry out the first modifications to the original version of the AUDIT ( S1 Appendix ). These adjustments focused on assessing the cultural relevance of each item in relation to the construct of hazardous alcohol consumption and the population of Guaviare. Two groups of community experts were formed according to the selection criteria, and an in-person meeting was held with each group—one in San José del Guaviare and the other in Charras-Boquerón, Guaviare, Colombia. During these meetings, the nominal group technique was implemented ( S3 Appendix ). Subsequently, the research team met virtually to resolve discrepancies and make further adjustments based on the results of the nominal group technique. This process led to the development of the first culturally adapted version of the AUDIT in both formats (self-administered and interview) ( S4 Appendix ). Phase 2. Participants in this phase completed field testing using the first adapted version of the AUDIT ( S4 Appendix ). For the self-administered format, the researcher provided a printed paper version of the scale, which was completed directly by the participants. In the interview format, the researcher read the instructions and questions aloud and recorded the participant’s responses on the printed form. Afterwards, the completed paper forms were entered into a digital database. Once the scale had been completed, the researcher conducted a cognitive interview lasting approximately 30–40 minutes, using audio recording and note-taking to collect information. Cognitive interviews followed an interpretivist approach, as described by Miller et al. [ 27 ], and were analyzed through a five-step synthesis and reduction process. First, open-ended probing questions were used to explore how each participant understood and responded to each scale item ( S5 Appendix ); examples included “What did you understand from the instructions?”, “What did you understand by the question…?”, “What do you think you are being asked?”, “Did you have any difficulty answering the question? Why?”, “What did you understand by the answer option…?”, and “How did you arrive at that answer?” Second, responses were summarized to capture how each participant interpreted the questions, formulated their answers, and any difficulties experienced while completing the scale. Third, responses were compared across participants to identify common patterns. Fourth, findings were organized according to administration format (interview or self-administered) to identify potential differences between subgroups. Finally, conclusions were drawn regarding how participants interpreted the questions and formulated their responses, whether difficulties arose during completion, and whether differences were observed between the interview and self-administered formats. The audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, and transcription codes were applied to capture both verbal and nonverbal language. The research team made new adjustments to the first adapted version of the AUDIT based on the results of the cognitive interviews. This process resulted in the final culturally adapted version of the AUDIT, reflecting the sociocultural context of a group of adults from Guaviare, Colombia ( S6 Appendix ). Analysis A descriptive analysis was conducted of the participants’ sociodemographic characteristics. Errors made by participants, the number of unanswered questions, and the number of questions with multiple responses were described. In addition, the modifications made by the research team and community experts to the original AUDIT in both formats were documented. A thematic analysis [ 28 ] of the cognitive interviews was performed to assess potential differences in interpretive patterns and response processes between participants who used the interview format and those who used the self-administered format. This analysis identified the degree of overlap between the two perspectives (researcher and participant). All cognitive interviews were transcribed and coded using a deductive, top-down thematic analysis based on a codebook containing predefined categories and subcategories ( Fig 2 ), which were established according to the interview guide. The researcher’s field notes and participants’ responses to the AUDIT were also reviewed. The analysis focused on data reduction and the identification of broad conceptual themes. Coding was conducted using NVivo 14 software [ 29 ]. 10.1371/journal.pone.0359625.g002 Fig 2 Codebook for cognitive interviews. The codebook was organized into five categories corresponding to the parts of the scale that were adapted, each classified according to three subcategories: comprehension, needs and recommendations, and barrier. Ethical clearance This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hospital Universitario San Ignacio and Pontificia Universidad Javeriana on March 7, 2024 (Minutes No. 04/2024). All participants were adults and provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Inclusivity in global research. The study was designed and conducted by Colombian researchers in collaboration with local stakeholders. Before data collection, the informed consent form was pilot-tested with three community members from Guaviare to ensure clarity and comprehension. As part of our ethical commitment to the Guaviare community, the study was presented to and approved by the Secretaría de Salud del Guaviare (Guaviare Department Health Secretariat), Colombia, and reviewed and endorsed by community leaders from the township of Charras-Boquerón before implementation. Community members were actively engaged throughout the cultural adaptation process through nominal group sessions and cognitive interviews, and their feedback directly informed revisions to the adapted AUDIT. To promote local capacity building, training on hazardous alcohol consumption screening was provided to healthcare personnel in Guaviare. One of the co-authors is a member of the study community. Additional information regarding the ethical, cultural, and scientific considerations specific to inclusivity in global research is provided in S7 Appendix . Given that most participants did not know how to mark their responses to the questions, despite the presence of an explanatory note at the beginning of the scale, it was decided to include written instructions accompanied by a diagram for each question. Although most participants considered that the names and images in the visual guides for alcoholic beverages and containers were clear and contextually appropriate for the region, some offered recommendations and suggested adjustments. They recommended changing the image of “guarapo” (a traditional fermented beverage made from sugarcane juice), as this beverage is more commonly stored in a pot or gallon container. They also suggested adding other distilled alcoholic beverages consumed in the region, such as vodka and gin. In addition, participants indicated that beverages regarded as cocktails (such as “piña colada” and whisky cream) should be included. They further considered it important to add an image of a glass in the visual guide, since it is one of the most used vessels for drinking alcoholic beverages. On the other hand, participants experienced difficulties understanding the question and instructions in these visual guides, specifically because it was unclear how to mark the response immediately before the images, and the time frame referenced in the question (past year) was not specified. III) Questions. Regarding the words or expressions used in the AUDIT items—such as “injured,” “on an empty stomach,” “has shown concern,” “feelings of guilt,” and “has been unable to remember”—most participants understood them appropriately. Likewise, items 1, 5, 6, 8, 9, and 10 were the most frequently understood. To date, only one adaptation of this scale (interview format) has been conducted in the country, specifically among a university population in Bogotá [ 35 ]. There are notable differences between the adaptation proposed by Riveros et al. and the present study. For instance, the previous adaptation neither defined “alcoholic beverage” nor modified the term “consumption” in AUDIT’s item 1 [ 35 ]. These differences are likely attributable to the characteristics of the study population and the absence of qualitative methodologies during the adaptation phase—such as interviews or focus groups to explore community perceptions and language comprehension. In contrast, we implemented several cultural adjustments and added instructions and visual guides of alcoholic beverages and containers according to the sociocultural context. These modifications aimed to reduce measurement bias and facilitate participants’ ability to recall and accurately comprehend each question and response option. Although the WHO AUDIT manual suggests that this scale can be used in any cultural context, the social dynamics surrounding alcohol consumption in each community—particularly in those with disadvantaged socioeconomic conditions—lead to variations in language and in the definitions of alcoholic beverages. Therefore, the absence of an adaptation for such communities may result in measurement errors and have implications for both clinical practice and research processes [ 5 , 36 ]. In this regard, having this new version of the AUDIT adapted for adults in Guaviare represents an opportunity to improve the early detection and timely treatment of this issue in the region. Likewise, cultural adaptations related to alcohol consumption are particularly important in low- and middle-income countries, as they allow for more valid screening in settings where this problem is increasingly [ 36 ]. Although the ideal administration of the AUDIT is based on the use of a standard drink guide, in this study a visual guide depicting the different types of alcoholic beverages commonly consumed in the region, as well as a guide to the containers used for their consumption, was included in order to facilitate participants’ recall and understanding of the questions [ 36 , 37 ]. This decision was made for several reasons. The main one is that, in Colombia, there is currently no official definition of a standard drink, and it is not advisable to use standard drink guides from other countries, given that the Guaviare department is multicultural and it is common for individuals to consume traditional beverages that are homemade, produced through non-standardized methods, and have variable or unknown alcohol content. Furthermore, the community uses various types of containers to drink these beverages, making it difficult to objectively determine the milliliters consumed per person. In addition, terminology such as “grams of alcohol” or “standard alcoholic drink” is unfamiliar to the population of Guaviare and could cause confusion when responding to the questionnaire items. Notably, the cultural adaptation process revealed a form of cultural invisibility surrounding alcohol consumption: traditional fermented beverages such as “guarapo” were not initially perceived by participants as alcoholic beverages, despite being one of the most frequently consumed alcoholic beverages in rural regions of Colombia [ 38 ]. This finding suggests that culturally embedded drinking practices may remain unrecognized as alcohol consumption, potentially compromising the validity of alcohol consumption measures in this population. In response to this finding, several cultural adaptations were made to the instrument, including the incorporation of a definition of alcoholic beverages that explicitly included fermented beverages, a visual guide illustrating these beverages, and repeated reminders of this definition throughout the instrument. These modifications were intended to reduce measurement bias by helping participants recognize fermented beverages as alcoholic beverages and recall occasions on which they had consumed alcohol. Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge that a measurement of bias due to individual underreporting may persist—a phenomenon widely documented in the literature and frequently observed when assessing alcohol consumption [ 39 ]. Following the linguistic and cultural adaptation of the AUDIT scale, all items were found to be relevant and comprehensive with respect to the construct of hazardous alcohol consumption among adults in Guaviare. On the other hand, the type of format or method of administration of the scale (interview or self-administered) is a subjective decision that depends on each participant. In other words, based on the results of Phase 2 of this study, it is not possible to recommend using only the interview or the self-administered format in clinical or research contexts. Several studies have suggested that allowing participants to choose the format may increase the validity of their responses, particularly in populations with sociocultural diversity or low educational levels [ 40 , 41 ]. For this reason, a question regarding participants’ format preference was included in the instructions. Limitations The sample size was determined using a purposive, criterion-based approach aimed at ensuring heterogeneous representation across relevant sociodemographic characteristics, rather than information saturation or interpretative redundancy, which were not formally assessed; it is therefore possible that additional participants could have contributed further insights not captured in the current analysis. Because alcohol consumption is a sensitive topic, participants in the cognitive interviews may have been reluctant to openly express difficulties in understanding certain items, potentially limiting the depth of the qualitative feedback obtained. This reluctance may also reflect the influence of social desirability, a well-documented source of bias in self-reported alcohol consumption. In addition, because all participants were Spanish-speaking, the cultural adaptation findings may not be generalizable to populations who do not speak Spanish. Finally, this study assessed only the content and face validity of the culturally adapted AUDIT. Other psychometric properties, including reliability and construct validity, are currently being evaluated and will be reported in a subsequent publication. Conclusion This study presents the first culturally adapted version of the AUDIT for adults in the Colombian Amazon, addressing a critical gap in validated screening instruments for populations that have historically had limited access to context-appropriate assessment tools. This process yielded a Spanish-language version of the AUDIT with robust evidence of content and face validity, tailored to the cultural realities of Guaviare. These findings also underscore the importance of cultural adaptation across different contexts, as alcohol-related practices vary substantially between populations; even traditional beverages such as “guarapo” can become normalized to the point of not being recognized as alcoholic, requiring careful adjustments to improve the accuracy of alcohol consumption measurement. This methodological approach offers a transferable framework for adapting and validating screening instruments in other diverse or resource-constrained settings, contributing to global efforts to strengthen early detection of alcohol consumption and improve public health strategies worldwide.
