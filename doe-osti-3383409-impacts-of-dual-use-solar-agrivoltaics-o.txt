3. Executive Summary
This project contributes applied research to better understand the impacts of
agrivoltaics on farm microclimates, crop productivity, and economics at the farm and
sector level. The site trial research is divided into two sets of site trials integrated into
commercial farm and agrivoltaic operations – one set on annual vegetable and hay
crops and one on perennial cranberry bog operations. The economic research
developed methods for recording and evaluating changes in farm operations and costs,
and on the public perception of and willingness-to-accept agrivoltaics.
The vegetable and hay site trials examined agrivoltaic systems under field conditions
with a focus on microclimate variation, crop response, and on-farm performance. The
research developed microclimate monitoring stations capable of continuous, replicated
measurement, capable of detecting many differences in environmental conditions
across short distances within arrays. These data characterize distinct microclimates in
arrays that vary by position and time of day. Analysis emphasized that commonly used
summary metrics are insufficient on their own, and that interpretation requires
consideration of duration, magnitude, and biological relevance.
In parallel, the research evaluated several crop types and observed responses that
were largely consistent with differences in light availability, with additional influence from
temperature and crop-specific physiology. Crop yield and general plant performance
was influenced by species and location within the array. In general, lower light-demand
crops performed well within arrays while higher light-demand crops experienced more
challenges and potential drawbacks. This work adds to the field by clearly identifying

 Page 2 of 56

distinct microclimates within agrivoltaic systems, evaluating crop response patterns in a
Northeast climate, and studying system performance under on-farm conditions.
This on-farm work also revealed key considerations under farm management at scale
and provided empirical data that can be used by farmers, researchers, and policy
makers. Performance under active farm conditions provides a basis for evaluating
performance beyond controlled settings. This provides insight into how these systems
relate to, and differ from, typical farming practices. This information supports more
informed assessment of agrivoltaic systems.
At the cranberry site trials, the research (1) assessed eco-physiological and biochemical
crop responses on a commercial cranberry bog grown under an agrivoltaics system with
different row spacings in the year immediately after installation, and (2) analyzed the
effects on yield and fruit quality over two growing seasons following installation.
These activities helped improve understanding of how cranberry responds
physiologically to shading from agrivoltaics. The research measured the productivity of
cranberry after installing solar panels, revealing the trade-off between shading from
energy production and yield. The data serves demonstrates how perennial fruit crops
are expected to behave under shading from agricultural systems.
The economic portion of the project advanced the study of agrivoltaics by addressing
farm-level decision-making and public acceptance of land-use change to accommodate
agrivoltaics. The research team designed detailed daily logbooks and whole-farm cost
tracking systems tailored to multiple crops, enabling consistent measurement of labor,
input use, and financial performance under both conventional and dual-use solar
systems. These tools were aligned with established enterprise budgets and structured
to integrate with financial modeling platforms, supporting forward-looking analysis of
production trade-offs. In parallel, the team conducted an extensive interdisciplinary
literature review spanning economic adoption models, renewable energy policy, and
social acceptance, synthesizing insights from academic, policy, and stakeholder
sources. Building on this foundation, the project implemented a large-scale discrete
choice experiment across 6,750 U.S. consumers, incorporating randomized information
treatments and behavioral elicitation (risk and social preferences), and applied mixed
logit modeling to estimate preferences, willingness to pay, and heterogeneity across
populations. Together, these methods contribute to the field by providing one of the first
comprehensive frameworks that combines farm-level cost accounting, behavioral
economics, and experimental valuation to evaluate agrivoltaic systems.
The findings highlight both the opportunities and challenges of agrivoltaics for the public
and agricultural sectors. While solar energy generation can substantially improve farm
financial outcomes, observational evidence from on-farm integration in Massachusetts
suggests that crop production under solar arrays often incurs higher costs, reduced
yields, and operational constraints, potentially leading to lower overall food production.
At the same time, results from the choice experiment reveal significant heterogeneity in
public acceptance, with rural communities—who are most likely to host installations—
expressing less support compared to urban and suburban populations. These outcomes
underscore an important distributional tension, where the benefits of renewable energy
may not align with local perceptions or agricultural priorities. For the public, this

research provides actionable insights for designing more effective and equitable
policies, including compensation mechanisms and future research questions that better
support co-production of food and energy. Ultimately, the project informs policymakers,
farmers, and stakeholders on how to balance renewable energy expansion with
agricultural sustainability and community acceptance.

6. Project Objectives
Solar co-located with agriculture, also known as agrivoltaics or dual-use solar, has
become an area of increasing interest to farmers, solar developers, and local
policymakers. However, insufficient data exists to allow farmers, solar project
developers, and policymakers to make informed decisions about the impacts of this
technology on farm productivity and financial viability, local and regional agricultural
economies, solar project economics, and public welfare. The overall project objectives
are: 1) to contribute significant research to better understand the impacts on agriculture
productivity of agrivoltaic installations, 2) to assess the role of agrivoltaics on farm
financial viability and the agricultural economy broadly, and 3) to disseminate this
information to research and stakeholder communities. The project offers data and
analyses to assist farmers, solar developers, and policymakers in making informed
decisions to design, operate, and encourage farms that enhance the value of solar and
agriculture.
A differentiator of this research effort has been the establishment of farm site trials
within commercial agrivoltaic arrays and in coordination with active farming of the site.
This on-site farm research raised additional challenges but also provided insights into

 Page 7 of 56

the impacts of the array on farming logistics and farmer behaviors over a broader range
of crops, farm soils, and solar array designs than a standard research site trial.
All the agrivoltaic sites engaged in this research conformed to the requirements of the
Massachusetts solar incentive program (SMART) and met the eligibility requirements for
the Agricultural Solar Tariff Generation Unit or agrivoltaic incentive adder. This policy
differentiates these arrays from many others in the U.S., both in terms of the
requirements for elevated and spread-out solar array design and for approved farm
plans, and for the financial incentives and revenue streams that impact the farmers and
landowners.
To accomplish this research and meet our objectives, the research effort was structured
around three research themes – site trial monitoring, economic impacts, and outreach.
The site trial monitoring was structured and largely repeated across the 4 agricultural
years over the project duration (Tasks 1, 5, and 8). The economic impact made use of
data and observations of the site trials and other methods accomplished across Tasks
2, 3, 6, and 9). Project outreach and dissemination, including the convening of our
Project Advisory Committee was accomplished in Tasks 4, 7, and 10). These tasks and
sub-tasks, along with their applicable milestones and go/no-go decision points are
itemized below as provided in the project Statement of Project Objectives.

7. Project Results and Discussion
Site Trials: Tasks 1, 5, and 8 – Vegetable and Hay Sites
Discussion of Results: Task 1 – Determine Pre-Construction Baseline Data and
Construction Impacts, and Prepare Research Designs for Agricultural Site Trials
In 2022, the initial experimental design was developed, and sensor stands were
designed and tested (Figure 7.1). The design included placing sensor clusters in
transects to capture positional variation within arrays and the control (Figure 7.2) with
three in-array replications, and controls in open field conditions with three replications.
Arrays included both fixed-tilt with gaps every third panel (Grafton vegetable and
Grafton grazing) and single-axis-tracking with bifacial panels (Monson hay).

Also in 2022, field data from sensors (PAR, air temperature, relative humidity, leaf
wetness, rainfall, soil moisture, soil temperature) was collected under the vegetable
array (butternut squash, lettuce) in Grafton and the hay array in Monson, as well as their
respective control areas. The primary use of this data, collected at 5-minute intervals,
was to confirm the feasibility of the field data collection system, identify trends and
anomalies in the data, and refine the overall research design for the 2023 season.
The Grafton grazing site was not established as expected in 2022 and was not
subsequently pursued as a research site trial. In 2023, the research effort re-focused on
the Hadley vegetable site (single-axis tracking), which was constructed by 2023 but did
not have tracking until mid-2024.

Discussion of Results: Task 5 – Establish Plantings at Five Agricultural Site Trials
and Collect Data
Planting of butternut squash and lettuce (two plantings in succession) took place in
2023. The Hadley vegetable site was planted in late-season broccoli in 2023. The
Monson hay site was already established before the beginning of the project and did not
require annual plantings because hay is a perennial crop. Farm operations were
conducted by those responsible for farm management, not the research team.
In 2023, many additional sensors were purchased and deployed on sensor stands
designed and build by the team, and field data from sensors was collected in the
Grafton vegetable array, the Hadley vegetable array, and the Monson hay array, and
their respective control plots. Additional data collection included SPAD (chlorophyll)
measurements, fresh and dry biomass, yield, marketable yield, and field observations of
growth stage, pest/disease damage, and physiological condition.
Data cleaning and analysis commenced in earnest in the winter of 2023-24. Revisions
to the plan for field data collection were completed. Hundreds of statistical differences
were identified, illustrating distinct microclimate differences within the array, and in the
array compared to the control (example in Table 7.1).

Discussion of Results: Task 8 - Conduct Field Research at Five Agricultural Site
Trials and Collect Data
In the Grafton vegetable array, planting of butternut squash and lettuce (two plantings in
succession) took place in both 2023 and 2024. There were no differences to lettuce
yields as caused by the array versus control. A lettuce pathogen was present that lead
to yield loss, but this was not clearly or distinctly attributed to the array (Figure 7.3).

Individual butternut squash plants did not show a yield penalty, but the butternut
population was below standard field conditions. Alas, on a per acre basis, yield would
be reduced. This system is inconclusive because there was significant vining into
sunnier areas as well as significant weed pressure, especially close to the panels
(Figure 7.4). The weed pressure was in part responsible for the reduced population.

In Hadley, late-season broccoli was planted in 2023 and 2024. In 2023, the panels were
not yet tilting. Plants under the dripline experienced significant damage (Figure 7.5)
from heavy rains which led to no yield. Plants in other bays, planted earlier in the

In 2024, the broccoli planting was preceded by early-season spinach, but the farmer did
not manage or harvest the crop. The broccoli yield in the array had the potential to
significantly outperform the yield in the control. However, a physiological disorder
(Figures 7.7 and 7.8) resulted in reduced marketability. The result is not explicitly
explained by the influence of the array, likewise a causal influence of the array cannot
be ruled out.
The Monson site was managed for hay in 2024, with notable variance from common
hay management practices. The open-field control was prioritized for haying, perhaps
due to ease of management. Haying in the area was quite delayed, reducing the field
quality in the array compared to the control.

Soil Health Activities
At the Monson site, soil samples were taken in 2024 for CASH analysis, including
penetrometer readings. The lack of pre-construction samples ultimately limited the
ability to attribute main treatment effects directly to agrivoltaic installation. However, the
detection of significant treatment across year interactions for soil pH and
respiration indicates that soils beneath the solar array experienced distinct temporal
dynamics compared to control soils at this site.
In the absence of baseline data, these differences cannot be confidently attributed
to agrivoltaic installation nor pre-existing spatial variability or historical management
differences. This distinction reinforces conclusions from systematic reviews that
emphasize the importance of pre-installation land use and management history in
shaping observed soil carbon patterns under solar development (Carvalho et al., 2024;
Krasner et al., 2025).
At the Grafton site, soil samples were taken in 2024 for CASH analysis, including
penetrometer readings. Bulk density samples were also taken. Additional samples were
taken to examine soil aggregate stability and respiration according to bed location within
the array. Results from the Grafton vegetable site highlight the importance of
management context when interpreting soil physical indicators. Bulk density differed
significantly between agrivoltaic and control plots, but measurements were collected in
the fall of 2022 after construction.

                                         Page 18 of 56

The lack of a baseline sample again impacted the ability to draw conclusions.
Additionally, soils beneath the array were subject to annual tillage associated with
vegetable production, while control plots experienced reduced or no tillage. Soil trends
within the array could be attributed to the effects of annual tillage on reducing
compaction in the soil surface. Although the strength of our inference is limited, our data
points to a possible pattern where installation of agrivoltaics increases soil compaction,
which is then remediated to some degree by tillage within agrivoltaics.
This interpretation aligns with broader agronomic literature showing that annual tillage
temporarily reduces bulk density, while reduced-tillage systems often exhibit higher bulk
density in the short term despite improvements in aggregate stability and long-term soil
structure. Systematic reviews of solar-soil interactions similarly conclude that soil
physical outcomes are strongly shaped by post-installation management rather than by
panel presence alone (Carvalho et al., 2024; Krasner et al., 2025).
At the Hadley site, soil samples were taken in 2024 and 2025 for CASH analysis,
including penetrometer readings. Bulk density samples were also taken annually.
At the Hadley vegetable site, where pre-construction data were available, most soil
health indicators changed similarly over time in agrivoltaic and control plots. While
surface hardness exhibited a treatment × year interaction, bulk density showed
a significant main effect of treatment without a corresponding treatment across year
interaction. This indicates that bulk density differed between agrivoltaic and control plots
but did not increase or diverge over time following installation.
The absence of a temporal interaction suggests that differences in bulk density are
more likely attributable to site-specific factors or management practices rather than to
progressive compaction caused by agrivoltaic infrastructure. In intensively managed
annual vegetable systems, short-term agrivoltaic effects on soil physical properties may
therefore be difficult to distinguish from background variability associated with tillage,
traffic, and seasonal soil moisture conditions.
These findings suggest that early soil responses following agrivoltaic installation in
vegetable systems may be subtle and easily overshadowed by annual management
practices, including tillage, traffic, and crop rotation. Similar conclusions have been
drawn from agrivoltaic vegetable trials in the Northeast, where year-to-year weather
variability and management effects were stronger drivers of system performance than
panel presence alone (Doubleday et al., 2025; Sturchio et al., 2025). In intensively
managed annual systems, short-term agrivoltaic effects on soil health may therefore be
difficult to detect without longer monitoring periods.

Discussion of Results: Task 1 – Determine Pre-Construction Baseline Data and
Construction Impacts, and Prepare Research Designs for Agricultural Site Trials
During the first project year, pre- and post-construction baseline data were collected at
cranberry trial sites to document installation-related impacts on cranberry bogs. These
assessments focused on soil compaction, and aboveground biomass within established
cranberry canopies. Baseline measurements provided a reference for evaluating
subsequent agronomic and physiological responses following installation of solar
arrays. Following installation of fixed tilt solar panels at a height of 3.0 m above the plant
canopy, four shading treatments were evaluated: an uncovered control area and row
spacings of 9.0 m, 7.0 m, and 5.0 m.
There were no significant differences in pre- and post-soil compaction at the cranberry
results. The mitigation measures of laying mats on top of the bogs were successful in
protecting them. In terms of biomass, there were no differences across the site pre-
construction. Post construction, the number of uprights (short, vertical stems, that grow
from horizontal runners bearing the flowers and fruit) per unit area differed significantly
among shading treatments (Priya et al., 2025). Upright density was highest in the
control and 30% shade treatments, which did not differ from each other, while values
declined significantly at 35% shade and were lowest at 37% shade. In a similar manner,
total upright biomass also varied significantly with shading. Biomass was highest in
control and the 30% shade treatment; lower biomass was measured for vines grown
under 35% and 37% shade.
Construction schedules varied across sites, with some trial locations not reaching post-
construction readiness until 2024. The Ring Road (Kingston: Howes cranberry) was the
first site available for comprehensive post-construction monitoring.

Detailed Explanation on the Daily Logbook
A daily logbook captures daily farming activities which fit into variable costs. The
logbook separates control field and treatment fields. Here, the control field refers to the
cropland without the installation of dual-use array, while the treatment fields refer to the
croplands with the installation of array.
To design the logbook, the researchers first surveyed published literature, extension
tools, agricultural sector trade group resources, and enterprise budgets for all crops
proposed for the site trials. The team met with the PAC and project partners to gather
information regarding what to include in the data collection, how to best structure and
format the tools, and how to collect the data. In the meetings, it was determined that the
labor costs and changes in equipment and practices that can result from working under
and around dual-use installations are likely to be the most critical differences between

the control and treatments in the site trials. The detailed labor cost tracking tool was

Discussion of Results: Tasks 6 and 9 – Draft Choice Experiment, Collect Data,
and Develop Models for Economic Evaluation
Public Acceptance Choice Experiment Overview
We designed, fielded, and are analyzing data from a large-scale online discrete choice
experiment of 6,750 adult apple consumers across 37 temperate U.S. states with
commercial-scale apple production, as identified with USDA data. Respondents made
repeated binary choices about purchasing apples from a farm stand located on land
used for traditional apple production, agrivoltaics, utility-scale solar, or housing
development, versus not purchasing apples. Choice attributes also included the
presence of solar subsidies, the share of land-use revenues accruing to farmers, apple

                                           Page 26 of 56

price, and travel distance. The experiment incorporated randomized information
treatments about agrivoltaics and embedded incentivized and non-incentivized
elicitations of risk preferences and social preferences via a dictator game.
We estimate preferences using mixed logit models, including interactions with
demographics, attitudes, information treatments, behavioral measures, and respondent
rurality. The split-sample incentive design is used to test whether experimentally elicited
risk and social preferences, and the presence of real monetary incentives,
systematically affect acceptance of agrivoltaics and stated land-use tradeoffs. The
information treatments design is used to test whether positive or negative information
about this new technology impacts public acceptance of agrivoltaics.
This study contributes the first large-scale experimental valuation of agrivoltaics relative
to both the agricultural status quo and alternative land conversions in the U.S., explicitly
accounting for compensation structures, behavioral preferences, and rural–urban
heterogeneity. The findings can provide direct guidance for renewable energy siting,
agricultural land protection, and the design of community benefit agreements, policy
mechanisms, and subsidy programs, highlighting the need for place-sensitive policy
approaches when integrating renewable energy infrastructure into working agricultural
landscapes.
Apples were selected, even though they are not subject to the agrivoltaic research, as
they represent the second most purchased fruit in the U.S., thereby maximizing the
external validity of the experiment’s price mechanism. We are interested in public
perceptions of changes in land use from traditional agricultural food production to
agrivoltaics, utility-scale solar, or development. Agritourism represents a popular activity
in which the public actively engages with the current land use of agricultural food
production and grounds the experiment in respondents’ personal experiences.
The survey instrument consisted of the following components: screening, preference
elicitations, information treatments, a discrete choice experiment, and
attitudinal/policy/demographic questions.
 A. Screening Criteria
 Participants were required to meet several eligibility criteria prior to survey
 participation:
 • Be at least 18 years of age and provide informed consent.
 • Have purchased apples in the past or be likely to purchase apples in the future.
 • Have participated in agritourism in the past or indicate likelihood of future
 participation.
 • Pass an attention check question.
 • Reside in one of the 37 apple-producing states.
 B. Preference Elicitations
 Respondents participated in preference elicitations designed to measure risk and
 social preferences. Risk preferences were elicited using a standard gamble
 framework. Social preferences were elicited using a dictator game in which

                                        Page 27 of 56

respondents allocated funds between themselves and organizations representing
different policies or value positions.
The sample was randomly divided into two groups. One group received non-
incentivized elicitation tasks. The second group received incentivized tasks, where
20 percent of respondents were randomly selected to receive payment based on
one of their elicitation choices. This experimental variation was designed to test
whether incentivized preference elicitation affected subsequent acceptance of
agrivoltaic technology.
C. Utility Scale Solar and Agrivoltaic Information
Following preference elicitations, respondents were presented with neutral
informational prompts describing utility-scale solar development and agrivoltaics.
The descriptions were designed to avoid framing bias and did not favor any specific
development outcome.
Following the neutral/ descriptive information, we tested whether information
treatments regarding agrivoltaics impacted public acceptance of agrivoltaics. The
sample was randomly divided into five groups. The first group, the control, received
no additional information. The second group received positive information about
agrivoltaics only. The third group received negative information about agrivoltaics
only. The fourth and fifth groups received both positive and negative information, but
in different order (positive-then-negative, negative-then-positive).
D. Discrete Choice Experiment
Respondents completed eight discrete choice tasks. Each task presented three
development alternatives plus an opt-out option. Attribute levels were randomly
varied across respondents.
The experiment was designed to measure preferences for agrivoltaic development
attributes and to estimate willingness to pay and acceptance probabilities under
varying policy and development scenarios.
E. Attitudinal, Policy, Demographic Questions
Following the choice experiment, respondents answered additional questions related
to:
  • Attitudes toward solar panel placement.
  • Knowledge and perceptions of agrivoltaics.
  • Policy rankings and support for alternative development strategies.
  • Demographics
These measures allow for integration of behavioral, attitudinal, and preference-
based components within a unified econometric framework.

been accepted for a conference presentation at the Agricultural and Applied Economics
Association Annual Meeting in July 2026. This work completes EOP-B.

 A challenge for on-farm research is that you need a site that has really homogeneous

 sample every single area and then analyze all of your data relative to that individual

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
Site Trials – Vegetable and Hay Sites
Microclimate
Our experiments are an example of exceptional microclimate data collection. We
engineered simple but unique and effective microclimate monitoring stations. These
stations were removable to allow for field operations but were also stable enough to
withstand adverse weather and still provide good data collection. Although microclimate
measurements are not new, the extent of continuous, replicated, microclimate data
collection in our experiments allowed us to detect and characterize distinct differences
in zones only a few feet away from each other. This provided us with the data
necessary to understand primary abiotic drivers in agrivoltaic systems responsible for
biological crop responses. Our system contributes novelty to the field of microclimate
monitoring at large in addition to providing substantial new information to the field of
agrivoltaics, particularly in a temperate climate.
Although data has been statistically analyzed and graphed by the time of this report,
interpretation of that data remains in progress as we continue to prepare manuscripts.
We have identified hundreds of statistical differences in microclimate parameters.
Current work is parsing these differences into abiotic realities versus biologically
meaningful results. It is key to understand that just because an abiotic factor is
statistically significant, there is no transitive property to suggest it will be biologically
meaningful. For example, we have captured temperature reductions during the hottest
part of the day within parts of the array. The magnitude and duration of ambient
maximum temperature, compared to the magnitude and duration of a temperature
reduction in the array, interpreted in combination with species-specific physiology,
determines if that statistically significant temperature reduction (abiotic) is likely to drive
a physiological response in the crop (biologically meaningful).
Although the complete interpretation pathway remains complex, there are readily
accessible big picture conclusions and contributions:
 • The eastern, center, and western zones of an agrivoltaic bay with north-south
 orientation and single-axis tracking differ from the microclimate conditions of the
 control.
 • The eastern and western edge effects are not uniform; each edge is a distinct
 microclimate.
 • Both the eastern and the western edge differ from the microclimate in center of
 the bay. This verifies the presence of at least three unique microclimates within
 the array.
 • There are different magnitudes of variation between the eastern edge and the
 western edge relative to open-field conditions. The western edge experienced
 the most substantial variation in temperature and light.

                                         Page 38 of 56

 • The variation between the eastern and western edge occurs at different times of
 the day due to movement of the sun, leading to unique biological impacts.
 • The western edge experiences a substantial reduction to photosynthetically
 active radiation (PAR) intensity during peak daylight hours. In other words, the
 western edge gets less light that plants can use during the best light hours of the
 day compared to the eastern edge.
 • The western edge also experiences a temperature reduction during peak daylight
 hours. The negative consequences associated with reduced light were not clearly
 overcome by potentially beneficial consequences associated with reduced
 temperature in our study.
 • The array cools down faster than the control during sunset, but we detected
 slight, albeit statistically significant, heat retention within the array at night once
 the sun had gone down. The effect was more pronounced during cool weather
 and short days at the end of the growing season.
 • Careful data analysis was exceedingly critical. The heat retention effect was only
 statistically significant when looking at aggregated, season-long data. When the
 data was further analyzed by month, position, etc. the statistical significance was
 lost, though the trend remained visible in the graphed data.
 • It is possible to both over fractionate and under fractionate microclimate data on
 the temporal scale. Both can lead to misleading results and interpretations.
 Descriptive statistics alone, like mean, maximum, and minimum common in the
 literature, are inadequate to understand and explain agrivoltaic microclimates.
 Caution should be used when working with aggregated descriptive statistics that
 do not consider duration, magnitude, and biological relevance.
 o For example, a lettuce plant does not care if the daily average temperature
 is 60°F, with half the day being 55°F and the other half being 65°F. A lettuce
 plant does care if half of the day is 100°F and the other half is 20°F. A
 lettuce plant can also tolerate a brief exposure to these minimum and
 maximum extremes, but it is the duration of exposure that matters. Thus,
 min, max, and mean alone are insufficient metrics for assessment.
 o The continuous, replicated monitoring in this experiment provided not only
 novel empirical data, but also demonstrates the importance of the
 fundamental methodology that we established to measure, analyze, and
 interpret these systems.

Effect of Human Behavior
The abiotic array has clear effects on the abiotic microclimate. Subsequently, there are
effects on crop growth, a biotic response. Typically, it is our goal in agricultural science
to first isolate and understand these interactions so that we can evaluate the system,
and if appropriate, optimize the system. Next, the system is studied on farm where the
farmer’s management – equipment, knowledge, time, interest, etc. – will have an effect

                                         Page 39 of 56

on system performance. When the fundamental mechanisms are understood, we can
isolate and address the on-farm management challenges.
In the case of agrivoltaics, data from proper experiments as yet remains limited,
particularly in temperate climates. In this experiment, steps in the iterative research
process were skipped as a matter of necessity; the project called for research on
commercial sites under active production. In part to study active sites, in part because a
proper research site at scale simply did not exist. A resulting consequence is difficulty in
separating the abiotic effects induced by an array from the human effects of
management. Sometimes, human effects prevented data collection of the fundamental
system altogether.
While the data suggests light was the primary driver of crop responses, and
temperature remains an interesting consideration, we observed human behavior and
management challenges that also played a role. Examples include the following:
 • At two sites, farm operators were part-time growers and were not financially
 dependent on agricultural revenue. As a result, crop management intensity was
 far lower relative to the commercial vegetable site. This greatly impacted the crop
 data and even impacted our ability to collect microclimate data.
 • The two aforementioned farmers were also relatively novice. This made it even
 more difficult for them to manage the challenges of an advanced agrivoltaic
 system.
 • The combination of limited agricultural experience, limited time on-farm, and
 limited financial dependency on crop revenue was evident in yields, crop quality,
 weed pressure, and inconsistent fertility management.
 • Agrivoltaic systems may introduce additional management complexity relative to
 conventional production. Successful implementation may require experienced
 operators in addition to targeted technical support.
 • Our third and most successful research site was on a commercial vegetable
 farm. The farm is an experienced operation with a full staff. This farmer was well-
 prepared to work in an advanced, agrivoltaic system.
 • Harvest crews also demonstrated unique behavior. When harvesting sweet corn
 in 2025, we observed that the farm field crew did not pick approximately 6% or
 less of the corn in the control. Within the array, even in the best rows
 approximately 12% or more of the corn was not picked. In some rows, as much
 as 100% of the corn was not picked. It is unclear if the completely unpicked rows
 were due to the crew not believing it to be marketable or because the ears were
 physically very low to the ground compared to typical plants, making harvest
 much slower and more intense. The observed farm crew harvest decisions
 largely mirrored our own empirical assessments of marketable and non-
 marketable corn.

Yield
Our original hypothesis was plants with lower light saturation points will be fine, and
plants with higher light saturation points will demonstrate reduced performance.
 • As expected, broccoli and lettuce largely performed well in the array compared to
 open-field controls when the panels were tracking. In one year, before the panels
 were connected to the grid and tracking, the row below the dripline did
 experience substantial damage that led to no harvestable plants. This was due to
 water coming off the drip line and damaging young plants. Broccoli planted
 earlier in the same field, well before heavy rains, did not demonstrate this issue.
 In the second year, when panels were tracking, plants demonstrated a
 physiological disorder in the stems within the array that made them
 unmarketable. As a result, marketable yield in the array was much lower than the
 control. However, we cannot conclude that disorder was caused by the array
 itself; this may be an artifact in the data and a result of an unmeasured and as of
 yet unidentified underlying field condition. Therefore, on a fresh weight and dry
 weight basis, broccoli performed well within the array and produced more yield
 than the control – albeit unmarketable.
 • As expected, sweet corn demonstrated a variety of yield reductions when grown
 in the array.
 o Some plants formed only very small ears that were unmarketable.
 o On plants with fully formed ears, individual ears were smaller in the array
 compared to the control (length, diameter, weight).
 o Many ears in the array were unmarketable by USDA Grade 1 standards due
 to poor tip fill.
 o It is understood that there is crop loss due to the unplanted area below the
 array. This is presented as a tradeoff to dual purpose land use in exchange
 for energy production. However, crop loss was further exacerbated when
 planting corn. When there were 8 rows planted in the agrivoltaics bays, row
 1 and 8 (eastern most and western most in the bay) were almost 100%
 unmarketable. This was determined by both empirical evidence and farm
 crew harvest behavior. That is a 25% yield reduction on top of reduced yield
 per plant.
 o Yield reductions can be explained in part due to light available for
 photosynthesis and subsequent growth. However, we also observed
 phenological delays in the array, with variation relative to row. Corn is a
 wind-pollinated crop dependent on consistent pollen shed (male
 component) and receptive silks (female component) to result in ear
 development (offspring formed from sexual reproduction). If timing is
 misaligned or there is insufficient pollen for silks, tip fill of the ear is reduced.
  This should be monitored in other fruiting crops (corn is a fruit) as some crops
 are quite sensitive to light and temperature when forming and opening male and
 female flowers.

                                         Page 41 of 56

  In lettuce we eat the vegetative leaves and in broccoli we eat the immature flower
 head, so neither crop is sensitive to this particular phenomenon.
 • Although individual butternut squash plants did not show a yield penalty, the
 butternut squash population was very low at the associated research site due to
 challenges with planting and weed management. Plants vined into the areas with
 more sunlight. At full population, added competition due to sunlight seeking
 behavior could result in reduced yields; we would categorize our butternut results
 as inconclusive.

Technical Management
Challenges that remain, even for advanced growers, include:
 1. Weed management in the unfarmable area below the panels. Tillage is risky and
 technically challenging. Herbicides and/or physical labor are options for removal.
 2. Fertilizer application. At both vegetable sites, we observed unintentional fertilizer
 overlaps when the fertilizer was broadcast too widely and / or planted areas that
 received less fertilizer if the fertilizer was broadcast too narrowly and the tractor
 could not reach the edges. These are well-known technical challenges in open-
 field crop production (vegetables, corn, grain, etc.) with known solutions.
 However, some of those solutions do not apply in agrivoltaics (i.e. intentional
 broadcast overlap common in corn that results in a full dose of fertilizer and
 reduces the risk of any unfertilized areas). Different equipment or more time
 calibrating the spreader can solve this problem.
 3. Uneven crop maturation is a normal response to substantial difference in light
 availability, and it was observed in our studies. To maximize harvest of saleable
 produce, multiple harvests may be required as well as additional time scouting
 for ripe product. Both add time and technical challenges to harvest, limits options
 for mechanical harvest, and may result in additional crop loss. Alternatively, a
 less uniform crop may be harvested at once, but this would limit options for
 selling the product and could result in lower valuation of the composite harvest.
 4. Skilled operators are required when heavy equipment is used within the array to
 avoid accidental contact with the panels or posts. This may delay field operations
 in the array and impact crop performance.
 5. Crop selection remains the biggest question.
  Some generalizations can be made to identify good candidates (low light
 versus high light saturation points) but there is no certainty until a crop is
 tried. Individual array configuration will also impact system performance.
  Crop rotation is critical to break up disease and pest cycles so multiple plant
 families are needed for rotation. For example, Brassicaceae is a large family
 including broccoli, kale, cabbage, mustard greens, etc. It is reasonable to
 suspect these plants are worthwhile trying in agrivoltaic systems. However,
 they are not suitable for crop rotation purposes with one another because
 they are largely subject to the same pathogens and pests.
                                        Page 42 of 56

 • At one of our research sites, the field has Phytophthora, a soil pathogen
 with a wide host range (the organism is an oomycete; similar in nature to a
 fungus though not taxonomically so). Phytophthora is nearly impossible to
 eradicate once present and can persist in the soil for decades. At this site,
 crop rotation is already limited due to presence of this pathogen. So,
 existing site compatibility with crop rotation is a factor to consider.
  Field operations are a key consideration. Crops that require somewhat
 frequent spraying (plant growth regulators, foliar fertilizer applications,
 pesticides) and/or large equipment for management / harvest are risky to
 introduce to sites with array infrastructure that must be worked around.
 Barriers to managing high-need crops are anticipated to result in crop loss.

Bias
Experimental
The ongoing caveat with this field research is the confounding variable of spatial
location within the field. This was a known challenge since inception. Traditionally,
agronomy research is conducted using a randomized complete block design. Such
designs are intended to account for natural in-field variation; the impact is captured
within the random replication variable and is a part of the experimental error. On-farm
experiments typically employ a strip design because farmers are not apt to farm in
randomized complete block designs. Strip designs are not free from influence of
underlying spatial effects, but it is commonly seen as an appropriate compromise.
This agrivoltaics research is fundamentally unique in that this spatial confounding
variable must be accounted for in interpretation of both microclimate and yield data.
Corrective measures include statistical methodologies, like Bonferroni corrections that
make type 1 errors less likely (false positive). In this experiment, an example of a type 1
error would be declaring a yield reduction due to the solar panels if no such reduction
existed that is attributable to the array.
Having only three replications per year limits the degrees of freedom in the model,
decreasing the likelihood of a type 1 error, though increasing the likelihood of a type 2
error (false negative). In this experiment, an example of a type 2 error would be
reporting that the array did not have a cooling effect if it in fact did have a cooling effect
that was not detectable under the statistical power available in this study.
Robust data collection and careful analysis and interpretation – which operates with the
understanding of these challenges – allows for sound analysis that provides meaningful
data to the field. We are also able to dismiss the confounded variable if we choose to
compare the microclimate zones to one another rather than back to the control, which
we have also done. When doing so, we demonstrate that we do not require a control to
isolate unique microclimate zones and location-specific yield effects in the bay.

                                         Page 43 of 56

Human
Human factors are an inherent component of field-based, on-farm research. Maintaining
scientific integrity requires that data interpretation remain grounded in empirical
evidence, independent of stakeholder preferences or perceived system value.
Throughout this project, we encountered challenges related to differences in
perspective among various project participants. Agronomic research focuses on
quantifying biological and abiotic responses, whereas other stakeholders and project
contributors may prioritize economic, policy, or technology-development outcomes. At
times, distinctions between numerical results, biologically meaningful outcomes, and
broader value-based interpretations were difficult to separate in collaborative
discussions. For example, observed yield reductions represent empirical findings.
Whether such reductions are acceptable depends on economic, operational, or policy
context, which falls outside the scope of agronomic measurement. Our role was to
document system performance under observed conditions and clearly communicate
those results.
The agronomy team maintained a data-driven approach throughout the study.
Conclusions are supported by statistical analysis and observable field conditions.
Farmer management was considered as an experimental variable inherent to on-farm
research, similar to weather, rather than as a value judgment.
Lessons learned from this experience include:
 • Clearly defining roles and decision authority within multidisciplinary teams.
 • Establishing early agreement on research questions versus system-advocacy
 goals.
 • Maintaining consistent communication protocols to ensure interpretation remains
 grounded in empirical evidence.
 • Recognizing that stakeholder perspectives may differ, but preserving analytic
 independence is essential to research credibility.
Future projects would benefit from clearly articulated governance structures that protect
scientific independence while facilitating interdisciplinary collaboration.

Soil Health Findings
  1. Agrivoltaic effects were inconsistent across sites; agricultural systems and
 underlying site conditions are likely to be primary drivers.
 Across sites, relatively few soil health indicators exhibited statistically significant
 treatment across year interactions. Where interactions were detected,
 they indicated differing trajectories over time rather than differences
 between agrivoltaic and control plots. Existing field conditions and on-going field
 management practices are significant factors in these studies in addition to the
 simple presence or absence of an array.

                                          Page 44 of 56

  2. Soil moisture was a significant factor; microclimate heterogeneity within the array
 was detectable and reflected by soil respiration.
 Aggregate stability and respiration varied across bed locations, reflecting spatial
 heterogeneity in moisture distribution and microclimatic conditions within the
 array. Aggregate stability tended to be lower in drier bed positions and higher in
 locations receiving greater moisture inputs, such as near panel drip edges. Soil
 respiration followed a similar pattern, with higher values observed in wetter
 microsites and lower values in drier locations. These results indicate that micro-
 scale variation in moisture availability within the agrivoltaic system influenced soil
 biological and physical processes; wetter microsites consistently exhibited higher
 respiration rates than drier positions within the array.
  3. Soil physical indicators varied by site and management context.
 At vegetable sites, bulk density differed between agrivoltaic and control plots;
 however, no site exhibited a significant treatment × year interaction for bulk
 density, indicating no evidence of progressive compaction following installation. At
 the Hadley site, bulk density showed a significant treatment effect but did not
 change differentially over time, suggesting persistent site or management
 contrasts rather than ongoing infrastructure-driven compaction. At the Grafton
 site, bulk density showed a significant treatment effect, with bulk
 density trends over time best explained by contrasting tillage regimes.
  4. Early soil responses following agrivoltaic installation in vegetable systems may be
 subtle and overshadowed by annual management practices.
 At the Hadley site, where pre-construction data were available, most soil health
 indicators changed similarly over time in agrivoltaic and control plots. This
 suggests that, within intensively managed annual vegetable systems,
 short-term agrivoltaic effects on soil health may be difficult to detect relative to
 year-to-year environmental variability and management impacts.
  5. Context, scale, and time influence interpretations.
 Soil texture, crop system, baseline conditions, and management intensity strongly
 influenced soil responses to agrivoltaic systems. Whole plot treatment
 comparisons may obscure important within array processes driven by soil
 moisture and microclimate heterogeneity. Detecting agrivoltaic impacts on soil
 health – particularly physical properties – likely requires longer monitoring periods
 than are typical of short-term field studies.

Site Trials – Cranberry Sites
The cranberry component of this project included baseline construction-impact
assessment in 2023, comprehensive post-construction physiological, biochemical, yield,
fruit quality, and microclimate monitoring at the Ring Road ‘Howes’ site in 2024, and
multi-site yield and fruit quality assessment in 2025, along with continued microclimate
monitoring at Ring Road. Construction delays limited the number of sites available for
full post-installation monitoring in the early project period, and changes in the funding

                                       Page 45 of 56

landscape constrained the scope of physiological and biochemical monitoring in later
years.
Despite these constraints, the project generated one of the first multi-year datasets
linking microclimate modification under agrivoltaic systems with cranberry physiological
and biochemical performance, yield, and fruit quality responses. The results
demonstrated strong first-year sensitivity of cranberry to shading, followed by partial
acclimation under moderate shading in subsequent seasons. These findings reinforce
the importance of multi-year evaluation when assessing agrivoltaic compatibility in
perennial horticultural systems.
While moderate shading (30% and 35%) alleviated environmental stress, as reflected
by improved leaf water status and reduced electrolyte leakage, these same treatments
suppressed key physiological and metabolic functions, including photosynthetic activity,
carbon reserve accumulation, and the biosynthesis of stress-related osmolytes and
antioxidant compounds. Under higher shading (37%), these reductions were even more
pronounced, indicating a potential threshold beyond which the benefits of shading are
outweighed by declines in assimilated production and secondary metabolism.
In the 2024 agricultural year, yield, berry weight and fruit color declined significantly in
all agrivoltaic treatments compared with unshaded control. In the subsequent 2025
agricultural year, yield, berry weight, fruit color, total soluble solids (TSS) were not
significantly different in the lower agrivoltaic shading treatment of 30% compared with
the control, indicating crop acclimation to sustained partial shading. These results
showed that cranberry exhibits a pronounced first-year sensitivity to agrivoltaic shading
followed by partial physiological acclimation, emphasizing the importance of multi-year
evaluation when assessing agrivoltaic compatibility in perennial horticultural systems.

Significant Accomplishments and Lessons Learned
A key accomplishment of this work was the successful collection of continuous
microclimate data over two growing seasons (2024-2025) in an active commercial
cranberry bog under agrivoltaic infrastructure. Coupled with detailed physiological and
agronomic measurements in 2024 and yield and fruit quality assessments across two
sites in 2025, this dataset provides rare, field-based evidence of how agrivoltaic
systems modify the cranberry physiological and biochemical response and crop
performance.
While much of the existing agrivoltaics literature emphasizes general plant physiological
responses, this study extended beyond basic physiological metrics to include in-depth
biochemical indicators of stress response and metabolic adjustment. Measurements of
osmolyte accumulation, membrane stability, pigment composition, and antioxidant-
related compounds provided mechanistic insight into how cranberry plants respond to
altered light and microclimate conditions under solar arrays. This integrated
physiological and biochemical approach strengthens interpretation of observed yield
and fruit quality responses and contributes new knowledge relevant to perennial
specialty crop systems.

                                        Page 46 of 56

Major challenges included construction delays that constrained early site availability and
changes in the funding landscape that reduced the scope of multi-year physiological
monitoring. Lessons learned emphasize the importance of aligning construction
timelines with research plans, ensuring continuity of funding for multi-year studies in
perennial systems, and prioritizing microclimate monitoring as a core component of
agrivoltaic research to support interpretation of crop responses. The study also
underscored the logistical challenges of conducting detailed field-based physiological
and biochemical measurements in commercial production systems, emphasizing the
need for careful coordination with growers and flexibility in experimental design.

Economic Impacts and Public Perceptions
This section summarizes findings from the economic and observational components of
the project examining agrivoltaic systems and their implications for land use, farm
financial outcomes, and public acceptance.

Public Acceptance of Agrivoltaics
Results from the discrete choice experiment provide insight into public valuation of
agrivoltaic systems, particularly the transition from existing agricultural land use to
agrivoltaic production. The experiment compared agrivoltaic land use to alternative
development pathways, including conventional utility-scale solar and residential housing
development.
Preliminary analysis indicates substantial heterogeneity in preferences across
geographic populations. Respondents residing in rural areas—where agrivoltaic
installations would most likely be located—tend to view agrivoltaic development less
favorably relative to maintaining current agricultural land uses. In contrast, respondents
residing outside rural areas, who are more likely to encounter agrivoltaic installations
through travel or recreation, generally view agrivoltaics as a positive change relative to
existing agricultural land use.
These findings suggest that perceived benefits and costs of agrivoltaic deployment are
distributed unevenly across populations. Rural communities that host installations may
perceive agrivoltaics as a loss relative to traditional agricultural land use. As a result,
successful deployment strategies may require compensation mechanisms or policy
tools designed to address perceived local losses.

Limitations of Secondary Data for Economic Impact Analysis
The original research design proposed evaluating broader economic impacts of
agrivoltaic adoption using secondary datasets from the U.S. Department of Agriculture
(USDA). However, several factors limited the feasibility of this analysis.
First, project funding interruptions delayed access to several data sources and
constrained the ability to conduct the planned analyses within the project period.
Second, the structure of existing USDA renewable energy datasets does not allow clear

                                        Page 47 of 56

identification of agrivoltaic installations. Current datasets do not distinguish between
agrivoltaic arrays and conventional utility-scale solar arrays, nor do they distinguish
between arrays operated directly by farmers and those operated by external
developers. In addition, available datasets do not consistently differentiate between
utility-scale installations and smaller on-farm solar systems. These limitations make it
difficult to construct reliable datasets capable of estimating the economic impacts of
agrivoltaic deployment at regional or national scales.

Observational Evidence from On-Farm Trials
Although the project was unable to complete the originally proposed large-scale
economic impact modeling, the project generated a substantial body of observational
evidence from on-farm agrivoltaic trials. These observations provide insight into the
practical and financial implications of agricultural production beneath solar arrays.
Financial vs. Economic Impacts
It is important to distinguish between farm financial impacts and broader economic
impacts. Based on available observations, revenue from electricity generation
substantially exceeds potential revenue from crop production beneath agrivoltaic arrays.
Although contractual data between developers and farmers are not publicly available,
interviews and observational evidence suggest that energy revenues provide the
primary financial return from these systems. This creates a potential misalignment of
incentives. Once agrivoltaic arrays are installed and energy generation begins, the
financial incentive to maintain high levels of agricultural productivity beneath the arrays
may be limited.
While the farm financial revenues from energy production are likely to substantially
improve a farm operation’s overall financial standing, it appears that crop production
under agrivoltaic arrays in the field crop context are likely to significantly increase
production costs while reducing marketable yield. At a certain point, it is likely that the
cost of responsible crop production for the purpose of harvesting marketable yield will
be higher than the revenues from crop production. When the cost of food production is
higher than revenues from food production, there is no longer a profit motive for crop
production under agrivoltaics, and rational farmers will choose to minimize cost, which
will further reduce marketable yield. This is a reasonable path for a farmer to take, but it
is contradictory to the agrivoltaic concept, which is to co-produce both food and energy.
Aggregating multiple rational farm operators’ financial decisions to minimize cost of
production, we would expect that the aggregate food production under agrivoltaics is
reduced. The trade-off is that there will be additional renewable energy produced. The
aggregate economic impact is likely to be a reduction in food production, which will
impact supply chains and other market mechanisms, increasing per unit costs for area
farms and likely reducing the economic impact of food production. It was outside of the
scope of this study to estimate the trade-off between regional economic impacts of solar
energy and food production, but that would be the logical next step to estimate the
regional economic impact of shifting away from food production and into energy
production.

                                        Page 48 of 56

Crop Production Observations
Several crop trials were observed during the project period, including leafy greens, corn,
butternut squash, broccoli, and hay production. Outcomes varied substantially by crop
type and management conditions.
Butternut squash trials suggested that crop characteristics influenced outcomes. As a
vining crop, butternut squash plants tended to grow toward sunlight beyond the shaded
areas beneath solar arrays. As a result, fruit production beneath shaded portions of the
arrays was limited.
Corn production exhibited significant variation in pollination and maturation across
locations beneath the arrays. Uneven maturation required multiple harvest passes
through the field, increasing labor requirements substantially. In some cases, farmers
left portions of the crop unharvested.
Broccoli trials demonstrated that agronomic growth could occur beneath arrays, but
plant disorders reduced marketability. While the research design did not allow causal
attribution of loss of marketable yield to the agrivoltaic environment, the financial
outcome was a lack of marketable yield.
Hay Production and Equipment Constraints
Hay production trials highlighted several structural barriers associated with operating
beneath solar arrays. In the observed case, the landowner managing the site was not a
professional farmer and lacked experience in commercial hay production. Operating
beneath solar arrays required specialized equipment due to height restrictions imposed
by array structures. The landowner purchased specialized equipment at an estimated
cost of approximately $45,000. Such capital investments may present barriers for many
farm operators. In addition, harvesting decisions frequently favored areas outside the
array structures, particularly during wet conditions. These decisions resulted in hay
quality that did not meet typical commercial market standards.

Management and Behavioral Observations
Observations from vegetable production trials indicated deviations from typical
commercial farming practices in fertilization, weed management, and harvesting. In
several cases, fertilization was uneven and weed control was limited compared with
standard commercial production systems. These outcomes suggest that farmers may
invest less management effort beneath agrivoltaic arrays when energy production
revenue provides the primary economic return from the land.

9. Path Forward
This research project using on-farm site trials has provided significant results, and has
revealed lessons and opportunities for additional research. These lessons and
recommendations or organized below based on our on-farm research site trials and our
economic research.
On-Farm Site Trails – Lessons and Future Research Recommendations
Lessons
  • Experimental design and budget are complicated. There are many challenges
 to design experiments around undeveloped agrivoltaic sites. We would
 recommend selecting sites that are already constructed, because development is
 stop-and-start, often held up by building or permitting delays. It is difficult
 to communicate with developers, take pre-construction samples, manage budget
 periods, and manage hiring of support staff. It’s also very difficult to create an
 experimental design and appropriate budget for a site that does not yet exist.
  • Farmer selection is crucial to obtain data that is of value. Research should
 prioritize experienced farmers whose interest is primarily agronomic, as opposed to
 farmers who are primarily motivated by agricultural adders and solar
 payments. Work should focus on sites that have been in agricultural production
 previously, with well-managed soils of appropriate pH and nutrient
 concentrations. It’s hard to interpret data from fields that are not productive to start
 with. Ideally farmers would have control over their on-farm operations, so that field
 activities occur on time and with sufficient communication.

                                       Page 50 of 56

• Broad and active project management is required. It is complicated to navigate
  experimental design, competing farmer needs, and scientific rigor. Projects may
  have multiple parties, intermediaries, and numerous participants. Active project
  management is needed to coordinate between numerous researchers, solar
  developers, consultants, legal teams, farm owners, and field managers.
• Adequate controls are necessary. Absence of adequate controls should be a deal
  breaker. Construction activities and fence perimeter limit that amount of area left
  for control. A large enough area is required to take a sufficient number
  of samples. There should also be a robust plan for pre-construction sampling,
  which is often timing-dependent or stop and start.
• Stipends should be included for farmer participation. It takes a lot of time for each
  farmer to communicate with researchers and to collect data. Engagement should
  be rewarded.
• Soil texture affects everything. For example, dry sandy soils won’t perform well
  during excessive heat, regardless of influence of agrivoltaics. From the outset of
  the experiment, soil texture should be considered.
• Soil preparation is required prior to production. There may be a need to assess
  interference of rocks, plan for rock picking in cultivated beds, and leave time for
  adjusting soil pH. Further, soil health changes occur over longer periods of time,
  so it’s not effective to do 2-year study.
• There are different motivations between solar developers compared to
  researchers. Tension arises in these multi-partner projects when some parties are
  promoting agrivoltaics success when researchers must remain neutral and wait for
  data to inform their conclusions. Some farmers are primarily participating in this
  research because it’s a contract requirement, and not because they want to or
  have the capacity.
• Refine MDAR’s Dual Use Shading Tool (link). This tool currently does not consider
  slope. Grade does affect shadow length, so further study should refine this tool.
  Additionally, it should validate PAR predictions with actual field data.
• Study the differences between different array configurations. Each site has one
  spacing and array type, which makes it difficult to compare sites. Dual use solar is
  not a singular treatment: there are fixed arrays, tracking arrays, and a wide
  variation in spacing. Regional differences in soil texture, crop requirements,
  microclimates make it very difficult to interpret data to recommend new
  technology and design on additional sites.
• Wireless sensors vastly increased overall costs and logistics. Large amounts of
  data need to be budgeted for in terms of data management and analysis.
• Construction of sensors and stands. Microclimate sensors are usually designed to
  be permanently installed, but in annual cropping systems or rotational grazing, this
  is not possible. Removable sensors and stands took a lot of time to transport,
  install, remove temporarily, re-install, and store over winter.

                                    Page 51 of 56

  Future Research Recommendations
  • While not biologically relevant in our experiments, the slight heat retention within
 the array overnight would be interesting to explore during cold months in perennial
 / overwintering crops. Any anticipated yield reductions due to reduced light during
 the growing season would have to be weighed against the potential to mitigate cold
 damage during extreme cold events.
  • Broccoli performance was clouded by unique field incidents. One being runoff from
 the dripline of non-tracking panels, the other being an unidentified physiological
 issue that we cannot confidently attribute to the array itself. Future work should
 continue to monitor damage to plants below the dripline and evaluate the
 associated risk. This may impact planting / transplanting timing decisions when
 heavy rains are expected. Additionally, work should continue to monitor for
 physiological disorders that result in unmarketability. At this time, we cannot
 conclude if the array was causal or is simply a red herring. It is also possible that
 reduced temperatures were beneficial for broccoli yield, but because of the
 physiological disorder we cannot make this claim based on this research.
  • Next steps should seek to identify more crops that are largely non-responsive or do
 not negatively respond to the three distinct microclimates. Crop rotation must be
 considered to break pest and disease cycles in order for the system to be viable.
  • In the unplanted area below the panels, where weeds can be challenging to
 manage, pollinator or cover crops are interesting options for further study,
 especially perennials.

Economic Research – Future Research Recommendations
Overall, the project findings suggest that agrivoltaic systems introduce both
opportunities and challenges for agricultural production. While energy generation may
provide financial benefits, maintaining financially viable agricultural production beneath
arrays likely requires additional research, improved system design, and clearer policy
incentives and enforcement mechanisms.
Future research should prioritize controlled on-farm experimental trials designed to
identify best management practices for crop production under solar arrays, and to
prioritize Agricultural Extension curriculum for grower best practices in agrivoltaic
systems, as well as policy standards and enforcement mechanisms for agencies tasked
with implementing agrivoltaic incentives. Coordinated research efforts currently
underway in states including New Jersey and New York may provide valuable insights
for optimizing agrivoltaic systems.
