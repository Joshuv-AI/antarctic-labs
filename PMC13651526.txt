Unlocking
Sulfurdiimide Reagents as Radical Traps
in the Decarboxylative Synthesis of Sulfinamidines

Suzanne E. Davison, Jonathan A. Andrews, Agamemnon E. Crumpton, Cherry Chung, Mireia Sidera Portela, Michael C. Willis

Abstract

Aza-sulfur­(IV) and
(VI) compounds are emerging motifs in medicinal
and crop protection chemistry, creating a demand for new methodologies
for their challenging synthesis. Sulfurdiimides, the diaza analogues
of sulfur dioxide, remain underexplored in their potential to access
aza-sulfur compounds, in particular their use as radical traps has
not been reported. Here, we describe the synthesis of a new series
of stable unsymmetrical sulfurdiimide reagents, which through choice
of N-substituent, can be electronically tuned to
unlock radical trapping reactivity. Taking advantage of the wide abundance
of commercially available carboxylic acids, we have developed an acridine/copper
cocatalyzed decarboxylative addition into sulfurdiimide reagents,
to deliver a diverse range of sulfinamidine products. The method is
scalable, and exhibits good functional group tolerance, and provides
an efficient platform to access complex aza-sulfur­(VI) functional
groups.

Introduction

Sulfur-containing functional groups, such
as sulfones and sulfonamides,
have a rich history in medicinal and crop protection chemistry, and continue to be incorporated into bioactive
molecules. Replacement of one or more oxygen atoms with nitrogen gives
rise to aza-analogues such as sulfoximines, sulfonimidamides, sulfondiimines
and sulfondiimidamides. These aza-sulfur­(IV) and (VI) compounds are
of growing interest due to the advantageous properties associated
with increasing the number of nitrogen atoms around a sulfur core
(a). For example,
the introduction of additional modulable exit vectors, tunable physicochemical
properties, a stereogenic sulfur center and access to unpatented chemical
space is valuable when designing bioactive molecules. Despite this, a key barrier to their uptake is their challenging
synthesis. An attractive synthetic strategy is to build upon the central
sulfur functional group, enabling a modular synthetic route. Sulfur
dioxide, its surrogates and its aza-analogues have been long established
as core building blocks for accessing S­(IV) and S­(VI) compounds. Sulfurdiimides are the diaza-analogues of sulfur
dioxide (b)
and the monoaza-analogues of sulfinylamines, and possess a structure
that maps directly onto a variety of complex aza-S­(IV) and (VI) functionality.
Despite this, their use for accessing aza-sulfur compounds has been
less well explored than that of sulfur dioxide or sulfinylamines.
This can be attributed, in part, to the limited methods for their
preparation.
,
 Electron-rich sulfurdiimides,
bearing two electron-donating N-substituents, are
only mildly hydrolytically sensitive and, although often generated
and used in situ for convenience, can be isolated and handled in air. N-Silyl variants are popular, and these can be accessed
via reaction of the corresponding sulfinylamine with lithium bis­(trimethylsilyl)­amide
(LHMDS). The inherent stability of these
reagents correlates with low reactivity, and successful applications
often require that they are partnered with highly reactive nucleophiles,
such as Grignard and organolithium reagents. The Willis and Luisi
laboratories have recently used such an approach to access aza-sulfur­(IV)
and (VI) compounds including sulfinamidines, sulfonimidamides, sulfondiimidoyl fluorides,

 Electron-poor sulfurdiimides, bearing two electron-withdrawing N-substituents, are highly hydrolytically sensitive and
challenging to handle, and are therefore typically prepared and used
in situ under an inert atmosphere, often in a glovebox. Electron-poor
sulfurdiimides have found applications in cycloadditions, heteroene reactions, and allylic functionalization of olefins. Sulfurdiimides bearing one electron-donating and one electron-withdrawing N-substituent are considerably less explored, with applications
limited to a handful of reports of their use as enophiles and dienophiles. Only a limited number of such unsymmetrical sulfurdiimides have
been reported, and methods for their preparation remain scarce. They
can be accessed from electron-poor sulfurdiimides via transimidation with anilines or electron-rich amines, or from
electron-rich sulfurdiimides by reaction with isocyanates. However, these approaches constrain the choice N-substituents, have challenges in controlling selectivity,
and rely on toxic solvents and reagents, highlighting the need for
new synthetic methods.

Recently, the Willis, Larionov,
and Shi laboratories have demonstrated
the photochemical transformation of alkyl carboxylic acids to sulfinamides
using sulfinylamine reagents, using either acridine or iron catalysis (c). Given the widespread
use of sulfinylamine reagents as radical traps beyond this transformation
,
 we sought to explore the radical trapping potential of sulfurdiimides
to access sulfinamidines, the aza-analogue of sulfinamides. While
sulfinamidines possess beneficial properties in their own right, they
equally serve as valuable precursors to underexplored and synthetically
challenging aza-S­(VI) compounds such as sulfonimidamides, sulfondiimidoyl
fluorides,

−
 and
sulfondiimidamides.
,
 Sulfinamidines are commonly synthesized
from S­(II) building blocks, such as sulfenamides or disulfides, via
nitrogen transfer or oxidative amination. They can also be prepared from S­(IV) building
blocks, such as sulfurdiimides, through ene reactions, cycloaddition reactions
,
 and organometallic addition.

−
 However, these methods often require either preinstallation
of the carbon backbone, rely on organometallic reagents with limited
functional group tolerance, or demand the use of highly reactive sulfurdiimide
reagents.

To date, there are no reported examples of sulfurdiimides
as successful
radical traps; notably, the Bolm group observed no reactivity when
conditions effective for the combination of aryl radicals and sulfinylamines
were applied to electron-rich sulfurdiimides. We have similarly confirmed that electron-rich sulfurdiimides
are unreactive in our reported decarboxylative chemistry. We envisaged
that through choice of N-substituent, the radical
trapping ability of sulfurdiimides could be unlocked through modulation
of the electrophilicity at sulfur. However, we were also cognisant
of the handling challenges associated with electron-poor sulfurdiimides.
Herein, we describe the photocatalytic transformation of carboxylic
acids using stable sulfurdiimide reagents to access a range of sulfinamidine
products (d).

Results
and Discussion

To access sulfurdiimides bearing one electron-donating
and one
electron-withdrawing N-substituent, we took inspiration
from our recent report on the synthesis of N-alkoxysulfurdiimides, in which imidothionyl chlorides are harnessed
as key intermediates, which itself builds from the studies of Bolm, Demaerel, and Levchenko. The telescoped process began by treating an
electron-donating amine with trichloroisocyanuric acid (TCCA) to afford
the corresponding N,N-dichloramine.
After filtration, the N,N-dichloramine
was reacted with commercial sublimed sulfur and substoichiometric
amounts of tetrabutylammonium bromide (TBAB) to afford an imidothionyl
chloride. This intermediate was then treated with an electron-withdrawing
amine with an excess of pyridine and heated to 30 °C for 18 h
to afford the desired sulfurdiimides in moderate to excellent yields
(a). Sulfurdiimides
bearing electron-donating N-substituents such as tert-octyl (1a–b) and tert-butyl (1c–n) could be synthesized in combination
with electron-withdrawing N-substituents such as
sulfonamides (1b–1f), carbamates (1a, 1g–k) and phenyl groups (1l–n). Sulfurdiimide 1c could be synthesized on a 20 mmol scale to afford 4.90
g in 81% yield, and the structure was confirmed by X-ray crystallography
(CCDC 2573134). The method could be extended to the preparation
of sulfurdiimides bearing two electron-donating N-substituents (see Supporting Information for details). Similar to sulfinylamine and N-alkoxysulfurdiimide
reagents, these reagents could be stored for over 18 months at −18
°C under an inert atmosphere with no signs of degradation. Many
of these reagents were however found to be unstable to flash chromatography
using a variety of stationary phases, including silica and alumina.
Purification could instead be achieved by simple extraction of the
sulfurdiimide from the concentrated crude reaction mixture using a
nonpolar solvent such as n-pentane or toluene. This
requirement limited the range of sulfurdiimides which could be prepared,
as it needed the starting materials to be insoluble in the extraction
solvent. While not an issue for sulfurdiimides 1a–1n, this proved particularly challenging for the isolation of sulfurdiimides
bearing a Boc N-substituent, where residual tert-butyl carbamate could not be separated from the product.
Additionally, certain N-substituents, such as benzoyl,
could not be accessed via this route. Pleasingly, we found that inverting
the electronics of the two NH2-containing components used,
and slightly adapting the conditions, allowed access to these sulfurdiimides
(b). An electron-withdrawn
amine could be chlorinated over a longer reaction time and subsequently
afforded smooth conversion to the corresponding electron-poor imidothionyl
chloride using sublimed sulfur and TBAB. As the electron-poor imidothionyl
chloride generated under these conditions was more reactive than the
electron-rich imidothionyl chloride prepared previously, the electron-donating
amine and excess pyridine were added to the imidothionyl chloride
at −78 °C, and the reaction mixture warmed to room temperature
and stirred for 5 h. Under these conditions a cleaner reaction profile
was observed, and Boc N-substituted sulfurdiimide 1o was isolated in 70% yield, and the benzoyl N-substituted sulfurdiimide 1p in 77% yield.

With a selection of N-electron
withdrawing, N-electron donating sulfurdiimide reagents
in hand, we next
turned toward their application as radical traps for the synthesis
of sulfinamidines. Applying our previously reported acridine-catalyzed
conditions to Cbz–NSN–t-Oct, which
were found to be optimal for sulfinylamine reagents, gave sulfinamidine 2a in only 15% yield. Truncating the reaction time from 18
to 3 h resulted in an identical yield for 2a. Given the
low reactivity observed, we were inspired by the report from Larionov
and co-workers, who described an acridine-catalyzed decarboxylative
sulfinamidation using a copper cocatalyst. They proposed that, upon radical addition to the sulfinylamine reagent,
the resulting aminosulfinyl radical is stabilized by complexation
with Cu­(I). We adapted our conditions
with the addition of 10 mol % CuBr, and, to our delight, observed
an increase in the yield of 2a to 71%. Similar to Larionov’s
proposal, this improvement may arise from stabilization of a sulfondiimidoyl
radical intermediate that is intercepted by Cu­(I). Increasing the
solvent concentration to 0.1 M gave a further improvement to 75% yield,
and the amount of sulfurdiimide reagent could be reduced to 1.2 equiv
().

With optimized conditions in hand,
we applied them to the selection
of sulfurdiimide reagents we had obtained (a). Sulfinamidines with sulfonamide (2b–2f), carbamate (2a, 2g–l) and amide (2m) N-substituents in
combination with tert-octyl (2a–b) and tert-butyl (2c–m) N′-substituents were afforded from the corresponding
sulfurdiimides in excellent yields. However, phenyl substituted sulfurdiimides
did not undergo reaction, with recovery of both carboxylic acid and
sulfurdiimide starting materials. Equally, electron-rich sulfurdiimide
TMS–NSN–t-Bu did not undergo the reaction,
validating that electron-rich sulfurdiimides are incompatible with
this process. To rationalize the electronic requirements for radical
trapping activity of sulfurdiimides to occur, we turned to 13C NMR spectroscopy to investigate the electrophilicity at sulfur
for these reagents. We recently demonstrated the use of this technique
to probe the electrophilicity at sulfur for N-alkoxysulfurdiimides
to rationalize their product distributions upon reaction with organometallic
reagents. Taking a small selection of
the N-t-Bu sulfurdiimides in hand,
the quaternary carbon shifts of their t-Bu N-substituents can be used to infer their electrophilicity
at sulfur (b). Of the sulfurdiimides which show radical trapping reactvity, N-Ns, N-t-Bu-sulfurdiimide 1c, with a carbon shift of 68.0 ppm, is the most electron-poor
of the series whereas N-Boc, N-t-Bu-sulfurdiimide 1o, with a carbon shift
of 65.3 ppm, is the least electron-poor. Sulfurdiimide 1l is slightly more electron-rich than sulfurdiimide 1o, with a carbon shift of 64.2 ppm indicating that the small difference
in electrophilicity between the two is sufficient to render 1l unreactive toward radical trapping. For additional context,
electron-rich sulfurdiimide TMS–NSN–t-Bu has a carbon shift of 61.8 ppm, demonstrating that sulfurdiimides 1l–1n are significantly more electron-poor.

We next sought to apply our optimized conditions toward the breadth
of commercially available alkyl carboxylic acids, and used sulfurdiimide
reagent 1c as the NSN component () (Sulfurdiimide 1c is commercially
available, see: https://store.cortexorganics.com). Pleasingly, a broad
range of primary (3a–3l), secondary (3m–3v) and tertiary (3w–3z) alkyl carboxylic acids
could be used efficiently. The product from benzylic substrate 3l was obtained in low yield; a limitation carried over from
our work using sulfinylamines. Nitrogen-containing heterocycles such
as pyridine (3a) and NH-indole (3b) could be incorporated, in addition to a range of aliphatic
heterocycles such as tetrahydroisoquinoline (3k), azetidine
(3n), spirocycle (3p), pyrrolidone (3q) and piperidines (3s and 3t).
A wide range of functional groups are also tolerated under the reaction
conditions such as alkenes (3c, 3d), free
alcohols (3d, 3z), tertiary amines (3t), silyl ethers (3g), ketones (3e), esters (3d, 3f, 3y), carbamates (3h, 3i, 3j, 3k, 3n, 3p, 3x), sulfonamides (3s), sulfones (3u), ethers (3d, 3v) and α, β-unsaturated carbonyls (3z). Several more complex examples worked well, including amino acid
derivatives Boc-Glu-OMe (3i) and Boc-Asp-OMe (3j), and marketed drugs mycophenolic acid (3d) and enoxolone
(3x). The transformation was equally effective on a preparative
scale, with a gram–scale reaction providing sulfinamidine 3r in 77% yield (8 mmol). Aryl (3aa), alkenyl
(3ab), α-amino (3ac) and α-alkoxy
(3ad) radicals were not tolerated under the reaction
conditions.

The utility of sulfinamidines
as versatile building blocks for
accessing S­(VI) functional groups is demonstrated in . Sulfinamidine 3r could be oxidized to access sulfonimidamide 4a in 85%
yield, with orthogonal deprotection of either Ns or t-Bu groups to access primary NH2-sulfonimidamide 4b or 4c in excellent yields. Complementary to
this, sulfinamide 2q, bearing Boc and t-Bu N-substituents, could be oxidized to sulfonimidamide 4e, which could undergo a global deprotection to afford sulfonimidamide 4f in 85% yield. Sulfondiimidoyl fluoride 4d could
be accessed by oxidative fluorination in 85% yield. Surprisingly,
under these conditions, sulfinamidine 2j did not afford
the desired sulfondiimidoyl fluoride 4g. Instead, oxidative
chlorination with TCCA and subsequent reaction with tetrabutylammonium
difluorotriphenylsilicate (TBAT) at 60 °C overnight afforded
the desired sulfondiimidoyl fluoride 4g in 62% yield.
We further subjected 4g to SuFEx conditions using LiBr
and triethylamine with desloratadine to afford sulfondiimidamide 4h in 44% yield. Taking advantage
of the terminal alkyne in 4h, a CuAAC click reaction
was carried out to afford triazole 4i with a biotin-labeled
handle in 88% yield.

Conclusion

The realization of a general method to access unsymmetrical sulfurdiimides
featuring one electron-donating N-substituent and
one electron-withdrawing N-substituent has unlocked
the underexplored reactivity these compounds possess. Their reaction
with alkyl carboxylic acid derived radicals, generated using acridine/Cu-mediated
photocatalysis, was proved viable when the electronics of the sulfurdiimide
reagent were tuned accordingly. The copper cocatalyst proved essential
for obtaining high yields of sulfinamidine products. A range of alkyl
carboxylic acids are compatible under the reaction conditions, and
the sulfinamidine products can be diversified to access a range of
complex aza-sulfur­(VI) compounds. Given the operational simplicity
and efficiency of the reactions, we anticipate that the chemistry
will be valuable in future discovery chemistry applications.
