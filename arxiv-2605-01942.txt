Combinatorial Analysis of Dyadic and Quasi-Dyadic Codes
Anthony Gómez-Fonseca, Gretchen L. Matthews, Kirsten D. Morris, Tefjol Pllaha

Abstract
Quantum low-density parity-check (QLDPC) codes offer a promising route to scalable fault-tolerant quantum computation, but their performance under iterative decoding is strongly influenced by short-cycle structure and other harmful subgraphs in the associated Tanner graphs. This paper develops an algebraic framework for constructing and analyzing (Q)LDPC codes from dyadic and quasi-dyadic matrices—translation-invariant 2 ℓ × 2 ℓ 2^{\ell}\times 2^{\ell} binary matrices specified compactly by a signature row and forming a commutative ring with recursive block structure. Leveraging this structure, we relate cycles in lifted Tanner graphs to tailless backtrackless closed walks in the protograph and derive efficient, implementable methods to enumerate and control short cycles (notably 4 4 -, 6 6 -, and 8 8 -cycles). We introduce dyadic-aware PEG-style construction algorithms that use forbidden sets of shifts to maximize attainable girth when possible and otherwise minimize the multiplicity of the shortest cycles at the target girth. Motivated by error-floor phenomena, we further characterize and explicitly enumerate absorbing sets in key dyadic layout boundary cases, identifying configurations that induce abundant ( a , 0 ) (a,0) -absorbing sets. Finally, we propose CSS-oriented dyadic constructions that satisfy commutation constraints by design and demonstrate via belief-propagation simulations that reducing short-cycle multiplicity can yield substantial decoding gains even when girth cannot be increased.

Introduction

Quantum error correction is a central prerequisite for scalable quantum computing, and the current momentum around
quantum low-density parity-check
(QLDPC) codes reflects a broader push toward code families that combine strong protection with implementable, sparse stabilizer measurements. In the CSS framework, this reduces to constructing sparse parity-check matrices
H_{X}
and
H_{Z}
that satisfy the commutation constraint
H_{\sf Z}H_{\sf X}^{\top}=0
while still producing Tanner graphs with favorable combinatorial structure—especially large girth and controlled populations of short cycles. Short cycles are well known to degrade iterative decoding and to raise the error floor; moreover, in quantum settings, they can exacerbate syndrome-based iterative decoder failures through small problematic substructures
[
8
,
26
]
.

In this work we study—and exploit—the algebraic structure of
dyadic
and
quasi-dyadic
matrices to build and analyze families of (Q)LDPC codes. A dyadic matrix
D\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{N\times N}
(with
N=2^{\ell}
) is determined by a
signature
row
\sigma(D)
via the translation-invariant rule
D_{{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}}=D_{{\bf 0},{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{y}}}
, yielding a commutative ring with strong recursive block structure. This dyadic framework provides two key advantages for code design: (i) the lifting factor is naturally a power of two, and (ii) products and sums of dyadic permutation matrices encode walk composition in lifted Tanner graphs through simple “permutation shifts.” These features make dyadics an appealing substrate for protograph-based constructions and for CSS constraints, while enabling a level of cycle accounting that is typically costly for unstructured LDPC ensembles
[
14
,
22
]
.

Our first contribution is a
cycle and girth analysis toolkit
specialized to dyadic and quasi-dyadic lifts. We connect cycles in the lifted Tanner graph to tailless backtrackless closed (TBC) walks in the protograph and show how powers of the (block) adjacency matrix enumerate protograph walks whose permutation-shift labels determine whether they lift to genuine cycles. Building on this correspondence, we derive efficient counting strategies for short cycles—starting with explicit, implementable conditions and formulas for enumerating
4
-cycles in both single-dyadic and protograph-based quasi-dyadic constructions, and extending the analysis to
6
- and
8
-cycles. These counts are not merely descriptive: they become design constraints and objective functions for construction and optimization
[
10
,
11
]
.

Second, we introduce
dyadic-aware PEG-style construction methods
that leverage “forbidden sets” of permutation shifts to avoid (or delay) the formation of short cycles during matrix assembly. Because choosing a single
1
in a dyadic signature row induces an entire dyadic permutation block, dyadic lifting changes the effective granularity of edge placement—and this, in turn, allows a substantial reduction in construction complexity compared to applying PEG directly to the full lifted Tanner graph. We present practical algorithms that (i) maximize attainable girth when possible and (ii) further
minimize the number of short cycles
at the target girth when avoidance is no longer feasible
[
16
,
10
]
.

Third, motivated by the role of harmful substructures in iterative decoding, we analyze
absorbing sets
in Tanner graphs arising from dyadic layouts. We characterize boundary cases (e.g.,
m\times 1
and
1\times n
arrays of dyadic permutations) and identify configurations that yield disconnected unions of complete bipartite components, for which absorbing sets can be enumerated explicitly. This clarifies when dyadic structure may inadvertently introduce an abundance of
(a,0)
-absorbing sets and when certain layouts can be ruled out due to unavoidable problematic combinatorics
[
8
,
24
]
.

Finally, we propose and evaluate CSS-oriented
code constructions from dyadic permutations
that naturally satisfy commutation constraints through structured pairing and permutation choices, and we compare them to recent quasi-dyadic CSS constructions whose Tanner graphs inevitably have girth four. Using belief-propagation simulations as a proof of concept, we demonstrate that even when girth cannot be increased, reducing the multiplicity of the shortest cycles can yield substantial decoding gains; when larger girth is achievable, combining girth control with short-cycle minimization improves performance further
[
3
,
18
]
.

Notation.
All vectors will be row vectors and will be denoted with bold letters. Matrices will be denoted with italic capital letters. The all-zero and all-one vectors in
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n}
will be denoted
{\bf 0}_{n}
and
{\bf 1}_{n}
, respectively.
The zero matrix and the all-one matrix will be denoted
\text{0}_{n}
and
\text{1}_{n}
, respectively. The subscript will be dropped when the context is clear.
The row space and the (right) kernel of a matrix
A
will be denoted
{\sf rs}(A)
and
{\sf ker}(A)
, respectively.
The space of binary
m\times n
matrices is denoted by
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{m\times n}
. Given
A\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{m\times n}
, the entry of
A
in Column
i
and Row
j
is denoted by
A_{i,j}
. The transpose of
A
is denoted by
A^{\top}
.
We will use the notation
[n:m]:=\{n,n+1,\ldots,m\}
and
[n]:=[1:n]
.

A binary linear
[n,k]
code
{\mathcal{C}}
is a
k
-dimensional subspace of
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n}
. We will use the term code to mean binary linear code. The parameter
n
is called the length of the code.
The support of a vector
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n}
is
{\sf supp}({\mathbf{x}}):=\{i\in[n]\mid x_{i}\neq 0\}
.
The Hamming weight of a vector
{\mathbf{x}}
is
{\sf wt}({\mathbf{x}}):=|{\sf supp}({\mathbf{x}})|
, and the Hamming distance of
{\mathcal{C}}
is
d({\mathcal{C}})={\sf min}\{{\sf wt}({\mathbf{x}})\mid{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}},{\mathbf{x}}\neq{\bf 0}_{n}\}
.
If the code has Hamming distance
d
, then it is called an
[n,k,d]
code.
For a code
{\mathcal{C}}
, a matrix
M
is called a generator matrix if
{\sf rs}(M)={\mathcal{C}}
and a parity-check matrix if
{\sf ker}(M)={\mathcal{C}}
. Note here that we require neither generator matrices nor parity-check matrices to be full rank. The dual of
{\mathcal{C}}
is defined as
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}:=\{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n}\mid{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{c}}^{\top}=0,\text{ for all }{\mathbf{c}}\in{\mathcal{C}}\}
.
We have that
{\sf dim}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})=n-k
. By the definition, it follows that a generator matrix of a code is a parity-check matrix of its dual and vice-versa, that is,
{\mathcal{C}}={\sf rs}(G)={\sf ker}(H)
if and only if
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}={\sf rs}(H)={\sf ker}(G)
.
A code
{\mathcal{C}}
is called self-orthogonal (resp., self-dual) if
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
(resp.,
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
). Note that for self-dual codes we necessarily have
k=n/2
and self-dual codes of odd length cannot exist. Analogously, a code
{\mathcal{C}}
is called dual-containing if
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}
.
Since
({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})^{\perp}={\mathcal{C}}
, we have that
{\mathcal{C}}
is self-orthogonal if and only if
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
is dual-containing.
The focus of this paper is
low-density parity-check codes
(LDPC), that is, codes
{\mathcal{C}}={\sf ker}(H)
where
H
is a sparse matrix.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Dyadic Matrices

Let
\ell
be a natural number, and let
N=2^{\ell}
. We will consider
N\times N
binary matrices whose rows and columns will be indexed using elements of
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{\ell}
via the bijection

{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{\ell})\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{\ell}\leftrightarrow x=1+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}x_{i}2^{i-1}\in[N].

(1)

Given
M\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{N\times N}
,
under this bijection, the elements of
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{\ell-1}\times\{0\}
index the “left” half of columns and “top” half of rows, meaning columns
[N/2]
and rows
[N/2]
of
M
, whereas the elements of
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{\ell-1}\times\{1\}
index the “right” half of columns and the “bottom” half of rows, meaning columns
[N/2+1:N]
and Rows
[N/2+1:N]
of
M
.

Definition 2.1
.

A matrix
D\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{N\times N}
is called a
dyadic matrix
if its entries satisfy
D_{{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}}=D_{{\bf 0},{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{y}}}
for all
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{\ell}
.
The
signature
of a dyadic matrix
D
, denoted
\sigma(D)
, is the first row
D_{{\bf 0},\bullet}
of
D
. For
{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{N}
, we will denote by
D_{\mathbf{v}}
the corresponding dyadic matrix with signature
{\mathbf{v}}
, that is,
\sigma(D_{\mathbf{v}})={\mathbf{v}}
.
A
quasi-dyadic matrix
is a block matrix where its blocks are dyadic matrices.

It is well-known
[
14
]
that the collection of
N\times N
dyadic matrices is isomorphic to the polynomial ring
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}]/\langle x_{1}^{2}+1,\ldots,x_{\ell}^{2}+1\rangle
. This in turn immediately implies that the set of
N\times N
dyadic matrices forms a commutative ring, which we will denote
{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}
.
Beyond commutativity, we list some additional elementary properties below; see
[
22
]
and the references therein.

Proposition 2.2
.

For
N=2^{\ell}
, the following hold.

(1)

The set of
N\times N
dyadic matrices is

{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
B&A\end{bmatrix}\middle|A,B\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell-1}\right\}

where
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}=\left\{\left[0\right],\left[1\right]\right\}
, and
\mid{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}\mid=2^{N}
.

(2)

Dyadic matrices are symmetric.

(3)

The zero matrix and the identity matrix in
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{N\times N}
are dyadic.

(4)

Let
D\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}
. Then,
D^{2}=\text{0}_{N}
if
{\sf wt}(\sigma(D))
is even, and
D^{2}=I_{N}
if
{\sf wt}(\sigma(D))
is odd.

(5)

For any
{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{N}
,
D_{{\mathbf{u}}}+D_{\mathbf{v}}=D_{{\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{v}}}
.

Notice that for any
{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{N}
,

D_{\mathbf{v}}=\begin{bmatrix}D_{\mathbf{u}}&D_{\mathbf{w}}\\
D_{\mathbf{w}}&D_{\mathbf{u}}\end{bmatrix}.

(2)

where
{\mathbf{v}}=({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{w}})
with
{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{w}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{N/2}
.

Remark 2.3
.

Let
D\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}
be a dyadic matrix. Then, by definition,
{\sf supp}(\sigma(D))\subseteq[N]
.
Under the bijection (
1
), we can view this support as a subset of
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{\ell}
.
Along this line, for
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{\ell}
, we will denote
\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{N}
the indicator vector with 1 in position
{\mathbf{x}}
and
0
else.
Thus,
\{\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}\mid{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{\ell}\}
is the standard basis of
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{N}
.

\square

Definition 2.4
.

A dyadic matrix
D
is called a
dyadic permutation matrix
if
{\sf wt}(\sigma(D))=1
.

Notice that for all
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{\ell}
,
D_{\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}}
is a dyadic permutation matrix. For emphasis, we write
P_{{\mathbf{x}}}:=D_{\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}}
.
Alternatively, we will also denote the same dyadic permutation as
P_{x}
where
x\in[N]
is the integer that corresponds to
{\mathbf{x}}
via (
1
).
Even though the set
{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}
is closed under multiplication, the signature of a product of dyadic matrices is not in general easily expressible in terms of the signatures of its factors. The next result shows that a nice expression is possible for dyadic permutation matrices.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{\ell}
. Then
{\sf supp}(\sigma(D\cdot P_{{\mathbf{x}}}))={\mathbf{x}}+{\sf supp}(\sigma(D))
for all
D\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}
.
As a consequence,
P_{{\mathbf{x}}}P_{{\mathbf{y}}}=P_{{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{y}}}
.

A code
\mathcal{C}
is said to be dyadic (resp., quasi-dyadic) if it has a parity-check matrix that is dyadic (resp., quasi-dyadic). Such codes are reproducible, as defined in
[
30
]
, and have compact representations. Indeed, it has a parity-check matrix that depends only on a single vector, its signature. We note that dyadic and quasi-dyadic codes may also have parity-check matrices that do not reflect this structure, since any matrix
A
with
{\sf ker}(A)=\mathcal{C}
is a parity-check matrix for
\mathcal{C}
.

2.2
LDPC Codes

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a quasi-dyadic LDPC code, either
(d_{v},d_{c})
-regular or irregular, with blocklength
2^{\ell}\cdot n_{v}
based on the
n_{c}\times n_{v}

protograph

[
33
]
described by the matrix
B={(b_{ij})}_{n_{c}\times n_{v}}
, where
b_{ij}
is a nonnegative integer for
i\in[n_{c}]
and
j\in[n_{v}]
, and where
[l]=\{0,1,\dots,l-1\}
. Then
\mathcal{C}
can be described by a (scalar) parity-check matrix
H={(H_{ij})}_{n_{c}\times n_{v}}
, where each
H_{ij}
, for
i\in[n_{c}]
and
j\in[n_{v}]
, is a summation of
b_{ij}
(non-overlapping)
2^{\ell}\times 2^{\ell}
dyadic permutation matrices if
b_{ij}
is nonzero, and the
2^{\ell}\times 2^{\ell}
all-zero matrix if
b_{ij}=0
. Graphically, this operation is equivalent to taking a
2^{\ell}
-fold graph cover, or
lifting
, of the protograph. Here,
N=2^{\ell}
is called the lifting factor.

From the parity-check matrix
H
, we construct a bipartite graph
G=(V,E)
, called a
Tanner graph

[
32
]
, by considering
H
as its biadjacency matrix.
This bipartite graph represents the quasi-dyadic LDPC code
\mathcal{C}
obtained from
H
. The set
V
is the set of vertices (or nodes) and
E
is the set of edges. Label the vertices of
G
by
v_{a}
, for
a=0,1,2,\dots,|V|-1
, and the edges by
e_{b}
, for
b=0,1,2,\dots,|E|-1
. Each edge
e_{b}
has the form
e_{b}=(v_{a},v_{c})
, for some
v_{a},v_{c}\in V
, and the vertices
v_{a}
and
v_{c}
are called the
endpoints
of
e_{b}
. A
walk

W
of length
m
in the graph
G
is an alternating sequence
W=v_{0}e_{1}v_{1}e_{2}\cdots v_{m-1}e_{m}v_{m}
of vertices and edges such that
e_{l}=(v_{l-1},v_{l})\in E
for all
1\leq l\leq m
. The first vertex appearing in the alternating sequence,
v_{0}
, is called the
base point
of
W
. A walk
W
is said to be
closed
if the two endpoints are the same, this is, when
v_{0}=v_{m}
. A closed walk
W
is
backtrackless
if
e_{l}\neq e_{l+1}
for all
l=1,2,\dots,m-1
. A backtrackless closed walk
W
is
tailless
if
e_{m}\neq e_{1}
, and
W
is called, in this case, a TBC walk. A
cycle
is a closed walk
W
having distinct vertices and distinct edges, and if its alternating sequence has
k
edges in it, then we call
W
a
k
-cycle. The length of a shortest cycle is called the
girth
of the graph.

2.3
CSS Codes

For a positive integer
n
, the Pauli group on
n
qubits
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
is generated by
n
-fold Kronecker products of the Pauli matrices

{\sf I}_{2},\quad{\sf X}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\quad{\sf Z}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix},\quad{\sf Y}=i{\sf X}{\sf Z}.

(3)

Then, a stabilizer group
{\mathcal{S}}
is a subgroup of
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
that contains only commuting matrices but not
-{\sf I}_{2^{n}}
.
If we write a stabilizer
{\mathcal{S}}
in terms of its generators
\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\}
, then the corresponding quantum stabilizer code is the
2^{n-k}
dimensional subspace of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2^{n}}
consisting of all the states
|\psi\rangle
that are stabilized by all of the generators
s_{i}
, that is
s_{i}|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle
.

It is convenient to think of the Pauli matrices in terms of the binary representation
{\sf I}_{2}\leftrightarrow(0,0)
,
{\sf X}\leftrightarrow(1,0)
,
{\sf Z}\leftrightarrow(0,1)
, and
{\sf Y}\leftrightarrow(1,1)
.
Then, any
n
-qubit Pauli matrix
P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
can be represented as
p=(p_{\sf X}\mid p_{\sf Z})\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2n}
where
p_{\sf X},p_{\sf Z}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n}
have ones at those positions where
P
has
{\sf X}
and
{\sf Z}
respectively.
With this representation, a stabilizer group yields a full rank
k\times 2n
matrix
H=(H_{\sf X}\mid H_{\sf Z})
.
Two stabilizers commute if and only if their respective representations
h=(h_{\sf X}\mid h_{\sf Z}),g=(g_{\sf X}\mid g_{\sf Z})
of
H
are orthogonal with respect to the symplectic inner product
h\odot g:=h_{\sf X}g_{\sf Z}^{\top}+h_{\sf Z}g_{\sf X}^{\top}
.
Cumulatively, this leads to

H\odot H:=H_{\sf X}H_{\sf Z}^{\top}+H_{\sf Z}H_{\sf X}^{\top}=\text{0}.

(4)

An important class of stabilizer codes are the Calderbank-Shor-Steane (CSS) codes
[
5
,
31
]
, defined by a pair of classical linear codes
{\mathcal{C}}_{\sf X},{\mathcal{C}}_{\sf Z}\subset{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n}
such that
{\mathcal{C}}_{\sf X}^{\perp}\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{\sf Z}
.
This condition forces two respective parity-check matrices
H_{\sf X}
and
H_{\sf Z}
to satisfy
H_{\sf Z}H_{\sf X}^{\top}=\text{0}
and thus the matrix

H=\left[\!\!\begin{array}[]{c|c}H_{\sf X}&0\\
0&H_{\sf Z}\end{array}\!\!\right]

(5)

satisfies (
4
) and defines a stabilizer code.
If
{\mathcal{C}}_{\sf X},\,{\mathcal{C}}_{\sf Z}
have parameters
[n,k_{\sf X},d_{\sf X}]
,
[n,k_{\sf Z},d_{\sf Z}]
respectively, then the corresponding quantum code, which will be denoted
{\mathcal{Q}}({\mathcal{C}}_{\sf X},{\mathcal{C}}_{\sf Z})
, has parameters
[\![n,k_{\sf Z}+k_{\sf X}-n,\geq\min\{d_{\sf Z},d_{\sf X}^{\perp}\}]\!]
where
d_{\sf X}^{\perp}
denotes the minimum distance of
{\mathcal{C}}_{\sf X}^{\perp}
.

A quantum LDPC (QLDPC) code then is a quantum code with low-weight stabilizers or equivalently sparse
H
matrix.

Code Construction

3.1
Discussion of dyadic and quasi-dyadic codes

Before introducing specific constructions, we discuss some general properties of dyadic and quasi-dyadic matrices along with their built-in duality, which in turn makes them appealing to the CSS construction.

Theorem 3.1
.

Given a dyadic matrix
D\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}
, if
{\sf supp}(\sigma(D))\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{\ell}
is a subspace (or coset), then
{\sf rank}(D)=N/|{\sf supp}(\sigma(D))|
.

Proof.

Let
S:={\sf supp}(\sigma(D))={\sf supp}(D_{{\bf 0},\bullet})=\{{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{x}}_{\omega}\}
be a subspace of
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{\ell}
of dimension
k
.
For the
{\mathbf{x}}
th row of
D
, by definition, we have
d_{{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}}=d_{{\bf 0},{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{y}}}
.
Thus we have that
{\sf supp}(D_{{\mathbf{x}},\bullet})={\mathbf{x}}+{\sf supp}(D_{{\bf 0},\bullet})
; see also Proposition
2.5
(b). Since
S
is a subspace, every row
D_{{\mathbf{x}},\bullet}
of
D
is either supported on
S
if
{\mathbf{x}}\in S
or on the coset
{\mathbf{x}}+S
if
{\mathbf{x}}\notin S
.
Of course
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{N}
is a disjoint union of
2^{\ell-k}=N/|S|
cosets of size
2^{k}
.
If
S
is subspace, then the first
2^{k}
entries in the signature
\sigma(D)
are ones.
Thus,
D
can be put in block-diagonal form
{\sf diag}(\text{1}_{2^{k}},\cdots,\text{1}_{2^{k}})
.
If
S
is a coset, then by multiplying with a dyadic permutation as in Proposition
2.5
, the resulting dyadic matrix will have the same block-diagonal form.
Thus, there are
N/|S|
blocks and each block has rank 1, and the result follows.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
D\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}
. If
{\sf wt}(\sigma(D))=2
then
{\sf rank}(D)=N/2
.

.

Thus, there are 28 additional dyadic matrices of rank 4 corresponding to signatures of weight 6.
Including the extreme cases

Recall that there are

{\mathcal{C}}={\sf rs}(D)
is an
[N,2^{\ell-k},2^{k}]
code whereas
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
is an
[N,N-2^{\ell-k},2]
code.

Proof.

1) Let
D\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}
be a dyadic matrix such that
{\sf wt}(\sigma(D))
is even. Then,
D^{2}=\text{0}_{N}
and thus
{\sf rs}(D)\subseteq{\sf ker}(D)
.
Hence,
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}={\sf ker}(D)
and the code
{\mathcal{C}}
is self-orthogonal.

2) The statement of the dimensions follows directly by Theorem
3.1
. The statement on the minimum distance of
{\mathcal{C}}
follows by the fact that, as also highlighted in the proof of the same Theorem,
D
can be permuted to a block diagonal form where each block is
\text{1}_{2^{k}}
. Row permutations of course do not change the row space and column permutations do not change the weight. Therefore,
d({\mathcal{C}})=2^{k}
.
For
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
, by the same argument,
D
has (at least) two equal columns. Thus
d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})=2
.
∎

In general, if
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are two matrices such that

H_{1}H_{2}^{\top}=\text{0}

(6)

then the codes
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}={\sf ker}(H_{1})
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}={\sf ker}(H_{2})
satisfy the CSS condition
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{\perp}\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
.
This condition is easily met with dyadic matrices due to their built-in duality, providing a source for CSS codes.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
D_{\mathbf{u}},D_{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
be two dyadic matrices. Then
D_{\mathbf{u}}\cdot D_{\mathbf{v}}=\text{0}_{N}
if and only if
{\mathbf{u}}\in{\sf ker}(D_{\mathbf{v}})
if and only if
{\mathbf{v}}\in{\sf ker}(D_{\mathbf{u}})
.

Proof.

Note that
\sigma(D_{\mathbf{u}}\cdot D_{\mathbf{v}})={\mathbf{u}}D_{\mathbf{v}}
.
Thus,
D_{\mathbf{u}}\cdot D_{\mathbf{v}}=\text{0}_{N}
if and only if
{\mathbf{u}}D_{\mathbf{v}}=\sigma(D_{\mathbf{u}}\cdot D_{\mathbf{v}})={\bf 0}_{N}
if and only if
{\mathbf{u}}\in{\sf ker}(D_{\mathbf{v}})
.
Since
D_{\mathbf{u}}\cdot D_{\mathbf{v}}=D_{\mathbf{v}}\cdot D_{\mathbf{u}}
, everything is equivalent with
{\mathbf{v}}\in{\sf ker}(D_{\mathbf{u}})
.
∎

Example 3.7
.

Let
{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{16}
be such that
{\sf supp}({\mathbf{v}})=\{1,5,6,7,11,14\}
. Then
{\sf rank}(D_{\mathbf{v}})=6
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}={\sf ker}(D_{\mathbf{v}})
is a
[16,10]
code.
Choosing
{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
with
{\sf supp}({\mathbf{u}})=\{4,5,6,8,10,13\}
, we have that
{\sf rank}(D_{\mathbf{u}})=6
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}={\sf ker}(D_{\mathbf{u}})
is also a
[16,10]
code such that
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{\perp}\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
. Direct computation yields
d({\mathcal{C}}_{1})=d({\mathcal{C}}_{2})=4
and thus
{\mathcal{Q}}({\mathcal{C}}_{1},{\mathcal{C}}_{2}^{\perp})
is a
[\![16,4,4]\!]
CSS code.

Choosing
H_{1}=H_{2}=D
to be a dyadic matrix such that
{\sf wt}(\sigma(D))
is even, then condition (
6
) is satisfied automatically (recall that dyadic matrices are symmetric), and as described above, we obtain CSS codes.
Another construction is to choose

H_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\end{bmatrix},\quad H_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}B&A\end{bmatrix}

(7)

where
AB=BA
, in which case
H_{1}H_{2}^{\top}=\text{0}
.
One example of such method is the class of
generalized bicycle codes

[
27
,
20
]
, where the matrices
A
and
B
are chosen to be (versions of) circulant matrices so that the commutativity condition is automatically satisfied. Yet again, because dyadic matrices commute, they can be used in such a construction.
Moreover, if
A
and

are dyadic matrices, then
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are halves of another dyadic matrix.

Example 3.8
.

Let
D_{1},D_{2}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}
be such that
{\sf rank}(D_{1})={\sf rank}(D_{2})=N/2
. Then,
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}={\sf ker}(D_{i})
is self-dual, which in turn yields an
[\![N,0,d({\mathcal{C}}_{i})]\!]
CSS code.
In (
7
), let us take
H_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}D_{1}&D_{2}\end{bmatrix},H_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}D_{2}&D_{1}\end{bmatrix}
.
Let us now consider the CSS pair
(\widehat{{\mathcal{C}}}_{1},\widehat{{\mathcal{C}}}_{2})
where
\widehat{{\mathcal{C}}}_{i}={\sf ker}(H_{i})
.
Note that

{\sf dim}(\widehat{{\mathcal{C}}}_{i})=N+{\sf dim}({\mathcal{C}}_{1}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{2}),

and thus, such CSS pair will yield an
[\![2N,K,d]\!]
CSS code where

K=2{\sf dim}({\mathcal{C}}_{1}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{2}),\quad d\geq\min\{d({\mathcal{C}}_{1}),d({\mathcal{C}}_{2})\}.

In such a way, it is possible to construct a
[\![64,16,8]\!]
CSS code.

3.2
Codes from quasi-dyadic matrices

The
hypergraph product codes

[
34
]
are a class of CSS codes constructed from two matrices
A\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{m_{A}\times n_{A}},B\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{m_{B}\times n_{B}}
and

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}A\otimes I_{m_{B}}&I_{m_{A}}\otimes B\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle H_{2}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}I_{n_{A}}\otimes B^{\top}&A^{\top}\otimes I_{n_{B}}\end{bmatrix},

which straightforwardly satisfy (
6
). In fact, (
6
) is again satisfied if the matrices
A,B
take entries from any commutative ring. The
lifted product codes

[
28
]
are obtained from the hypergraph product construction using the commutative ring of circulant matrices. Since dyadic matrices form a commutative ring as well, they can be used in such construction.
Note that an
c\times v
matrix over the ring
{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}
is simply an
cN\times vN
block matrix where each block is an
N\times N
dyadic matrix, that is, a quasi-dyadic matrix. We will denote
{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}^{c\times v}
the ring of such matrices.

Example 3.9
.

With quasi-dyadic matrices in
{\mathcal{D}}_{2}^{3\times 4}
it is possible to further improve upon Example
3.7
. Indeed, there exists a
[\![16,6,4]\!]
CSS code, which is the highest achievable distance for the given length and dimension
[
12
]
. Similarly, quasi-dyadic matrices in

can be used to construct a
[\![20,6,4]\!]
CSS code.

We now give the main construction—a generalization of
[
18
]
—that uses permutation dyadic matrices.

Construction 3.10
.

Fix a positive integer
\omega
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{\ell}
for

.
Let
\sigma,\tau
be two permutations of
[\omega]
of order

\omega\times 2\omega
block quasi-dyadic matrices
H_{\sf X}=\left[H_{{\sf X},L}\mid H_{{\sf X},R}\right]
and
H_{\sf Z}=\left[H_{{\sf Z},L}\mid H_{{\sf Z},R}\right]
where

\displaystyle H_{{\sf X},L}=\left[P_{{\mathbf{x}}_{\sigma^{(i-1)}(j)}}\right]_{i,j\in[\omega]},\,\,

\displaystyle H_{{\sf X},R}=\left[P_{{\mathbf{y}}_{\sigma^{(i-1)}(j)}}\right]_{i,j\in[\omega]},

\displaystyle H_{{\sf Z},L}=\left[P_{{\mathbf{y}}_{\tau^{(i-1)}(j)}}\right]_{i,j\in[\omega]},\,\,

\displaystyle H_{{\sf Z},R}=\left[P_{{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau^{(i-1)}(j)}}\right]_{i,j\in[\omega]},

and each of the blocks is a dyadic permutation matrix.

In Construction
3.10
, we choose the permutations
\sigma
and
\tau
such that
H_{\sf X}H_{\sf Z}^{\top}=\text{0}
.
For this, we must have

\sum_{k=1}^{\omega}P_{{\mathbf{x}}_{\sigma^{(i-1)}(k)}}P_{{\mathbf{y}}_{\tau^{(j-1)}(k)}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\omega}P_{{\mathbf{y}}_{\sigma^{(i-1)}(k)}}P_{{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau^{(j-1)}(k)}}

(8)

for all

.
Since the product of dyadic permutation matrices is again a dyadic permutation matrix (see Proposition

) and since
every dyadic matrix is a unique sum of dyadic permutations
(see also (

8
) and vice versa.
Such condition can be easily achieved by choosing

to be cyclic permutations.
For simulations,
\sigma,\tau
are also optimized so that the resulting codes have designated girth and small number of short cycles.

We will compare our Construction

with the recent Construction B of
[

]
, which we lay out next.

Construction 3.11
.

Fix an even integer

and

.
Consider the quasi-dyadic matrix

H_{P}=\begin{bmatrix}P&P_{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}&P&P_{{\mathbf{x}}_{2}}&\cdots&P&P_{{\mathbf{x}}_{v}}\\

where

is a fixed dyadic permutation and

’s are the corresponding dyadic permutations.

Remark 3.12
.

In Construction

, the arrangement of the dyadic permutations

and

is chosen so that

and thus yielding a dual-containing CSS code. However, by construction (see

4
Girth Analysis

In this section, we present the girth and cycle analysis of dyadic and quasi-dyadic codes. Specifically, in Sections
4.2
and
4.3
, we propose strategies to count 4-cycles in the Tanner graph of dyadic and quasi-dyadic codes, respectively; the rest of the analysis is presented in Appendix
A
. First, we present the background required to establish a connection between the cycles in the Tanner graph and the TBC walks in the protograph.

4.1
Connection Between TBC Walks in the Protograph and Cycles in the Tanner Graph

Let
H
be the parity-check matrix of a dyadic or quasi-dyadic code and let
G=(V,E)
be the corresponding Tanner graph. The
adjacency matrix

A=(A_{ij})
is the symmetric binary matrix with
A_{ij}=1
if
(v_{i},v_{j})\in E
, and
A_{ij}=0
otherwise. After some reordering of the vertices, if necessary, we can write
A
, for either the scalar or block representation of
H
, in the compact expression

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&H\\
H^{\mathsf{T}}&0\end{bmatrix}.

(10)

The powers of
A
, and in particular the matrices

B_{t}(H)=\left(HH^{\mathsf{T}}\right)^{\lfloor{t/2}\rfloor}H^{(t\mod 2)},\quad t\geq 0,

give information about the walks in the Tanner graph
[
35
]
. It is not difficult to see that, for any nonnegative integer
t
, we have

A^{2t}=\begin{bmatrix}B_{2t}(H)&0\\
0&B_{2t}(H^{\mathsf{T}})\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad A^{2t+1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&B_{2t+1}(H)\\
B_{2t+1}(H^{\mathsf{T}})&0\end{bmatrix}.

(11)

Theorem 4.1

(
[
23
]
)
.

Given an adjacency matrix
A
, the entry
{(A^{m})}_{ij}
is equal to the number of walks of length
m
between the vertices
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
.

Similarly to the cycle analysis of QC-LDPC codes presented in
[
11
]
, the block representation of quasi-dyadic LDPC codes allows for a reduction in the complexity of our computations.
The
k
th power of the scalar adjacency matrix
A
of the Tanner graph can be used to determine the number of
k
-walks between any two vertices, as we have seen in Theorem
4.1
. The
k
th power of the block version of the adjacency matrix can be used to describe the edges traversed in a
k
-walk between any two vertices in the protograph. For example, if
A
is the block version of the adjacency matrix in (
10
), then

{(A^{2})}_{ij}=\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{n_{c}+n_{v}-1}A_{il}A_{lj},

and every term of
{(A^{2})}_{ij}
is a product of the form
P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{il}}P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{lj}}=P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{il}+{\mathbf{c}}_{lj}}
, where
P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{il}}
and
P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{lj}}
come from
A_{il}
and
A_{lj}
, respectively. Each one of the two dyadic permutation matrices
P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{il}}
and
P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{lj}}
corresponds to a unique edge in the protograph, and the order in which they appear in the product is the order used to traverse the walk in the protograph. The index
{\mathbf{c}}_{il}+{\mathbf{c}}_{lj}
, in consequence, corresponds to the two edges traversed from vertex
v_{i}
to vertex
v_{l}
to vertex
v_{j}
in the protograph. In the same way, every term of
{(A^{3})}_{ij}
is a product of the form
P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{il}}P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{lk}}P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{kj}}=P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{il}+{\mathbf{c}}_{lk}+{\mathbf{c}}_{kj}}
, and the index
{\mathbf{c}}_{il}+{\mathbf{c}}_{lk}+{\mathbf{c}}_{kj}
corresponds to the three edges traversed in the protograph from vertex
v_{i}
to vertex
v_{l}
to vertex
v_{k}
to vertex
v_{j}
. In general, every term of
{(A^{m})}_{ij}
is of the form
P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{il_{1}}}P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{l_{1}l_{2}}}\cdots P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{l_{m}j}}=P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{il_{1}}+{\mathbf{c}}_{l_{1}l_{2}}+\cdots+{\mathbf{c}}_{l_{m}j}}
, and each one of them corresponds to a walk of length
m
and the specific order in which it is traversed.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
11
]
)
.

Given an adjacency matrix
A
, every term of
{(A^{m})}_{ij}
is of the form
P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{il_{1}}}P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{l_{1}l_{2}}}\cdots P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{l_{m}j}}
and corresponds to a walk of length
m
between the vertices
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
in the protograph.

Definition 4.3
.

The index
{\mathbf{c}}_{il_{1}}+{\mathbf{c}}_{l_{1}l_{2}}+\cdots+{\mathbf{c}}_{l_{m}j}
corresponding to the product
P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{il_{1}}}P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{l_{1}l_{2}}}\cdots P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{l_{m}j}}
in Theorem
4.2
is called a
permutation shift
.

Suppose that
P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{il_{1}}}P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{l_{1}l_{2}}}\cdots P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{l_{m}j}}
and
P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{il_{1}}^{\prime}}P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{l_{1}l_{2}}^{\prime}}\cdots P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{l_{m}j}^{\prime}}
are two terms of
{(A^{m})}_{ij}
describing two
m
-walks between vertices
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
in the protograph. Then the combination

P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{il_{1}}}P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{l_{1}l_{2}}}\cdots P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{l_{m}j}}P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{l_{m}j}^{\prime}}\cdots P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{l_{1}l_{2}}^{\prime}}P_{{\mathbf{c}}_{il_{1}}^{\prime}}

of the first walk and the reversal of the second one describes a closed
(2m)
-walk that starts and ends at the vertex
v_{i}
, and that has the vertex
v_{j}
midway. Hence, the entries
{(A^{m})}_{ij}
of the power

describe all the
m
-walks in the protograph and can be used to count certain cycles in the Tanner graph.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a protograph described by matrix
B={(b_{ij})}_{n_{c}\times n_{v}}
. Each row and each column of
B
corresponds to a check node and a variable node in the protograph, respectively. Once a lifting factor
N=2^{\ell}
is chosen, for each vertex

in the protograph, either a check node or a variable node, we create
N
copies of it and denote them by
\tilde{v}^{l}
, for

. For each edge

in the protograph, there is, associated with it, a dyadic permutation matrix

, where

. Once the value for

is chosen, we create
N

and denote them by
\tilde{e}^{l}
, for

, where

.
If we let

4.2
Cycles in Dyadic Codes

Let
D=(D_{{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}})
be an
N\times N
dyadic matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, where
N=2^{\ell}
. The integer
\ell
will represent the degree of the graph lift. Suppose that
\sigma(D)
has weight
\omega
and that
{\sf supp}(\sigma(D))=\{x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{\omega-1}\}\subseteq[N]
. Then we can write
D
as a summation of
\omega
dyadic permutation matrices

D=P_{{\mathbf{x}}_{0}}+P_{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}+P_{{\mathbf{x}}_{2}}+\cdots+P_{{\mathbf{x}}_{\omega-1}}.

(12)

If
\omega=1
, then
D=P_{{\mathbf{x}}_{0}}
, so its Tanner graph is acyclic. If
\omega\geq 2
, then the girth of
D
is 4. To see why this is the case, first notice that
\{x_{0},x_{1}\}\subseteq{\sf supp}(\sigma(D))
. Let
{\mathbf{c}}={\mathbf{x}}_{0}+{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
.
By assumption,

4.3.1
Counting 4-cycles

We have seen that the 4-cycles in the Tanner graph are projected onto either TBC walks of length 2 that are traversed twice or TBC walks of length 4, in the protograph, and these walks can be studied by computing the product
HH^{\mathsf{T}}
according to Theorem

2\times 2
submatrix of the form

H_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{u}}&P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{v}}\\
P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{u}}&P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{v}}\end{bmatrix}.

In terms of notation, an index of the form
{\mathbf{h}}_{u}
indicates that the dyadic permutation matrix
P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{u}}
appears in the
h
th row and
u
th column of

[{\mathbf{h}}_{u},{\mathbf{i}}_{u},{\mathbf{i}}_{v},{\mathbf{h}}_{v}]

{\left(P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{v}}\right)}^{-1}\cdot P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{v}}\cdot{\left(P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{u}}\right)}^{-1}\cdot P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{u}}=P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{v}}\cdot P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{v}}\cdot P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{u}}\cdot P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{u}}=P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{u}+{\mathbf{i}}_{u}+{\mathbf{i}}_{v}+{\mathbf{h}}_{v}},

so we require that

to avoid 4-cycles in the Tanner graph. This is the case since the only dyadic permutation matrix that has fixed points is the identity matrix, so we need to make sure that the left-hand side is not equal to

By Lemma

4.4
Dyadic-PEG and Cycle Optimization

The short cycles in the Tanner graph of LDPC codes are known to play an important role in their performance
[
21
,
15
]
, which motivates the search for LDPC codes from graphs of large girth. However, even if two LDPC codes have the same girth, their performance can be dramatically different if they have a different number of short cycles
[
15
]
. In an attempt to construct quasi-dyadic LDPC codes with a large girth and a small number of short cycles, we present some construction strategies using a PEG-like approach similar to that presented in
[
10
]
.

The Progressive Edge-Growth (PEG) algorithm
[
16
]
was originally proposed as a general method for constructing Tanner graphs with large girth. This algorithm works by establishing edges between symbol and check nodes in an edge-by-edge manner while satisfying some node degree sequence. The creation of a new edge in the Tanner graph occurs in a position where the impact on the girth is minimized, meaning that edges forming cycles are avoided for as long as possible. In terms of parity-check matrices, the algorithm starts creating a parity-check matrix from an all-zero matrix, placing ones in particular entries that avoid, or delay if unavoidable, the creation of cycles while preserving either a column or row weight distribution.

The complexity of the PEG algorithm scales as
\mathcal{O}(mn)
, where
m
is the number of check nodes and
n
is the number of symbol nodes in the Tanner graph. In a quasi-dyadic LDPC code, these values are given by
m=n_{c}N
and
n=n_{v}N
. Due to the nature of dyadic matrices, once an edge is created between the first check node and some variable node in a given
N\times N
block, which corresponds to adding a 1 to the signature row of that
N\times N
dyadic matrix, the remaining
N-1
edges corresponding to that block will also be created. This allows for a reduction in the complexity of the construction when combined with the girth conditions presented in this section and Appendix
A
. To propose this low-complexity PEG algorithm, we start with a definition.

Definition 4.20

(
[
10
]
)
.

Let the parity-check matrix
H
of a quasi-dyadic LDPC code be as in (
14
) and let
N=2^{\ell}
be the lifting factor. The
forbidden set

\mathcal{F}_{{\mathbf{a}}}^{g,N}
of the index
{\mathbf{a}}
, corresponding to the dyadic permutation matrix
P_{{\mathbf{a}}}
in
H
, is the set of elements in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell}
such that if
{\mathbf{a}}
assumes any value in this set, then
\text{girth}(H)\leq g
.

Remark 4.21
.

Notice that the description of the forbidden set
\mathcal{F}_{{\mathbf{x}}}^{g,N}
of an index
{\mathbf{x}}
corresponding to the dyadic permutation matrix
P_{{\mathbf{x}}}

are given symbolically. In practice and as we will see in a moment, the dyadic permutation matrices are chosen sequentially (one at a time), which means that at a given iteration, some of the indices have not been assigned an element of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell}
and should not be involved in the computations of that specific forbidden set. As an illustration,
consider the matrix

H=\begin{bmatrix}P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{0}}&P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{1}}&P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{2}}&\cdots&P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{n_{v}-1}}\\
P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{0}}&P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{1}}&P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{2}}&\cdots&P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{n_{v}-1}}\end{bmatrix}

and suppose that only the dyadic permutation matrices
P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{0}}
,
P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{1}}
, and
P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{0}}
have been chosen with, say,
{\mathbf{h}}_{0}={\mathbf{h}}_{1}={\mathbf{i}}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
, and that we want to choose
P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{1}}
. Then we have
\mathcal{F}_{{\mathbf{i}}_{1}}^{4,N}=\left\{{\mathbf{h}}_{1}+{\mathbf{h}}_{0}+{\mathbf{i}}_{0}\right\}=\left\{\mathbf{0}+\mathbf{0}+\mathbf{0}\right\}=\left\{\mathbf{0}\right\}
. This means that
{\mathbf{i}}_{1}\neq\mathbf{0}
guarantees girth larger than 4. Notice that the element
{\mathbf{h}}_{1}+{\mathbf{h}}_{2}+{\mathbf{i}}_{2}
, for example, is not an element of
\mathcal{F}_{{\mathbf{i}}_{1}}^{4,N}
since
{\mathbf{h}}_{2}
and
{\mathbf{i}}_{2}
have not been assigned an element of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell}
before this iteration.

\square

A natural way to combine the PEG algorithm with the forbidden set approach a as follows. First, we need to decide in what order we construct the parity-check matrix (
14
), that is, in what order we assign elements in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell}
to the indices. Two common orderings are row-by-row

({\mathbf{x}}_{0,0},{\mathbf{x}}_{0,1},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{0,n_{v}-1},{\mathbf{x}}_{1,0},{\mathbf{x}}_{1,1},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{1,n_{v}-1},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{n_{c}-1,0},{\mathbf{x}}_{n_{c}-1,1},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{n_{c}-1,n_{v}-1})

and column-by-column

({\mathbf{x}}_{0,0},{\mathbf{x}}_{1,0},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{n_{c}-1,0},{\mathbf{x}}_{0,1},{\mathbf{x}}_{1,1},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{n_{c}-1,1},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{0,n_{v}-1},{\mathbf{x}}_{1,n_{v}-1},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{n_{c}-1,n_{v}-1});

a random ordering is another option. After choosing the ordering and prescribing a target girth
g
, which is usually taken to be the maximum attainable girth (since PEG tries to guarantee maximum girth), we compute the forbidden set
\mathcal{F}_{{\mathbf{x}}}^{g,N}
of the first index
{\mathbf{x}}
in the ordering and assign to it an element from the set
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell}\backslash\mathcal{F}_{{\mathbf{x}}}^{g,N}
at random. For the second index
{\mathbf{y}}
, we compute
\mathcal{F}_{{\mathbf{y}}}^{g,N}
and assign to it an element from the set
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell}\backslash\mathcal{F}_{{\mathbf{y}}}^{g,N}
at random. The algorithm continues in this fashion for as long as possible. At some point, if
\ell
is not large enough, then there exists the possibility that the set
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell}\backslash\mathcal{F}_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{g,N}=\emptyset
for some index
{\mathbf{z}}
. In this case, the algorithm decreases the target girth to
g-2
and repeats the same strategy, only reducing the target girth when needed. In the quasi-dyadic case, the maximum attainable girth in a lifting of (
14

Absorbing Set Analysis

While short cycles influence decoder convergence, error-floor behavior in iterative decoding is often dominated by more subtle subgraphs. In this section, we turn our attention to absorbing sets, which capture persistent local trapping configurations beyond pure cycle structure. Absorbing sets are combinatorial objects studied in classical coding theory
[
8
,
6
,
7
,
4
,
24
]
and recently analyzed in the quantum setting as well
[
25
,
26
]
. They have been shown to be the dominant problematic structure affecting the error floor for certain families of LDPC codes, as well as being responsible for many instances of decoding failure under the Gallager B syndrome-based iterative decoder for quantum LDPC codes. Thus, it is a natural question to characterize the presence of absorbing sets for codes arising from dyadic and quasi-dyadic matrices. First, we recall that given a graph
G=(V,E)
and subset of vertices
W\subseteq V
, the
induced subgraph

G_{W}
is the graph obtained by taking all vertices in
W
and all edges incident to these vertices.

Definition 5.1

(
[
8
]
)
.

An
(a,b)
-absorbing set
\mathcal{A}
in a Tanner graph
G
is a subset of
|\mathcal{A}|=a
variable nodes such that the induced subgraph
G_{\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{A})}
has
b
odd degree check nodes and every variable node
v\in\mathcal{A}
has strictly more even than odd degree neighbors in
G_{\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{A})}
.

We first make some observations about the “boundary cases” for dyadic and quasi-dyadic constructions. Observe that Tanner graphs arising from
m\times 1
arrays of dyadic permutation matrices are acyclic and have no absorbing sets. Indeed, an
m\times 1
array of dyadic permutation matrices has a Tanner graph
G
with all check nodes of degree one, which means both that
G
is acyclic and it has no absorbing sets. Thus, while it is not interesting to consider
m\times 1
arrays of dyadic permutation matrices with respect to absorbing sets,
1\times n
arrays of dyadic permutation matrices and their absorbing sets deserve attention.

Unless stated otherwise, we count absorbing sets whose induced subgraph is connected.

Lemma 5.2
.

Consider a
1\times n
array
M
of
2^{\ell}\times 2^{\ell}
dyadic permutation matrices. The corresponding Tanner graph is acyclic and has exactly
2^{\ell}(2^{n-1}-1)
absorbing sets, all of which are
(a,0)
-absorbing sets for
a=2k
even and
2\leq a\leq n
.

Proof.

Let
G
denote the Tanner graph corresponding to
M
. Since
M
is a
1\times n
array, all variable nodes have degree
1
, so
G
is acyclic.
Each check node is incident to exactly one variable node in each block of
M
.
Thus, each check node is incident to
n
variable nodes, and the Tanner graph
G
consists of
2^{\ell}
disconnected star graphs, meaning complete bipartite graphs
K_{1,t}
, one per check node.

To count the number of absorbing sets, we count the number of ways to choose an even number of variable nodes from each cluster of
n
variable nodes and then multiply by
2^{\ell}
to denote the
2^{\ell}
clusters forming star graphs. Then the total number of absorbing sets for

is

2^{\ell}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}\binom{n}{2k}=2^{\ell}(2^{n-1}-1).\\

We note that each cluster has
2^{n-1}-1
ways of forming absorbing sets since we exclude selecting
0
variable nodes from the group of
n
nodes.
∎

Next, we consider various configurations of quasi-dyadic whose corresponding Tanner graphs contain complete bipartite graphs. We then present a unified description of their absorbing set structure.

7
Conclusions and Future Research

This paper demonstrates how the dyadic and quasi-dyadic structure can be leveraged as a practical design framework for (Q)LDPC codes by enabling efficient cycle enumeration, dyadic-aware PEG/forbidden-set constructions that either increase girth when possible or, when dyadic constraints limit girth growth, reduce the multiplicity and organization of the shortest cycles, and by providing initial characterizations of absorbing-set behavior for key structured layouts.

We have shown how systematic control of short-cycle multiplicity and bad layouts can yield tangible iterative-decoding gains. It remains open to establish the fundamental limits of dyadic lifts, including tight bounds on achievable girth and minimal short-cycle multiplicities along with structure–randomness trade-offs and extending absorbing-set results to general quasi-dyadic ensembles and identifying dyadic invariants that predict the error floor. It would also be interesting to explore
strengthening the constructions and to determine whether forbidden sets are complete for lengths 6 and 8 pathologies along with what optimization objectives best correlate with performance (e.g., cycle counts).
Along the same lines, in order to break the girth
g\leq 8
bound for dyadic lifting of the all-one protograph, we will explore in future work
n
-adic lifting of
irregular
protographs.
Preliminary results already show that high girths are indeed achievable and a similar cycle analysis is feasible.

Another direction worth pursuit involves addressing quantum-specific issues such as distance and rate scaling for dyadic CSS families along with consideration of how dyadic regularity may improve syndrome extraction and decoder parallelism and generalizations to other group-ring lift families beyond characteristic
2
.

A.2
Cycles in Protograph-based LDPC Codes from Dyadics (Continuation)

A.2.1
Counting 6-cycles

The 6-cycles in the Tanner graph of protograph-based LDPC codes are projected onto a
3\times 3
submatrix of the form

H_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{u}}&P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{v}}&P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{w}}\\
P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{u}}&P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{v}}&P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{w}}\\
P_{{\mathbf{j}}_{u}}&P_{{\mathbf{j}}_{v}}&P_{{\mathbf{j}}_{w}}\end{bmatrix}.

The corresponding TBC walks of length 6 in the protograph obtained from this submatrix are given by

[{\mathbf{h}}_{u},{\mathbf{i}}_{u},{\mathbf{i}}_{m},{\mathbf{j}}_{m},{\mathbf{j}}_{u^{\prime}},{\mathbf{h}}_{u^{\prime}}]
,
[{\mathbf{i}}_{u},{\mathbf{h}}_{u},{\mathbf{h}}_{m},{\mathbf{j}}_{m},{\mathbf{j}}_{u^{\prime}},{\mathbf{i}}_{u^{\prime}}]
, and
[{\mathbf{j}}_{u},{\mathbf{h}}_{u},{\mathbf{h}}_{m},{\mathbf{i}}_{m},{\mathbf{i}}_{u^{\prime}},{\mathbf{j}}_{u^{\prime}}]
for

0\leq h<i<j\leq n_{c}-1
and
u,m,u^{\prime}\in[n_{v}]
. In
[
11
]
, it was shown that these three TBC walk patterns are equivalent, so only one of them is enough to describe all 6-cycles in the Tanner graph. The first TBC walk pattern gives the product

\displaystyle{\left(P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{u^{\prime}}}\right)}^{-1}\cdot P_{{\mathbf{j}}_{u^{\prime}}}\cdot{\left(P_{{\mathbf{j}}_{m}}\right)}^{-1}\cdot P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{m}}\cdot{\left(P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{u}}\right)}^{-1}\cdot P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{u}}

\displaystyle=P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{u^{\prime}}}P_{{\mathbf{j}}_{u^{\prime}}}P_{{\mathbf{j}}_{m}}P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{m}}P_{{\mathbf{i}}_{u}}P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{u}}

\displaystyle=P_{{\mathbf{h}}_{u}+{\mathbf{i}}_{u}+{\mathbf{i}}_{m}+{\mathbf{j}}_{m}+{\mathbf{j}}_{u^{\prime}}+{\mathbf{h}}_{u^{\prime}}},

so we require that

{\mathbf{h}}_{u}+{\mathbf{i}}_{u}+{\mathbf{i}}_{m}+{\mathbf{j}}_{m}+{\mathbf{j}}_{u^{\prime}}+{\mathbf{h}}_{u^{\prime}}\neq\mathbf{0}
to avoid 6-cycles in the Tanner graph. From this observation, we have that
\text{girth}(H)>6
if and only if for each
0\leq h<i<j\leq n_{c}-1
, all the elements in each one of the msets

\left\{{\mathbf{h}}_{u}+{\mathbf{i}}_{u}+{\mathbf{i}}_{m},{\mathbf{h}}_{u}+{\mathbf{j}}_{u}+{\mathbf{j}}_{m}\mid u\in[n_{v}]\backslash\{m\}\right\},\quad m\in[n_{v}],

contains distinct elements. To count 6-cycles in the Tanner graph, we rewrite these conditions. Consider the following theorem.

Theorem A.4
.

Let
H
be as in (
14
). For

0\leq h<i<j\leq n_{c}-1
and
m\in[n_{v}]
, consider the following msets

\displaystyle A_{1,m}^{h,i,j}

\displaystyle=\left\{(u,{\mathbf{h}}_{u}+{\mathbf{i}}_{u}+{\mathbf{i}}_{m})\mid u\in[n_{v}]\backslash\{m\}\right\},

\displaystyle A_{2,m}^{h,i,j}

\displaystyle=\left\{(u,{\mathbf{h}}_{u}+{\mathbf{j}}_{u}+{\mathbf{j}}_{m})\mid u\in[n_{v}]\backslash\{m\}\right\}.

For

u,u^{\prime}\in[n_{v}]
, let
(u,{\bm{\alpha}}_{u})\in A_{1,m}^{h,i,j}
and
(u^{\prime},{\bm{\alpha}}_{u^{\prime}})\in A_{2,m}^{h,i,j}
be such that
{\bm{\alpha}}_{u}={\bm{\alpha}}_{u^{\prime}}
. Then the repetition

{\bm{\alpha}}_{u}={\bm{\alpha}}_{u^{\prime}}
lifts to a collection of 6-cycles in the Tanner graph if

u\neq u^{\prime}
. The total number of 6-cycles in the Tanner graph,
\mathcal{N}_{6}
, is given by

\mathcal{N}_{6}=2^{\ell}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h,i,j\in[n_{c}]\\
h<i<j\end{subarray}}\sum_{m\in[n_{v}]}\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell}}\rho_{A_{1,m}^{h,i,j},A_{2,m}^{h,i,j}}({\bm{\alpha}}),

(17)

where

\rho_{A_{1,m}^{h,i,j},A_{2,m}^{h,i,j}}({\bm{\alpha}})
denotes the number of times the element
{\bm{\alpha}}
is repeated between the msets

A_{1,m}^{h,i,j}
and
A_{2,m}^{h,i,j}
in the sense described above.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.6
, the 6-cycles in the Tanner graph are projected onto TBC walks of length 6 in the protograph and these walks can be studied by computing the product
HH^{\mathsf{T}}H
as a consequence of Theorem
4.2
. To determine all possible TBC walk patterns, we use the product
H_{3}H_{3}^{\mathsf{T}}H_{3}
instead, but if
H
has less than three rows, then any pattern arising from these additional rows are omitted in the computations of 6-cycles. Once
H_{3}H_{3}^{\mathsf{T}}H_{3}
has been computed, we use the strategy of combining walks following Definition
4.3
to compute all possible TBC walk patterns, and then use Definition
4.13
to compute all nonequivalent TBC walk patterns. This gives us the unique TBC walk pattern
[{\mathbf{h}}_{u},{\mathbf{i}}_{u},{\mathbf{i}}_{m},{\mathbf{j}}_{m},{\mathbf{j}}_{u^{\prime}},{\mathbf{h}}_{u^{\prime}}]
if
u\neq m
,
m\neq u^{\prime}
, and
u^{\prime}\neq u
. If
{\mathbf{h}}_{u}+{\mathbf{i}}_{u}+{\mathbf{i}}_{m}+{\mathbf{j}}_{m}+{\mathbf{j}}_{u^{\prime}}+{\mathbf{h}}_{u^{\prime}}=\mathbf{0}
, then
{\mathbf{h}}_{u}+{\mathbf{i}}_{u}+{\mathbf{i}}_{m}={\mathbf{h}}_{u^{\prime}}+{\mathbf{j}}_{u^{\prime}}+{\mathbf{j}}_{m}
, so we have 6-cycles in the Tanner graph every time this repetition happens. In this case, this repetition contributes
2^{\ell}
6-cycles. If we sum over all sets of three of rows
(h,i,j)
,
h<i<j
, and all
m\in[n_{v}]
, (
17
) follows.
∎

Remark A.7

, the product
