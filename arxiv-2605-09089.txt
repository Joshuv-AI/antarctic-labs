Field-Localized Forgery Detection for Digital Identity Documents
Abhishek Kumar, Riya Tapwal, Carsten Maple, Mark Hooper

Abstract
Digital identity verification systems used in remote onboarding rely on document images to authenticate users, making them vulnerable to localized manipulations of key identity fields such as facial photographs and textual information. Existing forgery detection methods, developed primarily for natural-image forensics, show limited transferability to structured identity documents. We propose FLiD, a lightweight field-localized framework that targets critical identity regions rather than processing full-document images. A fine-tuned object detector first localizes face and text fields; a frozen MobileNetV3-Small backbone then extracts compact field-level embeddings, which are classified by lightweight neural network with only 191K trainable parameters. FLiD achieves AUC scores of 0.880 (face), 0.954 (text), and 0.923 (both-field attacks), with corresponding EERs of 18.05%, 11.61%, and 15.16%, representing absolute reductions of 29–35 percentage points over a full-document baseline trained from scratch. FLiD also consistently outperforms general-purpose manipulation detectors (TruFor, MMFusion, UniVAD) across all attack scenarios while requiring 13 × \times fewer parameters and 21 × \times fewer FLOPs.

Introduction

Forgery detection in identity documents has become a critical challenge in
modern digital identity verification systems, where banks, fintech
platforms, telecom providers, and other third-party services routinely
require users to upload photographs or scanned copies of identity
documents for remote authentication. The reliance on such remote
onboarding workflows has increased significantly following the COVID-19
pandemic, during which physical verification procedures and
contact-based biometric modalities such as fingerprint acquisition were
substantially replaced by fully digital verification
pipelines
[
28
]
. Consequently, identity verification
systems increasingly rely on visual inspection of submitted document
images rather than direct access to centralised identity databases
[
21
,
1
]
.
These systems often combine document analysis with face matching between
the identity card and a live selfie or captured photograph. This
dependence on document images makes such systems vulnerable to localised
manipulations, including face replacement, text editing, or both-field
field tampering, which can enable identity fraud while remaining
visually difficult to detect through manual inspection
[
22
,
29
]
.

Identity documents differ from natural images, as they
contain structured layouts composed of semantically meaningful fields
such as facial photographs, textual identity information, security
textures, and machine-readable zones
[
21
,
22
]
. Even small localised
modifications, such as altering a date of birth, modifying a document
number, or swapping the facial photograph, can compromise system
integrity and lead to financial or regulatory risks
[
29
]
.

These examples demonstrate how localised alterations affecting specific

the authenticity of the document.

In recent years, relatively few publicly accessible studies have
specifically targeted identity-document forgery detection. In practice,
many industrial identity verification systems rely on proprietary
datasets and internally developed detection models that are not publicly
released due to privacy, regulatory, and security considerations
[
8
,
24
]
. Since
such datasets typically contain sensitive personal identity information,
both the training data and the deployed detection pipelines remain
closed-source, limiting reproducibility and motivating the need for
open, efficient, and domain-specific approaches. At the same time,
general image forgery detection has been extensively studied using
deep learning–based approaches. Methods such as
TruFor
[
10
]
, UniVAD
[
9
]
, and MMFusion
[
23
]
have
demonstrated strong performance in detecting synthetic or manipulated
visual content by leveraging transformer architectures, multimodal
fusion mechanisms, or anomaly-based localisation strategies. However,
these approaches are primarily designed and evaluated on natural-image
manipulation benchmarks, where the objective is to detect edits such as
object insertion, removal, or generative-image synthesis, and are
therefore not optimised for the structured and multimodal characteristics
inherent to identity documents. Consequently, their performance often
degrades when applied directly to real-world identity verification
scenarios.

To address these challenges, we propose FLiD (Field-Localized Identity Document Forgery Detection), a lightweight,
field-aware forgery detection framework that explicitly models identity
documents as structured collections of semantic identity fields. Rather
than processing the entire document indiscriminately, our approach
focuses on the semantic regions most susceptible to tampering, namely
the facial photograph and textual identity fields. A fine-tuned object
detection module is first employed to localise and extract these key
identity regions, enabling consistent preprocessing across heterogeneous
document layouts and formats. The extracted regions are then encoded
using a compact feature backbone to generate efficient yet semantically
meaningful embeddings. For documents containing simultaneous
manipulations across multiple fields, embeddings from both facial and
textual regions are jointly processed by a lightweight classification
module.

Extensive experiments demonstrate that the proposed pipeline, evaluated via 5-fold stratified cross-validation,
achieves AUC(Area Under the Curve) scores of
0.880\pm 0.054
,
0.954\pm 0.016
, and
0.923\pm 0.036
for face-only, text-only, and both-field manipulation scenarios, respectively, while reducing EER(Equal Error Rate) by up to
35.64
percentage points over the baseline. These gains are achieved with only
191
K trainable parameters and
21\times
fewer FLOPs than the baseline, providing an effective and computationally efficient solution for real-world digital identity verification systems.

3
Our Methodology

We propose an identity-aware forgery detection system that focuses on semantically meaningful regions of digital identity documents to improve detection reliability and computational efficiency. Our key insight is that structured identity documents require a fundamentally different detection strategy than natural images: rather than learning dense pixel-level representations, it suffices to extract compact features from semantically critical fields. This principle leads to a compositional design, frozen pretrained features, field-level ROI extraction, and lightweight classification, that achieves strong detection with only 191K trainable parameters while ensuring reproducibility and suitability for edge deployment. As shown in Fig.
2
, our pipeline begins by localizing key identity fields, specifically the facial image and textual regions, using a fine-tuned YOLOv8 detector trained on manually annotated identity data. This step separates the input identity document into structured components and enables consistent extraction of identity-relevant regions across diverse document layouts. The detected face and text regions are then cropped and passed through a lightweight MobileNetV3 feature encoder to generate compact embedding representations that capture visual characteristics and potential manipulation artifacts. For documents containing manipulations across multiple identity fields, embeddings from both facial and textual regions are concatenated to form a unified multi-field representation, which is subsequently processed using a lightweight neural network classifier. This classifier learns discriminative patterns from identity-specific features and enables effective classification of identity documents as genuine or forged.

3.1
Dataset Description

We evaluate our method on the publicly available FantasyID dataset
[
12
]
, a benchmark specifically designed for digital identity-document forgery detection. FantasyID comprises 362 unique ID designs, with 1,086 bona fide captured images obtained under realistic KYC-like conditions using multiple acquisition devices. The dataset simulates real-world deployment by printing and recapturing identity cards, introducing device-specific artifacts and appearance variability. Forgery samples are generated using state-of-the-art manipulation techniques targeting semantically meaningful identity fields, including face-swapping methods (InSwapper, FaceDancer) and text-editing/inpainting models (DiffSTE, TextDiffuser2), and are categorized into face-only, text-only, and both-field face+text attacks. FantasyID has been adopted in the DeepID ICCV Challenge, establishing it as a community-recognized benchmark for digital identity forgery detection. The dataset is openly available for research use.

3.2
Identity Field Localization Using YOLOv8

Identity documents exhibit significant structural variability across countries and issuing authorities, with different layouts, field placements, and visual formats. The Fantasy-ID dataset contains identity cards originating from 13 different countries, resulting in substantial variation in the spatial location and appearance of identity fields such as facial photographs and textual information. As an initial step, we manually annotated and cropped the face and textual regions for all images in the dataset, creating paired full-document and cropped-field versions. These manually prepared crops served two purposes: (i) enabling controlled preliminary experiments to validate the effectiveness of field-level analysis, and (ii) providing supervision data for training an automatic field-localization model. To automate this process, we adopt YOLOv8, a lightweight real-time object detection architecture, for detecting identity-document fields. YOLOv8 was selected due to its stable implementation, strong detection performance, and mature pretrained ecosystem. Since field localisation acts only as a preprocessing stage in our pipeline, this
model provides reliable region detection without introducing additional architectural complexity. Let an input identity document image be denoted as
I
. The detector predicts a set of bounding boxes
\mathcal{B}=\{b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{K}\},
where each bounding box is defined as
b_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i},w_{i},h_{i},c_{i}),
representing the center coordinates, width, height, and predicted class label (face or text), respectively. The detected identity regions are then extracted through a cropping operation

I_{i}=\text{Crop}(I,b_{i}),

producing localized identity-field images
I_{i}
that are subsequently used for feature encoding. However, direct application of the pretrained YOLOv8 model resulted in inconsistent localization performance due to the domain-specific characteristics of identity documents and the variability in document layouts. Therefore, we fine-tuned YOLOv8 using the manually annotated face and text bounding boxes to learn identity-specific field localization. After fine-tuning, the detector is able to reliably identify face and textual regions across heterogeneous document formats, enabling consistent extraction of semantically meaningful identity fields for downstream forgery detection.

3.3
Feature Encoding using MobileNetV3

Following semantic region extraction, each detected identity field (face and textual region) is transformed into a compact visual representation using a lightweight convolutional feature encoder. We adopt MobileNetV3-Small pretrained on ImageNet-1K as the backbone due to its favorable trade-off between computational efficiency and representational power, making it suitable for deployment in large-scale identity verification systems where inference latency is critical. The MobileNetV3 backbone weights remain frozen during training; only the downstream classification head is optimized. This frozen-backbone design avoids overfitting on limited identity-document data while retaining the rich visual representations learned from large-scale pretraining.

Let
I_{f}
and
I_{t}
denote the cropped face and text field images extracted from an identity document. Each cropped region is resized to a fixed spatial resolution of
224\times 224
and normalized using ImageNet statistics prior to feature extraction. The processed regions are then passed through the MobileNetV3 encoder
\phi(\cdot)
, producing 576-dimensional field-level embeddings:

\mathbf{e}_{f}=\phi(I_{f}),\quad\mathbf{e}_{t}=\phi(I_{t}),

where
\mathbf{e}_{f},\mathbf{e}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{576}
denote the resulting feature vectors obtained from the final global average pooling layer. These embeddings capture identity-relevant visual cues such as facial geometry, skin-texture consistency, character rendering artifacts, and localized editing inconsistencies that may arise from tampering operations. To ensure efficient training and reproducibility, embeddings are precomputed and stored for all samples, enabling the subsequent classification stage to operate directly on compact feature representations without repeatedly processing raw images. This design reduces the trainable parameter count to only 191K in the classification head, compared to 2.55M trainable parameters required by the baseline approach, significantly accelerating convergence while preserving discriminative information necessary for forgery detection.

3.4
Multi-Field Feature Fusion and Classification

Forgery detection in identity documents often depends on analyzing
multiple semantic identity fields such as the facial photograph and
textual identity information. To incorporate information from these
distinct regions, the embeddings extracted from the face and textual
fields are combined to form a unified document-level representation.
Specifically, the field-level embeddings are concatenated as

\mathbf{z}=[\mathbf{e}_{f}\;||\;\mathbf{e}_{t}],

where
\mathbf{e}_{f},\mathbf{e}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the face
and text embeddings respectively, and

\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
represents the combined multi-field
feature vector. The concatenated representation is then processed by
a lightweight
feed-forward classification head

g(\cdot)

consisting of
L{=}5
fully connected layers with ReLU activations
and dropout regularisation:

\mathbf{h}_{l}=\operatorname{Dropout}\!\bigl(\operatorname{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_{l}\,\mathbf{h}_{l-1}+\mathbf{b}_{l})\bigr),\quad l=1,\dots,L{-}1,

\hat{y}=\mathbf{W}_{L}\,\mathbf{h}_{L-1}+\mathbf{b}_{L},

where
\mathbf{h}_{0}=\mathbf{z}
,
\mathbf{W}_{l}
and
\mathbf{b}_{l}

are learnable parameters, and
\hat{y}
is the output logit.
The model is optimised using binary cross-entropy loss with a
class-balancing weight
w^{+}=n_{\text{neg}}/n_{\text{pos}}
to
handle label imbalance:

\mathcal{L}=-\bigl[w^{+}\,y\log\sigma(\hat{y})+(1-y)\log\bigl(1-\sigma(\hat{y})\bigr)\bigr],

where
y\in\{0,1\}
denotes the ground-truth label indicating
bona fide or forged identity documents, and
\sigma(\cdot)
is
the sigmoid function applied internally by the loss. This
lightweight fusion-based classification module enables efficient
utilization of identity-specific features extracted from multiple
document regions while maintaining low computational complexity
suitable for real-time digital identity verification pipelines.

4
Performance Evaluation

4.1
Experimental Setup

All experiments employ a two-stage pipeline. Region-of-interest (ROI) fields are first localized using a fine-tuned YOLOv8 detector, after which 576-dimensional feature embeddings are extracted from the detected regions using a MobileNetV3-Small backbone pretrained on ImageNet-1K with the classification head removed. Input crops are resized to
224\times 224
and normalized using standard ImageNet statistics. The resulting frozen embeddings are processed by a lightweight neural network classifier for binary forgery detection (bona fide vs. attack). For the face attack scenario, data augmentation is applied to mitigate limited sample size; detailed augmentation strategies are provided in the supplementary material. The single-modality classifiers employ a five-layer fully connected neural network with ReLU activations and dropout (0.2–0.3), while the both-field attack model uses an analogous architecture with an expanded input layer. All models are trained using Adam (learning rate
10^{-3}
, weight decay
10^{-4}
, batch size 32) with binary cross-entropy loss with logits (
BCEWithLogitsLoss
) and class rebalancing via
pos_weight
. A
ReduceLROnPlateau
scheduler (factor 0.5, patience 5) and early stopping (patience 15, maximum 100 epochs) are applied. We employ stratified 5-fold cross-validation with an 80/20 train–test split in each fold. The split ratio is consistent with the protocol used by González and Tapia
[
8
]
. To ensure split integrity on this small-scale dataset, we verified that no document-level leakage exists across train and test partitions: the face attack split has zero document overlap. For the both-field attack scenario, 73 identity labels appear in both partitions; however, since the MobileNetV3 backbone is frozen and never updated on training data, we confirm this overlap is benign and does not inflate test performance (see Supplementary for full analysis). Due to the limited dataset size, no separate validation set is used, and all ISO/IEC 30107-3 metrics are computed on unseen test data. Experiments are implemented in PyTorch 2.2.0 on an Apple M-series processor.

Baseline:
As our primary baseline, we reimplement the approach of González and Tapia
[
8
]
, which trains a MobileNetV2 from scratch (random Kaiming initialization, no ImageNet pretraining) on full-document images resized to
448\times 448
with a 2-class softmax classifier head, optimized using Adam (learning rate
10^{-5}
). For both-field attacks, the baseline employs a two-stage cascade: separate face and text models are trained independently, and the final bona fide score is computed as
\min(P_{\text{face}}(\text{Real}),\;P_{\text{text}}(\text{Real}))
, classifying the document as forged if either field is flagged. This cascade design follows the paper’s original protocol. All baseline models are trained on the same data splits and evaluated under identical metrics for a fair comparison.

Evaluation Metrics:

To ensure a fair and standards-compliant comparison, we adopt the ISO/IEC 30107-3
[
4
,
3
]
PAD evaluation protocol, consistent with González and Tapia
[
8
]
. We report the Attack Presentation Classification Error Rate (APCER), which measures the proportion of attack samples incorrectly classified as bona fide, and the Bona Fide Presentation Classification Error Rate (BPCER), which quantifies the proportion of genuine samples misclassified as attacks. From these threshold-dependent measures, we derive the Equal Error Rate (EER), corresponding to the operating point where APCER and BPCER are equal. Additionally, we report BPCER
AP
values (e.g., BPCER
10
, BPCER
20
, BPCER
50
), which measure the false rejection rate of genuine users while fixing APCER to a predefined security level. These metrics collectively characterize the security–usability trade-off of the PAD system under deployment-relevant operating conditions. For completeness, we also report standard decision-level metrics including Accuracy and F1-score, as well as ROC curves and AUC to assess ranking performance across thresholds.

4.5
Comparison with General-Purpose Forgery Detectors

Research on forgery detection for structured digital identity documents remains limited. To contextualise performance, we compare FLiD against state-of-the-art image manipulation detection models originally developed for natural-image forensics. These methods provide publicly available pretrained models and weights. Although they are not specifically designed for identity-document forgery detection, evaluating them allows us to assess how well general-purpose manipulation detectors transfer to structured digital identity data.

reports Accuracy and Precision across face-only, text-only, and both-field manipulation scenarios. Overall, general-purpose manipulation detectors show limited transferability to structured digital identity documents. While TruFor achieves moderate performance in certain cases (e.g., 76.80% accuracy under text attacks), precision remains lower, indicating increased false positives. MMFusion and UniVAD degrade further across scenarios, highlighting the difficulty of distinguishing structured identity edits from benign layout variations. In contrast, FLiD consistently achieves the highest performance across all attack types, reaching 81.66%/69.23% (Acc/Prec) for face attacks, 88.39%/90.32% for text attacks, and 84.85%/90.48% for both-field manipulations. 4.6
Ablation Study

We conduct a systematic ablation study to validate each design choice
(full results in the supplementary material). Key findings: (i)field-level
ROI extraction yields a +0.44 AUC gain over whole-image input;
(ii)YOLO-detected and manual coordinate crops produce nearly identical
results, confirming robustness to localization noise; (iii)frozen backbone
outperforms end-to-end fine-tuning (AUC 0.925 vs. 0.507 for face),
validating that pretraining transfer with a fixed backbone avoids overfitting;
(iv)MobileNetV3-Small outperforms EfficientNet-B0 and ResNet-18 alternatives.

4.7
Limitations and Future Directions.

FLiD currently relies on visual feature representations to detect inconsistencies within identity-document fields and achieves strong performance on FantasyID (AUC =
0.954\pm 0.016
). In the current dataset, most text-editing attacks introduce subtle but detectable visual artifacts such as font inconsistencies, blending boundaries, or resolution mismatches, which can be effectively captured by frozen ImageNet features without requiring OCR or language-specific priors. This design enables a language-agnostic pipeline that generalises across diverse document templates and scripts. As generative editing tools continue to improve, however, future manipulations may become visually seamless while remaining semantically inconsistent. An important direction for future research is therefore multimodal visual–linguistic fusion, where appearance-based embeddings are complemented with OCR-derived semantic signals to detect semantically implausible but visually plausible edits.

Beyond binary detection, an additional direction is
forgery localisation
. Extending FLiD to produce pixel- or patch-level tampering maps would allow the system to highlight
where
a manipulation occurs within a field, supporting forensic analysis and human-in-the-loop verification workflows. Complementary to this, integrating model explainability techniques such as Grad-CAM or attention-based saliency visualisations could provide interpretable evidence of why a document is flagged as forged, improving transparency and operational trust in real-world deployments. Finally, while the current evaluation is conducted on FantasyID, one of the most diverse publicly available identity-document forgery benchmarks, future work will extend the evaluation to additional identity-document datasets to further assess cross-domain robustness and generalisation.

5
Conclusion

This paper introduced FLiD, a lightweight field-localized framework for detecting forgeries in structured digital identity documents. Unlike general-purpose manipulation detectors that analyse entire images, FLiD explicitly models identity documents as structured compositions of semantic fields and performs forgery detection at the field level. By combining identity-field localisation with compact feature extraction and a lightweight classifier, the proposed approach effectively captures inconsistencies introduced by face and text manipulations while maintaining very low computational complexity. Extensive experiments on the FantasyID benchmark demonstrate that FLiD consistently outperforms existing general-purpose forgery detectors across multiple manipulation scenarios while using significantly fewer parameters and computational operations. These results highlight the importance of domain-aware modelling for identity-document analysis and show that accurate and reliable forgery detection can be achieved with a compact and efficient architecture suitable for real-world digital identity verification systems.
