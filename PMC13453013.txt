Iron metabolism and ferroptosis in cardiovascular diseases: progress in the mechanism and pharmacological intervention
Authors: Yi Jiang, Jing Zhang, Liuting Zeng, Yulin Li, Dongjin Wang, Yang Li, Lingyun Sun, Fudong Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accumulating evidence highlights the pivotal role of ferroptosis in diverse cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) such as atherosclerosis, aortic aneurysm, aortic dissection, vascular aging, calcific aortic valve disease, myocardial ischemia reperfusion injury, cardiomyopathy, and heart failure. Studies on the molecular mechanism of ferroptosis have revealed potential targets for the treatment of CVDs, and a large number of studies have reported evidence of drug-mediated ferroptosis and iron metabolism mechanisms intervening in CVDs. This review delves into the latest molecular characteristics of ferroptosis, associated biological processes, and regulatory pathways. Notably, various compounds like iron chelators (e.g., deferoxamine, deferiprone), chloroiodohydroxyquin and its derivatives, antioxidants (e.g., vitamin E, lipoic acid, selenium), Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), etc., have demonstrated efficacy in animal studies against CVDs, offering cardiovascular protection. In summary, this review outlines the role of ferroptosis in CVDs, the regulatory effects of relevant drugs (including natural products) on ferroptosis within cardiovascular contexts, aiming to provide valuable insights for CVDs management and the development of novel therapeutics.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) comprise a range of conditions affecting the circulatory system, including coronary artery disease, arrhythmias, chronic heart failure (CHF), pulmonary hypertension, and ischemic cardiomyopathy [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Affecting over 500 million people worldwide, CVDs remain a leading cause of death, accounting for approximately 32% of global deaths [6,7].

Iron is an essential element trace for the orderly progress life of activities in eukaryotic cells participating, in the regulation of the cell cycle, cell metabolism, DNA synthesis, ribosome biogenesis, and various cyclic processes [8,9]. In the human body, iron is mainly derived from two sources: macrophages clear senescent and damaged red blood cells through phagocytosis, and the intestine absorbs dietary iron [10]. Maintaining strict iron homeostasis is the basis for ensuring normal life activities. In this process, iron - regulatory proteins (IRPs) and iron - responsive elements (IRE) synergistically regulate the release of cellular iron by sensing the iron ion concentration in mitochondria [10]. In addition, IRPs can also regulate the expression of key genes such as ferritin light chain (FTL)/ferritin heavy chain (FTH) and ferroportin (FPN), thereby comprehensively regulating the production, storage, and transport of iron to maintain intracellular iron homeostasis [11]. At the level of iron transport, transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1) promotes cellular iron uptake, while FPN1 mediates iron efflux; up - regulating FPN1 or down - regulating TFR1 can reduce intracellular iron levels, thereby reducing the cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis [12,13]. Meanwhile, the overexpression of divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1) can promote the entry of divalent iron ions into the labile iron pool, exacerbating intracellular iron accumulation [14]. As an iron - storage protein, ferritin protects cells from oxidative damage by chelating active iron, thus inhibiting the of occurrence ferroptosis [15]. The process of ferritin degradation induced by autophagy is called "ferritinophagy", which can release free iron ions and increase the intracellular ferrous iron level, and it is one of the important mechanisms by which autophagy promotes ferroptosis [16]. Excessive free iron ions in cells generate a large amount of reactive oxygen species (ROS) through the Fenton reaction, inducing oxidative damage, which is the core basis for the occurrence of ferroptosis [17]. Hepcidin, as the core regulator of iron homeostasis, also participates in the regulation of the ferroptosis process [18,19]. In summary, an excessively high level of free iron ions in cells is the basis for inducing ferroptosis and subsequently triggering various vascular injury - related diseases [20]. Therefore, targeting and improving cell - iron - dependent ferroptosis is of great significance for improving the prognosis of CVDs.

Emerging evidence highlights the critical role of iron homeostasis in cardiovascular pathophysiology. Beyond its traditional roles in oxygen transport and energy metabolism, disrupted iron dynamics have been linked to lipid peroxidation, inflammatory cascades, and redox imbalance, processes key to CVDs progression [21,22]. Intracellular iron is primarily stored within ferritin nanocages, composed of 24 subunits of FTH1 and FTL, protecting against iron-induced oxidative damage [23]. The selective breakdown of ferritin through ferritinophagy, mediated by nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4), has emerged as a key regulator of iron bioavailability and susceptibility to ferroptosis [24]. Ferroptosis, distinct from apoptosis or necrosis, is an iron-dependent form of cell death characterized by redox imbalance and iron accumulation [25]. This unique cell death involves the oxidation of membrane lipids, leading to functional impairment, structural damage, and cell death [[26], [27], [28]].

Previous studies have shown the significant role of ferroptosis in various diseases, including metabolic, inflammatory, acute organ damage, cancer, and neurodegenerative diseases [[29], [30], [31]]. Recent research has emphasized the importance of ferroptosis and its regulatory mechanisms in CVDs such as myocardial ischemia reperfusion injury (MIRI), cardiomyopathy, and HF [[32], [33], [34], [35]]. Thus, the development of ferroptosis in CVDs offers potential new therapeutic targets for disease treatment. Despite increasing interest, current reviews have not comprehensively integrated the dual aspects of iron metabolism dysregulation and ferroptosis execution in cardiovascular contexts [36,37]. With hundreds of publications on ferroptosis annually since its introduction in 2012, there is a timely need to consolidate mechanistic advances and translational applications (Figure_ 1). Inducers and inhibitors of ferroptosis

Following the discovery of erastin as a ferroptosis inducer, ferroptosis-based strategies for targeting tumor cells, notably drug-resistant cancer cells, have attracted extensive scholarly attention. In contrast, relevant therapeutic agents for cardiovascular disorders are still limited [38,39]. Regulatory strategies primarily focus on targeting key enzymes in inhibitory regulatory axes and their pathway inhibitors to induce ferroptosis, while inhibitors of positive regulatory aixes suppress it [27,40]. Inducers of ferroptosis

Classical ferroptosis inducers primarily target the System Xc−-Glutathione Peroxidase 4 (GPX4) axis. In the context of System Xc− modulation, among the inducers acting on the Xc− system, erastin inhibits cystine uptake, leading to glutathione (GSH) depletion and GPX4 inactivation. However, its poor solubility and stability limit its direct in vivo application. Its analogs such as piperazine erastin and imidazolone erastin (IKE), and the latter shows better pharmacokinetic properties in animal models [[41], [42], [43]]. Erastin and its analogs, which inhibit the Xc− system, lead to ferroptosis in cells. Relevant effects have been observed in Friedreich ataxia, Vascular calcification, and Diabetic Cardiomyopathy.

GPX4 inhibitors, as ferroptosis-inducing agents, effectively trigger ferroptosis in cells by promoting lipid ROS generation [61]. The GPX4 inhibitor RAS synthetic lethal molecule 3 (RSL3) was first reported in 2008 [62]. It can covalently bind to the selenocysteine residue at the active site of GPX4, thereby irreversibly inhibiting the function of GPX4 [44,45]. Different from RSL3, Ganglioside M3 (GM3) does not directly target GPX4. It interferes with iron uptake by binding to the extracellular domain of TFR1 and is regulated by Krüppel-like factor 9 (KLF9) [46]. It restricts the availability of iron to indirectly weaken the antioxidant function of GPX4. This mechanism provides a new perspective for understanding the upstream regulation of ferroptosis.

Inhibitors of ferroptosis

Inhibitors of ferroptosis primarily aim to hinder iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, including radical-trapping antioxidants (RTA), iron chelators, and various broad-spectrum inhibitors [63]. Through high-throughput screening methods, Ferristatin-1 (Fer-1) has emerged as a powerful inhibitor of ferroptosis, proficiently halting ferroptosis triggered by erastin and RSL3 [64]. Currently, Fer-1 has been applied in the research of CVDs such as atherosclerosis (AS), abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA), MIRI, and Friedreich's ataxia (FRDA) [42,[49], [50], [51]]. In addition to Fer-1, other small molecule inhibitors also show potential for cardiovascular protection: Iron chelators (DFO, Deferoxamine; DFP, Deferiprone; DFX, Deferasirox) alleviate iron-overload cardiomyopathy by reducing the level of free iron [47,48]. Recent investigations reveal that Liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1), a specific inhibitor of lipid peroxidation, reduces ROS production in GPX4−/− cells via a mechanism independent of protein targeting [65].

In contrast, 7-dehydrocholesterol reductase (DHCR7) was not initially identified as a key regulatory factor. However, recent studies have uncovered that DHCR7 converts 7-Dehydrocholesterol (7-DHC) to cholesterol [66]. Notably, exogenous administration of 7-DHC confers protection against ferroptosis across various cell lines. This protective effect was also evident in a genetic model lacking GPX4, establishing 7-DHC as a natural suppressor of ferroptosis [52]. Nimodipine is a calcium channel blocker that can reduce Activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) expression and inhibit AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activation, thereby alleviating autophagy-dependent ferroptotic cell death [57]. Nicorandil is a K+ channel opener that can activate AMPK to upregulate Parkin expression and downregulate acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) levels, thus inhibiting mitochondrial ferroptosis [58]. MitoTEMPO can specifically scavenge mitochondrial ROS and restore mitochondrial integrity, thereby blocking the main source of lipid peroxidation [59]. Bortezomib is a proteasome inhibitor that can maintain protein homeostasis (proteostasis) and prevent the accumulation of misfolded proteins, which would otherwise make cells more sensitive to ferroptosis [59]. Hydrogen sulfide (H2S), as an endogenous gaseous signaling molecule, exerts an anti-ferroptotic effect by increasing Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity and reducing malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, thereby alleviating lipid peroxidation [60].

In recent years, some natural products have also been reported to possess ferroptosis inhibitory activity (Table 3). Different from direct - acting molecules such as Fer-1, these natural products usually exert their effects in a multi-target and indirect manner. For example, they can activate the Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2/antioxidant response element (NRF2/ARE) antioxidant pathway, up-regulate the expression of GPX4 and Solute Carrier Family 7 Member 11 (SLC7A11), or scavenge various ROS. Their scope of action far exceeds the ferroptosis pathway, and they can also inhibit apoptosis, autophagic death, and inflammatory responses. The role of ferroptosis and iron metabolism in CVDs

CVDs remain the predominant global cause of mortality, highlighting the urgency to intensify both preventive and therapeutic measures [77]. Cardiomyocytes, as terminally differentiated cells possessing restricted regenerative potential, are exceptionally susceptible to cell death. This initiates an unrecoverable structural remodeling and a decrement in cardiac function. Mounting evidence points to ferroptosis as a key factor leading to cardiac decompensation and end-stage cardiac malfunction [78]. In addition, ferroptosis also plays a crucial role in vascular diseases and heart valves.

Iron metabolism, ferroptosis, and vascular wall lesion

Atherosclerosis

Mechanisms of ferroptosis in atherosclerosis

AS is the main pathophysiological basis of CVDs, manifested as chronic inflammatory arterial remodeling, which is characterized by the formation of lipid plaques [79]. In advanced atherosclerotic lesions, plaque rupture leads to structural instability, which in turn causes vascular occlusion and ischemic necrosis. The pathogenesis of this disease involves complex interactions among endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and macrophage-mediated lipid retention.

Disturbances in iron homeostasis have a bidirectional impact on the development of AS. The classic "iron hypothesis" states that systemic iron overload increases the risk of CVDs, while controlling iron deficiency can protect blood vessels [80]. Histopathological studies have confirmed that atherosclerotic plaques have a higher iron deposition compared to healthy blood vessels, and this phenomenon can be observed from the early stages of atherosclerosis [81]. Macrophages recycle iron from senescent red blood cells, and intra-plaque hemorrhage exacerbates the phagocytosis of red blood cells, which is considered a key mechanism for iron deposition [82].

On the one hand, iron accumulates in macrophages with low Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) expression. These macrophages have increased iron sensitivity due to insufficient oxidative phosphorylation metabolism [59]. This sensitivity stems from the activation of the heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) - lactate dehydrogenase B (LDHB) axis, leading to lon peptidase 1 (LONP1) - mediated degradation of mitochondrial mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM), and subsequently causing bioenergetic collapse. Under hypoxic conditions, the degradation of Egl-9 family hypoxia-inducible factor 3 (Egln3) maintains the stability of hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF1α), which also enhances the transcription of HO-1, establishing an axis of ferroptosis - plaque instability [83]. Recent studies have found that tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase 5 (TRAP/ACP5) is highly expressed in macrophages of AS plaques. It promotes macrophage ferroptosis and M1 polarization by regulating the phosphorylation of voltage-dependent anion channel 3 (VDAC3), thereby exacerbating the progression of AS [84].

On the other hand, an increase in intracellular iron concentration enhances the uptake of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL), hinders cholesterol efflux, and accelerates the formation of foam cells [85]. This process leads to a decrease in GPX4 expression, an excessive production of ROS, and an exacerbation of lipid peroxidation - all three together exacerbate intracellular oxidative stress [49,[86], [87], [88], [89], [90]], directly aggravating endothelial dysfunction and ultimately increasing plaque instability [91].

Therapeutic interventions targeting ferroptosis in atherosclerosis

In terms of treatment, Bai et al. found that administration of saturated iron transferrin enhanced ox-LDL-induced ferroptosis in aortic endothelial cells, and this effect could be attenuated by iron chelators [49]. The crucial role of iron overload in ferroptosis induction was further verified by the ferric ammonium citrate (FAC) model. Zhao et al. found that Fer-1 could rescue the viability of FAC-treated cells [92]. In vivo studies also showed that FAC could exacerbate ox-LDL-induced foam cell damage in a high-fat model. In contrast, iron chelators (such as DFO) and dietary iron restriction could significantly reduce iron deposition, alleviate cell damage, reduce plaque volume, and enhance plaque stability [47,93,94]. Consistent with these findings, studies on RAW264.7 macrophages confirmed that both Fer-1 and DFO could upregulate the expression of GPX4 and xCT and alleviate ox-LDL-induced damage. At the molecular level, RSL3 selectively inactivates GPX4, triggering ferroptosis through ROS accumulation and elevated labile iron pool (LIP) levels [44]. Notably, RSL3-induced ferroptotic cells produce oxidized phospholipids (SAPE-OOH), which bind to Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) on phagocytes, promoting cell phagocytosis and thus spreading ferroptosis [95]. The ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 partially inhibited iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation in ApoE−/− and toxemia, while restoring the expression of SLC7A11 and GPX4 and alleviating endothelial cell dysfunction [49].

In natural product intervention studies, the dietary flavonoid quercetin has been found to alleviate ox-LDL-induced endothelial ferroptosis by restoring phosphofurin acidic cluster sorting protein 2 (PACS2) expression and inhibiting the HO-1 pathway, thereby improving endothelial dysfunction and the stability of atherosclerotic plaques [73]. In addition, the NRF2 signaling pathway has emerged as a crucial defense mechanism against CVDs [96]. For example, the active compound polydatin extracted from Polygonum cuspidatum inhibits ferroptosis by activating the nuclear translocation of NRF2 and upregulating the expression of GPX4 and SLC7A11, thereby exerting anti-atherosclerotic effects and protecting vascular endothelium in vitro and in vivo models [97]. Specifically, NRF2 regulates iron metabolism by controlling the expression of FTH1 and FTL, which are essential for iron storage and detoxification [98]. Meanwhile, NRF2 also regulates lipid peroxidation by modulating the expression of xCT and GPX4 [99], and is involved in maintaining iron homeostasis, responding to oxidative stress, activating inflammatory cytokines, and regulating cell growth or apoptosis in an iron-dependent manner. Therefore, therapeutic strategies targeting NRF2 may offer new approaches for the treatment of AS. However, the role of NRF2 in AS is significantly dependent on cell types and disease stages. In early plaques, NRF2 activation can protect endothelial function and inhibit apoptosis of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs). However, in advanced lesions, the continuous and excessive activation of NRF2 may promote lipid accumulation in macrophages and the formation of foam cells, and even induce the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs towards calcification, thereby exacerbating plaque instability [96]. Therefore, targeted NRF2 therapy for AS requires precise regulation of its activation intensity and action window in the future.

Similarly, 6-Gingerol (6-Gin) is the primary active ingredient in ginger. It alleviates AS by inhibiting endothelial cell ferroptosis [75]. Specifically, 6-Gin suppresses iron accumulation, inflammatory responses, and lipid peroxidation. This effect is supported by decreased levels of ROS and MDA. Concurrently, it restores GSH levels and promotes cell proliferation. Mechanistically, these protective effects are driven by the nuclear translocation of NRF2 and the subsequent upregulation of HO-1 and quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1) [75]. Furthermore, the NRF2 inhibitor all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) abrogates these beneficial effects, confirming that 6-Gin suppresses ferroptosis via activation of the NRF2/HO-1 pathway. On the other hand, pharmacological interventions targeting mitochondrial ROS (MitoTEMPO) or proteostasis (bortezomib) can restore mitochondrial integrity, showing potential for treatment [59] (Figure_ 2). Notably, the activation of the NRF2/HO-1 pathway also bidirectional effects in atherosclerosis [100]. Although HO-1 has antioxidant effects, the free iron and CO produced by its catalysis of heme degradation may promote oxidative damage and inflammation when in excess, especially in an iron-overloaded environment. In addition, the transient activation of NRF2 is beneficial for scavenging ROS, while long-term chronic activation may deplete the intracellular GSH reserve and weaken the subsequent antioxidant capacity. Aortic aneurysm

Mechanisms of ferroptosis in aortic aneurysm

AAA is defined as progressive dilation of the abdominal aorta exceeding 50% of its normal diameter [101,102]. Pathological processes driving AAA development include oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, VSMCs loss, extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, and vascular calcification [103].

Ferroptosis is implicated in AAA pathogenesis, with studies detecting iron accumulation and dysregulated expression of transferrin, TFR1, and ferritin in AAA arterial walls [55,104]. These findings suggest tissue iron overload contributes to aortic wall destabilization [105]. In terms of oxidative stress, GPX4, a key ferroptosis regulator that eliminates lipid peroxides, alleviates AAA progression. Reduced GPX4 expression in AAA models promotes lipid peroxide accumulation and ferroptosis, whereas GPX4 upregulation inhibits AAA formation [106,107]. Clinically, Ji et al. observed diminished GPX4 levels in hypertensive patients correlated with a pro-inflammatory VSMCs phenotype. Wiernicki et al. further documented impaired antioxidant enzyme activities across AAA disease stages, highlighting GPX4/GSH system disruption as a driver of ROS-dependent ferroptosis [108]. Collectively, these studies emphasize ferroptosis-mediated redox imbalance in AAA and underscore therapeutic potential in targeting this pathway [109].

In terms of the absence of VSMCs, Ferroptosis critically regulates VSMCs dysfunction and loss. Ji et al. reported that ferroptosis induces VSMCs dedifferentiation, while the ferroptosis inducer RSL3 exacerbates neointima formation and VSMCs phenotypic switching [45]. Specifically, RSL3 downregulates contractile markers (smooth muscle myosin heavy chain, SMMHC; calmodulin 1, CaM1) and upregulates synthetic markers (osteopontin, OPN) in rat VSMCs. Mechanistically, high hydrostatic pressure (HHP) upregulates iron accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis genes in VSMCs, depleting GSH and H2S to trigger ferroptosis. Similarly, cigarette smoke extract (CSE) induces GSH depletion and lipid peroxidation in VSMCs, precipitating ferroptotic cell death [110]. Zhang et al. identified ganglioside GM3 as a ferroptosis promoter via multi-omics analysis, demonstrating that GM3 binds TFR1's extracellular domain to disrupt holo-transferrin (holo-Tf)/TFR1 interaction. Ruptured AAA patients exhibit more pronounced aortic calcification than elective surgery patients, with calcification heterogeneity reflecting disease progression [112,113]. Mechanistic studies have revealed that ferroptosis directly promotes VSMC calcification. For instance, the ferroptosis inducer erastin depletes GSH and induces VSMC calcification [41]. Furthermore, Chen et al. demonstrated that PA activates the cGAS-STING pathway to induce ferritinophagy-dependent ferroptosis in VSMCs, providing a novel mechanistic link between lipid metabolism, iron homeostasis, and vascular calcification [115].

Therapeutic interventions targeting ferroptosis in aortic aneurysm

Given the central role of ferroptosis in AAA pathogenesis, targeting this cell death pathway has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. The ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 has been shown to restore mitochondrial homeostasis by suppressing ROS overproduction and mitochondrial hyperpolarization, thereby inhibiting VSMC phenotypic transition [50]. In the context of vascular calcification, Fer-1 dose-dependently inhibits mineral deposition and mitigates calcification in vitamin D3-overloaded mice [41]. These findings suggest that pharmacological inhibition of ferroptosis not only preserves VSMC function but also counteracts the calcification process that exacerbates AAA progression.

Beyond Fer-1, other interventions have demonstrated efficacy. Sawada et al. showed that dietary iron restriction in Ang II-induced AAA models reduces iron deposition, oxidative stress, and inflammation, leading to decreased AAA incidence [116]. Iron chelation strategies, though primarily studied in atherosclerosis, may also hold promise for AAA. In addition, Han et al. identified PIM1 as an iron death-related biomarker, opening up new avenues for the research of diagnostic and therapeutic targets [117].

Aortic dissection

Mechanisms of ferroptosis in aortic dissection

Aortic dissection (AD) is an acute and life-threatening vascular condition. Notably, the mortality rate of untreated dissection reaches 50% within 48 h [118,119]. This disorder arises from a tear in the aortic intima and media, resulting in the formation of true and false lumens [120]. A critical pathological hallmark of AD involves the loss of VSMCs and disruption of elastic fibers within the aortic media [[121], [122], [123]]. Emerging evidence indicates a close association between ferroptosis and AD pathogenesis [124].

Compared to healthy controls, the aortic wall in thoracic aortic dissection (TAD) patients exhibits significant reduction in extracellular superoxide dismutase (EC-SOD). Concurrently, elevated MDA levels in aortic homogenates reflect excessive lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress. Chen et al. demonstrated marked downregulation of SLC7A11, ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1), and GTP cyclohydrolase 1 (GCH1) expression in the AD aortic wall, indicating suppression of the Xc− - GPX4, FSP1 - coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10), and GCH1-tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) antioxidant systems [125]. Therapeutic interventions targeting ferroptosis in aortic dissection

Therapeutic strategies targeting oxidative stress and redox balance show efficacy in AD management. In the mouse β-Aminopropionitrile (BAPN) + angiotensin II (ANGII) model, H2S attenuates IL-6-induced ROS and nitric oxide (NO) production in VSMCs, inhibits their transition to a synthetic phenotype, while enhancing aortic SOD activity, reducing MDA levels, and decreasing AD incidence and mortality [60]. Similarly, ursodeoxycholic acid upregulates NRF2 expression, maintains redox enzyme activity (Cu/Zn-SOD, Mn-SOD, CAT), inhibits VSMCs apoptosis, and delays AD progression [71]. Notably, the ferroptosis inhibitor BRD4770 effectively stabilizes the Xc−-GPX4, FSP1-CoQ10, and GCH1-BH4 systems, suppressing ferroptosis and inflammatory infiltration in aortic VSMCs, thereby preventing AD development [55,126]. Mechanistically, methyltransferase-like protein 3 (METTL3) promotes ferroptosis induced by IKE or cystine deprivation, while its inhibition protects VSMCs from ferroptotic death; overexpression of SLC7A11 or FSP1 reverses this effect in METTL3-high VSMCs [127]. Furthermore, histone acetyltransferase P300 deletion exacerbates VSMCs ferroptosis via activation of the HIF-1α/HO-1 pathway [128]. Recent studies highlight silymarin, a natural flavonoid, as exerting cardioprotective effects by alleviating VSMC ferroptosis through regulation of HO-1, iron homeostasis, and ER stress-induced serine biosynthesis [72]. Additionally, CD4+ T-cell ferroptosis contributes to AD pathogenesis; restoration of CD4+ T-cell function through targeting CD36 and ferroptosis represents a promising therapeutic strategy for improving postoperative outcomes [129]. Collectively, these findings unveil novel avenues for AD drug development.

Vascular aging

Vascular aging refers to the gradual degenerative changes that occur in the vascular system, mainly manifested as thickening of the arterial wall, rupture of elastic fibers, and accumulation of atherosclerotic plaques [130]. The latest research shows that ferroptosis plays an important role in accelerating vascular aging. This mechanism mainly leads to endothelial dysfunction through oxidative stress and increases arterial stiffness by regulating the senescence of VSMCs [131].

In senescent vascular VSMCs and arteries of aged mice, activated ferroptosis signals lead to nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) depletion, followed by the secretion of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [132]. Ferroptosis promotes the senescence of VSMCs through the NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy pathway, exacerbates vascular inflammation, and promotes pathological calcification. Notably, inhibition of ferroptosis triggers the nucleocytoplasmic translocation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), effectively preventing the formation of ferritin complexes and thus preventing ferritinophagy. These important findings reveal the molecular link between iron dysregulation and vascular aging, paving the way for new therapeutic approaches targeting age-related CVDs.

It should be noted that vascular aging is a progressive degenerative process involving multiple factors. Despite the therapeutic potential of ferroptosis inhibitors demonstrated in preclinical studies, ferroptosis-targeted drugs for vascular aging still await systematic clinical validation and translational advancement.

Iron metabolism, ferroptosis and heart disease

Calcific aortic valve disease

Mechanisms of ferroptosis in calcific aortic valve disease

Calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD) is a degenerative disorder characterized by ECM calcification and obstruction of the left ventricular outflow tract. Key molecular mechanisms underpinning CAVD encompass endothelial injury, lipid accumulation, sterile inflammation, ECM remodeling, and hydroxyapatite formation [133,134]. Emerging evidence ​indicates a crucial role for iron in both valvular and arterial calcification [135]. Specifically, calcified and stenotic aortic valves exhibit features of intra-leaflet hemorrhage, accumulation of red blood cells, and iron deposition. Notably, exposure of human valve interstitial cells (hVICs) to excessive iron induces iron overload and triggers alterations in gene expression.

Ferritin, a recognized iron chelator possessing ferroxidase activity, is highly expressed (H-ferritin) in stenotic aortic valves even in the absence of significant iron accumulation; this expression is accompanied by elevated levels of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) [[136], [137], [138]]. Crucially, iron overload in VICs induces downregulation of TFR1 and upregulation of ferritin light and heavy chains, thereby increasing intracellular iron levels. This elevated iron concentration subsequently activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, resulting in enhanced cell proliferation, reduced elastin production, and elevated levels of TNF-α and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) [139]. Proliferation may lead to cysteine deficiency, which disrupts the Cys - GSH - GPX4 ferroptosis defense mechanism, thereby rendering VICs more susceptible to lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis triggered by ROS. Crucially, recent transcriptomic evidence identifies the arachidonate 5-lipoxygenase (ALOX5) - ACSL4 axis as the dominant pathway driving this valvular lipid peroxidation, and elevated arachidonic acid levels have been shown to independently predict the clinical incidence of aortic stenosis [140]. Subsequently, macrophages clear these ferroptotic cells, initiating inflammatory responses and promoting calcification.

Therapeutic interventions targeting ferroptosis in calcific aortic valve disease

In an aortic calcification model associated with chronic kidney disease (CKD), the application of iron-based phosphate chelators, such as chitosan and iron citrate, has been found to mitigate the osteoblast phenotypic transformation in vascular endothelial cells and VSMCs [137,138]. Conversely, in a CKD model subjected to iron supplementation, upregulation of ferritin is induced and autophagy is enhanced, consequently reducing apoptosis in the context of CAVD [137,141,142]. Additionally, pharmacological inhibition of ALOX5 using Zileuton effectively reverses lipid peroxidation and calcification in VICs, significantly reducing valve thickness and improving hemodynamic function in vivo [140].

Myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury

Mechanisms of ferroptosis in Myocardial Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury

MI results from the death of myocardial cells and subsequent ventricular remodeling due to coronary artery occlusion, often resulting in a poor prognosis. Unfortunately, early intervention strategies for MI remain limited [143]. Recent studies continue to explore novel cardioprotective pathways. For instance, Varanasi et al. demonstrated that Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor B (VEGF-B) signaling through its co-receptor Neuropilin-1 (NRP1) confers protection against ischemic injury by reducing ferroptosis activation, lowering ROS, and preserving mitochondrial integrity in cardiomyocytes [144]. Recent research sheds light on ferroptosis as a significant factor contributing to cardiomyocyte death during MIRI. The pathogenesis of MIRI encompasses a complex interplay of various pathological processes, including inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and dysregulation of intracellular iron and calcium homeostasis, ultimately leading to apoptosis and necrosis [[145], [146], [147]]. In murine models of MIRI induced by left anterior descending coronary artery ligation, Baba et al. observed iron accumulation within myocardial scar tissue as soon as 30 min post-injury, with TFR1-mediated iron overload contributing to cardiomyocyte death [148].

During MIRI, cardiomyocytes display dynamic metabolic changes leading to multiple stress responses. These include misfolded protein accumulation, aberrant lipid synthesis, mitochondrial energy metabolism impairment, and excessive free radical generation, all of which can converge on ferroptosis through several interconnected mechanisms:(1)ACSL4-Dependent Lipid Remodeling: In rat MIRI models, ischemia-reperfusion intervention triggers an upregulation of ACSL4, facilitating the conversion of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) to phosphatidylethanolamines (PEs) [149]. This is compounded by lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3) - mediated esterification and the pro-oxidative actions of 15-lipoxygenase/phosphatidylethanolamine-binding protein 1 (15-LOX/PEBP1) complex [51]. Increased lipid peroxidation accelerates cardiomyocyte ferroptosis and cardiac dysfunction, a process that can be mitigated by Fer-1 via disruption of this enzymatic complex.(2)NRF2 Signaling: Under physiological conditions, NRF2 interacts with kelch-like ech-associated protein 1 (KEAP1) and promotes the degradation of KEAP1. During oxidative stress, NRF2 dissociates from KEAP1, translocating to the nucleus to activate AREs [150]. In the context of MIRI, cardiomyocytes upregulate NRF2, enhancing the expression of various antioxidant enzymes such as HO-1 and SOD [151,152]. Notably, NRF2 not only promotes HO-1 expression but also regulates GPX4 and GSH synthesis, directly inhibiting ferroptosis. However, NRF2 knockdown reduces the expression of SLC7A11, impairing System Xc− activity and GSH production, which consequently diminishes GPX4 levels [153]. Thus, the KEAP1/NRF2/ARE axis plays a multifaceted role in mitigating oxidative stress and ferroptosis. However, during ischemia, activating NRF2 can play a protective role by preserving antioxidant reserves. In the early stage of reperfusion, the sudden surge in NRF2-driven HO-1 expression may release labile iron and amplify the Fenton reaction, exacerbating oxidative damage [154].(3)ATF4-Mediated ER Stress: ATF4 serves as a pivotal mediator of ER stress responses. During MIRI, increased ATF4 expression correlates with lipid peroxide accumulation and the expression of ER stress-related proteins [155,156]. ATF4 activation, however, may downregulate SLC7A11, further compromising System Xc− activity and enhancing susceptibility to ferroptosis [157]. However, mild or transient endoplasmic reticulum stress activates the adaptive unfolded protein response (UPR) program, upregulates antioxidant enzymes and chaperone molecules, and thus protects against oxidative damage. Prolonged or severe endoplasmic reticulum stress triggers C/EBP homologous protein-mediated (CHOP) apoptosis and ferroptosis [158]. The transition between cell adaptation and death depends on the duration and intensity of ATF4 activation and the redox state of [159]. Conversely, Beclin1 downregulation elevates SLC7A11 and GPX4 levels, reducing ferroptosis [[160], [161], [162]]. Expanding on this interplay, sphingosine kinase 2 (Sphk2) has been identified as a novel ferroptosis suppressor in ischemic hearts: Sphk2 stabilizes heat shock protein 90 (HSP90), which promotes transcription factor EB (TFEB) nuclear translocation, enhances lysosome biogenesis, and facilitates autophagic clearance of lipid-ROS, thereby mitigating lipid peroxidation and post-MI cardiac injury [163]. However, autophagy in MIRI is a double-edged sword. Basal autophagy plays a protective role by clearing damaged mitochondria, but excessive autophagy triggered by overactivation of AMPK can degrade GPX4 and ferritin, promoting ferroptosis [164]. While upregulation of Rubicon in the late stage of reperfusion inhibits autophagic flux, to leading the accumulation aut ofophagosomes, which in turn triggers excessive autophagy and ultimately induces autosisPM [165]. Therefore, autophagy regulation should focus on restoring homeostasis rather than simply inhibiting or activating it.

However, autophagy in MIRI is a double-edged sword. Basal autophagy plays a protective role by clearing damaged mitochondria, but excessive autophagy triggered by overactivation of AMPK can degrade GPX4 and ferritin, promoting ferroptosis [164]. While upregulation of Rubicon in the late stage of reperfusion inhibits autophagic flux, to leading the accumulation aut ofophagosomes, which in turn triggers excessive autophagy and ultimately induces autosisPM [165]. Therefore, autophagy regulation should focus on restoring homeostasis rather than simply inhibiting or activating it.

Therapeutic interventions targeting ferroptosis in Myocardial Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury

Notably, the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 has been shown to markedly reduce MIRI severity in ex vivo rat models, highlighting ferroptosis as a viable therapeutic target [156]. In line with this observation, long-term pharmacological strategies for managing MIRI are increasingly focused on the regulation of ferroptosis. Commonly used cardiovascular drugs, including calcium channel blockers, β-blockers, and statins, have been implicated in modulating ferroptosis-related pathways [166]. For instance, the calcium channel blocker nimodipine may suppress autophagy through reduced ATF4 expression and AMPK inhibition [167]. Similarly, β-blockers may contribute to ferroptosis attenuation by interfering with Beclin1, potentially via adrenergic signaling pathways linked to autophagy [168]. Atorvastatin, a widely prescribed statin, has been shown to enhance ventricular remodeling by inhibiting ferroptosis in isoproterenol-induced injury models [54].

Beyond these established agents, single-cell sequencing has revealed Hspa8 as a potential diagnostic marker for MIRI; Hspa8 mitigates ferroptosis by facilitating ferritinophagy to clear free iron, preserving mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation capacity and suppressing ROS accumulation [169]. Septic cardiomyopathy

Mechanisms of ferroptosis in septic cardiomyopathy

Infection represents a predominant cause of mortality in acute and critically ill patients, frequently complicated by infectious cardiomyopathy, a severe condition associated with substantially elevated fatality rates among infected individuals [170]. Clinically, this cardiomyopathy manifests as left ventricular dilation, systolic and diastolic dysfunction, reduced ejection fraction, and hemodynamic shock [171]. The underlying myocardial impairment involves multifaceted mechanisms including apoptotic cell death, dysregulated inflammatory mediator release, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress.

Critically, ferroptosis emerges as a pivotal driver of myocardial injury in infectious cardiomyopathy [172]. Exacerbated oxidative stress constitutes a well-established pathological hallmark of this condition. Cardiomyocyte redox imbalance is characterized by excessive generation of ROS/reactive nitrogen species (RNS), coupled with depletion of endogenous antioxidant reserves. The cellular antioxidant defense system comprises enzymatic components (e.g., SOD, GPX, catalase(CAT)) and non-enzymatic elements (e.g., reduced GSH, vitamins A, C, and E, melatonin, specific metal ions). In a lipopolysaccharide (LPS) - induced infected mouse model, the expression of NCOA4 is upregulated, promoting the decomposition of ferritin and the release of free iron. During infection, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) directly or indirectly suppress hepatic biosynthesis of the selenoprotein GPX4 [170,173]. Consequently, diminished GPX4 quantity and activity in cardiomyocytes trigger lipid peroxidation, inflammatory factor activation, amplified oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction [174], collectively exacerbating myocardial damage [175,176].

Mitochondrial iron overload increases the production of ROS and oxidative stress, leading to impaired mitochondrial respiratory function, depolarization of membrane potential, and mitochondrial swelling or rupture [177,178]. The mitochondrial calcium uniporter (MCU) is one of the important pathways for Fe2+ to enter cardiac mitochondria [179,180].

Therapeutic interventions targeting ferroptosis in septic cardiomyopathy

To counteract ferroptosis-induced myocardial injury, several pharmacological strategies have been investigated. In terms of treatment, it is particularly important to prevent the penetration of intracellular free iron into mitochondria and inhibit oxidative stress within organelles. The MCU inhibitor Ru360 provides myocardial protection by inhibiting the generation of ROS [181]. Aloe-emodin significantly inhibits iron overload and lipid peroxidation in cardiomyocytes in an isoproterenol-induced infected rat model, thereby enhancing cardiac function [57]. Dexmedetomidine may counteract infection - induced ferroptosis of cardiomyocytes and cardiac injury by increasing the expression of GPX4 [182]. The heavy - metal scavenger metallothionein (MT) can reduce oxidative stress and ferroptosis, and alleviate LPS - induced cardiac remodeling and systolic dysfunction [183].

Cardiomyopathy induced by doxorubicin

Mechanisms of ferroptosis in cardiomyopathy induced by doxorubicin

Doxorubicin (DOX) is a widely used anthracycline anticancer drug that often causes dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) [184]. DCM is typically defined as the presence of left ventricular or biventricular dilation or systolic dysfunction without any abnormal load conditions (such as primary valvular diseases) or severe coronary artery disease that would cause ventricular remodeling [185].

Experimental studies reveal that DOX potentiates NRF2-mediated transcriptional activation, initiating heme degradation, iron overload, and lipid peroxidation cascades that culminate in cardiomyocyte ferroptosis [186]. Similarly, the SIRT1/NRF2 pathway exhibits a dual role in DOX-induced cardiomyopathy. On one hand, sirtuin1 deficiency exacerbates DOX-induced cardiac ferroptosis via dysregulation of the NRF2/KEAP1 axis [187]; on the other hand, excessive SIRT1 activation may deacetylate and activate p53, thereby promoting apoptosis, whereas moderate SIRT1 activity helps maintain mitochondrial function [188]. Moreover, protein arginine methyltransferase 4 (PRMT4) promotes ferroptosis by suppressing the NRF2/GPX4 pathway, thereby aggravating cardiac injury [189]. Complementary mechanisms include EP1-mediated activation of protein kinase C (PKC)/NRF2 signaling [190] and phenacetin-induced SIRT1/NRF2 pathway activation [191], both conferring protection against ferroptotic cell death.

Mitochondrial dysregulation plays a central role in DOX cardiotoxicity. DOX penetrates mitochondrial DNA and disrupts aminolevulinic acid synthase 1 (ALAS1)-dependent heme synthesis, triggering ferroptosis [192]. The mitochondrial outer membrane protein fun14 domain containing 2 (FUNDC2) interacts with GSH transporter SLC25A11 to regulate mitoGSH homeostasis, critically influencing DOX-induced injury [192,193]. Epigenetic modifications further modulate ferroptosis sensitivity. Methyltransferase-like protein 14 (METTL14) promotes DOX-induced ferroptosis via the kcnq1 opposite strand/antisense transcript 1 (KCNQ1OT1) - miR-7-5p - transferrin receptor (TFRC) axis [194], while the ubiquitin ligase mitochondrial ubiquitin ligase (MITOL)/membrane-associated ring-ch-type finger 5 (MARCH 5) regulates GSH homeostasis to influence cardiomyocyte vulnerability [195].

Therapeutic interventions targeting ferroptosis in cardiomyopathy induced by doxorubicin

Pharmacological inhibition of ferroptosis significantly attenuates DOX cardiotoxicity, reduces myocardial iron deposition, suppresses HO-1 expression, and improves survival in murine models [196]. Notably, 2-vinyl-10H-phenothiazine derivatives, ferroptosis inhibitors with minimal impact on human ether-a-go-go related gene (hERG) channel activity, demonstrate therapeutic efficacy against DOX-induced cardiomyopathy [197]. Decitabine exerts cardioprotection by modulating HMGB1 and mitochondrial iron chelation [198,199]. In addition, biomimetic nanozymes alleviate DOX-induced cardiotoxicity through their synergistic antioxidant effects and GPX4 repair ability [200]. SeMet enhances the pharmacological activity of GPX4, thereby alleviating DOX-induced cardiomyopathy [56]. Ethoxyquin, a potent free radical scavenger, effectively prevents ferroptosis and alleviates DOX-induced cardiotoxicity [201]. Finally, salidroside inhibits DOX-induced cardiomyopathy by regulating ferroptosis-dependent pathways [202]. α-tocopherol (α-Toc), a well-known antioxidant, can effectively prevent lipid peroxidation and alleviate DOX-induced cardiotoxicity [[203], [204], [205], [206]]. Protosappanin A (PrA), extracted from Sappan Lignum, has antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic properties. PrA directly interacts with ACSL4 and FTH1, prevents the phosphorylation of ACSL4, and avoids the autophagic degradation of FTH1. This action protects the heart from DOX-induced myocardial injury and alleviates ferroptosis-related mitochondrial dysfunction [74]. In the treatment of DOX-induced cardiomyopathy, using inhibitors such as Fer-1 and various natural substances to inhibit ferroptosis has become a viable treatment strategy [195,207,208].

Metabolic cardiomyopathy

Diabetic cardiomyopathy

Mechanisms of ferroptosis in diabetic cardiomyopathy

Diabetic cardiomyopathy is defined as abnormal myocardial structure and ventricular dysfunction occurring in diabetic patients independent of conventional cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension or coronary artery disease [209,210]. The pathogenesis of diabetic cardiomyopathy involves multifactorial mechanisms, with oxidative stress serving as a pivotal trigger for ferroptosis, a key driver of cardiomyocyte injury [211]. Sustained hyperglycemia promotes accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), which exacerbate lipid ROS generation, thereby inducing cardiomyocyte ferroptosis. This process ultimately manifests as cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, fibrosis, and cardiac dysfunction [212].(1)Oxidative stress: In experimental cardiomyopathy models, downregulation of SLC7A11 and GSH coincides with dysregulation of oxidative stress markers including MDA and CAT. Both clinical and preclinical studies underscore the critical role of ROS (e.g., superoxide anions, hydroxyl radicals, hydrogen peroxide) in cardiomyopathy progression. Iron overload in diabetic patients amplifies this cascade through Fenton reaction-derived free radicals. Enhancing endogenous antioxidant defenses effectively suppresses ferroptosis and attenuates cardiomyopathy development [212]. Notably, upregulation of free radical-scavenging enzymes such as SOD and CAT significantly mitigates oxidative stress and alleviates diabetic cardiomyopathy pathology.(2)Antioxidant: NRF2, a master regulator of antioxidant responses, plays a central role in diabetic cardiomyopathy pathogenesis. NRF2 knockout increases susceptibility to diabetic cardiomyopathy, whereas its overexpression markedly inhibits disease progression [213]. Upstream regulators of NRF2 include phosphorylated extracellular signal-regulated kinase (p-ERK), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), PKC, and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) [214]. Recent studies have shown that the activation of AMPK promotes the nuclear translocation of NRF2, thereby regulating the expression of downstream genes [215,216]. Critically, activation of the NRF2/HO-1/GPX4 pathway attenuates oxidative stress, inhibits ferroptosis, and promotes cardiac repair in diabetic cardiomyopathy models [212,217]. Fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) inhibits hepatic ferroptosis by enhancing HO-1 ubiquitination and NRF2 activity during iron overload [218]. In diabetic cardiomyopathy, FGF21 primarily alleviates cardiac damage by stabilizing ferritin and prolonging its half-life [219]. The ATF4 - FGF21 - ferritin axis, with ATF4 as an upstream regulator of FGF21, represents a promising therapeutic target for diabetic cardiomyopathy [220].

Therapeutic interventions targeting ferroptosis in diabetic cardiomyopathy

Heat shock transcription factor 1 (HSF1) confers protection against high glucose-induced diabetic cardiomyopathy by upregulating GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression, thereby reducing iron deposition and preventing lipid peroxidation as well as cardiomyocyte ferroptosis [222]. This protective role of HSF1 is partially mediated by AMPK. In addition, the cardioprotective effect of resveratrol (a non-flavonoid polyphenol) relies on the upregulation of HSF1 [68]. In a recent study, type 1 diabetic mice showed a smaller post-ischemic infarct size at 1 week of diabetes, which was paralleled by reduced cardiac ferroptosis; however, at 5 weeks of diabetes, the infarct size became significantly larger, accompanied by a marked increase in ferroptosis levels [43]. Notably, treatment with the antioxidant N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC) attenuated post-ischemic ferroptosis and oxidative stress, thereby reducing infarct size, whereas the ferroptosis inducer erastin abrogated this cardioprotection [43]. Together, these findings indicate that the progression of diabetes exacerbates myocardial susceptibility to MIRI through ferroptosis-dependent mechanisms, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting ferroptosis to alleviate MIRI in diabetic hearts.

Beyond the AMPK-HSF1 axis, other signaling pathways also contribute to cardioprotection against diabetic cardiomyopathy. For example, as an NRF2 activator, SFN can improve oxidative stress, inhibit inflammation, and prevent cardiac remodeling through the AMPK/AKT/GSK3β/NRF2 axis [223,224]. Furthermore, nicorandil, an ATP-sensitive potassium channel opener, protects against mitochondrial-related ferroptosis in diabetic cardiomyopathy by modulating the AMPK-Parkin-ACSL4 pathway [225]. Notably, the protective effects of NRF2 activators such as SFN are dependent on the synergy of AMPK, but the activation level of AMPK needs to be strictly controlled. Low-dose AMPK agonists can promote the nuclear of translocation NRF2, while high doses may induce excessive autophagy and weaken the survival of cardiomyocytes [226]. Preclinical studies have shown that the balance of the AMPK/GSK3β axis is crucial for cardiac remodeling, and excessive inhibition of GSK3β may actually promote fibrosis [227]. Therefore, future treatment strategies should focus on allosteric modulators that can restore the homeostasis of signaling pathways, rather than drugs that strictly activate or inhibit these kinases.

Iron-overload cardiomyopathy

Mechanisms of ferroptosis in iron overload cardiomyopathy

Iron overload cardiomyopathy is the leading cause of death in patients with transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia and also affects patients with primary hereditary hemochromatosis carrying HFE mutations. This condition leads to a gradual deterioration of cardiac electromechanical function and exacerbates left ventricular diastolic dysfunction [228,229]. Iron overload mainly generates intracellular ROS through the Fenton reaction, damaging cardiomyocytes by disrupting mitochondrial function and dynamics. Mice lacking NRF2 develop age-related cardiomyopathy, characterized by severe oxidative stress, degradation of sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase 2a (SERCA2A), and iron accumulation. However, NRF2 deficiency prevents further cardiac iron accumulation when mice are fed an iron-overload (IO) diet [230].

Therapeutic interventions targeting ferroptosis in iron overload cardiomyopathy

Iron chelators, such as DFP, DFO, and DFX, can reduce iron content in the myocardium, decrease ROS production, alleviate mitochondrial damage, and enhance cardiac autonomic function and contractility in iron-overloaded thalassemia mice [48]. Antioxidants like N-acetylcysteine improve myocardial mitochondrial function and left ventricular systolic function in iron-overloaded rats by effectively reducing systemic and cardiac iron levels, decreasing oxidative stress in plasma and the heart [231]. Calcium channel blockers may provide a way to prevent iron overload cardiomyopathy in patients with transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia by reducing iron concentration in the myocardium [232].

Hereditary cardiomyopathy

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), a genetic disorder affecting the myocardium, represents a significant risk factor for sudden arrhythmic death and HF [233]. Belonging to the NDRG family, NDRG1 functions primarily to inhibit cell proliferation and migration. Studies have indicated that deficiency of NDRG1 leads to iron overload in cardiomyocytes [238,239]. This iron overload, in turn, increases susceptibility to ferroptosis, elevates ROS levels, and exacerbates lipid peroxidation. In terms of treatment, iron chelators have been shown to upregulate NDRG1 expression. Crucially, Yuan et al. demonstrated that NDRG1 effectively alleviates Ang II-induced pressure overload cardiac hypertrophy in mice by regulating iron metabolism and inhibiting ferroptosis [240].

Friedreich's ataxia

FRDA is an autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disease, primarily characterized by progressive neurological decline, sensory impairment, and HCM [241]. The pathogenesis of FRDA stems from the abnormal expansion of guanine-adenine-adenine (GAA) trinucleotide repeats in both alleles within the first intron of the frataxin (FXN) gene, leading to epigenetic silencing and consequent frataxin protein deficiency. Premature death in FRDA patients is predominantly attributed to HCM. Recent mechanistic investigations by Cotticelli et al. revealed that, in comparison to normal controls, mouse fibroblasts harboring FRDA-associated gene mutations and repeat expansions demonstrate significantly increased sensitivity to ferroptosis and its inducer erastin [42]. Furthermore, administration of ferroptosis inhibitors, including Fer-1, SRS11-92, and XBJ-5-131, to these mutated cells conferred notable protective effects. These findings strongly suggest that inhibition of ferroptosis could represent a promising therapeutic strategy for targeting the underlying cellular pathophysiology in FRDA.

Heart failure

Mechanisms of ferroptosis in chronic heart failure

CHF, as the terminal stage of CVDs, represents a leading cause of mortality with age-dependent increases in incidence and fatality rates. The pathogenesis of HF involves intricate interactions among multiple pathophysiological mechanisms [242]. Epidemiological and experimental evidence indicates that under hemodynamic stress (e.g., hypertension), compensatory cardiomyocyte hypertrophy may progress to maladaptive remodeling, ultimately culminating in HF. This process is exacerbated by diverse forms of cell death, including apoptosis, necrosis, and autophagy (Supplementary figure_1), which collectively contribute to myocardial cell loss [243]. Notably, ferroptosis emerges as a critical contributor to HF pathogenesis. Patients with diabetes-related HF exhibit aberrant myocardial iron metabolism, further underscoring its significance [244,245].

NADPH oxidase (NOX), a transmembrane electron transporter, catalyzes oxygen conversion to superoxide anions. NOX-driven oxidative reactions generate substantial intracellular ROS [246,247]. Its overactivation elevates superoxide anion concentrations, thereby promoting ferroptosis. Chen et al. demonstrated that NOX suppression mitigates HF severity by attenuating ferroptosis [248]. NOX activity is predominantly regulated through three pathways:(1)Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP4)/p53 pathway: DPP4 functions as a transcriptional cofactor. p53 deficiency enhances DPP4-NOX binding, driving superoxide accumulation and ferroptosis. Conversely, p53 competitively binds DPP4 to suppress NOX activity and ferroptosis.(2)Arachidonic acid/PKC pathway: PKC-mediated phosphorylation activates NOX. Elevated intracellular arachidonic acid levels trigger PKC-dependent NOX activation, amplifying oxidative radical generation and ferroptosis susceptibility.(3)Transcriptional co-activator TAZ and angiopoietin - like protein 4 (ANGPTL4) pathway: TAZ modulates ANGPTL4 activity, indirectly regulating NOX function. TAZ knockdown reduces ferroptosis sensitivity, with activity exhibiting cell density dependence: higher density correlates with enhanced TAZ activity and ferroptosis vulnerability [249].

Mechanistic studies further elucidate ferroptosis regulation in CHF. Wang et al. [250], reported that MLK3/JNK/p53 pathway inhibition upregulates GPX4 expression, reducing cardiomyocyte ROS levels and ferroptosis, thereby ameliorating chronic HF. Recent studies have specifically highlighted ferroptosis in the context of hypertension-induced HF. In Dahl salt-sensitive rats, a high-salt diet induced cardiac fibrosis with decreased xCT/GPX4 expression and elevated iron/MDA levels, confirming ferroptosis involvement in target-organ damage [251]. Collectively, these findings underscore the significance of ferroptosis in cardiomyocyte death and its pivotal role in CVDs progression.

Therapeutic interventions targeting ferroptosis in chronic heart failure

In CHF, the SIRT1/NRF2 axis exhibits time-dependent changes. During the early compensatory stage, SIRT1 and NRF2 are upregulated to counteract oxidative stress [252]. However, as HF progresses, their expression decreases due to NAD+ depletion and chronic inflammation [253]. In the early stage, pharmacological activation of this axis may be beneficial, but continuous high-level activation in the failing heart may accelerate energy consumption and autophagy-dependent cell death [254]. Additionally, parallel in vitro and in vivo work demonstrated that high salt promotes cardiomyocyte ferroptosis by suppressing the SIRT1/NRF2 pathway, an effect reversed by the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 [255]. A high-salt environment not only suppresses SIRT1/NRF2 but also activates pro - inflammatory NF-κB, forming a vicious cycle. Therefore, restoring this axis of balance, rather than simply upregulating it, may be a more rational strategy for managing CHF.

Furthermore, Marchf6 has emerged as a cardioprotective E3 ligase that accelerates ACSL4 degradation, thereby limiting ferroptosis and attenuating hypertensive cardiac remodeling [256]. Chen et al. demonstrated that NOX suppression mitigates HF severity by attenuating ferroptosis [248]. Similarly, Puerarin is a phytoestrogen with antioxidant properties that can prevent HF induced by pressure overload by alleviating ferroptosis. It improves cardiac function and reduces cardiomyocyte death by reducing the labile iron pool (LIP/LMWI) and lipid peroxidation (TBARS, 4-HNE), downregulating the expression of NOX4, and upregulating the ferroptosis-related proteins GPX4 and FTH1 [57]. Baicalin, a flavonoid glycoside extracted from Scutellaria baicalensis, inhibits ferroptosis during myocardial injury. It reduces the accumulation of Fe2+ and lipid peroxidation (specifically ROS and MDA), downregulates ACSL4 expression, and suppresses TFR1 signaling as well as autophagy mediated by ferritin involving NCOA4, thereby alleviating cardiomyocyte ferroptosis [67].

Discussion

With the proposal and deepening of the concept of ferroptosis, its central role in the onset and progression of CVDs has been confirmed by numerous studies. This review systematically summarizes the core regulatory axes of ferroptosis (System Xc−/GSH/GPX4, iron metabolism, and lipid peroxidation) and their molecular mechanisms in specific cardiovascular pathological environments such as atherosclerosis, cardiomyopathy, and ischemia-reperfusion injury. Although significant progress has been made in current research, precise intervention against ferroptosis in the cardiovascular system still faces many challenges. To translate basic discoveries into clinical benefits, future research should focus on breakthroughs in the following three key areas: analysis of the mechanism network, development of specific biomarkers, and clinical translation of targeted therapeutic strategies.

Analysis of the mechanism network

Most existing studies focus on single GPX4 or iron metabolism pathways. However, the complexity of the cardiovascular system requires us to deepen the understanding of mechanisms from the perspectives of multi - dimensional and multi-organelle interactions. First, the cell - specific regulatory networks urgently need to be clarified. In atherosclerotic plaques, there are significant heterogeneities in the sensitivity to ferroptosis and regulatory mechanisms among endothelial cells, VSMCs, and macrophages. For example, the mechanism of heme - iron processing in macrophages and iron metabolism reprogramming during their transformation into foam cells remains unclear. Future single - cell sequencing and spatial metabolomics studies should focus on mapping the landscape of ferroptosis in different pathological stages and different cell sub-populations, especially on analyzing the underlying reasons for the differences in iron-handling ability and ferroptosis susceptibility between TREM2+ macrophages and normal macrophages. Second, organelle interaction and sub-cellular localization regulation are another key frontier. This review mentions that mitochondrial dysfunction is a driving factor of ferroptosis, but there are still large gaps in understanding the role of the interactions between mitochondria, ER, and lysosomes in ferroptosis in CVDs. For example, the mechanism by which ER stress regulates SLC7A11 through ATF4 has begun to emerge in MIRI, and the role of mitochondria - associated endoplasmic reticulum membranes (MAMs) in iron transport and lipid synthesis needs to be further explored. In addition, the activity of the ferritinophagy receptor NCOA4 is not only regulated by autophagic flux. How its post-translational modifications in cardiomyocytes and VSMCs respond to hemodynamic stress or metabolic stress is key to revealing the mechanism of iron metabolism disorders in hypertension and diabetic cardiomyopathy. Finally, the compensatory effects of non - classical ferroptosis defense pathways deserve attention. The FSP1 - CoQ10 and GCH1 - BH4 axes have been confirmed as supplementary defense mechanisms to the GPX4 system in specific CVD (such as AD), but the dynamic balance and mutual compensation rules of these systems in different types of CVD are still unclear (Supplementary figure_2). Analyzing the regulatory switches of these bypass pathways is crucial for overcoming drug resistance and finding combination therapy targets.

Ferroptosis presents a "double-edged sword" characteristic in the regulation of different cell types and disease stages of CVDs. For example, NRF2 resists ferroptosis in endothelial cells, but its over - activation in macrophages can promote lipid accumulation; AMPK initiates an energy - saving program during the ischemic period, but may induce autophagic death during the reperfusion period; Autophagy maintains basic homeostasis, but both excessive and insufficient autophagy can lead to pathology; The UPR of endoplasmic reticulum stress has both adaptive and pro - apoptotic dual aspects; SIRT1 increases compensatorily in the early stage and decreases due to NAD+ depletion in the late stage. These temporal and dosage heterogeneities are likely the fundamental reasons for the repeated setbacks in the clinical translation of drugs targeting these pathways. Therefore, for specific cell types at specific disease stages, how to balance the harmful and protective effects and develop drugs with appropriate regulatory functions is a necessary approach for the precise medical translation of ferroptosis - related therapies.

Discovery and validation of biomarkers

The clinical application of ferroptosis as a therapeutic target is largely limited by the lack of specific and dynamically monitorable biomarkers. Current research mostly relies on end-point histological detection (such as MDA, 4-HNE, and GPX4 Western blotting), which cannot meet the needs of early clinical warning and efficacy monitoring.

Circulating molecular biomarkers: It is an urgent task to find ferroptosis-specific products that can be detected in peripheral blood. In addition to conventional oxidative stress indicators, attention should be focused on the potential of oxidized phosphatidylethanolamine (SAPE-OOH) isomers or specifically oxidized cardiolipin as products at the end of lipid peroxidation. Moreover, although free iron levels and transferrin saturation can reflect iron load, they lack specificity in local vascular injury. Recent studies have found that free heme or soluble TFR derived from red blood cells are indicative in events related to intra-plaque hemorrhage. In the future, their predictive value for aortic dissection or aneurysm rupture needs to be verified in large-scale cohorts.

Imaging biomarkers such as cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) or T2* mapping techniques can be used to non-invasively assess iron deposition in the myocardium and blood vessel walls. In ischemic cardiomyopathy and iron overload cardiomyopathy, CMR iron load assessment has been used to guide iron chelation therapy. The future challenge lies in how to distinguish the "bystander effect" of iron deposition from the "pathological effect" of directly inducing ferroptosis. Developing new molecular probes (positron emission tomography (PET) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contrast agents) targeting lipid peroxidation products will be an important direction for realizing in vivo visualization of ferroptosis and guiding precise drug use.

The clinical translation path of treatment strategies

Although small molecule inhibitors such as Fer-1 and Lip-1 have shown significant cardiovascular protective effects in animal models, their physicochemical properties (metabolic instability, poor water solubility) limit direct clinical translation. Moreover, at present, most of these findings are mainly limited to the preclinical stage and still need to be verified by human studies. Future therapeutic development should follow diversified and precise strategies.

Repurposing old drugs and pre-clinical optimization: Regulating ferroptosis based on existing clinical drugs is a shortcut to shorten the translation cycle. For example, the impact of lipid - lowering statins on CoQ10 synthesis suggests that they may have side effects of promoting ferroptosis, which requires careful evaluation in specific patient populations. Metformin or sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors have been proven to inhibit ferroptosis by activating the AMPK/NRF2 pathway, providing a new explanation mechanism for their cardiovascular benefits. For new inhibitors, efforts should be made to develop second - generation ferroptosis inhibitors with better pharmacokinetic properties, such as deuterated polyunsaturated fatty acids (D - PUFAs) or Fer-1 analogs encapsulated in nanoliposomes.

Precise intervention targeting key molecules: Differentiated strategies should be adopted for different CVD subtypes. Vascular disease: The focus is on intervening in ferroptosis related to the phenotypic transformation of VSMCs. Targeting ACSL4 to inhibit lipid remodeling or regulating the POSTN/cGAS - STING pathway to block ferroptosis related to vascular calcification is expected to stabilize plaques without affecting systemic iron metabolism. Cardiomyopathy and HF: Activating the endogenous antioxidant defense is the core strategy. Activating the NRF2 pathway through genetic or pharmacological means (such as SFN and resveratrol) can not only enhance GPX4 synthesis but also coordinately regulate the expression of FTH1 to enhance iron storage. In addition, targeting mitochondrial - specific antioxidants (MitoTEMPO) or MCU inhibitors to block mitochondrial iron overload represents a potential remedial therapy for metabolic failure in end - stage HF.

Development of natural products

Natural compounds such as baicalein, silymarin, and protosappanin A have shown the advantage of low-toxicity anti-ferroptosis through multi-target regulation (antioxidation, iron chelation, and inhibition of ACSL4). In the future, chemical proteomics technology should be used to accurately map the direct targets of these natural products, and structural optimization should be carried out based on their active skeletons in order to obtain first - in - class cardiovascular protective new drugs.

However, there are still deficiencies in ferroptosis research. For example, in the elderly population, iron accumulation coexists with chronic inflammation, and the regulation of ferroptosis may be more complex; there is almost no evidence of ferroptosis in children with CVDs. Currently, there are no clinical studies targeting these two populations, which represents an important future direction. In summary, ferroptosis provides a brand - new pathophysiological perspective for overcoming intractable CVDs. Future research can achieve systematic breakthroughs in specific ins regulatory networks, multimodal biomarkers, and translatable drug system delivery; it is expected to promote the translation of ferroptosis - targeted therapeutic strategies from basic research to clinical practice in cardiology, bringing revolutionary changes to improving the prognosis of cardiovascular disease patients worldwide.

Limitations

Despite the promising therapeutic potential of targeting ferroptosis discussed throughout this manuscript, several important limitations must be acknowledged. First, the disease specificity of ferroptosis-driven pathology remains incompletely defined; not all conditions involving cell death or oxidative stress may benefit from ferroptosis inhibition, and the contribution of ferroptosis varies substantially across different diseases and even across stages of the same disease. Second, the optimal timing of intervention is challenging to determine, as ferroptosis may play divergent roles in early versus late disease phases, and premature or delayed intervention could be ineffective or even detrimental. Third, systemic modulation of iron metabolism carries risks of off-target effects on other organs, including potential hepatotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, and disruption of normal iron homeostasis in tissues with high iron turnover. Fourth, while natural compounds are often proposed as ferroptosis modulators, theey have pharmacological limitations such as poor solubility, low bioavailability, off-target bioactivity, and unknown long-term safety profiles. The unclear composition of relevant traditional Chinese medicine extracts has a certain impact on their transformation. Collectively, these caveats underscore that targeting ferroptosis is not a universally applicable strategy and requires careful context-dependent evaluation before therapeutic implementation.
