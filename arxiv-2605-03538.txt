Robust Beamforming for Cell-Free Systems with Parallel-Plate-Waveguided Dynamic Metasurfaces
Konstantinos D. Katsanos, Panagiotis Gavriilidis, George C. Alexandropoulos

Abstract
Dynamic Metasurface Antennas (DMAs) constitute a promising solution for extremely large antenna arrays, requiring lower power consumption and reduced hardware cost as compared to conventional phased arrays. In this paper, we consider a cell-free Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing (OFDM) system comprising multiple Base Stations (BSs) equipped with parallel-plate-waveguided DMAs, which aims to serve multiple users in the downlink direction. Focusing on a realistic frequency-selective model for the response-tunable elements of each DMA panel, and targeting to surpass the necessity of centralized designs that rely on a central processing unit with high computational power, we present a distributed optimization framework with minimal control information exchange for the frequency-selective analog and digital beamforming matrices of the multiple BSs, having the system spectral efficiency maximization as the design objective. Considering imperfect Channel State Information (CSI) availability at each BS, we devise a parallel decomposition framework for the configuration of the tunable parameters of each DMA-based BS. Our numerical results showcase the robustness of the proposed distributed beamforming design over different CSI conditions, and quantify the critical role of taking into account mutual coupling during the DMA design process.



I
Introduction

The upcoming sixth Generation (6G) of wireless networks is expected to leverage metasurface-based antenna technology to realize eXtremely Large (XL) arrays
[
1
]
. These architectures offer a scalable solution, where increasing the number of metamaterial elements enhances directivity and spatial degrees of freedom without a proportional increase in power consumption. This is due to the fact that signal manipulation is primarily performed in the analog domain, reducing the need for large chunks of Radio-Frequency (RF) chains.

A prominent implementation of such architectures is the Dynamic Metasurface Antenna (DMA), typically realized using planar structures formed by stacking microstrip lines
[
1
,
2
]
. Each line is individually fed and loaded with reconfigurable metamaterial elements. In this paper, we instead consider a DMA architecture based on a 2D waveguide, namely the Parallel Plate Waveguide (PPW)
[
3
]
. Unlike microstrip-based designs, the PPW constitutes a truly planar structure that does not rely on stacking linear arrays, and can naturally scale to XL apertures by increasing the plate dimensions. Importantly, this does not require per-line feeding and can, in principle, operate with a single RF chain. Additionally, the PPW supports the fundamental TEM mode, which has no lower cutoff frequency, leading to more favorable broadband characteristics
[
4
]
.

Concurrently, the cell-free network paradigm has gained substantial interest in recent years, mainly due to its potential to mitigate the inherent limitations of conventional cellular architectures
[
5
]
. Nonetheless, typical cell-free deployments rely on Central Processing Units (CPUs) with high computational capabilities, which coordinate transmissions over dedicated backhaul links to the distributed Base Stations (BSs)
[
6
]
. Very recently, in
[
7
]
, the use of DMAs within cell-free systems was introduced, demonstrating their advantages for joint user association and efficient transmit beamforming, when the design objective is the weighted sum-rate maximization. In addition, the importance of partially decentralized strategies to alleviate the computational burden at the CPU was highlighted.

This paper investigates a cell-free Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing (OFDM) system with multiple (BSs), each equipped with a PPW-based DMA, that jointly serves several single-antenna User Equipments (UEs) in the downlink. Explicitly accounting for the frequency-selective DMA behavior, we develop a distributed design of the analog and digital beamforming matrices under imperfect Channel State Information (CSI) conditions. Simulation results demonstrate the robustness of the proposed design, and highlight the critical role of incorporating mutual coupling in the DMA design.

II
PPW-Based DMA and Channel Modeling

Each multi-antenna BS in the considered cell-system is equipped with a DMA realized through a PPW of height
h
, filled with air
[
3
]
. The metasurface is particularly excited by multiple feeds placed inside the waveguide, and radiates through subwavelength metamaterial elements etched on the upper plate. Due to their electrically small dimensions, these elements can be accurately described using the discrete dipole approximation
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. To this end, the response of each metamaterial element is described by a polarizability tensor that relates the local magnetic field to the induced magnetic dipole moment. However, for the class of slot-based elements considered in this paper, the dominant response occurs along a single direction. Consequently, each element is characterized by a scalar polarizability coefficient, and we assume the dominant axis of the metamaterial to be along
x
, while the metasurface is assumed to lie on the
x
-
y
plane at
z=0

Let
m_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
denote the magnetic dipole moment of each
n
-th element

n
-th element. Although the polarizability is generally frequency dependent, i.e.,

H_{\text{loc},n}=H_{0,n}+\sum_{j=1,\,j\neq n}^{N}G_{n,j}\,m_{j},

(1)

where

n

n

[

The feeds inside the PPW of each DMA are modeled as independently driven thin-wire current sources placed at positions

\forall i=1,\ldots,N_{f}

\!\!\!\!H_{0,n}\!\triangleq\!\frac{\jmath\beta}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{f}}I_{i}H^{(2)}_{1}\!\left(\beta|\mathbf{r}_{n}-\mathbf{p}_{i}|\right)\sin\!\!\left(\!\!\mathrm{atan}\left(\!\frac{r_{y_{n}}-p_{y_{i}}}{r_{x_{n}}-p_{x_{i}}}\!\right)\!\!\right)\!\!,\!\!\!

\mathbf{V}

n

(4)

where
f_{0,n}\in\mathbb{R}

n
-th element, and

models radiation damping in the PPW, and follows from power conservation

8
]

II-A

Free-Space Channel Model

The electric field radiated in free space can be expressed as a function of the magnetic dipole moments, via:
\mathbf{e}_{{\rm sc},n}(\mathbf{r})\triangleq\jmath\omega\mu_{0}\big[\nabla\times\tilde{\mathbf{G}}_{\rm FS}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{n})\big]\mathbf{m}_{n}

\tilde{\mathbf{G}}_{\rm FS}\in\mathbb{C}^{3\times 3}
denotes the free-space dyadic Green’s function
[
11
, eq. (8.61)]
. The expression is doubled to account for the image dipole created by the metallic plate of the waveguide. By adopting the far-field approximation, let
\mathbf{s}_{u}
denote the observation point. Taking the origin at the center of the transmit aperture,
R_{u}\triangleq\|\mathbf{s}_{u}\|
denotes the distance from the aperture center to the observation point, and
(\theta_{u},\phi_{u})
represents the corresponding elevation and azimuth angles. Moreover, letting
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{u}\triangleq[\sin\theta_{u}\cos\phi_{u},\,\sin\theta_{u}\sin\phi_{u},\,\cos\theta_{u}]^{\rm T}
be the unit vector pointing from the transmit DMA center toward
\mathbf{s}_{u}
, then, the electric field radiated by the
n
-th dipole can be expressed as:

\displaystyle e_{{\rm sc},n}^{\theta}(\mathbf{s}_{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta\beta^{2}}{2\pi R_{u}}e^{-\jmath\beta R_{u}}e^{\jmath\beta\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{u}^{\rm T}\mathbf{r}_{n}}\,m_{n}\sin(\phi_{u}),

(5)

\displaystyle e_{{\rm sc},n}^{\phi}(\mathbf{s}_{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta\beta^{2}}{2\pi R_{u}}e^{-\jmath\beta R_{u}}e^{j\beta\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{u}^{\rm{T}}\mathbf{r}_{n}}\,m_{n}\cos(\phi_{u})\cos(\theta_{u}),

(6)

where
\eta\triangleq 120\pi
denotes the free-space impedance. The electric-field components radiated toward the observation point
\mathbf{s}_{u}
can then be written with respect to the stacked dipole moment vector
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times 1}
as:
e_{\rm sc}^{\theta}(\mathbf{s}_{u})=\mathbf{h}_{\theta}^{\rm T}(\mathbf{s}_{u})\mathbf{m}
and
e_{\rm sc}^{\phi}(\mathbf{s}_{u})=\mathbf{h}_{\phi}^{\rm T}(\mathbf{s}_{u})\mathbf{m}
, where
\mathbf{h}_{\theta}(\mathbf{s}_{u}),\mathbf{h}_{\phi}(\mathbf{s}_{u})\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times 1}
collect the contribution of each metasurface element toward the observation direction. Their
n
-th entries are given as follows:

\displaystyle[\mathbf{h}_{\theta}(\mathbf{s}_{u})]_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta\beta^{2}}{2\pi R_{u}}e^{-\jmath\beta R_{u}}e^{\jmath\beta\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{u}^{\rm T}\mathbf{r}_{n}}\sin(\phi_{u}),

(7)

\displaystyle[\mathbf{h}_{\phi}(\mathbf{s}_{u})]_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta\beta^{2}}{2\pi R_{u}}e^{-\jmath\beta R_{u}}e^{\jmath\beta\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{u}^{\rm T}\mathbf{r}_{n}}\cos(\phi_{u})\cos(\theta_{u}).

(8)

Assume that each BS serves
U
UEs, where each
u
-th UE (
u=1,\ldots,U
) is modeled as an ideal thin-wire dipole of length
\ell_{u}
with orientation unit vector
\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{u}\triangleq[\sin\vartheta_{u}\cos\varphi_{u},\sin\vartheta_{u}\sin\varphi_{u},\cos\vartheta_{u}]^{\rm T}
. Then, the received signal, which corresponds to the open-circuit voltage induced along the wire, is given by the line integral of the electric field along the dipole. By assuming that the electric field remains approximately constant over the antenna length, the induced voltage is approximated as
\ell_{u}\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{u}^{\rm T}\mathbf{e}
, where we use the spherical decomposition
\mathbf{e}\triangleq e^{\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{u}+e^{\phi}\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{u}
, with
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 1}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 1}
being the spherical polarization unit vectors. This leads to the polarization projection coefficients
\gamma_{u,\theta}\triangleq\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{u}^{\rm T}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{u}
and
\gamma_{u,\phi}\triangleq\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{u}^{\rm T}\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{u}
. Consequently, the polarization-projected channel, that maps from metasurface dipole moments to voltage at each
u
-th UE, is expressed as follows:

\mathbf{h}_{u}\triangleq\ell_{u}\left(\gamma_{u,\theta}\mathbf{h}_{\theta}(\mathbf{s}_{u})+\gamma_{u,\phi}\mathbf{h}_{\phi}(\mathbf{s}_{u})\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times 1}.

(9)

III-B

Design Problem Formulation

The achievable instantaneous sum-rate performance (measured in bits per second per Hz (bits/s/Hz)) for the considered multi-carrier cell-free system can be compactly expressed as a function of the
BK
analog,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}_{\rm RF}\triangleq\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{{\rm RF},1}[1],\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{{\rm RF},B}[K]\}
, and
BKU
digital,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}\triangleq\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1,1}[1],\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{B,U}[K]\}
, beamformers, as follows:

\mathcal{R}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}_{\rm RF},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}\right)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{u=1}^{U}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log_{2}(1+{\rm SINR}_{u,k}),

(13)

with the Signal-to-Noise-plus-Interference Ratio (SINR) per
k
-th SC at each
u
-th UE included in this expression given by:

{\rm SINR}_{u,k}=\frac{\left\lvert\sum\limits_{b=1}^{B}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}_{b,u}^{\rm H}[k]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{{\rm RF},b}[k]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{f,b}[k]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u}[k]\right\rvert^{2}}{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}q=1,\\
q\neq u\end{subarray}}^{U}\left\lvert\sum\limits_{b=1}^{B}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}_{b,u}^{\rm H}[k]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{{\rm RF},b}[k]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{f,b}[k]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,q}[k]\right\rvert^{2}+\sigma_{u,k}^{2}}.

(14)

In this paper, assuming imperfect knowledge of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}\triangleq\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}_{1,1}[k],\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}_{B,U}[k]\}
at the central node being responsible for all BSs hybrid analog and digital beamforming configurations, we focus on solving the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\mathcal{OP}:\,

\displaystyle\max_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}_{\rm RF}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0}),\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}}\,\,\,\mathcal{H}_{0}\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}}\left\{\mathcal{R}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}_{\rm RF}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0}),\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.51074pt\text{s.t.}\quad\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}_{b}[k]\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}\leq P_{b}^{\max}\;\forall b,

where the expectation is taken over the estimation errors of all
BUK
channel gain vectors in
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}
, and
P_{b}^{\max}
denotes the maximum transmit power per BS. The notation
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}_{\rm RF}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0})
explicitly indicates that each analog beamforming matrix is parameterized by the resonant strength vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0}\triangleq\{\alpha_{0,1,1},\dots,\alpha_{0,B,N}\}
, which is the tunable parameter of the Lorentzian model in (
4
).
Accordingly, our goal is to maximize the adopted performance metric with respect to the resonant strength factors
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b,n}

\forall b,n
, which affect
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}}_{\rm RF}
. In addition, as will be revealed in the next section, we propose a distributed (i.e., per BS) design solution, according to which the deployed BSs cooperate with minimal information exchange among them, either in coordination by a CPU or in a decentralized manner
[
12
]
. It is also noted that the power constraint in
\mathcal{OP}
serves as a surrogate for the actual transmit power. This is because the digital precoding variables are defined in terms of currents, i.e.,
\mathbf{i}_{b}=\mathbf{V}_{b}\mathbf{s}
, as described in Section
II
. Accurately relating this quantity to the consumed power would require accounting for the effective input impedance of the feeding structure, which is beyond the scope of this paper.

IV
Distributed Sum-Rate Maximization

To treat
\mathcal{OP}
in a parallel way, we make use of the structured surrogate function (see, e.g.,
[
13
]
and references therein), according to which
\mathcal{OP}
’s objective
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is decomposed as
\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{t}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}^{t})=\sum_{b=1}^{B}\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{0,b}^{t}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{b},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}^{t})
, with:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{0,b}^{t}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{b},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}^{t})\triangleq(1-\rho^{t})\mathcal{H}_{0,b}^{t-1}+\rho^{t}\hat{h}_{0,b}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{b},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}^{t})

(15)

\displaystyle+(1-\rho^{t})(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{0,b}^{t-1})^{\rm T}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{b}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{b}^{t})-\frac{\tau}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{b}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{b}^{t}\|^{2},

where
\hat{h}_{0,b}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{b},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}^{t})\triangleq\frac{1}{B}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}^{t})+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{b}}^{\rm T}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}^{t})(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{b}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{b}^{t})
. In the previous two expressions,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{b}\triangleq[(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u}[k])^{\rm T},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b}^{\rm T}]^{\rm T}

\forall u,k
collects the design variables for the
b
-th BS and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}
represents the entire set of variables at the algorithmic iteration

, while
\rho^{t}\in(0,1]

\rho^{0}=1
, and
\tau>0

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{0,b}^{t}

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{0,b}^{t}\triangleq(1-\rho^{t})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{0,b}^{t-1}+\rho^{t}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{b}}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}^{t}),

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{b}^{t+1}\triangleq(1-\gamma^{t})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{b}^{t}+\gamma^{t}\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{b}^{t}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u}[k],\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}^{t})=-\frac{\tau}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u}[k]-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u}^{t}[k]\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\rho^{t}\Re\left\{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u[k]}}^{\rm H}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}^{t})(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u}[k]-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u}^{t}[k])\right\}

\displaystyle+(1-\rho^{t})\Re\left\{(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{0,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}},b}^{t-1})^{\rm H}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u}[k]-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u}^{t}[k])\right\},

which is clearly a concave function with respect to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u}[k]
.

We first compute the gradient vector
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u[k]}}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}^{t})
, whose derivation leads to the following compact expression:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u[k]}}\mathcal{R}=\frac{1}{\ln(2)}\left[\!\frac{1}{\operatorname{MUI}_{u,k}^{t}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}}_{b,u}[k]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u}^{t}[k]\!+\!r_{u,u,k}^{t}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{b,u}[k])\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\!\!\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}q=1,\\
q\neq u\end{subarray}}^{U}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{q,k}^{t}(1\!+\!\operatorname{SINR}_{q,k}^{t})^{-1}}{(\operatorname{MUI}_{q,k}^{t})^{2}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}}_{b,q}[k]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u}^{t}[k]\!+\!r_{q,u,k}^{t}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{b,q}[k])\!\right]\!\!,

where the following definitions have been used:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{q,k}^{t}

\displaystyle\triangleq\left\lvert\sum\limits_{b=1}^{B}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{b,q}^{\rm H}[k]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,q}^{t}[k]\right\rvert^{2},

(18)

\displaystyle\operatorname{MUI}_{u,k}^{t}

\displaystyle\triangleq{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q=1,q\neq u\end{subarray}}^{U}\left\lvert\sum\limits_{b=1}^{B}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{b,u}^{\rm H}[k]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,q}^{t}[k]\right\rvert^{2}+\sigma_{u,k}^{2}},

(19)

along with
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}}_{b,u}[k]\triangleq\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{b,u}[k]\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{b,u}^{\rm H}[k]
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{b,u}[k]\triangleq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{f,b}^{\rm H}[k]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{{\rm RF},b}^{\rm H}[k]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}_{b,u}[k]
, as well as
r_{q,u,k}^{t}\triangleq\sum_{b^{\prime}=1,b^{\prime}\neq b}^{B}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{b^{\prime},q}^{\rm H}[k]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b^{\prime},u}^{t}[k]
. Then, after formulating the Lagrangian function for
\mathcal{OP}_{1}
and equating its first-order derivative with respect to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u}[k]
to the zero vector, the optimum linear precoding vector
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}_{b,u}^{t}[k]
can be obtained as:

\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}_{b,u}^{t}[k]=\frac{1}{\tau+2\lambda_{b}}\!\left(\rho^{t}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u[k]}}\mathcal{R}\!+\!(1\!-\!\rho^{t})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{0,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}},b}^{t-1}+\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u}^{t}[k]\right)\!.

(20)

In this optimal solution,
\lambda_{b}\geq 0
denotes the Lagrange multiplier for the convex maximum transmit-power constraint, which satisfies Slater’s condition, and thus can be efficiently obtained via the bisection search method.

IV-B

Resonance Strength Optimization at Each
b
-th BS

The resonance strength parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b}
for the PPW-based DMA at each
b
-th BS can be obtained by solving the following simplified unconstrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\mathcal{OP}_{2}:\,\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}_{0,b}^{t}\triangleq\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b}\in\mathbb{R}}\,\,\,\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{0,b}^{t}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}^{t}),

whose objective function is equal to:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{0,b}^{t}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}^{t})=-\frac{\tau}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b}^{t}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\left(\rho^{t}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b}}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}^{t})+(1-\rho^{t})(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{0,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b}}^{t-1}\right)^{\rm T}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b}^{t}),

which is clearly a concave optimization problem with respect to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b}
. Hence, its unique optimal solution can be derived by comparing the objective’s derivative with the zero vector, yielding the following closed-form expression:

\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}_{0,b}^{t}=\frac{\rho}{\tau}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b}}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}^{t})+\frac{1-\rho^{t}}{\tau}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}_{0,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b}}^{t-1}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b}^{t}.

(21)

Evidently, according to the above expression for the optimal
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}_{0,b}
in each algorithmic iteration
t
, it is crucial to compute the gradient
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b}}\mathcal{R}
, whose expression is given by:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b}}\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{t},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\xi}}^{t})=-\frac{2}{\ln(2)}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b,k}}^{\rm T}

(22)

\displaystyle\times\Re\left\{\operatorname{vec}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{{\rm RF},b}^{-\rm H}[k]\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{W}}_{{\rm RF},b}^{-\rm H}[k]\right)\right\},

where the following matrix definitions have been used:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}_{k}

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{u=1}^{U}\frac{(1+\operatorname{SINR}_{u,k}^{t})^{-1}}{(\operatorname{MUI}_{u,k}^{t})^{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{u,k},

(23)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{u,k}

\displaystyle\triangleq\operatorname{MUI}_{u,k}^{t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}_{u,k}-\mathcal{F}_{u,k}^{t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}_{u,k},

(24)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}_{u,k}

\displaystyle\triangleq\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{b,u}[k]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u}^{t}[k]\!+\!r_{u,u,k}^{t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}_{b,u}[k]\right)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,u}^{t,\rm H}[k]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{f,b}^{\rm H}[k],\!\!\!\!

(25)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}_{u,k}

\displaystyle\triangleq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}_{u,k}^{1}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}_{u,k}^{2},

(26)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}_{u,k}^{1}

\displaystyle\triangleq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{b,u}[k]\left(\sum_{q=1,q\neq u}^{U}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,q}^{t}[k]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,q}^{t,\rm H}[k]\right)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{f,b}^{\rm H}[k],

(27)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}_{u,k}^{2}

\displaystyle\triangleq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}_{b,u}[k]\left(\sum_{q=1,q\neq u}^{U}r_{q,u,k}^{t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{b,q}^{t,\rm H}[k]\right)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{f,b}^{\rm H}[k],

(28)

with
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}_{b,u}[k]\triangleq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}_{b,u}[k]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}_{b,u}^{\rm H}[k]
. In addition, the matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b,k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}\times N}
represents the Jacobian matrix defined as
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b,k}}\triangleq\frac{\partial\operatorname{vec}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{b}^{*}[k]}{(\partial\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b})^{\rm T}}
, with
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{b}^{*}[k]\triangleq(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{b}^{*}[k])^{-1}
(the latter matrix appears in (
10
)). More specifically,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b,k}}
is a sparse matrix whose entries result from differentiating each entry of the Lorentzian function with respect to each entry of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b}
, and it can be computed as follows:

[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b,k}}]_{m,\ell}=\begin{cases}-\frac{f_{0,\ell}^{2}+f_{k}^{2}}{\left([\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b}]_{\ell}f_{0,\ell}\right)^{2}},&m=(\ell-1)N+\ell,\\[4.30554pt]
\quad\quad\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(29)

where
m=1,\dots,N^{2}
and
\ell=1,\dots,N
. The detailed derivation of
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}_{0,b}}\mathcal{R}
is omitted due to space limitations.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied a cell-free system comprising multiple BSs equipped with identical PPW-based DMAs, and performing downlink OFDM transmissions to serve multiple single-antenna UEs. We focused on distributed beamforming aiming to maximize spectral efficiency under imperfect CSI conditions. Our numerical investigations showcased that the proposed design maintains strong robustness against imperfect CSI, while, taking into account mutual coupling during the DMA design process, yields enhanced performance.
