Executive Summary:
We quantified the economic and technical risks associated with different cultivation
strategies and crop protection approaches through an integrated program of indoor lab
studies, cultivation optimization and simulation, and robust outdoor cultivation
campaigns. We generated data from different cultivation strategies tested side by side under
relevant outdoor conditions for quantification of technical, economic and sustainability risks and
impacts.
A robust modeling framework with validated dynamic biomass growth model was developed and
further improved.
A suite of novel real-time sensors was developed and deployed for nutrient and water
quality monitoring with commercial potential, improved process monitoring and control, and
pathways identified to reducing costs relative to other real-time sensors platforms.
Multiple pest models were established including amoeba, aphelid (2), and bacterial (4) and
established PCR and RT-PCR assays for routine monitoring of new, and now known,
contaminants to guide identification and development of additional mitigation strategies.
96-well plate-based techniques for culture health monitoring offer promise for streamlining data
collection from production ponds but for the assays explored, they did not offer the ability to
detect contamination or declines in culture health earlier than standard observations such as
microscopy. They do offer opportunity for increased sample analysis throughput, though it’s
likely cost prohibitive in a production environment.
Novel, high-quality, publicly available cultivation datasets and a simple set of python
libraries to easily access AzCATI datasets pre-merged and structured for modern data-science
applications, automated analysis APIs, and controlling experiments were generated.
100% Batch cultivation is cost prohibitive without significant gains in productivity relative
to semi-continuous production, though a hybrid semi-continuous and batch facility could
potentially compete economically with semi-continuous only facilities while reducing risks of
complete shutdown due to pond crashes.

1. Introduction:
This project was proposed in response to the FY19 Bioenergy Technologies Office Multi-Topic
Funding Opportunity Announcement DE-FOA-0002029, Area of Interest Subtopic 1 (AOI1):
Cultivation Intensification Processes for Algae. The main challenge identified by the FOA was “in
translating results between laboratory research systems and larger-scale outdoor (or mass
culture) systems. These difficulties limit reliable experimental durations, adequate and
representative experimental volumes of material, and results that can be reproduced reliably. By
overcoming the challenge in translating results between laboratory and mass cultures, the
objective of AOI 1 is to increase the harvest yield, robustness, and quality of algae cultivation for
biofuels and bioproducts”.
Further, the FOA stated that “Successful applications: will accomplish the objective by
showing closer correlation between promising laboratory results and “mass culture” campaigns
resulting in “high-performance” cultivation outcomes to grow and harvest kilograms of algae
and/or equivalent amounts of secreted algae products in a cultivation campaign… Specifically,
successful applications will:

  Identify algae strain traits of interest and show that the trait(s) of interest can be
 reproduced in outdoor, field-scale research;
  Undergo multiple indoor/outdoor/indoor cycles to develop, test, and optimize
 practices and technologies that enable delivery of the expected performance in
 the largest scale field trials;
  Generate data from cultivation campaigns that could be used in techno-economic
 models to result in lower algal biofuel minimum fuel selling prices and in lifecycle
 models to assess energy balance and energy return on investment; and
  Disseminate results to broader audiences.”
To meet the objectives of the FOA, we proposed a team comprising of 6 participants, each with
specific expertise in being able to deliver successful project outcomes in support of BETO’s
Advanced Algal System program goals of biofuel modeled MFSP of $3/GGE with a trajectory
towards $2.5/GGE. In large measure this project was successful in meeting the overall project
requirements outlined above. However, we did have some significant challenges that we were
unable to completely overcome within the scope of this project. The appearance early in the
project of a novel bacterial contaminant was particularly devastating to productivity and
robustness of our strains of interest and while we met the intermediate project go/no go, our end
of project targets for improvements in productivity and robustness were not met.

Project Background and Justification: Current decision-support models (TEA, LCA, and
growth/productivity) for large-scale algae cultivation systems lack critically important,
quantitative culture-failure risk data (Dong et al 2016, Somers et al 2019, Quinn 20112, Cruce et
al 2019). At very large scales, semi-continuous versus full-batch cultivation strategies present
very different risk profiles, especially with respect to the consequences of culture failures from
pathogens, grazers, and competitors (Quinn et al 2012). Full-batch operations, as demonstrated
by Huntley and Beal (Huntley et al 2015), typically require larger investments in seed-train
systems which lower the financial risks associated with culture failure by speeding up the restart
process. These unknowns around failure rates associated with semi-continuous versus full-
batch operations constitute a critical knowledge gap that must be closed to guide major
investments in commercial algal biofuel
production, while also creating the
foundational data necessary to enable crop
insurance.

 Large-scale, semi-continuous cultivation is often assumed to be required for biofuels,
because the cultivation footprint is largely composed of low-cost, open raceway ponds (ORPs).
Similarly, the seed-inoculum scale-up is assumed to consist of successive culture transfer
between ORPs of increasing size and represent ≤10% of the overall areal production footprint
(Benemann et al 2012). However, at very large scales, this strategy amplifies the economic risk
of culture failures, due to very long scale-up recovery times (Quinn 2012, Davis et al 2016).
Mitigating this risk requires both significant operational knowledge and CAPEX/OPEX
investment in crop protection and pest-management strategies as a necessary hedge against
failures (McBride et al 2014). Alternatively, larger investment in intensified photobioreactor
(PBR) seed-inoculum scale-up capacity—coupled with complete batch-mode ORP harvests—
can manage cultivation failure risk via an avoidance mechanism, minimizing algal seed culture
exposure time in ORPs and maximizing post-failure recovery speed (Huntley et al 2015,
Nagappan et al 2019, Narala et al, 2016). The precise advantages and feasibility of either
approach is difficult to disambiguate, let alone confidently implement broadly, as cultivation risks
are likely to show significant strain-, location-, and seasonal-dependencies.
Regardless of operational scenario, algae cultivation at scales that can support
commodity-product prices requires:
 • Cultivars with high productivity and robustness
 • Comprehensive monitoring programs to determine and maintain optimal growth
 conditions
 • Defined agronomic best-practices—including integrated pest-management
 • Supporting tools and data-management structures that are both implementable and
 effective ($) at enabling data-driven decision-support models for large-scale production

2. Task Structure and Project Management and Reporting:
Project Approach: We proposed the development and integration of four key strategies to
quantify cultivation risks and their economic and sustainability impacts. The first three were
seed-train/cultivation strategies, including i) direct comparison of batch vs. semi-continuous
cultivation modes ii) integrated pest management, iii) and tightly coupled indoor-outdoor-indoor
experimentation. The fourth strategy, iv) novel sensor development and deployment, was to
drive process monitoring, optimization and control, while providing a robust framework for data-
driven decision-support models to evaluate the other strategies.
Our overall approach consisted of four (4) main tasks as outlined below (including partner
participation on each task (Figure 2)):
Task 1: Addressing culture failure risks and quantifying impacts (ASU/DTI)
 • Multi season cultivation trials comparing semi-continuous vs. batch operation
 • Crop protection through integrated pest management
 - Chemical and physical/mechanical means for crop protection
 - Regulatory aspects/barriers to deployment
Task 2: Integrated Lab to Field to Lab (LFL) to optimize cultivation performance
(ASU/LANL)
 • Whole culture and single cell phenotyping
 - Developing 96-well plate diagnostics workflow including flow cytometry assays for
 monitoring culture health
 • Environmental simulation with ePBR’s based on retrospective scripts
 - Iterative indoor/outdoor flow and ‘omics’ approach to track and understand culture
 health/stress as a function of key operational variables (e.g., seed train/culture age
 and abiotic/biotic crash events)
Task 3: Optimized process monitoring for improving performance (QBI/Burge/ASU)
 • Novel sensor development for continuous, real-time monitoring of key cultivation
 parameters including water quality and nutrients
 • Data integration platform to support decision-supported cultivation improvements
 - Goal  AI/machine learning ready data sets with cloud-based, open access
 database and analysis platform
Task 4: Sustainability assessment - coupled TEA and LCA (CSU)
 • Concurrent TEA/LCA/resource assessment
 • Dynamic thermal and growth model development integrated with crash model

Project Performance Baseline: We identified our performance baseline for cultivation metrics
as required by the FOA using full year cultivation data from 4.2 m2 ORP’s which at the time
were, and still remain, the standard for collecting state of technology (SOT) data on cultivation
productivity and robustness for BETO. Figure 3 shows the fiscal year (FY) 2019 value for annual
average (i.e., September 2018 thru August 2019) obtained with an implied strain rotation

approach with Monoraphidium minutum (26BAM) as the cool season strain and Acutodesmus
obliquus (UTEX393) as the warm season strain. Our annual average value of 15.9 g/m2-day
was used as our established baseline. Details on the SOT cultivation trial protocols and overall
multi-year performance of these strains can be found in McGowen et al 2017, 2022. For
robustness, we used a set of metrics that were established under the ATP3 program and
detailed in Harmon et al 2021, in particular mean time to failure (MTTF) which represents the
amount of time in days between starting a cultivation pond and that pond failing due to culture
crash from contamination or other event. As shown in Figure 3, MTTF was determined on a
seasonal and annual average basis for 2018 (CY) with an annual average MTTF of 30 days.
Note that in 2018, no crashes were observed in the winter season. The productivity and
robustness metrics in Figure 3 were used at the start of the project during BP1 for the initial
project verification and set the criteria to measure progress against for our intermediate go/no
go and end of project goals as described below.

3. Results by Task
Task 1: Addressing culture failure risks and quantifying impacts
Task Summary: We conducted concurrent, replicated field trials using batch and semi-
continuous operational modes at the AzCATI testbed in 4.2 m2 ORPs mimicking different seed
train strategies using both flat panel and tubular bioreactors as high-density seed train source
and supplying data on productivity and robustness into Task 4. These trials included seasonal
strain selection/rotation and integrated pest management. The trials were informed by and
leveraged past and ongoing SOT cultivation trials from our DISCOVR project (PNNL led) and
other research projects using well characterized, and robust strains (namely 26BAM and
UTEX393). This task generated empirical data to better understand the tradeoffs of
CAPEX/OPEX decisions and their impact on culture failure risk and biomass productivity and
quality. This task had 2 subtasks:
 - Subtask 1.1: Multi-season cultivation trials comparing batch vs. semi-continuous
 operational modes.
 - Subtask 1.2: Crop Protection through Integrated Pest Management
Subtask 1.1: Multi-season cultivation trials comparing batch vs. semi-continuous
operational modes.
Subtask Summary: Subtask 1.1 was intended to measure productivity differences and crash
rates for batch vs semi-continuous cultivation as a means for enhanced net productivity. We
conducted field trials using semi-continuous seed production in high-productivity PBRs as the
inoculum source for 1) ORPs operated in a full-batch mode and 2) ORPs operated semi-
continuously. We ran the two best brackish media strains in the AzCATI/BETO portfolio,
Acutodesmus obliquus (UTEX393) and Monoraphidium minutum (26BAM), representing
summer/fall and winter/spring strains, respectively. During seasonal transitions, we explored
crop rotation scenarios under both operational modes as well as current best practices for
mitigating contamination and assessed MTBF for both operational scenarios. The overall
premise for this project’s cultivation work is outlined in Figure 4.

The cultivation systems of interest for this project are shown below in Figure 5 and included our
standard 4.2 m2 ORP raceways operated at depth of 20 cm with a nominal volume of 820L as
well as several versions of photobioreactors (PBR’s) in either flat panel or tubular format for
seed train scale-up. The overall description and operations for standard SOT cultivation runs is
as described previously in McGowen et al 2022 but in brief, our baseline operational mode is
semi-continuous cultivation where a portion of the pond is periodically removed, and make-up
water, salt, and nutrients are added back to the pond and the cultivation run continues. This
contrasts with batch cultivation where the entire culture volume is harvested, and the pond is
restarted from the seed train. These two operation modes represented the current best
practices as described in literature but little to no data existed with these operational scenarios
being compared side-by side.

Figure 5: AzCATI Cultivation Scale Up. a.) indoor 15L flat panels, b.) greenhouse (GH) 110L flat
panels (natural light), c.) Helix 500L tubular reactor (artificial light), d.) Lgem 750L tubular
reactor (natural light), and e.) 4.2 m2 ORP’s (820L at 20 cm depth, natural light).

For a PBR based seed train, it is key to be able to run the tools for as long as possible under
semi-continuous cultivation conditions providing a continuous high quality seed source to ORP’s
and to support rapid re-seeding of ORPs in the event of a culture crash and or as the means to
maintain ORP’s under full batch harvest operation. Key performance factors to understand the
tradeoffs between continuous versus batch ORP pond operation, include a) initial starting/reset
concentrations in the PBRs and the ORPs, b) final concentration in the PBR’s (avoid any light
limited operation), c) volumetric growth rates, d) fouling of the PBR’s, and e) contamination
control. All factor into the overall operational costs plus of course the CAPEX of the systems. In
particular, the last two items of fouling and contamination control are especially critical in
understanding the operational costs as its imperative to maintain maximum productivity and up-
time of the PBRs for as long as possible to justify the cost of the PBR’s. Establishing the initial
baseline performance metrics for the stains of interest was a key objective of the early
cultivation trails for this project.

Cultivation Calendar Year 2020: One of the first experiments we completed for this project was
to look at high productivity, high density PBR seed source feeding batch ORP’s and comparing
that up against ORP’s operated under a semicontinuous regime. 26BAM was cultivated in our
standard SOT indoor scaleup from plates to tissue culture flasks (150ml) to 800 ml bubble
columns and then into 15L flat panels (Figure 5a) all under nutrient replete conditions and
constant temperature and 24 hr light. The Helix was inoculated with culture from the 15L panels
and then culture from the helix was used to inoculate the outdoor Lgem reactor.
The overall productivity data for both the Helix and Lgem are summarized in Table 2. The
overall volumetric productivity in the Helix was 0.6 to 0.7 g/L-day after the initial growout. These
values are consistent with past performance for cultivation with this tool across a wide range of
phototrophic strains and represents the high end of productivities we have observed with this
tool with 24 hr constant light (700 µmol m-2 sec-1) and temp control maintained ~25-28 oC.
Controlling dissolved oxygen (DO) in closed PBRs is also critical to avoid significant reduction in
overall growth rates due to oxygen inhibition of photosynthesis. We typically run the Helix at
higher DO set points of 15-20 mg/L using nitrogen gas injection in order to strip the O2 from the
system. The Lgem operates under a mixed pumped flow regime (air injection as well as
mechanical pump) and thus DO control is less of an issue relative to the Helix or other closed
PBR systems. This is a well-known issue with PBR’s and one that is highly dependent on the
overall design of the PBR, in particular the length of the PBR itself. Throughout the 21-day run
in the Helix with 26BAM, we did not observe any significant fouling of the PBR.
Table 2: Productivity summary for 26BAM in the HLX and Lgem systems operated in

Looking closely at the actual harvest yield productivity differences for 26BAM between batch
and semi-continuous cultivation shows that when including the contribution of the seed, the
batch ponds outperform the semi ponds by almost 54% (11.7 vs 7.57 g/m2-day), however as
mentioned earlier, exactly how we’ll treat the seed contribution and the days and unit production
area that contributes to making that seed depends on the scaled process modelling and its
assumptions for how an actual farm would operate, so for now it’s important to look at the effect
of the seed source itself and NOT the actual contribution of that biomass to the overall
productivity. If we remove the seed contribution to the overall yield from the batch ponds,
designated as “- seed”, we see that the productivity for the two operational scenarios is
essentially equal at 7.48 versus 7.57 g/m2-day for batch and semi-continuous, respectively
(Figure 9). Moving forward in this report, unless otherwise stated, we will remove the
contribution of the seed biomass when comparing batch to semi-continuous.

Figure 9: AHYP-H2H comparisons for batch versus semi over the course of the cultivation trial
(Left graph) and the summary value for the full 33 days of cultivation (right graph) with (+seed)
and without (-seed) contribution of the biomass coming from the PBR.

Based on experience, and in the absence of significant pest pressure (e.g., in the winter), we
have not seen that seed source makes much difference in overall slope productivity when
comparing batch versus semi-continuous cultivation. And while we had the unexpected opposite
issue of our seed source showing contamination, given the level of contamination observed in
batch ponds (except for the one pond highlighted in Figure 7) and subsequent cultivation
experience with this strain and UTEX393 when contamination present at low levels, these early
results reinforced that biomass productivity in the absence of significant pest pressure is not
affected by seed source/operation mode. However, a key hypothesis for this project was that as
pest pressure increases, we would see a significant difference in robustness and likely overall
productivity between the two operating conditions.
Cultivation Calendar Year 2021: UTEX393 was cultivated for two separate experiments in late
spring and early summer 2021. The first experiment was to look at UTEX393 seed train through
the Helix tubular PBR since we wanted to maintain better environmental control (i.e., culture
temperature) and we had observed elevated contamination pressure from predatory bacterial

In an after-action review of our cleaning and sanitation protocols, it is not clear that anything
done on 9/30/2022 post-crash cleaning of ponds for redeployment was materially different than
past same day launches, even after a crash. However, in reviewing with field personal, a few

place to review protocols and seek out possible sources for carryover of contamination from one
run to the next. However, while we can be thorough at small experimental scales we are

Our protocol for cleaning and sanitizing ponds and probes/sensors for the project was as follows
(changes implemented in red text):
1) At the end of trail or after a contamination crash, bleach is added to the ponds at 1oz/gallon
 rate (500 ppm active chlorine), allowed to sit for 15 min and then drained to sanitary sewer
 once cultures area at pH <10.
2) Empty pond is cleaned with a gas-powered pressure washer and heavy biomass buildup is
 scrubbed with Scotch-Brite pad and power washed again. Heavy staining on the ponds
 (from media) is removed with a concentrated muriatic acid solution and rinsed. Spargers are
 removed and scrubbed free of any biomass buildup and soaked in bleach water (1 oz/1
 gallon)
 a. note – field sterilizing spargers has now been identified as possible risk point as
 spargers should sit for an extended period of time to ensure bleach solution has
 reached into ceramic.
 b. New procedure implemented this winter is to swap spargers completely post-crash or
 strain change and we are no longer field sterilizing if needing to redeploy ponds
 same-day.
3) After pond cleaning, all surfaces are coated with a 1% solution of Virkon S disinfectant and
 Viricide. This is left to soak in the sanitation solution for 10 minutes and flushed with clean
 water before filling or transferring the culture.
 a. Under extreme contamination conditions, areas surface treated with Clidox S
 disinfectant at the sanitation or sterilization rate.
 b. The contact time for disinfection is 5 minutes and can be rinsed and returned to
 normal production afterward.
4) Transfer hoses and pump equipment: all hoses and equipment touching the biological
 culture are sanitized by circulating a generic bleach solution (6% sodium hypochlorite at

The main observations from cultivation trails from this project include:
 a) Culture productivity, even under batch mode, was not enough to regain
 performance of cultures, in particular for UTEX393 relative to peak observed in
 2019, and cultures could still experience rapid crashes outdoors without
 intervention when contamination pressure is high.
 b) Even though peak performance was not attained, batch cultivation routinely
 outperformed semicontinuous operation when contamination pressure was
 significant, with little to no difference between cultivation modes if contamination
 pressure low.
 c) Even with intervention, current methods only prolonged marginally the time to
 failure with ponds still crashing, in particular under semi-continuous mode.
 d) Determining seed culture health via microscopy and observed growth rate is not
 enough to ensure seed quality.
 e) The presence in cultures via endpoint PCR of known bacterial or aphelid
 pathogens is not a determinative indicator that culture will crash.
 - It is not yet determined what the level contamination impacts growth but it
 likely is well below where traditional measures of culture health (i.e.,
 microscopy) indicate an issue.
 - More careful indoor experimentation in titrated crash assays needed with
 RT-PCR tracking of deleterious agents.
 - Ideally, we need at a minimum to have seed that is not positive for any
 known contaminants via end-point PCR.
 f) The data indicated that cleaning between crashes with standard protocols in use
 for years, may not be enough to remove bacterial (or fungal) pests.
 - Risk of system contamination with pest reservoirs building up in cultivation
 equipment points to larger risks at scale as the ability to effectively clean and
 sterilize a crashed pond will be limited, if not impossible at >>1 acre scale.

 49

 - It is also clear that there are likely substantial environmental pest reservoirs
 now on the AzCATI site and that they can be transient (e.g., FD01

predator's growth rate, but this should not be true of batch treatment – if you knock the predator
population down by 1,000X (3-log), it will take 10 doubling times to get back to that level again –
and if you get to four or five log reduction, it will take much longer. The basic equation for
recirculation mode is:
 dn /dt = n0 (g - flow / volume)
where n0 is the initial predator density. To prevent the predators from growing, the right-hand
side of the equation needs to be negative. Hence the flow rate needs to be higher than growth
rate of the amoeba times volume. Though the amoeba growth rate is not known, estimates
based on literature indicate we need a flow rate of at least 1.5 L/min to mitigate any
contamination, but we need to run faster in order to be able to turn over the pond in an
acceptable amount of time. Our max flow rate for the PEF unit appears to be around 6 LPM, so
in order to fully treat a pond at 5000L it would take around 13.75 hrs which would theoretically

parameters in biologically active systems. These composite signal patterns are most prominent
in photosynthetically active systems such as microalgae cultivation and plants, but also behave
consistently across other microbial processes such as fermentation and anaerobic conditions.

cleaning and sterilization (and thus destruction of the biofilm) of the sensor during operations
(e.g., in event of crash). We have shown that under typical cultivaiton conditons and across
several algae strains, and media types from brackish to full marine, biofilm formation and thus
stabilization of the signal off the probe is rapid (with hours to <<1-2 days). An example of this

Investigations & Initial Analysis: Unlike chemical, operational, and pathogenic disruptive
events, algae takeover events can be far subtler to detect than typical pond crashes and other
disruptions. Neither the color, biomass yield, nor cell morphology may change substantially
during a takeover event, making these events much harder to detect and limiting intervention
options. A pond-takeover event was discovered in March of 2021 for a winter run that was
terminated on 3/5/2021 for UTEX393. This was not determined in real time during the run but
confirmed only at the very end of the run when microscopy clearly showed a different
morphology for what was supposed to be UTEX393. During the cultivation trial, routine
microscopy did not reveal any dramatic changes throughout February 2021, but it is important to
note we did see signs of culture distress with the presence of bacterial predation, albeit at a low
level in early February but it did not lead to full culture collapse (likely due to the lower
temperatures in February) but may have made things favorable for another strain to dominate.
The overall macroscopic aspects of the culture did not change throughout the run giving no
indication of issues (Figure 117). However, upon closer inspection and review of microscopy at
the end of the run and based on some other microscopy work on indoor cultures as well as
some cultivation work in a different media with UTEX393 seed from our lab where an algal
contaminant took over, we realized we might have a low level of an algal contaminant in our
UTEX393 seed stock. This appeared to be a chlorella sp. and we attempted to confirm via PCR

 103

using our CAPS assay on the end points from the outdoor cultivation run as well as our current
seed stocks (Figure 118). There were some morphology shifts where the UTEX393 culture gets
more round with time outdoors, so the shift was subtle. As the endpoints of the cultivation run
were all clearly a different strain, we sent those bands out for sequencing to confirm identity and
also pulled retains of liquid culture from the run to perform a time course to better pinpoint
where the strain transition took place (Figure 119). This analysis uncovered a strain change
from UTEX393 to what we later identified as Chlorella vulgaris (which has a 100% match to our
AzCATI LRB1201 strain). The takeover likely started sometime between February 9-16th, and
completely takes over the pond by February 23rd (see Figure 1119). It’s important to note that
the results, from a thorough review of the routine microscopy to the PCR and sequencing work
all occurred over the course of several months after the cultivation run was already completed
so their ability to impact real-time decision making (other than perhaps thorough more
microscopy), was a non-factor.

Subtask 3.2: GFP-based sensor development for real-time nutrient monitoring. (M4-48)
Subtask Summary: Develop and deploy the Qubit GFP-biosensor system for nutrient
monitoring. The QBI biosensor uses engineered bacteria, which respond to the introduction of
specific nutrients/contaminants by producing a photoactive reporter that is monitored using
customized optics. Many sensor strains can be patterned onto a single microfluidic device,
which is the size of a US quarter, making the technology both customizable and expandable.
The sensor runs continuously, reporting the concentration of target compounds in real-time for a
fraction of the cost of other on-line sensors (which can typically only detect one or two
compounds). A single QBI sensor could replace a suite of sensors, kits, and technicians
required by a facility, and can be customized to the specific needs of a facility. The data output
can also be used as feedback to control specific processes, such as the organic loading rate of
a digester or nutrient dosing in an algal pond or trigger harvesting at a specific timepoint once
nutrients are deplete in the media.
For this project, QBI field tested a Qube sensor at the AzCATI research site to evaluate real-
world performance in continually sensing nutrient concentrations in algae culture. We initially
tested our NH4 sensing strain by sensing NH4 concentration in a single culture pond. Next, we
multiplexed our hardware to simultaneously sense NH4 concentration in parallel in three
neighboring culture ponds operated in replicate. Finally, we tested our PO4 sensing strain by
sensing PO4 concentration in parallel in the same three ponds. Although we were able to
successfully calibrate our sensor responses to independent grab samples, the harsh
environmental conditions at AzCATI required several cycles of learning and sensor hardening.
We provide full details of that progression here.
Throughout this grant, QBI visited the AzCATI site 16 times. In our initial deployments, we
aimed to install a Qube to sense ammonia concentration in algae culturing ponds with multiple
strains cultured in BG-11 (5 PPT salinity, i.e. “brackish”) and F/2 (35 PPT salinity, i.e. “seawater”)
media. We expected NH4-N concentration to range from 70 to 0 PPM as the growing culture
depleted the medium.

During our first site visit in March of 2021, we installed a Qube sensor with UPS module
adjacent to algae culturing Pond SPW 22 (see Figure 129a). We wired the sensor to ethernet
and built a recirculation loop between Pond SPW 22 and the Qube’s internal tangential flow filter
(TFF) (see Figure 129b, top left). Pond culture was drawn from the inlet tube by a peristaltic
dispensing pump, pushed through a dual-stage mesh filter into the TFF, and then returned to the
pond via two TFF outlets (see Figure 129c).
Because the sensor inlet stream had a heavy particle load (algae cells), we recirculated the
pond culture through a TFF with 1 mm filter lumen diameter and 500 kDa pore size. Most of the
culture passed directly through the cylindrical lumen and returned to the pond. However, a
small fraction of the culture was filtered through the 500 kDa pores, drawn through a 0.1 µm
sterile syringe filter, and pumped into the Qube inlet fluidics. A fraction of this filtered flow
passed to the microfluidic sensing chip (2.5 ml/day), and the residual was shunted to the pond
to minimize sensing delay due to fluid transit time.
We installed a dual-stage mesh filter upstream of the Qube’s internal filtration system to prevent
aggregated algae cells from clogging the TFF’s lumen (see Figure 129d). Pond culture was
sequentially filtered through 50 and 80 Mesh screens before entering the TFF. Differential
pressure across the mesh screens could be calculated from the upstream and downstream
gauges and indicated when cleaning of the screens was necessary. The downstream filter
pressure gauge additionally measured the pressure drop across the TFF to indicate any fouling
of the TFF lumen.

flow meter days prior to observing bubbles, we believe that our bubble traps were initially

productivity increased.

during our second visit to AzCATI, such that all TFF filtrate was degassed by sequentially

Given our success in sensing PO4 in both pure water and BG-11 culture medium backgrounds

The most significant challenge in operating at this site had been the overheating of parts due to
extreme ambient temperatures. We found that summer temperatures exceeded the cooling
capacity of the Qube’s 139 W thermoelectric module (TEM), causing the sensor internal
temperature to deviate from a stable 37°C (99°F) to up to 43°C (110°F) and potentially resulting
in TEM failure. Because biochemical processes are temperature dependent via the Arrhenius
equation, our biosensor signal deviates under unstable temperature conditions. Additionally, we
found that several sensor hardware components failed without proper temperature control. The
TFFs in the CMs quickly dried out, destroying their hollow fiber membranes. The PharMed BPT
tubing in the peristaltic pumps permanently deformed or cracked, stopping flows. Lastly, the six
24 VDC solenoid valves used in the 2-way valve manifolds to “cross over” the water streams
(see Figure 135) overheated during continuous use and failed to seal properly.
Operating in these hot conditions required us to engineer a more efficient cooling system.
Fundamentally, the internal cooling efficiency of a TEM is limited by the external ambient
temperature. Cooling the interior side of the Peltier junction requires heating the exterior side,
and operational efficiency is limited by the rate which excess heat can be transferred from the
exterior side to the environment. When the environmental temperature is high, more power
must be applied to the Peltier junction to raise the exterior side temperature above ambient in
order to establish this temperature gradient. A simple method to increase TEM efficiency is to
lower the ambient temperature in order to establish a higher temperature gradient and thereby
increase the rate of heat transfer from the exterior side.

During a few early deployments, our Qube electronics experienced hardware and/or software
lock-ups, which required a technician to power cycle the unit. To increase the robustness of our
sensor during extended runtimes, we implemented two complementary watchdog systems that
monitor for irregular behavior and can automatically trigger system resets. We designed a
layered watchdog system such that a “soft” watchdog first attempts to trigger an organized
system reboot. If that fails, a “hard” watchdog cycles power to the System-on-Chip (SoC) to
force a system reboot.
The soft watchdog monitors for proper software operation using a process on the SoC itself. An
administrative process based on a systemctl service reboots the SoC using Linux’s “reboot”
command if our custom core software, WWCS, does not respond appropriately. WWCS is our
Java-based software that organizes communication with microcontrollers, central servers, and a
device-local database. The soft watchdog regularly requests a computational register from
WWCS using a localhost HTTP communication packet, and this register should be zero in value
if the system is operating appropriately. If the register is nonzero or if WWCS does not respond
in the event of a crash, then the watchdog begins a timer that triggers a system reboot after 5
minutes. Successful communication providing the correct register value resets this timer. To
avoid the possibility of getting stuck in a loop of system reboots, the watchdog requires at least
one successful communication before it starts the timer in response to a failure. If a SoC reboot
is finally triggered by the soft watchdog, then the Linux operating system on the SoC
systematically shuts down all processes and performs an organized reboot, giving all SoC
processes a short window of time to save important data and terminate.
In the rare case where the SoC locks up and cannot properly run the soft watchdog process, we
implemented a hard watchdog that operates outside the SoC and allows for a full hardware
restart of the SoC. The software for the hard watchdog runs on our Nordic microcontroller that
communicates with Qube hardware systems and processes commands from the SoC. The
hard watchdog operates much like the soft watchdog in that it requires regular valid input (well-
formed communication packets from WWCS) to reset a timer (default 10 min) that otherwise
triggers a reset upon expiration. The hard watchdog is activated by WWCS on the SoC before
the reset timer is started, which prevents an infinite restart loop if the SoC boots slowly. A hard
reset is triggered by the Nordic microcontroller sending a 5 VDC control signal to open a
programmable mechanical relay to disrupt power to the SoC for 10 s. Around 30 s after power
is restored to the SoC, WWCS communication resumes and the hard reboot procedure is
complete.

boot a new PO4 sensing chip, using the same chip design. Because the typical BG-11 media
recipe used at AzCATI includes only inorganic P in the form of potassium phosphate dibasic
(K2HPO4) at a final concentration of 54.77 mg/L (0.314 mM), each freshly inoculated pond
exclusively contains orthophosphate at a final concentration of 9.74 ppm PO4-P. We initially
diluted this pond culture 16X in our CMs to bring the maximum PO4-P concentration to 609 ppb
at our microfluidic chip. Although this matches the dynamic range of our sensing cells as
measured in lab, we found the strain response at AzCATI to be noisy. Therefore, we decreased

 130

the dilution rate to 4X, which increases the phosphate concentration at the sensing cells to 0—
2,435 ppb PO4-P. Although we have sometimes observed saturation of the phoB genetic
regulatory circuit response at the high end of this range in our lab, we found that it elicits a
stronger strain response without saturation at AzCATI.
After adjusting this dilution rate and performing hardware maintenance, we booted a new
microfluidic chip in which three of four independent analysis lanes were fed filtered algae culture
from three ponds, analogous to the NH4 sensing experiment shown previously. Figure 146,

SPW 22, 24, and 26, respectively, over the first eight days of runtime. Here, triplicate

Continuous Qube measurements generally fall within the 15% accuracy milestone, with most
exceptions at sharp PO4 transitions during pond harvesting and reset events. This is due to the
finite time required to turn over the ~2 mL mixing tube volume such that it reflects the new pond
culture. We also note that Qube PO4 measurements tend to fall faster than grab sample
measurements following culture resets. This may be due to a nonlinearity in the genetic circuit
response and could likely be compensated for by implementing a nonlinear calibration scheme.
Generally, Qube PO4 readings at AzCATI appear noisier than analogous readings performed in
lab, and we continue to investigate the source of this noise. The AzCATI sensor hardware
configuration is unique compared to other builds in that we are continuously monitoring three
ponds in parallel. This required the implementation of multichannel peristaltic pumps and
manifolds to split flows across multiple lanes of a novel microfluidic chip. However, due to
limited hardware and electronics capabilities in the current prototype, our flow control schemes
remain specific to one flow path, with the assumption that parallel paths are controlled similarly.
Some of the observed signal noise may be due to pumping, mixing, and flow splitting operations
having variability across parallel flow paths.

In conclusion, throughout this project, the challenging deployment environment at AzCATI has
been invaluable in stress-testing our Qube biosensor and revealing potential improvements in
its design. We have learned many lessons, and we are currently applying them amongst a
team with expertise in industrial design, mechanical and electrical engineering, and software
development to develop a revised Qube prototype that brings the hardware equivalent of eight
CMs inside a single enclosure. This major revision allows up to four unique process waters to
be drawn into the sensor and analyzed by comparison to four unique reference waters. Up to
11 reagents can be addressed to each of the eight mixing tubes using a manifold system with
pressurized bottle headspaces. Following optional reagent dosing, mixing, and heating steps,
colorimetric analysis can be performed on the process and reference water mixture in each
mixing tube before that solution flows to an independent microfluidic chip lane for biosensor
analysis. All flows into and out of each of the eight mixing tubes are metered with feedback
loops to ensure proper dispensing. Furthermore, all flows to the chip are driven by pressurized
headspaces to generate non-pulsatile flows. The new design integrates three independent
temperature-controlled compartments for culturing engineered strains in the microfluidic chip at
37°C, stably storing reagents at 4°C, and maintaining a stable 20—30°C temperature in the
main enclosure. We are now in the final stages of developing this new prototype and are
excited to field test its expanded in future projects.

Task 4: Sustainability assessment: coupled techno-economic and life cycle modeling.
Sustainability modeling was proactively used during the project to guide experimentation and to
focus research and development on high impact areas. The modeling work has been published
across different studies and leveraged experimentally-validated process models, including
dynamic biological growth modeling [1], [2], [3], [4], with concurrent TEA and LCA [3] to evaluate
different cultivation strategies through the metrics of minimum selling price and global warming
potential [4]. The LCA examined 10 additional environmental impact categories in the areas of
air pollutants, water scarcity, health impacts, and resource depletion.[3] Model uncertainty and
variability in data were addressed through scenario, sensitivity, and Monte Carlo analysis [4].
Sustainability modeling worked focused on comparing the economic viability of semi-continuous
and batch cultivation. Both cultivation strategies were modeled by using the NREL Farm Model
[5] as the baseline system, where ponds are inoculated by an inoculum system consisting of a
stage of horizontal photobioreactors (PBRs), covered ponds, and lined ponds. A high-density
seed train consisting of flat panel PBRs in a greenhouse, outdoor flat panel PBRs, and lined
ponds was also modeled for comparison. The process flow diagram of the process is shown in
Figure 149. Lined and covered ponds operated within the different seed train configurations
were assumed to be operated in batch. Similarly, cultivation batch ponds were assumed to
provide a 20% increase in productivity due to the use of fresh seed for inoculation. On the other
hand, semi-continuous ponds were assumed to be inoculated every 54 days, based on pond
reliability data collected at ASU. Costing data for the seed train unit processes were obtained
from literature [5], [6], [7]. Additional data used to model the seed trains and cultivation in semi-
continuous and batch ponds is provided in Table 18 and Table 19, respectively.

Growth Model
The biological growth model, described in published work [1], [8], has been a powerful tool for
understanding bulk outdoor conditions of pilot and commercial-scale open algae systems.
Throughout the project, experimental data has been utilized to parameterize growth models.
Specifically, data for growth campaigns of the strain Scenedesmus obliquus (UTEX 393) at the
pre-pilot scale has been utilized to validate biological growth model, which in turn enable robust
TEA and LCA [1], [2], [3], [4]. For a comprehensive explanation of the growth model and
validation efforts for the strain UTEX 393, readers are referred to Quiroz et al. 2021. [1].
In addition to the validation of UTEX 393, growth modeling work has also included model
validation work with the strain Picochlorum celeri. Data for model parameterization was obtained
from pre-pilot scale growth campaigns at the Arizona Center for Algae Technology and
Innovation (AzCATI). This data includes outdoor concentration measurements, along with
corresponding pond temperature and photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) measurements.
Cultivation trials conducted from August to October 2019 and from June to September 2021
were used for model parameterization. Outliers were discarded based on the grams per PAR
ratio for specific harvests. This statistical analysis resulted in the inclusion of 16 harvests from
2019 and 28 harvests from 2021.
Primary model inputs include the saturation light intensity and optimal temperature of the strain,
which were retrieved from the literature. Picochlorum celeri is a temperature tolerant strain, with
maximum temperatures reported to reach 45°C and above [9], [10]. Similarly, the optimal
temperature range for this strain has been reported to range between 35 and 40°C [9], [10]. In
addition to its high thermotolerant characteristics, this strain has been shown to perform in high
light intensities ranging from 1000 to 2000 µmol m-2 s-1, as reported by Weissman et al. [11].
Respiration losses for a similar Pico strain were found to range between 0% and 12% [12]. The
remaining strain characteristics such as optical density coefficient and photon efficiency were
empirically determined. Strain characterization parameters and values for the two different
validations are provided in Table 20.

Summary

Sustainability is key to natural resource management. It involves and considers interdependent
variables such as operational efficiency, minimization of environmental and socio-economic
impact. It has become increasingly evident that continued reliance on fossil fuel energy resources
is unsustainable, owing to both depleting world reserves and the greenhouse gas emissions
associated with their use. Therefore, there are vigorous research initiatives aimed at developing
alternative renewable and potentially carbon-neutral solid, liquid, and gaseous biofuels as
alternative energy resources. Based on current knowledge and technology projections, third-
generation biofuels derived explicitly from microalgae are a technically viable alternative energy
resource that is devoid of the major drawbacks associated with first- and second-generation
biofuels. Microalgae are photosynthetic microorganisms with simple growing requirements (light,
sugars, CO2, N, P, and K) that can produce lipids, proteins, and carbohydrates in large amounts
over short periods. These products are transformed into both biofuels and valuable co-products
through different processes. However, several challenges are yet to overcome to commercialize
biofuel from algae. Key among those challenges is the management of contamination in open
ponds; biomass cannot be produced economically without a technology to control contamination.
One of the promising chemicals to reduce contamination is fluazinam,but legalization for
commercial or large-scale production of algae is still an issue.

This report is to draw possible risk assessment strategies based on scientific literature in various
sections of the environment and human health where the application of fluazinam followed by the
technical limitations to analyze fluazinam and viable pest management strategy to mitigate
contamination. This report also encourages the potential algal production company of the future
who are currently on an experimental stage to implement programs related to safety, pest
management for the application of fluazinam for algal production. Moreover, it made an effort to
make a distinction between agriculture setting and algal cultivation to encourage the regulatory
agencies to change the perspective regarding algal advancement, and the possible risk
associated with it.

1. Introduction
With the increase in the population, the demand for resources has also increased drastically. In
this era of technology, energy is one of the vital resources needed to fulfill basic human needs.
But the present resources for energy production are not sustainable, and it has thus become a
global issue because of its dynamics. Currently, there is several ongoing research to find a
sustainable and economic resource. But one resource has shown tremendous potential and
promise to be a solution to the problem is algae. People have known cultivation of plants for
centuries but have shown interest in algae recently. Since the last 15 years, people have realized
the potential of algae, especially micro-algae and its application in almost all the spheres of human
life from food, feed, cosmetics, aquaculture, nutraceutical, and pharmaceutical industries to
biofuels. Currently, there are more than 7000 species of green microalgae found growing in a
variety of habitats.1 Each of them has its potential in terms of producing the necessary chemical
for the process. There is a never-ending bioprospecting potential to find a compatible and efficient
strain. Algae can produce fuel, but it requires time and monetary efficient vegetative cultivation.
Industrial algal cultivation is a relatively new concept from traditional algal cultivation because of
the complex life cycle and diversity of algae, the most problematic part of the cultivation process
is the susceptibility to contamination, which acts as a resistor to growth. Contamination can be
from different viruses, bacteria, and fungi.1 These complexities and vibrant nature of algae make
its cultivation difficult, and it is necessary to establish a well-defined pest management rule and
regulation by EPA for algal cultivation. However, the complex life of algae makes it impossible to
do a complete environmental risk analysis, and the different biological, chemical characteristics

 149

and nature compare to plant, suggests that adopting the same pest-management on plant
cultivation, may not work. Fluazinam is one of the chemicals used to control the contamination
and maximize the growth of algae. IUPAC's name is 3-chloro-N-(3-chloro-5-trifluoromethyl-2-
pyridyl)-α,α,α-tri fluoro-2,6-dinitro-p-toluidine is a fungicide with broad-spectrum activities,
commercialized since the early 1990s.1 However, the application of fluazinam is not yet legal for
commercial-scale production of algal cultivation. Hence, this report is an approach to determine
steps and regulatory framework for its applicability and provides a vision for contamination and
integrated pest management.

1.1. Fluazinam mechanism of action in algae
The most challenging step in the process of algae cultivation is to control the contamination during
the maximum growth period or the activation stage, meaning when the ETC (electron transfer
chain) followed by the building of proton gradient is at its peak level. One of the primary reasons
for the contamination is a fungal infection, which is not only a biological problem but also poses
an economic challenge on an industrial scale.3 Fluazinam belongs to the secondary amine group
and cited as an uncoupler of oxidative phosphorylation; this compound inhibits reactions most
likely on some proton-coupled uptake process necessary for fermentative growth in the cell.3
Besides, some of the functional characteristics of fluazinam are: it controls every stage of the
fungal life cycle, multi-cite action, no cross-resistance, safe, and beneficial. Moreover, fluazinam,
as a fungicide in liquid culture, has the potential for the growth of Haematococcus pluvialis in a
single dose.2

Many recent studies on Fluazinam solubility in water have shown that both parent and

in temperature and pH, the hydrolyzation of the compound also tends to increase at pH 7 and
50°c (DT50= 2.7 to 4.5d) and at pH 9, 50°c (DT50= 3.5 to 9.5d). So, it is crucial to maintain the
volume of the fungicides to its minimum as Lipid production from algal cultivation for biofuel
requires relatively high pH.
Photolysis: Investigation of fluazinam photolysis in water (pH 5) at 20 degrees Celsius when
exposed to simulated sunlight (filtered xenon lamp) show that aqueous photolysis may contribute

above data, is a possibility.

Groundwater contamination by fluazinam is of the least concern as it requires the soil metabolite

algae cultivation must be less strict because the amount of algae cultivation is much less than

3.4 Humans
There have been numerous studies carried out from finding fluazinam interaction with humans to

Considering that, the regulatory agency needs to study and frame separate rules and regulations
for storage, disposal, and application of Fluazinam to algal cultivation because the present rules
and regulations are limited to plant forms.

4. Pathway to risk assessment of fluazinam use in algae cultivation
Risk assessment is the process for evaluating the potential health and ecological effects of a
pesticide. It is very crucial to the process of making decisions about fluazinam. Most of the
elements of risk assessment of fluazinam application in algae cultivation would remain the same
as plant cultivation considering the potential reach of fluazinam in all the spheres of the
environment. However, the degree of certainty is going to be different in the case of algae species

4.7 Risk to the aquatic organism
Aquatic risk assessment procedures for fluazinam should follow a tiered approach.5

i) The first tier is the basis of standardized laboratory toxicity testing with standard test species.
The outcome of these tests is divided with a safety factor (e.g., 10 for chronic and 100 for acute
toxicity data) to establish a maximum permissible concentration. The first tier usually yields a
conservative risk characterization when compared with threshold concentrations for toxic effects
observed in aquatic model ecosystems.5

 155

ii) In case first-tier assessments indicate that there may be concerns, higher-tier approaches may
be used to determine potential impacts on freshwater ecosystems under more environmentally
realistic conditions before admittance can further be evaluated. Suitable procedures may include
microcosm studies or additional toxicity tests to construct species sensitivity distribution curves
(SSD -Species sensitivity distribution approach).5

iii) One of the endpoints for regulatory decision making may be the HCp. The HCp is the
hazardous concentration top% of the species based on the SSD. For example, if the SSD is based
on NOECs (no-observed-effect concentration), the HC5 is the concentration below the NOECs
for 95% of the species.

iv) It is common practice to use the HC5 to derive the acceptable regulatory concentration .8 In
principle, and species sensitivity distributions are based on acute or chronic toxicity data. In case
of the choice for acute toxicity data, different ECS values can be chosen, e.g., EC10s or EC50s.
When an acute HC5 is used to estimate a short-term concentration below which effects on aquatic
communities are considered acceptable, on a theoretical basis, one may find the use of acute
EC10s more appropriate as SSD-input values than acute EC50s since the former are closer
estimates to acute NOECs. The evaluation of the acute HC5 as an adequate estimator of safe
concentrations in the field is still in progress.

v) In this light, comparisons between the outcomes of SSDs and effects on freshwater ecosystems
were made for insecticides and herbicides.13 In contrast to herbicides and insecticides,
comparisons of SSDs and impact on freshwater ecosystems have rarely been published for
fungicides since little information is available in the open domain about this group of compounds.6
In one of the papers, "The species sensitivity distribution approach compared to a microcosm
study..12 It is concluded that combining the data for insecticides, herbicides, and fungicides show
that lower limit acute HC5 values, with high certainty, are more economical than reported Effect
Class 1–2 concentrations.7For fluazinam, the median EC10-based HC5, is also protective.
Compared to insecticides and herbicides, a relatively low number of model ecosystem studies
and SSDs are currently published for fungicides.

9. Open field vs. enclosed greenhouse vs. closed system
Fluazinam's application in the algae industry has three different environments, and depending on
the type of production, workers may have to work in it. The objective of the design of these three
systems is to increase the production of microalgae. However, the practical application for a
commercial scale in terms of economic and technological efficiency is very much different. The
enclosed greenhouse open ponds or fields and closed system, the working environment will be
different in terms of temperature, heat balance, air circulation and amount of fluazinam
application, exposure to the environment, and risk associated with it.

9.1 Closed system
The closed system provides a variety of benefits in algal cultivation. The Greenhouse lighting
Control, humidity Control, Carbon Dioxide Control, Air Temperature Control, and especially the
surface area to volume ratio (s/v) makes an ideal environment for algal growth.8
One of the main problems in outdoor algal cultivation is limited productivity, which appears to be
the result of the following factors:
i) rare light availability to the cells in the deeper layers of the ponds
ii) photoinhibition in the summer caused by very high light intensities in the upper layer of the
ponds.
iii) above optimal day temperatures in the summer and suboptimal temperatures in the winter
iv) increased respiration and hence a loss of biomass caused by high night temperatures in the
summer
v) fluctuating salinity caused by intensive evaporation and
vi) inhibition of growth because of elevated oxygen concentrations.9
vii) The other major problem associated with the outdoor cultivation of algae is the collapse of the
cultures because of contamination by bacteria, fungi, etc.
For example, Vertical tubular photobioreactor, stirred tank photobioreactor, airlift photobioreactor,
etc.

11. Integrated pest management in open ponds
Algal production for biofuel in commercial-scale may provide a viable alternative to fossil fuels.
Still, overcoming numerous challenges before this can become an economic reality is necessary.
Apart from labor cost and capital cost, the main factor responsible for adding a significant capital
expense or reducing the outcome from production is the contamination of algae. Despite open
ponds submaximal productivities, this pond is so far the most cost-effective technology for large
scale production for certain algal species. Still, many of the algal species has not yet been

 164

optimized for open pond technology. The primary challenge with growing algal species in open
ponds is cultural contamination and population crashes. Open ponds are an excellent habitat for
the contaminants that act as a competitor such as bacteria, parasites (fungi, protozoa), etc.

Several strategies have been implemented for mitigation of the impact caused by a contaminant
such as
1. Extreme culture environment: One of the approaches in producing algae in commercial-scale
using open pond technology is maintaining an extreme environment. Such as high salinity, high
alkalinity, and high nutritional status, etc. but even though the process is cost-effective to produce
biofuel, it is supported by the high value of non-fuel products.

2. Chemical deployment: In this strategy, a chemical is introduced into the culture in a way that it
differentially alters the environmental conditions, advantageous to target algae strain and
disadvantageous to contaminants. Example: the use of hypochlorite Nannochloropsis cultures to
control protozoa and disadvantage invasive strains. Ammonia has been used to alter the pH to
target rotifers and cladocerans in an open pond. 9

None of this chemical strategy has been successful in commercial scale or for an extended period
in an open pond. So, considering the diverse nature of algae and its processes, there should be
the practice of integrated pest management or, in this case, contamination management in an
open pond system. So, the implementation of integrated pest management is significant.

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) is a practical and environmentally sensitive approach to pest
management that relies on a combination of common-sense practices. IPM programs use current,
comprehensive information on the life cycles of pests and their interaction with the environment.
This information, along with available pest control methods, is used to manage pest damage by
the most economical means and with the least possible hazard to people, property, and the
environment. Currently, there are no IPM strategies for algae production in open ponds as if in
agricultural settings. But the same can be applied for algal cultivation. However, the processes to
approach the principles would be different, considering the diversity of contaminants in an open
pond. IPM presupposes that the contaminants impacting algae growth are known is tracked and
that strategies that will differentially disadvantage their growth rates for the growth rate of algae
are also known. A critical element of this solution is having a good lab model for the pests that
enable low-cost evaluation of a large-parameter space to ensure that the best strategies can be
devised at the lowest cost and are translatable to the field.
