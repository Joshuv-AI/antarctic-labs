Executive Summary. Our project, called SPIRAL (Science & Processing Informed by Rational
Algorithmic Learning), provided scientific progress that will make it easier to create solar cells
from new materials. The familiar silicon solar cells that adorn roofs to create electricity suffer from
being, ironically, very energy-intensive to make. But finding a comparable material to replace it is
far from easy. This was our challenge.
To tackle this challenge, we combined the emerging power of AI with physical laboratory
experiments. We focused on a material called a metal halide perovskite (usually shortened to
“perovskite”) that is as efficient at producing electricity as silicon but too delicate (attacked by
water and UV radiation) for being commercialized. On the computational side, we created new AI
codes to approach this task. The novelty of our approach was to include more of the underlying
chemistry that defines how the atoms that make up the material interact with each other. Our
results strongly suggest that this is important for making better materials and our AI approach
worked well in that regard. The experimental results were invaluable for giving us important insight
into the way that the ingredients of the material mix together, react to form different clusters and
then are heat-treated to form the solid crystal solar cell material. The experiments and AI
predictions worked well as partners to uncover the delicate and subtle way that the “ingredients”
of the material come together.
As a result of this award, we created three new computer codes that we have made available to
the general public with instructions on how to use them. We have also created a lot of data
describing the nature of the solar cell material’s initial ingredients in solution and the nature of the
final product. All these data are also publicly available. We hope that this will allow other scientists
to expand on our work and be inspired to test other materials. Our AI codes have the advantage
of being flexible enough to work for any other material that looks promising enough to test for a
range of different applications, from materials to allow us to transport and live in other planets, to
better polymers for textiles, to materials that can take otherwise wasted heat and turn it into
electricity, or suggest alternatives to rare and/or valuable elements to protect us against supply-
chain shortages and enhance our independence for national security.
We have educated many young scientists as a result of this award: 3 postdoctoral research
fellows, 12 PhD students, 3 MS students, and 4 undergraduate students. Graduates of our
program have generally stayed working in the U.S. in companies, government labs, become
entrepreneurs, or stayed in academia. These young graduates will spend decades here improving
the quality of life of our citizens.

Original Hypotheses and Main Findings: We hypothesized that machine learning algorithms
would be dramatically more effective in identifying suitable synthesis and processing conditions if

 1

we supplied these algorithms with new information-rich inputs. To explore this hypothesis, we
worked to accelerate the synthesis of energy relevant materials with targeted stability and
durability through the synergistic efforts of experimental, computational, and machine learning
approaches. Our premise is that the fabrication of device-quality materials can be achieved
through a comprehensive understanding of, first, how these materials grow and, second, the
impact of growth pathways on materials properties, device performance, and reproducibility. Over
the four years of funding: 1) We developed three new machine learning (ML) algorithms,
compatible with small data sets, specifically for materials discovery: Nested AutoEncoders,1 a
Hierarchical Transfer Learning Method,2 and PAL 2.0.3 We demonstrated the power of these
algorithms to discover materials for a range of applications. 4-5; 2) We worked to decipher
crystallization and assembly pathways of metal halide perovskites as a function of solution
chemistry and processing variables using a combination of advanced spectroscopies to probe
solution speciation for organic perovskites, coupled tightly with molecular simulation and ML
approaches.7-10; and 3) We designed materials and with tailor-made electronic and ionic
conductivities and optimized processing methods for reproducible device fabrication. 11,12 This
work required the development of the first microscopic theory of electric force microscopy,
enabling the non-contact measurement of thin-film electrical properties and a scanning probe
method for measuring nanosecond photoconductivity transients. 13-15 The tools developed here
are materials-agnostic, broadening their ability to accelerate the transformation of energy systems
through the development of stable and reproducible devices from low-cost, low-energy, solution-
processing protocols, earth-abundant components, and ones that are agile in the event of element
shortages.

PAL 2.0’s Bayesian optimization approach deploys physico-chemical insight into the surrogate
model.3 This method is particularly efficiency in low-data, high-cost experimental domains which
dominate research lab-scale data of the type we typically encounter. Bayesian optimization
inherently gives access to uncertainty quantification. A key strength of PAL lies in its combination
of: (a) feature engineering, initially based on decision trees like LASSO and XGBoost but now
extended, promisingly, to Boruta SHAP analysis, (b) a simple neural network prior, and (c) a
Gaussian process surrogate model. A major PAL asset is that the user does not have to be an
expert, knowing which property(s) is/are most correlated with the optimization target. Our feature
engineering approach (Task (a)) takes care of this autonomously. PAL 2.0 is publicly available
via a GitHub repository.16
We have tested PAL 2.0 in applications covering small datasets (as few as 20 points) and one
large one (70,000 data points), considered one or two objectives, and in situations where we are
not experts in the optimization targets, only in the Bayesian aspects. PAL is flexible enough to be
able to use either “synthetic” (computational) data or experimental data. Applications and results
of PAL 2.0 so far include the following:
  i) Use of a small computational parameter space (240 candidates) to determine the optimum
 solvent or halide for perovskite solubility and hence control over the speed of
 crystallization kinetics. This offered a route to prevent the formation of unwanted
 crystalline intermediates.3 This used only DFT data as its input. With Estroff, we re-
 evaluated earlier PAL 1.0 predictions that were validated against X-ray diffraction for two
 high-performers, two middling-performers and two low-performing systems.7
  ii) Stress-testing PAL 2.0’s ability to sort through a large experimental database (70,000
 candidates) efficiently and accurately. Deshwal et al. had earlier used Bayesian
 optimization to search a large database of 2D and 3D Covalent Organic Framework
 materials to find one that could store the most methane for energy storage applications.
 Using the same number of candidates (around 1%) as in Deshwal et al.’s study, PAL found
 the same best COF material but did so much faster (taking 2-3 iterations compared to
 Deshwal’s ~100).3 This speaks to the appropriateness of our surrogate model, largely
 through the strength of our feature engineering step.
 iii) Closed-loop discovery of strong MPEA (multiple principal element alloy) for spacecraft
 parts. With an experimental database of 300 alloys, we made predictions of better 5-
 element alloys that were screened for cost, manufacturability and availability. Remaining
 viable predictions that met these criteria were experimentally fabricated and characterized
 for strength and these data fed back into the database. The incrementally enlarged data
 base was then used to retrain the PAL 2.0 predictions and the process repeated. After
 three iterations, PAL predicted what became the three strongest alloys (new materials)
 and found five of the eight strongest alloys in the PAL-expanded database of around 310
 materials.4 Importantly, PAL’s predictions found so-called “out of distribution” MPEA alloy
 materials in which new elements in the periodic table were involved. For instance, none
 of the original 300 alloys included silicon, but PAL found several Si-containing alloys that
 were particularly high-performers. This could be invaluable for finding replacements for
 elements that have supply-chain fragility or high cost or poor manufacturability.
 iv) Tiny experimental dataset (~12 points) with dual objectives to make an Al alloy as strong
 as possible with as little tendency to corrode as possible for an orthopedic screw
 application. The initial 12 data points strained PAL’s ability to the limit. It was large enough

 3

 to use feature engineering to good effect and help our experimental collaborators predict
 properties that PAL suggested were important.5,17 Ongoing PAL predictions (unpublished)
 will move the field closer to meeting both objectives.
 v) De novo design of high-performing conducting polymers using only computational data.
 We created an extensive DFT library of ~8000 synthetic (computationally posited)
 polymers all of which included a central DPP block, from which we used PAL to find the
 optimal Gibbs free energy of solvation.6 Using PAL 2.0, we found a minimum free energy
 (most favorable) at a dielectric constant of about k=10. This result was validated by

 linked the quadrupole moment to the best candidates. We repeated this approach for IDT-
 based polymer class and found similarly well validated results experimentally, suggesting
 that this is a fruitful and resource-conservative way to conduct the optimization of
 processing conditions as well as the identification of optimal compositions. 6
The test cases above position PAL at the leading edge of Bayesian optimization-based materials
discovery.

3) Machine-learned interatomic models (ML-IAMs) using ChIMES (Lindsey). While ab initio
simulations provide critical insights into speciation, they are limited in accessible spatio-temporal
scale due to their expense. To enable accelerated studies of speciation and nucleation from the
liquid phase, Lindsey has developed physics-informed, machine-learned interatomic models
(IAMs) using the ChIMES framework. The ChIMES framework's hierarchical structure enables
modular development of high-fidelity interatomic potentials, making it possible to accurately model
multi-element perovskites and extend these models in the future to incorporate solvent
interactions. This approach offers quantum-accurate predictions at a fraction of the computational
expense, making large-scale and long-timescale simulations feasible. But applying ML-IAMs to
solution-phase perovskite synthesis is a significant challenge due to the presence of many
chemically distinct elements.
High-quality training sets are essential for machine-learning models to reliably predict material
properties. However, traditional methods for producing these datasets are often time-consuming
and computationally expensive, especially when dealing with systems that involve multiple

 5

elements. To address this challenge, we designed an innovative workflow that uses random
atomic perturbations to produce diverse material configurations. Rather than relying on lengthy
molecular dynamics simulations at elevated temperatures, this method creates a wide range of
realistic structures by introducing controlled random movements in atomic positions. Each
resulting structure is then assessed using quantum simulations to obtain energies, forces, and
stresses. This streamlined procedure allows us to efficiently sample configurations representative
of both typical and extreme material dynamics.
We tested this new sampling approach on simpler two-element systems such as PbBr2. As shown
in Fig. 4, our comparison demonstrated that expanding the number and type of sampled
structures led to models that were both faster to train and better at predicting material
performance. Most importantly, the new sampling method reduced training set generation costs
dramatically, from approximately 100,000 CPU hours for 50 training snapshots down to about
18,000 CPU hours for 200 distinct training configurations.
Building on this success, we adapted the method for more complex, three-element perovskites,
demonstrating its utility and adaptability across different material systems. By establishing this
efficient workflow, we have paved the way for rapid model development and future expansion to
a broader range of materials. This breakthrough not only reduces the time and computational
resources needed for model training but also provides a flexible template that can be used with
other perovskite derivatives or other rigid crystalline materials.

Building on the efficient training set generation approach, we evaluated model performance at
three key temperature state points: room temperature (300 K), moderate heating (500 K), and
well above the melting point (1000 K). While the models were robust at lower temperatures,
simulations at higher temperatures and under changing conditions like stress or pressure
revealed significant challenges. The models sometimes lost stability or encountered scenarios
outside the range of their training data, such as melting behaviors that caused catastrophic
simulation breakdowns.
To solve this, we introduced an advanced automated approach known as “active learning.” This
method improves the model by identifying challenging areas and generating new, targeted data
for those specific situations. We expanded our training sets to include examples of the material
transitioning from a solid to a molten salt or ionic liquid phase, better reflecting the real states
found near the melting phase boundary in practical environments. This enhancement is critical,
as real-world perovskite devices may undergo similar transformations during operation.

Through repeated cycles of active learning and broadened data sampling, our models steadily
improved in both stability and reliability at high temperatures and across different physical states;
see Fig. 5. As a result, these models can now predict perovskite behavior over a much wider
range of conditions relevant to device manufacturing and operation. Our solutions open new
opportunities for designing materials with enhanced stability, supporting next-generation
technologies. These models will enable investigations into melt-phase nucleation processes,
including speciation in the melt and halide partitioning behaviors in mixed-halide lattices,
phenomena that are challenging to study experimentally or with conventional simulation methods.

Deciphering crystallization and assembly pathways as a function of solution chemistry
During the four years of the project, we combined advanced spectroscopies with molecular
simulation and ML approaches to answer the following questions: 1) How does solution speciation
define the energy landscape for nucleation? 2) How to characterize and predict (experimentally
and computationally) solution speciation as a function of solution composition and to identify the
most critical species in solution leading to nucleation? 3) How to assemble these species to
capture nucleation and growth phenomena and guide materials properties, employing information
transfer across 10 to 15 orders of magnitude in space and time?
1) Stability of iodoplumbate complexes in solution: In collaboration with a team at PNNL (through
a DOE SCGSR award), Clancy made huge strides in using DFT and AIMD simulations to uncover
the more important species existing in MHP precursor solutions, which was previously unknown.
We found that lower-order iodoplumbates were the important stable species in DMF (especially
PbI2).8 This is a major step towards being able to simulate MHP nucleation with atomic-scale
precision. We are initiating new connections to NLR (with Drs. R. Kerner and J. Berry) and will
continue the collaborations with PNNL.

4) Thermodynamic and Kinetic Modulation of Methylammonium Lead Bromide Crystallization.
Estroff and Tao, together with an SCGRS funded student, reported the first in situ AFM growth
study of MHPs, which provides quantitative information on the kinetics of MAPbBr 3 growth on
steps produced by screw dislocation spirals. 7 By tuning the temperature to change solution
supersaturation, we observe growth hillocks on the {100} facet and track the growth/dissolution
dynamics in the spiral growth regime. Temperature-dependent step velocity measurements
demonstrate that increasing concentration of formic acid (FoA) decreases both the solubility of
MAPbBr3 and the kinetic coefficient (b) of step movement. Moreover, solution 1H-NMR
measurements show that the tumbling motion of the MA ions is inhibited by FoA. Our findings
establish a direct correlation between the mesoscale crystal growth kinetics and the molecular-
scale interactions between organic additives and constituent ions, providing unprecedented
insights for developing predictive syntheses of MHP crystals.
Design of materials and with tailor-made electronic and ionic conductivities and optimized
processing methods for reproducible device fabrication.
During the four years of the project, we developed new synthetic and processing methods to
achieve reproducible thin-film and device fabrication, as well as combining computational
methods with state-of-the-art electronic measurements to answer the following question: How are
light-induced defects related to electronic properties, material stability, and device durability in
these materials under in operando conditions? We developed methods to measure mobile defects
in the materials, in the dark and under perturbation from light and/or electrical bias, using a
combination of high-throughput qualitative optical and electrical measurements in combination
with quantitative electrical scanning probe microscope measurements, backed by rigorous
electrodynamic theory, to determine defect mobility, concentration, and lifetime.
Development of synthesis and processing methods:
Improved Reproducibility of Metal Halide Perovskite Solar Cells. Moore and Choi developed an
automated gas quenching (GQ) system to improve perovskite solar cell reproducibility at the lab-
scale.11 We used in-situ photoluminescence (PL) to monitor perovskite film formation as a function
of glove box atmosphere and conclude that antisolvent quenching is more sensitive to lingering
precursor solvents than the GQ method. We observed better reproducibility with GQ than
antisolvent quenching because it maintains a more consistent atmosphere in the glove box. The

 9

automated GQ process leads to high-performing devices, reproducible both batch-to-batch and
researcher-to-researcher. Insights into GQ film formation as a function of solvent atmosphere and
quench velocity will inform future studies on large scale fabrication systems. This system was
implemented at Cornell in the Marohn and Estroff labs and used to fabricate over 80 formulations
of a dual cation, dual halide lead perovskite (Kaipa, A; MS thesis; Cornell University; 2025). These
samples were characterized by scanning electron microscopy and X-ray diffraction and used as
a resource for training Clancy’s ML algorithms. We plan to analyze their electrical properties
using electric force microscopy in the coming months. These measurements will enable us to
test the hypothesis that the crystallization pathway affects (can be used to control) a sample’s
electrical properties.
Mechanistic study of CsPbBr₃ nanorods formed by solution deposition on cleaved mica (Tao).
We investigated heterogeneous, substrate-directed nucleation of CsPbBr₃ under confined
solution growth between cleaved muscovite mica surfaces, and quantified the nucleation and
growth of CsPbBr₃ by measuring nanorod size/orientation using automated SEM image analysis
and Fourier-transform (FFT) metrics (Fig. 8). Mica promotes rapid, oriented nucleation of
CsPbBr₃ nanorods. The nanorods exhibit three discrete preferred in-plane growth directions
(three dominant peaks 50°, 100° and 150° in the FFT and orientation histograms), indicating
strong coupling between nanorod alignment and the underlying mica lattice symmetry. A key next
step is to use high-resolution structural imaging to directly resolve the perovskite–mica interfacial
registry (epitaxial/incommensurate matching) that seeds these discrete orientations.

Development of new scanning probe methods and analyses for optoelectronic property
characterization.
During the four years of the project, we worked to: (1) Demonstrate that the crystallization pathway
affects the steady-state conductivity (electronic and ionic), conductivity dynamics, and charge
carrier recombination dynamics in 3D metal-halide perovskites (MHP) and layered 2D hybrid
Ruddlesden-Popper phases (HRPPs). (2) Measure MHP and HRPP electronic conductivity, ionic
conductivity, and photostability across a wide array of chemical compositions, crystallization
routes, and substrates; develop an accompanying microscopic theory valid for both MHPs and
HRPPs. In the following summary, the underlined paragraph headings mirror the tasks delineated
in the grant. This work employed novel non-contact electric force microscope experiments
available in Marohn’s laboratory at Cornell, Fig. 9(a). A central challenge in measuring
perovskite electrical properties is that light creates ionic conductivity. This ionic conductivity is
attributed to halide vacancies created through a chemical reaction involving photogenerated
holes, Fig. 9(b).
Quantify both electronic and ionic conductivity. In Marohn’s previously published broadband
local dielectric spectroscopy (BLDS, doi:10.1021/acsenergylett.6b00722) and phase-kick electric
force microscope (pk-EFM, doi:10.1126/sciadv.1602951) experiments, we fit data using an
electro-mechanical impedance model in which the sample was represented as a resistor and
capacitor. Efforts to develop a heuristic equation for converting the best-fit capacitance and
resistance into a microscopic dielectric constant, charge density, and conductivity were
unsuccessful, giving unphysical results. In collaboration with Cornell theorist Roger Loring, we
developed the first microscopic theory of the electric force microscope experiment. Here
the cantilever is modeled as a point charge attached to a harmonic oscillator; the point charge
interacts through a reaction field with a semiconductor (backed by a metal) having a specified
thickness, complex dielectric constant, charge density, and conductivity. The theory correctly
predicted the non-linear dependence of frequency shift and dissipation on light intensity (i.e.
charge density) seen in CsPbBr3 by Tirmzi, Dwyer, and Marohn
(doi:10.1021/acsenergylett.6b00722). Furthermore, it enabled the estimation of sample
conductivity from BLDS spectra.13
Loring’s point-charge theory failed, however, to predict the size of the frequency shift, dissipation,
and BLDS spectrum correctly and, for this reason, did not allow us to estimate sample dielectric
constant or charge density. Marohn and Loring next developed an image-charge model that
enabled the numerical computation of frequency shift, dissipation, and BLDS spectra for a

Marohn also devised a new experiment to probe material quality in lead-halide perovskites:
measuring light-induced deformation in single millimeter-scale crystals of methylammonium
lead tribromide and methylammonium lead triiodide. These experiments used laser
interferometry to measure sub-angstrom displacements on the microsecond timescale following
illumination with variable wavelength light. In most crystals, upon turning on illumination we
observed a rapid deformation (10 seconds) followed by slow deformation (10's of seconds); upon
turning off the illumination, a rapid relaxation was observed which did not reach baseline. The
photodeformation depended on crystal quality, was large even for below-bandgap irradiation, and
was not eliminated by cooling. These and other observations suggest that the photodeformation
arises not from photogenerated electronic carriers (Zhou et al., doi:10.1038/ncomms11193) or
light-induced vacancies (Tirmzi et al., doi:10.1021/acsenergylett.6b00722; Kim et al.,
doi:10.1002/anie.202005853) but from the interaction of photogenerated electronic carriers with
grain boundaries, vacancies, or other atomic defects in the crystal. A manuscript describing this
discovery is in preparation.

Low-energy pathways lead to self-healing defects in CsPbBr3 (Clancy, Marohn, Moore). This
 research tackled a critical question in the field of metal

 (and not just form) under operational conditions, and are
 these materials able to successfully recover from defect
 formation? Focusing on CsPbBr₃, the team conducted a
 comprehensive computational study using density

 migrate and recombine within the crystal lattice. We
 discovered a cooperative “domino effect” mechanism,

 enables low-barrier, long-range in isolation, but in a cascade of coordinated atomic motions
 healing mechanism for defects in that allows a distant vacancy to be healed (Fig. 12).12 This

 concerted behavior leads to surprisingly low activation
 energies for defect migration (~0.5 eV) closely matching
experimental measurements by Marohn of slow, thermally activated conductivity recovery. This
work goes beyond literature calculations of formation energies by providing a database of defect
migration pathways, corrections for charged defect calculations, and consideration of
