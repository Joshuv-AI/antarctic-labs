CAE-BrainNet: a statistically validated class-adaptive attention ensemble model for explainable brain tumor classification from MRI
Authors: Mehedi Hasan Meraj, Mashfiquzzaman Tajbid, Mayen Uddin Mojumdar, Narayan Ranjan Chakraborty, Mohammaed Jabed Morshed Chowdhury, Kamanashis Biswas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeep learning has further accelerated progress in automated Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) based classification of brain tumors, whereas prior studies have often reflected critical limitations such as narrow classification scope, insufficient Explainable Artificial Intelligence, weak statistical validation, and incomplete deployment metrics. These ultimately impede clinical trust and practical deployment. This study presents the CAE-BrainNet, a statistically validated Class-Adaptive Attention Ensemble model, which integrates representations of EfficientNetV2-M, DenseNet201, and ConvNeXt-Base dynamically, with complementary inductive biases based on different tumor morphology. Unlike uniform ensembles, a learned class-adaptive attention mechanism, parameterised by a 4× 3 weight matrix with a trainable temperature scalar, adaptively leverages model-specific morphological strengths, serving as the primary performance driver confirmed by ablation analysis. A hybrid optimization strategy based on grid search refinement via gradient-based optimization is also employed to estimate optimal hyperparameters. In addition, McNemar’s and Cochran’s Q tests were conducted, proving that the accuracy gains are statistically significant and not stochastic in nature. Experimental results show that the proposed CAE-BrainNet achieves 99.39% accuracy on a challenging four-class benchmark, with clinically critical 99.89% specificity in the identification of healthy tissue. To overcome the limitations of narrow scope and generalizability, zero-shot transfer assessment on the independent BRISC2025 dataset yields 98.10% accuracy, statistically validated against all base models, confirming cross-dataset robustness without retraining. Quantitative explainability via Grad-CAM, AWCLF, and IoU spatial agreement, combined with formal hypothesis testing and publicly accessible deployment at https://huggingface.co/spaces/Meraj-21/Brain-Tumor-Classification-Using-Grad-CAM, makes CAE-BrainNet a rigorous, transparent, and clinically deployable benchmark for brain tumor classification.Keywords: Brain tumor classification, Class-adaptive ensemble, Computer-aided diagnosis (CAD), Explainable AI (XAI), Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), Statistical validation

Introduction

Brain tumors are one of the primary causes of cancer-related deaths and are also known to have high morbidity and mortality rates. Early and accurate diagnosis of cancer is still a pressing concern in the medical field because it can significantly increase the cure rate and allow for personalization of treatment [1, 2]. MRI is a commonly used non-invasive diagnostic tool for observing abnormalities in the brain tissues [3–5]. However, the analysis of images obtained from various diagnostic tools like MRI tends to be intensive, with significant variations in diagnosis performed by different observers [3, 6].

The area of automatic analysis of brain tumors has developed significantly with the advent of Deep Learning (DL), accounting for the significant shift from traditional machine learning (ML) approaches relying upon hand-crafted features [5–7]. The latest solutions use Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers (ViTs) for this particular task [6, 8]. Initially, successful approaches utilized transfer learning (TL) [9] models like VGG-16, ResNet-50, and InceptionV3 for the purpose of tumor type classification, including Glioma, Meningioma, and Pituitary tumors in particular [3, 10]. Recent literature highlights two primary technical strategies utilized to overcome existing diagnostic hurdles:

Ensembling and hybrids: The combination of different classifiers is always better in making models more robust as they have different feature representations, and in many cases, give more accurate gains than a single learner.

New CNN architectures: Many researchers have developed advanced CNN architectures like multi-scale attention networks and customized residual networks with accuracy of more than 98% in many cases [1, 11, 12]. These architectures perform well in detecting tumors and classifying MRI images [4, 13].

Technological advancements are associated with growing clinical complexity. The current system of classifying tumors includes molecular parameters like Isocitrate Dehydrogenase (IDH) mutational status and 1p/19q codeletion in addition to conventional histology [14, 15]. The system needs to be able to handle data variability and provide confidence in addition to accuracy [5]. The challenges include the issue of heterogeneity and the unavailability of sufficient datasets [7, 16].

Despite the high accuracy rates being documented in diagnosis, current DL approaches are still fragmented in terms of usefulness and not clinical in comprehensiveness. Four major gaps in research currently persist and constitute the problem this research aims to address:

Scope and generalizability gap—narrow task and missing cross-dataset validation: Most methods claim high accuracy in simplified binary (malignant versus benign) or three-way classification problems, and rarely benchmarking against the clinically realistic four-class problem (Glioma–Meningioma–Pituitary–No Tumor). More importantly, cross-dataset evaluation on independent cohorts remains largely absent, rendering claims of clinical readiness premature.

The objective of this research is to accomplish robust multiclass classification based on the classification of brain tumors in the images obtained from the MRI scans (Glioma, Meningioma, Pituitary, No-Tumor) in one robust DL framework.

With this in mind, this study focuses on designing a diagnostic tool that addresses the lack of transparency and validation in the current literature. The primary technical and methodological contributions of this study are outlined below.

This class-adaptive algorithm obtained higher accuracy on the benchmark than the best single base model. Literature review

Automated classification of brain tumors, mainly glioma, meningioma, and pituitary tumors, from MRI has become a topic of significant interest in medical image analysis. Recently, DL, especially CNNs, has emerged as the state-of-the-art and replaced traditional ML methods that rely on explicit feature extraction. The literature demonstrates a rapid progression from using standard TL models to highly specialized custom architectures and hybrid systems, all aimed at the highest possible diagnostic accuracy.

One of the most common and successful methods utilizes pre-trained TL models. A number of studies have benchmarked common architectures, including Xception, InceptionV4, and VGG-16. For example, works by Amarnath et al. [21] and Disci et al. [22] both found Xception performed best for 4-class classification on the large Kaggle dataset with results of 98.17% and 95.27% (test), respectively. Bibi et al. [23] also had success with InceptionV4, reaching an accuracy of 98.7% on a 4-class problem. Malakouti et al. [24] illustrated the strength of fine-tuning in adjusting GoogLeNet for a 2-class problem, achieving the very high accuracy of 99.3%. Other popular models, such as VGG-16, also performed well, as demonstrated by Shamshad et al. [25] (97.2%) and Srinivas et al. [26] (96.0%, 2 class); however, these works also comment on the significant computational cost, often requiring training times greater than 3000 s. Further supporting the efficacy of these pre-trained networks, Rastogi et al. [27] demonstrated that a fine-tuned Xception model outperformed several other transfer learning architectures, achieving a 96.11% accuracy in distinguishing tumorous from non-tumorous MRI scans.

A number of works build further on this by developing novel CNN architectures that are specifically tailored for brain tumor features. Examples include the custom residual and regional CNN, called Res-BRNet, which was introduced by Zahoor et al. [20] and reached 98.22% on the large 4-class dataset. Rahman et al. [28] proposed a Dilated Parallel Deep CNN (PDCNN) to capture features across multiple scales, reaching 98.35%. Other researchers have developed custom 22-layer CNNs-the work of Alanazi et al. [29] resulted in 96.90%-or dense custom architectures as seen in Özkaraca et al. [30], or optimized standard CNNs with metaheuristic algorithms, such as Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO), with 98.0% achieved by Kurdi et al. [31]. These studies indicate that architectures developed specifically for MRI data can outperform generic and pre-trained models. Similarly, as a part of enhancing the model structure and clinical transparency, Afnaan et al. [32] used an optimized ResNet architecture with XAI methods such as Grad-CAM and LIME to detect tumors with high precision. Also, in the same spirit of making adaptations to suit this task, Ilani et al. [33] repurposed the U-Net architecture by adapting segmentation networks to brain tumor classification, achieving 98.56% accuracy.

The third trending topic is designing hybrid and ensemble systems: combining DL with other computational methods. One of the highest accuracies reported, 99.31%, was obtained by Nassar et al. [34], using a hybrid majority vote among the outputs of five different pre-trained models, effectively leveraging the strengths of each one. In another vein, Asiri et al. [35] used a two-stage pipeline that first executed an Independent Component Analysis (ICA) and neural network (NN) image enhancement algorithm, followed by feeding the result to a traditional Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifier and obtained 98.9% accuracy with an incredible inference time of only 0.43 s. Another hybrid model was proposed by Sahoo et al. [36], where a Progressively Growing Generative Adversarial Network (PGGAN) was coupled with a Scatter Sharp Optimized CNN (SSO-CCNN) and reached an accuracy of 97.41%.

Despite reporting exceptionally high accuracies, this body of literature exposes several important research gaps. First, many papers report near-perfect accuracy on simpler 2-class (e.g., Malakouti et al. [24] and Kurdi et al. [31]) or 3-class (e.g., Nassar et al. [34] and Asiri et al. [35]) classification problems; thus, direct comparison between models is challenging. This prevents the determination of whether their performance would hold on the more complex 4-class (glioma, meningioma, pituitary, no-tumor) task or if the high performance is due to model architecture or problem simplicity. Second, the black box problem remains largely unaddressed. Only a few papers, such as those by Alshuhail et al. [37] and Zahoor et al. [20], implement any form of XAI, including Grad-CAM or t-SNE, which is a critical barrier to clinical trust. Third, the lack of formal statistical validation is a prominent gap; the vast majority of papers fail to perform statistical tests, such as McNemar’s and Cochran’s Q test, to confirm that their model’s superior performance is statistically significant. Finally, the computational cost is not consistently reported, with many studies not reporting training or inference times, making it difficult to assess their clinical viability.

Methodology

To address the identified research gaps, CAE-BrainNet, a class-adaptive attention ensemble framework for comprehensive brain tumor classification is proposed. Unlike previous studies that apply uniform ensemble weights across all tumor types, a learned 4× 3 attention weight matrix governed by a trainable temperature scalar enables tumor-specific model contributions, where each base classifier contributes differentially depending upon the tumor class to be predicted. Fig. 1 depicts the complete workflow, which systematically addresses each of the identified research gaps through rigorous methodology and validation.

Complete workflow of CAE-BrainNet framework

Four key gaps are directly addressed: First, the framework benchmarks on the complete 4-class task, and cross-dataset generalizability is validated through zero-shot transfer on the independent BRISC2025 dataset without retraining which is enabled by the complementary inductive biases of three architecturally diverse CNN backbones fused through class-adaptive attention. Second, comprehensive explainability is implemented through ensemble-level Grad-CAM visualization, new AWLCF, and quantitative spatial agreement via IoU. Third, formal statistical hypothesis testing through McNemar’s and Cochran’s Q tests establishes that results achieve significance at p < 0.001, supported by Cohen’s g effect sizes consistently exceeding the large-effect threshold across all pairwise comparisons. Fourth, both training and inference times are provided to demonstrate clinical feasibility, and practical accessibility is ensured by deploying the final model as a live, interactive demonstration on the Hugging Face platform.

To ensure reproducibility and prevent data leakage, the 7023-image dataset was partitioned into a training set and an independent test set, maintaining an 81:19 ratio via stratified sampling. The test set remains completely isolated until final evaluation. All decisions regarding hyperparameter optimization, tuning of weights, and model selection have been done using 5-fold cross-validation (CV) on the training set alone. All experiments use a fixed random seed of 42 to support reproducibility.

Dataset acquisition

The dataset was constructed by combining three publicly available brain tumor MRI repositories so that a wide variety of tumor morphologies and imaging characteristics could be included [38]. The combined dataset consists of 7023 T1-weighted contrast-enhanced MRI images categorized systematically into four classes as described in Fig. 2: glioma with 1621 images representing 23.1% of the dataset, meningioma with 1645 images representing 23.4%, pituitary tumor with 1757 images representing 25.0%, and no-tumor with 2000 images representing 28.5%.

Distribution of 7023 brain tumor MRI images across four classes with relatively balanced representation

The Figshare brain tumor dataset, compiled by Cheng et al. [39], served as the main source and contains 3064 verified T1-weighted contrast-enhanced images representing three different types of tumors. The second source was the SARTAJ Brain Tumor Classification dataset [40]; however, preliminary validation experiments revealed systematic misclassification of glioma images in this repository. In order to fix this problem, the problematic glioma images were replaced with verified samples originating from the Figshare repository [39], which have undergone thorough radiologist verification. Images of the no-tumor class were exclusively obtained from the Br35H brain tumor detection dataset [41] to properly represent healthy brain anatomy. The approximate integration ratio of Figshare:SARTAJ:Br35H sources was 4:3:2 [38].

The dataset was divided into two separate folders: Train and Test. The Train folder consisted of 5712 images, which were used for training and validation purposes. The Test folder remained untouched during training and validation. This folder consisted of 1311 images, which were used for final testing only.

BRISC dataset: In order to strictly assess the external validity of the proposed model, an independent zero-shot transfer validation was performed on the basis of the newly introduced BRISC dataset [42]. According to Fateh et al. [42], the BRISC dataset is a collection of high-resolution contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MRI scans that have been aggregated across a number of open datasets and carefully annotated by qualified radiologists and physicians. To perform this independent validation step, we used test folder (containing 1000 images) of the dataset with strictness, i.e., Glioma (254), Meningioma (306), No Tumor (140), Pituitary (300) to evaluate the extent to which the model can generalize to different data distributions and scanner properties. Base model architecture and training

Three CNN architectures namley, EfficientNetV2-M [46], DenseNet201 [47], and ConvNeXt-Base [48] are selected as base classifiers, as shown in Fig. 5. These architectures were chosen to increase ensemble diversity by incorporating different inductive biases. EfficientNetV2-M uses compound scaling along with inverted residual blocks in order to keep the parameters efficient. DenseNet201 uses dense connectivity in order to maximize feature reuse across layers. ConvNeXt-Base rethinks ResNet by introducing depthwise convolutions and training tricks from vision transformers. Though the recent designed transformer families, like Swin Transformer V2 [49], ViT-B/16 [50], MaxViT [51] were evaluated by empirical performance matching on the same benchmark, they yielded inferior performance and were more architecturally similar providing less representational diversity than the selected CNN trio. The three chosen architectures selected maximize the differences in inductive biases, (i.e. compound scaling, dense feature reuse and depthwise modernisation), so that each backbone captures complementary morphological signal dynamics that uniform or transformer based ensembles cannot reconstruct. Weight optimization and ensemble model

This study provides a class-adaptive weighted fusion architecture to enable model specialization for different tumor types, unlike other uniform weighting or majority voting. For instance, it is likely that ConvNeXt can better capture textured gliomas through its depthwise convolutions. On the other hand, EfficientNetV2-M with compound scaling can excel at smooth meningiomas, while DenseNet optimizes for vascular pituitary tumors.

The proposed CAE-BrainNet framework, as illustrated in Fig. 6, extracts deep features from each base model’s penultimate layer such as complex tumor boundary morphologies, multi-scale texture variations, and localized spatial relationships within the MRI slices. Experimental protocol and evaluation framework

In this section, the evaluation method, explainability analysis, statistical validation framework, and experimental setup are described. The components are described separately from the architectural methodology for clarity and are consistent with best practices for reporting clinical AI studies [5].

Model evaluation

Model performance is evaluated on the basis of standard multi-class classification metrics on the held-out test set. Accuracy measures the overall correctness of classification across all four tumor classes. Precision measures the proportion of positive predictions for each class that are actually correct, while recall measures the proportion of actual positives that are correctly identified. Specificity evaluates the model’s ability to identify negative cases correctly, calculated as the ratio of true negatives to the sum of true negatives and false positives. The F1-score provides the harmonic mean of precision and recall, balancing both metrics. Furthermore, the Area Under the Curve (AUC) is employed to quantify the model’s discriminative ability to distinguish between tumor classes across all possible classification thresholds. Agreement-weighted LIME with consistency filtering (AWLCF). Standard LIME [57] is not an accurate method for ensemble models because it only provides an explanation based on the dominant bias of the ensemble. When different models have different architectural biases and disagree in the spatial location of the explanation, the ensemble explanations are not accurate. Therefore, an Agreement-Weighted LIME with Consistency filtering (AWLCF) method is proposed for CAE-BrainNet.

Statistical analysis

This study executes an extensive pairwise and multiple model comparison using non-parametric tests suitable for classification tasks with dependent samples to establish statistical significance and validate performance improvements. All statistical analyses are performed at a significance level of α = 0.05 with exact p-values reported to six decimal places.

For pairwise model comparisons, McNemar’s test [58] is utilized to assess whether the ensemble model exhibits statistically significant gains over individual base models. McNemar’s test examines the null hypothesis of two related classifiers having equal error rates via a 2 × 2 contingency table of correct and incorrect predictions:

Model deployment

To make this work accessible and verifiable by other researchers, the trained ensemble model is made available as a free web application. The system runs on a Flask [60] backend that manages the prediction process. TensorFlow runs the base models and PyTorch [61] handles the ensemble’s attention mechanism, connected through standard web API endpoints for rapid inference. The model is saved using PyTorch’s standard format (.pth files), ensuring cross-platform compatibility.

When processing images, the system follows the exact same steps used during training—images are resized to 224 × 224 pixels and standard ImageNet normalization is applied to maintain consistency. Users can view predictions from individual models or the combined ensemble result, and Grad-CAM heatmaps can be generated on demand to visualize the model’s diagnostic focus regions. The application is hosted on Hugging Face Spaces [62] at https://huggingface.co/spaces/Meraj-21/Brain-Tumor-Classification-Using-Grad-CAM, providing GPU computing resources and ensuring continuous availability. This deployment addresses important concerns in medical AI: transparency, reproducibility, and accessibility, while keeping patient data private since all image processing occurs client-side.

Results

This section presents a thorough comparison of CAE-BrainNet with six of the competing architectures: three CNN-based base learners and three recent transformer-based models. It is evaluated on five dimensions: (i) held-out test set performance on the merged dataset (N=1311), (ii) 5-fold CV stability, (iii) per-class diagnostic analysis, (iv) zero-shot transfer to independent BRISC2025 dataset () without retraining, and (v) component-level ablation. A rigorous statistical validation model is used to test the primary benchmark and the transfer benchmark to distinguish between algorithmic progress and random fluctuation. Interpretability is measured by ensemble-level Grad-CAM, spatial agreement of IoU, and a new analysis, AWLCF. Each test is done at α = 0.05 with 95% WI confidence intervals.

AWLCF: Agreement-weighted LIME with consistency filtering. LIME can be applied to ensemble models to obtain explanations that are biased by the dominant model instead of the overall rationale of the ensemble when architecturally diverse backbones serve dissimilar spatial regions. Figure 14 illustrates this limitation directly. The single base model LIME maps are activated widely throughout the brain, and each model emphasizes a distinct yet partially overlapping set of superpixels, resulting in more model-specific than pathology-specific explanations. These various activations are aggregated in the CAE-BrainNet LIME column reflected in the raw ensemble explanation and consequently remains diffuse. The AWLCF column illustrates the benefit of the proposed method: when ensemble importance is gated using cross-model consistency only the superpixels which are consistently activated by all three backbones are retained. Also, AWLCF reduces the wide, noisy activation to a sparse, anatomically localized set of spots. In the case of Glioma, the superpixels that are of interest are directly associated with the tumour boundary that is marked as infiltration in the processed image. In the case of Pituitary, attention concentrates on the sellar region. For Meningioma, the dural attachment site is accurately identified. This accuracy is clinically essential: a radiologist consulting the explanation does not need to resolve conflicting activation signals from different models but instead receives a single, reliable map representing where the ensemble collectively agrees the pathology lies. AWLCF is able to visualise the ensemble and capable of authenticating it, giving it quantitative evidence that the decision basis of CAE-BrainNet is architecturally coherent and anatomically based.

Discussion

The CAE-BrainNet was created in response to the most important gaps in the existing state of automated brain tumor classification that restrict clinical implementation. Although the current methods have proven to be very accurate, a critique of our analysis indicates that the methods are not always methodologically rigorous, transparent, and accessible to implement in the actual world. The architectural innovation, strict validation, and full explainability combine these limitations in a systematic way, which is discussed in this work.

One of the key weaknesses in the current body of literature on the classification of brain tumors is the lack of formal statistical testing of hypothesis to prove model superiority. The majority of the studies indicate the point estimates of accuracy, with no assurance that improvements are statistically significant or just the products of chance changes [21–26, 28, 30, 31, 35, 36]. This gap is also evident in the most recent literatures published in 2025 [27, 32, 33] that report competitive accuracies, yet none employ formal hypothesis testing. To fill this very important gap, CAE-BrainNet provides a solution to this by applying both the McNemar test of model comparison and Cochran Q test of ensemble comparison. We find that the 99.39% accuracy of the CAE-BrainNet is numerically and statistically significant as compared to individual base models. Moreover, this statistical framework is also applied to the zero-shot BRISC2025 evaluation. The results confirm that CAE-BrainNet’s superiority is not confined to a single dataset but reflects genuinely transferable representations, as reported in Table 12. Such a strict validation system will guarantee that the reported increase in performance is not due to chance happenings because the experiment found some favorable data partitions.

Black-box characteristic of DL models is a considerable obstacle to the clinical implementation because healthcare providers must obtain explainable evidence to be able to trust and confirm the automated diagnoses. Although some studies have included methods of visualization, like Grad-CAM or t-SNE [20, 37], this is frequently a partial implementation or only on a subset of examples. Afnaan et al. [32] is the nearest predecessor work in the integration of Grad-CAM and LIME with a CNN classifier, but their explanations are implemented on a single model and do not address the inherent issue of conflicting spatial activation when using architecturally diverse ensemble architectures. CAE-BrainNet shows detailed Grad-CAM visualizations on all three base models on all predictions, allowing clinicians to check visually that the model focuses on medically-important tumor areas as opposed to artifacts. Furthermore, the proposed AWLCF method is an extension of this that gates ensemble LIME importance scores with cross-model consistency to yield a single, anatomically localized saliency map that indicates where the ensemble is jointly agreeing the pathology is located. This is a clinically actionable result that is not available with Grad-CAM alone or low level LIME with multi-backbone ensembles. Hence, This is a fully explainable framework that makes the model a transparent diagnostic support that can sensibly be incorporated into clinical practice.

Computational efficiency is critical to practice, but most high-performing models have a prohibitive training time (often more than 3000 s per epoch [26]) or do not even report inference costs [20–25, 28–31, 34, 36, 37]. The accuracy of CAE-BrainNet is competitive with both the training (1–2 min per epoch) and the overall inference pipeline (including 4 models predictions and Grad-CAM generation of 3 base models) taking 16.58 s. In addition to computational efficiency, the crisis of reproducibility is also resolved by publishing the entire model on HuggingFace, which is available free of charge to researchers and clinicians everywhere. Such open deployment has a stark contrast with the common framework of not disseminating trained models, thus being in a position to provide independent validation and quicker translational research.

Numerous studies are virtually perfect in case of simplified binary or three-class tasks [24, 31, 34, 35], which creates doubts of extrapolation to complex diagnostic contexts. CAE-BrainNet was specifically trained and tested to the four-class problem (glioma, meningioma, pituitary, no-tumor) that is more realistic compared with the real clinical decision-making. The generalization to external cohorts by means of U-Net adapted architecture is also reported by Ilani et al. [33] but without statistical evidence that the increase in the accuracy of transfer is significant. Conversely, CAE-BrainNet zero-shot transfer to BRISC2025 is complemented by McNemar’s test and Cohen’s g at every point of comparison, which offers statistically validated cross-dataset generalization outcome in the field of research. Through the ability to show strong performance on this holistic assignment, This frameowrk givea an indication of true diagnostic ability rather than artificially simplified benchmark performance.

The advantageous results of CAE-BrainNet (99.39%) may be explained by the complementary influence of base models orchestrated by the process of attention class-adaptive. Individual base models have different performance profiles concerning the tumor classes as explained in Sect. 5.2. EfficientNetV2-M is less sensitive to infiltrative glioma boundaries whereas DenseNet201 exhibits poor performance on meningioma textures. The class-adaptive attention mechanism helps to overcome these two complementing weaknesses through dynamically weighting greater importance values to the model best convinced in each tumor class. As an example, in the classification of gliomas, the ensemble puts more weight on DenseNet201, which in fact uses the comparative power to this classification. This adaptive weighting scheme represents a paradigm of a Mixture of Experts which is much more effective at capturing the heterogeneous nature of brain tumors, as compared to the homogeneous ensemble averaging.

One important aspect of clinical implementation is the performance of the model over the category of the “No Tumor” where false positive outcomes may lead to unwarranted patient anxiety and needless invasive follow-up. On this class, CAE-BrainNet has almost perfect specificity (99.89%), which shows outstanding discrimination between pathological and normal tissue. The essential high specificity is needed to make the clinical acceptance possible since the risk of exposing healthy patients to unwarranted diagnostic tests or psychological trauma caused is minimized. The high sensitivity of the model identifying the normal cases with high confidence rate and the large sensitivity in detection of tumors places the model as a valuable screening tool that can be used to supplement the radiological processes without giving too much false alarms.

Limitations and future work

Despite these contributions have been made, there are certain directions of research that are still open. Although the present work focuses on four tumor classes, the next clinically useful step would be to expand the framework to include rarer subtypes, including ependymoma or oligodendroglioma, as well as more finely-grained molecular classification tasks, such as IDH mutation status. Architecturally, the ensemble pipeline requires 343.4,ms/image because of three complete CNN forward passes. Training a much lighter version through knowledge distillation or systematic pruning would make it more applicable to resource-limited clinical environments. Additionally, in as much as the AWLCF approach is progressive on the interpretability front, future research needs to seek regulatory-grade explanation instruments that satisfy the new AI benchmarks. Lastly, future multi-center validation and incorporation of multimodal information that includes MRI and genomic profiles will be essential to streamline the model to meet the current neuro-oncology practice.

Conclusion

This study introduces CAE-BrainNet, a class-adaptive attention ensemble, which systematically fills four longstanding gaps in automated brain tumor classification: narrow task scope, unquantified explainability, no statistical validation, and no deployment metrics. Using a combined EfficientNetV2-M, DenseNet201, and ConvNeXt-Base with a learned 4 × 3 attention matrix and a trainable temperature factor, the model attains 99.39% four-class accuracy. This dominance is statistically verified across all baselines through the McNemar and Cochran Q tests with large effect sizes of Cohen g. Moreover, zero-shot transfer to independent BRISC2025 dataset produces 98.10% accuracy with no retraining and is statistically superior to all models except ViT-B/16. To overcome the clinical interpretability challenge, the AWLCF method proposed provides anatomically localized and ensemble consistent saliency maps. The clinical readiness profile is complete with full runtime transparency and open deployment at the URL https://huggingface.co/spaces/Meraj-21/Brain-Tumor-Classification-Using-Grad-CAM. Future efforts will focus on prospective multi-center validation, 3D volumetric modeling, as well as expansion to molecular tumor classification.
