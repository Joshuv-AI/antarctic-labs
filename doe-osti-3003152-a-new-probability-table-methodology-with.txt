 A New Probability Table Methodology with
 Covariances on Probability Tables
 William Fritsch, Denise Neudecker, Noah Walton
 October 2025

 Abstract
 Through this work, we test a new methodology of evaluating proba-
 bility tables informed by a combination of physics models and experimen-
 tal data. This methodology uses generalized least squares to produce a
 joint mean and covariance for average cross sections and probability ta-
 bles. This methodology has been tested and validated using total Monte
 Carlo. Attempts were made to parameterize the probability table us-
 ing a formulation provided in [1]. Although the normal inverse Gaussian
 fit was successful for the total cross section probability distribution, the
 rational-function fit to the capture, inelastic, and fission cross sections was
 unsuccessful due to time constraints.

1 Introduction
Traditionally, probability tables are generated using NJOY [2] using only aver-
age cross sections are informed by experiments. However, we wish to expand
this methodology to include high-fidelity physics models that provide average
cross sections and probability tables (PTs) into the evaluation. Additionally,
we wish to produce evaluated probability tables with associated covariances.
 The process to generate evaluated average cross sections, PTs, and associ-
ated covariances is shown in the flow diagram in Fig. 1: first, the basic method-
ology will take model average cross sections from CoH3 [3], and generate proba-
bility tables from the CoH3 average cross sections, using a high-fidelity sampler
with resonance statistics. CoH3 is a Hauser-Feshbach physics model that pre-
dicts the magnitude and trend of average cross sections. Next, the average cross
sections and probability tables will be updated with cross section measurement
via generalized least squares (GLS). The output will be a set of probability
tables with associated covariance matrices.
 At the time of this report, there is no ENDF-6 [4] format for probability
tables nor covariance matrices because they are usually handled after evaluation
within the processing codes such as NJOY [2]. This work is a part of a larger
NEUP project which will use this high-fidelity probability table generator to
generate probability tables for OpenMC transport [5]. The probability table
uncertainty will be propagated to system observables in a benchmark example.

In practice, the sensitivity matrix, S, can be separated into two parts: a selection

2.1.1 Peelle’s Pertinent Puzzle and IRLS
The uncertainties provided in the field of nuclear data are typically in the form
of relative uncertainties, i.e., relative to the true quantity (average cross sections
in this case). Of course, the true quantities are unknown and GLS is used to
estimate such quantities. A naive approach where the uncertainties are set rela-
tive to the data results in biased results, commonly known as Peelle’s Pertinent
Puzzle [6]. The best approach is to iteratively update the experimental covari-
ance matrix with the best estimate of the mean until convergence is reached;
this process is called iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) [7]. The data
covariance matrices are updated as follows:

2.3.3 Monte Carlo Sampling for Mean and Covariance
To fit these new distributions with provided uncertainty, a Monte Carlo ap-
proach is taken. 20 Gaussian samples are taken from the mean and covariance
for the tabular PT parameters. For each sample, the tabular PT was fit with
the NIG and barycentric rational functions. Then, the mean and covariance for
the new parameters is calculated.

3 Preparing the Data
3.1 Model Prior Data
The prior model data was provided by Kazuki Fujio. The average cross sections
were sampled using the CoH3 model [3]. Paired with the average cross sections,
probability tables (PTs) were sampled using Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble
resonance sampling.
 The probability tables were generated by binning the sampled cross section
realizations. If the total cross section lie within a bin, its frequency is tallied
as a probability. The average total, elastic, capture, inelastic, and fission cross
sections are also recorded for the bin. In the case of the samples provided,
Kazuki Fujio used 20 bins with consistent probabilities. At the time of this
report, 500 samples were provided. The number and quality of these samples is
subject to change.

 Due to the way that the PTs were generated, the average of the cross sections
from the provided PTs did not match the provided average cross sections. The
PTs were scaled linearly to consistency with the corresponding average cross
sections. This subsequently broke the sum rule for the probability tables: the
reaction cross section in each bin of the probability table must sum to the total
in each bin. This sum rule was restored by recalculating the elastic cross section
as the total cross section minus each reaction cross section excluding elastic for
each bin. At the time of this report, Kazuki Fujio is working to resolve this
issue as well.
 The mean and covariance matrix was calculated from the samples using
equations (1) and (2). The covariance matrix for the prior model data is shown
in figure 2. The mean and covariance matrix includes all of the PT parameters,
including bin edges, probabilities, and the average cross sections within the bins.

3.2 Experimental Data
Three experimental datasets were used. Harvey 1988 (EXFOR No. 13632018)
[9] for total cross section, Mosby 2018 (EXFOR No. 14794002) [10] for capture
cross section, and Weston 1992 (EXFOR No. 13488003) [11] for fission cross
section. Uncertainty quantification had been previously performed by Denise
Neudecker and Noah Walton for all three datasets using ARIADNE [12]. The
uncertainty-quantified data was used.
 The Harvey data was averaged, but the energy bins in EXFOR were not
large enough to account for the cross section fluctuation. Therefore, a larger
bin structure was used, provided by Noah Walton. Harvey reported negligible
statistical uncertainty, manifesting as unrealistically high correlation, and in-
stability in the GLS algorithm. A regularization uncertainty was applied. This

 The Mosby data was well averaged and had no problems. The Mosby data

4 Results
Figure 4 shows the prior model and posterior model alongside the experimental
EXFOR data. The posterior results move towards the experimental data while
maintaining the shape of the prior model. The posterior correlation matrix
is shown in figure 5. Figure 6 shows the probability table as a probability
density function and as a cumulative probability function. Consistent with the
average cross sections in figure 4, the curve scaled to match the lower posterior
average cross section. The variance clearly reduces in CDF-space, but remains
consistent in PDF-space. The PDF does not reduce in variance because of the
large correlation between cross-sections. The capture and fission cross sections
at 15 keV are shown in Figure 7. Analogous to the update in the average
capture and fission cross sections in figure 4, the mean values for the capture
cross section bins in figure 7 do not move and the fission cross section bins move
to lower fission cross sections. Based on the statistical variation in the cross
section bins for capture and fission, more prior samples are suggested.

for the posterior results. The last rule is true except occasionally for the first
bin. This is because the first bin is heavily weighted towards the upper bin
boundary. Ocasionally, the first bin total cross section exceeds the upper bin
boundary by a negligible amount.

4.1.1 Total Monte Carlo (TMC) Validation
TMC was used to validate whether the linearity assumptions inherent to the
GLS algorithm were appropriate to make. GLS is limited by assumptions of
linearity and normality which certainly do not hold for the PT parameters
(see figure 8). TMC is not limited by linearity and normality. By comparing
the results of GLS to TMC, we can see if the assumption violation produces
significant error in the final results.
 TMC produces inaccurate results when all three datasets are used. This
is because the effective sample size (equation (14)) is nearly 1 when all three
datasets are used, inflating the uncertainty to infinity. This is a result of three
factors:

A.1 Testing with a Synthetic Data Generator
Using a synthetic data generator, we can see the bias of these methods. The
synthetic data generator uses synthetic 6 mm Ta-181 transmission realizations
provided by SynDat [13], replicating data by Jesse Brown [14] in the 3-keV to
4-keV range. The bin spacing also matches the real experiment: 345 bins, uni-
form in time of flight, starting at around 2.3-eV and ending at around 3.6-eV.
The synthetic data generator is rather simple: no corrections or systematic un-
certainties were involved. Background and differential efficiencies are neglected
for this toy problem.

 As seen in figure 12, weighted averaging has the expected biased estimate.
Simple and bin averaging in transmission space produces an unbiased estimator
of the average transmission. Simple averaging works for this case since transmis-
sion is a linear transformation of the raw counts which are Poisson distributed. A
linear transformation of Poisson data is still Poisson. Simple averaging produces
an unbiased estimate of Poisson distributed data. However, there is a non-linear
transformation between transmission and cross section, shown by equation (28).
This non-linear transformation makes the data no longer Poisson distributed,
resulting in the simple averaging bias seen in figure 12. Any thick-sample yield
measurement is subject to the same problems. Fission measurements are mostly
unaffected because they use thin samples.
 Simple averaging in transmission/yield space is not a cure-all fix. Non-
Poisson data still appears if the open counts are not smoothed or for fission
clean ratio measurements. A new methodology should be created to better
estimate the average cross sections for these spaces. To experimentalists, we
suggest providing bin-averaged cross sections along with fine-grid data for truly
unbiased average cross sections.
