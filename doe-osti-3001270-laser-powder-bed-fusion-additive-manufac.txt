Introduction

 Introduction
Next generation fission and fusion nuclear reactors require materials that can withstand operating
temperatures greater than 500 °C, neutron irradiation doses of up to 200 displacements per atom (dpa),
and potentially corrosive coolants such as the alkali liquid metals sodium, lithium, and NaK (Na33K
eutectic alloy). Refractory alloys, such as Nb1Zr (Nb-1wt%Zr) and Molybdenum alloy TZM (Mo-
0.5wt%Ti-0.08wt%Zr) have been traditionally considered viable candidates for advanced fission and
fusion reactor concepts. However, it is relatively difficult to generate complex geometries of interest
from these alloys using traditional manufacturing methods. In addition, there needs to be a concentrated
effort to address refractory metal challenges at elevated temperature operation. In order to generate
complex geometries of interest, modern manufacturing techniques are considered to increase the
technological readiness level (TRL), cost-effectiveness, and schedule savings. This work focused on the
continued development of laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) additive manufacturing (AM) to improve
both design flexibility, evaluate microstructure and properties, and ultimately accelerate the TRL and
qualification of these processes and alloys for components to potentially be put into service.

Niobium alloy Nb1Zr was identified through a down-selection process outlined in previous reports [1, 2]
as a candidate to develop in L-PBF AM. Historically, Nb1Zr had been explored for high temperature fast
spectrum fission reactors for both terrestrial and space applications [3-5]. Molybdenum alloy TZM has
also been considered for these reactor concepts due to exceptional high-temperature strength, creep
resistance, and stability under irradiation [4]. L-PBF AM of TZM has previously been investigated at
LANL under the Microreactor program, NASA, ORNL, and in academia. However, due to the crack
prone nature of TZM, L-PBF AM of TZM resulted in significant microcracking and additional
development is required to pursue viable maturation [2, 6]. Other AM methods have been found to be
more successful in printing TZM, and those alternatives approaches are discussed in this effort.

The efforts detailed in this report focused on continued development of Nb1Zr through L-PBF and
development of TZM via L-PBF and electron powder bed fusion (E-PBF). The objective of this work was
to further the development of these AM techniques for the chosen refractory alloys, elucidating and
addressing associated challenges through characterization of several demonstration builds. At LANL,
Nb1Zr builds were completed using an EOS M290 and M400 machines, and a refractory alloy-dedicated
L-PBF system, the Xact Metal XM200G, was installed. The XM200G primary purpose was to do the
Nb1Zr parameter development process; however, due to difficulties associated with the machine
installation and qualification process, it was decided to pivot development to the larger M400 and M290
machines. Although the supply of Nb1Zr powder was limited, it was sufficient to generate sub-scale
metallographic specimens for the purpose of parameter development. This was first accomplished on the
EOS M400 then the M290 due to machine schedule availability.

Further development of TZM has been initiated at the University of Texas El Paso (UTEP) under contract
with LANL to use both a heated build envelope L-PBF machine and E-PBF machine that have been
found in the literature to mitigate microcracking. UTEP was provided with TZM powder and build plates
to support parallel TZM parameter development across both machines. As part of the contract, UTEP
will also be conducting microstructural characterization once optimized process parameters have been
identified. The optimized process parameters for each machine will be used to generate a series of
metallographic, mechanical, and surface finish specimens for subsequent characterization and testing.

In the next section, we provide a detailed discussion of the methodology used for investigating the
feasibility of leveraging these alloys for use in advanced reactor applications.

Methodology

 Methodology
Manufacturing refractory metals via L-PBF AM can present a formidable set of challenges that stand
apart from the processing of more common metal alloys. The inherent properties of refractory metals,
whose exceptionally high melting temperatures (ranging from 2,400 °C to 3,400 °C) require higher power
lasers and careful control of process parameters to achieve and maintain these elevated temperatures
within the L-PBF system. Furthermore, the optimal processing window for refractory metals in L-PBF is
often very narrow, due to the temperature difference between melting and vaporization temperatures. This
delicate balance makes it difficult to fully melt the powder without incurring excessive evaporation and
porosity in the final parts.

Refractory metals, particularly Nb and Ta, exhibit a high degree of oxidation susceptibility at elevated
temperature, which can severely degrade their mechanical properties if not processed in a sufficiently
inert atmosphere. Maintaining a sufficiently oxygen free inert atmosphere throughout the L-PBF process
is crucial to prevent oxidation and ensure the desired material characteristics. Another critical challenge
is the buildup of significant residual stresses within refractory metal parts, a consequence of the high
thermal gradients and rapid cooling rates inherent to the L-PBF process. These stresses can lead to
warping, cracking, or delamination, necessitating the use of optimized process parameters. In addition,
the vast majority of commercial off-the-shelf L-PBF platforms have a limited build envelope heating
capability (generally a maximum of 200 °C). In the case of Mo and W alloys that are inherently fracture
prone require a build envelope heated to multiples of their ductile to brittle transition temperature (DBTT)
to mitigate microcracking during the AM process. This limitation can pose a challenge in maintaining the
necessary temperature control for Mo- and W-based AM.

Finally, refractory metal parts produced by L-PBF often require extensive post-processing, such vacuum
stress relieving (annealing) and machining to achieve the desired final geometry, mechanical properties,
and surface finish. As with all AM based methods, post-processing constitutes the majority of the time
and cost of arriving at a final component. To overcome these challenges, the entire AM life cycle, from
feedstock characterization through placing parts into service requires careful planning to determine the
optimized process flow to meet the desired application requirements.

The EOS M290 is a ubiquitous medium sized industrial L-PBF printer designed for the production of
high-quality metal parts. It features a build volume of 250x250x290 mm and can process a wide range of
metal powders. The M290 utilizes a 400 W fiber laser at 1063 nm to melt the metal powder, in a layer-by-
layer process to build up the desired 3D object, allowing for relatively fast build speed and excellent part
geometric resolution. When combined with design for AM (DfAM) principles and appropriate post-
processing highly complex components made from difficult to form alloys can be generated to part
tolerances. The machine utilizes an inert argon atmosphere to prevent oxidation during printing. The
XM200G is a smaller L-PBF system, with a build volume of 150x150x150 mm and utilizes a 400 W /
1063 nm laser to melt the metal powder. It can process a variety of materials and operates in an inert
argon environment, making it a suitable system for development parameters in small scale before
switching to the larger EOS M290 or EOS M400.

When printing refractory metals the process parameters that must be carefully controlled include the laser
power (generally towards the upper end of laser power), scan speed (generally slow to maintain melt pool
stability), hatch spacing, layer thickness (generally < 40 μm), powder particle size and distribution
(generally < 45 μm), and the use of an inert argon atmosphere, to prevent oxidation. For Ta and Nb the
preheating of the build platform to 80-200 °C is sufficient but for Mo and W a preheat of between 800-
1200 °C is necessary. Optimizing these parameters, along with post-processing steps like vacuum stress
relief, is crucial to achieving high-quality refractory metal parts using L-PBF AM technology.

Results & Discussion

 Results & Discussion
The Nb1Zr parameter development process was initiated by conducting a literature review which
identified several publicly available process parameters for another niobium alloy C103 (Nb-10wt%Hf-
1wt%Ti), which itself is a highly mature alloy used in L-PBF AM primarily for aerospace propulsion
applications. A comparison of the thermophysical property differences between Nb1Zr and C103 was
used to calculate a predicted process window that would accelerate the parameter development process.
Nb1Zr powder was procured from Heegar Materials with the following requirements: plasma
spheroidized with inert gas, particle size distribution (PSD) of 15-45 μm with d10 of 20 μm, d50 of 30
μm, d90 of 40 μm, a purity of 99.9% (metals basis), and a quantity of 30 kg. Ti6Al4V was chosen as the
build plate material since it has a well-known compatibility with Nb-alloys, it is relatively inexpensive,
and was available within the existing plate stockpile. A design of experiments (DOE) was conducted that
varies laser speed, laser power, powder layer thickness, and hatch spacing (distance between adjacent
laser scan paths). The DOE generated a series of test conditions that were used to inform the subsequent
series of test specimen builds to be conducted iteratively and informed through metallurgical
characterization. The metallographic specimen used was designed to optimize L-PBF AM process
parameter development by having a small size, including a 30° unsupported overhang surface angle that
facilitates rapid disqualification of parameters for either having too low a volumetric energy density (Ev)
that results in crumbling or too high Ev that result in distortion and burn-out. The specimen also has a 2
mm diameter at the part to plate interface that enables specimens to be removed with hand tools such as
needle nose pliers and eliminates the need for more costly and time consuming band-saw or wire-EDM
options. An example of a build layout of several metallographic specimens created using Autodesk
Netfabb is shown in Figure 1, while various process parameters and scan order was applied in EOSPrint
2.13, followed by uploading to the EOS M290. The build direction (Z) is perpendicular to the build plate
(XY plane), with specimen semi-major axis aligned to Z. The typical machine initialization process of
plate leveling, pre-heating to 80 °C, and inerting the build chamber to < 0.1 % oxygen was conducted
followed by initiating the print process.

As previously stated, staring parameters for C103 were used as a starting point and thermophysical
properties were used to establish the processing window, but an experimental test matrix had to be
conducted to validate the result for each alloy on each machine since there can be minor differences in
performance that do impact the microstructure and resulting mechanical properties. Specimens placement
and scanning order followed L-PBF AM best practices. Specimens were placed staggered in X and Y
directions with adequate spacing to prevent cascade type failure, spatter deposition, and scan order was
against the argon flow (from the bottom to top direction). Due to the limited amount of powder the
specimen array was placed adjacent to the dose chamber to prevent a “short feed” type failure, although
the laser angle of incidence would be higher when compared to plate center placement. Specimens were

After specimen printing, the build plates with the attached specimens were removed from the build
envelope, de-powdered, and specimens then removed from the build plate by mechanically. Specimens
were then cleaned and underwent metallographic preparation to include sectioning (in XZ and XY
planes), mounting, grinding, and polishing. Specimens underwent initial microstructural characterization
through low magnification optical microscopy (OM) as shown in Figure 2.

 Figure 2. Optical micrograph of Nb1Zr specimen 5 (XY plane) printed on the EOS M400.

Micrographs reveal a relatively high density with a defect density that would be expected at an early stage
of development. A combination of porosity and cracks are evident and are expected to be mitigated with
subsequent iterative parameter development. The infill parameters are the only values included in this
study and once infill is optimized, then that Ev value is used to generate ratioed values that act as starting
points for developing down skin, up skin, and contour process parameters. It must be noted that infill
parameters are largely responsible for resultant microstructure and thermophysical properties. Down
skin, up skin, and contours are primarily used to improve the as-built (AB) surface finish, mitigate burn-
out, and surface connected porosity. The AB surface finish or properties are not typically used in the final

Specimens were sectioned and observed across the XZ plane to show defect density as a function of the
build direction. As can be seen across specimens 1 through 8, there is a substantial porosity and cracking
density, which is expected this early in the development process. Specimen 6 shows spatial variability
with some regions showing very low porosity distribution, which is an unexpected result since the
parameters were not varied spatially throughout the specimen. From these results we select parameters
from specimen 6 and 7 for further development, specifically modulating P, followed by minor
modification to D. It is expected that finding an optimal infill parameter set will take on the order of 4 to
5 more DOE iterations and the data presented in this document only constitute 2 iterations. Additional
characterization that is needed to ensure elemental distribution variability is not occurring will require
energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) of both the powder and printed specimens.

After a set of near-optimized Nb1Zr process parameters were established, they were then used to generate
a subscale demonstration article that possessed the prototypic geometries of interest for an advanced
reactor. In this case, a heuristic subscale reactor core block segment was designed and printed. The block
has fuel slots with a 10 mm diameter, a 1 mm minimum web thickness, and overall block thickness of 10
mm. The printed demonstration article and geometric dimensions are shown in Figure 4.

Conclusions & Future Work

 Conclusions & Future Work
Nb1Zr initial L-PBF AM build parameters were identified through the iterative parameter development
process. Metallographic results are insufficient to characterize how L-PBF AM Nb1Zr compares to
traditional manufacturing counterparts and require more in-depth characterization.

A series of tasks have been proposed to be completed in FY26 and include:

Stress Relief (SR) Heat Treatment Optimization: L-PBF AM specimens will be heated treated at 800 °C,
900 °C, 1000 °C, and 1100 °C for 1 hour in a vacuum better than 10-5 Torr, using Ta foil wrapping to
prevent oxidation from residual oxygen. Once the SR schedule is identified, all subsequent specimens
will undergo this heat treatment prior to subsequent characterization.

Specimen Build on M290: A more thorough specimen array will be printed to include metallurgical (x2),
surface finish as a function of overhang (x6) sets, tensile (x12), and creep (x3) specimens. All specimens
will be heat treated to the SR condition except for one AB metallographic specimen.

Microstructural Characterization: OM, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), EDS, Electron Backscatter
Diffraction (EBSD), and the Archimedes method to determine density will be applied in both the AB and
SR conditions by an offsite commercial vendor.

Chemical Mechanical Process (CMP): Nb1Zr requires a coating to operate at elevated temperature due to
the active oxidation behavior. Coating methods have been developed assuming a machined surface finish
and the adherence and performance of the coating is dependent on the surface finish condition. There,
specimens will undergo CMP to characterize surface material removal (SMR) vs. areal surface finish (Sa)
by REM Surface Engineering. An optimized SMR and resultant Sa will be selected for subsequent
coating. In addition, the SMR is required to add sacrificial stock in the design phase to print an
adequately oversized component to meet geometric dimension and tolerance requirements that must be
satisfied in an applied environment.

Electropolish (EP): If resources allow, the EP SMR vs. Sa will be characterized by Sigma-2 at LANL.

Coating: R512E is a legacy silicide-based coating applied to alloys. Surface finish specimens in the AB,
CMP, and EP conditions will undergo R512E coating by commercial vendor Hitemco.

Sa vs. Overhang Angle: The surface finish will be characterized for AB, CMP, EP, AB + coated, CMP +
coated, EP + coated conditions by Sigma-2. The results will provide a thorough understanding of how the
surface finish as a function of angle and coating will impact the post-processing approach and component
level performance in a service environment.

Tensile Testing: Subscale tensile specimens will be tested at 20 °C, 800 °C, 1000 °C, 1200 °C in vacuum
of 10-4 Torr by the University of Maryland (UMD) using a Gleeble load frame. 12 specimens will be
tested: 3 tensile specimens per test condition x 4 test conditions = 12 tensile specimens. Digital image
correlation (DIC) will be used to measure strain during the test.

Micro-Scale Creep Testing: 800 °C, 1000 °C, 1200 °C in high vacuum will be conducted at LANL if
resources allow. The subscale and accelerated test approach allows for data to be collected that can
inform a model that has been shown to accurately predict creep behavior in other alloys.

The proposed work can be accomplished by completing one or two additional specimen builds as shown
in Figure 4.

To address the potential need for TZM, UTEP was funded at the end of FY25 to conduct parameter
