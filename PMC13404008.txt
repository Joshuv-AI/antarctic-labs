Microlaryngoscopic Management of a Congenital Laryngeal Web in a Young Adult Female
Authors: Samantha Spence, Victoria Wittgen, Blake Sparkman, Austin T Armstrong, John F Eisenbeis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCongenital laryngeal webs (CLW) are rare anomalies that result from failed recanalization of the larynx, resulting in abnormal tissue that can cause dysphonia, hoarseness, or stridor. Although the vast majority of cases are diagnosed and managed operatively before early childhood, we report a rare case of an adult laryngeal web in a 20‐year‐old female who presented with a lifelong whisper‐like voice and difficulty breathing with exertion. Visualization with flexible laryngoscopy revealed a high‐grade CLW extending from the anterior commissure to the vocal process of the arytenoids bilaterally, resulting in limited vocal cord abduction. Despite standard recommendations for open airway surgery, the patient was selected to undergo microlaryngoscopic cold steel division of the glottic web with topical mitomycin C application to reduce scar formation and potential further voice and breathing impairment. Following a successful operation and postoperative speech therapy, the patient had markedly improved phonation and breathing. The present work is the first documented case of a high‐grade adult CLW managed endoscopically with cold steel lysis and mitomycin C application, demonstrating these patients may achieve significant functional improvement using this approach while avoiding the morbidity of open approaches.Keywords: congenital laryngeal web, microlaryngoscopy, mitomycin C, voice

Introduction

Congenital Laryngeal Webs (CLW) represent a rare aberration of embryological development in which the larynx fails to undergo normal recanalization (restoration of a patent airway or lumen after obstruction or closure). The result is a persistent web of tissue spanning between structures in the larynx, most commonly the true vocal folds (TVF) [1]. Symptoms typically present within the first months of life, warranting evaluation and intervention during infancy or early childhood. Rarely, CLW is discovered in adults and only a handful of cases are reported in the literature. There are no current standard treatment guidelines recommending a surgical approach for CLW. The degree of glottic involvement, characterized by the Cohen classification [2], and the severity of airway obstruction typically guides management. The primary goals of surgery are to optimize airway patency and improve voice [3]. We present a unique case of a severe CLW in a young adult female successfully managed with microlaryngoscopic cold steel lysis and mitomycin C application.

Case History and Examination

A 20‐year‐old female from St. Vincent and the Grenadines presented through World Pediatrics (www.worldpediatrics.org) for evaluation of lifelong history of dysphonia. At birth, she was reported to have mild stridor and no cry. Growing up, her voice had a severely asthenic, whisper‐like quality. She experienced shortness of breath and stridor with exertion. Flexible laryngoscopy revealed a membranous web extending from the anterior commissure (the junction where the true vocal folds meet at the front of the larynx) to the vocal process of the arytenoids (paired cartilages in the posterior larynx that control vocal fold movement and tension) bilaterally, tethering the vocal cords, limiting their abduction, and obstructing the anterior 70% of the airway (Cohen type III CLW) (Figure 1).

Diagnosis and Treatment

The surgical plan was to proceed with microlaryngoscopic intervention with the goal to create two distinct vocal folds, each with a vibratory surface.

Intraoperative direct microlaryngoscopy redemonstrated the CLW (Figure 2) and revealed the web extended into the subglottis (the airway region immediately below the vocal folds extending to the lower border of the cricoid cartilage) approximately 3–4 mm. The web was initially compressed in the midline using upward laryngeal alligator forceps and incised using upward biting laryngeal scissors, separating the two vocal folds. The web was excised anteriorly up to 2 mm short of the commissure and inferiorly to the level of the cricoid cartilage (Figure 3). Topical mitomycin C (0.04 mg/mL) was applied to the exposed tissue on a small cottonoid pledget for 4 min (Figure 4) to prevent scar formation.

Conclusion and Results

The patient was discharged on an oral steroid taper, voice rest, and instructed to sniff repeatedly to abduct the TVFs to prevent adhesions. Six days postoperative, the patient reported significant improvement in breathing, especially during sleep. Her voice remained rough and asthenic. Flexible laryngoscopy revealed intact bilateral TVF mobility without webbing and subglottic fibrinous exudate consistent with mitomycin C application (Figure 5A). Two weeks postoperative examination revealed increased exudate and normal vocal fold movement (Figure 5B). The patient's voice was very weak, prompting the initiation of speech therapy (ST) with voicing exercises. Three weeks postsurgery, she could produce short sentences but experienced vocal fatigue with louder speech. Flexible laryngoscopy at this time showed mild TVF mobility reduction and early subglottic granulation tissue (vascular connective tissue that forms during wound healing which can contribute to scarring or obstruction when excessive) (Figure 5C). In‐office percutaneous Kenalog (0.5 cc triamcinolone 40 mg/mL) injection was performed to the anterior vocal folds and subglottis. One week later, vocal projection and speech duration improved markedly, with improved TVF mobility and two distinct vibratory surfaces on exam (Figure 5D). Six weeks postsurgery, the subglottic airway was well‐healed and bilateral TVFs demonstrated synchronized vibration down the full length of the membranous cord (Figure 5E,F). She returned to St. Vincent and the Grenadines with recommended continued practice of vocal exercises. Three months postoperative, she reported a normal voice and exercise tolerance.

Discussion

Congenital laryngeal webs rarely remain undiagnosed into adulthood. There is limited data regarding optimal adult management. Surgical intervention is generally warranted for symptomatic webs causing airway obstruction or dysphonia. Low‐grade webs (Cohen I‐II) not extending into the subglottis or involving the posterior commissure can typically be managed endoscopically using either a CO2 laser or cold instrument excision [1]. In contrast, endoscopic treatment alone of grade III‐IV webs is considered less effective as the associated cartilaginous subglottic stenosis often requires open airway surgery [4]. The present case is novel in that our patient was successfully managed with a minimally invasive approach, despite her high‐grade web with subglottic extension.

Treatment of laryngeal obstruction presents a major surgical challenge due to high rates of postoperative scarring and restenosis [5], especially when involving the vocal folds [3]. Traditionally, postoperative stenting with endolaryngeal silastic keels (V‐shaped silicone stents temporarily placed between vocal folds) has been used to prevent mucosal adhesion during healing [1, 4]. However, antimitotic agents such as mitomycin C have been explored as an alternative to physical stenting through modulation of wound healing [1, 4]. Mitomycin C is an antibiotic with both antineoplastic and antiproliferative properties and works by inhibiting fibroblast proliferation and protein synthesis. This allows for the suppression of scar formation without damaging mucosal and epithelial growth [3, 5]. Though mitomycin C has previously shown success in airway stenosis management [5], there are very few accounts of its use following CLW lysis which are largely limited to mild webs without subglottic involvement [3]. Our case represents the first documented use of mitomycin C following lysis of a severe (Cohen grade III‐IV) web with subglottic extension.

Furthermore, this case demonstrates the importance of ST in postoperative CLW patients. Voice requires coordination between an exhalation effort and TVF opposition to create a sound wave. For our patient, who never had sustained normal speech, learning this coordinated behavior required the intervention of conventional voice therapy techniques and practice by the patient. The outcome for this patient was ideal, creating a loud, comprehensible, sustainable voice.

This case also confirms the safety and feasibility of microlaryngoscopic treatment of CLW in adults. Failure to diagnose and treat a laryngeal web early in life, or inability to access advanced surgical care, has significant functional and social impacts on patients. Thus, early recognition and treatment of these anomalies with surgical intervention and ST is essential to optimizing patient quality of life.

Conclusion

This report represents a rare case of a high‐grade CLW managed in an adult patient and the only documented case of the successful use of endoscopic lysis with mitomycin C application in an adult CLW patient. This case also discusses the role of ST in establishing normal voice in the CLW patient. In rare diagnoses such as severe CLW, treatment flexibility and adaptability are paramount in the absence of set clinical guidelines.
