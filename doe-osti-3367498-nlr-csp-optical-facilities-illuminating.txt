Executive Summary
The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) is actively engaged in a comprehensive project
aimed at strengthening its Concentrating Solar Power (CSP) optical facilities through strategic
repair, maintenance, and upgrades to support ongoing and future research needs, specifically in
the development and characterization of low-cost, high-performance reflective and absorber
materials and components essential for converting concentrated sunlight into energy. The project’s
core objectives are to enhance stakeholder outreach, ensure mission-critical equipment is well-
maintained and efficiently utilized, and advance laboratory capabilities through new acquisitions
or developments, including Virginia Tech’s solar absorber coatings.
A key aspect of this project is extensive stakeholder engagement, primarily through an Advisory
Board comprising 8 members from Europe and the US, which provides crucial guidance, identifies
research gaps, and promotes collaborations. The Board consistently affirms the unique value of
NLR’s CSP Optical Facilities in driving CSP cost reduction and performance improvement,
recommending an expanded scope beyond heliostats to include other mirror types like troughs and
linear Fresnel, as well as broader research areas such as thermochemical solar fuel and
semiconductor chip fabrication. Outreach activities have included submitting an abstract on “CSP
Optical Facilities at NLR” for SolarPACES 2022, attending the AUVSI XPONENTIAL
conference to explore Uncrewed Aerial Systems (UAS) solutions, and the Non-Intrusive Optical
Tools (NIO) team securing a $25,000 commercialization award from the U.S. Department of
Energy’s Office of Technology Transitions (OTT). NLR researchers have also conducted visits to
CSP plants like Heliogen and Ivanpah to understand optical issues and industry needs, and fostered
collaborations with European partners such as AGC, CIEMAT, PROMES, and DLR on
certification processes and standardization. The Advisory Board emphasized the importance of
publications and customer reports as success factors and suggested diversifying funding sources,
streamlining facility access with credit card payments, and advocating for additional DOE funding
to enhance NIO, Distant Observer (DO) work, and High Flux Solar Furnace (HFSF) upgrades.
In terms of facility maintenance and utilization, NLR has made continuous progress. All Ci5000
weatherometers are operating to manufacturer specifications, with Ci5000-1 and -2 modified for
large samples, and Ci5000-4 and -5 certified to ISO 17025 and ISO 9001. The Tenney
environmental chamber experienced prolonged cooling and humidity sensor issues, which were
eventually addressed by replacing the sensor and implementing operational temperature limits.
The Ultra-Accelerated Weathering System (UAWS) suffered hail damage to its specialized
mirrors, limiting its full UV dosage capability; significant refurbishment of its tubing, chillers,
thermocouples, and LabView data acquisition system was completed, and a pyrheliometer and
pyranometer were installed and aligned to ensure accurate solar exposure measurements. DLR has
offered to explore fabricating replacement mirrors for the UAWS. The older Lambda 1050
spectrophotometer faced persistent issues due to a faulty preamplifier, deteriorated optics, and a
damaged detector; however, a new PerkinElmer Lambda 1050+ was delivered and installed by
October 2023, with training provided to key users, marking a significant upgrade in material
characterization capabilities for both absorbers and reflectors. The Total Absolute Measurement
System (TAMS), an accessory for the Lambda 1050+, was delivered in July 2024 to provide highly
accurate specular reflectance measurements as a function of angles, with advanced training
planned for FY25 Q1. The Surface Optics SOC-410 reflectometer and ET-100 emissometer have

experienced intermittent startup failures, prompting requests for evaluation and repair. Due to a
suspected flow switch failure The FTIR system was sent to the manufacturer for repair.
NLR has transitioned its equipment utilization tracking system from MOSS to XUSS, then to
WPC, and finally to “Lab Reservations”, though challenges in consistent data capture during these
transitions have been noted. The HFSF continues to be highly utilized for durability testing (e.g.,
CSP industry, Defense, Academia, etc.) and thermochemical research (e.g., Niigata University).
A second testing chamber for HFSF has been designed and partially installed to improve
operational efficiency by allowing one chamber to be on-sun while the other undergoes its
dark/humid cycle. The original water calorimeter developed a leak, necessitating a redesign and
fabrication of a new, more manufacturable unit. A private company has been engaged for heliostat
program upgrades and troubleshooting, and a forecasting tool is being developed to predict HFSF
throughput with 87% accuracy. The Solar Industrial Mesa Test Area (SIMTA) has been
repurposed for UAS maintenance and research, supporting HelioCon and other projects, and a new
heavy-lift, Blue-sUAS certified drone (IF1200A by Inspired Flight) was acquired to enhance NIO
capabilities. The UAS equipment was planned to move to the Outdoor Technology in-situ
Validation (OTiVA) facility at Flatirons.
Significant advancements have been made in developing and integrating new capabilities. The
deployment of NIO and Beam Characterization System (BCS) capabilities at the OTiVA facility
on the Flatirons campus is underway, involving the installation of a shipping-container mounted
target after earlier designs proved structurally or financially infeasible. A Heliuss prototype
heliostat was procured for testing, and new funding was secured to expand NIO for third-party
heliostat evaluation services, including capabilities for opto-mechanical error assessment and sun
shape/atmospheric attenuation measurements. Initial tests combining NIO drone videos and BCS
images have been successfully conducted. NLR’s indoor optical laboratory has relocated to a new
space at Flatirons campus, now called the Helio-OPAL (Heliostat Opto-mechanical Performance
Assessment Laboratory), which accommodates new deflectometry (ReTNA) and photogrammetry
capabilities. NLR continues to develop ReTNA software, offering advantages over SOFAST for
mirror facet measurements. Photogrammetry software licenses were renewed, providing critical
camera calibration and 3D modeling tools that have been successfully demonstrated on the HFSF
heliostat and a mini-heliostat. A Turning Mirror capability was developed for the HFSF, allowing
for bottom-up concentrated solar exposure that more accurately mimics solar field conditions for
particle receiver and component testing; this actively cooled system provides remotely adjustable
reflection angles and has been designed, fabricated, and validated. In collaboration with Virginia
Tech, NLR conducted short-term cyclic tests (50 cycles) on flat Inconel samples coated with solar
selective absorbers at 700°C and 750°C. Long-term cyclic testing (initially planned for 300 cycles,
completing 164 cycles due to inclement weather) for both flat and cylindrical samples at 750°C
was initiated and has been completed (June 30th, 2025), also showing excellent stability. Modeling
efforts focused on extending the Material-Kinetics-Optics (MKO) model to describe spectral
changes and account for rough and fractal surfaces, using Kramers-Kronig consistent complex
indexes of refraction as functions of wavelength. The corresponding results will be published
separately.
NLR’s Advanced Optical Materials Labs successfully achieved ISO 9001:2015 certification in
June 2023, demonstrating a commitment to quality management and continuous improvement.
Additionally, Ci5000-4 and -5 weatherometers received ISO 17025 certification from Atlas. The
Advisory Board underscored the importance of pursuing accreditation for CSP mirror facet

certification and calibration, potentially through collaborative round-robin tests with European
partners to establish international standards. The development of a standard for absorber
measurements was identified as a critical project, with CIEMAT expressing strong interest in
collaboration, and NLR encouraged to lead a specific subtask within SolarPACES Task 3.
Discussions with PSA regarding uncertainty analysis for spectrophotometers and HFSF were also
planned.
Despite significant progress, several challenges remain, including the UAWS’s hail damage
requiring full mirror replacement and refurbishment. HFSF scheduling continues to be challenging
due to high demand, necessitating efforts to optimize utilization and the development of a new
forecasting tool. The development of the BCS system experienced delays due to structural
infeasibility and budget constraints, leading to multiple design iterations and difficulties in
acquiring a suitable prototype heliostat, and a newly acquired drone sustained minor damage
requiring vendor repair. The Virginia Tech collaboration faced substantial delays due to a
protracted Non-Disclosure Agreement (NDA) and inclement weather, necessitating a No Cost
Time Extension (NCTE) request to DOE. Furthermore, the transition of lab utilization tracking
systems from MOSS to XUSS, WPC, and then “Lab Reservations” encountered issues in
consistently capturing all data. The Advisory Board consistently recommended diversifying
funding sources (private sector, other DOE programs), investigating credit card payment options
for easier facility access, and advocating for additional DOE funding to support NIO and DO work,
HFSF upgrades, and the overall expansion of CSP Optical Facilities. Looking ahead, NLR plans
to continue maintenance and calibration of mission-critical equipment, review new capability and
upgrade requests, update its website and prepare for workshops, develop a FY25-27 Lab Call Core
proposal to extend the CSP Optical Facilities Core project, refine absorber degradation modeling,
and explore new collaborations with entities such as the Defense sector, Automotive and CSP
industry. Meetings with the Advisory Board will continue to discuss progress and future action
items.

1 Background and Objectives
The global energy sector is undergoing a major transition as the demand for reliable energy
solutions intensifies. Concentrating Solar Thermal Power (CSP) technologies are emerging as
critical players in this transformation, offering unique advantages in energy generation, storage,
and grid integration. These technologies complement other reliable energy systems, addressing
intermittency challenges and providing a pathway toward decarbonizing the energy landscape.
This introduction delves into the background of CSP technologies and their significance, outlines
the high-level objectives of CSP development in alignment with both the U.S. Department of
Energy (DOE) and the National Laboratory of the Rockies’s (NLR) missions.

1.1 Background and Importance of CSP Technologies

 • What is Concentrating Solar Thermal Power (CSP)?
CSP is an energy technology designed to harness the sun’s energy to produce electricity or deliver
heat (“Concentrating Solar-Thermal Power | Department of Energy,” n.d.). Unlike photovoltaic
(PV) systems, which convert sunlight directly into electricity, CSP uses mirrors or lenses to
concentrate sunlight onto a small area, generating high-temperature heat. This thermal energy can
be used for direct process heat or used to produce steam that drives a turbine to generate electricity,
making CSP a versatile and scalable, reliable and resilient energy solution. CSP differs from other
energy technologies in its capacity to store thermal energy for later use, allowing the delayed
generation of electricity or use of heat even after the sun sets. This ability to provide dispatchable
heat and power makes CSP a critical component for future power grids that require firm energy
sources to complement other power generation systems.
 • Importance of CSP in the Modern Energy Landscape
CSP addresses several pressing challenges faced by energy systems globally. As countries commit
to more reliable energy in their power grids, CSP technologies are positioned to help overcome
key barriers to progress:
 o Grid Flexibility with Thermal Energy Storage: CSP systems are typically paired
with thermal energy storage (TES), such as molten salt storage, which allows excess solar energy
to be retained and dispatched during periods of peak demand. This capability enhances the
reliability of energy systems, mitigating the natural intermittency of solar energy and reducing
dependence on fossil fuel-based backup systems.
 o Scalability and Dispatchability: Unlike solar PV, which offers energy production
during sunlight hours only, CSP systems can provide scalable energy generation, dispatching
power on-demand to meet grid requirements. This makes CSP particularly valuable for stabilizing
the grid in regions with high reliable energy penetration.
 o Reduction in Carbon Emissions: By replacing conventional power plants that rely
on coal or natural gas, CSP systems have the potential to significantly lower greenhouse gas
emissions. Each megawatt-hour (MWh) of CSP-generated electricity displaces approximately one
metric ton of CO2 emissions.
 o Economic Development and Job Creation: CSP infrastructure investments create
opportunities for local industries, particularly in manufacturing, construction, and maintenance.

 1

Additionally, utility-scale installations generate long-term employment in regions where CSP is
developed.
 • CSP Technology Variants
CSP technologies are categorized based on the method of concentrating sunlight. The four main
types include (Mehos et al., 2020):
 o Parabolic Trough Systems: Utilizes curved, parabolic mirrors that focus sunlight
onto a linear receiver tube, where heat transfer fluid absorbs concentrated solar energy.
 o Power Tower Systems: Uses an array of heliostats (sun-tracking mirrors) to reflect
sunlight onto a central receiver located atop a tower. This technology supports higher operating
temperatures, improving efficiency.
 o Dish/Engine Systems: Employs a parabolic dish that concentrates sunlight onto a
receiver at its focal point; this receiver heats a working fluid to drive a Stirling engine.
 o Linear Fresnel Reflectors: Utilizes flat or slightly curved mirrors that concentrate
sunlight onto elevated receivers, offering simpler designs at reduced costs.
 • The Broader Impact of CSP Technologies
CSP aligns with the broader goals of reliable energy deployment by offering solutions that go
beyond mere electricity generation:
 o Integration with Industrial Applications: CSP systems, especially power towers,
can be adapted for direct use in industrial processes such as water desalination, hydrogen
production, and enhanced oil recovery, etc.
 o Energy Independence: By harnessing abundant solar resources in regions like the
Southwestern United States, Australia, and North Africa, CSP empowers countries to produce
energy domestically, reducing reliance on imported fossil fuels.
 o Environmental Resilience: With its ability to produce, dispatchable energy during
extreme weather events, CSP contributes to energy system resilience against seasonal variation
impacts.

As global energy systems continue their transformation, the growth of CSP technologies presents
an opportunity to shape a decarbonized, equitable energy future. Their unique attributes position
CSP as a cornerstone technology for mitigating the climate crisis and delivering long-term, reliable
energy solutions.

1.3 Project Objectives
NLR’s premier solar laboratories are committed to a critical mission: supporting industry,
academia, and national laboratories in the development and characterization of low-cost, high-
performance reflective and absorber materials and their components. This is a key element in
advancing the conversion of concentrated sunlight into energy. To successfully accomplish this
mission, it is imperative that the core laboratory equipment is unique, maintained in excellent
working order, and represents the newest, state-of-the-art technology. The project’s core focus is
on the repair and maintenance of existing equipment, alongside the advancement of research

 3

techniques through upgraded equipment. To align with the overarching mission, the project breaks
down its goals into specific objectives that ensure measurable progress:
 • First, the project aims to increase NLR’s outreach to actively solicit stakeholder feedback,
from both internal and external sources. This feedback is crucial for identifying research
requirements and highlighting NLR’s laboratory capabilities, thereby ensuring that the research
conducted remains state-of-the-art.
 • Second, it includes the completion of maintenance for mission-critical equipment and the
continued use of the MOSS (now Lab Reservations) database for tracking equipment repair and
utilization rates to uphold operational efficiency.
 • Third, the project mandates the purchase or development of unique lab equipment
identified and recommended by a newly formed Advisory Group, internal or external researchers,
and emerging research requirements. This also entails providing the DOE with an annual
assessment of equipment requirements for project milestones, as well as identifying major
upgrades or equipment for purchase, subject to DOE concurrence, including the development of
unique test equipment to enhance lab capabilities.
 • Furthermore, for fiscal years FY25Q1 and FY25Q2, the project includes the fabrication of
solar absorbers from Virginia Tech (VT), alongside their characterization, testing at the HFSF, and
associated modeling activities to improve absorber designs.
NLR’s role in facilitating these advancements has been pivotal. From field-testing new heliostat
designs to refining weather-durability tests for solar mirrors, NLR provides the research
infrastructure, technical expertise, and stakeholder collaboration opportunities necessary to bridge
innovation with deployment.

1.4 Project Execution
This section details the execution of the three primary tasks central to this project: stakeholder
engagement, facility maintenance and utilization, and the development of key new and upgraded
capabilities. The work described herein aims to strengthen NLR’s CSP optical facilities through
strategic feedback, diligent upkeep, and technological advancement, ensuring they remain state-
of-the-art resources for the CSP community.
Task 1: Stakeholder Engagement
A core objective of this project is to increase outreach and incorporate feedback from internal and
external stakeholders to guide research priorities and highlight laboratory capabilities. To this end,
a primary activity was the formation of an NLR Facilities Advisory Board, comprising eight
members from industry, academia, and research institutions in the US and Europe. The board’s
objectives are to:
 • Identify gaps in research tools and critical equipment.
 • Provide guidance on the effective use of NLR’s facilities to maintain relevance.
 • Identify potential stakeholders and facility users.
 • Promote collaboration through multi-organization projects.
Regular meetings were held to discuss a strategic vision for the facilities, leading to the creation
of key action items. The board provided valuable feedback on several fronts. They emphasized the
need to expand access to facilities for smaller projects, which has become difficult without a well-
funded DOE project or a CRADA. They also highlighted critical industry issues such as soiling,

 4

hail damage, and the need for advanced metrology development. Based on this feedback, the board
recommended prioritizing upgrades to the Ultra-Accelerated Weathering System (UAWS) and the
Lambda 1050 Spectrophotometer.
In response to this strategic guidance, NLR undertook several outreach initiatives. Researchers
visited European facilities and US-based CSP plants like Ivanpah and Heliogen to learn about
industry challenges and promote NLR’s capabilities. These visits successfully identified new areas
for collaboration, with agreements made to test industry samples at NLR. The team also promoted
the facilities at major conferences such as SolarPACES and SPIE. A significant achievement under
this task was the successful participation of the Non-Intrusive Optical (NIO) team in the Energy I-
Corps program, which involved over 75 stakeholder interviews and resulted in a $25,000
commercialization award. This direct stakeholder engagement also led to new funding to build up
a third-party evaluation capability for heliostat prototypes at the Flatirons campus.
Task 2: Maintenance and Utilization
This task focuses on the completion of maintenance for mission-critical equipment and tracking
equipment utilization to ensure the labs operate efficiently and reliably. Routine maintenance and
calibration schedules were established for all critical equipment in the FTLB 158-02 and FTLB
158-03 labs, with activities logged in a central database. Equipment usage was tracked through a
series of reporting systems, starting with MOSS, transitioning to XUSS, and later to “Lab
Reservations”. While this transition caused some temporary issues with data capture, the system
provides valuable reports on tool availability and utilization rates.
A major achievement under this task was receiving ISO 9001:2015 certification for the Advanced
Optical Materials Labs. This certification enhances customer satisfaction by providing certified
measurements and formally recognizes the lab’s commitment to quality management.
Additionally, two of the Ci5000 weatherometers were certified by Atlas to the ISO 17025 standard.
The facilities and their equipment are actively used and adapted to meet diverse research needs.
For instance, the Solar Industrial Mesa Test Area (SIMTA) has been repurposed for a solar thermal
energy storage (LIMOSA) project following the decommissioning of a large solar tracker. The
back lab space at SIMTA also supports Unmanned Aerial System (UAS) maintenance and
research. In another example of flexible utilization, the Ci5000 weatherometers were modified
with custom-built doors to accommodate large 50cm x 50cm samples for the NLR Windows group.
However, the maintenance of aging equipment presented significant challenges. The 15-year-old
Lambda 1050 spectrophotometer suffered from persistent intermittent failures, deteriorated optics,
and a damaged detector that service engineers could not fully resolve, affecting data quality.
Similarly, the SOC-410 reflectometer and ET-100 emissometer, also over 15 years old,
experienced start-up failures, raising concerns about their longevity. The Tenney environmental
chamber also required repairs to its cooling system and a replacement humidity sensor after it was
damaged by operating above its rated temperature.
Task 3: Key New/Upgraded Capabilities
This task is dedicated to purchasing, upgrading, or fabricating unique lab equipment each year to
improve existing capabilities, based on recommendations from stakeholders and research needs.
A central focus has been the development of advanced optical metrology at the Flatirons campus,
including the deployment of NIO and BCS capabilities. This involved acquiring a new heavy-lift
drone (Inspired Flight IF1200A) for NIO development and navigating significant structural and

 5

budgetary challenges to design and install a functional BCS target. After abandoning initial plans
for a tower-mounted or ground-actuated target due to high costs, a practical shipping-container
mounted target was successfully built and installed. In parallel, NLR moved its indoor optical lab
to a new space named the Heliostat Opto-mechanical Performance Assessment Laboratory (Helio-
OPAL), which will utilize a new, more flexible deflectometry system (ReTNA) and advanced
photogrammetry capabilities.
Significant upgrades were also made to other key facilities. At the HFSF, a turning mirror was
modified to reflect the concentrated solar beam upward, a new capability that mimics solar field
exposure angles for receivers. The HFSF has been exceptionally busy with a full schedule of TSA-
funded projects, so to improve productivity for long-duration tests, a second specialized chamber
was designed and installed for the BrightSource material durability project, allowing one chamber
to be on-sun while the other is in its dark/humid cycle. The Ultra-Accelerated Weathering System
(UAWS), which was damaged in a hailstorm, was also refurbished with a redesigned data
acquisition program and the installation of a new pyrheliometer and pyranometer for more accurate
solar exposure measurements.
Finally, this task encompassed a major collaborative project with Virginia Tech (VT) to test and
model novel absorber coatings. NLR conducted extensive short-term (50 cycles) and long-term
(164 cycles) thermal and humidity cyclic testing on VT’s coatings at the HFSF. The results were
highly successful, demonstrating the coatings’ excellent endurance at temperatures up to 750°C
with minimal changes in efficiency. In support of this work, NLR’s modeling team developed new
capabilities to extend its material-kinetics-optics (MKO) model, creating a new optical fitting
model to handle Kramers-Kronig consistent spectra and a simplified model to account for light
scattering on the coatings’ fractal surfaces.

2 Stakeholder Engagement and Feedback
This chapter details the crucial role of stakeholder engagement in guiding the development and
enhancement of NLR’s CSP Optical Facilities. Through active outreach and an established
advisory board, NLR seeks to align its capabilities with the evolving needs of industry, academia,
and national laboratories, ensuring its research remains at the forefront of state-of-the-art

2.1 Overview of Advisory Board
Stakeholders are considered paramount for providing guidance and support as the CSP Optical
Materials Lab aims to reach new heights. The objective is to establish an advisory board
comprising representatives from all groups with a key stake in the CSP Optical Materials Lab
program. This ensures that NLR’s program effectively addresses the needs of the broader CSP
community and aligns with the Department of Energy’s (DOE) mission and objectives. The
Advisory Board consists of eight members, with three from Europe and five from the United
States. The Board’s primary objectives are multifaceted:
 • To assist the NLR CSP team in identifying research tool gaps and critical equipment
 requirements.
 • To offer guidance and feedback on the effective and efficient utilization of NLR’s CSP
 Optical and Materials Facilities, thereby maintaining their impact and relevance.
 • To help pinpoint key stakeholders and potential facility users.
 • To foster collaboration through multi-organization projects.

2.3 Key Feedback and Recommendations from the Advisory Board
The Advisory Board has provided extensive feedback and recommendations, shaping the strategic
direction of NLR’s CSP Optical Facilities.
 • Expanding Access to Facilities
The Board highlighted the need to expand access to tools at NLR, including advanced microscopy
from other groups, so CSP stakeholders can utilize them. They suggested that the CSP Optical
Facilities Core program could facilitate access to such equipment and additional capabilities. A
key recommendation, particularly from European members, was for government funds to support
the utilization of CSP facilities, similar to how the European Commission funds researchers to use
CIEMAT-PSA facilities in Spain. This model, exemplified by the SFERA projects, helps
overcome financial constraints for users of publicly funded facilities. In the US, it was noted that
access often requires significant DOE-sponsored projects or CRADAs, making it difficult for
smaller efforts, thus suggesting a revival of DOE-funded tasks for smaller projects. The Board also
emphasized leveraging existing equipment rather than consistently purchasing new ones.
 • Support for HelioCon and Industry
The Board identified several high-priority areas for the HelioCon project, including soiling
characterization of reflective materials, heliostat performance, and mirror degradation. Hail
damage was also singled out as a critical issue for heliostats. They suggested that NLR’s CSP
Optical Facilities should expand its scope beyond heliostats to include other mirror types like
troughs and linear Fresnel reflectors, which are relevant for process heat and desalination. Broader
research areas, such as thermochemical solar fuel and other solar thermal applications, should also

 9

be considered, drawing inspiration from European facilities that are open to diverse research.
Diversifying the use of facilities has been a recurring theme in advisory board meetings.
 • Important Issues for Industry
 o Hail Damage: A major concern for investors and stakeholders, particularly in
California (e.g., Ivanpah). The Board suggested evaluating the impact of hail damage before
developing new tools and noted that DLR and CEA use “steel ball” tests instead of “ice ball” tests
due to insufficient industry demand. However, the “ice ball” test was deemed useful for reflective
films where damage assessment through accelerated testing is critical. The Board also suggested
investigating ways to protect UAWS mirrors against future weather events, and a member offered
to check with DLR for fabricating replacement curved mirrors.
 o Soiling: There is a need for better field measurement systems, such as a drone-
based approach. Research topics of interest include soiling forecasting, site assessment based on
environmental data, development of soiling models, and understanding how soiling affects
scattering at different incidence angles. NLR was encouraged to engage with SolarPACES, which
has a funded soiling task.
 o Metrology Development: For aging tests, developing a climate chamber that
combines more contributing factors remains a challenge. The Board noted the need for dynamic
testing packages and collaboration to improve tools for NIO demonstrations in industrial fields.
 • Tools Development (UAWS, Lambda 1050 Complementary Equipment)
The Board recommended evaluating the viability and stakeholder interest in the Ultra-Accelerated
Weathering System (UAWS), which was damaged by a hailstorm. They also strongly advocated
for developing complementary equipment to the Lambda 1050 spectrophotometer that would
enable the measurement of full-spectrum specular reflectance, a critical industry need not currently
met by commercial equipment. European partners expressed willingness to support this effort,
proposing round-robin tests to establish an international standard once the equipment is developed
in both Europe and the US. This led to the procurement of a new Lambda 1050+ and the Total
Absolute Measurement System (TAMS).
    • Certification (ISO 9001, AS9100)
The Board believed that certification of NLR’s CSP Optical facility, particularly ISO 9001, would
be highly valuable for industry. Some stakeholders mistakenly believed the facilities were already
accredited due to NLR’s strong reputation. Drawing on DLR’s experience in Europe, which
achieved ISO 9001 certification and working towards ISO 17025, NLR was encouraged to pursue
accreditation for CSP mirror facets first, then expand to other areas. NLR’s Advanced Optical
Materials Lab successfully achieved ISO 9001:2015 certification on June 15, 2023, demonstrating
a commitment to quality management for calibration and efficiency measurements of CSP
materials. Ci5000-4 and Ci5000-5 weatherometers have also been certified by Atlas (ISO 17025)
and are prioritized for certified CSP work. Following this success, DOE suggested pursuing
AS9100 certification for the HFSF, which is particularly relevant for the Aerospace and Defense
Industry. NLR’s certification team, initially less experienced with AS9100, is now trained and
assisting in preparations for certification in the next year.
 • Uncertainty Analysis
The Board provided feedback on the determination of measurement uncertainties for the upcoming
Core project (FY25-27). They suggested not focusing on uncertainty related to weathering

 10

chambers, as it is challenging to decouple uncertainty from the chamber versus the sample itself.
For spectrophotometer measurements, they recommended including Type B uncertainty
(originating from the equipment) in future round robins, noting its variability with equipment age.
Based on PSA’s experience, NLR plans to meet with the PSA team to discuss uncertainty analysis
for both spectrophotometers and the HFSF, and round robins are planned. The Board noted the
challenge in determining uncertainty for typical thermochemistry reactors due to their specificities.
When discussing full mirror facets, the Board observed that variations in silver layer thickness
during factory fabrication could lead to higher reflectance in certain areas, increasing measurement
uncertainty, suggesting more measurements on larger samples for accurate evaluation.
 • Developing a Standard for Absorbers
The Board recognized the importance of developing a standard for absorber measurements, with
CIEMAT expressing strong interest in collaboration. They recommended that NLR get involved
with SolarPACES and lead a specific subtask within SolarPACES Task 3 focused on the
standardization of absorber measurements.
 • Diversification of Funding and Payment Options
The Board suggested exploring ways to diversify funding from the private sector and other DOE
programs. They also recommended investigating easy payment options for facility use, such as
credit card payment, to facilitate engagement with small companies and external users.
 • Website Update and Virtual Tour
Updating the CSP Optical Facilities website is a DOE milestone and part of a larger NLR CSP
team objective. Planned actions include brief equipment descriptions, links to detailed information,
and connections to other NLR groups for additional capabilities. Critically, the Board suggested
implementing a virtual (3D) tour of the Optical Facilities to enhance accessibility and reach.
 • Workshops, Webinars, and Tutorials
The Board provided strategies for organizing workshops, webinars, and tutorials. They advised
starting with virtual events due to potential stakeholder travel constraints, noting that successful
virtual events might encourage later onsite visits. For workshops, the goal is to raise interest in
specific topics such as standardization, aging, life cycle, soiling, cleaning, and performing
measurements correctly. European colleagues shared experiences with two-day workshops, with
the second day dedicated to discussing industry issues and identifying collaborations. For webinars
and tutorials, key topics related to the HFSF, degradation studies, and cleaning were suggested.
These could be recorded to disseminate knowledge beyond CSP. Learning from HelioCon’s
webinar organization and Northeastern University’s e-training courses was also recommended,
alongside recruiting external speakers and considering e-training with solar companies. The Board
also suggested coordinating with HelioCon’s education and outreach efforts for tutorials.
 • Community Outreach
To raise awareness and provide professional services, community outreach strategies included
exploring social events with city or state coordination, as well as onsite visits. The Board also
suggested contacting and getting involved with standard organizations, promoting incentives like
Business Vouchers or the Solar Prize, and engaging DOE to support CSP companies.
 • Publications as Success Factors

The Board emphasized that both confidential reports for private customers and publicly available
publications (journal articles, posters) should be considered success factors.
 • Additional Bord Members and Meeting Structure
Recent feedback suggested adding a Board member. Given the project’s well-developed nature,

absent members, and updates circulated between meetings to avoid conflicts with major CSP

2.4 National and International Collaborations
NLR, in collaboration with Sandia National Laboratory, organized a trip to Europe from March
21st to March 31st, 2023, to visit both existing and potential collaborators’ facilities. Paul Ndione
and Guangdong Zhu from NLR visited AGC, a glass manufacturer in Belgium, touring their
mirrors production plant and R&D center, and discussing potential research collaborations. They
also toured CIEMAT’s CSP facilities in Almeria, Spain, meeting with the director and lead
researchers to identify common interests and future collaboration opportunities. In Almeria, they
met with CSP Services, a DLR spin-off, to discuss industry challenges and potential
collaborations. Paul Ndione further toured PROMES-CNRS in France, identifying research areas
for potential collaboration. This trip significantly enhanced NLR’s understanding of glass and
mirror coating fabrication challenges and established key areas for future international
collaboration, including leveraging AGC’s contacts for studying mirror durability in the field. Two
follow-up meetings with European partners and industry on solar receivers had been completed to
initiate international collaboration, aiming to establish guidelines for solar receiver coating tests

3.2 FTLB 158-02 Facilities
The FTLB 158-02 facility is equipped with specialized optical characterization instruments,
forming the backbone of NLR’s materials analysis capabilities. The Lambda 1050
spectrophotometer, a core tool for evaluating absorber and reflector performance, encountered
intermittent operational problems since late FY22Q3. Extensive troubleshooting efforts, including
repeated consultations with the manufacturer PerkinElmer and the suspicion of a faulty
preamplifier board for the 150mm integrating sphere, proved to be prolonged due to backordered
parts. Eventually, a more comprehensive diagnostic performed by PerkinElmer engineers
identified deteriorated optics and a damaged detector, significantly compromising the old system’s
ability to provide reliably intense signals, effectively rendering it unusable for the high-quality,
precise data NLR requires for cutting-edge research. Although the old instrument retained some
limited functionality, being reliable for transmittance and reflectance measurements in the 250-
860 nm range, its reliability for critical, full-spectrum analysis, especially for sensitive absorber
samples, was diminished. To comprehensively address these issues and meet modern research
demands, a new PerkinElmer Lambda 1050+ spectrophotometer was ordered and successfully
delivered in October 2023. This crucial upgrade, visually (depicted in Figure 20) marks a
significant leap in NLR’s capabilities, particularly for supporting the HelioCon project and
evaluating advanced absorber and reflector materials crucial for next-generation CSP systems.
Following its installation and setup in October 2023, two PerkinElmer service engineers, including
their master engineer, visited NLR in mid-November 2023. They provided comprehensive training
to three NLR researchers, equipping them with a fundamental understanding of the new system
and enhancing their ability to accurately evaluate data quality for sensitive absorber samples,

 15

thereby minimizing backlogs and project delays. To minimize potential issues associated with
multiple users and ensure optimal performance, the operation of the new Lambda 1050+ is
currently limited to these three formally trained personnel. An annual maintenance plan with
PerkinElmer has also been established to ensure its long-term performance and reliability. A
further, highly anticipated enhancement, the Total Absolute Measurement System (TAMS,
depicted in Figure 21), a critical accessory for the new Lambda 1050+, was successfully delivered
on July 1, 2024. A PerkinElmer technician has already expertly installed the system, with advanced
training from PerkinElmer technicians scheduled for July and early FY25-Q1. This system will
provide the highest degree of flexibility and accuracy in measuring specular and diffuse
transmission and reflectance as a function of angles. Critically, it enables bidirectional reflectance
distribution function (BRDF) and Bidirectional Transmittance Distribution Function (BTDF)
measurements, a capability of significant interest to Defense stakeholders and industry partners
like Heliogen, with whom NLR is actively seeking collaboration in this advanced measurement
area.

3.5 High Flux Solar Furnace (HFSF) Maintenance and Repair
The HFSF is a heavily utilized user facility that supports a demanding research schedule for
numerous stakeholders, including industrial partners like CSP, Defense, Aerospace, as well as
academic collaborators such as Niigata University and Virginia Tech. This high demand
necessitates a robust and continuous program of maintenance, calibration, repair, and strategic
upgrades to ensure its reliability and expand its research capabilities. This section focuses on the
maintenance and repair aspect while strategic upgrades are reported in section 4.3.
 • Routine and Scheduled Maintenance and Calibration: To manage its complex
operations and a fully utilized schedule, NLR has instituted a structured maintenance and
calibration plan. A physical logbook and detailed spreadsheets have been created to track all
activities, log issues, and alert users of upcoming requirements for any equipment, sensors, or
systems used in HFSF operations. Key scheduled maintenance activities are planned quarterly to
ensure the facility’s operational integrity. For instance, upcoming tasks include:
 • Mirror Alignments: The heliostat and primary mirror arrays require periodic alignment
checks. Aligning the heliostat mirrors is a particularly labor-intensive task, demanding a team of
3-4 people for up to two weeks.
 • Mechanical Servicing: This involves essential lubrication tasks such as greasing and
changing the oil in the heliostat’s tracking gearboxes, as well as cleaning and greasing the
guiderails and chains for the attenuator and shutters.
 • System Calibrations: The system’s pyrheliometer and heat flux transducers are regularly
calibrated to ensure measurement accuracy. Water calorimetry measurements are also performed
periodically to confirm the furnace’s power output.

 20

 • Heliostat System Repair and Upgrades: The HFSF’s heliostat has been a focus of
significant repair and upgrade efforts, often requiring specialized external support from Genesis
Engineering, the company that originally programmed and upgraded the system. A private
company was contracted for several critical tasks, including updating the PC software license and
communications, installing a new ethernet cable, and general troubleshooting. An evaluation and
simulation of the tracking program, along with the implementation of new calibration offsets and
homing procedures, is also planned to optimize performance.
A critical and unexpected issue arose when the battery in the heliostat’s remote-control tablet failed
catastrophically, destroying the device. This tablet is essential not only for external operation but
also as a backup control system. A replacement tablet was quickly acquired from NLR’s excess
computer supply and temporarily programmed with remote access until the proper control software
could be reloaded by Genesis Engineering.

3.6 Feedback and Impact of Operations and Maintenance Work
The comprehensive operations and maintenance (O&M) efforts outlined in Task 2 are not merely
about keeping equipment running; they represent a fundamental commitment to NLR’s core
mission and significantly impact its strategic objectives, stakeholder relationships, and potential
for groundbreaking research. This meticulous work is deeply intertwined with Task 1: Stakeholder
Engagement and Feedback and Task 3: Key New/Upgraded Capabilities, directly contributing to
DOE goals and attracting diverse customers and collaborators.
 • Impact on NLR’s Mission and DOE Goals
The continuous maintenance and strategic upgrades ensure that NLR’s facilities remain unique, in
good working order, and state-of-the-art, which are explicit objectives of this project. By providing
reliable and highly accurate testing and characterization capabilities, NLR directly supports the
DOE’s overarching goal of accelerating the development of low-cost, high-performance reflective
and absorber materials. The ISO 9001:2015 certification for the Advanced Optical Materials Lab,
covering calibration and efficiency measurements, is a testament to this commitment to quality.
This certification, along with the pursuit of AS9100 for the HFSF for the Aerospace and Defense
Industry, not only validates NLR’s internal processes but also enhances its credibility and
attractiveness as a trusted partner for critical national initiatives. These certifications signify
adherence to rigorous quality standards, which is crucial for the reliability and bankability of CSP
technologies.
 • Connection to Stakeholder Engagement and Feedback
Many O&M activities are direct responses to the insightful feedback from the Advisory Board.
For instance, the refurbishment and ongoing upgrades to the UAWS, including the installation of
pyrheliometers and pyranometers for accurate UV dose measurement, directly address the Board’s
concern about hail damage and the viability of accelerated weathering tools. The Board’s strong
recommendation for complementary equipment to the Lambda 1050 spectrophotometer that
enables full-spectrum specular reflectance measurements was a direct catalyst for the acquisition
of the new Lambda 1050+ and the Total Absolute Measurement System (TAMS). This
responsiveness fosters trust and validates the Board’s role, reinforcing their belief that NLR’s
facilities are “of great value, and a great resource” for the CSP community. The regular reporting
of equipment utilization, now transitioning to the "Lab Reservations" system, serves to provide

 21

transparency and informs the “hierarchical list of equipment and O&M needs” derived from
stakeholder input.
 • Enhancing Collaboration and Attracting Customers/Collaborators
The impact of robust O&M extends to NLR’s ability to engage with a diverse array of partners.
 o Industry: The operational readiness of the HFSF, supported by routine
maintenance and upcoming upgrades (like the new water calorimeter and dual-chamber design),
enables crucial Technical Service Agreements (TSAs) with Defense and Aerospace companies for
material durability and flash tests. A major CSP company has expressed keen interest in the BRDF
measurement capabilities of the new TAMS system, opening doors for direct industry
collaboration. The refurbished UAWS, once fully online, is also a valuable tool that Heliogen has
shown interest in.
 o Academia: The testing of Virginia Tech’s absorber coatings at the HFSF is a prime
example of NLR’s support for academic research, demonstrating the furnace’s capabilities for
high-temperature material evaluation. Similarly, successful 4-week test campaigns with Niigata
University for thermochemical CO2 recycling highlight NLR’s role as a user facility for advanced
energy research.
 o National Laboratories: Ongoing collaborations with Sandia National Laboratory
for NIO validation and SOFAST development benefit from NLR’s maintained optical metrology
capabilities.
 o International Partners: European partners, including CIEMAT and PROMES-
CNRS, are interested in round-robin tests for specular reflectance and the standardization of
absorber measurements, leveraging NLR’s upgraded equipment and expertise. The planned drone-
based measurements at CIEMAT-PSA’s heliostat field exemplify the global reach of NLR’s
O&M-enabled capabilities.
 o DoD: The pursuit of AS9100 certification for the HFSF, driven by DOE
recommendations and supported by a trained NLR certification team, aims to attract projects from
the Aerospace and Defense Industry, directly responding to strategic national interests. Interactions
with NLR’s DoD Business Development Team are actively seeking to expand these opportunities.
 • Connection to Other Tasks (New Capabilities - Task 3):
O&M is the bedrock upon which new capabilities are built. The successful installation and training
for the Lambda 1050+ directly enabled the delivery and impending integration of the TAMS
system. The meticulous maintenance of SIMTA lab space for UAS directly supports the
development of NIO and BCS capabilities at the Flatirons campus, including flight tests with the
new IF1200A drone and the installation of custom BCS targets. Without robust O&M, the
aspiration for new metrology capabilities at Flatirons for third-party heliostat evaluation services
would be severely hampered. The development of a new turning mirror for the HFSF, allowing
for bottom-up concentration, enhances the furnace’s versatility for particle receiver research,
building upon existing HFSF operational integrity.

 4.3.2 Turning Mirror for Reflectance Angle Modification
A significant new capability developed and completed for the HFSF is the turning mirror for
modifying the reflectance angle for bottom-up concentration. While using a mirror to redirect a
concentrated beam is not new (with an original turning mirror shown tilted into position in Figure
13 providing historical context), this specific capability addresses a critical need for mimicking
solar exposure angles found in heliostat fields for tower systems. The HFSF’s focal point typically
comes in at a positive 6.9-degree angle, which is opposite to how receivers are typically exposed
in a solar field. This new system allows for the redirection of the beam to provide an upward angle,
crucial for gravity-fed systems like particle receivers. Design, fabrication, and testing were
completed in Q4 FY22 to modify the reflectance angle for bottom-up distribution, which more
closely simulates the angles found in a heliostat field for tower system. Design, fabrication, and
initial testing of this new turning mirror were completed, utilizing existing mirror cooling plates
and a powered telescopic pillar to minimize costs. The final design, incorporating 80/20 extruded
aluminum with adapter plates to interface with the cooling plate and pillar, is visible in Figure 13.

 4.3.3 HFSF Utilization and Solar Data Analysis

Figure 15. (a) A comparison in days with solar irradiance > 600 W/m2 between 2021 and 2014. (b)
 Solar irradiance data over 850 W/m2 divided by months/year starting with January for 2014 (top

Efficient scheduling of the HFSF, particularly for on-sun research, remains a challenge due to the
pervasive belief that solar research is primarily limited to summer months. To address this, NLR
has undertaken an analysis of historical solar radiation data from its Solar Radiation Research
Laboratory (SRRL), located adjacent to the HFSF, spanning back to 1981. Data from 2021 (the
last full recorded year) and 2014 (randomly selected) on Normal Incident Pyrheliometer (NIP) and
wind were meticulously analyzed, as these factors significantly affect solar research. This analysis,
illustrated in Figure 15a, revealed that while Colorado boasts many sunny days, the number of
days with sufficiently high solar irradiance for HFSF operations (e.g., above 600 W/m2 or 850
W/m2 for temperatures above 700°C) varies significantly by month. Surprisingly, some of the
highest solar values are recorded from January through March, June, and October, as detailed in
Figure 15b, which displays solar irradiance data over 850 W/m2 by month for selected years. The
collection of a wider expanse of data, preferably over an entire calendar year, will help to
understand the average availability of this unique system more accurately, with current data from
January-April 2024 showing an average sun duration of 3.62 ± 0.54 hours per day for solar
intensity greater than 750 W/m2. Presenting this data to stakeholders aims to help spread research
time throughout the year, optimizing the furnace’s utilization beyond traditional summer-centric
scheduling.

4.6 Virginia Tech Absorber Coatings Project
This collaborative project with Virginia Tech (VT) is a significant component of Task 3,
encompassing the fabrication, characterization, and high-flux solar furnace (HFSF) testing of
novel absorber coatings, alongside NLR’s modeling efforts. Due to delays in finalizing the Non-
Disclosure Agreement (NDA), which was only completed on May 30th, 2024, the project
experienced over six months of delay. This necessitated a No Cost Time Extension (NCTE) request
to the DOE for the project, seeking to extend the project’s end date to March 30, 2025, instead of
September 30, 2024. The project was further extended to June 30, 2025, due to inclement weather
during the winter.

 4.6.1 Objectives and Sample Fabrication
The overall objectives of the Virginia Tech tasks are to prepare sample coupons with absorber
coatings for testing at NLR’s solar furnace under various temperatures, durations, and other
conditions. Two rounds of tests, a short-term and a long-term were planned. For short-term testing,
Virginia Tech fabricated solar absorber coatings on flat coupons of Inconel substrates followed by
annealing. Virginia Tech sent a set of 16 flat samples to NLR for initial optical characterization
and testing. For long-term testing, four flat coupons and a set of three Inconel cylindrical substrates
with identical absorber coatings were prepared and sent to NLR.

 4.6.2 Short-Term Cyclic Testing at HFSF
NLR conducted the first round of flat plate sample testing at the HFSF, with sixteen samples (in
sets of four) undergoing 50 light cycles at target temperatures of 650°C, 700°C, 750°C, and 800°C.
Each light cycle consisted of a specific on-sun exposure time, with the shutter and attenuator
opening gradually to reach the set temperature. Before each light cycle, samples were subjected to
dark cycle in a humidity environment, simulating nighttime conditions.

 4.6.3 Long-Term Cyclic Testing at HFSF
Following the successful short-term tests, long-duration testing (initially planned for 300 cycles
on both flat and cylindrical samples) was initiated. The focus during FY25Q3 was on conducting
and completing this long-term cyclic testing on-sun at the NLR HFSF. Absorber coatings
deposited on four flat inconel substrates and three cylindrical substrates were tested on-sun under
cyclic temperature and humidity conditions, with maximum surface temperatures reaching up to
750°C. As of June 30, 2025, 164 cycles were completed on both the flats and cylinders samples.
Results of these tests and samples characterizations before and after testing (morphology and
optical) will be published separately.

 4.6.4 NLR Modeling Task
The NLR modeling task aims to extend its existing material-kinetics-optics (MKO) model for
absorber degradation in three fundamental ways, leveraging the experimental data from Virginia
Tech.

 42

4.6.4.1 Background
The existing MKO code can fit time-dependent spectra by separately fitting optical coefficients
for each wavelength, sharing a single kinetic model. However, this brute-force approach is
inefficient as it does not leverage the smoothness of optical spectra and ignores Kramers-Kronig
(K-K) relations. The focus here is on extending our existing MKO model for absorber degradation
in three ways:
 • Extend the material model to apply to model surface reflectance and absorptance spectra.
 • Extend the optical reflectance model to account for the rough, fractal nature of the
 scattering surfaces
 • Apply the MKO model to fit the measured changes in the absorptance spectra of advanced
 fractal solar absorbing coatings.

4.6.4.2 Methods
 • K-K consistent models for fitting absorptance spectra
As described in previous reports, we coded K-K consistent model functions of wavelength () for
the real (n) and imaginary (k) parts of the complex index of refraction and fit the measured
absorptance to a linear combination of these functions. The details about the numbers of functions
used and how the fits were performed for our data sets will be published separately.
 • Modification of reflectance to account for fractal surface roughness
Simulations to compare reflectance for fractal vs. flat surfaces at visible, near infrared, and infrared
wavelengths were performed.
The main goal of this modeling approach is to get enough material information at each time to
support kinetic modeling for how the coating changes over time to enable extrapolations and
possibly to identify mechanisms. Results of the modeling will be published separately.

5.3 General Philosophy on Publications and Dissemination
The Advisory Board has provided valuable feedback regarding NLR’s publication strategy,
emphasizing that both confidential reports tailored for private customers or stakeholders and
publicly available publications (such as journal articles, conference papers, and posters) should be
considered essential success factors. This balanced approach ensures that NLR meets the specific
needs of its industrial partners while simultaneously contributing to the open scientific knowledge
base. The Board also highlighted the importance of generating high-quality technical reports on
optical materials for CSP applications. Such reports, providing a reassessment of the current state
and directions of optical materials and reflective coatings, including updates on related
technologies, their current state-of-the-art, and lessons learned (what has worked and what hasn’t),
would be immensely beneficial. Unlike many journal articles, technical reports are generally
publicly available and offer ease of access, facilitating broader knowledge dissemination and
serving as a valuable resource for the CSP community. NLR continuously strives to align its
dissemination efforts with these recommendations to maximize the impact of its research.

6 Lessons Learned
6.1 Commercialization Pathways
To enhance the market viability and commercial adoption of technologies tested at NLR’s CSP
Optical Facilities, several strategic pathways are recommended, focusing on tested coatings,
drone-based heliostat management, and global standardization.

 6.1.1 Market Viability of Tested Coatings
A key step toward commercializing advanced coatings is to develop a comprehensive technical
report compiling and reassessing current optical materials and reflective coatings in CSP
applications. This report should detail state-of-the-art developments, performance successes and
failures, and updates on related technologies to provide valuable insights to stakeholders. Engaging
with European partners, such as DLR, is crucial for exchanging knowledge and jointly developing
improved coatings. This collaboration can be fostered through round-robin testing and the
development of international standards to ensure uniform global certification. Furthermore,
research should be customer-driven, utilizing stakeholder questionnaires to identify pressing
industry needs. Prioritizing research on coatings that address market barriers like hail damage,
abrasion resistance, and mirror degradation will ensure a clear return value for CSP research
investments.

 6.1.2 Leveraging Drone-Based Monitoring for Heliostat Management
Drone technology presents a significant opportunity for improving the operational efficiency of
CSP plants. To commercialize this, NLR should focus on several key areas:
 • Technology Development and Durability: NLR’s NIO tools can be enhanced by
integrating high-resolution cameras and specialized sensors into drones. Incorporating
photogrammetry features would enable higher precision measurements of heliostat surfaces and
shapes from greater distances. Given challenges like minor drone damage during testing, it is
important to improve durability with reinforced frames, weather-resistant components, and robust
navigation systems for reliable operation. The newly acquired IF1200A drone, a heavy-lift system
with open-source software, longer flight times, and higher payload capacity, represents a
significant upgrade over previous models.
 • Field Applications and Industry Partnerships: Drones should be deployed for real-time
data collection on heliostat performance, reflectivity, and soiling levels to improve predictive
maintenance. Developing optimized flight patterns and algorithms is necessary to efficiently
monitor large heliostat fields as well as creating a service model offering drone-based monitoring
and data analysis to plant operators. Collaborative testing campaigns with facilities like CIEMAT-
PSA are vital for validating these drone-based systems in operational environments. An NLR team
visit to Almeria, Spain, is scheduled to advance this collaborative work. Organizing workshops
and demonstrations at CSP facilities will showcase these capabilities to plant operators and
encourage adoption.
 • Measurement Accuracy and Commercialization: To ensure data reliability, improved
calibration tools are needed to correlate drone brightness measurements with reflectivity values
for soiling assessments. Drones can also be equipped with additional sensors to measure sun shape

 47

and atmospheric attenuation, helping operators optimize heliostat configurations. A viable
commercial service model could be developed, offering drone technology and data analysis to CSP
operators on a subscription or one-time basis. Engaging stakeholders through surveys will help
tailor these offerings to meet specific industry needs.
 • Long-Term Vision: The long-term vision includes integrating drone-gathered data with
AI-based predictive maintenance algorithms to anticipate heliostat failures. Collaborating with
organizations like SolarPACES to establish industry standards for drone-based inspections would
accelerate adoption and ensure uniformity across the sector.

 6.1.3 Round-Robin Certification, Global Standardization
Establishing global standards through certification is a critical pathway to ensuring the reliability
and performance of CSP technologies worldwide.
 • Enhancing Testing Protocols: NLR should continue to lead and participate in
international round-robin testing with partners like CIEMAT-PSA and DLR. The round-robin
testing protocols should be expanded to include not just standard Type A uncertainties (sample
measurement variability) but also Type B uncertainties, which originate from equipment and
environmental factors, for more comprehensive data validation. These tests will validate
measurement accuracy and address discrepancies. A key focus should be on developing new
equipment for full-spectrum specular reflectance measurements, a critical need for modern CSP
applications, through collaboration with European partners.
 • Developing International Certification: Building on NLR’s successful ISO 9001
certification, the goal is to create a cohesive international certification protocol in collaboration
with bodies like SolarPACES. Lessons from DLR’s accreditation efforts should be incorporated
as NLR explores advanced certifications like ISO 17025 for expanded testing capabilities. This
framework should be extended to new components, including absorber measurements and anti-
soiling coatings, to ensure quality as new technologies enter the market.
 • Global Collaboration and Outreach: Joint workshops with European teams should be
hosted to educate stakeholders on standardized methods and certification requirements.
International conferences like SolarPACES serve as important venues for presenting progress and
gathering industry feedback. Establishing a dedicated SolarPACES subtask focused on
certifications would formalize these collaborative efforts.

 6.1.4 Additional Commercialization Strategies
To further support these pathways, several overarching strategies are recommended. Hosting
workshops at CSP facilities allows stakeholders to experience technologies firsthand, promoting
awareness and collaboration. The CSP Optical Facilities website should be updated with virtual
tours and comprehensive equipment details to improve visibility and accessibility for potential
customers. Finally, industry engagement should be simplified through flexible payment options
like credit cards, open-door policies for smaller tasks, and hybrid funding models to attract a
broader range of companies without requiring complex DOE contracts.

6.2 Funding and Partnership Diversification Across Key Sectors
A primary recommendation is to diversify funding streams beyond traditional DOE grants to
ensure long-term sustainability and foster cross-industry innovation. This involves proactively
engaging with the private sector, academia, and other government agencies.

 6.2.1 Engaging with the Private Sector
 • Private Sector Engagement: NLR should establish Technical Service Agreements
(TSAs) and cost-share programs to attract private companies, including CSP manufacturers,
energy utilities, and engineering firms. The ongoing work with partners like Dash2 Labs and others
demonstrates the potential for these collaborations. An advisory board recommendation
emphasizes making the facilities easier to use for smaller companies that may not have a DOE
contract, suggesting a hybrid funding approach and open-door policies for smaller tasks.
 • Defense and Aerospace: There are significant opportunities for collaboration with the
Department of Defense (DoD) and defense and aerospace -related companies like the ones that
NLR is already working with. These partners have shown substantial interest in using NLR’s
facilities, particularly the HFSF, for high-temperature materials and component durability testing.
Ongoing and future Technical Service Agreements (TSAs) with these entities provide a stable
funding source and position NLR as a critical facility for defense applications. Research requiring
high-priority government DX ratings presents a unique opportunity, as it necessitates secure,
exclusive facility access that NLR can provide.
 • Automotive Industry: The automotive sector presents a viable market, particularly in the
areas of optical characterization and material durability. NLR’s expertise could be applied to
improve the corrosion protection of lacquers, building on technologies like salt spray testing
originally developed by the automotive industry. The capabilities of the UAWS, Lambda 1050+
spectrophotometer and accelerated weathering systems are applicable to materials used in
automotive applications.
 • High-Temperature Process Industries and Critical Minerals: NLR’s facilities can
support industries involved in advanced manufacturing and chemical processing that require high
temperatures, such as pyrolysis and thermochemical recycling. The successful collaboration with
Niigata University on thermochemical CO2 recycling showcases this potential. Furthermore, NLR
can play a role in the evaluation of critical minerals and materials. The facilities are equipped to
measure the radiative properties of novel particles and nanostructured materials for
thermochemical energy storage, supporting projects from SBIR/STTR awardees (like the ones
from Hit Nano Inc. and Advanced Materials Scientia, LLC).

 6.2.2 Academic and International Partnerships
 • Universities and Academic Research: Strengthening partnerships with academic
institutions is crucial. NLR should pursue joint grants through bodies like the National Science
Foundation (NSF). By offering access to unique tools like the UAWS and HFSF, NLR can attract
graduate students and researchers working on material science, solar fuels, and advanced optics.
Formalizing student internship programs and collaborations with universities on high-temperature
reactor modeling or particle receiver development can cultivate a talent pipeline and drive
innovation.

 49

 • International Partnerships: NLR should pursue joint grants with international partners
through programs like Horizon Europe. Formalizing student internship programs and
collaborations with institutions like CIEMAT-PSA and PROMES-CNRS on joint priorities such
as drone-based heliostat characterization and standards development is crucial. European partners
are vital for co-hosting workshops and developing international standards through round-robin
testing campaigns.

 6.2.3 Leveraging Quality Certifications for Market Advantage
Achieving and promoting quality management certifications is a powerful tool for
commercialization, enhancing credibility and attracting high-value partners.
 • ISO 9001 Impact: The successful attainment of ISO 9001:2015 certification for the CSP
Optical Facilities is a major milestone that officially validates NLR’s quality management system
for calibration and testing services. This certification enhances customer satisfaction and
demonstrates a commitment to continual improvement, which is highly valued by industrial
partners who may have previously assumed the facilities were already accredited based on
reputation alone. Two of the Ci5000 weatherometers are also certified under ISO 17025 by Atlas,
prioritizing them for work requiring certified testing.
 • Pursuing AS9100 for Aerospace and Defense: As suggested by the DOE, pursuing
AS9100 certification is a strategic next step. AS9100 is the primary quality management standard
for the aerospace and defense industries, and achieving it would significantly strengthen NLR’s
position to secure high-value contracts from partners like the DoD, Defense contractors and the
Aerospace industry. This certification aligns with the growing demand from these sector and
signals NLR’s capability to meet their stringent quality requirements.

key performance indicators such as the number and diversity of active users, the volume of cross-
boundary collaborations, and equipment utilization rates.

collaborative projects that utilize facility resources. University outreach would be enhanced by
hosting workshops for academic participants that combine facility presentations with hands-on
demonstrations, inviting faculty and students to propose topics for future collaboration. These
initiatives would cultivate a skilled workforce and ensure NLR’s research remains at the forefront
of innovation.
By implementing these interconnected strategies, NLR can significantly expand the utilization of
its CSP Optical Facilities, secure a sustainable and diverse funding base, and solidify its role as a
global leader in energy research infrastructure.

 6.3.1 Improving Accessibility for All Users and Community Engagement
 • Access for external users: Streamlining access for external users, particularly small and
medium-sized enterprises and academic researchers, is critical for increasing facility utilization.
The Advisory Board has strongly recommended implementing a credit card payment option to
reduce administrative delays for users without DOE contracts. Additionally, launching an
automated online booking system would allow users to view equipment availability and schedule
lab time directly, enhancing efficiency and user satisfaction.
 • Community Engagement: Public engagement would be conducted through facility open
houses, social media channels, and simplified technical blogs that demystify CSP technologies and
highlight how taxpayer investments contribute to energy innovation.

7 Conclusions
This multi-year project successfully executed its core mission to strengthen National Laboratory
of the Rockies (NLR)’s Concentrating Solar Power (CSP) Optical Facilities, ensuring their
continued relevance as state-of-the-art resources for industry, academia, and the broader research
community. Through a structured approach encompassing strategic stakeholder engagement,
diligent maintenance and utilization, and the targeted development of new and upgraded
capabilities, the project has not only maintained but significantly enhanced NLR’s capacity to
support the development of low-cost, high-performance solar materials and components.
A key pillar of this initiative was the strategic integration of stakeholder feedback to guide facility
development. The formation and active engagement of an international Advisory Board proved
invaluable, providing critical insights that directly shaped research priorities and investment
decisions. The Board’s recommendations consistently highlighted pressing industry needs, such
as addressing hail damage, soiling, and advancing metrology, which led directly to the
prioritization of major equipment upgrades, including the High Flux Solar Furnace (HFSF), the
Lambda 1050 Spectrophotometer and the Ultra-Accelerated Weathering System (UAWS).
Furthermore, proactive outreach, including visits to US and European industrial sites and a strong
presence at major conferences, fostered new collaborations and reinforced NLR’s role as a central
hub for CSP innovation. This strategic engagement was instrumental in securing new funding for
a third-party heliostat evaluation capability at the Flatirons campus, a direct response to identified
stakeholder needs.
The project demonstrated a profound commitment to operational excellence through diligent
maintenance and enhanced facility utilization. A major accomplishment was achieving ISO
9001:2015 certification for the Advanced Optical Materials Labs, a formal recognition of the
quality and reliability of NLR’s measurement capabilities that enhances customer satisfaction and
credibility. The team successfully navigated significant challenges posed by aging and failing
equipment, methodically repairing critical systems like the Tenney environmental chamber and
Ci5000 weatherometers while justifying and executing the replacement of the irrecoverable 15-
year-old Lambda 1050 spectrophotometer. The project also showcased adaptability by repurposing
the SIMTA site for a new thermal energy storage project (LIMOSA), ensuring maximum value
from existing infrastructure.
Perhaps most significantly, the project delivered on its promise to advance research techniques by
introducing and upgrading key capabilities. At the Flatirons campus, the development of the Helio-
OPAL laboratory and the deployment of new optical metrology tools like the Non-Intrusive
Optical (NIO) system and Beam Characterization System (BCS) represent a major step forward in
heliostat evaluation. Overcoming considerable budgetary and logistical hurdles, the team
successfully installed a functional BCS target and acquired prototype heliostats, establishing a
foundation for next-generation R&D. At the HFSF, innovative modifications, such as the new
turning mirror and a dual-chamber system for durability testing, have expanded research
possibilities and improved operational efficiency. The successful refurbishment of the hail-
damaged UAWS, complete with a modernized data acquisition system and new solar radiometers,
has restored a critical asset for accelerated materials testing.
Finally, the collaborative project with Virginia Tech stands as a testament to the integrated
capabilities of the facilities. Through extensive on-sun cyclic testing at the HFSF, NLR

 53

demonstrated the exceptional high-temperature durability of Virginia Tech’s novel absorber
coatings, with samples showing minimal efficiency degradation even after 164 cycles at 750°C.
This empirical work was complemented by the development of sophisticated new modeling tools
to analyze material degradation on rough, fractal surfaces, pushing the boundaries of predictive
science in CSP.
In conclusion, this project has systematically reinforced NLR’s CSP Optical Facilities, aligning
them with the evolving needs of reliable energy. Through strategic foresight, operational rigor,
and targeted technological upgrades, the facilities are now better equipped than ever to accelerate
the development, characterization, and deployment of advanced CSP technologies, thereby
fulfilling their critical mission in the national and global transition to a reliable energy future.
