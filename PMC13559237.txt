Development and internal validation of an interpretable machine learning prognostic prediction model for postoperative delirium after esophagectomy
Authors: Xiaochen Meng, Wandi Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative delirium (POD) is a frequent neurological complication associated with adverse outcomes after esophagectomy. Existing risk-assessment tools may provide limited individualized prognostic information. This study aimed to develop and internally validate an interpretable machine learning (ML) prognostic prediction model for POD after esophagectomy.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included 1,089 patients who underwent esophagectomy for esophageal cancer at Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University between May 2022 and January 2025. Patients with pre-existing cognitive disorders or severe organ dysfunction were excluded. POD was assessed during the first 7 postoperative days or until discharge using the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) or the Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU). Before data preprocessing, patients were allocated to training and internal test cohorts using stratified random sampling at a 7:3 ratio. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE). Candidate predictors were screened within the training cohort using Boruta and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. Eight ML algorithms were optimized using grid search with stratified 5-fold cross-validation. Model discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility were evaluated in the internal test cohort, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) estimated using 1,000 bootstrap resamples. Model interpretation was performed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).ResultsThe cohort had a median age of 70 years, and 75.9% of patients were male. POD occurred in 34.0% of patients, predominantly within the first three postoperative days. Categorical boosting (CatBoost) achieved the numerically highest test-set area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.930 (95% CI: 0.900–0.959), with an accuracy of 86.5%, a recall of 0.862, a specificity of 0.866, an F1 score of 0.810, and a Brier score of 0.095. Calibration and decision curve analyses indicated satisfactory agreement and potential clinical utility. The final model included age, monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), C-reactive protein (CRP), preoperative polypharmacy, prognostic nutritional index (PNI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, surgical approach, and intraoperative blood transfusion. SHAP analysis identified MLR, age, and CRP as the most influential predictors.ConclusionAn interpretable ML prognostic prediction model was developed and internally validated for estimating POD risk within 7 days after esophagectomy. Although CatBoost demonstrated strong internal performance, prospective multicenter external validation is required before clinical implementation.Keywords: Postoperative delirium (POD), machine learning (ML), esophagectomy, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), interpretable model

Highlight box

Key findings

• In this retrospective cohort of 1,089 patients undergoing esophagectomy for esophageal cancer, postoperative delirium (POD) occurred in 34.0% of cases.

• Among eight machine learning (ML) algorithms, the categorical boosting (CatBoost) model demonstrated the numerically highest overall performance, with favorable discrimination, calibration, and potential clinical utility.

• The most influential predictors of POD were monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio, age, C-reactive protein, polypharmacy, and prognostic nutritional index.

What is known and what is new?

• POD is a common and serious complication after major surgery, including esophagectomy, and is associated with adverse outcomes. Existing risk assessment tools and conventional regression-based models may be limited in their ability to capture complex nonlinear relationships among perioperative variables.

• This study developed and internally validated an interpretable ML prognostic prediction model specifically for POD in esophageal cancer patients. Using routinely available perioperative data, CatBoost showed the numerically highest performance among the evaluated algorithms, while SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis provided global and patient-level explanations of model predictions.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The final model was deployed as an open-access web calculator and may facilitate patient-level perioperative risk stratification and help identify patients who could benefit from enhanced delirium-prevention measures and closer postoperative monitoring.

• The identified contributions of systemic inflammatory and nutritional markers support their consideration in future POD risk-assessment research and clinical evaluation, although the model does not establish causal relationships.

• Because this is a single-center retrospective framework, future multicenter prospective studies and external validation are strictly required before widespread clinical implementation.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer, the seventh most common malignancy globally, remains a significant public health challenge with persistently high morbidity and mortality rates (1). Although multimodal therapies have improved outcomes, esophagectomy remains a cornerstone of curative treatment for patients with resectable disease. However, esophagectomy is one of the most invasive procedures in gastrointestinal and thoracic surgery and is frequently accompanied by various complications (2). Among these complications, postoperative delirium (POD) stands out as a critical yet underappreciated sequela. Characterized by acute disturbances in attention, cognition, and psychomotor function, POD usually occurs within the early postoperative period and is closely associated with prolonged hospitalization, delayed functional recovery, increased mortality, and long-term cognitive decline (3). Patients undergoing esophagectomy may have a risk profile that differs substantially from that of general surgical or intensive care populations. In addition to advanced age and pre-existing comorbidities, these patients commonly experience cancer-related systemic inflammation, malnutrition, impaired pulmonary reserve, major thoracoabdominal surgical trauma, prolonged anesthesia, postoperative pain, mechanical ventilation, and intensive postoperative monitoring (4-6). POD in this population may therefore arise from interactions among baseline vulnerability, inflammatory and nutritional status, medication exposure, and the magnitude of anesthetic and surgical stress (7,8). Consequently, models developed in heterogeneous surgical or intensive care populations may not be directly transportable to patients undergoing esophagectomy (9,10).

Several prediction models have been developed for POD in broader surgical populations. In a retrospective study of 24,885 adults undergoing diverse surgical procedures, ML models based on 115 preoperative electronic health record (EHR) variables achieved higher discrimination than a clinician-guided logistic regression (LR) model (11). Furthermore, other noncardiac surgery models developed in older patients have incorporated preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative variables and have shown acceptable discrimination (9,12). However, their performance decreased during external validation, and their prediction time points and candidate predictors differ from those relevant at the completion of esophagectomy (13). More recently, esophagectomy-specific POD nomograms have been reported and achieved strong predictive performance (14,15). Although these models provided important evidence that POD after esophagectomy can be predicted using routinely available clinical variables, these models are mainly based on multivariable LR or nomogram construction, which may not fully capture complex interactions among inflammatory, nutritional, anesthetic, and surgical variables (16). Furthermore, some models include variables—such as postoperative infection, pain, and duration of mechanical ventilation—that may only become available after substantial perioperative exposure, thereby limiting their usefulness for earlier risk stratification (14). Additionally, conventional nomograms provide global risk estimates but offer limited patient-level explanation of why an individual patient is classified as high risk. These limitations may restrict bedside interpretability and clinical integration.

Machine learning (ML), a transformative branch of artificial intelligence (AI), may complement conventional regression when clinically important associations involve nonlinear patterns, threshold effects, or interactions that have not been specified in advance (17,18). For example, the association between systemic inflammation and POD may vary according to age, nutritional status, comorbidity burden, and surgical stress. Tree-based ensemble algorithms can model such structures directly from tabular clinical data (19). Nevertheless, ML does not inherently guarantee superior predictive performance and may produce overly optimistic estimates when sample size, model complexity, hyperparameter tuning, and internal validation are inadequately addressed (20). Consequently, the incremental value of ML must be evaluated against an appropriate regression-based benchmark. To prevent data leakage, all data-dependent preprocessing and hyperparameter optimization must be strictly confined to the training cohort, with final validation performed in an independent dataset (21,22). Beyond rigorous validation, overcoming the “black box” nature of ML is essential for clinical adoption. Explainability methods, such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), bridge this gap by quantifying the contribution of each predictor to both the global model behavior and the individual patient’s predicted risk (23).

Therefore, this study aimed to develop and internally validate an interpretable ML-based prognostic prediction model for POD occurring within 7 days after esophagectomy for esophageal cancer. The intended prediction time point was the completion of surgery, when all included preoperative and intraoperative predictors were available but before POD onset. Using routinely available perioperative clinicopathological variables, we compared eight candidate algorithms and evaluated model discrimination, sensitivity, specificity, calibration, and decision-curve-based clinical utility. SHAP analysis was subsequently applied to the best-performing model to identify global predictor importance and patient-specific contributions. The resulting model is intended to support thoracic surgeons, anesthesiologists, intensivists, and perioperative care teams in identifying patients who may benefit from intensified postoperative surveillance and preventive management. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0923/rc).

Methods

Study cohort

This retrospective study used data from patients treated at Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University, Shenyang, China, between May 2022 and January 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (I) age ≥18 years; (II) histologically confirmed esophageal cancer; (III) completion of esophagectomy; and (IV) availability of complete POD assessment records. Exclusion criteria were: (I) concurrent diagnosis of other malignancies; (II) severe organ dysfunction, such as renal or cardiac insufficiency; (III) missing data exceeding 20% of the required variables; (IV) in-hospital death; and (V) preexisting cognitive disorders (e.g., Parkinson’s disease or schizophrenia) or prior episodes of POD.

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University (No. 2025PS1369K). Informed consent was waived because this retrospective study used anonymized clinical data and posed no additional risk to participants.

Sample size calculation

Because this was a retrospective single-center cohort study, the available sample size was determined by all consecutive eligible patients who underwent esophagectomy during the prespecified study period, rather than by prospective recruitment to a fixed sample-size target.

To assess the adequacy of the available development sample, a post hoc sample-size calculation was performed using the framework proposed by Riley et al. for binary-outcome prognostic prediction models and implemented in the pmsampsize package in R (24). To account for the dimensionality of the complete feature-selection process, the calculation was based on all 45 candidate predictor parameters considered before variable selection, including the additional parameters required for multicategorical predictors. Assuming an anticipated C-statistic of 0.75 and a POD prevalence of 34.0%, the estimated minimum development sample was 2,168 patients, including approximately 738 POD events, corresponding to a target shrinkage factor of 0.90. The available development cohort comprised 763 patients, including 261 POD events, and therefore did not meet this conservative theoretical recommendation. To reduce the risk of overfitting within the available sample, candidate predictors were prespecified on clinical grounds; preprocessing, feature selection, and hyperparameter tuning were restricted to the training cohort; model complexity was controlled through regularization and restricted hyperparameter grids; and stratified five-fold cross-validation was used during model development. Model performance was subsequently evaluated in a held-out internal test cohort of 326 patients, including 109 POD events and 217 non-events. These event counts provided a reasonable basis for initial internal performance assessment but were not interpreted as guaranteeing model stability or as a substitute for formal external validation. POD assessment

POD was assessed twice daily (08:00–10:00 and 20:00–22:00) by trained clinicians throughout the entire first 7 postoperative days or until discharge, whichever occurred first. This assessment schedule was intended to capture delirium occurring during both the early ICU phase and the subsequent ward-based recovery period. For patients admitted to a general ward, POD was assessed using the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) (25), whereas the Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU) was used for patients receiving postoperative care in the ICU (26). Both CAM and CAM-ICU criteria require the presence of four features: (I) acute onset and fluctuating course; (II) inattention; (III) disorganized thinking; and (IV) altered level of consciousness. POD is diagnosed if the patient exhibits both (I) and (II), along with either (III) or (IV). POD was assessed by a dedicated team of senior ward nurses and attending physicians who had received standardized training in the administration of CAM/CAM-ICU. The clinical staff responsible for POD assessment were not involved in predictor selection, data analysis, or model development and were unaware of the subsequent ML predictions. For patients discharged before postoperative day 7, POD assessment ended at discharge, and no post-discharge delirium assessment was performed.

Data collection

Candidate predictors were prespecified before model development based on clinical plausibility, routine availability in perioperative practice, and previously reported associations with POD or related postoperative outcomes (27-29). Variables extracted from EHRs were grouped into demographic characteristics, baseline comorbidities, medication exposure, tumor-related factors, surgical and anesthetic variables, inflammatory and nutritional markers, and early perioperative physiological indicators available by the completion of surgery.

Demographic variables included age, gender, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, and alcohol consumption history. Comorbidities encompassed hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chronic renal insufficiency, stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA), obstructive sleep apnea, and vision or hearing impairment. Preoperative polypharmacy was categorized as ≥5 concurrent medications. Surgical and anesthetic variables included American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, anesthesia duration (h), surgical duration (h), estimated blood loss (mL), intraoperative blood transfusion, surgical approach, and intraoperative anesthetic medications. Tumor-related variables comprised clinical stage (I–IV), tumor location, and neoadjuvant treatment history. Postoperative ICU admission during hospitalization was also recorded. Laboratory data were obtained from tests performed 1–3 days before surgery, including hemoglobin, white blood cells (WBC), platelets, lymphocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, C-reactive protein (CRP), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine (Scr), electrolytes (sodium, potassium, calcium), and albumin levels. Derived hematologic ratios—platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), and prognostic nutritional index [PNI, calculated as albumin (g/L) + 5 × lymphocyte count (×109/L)]—were computed to assess systemic inflammation and nutritional status.

Variable preprocessing

Prior to preprocessing and model development, the dataset underwent standardized data cleaning procedures. Duplicate records were identified and removed when applicable. Variable coding and data formats were standardized across the dataset, and categorical variables were converted into factor format for analysis. Implausible or biologically inconsistent values were checked against source records whenever possible. Variables with excessive missingness (>20%) were excluded from subsequent analyses.

Following initial data cleaning, the cohort was split into training and internal test cohorts using stratified random sampling at a 7:3 ratio to preserve the distribution of POD events. The partition was generated using a prespecified random seed of 345, thereby preserving a similar outcome proportion in both cohorts and ensuring reproducibility. To minimize potential information leakage, the training cohort was used exclusively for imputation-model estimation, preprocessing, feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, threshold selection, and model fitting. The held-out internal test cohort was not used for any model-development decision and was reserved for final internal performance evaluation.

A comprehensive analysis of the missing-data pattern revealed that no candidate variable exceeded this 20% threshold (Table S1 and Figure S1). Missing values were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) after the training–test split (30). Continuous variables were imputed using predictive mean matching, whereas binary and multicategorical variables were imputed using logistic and multinomial regression, respectively. Five completed datasets were generated using 10 iterations per imputation. The training and internal test cohorts were imputed separately using the same variable-specific imputation methods. The imputation models for the training cohort were fitted using training data only, whereas separate imputation models were fitted within the internal test cohort. Thus, the internal test cohort did not influence imputation of the training data, model tuning, threshold selection, or model fitting. Nevertheless, because separate imputation models were fitted in the two cohorts, the imputed values reflected the covariate distributions and associations within each cohort. This limitation was addressed through an additional complete-case sensitivity analysis.

After imputation, continuous variables, such as age, laboratory results, and surgical duration, were standardized using means and standard deviations estimated from the training cohort, and categorical variables, such as comorbidities and medication use, were converted to binary indicators using training-derived one-hot encoding rules. These preprocessing parameters were applied unchanged to the internal test cohort.

Feature selection

To optimize clinical utility and ensure feature accessibility, feature selection was conducted using Boruta’s algorithm (31) and the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression (32). Boruta’s algorithm employed a robust iterative approach to identify significant predictors by systematically comparing the importance of original features against randomly permuted “shadow variables”. Features demonstrating statistically significant superiority over their shuffled counterparts were retained as critical predictors, whereas those lacking predictive relevance were excluded. To further mitigate multicollinearity and refine the feature subset, LASSO regression was applied. This method utilizes an L1 regularization penalty to constrain the absolute sum of regression coefficients below a predefined threshold, thereby compressing non-informative coefficients to zero. Variables retaining non-zero coefficients after regularization were selected as key predictors, effectively balancing model simplicity with predictive accuracy. The optimal penalty parameter (λ) was determined via ten-fold cross-validation, with λ.1se (one standard error from the minimum mean squared error) adopted to prioritize generalizability over minimal training error. The final feature set was derived from the intersection of variables identified by both Boruta and LASSO methodologies, ensuring a parsimonious yet robust predictor ensemble.

Model development and comparison

Prognostic prediction models for POD were developed using the selected variables and eight supervised ML algorithms: LR, random forest (RF), multilayer perceptron (MLP), categorical boosting (CatBoost), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), support vector machine (SVM), K-nearest neighbors (KNN), and light gradient boosting machine (LGBM) to predict POD. LR served as an interpretable reference model. The remaining algorithms represented complementary modeling families for structured clinical data. RF was included as a bagging-based tree ensemble, whereas CatBoost, XGBoost, and LGBM were selected for their ability to capture nonlinear associations, threshold effects, and interactions among perioperative predictors. The MLP represented a neural-network approach but was expected to be more sensitive to feature scaling, regularization, architecture, and sample size. SVM and KNN represented kernel-based and distance-based approaches, respectively. All algorithms were compared using the same training, cross-validation, and internal test framework to assess whether flexible nonlinear methods improved prediction beyond penalized regression while accounting for differences in complexity, calibration, and overfitting.

Hyperparameter optimization was conducted exclusively within the training cohort using grid search coupled with 5-fold cross-validation. The selected hyperparameters were subsequently used to refit each algorithm on the entire training cohort (details are provided in Table S2). Final model performance was evaluated in the held-out internal test cohort, which was not used for preprocessing, feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, threshold selection, or model fitting, thereby minimizing the risk of data leakage. To quantify statistical uncertainty, bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations was performed within the test cohort to estimate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for discrimination, calibration, and classification metrics.

Quantitative assessment of model performance was conducted using the following metrics, selected for their clinical relevance and alignment with established ML standards (33,34): area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC): quantifies the model’s discriminative capacity across classification thresholds; accuracy: represents the proportion of correctly classified instances relative to the total sample; precision: indicates the ratio of true positive predictions to all positive predictions; recall (sensitivity): measures the model’s ability to correctly identify true positive cases; specificity: reflects the model’s capacity to correctly recognize true negative cases; F1 score: harmonizes precision and recall into a single metric, calculated as the harmonic mean of the two; Brier score: evaluates the calibration of predicted probabilities, with lower values indicating superior probabilistic accuracy; log loss: assesses classifier performance by penalizing deviations between predicted probabilities and true class labels; calibration slope: evaluates how well predicted probabilities reflect actual outcomes, with a slope of 1 indicating perfect calibration; intercept-in-large: measures whether the average predicted probability matches the overall observed event rate, with a value of 0 implying perfect alignment between the mean prediction and the empirical event rate.

For threshold-dependent performance assessment, a model-specific classification threshold was selected by maximizing the Youden index. Threshold selection was based exclusively on pooled out-of-fold predicted probabilities from the training cohort (Table S3). This procedure identified the training-derived operating point with the greatest combined sensitivity and specificity while avoiding optimization using the internal test cohort. The selected threshold was subsequently applied unchanged to the internal test cohort to calculate accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 score, and other threshold-dependent measures.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the potential impact of missing-data handling on model performance, a complete-case sensitivity analysis was conducted. Complete cases were defined as patients with no missing values across any original variable in the raw dataset. The original training–internal test assignment established before imputation was retained, and patients with incomplete records were excluded separately from the training and internal test cohorts. No new random partition was performed in the complete-case sample. All eight ML algorithms were refitted in the complete-case training cohort using the hyperparameters and model-specific classification thresholds selected in the primary analysis, and were subsequently evaluated in the corresponding complete-case internal test cohort.

Model interpretability

SHAP was employed to enhance the interpretability of the ML model by quantifying the contribution of individual features to predictions of POD (35). Rooted in cooperative game theory, SHAP assigns each feature a Shapley value, representing its marginal impact on the model’s output. Features with larger absolute SHAP values were deemed more influential in predicting POD, with positive values indicating a propensity to increase POD risk and negative values suggesting protective effects. To elucidate global and instance-level decision-making, summary plots visualized the magnitude and directional influence of features across the cohort, while dependency plots revealed nonlinear interactions between pairs of variables. Additionally, waterfall plots for individual patients illustrated how specific feature values synergistically shifted the prediction toward higher POD risk. The baseline value in the SHAP waterfall plot represents the expected log-odds (margin) of the model prior to the logistic transformation into a probability.

Development of an online clinical web-tool

To bridge the gap between ML model development and bedside clinical application, the finalized CatBoost model was integrated into an interactive, open-access R web application built on the Shiny framework. The application features a user-friendly graphical interface that requires no programming expertise from clinicians. To facilitate rapid clinical assessment, the web tool provides automated risk stratification (high vs. low risk) based on an optimal probability threshold. In this study, the probability threshold was set at 28.5%, which was derived exclusively from pooled out-of-fold predictions within the training cohort by maximizing the Youden index. To ensure rigorous validation and prevent information leakage, the internal test cohort was strictly excluded from the threshold selection process.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2, https://www.r-project.org/) and SPSS statistical software (version 27.0, IBM Corp.). Continuous variables were assessed for normality via the Shapiro-Wilk test and reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) due to their non-normal distribution. Between-group comparisons of continuous variables were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and proportions (%) and analyzed with Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A two-tailed P value <0.05 was adopted to define statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 1,089 patients with esophageal cancer were included in the analysis (the full patient selection flow, including the exact number of patients excluded at each step and the specific reasons for exclusion, is detailed in Figure 1). The cohort had a median age of 70 years (IQR, 58–80 years). The majority of patients were male (75.9%) and received polypharmacy (55.8%). Clinical tumor stage at I–II comprised 86.2% of the cohort, and 65.1% of tumors were located in the middle thoracic region. Within the entire cohort, 370 patients (34.0%) developed POD.

To elucidate the clinical course of POD, we analyzed the temporal distribution of delirium onset among the 370 patients who developed POD. The period of greatest vulnerability occurred during the early postoperative phase, with most cases diagnosed between postoperative days 1 and 3. Specifically, 75.7% of cases occurred within the first 72 hours. The daily incidence is detailed in Table S4. Notably, because the study focused on a 7-day perioperative risk period, cases occurring beyond this observation window were not included.

The demographic and clinicopathological characteristics of all patients are detailed in Table 1. Significant differences were observed between patients who developed POD and those who did not in terms of age, smoking status, preoperative polypharmacy, intraoperative benzodiazepine usage, ASA classification, surgical approach, anesthesia duration, surgical duration, intraoperative blood transfusion, CRP level, albumin level, lymphocytes, PLR, NLR, MLR, and PNI (P<0.05).

Selection of predictor variables

To identify the optimal predictors for POD in patients undergoing esophagectomy, a dual-method feature selection approach integrating the Boruta algorithm and LASSO regression was implemented (Figure 2). The Boruta algorithm, illustrated in Figure 2A, assessed feature importance by comparing candidate variables with randomly permuted shadow features. This process identified ten significant predictors (in green): blood transfusion, NLR, surgical approach, ASA classification, PLR, PNI, preoperative polypharmacy, CRP, age, and MLR. Subsequently, LASSO regression analysis was performed to evaluate variable stability and importance through coefficient path trajectories across varying λ values (Figure 2B). Ten-fold cross-validation determined the optimal regularization parameter (λ.1se =0.032), yielding eight non-zero coefficients: preoperative polypharmacy, ASA classification, surgical approach, blood transfusion, CRP, age, MLR, and PNI (Figure 2C). Intersection analysis of the results from both methods revealed eight consensus predictors (Figure 2D): preoperative polypharmacy, ASA classification, surgical approach, blood transfusion, CRP, age, MLR, and PNI. These were retained for subsequent model development.

Model development and performance comparison

Hyperparameter tuning for each ML model was conducted via grid search with 5-fold cross-validation in the training cohort. Among the eight algorithms, CatBoost showed the numerically highest discrimination in both cohorts. In the training cohort, CatBoost achieved an AUC of 0.936 (95% CI: 0.919–0.954), compared with 0.930 (95% CI: 0.912–0.948) for XGBoost and 0.929 (95% CI: 0.911–0.947) for LightGBM (Figures 3A; Table S5). In the internal test cohort, CatBoost maintained the numerically highest discriminative performance with an AUC of 0.930 (95% CI: 0.900–0.959) (Figures 3B,4A; Table S5). At the predefined probability threshold of 28.5%, the CatBoost model achieved a recall of 0.862 (95% CI: 0.800–0.919) and a specificity of 0.866 (95% CI: 0.820–0.908) in the internal test cohort. Its accuracy was 86.50% (95% CI: 82.52–89.88%), precision was 0.764 (95% CI: 0.687–0.835), and F1 score was 0.810 (95% CI: 0.752–0.861). The corresponding log loss was 0.313 (95% CI: 0.253–0.380) (Figure 3B and Table S5).

Calibration was assessed using the Brier score, calibration slope, calibration intercept, and calibration plot. In the internal test cohort, CatBoost had the lowest Brier score among the evaluated models, at 0.095 (95% CI: 0.074–0.118). The calibration slope was 0.981 (95% CI: 0.807–1.259), close to the ideal value of 1, and the calibration intercept was 0.081 (95% CI: −0.273 to 0.421), close to the ideal value of 0 (Figure 3B and Table S5). Visual assessment of the calibration curve also showed generally close agreement between predicted and observed POD risks, although the uncertainty in the calibration estimates should be considered when interpreting individual risk predictions (Figure 4B).

Finally, decision curve analysis (DCA) revealed that the CAT model provided greater net benefit than the treat-all and treat-none strategies across a broad range of evaluated threshold probabilities in both the training and test sets (Figure 4C). Collectively, these findings supported the selection of CatBoost as the final model for further evaluation and implementation in the online risk calculator.

Complete-case sensitivity analysis

To assess whether the primary findings were sensitive to the approach used for handling missing data, a complete-case sensitivity analysis was performed. A total of 482 patients with no missing values across any original variable were retained, comprising 326 patients in the training cohort and 156 in the internal test cohort. As shown in Table S6, discrimination remained generally strong across all eight algorithms, although the relative ranking of the models differed slightly from the primary multiple-imputation analysis. In the complete-case internal test cohort, KNN achieved the numerically highest AUC of 0.913 (95% CI: 0.867–0.958), whereas CatBoost achieved an AUC of 0.910 (95% CI: 0.861–0.959). Using the CatBoost-specific threshold of 0.285 derived from the primary analysis, CatBoost yielded a sensitivity of 0.827, a specificity of 0.875, an accuracy of 85.90%, a precision of 0.768, and an F1 score of 0.796. These findings indicate that the model retained a reasonably balanced threshold-dependent performance despite the reduced complete-case sample size. Furthermore, CatBoost demonstrated a Brier score of 0.109, a calibration slope of 0.883, and a calibration intercept of 0.242 in the complete-case internal test cohort. These estimates suggest broadly acceptable, though imperfect, calibration and should be interpreted cautiously in view of their uncertainty and the smaller sample size. Although the relative model rankings shifted slightly, the overall predictive performance remained robust, confirming that the main conclusions were not substantially dependent on the missing-data handling strategy.

Feature importance was further visualized using a summary plot (Figure 5B), where each point represents an individual patient sample. To delineate the additive effects of predictors on specific cases, SHAP waterfall plots were generated (Figure 5C,5D). Bar widths in both plots correspond to the absolute magnitude of each feature’s contribution, with yellow and red hues denoting positive and negative directional effects, respectively. In Figure 5C, representing a POD-positive prediction, MLR at 1.51 (SHAP =+3.64), age at 73 years (SHAP =+0.961), PNI at 35 (SHAP =+0.847), the presence of preoperative polypharmacy (SHAP =+0.769), CRP (≥5 mg/L) (SHAP =+0.624), and receiving intraoperative blood transfusion (SHAP =+0.515) collectively yielded a final predicted log-odds (margin) of 7.35 relative to a baseline risk of 0. Conversely, Figure 5D demonstrated a clinically representative POD-negative prediction, dominated by substantial negative (risk-reducing) contributions from age at 50 years (SHAP =−1.63), MLR at 0.31 (SHAP =−1.02), ASA classification at I (SHAP =−0.776), CRP (<5 mg/L) (SHAP =−0.684), and the absence of preoperative polypharmacy (SHAP =−0.442).

Deployment of the online risk calculator

To facilitate clinical implementation, an interactive web-based risk calculator based on the optimal CatBoost model was developed and deployed (https://machine-learning-pod.shinyapps.io/pod-prediction-calculator/). As illustrated in Figure 6, clinicians can input eight routinely collected preoperative and intraoperative variables through a user-friendly interface to instantly generate a patient’s personalized predicted probability of developing POD after esophagectomy. Crucially, the calculator automatically classifies patients into high- or low-risk groups using the training-derived probability threshold of 28.5%. This web-based tool enables rapid risk stratification at the bedside without manual calculation or local software installation, thereby enhancing the clinical applicability of the proposed model.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically developed and compared eight widely used ML models to identify an optimal prognostic prediction model for POD risk stratification in patients undergoing esophagectomy. Among the evaluated models, CatBoost demonstrated the numerically highest overall performance, with favorable discrimination, calibration, and threshold-dependent classification characteristics in both the training and internal test cohorts. Using eight routinely available clinical variables, the final model provided individualized estimates of POD risk and identified MLR, age, CRP, preoperative polypharmacy, and PNI as the most influential predictors. To our knowledge, this represents one of the first interpretable ML-based predictive frameworks specifically developed for POD risk estimation after esophagectomy using perioperative clinical features. Furthermore, SHAP analysis enhanced model interpretability by providing both global feature importance rankings and individualized explanations of risk contribution. These findings suggest that the CatBoost model may serve as a potential perioperative risk-stratification tool to help identify patients who may benefit from enhanced delirium-prevention strategies and closer postoperative monitoring.

The incidence of POD in this cohort was 34.0% among patients undergoing esophagectomy, aligning with prior reports (range: 9.2–50%) in elderly and high-risk surgical populations (7). These findings further highlight the clinical significance of POD as a critical complication following esophagectomy. Although several scoring systems and mental status evaluation tools have been proposed for POD risk assessment (36,37), their application in routine clinical practice remains challenging due to variable predictive performance and limited validation across different populations (38). Recent studies have attempted to improve POD prediction by integrating clinical and laboratory variables. For example, Chen et al. developed a nomogram incorporating six predictors for POD after esophagectomy in 924 patients with esophageal cancer, achieving an AUC of 0.832 (15). Similarly, Shen et al. validated a nomogram in 396 patients and reported AUC values ranging from 0.871 to 0.911 (14). However, these approaches primarily relied on traditional LR models, which may have limitations in capturing nonlinear relationships and complex interactions among clinical variables. In contrast, ML algorithms can identify multidimensional patterns within heterogeneous clinical data and have shown potential advantages in delirium prediction across different surgical populations (39). Nevertheless, ML-based POD prediction frameworks specifically developed for patients undergoing esophagectomy remain insufficiently explored, representing an important unmet need in perioperative risk assessment.

To address this critical gap, our study developed and internally validated interpretable ML prognostic models for predicting POD in esophageal cancer patients following esophagectomy. Among the eight evaluated ML algorithms, the CatBoost model demonstrated the numerically highest discrimination in both the training and internal test cohorts, achieving an AUC of 0.930 in the internal test cohort. Leveraging its unique ordered boosting strategy and symmetric tree structure, the CatBoost algorithm inherently mitigates overfitting while optimizing feature interactions (40). The final model was therefore selected based on its overall performance profile, including discrimination, threshold-dependent classification performance, calibration, and decision-curve analysis. From a clinical perspective, the intended role of the CatBoost model is perioperative risk stratification rather than diagnosis of POD. At the training-derived probability threshold of 28.5%, selected by maximizing the Youden index using pooled out-of-fold predictions, the model achieved a sensitivity of 0.862 and a specificity of 0.866 in the internal test cohort. This balance may allow clinicians to identify patients who are more likely to benefit from enhanced delirium-prevention strategies and closer postoperative surveillance while limiting unnecessary allocation of additional monitoring resources to lower-risk patients. Nevertheless, the optimal balance between false-negative and false-positive classifications may vary according to institutional resources, clinical priorities, and the consequences associated with missed high-risk patients.

Beyond discrimination, reliable probability estimation is essential when a prediction model is intended to support individualized risk assessment. The CatBoost model demonstrated favorable calibration characteristics, including a Brier score of 0.095, a calibration slope close to 1, and an intercept-in-large close to 0 in the internal test cohort. These findings suggest good agreement between predicted and observed POD risks within this study population. However, calibration performance may change when applied to populations with different patient characteristics, surgical practices, or baseline POD incidence; therefore, external validation and potential recalibration will be necessary before broader clinical application. DCA further suggested that the CatBoost model could provide greater net benefit than treating all patients uniformly or applying no predictive strategy across the evaluated threshold range. This finding supports the potential clinical utility of the model as a risk-stratification tool, particularly when predictions are used to inform preventive measures and monitoring intensity. Importantly, the present study did not evaluate whether model-guided interventions improve clinical outcomes, and prospective clinical-impact studies are required to determine whether implementation translates into reduced POD incidence or improved patient recovery.

ML technology is frequently perceived as a “black box” due to its opaque prediction mechanisms and limited interpretability. Consequently, elucidating the decision-making processes of ML models is critical for fostering clinicians’ trust and identifying potential biases—an imperative addressed in this study through the implementation of SHAP, a method offering both global and local model explanations (41). Globally, the SHAP summary plot delineates the aggregate influence of each feature on model predictions. In our analysis, the most significant predictors of POD included age, preoperative polypharmacy, ASA classification, surgical approach, blood transfusion, CRP, MLR, and PNI. The predictors retained in the final model were clinically coherent and represented complementary pathways implicated in POD. Advanced age, higher ASA classification, and preoperative polypharmacy may reflect reduced physiological reserve, multimorbidity, and greater vulnerability of the aging brain to perioperative stress (42-44). Polypharmacy may additionally increase exposure to sedative or anticholinergic medications and clinically important drug–drug interactions (45).

Surgical approach and intraoperative blood transfusion may capture the magnitude and complexity of operative stress. Open esophagectomy generally entails greater tissue injury and inflammatory activation than minimally invasive surgery (46), whereas transfusion may serve as a marker of blood loss, operative complexity, impaired oxygen delivery, and transfusion-related inflammatory responses (7). These factors may amplify systemic and neuroinflammatory pathways involved in delirium (15,47). The inflammatory and nutritional markers were particularly relevant in patients undergoing esophagectomy. Esophageal cancer is commonly accompanied by tumor-related inflammation, dysphagia, weight loss, and impaired nutritional reserve. CRP reflects systemic inflammatory activity (48), whereas the MLR represents the balance between innate inflammatory activation and adaptive immune competence (49). Elevated inflammatory activity may promote endothelial dysfunction, blood–brain barrier disruption, and neuroinflammatory signaling (50). The PNI combines serum albumin and lymphocyte count and therefore captures both nutritional and immune status. A low PNI may indicate malnutrition, impaired immune competence, and reduced capacity to tolerate major thoracoabdominal surgery (29,51,52). The combined influence of age-related vulnerability, inflammation, malnutrition, and surgical stress provides a biologically plausible explanation for the contribution of these predictors to POD risk after esophagectomy.

To assess the robustness of our findings to missing-data handling, we conducted an all-variable complete-case sensitivity analysis. Although the relative ranking of the algorithms changed, overall predictive performance remained broadly comparable between the primary multiple-imputation analysis and the smaller complete-case cohort. CatBoost achieved an AUC of 0.930 in the primary analysis and 0.910 in the complete-case internal test cohort. At the previously established threshold of 0.285, it retained a sensitivity of 0.827 and a specificity of 0.875. The modest differences between the two analyses may reflect the reduced complete-case sample size, changes in cohort composition, and uncertainty associated with both imputation and complete-case selection. Overall, these findings suggest that the principal conclusions were not substantially dependent on the missing-data handling strategy, although residual uncertainty related to the missing-data mechanism cannot be excluded. Furthermore, to facilitate patient-level application, we developed an open-access interactive web-based risk calculator integrating the final CatBoost model. The calculator provides individualized POD probabilities and corresponding risk classifications using the training-derived threshold of 28.5%, allowing clinicians without programming expertise to apply the model in practice. The tool is intended to support, rather than replace, clinical judgment. Patients identified as higher risk may be considered for enhanced multicomponent delirium-prevention approaches, including optimization of pain management, sleep and environmental interventions, early mobilization, and closer postoperative monitoring. However, decisions regarding additional specialist involvement, such as geriatric or psychiatric consultation, should remain based on comprehensive clinical assessment rather than model output alone.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective, single-center design and limited sample size may restrict model transportability. Although regularization, cross-validation, out-of-fold prediction, and held-out internal evaluation were used, residual optimism cannot be excluded; therefore, the web calculator should be regarded as a research prototype pending prospective multicenter external validation, recalibration, and clinical-impact assessment. Second, several potentially important predictors such as standardized baseline cognitive assessments were unavailable, while postoperative variables such as major complications were excluded to preserve the intended prediction time point and avoid temporal leakage. Third, MICE was performed after cohort splitting, with the training and internal test cohorts imputed separately. Although complete-case sensitivity analyses showed consistent results, uncertainty related to the missing-data mechanism and imputation assumptions remains. Fourth, POD was assessed only during the first 7 postoperative days or until discharge. Delirium occurring after discharge or beyond this period may have been missed, and differences in surveillance intensity may have influenced detection. Fifth, internal validation was based on a single training-testing split, and performance estimates may be sensitive to the specific partition. Although bootstrap resampling was used to quantify uncertainty, external validation in independent cohorts remains necessary. Finally, this model was developed for prediction rather than causal inference. Associations involving intraoperative medications, transfusion, surgical approach, and other perioperative factors should therefore be interpreted as predictive rather than causal and do not imply that modifying these factors would directly reduce POD incidence.

Conclusions

This study developed and internally validated interpretable machine-learning prognostic models for estimating the risk of POD within 7 days after esophagectomy. Among the evaluated algorithms, CatBoost showed the best overall internal performance, with favorable discrimination and calibration. The resulting online calculator integrates routinely available perioperative variables and may support individualized risk stratification at the completion of surgery. However, these findings remain preliminary. The model was developed and internally evaluated in a retrospective single-center cohort, without external validation, and the available development sample was smaller than the theoretical minimum estimated using all candidate predictor parameters. The web calculator should therefore be regarded as a research prototype rather than a tool ready for routine clinical use. Prospective multicenter external validation, recalibration where necessary, and formal clinical impact assessment are required before implementation.
