 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

 The Gulf Coastal Plain of Alabama is a region with proven geologic carbon storage
potential (e.g., Southeastern Regional Carbon Sequestration Partnership Phase III
Anthropogenic Test and Phase II Plant Daniel Test, Citronelle Enhanced Oil recovery test)
and current interest in carbon capture and storage hub development. For over 20 years, the
Geological Survey of Alabama (GSA) has been involved in carbon capture, use, and storage
(CCUS) research, resulting in the accumulation of a significant amount of CCUS relevant
geologic and geophysical data in the GSA records. Additionally, the State Oil and Gas Board
of Alabama (OGB) holds large amounts of data relating to existing oil and gas wells and
other infrastructure in the region. Much of the OGB data are available online but not all of
them are machine-actionable; most of the GSA data are either not available online or are in
multiple databases, which may include duplicate information. The goal of the Alabama
Carbon Storage: Data Sharing and Engagement project was to make existing CCUS-related
data publicly available and to fill in data gaps where possible. To assist the CCUS industry in
site selection and planning and to improve transparency for communities potentially
impacted by CCUS development, geologic data relating to stratigraphy, structure, and
reservoir characteristics; existing boreholes, water wells, and oil and gas wells; and digital
geophysical well logs were collated and standardized. Data gaps were also addressed. Legacy
data, mostly from CCUS research projects, were compiled into a single database.
Stratigraphic tops picks were defined and standardized across the study area, and structural
and reservoir characterization data were checked for duplications. Pre-existing Log ASCII
Standard (LAS) files for geophysical well logs were mapped, new stratigraphic and structural
data and LAS files were created to fill geographic data gaps, and additional core analyses
were digitized to supplement the dataset. The aim of the data collection and curation was to
create more machine-actionable data than was previously available. In addition to the data
products, links to other websites with relevant information (e.g., National Pipeline Mapping
System, U.S. Environmental Protection Agency Class VI Underground Injection Control
regulations) were compiled and educational resources were created. All the data products,
resources, and links are available on the project website.

 INTRODUCTION

 The deep subsurface of the Gulf Coastal Plain of Alabama has been actively drilled
since oil was first discovered in Alabama in 1944 (Pashin and others, 2000). Oil and gas are
produced from Upper Jurassic- through Cretaceous-aged formations, as well as shallow
Miocene-aged reservoirs (Figs. 1, 2). The Norphlet Formation, Smackover Formation, and
Miocene undifferentiated produce oil and gas both onshore and offshore (state and federal
waters). The Sligo Formation, Rodessa Formation (Donovan sandstone), Paluxy Formation,
Washita and Fredericksburg Groups undifferentiated, lower Tuscaloosa Group, Eutaw
Formation, and Selma Group are all petroleum producers in the study area. Additionally,
several geologic units are used for produced water disposal. Proven injection zones include
the Paluxy Formation, Washita and Fredericksburg Groups undifferentiated, lower
Tuscaloosa Group, and Wilcox Group.

 Due to the number of potential carbon dioxide (CO2) injection zones and the
industrial corridor just north of Mobile, southwestern Alabama has been the focus of several
carbon capture, use, and storage (CCUS) research projects for over 20 years. These projects
include early assessments of potential, part of Southeastern Regional Carbon Sequestration
Partnership (SECARB) Phase I (Pashin and Payton, 2005), offshore assessments such as
Southeast Offshore Storage Resource Assessment (SOSRA) and SECARB-Offshore (Gray
and others, 2018; Wernette and others, 2022), and two U.S. Department of Energy funded
CCUS demonstration projects (Koperna and others, 2012; Walsh and others, 2014). These
projects have demonstrated the suitability of the geology in southwestern Alabama to store
CO2.

 The Geological Survey of Alabama (GSA) holds CCUS relevant data, not only from
the 20 years of work on the topic but from the entire 175-year history of the agency. The
State Oil and Gas Board of Alabama (OGB) maintains records on all petroleum wells drilled
since 1945, including geophysical well logs, core analyses, location data, and completion
information. Additionally, the OGB maintains records for a number of petroleum exploration
wells drilled before its founding. In making this information more readily available, the
Alabama Carbon Storage: Data Sharing and Engagement (ACS:DSE) project will help the
development of CCUS projects in the study area.

 1

 PURPOSE AND SCOPE

 This report is the final technical report on ACS:DSE project activities. The goals of
the ACS:DSE project were to compile geologic, geophysical, infrastructure, and other
relevant CCUS datasets for the study area and develop a geologic model of the study area;
develop an online platform to serve data to stakeholders; engage with the public, students,
and industry to educate them about CCUS and the data platform; and ensure energy and
environmental justice is central to all aspects of the project. Datasets compiled and expanded
include formation depths and elevations, digital geophysical well logs, reservoir properties,
geologic structures, and geologic models. The geologic data were used to create a three-
dimensional geologic model, structure grids, structure contour maps, and fault trace maps. In
addition to downloadable datasets, links to CCUS relevant regulatory agencies (e.g., OGB,
U.S. Environmental Protection Agency) and sources for infrastructure and educational
information were included on the website. Educational materials on CCUS for use by K-12
teachers were produced as part of the ACS:DSE project. A website
(https://www.gsa.state.al.us/gsa/energy/carbonstorage) was developed to deliver the data,
model products, education materials, and links to the public.

 GEOLOGIC BACKGROUND

 STRATIGRAPHY

 The study area lies within the East Gulf Coastal Plain physiographic section of
Alabama (Fig. 1) and adjacent offshore areas, and forms part of the Gulf of Mexico
Sedimentary Basin. This region encompasses the eastern Mississippi Interior Salt Basin and
several structural highs, including the Wiggins arch, Conecuh ridge, and Choctaw uplift, as
well as associated faults and folds (Figs. 2, 3). The area is underlain by a thick succession of
Mesozoic and Cenozoic sedimentary rocks (Fig. 3; Szabo and others, 1988). Upper Triassic
through Lower Cretaceous strata are confined to the subsurface, whereas Upper Cretaceous
through Holocene strata crop out in a series of arcuate, belts that trend west-east to
northwest-southeast across Alabama and dip gently south-southwest (Fig. 4; Szabo and
others, 1988). The Mesozoic succession represents the primary interval of interest for CCUS
due to its substantial thickness, lateral continuity, reservoir qualities, and favorable burial
depth for long-term CO₂ storage.

 TRIASSIC - JURASSIC STRATIGRAPHY

 The Upper Triassic Eagle Mills Formation is the oldest Mesozoic unit in the study
area and has limited lateral extent. The formation consists of red beds, sandstones, and
conglomerates deposited in grabens formed during continental rifting in the late Triassic
(Mink and others, 1990).

 The Middle Jurassic Werner Formation lies unconformably on the Eagle Mills
Formation and consists of anhydrite and conglomerates. The Werner Formation has a varying
thickness with unknown lateral extent. In some areas the upper anhydrite intertongues with
the overlying Louann Salt. The Louann Salt overlies the Paleozoic rocks of the Appalachian–
Ouachita orogenic belt, older Jurassic units, and crystalline basement rock and marks a
transition to passive-margin sedimentation. Salt thickness varies from absent where
sediments are welded to basement, to over 1,000 feet in broad salt pillows, and to more than
10,000 feet in diapirs, reflecting regional salt tectonics (Hughes, 1968).

 The Upper Jurassic formations include the Norphlet Formation (continental clastics)
and the Smackover Formation (marine carbonates). In the southwestern part of the study
area, these units occur at depths exceeding 10,000 feet below sea level (Fig. 5). Net sand
thickness in the Norphlet Formation exceeds 500 feet in the Mississippi Interior Salt Basin
but thins or is absent along regional ridge complexes, whereas the Smackover Formation
thickness ranges from less than 1 foot over portions of the ridge complexes to more than
1,000 feet in offshore areas (Tolson and others, 1983; Mink and others, 1985; Mink and
others, 1990).

 Overlying the Smackover Formation, the Haynesville Formation consists of the
Buckner Anhydrite Member and an uppermost sequence of interbedded siliciclastic strata
containing minor anhydrite as distinct beds or as an accessory mineral. The Buckner
Anhydrite Member has a maximum thickness of approximately 660 feet in the Mississippi
Interior Salt Basin but thins along the crest of the Wiggins and Conecuh ridges and is absent
in up-dip areas. Excluding the Buckner Anhydrite Member, the Haynesville Formation
thickness ranges from 80 feet in northern Choctaw County to 2,500 feet in the Mississippi
Interior Salt Basin (Tolson and others, 1983). Oil is produced from the Frisco City sand, an

 LOWER CRETACEOUS STRATIGRAPHY

 The Lower Cretaceous section in the study area includes, in ascending order, the
Hosston Formation, the Sligo Formation, the Pine Island Formation, the Donovan sandstone
(an informal unit), the Rodessa Formation, the Ferry Lake Anhydrite, the Mooringsport
Formation, the Paluxy Formation, and the Washita and Fredericksburg Groups
undifferentiated. In the northern and eastern parts of the study area, these units are not
differentiated and are collectively referred to as the Lower Cretaceous undifferentiated.

 The Hosston Formation is a siliciclastic sequence with a maximum thickness of 1,650
feet in Mobile and Baldwin Counties. Overlying the Hosston Formation, the Sligo Formation
has a maximum thickness of about 540 feet and consists of very fine- to coarse-grained,
calcareous quartzose sandstone; mottled, fine-crystalline limestone; shale; shales and
limestones become more common to the south (Raymond and others, 1988). The thin Pine
Island Formation consists predominantly of shale.

 The Donovan Sandstone has traditionally been assigned to the Rodessa Formation
(Eaves, 1976). The Donovan Sandstone consists of three intervals: (1) a lowermost unit of
approximately 400 feet of fining and thinning upward sequence of sandstone, conglomerate,
and mudstone; (2) a middle interval less than 200 feet thick of interbedded sandstone and
mudstone; and (3) an uppermost sandstone about 500 feet thick with high oil saturation. The
lowermost interval is saturated with hypersaline waters and lacks economically significant oil
resources (Pashin and others, 2008). Southward, Donovan sandstones transition to a thick
carbonate sequence (Petty, 1999). In the Citronelle field, 52 separate sandstone zones in 330
separate reservoirs produce from the Donovan Sandstone through Sligo Formation interval
(Eaves, 1976). These intervals are discontinuous, reflecting the fluvial character of the
formation (Eaves, 1976).

 Overlying the Rodessa Formation, the Ferry Lake Anhydrite consists primarily of
anhydrite, shale, and limestone and forms a major regional seal for hydrocarbon reservoirs in
the Donovan Sandstone through Sligo Formation interval (Nunnally and Fowler, 1954;
Raymond and others, 1988). In southwest Alabama, the formation reaches a depth of around

 8

10,500 feet with a maximum thickness of 240 feet. The unit shallows to the north and thins to
22 feet across the Citronelle dome (Raymond, 1995).

 Overlying the Ferry Lake Anhydrite, the Mooringsport Formation has a maximum
thickness of approximately 300 feet and thins to about 170 feet near the Florida state line
(Raymond, 1995). In up-dip areas, the Mooringsport Formation consists predominantly of
shale with fossiliferous limestone interbeds in the lower part. Northward, the unit loses its
distinctive lithologic character and cannot be distinguished from the overlying Paluxy
Formation.

 The Paluxy Formation has a maximum thickness of 1,250 feet and consists of
interbedded fine- to medium-grained sandstone, shale, mudstone, and limestone, with the
upper part containing coarse conglomeratic sandstone (Dinkins, 1969). Oil is produced
locally from the Paluxy Formation, with net sandstone thickness ranging from 50 to 750 feet
(Pashin and others, 2008).

 The Washita and Fredericksburg Groups undifferentiated reaches a maximum
thickness of 2,100 feet thick in southwest Alabama, with net porous sand thickness
exceeding 1000 feet in Mobile and Baldwin Counties (Fig. 5). The unit consists of locally
very fine- to medium-grained quartzose sandstone interbedded with shale and limestone
(Raymond and others, 1988). Similar to other Lower Cretaceous units in the region, the unit
loses its distinctive lithologic character towards the up-dip limits to the north and east.
However, the presence of limestone provides a key diagnostic feature for distinguishing the
unit from the underlying Paluxy Formation. The uppermost interval of sandstone, limestone,
and shale has been designated as the Dantzler Formation (Hazzard and others, 1947).

Formation is about 250 feet thick, consisting of shale interbedded with lesser amounts of
glauconitic sandstone. The Eutaw Formation offers limited CCUS potential relative to the
upper Tuscaloosa Group.

 The Selma Group has a maximum thickness of about 1,800 feet and is dominated by
chalk. Chalk has exceptionally low permeability, providing an effective seal, although
fractures and faults may enhance local permeability. Notably, the first commercial oil
discovery in Alabama was produced from fractured chalk reservoirs (Current, 1948; Pashin
and others, 2000).

 CENOZOIC STRATIGRAPHY

 The Paleogene through Holocene section has a maximum thickness of 6,000 feet and
consists of shale, sandstone, and limestone. Major units include the Midway Group, Wilcox
Group, Claiborne Group, Jackson Group, Vicksburg Group, Chickasawhay Limestone,
Paynes Hammock Sand, Miocene undifferentiated, and Citronelle Formation. Onshore,
Cenozoic sands are above the primary interval of interest for CCUS; offshore, however,
deeper sands in both state and federal waters may provide potential storage targets (Hills and
Pashin, 2010; Gray and others, 2018).

 TECTONIC SETTING

 The Mississippi Interior Salt Basin is a sub-basin of the Gulf of Mexico Sedimentary
Basin that is floored by the Jurassic-aged Louann Salt. Salt movement, driven primarily by
sediment loading, determines the structural styles of the basin. Only one salt dome (McIntosh
dome) penetrates the overlying strata within the Alabama portion of the Mississippi Interior
Salt Basin; salt pillows underlie several domes (Hatchetigbee anticline, Chatom dome, and
Citronelle dome) containing significant oil and gas fields in the basin (Mink and others,
1990).

 The Mississippi Interior Salt Basin is bounded to the north and east by a peripheral
fault system, including the Gilbertown fault system, the West Bend fault system, and the
Mobile graben, and to the south by a Paleozoic basement high, the Wiggins arch (Fig. 2).

 11

Southeast of the Mississippi Interior Salt Basin, the peripheral fault system in Alabama
includes the Pollard fault system. Some peripheral faults are basement involved (Guthrie and
Raymond, 1992) and some are salt-detached. The salt-detached faults generally show
synsedimentary growth in the Cretaceous section, and the hanging-wall blocks of major
faults are often welded to the subsalt section, while the footwalls often have salt-cored uplifts
adjacent to the faults (Pashin and others, 2008). The three-way closures formed by the uplifts
and faults are sites of oil and gas accumulations.

 Basement structures, the Wiggins arch, the Baldwin high, and the Conecuh ridge
complex, appear to form a series of horsts and grabens (fig. 2) deep in the subsurface. These
structures were likely formed during early Mesozoic rifting (Guthrie and Raymond, 1992).
Jurassic and Lower Cretaceous formations are typically thinner or absent over the basement
highs.

 PROJECT METHODOLOGY AND OUTCOMES

 The ACS:DSE project objectives included compiling CCUS geologic, geophysical,
and infrastructure data; constructing a geologic model of the study area based on these data;
developing an online platform to serve relevant data to stakeholders; and engaging with the
public, students, and industry. Compiled geologic and geophysical data include newly
digitized geophysical well logs, stratigraphic and structural data, and reservoir
characteristics. The geologic and geophysical data were used to construct a structural model
of the study area in S&P Global’s Petra (v.3.15.3) geological mapping software. Both the
compiled data and products from the model are available on the online platform designed for
the project.

 RESERVOIR PROPERTY DATA

 Reservoir porosity and permeability data are available from the OGB as scanned core
analysis reports. These reports were copied into a spreadsheet in legacy projects primarily by
manually re-typing the data; additional reports were digitized using optical character
recognition for the ACS:DSE. For wells with sufficient stratigraphic data, formation average
(arithmetic mean) porosity and permeability were calculated. A total of 6,114 porosity and
permeability measurements from 170 wells were added to the database (Fig. 9). All reservoir
property data (individual measurements and formation averages) are available on the project
website.

 GEOLOGIC MODEL CONSTRUCTION

 The well information, stratigraphic and structural data, reservoir property data, and
LAS files were loaded into Petra to create a three-dimensional structural model of the study
area. Structure contour maps and bubble maps were used to check datum elevations and

 16

stratigraphic tops and to delineate fault traces for different formations (Figs. 10, 11; Table 2).
The stratigraphic data and fault traces were used to produce structure contour grids and
structure contour maps in the Petra Map Module using the “simple weighting without slopes
(moving averages)” algorithm. Structure contour maps based on the grids were used to create
ArcGIS shapefiles and PDF files. Shapefile contours are based purely on the mathematical
grid; however, the PDF version of structure contour maps may be edited for geologic
reasonableness near fault tips and in areas of sparse well control. Structure grids, structure
contour and fault trace shapefiles, and other maps (PDF files) are available on the project
website.

 INFRASTRUCTURE AND DEVELOPMENT DATA

A variety of information on infrastructure and regulations are needed for a successful CCUS
project. While only some of these data are available from the GSA and OGB, links to
appropriate data sources are provided on the project website. The locations of pre-existing
wells (petroleum and potable water) are important considerations for CCUS projects. Two
databases available on the OGB (petroleum) and GSA (potable water) websites provide much
of the information needed for a commercial CCUS project. The locations of existing
pipelines and their associated rights-of-way are also key to project success; a link to the
national pipeline mapping system is included on the website. In Alabama, pore space
ownership belongs to the surface property owner. Each county tax assessor’s office maintains
a website listing ownership information; a link to the Alabama Department of Revenue
aggregate list of these county websites is available on the project website. Compliance with
the OGB and U.S. Environmental Protection Agency Class IV Underground Injection
Control regulations is also key to project development; links to both are included on the
project website.

 The data correlated, updated, and created for the ACS:DSE project will help groups
interested in CCUS in Alabama. The stratigraphic, structural, and reservoir property data will
help industry determine the suitability of an area for a CCUS project. This data along with
the additional LAS files can speed the development of structural and reservoir models for a

identification of porous zones in a well. While the data may benefit the CCUS industry,
making them available may also help build public trust by increasing transparency and
making it easier for concerned groups to consult independent experts. Educational resources
will help disseminate information about CCUS, particularly in Alabama. Outside of the

 NEXT STEPS

 Beyond award DE-FE0032373, the GSA plans to continue adding data and expanding
the scope of the datasets and geographic area covered as funding and time allow. Future
updates may include additional data, such as bottom-hole temperature, salinity, and pressure,
as these data become available. Over time, the study area is expected to cover more areas of
Alabama. As new projects generate new stratigraphic and structural data, datasets on the
website and the GIS web viewer will be updated accordingly. Expanding both geographic
coverage and the datasets will support not only the CCUS industry, but also other geologic
efforts.
