Strictly localized orbitals from spatial partitioning with the discontinuous Galerkin method
César Feniou, Julien Toulouse

Abstract
We present a rigorous electronic-structure theory of strictly localized orbitals associated with a spatial partition of the one-electron Hilbert space that remains well defined in the complete basis-set limit. Each strictly localized orbital is supported on a spatial domain and may be discontinuous at domain interfaces. Using the interior-penalty discontinuous Galerkin method, these strictly localized orbitals can be employed in variational electronic-structure calculations despite their discontinuities at domain interfaces. As a proof of concept, we present numerical illustrations on one-dimensional diatomic model systems. They show that variational calculations can be carried out in a basis of strictly localized orbitals while maintaining good agreement with conventional calculations. Moreover, these strictly localized orbitals naturally lead to chemically intuitive representations of many-electron wave functions in the spirit of valence-bond theory.



I
Introduction

Orbitals play a central role in quantum chemistry, providing a practical representation of many-electron wave functions and enabling chemically intuitive descriptions of electronic structures. However, orbitals are not uniquely defined. In fact, any invertible transformation of the orbitals leaves a full-configuration-interaction wave function invariant, leading to a wide range of possible definitions of orbitals with distinct computational and interpretational properties. Among these, localized orbitals (see, e.g., Ref.
BenEvaLeiAnd-INC-21
) are particularly valuable, as they offer a compact and chemically transparent picture of bonding while also improving computational efficiency through sparsity and reduced entanglement.

A variety of schemes to define localized orbitals have been developed over the years (see, e.g., Ref.
HoyJor-CR-16
). A first family of schemes consists in variational optimization of localization functionals. In this family, the three most used schemes are the one of Boys which minimizes the sum of the orbital spreads
Boy-RMP-60
, the one of Edmiston and Ruedenberg which maximizes the sum of the orbital self-repulsion energies
EdmRue-RMP-63
, and the one of Pipek and Mezey which maximizes the sum of orbital partial charges on nuclei
PipMez-JCP-89
. For periodic solids, the equivalent of the Boys localized orbitals are called maximally localized Wannier functions
MarMosYatSouVan-RMP-12
. A second family of schemes consists in starting from a set of localized orbitals that do not minimize the energy and find a small transformation of these orbitals that minimizes the energy
Dau-CPL-74
;
RubPovMalRei-JCP-97
;
LiLiSuoLiu-ACR-14
. A third family of schemes consists in defining nonorthogonal localized orbitals by expanding them on localized basis functions, such as Gaussian-type orbital (GTO) basis functions, centered on a single atom or a limited set of atoms of the molecule
StoWagPre-TCA-80
;
KhaHeaBel-JCP-06
;
SirGenCivPieGhi-TCA-07
(see also Ref.
MccFauZhuOgoQiuWhiRabLin-NJP-20
;
FauWuLin-RMS-22
). This last family of nonorthogonal localized orbitals are used in particular in classical valence-bond (VB) theory
ShaHib-BOOK-08
but they have the serious drawback that their definition is tied to the basis set and tend to lose their meaning for large basis sets.

None of the previously mentioned schemes give orbitals strictly localized on a single atom or a single fragment of the molecule, where by strictly localized orbitals we mean orbitals which are strictly non-zero only on the spatial domain defining a single atom or a single fragment of the molecule, a property that may be desirable for both computational and interpretational purposes. Mayer
May-CJP-96
;
May-IJQC-14
proposed to define such strictly localized orbitals based on a spatial partitioning such as the one provided by the quantum theory of atoms in molecules (QTAIM)
Bad-BOOK-94
. The idea is to generate localized orbitals for an atom by truncating canonical molecular orbitals to the spatial domain corresponding to the QTAIM basin of this atom, and then orthonormalize them. These so-called effective atomic orbitals have been calculated for a few molecules and have the nice feature of reconciling Hilbert-space analysis with real-space analysis
RamSalMay-JCP-13
. In this last study, the effective atomic orbitals were calculated at posteriori from canonical molecular orbitals. Since these strictly localized orbitals are discontinuous on the boundaries of atomic basins, they have infinite kinetic energy and thus do not constitute a proper orbital basis to straightforwardly calculate the energy, which may be one reason for which they have not been adopted yet. It has been proposed alternative ways of defining orbitals either based on QTAIM
CioLia-JCP-98
;
Pon-JMC-98
;
TiaFraBlaMacSirMar-PCCP-11
or on a fuzzy definition of atoms in a molecule
MaySal-JCP-09
;
May-IJQC-14
, but the resulting orbitals are no longer strictly localized.

In applied mathematics, discontinuous Galerkin methods are a class of numerical methods to solve differential equations using basis functions that are only piecewise continuous, such as discontinuous piecewise polynomials (see, e.g., Ref.
DipErn-BOOK-12
). The discontinuous Galerkin approach was first introduced in electronic-structure theory by Lin and coworkers
LinLuYinE-JCP-12
;
HuLinYan-JCP-15
;
ZhaLinHuYanPas-JCP-17
;
LiLin-SIAM-19
. These authors used the interior-penalty discontinuous Galerkin method to generate a reduced basis set made of adaptive strictly localized basis functions in order to speed up periodic Kohn-Sham (KS) calculations.

In the present work, we combine the idea of strictly localized orbitals based on a chemically relevant spatial partitioning and the interior-penalty discontinuous Galerkin method. By allowing one to perform energy minimization with discontinuous strictly localized basis functions, the interior-penalty discontinuous Galerkin method provides a rigorous variational formulation for the effective atomic orbitals of Mayer and coworkers
May-CJP-96
;
RamSalMay-JCP-13
;
May-IJQC-14
. More generally, the interior-penalty discontinuous Galerkin method allows one to formulate a well-defined electronic-structure theory of strictly localized orbitals in quantum chemistry in the infinite-dimensional setting (i.e., in the limit of a complete basis set), which is thus properly independent from the choice of basis set.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
II
, we provide the theory of strictly localized orbitals based on the interior-penalty discontinuous Galerkin method. In Section
III
, we give numerical illustrations on one-dimensional (1D) model systems. Finally, Section
IV
contains our conclusions.
Atomic units are used throughout this work.

II
Theory

II.1
Exact molecular ground-state electronic-structure theory

We start by a brief review of the mathematical formulation of the exact molecular electronic-structure theory (for a general mathematical review, see e.g. Ref.
CanDefKutLebMad-BOOK-03
). We consider
N
electrons in a molecule with position-spin coordinates
\mathbf{x}_{i}=(\mathbf{r}_{i},\sigma_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\Sigma
where
\Sigma=\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}
is the set of spin coordinates. The one-electron Hilbert space is
{\mathpzc{h}}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\Sigma,\mathbb{C})
, i.e. the space of square-integrable functions, with inner product denoted as
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. The spin-orbital space
{\mathpzc{w}}\subset\mathpzc{h}
is taken as the first-order Sobolev space

\textstyle{\mathpzc{w}}=H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\Sigma,\mathbb{C})=\{\phi\in\mathpzc{h}\;|\;\nabla\phi\in\mathpzc{h}^{3}\},

(1)

representing the space of square-integrable functions with gradient also square integrable.
This spin-orbital space allows one to define the action of the one-electron kinetic-energy operator
\hat{t}=-(1/2)\Delta
via its sesquilinear form for any two spin-orbitals
\phi,\psi\in{\mathpzc{w}}
as

\langle\phi,\hat{t}\;\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla\phi,\nabla\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\Sigma}\nabla\phi^{*}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\psi(\mathbf{x})\text{d}\mathbf{x}.

(2)

In this sense,
{\mathpzc{w}}
is the largest spin-orbital space ensuring finite kinetic energy (the so-called form domain of the operator
t
). In the special case where
\psi
is in the second-order Sobolev space (the domain of the operator
t
), i.e.
\psi\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\Sigma,\mathbb{C})=\{\phi\in\mathpzc{h}\;|\;\Delta\phi\in\mathpzc{h}\}\subset{\mathpzc{w}}
, we recover by integration by parts the familiar Laplacian form of the kinetic-energy operator: for any
\phi\in{\mathpzc{w}}
,
\langle\phi,\hat{t}\;\psi\rangle=-(1/2)\langle\phi,\Delta\psi\rangle
.

The
N
-electron Hilbert space is given by the
N
-fold antisymmetrized tensor product of one-electron Hilbert spaces, i.e.
{\cal H}=\bigwedge^{N}{\mathpzc{h}}
with inner product still denoted as
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. The
N
-electron molecular Born-Oppenheimer non-relativistic Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\hat{T}+\hat{V}_{\text{ne}}+\hat{W}_{\text{ee}},

(3)

where
\hat{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{t}_{i}
is the total kinetic-energy operator,
\hat{V}_{\text{ne}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{\text{ne}}(\mathbf{r}_{i})
with
v_{\text{ne}}(\mathbf{r}_{i})=-\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\text{n}}}Z_{k}/|\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{R}_{k}|
is the nuclei-electron interaction depending of the positions
\{\mathbf{R}_{k}\}
and charges
\{Z_{k}\}
of the
M_{\text{n}}
nuclei, and
\hat{W}_{\text{ee}}=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}1/|\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{r}_{j}|
is the electron-electron interaction. According to the variational theorem, the ground-state energy
E_{0}
can be expressed as an infimum

E_{0}=\inf_{\Psi\in{\cal W}}\langle\Psi,\hat{H}\Psi\rangle,

(4)

where the search is over the space of admissible normalized
N
-electron wave functions
\Psi

\textstyle{\cal W}=\Big\{\Psi\in\bigwedge^{N}{\mathpzc{w}}\,\;|\;\langle\Psi,\Psi\rangle=1\Big\}\subset{\cal H},

(5)

which is the largest space of normalized
N
-electron wave functions having finite kinetic energy and in fact finite expectation value of the whole Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
.

Let us consider now a (complete) spin-orbital orthonormal basis
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathpzc{w}
of the one-electron Hilbert space
\mathpzc{h}
.
The space of admissible normalized
N
-electron wave functions can then be explicitly written as

\displaystyle{\cal W}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\{\Psi=\sum_{I\in{\cal D}}C_{I}\;\psi_{I_{1}}\wedge\psi_{I_{2}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\psi_{I_{N}}\;|\;\langle\Psi,\Psi\rangle=1;(C_{I})_{I\in{\cal D}}\in\mathbb{C}^{\cal D}\Big\},

(6)

where
\psi_{I_{1}}\wedge\psi_{I_{2}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\psi_{I_{N}}
is a Slater determinant (i.e., a normalized antisymmetrized product of
N
spin-orbitals),
{\cal D}
is the set of ordered spin-orbital labels of all
N
-electron Slater determinants

{\cal D}=\left\{I=(I_{1},I_{2},...,I_{N})\in\mathbb{N}^{N}\;|\;I_{1}<I_{2}<\cdots<I_{N}\right\},

(7)

and
(C_{I})_{I\in{\cal D}}
is a vector of complex coefficients.
The choice of the spin-orbital basis
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is completely arbitrary: any wave function
\Psi\in{\cal W}
can be expressed with any spin-orbital basis (but of course with different coefficients
C_{I}
). One can exploit this freedom for defining a basis of localized spin-orbitals. As we will see in the next section, part of this arbitrariness in the definition of the spin-orbitals is lost when restrictions are introduced on the variational wave-function space
{\cal W}
.

II.2
Constraints on orbitals by restrictions on the variational wave-function space

In variational electronic-structure methods, we usually consider a subset
{\cal D}^{\prime}\subset{\cal D}
of
N
-electron Slater determinants and a possibly constrained set of coefficients
{\cal C}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{C}^{\cal D^{\prime}}
, and the associated variational energy is obtained as

E_{0}^{\prime}=\inf_{\Psi\in{\cal W}^{\prime}}E[\Psi],

(8)

where
E[\Psi]
is a functional of
\Psi
and
{\cal W}^{\prime}\subset{\cal W}
is the subspace of admissible normalized
N
-electron wave functions

\displaystyle{\cal W}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\{\Psi=\sum_{I\in{\cal D}^{\prime}}C_{I}\;\phi_{I_{1}}\wedge\phi_{I_{2}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\phi_{I_{N}}\;|\;\langle\Psi,\Psi\rangle=1;(C_{I})_{I\in{\cal D}^{\prime}}\in{\cal C}^{\prime};\{\phi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}=\hat{U}\{\psi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}},\;\hat{U}\in{\cal U}({\mathpzc{h}})\Big\},

where
{\cal U}({\mathpzc{h}})
designates the set of all unitary transformations on
{\mathpzc{h}}
. Thus, in the minimization in Eq. (
8
), the coefficients
(C_{I})_{I\in{\cal D}^{\prime}}
of the Slater determinants are varied, as well as the spin-orbitals
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of these Slater determinants via unitary transformations
\hat{U}
of the starting spin-orbital orthonormal basis
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
. This encompasses variational wave-function methods for which
E[\Psi]=\langle\Psi,\hat{H}\Psi\rangle
, such as Hartree-Fock (HF) or multi-configuration self-consistent-field (MCSCF) in a complete basis set
Lew-ARMA-04
, but also density-functional-theory (DFT) approaches (see, e.g., Ref.
Tou-INC-23
) for which
E[\Psi]=\langle\Psi,\hat{H}^{\prime}\Psi\rangle+{\cal E}[\rho_{\Psi}]
where
\hat{H}^{\prime}
is an effective Hamiltonian and
{\cal E}[\rho_{\Psi}]
is a functional of the one-electron density
\rho_{\Psi}
of
\Psi
. For DFT approaches,
E_{0}^{\prime}
can in principle be equal to the exact ground-state energy
E_{0}
. The orthonormality condition on the set of spin-orbitals
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
can also be relaxed (see, e.g., Ref.
Ols-JCP-15
) by just requiring the transformation
\hat{U}
in Eq. (
) to be invertible and not unitary. The obtained spin-orbitals
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
then form a nonorthogonal basis, also called a Riesz basis in the infinite-dimensional setting
Chr-BOOK-03
;
Hei-BOOK-11
.

For a given choice of determinant set
{\cal D}^{\prime}
, a minimizing wave function
\Psi_{0}^{\prime}
in Eq. (
8
), assuming it exists, is written with two subsets of occupied spin-orbitals:

(1)

a set of
M_{\text{c}}

core
(or inactive) spin-orbitals (
M_{\text{c}}\leq N
) which are occupied in all Slater determinants listed in
{\cal D}^{\prime}

{\cal O}_{\text{c}}=\{\phi_{i}\;|\;\forall I\in{\cal D}^{\prime},i\in I\},

(10)

where
i\in I
means that the label
i
is one of the spin-orbital labels of
I
;

(2)

a set of
M_{\text{a}}

active
spin-orbitals which are occupied in at least one Slater determinant listed in
{\cal D}^{\prime}
and unoccupied in at least another one

{\cal O}_{\text{a}}=\{\phi_{i}\;|\;\exists\;I,J\in{\cal D}^{\prime},i\in I,i\not\in J\}.

(11)

It is always possible to choose active spin-orbitals that are orthogonal to the core spin-orbitals. In this case, we have the following orthogonal direct-sum decomposition of the one-electron space
{\mathpzc{w}}

{\mathpzc{w}}={\mathpzc{w}}_{\text{c}}\oplus{\mathpzc{w}}_{\text{a}}\oplus{\mathpzc{w}}_{\text{v}},

(12)

where
{\mathpzc{w}}_{\text{c}}=\text{span}({\cal O}_{\text{c}})
is the core spin-orbital space,
{\mathpzc{w}}_{\text{a}}=\text{span}({\cal O}_{\text{a}})
is the active spin-orbital space, and
{\mathpzc{w}}_{\text{v}}
is the
virtual
(or secondary) spin-orbital space (the orthogonal complement of
{\mathpzc{w}}_{\text{c}}\oplus{\mathpzc{w}}_{\text{a}}
in
{\mathpzc{w}}
).

There is full freedom in choosing a spin-orbital basis within the core space
{\mathpzc{w}}_{\text{c}}
and a spin-orbital basis within the virtual space
{\mathpzc{w}}_{\text{v}}
. The situation within the active space
{\mathpzc{w}}_{\text{a}}
is more involved and depends on the choice of the determinant set
{\cal D}^{\prime}
and coefficient set
{\cal C}^{\prime}
. For general sets
{\cal D}^{\prime}
and
{\cal C}^{\prime}
, the energy minimization in Eq. (
8
) imposes some constraints on the active spin-orbitals.
There are two important special cases in which the situation for the active space is simple:

•

Single-determinant methods (such as HF or KS): one considers only one Slater determinant, i.e.
{\cal D}^{\prime}=\{(1,2,...,N)\}
. There is no active spin-orbitals, i.e.
{\cal O_{\text{a}}}=\emptyset
and
{\mathpzc{w}}_{\text{a}}=\{0\}
.

•

Complete-active-space self-consistent field (CASSCF): one considers linear combinations of all the Slater determinants generated by occupying the active spin-orbitals with
N-M_{\text{c}}
electrons while keeping the
M_{\text{c}}
core spin-orbitals always occupied, i.e.

{\cal D}^{\prime}=\{I\in{\cal D}\;|\;I_{1}=1,I_{2}=2,...,I_{M_{\text{c}}}=M_{\text{c}},\;\text{and}\;I_{N}\leq{M_{\text{c}}+M_{\text{a}}}\}
and
{\cal C}^{\prime}=\mathbb{C}^{{\cal D}^{\prime}}
. Then, the energy minimization in Eq. (
8
) does not impose any constraints on the active spin-orbitals, i.e. we have full freedom in choosing a spin-orbital basis within the active space
{\mathpzc{w}}_{\text{a}}
.

We thus see that imposing restrictions on the variational wave-function space reduces the arbitrariness in the definition of the spin-orbitals via the decomposition in Eq. (
12
). If one does not want to raise the energy, only the remaining freedom could be exploited to define localized spin-orbitals. Sometimes, the variational wave-function ansatz tends to automatically give fairly localized active spin-orbitals after energy minimization, such as in the generalized VB
BobGod-INC-77
;
GodDunHunJef-ACR-73
and spin-coupled VB
CooGerRai-CR-91
theories. Note of course that in the limit of the exact theory, i.e.
{\cal D}^{\prime}={\cal D}
and
{\cal C}^{\prime}=\mathbb{C}^{{\cal D}}
, all the spin-orbitals are active, i.e.
{\mathpzc{w}}={\mathpzc{w}}_{\text{a}}
, and we recover total freedom in choosing a spin-orbital basis.

II.3
Localized orbitals from spatial partitioning and the discontinuous Galerkin method

Inspired by the work of Mayer and coworkers
May-CJP-96
;
RamSalMay-JCP-13
;
May-IJQC-14
and the work of Lin and coworkers
LinLuYinE-JCP-12
;
HuLinYan-JCP-15
;
ZhaLinHuYanPas-JCP-17
;
LiLin-SIAM-19
, we propose to rigorously define subspaces of localized spin-orbitals based on a spatial partitioning and the interior-penalty discontinuous Galerkin method. We thus consider a partition
{\cal P}
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}

{\cal P}=\left\{\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2},...,\Omega_{M_{\cal{P}}}\right\},

(13)

such that
\{\Omega_{p}\}_{p=1,...,M_{\cal{P}}}
are
M_{\cal{P}}
open disjoint domains of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
\bigcup_{p=1}^{M_{\cal{P}}}\overline{\Omega}_{p}=\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\overline{\Omega}_{p}
designates the topological closure of
\Omega_{p}
. The set of distinct interfaces between two domains is

{\cal S}_{\cal P}=\left\{S=\partial\Omega_{1}\cap\partial\Omega_{2}\;|\;\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}\in{\cal P},\;\Omega_{1}\not=\Omega_{2}\right\},

(14)

where
\partial\Omega
designates the boundary of the domain
\Omega
. For example, for a given molecule, it could be the atomic partition realized by the Voronoi polyhedra
Med-JCP-86
generated by the nuclear positions (appropriately modified to account for the different sizes of atoms
Bec-JCP-88b
;
RouPeeAls-JMS-01
), or the QTAIM atomic partition
Bad-BOOK-94
, where the space is divided into atomic basins
\Omega_{p}
separated by interatomic surfaces (separatrices)
S
defined as having zero local flux in the gradient vector field
\nabla\rho_{\Psi}
of the one-electron density
\rho_{\Psi}
of a given wave function
\Psi
. It could also be for instance the non-atomic partition realized by the electron localization function (ELF)
BecEdg-JCP-90
;
SilSav-NAT-94
.

The partition
{\cal P}
generates an orthogonal direct-sum decomposition of the one-electron Hilbert space

{\mathpzc{h}}={\mathpzc{h}}_{\Omega_{1}}\oplus{\mathpzc{h}}_{\Omega_{2}}\oplus\cdots\oplus{\mathpzc{h}}_{\Omega_{M_{\cal P}}},

(15)

where
{\mathpzc{h}}_{\Omega}=L^{2}(\Omega\times\Sigma,\mathbb{C})
is the one-electron Hilbert space restricted to the domain
\Omega
. It is then natural to consider the
broken
spin-orbital space
{\mathpzc{w}}_{\cal P}
obtained with the following direct sum

{\mathpzc{w}}_{\cal P}={\mathpzc{w}}_{\Omega_{1}}\oplus{\mathpzc{w}}_{\Omega_{2}}\oplus\cdots\oplus{\mathpzc{w}}_{\Omega_{M_{\cal P}}},

(16)

where
{\mathpzc{w}}_{\Omega}=H^{1}(\Omega\times\Sigma,\mathbb{C})
is the spin-orbital space restricted to the domain
\Omega
. The space
{\mathpzc{w}}_{\cal P}
is known as the piecewise or broken first-order Sobolev space
DipErn-BOOK-12
and can also be written as

\textstyle\mathpzc{w}_{\cal P}=\Big\{\phi\in\mathpzc{h}\;|\;\forall\Omega\in{\cal P},\phi_{|\Omega\times\Sigma}\in{\mathpzc{w}}_{\Omega}\Big\},

(17)

where
\phi_{|\Omega\times\Sigma}
designates the restriction of
\phi
to
\Omega\times\Sigma
. In other words, for a broken spin-orbital
\phi\in\mathpzc{w}_{\cal P}
,
\nabla\phi
is only required to be square integrable on each domain of the partition but not across the domains, i.e. it can have infinite kinetic energy
\langle\phi,\hat{t}\;\phi\rangle
due to discontinuities at the interfaces between the domains. Thus, the broken spin-orbital space
{\mathpzc{w}}_{\cal P}
is larger than the normal spin-orbital space
{\mathpzc{w}}=H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\Sigma,\mathbb{C})
in which we would a priori want to work, i.e.
\mathpzc{w}\subset\mathpzc{w}_{\cal P}
. Yet, it would be desirable to work in the broken spin-orbital space
{\mathpzc{w}}_{\cal P}
since, the direct-sum decomposition in Eq. (
16
) would then give us a natural and rigorous definition of subspaces of strictly localized spin-orbitals, independent from any finite basis-set approximation. This is precisely what the discontinuous Galerkin method allows one to do, i.e. to work on the broken spin-orbital space
{\mathpzc{w}}_{\cal P}
.

The symmetric interior penalty version of the discontinuous Galerkin method
Arn-SIAM-82
;
ArnBreCocMar-SIAM-02
;
DipErn-BOOK-12
allows one to handle the decomposition in Eq. (
16

\mathpzc{w}
in Eq. (
2

\langle\phi,\hat{t}_{\cal P}\;\psi\rangle
on the broken spin-orbital space
\mathpzc{w}_{\cal P}
. For simplicity, we will give the expression of the sesquilinear form
\langle\phi,\hat{t}_{\cal P}\;\psi\rangle
for two broken spin-orbitals
\phi
and
\psi
assumed to be sufficiently regular inside each domain

of the partition. It is
LinLuYinE-JCP-12
;

\displaystyle\langle\phi,\hat{t}_{\cal P}\;\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\Omega\in{\cal P}}\int_{\Omega\times\Sigma}\nabla\phi_{|\Omega\times\Sigma}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\psi_{|\Omega\times\Sigma}(\mathbf{x})\text{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{S\in{\cal S}_{\cal P}}\Biggl[\int_{S\times\Sigma}\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{S}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\{\!\{\nabla\psi\}\!\}_{S}(\mathbf{x})\,\text{d}s(\mathbf{r})+\int_{S\times\Sigma}\{\!\{\nabla\phi\}\!\}_{S}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\llbracket\psi\rrbracket_{S}(\mathbf{x})\,\text{d}s(\mathbf{r})\Biggl]

\displaystyle+\alpha_{\text{pen}}\sum_{S\in{\cal S}_{\cal P}}\int_{S\times\Sigma}\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{S}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\llbracket\psi\rrbracket_{S}(\mathbf{x})\,\text{d}s(\mathbf{r}),

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{x}\in S\times\Sigma,\;\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{S}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\phi|_{\Omega_{1}\times\Sigma}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{n}_{\partial\Omega_{1}}(\mathbf{r})+\phi|_{\Omega_{2}\times\Sigma}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{n}_{\partial\Omega_{2}}(\mathbf{r})

, we have
\langle\phi,\hat{t}_{\cal P}\;\psi\rangle=-(1/2)\langle\phi,\Delta\psi\rangle
, which can be checked by integration by parts. The third term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
18
) ensures symmetry, i.e.
\langle\phi,\hat{t}_{\cal P}\;\psi\rangle=\langle\psi,\hat{t}_{\cal P}\;\phi\rangle^{*}
. Finally, the fourth term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
18
) introduces penalties for jumps across the interfaces. For a sufficiently large positive value of
\alpha_{\text{pen}}
, the kinetic energy of any broken spin-orbital
\phi
with non-negligible jumps at interfaces is sufficiently large for not altering the total energy minimum when
\hat{t}_{\cal P}
is used in the energy minimization.

Starting from the broken spin-orbital space
\mathpzc{w}_{\cal P}
, we now define a corresponding space of admissible normalized
N
-electron broken wave functions

\textstyle{\cal W}_{\cal P}=\Big\{\Psi\in\bigwedge^{N}{\mathpzc{w}}_{\cal P}\,\;|\;\langle\Psi,\Psi\rangle=1\Big\}\subset{\cal H},

(21)

and a
N
-electron Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{\cal P}=\hat{T}_{\cal P}+\hat{V}_{\text{ne}}+\hat{W}_{\text{ee}},

(22)

where
\hat{T}_{\cal P}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{t}_{{\cal P},i}
is the total kinetic-energy operator corresponding to the one-electron operator
\hat{t}_{\cal P}
. The exact ground-state energy
E_{0}
can then be expressed as an infimum over broken wave functions
\Psi\in{\cal W}_{\cal P}

E_{0}=\inf_{\Psi\in{\cal W}_{\cal P}}\langle\Psi,\hat{H}_{\cal P}\Psi\rangle,

(23)

provided, we recall, that the penalty parameter
\alpha_{\text{pen}}
in Eq. (
18
) is sufficiently large for not altering the energy minimum.

Thus, the interior-penalty discontinuous Galerkin method allows us to use the broken spin-orbital space
\mathpzc{w}_{\cal P}
as a variational spin-orbital space. The direct-sum decomposition of this space in Eq. (
16
) then means that we can define orthonormal bases of strictly localized spin-orbitals. To this end, let us consider an orthonormal basis
\{\tilde{\phi}_{p,\mu}\}_{\mu\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathpzc{w}_{\Omega_{p}}
of the one-electron Hilbert space
{\mathpzc{h}}_{\Omega_{p}}
for the domain
\Omega_{p}
. If, for each function
\tilde{\phi}_{p,\mu}
, we introduce the function
{\phi}_{p,\mu}
which extends it to the whole domain as

\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\Sigma,\;{\phi}_{p,\mu}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\tilde{\phi}_{p,\mu}(\mathbf{x})&\text{if}\;\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{p}\times\Sigma\\
0&\text{if}\;\mathbf{x}\not\in\Omega_{p}\times\Sigma\end{cases},

(24)

we obtain indeed a broken spin-orbital orthonormal basis
\{\phi_{p,\mu}\}^{p=1,...,M_{\cal P}}_{\mu\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathpzc{w}_{\cal P}
of the one-electron Hilbert space
\mathpzc{h}
. The basis function
\phi_{p,\mu}
corresponds to a broken spin-orbital with spatial support on the domain
\Omega_{p}
. Note that two spin-orbitals
\phi_{p,\mu}\in{\mathpzc{w}}_{\Omega_{p}}
and
\phi_{p^{\prime},\nu}\in{\mathpzc{w}}_{\Omega_{p^{\prime}}}
for two different domains
{\Omega_{p}}\not={\Omega_{p^{\prime}}}
are automatically orthogonal since they do not overlap. The space of admissible normalized
N
-electron broken wave functions can then be explicitly written as

\displaystyle{\cal W}_{\cal P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\{\Psi=\sum_{I\in{\cal D}_{\cal P}}C_{I}\;\phi_{I_{1}}\wedge\phi_{I_{2}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\phi_{I_{N}}\;|\;\langle\Psi,\Psi\rangle=1;(C_{I})_{I\in{\cal D}_{\cal P}}\in\mathbb{C}^{{\cal D}_{\cal P}}\Big\},

(25)

where
\phi_{I_{1}}\wedge\phi_{I_{2}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\phi_{I_{N}}
is a Slater determinant made of
N
localized spin-orbitals
\{\phi_{I_{n}}\}_{n=1,...,N}
with the double index
I_{n}=(p_{n},\mu_{n})\in\llbracket 1,M_{\cal P}\rrbracket\times\mathbb{N}
and
{\cal D}_{\cal P}
is the set of ordered localized spin-orbital labels of all
N
-electron Slater determinants

{\cal D}_{\cal P}=\left\{I=(I_{1},I_{2},...,I_{N})\in(\llbracket 1,M_{\cal P}\rrbracket\times\mathbb{N})^{N}\;|\;I_{1}<I_{2}<\cdots<I_{N}\right\},

(26)

where here “
<
” refers to the lexicographic order of pairs of numbers.

For any given domain
\Omega_{p}
, the spin-orbital basis
\{\phi_{p,\mu}\}_{\mu\in\mathbb{N}}
for this domain is arbitrary. One possible choice is to use the eigenfunctions of the one-particle reduced density matrix for the domain
\Omega_{p}
, defined as follows. For a wave function
\Psi\in{\cal W}_{\cal P}
, the total one-particle reduced density matrix is

\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=N\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\Sigma)^{N-1}}\Psi^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}_{2},...,\mathbf{x}_{N})\;\Psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{2},...,\mathbf{x}_{N})\text{d}\mathbf{x}_{2}...\text{d}\mathbf{x}_{N},

(27)

and can be expressed in the broken spin-orbital orthonormal basis
\{\phi_{p,\mu}\}^{p=1,...,M_{\cal P}}_{\mu\in\mathbb{N}}
as

\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{p=1}^{M_{\cal P}}\sum_{p^{\prime}=1}^{M_{\cal P}}\sum_{\mu=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{p,\mu;p^{\prime},\nu}\phi_{p,\mu}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{p^{\prime},\nu}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),

(28)

where
\gamma_{p,\mu;p^{\prime},\nu}=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\Sigma)^{2}}\phi_{p,\mu}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\;\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\;\phi_{p^{\prime},\nu}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\text{d}\mathbf{x}\text{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
are the matrix elements of
\gamma
. The one-particle reduced density matrix for the domain
\Omega_{p}
can then be defined as

\gamma_{p}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{p=1}^{M_{\cal P}}\sum_{\mu=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{p,\mu;p,\nu}\phi_{p,\mu}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{p,\nu}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),

(29)

which is zero for
\mathbf{x}\not\in\Omega_{p}
or
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\not\in\Omega_{p}
.
The eigenfunctions
\Big\{\phi_{p,\mu}^{\text{nat}}\Big\}_{\mu\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\gamma_{p}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
may be called natural spin-orbitals for the domain
\Omega_{p}
and are given by the eigenvalue equation

\int_{\Omega_{p}\times\Sigma}\gamma_{p}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\phi_{p,\mu}^{\text{nat}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\text{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=n_{p,\mu}\,\phi_{p,\mu}^{\text{nat}}(\mathbf{x}),

(30)

where the eigenvalues
n_{p,\mu}
are occupation numbers. For the QTAIM partition, these eigenfunctions
\Big\{\phi_{p,\mu}^{\text{nat}}\Big\}_{\mu\in\mathbb{N}}
correspond to the (correlated) effective atomic orbitals
May-IJQC-14
. They could also be considered as a strictly localized version of the natural atomic orbitals of Weinhold (see, e.g., Ref.
Wei-JCC-12
). Alternatively, by considering the eigenfunctions of the one-particle reduced density matrix for two adjacent domains corresponding to two bonded atoms, we could define a strictly localized version of natural bond orbitals
Wei-JCC-12
.

For restricted variational wave-function spaces, as discussed in Section
II.2
, the localization constraints, i.e. imposing that
\phi_{p,\mu}
has spatial support on the domain
{\Omega_{p}}
, are generally incompatible with full energy minimization. In other words, the two direct-sum decompositions in Eqs. (
12
) and (
16
) are generally incompatible. There are then two possibilities for restricted variational wave-function spaces. The first possibility is to fully minimize the energy by releasing the localization constraints, i.e. performing a common unitary (or, more generally, invertible) transformation
\hat{U}
mixing all the strictly localized spin-orbitals
\{\phi_{p,\mu}\}^{p=1,...,M_{\cal P}}_{\mu\in\mathbb{N}}
. This is equivalent to Eqs. (
8
) and (
), but starting from the strictly localized spin-orbitals
\{\phi_{p,\mu}\}^{p=1,...,M_{\cal P}}_{\mu\in\mathbb{N}}
, which are just used as a discontinuous basis set, in the spirit of Refs.
LinLuYinE-JCP-12
;
HuLinYan-JCP-15
;
ZhaLinHuYanPas-JCP-17
;
LiLin-SIAM-19
. The second possibility is to minimize the energy with the localization constraints, i.e. for each domain
\Omega_{p}
performing a separate unitary (or, more generally, invertible) transformation
\hat{U}_{p}
among the strictly localized spin-orbitals
\{\phi_{p,\mu}\}_{\mu\in\mathbb{N}}
of this domain. In this latter case, the lowest energy is not reached, but the obtained orbitals remain strictly localized. This gives a version of the VB self-consistent-field method
LenBal-JCP-83
with well-defined strictly localized spin-orbital subspaces, even in the complete basis-set limit.

Even with restricted variational wave-function spaces, in the complete basis-set limit, the minimizing energy and wave function will be independent from the penalty constant
\alpha_{\text{pen}}
, provided it is large enough. In a finite basis set, the results may be depend on
\alpha_{\text{pen}}
, and there might be tradeoff between a too small
\alpha_{\text{pen}}
which can lead to strongly discontinuous orbitals at the interfaces and a too large
\alpha_{\text{pen}}
which can lead to orbitals far from being optimal away from the interfaces.

II.4
Comparison with valence-bond theory and chemical interpretability

For an atomic partition such as the QTAIM one, if we write a broken
N
-electron wave function,
\Psi=\sum_{I}C_{I}\;\Phi_{I}
, with chemically meaningful spin-singlet configurations
\{\Phi_{I}\}
constructed from strictly localized spin-orbitals
\{\phi_{p,\mu}\}
, we obtain a type of classical VB expansion
ShaHib-BOOK-08
where each spin-orbital
\phi_{p,\mu}
is spatially supported in an atomic domain
\Omega_{p}
.

In the classical VB approach, localized orbitals are defined in the approximate finite-dimensional setting using the structure of standard quantum-chemistry atom-centered basis sets
\{\chi_{p,\mu}\}_{\mu=1,...,m_{p}}^{p=1,...,M_{\text{n}}}\subset{\mathpzc{w}}
where
p
runs over the
M_{\text{n}}
nuclei and
m_{p}
is the number of basis functions for atom
p
. These basis sets are thus naturally partitioned into
M_{\text{n}}
sets of basis functions
\{\chi_{p,\mu}\}_{\mu=1,...,m_{p}}
centered on atom
p
. Typically, the basis functions
\chi_{p,\mu}
are GTO functions (see, e.g., Ref.
HelJorOls-BOOK-02
). The nonorthogonal localized orbitals
\{\psi_{p,\mu}\}
used in classical VB are then defined by expanding
\psi_{p,\mu}
only on the atomic basis functions centered on atom
p

StoWagPre-TCA-80

\psi_{p,\mu}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{m_{p}}c_{p,\nu}\;\chi_{p,\nu},

(31)

with coefficients
c_{p,\nu}\in\mathbb{C}
. More generally,
p
may index a group of atoms (or fragment) of the molecule. This is known as “strictly localized orbitals”
ShaHib-BOOK-08
or, sometimes, as “extremely localized orbitals”
SirGenCivPieGhi-TCA-07
or “absolutely localized orbitals”
KhaHeaBel-JCP-06
(see also Ref.
MccFauZhuOgoQiuWhiRabLin-NJP-20
;
FauWuLin-RMS-22
). Note, however, that this corresponds to strict localization in the set of indices of basis functions, but not strict localization in real space since the atomic basis functions have infinite extent. Even though this definition of localized orbitals is convenient for practical calculations, it has the serious drawback of depending on the partition of the basis set used, which tends to become meaningless in the limit of a complete basis set. Indeed, for example, a complete basis set for any molecule can formally be obtained by a complete GTO basis set centered around a single atom, in which case there is only one set in the partition, and Eq. (
31
) does not impose any localization constraints.

By contrast, in the present approach, the partition of the broken spin-orbital orthonormal basis into
M_{\cal P}
sets of spin-orbitals
\{\phi_{p,\mu}\}_{\mu\in\mathbb{N}}\subset{\mathpzc{w}}_{\Omega_{p}}
is defined independently of an underlying basis set but directly in the infinite-dimensional setting. In other words, the subspaces of localized spin-orbitals are defined in the complete basis-set limit. Also, contrary to the classical VB approach, the present localized spin-orbitals are orthonormal and strictly localized in space, and two spin-orbitals belonging to two different domains have vanishing overlap. The VB-like structure
\Phi_{I}
are thus also orthonormal and the weight of the VB-like structure
\Phi_{I}
in the total wave function
\Psi
can be unambiguously defined as
W_{I}=|C_{I}|^{2}
(the so-called Chirgwin-Coulson, Löwdin, and inverse weights of the usual VB approach
ShaHib-BOOK-08
all reduce to this formula for orthonormal VB structures).

Another nice interpretative feature of the strictly localized spin-orbitals
\{\phi_{p,\mu}\}^{p=1,...,M_{\cal P}}_{\mu\in\mathbb{N}}
is that the electron population on the domain
\Omega_{p}
is simply given by

N_{p}=\int_{\Omega_{p}\times\Sigma}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})\text{d}\mathbf{x}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{p,\mu;p,\mu}.

(32)

For the QTAIM partition,
N_{p}
is the QTAIM electron population on atom
p
. Interestingly, owing to the orthonormality and strict localization of the spin-orbital basis, it also corresponds to the Mulliken
Mul-JCP-55
and Löwdin
Low-JCP-50
electron populations, which are identical for an orthonormal basis. This point was already pointed out in Refs.
RamSalMay-JCP-13
;
May-IJQC-14
.

II.5
Practical construction of finite strictly localized spin-orbital basis sets

In practice, we need to choose finite strictly localized spin-orbital orthonormal bases
\{\tilde{\phi}_{p,\mu}\}_{\mu=1,..,m_{p}}\subset\mathpzc{w}_{\Omega_{p}}
for each domain
\Omega_{p}
. When
\Omega_{p}
is an atomic domain, the simplest possibility is to take a standard quantum-chemistry atom-centered basis set
\{\chi_{p,\mu}\}_{\mu=1,...,m_{p}}^{p=1,...,M_{\text{n}}}
(for example, a GTO basis set) and restrict the basis functions
\chi_{p,\mu}
centered around the nucleus
p
to the domain
\Omega_{p}

\tilde{\chi}_{p,\mu}=\chi_{p,\mu\;|_{\Omega_{p}\times\Sigma}}.

(33)

These truncated basis functions may then be orthonormalized, e.g. using Löwdin symmetric orthonormalization
Low-JCP-50
,

\tilde{\phi}_{p,\mu}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{m_{p}}[S_{p}^{-1/2}]_{\mu,\nu}\;\tilde{\chi}_{p,\nu},

(34)

where
[S_{p}]_{\mu,\nu}=\langle\tilde{\chi}_{p,\mu},\tilde{\chi}_{p,\nu}\rangle=\int_{\Omega_{p}\times\Sigma}\tilde{\chi}_{p,\mu}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\,\tilde{\chi}_{p,\nu}(\mathbf{x})\,\text{d}\mathbf{x}
are the matrix elements of the overlap matrix of the truncated basis functions of domain
\Omega_{p}
. Extension of the basis functions
\tilde{\phi}_{p,\mu}
to the whole domain according to Eq. (
24
) then leads to a finite strictly localized atomic spin-orbital orthonormal basis
\{\phi_{p,\mu}\}_{\mu=1,..,m_{p}}^{p=1,...,M_{\text{n}}}\subset\mathpzc{w}_{\cal P}
.

A more sophisticated possibility proposed in Refs.
LinLuYinE-JCP-12
;
HuLinYan-JCP-15
;
ZhaLinHuYanPas-JCP-17
in the context of periodic KS calculations is to generate the basis set on-the-fly in each system. For each domain
\Omega_{p}
, the basis functions
\{\chi_{p,\mu}\}_{\mu=1,...,m_{p}}
are calculated as the
m_{p}
first eigenfunctions of a one-electron (effective) Hamiltonian
\hat{h}
(such as the HF or KS Hamiltonian)

\hat{h}\;\chi_{p,\mu}=\varepsilon_{p,\mu}\;\chi_{p,\mu},

(35)

on a domain
\Omega\supset\Omega_{p}
with some boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
. The basis functions
\{\chi_{p,\mu}\}_{\mu=1,...,m_{p}}
are then restricted to
\Omega_{p}
and orthonormalized (and possible near-linear dependencies are eliminated). The resulting basis set has been called adaptive local basis set. In Ref.
LiLin-SIAM-19
a variant of this approach was also proposed which constructs the local basis functions using the Hamiltonian
\hat{h}
on the whole domain and not a restricted domain
\Omega
. For a QTAIM domain
\Omega_{p}
, this last variant is in fact equivalent to the procedure proposed by Mayer to obtain (uncorrelated) effective atomic orbitals
May-CJP-96
;
May-IJQC-14
.

Whatever the practical approach used, we would like to emphasize that the general strategy of constructing a basis set for the global Hilbert space
{\mathpzc{h}}
by patching together local basis sets for the local Hilbert subspaces
{\mathpzc{h}}_{\Omega_{p}}
solves the overcompleteness problem
KinStaNew-CPL-75
of the multicenter basis sets used in quantum chemistry. In the limit where the local basis sets are complete for the local Hilbert subspaces
{\mathpzc{h}}_{\Omega_{p}}
, their union properly forms a complete basis set for the global Hilbert space
{\mathpzc{h}}
.

When
\Omega_{p}
is an atomic domain, the localized spin-orbital basis sets bear some resemblance with some compactly supported basis sets, namely the numerical atomic orbitals
JunPazSanArt-PRB-01
or numeric atom-centered orbitals
BluGehHanHavHavRenReuSch-CPC-09
which consist in atomic orbitals calculated with a radial confining potential such as the orbitals are strictly zero beyond a spherical cutoff radius. However, the latter approach is not based on a partition of space and thus it is not clear how to formally reach the complete basis-set limit.

We give now the expressions of the one- and two-electron integrals in a truncated non-orthogonal basis set
\{\tilde{\chi}_{p,\mu}\}_{\mu=1,..,m_{p}}^{p=1,...,M_{\cal P}}
where
\tilde{\chi}_{p,\mu}
has support in the domain
\Omega_{p}
. For two functions
\tilde{\chi}_{p,\mu}
and
\tilde{\chi}_{p^{\prime},\nu}
either on the same domain (
p=p^{\prime}
) or on two different domains (
p\not=p^{\prime}
) sharing an interface (
\partial\Omega_{p}\cap\partial\Omega_{p^{\prime}}\not=\emptyset
), the kinetic-energy integrals are

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{\chi}_{p,\mu},\hat{t}_{\cal P}\;\tilde{\chi}_{p^{\prime},\nu}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\delta_{p,p^{\prime}}\int_{\Omega_{p}\times\Sigma}\nabla\tilde{\chi}_{p,\mu}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\tilde{\chi}_{p,\nu}(\mathbf{x})\,\text{d}\mathbf{x}

(36)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}\Biggl[\int_{(\partial\Omega_{p}\cap\partial\Omega_{p^{\prime}})\times\Sigma}\tilde{\chi}_{p,\mu}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{n}_{\partial\Omega_{p}}(\mathbf{r})\cdot\nabla\tilde{\chi}_{p^{\prime},\nu}(\mathbf{x})\,\text{d}s(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle+\int_{(\partial\Omega_{p}\cap\partial\Omega_{p^{\prime}})\times\Sigma}\nabla\tilde{\chi}_{p,\mu}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\partial\Omega_{p^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{r})\,\tilde{\chi}_{p^{\prime},\nu}(\mathbf{x})\,\text{d}s(\mathbf{r})\Biggl]

\displaystyle+\alpha_{\text{pen}}\int_{(\partial\Omega_{p}\cap\partial\Omega_{p^{\prime}})\times\Sigma}\tilde{\chi}_{p,\mu}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{n}_{\partial\Omega_{p}}(\mathbf{r})\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\partial\Omega_{p^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{r})\,\tilde{\chi}_{p^{\prime},\nu}(\mathbf{x})\,\text{d}s(\mathbf{r}).

For two functions
\tilde{\chi}_{p,\mu}
and
\tilde{\chi}_{p,\nu}
on the same domain
\Omega_{p}
, the nuclei-electron integrals are

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{\chi}_{p,\mu},\hat{v}_{\text{ne}}\;\tilde{\chi}_{p,\nu}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{p}\times\Sigma}\tilde{\chi}_{p,\mu}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\,v_{\text{ne}}(\mathbf{r})\,\tilde{\chi}_{p,\nu}(\mathbf{x})\,\text{d}\mathbf{x}.

(37)

Finally, for two functions
\tilde{\chi}_{p,\mu}
and
\tilde{\chi}_{p^{\prime},\nu}
either on the same domain (
\Omega_{p}=\Omega_{p^{\prime}}

\Omega_{p}\not=\Omega_{p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{\chi}_{p,\mu}\,\tilde{\chi}_{p^{\prime},\nu},\hat{w}_{\text{ee}}\;\tilde{\chi}_{p,\lambda}\,\tilde{\chi}_{p^{\prime},\sigma}\rangle

\displaystyle\int_{(\Omega_{p}\times\Sigma)\times(\Omega_{p^{\prime}}\times\Sigma)}\frac{\tilde{\chi}_{p,\mu}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\,\tilde{\chi}_{p^{\prime},\nu}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\,\tilde{\chi}_{p,\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\,\tilde{\chi}_{p^{\prime},\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{2})}{|\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{r}_{2}|}\text{d}\mathbf{x}_{1}\text{d}\mathbf{x}_{2}.

(38)

These are the only non-zero integrals. Note in particular that the kinetic-energy integrals in Eq. (

Pow-INC-11
). Thanks to the strict locality of the basis functions, the number of two-electron integrals scales as

III
Numerical illustrations

In this section, we illustrate the use of strictly localized orbitals based on a spatial partitioning and the interior-penalty discontinuous Galerkin method on 1D model systems. We performed the calculations reported in this section using an in-house code publicly available in Ref.
FenTou-PROG-26
.

III.1
One-electron diatomic molecules

We first consider a 1D model of a one-electron diatomic molecule. On the spinfree Hilbert space
\mathpzc{h}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C})
, the one-electron Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{h}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\text{d}^{2}}{\text{d}x^{2}}+v_{\text{ne}}(x)

(39)

where
v_{\text{ne}}(x)
is the nuclei-electron potential

\displaystyle v_{\text{ne}}(x)=-Z_{\text{L}}v(x-R_{\text{L}})-Z_{\text{R}}v(x-R_{\text{R}}),

(40)

corresponding to a left (L) nucleus and a right (R) nucleus with charges
Z_{\text{L}}
and
Z_{\text{R}}
at positions
R_{\text{L}}=-R/2
and
R_{\text{R}}=R/2
. For
v
, we use the soft-Coulomb model potential
WagStoBurWhi-PCCP-12
;
Li-JMC-22

\displaystyle v(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^{2}+a^{2}}},

(41)

with regularization parameter
a=0.2
.
We want to find the lowest-energy molecular orbitals
\psi_{i}
and orbital energies
\varepsilon_{i}

\displaystyle h\psi_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}\psi_{i}.

(42)

We will compare three different types of calculations: a reference grid calculation, a standard calculation in a Hermite-Gaussian basis set, and a discontinuous-Galerkin calculation in a truncated Hermite-Gaussian basis set.

Reference grid calculation.

To obtain a nearly-exact reference, we use a spatial grid discretization with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, leading to the finite-difference grid Hamiltonian

\displaystyle h^{\rm grid}_{k,k^{\prime}}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta_{k,k^{\prime}+1}+\delta_{k,k^{\prime}-1}-2\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}}{\Delta x^{2}}+v_{\text{ne}}(x_{k})\delta_{k,k^{\prime}},

(43)

on an interval
[-L,L]
with
L=6.0
and grid spacing
\Delta x=0.015
. The grid Hamiltonian is then diagonalized to obtain the reference molecular orbitals
\psi_{i}
on the grid and the reference orbital energies
\varepsilon_{i}
.

Standard calculations in a Hermite-Gaussian basis set.

We use a one-electron basis set
\{\chi_{p,n}\}_{n=0,...,n_{\text{max}}}^{p=\text{L},\text{R}}
of normalized Hermite-Gaussian functions centered on the two nuclei,

\chi_{p,n}(x)=N_{n}H_{n}\!\left(\sqrt{2\alpha}(x-R_{p})\right)\exp[-\alpha(x-R_{p})^{2}],

(44)

where
n\geq 0
is an integer quantum number,
H_{n}
are the Hermite polynomials,
N_{n}=\Big(\sqrt{2\alpha}/(2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi})\Big)^{1/2}
is the normalization factor, and
\alpha>0
is a real constant. For a single nucleus
p
, it is well known that the set
\{\chi_{p,n}\}_{n=0,...,\infty}
is a complete orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C})
for any fixed exponent
\alpha
. However, for two nuclei,
\{\chi_{p,n}\}_{n=0,...,\infty}^{p=\text{L},\text{R}}
is an overcomplete basis. This is known as a frame in functional analysis
Chr-BOOK-03
;
Hei-BOOK-11
. Unless indicated otherwise, we use a maximal quantum number
n_{\text{max}}=10
, and the same exponent
\alpha=1.5
in all basis functions, instead of multiple exponents in order to avoid optimizing them. Except for that, this basis set is quite similar to the GTO basis sets widely used in quantum chemistry.

To calculate the orbitals and orbital energies, we first construct a global orthonormal basis
\{\phi_{\mu}\}_{\mu=1,...,M}
(where
M=2(n_{\text{max}}+1)
) by Löwdin symmetric orthonormalization of
\{\chi_{p,n}\}_{n=0,...,n_{\text{max}}}^{p=\text{L},\text{R}}
, and then diagonalize the Hamiltonian in this orthonormal basis. This leads to the molecular orbitals as linear combinations of the basis functions
\phi_{\mu}
,

\psi_{i}(x)=\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}c_{\mu,i}\,\phi_{\mu}(x).

(45)

IV
Conclusion

We have presented a rigorous electronic-structure theory of strictly localized orbitals associated with a spatial partition of the one-electron Hilbert space that remains well defined in the complete basis-set limit. Each strictly localized orbital is supported on a spatial domain and may be discontinuous at domain interfaces. For a QTAIM partition, the effective atomic orbitals introduced by Mayer provide an example of such strictly localized orbitals. Using the interior-penalty discontinuous Galerkin method, these strictly localized orbitals can be employed in variational electronic-structure calculations despite their discontinuities at domain interfaces.

Numerical illustrations on one-dimensional diatomic model systems serve as a proof of concept for the proposed framework. They show that variational calculations can be carried out in a basis of strictly localized orbitals while maintaining good agreement with conventional calculations. The localized representation also enables a direct decomposition of many-electron wave functions into domain-pair contributions, providing a transparent interpretation of the ionic and covalent character of chemical bonds.

In future work, the present approach should be tested on real molecular systems. The present framework of strictly localized orbitals based on spatial domain decomposition could be useful in VB theory
ShaHib-BOOK-08
and in local-correlation methods
Nag-CS-24
, and may also offer a natural framework for quantum embedding methods based on spatially localized subsystems
JonMosSchRat-JACS-20
.
