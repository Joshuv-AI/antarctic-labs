Independent factors associated with ICL haptic position: a multivariate analysis of anterior and posterior segment parameters
Authors: Qin Wang, Rundong Yang, Qizhi Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo analyze anterior segment anatomy in relation to the ICL haptic position, and identify independently associated factors using multivariate analysis.MethodsOne month after ICL implantation, ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) was used to observe the position of the ICL haptics. Patients were classified into three groups based on haptic position: ciliary sulcus, ciliary body, and under the ciliary body. Preoperative ocular structural parameters were collected. One-way ANOVA was used to compare the parameters among the three groups, and multivariate multinomial logistic regression was performed to identify factors independently associated with haptic position.ResultsThe numbers of patients (eyes) with haptics in the ciliary sulcus, ciliary body, and under the ciliary body were 34 (47 eyes), 26 (42 eyes), and 20 (38 eyes), respectively. Univariate analysis showed significant differences in vault, white-to-white distance (WTW), anterior chamber depth (ACD), spherical equivalent (SE), anterior chamber volume (ACV), lens thickness(LT), crystalline lens rise (CLR), maximum ciliary body thickness (CBTmax), trabecular-ciliary angle (TCA), and iris-ciliary process distance (ICPD) among the three groups (all P < 0.05). Multivariate multinomial logistic regression revealed that age (OR per 5 years = 1.21, 95% CI 1.06–1.39, P = 0.006), SE (OR per 1 D = 1.84, 95% CI 1.24–2.74, P = 0.003), and ICL size (OR per 1 mm = 0.022, 95% CI 0.002–0.279, P = 0.003) were independently associated with ciliary sulcus haptic placement compared with under the ciliary body placement.ConclusionsThe position of ICL haptics strongly correlates with vault. Haptic position is independently associated with age, SE, and ICL size. The apparent associations of ACV and ciliary body morphology with haptic position, as observed in univariate analyses, may be due to confounding factors such as age.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10792-026-04184-7.

Introduction

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) -approved implantable collamer lens (ICL, STAAR Surgical, Nidau, Switzerland) for correcting myopia and astigmatism is an extremely hydrophilic and highly biocompatible flexible collagen copolymer. The lens is surgically implanted into the posterior chamber behind the iris, with its four haptics positioned in the ciliary sulcus. It has evolved from the original ICL V1 to the ICL V5c [1]. In recent years, ICL implantation has been increasingly used to correct low to moderate myopia and has become an alternative for patients with refractive errors who cannot undergo corneal laser surgery [2]. Numerous studies have demonstrated its good predictability, safety, efficacy, and stability [3]. As a result, ICL implantation increased substantially. In June 2024, STAAR officially announced that more than 3 million ICL implants had been placed worldwide. Nevertheless, complications following ICL implantation continue to be reported, most of which are related to abnormal vault. Among them, the chance of secondary surgery due to vault problems was 0.8%-3.4% [3, 4]. Abnormal vault is the cause of complications including high IOP, anterior subcapsular cataract, iris pigment dispersion, and corneal endothelial decompensation.

Ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) is a high-frequency ultrasound technique that can display the anatomy of the anterior segment of the eye [5]. It can visualize the position of the ICL haptics postoperatively [6, 7] and is therefore an effective method for postoperative evaluation. After implantation, the ICL is positioned in the posterior chamber, with its four haptics located in the ciliary sulcus. However, studies have found that not all ICL haptics are located in the ciliary sulcus. This was first discovered in the research of Zhang X et al. [6, 8]. In clinical practice, an abnormal ICL haptic position can cause the ICL to tilt within the posterior chamber. When all ICL haptics are located under the ciliary body, this is likely to result in a low vault and increased rotational instability (Fig. 1) [9].Fig. 1ICL haptic position and corresponding vault. a Abnormal haptic position causing ICL tilt; b Haptics in the ciliary sulcus—high vault; c Haptics on the ciliary body—normal vault; (d) Haptics under the ciliary body—low vault

The factors affecting ICL haptic position diversity have not been fully elucidated. Therefore, more research is needed to explore their influencing factors. This study used UBM to observe the position of the ICL haptic one month after ICL implantation and to identify factors independently associated with haptic position using multivariate analysis, aiming to reduce the incidence of abnormal haptic position after ICL implantation and improve the safety of ICL implantation.

Subjects and methods

Subjects

Patients with refractive errors who underwent ICL V4c implantation at Chongqing Eye and Vision Care Hospital. Aier Eye Hospital Group, from March 2021 to December 2021 were enrolled in this study. The inclusion criteria were:(1) age between 18 and 40 years; (2) the refractive state was stable for more than 2 years, and the annual change was not more than − 0.50 D; (3) central anterior chamber depth(ACD) ≥ 2.8 mm; (4) corneal endothelial cell count (ECD) ≥ 2000 cells /mm2; (5) normal intraocular pressure (IOP) (10-21 mmHg); (6) high-quality UBM images; (7) The size of implanted ICL was determined strictly according to the manufacturer ‘s recommendations. The exclusion criteria were: (1) Long-term use of drugs that constrict the pupils, resulting in morphological deformation or changes in the pupils; (2) Presence of various eye diseases, including glaucoma, traumatic diseases, ciliary cysts, corneal lesions, and active ocular inflammation; (3) History of iris laser treatment or other ocular surgeries; (4) Patients with severe systemic diseases, mental illnesses (depression, mania, etc.), and those who were breastfeeding or pregnant. According to the principles of the Helsinki Declaration, all patients provided informed written consent. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Ethics Review Committee of Chongqing Eye and Vision Care Hospital, Aier Eye Hospital Group (Approval No. IRB2023007).

Examinations

All subjects underwent comprehensive ophthalmic examinations, including uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA), best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), SE, IOP, slit-lamp microscopy, scanning laser ophthalmoscopy (SLO; Nikon, Japan), B-scan ultrasonography (Tianjin Maida Medical Technology Co., Ltd., China), and anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT; Carl Zeiss, Germany).

ECD was measured using a non-contact specular microscope (Topcon, Japan); White to white distance (WTW), ACD, Central corneal thickness (CCT),anterior chamber volume (ACV), flat corneal curvature (K1), steep corneal curvature (K2), mean corneal curvature (Km), and lens thickness (LT) were measured by Pentacam (OCULUS, Germany); Axial length (AL) was measured by IOL Master 500(Carl Zeiss, Germany).

The posterior chamber structure was examined by UBM (Tianjin Maida Medical Technology Co., Ltd., China). The UBM was performed by two experienced operators (XFF and YL). Vertical (6 to 12 o ‘clock) and horizontal (3 to 9 o ‘clock) line images were obtained. Each scan showed clear reflections of the scleral process, anterior chamber angle, iris, ciliary body, zonular fibers, and the anterior surface of the lens. To ensure the image quality, three repeated measurements were performed. ACD, horizontal sulcus-to-sulcus diameter (h-STS) from 3 to 9 o ‘clock, vertical sulcus-to-sulcus diameter (v-STS) from 12 to 6 o ‘clock, the crystalline lens rise (CLR), and other parameters of the posterior chamber (2, 4, 8, and 10 o ‘clock) were measured by another experienced physician (QW) using the linear distance measurement and angle measurement function of the UBM device. The average of three measurements was calculated. The mean value of the parameters from the four sites was used for statistical analysis. Surgical procedure

The appropriate ICL V4c size was calculated using the online formula provided by STAAR Surgical. All surgeries were performed by the same experienced surgeon (ZQZ) following standard surgical protocols. The ICL V4c was positioned horizontally (3–9 o’clock). All procedures were completed successfully without intraoperative complications. Postoperatively, steroid and antibiotic eye drops were prescribed four times daily for two weeks.

Follow-up

All patients underwent follow-up examinations at 3 h, 1 day, 1 week, and 1 month after ICLV4c implantation, including UCVA, BCVA, and slit lamp microscopy. At the one-month postoperative follow-up, UBM was used to verify the position of the ICL V4c haptics, measure the vault, and record the haptic position. Statistical analysis

SPSS (26.0, IBM) software was used for statistical analysis. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and non-normally distributed variables as median (interquartile range) [M (P25, P75)]. Descriptive statistical analysis was used to analyze the distribution of haptic position. One-way analysis of variance was used to compare the age and ocular parameters among the three groups of different haptic position groups, and the LSD-t test was used for pairwise comparison. The Kruskal–Wallis H test was used to compare skewed distribution data. Multivariate multinomial logistic regression was performed to identify factors independently associated with haptic position, with the under the ciliary body group set as the reference category. All variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were simultaneously entered into the multinomial logistic regression model (forced entry). The results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 80 patients (127 eyes) were included. 34 patients (47 eyes) were in the ciliary sulcus group, 26 patients (42 eyes) were in the ciliary body group, and 20 patients (38 eyes) were under the ciliary body group. Gender distribution did not differ significantly among the three groups (χ2 = 5.73, P = 0.054). The results of the comparison of parameters among the three groups are shown in Table 1. Significant differences were observed in vault, IOP, age, ICL size, ACD, WTW, ACV, LT, CLR, CBTmax, TCA, and ICPD among the three groups (all P < 0.05). The haptics in the ciliary sulcus group had the highest vault, those in the ciliary body group had an intermediate vault, and those in the under the ciliary body group had the lowest vault (Fig. 4). Comparison of parameters between three groups of different haptic positions

The comparison of age among the three groups showed a statistically significant difference (P < 0.01). There was no statistically significant difference between the haptics in the ciliary sulcus group and the ciliary body group (P > 0.05). Patients with haptics located under the ciliary body were the oldest.

IOP differed significantly among the three groups (P < 0.001), with pairwise comparisons also showing statistical significance. The highest IOP was observed when the haptics were located under the ciliary body.

SE differed significantly among the three groups (P < 0.001), with the under the ciliary body group having the highest absolute SE (Fig. 4).

ICL size differed significantly among the three groups (P < 0.01). In pairwise comparisons, significant differences were observed between the ciliary body group and the under the ciliary group (P < 0.01), as well as between the ciliary sulcus group and under the ciliary group (P < 0.01). The under the ciliary body group had the largest ICL size, significantly larger than that in the ciliary sulcus and ciliary body groups.

The comparison of ACV among the three groups showed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05). Intergroup comparisons revealed a significant difference in ACV between the ciliary sulcus group and the under the ciliary group (P < 0.05), with the under the ciliary group exhibiting a 39.46 mm3 larger ACV than the ciliary sulcus group (Fig. 4).

CBTmax was significantly higher in the ciliary sulcus and ciliary body groups compared with the under the ciliary body group (both P < 0.01; Fig. 4). TCA was significantly lower in the ciliary sulcus group compared with both the ciliary body group and the under the ciliary body group (both P < 0.01; Fig. 4). ICPD differed significantly among all three groups, with the under the ciliary body group showing the largest value, followed by the ciliary body group, and the ciliary sulcus group the smallest (all P < 0.05; Fig. 4).

To further explore the relationship between ICL size and haptic position, we calculated the difference between ICL size and the mean sulcus-to-sulcus diameter (ICL size—mean STS). As shown in Table 1, the Kruskal–Wallis test revealed a significant overall difference among the three haptic position groups (P < 0.001). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction showed that the under the ciliary body group had a significantly larger difference compared with both the ciliary sulcus group (adjusted P = 0.010) and the ciliary body group (adjusted P = 0.001). No significant difference was observed between the ciliary sulcus and ciliary body groups (adjusted P = 1.000). These findings support the hypothesis that a larger ICL size relative to the sulcus diameter is associated with under the ciliary body haptic placement.

A multivariate multinomial logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors that are independently associated with haptic position, with the under the ciliary body group as the reference category. As shown in Table 2, after adjusting for potential confounders, age, SE, and ICL size remained significantly associated with haptic position. Specifically, each 5-year increase in age was associated with a 1.21-fold higher likelihood of ciliary sulcus placement compared with under the ciliary body placement (95% CI 1.06–1.39, P = 0.006). Each 1 D increase in SE was associated with a 1.84-fold higher likelihood of ciliary sulcus placement (95% CI 1.24–2.74, P = 0.003) and a 1.59-fold higher likelihood of ciliary body placement (95% CI 1.06–2.39, P = 0.027). Regarding ICL size, each 1 mm increase was associated with a lower likelihood of ciliary sulcus placement (OR = 0.022, 95% CI 0.002–0.279, P = 0.003). Discussion

In this study, vault was strongly correlated with haptic position: the highest vault was associated with ciliary sulcus placement, and the lowest with under the ciliary body placement. Multivariate multinomial logistic regression identified age, spherical equivalent (SE), and ICL size as the only independent predictors of haptic position. In contrast, several parameters that differed significantly in univariate analyses—including anterior chamber volume (ACV), maximum ciliary body thickness (CBTmax), trabecular ciliary angle (TCA), and iris‑ciliary process distance (ICPD)—did not retain statistical significance after multivariate adjustment. These findings indicate that although these anatomical parameters differ among haptic position groups, they are not independent predictors.

Our findings align with recent studies highlighting the importance of age and SE in vault prediction. Alfonso et al. [10] and Trancón et al. [11] reported that vault decreases with age, consistent with our observation that older patients are more likely to have under the ciliary body haptic placement. Tan et al. [12] found that higher myopia was associated with haptic placement under the ciliary body, and our multivariate analysis confirms SE as an independent predictor. However, our results differ from studies that reported ciliary body morphology as an independent predictor of vault [13–14]. This discrepancy likely arises from differences in statistical approach: those studies used univariate or simple linear regression without adjusting for age—a well‑known confounder. When age was included, the independent effect of ciliary body parameters diminished, suggesting that age may be a more fundamental determinant. A recent meta‑analysis by Zhang et al. [15] also emphasized age as a key factor influencing vault.

The independent association of age with haptic position may be explained by age‑related ocular changes, including increased lens thickness, greater crystalline lens rise, pupil narrowing, and iris deformation [15–16]. These changes reduce the available space in the posterior chamber, making under the ciliary body haptic placement more likely in older patients. The association of higher myopia with ciliary sulcus placement (higher vault) may be related to the thinner, lighter ICLs used for lower corrections, which could be more easily displaced anteriorly. Regarding ICL size, our finding that larger ICLs are associated with under the ciliary body placement may seem counterintuitive, but a similar trend was noted by Tan et al. [12] and is supported by our exploratory analysis. Specifically, the difference between ICL size and mean sulcus‑to‑sulcus diameter was significantly larger in the under the ciliary body group (Supplementary Table 2). This supports the hypothesis that an oversized ICL (relative to the sulcus diameter) cannot rest stably in the ciliary sulcus and may override or slip under the ciliary body—a phenomenon reported in other studies [12].

IOP differed significantly among groups in univariate analysis but did not in multivariate analysis, suggesting that age or SE may explain the observed differences. Low vault may impair aqueous humor circulation through the central port [17]; however, further studies are needed.

These findings have important clinical implications. Preoperative assessment for ICL implantation should prioritize patient age, SE, and careful ICL size selection. The finding that ciliary body morphology is not independently associated with haptic position suggests that routine preoperative assessment of ciliary body parameters may not be necessary when age, SE, and ICL size are already considered.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective, cross-sectional, single-center design may introduce selection bias. Second, haptic position classification was performed by a single observer without formal interobserver agreement assessment, which may affect reproducibility. Third, the regression model included a relatively large number of predictors (n = 12) relative to the sample size (127 eyes), reflected in the wide confidence intervals for some estimates; therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution and require external validation. Fourth, both eyes from some patients were included without adjustment for inter-eye correlation. While a sensitivity analysis selecting one eye per patient yielded consistent results, this remains a potential source of bias, and future studies should consider mixed-effects models or generalized estimating equations.

In summary, haptic position is closely related to vault. Although univariate analysis revealed significant differences in ciliary body morphology among haptic position groups, multivariate analysis demonstrated that these differences are not independent but are largely explained by age, SE, and ICL size. The true independent predictors of haptic position are age, SE, and ICL size. Therefore, preoperative assessment for ICL implantation should prioritize patient age, SE, and careful ICL size selection.
