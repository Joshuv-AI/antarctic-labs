Beyond TNM staging: validation of a CT-derived nutritional-inflammatory composite model (RFMmSIS) for prognostication in resectable gastric cancer
Authors: Ji-Yun Zhu, Qing Zhong, Jun-Hua Yu, Dong Wu, Yu-Qin Sun, Zhi‐Yu Liu, Guang-Tan Lin, Zi‐Hao Yao, Zhi‐Xin Shang‐Guan, Jun-Yu Chen, Cai-Ming Wen, Tao-Yuan Qiu, Jia‐Bin Wang, Jian‐Xian Lin, Qi‐Yue Chen, Ju‐Li Lin, Jian‐Wei Xie, Ping Li, Chao‐Hui Zheng, Chang‐Ming Huang

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrognostic models for resectable gastric cancer rarely integrate adipose tissue and immune-inflammatory parameters. This study evaluated the predictive value of preoperative relative fat mass (RFM) and the modified systemic inflammation score (mSIS), and explored their potential synergy with nanomedicine-based theranostics.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 518 patients who underwent radical gastrectomy across three tertiary centers in China (overall enrollment period: 2010–2019). Preoperative CT-derived waist circumference at L3 was used to calculate RFM, with the optimal cutoff determined by ROC analysis using the Youden index. Univariate and multivariate Cox regressions identified independent prognostic factors and constructed the nutritional-inflammatory composite parameter RFMmSIS. Predictive performance was assessed using AUC, AIC, BIC, NRI, IDI, and DCA. Postoperative follow-up was conducted every 3 months during the first 2 years, every 6 months during years 3 through 5, and annually thereafter.ResultsPatients were randomly allocated to training (n = 362) and validation (n = 156) cohorts. RFM outperformed BMI for survival prediction (AUC: 0.584 vs. 0.503, P < 0.05) and was associated with worse 3-year overall survival (OS) (64.1% vs. 80.0%, P = 0.014) and disease-free survival (DFS) (57.7% vs. 78.4%, P = 0.005). Multivariate analysis identified RFM (HR: 2.355) and mSIS grade 2 (HR: 1.657) as independent prognostic factors. The composite RFMmSIS stratified patients into high-, intermediate-, and low-risk groups, with higher scores associated with increased severe complications (Clavien-Dindo ≥IIIa: 4.7% vs. 4.2% vs. 15.2%, P = 0.005 in training cohort) and poorer 3-year OS/DFS (high-risk: 44.3%/41.0%). RFMmSIS demonstrated superior predictive accuracy for OS and DFS compared with operative extent and pN stage (all P < 0.05).ConclusionsPreoperative RFMmSIS is a clinically feasible predictor of short-term complications and long-term survival in gastric cancer, offering enhanced prognostic stratification beyond traditional parameters and providing a rationale for integrating nutritional-inflammatory risk assessment with nanomedicine-based theranostic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16549-3.Keywords: Gastric cancer, RFM, mSIS, Overall survival, Disease-free survival, Short-term outcome, Long-term outcome

Introduction

Gastric cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignancies of the digestive tract worldwide, ranking fifth in both incidence and mortality [1]. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated a significant association between body composition and the progression and prognosis of gastric cancer [2]. For instance, sarcopenia has been associated with adverse clinical outcomes and increased chemotherapy-related toxicity in elderly patients [3], whereas obesity is recognized as a significant risk factor for gastric cancer development and progression [4]. Relative fat mass (RFM), an imaging-based anthropometric index calculated using height, waist circumference, and sex, has demonstrated superior accuracy compared with body mass index (BMI) in predicting obesity-related risks across diverse populations, particularly among older adults and women [5–8]. Recent studies have highlighted its strong correlation with cardiovascular outcomes (AUC = 0.86) [5–8]. Furthermore, ethnic-specific variations in RFM-defined obesity rates and sex-specific thresholds underscore its adaptability to heterogeneous cohorts, as supported by multi-ethnic validation studies [9, 10]. However, the prognostic value of RFM in gastric cancer remains unexplored.

Furthermore, chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation represent pivotal mechanisms underlying tumorigenesis and tumor progression [11, 12]. The modified systemic inflammation score (mSIS), a serological composite marker integrating systemic inflammatory and immune status, can dynamically reflect changes in the inflammatory and immune milieu and has demonstrated superior predictive accuracy for gastric cancer prognosis [13, 14]. Previous studies have demonstrated that elevated preoperative mSIS is significantly associated with increased postoperative complications and poorer prognosis in patients with gastric cancer.

Given the paucity of data regarding the predictive value of RFM for short-term and long-term outcomes in patients undergoing gastric cancer surgery, combined with the established prognostic significance of preoperative serum inflammatory and immune markers, this study aimed to evaluate the predictive efficacy of RFM and preoperative mSIS for survival in patients with resectable gastric cancer using multi-center data. Furthermore, we sought to integrate mSIS and RFM to develop a novel nutritional-inflammatory composite parameter, RFMmSIS, and investigate its predictive value for both short-term and long-term outcomes in this patient population.

Furthermore, recent advances in cancer nanomedicine have introduced multifunctional nanoparticles capable of simultaneous diagnostic imaging and targeted therapy (theranostics) for gastrointestinal malignancies [15, 16]. However, the clinical translation of nanomedicine in gastric cancer remains limited by the lack of robust preoperative risk stratification tools that can identify patients who would most benefit from nanoparticle-based drug delivery or image-guided interventions [17]. Integrating conventional CT-derived nutritional-inflammatory indices with nanomedicine platforms may bridge this gap, enabling personalized theranostic strategies tailored to tumor biology and host metabolic status.

Methods

Patients and study design

This retrospective study enrolled a total of 552 patients with histologically confirmed primary gastric adenocarcinoma, comprising 409 patients from an imageomics research cohort at Fujian Medical University Union Hospital (January 2010 to December 2013) [18], and 143 patients who underwent radical gastrectomy at the First Affiliated Hospital of Ningbo University (n = 113) and Quzhou People’s Hospital (n = 30) (January 2017 to December 2019). Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) underwent D2 lymph node dissection for gastric cancer; (2) no evidence of distant metastasis or invasion of adjacent organs (pancreas, spleen, liver, colon, etc.) on preoperative imaging or intraoperative examination; (3) histologically confirmed gastric adenocarcinoma (pathological stage pT1-T4a, pN0-3, pM0); and (4) availability of complete preoperative abdominal CT images. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) residual gastric cancer or neuroendocrine carcinoma; (2) receipt of neoadjuvant chemotherapy prior to surgery; (3) underwent palliative gastrectomy; (4) death within 30 days postoperatively; and (5) incomplete clinical or pathological data. Pathological staging was performed according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system. Patients with stage II/III gastric cancer were treated according to the East Asian standard (D2 gastrectomy followed by adjuvant chemotherapy). For patients with stage II or advanced gastric cancer, adjuvant chemotherapy based on 5-fluorouracil (typically oxaliplatin combined with capecitabine [Xeloda] or S-1) was recommended as the standard regimen.

Patient selection across the three tertiary centers followed uniform inclusion and exclusion criteria prospectively established by the study steering committee. All gastric resections with D2 lymphadenectomy were performed by board-certified gastrointestinal surgeons according to the Japanese Gastric Cancer Treatment Guidelines. Core perioperative standards, including curative intent (R0 resection), standardized anesthetic management by dedicated hepatobiliary anesthesia teams, and consistent postoperative care pathways, were shared across the three centers. While minor institutional variations in nursing details and analgesic modalities were inevitable, these were not expected to influence the primary oncologic outcomes or the predictive association of preoperative RFMmSIS.

Relative fat mass and blood samples

A single axial CT image at the level of the third lumbar vertebra (L3) was selected to quantify waist circumference, as this anatomical landmark is strongly correlated with total body adipose tissue volume (Supplementary Fig. 1) [19]. Preoperative CT scans were performed within 1–2 weeks following diagnosis to ensure accurate tumor staging. Two trained researchers independently measured waist circumference at the L3 level using standardized protocols; any discrepancies were resolved by consensus or by a third expert investigator. All CT images were de-identified prior to analysis and were processed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) following institutional ethical approval (approval number: 2021RS131). All enrolled patients underwent standardized abdominal CT imaging according to a unified protocol to ensure data comparability. CT scans were performed using scanners from identical or comparable manufacturers. Patient preparation included fasting for ≥ 6 h, supine positioning with arms elevated to minimize respiratory motion artifacts, and scanning coverage extending from the diaphragmatic dome to the pubic symphysis. Quarterly device calibration and cross-validation were performed according to established CT body composition analysis guidelines. All scanners operated at 120 kVp with a slice thickness of 5 mm. Iodinated contrast agent (1.5 mL/kg, maximum 100 mL) was administered intravenously at a rate of 3–4 mL/s, with portal venous phase imaging acquired 60–70 s post-injection. Specific scanner models included GE LightSpeed VCT (GE Healthcare), Siemens Somatom Definition AS+ (Siemens Healthineers), and Philips Brilliance iCT (Philips Healthcare).

Blood samples were collected and analyzed within 7 days prior to surgery. Laboratory parameters included fibrinogen, platelet count, white blood cell count, neutrophil count, albumin, hemoglobin, and lymphocyte count. The mSIS was calculated as follows: Grade 0 (albumin ≥ 40 g/L); Grade 1 (albumin < 40 g/L and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio [LMR] ≥ 3.4); Grade 2 (albumin < 40 g/L and LMR < 3.4). These mSIS grading criteria (albumin < 40 g/L and LMR < 3.4) were originally proposed by Lin et al. [20] and have been subsequently validated in gastric cancer cohorts.

Definition and follow-Up

The primary endpoints of this study were overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS). Secondary endpoints included postoperative complication rates. Overall survival was defined as the interval from the date of surgery to the date of death from any cause or the last follow-up. Disease-free survival was defined as the interval from the date of surgery to the date of first documented recurrence at any site or death from any cause. For DFS analysis, patients who died without documented tumor recurrence were censored at the date of last follow-up. Postoperative complications were defined as adverse events directly related to the surgical procedure occurring within 30 days following gastrectomy, excluding sequelae attributable to tumor progression or incurable disease. The severity of complications was graded according to the Clavien-Dindo classification system [21], with grades IIIa and above classified as severe complications (excluding deaths within 30 days postoperatively). Postoperative follow-up assessments were performed every 3 months during the first 2 years, and every 6 months during the third year. Postoperative follow-up assessments were performed every 3 months during the first 2 years, every 6 months during years 3 through 5, and annually thereafter until death or loss to follow-up. The last follow-up date was August 31, 2023. Routine follow-up evaluations included physical examination, laboratory tests, chest radiography, and abdominal-pelvic ultrasonography or computed tomography, as well as annual endoscopic surveillance.

Statistical analysis

Patients were randomly allocated to a training cohort (n = 362) and a validation cohort (n = 156) at a 7:3 ratio using a computer-generated randomization method in R software (version 3.6.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Randomization was performed using the createDataPartition() function from the caret package, with a fixed random seed (set.seed = 123) to ensure full computational reproducibility. In the training cohort, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis with the Youden index was used to determine the optimal cutoff value for RFM as a predictor of OS. This cutoff value was subsequently applied to the validation cohort.

Model goodness-of-fit was evaluated using the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC). Model discrimination improvement was assessed using the net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed using the ggDCA package in R software to evaluate the clinical utility of the predictive models.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 3.6.0). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using the independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, depending on data distribution. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to assess correlations between continuous variables.

Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed using logistic regression for binary outcomes and Cox proportional hazards regression for time-to-event outcomes. Multivariable models adjusted for established prognostic factors including pathological T and N stage, operative range (total, distal, or proximal gastrectomy), and, for postoperative complication analyses, operation time and intraoperative blood loss. Hazard ratios (HRs) and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Fig. 1), a total of 518 patients with gastric cancer were ultimately enrolled and randomly allocated to a training cohort (n = 362) and a validation cohort (n = 156) at a 7:3 ratio. In the training cohort, 221 patients (61.0%) were aged ≥ 65 years, and 268 patients (74.0%) were male. The baseline characteristics of patients in the validation cohort were comparable to those in the training cohort. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups with respect to age, sex, preoperative BMI, tumor size, pT stage, pN stage, tumor location, or histological differentiation (all P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Survival prognostic features of high and low RFM groups

ROC curve analysis in the training cohort demonstrated that RFM exhibited significantly better predictive performance for survival compared with BMI (AUC: 0.584 vs. 0.503, P < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 2), with consistent findings observed in the validation cohort. Using the optimal cutoff value of 62.32 for RFM, 425 patients (82.1%) were classified into the high-RFM group and 93 patients (17.9%) into the low-RFM group (Supplementary Table 1). Patients in the high-RFM group exhibited significantly worse overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) compared with those in the low-RFM group (3-year OS: 64.1% vs. 80.0%, P = 0.014; 3-year DFS: 57.7% vs. 78.4%, P = 0.005) (Fig. 2).

Comparative ROC curve analysis of preoperative inflammatory markers revealed that mSIS demonstrated the highest AUC value among the evaluated indicators (mSIS > LMR > SII > NLR) (Supplementary Fig. 2). Based on mSIS scores, patients were categorized into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups. The low-mSIS group exhibited significantly better 3-year OS compared with the intermediate- and high-mSIS groups (78.5% vs. 66.1% vs. 50.2%, P < 0.001), with similar findings observed in the validation cohort (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Univariate Cox regression analysis identified preoperative mSIS, surgical procedure, pT stage, pN stage, and preoperative RFM as factors significantly associated with both OS and DFS in the training and validation cohorts (all P < 0.05). Multivariate Cox regression analysis further demonstrated that preoperative RFM, mSIS, pT stage, and pN stage were independent prognostic factors for OS and DFS in both cohorts (all P < 0.05) (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 2).

Establishment of a new survival prediction indicator based on RFM and mSIS

Based on the Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox regression results, and following confirmation of no significant correlation between RFM and mSIS (r = -0.082, 95% CI: -0.168 to 0.005, P = 0.068) (Supplementary Fig. 4), preoperative RFM and mSIS scores were integrated to develop a novel composite parameter, RFMmSIS.

The RFMmSIS scoring system was established as follows:

RFMmSIS-H (high-risk group): High RFM combined with mSIS score of 2.

RFMmSIS-H (high-risk group): High RFM combined with mSIS score of 2.

RFMmSIS-L (low-risk group): Low RFM combined with mSIS score of 0.

RFMmSIS-L (low-risk group): Low RFM combined with mSIS score of 0.

RFMmSIS-M (intermediate-risk group): The remaining four subgroups, which exhibited similar survival rates with no statistically significant differences in pairwise comparisons (all P > 0.05). (Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 3).

RFMmSIS-M (intermediate-risk group): The remaining four subgroups, which exhibited similar survival rates with no statistically significant differences in pairwise comparisons (all P > 0.05). (Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 3).

Predictive performance of preoperative RFMmSIS for short-term outcomes

The overall postoperative complication rates in the low-, intermediate-, and high-risk RFMmSIS groups were 11.6%, 10.4%, and 29.1%, respectively (P < 0.001), in the training cohort, and 14.3%, 16.3%, and 35.5%, respectively (P = 0.051), in the validation cohort (Supplementary Table 4). The distribution of specific complication types across RFMmSIS risk groups is detailed in Supplementary Table 5. In the training cohort, high-risk patients exhibited significantly elevated rates of pulmonary infection (15.2% vs. 5.4–7.0%, P = 0.023) and wound complication (3.8% vs. 0%, P = 0.020) compared with low- and intermediate-risk groups. Anastomotic leakage showed a consistent directional trend (2.5% vs. 0%, P = 0.060), while intra-abdominal infection did not differ significantly across groups (4.7–7.6%, P = 0.601). The validation cohort showed consistent directional trends for all complication types (e.g., pulmonary infection: 22.6% vs. 7.7–9.5%, P = 0.070), although the smaller sample size limited statistical power for subtype-specific comparisons. The severity of complications was graded according to the Clavien-Dindo classification system (Supplementary Table 6). Severe complications (grade ≥IIIa) occurred in 4.7%, 4.2%, and 15.2% in low-, intermediate-, and high-risk RFMmSIS groups, respectively, in the training cohort (P = 0.003), with consistent trends in the validation cohort (4.8%, 7.7%, and 19.4%, P = 0.111), suggesting a correlation between RFMmSIS risk group and complication severity.

Univariate logistic regression analysis in the training cohort identified sex, age ≥ 65 years, operative time, intraoperative blood loss, tumor size, pN stage, and RFMmSIS as factors significantly associated with postoperative complications. In the validation cohort, sex, operative time, tumor size, and RFMmSIS were significantly associated with postoperative complications. Multivariate logistic regression analysis in the training cohort demonstrated that operative time (odds ratio [OR]: 3.51, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.00-4.21, P = 0.027) and RFMmSIS (OR: 7.49, 95% CI: 2.05–27.35, P < 0.001) were independent predictors of postoperative complications (Supplementary Tables 7 and Supplementary Table 8), with consistent findings in the validation cohort.

ROC curve analysis revealed that RFMmSIS exhibited superior predictive performance compared with operative time (AUC: 0.692 vs. 0.651, P < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 6). AIC analysis demonstrated better goodness-of-fit for RFMmSIS compared with operative time (296.4 vs. 302.1, P < 0.05). NRI and IDI analyses indicated that RFMmSIS improved predictive accuracy by 12.2% and 1.7%, respectively, compared with operative time (Supplementary Table 9). Decision curve analysis demonstrated that RFMmSIS provided greater net clinical benefit for predicting postoperative complications, with similar findings observed in the validation cohort (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Predictive performance of preoperative RFMmSIS for long-term outcomes

Kaplan-Meier survival curves for OS and DFS according to RFMmSIS risk groups are presented in Fig. 3. In the training cohort, the 3-year OS rates for the high-, intermediate-, and low-risk groups were 44.3%, 71.3%, and 87.5%, respectively. The corresponding 3-year DFS rates were 41.0%, 64.5%, and 87.4%, respectively. These results demonstrated good discriminatory ability among the three risk groups, with all pairwise comparisons reaching statistical significance (all P < 0.05).

Univariate and multivariate analyses of the factors associated with OS and DFS in Training cohort with RFMmSIS

ROC curve analysis revealed that RFMmSIS exhibited superior predictive performance compared with the extent of surgical resection and pN stage for both OS and DFS (OS AUC: 0.689 vs. 0.567 vs. 0.609; DFS AUC: 0.624 vs. 0.537 vs. 0.594) (Supplementary Fig. 7). AIC analysis demonstrated better goodness-of-fit for RFMmSIS compared with the extent of surgical resection and pN stage (OS: 158.4 vs. 521.8 vs. 501.0; DFS: 500.2 vs. 516.6 vs. 515.8; all P < 0.05). BIC analysis further supported the superiority of RFMmSIS (OS: 164.5 vs. 533.5 vs. 520.5; DFS: 519.7 vs. 528.3 vs. 527.6; all P < 0.05). NRI and IDI analyses indicated that RFMmSIS improved predictive performance for OS and DFS compared with the extent of surgical resection (Supplementary Table 11). Decision curve analysis demonstrated that RFMmSIS provided greater net clinical benefit for predicting OS and DFS across a range of probability thresholds (Supplementary Fig. 8), with similar trends observed in the validation cohort.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the clinical utility of RFM, an obesity-related anthropometric index, for predicting both short-term and long-term outcomes in patients with resectable gastric cancer using multi-center data. This study was designed to complement existing prognostic indicators such as BMI while incorporating the impact of preoperative immune-inflammatory status on patient prognosis. Our findings demonstrate that both RFM and mSIS are independent predictors of long-term outcomes in gastric cancer patients. Furthermore, the novel nutritional-inflammatory composite parameter, RFMmSIS, developed in this study, exhibited superior predictive performance for both postoperative complications and long-term survival compared with other clinical indicators.

Obesity is a well-established risk factor for various common cancers, including gastric cancer. Although various obesity indices, such as body mass index (BMI), body fat percentage (BFP), waist circumference (WC), and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), are widely used for preoperative assessment of gastric cancer patients due to their convenience and objectivity, these measures may not accurately reflect body composition or the specific impact of the perioperative state on postoperative outcomes [22–24]. Recent meta-analyses and large-scale validation studies have confirmed the clinical utility of RFM. A 2024 prospective cohort study in community-dwelling older adults confirmed RFM’s superior discriminatory accuracy for cardiometabolic risk compared with BMI [25]. Additionally, a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis in patients with oesophago-gastric cancer demonstrated that sarcopenic obesity—characterized by low skeletal muscle mass combined with high visceral fat mass—was significantly associated with worse postoperative outcomes, supporting the prognostic value of comprehensive body composition assessment beyond simple anthropometric measures [26]. Beyond these epidemiological associations, the biological rationale for RFM’s superiority over BMI lies in its direct quantification of visceral adiposity, which is more strongly associated with metabolic dysregulation and chronic low-grade inflammation. Visceral adipose tissue acts as an endocrine organ, secreting pro-inflammatory adipokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α, leptin) and promoting insulin resistance, creating a tumor-promoting microenvironment that BMI-which cannot distinguish between muscle, subcutaneous fat, and visceral fat-fails to capture. Moreover, the biological plausibility of adiposity-driven inflammation worsening oncologic outcomes is well-established by recent research. Excess visceral adiposity promotes a chronic pro-inflammatory state through sustained secretion of IL-6, TNF-α, and C-reactive protein, which drive tumor cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastatic spread. Additionally, adipose tissue infiltration by pro-tumorigenic immune cells (M2 macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells) creates an immunosuppressive microenvironment that impairs anti-tumor immunity and may reduce chemotherapy efficacy. In the present study, the combination of the adiposity assessment index RFM with the systemic immune-inflammatory marker mSIS (RFMmSIS) demonstrated significantly better predictive performance compared with operative time for predicting postoperative complications (AUC: 0.629 vs. 0.580). Moreover, relying solely on obesity-related indicators to assess long-term survival prognosis in gastric cancer patients may overlook other crucial immune-inflammatory factors, which may be more reflective of long-term survival status than obesity indices such as BMI [27, 28].

While the preceding discussion addresses local adipose-driven inflammation, systemic inflammatory markers provide a complementary, circulating perspective on host-tumor interactions. Systemic inflammatory-nutritional indicators can reflect the activity of tumor-related inflammation and the patient’s overall inflammatory and nutritional status, as well as surgical tolerance, thereby enabling effective assessment of treatment outcomes and prognosis in gastric cancer patients [29, 30]. In the present study, comparative analysis of preoperative serum inflammatory markers revealed that mSIS exhibited superior predictive performance for OS compared with other evaluated indicators (AUC: mSIS > LMR > SII > NLR). Based on these findings, we developed the novel nutritional-inflammatory composite parameter RFMmSIS, which integrates both nutritional status and systemic inflammation. Elevated preoperative RFMmSIS may indicate increased systemic inflammatory response, active tumor progression, exacerbation of chronic inflammation secondary to tumor burden, or a combination of these factors. Similar to BMI and other immune-inflammatory markers reported in previous studies [31], RFMmSIS may serve as a surrogate marker of tumor burden and could represent a dynamic factor that changes over time in response to treatment. Our results demonstrate that RFMmSIS exhibited significantly better predictive accuracy compared with pathological stage, extent of surgical resection, and other single clinical indicators for both OS and DFS. Furthermore, previous research has demonstrated that, given the multifactorial nature of tumorigenesis and tumor progression, composite indicators generally outperform single markers in predictive performance [32]. Therefore, we believe that RFMmSIS represents a more accurate predictor of long-term prognosis in gastric cancer.

From a clinical perspective, the pathological nodal (pN) stage in the 8th edition of the AJCC TNM staging system remains the most critical factor for assessing lymph node metastasis and predicting prognosis in gastric cancer [33]. Additionally, the extent of surgical resection significantly impacts patient outcomes, as surgical resection not only achieves physical tumor removal but also represents a key determinant of survival and quality of life [34]. However, substantial heterogeneity in prognosis exists even among patients with identical pN stages and surgical approaches. In the present study, following univariate and multivariate analyses, we performed head-to-head comparisons of RFMmSIS with the extent of surgical resection and pN stage for survival prediction. RFMmSIS demonstrated significantly higher predictive accuracy for both OS and DFS compared with pN stage. Furthermore, decision curve analysis confirmed the superior clinical utility of RFMmSIS compared with the extent of resection and pN stage. Therefore, RFMmSIS may serve as a valuable adjunct to the TNM staging system, potentially enhancing postoperative risk stratification and guiding personalized follow-up strategies for gastric cancer patients. While RFMmSIS is primarily a prognostic tool rather than a determinant of surgical extent, high RFMmSIS scores may inform adjuvant therapy intensity. Specifically, patients in the high-risk group may benefit from more intensive adjuvant chemotherapy regimens or closer postoperative surveillance. However, RFMmSIS should complement rather than replace existing staging systems in surgical decision-making. On the other side, compared with validated prognostic nomograms such as the MAGIC prediction model and the Italian Research Group for Gastric Cancer (GIRCG) nomogram, RFMmSIS offers the advantage of requiring only routinely available preoperative CT and laboratory parameters without complex calculations. While head-to-head comparison with these nomograms was not performed in this study, RFMmSIS demonstrated superior discriminatory performance compared with pN stage and extent of resection (OS AUC: 0.689 vs. 0.609 vs. 0.567), suggesting comparable if not superior clinical utility.

Furthermore, our findings may have implications for future clinical management strategies through a tiered intervention framework. First, according to ESPEN guidelines, patients with elevated RFMmSIS scores may benefit from hypocaloric, high-protein diets (e.g., 1.5 g/kg/day protein) combined with aerobic exercise (e.g., 30 min of brisk walking daily) to mitigate muscle loss and metabolic dysregulation [35]. Second, given the pro-inflammatory milieu inherent to high RFMmSIS, short-term use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or agents targeting the interleukin-6 (IL-6) pathway may help suppress pro-tumorigenic inflammatory microenvironments in these patients [36, 37]. Third, regarding postoperative adjuvant therapy, patients with elevated RFMmSIS scores may warrant more intensive chemotherapy regimens or closer surveillance; the potential interaction between systemic inflammatory status and immunotherapy efficacy represents a promising avenue for future investigation [38, 39].

Finally, while this cohort was derived from East Asian populations, the biological mechanisms linking visceral adiposity and systemic inflammation to oncologic outcomes are generalizable across ethnicities [27]. However, the absolute cutoff of 62.32 derived from our East Asian cohort may shift in Western populations with higher baseline BMI distributions, necessitating external validation. Recent evidence from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA, n ≈ 1,500) demonstrates that BMI obscures substantial racial/ethnic variation in visceral adiposity assessed by CT [40]. In this multi-ethnic cohort, visceral adiposity predicted metabolic risk even after accounting for BMI [40], underscoring that directly measured adipose compartments capture metabolically active fat more accurately than BMI across ethnicities. These findings underscore a key advantage of RFM over BMI: by incorporating waist circumference—a robust proxy for visceral adiposity—and height, RFM directly captures the metabolically active fat compartment that drives oncologic risk, rather than total body mass. The original RFM algorithm was developed and validated across diverse ethnic groups [8], and its superiority over BMI in cardiometabolic risk prediction has been confirmed in multi-ethnic settings [5, 8, 10]. Therefore, while the absolute RFM cutoff may require recalibration in Western cohorts, the composite principle of RFMmSIS—identifying patients with concomitant high visceral adiposity and systemic inflammation—should remain biologically valid across ethnicities. Furthermore, the rising incidence of non-cardia gastric cancer among young adults in Western populations, such as US non-Hispanic whites, often associated with obesity and metabolic dysregulation, suggests that nutritional-inflammatory prognostic tools like RFMmSIS may become increasingly applicable in Western settings [41].

Looking beyond conventional prognostication, the integration of RFMmSIS with emerging nanomedicine platforms represents a promising translational avenue. The convergence of nutritional-inflammatory risk stratification and nanomedicine holds substantial promise for advancing gastric cancer management [15]. Recent progress in cancer nanotechnology has demonstrated that multifunctional nanoparticles can simultaneously achieve enhanced CT/MRI contrast imaging, targeted drug delivery, and modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment [42, 43]. For instance, gadolinium-doped copper sulfide nanoparticles modified with tumor-targeting ligands have been shown to enable bimodal magnetic resonance/fluorescence imaging of gastric cancer and lymph node metastasis, while concurrently serving as photothermal therapy agents [42]. Similarly, folic acid-functionalized superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles have been developed as both T2-weighted MRI contrast agents and PD-L1 siRNA delivery systems for gastric cancer, effectively overcoming immune resistance [43]. From the perspective of our findings, patients classified as high-risk RFMmSIS—characterized by excess visceral adiposity and systemic inflammation—may represent an ideal population for nanomedicine interventions. The enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, which facilitates nanoparticle accumulation in tumor tissues, is known to be influenced by tumor vascularity and the inflammatory milieu; thus, the pro-inflammatory, angiogenic microenvironment associated with high RFMmSIS may paradoxically enhance nanoparticle delivery efficiency [17]. Moreover, the lipophilic nature of visceral adipose tissue and the associated metabolic dysregulation could be leveraged by adipose-targeting or pH-responsive nanoparticles to achieve selective drug release [16]. Looking forward, the integration of RFMmSIS with nanotheranostic platforms could enable a “see-and-treat” paradigm: preoperative RFMmSIS assessment identifies high-risk patients, followed by administration of multifunctional nanoparticles that simultaneously report tumor inflammatory status via imaging and deliver targeted payloads (e.g., anti-inflammatory agents or immune adjuvants) to reverse the immunosuppressive microenvironment [44, 45]. This synergistic approach aligns with the broader vision of precision nanomedicine, where conventional clinical stratification tools are augmented by nanotechnology to achieve individualized diagnosis and therapy [45].

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design and single-region (East Asian) derivation limit the direct applicability of our exact cutoff values to Western populations, although the biological rationale and metric design of RFMmSIS support cross-ethnic applicability as discussed above. Second, while we have demonstrated the potential of RFMmSIS for assessing postoperative complications and prognosis, additional research is needed to elucidate its association with recurrence patterns and chemotherapy response. Future studies should address these aspects to provide more comprehensive clinical guidance. Third, the exclusion of patients who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy, while ensuring sample homogeneity, may limit the generalizability of our findings to patients undergoing neoadjuvant therapy [18]. Fourth, although we performed comparative analyses with BMI and other inflammatory markers, we did not directly compare RFMmSIS with body composition indices related to sarcopenia, which limits our ability to fully characterize the nutritional predictive value of RFMmSIS. Future studies should incorporate additional laboratory parameters and comparative indicators.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study developed a novel nutritional-inflammatory composite parameter, RFMmSIS, using multi-center data, providing new insights into perioperative systemic nutritional and inflammatory status in gastric cancer patients. RFMmSIS represents a clinically accessible and effective predictor of both short-term complications and long-term survival in gastric cancer patients. We recommend incorporating RFMmSIS assessment into routine preoperative evaluation alongside standard tumor staging and RFM measurement requires only height and waist circumference data derived from routine preoperative abdominal CT scans, making it highly feasible in clinical practice without additional cost or patient burden compared with BMI or specialized sarcopenia assessments requiring third-party software in the future.
