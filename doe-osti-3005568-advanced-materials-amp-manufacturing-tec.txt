Executive Summary

In FY24, Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) collaborated with Argonne National
Laboratory (ANL) and Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) to develop a machine-agnostic
process parameter development and maintenance plan for additive manufacturing (AM) of 316H
stainless steel. This effort aimed to increase the manufacturing readiness level of AM parts and
systems.
The team generated statistically relevant data sets of critical design properties and associated
behaviors, providing manufacturing windows for use by the DOE complex. Building on FY23
research, this study emphasized the importance of material readiness independent of machine
type, enabling rapid qualification and certification of AM parts and systems.

The LANL team conducted a comprehensive investigation to determine the necessary machine
maintenance plan and its relationship with processing parameters, mechanical behavior, and
size effects. The study also explored non-destructive evaluations, the impact of surface finish on
fatigue life, and the effects of electro-polishing and various post-processing techniques on
fatigue life. Additionally, the team established the Peregrine code (developed by ORNL) and
developed machine learning (ML) models to support these efforts.

Objective

Currently, the ASME Code has only approved six materials for nuclear construction at elevated
temperatures, underscoring the need for a more efficient material qualification process. The
traditional approach, which relies on extensive testing and modeling, can be lengthy and
resource-intensive, requiring a large dataset for designers and engineers to reference during the
design and selection process. The Additive Manufacturing Maturation and Transition (AMMT)
program aimed to integrate the development of advanced materials and manufacturing
techniques, tailored to specific application requirements, design constraints, and functional
properties. This approach enables a rapid evaluation pathway for qualification and insertion of
new materials.
In FY23, LANL conducted baseline characterization of AM 316H stainless steel specimens.
Building on this foundation, LANL expanded the dataset in FY24 through a comprehensive
investigation. This included destructive testing to further understand the material's behavior,
non-destructive testing to evaluate the material's integrity, and the development of a machine-
agnostic process parameter framework to ensure consistency across different fabrication
platforms. Additionally, LANL created a comprehensive machine maintenance plan to ensure
the reliability and performance of AM machines and collected in-situ imaging data during the
fabrication process to gain insights into material behavior. The team also explored various post-
processing methods to enhance material properties and developed ML models to predict
material behavior and properties based on process parameters and material characteristics.
The data collection, analysis, archiving, and reporting of these results from all participating sites
have been completed for FY24. The LANL results and conclusions are presented in the
following sections of this report.

 Machine Agnostics and Machine Maintenance Plan
Establishing a machine-agnostic process parameter and machine maintenance plan is crucial
for achieving manufacturing readiness for AM parts and paving the way for rapid qualification
and certification. However, the qualification process itself is intricate and time-consuming,
necessitating meticulous analyses, measurements, and assessments throughout the entire
fabrication process, as well as post-acceptance testing. The complexity arises from the
numerous variables influencing the AM process, including the type of AM system, system
health, material properties, part design, part location, and operator expertise.

Currently, there is no single, unified standard that captures the qualification procedures for
metal AM, due to the diverse requirements and nuances of different AM systems. Instead,
standard organizations have collaborated to develop parallel yet distinct standards for AM
metals, as evident in literature, the AMMT 316H work packages, and previous LANL research.
To address this challenge, a machine-agnostic process parameter plan can be distilled into four
key steps, initiated by the specific application and customer request, as illustrated in Figure 1.
This structured approach can help navigate the complex qualification process, ensuring the
reliability and performance of AM parts and systems.

 Figure 1: Machine agnostics process plan.

The first step in the machine-agnostic process parameter and machine maintenance plan
focuses on the critical triumvirate of design, feedstock, and equipment. To ensure the integrity of
the additive manufacturing process, the required feedstock must meet stringent material
specifications outlined by engineers, accompanied by vendor certification and rigorous material
testing to verify compliance. Furthermore, material characterization testing is performed
throughout the feedstock's life cycle during fabrication, guaranteeing that optimal processing
parameters can be achieved to produce parts with full density. Additionally, the equipment's
health is meticulously maintained and archived for future certification, involving regular
preventative maintenance and health checks performed by operators at frequent intervals.

The subsequent steps, 2-4, are contingent upon the specific standard required for the
application or specified by the customer. For a comprehensive understanding of these steps,

1.0 Design

The design of an AM part is primarily driven by the specific application need, taking into account
associated restrictions such as size and material, as well as limitations including performance
behavior and lifespan. Once these parameters are defined, the geometry of the part can be
designed. However, it is crucial to consult with AM operators at this stage to determine whether
the design should incorporate supports or geometry alterations to facilitate fabrication and avoid
potential build failures. The size of the part is also a critical consideration, as it may require
different build volumes to produce a monolithic part or necessitate the incorporation of joining
points throughout the part if it must be fabricated in sections and assembled later.

As the design takes shape, it must undergo a series of rigorous reviews to ensure its viability.
This involves assessing design limitations based on input from application engineers and AM
operators, identifying and addressing any engineering anomalies, and finally, obtaining approval
from a qualified engineer. This collaborative approach ensures that the design is optimized for
AM, minimizing the risk of errors and maximizing the chances of successful fabrication. By
considering the intricacies of the AM process and incorporating the expertise of various
stakeholders, a robust and effective design can be achieved.

1.1 Feedstock

Metal powder behavior and characteristics play a vital role in AM, as they directly impact the
quality of the final parts. The powder serves as the fundamental basis for AM, and its properties
must meet specific requirements to ensure optimal manufacturing capability and as-built part
properties. Therefore, it is essential to carefully select and handle metal powders that meet the
necessary standards.

The manufacturing method of metal powder significantly influences its characteristics. Water
atomization, although not widely used, can produce powders with higher oxygen concentrations
and irregular morphologies due to the fast cooling and solidification rates. Gas atomization, on
the other hand, is the preferred method, as it yields powders with lower oxygen content, less
porosity, and a spherical morphology, which enables easy flow and dense packing. This is
particularly beneficial for laser-powder bed fusion (L-PBF) systems, where optimal spreadability
and minimal voids are crucial. Plasma atomization is commonly used for reactive metals and
high-purity powders.

To ensure the quality of AM 316H metal powder, it is essential to purchase from a manufacturer
or vendor who can meet the required powder specifications and characteristics. Furthermore,
handling and storage requirements must be strictly followed to control powder properties before
and after AM fabrication. This includes guidelines for powder addition and removal, cleaning
protocols, humidity-controlled storage, and designated areas for safe storage. Manufacturers
often provide specific requirements, and a certification report stating the powder specifications,
Lot/Heat ID, and vendor information is also necessary as described in References 4-14. By
adhering to these guidelines, the integrity of the metal powder can be maintained, resulting in
high-quality AM parts.

1.1.1 Pedigree

The Kansas City National Security Campus's reference document, 9960010 (U) Selective Laser
Melting Powder, provides a comprehensive guide for the characterization of AM powder used in
LPBF and selective laser melting (SLM) processes. This document, described in Reference 15,
outlines the essential requirements for AM powder, which are further elaborated upon in this
section.

To ensure the quality and consistency of AM powder, manufacturers and vendors must provide
a certification report that includes the specifications and Lot/Heat ID of the powder. This report
should contain a full chemical analysis, including electron-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDX)
or inductively coupled plasma - mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), Laboratory Equipment
Corporation (LECO), mass spectrometry, and general alloy identification per SAE HS-1086 for
elemental analysis. This analysis is crucial in determining whether the powder chemistry
composition meets the requirements for the intended application. It is strongly recommended
that customers perform their own chemical analysis upon receiving the powder to ensure
consistent results throughout its shelf life.

In addition to chemical analysis, several tests should be performed to evaluate the behavior of
the metal powder. These tests include particle size and distribution (PSD) analysis by laser
diffraction, Hall Flow, Apparent Density, Tap Density, Skeletal density, Carney Flow, Powder
Rheometer, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging to capture the morphology of the
metal powder. Equivalent forms of analysis may be accepted.

The determination of PSD is particularly important, as it reveals the distribution of powder
particles within the accepted average particle size range (~30um). Particles that are too small
risk being evaporated by the laser during the build process, causing issues with powder flow
and potentially leading to inclusions and voids in the weld pool. Conversely, particles that are
larger than the average particle size can form agglomerates, which negatively affect the
flowability of the powder and may lead to surface roughness in as-built parts. Satellites, which
occur when small neighboring particles partially fuse to larger particles, can also break off
during recoating and contribute to a wider PSD curve. These defects can ultimately lower the
density and structural soundness of the material.

physical characteristics and bulk properties of the powder. These tests are crucial in
determining whether a well-packed and uniform powder layer can be achieved across the entire

1.1.2 Recycling Powder

To guarantee the quality and performance of reused powder in LPBF, it is essential to conduct
periodic testing of its chemistry composition, PSD, density, flowability, and morphology. This
rigorous testing regimen ensures that all powder characteristics remain within acceptable
ranges, enabling the powder to behave optimally during the LPBF process. While slight
variations in composition may occur after multiple recycling cycles without refreshing, the
oxygen content is a notable exception. The increase in oxygen levels can be attributed to
various factors, including contaminants, oxidation, vaporization from the powder when exposed
to the laser, and handling in ambient air [23-26]. A study by Yusuf et al. on 316L stainless steel,
a material closely related to 316H, demonstrated that even after 20 reuse cycles without
refreshing, the composition remained within the accepted ran

As emphasized in Section 1.1, PSD and powder morphology testing are essential for
characterizing recycled powder. The flowability and density of the powder are intimately linked
to its morphology, with spherical powders providing optimal flowability and homogeneous layer
packing [23]. Consequently, flowability and density have a direct impact on consistent layer
deposition and lasing, ultimately leading to the fabrication of highly dense parts. Based on
literature reviews and the collective experience of LANL, ORNL, and ANL, it is safe and cost-
effective to recycle powder, provided that rigorous testing and data set practices are in place.

Each reused powder batch should be designated as a "recycled batch," with the number of
recycling cycles carefully archived in the data sets to maintain a detailed powder history.
Interstitial elements, such as oxygen, must be closely monitored in recycled batches, as various
factors can contribute to their increase. While slight changes in oxidation levels may not
negatively impact powder performance [21-22], significant increases in oxygen can ultimately
compromise as-built material properties, underscoring the importance of periodic testing.

To maintain powder quality, a refreshing procedure can be employed to keep all elements within
acceptable ranges. This involves adding virgin powder to the used powder to control interstitial
elements, such as oxygen and nitrogen, which may be present. Prior chemical analysis is
necessary to determine the optimal amounts of virgin powder to be added, followed by a
thorough blending method to mix the powders. It is essential to archive the amount of powder
used, the refresh amount added, blending method and duration, and before and after chemical
analysis in the data set for future reference. Refreshing and recycling must be performed with
caution, as poor powder morphology and non-compliant alloy composition can lead to defects,
increased surface roughness, and compromised properties.

1.1.3 Porosity and Voids

Porosity and voids are common defects that can arise in fabricated parts, often as a result of
inadequate fusion between layers, gas absorption, and keyholing. These defects can lead to a
decrease in part density, ultimately compromising mechanical performance, particularly when
subjected to fatigue loadings. Fortunately, these defects can be controlled and eliminated by
optimizing processing parameters to achieve laser power stability and complete fusion. This is
achieved by attaining an optimal energy density, characterized by a normal "convection mode"
shaped melt pool, which ensures adequate penetration and overlap with previous layers and

1.2.1 Machine Qualification

To ensure the reliability and consistency of metal AM machines, certain crucial steps must be
taken for machine qualification. First and foremost, all machines must undergo routine
preventative maintenance (PM) performed by a qualified individual. This maintenance involves
replacing worn or faulty consumables and verifying that the machine passes all calibration tests,
including focal and scanner calibrations, upon completion of the PM. Furthermore, most AM
machines require a qualification build to determine the x and y scaling factors and beam offset
for a given layer thickness. This process is material-dependent, as values differ for each
material and may vary after PM. A typical qualification build for Electro Optical Systems (EOS)
machines is illustrated in Figure 6, where measurements are taken at multiple points throughout
the build to calculate the scaling and laser offset values. It is essential that each AM system has
a specific qualification build tailored to its unique requirements, ensuring that the machine is
operating within optimal parameters.

 Figure 6: EOS qualification build.

Following the initial qualification steps, a full-scale "machine health" build should be performed
to ensure that the AM machine is operating within optimal parameters. This build serves as a
critical assessment of the machine's performance, verifying that powder is being uniformly
distributed, air flow is consistent, and laser beam behavior is consistent across the build
platform. Any variations in these factors could negatively impact the quality of the fabricated
parts. A comprehensive "machine health" build should incorporate a range of components that

enable thorough evaluation, including those suitable for density analysis, metallography,
mechanical testing, and surface roughness measurements. A prime example of such a build is

During the early stages of fabrication, it is crucial to closely monitor the build process,
particularly within the first dozen layers, to identify any potential issues that may propagate as
the build progresses. This includes detecting powder shortages, improper welds, or non-uniform
powder dispersion, which can be indicated by areas not entirely covered with powder. Failure to
address these issues can lead to a range of problems, including lack of fusion, increased
porosity, curling or warping of parts, jamming, or incomplete builds. If the powder
characterization and behavior have been previously analyzed and the build platform is properly
leveled, powder shortage and non-uniformity can be corrected during the early stages of
fabrication by adjusting the powder dosing amount. This practice should be adopted for every
build, not just the "machine health" builds.

Upon completion of the build, the components should be heat-treated to relieve residual
stresses, machined off the build plate, and subjected to a comprehensive analysis. This
includes density measurements and metallography to determine if full density and complete
fusion were achieved. Part-to-part variations in density or microstructure within the build may
indicate improper airflow, which can push debris over other parts instead of into the filter
system. Mechanical testing should be performed to ensure that the mechanical behavior of the
parts falls within the acceptable range for additive manufacturing. Finally, surface roughness
measurements should be conducted to assess the consistency and sufficiency of airflow along
the entire build platform. The results shown in Figures 8 and 9 demonstrate the significant
impact of inconsistent airflow on surface roughness and mechanical properties, respectively.

1.3 Machine Health Monitoring & Archiving

The state of the machine is essential in additive manufacturing, as it directly impacts the quality
and reliability of the fabricated parts. To operate at optimal capability, the machine must be in
good health, which is influenced by various factors, including regular maintenance between
builds, biyearly or yearly preventive maintenance performed by qualified personnel, machine
cleanliness, facility conditions, and operator proficiency.

Designating machines for specific materials can minimize the risk of cross-contamination, but
it's important to regularly inspect and maintain moving components, such as leveling platform
gears, recoating arms, and powder dosing dispensers, to ensure they are functioning correctly
and in a clean state. These components, comprising gears, motors, and precision motion parts,
can wear over time, leading to setup and operational difficulties. While it may not be feasible to
visually inspect these components, their proper functioning can be verified by leveling the build
platform, moving platforms freely, removing obstructing powder, and cleaning or replacing felt
seals.

The facility's cleanliness and organization also play a critical role in maintaining machine health.
The room should be regularly cleaned to prevent cross-contamination, ensure operator safety,

1.3.1 Part-Part Production Variability

In AM, parts are prone to variations both within a build and between builds, due to deviations
that can occur along the build platform. These deviations can arise from various factors,
including inconsistent powder layer deposition, non-uniform airflow, differences in laser beam
intensity at different locations, powder characteristics, and asymmetrical part geometry. As a
result, it is essential to consider all major aspects of the AM process, including material
selection, data preparation, and process parameters, before fabrication. Each of these factors
can significantly impact the variability of parts, and therefore, must be carefully evaluated and
controlled to ensure consistent and high-quality output. The key AM aspects that determine the
quality of fabricated parts are succinctly illustrated in Figure 10 [31], highlighting the complexity
and interdependence of these factors in achieving optimal results.

The quality of the fabricated component is heavily influenced by the raw materials used, which
is why regular characterization of the powders' composition and behavior is crucial. In the data

fabrication orientation, identify potential defects, and determine the need for support structures.

The process parameters, which rely on laser stability and adequate energy density, are critical
in achieving full density parts. The key parameters that contribute to the bulk properties of parts
include laser hatch distance, laser speed, laser power, scanning strategy and rotation, beam
offset, and spot size. These parameters work together to influence the thermal behavior of the
melt pool, mechanical strength, surface roughness, and dimensional accuracy. If any
inconsistencies are detected in density, mechanical behavior, or surface roughness during the
AM process, additional builds, analysis, and machine adjustments should be performed until
results are repeatable and reliable.

 RUS

Resonant Ultrasound Spectroscopy (RUS) is a NDE method that offers a powerful tool for
determining the moduli of AM metals. The material science tetrahedron, illustrated in Figure
17(a), aptly demonstrates the intricate relationships between the various aspects of metal
behavior. Specifically, the structure and part properties are shaped by the fabrication process,
which in turn affects the overall performance of the part. Each of these aspects can be
characterized to understand their interdependencies. Similarly, the capabilities of RUS can be
represented by a tetrahedron, as shown in Figure 17(b). Here, a specific geometry is associated
with a density that is directly influenced by the defects introduced during fabrication and any
unresolved issues resulting from post-processing. This, in turn, impacts the material properties,
which directly influence the elastic moduli and, by extension, the mechanical behavior. RUS,
combined with finite element analysis, can be employed to calculate these moduli. In FY24, a
series of RUS screenings were conducted to investigate the heterogeneity of fabricated 316H
samples at various processing parameters, providing valuable insights into the material's
behavior.

frequencies, making adjustments and eliminating errors until the calculated frequencies
converge with the measured frequencies. The calculation process, depicted in Figure 18,

 Electro-polishing

AM metals have the potential to exhibit high strength retention, but surface roughness can
significantly impact fatigue performance. The layer-by-layer fusion process inherent to L-PBF
can result in rough surfaces, characterized by partially melted particles and laser track lines. To
fully understand the impact of these surface roughness features on mechanical performance, it
is essential to investigate the effects of surface finishing after additive manufacturing.

While various surface finishing techniques are available, physical-based methods are often
limited to simple, line-of-sight paths, which can be a constraint when working with complex
shapes. Additive manufacturing offers the unique advantage of creating complex geometries
with relative ease. Chemical and electrochemical methods, such as electrochemical surface
finishing, are being explored as effective ways to polish materials across complex shapes.
Electrochemical polishing, in particular, offers the distinct advantage of tunable surface feature
targeting through input signal control. By applying an electrical bias across two electrodes in an
electrolyte solution, electrochemical reactions are catalyzed, enabling the selective removal of
material from "high points" and resulting in a polished surface.

In this study, electropolishing was tested using a commercial electropolishing solution (EPS
2500) and a home-made salt electrolyte, comprised of water, salt, and polyethylene glycol
(PEG). The PEG electrolyte, initially optimized for 316L stainless steel, was verified to be
effective for 316H stainless steel as well. A pulse waveform was developed to target partially
melted particles using the PEG electrolyte, making surfaces more uniform and minimizing
material loss. A 2-step electropolishing procedure was employed, involving the PEG electrolyte

AMMT – 316H SS
Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 3-19

to remove partially melted particles, followed by polishing in the commercial solution to rapidly
target mesoscale waviness and homogenize the surface chemistry.

To validate the polishing process, initial rectangular coupons of varied build angles were tested,
followed by the polishing of cylindrical samples representative of tensile bars. These cylinders
were polished using two different schemas, as specified in Table 1.

 Machine Learning and Modeling

In the realm of metal parts produced via L-PBF AM, post-manufacturing stress and strain
measurements are typically conducted through tensile testing. The resulting stress-strain curve
provides invaluable insights into the material's mechanical properties, including its elastic limit,
yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, and Young's modulus. Stakeholders are keenly
interested in understanding the intricate relationships between these material properties and the
stress-strain curve. To address this, researchers turned to AI and ML to predict stress-strain
curves of AM parts based on known metal properties. This innovative work was undertaken by
Sharmistha Chakrabarti and Natan Debardeleben from the High Performance Computing and
Design group at LANL, with their findings presented in this section.

For training the model, a simple neural network called a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) is used. An
MLP is a straightforward but widely used type of neural network. It consists of multiple layers—
an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer—where each layer is made up of
units called neurons. In this structure, information flows in one direction – from the input layer,
through the hidden layers, to the output layer. Each layer processes the data it receives and
passes it to the next layer. The network improves its ability to make predictions, like forecasting
stress and strain, by adjusting the connections between neurons based on examples it has
seen. Figure 40 shows a schematic diagram of an MLP with two hidden layers, along with input
and output layers.

 Figure 40: A schematic diagram of a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) neural network.

The network takes in inputs such as thickness, width, time, build direction, manufacturer, post-
fabrication heat treatment, and build parameters, and it outputs predictions for stress and strain.
The first hidden layer of the network contains 64 neurons, while the second has 32 neurons.
The dataset, which includes information on 214 specimens, is divided into training and testing
sets. Data from the remaining 4 specimens is set aside to assess the model's predictive
accuracy. The MLP model is trained using the training data, and its performance is evaluated on
the testing data. Several hyperparameters, such as epochs, batch size, and learning rate, can
be adjusted. Different combinations of these hyperparameters are experimented with to
determine their effect on the model's performance.

An MLP neural network is trained to predict the stress-strain curve of additively manufactured
parts based on metal properties and fabrication methods used. The MLP model performs
reasonably well in some cases (like in (a)) but shows significant discrepancies in others. This
suggests that while the model can capture the general trend of stress-strain relationships, it may
struggle with certain material conditions or specific specimens. These differences could be due
to variations in the specimens that the model wasn't fully trained on, highlighting the need for
further tuning, more representative training data, or possibly a more complex model. Exploring
more advanced models, such as recurrent neural networks (RNNs) or transformer-based
architectures, might help address the issues with variability and improve the model’s
robustness.

Some of the observations and results were discussed with the stakeholders, whose insights
were invaluable. Figure 42 illustrates six example specimens that exhibit varying stress-strain
curves, despite being produced by the same manufacturer and using identical build parameters.
The stakeholders acknowledged that such variations in maximum force/stress experienced and
the distance traveled before catastrophic failure (breakage at the end of the test) are
anticipated. They also identified that the stress-strain curve for Specimen 1 likely reflects a data
collection issue. Additionally, while Specimens 2, 4, and 6 display a sudden fracture at the end
of the test, with the material clearly breaking into two, Specimen 3 demonstrates the gradual
decline characteristic of a highly ductile material that stretches steadily until failure. Specimen 5
shows a pattern where the material partially breaks, then stretches, and repeats this process
before ultimately failing. The stakeholders also decided to uniformly trim the tails of the curves
across all samples to ensure consistent data processing for each specimen. An updated dataset
is received.
