Rare ALK Fusions in NSCLC—Expanding the Boundaries of Precision Oncology
Authors: Aaron C. Tan, Benjamin Y. Kong, Molly S.C. Li
Published: 2026

Expanding the Spectrum of ALK Fusions in NSCLC

The therapeutic landscape for advanced ALK-rearranged NSCLC has evolved in recent years, particularly with the durable efficacy of lorlatinib in the first-line setting, achieving a median progression-free survival of more than 7 years.1 This has redefined our approach to patients with metastatic disease in this molecular subtype, enabling long-term disease control. Historically, EML4-ALK has been the canonical fusion; however, with advances in the implementation of next-generation sequencing (NGS), there are increasing reports of rare and non-canonical ALK fusions and fusion partners. In recent issues of JTO Clinical and Research Reports, several case reports have described rare and non-canonical ALK fusions, highlighting the collective importance of detection, actionability, and treatment resistance. Kanno et al.2 described a patient with a structurally complex intra-ALK inversion that was missed by conventional molecular testing but was detected through comprehensive NGS on liquid biopsy. In addition, Jin et al.3 reported a patient with a novel PPP1CB-ALK fusion, and Hsu et al.4 reported two patients with novel ZFPM2-ALK and TRIM24-ALK fusions, respectively. Importantly, all four patients responded to alectinib therapy, supporting the potential clinical actionability of rare and non-canonical ALK fusions.

Limitations and Advances in Molecular Detection

Current approaches to detecting ALK fusions in clinical practice include immunohistochemistry (IHC), fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), and NGS. NGS approaches can be further defined by the use of DNA- or RNA-based sequencing and tissue versus liquid biopsy. Conventional assays, such as FISH and RT-PCR, however, may have reduced sensitivity for uncommon rearrangements. FISH, for example, may not fully characterize complex genomic events. RT-PCR will miss novel fusion partners. For NGS assays, DNA-based sequencing may also miss rearrangements due to complex structural variants or intronic breakpoints outside targeted regions. Depending on the assay, RNA-based sequencing methods may have advantages in detecting both known and novel fusion partners and defining fusion breakpoints. However, RNA-based sequencing can be technically more challenging and increase costs. Nevertheless, the case reported by Kanno et al.2 illustrates how a rare intra-ALK inversion was missed on routine molecular profiling but was detected through broader sequencing and was orthogonally validated with IHC. This demonstrates the potential utility of broader and deeper molecular profiling, particularly in patients with a phenotype suggestive of an oncogenic driver, such as younger never-smoking patients. As the number of targetable alterations in NSCLC continues to expand, and the use of multiplex assays such as NGS becomes more accessible, the detection of rare and non-canonical ALK fusions is likely to increase.

Clinical Activity of ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors in Rare and Non-Canonical Fusions

The key question in clinical practice is the actionability of rare and non-canonical fusions. Crucially, not all ALK rearrangements are oncogenic, requiring an intact ALK kinase domain to lead to constitutive activation. When atypical or novel ALK rearrangements are identified, orthogonal validation using complementary assays, such as ALK IHC or other molecular platforms such as RNA-based NGS or even methylation-based epigenotyping,5 may help confirm the biological relevance and clinical actionability of these findings.6 However, even with an initial response, there may also be variability in the durability and depth of response. In the ALEX7 and CROWN8 studies, for example, early progressors were more likely to be ALK IHC positive but NGS negative for an ALK fusion. As the treatment landscape evolves with an expanding repertoire of ALK inhibitors, the potential variability in efficacy is another open question. Ultimately, with limited prospective evidence in clinical trials for rare and non-canonical fusions, real-world evidence and collaborative registries may be needed to build a robust evidence base to select the most appropriate therapy.

Resistance Mechanisms and the Role of Molecular Monitoring

The two cases reported by Hsu et al.4 initially responded to treatment but ultimately developed disease progression. Repeat molecular profiling identified distinct resistance mechanisms: one patient acquired a CDKN2A/B deletion, whereas the other developed on-target ALK kinase domain mutations (F1174V and I1171N) and MYC amplification. Recent advances in ALK-directed therapy have further increased the clinical utility of resistance profiling. Neladalkib, a fourth-generation ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitor, has reported promising efficacy in patients with advanced ALK-rearranged NSCLC after disease progression on conventional ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors. Notably, responses were enriched in patients harboring on-target ALK resistance mutations, particularly G1202R, whereas activity in those without G1202R was modest.9 Its high selectivity for mutant ALK and improved central nervous system penetration further support its role in patients with on-target resistance or central nervous system (CNS) progression.

In addition to next-generation ALK inhibition, repeat molecular profiling may identify novel therapeutic vulnerabilities. In the first case,4 the acquisition of CDKN2A/B deletion is worth noting, as approximately 70% of such tumors are accompanied by MTAP deletion. Studies have revealed that MTAP loss is frequent in ALK-rearranged NSCLC (up to 45%). Exploiting synthetic lethality, protein arginine methyltransferase 5 inhibitors have reported antitumor activity in MTAP-deficient models and are synergistic when combined with targeted therapy.10 In the second case,4 MYC amplification was identified, a well-recognized mechanism that promotes cancer progression and drug resistance. Recently, MYC antagonists and molecular glue degraders have been developed and are now being tested in clinical trials.11 Collectively, these cases illustrate the importance of comprehensive molecular profiling at diagnosis and disease progression to identify both ALK-dependent and ALK-independent resistance mechanisms and to expand opportunities for precision therapy.

Implications for Precision Oncology and Future Directions

To close the gap between academic precision oncology and real-world access to treatment, it is evident from the case reports presented that biomarkers must form part of routine clinical care, because clinical phenotypes do not reflect the nuanced molecular subtypes detected by NGS. Emerging biomarkers may help predict whether a patient will have primary or acquired resistance to targeted or non-targeted therapy.12 It follows that access to testing infrastructure and expert interpretation of complex genomic results must be available on a real-time basis where possible. Resource limitations may mean that infrastructure-dependent expertise needs to be concentrated in academic centers. Examples include access to endobronchial ultrasound biopsy for staging at diagnosis and longitudinal analysis and molecular multidisciplinary tumor boards to interpret genomic driver alterations. However, the need for specialty diagnostics invariably raises the challenge of equitable access to care, regardless of geography or socioeconomic status.13 Machine-learning–driven bioinformatics tools that automatically match genomic results with available treatments exemplify efforts that may negate shortages in specialist expertise at the point of care, which could be applied in both low- and high-resource settings.14

As rare alterations and matched targeted therapies become more complex, it is likely that a model of phase 3 placebo-controlled studies for drug approval will no longer be fit for purpose. Because many NSCLC driver mutations and fusions are also found in other cancer types, proactive biomarker testing should become part of routine clinical practice. Tumor-agnostic United States Food and Drug Administration (US FDA) approvals for targeted therapies against rare alterations, such as BRAF V600E, HER2 overexpression, and RET fusions, suggest that approval pathways may be possible for increasingly rare oncogenic drivers. According to the ESMO Tumour-Agnostic Classifier and Screener framework, which is used to classify alterations that may hold tumor-agnostic potential, a response rate of at least 20% in at least two-thirds of tumor types, with at least five assessable patients per tumor type, is required.15 The case reports described do not meet these criteria. This further highlights the need to collect real-world biomarker and outcome data not only in lung cancer but also across other cancer types to support future treatment approvals.
