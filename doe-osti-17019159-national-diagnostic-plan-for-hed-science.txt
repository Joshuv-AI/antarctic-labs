Executive Summary
National Diagnostic Plan for HED Science, September
2026
Purpose and Strategic Context

The National Diagnostic Plan (NDP) coordinates diagnostic development across the
National Nuclear Security Administration’s high-energy-density science complex,
including the National Ignition Facility, Z, and OMEGA. These capabilities provide the
measurements needed to test simulations, validate models, understand ignition and burn
physics, and support Stockpile Stewardship Program missions in materials,
hydrodynamics, radiation effects, and thermonuclear burn.

The National Diagnostics Working Group has organized the effort into three
complementary categories:

 • Transformational diagnostics, requiring major national investments and
 providing capabilities unavailable from existing instruments.
 • Broad diagnostics, coordinated across laboratories to improve measurement
 precision and consistency.
 • Local diagnostics, developed by individual facilities to address specific
 experimental requirements.

The September 2026 plan reflects substantial progress in deploying diagnostics for multi-
megajoule experiments, while establishing the technical foundation for NIF Enhanced
Yield Capability, the Z extended-performance initiative, and a potential next-generation
high-yield HED facility.

Major Successes
1. Diagnostic capabilities were central to achieving ignition

The diagnostic suite described in this plan played a direct role in the National Ignition
Facility’s landmark December 2022 result, in which a fusion target achieved ignition and
net energy gain for the first time. Measurements from systems including DIXI, the
neutron time-of-flight lines of sight, and the nuclear imaging systems provided the yield,
burn, and confinement data needed to confirm the result and validate the underlying
physics models. This milestone demonstrated that the national diagnostic program is not
merely tracking experimental progress, but is a core enabling capability for the highest-
priority outcomes in the Stockpile Stewardship Program. The investments recommended

in this plan build directly on that foundation, extending the same diagnostic approach to
the higher-yield regimes now within reach.

2. Diagnostic capabilities have scaled with ICF performance

The diagnostic program has successfully supported the transition from sub-megajoule
experiments to multi-megajoule ignition-class shots. Existing systems have been
hardened, relocated, or upgraded to operate in increasingly severe neutron and x-ray
environments.

Notable accomplishments include:

 • DIXI and Polar DIXI have routinely provided time-resolved x-ray images on
 multi-megajoule NIF implosions. Upgrades are expected to support yields
 approaching approximately 20 MJ.
 • HYXI Phase 1 was commissioned on NIF, adding an orthogonal imaging line of
 sight and enabling time-integrated measurements for higher-yield implosions.
 • Nuclear Imaging Systems now provide neutron and gamma-ray images from
 multiple lines of sight, supporting three-dimensional reconstruction of the burning
 plasma, cold fuel, and remaining ablator.
 • GRH pulse-dilation technology has achieved approximately 10 ps temporal
 resolution, enabling substantially improved burn-width measurements.
 • Five neutron time-of-flight lines of sight now provide high-precision
 measurements of ion temperature, hot-spot bulk velocity, and down-scatter ratio.
 • FIDDLE, the NIF time-resolved diffraction platform, demonstrated multi-frame
 x-ray diffraction measurements of evolving material phases.
 • SCORPIONZ was successfully commissioned on Z, providing high-temporal-
 resolution x-ray emission measurements.
 • OMEGA continues to serve as a highly productive development and qualification
 platform for advanced diagnostics later deployed at NIF and Z.

3. National collaboration is producing measurable leverage

The NDP has created a mature cross-laboratory development structure involving LLNL,
LANL, SNL, LLE, MIT, General Atomics, AWE, NNSS, and international collaborators.
The FY26 NDWG meeting included 146 participants from institutions around the world.

This collaboration has enabled:

 • Transfer of neutron imaging technology from NIF to Z through the NEMESIS
 project.
 • Shared development of hCMOS, pulse-dilation, neutron time-of-flight, gamma
 spectroscopy, and x-ray diffraction technologies.
 • Common approaches to calibration, analysis, detector hardening, and high-
 performance computing.

 • More efficient use of expensive experimental shots by obtaining multiple
 measurements on a single target.

4. hCMOS and pulse-dilation technologies are becoming foundational

hCMOS sensors and pulse-dilation drift tubes now support more than 25 diagnostic
applications across the complex. Together, they provide:

 • Nanosecond-scale multi-frame imaging.
 • Time gating to suppress backgrounds.
 • Radiation tolerance for high-yield environments.
 • Direct detection of x rays or electrons.
 • Improved data return from individual shots.

The Hyperion sensor is being developed specifically for HYXI and higher-yield NIF
operations. Additional sensor work includes GaAs detectors for higher-energy x rays,
improved Icarus devices, and future detector architectures requiring national cost sharing.

5. The program has established a credible high-yield diagnostic pathway

The plan recognizes that diagnostic requirements will change significantly as yields
increase. The current approach combines:

 1. More remote detector locations.
 2. Radiation-hardened detectors and electronics.
 3. Improved shielding and signal transport.

This strategy is already being applied to HYXI, HY NIS, HY nTOF, HIDALGO, OSB-
VISAR, Aragog, eDante, and classified diagnostic variants.

Future Direction and Priorities
1. Complete the transition to high-yield operation

The near-term priority is to make the existing diagnostic suite reliable and sustainable for
10 to 30 MJ NIF experiments and the corresponding high-performance Z program.

Key activities include:

 • Commissioning later phases of HYXI, including the Hyperion hCMOS backend.
 • Completing NIS snout, detector, collimation, and gamma-imaging upgrades.
 • Deploying HY nTOF systems with improved detector linearity and selectable
 attenuation.

 • Developing HIDALGO, a high-dynamic-range gamma reaction history
 diagnostic capable of measuring the onset and evolution of ignition over a
 dynamic range of at least 1,000.
 • Relocating or replacing VISAR through OSB-VISAR and advancing the full
 HRV capability.
 • Completing FALCON for time-resolved and classified x-ray diffraction.
 • Developing high-energy x-ray detectors capable of efficient detection above 15
 keV.

2. Prioritize diagnostics that directly constrain ignition physics

The most consequential future measurements are those that resolve the evolution of
temperature, density, areal density, mix, flow, and burn rate in time and space.

High-value priorities include:

 • Time-resolved neutron spectroscopy, to measure the evolution of ion
 temperature, areal density, neutron yield, and flow.
 • Advanced gamma-ray reaction history, to capture the early onset of alpha
 heating and the evolution of burn.
 • Three-dimensional neutron and gamma imaging, including improved polar-
 line-of-sight capability.
 • Higher-resolution nuclear imaging, including reaction-in-flight and spatially
 resolved ion-temperature measurements.
 • Improved hohlraum plasma measurements, particularly through a new 4ω
 ultraviolet Thomson-scattering probe on NIF.
 • Advanced gamma imaging, including time-resolved and energy-resolved
 approaches.

These measurements will reduce dependence on model-derived quantities and provide
more direct tests of the physics controlling ignition, confinement, mix, and burn
propagation.

3. Establish a common national analysis and data infrastructure

Analysis time and sustainment are emerging constraints. The NIS program illustrates the
issue: complex, labor-intensive analysis and aging hardware limit throughput.

The proposed Diagnostic Image Reconstruction Toolbox, or DIRT, should be treated as
a strategic national capability. It would:

 • Replace aging, facility-specific analysis codes.
 • Provide a common Python-based architecture.
 • Support classified and unclassified workflows.
 • Use high-performance computing for faster reconstruction.
 • Enable consistent analysis across NIF, Z, and OMEGA.

 • Facilitate incorporation of new imaging modalities and automated calibration.

Similar national approaches should be considered for synthetic data, uncertainty
quantification, detector calibration, and automated analysis.

4. Prepare now for EYC and a future high-yield facility

NIF’s Enhanced Yield Capability is expected to reach approximately 50 to 70 MJ in the
early-to-mid 2030s. A potential next-generation high-yield facility could operate at
substantially higher yields in the mid-2040s.

The NDP recommends a staged roadmap:

 1. Assess existing diagnostic performance as a function of yield.
 2. Define prioritized requirements and technology risks.
 3. Coordinate EYC, Z extended performance, and future high-yield facility plans.
 4. Develop and test hardened diagnostic prototypes on both laser and pulsed-power
 platforms.
 5. Deploy qualified systems before high-yield experimental campaigns begin.
 6. Preserve fallback options for diagnostics with uncertain technical paths.

Early investment is essential because several diagnostics require long development
timelines, facility infrastructure, classified interfaces, specialized detectors, and extensive
calibration.

Recommendations for NA-11 Leadership
 1. Maintain national coordination through the NDWG. The collaborative model
 is delivering capabilities more efficiently than independent facility development.
 2. Protect high-priority transformational diagnostics from short-term funding
 volatility. Interruptions to projects such as HIDALGO, HYXI, TRNS, UVTS,
 HRV, and DIRT create schedule and workforce risks that are difficult to recover.
 3. Prioritize direct measurements of ignition evolution. Time-resolved neutron
 spectroscopy, high-dynamic-range gamma reaction history, three-dimensional
 nuclear imaging, and hohlraum plasma measurements offer the greatest potential
 to reduce model uncertainty.
 4. Fund sustainment as a strategic requirement. Aging cameras, optical links,
 photomultipliers, calibration systems, and analysis software increasingly threaten
 data continuity.
 5. Develop a coordinated EYC and future high-yield diagnostic roadmap.
 Requirements, shielding, facility penetrations, classified operations, detector
 development, and analysis infrastructure must be planned together.

 6. Promote common national infrastructure. Shared sensors, calibration
 standards, synthetic data, uncertainty tools, and analysis software will reduce

Bottom Line

The national diagnostic program has successfully enabled ignition-class experiments and
has demonstrated strong returns from coordinated investment. The next phase must move
beyond simply surviving higher yields. It should deliver direct, time-resolved,
multidimensional measurements of the physics that governs ignition, burn propagation,
confinement, mix, and material response. Sustained national prioritization now will
determine whether the complex can fully exploit NIF EYC, advance Z performance, and
prepare credible diagnostic capabilities for a future high-yield facility.

This same diagnostic foundation enabled the confirmation of ignition and net energy gain
at NIF in December 2022 — proof that sustained investment in this program delivers
results at the highest level of national priority.

II: Recent activities of the National Diagnostic Working Group (NDWG)

IIa: ICF and HED Diagnostics: Background and Mission

ICF and High Energy Density (HED) physics experiments involve phenomena that
occur on timescales measured in picoseconds and spatial scales measured in
micrometers (or microns). Observable information is conveyed in photons with
energies ranging from visible light to MeV gamma rays, and in charged and neutral

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 8

particles from fusion reactions. Progress in ICF and HED has been dependent for
decades on the development and innovation of new instruments and techniques to
measure the observables with increasing temporal, spatial, and energy resolution,
and with the ability to gather more data in a single experiment.
Since 2008 there have been regular NDWG meetings, initially to foster national
participation in the diagnostics for the NIF and recently to coordinate research and
development of HED diagnostics across the ICF sites for NIF, Z and OMEGA. By
coordinating efforts, each site is able to capitalize on the advances made at the
other sites, share expertise, and incorporate new techniques into their own
programs.

The national diagnostics development effort is divided into three groups:

 • Transformational Diagnostics: diagnostics requiring a major national effort
 with the potential to transform experimental capability for the most critical
 science needs across the complex.

 • Broad Diagnostics: diagnostic efforts and techniques requiring significant
 national efforts which will enable new or more precise measurements across
 the complex.

 • Local Diagnostics: important diagnostics that implement known technology
 for a local need and are identified by facility management responding to the
 needs of the local user community.
The NNSA ICF and HED programs support stockpile stewardship through the four
principal missions shown in Table 2. The facilities also support National Security
Applications (NSA) with other national agencies and basic science conducted mainly
in collaboration with universities. The decisions on which new diagnostics to
develop depends on a combination of the diagnostic usage at the various facilities,
the prioritized needs of the programs, the status of technology, and the requests
from the user community. Each facility has a different process for making these
decisions for local diagnostics. For transformational and broad diagnostics, the
development activities are prioritized, advocated for, and concerns are raised as
appropriate by the appointed leaders of the NDWG. The core leadership of the NDWG
as appointed by the ICF Execs are David Bradley (Lawrence Livermore National Lab,
LLNL), Sean Regan (Laboratory for Laser Energetics, LLE), Eric Harding (Sandia
National Laboratories, SNL), and Tana Morrow (Los Alamos National Laboratory,
LANL) with consistent support from Joe Kilkenny and Christine Mariscal (General
Atomics, GA), Alastair Moore, and Andy Mackinnon (LLNL), Maria Gatu Johnson
(Massachusetts Institute of Technology, MIT), Chuck Sorce and Steven Ivancic (LLE),
Michael Jones (SNL), and Steven Batha and Yong Ho Kim (LANL).

IIIb-1: Pulse-dilation Drift Tube

Two key technologies developed within the ICF program are revolutionizing gated x-ray
detectors. These technologies are pulse-dilation tubes developed in collaboration between
General Atomics, Kentech Instruments, and LLNL, and fast-gated hybrid complementary
metal-oxide-semiconductor (hCMOS) sensors developed at SNL. The scope of the

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 11

hCMOS development has caused this effort to be covered in a new section specifically
addressing hCMOS ROICs and diodes, see hCMOS section. Pulse-dilation coupled with
hCMOS sensors provides a transformative capability of time gating for multiple frames
along a single line-of-sight (SLOS) at gate times as short as 10 ps. Pulse-dilation coupled
with other high-speed photodetectors, such as photomultiplier tubes, provides a manner
in which to provide instrument response functions as short as a few ps.

Used on its own, the hCMOS sensor can provide direct detection of multi-keV x-rays or
electrons at gate times ≥ 1 ns as well as visible to near IR laser beams. Pulse-dilation
SLOS framing cameras are transformative because they enable multiple time-gated
detection of x-ray images or spectra at frame rates never before possible. Spectra are
recorded using curved crystals. Images are recorded with curved crystals or multi-layer
mirror optics, which can have superior spatial resolution and energy selectivity over
pinhole/slit optics, but are too expensive or take up too much space to create an array of
many images along many lines-of-sight. hCMOS sensors are transformative because they
provide multi-frame direct detection of x-rays at nanosecond frame-rates from the same
active area (pixel) in a technology that can be customized for the x-ray energy. Existing
sensors have good sensitivity for x-rays with a photon energy up to ~10 keV and new
technology development is planned for capability up to ~50 keV.

The hCMOS and SLOS technologies are establishing themselves as critical tools for the
NNSA stockpile stewardship program across nearly all the science campaigns and ICF.
For thermonuclear burn experiments, SLOS technology enables time-resolved 3D
imaging with sufficient spatial resolution to diagnose failure modes of high convergence
implosions. In opacity experiments, hCMOS sensors enable time-resolved measurements
of the plasma evolution essential for addressing systematic differences between models
and data. In strength and phase experiments of high-pressure plutonium, hCMOS sensors
will enable time-sequence measurements on a single shot (instead of using multiple shots
to map out the evolution), which improves the accuracy through elimination of shot-to-
shot variability and reduces the overall use of Pu on the facilities. In hostile environment
experiments, high-energy hCMOS sensors will enable more detailed understanding of x-
ray source physics through enhanced imaging capability that will improve the fidelity of
the x-ray test environment for more precise evaluation of component survivability. There
are now more than 10 diagnostics using hCMOS/SLOS technology with several more in
the development phase.

While the SLOS cameras, both at the NIF and at the Omega laser facility have unlocked
new experimental capabilities and have demonstrated how collaboration on diagnostic
technologies at the national level leads to innovation, they are not in fact the first type of
instruments leveraging the pulse-dilation drift tube technologies. Since 2014, the Dilation
X-Ray Imager (DIXI) is providing X-ray imaging for ICF experiments with a temporal
resolution under 10 ps that is more adequate to resolve the rapid evolution of imploding
DT capsules than the 100 ps temporal resolution achieved by MicroChannel Plate (MCP)

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 12

based framing cameras. hCMOS technology was not available at the time of the
commissioning of DIXI, which instead uses a traditional MCP detector as a backend
detector. In 2021, a second DIXI camera was installed on NIF. Located near the
target chamber pole (henced named Polar DIXI, or PDIXI), it supplements DIXI to
provide a second X-ray imaging line-of-sight on high-yield ICF experiments. As the
implosions’ performance has increased, the PDIXI camera has been steadily
upgraded to continue provide high-quality data and support the National Science
Goal 5.2, “Experimentally asses 10 MJ class platform on NIF” of the HED&IS 10-year
plan. In FY25, PDIXI has routinely delivered time-resolved images of multi-MJ
implosions, including on record energy gain shot N250406. The modifications
applied to PDIXI have been retrofitted to the DIXI camera in FY24 and FY25, and
both cameras now serve as the primary time-resolved X-ray imaging lines of sight
on 10 MJ class implosions. The first use of the upgraded DIXI camera on a DT shot
on NIF occurred in October 2025 (shot N251024), and more uses have occurred
since then. The imaging performance of the two DIXI cameras has been evaluated on
high-yield experiments and it has been estimated that they will provide high-quality
data at fusion energy yields up to 20 MJ. This assessment was performed as part of a
DOE Level 2 Milestone (MRT: 8818) and documented in a memo to NNSA in May
2026.

The High Yield X-ray Imager (HYXI) is being developed at LLNL as an evolution from
the DIXI cameras, leveraging hCMOS technology, and also with the objective to support
the goal identified in paragraph 5.2 of the FY24 HED&IS Program Plan, “Experimentally
assess 10 MJ class platform on NIF”, while serving as the first step to evaluate the
feasibility and the technology developments required to support higher implosion yields
(paragraph 5.14, “Experimentally assess 30 MJ class platform on NIF”). HYXI is the
first time-resolved gated x-ray detector designed with the intent to provide high-quality
data on ICF shots delivering fusion yields at or above 10 MJ. HYXI will allow the
collection of a series of snapshots of the DT capsule emission throughout the implosion,
which will be used to measure its size and shape with 15 ps resolution. One of the
performance requirements of HYXI is to provide images with signal-to-background ratios
(S/B) of 25 on 10 MJ shots. This will be a major improvement compared to the current
NIF capability based on the DIXI detectors, which have demonstrated S/B of ~16 at 3 MJ
on the first fusion ignition experiment in December 2022. To achieve this, HYXI will
leverage both the pulse-dilation drift tube and hCMOS sensor technologies. The former
permits greater control of the location of the detector elements sensitive to neutron-
induced background, which can be placed further away from the source and shielded
from direct line-of-sight particles, while the latter has demonstrated excellent radiation
tolerance on previous NIF experiments and other radiation testing facilities. A new
hCMOS sensor design named HYPERION is being developed as part of the HYXI
project, which will bring additional radiation tolerance by reducing the interaction cross-
section of several internal components with neutrons and gamma rays. A Final Design
Review (FDR) of HYPERION was held in May 2024, and a first batch of sensors was
received in Q4 FY26. The development of the associated sensor electronics will take
place throughout FY27 et FY28, with commissioning expected in late FY28. HYXI itself

will be commissioned in successive phases on NIF. The first one was implemented in Q4
FY25 and allows for time-integrated data collection using imaging plates, along an
orthogonal line of sight to the ones currently being provided by the DIXI cameras. The
first experiment utilizing this capability was NIF shot N251024. The second phase (Q3
FY28) will bring the ability to collect initial time-resolved images using the existing
backend gated detector used in the DIXI cameras, however images meeting the full

held at the end of September 2024. Procurement of long lead time items, such as the high
voltage pulser electronics, has already begun, with some prototype parts already

The Omega Hot-Spot X-ray Imager (XRHSI) will provide a 3rd time-resolved x-ray line
of sight for the OMEGA laser system in support of the goals in Section 5.7 of the
HED&IS Program Plan “Determine the limit of implosion efficiency (X) on OMEGA”.
The XRHSI builds on lessons learned in the development cycle of SLOS instruments
employing a longer drift region and modest demagnification. The XRHSI completed a
Final Design Review in June 2026 and a contract with General Atomics is underway for
the fabrication of the drift tube and qualification tests to be conducted in the next 18
months. The x-ray imager components are currently being fabricated with an install date
on OMEGA scheduled for the winter maintenance period occurring in Dec 2026.

The OMEGA cryo campaign now reaches yields that make high bandwidth DT-gamma
measurements possible with a pulse-dilation (PD-PMT) enhanced gamma-ray history
diagnostic (GRH). The GRH with PD-PMT will provide a 10 ps instrument IRF. This
will make measuring burn width of 10 ps and shot to shot variations in burn width of 5 ps
possible. A project to integrate a GRH at OMEGA was kicked off in FY 24 to support a

IIIb-2: Ultraviolet Optical Thomson Scattering (UVTS)

UVTS on NIF will be used to measure the electron density, electron temperature, ion
temperature and flow velocity of hohlraum plasmas in a well-defined localized region.
The current NIF diagnostic suite is unable to reliably measure these parameters. The data
is required to properly benchmark modeling of ICF hohlraums, in support of the ICF
program goal for developing a predictive hohlraum model. The development of a
predictive hohlraum model supports the following goals identified in the FY24 HED&IS
ICF 10-year science plan:
 Goal Description
 5.2 Experimentally assess 10 MJ class platform on NIF
 5.4 Assess prospects for high yield with Laser Indirect Drive and
 Laser Direct Drive
 5.14 Experimentally assess 30 MJ class platform on NIF

Generally, optical Thomson scattering measures the spectrum of the light scattered from
a mono-chromatic probe beam by a plasma, to make temporally and spatially resolved
measurements of the key parameters of plasmas such as temperature, density, and flow
velocity. It is the gold standard measurement for plasma properties used in tokomaks and
at lower densities in ICF.

On NIF, Z, and OMEGA a sub-critical density plasma produces high pressures to drive
shocks or to inhibit hohlraum wall motion for all SSP programs. There are currently no
direct measurements of the parameters of these plasmas. The behavior of these plasmas

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 15

needs to be better understood. Measurements of the plasma environment are critically
important to better understand hohlraum behavior, and to benchmark radiation
hydrodynamics codes that presently fail to predict the complex dynamics inside
hohlraums. On NIF an ultra-violet probe laser beam makes Thomson Scattering a
transformative diagnostic, allowing it to probe to high density. UVTS can also be used to
access plasmas parameters for laser direct drive on the NIF.

The time evolution of the hohlraum plasma density (ne), temperature (Te), flow velocity
and turbulence levels are critical parameters for understanding the x-ray drive for all
HED hohlraum applications. Likewise, in long scale length plasmas such as Laser Direct
Drive (LDD) on NIF, measurements of ne, Te, and turbulence above the critical density
are important for understanding the drive pressure and its uniformity. These fundamental
parameters will be uniquely measured, without recourse to integrated rad-hydro models,
using Ultraviolet Thomson Scattering (UVTS) on the NIF. These parameters need to be
measured at high densities (ne is calculated to be > 1021 e/cc in a NIF hohlraum) and in
the presence of the powerful NIF heater beams. These requirements drive the need for
anultraviolet Thomson scattering probe laser beam, in order to avoid significant
absorption and refraction in the plasma and so that the signal is not swamped by
scattering of the 351 nm NIF drive beams. Background plasma emission and other
sources of non-Thomson scattered light indicate that to exceed a signal-to-noise of unity
the Thomson scattering probe laser must be 1-10J in 1ns at 210 nm, see J. S. Ross et al.,
Rev. of Sci. Instrumen. 87, 11E510 (2016).

There are also many less challenging experimental configurations which can benefit from
3ω UVTS on the NIF and are benefitting from 4ω UVTS on OMEGA [for example see J.
Katz et al., Rev. Sci. Instrumen. 83, 10E349 (2012)]. These include source development
for radiation effects experiments, experiments designed to study energy transport in
foams, and collision-less shocks in counter-streaming plasmas. OTS has been
implemented on Nova, Trident, JLF and OMEGA although in less stressing conditions
than an ignition hohlraum and LDD on NIF. UVTS on the NIF is therefore a
transformative diagnostic because the short wavelength of the probe opens new windows
in plasma density even after five decades of Thomson scattering from high-temperature
plasmas.

The detector for UVTS is a dual spectrometer multiplexing onto an ultraviolet sensitive
streak camera. The detector was designed and built in FY16 [P Datte et al., (IFSA 2015)
IOP Publishing], and is shown in Fig 2. It has already been used for a range of successful
3ω OTS measurements on the NIF, measuring the parameters of relatively low-density
plasma for planar laser plasma instability experiments, Magnetized Liner Inertial Fusion
(MagLIF) experiments, and discovery science laboratory astrophysics experiments. Data

A review of the project was started in late FY24 to determine the best path forward.
While no successful 5ω measurements having yet been made, successful measurements
using a 3ω probe beam indicate that there is no underlying physics reason why a
Thomson measurement inside a hohlraum is unfeasible. Instead, it seems likely that our

techniques used to make 3ω TS measurements work well very early in the experiment

In FY25 we carried out a study of alternative Thomson scattering probe beam options.
This review focused on development of a probe design that would allow a significant
relaxation of the requirements for coalignment tolerance and wavefront quality, greatly
increasing the probability of measurement success compared to the tightly speced 5ω
system. The review concluded that a 4ω (263 nm) probe beamline launched from a
dedicated laser port has the potential to provide much greater probe energy (~kJ)
compared to the 5ω scheme (~1J), allowing a significant relaxation of pointing,
wavefront and timing requirements for the OTS probe beam. This system would leverage
the alignment scheme used for the 192 NIF beams to reduce shot cycle time and therefore
opportunity cost of OTS experiments. The additional probe energy available with a larger
beamline also allows us to extend the duration of OTS measurements from 1ns to perhaps
a full 8ns NIF laser pulse, greatly increasing the amount of data that we are able to collect
in a single experiment.

In FY26 an experimental measurement was carried out to assess the background
emission from a hohlraum target in the 4ω Thomson measurement waveband. The
data collected in this experiment showed no significant source of unexpected
background, opening the way for a future 4ω probe capability on the NIF.

The 4ω probe scheme as outlined in our review has received broad support from
our external Thomson scattering expert group, and we are currently in the process
of developing physics requirements for the system and preliminary discussions of
the potential laser architecture with the LLNL laser science groups. It is expected
that the new beamline will reuse the existing OTSL Pre-Amplifier Module (PAM) and
some of its laser amplifiers, and should be able to be built in the existing OTSL laser
room in SW1. We expect to propose an engineering project to implement the probe
capability starting FY28, with the schedule somewhat dictated by the state of
progress of the OSB-VISAR project.

IIIb-3: 3D Neutron Imaging System

Three-dimensional (3D) imaging of thermonuclear (TN) burn provides the size and
shape of the burning region and cold fuel, which has been instrumental in guiding
the ICF campaigns by helping identify design changes used to improve drive
symmetry or mitigate the effects of engineering features like fill tubes and tents.
Gamma imaging capability under development will indicate the location of the

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 18

ablator material, where it has mixed into the fuel, and provide data that can be used
to validate mix modeling.

Nuclear imaging systems have been installed on two equatorial lines of sight (LOS)
at the National Ignition Facility (NIF) - NIS1 on 90-315 and NIS3 on 90-213 - and
one polar LOS, NIS2 on 5-22 (see Figure 3). Each system consists of two main
components: a pinhole aperture (PHA) array that projects neutron and gamma
images down a line of sight, and a detector system that records the images formed
by the PHA.

 Figure 3: The Neutron Imaging System on NIF with current capabilities of each LOS identified in the table

In contrast, the two existing equatorial lines-of-sight use scintillator-based detectors
with time-gated cameras in addition to image plates. The gating allows particles to
be separated by time of flight. Since the active systems each currently have three
cameras, they can collect up to three independent images from the LOS. Typically,
one camera is gated to capture an image of the 14 MeV neutrons providing
information on the size and shape of the fusion burn region. The second camera is
gated to capture an image of the neutron that have scattered and lost energy
providing information on the distribution of the cold fuel. These down-scattered
neutrons, gated in the 6-12 MeV range, are fewer in number and arrive after the 14
MeV neutrons. A third camera is gated earlier at the photon arrival time and acts as
a gamma ray imager, providing information on the remaining ablator by imaging the
4.44-MeV gamma rays produced by inelastic scattering of the 14 MeV neutrons in
the ablator material through the 12C(n,n’γ) reaction. Due to the difficulty of placing
an active system in the polar DIM of NIF, the NIS2 lacks an active camera system
and employs only image plate as detectors. Thus, the NIS2 neutron images are
energy integrated with the signal dominated by the greater number of un-scattered
primary neutrons.

Existing Priorities
The NIS team currently has four major existing priorities:

1. Maintenance & Operation of the current systems – setup, analysis, and
repairs

The NIS currently operates up to eleven separate detectors on ICF shots across three
lines of sight to routinely reconstruct the energy integrated neutron source, the
primary DT neutron source, the downscatter neutrons interacting with the cold fuel,
and the fluence compensated downscatter which represents the cold fuel density
(although not exactly; see forthcoming paper) as a function of position. The setup is
expected-yield dependent, with camera and MCP settings required to be calculated
and setup for every shot to maximise the likelihood of success within the yield range
expected by the experimental RI. This requires constant effort for setup and
validation which is a significant continuous activity for the NIS team.

The NIS data collected on these eleven detectors require sophisticated analyses,
making the current NIS image analysis process labor-intensive and relatively
slow. To address this challenge, in the short term the NIS team has migrated to
leverage High Performance Computing (HPC) resources for multi-process
computing for speed-up of the current code. Medium term, manual processes are
being scripted to allow “typical” datasets to be better automated. Long term, the
code-base will be re-written through a cross-laboratory effort into python. This will
be built from the ground up for HPC-leveraged speed-optimised code [see Proposed
Transformative Thursts, section 1]. Each of these will reduce the total time required

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 20

to reconstruct a single experiments NIS source images, allowing more analysis to be
produced for the same person-hours effort.

The NIS has also experienced failures of multiple MCP gating units and cameras in
the last three years, including 3 cameras in the past 6 months. These failures have
consumed time and energy to replace or repair and for recalibration, but these
efforts are mandatory to maintain operation of the NIS and the calibrations are an
ongoing effort. In FY 26, the team developed a manual optical flatfied
characterization system, it is being benchmarked against historical neutron flatfield
data. The currently used SI-1000s cameras with an integrated taper are no longer
purchasable, so effort is being spent to test newer CIS-40 cameras for potential
integration and replacement.

2. Preparation for 20 MJ operation – snout upgrades, detector upgrades,
possible camera system upgrades

During the FY26 timeframe, NIF progressed through an NNSA L2 milestone to
upgrade the NIS systems to support operations up to 20 MJ of yield. This complex
effort involves separate projects to upgrade snouts that hold the NIS apertures as
well as a project to upgrade the detector systems. The snout upgrades to the lines of
sight along TC(090,315) NIS1 and TC(090,213) NIS3 are complete, and designed to
withstand a debris wind factor of 80. This corresponds roughly to a yield of 30 MJ,
which supports operation into the NIF’s upcoming Enhanced Yield Capability (EYC)
upgrade. The upgrades included strengthening multiple structural components,
changing snout material from aluminum to stainless steel to reduce long-lived
nuclear activation states, and making systems more ergonomic to ease worker
installation and removal processes. A later phase of the project will upgrade the
snout and pinhole array along the polar line-of-sight TC(005,225) NIS2. Upgrades to
the detection system are also planned, including thinning the scintillator and
moving image plates further from the source, both to reduce signal intensity on
highest-yield shots. It is not believed that displacement damage or neutron
interrupts are involved in the damage of the camera systems or that they would be
significant even at 20 MJ.

3. Gamma-ray imaging

The systems’ original plastic scintillators are not optimal for the gamma imaging. In
FY25, GYGAG, a novel high-z scintillator, was developed to better optimise gamma-
ray sensitivity and resolution of NIS1. GYGAG is made in-house at LLNL and is not a
commercially available production-level scintillator. In FY26 an NaI scintillator was
installed on NIS3, to compare the two scintillator types. NaI is commercially
available at a lower price point, and this comparison will be used to evaluate
imaging efficacy and value-for-money.

4. The NEMESIS project for technology transfer to Z

A technical transfer project known as NEMESIS is underway to provide neutron
imaging for the Sandia Z facility. This is a time-integrating 2-D neutron imager
optimized for primary DT neutrons from the stagnation in MDD-ICF. Novel
techniques for large field-of-view imaging are being explored. The primary purpose
is to measure the volume and shape of the fusing plasma in ICF experiments. For
Phase A, the technology developed for the NIF is being used to develop a high-
resolution neutron imaging system for the Sandia Z facility. In FY25, LANL began the
fabrication of the Phase A pinhole array. In FY27, LANL will complete the pinhole
array metrology, assemble the pinhole array in the cradle, and deliver it to LLNL.
LLNL will perform cradle metrology and deliver it to the Z facility. LANL will also
use the metrology data to forward model the as-built pinhole array, which will
enable data analysis, including classified analysis, for NEMESIS by LANL.

In FY 27, the NEMESIS team will investigate physics need for an active system, LANL
will lead efforts to test components and develop requirements to inform project

Proposed Transformative Thrusts

There are also five major proposed transformative thrusts:

1. DIRT
LANL’s NIS reconstruction relies on a capable but complex legacy code whose
primary developers are no longer at LANL. Its NIF-specific data structures
complicate extension to other facilities and new diagnostic modalities. A recently
developed LLNL toolbox provides rapid, independent, reconstructions of primary
and 2D downscattered images, but lacks capabilities for aperture design and 3D
reconstruction. In order to minimize duplicated effort, discussions are underway to
build a joint, modern toolbox between the two labs (and other participating DOE
sites). This Diagnostic Image Reconstruction Toolbox (“DIRT”) will provide an
integrated reconstruction toolkit with a modern, modular Python architecture,
preserving existing analysis capabilities while improving maintainability,
computational performance, and support for measurements at NIF, Z, and Omega.
Development would be coordinated across LANL, LLNL, and SNL, with tasks
organized around subject-matter expertise and code maintained in a shared,
version-controlled DOE repository. A corresponding repository on classified

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 22

systems will support classified analysis applications. The toolkit will be designed for
high-performance computing and integration of new reconstruction capabilities as
the advanced imaging modalities mature. The principal development effort is
planned for FY27–FY28, with migration of legacy capabilities targeted for
completion around FY29. This effort will support sustainable NIS operations and
provide a common analysis foundation for the joint diagnostic program.
2. Advanced Neutron Modalities

Higher neutron yields create opportunities to extend NIS beyond primary and
conventional down-scattered neutron imaging. Spatially resolved ion-temperature
measurements, additional down-scattered energy bands, and reaction-in-flight
(RIF) imaging would provide complementary information on fuel conditions and
mix for comparison with simulations.

Spatially resolved ion temperature. Conventional NIS cameras record a single
time-of-flight, or neutron-energy, interval per detector. Multi-frame cameras have
lower collection efficiency, but ignition and multi-MJ yields may provide sufficient
signal to record multiple energy intervals from one scintillator and infer spatially
resolved ion temperature. Building on a one-dimensional demonstration at Omega
in 2022, published in 2025, the current NSF-supported graduate research will
transition to programmatic support in FY28. The goals are to demonstrate two-
dimensional temperature imaging by FY28 Q4 and three-dimensional imaging by
FY30 Q4.
Low-energy neutron imaging. Scattering kinematics make each down-scattered
energy band sensitive to fuel density within an annular cone about the line of sight.
Recording multiple energy bands, extending down to the backscatter edge, would
sample additional regions of the cold fuel, including the far side relative to the
imager. These measurements could provide a more complete picture of the cold-fuel
density distribution than the current down-scattered imaging band alone.
RIF neutron imaging. RIF neutrons are produced by fusion reactions involving fuel
ions accelerated in preceding collisions. Their sensitivity to mix and charged-
particle stopping makes RIF imaging a potentially valuable additional diagnostic.
Initial tests are possible with existing NIS hardware, although the low signal level
remains a principal challenge. Approximately one year of dedicated testing and
analysis is anticipated to establish validated results.
The unused fourth camera positions on NIS1 and NIS3 provide opportunities to
develop these measurements. Initial RIF tests can use an existing camera; adding a
fourth camera on NIS1 would support further modality testing, while the NIS3
position could initially support ion-temperature imaging tests. Implementation will
depend on camera availability and coordination with other proposed uses of these
positions. Replacement of failed cameras has delayed these additions.

Additionally, a LANL LDRD is beginning in FY27 to develop Shape Quantification and

3. High-Resolution imaging

To increase spatial resolution, a new PHA may need to designed. The current spatial
resolution of NIS is limited, with neutron resolution around 10 µm and gamma
resolution around 15 µm. It may be possible to attain a spatial resolution of 5 µm or
finer to more effectively extract mix information, a capability that may be
particularly useful for the double shell campaign. To do this requires study of the
effect of the existing metrology on the resolution and possible changes to aperture
design and metrology that could improve it. The spatial resolution of the neutron
and gamma imaging system will be enhanced through the simulation and design
(FY26), and potential fabrication (FY27), and deployment (FY28) of a new pinhole
aperture on one of the equatorial NIS systems (NIS1 or NIS3).

3. Classified data acquisition capability

A need has been identified to allow acquisition of classified data with the NIS. This
capability does not exist and has not been designed into the existing NIS. If a project
to allow classified operation is created, it may require changes to hardware, possible
extensive addition of cabling and changes to operation. A thorough examination of
the need and assessment of the effort and cost is needed to understand the scope
and ultimate activities of the project. As discussed above, classified data acquisition
is already available and will be part of the DIRT initiative.

capsules and more mass remaining to increase confinement. Understanding the

5. NIS2 upgrade to complete 3D nature of the systems

The existing 3D reconstructions of the burn region use the energy-integrated image
from the NIS2 rather than a time-gated image, and no down-scattered or gamma-ray
images can be obtained from the polar direction. The next significant improvement
to the 3D neutron and gamma imaging would be to develop energy resolved imaging
on NIS2 (the polar imager), providing true three-axis views for neutrons and
gammas.

One such solution is the installation of an Active System on NIS2, akin to the
cameras used on NIS1 and NIS3. Facility siting studies have been performed with
the conclusion that the likely location of the system would be on the roof of the NIF,
a challenging and expensive location, barring development of faster high-resolution
scintillators and camera development.

A second, more exploratory approach is to research energy-thresholded activation
imaging. This technique has been employed for proton-deuteron selective imaging
on Omega-EP, irradiating metal plates with different activation thresholds. These
are then places on Imaging Plate (IP) detectors, imprinting the activated shape data
onto the scannable IP. An FY28 LDRD will be submitted to explore the neutron
counting metrics to determine if this is viable for fusion neutrons with available and
stable metals.

IIIb-4: Gamma Spectroscopy (GCD)
High-energy γ rays generated from inertial confinement fusion (ICF) experiments
provide unique and important information for studying the dynamics of the nuclear
phase of ICF process, because the energy spectra and amount of γ rays produced are
a direct signature of the nuclear fusion reactions themselves. For example, the 16.75
MeV γ-ray line originating from the nuclear fusion reaction between deuterium (D)
and tritium (T) can offer reaction history information, such as the time of peak
reaction rate (i.e., bang time) and how long thermonuclear conditions are
maintained (i.e., burn width). Due to their high-energy and penetrating nature, γ
rays are the most unperturbed fusion products which can preserve the original birth
information of the fusion process. Fusion γ rays provide a direct measure of nuclear
reaction rates (unlike x rays) without being compromised by Doppler spreading
(unlike neutrons). The metrics the GCD/GRH observe are routinely used as a
baseline, fundamental metrics to understand the basic implosion trajectory and
energy balance of the formed fusion hot spot. Additionally, the yield of 4.44 MeV γ-
rays from the inelastic scattering of the 14 MeV neutrons on the ablator material
measure the compression and mass remaining of the remaining ablator, HT fusion
yield and short pulse MeV photon spectra are all measured by the GRH/GCD. Table I

Two types of gamma spectroscopy diagnostics are currently operational on NIF (see
Figure 5): the four-channel Gamma Reaction History (GRH) instrument and the
single-channel, torpedo-like Gas Cherenkov Detector (GCD). The GRH uses a folded
design, requiring it to be operated outside the target chamber, but allowing the
photomultiplier tube (PMT) to be better shielded from x rays or other sources of
background radiation, such as neutron-induced secondary γ rays. Since the
inception of National Ignition Campaign in 2010, the folded-design GRH diagnostic
on NIF has measured time-resolved measurements of fusion γ rays from implosions.
While Cherenkov conversion process and its optical collection together provide a 10
ps temporal response, however, GRH is limited in temporal resolution by ~ 100 ps
due to temporal response of the PMT.

As a testbed, the linear-design GCD has been developed to improve temporal
resolution down to 10 ps by incorporating a Pulse Dilation (PD) PMT technology.
High band-width γ reaction histories measured by the GCD/PD-PMT confirmed that
the fusion burn widths measured by the slower instrument, GRH/PMT, are still in
good agreement with the faster GCD/PD-PMT data. However, the 10 ps GCD/PD-
PMT measurements (using CO2) have been limited by the presence of a long-
duration (~ 500 ps) tail on the fusion signal, which is not observed on the better-
shielded folded-design GRH (also using CO2). It has been confirmed that the long-
duration tail is produced in the gas cell and is not an artifact of the PD-PMT or
recording system. The current hypothesis is that the tail is caused by sub-threshold
γ rays (i.e., γ rays with energy lower than GCD’s energy threshold at given gas

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 27

pressure) which induce scintillation in the gas. These sub-threshold γ rays are likely
caused by neutron-induced secondary reactions on the hohlraum wall and
thermomechanical package (TMP) used in indirect-drive cryogenic NIF implosions.
The folded-design GRH does not see the tail due to the convoluted optical path used
in the GRH, which efficiently transports the forward-directed Cherenkov radiation,
but may not be efficiently collecting the scintillation, which is isotropic. As a near-
term resolution, neon (Ne) gas was used in the GCD/PD-PMT because Ne produces
enough late-time tail signal so it can be measured at high thresholds without the
Cherenkov signal (i.e., > 17 MeV), and its longer decay time makes the temporal
signature distinguishable from the DT gamma signal. This effort was abandoned
because of high cost of Ne.

Existing Priorities
The GRH team currently has three major existing priorities:

1. High-dynamic range GRH (HIDALGO)

The ICF and HED Programs have determined a need for a gamma reaction
diagnostic with a dynamic range of at least 1000. The current GRH achieves a signal-
to-background up to ~ 12 due to its design, the high radiation environment in the
NIF target bay and its weight constrain. Due to their limited signal-to-background,
only the peak of the fusion burn is measured and measurements cannot detect the
earlier onset of alpha heating, where the slope of reaction history changes the most
abruptly. To record the onset of alpha heating, a yield-rate coverage of at least 1000
will be needed.

HIDALGO, is proposed to be a high dynamic range detector with low background
and will provide reaction history data at higher signal-to-background ratio than is
possible with the existing GRH. To accomplish this improvement, the optical
detectors (i.e., PMT or PD-PMT) must be protected from the high radiation
environment in the target bay because the direct interaction of x rays and neutron-
induced γ rays are dominant background sources. The existing GRH diagnostic was
designed to operate below 1 MJ to give optimum performance on the path to
ignition. Implementing the PD-PMT on GRH has extended the operating yield limit,
but at the 3-4 MJ yield range we are again approaching the operational limit. The
HIDALGO diagnostic is a necessity for extending reaction history measurements
beyond the 20 MJ range into 70MJ.
A need has been identified to allow acquisition of classified data with HIDALGO.
Significant effort will be needed to enable the infrastructure of NIF to support
classified operations. We are proposing to collocate with other classified diagnotics
to reduce the overall cost to the program.

4. Improving temporal resolution of one-channel of GRH

GRH cell D was upgraded with a PD-PMT in FY23. GRH is now capable of measuring
burn width at 2 ps uncertainty and at a yield range of up to 20 MJ. Comparisons
between GRH and GCD showed that the gas scintillation issue GCD is facing is not an
issue on GRH. The dilation window was characterized and the results implemented
in data analysis. Space charge broadening was investigated using benchtop
measurements. The operational envelop acceptable space charge broadening was
established based on the measurements. A space charge correction algorithm was
established. Charge depletion caused a precursor and background was identified as
a limiting factor for burn width measurements on high yield shots. Charge depletion
contributes to widening the measured burn width over the real burn width. This
issue was mitigated using gating. The gating window timing was dialed using bench
top measurements and gating was successfully tested on NIF shot N250930-002.
Background might still be an issue with increasing yields and the need to reduce
signal strength relative to the background. Potential sources of the remaining
background are the PD-PMT input window and the pressure window. A plan to
investigate background sources and possible mitigation was developed. First a
baffle designed to block light emitted by the pressure window at angles large than
5°. If unsuccessful in mitigating the background the background contribution of the
PD-PMT input window can be evaluated by blocking off light entering the PD-PMT. A
SAVI routine for automated analysis of the PD-PMT data on the GRH cell-D was
developed and implemented. The Mach-Zehnder optical data link on GRH is in bad
repair and needs to be improved to guarantee reliable data delivery. Replacement of
failing components is planned and the options of incorporating new bias controllers
for the Mach-Zehnder optical modulators will be investigated.

1. Operation at High Yields

Over the last several years, a dedicated effort has been made to increase the upper
yield limit of the current GRH system. In addition to the 1ω fiducial directly injected
into the scope implemented in FY25, which facilitates operation at high yield by
decoupling signal intensity from fiducial amplitude, this year gating of the
MicroChannel Plate inside the Pulse Dilation Photomultiplier Tube (PD-PMT) was
successfully implemented to cut out large backgrounds detected before the physics
signal, starting on NIF shot N250930. Detailed investigations into the source of
remaining backgrounds were conducted by means of simulations and shot data
analysis. Based on the background arrival time with respect to the physics peak, it’s
thought to arise predominantly from the PD-PMT input window, although there is a
possibility that the pressure window at the interface with the gas cell also
contributes to it. In FY26, hardware will be tested in-situ to definitively identify
where the source is and remove it when possible. However, based on the current
PD-PMT performance, it’s been extrapolated to return useful physics data up to at
least 20 MJ.

Work is also ongoing to recalibrate and optimize the Mach-Zender (MZ) signal
transport system to reduce reflections and any instrument-induced artifacts in the
data. The current GRH system uses one PD-PMT and three standard PMTs.
Historically the standard PMTs return burnwidth and bangtime for implosions <0.3
MJ. Due to detector replacements and changes in the MZ systems, the calibrations
for these items need to be rejuvenated. This is ongoing work and has been
scheduled for early FY27.

Above 20 MJ, a new high-dynamic range gamma reaction history diagnostic has
been proposed, HIDALGO, as described below.

Proposed Transformative Thrusts

3. Exploring alternative ultra-fast detector concepts-

Despite the achieved unprecedented ~10 ps time resolution achieved, high gain,
highly igniting ICF implosions are predicted to have extremely narrow burn widths
(15 to 20 ps), where even the current detector may not be enough. Furthermore, if
even faster time resolution could be achieved, new physics phenomena - like
observing the movement of rattling, rebounding shocks – could be temporally
observed.

There are a couple advanced concepts that could achieve single or sub-picosecond
temporal resolution. One example led by General Atomics and LLNL is an etalon
technique, an optical cavity with transmissive media and parallel reflecting surfaces,
that is sensitive to the incident radiation. Energy deposited in the etalon by the
radiation changes the refractive index of the media quasi-instantaneously (of order

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 32

a picosecond), changing the global reflectance and transmittance of the etalon. The
cavity, with length dimension <<1 mm, can have response time <10 ps. Preliminary
tests of possible etalon materials have been conducted, and proof of principle has
been accomplished.

A LANL LDRD project is funded to investigate the use of coherent transition
radiation to measure burn width of DT reaction histories. The gamma signal will be
converted to Compton electrons and further into GHz and THz radiation. The burn
width will be imprinted in the frequency spectrum cut off, taking the measurement
from time domain to time integrated frequency domain. A transition radiation
detector could be built much cheaper than a gas Cherenkov detector with pulse
dilation and could have sub ps temporal resolution but might have issues extracting
burn shapes.

3. Gamma-to-Electron magnetic Spectrometer (GEMS)

GEMS is a gamma ray spectrometer, giving up the fast time resolution of the
Cherenkov technique for energy resolution of the created gamma ray spectrum,
allowing novel physics measurements. The GEMS would allow the first-time
measurement of the MeV spectra created from ICF implosions. It would have a
slower time response (~1 ns) and so would be integrated over the implosion. GEMS
would answer fundamental physics questions about the nuclear physics reactions
that create the MeV spectra, which currently have high uncertainty. GEMS would
give very precise ablator areal density of arbitrary materials. Different
measurements and theoretical predictions DT fusion gamma spectrum are not in
agreement. GEMS could provide a high-resolution spectrum measurement of the DT
fusion gamma spectrum.

IIIb-5: Time resolved neutron spectrum

The goal of the proposed transformational time resolved neutron spectrum (TRNS)
diagnostic is to infer neutron yield (Y), ion temperature (Tion), compression (through the
down-scatter ratio, dsr) and directional flow (vflow) as a function of time. Current neutron
spectrometers all make a single burn-integrated measurement of these quantities. The top-
level physics requirement for TRNS were summarized in Kunimune et al, 2021 [A],
which concluded based on ensemble simulations that dρR/dt should be measurable to ±60
mg/cm2 / 100ps and dTion/dt to ±1.9 keV/ 100ps at bang time. While the exact numbers
depend on ignition dynamics, any time-resolved information for these quantities that
could be obtained would be more than currently available; there is no hard cut off below
which time resolved information would not be useful. TRNS output would strongly
constrain simulations and our understanding of ignition physics (see Fig. 6, Ref. [B]),
providing critical information towards the long-term goals of the ICF program of
increasing gain (G>>1) and understanding the limits of gain at the NIF.

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 34

Fig. 6. xRAGE simulations by Brian Haines at LANL showing (left) ρR vs time and (right) neutron
production rate vs time for implosions below, at and above the “ignition cliff”. Below the cliff, burn
occurs mainly at peak compression. On the cliff, burn occurs during disassembly, and the
disassembly quenches the burn. Above the cliff, burn takes off rapidly and disassembles faster. Time-
resolved neutron spectra would enable direct measurement of peak ρR for these implosions (not just
burn-weighted ρR), direct measurement of confinement time, and a determination of when the
capsule ignites (using ρR vs time and Tion vs time). This information would be highly constraining for
simulations and for our understanding of ignition physics. Reproduced from Ref. [B].

Key information that would be obtained includes:

 • Plasma conditions at the start of ignition from Tion(t). This would be used to
 evaluate the timing of cross-over into the ignition regime. More robust implosions
 can ignite before peak compression, which changes the relative timing of burn
 and peak compression. For example, N210808 is believed to have ignited right at
 peak compression, which is the latest you could possibly ignite, leading to burn
 substantially after peak compression and burn as the capsule is exploding. This
 trend has continued with more recent ignition shots on the NIF. As NIF laser
 capabilities increase in power and energy, the ability to drive implosions harder
 could lead to ignition at or before peak compression.
 • Confinement time inferred from the width of dsr(t). Pure burn width
 measurements currently made are in some sense unrelated to confinement because
 they do not correlate with compression.
 • Peak compression inferred from the max of dsr(t). In contrast, a time-integrated
 dsr is neutron weighted and will sometimes provide information about peak
 compression, sometimes not; this measurement is not comparable between shots.
Currently, THD implosions are frequently used as a surrogate to address some of these
questions, but this is not a perfect method, due to the high sensitivity to small target
imperfections. TRNS would also eliminate the need for THD implosions to determine
“burn-off” conditions, saving of order $1M per configuration.

At the Spring 2026 meeting of the National Diagnostics Working Group, time-resolved
diagnostics, including TRNS, were highly requested by the community in the breakout
session for Next Generation High Energy Density Facility Diagnostics. This breakout
charged the group of 100+ scientists with the task: Identify key diagnostics and
development approaches on the Diagnostic Roadmap for the Next Generation High
Energy Density (NGHED) facility. Time-resolved measurements were desirable to

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 35

benchmark new designs, diagnose applications of ignition, and understand the
dynamics of burn propagation in an NGHED facility. Recent published work
emphasizes the different phases of the imploding system including compression,
mass ablation, thermal runaway, and expansion [S,T]. These works are based on
using gamma reaction history coupled with modeling to reconstruct the
temperature-density evolution of the implosion. With TRNS, we could directly
measure this desired evolution, and evaluate burn propagation.

In summary, TRNS would provide an unprecedented window into the dynamic energy
balance of implosions not accessible with any current diagnostics, and can be anticipated
to uncover “unknown unknowns” [C].

Historically, the primary technique pursued for making this measurement has been time-
resolved Magnetic Recoil neutron Spectrometry (MRSt). While much progress has been
made towards an MRSt design [A, D-M], there is substantial uncertainty about the
feasibility of the proposed backend detector concepts, and further development of MRSt
has been put on hold pending a resolution to this question. In FY25, development work
was undertaken on a simplified proposed MRSt backend detector concept using printed
circuit board (PCB) technology, proposed by LLNL’s Clement Trosseille. The idea of the
PCB concept is to split the MRSt focal plane into 1cm wide bins each covering ~100keV
energy, with the signal within each bin passively time-aligned using varying-length PCB
traces before recording each bin on a fast digitizer channel. Prior to the PCB, a micro-
channel plate (MCP) will be used to convert incident deuterons to electrons. The FY25
effort involved investigating how to minimize cross talk between and impedance match
PCB traces all while considering size constraints. Progress was made as reported in a
qualifying exam paper by MIT graduate student Audrey DeVault [M], but more work
remains to prove this technology as an MRSt backend detector concept.

An alternative proposed technique is the so-called tomographic neutron time-of-flight
(nTOF) technique, which uses multiple time-of-flight detectors along a single line of
sight but located at different distances from the source [N-Q]. Recently Charlotte Stuart,
a graduate student at the University of Oxford, has implemented the analytic framework
of Appelbe and Crilly [R] into a computational algorithm, with promising results. Her
publication was recently accepted to Physics of Plasmas, and demonstrates the method’s
ability to reconstruct time-evolving temperature and hotspot velocity from synthetic
nTOF data [U]. A key advantage of this moments-based approach is that it requires only
a minimal number of detectors, reducing the diagnostic complexity needed to recover
time-dependent source properties. Measuring higher order spectral moments is a critical
aspect of this technique, and so revisiting/updating earlier work on this [V] is a next step.
Beyond that, the subsequent step will be to include real-world instrument response
functions and noise, and evaluate the method’s solutions. Obtaining a time-resolved dsr
measurement using this method also remains an open question.
To support and complement this work, researchers at Imperial College London, MIT, and
LLNL are developing numerical tools to investigate realistic single line-of-sight
diagnostic configurations. At Imperial, a synthetic data pipeline based on the NeSST
code [X] has been developed to generate nTOF data with different detectors and noise

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 36

levels from a single source function. At MIT and LLNL, a NIF-specific synthetic data
generator has been developed, capable of producing synthetic collinear nTOF
observations from multiple detectors with variable distance to target chamber center, time
resolution, and instrument response function. These data pipelines have been used to
investigate diagnostic sensitivity and correlation with time varying source parameters.
Furthermore, at Imperial, large synthetic datasets have been used to train convolutional
neural networks to recover the time varying source parameters. This allows semi-
automated optimization of diagnostic configurations.
An MIT graduate student, Audrey DeVault, has implemented an updated version of the
Analog Monte Carlo Reconstruction Technique [W,P] and has reconstructed time-
evolving temperature and downscatter profiles from synthetic data generated to model
NIF implosions for a variable number of collinear detectors with tunable distances, time
resolutions and instrument response functions. Realistic noise, imitating that observed on
experimental nTOF data collected at the NIF, is also implemented. The reconstructed
time-resolved neutron energy spectra, as well as the retrieved temperature and areal
density profiles, are directly compared to those used to generate the synthetic data to
quantify the reconstruction accuracy. In this way, a variety of diagnostic configurations
and sensitivities can be evaluated to understand the minimum requirements of an
effective tomographic nTOF scheme at the NIF. The next step will be to include realistic,
detector-dependent instrument response functions enabling the investigation of specific
detectors and materials, and evaluate the technique’s success through documentation of
retrieval accuracies for a wide variety of detector schemes.
An alternative technique to acquire only time-resolved ion temperature has also been
investigated using a gamma reaction history (GRH) diagnostic with a series of converter
plates mounted in front. Placed close to the source, the technique relies on converting
incoming neutrons to gammas via (n,n’γ) reactions with the foil, and then the gamma
particles traveling at the speed of light, i.e., ~6x faster than the neutrons, toward the
Cherenkov detection system. The analysis of these multiple foils at slightly different
distances is similar to that mentioned above, and there could be overlap in developing the
two techniques perhaps.

A national TRNS working group continued to meet throughout FY26, and adjusted core
membership to include international participants. Brian Appelbe at Imperial College, as
well as graduate students and collaborators, have met regularly with scientists and
students at MIT and LLNL.

In general, progress towards implementation of TRNS hinges on a decision on path
forward along with funding allocations. Within current funding levels, progress will
remain slow.

IIIb-6: High Resolution Velocimeter (HRV)

The current VISAR instrument on NIF is a highly reliable, versatile, high precision
diagnostic which is run as a primary diagnostic on 100+ shots per year. It provides
space- and time-resolved measurements (in one spatial dimension) of the velocities
of free surfaces, shocks in transparent media and the motion of internal interfaces
behind windows in dynamically driven targets. The two-dimensional data sets can
be analyzed to infer space- and time-resolved pressure in the driven target as a
function of time. The velocimeter can therefore be regarded as a high precision

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 39

pressure gauge. About a dozen shots per year are devoted to shock tuning for the
ICF program which help to set the timing and initial symmetry of the drive on the
capsule. Such tuning experiments are required every time a new target design is
developed. The bulk of VISAR shots are devoted to various of the stockpile
assessment platforms indicated in more detail below. In all cases these platforms
require precise and accurate measurements of target pressure history.

New programmatic needs that exceed the current capabilities were identified which
motivated the inception of a new High Resolution Velocimeter (HRV) instrument
including a new High-Resolution 1D VISAR, as well as a new High-Resolution 2D
VISAR capability together with a visible streaked pyrometry system. The 2D VISAR
can provide important data sets on the seed-level non-uniformities that underlying
the Richtmyer-Meshkov and Rayleigh-Taylor instabilities commonly found to occur
in dynamically-driven targets in the context of ICF, and which are of general interest
in the HEDS community.

The current NIF Velocimetry Velocity (Doppler) Interferometer System for Any
Reflector (VISAR) can track the velocity of a moving reflecting surface with sub %
precision using a visible Laser probe at 660 nm and an interferometer to transform
Doppler shift into a fringe phase shift proportional to the velocity of the reflector. A
time-delay element (etalon) determines the velocity sensitivity. Specifically, the NIF
VISAR is a line-imaging (1D) instrument: a f/3 optical-relay images the target onto
the entrance slit of a streak camera fitted with a CCD detector. The system therefore
records the velocity time-history for ~100 resolution elements corresponding to
different spatial positions onto the target. Two field-of-view options are available:
1mm and 2 mm. The spatial resolution is currently limited by the streak cameras.
Space-time distortion inherent to streak-tube technology are also strongly limiting
the performance near the edges of the field of view. The time-resolution is currently
around 30 ps over a 30 ns streak.

The ICF program needs to accurately track the relative timing and speed of the
multiple shock waves launched into ICF capsules (shock timing experiments) to
benchmark radiation-hydrodynamic simulations of high-yield DT implosions. There
is also a need to understand capsule and shock-front non-uniformities which
degrade implosion quality as even very small velocity variations can seed major
degradation in the implosion quality. There is therefore a need to document the
velocity distribution in-flight, with micrometer spatial resolution and meter-per-
second velocity sensitivity.

HED Stockpile Stewardship Science programs perform critical experiments to study
the Equation of State (EOS), strength, phase transitions and short range
microstructure of High-Z materials using the PHPEOS, RampEOS, RMStrength,
TARDIS and EXAFS platforms which all use VISAR as a primary diagnostic. There is a

IIIb-7: Time-Resolved X-ray Diffraction

X-ray diffraction is a well-established technique to identify phases and document
phase transitions in compressed materials of interest to the SSP. X-ray diffraction is
the most discriminating method to identify the phase of a solid material because it
reflects the distances between atoms with high precision. The technique has now
been developed for use in HED experiments at NIF, OMEGA, and Z.

Early work on NIF has been limited to using TARget Diffraction In-Situ (TARDIS) to
obtain one or two snapshots per very expensive laser shot. Time-resolved x-ray
diffraction will build on the demonstrated success of the platform to provide a
significant enhancement to the HED materials diffraction program by providing
multiple snapshots during a single laser shot with one target. The first diagnostic
commissioned in the XRDt campaign was the Gated Diffraction Development
Diagnostic (G3D); this diagnostic was developed to confirm the feasibility of hCMOS
sensors to be used in the NIF environment. The Flexible Imaging Diffraction
Diagnostic for Laser Experiments (FIDDLE) was built and was fielded on NIF for the
first time in 2023 (Figure 8). There are five Icarus hCMOS sensors in FIDDLE to
maximize the diffraction angle. These hCMOS sensors collect 4 x 2 ns images, with
an interframe time of 2 ns, for a total collection time of 14 ns.

The ability to observe material phase at multiple times during a solid-solid or solid-
liquid phase transition within a single laser experiment will substantially constrain
equilibrium material phase boundaries and enhance our understanding of kinetic
effects that drive or inhibit phase transformation in materials at high densities.
Moreover, we anticipate that the time-gated quality of these measurements will
improve the signal to noise of measurements as background signals (which are
substantial) are not integrated over the entire experiment. With this platform our
facilities will be able to make multiple diffraction measurements of a solid material
compressed to multi-Megabar pressures over timescales as long as tens of
nanoseconds.

IIIb-8: >15 keV X-ray Detection (DHEX)

Efficient detection of high-energy X-rays (DHEX) with multi-frame capability is crucial
for advancing high energy density (HED) applications and programs. This capability is
increasingly important as experiments probe plasmas with higher density, temperature, or
larger volume, requiring higher X-ray energies.

Developing multi-frame, high-energy X-ray detectors is a priority for numerous HED
facilities worldwide. Such detectors would enable observation of the evolution. Of a
single driven target on sub-nanosecond timescales. They would improve data quality by
consolidating measurements that would otherwise require multiple shots, reducing
uncertainty caused by variations in target and laser conditions. They would also lower
experimental costs and minimize uncertainties associated with comparing trends across
multiple experiments.

The current effort to detect high-energy (HE) X-rays falls into three categories: bulk

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 44

diode detecotrs, electron-based photocathode detectors, and fast scintillator materials.
The first two approaches differ in temporal resolution, ns vs 10s of ps scales.

Current photodiode detectors use 25 to 50 µm Si diodes optimized for approximately 5
keV X-rays. Thicker 100 to 200 µm diodes would improve absorption of high-energy X-
rays, but would also slow the response and increase pixel blooming. Even at greater
thicknesses, absorption is only about 10% at 25 keV, with response times of 5 to 10 ns.
Other diode materials under development such as GaAs and Ge are of lower TRL but are
promising with regards to improved X-ray detection at higher energies while having a
fast expected temporal response. Ongoing work at SNL includes 40µm-thick, pixelated
GaAs diodes with an expected 40% absorption @25 keV, and 0.44 ns expected temporal
response. LLNL was working with UC Davis on a Ge pixelated diode with expected 50%
absorption @ 25 keV for 60 µm Ge with a temporal response of 1 ns.

The diodes can be integrated with fast Read Out Integrated Circuits (ROICs), which can
saturate under hard X-rays, prompting higher-capacity capacitors and charge dumping.
High-k MIM capacitors have increased full-well capacity up to fivefold without major
redesigns. A testable GaAs hCMOS sensor was demonstrated in FY23, though shorting
prevents nanosecond framing. GaAs yield and defect issues remain under investigation,
while InP is being evaluated for improved efficiency above 30 keV. Currently
improvements in wafer quality, packaging, and process control are being prioritized to
enhance overall device reliability and yield.

Photocathode-based diagnostics offer ultrafast (ps-range) resolution but have poor
efficiency above 10 keV, with DQE below 0.5% due to broad electron distributions.
Geometric structures, including MCPs and structured photocathodes, can improve QE,
with gold structures achieving a 3.4× gain at 7.5 keV. However, temporal fidelity,
coatings, signal quality, and space-charge effects remain challenges. MCP photocathode
development, including modeling and characterization efforts are currently paused by
resource limitations.

IIIb-9: hCMOS

The hCMOS technology developed at Sandia’s Microsystems Engineering Science &
Applications (MESA) facility is the world’s fastest, multi-frame, burst mode imaging
sensor capable of capturing images on the nanosecond timescale. The hCMOS
technology provides three key benefits to many ICF and HED diagnostics under
development: time-sequenced imaging from the same sensor area, background
reduction through time gating, and operation under high neutron fluences (i.e.,
sensors are radiation hardened). The hCMOS sensors and supporting camera
electronics are currently the most radiation tolerant imagers on NIF and Z. This

radiation tolerance and ability to gate out unwanted background is a growing
benefit to the programs for both high yield and high background experiments that
are frequently encountered at NIF, Z, and Omega.

For thermonuclear burn experiments, hCMOS (in conjunction with SLOS

diagnose failure modes of high convergence implosions. In opacity experiments,

variability and reduces the overall use of Pu on the facilities. In hostile environment

will improve the fidelity of the x-ray test environment for more precise evaluation of

IV: Broad Diagnostic Efforts
The NDWG defined a class of diagnostics or diagnostic related activities which benefits
from significant national efforts and will enable new or more precise measurements
across the complex. These are: Precision nToF, Mix, and Te, Image Analysis, Hard X-
Ray Detectors, X-ray Doppler Velocimetry (XDV), and Synthetic Data. There is also an
ongoing effort to maintain the capability to scan imaging plates with high accuracy and
consistency across the complex.

One part of the precision nToF activity is to continue improving the measurement
accuracy of the hot-spot velocity inferred from the nToF spectrum. Existing nToF
detectors provided the first evidence that DT implosions at both NIF and Omega have a
residual bulk velocity limiting the efficiency of the implosion. Both direct and indirect
drive experiments have confirmed the adverse impact of residual hot-spot velocity on
fusion yield and was confirmed with simulations.

Measuring the hot-spot velocity requires better than 10000:1 precision owing to the
relatively small drift velocity ~10-100km/s that modifies the birth neutron spectrum that
has a velocity associated with the 14 MeV neutron of 51233km/s. A simple hot-spot
model asserts that the mean neutron energy is modified by both bulk hot-spot motion and
the average kinetic energy (or ion temperature) of the D and T reactants. To characterize
the 3D velocity vector and ion temperature a minimum 3 or 4 independent nToF detectors
are required at quasi-orthogonal positions around the target chamber. Each of these must
be sufficiently well collimated so that the neutron spectrum is not significantly impacted
by the scattering environment and of sufficient temporal response to meet the precision
requirements described above. This need has led to a multi-laboratory goal within the
nToF community to implement more collimated lines of sight and faster fused-silica
Cherenkov nToF detectors to better characterize the bulk flow. In addition adding a fifth
detector on NIF has improved the accuracy of the velocity measurement as shown in

Advanced Stagnation Diagnostics for Z:

After a decade of intense investment, there are well established diagnostic capabilities
demonstrated at the NIF that are used to make quantitative measurements on ICF
plasmas. Many of these diagnostics were developed as national collaborations toward the
goal of determining the efficacy of NIF to achieve ignition. Starting in late 2020 a tech-
transfer effort was initiated to transfer key measurement capabilities and engineer for use
at Z. These demonstrated capabilities on the NIF is bringing the measurement maturity
for characterizing magnetic direct drive (MDD) fusion plasmas on Z up to the long-
standing ability of the NIF. Three types of measurements were identified: (1) x-ray
emission durations using x-ray streak cameras (SCORPIONZ), (2) improved neutron
time-of-flight detectors (ZNTOF) on multipe lines of Sight, and (3) 2D-neutron imaging
(NEMESIS) of Z-pinch implosions.

Nuclear burn volume, burn and confinement time, burn magnitude (and spectra), and ion
burn temperature are fundamental to quantifying burn performance. At present, Z has
limited capabilities to measure neutron production duration and no 2D neutron imaging
capability. Routine 1D-neutron imaging is performed but is limited to a spatial resolution
of 0.3 mm. In addition, there are no capabilities to disambiguate effects of motion
broadening of neutron spectra from a thermonuclear ion temperature. While substantial
understanding can be gained with integrated measurements, multi-dimensional spatially
and temporally resolved data is critical to assess key questions on performance and
scaling and to utilize burning platforms for stockpile science. The physics requirements
for spatial and temporal resolution in nuclear and x-ray measurements in some MDD
platforms require existing capabilities on Z to improve by roughly an order of magnitude.
The technology to make each of these measurements on NIF are routine and in some
cases have been deployed along multiple lines-of-sight.

2.2. Capsule Response Local diagnostics:
For laser indirect drive the hohlraum produces a large flux of soft x-rays which are
absorbed in the capsule containing the DT fuel. The outer wall of the capsule (the
“ablator”) is heated by the x-rays and expands rapidly, driving a series of shocks that
travel through the ablator and fuel, leading to a quasi-spherical implosion that both
compresses and heats the DT fuel. Capsule response diagnostics are required to
measure the symmetry and performance of the ablator and fuel as it implodes.
Capsule response diagnostics are shown in Fig.13. Just after the completion of NIC,
the ablator uniformity, density and symmetry were measured using backlit 2D x-ray
radiography (2DCONA), using an x-ray backlighter to provide a shadow of the
ablator imaged via a pinhole array onto a gated framing camera (GXD). This
diagnostic provided critical insight into the deleterious effects of the capsule
support tent seeding a hydrodynamic instability, which led to non-uniform
implosion and mixing of ablator into the fuel and hot spot — data that helped drive
changes to the laser pulse shape and support-tent thickness, and paved the way to
the HDC capsules that achieved ignition in 2022.
The single line of sight imaging diagnostic (SLOS), one of the nine NDWG
transformational diagnostics, replaced the gated x-ray imagers as the detector for
2DCONA. Coupling SLOS with a mono-chromatic imaging crystal (CBI-SLOS) enabled
ablator radiography measurements to overcome capsule self-emission within
~100ps of bang-time, using the crystal’s narrow bandwidth to discriminate
backlighter x-rays from broadband self-emission. This technique has been used to
infer mixing of ablator material into the hot spot. Because the SLOS prototype

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 53

proved difficult to calibrate and operate routinely, the program moved to short-
pulse x-rays with image plates, most notably the Dual Crystal CBI (DCBI) system
fielded since late FY24, which uses two narrowband crystals to image emission from
dopant material (tungsten) into the hot spot with either image plates or HGXD
detectors.
To improve the precision and throughput of CBI/DCBI radiography, an Automatic
Alignment System (CBI ATLAS) is being developed to replace the current manual,
operator-dependent alignment process with a laser-referenced automated system.
Because CBI/DCBI resolution is highly sensitive to final placement, this upgrade is
expected to improve image quality and reduce shot-to-shot alignment scatter so that
differences between images more faithfully reflect target physics. CBI ATLAS will
also enable co-fielding of DCBI with the new NIS-3 neutron-imaging snout, allowing
paired x-ray and neutron measurements from the same target volume for the first
time, while eliminating roughly two hours of operator time per shot.
Implosion trajectory and ablator density are also measured using one-dimensional
x-ray backlighting (1DCONA/DISC), which projects a 1D slit image onto an x-ray
streak camera and continues to provide a wealth of data on shock transit through
ablator materials for both the ICF and HED programs.

room in the Operational Support Building (OSB) outside the NIF Target Bay,
allowing the current Target Bay interferometer system to eventually be
decommissioned. The upgrade also adds a third interferometer leg for increased
dynamic range and includes new opto-mechanical beam transport, control system,
and facility infrastructure. A companion VISAR Target Qualification Station (VTQS)
is also being commissioned to provide quantitative reflectance maps of VISAR
targets during and after fabrication, helping the shot Responsible Individual assess
target quality and plan VISAR alignment.
2.3. Stagnation Local diagnostics:
As the capsule approaches peak compression, the hot spot begins to approach
maximum density and temperature conditions and produces copious neutrons and
x-rays. These particles and photons are used to diagnose stagnation conditions
~100-200ps around time of peak compression. As shown in Fig.14, a large number
of diagnostics were commissioned on NIF to characterize stagnation conditions
during the NIC. Since then, there have been multiple advances that are greatly
improving understanding of these challenging implosions. Some notable examples
are; (1) the use of 5 neutron time of flight detectors to provide high precision

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 54

measurements of neutron ion temperature and the bulk velocity of the neutron
emitting hot spot; (2) the use of the multi-kJ, 30ps ARC laser to provide a higher
energy X-ray backlight images of the compressed fuel at stagnation, via Compton
scattering; (3) multiple Neutron and X-ray imaging lines of sight to provide 3D
reconstructions of the neutron and x-ray emitting volume at stagnation. These
diagnostics are continuing to provide powerful insights into degradation
mechanisms that prevent NIF implosions from reaching their designed
performance. The contribution of these diagnostics to the achievement of ignition
has been described in detail in a recent Physics of Plasmas publication [J. Kilkenny
et al., “The crucial role of diagnostics in achieving ignition on the National
Ignition Facility (NIF)” Phys. Plasmas 31, 080501 (2024)].

Stagnation local diagnostic status in FY26:
HYXI (High Yield X-ray Imager): A new x-ray imaging system, HYXI, is
progressively replacing the combination of hardened x-ray framing cameras (HGXD,
used up to ~2x1016 neutron yield), the remote ARIANE framing camera (~1x1017
neutrons), and the film-based polar and equatorial DIXI systems that have carried
time-resolved and time-integrated hot-spot shape and brightness measurements to
the highest yields achieved on NIF to date. HYXI phase-1 — which adds a third,

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 55

orthogonal line of sight alongside the existing polar (pDIXI) and equatorial (eDIXI)
systems — was commissioned on NIF shot N251024-001 and now provides time-
integrated x-ray imaging of imploding capsules approaching ignition. It uses a
hybrid pinhole/penumbral aperture array held by the newly developed NIS-3 snout
and a large-area imaging-plate detector filtered with titanium foils for spectral
discrimination, enabling three-dimensional reconstruction of implosion-core
density, temperature, and atomic mix fraction and improving our understanding of
degradation mechanisms such as compression loss and radiative cooling. Later
phases of HYXI will introduce the new Hyperion hCMOS sensor and its associated
20MJ-compatible readout electronics — currently in development under a
foundational hCMOS radiation-hardening effort shared with other HED&IS
applications — to add time resolution, expand the active detection area, and
improve signal-to-background, replacing the current MCP/phosphor/film-based
backend.
Neutron imaging system hardening (HY NIS and PHYS): The Nuclear Imaging
Systems (NIS), which locate pinhole arrays ~20cm from the fusion source to focus
neutron and gamma images, are being hardened for high-yield operation across
multiple lines of sight (HY NIS), including a dedicated effort (PHYS) to harden the
polar imaging snout, which sits closest to the source and must support dual use with
other polar diagnostics. LANL is manufacturing a new high-yield pinhole array and
the associated design modifications needed to support high yields on the equatorial
lines of sight, while LLNL is developing variable-thickness beamline filtering to
shield the remote (~25m) electronic detectors from high-yield backgrounds.
Separately, an upgrade of the NIS_1 gamma-imaging channel with a higher-
resolution GyGAG scintillator, completed on a full line of sight in FY26, now allows
simultaneous measurement of the shape of the neutron-emitting hot spot, the DT
fuel, and the carbon ablator on a single line of sight; a second line of sight using a
new Sodium Iodide scintillator is planned to add a further constraint on the state of
the stagnating plasma.
HIDALGO (next-generation Gamma Reaction History): The Gamma Reaction
History (GRH) diagnostic, co-developed by LANL and LLNL to measure the time of
peak fusion emission and burn duration via Compton-converted gamma radiation
and Cherenkov detection, has been operating with a pulse-dilation photomultiplier
(PDPMT) channel offering ~10ps temporal resolution (versus ±50ps/±100ps for the
original PMT-based system), together with a gated microchannel plate that rejects
neutron-induced backgrounds — producing good signal-to-background on multi-MJ
shots (e.g., the 3.6MJ yield shot N250930). Building on this success, LANL and LLNL
are jointly developing a next-generation High Dynamic range GRH capability
(HIDALGO) intended to extend time-resolved burn-history measurements —
bangtime, burn duration, and temporal evolution of the fusion reaction — across a
much wider dynamic range, from early low-yield platform-development shots
through 20+ MJ ignition experiments and ultimately toward the 50-70 MJ regime
anticipated for EYC. A joint System Requirements Review, informed by LANL-
delivered simulation-validated specifications for the 50-70 MJ range (targeting

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 56

~10ps temporal resolution) and background studies for platforms of interest, is a
near-term milestone for this effort.
RTNADS: Absolute neutron yield is measured using zirconium Nuclear Activation
Detectors (Well_NADS). DT fuel uniformity is also measured by a distribution of
chamber nuclear activation detectors (RTNADS), which since 2020 has provided
automatic analysis of low-mode fuel uniformity at ~40 locations (versus 18 for the
earlier FNADs system), enabling fuel maps with higher Legendre modal content
(L<4 versus L<2). This detector is now run routinely on all high-yield shots. Because
the LaBr detectors induce a background that takes time to decay — constraining the
interval between high-yield DT shots to roughly two weeks — the PMT detectors
are removed from high-yield shots and reinstalled on a subset of Zr pucks afterward
to protect them from high neutron yields, providing a low-mode (L<2) fuel
uniformity map that is compared to downscattered neutron images from the NIS
systems.
NTOF_20 (and hardening for high yield, HY nToF / NTOF-C): As described in the
broad diagnostics section, a suite of five Neutron Time of Flight systems (NTOF_20;
each ~20m from the center of the chamber) measures ion temperature and hot spot
bulk velocity via broadening of the neutrons emitted from the hot spot at peak
compression, and also measures the fraction of neutrons down-scattered by the DT
fuel (the Down Scattered Ratio, DSR), giving five measurements of fuel density
around the implosion. These diagnostics have been developed via a decade-long
collaboration between LLNL, LLE, LANL and Sandia. High-temporal-resolution
quartz detectors on the five NTOFs are also used to routinely measure hot-spot bulk
velocity, a key degradation indicator, since bulk velocities above ~50km/s are a
leading indicator of implosion asymmetry that reduces the conversion of kinetic
energy to hot-spot internal energy. To extend this capability to higher yields, newly
upgraded photomultiplier tubes are being installed for improved response linearity,
together with selectable attenuating filters in the collimated beamlines to prevent
detector saturation at high yield (HY nToF), with deployment on multi-MJ
experiments targeted in the near term. In parallel, this hardened capability is being
configured and qualified for use on classified volume-burn experiments (NTOF-C).
4. Local diagnostics supporting HED science experiments on NIF:
The diagnostic suites support experiments that generate weapon-relevant HED
conditions in specialized laboratory environments for NIF, Omega and Z. These are
generally experiments that use a subset of the facility diagnostics to study focused
physics problems relevant to weapons science in the general areas of radiation flow
and material properties at high pressures (including equation of state, opacities, and
material strength), as shown in Fig.15. On NIF the VISAR system is used for shock
timing in ICF implosions and for equation of state studies that have provided
important data on a range of materials for the national HED program; consistent
with the OSB-VISAR upgrade covered in Section 2.2, the VISAR detection system is
being made compatible with multi-MJ yields by relocating it to a shielded location
outside the NIF Target Bay. For material-property (equation-of-state) experiments

involving high-Z samples, a new Equation-of-State Catcher system (EOS Catcher) is
being deployed to capture and limit the dispersion of scattered high-Z debris in the
target chamber, keeping accumulated high-Z material within administrative limits.
X-ray diffraction is another important diagnostic that directly measures crystal
structure, adding a further constraint on the equation of state in HED experiments;
on NIF, the TARDIS diagnostic has been the workhorse for characterizing crystal
structure of materials at high pressure.

With the development of volume burn platforms that utilize opaque metal shells
(pushered single shells and double shells), diagnostics capable of constraining high-

levels of mix that occurred. These capabilities exist at NIF but must be regularly
maintained and improved for future use. The NIF Gaseous radchem diagnostic

5. Development of high yield diagnostics for Enhanced Yield Capability (EYC)
and beyond:
Sections 2 through 4 described the current generation of hohlraum-drive, capsule-
response, stagnation, and HED-science diagnostics on NIF; this section turns to how
that suite scales toward higher yield. Diagnostic development for multi-MJ yields
has been an ongoing effort at the National Ignition Facility since its earliest
experiments in 2010, when neutron yields were in the ~1kJ range. Most of these
improvements have involved incorporating three main elements: (a) moving
detector systems to more remote locations, (b) incorporating hardened detectors,
such as film, CMOS or hCMOS, and (c) incorporating line-of-sight shielding of
detector systems. Applying these elements has allowed NIF to provide critical data
for HED science as the yield has increased by more than three orders of magnitude
over the past decade of NIF experiments.
For example, time-resolved x-ray imaging with framing cameras evolved from non-
radiation-hard CCD cameras (GXD) placed 1.2m from the x-ray source, to radiation-
insensitive optical film in HGXD at 1.2m, then optical film at 7m with ARIANE, and
most recently to film-based pulse-dilation DIXI diagnostics. The next step in this
evolution is the High Yield X-ray Imager (HYXI) covered in Section 2.3, whose phase-
1 imaging-plate-based system is now commissioned on NIF, with later phases
planned to incorporate the new Hyperion hCMOS sensor to push time-resolved
imaging performance further while also improving data quality and operational
efficiency relative to the current film/MCP-based approach.

The planned upgrade to NIF laser energy, Enhanced Yield Capability (EYC), is
envisioned to produce neutron yields in the 50-70 MJ range, opening up a broader
and deeper set of weapons science questions than are accessible today, and is
expected in the early-to-mid 2030s. EYC is the critical bridge between current NIF
experiments — which are targeting yields of 10-30 MJ over the next 5-7 years —
and the higher-yield regimes needed for future weapons science. Based on
experience with NIF implosions, reaching the full 50-70 MJ range is expected to
require 3-4 years of sustained effort after EYC energy levels become available, so the
diagnostics needed to support those campaigns, including new exemplar platforms,
must be in place before EYC comes online; diagnostics for exemplar experiments are
expected to be ready around 2035-2037, once EYC achieves repeatable, robust
burning-plasma conditions. There is flexibility in this schedule if exemplar
experiments need to begin earlier, which could be accomplished by rebalancing
priorities in FY31 and beyond and starting exemplar design work sooner.

Introduction: Scientific advances in High Energy Density (HED) physics are realized with
an innovative research and development program of visible, ultraviolet, x-ray, gamma ray,

constructive collaboration between diagnostic groups at the major HED facilities:

for the National Diagnostics Working Group, of which LLE is an active participant.

4pi Areal Density measurement on OMEGA
This project is for the precursor for designing and building a third highly-collimated line-
of-sight (LOS) for areal density measurements of the compressed shell and DD and DT
neutron yields and ion temperatures of the hot spot of OMEGA implosions. The addition
of a third LOS is required with the currently installed spectrometers on OMEGA is
crucial to achieve a full 4 pi averaged areal density used in the modified Lawson criteria.
To achieve full reconstruction without an assumption on the direction of the low mode
(i.e., l = 1), up to four spectrometers are required to measure the scattered neutron
spectra. Therefore, with four strategically located and highly collimated lines of sight, a
reconstruction of the areal-density distribution over 4π can be inferred without an
assumption on the direction of the low mode. All available ports on OMEGA were
considered, including H12, H13, H17, P2, P8, and P10. Only H12 and P2 are within the

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 73

defined region, team proceed with scoping theses locations. At 16 – 22 m along H12, the
neutron path flows through the OMEGA stage-F alignment sensor package (FASP) and
then into the OMEGA capacitor bay with the power conditioning units (PCUs). With
these interferences, placing the new LOS along H12 was deemed not a viable location.
For the new LOS along P2, the detector would be placed directly above the OMEGA
Viewing Gallery. The team has identified a way to fit both the detectors and collimator in
this area, reinforcements to the viewing gallery structure would be needed to
accommodate the equipment weight. The challenge along P2 is that the distance
requirement places the detector within the OMEGA Target Bay, a high neutron
environment that would cause insufficient signal to background. Significant shielding
would be needed to meet physics requirements.

UV-Raman Spectroscopy
A single-shot, nanosecond-scale Raman spectrometer is being developed for the OMEGA
Laser System at the Laboratory for Laser Energetics. Raman scattering is an incredibly
weak inelastic scattering process – about 1 in 10 million photons will Raman scatter.
Spectrally resolving Raman scattered light provides detailed information on the structure,
bond character, and temperature of materials without relying on that material’s ability to
scatter x-rays. This technique is routinely implemented over longer time scales; however,
the short time scales of laser-driven experiments make diagnostic design for a Raman
spectrometer on a laser facility a challenge. Laser-compressed materials are probed with
1-10 mJ of a 5-ns, 266-nm laser, taking advantage of the 1/λ4 factor in Raman intensity to
produce enough signal for a measurement. The resulting Raman scattered light is
spectrally resolved with 0.2 nm of spectral resolution over a 10 nm window. The

implementation of Raman spectroscopy on a large-scale laser facility fills a critical gap in
measurement capabilities at high energy-density conditions.

Time-resolved Diffraction
The first time-resolved diffraction measurements on OMEGA EP were made during the
TRXRDEP-23A campaign. Principal Investigators D. Polsin, LLE, and L. R. Benedetti,
LLNL, added an x-ray framing camera to the powder x-ray diffraction image-plate
diagnostic (PXRDIP) and recorded the body-centered cubic to hexagonal close-packed
(bcc-to-hcp) phase transition in shocked iron. The time-resolved x-ray diffraction (XRD)
measurements utilizing a 6.7-keV, 1.25-ns x-ray backlighter captured the unshocked
(pure bcc), shock transit (mixed bcc and hcp phase), and shock breakout on a shot-by-
shot basis [53].
The figure shows the XRD data captured on the four-strip x-ray framing camera
surrounded by image plates. The framing camera strips (each with a temporal resolution
of 400 ps) are in the center of each image and spatially aligned with the time-integrated
image-plate data. The growth of the high-pressure hcp phase of iron out of the ambient
bcc phase can be seen from left to right as the shock enters the iron (left-hand panel) and
at shock breakout (right-hand panel). Acquiring high-quality and high-temporal–
resolution data required mitigating sources of x-ray fluorescence in the x-ray framing
camera and blocking direct lines of sight from the x-ray backlighter to the detector. This
campaign represents a significant milestone in the development of an experimental
platform to collect time-resolved x-ray diffraction measurements on a single laser shot.

X-ray Hot Spot Imaging
 A deep-learning convolutional neural network (CNN) has been used to infer the low-
mode shape of the hot-spot emission of deuterium–tritium (DT) laser-direct-drive
cryogenic implosions on OMEGA [54,55]. The CNN was trained on a 3D radiation-

stagnation. The CNN accounts for the lack of an absolute spatial reference and the
different bands of photon energies in the x-ray images. Previous work [26] has studied

implosion performance. A current analysis of 19 DT cryogenic implosions indicates there

Nuclear Reaction Product Detection

 The nuclear science community has an increased interest in the structure of light
nuclei and their interactions at energies approaching 1 MeV. Many of these reactions

LLNL-TR-xxxxxxx 77

have been previously measured in this energy range using accelerators; however,
experimental results disagree and have large uncertainties. In addition, several of these
product half-lives are too short (100-ms to 10 s) for removing samples and conducting
activation measurements. Therefore, a technique using target normal sheath acceleration
(TNSA) was developed on the Multi-Terawatt (MTW) laser to measure light-ion
reactions at MeV energies that allows access to these short-lived half-lives. A successful
measurement from a joint MTW experimental campaign between SUNY Geneseo,
Houghton University, and LLE took place using the Houghton–Geneseo short-lived
isotope counting system and the Geneseo particle time-of-flight detector. This Phoswich
detector is designed to detect low-intensity, low-energy beta particles efficiently in a
higher-energy ambient background. For this design, there is a combination of scintillators
with dissimilar pulse shape characteristics optically coupled to each other and to a
common photomultiplier tube(s). Pulse-shape analysis distinguishes the signals from the
two scintillators, identifying in which scintillator the event occurred. The reaction was
used to show that the proof-of-principle was 7Li(d,p)8Li, for which the 8Li product has a
half-life of 840 ms that was measured to well within the accepted uncertainty from past
measurements. The nextstep in development is to field this detector on OMEGA to diagnose
the 7Li(t,α)6He* reaction.

Upgrade DISC: The existing DIM Insertable Streak Camera (DISC) will be upgraded
with improved electron optics to improve the spatial resolution over the whole 24mm
active area of the cathode. The microchannel plate fiber optic detector will also be
replaced with a direct electron detection CMOS detector.
CBI: Crystal Backlit Imaging; this diagnostic uses spherical Bragg crystals to provide
narrow band (dE/E < 0.5%) high resolution imaging (<10 micron) at discrete photon
energies (6keV – 16keV) for ICF and HED applications.
HGXD-Rad Hard Camera: Replace the optical film that is used in the current Hardened
Gated X-ray framing camera Diagnostic (HGXD) with radiation hardened CMOS sensors

Z_VISAR: This is 1-D imaging open-beam VISAR system developed in collaboration
between SNL and LLNL for use on Z. The primary application is measuring current
delivery to MDD-ICF targets.
Prec.nToF/Quartz Detector: Precision Neutron time of Flight diagnostic will replace
existing Bibenzyl scintillator as the neutron detector with device using Cherenkov
emission in quartz crystals. This fast optical signal reduces the instrument response
function (IRF) from its current many nanoseconds to ~350 of pico-seconds. This
effectively reduces the DT Tion systematic uncertainty due to the IRF to around 50 eV.
Further, since the crystal is also sensitive to gammas produced at bangtime, the detector
will also improve the accuracy of fluid velocity measurements by through direct
measurement of the neutron-gamma flight time difference along a given flight path.
RTNADS: The real time Nuclear Activation Diagnostic uses in situ Zirconium activation
coupled to photomultipliers to provide a real time spatial measurement of the neutron
flux at the NIF target chamber after a high yield shot (>5e14 neutrons). This diagnostic

will provide a spatial map of relative un-scattered neutron flux at least 24 locations
around the NIF target chamber. This data is designed to be used to infer the uniformity of
the compressed DT fuel in an ICF implosion.
Contaminated Control VISAR: This diagnostic modifies the VISAR debris shield so
that VISAR can be used whenever NIF is used to study hazardous material such as “high
Z”.
SPBT upgrade: South-Pole Bang-Time will be upgraded to provide new HAPG crystals
and Indium Phosphide detectors that will characterize the time of peak x-ray capsule
emission at both 15 and 30keV. This will be used to obtain a slope temperature
measurement at x-ray energies that are transparent to the imploded shell in high
convergence implosions.
Crystal calibration facility: X-ray calibration station inside NIF facility, to measure
absolute sensitivity and sensitivity vs x-ray energy of NIF spectrometer snouts in the
geometry in which they are used and to track performance vs time.
U.V. Interferometry-polarimetry: Optical diagnostic for measure density profiles and
magnetic fields in low density plasmas, relevant for discovery science and hohlraum
science.
EXAFS spectrometer: High resolution spectrometer for measuring Absorption Fine
Structure near absorption edges. This is a sensitive temperature diagnostic in shock
heated plasmas. This design allows crystals to be changed to look at the different
absorption edges.
DIXI Polar: “Dilation Imager for X-rays at Ignition”. A DIXI high temporal resolution
x-ray imager will be mounted on the Polar DIM of the NIF target chamber. This
diagnostic utilizes a time dilation drift tube to obtain x-ray images of high yield
implosions from the pole with a time resolution better than 10 ps. This kind of time
