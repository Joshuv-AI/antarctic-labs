Psychosocial factors influencing HPV vaccination intention among male college students in Hangzhou city: A cross-sectional study based on an extended theory of planned behavior model
Authors: Zhengkai Gan, Genqiang Mo, Peng Fei Yu, Zhewei Feng, Xiejun Gu, Hui Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination for males is critical for reducing the global burden of HPV-related diseases. This study utilized an extended Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) framework to identify determinants of vaccination intentions among Chinese male undergraduate students. A cross-sectional study was conducted between March and May 2025 in Hangzhou, China, recruiting 1,310 participants via multi-stage stratified cluster sampling. The dependent variable was vaccination intention. Independent variables included sociodemographics, TPB constructs (behavior attitude [BA], subjective norms [SN], and perceived behavioral control [PBC]), HPV knowledge and risk perception. We employed a three-step hierarchical linear model (Model 1: sociodemographics; Model 2: adding TPB constructs; Model 3: adding HPV knowledge/risk) and structural equation modeling (SEM) to test latent paths. Among the 1,310 participants (balanced across four academic years), 62.7% expressed a willingness to receive the HPV vaccine. Hierarchical regression revealed that sociodemographics explained 2.1% of the variance in vaccination intention (Model 1), which increased to 25.1% with the inclusion of TPB constructs (Model 2), and reached 32.7% in the final model (Model 3) (F = 126.245, p < .001). SEM analysis further validated the model (χ2/df = 2.102, RMSEA = 0.068, GFI = 0.968), accounting for 75.9% of the total variance in behavioral intention. PBC was identified as the most powerful predictor (β = 0.527, p < .001), followed by BA, SN and HPV-related knowledge. Additionally, higher self-assessed risk of infection significantly predicted stronger intentions. The results of this study indicated that increasing vaccination rates requires policy interventions to improve vaccine accessibility and affordability, alongside fostering positive attitudes and social support through targeted health education.KEYWORDS: Human papillomavirus (HPV), vaccine intention, Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), male college students, China

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection is one of the most common sexually transmitted diseases worldwide,1 and the average lifetime probability of acquiring HPV to be 84.6% for female and 91.3% for male.2 The high-risk HPV persistent infections not only can lead to cervical cancer but also account for 88% of anal cancer, 31% of oropharyngeal cancer, 78% of vaginal cancer, 25% of vulvar cancer, and 50% of penile cancer worldwide.3 According to the Global Cancer Statistics 2022 (GLOBOCAN), approximately 662,000 new cases of cervical cancer were diagnosed globally in 2022, with China accounting for roughly 150,600 of these cases.4 Regarding males, HPV-related cancers represent a substantial burden; global estimates suggest approximately 95,000 new cases annually, primarily comprising anal cancer (24,000 cases), penile cancer (19,200 cases), and HPV-driven oropharyngeal cancer (43,800 cases).5 Moreover, male HPV infections often present no obvious clinical symptoms6 and can transmit the HPV virus to female through sexual activity, making it easier for female to experience persistent HPV infections, amplifying the overall disease burden.7,8 Substantial evidence supports HPV vaccination as an effective measure of preventing HPV infection and related cancers,9 with gender-neutral programs proving more cost-effective than female-only strategies.10 Vaccinating males not only reduces their risk of HPV infection and related diseases but also protects female partners by curbing HPV transmission, thereby assisting in the prevention of cervical cancer. As of February 2025, globally, 148(76%) of the 194 World Health Organization (WHO) member states had introduced the HPV vaccine into their national immunization programs, and 76 countries had implemented vaccination programs for both sexes.11 In 2025, China approved the quadrivalent HPV vaccine and the nine-valent HPV vaccine (imported) for vaccination of males aged 9–2612 and 16–2613 respectively. Furthermore, the Chinese government included the vaccination of domestic bivalent HPV vaccines for females aged 13 and above in the national immunization program,14 marking a new stage of “co-prevention of HPV-related cancers and diseases for both men and women” in China.

Male college students represent a sexually active young group whose continual exposure to HPV is driven by a combination of high-risk sexual behaviors. Recent surveys in China indicate that 37.3% of sexually active male students reported having multiple sexual partners within the previous 6 months.15 Furthermore, consistent condom use in this population remains suboptimal, with reported rates of 46.9% for heterosexual encounters and 36.4% for homosexual encounters.16 Such behaviors, combined with limited knowledge about HPV,17 contribute to higher rates of new HPV infections in this demographic,18 making the promotion of HPV vaccination among male college students particularly urgent. In high-income developed countries, the first-dose HPV vaccination coverage for men in the target age group was higher than 57%, and the full-dose vaccination coverage was higher than 46%.11 However, the HPV vaccination rate among male population in China is almost negligible. Existing studies conducted in China Guangdong and Niaxia have indicated that the willingness rate of male college students to receive the HPV vaccine ranges from 59.4% to 80.3%.19,20 Influencing factors are diverse, including socioeconomic and demographic characteristics, psychological and cognitive factors, knowledge about the HPV vaccine, sexual history and behaviors, perceived vaccine cost and effectiveness, and governmental policies or recommendations.17,21,22 Despite these preliminary findings, research specifically focusing on the HPV vaccination willingness and underlying psychological motivations among male college students is notably lacking in Zhejiang province, which is an economically developed coastal region in eastern China and has been a pioneer in HPV prevention policies, including implementing a free vaccination program for eligible girls. With the approval of HPV vaccines for the male population in China, it is essential to conduct research on vaccination willingness and its related influencing factors to promote vaccination.

Health behavior change is a complex process driven by multiple psychological and social factors.23 To deeply analyze the determinants of HPV vaccination willingness among male university students, our study employs the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) as its theoretical framework. The TPB is a well-established social psychological model24,25 for predicting and explaining health-related behavior, which posits that an individual’s behavioral intention is the most direct determinant of action, and that intention is influenced by three factors: behavior attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control. The TPB has been well applied in examining HPV vaccine intention and uptake across a range of populations, including studies carried out in North America and Europe.26–28 In one of the studies that tested the TPB in a population of U.S. college men (aged 18–25 y), 58% of variance in intention was accounted for.29 In another study conducted in southeastern United States among college students (aged 18–25 y, both sexes), the regression showed that behavior attitude and subjective norms were significant in predicting intention.30 In a study that aimed to examine HPV vaccine intention among U.S. male college students (aged 18–26 y), the authors observed that intention was predicted by behavior attitudes toward getting HPV vaccinated, and subjective norms.26 In another U.S.-based study involving male college students (aged 18–24 y), subjective norms and PBC were predictors of intention among college students.31 Furthermore, evidence from international studies, particularly those in Western settings32 and Asian countries,33 indicates that the knowledge of HPV and HPV vaccine appears to be a lingering low level among college students, which is an individual-level and modifiable factor associated with HPV vaccination intentions.34 Therefore, this study included knowledge of HPV and HPV vaccine as an additional construct, extending the TPB.

In summary, this study aims to investigate the social and psychological determinants of HPV vaccination intention among male university students in Hangzhou city. The structural equation modeling (SEM) is a powerful statistical tool that can simultaneously test multiple relationships and account for measurement error, which is ideal for examining the complex pathways proposed by the expanded theory of planned behavior (ETPB). By employing the ETPB and SEM, we will identify critical factors influencing their willingness. The findings are intended to provide insights for developing targeted interventions to improve HPV vaccination rates among college students, ultimately contributing to the HPV prevention framework.

Methods

Study design and participants recruitment procedure

A cross-sectional study design was employed in this study, which was conducted between March and May 2025, employing a multi-stage stratified cluster sampling method. First, the 10 administrative districts of Hangzhou were stratified into three tiers (high, medium, and low) based on their 2024 per capita GDP. The criteria for these tiers were defined as follows: high (per capita GDP > ¥200,000, approx. $28,090), medium (¥150,000–¥200,000, approx. $21,067–$28,090), and low (per capita GDP < ¥150,000, approx. $21,067). For context, China’s national per capita GDP in the same year was approximately ¥95,700 ($13,441). One district was randomly selected from each tier to ensure socioeconomic diversity: Binjiang District (high tier), Qiantang District (medium tier), and Lin’an District (low tier). Subsequently, one public university was selected from each respective district to represent the local student population: Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (in Binjiang District), Hangzhou Normal University (in Qiantang District), and Hangzhou Medical College (in Lin’an District). While medical programs at Zhejiang Chinese Medical University and Hangzhou Medical College typically require 5 y of study, the majority of undergraduate programs at all three institutions follow a standard four-year curriculum. To maintain sample comparability, we included students from the first to the fourth year of study across all disciplines and institutions. These institutions were selected for their comparable administrative structures and student profiles, which helps minimize potential institutional-level confounding effects while providing a comprehensive reflection of the student population across different economic settings within the city. We calculated the sample size estimation for the cross-sectional survey using the following formula:

Zmin = Z1−α/22×P×1−pd2

where d represents the permissible error, set at 0.03 based on expert experience; α is the type I error, set at .05 as per standard practice; and P is the Vaccination Willingness Rate Among Male College Students in Mainland China, which is 47.0% according to Zhang et al.35 The estimated minimum sample size was 1,064 individuals. After accounting for factors such as the rate of lost visits and refusal of visits, an additional 15% was added to the estimated sample size, and the final sample size was around 1,224 individuals (rounded to 1,200 for practical recruitment purposes). Finally, we recruited a sample of 400 male undergraduate students from each university. The sample included at least 100 students from each academic year (covering the first to the fourth year of study). Although some programs last longer than 4 y, our survey focused on the first to fourth-year undergraduate students, which represent the standard curriculum duration across the selected universities. Several classes were randomly selected from each academic year, and all male university students in these classes were included in the study. The inclusion criteria for participants were (1) full-time male undergraduate student and (2) voluntary participate in the questionnaire survey. The exclusion criteria were (1) declining to participate, and (2) having received the HPV vaccine before the start of the study. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (Approval No. 202453).

The questionnaire link was generated via the Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn/)36 platform. Dissemination did not rely on open-access social media. Instead, the link was sent privately to the class teachers of the randomly selected classes. These teachers distributed the link exclusively within their class WeChat groups, and all male students in the class were invited to participate in the survey. A Quick Respond (QR) code linked to the Wenjuanxing survey was embedded within the announcement. Participants accessed the questionnaire by scanning a QR code with their mobile devices (e.g., smartphones, tablets, or laptops). An informed consent form containing a description of the survey’s purpose, voluntary nature, and confidentiality of responses was presented before the full questionnaire, and only the participants who provided electronic consent that they accessed and proceeded to complete the full questionnaire. To ensure data quality and provide immediate support, a standardized online administration procedure was implemented. For each participating university, a member of the research team hosted a live online information session within the class WeChat group. During this session, the researcher outlined the study’s objectives, confirmed the voluntary nature of participation, and ensured anonymity and the right to withdraw. Immediately following the briefing, the survey link was distributed within the same WeChat group, and the research team member remained active for a 60-minute response window to address participant queries in real-time. The information session and data collection were conducted in a single sitting for each class group. Participants were allowed up to 60 minutes to access and complete the questionnaire.

Measures

The survey instrument used in this study was a self-designed scale, developed through an extensive literature review29,37,38 and expert consultation, and grounded in the theoretical framework of the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). To ensure content validity, a preliminary version was reviewed by a panel of three experts in epidemiology, immunology, and health education. The questionnaire was subsequently pilot-tested among 15 students selected through convenience sampling from Zhejiang Chinese Medical University, who represented the target population of the main study. Their feedback was used solely to refine wording for clarity and readability. The data collected during this pilot phase were excluded from the final dataset to avoid contamination and ensure the independence of the primary analysis. The final structured questionnaire elicited participants’ sociodemographic characteristics – including academic grade, parental education level, monthly expenses, sexual orientation, and sexual history – alongside a self-assessment of perceived HPV susceptibility. This study utilized an extended TPB model, which posits that behavioral intention is determined by behavior attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control; notably, HPV-related knowledge (HPVK) was incorporated as an independent variable, given its documented direct influence on health behaviors within an extended TPB model.37 Therefore, we propose:

The behavior attitudes exert a positive influence on male college students’ intention to receive the HPV vaccine.

The subjective norms positively impact male college students’ inclination to receive the HPV vaccination.

The perceived behavioral control has a positive effect on male college students’ HPV vaccination intention.

The knowledge of HPV and HPV vaccine positively affects male college students’ intention to get HPV vaccination.

Behavior attitude

Behavior attitude is defined as an individual’s favorable or unfavorable evaluation of a particular behavior.24 In this study, this construct was measured by four items intended to gauge the perspectives of male college students regarding HPV vaccination (labeled BA1–BA4). The items included: BA1: “I believe getting the HPV vaccine can prevent HPV infection-related diseases such as male genital warts”; BA2: “Getting the HPV vaccine is a correct and wise choice”; BA3: “It is better to get the HPV vaccine earlier than later”; BA4: “Getting the HPV vaccine is beneficial for the health of my own or others.” Data were collected using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), with participants rating items based on their individual perspectives. The total score for this construct was calculated as the sum of these four items, with higher scores indicating a more positive attitude toward HPV vaccination

Subjective norm

The subjective norm is defined as an individual’s belief regarding the extent of support or opposition from important others for performing a specific behavior.24 The Subjective Norm Scale consists of four items, including: SN1: “My classmates, friends and partners suggest me getting the HPV vaccine”; SN2: “My parents and relatives support me getting the HPV vaccine”; SN3: “I would get the HPV vaccine because the medical staff advised me to get it”; SN4: “I would get the HPV vaccine because the government department or social media encourage HPV vaccination.” All items were evaluated on a 5-point Likert scale, scored from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5). The total score was calculated, where a higher score reflects a stronger perceived social norm in favor of HPV vaccination.

Perceived behavioral control

Perceived Behavioral Control (PBC) refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to perform a specific behavior.24 In this study, the PBC scale assessed the confidence of male college students regarding their capacity to make autonomous decisions about receiving the HPV vaccine. The scale consists of four items, such as: PBC1: “I can decide for myself whether to get the HPV vaccine”; PBC2: “The financial capacity of my family can afford the cost of my HPV vaccination”; PBC3: “I have sufficient time and energy to support my HPV vaccination”; and PBC4: “It is quite convenient to make an appointment and get the HPV vaccine.” Participants provided responses using a 5-point Likert scale, where (1) indicates strong disagreement and (5) indicates strong agreement. A total score was calculated for this construct, with a higher score indicating greater perceived control over the vaccination process.

The knowledge of HPV and HPV vaccine

Research indicates a positive association between an individual’s knowledge concerning HPV and their vaccination status.34,37 In the present study, the HPV-related knowledge of male college students was assessed using a nine-item scale, which asked were centered on the pathogen characteristics, transmission, clinical manifestation, infection outcome, infected population, prevention and treatment of HPV as well as the best time for HPV vaccination and the number of doses for male. Knowledge was graded on 9 points (a point for each question). For this study, a score of six and above was considered as good knowledge and below as poor knowledge. A total score was calculated for each participant, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge.

Behavior intention

Behavioral Intention (BI) refers to the readiness of male college students to receive the HPV vaccine in the future. This construct was measured using three 5-point Likert scale items: BI1: “I would consider getting the HPV vaccine”; BI2: “I shall endeavor to get the HPV vaccine”; BI3: “I am willing to invest time and money in getting the HPV vaccine.” A total score was calculated for each participant, with higher scores indicating higher intention to vaccinate.

Data quality control

To ensure the validity and reliability of the data, a rigorous data cleaning process was implemented. Questionnaires were excluded from the final analysis if they met any of the following criteria: (1) completion time was less than 2 minutes (suggesting insufficient engagement); (2) participants did not meet the inclusion criteria (e.g., not currently enrolled as male undergraduates); or (3) participants exhibited highly patterned responses (e.g., selecting the same option for all items across the questionnaire).

Data analyses

Statistical analyses were performed utilizing IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0) and IBM SPSS AMOS (version 26.0). To evaluate the study hypotheses, a two-phase analytical approach was applied.35 First, participant sociodemographic profiles were summarized using frequency distributions and descriptive statistics in SPSS. Next, a three-step hierarchical multiple regression analysis was executed to determine how predictor variables affect intention. Step one entered demographic characteristics to control for baseline individual variations. Step two introduced the core Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) components: behavior attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control (PBC). Step three added self-assessed HPV risk perception, as well as HPV and HPV vaccine-related knowledge, into the model to gauge their additional predictive value. This sequential modeling clarified the unique predictive weight of each variable group while controlling for previous factors, and calculated the incremental R-squared variance explained by the newly integrated constructs.

Furthermore, structural equation modeling (SEM) was executed with AMOS 26.0 for a comprehensive evaluation of the hypotheses. This method accounted for inter-variable relationships, simultaneously assessing the theoretical model’s overall fit alongside the direct impacts of behavior attitude, subjective norm, PBC, and HPV-related knowledge on vaccination intention. Before running the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), the measurement model’s internal consistency was confirmed by calculating Cronbach’s alpha coefficients. Subsequently, CFA was applied to establish the validity and reliability of all measured constructs. To optimize the measurement model, items with factor loadings below 0.5 were excluded. Convergent validity was determined using the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) alongside Composite Reliability (CR). Discriminant validity was assessed using the Fornell-Larcker criterion, where the square root of the AVE for each construct was compared with its correlation coefficients with other latent variables. Finally, the robustness of the analytical model was validated employing several goodness-of-fit indicators, specifically the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), and Adjusted Goodness of Fit Index (AGFI). To examine the underlying mechanism, we conducted a mediation analysis using bootstrapping (5,000 resamples) to estimate the indirect effects of HPV-related knowledge on intention through BA, SN, and PBC, with the test level set at .05.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

From March to May 2025, a total of 1,380 questionnaires were collected. Following the data quality control procedures described in the methods section, 1310 (94.93%) were included in the analysis. Specifically, 70 questionnaires were excluded for the following reasons: (1) 24 questionnaires had a completion time of less than 2 minutes; (2) 27 participants did not meet the inclusion criteria (e.g., not currently enrolled as male undergraduates); and (3) 19 questionnaires were excluded due to highly patterned responses. The characteristics of the participants are summarized in (Table 1), categorized by sociodemographic background, sexual history, HPV risk perception, and knowledge of HPV and the HPV vaccine. Regarding sociodemographic background, respondents were relatively evenly distributed among freshman (25.3%), sophomore (27.0%), junior (23.6%), and senior (24.1%). Most participants’ parents had an education level of senior high school (31.7%) or university diploma and above (30.9%). Over half of the students reported monthly living expenses of 1000–1999 yuan (57.3%). Regarding sexual history, 13.9% of the participants reported having a sexual history, and 89.8% identified as heterosexual. In terms of risk perception, a high proportion of students (89.9%) believe that they are less likely to be infected with HPV. Finally, concerning knowledge of HPV and the HPV vaccine, 89.0% of respondents had heard of HPV, 86.2% had heard of the HPV vaccine, and only 58.6% of respondents scored 6 or above on the knowledge assessment. Among 1,310 participants, 821 (62.7%) were willing to be vaccinated against HPV.

Descriptive statistics of TPB constructs and HPV knowledge

The responses to the 9 knowledge items and the TPB constructs were summarized in Table S1 (Table S1 in the Supplementary Material). Overall, the participants demonstrated moderate knowledge regarding HPV and HPV vaccine, with the correct rate for individual items ranging from 18.32% to 86.18%. Participants demonstrated the highest level of awareness regarding the non-gender-specific nature of HPV infection (Q3, 86.18%) and methods for preventing HPV infection (Q9, 82.82%). Conversely, knowledge gaps were most evident concerning the required doses of the vaccine for males (Q6, 18.32%) and the optimal time for vaccination (Q5, 45.11%). Regarding the TPB constructs, the mean scores for BA, SN, PBC, and BI were 18.66 (SD = 2.43), 17.64 (SD = 3.27), 16.77 (SD = 3.46), and 13.35 (SD = 2.24), respectively.

Hierarchical regression analysis of factors influencing the willingness to receive HPV vaccine

We used hierarchical regression analysis to determine which factors influenced this willingness (Table 2). In Model 1, demographic variables were incorporated, which collectively explained 2.10% of the variance in vaccination willingness. The results revealed that having heard of the HPV vaccine was a significant predictor of vaccination willingness (β = 0.155, p < .01). However, having heard of HPV itself did not show a statistically significant association with vaccination willingness (β = −0.097, p > .05). Other demographic factors, such as academic grade, parental education level, monthly living expenses, knowledge of HPV itself, sexual orientation, and sexual history, showed no significant association. In Model 2, we added the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) constructs: behavior attitude, perceived behavioral control, and subjective norms. The model’s explanatory power increased substantially, accounting for 25.10% of the variance in vaccination willingness (F = 123.103, p < .001). Regarding the factors influencing willingness, all three TPB constructs were significant positive predictors: perceived behavioral control emerged was the strongest predictor of vaccination willingness (β = 0.273, p < .001), followed by subjective norms (β = 0.196, p < .001), and behavior attitude (β = 0.139, p < .001). Meanwhile, “having heard of the HPV vaccine,” which was significant in Model 1, remained significant in Model 2, while other demographic variables did not show significant associations. In Model 3, we further added HPV-related health knowledge and awareness. This final model confirmed the significant associations between these factors and vaccination willingness. Perceived behavioral control had the strongest effect (β = 0.253, p < .001), followed by subjective norms (β = 0.193, p < .001), HPV-related knowledge (β = 0.113, p < .001), and behavior attitude (β = 0.103, p < .001). Additionally, students who perceived a higher personal risk of HPV infection were more inclined to be vaccinated (β = 0.108, p < .001). The final model explained 32.70% of the variance in vaccination intentions (F = 126.245, p < .001), indicating that the decision-making process is multifaceted.

Reliability and validity of the measurement model

Factor loadings for each item and modification indices were systematically evaluated for the entire scale. To optimize the measurement model and improve construct validity, items with loadings below the critical threshold of 0.5 were removed (specifically, items HPVK1–6, HPVK9). A comprehensive reliability and validity analysis was then conducted on the finalized questionnaire, with findings detailed in (Table 3). Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for behavior attitude, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, knowledge related to HPV and HPV vaccine and behavior intention were 0.939, 0.932, 0.899, 0.783, and 0.918, respectively. All values of Cronbach’s α were close to or exceeded 0.80, confirming that each item met satisfactory levels of reliability. Additionally, convergent validity was determined by reviewing the average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) for each construct. In our study, all values of AVE and CR were greater than 0.664 and 0.796, respectively, thus providing evidence that the convergent validity is solid. In terms of discriminant validity, which is acceptable when the square roots of the AVE values for each construct were greater than the correlations among the latent variables. As showing in diagonal in (Table 4), the square roots of the AVE values were all greater than the related correlation coefficients, confirming discriminant validity. Collectively, these psychometric assessments demonstrate that the instrument exhibits high internal consistency, solid convergent validity, and adequate discriminant validity. The findings confirm that the measurement model is robust and reliable, providing a sound basis for the subsequent structural equation modeling analysis.

Structural equation model fitting and analysis

We developed a structural equation model to test the relationships between 4 latent and 17 observed variables influencing HPV vaccination intention among male college students and tested the fit of the structural model before the path analysis. The results demonstrated that the overall fit indices of the measurement model were satisfactory, with the following values recorded: χ2 = 353.196, χ2/df = 2.102, RMSEA = 0.068 (90% CI: 0.025–0.047), GFI = 0.968, AGFI = 0.953, CFI = 0.909, and NFI = 0.911. Collectively, these indices provide strong support for our proposed structural model.

The SEM results of our model are depicted in (Figure 1), with all the paths and relations were significant and supporting all research hypotheses. The standardized coefficient for the BA-BI path (H1) was 0.28, with a t-value of 2.60 (p < .001), indicating that stronger attitudes correlate with a greater intention to vaccinate against HPV. Regarding the HPVK-BI path (H2), the standardized coefficient was 0.28, with a t-value of 2.60 (p < .001), suggesting that higher knowledge of HPV and HPV vaccine is associated with a stronger intention to vaccinate against HPV. The SN-BI path (H3) had a standardized coefficient of 0.22 and a t-value of 2.47 (p < .001), indicating that individuals experiencing more social pressure and stronger subjective norms are more inclined to vaccinate against HPV. The PBC-BI path (H4) had a standardized coefficient of 0.43 and a t-value of 6.22 (p < .001), demonstrating that individuals with greater behavioral control have a higher intention to accept HPV vaccination. Consequently, individuals with positive attitudes, extensive knowledge, strong subjective norms, and high behavioral control are more likely to intend to vaccinate. The model explained 75.9% of the variance in behavioral intention to vaccinate against HPV.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the factors influencing HPV vaccination intention among male university students, utilizing an extended Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) model. A significant gap was observed between high HPV awareness and insufficient in-depth knowledge, accompanied by a marked optimistic bias regarding personal infection risk. Demographic variables (e.g., academic grade, parents’ education, monthly expenses) did not significantly predict intention after accounting for psychosocial factors. Our structural equation model revealed that perceived behavioral control (PBC) was the most powerful predictor (β = 0.527, p < .001), followed by behavior attitude (β = 0.330, p < .001) and subjective norms (β = 0.132, p < .001) were significant determinants. Fourth, HPV-related knowledge, when incorporated into the extended TPB model, showed a significant but modest effect on intention (β = 0.039, p < .001). The integrated model successfully explained 75.9% of the variance in vaccination intention, underscoring the multifaceted nature of this health decision.

Our data reveal a significant gap between awareness and good knowledge. While a high percentage of participants had heard of HPV (89.01%) and the HPV vaccine (86.18%), only 58.63% achieved a passing score on the knowledge assessment. This suggests that although the awareness of HPV and the HPV vaccine was relatively high among male college students, their knowledge about the symptoms, available HPV treatments, consequences, risk factors, and the HPV vaccine mechanism was still insufficient. This is also consistent with previous literature stating male college students have high levels of HPV awareness and moderate levels of HPV knowledge.17,39,40 The knowledge gap observed in our study was likely attributable to China’s initial HPV vaccination policies (2016–2024), which primarily focused on female populations due to limited vaccine supply and a focus on cervical cancer prevention.41,42 Specifically, our results showed that while general awareness (e.g., transmission methods) was high (86.18%), students struggled significantly with logistical details, such as the required vaccine doses for males (correct rate 18.32%) and optimal vaccination timing (correct rate 45.11%). This disparity suggests that current public messaging has successfully reached students but failed to deliver actionable information. Therefore, future health education sessions on campus should move beyond simple awareness-raising and focus on “health literacy empowerment,” specifically by providing clear, accessible information regarding the HPV vaccine schedule, cost, and access points to bridge the gap between knowing about HPV and knowing how to prevent it. Furthermore, a striking 89.92% of students perceived their personal risk of HPV infection as low. This optimistic bias was a well-documented barrier to vaccination willingness or behaviors43,44 and, in this context, likely suppresses the perceived urgency of HPV vaccination.45 Our regression analysis corroborates this, showing that a higher self-assessed likelihood of infection was a significant predictor of vaccination willingness (β = 0.108, p < .001), in line with other studies finding that individuals who perceive a higher risk of HPV infection and a higher severity of HPV-related cancers were more likely to receive the HPV vaccine.46,47 The novelty of this finding lies in its practical implication: interventions targeting male university students in China must go beyond attitude change and focus on reducing structural barriers, such as integrating the HPV vaccine into insurance schemes and establishing on-campus vaccination programs.

Interestingly, most traditional demographic variables, such as academic grade, parents’ education level, and monthly living expenses, did not show a significant direct effect on vaccination willingness in our regression models once psychosocial factors were included. The findings stand in contrast to previous studies conducted in China. Specifically, Li et al.46 surveyed college students in Beijing, finding that sexual activity and higher monthly expenses were significant predictors of vaccination willingness. Similarly, Jia et al.48 conducted an online study among male college students in some parts of China, identifying that being in a romantic relationship significantly increased vaccination willingness. The discrepancy between our findings and these studies may be attributed to the specific demographic and geographical contexts of our sample. Despite these variations, the novelty of our finding lies in its theoretical implication: it suggests that psychosocial constructs, as defined by the TPB, are more proximal and powerful determinants of vaccination intention than static demographic characteristics. The decision to vaccinate appears to be less about who the student is and more about how they think, feel, and perceive their social and logistical environment regarding the HPV vaccine. It underscores the need for future studies to explore the multifaceted influences on health behavior intentions within varied demographic contexts.

Notably, our results showed that the perceived behavioral control (PBC) as the strongest predictor of HPV vaccination intention among male university students, which is similar to those of research carried out in China,49–51 but different from the report of western countries, where subjective norms or attitudes often exerted a stronger influence.26,29,38 This discrepancy likely stems from the fact that in many Western countries, HPV vaccination for males is often integrated into national immunization programs or school-based vaccination initiatives, which significantly lowers both financial and logistical barriers. PBC is determined by both internal factors, such as self-efficacy and personal competencies,52 and external factors, including access to resources, opportunities, information, and the affordability of the behavior. The perception of greater resources and fewer barriers enhances individual’ perceived behavioral control, which in turn enhances their intention to receive HPV vaccination. For male university students in China, this may point toward perceived barriers such as the cost of the HPV vaccine (the cost for the three-dose vaccination series of the quadrivalent or nine-valent HPV vaccine is approximately 2,400 yuan and 4,000 yuan, respectively, which is not covered by medical insurance and requires individuals to pay out of pocket), the inconvenience of the multi-dose schedule, or a lack of clear information on where and how to get vaccinated. These external barriers likely amplify the role of PBC relative to other factors in this population. The novelty of this finding lies in its practical implication: interventions targeting male university students in China must go beyond attitude change and focus on reducing structural barriers, such as integrating the HPV vaccine into insurance schemes and establishing on-campus vaccination programs.

The urgency of addressing these barriers is further highlighted by the contrast with current female-focused vaccination strategies in China. Recently, China has included HPV vaccination for school-aged girls in its national immunization program,14 and several regions have subsidized HPV vaccination specifically for school-aged girls. These strategies have significantly improved accessibility and uptake among females by removing financial and logistical hurdles. Conversely, these strategies have not been systematically extended to the male population. This disparity stems primarily from two factors: first, national public health priorities in China remain focused on cervical cancer prevention in females, and thus HPV vaccination for males has yet to be formally incorporated into national or local immunization programs. Second, the public perception of the HPV vaccine remains strongly gendered, often viewed exclusively as a “women’s health” vaccine, which limits public discourse and policy advocacy for male inclusion. Consequently, while females increasingly benefit from top-down policy support, male university students are left to navigate the high costs and logistical difficulties on their own, further exacerbating the importance of PBC as a determinant of their vaccination intention.

Both behavior attitude and subjective norms emerged as significant determinants of male university students’ intentions to vaccinate against HPV, consistent with earlier TPB-based studies on vaccination intent predictions.37,38,51 The regression coefficients indicate that attitude has a stronger impact on male university students’ intention to receive the HPV vaccine than subjective norms. This suggests that their intentions are more influenced by personal evaluations, feelings, and a sense of responsibility regarding HPV vaccination. Within the TPB framework, attitude reflects not only male university students’ perceptions of the HPV vaccine’s safety and efficacy53 but also their emotional experiences regarding vaccination (such as fear or embarrassment). To some extent, it even embodies their sense of responsibility toward their partners’ health.48,54 When the perceived health benefits and altruistic value outweigh concerns about costs or side effects, a positive attitude becomes the core endogenous driving force for vaccination intention. Despite being the least potent influence on intention relative to attitude and perceived behavioral control, subjective norms still warrant careful consideration. Several studies have reported that a physician’s recommendation is the strongest predictor of HPV vaccination among adolescents and young adults,55,56 parental approval and the desire to meet parental expectations continue to play a pivotal role in vaccination decisions.51 Furthermore, peer norms are considered quite crucial in influencing health-related intentions of college students.57,58 In our study, although subjective norms contributed less than attitude, their significant effect indicates that social influences cannot be overlooked. The novelty of these findings lies in highlighting the primacy of internalized personal judgment over external social pressure. This suggests that interventions should adopt a synergistic approach that addresses behavioral attitudes and subjective norms concurrently, rather than in isolation. Specifically, initiatives should utilize trusted messengers – such as physicians, peers, and health educators – to deliver evidence-based information. This strategy serves a dual purpose: it empowers students to internalize the health and altruistic benefits of vaccination (fostering positive attitudes) while simultaneously normalizing the behavior through influential social endorsements (strengthening subjective norms). By embedding factual education within a supportive social framework, interventions can more effectively convert vaccination intention into actual uptake among male university students.

Our study extended the TPB model by incorporating HPV-related knowledge as a direct predictor, and the results confirmed its significant impact on intention (β = 0.039 in SEM). This finding is consistent with previous studies,37,39,48 reinforcing that knowledge is a cornerstone of health-related decision-making. Male college students with a better understanding of HPV transmission, the male-specific risks (such as genital warts, anal and oropharyngeal cancers), and vaccine preventive effects are better equipped to form a positive attitude and a stronger intention to vaccinate.59 The hierarchical regression analysis further isolated the importance of initial awareness, as simply having “heard of the HPV vaccine” was a significant predictor in the initial demographic model (β = 0.155, p < .01). This suggests a two-step process: initial awareness opens the door to further information seeking, and deeper knowledge then solidifies the intention to act. The novelty of our finding is that it empirically validates knowledge as a distinct direct predictor within the TPB framework, separate from its indirect effects through attitude. This offers a theoretical justification for including knowledge-enhancing components in intervention designs, beyond simply promoting awareness. Crucially, our mediation analysis provides a mechanistic explanation for this: knowledge acts not only as a direct predictor but also as an antecedent that activates the TPB psychological pathways. By enhancing students’ understanding of HPV, interventions can foster more favorable attitudes, strengthen supportive social norms, and bolster perceived behavioral control, which collectively translate into stronger vaccination intentions. This suggests that the ‘knowledge-to-action’ gap is effectively bridged when cognitive inputs (knowledge) are integrated with psychological drivers (TPB constructs).

Strengths

This study possesses several notable strengths. First, it applies a theoretically grounded extended TPB model to an understudied population – male university students in China – offering timely insights given recent policy shifts. Second, the model demonstrates exceptional predictive performance, explaining 75.9% of the variance in vaccination intention. This high explanatory power suggests that the selected psychosocial determinants provide a comprehensive and robust framework for understanding the complex decision-making process in this population. Third, the integration of knowledge as a distinct predictor extends the traditional TPB framework, allowing for a more nuanced understanding of the knowledge-intention pathway. Fourth, we employed a rigorous multi-stage stratified cluster sampling method, stratifying by regional GDP. This systematic approach significantly enhances the external validity and credibility of our findings by mitigating selection bias and ensuring the representativeness of our sample across institutions of varying economic contexts. Finally, the measurement model was rigorously established; the instruments demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α: 0.783–0.939) and structural validity (confirmed via CFA), ensuring that our conclusions are based on reliable and statistically sound data.

Limitations

Our study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, concerning the representativeness of our sample, the demographic composition of our sample may introduce specific biases. While we ensured economic diversity through stratified sampling, our target population was intended to be the broader cohort of male university students in China; however, the sample is predominantly composed of students from specific academic disciplines. Students in health-related or medical majors may possess higher baseline health literacy and more favorable attitudes toward vaccination compared to those in non-health-related fields. This potential ‘selection bias’ based on disciplinary background might lead to an overestimation of vaccine knowledge or willingness within the broader student population. Furthermore, our recruitment was restricted to public comprehensive universities. The findings may not fully generalize to students in vocational or private colleges, where access to sexual health education and campus health services might differ significantly. Furthermore, the voluntary participation likely introduced self-selection bias, potentially leading to an overestimation of health literacy or behavioral intentions. To mitigate this, we used systematic stratification to ensure economic diversity; nevertheless, future research should expand to more diverse cohorts (e.g., vocational institutions) to validate these findings across broader settings. Additionally, excluding students who had already received the HPV vaccine helps isolate the decision-making process, but it limits our findings to the “unvaccinated population” only. Second, the cross-sectional nature captures associations only at a single point in time. Consequently, we cannot infer causal relationships. This prevents us from determining whether knowledge drives intention or vice versa. To address this, future longitudinal studies are warranted to track how intentions translate into actual vaccination behavior, providing stronger evidence for causality. Third, the reliance on self-reported data introduces potential social desirability and recall biases, particularly on sensitive topics like sexual history. This may result in an underestimation of risk-related behaviors. To mitigate this, we ensured full anonymity and confidentiality during data collection to encourage honest reporting. Fourth, the student-based sample restricts our findings’ applicability to the general population. Notably, we acknowledge as a limitation that exact age data were not collected. However, given our focus on university students, the ‘Grade’ variable (Freshman, Sophomore, Junior, Senior) serves as an effective proxy for age progression within the cohort. The impact of this is that the determinants of vaccine hesitancy identified here may differ in older or non-academic populations. However, it is important to note that our sample’s age group (typically 18–24 y) corresponds to a primary target population for HPV vaccination, as this period represents a critical window for sexual initiation and independent health decision-making. Therefore, while we acknowledge the limited generalizability to older or non-academic adults, our findings remain highly relevant to the key demographic for public health interventions. Future research should replicate this model in community-based samples to test the robustness of our framework. Finally, while the expanded TPB model achieved 75.9% explanatory power, it lacks integration with other frameworks like the Health Belief Model, and our knowledge dimension relied on a limited number of items. This suggests we may have underestimated the complex influence of knowledge. As a recommendation, future studies should incorporate more comprehensive knowledge scales and integrate complementary theoretical frameworks to capture a wider spectrum of antecedents to vaccination behavior.

Conclusion and recommendations

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that 62.7% of male university students intend to receive the HPV vaccine, a process driven by a complex interplay of TPB constructs – perceived behavioral control, attitudes, social norms, and, notably, functional knowledge. While general awareness is high, our findings reveal a critical ‘knowledge-to-action’ gap characterized by a superficial understanding of HPV-related risks and a persistent optimistic bias regarding personal infection likelihood.

Theoretically, our validation of knowledge as a direct predictor within the extended TPB framework underscores that cognitive inputs are foundational to behavior change. Practically, our findings suggest that to increase vaccination uptake, public health interventions must transition from broad awareness-raising to targeted, structural strategies. First, for routine practice, campus health services and clinicians should prioritize personalized risk communication to counter optimistic bias and enhance functional health literacy, emphasizing vaccine safety and altruistic benefits. Second, in terms of policy, the focus must shift toward reducing logistical and financial barriers by integrating HPV vaccines into insurance schemes and school-based programs, thereby transforming intention into action. Finally, to advance future research, it is essential to move beyond cross-sectional designs toward longitudinal studies that track the trajectory from intention to behavior, while extending the scope to more diverse, non-academic, and vocational cohorts. By aligning cognitive understanding, psychological drivers, and structural accessibility, we can effectively bridge the vaccination gap and reduce the future burden of HPV-related diseases in this critical population.
