 SUMMARY
Differently processed Cr coatings on Zircaloy cladding were assessed for coating strain to
failure. This report includes i) quantification of Cr coating strain to failure for several coating
variants, ii) incorporation of high throughput analysis developed utilizing artificial intelligence,
and iii) develop a hoop direction coating strain to failure testing approach for high temperatures
that doesn’t require digital image correlation strain measurements. Room temperature strain to
failure measurements indicated that heat treatment had an impact on strain to failure of the
coating, with increasing heat treatment temperature increasing the average strain to failure of the
Cr coating.

  1. Introduction
 Nuclear fuel cladding serves as the barrier between the nuclear fuel and coolant in fission
reactors, where cladding tubes must withstand harsh irradiation environments, large temperature
gradients, and contact with the primary coolant. In pressurized water reactors (PWRs), cladding is
typically made of Zr alloys primarily because Zr has low parasitic thermal neutron absorption.
However, Zr-based cladding undergoes rapid oxidization and hydrogen pickup in high temperature
water or steam, which degrades the mechanical properties. This is of particular concern during a
loss-of-coolant accident (LOCA) or station black-out (SBO), such as what occurred in the 2011
Fukushima Daiichi accident. Due to the rapid oxidation of the Zr cladding, excess hydrogen
accumulated and subsequently caused an explosion [1].

 In response to the events at Fukushima Daiichi nuclear power plant, accident tolerant fuel (ATF)
system strategies have been investigated, such as changing the cladding material or applying a
coating on Zr cladding. ATF strategies aim to: 1) provide valuable time for managing and 2)
decrease the severity of accidents. A leading ATF strategy has been coating Zr cladding tubes with
Cr, as Cr coatings have exhibited resistance to high temperature steam oxidation and hydrothermal
corrosion [2-6]. Strong adhesion of the Cr coating to the Zr cladding tube is imperative to maintain
oxidation resistance during cladding deformation due to cladding creep down, pellet-cladding
interactions, etc. [6]. In this study, the impact of thermal treatments on Cr coated Zircaloy are
investigated for strain to failure of the Cr coatings.

  2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Materials

 Cold-spray Cr coated Zircaloy-4 tubes with six different coating parameters were provided by
University of Wisconsin Madison. Two different gases, N2 and He, were used during spray coating.
Specimens were in the as-received state or had received either a 600 °C heat treatment for 24 hours
or 1000 °C for 1 hour heat treatment. Details on the cold-spray coating method can be found in
Maier et al. [2]. Previous TEM characterization can be found in [7].

2.2. Ring tension testing and digital image correlation

 Ring tension testing was utilized to apply strains variable with position on the ring and the
moment in time during the test. These were performed on a screw driven load frame with a test
fixture described in Hayne et al. [8]. Figure 1(a) shows the load frame mounted to a table with
lights and cameras for digital image correlation (DIC). Figure 1(b) shows a closer view of the
load frame with test fixture and sample. Each ring specimen was mounted over two mandrels,
Figure 1(b)-(d). Care was taken to make sure the specimen was level. A preload of less than 10 N
was applied to the sample to hold it in place. While 10 N is inconsequential for surface failure, it
was later noted that even this small preload deformed the specimen enough to challenge the
circular region select for DIC in later image analysis.

 The tension tests were performed under displacement control at a rate of 0.5 mm/min.
Previous measurements with a similar setup showed that the side camera tends to lose focus as
the mandrels move and the ring deforms. To correct for this, the load frame was set to stop every

2.3 Digital image correlation strain measurements

 For DIC analysis, a camera was mounted pointed vertically down at the sample, Figure 1(c).
For failure detection, a camera was also mounted looking roughly 45 degrees down at the sample
from the side, Figure 1(d). Lighting was adjusted to optimize both surface illumination and glare
reduction. Glare is particularly challenging from the side angle because the cleaned coating
surface is reflective and round.

09/30/2025                                                                                        3

 Strain was tracked dynamically with spatial resolution through Digital Image Correlation
(DIC). In order to perform DIC, specimens must be prepared with a “speckle pattern” on the
surface which establish high-contrast geometries which can be tracked through imaging.

 The speckle pattern was applied by first mounting the specimens to a backing plate with an
adhesive tape. An airbrush was then used to thoroughly paint each specimen with several white
coats as a background. The final airbrush coat is with a dark black paint and is only applied over
a single pass from a distance. This provides the high-contrast speckle pattern to be tracked later.
Figure 1(c) illustrates the speckle pattern.

 Because images will also be taken from a side perspective with the intent of observing
surface failures, the DIC paint needed to be removed from the coating surfaces on the sides. This
was done by gently brushing the side surfaces with an acetone-dipped swab. The difference
between the painted top surface and cleaned side surface is shown in Figure 1(d).

 DIC strain analysis was performed with commercial software, VIC-2d 7. The analysis
requires a batch of images, selection of a region of interest where the speckle pattern is painted,
and the selection of various analysis parameters. The analysis then tracks the speckles from the
first image as they move through each sequential image providing a strain map for each frame.
The data is then exported with every frame having an associated .csv data file which details the
original and current pixel location of every tracked pixel along with the directional and Von
Mises strains at that location. A map of these strains is overlayed on top of an image in Figure 2.

2.4 Coating failure detection

 A novel method of failure detection was created for this study involving automated computer
vision image analysis largely leveraging LANL’s internal version of Claude 3.7 Artificial
Intelligence.

 Surface failures during these tests manifest as thin dark fractures. The goal of this failure
detection was to identify on which frame fractures began, where on the specimen each fracture
was located when it began, and correlate that location to the strain map generated by VIC-2d 7.
Multiple fractures can then be aggregated for a statistical analysis of the strain to failure.

 In the first step of analysis, a line is generated to follow the curve of the outer surface of the
specimen, and the darkness of each pixel along that line is extracted as a “lineout”. To blur out
statistical outliers like perhaps a dirt speck on the surface, several more lineouts are taken as well
with prescribed spacing and averaged together. These lineouts are spaced only in the x direction
because fractures also occur on that axis, so averaged lineouts should amplify the darkness of
fractures while blurring random darkness elsewhere. A peak detection algorithm then analyzes
the averaged lineout to look for dark peaks. Figure 5 illustrates.

Figure 3 - The original image and lineout location is shown left. The darkness profile of that
lineout is shown right with triangles placed on the automatically detected dark peaks.

 The fracture detection across an entire test however was inconsistent. Sometimes a particular
location could be defined as a crack in frame N-1 and in frame N+1 but somehow be missed in
frame N. This could be improved with training to refine the peak detection parameters, but the
varied conditions of each test and limited validated data availability make this prohibitively
challenging. Instead, a waterfall plot of every lineout across the test was aggregated as illustrated
in Figure 4(a). This provides a much more digestible aggregate perspective of the entire test with
clear stripes visible in the bottom of the image, representing fractures. The onset of the fractures
is noticeable by the onset of those dark stripes which form a triangular shape from the outside to
the center. During the test, strain grows from the center outward, so we see from the image that
these fractures are initiated as the strain grows.

 These fractures become even more apparent when we overlay the crack detection algorithm
illustrated in Figure 5 onto each row of the image. Illustrated in Figure 6 (b), we see the fractures
highlighted clearly. We can also see some noise, partially attributable to an overtuned peak
detection algorithm but also with possible physical meaning as well, discussed in the Results
section. It was observed that the algorithm tended to perform better with increased lighting. The
waterfall plots make it very apparent that part of the ring may be especially shaded and result in
reduced data quality, emphasizing the importance of proper lighting.

Figure 4 - (a) Waterfall plot where each row of pixels is a darkness lineout from the right,
progressing through the test from top to bottom. (b) The waterfall darkness lineout with
overlayed peak detection highlighted in red.

 From this plot, the onset of each fracture is aggregated for the test. The x coordinate in the
plot correlates to the location on the right, and the top pixel of any individual fracture correlates
to the frame in which it occurred. With the location and time (frame) known, strain can be
extracted from the VIC-2d 7 output. However, some further processing is helpful. The strain
analysis does not quite extend fully to the edge of the sample which is where the fracture is
occurring [Beck et al.]. In order to get the strain there, some extrapolation is performed. A matrix
of pixels is generated to extend beyond the VIC generated strain map, and a 2d cubic
interpolation method is used to extend the surface profile of the strain profile out to the edge of
the surface. The strain value correlated to the location of the fracture at onset is then extracted.

  3. Results and Discussion
 An aggregate of the resulting strain to failures is illustrated in Figure 5 as box plots. The box
plots mark quartiles in the data, with the boxes representing the second and third quartile.
Individual measurements of cracks are presented as circles. In the Helium deposited coating
samples there is a trend in strain to failure with increasing temperature. Notably the sample that
had the 1000 °C heat treatment did not exhibit observable Cr coating cracks except near the
fracture of the ring. An important note specific for the samples heat treated at 1000 °C, the
Zircaloy would have undergone a phase transition and an intermetallic phase develops at the
coating-substrate interface. The as-deposited Cr coating cracked at a mean strain of 0.9 % strain
while the sample with the 600 °C for 24 hour heat treatment had a median strain for cracking of
1.4 %.

 For the case of the N2 gas deposited coatings the strain at cracking for the as-deposited
coating was higher than that from the He gas deposited coating. The heat treated N2 gas
deposited coating samples were tested, however, analysis failed due to discolorations on the
coatings that remained even after cleaning with acetone. The large dark spots which
overwhelmed the analysis by dramatically lowering the contrast between dark fractures and these
spots. Improvements could be made to make the analysis more robust, or more careful surface
preparation could be performed.

 The waterfall plots highlight that the grooves in the coatings due to post coating polishing are
the typical location of crack formation. Original peak detection analysis was showing fractures in
these areas which at first seemed overtuned, but on observation of the full waterfall plot it is
evident that fractures tend to occur in the troughs of the original texture. It is possible therefore
that a better polish of the surface coating may improve strain to failure.

  4. Conclusions

 Cladding coatings are important for current and next generation reactor fuel systems, this
project developed and utilized high throughput mechanical evaluation of cladding coatings.
Materials for testing were produced at the University of Wisconsin Madison by Dr. Tyler Dabney
and Professor Kumar Sridharan. These included six variants of Cr coatings on Zircaloy-4. In the
as-received, heat treated at 600 °C for 24 hours or 1000 °C for 1 hour conditions. These samples
were ring-pull tension tested at room temperature while measuring local strain with digital image
correlation and the coatings with separate cameras. The sample of the He processing gas and 1000
°C heat treatment for 1 hour was an anomaly and did not produce visible cracking of the coating
despite a strain at the coating to conform to the mandrel of approximately 4 % in the hoop direction.

 • Failure distributions of Cr coatings on Zircaloy-4, subjected to different heat treatments.

 • Development for higher throughput analysis was achieved by utilizing advances in
 artificial intelligence to develop computer code efficiently.

 • A high temperature coating failure fixture was developed that does not require digital
 image correlation for analysis.
