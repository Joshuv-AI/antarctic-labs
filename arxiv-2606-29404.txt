A Multi-Body Dobrushin-Sokal Criterion – Part II
Jan Philipp Neumann

Abstract
. We prove a sufficient condition for the absolute convergence of Mayer cluster expansions of log-partition and correlation functions applicable to lattice gases with possibly complex-valued multi-body interactions. Not only are several classical results subsumed but a partition scheme for spanning hypergraphs also makes our methods well-suited for treating stronger multi-body interactions, including higher-order hard-core repulsion in the context of hypergraph independence polynomials. Furthermore, our approach is easily combined with the Gruber–Kunz condition to produce extended convergence results for the polymer expansion of lattice gases, rivalling those obtained not too long ago by Nguyen and Fernández (2024). Mathematics Subject Classification : 82B20 (05C65, 05C70). Keywords : Classical lattice gas; multi-body interactions; grand partition function; cluster expansions; Kirkwood–Salsburg hierarchy; polymer expansion.



1.
Introduction

The present article deals with new convergence criteria for the cluster and polymer expansions of classical lattice gases with multi-body interactions.
Although essentially self-contained, it complements
[
20
]
, henceforth just “Part I”, where the author derives a sufficient condition for non-vanishing of partition functions.
Results on the convergence of cluster expansions are only alluded to there.
Here, they are stated and proven.

For pairwise interactions, there is an extensive body of literature on the matter:
the Kotecký–Preiss condition, extended by Ueltschi and Poghosyan, see
[
18
,
29
,
21
]
, is concise, widely applicable and used to this day;
a slightly superior and similarly concise criterion is that of Dobrushin and Sokal for repulsive lattice gases, see
[
3
,
4
,
28
,
26
]
;
the even better, though a little more involved, Fernández–Procacci condition is again amenable to wider generalisation, see
[
7
,
2
,
5
,
13
,
15
]
.
Particularly in the last one of these references, Jansen and Kolesnikov thoroughly described how to leverage the Kirkwood–Salsburg hierarchy for correlation functions to obtain a systematic approach to convergence criteria, including all of the above.

This systematic approach remains perfectly valid in the case of multi-body, i.e., higher-order, interactions but the previously trivial combinatorics of the Kirkwood–Salsburg integral kernel become considerably more complicated.
These complications can be partially sidestepped, provided a two-particle potential amenable to, say, the generalised Kotecký–Preiss condition is extended by a stable higher-order interaction of finite range, cf.
[
19
,
27
]
, but, compared to the purely pairwise case, the intermediate estimates seem somewhat crude and we are unwilling to assume finite range outright.
Instead, we tackle the diagrammatic interpretation of the kernel and use a novel partition scheme for spanning hypergraphs (Lemma
4.1
) to derive our first main result, Theorem
2.1
, an extension of the Dobrushin–Sokal criterion to classical lattice gases with site-wise hard-core self-repulsion and otherwise complex multi-body interactions.
As in the conditions listed above for pairwise interactions, only one inequality per site is required, involving only a single auxiliary function, called
\alpha
here, on the underlying lattice in addition to the innate model-defining parameters.
A variant of this combination of the Kirkwood–Salsburg hierarchy with our partition scheme for coverings is included in Part I as an alternative proof method.
But only here do we extend its application to derive convergence of cluster expansions.

Our main result comes on the heels of recent progress regarding zero-free activity polydiscs for hypergraph independence polynomials, i.e., partition functions of lattice gases with pure multi-body hard-core repulsions.
More precisely, our original derivation of Theorem
2.1
, cf. Part I, consisted in generalising approaches by Galvin et al. in
[
10
]
and Bencs and Buys in
[
1
]
.
The latter two authors were able to reproduce the same optimal uniform radii in terms of global degree bounds that had previously only been established for standard graphs, i.e., for pairwise hard-core interactions, cf., e.g.,
[
26
]
.
Their intricate way of isolating the interaction terms from each other is functionally replaced here by inserting our aforementioned partition scheme into the Kirkwood–Salsburg hierarchy.
In turn, this replacement was spurred on by the realisation that, if we choose the constant auxiliary function
\alpha=1
in our condition, we obtain a direct improvement of Gallavotti and Miracle-Solé’s condition in
[
8
]
, derived explicitly by bounding the Kirkwood–Salsburg kernel.
The latter improvement, which extends to the variants in
[
9
,
25
]
, can, in fact, be derived by a banal observation without any need for our partition scheme.
The same is true of our criterion whenever
\alpha\geq 1
but the partition scheme is so far necessary to bridge the gap to Galvin et al.’s result as well as that of Bencs and Buys, where
\alpha<1
, cf. Section 5 of Part I.

There are more results on cluster expansions with multi-body interactions out there.
The interested reader may have a look at the introductions of
[
17
,
10
,
6
]
and the references listed therein.
Here, however, we avoid overly technical a priori assumptions and still derive multi-body variants of the Dobrushin–Sokal and Kotecký–Preiss conditions, at least in the lattice gas setting.
There is also the recent preprint
[
12
]
on analyticity in continuum systems with repulsive multi-body potentials of finite range.
Although it does not consider cluster expansions, it would be interesting to see if this result of Helmuth, Pappik and Perkins’s can be extended to a more general class of systems.

There is yet another aspect to cluster expansions for lattice gases, also merely alluded to in Part I.
As described by Gruber and Kunz in
[
11
]
, the partition function of an essentially arbitrary lattice spin system equals that of another lattice gas whose sites/particles are non-empty finite subsets, dubbed “polymers”, of the initial lattice, see also
[
22
,
6
]
.
More importantly, the polymers in this reformulation only interact via a pairwise hard-core interaction prohibiting overlap so all of the classical cluster expansion results apply.
Given the special structure of polymers, we, as well as others, still opt for the Gruber–Kunz convergence condition, itself a corollary to the Fernández–Procacci criterion or a polymer-specific exact criterion, see
[
7
,
2
]
or
[
15
]
, respectively.
The caveat in this simplification is the fact that the combinatorial complexities of the previous cluster expansions are part of computing and bounding the individual polymer activities.

Nevertheless, it contains a desirable (from a physical perspective) transition from pre-polymer activity
z
to
\frac{z}{1+z}
and our first approach can be adapted to yield a convergence condition, see Theorem
2.4
, which extends to parameter ranges beyond where Theorem
2.1
applies.
In particular, we obtain notable improvements over the conditions in Procacci and Scoppola’s
[
22
]
as well as Gallavotti, Miracle-Solé and Robinson’s
[
9
]
, cf. also
[
25
]
, in the form of Corollary
2.5
here.
For a comparison with a more recent result, we consider that of Nguyen and Fernández in
[
6
]
.
While their approach applies to far more general lattice systems, our method has the advantage of covering stronger interactions, including hard-core ones.

The paper is structured as follows:
Section
2
introduces the basic notation, states and discusses the first main result, Theorem
2.1
, and then does the same for the second main result, Theorem
2.4
, on the polymer expansion;
Section
3
builds the framework for efficiently manipulating the relevant power series in what is sometimes called “Ruelle’s algebraic method”;
this allows us to systematically extend our lattice gas notation in Section
4
, which also ends with our partition scheme for spanning hypergraphs in the form of Lemma
4.1
;
the final Section
5
collects the proofs of our main results.

2.
Setup and main results

2.1.
Configurations on a lattice

Our
lattice

\mathbb{X}
is a fixed finite or countably infinite set of
sites
for particles to occupy.
Ignoring assignments of infinitely many particles, a
configuration
is a
multi-index
in

\mathbf{N}:=\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{X}):=\{\mathbf{n}=(n_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{X}}\mid|\mathbf{n}|<\infty\},

where
|\mathbf{n}|:=\mathbf{n}[\mathbb{X}]
with
\mathbf{n}[S]:=\sum_{x\in S}n_{x}
for arbitrary
S\subset\mathbb{X}
.
In view of the evident identification with finite counting measures, we use the
Dirac measure

\delta_{x}
in
x\in\mathbb{X}
to denote the multi-index
{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{x\}}
.
It is also often convenient to enumerate or label particles.
More specifically, for an arbitrary finite index set
J
of cardinality
|J|\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we use the symmetrisation map

\mathbb{X}^{J}=\{\mathbf{x}=(x_{j})_{j\in J}\mid\forall j\in J:x_{j}\in\mathbb{X}\}\to\{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}\mid|\mathbf{n}|=|J|\},\quad\mathbf{x}\mapsto\sum_{j\in J}\delta_{x_{j}},

to identify a
|J|
-particle configuration with any
tuple
in its preimage.
Furthermore, configurations with at most one particle per site are called
simple
here and are identified with finite
subsets
via the bijection

\mathbf{F}:=\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{X}):=\{X\Subset\mathbb{X}\}\to\mathbf{N}\cap\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{X}},\quad X\mapsto{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{X}=\sum_{x\in X}\delta_{x}.

Another convenient identification consists in treating the obvious bijection

\mathbf{N}(\Lambda)\to\{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}\mid\mathbf{n}[\mathbb{X}\setminus\Lambda]=0\}

for arbitrary
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{X}
as an inclusion of sets, mirroring the a priori inclusions
\Lambda^{J}\subset\mathbb{X}^{J}
and
\mathbf{F}(\Lambda)\subset\mathbf{F}
.

2.2.
Simple interaction, Boltzmann factor and Ursell function

We now fix an
interaction potential

V:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{C}\cup\{+\infty\}
.
Both
V
and the
Mayer function

f:=\mathrm{e}^{-V}-1
, where
\mathrm{e}^{-\infty}:=0
, are also defined on finite tuples over and subsets of
\mathbb{X}
via the above identifications.
The
Boltzmann factor

\kappa:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{C}
can then be defined by setting

\kappa(\mathbf{x}):=\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{S\subset J:S\neq\varnothing}V(\mathbf{x}_{S})}=\prod_{S\subset J:S\neq\varnothing}(1+f(\mathbf{x}_{S}))=\sum_{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{H}_{J}}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{h}}f(\mathbf{x}_{e})

for every tuple
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{X}^{J}
with finite index set
J
, where
\mathbf{x}_{S}:=(x_{j})_{j\in S}
is the restriction/projection of
\mathbf{x}
to an index subset
S\subset J
while

\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{V}}:=\{\mathfrak{h}\subset\{e\Subset\mathbb{V}\mid e\neq\varnothing\}\}

denotes the set of
hypergraphs
(sets of
edges
) on an arbitrary
vertex
set
\mathbb{V}
.

The characterisation of
\kappa
in terms of hypergraph weights provides a direct and intuitive approach to characterising the
Ursell function
, which is the unique
\varphi:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying
\varphi(0)=0
and

\kappa(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}_{J}}\prod_{P\in\mathfrak{p}}\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{P})

(1)

for every tuple
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{X}^{J}
with finite index set
J
, where we denote by

\mathfrak{P}_{U}:=\{\mathfrak{p}\subset\{P\subset U\mid P\neq\varnothing\}\mid U=\textstyle\bigsqcup_{P\in\mathfrak{p}}P\}

the set of unordered
partitions
of an arbitrary set
U
into non-empty subsets.
Clearly, for every set
\mathbb{V}
, we have a cluster decomposition

\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{V}}=\bigsqcup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}_{\mathbb{V}}}\{\mathfrak{h}=\textstyle\bigsqcup_{P\in\mathfrak{p}}\mathfrak{h}_{P}\mid\forall P\in\mathfrak{p}:\mathfrak{h}_{P}\in\mathfrak{C}_{P}\},

denoting by
\mathfrak{C}_{\mathbb{V}}\subset\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{V}}
the set of
cluster hypergraphs
on
\mathbb{V}
, i.e., for
\mathbb{V}\neq\varnothing
,
\mathfrak{C}_{\mathbb{V}}
coincides with the set of all
\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{V}}
such that
\mathbb{V}
is
connected
with respect to
\mathfrak{h}
in the standard sense whereas
\mathfrak{C}_{\varnothing}:=\varnothing
since connected components are always non-empty.
One easily verifies that the characterisation of
\varphi
via (
1
) necessitates

\varphi(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{C}_{J}}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{h}}f(\mathbf{x}_{e})

for every tuple
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{X}^{J}
with finite index set
J
.

Throughout the entire article, we assume
V
to be
simple
in the sense that
\kappa
vanishes outside
\mathbf{F}
or, equivalently,
\kappa(2\delta_{x})=\mathrm{e}^{-2V(x)-V(2\delta_{x})}=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
.
A lot of the theory presented here does not depend on this blanket assumption but our actual results rely on it.

2.3.
Partition function and Mayer series

If
z=(z(x))_{x\in\mathbb{X}}
is a collection of commuting abstract
activity
variables, we can define the formal power series

Z(z):=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}}\frac{z^{\mathbf{n}}}{\mathbf{n}!}\kappa(\mathbf{n})\quad\text{and}\quad M(z):=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}}\frac{z^{\mathbf{n}}}{\mathbf{n}!}\varphi(\mathbf{n}),

respectively called grand
partition function
and
Mayer series
, without issue.
In these multivariate series, we use the notation

z^{\mathbf{n}}:=\prod_{x\in\mathbb{X}}z(x)^{n_{x}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{n}!:=\prod_{x\in\mathbb{X}}n_{x}!

for
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}
(only finitely many factors differ from
1
) and the definition of
\varphi
is precisely equivalent to the identity
Z(z)=\exp(M(z))
.
It readily follows that, given
x\in\mathbb{X}
, the three series

Z(z)^{-1}\frac{\partial Z}{\partial z(x)}(z),\quad\frac{\partial M}{\partial z(x)}(z)\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}}\frac{z^{\mathbf{n}}}{\mathbf{n}!}\varphi(\delta_{x}+\mathbf{n})=:\rho(x,z)

are formally identical.
We call the last one the (reduced) one-point
correlation
of
x
.
Restriction of these series to some
reference volume

\Lambda\subset\mathbb{X}
is implemented via

Z_{\Lambda}(z):=Z(z{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}),\quad M_{\Lambda}(z):=M(z{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda})\quad\text{and}\quad\rho_{\Lambda}(x,z):=\rho(x,z{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}).

It is particularly noteworthy that our restricted correlation does not categorically vanish as soon as
x\in\mathbb{X}\setminus\Lambda
, in which case we refer to

\widehat{z}_{\Lambda}(x,z):=z(x)\rho_{\Lambda}(x,z)

as an
effective activity
, a notion taken from, e.g.,
[
26
,
17
,
14
,
16
]
.
This is a slight departure from the standard notation due to convenience here.

2.4.
The Dobrushin-Sokal criterion

The simplicity of
V
means that, for every
x\in\mathbb{X}
, we can write

Z(z)=\sum_{X\Subset\mathbb{X}}z^{X}\kappa(X)=\sum_{X\Subset\mathbb{X}\setminus\{x\}}z^{X}\kappa(X)+z(x)\sum_{Y\Subset\mathbb{X}\setminus\{x\}}z^{Y}\kappa(\{x\}\cup Y).

The second identity is formally equivalent to the observation that

Z_{\{x\}\cup\Lambda}(z)=Z_{\Lambda}(z)(1+\widehat{z}_{\Lambda}(x,z)),

for every
x\in\mathbb{X}
and
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{X}\setminus\{x\}
, which comes with the two related formulas

M_{\{x\}\cup\Lambda}(z)-M_{\Lambda}(z)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{(-1)^{m-1}}{m}\widehat{z}_{\Lambda}(x,z)^{m}=\log(1+\widehat{z}_{\Lambda}(x,z))

and

z(x)\rho_{\{x\}\cup\Lambda}(x,z)=\widehat{z}_{\Lambda}(x,z)\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}(-1)^{m}\widehat{z}_{\Lambda}(x,z)^{m}=\frac{\widehat{z}_{\Lambda}(x,z)}{1+\widehat{z}_{\Lambda}(x,z)}.

Note that the above logarithmic and geometric series have radius of convergence equal to 1, indicating why we aim for bounds of the form
|\widehat{z}_{\Lambda}(x,z)|<1
upon evaluation in
\mathbb{C}
below.

Fix two functions
r:\mathbb{X}\to[0,1)
and
\alpha:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}:=[0,+\infty)
related by the two equivalent pointwise identities
r=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}
and
\alpha=\frac{r}{1-r}
.
We call
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{X}

r
-finite if the mutually equivalent conditions

r[\Lambda]:=\sum_{x\in\Lambda}r(x)<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad(1+r)^{\Lambda}:=\prod_{x\in\Lambda}(1+r(x))<\infty

hold.
These sums and products a priori exist in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}:=[0,+\infty]
by monotone convergence.
Analogous notation, particularly
\alpha^{S}=\prod_{x\in S}\alpha(x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
for
S\Subset X
, is frequently used in the following.
Note also that

(1-r)^{\Lambda}=\frac{1}{(1+\alpha)^{\Lambda}}>0\quad\text{and}\quad-\log(1-r)[\Lambda]=\log(1+\alpha)[\Lambda]<\infty,

where
1/\infty=0
, are each equivalent to
\Lambda
being
r
-finite.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
z:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{C}
and suppose that
V
is a simple interaction potential with Mayer function
f=\mathrm{e}^{-V}-1
and Ursell function
\varphi
satisfying

|z(x)|\prod_{X\Subset\mathbb{X}:x\in X}\max\{|\mathrm{e}^{-V(X)}|,1+|f(X)|\alpha^{S}\mid\varnothing\neq S\subset X\setminus\{x\}\}\leq r(x)

(2)

for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
.
Then, for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
, we have

\sup_{\Lambda\subset\mathbb{X}\setminus\{x\}}|\widehat{z}_{\Lambda}(x,z)|\leq\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{X}\setminus\{x\})}\frac{|z|^{\delta_{x}+\mathbf{n}}}{\mathbf{n}!}|\varphi(\delta_{x}+\mathbf{n})|\leq r(x)<1

(3)

and

\sup_{\Lambda\subset\mathbb{X}}|z(x)\rho_{\Lambda}(x,z)|\leq\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}}\frac{|z|^{\delta_{x}+\mathbf{n}}}{\mathbf{n}!}|\varphi(\delta_{x}+\mathbf{n})|\leq\alpha(x)<\infty.

We then use the effective activity bounds to control partition functions and Mayer series as indicated above.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
z:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{C}
and suppose that
V
is a simple interaction potential with Boltzmann factor
\kappa
and Ursell function
\varphi
satisfying (
3
) for every
x\in\mathbb{X}
. Then, for every
r
-finite
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{X}
, we have

0<(1-r)^{\Lambda}\leq|Z_{\Lambda}(z)|\leq\sum_{X\Subset\Lambda}|z|^{X}|\kappa(X)|\leq(1+r)^{\Lambda}<\infty,

|M_{\Lambda}(z)|\leq\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}(\Lambda)}\frac{|z|^{\mathbf{n}}}{\mathbf{n}!}|\varphi(\mathbf{n})|\leq-\log(1-r)[\Lambda]<\infty

and, in particular,
Z_{\Lambda}(z)=\exp(M_{\Lambda}(z))\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
.

Together, Theorem
2.1
and Corollary
2.2
constitute a stronger version of the main result of Part I.
While the condition (
2
) is the same, the conclusions regarding the absolute convergence of cluster expansions, in particular that of the Mayer series, are new.
The cluster expansion setting is also what easily allows the extension to arbitrary reference volumes
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{X}
in Theorem
2.1
and at least
r
-finite ones in Corollary
2.2
.

2.5.
A few comments on our main result

The initial term
|\mathrm{e}^{-V(X)}|
inside each maximum in (
2
) corresponds to
S=\varnothing
.
For
X=\{x\}
, it is the only term and is traditionally absorbed into
z(x)
.
For
X\supsetneq\{x\}
, the term is obviously irrelevant to the maximum whenever the potential
V(X)
is repulsive in the sense that
|e^{-V(X)}|\leq 1
.
Our titular naming choice of the criterion (
2
) comes from the fact that, for repulsive pair potentials, i.e.,
\mathrm{Re}\,V\geq 0
and
V(X)=V(X){\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{|X|=2\}}
for all
X\Subset\mathbb{X}
, it turns into

|z(x)|\prod_{y\in\mathbb{X}\setminus\{x\}}(1+|f(\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})|\alpha(y))\leq r(x)=\frac{\alpha(x)}{1+\alpha(x)},

which is just another way of writing Sokal’s condition from
[
28
,
26
]
.
Dobrushin had previously established this condition for hard-core pair interactions, where
|f(\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})|\in\{0,1\}
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{X}
, see
[
3
,
4
]
.
Observe also that, even in the multi-body case, (
2
) just becomes

|z(x)\mathrm{e}^{-V(x)}|(1+\alpha(x))\prod_{X\Subset\mathbb{X}:\{x\}\subsetneq X}(1+|f(X)|\alpha^{X\setminus\{x\}})\leq\alpha(x)

whenever
\alpha\geq 1
, i.e,
r\geq 1/2
, pointwise.

Dobrushin’s condition is itself a refinement of the one Kotecký and Preiss introduced in
[
18
]
, also dealing only with hard-core pair potentials.
Later extensions to repulsive and even stable pair interactions were made by Ueltschi and then Poghosyan and Ueltschi in
[
29
]
and
[
21
]
, respectively.
A priori stability assumptions are ubiquitous in equilibrium statistical mechanics, cf., e.g.,
[
25
]
,
[
8
,
9
]
,
[
21
,
24
,
13
,
15
]
.
They are used to control non-repulsive parts of
V
.
Here we note that (
2
) implies the (local) stability condition

|z(x)\mathrm{e}^{-V(x)}|\mathrm{e}^{c(x)}=|z(x)\mathrm{e}^{-V(x)}|\prod_{X\Subset\mathbb{X}:\{x\}\subsetneq X}\max\{|\mathrm{e}^{-V(X)}|,1\}\leq r(x)

with
c(x):=\sum_{X\Subset\mathbb{X}:\{x\}\subsetneq X}\max\{-\mathrm{Re}\,V(X),0\}
.
In fact, we do not see room for direct improvement of our main result through intermediate stability bounds.
However, for a close comparison with extensions of the Kotecký–Preiss condition, let
V(x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
and suppose that
\alpha=|z|\mathrm{e}^{c+a}
with
a:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
In this case, (
2
) holds whenever

|z(x)|e^{c(x)+a(x)}+\sum_{X\Subset\mathbb{X}:\{x\}\subsetneq X}|f(X)|\max_{S\subset X\setminus\{x\}:S\neq\varnothing}\{(|z|\mathrm{e}^{c+a})^{S}\}\leq a(x)

and, for pairwise interactions, this essentially reads like the condition from
[
21
]
, cf. also
[
15
]
.
The summand
|z(x)|e^{c(x)+a(x)}
implicitly has the prefactor
|f(2\delta_{x})|=1
.
Returning to the formulation in terms of
\alpha
, a well-known optimisation argument, omitted here but included in Part I for the reader’s convenience, yields the following.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
z:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{C}
and suppose that
V
is a simple interaction potential with Mayer function
f=\mathrm{e}^{-V}-1
satisfying
V(x)=0
as well as

|z(x)|\leq\mathrm{e}^{-c(x)-1}C^{-1}

for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
, where
C:=\sup\{1+\sum_{X\Subset\mathbb{X}:\{y\}\subsetneq X}|f(X)|\mid y\in\mathbb{X}\}<\infty
. Then the conclusions of Theorem
2.1
hold with constant
\alpha=C^{-1}\leq 1
.

As far as we can tell, this result is new in the case of multi-body potentials.
For pairwise interactions, it is essentially a classic, see, e.g.,
[
25
]
, and applies to more abstract settings like in
[
21
,
15
]
.

A refinement of Dobrushin’s condition and that of Kotecký–Preiss for hard-core pair interactions was provided by Fernández and Procacci in
[
7
]
, see also
[
2
]
and, for extensions to general pairwise repulsions and stable pair potentials,
[
5
,
13
,
15
]
.
We only use one of its corollaries in the polymer reformulation of our lattice gas below.
Concise extensions to multi-body potentials seem out of reach so far.

2.6.
The subset polymer gas

The polymer expansion consists in rewriting a wide variety of lattice systems, say, on
\mathbb{X}
, as a classical lattice gas on the countable set

\mathbb{P}:=\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{X}):=\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{X})\setminus\{\varnothing\}\subset\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{X})

of
polymers
over
\mathbb{X}
.
The relevant Boltzmann factor
\kappa_{\mathbb{P}}:\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{P})\to\{0,1\}
is canonically given by the indicator of pairwise non-overlap, i.e.,

\kappa_{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{P}):={\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\mathbf{F}}({\textstyle\sum_{j\in J}P_{j}})={\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{\forall j,k\in J:j\neq k\Rightarrow P_{j}\cap P_{k}=\varnothing\}}=\sum_{\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{G}_{J}}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{g}}(-{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{\bigcap_{j\in e}P_{j}\neq\varnothing\}})

for all finitely indexed polymer tuples
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{P}^{J}
, where

\mathfrak{G}_{\mathbb{V}}:=\{\mathfrak{g}\subset\{e\subset\mathbb{V}\mid|e|=2\}\}\subset\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{V}}

denotes the set of
standard graphs
on an arbitrary vertex set
\mathbb{V}
.

\kappa_{\mathbb{P}}
clearly corresponds to the purely pairwise hard-core potential and Mayer function
V_{\mathbb{P}}:\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{P})\to\{0,+\infty\}
,
f_{\mathbb{P}}:=e^{-V_{\mathbb{P}}}-1:\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{P})\to\{0,-1\}
with

V_{\mathbb{P}}(\delta_{P}+\delta_{Q}):=+\infty{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{P\cap Q\neq\varnothing\}}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{\mathbb{P}}(\delta_{P}+\delta_{Q})=-{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{P\cap Q\neq\varnothing\}}

for all
P,Q\in\mathbb{P}
.
Since
\varnothing\notin\mathbb{P}
,
V_{\mathbb{P}}
is obviously simple in our sense.

Given a collection of
polymer activities

w=(w(P))_{P\in\mathbb{P}}
,
we then define the a priori formal grand
polymer partition function

\Xi(w):=Z_{\mathbb{P}}(w):=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{P})}\frac{w^{\mathbf{n}}}{\mathbf{n}!}\kappa_{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{n})=\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\Subset\mathbb{P}}w^{\mathfrak{p}}{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{\forall P,Q\in\mathfrak{p}:P\neq Q\Rightarrow P\cap Q=\varnothing\}}

as well as its formal logarithm, the
polymer Mayer series

\Omega(w):=M_{\mathbb{P}}(w):=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{P})}\frac{w^{\mathbf{n}}}{\mathbf{n}!}\varphi_{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{n}),

where the polymer Ursell function
\varphi_{\mathbb{P}}:\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{P})\to\mathbb{Z}
, defined analogously to
\varphi
, takes its classical form, i.e, for all finitely indexed tuples
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{P}^{J}
, we have

\varphi_{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{P})=\sum_{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{C}_{J}}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{h}}f_{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{P}_{e})=\sum_{\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{CG}_{J}}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{g}}(-{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{\bigcap_{j\in e}P_{j}\neq\varnothing\}}).

Here and below,

, which coincides with the set of connected standard graphs whenever
\mathbb{V}\neq\varnothing
.
Accordingly, the classical way of writing the Mayer series is

\Omega(w)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{P}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}w(P_{j})\sum_{\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{CG}_{n}}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{g}}(-{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{\bigcap_{j\in e}P_{j}\neq\varnothing\}})

with the obvious shorthand
\mathbb{P}^{n}=\mathbb{P}^{\{1,\ldots,n\}}
and
\mathfrak{CG}_{n}:=\mathfrak{CG}_{\{1,\ldots,n\}}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Omitting a discussion of (reduced) polymer correlations, restriction to (polymers over) a reference volume
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{X}
is implemented through

\Xi_{\Lambda}(w):=\Xi(w{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\mathbb{P}(\Lambda)})\quad\text{and}\quad\Omega_{\Lambda}(w):=\Omega(w{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\mathbb{P}(\Lambda)}).

2.7.

For better readability, we assume that our simple potential

2.8.
Relation to existing results

For comparisons against more classical results, first consider a physical potential

If

so, in the high-temperature regime, the more relevant first inequality in (
4

, provided

and the above convergence are somewhat uniform over
\mathbb{X}
.
If additionally

, indicating an absence of phase transitions.
With this in mind, we define the function

D_{A}:\mathbb{X}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\quad x\mapsto\sum_{X\Subset\mathbb{X}:\{x\}\subsetneq X}|V(X)|A^{X},

for

, which directly inherits the

3.
Preliminaries on power series and coefficient operations

Our power series are mostly of the form

\pi^{z}[g]:=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}}\frac{z^{\mathbf{n}}}{\mathbf{n}!}g(\mathbf{n})=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{X}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}z(x_{j})g(\mathbf{x})

where the coefficient
g:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
takes values in
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{A}=\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
or sets of formal power series with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}
or
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.
The collection
z=(z(x))_{x\in\mathbb{X}}
of a priori formal variables may be evaluated as a function
\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{A}
in the absence of convergence issues.
We think of
\pi^{z}[\bullet]
as the integral with respect to a non-normalised multivariate
Poisson distribution
with intensity
z
and employ measure-theoretic conventions when evaluating
z
more concretely.
We do not claim any novelty for the contents of this section which are easily extrapolated from sections in, e.g.,
[
25
,
8
]
.
Our self-contained reiteration is streamlined to fit our notation.

3.1.
Poisson convolution

Given
g,h:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
, we can define their
convolution

g\ast h:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
by setting

g\ast h(\mathbf{n}):=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{N}}\binom{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{k}}g(\mathbf{k})h(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{k})\quad\text{or}\quad(g\ast h)(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{K\subset J}g(\mathbf{x}_{K})h(\mathbf{x}_{J\setminus K})

for all
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}
or
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{X}^{J}
with finite index set
J
.
Recalling that

\binom{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{k}}:=\prod_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\binom{n_{x}}{k_{x}}=\prod_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\frac{n_{x}!}{k_{x}!}{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{k_{x}\leq n_{x}\}}=\frac{\mathbf{n}!}{\mathbf{k}!}{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{\mathbf{k}\leq\mathbf{n}\}}

is the multivariate binomial coefficient for
\mathbf{n}=(n_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{X}},\mathbf{k}=(k_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\in\mathbf{N}
, the two definitions are equivalent to the formal identity

\pi^{z}[g\ast h]=\pi^{z}[g]\cdot\pi^{z}[h].

(6)

In particular, the
\ast
-operation is also associative, commutative and
\mathbb{A}
-bilinear.

More broadly, if
L
is an arbitrary finite set and
g_{l}:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
for every
l\in L
, the convolution
\mathop{\scalebox{2}{\raisebox{-1.18399pt}{$\ast$}}}_{l\in L}g_{l}:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
is given by

\mathbf{n}\mapsto\sum_{(\mathbf{k}^{(l)})_{l\in L}\in\mathbf{N}^{L}}\binom{\mathbf{n}}{(\mathbf{k}^{(l)})_{l\in L}}\prod_{l\in L}g_{l}(\mathbf{k}^{(l)})\quad\text{or}\quad\mathbf{x}\mapsto\sum_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathfrak{P}_{J}^{(L)}}\prod_{l\in L}g_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{P_{l}})

with
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}
or finitely indexed
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{X}^{J}
, respectively, where

\displaystyle\binom{\mathbf{n}}{(\mathbf{k}^{(l)})_{l\in L}}:=\prod_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\binom{n_{x}}{(k^{(l)}_{x})_{l\in L}}=\frac{\mathbf{n}!}{\prod_{l\in L}\mathbf{k}^{(l)}!}{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{\sum_{l\in L}\mathbf{k}^{(l)}=\mathbf{n}\}}

denotes the multivariate multinomial coefficient and

\mathfrak{P}_{J}^{(L)}:=\{{\textstyle\mathbf{P}\in\{S\subset J\}^{L}\mid J=\bigsqcup_{L\in L}P_{l}}\}

the set of
L
-indexed partitions
of
J
into pairwise disjoint but
possibly empty
subsets.
In the above situation, we likewise formally obtain

\pi^{z}[{\textstyle\mathop{\scalebox{2}{\raisebox{-1.18399pt}{$\ast$}}}_{l\in L}g_{l}}]=\prod_{l\in L}\pi^{z}[g_{l}].

(7)

If
g_{l}=g:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
for every
l\in L
, we also write
g^{\ast|L|}=\mathop{\scalebox{2}{\raisebox{-1.18399pt}{$\ast$}}}_{l\in L}g_{l}
.
To include the case
L=\varnothing
, we define the empty convolution as
1_{\ast}:={\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{0\}}:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
, the
\ast
-identity element.

Note also, that for every finite set
L
and all
g:\mathbf{N}^{L}\to\mathbb{A}
, we can define

G:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A},\quad\mathbf{n}\mapsto\sum_{(\mathbf{k}^{(l)})_{l\in L}\in\mathbf{N}^{L}}\binom{\mathbf{n}}{(\mathbf{k}^{(l)})_{l\in L}}g((\mathbf{k}^{(l)})_{l\in L}),

(8)

i.e.,
G=\sum_{(\mathbf{k}^{(l)})_{l\in L}\in\mathbf{N}^{L}}g((\mathbf{k}^{(l)})_{l\in L})\mathop{\scalebox{2}{\raisebox{-1.18399pt}{$\ast$}}}_{l\in L}{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{\mathbf{k}^{(l)}\}}
, and formally, by (
7
),

\pi^{z}[G]=\sum_{(\mathbf{k}^{(l)})_{l\in L}\in\mathbf{N}^{L}}\left(\prod_{l\in L}\frac{z^{\mathbf{k}^{(l)}}}{\mathbf{k}^{(l)}!}\right)g((\mathbf{k}^{(l)})_{l\in L}).

(9)

With the implicit measure-theoretic conventions, monotone and dominated convergence yield the following.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
z:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{A}
,
L
a finite set,
g:\mathbf{N}^{L}\to\mathbb{A}
and
G:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
given by (
8
).
Then we have the following:

(i)

If
\mathbb{A}=\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, then (
9
) holds unconditionally as an identity in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

(ii)

If
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{C}
, then (
9
) holds as an identity in
\mathbb{C}
whenever

\sum_{(\mathbf{k}^{(l)})_{l\in L}\in\mathbf{N}^{L}}\left(\prod_{l\in L}\frac{|z|^{\mathbf{k}^{(l)}}}{\mathbf{k}^{(l)}!}\right)|g((\mathbf{k}^{(l)})_{l\in L})|<\infty.

3.2.
Convolution power series as coefficients

Given
g:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
with
g(0)=0
, we can define the
\ast
-exponential

\mathrm{e}^{\ast g}:=\exp_{\ast}g:=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{g^{\ast m}}{m!}:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}

since, for every finitely indexed tuple
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{X}^{J}
, we have

\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{g^{\ast m}}{m!}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathfrak{P}_{J}^{(\{1,\ldots,m\})}}\prod_{l=1}^{m}g(\mathbf{x}_{P_{l}})=\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}_{J}}\prod_{P\in\mathfrak{p}}g(\mathbf{x}_{P}),

the last sum and product having only finite index sets.
More explicitly,
g(0)=0
makes the inner sum in the middle effectively run over ordered partitions of
J
into
m
non-empty subsets and discarding the ordering cancels the factor
1/m!
.
Convergence being a non-issue, the usual argument yields

\mathrm{e}^{\ast\sum_{l\in L}g_{l}}=\mathop{\scalebox{2}{\raisebox{-1.18399pt}{$\ast$}}}_{l\in L}\mathrm{e}^{\ast g_{l}}

whenever
L
is a finite index set and
g_{l}:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
with
g_{l}(0)=0
for all
l\in L
.

If, e.g.,
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{C}
, then, by the same token, we can define the
\ast
-logarithm

\log_{\ast}h=\log_{\ast}(1_{\ast}+g):=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{(-1)^{m-1}g^{\ast m}}{m}:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}

and the
\ast
-reciprocal
, defined via the geometric series,

h^{\ast-1}=(1_{\ast}+g)^{\ast-1}:=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}(-1)^{m}g^{\ast m}:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}

of
h=1_{\ast}+g:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
with
h(0)=1
.
Again, all relevant sums implicitly run over finite index sets when evaluating on some
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}
so the usual arguments also yield the expected behaviour:

\log_{\ast}\mathrm{e}^{\ast g}=g,\quad\mathrm{e}^{\ast\log_{\ast}h}=h\quad\text{and}\quad(h^{\ast-1})\ast h=h\ast(h^{\ast-1})=1_{\ast}

as well as

\log_{\ast}({\textstyle\mathop{\scalebox{2}{\raisebox{-1.18399pt}{$\ast$}}}_{l\in L}h_{l}})=\sum_{l\in L}\log_{\ast}h_{l}\quad\text{and}\quad\left(\mathop{\scalebox{2}{\raisebox{-1.18399pt}{$\ast$}}}_{l\in L}h_{l}\right)^{\ast-1}=\mathop{\scalebox{2}{\raisebox{-1.18399pt}{$\ast$}}}_{l\in L}h_{l}^{\ast-1}

whenever
L
is a finite index set and
h_{l}:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
with
h_{l}(0)=1
for all
l\in L
.
Note, that for every finitely indexed
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{X}^{J}
with
J\neq\varnothing
, we explicitly obtain

(\log_{\ast}h)(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}_{J}}(-1)^{|\mathfrak{p}|-1}(|\mathfrak{p}|-1)!\prod_{P\in\mathfrak{p}}h(\mathbf{x}_{P}),

a known Möbius inversion formula along partitions, following the same argument as above.

Since the above series, with
g:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
,
g(0)=0
and
h=1_{\ast}+g
, are pointwise finite sums, (
7
) formally yields

\pi^{z}[\mathrm{e}^{\ast g}]=\pi^{z}[\exp_{\ast}g]=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{\pi^{z}[g]^{m}}{m!}=\exp(\pi^{z}[g])=\mathrm{e}^{\pi^{z}[g]}

and, provided
\mathbb{A}
is suitable, also

\pi^{z}[\log_{\ast}h]=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{(-1)^{m-1}\pi^{z}[g]^{m}}{m}=\log(1+\pi^{z}[g])=\log(\pi^{z}[h])

and
\pi^{z}[h^{\ast-1}]=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}(-1)^{m}\pi^{z}[g]^{m}=(1+\pi^{z}[g])^{-1}=\pi^{z}[h]^{-1}
.
The validity of the latter identities upon evaluation over
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{C}
is mediated by the case
\mathbb{A}=\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
via monotone and dominated convergence and Lemma
3.1
.
Treating the first of the above convolution power series
is quite straightforward, given the exponential series’s positive coefficients and infinite radius of convergence over
\mathbb{C}
as well as the convention
\mathrm{e}^{+\infty}=+\infty
.
For the logarithmic and reciprocal/geometric cases, one needs to observe that

\log(1\pm\zeta)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{(\mp 1)^{m-1}\zeta^{m}}{m}\quad\text{and}\quad(1\pm\zeta)^{-1}=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}(\mp 1)^{m}\zeta^{m}

as well-behaved identities in
\mathbb{C}
necessitate
|\zeta|<1
.

3.3.
Derivation via shifting

By viewing
\pi^{z}[g]=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}}\frac{z^{\mathbf{n}}}{\mathbf{n}!}g(\mathbf{n})
, for given
g:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
, as an abstract Taylor series, one effortlessly sees that

\frac{\partial}{\partial z(x)}\pi^{z}[g]=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}}\frac{z^{\mathbf{n}}}{\mathbf{n}!}g(\delta_{x}+\mathbf{n})=\pi^{z}[D^{x}g]

formally holds with
D^{x}g:=g(\delta_{x}+\bullet)
for every
x\in\mathbb{X}
.
The Leibniz product rule for the partial derivatives on the left-hand side directly translates into

D^{x}(g\ast h)=(D^{x}g)\ast h+g\ast(D^{x}h)

(10)

for arbitrary
x\in\mathbb{X}
and
g,h:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
.
More generally, for every
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbf{N}
and
g:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
, we can take formal derivatives of higher-order to obtain

\frac{\partial^{|\mathbf{m}|}}{(\partial z)^{\mathbf{m}}}\pi^{z}[g]=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}}\frac{z^{\mathbf{n}}}{\mathbf{n}!}g(\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{n})=\pi^{z}[D^{\mathbf{m}}g]

with
D^{\mathbf{m}}g:=g(\mathbf{m}+\bullet)
and, given a finite set
L
and
g_{l}:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
for every
l\in L
, (
10
) generalises to

D^{\mathbf{m}}\mathop{\scalebox{2}{\raisebox{-1.18399pt}{$\ast$}}}_{l\in L}g_{l}=\sum_{(\mathbf{k}^{(l)})_{l\in L}\in\mathbf{N}^{L}}\binom{\mathbf{m}}{(\mathbf{k}^{(l)})_{l\in L}}\mathop{\scalebox{2}{\raisebox{-1.18399pt}{$\ast$}}}_{l\in L}D^{\mathbf{k}^{(l)}}g_{l}.

(11)

Note that the latter can be read as a convolution formula with respect to
\mathbf{m}
if
\mathbb{A}
and its innate multiplication are replaced by
\{g:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}\}
and
\ast
, respectively, and that substituting a finitely indexed tuple
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{X}^{J}
for
\mathbf{m}
would allow writing the right-hand side of (
11
) as a sum over
\mathfrak{P}_{J}^{(L)}
.

Of particular relevance is the interaction of this derivation with
\ast
-exponentials and
\ast
-logarithms.
Namely, let
g:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
with
g(0)=0
and verify that
D^{x}\mathrm{e}^{\ast g}=\mathrm{e}^{\ast g}\ast(D^{x}g)
for every
x\in\mathbb{X}
and, more generally,

D^{\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{e}^{\ast g}=\mathrm{e}^{\ast g}\ast\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}_{J}}\mathop{\scalebox{2}{\raisebox{-1.18399pt}{$\ast$}}}_{P\in\mathfrak{p}}D^{\mathbf{x}_{P}}g

(12)

for every finitely indexed tuple
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{X}^{J}
.
If, e.g.,
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{C}
and
h=1_{\ast}+g
, analogous instances of the chain rule and Faà di Bruno’s formula include
D^{x}\log_{\ast}h=h^{\ast-1}\ast(D^{x}h)
for every
x\in\mathbb{X}
as well as

D^{\mathbf{x}}\log_{\ast}h=\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}_{J}}(-1)^{|\mathfrak{p}|-1}(|\mathfrak{p}|-1)!\mathop{\scalebox{2}{\raisebox{-1.18399pt}{$\ast$}}}_{P\in\mathfrak{p}}(h^{\ast-1}\ast D^{\mathbf{x}_{P}}h)

(13)

whenever
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{X}^{J}
with non-empty finite index set
J
.

The derivation operators also provide us with a nice way of framing the superposition principle, which essentially says that Poisson distributions form a measure-theoretic convolution semigroup with respect to the addition of their intensities.
Given
g:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
, we collect the values

\Pi^{z}[g](\mathbf{m}):=\pi^{z}[D^{\mathbf{m}}g],\quad\mathbf{m}\in\mathbf{N},

into a formally well-defined function
\Pi^{z}[g]
that, by (
11
) and (
7
), satisfies

\Pi^{z}[{\textstyle\mathop{\scalebox{2}{\raisebox{-1.18399pt}{$\ast$}}}_{l\in L}g_{l}}]=\mathop{\scalebox{2}{\raisebox{-1.18399pt}{$\ast$}}}_{l\in L}\Pi^{z}[g_{l}]

whenever
L
is a finite set and
g_{l}:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
for all
l\in L
.
Analogously, (
12
) formally yields

\Pi^{z}[\mathrm{e}^{\ast g}]=\mathrm{e}^{\pi^{z}[g]}\cdot\exp_{\ast}(\Pi^{z}[g]{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\mathbf{N}\setminus\{0\}})

whenever
g:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
with
g(0)=0
, and, if, additionally,
\mathbb{A}
is suitable and
h=1_{\ast}+g
, then (
13
) translates into

\Pi^{z}[\log_{\ast}h]=\log(\pi^{z}[h])\cdot 1_{\ast}+\log_{\ast}(\pi^{z}[h]^{-1}\cdot\Pi^{z}[h]).

The aforementioned superposition principle can now be phrased as follows:
if
\tilde{z}=(\tilde{z}(x))_{x\in\mathbb{X}}
is another collection of abstract variables commuting among each other and with those in
z
, then

\Pi^{z+\tilde{z}}[g](\mathbf{m})=\Pi^{z}[\Pi^{\tilde{z}}[g]](\mathbf{m})

(14)

formally holds for all
g:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
,
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbf{N}
.
For proving the latter identity, it suffices to consider the case
\mathbf{m}=0
since clearly
\Pi^{z}[g](\mathbf{m})=\Pi^{z}[D^{\mathbf{m}}g](0)
by definition.
For
\mathbf{m}=0
, (
14
) reads

\pi^{z+\tilde{z}}[g]=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{N}}\frac{(z+\tilde{z})^{\mathbf{n}}}{\mathbf{n}!}g(\mathbf{n})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{N}}\frac{z^{\mathbf{k}}}{\mathbf{k}!}\sum_{\tilde{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbf{N}}\frac{\tilde{z}^{\tilde{\mathbf{k}}}}{\tilde{\mathbf{k}}!}g(\mathbf{k}+\tilde{\mathbf{k}})=\pi^{z}[\Pi^{\tilde{z}}[g]]

and easily follows from the multivariate binomial formula

(z+\tilde{z})^{\bullet}=z^{\bullet}\ast\tilde{z}^{\bullet}:\mathbf{n}\mapsto(z+\tilde{z})^{\mathbf{n}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{N}}\binom{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{k}}z^{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{z}^{\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{k}}.

As usual, monotone and dominated convergence allow for according identities after evaluating in the cases
\mathbb{A}=\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{C}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
z,\tilde{z}:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{A}
,
g:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbf{N}
.
Then we have the following:

(i)

If
\mathbb{A}=\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, then (
14
) holds unconditionally as an identity in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

(ii)

If
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{C}
, then (
14
) holds as an identity in
\mathbb{C}
whenever

\Pi^{|z|+|\tilde{z}|}[|g|](\mathbf{m})=\Pi^{|z|}[\Pi^{|\tilde{z}|}[|g|]](\mathbf{m})<\infty.

As indicated above, the binomial formula is a special instance of our Poisson convolution so the superposition principle, here (
14
) / Lemma
3.2
, and (
9
) / Lemma
3.1
turn out to be two sides of the same coin.

4.
General correlations and the Kirkwood–Salsburg hierarchy

We extend the potential
V
, the Boltzmann factor
\kappa
and the Ursell function
\varphi
to functions on
\mathbf{N}\times\mathbf{N}
, where the second argument, readily omitted if equal to the mutually identified vacuum configurations
0\in\mathbf{N}
,
()\in\mathbb{X}^{\varnothing}=\mathbb{X}^{0}
or
\varnothing\in\mathbf{F}
, is usually called the
boundary condition
.
Namely, let

V:\mathbf{N}\times\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{C}\cup\{+\infty\},\quad(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{b})\mapsto V(\mathbf{n}\mid\mathbf{b}):=\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbf{N}}\binom{\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{c}}V(\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{c}),

i.e.,
V(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})=\sum_{S\subset K}V(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}_{S})
for all finitely indexed tuples
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{X}^{J}
,
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{X}^{K}
, where the sum of tuples corresponds to their concatenation. The conditional Boltzmann factor is then

\kappa:\mathbf{N}\times\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{C},\quad(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{b})\mapsto\kappa(\mathbf{n}\mid\mathbf{b}):=\mathrm{e}^{-\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{N}\setminus\{0\}}\binom{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{k}}V(\mathbf{k}\mid\mathbf{b})}

An important observation here is the
conditional multiplicativity

\kappa(\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{n}\mid\mathbf{b})=\kappa(\mathbf{m}\mid\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b})\kappa(\mathbf{n}\mid\mathbf{b})

(15)

for all
\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbf{N}
.
Note also that, for all
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbf{N}
, we have
\kappa(0\mid\mathbf{b})=1
and can therefore extend and rewrite (
1
) in the forms

\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})=\exp_{\ast}\varphi(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b}):=\log_{\ast}\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b}),

(16)

where
\ast
-exponential and
\ast
-logarithm only act on the respective unspecified first arguments here and the second identity is used as the definition of the conditional Ursell function
\varphi:\mathbf{N}\times\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{C}
.

Recall that we assume simplicity of
V
, meaning
\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})
vanishes outside

\mathbf{F}_{\mid\mathbf{b}}:=\{X\Subset\mathbb{X}\mid\mathbf{b}[X]=0\}\subset\mathbf{F}_{\mid 0}=\mathbf{F}\subset\mathbf{N}

for any fixed
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbf{N}
, i.e., that
\kappa(x\mid x)=\mathrm{e}^{-V(x)-V(2\delta_{x})}=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
.

4.1.
Convolutional Boltzmann factors

For all
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbf{N}
, the
\ast
-reciprocal of
\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})=1_{\ast}+\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b}){\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\mathbf{N}\setminus\{0\}}
exists and we can also define

\psi(\mathbf{m},\bullet\mid\mathbf{b}):=\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})^{\ast-1}\ast D^{\mathbf{m}}\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})

for every
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbf{N}
, yielding a trivariate function
\psi:\mathbf{N}\times\mathbf{N}\times\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{C}
.
Not only is
\psi(0,\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})=1_{\ast}
but (
15
) extends to the convolutional version

\psi(\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{n},\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})=\psi(\mathbf{m},\bullet\mid\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b})\ast\psi(\mathbf{n},\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})

(17)

for any given
\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbf{N}
.
To see this, use (
15
) twice to write

\psi(\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{n},\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})=\kappa(\mathbf{m}\mid\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b})\kappa(\mathbf{n}\mid\mathbf{b})\cdot\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b})\ast\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})^{\ast-1},

convolve with
1_{\ast}=\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b})^{\ast-1}\ast\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b})
and again use (
15
) twice as

\kappa(\mathbf{m}\mid\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b})\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b})=D^{\mathbf{m}}\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b})

and
\kappa(\mathbf{n}\mid\mathbf{b})\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b})=D^{\mathbf{n}}\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})
.

Next, we relate
\psi
and
\varphi
.
Using (
16
) in (
12
), we obtain

\displaystyle\psi(\mathbf{x},\bullet\mid\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{y})^{\ast-1}\ast\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{y})\ast\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}_{J}}\mathop{\scalebox{2}{\raisebox{-1.18399pt}{$\ast$}}}_{P\in\mathfrak{p}}D^{\mathbf{x}_{P}}\varphi(\bullet\mid\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}_{J}}\mathop{\scalebox{2}{\raisebox{-1.18399pt}{$\ast$}}}_{P\in\mathfrak{p}}D^{\mathbf{x}_{P}}\varphi(\bullet\mid\mathbf{y})

for all finitely indexed tuples
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{X}^{J}
,
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{X}^{K}
and, if additionally
J\neq\varnothing
, plugging (
16
) into (
13
) yields the Möbius inverse formula

\displaystyle D^{\mathbf{x}}\varphi(\bullet\mid\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}_{J}}(-1)^{|\mathfrak{p}|-1}(|\mathfrak{p}|-1)!\mathop{\scalebox{2}{\raisebox{-1.18399pt}{$\ast$}}}_{P\in\mathfrak{p}}(\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{y})^{\ast-1}\ast D^{\mathbf{x}_{P}}\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{y}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}_{J}}(-1)^{|\mathfrak{p}|-1}(|\mathfrak{p}|-1)!\mathop{\scalebox{2}{\raisebox{-1.18399pt}{$\ast$}}}_{P\in\mathfrak{p}}\psi(\mathbf{x}_{P},\bullet\mid\mathbf{y}).

(18)

In particular, for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbf{N}
, we have

\psi(\delta_{x},\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})=D^{x}\varphi(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})

(19)

and, by simplicity of
V
, (
18
) yields

D^{m\delta_{x}}\varphi(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})=(-1)^{m-1}(m-1)!\psi(\delta_{x},\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})^{\ast m}.

(20)

4.2.
Controlling partition functions and Mayer series via effective activities

Given
\mathbf{m},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbf{N}
, we now formally define

\displaystyle Z(\mathbf{m},z\mid\mathbf{b})

\displaystyle:=\pi^{z}[D^{\mathbf{m}}\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})]=\Pi^{z}[\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})](\mathbf{m}),

\displaystyle M(\mathbf{m},z\mid\mathbf{b})

\displaystyle:=\pi^{z}[D^{\mathbf{m}}\varphi(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})]=\Pi^{z}[\varphi(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})](\mathbf{m}),

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbf{m},z\mid\mathbf{b})

\displaystyle:=\pi^{z}[\psi(\mathbf{m},\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})]

and call these the
\mathbf{m}
-rooted
\mathbf{b}
-conditional partition function, Mayer series and correlation, respectively.
Independently of one another, we may notationally suppress not only the boundary condition
\mathbf{b}=0
but also the root configuration
\mathbf{m}=0
.
In particular, Section
2
is consistent with

Z(z)=Z(0,z\mid 0),\quad M(z)=M(0,z\mid 0)\quad\text{and}\quad\rho(x,z)=\rho(\delta_{x},z\mid 0)

for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
, where we note (
19
).
Likewise, we consistently set

Z_{\Lambda}(\mathbf{m},z\mid\mathbf{b}):=Z(\mathbf{m},z{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}\mid\mathbf{b}),\quad M_{\Lambda}(\mathbf{m},z\mid\mathbf{b}):=M(\mathbf{m},z{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}\mid\mathbf{b})

and
\rho_{\Lambda}(\mathbf{m},z\mid\mathbf{b}):=\rho(\mathbf{m},z{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}\mid\mathbf{b})
for every
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{X}
.

Building on the relations sketched before Theorem
2.1
, we extend a given
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{X}
by some
x\in\mathbb{X}\setminus\Lambda
and express the changes in partition function and Mayer series in terms of the effective activity
\widehat{z}_{\Lambda}(x,z):=z(x)\rho_{\Lambda}(x,z)
.
Boundary conditions are straightforward to include, hence omitted here.
We use (
14
) to formally equate
Z_{\Lambda\cup\{x\}}(z)=\pi^{z{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{x\}}+z{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}}[\kappa]
with

\pi^{z{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{x\}}}[\Pi^{z{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}}[\kappa]]=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{z(x)^{m}}{m!}Z_{\Lambda}(m\delta_{x},z)=Z_{\Lambda}(z)+z(x)Z_{\Lambda}(x,z),

where the last equality is due to
V
’s simplicity.
If, for the last term, we then insert
Z_{\Lambda}(x,z)=\pi^{z{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}}[\kappa\ast\psi(x,\bullet)]=Z_{\Lambda}(z)\rho_{\Lambda}(x,z)
, see (
6
), to arrive at

Z_{\Lambda\cup\{x\}}(z)=Z_{\Lambda}(z)(1+\widehat{z}_{\Lambda}(x,z)).

(21)

To derive the logarithmic variant of the latter,
M_{\Lambda\cup\{x\}}(z)=\pi^{z}[\varphi{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\mathbf{N}(\Lambda\cup\{x\})}]
is formally decomposed into the sum of
\pi^{z}[\varphi{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\mathbf{N}(\Lambda)}]=M_{\Lambda}(z)
and

\pi^{z}[\varphi{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\mathbf{N}(\Lambda\cup\{x\})\setminus\mathbf{N}(\Lambda)}]=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{z(x)^{m}}{m!}M_{\Lambda}(m\delta_{x},z),

more or less using (
14
) again.
Simplicity of
V
, through (
20
) and (
7
), gives

M_{\Lambda}(m\delta_{x},z)=\pi^{z{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}}[D^{m\delta_{x}}\varphi]=(-1)^{m-1}(m-1)!\rho_{\Lambda}(x,z)^{m}

for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
In total, we obtain the formal identity

\pi^{z}[\varphi{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\mathbf{N}(\Lambda\cup\{x\})\setminus\mathbf{N}(\Lambda)}]=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{(-1)^{m-1}\widehat{z}_{\Lambda}(x,z)^{m}}{m},

(22)

the latter sum representing the logarithm of the factor
1+\widehat{z}_{\Lambda}(x,z)
in (
21
).

4.3.
Controlling correlations and the Kirkwood–Salsburg hierarchy

Given
\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbf{N}
, applying (
6
) to (
17
) formally yields

\rho(\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{n},z\mid\mathbf{b})=\rho(\mathbf{m},z\mid\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b})\rho(\mathbf{n},z\mid\mathbf{b}),

(23)

in addition to the unconditional identity
\rho(0,z\mid\mathbf{b})=1
.
By comparison with (
15
), we can view the correlation
\rho(\mathbf{m},z\mid\mathbf{b})
as an expansion around the Boltzmann factor
\kappa(\mathbf{m}\mid\mathbf{b})=\psi(\mathbf{m},0\mid\mathbf{b})=\rho(\mathbf{m},0\mid\mathbf{b})
as such.

We combine the latter insight with the
Kirkwood–Salsburg hierarchy
, deduced as follows.
The binomial formula

1_{\ast}={\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{0\}}:\mathbf{n}\mapsto(1-1)^{\mathbf{n}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{N}}\binom{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{k}}(-1)^{\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{k}}=(1\ast(-1)^{\bullet})(\mathbf{n}),

yields
1^{\ast-1}=(-1)^{\bullet}=(-1)^{|\bullet|}
as the

-reciprocal of the constant function
1={\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\mathbf{N}}
.
Another trivariate function
\gamma:\mathbf{N}\times\mathbf{N}\times\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{C}
is then given by

\gamma(\mathbf{m},\bullet\mid\mathbf{b}):=\kappa(\mathbf{m}\mid\bullet+\mathbf{b})\ast 1^{\ast-1}=\kappa(\mathbf{m}\mid\bullet+\mathbf{b})\ast(-1)^{|\bullet|}

for all
\mathbf{m},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbf{N}
.
Given

\kappa(\mathbf{m}\mid\mathbf{n}+\bullet+\mathbf{b})D^{\mathbf{n}}\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})=(\gamma(\mathbf{m},\bullet\mid\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b})\ast 1)D^{\mathbf{n}}\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b}),

which, upon convolution with
\kappa(\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})^{\ast-1}
and rearranging, gives

\psi(\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{n},\bullet\mid\mathbf{b})=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathbf{N}}\binom{\bullet}{(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})}\gamma(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{k}\mid\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b})\psi(\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{b}).

(24)

If we apply (

\rho(\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{n},z\mid\mathbf{b})=\pi^{z}[\gamma(\mathbf{m},\bullet\mid\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{b})\rho(\mathbf{n}+\bullet,z\mid\mathbf{b})].

(25)

]

The usual Picard iteration can also be framed as a device to inductively control the dominating absolute series as in, e.g.,

4.4.
Conditional hypergraphs

For the diagrammatic viewpoint, we use the conditional Mayer function
f:=\mathrm{e}^{-V}-1:\mathbf{N}\times\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{C}
to write

\kappa(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{H}_{J}}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{h}}f(\mathbf{x}_{e}\mid\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{H}_{J\mid K}}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{h}}f(\mathbf{x}_{e\cap J}+\mathbf{y}_{e\cap K})

for arbitrary finitely indexed tuples
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{X}^{J}
,
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{X}^{K}
.
Given implicitly disjoint sets
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{V}
, the conditional hypergraph notation means

\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}}:=\{\mathfrak{h}\subset\{e\Subset\mathbb{U}\sqcup\mathbb{V}\mid e\cap\mathbb{U}\neq\varnothing\}\},

i.e.,
\mathbb{V}
consists of purely auxiliary vertices unable to support edges alone.
We do not use the diagrammatic representations of
\varphi
or
\psi
, cf.
[
15
]
, but that of
\gamma
is essential to our argument.
Let
x\in\mathbb{X}
in addition to the finitely indexed tuples
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{X}^{J}
,
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{X}^{K}
.
Then
\kappa(x\mid\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})
, on the one hand, equals

\sum_{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{H}_{\{0\}\mid J}}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{h}}f(x+\mathbf{x}_{e\setminus\{0\}}\mid\mathbf{y})=\kappa(x\mid\mathbf{y})\sum_{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{H}_{J}}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{h}}f(x+\mathbf{x}_{e}\mid\mathbf{y})

and, by definition of
\gamma
, it also coincides with
\sum_{S\subset J}\gamma(x,\mathbf{x}_{S}\mid\mathbf{y})
.
A diagrammatic solution is provided by tracking the set
\mathrm{span}(\mathfrak{h}):=\bigcup_{e\in\mathfrak{h}}e
for an arbitrary hypergraph
\mathfrak{h}
since, for a given vertex set
\mathbb{V}
, we clearly have

\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{V}}=\bigsqcup_{S\subset\mathbb{V}}\mathfrak{S}_{S}\quad\text{with}\quad\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{V}}:=\{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{V}}\mid\mathrm{span}(\mathfrak{h})=\mathbb{V}\}.

Comparison with the above representations of
\kappa(x\mid\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})
leads to

\gamma(x,\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})=\kappa(x\mid\mathbf{y})\sum_{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{S}_{J}}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{h}}f(x+\mathbf{x}_{e}\mid\mathbf{y}).

The latter expression can also be expanded further into

\gamma(x,\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})=\kappa(x\mid\mathbf{y})\sum_{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{S}_{J\mid K}}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{h}}f(x+\mathbf{x}_{e\cap J}+\mathbf{y}_{e\cap K}),

(27)

with
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}}:=\{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}}\mid\mathrm{span}(\mathfrak{h})\supset\mathbb{U}\}
for arbitrary sets
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{V}
.
If
V=V{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{|\bullet|\leq 2\}}
or equivalently
f=\mathrm{e}^{-V}-1=f{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{|\bullet|\leq 2\}}
, the latter reads

\gamma(x,\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})=\kappa(x\mid\mathbf{y})\prod_{j\in J}f(x+x_{j})

and is straightforward to bound, cf.
[
25
,
29
,
21
,
7

2
,

]
to name a few.
Bounding the

summands in

individually is also an option, see
[

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V}
be finite sets.
There exist maps
\mathfrak{m}:\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}}\to\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}}
and
\mathfrak{M}:\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}})\to\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}}
such that, for all
\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}})
, we have

\{\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}}\mid\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})=\mathfrak{h}\}=\{\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}}\mid\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}\subset\mathfrak{M}(\mathfrak{h})\}.

In particular, such maps yield

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}}=\bigsqcup_{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}})}\{\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}}\mid\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}\subset\mathfrak{M}(\mathfrak{h})\}.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary total order
\preceq
on
\{e\Subset\mathbb{U}\sqcup\mathbb{V}\mid e\cap\mathbb{U}\neq\varnothing\}
, denoting
\prec
the version that excludes equality.
For each
\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}}
, we define

\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{h}):=\{e\in\mathfrak{h}\mid e\cap\mathbb{U}\not\subset\mathrm{span}(\{e^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{h}\mid e^{\prime}\prec e\})\}.

and it is easy to see that
\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{h})
can be assembled iteratively by considering the edges of
\mathfrak{h}
in ascending order, that

\mathrm{span}(\{e^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{h}\mid e^{\prime}\prec e\})\cap\mathbb{U}=\mathrm{span}(\{e^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{h})\mid e^{\prime}\prec e\})\cap\mathbb{U}

for all
e\in\mathfrak{h}
and that indeed
\mathrm{span}(\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{h}))\cap\mathbb{U}=\mathrm{span}(\mathfrak{h})\cap\mathbb{U}=\mathbb{U}
.
We thereby obtain a map
\mathfrak{m}:\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}}\to\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}}
with the image

\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}})=\{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}}\mid\forall e\in\mathfrak{h}:e\cap\mathbb{U}\not\subset\mathrm{span}(\{e^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{h}\mid e^{\prime}\prec e\})\}

and, for all
\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}})
and
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{U}\mid\mathbb{V}}
, we have
\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})=\mathfrak{h}
if and only if

\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}\subset\mathfrak{h}\sqcup\{e\Subset\mathbb{U}\sqcup\mathbb{V}\mid\varnothing\neq e\cap\mathbb{U}\subset\mathrm{span}(\{e^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{h}\mid e^{\prime}\prec e\})\}=:\mathfrak{M}(\mathfrak{h}).

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.1

5.2.
Proof of Theorem
2.1
- derivation of the criterion

We now utilise Lemma
4.1
to insert an intermediate bound into the the hypothesis of Lemma
5.1
.
Given
x\in\mathbb{X}
and
B,Y\Subset\mathbb{X}\setminus\{x\}
with

, (
27
) yields

\gamma(x,Y\mid B)=\kappa(x\mid B)\sum_{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{S}_{Y\mid B}}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{h}}f(x+e).

We bound the sum over
\mathfrak{S}_{Y\mid B}
by first expanding it via

\sum_{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{S}_{Y\mid B}}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{h}}f(x+e)=\sum_{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{S}_{Y\mid B})}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{h}}f(x+e)\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{M}(\mathfrak{h})\setminus\mathfrak{h}}(1+f(x+e)),

where
\mathfrak{m}:\mathfrak{S}_{Y\mid B}\to\mathfrak{S}_{Y\mid B}
and
\mathfrak{M}:\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{S}_{Y\mid B})\to\mathfrak{S}_{Y\mid B}
are the functions from the proof of Lemma
4.1
with
\mathbb{U}=Y
and
\mathbb{V}=B
.
Writing
1+f=\mathrm{e}^{-V}
and multiplying by
\kappa(x\mid B)=\mathrm{e}^{-V(x\mid B)}=\prod_{e\subset B}\mathrm{e}^{-V(x+e)}
gives

\gamma(x,Y\mid B)=\prod_{e\subset B}\mathrm{e}^{-V(x+e)}\sum_{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{S}_{Y\mid B})}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{h}}f(x+e)\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{M}(\mathfrak{h})\setminus\mathfrak{h}}\mathrm{e}^{-V(x+e)}.

If we then take absolute values and multiply by
\alpha^{Y}
, we get

\displaystyle|\gamma(x,Y\mid B)|\alpha^{Y}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\prod_{e\subset B}|\mathrm{e}^{-V(x+e)}|\sum_{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{S}_{Y\mid B})}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{h}}|f(x+e)|\alpha^{Y\cap e\setminus\mathrm{span}(\mathfrak{h}_{\prec e})}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{M}(\mathfrak{h})\setminus\mathfrak{h}}|\mathrm{e}^{-V(x+e)}|

with
\mathfrak{h}_{\prec e}=\{e\in\mathfrak{h}\mid e^{\prime}\prec e\}
and

\alpha^{Y\cap e\setminus\mathrm{span}(\mathfrak{h}_{\prec e})}\leq\max\{\alpha^{S}\mid\varnothing\neq S\subset e\setminus B\}
for each
\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{S}_{Y\mid B})
and
e\in\mathfrak{h}
.
For every
e\Subset\mathbb{X}\setminus\{x\}
, we then use the shorthand

\mathrm{m}(x,e\mid B):=\max\{|\mathrm{e}^{-V(x+e)}|,1+|f(x+e)|\alpha^{S}\mid\varnothing\neq S\subset e\setminus B\}

and note that
\mathrm{m}(x,e\mid B)\geq 1
whenever
e\setminus B\neq\varnothing
so the above implies

\displaystyle|\gamma(x,Y\mid B)|\alpha^{Y}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\prod_{e\subset B}\mathrm{m}(x,e\mid B)\sum_{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{S}_{Y\mid B})}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{h}}(\mathrm{m}(x,e\mid B))-1)\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{M}(\mathfrak{h})}\mathrm{m}(x,e\mid B)

\displaystyle\quad=\prod_{e\subset B}\mathrm{m}(x,e\mid B)\sum_{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{S}_{Y\mid B}}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{h}}(\mathrm{m}(x,e\mid B))-1).

The last identity is a reverse application of Lemma
4.1
and summing over the possible choices of

nets us

\displaystyle\sum_{Y\Subset\mathbb{X}\setminus(\{x\}\cup B)}|\gamma(x,Y\mid B)|\alpha^{Y}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\prod_{e\subset B}\mathrm{m}(x,e\mid B)\sum_{Y\Subset\mathbb{X}\setminus(\{x\}\cup B)}\sum_{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{S}_{Y\mid B}}\prod_{e\in\mathfrak{h}}(\mathrm{m}(x,e\mid B))-1)

\displaystyle\quad=\prod_{e\Subset\mathbb{X}\setminus\{x\}}\mathrm{m}(x,e\mid B).

(32)

The last equality is based on equating
\bigsqcup_{Y\Subset\mathbb{X}\setminus(\{x\}\cup B)}\mathfrak{S}_{Y\mid B}
with

\{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{X}\setminus(\{x\}\cup B)\mid B}\mid|\mathrm{span}(\mathfrak{h})|<\infty\}=\{\mathfrak{h}\in\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{X}\setminus(\{x\}\cup B)\mid B}\mid|\mathfrak{h}|<\infty\},

noting again that
\mathrm{m}(x,e\mid B)\geq 1
for all
e\Subset\mathbb{X}\setminus\{x\}
with
e\setminus B\neq\varnothing
.

At this point, it is evident that all the hypotheses of Lemma
5.1
are simultaneously satisfied whenever
|z(x)|\prod_{e\Subset\mathbb{X}\setminus\{x\}}\mathrm{m}(x,e|\varnothing)\leq r(x)
, i.e.,

|z(x)|\prod_{e\Subset\mathbb{X}\setminus\{x\}}\max\{|\mathrm{e}^{-V(x+e)}|,1+|f(x+e)|\alpha^{S}\mid\varnothing\neq S\subset e\}\leq r(x),

for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
, which is precisely the hypothesis (
2
) of Theorem
2.1
, whose conclusions are, on the other hand, subsumed by those of Lemma
5.1
.

\square

|\widehat{z}_{\Lambda\setminus\{x\}}(x,z)|\leq\xi_{\Lambda\setminus\{x\}}(x)\leq r(x)<1,

(33)

for all
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{X}
and
x\in\mathbb{X}
.
Simple boundary conditions are easily included but omitted for better readability, just like in the derivations of (
21
) and (

), which the following arguments are based upon.

Suppose that
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{X}
and
x\in\mathbb{X}\setminus\Lambda
.
We reiterate the derivation of (
21
) but with absolute values inserted everywhere. Lemmas
3.2
and
3.1
thereby yield

\pi^{|z|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{x\}\cup\Lambda}}[|\kappa|]\leq\pi^{|z|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}}[|\kappa|](1+\xi_{\Lambda}(x))

and, starting from
\pi^{|z|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\varnothing}}=|\kappa(0)|=1=(1+r)^{\varnothing}
, we can inductively apply (
33
) to obtain
\pi^{|z|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}}[|\kappa|]\leq(1+r)^{\Lambda}
whenever
\Lambda
is finite and, for arbitrary
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{X}
, the latter bound then also follows by monotone convergence.
In particular, if
|Z_{\Lambda}(z)|\leq\pi^{|z|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}}[|\kappa|]<\infty
, the absolute values can be dropped to obtain

Z_{\{x\}\cup\Lambda}(z)=Z_{\Lambda}(z)(1+\widehat{z}_{\Lambda}(x,z))\in\mathbb{C}

as well as
|Z_{\Lambda}(z)|(1-r(x))\leq|Z_{\{x\}\cup\Lambda}(z)|\leq|Z_{\Lambda}(z)|(1+r(x))
.
Accordingly,

0<(1-r)^{\Lambda}\leq|Z_{\Lambda}(z)|\leq\pi^{|z|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}}[|\kappa|]\leq(1+r)^{\Lambda}<\infty

follows inductively if
\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{X}
and combining monotone and dominated convergence extends the result to the case of merely
r
-finite
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{X}
.

The mostly analogous argument for our Mayer series imitates the derivation of (

), where Lemmas
3.2
and
3.1
give

\pi^{|z|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\{x\}\cup\Lambda}}[|\varphi|]\leq\pi^{|z|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}}[|\varphi|]+\pi^{|z|}[|\varphi|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\mathbf{N}(\{x\}\cup\Lambda)\setminus\mathbf{N}(\Lambda)}]

with

\pi^{|z|}[|\varphi|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\mathbf{N}(\{x\}\cup\Lambda)\setminus\mathbf{N}(\Lambda)}]\leq\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\xi_{\Lambda}(x)^{m}}{m}\leq\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{r(x)^{m}}{m}=-\log(1-r(x))<\infty,

cf. (
33
).
In particular, this already justifies

\pi^{z}[\varphi{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\mathbf{N}(\{x\}\cup\Lambda)\setminus\mathbf{N}(\Lambda)}]=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{(-1)^{m-1}\widehat{z}_{\Lambda}(x,z)^{m}}{m}=\log(1+\widehat{z}(x,z))\in\mathbb{C}.

Sidestepping a proper induction argument, we now fix some total order
\preceq
on
\Lambda
and set
\Lambda_{\prec x}:=\{y\in\Lambda\mid y\prec x\}
for all
x\in\Lambda
, where
\prec
again denotes
\preceq
without equality.
Since clearly

\mathbf{N}(\Lambda)=\{0\}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{x\in\Lambda}(\mathbf{N}(\{x\}\cup\Lambda_{\prec x})\setminus\mathbf{N}(\Lambda_{\prec x}))

and
\pi^{|z|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\varnothing}}[|\varphi|]=|\varphi(0)|=0
, we have

\pi^{|z|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}}[|\varphi|]=\pi^{|z|}[|\varphi|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\mathbf{N}(\Lambda)}]=\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\pi^{|z|}[|\varphi|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\mathbf{N}(\{x\}\cup\Lambda_{\prec x})\setminus\mathbf{N}(\Lambda_{\prec x})}]

and bounding each summand according to the above yields

\pi^{|z|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}}[|\varphi|]\leq-\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\log(1-r(x))\leq-\log(1-r)[\Lambda].

Hence, if
\Lambda
is
r
-finite, then
|M_{\Lambda}(z)|\leq\pi^{|z|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}}[|\varphi|]\leq-\log(1-r)[\Lambda]<\infty
, so

|Z_{\Lambda}(z)|\leq\pi^{|z|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}}[|\kappa|]=\pi^{|z|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}}[|\exp_{\ast}\varphi|]\leq\mathrm{e}^{\pi^{|z|{\mathchoice{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.0mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-4.5mu\mathrm{l}}{1\mskip-5.0mu\mathrm{l}}}_{\Lambda}}(z)[|\varphi|]}<\infty

and Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
let us drop all absolute values to arrive at

Z_{\Lambda}(z)=\exp(M_{\Lambda}(z))=\prod_{x\in\Lambda}(1+\widehat{z}_{\Lambda_{\prec x}}(x,z))\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\},

the latter identities also yielding the bounds

0<(1-r)^{\Lambda}\leq|Z_{\Lambda}(1)|\leq(1+r)^{\Lambda}<\infty.

With this, the proof of Corollary
2.2
is complete.
