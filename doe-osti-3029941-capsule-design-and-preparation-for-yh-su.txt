 ABSTRACT

Advanced nuclear systems for terrestrial microreactors and space applications require moderator materials
with high thermal stability, hydrogen retention, and efficient neutron moderation. Yttrium hydride (YHx) is
a promising candidate, but its irradiation performance and hydrogen stability remain insufficiently
understood. To address this, Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) and Los Alamos National Laboratory,
supported by the US Department of Energy’s Nuclear Science User Facilities program, have initiated an
irradiation campaign in ORNL’s High Flux Isotope Reactor. The experiment employs six irradiation
capsules containing YHx specimens (H/Y ≈ 1.9) designed for three target temperatures (300°C, 400°C, and
500°C) and two distinct neutron fast fluence levels. This report documents the irradiation test matrix,
capsule design, specimen precharacterization, and experiment readiness for insertion. Planned
postirradiation examinations will provide new insights into hydrogen retention and microstructural stability,
advancing the understanding of YHx as a moderator for compact reactor applications.

 1. INTRODUCTION

The pursuit of compact, high-performance nuclear systems, whether for terrestrial microreactors or space
nuclear power applications, has highlighted the importance of advanced moderator materials with
exceptional thermal stability, hydrogen retention, and neutron moderation performance [1-3]. Among the
candidate materials, yttrium hydride (YHx) has emerged as a promising solid-state moderator due to its
favorable thermophysical properties, low neutron absorption cross section, and compatibility with high-
flux neutron environments [4]. Solid hydrides like YHx are efficient neutron moderators, offering both high
hydrogen density and structural integrity at elevated temperatures. However, under neutron irradiation,
hydride lattices are susceptible to displacement damage [5, 6]. Hydrogen atoms may be displaced from their
interstitial lattice positions, potentially forming extended defects (e.g., dislocation loops) that may
accelerate hydrogen loss. Understanding these degradation mechanisms is crucial for the reliable
deployment of hydride moderators in radiation-intense environments, such as those that will be present in
future compact space reactors.

A collaborative irradiation experiment testing between Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) and Oak
Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) was initiated under the Nuclear Science User Facilities program. This
effort is intended to provide a fundamental understanding of how YHx degrades under neutron irradiation,
particularly the mechanisms governing hydrogen retention and microstructural stability. ORNL’s High Flux
Isotope Reactor (HFIR) and an existing irradiation capsule design will be leveraged to irradiate YHx
specimens at controlled temperatures of 300°C, 400°C, and 500°C to two levels of fast fluence. These
temperature conditions were chosen to suppress recovery mechanisms and minimize hydrogen diffusional
processes, allowing the assessment of fast fluence effects on hydride behavior. This approach isolated
concurrent phenomena such as radiation damage, thermal recovery, and hydrogen transport.

This report documents the irradiation test matrix, capsule design, specimen preparation, and status of the
experiment assembly regarding HFIR insertion.

 2. IRRADIATION TEST MATRIX

The irradiation test matrix is composed of six irradiation capsules (ID YHL01 to YHL06), each containing
16 YHx specimens, with x around 1.9. The targeted H/Y ratio corresponds to a composition known to
preserve the mechanical integrity of hydride moderators during the demanding operating conditions of
microreactors. Table 1 summarizes the test matrix. The irradiation conditions span three specimen target
irradiation temperatures (300°C, 400°C, and 500°C) and two distinct levels of neutron fast fluence
(E > 0.1 MeV) that differ from one another by at least a factor 2. The actual values are determined by the
experiment design (see Section 3).

 3. EXPERIMENT DESIGN

The experiment design of the YHL capsules is based on a previously developed HFIR capsule design that
supported the YHXT irradiation campaigns [5, 7]. The core difference from the previous YHXT## capsules
is the utilization of higher hydrogen loading. The H/Y ratios of the YHXT capsules ranged from 1.69 to
1.83. In this design (YHL), the YHx specimens are ultimately modeled at a 1.9 ratio, though some of the
as-fabricated specimens exhibit higher hydrogen loading (see Section 4). Small differences in hydrogen
content minimally affect the material heat generation rate—for example, YHx specimens with x = 1.9 and
x = 2 have heat generation rates of 45.3 and 45.6 W/g, respectively. Figure 1 shows a general capsule layout.
Sixteen disk specimens are sandwiched between two sets of SiC thermometry (TM), which sit on top of a
SiC retainer specimen. The specimens are nominally 6 mm in outer diameter (OD) and 0.5 mm thick. This
structure is mirrored, and contact is maintained between both sides with SiC retainer springs. This stack of
SiC and specimens is slid into a rectangular cutout molybdenum holder. The holder features small tabs at
the top and bottom to hold concentricity within the aluminum housing. Molybdenum end caps are welded
onto the holder via an electron beam trepan weld. Afterward, the holder is loaded into a glove box with
ultra-high-purity (UHP) helium and sealed via a tungsten inert gas weld. The holder is loaded into an
aluminum capsule housing with molybdenum insulator disks on the top and bottom. The housing end cap
is then welded into place, backfilled with an inert gas mixture (see Table 2), and seal welded. The
molybdenum holders are always filled with 1 atm UHP helium, and the gas mixture between the holder and
housing is varied to modulate the thermal properties of the temperature-regulating gas gap. The insulator
disk functions largely by providing another contact conductance gap rather than relying on the thermal
conductivity properties of the disk. In addition to the insulator disk, standoffs are present on the end caps
to mitigate axial heat losses. As in the previous design, experiment location and holder–housing gas gap
conditions determine the ultimate temperature of the specimens during the irradiation.

 4. SPECIMEN PRECHARACTERIZATION

The YH specimens were fabricated at LANL. Disks of the desired geometry (6 mm in diameter and 0.5 mm
thick) were fabricated from a high-purity yttrium material before being hydrided in a furnace. The certificate
of analysis of the high-purity yttrium material is available in APPENDIX A. A 6%Ar/H2 gas mixture was
flown through a 1,200°C tube furnace located inside an argon glovebox. Gas flow was held constant at 1
atm (with hydrogen partial pressure of 0.06 atm). The temperature was adjusted throughout the hydriding
process to reach the desired H/M without cracking. Literature pressure–composition–temperature values
and previous knowledge were used to determine the temperatures and soak times. Figure 4 shows a batch
of YHx specimens upon removal from the hydriding furnace. Five batches of specimens were fabricated to
provide YHx specimens for HFIR irradiation and additional specimens for backup and unirradiated testing.
Each batch contains between 10 and 40 YHx specimens.

 4

 Figure 4. As-fabricated YHx specimens after removal from the hydriding furnace

Precharacterization was performed at both LANL and ORNL and included hydrogen content
measurements, optical imaging, dimensional and mass measurements, and microscopy. The hydrogen
content of individual specimens was determined at LANL from the weight differential before and after
hydriding. Optical imaging was also performed at LANL; pictures of the front, back, and side of each
specimen were collected. Figure 5 shows an example of pictures collected. Upon receipt at ORNL, the
specimens were inspected for diameter, thickness, and mass. The diameter and thickness measurements
ensured the proper fitting of the specimens in the capsules. Two diameters and two thicknesses were
recorded per specimen. The specimens were laser-engraved with a unique ID on one side, ensuring their
traceability throughout the irradiation testing. The thickness profile for a subset of specimens was also
recorded using a Keyence digital microscope. High-energy x-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements on a
subset of specimens were also performed at the National Synchrotron Light Source II using the high-energy
x-rays available at the Pair Distribution Function beamline at Brookhaven National Laboratory. Specimen
selection and loading into each capsule were guided by several considerations: ensuring proper fit within
the capsule, grouping specimens in each row with similar thicknesses to maintain uniform stack geometry,
and targeting a capsule-average H/Y ratio close to the desired 1.9. Because hydrogen partial pressure in the
sealed capsule equilibrates across specimens during irradiation, the average capsule H/Y ratio is the most
meaningful parameter for assessing hydrogen loss or retention, both in irradiation capsules and in moderator
canning for qualification studies. Based on these criteria, the chosen loading resulted in average H/Y ratios
between 1.93 and 1.96 across the capsules. For capsule YHL06, no XRD measurements were performed
on the selected specimens because backup specimens were used following failure of the holder weld leak
test; the originally intended specimens were damaged and could not be irradiated. APPENDIX B
summarizes the data collected on each specimen as part of this irradiation testing.

 5. EXPERIMENT ASSEMBLY

Prior to experiment assembly, all components were inspected, engraved, and cleaned in accordance with
HFIR-approved drawings and procedures. The capsule parts layout is the first step of assembly (see Figure
6[a]), where the IDs and quantities of all the parts are verified in accordance with the assembly QA
documentation. The internal components of the holder are stacked into an assembly tool (see Figure 6[b])
and loaded into the holder (see Figure 6[c]). The molybdenum end caps are then welded on each end of the
molybdenum holder via an electron beam trepan weld. The holder is then backfilled with UHP helium and
sealed via a tungsten inert gas weld. To ensure that the holder is leak tight, a bubble test and a helium leak
test are performed. A picture of a holder end cap after welding and testing is shown in Figure 7. All holders
except the YHL06 holder were successfully assembled, welded, and tested. The YHL06 required re-built
after failed weld leak test. The welding issue on the YHL06 holder led to most of the YHx specimens
initially intended for irradiation being damaged. Another set of specimens was thus selected for the rebuild
of capsule YHL06.

The complete holder is then loaded into the capsule housing. The aluminum housing end cap is welded to
the aluminum housing using an electron beam weld before the capsule is backfilled with the designed gas
fill (see Table 2) and the final seal weld is performed (gas tungsten arc weld). Figure 8 shows the two types
of welds on the capsules. The welded capsule is leak tested via a helium leak test, hydrostatic test, and final
helium leak test. The successfully tested capsule is finally cleaned and delivered to HFIR along with its
fabrication package for insertion in the core.

Out of the six capsules of this campaign, four capsules (YHL01, YHL02, YHL04, and YHL05) have been
successfully assembled, welded, tested, and delivered to HFIR. They are scheduled to start irradiation in
cycle 513, starting October 28, 2025. Figure 9 shows these four complete capsules ready for insertion. The
remaining two capsules (YHL03 and YHL06) are slated for irradiation in HFIR cycle 514, starting February
17, 2026. Capsule YHL06 required re-fabrication after its holder failed post-weld testing, and capsule
YHL03 final capsule welding was delayed to be completed alongside the rebuilt YHL06 capsule since both
share the same gas fill.

 6. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK

This irradiation campaign marks an important step in developing a fundamental understanding of the
irradiation performance of YHx, particularly in terms of microstructural and thermal property changes under
neutron displacement damage. To facilitate this, ORNL’s standard capsule design was employed with small
disk specimens to reduce temperature gradients and enhance thermal conductivity. Low irradiation
temperatures were targeted to minimize hydrogen diffusional processes and focus on irradiation-induced
effects. Four out of the six capsules of this campaign have been successfully assembled, welded, and tested,
and are scheduled for insertion into HFIR during cycle 513, beginning on October 28, 2025. The other two
capsules are being assembled and are anticipated for insertion into HFIR cycle 514, starting on
February 17, 2026.

Following irradiation, the capsules will be transferred to the Irradiated Materials Examination and Testing
hot cell facility at ORNL for disassembly. Upon opening, SiC TM and YHx specimens will be retrieved.
TM will be analyzed at ORNL’s Low Activation Materials Development and Analysis laboratory to
determine actual irradiation temperatures, which will be compared with the predicted TM average
temperatures (see Section 3). Postirradiation examination on the YHx specimens will include advanced
electron microscopy and thermal property characterization to further understand material behavior.
