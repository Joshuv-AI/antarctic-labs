1. Introduction
Engineering design teams increasingly recognize the opportunity to improve system
cybersecurity through initial design and engineering decisions. These opportunities reduce the
need to contend with costly or even intractable issues later in the development process and
reduce the number of cyber risks that security teams must manage and monitor. Appraising
cybersecurity considerations at the beginning of the system life cycle also facilitates expanding
the spectrum of potential solutions beyond digital monitoring and controls, creating new
opportunities to secure systems using physical and mechanical approaches.

Cybersecurity is more than a set of information technology (IT) and operational technology (OT)
technical requirements; it is also an organization-wide challenge that requires executive
leadership. Cybersecurity is not an IT/OT problem to “solve,” but a prevalent and persistent
challenge that requires attention and proactive actions across all divisions of an organization.
Likewise, cybersecurity is more than a software or technology issue—it encompasses
engineering designs and human behavior, among many other dimensions, and it requires
cultural and behavioral adaptations as well as technical solutions. These nontechnical
dimensions have become targets for attack, as adversaries understand that disrupting the
executive management functions cascades to all other facets of organizational operation.

This guide builds on past work on the National Cyber-Informed Engineering (CIE) Strategy. In
September 2023, the U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) CIE Program released the Cyber-
Informed Engineering Implementation Guide, Version 1.0. (U.S. Department of Energy 2023).
The guide represents the thought leadership of more than 115 contributors from a
multidisciplinary CIE Community of Practice (COP), led by researchers at the Idaho National
Laboratory and the National Laboratory of the Rockies. The CIE Implementation Guide took a
crucial step toward providing guidance for engineers to incorporate CIE principles throughout
the design and engineering process and meeting a key National CIE Strategy recommendation
(U.S. Department of Energy 2022).

While the CIE Implementation Guide provides a resource for engineers wishing to incorporate
CIE into organizational processes, CIE is not something that can be siloed as an “engineering
problem.” The most effective incorporation of CIE extends throughout all organizational
divisions, permeating strategic planning and management, financial management, acquisition
processes, and other programs that might not be seen as engineering per se, but whose work
influences an organization’s operation and infrastructure. This guide takes select CIE principles
and transposes them to familiar strategic planning taxonomy to support CIE integration into the
practices of executive leaders.

1.1. What Is “CIE”?
CIE expands cybersecurity decisions into the engineering space—not by asking engineers to
become cyber experts but by calling on engineers to apply engineering tools and make
engineering decisions that improve cybersecurity outcomes. CIE examines the engineering
consequences that a sophisticated cyber attacker could achieve and drives engineering
changes that may mitigate, limit, or eliminate those consequences. This practice also allows the

engineering team to better tailor digital system protections according to the overall system risk
and to factor impacts of digital failure or cyberattacks into the overall system development
process. Ultimately, using CIE to address cyber-physical risks is becoming a part of the overall
“standard of care” for engineering design. While these concepts can be most fruitful when
considering the design of a new system, they are useful to consider even for a currently existing
system to raise the engineering-based protections that system employs for digital failure or
cybersecurity risk.

1.2. About the CIE Program
Researchers first defined a CIE approach in 2017 as a set of principles to consider when solving
an engineering problem (Anderson, et al. 2017). At the direction of congress, the National CIE
Strategy (U.S. Department of Energy 2022) was developed by an executive task force
assembled by DOE’s Office of Cybersecurity, Energy Security, and Emergency Response
(CESER), which included energy sector asset owners and operators; vendors and
manufacturers; standards organizations; research and academic institutions; National
Laboratories; and government agencies. The strategy outlines strategic recommendations
across five pillars of action (Figure 1).
Figure 1. CIE strategy pillars

Since the strategy’s publication in June 2022, the DOE-led CIE Program 1 has grown and
evolved, with multiple efforts underway to implement key recommendations. Bolstering these
efforts is the CIE COP, which was formed by the CIE Program and quickly grew to include more
than 200 practitioners to help translate CIE concepts into technical requirements and drive the
development of a robust CIE body of knowledge and resources.

1.3. Importance of the Board of Directors and C-Suite for Adopting CIE
Support from organizational executive leadership is essential for the success of implementing
CIE in organization processes. The success of organizational approaches to both security and
management are critically tied to support from C-suite executives and organizational leadership
(Garcia-Granados and Bahsi 2020). Influence from an organization’s strategic executives is
imperative for shifting an organizational strategy from embedded but outdated approaches to
cybersecurity to new concepts for strategic planning, management, and operation (Boone
2017). As a CIE program matures, an organization will need to adapt to the new lens presented
by the CIE principles at both the process and strategic levels, shifting an organizational strategy
from embedded but outdated approaches to cybersecurity to new concepts for strategic
planning, management, and operation (Boone 2017). As a CIE program matures, an organization
will need to adapt to the new lens presented by the CIE principles at both the process and
strategic levels.

CIE REQUIRES ORGANIZATIONAL CULTURE CHANGE
When incorporated as a strategic priority by the C-suite, cybersecurity can turn from reactive
and defensive to a value-adding strategic advantage (Boone 2017). Effective, unified strategic
approaches demand disruptive organizational change, which traditionally has been difficult to
effect in the face of organizational inertia (Andriole 2021). As such, efforts to integrate CIE as an
organizational priority can encounter resistance in the face of established culture and norms.
Principle 12 most directly captures a need for organizational adaptation to ensure that all
organizational personnel, decisions, and behavior align with the aspirations and principles of CIE.
Managing a change in culture at both organizational and personal levels requires engaging with
people’s emotions and personalities (Andriole 2022; Daly 2011).

Traditional engineering practices consider cybersecurity once a system is designed (Smith, Le
Blanc and Mandelli 2023). Compared with the application of cybersecurity defenses and
controls on existing systems, CIE requires a holistic systems approach to integrate cybersecurity
concerns throughout design and engineering. This approach begins in the earliest stages of
system design, continues through component acquisition and personnel interaction with the
systems throughout the system’s lifecycle, and ultimately to decommissioning. It is defined by
decisions in and interdependencies among the organizational mission, existing and future facility
design variables, and the design of systems (Eggers, et al. 2023). Incorporating CIE at all stages
of organizational planning and operation requires permeation of cybersecurity as a critical
outcome at every stage of the engineering planning processes, as safety has become a
ubiquitous influence on organizational cultures.

CIE REQUIRES ADAPTIVE COST/BENEFIT ANALYSIS
Organizational leadership tends to focus more on internal and tangible variables and factors than
external and less finite variables (Garcia-Granados and Bahsi 2020). Cyber risks are traditionally
seen as inherently financial risks stemming from choices of technological expenses (Barsky
2021). CIE is a long-term approach to processes that does not lend itself to near-term, finite
measures in individual technological expenses or financial liabilities. CIE is better weighed
against a longer-term view that shifts the strategy from “‘how much money?’ to ‘can we

strategically afford the consequences of not investing?’” (Barsky 2021, 10). This is a more
challenging argument, to be sure.

A September 2024 publication from the CIE program (Rebori-Carretero, et al. 2024) provides a
detailed examination of challenges in quantifying benefits of CIE and recommends approaches
by which benefits of CIE can be quantified. Beginning with common challenges like competing
priorities for limited resources and insufficient availability of cyber-event data to enable
quantitative analysis, the document examines how traditional security investment metrics are
insufficient for CIE. The report provides five subsequent options for more accurately
characterizing benefits of CIE to highlight prospective benefits for an organization.

1.4. How to Use This Guide
CIE is a philosophy that guides a series of ongoing processes, so its methods and approaches
are both dynamic and unique to each case, preventing an authoritative or prescriptive approach.
As with other CIE Program literature, this guide complements—but does not replace—the
application of cybersecurity standards or practices currently in place within an organization.
While other CIE Program guides focus on the technical integration of CIE into an organization’s
OT infrastructure and processes, this guide focuses on how engineering teams can garner C-
suite and board support for integrating CIE into the organization’s culture and management
philosophy and strategy.

This guide discusses different roles played by members of the C-suite and the board of
directors, and how those roles might influence CIE implementation. These points of view are
important to consider, both to frame the strategy in terms that are meaningful to the priorities of
different executive positions and because they help illuminate different facets of the
implementation guidance, which can improve the CIE program. The characterization of the roles
and priorities within the executive leadership are, of course, exemplars and thereby not
necessarily universal.

The third section of this guide breaks down the 2023 CIE Implementation Guide and distills
points that might be most applicable in an organization’s strategic planning and management
processes. This is to highlight those facets of the CIE guidance that are most relevant at a
strategic level and avoid bogging a process down in considerations best approached at the
programmatic level.

2. Relevance of CIE Principles to Executive Management
CIE provides a powerful strategic planning input. Integrating secure engineering considerations
into the conception, design, build, and operation of physical systems to reduce impacts of
cyberattacks shapes systems around the desired outcome, rather than aligning with imposed
financial or other criteria. Starting at the desired end-state and building back to the necessary
resources and other requirements is “the most coveted” approach to systems engineering,
avoiding measuring “outputs, rather than outcomes” (Barsky 2021, 10). Using CIE principles as
engineering inputs also provides an adaptable but broadly applicable framework within which to
design systems. While changing system processes may take time to aggregate, it is the
aggregation of process-level changes and adaptations that provide the momentum to shift
strategic direction (Levinthal 2017).

2.1. CEO: Relevant Strategic Priorities
The chief executive officer (CEO) is both the leader of the strategic agenda and the primary
authority in interpreting performance analytics to assure the organization meets the strategic
vision. The CEO can unify efforts by affording a place for representatives of leadership across
the organization, providing the essential linchpin to align efforts from the IT, OT, communications,
and legal teams, as well as the many other subdivisions of an organization that need to
collaborate on effective strategies and responses (DiStaso 2018). The CEO (and other senior
leadership, as organizational structure warrants) should collaborate in the analysis of systems
performance when implementing CIE principles, balancing business strategy and strategic
management priorities with the technical performance data. Table 2 presents CIE principles

2.5. The Board of Directors: Relevant Strategic and Oversight Priorities
Strategies that demand heavy financial investment to change organizational practices and
culture, as are required for CIE, require support from the organization’s board of directors to
impel the necessary changes and overcome internal resistance (Andriole 2021). An
organization’s board generally performs high-level strategic planning, management, and
oversight. Boards of directors are also responsible for the highest level of risk management (e.g.,
one risk they manage is the risk that executives will misrepresent the performance and financial
health of the company to cash in on bonuses). Boards typically address this risk by retaining
independent auditors to report on the "real" status of the company. The board should set the
tone company-wide for the greatest risks—cyber and other (e.g., risks that threaten the
existence and viability of the company).

Rapidly evolving technological environments demand increased engagement by boards and
proactive engagement that anticipates future challenges (Barsky 2021). CIE is well suited to the
purpose of a board, as it addresses fundamental capabilities rather than anecdotal threats (Lee
2023). The primary purpose of the board is to develop and define the organization’s guiding
philosophy and to oversee performance to ensure the organization meets its goals. Setting
performance benchmarks and reviewing competitor/industry case studies and scenarios are
primary concerns for a board (Lee 2023).

Government regulations define “what” organizations need to care about and account for, and it
is then up to the asset owners and operators to define “how” they meet those requirements (Lee
2023). Rather than focusing on the consequence of not adopting an initiative, boards should
frame a need within the regulations and other criteria by which the organization will be held to
account and then extrapolate how the proposed strategy can avoid a high-consequence event
or produce the desired benefit (Lee 2023).

A September 2024 publication from the CIE program (Ohrt, et al. 2024) contextualizes CIE
within an enterprise risk management (ERM) framework. ERM guides organizational strategic
planning within an evaluation of existing and anticipated threats and opportunities to bolster
organization resilience, inform decision making, and respond to internal and external challenges.
The report illustrates the integration of CIE into ERM processes and highlights how CIE can
amplify the goals and benefits of ERM.

3. CIE Principles as Strategic Planning Inputs
CIE principles are most effective when an organization considers how they can optimize both
organizational strategy and culture. CIE is more than an overlay to be used in planning and
control processes; it should permeate organizational culture akin to safety. While changing
organizational culture and behavior may seem daunting, the incorporation of CIE principles into
relevant training, monitoring, and enforcement processes will help ensure that the desired new
behaviors can overcome the inertia of established habits and expectations (Chin, et al. 2017).
Personnel education and training informed by CIE should include why adaptations are necessary
and what changes are appropriate to help organizational personnel understand their role in
those adjustments (Barsky 2021). Going beyond “what to do” to include the impacts of why and
what consequences are being avoided can ease changes to culture and manage personnel
expectations.

CIE implementation benefits from incorporation into organizational strategic planning. Strategic
planning and adjustments to strategic management, risk management, and performance
management are where organizational leaders can have the greatest impact (Barsky 2021).
Successful deployment of the new strategic direction—particularly when relevant to
cybersecurity—requires organization-wide strategic communication to ensure the necessary
education and buy-in from personnel and stakeholders (DiStaso 2018). In addition to planning
and communicating about a new strategic plan, executive leadership can play a significant role
in developing requirements for and managing security strategy and related programs (Garcia-
Granados and Bahsi 2020).

Strategic planning analyses can be further enhanced by translating conventional strength,
weakness, opportunity, and threat (SWOT) analyses into a more granular format like that Bryson
proposes (Bryson 2017, 39). In Bryson’s model, “strengths” and “weaknesses” are expanded
under three categories of “Internal Environment” considerations (i.e., Resources, Present
Strategy, and Performance) while “opportunities” and “threats” comprise considerations in the
three “External Environment” categories (i.e., Forces and Trends, Key Resource Controllers, and
Competitors and Collaborators). Forward-looking appraisal of opportunities and threats
(“challenges,” in Bryson’s model)—particularly through the “forces and trends” subsection of
Bryson’s model—can include consideration of relevant regulations and oversight beyond the
scope of impacts to organizational operation. Instead, strategic planning that proactively
engages external influences can allow the organization to take control over the regulatory,
economic, social, and other factors that shape its environment (Levinthal 2017).

3.7. Principle 7: Interdependency Evaluation
How do I understand where my system can impact others or be impacted by others?

OT systems are interdependent with IT systems. The world's absolute most bullet-proof OT
security programs are not effective if physical operations have to shut down every time IT is
infected with ransomware. Physical operations depend on IT systems as well, not merely OT
systems. Consider the impacts that Maersk shipping felt from NotPetya: Ships still came into
port and their engines and navigation systems worked, nobody ran aground, and cranes could
still take the containers off the vessels and put them on waiting 18-wheelers, but nobody could
print the waybills for the containers to tell the truckers where to deliver them. The container
tracking system was an IT resource, and IT was crippled by NotPetya (Greenberg 2019).

 • Resources:
 o Concept Phase: What dependencies does the conceptual system have on other
 internal business systems or infrastructures, including subsystems within the
 conceptual system that are specially or uniquely required for the overall
 conceptual system to operate, and what is the potential exposure of those
 subsystems to cyber risk? (U.S. Department of Energy 2023, 72)
 o Concept Phase: What inputs do the system’s critical functions require that are

4. Conclusion
CIE is a strategic investment in safeguarding an organization against increasingly sophisticated
cyber threats. Embedding cybersecurity into engineering processes from the outset reduces the
likelihood of catastrophic incidents that could incur far greater costs down the line. This guide
demonstrates how CIE not only protects assets but also positions an organization competitively
by ensuring resilience and trustworthiness in its operations.

This guide offers strategic planning leadership within energy asset-owner organizations an
introduction to CIE principles that can inform organizational strategic planning. Members of a
board of directors, C-suite, and other leaders can use this guide to understand how CIE may be
adopted and incorporated into strategic goals and decisions. Engagement of and support from
executive leadership for a program that promotes CIE benefits not only the technical and
engineering concerns within the organization but the overall operation of the organization.

CIE needs to be an integral part of an organization’s strategic plan. Cybersecurity can no longer
be the sole province of engineering, OT, or IT personnel, nor the responsibility of a CIO/CTO.
The entire executive management of an organization must see cybersecurity as intrinsic to
strategic direction, planning, and management, and pursue a holistic approach organization-
wide rather than siloing responsibilities and budgets in different subdivisions (Barsky 2021). The
C-suite is responsible for identifying and pursuing organization-wide priorities and processes,
leaving execution and capture of outcomes to process leads (Chin, et al. 2017). While embedded
technical leads will necessarily be responsible for capturing the results of the program, ultimate
analysis of the overall value of the program needs to remain with the C-suite. The appearance of
effectiveness of CIE may need the broader and longer-term view of organizational strategic
leadership to accurately appraise benefits and the impact of outcomes (Levinthal 2017).
