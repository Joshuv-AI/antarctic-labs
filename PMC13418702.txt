Characterization of persistent HPV-specific activated T cells in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Hiromasa Ishihara, Hirofumi Shibata, Daisuke Muraoka, Ayako Demachi-Okamura, Takanari Okamoto, Ryo Mizuta, Yasunori Fukushima, Yusuke Sugita, Aiko Ogasawara, Reina Nishida, Shintaro Beppu, Hoshino Terada, Daisuke Nishikawa, Hidenori Suzuki, Katsuhiro Masago, Eiichi Sasaki, Noriaki Sakakura, Rui Yamaguchi, Nobuhiro Hanai, Takenori Ogawa, Hirokazu Matsushita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman papillomavirus–positive oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (HPV + OPSCC) generally has favorable outcomes yet remains prone to late recurrence. Durable control likely depends on persistent tumor-specific CD8+ T cells, but their persistence and function in metastasis are poorly understood.MethodsWe integrated bulk RNA sequencing (n = 56) with single-cell RNA and paired T-cell receptor (TCR) sequencing of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells (n = 4), including a longitudinal primary–lung metastasis pair obtained three years after curative therapy. HPV genotyping, HLA typing, epitope prediction, and NFAT-reporter Jurkat-luciferase assays were used to identify and functionally validate HPV16 E6/E7-specific TCRs. Repertoire tracking and single-cell/bulk transcriptomics were evaluated.ResultsHPV + tumors showed higher inferred CD8+ T cell infiltration and more favorable prognosis than HPV– tumors. Single-cell analysis revealed oligoclonally expanded exhausted clusters enriched in HPV tumor-specific CD8+ T cells. We isolated and functionally validated nine patient-derived HPV16 E6/E7-specific TCRs. High-avidity receptors recognizing an alternatively spliced region in E6 (aa 49–110; E6*) persisted in metastatic lesions, whereas lower-avidity clones, including an E7_11–19-specific TCR currently under clinical investigation, were lost. Compared with the primary tumor, metastatic lesions showed reduced stem-like/TCF7-associated CD8+ T-cell populations and enrichment of HPV-specific CD8+ T cells expressing KLRB1 and ZNF683, consistent with a tissue-adapted TRM-like transcriptional state with inhibitory signaling features.ConclusionsHigh-avidity, KLRB1 + HPV-specific CD8+ T cells represent a persistent effector subset with prognostic and therapeutic relevance to HPV + OPSCC. Their persistence in metastatic lesions and association with reduced recurrence risk support further investigation of KLRB1-associated HPV-reactive T-cell states as biomarkers and immunotherapeutic targets.

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is a common malignancy, and a paradigm shift has occurred with recognition of human papillomavirus (HPV), particularly HPV16, as a major driver of oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC). 1

HPV-positive OPSCC (HPV + OPSCC) is now established as a distinct clinical and biological entity, characterized by unique molecular features, immune profiles, and epidemiological patterns. Persistent infection with oncogenic HPV types, predominantly HPV16 and to a lesser extent HPV18, drives carcinogenesis through expression of viral oncoproteins E6 and E7. These proteins functionally inactivate host tumor suppressors p53 and retinoblastoma (Rb), respectively, leading to disruption of cell cycle control, genomic instability, and evasion of immune surveillance. 2 HPV positivity is associated with a favorable prognosis, including higher overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS), compared to HPV-negative OPSCC.

Standard treatment for localized HPV + OPSCC, including surgery or chemoradiotherapy (CRT), offers high cure rates but often results in long-term functional impairments. Despite this, up to 25% of patients with HPV + OPSCC experience late recurrences or distant metastases, 3 highlighting the need for more refined prognostic biomarkers and durable therapeutic strategies.

In the recurrent or metastatic setting, current regimens incorporate platinum-based chemotherapy, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)–targeted agents such as cetuximab, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), including anti–PD-1 antibodies; however, objective response rates to ICIs remain modest at approximately 20%, 4 and reliable biomarkers to predict therapeutic response are still lacking.

Cancer develops and progresses through dynamic interactions with the immune system, passing through phases of elimination, equilibrium, and escape, a process known as cancer immunoediting. 5 Immune escape mechanisms include the loss of tumor-specific antigens, 6 β2-microglobulin (B2M) deficiency, 7 and defects in antigen presentation machinery. 8 A key focus is the role of TCF1 + stem-like exhausted CD8+ T cells, which sustain long-term antitumor immunity and mediate responses to immune checkpoint blockade 9 , 10

However, how these long-lived tumor-reactive T cells are maintained or altered in the tumor microenvironment, and how their dynamics differ between primary and recurrent or metastatic lesions, remain insufficiently understood.

In HPV + OPSCC, HPV-derived antigens such as E6 and E7 are attractive targets for immunotherapy. Adoptive cell therapies (ACT), including chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells and T cell receptor–engineered T cells (TCR-T), have gained increasing attention. 11 , 12 Notably, clinical trials of TCR-T cells targeting HPV-derived E6 and E7 antigens are currently underway in HPV-associated cancers. 13–15

To better understand HPV-specific T cell biology, we conducted an in-depth immunogenomic analysis of HPV + OPSCC, integrating bulk RNA sequencing to assess immune-related gene expression with single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and paired single-cell T cell receptor sequencing (scTCR-seq) in selected patient samples. Notably, our study includes longitudinal sampling from a patient who developed pulmonary metastasis three years after definitive treatment of the primary tumor, enabling direct comparison of HPV-specific T cell subsets between the primary tumor and its metastatic recurrence.

With this approach, we seek to define phenotypic and clonal characteristics of HPV-specific T cells in HPV + OPSCC, uncover mechanisms underlying their persistence or loss, and provide insights to guide biomarker development and design of next-generation immunotherapeutic strategies.

Methods

Patients and data sets

Clinical tumor samples and matched blood samples were collected from 56 patients with oropharyngeal cancer and its metastatic lung cancer who underwent surgery or biopsy at Aichi Cancer Center between April 2019 and August 2023 and Gifu University Hospital between January 2023 and August 2023. Patient sex was recorded for all cases, and both male and female patients were included in the analysis, with a median age at diagnosis of 65 years (range, 24–85).

Samples from which sufficient RNA could not be extracted were excluded prior to library preparation. The final analytical cohort consisted of 56 patients (Supplementary Table S1), and all included samples passed sequencing-depth and quality control criteria.

This study was approved by the institutional review board of Aichi Cancer Center (approval no. 2018-2-20, approved on 2/12/2019; approval no. 2018-2-28, approved on 26/4/2019) and by the institutional review board of Gifu University Hospital (approval no. 2022-134, approved on 6/10/2022).

Whole-exome and RNA sequencing data

WES and RNA-seq were performed as previously described. 16 Briefly, for WES, sequencing libraries of genomic DNA from three samples (GFHN002, OPK019, and OPK19-2; Pt.1 and Pt.2) and matched PBMCs were prepared. For RNA-seq, bulk RNA-seq libraries were prepared individually from tumor samples of 57 lesions obtained from 56 patients with oropharyngeal cancer, including one metastatic lung tumor.

HPV detection, genotyping, and viral gene expression analysis from bulk RNA-seq data

Bulk RNA-seq data from 56 OPSCC patients were analyzed using the HPV-EM algorithm 17 to detect and genotype HPV. For HPV16-positive tumors, viral gene expression (E1, E2, E5, E6, E7, L1, L2) was quantified as reads per kilobase (RPK) using HISAT2 (v2.2.0; RRID:SCR_015530) 18 for alignment against the full-length HPV16 reference genome (NCBI NC_001526.4) and SAMtools (v1.19; RRID:SCR_002105) 19 for processing. Expression levels across genomic positions were visualized using IGV (v2.18.1; RRID:SCR_011793).

Immune cell composition estimation from bulk RNA-seq data

The relative abundance of tumor-infiltrating immune cell populations was estimated from bulk RNA-seq expression profiles using xCell (v1.1.0; RRID:SCR_026446), 20 which infers enrichment scores for 64 immune and stromal cell types based on gene expression signatures. xCell was run with default parameters on bulk RNA-seq expression matrices, and cell-type scores were compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test unless otherwise specified.

HLA typing and HLA class1 HPV epitope binding prediction

HLA types were determined from the WES data of normal and tumor genomic DNA using ALPHLARD. 21 CD8+ T cell epitopes derived from HPV proteins E6 and E7 were predicted using netMHCpan4.1 (RRID:SCR_018182) 22 based on the predicted human leukocyte antigen (HLA-A, B, C) results for each patient. A total of 48 peptides of 9-10 amino acids in length with percentile ranks below 1 were predicted (Supplementary Table S9) and synthesized at greater than 90% purity by Biologica and subsequently resuspended in DMSO. A peptide pool was prepared containing E6 (35 peptides) and E7 (13 peptides). The following protein sequences were used for predicting and generating HPV16 peptides: E6 (NCBI NP_041325.1), and E7 (NCBI NP_041326.1).

Flow cytometry

Fresh tumor digests (FTD) were prepared using the gentleMACS tumor dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec, Auburn, California, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell counting was performed by manually using a hemocytometer. FTD (2–5 × 10^6 cells/tube) were cryopreserved with CP-1 (Kyokutoseiyaku, Tokyo, Japan) and kept in a deep freezer (−135 °C) until use. Cryopreserved FTD (2–5 × 10^6 cells) were thawed in RPMI and then stained after blocking Fc receptors using Human BD Fc Block (BD Biosciences, San Jose, California, USA). APC anti-human CD3 (clone UCHT1; Cat. No. 300439, RRID: AB_2562045; BioLegend), PE anti-human CD4 (clone OKT4; Cat. No. 317410, RRID: AB_ 571955; BioLegend) and FITC anti-human CD8 (clone SK1; Cat. No. 344704, RRID: AB_1877178; BioLegend). Zombie NIR™ Fixable Viability Kit (BioLegend) was used to exclude dead cells.

For lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs), the expression of B cell markers (CD19, CD20) and HLA class I molecules (HLA-A, B, C) was confirmed using PE anti-human CD19 (clone HIB19; BioLegend, RRID:AB_312937), APC anti-human CD20 (clone 2H7; BioLegend, RRID:AB_314261), and PE anti-human HLA-A, B, C (clone W6/32; BioLegend, RRID:AB_314992) antibodies.

For Jurkat cells stably expressing TCRαβ, cells were transduced with the target TCRαβ genes via retroviral vectors and selected with puromycin. Expression of TCRαβ and CD3 molecules was verified by staining with PE anti-human TCRαβ (clone WT31; Invitrogen, RRID:AB_10739264) and APC anti-human CD3 (clone UCHT1; BioLegend, RRID:AB_2562045) antibodies.

Stained cells were analyzed using an LSRFortessa X-20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and data were processed using FlowJo v10.10.0 (BD Biosciences, RRID:SCR_008520). Flow cytometric acquisition and analysis were performed using standardized protocols and predefined gating strategies to minimize operator-dependent bias.

Preparation of single-cell complementary DNA libraries

Single-cell RNA/TCR sequencing of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells was performed on a total of four samples (GFHN002, GFHN005, OPK019, OPK19-2) from three patients (Pt.1:GFHN0002, Pt.2: OPK019 and OPK19-2, Pt.3:GFHN005) with HPV positive OPSCC (GFHN002, OPK019, GFHN005), including a case with lung metastases (OPK19-2). From FTDs, CD3+CD4-CD8+ cells were sorted on an Aria III (BD Biosciences). GEM (Gel Bead-In EMulsions) generation and barcoding from single-cell suspensions (5000–10,000 CD8+ T cells) was performed using the Chromium Next GEM Single-Cell 5’ GEM Kit V.2 (PN-1000244) on the Chromium Next GEM Chip K Single-Cell Kit (PN-1000286) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, California, USA). After GEMs were broken and pooled complementary DNAs (cDNAs) were amplified, TCR target amplification was done using a TCR Amplification Kit (PN-1000252). The TCR and gene expression cDNA libraries were constructed using the Library Construction Kit (PN-1000190). The cDNA quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent, Santa Clara, California, USA) and sequenced using an HiSeq System (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA) with a pair-end 150 bp sequencing strategy.

Preprocessing of paired scRNA-seq and scTCR-seq data

Raw sequencing data for RNA expression and VDJ from human CD8+ T scRNA-seq were processed using Cell Ranger software (V.7.1.0; 10x Genomics, RRID:SCR_017344). RNA expression data were aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome and VDJ sequencing data to the GRCh38 VDJ reference pre-built by 10x Genomics (refdata-gex-GRCh38-2020-A) (Zheng et al, 2017). Gene expression count matrices were imported into the R package Seurat (V.4.3.0.1; RRID:SCR_016341) using R (V.4.2.3). Genes found to be expressed in <3 cells, as well as cells with <200 expressed genes, were excluded from the analysis. TCR genes were removed from the count data before downstream analysis. Cells were filtered to retain those with ≤15% mitochondrial RNA content and with unique molecular identifier counts between 200 and 5000.

RNA expression data were normalized using SCTransform 23 with ‘vst.flavor = “v2”’, correcting for sequencing depth and other confounding factors. Variance-stabilizing transformation was applied, and features were selected based on their residual variance.

For the primary four-sample integration analysis, datasets from GFHN002, GFHN005, OPK019, and OPK19-2 were integrated. The 3000 most variable features were identified using `SelectIntegrationFeatures()`, and datasets were prepared using `PrepSCTIntegration()`. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed separately for each dataset using the selected features. Cross-dataset anchors were identified using `FindIntegrationAnchors()`, and harmonized datasets were generated using `IntegrateData()`. Clustering was performed using `FindClusters()` with the resolution set to 0.2, and dimensional reduction was performed using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP).

In addition, an exploratory two-sample integration analysis using OPK019 and GFHN002 was independently performed for detailed characterization of exhausted T-cell heterogeneity and candidate TCR selection. The two-sample integration analysis was performed using the same workflow as the four-sample integration analysis. To enable more detailed subclustering, clustering for the two-sample analysis was performed using `FindClusters()` with the resolution set to 0.6.

For functional screening, we selected the top 20 most frequent clonotypes per exhausted cluster and sample, removed duplicates across clusters, yielding 38 clonotypes (OPK019) and 30 clonotypes (GFHN002) for testing.

TCR repertoire and clonotype analysis

Bulk TCR sequencing of PBMC samples was performed by ImmunoGeneTeqs (Chiba, Japan) based on TCRβ repertoire profiling. Briefly, RNA extracted from PBMC samples was reverse-transcribed into cDNA, followed by amplification of TCRβ genes using common primers to evaluate the TCR repertoire.

In scRNA/TCR-seq analyses, TCR clonotypes were analyzed using the scRepertoire package. Clonal overlap analyses were performed using the clonalOverlap() function with clonecall = “strict”. Under this definition, clonotypes were defined based on paired TRA/TRB information using exact paired CDR3α–CDR3β nucleotide sequences. In PBMC bulk TCR-seq, clonotypes were defined based on CDR3β sequences only. Because clonotype definitions differed between platforms, scRNA/TCR-seq and PBMC bulk TCR-seq data were analyzed separately, and direct cross-platform comparisons were not performed.

In the longitudinal case (OPK019 → OPK19-2), clonotypes were considered “persisting” if detected in both lesions, “lost” if detected in the primary tumor but undetected in the metastatic sample, and “new” if detected in the metastatic tumor but not in the primary tumor.

Differential expression analysis, gene set enrichment analysis and single sample GSEA

Differential gene expression analysis between cell populations was performed using the FindMarkers() function in Seurat with the “MAST” method, 24 incorporating sample identity as a latent variable to account for inter-sample variability. Fgsea (v1.24.0; RRID:SCR_020938) was performed to evaluate T cell signaling, proliferation and cytokine production using gene sets in MSigDB v2024.1.HS (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb).

Clinical and survival analysis

Clinical variables, estimated CD8+ T-cell infiltration inferred by xCell deconvolution, and the KLRB1/CD8A ratio calculated from normalized bulk RNA-seq expression values were evaluated in relation to recurrence-free survival and overall survival using Kaplan–Meier analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. Exploratory Cox proportional hazards analyses in HPV-positive cases included the standardized log2-transformed KLRB1/CD8A ratio and smoking exposure measured by pack-years. Additional sensitivity analyses were performed adjusting for estimated CD8+ T-cell infiltration inferred by xCell deconvolution.

HLA expression vectors

HLA expression vectors, including pcDNA3.1(+)/HLA-A*02:01, A*02:06, A*24:02, B*15:18, B*51:01, B*56:03, C*07:02 and C*07:04 were generated by cloning HLA genes from peripheral blood mononuclear cells from healthy individuals carrying the respective HLA. pcDNA 3.1(+)/HLA-B*52:01, C*12:02 and C*14:02 were purchased from RIKEN BRC. 25

Generation of antigen-presenting cells

EBV-transformed B lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs) as autologous B-cell antigen-presenting cells (B-APCs) were established by transforming PBMCs with culture supernatant from B95-8 BAC 26 gifted from Dr. Kanda T (Tohoku Medical and Pharmaceutical University) as described previously. 27 Cells obtained from expanded cultures were used as target cells possessing patient-specific HLAs. HEK293T cells (RRID:CVCL_0063) or KP3 cells (JCRB0178.0) transiently expressing HLA were prepared. Lipofectamine 2000 (5 µL; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was diluted in Opti-MEM (250 µL), incubated for 5 min, and mixed with Opti-MEM (250 µL) containing the HLA expression plasmid (2.5 µg). After 20 min, the lipid–DNA complexes were added to 5 × 10^5 HEK293T cells or KP3 cells. Medium was replaced 6 h later, and cells were used as target cells 2 days post-transfection. T2 cells (174 × CEM.T2; RRID:CVCL_2211) expressing HLA were also established. HLA genes were cloned into pMXs-IRES-puro (pMXs-IRES-puro-HLA). Retrovirus was produced by transfecting Phoenix-GP (ATCC CRL-3215; RRID:CVCL_H718) cells expressing GaLV envelope. T2 cells were transduced and selected with puromycin (0.3 µg/mL). Puromycin-resistant cells were used as HLA-expressing targets.

Generation of TCR-expressing Jurkat cells

For transient TCR expression, eBlocks (IDT) encoding the variable regions of TCRα and TCRβ chains were PCR-amplified and assembled into linearized plasmid vectors containing the corresponding constant regions using the Gibson Assembly method, as previously described. 28 , 29

TAP fragments of TCRα and TCRβ, together with the NFAT-reporter construct pGL4.30 (luc2P/NFAT-RE/Hygro; Promega E8481), were electroporated into Jurkat cells lacking endogenous TCRα and TCRβ expression using the Neon Electroporation System (Thermo Fisher). These cells were generated from the TCRβ-deficient J.RT3-T3.5 cell line (ATCC TIB-153; RRID:CVCL_1316) by additional CRISPR–Cas9-mediated disruption of the endogenous TCRα locus (TRAC) using a previously reported method. 30

The resulting cells, referred to as TAP-TCR-Luc-Jurkat cells, transiently expressed TCRs. For stable expression, synthesized TCRβ chain genes encoding a VDJ region (eBlocks), codon-optimized TCRβ constant regions linked via a self-cleaving P2A peptide, synthesized TCRα chain genes encoding a VJ region (eBlocks), and codon-optimized TCRα constant regions were assembled into a linearized pMXs-IRES-puro retroviral vector using the NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (New England Biolabs). The resulting construct (pMXs-TCRβ-P2A-TCRα-IRES-puro, or pMX-IRES-puro-TCR) was transfected into Phoenix-GP cells expressing the GaLV envelope to produce retrovirus. Jurkat cells (CD8-J2) were transduced with the retrovirus and cultured in the presence of puromycin (1.0 μg/mL) to obtain Stable Jurkat-TCR cells.

Detection of antigen-specific responses.

Autologous LCLs, KP3 cells transiently expressing HLA or T2 cells stably expressing HLA were used as target cells. Peptides were added at a concentration of 20 µg/ml for screening or at titrated concentration for avidity assays. Target cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 25 uL/well, at a density of 2 × 10^4/well for LCLs and 5 × 10^4/well for KP3 and T2 cells.

The effector cells created (TAP-TCR-Luc-Jurkat cells or Stable-TCR-Luc-Jurkat cells) were seeded 5 × 10^4 cells/25uL in each well and cultured overnight in the presence of target cells with or without antigen peptides. For Stable-TCR-Luc-Jurkat cells, the NFAT-reporter construct pGL4.30 [luc2P/NFAT-RE/Hygro] was electroporated before seeded.

After about 14 hours of co-culture when TAP-TCR-Luc-Jurkat cells or 4 hours of co-culture when Stable-TCR-Luc-Jurkat cells were used, luciferase substrate solution (Promega Steady-Glo LuciferaseAssay System) was added to each 50 µL/well and allowed to react at room temperature for 10 min. The mixture was suspended and 80 µL transferred to half-area white plates (Greiner LUMITRAC 675075) and the activation and luminescence of the NFAT reporter gene was measured with an EnSpire multimode plate reader (PerkinElmer).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using R version 4.2.3 (R Core Team (2023). R: A language and environment for statistical computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The comparison of HPV+ /− gene set and T-cell marker expression was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Mann–Whitney U-test equivalent). Overall survival (OS) and relapse-free survival (RFS) were evaluated using the Kaplan–Meier method with the log-rank test. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For gene set enrichment analyses, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied, and FDR q values < 0.05 were considered significant unless otherwise specified. This study was observational in nature, and no randomization was applied. Data processing and downstream analyses were performed using predefined computational pipelines, minimizing investigator-dependent bias. Sample size was determined by the availability of clinical specimens, and no formal power calculation was performed. All analyses were performed using publicly available software as described above. Custom scripts are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Results

HPV status, viral transcription, and CD8+ T cell infiltration characterize the immunogenomic landscape of the OPSCC cohort

The overall scheme of this study is shown in Figure 1A. Bulk RNA-seq was performed on 56 oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) samples to define the immunogenomic landscape (Supplementary Table S1, S2). Single-cell RNA and TCR sequencing of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells were performed on four samples (Table 1), including a longitudinal pair from the same patient: OPK019 (primary tumor) and OPK19-2 (lung metastasis three years post-curative treatment). HPV tumor-specific CD8+ T cells were identified and functional profiles were analyzed. In OPSCC, HPV was detected in 35/56 cases (62.5%), predominantly HPV16 (28/35, 80%; Figure 1B). Viral gene analysis confirmed high expression of E6 and E7 transcripts in HPV16-positive cases (Figure 1C). Immune deconvolution using xCell revealed significantly higher infiltration of dendritic cells, B cells, and CD8+ T cells in HPV-positive OPSCC, particularly central memory and effector memory subsets, whereas neutrophil infiltration was more prominent in HPV-negative tumors (Figure 1D, Supplementary Figure S1, Supplementary Table S3, S4). Kaplan–Meier analysis confirmed the expected favorable prognosis in HPV-positive compared with HPV-negative OPSCC (Supplementary Figure S2A, B). Stratification using immune cell densities estimated by bulk RNA-seq deconvolution analysis demonstrated that, although less striking than in HPV-negative OPSCC, lower CD8+ T cell infiltration in HPV-positive OPSCC tended to be correlated with recurrence (Supplementary Figure S2C, D). In HPV-positive OPSCC, smoking exposure of ≥15 pack-years and a history of alcohol consumption were associated with higher recurrence rates (Supplementary Figure S2E, F).

Single-cell analysis reveals exhausted CD8+ T cells with oligoclonal expansion in HPV-positive cancer

High-throughput single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and single-cell TCR sequencing (scTCR-seq) were performed on CD8+ T cells isolated by flow-cytometric gating from four samples (Supplementary Figure S3). A total of 39,603 CD8+ T cells (GFHN002: 14,167; GFHN005: 8,182; OPK019: 11,711; OPK19-2: 5,543) were analyzed, revealing seven clusters by UMAP (Figure 2A, 2B). Heatmap analysis demonstrated distinct transcriptional programs (Figure 2C). G1 was characterized by high expression of naïve/memory-associated genes such as SELL, CCR7, and IL7R, whereas G2 showed elevated expression of cytotoxic effector molecules including GZMB, IFNG, and PRF1. G6 displayed strong expression of proliferation markers (MKI67, PCNA).

G3 was characterized by high expression of heat shock protein (HSP) genes (HSPA1A, HSPA1B, HSP90AA1) together with activation-associated genes, and was therefore annotated as HSP-high CD8+ T cells. In contrast, G4 was defined by strong expression of canonical exhaustion-related genes, including PDCD1, CTLA4, ENTPD1, ITGAE, and CXCL13, consistent with exhausted T cell clusters previously identified in lung TILs and malignant pleural effusions (MPEs) from our earlier studies. 16 , 29 In addition, G5 was distinguished as NK/γδ-like CD8+ T cells with elevated expression of NKG7, TRDV2, and TRGV9. G7 showed high expression of mitochondrial genes (MT-ATP6, MT-CO1, MT-CO2), and was annotated as mitochondrial-high CD8+ T cells.

ScTCR-seq revealed TCRs of cells in G4: This exhausted cluster showed significant overlap with those in G3: HSP-high cluster and G6: Proliferated cluster (Figure 2D and Supplementary Table S5, S6), suggesting lineage relationship. Furthermore, G4 exhibited pronounced oligoclonality with hyperexpanded clones in all four samples (GFHN002, GFHN005, OPK019, and OPK19-2), indicating antigen-driven oligoclonal expansion followed by exhaustion in HPV + OPSCC tumors (Figure 2E, 2F).

Identification and validation of tumor-infiltrating HPV16 E6/E7-specific TCRs

To identify tumor-specific CD8+ T cell populations, we focused on the exhausted clusters according to our previous study. 29 As an exploratory analysis for detailed characterization of exhausted T-cell heterogeneity and candidate TCR selection, we independently performed higher-resolution re-clustering using two samples (GFHN002 and OPK019). This analysis identified exhausted clusters C10 and C11, which exhibited high expression of exhaustion-associated genes and largely consisted of cells corresponding to the G4 exhausted cluster identified in the primary four-sample integrated analysis (Supplementary Figure S4A–E).

Many of these clonotypes also overlapped with other clusters (Supplementary Table S5-8) and exhibited similar transcriptional profiles (Supplementary Figure S5, S6), suggesting related transcriptional states among clusters.

From the 20 most frequent clonotypes from each cluster excluding duplicates, 38 clonotypes from OPK019 and 30 from GFHN002 were selected (bold in Supplementary Table S5-8). Antigen recognition was tested against 48 predicted E6/E7 peptides (NetMHCpan4.1; Supplementary Table S9) using autologous B-LCL pulsed with pools (1–7 for OPK019; 1–10 for GFHN002; Supplementary Figure S7). TCR-TAP/luc-CD8-J2 co-culture identified specific responses in 4/38 clonotypes (OPK019) and 5/30 in GFHN002 (Figure 3A, 3B). In total, nine TCRs were identified. Furthermore, the individual reactive peptides within pools were subsequently identified (Figure 3C, 3D). HLA restriction was determined using Jurkat cells stably expressing each TCR with target HLA-negative cell lines (HEK, KP3, T2; Supplementary Figure S8, S9; Supplementary Table S10). These results demonstrate successful identification of functional HPV E6/E7-specific TCRs in the tumor.

Phenotypic diversity of HPV-specific CD8+ T cells

HPV tumor-specific CD8+ T cells were predominantly enriched in the G4 exhausted cluster, but also detected in other clusters such as G1 and G2, corresponding to naïve and activated T cell states, respectively (Figure 4A), consistent with a previous report. 31 Furthermore, differential expression gene (DEG) analysis comparing HPV tumor-specific T cells with other T cells revealed that CXCL13 was exclusively expressed in the tumor-specific T cell population (Figure 4B, 4C). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that gene sets related to antiviral responses, T cell activation, and interferon-α production were significantly enriched in HPV tumor-specific T cells (Figure 4D, Supplementary Table S11), indicating that these cells retain activation signatures despite exhaustion.

Functional avidity of TCRs and location of HPV epitopes

To assess signal strength of the nine identified TCRs, NFAT-reporter luciferase assay was performed using Stable-TCR-Luc-Jurkat cells expressing the corresponding full-length TCRα/β chains as previously described 16 , 29 (Supplementary Figure S8). logEC50 values of the nine Stable-TCR-Luc-Jurkat cells ranged from 2.92 to 5.00 (Figure 5A). TCRs recognizing HPV16 E6 amino acids 49–110 (TCRg15, g17, g21, g28, o15, o35) exhibited significantly lower log10EC50 values, indicative of higher functional avidity, compared with other E6/E7 targeting TCRs (TCRo13, o24, g09; p = 0.024; Figure 5B). Intriguingly, RNA-seq analysis of study samples and the HPV + Caski cell line 32 confirmed alternative splicing in this region, producing the HPV E6*variant (Figure 5C). These findings indicate an association between E6-region alternative splicing and recognition of peptides derived from this region.

Comparison of HPV-specific T cells and clonotype-specific dynamics between primary and metastatic tumors

In OPK019, a comparison of cluster distributions between the primary oropharyngeal tumor (OPK019) and the metastatic lung lesion (OPK19-2) revealed fewer G1 (naïve/memory T cells) and G3 (HSP-high T cells) clusters at the metastatic site (Figure 6A, 6B). Gene expression analysis showed that HPV-specific T cells in the primary tumor expressed high levels of heat shock protein (HSP)-related genes, whereas those in the metastasis upregulated ZNF683, KLRB1, and PTPN22 (Figure 6C, Supplementary Figure S10A, 10B). Notably, the expression of the HPV-specific T-cell marker CXCL13 was lower in the metastatic tumor compared with the primary tumor.

Clone-level analysis showed that two TCRs with relatively high avidity (TCRo15 and TCRo35) persisted in the lung metastasis, whereas two lower-avidity TCRs (TCRo13 and TCRo24) were lost (Figure 6D). We further examined TCR clonotype dynamics in peripheral blood and the tumor microenvironment (Figure 6E, 6F). Compared with PBMCs, the tumor exhibited more pronounced changes in TCR repertoire between the initial diagnosis and recurrence. Notably, the two TCRs (TCRo13 and TCRo24) lost in the lung metastasis were undetectable in peripheral blood at both diagnosis and recurrence, whereas the two persisting TCRs (TCRo15 and TCRo35) were present at baseline but eventually disappeared at recurrence (Figure 6G).

KLRB1 expression in HPV tumor-specific T cells and its association with recurrence

In the primary tumor, persisting T cells (TCRo15 and TCRo35) expressed higher levels of KLRB1 than those that disappeared (TCRo13 and TCRo24) (Figure 7A). In the broader cohort, bulk RNA-seq analysis showed that the KLRB1/CD8A ratio was higher in non-recurrent HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancer cases (Figure 7B), and a higher KLRB1/CD8A ratio showed a trend toward association with improved recurrence-free survival in HPV-positive cases (Figure 7C). In exploratory Cox proportional hazards analyses, this association remained directionally consistent after adjustment for smoking exposure and was also observed in sensitivity analyses adjusting for estimated CD8+ T-cell infiltration (Supplementary Table S12).

Discussion

In this study, we performed a high-resolution analysis of CD8+ T cell dynamics and TCR clonotype changes in HPV-associated oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (HPV + OPSCC) by integrating bulk and single-cell RNA/TCR sequencing.

Inclusion of a longitudinal primary–metastasis pair with lung metastasis arising three years after curative treatment enabled direct in vivo tracking of tumor-reactive TCR clonotypes. This analysis revealed selective clonal persistence and loss after disease progression, thereby providing important insights into the persistence of tumor-specific T cells and mechanisms of immune escape. In our cohort, both the abundance and phenotypic features of intratumoral CD8+ T cells were associated with the risk of metastasis and recurrence, consistent with previous reports based on TCGA data. 33

We previously reported the presence of tumor-specific T cells in exhausted T cell populations from primary lung tumors and pleural effusions. 16 , 29 Previous studies have suggested that highly clonal exhausted CD8+ T cells in HPV-related cancers are enriched for HPV-specific clones, 34 although this has not been experimentally validated. In the present study, consistent with those findings, we identified CD8+ T cells carrying HPV16 E6/E7-specific TCRs within these exhausted subsets. Moreover, these TCR sequences may serve as a valuable resource for development of TCR-engineered T cell (TCR-T) therapies. Thus, our results confirm the enrichment of HPV-specific clones in exhausted subsets in OPSCC and provide a translational bridge toward adoptive TCR-T strategies.

Longitudinal immune profiling revealed dynamic changes in tumor antigen-specific T cell clones, including persistence and loss. A striking finding was the complete loss of T cells specific for the E7_11-19 epitope in metastatic lung lesions, despite ongoing E6/E7 antigen expression in metastases. This suggests selective elimination of antigen-specific T cells within an immunosuppressive environment, underscoring the importance of monitoring epitope stability and clonal dynamics longitudinally in guiding TCR-based therapies. Because the clinical efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) relies on the presence of pre-existing tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells that can recognize tumor antigens and become reactivated upon PD-1 blockade, 35 the absence or reduction of such antigen-specific T cell clones may underlie resistance to ICI monotherapy.

In such cases, adoptive T cell therapies, particularly TCR-T cells, may be warranted. Indeed, a clinical trial of HPV16 E7_11-19 specific TCR-T cells is currently ongoing (NCT02858310), 13 alongside other approaches targeting HPV16 E6 14 and strategies using iPSC-derived T cells 36 for HPV-associated cancers. Our data emphasize that real-time repertoire surveillance should be integrated into clinical protocols to optimize ICI and TCR-T strategies, particularly to anticipate epitope loss or clonal attrition.

Surviving HPV-specific T cell clones were characterized by high-avidity TCRs capable of responding under low antigen concentrations. Their target region corresponded to a poorly expressed segment of an HPV16 E6 splice variant (E6*, residues 49–110). 37 This indicates that clones capable of responding to scarce antigens are selectively retained under immunosuppressive conditions, reflecting evolutionary pressure in the tumor microenvironment. Detection of E6*highlights that viral transcript heterogeneity may modulate antigen presentation; epitope prioritization and TCR design should therefore be splice-aware, and multi-epitope strategies spanning splice junctions could mitigate immune escape. This finding suggests that viral splicing diversity directly shapes T cell persistence and that epitope design for immunotherapy must account for splice variants to ensure durable responses.

Eberhardt et al. reported that HPV-specific PD-1+ TCF7+ CCR7+ CD8+ T cell, consistent with stem-like T cells/progenitor exhausted (Tpex) cells, 38 are present in primary tumors at diagnosis. 31 In our study, however, these cells (corresponding to cluster G1) were restricted to the primary tumor and absent in metastases, suggesting that the loss of stem-like T cells may represent an additional barrier to effective immunotherapy. Thus, preserving or replenishing stem-like T cells should be considered a key objective in optimizing immunotherapy for metastatic HPV + OPSCC.

By contrast, the metastatic tumor harbored T cells expressing ZNF683 (Hobit), KLRB1 (CD161) and PTPN22. Although TRM cells were traditionally considered non-circulating populations restricted to barrier tissues such as skin and mucosa, 39 recent evidence indicates that circulating TRM-like cells expressing CD161, ITGAE (CD103) and ZNF683 (Hobit) can mediate systemic immune responses upon preoperative ICI therapy. 40 , 41 Notably, the frequency of ZNF683 + T cells prior to ICI has been linked to therapeutic response, 42 consistent with outcomes in the KEYNOTE-689 trial. 43

Our findings demonstrated that HPV-specific CD8+ T-cell clones persisting in both primary and metastatic lesions exhibited high KLRB1 (CD161) expression, which is a hallmark of multifunctional T cells with cytotoxic, tissue-homing, and long-term memory properties. 44 , 45 This observation aligns with a recent report showing that CD161 + cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) in HPV + OPSCC displayed a “T cell-inflamed” transcriptional program and retain functional reversibility with ICI. 46 That study further demonstrated that intratumoral CD161 + CTL density in HPV + tumor is associated with favorable treatment response and prolonged survival, supporting CD161 as a prognostic and therapeutically actionable marker. However, accumulating evidence, including transcriptomic profiling of HPV-associated OPSCC, indicates a context-dependent regulatory role for CD161 + TRM cells. 34 Dense infiltration of these cells can impair follicular helper (Tfh)-B cell interactions through reduced expression of CLEC2D (CD161 ligand) in B cells, thereby hindering tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) formation and attenuating ICI responsiveness. 34

In line with this dual model, we observed lower expression of CXCL13, which is known to promote B-cell recruitment and TLS organization in other cancers, 47–49 in HPV-specific T cells in metastatic lesions compared with primary tumors. This reduction likely reflects diminished TLS engagement and an immunoregulatory adaptation of TRM-like CD8+ T cells in the metastatic environment. Furthermore, metastatic lesions exhibited increased PTPN22 expression, which inhibits TCR signaling. 50 Given that PTPN22 blockade enhances antitumor immunity, 51 targeting this pathway may improve therapeutic efficacy.

Taken together, these findings suggest that KLRB1 expression may reflect distinct functional states depending on the tumor microenvironment and clinical context, including broader activation or exhaustion-associated states in HNSCC beyond HPV-specific T-cell responses, and the current data do not support a definitive interpretation of its biological role.

Across our cohort, a higher KLRB1/CD8A ratio in primary tumors was associated with reduced recurrence, suggesting a potential relationship between CD161 + memory-like CD8+ T-cell states and favorable clinical outcome in HPV + OPSCC. Overall, these findings raise the possibility that TRM-like CD161 + CD8+ T-cell populations may be associated with persistent intratumoral HPV-reactive T-cell states in metastatic HPV + OPSCC. However, because these longitudinal observations were derived from a single paired primary–metastatic case, further validation in larger longitudinal cohorts will be necessary.

This study has several limitations. First, single-cell analyses were limited to four samples from three patients, including one longitudinal pair. Second, functional avidity was assessed using Jurkat reporter systems rather than primary T cells, which made it difficult to perform tumor-killing assays. Third, peptide screening focused on predicted 9–10-mer epitopes in a restricted HLA context. In addition, only the top 20 most frequent clonotypes from each exhausted cluster were selected for functional screening, and rare HPV-reactive high-avidity clonotypes may therefore have been missed. Fourth, clonotype detection depended on sequencing depth, cell recovery, and read counts in both scRNA/TCR-seq and bulk TCR-seq analyses. Therefore, clonotypes classified as “lost” or “new” may in some cases represent low-frequency clones below the detection threshold rather than complete biological disappearance or emergence, particularly in stressed or mitochondrial-high cell populations with reduced TCR transcript recovery. Fifth, external validation using independent public scRNA-seq datasets and larger longitudinal cohorts was not performed in the present study. Despite these limitations, our findings establish a framework for linking longitudinal TCR dynamics to clinical outcome and therapeutic design in HPV + OPSCC.

In conclusion, we identified tumor-specific TCRs within exhausted CD8+ T cells in HPV + OPSCC, demonstrating that some high-avidity clones persist even in metastatic lesions.

HPV-specific T cells with elevated KLRB1 (CD161) expression were associated with persistent clonotypes, and a higher KLRB1/CD8A ratio was associated with reduced recurrence risk in HPV-positive OPSCC. These findings suggest that KLRB1 expression may reflect persistent HPV-reactive T-cell states and warrant further validation in larger independent cohorts.
