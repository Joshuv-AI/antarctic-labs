Rigidity of McMullen Julia sets
Yan Gao, Luxian Yang, Jinsong Zeng

Abstract
. We provide a complete quasisymmetric classification of the Julia sets of postcritically finite McMullen maps f λ ​ ( z ) = z n + λ / z n f_{\lambda}(z)=z^{n}+\lambda/z^{n} with λ ∈ ℂ ∗ \lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*} and n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 , and prove that the quasisymmetry group of each such Julia set is exactly the finite dihedral group generated by the natural symmetries of the map. These results establish quasisymmetric rigidity for all topological classes in this family, including Sierpiński-like carpets, necklaces, and clusters, and provide the first known examples of rigid Julia sets in each of the three classes.

1.
Introduction

Quasisymmetric geometry of planar fractal sets has attracted considerable interest recently. A homeomorphism
h:X\to Y
between subsets of the Riemann sphere
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
is
quasisymmetric
if it extends to an orientation-preserving homeomorphism over
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
and there exists a homeomorphism
\eta:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that for any distinct
u,v,w\in X
,

\frac{\sigma(h(u),h(v))}{\sigma(h(u),h(w))}\leq\eta\!\left(\frac{\sigma(u,v)}{\sigma(u,w)}\right),

where
\sigma
is the standard spherical metric.

A central problem in quasiconformal geometry is to classify fractal sets up to quasisymmetric equivalence, and to investigate the quasisymmetric rigidity for canonical representatives of each class
[
Bon06
]
. A subset of the Riemann sphere is said to be
quasisymmetrically rigid
if every quasisymmetric self-homeomorphism of this set is the restriction of a Möbius transformation of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
or an even more restrictive map.

Quasisymmetric rigidity has been established for several types of fractal sets, including the standard Sierpiński carpet
S_{p}
for odd integer
p\geq 3

[
BM13
,
BM20
]
, the slit carpet
S_{2}

[
Mer10
]
, and the Schottky sets
[
BKM
,
Mer12
]
.

Conformal dynamics provides another rich source of planar fractal sets, including Julia sets of rational maps and limit sets of Kleinian groups. These two families often produce topologically equivalent fractals, which are conjecturally never quasisymmetrically equivalent, with the exception of two cases: Jordan curves and
\overline{\mathbb{C}}

[
LLMM
]
.

By
[
BLM
]
, a Sierpiński carpet arising as the Julia set of a
postcritically finite
(i.e., all critical points are eventually periodic,
PCF
for short) rational map is quasisymmetrically rigid, and this result was extended to some non-PCF cases in
[
QYZ
]
.
In contrast, many other Julia sets are known to lack quasisymmetric rigidity. Examples include the Basilica Julia set
[
LM
,
LMM
]
, Apollonian gasket Julia sets
[
LLMM
]
, and finitely ramified fractal Julia sets
[
BF
]
.

In this paper, we study the quasisymmetric rigidity for Julia sets of PCF rational maps in the
McMullen family

f_{\lambda}(z)=z^{n}+\dfrac{\lambda}{z^{n}},

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}=\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
n\geq 2
is an integer. This family has been shown to exhibit a variety of interesting dynamical and topological phenomena; see, for example
[
DLU
,
QWY
,
QRWY
]
.

There exist infinitely many PCF parameters
\lambda
for which the maps
f_{\lambda}
have Sierpiński carpet Julia sets. Devaney and Pilgrim posed a question on classifying these Julia sets up to quasisymmetric equivalence
[
DP
]
.
One such map is
f(z)=z^{2}-1/(16z^{2})
. The authors of
[
BLM
]
speculate that the two Möbius transformations

\xi_{1}(z)=\textbf{i}z\quad\text{and}\quad\xi_{2}(z)=\frac{1}{4z}

generate the quasisymmetry group of the Julia set
J_{f}
; see Figure
1
.

There also exist infinitely many PCF parameters
\lambda
for which the Julia sets of
f_{\lambda}
are not Sierpiński carpets. As will be shown in Theorem
1.8
, the Julia sets of PCF McMullen maps fall into four distinct topological classes: Sierpiński carpets, Sierpiński-like carpets, necklaces, and clusters. Figure
1
illustrates examples of each class.

The main results of the paper resolve the quasisymmetric classification and rigidity problems for Julia sets of all PCF McMullen maps. In particular, we confirm the above speculation raised in
[
BLM
]
, and answer the question of Devaney and Pilgrim
[
DP
]
.

1.1.
Statement of main results

For a McMullen map
f_{\lambda}(z)=z^{n}+\lambda/z^{n}
, the integer
n\geq 2
is called its
exponent
.
It is easy to check that the Julia set of
f_{\lambda}
is invariant under the rotation
z\mapsto e^{\pi\textbf{i}/n}z
and the involution
z\mapsto\sqrt[n]{\lambda}/z
(Lemma
2.2
). Hence, the group of all quasisymmetric self-homeomorphisms of the Julia set (called the
quasisymmetry group
) contains the group
G_{\lambda}
generated by these two Möbius transformations. The following theorem shows that this inclusion is in fact an equality.

Theorem 1.1

(Quasisymmetry group)
.

Let
f_{\lambda}
be a PCF McMullen map of exponent
n\geq 2
. Then the quasisymmetry group of the Julia set of
f_{\lambda}
is exactly
G_{\lambda}
.

Haïssinsky and Pilgrim proved that for McMullen maps with Cantor-circle Julia sets (non-PCF case), distinct exponents imply quasisymmetric inequivalence
[
HP
]
. Since the order of
G_{\lambda}
is a quasisymmetry invariant, we obtain a corollary to Theorem
1.1
.

Corollary 1.2
.

If two PCF McMullen maps have distinct exponents, then their Julia sets are not quasisymmetrically equivalent.

Theorem
1.1
implies that if the Julia set of
f_{\lambda}
is a Sierpiński carpet, then it is not quasisymmetrically equivalent to the standard Sierpiński carpet
S_{p}
. As non-elementary Kleinian limit sets have infinite quasisymmetry groups, Theorem
1.1
excludes quasisymmetric equivalence between such limit sets and PCF McMullen Julia sets.

The following result gives the quasisymmetric classification of Julia sets of PCF McMullen maps. For a set
E\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{C}}
and
a\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
, we write
aE=\{az:z\in E\}
.

Theorem 1.3

(Quasisymmetric classification)
.

Let
f_{\lambda}
and
f_{\tilde{\lambda}}
be PCF McMullen maps with the same exponent
n\geq 2
, and let
J
and
\tilde{J}
be their Julia sets, respectively. Then
J
and
\tilde{J}
are quasisymmetrically equivalent if and only if
\tilde{\lambda}=e^{\textbf{i}\theta}\lambda
with
\theta=2\pi k/(n-1)
for
k\in\{0,\ldots,n-2\}
. In this case, we have
\tilde{J}=e^{\textbf{i}\theta/(2n)}J
.

Geometrically, Theorem
1.3
reflects the rotational symmetry of the parameter space. For a fixed exponent
n
, the connectedness locus

\Lambda_{n}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}:\text{the Julia set of $f_{\lambda}$ is connected.}\}

is invariant under the rotation
\lambda\mapsto e^{2\pi\textbf{i}/(n-1)}\lambda
, see Figure
2
. This parameter rotation corresponds precisely to a rotation of the Julia set by
e^{\pi\textbf{i}/(n(n-1))}
, yielding a quasisymmetrically equivalent copy. Theorem
1.3
asserts that this is the
only
source of quasisymmetric equivalence; hence the quasisymmetric moduli space of PCF McMullen Julia sets is the quotient of
\Lambda_{n}
by this
(n-1)
-fold rotation. In particular, for
n=2
the parameter rotation is trivial, so distinct parameters always produce Julia sets that are not quasisymmetrically equivalent.

As an interesting byproduct of these rigidity results, we obtain the following:

Corollary 1.4
.

A PCF McMullen map is uniquely determined by its Julia set.

Proof of Corollary
1.4
.

Suppose that
f_{\lambda}
and
f_{\tilde{\lambda}}
are two PCF McMullen maps with exponents
n,\tilde{n}\geq 2
respectively. Assume that
J=\tilde{J}
. Then by Corollary
1.2
,
n=\tilde{n}
. Applying Theorem
1.3
to
\xi=\textup{id}
, we obtain
\tilde{\lambda}=e^{\textbf{i}\theta}\lambda
, where
\theta=2k\pi/(n-1)
for some integer
k
satisfying
0\leq k<n-1
, and the rotation
z\mapsto e^{\textbf{i}\theta/(2n)}z
belongs to the quasisymmetry group of
J
. By Theorem
1.1
, there exists an integer
m
such that
\theta/(2n)=m\pi/n
. This implies
k=m(n-1)
. The condition
0\leq k<n-1
forces
k=0
. Hence,
\lambda=\tilde{\lambda}
.
∎

g\circ f_{\lambda}\neq f_{\lambda}\circ g

.

Let
f_{\lambda}
and
f_{\tilde{\lambda}}
be PCF McMullen maps with the same exponent
n\geq 2
, and let
J
and

be their Julia sets, respectively. Suppose that
\xi:J\to\tilde{J}

\theta=2\pi k/(n-1)

(1)

1.2.
Outline of the proof and main techniques

In
[
BLM
]
, the authors observe that any quasisymmetry between Sierpiński carpet Julia sets of PCF rational maps forces a hidden relation between the underlying dynamics:

Proposition 1.6

(
[
BLM
, Theorem 1.4]
)
.

Let
J
and
\tilde{J}
be Sierpiński carpet Julia sets of PCF rational maps
f
and
\tilde{f}
, respectively. If
\xi:J\to\tilde{J}
is a quasisymmetry, then there exist integers
m^{\prime},l\geq 1
and
m\geq 0
such that

\tilde{f}^{m^{\prime}}\circ\xi=\tilde{f}^{m}\circ\xi\circ f^{l}\text{\,\,on }J.

From this, they prove quasisymmetric rigidity for Sierpiński carpet Julia sets. We observe that the passage from the dynamical relation in Proposition
1.6
to the Möbius property works in wider generality: it applies to any PCF rational map whose Fatou domains are Jordan domains:

Proposition 1.7
.

Let
f
and
\tilde{f}
be PCF rational maps with non-empty Fatou sets such that each Fatou domain of
f
is a Jordan domain. Suppose
\xi:J\to\tilde{J}
is a quasisymmetry satisfying the dynamical relation

(1.1)

\tilde{f}^{m^{\prime}}\circ\xi=\tilde{f}^{m}\circ\xi\circ f^{l}\text{\,\,on\,\,\,}J

for some integers
m^{\prime},l\geq 1
and
m\geq 0
.
Then
\xi
is the restriction of a Möbius transformation, and (
1.1
) still holds on
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
(also denoted by
\xi
). In particular,
\xi
maps each Fatou domain of
f
and its center to those of
\tilde{f}
.

To prove Theorem
1.5
, we establish the dynamical relation (
1.1
) for all PCF McMullen maps (Sections
5
–
7
). Proposition
1.7
then implies that
\xi
is the restriction of a Möbius transformation. Tracking the images of the critical Fatou domains
B
and
T
(containing
\infty
and
0
, respectively) under the dynamical relation, we find that
\xi
fixes
0
and
\infty
up to an element of
G_{\lambda}
, whence
\xi(z)=az
. A comparison of Laurent expansions at infinity yields
|a|=1
; comparing local degrees further forces
a^{2n}
to be an
(n-1)
-th root of unity. This identifies
\xi
as the rotation
z\mapsto e^{\textbf{i}\theta/(2n)}z
and yields Theorem
1.5
.

The proof of the dynamical relation (
1.1
) proceeds differently for each topological class of Julia sets. We therefore need the following topological classification theorem for McMullen maps.

A
continuum
is a compact and connected subset of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
containing at least two points. A continuum is called
full
if its complement in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
is connected. A point
z
in a compact set
E
is called
buried
in
E
if it does not lie on the boundary of any component of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus E
. A continuous onto map
\pi:\overline{\mathbb{C}}\to\overline{\mathbb{C}}
is a
quotient map
if
\pi^{-1}(z)
is a singleton or a full continuum for all
z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}
.

These notions allow us to describe the four possible topological classes of Julia sets for PCF McMullen maps precisely.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
J
be the Julia set of a PCF McMullen map
f_{\lambda}
with exponent
n\geq 2
. Then exactly one of the following occurs:

(1)

J
is a Sierpiński carpet;

(2)

J
is a
Sierpiński-like carpet
, i.e., the image of a Sierpiński carpet by a quotient map over
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
that is injective on the buried point set;

(3)

J
is a
necklace
, i.e., the image of a Cantor-circle by a quotient map over
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
that is at most two-to-one on the Cantor-circle;

(4)

J
is a
cluster
, i.e., the Fatou domains
B
and
T
(containing
\infty
and 0, respectively) can be joined by a curve meeting
J
in countably many points.

Moreover, Case
(3)
occurs only when
n\geq 3
.

Figure
1
shows examples of each class. We note that all necklace Julia sets of the same exponent are topologically equivalent and have an infinite homeomorphism group. In contrast, cluster Julia sets of McMullen maps exhibit a stronger rigidity: they are topologically rigid (hence also quasisymmetrically rigid); see Theorem
7.1
.

The cases of Sierpiński carpet and necklace Julia sets are well understood
[
DLU
,
QWY
,
QRWY
]
. Theorem
1.8
completes the classification by showing that the remaining Julia sets of PCF McMullen maps are either Sierpiński-like or cluster.
To this end, we use the tool of Fatou chains developed in
[
CGZ
]
(see Section
3.1
), instead of the Yoccoz puzzle technique in previous works.

Building on the classification of Theorem
1.8
, we establish the dynamical relation by treating each topological class separately. The outline is as follows.

For Sierpiński carpet Julia sets, relation (
1.1
) has been established in
[
BLM
]
, and the proof depends essentially on the quasisymmetric rigidity of Schottky maps
[
Mer14
]
and the uniformization of Sierpiński carpets
[
Bon11
]
.

For Sierpiński-like carpet and necklace Julia sets, we use a lifting argument: blowing up
J
to a Sierpiński carpet and a standard Cantor-circle, respectively; lifting
\xi
to a quasisymmetry
\tilde{\xi}
between the blown-up Julia sets of model maps
g
; then showing that
\tilde{\xi}
and
g
satisfy (
1.1
), which projects down to
\xi
and the original McMullen maps.

Specifically, when
J
is a Sierpiński-like carpet, such a model map exists by
[
CGZ
, Theorem 1.6]
. The heart of the argument is to prove that the lifted map
\tilde{\xi}
is quasisymmetric; this relies on a distortion estimate (Lemma
5.2
) controlling the diameters of iterates. Once
\tilde{\xi}
is known to be quasisymmetric, Proposition
1.6
applies and yields the required dynamical relation (Theorem
5.1
).

When
J
is a necklace, we prove that it can be blown up to a standard Cantor-circle, the Julia set of a model map
g
(Theorem
4.3
). Strikingly, although the Cantor-circle admits infinitely many quasisymmetries, the rule that “equivalent points are mapped to equivalent points” forces the lift
\tilde{\xi}
to be a rotation, which therefore satisfies the desired relation (Theorem
6.2
).

For cluster Julia sets, we introduce the connecting number, a topological invariant: simply count how many components connect two Fatou domains. We prove that
B
and
T
are the only pair with the maximal count, so any homeomorphism must preserve these two domains. Moreover, the count records how deep each Fatou domain sits in the cluster. Using this, we argue inductively that the homeomorphism respects the entire internal structure, then label the Fatou domains to match the two dynamics, and pass to the limit to obtain the conjugacy (Theorem
7.1
).

1.3.
Further problems and generalizations

The arguments in this paper lead to several natural generalizations. Theorem
5.1
is stated for general PCF rational maps with Sierpiński-like carpet Julia sets. Together with Proposition
1.7
, this gives quasisymmetric rigidity for such Julia sets in general. Similarly, the necklace case admits a natural generalization via folding rational maps, introduced and studied extensively in
[
CPT2
, Section 8]
; it seems likely that the Cantor-circle lifting argument applies to these maps as well, and their quasisymmetric rigidity is the subject of our ongoing work.

In a broader context, Cui together with the first and third authors have shown that the dynamics of any PCF rational map with non-empty Fatou set decomposes into three types of subsystems: exact subsystems, annular subsystems, and cluster subsystems
[
CGZ
, Theorem 1.7]
. Sierpiński-like carpets arise as special cases of exact subsystems; necklaces are the simplest instances of annular subsystems; and cluster Julia sets of McMullen maps together with polynomial Julia sets are special cases of cluster subsystems. In general, a cluster Julia set is one in which every pair of Fatou domains can be joined by a curve meeting the Julia set in at most countably many points. Our results therefore cover the simplest representatives of each subsystem type.

This motivates the following problem:

Problem 1
.

If a PCF rational map has non-empty Fatou set and its Julia set is not a cluster, does it necessarily exhibit quasisymmetric rigidity?

In contrast, cluster Julia sets behave quite differently. As shown by Lyubich and Merenkov
[
LM
]
, the Basilica Julia set admits quasisymmetric self-maps that are not Möbius; similarly, Apollonian gasket Julia sets
[
LLMM
]
and finitely ramified fractal Julia sets
[
BF
]
are known to possess nontrivial quasisymmetry groups. To date, the cluster Julia sets of PCF McMullen maps remain the first known examples in the cluster family to exhibit quasisymmetric rigidity.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
proves some basic properties of McMullen maps. Section
3
studies the symmetry of Fatou chains. Section
4
presents the blow-up theorems for Sierpiński-like and necklace Julia sets and gives the proof of Theorem
1.8
. Sections
5
,
6
and
7
establish the dynamical relation for PCF McMullen maps whose Julia sets are Sierpiński-like carpets, necklaces and clusters, respectively. Section
8
completes the proof of the rigidity theorems (Theorem
1.5
).

2.
Topology, symmetries and Fatou domains

This section develops the topological properties and symmetries of McMullen maps. We establish a rotation-invariant continuum lemma, and employ the dihedral symmetry to control the geometry of the Julia sets and Fatou domains.

2.1.
A topological lemma

We begin with a topological lemma to be used repeatedly below. We say that a set
E\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{C}}

separates
two points
x,y\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}
if
x
and
y
lie in different connected components of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus E
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
E\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0,\infty\}
be a continuum and let
m\geq 2
be an integer.

(1)

If
E\cap e^{\textup{{i}}\theta}E\neq\emptyset
for some
\theta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
, then
K=\overline{\bigcup_{k\geq 0}e^{\textup{{i}}k\theta}E}
separates
0
and
\infty
.

(2)

If
E
separates
0
and
\infty
, then for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
,
E\cap e^{\textup{{i}}\theta}E\neq\emptyset
.

(3)

If
e^{\textup{{i}}\theta}E=E
for some
\theta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus 2\pi\mathbb{Z}

E
separates 0 and
\infty

z
and
e^{\textbf{i}k_{0}\theta}z
. By Statement (1),

\Gamma=\gamma\cup e^{\textbf{i}k_{0}\theta}\gamma\cup\cdots\cup e^{\textbf{i}k_{0}(q-1)\theta}\gamma

separates
0
and
\infty
. Since
E=e^{\textbf{i}k_{0}\theta}E
and
\gamma\cap E=\emptyset
, we have
\Gamma\cap E=\emptyset
. Hence
\Gamma\subseteq U
. This contradicts the fact that
U
does not separate
0
and
\infty
.

(4) Suppose that
\omega_{0}E\cap E\not=\emptyset
for some non-primitive
m
-th root of unity
\omega_{0}
. Then there exist
z,\omega_{0}z\in E
joined by an open arc
\gamma\subseteq A
.
By Statement (1),

\Gamma=\overline{\gamma}\cup\omega_{0}\overline{\gamma}\cup\cdots\cup\omega_{0}^{m-1}\overline{\gamma}

separates
0
and
\infty
. Since
\omega_{0}
is non-primitive, there exists
\omega
such that
\omega^{m}=1
and
\omega\neq\omega_{0}^{i}
for any integer
i\geq 0
. By Statement (2),
\Gamma\cap\omega\Gamma\neq\emptyset
. Hence
\overline{\gamma}\cap\omega\omega_{0}^{j}\overline{\gamma}\neq\emptyset
for some integer

. If the intersection occurs only at the endpoints of
\gamma
, then
\omega=\omega_{0}^{i}
for some integer
i\geq 1
, a contradiction. Thus
\gamma\cap\omega\omega_{0}^{j}\gamma\neq\emptyset
. Since
\gamma\subseteq A
and
(\omega\omega_{0}^{j})^{m}=1
with
\omega\omega_{0}^{j}\neq 1
, the hypothesis yields
(\omega\omega_{0}^{j})A\cap A=\emptyset
, a contradiction.
∎

2.2.
Symmetries of McMullen maps

The results in this subsection apply to all McMullen maps

f_{\lambda}(z)=z^{n}+{\lambda}/{z^{n}},

where
n\geq 2
is an integer and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
. For simplicity, we write
f=f_{\lambda}
.

Let
J
denote the Julia set of
f
. Note that
\infty
is a superattracting fixed point of
f
and that
f^{-1}(\infty)=\{0,\infty\}
. We write
B
for the immediate basin of
\infty
, and let
T
be the component of
f^{-1}(B)
containing
0
. The map
f
has
2n
simple critical points

\textup{Crit}=\{\sqrt[2n]{\lambda}\,\omega:\omega^{2n}=1\},

called the
free
critical points, and two
free
critical values
\pm v=\pm 2\sqrt{\lambda}
. Moreover,
f
satisfies
f(-z)=(-1)^{n}f(z)
; hence
f(v)=f(-v)
when
n
is even and
f(v)=-f(-v)
when
n
is odd.

Recall that
G_{\lambda}
denotes the group generated by the rotation
z\mapsto e^{\pi\textbf{i}/n}z
and the involution
z\mapsto\sqrt[n]{\lambda}/z
, which is the dihedral group
D_{2n}
of order
4n
.

Lemma 2.2

(
G_{\lambda}
-invariance of the Julia set)
.

Let
\omega
be a
2n
-th root of unity, and
\tau
be the involution given by
z\mapsto\tilde{\omega}/z
, where
\tilde{\omega}^{n}=\lambda
. Then

(1)

J=\omega J
,
B=\omega B
, and
T=\omega T
.

(2)

\tau(B)=T,\tau(T)=B
and
\tau(J)=J
.

Proof.

The proof relies on the symmetry satisfied by McMullen maps
f
, namely

(2.1)

f(\omega z)=\omega^{n}f(z)

and
f\circ\tau(z)=f(z)
.
This implies that escaping to infinity under iteration of
f
is preserved by both maps
z\mapsto\omega z
and
\tau
. Consequently, the attracting basin of infinity, and therefore its boundary, which is the Julia set
J
, is invariant under these transformations. The remaining statements follow from a direct verification using the definitions of
B
and
T
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
U
be a Fatou domain of
f
such that
U\neq B,T
and
f(U)\neq T
. Then
\omega\overline{U}\cap\overline{U}=\emptyset
for any
\omega\neq 1
with
\omega^{2n}=1
.

Proof.

Since
\omega J=J
for any
\omega\neq 1
with
\omega^{2n}=1
(Lemma
2.2
), the set
\omega U
is also a Fatou domain of
f
. By Lemma
2.1
(3),
U\cap\omega U=\emptyset
. Thus,
E=\overline{U}
and
A=U
satisfies the assumption of Lemma
2.1
(4). We then obtain that
\omega\overline{U}\cap\overline{U}=\emptyset
when
\omega
is non-primitive.

If
\omega
is primitive, then
\omega^{n}=-1
. By symmetry (
2.1
), we have
f(\omega\overline{U})=-f(\overline{U})
. Since
f(U)\neq B,T
by assumption, we have
\omega^{k}f(U)\cap f(U)=\emptyset
whenever
\omega^{k}\neq 1
by Lemma
2.1
(3). Note that
-1
is a non-primitive
2n
-th root of unity for
n\geq 2
, we apply Lemma
2.1
(4) to
E=\overline{f(U)}
and
A=f(U)
with
\omega_{0}=-1
to obtain

(-\overline{f(U)})\cap\overline{f(U)}=\emptyset.

Since
f(\omega U)=-f(U)
, it follows that

f(\omega\overline{U}\cap\overline{U})\subset f(\omega\overline{U})\cap f(\overline{U})=(-\overline{f(U)})\cap\overline{f(U)}=\emptyset.

Hence,
\omega\overline{U}\cap\overline{U}=\emptyset
. Now the proof is complete.
∎

The following lemma implies that the parameter space of McMullen maps also has the rotational symmetries; see Figure
2
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
f=f_{\lambda}
be a McMullen map with exponent
n\geq 2
.

(1) The Julia sets of
g(z):=\omega f(z)
and
f
coincide for any
2n
-th root
\omega
of unity.

(2) Suppose
\tilde{\lambda}=e^{{\bf i}\theta}\lambda
with
\theta={2\pi k}/{(n-1)},k=1,\ldots,n-2
. Let
\tilde{J}
be the Julia set of
f_{\tilde{\lambda}}
. Then
\tilde{J}=e^{{\bf i}\theta/(2n)}J
.

Proof.

(1) By the symmetry (
2.1
),

g^{2}(z)=\omega f(\omega f(z))=\omega\cdot\omega^{n}f^{2}(z)=\omega^{n+1}f^{2}(z).

By induction,
g^{k}(z)=\omega^{a_{k}}f^{k}(z)
for all
k\geq 1
, where
a_{k}=(n^{k}-1)/(n-1)
. Consequently, a point
z
tends to
\infty
under iteration of
f
if and only if it does so under
g
. Hence
f
and
g
have the same attracting basin of
\infty
, and therefore
J_{g}=J
.

(2) Let
\xi(z)=e^{\textbf{i}\theta/(2n)}z
be a rotation. The conjugate map

h(z)=\xi\circ f\circ\xi^{-1}(z)=e^{\textbf{i}\theta/(2n)}\left(\frac{z^{n}}{e^{\textbf{i}\theta/2}}+\frac{\lambda e^{\textbf{i}\theta/2}}{z^{n}}\right)

equals
e^{-\pi\textbf{i}k/n}f_{\tilde{\lambda}}(z)
, where
\tilde{\lambda}=e^{\textbf{i}\theta}\lambda
. By Statement (1) the Julia sets of
h
and

coincide. Therefore
\tilde{J}=\xi(J)
.
∎

2.3.
Fatou domains
B
and
T

We now assume that the McMullen map
f
is PCF with exponent
n\geq 2
. Then
B\neq T
and
J
is connected and locally connected. Since both
B
and
T
are simply connected, there exist unique Riemann mappings

\phi_{B}:B\to\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}\ \textup{ and }\ \phi_{T}:T\to{\mathbb{D}}

satisfying
\phi^{\prime}_{B}(\infty)>0
and
\phi^{\prime}_{T}(0)>0
.

The maps
\phi_{B}
and
\phi_{T}
are called
Böttcher maps
of
f
on
B
and
T
, respectively. We define
\phi
on
B\cup T
by
\phi|_{B}=\phi_{B}
and
\phi|_{T}=\phi_{T}
.
By the uniqueness of Riemann maps and Lemma
2.2
, we have
\phi(\omega z)=\omega\phi(z)
.
The preimages of radial rays in
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
of
\phi
are called
internal rays
in
T
and
B
, respectively.

The Yoccoz puzzle technique was employed in
[
QWY
]
to prove that for a McMullen map with connected Julia set and exponent
n\geq 3
, the basin
B
is a Jordan domain. Their approach fails for
n=2
, leaving open whether
\partial B
is a Jordan curve in this case. When
f
is PCF, by using Lemma
2.1
, instead of the Yoccoz puzzle technique, we obtain the following result: both
B
and
T
are Jordan domains.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
f
be a PCF McMullen map with exponent
n\geq 2
. Then the Fatou domains
B
and
T
are Jordan domains.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction and assume
\partial B
is not a Jordan curve. Since
B
is simply connected and
\partial B
is locally connected,
K=\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus B
is a full continuum and each component of
\textup{int}(K)
is a Jordan domain. Let
\Omega_{0}
be the component of
\textup{int}(K)
containing
0
.

We claim that
f
maps any component
\Omega\neq\Omega_{0}
of
\textup{int}(K)
onto a component of
\textup{int}(K)
. In fact, the set
f(\Omega)
is disjoint from
B
since
f^{-1}(\infty)=\{0,\infty\}
. Moreover,
f(\partial\Omega)\subseteq\partial B=\partial K
. Consequently, the open connected set
f(\Omega)
is a component of
\textup{int}(K)
. This proves the claim.

Since for any point
z\in\Omega_{0}
, the Julia set
J
is contained in the closure of the backward orbit
O^{-}(z)=\{w:f^{k}(w)=z,k\in\mathbb{N}\}
, there exists a component
\Omega\neq\Omega_{0}
of
\textup{int}(K)
such that
f(\Omega)=\Omega_{0}
. Otherwise,
f^{-1}(\Omega_{0})\subset\Omega_{0}
implies that
O^{-}(z)
is contained in
\overline{\Omega_{0}}
. This contradicts that
\partial B\setminus\overline{\Omega_{0}}\neq\emptyset
.

Let
\omega
be a primitive
2n
-th root of unity. Since
\omega\overline{B}=\overline{B}
(Lemma
2.2
), it follows from Lemma
2.1
(3) that
the
2n
domains
\omega^{k}\Omega,k=0,\ldots,2n-1
are pairwise disjoint, and are all mapped onto
\Omega_{0}
by the symmetry (
2.1
). Note that the closures of any two components of
{\rm int}(K)
intersect in at most one point. Then there exists
w\in\partial\Omega_{0}
which does not lie in the intersection
\partial\Omega_{0}\cap(\bigcup_{k=0}^{2n-1}\omega^{k}\overline{\Omega})
, and
2n
distinct points
z_{k}\in\omega^{k}\overline{\Omega}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{0}},k=0,\ldots,2n-1
such that
f(z_{k})=w
. As
T\subset\Omega_{0}
and
f(\partial T)=\partial B
, there also exists a point
z\in\overline{\Omega}_{0}
such that
f(z)=w
. Thus,
f^{-1}(w)
consists of at least
2n+1
distinct points, a contradiction to the degree of
f
. Therefore,
\partial B
is a Jordan curve.

We now show, by contradiction, that
\partial T
is a Jordan curve. Assume that there are two internal rays of
T
landing at the same point
z\in\partial T
. Since
\partial B
is a Jordan curve, their images under
f
form a single internal ray
R
of
B
landing at
f(z)\in\partial B
. Clearly,
z
is a free critical point. Let
W
be the bounded domain enclosed by these two rays. Then

W\subseteq\Omega_{0}\textup{ and }f(W)\supseteq\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{R}.

Thus,
W
contains a component of
f^{-1}(W)
. By the Lefschetz fixed point theorem,
W
contains at least one fixed point of
f
.

By symmetry (
2.1
), all free critical points of
f
lie on
\partial\Omega_{0}
, each receiving at least two internal rays of
T
. Similar arguments as above show that each bounded domain enclosed by the two internal rays landing at a free critical point contains a fixed point of
f
. Hence,
\Omega_{0}
contains at least
2n
fixed points of
f
. Moreover,
f:\overline{B}\to\overline{B}
is conjugate to
z\mapsto z^{n}
on
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\mathbb{D}
, so
f
has
n
fixed points on
\overline{B}
. Hence,
f
would have at least
3n
fixed points in total. This contradicts that a rational map of degree
2n
has exactly
2n+1
fixed points counted with multiplicity and
n\geq 2
. Therefore,
\partial T
is a Jordan curve.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
f
be a PCF McMullen map with exponent
n\geq 2
. Then
\partial B\cap\partial T\neq\emptyset
if and only if
v\in\partial B
. In this case,
\partial B\cap\partial T=\textup{Crit}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\partial B\cap\partial T\neq\emptyset
. For any
z\in\partial B\cap\partial T
, internal rays in
B
and
T
landing at
z
map under
f
to a single internal ray of
B
; hence
z
is a free critical point and
f(z)=v\in\partial B
. Thus
\partial B\cap\partial T\subseteq\textup{Crit}
.

Conversely, suppose
v\in\partial B
. Then
-v\in\partial B
by Lemma
2.2
. Let
c\in\textup{Crit}
with
f(c)=v
. Since
c
is a simple critical point,
f
near
c
is locally a two-to-one branched cover. The internal ray of
B
landing at
v
lifts under
f
to two rays landing at
c
. Since both
\partial B
and
\partial T
are Jordan domains, the two rays lie in distinct components of
f^{-1}(B)=B\cup T
; hence one is in
B
and the other in
T
. This implies
c\in\partial B\cap\partial T
. The same holds for preimages of
-v
. Thus
\textup{Crit}\subset\partial B\cap\partial T
and
\partial B\cap\partial T\neq\emptyset
.

Combining the two directions yields
\partial B\cap\partial T=\textup{Crit}
in this case.
∎

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
f
be a PCF McMullen map with
n\geq 2
. Then every Fatou domain eventually iterated onto
B
is a Jordan domain. If
f
has no bounded attracting cycles, then exactly one of the following holds:

(1)

f^{k}(v)=\infty
for some integer
k\geq 0
. Then
k\geq 2
and
J
is a Sierpiński carpet.

(2)

v\in\partial B
. This occurs if and only if
\partial B\cap\partial T\neq\emptyset
. In this case,
\partial B\cap\partial T=\textup{Crit}
and thus
J
is a cluster.

(3)

v\in\partial T
. Then there exists a bounded Fatou domain
U
such that
\overline{U}
and
e^{\pi\textup{{i}}/n}\overline{U}
meet at a free critical point, and such that

f^{-1}(\overline{T})=\bigcup_{k=0}^{2n-1}e^{\pi\textup{{i}}k/n}\overline{U}

is a degenerate annulus.
When
n=2
, this case does not occur.

(4)

f^{k}(v)\in\partial T
for some integer
k\geq 1
. Then the components of

T\cup f^{-1}(T)\cup\cdots\cup f^{-k}(T)

are Jordan domains with pairwise disjoint closures; the free critical values
\pm v
lie on the boundaries of two of them, and the preimage of each such closure is a full continuum whose interior consists of two Jordan domains.

(5)

v
is buried in
J
. Then
J
is a Sierpiński carpet.

Proof.

We first claim: if
\partial B\cap P_{f}=\emptyset
, then every Fatou domain eventually mapped onto
B
is a Jordan domain with pairwise disjoint closures. Here
P_{f}
denotes the postcritical set of
f
. Since
\partial B
is a Jordan curve (Lemma
2.5
) and
\overline{B}
contains only one postcritical point, each component of
f^{-i}(\partial B)
(
i\geq 1
) is a Jordan curve; any Fatou domain mapped onto
B
by
f^{i}
is bounded by such a component. For disjointness, if two such domains map to
B
by
f^{i}
and
f^{j}
with
i\leq j
, forward iteration by
f^{i}
sends their boundaries into
\partial B
and
f^{-(j-i)}(\partial B)
, which are disjoint because
\partial B\cap P_{f}=\emptyset
.

If
f
has a bounded attracting cycle, then
\partial B\cap P_{f}=\emptyset
, and the claim implies that any Fatou domain iterated onto
B
is a Jordan domain.

(1) Since
0
is the only pole and
v\neq 0
, we have
k\geq 2
. In this case
\partial B\cap P_{f}=\emptyset
, so the claim applies. Since
J
is locally connected, then it is a Sierpiński carpet.

(2) It follows from Lemma
2.6
and the definition of cluster Julia sets in Theorem
1.8
.

(3) By symmetry
-v\in\partial T
. Lemma
2.6
implies
\partial B\cap\partial T=\emptyset
. Let
A=\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{B\cup T}
. The Riemann-Hurwitz formula implies that every component of
f^{-1}(A)
is an annulus.
Since

\partial B\cup\partial T\cup f^{-1}(\partial T)=\partial f^{-1}(A),

f^{-1}(A)
consists of two sub-annuli
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
, each sharing a boundary component with
A
. Furthermore,
\partial A_{1}\cap\partial A_{2}
is precisely the set of
2n
free critical points. Hence
f^{-1}(T)=A\setminus\overline{A_{1}\cup A_{2}}
and it consists of
2n
Fatou domains, all of which are Jordan domains. Since
\pm v\notin\overline{f^{-1}(T)}
, Fatou domains iterated into
f^{-1}(T)
are Jordan domains.

A comparison of the conformal moduli of
A
,
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
together with Grötzsch’s inequality gives

\textup{mod}(A)>\textup{mod}(A_{1})+\textup{mod}(A_{2})=\frac{2}{n}\textup{mod}(A),

which forces

. Therefore, this configuration cannot occur when
n=2
.

(4) By symmetry
f^{k}(-v)=(-1)^{n}f^{k}(v)\in\partial T
. Since Case (4) is distinct from Case (2), we have
v\notin\partial B
; Lemma
2.6
then yields
\partial B\cap\partial T=\emptyset
. Consequently
\partial B\cap P_{f}\neq\emptyset
(because
f^{k+1}(v)\in\partial B
), so the initial claim does
not
apply directly. We argue separately.

Step 1: Jordan domain property.

Since
f^{k}(v)\in\partial T
, the points
\{v,f(v),\ldots,f^{k-1}(v)\}
lie in the Julia set, hence are disjoint from every Fatou domain

(
i\leq k
). Therefore for each

, the map

is a covering; each component of
f^{-i}(T)
maps homeomorphically onto a component of
f^{-(i-1)}(T)
and is thus a Jordan domain. Closures at the same level are disjoint because they are bounded by distinct components of
f^{-i}(\partial T)
, which are disjoint Jordan curves.

Step 2: Disjointness across levels.

Let
U\subseteq f^{-i}(T)
and
V\subseteq f^{-j}(T)
with
i<j\leq k
. If
\overline{U}\cap\overline{V}\neq\emptyset
, then
f^{i}(\overline{U}\cap\overline{V})\subseteq\partial T\cap f^{-(j-i)}(\partial T)
. Since the critical orbit
\{v,f(v),\ldots,f^{k-1}(v)\}
avoids
\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{k}f^{-(\ell-1)}(T)
,
\partial T
is disjoint from
f^{-(j-i)}(\partial T)
, a contradiction. Hence closures at different levels are also disjoint.

Step 3: Location of critical values.

Since
\pm v\in f^{-k}(\partial T)
, each lies on the boundary of some component of

; denote them by
U_{+}
and
U_{-}
. If
U_{+}=U_{-}
, then both
\pm v\in\partial U_{+}
, so
\overline{U_{+}}\cap-\overline{U_{+}}\neq\emptyset
, contradicting Lemma
2.3
. Hence
U_{+}\neq U_{-}
.

Step 4: Preimage structure.

Consider
f^{-1}(\overline{U_{+}})
. The interior
f^{-1}(U_{+})
consists of
2n
components, each a Jordan domain. The
n
simple critical points mapping to
v
lie on the common boundary of these components; each such critical point is adjacent to exactly two of them. Consequently
f^{-1}(\overline{U_{+}})
splits into
n
connected components. Each component is the closure of two Jordan domains meeting at a single critical point. The same description holds for
f^{-1}(\overline{U_{-}})
.

(5) This follows directly from the claim, since
v
buried in
J
implies
\partial B\cap P_{f}=\emptyset
.
∎

3.
Fatou chains

This section studies the maximal Fatou chain of a PCF McMullen map with bounded attracting cycles, establishing the topological properties needed for the blow-up theorems in Section
4
.

In this section we suppose
f
is such a map with exponent
n\geq 2
; thus the free critical value
v
or
-v
(or both) lies in a periodic attracting cycle. Denote by
\mathcal{U}
the union of immediate basins of all bounded attracting cycles. There are two cases:

Case 1.

n
is even. Then
\mathcal{U}
contains exactly one critical value. Indeed, the critical values
\pm v
have the same image, so at most one domain in
\mathcal{U}
can contain a critical value.

Case 2.

n
is odd. Then the symmetry (
2.1
) implies that
\mathcal{U}
consists of one or two Fatou cycles and contains both critical values
\pm v
. Moreover, we have
\mathcal{U}=-\mathcal{U}
.

3.1.
Maximal Fatou chains

We now give the definition of maximal Fatou chains. Set
\mathcal{E}^{1}=\overline{\mathcal{U}}
. For
k\geq 1
, define
\mathcal{E}_{k}^{1}
inductively as the union of all components of
f^{-k}(\mathcal{E}^{1})
that intersect
\mathcal{E}^{1}
. As
f(\mathcal{E}^{1})\subset\mathcal{E}^{1}
, we have

\mathcal{E}^{1}\subseteq\mathcal{E}^{1}_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{k-1}^{1}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{k}^{1}\subseteq\cdots

and each set in this chain has the same number of components.

If
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{1}=\mathcal{E}^{1}
, then
\mathcal{E}_{k}^{1}=\mathcal{E}^{1}
for all
k
, and
\mathcal{E}^{1}
is
stable
, i.e.,
f(\mathcal{E}^{1})=\mathcal{E}^{1}
and every component of
\mathcal{E}^{1}
is a component of
f^{-1}(\mathcal{E}^{1})
.

If
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{1}\neq\mathcal{E}^{1}
, we iterate the construction: let
\mathcal{E}^{2}=\overline{\bigcup_{k}\mathcal{E}_{k}^{1}}
and for
k\geq 1
define
\mathcal{E}_{k}^{2}
as the union of all components of
f^{-k}(\mathcal{E}^{2})
that intersect
\mathcal{E}^{2}
. If
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{2}=\mathcal{E}^{2}
, then
\mathcal{E}^{2}
is stable. If not, we repeat the procedure with
\mathcal{E}^{2}
playing the role of
\mathcal{E}_{1}
, thereby generating a sequence of invariant sets
\mathcal{E}^{1},\mathcal{E}^{2},\ldots,\mathcal{E}^{m},\ldots
. Since the number of components of
\mathcal{E}^{m}
is non-increasing, there exists an index
m_{0}
such that

\#\textup{Comp}(\mathcal{E}^{m})=\#\textup{Comp}(\mathcal{E}^{m_{0}})\textup{
for all }m\geq m_{0},

where
\textup{Comp}(\cdot)
denotes the collection of all components of a set. If a component
E
of
\mathcal{E}^{m}
is strictly contained in a component
E^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{m}
for some
m\geq m_{0}
, then for any point
z\neq v_{\pm}
in
f(E)\cap\mathcal{E}^{1}
we have

\#(f^{-1}(z)\cap E)<\#(f^{-1}(z)\cap E^{\prime})\leq 2n.

Consequently, there is a large integer
N
for which
\mathcal{E}^{N}=\mathcal{E}_{1}^{N}
. It is clear that
\mathcal{E}^{N}
is stable. We call
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}^{N}
the
maximal Fatou chain
of
f
( generated by
\mathcal{U}
). For every integer
m\geq 1
, the continuum
\mathcal{E}^{m}=\overline{\bigcup_{k}\mathcal{E}_{k}^{m-1}}
is called the
level-
m
Fatou chain
.

The following lemma characterize the topological and combinatorial properties of the maximal Fatou chain.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose a PCF McMullen map
f
has bounded attracting cycles and
\mathcal{U}
is the union of immediate basins of all bounded attracting cycles. Let
\mathcal{E}
be the maximal Fatou chain of
f
generated by
\mathcal{U}
. Then

(1)

each component of
\mathcal{E}
is a full continuum containing at most one critical value;

(2)

every two points in a component
E
of
\mathcal{E}
can be joined by a curve in
E
passing through at most countably many points in the Julia set
J
;

(3)

if
\mathcal{E}\cap\partial B\neq\emptyset
, then every component of
\mathcal{E}
intersects
\partial B
at a single point.

The proof of Lemma
3.1
relies on the following rotational disjointness property of Fatou chains. Recall that
n\geq 2
is the exponent of
f
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
1\leq m\leq N
, the following statements hold.

(1)

If
n
is even, then
\omega\mathcal{E}^{m}\cap\mathcal{E}^{m}=\emptyset
for any
\omega
with
\omega^{2n}=1
and
\omega\neq 1
.

(2)

If
n
is odd, then
\omega\mathcal{E}^{m}\cap\mathcal{E}^{m}=\emptyset
for any
\omega
with
\omega^{2n}=1
and
\omega\neq\pm 1
.

(3)

If
n
is odd, then
\mathcal{E}^{m}=-\mathcal{E}^{m}
. Moreover, if
E
is a component of
\mathcal{E}^{m}
, then
-E
is a component of
\mathcal{E}^{m}
disjoint from
E
.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
under Lemma
3.2
.

(1) To prove that every component of
\mathcal{E}
is a full continuum, it suffices to show that
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\mathcal{E}
is connected.

Note first that
0
and
\infty
lie in the same component
\Omega_{0}
of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\mathcal{E}
; for otherwise, some component
E
of
\mathcal{E}
would separate
0
and
\infty
. Then
e^{\pi\textbf{i}/n}E
would also separate
0
and
\infty
. By Lemma
2.1
(2), we would have
e^{\pi\textbf{i}/n}E\cap E\neq\emptyset
, contradicting Lemma
3.2
(1) and (2) since
n\geq 2
.

Assume that some components of
\mathcal{E}
are not full. Then
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\mathcal{E}
has a component
\Omega\neq\Omega_{0}
. For any
k\geq 1
, the set
f^{k}(\Omega)
is open and connected with
\partial f^{k}(\Omega)\subseteq f^{k}(\partial\Omega)\subseteq\mathcal{E}
, so
\Omega_{0}
is either contained in
f^{k}(\Omega)
or disjoint from it. Hence
\Omega
cannot be a Fatou domain eventually mapped to
B
; and since
\mathcal{E}
is stable and contains
\mathcal{U}
, it cannot be a Fatou domain eventually mapped to
\mathcal{U}
either. Therefore,
\Omega\cap J\neq\emptyset
.

Let
k_{0}\geq 1
be the smallest integer such that
0\in f^{k_{0}}(\Omega)
. Since
\Omega_{0}
contains
0,\infty
, we have
\infty\in f^{k_{0}}(\Omega)
and
\infty\notin f^{k_{0}-1}(\Omega)
. Hence
f^{k_{0}-1}(\Omega)
contains the pole
0
, a contradiction. Therefore
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\mathcal{E}
is connected, and consequently every component of
\mathcal{E}
is a full continuum.

If a component
E
of
\mathcal{E}
contains both
\pm v
, then
E\cap-E\neq\emptyset
, contradicting Lemma
3.2
(1)(3). This proves Statement (1).

(2) By
[
CGZ
, Theorem 1.5]
, for any component
E
of
\mathcal{E}
, there exist a quasiconformal map
\psi
on
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
and a PCF polynomial
P
such that
\psi(E)
is the filled Julia set of
P
and
P\circ\psi=\psi\circ f^{p}
, where
p
is the period of
E
. Hence,
E
is locally connected.

For any two points in
E
, let
\gamma\subset E
be an arc joining these two points such that its intersection with any Fatou domain consists of two internal rays. By
[
GZ
, Proposition 2.3]
,
\psi(\gamma)\cap J_{P}
is a countable set. Hence every two points in
E
can be joined by a curve in
E
passing through at most countably many points in
J
.

(3) Now suppose
\mathcal{E}\cap\partial B\neq\emptyset
. Since
\mathcal{E}\cap\partial B
is
f
-invariant and
\partial B
is a Jordan curve, each component of
\mathcal{E}\cap\partial B
is a single point: otherwise, by the expanding property of
J
, we would have
\partial B\subseteq\mathcal{E}
, a contradiction to the fact that each component of
\mathcal{E}
is a full continuum.

To show that every component of
\mathcal{E}
intersects
\partial B
at a single point, by Lemma
3.2
and Statement (1), it suffices to prove the connectivity of the open set

W=\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\cup\overline{B}),

where
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}:=\bigcup_{\omega^{2n}=1}\omega\mathcal{E}.

Denote by
\Omega_{0}
the component of
W
containing
T
. If
W
were disconnected, as in the proof of Statement (1), there would exist another component
\Omega\neq\Omega_{0}
of
W
such that
f(\Omega)
covers
T
. Since
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\cup\overline{B}
is invariant under the rotation of
2n
-th root of unity, it follows from Lemma
2.1
(3) that
\{\omega\Omega,\omega^{2n}=1\}
are
2n
pairwise disjoint components of
W
. The symmetry (
2.1
) implies
\Omega_{0}\subseteq f(\omega\Omega)
.

On the other hand, the boundary of
f(\Omega_{0})
is contained in
\partial B\cup\mathcal{E}\cup-\mathcal{E}
. By Lemma
3.2
(1) and (2),
\partial W\cap\partial B
contains some arcs. Since
f
preserves orientation on these arcs, we obtain
f(\Omega_{0})\cap\Omega_{0}\not=\emptyset
. Consequently
T\subseteq\Omega_{0}\subseteq f(\Omega_{0})
. This implies that
T
has at least
2n+1
pre-images under
f
. This leads to a contradiction. Hence,
W
is connected, which implies every component of
\mathcal{E}
intersects
\partial B
at a single point.
∎

3.2.
Disjointness under rotations

This subsection is devoted to proving Lemma
3.2
. Recall that for each
k\geq 1
,
\mathcal{E}_{k}^{m}
is the union of all components of
f^{-k}(\mathcal{E}^{m})
intersecting
\mathcal{E}^{m}
. The following result is a corollary of
[
CGZ
, Lemmas 6.1 and 6.3]
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Fix an integer
m\geq 1
. Let
E_{k}
be a sequence of sets such that each
E_{k}
is a component of
\mathcal{E}_{k}^{m}
and
E_{k}\subset E_{k+1}
. Let
E_{\infty}=\bigcup_{k\geq 1}E_{k}
. Then any two points in
\overline{E_{\infty}}
can be joined by an open arc within
E_{\infty}
.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

The proof goes by induction on the level
m
. We first check that the statements in this lemma hold for
\mathcal{E}^{1}=\overline{\mathcal{U}}
.

(1) Assume
n
is even. Suppose for contradiciton that the statement is false. Then there exist a
2n
-th root of unity
\omega_{0}\neq 1
, a component
U
of
\mathcal{U}
, and a point
z\in\overline{U}
such that
\omega_{0}z\in\overline{U^{\prime}}
for some component
U^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{U}
. Lemma
2.3
gives
U\neq U^{\prime}
. By symmetry
f(\omega_{0}z)=\omega_{0}^{n}f(z)
and the fact that
n
is even, we have
f^{2}(\omega_{0}z)=f^{2}(z)
.
This implies
z\notin U
: for otherwise
f^{2}(U)=f^{2}(U^{\prime})
, contradicting the periodicity of
U
and
U^{\prime}
. We then obtain that

(3.1)

e^{k\pi\textbf{i}/n}\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{U}=\emptyset\text{ for every }k=1,\ldots,2n-1.

Since
z\in\partial U
and
\omega_{0}z\in\partial U^{\prime}
, and
f^{2}(\omega_{0}z)=f^{2}(z)
, the boundaries of
f^{2}(U)
and
f^{2}(U^{\prime})
share the point
f^{2}(z)
. As
U
and
U^{\prime}
have the same period,
\partial U\cap\partial U^{\prime}
contains at least one point, say
x
. If
\omega_{0}
is non-primitive, then by equation (
3.1
), we can apply Lemma
2.1
(4) with
E=\overline{U\cup U^{\prime}}
,
A=U\cup\{x\}\cup U^{\prime},\omega=e^{\pi\textbf{i}/n}
and
\omega_{0}
to obtain that
\omega_{0}E\cap E=\emptyset
. This contradicts
\omega_{0}z\in E\cap\omega_{0}E
.

If
\omega_{0}
is primitive, then
\omega_{0}^{n}=-1
, so
f(z)\in\partial f(U)
and
-f(z)\in-\partial f(U^{\prime})
. Since
U
and
U^{\prime}
are periodic,
f(U)\neq f(U^{\prime})
. Replacing
(U,U^{\prime},z,\omega_{0})
by
(f(U),f(U^{\prime}),f(z),-1)
in the previous argument then gives a contradiction. This completes the proof of (1).

(2) Assume
n
is odd. Suppose for contradiction that the statement fails. Then there exist a
2n
-th root of unity
\omega_{0}\neq\pm 1
, a component
U
of
\mathcal{U}
, and a point
z\in\overline{U}
such that
\omega_{0}z\in\overline{U^{\prime}}
for some component
U^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{U}
. Lemma
2.3
implies
U\neq U^{\prime}
and similarly
U\neq-U^{\prime}
as
-\omega_{0}z\in-\overline{U^{\prime}}
. Thus,
\pm U
and
\pm U^{\prime}
are four distinct Fatou domains.

Since
\mathcal{U}=-\mathcal{U}
, we may replace
(U,U^{\prime},\omega_{0})
by
(-U,-U^{\prime},-\omega_{0})
if necessary and assume
\omega_{0}^{n}=1
.
By symmetry
f(\omega_{0}z)=\omega_{0}^{n}f(z)
, we have
f(z)=f(\omega_{0}z)
.

We claim
z\notin U
. For otherwise,
z\in U
and
\omega_{0}z\in U^{\prime}
and thus
f(U)=f(U^{\prime})
, contradicting the periodicity of
U
and
U^{\prime}
. The claim implies

(3.2)

e^{k\pi\textbf{i}/n}\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{U}=\emptyset\text{ for every }1\leq k\leq 2n-1\text{ and }k\not=n.

By the claim,
z\in\partial U
and
\omega_{0}z\in\partial U^{\prime}
. As in the proof of Statement (1),
\partial U\cap\partial U^{\prime}
contains a point
x
.
By equation (
3.2
) and the fact that
\pm U
and
\pm U^{\prime}
are pairwise disjoint when
k=n
, we can apply Lemma
2.1
(4) with
E=\overline{U\cup U^{\prime}}
,
A=U\cup\{x\}\cup U^{\prime},\omega=e^{\pi\textbf{i}/n}
and the non-primitive
\omega_{0}
to obtain that
\omega_{0}E\cap E=\emptyset
. This contradicts
\omega_{0}z\in E\cap\omega_{0}E
,
completing the proof of Statement (2).

(3) When
n
is odd, we have
\mathcal{U}=-\mathcal{U}
, and the same holds for its closure. If a component
E
of
\mathcal{E}^{1}=\overline{\mathcal{U}}
satisfies
E\cap-E\neq\emptyset
, then
E=-E
. By Lemma
2.1
(1),
E
separates
0
and
\infty
. Lemma
2.1
(2) then gives
E\cap\omega E\neq\emptyset
for every
2n
-th root of unity
\omega
, contradicting Statement (2). This proves Statement (3).

We now proceed by induction on the level
m
of Fatou chains. Assuming the statements of this lemma hold for
\mathcal{E}^{m}
, we shall prove they also hold for
\mathcal{E}^{m+1}
. Since
\mathcal{E}^{m+1}=\overline{\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\mathcal{E}^{m}_{k}}
, we first establish the following claim.

Claim.

For any
k\geq 1
, the statements in the lemma still hold for
\mathcal{E}^{m}_{k}
.

Proof of the claim.

We prove Statements (1)-(3) only for
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{m}
; the cases
k\geq 2
follow by a similar induction and are omitted.

(1) Assume
n
is even. If
\omega^{n}=-1
, then
f(\omega\mathcal{E}_{1}^{m})=-f(\mathcal{E}_{1}^{m})=-\mathcal{E}^{m}
. By induction, we have
\mathcal{E}^{m}\cap-\mathcal{E}^{m}=\emptyset
. Since
f(\mathcal{E}_{1}^{m})=\mathcal{E}^{m}
, it follows that
\omega\mathcal{E}_{1}^{m}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1}^{m}=\emptyset
.

If
\omega^{n}=1
and
\omega\neq 1
, suppose for contradiction that
\omega E\cap E^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
for two components
E
and
E^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{m}
. Since
f(\omega E)=f(E)\subseteq\mathcal{E}^{m}
, we have
\omega E\subseteq E^{\prime}
and
f(E)\subseteq f(E^{\prime})
. Hence
f(E)\cap f(E^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
, and since distinct components of
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{m}
map to distinct components of
\mathcal{E}^{m}
, we conclude that
E^{\prime}=E
. Therefore
\omega E\subseteq E
, which gives
E=\omega E
.
Hence
E=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\omega^{i}E
.
By Lemma
2.1
(1),
E
separates
0
and
\infty
. Choose an
n
-th root
\omega_{0}
of
-1
. Lemma
2.1
(2) then yields
E\cap\omega_{0}E\neq\emptyset
. However, the first subcase already showed
E\cap\omega_{0}E=\emptyset
. This contradiction proves Statement (1).

(3) Assume
n
is odd. Let
E
be a component of
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{m}
containing a component
E_{0}
of
\mathcal{E}^{m}
, and let
K=f(E)
. Then
K
is a component of
\mathcal{E}^{m}
. By the induction hypothesis,
-E_{0}\neq E_{0}
and
-K\neq K
, and both
-E_{0}
and
-K
are components of
\mathcal{E}^{m}
. Since
n
is odd,
f(-z)=(-1)^{n}f(z)=-f(z)
, which yields
f(-E)=-K
. Hence,
-E
is a component of
f^{-1}(-K)
disjoint from
E
(as
K\neq-K
). Because
-E_{0}\subset-E\cap\mathcal{E}^{m}
, the component
-E
intersects
\mathcal{E}^{m}
, and therefore is a component of
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{m}
. This proves Statement (3).

(2) Assume
n
is odd. Suppose for contradiction that
\omega E\cap E^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
for some components
E,E^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{m}
and some
2n
-th root of unity
\omega\neq\pm 1
. By Statement (3), we may replace
(\omega,E,E^{\prime})
with
(-\omega,-E,-E^{\prime})
if necessary and assume
\omega^{n}=1
. Then
f(\omega E)=f(E)\subseteq\mathcal{E}^{m}
. The same argument as in Statement (1) gives

E=\omega E=\bigcup_{i=1}^{2n}\omega^{i}E.

By Lemma
2.1
(1), we conclude that
E
separates
0
and
\infty
. Lemma
2.1
(2) then yields
E\cap-E\neq\emptyset
, contradicting Statement (3). This proves the claim.
∎

It remains to verify that the statements in the lemma hold for
\mathcal{E}^{m+1}
. The argument is analogous to the proof for
\mathcal{E}^{1}
given earlier. Set
\mathcal{E}^{m}_{\infty}=\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\mathcal{E}_{k}^{m}
. Then

f(\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{m})=\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{m}\,\textup{ and }\,\mathcal{E}^{m+1}=\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{m}}.

(1) Assume
n
is even. By the previous claim we have

(3.3)

\mathcal{E}^{m}_{\infty}\cap e^{k\pi\textbf{i}/n}\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{m}=\emptyset
for any
k=1,\ldots,2n-1
.

Suppose for contradiction that Statement (1) fails for
\mathcal{E}^{m+1}
. Then there exist
z,\omega_{0}z\in\mathcal{E}^{m+1}
for some
2n
-th root of unity
\omega_{0}\neq 1
. Hence there are sequences
\{E_{k}\}
and
\{E^{\prime}_{k}\}
of components of
\mathcal{E}_{k}^{m}
, with
E_{k}\subset E_{k+1}
and
E^{\prime}_{k}\subset E^{\prime}_{k+1}
, such that

and
\omega_{0}z
are contained in the closures of

and

, respectively.

By symmetry
f(\omega_{0}z)=\omega_{0}^{n}f(z)
and the fact that
n
is even, we have
f^{2}(\omega_{0}z)=f^{2}(z)
.
Then
f^{2}(\overline{X})
and
f^{2}(\overline{Y})
share the point
f^{2}(z)
. As
X
and
Y
are periodic with the same period,
\overline{X}\cap\overline{Y}
contains at least one point, say
x
.

Suppose first that
\omega_{0}
is non-primitive. Then by relation (
3.3
) and Proposition
3.3
, one of the following two cases occurs:

\bullet
If
X=Y
, we can apply Lemma
2.1
(4) with
E=\overline{X},A=X,\omega=e^{\pi\textbf{i}/n}
and
\omega_{0}
to obtain that
\omega_{0}E\cap E=\emptyset
. This contradicts
\omega_{0}z\in E\cap\omega_{0}E
.

\bullet
If

, we can apply Lemma
2.1
(4) with

and
\omega_{0}
to obtain that
\omega_{0}E\cap E=\emptyset
. This contradicts
\omega_{0}z\in E\cap\omega_{0}E
.

If
\omega_{0}
is primitive, then
\omega_{0}^{n}=-1
, so that
f(z)\in f(\overline{X})
and
f(\omega_{0}z)=-f(z)\in f(\overline{Y})
. Replacing
(X,Y,z,\omega_{0})
by
(f(X),f(Y),f(z),-1)
in the previous argument then gives a contradiction. This completes the proof of Statement (1).

(2) Assume
n
is odd. Suppose for contradiction that the statement fails. Then there exist a
2n
-th root of unity
\omega_{0}\neq\pm 1
, and a point
z\in\overline{X}
such that
\omega_{0}z\in\overline{Y}
. Here
X
and

are constructed similarly in (1). The previous claim implies

(3.4)

e^{k\pi\textbf{i}/n}\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{m}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{m}=\emptyset\text{ for }k\in\{1,\ldots,2n-1\}\setminus\{n\}\text{ and }\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{m}=-\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{m}.

Then by relation (
3.4
) and Proposition
3.3
, one of the following two cases occurs:

Case 1.
X=Y
. Since

by the claim, combining with Proposition
3.3
, we apply Lemma
2.1
(4) with
E=\overline{X},A=X,\omega=e^{\pi\textbf{i}/n}
and
\omega_{0}
to obtain that
\omega_{0}E\cap E=\emptyset
. This contradicts
\omega_{0}z\in E\cap\omega_{0}E
.

Case 2.
X\not=Y
. Clearly
X\neq-Y
, for otherwise, by replacing
\omega_{0}
with
-\omega_{0}
, the above case occurs.
We may replace
(X,Y,\omega_{0})
by
(-X,-Y,-\omega_{0})
if necessary and assume further that
\omega_{0}^{n}=1
. Then by symmetry
f(\omega_{0}z)=\omega_{0}^{n}f(z)=f(z)
. As in (1),
\overline{X}\cap\overline{Y}
contains a point
x
.
Note that the four sets
\pm X
and

are pairwise disjoint. We apply Proposition
3.3
and Lemma
2.1
(4) with
E=\overline{X\cup Y},A=X\cup\{x\}\cup Y,\omega=e^{\pi\textbf{i}/n}
and
\omega_{0}
to obtain that
\omega_{0}E\cap E=\emptyset
. This contradicts
\omega_{0}z\in E\cap\omega_{0}E
.
This completes the proof of Statement (2).

(3) Assume
n
is odd. By relation (
3.4
),
\mathcal{E}^{m+1}=-\mathcal{E}^{m+1}
. Moreover, if
E
is a component of
\mathcal{E}^{m+1}
, so is

. If

, then

separates

and

. Choose a
2n
-th root of unity
\omega_{0}\neq\pm 1
. Lemma
2.1
(2) gives

, contradicting Statement (2). This completes the proof of Statement (3) and hence of Lemma

.
∎

4.
Blow-up Theorems

In this section, we first recall the blow-up theorem for exact subsystems established in
[
CGZ
, Theorem 1.6]
. We then prove a new blow-up theorem for McMullen maps whose two free critical values lie on
\partial T
, blowing up the corresponding Julia sets to Cantor-circles. We also complete the proof of Theorem
1.8
here.

4.1.
Blow up of exact subsystems

Let
f
be a PCF rational map. Recall that
P_{f}
denotes its postcritical set

\{f^{k}(c):c\textup{ is a critical point of $f$ and $k\geq 1$}\}
.

Suppose
\mathcal{V}_{1}\subset\mathcal{V}
are open sets such that
\partial\mathcal{V}
is contained in the Julia set of
f
and each component of
\partial\mathcal{V}
contains more than one point. We say that
f:\mathcal{V}_{1}\to\mathcal{V}
is an
exact subsystem
if

(1)

\mathcal{V}
has finitely many components, each of which is finitely connected;

(2)

\mathcal{V}_{1}
is the union of some components of
f^{-1}(\mathcal{V})
;

(3)

each component of
\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{1}
is a full continuum disjoint from
P_{f}
.

Theorem 4.1

(
[
CGZ
, Theorem 1.6]
)
.

Let
f
be a PCF rational map and let
f:\mathcal{V}_{1}\to\mathcal{V}
be an exact subsystem such that
\mathcal{V}
is connected. Denote

\mathcal{V}_{n}=(f|_{\mathcal{V}_{1}})^{-n}(\mathcal{V})\quad\text{ and }\quad E=\bigcap_{n\geq 1}\overline{\mathcal{V}_{n}}.

Then there exists a PCF rational map
g
, a continuum
K_{g}\supset J_{g}
with
g^{-1}(K_{g})=K_{g}
, and a quotient map
\pi:\overline{\mathbb{C}}\to\overline{\mathbb{C}}
such that

(1)

components of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus K_{g}
are all Jordan domains with pairwise disjoint closures;

(2)

E=\pi(K_{g})
and
f\circ\pi=\pi\circ g
on
K_{g}
;

(3)

for any point in
\bigcap_{k>0}\mathcal{V}_{k}
, the fiber
\pi^{-1}(z)
is a single point;

(4)

for any component
B_{n}
of

. Its preimage

is a full connected compact set contained in a component of
J_{\tilde{f}}
(a circle). If
h^{-1}(z)
were not a single point, it would be an arc and would eventually map onto a whole circle in
J_{\tilde{f}}
. Its image under
h
would then be a single point in
J
, impossible. Therefore
h^{-1}(z)
is a singleton.

Finally,
g
and
\tilde{f}
are quasiconformally conjugate in an annular neighborhood of
h^{-1}(\partial B)
.
The dynamics
\tilde{f}:\tilde{f}^{-1}(A)\to A
(defined in Claim 1) and
g
are combinatorially equivalent in the sense of
[
McM
, Appendix A]
. Hence, by
[
McM
, Theorem A.1]
, there exists a quasisymmetric conjugacy
\psi:J_{g}\to J_{\tilde{f}}
.
Then
\pi=h\circ\psi:J_{g}\to J
is the required semi-conjugacy, completing the proof of the theorem.
∎

4.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.8

The blow-up theorems above reduce complicated Julia sets to two simple models. Together with the properties of Fatou chains from Section
3
, this gives the full classification of Theorem
1.8
, which we now prove.

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

Let
f
be a PCF McMullen map. Recall that
B
and
T
are the Fatou domains containing
\infty
and
0
respectively, and both are Jordan domains by Lemma
2.5
. We distinguish two main cases.

Case 1.

f
has no bounded attracting cycles.
Then Proposition
2.7
classifies the dynamics into five subcases, which translate directly into the four topological classes:

(i)

f^{k}(v)=\infty
for some
k\geq 2
, or
v
is buried in
J
. Then
J
is a Sierpiński carpet (Proposition
2.7
(1), (5)).

(ii)

v\in\partial B
. Then
\partial B\cap\partial T=\textup{Crit}
by Lemma
2.6
, so
J
is a cluster (Proposition
2.7
(2)).

(iii)

v\in\partial T
. In this case
n\geq 3
and Theorem
4.3
blows
J
up to a Cantor-circle; hence
J
is a necklace (Proposition
2.7
(3)).

(iv)

f^{k}(v)\in\partial T
for some
k\geq 1
. Corollary
4.2
blows
J
to a Sierpiński carpet, so
J
is a Sierpiński-like carpet.

Case 2.

f
has bounded attracting cycles.
Let
\mathcal{U}
be the union of the immediate basins of all bounded attracting cycles, and let
\mathcal{E}
be the maximal Fatou chain generated by
\mathcal{U}
(see Section
3.1
). Lemma
3.1
provides the following key facts: each component of
\mathcal{E}
is a full continuum; any two points in a component can be joined by a curve meeting
J
in at most countably many points; and if
\mathcal{E}\cap\partial B\neq\emptyset
, then each component of
\mathcal{E}
meets
\partial B
at a single point.

(i)

\mathcal{E}\cap\partial B=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{E}=\overline{\mathcal{U}}
. Then
\mathcal{E}
has finitely many components, each a Jordan domain, and the closures of any two are disjoint. Every other Fatou domain eventually maps to
\mathcal{E}
or to
B
, and
P_{f}\cap J=\emptyset
. Hence, every Fatou domain is a Jordan domain with pairwise disjoint closures, and
J
is a Sierpiński carpet.

(ii)

\mathcal{E}\cap\partial B=\emptyset
but
\mathcal{E}\neq\overline{\mathcal{U}}
. Then by Corollary
4.2
,
J
can be blown up to a Sierpiński carpet, hence is a Sierpiński-like carpet.

(iii)

\mathcal{E}\cap\partial B\neq\emptyset
. By Lemma
3.1
(3), each component of
\mathcal{E}
meets
\partial B
at a single point. Since
f^{-1}(B\cup\mathcal{E})=B\cup T\cup f^{-1}(\mathcal{E})
, any component of
f^{-1}(\mathcal{E})
containing a critical point meets both
\partial T
and
\partial B
, as
f^{-1}(B)=B\cup T
and the critical point is a branch point between these two preimages. Lemma
3.1
(2) then yields a curve inside that component joining a point of
\partial B
to a point of
\partial T
and passing through at most countably many Julia points. Thus,
J
is a cluster.

In every subcase,
J
falls into exactly one of the four topological classes, and the necklace case occurs only for
n\geq 3
. This completes the proof.
∎

5.
Dynamical relations for Sierpiński-like carpets

In this section we prove Theorem
5.1
, which gives the dynamical relation forced by quasisymmetries between Sierpiński-like carpet Julia sets. The statement covers general PCF rational maps with Sierpiński models; for McMullen maps, Corollary
4.2
reduces it to the corresponding dynamical relation.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
J
and
\tilde{J}
be the Julia sets of PCF rational maps
f
and
\tilde{f}
, respectively. Suppose there exist Sierpiński rational maps
g
and
\tilde{g}
, and quotient maps
\pi
and
\tilde{\pi}
over
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
such that

(1)

\pi\circ g=f\circ\pi
on
J_{g}
and
\tilde{\pi}\circ\tilde{g}=\tilde{f}\circ\tilde{\pi}
on
J_{\tilde{g}}
;

(2)

\pi
and
\tilde{\pi}
are injective on the buried point sets;

(3)

for any Fatou domain
U
of
g
, any two points in
\pi(\overline{U})
can be joined by a curve that meets
J
in at most countably many points; the analogous statement holds for
\tilde{g}
and
\tilde{\pi}
.

Suppose
\xi:J\to\tilde{J}
is a quasisymmetry. Then there exist integers
m^{\prime},l\geq 1
and
m\geq 0
such that

\tilde{f}^{m^{\prime}}\circ\xi=\tilde{f}^{m}\circ\xi\circ f^{l}\quad\text{on }J.

Remark.
If
\deg f=\deg\tilde{f}
, then since
\xi
is a homeomorphism, comparing degrees on both sides of the relation gives
m^{\prime}=m+l
.

To apply the lifting argument introduced in Section
1.2
, we need a distortion lemma in both directions, controlling diameters under pushforward and pullback. This generalizes
[
BLM
, Lemma 4.1]
.

For a constant
C\geq 1
, we write
a\asymp b
and say
a
and
b
are
comparable
for the constant
C
whenever
b/C\leq a\leq Cb
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
f
be a subhyperbolic rational map. Fix a constant
C\geq 1
. Then for every sufficiently small
\epsilon_{0}>0
, there exists a constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(C)\geq 1
such that the following holds:

Let
\alpha\subseteq\beta
be small connected sets containing more than one point and intersecting the Julia set of
f
. Let
\alpha_{k}=f^{k}(\alpha)
and
\beta_{k}=f^{k}(\beta)
, where
k
is the largest integer such that
\textup{diam}(\beta_{k})<\epsilon_{0}
.

(1)

(pushforward) If
\textup{diam}(\alpha)\asymp\textup{diam}(\beta)
for the constant
C
, then

\textup{diam}(\alpha_{k})\asymp\textup{diam}(\beta_{k})\textup{ and }\textup{diam}(\beta_{k})\asymp\epsilon_{0}

for the constant
C_{1}
.

(2)

(pullback) If
\textup{diam}(\alpha_{k})\asymp\textup{diam}(\beta_{k})
for the constant
C
, then
\textup{diam}(\alpha)\asymp\textup{diam}(\beta)
for the constant
C_{1}
.

Proof.

Since
f
is subhyperbolic,
P_{f}\cap J
is a finite set. So there exist
\epsilon_{0}>0
and an integer
N\geq 1
such that for any point
p
in the Julia set
J
and any
m\geq 1
, each component of
f^{-m}(B(p,2\epsilon_{0}))
is a disk on which the degree of
f^{m}
is at most
N
.

Let
k
be as in the statement. Let
M
denote the supremum of the spherical derivative of
f
. Then
\textup{diam}(\beta_{k})\geq\epsilon_{0}/M
. Fix
p\in\alpha\cap J
and set
B=B(f^{k}(p),2\epsilon_{0})
. Let
B_{k}
be the component of
f^{-k}(B)
containing
p
.
Choose conformal maps
\psi:B_{k}\to\mathbb{D}
and
\phi:B\to\mathbb{D}
with
\psi(p)=0
and
\phi(f^{k}(p))=0
. Then

h:=\phi\circ f^{k}\circ\psi^{-1}:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}

is a proper map fixing
0
with
\deg(h)\leq N
.

Claim.

Fix
r\in(0,1)
and let
U_{r}
be the component of
h^{-1}(\mathbb{D}(0,r))
containing
0
. Then every
x\in\partial U_{r}
satisfies

r\leq|x|\leq 4r^{1/N}.

Proof of the claim.

The lower bound
r\leq|x|
follows from the Schwarz Lemma.

For the upper bound, pick
x_{0}\in\partial U_{r}
with
|x_{0}|=\max_{x\in\partial U_{r}}|x|
. The restriction

h:\mathbb{D}\setminus\overline{U_{r}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{D}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}(0,r)}

is a proper map of degree at most
N
, so the monotonicity of conformal modulus gives

\frac{1}{2\pi N}\textup{ln}\frac{1}{r}\leq\textup{mod}(\mathbb{D}\setminus\overline{U_{r}})\leq\mu(|x_{0}|),

where
\mu(t)=\textup{mod}(\mathbb{D}\setminus[0,t])
for
0<t<1
.

The standard estimate
\mu(t)<\frac{1}{2\pi}\textup{ln}\frac{4}{t}
holds; see
[
Ah
]
. Combining this with the previous inequality yields
|x_{0}|\leq 4r^{1/N}
, and hence
|x|\leq 4r^{1/N}
for every
x\in\partial U_{r}
.
∎

Since
\textup{diam}(\beta_{k})\geq\epsilon_{0}/M
, it follows from the Koebe distortion theorem that there exists a positive number
\delta_{0}
such that
\textup{diam}(\phi(\beta_{k}))>\delta_{0}
.

(1) Set
r=\textup{diam}(\phi(\beta_{k}))/4
and let
U
be the component of
h^{-1}(\mathbb{D}(0,r))
containing
0
. Since
\textup{diam}(\phi(\beta_{k}))=4r
, the set
\phi(\beta_{k})
cannot be contained in
\mathbb{D}(0,r)
; hence
\psi(\beta)\not\subseteq U
, and
\partial U
meets
\psi(\beta)
. By the claim, any
x\in\partial U\cap\psi(\beta)
satisfies
|x|\geq r
, so
\textup{diam}(\psi(\beta))\geq r\geq\delta_{0}/4
.

Since
\textup{diam}(\alpha)\asymp\textup{diam}(\beta)
, Koebe distortion yields
\textup{diam}(\psi(\alpha))\geq\delta_{1}
for some
\delta_{1}=\delta_{1}(C)>0
. Applying the claim to the component of
h^{-1}(\mathbb{D}(0,\textup{diam}(\phi(\alpha_{k}))/4))
containing
0
, whose boundary intersects
\psi(\alpha)
, we obtain

\textup{diam}(\phi(\alpha_{k}))\geq(\delta_{1}/8)^{N}.

As
\alpha_{k}\subseteq\beta_{k}
, we also have
\textup{diam}(\phi(\alpha_{k}))\leq\textup{diam}(\phi(\beta_{k}))\leq 2
. Thus,
\textup{diam}(\phi(\alpha_{k}))\asymp 1
, and Koebe distortion implies
\textup{diam}(\alpha_{k})\asymp\epsilon_{0}\asymp\textup{diam}(\beta_{k})
.

(2) The argument is similar. By assumption and Koebe distortion,
\textup{diam}(\phi(\alpha_{k}))\asymp\textup{diam}(\phi(\beta_{k}))
. Let
r=\textup{diam}(\phi(\alpha_{k}))/4
. The component
U
of
h^{-1}(\mathbb{D}(0,r))
containing
0
has boundary intersecting
\psi(\alpha)
. The claim gives
\textup{diam}(\psi(\alpha))\geq r\geq\delta_{2}>0
, hence
\textup{diam}(\psi(\alpha))\asymp\textup{diam}(\psi(\beta))
. The conclusion follows by Koebe distortion.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

The proof is divided into the following three steps.

Step 1. Inducing the homeomorphism
\mathbf{\tilde{\xi}}
.

We shall show that
\xi
induces a homeomorphism
\tilde{\xi}:J_{g}\to J_{\tilde{g}}
.

We first match the closures of the Fatou domains. Set

and
\tilde{E}=\xi(E)
for a Fatou domain

of

. We claim that
\tilde{E}=\tilde{\pi}(\overline{\tilde{U}})
for a unique Fatou domain
\tilde{U}
of
\tilde{g}
.

To see this, note that by condition (3), any two points of
E
can be joined by a curve meeting
J
in at most countably many points. Since
\xi
is a homeomorphism,
\tilde{E}
has the same property in
\tilde{J}
. Therefore
\tilde{E}
cannot meet two distinct sets of the form
\tilde{\pi}(\overline{\tilde{U}})
: any curve in
\tilde{E}
joining a point in one such set to a point in another would have to intersect
\tilde{J}
in uncountably many points, which is impossible. Hence
\tilde{E}\subseteq\tilde{\pi}(\overline{\tilde{U}})
for some Fatou domain
\tilde{U}
of
\tilde{g}
. Applying the same argument to
\xi^{-1}

Let

and
\tilde{X}\subseteq J_{\tilde{g}}
denote the buried point sets of
J_{g}
and
J_{\tilde{g}}

\pi
maps

, and the Fatou domains of
\tilde{g}

\xi
is a homeomorphism, the claim above implies that
\xi
sends

bijectively onto
\tilde{\pi}(\tilde{X})
. Therefore the composition

\tilde{\xi}:=\tilde{\pi}^{-1}\circ\xi\circ\pi:X\to\tilde{X}

is a well-defined bijection between the buried point sets.

Now we extend
\tilde{\xi}
to the peripheral circles by using prime ends. Since

is a Jordan domain, its boundary
\partial U
is identified with the Carathéodory boundary

of cross-cuts in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{U}

so that its endpoints lie on

and its interior buried in

is a quotient map sending

\pi
induces a homeomorphism from
\mathrm{Cara}(\partial U)
to
\mathrm{Cara}(\partial E)

The same reasoning applied to
\xi
and
\tilde{\pi}
yields homeomorphisms between

and

by composing these maps:

\tilde{\xi}|_{\partial U}=\left(\tilde{\pi}^{-1}\circ\xi\circ\pi\right)|_{\mathrm{Cara}(\partial U)}.

Doing this for every Fatou domain of

\tilde{\xi}:J_{g}\to J_{\tilde{g}}

and

\textup{diam}(\alpha^{\prime})\asymp\textup{diam}(\beta^{\prime})

\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime}
lie in
J_{g}
; applying Claim 1 gives
\textup{diam}(\tilde{\xi}(\alpha^{\prime}))\asymp\textup{diam}(\tilde{\xi}(\beta^{\prime}))
for a constant
C_{3}=C_{3}(C_{1})
. Thus,
\textup{diam}(\tilde{\xi}(\alpha))\asymp\textup{diam}(\tilde{\xi}(\beta))
. Hence,
\tilde{\xi}
is quasisymmetric on
J_{g}
.

Step 3. Final Conjugacy.

Since
\tilde{\xi}:J_{g}\to J_{\tilde{g}}
is a quasisymmetry between Sierpiński carpet Julia sets, by Proposition
1.6
, there exist integers
m^{\prime},l\geq 1
and
m\geq 0
such that

\tilde{g}^{m^{\prime}}\circ\tilde{\xi}=\tilde{g}^{m}\circ\tilde{\xi}\circ g^{l}\quad\text{on }J_{g}.

Restricting to the buried point set
X
(which is dense), and using the conjugacies
f\circ\pi=\pi\circ g
and
\tilde{f}\circ\tilde{\pi}=\tilde{\pi}\circ\tilde{g}
, together with the relation
\xi\circ\pi=\tilde{\pi}\circ\tilde{\xi}
on
J_{g}
, we obtain

\tilde{f}^{m^{\prime}}\circ\xi=\tilde{f}^{m}\circ\xi\circ f^{l}\quad\text{on }\pi(X).

By the continuity of
f
,
\tilde{f}
, and
\xi
, and the density of
\pi(X)
in
J
, this equality holds on the whole Julia set

. This completes the proof.
∎

Step 2. Preserving the tower structure

Having established that
\xi
preserves
B
and

, by precomposing
\xi
with an involution in

if necessary, we may assume
\xi(B)=\tilde{B}\text{ and }\xi(T)=\tilde{T}.

preserves the entire tower:

Proposition 7.3
.

For all
i\geq 0
, we have

(7.3)

\xi(\mathcal{V}_{i})=\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{i},\quad\xi(\mathcal{T}_{i})=\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{i},\quad\xi(\mathcal{K}_{i})=\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{i}.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
i
. We begin with the base cases
i=0,1
.

For
i=0
, we have
\mathcal{V}_{0}=\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{B}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{0}=\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\tilde{B}}
. By Lemma
7.2
and the inductive assumption,
\xi(\mathcal{V}_{0})=\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{0}
and

\mathcal{K}_{0}
consists exactly of those components of
\mathcal{K}
that connect

and

.

For
i=1
, fix a component
V_{1}
of
\mathcal{V}_{1}
. For a Fatou domain
\Omega\subset V_{1}
, define the generalized connecting number by

\ell_{1}(\Omega):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\#\{\text{components of }\mathcal{K}\text{ connecting }\partial V_{1}\text{ and }\Omega\},&\hbox{{in Case (A)};}\\[5.0pt]
\#\{\text{Fatou domains connecting }\partial V_{1}\text{ and }\Omega\},&\hbox{in Case (B).}\end{array}\right.

Since
f
is conformal on
V_{1}
, the arguments in Lemma
7.2
show that
\ell_{1}(\Omega)\leq 2n
, with equality if and only if
\Omega=T_{1}
for some component

. As
\ell_{1}
is a topological invariant and
\xi(V_{1})=\tilde{V}_{1}
, the map
\xi
sends each such

to a component
\tilde{T}_{1}
of
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
; hence
\xi(\mathcal{K}(T_{1}))=\mathcal{K}(\tilde{T}_{1})
. Recalling that
\mathcal{K}_{1}=\bigcup\mathcal{K}(T_{1})
, where the union is taken over all components

, we conclude that (
7.3
) holds for
i=1
.

Assume (
7.3
) holds for all
j\leq i-1
.
We have
V_{i}=V_{i-1}\setminus(\overline{T_{i-1}}\cup\mathcal{K}_{i-1})
.
By the induction hypothesis,
\xi
maps
V_{i-1}\setminus\overline{T_{i-1}}

bijectively onto
\tilde{V}_{i-1}\setminus\overline{\tilde{T}_{i-1}}
.
The components of
\mathcal{K}(T_{i-1})
are characterized
as the components of
\mathcal{K}
connecting
\partial V_{i-1}
and
\partial T_{i-1}
, and this property is preserved by
\xi
.
Hence
\xi(V_{i})=\tilde{V}_{i}
.

The equality

follows by analogous argument: defining a generalized connecting number
\ell_{i}
for Fatou domains in
V_{i}

\xi

imply that
\ell_{i}(\Omega)\leq 2n

Finally,

connecting

Lemma
7.4
gives
\eta(T_{i})=\eta_{*}(\xi(T_{i}))

for all
i\geq 1
. Therefore, for
i\geq 2
, we have

\eta_{*}(f_{*}\circ\xi(T_{i}))=\sigma(\eta_{*}(\xi(T_{i})))=\sigma(\eta(T_{i}))=\eta(f(T_{i}))=\eta_{*}(\xi\circ f(T_{i})).

Since the labeling is faithful (distinct components have distinct labels),
we conclude
f_{*}\circ\xi(T_{i})=\xi\circ f(T_{i})
for every component
T_{i}
of
\mathcal{T}_{i}
and every
i\geq 1
.

Since both
f
and
\tilde{f}
are postcritically finite,

\textup{diam}(T_{i})\to 0
and
\textup{diam}(\tilde{T}_{i})\to 0
as
i\to\infty
.
Every point
z\in J
is the limit of a sequence of components

T_{i}\subset\mathcal{T}_{i}
. By continuity, taking limits in the identity

f_{*}\circ\xi=\xi\circ f
on each
T_{i}
yields

f_{*}\circ\xi(z)=\xi\circ f(z)
. Since
z
was arbitrary, this holds for all
z\in J
. This completes the proof of Theorem
7.1
.
∎

8.
Quasisymmetric rigidity of the Julia sets

In this section, we complete the proof of Theorem
1.5
.
Before the proof, we outline the proof of Proposition
1.7
, with further details referring to
[
BLM
, Theorem 1.4]
or
[
QYZ
, Theorem 5.2]
.

Sketch of the proof of Proposition
1.7
.

Since
f
is postcritically finite, there exists a system of Böttcher maps on its Fatou domains that are conformally conjugate to
z\mapsto z^{\delta}
on
\mathbb{D}
piecewise
[
BLM
, Lemma 3.3]
. By the dynamical relation in the condition and
[
BLM
, Lemma 7.1]
,
\xi
can be conformally extended to the periodic Fatou domains; and further extended to the entire Fatou set via iterative liftings of
f
and
\tilde{f}
.

The extended
\xi
is a homeomorphism of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
, conformal on the Fatou set, and quasisymmetric on the Julia set. Moreover, it maps Fatou centers to Fatou centers.

Next, we only need to verify that
\xi
is a Möbius transformation. The key observation is that boundaries of the Fatou domains of
f
and
\tilde{f}
are uniform quasicircles
[
QYZ
, Proposition 3.5]
. Let
U
be a Fatou domain of
f
and
V=\xi(U)
. By the removability of quasicircles for quasiconformal maps and the Schwarz reflection principle on
\partial\mathbb{D}
, the restriction
\xi:\overline{U}\to\overline{V}
is uniformly quasisymmetric.

Now, the maps restricted to closures of Fatou domains are uniformly quasisymmetric, and to
J
is also quasisymmetric. As shown in the latter part of the proof of
[
Bon11
, Proposition 5.1]
,
\xi
is quasiconformal on
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
.

Since the Julia set
J
has measure zero
[
QYZ
, Theorem 3.8]
,
\xi
is a quasiconformal map that is conformal on
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus J
, which has full measure in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
. Thus,
\xi
is 1-quasiconformal, and this implies
\xi
is a Möbius transformation.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

By Lemma
2.4
, multiplying
\lambda
by
e^{2\pi{\bf i}k/(n-1)}
only rotates the Julia set by
e^{\pi{\bf i}k/(n(n-1))}
. Thus we may normalize:
\arg(\lambda),\arg(\tilde{\lambda})\in[0,{2\pi}/(n-1))
.
Moreover, if
J
is a cluster, we replace
\tilde{f}
with
f_{*}
as in Theorem
7.1
.

For Sierpiński carpet Julia sets, Proposition
1.6
gives the dynamical relation (
1.1
); for Sierpiński-like carpet Julia sets, Corollary
4.2
reduces them to the Sierpiński model and Theorem
5.1
yields (
1.1
); for necklace and cluster Julia sets, Theorems
6.2
and
7.1
give, after precomposing
\xi
with an element of
G_{\lambda}
if necessary, conjugacy relations of the form (
7.1
), which are special cases of (
1.1
). Thus the quasisymmetry
\xi
satisfies (
1.1
) on
J
in all four topological cases. Proposition
1.7
then implies that
\xi
extends to a Möbius transformation on
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
, and the relation still holds on
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
. Moreover,
\xi
maps each Fatou domain of
f
and its center to those of
\tilde{f}
.

Since
\xi
is a homeomorphism and
\deg f=\deg\tilde{f}
, comparing degrees in (
1.1
) gives
m^{\prime}=m+l
, i.e.,

\tilde{f}^{m+l}\circ\xi=\tilde{f}^{m}\circ\xi\circ f^{l}.

Furthermore, for any integer
q\geq 1
, applying the relation repeatedly shows that

\tilde{f}^{q(m+l)}\circ\xi=\tilde{f}^{qm}\circ\xi\circ f^{ql};

indeed, the induction step follows by composing the relation on the left with
\tilde{f}^{qm}
. Hence we may replace
(l,m)
by
(ql,qm)
and assume
l\geq 5
and
m\geq 0
.

Claim 1.

As a collection of Fatou domains,
\xi\{B,T\}=\{\tilde{B},\tilde{T}\}
.

Proof of Claim 1.

For necklace and cluster Julia sets the claim is clear, since
\xi
is a conjugacy (Theorems
6.2
and
7.1
). Thus we assume
J
is a Sierpiński or Sierpiński-like carpet.

Suppose
U:=\xi^{-1}(\tilde{T})\notin\{B,T\}
. From the dynamical relation we have

\textup{deg}(\tilde{f}^{m+l}\circ\xi,U)=\textup{deg}(\tilde{f}^{m}\circ\xi\circ f^{l},U)=n^{m+l},

which forces
\deg(f^{l},U)=n^{l}
. Hence
f
has degree
n
on each domain in the orbit segment
U,f(U),\dots,f^{l-1}(U)
, so each contains a critical point of local degree
n
. Since free critical points of
f
are simple, this forces
n=2
.

Since
f^{-1}(T)
contains no critical point (as the critical values
\pm 2\sqrt{\lambda}\neq 0
), the orbit segment avoids
f^{-1}(T)
.
With
l\geq 5
and only four free critical points, two terms in the orbit segment coincide; thus the segment contains a Fatou domain cycle. Moreover, the two free critical values of
f
are critical points. Since
n=2
, any two free critical points
c
and
c^{\prime}
satisfy
f^{2}(c)=f^{2}(c^{\prime})
. Thus, the four Fatou domains
V_{i}
(
i=0,1,2,3
) containing free critical points, one of them being
U
, satisfy

f^{l}(V_{i})=f^{l}(U)\text{ and }\textup{deg}(f^{l},V_{i})=n^{l}.

Combining this with the dynamical relation, we obtain

\textup{deg}(\tilde{f}^{l+m},\xi(V_{i}))=\textup{deg}(\tilde{f}^{m}\circ\xi\circ f^{l},V_{i})=\textup{deg}(\tilde{f}^{l+m}\circ\xi,U)=n^{l+m},

and

\tilde{f}^{m+l}(\xi(V_{i}))=\tilde{f}^{m}(\xi(f^{l}(U)))=\tilde{B}.

Hence, at least two of
V_{0},\dots,V_{3}
are sent by
\xi
to Fatou domains other than
\tilde{B}
and
\tilde{T}
, and these domains are iterated onto
\tilde{B}
. This is impossible because
\tilde{f}^{-1}(\tilde{T})
contains no critical points.
Therefore,
\xi^{-1}(\tilde{T})\in\{B,T\}
. The same argument applies to
\tilde{B}
. This completes the proof of Claim 1.
∎

Next, by precomposing an element of
G_{\lambda}
if necessary, we may assume
\xi(B)=\tilde{B}
and
\xi(T)=\tilde{T}
. Thus,
\xi
fixes
0
and
\infty
. Write
\xi(z)=az
. We perform Laurent expansions for
\tilde{f}^{m+l}\circ\xi
and
\tilde{f}^{m}\circ\xi\circ f^{l}
in a punctured neighborhood of infinity.
Since
\tilde{f}^{m+l}\circ\xi=\tilde{f}^{m}\circ\xi\circ f^{l}
, the coefficients of their highest-order terms coincide. It implies
|a|=1
.

Claim 2.

\xi(f^{-1}(T))=\tilde{f}^{-1}(\tilde{T})
.

Proof of Claim 2.

Still, it suffices to consider the case where
J
is a Sierpiński or Sierpiński-like carpet. Let
U
be a Fatou domain in
f^{-1}(T)
and
\tilde{U}=\xi(U)
. Since

\textup{deg}(\tilde{f}^{m}\circ\xi\circ f^{l},U)=\textup{deg}(\tilde{f}^{m+l},\tilde{U})=n^{m+l-1},

we have
\tilde{U}\subseteq\tilde{f}^{-1}(\tilde{T})
whenever
n\geq 3
.
For
n=2
, either
\tilde{U}\subseteq\tilde{f}^{-1}(\tilde{T})
or
\tilde{U}
is critical and
\tilde{f}^{2}(\tilde{U})=\tilde{T}
. It suffices to exclude the latter case.

By considering the inverse
\xi^{-1}
,
U
is critical and
f^{2}(U)=T
. A direct computation shows
\lambda=\tilde{\lambda}=-{1}/{16}
; the free critical points of
f
and
\tilde{f}
are
\{\pm\frac{1}{2}e^{\pi\textbf{i}/4},\pm\frac{1}{2}e^{-\pi\textbf{i}/4}\}
, and the roots of
f
and
\tilde{f}
are
\{\pm{1}/{2},\pm\textbf{i}/2\}
.

Note that the union of roots and free critical points of
f
(resp.
\tilde{f}
) is evenly distributed on the circle
\{|z|={|\lambda|}^{1/4}\}
. The dynamical relation implies that
\xi(f^{-1}(T))
consists of four critical Fatou domains of
\tilde{f}
. By considering the inverse
\xi^{-1}
, similarly
\xi^{-1}(\tilde{f}^{-1}(\tilde{T}))
consists of four critical Fatou domains of
f
. Thus
\xi
interchanges the roots of
f
with the free critical points of
\tilde{f}
.

By composing an element of
G_{\lambda}
if necessary, we may assume
\xi(z)=e^{\pi\textbf{i}/4}z
. Suppose
f(z_{0})={1}/{2}
. Then the local degree of
\tilde{f}^{m}\circ\xi\circ f^{l}
at the point
z_{0}
equals
2^{m+l-2}
. However, since neither
\xi(z_{0})
nor
\tilde{f}(\xi(z_{0}))
is a critical or root point of
\tilde{f}
, the local degree of
\tilde{f}^{m+l}\circ\xi
at
z_{0}
is strictly less than
2^{m+l-2}
, contradicting the dynamical relation.
∎

By Claim 2, the
2n
-th roots of
-\lambda
are mapped onto those of
-\tilde{\lambda}
under the affine transformation
\xi
. Since
|a|=1
, we have
\tilde{\lambda}=a^{2n}\lambda
and

a=e^{\textbf{i}\theta}
with

\theta=\frac{\textup{arg}(\tilde{\lambda})-\textup{arg}(\lambda)}{2n},

up to precomposing
\xi
with a rotation in
G_{\lambda}
.

It remains to show that
a^{2n}
is an
(n-1)
-th root of unity. Suppose otherwise. Then
a^{-2n(n-1)}\neq 1
, so
a^{-(n-1)}
is not a
2n
-th root of unity. Define
h=\xi\circ f\circ\xi^{-1}
. A direct computation gives

(8.1)

h(z)={a^{-(n-1)}}\tilde{f}(z).

Since
h
is conjugate to
f
via
\xi
, we have
J_{h}=\xi(J)=\tilde{J}
. Thus, the identity map
\textup{id}:J_{h}\to\tilde{J}
is a quasisymmetry. By the corresponding rigidity theorem (Proposition
1.6
, Theorems
5.1
,
6.2
, or
7.1
), there exist integers
l^{\prime}\geq 5
and
m^{\prime}\geq 0
such that

\tilde{f}^{l^{\prime}+m^{\prime}}=\tilde{f}^{m^{\prime}}\circ h^{l^{\prime}}\text{ on }\overline{\mathbb{C}}.

Pick a point
z_{0}
that is not a critical point of
\tilde{f}
but whose image
\tilde{f}(z_{0})
is a root of
\tilde{f}
. Such a point
z_{0}
exists since
\tilde{f}
has only two critical values and
2n
roots. Thus, the local degree of
\tilde{f}^{l^{\prime}+m^{\prime}}
at
z_{0}
satisfies

\textup{deg}(\tilde{f}^{l^{\prime}+m^{\prime}},z_{0})=n^{l^{\prime}+m^{\prime}-2}.

Note that the roots and critical points of
h
coincide with those of
\tilde{f}
by (
8.1
). Since
a^{-(n-1)}
is not a
2n
-th root of unity by assumption, the point
h(z_{0})=a^{-(n-1)}\tilde{f}(z_{0})
is not a root of
h
. Hence, the local degree of
\tilde{f}^{m^{\prime}}\circ h^{l^{\prime}}
at
z_{0}
satisfies

\textup{deg}(\tilde{f}^{m^{\prime}}\circ h^{l^{\prime}},z_{0})\leq 2n^{l^{\prime}+m^{\prime}-3}.

The dynamical relation forces these two degrees to coincide, which can happen only if
n=2
,
h(z_{0})
is a critical point of
h
, and
h^{2}(z_{0})
is a root of
h
. Since
h
is conjugate to
f
via
\xi
, the point
c_{0}:=\xi^{-1}(h(z_{0}))
is a critical point of
f
, and
f(c_{0})
is a root of
f
. A direct computation gives
f(z)=z^{2}-{1}/{(16z^{2})}
.

Now pick a point
w_{0}
that is not a critical point of
h
but whose image
h(w_{0})
is a root of
h
. A similar argument yields
\textup{deg}(\tilde{f}^{m^{\prime}}\circ h^{l^{\prime}},w_{0})=2^{l^{\prime}+m^{\prime}-2}
. By the assumption that
a^{n-1}
is not a
2n
-th root of unity, the point
\tilde{f}(w_{0})=a^{n-1}h(w_{0})
is not a root of
\tilde{f}
. Thus,
\textup{deg}(\tilde{f}^{l^{\prime}+m^{\prime}},w_{0})\leq 2^{l^{\prime}+m^{\prime}-2}
. The dynamical relation forces equality. Thus,
\tilde{f}(w_{0})
is a critical point of
\tilde{f}
and
\tilde{f}^{2}(w_{0})
is a root of
\tilde{f}
. This implies
\tilde{f}(z)=z^{2}-1/(16z^{2})
.

The roots of both
f
and
\tilde{f}
are
\{\pm 1/2,\pm\textbf{i}/2\}
. By Claim 2,
\xi
maps the roots of
f
onto those of
\tilde{f}
. Thus,
a^{2n}=1
, which is obviously an
(n-1)
-th root of unity. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎
