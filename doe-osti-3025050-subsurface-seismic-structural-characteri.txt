Executive Summary:
This final report summarizes work performed under award DE-FE0032369 (DOE-FOA-0002799,
AOI 2) during the period of 12/1/2023 to 11/30/2025. Tasks performed in this project contribute
to the accelerated, safe, and socially equitable deployment of Carbon Capture Utilization and
Storage (CCUS) in the San Juan Basin (SJB). The project team compiled existing and interpreted
new subsurface data in the SJB to provide stakeholders in the broader Four Corners region with
crucial information to fill the subsurface knowledge gaps needed to enable the deployment of
carbon management activities. Newly interpreted 2D seismic and well log data combined with
previous subsurface interpretations identify fault locations and delineate 3D characteristics of the
Hogback monocline. Also, the project team characterized the present-day thermal regime of the
SJB as well as its thermal tectonic history using low temperature thermochronometers. Finally, the
team provided a probabilistic resource assessment of the SJB and a novel machine learning (ML)
approach to subsurface characterization. All community benefits task commitments were fulfilled
to the maximum extent allowable under DOE guidance. Core outreach, workforce, and Justice40
objectives were successfully delivered. The team worked on Diversity, Equity, Inclusion, and
Accessibility (DEIA)-related activities prior to January 28, 2025.

This project complements concurrent DOE CCUS projects by improving the understanding of the
impacts of CO2 injection to ensure safe, secure, efficient, and affordable injection and containment
in a socially equitable way. The data and work products developed, found on the DOE EDX portal,
are helpful in developing regional-specific scenarios that guide CCUS decision-makers and
industry stakeholders in the SJB and the Four Corners region.

Introduction
The study area is located in the northwest portion of the SJB, which was formed approximately
75-80 million years ago as a result of the Laramide orogeny (Fig. 1.1; Cather et al., 2004,
Dickinson et al., 1988, Thacker et al., 2023). Steep structural features define the basin margins and
are associated with uplifts and folds, such as the Hogback Monocline (Fassett, 1991). The SJB
contains sedimentary fill ranging from 2 to 4 km in thickness, including Paleozoic to Cenozoic
rocks overlying the Precambrian crystalline basement (Fig. 1.2; Brister and Hoffman, 2002;
Rudolph et al., 2025).

 Figure 1.1. Hillshade map of New Mexico with sedimentary basin outlines. The red polygon in
the upper northwest corner highlights the modeling area and the licensed 2D seismic lines (light
 blue) utilized in this project.

 5

The SJB has been affected by multiple tectonic events causing crustal deformation and subsidence.
Initially, Precambrian accretion established the structural fabrics that later played an important role
in basin development. During the Late Paleozoic, the region was affected by the Ancestral Rocky
Mountains, which produced basement highs and influenced the initial structural architecture.
During the Mesozoic, the basin experienced subsidence in response to tectonism along the western
margin of North America associated with the Sevier orogeny. The present-day asymmetric basin
geometry is a consequence of compression during the Late Cretaceous–early Paleogene Laramide
Orogeny. During the Cenozoic, uplift of the Colorado Plateau led to exhumation and internal
deformation in some areas (Cather et al., 2019; Karlstrom et al., 2022; Yonkee and Weil, 2015).

The project study area spanned the main topographic expression of the Hogback Monocline. This
area shows evidence of structural inversion, with major reverse faults reactivated during the
Laramide Orogeny, as well as some basal faults that were not reactivated. Previous work
conducted in the SJB, utilizing seismic surveys, identified basin and fault geometries. Some studies
focused on subsurface compilations and the definition of fault geometries (Taylor and Huffman,
1998; Hart, 2021) that relied primarily on well data for mapping combined with surface geology
(Craigg, 2001; Cather, 2004). The compilation and subsurface interpretation conducted by Taylor
and Huffman (1998) presents the mapped faults within the basin and the major structural trends of
the region.

This project primarily focused on identifying structures and defining thicknesses associated with
potential carbon-sequestration targets. For the depth-domain seismic interpretation in this study,
the team identified approximately 20 seismic horizons and six major faults associated with the
Hogback Monocline fault system. The seismic interpretation includes the Upper and Lower
Jurassic formations, including the Morrison, Saltwash, Bluff, Summerville, Todilto, Entrada,
Carmel/Dewey Bridge, and Wingate formations (Fig. 1.2). The structural framework includes
reverse faults, high-angle thrust faults, and non-reactivated basal faults. Some structural features
control both the geometry and thickness distribution of the Jurassic units and therefore play a
critical role in assessing the potential for carbon sequestration within the study area.

Within the SJB, modern heat flow is variable and does not correlate to modern structural basin
features (Blackwell et. al., 2011). The northern, southeastern, and western areas of the basin have
anomalously high heat flow and modern temperature gradients, and these are anomalies are
interpreted to have formed in response to heating by the adjacent San Juan Volcanic Field to the
north and/or the Valles Caldera to the east (Reiter and Clarkson, 1983; Law, 1992). Thermal
characterization conducted as part of this study compiled bottom hole temperatures (BHTs) with
temperature ranging from 14 to 162 °C into a project database for boreholes with depths ranging
from 90 m to 4,400 m. Apatite and zircon thermochronology samples for twelve aliquots from
Paleogene, Cretaceous, and Pennsylvanian formations in outcrop, cuttings, or core are interpreted
as being mostly thermally reset. The calculated dates range from 0.9 ± 0.03 Ma to 122.0 ± 5.7 Ma
for the apatite samples and from 34.3 ± 2.7 Ma to 587.9 ± 14.7 Ma for the zircon sample.

To update storage models, additional wells were incorporated to improve structural calibration and
petrophysical constraints. These well logs provided key insights into lithology, depositional facies,
and reservoir heterogeneity, helping improve the representation of subsurface geological
properties. Porosity logs were used to estimate reservoir storage capacity and characterize the
petrophysical properties of both the storage formation and adjacent units. Using these datasets,
spatial porosity distributions were generated through geostatistical interpolation and property
modeling for the Entrada Formation (the primary storage reservoir) and the overlying sealing
formations that act as caprock for CO₂ containment. This characterization is critical for assessing
storage efficiency, injectivity, and long-term containment in carbon capture and storage systems.

In the ML section of this study, seismic attributes were extracted for wells that intersect seismic
lines and matched with log derived measurements such as volume of shale (Vsh) values calculated
from gamma-ray. Vsh was selected as the target variable because it reflects shale content and
strongly influences key reservoir properties such as porosity, permeability, and fluid saturation.
Machine learning models were trained after data pre-processing to predict lithological properties
from seismic attributes. Model performance was evaluated using coefficient of determination (R²)
and root mean squared error. The results indicate strong agreement between predicted and
measured Vsh. Additional validations were implemented including blind well tests, residual
analysis, and Shapley feature importance evaluation, all confirming strong predictive capability.
The trained model was then applied to 2D seismic lines to generate spatially continuous Vsh
predictions. This workflow integrated reprocessed seismic data, conditioned well logs, attribute
analysis, and ML to enable reliable basin wide estimation of shale volume, supporting reservoir
characterization, stratigraphic interpretation, facies analysis, prospect evaluation, and carbon
sequestration studies.

Chapter 1 - Compiling Geological Data
1.1 - Evaluation of existing well log data

Project researchers compiled all available open-source data from the New Mexico Oil
Conservation Division (NMOCD) database within the study area (Fig. 1.1.1). Initially, these data
consisted mainly of well logs and related information stored in TIFF, PDF, and JPG archive
formats. All files were transferred to a regional project database and converted into digital LAS
format. A total of 293 well logs were utilized in this project to achieve the primary objectives.

 Figure 1.1.1. Location of borehole logs employed during the execution of project DE-
 FE0032369. The red polygon is the project modeling area, and the stateline is in white. See
 Figure 1.1 for regional location.

In addition, the team incorporated legacy data curated by the New Mexico Bureau of Geology and
Mineral Resources (NMBGMR). Most of this information is unique and was originally transferred
directly from industry sources to the NMBGMR. These legacy datasets, originally available as
printed logs, were digitized and converted to LAS format. All datasets are stored in the main
project database and were carefully filtered to remove duplicate files.

The main objective of this project was to develop a subsurface structural model of the Hogback
Monocline and to characterize the thermal properties of the SJB. To achieve this goal, all available

 9

well logs were compiled within the study area. Because this work focuses on building a geological
framework model, the primary inputs were logs that allow the identification of formation tops and
lithology.

The well logs used for lithology identification include gamma ray and spontaneous potential logs.
Resistivity logs were interpreted to identify formation fluids, while neutron, density, and sonic
logs were used to calculate petrophysical properties such as porosity and permeability. In addition,
the team integrated data from different projects conducted by New Mexico Tech in recent years.
With appropriate citations in the project references, these include the New Mexico Wells internal
database, the NMBGMR database, previous CarbonSAFE project data, and unpublished NMOCD
data.

1.3.2 - Processing seismic data

The 2D seismic lines were processed by Agile Seismic LLC under the supervision of project
members. The aim of this work was to reprocess multiple 2D land-seismic datasets in the SJB,
New Mexico, primarily across the Hogback Monocline. These seismic data came from different
surveys acquired primarily during the 1960s, with one seismic line acquired in 1973 and another
in 1991. This reprocessing effort integrated all surveys into a single multi-2D seismic dataset,
treating these lines as a pseudo-3D seismic survey where possible. The objective of this processing
was to improve the seismic imaging of this legacy data for CCUS purposes, especially in areas
near the injection wells.

To improve seismic imaging quality, the team employed a combination of proven seismic
processing and imaging technologies along with internal algorithms developed by Agile Seismic
LLC. The main tools used in this reprocessing included Time-Frequency Domain (TFD) filtering,
refraction and residual statics, and pre-stack and post-stack interpolation. The principal imaging
methods were Kirchhoff pre-stack time migration (PSTM) and pre-stack depth migration (PSDM),
as well as Gaussian Beam Migration (GBM) and Reverse Time Migration (RTM). The results of
this seismic processing include multi-2D stacks (Stack, Post Stack Time Migration (PoSTM), and

 14

Post Stack Depth Migration (PoSDM) stacks, PSTM, PSDM, GBM, and RTM gathers and stacks
for each 2D seismic line. In addition, multi-2D velocities and stacks (PSTM and PSDM) were 3D-
interpolated over the entire survey area to produce pseudo-3D PSTM and PSDM velocities and
pseudo-3D PSTM and RTM stacks.

The vendor's legacy data was delivered in a PSTM format. Although geology plays an important
role in seismic imaging, depth imaging is required because of the complexity of the main structure,
the Hogback Monocline. Therefore, Kirchhoff PSDM, GBM, and RTM were applied in addition
to Kirchhoff PSTM.

There were many challenges in improving the seismic imaging because the data are very old and
sparse, with low resolution in some areas, except for seismic line AYH-1. Most of the data were
acquired in the 1960s, while line AYH-1 was acquired in 1991. Most of the lines have low fold

 15

(6), whereas seismic line AYH-1 has a fold of 64. In addition, maximum offsets are around 6,500
ft, while seismic line AYH-1 exceeded 12,000 ft. A common rule of thumb in depth imaging is
that maximum offsets comparable to the target depth are needed to resolve velocity at that depth.
However, only line AYH-1 meets this requirement. This limitation leads to ambiguity in the
derivation of 2D velocities. To reduce this velocity ambiguity, a 3D-interpolated velocity model
was derived from the individual 2D velocities and then smoothed to produce a pseudo-3D velocity
(Fig. 1.3.2). Reconciled isotropic 2D velocities were then extracted from this pseudo-3D velocity
along the seismic line profiles.

Figure 1.3.2. PSTM (Pre-Stack time migration) velocities of the 2D seismic lines utilized in this
 project. PSTM velocities generally increase with depth (from blue to red).

Because these seismic datasets were acquired independently and do not share midpoints between
the 2D surveys, pseudo-3D PSTM and pseudo-3D PSDM velocities were generated to ensure good
time- and depth-migration ties. However, pseudo-3D interpolation of the seismic data did not yield
good results except in the vicinity of individual lines because the variations in seismic velocities
required for imaging are smaller than the variations in the seismic data; in other words, the spatial
frequency of the velocity field is lower than that of the seismic data.

Another major challenge was associated with the derivation of anisotropic PSDM velocities. To
derive anisotropic velocities, interpreted horizons and their corresponding markers are required.
For this purpose, the team used the most continuous seismic reflectors and their respective
markers, from top to bottom: Menefee, Dakota, Entrada, and Precambrian. Initially, only the
Dakota and Precambrian horizons and markers were used. These horizons were interpreted in the

 16

time domain and converted to depth. Well ties were then performed to review and edit the markers
with respect to seismic reflector continuity. Using these two horizons and their corresponding
markers, anisotropic velocities were derived for each line, and reconciled anisotropic 2D velocities
were produced through pseudo-3D velocity construction. These reconciled 2D velocities provided
good ties among the multi-2D lines.

Because the primary CCUS target is the Entrada Formation, Entrada markers were subsequently
requested, and the Entrada horizon was interpreted on all lines. Menefee markers and the
interpreted Menefee horizon were also included on all lines. Interpretation of the Menefee and
Entrada horizons was more challenging than that of the Dakota and Precambrian horizons. In
addition, the Entrada horizon is close to the Dakota horizon, and small errors in horizon or marker
interpretation may lead to large errors in velocity calibration. A new, well-calibrated pseudo-3D
velocity model was therefore produced using all four horizons and markers. Calibrated and
reconciled 2D velocities were then extracted along the line profiles from this pseudo-3D velocity
model.

These calibrated 2D PSDM velocities were used for the final anisotropic depth migrations using
Kirchhoff PSDM, GBM, and RTM (Fig. 1.3.3). The migrated stacks were then post-processed,
including noise removal and amplitude balancing. The migrated stacks show good ties at line
intersections because pseudo-3D velocities were used, ensuring sample-by-sample velocity
consistency at the intersections. However, some residual mis-ties remain, attributed to phase shifts
caused by different seismic sources and to vertical shifts related to 2D effects and differences in
statics. Statics could not be derived and applied in a pseudo-3D sense and were instead derived
and applied separately for each line. Residual mis-tie analysis and correction were performed in
Petrel to produce the final reprocessed anisotropic depth-migrated stacks.

1.3.3 - Interpreting seismic data

The seismic interpretation process was conducted at different stages during the development of the
project to evaluate and compare the impact of seismic processing on imaging quality. The
interpretation workflow included: (1) interpretation of legacy seismic data without reprocessing in
the time domain, (2) interpretation of preliminary processed seismic data in the time domain, and
(3) interpretation of the final seismic processing results in the depth domain. These stages were
conducted to assess improvements in seismic imaging, reflector continuity, structural definition,
and overall interpretability of the seismic data.

(1) Interpretation of Legacy Seismic Data without Reprocessing in the Time Domain

The interpretation of the unprocessed legacy seismic dataset in the time domain is a common task,
in which the team explored the original field data, acquisition parameters, processing workflows,
and their limitations. There are several limitations associated with outdated processing
methodologies and technology; although these seismic lines preserve major geometric features and
dip changes related to structural complexity, poor seismic imaging is common in some areas. At
this stage, the team interpreted the most continuous reflectors, major discontinuities, and the
relationships between folding and faulting.

The use of legacy data without reprocessing limits the correction of velocity inaccuracies, noise
attenuation, and amplitude preservation. In addition, because these seismic data come from

 18

different surveys processed independently, the likelihood of mismatches between seismic lines
increases during horizon picking and fault mapping. The vendor's legacy seismic lines were
provided in the time domain, with three versions available: stack, migrated, and PSTM.

An overview of seismic quality and interpretation was conducted using the PSTM seismic data, as
this processing approach accounts for lateral velocity variations, improves the imaging of faults
and complex structures, and generally provides a more geologically reliable time-domain image.
Horizon picking was carried out using borehole data with sonic logs; these data were used to
generate velocity logs for depth-to-time conversion. Well tops converted to the time domain were
then used to guide horizon interpretation. Overall, most seismic lines exhibit good lateral
continuity in the shallow and intermediate sections. The best seismic imaging is observed on line
AYH-1, whereas all lines show some degree of degraded imaging at deeper intervals, characterized
by reduced reflector continuity. Vertical resolution is moderate, while lateral resolution allows the
tracking of several seismic reflectors across the sections. However, limited resolution at shallow
levels along the Hogback Monocline is common and is likely related to the original processing
parameters. In general, seismic resolution is strongly influenced by the structural complexity of
the Hogback Monocline, where significant changes in reflector dip are observed.

(2) Interpretation of Preliminary Processed Seismic Data in the Time Domain

The seismic data were interpreted using the Kirchhoff PSTM version in the time domain. During
this process, the data were normalized to visualize and compare them with the raw seismic data
without reprocessing. In this interpretation task, the main reflectors were tracked, and borehole
data, including sonic and velocity logs, were used to tie the seismic interpretation. Approximately
65 boreholes across the study area, with different depths and velocity logs, were used to support
the time-domain seismic interpretation.

This newly processed seismic time-domain dataset aimed to identify structural features and
depositional stratigraphic geometry in the SJB. Time-domain interpretation allows direct use of
seismic lines while acknowledging the limitations posed by velocity uncertainties and poor seismic
imaging in structurally complex areas such as the Hogback Monocline. In general, this new version
improved imaging in some areas along this structure and enhanced both vertical and horizontal
resolution.

Overall, the seismic lines processed with this reprocessing workflow display more laterally
continuous reflectors in the shallow-to-intermediate intervals, even along the Hogback Monocline,
where they were absent in previous non-reprocessed versions. These reflectors generally display
subparallel geometries in the eastern part of the study area, in contrast to the western area, which
shows greater disturbance and disruption along the topographic expression of the monocline. In
this western sector, features such as steeply dipping reflectors, seismic shadow effects, and locally
chaotic seismic character are observed and are linked to structural complexity.

 19

All seismic lines were shifted using a Digital Elevation Model (DEM). This DEM was initially
uploaded in feet with elevation values and later converted to time to support seismic interpretation
and guide surface control in the time domain. For this calculation, the project reference datum of
7,000 ft and a replacement velocity of 8,000 ft/s were used, and a new time-domain DEM was
generated. The seismic lines were interpreted from north to south to visualize the Hogback
Monocline's influence on the basin and ongoing deformation.

In this project, all seismic lines were processed using consistent processing parameters to optimize
the integration of different surveys. A grid of 10 2D seismic lines was interpreted, totaling
approximately 245 miles. Through this time-domain interpretation, the main structural and
stratigraphic features were characterized. This workflow was developed using updated well tops
in the Petrel (Schlumberger) project, supported by stratigraphic well correlation. The new
processing allows the identification of continuous and clear reflectors that dip to the southeast and
outline the Hogback Monocline, in contrast to the subparallel reflectors of the undeformed regional
level to the east (Fig. 1.3.4).

  Figure 1.3.4. Western side of the seismic line AYH-1. In this line the folded reflectors of the
 Hogback Monocline are juxtaposed with the undeformed reflectors in the SJB.

Seven seismic dip lines are oriented NW–SE. From north to south, dip variations are observed
along the Hogback Monocline trend. The three strike seismic lines are oriented perpendicular to
the dip lines. These data illustrate the influence of the basin's structural features on its seismic
response. To characterize and compare the seismic interpretation, the lines were divided into three
levels: upper, middle, and basal (bottom). These levels were defined based on seismic reflector
character, continuity, and resolution (Fig. 1.3.5).

 Figure 1.3.5. Western side of the seismic line AYH-1 in the time domain. The seismic
interpretation levels defined by the authors to conduct the seismic interpretation are highlighted.

The upper level comprises seismic reflectors between 0 and ~800 ms Two-Way Travel time (TWT)
to the east of the Hogback Monocline. In the northern region, the deepest formation at this level is
the Lewis Shale Formation (Upper Cretaceous), whereas in the central and southern parts of the
study area, this reflector corresponds to the Menefee Formation (Upper Cretaceous). Major
reflector discontinuities are present near the top of this level compared to the guide horizons
located in the middle and lower parts of the upper level. In the western area, along the hinge of the
Hogback Monocline, the reflectors of this level range between 0 and ~500 ms TWT, where weak
reflectors and low-energy seismic character are common. Regarding fault influence, steepening of
dips is observed along the fault trace, but there is no clear evidence that this fault cuts through the
entire sequence to the surface.

The middle level generally ranges from ~750 to ~1600 ms TWT on the eastern side of the Hogback
Monocline. In this interval, reflectors display subparallel continuity, and higher-energy reflectors
are observed toward the base of the level. In the north, the deepest formation within this level is

 21

the Chinle Formation (Upper Triassic), while to the south, the base of this level is around ~1400
ms TWT. Along the Hogback Monocline, reflectors in this level occur between ~500 and ~1200
ms TWT, with increasing energy toward the base. The fault truncates the sequence to the north,
whereas some seismic lines farther south show no clear evidence of faulting and instead display
folding.

The basal (bottom) level is defined based on the seismic response of the lithological units and the
basement. To the east of the study area, this level ranges from ~1600 to ~2300 ms TWT and is
mainly associated with the basement horizon, which in this project is defined as the top of the non-
sedimentary rocks within the SJB. Along the Hogback Monocline, this basal level is located
between ~1200 and ~1600 ms TWT. Fault displacement affects this level from north to south.

In summary, the Hogback Monocline is clearly identified on the seismic lines. From north to south,
the structural feature changes its topographic and seismic relief, decreasing toward the south. In
addition, the presence of seismic shadow zones suggests that this fault system may be segmented
and does not affect all stratigraphic levels uniformly. At this stage, the interpretation allows the
team to define and locate the main structures and to assess the influence of the Hogback Monocline
on basin development and sedimentary infill. However, because the seismic lines are interpreted
in the time domain, the thickness and true dimensions of these layers cannot yet be directly
measured or quantified.

(3) Interpretation of the final seismic processing results in the depth domain.

The 10 seismic lines were interpreted in the depth domain using RTM. This processing method
improves illumination of complex geology and performs particularly well in areas with steep dips
and strong velocity contrasts, though it is computationally demanding and expensive. Previously,
the seismic data were interpreted in the time domain using different processing parameters. In this
depth-domain interpretation, the main objective is to define the principal structural features, such
as the Hogback Monocline, and to evaluate their influence on basin sedimentary infill.

In general, the seismic lines show depositional variations from west to east and thickness changes
from north to south. These variations are mostly linked to the different structures that affect the
study area. Within the study area, basement uplifts, reverse faults associated with the Laramide
orogeny, and some non-reactivated normal faults are present. In terms of deformation, the highest
degree of structural complexity is concentrated along the trend of the Hogback Monocline and in
the hanging wall of the fault system associated with this fold. Toward the east, the seismic
reflectors are relatively flat and do not show folding compared with the western side.

and the expression of the Hogback Monocline. In this area, the main structural features are

Chapter 3 - Paragenetic Data
3.1 - Compilation of Paragenetic Data

In the SJB, the team’s literature review of previously published data surrounding paragenesis
found one study focused on the Upper Jurassic Morrison Formation using core data from the
Grants Uranium District in the southern SJB and data from basin-margin outcrops, all outside of
this current project’s study area (Hansley, 1990). Halsey (1990) found that diagenesis of the
Morrison Formation was dominated by early pore-filling calcite and mixed-clays in the southern
SJB and quartz cement to the north. Early diagenesis was controlled by warm, deep-basin water
influx during burial. While in the southern basin, where uranium ore-bearing sandstones are
concentrated, diagenesis was controlled by soluble organic complexes from water/rock interaction.
Ultimately, uplift during Early Cretaceous and Paleogene caused migration of meteoric waters
through permeable sandstones and precipitation of kaolinite.

Chapter 4 - Geologic Modeling

4.1 - Creation of the model

To create the 3D geologic model, the team utilized well data from the NMOCD database, the
NMBGMR Subsurface Library, and commercial well log providers, plus field samples from the
NMBGMR to gather relevant well data including: formation tops, petrophysical characteristics,
bottom hole temperatures and pressures, formation pressures, locations of perforations or hydraulic
fracturing in the wells, formation water chemistries, and injection pressures. All 2D and 3D seismic
data were incorporated into the geological model to improve local structural interpretation, fault
geometry, and constraints on porosity and permeability. The model area is in the northwestern
SJB, defined by the coverage of the project 2D and 3D seismic data (Fig. 4.1.1).

 Figure 4.1.1. 2D seismic lines employed for the construction of the geological framework.

The potential for CO₂ capture and sequestration were evaluated in the Upper Jurassic units. The
geological model includes eight horizons, from the top of the Brushy Basin Formation (Upper
Jurassic) to the Wingate Formation (Lower Jurassic). In addition, the model incorporates five
faults, selected based on their length and their potential impact on the CO₂ sequestration system.
Together, these inputs provide the structural and stratigraphic framework needed to assess the
feasibility of these geological formations as potential CO₂ reservoirs. In this modeling process, the
workflow outlined below was followed:

(1) Defining and building the stratigraphic and structural framework from subsurface data

 36

Building the structural and stratigraphic framework involved integrating all available information
used during seismic interpretation and well correlation. This framework consists of five faults and
eight horizons (Fig. 4.1.2). The faults are linked to the Hogback Monocline fault system, and, to
simplify the modeling, four faults with similar strike and dip that belong to the same fault system
were merged into a single fault. The seismic horizons included in this model include: Morrison,
Saltwash, Bluff, Summerville, Todilto, Entrada, Carmel/Dewey Bridge, and Wingate.

  Figure 4.1.2. Carbon sequestration surface horizons employed in the model developed in this
 project.

All the subsurface information was used to develop the 3D model through interpolation of seismic
interpretation traces and well correlations. This model was generated in the depth domain. The
seismic horizons were defined following the principle of top and bottom surfaces according to the
seismic reflector response. The faults were interpreted and modeled after their kinematics were
tested and validated against the geological framework; this procedure is described in more detail
in Subtask 4.3. Together, these inputs were used to construct the 3D grid.

The interpreted surfaces were used as input to build the grid using the corner-point algorithm, a
method that allows modeling of complex geometries and reservoir structures. The grid has a
resolution of 550 × 550 cells, which is sufficient to visualize and represent geological variations
at the scale of the study area. This grid-building approach facilitates the integration of structural
and sedimentological data, thereby better representing the geometry and spatial distribution of
petrophysical properties.

 37

The model framework includes a basal surface with depths ranging from approximately 2,000 to
8,500 feet subsea. Figure 4.1.3 shows the structural contour map of the Wingate Formation, which
highlights structural highs along the eastern side of the Hogback Monocline trend and a major
depocenter in the north-central part of the study area. This surface analysis allows identification
of the overall morphology and the influence of structural features on basin architecture.

 Figure 4.1.3. Bottom surface map of the Wingate Formation.

(2) Defining and building the grid and box framework based on the modeled surfaces and
faults

Within the model, the team included one fault associated with the Hogback Monocline fault system
(Fig. 4.1.4). This fault system is a reverse structure that forms a seal due to fault displacement. The
fault was identified through seismic interpretation, where the seismic response shows abrupt
changes in the guide reflectors and high-amplitude contrasts. This response is characteristic of
faulting and is used to define structural blocks and guide the workflow of the structural model.

 38

  Figure 4.1.4. Entrada Formation surface versus the regional fault system associated with the
 Hogback Monocline.

Regarding the skeleton grid, three surfaces were created that represent the upper, middle, and lower
pillars; these levels are used to build the final 3D grid. This grid is constrained by subsurface data
interpreted from seismic and borehole data within the study area. The model uses only data within
the surface horizons of interest, which correspond to the Upper and Lower Jurassic units.

For this modeling, zones were defined according to the tops and bottoms of the different surface
horizons. For layering, the team applied a fine vertical discretization of 40 layers to model the
petrophysical properties of each surface with greater precision. This subdivision ensures that the
geometry influences the distribution of petrophysical properties and captures both vertical and
lateral variations. This configuration facilitates modeling and simulation to evaluate fluid
dynamics and storage challenges in the study area.

4.2 - Updating storage model

A total of 81 additional wells were incorporated into the interpretation and the model was updated
to provide structural calibration and petrophysical constraints. Of these wells, 50 contain gamma
ray logs. The gamma ray responses were used to identify well tops for the various horizons. In
addition, 27 wells include porosity logs, which were used to characterize reservoir quality and
estimate the distribution of effective porosity within the storage interval. These well log datasets
provide critical information on lithology, depositional facies, and reservoir heterogeneity, enabling
a more accurate representation of the subsurface geological properties. Porosity logs were
subsequently used to quantify reservoir storage capacity and to characterize the petrophysical
properties of the storage formation and adjacent stratigraphic units. Using these well-based

 40

petrophysical datasets, spatial distributions of porosity were generated through geostatistical
interpolation and property modeling techniques. These distributions were developed for the
primary storage formation, the Entrada Formation, as well as for the overlying sealing formations
that provide the caprock necessary for secure CO₂ containment. The characterization of both
reservoir and seal properties is essential for evaluating storage efficiency, injectivity, and long-
term containment within carbon capture and storage systems.

All structural interpretations, stratigraphic surfaces, and log derived petrophysical properties were
subsequently integrated and updated to construct the 3D geological grid used for reservoir
modeling. The interpreted horizons served as primary structural inputs for grid generation using
the structural gridding algorithm, a widely adopted approach in reservoir modeling that allows for
the accurate representation of complex geological structures. The structural method enables the
model to honor structural discontinuities such as faults and stratigraphic thickness variations while
maintaining grid flexibility and numerical stability. Through this method, the structural framework
is translated into a discretized grid that captures fault offsets, layer geometries, and reservoir
compartmentalization.

This grid building approach facilitates the integration of structural interpretation, stratigraphic
architecture, and sedimentological information within a unified modeling framework. By
combining seismic structural geometry with well based structure and petrophysical data, the
resulting 3D model provides a realistic representation of both the reservoir and sealing formations.
The grid therefore captures the geometry of the storage complex, the spatial variability of
petrophysical properties all of which are critical parameters for evaluating CO₂ storage.

4.3 - 3D fault model

The structural modeling was performed using input from seismic interpretation, in which fault
displacement, kinetics, and thickness variations were detected and validated, along with well
correlations in areas with no seismic coverage. This fault modeling serves to analyze the structural
relationship versus the sedimentary infill and complex geometries that could allow fluids to leak.
To build the structural fault 3D model, the team followed the next steps:

(1) Set up the geometry

(2) Fault framework modeling

(3) Horizon modeling

 43

The processes regarding setting up the geometry and modeling the horizons were previously
explained in Section 4.1. This section will focus mainly on fault framework modeling, which
concerns fault modeling and the analysis of relationships between faults.

Fault framework modeling

Fault framework modeling is a critical step in constructing the 3D geological model, but some
areas lack sufficient data to build a more complex fault model because the available data are
unevenly distributed and of variable density. In this project, six faults were modeled from the
seismic interpretation as smoothed fault surfaces. Each fault represents the best possible fit to the
input data; however, a limited amount of extrapolation relative to the initial interpretation was
applied on a case-by-case basis.

In this project, faults were interpreted using seismic lines and borehole data. Fault control points
were applied by analyzing the seismic, borehole, and topographic responses. In addition, the team
validated the fault trends against the seismic horizon surfaces, which complement each other. This
integrated analysis allowed the team to infer the spatial distribution of faults, especially in areas
with low seismic coverage (Fig. 4.3.1).

 Figure 4.3.1. Fault distribution trend vs seismic and borehole data used to validate the fault
 displacement within the study area.

For data integration, the team used six seismic lines that cross the major fault segments
approximately perpendicular to their strike to characterize each structure. To validate extrapolation
between seismic lines, the team used borehole data and constructed cross-sections with the same
orientation to ensure consistent interpretation. The fault network was evaluated to analyze
connectivity between fault segments and assess fault properties, such as dip, angle, and kinematic
character, to model each fault accordingly. Once the faults were classified by their characteristics,
the team organized them into fault families; in this work, five families were identified. All of them
were modeled for fault analysis, but only one fault was considered for the simulation. This fault
merges segments that share similar geometry and displacement, helping to avoid software
modeling limitations in segmented areas, as is the case for the Hogback Monocline fault system.
(Fig. 4.3.2).

In addition, within this fault modeling framework, geological consistency was validated by
performing forward modeling of the main structures to test different geometries and assess the
accuracy of the team’s interpretation before finalizing the framework model. The team used 10
cross sections, in which the present geometry was constrained using the undeformed regional level
and stratigraphic thicknesses. Different fault displacements and dip angles were tested until the
current geometry was reproduced. In summary, five faults present in the study area were modeled;
however, for the geological framework and subsequent simulation, the team considered a single
merged fault that integrates the Hogback reverse fault system.

4.5 - Machine learning analysis of basin-wide geological data

4.5.1 Introduction

In recent years there has been a transformative shift in subsurface characterization methodologies
with the development and application of ML technologies. Traditional approaches to interpreting
geophysical data have historically relied on empirical relationships and physics-based models that
often struggle to capture the complex, nonlinear relationships inherent in subsurface formations
(Zhao et al, 2024). Machine learning offers a paradigm shift by enabling automated, data driven
analysis that can identify patterns and relationships in high dimensional geophysical datasets that
may be imperceptible to conventional methods (Aly, 2025).

Machine learning encompasses a diverse array of algorithms designed to learn patterns from data
without explicit programming. In geophysical applications, both supervised and unsupervised
learning paradigms have found widespread adoption. Supervised learning methods are trained on
labeled datasets where input features such as seismic attributes and well log measurements are

 49

mapped to known outputs such as lithological and petrophysical properties (Amosu and Sun,
2021). These trained models can then predict properties at unsampled locations or for new datasets.
Artificial neural networks, particularly deep learning architectures, have gained prominence due
to their ability to model highly nonlinear relationships and automatically extract hierarchical
features from raw data (Ahmed et al, 2023). Support vector machines excel in classification tasks
by finding optimal decision boundaries in high dimensional feature spaces, while ensemble
methods like random forests provide robust predictions by aggregating multiple decision trees
(Priezzhev et al, 2018). Recent advances have also explored recurrent neural networks, such as
Long Short Term Memory (LSTM) networks (Amosu and Sun, 2022), for sequential well log
prediction and time series analysis. Unsupervised learning techniques, including clustering
algorithms and dimensionality reduction methods, are valuable for exploratory data analysis, facies
identification, and pattern recognition in geophysical datasets where labeled training data may be
limited (Amosu et al, 2025, Koray et al, 2024).

Well logs and seismic data represent two fundamental sources of information for subsurface
modeling. Well logs provide high resolution, direct measurements of rock properties at discrete
borehole locations; seismic data, conversely, offers 3D coverage of the subsurface but at lower
resolution, capturing acoustic impedance contrasts that reflect geological structures and
lithological boundaries (Amosu et al, 2024 a). Well log analysis represents one of the earliest and
most successful applications of ML in petroleum geophysics. Machine learning algorithms have
been extensively applied to lithology classification, where multiple log curves, gamma ray,
resistivity, density, neutron porosity, sonic, are used as input features to predict rock types at each
depth interval (Zareba et al, 2023). Traditional lithology identification relies on manual
interpretation and empirical cutoffs, which can be subjective and time consuming. Machine
learning approaches automate this process and can identify subtle patterns in log responses that
distinguish lithological units with higher accuracy (Amosu et al, 2021). Well logs provide high
resolution, direct measurements at sparse spatial locations, while seismic data offers extensive 3D
coverage at lower resolution. Machine learning algorithms can effectively bridge this scale gap by
learning relationships between seismic attributes and well derived properties, then extrapolating
these relationships across the entire seismic volume. The integration of these complementary data
types through ML techniques has emerged as a powerful approach for comprehensive reservoir
characterization, enabling predictions of petrophysical properties, lithology classification, and
geomechanical properties across entire field volumes (Amosu et al, 2024 b). Seismic data
interpretation has been revolutionized by ML, particularly in the domains of reservoir
characterization, facies classification, and quantitative property prediction and recently in the use
of seismic attributes.

In this section, seismic attributes were extracted for wells intersecting seismic lines and matched
with log derived measurements of Vsh. Machine learning models were trained after data pre-
processing, to predict Vsh properties from seismic attributes. Model performance was evaluated

 50

using R² and RMSE. Additional validation was implemented including blind well tests, residual
analysis, and Shapley feature importance evaluation. The trained model was then applied to 2D
seismic lines to generate spatially continuous Vsh predictions. This workflow integrates
reprocessed seismic data, conditioned well logs, attribute analysis, and ML to enable reliable basin
wide estimation of shale volume, supporting reservoir characterization, facies analysis, carbon
sequestration, and other basin wide geological studies.

4.5.2 Seismic Attributes

A seismic attribute is any measure of seismic data that helps visually enhance or quantify features
of interpretation interest. Seismic attributes are derived quantities that highlight specific
characteristics of seismic waveforms, such as amplitude, frequency, phase, and coherence and
often serve as input features for ML models. These attributes capture information about subsurface
geology, including lithology, fluid content, porosity, and structural features, but their interpretation
through traditional methods can be ambiguous and requires extensive expertise. In the most
general sense, seismic attributes encompass all quantities derived from seismic data. Seismic
attributes are any quantity derived from seismic data to enhance information or interpretation that
is not directly visible in the traditional seismic image, leading to a better interpretation of the data
(Barnes, 2016). Seismic attributes are one of the most widely used means of detecting faults; they
provide analytical and quantitative measurements of data, thereby improving the analysis of
information obtained from seismic data. In general, three common categories of attributes are:
amplitude attributes, horizon (time) and interval attributes, and frequency attributes.

The development and application of seismic attributes have significantly advanced the field of
reservoir characterization and hydrocarbon exploration. By translating complex seismic
waveforms into interpretable geological indicators, attributes facilitate the delineation of structural
and stratigraphic features, such as faults, fractures, channels, and depositional facies (Chopra &
Marfurt, 2005). Furthermore, certain amplitude and frequency related attributes are sensitive to
variations in lithology, porosity, and fluid content, providing valuable indirect information about
reservoir quality and fluid distribution.

Seismic attributes can be broadly categorized into several classes, including instantaneous
attributes (e.g., amplitude, phase, frequency), geometric attributes (e.g., dip, curvature, coherence),
and higher-order or multivariate attributes (e.g., spectral decomposition, principal component
analysis) (Chopra & Marfurt, 2007). The integration of these attributes, often through advanced
visualization, clustering, and ML techniques, enhances seismic interpretation by enabling more
accurate mapping of reservoir heterogeneity and continuity. The systematic application of seismic
attributes, in conjunction with geological and petrophysical data, contributes to a more robust
understanding of subsurface architecture and reduces uncertainty in exploration and development
decision-making.

4.5.3 Methodology

Neural networks have been widely applied to predict reservoir properties directly from seismic
attributes, bypassing the need for intermediate rock physics models (Sitouah, 2009). By training
on well control points where both seismic attributes and measured reservoir properties are
available, ML models learn the complex, spatially varying relationships between seismic response
and subsurface characteristics. These trained models can then be applied to the entire seismic
lines/volume to generate continuous property distributions, such as porosity, lithology (Ivlev,
2023) and geomechanical properties (Amosu et al, 2024 b).

Several supervised ML regression models were implemented and comparatively evaluated,
including a Bagged Tree Ensemble (bootstrap aggregating of decision trees), a Fine Decision Tree,
a Fine Gaussian Support Vector Machine (SVM), and a Wide Neural Network. Model performance
was assessed using the R² and other statistics including mean absolute error (MAE), mean squared
error (MSE), and RMSE. Among the tested approaches, the Bagged Tree Ensemble achieved the
highest predictive accuracy (Table 4.5.2), indicating that the model explains approximately 92%
of the variance in the target variable. The superior performance of the ensemble method can be
attributed to variance reduction through bootstrap aggregation, improved robustness to overfitting
relative to a single decision tree, and its ability to model complex nonlinear relationships without
requiring extensive hyperparameter tuning.

A Bagged Tree Ensemble is a ML technique that enhances the accuracy and stability of decision
trees by combining multiple trees trained on different subsets of the data. The process begins by
creating several bootstrap samples from the original dataset, which are random samples taken with
replacement (Figure 4.5.1). Each of these samples is used to train a separate decision tree
independently. Once all trees are trained, their predictions are combined to make a final decision:
for regression tasks, the predictions are averaged, while for classification tasks, the final output is

 53

determined by majority voting. This ensemble approach, known as bagging, helps reduce the
variance of the model, making it much less prone to overfitting compared to a single decision tree,
while still retaining the interpretability and flexibility of tree-based models.

To facilitate multiline analysis and enable volumetric attribute computation, the 2D seismic lines

computed from both the individual 2D sections and the pseudo 3D volume to capture amplitude
variations, frequency content, phase characteristics, and geometric discontinuities associated with
lithologic and stratigraphic heterogeneity.

All seismic and well datasets were integrated within a unified spatial reference framework to
ensure geometric and positional consistency (Figure 4.5.3). Coordinate transformations were

Well logs underwent rigorous quality control procedures, including removal of spurious spikes,
correction for borehole environmental effects, normalization where required, and depth matching
across logging runs. Particular attention was given to ensuring internal log consistency before their
use in quantitative modeling.

comprised multiple predictor variables (seismic attributes) and the continuous response variable,

implemented to improve model robustness and computational efficiency. Volume of shale is
selected as a base predictor parameter because it is a critical petrophysical parameter that directly

reflects the proportion of fine-grained, clay-rich material within a reservoir. Volume of shale can
be readily calculated in many wells from gamma-ray logs. It also strongly influences other
reservoir properties such as porosity, permeability, and fluid saturation. Because of these
relationships, Vsh serves as a valuable predictor for a range of reservoir characteristics, enabling
more accurate modeling of reservoir quality.

The compiled dataset was partitioned into training and validation subsets to enable objective
performance evaluation. A stratified sampling approach was applied to preserve the statistical
distribution of Vsh values across both subsets. Multiple ML algorithms were implemented to
model the nonlinear relationship between seismic attributes and Vsh. These included decision
trees, ensemble methods, and other supervised learning techniques. Hyperparameter tuning was
performed using cross-validation within the training dataset to optimize model performance and
mitigate overfitting. Among the tested algorithms, the Bagged Tree Ensemble model demonstrated
superior predictive capability. Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating) improves model stability and
reduces variance by training multiple decision trees on bootstrapped subsets of the data and
aggregating their predictions. This ensemble approach effectively captured nonlinear relationships
and complex attribute interactions while maintaining robustness against noise in the predictor
variables. The selected model was subsequently applied to the 2D lines to generate spatially
continuous predictions of Vsh.

Model performance was quantitatively evaluated using statistical metrics, including R² and RMSE,

how well the predicted Vsh values matched observed log-derived values. The root mean square

insight into absolute predictive accuracy. Additional validation included visual comparison of
predicted versus observed Vsh curves at blind wells. The prediction of Vsh from seismic attributes
demonstrated excellent agreement with the measured data. Using an 80-20 training-test split, the
predictive model achieved an R² value of 0.92 on the test set, indicating that 92% of the variance

effectiveness of the model in capturing the underlying relationship between seismic response and

4.6 Resource Assessment

4.6.1 Resource Assessment Introduction
The San Juan Basin represents an exceptionally favorable geological province for large-scale
carbon dioxide sequestration operations due to its distinctive structural configuration, extensive
areal distribution, and well-characterized tectonic framework. This asymmetric, northwest-
trending structural depression encompasses approximately 7,500 square miles across northwestern
New Mexico and southwestern Colorado. The basin's structural configuration is dominated by
remarkably simple, broad anticlinal and synclinal features characterized by extremely low-angle
dips. This structural simplicity is particularly advantageous for CO2 storage applications, as it
minimizes the risk of compartmentalization and facilitates predictable plume migration modeling.
Critically, the basin exhibits an absence of Quaternary-age faulting or seismically active fault
systems within the proposed storage complex, thereby substantially reducing geomechanical risks
associated with caprock integrity breach, induced seismicity, or preferential migration pathways
that could compromise containment security.

The Middle Jurassic Entrada Sandstone represents the primary target reservoir interval for CO2
sequestration operations, exhibiting exceptional reservoir quality characteristics that are critical

 62

for high-injectivity, high-capacity storage operations. This eolian-dominated sandstone
succession, deposited in an extensive erg (sand sea) environment, demonstrates remarkably
favorable petrophysical properties. Within the SJB, the Entrada deposits range from 100 to 275 ft
thick and consist of siltstones to medium/coarse-grained sandstones that are planar to trough cross-
bedded. The composition of the sandstones are mostly quartz and feldspars grains with scattered
rock fragments. Laminae of heavy minerals help define the cross-bedding within the dune surfaces.
Typically, the lower half of the Entrada Sandstone is finer grained, more cemented, and poorly
sorted than the upper section. Diagenesis of these sandstones play an important role in porosity
and permeability development, beyond simple compaction. Porosity and permeability are much
higher in the upper Entrada Sandstone throughout the basin because of feldspar diagenesis and
relative lack of cementation. Both core analyses as well as petrophysical analysis of porosity logs
demonstrate this conclusion. Within the upper Entrada, porosity ranges upward to approximately
20%, and the permeability ranges from 10 to over 500 mD. The lower Entrada is less than 10%
porosity and 1 mD permeability. Figure 4.6.1 shows a map of the porosity of the Entrada within
the project boundary.

 63

 Figure 4.6.1. Porosity map of the Entrada.
Deep saline aquifers are one of the principal geological formations suitable for anthropogenic CO₂
sequestration. Deep saline aquifers represent the largest volumetric storage resource globally,
though they exhibit some parameter uncertainty due to limited characterization relative to
hydrocarbon-producing formations (Pereira et al., 2023). Geological CO₂ sequestration in deep
saline formations involves injection at great depths. The depth at which CO₂ attains supercritical
conditions varies with formation specific pressure temperature trajectories. At the right depth,
pressure and temperature conditions induce supercritical or dense phase fluid behavior (Jiao and

 64

Surdam, 2013). Under supercritical conditions, CO₂ density ranges from approximately 50% to
80% of the density of water, exhibiting fluid dynamic behavior analogous to certain crude oils.
This density differential generates buoyant forces that drive upward migration of the supercritical
CO₂ plume, necessitating the presence of high-integrity caprock to impede vertical migration and
ensure long-term subsurface containment (Cuenca et al., 2025). Upon injection into the target
formation, supercritical CO₂ undergoes compression and occupies available pore space by partially
displacing in-situ fluids. In depleted hydrocarbon reservoirs, this displacement enhances oil
recovery while simultaneously creating substantial void volume for CO₂ storage. Prospective
storage volumes are estimated to range from a few percent to in excess of 30% of the bulk rock
volume, contingent upon formation-specific petrophysical properties.

4.6.2 Trapping Mechanisms and Storage Estimation
Physical trapping mechanisms constitute the primary near-term containment strategy in geological
storage assessments (Asante et al., 2021). Structural and stratigraphic trapping, wherein CO₂ is
confined beneath impermeable caprock formations within structural or stratigraphic closures,
represents the principal containment mechanism. Residual trapping, governed by capillary forces
that immobilize CO₂ in small pore throats following plume migration, is incorporated as a key
mechanism in storage efficiency workflows and constitutes a major uncertain input parameter.
Nonetheless, porosity may remain interconnected beneath the caprock seal, permitting lateral CO₂
migration. Under such conditions, supplementary trapping mechanisms are requisite for sustained
CO₂ containment. Geochemical trapping involves reactive interactions between injected CO₂,
formation brine, and reservoir minerals. Solubility (dissolution) trapping occurs when CO₂
dissolves into formation water, forming carbonic acid and increasing brine density, which induces
gravitational sinking and further downward migration into the formation. This dissolution
mechanism is routinely incorporated in long-term storage accounting and dynamic plume-
dissolution modeling. Mineral trapping, or in situ carbonation, involves the reaction of acidified
pore water with reactive carbonate minerals, precipitating stable carbonate minerals over
geological timescales. This mechanism is included in combined capacity and long-term retention
assessments and is considered critical for evaluating sites for permanent immobilization. CO₂
injection elevates reservoir pressure, and in the presence of formation water at low temperatures,
hydrate formation may occur. Hydrate formation can exert dual effects on CO₂ storage: it may
reduce effective storage capacity through flow blockage, or conversely, enhance storage capacity
by immobilizing CO₂ within an ice-like crystalline solid structure. Consequently, a fraction of the
injected CO₂ may remain sequestered over millions of years through these coupled physical and
geochemical mechanisms.

Methodologies for estimating storage capacity vary substantially across different geological
storage types. Capacity estimation for saline aquifers is particularly complex, as up to four distinct
trapping mechanisms may operate simultaneously or sequentially at different rates. In contrast,

 65

capacity estimation for oil or gas reservoirs is comparatively straightforward, benefiting from
extensive subsurface data acquired during exploration and production operations.

used in carbon storage assessment. Because the publication is designed for educators, students,
and the public, it creates a resource that can continue to support classroom discussion and early
exposure to CCUS-related careers after the project period.

Overall, the project’s workforce development contribution was to connect technical carbon
management work with education, career awareness, and regional opportunity. The activities
helped educators and students better understand the skills needed for CCUS and related subsurface

capable of supporting responsible energy development in the Four Corners region.

Chapter 6 - Coordination with other DOE Projects

This project (DE-FE0032369, hereafter referred to as the Hogback project), has collaborated with
other projects to host and disseminate geological data and characterization results in the SJB. Data
and work products from this project were made available to the DOE and other stakeholders in the
SJB and the Four Corners region. The Hogback project collaborated with the following projects:
Four Corners Regional Initiative (DE-FE0032363) to Accelerate CCUS Deployment
(DOE/NETL), The Carbon Utilization and Storage Partnership (CUSP, DE-FE0031837), and the
San Juan Basin CarbonSAFE Phase III project (DE-FE0031890). The San Juan Basin is the focus
of development and implementation of a commercial-scale CO₂ storage hub, centered on the
advancement of CCUS technology through the design and development of permanent geologic
storage facilities capable of supporting the capture and sequestration of carbon dioxide emissions.

The Hogback project emphasizes the collection, integration, and dissemination of subsurface data
and basin scale geological analysis needed to support CCUS activities. Collaboration between
these projects and other projects ensures that the development of CO₂ storage capacity proceeds
with robust geological understanding, effective stakeholder engagement, and transparent data
sharing. This project addresses key knowledge gaps in basin structure, fault distribution, thermal
history, and reservoir characteristics that are essential for evaluating the long-term viability and
safety of geologic CO₂ storage. Through seismic data reprocessing, geological modeling, and
probabilistic resource assessment, this project enhances the regional geological framework within
which the storage facilities envisioned in other projects will operate.

Some areas of coordination and collaboration are as follows:

Data Sharing:
A central point of interaction between the two projects is data sharing and geological
characterization. The San Juan Basin CarbonSAFE and other above mentioned projects generated
significant new geological and operational data through activities such as characterization well
drilling, reservoir testing, and site-specific geological analysis associated with Class VI injection
well permitting and development. These datasets were invaluable for improving regional
geological models and fed into the analytical work being conducted in the Hogback project.
Conversely, the improved seismic interpretations, structural mapping, and basin scale analyses
produced by the Hogback project provide critical context and risk-reduction for the storage
infrastructure being developed in these and future projects. This bidirectional flow of information
ensures that these projects benefit from a continuously improving understanding of the SJB.

Geological Risk Reduction:
Another important aspect of interdependence involves geological risk reduction. Successful long-
term CO₂ storage requires detailed knowledge of subsurface structures, including faults,
stratigraphic traps, reservoir properties, and sealing formations. The Hogback project specifically

 77

addressed these challenges by reprocessing and interpreting seismic datasets to better characterize
the structural framework of the western SJB, including features such as the Hogback monocline.
These efforts directly support projects in the SJB by reducing uncertainty in site selection, injection
well placement, and reservoir performance predictions. Improved understanding of subsurface
conditions also contributes to regulatory compliance, particularly with respect to Environmental
Protection Agency (EPA) Class VI injection well requirements, which demand extensive
demonstration of storage integrity and containment.

Basin History:
Thermal characterization and basin history studies conducted in the Hogback project also provide
valuable information relevant to other projects in the SJB. The thermal regime and tectonic history
of the basin influence reservoir properties such as porosity, permeability, and diagenetic alteration.
These factors determine the storage capacity, injectivity, and long-term stability of CO₂ reservoirs.
By improving knowledge of the basin’s thermal evolution and present-day heat flow, this project
helps refine reservoir models and supports the accurate estimation of storage capacity for the
injection wells proposed in other projects. Such insights are essential for designing injection
strategies that maintain reservoir integrity and avoid adverse pressure buildup.

Data Dissemination:
The projects are also interconnected through their data dissemination objectives. The projects
emphasize the importance of making geological data and research outputs available to
stakeholders, including the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), industry partners, regional CCUS
initiatives, and the broader scientific community. This coordinated data sharing approach ensures
that the results of site-specific investigations can inform regional studies conducted in the Hogback
project, while basin-scale insights from the Hogback project support ongoing engineering and
operational planning on a regional scale.

Public Outreach:
Public outreach and stakeholder engagement represent another area where the projects interact.
Several SJB projects include efforts to raise public awareness about CCUS technology and its role
in reducing anthropogenic carbon emissions while sustaining regional economic activity. The
Hogback project expands on this objective by developing outreach and education programs aimed
at industry stakeholders, local communities, and the general public. These programs emphasize
environmental justice and socially equitable deployment of carbon management technologies. By
combining the technical demonstration aspects of previous projects with the communication and
engagement strategies of the Hogback project, the overall CCUS initiative in the SJB can build
greater public understanding, trust, and acceptance.

In summary, the Hogback project functions as a complementary component of a comprehensive
strategy to deploy carbon capture and storage technologies in the SJB. It provides the geological
characterization, data integration, and stakeholder engagement necessary to support safe and

 78

effective deployment. The continuous exchange of data, shared institutional leadership, and
aligned objectives create a strong collaborative ecosystem between the projects. Together, they
contribute to advancing the technical, economic, and social viability of CCUS in the region,
supporting both emissions reduction goals and the sustainable development of energy
infrastructure in the Four Corners area.
