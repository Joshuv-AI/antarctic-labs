Abstract
For a family ℱ \mathcal{F} , it is called intersecting if F ∩ F ′ ≠ ∅ F\cap F^{\prime}\neq\emptyset for all F , F ′ ∈ ℱ F,F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F} . We use 𝒮 𝒟 ( ℱ ) = { F △ G : F , G ∈ ℱ } \mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})=\{F\triangle G:F,G\in\mathcal{F}\} to denote the family of symmetric differences of ℱ \mathcal{F} . In 2023, Frankl, Kiselev and Kupavskii conjectured that for any intersecting family ℱ ⊆ ( [ n ] k ) \mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k} with n > 10 ​ k n>10k , the inequality | 𝒮 ​ 𝒟 ​ ( ℱ ) | ≤ ∑ ℓ = 0 k − 1 ( n − 1 2 ​ ℓ ) |\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})|\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{2\ell} holds. They further observed that a proof for the range n > 3 ​ k 2 n>3k^{2} could likely be obtained via arguments similar to those in their earlier work, though no detailed derivation was given. In this paper, we establish the conjecture under the conditions n ≥ 100 ​ k ​ ln ⁡ k n\geq 100k\ln k and k ≥ 50 k\geq 50 . We also determine the extremal families, which are precisely a certain class of stars. A concentration inequality plays a central role in the proof.

Introduction

Let
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and let
\binom{[n]}{k}
be the family of all
k
-element subsets of
[n]
.
A family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
is called
intersecting
if
F\cap F^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
for all
F,F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
.
One of the cornerstones of extremal set theory is the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem
[
1
]
, which states that for
n\geq 2k
, any intersecting family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
satisfies

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n-1}{k-1}
, with equality only for
full star

\mathcal{S}_{x}=\{F\in\binom{[n]}{k}:x\in F\}
when
n>2k
. Subfamilies of
\mathcal{S}_{x}
are called
stars
.

Beyond the size, a natural direction is to study other set operations induced by an intersecting family.
For a family
\mathcal{F}
, define its family of
differences
as

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{F})=\{F\setminus F^{\prime}:F,F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}\}
.
Marica and Schönheim
[
12
]
proved the lower bound
|\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{F})|\geq|\mathcal{F}|
.
For intersecting families, Frankl
[
2
]
conjectured an upper bound: for
n>2k
and

\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
intersecting,

|\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{F})|\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{\ell},

with equality only for full stars. He proved this for
n\geq k(k+3)
.
Later, Frankl, Kiselev and Kupavskii
[
4
]
significantly improved the range to

n\geq 50k\ln k
for
k\geq 50
, and showed that the bound fails when
n<4k
via explicit counterexamples.
Analogous problems for intersections
F\cap F^{\prime}
have also been studied extensively; see e.g.
[
3
,
5
]
.

A closely related operation is the
symmetric difference

F\triangle G=(F\cup G)\setminus(F\cap G)
. For a family
\mathcal{F}
, let

\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})=\{F\triangle G:F,G\in\mathcal{F}\}.

For a full star
\mathcal{S}_{x}
, a direct calculation gives

|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{S}_{x})|=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{2\ell}.

In the same work
[
4
]
, Frankl, Kiselev and Kupavskii proposed the following conjecture,
which is the symmetric-difference analogue of the set-difference problem.

Conjecture 1.1

(Frankl, Kiselev, Kupavskii
[
4
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
be an intersecting family with
n>10k
. Then

|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})|\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{2\ell}.

They remarked that, using arguments analogous to those in
[
2
]
, one could likely prove the conjecture for
n>3k^{2}
, though no detailed proof was given. Moreover, they emphasized that even establishing the conjecture
for
n>100k\ln k
would already be very interesting.
In this paper, we prove the conjecture for
n\geq 100k\ln k
and
k\geq 50
,
and show that equality holds only for a certain class of stars.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
be an intersecting family with
n\geq 100k\ln k
and
k\geq 50
. Then

|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})|\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{2\ell},

where equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{x}
and
|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})|=|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{S}_{x})|
for some
x\in[n]
.

Our proof is inspired by the elegant method of Frankl, Kiselev and Kupavskii
[
4
]
for set differences, but its adaptation to the symmetric difference setting is nontrivial and requires new ideas, particularly in the diversity analysis and the application of the concentration inequality.
The proof proceeds in two stages. We first establish the conjecture for the range
n\geq 60k^{3/2}
using the diversity method of
[
4
]
, adapted to the symmetric difference setting. This already improves upon the previously suggested quadratic range
n>3k^{2}
. To extend the result to the logarithmic range
n\geq 100k\ln k
, we refine the analysis of the non-star case: a sharper estimation of the error terms yields an exponential gain that suffices as long as
\frac{n}{k}
is large enough relative to
\ln k
. The concentration step, which is the main technical ingredient, requires only this logarithmic condition.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
collects the necessary notation and tools. In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
1.2
for the range
n\geq 60k^{\frac{3}{2}}
. In Section
4
, we handle the remaining range
100k\ln k\leq n<60k^{\frac{3}{2}}
, thereby completing the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

2
Notation and tools

For a family
\mathcal{G}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
and an integer
\ell
, we write
\mathcal{G}^{(\ell)}=\{G\in\mathcal{G}:|G|=\ell\}
.
For example,

\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})^{(i)}=\{D\in\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F}):|D|=i\}.

For
A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
, we use the standard notation

\mathcal{G}(A,B)=\{G\setminus A:G\in\mathcal{G},\>G\cap B=A\}.

In the case
A=B
or
A=\emptyset
, we use the shorter form
\mathcal{G}(B):=\mathcal{G}(B,B)
and
\mathcal{G}(\overline{B}):=\mathcal{G}(\emptyset,B)
.
If
B=\{x\}
, then we set
\mathcal{G}(x):=\mathcal{G}(\{x\})
and
\mathcal{G}(\overline{x}):=\mathcal{G}(\overline{\{x\}})
.

For a family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
and integers
p\leq k\leq q
, we define the
p
-th level shadow

\Delta^{p}\mathcal{F}=\left\{P\in\binom{[n]}{p}:\exists F\in\mathcal{F},\ P\subseteq F\right\},

and the
q
-th level shade

\nabla^{q}\mathcal{F}=\{Q\in\binom{[n]}{q}:\exists F\in\mathcal{F},\ F\subseteq Q\}.

The classical Kruskal–Katona theorem
[
9
,
7
]
gives a lower bound on the size of the shadow. We will use the following convenient formulation due to Lovász
[
10
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

[
10
]

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
and write
|\mathcal{F}|=\binom{x}{k}
for some real number
x
with
k\leq x\leq n
. Then for any integer
p
with
0\leq p\leq k
,

|\Delta^{p}\mathcal{F}|\geq\binom{x}{p}.

Two families
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
and
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{\ell}
are called
cross intersecting
if
F\cap G\neq\emptyset
for all
F\in\mathcal{F},G\in\mathcal{G}
. Katona
[
8
]
observed that for
n\geq k+\ell
,
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
are cross-intersecting if and only if
\mathcal{F}\cap\Delta^{k}(\mathcal{G}^{c})=\emptyset
, where
\mathcal{G}^{c}=\{[n]\setminus G:G\in\mathcal{G}\}
. Combining this observation with Theorem
2.1
yields the following bound.

Theorem 2.2
.

For
n\geq k+\ell
, if
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
and
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{\ell}
are cross-intersecting and
|\mathcal{F}|\geq\binom{x}{n-k}
for some
n-k\leq x\leq n
, then
|\mathcal{G}|\leq\binom{n}{\ell}-\binom{x}{\ell}
.

Recall that
\mathcal{G}(\overline{H})=\{G\in\mathcal{G}:G\cap H=\emptyset\}
.
The following concentration inequality is crucial for our proof.

Theorem 2.3
.

[
4
]

Fix integers
m,\ell,\ell^{\prime},t
such that
m\geq t\ell+\ell^{\prime}
, fix
a>0
and set
\varepsilon=\dfrac{2a+\sqrt{8\ln 2}}{\sqrt{t}}
. Let
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\binom{[m]}{\ell}
be a family and set
\alpha:=\dfrac{|\mathcal{G}|}{\binom{m}{\ell}}
. Let
H
be distributed uniformly on
\binom{[m]}{\ell^{\prime}}
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{G}(\overline{H})|<(\alpha-\varepsilon)\binom{m-\ell^{\prime}}{\ell}\right]<2e^{-a^{2}/2}.

3
The range
n\geq 60k^{\frac{3}{2}}

3.1
Reduction to two lemmas

For a family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
, its
diversity
is defined as

\gamma(\mathcal{F})=\min_{x\in[n]}|\{F\in\mathcal{F}:x\notin F\}|.

Clearly
\gamma(\mathcal{F})=0
if and only if
\mathcal{F}
is a star. The concept of diversity was introduced by Lemons and Palmer
[
11
]
as a measure of how far an intersecting family deviates from a star. It has since then become a central tool in the study of large intersecting families. For any intersecting family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
, Frankl and Wang
[
6
]
proved that
\gamma(\mathcal{F})\leq\binom{n-3}{k-2}
holds for
n>36k
; this bound is currently the best known.

In our proof, we will not work directly with
\gamma(\mathcal{F})
but with the diversity of its shade. Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\nabla^{2k-1}\mathcal{F}
be the
(2k-1)
-th level shade of
\mathcal{F}
. For
n\geq 2k
, one can check that
\gamma(\mathcal{F})=0
if and only if
\gamma(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})=0
; and if
\gamma(\mathcal{F})\geq 1
, then

\gamma(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\geq\binom{n-k-1}{k-1}.

The following two lemmas are the core of the proof of Theorem
1.2
for
n\geq 60k^{\frac{3}{2}}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
be intersecting. Suppose that
n\geq 60k^{\frac{3}{2}}
,
k\geq 50
and
|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})|\geq\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{2\ell}
. Then

\gamma(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\leq\binom{n-\sqrt{k}-1}{n-2k}.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
be intersecting. Suppose that
n\geq 60k^{\frac{3}{2}}
,
k\geq 50
and
1\leq\gamma(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\leq\binom{n-\sqrt{k}}{n-2k}
. Then

|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})|<\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{2\ell}.

For
n\geq 60k^{\frac{3}{2}}
, Theorem
1.2
follows directly from the two lemmas above.
If
\gamma(\mathcal{F})=0
, then
\mathcal{F}
is a subfamily of a full star and Theorem
1.2
holds. If
\gamma(\mathcal{F})\geq 1
, then
\gamma(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\geq\binom{n-k-1}{k-1}>0
.
Suppose, for contradiction, that
|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})|\geq\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{2\ell}
. Then Lemma
3.1
implies
\gamma(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\leq\binom{n-\sqrt{k}-1}{n-2k}\leq\binom{n-\sqrt{k}}{n-2k}
, contradicting Lemma
3.2
. Thus, Theorem
1.2
holds in the range
n\geq 60k^{\frac{3}{2}}
.

3.2
Proof of Lemma
3.1

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
be intersecting and
|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})|\geq\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{2\ell}.
Denote
C:=\frac{n}{k}
,
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\nabla^{2k-1}\mathcal{F}
.

We first bound
\left|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})^{(2k-2)}\right|
from below
.

For
1\leq i\leq 2k-5
, we have

\frac{\binom{n-1}{i-1}}{\binom{n-1}{i}}=\frac{i}{n-i}\leq\frac{2k}{n-2k}<\frac{1}{2}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k-2}\binom{n-1}{2i-1}<

\displaystyle 2\binom{n-1}{2k-5}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2(2k-2)(2k-3)(2k-4)}{(n-2k+2)(n-2k+3)(n-2k+4)}\binom{n-1}{2k-2}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{16}{(C-2)^{3}}\binom{n-1}{2k-2}.

Since
|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})|\geq\sum\limits_{l=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{2l}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})^{(2k-2)}\right|\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{2i}-\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}\binom{n}{2i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{n-1}{2k-2}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-2}\binom{n-1}{2i-1}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{16}{(C-2)^{3}}\right)\binom{n-1}{2k-2}.

(3.1)

Next we relate
\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})^{(2k-2)}
to
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.

Every symmetric difference of size
2k-2
is contained in some member of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
. Moreover, each member of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
can contain at most
2k-1
such symmetric differences. Therefore,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})^{(2k-2)}|\leq(2k-1)|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|\leq 2k|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|.

(3.2)

For
C\geq 6
, we derive from (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|\geq\frac{3}{8k}\binom{n-1}{2k-2}=\frac{3(2k-1)}{8nk}\binom{n}{2k-1}\geq\frac{1}{2n}\binom{n}{2k-1}.

(3.3)

By (
3.1
), the density of
\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})^{(2k-2)}
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})^{(2k-2)}|}{\binom{n}{2k-2}}

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{16}{(C-2)^{3}}\right)\frac{n-2k+2}{n}

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{16}{(C-2)^{3}}\right)\left(1-\frac{2}{C}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{3}{C}.

We now apply the concentration inequality
.

Apply Theorem
2.3
to
\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})^{(2k-2)}
with
m=n
,
\ell=2k-2
,
\ell^{\prime}=2k-1
,
t=\lfloor\frac{C}{2}\rfloor-1
,
a=\sqrt{2\ln 8n}
,
\varepsilon=\frac{2a+\sqrt{8\ln 2}}{\sqrt{t}}
,
\alpha=\frac{|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})^{(2k-2)}|}{\binom{n}{2k-2}}\geq 1-\frac{3}{C}
.
A direct computation shows

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2\ln 8Ck}+2.4}{\sqrt{\frac{C}{2}-2}}<\frac{2\sqrt{2\ln(480k^{\frac{3}{2}})}+2.4}{\sqrt{30\sqrt{k}-2}}

\displaystyle<2\frac{\sqrt{2\ln(480)+3\ln{k}}}{\sqrt{29\sqrt{k}}}+0.17<0.86<0.9-\frac{3}{C}.

Then
\alpha-\varepsilon>\frac{1}{10}
.
Let
H
be distributed uniformly on
\binom{[n]}{2k-1}
.
By (
3.3
), we have
\mathbb{P}\left[H\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\right]\geq\frac{1}{2n}
.
Moreover, Theorem
2.3
gives

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})^{(2k-2)}\cap\binom{\overline{H}}{2k-2}\right|<\frac{1}{10}\binom{n-2k+1}{2k-2}\right]<2e^{-\frac{\mathstrut{a^{2}}}{2}}=\frac{1}{4n},

where
\overline{H}=[n]\backslash H
.
Therefore, there exists a set
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
such that
\left|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})^{(2k-2)}\cap\binom{\overline{F^{\prime}}}{2k-2}\right|\geq\frac{1}{10}\binom{n-2k+1}{2k-2}
. Without loss of generality, assume
F^{\prime}=[2k-1]
.

Finally, we analyze the structure of symmetric differences disjoint from
F^{\prime}
.

For each
S\in\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})^{(2k-2)}\cap\binom{\overline{F^{\prime}}}{2k-2}
, there exists an element
x\in[n]\backslash S
such that
S\cup\{x\}\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
. Since
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is intersecting, we have
\left(S\cup\{x\}\right)\cap F^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
. Thus,
x\in F^{\prime}
. Split the family
\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})^{(2k-2)}\cap\binom{\overline{F^{\prime}}}{2k-2}
into
\bigcup_{x\in F^{\prime}}\mathcal{E}_{x}
accordingly, where
S\in\mathcal{E}_{x}
implies
\{x\}\cup S\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
. The families
\mathcal{E}_{x}
,
x\in[2k-1]
are pairwise cross intersecting.
Let
m=n-2k+1
,
\ell=2k-2
.

Claim 3.1
.

For some
i\in[2k-1]
, we have
|\mathcal{E}_{i}|>\binom{m}{\ell}-\binom{m-\sqrt{k}}{\ell}
.

Proof.

For each
i\in[2k-1]
, consider the inequality

|\mathcal{E}_{i}|<|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F^{\prime}})^{(2k-2)}\cap\binom{\overline{F^{\prime}}}{2k-2}|-\binom{m-1}{\ell-1}
.
If this inequality holds for all
i\in[2k-1]
,
then for every
i
we have
\sum_{j\neq i}|\mathcal{E}_{j}|>\binom{m-1}{\ell-1}
.
We claim that one can partition
[2k-1]
into two disjoint nonempty subsets
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
such that

\displaystyle\Bigl|\bigcup_{i\in I_{1}}\mathcal{E}_{i}\Bigr|>\binom{m-1}{\ell-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\Bigl|\bigcup_{i\in I_{2}}\mathcal{E}_{i}\Bigr|>\binom{m-1}{\ell-1}.

(3.4)

Indeed, if there exists an index
j
such that
|\mathcal{E}_{j}|>\binom{m-1}{\ell-1}
, we may simply take
I_{1}=\{j\}
and
I_{2}=[2k-1]\backslash\{j\}
.
Otherwise, assume
|\mathcal{E}_{i}|\leq\binom{m-1}{\ell-1}
for all
i
.
Since
\sum_{j\neq i}|\mathcal{E}_{j}|>\binom{m-1}{\ell-1}
for every
i
, we can choose the smallest integer
t\geq 2
such that
\sum_{j=1}^{t}|\mathcal{E}_{j}|>\binom{m-1}{\ell-1}
.
Then
\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}|\mathcal{E}_{j}|\leq\binom{m-1}{\ell-1}
.
Consequently,
\sum_{j=t+1}^{2k-1}|\mathcal{E}_{j}|\geq\sum_{j=1}^{2k-1}|\mathcal{E}_{j}|-2\binom{m-1}{\ell-1}
.
Using the lower bound
\sum_{j=1}^{2k-1}|\mathcal{E}_{j}|=\left|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})^{(2k-2)}\cap\binom{\overline{F^{\prime}}}{2k-2}\right|\geq\frac{1}{10}\binom{n-2k+1}{2k-2}
, we obtain

\sum_{j=t+1}^{2k-1}|\mathcal{E}_{j}|\geq\frac{1}{10}\binom{m}{\ell}-2\binom{m-1}{\ell-1}>\binom{m-1}{\ell-1}.

Thus take
I_{1}=[t]
and
I_{2}=[t+1,2k-1]
satisfies (
3.4
).
Now observe that
\bigcup_{i\in I_{1}}\mathcal{E}_{i}
and
\bigcup_{i\in I_{2}}\mathcal{E}_{i}
are cross intersecting. Condition (
3.4
) contradicts Theorem
2.2
.

Thus, there exists some
i
such that
|\mathcal{E}_{i}|\geq|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F})^{(2k-2)}\cap\binom{\overline{F^{\prime}}}{k-1}|-\binom{m-1}{\ell-1}
.
It suffices to verify that the right-hand side is at least
\binom{m}{\ell}-\binom{m-\sqrt{k}}{\ell}+1
. First, note that

\frac{\ell}{m}=\frac{2k-2}{n-2k+1}\leq\frac{2}{C-2}<\frac{1}{60}
.
It follows that

|\mathcal{E}_{i}|\geq\biggl(\frac{1}{10}-\frac{\ell}{m}\biggr)\binom{m}{\ell}>\frac{1}{12}\binom{m}{\ell}.

It remains to show that
\binom{m-\sqrt{k}}{\ell}>\frac{11}{12}\binom{m}{\ell}
. Observe that

\displaystyle m-\ell-\sqrt{k}+1

\displaystyle=n-2k+1-2k+2-\sqrt{k}+1

\displaystyle>60k\sqrt{k}-5k>50k\sqrt{k}>20\ell\sqrt{k}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{m}{\ell}}{\binom{m-\sqrt{k}}{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{l}\frac{m-i+1}{m-\sqrt{k}-i+1}\leq\left(\frac{m-\ell+1}{m-\sqrt{k}-\ell+1}\right)^{\ell}=\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{k}}{m-\sqrt{k}-\ell+1}\right)^{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\biggl(\frac{\ell\sqrt{k}}{m-\sqrt{k}-\ell+1}\biggr)\leq e^{\frac{1}{20}}<\frac{12}{11}.

This completes the proof of the claim.
∎

Without loss of generality, we assume that the index
i
guaranteed by Claim
3.1
is
1
.

Claim 3.2
.

Let
A\subseteq F^{\prime}
be such that
1\notin A
. Then

|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(A,F^{\prime})|\leq\binom{m-\sqrt{k}}{m-2k+1+|A|}.

Proof.

Note that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(A,F^{\prime})\subseteq\binom{[n]\setminus F^{\prime}}{2k-1-|A|}
. For any
H\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
and any
T\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(A,F^{\prime})
, the sets
H\cup\{1\}
and
T\cup A
belong to
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
. Since
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is intersecting and
1\notin A,T
and
S\subseteq\overline{F^{\prime}}
, we have
H\cap T\neq\emptyset
.
Hence, the families
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(A,F^{\prime})
are cross intersecting. Applying Theorem

together with the bound

∎

Claim 3.3
.

|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\overline{1})|\leq\binom{n-\sqrt{k}-1}{n-2k}.

Proof.

Decomposing
|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\overline{1})|
according to the intersection with

(2)

(3)

(4.4)

Indeed,

\frac{\binom{m-r}{\ell}}{\binom{m}{\ell}}=\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\left(1-\frac{r}{m-i}\right)\geq 1-\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\frac{r}{m-i}\geq 1-\frac{r\ell}{m-\ell+1}.

Since

r\leq\frac{C}{30},\ \ell\leq 2k
and
m-\ell+1=n-4k+4\geq(C-4)k
,
we have

\frac{r\ell}{m-\ell+1}\leq\frac{C}{15(C-4)}<0.07.

Therefore,

M_{0}-\binom{m-r}{\ell}<0.07M_{0},

and (
4.4
) follows from (
4.3
).
We may assume that the index in (
4.4
) is
x=1
. For every
B\subseteq F^{\prime}
with
1\notin B
, recall that

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(B,F^{\prime})=\{T\subseteq[n]\backslash F^{\prime}:T\cup B\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\}.

The families
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(B,F^{\prime})
are cross intersecting. Indeed, if
A\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
T\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(B,F^{\prime})
are disjoint, then
A\cup\{1\}
and
T\cup B
would be disjoint members of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
. Since

|\mathcal{E}_{1}|>\binom{m}{\ell}-\binom{m-r}{\ell},

Theorem
2.2
gives

|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(B,F^{\prime})|\leq\binom{m-r}{m-2k+1+|B|}.

Summing this over all
B\subseteq F^{\prime}\setminus\{1\}
, we obtain

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\overline{1})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{B\subseteq F^{\prime}\setminus\{1\}}\binom{m-r}{m-2k+1+|B|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=0}^{2k-2}\binom{2k-2}{b}\binom{m-r}{m-2k+1+b}

\displaystyle=\binom{n-r-1}{n-2k}.

Consequently,

\gamma(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\leq\binom{n-r-1}{n-2k}.

4.3
Proof of Lemma
4.2

Let
C=\frac{n}{k}
. Suppose that
\gamma(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})=|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\overline{1})|
satisfies

1\leq\gamma(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\leq\binom{n-r}{n-2k}.

Then
|\gamma(\mathcal{F})|\geq 1
, and consequently

\gamma(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})\geq\binom{n-k-1}{k-1}=\binom{n-k-1}{n-2k}
.
Assume that
\gamma({\mathcal{F}^{\prime}})=|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\overline{1})|=\binom{x}{n-2k}
for some
x
with
n-k-1\leq x\leq n-r.

Put

h=n-x.

Then

r\leq h\leq k+1.

Define

\mathcal{G}=\{[2,n]\setminus F:F\in\mathcal{F}(\overline{1})\}.

Also define

\mathcal{G}^{\prime}=\{[2,n]\setminus F^{\prime}:F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\overline{1})\}\subseteq\binom{[2,n]}{n-2k}.

Then

|\mathcal{G}^{\prime}|=\binom{x}{n-2k}.

For
0\leq t\leq k-1
, define

\mathcal{G}^{k+t}=\{[2,n]\setminus T:T\in(\nabla^{k+t}\mathcal{F})(\overline{1})\}.

Thus,
\mathcal{G}^{k+0}=\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{k+k-1}=\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
.
Since
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\nabla^{2k-1}\mathcal{F}
, we have
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}=\Delta^{n-2k}{\mathcal{G}^{k+t}}
for every
0\leq t\leq k-1
.
Assume that
|\mathcal{G}^{k+t}|=\binom{y}{n-1-k-t}
. By Theorem
2.1
, we obtain
|\mathcal{G}^{\prime}|\geq\binom{y}{n-2k}
.
Since
|\mathcal{G}^{\prime}|=\binom{x}{n-2k}
, it follows that
y\leq x
.
Consequently,

|\mathcal{G}^{k+t}|\leq\binom{x}{n-k-t-1}.

Moreover, since
n\geq 4k
and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}=\Delta^{n-2k}{\mathcal{G}^{k+0}}=\Delta^{n-2k}{\mathcal{G}}
, we have
\Delta^{i}{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}=\Delta^{i}{\mathcal{G}}
for
1\leq i\leq 2k
.
By Theorem
2.1
, we have

\Delta_{0}:=|\Delta^{2k-2}{\mathcal{G}}|=|\Delta^{2k-2}{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}|\geq\binom{x}{2k-2}

We shall compare all error terms with
\Delta_{0}
.
For convenience, we write
\mathcal{SD}=\mathcal{SD(F)}
.
Observe that
|\mathcal{SD}|=|\mathcal{SD}(1)|+|\mathcal{SD}(\overline{1})|
.

We first consider
\mathcal{SD}(\overline{1})
.

For the layers below
2k-2
, the trivial bound gives

\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}|\mathcal{SD}(\overline{1})^{(2i)}|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}\binom{n-1}{2i}.

The remaining part
\mathcal{SD}^{(2k-2)}(\overline{1})
may arise from symmetric differences between two sets both in
\mathcal{F}(1)
, or both in
\mathcal{F}(\overline{1})
.

For any
H\in\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F}(1))^{(2k-2)}
, we have
{\{1}\}\cup H\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
. If
H\in\Delta^{2k-2}{\mathcal{G}}
, then there exists
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\overline{1})
such that
H\cap F^{\prime}=\emptyset
. This would imply
({\{1}\}\cup H)\cap F^{\prime}=\emptyset
, contradicting the intersecting property of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.
Hence,
H\notin\Delta^{2k-2}{\mathcal{G}}
.
Consequently,

|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F}(1))^{(2k-2)}|\leq\binom{n-1}{2k-2}-\Delta_{0}.

For any
D\in\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F}(\bar{1}))^{(2k-2)}
, we have
|\mathcal{SD}(\mathcal{F}(\overline{1}))^{(2k-2)}|\leq(2k-1)|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\overline{1})|=(2k-1)|\mathcal{G}^{\prime}|.

It follows that

\frac{|\mathcal{G}^{\prime}|}{\Delta_{0}}\leq\frac{\binom{x}{n-2k}}{\binom{x}{2k-2}}=\frac{\binom{x}{2k-h}}{\binom{x}{2k-2}}.

Here we use the standard convention that
\binom{x}{y}
for real
y
is defined via the Gamma function.
Since
2k-h\leq 2k-2
, we obtain

\frac{\binom{x}{2k-h}}{\binom{x}{2k-2}}=\prod_{s=2k-h+1}^{2k-2}\frac{s}{x-s+1}\leq\left(\frac{2k}{x-2k+3}\right)^{h-2}\leq\left(\frac{3}{C}\right)^{h-2},

where the last inequality follows from
x=n-h\geq n-k-1
and
C>300
.
By Lemma
4.3
(2), we have

(2k-1)|\mathcal{G}^{\prime}|\leq 2k\left(\frac{3}{C}\right)^{h-2}\Delta_{0}<\frac{1}{8}\Delta_{0}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{SD}(\overline{1})|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{2i}-\frac{7}{8}\Delta_{0}.

(4.5)

We next consider
\mathcal{SD}(1)
.

Any set in
\mathcal{SD}(1)
must be the symmetric difference of one set from
\mathcal{F}(1)
and one from
\mathcal{F}(\overline{1})
.
Hence, for any
D\in\mathcal{SD}(1)
, we have
|D|=2k-2j-1
with
1\leq j\leq k-1
.
For any
D\in\mathcal{SD}(1)^{(2k-2j-1)}
, there exist
A\in\mathcal{F}(1)
and
B\in\mathcal{F}(\overline{1})
such that
D=A\triangle B
. Then
|A\cap B|=j
and
|A\cup B|=2k-j-1
. Thus,
D
is contained in some member of
\nabla^{2k-j-1}(\mathcal{F}(\overline{1}))=(\nabla^{2k-j-1}\mathcal{F})(\overline{1})
. Each member of
\nabla^{2k-j-1}(\mathcal{F}(\overline{1}))
may contain at most
\binom{2k-j-1}{j}
such symmetric differences.
Therefore,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{SD}(1)^{(2k-2j-1)}|

\displaystyle\leq\binom{2k-j-1}{j}|\nabla^{2k-j-1}(\mathcal{F}(\overline{1}))|

\displaystyle=\binom{2k-j-1}{j}|\mathcal{G}^{2k-j-1}|

\displaystyle\leq\binom{2k-j-1}{j}\binom{x}{n-2k+j}.

(4.6)

On the other hand, the trivial bound gives

\displaystyle|\mathcal{SD}(1)^{(2k-2j-1)}|\leq\binom{n-1}{2k-2j-1}.

(4.7)

We claim that

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{n-1}{2k-2j-1}}{\binom{x}{2k-2}}\leq e^{\frac{4h}{C}}\left(\frac{3}{C}\right)^{2j-1}.

(4.8)

Indeed,

\frac{\binom{n-1}{2k-2j-1}}{\binom{x}{2k-2}}=\frac{\binom{n-1}{2k-2}}{\binom{x}{2k-2}}\cdot\frac{\binom{n-1}{2k-2j-1}}{\binom{n-1}{2k-2}}.

The first factor satisfies

\frac{\binom{n-1}{2k-2}}{\binom{x}{2k-2}}=\prod_{s=0}^{2k-3}\frac{n-1-s}{x-s}=\prod_{s=0}^{2k-3}\left(1+\frac{h-1}{x-s}\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{(2k-2)h}{x-2k+3}\right)\leq e^{\frac{4h}{C}}.

The second factor satisfies

\frac{\binom{n-1}{2k-2j-1}}{\binom{n-1}{2k-2}}=\prod_{s=0}^{2j-2}\frac{2k-2-s}{n-2k+2+s}\leq\left(\frac{2k}{n-2k}\right)^{2j-1}\leq\left(\frac{3}{C}\right)^{2j-1}.

This proves (
4.8
).

Using
x=n-h
, we have

\binom{x}{n-2k+j}=\binom{x}{x-(n-2k+j)}=\binom{x}{2k-h-j}.

It follows that

\frac{\binom{x}{n-2k+j}}{\binom{x}{2k-2}}=\frac{\binom{x}{2k-h-j}}{\binom{x}{2k-2}}=\prod_{s=2k-h-j+1}^{2k-2}\frac{s}{x-s+1}\leq\left(\frac{2k}{x-2k+3}\right)^{h+j-2}\leq\left(\frac{3}{C}\right)^{h+j-2}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\binom{2k-j-1}{j}\frac{\binom{x}{n-2k+j}}{\binom{x}{2k-2}}\leq\binom{2k}{j}\left(\frac{3}{C}\right)^{h+j-2}.

(4.9)

From (
4.6
)–
4.9
) and Lemma
4.3
(3), we obtain

\displaystyle|\mathcal{SD}(1)|\leq\binom{x}{2k-2}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\min\left\{e^{\frac{4h}{C}}\left(\frac{3}{C}\right)^{2j-1},\binom{2k}{j}\left(\frac{3}{C}\right)^{h+j-2}\right\}<\frac{1}{8}\Delta_{0}.

Combining this with (
4.5
), we get

\displaystyle|\mathcal{SD}|

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{SD}(1)|+|\mathcal{SD}(\overline{1})|

\displaystyle<\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{2i}-\frac{7}{8}\Delta_{0}+\frac{1}{8}\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{2i}-\frac{3}{4}\Delta_{0}<\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{2i}.

This completes the proof of Lemma
4.2
.
