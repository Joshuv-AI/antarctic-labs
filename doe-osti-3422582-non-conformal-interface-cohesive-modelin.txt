Abstract
The accurate simulation of boundary- and interface-dominated problems on complex geometries
remains challenging when boundary- or interface-fitted meshes are difficult to generate, particu-
larly for curved boundaries, polycrystalline microstructures, and dense interface networks. The
Shifted Boundary Method (SBM) alleviates this meshing burden by shifting the enforcement of
boundary conditions from the true boundary to a nearby surrogate boundary and recovering the
effect of the true boundary through geometric correction terms, thereby enabling standard finite
element spaces on non-boundary-fitted meshes. In this report, we develop a general shifted-
boundary and shifted-interface framework within the open-source MOOSE framework. We first
present a general SBM implementation for complex geometries on non-boundary-fitted meshes.
We then adopt the Shifted Interface Method (SIM) for internal interfaces and develop a unified
shifted-interface treatment in which the interface law is enforced on a surrogate interface and
the effect of the true interface is recovered through shifted jumps, fluxes, and tractions. This per-
spective brings scalar thermal-contact and vector-valued cohesive-zone mechanics into a single
framework, the latter realized as the Shifted Cohesive Zone Method (SCZM) and coupled with
history-dependent constitutive models from NEML2. We further extend the MOOSE mesh infras-
tructure to support cohesive-zone calculations on distributed meshes. The framework is verified
and demonstrated through three progressive studies: Poisson’s equation on a smoothed star-
shaped domain, a manufactured thermal-contact problem on a non-interface-fitted mesh, and
a two-dimensional polycrystalline representative volume element combining crystal plasticity
with cohesive grain-boundary interfaces. Across these studies, the shifted formulations repro-
duce boundary- and interface-fitted reference solutions with high fidelity, indicating that the pro-
posed framework provides an accurate and efficient route to boundary- and interface-dominated
simulations on arbitrary geometries without requiring fitted meshes.

1 Introduction
The Shifted Boundary Method (SBM) has emerged as an effective approach for solving partial
differential equations on non-boundary-fitted meshes. Rather than generating a mesh that con-
forms exactly to the true geometry, SBM imposes the boundary conditions on a nearby surrogate
boundary and recovers the effect of the true boundary through geometric correction terms. This
sidesteps the cost of body-fitted meshing, which is attractive for complex geometries, where gen-
erating high-quality boundary-fitted meshes is often expensive, difficult to automate, and fragile.
 SBM has been successfully applied to a wide range of problems, including fluid flow [1, 2, 3],
solid and fracture mechanics [4, 5, 6, 7, 8], moving-interface problems [9, 10], thermal flows [11],
and moving-boundary problems [12, 13]. Several variants of SBM have also been proposed to
improve accuracy, robustness, and flexibility, including O-SBM based on optimal surrogate selec-
tion [14], W-SBM [10], Gap-SBM [15, 16], and GSBM [17]. In addition, octree-based SBM imple-
mentations have shown that the method can be combined with adaptive mesh refinement and
parallel computing for large-scale simulations involving complex geometries [14, 3, 11, 18, 19].
 Despite these advances, a general implementation suitable for practical engineering simu-
lations is still lacking. Existing SBM implementations are often tailored to specific geometries,
physics, or discretization settings, and publicly available open-source implementations remain
limited. To the best of our knowledge, the open-source SBM implementation in MFEM provides
an important reference, but its geometry support is restricted and it is not designed as a general
arbitrary-shape workflow. Practical applications instead require a method that can handle com-
plex geometry descriptions, different element types, and standard multiphysics workflows. This
motivates a general shifted-boundary implementation in MOOSE, so that SBM becomes a reusable
capability within a production finite element framework.
 A second limitation is that most SBM developments have focused on external boundaries,
whereas many engineering applications also involve internal interfaces, such as thermal contact,
imperfect bonding, cohesive fracture, grain boundaries, and material interfaces. The challenge
in these problems is not the domain boundary but the interface law that must be enforced across
an internal surface. Resolving such interfaces with a fitted mesh requires the discretization to
conform to the entire interface network, which sharply complicates meshing, particularly for
polycrystalline microstructures or evolving interfaces.
 The Shifted Interface Method (SIM), originally introduced by Li et al. [9] for embedded inter-
face computations, provides a general strategy for treating interface conditions on non-interface-
fitted meshes: the interface condition is enforced on a nearby surrogate interface, while the effect
of the true interface is recovered through shifted jumps and fluxes. The Shifted Fracture Method
(SFM) can be viewed as a specialization of this idea to fracture and cohesive problems, in which
the fracture or cohesive condition is shifted from the true interface to a nearby surrogate inter-
face [5, 6, 20]. Existing SFM formulations, however, have largely targeted fracture-type problems
and have often been restricted to simplicial meshes such as triangles and tetrahedra, leaving room
for a more general formulation and implementation.
 In this work, we adopt the SIM viewpoint [9] and extend it into a unified treatment of scalar
and vector-valued interface problems, in which the interface condition is enforced on a surrogate
interface and the effect of the true interface is recovered through shifted jumps, fluxes, or trac-

tions. This perspective brings thermal contact and cohesive-zone mechanics into a single frame-
work: thermal contact is a scalar interface problem and cohesive-zone mechanics a vector-valued
one, so the Shifted Cohesive Zone Method (SCZM) can be interpreted as the mechanics special-
ization of SIM. A focused development of the SCZM, including manufactured-solution conver-
gence studies, single-interface traction-separation benchmarks, and two- and three-dimensional
crystal-plasticity applications, is presented in the companion paper [21]; here we instead empha-
size the unified shifted-interface viewpoint and its general implementation in MOOSE.
 Building on this perspective, the goal of this work is a general shifted-boundary and shifted-
interface framework in MOOSE: complex shapes on non-boundary-fitted meshes for boundary
problems, and scalar and vector interface laws on non-interface-fitted meshes for interface prob-
lems. The same shifted-interface idea thus serves both thermal contact and cohesive-zone me-
chanics.
 The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows. First, we develop a general
SBM implementation in MOOSE for complex geometries on non-boundary-fitted meshes. Second,
building on the Shifted Interface Method of Li et al. [9], we develop a unified shifted-interface
formulation in MOOSE that treats both scalar thermal-contact and vector-valued cohesive-zone
problems within a single framework. Third, we extend the MOOSE mesh infrastructure required
for cohesive-zone calculations on distributed meshes. Finally, we verify and demonstrate the
framework through three examples: a Poisson problem on a smoothed star-shaped domain, a
manufactured thermal-contact problem, and a two-dimensional polycrystalline representative
volume element combining crystal plasticity with cohesive grain-boundary interfaces.
 The remainder of this work is organized as follows. Chapter 2 introduces the common no-
tation and presents the shifted formulations for thermal contact and cohesive-zone mechanics.
Chapter 3 describes the MOOSE implementation, including the construction of surrogate domains,
geometric correction data, and distributed-mesh support for cohesive-zone simulations. Chap-
ter 4 presents the numerical studies used to verify and demonstrate the proposed framework.

as an intersection, thereby changing the parity and misclassifying the query point. Perturbing

dimensional configurations without requiring a user-defined geometric tolerance.
 In three dimensions, the crossing count is evaluated using segment–triangle intersection tests
with a small geometric tolerance. A corresponding half-open ownership rule for edges and ver-
tices shared by neighboring TRI3 facets has not yet been implemented. Configurations that re-
main ambiguous are resolved using the additional PCA-based ray directions described above. If
a user-specified ray direction is imposed and an unambiguous classification cannot be obtained,
the implementation reports an error.

with it the O (∥d∥ 2 ) remainder of the first-order Taylor corrections in (2.14) and (2.24), without
unnecessarily refining the entire background domain. The value of such geometry-driven adap-
tivity is well established for flow problems, where octree refinement around immersed boundaries
has been combined with SBM to resolve near-boundary features at a fraction of the cost of uni-
form refinement [18, 3]. For solid mechanics with path-dependent material response, however,
local refinement is far less settled: adapting the mesh requires transferring history-dependent in-
ternal variables, such as plastic strain and slip resistances, between the old and new elements, and
coarsening discards state in regions that may already have deformed plastically. Whether such
transfers preserve the accuracy of the constitutive update remains an active research question, so
the distance-based adaptivity is reported here as a geometric capability, and the crystal-plasticity
results in Chapter 4 are computed on fixed meshes.

3.1.3 Shifted interface kernel

The distance vector and true normal are finally supplied to the shifted interface kernels. At each
surrogate-interface quadrature point, the interface kernel evaluates the element and neighbor
fields, uses the single vector d from the surrogate interface to the true, zero-thickness interface
to shift both traces, and assembles the paired residual contributions for the minus and plus sides.
The true normal defines the local orientation of the interface response. Together with the surro-
gate normal, it also provides the area-correction factor ñ · n used to transfer fluxes or tractions
consistently to the surrogate interface [11, 21], while 𝝉SBM supplies the accompanying directional
correction [11, 21]. The interface constitutive relation is not restricted to the scalar thermal-
contact and vector-valued cohesive laws considered here; users may supply other interface laws
through the same material–kernel data path. Because these contributions use the standard MOOSE
residual and Jacobian assembly paths, the resulting linear or nonlinear system proceeds through
the usual MOOSE solve loop.

and its implementation deliberately mirrors the existing MOOSE Cohesive-Zone Method
(CZM) [22]. In CZM, four objects act in sequence at every quadrature point of an interface-fitted
mesh: an interface material computes the displacement jump in the local interface frame; a
traction-separation law returns the local traction t̂ together with 𝜕𝑡ˆ𝑖 /𝜕 ⟦𝑢⟧

3.2 Distributed-mesh support for BreakMeshByBlock and (S)CZM
The BreakMeshByBlock (BMBB) mesh generator duplicates nodes along subdomain interfaces so
that cohesive-zone models (CZM), thin-layer heat-transfer kernels, and other interface kernels
can act on a pair of elements that share an interface but no longer share nodes.
 Historically, BMBB relied on a number of hacks that prevented it from being followed by
prepare_for_use() and, most importantly, from running on a DistributedMesh. Three obsta-
cles were responsible, and they are of quite different natures: the first is a topological limitation
of libMesh, the second concerns how the duplicated nodes are named, and the third concerns
how much of the mesh each processor can actually see.

  (i) The neighbor relation is lost. Once a node is duplicated, the two elements on either side
 of the interface no longer share anything, so libMesh stops classifying them as neighbors
 and the interface kernels have no element pair to assemble on.
  (ii) The new identifiers are not unique. The identifier of a copy was taken from
 mesh->n_nodes(). On a ReplicatedMesh this is the global node count and the result is
 unique; on a DistributedMesh it is only the locally stored count, so two processors can
 hand the same identifier to two entirely different nodes.
 (iii) Each processor sees only part of the picture. A node owned by rank 𝑝 may be ghosted
 on several other ranks, and each of those ranks sees only some of the elements touching
 that node, hence only some of the blocks that meet there. Ranks working from different
 information reach different conclusions about whether the node should be split at all.

 The three subsections that follow take these obstacles in turn. Section 3.2.1 restores the neigh-
bor relation inside libMesh by registering the two sides of a split interface as a disjoint boundary
pair. Section 3.2.2 replaces the node counter by a closed-form identifier that every rank evalu-
ates to the same value without communicating. Section 3.2.3 makes the ranks agree on which
nodes must be split in the first place, by exchanging the connected-block information along the
interprocessor interface. The corresponding source changes are collected in [24] for libMesh and
in [25] for MOOSE.

3.2.1 Disjoint neighbor support in libMesh
We extend libMesh to support disjoint neighbors by identifying disconnected boundary
pairs [24]. Once the nodes along a subdomain interface have been duplicated, the two elements
sharing that interface no longer share any node, and the standard topological neighbor search
fails to relate them, which is illustrated in Figure 3.9. By recording the pair of disconnected
boundary sides created by the split, the neighbor relation is restored explicitly, so that interface
kernels can still assemble on the element pair and prepare_for_use() can be called after BMBB
has run.
 The recovery reuses the machinery that libMesh already provides for periodic boundary con-
ditions, as sketched in Figure 3.10. A periodic boundary condition registers a pair of sidesets

                                                  18                                      ANL-26/22

(Γ𝑎 , Γ𝑏 ) together with a translation t that maps one onto the other, and a single registration re-
lates every element pair along those two surfaces. BreakMeshByBlock follows exactly the same
pattern: when the interface is split, the two coincident sides of each block pair are assigned to
two distinct sidesets, blockA_blockB and blockB_blockA, and these are registered through

 mesh.add_disjoint_neighbor_boundary_pairs (Γ𝑎 , Γ𝑏 , t = 0) ,

the only difference from the periodic case being that the translation vanishes, because the two sur-
faces are geometrically coincident rather than separated by a period. During find_neighbors(),

rather than geometric, the duplicated nodes and their degrees of freedom remain independent,
so the solution may jump across the interface, while all neighbor_ptr()-based infrastructure,
including interface kernels, sparsity construction, and ghosting, traverses the interface as usual.

logical neighbor to the coupled-element set even though the two elements share no node that

3.2.2 Deterministic node identifiers across processors

Restoring the neighbor relation makes the split mesh usable again, but a second obstacle remains:
every copy of a split node needs an identifier, and every rank storing that copy must indepen-
dently assign the same one. The original implementation used mesh->n_nodes(), treating the
identifier space as a dense counter to which new nodes are appended.
 This works on a ReplicatedMesh, where every rank stores and traverses the entire mesh in
the same order, so the counter advances consistently. It fails on a DistributedMesh: n_nodes()
is only a rank-local count, global identifiers may be sparse, and ranks create node copies inde-
pendently and in different orders. As a result, different ranks can assign the same identifier to
different nodes and corrupt the mesh.

tributes. Let 𝑁 max be the exclusive upper bound on the original node identifiers returned by

4 Numerical results
We assess the proposed shifted-boundary implementation through three progressive studies. The
first solves Poisson’s equation on a smoothed star-shaped domain to verify the baseline SBM
machinery on a complex curved boundary. The second applies the shifted interface method (SIM)

4.3 SCZM for crystal plasticity on a 2D polycrystalline RVE
As a final and more demanding application, we apply SCZM to a two-dimensional polycrystalline
RVE that couples history-dependent crystal plasticity in the bulk with cohesive decohesion along
the grain-boundary network. This example illustrates the cohesive-zone capability of the present
framework; a broader suite of SCZM studies, including convergence verification, single-interface
traction-separation tests, and three-dimensional polycrystalline RVEs, is reported in the compan-
ion paper [21]. The microstructure consists of five grains, and an interface-fitted discretization
serves as the reference solution. The bulk response is supplied by the NEML2 [26] constitutive
library through a finite-strain single-crystal viscoplasticity model that combines isotropic elas-
ticity (𝐸 = 103 , 𝜈 = 0.3) with rate-dependent, slip-based plasticity and Voce hardening; the reader
is referred to [27] for the complete constitutive description. The internal variables, namely the
slip resistances and the lattice orientation, are advanced with an implicit backward-Euler integra-
tion that keeps the constitutive update stable throughout the loading history. The RVE occupies
[−0.6, 0.6] × [−0.4, 0.4] and is driven by 𝑢𝑥 = 0 along the left edge, 𝑢𝑦 = 0 along the bottom
edge, and a ramped displacement 𝑢𝑥 = 10−2𝑡 on the right edge, with the analysis carried out over
𝑡 ∈ [0, 100] in increments of Δ𝑡 = 1. Decohesion along the grain boundaries follows a bilinear
mixed-mode traction-separation law [28] with parameters 𝐾 = 50, 𝐺 𝐼𝑐 = 1, 𝐺 𝐼𝐼𝑐 = 3, 𝑁 = 3, 𝑆 = 5,
𝜂 = 2.2, and 𝜇 = 0.
 The interface-fitted reference is computed on a conforming quadrilateral mesh of character-
istic size ℎ Ω ≈ 2.92 × 10−3 (Figure 4.9). To probe convergence toward this reference, SCZM is
run on a family of non-interface-fitted quadrilateral background meshes with uniform spacing
ℎ = 0.2/2lvl for lvl = 0, . . . , 6.

5 Conclusions
The challenges of generating boundary- and interface-fitted meshes for complex geometries,
curved boundaries, and dense interface networks motivate computational frameworks that can
enforce boundary and interface conditions on non-fitted discretizations. This report addresses
these challenges by developing a general shifted-boundary and shifted-interface framework
within the open-source MOOSE framework. By shifting the enforcement of boundary and
interface conditions from the true boundary or interface to a nearby surrogate and recovering
their effect through geometric correction terms, the framework enables the use of standard finite
element spaces while avoiding the meshing burden associated with fitted discretizations. The
main contributions of this report are summarized as follows:

 • General SBM implementation in MOOSE: We develop a general shifted-boundary imple-
 mentation for complex geometries on non-boundary-fitted meshes, making SBM a reusable
 capability within a production finite element framework rather than a method tailored to
 specific geometries, physics, or discretizations.
 • Unified shifted-interface formulation: Building on the Shifted Interface Method (SIM)
 of Li et al. [9], we develop a unified shifted-interface treatment in MOOSE in which the
 interface law is enforced on a surrogate interface and the effect of the true interface is
 recovered through shifted jumps, fluxes, and tractions. This perspective treats the Shifted
 Fracture Method as a specialization and brings scalar and vector-valued interface problems
 into a single framework.
 • Distributed-mesh infrastructure: We extend the MOOSE mesh infrastructure, including
 BreakMeshByBlock and the cohesive zone model, to support cohesive-zone calculations on
 distributed meshes, enabling large-scale interface simulations.

 Taken together, these contributions place scalar and vector-valued problems on common in-
frastructure: the geometric machinery that identifies the surrogate boundary and evaluates dis-
tance vectors is the same machinery that drives the shifted interface kernels, and cohesive-zone
calculations built on it carry over from replicated to distributed meshes. The numerical studies
exercise this infrastructure across a widening range of physics: a single scalar field on a curved
domain (Section 4.1), thermal contact across a non-interface-fitted mesh (Section 4.2), and crys-
tal plasticity with cohesive grain boundaries (Section 4.3). In each case the shifted formulations
recover the fitted reference solutions in field accuracy, reaction forces, and damage evolution.
 Several avenues remain for future work:

 • Incorporating ideas from the Gap-SBM framework to achieve higher-order consistency by
 accounting for the geometric gap between the surrogate and true boundaries or interfaces.
 • Developing FFT-accelerated solvers. Because both SBM and SIM, collectively denoted as
 SB[I]M, retain a uniform rectangular or Cartesian grid while accurately treating arbitrar-
 ily shaped boundaries and interfaces, their structured discretizations are particularly well
 suited to FFT-based acceleration.

ANL-26/22                                     35

 • Coupling SB[I]M with adaptive or octree-based discretizations to improve computational
 efficiency for large-scale three-dimensional simulations.
 • Extending SIM to additional multiphysics interface problems, such as thermomechanical
 coupling, as well as to evolving interfaces.

 These developments would broaden the applicability of the proposed framework and further
establish shifted methods as general-purpose tools for boundary- and interface-dominated sim-
ulations.
