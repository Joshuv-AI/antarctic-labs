Overview and Background
This action plan template and guide have been designed to support light-duty or medium-duty municipal fleet
managers if they plan to incorporate electric vehicles into their fleets. The resource is an adaptation of content
created by the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR), with support from World Resources Institute (WRI), for
participants in the U.S. Department of Energy’s Energy to Communities peer-learning cohorts offering. Fifteen
participating municipalities joined monthly cohort workshops from July through December 2024 to learn and
plan for their own fleet’s adoption of electric vehicles. Clean Cities and Communities coalitions supported
participating municipalities throughout the cohort series by conducting fleet analysis and planning activities on
their behalf. This template is an adapted version of cohort activities.

Cohort workshops in this series included the following:

 • Workshop 1 - Planning for electric vehicles: Prepared participants to participate in the cohort and to
 gather data needed to start the planning process.
 • Workshop 2 - Understanding vehicle and charging needs: Provided approaches to vehicle replacement
 planning and phasing and evaluation of charging needs for vehicles and duty cycles.
 • Workshop 3 - Building your team and working with your electric utility: Outlined key internal and
 external stakeholders, including utilities, and when and how to engage them.
 • Workshop 4 - Assessing, upgrading, and future-proofing sites: Shared key considerations for siting
 chargers and strategies to minimize upgrades such as offsite charging and future-proofed infrastructure.
 • Workshop 5 - Calculating and managing costs: This workshop total cost of ownership and strategies for
 managing charging costs. It included funding and financing and procurement best practices.
 • Workshop 6 - Training, operations, and maintenance: This workshop focused on operations and
 maintenance considerations and workforce training needs related to vehicles and chargers.

How to Use This Guide
Municipal fleet managers can modify this template or use it as a starting place for their own planning process.
There are six sections, each of which requires information gathering and analysis. Fleet managers can assemble
a core project team of internal stakeholders to lead and facilitate the planning process. Please refer to Section 2
of this document for insights on how to develop this team.

This document can be used as a full plan outline, or sections can be pulled out to meet the fleet’s specific needs.
Complete each section in the plan template using instructions and template language as a guide. Remove both
instructions and template language before finalizing a plan document.

 • Instructions for each section are written in plain black text.
 • Template language or section outlines are written in red italicized text.

Executive Summary
Instructions to complete this section: Writing the Executive Summary will be the final step of completing your
plan once all other sections are complete. The Executive Summary should contain:

 • Your municipality’s purpose for incorporating electric vehicles (EV)
 • Any goals and targets
 • A brief description of the current fleet and suggested high-level approach to integration of those
 vehicles, including graphics
 • Key information on how your staff uses and stores vehicles and any associated implications for this plan
 • A brief description of challenges or barriers
 • An overview of recommended actions, which could include:
 o Municipality adopts fleet goals and targets
 o Institute a fleet policy and procurement plan for EVs that prioritizes procurement of battery EVs
 and right-sizing current fleet
 o Establish a committee to support infrastructure coordination
 o Add technology to vehicles to aid in route optimization, best use of vehicles, and data collection
 o Conduct an EV suitability assessment using fleet telematics and data analysis
 o Leverage utility, state, and federal funding and tax credits to reduce costs
 o Optimize charger procurement and installation by developing and staffing a cross-organizational
 charging infrastructure strategy.

Glossary
Instructions to complete this section: Provide a glossary of key definitions. A starting list is below. Users should
review the final plan and determine if certain definitions should be removed or added.

Example adapted from State of Colorado Zero-Emission Vehicle Transition Plan template:

alternative fuel vehicle (AFV) A vehicle that runs on a fuel other than traditional petroleum fuel such as
 gasoline or diesel

demand charge rate Additional fees that utilities charge customers for maintaining a constant
 supply of electricity

direct current fast charger (DCFC) Fastest electric vehicle charger. Charges a vehicle from as low as 20
 kilowatts to as high as 250 kilowatts or more.

electric utility A corporation, agency, authority, or other instrumentality that delivers
 electricity to the public. Included are investor-owned electric utilities,
 municipal and state utilities, federal electric utilities, and rural electric
 cooperatives.

electric vehicle (EV) A vehicle that can be powered by an electric motor that draws electricity
 from a battery and is capable of being charged from an external source

electric vehicle supply equipment Equipment that delivers electricity from a source to charge a plug-in electric
(EVSE) vehicle; also known as an electric vehicle charging station

fuel Includes gas, diesel, compressed natural gas, electricity, or other sources of
 energy for vehicle operation

Level 1 electric vehicle charger Slowest electric vehicle charger, delivers up to 2.5 kilowatts

Level 2 electric vehicle charger Medium electric vehicle charger, delivers up to 19.2 kilowatts

net present value (NPV) A method of calculating a return on investment. NPV is the sum of the
 present value of all cash inflows minus the present value of all cash outflows.

total cost of ownership (TCO) An estimate of expenses associated with the entire life cycle of a piece of
 equipment, including purchasing, maintenance, and decommissioning

zero-emission vehicle (ZEV) A vehicle that produces zero exhaust or emission of any criteria pollutant
 under all possible operational modes and conditions

1. Goals and Timeline
Effective plans begin by clearly laying out goals and milestones and their authority. Describe any existing goals
and timelines for electric vehicle (EV) integration.

Gather key stakeholders for a goal-setting conversation. This may include representatives from different
municipal departments, elected officials, transit authorities, schools, and community organizations. See Section
2 (Stakeholder Engagement) for additional considerations.

 1. As a team, answer the following questions:
 a. Does your municipality have an established goal or mandate for fleet electrification? What is the
 timeline? For example:
 ■ By 2030, XX% of the vehicles in the government fleet shall be zero-emission vehicles
 with at least XX% of the vehicles in fleet low-or-zero-emission vehicles.
 ■ By 2040, XX% of the vehicles in the government fleet shall be zero-emission vehicles
 with at least XX% of the vehicles in fleet low-or-zero-emission vehicles.
 ■ By 2050, XX% of the vehicles in the government fleet shall be zero-emission vehicles.
 b. Who set the goal or mandate? Is it legally binding?
 c. If there is not already a goal, is there support for developing a goal for fleet electrification? What
 might that goal include? Who would need to endorse this goal?
 d. How does fleet electrification connect to broader municipal goals?
 e. What is the purpose for electrifying your fleet (e.g., cost or fuel savings, career development for
 technicians, improved air quality, etc.)?
 f. What progress have you made to date?
 g. Are there other fleets within your local government, school district, or region who are
 electrifying their fleets? How might you coordinate or support each other’s efforts?
 2. Summarize your findings and record them below. Delete instructions from draft when complete.

2. Stakeholder Engagement
Municipal fleet electrification requires the collaboration and coordination of a variety of internal and external
stakeholders. It is important to map and understand stakeholder interests, influence, and potential impact on
fleet electrification efforts. This process can help you formulate strategies for effective communication,
collaboration, overcoming concerns, and gathering support.

 1. Gather your internal project team. This should include the municipal fleet manager, energy manager,
 sustainability coordinator, and facility infrastructure manager.
 2. As a team, fully populate the Stakeholder Mapping Template (columns A-K) for all current and potential
 project stakeholders.
 3. Develop strategies to build buy-in:
 a. Identify all champions. Describe their role and contributions to this effort. Discuss what
 motivates them and how you can build on their support.
 b. Identify those with whom you need to build buy-in. Discuss possible reasons for their reluctance
 or hesitation. How can you confirm and then address or overcome these concerns, such as
 leveraging messengers or partners? Discuss strategies to encourage and increase buy-in.
 c. Discuss engagement strategies to build long-term institutional support for this effort. Strategies
 to consider:
 i. Targeted briefings for elected officials and key leadership
 ii. Education and listening sessions with drivers and maintenance staff
 iii. Project advisory groups/steering committees that include key departments and external
 stakeholders
 d. Discuss engagement strategies to build long-term public support for this effort. Strategies to
 consider:
 i. Publish fleet electrification goals, plan, and progress reports on website
 ii. Include community groups in fleet electrification planning or working groups
 iii. Collaborate and share knowledge with other fleets that are electrifying
 iv. Establish partnerships around charging, including shared charging opportunities
 v. Communicate facilities upgrades and positive impacts
 4. Develop or expand the relationship with your utility:
 a. Identify the names of the electric utility(s) that provide power to your municipality. The
 Department of Energy’s U-Finder can help you identify and connect with the right person at
 your utility.
 i. Does your organization have an existing point of contact within the electric utility? This
 may be someone within your facilities team.
 ii. Is your electric utility contact the right person to support electrification efforts? Is there
 an EV champion or dedicated staff working on transportation electrification at the
 electric utility that might want to engage?
 iii. What is your current relationship with the electric utility? Is there history to be aware
 of, either positive or negative?
 iv. What departments do you need to involve in different phases of your project?
 b. Describe how the electric utility’s rate structure and demand charges may impact the operation
 of EVs. Describe your municipality’s strategy to minimize the impact.
 c. Describe how the long-term goals of your municipality and the electric utility align or conflict.
 How might this impact your transition?

 9

 d. If you have not yet reached out to your electric utility, set up a meeting with them to discuss
 your fleet electrification plans. Work with your internal utility lead, if necessary.
 5. Type up a summary and record below. Delete instructions from draft when complete.

Analyze current fleet data to determine which vehicles are most suitable for near-term, mid-term, and long-
term electrification. Consider your vehicle replacement process and budget, vehicle availability and cost,
electricity and fuel prices, charging needs, vehicle miles traveled, and emissions.

With your project team, discuss your vehicle replacement process.

 1. What is your current process and timing for replacing vehicles? What opportunities and challenges could
 this process create for fleet electrification?
 2. How might this process be changed to better enable fleet electrification?
 a. Who needs to be involved to change this process?
 b. What steps are needed to change this process?
 3. Do you have a low-emissions vehicle procurement policy?
 a. If yes, is it working well? Why or why not?
 i. Are there opportunities for improvement?
 b. If no, is there interest in developing a policy? What would that take?
 4. Summarize any recommendations and next steps.

4. Charging Infrastructure and Facilities Upgrades
Once you have compiled fleet data and started to prioritize vehicles for electrification, you can analyze charging
needs for those vehicles. This requires coordination with your facilities team and electric utility.

Assess charging needs for your fleet replacement plan.

 1. Review the top vehicles to replace based on your Fleet Landscape Analysis in Section 3. For those
 prioritized for near-term electrification, identify the specific facility names and locations where the
 vehicles are stored.
 2. For each facility, calculate the number of chargers needed for the vehicles prioritized for near-term
 electrification using the Facility Charging Needs Template:
 a. Use one of the Charging Needs tabs of the Facility Charging Needs Template to identify the
 maximum weekly mileage and the range of replacement EV of each priority vehicle to
 understand charging needs using telematics data.
 1. If you don’t have telematics data, divide annual vehicle miles traveled by 52 (or
 the number of weeks your vehicle operates per year) to get weekly mileage.
 Insert this into the template.
 ii. Identify the replacement vehicle’s range and insert it into the template.
 b. Use the same tab to calculate the estimated number of times the vehicle will need to be
 charged per week. The worksheet calculates this by dividing the weekly mileage by the total
 range of the vehicle.
 i. Example: Your vehicle travels 100 miles a week and has a range of 200. It needs 0.5
 charges a week to meet its basic function. This vehicle would not need to charge every
 day and could share a charger with another vehicle(s).
 ii. You can add a range buffer if desired by decreasing the range. For example, you could
 reduce the max range to 80% as a buffer for cold weather and battery depreciation. For
 example, you could take a max range of 250 miles and instead use 200 miles for your
 calculations.
 c. Add the number of charges needed per week per facility and identify your desired number and
 types (L2 or DCFC) of chargers.
 3. Discuss whether each facility can accommodate near-term charging needs for vehicles that are
 prioritized for electrification. Insert these details into the Facility Data tab of the Facility Charging Needs
 Template:
 a. Is the site owned or leased?
 b. Does the site have electrical capacity to install chargers?
 c. Would the parking layout or other operations need to be adapted to accommodate chargers?
 d. Is the facility located in or nearby a residential area? Does that residential area experience high
 pollution or noise levels? Could there be additional benefits to electrification in this area?
 4. Discuss other means of charging:
 a. Are there public chargers you could potentially use? Use the AFDC Alternative Fueling Station
 Locator to find nearby EV charging stations.
 b. Could chargers be installed at other facilities where the vehicles visit regularly (e.g., city hall,
 police stations, municipal buildings, garages, shops)?
 c. Are there other fleets or partners (e.g., a school district that is also electrifying) that you could
 coordinate with to lower the number of chargers needed?
 5. Determine the estimated number of chargers needed per facility:

 13

 a. Discuss with your team whether L2 chargers meet your needs or if DCFC would be needed,
 based on the analysis and discussions in the previous two steps.
 b. Calculate the number of L2 and DCFC needed per facility, based on the total charges needed per
 week across all vehicles at a particular location and the availability of other charging.

Host a discussion with the facilities team about electrification goals and potential sites.

 1. Share information on municipality’s goals and plans for fleet electrification:
 a. The scale of the electrification project will influence the facility requirements. Is your goal partial
 fleet electrification? Full fleet electrification? Other objectives, such as vehicle-to-grid or vehicle-
 to-building integration, may also play a role in determining the scale of the project. Share any
 key dates or deadlines.
 2. Discuss where vehicles are currently parked and implications for charging:
 a. Are there sites that are well-suited for near-term electrification?
 b. Are there any special considerations for certain locations? For example, are certain sites leased,
 and thus more challenging to update?
 c. Where are these facilities located? Are they within residential neighborhoods? What is the
 status of relationships between these facilities and residents?
 d. Is there a need to reconsider the vehicle parking strategy and approach to support fleet
 electrification?
 e. How might you phase facility upgrades over time to manage costs?
 3. Review electricity bill and discuss how facility electricity bills are handled:
 a. How might fleet electrification impact bills?
 b. Are there opportunities to reduce electricity demand at sites to accommodate future increases
 in electricity use due to charging? For example, has facility lighting been updated and resulted in
 additional capacity on a panel? If not, could you consider upgrading lighting to free up capacity?
 Could solar or battery storage help offset increases in electricity usage?
 c. Are there site electrical capacity constraints?
 d. Are there demand charges? How will you avoid incurring demand charges?
 e. How will you track and manage charging costs and who will be responsible?

Conduct site assessments using NLR’s EVI-LOCATE (Electric Vehicle Infrastructure - Locally Optimized Charging
Assessment Tool and Estimator) tool.

 1. Sign up for an EVI-LOCATE account at https://evi-locate.nlr.gov/
 2. Gather data that will be needed for the assessment (if you have not already gathered within the Fleet
 Characteristics Template). Data could include:
 a. Site addresses and boundaries for each site that you want to analyze
 b. Number of chargers to install at each site and information on charger specifications, which can
 include charger level, mount type, number of ports, whether they will be networked, and any
 available manufacturer details
 c. Electric utility details and assets, including transformers and service panels:
 i. Local electric utility name
 ii. If assessing or including transformer costs:
 1. Location, or preferred location, of existing or new transformers
 2. Amperage
 3. Power factor (conservative defaults included in EVI-LOCATE)
 4. Loading limit (conservative defaults included in EVI-LOCATE)

 14

 5. Secondary side voltage (located on transformer case)
 6. Power rating (located on transformer case)
 7. Existing peak power load (found within meter data).
 iii. If assessing or including service panel costs:
 1. Location, or preferred location, of current or new service panels
 2. Service voltage rating
 3. Physical capacity - Number of unused circuit breaker spaces
 a. L1 charger requires one breaker
 b. L2 charger requires two vertically adjacent breakers
 c. DCFC charger requires three breakers on three different phases of 480V
 power.
 4. Electrical capacity (may need electrical experience to calculate)
 a. Current rating in amps
 b. Total peak load in kilowatts.
 iv. What, if any, bollards, signage, painting or other indicator will be used to demarcate EV
 parking. Research and document associated costs.
 v. Locally determined project costs:
 1. State and local sales tax percentage
 2. Contractor overhead percentage
 3. Contractor profit percentage
 4. Bond costs percentage
 5. Permits and zoning percentage
 6. Contingency cost percentage.
 d. Enter the data into EVI-LOCATE as prompted and run the analysis. Refer to the short demo
 videos on YouTube for detailed instructions on how to use the tool:
 i. Intro to EVI-LOCATE
 ii. EVI-LOCATE Step by Step
 e. After completing site assessments for all potential sites that you are considering, review the
 results and discuss the following questions as a team:
 i. Which charger and service panel locations are most cost effective?
 ii. How can you check the EVI-LOCATE results against local electric utility and site plans to
 ensure they don’t conflict with underground utilities?
 iii. Do the EVI-LOCATE results change your electrification plans? How?
 iv. What outstanding questions need to be resolved?
 v. If the results are cost prohibitive, are there public chargers along key routes or at
 partner facilities that could reduce the number of chargers needed for fleet
 electrification?

5. Financial Planning
Analyze the costs of vehicles and charging and develop a financial plan.

Calculate total cost of ownership (TCO) for different vehicle options using Argonne National
Laboratory’s TechScape tool.

 1. Download and install TechScape by requesting a license. After installation, you can access the User
 Guide by clicking the “Help” button within TechScape.
 2. When you launch TechScape, select the “Market Production Vehicle Analysis” workflow.
 3. Gather data for the vehicles scheduled for replacement. For each vehicle scheduled for replacement,
 search the TechScape database to select the most suitable replacement vehicle available on the market.
 1. The Fleet Characteristics Template in Section 3 of this document is designed to support you in
 gathering this data.
 4. TechScape provides default assumptions for vehicle costs, fuel, maintenance, repairs, and other metrics
 needed for TCO. If your own assumptions are available, enter them into the tool to reflect your specific
 operating conditions. If not, proceed with the default cost data provided in TechScape.
 5. For questions or feedback regarding TechScape, please contact the TechScape team
 at techscape@anl.gov

After completing your TCO analysis and the site assessment completed in Section 4 above, review the results
and discuss the following questions as a team.

 1. What vehicles are most cost effective? Which are least?
 2. Are there ways to mitigate and manage costs such as charge management, offsite charging,
 complementary technology like solar and/or storage, or right-sizing fleets?
 3. Do the TCO analysis and site assessment results change your fleet electrification plans? How so? Do you
 need to reassess your plan goals, timelines, or strategy?
 4. How might you use the TCO and site assessment results to communicate with internal and external
 stakeholders identified in section 2?
 5. How will your planned transition impact your fleet’s finances in the short, medium, and long terms? Will
 you need to change your approach to budgeting due to this transition, such as long-term shifts in
 operating vs. capital budgets?
 6. How will your agency fund any necessary upfront infrastructure investments?
 7. Are there funding or financing opportunities that could offset upfront investments?

Identify funding and financing opportunities. As a team, identify which funding opportunities are of highest
interest, using the resources below. Document the key dates for those priority opportunities and how you can
prepare (e.g., analysis, engagement activities, partnership development, leadership meetings)?

 1. EV infrastructure funding resources:
 a. Electrification Coalition EV Funding Finder
 b. Lawyers for Good Government Elective Pay Filing Tools
 c. Atlas Public Policy Project Finance Hub
 2. Local, state, and utility funding, financing, and incentive options:
 a. Local taxes, millage, or other funding streams that could be used for municipal infrastructure
 b. State funding programs

 17

 c. Utility EV charging incentives, such as a make-ready program. You can use the U-Finder tool to
 find your utility and incentives
 d. State and federal tax credits for EVs and EV chargers. When will you be able to file for the
 credits, given that the asset must first be placed into service?
 3. Other alternative financing opportunities or opportunities for new revenue streams, such as vehicle-to-
 grid programs.

6. Operations and Maintenance
EVs will require new skills and training to operate and maintain. It is important to develop a plan to train your

 1. General Resources:
 a. The U.S. Department of Energy’s Federal Energy Management Program maintains an EV
 Champion Training Series, which includes video trainings on EV technology, financial
 considerations, EVSE, and driving EVs.
 b. The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) sets electrical standards for
 workplace safety which may be applicable, including OSHA 1910.132 which requires hazard
 assessment of mechanical, shock, chemical, and electrical explosion (arc flash). Mitigation of
 hazard can come from design, work practices, or personal protective equipment (PPE).
 c. SAE International is a source for engineering standards related to electric vehicles. They develop
 standards for charging, safety, and more.
 2. EV Technician Resources:
 a. The National Institute for Automotive Service Excellence (ASE) offers xEV High-Voltage Electrical
 Safety certifications that were developed to serve as a guide, sharing existing industry
 standards, concepts, and practices followed by individuals working in the automotive, truck, and
 commercial electrical industry.
 i.  xEV Electrical Safety Awareness Certification (Level 1) is designed for anyone who may
 encounter an EV in the workplace. This includes an individual who performs tasks in
 proximity of electrified propulsion vehicles in sales, service, repair, and/or related
 environments.
 ii.  EV Technician Electrical Safety Certification (Level 2) is for a service professional,
 technician, or specialist who has received high-voltage electrical training, has
 demonstrated skills and knowledge related to the construction, operation, and repair of
 electrically powered high-voltage vehicles, maintains an electrically safe working area,
 and uses required PPE. They have also received safety training to identify the hazards
 and reduce the associated risk.
 iii.  ASE also offers a Light Duty Hybrid/Electric Vehicle Specialist (L3) Test.

 19

 b. Weber State University offers online training and in-person boot camps for the ASE xeV
 certifications.
 c. The Electric Vehicle Standards Council is an industry-led effort to create the global standard for
 EV technician education, EV product safety, and EV retrofits and builds. EVSC maintains Certified
 Electric Vehicle Technician Standards.
 d. The Electronics Technicians Association (ETA) International also offers EV technician
 Competency Requirements and certification programs. Certification is for individuals interested
 in attaining training from an ETA International-approved EV school. In this program students will
 develop skills in safety, troubleshooting, and repairing of EVs. Due to the high voltage and safety
 issues involved, technicians are required to receive specialized training in both knowledge and
 hands-on skill items.
 3. EV Charging Resources:
 a. SAE International has produced a certification program to establish and verify the skills and
 knowledge of technicians who maintain, repair, and operate EV charging stations. The Electric
 Vehicle Supply Equipment (EVSE) Technician Certification is granted to technicians who have
 successfully completed a comprehensive training program and passed an exam on the EVSE
 Technician Standards and Body of Knowledge. Available training programs are listed on SAE’s
 website.
 b. The Electric Vehicle Infrastructure Training Program (EVITP) provides training and certification
 for electricians installing EVSE. The website includes a database to find trained contractors.
 4. First Responder Resources:
 a. The U.S. Department of Energy’s Alternative Fuels Data Center maintains a comprehensive list of
 first responder trainings and resources on their website of Electric Vehicle Safety Training
 Resources for First and Second Responders.
 b. The National Fire Protection Agency (NFPA) produces fire and life safety standards covering a
 wide array of risk exposure, including electrical safety. The organization offers free resources for
 EV safety, emergency response guides for specific vehicles, and more.
 i. NFPA 70E sets safety requirements for any worker exposed to 50 volts or more. Workers
 exposed to 50 or more volts must be trained to be “Qualified Electrical Workers”
 according to the Standard. Training is offered by NFPA and many others, and workers
 can receive certification as a Certified Electrical Safety Compliance Professional (CESCP).
