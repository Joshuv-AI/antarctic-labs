Rethinking Explanations: Formalizing Contrast in Description Logics
Yasir Mahmood, Arnab Sharma, Axel-Cyrille Ngonga Ngomo, Balram Tiwari

Abstract
There has been a growing interest in explaining entailments over description logic (DL) knowledge bases. The existing explanation formalisms focus on justifications to explain true axioms, and abductive reasoning to explain missing axioms in a knowledge base. However, these formalisms only point out the reasoning steps behind a (missing) entailment and lack a user-centered approach as they do not consider an inquirer’s needs, level of understanding, or prior knowledge. We propose contrastive explanations, aiming at answering “why an axiom P P (fact) is true instead of another axiom Q Q (foil)” over description logic knowledge bases. The motivation arises from the observation that when a user discovers that P P has occurred, they are often surprised because they anticipated the occurrence of another similar event Q Q . Furthermore, individual explanations for “why P P ” and “why not Q Q ” are unsatisfactory since a user expects to see the difference between P P and Q Q . In this work, we first present formal foundations of contrasting questions and then define contrastive explanations within description logics. To this end, facts include ABox assertions of the form C ⁡ ( x ) C(x) for a concept C C and individual x x . Possible foils for such facts are assertions C ⁡ ( y ) C(y) (contrasting against an individual y y ), or D ⁡ ( x ) D(x) (contrasting against a concept D D ). Additionally, we explore the properties of contrastive explanations in the DL ℰ ​ ℒ {\mathcal{EL}} and 𝒜 ​ ℒ ​ 𝒞 \mathcal{ALC} . We also provide an implementation of our definition and an experimental evaluation on KBs of varying sizes.

Introduction

Description logics (DLs)
[
5
,
20
]
are foundational to the Web Ontology Language (OWL), enabling precise knowledge compilation and automated reasoning. As a formal framework underpinning OWL, DLs allow for knowledge representation through concepts (classes), roles (properties), and individuals (instances).
With their well-defined semantics, expressive power, and favorable computational properties, DLs have emerged as fundamental tools in various fields, including ontology engineering
[
31
]
, knowledge representation
[
9
]
, and semantic web technologies
[
24
]
.
The success of DLs is corroborated by the development of an increasing number of ontologies based on these formalisms.
Within DLs, research has increasingly focused on explaining entailments, where explanations aim to uncover the reasoning that leads to certain conclusions in a knowledge base (KB).
In particular, this includes a specific type of explanation known as justifications
[
3
,
46
,
23
,
28
]
.
Given a KB
\mathcal{K}
and an axiom
P
, a justification for
P
in
\mathcal{K}
is a minimal (under set inclusion) subset
\mathcal{J}
of
\mathcal{K}
such that
\mathcal{J}
entails
P
. Essentially, a justification answers the question “why is
P
true” by assuming that
P
is indeed true. However, if
P
is not true, one natural question arises: what changes should be carried out in
\mathcal{K}
to achieve the entailment of
P
from
\mathcal{K}
? Abductive reasoning
[
43
]
offers an approach to address
this problem. Existing research to this end discusses ABox abduction
[
44
,
10
,
34
,
35
]
, TBox abduction
[
12
,
19
]
and concept abduction
[
17
]
.

A justification for
P
answers “why P” by including the actual axioms required to infer
P
from the given KB.
The adequacy and relevance of an explanation are determined by the reasoner’s inference skills, which do not necessarily consider the user’s preferences or interests.
It has been observed
[
36
]
that a “why” question is usually accompanied by a
contrast
reflecting the context in which the explanation for the question is demanded.
However, the question “why
P
” and an answer to it do not sufficiently address the aspect of reasoning that interests the user.
Integrating contrasts that compare a fact to an expected alternative (or foil), can capture the distinctions that help users understand why a particular outcome prevailed over another.
In response to these concerns, finding explanations by asking contrastive questions
[
36
,
30
,
41
,
40
]
has become a popular research area. This popularity can be credited to the intuitive nature of such explanations
[
40
]
.
From a cognitive perspective, contrastive questions encourage comparison, help decision-making, and improve understanding of the underlying factors
[
13
]
.
We illustrate this in the following example.

Example 1

Assume the context of hiring at a company, where a model is utilized to classify potential candidates for the position.
Suppose an applicant (Alice) was hired for the position but another candidate (Bob) wasn’t.
Then, Bob would be interested in querying “why Alice was hired and not him” rather than the mere reason “why Alice was hired” or “why he was not hired”.
I.e., Bob is interested in knowing the
difference
between him and the successful candidate.
\Box

This example highlights how explicitly stating the contrast allows the inquirer’s explanatory preference to be integrated into the inference process.
This viewpoint on contrastive explanations (CEs) offers a clearer understanding of the reasoning step in the presence of alternatives.
Moreover, the process of highlighting the differences between alternative and equally viable choices promotes more informed decisions.
The intuition lies in understanding the processes behind decision-making, shedding light on factors such as preferences, previous experiences, or the advantages of a choice over other alternatives.

Contrastive explanations are extensively studied for machine learning (ML) models, especially in the context of explainable AI
[
25
,
11
,
2
,
48
,
25
]
. By framing explanations in a contrastive manner, models can provide insights into why a particular decision was made and why it was favored over alternative choices. This approach not only enhances transparency and interpretability but enables users to better understand the underlying factors driving the model’s predictions or recommendations.
While CEs are commonly utilized to explain the predictions of ML models, they largely remain unexplored in the context of DLs
[
33
]
.
Given that OWL relies on DLs to establish hierarchical relationships and enable complex reasoning within ontologies, this gap is especially pronounced.
Notable medical ontologies (such as SNOMED CT
[
47
]
and GALEN
[
45
]
), use DLs to classify entities, supporting inferences about potential disease implications. This foundational reliance on DLs highlights the need for contrastive explanations to clarify why certain facts prevail over others in knowledge-based reasoning.

To bridge this gap, we propose CEs for DL KBs in this study.
Our focus is on introducing an additional explanation-based mechanism that is natural and beneficial in various applications where the user’s understanding of the decision is crucial.
To this aim, we introduce contrasting questions
for an event
P
which has manifested (known as the
fact
) against an event
Q
that does not manifest (called the
foil
).
This contrasting situation corresponds to the scenario when an inquirer finds out that
P
has occurred but they expected
Q
instead.
We argue that the separate answers to “why
P
” given by its justification, and “why not
Q
” via its abductive hypothesis, fail to depict the actual difference between
P
and
Q
.
Indeed, in such scenarios, an inquirer receives two explanations that they are to compare themselves.
Moreover, there is no way of defining the
best
contrastive explanation since the individual explanations for
P
and
Q
may not share
common features
.
In this work, we formalize how explanations for
P
and
Q
can share common features and identify what the preferred contrastive explanation means under a specific criterion.
To summarize, our contributions are as follows.

•

We define CEs for DL KBs. To achieve this, we adapt Lipton’s (
[
36
]
) approach to CEs where facts and foils are formulated as ABox assertions.

•

We present a general scheme for defining CEs, and give some instantiations for this scheme. We also highlight interesting aspects of each instantiation and their relevance for DL KBs.

•

We address several criteria for preferred explanations and explore their complexity theoretic properties in two DLs, namely
{\mathcal{EL}}
and
\mathcal{ALC}
.

•

We present a prototypical implementation and evaluate it on several KBs.

5

Finally, Section
6

Preliminaries

We give a short exposition on the DL
\mathcal{ALC}

[

,
20
]
.
The choice is for simplicity rather than a restriction, and our setting is not confined to specific DLs.

Let

, and

denote mutually disjoint sets of individual, concept,
and role names, respectively.
In
\mathcal{ALC}

C\mathrel{\mathop{{\mathop{:}}{\mathop{:}}}}=A\mid\neg C\mid C\sqcap C\mid C\sqcup C\mid\exists r.C\mid\forall r.C

and

We also use two special symbols

A TBox is a finite set of general concept inclusions (GCIs), i.e., axioms of the form

C(a)
(concept assertion) or

r(a,b)

, concept
C

\mathcal{T}
and

Reasoning Problems in DLs.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a KB and

\mathcal{K}\models\alpha

Then, we are interested in the subset-minimal part of

By a slight abuse of the notation, a justification is a subset of a KB, as
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{A}

Definition 1
(Justification)

Let
\mathcal{K}=(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{A})
be a KB, and let

be a query (an ABox assertion). Then, a

is a subset

such that (1)
\mathcal{J}\models P
, and (2)
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\not\models P
for every
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{J}
.

We denote by
\mathsf{JUST}(P,\mathcal{K})
the set of all justifications for
P
in
\mathcal{K}
.
Also, it is convenient to denote a justification for

by
\mathcal{J}(P)
when the context allows.

3
Contrastive Explanations in Description Logics

Recall that a
fact
is a true axiom which logically follows from a KB, and a
foil
is a false statement that is not entailed.
In this work, we focus on explaining ABox assertions.
As a result, facts (
P
) and foils (
Q
) both take the form
C(x)
or
D(y)
for concepts
C,D
and individuals
x,y
. For simplicity, we focus on concept assertions in this work, although one can also consider role assertions.

Let
\mathcal{K}=(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{A})
be a KB, and
P,Q
be two assertions over the signature of
\mathcal{K}
.
Then, we call the pair
(P,Q)
a
contrastive question
(CQ) if
P
is a fact and
Q
is a foil.
Observe that one can in general contrast a fact
P
against any foil
Q
.
Nevertheless, we adhere to Kean’s (
[
30
]
) observation that a contrast for a fact is
of the same type
as the fact itself.
Moreover, Lipton (
[
36
]
) also motivated certain combinations of facts and foils, having a
largely similar history, against which the differences stand out
.
These observations lead to exploring the combinations of facts and foils that are interesting and meaningful for DL KBs.

Instantiation for Facts and Foils

We instantiate
P
and
Q
in the contrastive question
(P,Q)
with DLs concept assertions.
Precisely, we let
P\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=C(x)
be a fact for a concept
C
and individual
x
.
Following the previous discussion, the three most intriguing foils for contrasting
C(x)
include
\neg C(x),C(y)
, and
D(x)
. The approach to contrasting a fact against its negation involves computing justifications and has been explored in-depth
[
28
,
23
]
.
Therefore, the classical justifications for ABox assertions can be considered a special case of our problem to computing CEs. However, the remaining two types of foils, i.e.,
C(y)
and
D(x)
, have not gained (enough) attention before.
Consequently, we focus on the cases when a foil is obtained from a fact
C(x)
by diverging the individual element
x
(
entity
contrasts) and when a concept is changed, resulting in
D(x)
(
concept
contrasts).
These instantiations arise naturally for the contrast as the two cases share certain
common features
with the given fact.
The notion of entity contrasts bears some resemblance to the existing work on explaining the difference between two individuals
[
33
]
within a KB.
Nevertheless, the underlying definitions differ substantially as the aforementioned work only considers ABox assertions to explain the difference.
Moreover, our approach aims to explain a foil within the context of a justification for the given fact, whereas the work by
[
33
]
seeks to minimize the differences between fact and foil in terms of the ABox assertions for both, which may incur significant computational cost.

Entity Contrasts.

Corresponding to facts of the form
C(x)
, we let foils
C(y)
, where
x,y
are two distinct individuals and
C
is a concept.
Choosing a contrasting individual
y
to compare the assertions
C(y)
and
C(x)
highlights the differences in relevant characteristics encompassed within the concept
C
.
That is, the CQ
(C(x),C(y))
allows one to contrast an individual inside
C
with the one not in
C
.
This emphasizes specific features of
C
, aiding in understanding why certain entities are asserted to be positive instances in
C
.

In
\mathcal{K}_{\text{run}}
(Ex.
2
),

\mathtt{Hired}(\text{Alice})
is a fact.
For a skeptical user interested in finding the differences between Alice and Bob regarding the hiring process,
\mathtt{Hired}(\text{Bob})
serves as an entity foil.
\Box

Concept Contrasts.

We propose a foil
D(x)
for a fact of the form
C(x)
, where
C
and
D
are two distinct concepts and
x
is an individual.
Introducing a contrasting concept for the same individual enables exploring the boundary between different concepts within a KB.

Example 6

In
\mathcal{K}_{\text{run}}
(Ex.
2
), one may want to know further why Bob is classified as a theorist instead of an AI expert.
Then, corresponding to the fact “
\mathtt{Theorist}(\text{Bob})
”, the assertion “
\mathtt{AI\text{-}Expert}(\text{Bob})
” serves as a concept foil.
\Box

3.1
Formalizing Contrastive Explanations

We build upon the framework of Lipton (
[
36
]
) and first present an account on what constitutes a CE.
Essentially, contrasting
P
and
Q
amounts to citing a difference between the reasons for
P
and the missing facts to entail
Q
in a KB
\mathcal{K}
.
This so-called
difference condition

[
36
,
41
]
requires identifying a
cause
for
P
and the
absence
of
corresponding events
in the history of
Q
’s
failure
.

Property 1
(Difference condition
[
36
]
)

A contrastive explanation for
(P,Q)
cites the difference between the reasons for
P
and
Q
.

The difference condition (P1) allows one to formulate a contrastive explanation scheme by combining the justifications for
P
and
Q
.
Since
Q
is not true in
\mathcal{K}
, a justification
\mathcal{J}(Q)
is determined by an abductive hypothesis
\mathcal{H}(Q)
in
\mathcal{K}
.
We define a general CE scheme for “
P
instead of
Q
” based on the
difference condition
“
\blacktriangleright
” as a set of axioms
\mathcal{E}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{J}(P)\blacktriangleright\mathcal{J}(Q)
.
Our general definition translates Lipton’s difference condition to facts and foils formulated in DLs, except it leaves room for the precise interpretation of the difference condition “
\blacktriangleright
”.

DL-Specific Instantiations for CEs.

We now propose two properties desirable for an instantiation of the difference condition “
\blacktriangleright
” to fulfill for CQs formulated in DLs.
These properties model Lipton’s intuition of the
similarity
and
difference
between the justifications for
P
and
Q
.
In essence, P1 accounts for axioms common in explaining the fact
P
and foil
Q
, which are irrelevant in explaining the difference between
P
and
Q
.
However, the justification
\mathcal{J}(Q)
contains both true and missing assertions about
Q
, which should be distinguished to highlight the
failure
of
Q
in
\mathcal{K}
(Property
2
).
Moreover, the two justifications
\mathcal{J}(P)
and
\mathcal{J}(Q)
may contain
similar
axioms, irrelevant to differentiate a fact from a foil (Property
3
).
We explain both principles against their possible instantiations.

Property 2
(Known versus assumed knowledge)

A CE for
(P,Q)
differentiates true assertions in the reasoning about
Q
from missing assertions for
Q
.

Principle
2
(P2) aims at answering the CQ
(P,Q)
by elaborating on the missing assertions causing
Q
’s failure.
Thus, an instantiation satisfies P2 if true and missing assertions about
Q
can be differentiated in the given CE.
It can be seen that the CEs
\mathcal{E}^{\triangle}
does not satisfy P2, whereas,
\mathcal{E}^{\text{lip}}
satisfies P2 trivially.

We next instantiate
\blacktriangleright
based on the symmetric difference with partition (
\mathcal{E}^{\triangle\text{-f}}
) by further separating true assertions from assumed assertions about
Q
.
Let
\mathcal{J}(Q)\in\mathsf{JUST}(Q,\mathcal{K})
and
\mathcal{H}(Q)
be the abductive hypothesis for
Q
in
\mathcal{K}
such that
\mathcal{H}(Q)\subseteq\mathcal{J}(Q)
.
We denote by
\mathcal{J}_{t}(Q)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{J}(Q)\cap\mathcal{K}
the axioms in
\mathcal{J}(Q)

true
for
Q
.
Then, define CEs
\mathcal{E}^{\triangle\text{-f}}
by further partitioning the justifications for
Q
.

Definition 3

Consider a KB
\mathcal{K}=(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{A})
and a CQ
(P,Q)
. A
contrastive explanation
for
(P,Q)
in
\mathcal{K}
based on the symmetric difference with full partition is a tuple
\mathcal{E}^{\triangle\text{-f}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\langle\mathcal{J}(P)\setminus\mathcal{J}_{t}(Q),\mathcal{J}_{t}(Q)\setminus\mathcal{J}(P),\mathcal{H}(Q)\rangle

where
\mathcal{J}(X)\in\mathsf{JUST}(X,\mathcal{K})
for
X\in\{P,Q\}
with
\mathcal{J}_{t}(Q)=\mathcal{J}(Q)\cap\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{H}(Q)=\mathcal{J}(Q)\setminus\mathcal{K}
.

\mathcal{E}^{\triangle\text{-f}}
differentiates axioms for
P
and
Q
as well as true assertions (
\mathcal{J}_{t}(Q)
) from the missing assertions (
\mathcal{H}(Q)
) about

.
For brevity, we rename each individual component in a CE
\mathcal{E}
as
\mathcal{E}_{\text{fact}}
(axioms for the fact),
\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil+}}

Example 10

For our running example, two CEs with full partition are
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\triangle\text{-f}}=\langle\mathcal{E}_{\text{fact}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil+}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}}\rangle

\mathcal{E}_{\text{fact}}

\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil+}}

,

\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}}

=\{\mathtt{publishesIn}(\text{Bob},\text{ML})\}
, and

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{\text{fact}}

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{\text{foil+}}

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{\text{foil-}}

A final challenge in our instantiation concerns the “common” assertions.
The two assertions about Alice and Bob being qualified are both required and true in
\mathcal{K}_{\text{run}}

As a result, these should be treated as

Our final principle allows us to treat
similar
(yet unequal) axioms for
P
and

Note that this characteristic corresponds to Lipton’s argument of citing the cause of
P
with no
corresponding event
in the history of the failure of

That is, the difference condition removes the same axioms in an explanation and similar ones for a fact-foil pair.

Property 3
(Accounting for similar axioms)

A CE for
(P,Q)
does not differentiate between “similar” true assertions about
P
and
Q

The standard set difference does not account for such similar axioms between
\mathcal{J}(P)
and
\mathcal{J}(Q)

Indeed, each CE based on the set difference contains assertions
\mathtt{Qualified}(\text{Alice})
&
\mathtt{Qualified}(\text{Bob})
.
Moreover, the definition of

We now specify criteria for similarity when entity and concept foils are considered.
Intuitively, for entity CQs
(C(x),C(y))

Likewise, for concept CQs

Definition 4
(Renamed axioms)

Let

\alpha

For

.
For
\beta=\mathtt{Theorist}(\text{Bob})

\beta[{\text{Theorist}\mapsto\text{AI-Expert}}]=\mathtt{AI\text{-}Expert}(\text{Bob})
.
\Box

To obtain our final instantiation, we modify the set difference operator to obtain
{}^{\tiny{X}}{\searrow}_{\tiny{Y}}

Definition 5

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a KB,

be concepts, and

Then, for two subsets

and
\mathcal{J}_{2}

•

\mathcal{J}_{1}^{\tiny{X}}{\searrow}_{\tiny{Y}}\mathcal{J}_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{1}\mid\alpha\not\in\mathcal{J}_{2}\text{ or }\alpha[x\mapsto y]\not\in\mathcal{J}_{2}\}
, if
(X,Y)=(x,y)
.

•

\mathcal{J}_{1}^{\tiny{X}}{\searrow}_{\tiny{Y}}\mathcal{J}_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{1}\mid\alpha\not\in\mathcal{J}_{2}\text{ or }\alpha[C\mapsto D]\not\in\mathcal{J}_{2}\}

The set difference operator
(^{\tiny{X}}{\searrow}_{\tiny{Y}})
removes axioms that are
similar
under renaming of concepts (resp., entities) to differentiate facts and foils.
This intuition corresponds to including axioms required to explain
P

are missing in the
history
of

.
For convenience, we denote the modified set difference as “
\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin]{45.0}{$\ddagger$}}
”.
We now present our final proposal. As before, we find it convenient to denote individual components of a CE as axioms relevant for either fact or foil.

includes

3.2
Handling Inconsistencies in Explanations

Our definitions for CEs in the previous section cannot handle the case when a justification for the foil does not exist.
Such a situation can arise when some axioms in the KB conflict with the foil itself.
This is inline with the setting of abduction
[
32
,
44
,
10
,
34
]
, which require a hypothesis to be consistent with the KB.
However, it might be impossible to find an explanation in certain cases involving incompatible facts and foils (i.e., both can not be entailed at the same time).

To illustrate this, let us reconsider our KB
\mathcal{K}_{\text{run}}
from Example
2
and add an axiom saying that “Theorist” and “AI-Expert” are two disjoint classes.
Thus, we obtain
\mathcal{K}_{\text{run}}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{K}_{\text{run}}\cup\{\mathtt{Theorist}\sqcap\mathtt{AI\text{-}Expert}\sqsubseteq\bot\}
.
We have that
\mathcal{K}_{\text{run}}^{\prime}\models\mathtt{Theorist}(\text{Bob})
due to the axioms
\{\mathtt{Publishes}(\text{Bob},\text{Math}),\mathtt{Theory}(\text{Math})\}
in
\mathcal{K}_{\text{run}}
.
Now, let us reconsider our CQ
R\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(\mathtt{Hired}(\text{Alice})
,
\mathtt{Hired}(\text{Bob}))
from before.
Here,
R
does not admit any explanation apart from the trivial CE
\langle\{\mathtt{Hired}(\text{Alice})\},\emptyset,\{\mathtt{Hired}(\text{Bob})\}\rangle
.
However, one might still want to obtain a non-trivial yet interesting explanation, for instance: “If Bob published in ML, he would have been hired, but publishing in ML makes him an AI-Expert which contradicts to Bob being a Theorist”.
To mitigate this, we add a final component to our definition of CEs, namely the set of (possibly empty) conflicts incurred when trying to entail the given foil.
In our running example, such a set of conflicts would include assertions
\{\mathtt{Publishes}(\text{Bob},\text{Math}),\mathtt{Theory}(\text{Math})\}
in
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
.

For a CE
\mathcal{E}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\langle\mathcal{E}_{\text{fact}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil+}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}}\rangle
such that
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}})\models\bot
, we define its
conflict
set in
\mathcal{K}
to be the subset-minimal
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
such that
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}})\not\models\bot
where
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{C}
. Intuitively, adding the missing axioms for the foil (
\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}}
) creates conflicts (
\mathcal{C}
) with the assertions in
\mathcal{K}
and removing
\mathcal{C}
gives an alternative and consistent scenario in which the foil can still be entailed.
Here, we consider
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
to only include ABox assertions about foil that are conflicting with
\mathcal{E}
.
This aligns with the existing notion of
counterfactual scenario

[
13
,
33
]
.
Henceforth, we assume that a CE
\mathcal{E}
has four components
\langle\mathcal{E}_{\text{fact}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil+}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}},\mathcal{C}\rangle
and we ignore
\mathcal{C}
in case there are no conflicts (i.e.,
\mathcal{C}=\emptyset
).

3.3
Preferred Contrastive Explanations

We now focus on selecting preferred explanations when multiple choices are available.
Traditionally, one expects explanations to be small and hence subset- or cardinality-minimal explanations are usually preferred. Thus, one may attempt to consider the similar criteria when defining preferred CEs.
We argue that subset minimality is inadequate when preferred CEs are requested.
Likewise, one may have additional preferences when faced with multiple cardinality-minimal CEs.
We illustrate these features next.

Example 13

Let
\mathcal{K}=(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{A})
where
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{1}(x),A_{2}(x)\}
and
\mathcal{T}=\{A_{1}\sqcap A_{2}\sqsubseteq E,E\sqsubseteq C\}
.
Further, let
P\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=C(x)
be a fact and
Q\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=C(y)
be the foil.
Then,
\mathcal{J}(P)=\{A_{1}(x),A_{2}(x),A_{1}\sqcap A_{2}\sqsubseteq E,E\sqsubseteq C\}
.
The CQ
(P,Q)
admits two CEs in
\mathcal{K}
of size
4
.
Namely,

•

\mathcal{E}^{\diamond}_{1}=\langle\{A_{1}(x),A_{2}(x),A_{1}\sqcap A_{2}\sqsubseteq E\},\emptyset,\{E(y)\}\rangle

•

\mathcal{E}^{\diamond}_{2}=\langle\{A_{1}(x),A_{2}(x)\},\emptyset,\{A_{1}(y),A_{2}(y)\}\rangle
.

Both CEs are cardinality- and subset-minimal.
But,
\mathcal{E}^{\diamond}_{2}

explains
Q

in the context of
P
, i.e., using the same concepts as
P
.
\Box

Since CEs are derived from justifications for
P
and
Q
, a
logic
al approach for preferred CEs involves evaluating the
similarity
between the two justifications.
This aligns with our intuition that CEs explain
P

within the context
of
Q
, and highlight the shared characteristics between the fact and the foil in a KB.
Alternatively, when explanations introduce conflicts with assertions already known about the foil, it might be desirable to prefer CEs that minimize such conflicts.
Intuitively, one is interested in explanations whose missing assertions for the foil do not (or minimally) contradict the assertions already in place.
However, computing conflict-minimal explanations can be computationally expensive
[
34
,
33
]
. Therefore, we leave a detailed discussion and theoretical analysis of conflict-minimal CEs to future work.
In what follows, we compare two CEs in terms of axioms relevant for the fact and foil while the incurred conflicts do not contribute in the comparison.
This way, the conflicts can still be computed efficiently since one does not require to find CEs with
globally minimal
conflicts.

In the following, we consider CEs according to Definition
6
.
Let
\mathcal{K}
be a KB,
(P,Q)
be a CQ, and
\mathcal{E}=\langle\mathcal{E}_{\text{fact}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil+}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}},\mathcal{C}\rangle
be a CE for
(P,Q)
.
The size of
\mathcal{E}
(
|\mathcal{E}|
) is defined as the number of axioms in
\mathcal{E}_{\text{fact}}\cup\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil+}}\cup\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}}
.
Moreover, let
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
be two CEs with
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\langle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{\text{fact}},\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{\text{foil+}},\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{\text{foil-}}\rangle
.
Then, we define
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
in a component-wise manner, i.e,
\mathcal{E}_{*}\subseteq\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{*}
for each
*\in\{\text{fact},\text{foil+},\text{foil-}\}
.
We define a CE
\mathcal{E}
for
(P,Q)
to be subset (resp., cardinality) minimal in
\mathcal{K}
if there is no CE
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\subsetneq\mathcal{E}
(
|\mathcal{E}^{\prime}|<|\mathcal{E}|
) for
(P,Q)
in
\mathcal{K}
.

We propose cardinality-based criteria for preferred CEs.
Intuitively, those explanations are preferred, in which justifications for facts and foils
resemble
the most or equivalently, when their
syntactic divergence
is minimal. To formalize this, we define the symmetric difference (
\blacklozenge
) based on our set difference (
\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin]{45.0}{$\ddagger$}}
)
as
A\blacklozenge B\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(A\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin]{45.0}{$\ddagger$}}B)\cup(B\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin]{45.0}{$\ddagger$}}A)
. We denote by
\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil+}}\cup\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}}
all foil axioms.

Definition 7

Let
R
be a CQ in
\mathcal{K}
, and
\mathcal{E}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\langle\mathcal{E}_{\text{fact}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil+}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}},\mathcal{C}\rangle
be a CE for
R
in
\mathcal{K}
.
The
R
-divergence for
\mathcal{E}
is defined as
\sigma^{\text{div}}(\mathcal{E})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=|\mathcal{E}_{\text{fact}}{\blacklozenge}\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil}}|
.

The intuition lies in preferring a CE in which the axioms required for the foil
coincide
with those used to justify the fact. This preference is motivated by the observation that a skeptical user either understands the reasoning underlying the fact or seeks to compare the foil against it. Consequently, a better CE aligns the assertions relevant to the foil as closely as possible with those used for the fact.
We define
\sigma^{\text{div}}
-preferred explanations as those that have the lowest divergence; hence,
\mathcal{E}
is
\sigma^{\text{div}}
-preferred if there is no explanation
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
with

Example 15

Let

4
Properties of Contrastive Explanations

Here, we address several computational and otherwise interesting properties for CEs in our framework.
We first consider the case of
{\mathcal{EL}}
for which abduction is trivial whereas contrastive explanations still turns out to be non-trivial.

The DL
{\mathcal{EL}}
allows concepts following the grammar rule
C\mathrel{\mathop{{\mathop{:}}{\mathop{:}}}}=A\mid C\sqcap C\mid\exists r.C
.
In particular, no constructor in
{\mathcal{EL}}
causes inconsistencies.
Therefore, the abduction is trivialized as for any
{\mathcal{EL}}
KB
\mathcal{K}
and assertion
Q=C(y)
:
\{C(y)\}
is an abductive hypothesis and a justification for
Q
.
Nevertheless, the question of which abductive explanations lead to preferred (cardinality-minimal and
\sigma^{\text{div}}
-preferred) CEs, still remains relevant.

We begin by proving that there always exists a CE with empty conflicts for a CQ
(P,Q)
in a KB
\mathcal{K}
formulated in
{\mathcal{EL}}
.
Unless specified otherwise, all results in this section hold for both (entity and concept) contrasts.

Proposition 1

Let
\mathcal{K}
be an
{\mathcal{EL}}
KB and
R=(P,Q)
be a CQ.
There exists at least one contrastive explanation for
R
in
\mathcal{K}
.

Proof

Since
P
is a fact, we have
\mathcal{K}\models P
.
Let
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{K}
be a justification for
P
in
\mathcal{K}
.
Now, assume

be the foil in

Since

is an
{\mathcal{EL}}

is an
{\mathcal{EL}}

is consistent.
As a result,
\mathcal{E}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\langle\mathcal{J},\emptyset,\{\alpha\}\rangle
is a contrastive explanation for
R
in
\mathcal{K}
.
Moreover, the size of
\mathcal{E}
is bounded from above by
|\mathcal{K}|+1
.

To determine if a CQ admits a CE of the given cardinality (divergence)

is

-complete for

.

Theorem 4.1

Let
\mathcal{K}
be an
{\mathcal{EL}}

a CQ for concept foil

,

\mathcal{K}
of size (divergence) at most
n

Proof

We first prove the membership and then turn towards the hardness.

Membership:

We present a procedure (called, A) that performs in non-deterministic polynomial time and decides whether an explanation of size at most
n
exists for
R
in
\mathcal{K}

|\mathcal{K}|=k

A has the following three steps.

1.

Non-deterministically guess three sets of axioms
S_{1},S_{2},S_{3}
, such that

(a)

S_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{K}
with
|S_{1}|\leq k

(b)
S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{K}
with
|S_{2}|\leq k

(c)

S_{3}

Determine whether

(b)

\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=S_{3}

3.

Accept if each question (a)–(c) in Step 2 is true.

To prove the correctness of A, we notice that every contrastive explanation can be written as a collection of three axioms sets.
We prove the following claim.

Claim

The procedure A involving Steps (1)–(3) performs in polynomial time and correctly decides the existence of a CE for
R
in
\mathcal{K}
of size at most
n
.

Proof of Claim

Suppose A accepts and let
S_{1},S_{2},S_{3}
are the (non-deterministic) choices made by A in Steps (1a)–(1c) on an accepting branch.
We observe that if

P
in
\mathcal{K}
of size at most
k

Therefore, although

may not be a subset-minimal set of axioms with

\mathcal{J}(P)
for
P
.
Moreover, if there is no abductive hypothesis for

in
\mathcal{K}
of size

in
\mathcal{K}
of size bounded by
n
.
This holds since an abductive hypothesis
\mathcal{H}(Q)
is included in an explanation for
R
.
As a result, the same claim holds for a justification for
Q
in
S_{2}\cup S_{3}
as in the case of
P
.
We take
\mathcal{J}(P)\subseteq S_{1}
and
\mathcal{J}(Q)\subseteq S_{2}\cup S_{3}
be justifications (hence, subset-minimal) for
P
and
Q
in
\mathcal{K}
, respectively.
Note that
\mathcal{J}(P)
and
\mathcal{J}(Q)
exist due to Step (2a) and (2b).
Then,
\mathcal{E}=\langle\mathcal{E}_{\text{fact}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil+}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}}\rangle
is a contrastive explanation for

, where
\mathcal{E}_{*}
is the same as defined in Step (2c).
Finally,
|\mathcal{E}|\leq n
since A accepts and Step (2c) returns true.

Conversely, suppose a contrastive explanation
\mathcal{E}=\langle\mathcal{E}_{\text{fact}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil+}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}}\rangle
of size at most
n
exists for
R
in
\mathcal{K}
.
Then, there is at least one choice of sets for the algorithm in Step (1).
Namely a justification
\mathcal{J}(P)
for

, an abductive hypothesis
\mathcal{H}(Q)
, and the corresponding justification

for

in

, that yields the contrastive explanation

.
Moreover, we have that

and

since

and
|\mathcal{K}|=k
.
Then, this choice of sets results in an accepting branch of A.
This completes the correctness proof.

Regarding the runtime, note that the non-deterministic guess in the first step of A requires polynomial time since the size of each guessed set is bounded by
n
and

.
Further, the entailment in the second step can be performed in polynomial time for
{\mathcal{EL}}
KBs.
Finally, the last step consists of merely size comparison.
Consequently, the claim regarding the runtime follows.

We present slight modifications to A for the membership regarding
\sigma^{\text{div}}
.
This includes guessing a set

in Step (1c) of size

instead, and checking whether
|S_{1}{\blacklozenge}S_{3}|\leq n

Then the correctness is established in the same way as in Claim
Claim
by observing that only an abductive hypothesis

for

can yield a contrastive explanation with

-index bounded by

.

This establishes the membership. We now turn towards proving

-hardness.

Hardness:
Recall that the problem of determining the existence of a justification for an entailment

from an
{\mathcal{EL}}

of a given cardinality

is

[

We reduce from this to our problem as follows.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be an
{\mathcal{EL}}

A,B
be two concepts over the signature of
\mathcal{T}
.
We let
\mathcal{K}=(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{A})

\mathcal{A}=\{A(x)\}
.
Moreover, we let

Further, a justification for

of size

of size

We let

be a fresh (dummy) concept not in the signature of

The concept

\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}\cup\{D\sqsubseteq\top\}

As a result, there is only one (trivial) abductive explanations for

.
Finally, we seek a contrastive explanation for
(P,Q)
of size

in
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
.
This prove the claim by noting that every justification
\mathcal{J}(\alpha)
for
\alpha
in
\mathcal{T}
of size at most

\langle\mathcal{J}(P),\emptyset,\{D(x)\}\rangle
for
(P,Q)
in
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
of size (
\sigma^{\text{div}}
)
n+2
at most.

We next observe that not all the “hardness results” transfer from their counterparts for justifications in
{\mathcal{EL}}
.
In particular, it is known that there exist families of knowledge bases
\mathcal{K}_{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and an axiom

P
has exponentially many justifications in
\mathcal{K}_{n}
.
We establish that a fact can admit exponentially many justifications, but still only few CEs for the CQ, involving the same fact.
This also presents a case where a query may admit significantly large and complex justifications, but a corresponding CP may admit smaller CEs.

Theorem 4.2

There is a family
\left(\mathcal{K}_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\right)
of
{\mathcal{EL}}
KBs, a fact
P
and a foil
Q
such that
P
admits exponentially many cardinality minimal justifications in each
\mathcal{K}_{n}
, whereas the CQ
(P,Q)
has a unique cardinality-minimal (
\sigma^{\text{div}}
-preferred) contrastive explanation in
\mathcal{K}_{n}
.

Proof

Following Baader et al.
[
4
]
, we let
\mathcal{T}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{A_{i-1}\sqsubseteq X_{i}\sqcap Y_{i},X_{i}\sqsubseteq A_{i},Y_{i}\sqsubseteq A_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
be a collection of TBoxes.
Moreover, let
\mathcal{K}_{n}=(\mathcal{T}_{n},\mathcal{A})
be the family of KBs where
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{0}(x)\}
.
Assume,
P\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=A_{n}(x)
and
Q\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=A_{n}(y)
.

P
has exponentially many justifications in
\mathcal{K}

[
4
, Example 1]
.
Namely, axioms
\mathcal{A}\cup\{A_{i-1}\sqsubseteq X_{i}\sqcap Y_{i},\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
are contained in every justification for
P
.
Further, every justification contains exactly one axiom from the set
\{X_{i}\sqsubseteq A_{i},Y_{i}\sqsubseteq A_{i}\}
for each
i\leq n
.
Since there are two possibilities for each
i\leq n
, this results in
2^{n}
many distinct justifications for
P
in
\mathcal{K}
of size
2n+1
.
Now, consider the foil
Q
and the CE-instance
(P,Q)
.
Then, the smallest CE (in size) is the one in which justifications for
P
and
Q
share the same TBox axioms.
Consequently,
\langle\{A_{0}(x)\},\emptyset,\{A_{0}(y)\}\rangle
is the only cardinality minimal (
\sigma^{\text{div}}
-preferred) contrastive explanation for
(P,Q)
in
\mathcal{K}_{n}
.

We conclude this section by addressing the complexity of computing CEs for CQs involving entity contrasts.
Here, it suffices to compute a justification for the fact, which also yields a
\sigma^{\text{div}}
-preferred CE for the given CQ.
Intuitively, given a justification
\mathcal{J}
for the fact, we can compute the
corresponding
CE by “simply” renaming ABox axioms in
\mathcal{J}
and finding the assertions for
Q
missing in the KB
\mathcal{K}
.
This algorithm applies to any DL considered in this paper. However, for DLs involving negations or some other form of inconsistencies (such as disjointness axioms), one additionally has to compute the conflicts sets involved.

Theorem 4.3

Let
\mathcal{K}
be an
{\mathcal{EL}}
KB, and
R
be a CQ involving a fact
P\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=C(x)
and foil
Q\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=C(y)
.
Then, for every
\mathcal{J}\in\mathsf{JUST}(P,\mathcal{K})
, there is a contrastive explanation
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{J}}
for
R
such that
\sigma^{\text{div}}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{J}})=0
.

Proof

We construct a CE
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{J}}
from
\mathcal{J}
by renaming individuals in the ABox assertions.
Let
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{J}\cap\mathcal{A}
(resp.,
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{T}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{J}\cap\mathcal{T}
) denote the ABox (TBox) axioms in
\mathcal{J}
.
Then, we observe that
\mathcal{J}_{Q}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{T}}\cup\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}[x\mapsto y]
is a justification for
C(y)
.
This can be established easily in every model of
\mathcal{J}
via a
bisimulation

[
20
,
5
]
that maps
x
to
y
and keeps all the remaining individuals unchanged.

Now, we let
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}[x\mapsto y]\cap\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{J}_{t}
and
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}[x\mapsto y]\setminus\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{J}_{m}
denote true and missing axioms for
Q
is
\mathcal{K}
, respectively.
This yields a contrastive explanation:
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{J}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\langle\mathcal{E}_{\text{fact}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil+}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}}\rangle
for
(P,Q)
where

\mathcal{E}_{\text{fact}}=\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin]{45.0}{$\ddagger$}}\mathcal{J}_{t}
,

\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil+}}=\mathcal{J}_{t}\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin]{45.0}{$\ddagger$}}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}
and finally
\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}}=\mathcal{J}_{m}
.
Here, the two justifications for
P
and
Q
share all the axioms in the TBox
\mathcal{T}
and every ABox assertion in the justification for
Q
is simply a renaming of those in
\mathcal{J}
.
As a result, we have that
\sigma^{\text{div}}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{J}})=0
by definition.

As a consequence of Theorem
4.3
, a contrastive explanation for CQs in
{\mathcal{EL}}
KBs with syntactic semantic divergence can be computed in polynomial time.

Corollary 1

Let
\mathcal{K}
be an
{\mathcal{EL}}
KB, and
R
be a CQ involving entity contrasts. Then, a CE
\mathcal{E}
for

in
\mathcal{K}

Nevertheless, Corollary

Interestingly, one can extend the correctness results in Theorem

Theorem 4.4

Let
\mathcal{K}
be an
\mathcal{ALC}

\mathcal{E}

\mathcal{ALC}

, we compute a justification
\mathcal{J}
for
P

\mathcal{K}
.

\mathcal{K}

Then,
\mathcal{J}_{Q}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{J}[x\mapsto y]

Now, additionally, one has to check whether

5
Experimental Evaluation for Entity Contrasts

We have implemented a first prototype to compute contrastive explanations involving entity contrasts.
In the following, we outline our evaluation in detail.

5.1
Method for Computing CEs

We implemented the approach described in the proof of Theorem
4.3
to compute preferred CEs in
{\mathcal{EL}}_{\bot}
.
Precisely, we compute a justification for the fact
[
27
]
and then determine a justification for the foil by renaming fact and foil individuals.
The final CE
\mathcal{E}
is determined by distinguishing the axioms for the foil already present in the KB and computing any conflicts involved.

The source code for our algorithm and details on the experimental setup can be found online
2
2

2

https://github.com/dice-group/CEs_DLs
.
Our algorithm is implemented in Java 11, using the OWL API
[
21
]
as the core framework for ontology loading, axiom access, and reasoning.
Reasoning services are provided by standard OWL reasoners, including both the HermiT
[
16
]
and ELK
[
29
]
reasoners. Unless stated otherwise, the experimental results reported in this paper are generated using ELK.
Finally, justifications for the fact and for the inconsistencies are computed using a black-box explanation approach based on the
BlackBoxExplanation
framework
[
27
]
.

Experiments were conducted on a dedicated Debian 11 (Bullseye) Linux server equipped with an x86_64 architecture and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU with 24 GB of memory.
Our experiments are executed via a custom Java-based experiment runner that automatically selects CQs (involving query, facts, and foils), executes the individual functions to compute justifications, and records detailed per-run statistics in CSV log files.
For post-processing and evaluation, we use the Python-based library
pandas
within a Jupyter notebook environment.
These scripts aggregate the CSV logs, filter invalid runs, and compute the summary statistics reported in the following section.

5.2
Benchmark KBs for Experiments

To evaluate the proposed contrastive explanation approach, we conducted experiments on a collection of real-world OWL KBs that are publicly available
[
39
,
42
]
.
The KBs we consider for our experiments are of different shapes and sizes, as they correspond to the ones used for ABox reasoning.
In total, we consider approximately 100 KBs as initial candidates for our experiments.
Due to practical memory and runtime constraints, we apply a filtering step based on the size and exclude KBs containing more than 10,000 axioms. This results in 41 KBs spanning a variety of domains and differing in size, structure, and ABox density, providing a diverse benchmark for evaluation.

For each selected KB, concepts (queries), facts, and foils are constructed automatically. A query corresponds to an OWL class selected from the signature of the underlying KB, subject to constraints that ensure the class is neither overly general nor trivial.
In particular, we consider queries that must have at least one positive instance (fact) and at least one negative instance (foil).
Moreover, we discard queries with an excessively large fraction of positive instances.

. A foil
b
is selected from the remaining individuals such that
C(b)
is not entailed from
\mathcal{K}
. We further apply additional filtering to ensure that the foil is semantically related to the fact, increasing the likelihood of meaningful contrastive explanations.
For each KB, we perform five independent runs using different randomly sampled combinations of queries, facts, and foils.
Each run is subject to fixed upper bounds on the number of candidate queries, as well as facts and foils per query.

Evaluation Results

We evaluate the proposed contrastive explanation method using quantitative measures that capture both the
sizes
of individual justifications and missing axioms for foil, as well as their computational cost.
Moreover, since our algorithm computes CEs in which TBox axioms are
shared
between the fact and fol, the reported measures are computed over ABox axioms.

For each successful run, we record the size of the ABox justification for the fact (
\mathcal{E}_{\text{fact}}
).
To analyze the foil,
we record the number of ABox axioms for the foil that are already present in the KB (
\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil+}}

the number of missing ABox axioms (
\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}}

In cases where adding
\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}}
leads to an inconsistency with the KB, we additionally compute the (size of the) conflicts (
\mathcal{C}
) over the ABox, i.e., minimal sets of axioms responsible for the inconsistency.
This allows us to quantify when CEs cause clashes with existing instance-level knowledge.
For each run, we also record the total execution time in milliseconds from the start of the procedure to the completion of consistency checking and the final explanation generation.
The runtime includes (i) justification computation for the fact, (ii) computing existing and missing axioms for the foil from its justification, (iii) consistency testing, and (iv) extracting conflict.

\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}}

Summary of Evaluation.

The evaluation shows that our current prototype performs rather efficiently to compute CEs.
Moreover, the computed explanations tend to be small, spanning up to four ABox axioms.
This is in line with the existing observations on the computation of justifications
[
27
]
and further explains how only a small number of assertions are absent for the foil.
Nevertheless, in most of the cases, fact and foil share too few axioms in their history, as seen by the

.
Eventually, there are only a small number of cases when the computed explanations conflict with the KB (
Conflict rate

6
Concluding Remarks

We introduce the concept of contrastive questions and their answers to DL KBs and highlights several challenges in their implementation.
Our contributions further include establishing principles to address these challenges and providing definitions for explanations that adhere to these principles.
We confirm that Lipton’s framework for contrastive explanations can be tailored to facts and foils formulated in DLs.
This is achieved by presenting three principles governing CEs in DLs.
Towards the end, we presented criteria for preferred contrastive explanations and provided further theoretical analysis for CEs.
We also present a prototype that demonstrates the practical readiness of our framework for CEs.

One motivation for CEs lies in their specificity to the contrast in a question since a contrast specified by an inquirer reflects their preference. For instance, in the explanation context
\mathcal{K}_{\text{run}}
from Example
2
, let us add an assertion about another individual, “Carl”, namely,
\{\mathtt{publishesIn}(\text{Carl},\text{ML})\}
.
Then, an explanation for CQ
(\mathtt{Hired}(\text{Alice}),\mathtt{Hired}(\text{Carl}))
depicts that Alice is qualified whereas Carl is not.
The comparison of this explanation with the one for
(\mathtt{Hired}(\text{Alice}),\mathtt{Hired}(\text{Bob}))
highlights how contrastive questions model an inquirer’s
preference
and a
context
requested by them in an explanation.

To the best of our knowledge, the notion of CEs has not previously been studied in the context of DLs, except for the specific case of entity foils
[
33
]
.
Our work can be considered preliminary in this area, and several interesting questions remain yet to be explored.
Interestingly, our definitions extend to any DL for which a notion of abductive hypothesis exists. This follows since our approach essentially provides a mechanism for combining the two justifications to explain the difference between a fact and a foil.
Considering the case of DLs with cardinality restrictions, one way to entail the foil in a CE is to (i) add missing role assertions via
\mathcal{E}_{\text{foil-}}
to satisfy
\geq
-constraints, and (ii) remove assertions via
\mathcal{C}
if some
\leq
-constraints are violated.
Nevertheless, a CE may not always exist when the two steps cannot be carried out simultaneously.

Future Work.

First, a detailed exposition of our approach for more expressive DLs and an implementation that can handle conflicts efficiently is on our list.
We aim towards a practical algorithm and an implementation that scales at least for
\mathcal{ALC}
with
cardinality
constraints, and
role hierarchy
.
The main complexity in our implementation arises from computing a justification for the fact and conflicts, which can be costly for large datasets; existing tools such as modules
[
50
]
could help improve runtime.
Moreover, computing contrastive explanations for the concept foils also seems an interesting yet challenging task.
On the empirical side, a user study should be conducted to assess the hypothesis of whether such explanations are preferred over
plain
justifications.
This would also allow us to evaluate different preference criteria for the best CEs and compare them. On the theory side, it remains to explore further the complexity of the decision and verification problems concerning a subset or cardinality minimal explanation, or a preferred CE. Lastly, one could look at how recent advances on abduction for inconsistent KBs can be used to define CEs while allowing conflicts
[
18
]
.

In line with existing literature, addressing privacy aspects is outside the scope of this paper.
Nevertheless, explanations can be generated only from selected subsets of the KB, for instance by restricting the signature
[
35
]
.
Alternatively, sensitive axioms can be filtered or abstracted before explanations are produced. Moreover, our approach relies on symbolic reasoning and complexity-aware algorithms rather than data-intensive learning, and hence requires comparatively modest computational and data resources, aligning with the goals of frugal AI.
