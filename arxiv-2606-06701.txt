1.
Introduction

The study of vanishing coefficients in the series expansions of infinite
q
-products, along with the periodic behavior of their signs, dates back at least to the work of Richmond and Szekeres
[
17
]
. They studied the coefficients of a number of products, including
\{d_{n}\}
defined by

F(q):=\frac{(q^{3},q^{5};q^{8})_{\infty}}{(q,q^{7};q^{8})_{\infty}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{n}q^{n},

where here and subsequently the standard notation
(a_{1},\dots,a_{j};q)_{\infty}:=(a_{1};q)_{\infty}\cdots(a_{j};q)_{\infty}
with

(a;q)_{\infty}:=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}(1-aq^{n})

is employed. Richmond and Szekeres used the Hardy-Ramanujan-Rademacher Circle Method to show that
d_{4n+3}=0
. They found a similar vanishing result for the coefficients of
1/F(q)
. These results were later generalized to infinite families of products in works by Andrews and Bressoud
[
2
]
, Alladi and Gordon
[
1
]
, and the third author of the present paper
[
13
]
. Proofs of these latter results relied on a special case of Ramanujan’s
{}_{1}\psi_{1}
summation formula.

Related results have appeared in a large number of works, including

. The strategies of proof in each case range from the Circle Method to the use of

In this paper, we derive conditions for vanishing that apply to
\{a_{n}\}
defined by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{2bm},q^{p-2bm};q^{2bn},q^{p-2bn};q^{p})_{\infty}}{(q^{p},-q^{bm},-q^{p-bm},-q^{bn},-q^{p-bn};q^{p})_{\infty}^{2}}

\displaystyle=(q^{bm},q^{p-bm},q^{bn},q^{p-bn},q^{p},q^{p};q^{p})_{\infty}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times(q^{p-2bm},q^{2bm+p},q^{p-2bn},q^{2bn+p};q^{2p})_{\infty},

for primes

Recall that the quintuple product is defined by

(1.2)

Q(z,q):=\frac{\langle z^{2};q\rangle_{\infty}}{(-z,-q/z;q)_{\infty}},

where
\langle a;q\rangle_{\infty}:=(a,q/a,q;q)_{\infty}

1.1

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}q^{n}=Q(q^{bm},q^{p})Q(q^{bn},q^{p}).

For usage in what follows, we recall two equivalent versions of the Jacobi triple product identity (see, e.g.,
[

Theorem 1
.

For
|q|<1
and
z\not=0
,

(1.3)

\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-z)^{n}q^{n^{2}}=\langle zq;q^{2}\rangle_{\infty},

and

(1.4)

\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-z)^{n}q^{n(n-1)/2}=\langle z;q\rangle_{\infty}.

We also recall the Quintuple Product Identity referred to above.

Theorem 2

(

.

For

(1.5)

\displaystyle Q(z,q)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}q^{(3n-1)n/2}z^{3n}(1-zq^{n})

\displaystyle=\langle z;q\rangle_{\infty}(qz^{2},q/z^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\langle-qz^{3};q^{3}\rangle_{\infty}-z\langle-q^{2}z^{3};q^{3}\rangle_{\infty}

(m,p)=1

Theorem 3
.

Let
Q(z,q)
be as at (

p\equiv 1\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
be a prime, and

,

be integers such that
p=m^{2}+n^{2}
. Let

be an integer, and let the sequence

be defined by

Q(q^{bm},q^{p})Q(q^{bn},q^{p})=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}a_{t}q^{t}.

Let

w\equiv\bar{2}(m+n)\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,p).

(1)

If
p\equiv 1\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,12)
,
one has

(1.7)

a_{pt+wb}=a_{pt+(w-3b)b}=0\qquad(\text{for all integers $t$}).

(2)

If
p\equiv 5\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,12)
, one has

(1.8)

a_{pt+(1-3b\bar{m})wb}=a_{pt+(1-3b\bar{n})wb}=0\qquad(\text{for all integers $t$}).

In a previous paper
[
8
]
, the present authors determined the
p
-dissection of the infinite product on the left side of (
1.6
) explicitly, and showed that the components of the
p
-dissection corresponding to the arithmetic progressions with vanishing coefficients vanished identically (due to the appearance of infinite products of the form
(1;q^{p^{2}})_{\infty}
). In that paper we also showed that in those arithmetic progressions where the coefficients do not all vanish, the sign of those coefficients was constant, and we determined those signs explicitly. Some combinatorial interpretations of the analytic identities were also given.

In the present paper we give a proof of the vanishing coefficient results in Theorem
3
, which is fundamentally different from the one given in
[
8
]
. This time we work with the product
Q(q^{bm},q^{p})Q(q^{bn},q^{p})(q^{2p};q^{2p})_{\infty}^{2}
(the additional factor
(q^{2p};q^{2p})_{\infty}^{2}
does not affect the vanishing of coefficients in arithmetic progressions modulo
p
). This time we use the Jacobi triple product identity (cf. Theorem
1
) to write

Q(q^{bm},q^{p})Q(q^{bn},q^{p})(q^{2p};q^{2p})_{\infty}^{2}=\langle q^{bm};q^{p}\rangle_{\infty}\langle q^{bn};q^{p}\rangle_{\infty}\langle q^{p-2bm};q^{2p}\rangle_{\infty}\langle q^{p-2bn};q^{2p}\rangle_{\infty}\\
=\sum_{r,s,u,v\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{F_{1}(r,s,u,v)}q^{G_{1}(r,s,u,v)},

where

\displaystyle F_{1}(r,s,u,v)

\displaystyle=r+s+u+v,

\displaystyle G_{1}(r,s,u,v)

\displaystyle=bmu+\frac{1}{2}pu(u-1)+bnv+\frac{1}{2}pv(v-1)

(1.9)

\displaystyle\phantom{adadsa}+r(p-2bm)+pr(r-1)+s(p-2bn)+ps(s-1).

We next restrict the last sum to contain only terms in which the exponent of
q
is congruent to
b\tau
modulo
p
with
\tau
representing one of the four residue classes
w
,
w-3b
,
(1-3b\bar{m})w
,
(1-3b\bar{n})w
that appear in the arithmetic progressions with vanishing coefficients in Theorem
3
. This can be achieved as follows. If

\displaystyle G_{1}(r,s,u,v)

\displaystyle\equiv b\tau\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,p)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow bmu+bnv-2bmr-2bns

\displaystyle\equiv b\tau\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,p)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow mu+nv-2mr-2ns

\displaystyle\equiv\tau\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,p)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow v

\displaystyle\equiv 2mr\bar{n}-mu\bar{n}+\tau\bar{n}+2s\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,p).

Hence if one makes the substitution

(1.10)

v\to 2mr\bar{n}-mu\bar{n}+\tau\bar{n}+2s+pv

in
F_{1}(r,s,u,v)
and
G_{1}(r,s,u,v)
, then the new sum will contain exactly those terms in which the exponent of
q
is congruent to
b\tau
modulo
p
.

Suppose this restricted sum is represented as

\sum_{(r,s,u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}}(-1)^{F_{2}(r,s,u,v)}q^{G_{2}(r,s,u,v)},

where the aim is now to show that this sum is identically zero.

Alternatively, writing

(1.11)

g(r,s,u,v):=\frac{G_{2}(r,s,u,v)-b\tau}{p}

(which corresponds to dividing the previous sum by
q^{b\tau}
and then replacing
q
by
q^{1/p}
), so that the aim now becomes showing that the series

(1.12)

\sum_{(r,s,u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}}(-1)^{f(r,s,u,v)}q^{g(r,s,u,v)}=:\sum_{N}c_{N}q^{N}

vanishes identically (for consistency of appearance we have used
f(r,s,u,v):=F_{2}(r,s,u,v)
). To make the discussion which comes next, which involves matrices and vectors, take up less space, we define

\mathbf{x}:=(r,s,u,v).

The vanishing of the sum (
1.12
) is achieved by showing that for each of the four arithmetic progressions in Theorem
3
there exists a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4\times 4}
and vector
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}
, such that the map

(1.13)

\mathbf{x}\longmapsto\mathbf{x}^{\prime}:=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b},

the matrix
\mathbf{A}
and the vector
\mathbf{b}
have the following properties:

(1.14)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{2}-\mathbf{I}

\displaystyle=0,

(1.15)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{I})\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle=0

(1.16)

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=g(\mathbf{x}),

(1.17)

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv f(\mathbf{x})+1\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,2).

Note that the first two properties show that the map at (
1.13
) is an involution, since

(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})^{\prime}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})+\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{A}^{2}\mathbf{x}+(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{I})\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{x}.

The second pair of properties show that for any integer
N
, the terms that contribute to the term
c_{N}q^{N}
at
(
1.12
) can be grouped together in pairs
((-1)^{f(\mathbf{x})}q^{g(\mathbf{x})},(-1)^{f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}q^{g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})})
of equal exponent in
q
but of opposite sign, so that the entire sum vanishes, giving the result.

Once the matrices
\mathbf{A}
and the corresponding vectors
\mathbf{b}
are found, verifying that the stated properties hold is a straightforward computation (although most easily done with a computer algebra system such as
Mathematica
). Finding the form of the matrices
\mathbf{A}
and the vectors
\mathbf{b}
involved finding these for enough primes and corresponding
\tau
to guess at the general form (which then can be verified, as indicated). We discuss this experimentation below.

2.
Numerical Investigations

Initially, for various primes
p
we considered the series expansion of products of the form
Q(q^{j},q^{p})Q(q^{k},q^{p})=:\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}a_{t}^{(i,j)}q^{t}
, for
1\leq j,k\leq p-1
and looked for arithmetic progressions modulo
p
in which all the coefficients vanished.

It soon became apparent that this vanishing coefficient phenomenon occurred only for primes
p\equiv 1\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
. Further investigation revealed that it occurred for pairs of exponents
(j,k)
of the form
(j,k)=(bm,bn)
, where
p=m^{2}+n^{2}
and
b\in\mathbb{N}
. These investigations suggested that in this situation,
a_{pt+cb^{2}+db}^{(bm,bn)}=0
for all integers
t
and some pair of integers
c
and
d
and also allowed us to guess formulae for
c
and
d
. In particular, for
p\equiv 1\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,12)
one pair was
(c,d)=(0,w)
, where
w=\bar{2}(m+n)\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,p)
.

One method of proof that occurred to us was the “involution” described above at (
1.13
)–(
1.17
). For example, for a given prime
p\equiv 1\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,12)
one finds
m
and
n
such that
p=m^{2}+n^{2}
and
3\mid m
and selects one of the
\tau
found experimentally, such as
\tau=w:=\bar{2}(m+n)
. Then using a computer algebra system such as
Mathematica
one follows through the steps described in the introduction as far as computing
g(\mathbf{x})
as described at (
1.11
).

Next, one sets up a matrix
\mathbf{A}
with entries
a_{i,j}
to be determined, and likewise a vector
\mathbf{b}
with entries
b_{k}
to be determined. One computes
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}:=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}
as at (
1.13
) and then the difference
g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-g(\mathbf{x})
, which we wish to be identically zero. Regarded as a polynomial in
r
,
s
,
u
and
v
, setting the coefficients of this polynomial equal to zero gives a system of fifteen quadratic equations in the
a_{i,j}
and
b_{k}
for which we seek integral solutions.

Each of these equations may have, in some cases, hundreds or even thousands of solutions, so attempting to solve all fifteen equations simultaneously with a single
Solve[ ]
command is not practical, time-wise. Our initial method was to solve the system one equation at a time (with some preliminary testing to determine which equations are most easily solvable), then once a quadratic equation has been solved, each solution is fed back into the system of equations, which has the effect of making some of the quadratic equations linear, and hence easily solvable for one variable in terms of the other variables. One then feeds these solutions into the system of equations, moves on to solve another quadratic equation and repeats the process with the resulting linear equations.

With careful management the method will eventually produce two solutions in a reasonable amount of time, one being the trivial solution (
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{I}
, the identity matrix, and
\mathbf{b}=\bf 0
) and the other being the “involution” solution we sought. However, the method was speeded up when one of us made the observation that
g(\mathbf{x})
may be represented as

(2.1)

g(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{t}}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{t}}\mathbf{x}+c,

for some matrix
\mathbf{Q}
, vector
\mathbf{z}
and integer
c
. Then
g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-g(\mathbf{x})=0
implies that

\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{t}}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{Q},

or that
\mathbf{A}
is in the automorphism group of
\mathbf{Q}
. There are commands, such as
qfauto( )
in PARI/GP, that will compute the generators of the automorphism group. These can be imported back into
Mathematica
and the full automorphism group generated. One then cycles through the full automorphism group, letting
\mathbf{A}
be each of these matrices in turn, giving numerical values for the
a_{i,j}
in the fifteen equations mentioned above, and then the remaining equations solved for the
b_{k}
.

Regardless of the method used, one then generates sufficient numerical data to identify the various sub-cases in which the matrices
\mathbf{A}
and vectors
\mathbf{b}
have different forms, and sufficient data to identify these forms. What follows is a somewhat idealized description of how experimentation led to the formulae for
\mathbf{A}
and

. For convenience, we restate this theorem.

Theorem
3
.

Let
Q(z,q)
be as at (
1.2
), let
p\equiv 1\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
be a prime, let

,

be integers such that

. Let

be an integer, and let the sequence

Let

(1)

If

and

in each of the four cases, verify that the entries are integers, and that the properties listed at (

and let

. As

by assumption, it is evident that one, and only one, of

and

must be equivalent to

(mod

). Thus, suppose that

.

In this case we have

\end{array}\right),

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\frac{m\left(-2n\bar{n}(w-3b)-6b+m+n\right)}{3p}\\

That the various matrix- and vector entries are integers follow from similar considerations to those in

, this time also using

.

4.
Some Examples

We next illustrate the results in Theorem
3
with some examples.

Example 1
.

(1) Let
p=37=6^{2}+1^{2}
(so take
m=6
and
n=1
), let
b>0
, and define the sequence
a_{t}
by

Q(q^{6b},q^{37})Q(q^{b},q^{37})=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}a_{t}q^{t}.

Since
37\equiv 1\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,12)
, we have

w\equiv\bar{2}(m+n)=\bar{2}(6+1)\equiv 22\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,37).

Then for all integers
t
,

a_{37t+22b}=a_{37t+22b-3b^{2}}=0.

(2) Let
p=29=5^{2}+2^{2}
(so take
m=5
and
n=2
), let
b>0
, and define the sequence
a_{t}
by

Q(q^{5b},q^{29})Q(q^{2b},q^{29})=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}a_{t}q^{t}.

Since
29\equiv 5\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,12)
,

\displaystyle w\equiv\bar{2}(m+n)

\displaystyle\equiv 18\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,29),

\displaystyle-3\bar{m}w

\displaystyle\equiv 24\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,29),

\displaystyle-3\bar{n}w

\displaystyle\equiv 2\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,29).

Then for all integers
t
,

a_{29t+24b^{2}+18b}=a_{29t+2b^{2}+18b}=0.

Remark 3
.

For any prime
p\geq 5
with
p=m^{2}+n^{2}
, one has by (
1.5
) that both
Q(q^{bm},q^{p})
and
Q(q^{bn},q^{p})
are
superlacunary
, which Ono and Robins
[
16
]
defined to mean that if either is expanded as a series in
q
, it has the form

\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}d(an^{2}+bn+c)q^{an^{2}+bn+c}

where
a,b,c
are rational and
a>0
(Ono and Robins take
a,b,c
to be integers, since replacing
q
with
q^{N}
for some integer
N
would clear all denominators). The authors point out in
[
16
]
that the product of two superlacunary series is lacunary and hence the product
Q(q^{bm},q^{p})Q(q^{bn},q^{p})
has a series expansion that is lacunary, and almost all the coefficients
a_{t}
in Theorem
3
are zero.

However, multiplying
Q(q^{bm},q^{p})Q(q^{bn},q^{p})
by any function whose series expansion is a series in
q^{p}
will not affect the vanishing of coefficients in arithmetic progressions modulo
p
, so we state the following corollary, which may have more interesting combinatorial implications on special partition functions.

Corollary 1
.

Let
Q(z,q)
be as at (
1.2
), let
p\equiv 1\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
be a prime, and
m>0
,
n>0
be integers such that
p=m^{2}+n^{2}
. Let
b>0
be an integer, let

w\equiv\bar{2}(m+n)\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,p),

and let the sequence
c_{t}
be defined by

(4.1)

\frac{Q(q^{bm},q^{p})Q(q^{bn},q^{p})}{(q^{p};q^{p})_{\infty}^{2}}=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}c_{t}q^{t}.

(1)

If
p\equiv 1\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,12)
, then for all integers
t
one has

(4.2)

c_{pt+bw}=c_{pt+b(w-3b)}=0.

(2)

If
p\equiv 5\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,12)
, then for all integers
t
one has

(4.3)

c_{pt+bw(1-3b\bar{m})}=c_{pt+bw(1-3b\bar{n})}=0.

As an application of Corollary
1
, we modify the products in Example
1
and consider just the case
b=1
(although the results translate directly for an arbitrary positive integer
b
).

Example 2
.

(1) Let the sequence
a_{t}
be defined by

\frac{Q(q,q^{37})Q(q^{6},q^{37})}{(q^{37};q^{37})_{\infty}^{2}}=(q,q^{6},q^{31},q^{26};q^{37})_{\infty}(q^{25},q^{35},q^{39},q^{49};q^{74})_{\infty}=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}a_{t}q^{t}.

Then for all integers
t
, one has

a_{37t+22}=a_{37t+19}=0.

(2) Let the sequence
a_{t}
be defined by

\frac{Q(q^{2},q^{29})Q(q^{5},q^{29})}{(q^{29};q^{29})_{\infty}^{2}}=(q^{2},q^{5},q^{24},q^{27};q^{29})_{\infty}(q^{19},q^{25},q^{33},q^{39};q^{58})_{\infty}=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}a_{t}q^{t}.

Then for all integers
t
, one has

a_{29t+42}=a_{29t+20}=0.

Remark 4
.

Unlike the coefficients in the series expansions in Example
1
, most coefficients in the series expansions in Example
2
are non-zero. Indeed it follows from a general result proven in
[
8
]
that apart from the two arithmetic progressions where coefficients vanish, the coefficients in the other arithmetic progressions are, apart from a few possible zero coefficients near the beginning, eventually either all positive or all negative, tending to either
+\infty
or
-\infty
.

5.
Concluding Remarks

Computer investigations suggest that vanishing coefficient results similar to those proven in the present paper for products of two quintuple products also hold for products of three- and four quintuple products.

For example, if the sequence
\{a_{n}\}
is defined by

Q(q,q^{31})Q(q^{5},q^{31})Q(q^{6},q^{31})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}q^{n},

then experiment suggests that

a_{31t+6}=a_{31t+16}=a_{31t+18}=0.

Similarly, if the sequence
\{b_{n}\}
is defined by

Q(q,q^{41})Q(q^{4},q^{41})Q(q^{5},q^{41})Q(q^{9},q^{41})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}q^{n},

then it appears that

b_{41t+21}=b_{41t+25}=b_{41t+26}=b_{41t+30}=0.

We will examine these phenomena in a subsequent paper.
