Increasing Utilization of Advanced Practice Providers in Spine Surgery from 2013 to 2022: A Medicare Part B Analysis
Authors: Mark Miller, Matthew Meade, Maneesha Palakurthi, Ruchir Nanavati, Nithin Gupta, William T. DiCiurcio, Omar Sbaih, Matthew B. Sherman, Christopher Kepler, Gregory Schroeder, Barrett Woods
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:In modern healthcare systems, the role of advanced practice providers (APPs), including nurse practitioners (NP) and physician assistants (PA), has increased in many settings, in step with the increased demand for healthcare services. The purpose of the present investigation was to evaluate trends in the prevalence, reimbursement, and characteristics of care provided by APPs in the treatment of spinal disorders in the United States from 2013 to 2022.Methods:A retrospective cohort study using the publicly available Medicare Provider Utilization and Payment database from the Centers for Medicare and Medicare Services was conducted on diagnostic, procedural, and therapeutic services provided by advanced practice providers from 2013 to 2022. Provider-type and billing codes were used to identify healthcare professionals providing care for spine patients, such as ordering radiographs, assisting in surgical procedures, or performing office visits. Trends were analyzed using linear regression modeling with projections generated through 2030.Results:Over the study period, the number of surgeons involved in spine care decreased from 3953 (71.2%) in 2013 to 3951 (66.7%) in 2022. The number of APPs who billed for the care of spine patients increased 23.3% from 1600 in 2013 to 1974 in 2022. The most common services billed for by APPs included radiographs and office visits. There were increased odds (OR: 1.20, 95% CI, 1.03-1.41, P < 0.001) of a spine provider in rural areas being an APP versus spine surgeon. Forecasting analysis for 2030 demonstrated continuation of these trends regarding the number of providers, services, and geographic distribution.Discussion:Increased utilization of APPs in the care of patients with spinal pathology is likely to continue with the growing prevalence of patients with spinal disorders.

The role of advanced practice providers, including physician assistants (PAs) and nurse practitioners (NPs), has expanded markedly over the past decade in the field of spine care, driven by increased workforce demands, supportive legislation, and evidence of improved patient outcomes.1 The 10-year job outlook predicts widespread growth of NPs at 46% and PAs at 28%, compared with just 3% for physicians.2 Especially in health professional shortage areas such as the midwestern United States, it is found that APPs practice with full authority and limited oversight, with recent legislation seeking to broaden the scope of APP practices.3 Multiple studies report that the institution of APPs contributes to improved patient care, including reduced length-of-stay, improved pain scores, and increased patient satisfaction.4,5

As we approach the inflection where “Baby Boomers” become sexagenarians and septuagenarians and reach retirement age, there is a growing concern for the future shortage of spine surgeons available to provide care to this growing population, particularly in rural communities within the United States.6 Previous literature has described the increased utilization of APPs to bridge the relatively wider gap in care in rural settings; however, a paucity of this literature has focused on spine-specific providers.7 The scope of practice of the APP in spine care varies depending on the care setting, needs of the healthcare team, and local governance. This may include assisting in the operating room, triaging consultations, performing physical examinations, ordering diagnostic tests, and conducting office visits.8 With adequate training, APPs are often skilled diagnosticians and can readily distinguish between surgical and nonsurgical conditions.9 In rural settings where there exists a relative scarcity of spine providers, APPs may serve an integral role in increasing access to care.

Also, in today's healthcare climate, practices are increasingly challenged to maintain financial viability amid declining reimbursement rates and shifting payment models.10 Standard fee-for-service models are being replaced with value-based care initiatives that aim to emphasize quality, efficiency, and patient outcomes.11 Given this changing landscape, spine practices, among others, are compelled to adapt by leveraging cost-effective care. Further understanding of how APPs are integrated into the spine practice model, including how their roles affect delivery of services and reimbursement, is critical for spine practices striving to maintain economic stability while adapting to new value-based care principles.

Although the role of APPs is well established in various orthopaedic subspecialties, there is a limited understanding of how their utilization specifically influences spine care delivery on a national scale. Prior studies have focused on their impact in general orthopaedics or primary care settings, with minimal data evaluation of the distribution, clinical roles, and financial impact of APPs within spine practices.4,5,7 Filling this gap in knowledge can assist in the ability of healthcare policymakers and administrators in making informed decisions regarding planning effective workforces and organizing reimbursements to optimize value in spine care.

The purpose of the present investigation is to evaluate trends in the prevalence, reimbursement, and characteristics of care provided by APPs in the treatment of the spine in the United States over the past decade. With appropriate training, advanced practice providers (APPs) may serve an integral role in the evolving landscape of spine care. Furthermore, their involvement has the potential to improve access to care in generally underserved and rural regions.

Methods

A retrospective cohort analysis was done on services provided by APPs from 2013 to 2022, using the publicly available Medicare Provider Utilization and Payment database from the Centers for Medicare and Medicare Services (CMS).12 This public data set contains information on services rendered and procedures performed upon Medicare Part B beneficiaries by providers who submit claims to CMS, representing more than one trillion dollars and 32% of healthcare spending.13 The data set provides information on the billing provider's credentials, geographic location, sex, and submitted billing claims. Rural classification was determined using Rural–Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes based on ZIP code–level population data, with areas representing populations below 50,000 categorized as rural. Alternatively, billing providers were classified as urban if they did not meet these criteria.

Spine surgeons were identified as those with credentials of “Doctor of Medicine,” “Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine,” or recognized equivalents with a specialty in “Orthopedic Surgery” or “Neurosurgery.” Only those surgeons who billed for the 30 most common spine procedures, such as single-level posterior lumbar arthrodesis (22612), or laminectomy, facetectomy, and foraminotomy performed on a single lumbar vertebral segment (63047), based on Common Procedural Terminology(CPT) Codes from previous literature, were included.14-16 As APPs do not bill with an associated specialty, APPs involved in the care of spine patients were identified by provider type as those with credentials of “Nurse Practitioner (NP)” or “Physician Assistant (PA)” who billed for the aforementioned CPT codes relevant to the care of spinal pathologies, as APPs are able to bill for assisting in various surgical procedures.17 Because Medicare billing data do not specify supervision arrangements, it was not possible to determine whether services were billed independently or under “incident-to” supervision models. Therefore, the present analysis reflects only services billed directly by APP providers in the Medicare data set. To verify this cohort, an internet search was done using data from a random selection of 100 APPs (50 NPs, 50 PAs) in the 2022 cohort to confirm their affiliation with either an orthopaedic spine or neurosurgical practice; 49 (98%) of PAs and 46 NP's(92%) were affiliated with an orthopaedic spine or neurosurgical practice, in accordance with previous literature describing similar methodology in the context of upper extremity care.18 Although a small proportion of APPs could not be definitively linked to a spine practice, the observed misclassification rate was low (<10%). Given the large sample size and consistent year-to-year trends, this degree of potential misclassification is unlikely to materially alter the observed temporal patterns. However, this limitation is acknowledged in the discussion.

Following cohort confirmation, services billed for by spine surgeons and associated APPs were collected. Average charges and standardized reimbursements for each service were gathered to determine reimbursement ratios. In addition, the number of office visits done by surgeons and APPs was tabulated annually. To analyze trends over time, linear regression models with interaction terms (year × provider or year × group) were used for various dependent outcomes. Using these models, predictions were generated for the years 2025 through 2030. For categorical comparisons, Chi-square tests were conducted to evaluate the associations between geographic area and provider type, as well as between provider sex and provider type. Odds ratios were also calculated to assess the strength of these associations. Significance was defined as a P value less than 0.05. All statistical analyses were done using R Studio (Version 4.5.0, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Provider Demographics

From 2013 to 2022, the number of spine providers increased from 5553 in 2013 to 5925 in 2022. The number of surgeons involved in spine care changed from 3953 (71.2%) in 2013 to 3951 (66.7%) in 2022. The number of neurosurgeons changed from 2142(38.5%) in 2013 to 2096(35.4%) in 2022. The number of orthopaedic spine surgeons changed from 1811(32.6%) in 2013 to 1855(31.3%) in 2022. APPs who billed for the care of spine patients increased by 23.3% from 1600(28.8%) in 2013 to 1974(33.3%) in 2022. In addition, NPs increased from 239 (4.3%) in 2013 to 389 (6.6%) in 2022. PAs grew from 1361 (24.5%) in 2013 to 1585 (26.8%) in 2022 (Table 1, Figure 1).

Interaction regression analyses from 2013 to 2022 demonstrated that proportions of the four provider types markedly changed over time. The proportion of spine care providers who were neurosurgeons decreased by 0.4%, and orthopaedic surgeons decreased by 0.2% each year (P < 0.001). NPs and PAs increased annually by 0.3% and 0.2%, respectively (P < 0.001). APPs as a whole increased by 0.5% annually, with surgeons decreasing by 0.5% (P < 0.001). Assuming continuation of the observed trend, it is estimated that the number of APPs billing for the care of spine patients may grow by 13.8% to 37.92% by 2030 (Table 1, Figure 1).

Billing Practices

From 2013 to 2022, the percentage of total spine care services provided by APPs rose from 10.5% to 17.2%. This trend, which is expected to continue, predicts 0.7% (P < 0.001, 95% CI, 0.6-0.7%) of total spine services shifting from surgeons to APPs annually. The most common codes billed for by APPs included assisting in laminotomy/laminectomy, established patient outpatient visits of 15 minutes, and insertion of a device during spinal arthrodesis (Table 2, Figure 2).

The proportion of outpatient office visits for spine care provided by APPs more than doubled from 7.2% in 2013 to 14.5% in 2022. Regression predicts a continuing increase of 0.8% (P < 0.001; 95% CI, 0.7-0.9%) annually of office visits provided by APPs and a corresponding decrease by surgeons. Practice Setting

Notable differences were observed between the split of APPs and surgeons providing care in rural areas. Chi-square analyses demonstrated a notable difference between the overall numbers of surgeons and APPs providing rural care from 2013 to 2022 (P = 0.022). A spine provider in a rural area has 1.20 (95% CI, 1.03-1.41) times higher odds of being an APP than a surgeon. The regression estimates that in rural areas, the proportion of surgeons is dropping by 1.6% yearly (P < 0.001; CI 95%, 1.1-2.0%), whereas in nonrural areas, the estimate is 0.5% (P < 0.001; CI 95%, 0.3-0.6%) (Table 4, Figure 3).

Discussion

The present investigation describes the increasing role of APPs, including physician assistants and nurse practitioners, in the delivery of spine care within the Medicare population from 2013 to 2022. This retrospective cohort analysis of the publicly available Medicare Provider Utilization and Payment database from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) demonstrates that the proportion of spine care delivered by APPs markedly increased, both in terms of providers and services rendered. As of 2022, within this data set, APPs comprised more than one-third of all spine care providers. Over the period of this study, the number of spine surgeons increased marginally, while the proportion of services rendered by APPs rose substantially. The trends highlighted in this study underscore the emerging shift in spine care workforce and the likelihood that APP-driven spine care will continue to expand.

This increasing role of APPs may represent one mechanism by which healthcare systems respond to projected workforce pressures, as approximately 40,000 APPs enter the workforce each year.19,20 Because of the time constraints imposed by surgical demands of a spine surgeon, clinic availability is typically limited to 1 to 2 days a week. The growing presence of APPs serves as a strategic response to current and projected shortages of spine surgeons in the outpatient setting. In a recent survey of patient perceptions within a neurosurgical clinic, patients preferred to see an APP in a “timely manner” rather than seeing the surgeon at a later date.21 Furthermore, several investigations have demonstrated successful utilization of APPs in screening back pain patients, identifying those likely to require surgical intervention, and reducing the number of nonsurgical referrals and decreasing surgical wait times.22,23 These findings were recently replicated in a study of 1398 patients with elective spine pathology, which demonstrated APPs familiar with spine pathology to have the highest surgical conversion rate when referring to a small orthopaedic spine surgery group.8

The present investigation in addition demonstrated that the proportion of APPs to surgeons providing spine care in rural areas has been especially affected, as rural APPs are predicted to continue to increase in their proportion of spine providers, with a corresponding decrease in rural spine surgeons. Although the odds ratio was statistically significant, the magnitude of the effect was modest, suggesting gradual rather than dramatic differences in rural provider composition. This is especially important to note, as previous investigations have discovered higher rates of spinal disease burden and spine surgery procedures in rural communities.24 The aging US population is already outpacing the number of spine surgeons, many of whom opt to practice in suburban/urban settings due to financial incentives, previous location of training, and established healthcare infrastructure.25-27 It has been proposed that well-trained APPs can improve access to care in rural areas through initial triaging, consulting/traveling clinics, integration of telehealth, and acting as “physician extenders.”25,28-30 With this, the rural setting represents a unique opportunity for APPs to have greater autonomy and involvement in procedures, both of which have been associated with increased job satisfaction.31 For example, in June of 2025, Oklahoma legislation approved provisions to remove physician assistant-physician practice agreements, along with other measures to expand APP prescriptive authority for schedule three through five prescriptions once a PA completes 6240 clinical hours to promote access for patients in rural and underserved areas.32 Although APPs may reduce barriers to care for spinal pathology, Hecht et al29 demonstrated that it is still quite common for APPs to practice in the vicinity of an orthopaedic surgeon. Even with the addition of formalized training for APPs to manage nonsurgical care, the broader issue of decreasing rates of rural spine surgeons remains.33 Therefore, efforts must be made to alleviate barriers to surgical spine care in rural areas by ultimately incentivizing spine surgeons to practice in these locations.

Given the robust rise in APP-centric care, it is important to understand the implications that this may have for the healthcare enterprise and patients alike. Although there is a clear role for APPs in the spine clinic, it is important to note that 99% of APPs report receiving “on-the-job training” with only 14 formal orthopaedic postgraduate programs available as of 2020.34 Given the aforementioned benefits of APPs and their robust integration into the care team, clarity with regard to defined roles of APPs within the spine-care team is also necessary to promote effective training to provide high-quality patient care, given the lack of widespread, formal, postgraduate training opportunities. As APPs have cited career development, autonomy, and procedural involvement as factors for improved retention/satisfaction, there may be a larger role for an increased number of formalized postgraduate subspecialty training programs.35 Although there is no clear standardized solution, some practices have employed formalized onboarding and educational programs, which APPs must pass through surgeon oversight, before becoming involved in clinical management.35 A recent review by Louie et al described their overwhelmingly positive experience at a tertiary academic center with the evolving role of spine APPs, beyond operating room and clinic assistance, to include performing clinical research, leading multidisciplinary conferences, assisting with quality improvement initiatives, as well as holding positions on societal leadership committees. Informal programs such as these further highlight the incredible asset that APPs are to the healthcare team and patients alike, while understanding that the ultimate surgical decision making falls upon the surgeon. Thus, with increasing numbers of APPs involved in spine care, the development and integration of formal training programs at the institutional and national level may more appropriately define the role and subsequent integration of APPs into the spine team.

The present study is not without limitations. First, the study included only the available CMS data from Medicare Part B beneficiaries, which may limit the generalizability of findings to those patients using private insurance. With this, APPs not included in the Medicare database may have been excluded, thus potentially underestimating their involvement in patient care. Charges represent submitted provider charges recorded in the Medicare data set, while reimbursements reflect standardized Medicare allowed payments. Because APPs and surgeons may bill for different mixes of services, reimbursement-to-charge ratios should be interpreted cautiously and may partially reflect differences in service type rather than provider efficiency alone. However, Medicare is the largest provider in the United States, and the utilization of these data has been the basis for similar studies performed in alternative surgical subspecialties.18 In addition, spine surgeons were identified as those performing the 30 most-common spine CPT codes, which may have excluded surgeons only performing lower volume spine procedures. With this, billing practices and patterns may differ among practice settings, and information concerning the APP's role in procedures that they billed for was unavailable. Although linear extrapolations of data were done in attempt to project future workforce conditions, workforce changes are often nonlinear and policy dependent. In addition, it is possible that some APPs may not have been associated with an orthopaedic spine or neurosurgical practice, despite our initial observation demonstrating more than 90% association rates. Possible billing or coding errors may also have been present, thus affecting reimbursement rates.

Conclusion

With projected shortages in the number of physicians caring for the spine, understanding the increasing role of APPs in the treatment of spinal pathologies is crucial to properly addressing a growing burden of spine-related disease. The results of this study suggest that the proportion of spine care delivered by surgeons may decrease relative to APPs as spine-related APPs increase, a trend that is notably seen in rural areas. As the APP becomes an integral part in removing barriers to access for care of spine disease, further work must be done to clearly define the scope of practice, understand the impact on resident education, and find the most effective methods for the implementation of APPs.
