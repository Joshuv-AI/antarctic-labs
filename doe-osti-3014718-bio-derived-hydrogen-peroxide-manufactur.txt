Executive Summary of CRADA Work:
The Perlumi project team worked on developing a novel route to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)
using methanol as the starting material. Global H2O2 annual production is around 4.5 billion
kilos, and is valued around $2.5 billion dollars. The pulp and paper industry specifically buys
about $1.5 billion of H2O2 in aggregate as a green alternative to chlorine containing compounds
for pulp and paper bleaching.

Perlumi’s novel H2O2 production system has many technical advantages over the
incumbent anthraquinone process. First, the anthraquinone process’s ultimate feedstock is fossil
natural gas, contributing to CO2 emissions. The anthraquinone process requires two gas and
liquid mixing steps, which are inherently energy intensive. And finally, the
anthraquinone process requires large central facilities to achieve economies of scale, which
means the H2O2 must be shipped thousands of miles to remote pulp and paper mills where it is
used for bleaching. Specialized rail cars are required to ship H2O2 safely, and the rail cars must
be returned empty (they can’t transport pulp and paper), adding additional costs and CO2
emissions for H2O2 for pulp and paper bleaching.

Summary of Research Results:
This project tested the Perlumi company’s process that uses an enzyme cascade to convert one
methanol, one water, and three molecular oxygen into three hydrogen peroxide and one CO2. The
system was designed from the ground up to avoid many problems in vitro enzyme systems
typically have. The process does not require any soluble cofactors (e.g., ATP, or NADH). This is
important because soluble cofactors are difficult to purify from products, are expensive to
purchase, can degrade quickly, and frequently dominate the process costs. Another advantage of
the new H2O2 technology is that it only requires one gas and liquid mixing step. Purification costs
are minimized because CO2 removes itself from the reaction vessel and H2O2 is usually sold as a
50/50 mixture with H2O because pure H2O2 is dangerous to ship and store. The nature of the
enzyme cascade basically limits the potential for unwanted side reactions. And finally, around
90% of global pulp and paper is produced in Kraft style mills, which produce a high
concentration biogenic methanol waste stream. Today, this waste stream is burned for low value
heat. The goal was to place H2O2 production system at Kraft pulp and paper mills enabling
upgrading the biogenic methanol into hydrogen peroxide on site for bleaching. Because the
methanol itself originally came from trees, (and therefore ultimately from atmospheric CO2), the
process was modeled to have a much lower emissions compared to the anthraquinone process. ​

However, during the course of this project, the participant conducted numerous interviews with
pulp and paper mills directly to see how they would value such a technology. It quickly became
clear that optimizing both costs and greenhouse gas emissions from the pulp bleaching process,
and upstream hydrogen peroxide production, was not very interesting to the pulp and paper mills.
They were much more interested in ways to increase the volume of paper moving through a mill,
and much less interested in reducing H2O2 costs, especially with unproven technology. Based on
these learnings, future work will be focused on pivoting the Perlumi technologies to address
alternate use cases. The program was invaluable in supporting the innovation team through these
early stages of technology development.
