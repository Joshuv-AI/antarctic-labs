Impact of ABO Blood System on Immunogenicity and Vaccine Efficacy of COVID‐19 Booster Vaccination—A Population‐Based Study of 3066 Individuals
Authors: Daniela Sieghart, Galateja Jordakieva, Caroline Dürrschmid, Claudia A Hana, Lan Vi Tran, Leonhard X Heinz, Helmuth Haslacher, Markus Zlesak, Valentin Marzoner, Philip Nasztl, Giulia Piccini, Alessandro Manenti, Emanuele Montomoli, Anselm Jorda, Clemens Fedrizzi, Timothy Hasenoehrl, Andrej Zdravkovic, Karolina Anderle, Ursula Wiedermann, Susanne Drapalik, Helmut Steinbrecher, Felix Bergmann, Christa Firbas, Barbara Wagner, Margherita Leonardi, Giulio Pierleoni, Matilde Ballini, Linda Benincasa, Serena Marchi, Claudia Maria Trombetta, Thomas Perkmann, Richard Crevenna, Markus Zeitlinger, Michael Bonelli, Daniel Aletaha, Helga Radner

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundABO blood group has been associated with SARS‐CoV‐2 susceptibility. Little data exist regarding the impact of ABO and Rhesus (Rh[D]) type on breakthrough infections and antibody responses following SARS‐CoV‐2 booster vaccination.MethodsThis multicenter, population‐based cohort study includes individuals ≥ 18 years who received a booster vaccination against SARS‐CoV‐2. Antibody levels against SARS‐CoV‐2 receptor binding domain (RBD) and neutralizing antibodies against wild‐type (WT) virus and Omicron variant were assessed at baseline, after 4 weeks and after 6 months. At 6 months follow‐up, self‐reported ABO and Rh(D) type, time to, and severity of breakthrough infections were collected.ResultsIn total, 3066 participants (mean age 49 [35–59], 62% female) were included in multivariable regression models, showing no association of anti‐RBD and neutralizing antibodies against wild‐type and Omicron variant with ABO or Rh(D) 4 weeks after booster vaccination. Time‐dependent Cox regression analysis showed significantly lower rates of breakthrough infections in patients with AB (hazard ratio [HR] 0.67 [0.50–0.90]; p = 0.008) compared to O, even after adjustment for relevant covariates and a trend for blood group B (HR 0.82 [0.66–1.01]; p = 0.06) compared to O; there were no associations between Rh(D) and breakthrough infection rates or between observed (asymptomatic to moderate) COVID‐19 symptom severity and ABO or Rh(D) blood group.ConclusionsABO or Rh(D) blood group was not associated with COVID‐19 severity or antibody responses following SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccination. Breakthrough infections were least common in blood groups AB and B. Although our data do not support previously discussed protective effects of anti‐A/B/Rh(D) agglutinins, differences in ABO group appear relevant for Omicron transmission.Keywords: break‐through infections, COVID‐19, mRNA vaccine, omicron type, SARS‐CoV‐2

Introduction

The ABO blood group system [1, 2], determined by polymorphisms at the 9q34.2 gene locus [3], involves distinct surface antigens on erythrocytes and tissues, for example, respiratory tract mucosa [4]. Associations between infections risk and ABO blood group have been reported for several infectious diseases [5]. The observed impact of ABO type on host susceptibility appears to be conveyed through a variety of mechanisms, for example, by ABO specific antigens acting as pathogen (co)receptors [1] and by regulation of immune reactions through blood group specific antibody responses [6]; anti‐ABO antibodies (agglutinins) against foreign blood group antigens can induce complement system‐mediated lytic activities [7]. ABO‐related immune reactions can be triggered in response to enveloped viruses' surfaces bearing blood group‐like carbohydrate antigen patterns, acquired from their previous host cell by glycosylation [8]. Further, studies have shown a varied intensity of immune responses to vaccines among ABO blood types, with specific genetic variations linked to differences in antibody responses [9] and vaccine efficacy [10].

Over the past few years, ABO blood group was repeatedly linked with SARS‐CoV‐2 infection risk. Particularly blood group O was commonly associated with lower infection risk, whereas a higher risk observed in blood type A [2, 11]. Anti‐A antibodies presence in blood type O plasma is thought to interfere with the Spike protein/ACE2‐dependent cell adhesion, potentially inhibiting SARS‐CoV‐2 entry [12]. However, data on ABO and Rh(D) impact on COVID‐19 severity and antibody responses to vaccination are limited and often conflicting. Whereas higher antibody responses were associated with blood types A and O appeared to enhance antibody persistency in certain COVID‐19 vaccine regimens [13], another study found no differences in reactogenicity or side effects from mRNA COVID‐19 vaccines, highlighting the complexity of immune responses [14]. ABO blood type appears to impact vaccination outcomes depending on disease and vaccine type. Whereas significant associations are reported for some vaccines (e.g., Hepatitis B) [9], others show no correlation (e.g., cholera) [15]. These findings might reflect a complex interplay between individual factors and vaccine immunogenicity, warranting further research to optimize vaccine strategies.

The aim of this study was to fill these gaps by assessing ABO blood group and Rh(D) impact on antibody responses to SARS‐CoV‐2 booster vaccination, infection susceptibility, and symptom severity during the Omicron variant period. By analyzing a large, multicenter cohort, this research provides a comprehensive understanding of these relationships, informing future vaccination strategies and public health policies.

Methods

Study Design and Population

We conducted a prospective, real‐world, cohort study (Eudra‐CT: 2021‐005094‐28; local ethics EK 1954/2021) enrolling 5000 participants at two centralized vaccination facilities in Austria as described previously [16, 17]. Adults (≥ 18 years) who had received primary immunization against COVID‐19 at least 4 months ago and were either boosted with BNT162b2 (30 μg) or mRNA‐1273 (50 μg) were included in this study between October 28, 2021, and January 21, 2022 [16, 17]. The study did not influence the choice of vaccination. Participants were followed up to 6 months after booster vaccination until June 30, 2022. At the final study visit, information on ABO blood group and Rhesus type was collected via questionnaire. Complete data on vaccination, follow‐up, and blood group status were available for 3066 participants, who comprised the final study cohort.

Data Collection

Participants were evaluated longitudinally with predefined assessments at the time of booster vaccination, 4 weeks thereafter, and at 6 months of follow‐up including demographics, medication use, and life‐style (smoking) [16, 17]. At each timepoint, serum samples were collected to detect antireceptor‐binding domain (anti‐RBD)‐antibodies and nucleocapsid (NC)‐antibodies (total population). In a subgroup of 723 participants, neutralizing antibodies against Wild‐type (WT) and Omicron variants were measured. Self‐reported data on health status, medication use, smoking behavior, prior SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, and vaccination history were obtained using standardized questionnaires [16]. At the Month 6 visit, the presence, date, and severity of COVID‐19 breakthrough infection were collected via questionnaire. To assure the reliability of self‐reported breakthrough infections, in a subgroup of > 60% of participants information was validated through official COVID‐19 test certificates by study staff. In addition, seroconversion or a significant increase of NC‐antibodies from Week 4 to Month 6 were considered breakthrough infections, accounting for asymptomatic or undiagnosed cases. Breakthrough infection was defined as participant‐reported or serologically detected SARS‐CoV‐2 infection occurring ≥ 4 weeks after booster vaccination. The study participants self‐reported the severity of symptoms using a numeric rating scale (0–10).

Serological Measurements

To detect anti‐NC antibodies due to previous SARS‐CoV‐2 infections, the qualitative Elecsys anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 assay was used [18]. Anti‐spike RBD antibody concentrations were quantified using Elecsys immunoassay (Roche Diagnostics) [19], verified for linearity by the manufacturer, with a quantitation range between 0.4 and 25,000 BAU/mL. To increase the upper limit of detection, stepwise dilution was performed (at 2500, 25,000, and 75,000 BAU/mL). All serological analyses were conducted within the certified infrastructure of the MedUni Wien Biobank [20] using automated immunoassay platforms (cobas e801 analyzer, Roche Diagnostics, Rotkreuz, Switzerland).

Neutralizing antibodies were measured at baseline and Week 4. Neutralizing activity was assessed using live‐virus assays, with titers expressed at the level associated with 50% inhibition in vitro, following established protocols [21]. The assessment was performed at VisMederi Srl (Siena, Italy).

Statistical Analysis

The analyses included 3066 participants who (1) had no prior SARS‐CoV‐2 infection at baseline, as determined by self‐report and absence of anti‐NC antibodies, and (2) for whom self‐reported ABO blood group data were available [17]. Given this sample size and four blood groups/two Rh types, the study is adequately powered to detect small to moderate differences in antibody levels and moderate differences in infection risk between blood groups.

Participant characteristics and immunogenicity data (antibody concentrations) were summarized using the mean with standard deviation (SD), median with interquartile range (IQR), geometric mean (GM) with corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI), or percentages, as appropriate. Results were presented for the overall study cohort and stratified according to ABO blood group [17]. Baseline characteristics were compared across blood groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables. Univariable analyses were conducted using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test and the Kruskal–Wallis test for nonnormally distributed variables. Associations between ABO blood group and anti‐RBD antibody concentrations or neutralizing antibody titers were assessed using multivariable linear regression models, with antibody measures specified as dependent variables. Models were adjusted for vaccination regimen, age, interval between primary and booster vaccination, and sex [17]. Due to right‐skewed distributions, antibody concentrations were analyzed on the logarithmic scale. Univariate differences in vaccine efficacy across different ABO‐blood groups were analyzed by comparing the frequency of breakthrough infections using chi‐square tests. Time‐to‐event analyses were visualized using survival curves, with group differences evaluated by rank‐based testing to analyze differences in time‐to‐incident breakthrough infection across different ABO‐blood groups. A multivariable Cox‐proportional hazard model was used to estimate the hazard of incident breakthrough infections depending on ABO‐blood group, adjusted for relevant covariates identified by literature (age, gender, vaccination strategy [heterologous: primary vaccination strategy with two doses of vector‐based vaccine (ChAdOx1) and booster vaccination with mRNA vaccine (BNT162b2 or mRNA‐1273) and homologous: primary vaccination with two doses of mRNA vaccine (BNT162b2 or mRNA‐1273) and booster with mRNA vaccine (BNT162b2 or mRNA‐1273)] as well as time between primary and booster vaccination) [16]. Entry date for study participants was the date of their booster vaccination, and exit date was the date of COVID‐19 infection. Participants without breakthrough infection were censored at the date of their follow‐up visit. Statistical analysis was performed using BlueSky Statistics 2024, GraphPad Prism 6 and IBM SPSS statistics, Version 28.

Results

A total of n = 4977 participants aged ≥ 18 years participated in the study; after excluding individuals with a history of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection and those lost to follow‐up, 3066 participants with complete information on the main study variables including ABO blood group and Rh(D) were available for analyses (Figure 1). The median age (IQR) of study participants was 49 (35–59) years, and 62% were female. The baseline characteristics of study participants stratified into the ABO blood group showed no statistically significant intergroup differences (Table 1). Likewise, no significant differences were observed in demographic characteristics across ABO blood groups. In contrast, the incidence of breakthrough SARS‐CoV‐2 infection differed significantly between groups and was therefore investigated in subsequent analyses.

Association Between Humoral Immune Response to Booster Vaccination and ABO Blood Group

No significant difference in anti‐RBD antibody levels 4 weeks after booster vaccination was found between ABO blood group (p = 0.74; Figure 2A) by Kruskal–Wallis test. In addition, Rh(D)+ participants showed similar levels of anti‐RBD antibodies (p = 0.7658) compared to Rh(D)− participants (Figure 2B) in Mann–Whitney test. In a subgroup of 723 patients, neutralizing anti‐RBD antibodies against WT and Omicron variants were measured. The neutralizing antibody level against WT (Figure 2C) and Omicron (Figure 2D) developed 4 weeks after the booster vaccination was similar across all ABO groups and Rh(D)+/Rh(D)− individuals (data not shown).

Anti‐RBD antibody levels after booster vaccination (4 weeks) among different ABO blood groups (A) and Rhesus (Rh[D]) type (B). Neutralization AB levels against (C) wild‐type (WT) and (D) Omicron and the ABO blood groups 4 weeks after booster vaccination. Group comparison was performed using Kruskal–Wallis test for independent variables. A p value of < 0.5 was considered significant.

The impact of ABO blood group and Rh(D) on anti‐RBD antibody levels was further analyzed in a multivariable linear regression model adjusted for vaccination strategy, age, time between primary and booster vaccination, and sex. There was no significant association between ABO blood groups and Rh(D) with anti‐RBD antibodies 4 weeks after booster vaccination. Factors with considerable impact on anti‐RBD antibodies identified were age (p < 0.001), antibody levels at baseline (p < 0.001), and days between primary and booster vaccination (p < 0.001; Table S1).

Association of Breakthrough Infection and ABO Blood Group

In total, 1092 (35.6%) participants reported a breakthrough infection within 6 months after booster vaccination with a statistically significant difference (p = 0.0027) of the frequencies of COVID‐19 breakthrough infections across ABO blood groups: The proportion of breakthrough infections was lowest in participants with blood groups B (126/420; 30%) and AB (59/210; 28%) compared to blood groups O (434/1182; 36.7%) and A (473/1254; 37.7%). Regarding Rhesus type, Rh(D)+ participants showed higher numbers of breakthrough infections (857/2216, 38.7%) compared to Rh(D)− participants (168/491, 34.2%).

Univariate time to event analyses showed a significantly higher probability of break‐through infections in subjects with blood groups O and A compared to subjects with blood groups B and AB (log‐rank p < 0.01; Figure 3).

Time to event analysis visualizing significant differences in probability of breakthrough infections between patients with blood groups O and A compared to B and AB.

Using a multivariate Cox regression analysis, we found a significantly lower adjusted hazard ratio (HR) for incident breakthrough infections for blood group AB (HR 0.67 [0.50–0.89]) and a trend towards lower HR for blood group B (HR 0.82 [0.66–1.00]) compared to blood group O. The Rhesus type was not significantly associated with the hazard of incident breakthrough infections, whereas age and anti‐RBD antibody levels 4 weeks after booster vaccination were found to be significant (Figure 4).

Hazard ratios (HR) of incident breakthrough infection within 6 months after booster vaccination against SARS‐CoV‐2 obtained via multivariate Cox‐proportional models. Differences in HR were found for different ABO blood groups but not the Rhesus type. The model was adjusted for age, gender, vaccination strategy (homologous vs. heterologous), time between primary and booster vaccination, and anti‐RBD antibody levels at week four (Anti‐RBD AB Week 4).

Association of ABO‐Blood Groups and Severity of Breakthrough Infection

Out of 1027 breakthrough infections, no severe conditions requiring hospitalization were reported. We found no difference in self‐reported symptom magnitude between the different ABO blood groups (Figure S1) and between Rh(D)+ and Rh(D)− subjects, respectively.

Discussion

This aim of this study was to identify the impact of the ABO blood group and Rh(D) on SARS‐CoV‐2‐related immune responses, such as antibody levels following vaccination, infection susceptibility, and COVID‐19 symptom severity in immunized individuals. Even though we neither observed any significant differences in circulating anti‐RBD nor in neutralizing antibodies levels against the WT and Omicron variant across ABO blood groups, we found significant differences in breakthrough infection frequency.

In our study, neither an association between ABO blood group nor Rh(D) and anti‐RBD antibody levels 4 weeks after booster vaccination was found. These findings are in line with most previous reports. In a small (N = 65) group of noninfected subjects with transfusion‐dependent thalassemia, antibody response following two doses of BNT162b2 vaccination was independent of the ABO blood group [22]. The authors attributed these outcomes to the mRNA‐induced spike proteins‐related pre‐existing glycosylation, which would not be expected to elicit ABO‐related differences in immune response [13, 23]. Similarly, in another small sample (N = 85), ABO blood groups again did not impact vaccine responses as assessed by anti‐RBD antibodies 2 weeks after primary vaccination with BNT162b2 [24]. In summary, ABO blood type might be an immune enhancer in individuals who had received ChAdOx1 nCoV‐19 (Oxford‐AstraZeneca/Vaxzevria) [13, 25] but not in solely mRNA‐based vaccine regiments [26].

Interestingly, we found significant differences in hazard for breakthrough infections during our follow‐up period. ABO, but not Rh(D) type, was associated with lower infection risk in vaccinated subjects. Interestingly, individuals with O or A blood groups were more susceptible to breakthrough infections than individuals with B or AB, with blood group A bearing the highest infection risk. Although these findings align with the higher infection risk consistently attributed to blood group A, blood group O has been commonly associated with lower infection risk. In contrast, blood group B and AB data are scarce in the literature [27]. ABO phenotype diversity in different populations was found to have an impact on transmission rates [28], particularly in population groups presenting with higher ABO variability, where an ABO compatibility between potential hosts is expected to be less likely [29]. This is further supported by the observation that the advantage mediated by anti‐A and anti‐B agglutinins regarding transmissions disappears in populations with a high blood group O prevalence, for example, in South American countries [29]. Thus, in data from Latin America, blood group O did not convey a lower susceptibility to SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, which was also observed in our data. In the Austrian general population, A and O are the most common blood groups, with a prevalence of 41% and 37%, respectively [30]. This might explain the lack of a previously reported protective effect of blood group O in our population sample. Female sex and younger age have been associated with lower SARS‐CoV‐2 infections susceptibility due to sex‐dependent higher circulating anti‐A agglutinins levels, and age‐dependent changes in anti‐A and anti‐B isoagglutinin titers [31]. Whereas age was also a risk factor for breakthrough infections in our study population, gender did not impact infection risk. The observed blood group‐related differences in infection susceptibility among our participants did not appear to be based on ABO‐specific agglutinins.

Similarly, although little data exist on the impact of Rh(D), an association between the more prevalent Rh+ blood group and a higher COVID‐19 risk has been reported by a few studies [11, 32, 33]. In our study, Rh(D) had no impact on any of the observed outcomes, which again implies that blood group‐associated agglutinins were less relevant in our population or in Omicron transmission compared to earlier SARS‐CoV‐2 mutations, specifically. In line with our findings, more recent studies reported no association between Rh(D) and susceptibility to COVID‐19 [34, 35].

Published data on potential associations between ABO blood types, disease severity, and/or outcomes in COVID‐19, such as hospitalization, ICU admission, and mortality, are conflicting [36]. A lower risk of severe outcomes for blood group O, particularly when compared to blood group A, was repeatedly described [37]. In another study, AB blood type was also linked to higher COVID‐19 symptom severity and mortality, whereas blood group B individuals showed a lower mortality risk [38]. In our study, the observed COVID‐19 symptom severity was mild to moderate, with no cases classifying as severe and requiring hospitalization; thus, a blood group stratified analysis regarding hospitalization and mortality was not feasible.

Limitations

Although the real‐world setting of our study, initially including almost 5000 individuals, and the timing of data collection, that is, fully covering the Omicron wave in Austria by that time, is an evident strength, several limitations must be addressed. First, no formal simple size calculation was conducted. Despite the large sample size and the detection of significant associations for blood groups B and AB, very small effects may remain undetected, and effect size estimates for blood group AB should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Second, information on the presence and time of breakthrough infections was based on participants self‐reporting, which might lead to reporting bias. However, data were cross‐checked with official PCR testing documents, available as digital COVID‐19 certificates for many participants and the presence of antibodies against nucleocapsid, which are triggered solely by infection and not by vaccination. This gives us confidence in the validity and reliability of self‐ reported breakthrough infections. Further, medication use was self‐reported, which may have introduced some degree of misclassification. In addition, only a small proportion of participants were receiving stable immunosuppressive therapy (n = 43). Other medication classes, such as antihypertensive, antithrombotic, and lipid‐lowering therapies, were not considered in the models because they have not been shown to substantially influence vaccine‐induced antibody responses or susceptibility to SARS‐CoV‐2 infection.

Compared to previous studies, we had a novel and more in‐depth approach to investigate the self‐reported COVID‐19 infection severity. Although specific factors associated with the risk of breakthrough infections, such as preventive measures, have not been considered, we were able to adjust for crucial factors such as age or type of vaccination. Nevertheless, based on the observational design of our study, establishing a causal relationship or a pathophysiological mechanism of any reported associations between ABO blood types and SARS‐CoV‐2 infection susceptibility is not possible. Lastly, in contrast to the extensively studied effects of ABO or Rh blood types on the general infection susceptibility and COVID‐19 severity, our findings add quantitatively significant data to the relatively scarce specific knowledge on breakthrough infections postvaccination.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is the first large Central European cohort to describe the impact of ABO blood groups on breakthrough SARS‐CoV‐2 infections in immunized individuals. ABO or Rh(D) blood group had no significant effect on COVID‐19 severity or COVID‐19 vaccination‐related antibody responses in our study sample. Interestingly, we observed the lowest breakthrough infection rates among individuals with B and AB blood types, the least common ABO blood groups in the local general population. Whereas the previously discussed protective effects of anti‐A/B agglutinins and Rh(D)‐specific circulating antibodies are not suitable for explaining this observation, our findings support the previously reported relevance of ABO compatibility on the risk of transmission of SARS‐CoV‐2, even during the Omicron variant‐dominated pandemic. Despite several limitations of the study, mainly the risk of reporting bias and the lacking sample size calculations and proof of causality, our data add to understanding the complexity of COVID‐19 vaccine immunogenicity and breakthrough infections.
