 ABSTRACT

This report provides a deep dive into how the stability of energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) standards
yielded insight into the shortfalls of using software to randomly select points for spectral acquisition when
using heterogeneous standards. Some procedural recommendations are included which are meant to
minimize the impact of anomalous reference standard spectra by detecting them before the standard is
implemented into data processing.

 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 BACKGROUND

The Applied Materials Analysis (AMA) group at the US Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) Oak Ridge
National Laboratory (ORNL) regularly uses scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and EDS in material
analyses as both a first screening step with automated particle analysis and for more in-depth analysis at
higher analytical fidelity.

EDS provides elemental makeup information of materials. To convert raw EDS count data to quantitative
elemental data, using elemental standards is common in analytical laboratories. EDS data are acquired on
standards at regular intervals during standard operating conditions. For every element i and chosen x-ray
line, the value ki may be derived, where ki = Iiunk./Iistd, where I is the net background-subtracted peak
intensity measured under similar conditions between the analyte and the standard. In practice, the use of
the k-ratio cancels out instrumental factors, although additional uncertainty remains in the final
quantitative analysis owing to varying material considerations for each analyte.

The AMA group’s laboratories are equipped with Geller Microanalytical Laboratory mounts 868 and 869
as reference standards for EDS quantification calculations. The EDAX EDS detectors on the Tescan
Mira3 SEM provide EDS spectra for the reference standards; they are collected annually for processing
sample data related to elemental quantifications throughout the year. The typical standards data
acquisition uses multiple randomly generated points around a user-selected location on the standard to
collect the data. Six points are used, and the five most consistent points are averaged for the reference
spectrum. The reference spectrum is applied for quantification methods, and the standard error is carried
into standard error estimates of elemental constituency. Standards, along with data acquisition methods,
are prescribed in WI-SEM-2 Elemental Standard Spectra Acquisition.

In 2026, as part of a quality control theme, an investigation into the long-term stability of the EDS
detectors was initiated. EDS standard data from 2023–2026 were used to identify any potential changes in
the spectra that might indicate detector drift or standard degradation over the years. Standard reference
spectra were available for 2022 but were excluded because the standards were refurbished after the 2022
acquisition and significant differences existed in the overall count rate before and after the refurbishment.

Spectral data files were automatically ingested into Microsoft Excel and plots were generated showing the
full spectrum of each standard for each year those data were available. Additionally, standards were
categorized by the number of elements in the standard because they have different live times for the
reference spectrum acquisition. The extended live time for multi-element standards was consistent for the
four years (i.e., 2023–2026) covered in this report, allowing same-standard comparisons regardless of live
time.

In addition to the generation of the plots, the Excel-based data analysis macro identified the peaks as well
as the associated peak area. This was accomplished for all peaks in each sample without regard to which

 5

specific element was generating the peaks. This method was chosen for its ability to assess the entire
standard rather than just the single element within the standard.

The sum of all identified peak areas as well as individual peak areas were compared for 2023–2026. To
avoid erroneous data in the zero-energy range, the first 10 channels (out of 4,096) were ignored. For first-
pass analysis, relative standard deviation (RSD) was calculated by the bulk standard deviation of summed
peak areas divided by the average of that sum. Follow-on analysis used the individual peak areas to
calculate the RSD on a by-peak basis using the same formula. Summed areas and individual peak areas
were not live-time-normalized because the RSD was being calculated from equal-time spectral
acquisitions. For inter-sample RSD comparisons, no normalization was used; just comparisons to each
standard’s RSD.

 2. SIGNIFICANT FINDINGS

Two standards were found to have incorrect spectra attributed to the Ag matrix they were embedded in.
The 2026 reference spectra for both LuF3 and Rh were identified as Ag; both were reacquired before
being added to the reference database and used for quantification. The Rh reference spectrum was found
to be incorrect for 2024 but it was not identified until this investigation. All Rh elemental identifications
for 2024 should be considered suspect.

2.1 INDIVIDUAL STANDARD RESULTS

2.1.1 LuF3 Standard

The most significant outlier was traced to the LuF3 standard. Its set of peaks were different compared to
previous years (Figure 1). This was immediately investigated. It was found that the standard has a matrix
that includes Ag in addition to the LuF3. The typical data acquisition uses multiple randomly generated
points around a user-selected location on the standard to collect the data, and the 2026 random selection
algorithm included the Ag matrix. During acquisition, the spectra are shown by the software but not
compared to previous values. The spectra met consistency criteria, so they were initially accepted. Later,
it was determined that the reference spectrum was not on the correct material. Figure 3 shows an SEM
image of the standard. The reference spectrum was reacquired manually after identification, and the
updated results were consistent with previous years.

peak-specific RSD (47.4% for 2.65 keV and 50.2% for 2.78 keV) in addition to a high total RSD of

previous years. The reference spectrum for 2024 casts doubts into Rh-identified particles for 2024 data.

 3. RECOMMENDED ACTIONS

3.1 ACQUISITION OF STANDARDS

Heterogenous standards should be investigated prior to EDS acquisition to differentiate the reference
material from the matrix holding it in place. These standards should be reviewed by the operator to locate
the best areas and manually selected locations should be used instead of the software-generated random
locations. This approach will ensure that only the reference material generates spectra. To aid in manual
selection, points should be verified by EDS to ensure the point falls on the material of interest.

3.2 REACQUIRE HgTe

Because of the low counts for the HgTe standard in 2026, the reference spectrum should be reacquired. If
the new spectrum continues to produce low counts compared to previous years, then determine the cause,
if possible.

3.3 DATA REVIEW

After all standards have been acquired, the results should be compared to previous results to ensure
consistency. Outliers should be investigated and reacquired. Only after the reference spectra are verified
should the new standards be considered suitable for implementation.

Any legacy processed sample data found to use questionable reference spectra should be brought to the
attention of the AMA group leader and/or program leader for potential reprocessing or data correction.

3.4 SELECTION OF STANDARDS

When possible, prioritize more consistent standards for quantification use to facilitate inter-year standard
comparisons as well as inter-year sample comparisons. For example, use the Ta standard instead of the
TaN standard for Ta quantification because the RSD is 3.6% instead of 11.0%. Nitrogen is omnipresent in
EDS measurements owing to its use as purge gas and thus is not typically quantified. Because the TaN
standard is problematic, we recommend not using the TaN standard.

In addition to using more consistent standards overall, the selection of reference spectra for individual
elements should be prioritized vs. standards that have more consistent performance for one element’s
peaks. For example, the reference for oxygen should use the ThO2 standard (3.6% RSD for O peak)
instead of the CeO standard (5.1% RSD for O peak).

3.5 FOR ADDITIONAL INFORMATION

For additional information, contact Jen Niedziela (niedzielajl@ornl.gov).

 4. CONCLUSIONS

This report provided a deep dive into how the stability of EDS standards yielded insight into the shortfalls
of using software to randomly select points for spectral acquisition when using heterogeneous standards.
Some procedural recommendations were included—they are meant to help minimize the impact of
anomalous reference standard spectra. Early detection prior to the standard’s implementation into data
processing is ideal and the AMA team’s aim is to increase data quality and overall efficiency.
