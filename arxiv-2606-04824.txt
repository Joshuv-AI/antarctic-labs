The minimum degree question for the Maker Breaker Domination Game
Jakob Führer, Georg Grasegger, Paul Hametner, Oliver Roche-Newton

Abstract
The Maker Breaker Domination Game is a two player game played on a graph G G in which the players take turns to claim a vertex from the graph. The aim of the Dominator is to claim the vertices of a dominating set, and the aim of the Staller is to prevent this. In this paper, we consider the following problem: for a given integer d d , what is the size of the smallest (with respect to the number of vertices) graph with minimum degree d d such that the Dominator loses going first? We write β ⁡ ( d ) \beta(d) to denote the answer to this question. We determine the precise value of β ⁡ ( d ) \beta(d) for d ≤ 3 d\leq 3 . For general d d it was known that 2 d + 1 ≤ β ⁡ ( d ) ≤ 2 d + 1 + 2 ​ d 2^{d+1}\leq\beta(d)\leq 2^{d+1}+2d ; the upper bound is due to a construction communicated to us by Valentin Gledel, while the lower bound follows from a simple application of the Erdős-Selfridge Theorem. We improve the lower bound to β ⁡ ( d ) ≥ 2 d + 1 + 2 \beta(d)\geq 2^{d+1}+2 .

Introduction

A Maker-Breaker game is a two-player positional game. The rules of the game are as follows. We are given a ground set
X
and a family
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{P}(X)
of
winning sets
. The players take turns to move, and each move consists of the player claiming an element of
X
. Once an element has been claimed by a player, it cannot be claimed again. The goal of the Maker is to claim all of the elements of a winning set
F\in\mathcal{F}
, while the goal of the Breaker is to prevent this from happening. One can consider the situations where the Maker or the Breaker has the first move, and moving this advantage from one player to another has the potential to significantly change the outcome of the game. See
[
7
]
for more background on Maker-Breaker games, as well as the more general topic of Positional Games.

In this paper, we study the Maker-Breaker Domination game on a finite graph
G=(V,E)
. This game was introduced by Duchêne, Gledel, Parreau and Renault
[
4
]
, based closely on earlier work of Brešar, Klavžar and Rall
[
3
]
. Recall that
U\subset V
is a
dominating set
if

\forall\,v\in V\setminus U,\,\exists\,u\in U\text{ such that }uv\in E.

The two players in the Maker-Breaker Domination game are called the
Dominator
and the
Staller
, and the players take turns to choose a previously unclaimed vertex from
V
. The goal of the Dominator is to claim all of the elements of a dominating set, and the goal of the Staller is to stop the Dominator from achieving this. One typically thinks of the Dominator as the Maker and the Staller as the Breaker, in which case, the family of winning sets is the family
\mathcal{F}
consisting of all dominating sets. It is also possible to view the Staller as the Maker by adjusting the definition of
\mathcal{F}
suitably, as we see later in this paper. The game ends when either the Dominator has claimed a dominating set (in which case the Dominator wins), or when all vertices have been claimed and the Dominator has not claimed a dominating set (in which case the Staller wins).

In particular, each game has exactly one winner. The following table is used to introduce the shorthand notation that we frequently use to describe the outcome on a given graph
G
.

Notation

Meaning

The Dominator has a strategy which ensures victory going second.

o(G)=\mathcal{N}

The Dominator has a strategy which ensures victory going first, and the Staller has a strategy which ensures victory going first.

o(G)=\mathcal{S}

The Staller has a strategy which ensures victory going second.

It was shown in
[
4
]
that each graph
G
has exactly one of these three possible outcome statuses. For ease of language and explanation, we shorten the phrasing above. For example, we say that
the dominator wins going second
if
o(G)=\mathcal{D}
. We use the notation
o(G)\geq\mathcal{N}
to say that the Dominator wins going first (while giving no information about who wins when the Dominator has the second move) and the notation
o(G)\leq\mathcal{N}
is used to describe the case when the Staller wins going first (while giving no information about who wins when the Staller has the second move).

For example, it is not difficult to show that
o(C_{n})\geq\mathcal{N}
for all
n\geq 3
. Indeed, one can show this with a
pairing strategy
(see also
[
4
]
).
If
n
is even, then the Dominator can pair off neighbouring vertices of the cycle and proceed to ensure that at least one element from each pair is claimed.
If
n

Moreover, it is not much more difficult to check that the Dominator wins on

A natural and general problem is to classify the graphs which have each possible outcome. However, obtaining such a classification appears to be a very difficult problem; determining the outcome of the game on a given graph is PSPACE-complete (see
[
4
]
). The examples above give some indication that the outcome of the Maker-Breaker Domination game is heavily dependent on the structure (i.e. the edge distribution) of the graph
G
. In particular, it appears that well-connected graphs offer an advantage to the Dominator. Meanwhile, low degree vertices are helpful for the Staller. The main aim of this paper is to study the relationship between the minimum degree of a graph
G
and the outcome of the game on
G
. Let
\delta(G)
denote the minimum degree of
G
.

A naive first question to ask is: do there exist graphs
G
with arbitrarily large minimum degree
d
such that
o(G)=\mathcal{S}
? The answer to this question is yes, and a beautiful construction of Gledel (private communication) shows that, for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a graph
G
with
2^{d+1}+2d
vertices such that the Staller wins going second. See
Section

3
for more details concerning this construction. On the other hand, it was observed in
[
4
]
that the Erdős-Selfridge Theorem (see
Corollary

8
) implies that the Dominator wins going first on any graph with minimum degree
d
and fewer than
2^{d+1}
vertices.

The question that we seek to answer in this paper is the following; what is the size (in terms of the number of vertices) of the smallest graph with minimum degree
d
such that the Staller wins going first. Let
\beta(d)
denote the answer to this question, that is,

\beta(d):=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\exists\,G=(V,E),\,|V|=n,\delta(G)=d,\,o(G)=\mathcal{S}\}.

The previous paragraph gives

2^{d+1}\leq\beta(d)\leq 2^{d+1}+2d.

(1)

The aim of this paper is to determine the precise value of
\beta(d)
for some small values of
d
. Our main result shows that the upper bound in
Equation

1
is optimal for
0\leq d\leq 3
.

Theorem 1
.

\beta(0)=2
,
\beta(1)=6
,
\beta(2)=12
,
\beta(3)=22
.

The proofs that
\beta(0)=2
and
\beta(1)=6
are straightforward and can be done by hand. Proving that
\beta(2)=12
could also still be done by hand, however, the computational tools we develop in order to determine
\beta(3)
deal with
\beta(2)
quite straightforwardly, and the manual proof requires quite some work, so we give only the computer assisted proof that
\beta(2)=12
.

We develop some theory in this paper which gives some structural information about graphs for which
o(G)=\mathcal{S}
, namely that such graphs have what we call an
RSI decomposition
, see
Section

5
. The proof of
\beta(3)=22
does then heavily rely on computer-aided computations. Breaking up graphs into its
RSI decomposition
allows us to greatly reduce the required computing resources.

One may notice that all values of
\beta(d)
that are determined by
Theorem

1
align with the upper bound from (
1
), which does not rule out the possibility that Gledel’s construction gives the best possible bound for all
d
. On the other hand, we are able to give a very small improvement to the trivial lower bound from (
1
). The lower bound
\beta(d)\geq 2^{d+1}+1
was implicit in the work of
[
2
]
, and this bound can be nudged forward slightly.

Theorem 2
.

For all
d\in\mathbb{N}
,

\beta(d)\geq 2^{d+1}+2
.

We also study the same problem for the case when the Staller has the first move. To this end, define

\beta^{\prime}(d):=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\exists\,G=(V,E),\,|V|=n,\delta(G)=d,\,o(G)\leq\mathcal{N}\}.

It turns out
\beta^{\prime}(d+1)
can be easily calculated using
\beta(d)
. We prove the following result.

Theorem 3
.

For all integers
d\geq 0
,

\beta^{\prime}(d+1)=\beta(d)+1.

An upper bound construction

In this section, we derive the upper bound for
\beta(d)
. This is given by a general graph construction introduced to us by Valentin Gledel.

Theorem 5
.

For every
d\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a graph
G
with
2^{d+1}+2d
vertices such that
o(G)=\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

We construct a bipartite graph
G=(X,Y,E)
with

|X|=2d,\,\ |Y|=2^{d+1}

such that the Staller wins the game on
G
going second. The elements of
X
and
Y
each come in pairs; let
a
,
b
,
A
and
B
be symbols for denoting vertices and let

X=\{a_{1},b_{1},\dots,a_{d},b_{d}\},

and let

Y=\bigcup_{s\in\{a,b\}^{d}}\{A_{s},B_{s}\}.

Vertices in
Y
are indexed by binary sequence
s\in\{a,b\}^{d}
, so we can define the edge set
E
as follows: for each sequence
s
, we draw an edge between the corresponding elements of
X
and both vertices
A_{s}
and
B_{s}
. For example, the sequence
s=(a,a,\dots,a)
gives rise to a complete bipartite subgraph
K_{d,2}
between the vertex sets
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\}
and
\{A_{s},B_{s}\}
.

A pairing strategy is used to show that the Staller wins. The Staller decomposes the vertex set into disjoint pairs

(a_{1},b_{1}),\dots,(a_{d},b_{d}),(A_{s_{1}},B_{s_{1}}),\dots,(A_{s_{2^{d}}},B_{s_{2^{d}}}).

After each move of the Dominator, the Staller responds by choosing the other vertex in the pairing. At the end of the game, there is some sequence
s\in\{a,b\}^{d}
such that the Staller has chosen all from
X
corresponding to this sequence, and the Staller has also claimed a vertex
x\in\{A_{s},B_{s}\}
. It follows that the Staller has claimed
x
and all of its neighbours, and thus this pairing strategy guarantees that the Staller wins.

The Erdős-Selfridge Theorem

This paper makes heavy use of a theorem of Erdős-Selfridge on winning strategies of Maker-Breaker games.

Theorem 7

(Erdős-Selfridge Theorem
[
5
]
)
.

Consider a Maker-Breaker game with a finite base set
X
such that
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{P}(X)
is the set of all winning sets for the Maker. If the Breaker goes first and

\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}2^{-|F|}<1,

then the Breaker has a winning strategy. If the Breaker goes second and

\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}2^{-|F|}<1/2,

then the Breaker has a winning strategy.

The following corollary rephrases
Theorem

7
in the context of the Maker-Breaker Domination game.

Corollary 8
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph such that

\sum_{v\in V}2^{-(\deg(v)+1)}<1.

Then
o(G)\geq\mathcal{N}
.

Given a graph
G
, we define the
potential of
G
to be the quantity

pot(G):=\sum_{v\in V}2^{-(\deg(v)+1)}.

With this notation,
Corollary

8
states that

pot(G)<1\implies o(G)\geq\mathcal{N}.

One may think of the potential
pot(G)
as “the initial potential for the Staller to win”. However, we can also regard the potential as a dynamic quantity that changes with each move. The family of winning sets is changing with each move. The idea here is that, after each move in a Maker Breaker game, we may reconsider the game as if it were to start from the current situation and then apply
Theorem

7
if possible.

For a game state
(G,D,S)
and a vertex
v\in V(G)
, we define the
game state degree of
v
to be

\displaystyle\gsdeg_{(G,D,S)}(v)=\begin{cases}\infty,&\text{if }N[v]\cap D\neq\emptyset,\\
\left|N[v]\setminus S\right|&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

If the game state is clear from the context, we may drop the subscript. The
Erdős-Selfridge potential
, or just
potential
of the game state
(G,D,S)
, is given by

\displaystyle\pot(G,D,S)=\sum_{v\in V(G)}2^{-\gsdeg(v)}.

Here we use the convention that
2^{-\infty}=0
. We often make use of the following version of the Erdős-Selfridge Theorem, specified for game states.

Theorem 9

(Erdős-Selfridge Theorem for game states)
.

Let
(G,D,S)
be a game state. If
\pot(G,D,S)<1
, then
o_{\mathcal{D}}(G,D,S)=\mathcal{D}
. If
\pot(G,D,S)<\frac{1}{2}
, then
o_{\mathcal{S}}(G,D,S)=\mathcal{D}
.

Proof.

For a game state
(G,D,S)
and an unclaimed vertex
v\in V(G)\setminus(D\cup S)
, define the potential of
v
to be

\pot_{(G,D,S)}(v):=\sum_{u\in N[v]}2^{-\gsdeg(v)}.

We prove that the Dominator can guarantee to win by always picking the vertex with the highest potential.

Note that the Dominator (respectively Staller) picking the vertex
v
in the game state
(G,D,S)
decreases (respectively increases) the potential of the game state by exactly
\pot_{(G,D,S)}(v)
. In other words

\displaystyle\pot_{(G,D,S)}(v)=\pot(G,D,S)-\pot(G,D\cup\{v\},S)=\pot(G,D,S\cup\{v\})-\pot(G,D,S).

(2)

Also note that if
(G,D_{1},S)
and
(G,D_{2},S)
are two game states with
D_{1}\subseteq D_{2}
and
v\in V(G)\setminus(D_{2}\cup S)
, then

\displaystyle\pot_{(G,D_{1},S)}(v)\geq\pot_{(G,D_{2},S)}(v).

(3)

If
d\in V(G)\setminus(D\cup S)
is a vertex with maximal potential in the game state
(G,D,S)
and
s\in V(G)\setminus(D\cup\{d\}\cup S)
is any vertex, then it follows from
Equation

2
and
Equation

3
that

\displaystyle\pot(G,D\cup\{d\},S\cup\{s\})

\displaystyle=\pot(G,D,S)-\pot_{(G,D,S)}(d)+\pot_{(G,D\cup\{d\},S)}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\pot(G,D,S).

Putting things together, we see that if
\pot(G,D,S)<1
and the Dominator sticks to his strategy as first player, he can guarantee that the potential is always less than
1
. At the end of the game, all the vertices are claimed, and the potential has to be a non-negative integer. The potential at the end of the game is thus
0
, which means that the Dominator won. If
\pot(G,D,S)<\frac{1}{2}
and the Staller is the first player, she can at most double the potential in her first move. After that we are again in the case of
\pot(G,D,S)<1
and the Dominator being the first player.
∎

A more detailed proof of a version of the result above for general Maker-Breaker games on hypergraphs can be found in
[
7
, Theorem 2.3.3]
. For us, it is important that the Dominator usually wants to decrease the potential, while the Staller usually wants to increase it.

As a first application of
Theorem

9
, we show the following.

Proposition 10
.

For all integers
d\geq 3
, we have
\beta^{\prime}(d+1)=\beta(d)+1
.

It is convenient to split off two lemmas.

Lemma 11
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices, and let
v\in V(G)
with
\deg(v)\geq\frac{n}{2}
. Then

o_{\mathcal{D}}(G-v)=o_{\mathcal{D}}(G,\emptyset,\{v\}).

Proof.

As the first player, the Dominator occupies exactly
\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
vertices at the end of the game, both on

Low Surplus: RSI-decompositions

In this section, we prove that
o(G)\geq\mathcal{N}
for graphs
G
on at most
21
vertices and of minimum degree
3
, which are in some sense almost regular. We use the following measure of how regular a graph is.

Definition 22
.

Let
G
be a graph, let
v\in V(G)
, and let
d
be an integer.
We define the
surplus of
G
with respect to
d
and the vertex
v
to be

\sur_{d}(G,v)=\sum_{x\in V(G)\setminus\{v\}}|\deg(x)-d|.

In most cases
v
is a maximum degree vertex and
d=3
is the minimum degree. In these cases, we simply write
\sur(G)=\sur_{d}(G,v)
and call it
surplus of
G
. The surplus only depends on the degree sequence, so we may also speak of the surplus of a degree sequence.

Before stating the main theorem of this section, let us first introduce a convenient reduction telling us that for proving lower bounds for
\beta(3)
, we only have to consider graphs that do not have edges between vertices of degree greater than
3
.

Definition 23
.

Let
G
be a graph with
\mindeg(G)=d
. Then
G
is called
reduced
if
uv\in E(G)
implies
\deg_{G}(u)=d
or
\deg_{G}(v)=d
.

Lemma 24
.

Let
n
and
d
be positive integers, and assume that
o(G)\geq\mathcal{N}
for all reduced graphs
G
on
n
vertices of minimum degree
d
. Then
o(G)\geq\mathcal{N}
for all graphs
G
on
n
vertices of minimum degree
d
.

Proof.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices with
\mindeg(G)=d
. By iteratively deleting edges between vertices of degree greater than
d
, we find a reduced spanning subgraph
G^{\prime}
of
G
. The assertion now follows from
o(G)\geq o(G^{\prime})
.
∎

What we prove in this section is the following.

Theorem 25
.

Let
G
be a reduced graph on at most
21
vertices with
\mindeg(G)=3
and
\sur(G)\leq 8
. Then
o(G)\geq\mathcal{N}
.

The rough plan for the proof is as follows. Assume that
G
contradicts
Theorem

25
, so that by
Corollary

21
there is a proper RSI-decomposition of
G
separating a maximum degree vertex
v\in V(G)
. Since
\sur(G)\leq 8
, the number of edges between
R
and
S
is at most
5
by the subsequent
Lemma

26
. In most cases, deleting the edges in
E(R,S)
yields two graphs,
G_{R}
and
G_{SI}
, one with outcome
\mathcal{D}
and the other one with outcome at least
\mathcal{N}
, contradicting
o(G)=\mathcal{S}
. The remaining cases are handled similarly. Checking that these two smaller parts have the desired outcomes happens in the subsequent R-lemmas and SI-lemmas, whose proofs heavily rely on brute-force computations.

Given an RSI-decomposition
(R,S,I)
of a graph
G
with minimum degree
d
, we often need to bound the number of edges between
R
and
S
or the number of vertices of degree less than
d
in
G[R]
. The following lemma provides us with a useful equality, giving us these desired bounds in terms of the surplus
\sur_{d}(G,v)
.

Lemma 26
.

Let
G
be a graph with
\mindeg(G)=d
, let
v
be a vertex of
G
, and let
(R,S,I)
be an RSI-decomposition of
G
separating
v
. Then

\displaystyle\sur_{d}(G,v)=\sum_{r\in R\setminus\{v\}}(\deg(r)-d)+e(R,S)+2e(S)+d(|I|-|S|)+2\sum_{i\in I}(\deg(i)-d).

(5)

Proof.

Note that since
I\subseteq\iso(G-S)
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in S}\deg(s)=e(R,S)+2e(S)+\sum_{i\in I}\deg(i).

Using the definition of surplus, we therefore have

\displaystyle\sur_{d}(G,v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in R\setminus\{v\}}(\deg(r)-d)+\sum_{s\in S}\deg(s)-d|S|+\sum_{i\in I}(\deg(i)-d)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in R\setminus\{v\}}(\deg(r)-d)+e(R,S)+2e(S)+d(|I|-|S|)+2\sum_{i\in I}(\deg(i)-d).

∎

In fact, we only use the lower bound on the surplus in
Equation

5
; that is, given an upper bound on the surplus, we use it to bound
e(R,S)
, for example. A very common application of
Lemma

26
tells us the following. Given a graph
G
and a vertex
v\in V(G)
such that
\mindeg(G)=3
and
\sur_{3}(G,v)\leq 8
, and given an RSI-decomposition
(R,S,I)
separating
v
, we have
e(R,S)\leq 5
. Note that this follows because each summand on the right of
Equation

5
is non-negative and because
|I|>|S|
.

The next lemma shows that the surplus of
G_{R}
can be bounded in terms of the surplus of
G
, even though a maximum degree vertex in
G_{R}
is not necessarily a maximum degree vertex in
G
.

Lemma 27
.

Let
G
be a graph with
\mindeg(G)=d
, let
v
be a maximum degree vertex of
G
, and let
(R,S,I)
be an RSI-decomposition of
G
separating
v
. If
w
denotes a maximum degree vertex in
G_{R}=G[R]
, then

\sur_{d}(G_{R},w)\leq\sur_{d}(G,v)-d(|I|-|S|).

Furthermore, if equality holds in the above inequality, we must have
\deg_{G_{R}}(w)=\deg_{G}(v)
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in S}(\deg_{G}(s)-d)\geq e(R,S)+d(|I|-|S|).

So by definition of surplus, we get

\displaystyle\sur_{d}(G,v)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in R\setminus\{v\}\end{subarray}}(\deg_{G}(r)-d)+\sum_{s\in S}(\deg_{G}(s)-d)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in R\setminus\{v\}\\
\deg_{G_{R}}(r)>d\end{subarray}}(\deg_{G_{R}}(r)-d)+e(R,S)+d(|I|-|S|)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\sum_{r\in R\setminus\{v\}}|\deg_{G_{R}}(r)-d|\right)+\deg_{G}(v)-\deg_{G_{R}}(v)+d(|I|-|S|),

where the third inequality holds because

e(R,S)\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in R\setminus\{v\}\\
\deg_{G_{R}}(r)<d\end{subarray}}|\deg_{G_{R}}(r)-d|+\deg_{G}(v)-\deg_{G_{R}}(v).

From this and the fact that

\displaystyle|\deg_{G_{R}}(v)-d|-|\deg_{G_{R}}(w)-d|\leq\deg_{G_{R}}(w)-\deg_{G_{R}}(v)\leq\deg_{G}(v)-\deg_{G_{R}}(v),

(6)

we indeed get

\displaystyle\sum_{r\in R\setminus\{w\}}|\deg_{G_{R}}(r)-d|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{r\in R\setminus\{v\}}|\deg_{G_{R}}(r)-d|\right)+\deg_{G}(v)-\deg_{G_{R}}(v)

\displaystyle\leq\sur_{d}(G,v)-d(|I|-|S|).

Furthermore, for equality to hold in
Equation

6
, we in particular must have
\deg_{G_{R}}(w)=\deg_{G}(v)
.
∎

Let us define the potential of a degree sequence
(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
to be
\sum_{i=1}^{n}2^{-d_{i}-1}
.
In the proof of many of the subsequent lemmas, we exploit the fact that if the potential of a degree sequence is less than
1
(respectively
\frac{1}{2}
), the Dominator wins going first (respectively second) on all graphs with this degree sequence. By bounding the potential after one step into the game, that is, after the Dominator claims a maximum degree vertex, we can find even more such degree sequences for which the Dominator wins on all graphs corresponding to that degree sequence.

Lemma 28

(One-step Erdős-Selfridge for degree sequences)
.

Let
G
be a graph with degree sequence
(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
, and assume that

\sum_{i=1}^{n-d_{n}-1}2^{-d_{i}-1}<\frac{1}{2}.

Then
o(G)\geq\mathcal{N}
.

Proof.

Let
v
be a vertex of maximum degree
d_{n}
. Then

\displaystyle\pot(G,\{v\},\{\})=\sum_{u\in V(G)\setminus N[v]}2^{-\deg(u)-1}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n-d_{n}-1}2^{-d_{i}-1}<\frac{1}{2}.

By
Theorem

9
we have
o(G,\{v\},\{\})=\mathcal{D}
and thus
o(G)\geq\mathcal{N}
.
∎

\displaystyle\max_{n\leq i\leq N-1}\ \ \frac{n-1-d_{N}+\min(d_{i},n-1-d_{N})}{2^{d+1}}+2^{-d_{i}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=n\\

\displaystyle=\frac{|B\setminus(N(v)\cup N(w))|}{2^{d+1}}+\frac{|N(w)\setminus N(v)|}{2^{d}}+2^{-d_{i}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=n\\

\textsc{PotentialOutcome}((P^{\prime},\emptyset,\emptyset),p)=\mathcal{S}
for some partial completion
P^{\prime}
of
P
. In this case it follows from the specification of
Line
3
that we indeed have a completion of
P^{\prime}
, and thus of
P
, on which the Staller wins.

We now show by induction on
n
that if
S_{n}
is defined and if
G
is a completion of
P
on which the Dominator loses, then there is
P_{2}\in S_{n}
such that
G
is a completion of
P_{2}
. The base case
n=0
is trivial, so let us consider the case
n\geq 1
.

Let
G
be a completion of
P
on which the Dominator loses, and assume that
S_{n}
is defined. By the induction hypothesis, there is a
P_{1}\in S_{n-1}
such that
G
is a completion of
P_{1}
. Consider the
n
-th iteration of the while loop. In
Line
7
we run over all the elements
P^{\prime}
of
S_{n-1}
. Consider the iteration of the for loop in which we run over
P^{\prime}=P_{1}
. Since
P_{1}
has a completion, namely
G
, on which the Dominator loses, we cannot have
out=\mathcal{D}
in
Line
8
so that
out\in\{\mathcal{S},\texttt{Unsure}\}
. Since
S_{n}
is defined, we do not return
\mathcal{S}
in
Line
9
so that
out=\texttt{Unsure}
. The vertex
v
we pick in
Line
12
has degree less than
d
in
P_{1}
. In
G
it thus has a neighbour
w\in N_{G}(v)\setminus N_{P_{1}}(v)
that we iterate over in the for loop in
Line
13
. Therefore,
G
is a completion of the PCC
P_{2}=(V(P_{1}),E(P_{1})\cup\{vw\})
. This
P_{2}
is added to
S
in
Line
14
and thus is an element of
S_{n}
, which finishes the induction step.

We already dealt with the case of the algorithm returning
\mathcal{S}
in
Line
9
, so let us assume it does not. The number of iterations of the while loop is bounded by
m+1
, since
S_{m+1}=\emptyset
in case it is defined. In particular,
S_{n}=\emptyset
for some
n\geq 1
, so that by the above observation there can not be a completion of
P
on which the Dominator loses. The algorithm also returns
\mathcal{D}
in
Line
19
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

33
.

By
Lemma

24
and
Theorem

9
we only have to deal with reduced graphs of minimum degree
d=3
, surplus at least
s=9
, and
16\leq N\leq 21
vertices.
Running
Algorithm

2
with this
d
and
s
and all
16\leq N\leq 21
, we obtain a list of
118
degree sequences, which can be found in
input_sequences.txt
.
We bunch up these degree sequences into
38
classes such that for every class there is a single
Nauty
command that generates all cores of graphs corresponding to this given degree sequence. These
Nauty
commands can be found in
cores_cmds.txt
.
Using
Algorithm

4
, we check all cores generated by all of these
Nauty
commands in
generate_and_check.sh
.
In order to parallelize, all core generations are split into blocks as provided by
Geng
. In total
3\,756\,845\,840
cores need to be checked which needs around 3184 hours of computation.
∎
