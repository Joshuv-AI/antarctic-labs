Executive Summary
The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit end-
use load shapes (electricity, gas, propane, or fuel oil) that cover a majority of the high-impact,
market-ready (or nearly market-ready) measures. “Measures” refers to retrofits that can be
applied to buildings during modeling.

An “end-use savings shape” is the difference in energy consumption between a baseline building
and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied. It
results in a time-series profile that is broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas,
propane, or fuel oil use) at each time step.

ResStock™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data
sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the
annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States.
The baseline model intends to represent the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in 2018.
Technical documentation for the inputs and assumptions in the baseline building stock model is
available in Reyna et al. (2025). Calibration and validation of the baseline model results are
available in the final technical report of the End-Use Load Profiles project (Wilson et al 2022).

This documentation focuses on a single end-use savings shape upgrade—Propane Furnace 95%
Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency (AFUE) and Fuel Oil Furnace 88% AFUE.

1 Technology Summary
This work examines the technical potential for replacing existing fuel oil and propane furnaces
across the U.S. residential building stock—but only in homes where these types of systems are
already installed. Though less widespread than natural gas systems, oil and propane furnaces
remain common in areas without pipeline gas service, particularly in older and colder climate
dwellings. Oil is most common in older or rural buildings in New England and the mid-Atlantic.

1.1 Introduction to Technology
Fuel-fired furnaces—including propane and fuel oil—are used to heat indoor environments. Fuel
ignites in the burner, and the flame heats a metal heat exchanger. A blower fan moves air over
the heat exchangers, which warms the air. Most furnaces use ducts to distribute heat, while
combustion exhaust is vented outside. In comparison, gravity furnaces do exist but are older and
not as common; as such in this study, we only model forced air furnaces.

1.2 Efficiency and System Types
Efficiency, measured by annual fuel utilization efficiency (AFUE) is a key feature of furnaces
heated by fuel oil or propane. In this measure upgrade, we include propane in the form of
liquified petroleum gas and fuel oil—typically number 2 heating oil.
1.2.1 Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency
AFUE is the standard rated efficiency for how efficiently a furnace converts fuel into heat at
rated conditions. The higher the AFUE rating, the more efficient the system is at converting fuel
into usable heat. Energy lost through exhaust gases, radiation, and other components decreases
efficiency.
AFUE is the total annual heat output divided by the total annual fuel input—on a scale of 0% to
100%. Note that AFUE does not include the electricity used by the air handler blower,
combustion blower, ignition system, or controls. ANSI/ASHRAE 103-2022 provides a procedure
for determining AFUE of residential central furnaces and boilers, which is a test cycle structured
to simulate ordinary heating conditions throughout the year—including seasons
(ANSI/ASHRAE 2022).
“Residential furnaces manufactured and distributed in commerce, as defined by 42 U.S.C.
6291(16) must meet the energy conservation standards specified in the Code of Federal
Regulations at 10 CFR 430.32” (DOE 2025). 80% AFUE furnaces are the minimum for non-
weatherized gas furnaces (i.e., natural gas and propane) manufactured from 2015 through Dec.
18, 2028. After Dec. 18, 2028, the minimum furnace is 95% AFUE. If an investigator would like
to understand equipment standard changes over time, a useful approach could blend data from
this study with data from the space heating and cooling (circa 2025) upgrade (Stenger, Liu,
Wilson, et al. 2025) as well as the natural gas furnace upgrade (Stenger, Liu, White, et al. 2025),
which model fuel oil, propane, and natural gas furnaces.
Dwellings upgraded from lower efficiency furnaces often have atmospheric vents. After the
upgrade, these will side vent rather than having an atmospheric vent, meaning some dwellings
with propane furnace could changes in their infiltration (HPXML 2025). In this study, we model

a 95% AFUE furnace consuming propane. For dwellings heated with fuel oil, we model an 88%
AFUE furnace as a higher-efficiency furnace (AHRI 2025).

3 Modeling Approach
This section outlines how propane and fuel oil furnace upgrades are modeled in ResStock using
the OpenStudio-HPXML workflow. ResStock assigns each dwelling unit a set of building
characteristics, including heating system type, efficiency, and fuel source, based on probabilistic
sampling logic informed by national housing stock data. Furnaces are modeled as forced-air
heating systems that distribute heat through ductwork. The primary variable representing an
upgrade is AFUE, which characterizes how efficiently the furnace converts fuel to usable heat
over a heating season. Ducts remain unchanged when heating systems are upgraded. ResStock
models fuel type (e.g., propane or natural gas) explicitly, influencing energy use calculations.
The model assumes consistent operation patterns across households, and scales furnace capacity
based on heating load requirements determined by building envelope characteristics and climate
zone. While equipment brand and installation quality are not modeled explicitly, the total heating
energy demand and fuel consumption are recalculated to reflect the standardized furnace and fuel
use.

These probability distributions are sampled to create a synthetic stock where each sample has an
assigned option for each characteristic for each dwelling unit to be simulated. ResStock utilizes
the OpenStudio-HPXML workflow to translate these dwelling characteristics into building
models ready to be simulated via OpenStudio® and EnergyPlus® through the use of an HPXML
file. For reference, the state of the OpenStudio-HPXML repository when the ResStock 2025
Release 1 dataset was created can be found here. Assumptions about the oil and propane furnace
upgrades that are not explicitly defined in ResStock are taken care of in the
BuildResidentialHPXML measure.

3.1 Propane and Fuel Oil Furnaces
In ResStock, building characteristics are translated to OpenStudio-HPXML parameters. Measure
upgrades, such as converting or replacing furnaces with higher-efficiency propane or fuel oil
systems, are modeled as changes to baseline building characteristics. This section outlines the
technical details of how propane and fuel oil furnace upgrades are implemented in the ResStock
workflow.

3.1.1 ResStock Inputs
Propane and fuel oil furnaces are modeled as forced-air heating systems serving homes with
central ducts. Each upgrade replaces the existing heating system with a furnace that uses either
propane or fuel oil, with efficiency defined by a target AFUE level: 88% AFUE for fuel oil
heated dwellings and 95% AFUE for propane heated dwellings. These furnaces are modeled
assuming that the efficiency is constant at the AFUE and that heating is always available if there
is a demand for it. This measure assumes the furnaces are always installed correctly, although
models exist in OpenStudio-HPXML to account for issues with installation that may degrade
performance. The furnaces supply air at a constant temperature to the ducts. Duct losses are then
calculated based on the supply temperature, airflow rate, and duct characteristics using a custom
routine in EnergyPlus.

For propane furnaces, the AFUE used here requires a condensing furnace, modeled with a side
vent. Lower efficiency equipment often uses a vertical atmospheric vent, as it vents at a higher
temperature and can rely on buoyancy. In OpenStudio-HPXML, the type of vent affects the

 5

infiltration in the home, as atmospheric vents can increase infiltration. This means this upgrade
can also decrease infiltration in these homes if they don’t have another atmospheric vent for the
water heater; see references for more details on when atmospheric flues are modeled (HPXML
2025).

In OpenStudio-HPXML, each central furnace is represented under:
“/HPXML/Building/BuildingDetails/Systems/HVAC/HVACPlant/HeatingSystem,” with type
“Furnace,” and a federal minimum efficiency or like-for-like efficiency replacement.
OpenStudio-HPXML maps these fields to an OpenStudio Furnace object, setting capacity,
efficiency, distribution, and fan characteristics. Below is the input schema support, both required
and optional fields.

4.3 Stock Energy Impacts
Out of 550,000 model building simulations (i.e., models), 31,876 models are applicable for the
propane and fuel oil furnace upgrade (5.8%), representing the 139,647,020 dwellings from
American Community Survey’s 2021, 5-year estimate of U.S. households (not including
territories). For the 31,876 applicable units, 13,200 use fuel oil and 18,676 use propane. For the
applicable models, the total baseline consumption is 302 billion kWh per year, and after the
propane and fuel oil furnace upgrade, the total consumption is 278 billion kWh per year, a 7.9%
savings.

Figure 6 shows that energy savings from the propane and fuel oil furnace upgrade mostly come
from heating-related end uses.

 12

 Figure 6. Annual energy consumption by fuel end use and heating fuel
In dwellings heated with fuel oil, energy used for heating from fuel oil reduced 9.3%, from 98.2
billion kWh to 89.1 billion kWh after the upgrade. In dwellings heated with propane, energy
used for heating from propane reduced 13.1%, from 101.4 billion kWh to 88.1 billion kWh after
the upgrade.

100% of energy savings for dwellings heated with fuel oil is sourced from fuel oil heating end
uses. While 93% of average annual energy savings in dwellings heated with propane come from
propane heating, cooling fans pumps using electricity composes 3%, heating fans pumps using
electricity compose 3%, and cooling using electricity compose 1% of annual average energy
savings, as shown in Figure 7. As mentioned in the Modeling Approach section, installation of
the propane furnace can improve infiltration, which explains these energy savings. Because of
this slight improvement in performance in propane furnace households, an annual average of 8.8
hours of unmet cooling hours are saved.
