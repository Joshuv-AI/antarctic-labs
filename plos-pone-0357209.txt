Study variation in estimating Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) from serum creatinine, cystatin C, age, and sex

Nora Fino, Ben Haaland, Lesley A Inker, Andrew Levey, Josef Coresh, Ogechi M Adingwupu, Tom Greene

ABSTRACT
Introduction: In routine clinical practice, kidney function is assessed by estimating the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) using equations that combine serum creatinine and/or cystatin C with age and sex. These equations are typically created by pooling datasets from multiple research studies and populations into a single dataset. We investigate implications of study and population variation on the relationship of GFR with its predictor variables. Methods: We combined data used to develop and validate the 2021 and 2012 The Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equations based on creatinine or cystatin C (13,391 individuals in 26 studies). We used random effects modeling to partition the deviations between measured GFR (mGFR) and estimated GFR (eGFR) into four components: predictable variation between studies of CKD vs. non-CKD populations, non-predictable variation between studies, patient-level error in eGFR within studies, and measurement error in mGFR. Results: Overall, the average eGFR was similar across different levels of the predictor variables between the random effect models and the original pooled CKD-EPI equations. However, the random effect model demonstrated substantial variation between studies, both between and within CKD and non-CKD populations. After accounting for the population type (CKD or non-CKD), approximately 10–40% of the deviations between eGFR and mGFR was attributable to study variation, 20–40% to measurement error in mGFR, and 20–70% to patient level errors in eGFR. Both pooled and random‐effects models generalized well across studies, whereas using equations derived from a single study leads to substantial overfitting and larger prediction errors. Conclusions: Single‐study GFR equations may underestimate cross-study prediction error by ignoring study‐level variability, especially in non-CKD populations. Our results largely corroborate the use of pooled datasets and the current form of the CKD-EPI equations. Despite measureable study-level heterogeneity, the pooled CKI-EPI apparach remains a pragmatic and broadly available solution for GFR estimation.

Introduction The glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is widely considered the best indicator of kidney function but it cannot be measured directly. Instead, standard clinical practice is to calculate an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) using estimating equations based on serum concentrations of endogenous filtration markers, usually creatinine and/or cystatin C, and demographic variables such as age and sex. Current guidelines recommend creatinine-based eGFR (eGFRcr) for general use, with cystatin C–based eGFR (eGFRcys) or combined eGFRcr-cys used when greater accuracy is needed [ 1 ]. The Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equations for estimating GFR [ 2 ] are the most common equations used for adults in practice [ 1 , 3 ]. The CKD-EPI equations are effective, with 80–90% of patients having eGFRs within 30% of their mGFR in both the general population and patients with CKD [ 2 ]. These equations were developed using pooled data from diverse groups, including patients with chronic kidney disease or diabetes, healthy kidney donor candidates, and individuals from North America and Europe. Combining across multiple datasets ensure that broad distributions of creatinine, cystatin C, age, and sex were represented. Pooling data across studies necessarily averages over differences between source studies, such as how GFR was measured or which patients were included. These factors may introduce variability in the marker-mGFR relationship that is not explicitly represented in the final clinical equation. Thus, even when pooled equations perform well for broad clinical use, it remains useful to quantify the extent of study-level variation and its implications for prediction uncertainty. Additionally, the relationship between GFR and its predictors may depend on clinical context. Just as predictive values of diagnostic tests often differ between screening populations and patients with known disease, the relationship between measured GFR with its predictor variables may vary across settings. For example, the relationship between mGFR and creatinine or cystatin C differs between studies focusing on healthy individuals, kidney donor candidates or other populations having few patients with substantially reduced GFR compared to studies whose entry criteria restrict to patients with CKD, as shown by Murata et al [ 4 ]. Our overall goal in this paper is to investigate the implications of study variation in the relationship of GFR with the predictor variables serum creatinine or cystatin C, age, and sex. We apply random effects models that account for study-to-study variation to quantify the magnitude and consquences of this variation and to assess whether it materially challenges the pooled approach. Methods Study populations & clinical context We used data from research studies and clinical populations ( S1 File Study Collaborators and S2 File : Table S1) associated with the CKD-EPI database. Specifically, we included data used to develop and validate the currently used CKD-EPI eGFR equations [ 2 ]: CKD-EPI 2009 and CKD-EPI 2021 creatinine equations (10 studies, 8,254 participants), CKD-EPI 2012 cystatin C equation (13 studies, 5,352 participants), and CKD-EPI 2021 creatinine and cystatin C equations (12 studies, 4,050 participants). In total, there were 13,398 unique individuals across 27 studies, as several studies overlap between the 2009, 2012, and 2021 datasets. We excluded one study (7 participants) due to its small sample size; therefore, our final analytic dataset contained 13,391 individuals in 26 studies. All studies received approval from the institutional review boards of the participating institutions. All studies have mGFR determined by urinary or plasma clearance of exogenous filtration markers; we calibrated mGFR using plasma clearance of iohexol by increasing it by 5% to align with other methods [ 2 , 5 , 6 ]. Creatinine and cystatin C measurements were standardized across studies (Supplemental Methods). To summarize the clinical contexts of the studies, we categorized the studies into two distinct groups: studies that explicitly enrolled individuals with CKD (CKD populations) and studies of individuals enrolled for other reasons (non-CKD populations). The non-CKD classification should not be interpreted as indicating that all participants were free of CKD; rather, these studies were not designed to enroll participants on the basis of established CKD status, and some individuals within these populations may have met clinical criteria for CKD. Statistical modeling framework Our primary goal is to characterize how the relationship between mGFR and its predictor variables varies across studies and to evaluate the impact of this variation on GFR estimation. The CKD-EPI equations estimated log-transformed mGFR from log-transformed creatinine and/or cystatin C, age, and sex, including two linear splines for creatinine and/ or cystatin C [ 2 ]. The general structure of these equations is provided in the Supplemental Methods. For simplicity, we first focus on study variation in the relationship of mGFR with creatinine, age, and sex; analyses for cystatin C with demographic variables follow similarly. Next, we applied a random effects model for eGFRcr. Random effects modeling represents a compromise between the complete pooling (as in the original equation) approach and the no-pooling approach (as in the study-specific models) [ 7 ]. Using a random effects model, we can explicitly distinguish the variation in mGFR between and within the different studies. Because we hypothesized that the mGFR–creatinine relationship differs between CKD and non-CKD populations, we allowed the creatinine coefficients to vary by study type. More details are provided in the Supplement, but the general form of the random effects models can be decomposed into five parts: log ( m G F R ) = [ population equation ] ⏟ ( 1 ) + [ deviation in the population equation for non − CKD studies vs . CKD studies ] ⏟ ( 2 ) + [ unpredictable study deviations ] ⏟ ( 3 ) + [ error in predicting true GFR within a given study ] ⏟ ( 4 ) + [ error as a result of measurement error in mGFR ] ⏟ ( 5 ) Where term (1) estimates the mean log-mGFR as a function of creatinine, sex, and age across all studies, Term (2) applies a fixed adjustment to allow for deviations between studies of CKD vs. predominantly non-CKD populations, term (3) comprises the random intercepts and slopes for log-creatinine, age, and sex, capturing additional study-specific variation around the adjusted equations in Terms (1) and (2) Term (4) reflects the within-study prediction error (with its standard deviation allowed to differ between CKD and non-CKD studies), and Term (5) reflects assay-related noise in mGFR itself. The random-effects structure was selected to capture study-specific deviations in the overall level of mGFR and in the associations of mGFR with the main filtration marker, age, and sex, while maintaining a parsimonious model that could be estimated across studies of varying sample size. We included random intercepts and random slopes for log creatinine or log cystatin C, age, and sex. We did not include separate random effects for each spline term because, within many studies, creatinine or cystatin C values were concentrated predominantly on one side of the knot; estimating separate study-specific random effects for each spline segment would therefore be unstable. Instead, a single random slope for the filtration marker was used to represent study-specific tilting of the marker–mGFR relationship Variance decomposition & model evaluation Using the final random effects models, we partitioned the total residual variance in log mGFR that remained after accounting for the fixed effects terms into three parts: between-study variation, within-study variation, and measurement error in mGFR (see Supplemental Methods). We assumed that log mGFR provides an unbiased estimate of log true GFR, and that the measurement error variance in log mGFR is 0.015 based on previous studies of repeated GFR measurements in the African American Study of Kidney Disease and Hypertension [ 8 ] (AASK) and The Modification of Diet in Renal Disease [ 9 ] (MDRD) studies (Table S2 in S2 File ). Given the log scale, this measurement error variance corresponds to a coefficient of variation (CV) of about 12.3% for the untransformed mGFR (i.e., on average, the measurement varies by about 12% relative to the true value; calculated as exp ( 0 . 0 1 5 ) − 1 = 1 2 . 3 % ). The variance decompositions should be interpreted as model-based estimates of the relative contributions of between-study variation, within-study patient-level variation, and mGFR measurement error; as such, summaries focus on the relative magnitude of these sources of variation under the fitted model. Lastly, we empirically assessed the impact of study variation on the performance of GFR estimating equations derived from single studies versus those pooled across multiple studies. Error was summarized using the root mean square error (RMSE) for predicted vs. measured GFR. For each study, we first computed RMSE of study-specific models using patient-level cross-validation within that study. We then assessed cross-study generalizability using a leave-one-study-out internal validation approach, fitting the model on all other studies combined and applying it to the held-out study. Next, we evaluated a pooled estimate that explicitly accounted for study effects by fitting the random-effects model (defined as above) to all other studies combined and applying the fixed-effect coefficients to the target study. To examine the performance of study-specific equations, we calculated the average RMSE obtained when applying each study-specific equation to all remaining studies, and repeated this analysis within study classifications (CKD vs. non-CKD populations). Finally, we assessed overfitting by calculating two ratios: the ratio of the average RMSE of single-study equations to the average within-study cross-validation RMSE, and the ratio of the average RMSE of single-study equations applied within the same study classification to the corresponding within-study cross-validation RMSE. As shown in the Supplemental Methods, when a single-study equation is applied to a new study, the total prediction error is approximately the sum of the individual-level variation and coefficient error, plus about twice the study-level variation. For example, in a CKD female with creatinine of 2.0 mg/dL, the study-specific total variance was 0.012, compared to 0.044 for individual-level variation, meaning a single-study equation would underestimate error by nearly 50%. Discussion CKD-EPI and other established GFR estimating equations were developed using pooled data from multiple studies to create versatile formulas for eGFR that are practical for clinical use. In our analyses, random effects models that explicitly allowed for study-level variation produced results consistent with the pooled equations, providing corroboration for the pooled data approach. At the same time, we revealed considerable variation in how measured GFR relates to creatinine, cystatin C, age, and sex across studies. The apparent success of the pooled equations despite study-level heterogeneity likely reflects several features of the CKD-EPI development strategy. First, pooling across many studies averages over idiosyncratic recruitment patterns and measurement contexts, producing an equation that estimates the mean relationship expected across a broad target population. Second, the major structure of the relationship between filtration markers and mGFR is captured by the CKD-EPI functional form, so allowing random study effects primarily widens uncertainty and captures study-specific deviations rather than changing the population-average equation substantially. Third, pooling protects against overfitting to any single study. Thus, the same heterogeneity that creates uncertainty for individual prediction also explains why equations developed in one study may not transport well, whereas pooled equations can remain robust for broad clinical use. Most of the variation across studies can be traced to systematic differences in how participants were recruited. Some studies enrolled individuals on the basis of disease status, such as patients with a clinical diagnosis of CKD or healthy kidney donor candidates, while others selected participants within specific ranges of creatinine or eGFRcr. These recruitment strategies created two broad classes of studies, which we have broadly described as CKD populations and non-CKD populations (even though some non-CKD populations included individuals with CKD), and much of the observed variation simply reflects difference between these study populations. This finding is consistent with the well recognized property predictive value of a diagnostic test depends on whether it is applied in a screening population or in patients with established disease. The change in the relationship between mGFR and its predictors depending on clinical context may seem like a useful insight to incorporate into equations for an individual patient; however, in practice clinicians often do not know whether a patient has CKD or how severe it is before ordering creatinine, and it is precisely in this setting of uncertainty that estimating equations are most commonly applied. The CKD-EPI equation, for instance, yields intermediate estimates of GFR between CKD and non-CKD populations, largely because non-CKD studies excluded participants with lower GFR, whereas CKD studies excluded those with higher GFR. Similar issues would be expected to arise for other GFR estimating equations developed from pooled populations, such as the EKFC equations [ 10 , 11 ]. As with all prediction equations, regression models for GFR estimates describe the average relationship between markers and mGFR with reasonable precision, but the uncertainty becomes much larger when applied to an individual. Our results provide quantification of the uncertainty in the eGFR. The magnitude of the uncertainty is consequential when eGFR values are close to decision thresholds. Small differences in eGFR may influence CKD staging, referral to nephrology, eligibility for therapies, medication dosing, or decisions to obtain confirmatory testing. In practice, this means that consistent with current clinical practice guidelines, an eGFR near a classification or referral threshold should be interpreted in clinical context, and confirmatory testing with cystatin C, combined creatinine-cystatin C eGFR, or measured GFR may be appropriate when the result would change management{, 2024 #185}. We were not able to incorporate all information that might be available to clinicians, such as extremes of muscle mass or diet. Prior work has not found that including common diseases or treatments as variables has improved the performance of creatinine-based eGFR equations [ 12 ]. Although “disease-specific” or “treatment-specific” eGFR equations could reduce variability in select populations, they may be impractical in clinical practice, as patients generally have more than one disease and undergo more than one treatment [ 13 , 14 ]. Our findings also illustrate why are estimating equations developed in a single study population are not recommended for application in widespread practice. When a GFR-estimating equation is developed within a single study, its reported error reflects primarily individual-level variation within that study and does not capture the study-to-study differences that arise when the equation is applied to new populations. Thus, internal validation can understate cross-study prediction error. Conversely, when an equation appears to perform differently at a new site, those differences may reflect expected study-to-study variation rather than a failure of the original equation. Limitations of this analysis include our categorization of studies into CKD versus non-CKD was necessarily broad and encompassed heterogeneous subgroups. The non-CKD populations include healthy donor candidates as well as patients with HIV or diabetes who had high levels of GFR, while the CKD populations include CKD patients who have undergone transplant as well as those with disease in the native kidneys. We considered more granular classifications, including populations with CKD, populations at risk for CKD, and healthy populations, but selected the two-level classification to preserve model interpretability and focus on the primary distinction in study-entry criteria. Residual heterogeneity within these categories may contribute to the observed study-level differences. Importantly, some of these differences may reflect true biological variation, such as in transplant populations, where small changes in GFR have critical implications and warrant further investigation. In addition, we did not incorporate measurement error in creatinine or cystatin C, and our variance decomposition assumed a measurement-error variance of 0.015 for log mGFR, corresponding to an approximate CV of 12% based on variation roughly 4–16 weeks apart in large multi-center studies. This value was chosen a priori from repeated GFR measurements in two of the included studies. If the true measurement-error variance were smaller, for example closer to reports of median within-person CVs near 6% [ 15 ] based on repeat measurements in 40 patients over a mean 62 days apart, then a smaller proportion of total variation would be attributed to mGFR measurement error and a larger proportion would be attributed to within-study patient-level variability. The estimated between-study component, however, would be largely unchanged because it is determined by the random study effects rather than by the assumed mGFR measurement-error variance. Accounting for marker measurement error and refining study classifications could further clarify sources of heterogeneity. Overall, our findings suggest that the CKD-EPI pooled equations are not subject to substantial residual confounding by study effects. Although measurable study-level heterogeneity exists, the pooled approach mitigates overfitting, accounts for study-to-study variation, and provides more reliable predictions than single-study equations. These results primarily provide empirical validation of the existing pooled CKD-EPI approach, while also quantifying the uncertainty that remains when any estimating equation is applied to an individual patient.
