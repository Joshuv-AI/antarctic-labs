1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This document is the Final Technical Report (FTR) for University of Minnesota (UMN) project on the
development of a novel sediment (e.g., sand) bypass technology for new stream reach development
(NSD). The project duration was slightly over four years and organized over two budget periods (BPs),
i.e., BP1 and BP2, with a Go/No-Go review at the end of BP1. The initial technology had a Technology
Readiness Level (TRL) of two (2) and, over the life of the project, advanced substantially to TRL 3 or 4.
UMN submitted a proposal of this project to the United States (U.S.) Department of Energy (DOE) in
response to the Water Power Technologies Office (WPTO) Funding Opportunity Announcement (FOA)
DE-FOA-0002080, Water Power Technologies Office 2019 Research Funding Opportunity, which included
a solicitation for proposals focused on the development of modular technologies for low-head
hydropower applications. The objectives of this project were to advance the design of a modular,
scalable, and cost-competitive sediment bypass system for hydropower dams and associated reservoirs
to mitigate the discontinuity in sediment transport associated with hydropower dams.
The project team was led by the UMN St. Anthony Falls Laboratory (SAFL) with partnering institutions
including Utah State University, Barr Engineering Company, and Voigt Consulting. The project team was
fortunate to access technical assistance from personnel with Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL).
ORNL supported UMN with initial design review, advanced materials and manufacturing (AMM), cost
modeling, and research on sand transport.
The first task of the project was the development of a Preliminary Design Package, which summarized
the initial design, research objectives, cost modeling, and plans for materials and manufacturing. ORNL
and DOE staff assisted UMN in refining the design package and finalizing the research roadmap.
An important attribute of this bypass approach is that it is designed to operate throughout the year,
capturing and bypassing sand at the same rate that it was naturally transported in the river system. This
is a distinguishing and novel feature of the system. Alternative approaches to mitigating reservoir
sedimentation (e.g. dredging, sluicing) occur irregularly and remove a large volume of sediment in a
short amount of time. The impact is that sediment concentration within the river become
superelevated, often with negative ecological and geomorphic consequences.
BP1 of the project focused on developing a 40% conceptual design for all components of the sediment
bypass system. Some of the early work focused on evaluating sand bed rivers and determining the range
of “typical” attributes of these systems (e.g. geomorphic properties, grainsize ranges, hydrology and
hydraulics, slopes). Through a large literature search, the project team explored hundreds of rivers and
extracted important characteristics necessary to further develop and optimize the design. The SMH
Explorer 1 by ORNL was used as a tool for finding example sites which have favorable conditions for
potential new stream reach dams with low head (< 10 m) on a sand bed river. We selected two locations
to serve as test cases for development – the Missouri River at Landsky, MT and the Kansas River at
Wamego, KS.
The initial sand bypass system design did not include pumps, using only the 10 meters of water head
behind the dam to drive the sand bypass system. This type of system is known as hydrosuction, where
the elevation drop across the system is sufficient to transport sediment out of the reservoir. The design

included arrays of guide vanes to steer sand toward inlets, sand capture inlets (suction) placed on the
river bed that ingest sand along with water, a series of manifolds that collected and combined water
into a smaller number of conveyance pipes, large diameter conveyance pipes that carried the low-
concentration sand-water mixture downstream of the dam, and a discharge apron that served to
redistribute the sand back into the river.
One way for reducing the number of sand capture inlets needed across the river involved developing in-
stream guide vanes that served to “steer” sand laterally and toward a collection area. BP1 included a
large effort in developing permeable vanes and examining optimal placement and orientation in the
river channel and then documenting, through laboratory experiments, the performance of the vanes.
The sediment bypass system also included consideration of bypass technologies for fine-grained
sediments like clays and silts. The concept used siphons at the dam itself to bypass this material. This
technology was removed from the project during the Go/No-Go review as the coarse sediment system
required more team resources with the addition of pumps to the design.
In addition to advancing design details of each component, major questions tackled during BP1 included:
 • How far upstream of the dam can a gravity driven hydrosuction system feasibly work?
 • Where in the reservoir should the sand capture inlets be located?
 • What are the sand transport processes at the possible capture locations?
 • What is the optimal design geometry of the sand inlet to minimize headloss?
 • What is the friction factor in the conduits and how does it vary with grainsize, concentration,
 velocity and pipe diameter?

BP1 also involved evaluating advanced material and manufacturing in partnership with ORNL. We
performed a baseline cost assessment that examined existing technologies used for reservoir
sedimentation and developed an initial cost model for the sand bypass system.
At the end of BP1, the project underwent a Go/No-Go review led by DOE which resulted in UMN
receiving a “Go” determination to continue with BP2 activities. The Go/No-Go review provided
important mid-project guidance to the project team and refinement of the BP2 scope of work. BP2
focused on developing an 80% conceptual design, finalizing evaluation of advanced material and
manufacturing, cost modeling, and full-system validation testing in a large laboratory flume.
Several major changes occurred in the system’s design resulting from insights gained during BP1 and
feedback received during the Go/No-Go review process. They included the following:
  • Include pumps in the design in order to extend the transport distance of the system.
  • Re-design from a single conduit spanning from the inlet to the dam to a 4-stage system.
  • Removal of the fine sediment bypass system from the system in order to focus more fully on sand
 bypass.
  • Determination that the capture location will be at the toe of the sand delta.

BP2 began with refocusing the design around the use of pumps to extend the transport distance of the
system while limiting energy and capital expenditure costs. This led to the separation of the sand
capture stage from the transport stage of the design leading to a 4-stage system.

 2

Stage 1 consists of sand intakes distributed across the river width to capture sand and water. The low
concentration slurry (<3 %vol) captured by the sand inlets is transported to the stage 2 component via
slurry pumps. Stage 2 involves the placement of a static dewatering and concentrating tank on an
adjacent riverbank. The slurry is dewatered and moved to stage 3. Stage 3 consists pumps to move high-
concentrated slurry from the settling tank to the dam and is able to do so over long distances. Stage 2
and Stage 3 leverage existing technologies often used in water and wastewater treatment and mining
applications. Much of the project was therefore focused on advancing the sand inlet nozzle design and
exploring health monitoring of a multi-inlet system. Stage 4 involves reintroducing sand to the river
downstream of the dam.
A major activity in BP2 was testing of the system in the large sand-transport flume at SAFL. The flume is
2.75 m wide and 1.8 m deep with sediment recirculation and high discharge capabilities. A reservoir
delta condition was established and a prototype sand inlet battery (three inlets) was deployed at the toe
of the delta. A large experimental series was conducted to optimize the design and performance of the
battery as well as validation testing.
Findings and lessons learned from the project were:
 • The UMN sand bypass system is a promising technology. It is novel in that no other solution for
 reservoir sedimentation offers near-continuous transport of sand. This attribute is important for
 river ecological and geomorphic health and would mitigate sand accumulation in upstream
 impoundments.
 • The original design of a single pipe and low-concentration hydrosuction system is not viable for
 application where long transport distances are needed. This is a limitation specific to the low-
 gradient and low-head (10 m) systems studied in this project.
 • There are potentially many applications for this technology beyond NSD sites. Existing non-
 powered dams, agricultural settings, or water intakes are all possible application areas.
 • The screening of the inlet is a key design component that was not resolved in this project.
 Clogging of sand inlets is a concern as rivers/streams carry organic and inorganic particulates.
 The next steps of the technology development will focus on screen systems.
 • Re-design to include a high-concentration slurry pump(s) for long distance transport of sand has
 large capital and operational costs but can extend the transport distance. Projects will need to
 assess the financial value of establishing sediment transport continuity across the NSD. The
 design leverages decades of experience and technology for pumping high-concentration sand-
 water slurries and water clarification.
 • AMM was considered in the project. Much of the design was at too early of a stage to support
 decision with using AMM. The sand inlets are one component that will benefit from AMM,
 providing both greater abrasion resistance and reduced manufacturing costs while preserving
 manufacturing precision needed for these components.
 • The project generated new knowledge that adds to basic research and applied engineering
 applications: a) a detailed survey of typical attributes of sand bed rivers in the US was
 conducted, b) detailed research on the friction factor within closed conduits for sand-water
 mixtures was performed and published in academic journals, c) laboratory research was
 performed on the sand transport, scour, and lateral transport in the vicinity of permeable guide
 vanes. This work is published in academic journals, outputs given in Section 12.

2. PROJECT MOTIVATION
The University of Minnesota (UMN) is developing a technology for sediment bypass at hydropower
dams and other impoundments. The technology will provide near-continuous bypass of sand from
upstream of the dam to downstream of the dam and will provide pseudo-continuity in sediment
transport across the reservoir-dam reach. The context for design focuses on New Stream-reach
Development (NSD), meaning locations on rivers that presently do not have a dam or hydropower
installed 2. Further, the dam design embraces Standard Modular Hydropower (SMH) components, such
that individual, scalable dam elements, which include hydropower generation, can be placed and
integrated together in a cost effective and efficient manner.
The Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) developed an important reference document titled,
Exemplary Design Envelope Specification for Standard Modular Hydropower Technology 3. This report
evaluates key SMH technologies (e.g., fish passage, electrical power generation, sediment bypass, and
others) and provides guidance on key performance objectives for each type of technology, where
performance objectives, in many cases, address major barriers for the growth of hydropower in the US.
The important take away here is that Witt et al., 2017 defines performance objectives that are critical
for the operational and/or environmental “services” that are presently lacking at hydro facilities and
are essential services that must be part of any NSD (and development of non-powered dams for that
matter).
Witt et al., 2017 provides an important framework for the SMH sediment bypass, which becomes
important when developing baseline technology assessment. The key take-aways as they pertain to
sediment bypass are as follows:
A. Prevent or minimize accumulation of sediment in the upstream reservoir
This is a key performance objective or “service” sought in the SMH module. The deposition of sand in
reservoirs results in upstream and downstream degradation of the local environment. Sediment
deposition in reservoirs ultimately causes operational and maintenance issue for hydropower owners.
B. Provide continuity in the natural sediment transport rate
It is important to understand that sediment is continually transported within healthy river and stream
systems. This is a natural process associated with water cycle, rock cycle and earth-surface dynamics –
healthy rivers adjust their slopes, widths, and depths to be in balance with water and sediment loads.
The rate of sediment transport in a river varies in time (hourly to seasonally), with hydrology of the
watershed, and rate of delivery of sediment into surface waters. Installation of a dam and reservoir
disrupts this natural sediment transport and results in undesirable geomorphic change - deposition of

3. OVERVIEW OF THE TECHNOLOGY
The UMN sediment bypass system is designed to prevent sedimentation within reservoirs - provide
continuity in natural sediment transport across a reservoir/dam, specifically sand bed river systems, and
is therefore limited to sand size particles or smaller.
The bypass system initially sought to adopt “hydrosuction”, which is a technique for reservoir sediment
management. In principle, hydrosuction utilizes the potential energy within the water stored behind the
dam to drive sediment bypass 4 5. Through this project, we identified technical and economic limitations
to hydrosuction for low head/low gradient systems and thus, mid-project, migrated the design to
include the addition of pumping systems to allow greater flexibility and scalability of the design.
The current form of the sand bypass system includes four stages. The first stage focuses on capture and
low-concentration transport of sand. The second stage involves dewatering and concentrating the sand,
the third stage is high-concentration transport to downstream of the dam, and the fourth stage is
returning sand to the river, distributing the sand in a way that doesn’t result in erosion or sediment
build-up. The four-stage design improves robustness and provides greater flexibility and scalability so
that it can accommodate a larger range of river types, dam head situations, and transport distances.

Design challenges
There are certain design challenges that needed to be overcome in the project and these are
summarized below:
 A. Limitation in initial available energy. A major design objective was to position the sand intakes
 as far upstream in the reservoir as possible – ideally at the upstream extent of the reservoir.
 Also, we sought to design a hydrosuction system for sand bypass that uses the potential energy
 behind the dam (no suction pumps). Given that the dam height was limited to a maximum of 10
 meters in the Funding Opportunity Announcement (FOA), and sand bed rivers, which are low
 gradient with very long reservoirs, there is a limit to how far upstream of the dam this system
 can extend.
 B. Site specific design versus Standard Modular designs. The objective of the SMH model for next
 generation hydropower is to develop standard, scalable designs. Traditional hydropower dam
 permitting, construction, and operation is a site-specific endeavor. Our objective was to develop

4. REVIEW OF SAND BED RIVERS
We carried out a review of the characteristics of sand bed rivers, with the purpose of targeting our
design to relevant stream parameters. Figure 4.1 illustrates the locations in the United States described
in reference materials used in a meta-analysis of sand bed river data. The figure helps visualize locations
of sand bed rivers within the U.S. Many physical characteristics including bed slope, sinuosity, width,
depth, median grain size of bed sediment (d50), and sediment composition were compiled and used to
create histograms, which gauge trends of sand bed rivers. In general, sand bed rivers tend towards
lower channel slopes with smaller particles; with median (d50) bed sediment of around 0.35 mm. The

7. SUMMARY OF VARIATION IN DESIGN OVER THE LIFE OF PROJECT
There were several adjustments in the approach and design of the bypass system over the life of this
project and these are summarized in this section of the report.
Eliminate fine sediment capture system from the design
Many sand-bed rivers carry a find-sediment load (silts and clays) which deposit by settling processes in
more distal regions of the reservoir. The fine sediment bypass system was proposed as a siphon-based
system located at the upstream face of the dam and was a fully separate system from the sand bypass
system. After receiving feedback during the Go/No-Go review, development of the fine-sediment
system was paused so that all focus could be placed on the sand bypass technology. This change was
reflected in the BP2 Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO).
Finalized optimal capture location for sand bypass inlets
The process of sand accumulation in reservoirs is through delta formation and progradation. Deposition
of sand typically occurs at the transition to backwater effect caused by the dam. Sand will deposit at this
transition, resulting in the formation of a delta. Sand will continue to transport across the top of the

 9

delta to the delta front and deposit at the depositional front or foreset and, over time, the river will
aggrade upward as the delta grows into the reservoir. Figure 7.1 illustrates the regrading and delta-
growth process from the initial reservoir (A) through the near complete infilling of the reservoir (D).

Figure 7.1. Schematic illustrating the idealized (2D) infilling of a sand delta into a hydropower reservoir. A) initial state after
dam construction. B, C and D illustrate infilling. Sedimentation is through upstream to downstream progradation of the delta
by deposition along the delta top and front.

The sinuosity of the river and the ratio of the river channel width to reservoir width adds complexity to
the infilling process. The delta may not grow uniformly across the width but often involves multi-thread
(braided and anastomosing) river planforms. Figure 7.2 includes aerial photographs of several sand bed
river/reservoir situations that illustrate the complexity of delta formation. Photograph A is of the
reservoir upstream of the Rapidan Dam on the Blue Earth River near Mankato, Minnesota. The dam was
completed in 1910 forming a reservoir over 6 miles long; Today the reservoir is 100% filled and contains
an estimated 12,000,000 yd3 of sediment. The photograph shows the highly sinuous river upstream and
the complex, multi-channel infilling. Photograph B is the upstream portion of Lake Pepin, a large natural
lake formed in the Upper Mississippi River. The Chippewa River flows into the Mississippi River near
river mile 764 with a high sand load. The natural delta formed at the mouth of the Chippewa acts to
limit the flow capacity of the Mississippi resulting in a backwater lake. Lake Pepin is 21 miles long. At the
upstream end of the lake, sand and silts are deposited forming a prograding delta, which is gradually
infilling the lake. The image shows the sinuous channel formations, bars, and islands that make up the
delta.

The processes associated with reservoir infilling are important to understand when determining 1)
where to locate the sand inlets for the bypass system and 2) how they should be designed to match the
transport physics. The inlet location was an open design variable at the onset of the project with the
following questions:
 • For NSD and a new reservoir, where is the optimal location to place the sand inlets? Should it
 be located at the upstream end of the reservoir to immediately start capturing sand but more
 expensive because of the long distances (many kilometers for low-gradient rivers). Or should it
 be located in the downstream portion of the reservoir, reducing costs for capital equipment but
 potentially needing to wait many decades before sand reaches the intakes.
 • What region of the sand wedge should be targeted for the design of the inlets? Sand is actively
 transported along the top of the delta or should we target the delta front (foreset)?
 • Can we develop technologies (vanes) to “steer” sediment transport with the river channel
 toward a smaller number of sand inlets? The vanes would be much lower cost compared to
 additional inlets and conduits. Where should these be located within the channel and what are
 the design details of the vanes?
 • How far can a pure hydrosuction (no pumps) bypass system operate effectively and what are
 the trade-offs of a bypass system with pumps?
At the start of BP2, we made the decision to focus the design of the inlets on the delta front rather than
upstream of the foreset. This decision was made because sand rapidly settles at this location in a
predictable way. By capturing sand at the toe of the foreset, we can effectively stall the further
progradation of the delta.
Complete permeable guide vane development
We developed a design for permeable guide vanes based on laboratory experiments and leveraging
previous research. The vanes serve to steer the transport pathways of sand within the fluvial portions of
the channel (on top of the delta) and were shown to be effective in reducing the number of inlets
needed by guiding sand toward inlets. The permeable vanes are viewed as an optional component of
the system. They are only effective within a fluvial portion of a reservoir system and will not work in the
deeper portions of a reservoir. The research and outcomes of this effort are summarized in Appendix A.
Additional of conveyance pumps to design
In BP1, our development work revealed serious limitations in the transport distance of a pure
hydrosuction system. After receiving feedback from the BP1 Go/No-Go review, we included pumps
within the design. This provides greater flexibility in where to locate the inlets within the reservoir.
Development of a 4-stage system
The design activities integrating pump(s) into the design led to further refinement of the bypass system.
The design was broken into a 4-stage pump system to reduce capital costs and increase energy
efficiency of the system.

Pumping system layout
Initial bypass design completed in BP1 examined using gravity flow through conduits for transport of the
sediment, but this was determined to be unfeasible. Pumps are required to provide additional energy to
transport the sediment through the pipeline.
The first iteration pump design examined pumping a dilute sand slurry directly from the collection
nozzles to downstream of the dam. This was also shown to be unfeasible, because the energy
requirements are on the same scale as the potential energy produced by the hydro dam. The greatest
energy consumption was from the long-distance pipeline portion of the transport system, and most of
the pumping energy was to transport the large volume of water through the pipeline. This led to a 4-
stage alternative that includes a dewatering/concentrating tank to remove the bulk of the water, and
greatly reduces energy requirements for pumping.
Pump type
The project team evaluated various types of pumps for this application. Centrifugal pumps are often
used for slurry transport due to their low capital cost and predictable maintenance needs. This comes at
the cost of efficiency and limitations on pumping pressure, which would require multiple pumps in
series to achieve the required pumping pressure and higher energy consumption. Positive displacement
pumps, such as piston diaphragm pumps, can have efficiencies of 90-95% and are designed for high-
pressure applications. A piston diaphragm pump has been selected for this long-distance pumping
application. The energy efficiency increase and design simplicity are the main considerations for this
choice. Another consideration for selecting a positive displacement pump is the ability to pump the
sediment slurry over a long distance. Centrifugal pumping would require multiple pumps in series and
multiple booster stations along the pump route to accommodate the maximum pump case pressures of
the pumps.

 Description Deliverables

The purpose of the health monitoring system for the sediment bypass system is to aid in the operation
and control of the sand intakes. Due to the harsh environment the sand intakes are deployed in,
constant monitoring of the pipe velocity and sediment concentration is required. Further, because it is
not practical to design an intake system that can handle the maximum sediment transport (bedload)
possible for a given river, it is planned to control the vertical position of the sand intakes to control the
amount of sand being ingested. During very high river discharge, the amount of sediment moving as
bedload could exceed what the intake system can handle resulting in pumps being overwhelmed,
clogging, or burial of the intakes. The health monitoring system is intended to measure the sediment
concentration and take action to reduce risk of clogging or burial. If the amount of sediment exceeds the
design maximum, the intakes can be raised above the riverbed reducing the amount of sand being
ingested preventing clogging or burial.
Prototype testing performed in the main channel was done to test a health monitoring concept where
the pressure differential across the inlet nozzle was measured. The concept involved measuring the
pressure differential, which equates to headloss, for a range of sediment concentrations. What would
result is a lookup table of headloss for known sediment concentrations. Once the lookup table is
established it could be used for vertical control of the intake. If the headloss, and therefore sediment
concentration, exceeded a set point value, the intakes would make an adjustment by raising X distance
above its current vertical position to reduce the amount of sand being sucked up.
However, during prototype testing in the main channel (see prototype testing section for details) no
relationship between sediment concentration and pressure differential measurements was observed. A
new technique for monitoring sediment concentration needs to be developed.

Review of screening technologies
Screening at water intakes is common in applications of water treatment, cooling water inlet structures,
hydropower generation, and several industrial processes. We studied these industries for ideas and
concepts that can inform the bypass design, including:
 • Bar screens with rakes– typically vertically oriented bars or groves that provide a coarse filter to
 intake water. In some cases, bar screens are the first filter because they can be structurally
 robust, able to withstand large forces from large trees or ice. As materials build up on the bar
 screen, either automated or manually operated rakes are used to remove material off the
 screen and into a waste trough or deck.
 • Traveling screen systems – mesh filter screen or panels of screens placed within an inlet
 channel and that are constantly rotating. The screen forms a continuous loop and, as it rotates,
 debris is caught on the screen and is lifted out of the water. High press sprayers clean off the
 debris and route into a waste stream. Clean screens return to the bottom of the channel and
 repeat the process.
 • Passive screens with air sparge – passive screens used for water intake and can have opening
 sizes that are quite small, keeping out aquatic organisms and debris. They are often fabricated
 as cylinders wrapped with wedge-wire with precision spacing to control hydraulic efficiency and
 filtering. Air sparge systems are often coupled with passive screens. As material collects on the
 screen surface, performance decreases and when this reaches a certain level, the water intake
 system is turned off and compressed air is pushed into the chamber, forcing air outward
 through the filter. This action pushes accumulated debris off the filter surface.
Review of regulations pertaining to screening for fish impingement
In general, federal, state and regional regulations establish maximum allowable velocity at the screen
surface. Velocities beyond the maximum velocity could result in fish being trapped or impinged against
the screen resulting in mortality. Regulations also can limit the width of screen openings to prevent fish
passage through the screen.
The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Clean Water Act section 316(b) is the most commonly
cited regulation. The intake velocity performance standard proposed by EPA for cooling water intake
structures (CWIS) is 0.5 ft/s 7. EPA 316(b) includes no guidance on screen opening size.
Other federal regulations by the National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS), establishes criteria for
protection of anadromous species, and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) has developed criteria

Coarse Sediment Intake – Main Channel Experiment
A laboratory scale coarse sediment intake system was tested in SAFL’s main channel. The main channel
is an open channel flume that is 2.75 m wide, 1.8 m deep, and 83.8 m long. The primary goal of the
laboratory scale experiment was to design and test multiple sediment intakes in a reduced array along
the channel cross section. Three intakes were designed to work together to intercept all the sediment
accumulating at a delta front and transport it as a slurry to a downstream position. The boundary
conditions were designed to mimic a sediment bypass system across a low head hydropower dam.
Design metrics to be assessed and validated include the spacing between intakes and the sediment
capture efficiency. Proof of concept experiments were designed to investigate health monitoring
options and burial recovery.
Upstream of the intakes consisted of a fluvial section that was created prior to testing. This portion of
the experiment is where the sediment transport occurred under equilibrium conditions. The main
channel tail gate was set at an elevation to impound water creating a reservoir and sustaining the
evolution of a delta. The coarse sediment intakes were placed at the bottom location of the delta to
ingest coarse sediment depositing along the delta.
Performance conditions used during the testing were informed by experiments performed in the test
loop. A range of river discharges were tested corresponding to a range of sediment transport. To
quantify the efficiency of the intakes to capture the sediment transported along the fluvial section, both
the bathymetry of the delta and the mass flux passing through the intakes were measured in time. The
main goal was to keep the delta invariant under increasing discharge and transport rate.
Experimental Setup
The coarse sediment intake system was tested in an erodible open channel section of the SAFL main
channel, characterized by a D50 = 0.49 mm sand. Three separate intakes were used to span the width of
the channel. The intakes were operated together as an intake battery and installed on a rack system that
allowed for vertical movement, up to 30 cm above the sediment bed. A 7.5 hp trash pump was used to
drive the flow for the three intakes. The intakes were located at the toe of a delta that was formed in
the channel. Figures 8.15 and 8.16 provide an overview of the main channel experimental setup.
The coarse sediment intakes are located at 35.93 m at the toe of the delta formed in the channel. A
fluvial section where sediment was piled up formed a riverbed running from approximately 18 m to 34

 24

m. The length was necessary to ensure uniform flow and sediment transport in equilibrium conditions.
The sediment layer in the fluvial section had an approximate thickness of 1300 mm. Water depths over
the fluvial section ranged from 100 to 300 mm depending on the discharge used for a test case and on
the evolution of bedforms. As water flowed through the fluvial section of the setup, sediment was
transported to the delta and was deposited. The water depth transitioned to 900 – 1100 mm
immediately downstream of the intakes where the sediment depth was set at approximately 500 mm
and monitored during the experiment. A check dam was used to hold sediment in place located at 39 m,

Coarse Sediment Intake System
The intake nozzles used in the main channel experiment utilized the design concepts of the prototype
intake but were scaled for use in the main channel. See Section 11 and Appendix B for more details on
the intake design. The sediment size used in the experiments were full scale, the velocities used during
testing were also representative of prototype scale. The opening of the intake was designed to maximize
the spanwise width which results in maximizing the capture area per intake and minimizes the number
of intakes required to span a river width.

 30

Three intakes were used to span the width (2.75 m) of the channel and were mounted to a support rack
system. The rack allowed for 30 cm of vertical motion control, controlled by a Velmex slide and stepper
motor. The intakes were spaced such that the channel width was evenly divided between each nozzle
creating a capture width of 917 mm. That is, each nozzle would capture sediment within ~459 mm on
either side.
A scour hole was created by each intake to increase the region where sediment is captured by each
intake. As sediment slid down the face of the delta it fell into a scour hole created by an intake. The
scour depth was set to 22 cm, this could be controlled by moving the intakes up and down via the
Velmex slide. Using the angle of repose for wet sand, approximately 30 degrees, the capture width was
estimated to be 1016 mm providing approximately 10% overlap between intakes ensuring sediment
deposited in the delta is captured.

Sediment Monitoring and Recirculation System
The amount of sediment captured and moved by the sediment intake system was measured by the
sediment monitoring and recirculation system (SMRS). The SMRS is comprised of a cross-channel slot
(bedload trap) into which sediment falls, a sediment-flux monitoring system that spans the width of
each bedload trap below the flume’s floor that continuously collects and weighs entrapped sediment,
and a pumping system that returns the sediment to the upstream part of the channel via a 20 cm
diameter pipe.
The sediment weight is measured at 5000 Hz with an average of the measurements being saved to a
data file every 1 second. Using the flow rate within the coarse sediment intake system and the sediment
weight in time, a sediment concentration by volume can be calculated.
As outlined above, two of the bedload traps were used for this experiment. A shroud was constructed
around the two traps/weigh pans. Sediment and water would exit the coarse sediment pipe system via a
screened outlet, see Figure 8.21, and sediment would fall on to the weigh pans and be measured by the
SMRS data acquisition (DAQ) system.

 32

Tailgate and Reservoir depth control
An adjustable height tailgate with a sharp-crested weir is located at the end of the channel. Reservoir
depth could be controlled as well as metering water discharge. A sharp-crested weir equation was used
to measure the flow rate in the channel and saved to a data file at 1 Hz.
Sediment Height Measurement
The sediment elevation, including delta geometry, was measured by a custom measurement carriage
system, see Figure 8.27. The carriage system traverses the length of the main channel to perform
measurements such as water surface elevation or subaqueous topography at different x and y locations.
Subaqueous topography was measured using a JSR Ultrasonic sonar pulser. The sonar signal is processed
in real-time as the cart traverses the flume to give sediment z elevation at specific measurement
locations. This allowed the delta position to be tracked in time throughout each experiment test case to
verify delta stability.

Figure 8.27: Measurement cart system. Sonar measurements are made by the measurement head which moves in the x,y,
and z directions.

Measurement System Summary
Pressure differential was measured across each intake nozzle using three Rosemount R-3051S pressure
transducers. The pressure differential was measured at 1 Hz sampling rate for the duration of a test. This

 33

measurement was used for monitoring headloss across the nozzle and to provide an indication of
increased sediment intake or blockage.
The pipe velocity of the sediment intake system was measured downstream of the intake pump, see
Figure 8.21, near the outlet to the weigh pans by a Rosemount magnetic flow meter model 8750W100.
Pipe velocity was measured at 1 Hz sampling rate for the duration of tests. This measurement provided
a measurement of the intake velocity.
Water elevation was measured by Massa Ultrasonic transducers. One water surface elevation
measurement was located on the measurement carriage and made measurements in conjunction with
sediment z elevation measurements. A second water surface elevation was made near the channel
tailgate and is used in the sharp-crested weir equation to get flow rate. Water surface measurements on
the carriage are made every x direction step which was set to 10 mm. Water surface near the tailgate
was measured every second.
Flow rate in the channel was measured using a sharp-crested weir equation and sampled and recorded
at 1 Hz. This measurement provided the flow rate of water in the fluvial section of the channel.
Sediment elevation was measured by the carriage system during experimental test runs using a sonar
pulse system. Approximately every 20 minutes during a test run a scan was performed to capture the
sediment topography upstream of the delta and as much as of the delta as was possible. Topography
scans were performed from x = 27000 mm to x = 35300 mm at specific y locations. The sonar
measurements provide a point measurement for the sediment elevation. Multiple point measurements
along the x or y axis are performed in a scan. For experiments outlined in following sections, sonar point
measurements were taken every 10 mm along the x axis from x = 27000 to 35000 mm and along specific
spanwise locations y = 917 mm and y = 1375 mm.
Test Procedure
Testing of the coarse sediment intake system started with baseline runs at several channel discharges
and deploying the intakes at a normal operating position.
Baseline tests are described as follows:
    •   Channel discharge (test conditions); 171, 258, 349, 482, 667 l/s
 o Each test condition was ran for several hours to test for steady state conditions
 o Discharge is initially low and ramped up to the desired value for a test condition
 o Flow is stopped and the channel is drained after each test condition
 • Prior to each test condition, the downstream sediment elevation is measured
 o sediment accumulation downstream of the intakes indicates some sediment is
 bypassing the intake system
 • Monitor sediment transport/sediment concentration of the coarse sediment intake system
 • Monitor delta position via sonar scans every 20 minutes during each test condition
 o Sonar scans at y = 917 mm and x = 27000 to 35000 mm with 10 mm increments

After baseline testing proved the system was working as intended, delta recovery tests were performed.
These tests were intended to test the ability of the system to recover after the system was off for a
prescribed period of time. During these tests, the intakes were lifted up away from the delta toe and the
delta was allowed to progress downstream.

Budget Period 2: Materials/Advanced Manufacturing evaluation
During the scoping of BP2, the sand inlet was selected as the most viable component for AMM for the
following reasons:
 • The sand inlet is subjected to abrasion by continuous sand transport. Advanced material may
 help slow the rate of abrasion and extend the life of the component.
 • The inlet shape is complex, designed to be hydraulically efficient with low headloss. Advanced
 Manufacturing, such as precision mold development, would allow rapid and cost-effective
 fabrication.
 • A single installation of the bypass system will require many inlets and the components must be
 replaceable as failures occur. Advanced manufacturing will allow the ability to reliably produce
 components quickly and cost effectively.

The proposed scope of work for BP2 involved the following objectives:
 • Explore several manufacturing techniques for functional demo articles in order to optimize the
 material and the manufacturing process.
 • Evaluate the use of thermoplastic and their composites as a building material for the intake
 nozzle. Thermoplastics have wear and friction resistant properties and have a significant
 advantage over many metals in that plastic is corrosion resistant and do not really need
 specialized coating to prevent corrosion. The combination of corrosion and abrasion can
 significantly reduce the operational life cycle of a part.
 • In order to screen and test an array of thermoplastic material composites, start with additive
 manufacturing (AM) method for the fabrication of the nozzles. AM can produce highly complex
 geometries without the need for a mold or a die (which require an initial capital investment and
 long lead time). At this stage, we will investigate different thermoplastic material, such as
 polycarbonates (PC), polyethylene terephthalate glycol (PETG), acrylonitrile butadiene styrene
 (ABS), or thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) with carbon fiber (CF) or glass fiber (GF). Moreover,
 without the need for a specialized mold or die, several designs and intake sizes can be
 evaluated.
 • Several prototypes will be manufactured at ORNL’s Manufacturing Demonstration Facility (MDF)
 and tested at SAFL to evaluate the effect of the operating conditions on the material.
 • Once the screening process is performed, one design and one or two materials will be chosen
 for the manufacturing scalability test. As these intake nozzles will be produced in large
 quantities, a low lead time high-rate manufacturing process should be considered – such as
 injection molding (IM) or compression molding (CM) processes. In this case an investment in a
 mold or a die (tooling) will be made and trials to dial in the processing parameters will be

Pumping distance
Both example sites were reconfigured to explore the effect of reducing the pumping distance to 1 km
instead of the 10 km for the Kansas River example and 5 km for the Missouri River example. The
collection system in the river remained unchanged, and the most notable difference was the long-
distance pumps and pipeline. The reduction in pumping distance made centrifugal pumps more viable,
and a direct change in pipeline costs due to a shortened length was observed.
The capital cost for the Kansas River example changed from 66.1 million United States dollars (MUSD) to
31.4 MUSD while the operating cost decreased from 7.19 $/t to 4.50 $/t for the shorter pumping
distance.
The capital cost for the Missouri River example changed from 90.2 MUSD to 46.9 MUSD while the
operating cost decreased from 1.80 $/t to 1.06 $/t for the shorter pumping distance.
The observed reduction in capital cost by roughly 50% along with an equivalent reduction in operating
cost indicates that opportunities should be explored where the pumping distance could be minimized.
This could include site locations where the river has a higher gradient or locations where bends in the
river could create a natural shortcut for a pumping route to reach the downstream portion of the dam
without following the same path as the river.

Introduction
This section summarizes the current design of the sand bypass system and is organized from upstream
to downstream starting with required baseline information on the river system followed by design of
Stages 1 through 4.

Siting of the Bypass System
 1. The system is designed for sand bed river systems. It is not designed for gravel sized particles or
 mixed-load (gravel and sand) rivers.
 2. The dam location will be determined independently of the location of the sand bypass system.
 3. Channel sinuosity/planform should be considered when siting the capture system.
 a. Sediment transport rates are not always consistent across a river cross-section,
 especially in meandering channels where thread of high velocity and thalweg alternate
 position through a bend.
 b. Channel sinuosity and larger bends in a river planform can help shorten the distance of
 the bypass pipe.
 4. Stage 1 sand inlets: The position of the sand inlets is a fixed cross-section in the channel and so
 it’s important to consider the following when selecting this location:
 a. Proximity to the Stage 2 and Stage 3 dewatering and pumping facilities.
 b. Sufficiently far upstream of the dam to provide the reservoir surface area and volume
 meeting design and operational objectives.
 c. An acceptable location for the end of the regraded fluvial river section. The sand bypass
 system and specifically the sand intakes will prevent sedimentation and delta growth
 beyond inlet locations. Upstream of the inlets, the reservoir will ultimately fill with sand
 and regrade to the mean pool elevation (See Figure 7.1).
 5. Stage 2 dewatering and concentrating: The dewatering facility will be located adjacent to the
 river system and should be on acquired or leased property. The dewatering facility will require
 electrical power, access roads and communications.
 6. Stage 3 high-concentration conveyance: These facilities are located adjacent to the
 concentrating tanks and include an array of water supply lines, pumps, and valves. The facility
 will require high voltage electrical power and other utilities, road access, and communication.
 7. Stage 3 conveyance pipes: One or more pipes will route high concentrated sand slurry from the
 dewatering tank to downstream of the dam, to the location where sand will be returned to the

 77

 river. The design should attempt to minimize this distance and access to property to route the
 pipes must be considered.
 8. Stage 4 sand discharge: This system should be sited adjacent to the river downstream of the
 dam. It will be a distributed discharge system, where multiple outlets or diffusers are used to
 distribute discharge water and sand across the channel.

River, hydrologic, and hydraulic parameters needed for system design
 1. Topographic/bathymetric maps and/or survey data of the river and river valley with the
 impoundment area of the reservoir. River bathymetry, channel and valley width, bankfull depth.
 2. Historic stage-discharge and stream gage data sufficient to calculate design flows (mean,
 extreme flows and other important design flows).
 3. Measured or calculated sand transport rates at the upstream extent of the reservoir. Transport
 rates should be estimated for a range of flow conditions.
 4. Typical grainsize distribution for sediment that is actively transported in the river.
 5. Survey of large wood and other debris to inform risks assessments.
 6. Design details for dam structure and location of powerhouse and spillways.
 7. Predictions of impoundment area post dam construction - calculating the expected area/volume

Final conceptual design package
Stage 1 – Sand capture and conveyance
Overview
Sand intakes are arranged into intake batteries, each containing 4-8 inlets. Intake batteries are
distributed across most of the river cross section. Flexible conduits from each inlet are connected to a
manifold, which consolidates flow into single suction pipe – one pipe for each battery. The inlet capture
is driven by a slurry pump(s) located on an adjacent riverbank. Discharge from the slurry pump is
directed into the top of the Stage 2 concentrating tank.
Guide Vane Design
Summary
The permeable guide vanes may be used to steer sediment laterally to the sand intakes, but
operation independently. They are an optional component of design. Design details of this
system are in Appendix A.

Sand Intake Design
Performance Objectives:
1. Capture sand-sized bed material delivered by the river and transport to the dewatering plant.
2. Designed to operate at 3% sediment concentration by volume (continuous) and peak at maximum
 concentration of 6% of sand-sized material, 0.05 to 2.0 mm.
3. Intake batteries designed to intake 80% of annual bedload in the river system.

 78

4. Operate continually with minimal maintenance.
5. Scalable design for deployments across a range of sand bed rivers and transport conditions.
Summary
The sand intake is positioned near the riverbed to ingest sand and water forming a low-concentration
slurry mixture. Connected to the sand intake is a manifold that serves as an intermediary pipe between
the intake and the low-concentration conduit.
The intake nozzle construction was evaluated during the project in collaboration with ORNL. A viable
option for fabrication is compression molding using thermoplastics and utilizing a two-piece design.

The use of plastics in the manufacturing of the nozzle enables a relatively complex geometry that can be
inexpensively manufactured. Abrasion from the sand and water mixture will slowly remove material

Performance Objectives

Summary
The sediment collected by the intake batteries has a very low solids content. Depending on operating

Stage 2 focuses on dewatering. A settling tank was selected as the appropriate technology for this task.
The settling tank is a large vessel with neither moving parts nor energy requirements (Figure 10.1 and
10.2). The sand-laden flow from the intake batteries is directed to the center of the tank and there are
multiple overflow outlets located at the outer perimeter of the tank. The flow velocities within the tank
are low due to the large diameter which allows sediments to fall out of the flow and concentrate at the
bottom of the tank. Positive displacement pumps draw high concentration slurry from the bottom of the
tank, and this concentrated sediment slurry is pumped downstream of the hydro dam (Stage 3).

or higher.

Stage 3 – Bypass, high concentration transport
Performance Objectives
1. Remove sand-water mixture from concentrating tank and reliably deliver over a long distance to

2. Minimize energy costs in design by maximizing sediment concentration in the pipe.
3. Utilize existing conveyance technology and knowledge in this design.
Summary
The energy and cost associated with transporting sand over long distances is one of the most
challenging parts of this design. The low gradient of the sand-bed rivers dictates that the sediment will
begin settling a long distance upstream when the dam is constructed. For the purposes of this study, the
transport distance was set to be 10 km (6.2 miles). This distance is within the capabilities of existing
technology and is successfully achieved in many locations across the US. The challenges are energy
consumption and pipe wear.
A piston diaphragm pump has been selected for this long-distance pumping application. We note that, if
the distance can be reduced, then centrifugal pumps should be re-evaluated. For the purposes of this
initial concept study, the optimal solids content has been assumed to be 50% solids by weight.
High pressure triplex diaphragm pumps along with an additional water supply, remove concentrated
sand-water slurry from the concentrating tank and convey to the dam site. Sizing of the diaphragm
pumps considers the discharge and flow velocities within the pipe, sand concentration, pipe material
and conduit length.

APPENDIX A – SUPPLEMENTAL INFORMATION ON DESIGN DEVELOPMENT OF
PERMEABLE VANE
A1 Introduction
In this project, the submerged permeable vane refers to a yawed, perforated, vertical wall that creates
lateral movement of sediment toward a desired location (coarse sediment intake). In the wake of the
horizontally yawed permeable vane, secondary vortices are generated whose rotational axes are
oriented along the streamwise direction, which provides auxiliary bed shear stress along the spanwise
direction. Such additional spanwise bed shear stress helps transport sediments laterally. An array of
permeable vanes can collectively work to constrain the lateral extent where sediments are transported
by the flow and reduce the number of intake pipes required for installations. One option available to the
GoSand design is installation of an array of permeable vanes upstream of the coarse sediment inlet
battery to facilitate sediment removal and reduce the number of inlets needed, thus lowering capital
costs.

A2 Performance Objectives:
Key performance objectives of the permeable vanes are defined below:
 A. The permeable vanes should operate passively with minimal maintenance and should be
 structurally stable over the full range of the river discharge.
 B. The foundation design should consider local scour and lateral loading from the skewed
 orientation of the vanes.
 C. The spacing of individual vane elements (streamwise and lateral) and total number of vanes in
 the array should be designed to achieve optimal divergence of sand transport while minimizing
 the number of vanes required.
 D. The permeable vanes should be designed to operate efficiently also with large wood, ice and
 other debris in the river.

A3 Basis of design/design assumptions:
The submerged permeable vane includes important design assumptions established at the beginning of
the project. We provide a concise summary of these below.
 A. The standard assumptions for the design of this component are: a) sand-bed river system, b)
 new dam construction on a natural river channel, c) 10 meter of available head at the dam.
 B. Overall costs of the sand-bypass SMH can be reduced by reducing the number of sand
 inlets/conduits. Our a priori assumption was that the coarse sediment intakes and conduit and
 associate structural and control systems will be the costliest components of the SMH
 technology. Using low-cost submerged vanes to concentrate the sediment transport into
 “capture zones” could be a cost-effective approach. This assumes the cost per unit width of
 installing vanes and maintaining their operation will be significantly less than the one of the
 intakes.

A4 Detailed technical description and drawings:
Submerged permeable vane design
The current design for the permeable vane and vane array is shown in Figure A.1 and A.2. The design is
meant to be simple, cost-effective and robust, and is a conceptual modification of the structure known
as an Iowa Vane 9. The permeable vane is formed from three components: 1) vertical tubular steel piling;
2) vane spacers; and 3) vane members. The steel pilings are driven into the river bed to a depth that
exceeds the estimated maximum local scour depth around the structure. The separation distance
between the two piles is equivalent to the vane width, W. The vane members and vane spacers are then
alternately slid onto the piles to form the permeable vane mesh. The installation method has not been
evaluated in detail at this time, however we believe this approach is amenable to efficient installation.

Vane porosity
Vane porosity is defined as the ratio of the opening area to the total frontal area of the structure.
Research was conducted to determine the optimal porosity recognizing that there is an inverse
relationship between porosity and net drag of the structure. We determined that 40% porosity is
optimal, promoting sediment lateral steering without excessive local scour.

Vane orientation angle
Orientation angle is the angle, in degrees to the transverse direction in the channel. Zero degrees is
perpendicular with flow and 90° is in-line to the flow. We determined that 45° is an optimal orientation
angle.
Vane width and height
Research was conducted exploring the height of the vane relative to water depth, including vane height
that extended above the water surface (emerging vanes). We adopted widely used design guidance for
Iowa Vane for the submergence and the aspect ratio, leading to vane height of 1/3 of the water depth
and vane height to width ratio of 1/3. Additional tests on the aspect ratios for emerging vanes support
the latter design guideline.
Vane streamwise and lateral spacing
Flume experiments focusing on velocity measurements in the wake of a submerged vane along with
bathymetry mapping under various vane configurations allowed us to determine optimal spacing of
individual vanes to operate within a staggered array. The streamwise spacing is six times the vane width
(6W) and the lateral spacing is two time the vane width (2W).
Expanding design from individual vane to vane arrays
The permeable vane described above is a stand-alone sub-component that will be installed within
channel in a fixed position with a fixed orientation angle to the mean flow. Multiple permeable vanes
will be installed forming an array with defined lateral and streamwise spacing (Figure A.2). An array of
vanes will be integrated with a battery of coarse sediment intakes and this vane-inlet complex is defined
as an “intake module”. It is likely that several intake modules will be installed across a wide river
channel.

A5 Discussion of performance verification:
The state-of-design for the permeable vane described above was achieved through a broad set of
experimentation and modeling. To get to this stage of design, the research team performed distinct
research and component testing experiments to inform the design. Details on this research are
included below.
The drag force exerted by an individual permeable vane on the flow is an essential variable that
determines the maximum scour around the vane and the lateral sediment flux directionality. To better
understand the influence of design parameters on scour and lateral transport, we conducted a series of
experiments in a wind tunnel to quantify the drag coefficient of permeable vanes under various vane
porosities and installation configurations. Details of the wind tunnel studies are summarized in
Appendix A8.
We carried out a set of open channel flow experiments under near-critical mobility conditions to
quantify the geometries of scour and deposit patterns generated by a permeable vane with varying
installation configurations (e.g. vane porosity, width, and orientation angle). Details from these
experiments are summarized in Appendix A8.
Using the results from the wind tunnel and flume experiments, we developed and calibrated a predictive
model for the maximum scour depth induced by permeable vanes, as a function of the various key
configuration variables. The modeling effort is described in more detail in Appendix A8.

 92

Additional open channel flume experiments were conducted to develop a metric to evaluate the
performance of a permeable vane in terms of lateral sediment flux directionality. The spanwise
centroids of the sediment deposit downstream of the vane, normalized by the characteristic length scale
of the scour volume, was used for a systematic quantification. Once the most favorable installation
configuration was determined for a single permeable vane, we performed flow velocity measurements
to describe the spatial wake evolution (e.g. expansion, deflection, and velocity deficit) behind the
permeable vane to optimize the spacing and installation of the array.
Lastly, we modified the metric for the lateral sediment flux directionality to objectively evaluate
performance of complicated multiple permeable vane array arrangements under both critical mobility
and live-bed conditions.

A8. Laboratory methods to optimize design
Wind tunnel experiments (15 cases)
Drag coefficient indicates how much flow momentum is absorbed by an object. In open channel flows
with an erodible bed, bed scour is generated when larger flow momentum is exerted on a hydraulic
structure. One of the important design parameters regarding the safety of an in-channel hydraulic
structure is the maximum local scour depth formed during high flow. Understanding the associate drag
coefficient of the structure allows the ability to model or predict maximum scour depth. Since the drag
coefficient varies with the orientation angle of the structure and porosity, we measured the drag
coefficients of the permeable vane within a wind tunnel (3 m long, 0.3 m width, and 0.32 m deep) in the
C. Edward Bowers Fluid Mechanics Laboratory, the University of Minnesota. Fifteen different scenarios
were modeling in the tunnel and results are discussed below.

Clear water experiments, 0.9 meter wide flume (17 cases)
These experiments were conducted under the near-critical mobility condition within the Tilting Bed
Flume (17 m long and 0.9 m wide) at the St Anthony Falls Laboratory (SAFL), University of Minnesota.
Here, the near-critical mobility condition indicates that the bed shear stress exerted by the flows on the
bed surface is approximately equal to the resistant shear stress of sediment grains. Thus, only local
scour and deposition are generated near the vane and negligible bed elevation changes are observed far
away from the vane. We evaluated the performance of a single permeable vane by quantifying the
effects of porosity (𝑝𝑝), vane width (𝑊𝑊), and orientation angle (𝜙𝜙) on the maximum lateral shift of the
sediment deposit behind the vane. Here, the porosity is defined as the ratio of the opening area to the
total frontal area. The maximum performance was observed when the vane has 0.40 porosity, height (H)
of 1/3h, and width of 3H (and/or 0.13b) among the parameters we explored, where h and b are the
water depth and channel width, respectively.
Additional velocity measurement experiments were carried out in the wake of a permeable vane with
the optimal installation configuration using an acoustic Doppler velocimetry (ADV). The spatial wake
evolution map was used to suggest the optimal row and lateral spacings in a permeable vane array by
ensuring that the downstream vane was not located in the wake-impacted region by the upstream one.
Such configuration allowed us to obtain the maximum flow momentum for each permeable vane and
the largest lateral sediment flux directionality. Our experimental results suggested the streamwise and
lateral spacings of 6W and 2W, respectively.
Live-bed experiments, 2.75m wide research channel
A short set of experiments were carried out under live-bed conditions in SAFL’s Main Channel facility - a
quasi-field scale open channel laboratory flume. The purpose of the experiments was to scale up the
design of a permeable vane array and test its performance regarding lateral sediment flux directionality
under more realistic flow conditions. Here we were able to simulate live-bed sediment transport
conditions - when the bed shear stress of the flow results in the formation migrating bedforms (e.g.
ripples and dunes). Live-bed is a more realistic flow condition observed in most sand bedded fluvial
channels. The Main Channel Facility is 85 𝑚𝑚 long, 2.75 𝑚𝑚 wide, and filled with approximately 0.4 𝑚𝑚
sediment bed consisting of nearly uniform quartz sand with median grain size of 0.42 𝑚𝑚𝑚𝑚. The
suggested permeable vane design parameters, determined in the clear water experiments discussed

APPENDIX B – SUPPLEMENTAL INFORMATION ON DESIGN DEVELOPMENT OF
SAND INLET NOZZLE
B1. Summary
Prototype development and performance testing of the coarse sediment intake nozzle occurred in a 24-
inch wide by 50-ft long water flume. Water was impounded upstream of a dam to create 12 inches of
water depth. Model scale prototype nozzles were installed in the flume at the toe of a sand delta. Flow
in the nozzle and conduit was driven by a pump which returned flow to the upstream end of the flume
creating recirculating flow.

B2. Objectives
The primary objectives of the physical model experiments were to:
 1. Test qualitative performance of model scale intake nozzles under various flow and sediment
 concentration conditions
 2. Quantify the intake nozzle performance, headloss and minimum operational velocities
 3. Experiment with burial tests for nozzle recovery during extreme events

B3. Test Setup and Measurements
The coarse sediment intake nozzle test setup was built in a 24-inch wide by 50 ft long water flume.
Water was impounded over a section of approximately 20 ft of the flume. A fluvial section of the flume
was created with a sand bed transitioning into a delta and eventually a reservoir. Scaling principles were
not used in the design of the fluvial section, delta, or reservoir. The purpose of the setup was to test the
performance of small-scale prototype nozzles and this setup provided a convenient method of moving
sediment to the intake nozzle. Suction for the intake nozzle was produced with a pump. Water and
sediment ingested by the intake nozzle travels to the pump through a 1.5-inch PVC pipe and returns to
the upstream end of the flume in a recirculating fashion. Sediment in the fluvial section of the flume is
transported as bed load and deposited on the delta eventually being ingested by the intake nozzle which
is installed at the toe of the delta. Additional sediment was introduced by using a sediment feeder
located directly above the delta. The sediment feeder was used to supplement sand moved by flow
within the flume and needed to test higher sediment concentrations.
Figure B1 shows a side view of a portion of the test setup. The impoundment dam can be seen in the
figure as well as the 1.5-inch conduit used in model prototype testing, nozzle support rack, and an intake
nozzle model prototype.

 104

Figure B1. Intake nozzle test setup.

Flow measurements within the 1.5-inch conduit were taken by a Pulsar Measurement DFM 6.1, which
uses Doppler technology to measure velocity. The meter is clamped onto the outside of the pipe. This
provides the bulk velocity within the 1.5-inch diameter conduit. Through conservation of mass the
average velocity and the nozzle opening could be calculated.
Sediment concentration within the conduit, and thus the amount of sediment being ingested by the
intake nozzle, was measured using a vertical counterflow meter. Figure B2 shows a portion of the
vertical counterflow meter. The vertical counterflow meter is created by an inverted ‘U’ section of pipe.
Four pressure taps are made in the inverted ‘U’ shape. Two taps on the flow-up side, and two taps on
the flow-down side. By measuring the differential pressure over the same distance on the vertically
ascending and descending legs, density of the flow mixture can be calculated. A fully mixed condition is
assumed in the vertical flow and the same wall friction is assumed in both legs. The average density of
the ascending and descending legs is then taken and related to the density of the sediment solids and
water to yield an instantaneous measurement of sediment concentration by volume. The measurement
was calibrated against manual measurements of sediment concentration by emptying flow from the
intake nozzle/pump into a large container and then weighing the captured sediment after removing
water and drying the sediment.

Conclusions CFD and next steps
We used large-eddy simulations coupled with immersed boundary method and a wall model to obtain
the mean flow statics and to improve the initial design of the sediment inlet. The simulations showed a
similar pressure drop along the centerline as measured in experiments. Subsequently, the simulations
were employed to get an insight into the flow. The initial design of the inlet was found to have locally
high velocity regions that will lead to higher losses. A design iteration is carried out to remove these
local high velocity regions and to achieve a smoother transition of the flow from the inlet to the pipe.
The simulation results showed that the new design (Inlet-B) with a longer inlet section compared to the
initial design (Inlet-A) achieved both objectives. Moreover, the new design also increased the minimum
centerline velocity that occurred near the inlet indicating its superiority for transporting sediment
compared to the initial design. Furthermore, for a given inlet design, the mean centerline velocity is
found to scale well with the inlet velocity. The mean centerline velocity curves normalized this way
overlapped for two Reynolds number investigated. This can be used to estimate the centerline velocities
at other operating conditions. Although a better performance is expected from the new inlet, it showed
local low velocity regions near the wall in the inlet section. Future design iterations will target to remove
these low velocity regions and to improve the design further.

For low pipe mean velocities U corresponding to low Reynolds number (Re = UDp/ν), an increase in

in a rise in friction factor which stems from the more dissipative interactions between sand particles, the
pipe surface and the fluid phase. These interactions, and the associated viscous forces, contribute to
dissipate more kinetic energy into heat. Increasing the sand size requires increased shear to resuspend
the grains. It thus leads to a larger amount of sand forming a stationary bed at relatively low discharge,
resulting in larger velocities in the unobstructed, upper portion of the pipe (for continuity of the fluid
phase in the reduced cross section), leading to higher viscous dissipation. This causes a rise in pressure
drop and friction factor with increasing sand size and concentration, as shown in Figure C17.
By increasing the pipe velocity and sustaining more intense turbulence, eddies mix the flow, entrain
sand in suspension, and distribute the grains from a (stratified) heterogeneous concentration towards a
uniform concentration in the cross section (homogeneous state). However, even if the friction factor
decreases with the Reynolds number, we remind that the head losses, proportional to fV2/2g, increase
as expected with increasing turbulence. Therefore, even in the heterogeneous and homogeneous
regimes (Figure C14 24 25), the viscous dissipation at the grain particle boundary layers, are expected to
increase with d50, CV and Re.
In particular, by increasing the sand sizes from d50 = 0.085 mm to d50 = 1.17 mm the flow kinetic energy
and shear stress that are needed to fully resuspend all the grains in the cross section is proportionally
dissipated. This is reflected in the occurrence of heterogeneous and saltation flow regimes, and in the
bed forming, leading to significant observed deviations of the friction factor as a function of sand
concentration. Essentially, the variability in flow regimes, under the pump head and frequency tested,
increases with coarsening sand, i.e. increasing d50, and the friction factor curves are more separated. For

APPENDIX D – SUPPLEMENTAL REPORT ON THE BASELINE COST MODELING

D1. Overview
This Technical Subject Review provides a summary of the baseline cost estimation developed in Budget
Period 1 for the University of Minnesota (UMN) SMH sand bypass system. The document provides a
summary of the motivation for the baseline estimate, a survey of available sediment management
approaches for reservoir sedimentation, key design assumptions in assessing baseline costs, details on
the baseline technologies selected, and overall cost and unit cost estimation. This study is done through
a collaboration between UMN, Barr Engineering Company, and Oak Ridge National Laboratory.

D2. Motivation – need for baseline cost estimation
In this project, UMN is developing a technology that will provide near-continuous bypass of sand from
upstream of the dam to downstream of the dam and will provide pseudo-continuity in sediment
transport across the reservoir-dam reach. The context for design focuses on New Stream Reach
Development (NSD), meaning locations on rivers that presently do not have a dam or hydropower
installed. Furthermore, the dam design embraces Standard Modular Hydropower (SMH) components,
such that individual, scalable dam elements, which include hydropower generation, can be placed and
integrated together in a cost effective and efficient manner. Details on SMH and NSD are not provided
here as they are assumed to be understood by the reader.
The technology being developed by the UMN team falls within the broad category of Reservoir
Sediment Management (RSM). Managing the sediment that accumulates within reservoirs is certainly
an age-old problem that includes not only hydropower stakeholders but also environmental, agriculture,
water supply, recreation and so forth. The point is that there are many different stakeholders with
interest in RSM and, therefore, there are many different approaches available to provide mitigation.
The objective here is to identify mitigation approaches that are comparable to the UMN sand by-pass
system and could, in fact, be a possible alternative approach for NSD and integration in a SMH dam. For
these relevant technologies, we seek to develop cost estimates for implementing these approaches. We
define these as baseline cost estimations. Later in the project, cost models developed for the UMN sand
by-pass system will be compared to the baseline costs as part of the overall technology assessment.

D3. Exemplary Design Envelope Specifications for SMH: Critical performance
objectives and how they impact baseline cost assessment
The Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) developed an important reference document titled,
Exemplary Design Envelope Specification for Standard Modular Hydropower Technology (Witt et al.,
2017). This report evaluates key SMH technologies (e.g., fish passage, electrical power generation,
sediment bypass, and others) and provides guidance on key performance objectives for each type of
technology, where performance objectives, in many cases, address major barriers for the growth of
hydropower in the US. The important take away here is that Witt et al., 2017 defines performance
objectives that are critical for providing the operational and/or environmental “services” that are

 136

presently lacking at hydro facilities and are essential services that must be part of any new stream
research development (and development of non-powered dams for that matter).
Witt et al., 2017 provides an important framework for the SMH sediment bypass, which becomes
important when evaluating baseline costs. The following criteria are highlighted from this document (pg.
C-1 and C-2):
 1. Deliver incoming sediment supply to sediment passage module inlet.
 2. Sustain conditions for transporting sediment across passage module.
 3. Minimize sediment deposition downstream of the SMH facility.
 4. Minimize river geomorphic change further upstream and downstream of SMH facility.
 5. Minimize fish habitat and water quality degradation due to sediment releases.
The key take-aways from Witt et al., 2017 as they pertain to sediment bypass are as follows:
A. Prevent or minimize accumulation of sediment in the upstream reservoir
This is a key performance objective or “service” sought in the SMH module. The deposition of sand in
reservoirs results in upstream and downstream degradation of the local environment. Sediment
deposition in reservoirs ultimately causes operational and maintenance issue for hydropower owners
(Morris and Fan, 1998; Kondolf et. al 2014; Morris 2022).
B. Provide continuity in the natural sediment transport rate
Equally important to the previous is the performance objective of providing some level of “continuity in
sediment transport”. It is important to understand that sediment is continually transported within river
and stream systems. This is a natural process associated with water cycle, rock cycle and Earth-surface
dynamics – healthy rivers adjust their slopes, width and depths to be in balance with water and
sediment loads (Morris, 2020).
The rate of sediment transport in a river varies in time (hourly to seasonally), with hydrology of the
watershed, and rate of delivery of sediment into surface waters. Installation of a dam and reservoir
disrupts this natural sediment transport and results in undesirable geomorphic change - deposition of
sediment in the reservoir (upstream impact) and sediment starvation, degradation, incision and
coarsening of the river downstream of the dam (downstream impact).
Witt et al., 2017 established the objectives for SMH sediment bypass to include continuity in sediment
transport and the UMN design fully incorporates this performance objective into our design. As we
evaluate alternative RSM technologies, the ability to provide continuity of transport must be considered.
C. Prevent or minimize unnatural degradation or aggradation downstream of the dam.
This performance objective is tightly bound to the previous take-aways and focuses on the stream
channel condition downstream of the dam. Re-introduction of sediment into the downstream channel
must be done at time scales and spatial scales that do not disrupt the natural channel morphology or
adversely impacts aquatic organisms or water quality.

D4. Brief review of UMN sand bypass module
A high-level overview of the UMN coarse sediment bypass system is provided here. More detailed
descriptions of the components are provided in other summary reports.

 137

The salient features of the module are summarized below:
 • The UMN sand bypass module is designed for sand bed rivers and sand size material (<2mm).
 • The module includes a sand by-pass system, referred to as the “coarse sediment bypass” as well
 as a fine-sediment bypass system (clays and silts). The sand inlet will be located far upstream of
 the dam whereas the fine-sediment inlets will be located near the dam face.
 • The module is designed to operate nearly-continually and accommodate a range of sand
 transport rates.
 • The sand by-pass involves four sub-system components. 1) permeable guide vanes, 2) sand inlet,
 3) sand and water conduit, 4) discharge apron.
 • The fine-sediment by-pass is considered an independent system with multiple inlets,
 conveyance piping and discharge piping.
 • The permeable guide vanes and sand bypass intakes are designed to work as a system. The
 guide vanes are static structures placed on the bed of the river with the purpose of “steering”
 sand to the intakes and effectively reducing the quantity and spatial extent of sand intakes
 required.
 • Guide vanes/sand inlets are placed across a river cross section and ingest sand as it travels into
 the reservoir within the capture-zone of the inlets. Sand is then routed through conduits to the
 dam, passing through the dam, and discharging downstream of the dam.
 • The sand by-pass system has real-time control and health monitoring sensors to assist in reliable
 performance of the system.
 • The discharge apron is the component name for the component associated with returning the
 bypass sand back to the river downstream of the dam. It is a distributed discharge system for
 low-impact, continuous discharge of sand and river water.

For the purposes of the baseline cost assessment, there are two important attributes of the UMN
system to highlight:
 1. The UMN system is being designed to operate continually throughout the year with the ability
 to capture and bypass sand at a rate that matches, on average, the incoming sand transport
 rate in the river. The design will capture sand toward the upstream end of the reservoir and
 discharge it downstream of the dam. For sand bed rivers, which are very low gradient, the
 distance upstream for the inlet could be on the order of several kilometers.
 2. The UMN systems incorporates an approach for distributed discharge of sand back into the
 river downstream of the dam. The objective here is to minimize negative geomorphic, and
 water quality changes and minimize impacts to aquatic organisms. Furthermore, sand is not
 “stored” during bypass rather it is captured and discharged continually.
 3. Because of the continuous operation of the system, it will be necessary to provide ongoing
 operation and maintenance for the bypass system. Natural rivers system have tremendous
 variability and are transporting more than just sand. Variability in transport rates, water flow,
 and debris load will require health monitoring and operational maintenance.

D5. Introduction of baseline technologies/approaches
In this section we provide a brief summary of the existing approaches for managing reservoir sediment.
RSM is a challenging issue and there is an international body of literature on this topic. Here we provide
only a high level summary of the primary approaches for RSM used across the world.
Figure 1 below is a summary graphic of the various approaches to reservoir sediment management
borrowed from Morrison and Fan (1998), which is the most common framework for RSM techniques
(Kondolf et al, 2014; Auel, 2014; Morrison and Fan, 1998). Three categories are typically defined:
Sediment Yield Reduction, Sediment Routing, and Sediment Removal. Within each of the categories are
approaches to managing sediment.

Figure 0.1. Overview of sedimentation countermeasures (from Morrison, 2020).

A. Sediment Yield Reduction
The Sediment Yield Reduction involves approaches to reduce erosion and/or prevent sediment from
entering the river or stream systems. While these are important consideration for a new reservoir
development, they are not directly relevant to the baseline cost estimate needed for this project. In
other words, retaining sediment in the watershed may reduce sediment transport in the river system
but this approach will not reduce the sediment transport to zero. Healthy rivers are in equilibrium with
sediment transport and we are focusing on developing a technology that allows that continuity to exist
even in the presence of a dam/reservoir. Therefore, we eliminate Sediment Yield Reduction from
further consideration here.

 139

B. Sediment Routing
Sediment routing involves approaches for bypassing a portion or all of a rivers’ natural sediment load
downstream either around the reservoir or through the reservoir and includes sediment sluicing,
venting of turbidity currents, and sediment bypass tunnels.
B1. Sediment Sluicing
Sediment sluicing or drawdown sluicing is a RSM approach that seeks to prevent sediment from
depositing in the reservoir. Low elevation gates are opened at the dam during high flow events, either
seasonal events or flood events, to promote incoming sediment to continue moving through the
reservoir and pass downstream of the dam. Several attributes of sluicing systems include:
 • The gates need to be designed to be large enough to pass high flows and prevent backwater
 effects in the reservoir (Kondolf et al., 2014).
 • The technique assumes the bulk of sediment transport occurs during high flow events. This is
 true for gravel and mix-load systems but is not necessarily the case for sand-bed rivers.
 • In some cases, sluicing results in remobilization of already-deposited sediment but this is
 typically not the primary objective of sluicing.
 • Because sluicing seeks to “pass” incoming sediment, the downstream sediment concentrations
 during the sluicing event are consistent with incoming sediment concentrations. In other words,
 sluicing does not result in overly elevated suspended sediment concentrations.
 • Outlets must be design to resist abrasion of sediment during operation. There are also example
 of low elevation gates becoming blocked or buried during sluicing and unable to close.
B2. Venting of turbid density currents
Turbidity currents are transport phenomena that form when the density of the suspended sediment-
water mixture is sufficient to create sustained transport of sediment-water mixture into the reservoir.
Commonly occurring during flood situation, the constant inflow of high concentration of fines and water
results in formation of turbidity current and the turbulence within the current is able to maintain
suspension of sediment allowing turbidity currents to travel tremendous distances and contribute to the
infilling of reservoirs.
Venting involves placement of gates that can be opened when turbidity currents are active. Similar to
sluicing, it is a technique that is operated intermittently and seeks to prevent deposition of fines during
high flow events.
 • This RSM technique is specific to systems with significant fine sediment (silt, sand and clay) that
 can form turbidity currents. River systems without fine-sediment load are not appropriate sites
 for venting RSM.
 • Gates are low elevation and utilized only during turbidity current formation.
 • The objective of venting is to prevent deposition of fine material within the reservoir
 • Venting typically captures material that reaches the dam.
 • In some designs, fine material venting involves routing the fine material through the power
 generation units but can result in abrasion issues.
B3. Sediment bypass tunnels
Construction of off-channel by-pass tunnels is a less-common RSM approach. The approach involves
constructing a large tunnel that connects the upstream portion of the river to a downstream location

 140

below the dam. A submerged guide-wall is often constructed across the river to divert sediment toward
a tunnel. When operated, water and sediment enter the tunnel and are conveyed downstream.
Bypass tunnels require steep gradients in the river and locations where the river has a substantial bend
(Kondolf et al., 2014). Typically operated under super-critical flow, the tunnels are operated only
partially full (with a free surface) and at Froude numbers greater than unity (shallow and fast flow) to
ensure sediment does not deposit within the tunnel. We could not find any examples of by-pass tunnels
constructed for sand-bed rivers and assume this is because the gradient/slope of these is too low.
Bypass tunnels are typically operated intermittently, during periods of substantial sediment transport
(Kondolf et al., 2014). In this way, they are similar to sluicing and venting where, assuming full capture of
transported material, the sediment flux in is equal to sediment flux out.
 • Sediment by-pass tunnels are associated with steep gradient systems and sited at a large bends
 in the river channel.
 • They are operated during sediment transport event (i.e. high discharge events)
 • Sediment is guided to the tunnel inlet by a submerged guide wall constructed in the bed of the
 river.
 • Tunnel are operated in different ways but are often as partially flow/free surface channel under
 supercritical flow.

C. Sediment Removal
Sediment removal involves RSM techniques that seek to empty or remove deposited sediment from the
reservoir. In general, these removal techniques happen periodically and require lowering or fully
emptying the reservoir.
C1. Sediment Flushing
Sediment flushing is similar to sediment sluicing in that low elevation gates are opened to allow
sediment to pass downstream of the dam. An important difference however is the flushing targets
removal of sediment that has already been deposited and is not necessarily scheduled with high flow or
large sediment transport events.
The typical operation of a flushing event involves opening low elevation gate to lower the reservoir pool.
Bottom shear stresses of the channel are thus increased, which allows erosion and transport of
deposited sediment. An important consequence of flushing is that the downstream sediment
concentration can become much higher than the upstream/incoming sediment concentration because
of acute streambed erosion. The concentration can become high enough to have negative
environmental impacts on aquatic organisms and channel morphology.
 • The purpose of flushing is to remove deposited sediment from the reservoir over a short period
 of time using the natural erosive power of the river channel.
 • Flushing involves lowering the reservoir pool by opening low-elevation gates at the dam. This
 induce higher velocities and greater bed shear stress on the sediment resulting in remobilization
 and transport.
 • Flushing is done periodically and is not specifically timed with high flow or storm events (Kondolf
 et al., 2014). This can result in rapid and sustained increase in sediment concentration and
 nutrient elevations downstream of the dam during the flushing event.

 141

 • Fine sediment deposits can “set-up” over time and become difficult to remobilize even during a
 flushing event. In some case, machinery is needed to move consolidated sediment into active
 flushing channel.

C2. Hydraulic Dredging
Hydraulic dredging removes and transports sediment in the form of a slurry using large volumes of
water. Because slurries from hydraulic dredging have higher water content than mechanically dredged
sediments, they require more space and time for dewatering and water management. Many types of
hydraulic dredging equipment are available, and are selected to meet site-specific needs, sediment
characteristics, transportation requirements, accuracy levels, removal depths and production rates.
Cutterhead equipment is among the most commonly used tools for hydraulic dredging (U.S. EPA, 1994
and 2005). Hydraulic dredging equipment can typically achieve overall higher production rates than
mechanical dredging, especially when used to dredge very large volumes of sediment (U.S. EPA, 1994).
Hydraulic dredging is also beneficial over mechanical methods in cases where the dredged sediment
needs to be transported a large distance to the disposal site.
 • Hydraulic dredging involves floating or barge mounted machinery to remove and transport large
 volumes of material from the riverbed. Dredge material is typically directed by pipeline to a river
 bank deposition area.
 • A cutter head can be used to break up cohesive consolidated material prior to be entrained into
 the conveyance piping of the dredge.
 • The dredge vessel moves systematically across the region to be dredged.
 • Hydraulic dredging is able to move high volumes of high concentrated slurry over a short period
 of time. Hydraulics dredging is performed periodically.
 • Dredging is expensive and therefore the dredge region is kept as small as necessary.

C3. Excavation
Dry excavation or mechanical dredging of wet sediments removes sediment through mechanical force,
typically an excavator or crane equipped with a traditional or environmental bucket placed on a working
barge. Sediment is lifted to the surface with approximately the same moisture content as in-situ
material and placed on a transport barge. Mechanical dredging is often needed for the removal of large
debris, cemented material, or in tighter spaces, where access with hydraulic dredge equipment and
associated pipelines may be difficult. Mechanical dredging typically results in higher sediment
resuspension rates than hydraulic dredging, although environmental dredge buckets can be used to
reduce resuspension (Interstate Technology & Regulatory Council [ITRC], 2014).
Mechanical removal of sediments can also be completed under dry conditions, after water has been
diverted or drained from the removal area, following construction of a containment barrier such as a
cofferdam. Dry removal has been used successfully on a number of projects and greatly reduces the
potential for resuspension of sediment when compared to wet dredging. Dry removal also allows for
visual inspection of the work area, use of more traditional excavating equipment, and generally requires
less sediment dewatering (after removal) than wet dredging, depending on the material characteristics.

E4. Recommendations/Discussion
Based on preliminary research for sand bed rivers, a large variability in most parameters was observed.
Based on observations from the histograms in figures G1.2 through G1.7, sand bed rivers tend towards

 151

lower channel slopes with smaller particles, median d50 around 0.35 mm. The width/depth ratio lies
mostly between 10 and 20 with the sinuosity less than 2. These parameters match well with the general
description from literature of sand bed rivers having a large width/depth ratio with low channel slopes.
This channel geometry is most influenced by sediment transport and its composition (particle size
distribution).
The sediment composition and transport are the basis for the river geomorphology of sand bed rivers.
Because sediment has the greatest influence over the geometry of the river and in turn its overall
sediment transport, more investigation was conducted to better understand the sediment movement
within a river. Sediment transport generally occurs at all flow rates in sand bed rivers and in the two
forms: bedload and suspended load. The percentage transported as bedload for a given year can vary
within a river from as high as 61% for the Chippewa River at Durand, WI and down to 35% for the
Chippewa River near Caryville, WI (Rose, 1992). Other rivers can see bedload percentages as low as 4%
to 13% for the Exu River in Brazil (Cantalice, 2013). These results reveal the varied traits of sand
movement in rivers. Additionally, Table F1 illustrates the variability in sand bed rivers across the United
States based on their sizes and sediment properties.
A simplified illustration of the variability in sediment transport is shown in Figure F8. This figure shows
the different mechanisms of sediment transport across the particle size range for sand as a function of
flow velocity for a hypothetical fixed stream depth. For example, if flow velocity is high in a river,
indicating greater energy in the system, one would expect greater transport in both suspended and
bedload. This is consistent with the findings of Molinas that bed-material concentrations are highly
variable in streams and depend on the unit stream power (Molinas, 2001). The unit stream power
increases with both flow velocity and flow depth, thus increasing sediment transport rates.

Figure E8: Sediment mobility diagram indicating critical range of sand (modified from Lemke, 2015)

 152

Sediment composition plays an important role as well in sediment transport. If most particles are small
within a river then it would be expected to see greater transport as suspended load than as bedload.
The mode of transport for the sediment and the sediment composition will also be a factor in how
sediment is deposited when velocity is reduced at the inlet of a reservoir and interacts with the
prograding delta of the dam reservoir. This interaction is important in the design of the inlets which
either capture bedload and coarser sediment at the delta front, or sediment that remains suspended
within the reservoir.
Event magnitudes and seasonal effects also need to be considered for sediment transport. The Channel
Restoration Design for Meandering Rivers published by the US Army Corps uses a term called effective
discharge to describe the flow which moves the most bed sediment load within the average annual
bedload (Soar and Thorne, 2001). As stated previously, sediment movement within sand bed rivers
occurs at even the lowest flows of sand bed rivers. It is observed that this effective flow can occur near
the minimum flow or the Bankfull flow for a given sand bed river (Soar and Thorne, 2001). This is
important in determining the operational range and sizing of the sediment bypass system. If a river has a
low effective flow and moves most of its bed sediment much below flood flows, a system can be sized
for these low flows and shut down for higher flows which do not deliver a large amount of bed sediment
in terms of the annual bedload. If the effective flow is near the Bankfull or flood flows, considerations
must be made under the conditions that most of the bed sediment may be delivered within a single
event of a given year. Additionally, suspended load cannot be neglected, as it can account for much of
the total load, and it does not always correlate linearly with the bed material over all ranges of flows. A
case study of the Exu River in Brazil showed a relatively constant ratio of bedload to suspended load
throughout normal seasonal events as both increased and decreased similarly with flow. In a large
event, this trend diverged and the ratio increased leaning towards more bedload at more extreme flows
(Cantalice, 2013). This is most likely due to how the particle size distribution interacts with the flow
within the river. Ultimately, this shows that sand bed rivers have a variety of parameters that go into
how and when a river moves its sediment. This must be considered in the design of the sediment
bypass, as it will have to be flexible to fit the sediment transport properties at a site.
One approach to choosing an example site is to target the median parameters of all rivers as described
above. However, the variability within the physical and sediment parameters make selection of a
“typical” sand bed river and “typical” conditions unreasonable. The only consistent observation across
all data collected is that it is variable from river to river and seasonally within a river. Given this it is
recommended to choose an example site that is potentially feasible for SMH development and has
adequate data to describe flow, geometry, and sediment with many observations over time. The
sediment bypass design should be flexible to accommodate sand bed rivers of varying sizes and

APPENDIX F - SELECTION OF EXAMPLE SAND BED RIVER SITES
F1. Introduction – Selection of Example Sand Bed River Sites
This section covers the process of choosing and selection of example sites for the design of a standard
modular hydropower (SMH) sediment bypass system. These example sites will be used to model
parameters associated with flow and sediment to mimic a natural system where there is potential for
the implementation of SMH. The SMH explorer by Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) was used as a
tool for finding example sites which have favorable conditions for a potential new stream reach dam
with low head (< 10 m) on a sand bed river as specified by the proposal of the project. Example sites are
considered in the context of providing physical parameters for sediment bypass development only; this
project does not propose actual dams at these or any other locations.

F2. Methods
Given the results of the preliminary research, it was decided to select example sites based on feasibility
of hydropower and availability of data (Appendix F, section F1). Regions of interest were identified using
the new stream-reach development (NSD) Methodology Report (Kao, 2014) and corresponding NSD
datasets from the National Hydropower Asset Assessment Program (NHAAP). These datasets gave
hydropower potential within huc10 sub-watershed areas.
Sand bed rivers from the preliminary research were identified and were sorted out such that the total
power potential in the huc10 area was greater than 4 MW and the average predicted head for a dam
was less than 10 m. These huc10 regions were then explored in SMH explorer to find reaches of rivers
which had the potential for at least one NSD. These reaches were then checked to see if a United States
Geological Survey (USGS) stream gauge was nearby and could provide data on flow and sediment
parameters such as particle sizes and suspended sediment discharge/concentration. Lastly, geometry
data collected in preliminary research was also checked to see if general geometry at a potential site
was available.
With stream reaches identified that have good power potential for new SMH development, USGS gauge
data, and geometry data, two sites were chosen which had higher capacity factors and lower inundated
surface area. A higher capacity factor relates to a greater percentage of the potential power being
generated throughout the year. A lower inundated surface area gives favorable conditions for the
sediment bypass system allowing for a shorter sediment transport pipe and/or less width of a reservoir
delta which the sediment intake system will need to span.
Statistics from SMH explorer and the USGS stream gages were compiled for the sites to provide more in-
depth details on the site conditions.

F4. Recommendations/Discussion
Given the possible sites in Tables G1 and G2, two sites were selected, Missouri River reach near Landsky,
MT and Kansas River reach at Wamego, KS. These reaches were chosen because they meet all
requirements of data which include: a geometry estimate, USGS history of flow and sediment, and are
feasible for hydropower. Despite meeting these requirements, the sites are significantly different.
Notable differences include the sediment size d50 of 0.18 mm for the Missouri and 0.65 mm for the
Kansas. Additionally, the Missouri River has a smaller overall cross-section but a steeper slope as shown
by the measurements in the lower half of Table G3. Despite this geometry, the mean annual flow is
much greater than that of the Kansas River as indicated in Table G3. These differences in geometry and
sediment place the two sites at reasonable positions within the survey of US sand bed rivers conducted
in the preliminary research and provide some good insight on variability between sand bed rivers.
Having these differing example sites will offer broader metrics for design considerations.
The majority of data availability is through the USGS gauges that are stationed on or near the reaches.
Figures G3 and G4 show average particle size distributions for both bed and suspended sediment based

 161

on many samples performed by USGS over time. Additional to sediment field measurements, the gauges
provided a long history of time stamped data for flow discharge, suspended sediment discharge,
suspended sediment concentration, and a variety of water quality data including temperature. All these
parameters can be compared across sampling times to create rating curves for the river. Altogether, this
USGS gauge data is important for properly modeling the example sites in experimental testing of the
bypass system.
Overall, the goal of this study was to survey sand bed rivers in the United States and select some sites
that have reasonable physical attributes and potential for SMH. This survey provided only an initial list of
possible sites and additional considerations may be made during the design and testing of the sediment
bypass system in the future. Only the preliminary literature research on sand bed rivers and the
resources available from ORNL were used in the selection of the two example sites and minimal
considerations for the sediment bypass design were made. This method in selection reflects future
implementation of the technology, as feasible sites for hydropower will be chosen for SMH and the
sediment bypass system must be somewhat flexible to adapt to a variety of sand bed rivers. It would be
unsuitable to choose a site that would be most favorable for the sediment bypass system, as the design
may not be challenged to fit the variability across the sand bed rivers of the United States.

 A novel sediment passage module design in support of Standard Modular Hydropower

A. PROJECT OBJECTIVES
  Objective 1: Advance and validate conceptual design for a sediment passage module targeting
 new stream-reach developments on low-gradient, sand bed rivers.
  Objective 2: Develop an integrated sediment passage system that can operate continually and
 limit deposition of coarse and fine sediment in the reservoir.
  Objective 3: Develop design for integrating the sediment by-pass module through a modular
 dam focusing on the hydraulic and structural aspects of passing the by-pass conduit through or
 over the modular dam.
  Objective 4: Create new knowledge on the feasibility of using submerged permeable vanes/grids
 to alter the flow field and steer course sediment toward the siphon inlets.
  Objective 5: Develop a coarse sediment inlet that allows near-continuous sand capture at
 multiple locations and maximizes capture zone.
  Objective 6: Advance the design of a primary sediment passage conduit for reliable performance
 under a range of hydraulic and sediment transport conditions.
  Objective 7: Develop a fine sediment inlet that allows near-continuous capture of silts and clays.
  Objective 8: Validate component design using physical experiments and/or CFD simulation.
  Objective 9: Working with ORNL, incorporate advanced materials and manufacturing techniques
 into components of the module design.

B. TECHNICAL SCOPE SUMMARY
The technical scope of work is focused on advancing the six components of the design from current
state (TRL2) to an advanced conceptual level (TRL4) of design with validation of performance, cost
estimation, and market planning. The six components include:
 • Component 1: Submerged permeable vanes/grids – permeable baffles placed on the bed of the
 river in the upstream region of the reservoir designed to steer coarse sediment towards the
 intakes.
 • Component 2: Coarse sediment inlet – intake structure designed to capture course sediment
 and deliver into the suction conduit.
 • Component 3: Sediment passage conduit - primary conduit that serves to transport coarse
 sediment from the upper reservoir to downstream of the dam.
 • Component 4: Fine sediment inlet – sediment capture system designed to capture and
 transport fine-grained sediment from the dam face to downstream of the dam.
 • Component 5: Section module for sediment passage conduit(s) – scalable structural
 component to allow primary sediment passage conduit of the sediment passage module to
 integrate into the primary modular dam. (The primary modular dam is not the focus of this
 project).
 • Component 6: Sediment passage distribution apron – scalable design for downstream input of
 by-pass sediment and water to the river downstream of the dam.

 Regents of the University of Minnesota
 A novel sediment passage module design in support of Standard Modular Hydropower

A. PROJECT OBJECTIVES
  Objective 1: Advance and validate conceptual design for a sediment passage module targeting
 new stream-reach developments on low-gradient, sand bed rivers.
  Objective 2: Develop an integrated sediment passage system that can operate continually and
 limit deposition of coarse and fine sediment in the reservoir.
  Objective 3: Develop design for integrating the sediment by-pass module through a modular
 dam focusing on the hydraulic and structural aspects of passing the by-pass conduit through or
 over the modular dam.
  Objective 4: Create new knowledge on the feasibility of using submerged permeable vanes/grids
 to alter the flow field and steer course sediment toward the siphon inlets.
  Objective 5: Develop a coarse sediment inlet that allows near-continuous sand capture at
 multiple locations and maximizes capture zone.
  Objective 6: Advance the design of a primary sediment passage conduit for reliable performance
 under a range of hydraulic and sediment transport conditions.
  Objective 7: Develop a fine sediment inlet that allows near-continuous capture of silts and clays.
  Objective 8: Validate component design using physical experiments and/or CFD simulation.
  Objective 9: Working with ORNL, incorporate advanced materials and manufacturing techniques
 into components of the module design.

B. TECHNICAL SCOPE SUMMARY
The technical scope of work is focused on advancing the six components of the design from current
state (TRL2) to an advanced conceptual level (TRL4) of design with validation of performance, cost
estimation, and market planning. The six components include:
 • Component 1: Submerged permeable vanes/grids – permeable baffles placed on the bed of the
 river in the upstream region of the reservoir designed to steer coarse sediment towards the
 intakes.
 • Component 2: Coarse sediment inlet – intake structure designed to capture course sediment
 and deliver into the passage conduit.
 • Component 3: Sediment passage conduit - primary conduit that serves to transport coarse
 sediment from the upper reservoir to downstream of the dam.
 • Component 4: Fine sediment inlet – sediment capture system designed to capture and
 transport fine-grained sediment from the dam face to downstream of the dam.
 • Component 5: Section module for sediment passage conduit(s) – scalable structural
 component to allow primary sediment passage conduit of the sediment passage module to
 integrate into the primary modular dam. (The primary modular dam is not the focus of this
 project).
 • Component 6: Sediment passage distribution apron – scalable design for downstream input of
 by-pass sediment and water to the river downstream of the dam.

 172

 • Component 7: Slurry pump - water-sediment slurry pump for intermittent operation and
 maintenance of coarse bypass system.
