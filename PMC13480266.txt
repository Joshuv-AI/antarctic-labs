Avoiding Diagnostic Anchoring
Authors: Justin Vincent, Sneha Vakamudi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: computed tomography, echocardiography, electrocardiogram, imaging, pericardial effusion, tamponade

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Madishetty et al1 present a case that highlights 3 tenets of evaluating pericardial disease that extend beyond the rarity of purulent pericarditis: thorough diagnostic evaluation of pericardial fluid, recognition of postdrainage constrictive physiology, and identification of the appropriate timing for surgery. Together, these elements offer a lesson in the management of pericardial illness—a rare but challenging entity.

In patients with active malignancy, a pericardial effusion is often presumed malignant. This assumption can delay recognition of alternative, potentially reversible, causes. In this case, comprehensive fluid analysis identified a gastrointestinal source consistent with contiguous infectious spread—a finding that changed both management and prognosis.

Effusions significant enough to result in tamponade or pericardiocentesis should prompt systematic evaluation of fluid regardless of a preexisting cancer diagnosis. Pericardial fluid analysis should routinely include cell count, Gram stain, bacterial and fungal cultures, and cytology.2,3 Light criteria should not be applied to pericardial fluid because normal pericardial fluid is exudative.2 When anaerobic bacteria are suspected, both aerobic and anaerobic cultures should be obtained.4 This comprehensive approach is especially important as purulent pericarditis—although uncommon—presents with nonspecific findings and carries high mortality. Delayed diagnosis may result in septic shock or irreversible constrictive physiology. A broad evaluation of pericardial fluid is not merely comprehensive medicine—it may be life-saving.

Following drainage, postprocedure imaging is important to ensure resolution of effusion and is the first-line modality for screening for constrictive physiology. Purulent pericarditis is strongly associated with progression to constriction, with an incidence of 52.75 cases per 1,000 person-years compared with 0.48% for viral or idiopathic etiologies.5 Hemodynamic findings of constriction may only emerge after tamponade physiology has been relieved. The 2025 American College of Cardiology (ACC) consensus statement defines effusive constrictive pericarditis as persistent constrictive physiology after drainage attributable to visceral pericardial inflammation.3 Thorough echocardiographic evaluation following drainage is therefore essential.

The core hemodynamic feature of constriction is interventricular dependence.3 In echocardiography, these changes manifest as a plethoric inferior vena cava, respirophasic septal shift, respiratory variation in mitral and tricuspid inflow (>25% and >40%, respectively), decreased lateral e’ velocity in the setting of preserved medial e' velocity (annulus reversus), elevated filling pressures despite decreased E/E’ values (annulus paradoxus), and late-diastolic expiratory hepatic vein flow reversal.3,6 Although no isolated echocardiographic finding is diagnostic, these findings in conjunction with clinical features of volume overload help confirm the diagnosis.

The 2025 ACC Expert Consensus Statement on Pericardial Disease reinforces the importance of multimodality imaging in the diagnosis of constriction.2 Cardiac magnetic resonance plays a complementary role, offering a valuable assessment of pericardial inflammation through T2 imaging (edema) and late gadolinium enhancement (neovascularization and inflammation).3,7 These findings help distinguish transient inflammatory from chronic constrictive pericarditis—a crucial distinction, as the former may resolve with anti-inflammatory therapy, whereas the latter typically requires more definitive surgical treatment.3,7

Although pericardiocentesis remains the immediate treatment for tamponade, definitive management of purulent pericarditis often requires more than simple drainage. Staphylococcus aureus and streptococci are the most common pathogens, although gastrointestinal flora may be implicated, especially in immunocompromised patients. The 2025 ACC consensus statement considers a pericardial window with surgical washout to be reasonable for recurrent large purulent effusions.3 The decision to proceed to surgery can be challenging in patients with active malignancy in whom procedural risk, immunocompromise, and overall prognosis complicate decision-making. Early multidisciplinary collaboration among cardiology, cardiothoracic surgery, infectious disease, and oncology is essential. In some patients, aggressive surgical management may achieve definitive source control; in others, the morbidity of surgery may outweigh the benefit depending on cancer stage and clinical trajectory. Local fibrinolytic therapy may be considered early to prevent effusion organization and progression to constriction.7 Other centers have reported protocols for instilling intrapericardial antibiotics in patients for whom invasive treatment options may be limited.8

For patients in whom infection and effusions resolve but constrictive physiology persists, treatment can be stratified based on the presence of inflammation. In constriction with evidence of ongoing active inflammation, a trial of anti-inflammatory therapy should be considered.3,7 When symptoms persist despite aggressive medical therapy, radical surgical pericardial resection is recommended to be performed at an experienced tertiary center.7

This case presents a rare but clinically significant diagnosis and emphasizes broader principles applicable to the evaluation and treatment of pericardial disease. Clinicians must maintain diagnostic rigor even when an underlying malignancy appears to offer an obvious explanation. The 2025 ACC Concise Clinical Guideline on the management of pericarditis provides a contemporary framework for the systematic evaluation of pericardial effusions, including comprehensive fluid analysis, structured assessment for constrictive physiology, and recommendations to inform the timing of both medical and surgical interventions.3,5 Comprehensive pericardial fluid analysis, vigilance for postdrainage constrictive physiology, and careful consideration of surgical timing are all essential aspects of care that reinforce the importance of avoiding anchoring bias when evaluating complex cardiovascular disease.
