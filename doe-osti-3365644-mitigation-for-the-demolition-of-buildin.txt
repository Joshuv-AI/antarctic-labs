 INTRODUCTION
The purpose of this letter report is to document the mitigation of adverse effects of a proposed
undertaking that would demolish Building 25-3153 in Area 25 of the Nevada National Security Site
(NNSS) in compliance with the terms of the 2024 Programmatic Agreement among the U.S. Department
of Energy and the Nevada State Historic Preservation Officer and the Advisory Council on Historic
Preservation Concerning the Protection of Historic Properties on the Nevada National Security Site, Nye
County, Nevada (NNSS PA). Building 25-3153 (State Historic Preservation Office [SHPO] Resource #
B19004) is a contributing element to the Nuclear Rocket Development Station (NRDS) Historic District
(SHPO Resource # D424), which has been determined eligible for listing in the National Register of
Historic Places (NRHP) under Criteria A, B, C, and D (Reno et al. 2023).
The proposed demolition constitutes an adverse effect. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) National
Nuclear Security Administration Nevada Field Office (NNSA/NFO) consulted with the SHPO on an
adverse effect finding for the undertaking and notified the SHPO of its intent to use the standard
mitigation in the NNSS PA on December 11, 2024 (Julian to Reed). The SHPO concurred on December
30, 2024 (Reed to Julian). Therefore, NNSA/NFO has prepared mitigation pursuant to the standard
mitigation in the NNSS PA Appendix D.I.A for contributing elements to recorded, NRHP-eligible
districts.
 MITIGATION CONTENTS
The standard mitigation for contributing elements to recorded, NRHP-eligible districts consists of an
updated Architectural Resource Assessment (ARA) form to document current conditions (Appendix
D.I.A), including high-quality digital images (Appendix D.I.A.1) and plans, drawings, or annotated
sketch plans (Appendix D.I.A.2), and a brief letter report describing mitigation contents and summarizing
the building’s historic significance (Appendix D.I.B).
This mitigation report documents the completion of mitigation for Building 25-3153 in accordance with
NNSS PA Appendix D. An ARA Update form was prepared to satisfy NNSS PA Appendix D.I.A (see
Appendix A). It provides additional information about the historic property, an individual NRHP
evaluation, and interior description, engineering drawings, and current and historical photographs. This
letter report fulfills NNSS PA Appendix D.I.B.
 DESCRIPTION OF BUILDING 25-3153
Building 25-3153, also known as the NRDS Fire Station, is the most prominent building remaining in the
complex near Gate 500 on Cane Springs Road at the south-central edge of the NRDS Historic District.
Building 25-3153 was the principal fire station for the NRDS and later provided emergency support to
adjacent areas. This is a one-story L-plan concrete block building with a flat roof (Figure 1). There are
four equipment bays located on the southern elevation and the eastern elevation contains the main
personnel entrance. Constructed in 1961 and used throughout the district’s period of significance (POS), the
building housed responders and equipment for emergencies in the NRDS district in support of nuclear
rocket development.

The personnel entrance on the eastern elevation opens into the medical treatment room. At the northern
end of the medical treatment room are three smaller rooms that were also either medical treatment rooms
or the room designated as the ward. The three rooms have painted concrete block walls and are simple
and unadorned.
The main space located within Building 25-3153 is the Apparatus Room. The Apparatus Room is
rectangular in shape with four large vehicle bays. Each bay has an identical overhead, roll-up vehicle door
on the south wall. The eastern bay was originally the shortest bay as there were two offices located at its
northern end, but they have since been removed.
The northern wall of the Apparatus Room originally had three doors and an entrance to the hallway
leading to the staff areas. These rooms have been greatly altered, and do not resemble their original uses.
The storage room is closed off, the kitchen has no kitchen or dining equipment, and the dormitory/day
room is an open room that has potentially been repurposed for various training exercises for staff at the
NNSS. In these rooms, the walls are painted concrete block and there are aluminum framed windows,
most have been painted in a camouflage style to block light. There is also a mechanical room at the
northeastern corner of the building that was blocked off. Behind the building, there are terraces
surrounded by a retaining wall (AR1) and a concrete pad for an electrical transformer (AR2).
Building 25-3153 has one main area that can be considered a character-defining space, the Apparatus
Room (Figure 2). The Apparatus Room is the largest part of the fire station. One area within the
Apparatus Room has been modified, where the walls of two adjacent offices were removed, but, overall
the space remains similar to its original construction. This includes the four bays for vehicles including
the four overhead, roll-up doors. This space reflects the original design and function of the building as a
fire station.

 SIGNIFICANCE SUMMARY
In 1956, the Atomic Energy Commission (now the U.S. Department of Energy) and the National
Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (later the National Aeronautics and Space Administration) selected
an area in the southwestern portion of today’s Nevada National Security Site (NNSS) to test reactors and
engines developed by Los Alamos National Laboratory and other private contractors under the Rover and
NERVA nuclear thermal rocket propulsion programs. The overall objective was to design an efficient and
reliable system to boost both piloted and autonomous rockets to the moon, Mars, and beyond.
Construction began in 1957 on the specialized test stands, maintenance, assembly, and disassembly
buildings, transportation system, and infrastructure that became the NRDS.
As the principal fire station for the NRDS, the building was part of the general support infrastructure.
Constructed in 1961 and used until the nuclear rocketry program ended, the building provided response
for emergencies in support of nuclear rocket development.
Fire Stations at the NNSS
The practice of firefighting and the construction of a fire station became a standard in every Euro-American
community in Nevada by the late-nineteenth century. Architecture of fire stations in Nevada has generally
followed the national trends, and have progressed as a combination of contemporary architectural
preferences, but with the need to function both as a place for fire-fighting equipment and for housing
firefighters. Although there was generally a consideration for the style of a typical fire station, the driving
focus behind its design was its functionality (Bertolini 2017).
Dating to 1951, the first fire station at the NNSS was located in Building 23-109 in the town of Mercury.
Mercury, like other towns on military bases, retained its own fire suppression detachment on site, which was

crucial due to the town’s remote location. The fire station in Mercury was originally designed as a service
station but was immediately adapted to serve as both a fire station and service station.
Fire station designs during the mid-century in Nevada favored Modern styles, often with an un-styled garage
space. The interiors were made to house larger equipment than in previous eras, with little extra space for
other gear. It was also during this time that offices became a standard addition to the design of a fire house,
along with accommodations such as locker rooms and bunk rooms for the fire personnel who would live
onsite or stay overnight (Bertolini 2017).
In 1966, Building 23-109, a demountable building, was modified to serve as the Housing Office for the
town of Mercury and a new fire station was constructed (Reno et al. 2018). The new building, Fire Station
23-425, served as the principal fire station for the entire NNSS. Fire Station 23-425 was constructed at the
corner of Sandstone Avenue and Buster Street, approximately 550 feet to the southwest of Building 23-109.
This fire station was designed specifically to serve as a fire station, which was reflected in its exterior
appearance. Three large bays with overhead doors dominated the central part of the building; rising in height
above the two wings that flanked it to the north and south. The central bays were for the apparatus room,
and the wing to the north housed mostly offices and administrative functions. The southern wing housed the
dormitory, locker room, and other accommodations necessary for fire safety personnel.
Two fire stations were built on the NNSS in 1961 in forward areas: Building 12-6 and Building 25-3153
located within the NRDS complex. Building 25-3153 was constructed as the fire station at the NRDS. This
fire station has four equipment bays with overhead metal doors; offices; and support areas. Unlike Building
23-109, this building is clearly identifiable as a fire station. The building was designed with the large
equipment bay doors as the focal point of the south elevation. The style of the building, while utilitarian, has
some mid-century architectural details, with a wide flat roof with overhanging eaves.
In 1968/1969, a fire station was constructed at the Control Point in Area 6 to replace the temporary
accommodations that were first occupied in 1952. The new fire station, 06-CP-70, was built to withstand
extreme ground shock and serve as a fallout shelter if necessary. This fire station placed emergency
responders almost 20 miles closer to the forward areas than the personnel at the Mercury fire station.
Building 06-CP-70 had a different style than its predecessors in Mercury, Area 12, and at the NRDS.
Constructed out of reinforced concrete with a low-gabled cement roof and no exterior windows, the building
was also built into the slope causing a partially subterranean lower level.
Individual Significance Evaluation
As an individual property, Building 25-3153 is not recommended eligible for listing in the NRHP.
Although the building and its functions were important in the day-to-day activities within the NRDS
district, there is no information indicating it was directly or importantly associated with any specific
scientific discoveries or events during its POS; thus, the fire station is not considered significant under
Criterion A. It is not considered significant under Criterion B, as there is also no evidence that the
property is related to the lives of significant persons in our past. Building 25-3153 is also not considered
significant under Criterion C. It is a utilitarian concrete block building with few defining characteristics. It
does not represent any distinctive characteristics of type, period, or method of construction or possess
high artistic value, nor is it the work of a master or a significant and distinguishable entity. Lastly, it is not
significant under Criterion D, as it has already been subject to research for its role in nuclear testing, fire
stations, and the NRDS at the NNSS. It is unlikely Building 25-3153 could yield more information that
could be considered important.

Integrity
Building 25-3153 is in good condition and retains all seven aspects of integrity to some degree to convey
its significance as a contributing element to the NRDS Historic District. It retains the integrity of location,
design, and setting. The integrity of materials in the interior of the building has been diminished because
some walls and features that were integral to the building as a fire station and medical facility have been
removed. The building also retains the integrity of workmanship, feeling, and association.

 6. Narrative Eligibility Justification
Provide a detailed explanation of the resource’s eligibility for the National Register, including supporting historic information, methods
for evaluation under the four criteria, discussion of the seven aspects of integrity, and conclusions about eligibility.

 Due to the limited scope of this investigation, the NRDS Fire Station (25-3153, B19004) was not individually
 evaluated for eligibility to the NRHP under any of the Secretary of the Interior’s Significance Criteria (36 CFR 60.4).
 For the purposes of the present survey, resources identified within the boundary of the Nuclear Rocket Development
 Station (NRDS) Historic District were only evaluated to determine if they contribute to the significance of the district.
 The NRDS Historic District is recommended eligible for listing to the NRHP under all four criteria as the testing
 center for the Rover/NERVA (Nuclear Engine for Rocket Vehicle Application) program from 1956 to 1973.
 Building 25-3153 was the principal fire station for the NRDS and later also emergency support to adjacent areas. This
 red building with its signage was a prominent introduction to Jackass Flats for workers throughout the NRDS period.
 Manipulation of massing, clever use of the wide eaves, and shadow effects from the pilasters along with use of glass
 block windows combine to indicate that much thought and some extra expense went into making a building with a
 striking appearance. This building contributes to the significance of the NRDS district as part of the general support
 infrastructure. Constructed in 1961 and used throughout districts period of significance, the building provided response
 for emergencies in the NRDS district in support of nuclear rocket development. The building’s Mid-century Modern style
 also stands out. Regarding integrity, the building remains substantially unaltered and retains all seven aspects of
 location, design, materials, setting, feeling, and association to convey its significance.
 The Fire Station is a contributing element to the NRDS Historic District.

 Fire Stations at the NNSS
 The practice of firefighting and the construction of a fire station became a standard in every Euro-American community
 in Nevada by the late-nineteenth century. Architecture of fire stations in Nevada has generally followed the national
 trends and have progressed as a combination of contemporary architectural preferences, but with the need to function
 both as a place for fire-fighting equipment and for housing firefighters. Although there was generally a consideration for
 the style of a typical fire station, the driving focus behind its design was its functionality (Bertolini 2017).
 Dating to 1951, the first fire station at the NNSS was located in Building 23-109 in the town of Mercury. Mercury, like
 other towns on military bases, retained its own fire suppression detachment on site, which was crucial due to the town’s
 remote location. The fire station in Mercury was originally designed as a service station but was immediately adapted to
 serve as both a fire station and service station.
 Fire station designs during the mid-century in Nevada favored Modern styles, often with an un-styled garage space.
 The interiors were made to house larger equipment than in previous eras, with little extra space for other gear. It was
 also during this time that offices became a standard addition to the design of a fire house, along with accommodations
 such as locker rooms and bunk rooms for the fire personnel who would live onsite or stay overnight (Bertolini 2017).
 The fire station in Building 23-109 included an equipment room that could fit three fire trucks, a bunk room, a locker
 room, and an associated office. The service station facilities included a wash stall and gas pumps outside. Likely due to
 the fact that Building 23-109 was not originally designed expressly to serve as a fire station, its exterior did not
 immediately convey its fire station use.
 In 1966, Building 23-109, a demountable building, was modified to serve as the Housing Office for the town of Mercury
 and a new fire station was constructed (Reno et al. 2018). The new building, Fire Station 23-425, served as the
 principal fire station for the entire NNSS. Fire Station 23-425 was constructed at the corner of Sandstone Avenue and
 Buster Street, approximately 550 feet to the southwest of Building 23-109. This fire station was designed specifically to
 serve as a fire station, which was reflected in its exterior appearance. Three large bays with overhead doors dominated
 the central volume of the building; rising in height above the two wings that flanked it to the north and south. The central
 bays were for the apparatus room, and the wing to the north housed mostly offices and administrative functions. The
 southern wing housed the dormitory, locker room, and other accommodations necessary for fire safety personnel. This
 fire station was centrally located in Mercury; however, there were smaller facilities located in the forward areas,
 including stations at the NRDS in Jackass Flats, at Control Point in Area 6, and in the Area 12 Camp.
 Two fire stations were built on the NNSS in 1961 in forward areas: Building 12-6 and Building 25-3153. Building 12-6
 was constructed in Area 12 Camp located at the northern end of the NNSS. Like Building 23-109, this fire station was
 also a demountable building. It was a typical box-type fire station, almost perfectly square, with three overhead doors
 dominating the front elevation. The offices and other associated services in the area wrapped around the side and rear
 elevations. It had a flat roof, and each elevation had windows of various sizes. There were entry doors on the south,
 east and north elevations (Reno et al. 2021).
 Building 25-3153 was constructed as the fire station at the NRDS. This fire station has four equipment bays with
 overhead metal doors and offices to the west and the support areas on the north. Unlike Building 23-109, this building
 is clearly identifiable as a fire station. The building was designed with the large equipment bay doors as the focal point
 of the south elevation. The style of the building, while utilitarian, has some mid-century architectural details, with a wide
 flat roof with overhanging eaves.
 In 1968/1969, a fire station was constructed in Control Point in Area 6 to replace the temporary accommodations that
 were first occupied in 1952. The new fire station, 06-CP-70, was built to withstand extreme ground shock and serve as
 a fallout shelter if necessary. This fire station placed emergency responders almost 20 miles closer to the forward
 areas than the personnel at the Mercury fire station. Building 06-CP-70 had a different style than its predecessors in
 Mercury, Area 12, and at the NRDS. Constructed out of reinforced concrete with a low-gabled cement roof and no
 exterior windows, the building was also built into the slope causing a partially subterranean lower level. The rear of the
 building, the east elevation, faced Mercury Highway and was where the equipment area was located. The elevation
 had three overhead doors, two of which were of the same smaller size and one larger door. The lower level contained

 NRHP Eligibility
 The Fire Station building is a contributing element to the NRDS Historic District, but it is not recommended individually
 eligible for listing in the NRHP under any of the Significance Criteria. Though it retains integrity, it lacks significance as
 outlined below.
 Criterion A. To be individually eligible for the National Register under Criterion A, properties must be associated with
 events or a series of events that have made a significant contribution to broad patterns of our history. Tlachac, in the
 historic context of the 1991 Nevada Comprehensive Preservation Plan, indicates that resources associated with
 nuclear testing may be eligible for listing in the NRHP. Regarding this criterion, Building 25-3153 is known to be a fire
 station in support of the NRDS complex. While the building and its function were important in the day-to-day activities of
 NRDS, there is no indication that it was directly and importantly associated with any important developments or
 scientific discoveries during the period of significance. Therefore, Building 25-3153 does not appear to be significant
 under Criterion A.
 Criterion B. Properties may be eligible for the National Register if they are associated with the lives of persons
 significant in our past. Although there is potential that important persons visited the fire station, there is no evidence
 that any person having a significant role in the nuclear rocket research program did their important work at the fire
 station. Therefore, Building 25-3153 is not recommended as significant under Criterion B.
 Criterion C. Properties may be eligible under Criterion C if they embody the distinctive characteristics of a type, period,
 or method of construction, or that represent the work of a master, or that possess high artistic values. Properties may
 also be eligible under this criterion if they represent a significant and distinguishable entity whose components may lack
 individual distinction. Building 25-3153 is a utilitarian, concrete block structure with no distinguishable characteristics
 and does not embody a type, period, or method of construction. The engineering drawings indicated that REECo was
 the engineer of the building, the on-site NTS contractor for construction of many buildings and structures. The building
 does not possess high artistic value, nor does it represent a significant and distinguishable entity. It does not appear to
 be significant under Criterion C.
 Criterion D. Properties significant under Criterion D must have the potential to yield further information about human
 history that can only be answered by the physical material of the resources. There are two requirements for a resource
 to be found significant under Criterion D: the property must have or have had information to contribute to our
 understanding of human history or prehistory, and the information must be considered important. Building 25-3153 has
 been subject to research on its role in fire stations and the NRDS. It is unlikely that it could yield more information that
 would be considered important. Building 25-3153 does not appear to be significant under Criterion D.
 Integrity
 Building 25-3153 is in good condition and retains all seven aspects of integrity to varying degrees to convey its
 significance as a contributing element to the NRDS Historic District. It retains the integrity of location, design, and
 setting. The integrity of materials in the interior of the structure has been diminished because some walls and features
 that were integral to the building as a fire station and medical facility have been removed. The building also retains the
 integrity of workmanship, feeling, and association.

 For a detailed description of the exterior of the building, see Reno et al. 2023 and the building’s original ARA form.
 The main entrance to the fire station is located on the eastern elevation and consists of two metal doors with a fixed
 light in the upper half of each door. The doors open into the medical treatment room. The room is open on the
 southern end but has three smaller divided rooms at the northern end. The eastern wall is painted concrete block
 and the western wall has white, square tile that is also present on the ceiling. On the eastern wall is a window of
 glass block four high and 12 across. There was originally a second glass block window on the southern wall of the
 medical treatment room; however, it has since been covered over by the same tile present on the west wall and
 ceiling, although it is still visible on the exterior. The west wall has one fixed pane window with the view into the
 Apparatus Room. There is also a built-in medicine cabinet on the west wall adjacent to the double doors leading into

 the Apparatus Room. At the northern end of the medical treatment room are three smaller rooms that were also
 either medical treatment rooms, or the room designated as the ward. The three rooms have painted concrete block
 walls and are simple and unadorned. In the smallest room, designated 104, there is a small, aluminum, two-light
 horizontal sliding window. At the northern end of the ward, there is a similar window, but larger in size.
 The main space located within Building 25-3153 is the Apparatus Room. The Apparatus Room is rectangular in
 shape with four large vehicle bays. Each bay has an identical roll-up vehicle door on the south wall. The eastern bay
 was originally the shortest bay, as there were two offices located at the bay’s northern end, but the office walls have
 since been removed.
 The northern wall of the Apparatus Room originally had three doors and an entrance to the hallway leading to the
 staff areas. The most western door that led into a storage room has been enclosed with a metal panel. The corridor
 leads into the staff and storage areas that include the shower room, the storage room, the kitchen and dining room
 and the dormitory/day room. These rooms have been greatly altered and do not resemble their original designs. The
 storage room is closed off, the kitchen has no kitchen or dining equipment, and the dormitory/day room is an open
 room that has potentially been repurposed for various training exercises for staff on the NNSS. In these rooms, the
 walls are painted concrete block and there are aluminum framed windows, most of which have been painted in a
 camouflage style to block light. There is also a mechanical room at the northeastern corner of the building that was
 blocked off.
 Building 25-3153 has one main area that can be considered a character-defining space — the Apparatus Room. The
 Apparatus Room is the main volume of the fire station. One area has been modified, where the walls of two adjacent
 offices were removed, but, overall the space remains similar to its original construction. This includes the four bays
 for vehicles including the four overhead, roll-up doors. This space reflects the original design and function of the
 building as a fire station.
