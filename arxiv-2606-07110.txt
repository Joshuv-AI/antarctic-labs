Entanglement from Expansion: High Rank-Width in Deterministic Graphs
Tristan Cam, Cyril Gavoille, Yvan Le Borgne, Simon Martiel

Abstract
Entanglement in quantum graph states is intrinsically linked to rank-width, a graph complexity measure introduced by Oum and Seymour. In this work, we enable the preparation of maximally entangled deterministic graph states in constant depth by developing a general method to derive lower bounds on the rank-width of regular graphs from their edge expansion. By bridging edge-isoperimetric inequalities with the strong chromatic index and Jelínek’s approach for lower bounding cut-rank, we systematically establish lower bounds for the rank-width of Cartesian products, including hypercubes, Hamming graphs, and grids. Extending this framework via Boolean function analysis, using a generalization of the Kahn–Kalai–Linial’s Theorem, we strengthen the bounds for all Cartesian products by a non-trivial logarithmic factor. These methods result in the discovery of deterministic families of graphs on n n vertices with a provably maximum rank-width Θ ⁡ ( n ) \Theta(n) . Our results fill the previous gap in the literature for deterministic graph families of rank-width greater than Θ ⁡ ( n ) \Theta(\sqrt{n}) .

Preliminary notions

Throughout the paper, all graphs considered will be simple, finite and undirected.

2.1
Rank-width and mim-width

A
cut
in a graph
G=(V,E)
is a pair of non-empty subsets such that
X\subsetneq V
, and
\overline{X}=V\setminus X
.
The
bi-adjacency matrix
of a cut
(X,\overline{X})
is the 0-1 matrix whose rows are indexed by
X
, columns are indexed by
\overline{X}
, and the entry in row
i
and column
j
is equal to 1 if and only if
i
and
j
are connected by an edge of
G
.
The
cut-rank
of
X
is the
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-rank of adjacency matrix of the cut
(X,\overline{X})
, written
\rho_{G}(X)
.

A tree is
subcubic
if every node has degree 1 or 3. A
rank-decomposition
of a graph
G=(V,E)
is a pair
(T,L)
of a subcubic tree
T
with at least two nodes and a bijection
L
from
V
to the set of all leaves of
T
. For each edge
e
of
T
,
T-e
induces a partition
(A_{e},B_{e})
of the leaves of
T
and we say that the width of
e
is
\rho_{G}(L^{-1}(A_{e}))
. Note that
\rho_{G}(X)=\rho_{G}(\overline{X})
for all
X\subseteq V
, so the choice of
A_{e}
or
B_{e}
does not change the width of
e
.

The
width
of a rank-decomposition
(T,L)
is the maximum width of edges in
T
. The
rank-width
of a graph
G
, denoted by
\mathrm{rw}(G)
, is the minimum width over all rank-decompositions of
G
. (If
G
has less than two vertices, then
G
has no rank-decompositions; in this case we say that
G
has rank-width 0.)

Similarly for the mim-width, over all decompositions
(T,L)
(called
branch-decomposition
for a general width), each edge
e
induces a partition
(A_{e},B_{e})
and thus a cut
(X,\overline{X})
. This time, the width of
e
is given by the function
\mathrm{mim}(X)
equal to the size of the maximum induced matching between vertices of
X
and
\overline{X}
. The width of a branch-decomposition
(T,L)
is the maximum width of edges in
T
.

The
mim-width
of a graph
G
, denoted by
\mathrm{mimw}(G)
, is the minimum width over all branch-decompositions of
G
.
Interestingly, a witness of large mim-width is also a witness of rank-width: finding a large induced matching
M
in the
cut-graph

G[\partial X]
induced by the edges
\partial X
of a cut
(X,\overline{X})
forces the bi-adjacency matrix
A(G[M])
of
G[\partial X\cap M]=G[M]
to be isomorphic to the identity matrix of size
|M|
(up to re-indexing). Then, it is clear that
A(G[M])\cong\mathbb{I}_{|M|}
has full-rank and this is a witness that the
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-rank of
A(G[\partial X])
is at least
|M|
. Thus, for all
G
,
\mathrm{rw}(G)\geqslant\mathrm{mimw}(G)
.

2.2
Jelínek’s lemma

The
cut-graph
with respect to a cut
(X,\overline{X})
in
G
written
G[\partial X]
is the subgraph induced by
\partial X
, i.e., the bipartite subgraph of
G
composed of the union of all the edges of
\partial X
. The
directed cut-graph
is the cut-graph where all its edges are directed from
X
to
\overline{X}
.

A
matching

M
in a graph
G
is an independent set of edges, i.e., such that no two edges of
M
have a common endpoints. For convenience, we denote by
V(M)
the set of endpoints of all the edges of
M
.

A matching
M
in a cut-graph is
acyclic
if the subgraph induced by
V(M)
, in the directed cut-graph, is a directed acyclic graph.
Moreover,
M
is
induced
if the subgraph induced by
V(M)
, in the cut-graph, is
M
itself. Obviously, any induced matching is also acyclic in the cut-graph. We sometimes refer to matchings in the cut-graph induced by
\partial X
as
(X,\overline{X})
-matchings.
It is well-known that (see
[
36
, Lemma 2.1]
for a short proof):

Lemma 3
.

For every graph
G
with
n\geqslant 2
vertices, if for every cut
(X,\overline{X})
of
G
such that
\min(|X|,|\overline{X}|)\geqslant n/3
, we have
\rho_{G}(X)\geqslant k~(\text{resp. }\mathrm{mim}(X)\geqslant k)

\mathrm{Then},~\mathrm{rw}(G)\geqslant k~(\text{resp. }\mathrm{mimw}(G)\geqslant k)

Intuitively, as every (rank,branch)-decomposition contains an edge whose removal will induce a cut with a
balanced
number of vertices on each side i.e. such that
\min(|X|,|\overline{X}|)\geqslant n/3
(
intermediate value theorem
argument), then to lower bound the maximum width over all edge in a decomposition, it is enough to lower bound the width of edges that induce balanced cuts. Therefore, when investigating the width of all decompositions, the most common approach is to find the minimum width over any balanced cut.
For rank-width in particular, Jelínek proposed the following method to lower bound the rank of a cut’s adjacency matrix.

Lemma 4

(
[
28
, Lemma 2]
)
.

Let
(X,\overline{X})
be a cut of a graph
G
having an acyclic matching
M
. Then,
\rho_{G}(X)\geqslant|M|
.

Then, the direct consequence of these lemma that we will use throughout this work is as follows.

Lemma 5

(Width from balanced cuts)
.

For every graph
G
with
n\geqslant 2
vertices, if for every cut
(X,\overline{X})
of
G
such that
\min(|X|,|\overline{X}|)\geqslant n/3
there exists an induced matching
M^{\ast}
of size at least
|M^{\ast}|\geqslant k
, then

\mathrm{rw}(G)\geqslant\mathrm{mimw}(G)\geqslant k.

2.3
Product of graphs

The
line graph

L(G)
of
G=(V,E)
is the graph that has as vertex set
E
with two vertices adjacent if and only if their corresponding edges in
V
are incident.

The
k
-th power of
G
, denoted by
G^{k}
, is a graph that has the same vertex set as
G
, and two vertices are adjacent if and only if they are within distance at most
k
in
G
.

The
Cartesian product

G\square H
of two graphs
G
and
H
is a graph that has as vertex set
V(G)\times V(H)
and as edge set
\{(u,x)(v,y):uv\in E(G)\text{ and }x=y\text{, or }xy\in E(H)\text{ and }u=v\}.

Given graphs
G_{1},\dots,G_{n}
, we will write their Cartesian product as

G=\bigsquare_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}\text{ or }\bigsquare_{i}G_{i}.

Additionally, given a graph
G
, we will denote by
(G)^{\square k}
the
k
-th Cartesian power of
G
, i.e. the graph

(G)^{\square k}=\bigsquare_{i=1}^{k}G.

2.4
Strong chromatic index

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, a
proper edge colouring
is a mapping
c:E\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
satisfying

c[(u,v)]\neq c[(v,w)],~\forall(u,v)\neq(v,w)\in E.

A
strong edge-colouring
of
G
is a proper edge-colouring such that no edge is adjacent to two edges of the same colour.
The
strong chromatic index
of
G
, often denoted as
\chi_{S}^{\prime}(G)
or
\chi_{2}^{\prime}(G)
, is the least integer
k
such that there exists a strong edge-colouring of
G
using
k
colours.
Notice that, if we denote by
\chi_{2}(G)
the
distance-2 chromatic number
of
G
, i.e. the smallest number of colours required to colour all the vertices of
G
, such that no two vertices within distance
2
share a colour, then the strong edge-colouring problem can related to colouring powers of graphs as follows
\chi_{S}^{\prime}(G)=\chi_{2}(L(G))=\chi(L(G)^{2}).

Thus, it is clear that any colour in a strong edge-colouring of
G
corresponds to a stable in
L(G)^{2}
, which precisely corresponds to an induced matching.
As, if
G
has a strong edge-colouring using
\chi_{S}^{\prime}(G)
colours and
|E|=m
, there exists a colour that is shared by at least
\frac{m}{\chi_{S}^{\prime}(G)}
edges, this means that there will always exist an induced matching
M\subseteq E
such that
|M|\geqslant\frac{m}{\chi_{S}^{\prime}(G)}
(and we can write
\nu_{S}(G)\geqslant\frac{m}{\chi_{S}^{\prime}(G)}
). Note that if
G
can be strongly edge-coloured using
\chi_{S}^{\prime}(G)
coloured, then the same goes for the induced subgraph
G[\partial X]
, for any
X\subseteq V
(such as a cut-graph, that we will use repeatedly).

Similarly, we can recall the much rarer parameter defined by
[
3
]
as part of their
r
-degenerate chromatic indices
\chi_{\mathrm{ac}}(G)=\chi_{1}^{\prime}(G)
the acyclic chromatic index such that the acylclic matching number of
G
satisfies
\nu_{\mathrm{ac}}(G)\geqslant\frac{m}{\chi_{S}^{\prime}(G)}
. Few results are known about these parameters, thus we will generally use the strong chromatic index (that has been computed for many families of graphs) unless an exact value
\chi_{S}^{\prime}(G)
is not known and we have to use the greedy upper bound. Indeed, we know that
\chi_{S}^{\prime}(G)=\chi(L(G)^{2})
, thus we can use the greedy upper bound

\chi_{S}^{\prime}(G)\leqslant\Delta(L(G)^{2})+1\leqslant 2\Delta(\Delta-1)+1,

by greedy coloring, as
L(G)
is
2(\Delta-1)
-regular so the maximum degree of
(L(G))^{2}
is at most
2\Delta(\Delta-1)+1

This gives the best general asymptotic upper bound for
\mathrm{mimw}(G)\geqslant\frac{\min|\partial X|}{\chi_{S}^{\prime}(G)}\geqslant\frac{\min|\partial X|}{2\Delta^{2}}
, but can be slightly improved for
\mathrm{rw}{G}\geqslant\frac{\min|\partial X|}{\chi_{\mathrm{ac}}(G)}\geqslant\frac{\min|\partial X|}{\Delta^{2}}
using the following lemma by
[

]

Lemma 6

Let
G
be a graph of maximum degree
\Delta

|E(G)|=m
, then

\chi_{\mathrm{ac}}\leqslant\Delta^{2}~\text{and}~\nu_{\mathrm{ac}}\geqslant\frac{m}{\Delta^{2}}

2.5
Isoperimetric inequalities

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with a cut
(X,\overline{X})
. The
edge boundary
of
X
, denoted by
\partial X
, is defined as

\partial X:=\left\{{uv\in E:u\in X,v\in\overline{X}}\right\}
.
The
edge expansion

h(G)
of
G
(also called
Cheeger constant
) is then defined as

h(G)=\min_{X\subsetneq V}\frac{|\partial X|}{\min(|X|,|\overline{X}|)}=\min_{0<|X|\leqslant|V|/2}\frac{|\partial X|}{|X|}.

In the following, we will be interested in graphs whose edge expansion is bounded away from 0.
We will then say that, given such a graph
G
and a cut
(X,\overline{X})
(where
X
is chosen to be the subset satisfying
|X|\leqslant|V|/2
), we can express the size of the edge boundary as

h(G)\cdot|X|\leqslant\mathscr{F}(|X|)\leqslant|\partial X|,
a so-called
edge-isoperimetric inequality
(as described by Tillich in
[
52
]
for instance).

We will use isoperimetric inequalities and strong chromatic index to lower bound rank-width.

3.1
Exact bounds based on the strong chromatic index

See

and since trivially

Because we chose

\mathrm{rw}(G)\geqslant\mathrm{mimw}(G)\geqslant\frac{\min|\partial X|}{\chi_{S}^{\prime}(G)}\geqslant\min_{p\in[\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{2}]}\frac{\mathscr{F}\left({p\,|V|}\right)}{\chi_{S}^{\prime}(G)}.

Proof.

From
[

]

and the average degree of

(see their Lemma 2.9).

Case 1.

\mathrm{rw}(G)\geqslant\frac{n\cdot h(G)}{3\Delta(G)^{2}}
and
\Delta(G)\leqslant k
, thus immediately
\mathrm{rw}(G)\geqslant\frac{nk}{6k^{2}}=\Omega\left({\frac{n}{k}}\right).

Case 2.
If we take the explicit lower bound on

and upper bound

given by
[

\displaystyle h(G)

\displaystyle\geqslant k^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}\log_{2}\log k+c_{0}\log_{2}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}})-1}~~\text{ for }c_{0}=c_{0}(\epsilon)\in[0,0.032]

\displaystyle\overline{d}(G)

\displaystyle\leqslant k^{2\log_{2}\log k+2\log_{2}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}})}

Then, we can state the following

By
\cref

\cref

\mathrm{rw}(G)=\Omega\left({\frac{n\cdot h(G)}{\overline{d}(G)\cdot\Delta(G)}}\right).

This implies that in

\mathrm{rw}(G)=\Omega\left({\frac{n\cdot\overline{d}(G)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{c}}}}{\overline{d}(G)\cdot k}}\right)=\Omega\left({\frac{n}{\overline{d}(G)^{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{c}}}\cdot k}}\right)=\Omega\left({\frac{n}{k^{2-\nicefrac{{1}}{{c}}}}}\right).

3.4
Spectral gap

Throughout this whole section, let
G
be a connected
d
-regular graph with
n
vertices. The Cheeger inequalities
[
2
]
give bounds the edge expansion using the spectral gap
\lambda=d-\lambda_{2}
:

\frac{\lambda}{2}\leqslant h(G)\leqslant\sqrt{2d\lambda}~,

where
\lambda_{1}\geqslant\lambda_{2}\geqslant\dots\geqslant\lambda_{n}
are the real eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix of
G
.

By standard spectral graph theory, the trivial eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix of G is
\lambda_{1}=d
and the first non-trivial eigenvalue is
\lambda_{2}
. If
G
is connected, then
\lambda_{2}<d
and it is known that
\lambda_{n}=-d
if and only if
G
is bipartite. Note that the lower bound is tight for hypercubes since
h(\mathcal{Q}_{n})=1
and the spectral gap is
\lambda=2
.

Corollary 16

(
Line graph
)
.

Let
G
be a connected
d
-regular graph with
n
vertices and have spectral gap
\lambda
. Let
L^{(k)}(G)
be the
k
-th iterated line graph of
G
of regularity
d_{k}
and
n_{k}
vertices.

\mathrm{rw}(G)~\geqslant~\frac{n\lambda}{12d^{2}}~~~~\text{and}~~~~\mathrm{rw}\left({L^{(k)}(G)}\right)~\geqslant~\frac{n_{k}\lambda}{12d_{k}^{2}}~=~\Omega\left({n_{k}^{\ 1-\nicefrac{{4}}{{k}}}}\right)

Proof.

From
[
44
]
, the spectral gap
\lambda
of
L(G)
is equal to that of
G
. Thus, the Cheeger inequalities apply identically and by
\cref
thm:algo and
\cref
lemma:acyclic we have for instance

\mathrm{rw}\left({L(G)}\right)\geqslant\frac{(nd)/2\cdot\lambda}{6\cdot(2(d-2))^{2}}\geqslant\frac{n\lambda}{48d}

which recovers from
\cref
cor:johnson:
\mathrm{rw}\left({J(n,2)}\right)=\mathrm{rw}\left({L(K_{n})}\right)\geqslant\frac{n\cdot n}{48n}=\Omega\left({\sqrt{N}}\right),~N=|V(L(K_{n}))|.

It is well known that if we iterate taking the line graph of itself, the number of vertices eventually grows to infinity (except when starting with a path, a cycle or a claw
[
32
]
) and of course the result from
[
44
]
implies that
\lambda
is invariant for any number of iteration of the line graph.

For
G
a
d
-regular graph on
n
vertices, the regularity of
L^{(k)}(G)
grows as
d_{k}=2^{k}(d-2)+2
and the number
n_{k}
of vertices of

is given by

n_{k}=n\cdot\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{d_{j}}{2}=n\cdot\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\left({2^{j-1}(d-2)+1}\right)\leqslant\left({\frac{d}{2}}\right)^{k}2^{\binom{k}{2}}.

Then, by the naive upper bound on
\chi_{S}^{\prime}\left({L^{(k)}(G)}\right)
and
\cref
thm:algo we have

\mathrm{rw}\left({L^{(k)}(G)}\right)\geqslant\frac{n_{k}\cdot\lambda}{6\cdot(2d_{k}(d_{k}-1)+1)}=\frac{n\cdot\lambda}{12}\cdot\frac{\left({\frac{d}{2}}\right)^{k}\cdot 2^{\binom{k}{2}-2k}}{(d-2)^{2}+\frac{3(d-2)}{2k}+\frac{\nicefrac{{5}}{{2}}}{2^{2k}}}\geqslant\frac{n\cdot\lambda}{12}\cdot\frac{\left({\frac{d}{2}}\right)^{k}}{(d-1)^{2}}\cdot\frac{2^{\binom{k}{2}}}{2^{2k}}=B.

This by itself does not give much intuition on how the explosion of the expansion in iterated line graphs affects rank-width.

Note that

rapidly grows as an exponential in

, while
d_{k}
is roughly exponential in
k
. Thus, an asymptotic analysis gives

\log_{n_{k}}B\geqslant\frac{(k-2)\ln d+\frac{k(k-7)}{2}\log 2}{k\ln d+\frac{k(k-3)}{2}\ln 2}=1-\frac{4\ln 2}{\Delta}-\frac{4\ln d}{k\Delta},

where
\Delta=k\ln 2+2\ln d-3\ln 2.

Therefore, this is asymptotically
\log_{n_{k}}B=1-\frac{4}{k}+\mathcal{O}\left({\frac{1}{k^{2}}}\right),
thus
\mathrm{rw}\left({L^{(k)}(G)}\right)=\Omega\left({n_{k}^{\ 1-\nicefrac{{4}}{{k}}}}\right).

∎

4.1
Boolean function analysis

Let

-regular graph with

Using all notations from the work of Sachdeva and Tulsiani

There is a natural notion of

given by the Cartesian product: given a vertex

for all

As we will only be considering balanced cuts where
|X|=np
for

generating

\mathrm{Var}(f)=4p(1-p)\in[\nicefrac{{8}}{{9}},1].

When defined according to such a function, the edges of the cut

Then,

\mathrm{Inf}_{i}(f)\cdot|\partial X|=|\partial_{i}X|.

and

5
Applications and discussion

5.1
Graph state preparation

A graph state
|G\rangle
on
n
qubits is a stabilizer state represented by an undirected graph
G
. To prepare
|G\rangle
, one only has to prepare the state
|+\rangle^{\otimes n}=|\overline{K_{n}}\rangle
and for each edge
\{u,v\}\in E(G)
apply a controlled-Z (CZ) operation on the corresponding qubits
u
and
v
. This method allows to prepare
|G\rangle
using exactly
\lvert E(G)\rvert
two-qubit gates. Moreover, by edge-coloring the edges of
G
in at most
\Delta(G)
matchings, each matching corresponds to a set of two-qubit gates that can be executed in parallel, so
|G\rangle
can be prepared with a circuit
C
of depth at most
\Delta(G)
using only two-qubits gates between neighbouring qubits, assuming all-to-all connectivity. To run
C
on a grid-like architecture (that does not allow long-distance gates), one can transpile
C
into an equivalent circuit
C^{\prime}
compatible with the restricted architecture, using for instance qubit routing: for a grid-like architecture, this entails a multiplicative overhead of
o(\sqrt{n})
on the depth of
C^{\prime}

[
9
]
.

This work provides excellent candidates to build maximum entanglement in constant depth through deterministic families of graphs of large rank-width. Most promising among these are the hypercube and the (near-)Ramanujan graphs. As a graph state,
|\mathcal{Q}_{k}\rangle
can be prepared in depth
k
assuming all-to-all connectivity, thus in logarithmic depth compared to the numbers of qubits, and depth
o(\sqrt{n}\log n)
on a grid-like architecture. Random regular graphs states have been shown to generate maximum entanglement
with high probability
by Ghosh, Hangleiter and Helsen
[
22
]
. These states can be prepared by a constant depth circuit and can be used in practical quantum advantage experiments. This works shows that all non-trivial
d
-regular (near-)Ramanujan graphs, some of which have explicit constructions
[
40
,
41
]
for fixed
d
, attain maximum rank-width of
\Theta(n)
. The corresponding graph states can be prepared in constant depth and have certified maximum entanglement, i.e. with probability 1. Therefore, these states exhibit the same good properties as the random regular graph states, while being deterministically defined thus guaranteeing entanglement.

\mathrm{mimw}(G)\leqslant\mathrm{rw}(G)\leqslant\mathrm{tw}(G)+1\leqslant\mathrm{pw}(G)+1~~\text{and}~~\mathrm{rw}(G)\leqslant\mathrm{cw}(G).

Thus, our lower bounds naturally apply to every other parameter. We can also use any upper bound on a parameter greater than the rank-width (asymptotically). For instance, for the
k
-dimensional grid we have
\mathrm{tw}(G_{k,m})=\Theta(m^{k-1})
(from the balanced separator being a
(k-1)
-dimensional hyperplane), thus

\Omega\left({\frac{m^{k-1}}{k}}\right)=\mathrm{rw}(G_{k,m})=\mathcal{O}(m^{k-1}).

Sunil Chandran and Kavitha recently gave the asymptotical value of the tree-width for the hypercube
[
8
]

\mathrm{tw}(\mathcal{Q}_{k})=\Theta\left({\frac{2^{k}}{\sqrt{k}}}\right)
. By
[
21
, Theorem 21]
, for graphs excluding some fixed
K_{t,t}
as a subgraph, both tree-width and clique-width are upper bounded by a polynomial factor on the rank-width.
\mathcal{Q}_{k}
excludes
K_{3,3}
as a subgraph for all
k
, showing that
\mathrm{rw}(\mathcal{Q}_{k})=\Omega(\mathrm{tw}(\mathcal{Q}_{k})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}})=\Omega\left({\frac{\sqrt[3]{n}}{\sqrt[6]{\log n}}}\right)
.
Our result gives the closer bounds

\Omega\left({\frac{n\log\log n}{\log n}}\right)=\mathrm{rw}(\mathcal{Q}_{k})=\mathcal{O}\left({\frac{n}{\sqrt{\log n}}}\right)

This gap is very tight, only a factor
\frac{\log\log n}{\sqrt{\log n}}
. This work did not manage to close the gap.

5.3
Final remarks

Interestingly, reminding of Jelínek’s work on the rank-width of the square grid
[
28
]
, our lower bound on the rank-width of Ramanujan graphs only used a matching-based technique to witness the asymptotic saturation of rank-width. By
[
55
]
, there exists many examples of graphs of mim-width
\Theta(1)
and rank-width
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
. Given a greedy algorithm to construct maximum acyclic matchings that performs asymptotically better than the greedy induced matching algorithm, our bound can be easily improved.
Also notice that often the isoperimetric inequalities used hold for any
X\subsetneq V(G)
, including very small subsets. These bounds capture small set expansion but these often are extremal cases that degrade the bound.
If one uses a finer definition of edge expansion only based on vertex subsets of balanced size, a stronger bound can likely be obtained.

Finally, we conjecture that the rank-width of the hypercube is
\Omega\left({\frac{2^{k}}{\sqrt{k}}}\right)
and matches its tree-width to give

\mathrm{rw}(\mathcal{Q}_{k})=\Theta\left({\frac{n}{\sqrt{\log n}}}\right)
