Executive Summary
We developed new software capabilities enabling simultaneous simulation of material mixing and
charged particle transport in Inertial Confinement Fusion (ICF) capsules. Los Alamos National
Laboratory (LANL) has proposed double-shell ICF capsule designs as an alternative approach to
achieving fusion ignition, offering advantages such as lower predicted temperatures for volumetric
ignition and eliminating the need for cryogenic deuterium-tritium (DT) ice layers. However, during
capsule compression, high-Z solid shell materials (like tungsten or molybdenum) mix with low-Z
DT fuel due to hydrodynamic instabilities, target engineering defects, and shock interactions. This
material mixing fundamentally alters how fusion-born alpha particles transport energy through the
capsule, directly impacting ignition performance. An existing LANL shock-hydrodynamics code
could simulate ICF with either charged particle transport or mixed materials independently, but
not both simultaneously. We addressed this limitation by implementing four key components: (1) a
material tracking system that assigns each particle a material handle identifying which material it
currently occupies, (2) a stochastic interface sampling routine using Chord Length Sampling (CLS)
based on the Levermore-Pomraning (LP) model to determine distances to material boundaries,
(3) a material ownership query system enabling particles to identify which material owns their
current location, and (4) source initialization routines ensuring newly created particles are correctly
assigned to their birth material. These components were integrated into the code’s primary raytrace
routine, material database system, and particle source modules. Furthermore, multi-dimensional
toy models of double-shell ICF capsules were created to validate the implementation. Currently,
it has been validated using 1D and 2D models, successfully demonstrating material switching as
particles traverse mixed regions. Although, this report will omit the 1D results for brevity. This
work represents the first step toward high-fidelity simulation of alpha particle energy deposition in
mixed-material regions of double-shell ICF capsules.

 However, during the compression phase of double-shell implosions, significant material mixing
can occur at the interface between the high-Z solid shell materials and the low-Z DT fuel. This
mixing arises from multiple physical mechanisms such as Rayleigh-Taylor instabilities at accelerating
interfaces, Richtmyer-Meshkov instabilities when shocks cross material boundaries, the dense pusher
decelerating into fuel, and target engineering defects [11, 12, 9]. Material mixing degrades ICF
capsule performance and fundamentally alters how fusion products transport energy. Fusion-born
alpha particles (3.5 MeV from DT fusion) are the primary mechanism for fuel heating and burn
propagation. When high-Z materials like tungsten mix into the DT fuel, the alpha particle stopping
power changes dramatically. The alphas deposit energy over shorter distances in tungsten compared
to DT. This can lead to localized energy deposition rather than distributed fuel heating, potentially
quenching the burn wave [9].
 Accurate simulation of charged particle transport through these mixed-material regions is crit-
ical for understanding particle-material interactions, predicting double-shell capsule performance,
and developing mitigation strategies. A current LANL multiphysics shock-hydrodynamics code can
simulate ICF capsule dynamics with material mixing, and separately can perform Monte Carlo
charged particle transport. However, the code lacked the ability to couple both physics capabilities
together. The charged particle transport module and material mixing models could not communi-
cate. Particles had no way to know which specific material they were traversing in a mixed zone,
possibly leading to incorrect energy deposition predictions.
 Our work this summer developed the coupling framework enabling particles to dynamically
track their local material environment and switch materials as they cross interfaces during trans-
port. Further work involved developing multidimensional toy models for simulating ICF capsule
implosions.

2 Background
We provided the necessary physical and computational context for understanding why accurate
mixed-material charged particle transport is necessary, beginning with the DT fusion reaction itself
and progressing through the computational framework developed to address this challenge.

2.1 DT Fusion Reaction and Alpha Particle Self-Heating
The deuterium-tritium (DT) fusion reaction is the primary energy source in ICF experiments:

                                D + T → n(14.1 MeV) + α(3.5 MeV)

 This reaction releases 17.6 MeV per event: the neutron carries 14.1 MeV while the alpha particle
(4 He2+ ) carries 3.5 MeV. These products behave fundamentally differently due to their charge
states. Neutrons, being electrically neutral, escape the capsule unimpeded and deposit their energy
externally. In contrast, charged alpha particles interact strongly with the plasma via Coulomb
collisions, slowing down and depositing their 3.5 MeV locally. This "alpha heating" is essential for
achieving ignition.
 The spatial distribution of alpha energy deposition depends critically on material composition.
In pure DT fuel at ICF conditions, 3.5 MeV alpha particles have ranges of tens of micrometers,
allowing distributed heating favorable for burn propagation. However, when high-Z materials like
tungsten (Z = 74) mix into DT fuel (Zeff ≈ 1.5), the stopping power increases dramatically and
alpha particles deposit energy in tungsten-contaminated regions rather than in pure DT, which
severely degrades capsule performance. Instead of uniform fuel heating to propagate the burn wave,
alpha energy becomes localized in tungsten-rich chunks, depositing energy in the high-Z material
rather than the surrounding plasma. This high-Z contamination prevents heating of the fuel and
terminates the fusion chain reaction, preventing ignition.
 Accurately simulating alpha transport through mixed-material regions is therefore crucial for
predicting capsule performance and developing mixing-resistant designs. This requires charged
particle transport models that dynamically track which material each particle occupies as it traverses
mixed zones. To address this challenge, we must first understand how the simulation code represents
mixed materials and transports particles through them. Crucially, the physics governing these
interactions is subzonal. The spatial scales of material mixing and local alpha deposition are far too
small to be directly resolved by the simulation mesh as one would with large-scale fluid structures.
Consequently, the model must rely on physics approximations to accurately capture these subzonal
behaviors without demanding prohibitive computational resolution.

2.2 Code Structure and Multi-Material Representation
The utilized LANL shock-hydrodynamics code employs a Lagrangian finite-element method where
the computational mesh moves with the material. In multi-material simulations, the code uses a
volume-of-fluid approach where each computational zone can contain multiple material phases. The
term "phase" here refers to one of the mixed contituents of diffuse mixture, and it does not refer
to the material’s state of matter (e.g. solid/liquid/gas). The code maintained a hierarchical data
structure containing zones, phases, sides, materials, and much more. The code tracks phase-specific
quantities alongside zone-averaged quantities. In double-shell ICF capsules during compression,

2.3 Original Charged Particle Transport
The Monte Carlo charged particle transport module tracks computational particles, each represent-
ing many physical particles, characterized by position, velocity, energy, mass, charge, and statistical
significance. Particles are transported through global timesteps via subcycling, where each parti-
cle advances through multiple substeps. The original raytracing algorithm computed competing
timescales each substep. The minimum timestep determined the substep length. The particle ad-
vanced, depositing energy based on zone-averaged material properties (e.g., density, temperature,
composition), with no awareness of which specific material phase it occupied in mixed zones.

2.5 The Coupling Challenge
To enable coupled mixed-material hydrodynamics and charged particle transport, four fundamental
capabilities were needed. Each particle must know which material it currently occupies. Particles
must detect when they cross material boundaries in mixed zones. A mechanism is needed to deter-
mine which material owns a particle’s current location. Finally, material-dependent physics such
as stopping power and cross-sections must update when materials change. These capabilities re-
quired modifications across multiple code systems: the particle data structure, raytracing algorithm,
material database, and source initialization.

3 Results
We successfully implemented coupled material mixing and charged particle transport by developing
four interconnected components integrated across the code’s particle transport, material database,
and source modules. The implementation enables particles to track their local material environment
and dynamically switch materials when crossing interfaces in mixed regions.

3.1 Material Tracking Array
We needed to assign each computational particle a material handle identifying which material it
currently occupies. We added a new integer array to the particle data structure. Each element
stores a material handle, which is an integer identifier pointing to a specific material object in the
code’s hierarchical database.
 To implement this, we added the new integer array to the material particle class variable list,
registering it in the code’s database system so it participates in all particle lifecycle operations (e.g.,
allocation, advection, parallel communication, deletion). The array is allocated and initialized for all
charged particle simulations. At particle birth, the integer array is set by querying which material
owns the particle’s initial side index. During transport, when particles cross material boundaries,
the integer array updates to the new material’s handle. Furthermore, the array is communicated
during parallel domain decomposition. Specifically, when particles move between processors, their
material handles transfer with them. This array, ultimately, provides the fundamental link between
the particle transport system and the material database, enabling particles to query material-specific
properties (e.g., density, temperature, stopping power) at any point during transport.

3.2 Stochastic Interface Sampling
We now needed to figure out how to calculate the time until each particle encounters a material
interface in mixed regions. We created a new module containing a subroutine that implements the
CLS algorithm [2]. This routine is called every substep for every particle to determine if the particle
will hit a material interface before it hits a mesh boundary or undergoes a reaction.

3.2.1 BHR3 Turbulence Coupling

To obtain physically realistic mixing length scales, we coupled the CLS algorithm with the BHR3
turbulence model. The implementation uses a broadcast query architecture. During particle trans-
port, the subroutine queries whether BHR3 is active for the particle’s material. If BHR3 responds,
a second query retrieves the turbulent length scale for the particle’s current zone. A responder
searches for the hydrodynamic phase associated with the particle’s zone and returns a turbulent
length. Physical bounds prevent numerical instabilities from extreme values, and a default mixing
length of 1 micron is used if BHR3 is not active.
 This coupling enables the chord length to vary spatially and temporally with the evolving tur-
bulence field, capturing the growth of Rayleigh-Taylor and Richtmyer-Meshkov instabilities from
initial perturbations through fully-developed turbulence.

3.2.2 Timestep Calculation

The routine processes each particle individually. For a given particle, it first calculates the particle’s
current speed by taking the magnitude of its three-dimensional velocity vector. If the particle is
essentially stationary or if mixing is disabled for the simulation, the routine returns an effectively
infinite time, meaning this particle will never be limited by material crossing during this substep.
 For moving particles in mixed regions, the algorithm samples a random distance to the next
material interface using the exponential distribution formula. It generates a uniform random number
between 0 and 1, takes its natural logarithm, multiplies by the negative average chord length, and
this gives a statistically sampled distance. Once the sampled distance is obtained, the routine
converts it to a time by dividing by the particle’s speed. This "mixing timestep" is then returned
for use in the main raytracing algorithm’s timestep calculation. The algorithm also ensures the
random number never equals exactly zero (which would cause logarithm singularities) by clamping
to a small positive value.
 Ultimately, this stochastic sampling captures the statistical nature of material mixing without
requiring explicit geometric reconstruction of subzonal interfaces. Each particle experiences a dif-
ferent sampled distance from the exponential distribution in Equation 1, and over many particle
and many timesteps, the ensemble behavior correctly represents transport through a mixed medium
with characteristic length scales given by the BHR3 turbulent field. The chord length should evolve
dynamically with the hydrodynamic simulation, providing physics-based mixing statistics consistent
with the underlying turbulent model.

3.3 Material Ownership Query
With the implementations, we required a way to enable particles to query which material owns
their current location when they cross material boundaries. We developed a broadcast routine that
allows a particle to ask "which material owns this particular mesh side?" The routine accepts a
side index and returns the material handle of the material that owns that location. It is important
to note that when referring to a "side", a "side" is a mesh entity that represents a simplex of the
decomposition of a polytope. Particle objects track their position on sides because it is easy to
implement a tracking algorithm on sides.
 When a particle needs to determine its material identity, the code broadcasts the particle’s side
index to all material objects in the simulation. Each material maintains an internal list mapping
its zones to the mesh sides they occupy. When a material receives the broadcast query, it loops
through its ownership list checking if any of its zones contains the requested side.
 If the material finds the side in its ownership list, it responds by returning its own material
handle. If the side doesn’t belong to that material, it remains silent. Eventually, one material will
respond with a valid handle, and this is the material that owns the particle’s current location. The
particle then stores this handle in its material tracking array entry.
 This broadcast-response mechanism was chosen over pre-computing global material ownership
maps because it’s more memory-efficient and naturally handles dynamic mesh refinement or material
creation during the simulation. Rather than maintaining a large array mapping every side in the
simulation to its owning material, we compute ownership on-demand only when particles actually
cross boundaries. For typical ICF simulations with millions of zones but only thousands of particles,
this on-demand approach is computationally efficient. Additionally, if no material responds to the
ownership query, which shouldn’t happen in properly configured problems, the routine detects this

3.4 Source Material Assignment
To ensure newly created particles are correctly assigned to their birth material, we properly initial-
ized the material tracking array for source particles. We established a new file containing routines
that initialize material tracking for particles generated by various source models.
 When the code generates new particles from a source, whether it’s a user saying "put 1000 alpha
particles at this radius with this energy" or a thermonuclear burn calculation determining that a
fusion reaction just occurred at a specific location, the particles are first created with their basic
properties. But, their material handles are initially undefined. The source material assignment
routine processes these newly created particles before they begin transport. For each new particle,
the routine looks up the particle’s initial side index, then broadcasts this side to all materials using
the same broadcast routine described in the previous subsection. The material that owns that side
responds with its handle, and this handle is stored in the particle’s material tracking array entry.

3.5 Integrated Raytracing with Material Switching
With all the material tracking software development, the last thing to do was integrate material
tracking into the primary particle transport loop, enabling dynamic material switching during trans-
port. We modified the core raytracing routine to include material switching logic throughout the
particle substep calculation. This is where all the previous components come together to create
fully coupled mixed-material transport.
 The raytracing loop processes each particle through global timesteps via subcycling, which is how
each particle takes many small substeps to traverse its assigned distance for the current timestep.
For each substep, the algorithm must determine how long that substep should be computing several
competing physical timescales and choosing the shortest one. The original code computed four com-
peting timescales. We added a fifth competing timescale by calling the stochastic interface sampling
routine. This mixing timestep asks "how long until this particle crosses a material interface?" using
the CLS algorithm.
 After computing all five timescales, the algorithm selects the minimum. This determines what
physical process limits the substep. The particle then advances. Its position updates based on its
velocity and the substep time. Its energy decreases based on the stopping power and the substep
time. Finally, its velocity updates to reflect the energy loss.
 After advancing the particle, the algorithm checks which timescale was limiting. We added
checks if the mixing timestep was smallest. This means the particle has crossed a material in-
terface before any other event occurred. When this happens, the algorithm sets a flag indicating
a material switch is occurring, then broadcasts the particle’s current side index to all materials
using the broadcast routine we developed to query which material owns a particular mesh side.
When the material ownership query returns, the algorithm compares the returned material handle
to the particle’s current material tracking array value. If they’re different, a material switch has
occurred. The algorithm updates the particle’s material tracking array to the new material handle.
When a material switch occurs, several material-dependent physics quantities must be recalculated
immediately, before the next substep begins.

 Furthermore, we found that during development the material tracking array could remain unini-

3.6 ICF Toy Model Development
We developed 1D and 2D toy models of the double-shell ICF capsules for future validation and
testing of our implementation. The 1D results are omitted in this report for brevity, however. The
test problems consisted of simplified multi-layer capsule geometries designed to capture the essential
physics of double-shell ICF capsules.

3.6.1 Without Coupled Material Mixing and Transport Implementation
To investigate the impact of material mixing on ICF capsule performance, we employed a La-
grangian shock-hydrodynamics code. The simulations were conducted in two-dimensions (2D) with
axisymmetric geometry to capture the transient hydrodynamic behavior and establish baseline per-
formance characteristics before enabling material mixing mechanisms. The total simulation time
extends to 10 ns with adaptive timestepping starting from an initial timestep 10 ps.
 We developed a simplified toy model of a double-shell ICF capsule with the following layer
structure from inside to outside: DT fuel → Tungsten → Glow Discharge Polymer (GDP)
→ Foam → Aluminum. The detailed geometric and material properties of each layer are sum-
marized in Table 3-1. The configuration consists of central DT fuel core (50:50 deuterium-tritium
atomic fraction, mf uel = 180 ng) surrounded by a tungsten pusher, GDP tamper, low-density foam
cushion, and outer aluminum ablator shell. In this configuration, the beryllium layer used in [2] was
eliminated to focus specifically on tungsten-DT fuel mixing in a more simplified manner. This con-
figuration represents a typical double-shell target design where tungsten serves as a high-Z pusher
providing inertial confinement, GDP acts as a tamper layer, foam provides a cushion for shock
smoothing, and aluminum forms the outer ablator shell.

 To enable hydrodynamic mixing between high-Z pusher and low-Z fuel, we implemented a spe-
cialized "mixed gas" material region that spatially overlays both the DT fuel core (r = 0 to
214 µm) and the tungsten pusher layer (r = 214 µm to 262 µm). This mixed gas region is con-
figured as a two-phase material system in the code’s material database, containing: (1) a DT gas
phase with properties inherited from the fuel material definition, and (2) a tungsten phase with
properties inherited from the pusher material definition. In the baseline simulation presented here,
although the mixed gas infrastructure is fully defined in the input deck (noregion = 1), it occupies
zero physical volume and remains inactive, these two phases exist as distinct materials in the sim-
ulation. This configuration establishes the necessary computational framework for the multiphase
BHR3 mixing model while providing an unmixed reference case. The physical expectation is that
under extreme compression, strong density gradients, and hydrodynamic instabilities (particularly
Rayleigh-Taylor instabilities at the pusher-fuel interface), these initially distinct material layers
(DT and tungsten pusher) would undergo turbulent interpenetration and atomic-scale diffusion.
When mixing is enabled in subsequent simulations, the BHR3 model will activate at t = 1 ns and
dynamically compute volume fractions of each phase throughout the mixed gas region. This two-

Critically, a mixed gas region was defined, overlapping spatially with the pusher layer to enable

 The mixed gas material represents the core innovation enabling our coupled mixing-transport
capability. Defined as a mixed gas, it initially occupies zero mesh volume but activates wherever
the BHR3 turbulent mixing model generates non-trivial volume fractions of multiple phases. More
specifically, when the mixed gas activates, the two DT and tungsten materials are both deleted
from the simulation and replaced by a single mixed gas material that models them and allows them
to diffuse into each other. Additionally, it uses a coupled pressure-temperature equilibrium model,
enforcing mechanical and thermal equilibrium between phases within each mixed zone while allowing
spatial variations in composition. The model activates at a start time of 1 nanosecond, shortly
before peak compression and remains active until a stop time of 100 seconds. Within the mixed
gas, two distinct phases coexist: a gas-phase fuel component and a solid-phase pusher component.
The fuel phase inherits the SESAME EOS 5267, 50-50 DT isotopic composition, non-equilibrium
temperatures, grey radiation, and fusion reaction capabilities from the pure fuel material definition.
The pusher phase inherits the gamma-law EOS and W-184 composition as before. Both phases
are initialized with placeholder density and temperature values that are overridden once the BHR3
model computes spatially-varying volume fractions throughout the mixed region. The important
physics linkage occurs when fusion-born alpha particles are created in the mixed gas material via
the SINGE module. These particles carry the material tracking array handles pointing to the mixed
gas material, and our CLS routine uses a placeholder chord length, stochastically switching particles
between fuel-phase and pusher-phase properties at exponentially distributed intervals along their
trajectories.
 The GDP tamper and CH foam cushion both use the SESAME EOS 7834 with SESAME opacity

hydrogen-1 by atom fraction, but have vastly different densities. The tamper’s density represents
solid polymer while the foam’s density is a highly porous open-cell structure to differentiate the two
regions. The aluminum ablator employs SESAME EOS 3720 and SESAME opacity 13719, with
pure Al-27 composition and solid density. Its initialization is unique, where instead of specifying
temperature, the ablator is initialized with specific internal energy computed as the the drive divided
by the mass. The drive in this case is 1.25 MW, representing the peak laser power. This energy is
deposited according to a time-dependent energy rate table that ramps linearly from zero at time =

it immediately drops to zero, creating a triangular pulse that delivers approximately 2.5 MJ total

implosion. The global radiation transport system uses grey diffusion with flux limiter multiplier set

4 Conclusion and Future Work
This work represents a significant advancement in multiphysics ICF simulation capability by suc-
cessfully coupling charged particle transport with material mixing. We developed and validated a
stochastic interface sampling algorithm based on the Levermore-Pomraning Chord Length Sampling
model, implementing universal material tracking, dynamic material switching logic, and integration
with existing physics calculations. The software can now be used to explore and understand the
physical phenomena occurring during material mixing between the fuel and high-Z shell materials.
Furthermore, we developed working toy models based on double-shell ICF capsule designs, which
lay the foundation for future validation and simulations of ICF experiments.

4.1 Near-Term Development Priorities
While the broadcast query architecture between the CLS subroutine and BHR3 codes is imple-
mented, we are currently encountering MPI communication errors when running simulations with
BHR3 active. Resolving these parallel computing issues is a high priority, as they prevent validation
of whether the turbulent length scale field correctly propagates to particle transport calculations.
Once stable, we must verify that the chord length varies spatially across zones as expected, the tur-
bulent lengths evolve during instability growth, and the coupling remains numerically stable across
the full parameter space of the double shell implosions.
 Additionally, our 2D axisymmetric toy model provided essential validation but will most likely
not capture all observed results in experiments. Extending the capability to 3D meshes requires
verifying that material handles and chord length sampling remain consistent across zones, meshes,
and boundaries. We should target near-term achievable 3D simulations first using simplified geome-
tries, progressively increasing geometric fidelity and resolution as we gain confidence in scalability
and physics accuracy.
 LANL and LLNL have also accumulated extensive experimental data on capsules where mixing
is suspected but not definitively measured. We should identify benchmark shots where diagnostic
data is sufficiently rich to constrain simulation inputs and enable quantitative comparison. Running
our coupled mix-transport capability on these shot geometries with realistic drive histories will test
whether predicted degraded mechanisms, such as reduced fuel heating and cooled hotspot, match
observed signatures. Discrepancies will guide model refinement (e.g., if simulations under-predict
mix losses), potentially resulting in the addition of physics like species separation or shock-driven
atomic mixing.

4.2 Long-Term Vision and Impact
Understanding mixing is only valuable if it informs design changes that preserve ignition conditions.
We should conduct systematic parameter studies exploring: (1) optimized layer thickness ratios to
minimize Rayleigh-Taylor growth rates while maintaining sufficient convergence; (2) engineered
density gradients; (3) modified drive pulse shapes for enhanced stability; and (4) alternative pusher
materials that reduce mix-induced transport losses even if some mixing occurs. Each strategy
require simulation runs spanning parameter space to map performance surfaces. This would be
computationally expensive but feasible with current resources.
 We could also look into coupled atomic physics in mixed regions. Our current implementation
assumes instantaneous atomic equilibrium after material switches. When a particle enters tung-

sten, we immediately compute ionization states and electron density. In reality, atomic relaxation
timescales may be comparable to or longer than mixing timescales in rapidly evolving interfaces.
This can lead to transient non-equilibrium stopping power where tungsten atoms in DT fuel may
remain partially ionized for finite time, or vice versa. Implementing non-equilibrium atomic ki-
netics coupled to material switching may refine stopping power predictions, particularly in high-Z
materials where ionization state strongly affects stopping power. This likely requires coupling to ex-
isting LANL atomic physics capabilities through database exchange or from developing new atomic
solvers.
 Overall, LANL’s double shell program represents one of several alternative pathways to ignition
beyond NIF’s baseline design. But it’s one of many concepts, and it’s possible that applying our
coupled mixing-transport capability across the diverse concepts to identify which designs are most
robust to mixing-induced degradation. This would potentially redirect programmatic focus toward
inherently stable configurations. For now, our coupled mixing-transport capability establishes the
foundational work for a new generation of ICF simulations that accurately predict the complex,
multi-scale interplay between hydrodynamic instabilities, material mixing, and charged particle
transport. Ultimately, we have enabled more confident performance predictions and more effective
experimental campaign planning as LANL pursues alternative pathways to achieving robust, high-
yield fusion ignition that can transition to practical fusion energy applications.
