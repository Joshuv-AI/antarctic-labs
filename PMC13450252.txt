Molecular epidemiology of Enterocytozoon bieneusi and Blastocystis in cats and dogs in Dhaka and Gazipur, Bangladesh
Authors: Anas Bin Harun, Abdullah Al Bayazid, Md Farhan Hasan, Ainun Nahar, Maksuda Akter Mily, Jannatul Bakia Tamanna, Joynti Saha, Md Ashiqur Rahman, SHM Faruk Siddiki, Md Morshedur Rahman, Md Robiul Karim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnterocytozoon bieneusi, a microsporidian fungus, and Blastocystis, a protist, are zoonotic enteric microbes causing gastrointestinal disorders in humans and various animals, including cats and dogs. This study aimed to determine the molecular prevalence, associated risk factors, genetic diversity, and zoonotic potential of these pathogens in feline and canine populations in Dhaka and Gazipur metropolitan areas. Fecal samples were collected from 197 cats and 120 dogs and analyzed using nested PCR targeting the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region of the ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene for E. bieneusi and a conventional PCR targeting the small subunit ribosomal (SSU) rRNA gene for Blastocystis. The overall prevalence of E. bieneusi was 9.1% in cats and 11.7% in dogs. Blastocystis was detected in 3.6% of cats and 10.0% of dogs, with feline infection occurring exclusively in kittens (<1 year). Multivariable analysis identified female sex and young age as the sole significant risk factors for E. bieneusi in dogs and for Blastocystis in cats, respectively. Molecular characterization identified four E. bieneusi genotypes (D, Henan V, BANEB17, and BANEB4) in cats and seven genotypes (PtEb IX, D, EbpC, Type IV, BANEB14, BANEB15, and BANEB16) in dogs. Phylogenetic grouping and sequence comparison confirmed genetic identity between several isolates and genotypes circulating in Bangladeshi children. Among these, four genotypes (BANEB14, BANEB15, BANEB16, and BANEB17) were novel, and the majority exhibited zoonotic potential. Similarly, six Blastocystis subtypes (ST1, ST3, ST4, ST6, ST23, and ST44) were identified in both cats and dogs, with ST1, ST3, and ST4 recognized for their zoonotic potential. These findings suggest a potential role for companion animals in zoonotic transmission, necessitating integrated One Health surveillance strategies.Keywords: Enterocytozoon bieneusi, Blastocystis, Molecular epidemiology, Zoonotic potential, Cats, Dogs

Introduction

In rapidly urbanizing societies, domestic cats and dogs are commonly kept as pets, leading to close human-animal contact that may facilitate the transmission of zoonotic pathogens. Within the One Health framework, which links human, animal, and environmental health (Atlas, 2012), companion animals are significant yet often overlooked reservoirs of zoonotic enteric pathogens. In densely populated cities like Dhaka and Gazipur in Bangladesh, where pets coexist with many free-roaming strays, the risk of cross-species transmission of enteric pathogens is a growing public health concern.

Among the enteric microbes capable of traversing the species barrier, Enterocytozoon bieneusi and Blastocystis have garnered increasing attention due to their ubiquity and genetic diversity. Historically regarded as pathogens of minor significance, advanced molecular tools have redefined our understanding of their taxonomy and epidemiology, revealing them as leading causes of waterborne and foodborne outbreaks (Stentiford et al., 2016; Rehena et al., 2025a).

E. bieneusi is an obligate intracellular pathogen belonging to the phylum Microsporidia, now classified within the kingdom Fungi rather than among parasitic protozoa (Keeling, 2009), and is the leading cause of human microsporidiosis worldwide. It causes chronic diarrhea and wasting syndrome in immunocompromised people, like those with AIDS or organ transplants (Matos et al., 2012), and is increasingly recognized as a cause of self-limiting diarrhea in healthy individuals (Li et al., 2019a). Transmission relies on resilient spores that contaminate water and food. E. bieneusi shows high genetic diversity, with over 800 genotypes based on the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region of the ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene, grouped into 13–15 phylogenetic groups. Group 1 is particularly concerning, as it contains zoonotic genotypes like D and Type IV that can infect humans, livestock, and pets (Wang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024).

Blastocystis is among the most common eukaryotic parasites in the human gut, infecting over one billion people globally (Stensvold and Clark, 2016). While its pathogenicity remains debated, with carriage ranging from asymptomatic colonization to severe irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), its zoonotic potential is indisputable. Blastocystis is genetically characterized into distinct subtypes (STs) based on the small subunit (SSU) rRNA gene, with 44 valid subtypes characterized across a broad spectrum of host species (Šejnohová et al., 2024; Cui et al., 2025). Of the 44 recognized subtypes, 16 have been reported in humans and the rest in non-human animal hosts, with several subtypes shared across both (Alfellani et al., 2013; Cui et al., 2025).

The spread and environmental persistence of infectious spores and cysts are facilitated by the high humidity and monsoon rainfall characteristic of subtropical Bangladesh (Rostami et al., 2020; Fattah et al., 2023). Rapid, unplanned urbanization in cities like Dhaka and Gazipur has led to densely populated areas and poor waste management, resulting in ongoing environmental contamination from fecal matter. Recent molecular studies reveal a high prevalence of zoonotic E. bieneusi and Blastocystis in zoo animals, livestock, and children (Karim et al., 2020, Karim et al., 2022, Karim et al., 2024; Rehena et al., 2025b). However, there exists a data gap concerning cats and dogs, despite their potential role as bridging hosts between environmental reservoirs and humans. Existing data on the molecular epidemiology of E. bieneusi and Blastocystis in these animals are nearly absent, and microscopy alone is inadequate for accurate identification.

Therefore, this study was designed to address this epidemiological blind spot by determining the prevalence, risk factors, and genetic diversity of E. bieneusi and Blastocystis in cat and dog populations in Dhaka and Gazipur. By analyzing genotypes and subtypes for their relatedness to zoonotic isolates, this research highlights the need for One Health strategies to control enteric infections in Bangladesh.

Materials and methods

Sampling

A cross-sectional copro-molecular study was conducted in the metropolitan areas of Dhaka and Gazipur, Bangladesh, between July 2023 and August 2025, to investigate the presence of E. bieneusi and Blastocystis in cats and dogs. The minimum sample size was calculated using the single-proportion formula, n = Z2P(1 − P)/d2, with a 95% confidence level and 5% precision (Pourhoseingholi et al., 2013). Assuming expected prevalences of 13% in cats and 7% in dogs, based on previously reported rates elsewhere (Xu et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2019a), the required minimum sample sizes were 174 cats and 100 dogs. To increase statistical power and account for potential exclusions, 317 animals were finally enrolled, comprising 197 cats and 120 dogs, all aged ≥6 months.

Most fecal samples were collected from household cats and dogs presented at veterinary clinics, with additional samples from stray and shelter animals. Specifically, the study sampled 153 clinic-presented cats, 28 household cats, and 16 shelter cats, as well as 44 clinic-presented dogs, 30 household dogs, and 46 shelter dogs from 12 veterinary clinics, 47 households, and 4 shelters across the two cities (Fig. 1). Animals were sampled regardless of the reason for their veterinary visit, encompassing both routine health checks and illness-related consultations. For each animal, data on age, sex, breed, cohabitation, vaccination, deworming, outdoor access, diet, water source, and recent gastrointestinal (GI) signs were recorded using a structured questionnaire. Fresh fecal samples were aseptically collected, then homogenized, filtered, centrifuged, aliquoted, and preserved in 2.50% potassium dichromate at 4 °C to inhibit microbial overgrowth and maintain the structural integrity of oocysts and spores during transport and storage. Sample processing and molecular analysis were conducted at the Zoonosis Research Laboratory, Department of Medicine, Gazipur Agricultural University (GAU), Bangladesh.

Geographic distribution and collection sources of sampled cats (n = 197) and dogs (n = 120) for this study.

DNA extraction and PCR amplification

Genomic DNA was extracted from 317 fecal samples using the FavorPrep™ Stool DNA Isolation Kit (Favorgen Biotech Corp., Taiwan) following the manufacturer's protocol. Prior to DNA extraction, the preservative (potassium dichromate) was removed by repeated washing with sterile distilled water to minimize its inhibitory effect on downstream PCR amplification. Eluted DNA was stored at −20 °C for subsequent PCR amplification.

Detection of E. bieneusi was performed using nested PCR targeting the ITS region of the rRNA gene. The primary reaction used primers EBITS3/EBITS4 and the secondary reaction used primers EBITS1/EBITS2.4 (Karim et al., 2022), both in 25 μL volumes using TaKaRa Taq™ DNA Polymerase (Toyobo, Japan). Thermal cycling conditions for the primary reaction were: 94 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 94 °C for 45 s, 57 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min; and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The secondary reaction used identical conditions except for a reduced annealing temperature of 55 °C, with 2 μL of primary PCR product as template, yielding a 392-bp amplicon. Blastocystis was detected using conventional PCR targeting a 600-bp fragment of the SSU rRNA gene with primers RD5/BhRDr (Li et al., 2019b) and reagent 2× Rapid Taq Master Mix (Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., China), with cycling conditions of: 94 °C for 7 min; 35 cycles of 94 °C for 45 s, 55 °C for 45 s, and 72 °C for 1 min; and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. All reactions were performed on an Applied Biosystems™ Thermal Cycler (Version 2.09). To ensure reproducibility, all samples were tested in triplicate, with a positive control (pig fecal DNA confirmed positive for E. bieneusi/Blastocystis) and a negative control (nuclease-free water) included in each run.

Nucleotide sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

All PCR-positive amplicons were purified and subjected to bidirectional Sanger sequencing (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai). Chromatograms were inspected for quality and double peaks using Unipro UGENE v51.0, after which consensus sequences were assembled to resolve potential heterozygosity. Taxonomic classification was achieved by querying the obtained sequences against the GenBank nucleotide database via BLASTn (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/blast). In line with the convention that each distinct 243-bp fragment of the ITS sequence of E. bieneusi is considered a separate genotype (Santin and Fayer, 2009; Nourrisson et al., 2024), an isolate was regarded as representing a novel genotype when its ITS sequence differed by at least one nucleotide (SNPs and/or indels) from all published genotypes. Blastocystis subtypes were additionally assigned using the PubMLST database (https://pubmlst.org/organisms/blastocystis-spp).

Phylogenetic reconstruction was performed in a Linux environment. Sequences were aligned with global reference sequences using MUSCLE v5.3, and ambiguous terminal regions were trimmed using trimAl v1.5. Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic trees were inferred with IQ-TREE v2.4.0 and visualized using iTOL. The E. bieneusi phylogenetic tree was rooted with the genotype CSK2 (GenBank accession no. KY706128), whereas the Blastocystis tree was rooted using Karotomorpha sp. (GenBank accession no. DQ431242). The best-fit nucleotide substitution model for each dataset was automatically selected using ModelFinder, and node support was assessed with 1000 bootstrap replicates. Additionally, median-joining haplotype networks were constructed in PopART v4.8.4 to visualize genealogical relationships and mutational distances among the identified isolates.

Statistical analysis

Epidemiological data were digitized in Microsoft Excel and imported into RStudio v2026.05.0 for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics summarized the demographic features of the study population. Univariable associations between each predictor and infection status were assessed using the chi-square test, or Fisher's exact test where one or more expected cell counts were less than 5. All predictors were entered simultaneously into the multivariable model. Multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using Cramér's V for all pairwise combinations; for cats, all pairs showed V < 0.4 (Table S1), indicating acceptable collinearity, while for dogs, cohabitation and water source showed moderate collinearity (Cramér's V = 0.877). However, both were retained as neither reached significance and the primary finding (female sex) was unaffected. Given the sparse positive counts across subgroups and quasi-complete separation in certain predictor categories, Firth penalized-likelihood logistic regression was used in place of standard maximum-likelihood logistic regression for all multivariable models, implemented via the logistf package in R (Heinze and Schemper, 2002). Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) based on profile likelihood, with reference categories defined as the group with the lowest infection prevalence for each variable. All reported p-values are unadjusted for multiple comparisons; given the exploratory nature of the risk factor analyses, findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. A significance threshold of p ≤ 0.05 was applied throughout.

Results

Prevalence of E. bieneusi and Blastocystis in cats and dogs

Primary screening by nested PCR amplification of the ITS region successfully yielded a 392-bp amplicon for E. bieneusi in 18 cats (9.1%). Similarly, PCR amplification of the SSU rRNA gene generated approximately 600-bp amplicons, confirming the presence of Blastocystis in 7 cats (3.6%). Risk factor analysis of E. bieneusi and Blastocystis infections in cats

Geographical variation in E. bieneusi prevalence was not statistically significant (p = 0.511), with prevalence rates of 12.1% and 8.5% observed in Gazipur and Dhaka, respectively (Table 1). Higher infection rates were observed in female cats (12.9%) and those aged 1–2 years (12.2%). However, neither sex nor age emerged as a significant predictor in the multivariable analysis. Cats sourced from friends and family and congregate management groups like shelter/breeder settings showed a non-significant trend toward higher odds compared to strays (OR: 4.82, 95% CI: 0.78–59.53, p = 0.096; and OR: 5.85, 95% CI: 0.90–73.57, p = 0.065, respectively). Variables including breed, deworming history, outdoor access, and diet showed no significant associations. Exploratory ROC and Random Forest analyses are presented in Fig. S1.

In contrast to E. bieneusi, Blastocystis infection showed a significant univariable association with age (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.016). It was confined to kittens under 1 year, with a prevalence of 6.6%; no cases appeared in older groups (Table 2). This association was also confirmed in multivariable analysis (OR: 11.45; 95% CI: 1.30–1419.19; p = 0.024). No other demographic or management factors, including sex, breed, location, or water source, were significantly linked to Blastocystis positivity.

Risk factor analysis of E. bieneusi and Blastocystis infections in dogs

Univariable analysis identified sex as a significant risk factor (χ2 = 8.88, p = 0.003) for E. bieneusi infection, with a markedly higher prevalence in females (22.0%) than in males (4.3%). This finding was confirmed by multivariable logistic regression (OR: 4.38; 95% CI: 1.28–18.32; p = 0.018). Although puppies (<1 year) and dogs from Gazipur City showed higher descriptive prevalence rates, these variables were not significant predictors in the multivariable model. Similarly, factors such as breed, deworming history, and water source showed no significant associations. Exploratory ROC and Random Forest analyses are presented in Fig. S2.

Interestingly, univariable analysis initially associated dogs' deworming history with Blastocystis infection (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.050); however, this counterintuitive association was not maintained in the multivariable model (p = 0.520), suggesting potential confounding by other variables. No other demographic or husbandry factors, including age, location, and water source, emerged as significant predictors, despite a higher prevalence in puppies.

Genotyping and phylogenetic analysis of E. bieneusi isolates

Nucleotide sequencing of the ITS region was performed after successful amplification of all E. bieneusi-positive samples. Sequence analysis revealed significant genetic diversity, especially in the canine population. In cats, four distinct genotypes were identified. The zoonotic genotype D was predominant, accounting for 83.3% of sequenced isolates, and was found across various ages and breeds. The remaining isolates included single occurrences (5.6% each) of the genotypes Henan V, BANEB4, and the newly identified BANEB17. BANEB17 differed from genotype D by a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) (Table S2).

Canine isolates showed even greater diversity, with seven genotypes identified among 14 samples. PtEb IX was the most common (35.7%), followed by zoonotic genotypes D (21.4%) and EbpC (14.3%). Additionally, four genotypes were detected (7.1% each): the known zoonotic genotype Type IV and three novel genotypes, BANEB14, BANEB15, and BANEB16. BANEB14, BANEB15, and BANEB16 differed by one SNP each from PtEb IX, EbpA, and D, respectively (Table S2).

Maximum likelihood phylogenetic analysis of the E. bieneusi ITS region was conducted to elucidate evolutionary relationships and zoonotic potential. The topology revealed that eight identified genotypes (D, Henan V, EbpC, Type IV, BANEB4, BANEB15, BANEB16, and BANEB17) clustered within zoonotic Group 1, while PtEb IX and BANEB14 clustered within dog-adapted Group 11 (Fig. 2). The minimal branch lengths among Group 1 isolates suggest a recent common ancestor with other zoonotic isolates reported worldwide. The novel genotype BANEB17 appears as a sister lineage to the main genotype D cluster. BANEB4, found in cats, and BANEB16, found in dogs, belong to the same cluster and are closely related to the Henan V isolate identified in humans. Meanwhile, BANEB14 and BANEB15 show evolutionary divergence from the PtEb IX and O lineage, respectively.

Subtyping and phylogenetic analysis of Blastocystis isolates

Blastocystis subtyping via SSU rRNA gene sequencing in the feline population identified three subtypes (STs), with ST1 being predominant (57.1%). ST4 was detected in 28.5% of cases, while ST3 occurred in a single isolate (14.3%). The canine population exhibited greater diversity, with six subtypes characterized among 12 isolates. ST1 was the most frequent (33.3%), followed by ST4 (25.0%) and ST6 (16.7%). Single isolates (8.3% each) were identified for ST3, ST23, and ST44.

Pairwise sequence comparison confirmed the genetic distinctness of these isolates. Intra-subtype nucleotide similarity ranged from 91% to 100% (e.g., ST1 isolates PV948180 and PX106826), whereas inter-subtype similarity was markedly lower, such as the 71% identity observed between ST4 (PV948179) and ST1 (PX106826). To resolve this intra-subtype diversity, subtype-level and allele-wise trees were constructed. The topology exhibited deep evolutionary divergence, with well-supported monophyletic clades corresponding to ST1, ST3, ST4, ST6, ST23, and ST44 (Fig. 3). ST1 isolates from both cats and dogs clustered with reference sequences from humans, indicating a close relationship with well-characterized zoonotic isolates. Allele-specific topology analysis revealed that allele 42 was dominant, with branch lengths close to those of allele 122 and allele 107 (Fig. 4). Conversely, alleles 4, 34, 36, and 106 were distant from Allele 42.

Haplotype analysis of E. bieneusi and Blastocystis isolates

The haplotype network for E. bieneusi illustrates the genealogical relationships and fine-scale mutational pathways among the identified sequences (Fig. 5A). The topology is dominated by a central, high-frequency node representing genotype D, suggesting it serves as the ancestral or founding haplotype within this population. Radiating from this hub are single-step branches leading to derived variants; notably, the novel variant BANEB17 is linked to the genotype D node by a single mutational step, reflecting the specific SNP distinguishing it from the ancestral form. A secondary cluster formed around genotype PtEb IX. The evolutionary pathway connecting the novel variant BANEB15 to the main lineage involved a median vector (hypothetical ancestor). The branch leading to BANEB15 is characterized by distinct mutational markers, signifying the specific SNP at position 264 that drove the divergence of this unique canine lineage.

Haplotype network of (A) E. bieneusi and (B) Blastocystis isolates. Black, smaller circles represent median vectors, indicating hypothetical, unsampled intermediate haplotypes required to connect the observed genotypes parsimoniously. Stripes on the connecting lines denote the number of SNPs between haplotypes.

In contrast, the Blastocystis network (Fig. 5B) exhibits a fragmented topology with unconnected subnetworks for each subtype, confirming deep genetic divergence. The ST1 subnetwork features a star-like configuration with a central, abundant haplotype shared by both cats and dogs. Minor variants radiate from this hub, separated by branches indicating 1–2 point mutations, further suggesting that these variants evolved directly from the dominant zoonotic subtype.

Discussion

The detection of E. bieneusi and Blastocystis in cats and dogs at rates comparable to those in local children suggests these microbes are well-established within the domestic transmission cycles, not just in sylvatic or livestock reservoirs (Karim et al., 2020, Karim et al., 2022, Karim et al., 2024). The feline prevalence of E. bieneusi (9.1%) exceeds the 0.0–4.5% reported in Japan and Thailand (Santín and Fayer, 2011; Mori et al., 2013). Its prevalence aligns with urban areas in China (6.0% to 13.0%) (Xu et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2019a; Li et al., 2020). This elevated prevalence likely results from the tropical monsoon climate, which helps spores persist, and dense urban animal populations (Rostami et al., 2020; Fattah et al., 2023).

Feline Blastocystis prevalence (3.5%) is lower than in Bangladeshi calves (14.7%) but mirrors the 5.4% found in local children (Karim et al., 2024), and rates from pet cats in Yunnan, China (2.3%) and Iran (4.0%) (Daryani et al., 2008; Deng et al., 2019). These figures are much lower than the 11.7–19.3% prevalence in stray or shelter cats in China and Pakistan (Zhao et al., 2024). The similarity between feline and pediatric infection rates suggests cats and children may share household exposure routes rather than separate transmission cycles.

Among dogs, the prevalence of E. bieneusi (11.7%) was marginally higher than in cats and consistent with the upper range reported across several Chinese provinces (6.0–15.0%) (Xu et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2019a), yet significantly higher than rates in Japan (0.8–4.4%) and the Czech Republic (1.6%) (Abe et al., 2009). Similarly, Blastocystis prevalence in dogs (10.0%) was congruent with findings from pet populations in China (6.0–13.0%) and Iran (10.0%) (Daryani et al., 2008; Deng et al., 2019), but far below the 70.0% reported in stray dogs in Australia and high-prevalence areas of India (Zhang et al., 2019b). Collectively, these data demonstrate that household cats and dogs in Dhaka and Gazipur serve as intermediate yet epidemiologically significant reservoirs of environmental contamination, reinforcing their role in the persistence of these microbes in urban settings.

The non-significant trend toward higher odds of E. bieneusi infection in cats from shelter/breeder settings compared to strays is directionally consistent with a cattery effect, in which high stocking density, close contact, and stress in breeding facilities promote efficient fecal-oral transmission (Feng et al., 2011). Age patterns showed that infections were more common in young adults and kittens but peaked in young adults rather than in older cats. Likewise, Blastocystis infection was confined to kittens with no positives in older cats, yielding a significant association in both univariable and multivariable analyses. The wide confidence interval for the multivariable estimate reflects the limited number of positive cases and warrants cautious interpretation. This sharp age stratification was also observed in calves and young children in Bangladesh (Karim et al., 2024). The pattern is consistent with immunological immaturity of the gut-associated lymphoid tissue in kittens and progressive development of slow, partial immunity through repeated environmental exposure in multi-cat households and breeding catteries (Stensvold et al., 2009).

Nearly fourfold higher odds of E. bieneusi infection in females contrast with most previous studies that report little or no sex-associated susceptibility to microsporidiosis (Santin and Fayer, 2009). Although reproductive status was not recorded in this study, the elevated risk in females may speculatively reflect periparturient immune relaxation during estrus, pregnancy, and lactation, as well as intense contact with infected puppies through grooming and nursing, thereby increasing oral exposure to spores (Wu and Jin, 2025). Puppies showed a higher prevalence than adult dogs, consistent with the recognized increased susceptibility of young animals to microsporidia due to immune immaturity (Vergneau-Grosset and Larrat, 2015). On the other hand, deworming history shows a counterintuitive univariable association, with dewormed dogs having a higher prevalence, likely reflecting confounding and misclassification rather than a causal effect. Routine deworming usually targets nematodes, not protozoa such as Blastocystis, so owners may deworm dogs with chronic GI symptoms caused by Blastocystis, leading to over-representation of symptomatic, infected animals in the dewormed group. Disruption of the gut microbiota by frequent anthelmintic treatment might also favor opportunistic colonization, consistent with susceptibility associated with dysbiosis (Scanlan et al., 2015). For both microbes in dogs, the exploratory ROC and Random Forest analyses should not be interpreted as confirmatory given the small positive sample sizes across all models. The absence of significant multivariable predictors for most variables likely reflects limited statistical power, and future larger studies should revisit these associations.

Genotyping of the E. bieneusi isolates revealed substantial overlap between animal-derived and human-derived lineages in Bangladesh. The predominance of genotype D and its genetic identity similarity to that previously reported in Bangladeshi children reinforce its status as a major zoonotic lineage (Matos et al., 2012; Karim et al., 2022). The detection of Henan V in a cat extends the known host range of this Group 1 genotype beyond non-human primates. It showcases the capacity of zoonotic E. bieneusi genotypes to cross species barriers in settings where humans and companion animals live in proximity (Li et al., 2019b). In dogs, the co-circulation of genotype D and the traditionally canine-adapted PtEb IX suggested a dual epidemiological role, with dogs maintaining both canid-focused and zoonotic transmission cycles. However, comparatively recent reports of PtEb IX in human patients challenge its strict host specificity and indicate that it may be an emerging zoonotic genotype (Zhang et al., 2019a). The identification of novel Group 1 variants (BANEB17 in cats; BANEB14, BANEB15, BANEB16 in dogs) that differ from known genotypes by only a few single-nucleotide polymorphisms suggests local microevolution driven by host immune pressure or transmission bottlenecks and underscores the need for continued molecular surveillance.

The Blastocystis subtyping data also support a shared transmission cycle between pet animals and humans. Cats harbored ST1, ST3, and ST4, the same trio reported in Bangladeshi children, with ST1 dominant in both hosts (Alfellani et al., 2013; Karim et al., 2024). This close overlap provides molecular evidence suggestive of shared or overlapping domestic transmission routes. ST4, typically associated with rodents, was found in both cats and children, suggesting spillover from peridomestic rodents to pets and humans (Santín and Fayer, 2011). Dogs carried a broader panel of subtypes (ST1, ST3, ST4, ST6, ST23, ST44), combining human-pathogenic lineages with subtypes usually linked to avian or other animal hosts, and thus likely act as mixing vessels for diverse Blastocystis lineages and as potential bridges between sylvatic, avian, and human cycles.

The convergence of E. bieneusi genotypes and Blastocystis subtypes between companion animals and children in Bangladesh, particularly the shared dominance of genotype D and ST1, underscores the potential zoonotic significance of household cats and dogs and supports their integration into One Health surveillance frameworks for enteric disease control. However, interpretation of these findings should consider key limitations. The cross-sectional design and absence of concurrent human or environmental sampling mean that shared genotypes can only indicate zoonotic potential, rather than confirm active transmission or its directionality. Sampling was predominantly clinic-based, which may overrepresent symptomatic animals and slightly inflate prevalence estimates relative to the general pet population. The small sample size of the sheltered animals might limit the statistical power of our multivariable models for this cohort and prevent an accurate comparative analysis with the free-roaming or household population. Finally, sampling was restricted to two urban centers, limiting generalizability to rural and peri-urban settings in Bangladesh. Future work should incorporate proper longitudinal designs, coordinated human-animal-environmental sampling, and broader geographic coverage to better resolve transmission dynamics and the public health significance of companion animal-associated enteric pathogens in Bangladesh.

Conclusions

The findings of this study accentuate a previously underappreciated facet of the epidemiology of enteric fungi and protozoa, justifying the integration of cats and dogs into regional One Health surveillance and mitigation frameworks. The molecular evidence gathered here demonstrates that companion animals in Dhaka and Gazipur harbor genotypes and subtypes of E. bieneusi and Blastocystis that are shared with those circulating in the local human population. The co-dominance of E. bieneusi genotype D and Blastocystis ST1 in household pets and Bangladeshi children suggests shared transmission cycles, though the cross-sectional design and the absence of concurrent human sampling preclude conclusions about the directionality or causality of transmission. The study further identifies critical points of intervention, as the significant association between young age and Blastocystis infection in cats and the trend toward higher E. bieneusi prevalence in managed congregate settings highlight specific windows of vulnerability where targeted hygiene practices could reduce transmission risk.
