ABSTRACT
This report summarizes key findings from a collaborative technical study of residential, non-grid-tied
vehicle-to-home (V2H) backup power systems in Commonwealth Edison’s (ComEd’s) service territory.
The work integrates (1) a feeder-level technoeconomic analysis (TEA) using historical outage-event
data and simulated electric-vehicle (EV) driving/charging profiles to estimate potential reliability and
customer interruption-cost impacts under V2H and vehicle-to-grid (V2G) adoption scenarios; (2)
controlled laboratory performance testing of a representative V2H backup ecosystem to characterize
transfer-to-backup behavior, sustained power delivery, efficiency trends, and repeatable reliability
limitations; and (3) a cybersecurity assessment aligned with NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0 and
ISO/SAE 21434 to evaluate interface-level risk drivers and identify program-relevant mitigations. Results
indicate that V2H can provide measurable resilience value, but outcomes are strongly context dependent
on outage patterns and the share of events that are “V2H-applicable.” Typical transfer-to-backup behavior
clustered on the order of minutes, but rare long-delay edge cases were observed (including an event
approaching 30 minutes) and should be treated as a reliability risk. High-power testing showed that
peak-rated output is not necessarily continuously deliverable; stable operation may require operation
below nameplate ratings and attention to thermal and installation constraints. The cybersecurity
assessment highlights a broad attack surface spanning commissioning, home networks, embedded
services, and cloud/OTA pathways, motivating minimum controls for secure onboarding, signed updates,
patch cadence, and coordinated vulnerability response for any scaled deployment.

Keywords: vehicle-to-home, V2H, backup power, resiliency, technoeconomic analysis, performance
testing, cybersecurity, ComEd

1 INTRODUCTION
Residential vehicle-to-home (V2H) backup power systems can improve outage resiliency by using
an electric vehicle (EV) battery to supply household loads during grid interruptions. While early
commercial offerings for residential backup-oriented bidirectional charging have emerged, most are
original-equipment-manufacturer (OEM) coupled, relatively high-cost, and supported by proprietary
hardware/software stacks that can limit interoperability and create uncertainty around long-term support.
At the same time, utilities and regulators have limited neutral, utility-side evidence describing how these
systems perform under representative residential conditions, what reliability limitations occur in practice,
and what minimum cybersecurity controls should be treated as prerequisites for broader deployment.
 This study was conducted within Commonwealth Edison’s (ComEd’s) Beneficial Electrification
(BE) R&D portfolio to assess the readiness of residential bidirectional solutions for backup-power use
cases in ComEd’s service territory. The work focuses on non-grid-tied, non-exporting residential V2H
configurations (i.e., systems that island the home during an outage and do not export to the distribution
grid). Grid-tied vehicle-to-grid (V2G) configurations are discussed for context, but were not directly
evaluated in laboratory testing.

1.1 Pilot/Research Scale
This project was undertaken to evaluate the feasibility and operating conditions under which residential
non-grid-tied V2H backup power could be accommodated through utility programs. The primary audience
includes ComEd program planning and distribution stakeholders seeking objective assessment of technical
feasibility, system-level constraints, and customer-facing reliability considerations. Secondary audiences
include (i) residential customers evaluating V2H for outage resilience and (ii) OEMs/EVSE/inverter
vendors, standards bodies, and cybersecurity stakeholders, because the work highlights practical interoperability,
reliability, and security requirements that must be addressed before broader deployment.
 The study’s scale is intentionally evidence-focused rather than deployment-focused: it combines (1)
feeder-level reliability/value modeling using historical outage-event records and simulated EV availability,
(2) controlled laboratory evaluation of a representative residential V2H backup ecosystem to characterize
repeatable performance behaviors and edge cases, and (3) a standards-informed cybersecurity assessment
to translate interface-level risks into minimum controls suitable for pilot gating and program design.

2 APPROACH
The Argonne scope under the ComEd BE R&D pilot combined (i) previous pilot and landscape review,
(ii) system-level reliability/value modeling as a technoeconomic analysis, (iii) laboratory performance
testing of a representative residential non-grid exporting V2H system, and (iv) a laboratory cybersecurity
evaluation of the afformentioned V2H system. Collectively, these elements were designed to translate
V2H concepts and findings into program-relevant evidence that can inform pilot design, performance
requirements, and readiness criteria for scaled deployment.

 • Technology and pilots landscape review: a structured review of residential V2H/V2X technologies,
 pilots, and standards to identify recurring deployment barriers, interoperability constraints, and key
 requirements.
 • Technoeconomic analysis (TEA): feeder-level outage-event data were paired with simulated EV
 driving/charging profiles to estimate how V2H and V2G adoption scenarios could change reliability
 outcomes and outage-related customer interruption costs.
 • Laboratory performance testing: controlled test suites characterized transfer-to-backup behavior,
 sustained power delivery and derating behavior, efficiency trends, and repeatable reliability
 limitations observed across operating conditions.
 • Cybersecurity assessment: interface-level risks were evaluated using a standards-based framing
 aligned with NIST CSF 2.0 and ISO/SAE 21434, with emphasis on practical mitigations and
 program-scale readiness criteria.

 Together, these elements provide an independent view of real-world readiness: what current systems
can deliver during outages, what constraints and edge cases must be translated into requirements, and
what cybersecurity and program safeguards should gate any broader adoption.

2.1 Technology, previous pilots, and standards landscape
Argonne conducted a technology and standards landscape review focused on residential V2H and related
bidirectional solutions. The review combined desk research with a structured master chart spanning
more than twenty programs and offerings across North America, Europe, and Asia, including utility-led
and OEM-led pilots, early commercial ecosystems, and announced (not yet widely available) residential
products. The review also distinguished between (i) non-grid-tied V2H backup configurations that do
not export to the grid and (ii) grid-interactive architectures (e.g., V2G/V2X) that require interconnection,
controls, and compensation pathways.
Current technology and pilots landscape (key themes)
Across the pilots reviewed, the market remains early-stage and largely demonstration-based. Programs are
typically small-scale (limited homes/vehicles/chargers), frequently enabled by jurisdiction-specific policy
or regulatory frameworks, and commonly framed around resiliency, peak shaving, and demand-response
objectives. While these goals can be compelling at the system level, pilots repeatedly surface scale barriers
that are especially acute in residential settings:

 • Installation complexity and cost: bidirectional hardware, transfer equipment, permitting/inspection,
 and (where applicable) interconnection approvals can extend timelines and narrow participation to
 early adopters.
 • Operational constraints: vehicle availability and state-of-charge management must preserve
 primary mobility needs, which can limit usable energy and reduce customer confidence.
 • Uncertain and temporary incentives: compensation structures are often pilot-specific or grant-supported,
 making long-term customer value propositions less durable.
 • Limited standardization: requirements and approval pathways vary across jurisdictions, reinforcing
 “pilot” status rather than a repeatable deployment model.

 Commercially positioned offerings (e.g., branded V2H ecosystems) are generally OEM-specific—pairing
a particular vehicle platform with associated EVSE/inverter/transfer equipment and app/cloud control.
This vertical integration can simplify end-user experience within an ecosystem, but it also increases vendor
lock-in and limits transparency and interoperability across brands. Overall, the landscape motivates neutral,
utility-side evaluation because marketing materials and pilot summaries often under-specify edge-case
behavior, continuous-power limits, and cybersecurity lifecycle practices.

 6/31

Standards overview (cybersecurity, communications, safety, grid integration)
From a cybersecurity and interoperability perspective, residential V2H sits at the intersection of consumer
IoT, automotive electronics, and grid-connected power electronics, inheriting requirements and attack
surfaces from all three domains. The most relevant standards can be grouped into four functional areas:

 • Cybersecurity: NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0 provides a technology-agnostic structure
 (Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover, Govern) applicable to V2H assets and data/control
 paths. ISO/SAE 21434 extends this into a road-vehicle cybersecurity engineering lifecycle,
 motivating threat analysis and risk assessment across the full chain (vehicle ↔ EVSE ↔ home
 integration hardware ↔ cloud/app pathways).
 • High-level communication: ISO 15118-20 defines secure EV–EVSE communication for charging
 and bidirectional power transfer (including certificate-based mutual authentication concepts).
 Related SAE documents describe V2G communication use cases and interoperability testing
 frameworks.
 • Safety: UL standards (including UL 9741 for bidirectional EVSE) and related safety frameworks
 govern equipment and installation expectations, especially when systems are paired with other
 home energy components.
 • Interoperability and grid integration: IEEE 1547, SAE J3072, and related interconnection/grid-support
 standards inform behavior under abnormal conditions and become central for grid-interactive
 V2G/V2X configurations (even if non-exporting V2H reduces interconnection scope).

 A consistent takeaway from the review is that strong frameworks exist “on paper,” but implementation
across today’s V2H market is uneven. Public documentation frequently emphasizes connector types and
basic backup functionality, while providing limited detail on higher-layer security controls, certificate
lifecycle management, local-interface hardening, and patch/update practices. Typical architectures also
introduce recurring exposure points: local Wi-Fi/BLE onboarding and app control, proprietary EV–EVSE
stacks layered on standardized connectors, legacy fieldbus/serial links between home energy components,
and cloud/OTA update channels. For program design, the landscape review supports incorporating due
diligence requirements (e.g., vendor attestation of relevant standard conformance where applicable, secure
commissioning and account-binding workflows, clear firmware signing/rollback expectations, and defined
patch cadence and coordinated vulnerability response) as prerequisites to scale.

3 TECHNOECONOMIC ANALYSIS (TEA): V2H/V2G FOR SYSTEM RELIABILITY
  IMPROVEMENT AND PEAK LOAD REDUCTION
3.1 Overview and purpose
Argonne developed a technoeconomic analysis (TEA) framework to estimate (i) the potential reliability
and customer interruption-cost value of residential, non-exporting vehicle-to-home (V2H) backup power
and (ii) the potential long-run distribution peak-support value of grid-tied residential vehicle-to-grid
(V2G) in the ComEd service territory. The analysis is intentionally top-down: historical outage-event
records and feeder load context are combined with simulated EV driving/charging and state-of-charge
(SOC) behavior to quantify the magnitude and sensitivity of benefits under adoption/enrollment scenarios.

3.2 Data inputs
 • Outage-event records : feeder interruption events (2013–2024) including event timing/duration,
 cause attribution, and residential customers affected within ComEd service territory.
 • EV driving and charging profiles: EV daily driving/charging profiles by census tracts simulated
 with Argonne’s Agent-based Transportation Energy Analysis Model (ATEAM) [32]. For this
 study, projected BEV penetration growth in a ComEd territory is based on a BEV growth scenario
 derived from Exelon’s projections that reflect an aggressive adoption trajectory in the Chicagoland
 area (33% of vehicle stock by 2035). The 15-minute resolution profiles are used to sample EV’s
 at-home availability and SOC at event start for the V2H reliability value analysis. Simulated
 vehicle-level charging load profiles represent unmanaged charging behavior, which are used to
 generate TOU-managed (V1G) and V2G charging profiles for the V2G peak-support analysis.
 • Feeder load context: residential load shapes (AMI-derived) and feeder base-load profiles used to
 translate discharge behavior and peak-hour impacts.

3.3 V2H TEA framework (top-down reliability and interruption-cost model)
Argonne applied a systemwide approach to estimate the average reliability improvement potential of
residential V2H adoption for 19 feeders within ComEd territory. Following discussion with ComEd, only
outage events attributed to weather or utility equipment failure were considered “V2H-applicable” and
eligible for potential duration reduction; outages attributed to other causes were assumed to be unaffected
by V2H. The duration of the eligible events vary widely, and they account for approximately half (49%)
of all recorded outage events (N=1,495) in the dataset.

3.5 Core V2H TEA Findings (ComEd case study)
Baseline reliability metrics varied substantially across 2013–2024 with no monotonic trend. The

3.6 V2G TEA Framework (feeder peak reduction / avoided distribution upgrade cost)
Argonne extended the TEA to explore potential distribution value streams from grid-tied residential

4 V2H SYSTEM PERFORMANCE TESTING
4.1 Purpose
The laboratory test campaign evaluated how a representative residential, non-grid-tied V2H backup
ecosystem performs under controlled but realistic operating conditions. The objective was to characterize
(i) transfer-to-backup behavior, (ii) steady and sustained power delivery (including operation near rated
output), (iii) end-to-end conversion efficiency trends, and (iv) repeatable reliability limitations that could
affect customer experience during an outage.

4.2 Test environment and system configuration
Testing was performed in a laboratory environment designed to emulate residential electrical conditions
while operating the system in fully islanded mode (i.e., the V2H system supplied all power demanded by
the emulated home). Programmable AC load equipment replayed residential load profiles derived from
measured household demand data, including representative “Hot Day,” “Nominal Day,” and “Cold Day”
load profiles to capture time-varying residential behavior.

 Figure 6. Diagram of the V2H backup system configuration used for testing.

4.3 Testing matrix
Tables 1–4 summarize the four suites of test cases executed in the performance testing schema. These
suites were selected to represent plausible residential backup operating environments and to capture both
typical behavior and boundary-case performance.
Definition of load demand. Here, “load demand” refers to the externally applied demand conditions
imposed on the system during islanded operation, i.e., the simulated grid environment created by the
programmable load bank and associated control sequence. Because the system operates in backup
(islanded) mode during these tests, the “grid” is not the utility grid; instead, it is represented by the applied
AC load profile (step loads, continuous loads, and variable residential-like profiles). Accordingly, “load
demand” evaluates whether the inverter and associated V2H hardware can supply the requested AC output
while maintaining stable operation (no protective limiting, faulting, or disengagement).
Test suite organization. Suite 1 (Test Cases 1–6) establishes baseline discharge performance across
deliberate boundary conditions in starting battery state: high vs. low starting SOC (100% vs. 5%)
and battery temperature (0◦ C cold vs. 23◦ C nominal), evaluated under both a constant 25 A (∼6 kW)
demand and variable residential-like demand. Suite 2 (Test Case 7) focuses on repeatability of the active
charging-to-discharging transition using a 6 kW demand repeated three times. Suite 3 (Test Cases 8–10)
targets inverter sustained high-power limits by applying 40 A, 38 A, and 36 A (9.6–8.64 kW) continuous
demands to empirically characterize thermal response, derating, protective limiting, or overheating-related
shutdown thresholds. Suite 4 (Test Cases 11–12) isolates system-level behaviors largely independent of
battery constraints, including verification of the bidirectional communication mechanism used during
V2H operation and characterization of no-load/timeout behavior.

DC-to-AC conversion efficiency (end-to-end vs inverter-only)

Figure 7. Efficiency results (plots comparing end-to-end DC→AC efficiency vs inverter-only DC→AC
efficiency across key cases).

Efficiency was evaluated at two reference points relative to the same AC output: (i) vehicle-reported DC
output to measured AC output (end-to-end), and (ii) measured inverter DC input to measured AC output

 15/31

(inverter conversion). Across representative tests, inverter-only conversion remained relatively stable at
∼95–96%, while end-to-end efficiency varied more widely due to vehicle-side auxiliary consumption
and operating state. Under a near-ideal steady operating condition, end-to-end efficiency averaged 91.2%
while inverter-only efficiency averaged 95.8%, implying a non-trivial difference attributable to upstream
(vehicle/DC-side) losses or auxiliary loads. Under cold-start or very-low-SOC conditions, end-to-end
efficiency decreased substantially even when inverter-only efficiency remained high, indicating that
vehicle-side behavior can dominate system-level performance.

Transfer-to-backup behavior (active charging vs non-active states)

 Figure 8. Transfer-to-backup timing summary.

Transfer initiation typically occurred within roughly 1–2 minutes, but timing depended strongly on the
system operating state at the transition. Cases executed while actively charging showed the fastest and
most repeatable transfers (average ≈1 minute 17 seconds), whereas non-active cases showed broader
variability and included uncommon long-delay events. Because rare long-delay behaviors can dominate
averages without reflecting typical performance, they are best treated as reliability edge cases rather than
“normal” operation.

pre-transfer behavior.

 A notable anomalous event required approximately 28 minutes to reach backup transfer initiation
and exhibited a prolonged oscillatory pre-transfer condition in the high-voltage battery current trace,

backup operation.

Sustained high-power operation near rated output

 Figure 10. High sustained-power results (AC output power vs time for 40 A, 38 A, and 36 A cases).

Sustained-load testing near the stated maximum backup output (9.6 kW at 240 V, 40 A) showed that
peak-rated output is not necessarily continuously deliverable under all conditions. At 40 A (9.6 kW),
output exhibited high variability with frequent reductions and interruptions. At slightly reduced demand
(38 A, 9.12 kW), stability improved markedly, with approximately 9.1 kW sustained for roughly 130
minutes before cessation of power delivery. At 36 A (8.64 kW), long-duration stable operation was also
observed following an early interruption. Overall, the results indicate a practically stable continuous-output
region below nameplate ratings in the tested installation, with site-dependent sensitivity driven by thermal
and ventilation conditions.

Repeatable reliability limitations (interruptions and timeouts)
Across multiple tests, intermittent mid-session interruptions were observed (dropouts followed by
recovery), including an early interruption pattern appearing within the first ∼10 minutes in several
runs. A targeted no-load test demonstrated that the system could remain energized for over an hour at
(near) zero external load, suggesting that the observed dropouts are not solely explained by a simple
“low-load auto-stop” mechanism.
 In addition, a distinct timeout termination mode was observed in which backup output dropped to
zero after approximately 25 minutes, consistent with a vehicle-side inactivity or internal timer threshold
that can disable export even when backup operation is desired. These behaviors represent operational
risks during extended outages and motivate clear operating envelopes, configuration guidance (where
available), and program requirements for persistence and recoverability.

4.6 Summary of performance implications
In this representative configuration, transfer-to-backup typically occurred on the order of minutes with
faster, more repeatable performance when transitions occurred from an already active charging state.
However, rare long-delay behavior (including an event approaching 30 minutes) was observed and should
be treated as a reliability risk for customer backup performance. High-power testing indicated that
continuous capability can be meaningfully below peak nameplate ratings and is sensitive to installation
and thermal constraints. Finally, recurring interruption and timeout behaviors highlight the need for
explicit requirements on persistence, deterministic recovery, and user/OEM guidance for extended outage
operation.

5 CYBERSECURITY ASSESSMENT (READINESS, TESTING, AND KEY
  RISK DRIVERS)
5.1 Purpose and scope
This project included an evidence-based cybersecurity assessment of a representative residential V2H
backup ecosystem. The intent was to validate whether expected baseline controls are observable and
enforced in practice during operation, rather than inferred from vendor documentation alone. The
assessment was framed using NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0 and ISO/SAE 21434 concepts
(including threat-analysis and risk-assessment thinking), with an emphasis on risks that could impact (1)
availability of backup power during outages, (2) integrity of system state/telemetry, (3) safe operation of
power electronics, and (4) customer trust.
 Testing was time-bounded and interface-driven in a controlled lab environment. Activities focused
on physical, near-field wireless, and networked interfaces accessible to realistic residential threat actors.
The work did not include vendor backend access, source-code review, destructive testing, or activities
intended to achieve full compromise. Findings should therefore be interpreted as observed posture and
exposure pathways that motivate mitigations, not a claim of comprehensive assurance.

5.2 Cybersecurity readiness review and architecture mapping
Argonne began with a documentation-based readiness review to establish an initial picture of the
ecosystem’s interfaces, identity/onboarding flows, and update pathways. Publicly available product
documentation and support resources were reviewed to identify expected controls and to guide a targeted
lab test plan.
 As part of this review, the team enumerated core assets and functional dependencies across the
ecosystem, including vehicle systems, EVSE hardware, inverter/transfer equipment, mobile applications,
residential networking infrastructure, and cloud/OTA services. For each asset, exposed interfaces,
authentication models, update pathways, and data flows were cataloged to support subsequent threat
modeling and test-case prioritization.

Results below reflect a snapshot in time captured during testing; software versions and exposure can

Physical access attack paths. Physical proximity offers the greatest potential impact but requires
the most restrictive access conditions. Testing focused on identifying exposed diagnostic/maintenance
surfaces and confirming authentication enforcement and information disclosure boundaries. When a

surface. Security scans also indicated software lifecycle issues (e.g., certificate status and framework
signatures). UART console access exposed boot logs and configuration details (information disclosure
contingent on physical access), but did not yield an interactive shell or authentication bypass during
testing.

5.6 Program-relevant implications and minimum controls
Findings indicate that commissioning workflows (BLE/Wi-Fi onboarding) and OTA/update infrastructure
are dominant security levers for pilot-to-program scaling. Minimum controls recommended as prerequisites
for broader deployment include: eliminating universal/static pairing credentials in favor of per-device
secrets; securing onboarding with modern authenticated mechanisms; maintaining software lifecycle
hygiene (certificate renewal, patch cadence); ensuring firmware is cryptographically signed with strong
verification and rollback protections; hardening OTA repositories against unauthorized upload/directory
exposure; and establishing coordinated vulnerability disclosure and incident-response expectations across
OEM/vendor/utility interfaces.

6 RECOMMENDATIONS
This section translates the technoeconomic analysis, hardware evaluation, and cybersecurity findings
into actionable guidance. Recommendations are ordered from highest-impact items (reliability-limiting
behaviors, sustained-operational constraints, customer-experience risks, and cybersecurity must-haves) to
enabling actions that improve program readiness and field confidence.

6.1 V2H and V2G technoeconomic analysis recommendations
 • Treat “V2H-applicable” outage share as a first-order screening metric. Because modeled
 benefits are strongly driven by the fraction of interruption duration attributable to V2H-applicable
 causes, use local outage-cause and duration distributions as an early gate for where V2H incentives
 are likely to be cost-effective.
 • Improve outage notifications and customer readiness pathways. Reliability value is amplified
 when customers can anticipate outages and ensure the vehicle is present/plugged with adequate
 SOC; program design should prioritize actionable notification (and guidance) for weather-related
 events and longer-duration risks.
 • Report results as ranges and sensitivities (not single-point forecasts). Maintain explicit “good
 year / poor year” bounding based on historical outage patterns; avoid implying monotonic benefit
 growth independent of realized outages.
 • For V2G planning, separate systemwide peak-reduction signals from feeder-specific NWAs.
 Use the avoided-capacity proxy as a long-run planning signal, but require feeder constraint studies
 and siting realism before claiming near-term localized deferral value.

6.2 V2H performance testing recommendations
 • Specify transfer-to-backup performance requirements. Require reliable progression to active
 backup within a defined window across representative states (active charging vs. non-active, varied
 SOC, realistic residential load steps), and explicitly test edge-case sequences observed in the lab.
 • Define continuous power capability as the primary metric. Require a sustained operating
 envelope (power vs. time) including derating behavior and clear thermal/installation constraints;
 treat nameplate peak power as insufficient for program qualification.
 • Qualify persistence and recovery behavior. Verify behavior under extended outages (tens
 of minutes to hours): prevent or clearly disclose timeouts, define deterministic recovery steps,
 and provide customer-visible state indicators (“in backup,” “limited by temperature,” “timeout
 imminent,” etc.).
 • Expand validation beyond a single configuration. Replicate key findings across additional
 makes/models or configurations (EV, EVSE, inverter/transfer equipment) and across environmental
 conditions relevant to ComEd’s territory (temperature, ventilation/installation constraints).

6.3 V2H cybersecurity recommendations
 • Make cybersecurity a participation prerequisite, not an add-on. Any scaled pilot should
 require baseline controls: secure commissioning/onboarding, strong authentication, encrypted
 communications, and role-appropriate access control across relevant interfaces.
 • Require signed updates and rollback protection. Firmware/software update mechanisms
 (including OTA pathways) should be authenticated, integrity-protected, and resilient to downgrade/rollback
 attacks, with documented patch cadence and support lifetimes.
 • Establish coordinated vulnerability disclosure and incident response. Program readiness should
 include vendor points-of-contact, disclosure timelines, and customer-impact playbooks aligned
 with a standards-informed framework (e.g., NIST CSF 2.0 functions and automotive cybersecurity
 practices).
 • Reduce home-network dependency risk. Provide clear guidance for home-network hygiene
 (segmentation where feasible, credential practices) and minimize insecure local services exposed
 on residential networks.

6.4 Technology readiness
Overall, results indicate residential V2H backup power can be meaningful within a defined envelope,
but readiness depends on explicitly managing (i) transfer-time variability across operating states, (ii)

 27/31

sustained-power/thermal behavior and any time-based shutdown modes, and (iii) cybersecurity controls
spanning commissioning, embedded services, and update pathways. A program should treat these as
gating items for expansion.

6.5 Next steps
 • Replication testing of key edge cases: repeat the long-delay transfer behavior and any observed
 timeout/shutdown modes to determine triggers, prevalence, and mitigations.
 • Field-realism validation: validate behaviors under representative residential installations (wiring
 lengths, ventilation, ambient conditions, customer load diversity) and document installation best
 practices.
 • Broaden system coverage: test additional equipment variants to determine which findings are
 system-specific versus ecosystem-wide patterns.
 • Operational guidance: translate findings into customer-facing playbooks (how to prepare, how to
 verify readiness, what to expect during transfer, what to do after a shutdown).

6.6 Program integration plan (stage-gate concept)
If ComEd pursues broader consideration of residential V2H backup, we recommend a staged approach
that prevents scale-up before minimum requirements are met:

 1. Gate 1 — Technical qualification: verified transfer performance, continuous-power envelope, and
 persistence behavior under representative conditions.
 2. Gate 2 — Cybersecurity readiness: secure commissioning, authenticated/encrypted interfaces,
 signed updates, defined patch cadence, and coordinated vulnerability response.
 3. Gate 3 — Installation and customer experience: validated installation guidance, clear customer
 eligibility/readiness criteria, and transparent disclosure of limitations (derating, timeouts, recovery
 steps).
 4. Gate 4 — Program design alignment: incentive/eligibility design aligned with outage risk
 and “V2H-applicable” value drivers; optional exploration of grid-tied pathways (V2G) only after
 interconnection and safety requirements are satisfied.

7 CONCLUSION
This study finds that residential V2H backup power is technically feasible and can deliver meaningful
resilience value when operated within a defined and clearly communicated envelope. The TEA indicates
benefits are plausible but strongly dependent on outage patterns and the share of interruption duration
attributable to V2H-applicable events. Laboratory evaluation shows transfer-to-backup is typically on
the order of minutes but includes important edge cases, and that peak-rated output is not necessarily
continuously deliverable without derating or thermal/operational constraints. Cybersecurity considerations
span the full ecosystem—commissioning, home networks, embedded services, and cloud/OTA update
pathways—and should be treated as prerequisites for any scaled pilot or programmatic support.
