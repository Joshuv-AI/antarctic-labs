2. OBJECTIVES
The primary objectives of this project are (1) to develop and demonstrate at the lab-scale technical
feasibility of producing an innovative functionally gradient composite transition joint part (GCTJ) that can
be used to connect and joint dissimilar metals, to cost effectively solve the critical challenges of premature
failure of the conventional dissimilar metal welds (DMW) under increased cyclic operating conditions of
fossil power plants. The additively manufactured GCTJ (AM-GCTJ) will be suitable for next generation
advanced ultra-supercritical (A-USC) power plant, and for retrofit or replacement of the conventional
DMWs in existing fossil power plants, to safely and economically operate in cycling modes for well beyond
their original 30-year design life; (2) we will manufacture and test the components with AC-GTJ, to advance
the technology readiness level to TRL-7, and manufacturing readiness level to MRL 6-7, for targeted
commercial applications identified by GE-Steam Power, the primary industry partner of project team.

3. PROGRESS OF PROJECT
The transition to advanced ultra-supercritical (A-USC) power plants is essential for improving efficiency
and reducing emissions in modern energy systems; however, it introduces significant materials challenges,
particularly in dissimilar metal welds (DMWs) that operate under extreme temperatures, pressures, and
increasingly cyclic conditions. Conventional DMWs suffer from premature failure due to sharp
compositional gradients and thermal expansion mismatch, leading to creep-fatigue damage and reduced
service life. This project addresses these critical limitations by developing additively manufactured graded
composite transition joints (AM-GCTJs), which enable a smooth compositional and mechanical transition
between dissimilar alloys, thereby mitigating stress concentration and enhancing durability. The
successful implementation of this technology is expected to significantly improve the reliability, safety,
and lifetime of both next-generation and existing power plants, while supporting flexible operation and
integration with renewable energy systems

Task 3 Transition Joint Design
3.1 Objective and Scope
This task focuses on applying ORNL’s integrated computational welding engineering (ICWE) tool to support
the development of an AM-GCTJ to eliminate the premature failure in DMWs under high-temperature and
cyclic loading conditions. Conventional DMWs are prone to degradation mechanisms driven by thermal
expansion mismatch, microstructural instability, and localized creep damage, which collectively
compromise their long-term structural integrity and service reliability.
To address these challenges, we employed the physics-guided ICWE framework (Zhang, Wang et al. 2020,
Zhang, Wang et al. 2020, Zhang, Wang et al. 2024) to design, engineering the GCTJ concept. This
framework integrates multiple key components, including the design of gradient material architectures,
mechanistic modeling to predict long-term material deformation and failure, and the manufacturability
of the additive manufacturing (AM) process to realize desired material distributions. Through this
integrated methodology, the transition joint design is grounded in both fundamental materials science
and practicality of the manufacturing processes at scale.
The design strategy targets specific performance metrics, including enhanced creep and creep-fatigue
resistance, reduction of stress concentration through improved stress redistribution, and control of failure
 7

modes to shift damage away from critical interface regions. Ultimately, this task aims to provide a scalable,
high-reliability joining solution for dissimilar metals, such as Grade 91 steel and 304H stainless steel, with
the goal of significantly extending the service life of critical components in power generation systems.
3.2 Design Concept: Gradient Composite Transition Joint (GCTJ)
An AM-GCTJ was developed as a fundamentally new joining method for dissimilar metals. Unlike
traditional welds that introduce abrupt material discontinuities at the interface, the GCTJ employs a
continuous variation in material and volume fraction across the transition region, as the schematics shown
in Figure 5. This graded architecture enables a smooth transition in material properties, including elastic
modulus, coefficient of thermal expansion, and creep resistance, etc., thereby minimizing the
thermomechanical incompatibility between dissimilar alloys.
The GCTJ design incorporates several key features to achieve this functionality: (1) a gradient material
zone is introduced between the two base materials, within which the material volume fraction gradually
evolves from one alloy to the other; (2) within this region, a composite or mixed-material architecture is
engineered to provide enhanced mechanical constraint and facilitate effective load sharing between
materials with different strength and creep resistance; (3) this tailored material distribution is enabled by
additive manufacturing, which allows precise spatial control of material distribution and geometry.

 Figure 5 Schematics of gradient composite transition joint design of plates and tubes.

Functionally, the gradient composite design provides multiple advantages. The smooth transition in
thermal expansion properties significantly reduces thermal mismatch stresses that typically arise during
heating and cooling cycles. At the same time, the composite nature of the transition zone suppresses
strain localization by redistributing stresses more uniformly across the joint. This results in improved load
transfer, particularly under high-temperature creep conditions, where the more creep-resistant material
can carry a greater portion of the load. Consequently, damage evolution is more controlled, reducing the
likelihood of premature failure at critical locations such as the weld interface or heat-affected zone. Overall,
the GCTJ concept establishes a new design approach for dissimilar metal joining, in which material
architecture is strategically engineered to directly control local deformation mechanisms and structural
performance.

3.3 ICWE-Based Design Methodology
To enable the systematic design and evaluation of the gradient composite transition joint, an Integrated
Computational Welding Engineering (ICWE) (Zhang, Wang et al. 2020, Zhang, Wang et al. 2020, Zhang,
Wang et al. 2024)approach was adopted. This framework provides a physics-based foundation for linking
material behavior, structural response, and joint design, allowing for predictive assessment of
performance under high-temperature and cyclic loading conditions. By integrating multi-physics
simulations with spatially resolved material descriptions, the ICWE methodology supports both the
development and optimization of the GCTJ architecture.

3.3.1 Mechanistic Modeling Framework for High Temperature

mechanisms governing the behavior of dissimilar metal joints at elevated temperatures. It accounts for
the temperature-dependent thermal expansion, elasticity, and plasticity. In addition, time-dependent
deformation at high temperature is modeled through creep constitutive behavior, including the evolution
of creep damage associated with cavity nucleation and growth. This coupling enables the framework to
capture the progression from initial stress redistribution to long-term deformation accumulation and
eventual failure.

3.3.2 Material Representation
A key aspect of the ICWE-based design is the representation of the transition joint as a spatially
heterogeneous material system, in which material properties vary continuously across the gradient region.
From a global perspective, the material properties at any location within the transition zone are
determined as functions of the local volume fractions of the constituent alloys. This enables a smooth
transition in key properties, including elastic modulus, coefficient of thermal expansion, yield strength,
and creep properties. At the local scale, however, the material behavior is governed directly by the
constituent material present at that location. No additional or artificially homogenized material models
are introduced to represent the gradient composite. Instead, the transition is achieved through the spatial
distribution of the original material properties, preserving the intrinsic physical behavior of each alloy.
This modeling strategy ensures that the predicted response remains physically meaningful and directly
traceable to experimentally characterized material behavior.

3.3.3 Design Parameters and Parametric Space
The ICWE-based framework enables systematic exploration of key design parameters that govern the
performance of the GCTJ. These parameters define the gradient profile, transition length, and geometry
are essential for optimization, as the schematics shown in Figure 5. These parameters directly influence
stress distribution, strain accumulation, and long-term deformation behavior under high-temperature and
cyclic loading conditions.
One of the primary design variables is the gradient profile, which defines how material composition
changes across the transition zone. A smooth transition in material properties reduces stress
concentration and suppresses strain localization. Both linear and nonlinear gradient distributions can be
considered, each influencing the stress and strain distribution differently. The transition length, often
characterized by the ratio of transition length (LT) to pipe outer diameter (OD), is another critical
parameter, as it determines the extent over which material properties evolve and directly impacts the
degree of stress transitioning and deformation control. In addition, the geometry configuration influences
the stress state and deformation response. Compared to plate joints, tubular structures experience
stronger constraint and multiaxial stresses. The ICWE framework enables direct evaluation of these effects,
supporting the identification of optimized transition joint designs for different applications.
3.4 Performance Evaluation and Validation
To evaluate and validate the proposed AM-GCTJ design, a series of numerical and experimental studies
were conducted, including creep, thermal fatigue, and creep-fatigue testing under service-relevant
conditions. A conventional DMW was also tested as a baseline for comparison.

3.4.1 Creep Performance
The ICWE framework was employed to simulate and analyze the creep behavior of both the GCTJ and the
conventional DMW. Tests were conducted at 650 °C with an applied stress of 90 MPa. As illustrated in the
simulation results (Figure 6), the GCTJ effectively reduces strain localization and redistributes deformation
more uniformly across the joint compared to the conventional DMW. In creep testing, the GCTJ exhibited
a substantial improvement in performance, including the reduction in strain accumulation and a shift in
the location of maximum deformation away from critical interface regions. In contrast, the DMW showed
pronounced strain localization near the weld interface and heat-affected zone, consistent with known
DMW failure mechanisms. Quantitatively, the predicted creep rupture life for the finger-shaped GCTJ
(~1650 hours) showed excellent agreement with experimental measurements, representing a six-fold
improvement over the DMW (~225 hours) under identical conditions. Similar performance gains were
observed in the ridge-shaped GCTJ. These results validate the fundamental design principle of the GCTJ,
namely, that a graded material architecture can effectively mitigate thermomechanical mismatch and
improve load sharing under creep conditions. Furthermore, the strong correlation between our modeling
and experimental data demonstrates the ICWE framework’s capability to accurately predict complex
deformation behavior and guide the design of advanced transition joints for high-temperature
applications.

 Figure 6 Comparative creep evaluation of GCTJs vs. conventional Grade 91/SS304H DMWs. Testing at 650 °C and
 90 MPa highlights the enhanced performance due to transitioning material distributions in GCTJ.

Leveraging the modeling framework, the creep performance of GCTJs was further evaluated over a wide
range of temperature and stress conditions. Figure 7 presents the predicted creep life of the AM-GCTJ in
comparison to conventional DMWs and Grade 91 base material from established literature data (Yaguchi,
Nakamura et al. 2016). The results indicate that the creep life of the GCTJ consistently falls between the
lower-bound DMW baseline and the upper-bound Grade 91 base metal. This suggests that the GCTJ design
effectively mitigates the premature joint failure, and the total service life remains ultimately governed by
the base metal of the less creep resistant constituent, in this case, the Grade 91 steel. The degree of life
enhancement depends on the operating conditions. Under high-stress, lower-temperature conditions, the
creep life of the GCTJ approaches that of the base metal, achieving more than a five-fold improvement
over conventional DMWs. Under high-temperature, low-stress conditions, the improvement is more
moderate. This shift reflects the transition from dislocation-controlled creep to diffusional creep

mechanisms. These results suggest the necessity of accounting for deformation mechanism transitions
when predicting the long-term reliability of transition joints in power plant environments.

The modeling tool was further applied to evaluate the performance of GCTJs in tubular structures, which
are more representative of practical applications. The geometry dimensions of the testing tubes are: 2”
outer diameter (OD), 10 mm through wall thickness, and 1” length of transition (LT) for the GCTJ. Figure 8
compares the predicted creep life of the tubular GCTJ against a conventional DMW baseline under various
internal pressure conditions at 650 oC. In such configurations, the joint is subjected to multiaxial stresses,
including hoop, axial, and radial components. Conventional DMWs in tubular geometries are particularly
susceptible to localized bending and strain accumulation near the weld interface, driven by the mismatch
in thermal expansion coefficients and the abrupt change in local geometry. These factors typically lead to
accelerated damage and premature interfacial failure. The tubular GCTJ demonstrated a life enhancement
consistent with the trends observed in plate sections. Under high internal pressure (short-term creep),
the creep life of the GCTJ approached that of the Grade 91 base metal. In this regime, the model predicted
failure modes similar to Grade 91 steel, with damage initiating at the tube's inner surface and propagating
outward, away from the transition zone. Under lower pressures, long-term creep, diffusional creep
dominates, and failure may occur within the transition zone, where the constraining effect induces strong
triaxial stresses, contributing to cavitation damage. Despite the failure mechanism transition, the GCTJ
consistently demonstrates significant creep life enhancement compared to conventional DMWs.

3.4.2 Thermal Fatigue and Creep Fatigue Performance
To evaluate the performance of GCTJ under cyclic service relevant conditions, a series of thermal fatigue
and creep-fatigue loading scenarios were investigated using the ICWE framework. Both simplified thermal
cycles and realistic startup–shutdown transients were considered to assess the deformation response of
GCTJs in comparison with conventional DMWs.
A simplified thermal fatigue loading cycle was first applied to isolate the effect of cyclic thermal loading.
The temperature increased from ambient to 650 °C over two hours, held for two hours, and then cooled
back to ambient over two hours. The specimens were constrained on one end and allowed to freely
expand on the other. This cycle was repeated to evaluate the progressive accumulation of deformation
under cyclic thermal loading. In addition, idealized startup–shutdown transients, including both cold start
and hot start conditions, were considered based on representative operating conditions from GE. These
loading profiles incorporate both thermal and mechanical effects, which are more representative of real
service environments.

3.4.2.1 Thermal Fatigue Behavior in Plate Joints
The deformation behavior of plate GCTJs under cyclic thermal loading was examined through both
simulation and DIC measurements. The results in Figure 9 and Figure 10 show that the ICWE model
accurately captures the spatial strain distribution and its evolution over multiple cycles. At elevated
temperature, higher strain was observed in the 304H stainless steel region due to its higher coefficient of
thermal expansion. The material yielded during the initial cycle and subsequently exhibited a shakedown
behavior, with minimal additional plastic strain accumulation in later cycles. In contrast, Grade 91 steel
accumulated more strain due to its lower creep resistance and reduced strain hardening capacity,
although the rate of accumulation decreased over successive cycles.

 13

Compared to conventional DMWs, the GCTJ significantly suppresses the strain distribution, as shown in
the Figure 11. In DMWs, strain is highly localized at the joining interface, particularly at the cap and root
regions on the Grade 91 side, leading to accelerated damage accumulation. In contrast, the GCTJ design
redistributes deformation across the transition region, resulting in a more uniform strain field and reduced
peak strain. After multiple thermal cycles, both the strain range and net strain accumulation in the GCTJ
were substantially reduced compared to the DMW. Importantly, while the critical locations in both
configurations remained in Grade 91 steel, the GCTJ exhibited more distributed strain, indicating improved
resistance to localized damage and crack initiation.

3.4.2.2 Thermal Fatigue and Creep-Fatigue Behavior in Tubular Structures
The performance of GCTJs was further evaluated in tubular geometries under cyclic startup–shutdown
transients, which introduce multiaxial stress states and more complex deformation behavior. The
simulation results are summarized in Figure 12. Under cold start conditions, where the temperature
variation is large, conventional DMWs exhibited severe strain localization at the inner surface (ID) near
the joining interface on the Grade 91 side. This resulted in high peak strain accumulation and increased

 15

risk of premature failure. In contrast, the GCTJ significantly reduced strain concentration, lowering the
maximum accumulated strain by more than 60% and shifting the critical location away from the interface
to regions with more distributed stress. Under hot start conditions, the overall strain accumulation was
reduced in both GCTJ and DMW due to the reduced temperature range. However, the GCTJ continued to
demonstrate enhanced performance, with reduced accumulated strain and more favorable strain
distribution. The critical locations in the GCTJ shifted depending on the loading condition, reflecting the
interaction between material distribution and thermal-mechanical loading. The model revealed that the
critical failure locations in the GCTJ are sensitive to the specific loading profile, reflecting a dynamic
interaction between the material gradient and the thermomechanical loading path. Quantitatively, cold-
start loading resulted in approximately 75% higher strain accumulation than hot-starts, primarily driven
by the intensified thermal stresses during the initial ramp-up. Across all simulated transient scenarios, the
GCTJ consistently suppressed the localized deformation that typically causes pre-mature failure of
conventional welds. By effectively managing the mismatch in thermal expansion and creep strength
through a gradient composite architecture, the GCTJ demonstrates the necessary robustness for long-
term service in power plant environments.

3.5 Scalability and Application Potential
The combined model-based investigations and experimental validation demonstrate that GCTJ design is
not only effective under specific testing conditions but also scalable to a wide range of geometries,
materials, and service environments relevant to high-temperature energy systems.
The validated ICWE-based modeling framework enables reliable prediction of creep deformation, strain
distribution, and failure behavior across different loading conditions and structural configurations. This
 16

predictive capability allows the GCTJ design to be systematically extended beyond laboratory-scale plate
specimens to more complex geometries, including tubular structures and large-diameter piping systems
commonly used in power plant applications.
The demonstrated performance improvements, including reduced strain localization and controlled
failure modes, indicate that the GCTJ concept can effectively address the long-standing reliability
challenges associated with conventional dissimilar metal welds. In particular, the ability to tailor material
architecture through gradient design provides a versatile pathway for adapting the transition joint to
different material combinations and operating conditions.
Furthermore, the parametric studies on transition length and geometry establish practical design
guidelines, such as the optimal LT/OD range, which can be directly applied to engineering-scale
components. The integration of additive manufacturing enables the realization of these complex gradient
structures, supporting scalable fabrication and potential deployment in industrial applications.
Overall, this task demonstrates that the GCTJ design, supported by a physics-informed modeling
framework, provides a scalable, adaptable, and high-performance solution for dissimilar metal joining in
demanding high-temperature environments. The approach offers significant potential for improving
structural reliability, extending service life, and enabling next-generation designs in advanced energy
systems.

pressing (HIP) workflow (as shown in Figure 13) to ensure precise control of material distribution, interface
integrity, and final microstructural stability. The process was specifically designed to avoid full
compositional melting between dissimilar alloys and to produce a solid-state composite transition with
constituents of known chemistry.

7.1 High-Temperature Creep Performance
An existing DOE-sponsored and experimentally validated high-temperature material model was employed
to numerically investigate the creep behavior of additively manufactured graded composite transition
joints (AM-GCTJs) and conventional dissimilar metal welds (DMWs). In parallel, both numerical
simulations and experimental creep tests were conducted on plate-type AM-GCTJ and DMW specimens
joining Grade 91 and 304 steels under an applied stress of 90 MPa at 650 °C. The geometries of the tested
specimens are shown in Figure 23 and Figure 24.

 Figure 24 Specimens Used for Testing: AM-GCTJ and Conventional DMW

For the conventional DMW, the numerical results indicate that creep strain preferentially accumulates in
the heat-affected zone (HAZ) of Grade 91, ultimately leading to failure within this region with a rupture
life of approximately 225 h. These predictions show good quantitative agreement with in-situ DIC
measurements in terms of both creep strain accumulation and rupture life (as shown in Figure and Figure ).
In contrast, for the AM-GCTJ, creep strain develops more slowly and is concentrated in the base metal of
Grade 91 rather than at the joining interface within the transition zone. This behavior is primarily
attributed to the significant reduction in effective stress in the transition region resulting from the specially
designed graded material distribution. Consequently, the AM-GCTJ exhibits an approximately fivefold
improvement in creep life compared with the conventional DMW.
Moreover, the AM-GCTJ design enhances the constraint effect and effectively reduces stress within the
critical region, leading to a shift in the failure location from the Grade 91 HAZ in the DMW to a region near
the boundary of the Grade 91 base metal in the AM-GCTJ. Overall, the transition-zone design suppresses
stress concentration in the joint region and results in more than a fivefold enhancement in creep life,
demonstrating the effectiveness of the graded architecture in improving long-term high-temperature
performance.

Task 8 Corrosion
Despite the mechanical advantages demonstrated by GCTJs, high-temperature corrosion remains a
primary limitation for their industrial deployment, particularly in advanced ultra-supercritical (A-USC)
power plant environments. During service, sulfate salts such as Na₂SO₄ and MgSO₄ can deposit on
component surfaces from combustion gases. At elevated temperatures, these deposits melt and form thin
ionic films that act as electrolytes, enabling rapid electrochemical attack on structural materials.
In dissimilar metal systems, compositional heterogeneity across the joint interface introduces an inherent
electrochemical potential difference, creating conditions favorable for galvanic corrosion. In the 304/P91
material pair, P91 exhibits a more negative corrosion potential and therefore acts as the anodic
component, experiencing preferential and accelerated degradation relative to the base-metal corrosion
behavior of either alloy. The geometry of the transition joint and the exposed surface area ratio between
dissimilar materials can further amplify galvanic effects, promoting localized corrosion and increasing the
likelihood of premature failure. Consequently, a systematic evaluation of corrosion behavior is essential
for the optimization and qualification of GCTJ designs.

8.3 Electrochemical Test Results
To investigate the factors driving the observed corrosion behavior, a series of electrochemical experiments
were performed using electrode couples with identical areas of P91 and 304. Figure a shows the change
of OCP of both P91 and 304 in molten 50 at% Na2SO4+50 at% MgSO4 salt at 700°C. Both OCPs show the
same trend: the initial drop then increase followed the stable stage in OCP. The initial drop in the OCP
ascribed to the dissolution of the thin oxide layer which is formed when slowly immersing the electrodes
into the molten salts. The subsequent rapid increase in the OCP infer that the spontaneous growth of the
oxide. After about half an hour, the stable OCP of 304 (-0.09 V vs. Pt) is about 0.11 V higher than that of
P91(-0.20 V vs. Pt), indicating that galvanic corrosion of 304 and P91 can occur readily in high-temperature
molten sulfate salts. The 304 with the more positive potential acts as the cathode in the galvanic couple,
while P91 acts as the anode, as shown in Figure b. The kinetic parameters, including anodic and cathodic
Tafel constants (βa and βc, respectively), for P91 and 304 in molten salts, and the galvanic current density
(Ig) and the galvanic potential (Eg), are shown in Figure . The corrosion potential of 304 is higher than that
of P91, indicating that P91 is more vulnerable to hot corrosion attacks. The intersection between the
cathodic branch of 304 and the anodic branch of P91 shows that the galvanic corrosion current density is
about 112 μA cm-2 with a galvanic potential of -0.16 V vs. Pt. This indicates that in the P91/304 galvanic
couple, P91 would serve as an anode which is in the active dissolution state while 304 will serve as the
cathode with high tendency towards passivation.
The steady-state Ig and potential of the P91/304 couple in the molten salts are recorded and plotted as a
function of time, as shown in Figure d. It is important that the Ig is a “net” current density that represents
the difference between the local anodic current and the local cathodic current on the electrode. Thus, the
positive Ig between 304 and P91 of equal area indicates the activity of anodic reactions on the P91
electrode is higher than that on the 304 electrode. An initial Ig can reach 500 μA cm-2, meaning vigorous
dissolution happens on the P91 electrode. After 1.5 h, the stable Ig between P91 (anode) and 304 (cathode)

Galvanic corrosion is inevitable at the interface of dissimilar metals. However, the prolonged corrosion
behavior of GCTJ is significantly different from that of DMW. For DWM, the corrosion depth observed
along the y-axis at the interface between P91 and ER309 is relatively consistent shown in Figure 47. In
contrast, GCTJ exhibits a gradient corrosion degradation along the y-axis, which is attributed to the novel
joining configuration of the dissimilar metals. Electrochemical test results (Figure ) indicate that 304
stainless steel, with a more positive corrosion potential, acts as the cathode, while P91 steel functions as
the anode in the galvanic couple. To elucidate this relationship, the ratio Sc/Sa is introduced, where Sc
represents the exposed surface area of the cathodic material and Sa denotes that of the anodic material.
In the context of DMWs, ER309 serves as the cathode due to its higher nobility compared to P91 steel. As
illustrated in Figure a, the GCTJ presents a gradient in the Sc/Sa ratio along the y-axis, whereas the DMW
maintains a fixed 1:1 ratio. The degradation depth of GCTJ and DMW after the 1 h and 5 h tests is
summarized in Figure b. As can be seen, the corrosion depth of GCTJ increases along with the rising Sc/Sa
in the range of 0.6-5.3, reaching an average 9.5 μm (after 1 h corrosion) and 41.2 μm (after 5 h corrosion),
significantly deeper than that on DMW (6.9 μm after 1 h corrosion and 15.9 μm after 5 h corrosion).
Beyond a Sc/Sa ratio of 5.3, the degradation depth of both 1 h corroded GCTJ and 5 h corroded GCTJ is
reduced. This enhanced corrosion resistance at the P91 triangle tip is likely due to the higher content of
Cr and Ni (10.6 wt%Cr, 0.8 wt%Ni) than that of P91 base metal (9.2 wt%Cr, 0.1 wt%Ni). Despite the
improved resistance, the P91 triangle tip still exhibits greater corrosion depth than that observed in DMW.

8.4 Scientific Summary
This work systematically investigated the high-temperature corrosion behavior of 304/P91 GCTJs under
molten sulfate salt environments relevant to advanced ultra-supercritical (A-USC) power plants. The
results demonstrate that corrosion degradation in GCTJs is governed by the combined effects of interfacial
microstructure and electrochemical surface area ratio, with galvanic coupling identified as the dominant
driver during prolonged exposure.

 48

❑ Interfacial Microstructure Effects
  • The P91 transition zone contains a dual-phase microstructure (ferrite + tempered martensite)
 formed during HIP.
  • Corrosion initiates preferentially in tempered martensite due to localized Cr depletion, carbide
 precipitation, and higher defect density.
  • SKPFM measurements reveal localized Volta potential fluctuations (~100 mV) associated with pit
 initiation sites.
  • Despite different initiation behavior, both phases show similar long-term degradation, indicating
 microstructure mainly affects early-stage corrosion.
❑ Electrochemical Surface Area Ratio Effects
  • Corrosion severity within the P91 transition zone correlates strongly with the cathode-to-anode
 surface area ratio (Sc/Sa).
  • P91 regions adjacent to larger exposed 304 areas experience deeper localized corrosion.
  • A near-linear increase in P91 anodic dissolution current density with Sc/Sa confirms surface area
 imbalance as the primary corrosion accelerator.
❑ Comparison with Conventional DMWs
  • Conventional DMWs, with a fixed surface area ratio, exhibit relatively uniform corrosion depths.
  • GCTJs show non-uniform, geometry-dependent corrosion due to variable Sc/Sa.
  • These results demonstrate the critical role of joint geometry in corrosion performance.
❑ Design Implications for A-USC Applications
  • Microstructure controls corrosion initiation, while geometry-driven galvanic effects control long-
 term corrosion severity.
  • GCTJ designs should minimize exposed anodic P91 area adjacent to cathodic materials.
  • Geometry-optimized GCTJs provide a viable path to improved corrosion resistance and long-term
 reliability in A-USC power plant service.

Task 10 In-field Test in SWRI
As the final major technical evaluation of the AM-GCTJ performance, this task aimed at validating the
performance of AM-GCTJ under thermal cyclic loading conditions expected in service. To this end, a one-
of-its-kind field test was purposely designed, engineered, and carried out at Southwest Research Institute
(SwRI®) in San Antonio, TX.
This purposely designed field test leveraged existing supercritical carbon dioxide sCO 2 thermal cyclic test
loop infrastructure at SwRI®. It utilized a high-pressure flow of sCO2 to thermally cycle the AM-GCTJ while
simultaneously applying internal pressure to mimic certain operational scenarios under which the
dissimilar metal weld would experience in service.
Flow loop conditions and test section temperatures were measured near material transition joints to
observe the condition of the piping during the tests. A digital image correlation (DIC) camera system used
to measure thermal strain in the areas of interest was installed prior to the field test, and the DIC data
along with the pressure and temperature data, which were over 2 Terabyte in total, were recorded and
delivered to Oak Ridge National Lab (ORNL) for further analysis. The successful test, accompanied by the
analysis and characterization of tested AM-GCTJ joint, would elevate the AM-GCTJ technology to a
technology readiness level (TRL) of 7.
10.1 Field Test Requirements and Setup
The field test experiment was designed to cycle the AM-GCTJ transition joint between 100°C and 620°C,
heated by a pressurized flow of sCO2, not to exceed 3,000 psi. All flow loop components were rated at or
above 207 Bar (3000 psi) at their respective maximum operating temperature. The cycle duration was 1-
1.5 hour.
To allow for simultaneous testing of different AM-GCTJ configurations in one testing loop, a total 10 AM-
GCTJ transition tube joints of three joint configurations and two diameters were installed onto two
sections of 1-inch and 2-inch pipe testing loops for thermal cycling field tests. Figure shows the sample
assembly layout schematics and the fabricated pipe components. For each testing loop, different AM-GCTJ
designs, designated as ridge type, pyramid type, and finger type, were welded in series. The experimental
design also allowed for testing conventional dissimilar metal weld (DMW) joints between SS304 and
P91/P92, as well as same metal weld joints of P91 to P91, or SS304 to SS304 on the same testing loops.
Standard weld and heat treatment procedures were used to add the required test section inlet and exit
pipes onto the provided sample pipes.
A total of 4 thermocouples were installed on each testing loop. The locations of these thermocouples
relative to the locations of AM-GCTJ and DMW are indicated in Figure . Both test loops were hydrostatically
tested to 4500 psi and held for 10 minutes, prior to the thermal cycling test. No leak or failure were
observed in the hydrostatic test.
Due to the first of a kind nature of the testing with additively manufactured transition joint, it was

10.2 Test Loop Operation Design
A test loop was developed such that hot and cold flow could be alternated between the two test sections
to cycle them between the desired temperatures. A simplified version of the test loop is shown in Figure .
Gaseous CO2 was pressurized to the loop pressure and circulated by pumps. Process temperature at the
circulating pump was limited to 100°C. The outlet was split into two directions: one to the cold section,
and the other through a recuperator and a heater to the hot section. The flowrate through the hot and
cold sections was regulated using a control valve. Block valves were used to determine whether a given
test section is heated or cooled.
Upon exiting the test sections, the flows combined and entered the recuperator, preheating the CO 2 for
the hot section. A heat exchanger was used to ensure that the flow was cooled sufficiently before entering
the pump.
To operate the system, the fabricated test loop was supplied with gaseous CO2, compressed air, and 480-
volt AC power. CO2 was supplied from the 12-ton facility storage tank, 135 psi compressed dry air from
the facility system was used to actuate all control valves, solenoids, and pneumatic pumps, and the
medium voltage power source was used to power ceramic heaters.
Operation was intended and setup to be conducted from a remote workstation that was connected to the
camera and data acquisition system through ethernet and USB cabling. This allowed operators to stay in
a safe environment, away from any high temperature equipment or potential CO2 leaks. The heaters were
controlled by a temperature controller reading temperatures from multiple surface thermocouples under
each of the heaters.

Task 11 Tech-economic Analysis TEA (GE)
The objective of this task is to provide a preliminary techno-economic analysis of the AM-GCTJ technology
versus the conventional dissimilar metal (DMW) weld approach for new HRSGs. Much of the design
information and spool material costs were based on values provided for one of the current combined cycle
plants. Although this plant includes multiple HRSGs, the analysis provided in this study is normalized for a
single HRSG. The overall results clearly show that AM-GCTJ technology provides a clear economic benefit
over DMW technology. For the base conditions used for this study, AM-GCTJ technology using similar size
tubes can provide a net present value savings of $534K over the life of a single HRSG as compared to using
DMW technology. AM-GCTJ technology also enables the use of larger size DMW pipes, resulting in
significant cost savings. Using larger pipe sizes, AM-GCTJ technology can provide a net present value
savings of $922K over the life of a single HRSG as compared to using DMW technology.

The labor cost represents the largest component of the spool total cost. For the DMW spools, the labor

cost. The labor estimate was based on information from field services from another HRSG (Plant B),
which had 504 DMW spools. The installation to replace all of these spools can be summarized as follows:
 • 30 days to replace 504 spools (3 shift)
 o 7 h per weld to cut, bevel, fit/purge/reheat, PWHT,RT/PAUT
 • 3 days to mobilize and another 3 days to demobilize
 o Includes set-up and equipment, EHS confined space entry monitoring, material handling,
 man-lift rental and scaffolding rental, safety orientation
 • The labor rate can vary depending on site location from $50/h to $150/h. A labor rate of $100/h
 was used as the base case.
 • The total cost to replace DMW 504 spools was estimated at $758K. This is an average of $1503
 per spool.
This techno-economic study was based on design parameters from Plant A because information was
available for spools at the same size as Plant B but also for larger size AM-GCTJ spools. Plant A had
similar size tubes as Plant B but had only 462 spools. The labor cost data provided for Plant B was
therefore adjusted for the fewer number of spools. It was assumed that the installation would still take
the same number of days. The spool replacement cost is directly related to the number of spools.
However, the mobilization and demobilization costs are essentially fixed for the same time duration. The
revised labor cost for Plant A, including both spool replacement and fixed costs, is $713. This is an
average of $1544 per spool.

CONCLUSION
This project successfully developed and demonstrated a novel additively manufactured graded composite
transition joint (AM-GCTJ) to overcome the premature failure of conventional dissimilar metal welds
(DMWs) in advanced ultra-supercritical (A-USC) power plants. By integrating a functionally graded
material architecture with a physics-based Integrated Computational Welding Engineering (ICWE) design
framework, the AM-GCTJ enables smooth transitions in composition and thermomechanical properties
between P91 and 304H alloys, significantly reducing stress concentration and mitigating creep-fatigue
damage. The technology was successfully validated through combined modeling, laboratory testing, and
large-scale prototype fabrication, demonstrating ~5–6× improvement in creep life, reduced strain
localization, and superior thermal fatigue resistance compared to DMWs. Scalable manufacturing using
LPBF, HIP, and optimized heat treatment produced dense, defect-free joints, while in-field testing under
up to 620 °C and 3000 psi over 338 cycles confirmed robust performance without failure (TRL 7). Although
corrosion is governed by galvanic effects with geometry-dependent behavior, design optimization
provides a pathway for mitigation. Techno-economic analysis further shows substantial lifecycle cost
savings (up to ~$922K per HRSG), establishing AM-GCTJ as a high-impact, commercially viable solution for
next-generation energy systems.
