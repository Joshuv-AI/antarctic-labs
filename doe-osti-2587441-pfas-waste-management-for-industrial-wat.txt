 AUGUST 2025 TIP OF THE MONTH

PFAS Waste Management for Industrial, Water, and Wastewater Treatment
Facilities

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), including perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS) and
perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), are widely used in industrial and consumer products due to their
unique water and oil repelling properties. These slow-degrading and bio-accumulating
compounds, often called "forever chemicals," are not typically removed by conventional
industrial and municipal water/wastewater (W/WW) treatment. This tip of the month provides
comprehensive strategies for managing PFAS-contaminated waste streams.

Understanding the various types of PFAS waste streams is the first step in managing them.
Primary PFAS waste includes contaminated process W/WW and solid materials directly from
the production and processing of PFAS-containing materials. These might come from
manufacturing facilities, firefighting training areas, or other industrial operations where PFAS
compounds are used or were historically present. Secondary PFAS waste results from
treatment and disposal mechanisms of primary PFAS waste streams, including spent activated
carbon, used ion exchange resins, membrane reject streams, PFAS-laden backwash water,
landfill leachate, incineration residuals, etc. TABLE 1 lists examples of the most common PFAS
waste streams in different industrial facilities.

Management Approaches to PFAS Waste Reduction
Successful management and mitigation of PFAS waste streams requires a comprehensive
approach incorporating PFAS monitoring, source reduction, PFAS treatment and remediation
strategies, and staff training and engagement as shown in FIGURE 1.

 FIGURE 1. Four Components of a Successful PFAS
 Measuring and monitoring Waste Management and Mitigation Approach

  Effective measurement and
  monitoring are critical for identifying
  PFAS sources and prioritizing waste
  streams while managing their end-of-
  life disposal. For accurate
  measurements, organizations should
  implement comprehensive sampling
  and analysis programs to track PFAS
  concentrations in waste, water, and
  disposal processes. This data can be
  used to optimize treatment approaches
  to reduce or eliminate PFAS
  discharges.

 Source reduction strategies
  Source reduction may use the
  information from monitoring to minimize
  PFAS waste generation by cutting the
  problem at the source. Consider these key strategies:

 o Alternative chemicals: Identify and use PFAS-free substitutes in manufacturing.
 o Industry collaboration: Wastewater treatment facilities should work with industrial
 customers that generate WW containing PFAS to implement pollution prevention,
 process modifications, and on-site treatment.

 o Segregation: Separate high-PFAS waste streams to minimize specialized
 treatment needs and overall waste generation.

  PFAS treatment and remediation strategies
 Optimizing existing treatment technologies can substantially reduce secondary waste
 generation while maintaining or improving PFAS removal efficiency.
 o Adsorption methods: Enhance Granular Activated Carbon (GAC) efficiency using
 lead-lag configurations; coal-based GAC performs better for long-chain PFAS.2
 o Ion exchange: Use PFAS-selective resins for higher capacity and regeneration
 potential.3

 o Custom solutions: Pilot site-specific GAC and IX resins for best results.
 o Pretreatment and regeneration: Remove competing ions to extend resin life and
 reduce waste.

 o Liquid waste: Utilize membrane or reverse osmosis for concentration.
 o Solid disposal: Improve sludge dewatering to reduce volume; assess landfilling
 versus incineration due to evolving regulations.4

  Staff training and engagement
 Communication, training, and engagement form an ongoing platform for mitigation of the
 PFAS waste streams. For awareness, staff should be trained on PFAS risks, handling, and
 waste reduction techniques. For proper management, training should include standard best
 practices of operations and of PFAS containment and disposal.

There are resources that can be leveraged to understand the PFAS waste streams, their
treatment methods, and how federal and local regulations are evolving. These are available via
Water Environment Federation, American Water Works Association, as well as other industry
associations such as American Chemistry Council.
