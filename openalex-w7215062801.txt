Abstract
Introduction: Developmental dyscalculia is traditionally defined as a specific difficulty in acquiring mathematical skills.
However, clinical observation and recent studies on comorbidities in neurodevelopmental disorders suggest that this
condition rarely presents in isolation. Objective: To propose an interpretive matrix in which dyscalculia is conceived as
a manifestation of a broader neuropsychiatric condition, termed General Psychiatric Disorder, based on the overlap of
pathophysiological mechanisms. Methods: An integrative literature review was conducted together with longitudinal
clinical observation of five patients with severe dyscalculia. Results: Mathematical difficulty in the patients was
systematically associated with deficits in multiple domains: visuospatial working memory, digital gnosis, lateralization,
spatial processing, comprehension of relational language, and social reciprocity. These findings partially overlap with
developmental Gerstmann syndrome. Discussion: The mathematical deficit does not appear to be the central problem,
but rather the phenotypic expression of profound alterations in shared cognitive mechanisms, such as the mirror-neuron
system and working memory. Conclusion: Understanding dyscalculia requires a systemic approach integrating
spatiality, attention, language, and embodied cognition. The proposed matrix constitutes an interdisciplinary clinical
hypothesis that requires future empirical testing.
Keywords: Dyscalculia; Neurodevelopmental disorders; Gerstmann syndrome; Cognition; Memory, short-term.

1. Introduction
Developmental dyscalculia is classically defined by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM5) as a specific learning disorder characterized by persistent difficulties in the acquisition of mathematical skills, not explained
by global intellectual disability, inadequate schooling, or acquired brain injury (APA, 2013). The prevalence of this disorder is
estimated to affect between 3% and 7% of school-age children (Morsanyi et al., 2018), with the first symptoms emerging around
4 to 6 years of age and formal diagnosis generally occurring after the child has had sufficient exposure to formal mathematics
instruction (Dehaene et al., 2003; Haberstroh & Schulte-Körne, 2019).
Historically, cognitive models of dyscalculia have focused on specific deficits in numerical processing. The Triple Code
Model proposed by Dehaene and colleagues (Dehaene et al., 2003) and Butterworth's "number module" hypothesis (Butterworth,
2010) suggest that dyscalculia results from an innate failure in the Approximate Number System (ANS), located in the bilateral
intraparietal sulcus (Stanescu-Cosson et al., 2000), generating signs and symptoms such as: difficulty memorizing basic
operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division) and distinguishing mathematical operation signs, as well as
sequencing steps in mathematical problems; swapping or reversing numbers; slowness in solving simple calculations; low selfconfidence in number-related contexts, with avoidant behavior in situations requiring number comprehension, such as games
and mathematics-related activities; difficulty counting, especially backward; difficulty determining which number is larger when
comparing simple numbers such as 2 and 6; deficits in creating strategies for calculations, such as finger counting; difficulty
organizing mathematical problems and completing calculations; difficulty understanding or associating a number with a word
(for example, that the number 7 is the same as the word "seven" and that both mean seven items); difficulty understanding
mathematical phrases such as "greater than" and "less than"; difficulty estimating time and understanding concepts of speed and
distance; and extreme difficulty with calculations more complex than addition (Haberstroh & Schulte-Körne, 2019).
However, careful clinical observation and emerging evidence in the neurodevelopmental disorders literature challenge
the notion that dyscalculia is a strictly isolated phenomenon. Recent studies highlight extremely high comorbidity rates among
neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), suggesting shared pathogenic mechanisms rather than independent diseases (Bonti et al.,
2024). Children with dyscalculia frequently present concomitant deficits in attention (ADHD), dyslexia, dysgraphia, and
alterations in motor coordination (Moll et al., 2021).
This article aims to propose an interpretive matrix in which dyscalculia is conceived as a manifestation of a broader
neuropsychiatric condition, termed General Psychiatric Disorder, based on the overlap of pathophysiological mechanisms. To
this end, an integrative literature review was combined with the longitudinal clinical observation of a case series, and the resulting

2. Methodology
We conducted a descriptive research (Risemberg et al., 2026a) using a quantitative approach regarding the number of
23 (twenty-three) articles selected to form the research corpus, and a qualitative approach regarding the discussions (Risemberg
et al., 2026b; Pereira et al., 2018).
The present study is characterized as an integrative literature review with the proposal of a theoretical model, grounded
in the longitudinal clinical observation of a case series.

2.1 Guiding question (PICO strategy)
To guide the integrative review, the following question was formulated using the PICO acronym:

•

P (Population/Problem): School-age children diagnosed with developmental dyscalculia.

•

I (Intervention/Exposure): Assessment of neuropsychiatric comorbidities, visuospatial deficits, digital gnosis,
and working memory.

•

C (Comparison): Not applicable (study of symptomatic overlap).

•

O (Outcome): Identification of shared cognitive and neurobiological mechanisms that justify the inclusion of
dyscalculia within a broader spectrum of neurodevelopmental disorders.

Guiding question: "What are the shared cognitive, instrumental, and behavioral mechanisms between developmental
dyscalculia and other neurodevelopmental disorders that support the hypothesis of a common neuropsychiatric matrix?"

2.2 Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Inclusion criteria:
1. Original articles, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses published in English, Portuguese, or Spanish.
2. Studies investigating the relationship between dyscalculia and executive functions (especially working memory),
spatial processing, digital gnosis, or embodied cognition.
3. Studies addressing comorbidity between neurodevelopmental disorders and developmental Gerstmann syndrome.
4. Classic texts fundamental to the theoretical formulation (e.g., Piaget, 1952; Luria, 1973).

Exclusion criteria:
1. Studies focused exclusively on acquired dyscalculia (secondary to acute focal brain lesions).
2. Articles without full-text access.
3. Conference abstracts, letters to the editor, or editorials without robust empirical/theoretical data.
4. Studies whose methodology did not allow clear correlations to be drawn with the domains under investigation.

2.4 Clinical observation
The empirical basis for formulating the hypothesis consisted of longitudinal clinical follow-up (mean duration of 4
years) of five school-age female patients with a primary diagnosis of severe dyscalculia. The patients were systematically
assessed for mathematical skills, executive functions, visuospatial processing, digital gnosis, laterality, language, and social
behavior.

3.2 Working memory and spatial processing
The literature consistently indicates that working memory deficits constitute one of the main cognitive correlates of
developmental dyscalculia. Although many children are able to understand basic mathematical concepts and simple numerical
information, they frequently present difficulties simultaneously maintaining and manipulating multiple elements in mind during
problem-solving (Szucs et al., 2013). A systematic review covering 75 studies published over the last two decades identified
visuospatial working memory and symbolic numerical processing skills as the most important predictors of mathematical
performance in children with dyscalculia (Mishra & Khan, 2023). Convergently, Szucs et al. (2013) showed that deficits in
visuospatial working memory and inhibitory control represent central features of the disorder, arguing that spatial processing
difficulties are intrinsically related to failures in working memory mechanisms.

These limitations directly compromise the learning and execution of mathematical skills, since arithmetic requires the
active maintenance of relevant information and intermediate results throughout sequences of calculations. Furthermore, reduced
working memory capacity is associated with lower efficiency in transcoding numbers between non-symbolic and symbolic
formats, a process fundamental to the construction of formal numerical knowledge (Rotzer et al., 2009). Deficits in spatial
working memory can also impair the formation and use of spatial numerical representations, such as the mental number line, as
well as the storage and retrieval of arithmetic facts from long-term memory. Consequently, seemingly simple operations can
become extremely difficult, not because the child is incapable of understanding the concepts involved, but because they cannot
sustain and manipulate the information needed to complete multi-step algorithms efficiently and accurately (Ashkenazi et al.,
2013).

3.3 Digital agnosia and embodied cognition
Frequent presence of digital agnosia (difficulty recognizing and identifying one's own fingers) was observed. From a
developmental standpoint, fingers constitute a child's first "mathematical material" (Moeller et al., 2012). The "embodied
cognition" perspective suggests that numerical understanding grows out of the motor and sensory experience of finger counting
(Soylu et al., 2018). Penner-Wilger and Anderson (2013) propose that the relationship between digital gnosis and mathematical
ability occurs due to the redeployment of neural circuits originally developed for finger representation for the purpose of
representing numbers.

3.4 Relational language and set theory
The patients demonstrated severe difficulty in comprehending relations of inclusion, membership, and hierarchy (e.g.,
"Is every insect an animal?"). Piaget (1952) considered operations such as seriation and classification to be foundational to the
concept of number. Additionally, failure was noted in interpreting popular sayings and linguistic structures with implicit
relational components ("If Peter is taller than John, who is shorter?"). Luria (1973) classified these constructions as "quasi-spatial
linguistic structures," whose comprehension requires the simultaneous synthesis of multiple relations, a function classically
associated with the parietal cortex.

4. Discussion
The results of the longitudinal clinical observation, integrated with the reviewed literature, point to a conception of
developmental dyscalculia that transcends the isolated modular deficit model. The following discussion articulates the findings
around three main axes: the overlap with Developmental Gerstmann Syndrome, the cognitive and genetic mechanisms shared
with other neurodevelopmental disorders, and the proposal of an integrative interpretive matrix.

4.1 Developmental Gerstmann Syndrome: overlap, spectrum, and neuroanatomical substrate
4.1.1 Historical origins and classic description
Gerstmann syndrome was originally described by Josef Gerstmann in 1924 and systematized in 1940, based on the
observation of adult patients with focal lesions in the left parietal lobe who concomitantly presented four signs: (1) digital
agnosia, an inability to identify, name, or recognize one's own fingers; (2) acalculia, difficulty performing arithmetic operations;
(3) agraphia, difficulty writing not resulting from a primary motor deficit; and (4) right-left disorientation, an inability to
distinguish the right from the left side of one's own body and in external space (Altabakhi & Liang, 2023). This four-fold

constellation was interpreted by Gerstmann as evidence of a lesion in the angular gyrus of the left inferior parietal lobule, a
region located at the temporoparietal junction and considered a high-level multimodal integration hub.
The nosological validity of the syndrome has historically been contested. Benton (1992) questioned whether the cooccurrence of the four signs was more frequent than would be expected by chance, arguing that they might represent independent
deficits grouped together by anatomical proximity. However, later functional neuroimaging studies revealed that the angular
gyrus is in fact a multimodal hub that participates simultaneously in numerical processing, finger representation, spatial
orientation, and writing, providing a neuroanatomical basis for the coherence of the syndrome (Altabakhi & Liang, 2023).

4.1.2 The developmental form: atypical maturation without focal lesion
In the pediatric literature, Kinsbourne and Warrington (1963) were the first to systematically describe "Developmental
Gerstmann Syndrome" (DGS) in children with learning difficulties without an apparent focal lesion. This seminal description
showed that the same constellation of symptoms observed in adults with parietal lesions can emerge from atypical maturation of
parietal networks during neurodevelopment, even in the absence of evident structural damage. Subsequently, Benson and
Geschwind (1970) expanded this characterization by reinforcing that DGS constitutes a distinct clinical entity, marked by relative
preservation of reading (a feature that differentiates it from pure dyslexia), in addition to presenting its own neuropsychological
profile.
Altabakhi and Liang (2023) emphasize that, in the developmental form, diagnosis is particularly challenging in the early
stages of childhood, since the parietal lobes undergo slow and progressive functional maturation. The four classic signs rarely
present completely and simultaneously in the child; partial or spectral variants are more common, in which two or three
components of the tetrad are present to varying degrees of intensity. This characteristic often makes diagnosis late or
underestimated, since each isolated component may be interpreted as a specific and independent learning difficulty.
A recent case report published in the Journal of the International Neuropsychological Society (Mow & Toomey, 2025)
documented DGS in a young adult woman with no history of neurological injury who was seeking academic accommodations
for mathematics difficulties in a university context. Neuropsychological assessment revealed impaired mathematical
performance, right-left disorientation, mirror writing, mild digital agnosia, and impaired visuomotor coordination, configuring a
pattern consistent with DGS and evidencing compensatory strategies developed over a lifetime. For the present work, this case
is a useful illustration: it shows that DGS can persist into adulthood in an underdiagnosed manner, causing significant functional
impact, and that standardized assessment protocols specifically designed for this population still do not exist.
Briot et al. (2026), in a systematic review published in the Journal of Neural Transmission, analyzed cases of DGS in
the context of complex neurodevelopmental disorders, including cases associated with subcortical stroke. The authors identified
that DGS frequently coexists with ASD, ADHD, and other neurodevelopmental conditions, and that a multidisciplinary approach
is essential for appropriate management. This finding reinforces the hypothesis that DGS is not an isolated nosological entity,
but part of a broader neuropsychiatric spectrum.

4.1.3 The angular gyrus as a multimodal hub: recent neuroimaging evidence
The current understanding of the angular gyrus as a multimodal integration hub is central to interpreting the overlap
between DGS and dyscalculia. Functional neuroimaging studies demonstrate that the angular gyrus simultaneously participates
in: (a) retrieval of arithmetic facts stored in long-term verbal memory; (b) orthographic processing and word reading; (c)
somatosensory representation of the fingers and digital gnosis; (d) spatial orientation and right-left discrimination; and (e)
integration of visual, auditory, and proprioceptive information (Altabakhi & Liang, 2023).

This multifunctionality partly explains why developmental dyscalculia is frequently accompanied by dyslexia,
dysgraphia, and spatial orientation difficulties (Han, 2025). Alterations in angular gyrus functioning, resulting from atypical
maturation, reduced functional connectivity, or decreased gray matter volume, can simultaneously compromise multiple
cognitive domains that depend on this important cortical integration region. Stanescu-Cosson et al. (2000) used neuroimaging to
show that the angular gyrus is especially activated during the retrieval of exact arithmetic facts, whereas the intraparietal sulcus
is more involved in approximate magnitude processing. This functional dissociation suggests that dyscalculia may have
neuroanatomically distinct subtypes: one subtype predominantly involving intraparietal sulcus dysfunction (deficit in number
sense and magnitude) and another predominantly involving angular gyrus dysfunction (deficit in fact retrieval and symbolic
integration), with the latter most closely resembling the profile observed in the patients of this series.
Recent functional neuroimaging evidence reinforces and refines this model. Starling-Alves et al. (2025), in a study
published in Scientific Reports, investigated resting-state functional connectivity (resting-state fMRI) in 46 first-grade children
with mathematical difficulties and 60 with typical performance. The results revealed a pattern characterized by hyperconnectivity
between the right subdivision of the intraparietal sulcus (hIP2) and the left hippocampus, concomitant with hypoconnectivity
between the PGa subdivision of the right angular gyrus and the ventral occipitotemporal cortex. This connectivity profile, which
combines increased and decreased functional connections across different circuits, suggests that mathematical difficulties do not
arise from a simple decrease in parietal activity. Instead, they reflect an atypical reorganization of the neural networks involved
in numerical processing, which helps explain the clinical heterogeneity observed in dyscalculia.
Schwizer Ashkenazi et al. (2025), in a study published in the Journal of Neuroscience Research, investigated effective
connectivity (that is, causal and directional interactions) among six regions of the numerical processing neural network, including
the anterior intraparietal sulcus, the premotor cortex, and the insula, in children with dyscalculia and children with typical
development. To do so, the authors employed Dynamic Causal Modeling (DCM). The results showed that the supplementary
premotor area (preSMA) plays a central orchestrating role in processing numerical order, and that children with dyscalculia
present aberrant modulation of input connectivity to the ventral premotor cortex, even in tasks in which behavioral performance
was preserved. In other words, the neural differences associated with dyscalculia are broader and deeper than behavioral
performance alone would suggest, affecting the dynamic organization of fronto-parietal networks.
Our findings corroborate this spectral, multiple-neural-network-based perspective. The patients assessed did not present
the complete DGS tetrad, but exhibited different combinations of its clinical components, in a pattern consistent with the
heterogeneous developmental parietal dysfunction described by recent neuroimaging studies. The overlap between DGS and
dyscalculia is therefore not merely descriptive: it points to a shared neurological substrate broader than that proposed by the
model centered on the intraparietal sulcus and the Approximate Number System (Dehaene et al., 2003; Stanescu-Cosson et al.,
2000), and provides a neuroanatomical foundation for the General Psychiatric Disorder hypothesis.

4.2 Comorbidity, shared mechanisms, and the crisis of diagnostic categories
The high rate of comorbidities observed in the patients, including signs of ADHD, autism spectrum disorder traits,
dysgraphia, and difficulties related to relational language, should not be interpreted as mere clinical coincidence. On the contrary,
this pattern is best understood as a manifestation of pathophysiological mechanisms shared among these disorders. Bonti et al.
(2024) argue that the phenotypic overlap among ADHD, Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), and Specific Learning Disorders is
so pronounced that it calls into question the clinical utility of rigidly separated diagnostic categories, proposing that these
disorders share common genetic and neurobiological pathways.
Corroborating this transdiagnostic perspective, Cai et al. (2025) found, in a longitudinal twin study, that children with
dyscalculia already presented, at 12 years of age, difficulties in multiple domains beyond mathematics, including attention, social

Disorder hypothesis, since it shows that dyscalculia is not a static and circumscribed phenomenon, but a dynamic condition

In the specific domain of working memory, Mohammed et al. (2024) reported, in a sample of 60 children, that the
dyscalculia group presented significant impairment of the visuospatial sketchpad, and that this deficit correlated directly with
difficulties in mathematical symbols and calculation. These data reinforce the findings of Szucs et al. (2013), who had already
identified visuospatial deficits as a dominant feature of dyscalculia, and broaden understanding by showing that this relationship
is replicable across different cultural and population contexts.
Comorbidity between dyscalculia and dyslexia deserves special attention. Han (2025) synthesized a decade of evidence
showing that both share deficits in working memory, visual perception, and spatial processing, with overlapping neural networks,
especially in the temporoparietal and occipital regions. This overlap suggests that these two conditions do not simply constitute
independent comorbidities, but rather represent distinct phenotypic expressions of a common neurodevelopmental vulnerability,
reinforcing one of the central arguments of the General Psychiatric Disorder hypothesis.
At the level of embodied cognition, Fischer et al. (2022) demonstrated that digital gnosis specifically predicts tasks
involving embodied number representation, whereas fine motor skills predict counting tasks. The distinction matters: it shows
that the relationship between the body and number is not generic, but specific and dissociable, since digital gnosis is an
independent predictor of numerical representation. This strengthens the hypothesis that the digital agnosia observed in the
patients is not an epiphenomenon, but a causal component in the genesis of dyscalculia.
This view proposes that a child's use of the fingers acts as a substrate for the development of number sense, meaning
that the motor-sensory deficit is not a mere side effect, but a causal component. The failure to recognize and the inability to
differentiate the fingers (digital agnosia) are directly correlated with deficits in magnitude processing and in performing basic
calculations, whose neurobiological basis is described in cognitive science and in psychiatry/neurology through Gerstmann
syndrome, already discussed above, which is related to lesions or dysfunctions in the left angular gyrus and adjacent regions of
the parietal lobe, the anatomical pathway responsible for integrating visual, spatial, motor, and symbolic information (Fischer,
2008).
In psychiatry, neuropsychology, and the study of Specific Learning Disorders such as dyscalculia, it is understood that
these findings call for integrating two theoretical frameworks. The "Manual-Numerical" Cognitive Theory cited above holds that
mental numerical representations are deeply intertwined with the sensorimotor system of the fingers, such that children who
receive training focused on finger representation significantly improve their numerical performance. This must be read together
with Stanislas Dehaene's Triple Code Model, in which mathematical knowledge depends on non-symbolic analog magnitude, a
verbal representation, and an Arabic visual representation, and with the disruption of that model observed in dyscalculia. This
reinforces that the neurodevelopmental disorder should not be seen merely as a failure of "mathematical logic," but as a more
systemic alteration in body representation and body schema. The inability to map the fingers generates a cascade of impairments
that affects the understanding of quantities, visuospatial ability, and, consequently, arithmetic competence (Pajot et al., 2025).

In this model, the deficit in number construction is not the central problem, but the phenotypic expression of profound
alterations in underlying cognitive systems. These systems include: (1) body schema and digital gnosis, mediated by the mirrorneuron system and the angular gyrus; (2) visuospatial working memory, anchored in the intraparietal sulcus and dorsolateral
frontal regions; (3) lateralization and spatial orientation, functions of the bilateral inferior parietal lobule; and (4) the capacity to
process relational and quasi-spatial syntax, associated with the parietal and prefrontal cortex.
The clinical heterogeneity observed among the patients, reflected in different combinations and degrees of intensity of
these components, is consistent with the hypothesis that General Psychiatric Disorder does not correspond to a single nosological
entity. Instead, it represents a spectrum of neurodevelopmental vulnerabilities that manifests variably in each individual. This
conception is close to the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) model of the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH), which
proposes organizing mental disorders by functional domains and underlying biological mechanisms rather than discrete
syndromic categories.
Under this matrix model, basic cognitive processes (such as numerical processing, subitizing, and the mental number
line) do not fail due to a purely "defective part" in the mathematical brain. Instead, the failure is viewed within a broader context
involving:

•

a) Shared vulnerability: Developmental dyscalculia shares genetic, neurobiological, and environmental factors
with other neurodevelopmental conditions, such as Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), dyslexia,
and Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD);

system (Haberstroh & Schulte-Körne, 2019).

From a clinical standpoint, this approach has direct and immediate therapeutic implications. An intervention centered
exclusively on repetitive arithmetic training is insufficient when the underlying mechanisms involve deficits in digital gnosis,
working memory, and spatial processing. Effective interventions should be multidimensional and include: (a) activities to
strengthen body schema and digital gnosis, such as tactile finger discrimination exercises; (b) visuospatial working memory
training supported by concrete manipulatives; (c) spatial orientation and laterality activities; and (d) explicit work with relational
language and quasi-spatial structures.

4.4 Study limitations
The present study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The clinical sample is small
(n = 5) and composed exclusively of female patients, which limits the generalizability of the findings. The absence of a formal
control group and standardized neuropsychological assessment instruments precludes definitive causal conclusions.
Furthermore, the proposed interpretive matrix constitutes a clinical hypothesis that, although consistent with the reviewed
literature, has not yet been subjected to prospective empirical validation with larger cohorts and controlled experimental
methodology. Future studies should include functional neuroimaging, genomic assessment, and longitudinal follow-up to test
the predictions derived from the proposed model.

5. Conclusion
Longitudinal observation of patients with dyscalculia reveals that mathematical difficulty rarely comes alone. It appears
to involve fundamental alterations in spatiality, digital gnosis, working memory, body schema, relational language, and social
reciprocity.
The proposed interpretive matrix suggests that dyscalculia should be understood within a broader neuropsychiatric
spectrum. Although consistent with recent evidence on embodied cognition and neurodevelopmental comorbidities, we
emphasize that this model constitutes a clinical hypothesis requiring rigorous empirical validation in future studies with larger
cohorts. Understanding dyscalculia requires studying not only the mathematical brain, but the body, space, attention, and
language in a systemic and integrated manner.
