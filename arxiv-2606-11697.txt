On Finite and Infinite Decompositions of Zero Mean Curvature Graphs
Priyank Vasu, Sam K Mathew, Rahul Kumar Singh, Rukmini Dey

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate finite and infinite decomposition formulas for zero mean curvature (ZMC) graphs in three-dimensional spaces with different metrics, including Euclidean space, Lorentz–Minkowski space, and isotropic 3 3 -space. We first derive new Euler–Ramanujan-type identities yielding decompositions for the conjugate of Scherk’s first minimal surface in terms of dilated catenoids. We then employ Weierstrass factorisation and power series techniques to obtain infinite decompositions for a broad class of ZMC graphs in isotropic 3 3 -space into helicoids, logarithmoids of revolution, and Enneper surfaces. We further extend these decomposition results to larger families of ZMC surfaces across these spaces by considering surfaces arising from the López–Ross transformation, Bonnet rotation, and a one-parameter family of metric deformations. We also investigate finite decompositions, establishing finite analogues of Scherk tower decompositions in both Euclidean and isotropic settings. In addition, we prove a theorem characterising finite decompositions of isotropic minimal surfaces. Finally, we discuss applications of the resulting decomposition theory to lamellar structures.



1
Introduction

It is now a well established fact that a connection exists between the height functions of certain zero mean curvature surfaces in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{E}^{3}:=(\mathbb{R}^{3},dx^{2}+dy^{2}+dz^{2})
and the Lorentz-Minkowski space
\mathbb{E}_{1}^{3}:=(\mathbb{R}^{3},dx^{2}+dy^{2}-dz^{2})
and some specific Euler-Ramanujan identities. The first such connection appeared in a paper by the physicist R. Kamien
[
11
]
, where a particular Euler-Ramanujan identity was used to show that Scherk’s first minimal surface in
\mathbb{E}^{3}
can be decomposed into an infinite superposition of dilated helicoids.

Further connections between Euler-Ramanujan identities and zero mean curvature surfaces were discovered by R. Dey and collaborators
[
9
,
8
,
7
]
. Using the Weierstrass-Enneper representation for certain classes of zero mean curvature minimal and maximal surfaces in
\mathbb{E}^{3}
, as well as Born-Infeld solitons, they derived new complex identities via specific Euler-Ramanujan identities. In a related work, M. S. Lone
[
12
]
established relationships between Euler-Ramanujan identities and a generalised class of minimal translation surfaces known as affine minimal translation surfaces. Kamien
[
11
]
showed that a family of Scherk’s first minimal surfaces admits finite decompositions into dilated copies of itself. Building on this idea, Dey et al.
[
6
]
obtained new finite decomposition formulas for Scherk’s first and second minimal surfaces and, via the Weierstrass–Enneper representation, for certain minimal and maximal surfaces. More recently, Paul et al.
[
13
]
used Euler–Ramanujan identities and Wick rotations to represent a one-parameter family of Scherk-type zero mean curvature surfaces in
\mathbb{E}_{1}^{3}
as infinite superpositions of dilated helicoids, while also deriving several finite decomposition formulas.

Despite these developments, the existing decomposition results are largely confined to specific examples since the underlying constructions depend heavily on particular Euler–Ramanujan identities. Consequently, known decomposition formulas are often derived on a case-by-case basis. In the present work, we adopt a different perspective by seeking a geometric framework for such decompositions that is independent of any specific Euler–Ramanujan identity. This approach allows us to identify broader classes of zero mean curvature surfaces exhibiting decomposition and provides a unified viewpoint for understanding previously known examples.

To motivate the present work, we recall that every minimal surface admits an associated conjugate minimal surface obtained by replacing each coordinate function with its harmonic conjugate in a conformal parametrisation.

The preceding developments naturally raise the following problem.

Question 1.

Does the conjugate surface of Scherk first minimal surface admit a Kamien-type decomposition? More precisely, can its height function be expressed as an infinite superposition of suitably dilated copies of the conjugate surface of helicoid?

The first objective of this paper is to answer Question 1. In Section 2, we establish such a decomposition between Scherk’s tower and
the catenoid.

Having established a decomposition for a particular surface, it is natural to investigate whether such representations occur more generally within the class of zero mean curvature surfaces. This motivates the following problem.

Question 2.

Which classes of zero mean curvature surfaces admit representations as infinite superpositions of suitably dilated surfaces? Can one characterize a family of surfaces for which Kamien-type decompositions hold?

Question 2 forms the central theme of the remainder of this paper. In Section 3, we investigate this question in the setting of a isotropic three-space and identify a family of isotropic minimal surfaces that admit such decomposition. We then extend the underlying framework to the Euclidean and Lorentz–Minkowski settings, obtaining analogous results for minimal and maximal surfaces. These results demonstrate that the decomposition is not confined to isolated examples but arises within a considerably broader geometric framework.

Motivated by Kamien’s finite decomposition
[
11
]
, one may further ask whether such finite representations occur more generally among zero mean curvature surfaces. This leads to the following question.

Question 3.

Which classes of zero mean curvature surfaces admit finite decompositions into suitably dilated copies of themselves?

In Section 5, we investigate this problem and identify broad classes of zero mean curvature surfaces that possess such finite decomposition formulas.

The paper is organised as follows.

In the second section, we motivate a theory of decompositions of graphical ZMC surfaces by examining the toy example of the decomposition of Scherk’s tower into catenoids in Euclidean space and Lorentzian space. We discover several identities that give an infinite decomposition of Scherk’s tower into catenoids in these two spaces. The existence of such identities points toward a space where a very clean decomposition can be found. This is precisely where the isotropic 3-space fits into our picture. We find clean identities that give a decomposition of the isotropic Scherk’s tower into a logarithmoid of revolution, which is an isotropic minimal surface (ZMC surface in isotropic 3-space. The striking thing about the graphical isotropic minimal surfaces are that they are harmonic functions). The results in this section motivate the use of the Weierstrass product formula for holomorphic functions in the coming sections. This section also provides a reason to delve deeper into the decomposition of graphical ZMC surfaces in isotropic 3-space.

In the third section, we prove various theorems regarding the decomposition of graphical ZMC surfaces in isotropic 3-space. We first consider the case where the height function of the surface is of the form
\operatorname{Re}(\ln(f(x+iy)))
or
\operatorname{Im}(\ln(f(x+iy)))
. It should be noted that this case is quite general; indeed, the examples of isotropic minimal surfaces given in the second section are of this form. To this end, we apply the idea of the Weierstrass product theorem and prove that dilated helicoids and dilated logarithmoids of revolution can be thought of as the building blocks of isotropic minimal surfaces, respectively, for the two forms of isotropic minimal surfaces we consider. We also see that every isotropic minimal surface can be decomposed into generalized Enneper surfaces; this is done using the power series representation of every holomorphic function. Finally, we apply a Bonnet rotation and the López–Ross deformation to graphical isotropic minimal surfaces and observe that there exists a rotation that is composed of dilated helicoids and dilated logarithmoids of revolution, with both types of terms appearing and their contributions weighted by
\cos(\theta)
and
\sin(\theta)
, respectively.

Next, in the fourth section, we take a graphical isotropic minimal surface and deform the metric by a single real parameter
c
, and then attempt to see the decomposition in this space with the metric parametrised by
c
. We observe that a decomposition similar to the one above is possible, with the only difference being that the building blocks here are no longer just dilated and rotated helicoids, logarithmoids of revolution, or generalized Enneper surfaces; rather, these are composed with a function that may not have a geometric meaning as simple as dilation or rotation.

In the fifth section, unlike the sections above, we deal with the finite decomposition theory. Similar to the second section, we first address the finite decomposition of Scherk’s tower in terms of dilated versions of itself in the Euclidean and isotropic cases. We also provide a theorem that gives a finite decomposition of isotropic minimal surfaces. In the next section, we present an application of this theory to the study of twisted grain boundaries.

2
A Concrete Example: Decomposition of Scherk’s Tower into Catenoids

As discussed in the introduction, it is known that Scherk’s doubly periodic minimal surface admits a decomposition in terms of helicoids via an Euler-Ramanujan identity. Recall that the conjugate surface of Scherk’s first surface is Scherk’s tower, while the conjugate surface of the helicoid is the catenoid. In this section, we investigate the analogous decomposition relation between their conjugate surfaces, namely Scherk’s tower and the Catenoid.

Before going into the further details, for the sake of completeness, we give the following well-known terminologies. A surface
S
in Euclidean space
\mathbb{E}^{3}
is minimal if it can be expressed locally as the graph of a smooth function
f(x,y)
satisfying the minimal surface equation:

(1+f_{x}^{2})f_{yy}-2f_{x}f_{y}f_{xy}+(1+f_{y}^{2})f_{xx}=0.

(1)

Analogously, a zero mean curvature surface in Lorentz-Minkowski space
\mathbb{E}_{1}^{3}
, when its height function
g(x,y)
is defined over a domain in the spacelike
xy
-plane, satisfies the maximal surface equation:

(1-g_{x}^{2})\,g_{yy}+2g_{x}g_{y}\,g_{xy}+(1-g_{y}^{2})\,g_{xx}=0.

(2)

2.1
Decomposition in Euclidean Space
\mathbb{E}^{3}

In this subsection, we investigate the decomposition relation between Scherk’s tower and the catenoid in three-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{E}^{3}
. We begin with the infinite product representation of the sine function:

\sin a=a\prod_{k\neq 0}\left(\frac{k\pi+a}{k\pi}\right).

(3)

Substituting
a=x+iy
and taking the natural logarithm of both sides yields:

\ln\left[\sin(x+iy)\right]=\ln(x+iy)+\sum_{k\neq 0}\ln\left[\frac{k\pi+x+iy}{k\pi}\right].

(4)

For notational convenience, let
x^{k}=x+\pi k
. Using the standard identity
\ln(x+iy)=\ln\left[\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right]+i\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)
, we obtain:

\sum_{k\neq 0}\ln\left[\frac{x^{k}+iy}{\pi k}\right]+\ln(x+iy)=\sum_{k\neq 0}\ln\left[\sqrt{\frac{(x^{k})^{2}+y^{2}}{\pi^{2}k^{2}}}\right]+\ln\left[\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right]+i\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{x^{k}}\right).

Consequently, we arrive at:

\ln\left[\sin(x+iy)\right]=\sum_{k\neq 0}\ln\left[\sqrt{\frac{(x^{k})^{2}+y^{2}}{\pi^{2}k^{2}}}\right]+\ln\left[\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right]+i\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{x^{k}}\right).

(5)

The imaginary part of the left-hand side is
\tan^{-1}\left[\tanh(y)\cot(x)\right]
. Equating this with the imaginary part of the right-hand side yields the celebrated Euler-Ramanujan identity:

\tan^{-1}\left[\tanh\alpha\cot\beta\right]=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta+k\pi}\right),

(6)

which, as demonstrated in previous works, provides an infinite decomposition of Scherk’s first surface in terms of helicoids.

We now consider the real part of equation
5
. First, we compute the real part of
\ln\left[\sin(x+iy)\right]
. Using the identity
\sin(x+iy)=\sin(x)\cosh(y)+i\cos(x)\sinh(y)
, we have:

\real\left[\ln\left[\sin(x+iy)\right]\right]=\ln\left[\sqrt{\sin^{2}(x)\cosh^{2}(y)+\cos^{2}(x)\sinh^{2}(y)}\right].
The expression under the square root can be simplified by employing
\cosh^{2}(y)=1+\sinh^{2}(y)
and
\cos^{2}(x)=1-\sin^{2}(x)
:
\real\left[\ln\left[\sin(x+iy)\right]\right]=\ln\left[\sqrt{\sin^{2}(x)+\sinh^{2}(y)}\right].
Equating this with the real part of the right-hand side of equation
5
yields:

\sum_{k\neq 0}\ln\left[\sqrt{\frac{(x^{k})^{2}+y^{2}}{\pi^{2}k^{2}}}\right]+\ln\left[\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right]=\ln\left[\sqrt{\sin^{2}(x)+\sinh^{2}(y)}\right].

Exponentiating both sides and squaring eliminates the logarithm and square root, giving:

(x^{2}+y^{2})\cdot\prod_{k\neq 0}\left[\frac{(x+k\pi)^{2}+y^{2}}{\pi^{2}k^{2}}\right]=\sin^{2}(x)+\sinh^{2}(y).

(7)

This we name as the
Conjugate Euler-Ramanujan Identity
.
Setting
y=0
in equation
7
yields the familiar product representation for
\sin^{2}(x)
:

x^{2}\cdot\prod_{k\neq 0}\left[\frac{(x+k\pi)^{2}}{\pi^{2}k^{2}}\right]=\sin^{2}(x).

(8)

Having established these analytic identities, we now turn to their geometric interpretation. Consider the implicit surface defined by:

\cosh^{2}(az)-\sinh^{2}(ay)-2\sin^{2}(bx)-c=0.

We determine the parameters
a
,
b
, and
c
for which this equation represents Scherk’s tower. Using the identities
\cosh^{2}(A)=1+\sinh^{2}(A)
and
\sin^{2}(A)=\frac{1-\cos(2A)}{2}
, together with the hyperbolic identity
\sinh^{2}(A)-\sinh^{2}(B)=\sinh(A+B)\sinh(A-B)
(see
[
10
]
, section 2.5.1.3, equation 8), we rewrite the surface as:
\sinh(a(z+y))\sinh(a(z-y))+\cos(2bx)-c=0.
Choosing
c=0
and
b=1/2
simplifies this to:
\cos(x)=\sinh(a(y+z))\sinh(a(y-z)).

Observe that the equation:
\cos(x)=\sinh\left(\frac{y+z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\sinh\left(\frac{y-z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
corresponds to Scherk’s tower rotated by
45^{\circ}
in the
yz
-plane. Thus, we choose
a=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
, which yields the implicit equation for Scherk’s tower:

\cosh^{2}\left(\tfrac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)-\sinh^{2}\left(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)-2\sin^{2}\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right)=0.

Solving for
z
gives the explicit graphical representation:

f_{\text{ScherkTower}}=\sqrt{2}\cosh^{-1}\Big[\sqrt{\sinh^{2}\left(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+2\sin^{2}\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right)}\,\Big].

(9)

Consequently, Scherk’s tower must satisfy the relation:
\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{f_{\text{ScherkTower}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+2\sin^{2}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right).
Applying equations
7
and
8
to this relation, we obtain:

\Big[\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}\Big]\cdot\prod_{k\neq 0}\Bigg[\frac{\left(\tfrac{x}{2}+k\pi\right)^{2}+\left(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}}{\pi^{2}k^{2}}\Bigg]=\sinh^{2}\left(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\sin^{2}\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right).

(10)

We now define a family of surfaces given implicitly by:

\cosh^{2}(z_{k})=\frac{\left(\tfrac{x}{2}+k\pi\right)^{2}+\left(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}}{\pi^{2}k^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\cosh^{2}(z_{0})=\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}.

(11)

These correspond precisely to dilated catenoids. Their explicit graphical representations are given by:

f^{k}_{\text{Dil.Cat}}=\cosh^{-1}\Bigg[\sqrt{\frac{\left(\tfrac{x}{2}+k\pi\right)^{2}+(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}})^{2}}{\pi^{2}k^{2}}}\;\Bigg]\quad\text{and}\quad f^{0}_{\text{Dil.Cat}}=\cosh^{-1}\Big[\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}}\Big].

(12)

From equations
12
and
10
, we deduce:

\prod_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\cosh^{2}\left(f^{k}_{\text{Dil.Cat}}\right)=\sinh^{2}\left(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\sin^{2}\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\prod_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\cosh^{2}\left(f^{k}_{\text{Dil.Cat}}\right)\Big|_{(x,0)}=\sin^{2}\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right).

(13)

Combining this result with the graphical representation of Scherk’s tower from equation
9
yields the following.

Theorem 2.1
.

Catenoid-Scherk’s Tower Identities

\prod_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\cosh^{2}\left(f^{k}_{\text{Dil.Cat}}\right)\Bigg|_{(x,y)}+\prod_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\cosh^{2}\left(f^{k}_{\text{Dil.Cat}}\right)\Bigg|_{(x,0)}=\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{f_{\text{ScherkTower}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\Bigg|_{(x,y)}

(14)

\prod_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\cosh^{2}\left(f^{k}_{\text{Dil.Cat}}\right)\Bigg|_{(0,y)}+2\prod_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\cosh^{2}\left(f^{k}_{\text{Dil.Cat}}\right)\Bigg|_{(x,0)}=\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{f_{\text{ScherkTower}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\Bigg|_{(x,y)}

(15)

Remark 2.2
.

2.2
Decomposition in Lorentzian Space
\mathbb{E}_{1}^{3}
via Wick Rotation

The main tool we employ for the decomposition in Lorentz-Minkowski space is the well-known
Wick rotation
. This technique can be briefly stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.3
.

[
2
]

Let
f(x,y)
be a solution to (
1
) in
\mathbb{E}^{3}
. If
f
is even (resp. odd) with respect to the
x
-and
y
-variables, then its Wick rotation
g(x,y)=f(ix,iy)
(resp.
g(x,y)=-f(ix,iy))
is a real solution to (
2
) in
\mathbb{E}_{1}^{3}
which is spacelike at least near the origin
o=(0,0)
and conversely.

From equation (
9
), we have
f_{ScherkTower}=z=\sqrt{2}\cosh^{-1}\big[\sqrt{\sinh^{2}(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}})+2\sin^{2}(\tfrac{x}{2})}\,\big]
, which can be rewritten as
\cosh^{2}(\tfrac{z}{\sqrt{2}})-\sinh^{2}(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}})=2\sin^{2}(\tfrac{x}{2})
. We then interchange the
x
and
z
variables; this rotation corresponds to a relabeling of coordinates and preserves the zero mean curvature property because the minimal surface equation is invariant under permutations of the coordinate axes when the surface is expressed as a graph over different coordinate planes (see
[
2
]
for more details). This yields
\cosh^{2}(\tfrac{x}{\sqrt{2}})-\sinh^{2}(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}})=2\sin^{2}(\tfrac{z}{2})
, which can be expressed graphically as
z=2\sin^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\cosh^{2}(\tfrac{x}{\sqrt{2}})-\sinh^{2}(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}})}
. The resulting function is even in both
x
and
y
, satisfying the parity condition required for Wick rotation. Applying the Wick rotation to this yields a spacelike solution to (
2
) corresponding to Scherk’s tower:
z=2\sin^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\cosh^{2}(\tfrac{ix}{\sqrt{2}})-\sinh^{2}(\tfrac{iy}{\sqrt{2}})}
. Using the identities
\cosh(ix)=\cos(x)
and
\sinh(iy)=i\sin(y)
, we obtain the explicit form of what we shall henceforth call the
Spacelike Scherk Tower
:

f_{\text{Spacelike Scherk Tower}}=2\sin^{-1}\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\cos^{2}\big(\tfrac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\big)+\sin^{2}\big(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\big)\big)}.

(16)

Implicitly, the spacelike Scherk tower can be expressed as
2\sin^{2}\left(\frac{f_{\text{Spacelike Scherk Tower}}}{2}\right)=\cos^{2}\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\sin^{2}\!\left(\frac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
. Applying equation (
8
) together with the identity
\cos^{2}\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=1-\sin^{2}\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
, we obtain:

\cos^{2}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\sin^{2}\!\left(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=1-\left(\tfrac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}\cdot\prod_{k\neq 0}\left[\frac{(\tfrac{x}{\sqrt{2}}+k\pi)^{2}}{\pi^{2}k^{2}}\right]+\left(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}\cdot\prod_{k\neq 0}\left[\frac{(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}}+k\pi)^{2}}{\pi^{2}k^{2}}\right]

(17)

Recall that the spacelike elliptic catenoid, a maximal surface, is defined by the equation
z=\sinh^{-1}\left(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right)
. Accordingly, we define the following families of dilated elliptic catenoids:

f^{k}_{\text{Dil.S.E.Cat(x)}}=\sinh^{-1}\Bigg[\sqrt{\tfrac{\left(\tfrac{x}{2}+k\pi\right)^{2}+\left(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}}{\pi^{2}k^{2}}}\;\Bigg]\quad\text{and}\quad f^{0}_{\text{Dil.S.E.Cat(x)}}=\sinh^{-1}\Big[\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}}\;\Big]

f^{k}_{\text{Dil.S.E.Cat(y)}}=\sinh^{-1}\Bigg[\sqrt{\tfrac{\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}}+k\pi\right)^{2}}{\pi^{2}k^{2}}}\;\Bigg]\quad\text{and}\quad f^{0}_{\text{Dil.S.E.Cat(y)}}=\sinh^{-1}\Big[\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}}\;\Big]

From the above definitions together with equation (
17
), we deduce:

1-\prod_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\sinh^{2}\left(f^{k}_{\text{Dil.S.E.Cat(x)}}\right)\Big|_{(x,0)}+\prod_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\sinh^{2}\left(f^{k}_{\text{Dil.S.E.Cat(y)}}\right)\Big|_{(0,y)}=\cos^{2}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\sin^{2}\!\left(\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right).

Combining this with equation (

) yields the following:

Theorem 2.4
.

Spacelike Elliptic Catenoid–Spacelike Scherk Tower Identity

1-\prod_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\sinh^{2}\left(f^{k}_{\text{Dil.S.E.Cat(x)}}\right)\Big|_{(x,0)}+\prod_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\sinh^{2}\left(f^{k}_{\text{Dil.S.E.Cat(y)}}\right)\Big|_{(0,y)}=2\sin^{2}\left(\frac{f_{\text{Spacelike ScherkTower}}}{2}\right)\Bigg|_{(x,y)}

(18)

.

.

The decomposition of Scherk’s surface into catenoids in both the spaces
\mathbb{E}^{3}

2.3
Decomposition in the Isotropic Space
\mathbb{I}^{3}

The isotropic space
\mathbb{I}^{3}
is defined as
\mathbb{R}^{3}
equipped with the degenerate metric
ds^{2}=dx^{2}+dy^{2}
. We refer to the zero mean curvature (ZMC) surfaces in this space as isotropic minimal surfaces. There are two variants of the Weierstrass-Enneper representation for an isotropic minimal surface. The first is referred to as the
minimal normal representation
, while the second is known as the
parabolic normal representation
. (see
[
4
,
1
]
for more details) We shall work with the minimal normal representation, given by:

X_{1}=\operatorname{Re}\int^{z}\left(F,-iF,2FG\right)\,dw

(19)

Let us consider the specific Weierstrass data
(F,G)=(1,\tfrac{1}{2}\cot z)
. We name isotropic minimal surface given by minimal normal representation and its conjugate with this Weierstrass Data to be Isotropic Scherk’s Tower and Isotropic Scherk’s Surface. Using this same Weierstrass data, we will now determine the corresponding isotropic minimal surfaces.

X_{1}(z,\bar{z})=\operatorname{Re}\int^{z}\left(1,-i,\cot z\right)\,dw\quad\text{and}\quad Y_{1}(z,\bar{z})=\operatorname{Im}\int^{z}\left(1,-i,\cot z\right)\,dw

This yields the following surfaces:

X_{1}(z)=\left(x,y,\operatorname{Re}[\ln(\sin z)]\right)\quad\text{and}\quad Y_{1}(z)=\left(y,-x,\operatorname{Im}[\ln(\sin z)]\right).

Applying the identity from equation
5
, we obtain:

\operatorname{Re}[\ln(\sin z)]=\sum_{k\neq 0}\ln\sqrt{\tfrac{(x+k\pi)^{2}+y^{2}}{k^{2}\pi^{2}}}+\ln\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{Im}[\ln(\sin z)]=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\tan^{-1}\left(\tfrac{y}{x-k\pi}\right)

(20)

Consider the Weierstrass data
(F,G)=(1,1/2z)
. While this data generates the classical catenoid and helicoid in Euclidean space, it yields the
logarithmoid of revolution
and the
isotropic helicoid
within the isotropic setting. The logarithmoid of revolution, defined by the graph
f(x,y)=\ln\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
, serves as the isotropic analogue of the Euclidean catenoid. By defining the dilated and translated logarithmoids as

f^{\mathbb{I}^{3}}_{\mathcal{L}^{(k)}}(x,y)=\ln\sqrt{\tfrac{(x+k\pi)^{2}+y^{2}}{k^{2}\pi^{2}}}\quad\text{for }k\neq 0,\quad\text{and}\quad f^{\mathbb{I}^{3}}_{\mathcal{L}^{(0)}}(x,y)=\ln\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}},

it follows from equation
20
and the definitions of the isotropic Scherk tower and isotropic Scherk’s doubly periodic minimal surface that the isotropic Scherk tower admits a decomposition into an infinite sum of these dilated logarithmoids. Analogously, the isotropic Scherk’s doubly periodic minimal surface can be expressed as an infinite sum of dilated isotropic helicoids.

To summarize, let
f^{\mathbb{I}^{3}}_{\mathcal{S}_{\text{tower}}}
denote the isotropic Scherk tower,
f^{\mathbb{I}^{3}}_{\mathcal{S}_{\text{surf}}}
the isotropic Scherk’s doubly periodic minimal surface,
f^{\mathbb{I}^{3}}_{\mathcal{L}^{(k)}}
the dilated logarithmoids of revolution, and
f^{\mathbb{I}^{3}}_{\mathcal{H}^{(k)}}
the dilated helicoids. We arrive at the following identities:

Theorem 2.7
.

a)
The Isotropic Scherk Tower–Logarithmoid of revolution Identity:

f^{\mathbb{I}^{3}}_{\mathcal{S}_{\text{tower}}}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f^{\mathbb{I}^{3}}_{\mathcal{L}^{(k)}}

b)
The Isotropic Scherk Surface–Helicoid Identity:

f^{\mathbb{I}^{3}}_{\mathcal{S}_{\text{surf}}}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f^{\mathbb{I}^{3}}_{\mathcal{H}^{(k)}}

3
Decompositions of Isotropic Minimal Surfaces

In the previous section, we investigated the decompositions of the Scherk tower and the Scherk doubly periodic minimal surface across various spaces. We observed that in the three-dimensional isotropic space
\mathbb{I}^{3}
, these minimal surfaces admit a remarkably clean infinite sum decomposition. In this section, we explore various types of decompositions for minimal surfaces within
\mathbb{I}^{3}
.

We first note that a function
h(x,y)
forms the graph of an isotropic minimal surface if and only if
h(x,y)
satisfies the Laplace equation:
\Delta h=0.
This implies that graphical isotropic minimal surfaces are precisely the harmonic functions.

We employ several approaches to achieve these decompositions. First, we utilize the Weierstrass Product Theorem to derive results that naturally establish decomposition relations between the isotropic analogues of the catenoid, the Scherk tower, the helicoid, and the Scherk doubly periodic surface.

3.1
Decomposition via the Weierstrass Product Theorem

Recall that since all harmonic functions are either the real or imaginary parts of holomorphic functions, every graphical isotropic minimal surface must also be the real or imaginary part of some holomorphic function. In this context, we focus our attention on isotropic minimal surfaces whose height functions are of the form:

h(x,y)=\operatorname{Re}(\ln f(z))\quad\text{or}\quad h(x,y)=\operatorname{Im}(\ln f(z)),

for some holomorphic function
f
. When
h
is defined by the real part, we obtain the following theorem regarding its infinite sum decomposition:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of nonzero complex numbers, and let
f(z)
be an entire function with zeros at
\{a_{n}\}
listed according to their multiplicities. Suppose
f
has a zero of order
k\geq 0
at the origin. If
X
is a graphical isotropic minimal surface with height function
h(x,y)=\operatorname{Re}(\ln f(z))
, then
h
admits a decomposition into the following components:

1.

A finite sum representing
k
logarithmoids of revolution, given by
k\ln|z|
;

2.

An infinite sum of dilated and translated logarithmoids of revolution, where the translation and dilation are determined by the factors
(1-z/a_{n})
associated with the nonzero zeros
a_{n}
of
f
;

3.

An infinite sum of graphical isotropic minimal surface given by harmonic polynomials
\operatorname{Re}(P_{n}(z/a_{n}))
, derived from the Weierstrass primary factors of the Weierstrass Product Formula for
f
;

4.

A graphical isotropic minimal surface given by harmonic function defined by
\operatorname{Re}(g(z))
, where
g(z)
is the entire function appearing in the Weierstrass Product Formula for
f
.

Moreover, since the Weierstrass Product representation of an entire function is not unique, the corresponding decomposition of the isotropic minimal surface is likewise not uniquely determined.

Proof.

Given that
f
is an entire function with the specified zeros, the Weierstrass Factorization Theorem (see Apppendix
7.1
, Theorem:
7.1
) guarantees that
f
can be expressed as:
f(z)=z^{k}e^{g(z)}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\left(\frac{z}{a_{n}}\right).

Taking the logarithm of both sides and extracting the real part, we obtain:

\operatorname{Re}[\ln f(z)]=k\ln|z|+\operatorname{Re}[g(z)]+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\ln\left|1-\frac{z}{a_{n}}\right|+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{Re}\left[P_{n}\left(\frac{z}{a_{n}}\right)\right].

This expression provides the precise decomposition for the height function
h(x,y)=\operatorname{Re}(\ln f(z))
, where each term corresponds to the geometric components identified in the proposition.
∎

Similarly, for the case where
h
is defined by the imaginary part of
\ln f(z)
, we obtain the following result regarding its infinite sum decomposition:

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of nonzero complex numbers, and let
f(z)
be an entire function with zeros at
\{a_{n}\}
listed according to their multiplicities. Suppose
f
has a zero of order
k\geq 0
at the origin. If
X
is a graphical isotropic minimal surface with height function
h(x,y)=\operatorname{Im}(\ln f(z))
, then
h
admits a decomposition into the following components:

1.

A finite sum representing
k
helicoids, given by
k\tan^{-1}(y/x)
;

2.

An infinite sum of dilated and translated helicoids, where each term is given by

\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{a_{n}^{2}x-a_{n}^{1}y}{|a_{n}|^{2}-a_{n}^{1}x-a_{n}^{2}y}\right)

associated with the nonzero zeros
a_{n}=a_{n}^{1}+ia_{n}^{2}
of
f
;

3.

An infinite sum of graphical isotropic minimal surface given by harmonic polynomials
\operatorname{Im}(P_{n}(z/a_{n}))
, derived from the Weierstrass primary factors in the product formula for
f
;

4.

A graphical isotropic minimal surface given by harmonic function defined by
\operatorname{Im}(g(z))
, where
g(z)
is the entire function appearing in the Weierstrass Product Theorem.

Furthermore, as the Weierstrass product representation of an entire function is not unique, the corresponding decomposition of the isotropic minimal surface is likewise not uniquely determined.

Proof.

The proof follows immediately by applying the argument of the previous proposition to the imaginary part of the logarithm of the Weierstrass factorization of
f(z)
. Specifically for (2), the expression for
\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{a_{n}^{2}x-a_{n}^{1}y}{|a_{n}|^{2}-a_{n}^{1}x-a_{n}^{2}y}\right)
can be calculated by expanding the expression for
(1-\tfrac{z}{a_{n}}):=X+iY
and calculating
\tan^{-1}(Y/X)
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

The theorems
3.1
and
3.2
provide a decomposition of isotropic minimal surfaces of the form
h(x,y)=\operatorname{Re}(\ln f(z))
or
h(x,y)=\operatorname{Im}(\ln f(z))
. We emphasize that these forms are remarkably general. Specifically, given any holomorphic function
H(z)
on a simply connected domain, we can write
H(z)=\ln(e^{H(z)})
. Since isotropic minimal surfaces are precisely the real or imaginary parts of holomorphic functions, any such surface can be represented in the required form by setting
f(z)=e^{H(z)}
. Thus, the decompositions derived via the Weierstrass Product Theorem are applicable to a vast class of isotropic minimal surfaces.

Next, we focus our attention on a specific subclass of isotropic minimal surfaces that can be decomposed purely into logarithmoids of revolution or isotropic helicoids. We formalize these cases in the following corollaries:

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
h
be an isotropic minimal surface defined by
h(x,y)=\operatorname{Re}(\ln f(z))
, where
f(z)
is an entire function admitting a product representation of the form
f(z)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-z/a_{n})
. Then
h
can be decomposed purely into an infinite sum of dilated and translated logarithmoids of revolution.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
h
be an isotropic minimal surface defined by
h(x,y)=\operatorname{Im}(\ln f(z))
, where
f(z)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-z/a_{n})
. Then
h
can be decomposed purely into an infinite sum of dilated and translated isotropic helicoids.

Remark 3.6
.

Recall that the isotropic Scherk tower and the isotropic Scherk surface are given by
h(x,y)=\operatorname{Re}(\ln\sin z)
and
h(x,y)=\operatorname{Im}(\ln\sin z)
, respectively. Since
\sin z
can be represented purely as a product of linear factors (up to a factor of
z
), they fit the conditions of the above corollaries. This explains why these surfaces admit such clean decompositions into infinite sums of dilated logarithmoids of revolution and dilated isotropic helicoids, respectively.

Next, we provide a sufficient condition on the function
f
that guarantees its decomposition purely into isotropic helicoids or logarithmoids of revolution. This can done using the M-test (see Appendix
7.1
, Theorem
7.3
) for the convergence of a product.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of nonzero complex numbers such that the sum
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_{n}|^{-1}<\infty
. Then the function
f(z)=z^{k}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-z/a_{n})
is a well-defined analytic function on the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
. Consequently, the functions
h
defined by
h(x,y)=\operatorname{Re}(\ln f(z))
and
h(x,y)=\operatorname{Im}(\ln f(z))
define graphical isotropic minimal surfaces on
\mathbb{D}
. These surfaces admit the following decompositions, respectively:

1.

A pure infinite sum of dilated and translated logarithmoids of revolution combined with a finite sum of
k
undilated logarithmoids;

2.

A pure infinite sum of dilated and translated isotropic helicoids combined with a finite sum of
k
undilated helicoids.

In both cases, the dilation and translation of the constituent surfaces are precisely determined by the zeros
\{a_{n}\}
of the analytic function
f
.

Proof.

The analyticity of
f(z)
on
\mathbb{D}
is guaranteed by the M-test for products, as the condition
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_{n}|^{-1}<\infty
ensures the uniform convergence of the partial products on compact subsets. This allows the logarithm of
f(z)
to be expanded into the series
k\ln z+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\ln(1 - z/a_n)
, which is well-defined and holomorphic on
\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0,a_{n}\}
. Taking the real and imaginary parts of this expansion yields harmonic height functions that define isotropic minimal surfaces. These components correspond precisely to the infinite sums of dilated and translated logarithmoids of revolution and isotropic helicoids, respectively.
∎

3.2
Decomposition via Power Series Expansion

The Weierstrass Product Theorem provides a decomposition that project the logarithmoids of revolution and helicoids as an important building blocks of an isotropic minimal surface. In this subsection we will try to see isotropic version of enneper surface as the building block of every isotropic minimal surface. We do this using a power series approach

Recall that the height function
h(x,y)
of any graphical isotropic minimal surface is harmonic and can therefore be expressed as the real or imaginary part of an analytic function. Since an analytic function admits a power series expansion, we can write

h(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}+\bar{c}_{n}\bar{z}^{n}.

(21)

Consider an isotropic graphical minimal surface of the form
\Phi(x,y)=(x,y,h(x,y))
. Because
h
is real-valued and harmonic, it can be represented as
h(x,y)=f(z)+\overline{f(z)}
for some holomorphic function
f(z)
, where
z=x+iy
.

Given the Weierstrass data
(\omega,g)=(dz,f^{\prime}(z))
, the minimal normal representation yields:

\displaystyle\Phi(z)

\displaystyle=\real\int\left(1,-i,2g\right)\omega

\displaystyle=\real\int\left(1,-i,2f^{\prime}(z)\right)dz

\displaystyle=\left(x,y,\real[2f(z)]\right)

\displaystyle=(x,y,f(z)+\overline{f(z)})

\displaystyle=(x,y,h(x,y)).

This leads to the following lemma:

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\Phi(x,y)
be an arbitrary graphical isotropic minimal surface given by
\Phi(x,y)=(x,y,h(x,y))
. The harmonicity of the height function
h
implies that
h=f(z)+\overline{f(z)}
for some holomorphic function
f
. Then, under the Weierstrass data
(dz,f^{\prime}(z))
, the minimal normal representation recovers
\Phi(x,y)
up to a translation.

We use this lemma to find the Weierstrass data of the polynomial type isotropic minimal surface
c_{n}z^{n}+\bar{c}_{n}\bar{z}^{n}
. From the above lemma, we obtain the Weierstrass data
(dz,nc_{n}z^{n-1})
, which is of the form
(dz,az^{n-1})
. This corresponds to the Weierstrass data of a scaled version of generalised Enneper minimal surface of order
n-1
, for
n>1
in the Euclidean case; accordingly in the isotropic 3-space, we call this surface a scaled isotropic generalised Enneper surface of order
n-1
.

For the case
n=0
, we have a plane parallel to the
xy
-plane, and for
n=1
, we have a plane which may or may not be parallel to the
xy
-plane. This, along with the fact that any harmonic function can be decomposed as

h(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(c_{n}z^{n}+\bar{c}_{n}\bar{z}^{n}),
gives us the following theorem:

Theorem 3.9
.

Every graphical isotropic minimal surface can be decomposed into an infinite sum of scaled isotropic generalised Enneper surfaces and two planar minimal surfaces, one of which is parallel to the
xy
-plane.

3.3
Decompositions of Isotropic Minimal Surfaces under the Bonnet Rotation and López–Ross Deformation

For a given Weierstrass data
(F,G)
, consider the two-parameter family of isotropic minimal surfaces
X_{\theta,\lambda}\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{I}^{3}
defined by applying the Bonnet rotation and the López–Ross deformation:

X_{\theta,\lambda}=\operatorname{Re}\int^{w}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-i\\
2\lambda G\end{pmatrix}\frac{e^{i\theta}}{\lambda}F\,dw.

(22)

Here,
\theta\in(0,2\pi]
is the Bonnet angle, and
\lambda\in(0,+\infty)
is the parameter for the López–Ross deformation. It is well known that
X_{\theta,\lambda}
is a zero mean curvature (ZMC) surface in
\mathbb{I}^{3}
. (see
[
1
]
for more details)

Recall that for any graphical isotropic minimal surface, there exists Weierstrass data of the form
(1,G)
(see Lemma
3.8
) whose image coincides with the surface. In this case, setting
w=u+iv
, we obtain:

X_{\theta,\lambda}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\operatorname{Re}\left(e^{i\theta}\int^{w}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-i\\
2\lambda G\end{pmatrix}dw\right)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\begin{pmatrix}u\cos\theta-v\sin\theta\\
v\cos\theta+u\sin\theta\\
\operatorname{Re}\int^{w}2\lambda e^{i\theta}G\,dw\end{pmatrix}.

The first two components form an invertible linear map
A_{\mathbb{C}}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, given by

A_{\mathbb{C}}(w)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\big(u\cos\theta-v\sin\theta,\;v\cos\theta+u\sin\theta\big),

which takes a complex number
w=u+iv
and returns a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Thus the surface is graphical, so we denote the first two components as
x
and
y
and the third component as
f(x,y)
. Explicitly,

f(x,y)=\operatorname{Re}\int^{A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)}2e^{i\theta}G\,dw.

This expression can be expanded as follows:

f(x,y)=\left(\operatorname{Re}\int^{A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)}2G\,dw\right)\cos\theta+\left(\operatorname{Re}\left(i\int^{A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)}2G\,dw\right)\right)\sin\theta.

Recall that
\operatorname{Re}(iz)=-\operatorname{Im}(z)
. Using this identity, we obtain

f(x,y)=\left(\operatorname{Re}\int^{A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)}2G\,dw\right)\cos\theta-\left(\operatorname{Im}\int^{A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)}2G\,dw\right)\sin\theta.

(23)

Hence, when the Weierstrass data is
(1,G)
, the Weierstrass–Enneper representation together with the Bonnet rotation and the López–Ross deformation yields a graphical isotropic minimal surface. Consequently, the third component gives the height function of such a surface.

First, consider the assumption that the height function is of the form
\int^{w}2G\,dw=\ln(f(w))
. Under this assumption, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of nonzero complex numbers, and let
f(z)
be an entire function with zeros at
\{a_{n}\}
listed according to their multiplicities. Suppose
f
has a zero of order
k\geq 0
at the origin. Let
X
be a graphical isotropic minimal surface with height function
h(x,y)=\operatorname{Re}(\ln f(z))
. If we apply a Bonnet rotation with angle
\theta
and a López–Ross deformation with parameter
\lambda
, then the resulting isotropic minimal surface is also graphical, with height function
h_{\theta,\lambda}
. Moreover,
h_{\theta,\lambda}
admits a decomposition into the following components:

1.

Helicoid contributions (from the
\sin\theta
terms):

(a)

A finite sum representing
k
helicoids, dilated and rotated by the linear transformation
A^{-1}
, with the height function scaled by
-\sin\theta
, given by the formula
-\sin\theta\,\left[h_{\text{Hel}}\circ A^{-1}\right](x,y)
, where
h_{\text{Hel}}(x,y)=\tan^{-1}(y/x)
. Here
A:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the linear transformation corresponding to
A_{\mathbb{C}}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

A=\frac{1}{\lambda}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.

(b)

An infinite sum of dilated, rotated, and translated helicoids, with the height function scaled by
-\sin\theta
, where each term is
-\sin\theta\,\left[h_{\text{dil.Hel}}\circ A^{-1}\right](x,y)
with

h_{\text{dil.Hel}}(x,y)=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{a_{n}^{2}x-a_{n}^{1}y}{|a_{n}|^{2}-a_{n}^{1}x-a_{n}^{2}y}\right),

associated with the nonzero zeros
a_{n}=a_{n}^{1}+ia_{n}^{2}
of
f
.

(c)

An infinite sum of graphical isotropic minimal surfaces given by scaled harmonic polynomials
-\sin\theta\operatorname{Im}\!\left(P_{n}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{a_{n}}Z\right)\right)
, where
Z=A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)
and
P_{n}
is derived from the Weierstrass primary factors in the product formula for
f
.

(d)

A graphical isotropic minimal surface given by the scaled harmonic function
-\sin\theta\operatorname{Im}(g(Z))
, where
g(z)
is the entire function appearing in the Weierstrass product formula for
f
.

2.

Logarithmoid of revolution contributions (from the
\cos\theta
terms):

(a)

A finite sum representing
k
logarithmoids of revolution, dilated and rotated by the linear transformation
A^{-1}
, with the height function scaled by
\cos\theta
, given by
\cos\theta\,\left[h_{\text{LogRev}}\circ A^{-1}\right](x,y)
, where
h_{\text{LogRev}}(x,y)=\ln\!\left(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right)
.

(b)

An infinite sum of dilated, rotated, and translated logarithmoids of revolution, with the height function scaled by
\cos\theta
, where each term is given by
\cos\theta\,\left[h_{\text{dil.LogRev}}\circ A^{-1}\right](x,y)
with

h_{\text{dil.LogRev}}(x,y)=\ln\!\left(\big|1-\tfrac{x+iy}{a_{n}^{1}+ia_{n}^{2}}\big|\right),

associated with the nonzero zeros
a_{n}=a_{n}^{1}+ia_{n}^{2}
of
f
.

(c)

An infinite sum of graphical isotropic minimal surfaces given by scaled harmonic polynomials
\cos\theta\operatorname{Re}\!\left(P_{n}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{a_{n}}Z\right)\right)
, where
Z=A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)
.

(d)

A graphical isotropic minimal surface given by the scaled harmonic function
\cos\theta\operatorname{Re}(g(Z))
, where
Z=A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)
.

Furthermore, because the Weierstrass product representation of an entire function is not unique, the corresponding decomposition of the isotropic minimal surface is likewise not uniquely determined.

Proof.

The proof of this proposition follows from equation (
23
). Observe that
\left(\operatorname{Re}\int^{w}2G\,dw\right)
is the height function of a graphical isotropic minimal surface and therefore admits a decomposition as given in Theorem
3.1
. Similarly,
\left(\operatorname{Im}\int^{w}2G\,dw\right)
is the height function of a graphical isotropic minimal surface and admits a decomposition as given in Theorem
3.2
.

These decompositions are then scaled by
\cos\theta
and
-\sin\theta
, respectively, and composed with
A^{-1}(x,y)
which represents a scaling by
\lambda
followed by an orthogonal transformation. Combining these ingredients yields the desired decomposition.
∎

As an immediate corollary, consider the case where the height function is of the form
\ln(f(z))
and the Weierstrass product formula for
f(z)
contains no Weierstrass primary factors. We then obtain the following result.

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of nonzero complex numbers such that the series
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_{n}|^{-1}<\infty
. Then the function
f(z)=z^{k}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-z/a_{n})
is a well-defined analytic function on the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
. Consequently, the functions
h
given by
h(x,y)=\operatorname{Re}(\ln f(z))
and
h(x,y)=\operatorname{Im}(\ln f(z))
define graphical isotropic minimal surfaces on
\mathbb{D}
.

If we apply a Bonnet rotation with angle
\theta
and a López–Ross deformation with parameter
\lambda
, then the resulting isotropic minimal surface is also graphical, with height function
h_{\theta,\lambda}
. Moreover,
h_{\theta,\lambda}
admits a decomposition into the following components:

1.

Helicoid contributions (from the
\sin\theta
terms):

(a)

A finite sum representing
k
helicoids, dilated and rotated by the linear transformation
A^{-1}
, with the height function scaled by
-\sin\theta
, given by the formula
-\sin\theta\,\left[h_{\text{Hel}}\circ A^{-1}\right](x,y)
, where
h_{\text{Hel}}(x,y)=\tan^{-1}(y/x)
.

(b)

An infinite sum of dilated, rotated, and translated helicoids, with the height function scaled by
-\sin\theta
, where each term is
-\sin\theta\,\left[h_{\text{dil.Hel}}\circ A^{-1}\right](x,y)
.

2.

Logarithmoid of revolution contributions (from the
\cos\theta
terms):

(a)

A finite sum representing
k
logarithmoids of revolution, dilated and rotated by the linear transformation
A^{-1}
, with the height function scaled by
\cos\theta
, given by
\cos\theta\,\left[h_{\text{LogRev}}\circ A^{-1}\right](x,y)
, where
h_{\text{LogRev}}(x,y)=\ln\!\left(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right)
.

(b)

An infinite sum of dilated, rotated, and translated logarithmoids of revolution, with the height function scaled by
\cos\theta
, where each term is
\cos\theta\,\left[h_{\text{dil.LogRev}}\circ A^{-1}\right](x,y)
.

In both cases, dilation is contributed by both the linear transformation
A^{-1}
(which encodes scaling by
\lambda
) and the zeros
\{a_{n}\}
of
f
, while rotation is contributed solely by
A^{-1}
(which encodes rotation by
\theta
). The translations are determined by the zeros
\{a_{n}\}
.

Next, one can also consider the effect of the Bonnet rotation and López–Ross transformation on the power series decomposition of isotropic minimal surfaces. Recall that in this type of decomposition, the key object is the Enneper surface. Starting from equation (
23
), we can rewrite it as

f(x,y)=\left(\operatorname{Re}\int^{A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)}2G\,dw\right)\cos\theta+\left(\operatorname{Re}\;i\int^{A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)}2G\,dw\right)\sin\theta.

Consider the power series expansion of
\int^{z}2G\,dw=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}
. From this we obtain

f(x,y)=\cos\theta\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{c_{n}z^{n}+\bar{c}_{n}\bar{z}^{n}}{2}\right)_{A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)}+\sin\theta\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{ic_{n}z^{n}-i\bar{c}_{n}\bar{z}^{n}}{2}\right)_{A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)}.

Using the identity
iz^{n}=\left(\exp(\tfrac{i\pi}{2n})\cdot z\right)^{n}
for
n\geq 1
(with the
n=0
case handled separately), this can be rewritten as

f(x,y)=\cos\theta\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{c_{n}z^{n}+\bar{c}_{n}\bar{z}^{n}}{2}\right)_{A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)}+\sin\theta\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{c_{n}z^{n}+\bar{c}_{n}\bar{z}^{n}}{2}\right)_{\exp(\frac{i\pi}{2n})\cdot A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)},

(24)

where for
n=0
the factor
\exp(\frac{i\pi}{2n})
is interpreted as
1
(since
z^{0}
is constant and rotation has no effect).

Notice that
\left(\frac{c_{n}z^{n}+\bar{c}_{n}\bar{z}^{n}}{2}\right)
represents the height function of an isotropic generalised Enneper surface, as seen in the discussion of the previous subsection on decomposition by power series. Composing this with
A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)
yields a rotated and dilated version of the isotropic generalised Enneper surface, which we denote by
h_{\text{dil.Enpr}(n)}
. Similarly, when we compose with
\exp(\frac{i\pi}{2n})\cdot A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)
, we obtain a rotated and dilated version of the isotropic generalised Enneper surface with an additional rotation by
\exp(\frac{i\pi}{2n})
, which is precisely the conjugate surface. We denote this by
h^{*}_{\text{dil.Enpr}(n)}
. With this, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 3.12
.

Let
X
be a graphical isotropic minimal surface with height function
h(x,y)
. Then
h
must be harmonic, and suppose it admits a power series representation of the form given in (
21
). If we apply a Bonnet rotation with angle
\theta
and a López–Ross deformation with parameter
\lambda
, then the resulting isotropic minimal surface is also graphical, with height function
h_{\theta,\lambda}
(as established earlier). Moreover,
h_{\theta,\lambda}
admits a decomposition into the following components:

1.

A plane parallel to the
xy
-plane given by
\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{c_{0}\cos\theta+ic_{0}\sin\theta}{2}\right)
.

2.

A plane given by
\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{c_{1}\cdot A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)}{2}\right)=\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{c_{1}\cdot A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)\exp(i\theta)}{2}\right)
.

3.

An infinite sum of dilated and rotated isotropic generalised Enneper surfaces
h_{\text{dil.Enpr}(n)}
(
n\geq 2
), with the height function scaled by
\cos\theta
.

4.

An infinite sum of dilated and rotated conjugate surfaces associated to the isotropic generalised Enneper surfaces (which are themselves isotropic generalised Enneper surfaces) given by
h^{*}_{\text{dil.Enpr}(n)}
(
n\geq 2
), with the height function scaled by
\sin\theta
.

Proof.

Equation (
24
) directly gives the decomposition. The first series consists of isotropic generalised Enneper surfaces
\frac{c_{n}z^{n}+\bar{c}_{n}\bar{z}^{n}}{2}
composed with
A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}
, which dilates and rotates the domain, and scaled by
\cos\theta
. The second series consists of the same expressions composed with
\exp(\frac{i\pi}{2n})\cdot A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}
, where the extra factor
\exp(\frac{i\pi}{2n})
rotates the
n
-th Enneper surface into its conjugate surface
h^{*}_{\text{dil.Enpr}(n)}
, scaled by
\sin\theta
. For
n=0
, the term
\frac{c_{0}}{2}
is constant, giving a plane parallel to the
xy
-plane after combining both series as
\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\frac{c_{0}\cos\theta+ic_{0}\sin\theta}{2}\bigr)
. For
n=1
, the two series combine to produce
\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\frac{c_{1}\cdot A_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}(x,y)\exp(i\theta)}{2}\bigr)
, a plane. For
n\geq 2
, the terms yield the required infinite sums of dilated and rotated Enneper surfaces and their conjugates. This establishes the theorem.
∎

4
Decompositions in a One-Parameter Family of Riemannian and Pseudo-Riemannian Spaces on
\mathbb{R}^{3}

In this section, we discuss the decomposition of zero mean curvature (ZMC) surfaces in a one-parameter family of pseudo-Riemannian and Riemannian spaces defined on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with a metric that depends on a real parameter
c
. We define this family as follows:

\mathbb{R}^{3}(c)=(\mathbb{R}^{3},\,dx^{2}+dy^{2}+c\,dz^{2}).

When
c=1
, this space is the usual Euclidean space; when
c=-1
, it becomes the Lorentz–Minkowski space; and when
c=0
, it becomes the isotropic three-dimensional space.

The ZMC surface equation for a graphical ZMC surface in this space is given by

(1+cf_{x}^{2})f_{yy}-2cf_{x}f_{y}f_{xy}+(1+cf_{y}^{2})f_{xx}=0.

The Weierstrass–Enneper representation for a ZMC surface in this space is

X_{c}=\operatorname{Re}\int^{w}\begin{pmatrix}1-cG^{2}\\
-i(1+cG^{2})\\
2G\end{pmatrix}F\,dw.

When
c=1
, the above represents a minimal surface; when
c=-1
, it represents a maximal surface; and when
c=0
, it represents an isotropic minimal surface. (see
[
1
]
for more details)

4.1
Decompositions under the
c-
family

We start with a graphical isotropic minimal surface. In Lemma
3.8
, we have shown that such a surface admits a Weierstrass–Enneper representation with Weierstrass data given by
(1,G)
. We apply a
c-
family deformation to this Graphical Isotropic Minimal Surface, and in this case, the Weierstrass–Enneper representation becomes

S_{c}=\operatorname{Re}\int^{w}\begin{pmatrix}1-cG^{2}\\
-i(1+cG^{2})\\
2G\end{pmatrix}\,dw=\begin{pmatrix}X(z,\bar{z})\\
Y(z,\bar{z})\\
Z(z,\bar{z})\end{pmatrix}.

(25)

Under this
c
-family deformation, the surface is again locally graphical at
(0,0)
if the map

\phi:(z,\bar{z})\longrightarrow(X+iY,\;X-iY)(z,\bar{z})

is locally invertible at
(0,0)
. One way to ensure this is to apply the inverse function theorem and verify that the determinant of the Jacobian matrix of the above map is nonzero:

\det\begin{pmatrix}(X+iY)_{z}&(X+iY)_{\bar{z}}\\
(X-iY)_{z}&(X-iY)_{\bar{z}}\end{pmatrix}\Bigg|_{(0,0)}=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&-c\,\overline{G^{2}}\\
-cG^{2}&1\end{pmatrix}\Bigg|_{(0,0)}=1-c^{2}|G(0,0)|^{4}\neq 0.

Hence we have the following Lemma

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
S_{c}(z,\bar{z})
be a graphical isotropic minimal surface. As shown in Lemma
3.8
, such a surface can be represented by a Weierstrass–Enneper representation with Weierstrass data
(1,G)
. If we apply a
c
-family deformation given by formula (
25
), then the resulting
c
-deformed ZMC surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}(c)
with Weierstrass data
(1,G)
is graphical near
(0,0)
provided that
1-c^{2}|G(0,0)|^{4}\neq 0
.

We can also discuss the graphical nature of the conjugate surface to this surface. The conjugate of a
c
-deformed surface is given by

S^{*}_{c}=\operatorname{Re}\int^{w}\begin{pmatrix}1-cG^{2}\\
-i(1+cG^{2})\\
2G\end{pmatrix}i\,dw=\begin{pmatrix}X^{*}(z,\bar{z})\\
Y^{*}(z,\bar{z})\\
Z^{*}(z,\bar{z})\end{pmatrix}.

(26)

We call the map which takes
(z,\bar{z})
to
(X^{*}+iY^{*},\;X^{*}-iY^{*})(z,\bar{z})
as

\phi^{*}:(z,\bar{z})\longrightarrow(X^{*}+iY^{*},\;X^{*}-iY^{*})(z,\bar{z}).

With this we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
S^{*}_{c}(z,\bar{z})
be the conjugate isotropic minimal surface to the graphical isotropic minimal surface
S_{c}(z,\bar{z})
. Since the Weierstrass data of
S_{c}(z,\bar{z})
is
(1,G)
, this conjugate isotropic minimal surface can be represented by a Weierstrass–Enneper representation with Weierstrass data
(i,G)
. If we apply a
c
-family deformation to this surface given by formula (
26
), then the resulting
c
-deformed ZMC surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}(c)
with Weierstrass data
(i,G)
is graphical near
(0,0)
provided that
1-c^{2}|G(0,0)|^{4}\neq 0
. In other words, if the minimal surface
S_{c}(z,\bar{z})
is graphical near
(0,0)
, then so is its conjugate minimal surface
S^{*}_{c}(z,\bar{z})
.

The proof of this lemma is straightforward and follows the exact same steps as the previous lemma.

With these lemmas, one can determine whether the
c
-deformed surface is graphical. Next, we assume that the map
\phi
is locally invertible around
(0,0)
. Now consider the
z
-component of this ZMC surface; it is either of the form
\operatorname{Re}\int^{w}2G\,dw
or
\operatorname{Im}\int^{w}2G\,dw
. We suppose that
\int^{w}2G\,dw
is of the form
\ln(f(w))
. Then we can apply the Weierstrass product theorem to this as we did in the previous section.

Thus, assume that
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is a sequence of nonzero zeros of
f(z)
listed according to their multiplicities, and suppose
f
has a zero of order
k\geq 0
at the origin. The Weierstrass Factorization Theorem guarantees that
f
can be expressed as

f(z)=z^{k}e^{g(z)}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\!\left(\frac{z}{a_{n}}\right).

Taking the logarithm of both sides and extracting the real part, we obtain

\operatorname{Re}[\ln f(z)]=k\ln|z|+\operatorname{Re}[g(z)]+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\ln\left|1-\frac{z}{a_{n}}\right|+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{Re}\!\left[P_{n}\!\left(\frac{z}{a_{n}}\right)\right].

Similarly, taking the logarithm of both sides and extracting the imaginary part, we obtain

\operatorname{Im}[\ln f(z)]=k\tan^{-1}(y/x)+\operatorname{Im}[g(z)]+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{a_{n}^{2}x-a_{n}^{1}y}{|a_{n}|^{2}-a_{n}^{1}x-a_{n}^{2}y}\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{Im}\!\left[P_{n}\!\left(\frac{z}{a_{n}}\right)\right].

We recall the following naming conventions:

h_{\text{Hel}}(x,y)=\tan^{-1}(y/x),\qquad h_{\text{dil.Hel}}(x,y)=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{a_{n}^{2}x-a_{n}^{1}y}{|a_{n}|^{2}-a_{n}^{1}x-a_{n}^{2}y}\right),

h_{\text{LogRev}}(x,y)=\ln\!\left(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right),\qquad h_{\text{dil.LogRev}}(x,y)=\ln\!\left(\big|1-\tfrac{x+iy}{a_{n}^{1}+ia_{n}^{2}}\big|\right).

Next we note that as a graph around
(0,0)
the height functions of
S_{c}
and
S_{c}^{*}
can be given as
\operatorname{Re}[\ln f(\phi^{-1}(x,y))]
and
\operatorname{Im}[\ln f([\phi^{*}]^{-1}(x,y))]
respectively. Now here
(x,y)
is changed to
\operatorname{Re}[\ln f(\phi^{-1}(x,y))]
and
\operatorname{Im}[\ln f([\phi^{*}]^{-1}(x,y))]
respectively. With this we have the following two theorem:

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of nonzero complex numbers, and let
f(z)
be an entire function with zeros at
\{a_{n}\}
listed according to their multiplicities. Suppose
f
has a zero of order
k\geq 0
at the origin. Let
X
be a graphical isotropic minimal surface with height function
h(x,y)=\operatorname{Re}(\ln f(z))
. Apply a
c
-family deformation to this isotropic surface and assume that the resulting
c
-deformed ZMC surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}(c)
is again graphical, with height function denoted by
h_{c}(x,y)
. Then
h_{c}
admits a decomposition into the following components:

1.

A finite sum representing
k
logarithmoids of revolution composed with
\phi^{-1}(z,\bar{z})
, given by
h_{\text{LogRev}}(\phi^{-1}(z,\bar{z}))
;

2.

An infinite sum of dilated and translated logarithmoids of revolution composed with
\phi^{-1}(z,\bar{z})
, given by
h_{\text{dil.LogRev}}(\phi^{-1}(z,\bar{z}))

3.

An infinite sum of graphical isotropic minimal surfaces given by the harmonic polynomials
\operatorname{Re}(P_{n}(z/a_{n}))
composed with
\phi^{-1}(z,\bar{z})
, where
P_{n}(z/a_{n})
is the polynomial derived from the Weierstrass primary factors in the product formula for
f
;

4.

A graphical isotropic minimal surface given by the harmonic function
\operatorname{Re}(g(z))
composed with
\phi^{-1}(z,\bar{z})
, where
g(z)
is the entire function appearing in the Weierstrass product formula for
f
.

The function
\phi:(z,\bar{z})\mapsto(X+iY,\;X-iY)
is given by

\phi(z,\bar{z})=\left(z-\overline{\int^{z}cG^{2}},\;\bar{z}-\int^{z}cG^{2}\right),

where
G=\dfrac{f^{\prime}(z)}{2f(z)}
.

Moreover, since the Weierstrass product representation of an entire function is not unique, the corresponding decomposition of the isotropic minimal surface is likewise not uniquely determined.

Proof.

The proof follows exactly the same steps as the proof of Theorem
3.1
, with the only difference being that every term is composed with
\phi^{-1}
. This composition arises because the height function of the surface is given by
\operatorname{Re}[\ln f(\phi^{-1}(z,\bar{z}))]
.

The formula for
\phi
given above can be easily computed from equation (
25
). Here
G
is the Gauss map of the isotropic graphical minimal surface
(x,y,h(x,y))
with
h(x,y)=\operatorname{Re}\ln(f(x+iy))
. In this case, we can apply Lemma
3.8
to obtain
G=\partial_{z}h
, and
\partial_{z}h
is equal
\dfrac{f^{\prime}(z)}{2f(z)}
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

The components of the decomposition above need not be ZMC surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
as
\phi^{-1}
is an arbitrary function with no geometric interpretation in general.

Similarly, for the case where
h
is defined by the imaginary part of
\ln f(z)
, we obtain the following result regarding its infinite sum decomposition:

\{a_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}

be an entire function with zeros at
\{a_{n}\}

. Then

admits a decomposition into the following components:

1.

A finite sum representing
k
helicoids composed with
(\phi^{*})^{-1}(z,\bar{z})
, given by the formula
h_{\text{Hel}}((\phi^{*})^{-1}(z,\bar{z}))

2.

h_{\text{dil.Hel}}((\phi^{*})^{-1}(z,\bar{z}))

4.2
Some Remarks

1.

From theorems
4.3
,
4.5
, and
4.6
, we see that the building blocks of ZMC surfaces across the
c
-family are essentially the helicoid, the logarithmoid of revolution, and the generalised isotropic Enneper surface.

2.

It should be noted that the helicoid given by
h(x,y)=\tan^{-1}(y/x)
is a ZMC surface in all spaces
\mathbb{R}^{3}(c)
. However, the situation is different for the logarithmoid of revolution and the generalised isotropic Enneper surface. Before explaining this, we introduce the following definitions.

Definition 4.7
.

We define a
c
-catenoid to be a ZMC surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}(c)
with Weierstrass data
(1,\frac{1}{2z})
. Similarly, we define a generalised
c
-Enneper surface to be a ZMC surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}(c)
with Weierstrass data
(1,z^{n})
.

We now examine the relationship between the
c
-catenoid and the logarithmoid of revolution, as well as the relationship between the generalised
c
-Enneper surface and the generalised isotropic Enneper surface. First, consider the
c
-catenoid given by

\operatorname{Re}\int^{w}\begin{pmatrix}1-\frac{c}{4z^{2}}\\
-i(1+\frac{c}{4z^{2}})\\
\frac{1}{z}\end{pmatrix}dw=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{z}{2}+\frac{\bar{z}}{2}+\frac{c}{8z}+\frac{c}{8\bar{z}}\\[6.0pt]
\frac{-iz}{2}+\frac{i\bar{z}}{2}+\frac{ic}{8z}-\frac{ic}{8\bar{z}}\\[6.0pt]
\ln|z|\end{pmatrix}.

Define
\phi_{c.\text{Cat}}(z,\bar{z})=\left(\frac{z}{2}+\frac{\bar{z}}{2}+\frac{c}{8z}+\frac{c}{8\bar{z}},\;\frac{-iz}{2}+\frac{i\bar{z}}{2}+\frac{ic}{8z}-\frac{ic}{8\bar{z}}\right)
as the first two components of the Weierstrass–Enneper representation of the
c
-catenoid. Note that the third component is the logarithmoid of revolution. The relationship between the height function
h_{c.\text{Cat}}
of the graph of the
c
-catenoid and the height function
h_{\text{LogRev}}
of the graph of the logarithmoid of revolution can then be expressed as

h_{c.\text{Cat}}=h_{\text{LogRev}}\circ\phi_{c.\text{Cat}}^{-1}.

(27)

Through a similar calculation, one can find the relationship between the height function
h_{c.\text{Enpr}(n)}
of the graph of the generalised
c
-Enneper surface and the height function
h_{\text{Enpr}(n)}
of the graph of the generalised isotropic Enneper surface. Consider the generalised
c
-Enneper surface given by

\operatorname{Re}\int^{w}\begin{pmatrix}1-cz^{n}\\
-i(1+cz^{n})\\
2z^{n}\end{pmatrix}dw=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{z}{2}+\frac{\bar{z}}{2}-\frac{cz^{n+1}}{2(n+1)}-\frac{c\bar{z}^{n+1}}{2(n+1)}\\[6.0pt]
\frac{-iz}{2}+\frac{i\bar{z}}{2}-\frac{icz^{n+1}}{2(n+1)}+\frac{ic\bar{z}^{n+1}}{2(n+1)}\\[6.0pt]
\frac{2z^{n+1}}{n+1}\end{pmatrix}.

Define
\phi_{c.\text{Enpr}}(z,\bar{z})=\left(\frac{z}{2}+\frac{\bar{z}}{2}-\frac{cz^{n+1}}{2(n+1)}-\frac{c\bar{z}^{n+1}}{2(n+1)},\;\frac{-iz}{2}+\frac{i\bar{z}}{2}-\frac{icz^{n+1}}{2(n+1)}+\frac{ic\bar{z}^{n+1}}{2(n+1)}\right)
. With this, we obtain the relation

h_{c.\text{Enpr}(n)}=h_{\text{Enpr}(n)}\circ\phi_{c.\text{Enpr}}^{-1},

(28)

where
n
is the degree of the Gauss map of the generalised
c
-Enneper surface.

Using equations (
27
) and (
28
), together with the fact that helicoids are ZMC surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}(c)
for every value of
c
, we conclude that the the helicoid, the
c
-catenoids, and the generalised
c
-Enneper surfaces can also be considered as the building blocks in the decomposition theory of ZMC surfaces of
\mathbb{R}^{3}(c)
.

3.

If, in theorems
4.3
and
4.5
, we assume that the function
f
admits a Weierstrass factorisation without any nonlinear polynomial in its primary factors, then it decomposes either into a purely infinite sum of logarithmoids of revolution composed with some function, or into a purely infinite sum of helicoids again composed with some function. The function with which the composition is performed is the same as that appearing in theorems
4.3
and
4.5
.

4.

One can also apply the Bonnet rotation and López–Ross transformation to this
c
-family of ZMC surfaces using the formula

S_{\theta,\lambda,c}=\operatorname{Re}\int^{w}\begin{pmatrix}1-c\lambda^{2}G^{2}\\
-i(1+c\lambda^{2}G^{2})\\
2\lambda G\end{pmatrix}\frac{e^{i\theta}}{\lambda}F\,dw.

(29)

One can take

if we assume that when

5
Finite Decompositions of Zero Mean Curvature Surfaces

In the last four sections, we have given a theory of infinite decompositions of ZMC surfaces in various spaces. In this section we will give a finite decomposition version of this theory. A related work has been done in
[
6
]
. We will make a case for this finite decomposition by showing the finite decomposition version of the example of catenoid and Scherk’s tower in various spaces.

First let us start with the conjugate Euler–Ramanujan identity
7
, which is the basis of all the identities we obtained in those concrete examples:

(x^{2}+y^{2})\cdot\prod_{k\neq 0}\left[\frac{(x+k\pi)^{2}+y^{2}}{\pi^{2}k^{2}}\right]=\sin^{2}(x)+\sinh^{2}(y).

Let
m
be a fixed finite positive integer. Then any integer
n
can be written as
n=mk_{n}+i
,
i=0,1,\dots,m-1
, with
k_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
. At this point we state a useful fact which will be useful in the rearrangement of the terms in the infinite product.

Suppose that
A=\prod_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}A_{n}
is a convergent infinite product, and also suppose that
A^{i}=\prod_{k_{n}=-\infty}^{\infty}A_{mk_{n}+i}
is also a convergent infinite subproduct of
A
for
i=0,1,\dots,m-1
. Then we have that
A=\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}A^{i}
. With this we can decompose the conjugate Euler–Ramanujan identity
7
as follows.

(x^{2}+y^{2})\cdot\prod_{k\neq 0}\left[\tfrac{(x+k\pi)^{2}+y^{2}}{\pi^{2}k^{2}}\right]=(x^{2}+y^{2})\cdot\prod_{k_{n}\neq 0}\left[\tfrac{(x+mk_{n}\pi)^{2}+y^{2}}{\pi^{2}m^{2}k_{n}^{2}}\right]\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}\left[\prod_{k_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\tfrac{(x+(mk_{n}+i)\pi)^{2}+y^{2}}{\pi^{2}(mk_{n}+i)^{2}}\right]\right].

The only thing to check for the above formula to work is whether each subproduct is convergent or not.

Let us start with the first subproduct:

(x^{2}+y^{2})\cdot\prod_{k_{n}\neq 0}\left[\frac{(x+mk_{n}\pi)^{2}+y^{2}}{\pi^{2}m^{2}k_{n}^{2}}\right]=m^{2}\left[(\tfrac{x}{m})^{2}+(\tfrac{y}{m})^{2}\right]\prod_{k_{n}\neq 0}\left[\frac{(\tfrac{x}{m}+k_{n}\pi)^{2}+(\tfrac{y}{m})^{2}}{\pi^{2}k_{n}^{2}}\right].

We know from the conjugate Euler–Ramanujan identity that the RHS of the above equation is convergent and is equal to
m^{2}\left[\sin^{2}(\tfrac{x}{m})+\sinh^{2}(\tfrac{y}{m})\right]
.

Next consider the
i^{th}
subproduct.

\displaystyle\prod_{k_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\frac{(x+(mk_{n}+i)\pi)^{2}+y^{2}}{\pi^{2}(mk_{n}+i)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{(x+i\pi)^{2}+y^{2}}{\pi^{2}i^{2}}\prod_{k_{n}\neq 0}\left[\frac{(x+(mk_{n}+i)\pi)^{2}+y^{2}}{\pi^{2}m^{2}k_{n}^{2}}\right]\prod_{k_{n}\neq 0}\left[\frac{\pi^{2}m^{2}k_{n}^{2}}{\pi^{2}(mk_{n}+i)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\big(\tfrac{x+i\pi}{m}\big)^{2}+\big(\tfrac{y}{m}\big)^{2}}{\pi^{2}(\tfrac{i}{m})^{2}}\prod_{k_{n}\neq 0}\left[\frac{\big(\tfrac{x+i\pi}{m}+k_{n}\pi\big)^{2}+\big(\tfrac{y}{m}\big)^{2}}{\pi^{2}k_{n}^{2}}\right]\prod_{k_{n}\neq 0}\left[\frac{m^{2}k_{n}^{2}}{(mk_{n}+i)^{2}}\right].

In the first step we applied a rearrangement to write the RHS of the first step into two infinite products. We use the fact that if
A=\prod_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}A_{n}
and
B=\prod_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}B_{n}
are convergent infinite products, then
C=\prod_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}A_{n}B_{n}
is a convergent infinite product and
C=AB
. We know from the conjugate Euler–Ramanujan identity that the first subproduct in the RHS of the above equation is convergent and is equal to
\frac{m^{2}}{\pi^{2}i^{2}}\left[\sin^{2}(\tfrac{x+i\pi}{m})+\sinh^{2}(\tfrac{y}{m})\right]
. The second term is the well known
i^{th}

Wallis Product
; we will discuss this more and its derivation in the Appendix
7.2
, Theorem
7.4
. But for now we will just state the equation:
\prod_{n\neq 0}\left[\frac{n^{2}}{(n+i/m)^{2}}\right]=\left[\frac{\pi i}{m\sin(\pi i/m )}\right]^{2}
.

Combining all the things, we find that the
i^{th}
product is convergent and its value is equal to
\frac{\sin^{2}(\tfrac{x+i\pi}{m})+\sin^{2}(\tfrac{y}{m})}{\sin^{2}(\pi i/m)}
.

Hence from all the discussion above in this section we get:

\sin^{2}(x)+\sinh^{2}(y)=m^{2}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}\operatorname{cosec}^{2}(\pi i/m)\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\left[\sin^{2}(\tfrac{x+i\pi}{m})+\sinh^{2}(\tfrac{y}{m})\right].

(30)

This equation will be known as the
finite product version of the conjugate Euler–Ramanujan identity
. Next we replace
x
by
x/2
and
y
by
y/\sqrt{2}
, to obtain:

\sin^{2}(x/2)+\sinh^{2}(y/\sqrt{2})=m^{2}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}\operatorname{cosec}^{2}(\pi i/m)\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\left[\sin^{2}(\tfrac{x+2i\pi}{2m})+\sinh^{2}(\tfrac{y}{m\sqrt{2}})\right].

We recall that
\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{f_{\text{ScherkTower}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+2\sin^{2}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right).
Using this we can see that the LHS of the above equation is
\frac{1}{2}\left[\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{f_{\text{ScherkTower}}(x,y)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{f_{\text{ScherkTower}}(0,y)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right]
. Now we apply a translation and dilation on the Scherk’s tower given by
(x,y)
going to
(\tfrac{x+2i\pi}{m},\tfrac{y}{m})
and denote the resulting dilated and translated surface as
f^{i}_{\text{Dil.ScherkTower}}(x,y)
. Then the product on the RHS is going to be:
\frac{1}{2}\left[\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{f^{i}_{\text{Dil.ScherkTower}}(x,y)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{f^{i}_{\text{Dil.ScherkTower}}(0,y)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right]
. When we apply this to equation
30
we obtain the following theorem:

Theorem 5.1
.

Finite decompositions of Scherk’s tower

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\left[\cosh^{2}\left(\tfrac{f_{\text{ScherkTower}}(x,y)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\cosh^{2}\left(\tfrac{f_{\text{ScherkTower}}(0,y)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad=m^{2}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}\operatorname{cosec}^{2}(\pi i/m)\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\cosh^{2}\left(\tfrac{f^{i}_{\text{Dil.ScherkTower}}(x,y)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\cosh^{2}\left(\tfrac{f^{i}_{\text{Dil.ScherkTower}}(0,y)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)\right].

(31)

Similarly one can also think about the finite decompositions of Scherk’s surface and Scherk’s tower in isotropic 3-space. We recall that the isotropic Scherk’s tower is given by
\operatorname{Re}(\ln(x+iy))
and the isotropic Scherk’s surface is given by
\operatorname{Im}(\ln(x+iy))

2.7
.

Recall that
f^{\mathbb{I}^{3}}_{\mathcal{S}_{\text{tower}}}(x,y)=\operatorname{Re}(\ln(x+iy))=\sum_{k\neq 0}\ln\sqrt{\tfrac{(x+k\pi)^{2}+y^{2}}{k^{2}\pi^{2}}}+\ln\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
, which can also be given by
\ln\left[\sqrt{\sin^{2}(x)+\sinh^{2}(y)}\right]
. But from the finite product version of the conjugate Euler–Ramanujan identity
30
we have that

\ln\left[\sqrt{\sin^{2}(x)+\sinh^{2}(y)}\right]=c+\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\ln\left[\sqrt{\sin^{2}(\tfrac{x+i\pi}{m})+\sinh^{2}(\tfrac{y}{m})}\right],

where
c=\ln\left[\sqrt{m^{2}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}\operatorname{cosec}^{2}(\pi i/m)}\right]
. Now from the definition of
f^{\mathbb{I}^{3}}_{\mathcal{S}_{\text{tower}}}(x,y)
we obtain:

f^{\mathbb{I}^{3}}_{\mathcal{S}_{\text{tower}}}(x,y)=c+\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}f^{\mathbb{I}^{3}}_{\mathcal{S}_{\text{tower}}}((x+i\pi)/m,y/m).

In an exactly similar way one can prove an identity for the isotropic Scherk’s surface. We give both of these results as the following theorem:

Theorem 5.2
.

a)
Finite decomposition of Isotropic Scherk Tower

f^{\mathbb{I}^{3}}_{\mathcal{S}_{\text{tower}}}(x,y)=c+\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}f^{\mathbb{I}^{3}}_{\mathcal{S}_{\text{tower}}}((x+i\pi)/m,y/m),

b)
Finite decomposition of Isotropic Scherk Surface

f^{\mathbb{I}^{3}}_{\mathcal{S}_{\text{surf}}}(x,y)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}f^{\mathbb{I}^{3}}_{\mathcal{S}_{\text{surf}}}((x+i\pi)/m,y/m),

where
c=\ln\left[\sqrt{m^{2}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}\operatorname{cosec}^{2}(\pi i/m)}\right]
, a constant.

6
Applications to Lamellar Structures

Twist grain boundary (TGB) phases in smectic-A liquid crystals consist of ordered arrays of screw dislocations, each locally modelled by a helicoid. Such structures have been extensively studied in the liquid crystal literature and are known to possess deep connections with classical minimal surfaces
[
11
,
15
,
16
]
.

The decomposition formulas obtained in Section
2.3
provide a natural geometric interpretation of these phases. In particular, the isotropic minimal surface associated with the Weierstrass data

(F,G)=\left(1,\frac{1}{2}\cot z\right)

has a height function that can be expressed as an infinite superposition of helicoidal contributions. This representation coincides with the classical description of a single twist grain boundary as a periodic array of screw dislocations. Consequently, our decomposition recovers the standard TGB geometry from the perspective of isotropic minimal surface theory. Furthermore, a single grain boundary is known to be topologically equivalent to Scherk’s first minimal surface, highlighting a classical connection between TGB phases and minimal surface geometry
[
11
]
.

Furthermore, the significance of finite and infinite superpositions of harmonic graphs extends beyond liquid-crystal models. Such constructions have also appeared in the study of biological membrane morphologies, including the endoplasmic reticulum and plant thylakoids, where arrays of helicoidal defects give rise to lamellar structures. (see
[
5
,
17
]
)

We begin by stating a version of Weierstrass Product Theorem (see

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}

We have the following theorem recalling a known result about finite decomposition.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let

. Let us fix

b_{k_{n}}=a_{n}

Then

\displaystyle z^{-k}e^{-g(z)}f(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{m-1}(1-\frac{z}{a_{n}})e^{P_{n}(\frac{z}{a_{n}})}\prod_{n=m}^{\infty}(1-\frac{z}{a_{n}})e^{P_{n}(\frac{z}{a_{n}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{m-1}(1-\frac{z}{a_{n}})e^{P_{n}(\frac{z}{a_{n}})}\prod_{r_{n}=0}^{m-1}\prod_{k_{n}=1}^{\infty}(1-\frac{z}{a_{n}})e^{P_{n}(\frac{z}{a_{n}})}

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{m-1}(1-\frac{z}{a_{n}})e^{P_{n}(\frac{z}{a_{n}})}\prod_{r_{n}=0}^{m-1}G_{r_{n}}(\frac{z}{a_{n}})

7.2
Wallis product formula

Let
i\in\{0,1,\dots,m-1\}
.
The
i^{th}
-Wallis product is given by the following theorem:

Theorem 7.4
.

\prod_{n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\left[\frac{\pi^{2}n^{2}}{\pi^{2}(n+i/m)^{2}}\right]=\left[\frac{\pi i}{m\sin(\pi i/m )}\right]^{2}
.

Proof.

We start by expanding the infinite product formula on the LHS:

\prod_{n\neq 0}\left[\frac{\pi^{2}n^{2}}{\pi^{2}(n+i/m)^{2}}\right]=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{n}{(n+i/m)}\right]^{2}\left[\frac{-n}{(-n+i/m)}\right]^{2}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{1}{1-\tfrac{i^{2}}{n^{2}m^{2}}}\right]^{2}=\left[\frac{1}{\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[1-\frac{\pi^{2}i^{2}/m^{2}}{n^{2}\pi^{2}}\right]}\right]^{2}.

Now we observe that the denominator is the infinite product formula for
\tfrac{\sin(i\pi/m)}{i\pi/m}
. Substituting this fact yields the required
i^{th}
Wallis product formula.
∎
