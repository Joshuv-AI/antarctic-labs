Post-traumatic dental care, mouthguard use, and preventive behaviour among athletes participating in sports with different contact intensities: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sinem Coşkun Albayrak, Ayfer Ezgi Yılmaz Çakıroğlu, Temel Çakıroğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSports-related dental and maxillofacial trauma remains a significant oral health problem in contact sports. This study aimed to investigate dental trauma experience, mouthguard use patterns, and post-traumatic dental care behaviours among elite combat sport athletes and to compare these outcomes according to sport contact intensity.MethodsA cross-sectional questionnaire-based study was conducted among 357 athletes involved in collision, contact, and limited-contact sports. Data on dental trauma history, mouthguard awareness and use, and post-traumatic dental care behaviours were collected. Comparisons between sport groups were performed using Pearson’s chi-square, Kruskal–Wallis, and multinomial logistic regression analyses (p < 0.05).ResultsMouthguard awareness was reported by 78.7% of athletes, and 84.9% perceived a risk of orofacial injury. Dental trauma during training or competition was reported by 37.3% of participants; however, only 21.1% sought professional dental care following injury. Mouthguard use was low, particularly during training, where 62.7% reported never using one. Athletes participating in collision sports more frequently believed that their sport required orofacial protection (p < 0.001) and reported more frequent mouthguard use during national and international competitions (both p = 0.001).ConclusionsDental trauma was common, whereas mouthguard use and post-traumatic dental attendance remained inadequate. Although awareness of mouthguards was relatively high, it did not consistently translate into preventive behaviours, particularly during training. Background

Sports-related injuries are common across a wide range of athletic activities, and their incidence increases proportionally with the intensity and physical contact inherent to the sport [1]. Among these injuries, oral and maxillofacial trauma represent a significant health burden, particularly in combat and contact sports where repeated direct impacts are unavoidable [2–5]. Sports involving striking, grappling, or high-speed physical engagement expose athletes to a markedly increased risk of dentoalveolar injury, facial fractures, and soft tissue trauma [6–8]. Recent epidemiological analyses have further confirmed that combat sports rank among the disciplines with the highest risk of maxillofacial injuries [2, 9].

Numerous studies from Turkey and other countries have reported high rates of dental trauma, especially in children and young adults, with prevalence ranging from 11% to 60% depending on age, competitive level, and sport type [10–16]. Recent evidence has also demonstrated that awareness of traumatic dental injuries and mouthguard use remains suboptimal even among organized youth athletes, highlighting the continuing need for preventive education and sports dentistry initiatives [17]. In combat disciplines such as boxing, taekwondo, karate, and kickboxing, dentoalveolar injuries constitute one of the most frequently reported injury types [9, 11, 12, 18]. Importantly, traumatic dental injuries may lead to irreversible sequelae including pulp necrosis, external root resorption, ankylosis, esthetic impairment, and long-term functional limitations [19–21]. Even when managed promptly, the biological consequences of dental trauma may persist for years, creating both clinical and psychosocial burdens for athletes.

Beyond prevention, post-traumatic dental care–seeking behaviour represents a critical yet underexplored component of athlete health management. While several studies have assessed trauma prevalence and mouthguard awareness, relatively few have systematically examined how athletes respond following dental injury, particularly whether they seek professional care after trauma [12, 21–23]. Knowledge gaps regarding emergency management of traumatic dental injuries and appropriate referral pathways have been documented even among competitive athletes [24, 25]. Recent evidence further suggests that awareness of dental trauma does not necessarily translate into appropriate preventive or care-seeking behaviours [17].

Another limitation in the current literature is the insufficient differentiation of sports according to contact intensity and exposure patterns. Injury profiles differ substantially between full-contact, limited-contact, and performance-based disciplines, yet comparative analyses of trauma experience, preventive awareness, and care-seeking behaviour across contact categories remain limited [1]. From a sports medicine and public health perspective, understanding these variations is essential for developing targeted educational and preventive interventions.

Therefore, this cross-sectional analytical study aimed to evaluate dental and maxillofacial trauma experience, mouthguard awareness and utilization, and post-traumatic dental care–seeking behaviour among athletes participating in combat and contact sports with varying levels of physical contact. By classifying sports according to contact intensity and associated orofacial injury risk, the study sought to compare trauma experience, preventive awareness, and protective behaviours between contact groups. By integrating trauma epidemiology, preventive behaviour, and post-injury response within a single analytical framework, this study addresses an important gap in sports injury research and contributes to the development of evidence-based preventive strategies for athletic populations.

Methods

Study population and design

This cross-sectional analytical study was conducted among athletes participating in combat and contact sports. A club-based convenience sampling strategy with a snowball sampling component was used. Eligible athletes were recruited through sports federations and sports clubs. To increase participation, respondents were also asked to share the study invitation and questionnaire with other eligible athletes within their sporting networks. Eligibility criteria included active participation in organised training and/or competition in combat or contact sports and willingness to complete the study questionnaire. No restrictions were applied regarding sex or competitive level. Eligible participants were aged between 9 and 71 years and were actively engaged in regular training and competition at amateur or professional levels.

The sample size of the study was calculated using G*Power version 3.1.9.4 [26]. For categorical data, the “Goodness-of-Fit Tests: Contingency Tables” option was used. Assuming a medium effect size (w = 0.30) based on Cohen’s criteria [27], a significance level of α = 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.95, a chi-square test of independence with 8 degrees of freedom (df = (5 − 1) × (3 − 1)) was performed, corresponding to a comparison of three sport groups and five response categories. The minimum required sample size was determined to be 253 participants. The final sample consisted of 357 athletes, which exceeded the calculated minimum sample size.

The study protocol was approved by the Lokman Hekim University Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2021/146). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment. For participants younger than 18 years, written informed consent was obtained from a parent or legal guardian.

Classification of sport disciplines

Sports were classified according to the system proposed by Rice and the Council on Sports Medicine and Fitness into collision, contact, and limited-contact categories [28]. Group I (collision sports) included boxing, kickboxing, wushu sanda, Muay Thai, mixed martial arts (MMA), wrestling, and ice hockey, in which intentional collisions with opponents or the playing environment are an inherent part of participation. Group II (contact sports) included basketball, football (soccer), taekwondo and karate, where routine physical contact occurs but generally with less force than in collision sports. Group III (limited-contact sports) included volleyball, handball, aikido in which contact with other athletes is infrequent or incidental. This classification was used to compare dental trauma experience, preventive awareness, and protective behaviours across sports with different levels of exposure to orofacial trauma, as determined by the physical characteristics of each discipline and supported by previous literature [1, 2, 17, 18, 23, 28].

Questionnaire and data collection

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire developed from previously published surveys investigating sports-related dental trauma, mouthguard awareness, and preventive behaviours among athletes [11–16, 29]. The questionnaire was adapted to address the objectives of the present study, with emphasis on dental trauma experience, post-traumatic dental care–seeking behaviour, awareness of different mouthguard types, and frequency of mouthguard use during training and competitions.

The questionnaire consisted of 23 items covering demographic characteristics, sport participation and exposure, history of dental trauma during training or competition, perceived risk of orofacial injury, dental consultation following trauma, awareness of mouthguards and mouthguard types, and self-reported frequency of mouthguard use during training, national competitions, and international competitions.

Content validity was assessed through expert review by specialists in sports dentistry, oral and maxillofacial radiology, oral and maxillofacial trauma, and sports sciences. Minor revisions were made to improve clarity and relevance before administration. As the questionnaire was designed as a descriptive epidemiological survey rather than a psychometric instrument, internal consistency reliability analysis was not performed.

Data were collected in person between November 2022 and December 2024 using a standardized data collection form (Supplementary File 1). The extended data collection period reflected the staggered recruitment of athletes from multiple sports clubs and disciplines and accommodated differences in training and competition schedules. Athletes with incomplete questionnaire responses or missing data regarding key study variables (n = 21) were excluded from the final analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were reported as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for Group I and Group II (n ≥ 50) and the Shapiro–Wilk test for Group III (n < 50). Age, sports experience, weekly training frequency, and daily training duration were not normally distributed (p < 0.05). Group comparisons for categorical variables were performed using Pearson’s chi-square test, whereas the Fisher–Freeman–Halton test was used when the assumptions of the chi-square test were not met. Cramer’s V coefficient was reported as a measure of effect size. Because age, sports experience, weekly training frequency, and daily training duration were not normally distributed, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was used to compare groups for continuous variables, and eta-squared (η²) was reported as the measure of effect size. When significant differences were detected, pairwise comparisons were performed using chi-square test or Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction.

To identify factors independently associated with sport group membership, multinomial logistic regression analysis with backward stepwise selection was performed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 383 athletes were enrolled in the study. After excluding 21 athletes with incomplete questionnaire responses or missing data for key study variables and five athletes participating in non-contact sports because the small sample size precluded meaningful statistical analysis, 357 athletes were included in the final analysis. The mean age was 21.22 ± 10.21 years, 61.6% were male, and 65.8% participated in individual sports. According to contact-intensity classification, 257 athletes (72.0%) were assigned to Group I, 75 (21.0%) to Group II and 25 (7%) to Group III. National-level competition experience and professional participation were reported by 34.5% and 33.9% of athletes, respectively. Participant characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Overall, 84.9% of athletes believed that their sport carried a risk of orofacial injury. Among these athletes, 61.9% considered such injuries likely to occur during national competitions, 57.1% during international competitions, and 63.3% during training sessions. Dental injury during training or competition was reported by 37.3% of participants. Among athletes who had experienced dental trauma, only 21.1% reported seeking dental care after the injury.

Preventive behaviours

Mouthguard use was limited, particularly during training. Most athletes reported never using a mouthguard during training sessions (62.7%), while only 9.0% reported always using one. Mouthguard use was higher during competitions than during training, although non-use remained common. During national competitions, 42.0% of athletes reported never using a mouthguard and 31.6% reported always using one. Similarly, during international competitions, 42.3% reported never using a mouthguard and 33.3% reported always using one. Comparison between sport groups

When participant characteristics were compared between sport groups, several differences were observed.

A significant difference with a small effect size was observed among the groups regarding the athlete level and group membership (p = 0.009, Cramer’s V = 0.163). A higher proportion of professional athletes in Group III than in Group I (60.0% vs. 30.4%, p = 0.009), whereas no significant differences were observed between Group I and Group II (p = 0.762) or between Group II and Group III (p = 0.141).

A significant difference with a small effect size was observed among the groups regarding the belief that the sport requires orofacial protection (p < 0.001, Cramer’s V = 0.276). Athletes in Group I more frequently believed that their sport required orofacial protection compared with athletes in Group II (92.6% vs. 78.7%, p < 0.001) and Group III (92.6% vs. 68.0%, p = 0.003), whereas no difference was observed between Group II and Group III (p = 1.000).

A significant difference with a small effect size was observed among the groups regarding the dental attendance frequency (p = 0.029, Cramer’s V = 0.199). Compared with Group II, athletes in Group I more frequently reported never attending dental visits (31.5% vs. 14.7%) and less frequently reported attending dental visits every three months (43.2% vs. 64.0%; p = 0.021). No significant differences were observed between Group I and Group III (p = 0.978) or between Group II and Group III (p = 1.000).

Age differed significantly among the groups, with a moderate effect size (p < 0.001, η² = 0.095). Significant differences between all pairwise comparisons, with age increasing progressively from Group I to Group III.

Sport experience differed significantly among the groups, with a moderate effect size (p < 0.001, η² = 0.080). Athletes in Group I had less sport experience than those in Groups II and III, whereas no significant difference was observed between Groups II and III (p = 0.397).

Weekly training frequency differed significantly among the groups, with a small effect size (p < 0.001, η² = 0.048). Athletes in Group II trained more frequently per week than those in Group I (4.47 ± 1.51 vs. 3.68 ± 1.39 days/week, p < 0.001), whereas no significant differences were observed between Groups I and III (p = 0.149) or Groups II and III (p = 1.000).

Daily training duration differed significantly among the groups, with a small effect size (p = 0.018, η² = 0.017). Athletes in Group I had shorter daily training durations than those in Groups II and III, whereas Groups II and III showed similar daily training durations (p = 1.000).

No significant differences were observed among the groups regarding sex, sport type, or participation in national-level competition (p = 0.589, p = 0.139, and p = 0.133, respectively). Characteristics according to sport group classification are presented in Table 4.

Discussion

The present study evaluated dental trauma experience, mouthguard awareness and use, and post-traumatic dental care–seeking behaviours among athletes participating in sports with different levels of physical contact. Overall, more than one-third of the athletes reported a history of dental trauma, whereas only one-fifth sought professional dental care following injury. Although awareness of mouthguards was relatively high, their regular use remained limited, particularly during training sessions. Athletes participating in collision sports were more likely to perceive the need for orofacial protection and reported more frequent mouthguard use during competitions than athletes participating in limited-contact sports. However, no significant differences were observed between sport groups with respect to previous dental trauma experience, mouthguard awareness, or post-traumatic dental care–seeking behaviour.

The prevalence of self-reported dental trauma observed in the present study is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that athletes participating in combat and contact sports remain at substantial risk of orofacial injuries [2, 3]. Shirani et al. [30] reported a high prevalence of maxillofacial injuries among combat sport athletes, while Patel and Moore [9] demonstrated that kickboxing athletes remain particularly vulnerable to facial trauma despite the use of protective equipment. Similarly, studies conducted in Turkish athletic populations have consistently identified dental trauma as a common sports-related injury among athletes participating in combat and contact sports [11, 12]. Furthermore, a recent systematic review by de Lima et al. [17] concluded that athletes participating in contact sports generally have a greater risk of dental trauma than those participating in non-contact sports, although injury occurrence varies considerably across individual sports. This sport-specific variability has also been demonstrated in studies of professional athletes, in which the pattern and frequency of craniomaxillofacial injuries differed substantially across sports, reflecting differences in injury mechanisms and exposure characteristics [31]. In contrast, no significant differences in self-reported dental trauma prevalence were observed among the collision, contact, and limited-contact sport groups in the present study. This discrepancy may reflect the multifactorial nature of sports-related dental injuries, which are influenced not only by the degree of physical contact but also by sport-specific characteristics, cumulative exposure, adherence to preventive measures, and athlete-related factors.

Another important finding of the present study was the low rate of professional dental attendance following traumatic injury. Despite the relatively high prevalence of self-reported dental trauma, only one-fifth of injured athletes sought professional dental care. This finding suggests that many athletes may underestimate the clinical significance of dental injuries or have insufficient awareness of the need for prompt professional evaluation. Previous studies have similarly reported inadequate knowledge regarding the emergency management and post-traumatic care of dental injuries among athletes [32, 33]. Likewise, Galić et al. [29] demonstrated that awareness of sports-related dental injuries does not necessarily translate into appropriate health-seeking behaviour following injury. Consistent with these findings, the recent systematic review by Silva-Sousa et al. [34] emphasized that early diagnosis and timely professional management are key determinants of favourable outcomes after sports-related traumatic dental injuries, whereas treatment delays increase the risk of complications such as pulp necrosis, inflammatory root resorption, and tooth loss. Therefore, the low rate of dental attendance observed in the present study should be regarded not only as a behavioural concern but also as a clinically important issue with potential long-term consequences for athletes’ oral health. These findings highlight the need for educational strategies targeting athletes, coaches, and sports organizations that promote not only injury prevention but also appropriate post-traumatic management and immediate referral for professional dental care following orofacial injuries [21, 25]. Interestingly, although routine dental attendance differed among the sport groups, post-traumatic dental care–seeking behaviour did not. This finding suggests that regular dental attendance alone may be insufficient to ensure appropriate management following sports-related dental injuries, highlighting the need for specific education regarding emergency dental trauma care.

Awareness of mouthguards was relatively high in the present study, with 78.7% of athletes reporting familiarity with their use. Comparable awareness levels have been reported among athletes in Croatia, Iran, India, and, more recently, among youth basketball players in Turkey [16, 23, 29, 35]. However, awareness alone does not appear sufficient to promote effective preventive behaviour. Although 84.9% of athletes recognised that their sport carries a risk of orofacial injury, knowledge regarding specific mouthguard types remained limited. In particular, only 22.8% of participants were aware of custom-made mouthguards, despite their superior fit, comfort, and protective effectiveness compared with prefabricated alternatives [36, 37]. This finding may reflect limited exposure to sports dentistry services and insufficient education regarding the benefits of professionally fabricated mouthguards. Similar knowledge gaps have been reported previously, with athletes demonstrating general awareness of mouthguards while lacking detailed knowledge regarding their types, indications, and clinical benefits [38, 39].

The discrepancy between awareness and actual mouthguard use observed in the present study is consistent with previous reports showing that knowledge of dental trauma prevention does not necessarily translate into routine use of protective devices during sports participation [16]. Despite relatively high awareness of mouthguards (78.7%), nearly two-thirds of athletes reported never using a mouthguard during training, while more than 40% reported never using one during either national or international competitions. Similar patterns have been reported among boxers, kickboxing athletes, martial arts athletes, soccer players, and youth basketball players, where awareness of mouthguards consistently exceeds actual utilization [16, 19, 33, 35]. Although mouthguard awareness did not differ significantly among the collision, contact, and limited-contact sport groups, significant differences in mouthguard use were observed during both national and international competitions, whereas no significant difference was found during training. These findings suggest that competitive settings may influence athletes’ decisions to use mouthguards more strongly than routine training sessions. Nevertheless, overall compliance remained suboptimal across all sport groups, indicating that awareness alone is insufficient to ensure consistent use of protective equipment. Recent qualitative studies have identified discomfort, impaired breathing, communication difficulties, and concerns regarding athletic performance as common barriers to regular mouthguard use [40, 41]. Therefore, preventive strategies should extend beyond awareness-raising initiatives by addressing practical barriers to mouthguard use and improving access to well-fitting, custom-made mouthguards, which offer superior fit, retention, comfort, and protective performance compared with prefabricated alternatives [42].

Although mouthguard awareness was similarly high across the collision, contact, and limited-contact sport groups, differences emerged in actual preventive behaviours. Specifically, mouthguard use did not differ significantly during routine training sessions, whereas significant differences were observed during both national and international competitions. This finding suggests that athletes may be more inclined to adopt protective equipment when participating in competitive events, where the perceived consequences of orofacial injuries may be greater. This interpretation is further supported by the observation that athletes participating in collision sports were significantly more likely to believe that their sport required orofacial protection than athletes in the contact and limited-contact groups. Together, these findings indicate that athletes’ perceptions of injury risk may influence the adoption of preventive behaviours, particularly in competitive settings. Previous studies have likewise identified perceived injury risk as an important determinant of mouthguard use [18, 35, 43]. Black et al. [18] emphasized that athletes participating in sports with a high likelihood of facial impact are generally more receptive to preventive strategies and the use of protective equipment. Similarly, Acosta-Figueroa and Sánchez-Alfaro [41] demonstrated that athletes’ perceptions of injury susceptibility, together with their attitudes toward mouthguards, substantially influence compliance with protective recommendations. Nevertheless, the absence of significant differences in mouthguard use during routine training suggests that preventive practices remain inadequate regardless of sport category, emphasizing the need for educational interventions that promote consistent mouthguard use during both training and competition.

Age emerged as an important characteristic distinguishing the sport groups. Athletes in the limited-contact group were substantially older than those in the collision and contact groups, and the multinomial logistic regression analysis confirmed that increasing age was independently associated with lower odds of belonging to Groups I and II rather than Group III. These findings should not be interpreted as indicating that age determines participation in a particular sport category; rather, they may reflect differences in the accessibility, competitive structure, and typical participation patterns of the sports represented within each group. The marked age differences among the groups may also have influenced risk perception, preventive behaviour, dental attendance, and cumulative exposure to sport and should therefore be considered when interpreting the between-group comparisons.

An additional finding of the present study was that sport participation duration differed significantly among the sport groups, with athletes in the contact and limited-contact groups generally reporting longer sporting experience than those participating in collision sports. However, these differences were not accompanied by consistently higher levels of mouthguard use, suggesting that longer participation in sport alone may be insufficient to promote preventive behaviours. This interpretation is supported by the multinomial logistic regression analysis, in which sport experience was independently associated only with the comparison between the contact and limited-contact groups, indicating that its influence may vary according to sport category rather than representing a universal determinant of preventive practices. Previous studies have similarly suggested that mouthguard use is shaped not only by individual experience but also by sport-specific culture, coaching practices, organisational expectations, and the availability of preventive education [15, 38, 39]. Therefore, preventive efforts should extend beyond increasing athletes’ knowledge and focus on fostering a sporting culture in which coaches, sports organizations, and healthcare professionals actively promote routine mouthguard use throughout both training and competition, particularly through the wider adoption of well-fitting custom-made mouthguards [18, 42]. The multinomial model also identified athlete level as an independent characteristic distinguishing the sport groups. This finding may reflect differences in training environments, institutional support, and access to preventive resources across athletes with different competitive statuses. Although athlete level was not evaluated as a direct determinant of mouthguard use, these results suggest that competitive characteristics should be considered when interpreting behavioural differences between sport groups.

The protective benefits of mouthguards have been well established. Experimental and clinical evidence demonstrates that mouthguards reduce both the incidence and severity of sports-related orofacial injuries by dissipating impact forces and decreasing stress transmission to the teeth, alveolar bone, and temporomandibular structures [7, 18, 34, 44, 45]. In addition to preventing dentoalveolar trauma, mouthguards may also reduce injuries to adjacent soft tissues and contribute to the protection of the temporomandibular joint during impact [7]. Among the available designs, custom-made mouthguards provide superior retention, fit, comfort, and shock absorption compared with stock or boil-and-bite alternatives, making them the preferred option for athletes participating in sports with a risk of orofacial injury [36, 37, 42]. Despite these well-recognized advantages, only 22.8% of athletes who were aware of mouthguards reported knowledge of custom-made mouthguards, indicating a substantial gap in awareness regarding the most effective form of protection. This finding underscores the need for targeted educational initiatives within sports dentistry and sports medicine that not only promote mouthguard use but also improve athletes’ understanding of the advantages of professionally fabricated mouthguards. Strengthening collaboration among dental professionals, coaches, sports organizations, and governing bodies may facilitate access to custom-made mouthguards and support their routine use throughout both training and competition.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the relationships between trauma experience, mouthguard awareness, and preventive behaviours. Second, all information was obtained through self-reported questionnaires and may therefore be subject to recall bias, reporting bias, and inaccurate recollection of previous traumatic events. Third, the study did not collect detailed clinical information regarding the type, severity, or treatment outcomes of dental and maxillofacial injuries. Consequently, differences in injury characteristics and their potential influence on preventive behaviour could not be evaluated. Another limitation of this study is the broad age range of the participants and the differences in age distribution among the sport groups. Although age was included in the multinomial logistic regression model, residual confounding cannot be excluded, particularly because the limited-contact group was relatively small and considerably older than the other groups. Age may influence cumulative sport exposure, risk perception, healthcare-seeking behaviour, and adherence to preventive measures, which may in turn affect sports-related dental trauma and preventive practices. Therefore, future studies should recruit more age-balanced sport groups or perform age-stratified analyses to better evaluate the relationship between sport category, dental trauma, and mouthguard use. In addition, the sample was predominantly composed of athletes participating in collision and individual sports, whereas the limited-contact group was comparatively small. This imbalance may have reduced the precision of between-group estimates and should be considered when interpreting the subgroup comparisons. Lastly, participants were recruited through sports federations and sports clubs using a club-based convenience sampling strategy with a snowball sampling component. Therefore, selection bias cannot be excluded, and the findings may not be fully generalizable to all athletes or to those from other sporting disciplines or geographic regions.

Conclusions

Despite relatively high awareness of sports-related dental trauma and mouthguards, actual mouthguard use remained low among athletes participating in collision, contact, and limited-contact sports, particularly during training sessions. More than one-third of athletes reported a previous dental injury, yet only one in five sought professional dental care following trauma. Although mouthguard awareness and training use did not differ significantly among sport groups, differences in mouthguard use were observed during national and international competitions. These findings suggest that awareness alone is insufficient to ensure consistent preventive behaviours and highlight the need for targeted educational strategies, improved access to appropriately fitted custom-made mouthguards, and greater collaboration among dental professionals, coaches, and sports organizations to promote routine mouthguard use and timely professional management of sports-related dental injuries.
