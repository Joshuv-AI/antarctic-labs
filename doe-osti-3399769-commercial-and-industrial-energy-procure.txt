1 Introduction
Across Africa, countries have incorporated a variety of new energy sources into their energy mix
to account for increasing demand. In Nigeria, electricity consumption per capita has grown
nearly 50% in the past 20 years (International Energy Agency n.d.-a). According to the
International Energy Agency, in Nigeria alone, energy consumption increased significantly from
2020 to 2023, with the industrial sector specifically nearly tripling its demand for electricity
during this period (International Energy Agency n.d.-b).

To address rising energy demand, Nigeria has established several recent policies and regulations
aimed at increasing electricity generation, improving the country’s transmission infrastructure,
and ensuring electricity reliably reaches end users, including commercial and industrial (C&I)
customers. Among these, the National Energy Policy of 2022 includes measures that seek to
expand generation capacity, modernize the transmission and distribution networks, and promote
supply and/or capacity additions, including decentralized energy and mini-grids (Energy
Commission of Nigeria 2022). The Mini-Grid Regulation of 2023 further streamlined and
clarified regulatory processes and has led to significant foreign investment across the country,
improving end user access to reliable, affordable energy (International Trade Administration
2023).

Despite this progress, Nigeria faces challenges resulting from an unstable grid and outdated
infrastructure. The Transmission Company of Nigeria (TCN) reports average transmission losses
of 7.79 megawatts (MW) for every 100MW input into the grid (Anyaogu 2024), compared to a
world average loss of 6 MW (World Bank Group n.d.-a). This results in a significant
deterioration of energy available to customers (Lee 2022). With energy demand continuing to
rise, these transmission losses create a pattern of instability that leads to further energy shortages
and results in estimated economic losses of $26.2 billion (USD) per year (World Bank Group
2021). Furthermore, Nigeria’s grid relies primarily on gas-fired power plants that are reported to
be “frequently out of service due to maintenance or fuel constraints” (Golobish et al. 2025). It is
estimated that C&I customers in Nigeria face lengthy blackouts multiple times per week
(Adenikinju 2005). Power instability for these customers leads to increased costs resulting from
system restart, lost time and output, spoiled materials, and damaged equipment (Adenikinju
2005).

C&I customers constitute a significant portion of Nigeria’s energy market, with much of the
energy demand coming from Nigeria’s manufacturing sector. As C&I entities evaluate their
options for stable energy supply, many are turning to on-site generation and storage to support
their operations.

Currently, C&I customers in Nigeria frequently turn to on-site diesel and/or petrol generation
sets (gensets) to provide backup energy during grid failures (Collins et al. 2022). Approximately
86% of companies currently operate or share a genset, meeting approximately half of the
country’s commercial energy needs (Green Energy Investment n.d.-a). Nigeria’s National
Bureau of Statistics estimates that, in some cases, customers rely on gensets for 15–18 hours a
day (The World Bank 2014).

 1

C&I customers also represent a significant market for other energy options, including off-grid
and grid-connected energy systems. C&I customers in Nigeria are more frequently turning to
other on-site generation sources such as solar photovoltaics (PV) and battery energy storage
systems (BESS) to meet their energy needs (Becker Büttner Held PartGmbB 2024). Several
studies show that although the capital expenditures for PV systems may be higher than
traditional gensets, PV and BESS are a cost-effective option for customers to meet their energy
needs long term (International Finance Corporation n.d.; Amadi et al. 2023). International
Finance Corporation analysis demonstrates the $USD/kWh range for C&I solar, where the cost
of C&I solar and C&I solar and storage decreases over time when considered with Nigeria’s
2018 feed-in tariff rates (International Finance Corporation n.d.). In addition, techno-economic
analysis of energy systems for critical communications services in Port-Harcourt, Nigeria, shows
that PV systems were 84.4%, 89.9%, and 87.7% more cost-effective for 5-year, 15-year, and 25-
year periods, respectively, compared to other generation options (Amadi et al. 2023). Projects
have been installed across the country at a range of sizes to meet C&I customer needs
(International Finance Corporation n.d.)

Other sources that produce energy or reduce reliance on grid-based power—such as
hydroelectric systems, geothermal systems, and energy efficiency measures—may also have
favorable financial outlooks for C&I customers in Nigeria, depending on their application and
use. For example, The World Bank’s Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency supported the
installation of distributed energy systems at two Nigerian breweries to increase the share of
energy purchased and stored from a hydroelectric power plant (World Bank Group n.d.-b).
Hydropower resources are abundant in Nigeria, with more than 14 gigawatts (GW) of potential
across the country (International Hydropower Association 2022). Geothermal energy also has
significant potential in Nigeria, primarily in Nigeria’s middle and southern regions (Emetere and
Akpan 2023); however, geothermal remains largely untapped with no active geothermal
installations in the country. Finally, Nigeria has taken several steps to encourage energy
efficiency measures, including the adoption of Minimum Energy Performance Standards for
energy-efficient appliances in the 2022 National Energy Policy. In addition, the 2022 National
Energy Policy highlights the country’s short-term goals to promote the use of energy-efficient
systems (such as heating and cooling systems) to decrease energy waste and increase
performance output (Energy Commission of Nigeria).

This guidebook is designed for C&I customers in Nigeria to use in the consideration of procuring
such on-site, captive energy systems to supplement their energy needs. This guidebook describes
the steps of the procurement process and details the relevant stakeholders, processes, and data
needed to make decisions during those steps. The guidebook comprises two major parts:

 3. Sections 2 and 3 detail key considerations for procuring and developing C&I energy
 systems in Nigeria, including policies, energy system types, procurement options, and
 customer considerations.
 4. Section 4 of the guidebook showcases the typical steps of a C&I procurement process,

2 Considerations for Energy Generation in Nigeria
Several factors outside of the procurement process affect C&I customers’ ability to deploy a
supplementary energy system, either on-site or off-site. This section details considerations for the
Nigerian context, including policies at the national level that impact power system
interconnection and development as well as a deep dive into two populous Nigerian regions:
Abuja and Lagos. It then considers different energy system options for customers before
describing the procurement methods that Nigerian C&I customers may use to obtain an on-site
energy system.

2.1 Power System Structure and Key Policies
National power systems are typically made up of three key segments: generation, transmission,
and distribution (KPMG Nigeria 2013). In Nigeria, although the government retains control of
TCN, private utilities, including generation companies (GENCOs) and distribution companies
(DISCOs), underwent large-scale privatization in 2013 as part of a government effort to reform
the power sector and stimulate growth (International Trade Administration 2023). GENCOs can
be either private independent power producers or state-run National Integrated Power Projects
(NIPP). Nigeria is currently home to 11 DISCOs that oversee the distribution of electricity to
C&I and residential customers across the country (Sompura et al. 2021). C&I customers looking
to install energy systems will need to coordinate with their DISCO for interconnection and any
distribution system upgrades.

The Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission (NERC), formed in 2005, is the main body
charged with independent regulation of the country’s electric power industry (NERC n.d.). The
Nigerian Bulk Electricity Trading Company manages the transfer of payments from end users to
DISCOs and ultimately to GENCOs, overseen by NERC (NERC n.d.). Figure 2 highlights
interactions between these entities.

Nigeria’s National Energy Policy sets the strategic direction for energy development in the
country. Updated in 2022, this policy highlights the country’s national targets for harnessing
domestic energy resources and ensuring the country can achieve greater energy security (Energy
Commission of Nigeria 2022). The National Energy Policy emphasizes a need across the country
for reliable, diverse energy that mitigates historical challenges with unreliability leading to losses
in the country’s national economy (Energy Commission of Nigeria 2022).

To further support improved electricity reliability and availability, Nigeria released the Energy
Transition Plan of 2022, which lays out the country’s targets for integrating variable renewable
energy sources in the power mix while supporting development goals, including improved
energy access, job preservation, and streamlined legislation (Nigeria Energy Transition Plan
n.d.). The Energy Transition Plan, now in the implementation phase, also guides Nigeria’s vision
for its energy economy and electricity access. Ultimately, these efforts show that, at the national
level, Nigeria is taking significant steps to increase the share of energy available to consumers
and boost power system reliability.

 5

Nigeria took additional measures to update its electricity regulations with the Electricity Act of
2023, which was aimed at reducing risks to customers across the power sector and improving
electricity supply and quality. These regulations were passed to increase the diversity of energy
resources (including inverter-based resources), enhance the efficiency of transmission, and
accelerate growth in power capacity (KPMG Nigeria 2023). The act also included mandates to
develop standards for power purchase agreements (PPAs). The Nigerian government has adopted
a “willing seller, willing buyer” policy that allows electricity to be transferred directly from
GENCOs to C&I customers (and other interested buyers) via direct PPAs (International Trade
Administration 2023). Additional standards for stand-alone PV systems, regulations for mini-
grids, and boundaries for feed-in tariff rates were also established (National Assembly of the
Federal Republic of Nigeria 2023).

In addition, Section 226 of the Electricity Act of 2023 gave NERC the power to regulate the
electricity industry. In September 2025, NERC released a draft of the Net Billing Regulations,
2025 (NERC 2025a). As of the writing of this report, the Net Billing Regulations 2025, are under
public comment and feedback by industry stakeholders and are not yet in effect (NERC 2025b).
The draft Net Billing Regulations 2025 set out a path for customers with grid-connected energy
systems ranging from 50 kilowatts-peak to 5 megawatts-peak to sell energy back to their local
utility (DISCO) with a ceiling of 30% of their average load (NERC 2025b). C&I customers
would receive credits for energy injected into the grid at a NERC-approved tariff rate on their
energy bills.

Finally, in 2023, Nigeria updated their Mini-Grid Regulation, which guides the deployment of
both stand-alone and grid-connected mini-grids throughout the country (Dadzie et al. 2024). This
Regulation has important implications for C&I customers looking to procure systems with
downstream distribution (selling energy to additional customers). Under the Mini-Grid
Regulation, mini-grids are defined as systems serving more than one customer, meaning C&I
customers looking to procure systems that service only their loads (also known as captive
systems) will not be subject to this regulation.

However, C&I customers looking to install mini-grids with downstream distribution will need to
apply for mini-grid permits in certain cases. The regulation stipulates mini-grids with less than
100 kilowatts (kW) of distribution capacity are not required to apply for a permit, whereas any
mini-grids exceeding 100 kW of distribution capacity must obtain a permit (Sompura et al.
2021). All resale of electricity to mini-grid customers is governed by the regulation. Mini-grids
exceeding 1 MW of generation capacity are no longer considered mini-grids under Nigeria’s
regulations and must obtain a generation license and a PPA if there will be external off-takers
(Sompura et al. 2021). Overall, the Mini-Grid Regulation was established and updated to fast-
track the approval process for mini-grids and accelerate deployment. As such, mini-grids have
been rapidly expanding across the country, and Nigeria has received several major investments
from international organizations to further mini-grid systems (International Trade Administration
2023).

Through all these regulations, Nigeria has taken concrete steps to establish a robust energy
system and develop the frameworks needed to support stakeholders in increasing energy
production, improving transmission, and ensuring reliable energy is delivered to consumers.

2.1.1 Abuja
Abuja, the capital of Nigeria, is a city within Nigeria’s Federal Capital Territory, an
administrative territory (“Federal Capital Territory” n.d.). As the administrative capital of the
country, Abuja has a high density of public institutions and large private corporations. Its
electricity needs are met in part by the Abuja Electricity Distribution Company (AEDC), but like
many parts of the country, the supply is supplemented by behind-the-meter solutions. This need
has yielded inquiries into the opportunities for utilities as well as developers to provide reliable
energy for C&I consumers (Collins et al. 2022).

Abuja’s C&I energy consumption landscape strongly reflects the economic activity distribution
in the Federal Capital Territory. The industrial sectors in Abuja include food and beverages,
pharmaceuticals, and construction industry inputs such as paints, chemicals, glass, and aluminum
products. With the development of the Abuja Industrial Park Free Zone, a concentrated zone
designed to host light and heavy industry, industrial output from the Federal Capital Territory is
expected to increase (Abuja Industrial Park 2023). Regardless of the industry, all C&I customers
are severely impacted by the electricity supply challenges and turn to alternative energy sources
to supplement the supply received from the AEDC (Green Energy Investment n.d.-a). Like most
of the country, this alternative energy is often sourced from diesel or petrol gensets.

The Nigerian National Bureau of Statistics reports that of customers in Abuja, only 54% of
customers are billing customers under the local utility (Nigerian National Bureau of Statistics
2024). This percentage of billing customers indicates potential gaps in knowledge with regards to
how customers are getting their energy. This is consistent with a country-wide metering gap
leading to several recent policy efforts to close this gap. NERC is leading the charge to
implement the Presidential Metering Initiative through the Meter Acquisition Fund (Nigerian
National Bureau of Statistics 2024). In this program, DISCOs, including AEDC, would have
access to a proportionate share of ₦21billion (~$14 million) to procure meters for their
customers at no cost. For a city with growing energy demands driven by increasing urbanization
and economic activity in the services industry (“Urbanization, Energy and Sustainable
Development” 2025), tackling the metering gap head on is expected to pave the way for
increased alternative energy adoption as well as improve grid performance.

In Abuja, private sector developers, as well as the Rural Electrification Agency through its
Energizing Economies Initiative, have been contributing significantly to covering the supply
shortfall from the AEDC, even though AEDC has no current net metering policy (REA-NEP
n.d.). The 2023 updates to the NERC Mini-Grid Regulation allow developers to further secure
site exclusivity, provide operational flexibility, and improve financing eligibility (Okediya
2024). These conditions have increased the alternative power generation market viability for
both commercial project developers and C&I businesses that are interested in making these
investments. However, these positive impacts will have to be implemented by NERC, as it
remains the regulatory body for the Federal Capital Territory, including Abuja. In support of all
these efforts, AEDC partnered with RMI to launch a pilot program for utility-enabled distributed
energy resource systems for C&I customers (covered in full in Section 3.1.4) (Collins et al.
2022). This program allows the DISCO to partner with a C&I customer to address system
upgrades associated with procurement of the energy system.

2.1.2 Lagos
Considered the economic capital of Nigeria, Lagos State, with a population of more than 17
million (World Population Review n.d.), is home to some of Nigeria’s fastest-growing industries.
In 2021, it was estimated that the C&I sector accounted for 64% of the city’s estimated total 30–
40 terawatt-hours of annual unconstrained electricity demand (The World Bank 2021). The
Lagos-Ogun Industrial Axis, the industrial corridor between Lagos and neighboring Ogun State,
is the location for about 60% of the country’s industrial activity, resulting in output from large-
scale, as well as more than 7,000 small- and medium-scale manufacturing businesses across
various sectors (Nwagwu and Oni 2015).

According to the Lagos Economic Development Updates 2025, Lagos contributed 22.36% of the
national gross domestic product in 2024 (Economic Intelligence Department, Ministry of
Economic Planning And Budget 2025). The energy demand of a local economy of this scale is
estimated to be about 9 GW, out of which only 1 GW is supplied from the national grids. This
supply gap is a combined result of generation challenges, transmission problems, and distribution
inefficiencies (Ale 2022). Electricity for C&I customers in Lagos is produced by off-grid sources
such as diesel and gas gensets and, increasingly, PV installations (Anaba 2021). This off-grid
production in Lagos has a total capacity of 15 GW with 260% of the cost of electricity from the
two main DISCOs, EKO Electricity Distribution Company and Ikeja Electric, that serve the area.
For the C&I consumers in Nigeria, many of whom are in Lagos, this represents a tremendous
operational cost (reaching up to 30% to 40% of total production cost for goods), which can
impact profitability, competitiveness, and growth (Green Energy Investment n.d.-b).

Some of the most prominent industries in Lagos with significant energy demands include
agriculture, food processing and beverages, chemicals and petrochemicals, cement, basic metal,
iron, and steel (U.S. Energy Information Administration n.d.). Over the last few years, the
banking, hospitality, and technology sectors have also experienced significant growth, thus
increasing their aggregated energy demands. Based on the energy intensity of these industries, it
is estimated the market value of C&I consumers for alternative energy supply such as distributed
PV could be between $639 million and $855 million in 2025 (International Finance Corporation
n.d.). However, for some of these businesses, the capital expenditures to secure these alternative
sources might be prohibitive. Additionally, other challenges that have impacted the adoption of
alternative energy technologies include a lack of quality assurance, as well as a lack of
experience in the procurement, engineering, and commissioning of the assets (International
Finance Corporation n.d.).

Initiatives such as the Lagos Urban Power program are dedicated to advancing the state’s efforts
to bridge the energy gap by supporting the implementation of the Lagos State Electricity Policy
(Urban Power n.d.). This is enabled by the Nigeria Electricity Act of 2023, which permits states
to establish state-based electricity markets and regulate generation, transmission, and
distribution. As a result, the Lagos State Electricity Regulatory Commission and the Lagos State
Electricity Agency were created (CLG Lawyers and Business Advisors 2025). Together, these
institutions develop and enforce frameworks around licensing, encourage off-grid electricity
solutions through incentives and programs, enforce tariff regulation in accordance with NERC
guidance, and advance consumer protection.

 8

Although the Nigerian Electricity Act of 2023 provides the opportunity for decentralization of
electricity regulation around the country, NERC regulations still hold sway and work in tandem
with the Lagos State Electricity Law. New provisions for billing, tariffs, interconnection, and
metering, indicate a move toward wider-spread metering in the Lagos electricity market. The
successful implementation of this law would usher in a system of net billing that would enable
customers to receive credit for their excess generation fed into the grid for redistribution. At the
time of publication of this guidebook, this remains a proposal for medium- to large-scale energy
installations (50 kilowatts-peak to 5 megawatts-peak).

Development of guidance around off-grid electricity project permitting as well as regulations on
storage tariffs is expected as the Lagos State Electricity Regulatory Commission’s work
continues to expand.

2.2 Energy Efficiency, Energy Generation, and Storage Options
There are a wide range of options C&I customers can explore for their energy systems. The
following sections detail several potential energy system options for Nigerian customers.

2.2.1 Energy Efficiency Upgrades
Although not a generation source, C&I customers can first explore energy efficiency upgrades to
their facilities to reduce energy consumption and cost and ensure any generation or storage
facilities are the appropriate size (and as minimal as possible). Even though Nigeria approved a
National Renewable Energy and Energy Efficiency Policy in 2015, many of these measures have
yet to be operationalized (Energy Commission of Nigeria 2022). However, the National Energy
Policy of 2022 did mandate that the country develop Minimum Energy Performance Standards
for energy-efficient appliances, making energy-efficient equipment available throughout the
country. In addition, in August 2025, NERC partnered with the United Nations Industrial
Development Organization to publish the Industrial Energy Efficiency Database, designed to
support industrial customers in adopting energy-efficient practices such as Standard ISO 50001. 1

Energy efficiency measures can be informed by undertaking an energy audit to assess facility
energy and water usage and identify opportunities for efficiency improvements (Kandt et al.
2021). For example, C&I customers may consider the procurement of energy-efficient systems
such as heating, ventilating, and air-conditioning systems; cooling systems; hot water heaters;
building insulation; and windows. These upgrades can contribute to the overall efficiency of C&I
facilities and reduce energy use for the C&I customer. Energy efficiency measures are typically
lower cost and have shorter payback periods than installing new energy generation sources,
meaning they are often a first step for C&I customers looking to reduce their electricity costs.
C&I customers looking to begin an energy audit may reference Lawrence Berkeley National
Laboratory’s “Industrial Energy Audit Guidebook: Guidelines for Conducting an Energy Audit
in Industrial Facilities” for initial steps (Hasanbeigi and Price 2010).

2.2.2 Diesel Generation Sets
Many customers in Nigeria have turned to diesel generation sets, or gensets, as their first option
to supplement their energy needs. As noted in the introduction, approximately 86% of companies
currently operate or share a genset, meeting approximately half of the country’s commercial
energy needs (Green Energy Investment n.d.-a). Nigeria has a significant oil market with both a
history of imports and domestic refining capabilities to meet the country’s demand for diesel
(International Energy Agency n.d.-c). For many customers, procuring a diesel genset is a first
step to ensure their business has access to a reliable, stable energy supply. Diesel gensets are
highly modular systems with a wide range of power ratings to meet customer needs. These
systems can be deployed quickly, making them a favorable choice for C&I customers responding
to rapid changes in their energy needs or supply. In addition, the country’s substantial diesel
genset market means that there is a ready supply of equipment providers, installers, and
operations and maintenance (O&M) providers (The World Bank 2014). However, customers
looking to procure a diesel genset system should be aware of diesel price fluctuations, which
may lead to changes in the system’s operating costs over its useful life.

2.2.3 Solar Photovoltaics
After traditional genset systems, C&I customers in Nigeria are most commonly exploring
investment in on-site solar PV generation systems (International Finance Corporation. n.d.).
Solar PV modules, commonly referred to as solar panels, use semiconductor material to convert
solar irradiance to DC electrical current (U.S. Department of Energy. n.d.). They are then paired
with an inverter that converts the energy to AC electrical current (in most cases), which can be
used by the loads of the system. Solar PV modules are typically roof-mounted or ground-
mounted and come in both bifacial and monofacial configurations to meet energy needs
(National Laboratory of the Rockies n.d.). Solar PV is best suited for sites with significant solar
resources, which Nigeria has, particularly in the northern regions of the country (Global Solar
Atlas n.d.). Figure 3 highlights Nigeria’s PV power potential by region, showing that where solar
resources are most plentiful in the north of the country, the techno-economic case for PV systems
may be most favorable. However, across the entire country, PV power potential lies primarily in
the mid to high range of kWh/day making PV a potential option for much of the country.

 10

 Figure 3. PV power potential in Nigeria. Source: Global Solar Atlas (n.d.)
Solar PV systems are also modular, meaning systems can be designed to accommodate more
modules to account for growth in energy consumption. This may be an option for C&I customers
who anticipate their energy needs may grow over time. This modularity allows solar PV to be
added to existing microgrids or energy systems, including diesel and/or petrol systems. For
customers with existing diesel or gas gensets/systems, solar PV systems may be a viable option
to supplement their existing system, depending on the configuration.

2.2.4 Battery Energy Storage Systems
Battery energy storage systems (BESS) can be coupled with grid or off-grid generation, and can
provide electricity to meet C&I load during times of inadequate electricity supply. This includes
times when generation may not be meeting customer needs, such as load shedding events, times
when solar PV systems may not be generating, or to provide additional power during an
unexpected event.

For grid-connected systems, BESS can provide additional services to customers. Nigeria does
not have time-of-use rates that charge customers extra for energy used during peak hours.
Instead, Nigerian energy customers are grouped into five Service Bands (A, B, C, D, and E),
corresponding to how much energy a customer can receive in a day. For example, Band A can
receive up to 20 hours/day, whereas Band E can receive up to 4 hours per day (Nwanma 2024).
Each band is then assigned a corresponding tariff to their energy consumption, where Band A
pays the highest tariff and Band E pays the lowest. As such, BESS systems could potentially

 11

assist C&I customers to move from one band to another, reducing their electricity tariff when
paired with another generation source.

BESS, particularly in combination with solar PV, can assist customers in reducing their overall
grid demand. Due to the Service Band structure, BESS does not currently support energy
arbitrage. Accordingly, customers installing BESS may not be able to benefit from arbitrage in
Nigeria as they would in economies with time-of-use rates.

Additionally, for grid-connected systems, BESS provides energy security and reliability in the
event of a grid outage. If the system is pulling energy from the grid during a period of grid
outage, grid instability, or fuel shortages, the system can quickly switch to the BESS as a backup,
ensuring critical energy needs of the customer are still met.

2.2.5 Geothermal Energy and Geothermal Heat Pumps
Geothermal power remains nascent in Nigeria. However, some areas of Nigeria have potential
for introducing traditional hydrothermal resources, such as the Ikogosi warm spring (Eyinla n.d.).
Geothermal power project development will be too large an undertaking for most C&I
customers, but for C&I customers with significant load and capital, geothermal energy may be an
option, as the sector develops. However, traditional geothermal energy is extremely site
dependent and relies on the presence of a hydrothermal resource.

Similarly, little information is available regarding the deployment of geothermal heat pump
systems in Nigeria. Although these systems do not generate electricity, they may be useful for
C&I customers looking to reduce their HVAC load. As the geothermal sector in the country
grows, geothermal heat pumps may be a valuable upgrade for C&I customers, given that they are
widely applicable, irrespective of most site conditions (Energy.gov n.d.).

2.2.6 Hydropower
Small hydropower resources remain largely untapped in Nigeria, with only 370 MW of small
hydropower systems installed as of 2022 (Ugwu et al. 2022). However, Nigeria’s abundant rivers
and water resources offer potential for additional small hydropower development across the
country (Figure 4). Given that these systems require significant capital to develop, they may only
be suited for well-capitalized C&I customers. In addition, hydropower resources may be
extremely dependent on the site of the customer and their location to the potential resource.
Ultimately, Nigeria’s Federal Ministry of Water Resources is the custodian of all waterways in
Nigeria and holds the responsibility of issuing water rights for hydropower generation to
customers and investors. C&I customers looking to invest in a small hydropower system must
procure water usage rights from the National Integrated Water Resources Management
Commission (Zarma 2017).

3 Procurement Options
C&I customers use several options both globally and in Nigeria to procure energy systems that
increase reliability and reduce energy costs. The following sections introduce different
procurement methods, as well as their considerations and application, in Nigeria.

3.1 On-Site Systems
Depending on load, as well as the available space, C&I customers may choose to procure an
energy system housed at their site, also referred to as an on-site system. Procurement methods
for on-site systems include:

 1. Direct investment: Outright purchase and ownership of an energy system with or
 without traditional financing mechanisms such as loans and corporate balance sheet
 financing.

 2. PPAs: Signed with developers; engineering, planning, and construction firms (EPC); or
 other institutions, PPAs allow C&I customers to purchase energy from an installed
 energy system at a set price per kilowatt-hour (kWh).

 3. Energy as a service (EaaS) agreements: EaaS agreements allow for C&I customers to
 procure an energy system at a fixed monthly subscription rate (not a rate per kilowatt-
 hour).

 4. Utility-enabled C&I systems: Although still nascent, utility-enabled C&I systems would
 allow for C&I customers to purchase energy from the system at a per-kilowatt-hour rate
 during the day or peak generation times and purchase energy from the local utility at
 night or at off-peak times.

 5. Mini-grid systems: Mini-grid systems involve C&I customers selling additional energy
 from their system to downstream loads in a localized area.

3.1.1 Direct Investment
C&I customers may choose to directly invest in their system. In this case, the C&I customer
would be considered the owner and operator of the system and would finance the system through
available capital or traditional financing methods such as loans or corporate balance sheet
financing. In many cases, the C&I customer contracts directly with a developer and/or EPC firm
to design and install the system (Figure 5).

Figure 5. Project development model for direct purchase of a C&I system. Source: Becker Büttner

Although direct investment procurement models allow for significant customer control over the
system, these models often require significant up-front capital and may not be feasible for small
or new C&I customers. Additionally, customer control of the system ultimately means the
customer is responsible for the system’s operation, maintenance, decommissioning, and end of
life. Customers who are less experienced in energy systems may not have the workforce needed
to attend to the needs of these systems or may need to contract a third party to meet these needs.
C&I customers may contract with developers and EPCs for continued O&M of the system if the
customer does not have the available workforce to do so.

3.1.2 Power Purchase Agreements
New regulations in Nigeria allow for independent power producers to sell energy to off-takers
(C&I customers) through direct PPAs (World Bank n.d.; German Energy Solutions Initiative of
the German Federal Ministry for Economic Affairs and Climate Action 2024). 2 PPAs govern the
sale and purchase of power produced by an installed system. PPAs are common both globally

On-site PPAs require little to no up-front capital from the C&I customer and may be a viable
option for new or small C&I customers. Developers typically maintain and operate the system,
which can be beneficial for C&I customers who do not have the capabilities to do so. In addition,
the fee structure of PPAs can provide benefits to both the customer (who receives discounted
energy provided by the system) as well as the developer, who may sell excess energy back to the
grid in a grid-connected system.

3.1.3 Energy as a Service
EaaS is a business model 3 in which a C&I customer will pay for energy from an on-site system
on a subscription basis. In these models, the system is owned, installed, and operated by a third
party, usually a developer. Unlike PPAs, EaaS customers pay a set subscription fee (not a fee per
kilowatt-hour of energy used) in exchange for energy, reliability, and system maintenance. EaaS
business models are emerging in Nigeria as a promising solution and are offered by several
developers. However, this business model is still nascent across the country and may not be
available in all regions or for all technologies.

EaaS models require no up-front capital for the installation of an on-site energy system, making
them a viable option for new or small C&I customers. For EaaS models, the customer retains no
ownership of the system and therefore does not need to account for system maintenance,
operation, decommissioning, and end of life. However, as a result of the subscription method of
EaaS systems, these systems provide a lower savings over the lifetime of the system compared to
direct procurement methods.

3.1.4 Utility-Enabled C&I Systems
DISCOs in Nigeria are considering additional routes for C&I customers to procure energy
systems. Several DISCOs have been involved in the deployment of utility-enabled microgrids
and are now approaching utility-enabled C&I projects (Dadzie et al. 2024). Utility-enabled
systems allow the C&I customer to receive electricity supply from the on-site energy system
during the day or at peak times and consume energy from the DISCOs grid at night or off-peak
hours, as stipulated by a contract (Dadzie et al. 2024). This allows the customer to ensure
reliable, cost-effective energy during their operations and as a backup during grid outages while
also creating incentives for the DISCO and the developer (Figure 7). Project contracts may
include upgrades to distribution infrastructure, long-term contracts between DISCOs and
developers, and unique regulatory guidelines that increase the share of reliable, cost-effective
electricity generation across the country (Dadzie et al. 2024).

These projects provide midlevel savings for the C&I customer over the life of the project while
ensuring incentives for DISCOs and developers. They do not require up-front capital from the
C&I customer as the developer and DISCO control all aspects of the system and the customer
pays for electricity from the system in a similar rate style to a PPA (₦/kWh). The customer does
not hold any ownership over the system. In this model, the developer or EPC is tasked with all
aspects of system construction, operation, and maintenance, which may be beneficial for C&I
customers who do not have the capabilities to maintain and operate their desired systems.

3.1.5 Mini-Grid Systems
As discussed, the Mini-Grid Regulation created a pathway for streamlined mini-grid deployment
across the country. C&I customers looking to procure small systems may consider configuring
their systems as mini-grids by providing downstream distribution to local customers. In these
cases, C&I customers will receive energy from the system and sell additional energy generated
by the system to the downstream consumers. A permit is not required for systems under 100 kW.
Systems between 100 kW and 1 MW may apply for a mini-grid permit. As of 2018, small and
medium C&I customers represent a growing share of mini-grid installed capacity in Nigeria,
approximately 20 MW total (Sompura et al. 2021). Mini-grids that are connected to the
distribution infrastructure must sign a tripartite agreement with the mini-grid developer, target
community, and the DISCO (Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission 2023). Registered

3.2 Off-Site Systems
Some C&I customers may not have the ability to procure on-site energy systems because of
factors such as site leases, system sizes (large systems exceeding the customer’s available space),
or unfavorable conditions (e.g., shading, orientation, roof condition). In these cases, customers
may wish to purchase energy from an off-site system.

3.2.1 Power Purchase Agreements
PPAs can also be used in the procurement of an off-site system. C&I consumers may contract
with a developer to procure an energy system away from their main location and transport
energy to the desired loads. Off-site PPAs are legal in Nigeria and hold many of the same
benefits as on-site PPAs; however, additional considerations apply to the development of off-site
PPAs. C&I customers should evaluate whether they already own a viable site for energy system
installation or whether a site lease is necessary. Additionally, the system will need to leverage
transmission infrastructure to transport energy from the system to the customer. Developers and
C&I customers must work with TCN to procure a “wheeling agreement,” which allows the
developer to use TCN’s existing grid infrastructure to send energy to the C&I customer
(Abdullahi 2021). 5 A wheeling agreement is signed between the independent power producer
and TCN and sets a per-kWh rate for use of the distribution system to transfer energy from the
generation site to the customer (Abdullahi 2021; Mission 300 Africa Energy Summit n.d.).

In addition, Nigeria’s power system structure allows for sleeved PPAs, in which C&I customers
purchase power from a system and electricity is supplied through TCN’s transmission
infrastructure or through their DICSCOs infrastructure (Baker McKenzie 2019). In these cases,
the customer must be approved by NERC, and customers and sellers will evaluate whether
transmission or distribution systems must be upgraded to deliver energy.

3.3 Customer Considerations
Several factors will contribute to a C&I customer deciding on the best system and procurement
method to suit their needs. These considerations include, but are not limited to:

Available capital – Several procurement methods require significant up-front capital from the
C&I customer. Customers should evaluate whether they have substantial capital or access to
financing to pursue a high-cost procurement method, such as direct investment, or whether costs
associated with the system should be prioritized in operational budgets, which may be possible
through PPAs or EaaS methods.

Available workforce and skills – In some cases, procurement methods will result in the C&I
customer obtaining ownership of the system. For these methods, the C&I customer is also
responsible for the O&M of the system. Customers who do not have experience with O&M for
energy systems may wish to pursue other procurement methods or procure additional third-party
contracts for the system’s O&M needs.

Interaction with local utility and transmission – For grid-connected systems, it will be
essential for C&I customers to understand their local utility’s policies and incentives to
interconnect customer-procured systems. It is critical to understand whether the system will
require updates to the distribution system, what the costs of these updates will be, and who will
be responsible for bearing those costs. This is also true for off-site PPAs, in which a wheeling
agreement will be needed to transport energy from the generation site to the customer. It is also
important for C&I customers to know if systems are capable of running in island mode, when
grid-supplied electricity is unavailable.

Available land and facilities – Energy systems have different land and space needs. C&I
customers should be sure to evaluate the available land, space, and facilities at their given site to
determine what generation systems will be an option for their needs. This includes ensuring there
is no shading of solar panels, or if solar panels are to be installed on a roof, that the roof is not in
need of an upgrade.

Site ownership – Many C&I customers lease their sites. It is important to be aware of the
remaining tenure on the leases to ensure the duration of any energy-specific contracts does not
exceed the length of the lease, given the risk that the lease for the site is not extended. If C&I
customers lease the site of their operations, they may need to renegotiate their site lease to
accommodate for the installation of the energy system. C&I customers should be careful to
understand the length and terms of the lease as well as the lifetime of the energy system. C&I
customers should avoid problems associated with split incentives, in which one party pays the
capital cost of a system but another party enjoys the financial benefits of the system. Certain
lease arrangements, such as triple-net leases which cover the rent of the system as well as the
maintenance, can help avoid these problems.

Energy needs, growth, and modularity – Some energy systems are modular, enabling the
customer to add generation to the system over time. C&I customers should consider whether
their energy needs are likely to evolve over time and determine if a modular energy system is
needed.

These considerations represent a small selection of the factors C&I customers will need to weigh
to determine what procurement method, system, or combination of options may be the best fit.
Additional factors may also influence the system’s component makeup, design, and technical
operation.

4 Step-by-Step Procurement Process
The procurement process involves several steps and stakeholders to ultimately result in a
functioning energy system. The following is a high-level summary of the steps for procurement
of an energy system, which are further detailed in the subsequent sections:

 1. Assess energy needs and conduct prefeasibility analysis – To understand the energy
 needs of a customer and what energy systems are applicable, customers first determine
 their historical energy consumption as well as what resources are available at their
 selected site.
 2. Develop a request for proposal (RFP) – Designing an RFP is critical to receiving
 proposals from energy system developers that align with the customer’s needs and
 priorities.
 3. Conduct site assessments – To properly size and design a system, developers will visit a
 C&I customer’s site to assess a project’s viability and submit a proposal.
 4. Evaluate proposals and select a winning bid – C&I customers will determine a
 proposal that best suits their needs and choose which one to move forward with.
 5. Negotiate contract – Once the winning bid is selected, customers will negotiate contract
 terms, including pricing, timelines, operation, maintenance, commissioning, and end-of-
 life management.
 6. Design, permit, construct, interconnect, and accept project – After the terms have
 been set, the developer will develop, construct, interconnect, and commission the energy
 system.
 7. Operate, maintain, and monitor project – Clear, robust, and enforceable contracts for
 O&M are critical to ensuring the system meets its anticipated lifetime and does not break
 down or degrade. System monitoring will inform customers when system repairs may be
 needed.
 8. Decommission project – Once the project has reached the end of its life,
 decommissioning procedures, including recycling or removal of components, will ensure
 the system is disposed of properly.
These steps are associated with a competitive procurement process for an energy system.
Competitive processes are designed to ensure the C&I customer can choose between the most
qualified developers and receive the best prices for their systems. Noncompetitive procurement
processes may differ in these steps. Additionally, the primary actor for each step differs. The
C&I customer is typically expected to lead on steps 1, 2, 4, and 5, depending on the procurement
method selected. At each step, other parties may be engaged, including developers, EPCs,
utilities, or others as needed. For example, a developer would typically lead on steps 3, 6, 7, and
8; however, utilities and EPCs are also heavily involved in step 7. The C&I customer may also
be expected to lead steps 7 and 8 for procurement methods in which they retain ownership of the
system. Figure 8 provides a visual overview of each step in the procurement process and the
associated party responsible for that step in a typical C&I procurement process.

4.1 Assess Energy Needs and Priorities and Conduct Prefeasibility
 Analysis
Assessing energy needs and determining the potential energy available at a site is critical to
determining the size of the system that a customer may need and what systems are best suited to
the customer’s context. The primary stakeholder for this step is the C&I customer but may also
include engineering firms, landowners, or tenants, depending on site specifics.

 22

Determining the amount of energy used, the timing of that energy use, and the costs associated
with its generation is the first step in understanding what type and size of system a customer may
need. Generally, C&I energy systems are smaller than utility-scale systems (>5 MW) and larger
than residential systems (0.6–2.5kW [Mercury Direct 2026]), but their sizes may vary widely
based on the customer’s activities.

C&I customers should begin by determining their current energy use. The monthly total
electricity usage can typically be found through customer utility bills as well as referring to
meter or submeter data, SCADA (supervisory control and data acquisition) systems, or other
sources of electrical usage data. At least one year’s worth of utility bills should be collected to
track seasonal changes in energy usage. Each bill should outline the month’s average energy
usage in kilowatt-hours. Once the average monthly usage is calculated (in kilowatt-hours),
customers can determine the potential size and type of system they may need. 6

As C&I customers go through their historical energy consumption, they should be careful to
identify the existing utility tariff rate the facility is subject to. This tariff rate can typically be
found on each monthly utility bill. These tariffs may be unique to the serving utility or
jurisdiction, and important information on these tariffs can often be found online on utility or
jurisdiction websites. If information is not available online, C&I customers should contact the
serving utility to ask about the tariff rate structure for their facility. Special attention should be
paid to what Service Band Tariff 7 the facility is on and whether any significant change in facility
load will result in a tariff rate change. It is not unusual for facilities to be “grandfathered” into an
expired tariff with a lower unit cost of electricity than existing tariffs, and for new construction
to trigger an update in the facility’s unit cost of electricity.

Facility technical staff should be consulted closely during this process, as they are often aware of
specific conditions or needs at a facility that project managers may not know about.

facility characteristics 8:

 planned? Are there plans to increase on-site staff, to make any major changes to the

4.1.2 Cost Considerations
Once utility bills have been collected and project priorities are understood, the customer can
determine potential cost savings from installation of a proposed system. This is commonly
determined by the simple payback calculation, which determines the number of years it takes for
energy bill savings from a project to offset the up-front capital costs of the proposed system.
Once a developer is selected, they should also perform these calculations. However, it may be
helpful to C&I customers to understand what these calculations mean and know how to perform
them if needed:


A potential unforeseen cost of system installation may include retrofit. Although some C&I PV
and BESS may be ground-mounted, a large portion of C&I PV systems are roof-mounted. In
these cases, it is essential to have the roof inspected to ensure it can withstand the weight of the
system, determine if other building infrastructure needs to be moved, and more (Nagarajan and
Zeya-Hazra 2024). If the roof is still under warranty, the roofing manufacturer should be
contacted to determine what rooftop PV systems, if any, are allowed without voiding the
warranty.

Other retrofit considerations may be necessary, including tree trimming/removal. Retrofit
considerations will vary, depending on the type of system; the recommendations here are typical
considerations for an on-site PV and BESS projects. It is essential to work with a qualified,
experienced developer and installer that can assess retrofit needs early in the project and
determine what additional resources may be necessary. Retrofits may add additional costs to the
total cost of the system, and the sooner these costs are identified, the better.

 24

Customers should also consider what upgrades to the existing electrical system or backup power
systems will be necessary to support the new project and if the existing electrical equipment is
due for a replacement in the near future. If a ground-mounted PV system is being considered,
customers must determine where the electrical service connection will be run and if this path
goes under parking lots or other paved surfaces. Furthermore, customers should consult with the
serving utility to determine if utility equipment off-site will need to be upgraded as a result of a
potential project; utilities may pass these costs on to the customer. In these cases, utility-enabled
C&I systems may be favorable to account for system upgrades. More information on these types
of agreements can be found in RMI’s Utility-Enabled Distributed Energy Resources Hub. 9

Finally, it is important to consider the ownership and occupancy of the facilities under
consideration. If the C&I customer does not own the facilities or land where they are considering
siting the system, they will need to procure a land lease agreement with terms that address
installation, operation, and maintenance of the system over its lifetime. If this is the case, the
customer should speak with their facility/landowner before beginning the procurement process.
Site leases will add additional costs to the total system cost.

4.1.3 Site Viability
Several factors can affect the suitability of a commercial or industrial facility for on-site energy
projects. 10 These factors include, but are not limited to:

 1. Is the facility fully owned by the customer, or is it under a long-term lease (i.e., 20 years
 or more)?
 2. What is the amount of usable roof space (for PV) or landscape available for solar power
 or other types of generation?
 3. Is the roof structurally sound, or are there plans to replace it before the useful life of a PV
 system? Is the pitch, or steepness, of the roof suitable for rooftop PV?
 4. Are there any potential obstructions, such as trees, walls, buildings, or other structures,
 that could impede solar generation?
 5. Does the facility have suitable power infrastructure or potential to upgrade?
 6. Have energy efficiency and other improvements already been made?
 7. Is this facility currently in compliance with local building codes? Examples of permits
 that should be considered include construction permits and as-built certification.
 8. Does the facility have up-to-date building insurance?
 9. Is there a recent Fire Safety Risk Assessment for the facility?

4.1.4 Deciding on a Project Delivery Method
Based on the size and complexity of the C&I facility, the type and complexity of the project
being considered, and the customer’s tolerance for risk, there are multiple project delivery
methods that can be used to move forward with a project. Developers may specialize in one or
more of these methods, and some will only be interested in using their preferred method. If the
customer is interested in working with a specific developer, they should contact that developer
and discuss what types of project delivery methods they prefer.

Three of the more common project delivery methods, along with their typical use cases,
strengths, and weaknesses, are:

 1. Design-bid-build (DBB): This is an older, familiar project delivery method. In a DBB
 project, the customer first issues an RFP for a design and selects an
 architecture/engineering firm (A/E). The selected A/E will deliver a design to the
 customer, at which point the customer will issue a second RFP, based on the design, to be
 bid on by developers. During the construction process, the A/E and the developer will
 meet frequently to coordinate on the implementation of the design. Some strengths of this
 project delivery method include well-defined roles and responsibilities, better control
 over the exact features of the design, and the fact that it is a traditional and well-
 understood delivery method. Potential weaknesses include conflicts between the designer
 and developer and a slower process overall resulting from the necessity of completing a
 design before the start of construction and a separate, secondary RFP process. DBB may
 be a good fit for projects that require a high degree of control over the design, projects
 that are relatively simple and well understood by designers and developers, and projects
 that do not have a tight deadline for implementation.
 2. Design-build (DB): Although considered somewhat less traditional by some developers,
 the DB project delivery method has been successfully used for more than half a century
 and should be familiar to most experienced developers. In the DB project delivery
 method, a single RFP is issued for a designer/developer. This designer/developer team
 may be a single company or may be a partnership between a traditional developer and an
 A/E firm. The RFP should include a simple feasibility analysis and the customer’s basic
 requirements for the project. Some customers may still choose to commission an A/E
 designer to provide a simple preliminary design to include with the RFP.

 Once a designer and a developer have been selected, they are responsible for fully
 designing the system in accordance with the customer’s RFP requirements and then
 implementing the project. Some strengths of the DB project delivery method typically
 include better coordination between the design and construction teams and a faster
 project overall. The quicker project completion is a result of the ability to overlap the
 design and construction phases and the need for only one RFP to deliver the project. A
 potential weakness is somewhat less control over the final design of the system. Also, the
 reliance on a single designer/developer team can become a liability if that team is not
 experienced or if any participants are unable to meet their contractual obligations for any
 reason. A DB delivery is a good option for customers who do not need a great deal of
 control over the final design of their system, customers who do not want to go through

 28

 the process of issuing multiple RFPs and mediating between a designer and developer, or
 customers who want to implement a somewhat-complicated project on a shorter schedule.
 3. Engineering, planning, and construction (EPC): This method is similar to the DB
 delivery method and shares many strengths and weaknesses. The primary difference
 between these two methods is that instead of bidding to complete a specific project as
 defined in the RFP, the EPC contractor is bidding to provide a specific system or
 capability—such as the provision of a specified amount of solar PV, battery, and
 microgrid capability—for a lump-sum price. Because of this difference in project
 delivery, much of the risk of an unsuccessful project is transferred from the C&I
 customer to the EPC contractor. The primary strength of this delivery method compared
 to the DB method is, as discussed previously, that if cost overruns are encountered during
 the project, the financial risk is not taken on by the customer but by the contractor. Like
 DB, EPC is suitable for complex projects requiring a high degree of coordination
 between the design and construction teams. The primary weakness of this delivery
 method compared to the other two methods described is that the customer will have the
 least amount of control over the final design of the system. For this reason, it is critical
 for a C&I customer to carefully consider and include every requirement for the project
 performance in the initial RFP. Many customers will hire a third-party engineer to
 develop the RFP and monitor the progress of the project to ensure high attention to detail.

4.2 Develop Request for Proposals
Once a C&I customer has conducted a simple feasibility analysis, determined there may be
potential for a suitable project at their facility, and decided on their preferred project delivery
method, the next step is to find an A/E or developer to partner with to move forward with the
project. The customer may have a pre-existing relationship with an experienced developer, or the
customer may instead issue an RFP to attract multiple bids from different developers. It is
considered an industry best practice, even when a customer has a pre-existing relationship with a
developer, to obtain at least three bids from developers to compare. Based on the project delivery
method chosen, there are multiple benefits to issuing an RFP, including (1) driving competition
between developers that could result in cost savings and/or improvements to the offering and (2)
driving design of the project. An RFP could be especially beneficial for larger or more complex
projects, including those that aggregate installations across multiple sites.

RFPs for energy systems generally include:

 • A full set of designs for the project, if using a DBB project delivery method
 • A preliminary design, feasibility study, and detailed project requirements for a DB
 project delivery method
 • A feasibility study, detailed facility and project requirements, and detailed performance
 requirements for an EPC delivery method
 • Terms of the contract, submission guidelines, and evaluation criteria.
The RFP is developed by the customer or a consultant working for the customer, and applicants
are generally developers, integrated design-builders, or EPCs who develop their own projects. To
receive several high-quality bids, the RFP will have to be shared proactively with developers
and/or industry groups.

4.3 Conduct Site Assessments
The developer, EPC, or consultant will need to conduct a site visit to assess the potential
technologies, project sizes, and other considerations that would inform the design and cost of the
installation.

 o The operational scheduling and working shifts

 safety requirements for on-site contractors, or similar
 o A laydown area that can be fenced off for materials, construction trailers, and
 large deliveries
 o The age of the roof and whether there are plans for the roof to be replaced in the

 o The lease term length (if the site is leased).

 o Locations of any meters or submeters

Whether the customer receives one bid from a sole-sourced developer or multiple bids in
response to an RFP, it is important to carefully evaluate the proposal(s).

 o Is the developer fully bonded and insured for a project of the anticipated size?
 2. Permits
 o Does the developer describe state, local, and national permit needs and understand

 3. Interconnection (for grid-connected systems)

bid. Documenting this selection process for future reference is considered a best practice and can
help inform future bid reviews.

Once the customer selects their developer, they will review and negotiate the contract terms
before ultimately signing and moving forward (step 5 in Figure 7). Depending on the type of
agreement (e.g., PPA, direct purchase) and the project delivery method chosen, the terms of
these contracts may vary.

4.5 Negotiate Contract
Once the C&I customer selects the winning bid, they can move into contract negotiations. There
are several aspects of the contract that could be negotiated. However, the customer will need to
weigh the benefits and likelihood of successful negotiations versus potential delays with the start
of the project.

 33

The following is a list of initial aspects of a contract that could be negotiated. Note that this list is
not comprehensive and that C&I customers should utilize their contracting team and legal
counsel to carefully assess the proposed agreement from the developer. For different
procurement methods, the following negotiations are common:

 • For any agreement:
 o Price – The customer will need to negotiate the price of the system, including
 total cost and payment periods.

 • For PPAs, EaaS, and similar methods:
 o Contract length – Customers will negotiate the duration of the contract. Shorter-
 term contracts will likely increase the cost as a result of compressed timelines.
 o Pricing structure – The pricing structure is critical to determining how the system
 will be paid for over time. This will include if the price is fixed or indexed, if
 there is an escalator, what the escalator or index is, and any associated
 assumptions about inflation, utility rates increases, or other factors.
 o Buyout clauses – If the customer wishes to end the contract prior to the agreed
 period, they may need to negotiate a buyout clause to pay for the system,
 including methods for determining how the system value would be determined.
 o Performance guarantees – Customers will need to negotiate protections, including
 level of guarantee (often expressed as a percentage) and compensation
 methodology, for system underperformance.
 • For off-site PPAs:
 o Power delivery – Customers should work with the selected developer to
 determine the point to which power is delivered and all necessary agreements
 with local utilities, DISCOs, and other stakeholders.

Negotiation is a necessary step to ensure the C&I customer receives the best price and service
possible for their system.

4.6 Design, Permit, Construct, Interconnect, and Accept Project
Once a developer has been selected and a contract has been negotiated and signed, the permitting
and interconnection processes will be started, and final design and construction can begin.
Depending on the project delivery method chosen, some construction may start while the design
is still being finished. Often this will include site clearing, underground work, or other
construction that will be necessary regardless of the final design of the system. However, before
any construction work begins, permitting will be required from the local authority having
jurisdiction. Depending on the location, the permitting process can vary considerably. This is one
reason why a developer with knowledge of the local permitting process is critical to project
success. 16

For C&I customers, Nigeria’s national-level efforts to expand the country’s energy system result
in permitting and licensing requirements for systems. Although building permits may still be
required, no licenses or NERC operating permits are required for captive generation systems up
to 1 MW (Green Energy Investment n.d.-a; Becker Büttner Held PartGmbB 2024), meeting the
needs of most C&I customers across the country. However, in cases in which the system may be
structured as a mini-grid, NERC’s updated guidelines require customers to apply for a mini-grid
permit for any system larger than 100 kW (Sompura et al. 2021). For systems that generate less
than 100 kW of energy, mini-grid permits are optional. For systems larger than 1 MW in
installed generation capacity, even for captive systems where all energy will be used by the C&I
customer, further licenses are needed and will depend on the system specifications.

Interconnection agreements are needed for systems that will be tied to the grid. Depending on the
customer’s utility requirements, these agreements will detail potential impacts to the grid, pricing
structures for energy sell-back, and other details as necessary. For systems that will not be grid-
connected, interconnection agreements are not necessary. Be aware interconnection agreements
can be costly for larger projects, especially if the utility determines that off-site infrastructure
will have to be upgraded to support new loads. Customers can avoid this risk by minimizing the
amount of new load being introduced with a project and by communicating early and often with
the utility about the size and scale of the project, especially if there will be a PPA or energy
export aspect to the project.

Procuring permits and interconnection agreements will be handled primarily by the developer
chosen to install the system. However, a simple understanding of the requirements can help
customers ensure their developer is delivering services that fall within the legal requirements for
installation.

Once permitting and interconnection agreements are obtained, the owner and any authorities
having jurisdiction have given their notices to proceed, and construction has begun, an on-site
C&I energy project will typically follow a standard construction timeline. Before any
construction has commenced, the developer should submit a construction schedule that matches
the requirement of the RFP and that includes milestones for mobilization, submittal review, long-
lead equipment purchases, equipment commissioning, and timelines for each major phase of
construction. Major events such as outages or commissioning should be indicated on the
construction schedule, and notification should be provided to the customer well in advance of
these events. Any construction activities that may impact the operations of the facility or disrupt
parking, freight, or other access to the facility should be specifically discussed with the customer
and clearly indicated on the construction schedule.

For DB or EPC projects, the developer will manage submittal review and equipment purchases
internally, providing notice to the customer as these reviews and purchases take place. For DBB
projects, the customer may be more directly involved in mediating the submittal review process
between the developer and the A/E designer. The customer should regularly review submittals to
ensure the equipment being purchased meets the requirements of the contract.

As the major phases of construction begin to wind down, a critical part of the construction
process begins—commissioning. The U.S. Department of Energy defines commissioning for
new construction as being “done to ensure that systems, subsystems, and equipment in new

 35

buildings operate properly. It includes performing design reviews, functional testing, system
documentation, and operator training throughout the project to make sure the building meets the
requirements as intended by each building owner and as designed by the building architects and
engineers.” 17 All major pieces of equipment and systems should go through the commissioning
process, and a commissioning plan should be submitted, reviewed, and approved before the
purchasing of major equipment. Commissioning of a system is typically the last activity
undertaken before the utility issues permission to operate and a C&I customer accepts a system.
Therefore, it is important for a representative of the customer to be present during the
commissioning process. Often a contract will include a requirement for an independent
commissioning agent without financial ties to the developer. The final commissioning report
should include reports for each of the major pieces of equipment commissioned, and the owner’s
representative should pay close attention to any discrepancies uncovered during the
commissioning process. The C&I customer should require that discrepancies uncovered during
the commissioning process are corrected before accepting a system as complete.

4.7 Operate, Maintain, and Monitor Project
Once major construction operations have been completed and all systems have been
commissioned and accepted, a schedule for system O&M must be determined. If the C&I
customer has chosen to directly procure an energy system, at this point they will have full
ownership of the system and be responsible for system maintenance. However, C&I customers
that are not comfortable or experienced with energy system O&M can choose to contract with a
third party, typically a developer or EPC, to operate and maintain the system.

System O&M will depend on the system components and the location and environment in which
the PV system is installed. PV-based systems generally require little maintenance outside of
regular schedules, but other generation sources, such as diesel generators, may require regular
maintenance to preserve the component’s life.

For PV-based systems, regular maintenance may include tasks such as module cleaning,
trimming of overhanging vegetation, inspection for corrosion, inspection of electrical
connections, inspection of mounting systems, replacing failed inverters, and more. Regular
maintenance helps prevent the need for corrective maintenance; however, system operators
should still prepare for corrective maintenance over the lifetime of the system. This may include
tasks such as repairing faults, reinstalling software, or replacing any system components
(National Renewable Energy Laboratory et al. 2018). For systems with BESS components,
maintenance may also include performance monitoring, visual inspection of terminals, and
ensuring the battery is charging and discharging at rates to preserve the battery’s health
(Awopone and Boateng n.d.). More information on system maintenance can be found in Smart
Energy Solutions for Africa’s “Practical Operation and Maintenance Manual for Solar PV
Systems” (Awopone and Boateng n.d.).

To guide system O&M tasks, system owners may have a monitoring system. System monitors
may come as a feature of certain system components (such as inverters or microgrid controllers)
or may be added to a system as a separate component. System monitors provide owners and

4.8 Decommission Project
Once a system component reaches the end of its life, system owners can choose whether to
repair, reuse, recycle, or dispose of the component. For some system components, particularly
those for inverter-based resources, secondary markets and applications may extend the usable
lifetime of the component.

When an energy system has reached the end of its usable life, the disposal of materials must be
carefully managed to ensure they are disposed of safely. Several components, such as PV
modules and batteries, contain materials that can be hazardous to health and environment when
disposed of incorrectly. However, specialized facilities can help recover critical materials such as
metals, glass, and other usable materials from some components.

There are no formal regulations in Nigeria guiding the disposal of electronic components for
energy systems (Osude 2025). However, steps should still be taken carefully to ensure these
materials are disposed of properly. Some manufacturers may offer programs for component
recycling or disposal, and C&I customers and/or developers should check with the component
manufacturers to understand options for disposal.

5 Conclusion
Customers in Nigeria are looking to alternative energy sources—such as diesel gensets, PV and
BESS, geothermal, and hydropower—to boost their energy security and reliability. For C&I
customers, the procurement of an energy system can make a difference in ensuring energy
disruptions do not lead to financial losses and alternative energy systems can provide energy
security.

Nigerian C&I customers have several options for the procurement of both on-site and off-site
energy systems, particularly PV and BESS systems. Understanding the steps of the procurement
process can ensure customers are informed and are able to negotiate a system that meets their
energy needs while also tending to their cost considerations. As C&I customers across Nigeria
undertake the steps needed to procure their own energy system, they can reference this
guidebook as a tool to guide the process.
