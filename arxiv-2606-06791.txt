Magnetic Field Walls in Flat-band Superconductors
Guodong Jiang, Aaron Dunbrack, Tero T. Heikkilä, Päivi Törmä

Abstract
Superconductors of different types have distinct magnetic properties; for example, they can form Abrikosov vortices or alternating normal-superconducting domains. We predict that, in flat bands, a superconducting phase exhibiting walls of magnetic flux is stable in an applied magnetic field. This phase relies on the lack of a single-particle energy penalty for forming condensates of any momentum in flat bands and, consequently, their superconducting free energy being a negative and periodic function of the vector potential at low temperatures. Using a minimal lattice-periodic model of free energy, we study two types of soliton modes of the wall phase: the kink and breather solitons. They determine the lower critical field and the high-field behavior of the wall phase, respectively. The competition between the walls and vortices in flat bands is also discussed. Our results suggest that flat bands help sustain superconductivity in the presence of large magnetic fields.



Superconductors can be classified into different types according to their magnetic properties, and show distinct structures of magnetic flux
Campbell and Evetts 1972
;
Blatter et al. 1994
;
Abrikosov 2004
;
Babaev and Speight 2005
;
Moshchalkov et al. 2009
;
Agterberg et al. 2014
. These classifications are based on the ratio between the penetration depth and the coherence length, which are derived from phenomenological theories typically assuming dispersive bands. Similar understanding does not yet exist for flat bands, which are of interest due to the superconductivity observed in twisted two-dimensional (2D) crystals
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011
;
Cao et al. 2018a
;
Cao et al. 2018b
;
Andrei et al. 2021
;
Balents et al. 2020
;
Kennes et al. 2021
;
Törmä et al. 2022
and the role of quantum geometry
Provost and Vallee 1980
;
Resta 2011
uncovered in superfluidity
Peotta and Törmä 2015
;
Liang et al. 2017
;
Yu et al. 2025
.

In dispersive bands, a condensate of finite momentum costs kinetic energy. On the contrary, in flat bands, the lack of a Fermi surface and absence of such a cost allows forming condensates of any momentum. Recent studies on the density-wave orders in multi-orbital flat bands
Jiang and Barlas 2023
;
Chen and Huang 2023
;
Han et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2025
;
Wang and Huang 2025
;
Lamponen et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2026
have revealed that in some lattice models superconductivity is energetically favoured for condensate momenta
in the entire Cooper-pair Brillouin zone
Jiang and Barlas 2023
;
Sun et al. 2025
;
Dunbrack et al. 2026
. Furthermore, the pairing susceptibility at high temperatures depends on the condensate momentum only through the Bloch wavefunction form factors
Han et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2026
. These features reflect the flexibility of flat bands in supporting condensates of any momentum, in contrast to dispersive bands. In the presence of a magnetic field, the magnetic vector potential
A
couples with the crystal momentum in the same way as the condensate momentum,
Q
, by replacing
\textbf{Q}\rightarrow(2e/\hbar)\textbf{A}
, with
e
the electron charge and
\hbar
the reduced Planck constant. Therefore, the above properties imply that flat-band superconductors, compared to dispersive band ones, may respond in a fundamentally different way to an external magnetic field.

In this Article, we predict a superconducting phase exhibiting walls of magnetic flux in the bulk of the superconductor. The wall phase emerges when the free energy density
f_{s}(\textbf{A})
of the superconductor remains negative for all vector potentials
A
along a high-symmetry direction of the crystal. This typically happens in multi-orbital flat bands because the wavefunction form factor, rather than the kinetic energy as in dispersive bands, determines the depairing of the order parameter; see Fig.
1
(a)-(b) for an illustration of the pairing and free energy density in the presence of a magnetic field for flat and dispersive bands. The global negativity of the free energy density leads to two types of soliton solutions, kink and breather solitons, to the Maxwell equations, corresponding to periodically arranged “walls” of magnetic flux inside the superconducting bulk, see Fig.
1
(c). At lower magnetic fields, the walls are separated, with the wall thickness given by the penetration depth; at higher ones, they merge so that the field penetrates the whole bulk, and diamagnetism gradually vanishes. The comparison between the kink state and the uniform superconducting state determines the lower critical field, while the comparison between the breather state and the normal state, without considering other microscopic ingredients, shows an absence of the upper critical field for the wall phase. The walls can form textures, e.g., grids. The wall solution is favored over vortices with a large core size due to the high energy cost of the vortex core in a flat-band superconductor. Our findings introduce a mechanism for the coexistence of superconductivity and magnetism that originates from band quantum geometry rather than the spin symmetry of pairing or the creation of normal state regions, e.g., vortex cores.

Magnetic field dependence of the free energy in a flat band
. We now briefly discuss the periodicity of the free energy density and then analyse the superconducting state in the presence of a magnetic field, using a simple but generic
cosine model
that captures the negativity and periodicity of the free energy density. The free energy density
f_{s}(\textbf{A})
has the periodicity of the Cooper-pair Brillouin zone defined by a time-reversal invariant momentum (TRIM)
K
, i.e., when
K
and
-\textbf{K}
differ by a reciprocal lattice vector. This periodicity arises because the two ways of singlet pairing,
(\textbf{k}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\textbf{A}\uparrow,-\textbf{k}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\textbf{A}\downarrow)
and
(\textbf{k}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\textbf{A}+\textbf{K}\uparrow,-\textbf{k}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\textbf{A}+\textbf{K}\downarrow)
are equivalent. Here,
A
is
gauge-invariant
since we remove the phase gradient from the superconducting order parameter. The function
f_{s}(\textbf{A})
of a model Hamiltonian can be calculated from the grand potential formalism in a locally self-consistent manner (see Methods
I
).

We consider a superconducting bulk material with geometry depicted in Fig.
1
(c). The magnetic field
H
is applied along a long axis (
z
axis), and we disregard the edge effects distorting the field direction. The superconductivity is assumed to occur in a band which is flat in the
k_{x}-k_{y}
plane. The vector potential
A
is restricted in the
xy
plane since its
z
-component vanishes. The current density is given by (see Suppl. Sec.
I
for details)

\textbf{j}(\textbf{r})=-\nabla_{\textbf{A}}f_{s}(\textbf{A}(\textbf{r})).

(1)

We then solve the Maxwell equations

\nabla\times\textbf{A}(\textbf{r})=\textbf{B}(\textbf{r}),\,\,\,\nabla\times\textbf{B}(\textbf{r})=\mu_{0}\textbf{j}(\textbf{r}),

(2)

(
\mu_{0}
is the vacuum permeability) together with Eq. (
1
) for the fields
\textbf{A}(\textbf{r})
,
\textbf{B}(\textbf{r})
.

As an ansatz, we seek a continuum solution of the form
\textbf{A}\parallel\hat{\textbf{y}}
and with translational symmetry along the
y
axis. We also assume that the
y
axis is parallel to a high-symmetry direction of the crystal, along which
\textbf{j}\parallel\textbf{A}
. These assumptions imply
\textbf{A}(x)=A_{y}(x)\hat{\textbf{y}}
,
\textbf{B}(x)=B_{z}(x)\hat{\textbf{z}}
and
\textbf{j}(x)=j_{y}(x)\hat{\textbf{y}}
. Then Eq. (
2
) leads to a second-order differential equation of
A_{y}
,

A_{y}^{\prime\prime}(x)-\mu_{0}\partial_{A_{y}}f_{s}(A_{y}(x))=0,

(3)

where
f_{s}(A_{y})
stands for
f_{s}(A_{x}=0,A_{y})
. In the London theory of superconductors, one uses

f_{s}(A_{y})\approx f_{s}(0)+\frac{1}{2}D_{s}A_{y}^{2},

(4)

with
D_{s}
the superfluid weight (stiffness), to get the exponential solution,
A_{y}(x)\propto\exp\{\pm\sqrt{\mu_{0}D_{s}}x\}
. For flat bands, instead, we consider the following
cosine model
,

f_{s}(A_{y})=-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}\cos\left(\frac{2ea}{\hbar}A_{y}\right),

(5)

where the two parameters
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}
(
\delta_{1}>\delta_{2}>0
) represent the average and oscillation amplitude of the condensation energy density along the high-symmetry line, respectively. We assume that the neighboring TRIMs in the
k_{x}-k_{y}
plane are separated by
\pi/a
, with
a
the lattice constant in the
xy
plane, e.g., considering the tetragonal lattice case. This model captures a few salient features. First, the
\Gamma
-point
\textbf{A}=0
and its replica at TRIMs are the global minima of free energy, where the condensation energy density is
\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}
. Second,
f_{s}(A_{y})
is negative, and its oscillatory behavior is simplified as a cosine function. Third, near the
\Gamma
-point,
f_{s}(A_{y})
reduces to the London relation, Eq. (
4
), with
D_{s}=4(ea/\hbar)^{2}\delta_{2}
.

;
Sun et al. 2025
;
Dunbrack et al. 2026

in Suppl. Sec.
II

III

often contains higher-order harmonic terms, affecting our results only quantitatively. Combining Eqs. (
3
) and (
5
) leads to the time-independent sine-Gordon equation for
A_{y}
,

A_{y}^{\prime\prime}(x)-2\mu_{0}\delta_{2}\frac{ea}{\hbar}\sin\left(\frac{2ea}{\hbar}A_{y}(x)\right)=0,

(6)

which admits two different forms of real solutions, the kink and breather soliton modes
Kuplevakhsky and Glukhov 2006

Gal’Pern and Filippov 1982

Gurevich and Vinokur 2003

The kink soliton solution and lower critical field
. The kink soliton solution has the form (see Methods
II
)

A_{y}(x)=\pm\frac{\hbar}{ea}\bigg[\sin^{-1}\tanh\bigg(\frac{x}{\lambda_{L}}\bigg)+\frac{\pi}{2}\bigg],

(7)

where the
\pm
sign is the soliton charge, indicating whether
A_{y}
increases or decreases by a step of
\pi\hbar/ea
in the positive
x
direction, and
\lambda_{L}=\hbar/2ea\sqrt{\mu_{0}\delta_{2}}
is the penetration depth along the high-symmetry direction. The charge
+1
case is plotted in Fig.
2
(a). The derivative
A_{y}^{\prime}(x)=B_{z}(x)
vanishes at the two TRIMs that the solution connects, which we choose as
A_{y}=0
and
\pi\hbar/ea
, indicating that these global minima of free energy in
A
-space correspond to a superconducting state with
zero

B
field (hereafter referred to as the “zero-field state”).

Then,
B_{z}(x)
and
j_{y}(x)
can be calculated from the derivatives of Eq. (
7
), which are proportional to
\text{sech}(x/\lambda_{L})
and
\text{sech}(x/\lambda_{L})\tanh(x/\lambda_{L})
, respectively, and are plotted in Figs.
2
(b) and (c). We note that a wall where
B_{z}\neq 0
extends in the
yz
plane (see Fig.
1
(c)), and
A_{y}
,
B_{z}
, and
j_{y}
exponentially decay on its both sides, with a decay length
\lambda_{L}
. At the center of the wall,
f_{s}
reaches its maximum value,
f_{s,\text{max}}=-\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}<0
, so the material stays superconducting in the entire wall region, unlike in vortex sheets proposed by Landau and Lifshitz in superfluid helium
Landau and Lifshitz 1955
;
Volovik 2003
(see also Suppl. Sec.
V
). The bidirectional distribution of the current
j_{y}
in the wall forms a pair of tightly bound antiparallel current planes.

Similar to the vortices in type-II superconductors, the walls are unstable until a magnetic field is applied. From thermodynamics
Landau and Lifshitz 2013
, the free energy functional
F_{s}^{H}=\int\,\mathrm{d}^{3}rf_{s}^{H}(\textbf{r})
, with

f_{s}^{H}(\textbf{r})=f_{s}(\textbf{A}(\textbf{r}))+\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\textbf{B}(\textbf{r})^{2}-\textbf{H}\cdot\textbf{B}(\textbf{r}),

(8)

attains a minimum in an applied
H
field. Only when the applied field is above a threshold, the lower critical field
H_{c1,w}
, the last term of Eq. (
8
) overcomes the energy cost of its first two terms, stabilizing one of the two kink modes.

The lower critical field can be solved by equating
F_{s}^{H}
of the kink solution to that of the zero-field state (see Methods
III
). We find that for the cosine model,

H_{c1,w}=\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\hbar}{\mu_{0}ea\lambda_{L}}\approx\frac{0.2\phi_{0}}{\mu_{0}a\lambda_{L}},

(9)

where
\phi_{0}=\pi\hbar/e
is the magnetic flux quantum. Once
H
is above
H_{c1,w}
, multiple kinks can show up, as long as their separations are much larger than
\lambda_{L}
(see Fig.
1
(c)).

Walls at high fields, breather solitons
. As the applied magnetic field gets stronger, the kinks tend to increase their density and even overlap to absorb more magnetic flux. To understand their evolution with the field, we invoke the breather soliton solution to Eq. (
6
) (see Methods
II
):

A_{y}(x)=\pm\frac{\hbar}{ea}\bigg[\text{am}\left(\alpha^{-1/2}\frac{x}{\lambda_{L}}\bigg|\alpha\right)+\frac{\pi}{2}\bigg],

(10)

where
am
is the Jacobi amplitude function, with its second argument,
\alpha
, related to the minimum
B
field between the walls,
B_{\text{min}}
, through

\frac{1}{\alpha}=1+\frac{B_{\text{min}}^{2}}{4\mu_{0}\delta_{2}}.

(11)

In Eq. (
10
), the
\pm
sign in the solution is still the soliton charge, which is determined by
\text{sgn}(B_{\text{min}})
.

With Eq. (
10
),
B_{z}(x)
and
j_{y}(x)
are then found to be proportional to other Jacobi elliptic functions,
\text{dn}(\alpha^{-1/2}\frac{x}{\lambda_{L}}|\alpha)
, and
\text{cn}(\alpha^{-1/2}\frac{x}{\lambda_{L}}|\alpha)\text{sn}(\alpha^{-1/2}\frac{x}{\lambda_{L}}|\alpha)
, respectively. We plot
A_{y}
,
B_{z}
and
j_{y}
for two
\alpha
values,
0.99
and
0.5
in Fig.
3
. These profiles oscillate with the period

\Delta x=\frac{\hbar}{ea}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{\mu_{0}\delta_{2}}}K(\alpha)=2\alpha^{1/2}K(\alpha)\lambda_{L},

(12)

where
K(\alpha)
is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind, which diverges at
\alpha=1
. We note that the kink solution, Eq. (
7
), can be viewed as the breather one in the
\alpha\rightarrow 1
limit, such that
\Delta x\rightarrow\infty
. As
\alpha
decreases from 1, the period
\Delta x
sharply decreases to finite values.

The nonzero derivative,
A_{y}^{\prime}(x)=B_{\text{min}}
, of Eq. (
10
) at the TRIM
A_{y}
values indicates that these free energy minima correspond to a superconducting state with
nonzero

B
field. It enables the solution
A_{y}
to traverse many TRIMs, as shown in Fig.
3
(a) for the charge
+1
case. The maximum (
B_{\text{max}}
) and minimum (
B_{\text{min}}
)
B

3
(b). Comparing the blue with the green curves, we find that as
\alpha
decreases, both
B_{\text{max}}
and
B_{\text{min}}
increase, and finally become almost equal,

B_{\text{max}}\approx B_{\text{min}}\approx 2\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{0}\delta_{2}}{\alpha}},

(13)

and their difference,
B_{\text{max}}-B_{\text{min}}
, vanishes as
\sim\sqrt{\alpha\mu_{0}\delta_{2}}
(see Methods
IV
). This implies that in breathers of small
\alpha
values, the field penetrates the sample almost uniformly.

Magnetic susceptibility
. The thermodynamic stability of breathers at high fields can be studied by inserting the breather solution into Eq. (
8
) and comparing it with the normal state,
f_{n}^{H}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\mu_{0}H^{2}
. The difference between the two is

\overline{\delta f^{H}}(\alpha,H)\equiv\frac{1}{\Delta x}\int_{0}^{\Delta x}\,\mathrm{d}x[f_{s}^{H}(x)-f_{n}^{H}(x)],

(14)

where the average over the period
\Delta x
allows for comparing breathers of different
\alpha
values. In Fig.
4
(a), we plot
\overline{\delta f^{H}}(\alpha,H)
for several fields
H>H_{c1,w}
. At fixed
H
, the system seeks the
\alpha
value to minimize
\overline{\delta f^{H}}
, corresponding to finding the minima of the curves. At large fields, these minima approach a constant value, namely the average free energy density of the model,
-\delta_{1}
(see the dashed line in Fig.
4
(a)).

Reading off the
\alpha
values of the minima of each
H
curve in Fig.
4
(a), we then compute the average magnetic susceptibility of the minimal breather,

\chi(H)=1-\frac{1}{\mu_{0}H}\frac{1}{\Delta x}\int_{0}^{\Delta x}\,\mathrm{d}xB_{z}(x).

(15)

In particular, we plot
\chi(H)
for small fields
H\gtrsim H_{c1,w}
in Fig.
4
(b). It quickly drops to zero as the breather is formed. That is, the system loses its diamagnetism while preserving the superconducting gap.

The finding that
\overline{\delta f^{H}}
of the minimal breather never reaches zero implies an absence of the upper critical field for the wall phase within the cosine model. Nevertheless, as the field increases and the breathers become denser, their spacing eventually becomes comparable to the lattice scale. Then, additional ingredients need to be appended. One possibility is an additional energy penalty arising from the gap amplitude gradients, i.e., terms resembling those in more microscopic models that are proportional to the coherence lengths. We note that flat-band superconductors typically have a small coherence length (can reach the lattice scale) due to their strong-coupling nature
Iskin 2023
;
Chen and Law 2024
;
Thumin and Bouzerar 2025
, which leads to a small correction to the free energy density of the minimal breathers shown in Fig.
4
(a).

Other arrangements of walls
. With the knowledge of the dynamics of Eqs. (
1
) and (

in

Walls along different directions can also appear together to form a grid, with

Competition against vortices
. Additional microscopic ingredients are also pertinent to the competition between the wall and the vortex phases. The walls have one extra dimension compared to vortices; therefore, a sparse array of walls (see Fig.
1
(c)) can absorb much more magnetic flux, thus has a much lower energy than a vortex lattice with equal density (see Methods
V
). This implies that their energies are comparable only when vortices have formed a much denser lattice than the wall array. Therefore, we deduce that the walls almost always win the competition against vortices if their lower critical fields are close to each other,
H_{c1,v}\approx H_{c1,w}
.

(b).

Discussions and outlook
. We discovered a magnetic field wall phase in flat-band superconductors originating from the negativity of its free energy at any vector potential. Above its lower critical field, it allows an almost complete penetration of the field, providing a mechanism for stabilizing superconductivity in large fields. As there is a large freedom in the wall arrangements, desired configurations could be realized by purposeful design of planar defects for pinning
Dolan et al. 1989
;
Prozorov et al. 2009
;
Kalisky et al. 2010
.

Although flat-band superconductivity poses stringent constraints on the hosting materials and requires the order parameter to be comparable with the bandwidth, we showed that a negative channel of free energy along a high-symmetry direction is sufficient for the wall formation. This allows the wall phase to be found in more general band structures. Moreover, the global negativity of the free energy with the condensate momentum can be realized in other correlated states of flat bands
Han et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2026
. Since the condition for the order parameter to be greater than the bandwidth can be achieved more easily in these states, it is a topic of future research to find whether the wall phase can be extended there. Our results suggest that flat-band superconductors can sustain high magnetic fields and form wall textures; this broadens our fundamental understanding of superconductivity and the scope of its applications.

Methods

I
Local self-consistency approximation for the superconducting free energy density

The local self-consistency approximation ascribes the spatial dependence of the free energy density
f_{s}
to the gauge-invariant vector potential
A
in an inhomogeneous superconductor, i.e., we write
f_{s}(\textbf{r})\approx f_{s}(\textbf{A}(\textbf{r}))
. This approximation becomes exact when the nonlocal effect of Cooper pairs
Pippard 1953
is extremely weak, i.e., when the coherence length
\xi
and other microscopic length scales are much smaller than the penetration depth
\lambda_{L}
.

Within the local-consistency approximation, the free energy as a function of
A
is calculated from the grand potential
\Omega
,

F_{s}(\textbf{A})=\Omega(\textbf{A},\Delta_{\textbf{A}},\mu_{\textbf{A}})+\mu_{\textbf{A}}N_{e},

(M1)

with
\mu_{\textbf{A}}
the chemical potential (which also depends on
A
locally) and
N_{e}
the number of electrons. The grand potential is evaluated from the mean-field Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(\textbf{A},\Delta_{\textbf{A}},\mu_{\textbf{A}})
,

\Omega(\textbf{A},\Delta_{\textbf{A}},\mu_{\textbf{A}})=-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\text{Tr}\{e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal{H}}(\textbf{A},\Delta_{\textbf{A}},\mu_{\textbf{A}})}\},

(M2)

with
1/\beta=k_{B}T
where
T
is the temperature and
k_{B}
the Boltzmann constant. All of the extensive quantities above, i.e.,
\Omega
,
F_{s}
and
N_{e}
, are counted in a volume
\mathcal{V}
(much larger than the scale of
\xi
) in which
A
is treated as a constant. The order parameter
\Delta_{\textbf{A}}
and the chemical potential
\mu_{\textbf{A}}
are solved from the local self-consistency equations, with the vector potential
A
treated with Peierls substitution in the Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
(see Suppl. Sec.
I
for further details). Then the free energy density
f_{s}(\textbf{A})=F_{s}(\textbf{A})/\mathcal{V}
, and the current density
j
, as given by Eq. (
1
), depends on
A

only
.

II
Solutions to the time-independent sine-Gordon equation

The kink and breather solutions to the time-independent sine-Gordon equation

y^{\prime\prime}(x)-a\sin(by(x))=0\,\,\,(a,b>0)

(M3)

can be obtained analytically, where for our case,

a=2\mu_{0}\delta_{2}\frac{ea}{\hbar},\,\,\,b=\frac{2ea}{\hbar}.

(M4)

Integrating Eq. (
M3
) with
\,\mathrm{d}y
once leads to

y^{\prime}=\pm\sqrt{-\frac{2a}{b}\cos(by)+c_{1}}

(M5)

where
c_{1}>0
is a constant of integration. The
\pm
sign labels the two branches of the solution,
y^{\prime}>0
or
<0
, corresponding to the charge of the soliton mode. For the solution to be real, we require
c_{1}\geq\frac{2a}{b}
.

To proceed, it is sufficient to restrict to the domain
0\leq y\leq\frac{2\pi}{b}
for the
+
case, or
-\frac{2\pi}{b}\leq y\leq 0
for the
-
case, since the “source” of Eq. (
M3
),
a\sin(by)
has the periodicity of
\frac{2\pi}{b}
and vanishes at
y=0
. Then, the solution being a kink or a breather depends on whether

y^{\prime}|_{y=0}=0\,\,\,\text{or}\,\,\,\neq 0,

(M6)

i.e.,
c_{1}=\frac{2a}{b}
or
c_{1}>\frac{2a}{b}
.

II.1
Kink solution

For the kink solution (
c_{1}=\frac{2a}{b}
), integration of Eq. (
M5
) leads to

y=\pm\bigg[\frac{2}{b}\sin^{-1}\tanh(\sqrt{ab}(x-x_{0}))+\frac{\pi}{b}\bigg],

(M7)

where
x_{0}
is the second constant of integration, corresponding to the center of the kink (can be taken as 0).

II.2
Breather solution

For the breather solution, since
c_{1}>\frac{2a}{b}
, we introduce another parameter
\alpha
to replace
c_{1}
,

\frac{1}{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{c_{1}b}{4a},

(M8)

with
0<\alpha<1
. Notice that (we substituted
A_{y}\rightarrow y
)

y^{\prime}|_{y=0}=\pm\sqrt{c_{1}-\frac{2a}{b}}\equiv B_{\text{min}},

(M9)

so
B_{\text{min}}
and
\alpha
has the relation

\frac{1}{\alpha}=1+\frac{b}{4a}B_{\text{min}}^{2},

(M10)

which gives Eq. (
11
).

Substituting the variable
y\rightarrow\frac{2\theta+\pi}{b}
in Eq. (
M5
), we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}&x=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{ab}}\int\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\theta}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{c_{1}b}{4a}-\sin^{2}\theta}}\\
\Longrightarrow\,\,\,&x-x_{0}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{ab}}F(\theta|\alpha),\end{split}

(M11)

where
F(\theta|\alpha)
is the elliptic function of the first kind

F(\theta|\alpha)=\int_{0}^{\theta}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1-\alpha\sin^{2}\theta^{\prime}}}.

(M12)

The inverse of Eq. (
M11
) is

\theta=\pm\text{am}\left(\sqrt{\frac{ab}{\alpha}}(x-x_{0})\bigg|\alpha\right),

(M13)

which finally leads to Eq. (
10
).

III
Calculation of the lower critical field

The lower critical field is calculated by equating the free energy
F_{s}^{H}
of the superconducting state with a single kink to that of the zero-field state:

\int\,\mathrm{d}^{3}r[f_{s}^{H}(\textbf{r},\text{kink})-f_{s}^{H}(\textbf{r},\text{zero-field})]=0.

(M14)

Since both states are translationally invariant in the
yz
plane, it can be expressed as an integral along the
x
direction,

S_{yz}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}x[f_{s}^{H}(x,\text{kink})-f_{s}^{H}(x,\text{zero-field})]=0,

(M15)

where
S_{yz}
is the area of kink wall in the
yz
plane. Here

\displaystyle f^{H}_{s}(x,\text{kink})=f_{s}(A_{y}(x))+\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}B_{z}(x)^{2}-HB_{z}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}\cos\bigg(\frac{2ea}{\hbar}A_{y}(x)\bigg)+\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}B_{z}(x)^{2}-HB_{z}(x),

(M16)

with
A_{y}(x)
the kink solution given by Eq. (
7
) (consider the charge
+1
case), and
B_{z}(x)
its derivative,

B_{z}(x)=A_{y}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\hbar}{ea\lambda_{L}}\text{sech}\left(\frac{x}{\lambda_{L}}\right).

(M17)

Furthermore,

f^{H}_{s}(x,\text{zero-field})=f_{s}(0)=-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}.

(M18)

It is convenient to introduce dimensionless variables

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{y}=A_{y}/\bigg(\frac{\hbar}{ea}\bigg),

\displaystyle\tilde{x}=\frac{x}{\lambda_{L}},

\displaystyle\tilde{H}=H/\bigg(\frac{\hbar}{\mu_{0}ea\lambda_{L}}\bigg),

\displaystyle\tilde{B}_{z}=B_{z}/\bigg(\frac{\hbar}{ea\lambda_{L}}\bigg).

(M19)

Then the equation to solve becomes (besides a factor
\delta_{2}\lambda_{L}S_{yz}
)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}\big[1-\cos(2\tilde{A}_{y}(\tilde{x}))+2\tilde{B}_{z}(\tilde{x})^{2}-4\tilde{H}_{c1,w}\tilde{B}_{z}(\tilde{x})\big]=0.

(M20)

Performing the integral gives
\tilde{H}_{c1,w}=2/\pi
.

IV
Maximum and minimum
B
fields

Using the relations

\displaystyle A_{y}^{\prime}(x)=B_{z}(x),

\displaystyle B_{z}^{\prime}(x)=-\mu_{0}j_{y}(x),

\displaystyle j_{y}(x)=-\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}A_{y}}f_{s}(A_{y}(x)),

(M21)

we find

\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\int\,\mathrm{d}(B_{z}(x))^{2}=\int j_{y}(x)\,\mathrm{d}A_{y}=-\int\,\mathrm{d}f_{s}.

(M22)

Integrating from
B_{\text{min}}
to
B_{\text{max}}
, this equation leads to

\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}(B_{\text{max}}^{2}-B_{\text{min}}^{2})=f_{s,\text{max}}-f_{s,\text{min}}=2\delta_{2},

(M23)

which holds for the breather mode of any
\alpha
value (including
\alpha\rightarrow 1
, the kink mode). This relation dictates that as
B_{\text{min}}
increases (consider the charge
+1
case,
B_{\text{min}}>0
) or
\alpha
decreases,
B_{\text{max}}
also increases, and finally the two become almost equal. Using the relation between
B_{\text{min}}
and
\alpha
, we obtain Eq. (
13
). Their difference vanishes as

B_{\text{max}}-B_{\text{min}}=\frac{2\mu_{0}(f_{s,\text{max}}-f_{s,\text{min}})}{B_{\text{max}}+B_{\text{min}}}\approx\sqrt{\alpha\mu_{0}\delta_{2}}.

(M24)

V
Energy of a wall array vs. a vortex lattice

We compare the energy of a sparse wall array (see Fig.
1
(c)) with a vortex lattice of equal density, i.e., the separation between neighboring kinks,
l
, in the array is equal to the distance between neighboring vortices in a vortex lattice. When these magnetic flux textures are stabler than the zero-field state, the last term of Eq. (
8
),
-\textbf{H}\cdot\textbf{B}(\textbf{r})
dominates over the other terms; therefore, the total energy gain at fixed
H
is approximately

L_{z}\int\,\mathrm{d}^{2}r\textbf{H}\cdot\textbf{B}(\textbf{r})=L_{z}H\Phi,

(M25)

where
L_{z}
is the sample dimension along
z
direction, and
\Phi=\int\,\mathrm{d}^{2}rB_{z}(\textbf{r})
is the total flux through a magnetic field texture.

The average magnitude of the
B
field in the flux region is
\sim\phi_{0}/a\lambda_{L}
for walls vs.
\sim\phi_{0}/\lambda_{L}^{2}
for vortices, while the area where the flux penetrates through is
\sim\lambda_{L}l
for walls vs.
\sim\lambda_{L}^{2}
for vortices. Therefore, the ratio between the total fluxes in the two magnetic field textures is

\frac{\Phi_{w}}{\Phi_{v}}\sim\frac{\frac{\phi_{0}}{a\lambda_{L}}\cdot\lambda_{L}l}{\frac{\phi_{0}}{\lambda_{L}^{2}}\cdot\lambda_{L}^{2}}=\frac{l}{a}\gg 1.

(M26)

We conclude that the wall array absorbs much more magnetic flux than the vortex lattice, therefore, according to Eq. (
M25
), the former has a much lower energy than the latter. For the energy of a vortex lattice to be comparable to a wall array, the vortex lattice has to be much denser than the wall array, requiring
H_{c1,v}<H_{c1,w}
.

VI
Vortex of the cosine model in the extreme type-II limit

We calculate the lower critical field,
H_{c1,v}
, of the cosine model for the vortex state in the extreme type-II limit, i.e., when the vortex core has a radius
r_{0}\ll\lambda_{L}
. In the process, we compare the behavior of
H_{c1,v}
between the flat and the dispersive bands in this limit.

When
\kappa=\lambda_{L}/\xi\gg 1
(
\xi
is typically of the same size as the core radius
r_{0}
), we still use the local self-consistency approximation and solve Eq. (
2
) in cylindrical coordinates
Campbell and Evetts 1972
. Note that in multi-orbital systems, there can be multiple
\lambda_{L}
and
\xi
, and they can be anisotropic; by
\lambda_{L}\gg\xi
, we mean that the variations of order parameters in real space are much slower than that of the magnetic field, such that the nonlocal effect can be neglected.

We generalize the cosine model, Eq. (
5
), to circular geometry,

f_{s}(\textbf{A})=-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}\cos\left(\frac{2ea}{\hbar}|A|\right),

(M27)

where
|A|
is the magnitude of
A
. The model of Eq. (
M27
) takes into account the boundedness of
f_{s}(\textbf{A})
and neglects the lattice anisotropy. Then, we write
\textbf{A}(\textbf{r})=A(r)\hat{\textbf{e}}_{\varphi}
,
\textbf{B}(\textbf{r})=B(r)\hat{\textbf{z}}
, and
\textbf{j}(\textbf{r})=j(r)\hat{\textbf{e}}_{\varphi}
, and Eq. (
2
) becomes

A^{\prime}(r)+\frac{A(r)}{r}=B(r),\,\,\,-B^{\prime}(r)=\mu_{0}j(r).

(M28)

By the definition of current density, Eq. (
1
), we have

\textbf{j}=-\frac{2ea}{\hbar}\delta_{2}\sin\left(\frac{2ea}{\hbar}|A|\right)\hat{\textbf{e}}_{\textbf{A}}=-\frac{2ea}{\hbar}\delta_{2}\sin\left(\frac{2ea}{\hbar}A\right)\hat{\textbf{e}}_{\varphi},

(M29)

where
\hat{\textbf{e}}_{\textbf{A}}
is the unit vector along the direction of
A
;
A(r)<0
, so
\hat{\textbf{e}}_{\textbf{A}}=-\hat{\textbf{e}}_{\varphi}
. This leads to

j(r)=-\frac{2ea}{\hbar}\delta_{2}\sin\left(\frac{2ea}{\hbar}A(r)\right).

(M30)

We introduce dimensionless quantities

\displaystyle\tilde{r}=r/\lambda_{L},

\displaystyle\tilde{A}=A/(\hbar/ea),

\displaystyle\tilde{B}=B/(\hbar/ea\lambda_{L}),

\displaystyle\tilde{H}=H/(\hbar/\mu_{0}ea\lambda_{L}).

(M31)

Then, Eq. (
M28
) leads to the following equation of
\tilde{A}(\tilde{r})
:

\tilde{A}^{\prime\prime}+\frac{\tilde{A}^{\prime}}{\tilde{r}}-\bigg[\frac{\tilde{A}}{\tilde{r}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\sin(2\tilde{A})\bigg]=0.

(M32)

Like the usual treatment of a single vortex in the
\kappa\gg 1
limit
Abrikosov 2017
, we split the vortex into the core region,
r<r_{0}
, and the outer region,
r_{0}<r<R_{0}
, where
R_{0}
is equal to a few times
\lambda_{L}
(see Extended Data Fig.
1
). The core is assumed to be completely normal, so
B(r)=\mu_{0}H
there, and hence we neglect the contribution from the term of the gradient on the amplitude of the order parameter to the free energy.

Extended Data Fig.

L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are two loops of radius

The gauge-invariant vector potential
A(r)
exponentially decays as
r\rightarrow\infty
, therefore, the outer loop integral at
R_{0}
vanishes,
\oint_{L_{2}}\textbf{A}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{l}=0
. The flux quantization condition, which arises from the quantized accumulated phase change of the order parameter along a closed loop, is thus

\oint_{L_{1}}\textbf{A}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{l}=-\phi_{0}.

(M33)

Put into dimensionless quantities, Eq. (
M33
) becomes

\tilde{A}(\tilde{r}_{0})=-\frac{\beta}{2\tilde{r}_{0}}.

(M34)

where
\beta\equiv a/\lambda_{L}
. For typical superconductors with dispersive bands,
\beta\sim 10^{-3}-10^{-2}
; for flat bands, its value depends on the strength of interaction and quantum geometry of the band. Before solving Eq. (
M32
), we derive the single vortex solution to the London model for dispersive bands.

VI.1
The London model of dispersive bands

To better compare the single vortex in a dispersive and a flat band, we first derive
H_{c1,v}
for the London model in the
\kappa\gg 1
limit. We expand Eq. (
M27
) up to the
O(A^{2})
term as an approximation of the free energy density for superconductors with dispersive bands at low temperatures:

f_{s}(\textbf{A})=-(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})+\frac{\delta_{2}}{2}\bigg(\frac{2ea}{\hbar}A\bigg)^{2}.

(M35)

Then the differential equation for
\tilde{A}
, Eq. (
M32
), becomes the modified Bessel equation,

\tilde{A}^{\prime\prime}+\frac{\tilde{A}^{\prime}}{\tilde{r}}-\bigg(\frac{1}{\tilde{r}^{2}}+1\bigg)\tilde{A}=0,

(M36)

whose (real) solution is the modified Bessel function of the second kind,

\tilde{A}(\tilde{r})=-\frac{\beta}{2}K_{1}(\tilde{r}).

(M37)

Here the prefactor is chosen to be
-\beta/2
to satisfy Eq. (
M33
) in the
\tilde{r}_{0}\rightarrow 0
limit, considering the asymptotic behavior of
K_{1}(\tilde{r})\sim\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}+\frac{\tilde{r}}{2}\ln\frac{\tilde{r}}{2}
as
\tilde{r}\rightarrow 0
. In Extended Data Fig.
2
(a), we plot
\tilde{A}(\tilde{r})
of Eq. (

) and its

\tilde{B}(\tilde{r})=\tilde{A}^{\prime}(\tilde{r})+\tilde{A}(\tilde{r})/\tilde{r}
for the region
\tilde{r}>\tilde{r}_{0}
.

To calculate the lower critical field, we equate the free energy line density of a vortex to that of the zero-field state. The free energy density difference is

\delta f^{H}(r)=f_{n}^{H}-f_{s}^{H}(\text{homog.})=-\frac{1}{2}\mu_{0}H^{2}+\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}

(M38)

in the region
r<r_{0}
; and

\displaystyle\delta f^{H}(r)=

\displaystyle f_{s}^{H}(r,\text{vortex})-f_{s}^{H}(r,\text{homog.})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\delta_{2}\bigg(\frac{ea}{\hbar}A(r)\bigg)^{2}+\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}B(r)^{2}-HB(r)

(M39)

in the region
r_{0}<r<R_{0}
. Put into dimensionless quantities, the difference of the free energy line density is

\displaystyle\delta F^{H}/L=\int\,\mathrm{d}^{2}r\delta f^{H}=\delta_{2}\lambda_{L}^{2}\bigg\{(1+d-2\tilde{H}^{2})\pi\tilde{r}_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle+\int_{\tilde{r}_{0}}^{\tilde{R}_{0}}2\pi\tilde{r}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{r}\big[2\tilde{A}(\tilde{r})^{2}+2\tilde{B}(\tilde{r})^{2}-4\tilde{H}\tilde{B}(\tilde{r})\big]\bigg\},

(M40)

where
d=\delta_{1}/\delta_{2}
and
L
is the length along
z
direction. Then, the critical field
\tilde{H}_{c1,v}
is solved from
\delta F^{H}/L=0
.

In the literature, three approximations are made to Eq. (
VI.1
) to obtain an analytical expression of
H_{c1,v}
for
\kappa\gg 1

Abrikosov 2017
. (i) First, the contribution from the normal region is neglected, since it is proportional to
\tilde{r}_{0}^{2}\sim\kappa^{-2}
. This approximation is justified as
r_{0}
approaches the lattice scale, but fails if
a\ll r_{0}
, even if
r_{0}\ll\lambda_{L}
. For the normal core energy to be negligible, we require

(1+d-2\tilde{H}^{2})\pi\tilde{r}_{0}^{2}\ll\int_{\tilde{r}_{0}}^{\tilde{R}_{0}}4\pi\tilde{r}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{r}\tilde{A}(\tilde{r})^{2}.

(M41)

Here, the
\tilde{H}^{2}
term can be neglected since roughly
\tilde{H}\propto\beta
; we choose
d=-0.9998
for the dispersive band model Eq. (
M35
), implying that
f_{s}
reaches zero at the critical vector potential
\tilde{A}_{c}=\sqrt{(1+d)/2}=0.01
. Then it leads to

10^{-4}\tilde{r}_{0}^{2}\ll\beta^{2}\ln\kappa,\,\,\,\text{or}\,\,\,\,\,\,r_{0}\ll 100(\ln\kappa)^{1/2}a

(M42)

which holds for
r_{0}\gtrsim a
only. (ii) Second, the leading contribution from
\tilde{A}^{2}
and
\tilde{B}^{2}
to the second line of Eq. (
VI.1
) is from
\tilde{r}_{0}<\tilde{r}<1
, so one can set
\tilde{R}_{0}=1
for their integrals. In this region,
\tilde{A}(\tilde{r})^{2}\gg\tilde{B}(\tilde{r})^{2}
(see Extended Data Fig.
2
(a)), so the
\tilde{B}^{2}
term can also be neglected. The
\tilde{H}\tilde{B}
term can be rewritten as (the upper limit of the integral is set to

)

-4\tilde{H}\int_{\tilde{r}_{0}}^{\infty}2\pi\tilde{r}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{r}\tilde{B}=-4\tilde{H}\frac{\phi_{0}}{\lambda_{L}^{2}\hbar/ea\lambda_{L}}=-4\pi\beta\tilde{H}.

(M43)

As a result, the equation
\delta F^{H}/L=0
reduces to

\int_{\tilde{r}_{0}}^{1}4\pi\tilde{r}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{r}\tilde{A}^{2}-4\pi\beta\tilde{H}_{c1,v}=0.

(M44)

(iii) Third, an inverse approximation,
\tilde{A}(\tilde{r})\approx-\beta/2\tilde{r}
, is made for Eq. (
M37
) in the region
\tilde{r}_{0}<\tilde{r}<1
(see Extended Data Fig.
2
(a) for a comparison between the two functions). With these approximations, one then integrates Eq. (
M44
) to get

\tilde{H}_{c1,v}=\frac{\beta}{4}\ln\frac{\lambda_{L}}{r_{0}}.

(M45)

Putting back the units (Eq. (
VI
)), we get

H_{c1,v}=\frac{\phi_{0}}{4\pi\mu_{0}\lambda_{L}^{2}}\ln\frac{\lambda_{L}}{r_{0}}.

(M46)

In Extended Data Fig.
2
(b), we provide numerical calculations to support this analysis. For
r_{0}\gg a
, the normal core increases the free energy substantially via the first term of Eq. (
VI.1
), making
\tilde{H}_{c1,v}
larger than the value given by Eq. (
M45
).

I
Gauge invariance of free energy and supercurrent

In BCS theory, the supercurrent density
j
is given by

\textbf{j}=-D_{s}\left(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}+\frac{\hbar}{2e}\nabla\varphi\right)=-D_{s}\textbf{A},

(S1)

where
D_{s}
is the superfluid weight,
\nabla\varphi
is the gradient of the order parameter phase, while
\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}
and
\textbf{A}=\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}+\frac{\hbar}{2e}\nabla\varphi
are the gauge-dependent and gauge-invariant vector potentials, respectively (the notation
\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}
comes from the gauge transformation in electrodynamics,
\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}=\textbf{A}+\nabla\chi
, with
\chi
a scalar function). Therefore,
j
is gauge-invariant. Below, we derive the gauge invariance within the mean-field framework and show that it is still valid when
j
has a nonlinear relation with
A
.

I.1
Current operator and gauge invariance

The vector potential
\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}
enters a tight-binding non-interacting Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
through the Peierls substitution,

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)})=\sum_{i\alpha,j\beta,\sigma}t^{\sigma}_{i\alpha,j\beta}e^{-i\frac{e}{\hbar}\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}\cdot(\textbf{r}_{i\alpha}-\textbf{r}_{j\beta})}c_{i\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{j\beta\sigma}.

(S2)

where
i,j
label the unit cell,
\alpha,\beta
label the orbitals inside a unit cell, and
\sigma
labels the spin. Here
\textbf{r}_{i\alpha}=\textbf{R}_{i}+\textbf{x}_{\alpha}
is the position of orbital
i\alpha
, with
\textbf{R}_{i}
,
\textbf{x}_{\alpha}
the position of unit cell
i
, and the position of orbital
\alpha
with respect to the unit cell, respectively. Spin-orbit coupling is neglected, and
t^{\sigma}_{i\alpha,j\beta}
is the parameter of hopping from orbital
j\beta
to
i\alpha
for spin
\sigma
, while
c^{\dagger},c
are creation and annihilation operators. Eq. (
S2
) describes the Hamiltonian in a local region where
\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}
can be viewed as constant.

On the other hand, the phase gradient enters the interaction through mean-field decoupling. We start from an on-site density-density interaction (both intra- and interorbital interactions
U_{\alpha\beta}
are involved)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{I}=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i,\alpha\beta}U_{\alpha\beta}\hat{n}_{i\alpha\uparrow}\hat{n}_{i\beta\downarrow}

(S3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\textbf{k}\textbf{k}^{\prime}\textbf{q},\alpha\beta}\frac{U_{\alpha\beta}}{N_{c}}c_{\textbf{k}+\textbf{q},\alpha\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{-\textbf{k}+\textbf{q},\beta\downarrow}^{\dagger}c_{-\textbf{k}^{\prime}+\textbf{q},\beta\downarrow}c_{\textbf{k}^{\prime}+\textbf{q},\alpha\uparrow},

where

c_{\textbf{k}\alpha\sigma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{c}}}\sum_{i}e^{-i\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{r}_{i\alpha}}c_{i\alpha\sigma},

(S4)

with
N_{c}
the number of unit cells. We assume singlet pairing, and do not require all
U_{\alpha\beta}
to be attractive, but the results can be generalized to other pairings. Assuming that the condensate is formed by Cooper pairs
(\textbf{k}+\textbf{q}\uparrow,-\textbf{k}+\textbf{q}\downarrow)
with fixed
q
(
2\textbf{q}=\nabla\varphi
is the phase gradient), mean-field decoupling leads to the reduced interaction

\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{\text{MF}}_{I}(\textbf{q})=\sum_{\textbf{k},\alpha\beta}(\Delta_{\alpha\beta}c_{\textbf{k}+\textbf{q},\alpha\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{-\textbf{k}+\textbf{q},\beta\downarrow}^{\dagger}+h.c.)+N_{c}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\frac{|\Delta_{\alpha\beta}|^{2}}{U_{\alpha\beta}},

(S5)

with order parameters

\Delta_{\alpha\beta}=-\frac{U_{\alpha\beta}}{N_{c}}\sum_{\textbf{k}}\langle c_{-\textbf{k}+\textbf{q},\beta\downarrow}c_{\textbf{k}+\textbf{q},\alpha\uparrow}\rangle_{\textbf{A}^{(\chi)},\textbf{q}},

(S6)

where
\langle\rangle_{\textbf{A}^{(\chi)},\textbf{q}}
represents the average in the condensate.

Note that both
\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}
and
q
parameterize the total Hamiltonian

\hat{\mathcal{H}}(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)},\textbf{q})=\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)})+\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{\text{MF}}_{I}(\textbf{q})-\mu\sum_{i\alpha\sigma}\hat{n}_{i\alpha\sigma},

(S7)

with
\mu
the chemical potential. Then,
\Delta_{\alpha\beta}
and
\mu
depend on
\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}
and
q
implicitly through self-consistency equations. We define the total current operator
\hat{\textbf{J}}
as
Mahan 2013
(
\textbf{J}=\textbf{j}\mathcal{V}
, with
\mathcal{V}
the volume)

\hat{\textbf{J}}(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)},\textbf{q})=\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{\mathcal{H}}(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)},\textbf{q}),\hat{\textbf{P}}],

(S8)

with
\hat{\textbf{P}}=-e\sum_{i\alpha\sigma}\textbf{r}_{i\alpha}c_{i\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{i\alpha\sigma}
the charge polarization. This definition ensures charge conservation by itself. The two terms in
\hat{\textbf{J}}
contributed by
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)})
and
\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{\text{MF}}_{I}(\textbf{q})
are

\displaystyle\hat{\textbf{J}}_{1}=

\displaystyle-\frac{ie}{\hbar}\sum_{i\alpha,j\beta,\sigma}t^{\sigma}_{i\alpha,j\beta}e^{-i\frac{e}{\hbar}\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}\cdot(\textbf{r}_{i\alpha}-\textbf{r}_{j\beta})}(\textbf{r}_{j\beta}-\textbf{r}_{i\alpha})c_{i\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{j\beta\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)})}{\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}},

(S9)

and

\displaystyle\hat{\textbf{J}}_{2}=

\displaystyle\bigg[\frac{ie}{\hbar N_{c}}\sum_{i\alpha\beta,\textbf{k}\textbf{q}^{\prime}}\Delta_{\alpha\beta}(\textbf{r}_{i\beta}e^{i\textbf{q}^{\prime}\cdot\textbf{r}_{i\beta}}c_{\textbf{k}+\textbf{q},\alpha\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{-\textbf{k}+\textbf{q}-\textbf{q}^{\prime},\beta\downarrow}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle+\textbf{r}_{i\alpha}e^{i\textbf{q}^{\prime}\cdot\textbf{r}_{i\alpha}}c_{\textbf{k}+\textbf{q}-\textbf{q}^{\prime},\alpha\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{-\textbf{k}+\textbf{q},\beta\downarrow}^{\dagger})\bigg]+h.c.,

(S10)

respectively, whereas the chemical potential term does not contribute. Using Eq. (
S6
), it is straightforward to verify that
\hat{\textbf{J}}_{2}
has zero expectation value in the condensate, therefore,

\langle\hat{\textbf{J}}\rangle_{\textbf{A}^{(\chi)},\textbf{q}}=\langle\hat{\textbf{J}}_{1}\rangle_{\textbf{A}^{(\chi)},\textbf{q}}=-\bigg\langle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)})}{\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}}\bigg\rangle_{\textbf{A}^{(\chi)},\textbf{q}}.

(S11)

With these established, we perform a gauge transformation, i.e., change of variables
\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}\rightarrow\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}-(\hbar/e)\delta\textbf{q}
,
\textbf{q}\rightarrow\textbf{q}+\delta\textbf{q}
to
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)},\textbf{q})
. This transformation amounts to replacing the basis
c_{\textbf{k}\alpha\sigma}\rightarrow\tilde{c}_{\textbf{k}\alpha\sigma}\equiv c_{\textbf{k}+\delta\textbf{q},\alpha\sigma}
while keeping the variables unchanged in these second-quantized operators, i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}-\frac{\hbar}{e}\delta\textbf{q},\textbf{q}+\delta\textbf{q})=\tilde{\hat{\mathcal{H}}}(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)},\textbf{q}),

\displaystyle\hat{\textbf{J}}(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}-\frac{\hbar}{e}\delta\textbf{q},\textbf{q}+\delta\textbf{q})=\tilde{\hat{\textbf{J}}}(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)},\textbf{q}),

(S12)

where
\tilde{\hat{\mathcal{H}}},\tilde{\hat{\textbf{J}}}
refer to the same operators as
\hat{\mathcal{H}},\hat{\textbf{J}}
but with new basis
\tilde{c}
. Then, the expectation values of these operators are invariants, e.g.,
\textbf{J}(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)},\textbf{q})\equiv\langle\hat{\textbf{J}}(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)},\textbf{q})\rangle_{\textbf{A}^{(\chi)},\textbf{q}}
satisfies

\textbf{J}(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}-\frac{\hbar}{e}\delta\textbf{q},\textbf{q}+\delta\textbf{q})=\textbf{J}(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)},\textbf{q}).

(S13)

Therefore, we conclude that the expectation values must be a function of
\textbf{A}=\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}+\frac{\hbar}{e}\textbf{q}
. It is noteworthy that Eq. (
S6
), and the other self-consistency equation determining
\mu
(the number equation),

\sum_{i\alpha\sigma}\langle\hat{n}_{i\alpha\sigma}\rangle_{\textbf{A}^{(\chi)},\textbf{q}}=N_{e},

(S14)

with
N_{e}
the average number of electrons in the condensate, are both in terms of expectation values, therefore,
\Delta_{\alpha\beta}
and
\mu
are also functions of
A
, and we denote them as
\Delta_{\textbf{A},\alpha\beta}
and
\mu_{\textbf{A}}
.

We emphasize that the gauge-invariance derived here does not rely on a specific relation between
J
and
A
. We do not seek an expansion of
\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}
in the Peierls substitution coefficient
e^{-i\frac{e}{\hbar}\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}\cdot(\textbf{r}_{i\alpha}-\textbf{r}_{j\beta})}
, so we lose no higher order terms. Therefore, the gauge invariance applies to our scenarios where
A
can reach the order of magnitude of the reciprocal lattice,
1/a
.

We also note that the superconductor cannot stay in a state with a constant vector potential
\textbf{A}_{0}
and nonzero current density
\textbf{j}=\textbf{j}_{\textbf{A}_{0}}\neq 0
in the entire space. The reason is that such a state simultaneously requires the
B
field to be spatial dependent (since
\nabla\times\textbf{B}=\mu_{0}\textbf{j}
), and
\textbf{B}=0
(since
\nabla\times\textbf{A}_{0}=0
), a contradiction. Therefore, in the region where
\textbf{j}\neq 0
, both
A
and
j
must vary in space. Throughout the derivation of the gauge-invariance above, we implicitly use the local self-consistency assumption that
A
can be locally treated as a constant, and the local self-consistency is established. This assumption is equivalent to stating that
\lambda_{L}\gg\xi
. Therefore, the results that the local physical quantities
j
,
\Delta
, and
\mu
are functions of a single variable
A
are only valid in this limit. As
\xi
becomes large,
A
is no longer directly associated with these quantities.

I.2
Relation between current and free energy

To calculate the current from the free energy, it is sufficient to consider a condensate with
\textbf{q}=0
and vector potential
A
. Since
\Delta
and

S7

\hat{\mathcal{H}}(\textbf{A},\Delta_{\textbf{A}},\mu_{\textbf{A}})
. Then, Eq. (

\displaystyle\textbf{J}(\textbf{A})=

\displaystyle-\bigg\langle\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial\textbf{A}}\bigg|_{\Delta_{\textbf{A}},\mu_{\textbf{A}}}\bigg\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\Xi}\text{Tr}\bigg\{\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial\textbf{A}}\bigg|_{\Delta_{\textbf{A}},\mu_{\textbf{A}}}e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal{H}}}\bigg\}=-\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\textbf{A}}\bigg|_{\Delta_{\textbf{A}},\mu_{\textbf{A}}},

(S15)

where
\Xi=\text{Tr}\{e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal{H}}}\}
is the grand partition function and

\Omega(\textbf{A},\Delta_{\textbf{A}},\mu_{\textbf{A}})=-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\text{Tr}\{e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal{H}}(\textbf{A},\Delta_{\textbf{A}},\mu_{\textbf{A}})}\}

(S16)

\Delta_{\textbf{A}}
and

\hat{\mathcal{H}}
with

.

The Helmholtz free energy of the grand canonical ensemble is
F_{s}(\textbf{A})\equiv\Omega(\textbf{A},\Delta_{\textbf{A}},\mu_{\textbf{A}})+\mu_{\textbf{A}}N_{e}
, and we find

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}F_{s}}{\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{A}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\textbf{A}}\bigg|_{\Delta_{\textbf{A}},\mu_{\textbf{A}}}+\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\Delta_{\textbf{A}}}\bigg|_{\textbf{A},\mu_{\textbf{A}}}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\Delta_{\textbf{A}}}{\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{A}}

\displaystyle+\bigg(\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\mu_{\textbf{A}}}\bigg|_{\textbf{A},\Delta_{\textbf{A}}}+N_{e}\bigg)\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\textbf{A}}}{\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{A}}=\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\textbf{A}}\bigg|_{\Delta_{\textbf{A}},\mu_{\textbf{A}}},

(S17)

where the second and third terms cancel by Eq. (
S6
) and (

\textbf{J}(\textbf{A})=-\frac{\,\mathrm{d}F_{s}}{\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{A}}.

(S18)

II
Free energy of flat-band superconductors at nonzero vector potential

Two properties of flat-band superconductors that distinguish them from conventional ones are discussed below.

(i) At low temperatures and near half filling, a flat-band superconductor has negative free energy at a vector potential
A
if the order parameter at
A
is nonzero.

The opposite direction of this statement is trivial, since whenever the order parameters vanish, the superconducting state becomes normal and
f_{s}(\textbf{A})=0
. To show (i), we consider a single flat band
m
that is isolated from other bands by a gap larger than the superconducting gap. However, the results below can be generalized to the cases of multiple flat bands if they are nearly degenerate.

We assume a condensate with
\textbf{q}=0
and a fixed
A
(since the free energy is gauge-invariant). We project the Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(\textbf{A},0)
of Eq. (
S7
) to the flat band to get
Jiang and Barlas 2023

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{(m)}(\textbf{A},0)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\textbf{k}}\textbf{d}^{\dagger}_{m\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{\text{BdG},\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})\textbf{d}_{m\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\textbf{k}}\xi_{m,\textbf{k}-\textbf{A}}+N_{c}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\frac{|\Delta_{\textbf{A},\alpha\beta}|^{2}}{U_{\alpha\beta}}.

(S19)

Here, time-reversal symmetry is imposed, implying that the band dispersion
\varepsilon^{\uparrow}_{m,\textbf{k}}=\varepsilon^{\downarrow}_{m,-\textbf{k}}
. We define
\xi_{m,\textbf{k}}\equiv\varepsilon^{\uparrow}_{m,\textbf{k}}-\mu_{\textbf{A}}
.
For perfectly flat bands,
\varepsilon^{\sigma}_{m,\textbf{k}}\equiv 0
, while
\mu_{\textbf{A}}
can be of the order of
\Delta_{\textbf{A},\alpha\beta}
, depending on the band filling. The Nambu spinor in the band basis,

\textbf{d}_{m\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})=\begin{pmatrix}&\sum_{\alpha}\psi^{*}_{m,\textbf{k}+\textbf{A},\alpha}c_{\textbf{k}\alpha\uparrow}\\
&\sum_{\alpha}\psi^{*}_{m,\textbf{k}-\textbf{A},\alpha}c^{\dagger}_{-\textbf{k}\alpha\downarrow}\end{pmatrix},

(S20)

with
\psi_{m,\textbf{k},\alpha}
the Bloch state eigenvector of the spin-
\uparrow
band, and

\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{\text{BdG},\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})=\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{m,\textbf{k}+\textbf{A}}&\Delta_{m,\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})\\
\Delta_{m,\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})^{*}&-\xi_{m,\textbf{k}-\textbf{A}}\end{pmatrix}

(S21)

is the band-projected BdG matrix, with

\displaystyle\Delta_{m,\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})\equiv\sum_{\alpha\beta}\psi_{m,\textbf{k}+\textbf{A},\alpha}^{*}\Delta_{\textbf{A},\alpha\beta}\psi_{m,\textbf{k}-\textbf{A},\beta}.

(S22)

Diagonalizing Eq. (
II
) and computing the free energy of the grand canonical ensemble, we obtain

\displaystyle F_{s}(\textbf{A})=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{\textbf{k}}\ln[(1+e^{-\beta E_{m,\textbf{k},+}(\textbf{A})})(1+e^{-\beta E_{m,\textbf{k},-}(\textbf{A})})]

\displaystyle+\sum_{\textbf{k}}\xi_{m,\textbf{k}-\textbf{A}}+N_{c}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\frac{|\Delta_{\textbf{A},\alpha\beta}|^{2}}{U_{\alpha\beta}}+\mu_{\textbf{A}}N_{e},

(S23)

where
E_{m,\textbf{k},\pm}(\textbf{A})
are the two eigenvalues of
\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{\text{BdG},\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})
.

We now focus on the ideally flat-band, half-filling, zero-temperature limit. In this limit,
\mu_{\textbf{A}}\rightarrow 0
for all
A
, therefore,
\xi_{m\textbf{k}}\rightarrow 0
and
E_{m,\textbf{k},\pm}(\textbf{A})\rightarrow\pm|\Delta_{m,\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})|
. We find

F(\textbf{A})=-\sum_{\textbf{k}}|\Delta_{m,\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})|+N_{c}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\frac{|\Delta_{\textbf{A},\alpha\beta}|^{2}}{U_{\alpha\beta}}.

(S24)

This expression can be further simplified using the gap equation. In this limit, the gap Eq. (
S6
) reads

\Delta_{\textbf{A},\alpha\beta}=\frac{U_{\alpha\beta}}{2N_{c}}\sum_{\textbf{k}}\psi_{m,\textbf{k}-\textbf{A},\beta}^{*}\psi_{m,\textbf{k}+\textbf{A},\alpha}e^{i\text{Arg}\{\Delta_{m,\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})\}}.

(S25)

Manipulating this with Eq. (
S24
), then, the second term of Eq. (
S24
) exactly cancels half of its first term, leading to

F_{s}(\textbf{A})=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\textbf{k}}|\Delta_{m,\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})|.

(S26)

Equation (
S26
) states that
|\Delta_{m,\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})|
is exactly twice of the condensation energy per
k
. For a given
A
, whenever the gap equation has nonzero solutions,
\Delta_{\textbf{A},\alpha\beta}
is a nonzero matrix. Caution is needed for that
|\Delta_{m,\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})|
may vanish at some
k
-points even if
\Delta_{\textbf{A},\alpha\beta}
is a nonzero matrix, but in general, it cannot vanish at every
k
-point. Thus, we conclude
F_{s}(\textbf{A})<0
whenever the order parameters are nonzero.

Comparison with BCS superconductors in dispersive bands.
The condensation energy of a superconductor in dispersive bands can deplete at a vector potential
A
even if
\Delta_{\textbf{A}}\neq 0
, i.e., through first-order transitions. This happens typically at temperatures well below
T_{c}
. In this case, the free energy density is approximately
f_{s}(\textbf{A})\approx f_{s}(0)+\frac{1}{2}D_{s}A^{2}
if the depairing effect is neglected, where the phase gradient term
\frac{1}{2}D_{s}A^{2}
drives
f_{s}
to zero at
A_{c}\approx\sqrt{2|f_{s}(0)|/D_{s}}
, whereas the order parameter
\Delta_{\textbf{A}}
may vanish at a much larger vector potential (the depairing momentum). Therefore, (i) simply tells us that for flat bands, the phase gradient cannot deplete the condensation energy in the low-temperature, half-filling limit; the superconducting-normal transition driven by depairing, if it exists, must be second-order.

(ii) For flat bands at low temperatures and near half filling, the solutions to the gap equation at different
A
only differ quantitatively.

This statement means that if the pairing mechanism exists in the orbital level, then the gap equation, in general, has nonzero solutions at all
A
and the order parameters at different
A
only differ by magnitude. We provide the arguments below that lead to this observation or assumption.

Instead of analyzing the magnitude of each entry of
\Delta_{\textbf{A},\alpha\beta}
, we multiply Eq. (
S25
) with
\psi_{m,\textbf{k}+\textbf{A},\alpha}^{*}\psi_{m,\textbf{k}-\textbf{A},\beta}
and sum over
\alpha,\beta
to get a self-consistency equation for
\Delta_{m,\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})
:

\Delta_{m,\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})=\sum_{\textbf{k}^{\prime}}O(\textbf{k},\textbf{k}^{\prime},\textbf{A})e^{i\text{Arg}\{\Delta_{m,\textbf{k}^{\prime}}(\textbf{A})\}},

(S27)

where

O(\textbf{k},\textbf{k}^{\prime},\textbf{A})=\sum_{\alpha\beta}\frac{U_{\alpha\beta}}{2N_{c}}\psi_{m,\textbf{k}^{\prime}-\textbf{A},\beta}^{*}\psi_{m,\textbf{k}^{\prime}+\textbf{A},\alpha}\psi_{m,\textbf{k}+\textbf{A},\alpha}^{*}\psi_{m,\textbf{k}-\textbf{A},\beta}.

(S28)

The r.h.s. of Eq. (
S27
) can be viewed as a summation of vectors in the complex plane. We notice that the region of
\textbf{k}^{\prime}\approx\textbf{k}
always contribute constructively, since in this region the phase
e^{i\text{Arg}\{\Delta_{m\textbf{k}^{\prime}}(\textbf{A})\}}
is close to that of
\Delta_{m\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})
, while

O(\textbf{k},\textbf{k}^{\prime},\textbf{A})\approx\sum_{\alpha\beta}\frac{U_{\alpha\beta}}{2N_{c}}|\psi_{m,\textbf{k}+\textbf{A},\alpha}|^{2}|\psi_{m,\textbf{k}-\textbf{A},\beta}|^{2}

(S29)

is real. The regions of
\textbf{k}^{\prime}
that are far from
k
instead cancel or contribute only partially. Equation (
S29
) also tells us that if there is a channel with large attractive interaction,
U_{\alpha\beta}>0
, dominating over other repulsive ones, then
O(\textbf{k},\textbf{k},\textbf{A})>0
and Eq. (
S27
) is very likely to have nonzero solutions. This result can be qualitatively stated as

|\Delta_{m,\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})|\propto U_{\alpha\beta}|\psi_{m,\textbf{k}+\textbf{A},\alpha}|^{2}|\psi_{m,\textbf{k}-\textbf{A},\beta}|^{2},

(S30)

with
U_{\alpha\beta}>0
the leading attractive interaction.

As discussed previously, since
|\Delta_{m,\textbf{k}}(\textbf{A})|
may vanish at some
k
-points, a better measure of the magnitude of the set of order parameters,
\{\Delta_{\textbf{A},\alpha\beta}\}
, is to average Eq. (
S30
) over the entire Brillouin zone, therefore the quantity

U_{\alpha\beta}\sum_{\textbf{k}}|\psi_{m,\textbf{k}+\textbf{A},\alpha}|^{2}|\psi_{m,\textbf{k}-\textbf{A},\beta}|^{2}

(S31)

provides a measure of ordering at each
A
. Equation (
S31
) shares the spirit of the concepts of “quantum geometric nesting” or “discrepancy” in recent studies
Han et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2026
, but is stated for the half-filling, zero-temperature limit.

Similar to the analysis of condensation energy in (i), the function
|\psi_{m,\textbf{k}+\textbf{A},\alpha}|^{2}|\psi_{m,\textbf{k}-\textbf{A},\beta}|^{2}
with fixed
k
can change with
A
drastically. However, the orbital composition in a band,
|\psi_{m,\textbf{k},\alpha}|^{2}
typically varies smoothly with
k
. This leads to the fact that the summation over
k
in Eq. (
S31
) makes it a smooth function of
A
. This point is further illustrated using 2D lattice models in Suppl. Sec.
III
.

Comparison with BCS superconductors in dispersive bands.
In the dispersive-band case, due to the strong dispersion and the consequent energetic penalty for momenta away from the Fermi level, the order parameter vanishes (depairs) at a vector potential much smaller than the reciprocal lattice constant (see the illustration in Fig.
1
(c)). In the flat band case, there is no energetic penalty, and the orbital structure of the wavefunctions, i.e., quantum geometry, determines pairing via the form factors. Therefore, (ii) is equivalent to stating that there is no complete depairing in flat bands in the limit considered.

Combining (i) and (ii), we conclude that half-filled flat-band superconductors at low temperatures in general have a negative free energy at all
A
. Depairing due to a finite vector potential
A
does not occur and lead to a superconducting-normal transition, provided that the form factor of Eq. (
S30
) remains finite at least at some
k
-points in the Brillouin zone.

IV
Dynamics of the Maxwell equations

Summary
: Equations (
1
) and (
2
) can be viewed as a periodic dynamical system governing the flow of the phase point
(\textbf{A},\textbf{B})
with spatial coordinates. Properties of the point
(\textbf{0},\textbf{0})
, i.e., the zero-field state, in the phase space are crucial, since the kink modes arise from disturbances from it at
H_{c1,w}
. We find that
(\textbf{0},\textbf{0})
is an attracting fixed point of the dynamical system
Strogatz 2024
. Consequently, a streamline starting near
(\textbf{0},\textbf{0})
either flows to it or to its replica shifted by TRIM vector potentials in the phase space, with the latter situation corresponding to the formation of kink walls.

Since the system is homogeneous along
z
direction, we impose
B_{x}=B_{y}=0
,
A_{z}=0
,
\partial_{z}A_{x}=\partial_{z}A_{y}=0
. The TRIM periodicity in
A
implies that the phase space of
(\textbf{A},\textbf{B})
, i.e.,
(A_{x},A_{y},B_{z})
, is the product of a torus and a line,
S^{1}\times S^{1}\times R
, but we consider the extended Brillouin zone for
A_{x}
and
A_{y}
for convenience.

Equation (
2
) contains three equations

\displaystyle\partial_{x}A_{y}-\partial_{y}A_{x}=B_{z},

\displaystyle\partial_{y}B_{z}=\mu_{0}j_{x}(A_{x},A_{y}),

\displaystyle\partial_{x}B_{z}=-\mu_{0}j_{y}(A_{x},A_{y}),

(S43)

for three variables
A_{x}
,
A_{y}
,
B_{z}
, and involves four velocity components,
\partial_{x}A_{y}
,
\partial_{y}A_{x}
,
\partial_{x}B_{z}
,
\partial_{y}B_{z}
. To be a fixed point requires the velocities to vanish, leading to:

B_{z}=0,\,\,\,j_{x}(\textbf{A})=j_{y}(\textbf{A})=0.

(S44)

Since we assume that
\textbf{A}=0
is the global minimum of
f_{s}(\textbf{A})
,
\textbf{j}(\textbf{A})=-\nabla_{\textbf{A}}f_{s}(\textbf{A})
vanishes there and
(\textbf{0},\textbf{0})
is a fixed point.

Next, we show that
(\textbf{0},\textbf{0})
is attracting in the periodic dynamical system along the high-symmetry line directions. A high-symmetry line (of
f_{s}(\textbf{A})
in
A
-space) forces the current density
j
to be parallel to the line direction as
A
moves along the line. Without loss of generality, we assume the high-symmetry line is along the
y
axis, so the relevant field components are
A_{y}
,
B_{z}
and
j_{y}
. These components must be functions of
x
only; if otherwise, they have
y
-dependence, e.g.,
B_{z}=B_{z}(x,y)
, then
j_{x}=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\partial_{y}B_{z}\neq 0
, contradicting
\textbf{j}\parallel\hat{\textbf{y}}
. Therefore, Eq. (
IV
) reduces to

\displaystyle\partial_{x}A_{y}(x)=B_{z}(x),

\displaystyle\partial_{x}B_{z}(x)=-\mu_{0}j_{y}(A_{y}).

(S45)

We now consider the current density of the cosine model,

j_{y}(A_{y})=-2\delta_{2}\frac{ea}{\hbar}\sin\left(\frac{2ea}{\hbar}A_{y}\right),

(S46)

and show that the phase point
(A_{y}=0,B_{z}=0)
is simultaneously a sink and source of the dynamical system, Eq. (
IV
). To illustrate this, we plot the velocity fields
(A_{y}^{\prime}(x),B_{z}^{\prime}(x))=(B_{z},\sin(2A_{y}))
(the constants
\mu_{0}
,
e
,
a
,
\hbar
,
\delta_{2}
are taken to be 1) in the phase space of
(A_{y},B_{z})
in Fig.
S5
, which shows that the streamlines can either come into or leave from
(0,0)
. Point
(A_{y}=\frac{\pi}{2},B_{z}=0)
is also a fixed point, but is a whirlpool center (neither a sink nor a source). Note:
A_{y}=\frac{\pi}{2}
is the local maximum of the cosine model, which is energetically unfavorable.

The streamlines of the kink and the breather solitons are indicated in Fig.
S5
. The kink mode has a relatively small
B_{z}
coordinate, and its streamline is essentially constrained in
n\pi\leq A_{y}\leq(n+1)\pi
, with

an integer, since the velocity vanishes at
(n\pi,0)
(indicated by colors). Therefore, the streamline starting from
(0,0)
must end at a TRIM, which is, itself, on the torus. Such a property about a fixed point is called “attracting but not Lyapunov-stable”
Strogatz 2024
. On the contrary, the breather mode has a large
B_{z}
coordinate, which instead can flow in the entire domain
-\infty\leq A_{y}\leq\infty
.

Below, we explain how a kink wall is related to the high-symmetry line. Imagine a phase point perturbed from
(0,0)
towards the high-symmetry line direction, i.e., its

becomes nonzero, and we study its subsequent flow. Using Eq. (
IV
), we find that Eq. (
M22
) holds, which leads to an indefinite integral in the phase space,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\int_{(0,0)}\,\mathrm{d}B_{z}^{2}=\int_{(0,0)}\,\mathrm{d}f_{s}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\,\,\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}B^{2}_{z,\text{end}}=f_{s,\text{end}}-f_{s,(0,0)}.

(S47)

Since the streamline can only end at a fixed point, where
B_{z}=0

. In the case when TRIMs are the only global minima of

in the

-space, the high-symmetry line must connect two TRIMs, which means that both sides of the wall correspond to the zero-field state. If along the high-symmetry line there is another global minimum of
f_{s}
with energy equal to the
\Gamma
-point, then the streamline ends there, which means the other side of the wall is a zero-field, but finite-momentum pairing state. We note that the analysis above relies on the periodicity of the dynamic system rather than the details of the cosine model; including high-order harmonic terms into
f_{s}(\textbf{A})
changes the results only quantitatively.

V
Quantized flux in magnetic field walls

In this section, we explain how the magnetic field walls manage to avoid requiring a normal core, focusing for simplicity on the kink solution. The central insight of this derivation is the importance of the lattice periodicity: at the points in the field wall where current vanishes,
A_{y}
is quantized to multiples of
\frac{\pi\hbar}{ea}
, which yields a multiple of the flux quantum
\phi_{0}=\frac{\pi\hbar}{e}
when integrated between lattice sites.

We begin by briefly reviewing the origin of flux quantization in BCS theory, where (per Eq. (
S1
))

\textbf{j}=-D_{s}\textbf{A}=-D_{s}\left(\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}+\frac{\hbar}{2e}\nabla\varphi\right).

(S48)

Integrating over a closed loop
\gamma
along which
\varphi
is always defined,

\oint_{\gamma}\textbf{j}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{l}=-D_{s}\left(\oint_{\gamma}\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{l}+\frac{\hbar}{2e}\oint_{\gamma}\nabla\varphi\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{l}\right).

(S49)

For simplicity, we consider only loops along which
\textbf{j}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{l}=0
. The magnetic flux
\phi
then quantizes as

\phi=\oint_{\gamma}\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{l}=-\frac{\hbar}{2e}\oint_{\gamma}\nabla\varphi\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{l}=N\phi_{0}

(S50)

where
N
is an integer.

The quantization condition Eq. (
S50
) is generally heavily constraining for translation-invariant 2D defects such as vortex sheets
Landau and Lifshitz 1955
;
Volovik 2003
. Integrating along the contour in Fig.
S6
(a), the integrated flux can be changed continuously by changing the width
w
of the rectangular region. The resulting continuously varying flux would violate the quantization condition Eq. (
S50
), which was based on having a continuous and well-defined condensate phase
\phi
at all locations. To resolve the contradiction (while keeping
\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}
smooth), there must be a discontinuous jump in
\phi
somewhere along the contour, indicating that there must be a normal core. This normal core is avoidable with certain specific, complicated order parameters, such as that of Helium-3
Volovik 2003
, but otherwise it is a topological requirement.

FIG. S6:
Both field walls and vortex sheets feature counterpropagating currents, but they are topologically distinct objects. (a) In vortex sheets, the gauge-invariant vector potential
A
vanishes far from the sheet. Because of this, there is necessarily a normal core (red plane), which evades flux quantization around the green loop
\gamma
. (b) In magnetic field walls, values of
A

\gamma_{2}
traverses a lattice vector, then the integral of
A
is a multiple of the flux quantum. The geometry has been chosen so that the vector potential
A
can have non-zero components only in the
y

In the cosine model, the above characterization breaks down because it is no longer the case that
\textbf{j}=-D_{s}\textbf{A}
; this must be replaced by the relationship between current and free energy given in Eq. (
1
). Nevertheless, as we show below, the flux quantization argument can be generalized to the magnetic field walls, without using the relation
\textbf{j}=-D_{s}\textbf{A}
.

In the region far from the field walls,
A
is approximately an equilibrium solution
\textbf{A}_{\textbf{m}}=\frac{\phi_{0}}{a}\textbf{m}
with
\textbf{m}=(m_{x},m_{y})
a vector of integers (in the geometry of Fig.
S6
(b)
m_{x}=0
). Note that

\int_{\textbf{r}_{0}}^{\textbf{r}_{0}+a(n_{x},n_{y})}\textbf{A}_{\textbf{m}}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{l}=\phi_{0}(n_{x}m_{x}+n_{y}m_{y})=\tilde{N}\phi_{0}

(S51)

is an integer multiple of the flux quantum. Hence, when the equilibrium solution is integrated from one site to another, the result is quantized if the two sites have the same sublattice index.

In the BCS case, we took the loop
\gamma
to have
\textbf{j}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{l}=0
along its length; for the cosine model, the argument is more subtle. We consider a loop
\gamma
which can be decomposed into collections of segments
\gamma_{1,2}
. We demand that the segments in
\gamma_{1}
be such that
\textbf{A}\cdot d\textbf{l}=0
along their length, and that the segments in
\gamma_{2}
be such that
\textbf{A}=\textbf{A}_{\textbf{m}}
along their length (potentially a different
m
for each curve segment in
\gamma_{2}
). Note that the curve in Fig.
S6
(b) has this property:
\gamma_{1}
consists of the segments passing through the field wall, and
\gamma_{2}
consists of the segments far from the field wall.

In this case, the flux quantization equation (
S50
) is modified to

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\oint_{\gamma}\textbf{A}^{(\chi)}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{l}=\oint_{\gamma}\textbf{A}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{l}-\frac{\hbar}{2e}\oint_{\gamma}\nabla\varphi\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{l}

\displaystyle=\int_{\gamma_{2}}\textbf{A}_{\textbf{m}}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{l}=\tilde{N}\phi_{0},

(S52)

showing that the magnetic flux is still quantized around such loops
\gamma
. (Note
\oint\nabla\varphi\cdot d\textbf{l}
vanishes because
\varphi
is globally defined in the magnetic field wall, which is a simply-connected superconducting region.) More macroscopically, the magnetic flux
per length of unit cell
is quantized to multiples of
\phi_{0}/a
. This quantization follows from the topological charge of the kink soliton (ultimately rooted in the periodicity of
f_{s}(\textbf{A})
).
