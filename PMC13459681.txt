“They hide because they fear”: Barriers and opportunities for tuberculosis control in rural Indonesia through the PRECEDE-PROCEED lens
Authors: Anita Lufianti, Bagoes Widjanarko, Eti Poncorini Pamungkasari, Ari Probandari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTuberculosis (TB) remains a significant public health challenge in rural Indonesia, where structural barriers, limited health literacy, and persistent stigma hinder effective disease control. Systematic exploration of contextual obstacles and opportunities for youth empowerment interventions in TB control remains limited.ObjectiveThis study explored contextual barriers and opportunities to enhance TB awareness and community involvement in rural Indonesia, using the PRECEDE-PROCEED model to identify predisposing, enabling, and reinforcing factors influencing TB control behaviours.MethodsAn exploratory qualitative study using a phenomenological approach was conducted in Central Java, Indonesia (February–August 2024). Data were collected through semi-structured interviews with 18 key informants and two focus group discussions with 11 participants. Reflexive thematic analysis was conducted using NVivo 12, with trustworthiness ensured through prolonged engagement, triangulation, member checking, and COREQ adherence.ResultsNine themes emerged across three domains. Predisposing factors included persistent TB misconceptions and stigma characterised by fear, shame, and social avoidance. Enabling factors comprised structural barriers (inadequate housing, poor ventilation), weaknesses in TB detection (human resource shortages, low TPT coverage), suboptimal health cadre effectiveness, and resource constraints. Reinforcing factors encompassed inconsistent treatment supervision, limited multi-stakeholder involvement, notably absent educational institution engagement, and programme evaluation gaps. Critically, these barriers interacted across domains through cascading mechanisms, with stigma amplifying system weaknesses and undermining supervision.ConclusionTB control in rural Indonesia faces interconnected barriers across all three PRECEDE-PROCEED domains. Young people and schools are underutilised resources with strong potential as agents of change, justifying the development of community-based youth empowerment interventions.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global public health challenge. The World Health Organization (WHO) reported approximately 10.6 million new cases and 1.3 million deaths in 2023. 1 Despite effective interventions, TB continues to disproportionately affect low- and middle-income countries, where poverty, limited health literacy, and stigma contribute to delayed diagnosis and sustained transmission.2,3

Indonesia bears a particularly heavy TB burden, ranking second globally after India with an estimated 1.06 million incident cases annually. 4 High prevalence persists especially in rural areas, where structural barriers (including overcrowded housing, poor ventilation, and limited access to quality healthcare) intersect with low public awareness, inadequate health-seeking behaviour, and pervasive stigma against TB patients.5,6

A persistent gap between TB policy and implementation in Indonesia highlights critical weaknesses. Community health cadres often lack training in behaviour change communication and contextual adaptation. 7 Most TB awareness programmes employ top-down communication models with limited community participation, 8 showing limited long-term impact in rural settings. 9 The absence of evidence-based models targeting adolescents as TB advocates represents a significant gap.

Growing evidence supports engaging young people as active participants in TB control. The WHO has emphasised community empowerment as essential for enhancing TB awareness. 10 Schools and youth groups are recognised as effective platforms for health literacy and peer education. 11 Community-based interventions involving local stakeholders have improved case finding and stigma reduction.7,12 However, few studies have explored youth empowerment models for TB control in rural Indonesian contexts.

The PRECEDE-PROCEED model provides a theoretical framework for health promotion by identifying predisposing, enabling, and reinforcing factors influencing health behaviours. 13 Systematic review evidence demonstrates that this model effectively improves health-related behaviours across diverse conditions, including tuberculosis. 14 By integrating this framework with community-based participatory principles, interventions can be tailored to address specific contextual barriers within target communities. The PRECEDE-PROCEED model is particularly suited to rural TB control because, unlike single-construct behavioural frameworks such as the Health Belief Model or the Theory of Planned Behaviour that focus mainly on individual cognition, it explicitly organises determinants across individual, organisational, and policy levels. This multilevel, ecological structure mirrors the intersecting cognitive, structural, and health-system barriers that characterise rural settings and directly indicates where multi-component interventions should be targeted.

This study aimed to explore contextual barriers and opportunities for enhancing TB awareness and community involvement in rural Indonesia, using the PRECEDE-PROCEED model to identify predisposing, enabling, and reinforcing factors. These findings will inform the development of community-based youth empowerment interventions for TB control in underserved rural settings.

Method

Study design

This study employed an exploratory qualitative research design using a phenomenological approach to understand the lived experiences, perceptions, and challenges faced by various stakeholders involved in tuberculosis (TB) control efforts in rural Indonesia. The phenomenological approach was selected to capture the essence of participants’ experiences with TB awareness, prevention, and community engagement, enabling an in-depth exploration of contextual factors influencing TB control at the community level. 15

The PRECEDE-PROCEED Model 13 guided both data collection and thematic analysis by categorising factors into predisposing (knowledge, attitudes, beliefs), enabling (resources, accessibility, skills), and reinforcing domains (social support, policies, supervision).

Study setting

The research was conducted in Grobogan Regency, Central Java Province, Indonesia, between February and August 2024. Grobogan Regency was purposively selected as a rural area with high TB burden, active TB control programmes through community health centres (Puskesmas), and existing NGO partnerships. The regency comprises 19 sub-districts served by 30 community health centres, with TB case notification rates exceeding the provincial average.

Participants and sampling

Participants were recruited using a purposive sampling strategy with a maximum variation approach to ensure representation from diverse stakeholder groups involved in TB awareness and control activities. This approach aimed to capture multiple perspectives on barriers and opportunities for TB control at the policy, implementation, and community levels.

Inclusion criteria encompassed six stakeholder groups: policymakers involved in TB programme policies (minimum one year experience), health centre heads with TB responsibilities, TB programme staff directly involved in case management, health cadres engaged in TB-related health promotion (minimum six months), health students aged 18–24 years, and community members aged 18 years or older. These terms are used consistently throughout: “health cadres” denotes trained community health volunteers (the local equivalent of community health workers, CHWs), and “TB programme staff” denotes Puskesmas-based TB officers responsible for case management.

Individuals unable to provide informed consent, those with communication impairments, and those currently receiving TB treatment were excluded. Individuals undergoing TB treatment were excluded to avoid potential psychological distress arising from discussing stigma and illness experiences during active treatment, and to keep the study focused on system- and community-level perspectives on barriers rather than personal illness narratives; the implications of this decision are addressed in the Study Limitations.

Sample size was determined by the data saturation principle, with data collection continuing until no new themes emerged from subsequent interviews. 16 Saturation was operationally defined as the point at which two consecutive interviews yielded no new codes or themes; this was monitored through analysis conducted concurrently with data collection. A total of 18 key informants participated in in-depth interviews, and 11 additional participants engaged in focus group discussions (FGDs).

Research team and reflexivity

The research team comprised the principal investigator (PI), a doctoral candidate in community health nursing, and two research assistants. The PI conducted all interviews and moderated FGDs. A reflexive journal documented assumptions and biases, with regular debriefing with academic supervisors. Potential insider bias from the PI’s nursing background was managed through peer debriefing with supervisors from different disciplines and attention to disconfirming evidence.

Data collection

Data were collected through two primary qualitative methods: semi-structured in-depth interviews and focus group discussions (FGDs).

Interview guide development . A semi-structured interview guide was developed based on the PRECEDE-PROCEED framework domains, covering TB perceptions, awareness activities, barriers to case finding and treatment, stakeholder roles, and suggestions for improving community engagement. Prior to pilot testing, the guide underwent content validation by three experts in public health, qualitative research methods, and TB programme management, whose feedback informed revisions to question wording, sequencing, and probes. It was then refined through pilot testing with two participants not included in the final sample.

In-depth interviews . Eighteen individual interviews were conducted at participant-chosen locations, lasting 45–90 minutes (mean: 62 minutes). All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim within 48 hours.

Focus group discussions . Two FGDs complemented individual interviews: FGD 1 involved health cadres (n=4, 95 minutes) and FGD 2 involved health students (n=4) and community members (n=3, 105 minutes), held at community health centres.

Field notes . Detailed field notes supplemented audio recordings and informed analysis.

Data analysis

Data analysis employed reflexive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework, chosen for its flexibility in identifying patterns within qualitative data while maintaining theoretical coherence with the PRECEDE-PROCEED framework. 17

Analysis involved familiarisation with transcripts, systematic coding using NVivo 12 (applying both inductive and PRECEDE-PROCEED-derived deductive codes), collaborative theme searching through visual mapping, theme review against the dataset, mapping final themes to the three framework domains, and report production with quotations translated and verified by a bilingual research assistant.

Rigour and trustworthiness

Methodological rigour was ensured by applying the trustworthiness criteria of Lincoln and Guba. 18 Credibility was established through prolonged engagement (six months), data triangulation across multiple sources and methods, peer debriefing, and member checking with five key informants. Transferability was supported through thick description; dependability through consistent protocols and audit trails; and confirmability through reflexive journaling and external audit. This study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines 19 to ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Health Research Ethics Committee (KEPK), Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Sebelas Maret (Approval Number: 148/UN27.06.11/KEP/EC/2024) prior to data collection. Additional research permission was obtained from the Grobogan Regency Health Office. All potential participants received written and verbal information about the study’s objectives, procedures, voluntary nature of participation, and right to withdraw at any time without consequences. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection commenced. For FGD participants, additional consent addressed confidentiality within group discussions.

Enabling factors

Beyond these predisposing influences, participants identified a range of enabling factors (environmental, health-system, and resource conditions) that determine whether TB control efforts can translate into effective practice.

Despite various efforts, TB incidence remains high. Environmental factors including overcrowding, poor ventilation, and inadequate sanitation contribute to transmission. Now, everyone must be involved, including professional organisations.” (KS, Head of P2M Section)“When discussing TB, we cannot only look at the treatment aspect. Many cases arise in densely populated areas, in tightly packed houses with minimal ventilation and poor air circulation. Reinforcing factors

Whereas enabling factors shape the capacity to act, reinforcing factors concern the feedback, supervision, and stakeholder support that sustain TB control behaviours over time.

Theme 7: Social support and treatment supervision

Social support significantly influences treatment success. Healthcare workers monitor patients through home visits and educate treatment supervisors (PMO). However, not all PMOs perform duties consistently due to family members’ busy schedules. Participants P1 and M3 expressed this:“The TB programme ensures that the PMO can provide or supervise medication according to standards. First, we perform the treatment at two locations. The first is the TB patient's home, and the second is specifically at the PMO.” (P1, TB Programme Staff)Initially, my father was instructed to take his medication under the supervision of a neighbour who was a PMO. However, to be honest, the person was also busy and sometimes did not come, so I supervised him. […] I was also afraid that if I got angry, he would feel even worse.” (M3, Community Member)

Summary of findings

The nine themes reveal interconnected barriers across predisposing (limited health literacy, stigma), enabling (structural barriers, detection weaknesses, cadre limitations, resource constraints), and reinforcing domains (inconsistent support, limited stakeholder engagement, weak evaluation). These barriers interact across domains: stigma drives resistance to screening, compounding system weaknesses and undermining supervision, perpetuating TB control challenges.

Discussion

This study identified multilevel barriers and opportunities influencing TB awareness and control in rural Indonesia through the PRECEDE-PROCEED framework, revealing interconnected challenges and dynamic cross-domain interactions.

Predisposing factors: The persistence of limited health literacy and deep-rooted stigma

Limited TB health literacy and deeply embedded stigma constitute significant predisposing barriers. Despite routine health education, fundamental misconceptions persisted, aligning with Indonesian evidence that accurate TB understanding remains inadequate in rural populations.20,21

TB-related stigma manifested as social avoidance, euphemistic language, and disclosure reluctance, patterns consistent with Indonesian studies,6,22 with 61% of TB patients across seven provinces experiencing stigma associated with guilt and fear. 6

Stigma appeared driven by deeply rooted emotional responses rather than information deficits alone, supporting its conceptualisation as a multidimensional phenomenon encompassing enacted, anticipated, and internalised stigma. 23 Addressing such complex stigma requires interventions targeting both cognitive and affective components rather than relying solely on information provision. This finding is particularly significant given that most current TB education programmes in rural Indonesia employ didactic, information-transfer approaches that may inadvertently reinforce stigma by framing TB primarily as a dangerous disease without adequately addressing the emotional and social dimensions of the illness experience.24,25

The PRECEDE-PROCEED framework emphasises addressing predisposing factors before sustainable behaviour change can occur. 13 Current top-down education approaches are insufficient, aligning with evidence that participatory, peer-led strategies are more effective in promoting health literacy and reducing stigma.8,26

Enabling factors: Structural determinants and health system constraints

Substantial structural barriers limit effective TB control. Environmental determinants (overcrowded housing, poor ventilation, and inadequate sanitation) were identified as significant contributors, corroborating global evidence on housing as a critical determinant of TB epidemiology.3,27 A systematic review confirmed that inadequate housing significantly increases pulmonary TB risk. Policymakers in our study recognised the need for intersectoral collaboration with housing and environmental agencies, yet such coordination remains poorly implemented, reflecting a persistent gap between policy recognition and operational reality in addressing social determinants of TB.

Detection system weaknesses (human resource shortages, geographic constraints, specimen quality issues, and low TPT coverage) compromise early case detection, mirroring findings from other high-burden settings.5,28 Low TPT coverage reflects a broader global challenge.29,30 Critically, community resistance stemming from stigma further hampers contact tracing, illustrating how predisposing and enabling factors interact synergistically to compound TB control challenges.

Cadre constraints (excessive workload, inadequate training, absent evaluation, and insufficient incentives) mirror challenges documented in CHW programmes globally.31,32 Cadres gravitating toward better-funded partner organisations underscores the need for sustainable incentive mechanisms.

Resource constraints further restrict programme reach, aligning with evidence that fragmented financing hampers TB programme performance. 33

Reinforcing factors: Gaps in support systems, multi-stakeholder engagement, and programme evaluation

Reinforcing factors encompass feedback, social support, and rewards that sustain behaviour change. 13 Our findings reveal both strengths and critical gaps in the mechanisms that reinforce TB control in this setting.

Treatment supervision through the PMO system was inconsistently implemented, with family members reporting availability constraints and training gaps. These findings align with research demonstrating that social support is a key predictor of treatment completion, yet its effectiveness depends on supporter capacity and connection to healthcare providers. The emotional burden described by family members serving as treatment supervisors highlights the need for strengthening educational and psychological support for those in caregiving roles.34–36

The MSI partnership strengthened active case finding over nearly five years, but educational institution engagement remains a significant missed opportunity. Despite participants identifying young people’s potential as “agents of change” within their families and communities, TB was rarely integrated into school programmes, despite global evidence that schools serve as effective health literacy platforms. 11

The absence of structured monitoring and impact assessment undermines adaptive programme management, reflecting global reliance on activity-based rather than outcome-focused evaluation. 37

Cross-domain interactions: A systems-level perspective

A critical contribution is identifying cascading cross-domain interactions: stigma (predisposing) drives resistance to screening, compounding detection system strain (enabling) and weakening supervision (reinforcing). Overburdened cadres cannot provide stigma-sensitive counselling, while absent programme evaluation leaves these interacting barriers undiagnosed, creating a self-sustaining cycle.

This extends conventional PRECEDE-PROCEED application by demonstrating that these domains function as dynamically interacting components. This has practical implications for intervention design: programmes addressing only one domain, such as health education alone without strengthening cadre capacity or evaluation mechanisms, are unlikely to achieve sustained impact because cascading effects of unaddressed barriers in other domains will undermine progress.

Toward youth empowerment: An evidence-informed intervention framework

Findings provide compelling justification for youth-based TB interventions. Addressing predisposing factors, youth ambassadors would require training in TB knowledge and stigma-sensitive communication; peer-led education has demonstrated effectiveness globally. 11 Addressing enabling factors, programmes would need institutional support through school integration and health facility coordination. Addressing reinforcing factors, structured supervision and feedback mechanisms would create accountability currently absent from cadre programmes.

Community-based TB interventions have demonstrated improvements in case finding and stigma reduction.7,12 Unlike information-based approaches alone, youth peer influence can address stigma through social norm change by modelling accepting attitudes and gradually shifting community norms around TB disclosure and support-seeking.23,24

Operationally, such a model could be structured in three phases. In a preparatory phase, health-student ambassadors would be recruited from existing health-professional study programmes and trained by Puskesmas TB staff in TB knowledge, stigma-sensitive communication, and basic counselling, guided by a standardised module and a competency check. In an implementation phase, ambassadors would deliver peer-led sessions and family outreach embedded within established school structures, integrating TB literacy into the national School Health Programme (Usaha Kesehatan Sekolah, UKS) and routine extracurricular activities rather than as a stand-alone campaign, while remaining linked to a named Puskesmas focal point for referral and supervision. In a sustainability phase, the programme would be anchored through a memorandum of understanding between health centres and schools, periodic refresher training, recognition or academic credit for student participation, and a simple monitoring dashboard tracking sessions delivered, referrals generated, and stigma indicators, thereby creating the structured supervision and feedback mechanisms currently absent from cadre programmes.

At the policy level, this model could be integrated with existing national structures rather than created in parallel. Linking youth TB activities to the National TB Programme’s community-engagement and active case-finding agenda, and embedding them within the UKS framework jointly governed by the health and education sectors, would provide an institutional home, a financing route through existing school-health and operational (BOK) mechanisms, and direct alignment with national End TB targets.

Integration with existing literature and distinct contributions

Our findings align with and extend existing knowledge on TB control in Indonesia. Stigma as an emotionally-rooted barrier corroborates recent Indonesian studies. 6 Structural barriers mirror analyses of TB as a multisectoral problem. 5

This study makes distinct contributions. First, we explicitly mapped barriers and opportunities to the PRECEDE-PROCEED framework, demonstrating how domains interact dynamically rather than operating in parallel. Second, the inclusion of six diverse stakeholder groups offers ecological breadth enabling triangulation of perspectives. Third, identifying youth and educational institutions as underutilised structural resources provides actionable direction for programme innovation. While youth engagement in health promotion is well-established globally, its systematic application to TB control in rural Indonesian contexts has received limited attention, and our findings provide empirical justification for such models.

Study limitations

The research was conducted in a single district (Grobogan Regency, Central Java), limiting transferability to other Indonesian regions, although detailed contextual descriptions facilitate applicability assessment. As a qualitative study with 29 participants, findings provide context-rich understanding rather than statistical generalisation. The 61.1% male sample may not fully capture gendered dimensions of TB stigma. Because stigma, disclosure, and caregiving roles in TB are often gendered, the predominance of male participants may have under-represented women’s experiences, particularly as informal treatment supporters, and the gendered dynamics of shame and concealment; findings relating to stigma should therefore be interpreted with this imbalance in mind. Individuals currently receiving TB treatment were excluded, and their perspectives might offer additional insights. Because people on treatment experience stigma, side effects, and supervision first-hand, this exclusion means the analysis reflects stakeholders’ perceptions of these issues rather than patients’ lived accounts, and may under-represent the most direct experiences of internalised stigma and treatment burden; future studies should include current patients to capture these perspectives. Combining health students and community members in FGD 2 may have introduced power dynamics. Social desirability bias may affect health worker responses despite mitigation efforts. As exploratory research, future implementation studies are needed to evaluate youth empowerment intervention effectiveness.

Implications

The findings carry important implications for policy, practice, and research. For policy, structural barriers necessitate integrating TB control with intersectoral policies addressing housing and environmental health, alongside sustainable cadre financing and evaluation mechanisms. For practice, top-down education should be supplemented with participatory, peer-led strategies addressing stigma, and structured youth ambassador programmes designed with attention to all three PRECEDE-PROCEED domains. For research, future studies should evaluate youth empowerment interventions through mixed-methods implementation research, with attention to cross-domain interactions, stigma-reduction pathways, and multi-site replication.

Conclusion

TB control in rural Indonesia faces multidimensional barriers across predisposing (misconceptions, stigma), enabling (structural determinants, system weaknesses, cadre limitations), and reinforcing factors (inconsistent supervision, limited institutional engagement, weak evaluation). These barriers interact dynamically, creating cascading effects. Young people and schools represent underutilised resources with potential as agents of change. These findings justify developing community-based youth empowerment interventions addressing gaps across all three PRECEDE-PROCEED domains through peer-led education, stigma reduction, and enhanced community engagement.
