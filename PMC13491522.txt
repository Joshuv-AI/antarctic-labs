Boswellia carterii Essential Oil and Constituents Alleviate Anxiety-like Behavior in Zebrafish Larvae Model
Authors: Veronica B. Maphanga, Maxleene Sandasi, Margaux J. Lim Ah Tock, Gill M. Enslin, Elliasu Y. Salifu, Pritika Ramharack, Alvaro M. Viljoen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Herbal medicines are increasingly recognized as alternative therapies for anxiety. Boswellia species are traditionally used in East Africa to manage central nervous system disorders. In the present study, Boswellia carterii, its major compound α-pinene, and the enantiomers (−)-α-pinene and (+)-α-pinene were investigated for potential anxiolytic activity using an in vivo zebrafish larvae model. Chiral analysis using Gas Chromatography-Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry confirmed α-pinene as a scalemic mixture of (−)-α-pinene and (+)-α-pinene in B. carterii essential oil. In behavioral assays, B. carterii essential oil exhibited significant anxiolytic activity at 50 and 100 μL/L (p < 0.05), and α-pinene at all tested concentrations (p < 0.05), as measured by anxiolytic effect indicators. When tested individually across a concentration range, both enantiomers displayed significant anxiolytic activity in vivo (p < 0.05). Molecular docking and MM/GBSA analysis further demonstrated the interactions of α-pinene and its enantiomers with various receptor targets, with the strongest binding affinity observed for the CB2 receptor, although pharmacological validation is still required. Both (−)- and (+)-α-pinene exhibited binding energies comparable to the known CB2 agonist AM1241, indicating that the in vivo anxiolytic-like effects were not distinctly enantioselective. The stereochemistry did, however, affect the predicted binding orientation in the active site and in-silico ligand–receptor interactions, supporting further investigation of the α-pinene enantiomers as lead compounds with activity at the CB2 receptor.

Introduction

For centuries, Ayurvedic, Traditional Chinese and African traditional medicine systems have employed plant-based remedies for the management of various central nervous system (CNS) disorders. Anxiety disorders have become prevalent in modern day society particularly due to increasing socio-economic pressures and pandemics such as COVID-19. Current pharmaceutical interventions, which include benzodiazepines, are increasingly considered undesirable due to unpleasant side effects and potential for dependence. Traditional medicines on the other hand, have become popular over the years as they are deemed safe, are relatively inexpensive and often have fewer side effects. Essential oils are highly valued plant-derived natural substances with a wide range of beneficial therapeutic effects as evidenced by long-standing traditional use and the results of numerous experimental studies. , Several in vitro and in vivo studies have reported the neuropharmacological effects of essential oils and their individual constituents. These effects include anxiolytic, antidepressant, sedative, anticonvulsant, and neuroprotective properties. − These findings suggest the beneficial potential effects of essential oils for diverse central nervous system-modulated effects, and use in the treatment of anxiety, epilepsy, and depression among other CNS conditions. ,

The Boswellia genus (family Burseraceae) comprises resin-bearing trees, with the essential oils being obtained from the oleo-gum resin through steam-distillation. , These resins, widely known as frankincense, have been valued for centuries in traditional medicine and cultural practices, particularly as incense and in the treatment of inflammatory and central nervous system disorders. Boswellia resins are used traditionally in East Africa for their relaxant properties and to treat several other ailments. , Moreover, Boswellia species have been reported to possess anxiolytic and anticonvulsant activity likely mediated through the GABA receptor as well as relaxant properties in aromatherapy. In addition, pharmacological activities of B. carterii also include alleviating chronic stress, inflammation, and labor pain among others.

Analytical techniques such as gas chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry with flame ionization detection (GC-MS/FID) are used for the structural identification of individual compounds and quantification of these compounds in complex essential oil matrices, due to the technique’s high sensitivity and high selectivity, ensuring accurate identification of compounds in such complex mixtures. − Boswellia (frankincense) species contain a diverse array of chemical constituents, the composition of which may vary depending on factors such as the climate, harvest conditions and geographical distribution. Major chemical constituents previously identified in the essential oil of B. carterii include α-pinene, α-thujene, camphene, sabinene, β-pinene, myrcene, limonene, α-phellandrene.

Although the mechanisms underlying the actions of essential oils are not yet fully understood, studies have shown that their constituents can be absorbed into the bloodstream, cross the blood–brain barrier, and enter the central nervous system and exert centrally mediated effects. The biologically active essential oil compounds are reported to include hydrogenated and oxygenated terpenes (monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes), oxygenated compounds such as ketones and alcohols, aromatic compounds such as phenols and methoxylated derivatives, coumarins and terpenoids (isoprenoids). ,

Some essential oils have been reported to contain chiral compounds. Chirality is an intrinsic feature of biological systems as stereochemistry plays an important role in biological activity. , Marked differences in pharmacological effects can be observed due to the different stereochemistry of otherwise identical compounds. Furthermore, one stereoisomer compound may present beneficial effects while the other may elicit toxicity or undesirable effects. These considerations highlight the importance of identifying and characterizing chiral constituents in essential oils to better understand their pharmacological relevance. Enantiomeric analysis by GC-MS utilizing a chiral column enables differentiation of the essential oil according to their enantiomeric profile, or “enantiotype”. In addition to experimental characterization, molecular docking can be applied to explore how different enantiomers orient within receptor binding pockets and how stereochemistry influences binding affinity, offering predictive insights into their likely pharmacological outcomes.

Although rodent models have been used to study stress-related disorders, their application to high-throughput screening in psychiatry research is limited by ethical concerns and practical constraints. Biomedical research increasingly emphasizes principles of the reduction, refinement, replacement and responsible (4Rs) use of animals. Over the years, the use of zebrafish (Danio rerio) larvae has become popular as a suitable alternative to mammalian models, offering compliance with the 4Rs while enabling rapid, high-throughput screening of potential neuroactive compounds. Zebrafish larvae exhibit quantifiable behavioral stress responses, including thigmotaxis and scototaxis, displayed from as early as 5 days postfertilization (5-dpf). The larvae also display simple motor behavior such as fast swimming capacity, and functional motor, sensory and stress regulating systems, making them a suitable model for behavioral studies. ,

Several studies have explored the anxiolytic and antidepressant-like effects of terpenes, key constituents of essential oils, and their potential interactions with various neural receptors, including serotonin (5-HT1A), N-methyl-d-aspartate, dopamine D1, and GABAA receptors. , These receptors represent key classes of neuropharmacological targets, with serotonin 5-HT1A and dopamine D1 belonging to the G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) family, while NMDA and GABAA receptors are ligand-gated ion channels implicated in anxiety regulation. Terpenes have been shown to modulate both ligand-gated ion channels and GPCRs, underscoring their potential role as neuroactive compounds whose pharmacological effects may possibly result from these interactions , and serotonergic system, particularly through the postsynaptic 5-HT1A receptor. Eucalyptol has demonstrated the ability to modulate GABAA receptors, which results in antidepressant and anxiolytic effects. The monoterpene (−)-borneol has been reported to bind to GABAA receptors through hydrogen bonding, contributing to its sedative and anxiolytic properties. Similarly, terpinolene and β-caryophyllene have shown anxiolytic effects in the zebrafish model, likely mediated via selective interaction with the cannabinoid CB2 receptor, a member of the GPCR family.

Previous studies on monoterpenes have primarily reported general anxiolytic or neuroactive effects without distinguishing between enantiomers, , while others have relied predominantly on in-silico docking approaches without behavioral validation. , Although stereochemical considerations are known to influence receptor binding, enantioselective anxiolytic effects remain insufficiently explored in vivo. In this study, α-pinene was investigated based on its identification as a major constituent of B. carterii essential oil, which demonstrated significant anxiolytic activity. The occurrence of α-pinene as a scalemic mixture made this compound an ideal candidate to explore the role of stereochemistry in biological activity. Furthermore, there was a need to determine whether the observed pharmacological effects of the essential oil can be attributed to specific enantiomers rather than the compound as a whole. Focusing on α-pinene and its enantiomers allowed for a targeted mechanistic investigation, enabling the evaluation of stereochemistry to receptor binding and anxiolytic effects, which would not be possible with a broader but less detailed chemical screening.

Results: Chemical Profiling of B. carterii, Chiral Separation of α-Pinene, Maximum Tolerated Concentration (MTC) as Well as Anxiolytic Activity Assay

Boswellia carterii has long been used in traditional medicine to alleviate central nervous system disorders. Herein, B. carterii essential oil, its major compound α-pinene and enantiomers were investigated for possible anxiolytic activity. To achieve this, the essential oil was chemically profiled to identify major compounds. This technique was followed by a chiral analysis to determine that α-pinene exists as a mixture of enantiomers. The essential oil and chemically characterized compounds were then tested biologically using an in vivo zebrafish model to assess their effects on anxiety-like behavior. Finally, in-silico modeling was used to explore how the molecules might interact with brain receptors involved in anxiety. By integrating chemical analysis, zebrafish behavioral screening and computational modeling, this study provides preliminary mechanistic support for the neuroactive potential of B. carterii essential oil and α-pinene enantiomers, while identifying predicted receptor interactions for future validation.

Stereochemistry of α-Pinene Enantiomers

Chiral gas chromatographic analysis of B. carterii essential oil was performed to determine the ratio of the α-pinene enantiomers present in the oil. Enantiomers were identified by comparing the retention times and mass spectra of pure standards with data from the NIST11 library. It was revealed that the elution order for the chiral pair followed a (−) to (+) pattern. Anxiolytic Activity of B. carterii Essential Oil and Selected Compounds

By 5 days postfertilization, zebrafish larvae exhibit complex behaviors, including thigmotaxisa tendency to remain near the periphery of novel environments, a reliable indicator of anxiety-like states. , The anxiolytic activity of B. carterii essential oil, its major compound α-pinene and the individual enantiomers was evaluated in the zebrafish larval model. In the current study, B. carterii essential oil, α-pinene, and its enantiomers showed a reduction in locomotor activity and significantly increased both the percentage distance traveled and time spent in the central arena during the dark phase, indicating reduced thigmotaxis and possible anxiolytic-like activity.

The Maximum Tolerated Concentration of B. carterii, α-Pinene and Its Enantiomers

The maximum tolerated concentration results revealed that B. carterii was nontoxic at 100 μL/L, while α-pinene and its enantiomers were all nontoxic at 10 μM. Based on the MTC, B. carterii, α-pinene and its enantiomers were tested across a range of concentrations to determine the best anxiolytic test concentration.

Physicochemical, Pharmacokinetic, Toxicity Characteristics and Molecular Docking of the Compounds

Toxicological Profiling

ProTox predicted acute toxicity (LD50), organ and toxicity endpoints for all the identified compounds. The scalemic mixture of α-pinene and its two enantiomers demonstrated acute toxicity that is above 3700 mg/kg, and none was predicted to have potential hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity. All the compounds were predicted to permeate the BBB and be metabolized by CYP2C9 enzyme system as shown in supplementary data 2.

Drug-likeness and ADME Properties of the Compounds

The physicochemical properties and drug-likeness evaluation of the scalemic mixture of α-pinene and the individual enantiomers were predicted using the SwissADME platform (Supplementary Data 3). The predicted properties are consistent with those expected for low molecular weight, highly lipophilic monoterpenes, with no hydrogen-bond donors or acceptors and a very low polar surface area. As anticipated, the scalemic mixture and individual enantiomers showed comparable physicochemical descriptor profiles, since most calculated SwissADME descriptors are achiral and are therefore not expected to differ substantially between enantiomers. The compound and its enantiomers have a molecular weight of less than 500 Da, fewer than 10 hydrogen bond acceptors and 5 hydrogen bond donors and adhere to Lipinski’s rule of five supporting oral drug-like physicochemical properties compatible with passive membrane permeation. However, α-pinene and its enantiomers are highly lipophilic, with predicted iLogP values of 2.63 and poor aqueous solubility predictions of logS −4.20, which may limit dissolution and, consequently, oral absorption, despite favorable predicted membrane permeability.

Furthermore, estimating gastrointestinal absorption and brain penetration reinforced these findings, providing additional insight into predicted gastrointestinal absorption and blood–brain barrier permeation. The compounds showed a SwissADME oral bioavailability score of 0.55, suggesting a moderate likelihood of achieving acceptable oral bioavailability; however, this should be interpreted as predictive as it is not a measured bioavailability value. However, as SwissADME also predicted low gastrointestinal (GI) absorption for all compounds, suggesting a potential limitation to gastrointestinal absorption.

Target Prediction and Intermolecular Interactions of Compounds Responsible for Anxiolytic Activity on Several Receptors

The selection of neurological targets in this study was guided by both in-silico target prediction and established neuropharmacological mechanisms underlying anxiety disorders. Initially, 15 putative protein targets were identified using SuperPred. These targets were subsequently filtered based on their documented involvement in anxiety-related pathways, resulting in the selection of seven receptors, namely cannabinoid receptor type 2 (CB2), dopamine D1 receptor, N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptor, monoamine oxidase A (MAO-A), serotonin receptors (5-HT2C and 5-HT7), and the voltage-gated sodium channel (SCP Type III). Anxiety disorders are characterized by dysregulation of interconnected neurotransmitter systems, including serotonergic, dopaminergic, glutamatergic. Therefore, the selected targets represent key nodes within these converging pathways, providing a biologically relevant framework for investigating anxiolytic activity. Molecular docking studies were carried out on seven predicted protein targets with validated known active compounds. Three of the seven predicted targets namely CB2, 5-HT2C and 5-HT7 demonstrated noteworthy binding scores as shown in supplementary data 4. Figure shows binding scores of α-pinene (−7.1 kcal/mol), (−)-α-pinene (−7.1 kcal/mol), (+)-α-pinene (−7.0 kcal/mol) and CB2 agonist AM1241 (−10.5 kcal/mol) as a positive control. To further validate the docking scores, ligand interaction plots were analyzed (Table ) to show the intermolecular interactions with key residues that stabilize the compounds within the active site of the CB2 receptor when compared to AM1241. Interaction plot analysis revealed that α-pinene and its enantiomers demonstrated hydrophobic interactions with Phe117, Val113 and Ile110. The indicated residues signify that all compounds interact with the CB2 receptor in a similar binding mode as AM1241. Furthermore, (−)-α-pinene showed an additional interaction with Phe183, Ile110 and Val113 amino acid residues similar to those of the positive control AM1241. Also, a comparison of the two enantiomers reveal that both enantiomers interact with residues such as Phe117, Phe183, Trp194, Val196, and Leu193. The binding being dominated by hydrophobic interactions with no observed hydrogen bonding potential. However, since docking primarily reflects geometric complementarity and not actual binding affinity, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were subsequently performed to further validate these interactions.

Analysis of Molecular Dynamics Simulations

Assessment of Stability of Simulated Systems

To assess the conformational stability of protein–ligand complexes over 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations, root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of the protein backbone was calculated for each system (Supplementary Data 5b). The RMSD time series for all systems reached a plateau after approximately 20–40 ns and remained stable with low amplitude fluctuations for the remaining simulation time (details in Supplementary data 5a). This pattern indicates that each protein ligand complex achieved conformational convergence within the 100 ns time scale. Furthermore, the average RMSD values for all targets fall within the typical range (2–3 Å) for well folded, stable globular proteins in molecular dynamics simulations. No system exhibited progressive drift or large, sporadic jumps, supporting the reliability and reproducibility of the reported averages. Therefore, the observed differences between enantiomers and reference compounds are reliable and not artifacts of insufficient equilibration or simulation length.

Assessment of α-Pinene and Its Enantiomers’ Impact on Target Flexibility

Conformational flexibility was assessed by estimating the root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) of the residues that constitute each protein target (Supplementary Data 6). Analysis of average RMSF values reveals that the effect of α-pinene and its enantiomers on residue level flexibility is both target dependent and enantiomer specific. At the CB2 receptor, α-pinene and enantiomer derived systems reduced backbone fluctuations relative to the apo state (1.23 Å), with (−)-α-pinene showing the lowest RMSF (1.06 Å), followed by scalemic α-pinene (1.10 Å) and (+)-α-pinene (1.20 Å). This indicates a general stabilizing effect on CB2, particularly for the (−)-α-pinene enantiomer, which outperformed the reference compound AM1241 (1.37 Å). In contrast, at the 5-HT2C receptor, the individual enantiomers increased RMSF considerably (both 13.48 Å) compared to apo (11.00 Å), matching the flexibility induced by paroxetine (13.02 Å). However, scalemic α-pinene uniquely lowered RMSF to 10.74 Å, slightly below the apo level, suggesting that the mixture rigidifies this otherwise flexible receptor. For the 5-HT7 receptor, (−)-α-pinene marginally reduced RMSF (10.03 Å) relative to apo (10.24 Å), whereas (+)-α-pinene and scalemic α-pinene increased fluctuations to 12.65 Å and 12.31 Å, respectively, indicating less favorable flexibility profiles. Collectively, these results demonstrate that (−)-α-pinene consistently reduces or does not increase flexibility at all three targets, whereas the effect of (+)-α-pinene and the scalemic α-pinene is target specific, with α-pinene showing an unexpectedly rigidifying action at 5-HT2C.

Discussion

The global burden of anxiety-related disorders and limitations of conventional pharmacotherapies has intensified the search for novel anxiolytic agents with favorable safety profiles. , Over the years, herbal medicines have emerged as alternative therapy for anxiety and other mental disorders due to beneficial safety profiles. Moreover, scientific evidence corroborates the use of essential oils to alleviate anxiety and depression , in animal studies. The pharmacological activities of Boswellia species were investigated in a previous study, where a hexane extract of B. sacra and the commercial extract of B. serrata demonstrated possible antiseizure activity on the zebrafish larvae and mouse model. This study showed that the Boswellia species (frankincense) displayed CNS activity and validated their ethnopharmacological relevance.

In the current study, GC-MS analysis revealed that the monoterpene, α-pinene (48.1%), is the major compound in B. carterii and has been reported to have diverse pharmacological properties such as antioxidant, anxiolytic, antidepressant activity and neuroprotective effects. Essential oils are generally administered and expressed as volume-based concentrations (μL/L) due to their heterogeneous composition and lack of a single defined molecular weight. In contrast, the compound α-pinene and its enantiomers were tested in micromolar concentrations to assess its specific pharmacological contribution under chemically defined conditions. Thus, the estimated α-pinene concentrations present in the essential oil treatments (approximately 39–310 μM) exceeded the concentrations tested for the isolated α-pinene compound (2.5–10 μM), these concentrations are not directly comparable because the essential oil represents a complex mixture rather than a pure compound. Furthermore, the essential oil demonstrated a conventional dose-dependent increase in activity, likely due to synergistic interactions among multiple phytochemical constituents. In contrast, α-pinene and its enantiomers exhibited greater activity at lower concentrations, followed by reduced efficacy at higher concentrations, suggesting a biphasic hormetic dose–response relationship. Such hormetic effects are commonly reported for phytochemicals and may arise from adaptive cellular responses at low doses and cytotoxic or inhibitory effects at higher doses. , Although this inverse dose–response pattern may be consistent with hormesis or other nonmonotonic dose–response relationships that have been described for numerous natural products and pharmacological agents, the precise mechanism underlying this response remains unclear. , Alternative explanations, including receptor desensitization, receptor saturation, or nonspecific behavioral effects associated with increasing concentrations, cannot be excluded. Similar nonmonotonic dose–response relationships have been reported for neuroactive compounds on neurotransmitters, where increasing agonist concentrations may reduce receptor responsiveness through adaptive regulatory mechanisms. Consequently, additional pharmacological and mechanistic studies are required to determine whether the observed response reflects a true hormetic phenomenon or other concentration-dependent biological processes. Furthermore, abrupt changes from light to dark induce anxiety on the zebrafish larvae which increase locomotor activity. Conversely, transition from dark to light decrease locomotor activity, thus the zebrafish larvae demonstrated a reduction in locomotor activity when treated with B. carterii at 50 and 100 μL/L and when treated with α-pinene, (−)-α-pinene and (+)-α-pinene at the lowest tested concentration of 2.5 μM during the dark phase of the light-dark transitions. Although, higher tested concentrations for the tested compounds (5 and 10 μM) also displayed an increase in the percentage distance traveled and time spent in the central arena they did not show a reduction in locomotor activity, which might be attributed to hyperactivity or neurotoxicity effects and therefore warrant further research for the observed behavioral effects of the tested essential oil and compounds.

Anxiety symptoms have been reported to be prevalent among schizophrenia patients, thus a previous study aimed to alleviate anxiety, depression and cognitive impairment on a ketamine-induced schizophrenia mouse model following treatment with α-pinene. This monoterpene significantly alleviated oxidative damage, cognitive deficiency, depressive and anxiogenic-like behavior in the brains of ketamine-induced schizophrenic mice model. Previous studies have demonstrated that α-pinene exerts its neuropharmacological effects via multiple receptor systems, including GABAergic signaling, 5-HT1A receptor activation, as well as β-adrenergic and D1 dopaminergic pathways, which are strongly implicated in the regulation of anxiety and depressive disorders. , In contrast, our findings identified CB2, 5-HT2C and 5-HT7 receptors as plausible molecular targets, suggesting alternative yet complementary mechanisms of action. Furthermore, CB2 receptor activation has emerged as a promising mechanism in neuropsychiatric conditions. Collectively, although α-pinene engages a broader receptor profile, including GABAergic and monoaminergic systems, the receptor pathways identified in our study demonstrate key processes underlying anxiety, thereby supporting their likelihood for complementary pharmacological strategies. However, further experimental data is required to validate the in-silico predictions of this study.

Enantiomers of α-pinene have been reported for potential antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory and antibacterial activity among others. Only a few studies have explored the CNS activity of these enantiomers, an example is a previous study, in which inhalation of (+)-α-pinene demonstrated differential electroencephalographic (EEG) responses in humans according to gender, with women showing more pronounced effects. Specifically, (+)-α-pinene showed an increase in absolute wave activities in women, suggesting enhanced relaxation and alertness states, which corroborates with the findings of the current study. In contrast, the present study revealed that both enantiomers produced comparable anxiolytic-like effects in a behavioral model, suggesting that the anxiolytic potential of α-pinene may not be strictly enantioselective.

The SwissADME server predicts that α-pinene and its enantiomers comply with Lipinski’s rule of five (RO5), supporting oral drug-like physicochemical properties, although this does not necessarily imply their suitability as lead drug candidates. The compound and its enantiomers were not predicted to be substrates of P-glycoprotein (Supplementary Data 3), an ATP-driven efflux pump that eliminates xenobiotics, they are therefore unlikely to undergo reduced absorption or CNS exposure due to efflux from epithelial and endothelial cells. This SwissADME predicted BBB penetration profile, together with the low molecular weight, low polarity and high lipophilicity supports the plausibility of passive CNS penetration, , but does not demonstrate efficient oral absorption or BBB penetration. This physicochemical profile does however indicate poor aqueous solubility, which could limit dissolution and oral absorption.

The comparable SwissADME profiles of the scalemic mixture and individual enantiomers are expected because most standard physicochemical descriptors are achiral and therefore do not capture stereoselective interactions in chiral biological environments. However, chirality can affect biological recognition, biological target engagement and molecular complexity. In this context, SwissADME/Lipinski-type descriptors are not expected to clearly differentiate the α-pinene enantiomers, because these descriptors largely reflect achiral physicochemical properties rather than stereoselective interactions within chiral biological environments. Thus, implementing or introducing a greater amount of stereochemical information for screening drug candidates that have chiral centers may be beneficial to fully understand and note the differences. In contrast molecular docking and MD simulation revealed that, despite their structural similarity, the enantiomers may exert different ligand-target interactions and binding effects, as reflected by the variations in binding affinity shown in Table . These limitations of predicted low gastrointestinal absorption and poor aqueous solubility, which may restrict oral bioavailability, pose formulation challenges in dosage form design and development. Despite this, novel advanced delivery techniques, such as lipid-based delivery systems, polymeric nanocarriers and pharmaceutically engineered crystals have been designed to improve the stability and pharmacokinetic properties of drugs in various bodily environments. Despite these constraints, the comparable ADME profiles of the scalemic mixture and the enantiomers indicate that any observed differences in biological activity are likely driven by stereoselective interactions with target receptors. This highlights the critical role of chirality in influencing ligand–receptor binding and pharmacological outcomes, reinforcing the importance of evaluating enantiomer-specific effects in drug discovery.

These properties are compatible with passive membrane partitioning and may support CNS exposure, particularly in view of the predicted BBB permeability. However, the overall ADMET profile should be interpreted cautiously, as the low predicted gastrointestinal absorption, limited aqueous solubility, high lipophilicity and poor lead-likeness may limit oral exposure and drug-development potential. Therefore, although α-pinene and its enantiomers may represent useful exploratory CNS-active scaffolds, the current in-silico findings do not justify strong claims of favorable oral bioavailability or lead-like suitability without further experimental pharmacokinetic validation.

The MM/GBSA analysis of the compounds against the CB2, 5-HT7, and 5-HT2C receptors was conducted to identify the most suitable anxiolytic target based on binding affinity. Also, this analysis was used to provide a refined binding affinity estimate to complement and strengthen the docking scores. The inclusion of the MM/GBSA-based assessment after MD simulation provides an extra layer of validity of our computational findings. Among the three receptors, the CB2 receptor consistently demonstrated the most favorable interactions with the compounds, with (−)-α-pinene showing binding energies comparable to the known CB2 ligand AM1241, albeit slightly lower than the reference. The endocannabinoid system exerts its effects through two primary receptors: CB1 and CB2. Historically, research has predominantly focused on the CB1 receptor in the context of anxiety and mood disorders, as CB2 was believed to be limited to peripheral tissues and absent from the brain. However, identified CB2 receptors in the brainstem, revealing a previously unrecognized central site of action for the endocannabinoid system. Unlike CB1, activation of CB2 receptors has been associated with anxiolytic and antidepressant-like effects without inducing the psychotropic or benzodiazepine-like side effects commonly linked to CB1 activity. Supporting this demonstrated that β-caryophyllene and terpinolene produced anxiolytic effects in zebrafish through CB2 receptor activation, rather than CB1. These findings align with the current study; however, the involvement of (−)-α-pinene in CB2 receptor activity has not been previously reported, highlighting the need for further investigation. In contrast, the compounds demonstrated moderate to lower affinities for the serotonin receptors (5-HT7 and 5-HT2C), with binding energies notably weaker than those of the reference ligands ergotamine and paroxetine. These results suggest that the CB2 receptor may represent a more promising target for anxiolytic activity among the studied compounds.

This study demonstrated that the essential oil obtained from B. carterii, along with its primary constituent, α-pinene, and the enantiomers (−)- and (+)-α-pinene, significantly reduce anxiety-like behavior in the zebrafish larvae model, as indicated by reverse-thigmotaxis behavior. These findings are consistent with prior studies on the anxiolytic effects of essential oil constituents, such as α-pinene and β-caryophyllene, in zebrafish and rodent models. , Chiral analysis verified the scalemic nature of α-pinene in B. carterii. Testing of its isolated enantiomers indicated that both (−)- and (+)-α-pinene exhibited anxiolytic-like activity, underscoring the significance of stereochemistry in pharmacological effects. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations revealed that α-pinene and its enantiomers interact with the CB2 receptor, with (−)-α-pinene demonstrating binding affinities similar to the selective CB2 agonist AM1241. These findings align with reports indicating the involvement of CB2 receptor pathways in terpene-mediated anxiolysis in zebrafish. , The differences in stereochemistry significantly influenced binding orientation within the CB2 active site, providing evidence that (−)-α-pinene may interact more favorably with cannabinoid target. These findings underscore the critical role of stereochemistry in ligand–receptor interactions, binding affinity, and orientation within the active site, and consequent pharmacological activity, as illustrated by the distinctive binding behaviors of (−)- and (+)-α-pinene with the CB2 receptor. While the in-silico assessment identified the CB2 receptor as the preferred interaction target and differentiated it from 5-HT2C and 5-HT7, which exhibited unfavorable energy differences relative to the positive controls. We recognize that the observed anxiolytic-like effects are unlikely to arise from CB2 engagement alone. The initial target panel was limited to seven candidates, and the possibility remains that the compounds engage additional receptors or signaling pathways not examined here. Polypharmacology, or synergistic effects across multiple targets, may contribute to the behavioral outcomes, and CB2 interaction should be viewed as providing a plausible, hypothesis-generating insight rather than a complete mechanistic explanation. Further pharmacological profiling across a broader target landscape, together with selective antagonist and genetic knockout studies, will be required to dissect the relative contribution of CB2 and any additional mechanisms underlying the anxiolytic activity. While α-pinene enantiomers demonstrate measurable interactions with the CB2 receptor, their relatively modest binding affinities may suggest that pharmacological efficacy in in vivo involves synergistic interactions. Moreover, these results indicate that α-pinene is a significant bioactive compound contributing to the anxiolytic properties of B. carterii. Its enantiomers appear to influence cannabinoid receptors through stereoselective interactions, thereby offering a possible pharmacological rationale for the traditional application of Boswellia species in the treatment of stress and anxiety disorders.

Conclusions

The data presented in the current study supports previous reports suggesting the possible CNS activity of B. carterii, α-pinene and its enantiomers, (−)-α-pinene (+)-α-pinene, which can be explored further as possible leads for anxiolytic drug development. The novelty of this study lies in the application of a zebrafish larvae model to investigate the anxiolytic activity of B. carterii, α-pinene, and its enantiomers. Moreover, by employing a zebrafish behavioral model that shares conserved neurochemical pathways with mammals, this study offers insights into how structural stereochemistry influences neurobehavioral outcomes. The predominance of α-pinene and other monoterpenes may underlie the anxiolytic-like effects observed in zebrafish larvae, supporting a potential link between the oil’s chemical composition and its behavioral activity in vivo. Although the results of the zebrafish model cannot be directly extrapolated to humans, the model provides a highly efficient and ethically favorable screening approach. Protox showed that the studied compounds demonstrate plausibility as drug leads, however, no further studies were done to validate the predictions and noted as a limitation of the study and warrant further research. Additionally, in-silico modeling substantiated the anxiolytic potential of the compounds via the CB2 receptor, with (−)-α-pinene displaying the most favorable binding interactions. The observed stereochemical effect on binding, observed between the enantiomeric pairs (+)/(−)-α-pinene, underscores the critical role of stereochemistry in receptor interaction. Overall, the data support the CB2 receptor as the most favorable anxiolytic target for further exploration and optimization of these compounds. However, the involvement of CB2 with these compounds is mainly through in-silico predictions, thus warranting experimental validation to corroborate the claims, considering the moderate binding affinities. Moreover, α-pinene and its enantiomers exhibit comparable physicochemical and pharmacokinetic profiles with favorable lipophilicity and BBB permeability supporting their potential as CNS-active agents. However, limitations such as poor aqueous solubility, low gastrointestinal absorption, and limited structural complexity may hinder their development as drug candidates.

Materials and Methods

Sourcing of Essential Oils, Pure Compounds, and Reagents

Boswellia carterii essential oil was sourced from Scatters Oils (Randburg, South Africa), assigned a laboratory reference number and stored at 4 °C until required for analysis. Pure compounds for α-pinene (98%), (−)-α-pinene (97%), (+)-α-pinene (98%), as well as dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), diazepam (DZP) and hexane (analytical reagent grade) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Merck (Pty) Ltd. (Modderfontein, South Africa).

Chemical Profiling Using Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry/Flame Ionization Detection (GC-MS/FID)

The essential oil was prepared as a 10% (v/v) solution in hexane (AR grade) and analyzed using an Agilent 6890N gas chromatograph, coupled to both a quadrupole mass spectrometer (MS) (Agilent 5973 Network) and a flame ionization detector (FID) (Chemetrix (Pty) Ltd., Midrand, South Africa). A volume of 1 μL of the solution was introduced into the GC inlet set at 250 °C using an auto sampler (Agilent 7683B Series). Separation of the compounds was achieved on an HP-Innowax, 60 m x 250 μm i.d. polyethylene glycol column (film thickness 0.25 μm). The column oven temperature was increased from 60 to 220 °C at 4 °C/min, held for 10 min, and then increased to 240 °C at a rate of 1 °C/min, to achieve a total run time of 60 min. High purity helium (99.99%, Afrox, South Africa) was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.4 mL/min with electron impact ionization (EI) at 70 eV. Mass spectra were obtained in the range of 35–550 m/z. The transfer line and FID temperatures were maintained at 280 and 300 °C, respectively. The MS source temperature was at 230 °C and the quadrupoles were held at 150 °C. The relative percentage area of each peak was calculated using the FID peak areas and tentative identification of the compounds was achieved using mass spectrometry and various commercial libraries such as NIST05a and MassFinder4, pure standards and by matching the retention indices calculated using a C8–C20 alkanes mix (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck SA (Pty) Ltd., Modderfontein, South Africa) as reference, with those found in literature.

Chiral Analysis Using Gas Chromatography-Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (GC-ToF-MS)

Chiral analysis of the essential oil and selected compounds was performed using a Leco Pegasus 4D, GC system (Leco Africa (Pty) Ltd., Kempton Park, South Africa) comprising an Agilent 6890A GC and a Time-of-Flight (ToF) mass spectrometer (MS). The system was operated in one-dimensional (1D) mode. The diluted sample (1% v/v in hexane) was introduced and a 1:100 split flow was applied using a Gerstel liquid autosampler (Leco Africa (Pty) Ltd., Kempton Park, South Africa). Chiral separation of the compounds within the essential oil and pure standards was achieved using a specific enantiomer capillary column (Restek Rt-Bdex-sm 30m × 250 μm ID × 0.25 μm film thickness). The elution order and identity of the analytes were confirmed by analysis of reference standards of the individual compounds, comparing the mass spectra with those in commercial libraries (NIST11) and the relevant retention time.

Helium (99.99% purity, Afrox, South Africa) was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow of 1.4 mL/min. The oven was programmed to start at 80 °C (held for 1 min), and the temperature was increased at a rate of 5 °C/min to 220 °C (5 min). The inlet was set at 220 °C. The MS transfer line temperature was maintained at 250 °C and the ion source was set at 200 °C. An ionization energy of 70 eV was applied, and data were acquired at 100 spectra/s over the range m/z 35–550. Peak width and a signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) >100 was considered during data processing. Leco ChromaToF-GC Software Version 4.51.6.0 was used for data processing and instrument control.

Zebrafish Maintenance and Breeding

The study was conducted at the Tshwane University of Technology (TUT) following the South African National Standards guidelines for the care and use of animals in research (SANS 10386:2021). Ethical approval was obtained from the Animal Research Ethics Committee (AREC) at TUT (Reference number: AREC2021/10/004).

Wild type laboratory-bred adult zebrafish were housed in a ZebTec Active Blue standalone aquatic system (Tecniplast, Italy) with polycarbonate fish tanks of different sizes. The fish were kept at standard laboratory conditions of 28.5 °C, pH of 7 and conductivity between 500 and 600 μS/cm, with light-dark cycles maintained at 14 h light/10 h dark in summer and 12 h light/12 h dark in winter. A fish density of five adult fish per liter of water was maintained in the tanks. The zebrafish were fed high protein brine shrimp (Artemia) once a day and dry tropical fish flakes twice a day. Embryo production was achieved by overnight gender separation of two male and three female fish in a breeding tank. In the morning, the separators were removed, and the fish allowed to mate for about an hour. The embryos were collected, rinsed, and allowed to grow in Petri dishes containing 0.3× Danieau medium at 28.5 °C. Daily media change was done, and embryos sorted under a microscope (Olympus IX71, Japan) to remove dead embryos and debris until the larvae were 5-dpf. Healthy larvae were selected for use in the subsequent assays.

Determination of Maximum Tolerated Concentration

The maximum tolerated concentrations were determined to establish the nontoxic concentrations of the essential oil and compounds. This assay is suitable for identifying appropriate concentrations to be used in the anxiolytic activity assay. The maximum tolerated concentration was determined by exposing zebrafish larvae to a range of compound concentrations and identifying the highest concentration that did not induce observable mortality, morphological abnormalities, or overt signs of toxicity over an 18-h period. Stock solutions of the essential oil (5 mL/L) and pure compounds (50 mM) were prepared in 100% DMSO and stored at −20 °C. The working solutions were freshly prepared to achieve a concentration range of 100–500 μL/L for the essential oil and 2.5–150 μM for the compounds. Larvae at 5-dpf were gently transferred into a 48 well plate (five larvae per well in duplicate for each treatment) using a plastic Pasteur pipet. The 0.3x Danieau medium was subsequently removed and 500 μL of each essential oil or compound were immediately added to the duplicate wells. The negative controls were 1% DMSO (vehicle control) and 0.3x Danieau medium (growth control). The plates were covered with aluminum foil and incubated for 18 h at 28.5 °C overnight. At the end of the incubation period, the larvae were observed for toxicity indicators under the microscope, which include hypo-activity, absence or decreased response to touch, loss of posture, kyphosis, lordosis, scoliosis, slow heartbeat, edema, precipitation, or death. A concentration was considered nontoxic if ≥6 of the 10 larvae were healthy after incubation.

Computational Methodology

Toxicology Profiling

ProTox (ProTox 3.0; AG Preissner; Charité Berlin), an online platform was used to predict acute toxicity (LD50), organ-specific toxicity (hepatotoxicity, neurotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and cardiotoxicity), and toxicological end points (carcinogenicity and blood–brain barrier (BBB) penetration). The toxicological profiling of the compounds is performed by employing molecular similarity, pharmacophore mapping, fragment-based propensities, and machine-learning models. The ProTox Web server integrates data from both in vitro assays (such as Tox21 assays and Ames tests) and in vivo studies enabling comprehensive toxicity prediction.

Drug-likeness and ADME Properties

The SwissADME online server was utilized to predict various physicochemical parameters, including molecular formula, molecular weight (MW), number of heavy atoms (HA), number of rotatable bonds (RB), number of hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA), and hydrogen bond donors (HBD). The lipophilicity (iLogP), water solubility (logS), pharmacokinetic properties (GI absorption, blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeation, and P-glycoprotein substrate (P-gp) status), drug-likeness (including Lipinski’s rule and bioavailability), and lead-likeness of the compounds were also estimated via the SwissADME online server. The canonical SMILES of the compounds were obtained from PubChem and submitted to the SwissADME server to predict the compounds’ absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) properties.

Biological Target Predictions

To evaluate the biological relevance of the potential drug leads, the SuperPred target prediction server was used to identify potential biological targets for all the compounds that exhibited favorable ADMET properties. Filters were applied during target identification, and only targets related to the anxiolytic pathway were retained for further analysis. The previously generated canonical SMILES were imported into the SuperPred target prediction system for processing.

Molecular Docking

Phytochemical Compounds Acquisition and Geometry Optimisation

The 3D structures of the active compounds were obtained on the PubChem database and geometrically optimized using Avogadro (version 1.2.0) through energy minimization. MMFF94s method integrated on Avogadro was used to optimize the molecular geometries of the compounds and the steepest descent algorithm for structural minimization. All the compounds were saved as .mol2 files, a 3D input file recognized as a ligand in UCSF Chimera software.

Protein Acquisition and Preparation

Putative protein targets for the selected compounds were predicted using the SuperPred Web server, which identifies biological targets based on structural similarity and pharmacophore mapping. These targets were subsequently screened in relation to their reported involvement in anxiety-related neurobiological pathways ,, and the key brain regions they affect. Following this filtering process, seven targets were identified as directly relevant to anxiety modulation and were selected for further molecular docking analysis. AlphaFold structures (Table ) for cannabinoid (CB2), dopamine D1, glutamate NMDA, monoamine oxidase A, serotonin 2C (5-HT2C), serotonin 7 (5-HT7), and sodium channel protein type III alpha subunit receptors were obtained as .pdb files. To identify the binding sites, the AlphaFold structures were superimposed with experimentally resolved structures retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank: CB2 receptor (PDB ID: 5ZTY), dopamine D1 (PDB ID: 7JVP), glutamate NMDA (PDB ID: 6IRA), monoamine oxidase A (PDB ID: 2Z5X), 5-HT2C (PDB ID: 8DPF), 5-HT7 (PDB ID: 7XTC) and sodium channel protein type III alpha subunit (PDB ID: 7W77) which are .pdb files recognized as a receptor input files in UCSF Chimera (version 1.11.2). The protein structures were prepared for molecular docking using the dock prep module in UCSF Chimera including the addition of hydrogens and partial residue replacement using the Dunbrack rotamer library, conversion of bromo-UMP to UMP, conversion of selenomethionine to methionine, and conversion of methylsulfonyl-dCMP to CMP. The AMBER.ff14SB force field was employed to distribute the Gasteiger charges on the protein molecule. The processed protein structures were then used for molecular docking, ensuring the reliability in the predicted ligand receptor interactions. All target proteins were prepared using UCSF Chimera, receptor-specific modifications were implemented according to structural and functional characteristics. G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR) targets (CB2, D1, 5-HT2C, and 5-HT7) were prepared by preserving transmembrane binding pockets, whereas ion channel proteins (NMDA and SCP Type III) required maintenance of channel architecture and biologically relevant subunits. In contrast, MAO-A preparation included retention of the catalytic FAD cofactor essential for enzymatic activity and ligand interactions.

Molecular Docking

Molecular docking was performed using the AutoDock Vina plugin (version 1.2.0) in UCSF Chimera with default docking parameters, a reliable method for predicting the binding affinity of compounds with their respective biological targets. The binding sites on the target proteins were identified based on the cocrystallized ligands in the receptor–ligand complex obtained from PDB database. Prior to docking, crystallographic water molecules and cofactors were removed, Gasteiger charges were assigned to the ligands, and nonpolar hydrogens were removed. Ligands were prepared to reflect their expected ionization state at physiological pH (7.4) using UCSF Chimera. Because α-pinene and its enantiomers are nonionizable hydrocarbons under these conditions, they were docked in their neutral forms. Where applicable, reference ligands were assigned physiologically relevant protonation states based on their ionizable groups and predicted pK a values before docking, all relevant tautomeric/protonation states were considered during docking. Using the AutoDock Vina plugin in UCSF Chimera, a grid box was constructed with coordinates as shown in supplementary data 8. A grid box encompassing all amino acid residues within 5 Å of the cocrystallized ligand was generated. The compounds were then docked into the prepared protein binding pocket providing valuable information on the compounds’ binding mode and geometric fit for comparison with known reference drugs. UCSF Chimera uses the iterative local search algorithm as the main scoring function to rank the ligand poses ensuring a favorable pose is achieved. Additionally, to validate the molecular docking, all the ligands (reference compounds) from the experimental cocrystallized structures were redocked in the active site of the selected proteins (supplementary data 9). This validation was necessary to ensure that our compounds bind in a manner similar to the reference native ligands.

Molecular Dynamics Simulation

Molecular dynamics simulations (MDS) were carried out for three of the targets to which the compounds bound favorably after docking, namely, CB2, 5-HT7 and 5-HT2C receptors. Molecular dynamics simulations are necessary to validate docking results and provide a more robust assessment of binding energy of all complexes over time. Using UCSF Chimera, hydrogen atoms were removed, and AMBER.ff14SB charges assigned to the compounds, which were then saved individually in .mol2 file format, while the protein structures were saved in .pdb format. Subsequently, all MDS were carried out using the graphic processing unit (GPU) version of the Particle Mesh Ewald Molecular Dynamics (PMEMD) of AMBER 18 software package. The ANTECHAMBER module was used to assign atomic partial charges to the compounds, applying the Restrained Electrostatic Potential (RESP) method along with the General AMBER Force Field (GAFF) protocol. The FF14SB in the Amber 18 suite was used to parametrize the protein. The Link Edit and PARM (LEaP) module of Amber 18 was then used to add missing hydrogen and heavy atoms. Depending on the complex, LEaP was also used to neutralize the systems by adding Na+ and Cl– counterions and solvated them in a TIP3P water box with a 10 Å buffer. LEaP was further used to generate the input and topology files for downstream processing. To eliminate unfavorable contacts during the molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, each system underwent energy minimization for 2000 steps. This involved an initial 1000 steps using the steepest descent method, followed by 1000 steps with the conjugate gradient algorithm, both performed without restraints. The systems were then gradually heated from 0 to 300 K under an NPT ensemble, using a harmonic restraint of 5 kcal/mol·Å2 and a Langevin thermostat with a collision frequency of 1 ps–1. All systems were then equilibrated at 300 K for 500 ps without restraints, maintaining constant pressure at 1 bar using the Berendsen barostat. The SHAKE algorithm was applied to constrain all bonds involving hydrogen atoms. A 100 ns unrestrained MD production run was then carried out for each system, using a target temperature coupling time of 2 ps and maintaining constant pressure at 1 bar. Analysis of the trajectories and coordinates generated from the MD run was carried through the CPPTRAJ and PTRAJ modules incorporated in Amber 18. The binding free energy was then estimated.

Statistical Analysis

All the statistical tests were performed in SPSS version 26 (IBM, New York). To determine the statistical differences between the negative control (1% DMSO), essential oil and compounds, the data were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov/Shapiro normal distribution test. Where data was not normally distributed, the nonparametric guidelines were followed. Kruskal–Wallis test followed by posthoc Dunn’s test were appropriate for multiple comparisons between group means and the mean of the negative control (p < 0.05).
