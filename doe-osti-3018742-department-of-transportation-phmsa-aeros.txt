ABSTRACT
This report discusses the work conducted at Sandia National Laboratories during the preliminary
phase (Phase-0) of the Department of Transportation (DOT) Pipeline and Hazardous Materials
Safety Administration (PHMSA) project tasked to better understand what happens to a high-
pressure liquid canister or vessel during a fire in which an accidental instantaneous release
occurs. Phase 0 had two main objects: (1) to establish a methodology for the high temperature
and pressure spray vessel release and (2) to characterize the spray droplet formation. For this
test series, diagnostic equipment such as the Malvern Spraytec, high-speed photometric cameras,
pencil gauges, and thermocouples were used. Additionally, the effect of surface tension on the
particle size distribution was evaluated with 1-liter of distilled water and with the same including
the addition of 0.5% soap/water mixture.

Results from this effort indicated that the plume formed from the instantaneous release in all
conditions primarily included two distinct particle size distributions including 217-312µm and
0.53µm on average. Soapy water formed a higher concentration of smaller mist (0.53µm) than
distilled water, potentially due to decreased surface tension with the added surfactant. Though
results showed good correlation to certain particle size distributions it is encouraged to conduct
additional tests like this with a wider range of liquids with different properties, which could affect
the overall particle size distribution when released.

1. INTRODUCTION
Aerosolized spray canisters are commonly used commercially available products to dispense a wide
variety of materials. The majority (75%) of these spray cans consists of a few layers of lacquered
tinplate (steel with a layer of tin) that are crimped together. Inside the can are two main components
including the material to be sprayed (i.e., paint, hairspray, cleaner, etc.) and a propellant. Often, a
stabilizing agent or other trace additions to improve the applicant material is added as well. (Marker
et al., 1998). To ensure safety in handling and storage all manufacturing and storge of any aerosol
product is governed by regulations. NFPA 30B provides commercial storage recommendations.
While this guidance includes substantial information on the hazard, it lacks guidance on potential
transportation hazards where stacking, atmosphere, and lack of suppression may differentiate the
envrironments. Additional technical studies behind the formulation of the NFPA 30B
recommendations and some FAA studies have not been found available in the open literature.

In a transportation accident, the containers may become punctured, resulting in release of most of the
contents at very high velocity. The energy surrounding the puncture or other motion often produces
enough heat to allow for self-ignition. Due to the atomization of the liquids and the rapid entrainment
of air, an explosive release or a high-intensity torch may result. Another significant possibility resulting
in the potential explosive release of the contents is that the commodities are exposed to a fire. The
fire can pressurize the contents to failure pressures of the can, and erupt with violent propulsion, and
cause further damage due to the jetting flames or the blast wave if an explosion occurs. During
transportation, there is fire potential due to the nearby presence of fuels involved in transportation,
usually gasoline or diesel.

Once a ruptured canister’s contents are lit, the buoyant forces and the thermal products will generate
turbulence that allow for mixing, that broadens the combustion zone, resulting in a torch-spray, or
explosive combustion. Liquids ejected from the container at a high rate of speed will atomize in the
air. Further downstream, the jet will degrade into turbulent eddies, providing a higher volume of
mixing conditions available for ignition and for sustained intense burning. The container contents
affect the volume of the region for possible ignition, which for a non-combustible rated content may
be zero or very small. Pressure and hole size may also play a role in the dynamics.

A set of studies have been described by the FAA concerned with aerosol canisters as part of personal
baggage on aviation flights (Blake, 1989, Blake et al., 1998, Hawthorne and Blake, 1995, Marker, 1998).
A wide variety of common personal use spray cans were evaluated and found to fall into a range of
responses, varying from non-ignited up to highly flammable. Other than these reports, there is little
additional work reported in the literature concerning potential flames generated from spray-cans
during transportation. FAA studies focused on the replacement of fluorocarbon propellants with
conventional hydrocarbons that represent a higher flammability risk. Follow-on studies considered
minor alterations to the formulations to decrease the flammability hazard.

Ignition theory uses the Damkholer number - the ratio of the characteristic timescales for reaction
and for diffusion of a flammable substance - and when plotted versus temperature, a functional space
detailing the ability to sustain or initiate reactions is developed. An ‘S’ shaped curve identifies the
critical space for the ignition and extinction of a reacting system, with the upper and lower branches
representing the range of theoretical ignition and extinction conditions (Williams, 1982). Ignition is
probabilistically related to the prevalence of viable ignition conditions in the spray, which is governed
by the volume that achieves a regime of possible ignition. The jetting behavior of a release creates an

 9

idealized environment rapidly mixing the bottle contents with air and atomizing the liquid. Atomized
liquid evaporates much more readily, aiding the fuel source for mixtures that have a non-combustible
propellant by providing an optimal environment for liquid evaporation. These sprays are complicated
by multiphase physics, reaction rate physics, fast processes, high-Mach behavior, mixing dynamics,
and multi-component behaviors.

The post-rupture aerosol size distribution is expected to vary considerably dependent on the material
properties of the contents, e.g., pressure, liquid viscosity and liquid surface tension. Rupture dynamics,
e.g., impulse time and shape of breach, may also impact droplet size and vapor concentration.
Ultimately, these effects need to be characterized to better understand the range of possibilities in
transportation accidents. In spray combustion, the Sauter mean diameter (D[3,2]) of droplets is used
to correlate the combustion performance to aerosol characteristics (Lefebvre, 1989). This diameter
takes into account the surface area to volume ratio of the droplet and is the basis of many empirical
combustion spray correlations.

This project was initially motivated by a study commissioned by the work sponsors. Cambridge
Systematics Inc. reviews the incidents and factors surrounding the transportation environment
involving aerosol canisters (CSI, 2020). Notably, they find the fire environment produced by incidents
to be the most pressing issue. As the data in the open literature are relatively sparse, this project has
a broader goal of exploring the dynamics of the pressurized release to help articulate safety regimes
and provide key data for future assessments.

2. METHODOLOGY

The Phase-0 PHMSA study was intended to serve two purposes: (1) establish a methodology for the
spray vessel system and (2) characterize the spray droplet formation after instantaneous release. This
section will describe the methodology for the experimental setup, the diagnostic instruments used,
and the real-time measurements that were used to characterize source term release. Source term release
in this study is defined as droplet size and concentration.

2.1. Experimental Setup

The tests were conducted at the Thermal Test Complex (TTC, building 6539) in the west parking
lot, located in Tech Area 3 at Sandia National Laboratories. (Figure 1)

 Figure 1: Sketchup drawing of west parking lot of TTC and Test location

A view of the test setup is shown below in Figure 2, showing the spray system setup in the west
parking lot of TTC. Instrumentation (TCs, pressure transmitter, heater, ICP pressure sensors, and
solenoid) can be seen in the same figure.

The spray system including the bottle, sensors, and valves was designed to hold pressures up to 300
PSI inside a vessel filled with mock commodities to create a near instantaneous release when a solenoid
valve is actuated. For these mock scenarios in Phase 0, only water and/or soapy water will be used to
characterize the sprayer and collect aerosol measurements from the release.

The entire spray system is bolted to a custom stand that has a built-in design to ensure the pressure
vessel can be positioned in whatever angle is desired (Figure 2). The stand was weighed down with
sandbags to counter the estimated 200 lbs. of force when the contents are released.

2.2. Experimental Hardware

2.2.1. Pressure Vessel

This custom pressure vessel was designed to replicate an aerosol pressure failure by releasing the
contents through the solenoid under high pressure (<300 PSI). The vessel was insulated in all but the
first test after it was noted that ambient temperature and wind had a significant effect on temperature
rise. Half inch thick Superwool insulation was used to wrap the pressure vessel, heater, and
thermocouples to give a more repeatable temperature increase as seen in Figure 3.

 Figure 3: Insulated pressure vessel and instrumentation.

 12

2.2.2.     Heater

A Watlow Mineral Insulated Band Heater was chosen as the heat source for the vessel because of its
high performance and efficient heat transfer, Figure 4. The heater was powered by a portable generator
supplying power to a spider box with 6 120v GFCI protected 20-amp outlets. The mineral insulated
(MI) band heater is a high-performance heater that incorporates Watlow’s proprietary mineral
insulation. According to the manufacturer, this material offers much higher thermal conductivity than
mica and hard ceramic insulators that are used in conventional heaters.

A thin layer of high thermal conductive MI material electrically insulates the element wire from the
inside diameter of the heater sheath. The result is more efficient heat transfer, which lowers element
wire temperatures and increases heater life.

 Figure 4: 1000w Watlow Mineral Insulated Band Heater.

2.2.3. Solenoid Valve

U.S. Solid High Pressure Solenoid Valve features a high-pressure, high temperature design which was
appropriate for this test series. The valve body was made from stainless steel with a seal made of
PTFE, which are resistant to high pressure transfer of liquids up to 100 bar. Additionally, because of
its material properties, this valve had excellent temperature resistance up to 248 ֯F. The valve provides
a valve flow coefficient (Cv) of 12.0 with the 1” pipe size inlet and outlet. The solenoid coil required
a 24v DC power supply to actuate the valve and used a relay inside the DAQ to trigger the solenoid
when the vessel pressure reached approximately 300 PSI.

 13

 Figure 5: U.S Solids High Pressure Solenoid Valve.

2.2.4. Pressure Transmitter
The transmitter, also known as transducers, converts pressure to an electrical signal that can be
interpreted by receiving devices such as our National Instruments DAQ. Prior to use, the transmitter
is calibrated from manufactures specifications regarding pressure range and output signal. As pressure
increases, the output signal from the transmitter will increase. Transmitters will only provide accurate
readings within the rated pressure range.

Transmitters with current output connect using two wires. The same wire is used to send a signal to
the receiver and to power the transmitter. Current does not lose signal over long distances and is not
affected by electrical interference from other devices.

Transmitters are rated NEMA 4X and IP65 or IP67 and are good for washdown and corrosive
environments which is one of the reasons we chose this pressure transmitter so it can withstand the
harsh environments of this and subsequent Phases of the tests.

 Figure 6: Ashcroft Pressure Transmitter.

 14

2.2.5. Needle Valve
The Swagelok Integral-Bonnet Needle Valve chosen was intended to serve as a secondary shutoff to
the pressure transmitter and serve as a flow limiting device to limit the outlet flow of pressure in the
pressure chamber just below the pressure transmitter to help prevent damage to the transmitter with
the sudden release of pressure. The max flow rate through the valve is 1.80 Cv (gallons per minute
with 1 psi pressure gradient).

 Figure 7: Shut off valve to the pressure transmitter chamber.

2.2.6. PCB Piezotronics ICP Pressure Sensor
The Pencil Probes (Figure 8) are designed to measure shock waves caused from explosions in air with
an extremely fast micro-second response time, with resonant frequency above 400k Hz. During this
study, we used a high-speed data acquisition system to record data from the pressure sensors at 100
Hz.

 Figure 8:Test setup with 2 ICP pressure sensors

The calibration reports in Appendix A show the sensitivity in mV/psi and mV/kPa and test data for
each probe to allow us to calibrate our data to a more precise decimal point.

2.2.7. Thermal Instrumentation
Temperature measurements were made at 3 locations with ASTM Type K thermocouple (TC) with
loose braid and fiberglass sleeving. Two of the TC locations were on the Watlow heater to show the

temperature of each side of the heater to indicate if the heater was operating correctly. The third TC

the TC’s were bench tested with calibrated TC meters to insure we were reading properly as seen in

2.3. Testing Setup and Procedure

The test protocol followed similar procedures for a fill with un-pressurized prior contents and near
ambient starting bottle temperature. Below is a step-by-step procedure conducted for each test in the
series. For this effort, two liquid material types were utilized, which included: Deionized Water and
Soapy Water. Soapy water was made of a mixture of 5mL Ajax® Ultra Triple Action Orange
dishwashing liquid to 1L of Deionized Water (0.5% Soapy Water).

 1. Remove solenoid from vessel and turn stand on its side.

 2. Make sure the bottle hand valve and pressure transmitter needle valves are shut off
 before adding water to ensure water does not get trapped in between the transmitter
 and the needle valve creating a water pocket that could possibly affect pressure
 readings.

 16

 3. Add 1000ml of Deionized water or Soapy Water to the vessel as measured by a
 liquid measuring cup with a least count of 5% of the full measurement.

 4. Re install the solenoid screwed securely to the bottle and turn the system back to level
 and open the needle valve to the pressure transducer to allow pressure to read on the
 transducer.

 5. Once bottle is full and valves are in the proper settings, begin the control portion of
 test by turning on the 1000w heater while checking heater TC’s to ensure the
 temperature is rising. Allow the heater to run and bring the bottle pressure up to
 desired pressure (300 PSI).
 6. At 275 PSI a countdown is initiated to allow the photometrics and aerosol teams know
 the release will occur in approximately 5 seconds.
 7. Turn on the high speed DAQ recording at 275 psi to get the pressure wave of the
 release.
 8. At 300 PSI actuate the solenoid and release the pressure.
 9. Once pressure is released shut off 120v power to the heater and lock out the heater

2.3.1. Meteorological Conditions
Meteorological conditions that included temperature, wind speed and wind direction were monitored
during the test series to inform plume direction or inconsistencies with the particle size distribution
during measurements. If wind speeds were too high or directed the plume away from the particle
sizers, the tests were paused to ensure accurate measurements. Data collected during the test series
came from a 10-meter tower located near the test site which is controlled by the Sandia National
Laboratories Meteorology Program. Data recorded during this test series can be found in Appendix
B.

2.4. Malvern Spraytec: Particle Size Distribution Measurements
During Phase 0, particle size distribution (PSD) was measured with the Malvern Panalytical’s Spraytec
instrument which is a laser diffraction system that measures droplet size and volume concentration
based on Fraunhofer light scatter and Mie Theory. The instrument data acquisition rate has a
frequency of 10kHz, which makes it ideal for rapid source term characterization. However, it is
important to note that the output measurement time is averaged in 1 second intervals. For Phase 0,
the Spraytec system was used to measure particle size distribution of the source term release from the
high pressure, high temperature vessel. Two Spraytec systems were used during the study to measure
the plume profile of the spray droplets at two distances at 15ft to 25ft from the point source release,
Figure 10. The instruments were setup to measure continuously at one second intervals with an open
optical bench. The bench guaranteed two conditions: (1) transmitter and detector alignment and (2)
unimpeded pathway for the spray plume.

  Figure 10: Malvern Spraytec Instruments 15ft to 25 ft from the high pressure, high temperature
 vessel.

To ensure the safety of the Spraytec closest to the high-pressure vessel after instantaneous release,
cinder block walls were erected in front of the transmitter and detector housings. This was determined
necessary during several dry runs conducted prior to the initial water release where pressure was
measured using pencil gauges at each of the housing positions. During these dry runs, it was also
determined that the second Spraytec did not need the same precautions, since it was positioned
approximate 5 ft further from the point source. Sandbags weighing approximately 5-10 lbs. each were
place on the stand to ensure secured placement during the test series. Figure 11 is a representative
diagram of the experimental setup conditions proposed. In this diagram there were two scenarios
where the Spraytec instrument were placed at different distances for added protection and to ensure
the instruments were aligned with the spray profile trajectory.

2.4.1. Malvern Spraytec Software Standard Operating Procedure (SOP)
The Malvern can be used in a variety of scenarios to measure either liquid droplets from spray mist
systems or suspended particulates in a liquid cell. For these tests, we used the open bench continuously
measurement type under the standard operating procedure (SOP). The SOP was setup with 1 second
averaged samples with a 300 mm lens type that measures nominal size ranges from 0.1 µm to 900.0
µm. The standard particle type was water with a refractive index of 1.33 and a dispersant type of air
with a refractive index of 1.00 with multiple scattering analysis enabled. As part of an automatic
process, background scattering signal is always measured according to its electronic and optical
components. This initial and automated process is then used to subtract any background signals prior
to test measurements. Electronic background considers the “noise” from the dark background, where
the laser beam is shut off during the initial measurements. Whereas the optical background considers
any contaminants on the optical windows or scattering due to daylight or artificial light. For these
measurements, a 4” PVC tube was placed over the transmitter and detector to ensure an accurate
electronic measurement due to direct angle of the daylight entering the detector during the
measurement, Figure 12.

4. DISCUSSION

4.1. Particle Size Distribution
Particle size distribution measured from the pressure vessel for water and 0.5% soapy water conditions
near the center and edges of the plume at approximately 15 feet from the source release were recorded
during these test series. Although data collected were limited to less than 5 second averaged
measurements at one second intervals, bimodal size distributions were primarily observed. Bimodal
distribution was described here as two distinct peaks in the data. In these data, the averaged large
droplet size formed during the release was between 217-312µm whereas the smaller mist averaged
particle size was approximately 0.53µm.
Further, for these data it was observed that the 0.5% soapy water had higher concentration of smaller
mist than water, potentially due to surface tension. Surface tension is described as the property that
resists expansion of liquid surface area (Lefebvre, 1989). Therefore, if aerodynamic forces, velocity, or
pressure are increased, additional particle breakup is likely, which decreases PSD. This effect in surface
tension was seen in recorded data when comparing water and soapy water measurements, Section 3.1.
It is important to note that standard deviation was highest at the smaller particle size range, deviating
in % volume concentration from 1-15%. Whereas the larger particle size range on average only
deviated by 1-3%, indicating that the larger droplets formed from the instantaneous release were more
consistent.
Additionally, several lessons learned were observed with the use of the Malvern Spraytec systems
including: (1) placement and protection of the system during a high-pressure release and (2) how
daylight can affect the electronic background during initial startup. Both lessons took several iterations
to better understand before resolved, but helped the team develop an experimental methodology for
future testing. For instance, the Malvern instruments could have been placed closer to the source with
the additional protective measures. The second Malvern at a standoff distance of 25-30ft, recorded
data from the water and soapy water mist, but the information was inconsistent, therefore emitted
from this report. In this case, external environmental factors like wind may have had an influence, as
the records show that wind speeds ranged from approximately 2-25 mph, Appendix B
Tests 25 and 26 were additional release conducted at the end of the test series. As mentioned, Test 25
tested geometric features of the nozzle, where as Test 26 tested plume angle. As seen in Figure 17, the
PSD did shift towards a smaller mist likely due to reduction in particle collision during the
instantaneous release. Although, this is an interesting measurement, it was not further pursued since
it’s a more idealistic release scenario, i.e. a crack or puncture in a spray vessel would likely not be
smooth or round. As for Test 26, the spray trajectory of the instantaneous release was outside the
pathlength where either Malvern could measure, therefore the measurements were nonconclusive.

5. CONCLUSION
Phase-0 of the DOT PHMSA test series had two intentions: (1) establish a methodology for the spray
system and (2) characterize the spray droplet formation after instantaneous release from the canister.
This phase was critical to understand particle formation from the pressure vessel in a safe but
controlled condition, which could not be done in later studies where ignition and fireball formation
would occur. Through several design iterations, the experimental setup and methodology necessary to
measure the spray plume was established by the team. Although, if future studies like this were
conducted, the Malvern Spraytec instruments would have been placed at a closer distance to the source
for a more representative PSD throughout the plume. None-the-less, this design setup helped inform
the experimental setup. Additionally, the analysis of the spray pressure inconsistencies discussed in
Section 4.2 should help inform and improve the experimental processed for the next phase.

This study also determined if liquid type regarding surface tension influenced particle size distribution.
As observed in the results of Section 3.1, using a detergent like soap lowered the interfacial tension
between liquids, thereby allowing the surface tension to be overcome by aerodynamic or pressure
forces to shift atomization. This shift likely increased the concentration of smaller mist observed in
the soapy water results. Therefore, addition of the surfactant did influence particle size distribution.
The team also evaluated the use of a different nozzle geometry with the addition of the smooth pipe.
From observation, the particle size distribution did shift to a smaller mist, however, additional
measurements would be necessary to confirm this finding.

Finally, Phase-0 also helped visualize what the spray profile would look like during an instantons
release with the use of the high-speed photometric data. These data were useful in visualization of the
trajectory of the liquid jet and plume, which ultimately could help inform the distance the fireball
could reach during ignition in the next phase of this project. These data also helped determine how
quickly the plume could form and dissipate during an accident scenario.
