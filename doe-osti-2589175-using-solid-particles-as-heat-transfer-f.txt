Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

NREL assisted the BRIDGE project led by the University of Colorado (CU), Boulder, on
developing granular flow and heat transfer simulation tool to serve for an enclosed particle-
receiver design. NREL will provide the particle receiver design configuration and operating
condition to assist the model setup and may provide certain testing results for model
verifications. NREL will attend project reviewing meeting and provide application back-ground
including concentrating solar energy technology to collaborate with CU researchers.

Summary of Research Results:

Note this report includes only Project 3 (Modification 2) under this CRADA number. Other
projects are unrelated and will be published separately.

To achieve the SunShot goal of 6¢/kWh, significant barriers in existing concentrating solar
power (CSP) technologies must be overcome. The state-of-the-art in CSP involves the use of
salts for thermal energy storage (TES) and as a heat transfer fluid (HTF). NREL has developed a
novel CSP system utilizing solid particles for heat storage and transfer exchanger. The critical
component in realizing this CSP system was a particle receiver. The BRIDGE project developed
modeling tools and guidance in assisting the particle receiver development. In addition, the
modeling tool developed had broad usage in chemical and material process designs. NREL tasks
focused on providing CSP and particle receiver design configurations to facilitate CU modeling
efforts on particle flow and heat transfer. Three main tasks were performed in three phases of the
project period of performance as described following.

Highlighted results from CU research work and NREL tasks are summarized as below:

When considering multiphase heat transfer in a DEM framework, a wide variety of transport
mechanisms arise since each particle is tracked throughout the simulation. For the gas-solid flow
in question, these mechanisms include: particle-wall conduction (PW), particle-fluid-wall
conduction (PFW), particle-particle conduction (PP), particle-fluid-particle conduction (PFP),
fluid-wall convection, and fluid-particle convection. Of these mechanisms, it has been found in
previous work with the hexagonal tube array that the PFW mechanism is the dominant mode of
heat transfer between gravity driven granular flows and an immersed boundary (the given
system) [1]. The physical model implemented to calculate PFW conduction is a minor adaptation
of the static fluid lens model developed for PFP conduction by Rong and Horio [2]. The
underlying principle of Rong and Horio’s model is that each particle is surrounded by an
interstitial fluid “lens” that is a fraction of the particle diameter thick. When this lens contacts
another body, in this case a wall, conduction occurs through the fluid lens to the particle. A
physical illustration of this is given below in Figure 1. It can therefore be surmised that particles
must be extremely close to a wall, within a fluid lens thickness, in order for PFW conduction to
occur. Furthermore, conditions which yield high solids concentrations are more likely to have
particles near a wall. Ultimately, for systems dominated by PFW conduction, configurations
which lead to high solids concentrations (low void fractions) near conducting bodies will result
in increased heat transfer.

 Figure 1. PFW conduction through the interstitial gas between the wall and particle.

Close inspection of the hydrodynamic profile for Baffle Configuration 1 (Figure 2(a)) shows that
low solids concentrations persist throughout the system and particularly after converging baffles.
To avoid regions of high void fraction the particles must be consistently forced into contact with
the walls. Therefore, a geometry change was made to remove the converging baffles in lieu of
only diverging type baffles. In addition, the vertical spacing between baffles was reduced to
maintain more uniform contact. It is also noted, that the decrease in vertical spacing caused an
increase in the number cut cells per system height. The boosted cut cell density ultimately forced
a reduction of system size in the y-direction, from 1[m] to 15[cm], to avoid overflow of memory

While Baffle Configuration 2 showed marked improvements over Baffle Configuration 1, it was
still believed that solids fractions were too low near boundary walls. From previous work with
gravity driven flows, it was known that solids at the inlet of the simulation begin to accelerate
under the force of gravity and cause the void fraction to increase exponentially as fall height
increases. In addition, void fraction profiles as a function of fall height are strongly related to
initial conditions. Therefore, by increasing the solids fraction at the simulation inlet, it was
hypothesized that the dilution of particles before contact with the first baffle could be reduced.

The effect of baffle apex angle upon the system heat transfer coefficient was explored once a
new baseline had been developed (Configuration 3 above). It should be noted that while the
baffles significantly contribute to the heat transfer in the simulations, they are not the intended
main source of heat transfer in the real system. Therefore, the focus for enhancing heat transfer
should be placed upon the domain walls and not the baffles. When altering the baffle apex angle
it was found that the heat transfer with the domain walls was not a strong function of apex angle,
but that the heat transfer with the baffles was. Specifically, that more obtuse baffle angles led to
higher baffle heat transfer coefficients due to enhanced particle-baffle contacts.
