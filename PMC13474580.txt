MCL-1 as a tumor microenvironment-linked survival node in cholangiocarcinoma
Authors: Hiroki Hashida, Atsushi Kobayashi, Masatoshi Akagami, Tomohiro Okamoto, Hirokazu Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cholangiocarcinoma remains a highly lethal malignancy characterized by marked biological heterogeneity, extensive desmoplasia, and limited long-term benefit from systemic therapy. Although recent therapeutic advances have modestly improved outcomes, prognosis remains poor, highlighting the need to define survival mechanisms that connect tumor-cell fitness with the tumor microenvironment. In this Perspective, we argue that myeloid cell leukemia-1 (MCL-1) should be regarded not simply as an anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family protein, but as a tumor microenvironment-linked survival node in cholangiocarcinoma. Experimental studies have shown that MCL-1 mediates resistance to TRAIL-induced apoptosis, is reinforced by inflammatory IL-6 signaling, and is upregulated by leukemia inhibitory factor through a PI3K/AKT-dependent pathway that protects tumor cells from chemotherapy-induced apoptosis. In parallel, stromal remodeling studies and FGFR-related mitochondrial MCL-1 biology suggest that MCL-1 sits at the interface of desmoplasia, cytokine-driven survival signaling, and mitochondrial fitness. In a commercially available cholangiocarcinoma tissue microarray, MCL-1 positivity was observed in 40 of 54 primary cholangiocarcinomas (74.1%). In unadjusted analysis, MCL-1 expression showed an exploratory association with tumor status (pT, Fisher’s exact P = 0.043), whereas no clear association was observed with sex, age, or nodal status. These findings support the practical plausibility of MCL-1 as a tissue-level indicator of survival-favored cholangiocarcinoma biology. Framing MCL-1 in this way may help reposition therapy toward disruption of tumor-promoting microenvironmental circuitry rather than tumor-cell killing alone.

Introduction

Cholangiocarcinoma remains one of the most treatment-refractory gastrointestinal malignancies. Despite advances in systemic therapy, durable disease control is achieved in only a subset of patients, and long-term survival remains unsatisfactory. This therapeutic limitation reflects not only the molecular heterogeneity of cholangiocarcinoma itself but also its highly desmoplastic and inflammatory tumor microenvironment, which supports tumor-cell survival, treatment resistance, and disease progression (1–4).

Within this setting, myeloid cell leukemia-1 (MCL-1) is a particularly attractive molecule. MCL-1 is a short-lived anti-apoptotic member of the BCL-2 family that blocks mitochondrial apoptosis and promotes cell survival under stress. In cholangiocarcinoma, however, the significance of MCL-1 appears to extend beyond a simple anti-cell death function. Available evidence suggests that MCL-1 integrates inflammatory cytokine signaling, stromal protection, and mitochondrial resilience, thereby functioning as a molecular bridge between tumor-intrinsic survival programs and tumor-extrinsic microenvironmental support (2–4).

In this Perspective, we propose that MCL-1 should be reframed as a tumor microenvironment-linked survival node in cholangiocarcinoma. We further integrate tissue-level findings obtained from a commercially available cholangiocarcinoma tissue microarray showing frequent MCL-1 expression in malignant tissue and enrichment in more advanced pathological status. Together with the existing literature, these findings support a model in which MCL-1 identifies a biologically aggressive and microenvironment-adapted subset of cholangiocarcinoma (2–11; Figure 2).

Recent cholangiocarcinoma primers and tumor microenvironment-focused reviews further support this framing. Current models describe cholangiocarcinoma as a disease sustained by reciprocal interactions among malignant cholangiocytes, fibroblast-rich stroma, inflammatory mediators, and extracellular matrix architecture rather than by tumor-cell intrinsic programs alone (2–4).

MCL-1 is embedded in the basal survival architecture of biliary malignancy

Early pathological studies demonstrated that MCL-1 and Bcl-xL, but not Bcl-2, are expressed across normal, dysplastic, and malignant biliary epithelium (5). This observation remains important because it suggests that biliary epithelial survival is not uniformly dependent on all anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins. Rather, selected molecules, including MCL-1, appear to be embedded in the basal survival logic of the biliary tract (5).

Functional studies further established that MCL-1 is not merely detectable in cholangiocarcinoma but actively mediates resistance to apoptosis. In human cholangiocarcinoma cells, MCL-1 was shown to confer resistance to TRAIL-induced apoptosis by blocking mitochondrial depolarization, Bax activation, cytochrome c release, and downstream caspase activation (6). These findings position MCL-1 upstream of mitochondrial apoptotic blockade and indicate that it is mechanistically active in cholangiocarcinoma survival (6).

More recent translational work has suggested that anti-apoptotic buffering in cholangiocarcinoma is distributed across a broader BCL-2 family network, including both MCL-1 and Bcl-xL (12). This is relevant because it implies that MCL-1 is unlikely to act as an isolated molecule. Instead, it participates in a wider survival program that may help explain the marked therapeutic resistance of this disease (12).

The tumor microenvironment reinforces MCL-1 through inflammatory cytokine signaling

A major reason to view MCL-1 through a tumor microenvironment-oriented lens is that its expression in cholangiocarcinoma is clearly shaped by inflammatory cytokines. IL-6, a key mediator in cholangiocarcinoma biology, upregulates MCL-1 and enhances TRAIL resistance via an Akt-dependent pathway (7). Neutralization of IL-6 signaling reduces Akt phosphorylation, lowers MCL-1 expression, and restores apoptotic sensitivity (7). These findings indicate that MCL-1 is not simply downstream of tumor-cell genetics, but also downstream of inflammatory microenvironmental signaling (7).

This cytokine-dependent survival program may become further stabilized through epigenetic mechanisms. SOCS3 silencing has been shown to sustain IL-6/STAT3 signaling and enhanced MCL-1 expression in cholangiocarcinoma, suggesting that MCL-1 upregulation can become embedded within a more durable survival state rather than reflecting only transient cytokine exposure (8). This point is relevant because it provides a mechanistic explanation for why anti-apoptotic buffering may persist even when the inflammatory context fluctuates (8).

A second important example is leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF). In cholangiocarcinoma, LIF and its receptor are increased in neoplastic cholangiocytes, and LIF is also present in stromal compartments (9). Functionally, LIF protects tumor cells from gemcitabine/cisplatin-induced apoptosis by activating PI3K/AKT and increasing MCL-1 expression (9). This observation is particularly relevant because it directly links a stromal cytokine to chemotherapy resistance through MCL-1. In this sense, MCL-1 functions as a molecular receiver through which the microenvironment converts paracrine signaling into tumor-cell persistence (9).

Desmoplasia, stromal remodeling, and MCL-1-centered survival

Cholangiocarcinoma is among the most desmoplastic epithelial cancers, and its stromal density is not merely structural. Cancer-associated fibroblasts, extracellular matrix components, and cytokine-rich stromal niches actively shape tumor behavior (4, 10). Experimental work using a genetic cholangiocarcinoma model with depletion of cancer-associated fibroblasts showed suppression of tumor outgrowth, reduction of tenascin C-rich extracellular matrix, and improved survival (10). Although those studies did not directly target tumor-cell MCL-1, they are conceptually important because they show that stromal remodeling alone can meaningfully alter tumor progression (10).

This is highly relevant to MCL-1. A fibroblast-rich, cytokine-rich, extracellular matrix-dense environment is precisely the type of niche expected to sustain anti-apoptotic dependence. Thus, MCL-1 may serve not only as a tumor-cell survival factor but also as a downstream readout of stromal support. High MCL-1 expression may therefore identify tumors that are especially adapted to, and protected by, their surrounding microenvironment (4, 7, 9, 10).

MCL-1 in cholangiocarcinoma also reflects mitochondrial fitness

Another reason MCL-1 deserves broader framing is that its role in cholangiocarcinoma extends beyond canonical apoptosis control. FGFR inhibition has been shown in cholangiocarcinoma cell-line and xenograft-related experimental models to induce loss of matrix-localized MCL-1 and necrotic cell death, suggesting that MCL-1 contributes to mitochondrial integrity and cell survival under stress (11). This is conceptually important because desmoplasia, inflammation, and metabolic stress are not independent phenomena in cholangiocarcinoma; they are overlapping pressures within the same tumor ecosystem (11).

If MCL-1 supports mitochondrial fitness in addition to apoptotic resistance, then its biological significance is deeper than that of a conventional biomarker. Rather than merely labeling apoptosis resistance, MCL-1 may mark a broader state of microenvironment-adapted survival competence (11).

Commercial tissue microarray and immunohistochemical evaluation

To provide tissue-level support for the proposed role of MCL-1 in cholangiocarcinoma, we performed immunohistochemical evaluation using a commercially available cholangiocarcinoma tissue microarray (Provitro TM0015, product no. 4012208) and an anti-MCL-1 antibody (Abcam, ab53709). MCL-1 immunoreactivity was evaluated using a semi-quantitative composite score based on staining intensity multiplied by the percentage of positive tumor cells. Cases with a composite score of 2–4 were classified as positive. Using this predefined cutoff, MCL-1 positivity was observed in 40 of 54 primary cholangiocarcinoma cases (74.1%). Weak staining was counted as positive when the composite score reached the cutoff. Scoring was performed independently by two evaluators and was conducted blinded to the clinicopathological annotation supplied with the tissue microarray.

Representative immunohistochemistry demonstrated heterogeneous cytoplasmic expression ranging from weak to strong staining in tumor cells (Figure 1). When metastatic lymph node lesions were available in the array, MCL-1 expression levels were comparable to those of the corresponding primary tumors.

Clinicopathological analysis suggested that MCL-1 expression was enriched in more advanced local disease. pT annotation was available for 50 of the 54 primary cholangiocarcinoma cases, whereas 4 cases lacked evaluable pT information and were excluded from the grouped pT analysis. MCL-1 positivity increased across tumor categories, from 1/3 in T1, 3/9 in T2, and 16/24 in T3 to 12/14 in T4, showing an exploratory association with tumor status in unadjusted contingency table analysis (Fisher’s exact P = 0.043), although this did not remain significant after multiple-testing adjustment (adjusted P = 0.171) (Table 1). By contrast, no clear association was observed with sex, age, or nodal status. Specifically, MCL-1 positivity was present in 17/25 women and 23/29 men, in 11/16 patients younger than 60 years and 29/38 patients aged 60 years or older, and in 10/17 node-negative versus 13/17 node-positive cases. These findings should be regarded as exploratory rather than definitive, but they provide tissue-level support for the idea that MCL-1 identifies a survival-favored subset of cholangiocarcinoma.

Therapeutic implications: from direct inhibition to TME-MCL-1 disruption

The present article does not claim that MCL-1 is already a validated stand-alone therapeutic target in cholangiocarcinoma. Rather, the available evidence suggests that MCL-1 provides a mechanistically useful bridge between tumor-intrinsic survival and tumor-extrinsic protection. In this context, a more credible therapeutic framework may be disruption of the TME-MCL-1 axis rather than MCL-1 inhibition alone (7–12).

Potential intervention points include IL-6- or LIF-associated signaling, stromal remodeling that reduces protective paracrine input, and rational combination strategies that lower the apoptotic threshold more effectively than single-node inhibition (7–10, 12). The reported synergy between Bcl-xL and MCL-1 inhibition in cholangiocarcinoma models supports the view that anti-apoptotic dependence in this disease is distributed across multiple BCL-2 family proteins (12). This suggests that MCL-1 may be most useful not as an isolated target, but as a context-defining biomarker within a broader survival network (12).

Discussion

The central message of this Perspective is that MCL-1 in cholangiocarcinoma should be understood less as an isolated anti-apoptotic protein and more as a tumor microenvironment-linked survival node. This reframing is particularly relevant because cholangiocarcinoma is a paradigmatic desmoplastic and inflammation-associated tumor in which therapeutic resistance is rarely explained by tumor-cell intrinsic programs alone (2–4, 7–11).

The literature supports this interpretation at several levels. Comprehensive reviews of cholangiocarcinoma biology have emphasized that this malignancy is defined not only by genomic heterogeneity but also by dense desmoplasia, complex stromal crosstalk, and an immune-active yet frequently ineffective tumor microenvironment (2–4). Against that backdrop, the older pathology literature showed that MCL-1 is embedded in the basal anti-apoptotic architecture of biliary epithelium (5). Functional studies then demonstrated that MCL-1 actively mediates TRAIL resistance (6), while cytokine studies showed that IL-6 and LIF reinforce MCL-1 expression through signaling pathways that protect cholangiocarcinoma cells from apoptosis (7–9). Stromal studies indicate that fibroblast-rich and extracellular matrix-rich microenvironments materially influence tumor progression (10), and mitochondrial studies suggest that MCL-1 contributes to cell survival beyond apoptosis blockade alone (11). Taken together, these observations justify moving MCL-1 out of the narrow category of an anti-apoptotic marker and into the broader language of tumor microenvironment-enabled survival circuitry (2–11). This broader interpretation also aligns with classic overviews that frame cholangiocarcinoma as a clinically heterogeneous disease with strong stromal conditioning, and with focused discussions of desmoplastic stroma as a therapeutic opportunity rather than a passive histologic bystander (13–15).

An additional reason to place MCL-1 at the center of discussion is that the broader MCL-1 literature increasingly argues against a purely apoptosis-restricted view of this molecule. Reviews focused on MCL-1 regulation and non-canonical biology emphasize that MCL-1 is controlled across multiple levels, including transcription, translation, proteostasis, and mitochondrial localization, and that its biological impact extends beyond conventional anti-apoptotic buffering (16, 17). In this broader framework, MCL-1 can plausibly be interpreted as a molecular integrator of environmental stress, cytokine signaling, and survival adaptation rather than as a static apoptosis marker alone (16, 17).

Our commercial tissue microarray-based findings strengthen this argument by providing a tissue-level correlate. In this analysis, MCL-1 positivity was detected in 40 of 54 primary cholangiocarcinomas (74.1%) using a predefined composite score based on staining intensity and percentage of positive tumor cells. Weak staining was counted as positive when the composite score met the cutoff, and scoring was performed independently by two blinded evaluators.

At the same time, these findings must be interpreted cautiously. The present analysis was based on a commercial tissue array and is tissue-based rather than mechanistically interventional. Thus, the observations do not prove that MCL-1 directly drives progression, nor do they establish prognostic independence. However, the array format also offers a practical strength: it provides standardized, de-identified tissue material with matched clinicopathological annotation, enabling reproducible comparison between malignant tissue categories. In the setting of a Perspective article, that degree of evidence is appropriate; the data do not serve as definitive proof, but they do ground the conceptual model in actual human tissue material.

Another important implication is that MCL-1 evaluation in cholangiocarcinoma should not be limited to a binary positive-versus-negative interpretation. Because MCL-1 sits downstream of inflammatory cytokines, stromal protection, and mitochondrial stress responses, its biological meaning is likely to be context-dependent. A more useful translational framework may be to assess MCL-1 together with markers of pathway activation and stromal biology, such as pAKT, IL-6/LIF-related signaling status, LIF receptor expression, desmoplastic stromal abundance, or fibroblast-rich microenvironmental patterns (4, 7–10). Such an approach would better reflect the concept advanced here: that MCL-1 is not merely a tumor-cell marker, but a node embedded within a multi-compartment survival system.

This issue also intersects with the evolving view of cholangiocarcinoma as a spatially and functionally compartmentalized disease. Recent work on the cholangiocarcinoma microenvironment has emphasized that fibroblast-rich niches, extracellular matrix architecture, and inflammatory cell composition may vary considerably across tumors and even within the same tumor, creating distinct biological territories that shape progression and treatment response (4, 18). In that setting, MCL-1 may be especially relevant as a candidate biomarker of microenvironment-adapted survival rather than simply of tumor-cell intrinsic anti-apoptotic tone.

The limitations of this perspective should also be stated clearly. Much of the available mechanistic literature has been generated in cholangiocarcinoma models rather than across the full spectrum of biliary tract cancers (2, 3). In addition, the present commercial array-based data are exploratory and do not establish survival associations or therapeutic predictiveness. Future work should therefore test whether MCL-1 expression retains biological or clinical value in larger consecutive cohorts, whether it correlates with established stromal or immune phenotypes, and whether it can help identify tumors most likely to benefit from rational combination strategies targeting cytokine signaling, stromal support, or mitochondrial stress adaptation (2–4, 7–12). In particular, whether MCL-1 tracks with desmoplastic burden, fibroblast activation, immune exclusion, or hypoxia-related phenotypes remains an important unanswered question in human cholangiocarcinoma tissues (4, 14, 18).

Taken together, the existing evidence justifies a more integrated view of MCL-1. Rather than treating it solely as a narrow apoptosis-related molecule, it may be more productive to regard MCL-1 as a survival node through which the tumor microenvironment stabilizes malignant persistence. That conceptual shift may help guide future translational work toward identifying which cholangiocarcinoma subsets are most dependent on microenvironmentally sustained anti-apoptotic signaling and how those dependencies might be therapeutically disrupted (4, 7–12). From a translational perspective, a biomarker that reflects both apoptotic buffering and microenvironmental adaptation may be especially useful in selecting patients for combination strategies that simultaneously target tumor-cell survival programs and stromal support mechanisms (10, 12, 14).

Conclusion

MCL-1 in cholangiocarcinoma should no longer be viewed only as a conventional anti-apoptotic protein. The available literature, together with commercial tissue array-based immunohistochemical findings, suggests that it links inflammatory cytokines, stromal protection, desmoplastic architecture, and mitochondrial fitness to treatment resistance and disease progression. In this sense, MCL-1 is best understood as a tumor microenvironment-linked survival node. Framed in this way, MCL-1 may become a useful biomarker for identifying survival-favored cholangiocarcinoma biology and a conceptual entry point for therapeutic strategies aimed at disrupting tumor-promoting microenvironmental circuitry.
