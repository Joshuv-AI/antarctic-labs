ABSTRACT
Characterization of clocks and inertial sensors, such as accelerometers and gyroscopes, typically
includes Allan variance analysis. Allan variance is ubiquitous in timing and navigation
communities which may appear niche compared with generalized spectral analysis. This note
provides some motivation for Allan Variance for audiences more familiar with spectral analysis.

1. INTRODUCTION

Allan variance is an important tool for analyzing non-stationary noise sequences. The initial
motivation dates back to the 1960s and focused on quantifying performance of atomic clocks [1].
Allan variance has has since become the de-facto method for characterizing noise in clocks [2],
accelerometers[3] and gyros[4]. Piecing together a generalized theoretical framework for Allan
variance is challenging for a few reason. First, so-called Allan variance was not presented in
Allan’s seminal publication [1]. Confounding the issue, application-specific jargon is typically
used when interpreting results (c.f. Table 3-1)[2, 3, 4]. The distinction between continuous-time
and discrete-time signals is often blurred. Perhaps more concerning is the finding that
high-resolution acquisition methods can distort the Allan variance[5]. In the broad scope of signal
processing applications, periodograms are arguably more popular than Allan variance. Roughly
60 years since inception, it is reasonable to ask whether Allan variance is still relevant today [6].
Indeed we find Allan variance analysis useful, particularly for estimating low-frequency spectral
content in long sequences. Additionally, reference methods for parameterizing noise simulators
and sensor fusion models begin with Allan variance analysis[7].

2. NOISE COLOR

Color is used in many fields to distinguish noise sources according to trends in their power
spectral densities [8]. Here we briefly define several noise colors, how they are related, and
notionally how to generate them from white noise.

For stochastic signal, 𝑥 𝑘 (𝑡), with power spectral density 𝑋 𝑘 ( 𝑓 ), the color of 𝑥 𝑘 (𝑡) refers to a
power-law relationship
                                           |𝑋 𝑘 ( 𝑓 )| 2 ≈ 𝛼𝑘 𝑓 𝑘 .                                       (1)
for some constant scale factor 𝛼 𝑘 . Specific color names are typically associated with specific1
integers 𝑘. For example, white noise describes 𝑘 = 0, or a flat power spectra. Noise spectra that
increases with 𝑓 quadratically (𝑘 = 2) is referred to as violet noise. Noise spectra that decrease
with 𝑓 quadratically (𝑘 = −2) are referred to as red noise. The color naming convention
intuitively relates the spectral content of the colors themselves. Red and violet are on opposite
ends of the visible light spectrum. Spectral content of red noise is concentrated at low
frequencies, while spectral content of violet noise is concentrated at high frequencies. White light
comprises all light colors with equal intensity which is analogous to white noise comprising equal
spectral power at all frequencies.

Violet noise can be generated by differentiating white noise as illustrated in Figure 2-1a. The
relationship between these noise colors can be easily verified through the differentiation property
of the Fourier transform. Analytically violet noise approaches infinity with frequency, however all
practical systems are arguably band-limited for some 𝑓 < ∞.

Red noise can be generated by integrating a white noise process as illustrated in Figure 2-1b. Red
noise is also referred to as Bownian noise, brown noise, or random walk. Red noise presents some
analytic challenges, such as its singularity at 𝑋R (0). Red noise is not stationary as its variance
increases with 𝑡. While non-stationarity does not preclude computation of the power spectral
density, non-ergodicity complicates low-frequency spectral analysis.

Additionally, blue and pink noise are associated with 𝑘 = 1 and 𝑘 = −1, respectively. In
electronics, pink noise is often referred to as flicker. Generating blue and pink noise from white
noise is relatively challenging. The typical approach involves constructing a filter to achieve the
linear frequency trend over a sufficient bandwidth [9, 10].

3.3. Labeling Noise Terms

For clocks and inertial sensors, power-law noise models are often parameterized using Allan
variance. Unfortunately there are many different labels used to reference the same noise terms as
shown in Table 3-1. For practical examples, see Fig. 3 [7] and [4]. Here we abbreviate Allan
standard deviation (ASD), Allan variance (AVAR), and random walk (RW). Additionally, Allan
variance is sometimes used as a generalized term and the distinction between standard deviation
and variance must be parsed in-context. For example, Allan variance may be illustrated using
plots of 𝜎 and not specifically 𝜎 2 [4].
Highlighting noise terms associated with an accelerometer, consider the accelerometer output is
acceleration. White noise on the output will effect white acceleration noise. If instead, the output
was first modeled as a velocity signal, integration would be required to convert the velocity signal
to an acceleration signal.
Alternatively, consider a velocity signal comprising both white and red noise. Differentiating the
velocity signal increases the power of the noise terms which produces violet and white noise in the
resulting acceleration signal. For this reason, violet noise for accelerometers are often referenced
as white velocity noise. Similarly, white acceleration noise can be considered velocity
random-walk which could be obtained by differentiating a velocity signal with red noise.

5. CONCLUSION

Allan variance is a method of characterizing spectral content of time-domain signals. Computing
two-sample variances mitigates effects of low-frequency content characteristic of non-ergodic
processes. While producing a single two-sample variance measurement would be noisy, averaging
multiple samples reduces uncertainty in the result. In the machine learning community, bootstrap
aggregation (bagging) refers to averaging the result of weak learners trained on subsets of
data[13]. If we consider a two-sample variance a weak-learner, Allan variance could be described
as bagged spectral estimation.
