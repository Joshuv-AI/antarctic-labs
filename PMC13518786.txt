Microbial drought resistance is achieved at the expense of soil carbon loss
Authors: Xuesen Pang, Chengjie Ren, Nianpeng He, Xinyi Zhang, Yanghui He, Zhenghu Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLife history strategies of soil microbiomes may determine their environmental adaptability and influence soil carbon-climate feedbacks. Here, we investigate trade-offs among microbial high yield (Y), resource acquisition (A), and stress tolerance (S) strategies and their consequences for soil carbon mineralization potential along an aridity gradient spanning 9.6 million square kilometers. Y-A-S strategies show nonlinear threshold responses to aridity, where a surge in S- and A-strategies and a sharp decline in Y-strategy occur once aridity exceeds critical levels. The aridity threshold for the Y-strategy occurs after those of S- and A-strategies, as increasing carbon allocation into stress tolerance and resource acquisition comes at the expense of growth. The Y-strategy negatively impacts, while A- and S-strategies positively impact soil carbon mineralization potential. Importantly, aridification intensifies these impacts. Overall, our findings suggest that variations in microbial Y-A-S strategies significantly influence soil carbon cycling and should be considered in microbial models.Subject terms: Microbial ecology, Carbon cycle

Introduction

The capacity of soil to sequester carbon is largely driven by soil microbiomes1,2. In specific, losses of soil organic carbon (SOC) associated with enhanced microbial decomposition could lead to accelerated global warming, while microbial-driven carbon sequestration in soils could potentially help to mitigate climate change3. This balance is mediated by microbial carbon use efficiency, which determines how much carbon is used for microbial growth (anabolic processes) versus respiration (catabolic processes)4. In addition, carbon use efficiency is also a measurement of microbial high yield (Y) strategy, which is suggested to be the consequence of reduced carbon investments in the construction of stress tolerance (S) and resource acquisition (A) strategies5. Therefore, Y-A-S life history strategies of soil microbiomes were suggested to determine microbial response to environmental changes and improve mechanistic understanding of soil carbon-climatic change feedback5,6. To the best of our knowledge, until now, no large-scale study has been conducted elucidating the consequences of the Y-A-S life history strategies on soil carbon turnover.

Climate warming-induced increase in potential evapotranspiration is faster than precipitation, resulting in worldwide aridification7. The responses of ecosystem processes to aridification are not consistently gradual; instead, they occur abruptly8,9. Beyond specific aridity thresholds, abrupt reductions in multiple structural and functional ecosystem attributes were reported8. These aridity thresholds are critical for policymaking because abrupt changes are hardly reversible once they happen, involving the shift of the ecosystem into an alternative stable state10. Recently, increases in aridity have also been found to result in drastic shifts in soil microbial biodiversity9 and alter soil biodiversity-multifunctionality relationships11. Some previous studies suggested that the adjustment of Y-A-S life history strategies regulates microbial adaptation to aridity12,13, while others reported that stress response genes did not vary significantly across the water gradient14, and a recent manipulation experiment found little evidence of Y-A-S trade-offs in response to drought15. Therefore, it remains unclear how aridity influences microbial carbon allocation into Y-A-S life history strategies and whether these strategies exhibit nonlinear responses to aridity at a biogeographical scale.

Importantly, a recent modelling study suggests that microbial communities with high carbon use efficiency (Y-strategy) positively affect SOC storage, highlighting the contribution of microbial residues to SOC formation1,16. In addition, results from a north-south forest belt study further support that high microbial carbon use efficiency can reduce CO2 emissions, even under conditions of high microbial SOC uptake17. Although microbial carbon allocation to A- and S-strategies may also contribute to SOC formation, these processes require additional energy and respiration costs. For example, aridity and its induced alkaline and resource-scarce soil conditions trigger microbial production of oxidases to degrade recalcitrant carbon, while oxidases are more unstable and energetically expensive than hydrolases18. Similarly, accumulating solutes in response to water stress is also energetically expensive19. Therefore, if aridity-induced water stress and carbon limitation shift microbial carbon allocation away from the Y-strategy toward A- and S-strategies, this shift would imply higher metabolic costs associated with increased respiratory demand, enhancing the potential mineralization rate6.

Here, we conduct a large-scale standardized field survey across 112 sites spanning 9.6 million square kilometers (Supplementary Fig. 1). Our study includes a wide aridity gradient, with aridity index (the ratio of precipitation to potential evapotranspiration, AI) ranging from 0.06 to 1.46. In this study, we quantify the Y-strategy using the 18O-water tracer method20, which allows researchers to tackle this important research question with accuracy. Microbial A-strategy is represented by carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus acquiring enzymatic activities per unit microbial biomass21,22, including β−1,4-glucosidase, leucine aminopeptidase, N-acetylglucosaminidase, peroxidase, phosphatase, phosphorylase, polyphenol oxidase, and urease. Microbial S-strategy is measured using metagenomics, indicated by genes associated with biomolecular damage repair, osmolyte production, protection from desiccation, and maintenance of cellular integrity from the Non-supervised Orthologous Groups (eggNOG) database5. In the context of the mounting evidence for aridity thresholds of ecosystem attributes and plant-soil-microbe interactions, we hypothesize that Y-A-S life history strategies also have aridity thresholds. In addition, we conduct a 180-day incubation experiment (at 25 °C with 60% water holding capacity) to quantify the potential mineralization rate of soil carbon. We aim to explore whether microbial Y-A-S life history strategies exhibit nonlinear responses to aridity, to identify the differences in aridity thresholds among these strategies, and to determine the consequences of shifts in Y-A-S life history strategies along the aridity gradient for SOC mineralization potential.

Results and discussion

Microbial adaptation to aridity by shaping Y-A-S strategies

Our large-scale study revealed that variations in microbial Y-A-S life history strategies along aridity gradients were nonlinear (Fig. 1a–c and Supplementary Fig. 2). Importantly, the aridity thresholds identified from the segmented regressions between Y-A-S strategies and aridity were significantly different (Fig. 1d), with the sequential series characterized by abrupt shifts in S-strategy, A-strategy, and Y-strategy at AI values of 0.593 [0.574, 0.611] (mean and 95% confidence intervals based on 200 bootstrap resamples), 0.473 [0.464, 0.482], and 0.436 [0.431, 0.442], respectively. All living things must undergo the agony of balancing between stress tolerance and growth yield in response to water limitation23. In general, water stress tolerance mechanisms are first activated to ensure survival in case water stress is prolonged23. Therefore, the priority of survival of microbial communities explains the first sudden change of S-strategy. Our results suggest that the aridity threshold for the Y-strategy occurs after those of the S- and A-strategies, as increasing carbon allocation into stress tolerance and resource acquisition comes at the expense of growth. We also explored the nonlinear response of microbial growth rate to aridity, and the aridity threshold for microbial growth is 0.513 [0.505, 0.521] (Supplementary Fig. 3). This result suggests that microbial growth remains resilient until a critical aridity is reached, beyond which carbon use efficiency collapses.

In natural terrestrial ecosystems with experimental datasets, we did not find Y-A-S trade-off triangle (Fig. 1e, f, Supplementary Table 1, and Supplementary Fig. 4) as proposed in the original framework by Malik et al.5. Specifically, our study did not find a negative correlation between A- and S-strategies and therefore did not support the proposed trade-off between stress-tolerance traits and investment in resource acquisition5. The permutational multivariate analysis of variance showed that microbial communities in arid and humid regions display distinct Y-A-S strategies (PERMANOVA: R2 = 0.16, P = 0.001, Fig. 1e). In addition, Procrustes test also found a significant difference (Procrustes test: M2 = 0.053, P = 0.333) in the correlations among Y-A-S strategies in arid and humid regions (Fig. 1f). In arid regions, the positive correlation between A-strategy and S-strategy (Fig. 1f; Supplementary Fig. 4c) (AI < 0.5) was expected because aridity results in both carbon limitation and water stress. However, in humid regions, S-strategy is decoupled from both Y-strategy and A-strategy (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 4), partly because carbon investment in S-strategy is lower in humid than that in arid regions, which had little influence on carbon consumption of growth and resource acquisition. Previous assumptions suggested that high microbial carbon use efficiency would increase enzymatic production, leading to higher soil carbon emissions. However, microbial communities were always limited by carbon24, and it is expected that a consistent tradeoff between Y- and A-strategies exists despite the variation of aridity21. Importantly, the negative correlation between Y- and A-strategies in arid regions was stronger than that in humid regions, as evidenced by a steeper slope and higher R2 value (Supplementary Fig. 4). We also found that soil carbon and nutrients consistently display nonlinear responses to aridity (Supplementary Fig. 5). Therefore, the abrupt decreases in soil carbon and nutrients beyond specific aridity thresholds may enhance the trade-off between Y- and A-strategies.

We observed an increase in the Y-strategy as aridity intensified, prior to reaching the aridity threshold. As SOC and nutrient concentrations rise with increasing aridity along the early aridity gradient firstly (Supplementary Fig. 5), the enhanced availability of substrates boosts enzymatic catalytic efficiency through greater saturation of enzyme active sites25. Therefore, the improvements in enzymatic catalytic efficiency reduce the necessity for costly investment in extracellular enzyme production (A-strategy), thereby shifting investment toward microbial growth efficiency (Y-strategy) (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 4a). However, once AI falls below the aridity threshold, water availability becomes a more significant factor in regulating the Y-strategy. An increased investment in S-strategy to maintain intracellular turgor pressure constrains the Y-strategy (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 4b)19. Incorporating microbial mechanisms and metrics into Earth system models represents a major and challenging research direction in the global change community3,26. Current microbial models focus solely on the effects of temperature in microbial carbon use efficiency, while overlooking the role of water availability3. The observed abrupt declines in microbial carbon use efficiency with increasing aridity suggest that these models may introduce substantial uncertainty in projections of soil carbon dynamics in arid regions. Therefore, these results may contribute to the development of microbial models in two ways. First, our large-scale dataset on microbial carbon use efficiency provides important parameters for current microbial models. Second, the influence of water availability on microbial carbon use efficiency should be incorporated into these models alongside temperature27.

Aridification amplifies the impacts of Y-A-S strategies

The random forest analysis suggests that AI, MAT, drought events, precipitation variability, net primary productivity, edaphic factors, and microbial Y-A-S strategies together explain 82% of the variation in soil carbon mineralization potential (measured at 60% water holding capacity and 25 °C; Fig. 2a). Although theoretical and modeling concepts support the regulation of Y-A-S strategies in soil carbon turnover, experimental validation remains limited5,6. We found that Y-strategy negatively impacts, while both A- and S-strategies positively impact soil carbon mineralization potential (Fig. 2c–e and Supplementary Fig. 6), supporting our second hypothesis. The partial least squares path model further demonstrated that the influence of Y-A-S strategies on SOC mineralization potential persisted even after controlling other environmental factors (Fig. 2b). Recent global modelling studies support the critical role of microbial carbon use efficiency in soil carbon storage, attributed to the contribution of microbial necromass carbon to stable mineral-associated carbon1. Here, our comprehensive study suggests that high microbial carbon use efficiency can also increase soil carbon storage by inhibiting its mineralization rate21.

We observed abrupt increases in soil carbon mineralization potential when AI dropped below 0.353 [0.350, 0.356] (Fig. 3), which followed the aridity threshold of Y-strategy. An investigation into catastrophic shifts across multiple ecosystem variables offers the ability to identify attributes that are more sensitive to buffering in response to aridity. This also opens opportunities for early warning indicators and the development of effective adaptation strategies8. Therefore, we suggest that the observed sudden increase in soil carbon mineralization potential resulted from abrupt decreases in Y-strategy. The adjustment of microbial Y-A-S strategies is critical for buffering aridity-induced soil carbon emissions and consequently improving soil carbon sequestration.

Additionally, the correlations between SOC mineralization potential and Y-A-S strategies in arid regions showed steeper slopes and higher R2 values than those in humid regions (Fig. 4a–c). The partial correlation analysis further suggests that the correlation between SOC mineralization potential and Y-A-S strategies in arid regions is stronger than that in humid regions, whether environmental factors are controlled or not (Fig. 4d). Thus, the impact of Y-A-S strategies on soil carbon mineralization potential is more pronounced in arid regions compared to humid regions (Fig. 4a–c). Several mechanisms contribute to this difference. First, correlations among Y-A-S strategies are weaker in humid regions than in arid regions (Supplementary Fig. 4). Together with greater resource availability in humid regions, a slight shift toward A- and S-strategies may therefore exert a weaker influence on SOC mineralization potential6. Second, all Y-A-S strategies and SOC mineralization potential varied more sharply with AI in arid regions than in humid regions (Figs. 1a–c and 3), which may further contribute to the stronger influence of Y-A-S strategies on SOC mineralization potential in arid regions. Third, we observed significantly higher ratios of microbial biomass carbon to SOC in arid regions than in humid regions (Supplementary Fig. 7), consistent with Mao et al.28, which may amplify the effects of microbial life-history strategies on SOC mineralization potential compared with other environmental factors. For example, soil reactive iron and aluminum play critical roles in the mineral protection of SOC against microbial decomposition, yet their concentrations decrease with increasing aridity29,30. Consequently, reduced mineral protection of SOC may increase microbial biomass carbon to SOC ratios and thereby enhance the influence of microbial life-history strategies on SOC mineralization potential in arid regions.

Our findings, based on the analysis of the most comprehensive experimental data on Y-A-S strategies, show that increases in microbial carbon allocation toward resource acquisition and stress tolerance come at the expense of growth efficiency. The responses of Y-A-S strategies to increases in aridity follow a series of sequential thresholds. Aridification-induced abrupt increases in A- and S-strategies cause a sudden decrease in Y-strategy. Importantly, our findings link microbial Y-A-S life history strategies to SOC mineralization potential. We suggest that once aridity exceeds all thresholds of the Y-A-S strategies, the SOC mineralization potential shows a sharp increase, highlighting the cascading effects of aridity-induced shifts in microbial life history strategies on soil carbon cycling at a biogeographical scale.

Soil carbon models are essential tools for scaling up measurements and mechanisms to predict global soil carbon dynamics6. Both Y- (carbon use efficiency) and A-strategies (microbial enzymes) have been represented in microbial models, such as MEND and CORPSE31,32. Our findings highlight the urgent need to incorporate microbial S-strategy into soil carbon models because of its significant influence on soil carbon mineralization potential. Moreover, current microbial models typically represent Y- and A-strategies as constant values or neglect their regulation by environmental factors. By revealing large variations in Y-A-S life history strategies along environmental gradients, our study provides a valuable dataset and empirical basis to improve and update microbial model representations.

Our study also raises several limitations that need further study. First, our conclusions are from a biogeographical observation; how microbial Y-A-S life history strategies respond to drought in different climate and soil conditions needs further validation by manipulation experiments. Second, both bioassays and metagenomic analyses were used to assess microbial Y-A-S life history strategies. However, it is difficult to capture all the processes representing the A- and S-strategies using bioassays alone. Although metagenomic analyses can partially overcome this limitation, ecosystem processes are driven more by active gene expression than by the mere presence of genes33. Therefore, advanced technologies of transcripts, proteins, and metabolites may provide more robust indicators of Y-A-S life history strategies. Third, considering the expensive cost of the measurements, we only measured Y-A-S strategies once, which may also introduce several limitations.

Methods

Sampling

Our large-scale sampling was conducted across China’s natural ecosystems between June and August 2022 (Supplementary Fig. 1). A total of 112 sites were sampled across China, including 49 grassland sites and 63 forest sites. Three plots were established at each site. For grasslands, the plots measured 5 × 5 m and were spaced more than 10 m apart, while plots for forests measured 20 × 20 m and were spaced more than 30 m apart. At each plot, topsoil samples (0–20 cm) were collected at ten random locations after removing the surface litter layer. These ten samples from each plot were then combined to form a composite sample, resulting in a total of 336 soil samples. The fresh soil was immediately sieved using a 2 mm mesh and homogenized in the field. Approximately 1 kg of the sieved and homogenized soil from each plot was stored on ice and transported to the laboratory. Upon arrival at the laboratory, each soil sample was further homogenized before being divided into two subsamples: one was air-dried for physicochemical analyses, while the other was stored at −20 °C for microbial analysis. The coefficient of variation of precipitation over the past 30 years (1993–2022) was used to represent precipitation variability. The frequency of SPEI values below −2 (extreme drought) during the past 30 years (1993–2022) was used to represent drought events.

Soil analysis

Soil texture was measured using a laser diffraction particle size analyzer (Mastersizer 2000, Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). SOC concentration was measured using a Multi N/C 2100 s analyzer after hydrochloric acid treatment to eliminate inorganic carbon. Soil total nitrogen was determined by acid digestion of soil samples with concentrated sulfuric acid in the presence of a catalyst mixture (copper sulfate and potassium sulfate). Total phosphorus was measured by digestion with a mixture of sulfuric acid and perchloric acid. The concentrations of nitrogen and phosphorus in the digested solutions were quantified using an AutoAnalyzer 3 (Bran + Luebbe, Germany).

Microbial high-yield (Y) strategy

Microbial Y-strategy was measured by microbial carbon use efficiency, which was determined using the substrate-independent method based on the incorporation of 18O from water into microbial DNA34. The frozen soil samples (−20 °C) were thawed on ice. The thawed soil was placed in a brown bottle for a 7-day pre-incubation at 25 °C in the dark at 60% water holding capacity to reactivate microbial activity and minimize potential rewetting effects. The bottle was covered with cling film with several holes, which not only allows gas exchange but also slows down water evaporation. After that, duplicate aliquots of 500 mg of each pre-incubated soil were placed into 2 mL brown chromatographic vials. For one replicate, the 18O content of soil water was adjusted to 20.0 at% 18O by the addition of H218O, and another replicate was added with the same volume of non-labeled Millipore ultrapure water. Subsequently, the vials containing soil samples were placed in 20 mL headspace bottles, and three blank bottles without soils were attached to each batch of test samples and set as blanks. Next, CO2-free air was flushed to obtain headspace concentrations of CO2 to ~0 ppm. Afterwards, all 18O labeled and control samples were incubated for 24 h at 25 °C. After that, vials containing labelled and control soils were capped, immediately frozen, and stored at −80 °C until DNA extraction.

Soil DNA was extracted with the FastDNA™ Spin Kit (MP Biomedicals) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. Total soil DNA concentrations were determined using the Picogreen fluorescence assay. Afterwards, the remaining DNA extracts were transferred into silver cups and dried at 45 °C for 5 h for water removal, and the silver capsules were folded and analyzed for the abundance of 18O and total O content using an IRMS-TC/EA (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). A gas-tight 5 mL syringe was used to collect headspace gas samples from each vial. The CO2 concentrations were immediately quantified by gas chromatography (Agilent 7890B, USA), calibrated with certified CO2 standard gases. Microbial respiration rates (ng C g−¹ h−¹) were calculated from the increase in headspace CO2 concentration for each sample and blank using the ideal gas law, soil dry weight, and incubation time35. The microbial biomass carbon was measured using the chloroform fumigation extraction method with a conversion factor of 0.4536.

Microbial resource acquisition (A) strategy

Microbial A-strategy was represented by carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus acquiring enzymatic activities per unit microbial biomass21,22, including β−1,4-glucosidase, leucine aminopeptidase, N-acetylglucosaminidase, peroxidase, phosphatase, phosphorylase, polyphenol oxidase, and urease. The activities of extracellular enzymes were measured using fluorimetric and colorimetric methods. The activities of individual enzymes were first normalized by microbial biomass carbon. The biomass-specific activities were then normalized to a range of [0, 1] based on their maximum and minimum values. The average of the normalized biomass-specific enzymatic activities was finally calculated to represent the A-strategy.

Microbial stress tolerance (S) strategy

Microbial S-strategy was measured by stress tolerance genes from metagenomic sequencing. Soil DNA was extracted from 0.5 g of a fresh soil sample using the FastDNA™ Spin Kit (MP Biomedicals), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality of the DNA extracts was assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. To obtain sufficient DNA for the metagenomic sequencing and to guarantee representation, six replicates were conducted from each soil sample.

The metagenome libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 to generate 150-bp paired-end reads at a greater sequencing depth, resulting in an average of 10 Gb of raw sequence data per sample. The reads aligned to the human genome were removed, and the lengths were trimmed with Sickle. To improve the reliability and quality of subsequent analyses, the sequencing reads were quality-filtered37. Adapter sequences were removed, reads were trimmed, and reads shorter than 50 bp or containing ambiguous bases (N) were discarded. To use only high-quality reads, the MEGAHIT software38 was used to assemble each sample individually. The unmapped reads from the single-sample assemblies were then pooled and co-assembled to obtain additional contigs. MetaGeneMark was used to predict the genes in the contigs (longer than 200 bp), and the per-base coverage depth across all contigs was calculated by mapping raw reads from each sample.

According to the Evolutionary Genealogy of Genes: Non-supervised Orthologous Groups (eggNOG) database, we obtained the abundance of genes associated with microbial stress tolerance, including biomolecular damage repair, osmolyte production, protection from desiccation, and maintenance of cellular integrity (Supplementary Data 1)5. The total abundance of stress tolerance genes was calculated by summing the read counts mapped to genes assigned to the S-strategy for each sample. The summed values were then normalized to a range of [0, 1] using min-max scaling to represent the S-strategy.

The min-max scaling method was used to convert Y-A-S strategies to a common scale within the range [0, 1], without altering their relationships with the aridity index and mineralization potential. However, the normalized data may not adequately capture the truly ecological meaning. For example, a normalized value of 1 for the Y-strategy corresponds to the maximum observed CUE within the dataset rather than a CUE of 1.

Soil carbon mineralization potential

A 180-day incubation experiment was used to measure the SOC mineralization potential. In specific, twenty grams of frozen soil was placed in a 250 mL brown jar for a 7-day pre-incubation at 25 °C in the dark with 60% water holding capacity. After 7 days of pre-incubation, we measured the CO2 emission rate on days 1, 3, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 50, 70, 90, 120, 150, and 180. The CO2 concentration was quantified using gas chromatography (Agilent 7890B, USA), and the CO2 emission rate was calculated using the ideal gas law, soil dry weight, and incubation time and expressed as ng C g−1 h−1. During incubation, all jars were capped with lids containing cling film with several holes, which allowed gas exchange while minimizing moisture loss from the soil. We adjusted the water-holding capacity during pre-incubation and incubation by weighing every three days. After that, the one-pool model39 was used to quantify the SOC mineralization potential by the data-assimilation approach (Supplementary Text 1).

Statistical analysis

To determine the best model for representing the relationships between the aridity index and microbial Y-A-S strategies, soil carbon mineralization potential, SOC, total nitrogen, total phosphorus, and microbial growth rate, several candidate models were fitted, including linear, quadratic, generalized additive, logarithmic, exponential, and segmented regression models. Model selection was based primarily on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), which penalizes model complexity to reduce overfitting and is particularly suitable for large-sample analyses40. Segmented regression provided the best fit for all variables (lowest BIC) except the S-strategy (Supplementary Table 2). For the latter, although the logarithmic model had slightly lower BIC, segmented regression was retained to maintain a consistent analytical framework across variables. This choice was further supported by the similar explanatory power of the segmented regression model (R² = 0.50) and the logarithmic model (R2 = 0.50; Supplementary Table 2). To assess the robustness of the identified aridity thresholds, 200 bootstrap resamples were performed to generate 95% confidence intervals for breakpoint locations and to characterize the variability of regression slopes before and after each breakpoint. The mean breakpoint across bootstrap resamples was subsequently used as a fixed threshold in the final segmented regression models.

Principal component analysis (PCA) (Z-score transformed; using the R package vegan) was used to explore the trade-offs among the Y-A-S strategies in arid regions (AI < 0.5) versus humid regions (AI ≥ 0.5). The cutoff value of AI = 0.5 was adopted to distinguish arid from humid regions for two reasons. First, according to the classification of the United Nations Environment Programme, regions with AI < 0.5 are categorized as arid and semi-arid, whereas regions with AI ≥ 0.5 are classified as dry sub-humid to humid41. Second, abrupt shifts in microbial Y-A-S strategies were observed along the aridity gradient at an AI value close to 0.5, with a mean estimated breakpoint of 0.501 (Fig. 1a–c). PERMANOVA was used to test the differences of microbial communities in arid and humid regions (using the R package vegan). Procrustes analysis was used to assess the concordance of correlations among Y-A-S strategies between arid and humid regions (using the R package vegan). The variance inflation factors were computed to check for multicollinearity among all predictors (Supplementary Table 3). The random forest analysis (using the ranger package) was used to identify the relative importance of predictors in governing soil carbon mineralization potential, considering the MAT, AI, drought events, precipitation variability, net primary productivity, clay plus silt content, and microbial Y-A-S life history strategies. In the random forest analysis, the dataset was partitioned into a training set (70%, out-of-bag (OOB)) and an independent test set (30%). A grid search with tenfold cross-validation was used to optimize model hyperparameters on the training data. Based on the root mean square error (RMSE) and OOB error, the final model was configured with mtry = 5, min.node.size = 1, and 1000 trees (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 4). Partial dependence plots were used to assess the effects of Y-, A-, and S-strategies while controlling for other variables. The partial least squares path model was used to explore the direct and indirect effects of climate (mean annual temperature and aridity index), climatic variability (drought events, precipitation variability), plant carbon input (net primary productivity), edaphic factors (clay plus silt content), and microbial Y-A-S strategies on SOC mineralization potential (using the R package plspm). Partial correlation was used to explore the effect of microbial life history on soil carbon mineralization potential after controlling for MAT, AI, drought events, precipitation variability, net primary productivity, and clay plus silt content (using the R package ppcor). All the statistical analyses were conducted using R 4.5.142.
