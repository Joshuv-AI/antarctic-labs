1.0 Summary
 Atmospheric processes over the Southern Ocean have a profound influence on regional and global
 climate. This is a part of the world where global climate models perform particularly poorly, with models
 persistently overpredicting the amount of sunlight reaching the Earth's surface (Regayre et al. 2020). A
 major challenge when trying to improve the representation of atmospheric processes in this part of the
 globe is the scarcity of observations capable of constraining our understanding. During 2024/25 a U.S.
 Department of Energy Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) User Facility field campaign
 (CAPE-k) deployed a suite of instrumentation at the kennaook/Cape Grim atmospheric monitoring station
 in Tasmania, Australia to provide detailed cloud-aerosol observations in this region in order to address the
 greatest source of uncertainty in climate models – atmospheric aerosols and how they influence cloud
 formation (Carslaw et al. 2013). In support of the ARM observations, an Australian Research Council
 (ARC)-funded project “Southern Ocean aerosols: sources, sinks and impact on cloud properties,” led by
 the Queensland University of Technology, complemented the ARM measurements with a suite of
 chemical measurements at the kennaook/Cape Grim site in northwestern Tasmania to provide more
 information on the processes controlling aerosol formation and growth. The deployment of the University
 of York LIF-SO2 instrument (Temple et al. 2025) in the ARM mobile facility container was part of this
 chemistry-focused deployment.

 Increased observational efforts are critical for understanding sources and sinks of Southern Ocean
 aerosols, in particular the role of marine micro-organisms (phytoplankton, algae) on aerosols formation,
 their properties and growth, cloud droplet formation, and the conversion of cloud droplets to ice crystals
 and precipitation. The observational focus of the ARC-funded project is aerosol chemical composition
 and their gaseous precursors. The remote nature of the Southern Ocean poses a significant analytical
 challenge when studying gaseous precursors, as low concentrations are often below the limits of detection
 of commercial instrumentation. Historically this has meant that observations have had to be
 time-averaged over days to weeks to achieve the limits of detection required. Although useful when
 considering overall average levels of aerosol precursors, this time-averaging obscures temporal variability
 that can provide insight into the controlling processes. Over recent years advances in analytical
 techniques have made high-time-resolution measurements of key gas-phase aerosol precursors achievable
 with sufficiently low limits of detection.

 Despite mixing ratios in the low parts per trillion (pptv) range, sulphur dioxide (SO2) plays a central role
 in the formation of cloud condensation nuclei in pristine marine environments. To date, real-time
 measurements of SO2 in pristine locations have been limited by the lack of instrumentation capable of
 measuring these low levels. Previous measurements in these environments have required long sampling
 periods (>7 days) to achieve the required detection limits, making process-level studies difficult. The
 University of York’s recently developed LIF-SO2 instrument uses laser-induced fluorescence to make
 highly sensitive measurements of SO2, with single-pptv precision on minute timescales. This instrument
 is orders of magnitude more sensitive than existing commercial SO2 detectors, and is the ideal tool for
 investigating SO2 and its impact on cloud formation in locations such as the Southern Ocean. The York
 LIF-SO2 was deployed in the ARM container between late November 2024 and early March 2025 to
 provide the first high-time-resolution measurements of SO2 in the Southern Ocean.

 This project represented only the third deployment of the York LIF-SO2 instrument, and the first where it
 was operated remotely for a period of time. Although issues did arise, the collection of approximately

12 weeks of data in this challenging environment was deemed a great success. A wide range of air masses
were sampled, providing exceptional insight into the dynamics of SO2 at this location, from tens of pptv
mixing ratios in remote ocean air to > 3 parts per billion (ppbv) when sampling air that had passed over
the Port Latta iron ore refinery facility. Due primarily to the issues described in the following paragraph,
limits of detection were not as low as hoped (approximately 20 ppt for 1-minute time averaging), but still
sufficient for the majority of the deployment to resolve ambient SO2 concentrations with time-averaging.
It is expected that 10-minute-averaged data will be optimal for limits of detection and ability to resolve
SO2 temporal dynamics.

The operation of the LIF-SO2 in this project posed several major challenges. The first of these were
logistical issues that meant the delivery of the instrument calibration cylinders were significantly delayed,
pushing back our deployment date by several months, and then the instrument itself got stuck in
Australian customs for several weeks. The duration of the deployment and the distance from the United
Kingdom were also challenges, meaning remote operation was required. Although this in itself was not an
issue, the fact that the instrument is entirely custom built and has only been operated on two short field
deployments previously, where it had at least one onsite operator at all times, meant diagnosing and
mitigating new issues took longer than if York scientists had been on site. This was an excellent learning
process for the York team, and significant thanks and credit go to the onsite ARM scientists, whose
excellent communication and support enabled issues to be resolved in as timely a manner as possible.

The most significant technical issue that impacted data coverage was that the laser wavelength continually
lost the SO2 spectral feature of interest. This issue had not been seen before, and was caused by the
LIF-SO2 being located within the ARM container directly below the air conditioning (AC) unit. The rapid
temperature changes observed during the AC cycle caused the laser wavelength to change faster than the
software could control, resulting in long periods of lost data early on in the deployment, especially during
periods of warm weather. A combination of physical insulation of the LIF-SO2 and software
improvements meant the impact of this problem waned as the deployment progressed.

Another issue was a gradual reduction in laser power over time. This is expected behavior for this
instrument, due to the hydroscopic nature of certain optical components, but the duration of this
deployment meant that after the first month of operation the laser power had become insufficient and a
York scientist had to visit the site during the first week of January 2025 to replace the degraded
components. Due to the delayed start date of the deployment, the end date for LIF-SO2 operation was
extended by a month to maximize data coverage, but the unit had to be returned to the United Kingdom in
mid-March due to other field commitments. A sustained period of instrument downtime was also
experienced for the month of February due to issues related to the inlet flow being unintentionally
blocked, but this was eventually resolved, enabling a final week of data collection before the instrument
had to be returned to Britain.

Overall, this deployment was deemed a great success and the data generated represents the first
high-time-resolution observations of SO2 in remote Southern Ocean air masses. The range of air mass
origins sampled will enable natural and anthropogenic SO2 sources to be characterized, and the diurnal
structure in the SO2 data will provide a unique constraint on our understanding of the gas-phase processes
that lead to its production and subsequent oxidation in this location.

2.0 Results
 The developmental nature of the LIF-SO2 instrument means the production of final data is not yet
 complete, as several complex issues still need to be accounted for in the data processing, so the data
 presented here is preliminary. These data have had all relevant calibrations and zeros applied, but further
 corrections need to be made to deal with specific issues experienced during the project. Work is also
 ongoing to ensure any inlet artifacts are accounted for correctly. Figure 1 shows a preliminary data time
 series for LIF-SO2 measurements made during this project, with periods of data impacted by known
 issues that are yet to be corrected for highlighted. The time series shows the data coverage achieved and
 also highlights the wide range of SO2 concentrations observed. This ranged from sub-10 ppt up to over
 3 ppb, such as the large spike on January 21st that back trajectory analysis indicates is most likely due to
 air that has passed over the Port Latta iron ore processing plant, as shown in Figure 2. This range of
 concentrations observed provides an excellent data set for exploring the contributors to SO2 at this
 location.

 Figure 1. Time series of preliminary 1-minute time-averaged SO2 mixing ratios measured by the
 University of York LIF-SO2 (blue). Colored vertical bars indicate data periods that require
 further data corrections due to the specific instrument issues indicated in the plot legend.

Figure 2. Windrose of preliminary SO2 mixing ratios, showing highest values from the direction of the
 Port Latta plant (ESE). Lowest mixing ratios observed are from the clean marine sector (SE

A major aim of our project is the study of SO2 dynamics in remote oceanic air masses, as these are poorly
represented in current models and observations capable of constraining our understanding are scarce.
Figure 3 shows the average diurnal concentration profile for a three days of “clean air” sampling in
January, showing a clear diurnal trend peaking in the afternoon consistent with photochemical production
from marine biogenic emissions of compounds such as dimethyl sulfide and methanethiol. Future work
will investigate this in more detail using supporting data of other sulphur-containing species.

 Figure 3. Average diurnal SO2 mixing ratio for a “clean marine” sampling period.

The data generated during this project will be used in multiple strands of analysis, and it is hoped will
result in several publications. The University of York team plan to use the SO2 data to investigate the
temporal variability observed and explore the sources of SO2 in this location, with the aim of a
publication presenting this unique data set and placing it in the context of previous work. The data will
also be shared with project partners, where it will be used in conjunction with the other chemical data

 4

 collected as part of the “Southern Ocean aerosols: sources, sinks and impact on cloud properties” study to
 provide insights into the chemical production of SO2 in the remote Southern Ocean. The final data will
 also be made publicly available, in the hope of encouraging further collaborations stemming from this
 work.
