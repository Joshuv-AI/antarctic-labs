 1.0 INTRODUCTION
 The U.S. Department of Energy’s Office of Nuclear Energy (DOE-NE) is responsible for advancing
 nuclear science and technology to meet the nation’s energy, environmental, and economic needs. Within
 this structure, NE-4 is the nuclear fuel cycle innovation and research arm, focusing on early-stage and
 crosscutting research, development, and demonstration (RD&D) that underpins future nuclear systems. Its
 mission is to develop the scientific foundations, tools, and technologies needed to enable safer, more
 efficient, and more sustainable nuclear energy, while reducing technical risks before large-scale
 deployment. The Material Recovery and Waste Form Development (MRWFD) campaign is a DOE-NE
 program focused on advancing technologies that enable a more sustainable and efficient nuclear fuel
 cycle. Its central mission is to develop advanced fuel recycle technologies that improve resource
 utilization, reduce the burden on geologic repositories, limit proliferation risks, and enhance overall
 economics.

 To achieve this, MRWFD works on recovering valuable materials from used nuclear fuel (UNF),
 including uranium, transuranics (TRU), and fission products, and enabling their reuse. The program is
 expanding beyond traditional uranium recovery to include more comprehensive recycling approaches that
 maximize material recovery while minimizing waste streams. It also addresses challenges across a wide
 range of fuel types, including those from advanced reactors, while supporting common technology needs
 and workforce development. The program is closely engaged with commercial developers to ensure
 program research addresses broad technology needs applicable to recycling in the United States.

 Overall, MRWFD serves as a critical enabler of advanced nuclear energy systems by providing the
 technologies and scientific understanding needed to recycle fuel and safely manage process waste. In
 doing so, it reduces key technical and economic barriers to closing the fuel cycle and supports the
 long-term viability of nuclear energy.

 The MRWFD campaign’s core mission is to develop advanced fuel recycle technologies to improve
 resource utilization, reduce repository burden, limit proliferation risk, and improve economics of the
 nuclear fuel cycle. The campaign also emphasizes integrated, crosscutting innovation. Its RD&D spans
 separations technologies, head-end processing, off-gas capture, and durable waste form development,
 bringing together expertise in radiochemistry, materials science, and systems analysis. By leveraging
 national laboratory capabilities and supporting university partnerships, it contributes to both technology
 development and workforce growth. Overall, MRWFD provides the scientific and engineering foundation
 needed to safely recycle nuclear materials and manage process waste, helping enable a more sustainable
 and resilient nuclear energy future.

 The MRWFD campaign has been at the leading edge in developing several significant breakthrough
 technologies over the last decade. These breakthrough technologies include:

 • The Co-Decontamination (CoDCon) process is a variant of the PUREX technology that is designed to
 generate a combined uranium and plutonium product. The process offers enhanced proliferation
 resistance since no pure plutonium is ever separated.

 • The Actinide Lanthanide Separation Process (ALSEP) is an aqueous process designed to separate
 minor actinides (americium and curium) from lanthanide and other fission products in a single step.
 Before ALSEP, the approach involved two processes using two different extractants because of the
 chemical similarity between the minor actinides and lanthanide fission products.

 1

 • The campaign has been at the international forefront of developing the electrochemical process for
 recycling UNF. Notable breakthroughs have been development of a solid cathode for recovering
 uranium-transuranium metals and an electroreduction technology to facilitate processing oxide UNF.

 • The campaign has sponsored the development of specific waste forms for a variety of process wastes
 arising from the recycle of UNF. For example, ceramic and glass-ceramic waste forms can
 accommodate higher concentrations of fission products and minor actinides compared to the
 established borosilicate glass waste form.

 • Solid media have been developed to absorb noble gases (krypton and xenon) from the headend off-
 gases. This technology has proved a viable alternative to the established but complex and energy-
 intensive baseline of cryogenic distillation.

 • The ZIRCEX technology uses chlorine gas to react with the zirconium cladding and subsequently
 dissolve it to separate it from UNF for either recycle or disposal. The campaign has identified less
 hazardous reactants and is developing the technology to operate at a lower technology.

 This strategic plan is intended to direct the technology maturation undertaken by the MRWFD campaign.
 The MRWFD campaign resides in the DOE-NE Office of Materials and Chemical Technologies (DOE
 NE-43). After placing the campaign into technical and political context, this plan describes the
 campaign’s mission and strategic objectives, followed by a strategy for approaching technology
 maturation and the priority areas for each of the campaign’s technical programs. Organizations and
 programs directly relevant to the campaign are described, followed by the campaign’s organization and its
 risks and opportunities.

 2.0 BACKGROUND TO USED NUCLEAR FUEL MATERIAL RECOVERY
 AND WASTE FORM DEVELOPMENT

 2.1 The Nuclear Fuel Cycle
 In the U.S., nuclear power plants generate about 67 GW, or 20% of commercial electric power and almost
 half of the nation’s low-carbon electricity generation. Moreover, nuclear energy is projected to expand in
 the U.S., with a goal of tripling current levels by 2050, adding ~200 GW of new nuclear power generation
 capacity through the construction of new reactors across the country; this includes plans to add 35 GW of
 new capacity by 2035 and maintain a sustained pace of 15 GW per year by 2040. However, nuclear power
 plants also produce highly radioactive UNF that results from the nuclear fission process. UNF must be
 isolated from the environment for thousands of years to protect human health and the environment.

 The Nuclear Waste Policy Act (P.L. 97-425), as amended, designated Yucca Mountain, NV, as the only
 candidate site for a national deep geologic repository for the disposal of UNF and other high-level nuclear
 waste. However, political and legal opposition to the project has indefinitely delayed the licensing,
 construction, and operation of the proposed Yucca Mountain repository. As a result, about 91,000 metric
 tons of UNF is stored at nuclear plant sites around the United States and grows by about 2,000 MT each
 year awaiting disposal.

 Direct disposal of UNF, as currently planned in the United States consistent with the Nuclear Waste
 Policy Act, is called the open (or “once through”) nuclear fuel cycle. However, interest in alternative
 nuclear fuel cycles in the U.S. has renewed among some policymakers and stakeholders primarily because
 of their potential to reduce the volume and toxicity of nuclear waste.

 Alternative “closed” nuclear fuel cycles look to recover fissile material from the UNF for recycle into
 nuclear reactors. About 95% of UNF consists of uranium from the fresh fuel that was originally loaded
 into the nuclear reactor and 1% consists of fissile plutonium produced from irradiating uranium. The
 remaining 4% of UNF consists of highly radioactive fragments of uranium and plutonium (fission
 products) that must be dispositioned and other actinides (primarily neptunium, americium, and curium).

 Fissile material can be recovered from UNF by a variety of technologies of varying levels of maturity
 demonstrated at laboratory and industrial scales. Fissile material recovery is known as “reprocessing.”
 Recovered material, such as uranium, can then be fabricated into fuel for recycle into reactors as
 illustrated in Figure 1. The U.S. developed technology for the reprocessing of UNF during World War II
 and during the Cold War operated some of the largest reprocessing facilities in the world, primarily for
 nuclear weapons material production. Commercial reprocessing activities in the United States ended
 during the 1970s and early 1980s because of rising costs and policy concerns about the potential
 worldwide growth of stockpiles of weapons-usable plutonium.

 Various degrees of closed fuel cycles can be envisioned and are summarized in Table 1. In principle,
 recycling all UNF actinides either to thermal or fast spectrum reactors could eliminate the need for
 permanent disposal except for fission products, which would be immobilized in glass or other material
 acceptable to the repository. A fully closed fuel cycle could be expanded to recover selected fission
 products for either short-term storage so those with short half-lives will decay to stable isotopes or
 alternative applications.

 4

 Partially closed cycles can also be envisioned. The least partial fuel cycle would include recovery of only
 uranium and its fabrication into nuclear fuel for recycle to thermal or fast reactors. At the next level,
 plutonium and uranium would be recovered together (to minimize proliferation risk) for recycle to either
 thermal or fast reactors. There are two groups of material recovery technologies that stand out for their
 relatively high maturity and these are described in the following sections.

 2.2 Aqueous-Based Reprocessing of UNF
 Aqueous-based technologies were developed as part of the Manhattan Project and subsequently the Cold
 War. The bismuth phosphate precipitation technology was deployed at Hanford for the Manhattan Project
 to separate plutonium for weapons purposes. Later, during the early Cold War, a solvent extraction
 technology called REDuction-OXidation (REDOX) marked a significant development in aqueous-based
 separations in improving selectivity for plutonium. Plutonium Uranium Extraction (PUREX), a solvent
 extraction technology, was developed and deployed in the late 1950s at Hanford and Savannah River to
 separate uranium and plutonium. The PUREX technology forms the basis for all subsequent commercial
 reprocessing plants in Russia, the United Kingdom, France, and Japan.

 The PUREX technology is based on the selective extractant tri-butyl phosphate, but variations on the
 flowsheet exist primarily on how the plutonium REDOX state is manipulated with consequences for the
 quantity of high-level waste produced. The amount of high-level waste has been continuously reduced
 with each evolution of aqueous-based technologies and development in waste management practice as
 illustrated in Figure 2. Note that the technologies are represented by their plant names. In particular, the
 PUREX plant at Hanford operated with evolutions of the PUREX technology. The PUREX technology
 reached its final deployed evolution in the Thermal Oxide Reprocessing Plant (THORP) at Sellafield,
 United Kingdom, La Hague, France, and Rokkasho Mura, Japan. These industrial plants have high
 capacity, processing up to 5 metric tons of UNF per day.

 Figure 3 shows a schematic of the process operations involved in aqueous-based reprocessing of UNF.
 The UNF is first typically treated by simple disassembly and chopping of fuel elements. Alternative
 treatment technologies might include voloxidation or chemical decladding. The treated UNF is then
 dissolved, typically in hot nitric acid with volatile fission products captured from the off-gas. Dissolution
 is followed by separation and purification of the desired UNF constituents before conversion into a
 powder form suitable for fuel fabrication. Waste products from separations and purification and off-gas
 treatment are immobilized for disposal or decay storage.

 2.3 Electrochemical-Based Reprocessing of UNF
 DOE has been advancing electrochemical processing of UNF since the 1980s, originally as an essential
 component of the Integral Fast Reactor concept at Idaho and Argonne national laboratories. The Integral
 Fast Reactor program was terminated in 1994, but the metallic UNF from the program’s sodium-cooled
 fast reactor, Experimental Breeder Reactor-II (EBR-II), required treatment to produce waste forms
 suitable for geologic disposal. Electrochemical processing of EBR-II’s UNF continues to produce ceramic
 and metallic waste forms suitable for disposal while also recovering uranium. In addition, flowsheets
 based on the electrochemical technology have been developed and tested to recover TRU actinides that
 supports a closed fuel cycle. The electrochemical technology is arguably preferred for reprocessing
 metallic UNF over aqueous-based technologies because it generates a metallic product that can be directly
 processed into fuel for recycle. That said, oxide UNF can also be reprocessed by electrochemical means if
 it is first electrochemically reduced to metallic form.

 Electrochemical actinide separations are accomplished in an electrorefiner but, like aqueous-based
 separations, other unit operations are needed to prepare the UNF and to process the separated product.
 Figure 4 illustrates a simplified flow diagram of the typical electrochemical process.

 UNF assemblies are disassembled and the pins are mechanically chopped into segments. The pin
 segments are loaded into a basket from the electrorefiner. Volatile fission products are released into the
 inerted cell atmosphere and captured in the atmosphere control system. Electroreduction of oxide UNF
 occurs at this stage if applicable. Electrorefining is an electrochemical process in which an impure metal
 is purified and is conducted in a molten salt that contains approximately 3-8 wt% uranium trichloride at
 elevated temperature (e.g., 500°C) in a lithium/potassium chloride (LiCl-KCl) eutectic melt. The metal
 waste form from the electrorefiner contains the noble metal fission products and cladding and is
 processed for geologic disposal. A fraction of the electrorefiner’s molten salt is treated to separate fission
 products and then recycled. The salt can also be periodically processed for disposal as a ceramic waste
 form by occluding it into zeolite, mixed with glass powder and heated to create a sodalite waste form.
 Product actinides separated in the electrorefiner are processed to prepare them for fabrication into fuel.

 Development of the electrochemical approach has not progressed beyond engineering scale, with most
 work performed in the U.S. and, to a lesser extent, South Korea.

 2.4 Fluoride Volatility-based Reprocessing of UNF
 The less mature fluoride volatility method is based on the direct fluorination of powdered used fuel with
 fluorine gas (F2) or other strong fluorinating agents such as nitrogen trifluoride (NF3) in a flame
 fluorination reactor or a fluidized bed, where volatile fluoride species, mostly uranium hexafluoride
 (UF6), are separated from the non-volatile species, including trivalent minor actinides and the majority of
 the fission products. The volatilization of Np and Pu depends on the operating conditions, high flame
 temperatures, and overstoichiometric fluorine increases their release. Lower flame temperature and sub-
 stochiometric fluorine minimize it. Other volatile species include noble metal fission products (Tc, Mo,
 Ru, Rh, Nb, etc.), Se and Te, noble gases (Ar, Kr, Xe), and, if present, C, H, Br, I. Although considerable
 work was performed in the 1950s, development of the fluoride volatility approach has not progressed
 beyond laboratory scale in the U.S. this century. Internationally, a small engineering-scale demonstration
 was accomplished in the Czech Republic in the early 2000s.

 3.0 THE MRWFD MISSION AND STRATEGIC OBJECTIVES

 3.1 The MRWFD Mission
 Nuclear energy is one of the most resilient, environmentally sustainable, and reliable energy sources on
 the grid today. DOE-NE works to advance nuclear energy science and technology to meet the nation's
 energy, environmental, and economic needs. As an applied energy research and development (R&D)
 organization, DOE-NE enables innovation, supports unique research infrastructure, and solves
 crosscutting challenges facing the nuclear energy sector to:

 • Enable continued operation of existing U.S. nuclear reactors.
 • Enable deployment of advanced nuclear reactors.
 • Develop advanced nuclear fuel cycles.
 • Maintain U.S. leadership in nuclear energy technology.
 • Enable a high-performing organization.

 Despite its prominent role, nuclear energy’s use presents challenges, such as mounting stockpiles of UNF
 and high-level waste and a potential for proliferation. Long-term resolution of these challenges entails
 developing sustainable systems that reduce waste while improving resource utilization and safety. To
 achieve its goals and objectives and to provide policymakers with tools to make informed decisions,
 DOE-NE 4, Office of Nuclear Fuel Cycle has established four R&D campaigns:

 • Systems Analysis and Integration is developing systematic, transparent, and objective processes to
 screen and evaluate a wide variety of proposed fuel cycles to identify potential solutions.
 • Advanced Fuels supports both existing and next-generation reactors by developing accident-tolerant
 light water reactor (LWR) fuel and advanced proliferation-resistant fuels for sustainable fuel cycles.
 • Used Fuel Disposition.
 • Material Protection, Control, and Accountability Technologies supports security and safeguards
 by developing tools and techniques to prevent the misuse of nuclear material.
 • Material Recovery and Waste Form Development is developing innovative processes to recover
 uranium and other materials from UNF while minimizing proliferation risk, reducing losses, and
 minimizing waste. This campaign also seeks transformational breakthroughs in waste forms suitable
 for geologic repository environments.

 The MRWFD campaign advances recycling technologies to establish a robust nuclear fuel supply chain
 and reduce waste consistent with the DOE-NE Strategic Plan Fiscal Years 2026 to 2030. The mission of
 the MRWFD campaign is to mature technologies applicable to recycling constituents of UNF with four
 strategic goals in mind:

 • Optimize use of fissile resources for power generation.
 • Minimize capacity requirements for geological and near-surface repositories.
 • Minimize proliferation risk.
 • Reduction of environmental emissions of radioactive and hazardous materials.

 9

 The campaign’s four strategic goals are not endpoints in themselves but continuously evolve as new
 information and technology comes to light. Therefore, this strategy is not exclusively directed toward an
 ultimate physical endpoint but on the level of maturity expected from its technology development
 activities.

 DOE has issued guidance for technology maturation and metrics to indicate the maturity of technologies
 applicable to its mission. This guidance is promulgated in DOE Guide 413.3-4A, Technology Readiness
 Assessment Guide (DOE 2015). The metric specific to technology maturity is the Technology Readiness
 Level (TRL), which ranges from 1 (least mature) to 9 (most mature). The word “readiness” in the metric
 indicates the purpose of technology maturation (or R&D) to ultimately be the application of the
 technology in an industrial production facility. Given that the purpose of technology maturation is for
 application, there is an alignment of TRLs and the Critical Decision (CD) milestones outlined in DOE
 Order 413.3B, Program and Project Management for the Acquisition of Capital Assets (DOE 2023).

 TRL 4 is the recommended maturity level to support CD-1, Analysis of Alternatives, and has been judged
 appropriate for initial U.S. commercial consideration. This level of maturity is further supported by the
 synonymy of “alternatives” with “options” in the FCT program’s goal of conducting science-based,
 engineering-driven research to fully evaluate and characterize the selected sustainable fuel cycle options.
 Therefore, the MRWFD campaign targets TRL-4 for its technology maturation activities. In this way, the
 campaign is ready to support, with technical options, the federal or commercial capital acquisition of
 UNF recycle facilities. Certain maturation activities applicable at higher TRLs than 4 may be warranted
 and directed by the campaign leadership. Generally, though, higher TRLs can only be completely
 achieved with an active facility design and construction and commissioning project. Broadly, TRL-4
 consists of validating the technology at laboratory or bench scale (typically no larger than a tenth of full-
 scale) with a broad range of simulants and smaller range of actual UNF if considered beneficial.

 3.2 MRWFD Strategic Objectives
 The following sections expand on the elements of the MRWFD mission outlined in Section 3.1 as
 strategic objectives.

 3.2.1 Optimize Utilization of Fissile Resources for Power Generation
 As the dominant source of nuclear fuel, uranium is mined in many countries and internationally traded as
 a commodity. There is concern that if the global use of nuclear power grows, the supply of uranium for
 fuel could tighten and lead to challenges securing fuel at economically viable prices in an
 environmentally sustainable manner. New reactor designs and fuel cycle options may help to alleviate
 concerns about fuel resources and waste management by enabling the extraction of more energy from
 UNF. Doing this would reduce the amount of fuel required to maintain the reactor fleet while leaving less
 used fuel to be managed. This strategic objective aims to develop technologies that facilitate the
 extraction of residual uranium and potentially other fissile actinides from UNF.

 3.2.2 Minimize Required Geological and Near-Surface Repository Capacity
 The capacity of geologic repositories is limited by the allowable packing density of UNF to manage decay
 heat. Therefore, this strategic objective looks to develop technologies that effectively and efficiently
 separate actinides and fission products with high decay heat (e.g., americium-241 and cesium-137,
 respectively) for alternate management. Examples of alternate management include transmutation of

 10

 long-lived actinides in a fast-spectrum reactor or decay storage of short-lived fission products. This
 strategic objective is exemplified by the work of Wigeland et al. (2006), who projected the improvement
 in repository (drift) loading resulting from separation of high heat-emitting TRU and fission products.
 Part of their work is illustrated in Figure 5, which shows the potential increase in drift loading resulting
 from increasing separation of cesium-137 and strontium-90. That said, the concentration of radionuclides
 in all wastes, whether destined for geologic or near-surface disposal, should be maximized within the
 acceptance constraints of the disposal facility. The aim of this objective is to develop technologies for
 extracting high-heat actinides and fission products from UNF and stable waste forms maximizing
 radionuclide loading.

 3.2.3 Minimize Proliferation Risk
 Commercial PUREX flowsheets implemented to date at industrial scale have separated pure plutonium

 4.0 MRWFD TECHNOLOGY MATURATION STRATEGY
 The technology maturation process adopted by the MRWFD campaign follows DOE Guide 413.3-4A
 (DOE 2015) and the principles outlined by Dixon et al. (2019). The technology maturation process uses a
 scale of TRLs that are linked to the federal critical decision milestones outlined in DOE Order 413.3B.
 This link ensures that technologies are sufficiently matured to enable informed policy and capital project
 decisions and minimize project risk. As stated in the MRWFD campaign mission, the campaign targets
 TRL-4 to support CD-1, Analysis of Alternatives. The definition and description of each TRL is provided
 in DOE G 413.3-4A and is repeated in Table 3. Therefore, R&D plans should aim to advance
 technologies to succeeding readiness levels up to TRL-4 within the MRWFD campaign. A technology
 may regress in the TRL scale as new applications for a technology are identified (e.g., resulting from new
 fuel cycles or end uses). Regression should not be considered “bad,” but simply facilitates management of
 the technology’s maturation for the new or modified application.

 4.1 Technology Scaling Strategy
 Engineering judgment should be employed to determine the laboratory or bench scale to use for each
 subject technology but is typically smaller than one-tenth scale at TRL-4. Nonetheless, the scale and
 configuration of equipment tested or used should be sufficient to represent the full-scale features under
 investigation.

 Laboratory or bench scale is interpreted as less than one-tenth the scale of the plant (typically fitting on a
 laboratory bench, fumehood, or glovebox), although application-specific engineering judgment should be
 applied. Laboratory-scale systems are used for process development with the means to closely control
 conditions such that responses can be accurately correlated to changes in those conditions. Typically, the
 laboratory-scale tests use standard off-the-shelf laboratory equipment except where needed for the
 specific technology. Results from laboratory-scale tests should lead to a process block flow diagram or
 low-fidelity process flow diagram. On the other hand, engineering-scale tests are mainly used to test the
 feasibility and stability (“robustness”) of a process, especially from an operations perspective.
 Engineering-scale equipment is typically a smaller version of full-scale such that testing enables
 finalization of design.

 The term “technology components” has, intentionally, a very broad meaning. For an instrument or other
 piece of hardware, the components are reasonably understood using the common understanding of the
 term. For a flowsheet technology, components might be the parts of the flowsheet serving a specific
 purpose. For example, a solvent extraction flowsheet based on a new organic extractant could have
 components related to extraction, scrubbing, stripping, and even solvent washing. Put another way, the
 components in this case refer to the unit operations described in a block flow diagram of the flowsheet
 concept.

 4.2 Technology Feed Material Strategy
 The chemical and physical characteristics of simulants should be representative of those expected from
 any pre-processing of actual UNF to prepare it for treatment with the subject technology (e.g., acid
 dissolution). Further, test simulants should cover the range of chemical and physical characteristics
 expected of actual UNF arising from fuel cycles applicable to the subject technology. Campaign staff are
 encouraged to apply ASTM Method C1750-21, Standard Guide for Development, Verification,
 Validation, and Documentation of Simulants for Hazardous Materials and Process Streams, in
 developing simulants.

 ASTM Method C1750-21 provides general considerations for the development, verification, validation,
 and documentation of performance-based simulants for high-level waste sludge. However, the
 underpinning concepts are equally applicable to UNF. As described by that standard, the first step should
 be to determine the purpose of the simulant in testing for the specific performance metric of the subject
 technology. The key processing properties required for development of the simulant can then be
 identified. Simulant requirements relative to those properties are then specified, and simulant design and

 16

 preparation methodology follow. Finalization of the simulant design is achieved once verification and
 validation confirms that the simulant meets the requirements throughout the testing duration.

 For EM applications, the range of simulants corresponds to the range of chemical and physical properties
 of the waste to be processed. Interpretation of the term is relatively straightforward given the waste likely
 arises from a variety of sources. For UNF, one metric resulting in a range of chemical properties will be
 burnup. Other considerations could be the type of UNF (e.g., accident-tolerant fuels) and initial
 enrichment. Additionally, the range of physical and chemical properties of the feed to the subject
 technology arising from a preceding operation should be considered. For example, the range of simulants
 for an advanced UNF dissolution technology could be representative of those arising from voloxidation.

 Consistent with the TRL-4 definition, results of tests with simulant should be verified in as many cases as
 practical with actual UNF consistent with material availability, safety, ALARA (as low as reasonably
 achievable), cost, and project risk criteria. Actual UNF must be pre-processed to simulate the anticipated
 full-scale application before it is used to test the subject technology. Therefore, tests with actual UNF
 must be planned with MRWFD campaign management so that other projects can leverage the activity.

 4.3 Use of Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning
 The MRWFD campaign is actively preparing for greater use of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine
 learning (ML) in its activities. For example, campaign reports beginning in 2012 are consolidated into a
 searchable and centralized location (a SharePoint file server) that can be interrogated by AI tools. The
 campaign is also assessing whether it should copy reports and underlying data to a high-performance
 computer to facilitate development of AI agents specific to its technical area. As well as reports, the
 campaign is also planning for its researchers to provide data sets either as appendices to reports or
 standalone files (e.g. spreadsheets) that could be analyzed by future AI/ML tools.

 5.1 Other DOE NE-4 Programs

 5.1.1 EBR-II Driver Fuel Processing
 EBR-II driver fuel treatment is an electrometallurgical process (pyroprocessing) that converts sodium-
 bonded metallic fuel from EBR-II into stable waste forms and usable uranium. Located at Idaho National
 Laboratory (INL), this method treats hazardous sodium-bonded fuel by separating uranium from fission
 products using high-temperature molten salt baths, facilitating final waste disposal and producing high-
 assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU) for advanced reactors. The processing information acquired
 through EBR-II driver fuel treatment is valuable to MRWFD in a variety of ways, notably including
 fission product behaviors during engineering-scale operations.

 5.1.2 Materials Protection Accounting and Control Technologies Campaign
 The MPACT mission is to support the U.S. nuclear fuel cycle front and back-end technology developers
 to effectively and economically address domestic safeguards requirements at facilities regulated by the
 Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) by:

 • Developing innovative technologies, analysis tools, and advanced integration methods for nuclear
 material control and accounting (MC&A).
 • Engaging with government and industry stakeholders early in the technology development process to
 enable a cost-effective implementation of safeguards and security by design.
 • Supporting the development of advanced reactors via MC&A support for enabling processes such as
 HALEU and advanced fuel fabrication, storage, and transportation.
 • Working with the U.S. national laboratories, U.S. nuclear industry, and U.S. universities through
 directly funded R&D programs.
 MPACT is important to the MRWFD campaign because nuclear material recovered from UNF requires
 control and accounting. Ideally, recovery processes should be matured with MC&A in mind.

 5.1.3 DOE NE-42 Systems Analysis and Integration Campaign

 common fuel cycle goals. This campaign uses information from MRWFD to develop fuel cycles for
 evaluation. Results from the evaluations then help MRWFD to prioritize maturation activities and define

 5.1.4 DOE NE-42 Advanced Fuels Campaign
 The AFC is responsible for leading R&D activities related to nuclear fuels and materials development.

 The AFC is important to the MRWFD campaign because actinides recovered from UNF would be

 5.2.2 DOE NE-8 Spent Fuel and High Level Waste Disposition
 In FY2027, DOE’s Office of SF&HLWD will combine the former Office of Disposal R&D and Office of
 Collaboration Based Siting into the Office of Siting and Disposal. These offices are developing an
 Integrated Waste Management system for storage, transportation, and disposal of spent nuclear fuel
 (SNF) and high-level radioactive waste.

 The Office of SF&HLWD and the MRWFD campaign both represent the back-ends of alternative nuclear
 fuel cycles. The former has historically been responsible for implementing current U.S. policy for SNF

 6.0 ORGANIZATIONAL PRINCIPLES

 6.1 Organizational Breakdown
 While there are likely many ways in which the MRWFD campaign could be organized, an organization
 based on technology type and maturity is logical given its mission to develop technologies to support a
 potential CD-1. As already described, there are two broad groups of technologies characterized by the
 approach taken to separating the desired constituents of UNF: aqueous based and electrochemical based.
 For example, “aqueous separations” includes aqueous dissolution and other pretreatment of UNF,
 precipitation and conditioning of separated material, as well as the separations themselves. Special
 technical initiatives might also warrant their own organizational element. For example, the fourth
 strategic objective highlights where technology advancements are needed to separate radionuclides that
 historically have been discharged into the environment.

 Finally, an organizational breakdown based on technology maturity is warranted. The transition from
 TRL-3 to TRL-4 marks the significant step from conducting fundamental research to prove scientific
 feasibility to applied research that develops the technology to demonstrate engineering feasibility. The
 experimental and theoretical approaches undertaken at TRL-2 and TRL-3 can be significantly different
 from those implemented at TRL-4.

 The CAMs then report to the national technical director (NTD), who manages the campaign and reports

 7.0 MRWFD TECHNICAL PRIORITY AREAS
 The MRWFD campaign’s work scope is guided by a 5-year strategy organized around the technical areas
 with priority areas that collectively advance UNF recycling from current operational needs through
 longer-range commercial deployment. The strategy is designed to balance near-term priorities – such as
 resolving immediate technical challenges in existing systems and sustaining current operations – with
 longer-term investments in process optimization, technology innovation, and cross-cutting infrastructure.
 Together, the priority areas ensure that the campaign maintains technical leadership in aqueous,
 electrochemical, and fluoride volatility reprocessing technologies. By pursuing incremental improvements
 to proven processes alongside higher-risk, higher-reward innovations, the strategy targets the United
 States to establish a technically mature and commercially viable domestic fuel recycling capability over
 the coming decade.

 7.1 Advanced and Innovative Aqueous Processes
 The advanced aqueous technical area is developing an advanced voloxidation and direct extraction
 integrated process which has promise as a simplified aqueous approach with improved management of
 challenging volatile fission products and improved proliferation resistance. The direct extraction process
 dissolves and purifies the majority of the uranium while leaving a fraction behind with the transuranium
 and fission product elements in solid form. The campaign is scaling and demonstrating this approach with
 representative UNF. Additionally, the campaign is reinvigorating the fluoride volatility approach to
 recycling UNF, which has received little attention since the 1950s. This approach has advantages in
 recycling high burnup UNF given it is a dry process.

 7.1.1 Priority Area 1 – Demonstrate Advanced Voloxidation
 Advanced voloxidation is the first step in processing after used fuel is declad, which enables its
 dissolution directly into an organic solvent. Voloxidation is the conversion of irradiated UO 2 fuel to a
 higher oxide of uranium via oxidation at elevated temperature. To enable direct dissolution, the irradiated
 UO2 is reacted with NO2 to yield a fine UO3 powder. The basic technology has previously been
 demonstrated, and the current effort is the production of a UO3 powder containing TRU and fission
 product oxides and with physical properties to consistently meet the direct dissolution targets. Voxidation
 also evolves volatile fission products, targeting iodine, which is difficult to recover via aqueous
 processing. The program will define the processing conditions and scale up the process equipment to meet
 product recoveries at a scale with eventual translation to industrial equipment. The product must be
 suitable for direct dissolution, and the equipment must be designed to accommodate remote operations,
 including the loading, unloading, and transfers of material between unit operations.

 7.1.2 Priority Area 2 – Mature Uranium Recovery in the Direct Extraction
 Process
 The initial strike of the direct dissolution process dissolves voloxidized irradiated UO3 powder in a
 nitrate-loaded solvent consisting of the extractant DEHiBA [N,N-di(2-ethylhexyl)-2-methylpropanamide]
 dissolved in dodecane or kerosene. The goal of the process is to consistently dissolve 95% of the uranium
 in the irradiated UO3 powder into the solvent, leaving the remaining 5% of uranium in a solid phase that
 also contains all remaining actinides and fission products.

 32

 The dissolution process generates three immiscible phases: uranium-loaded solvent; water released upon
 extraction of uranium; and the undissolved U, TRU, and fission products as mixed oxides and nitrates,
 though some nitrate will dissolve in the water phase. The equipment must allow for rapid dissolution to
 minimize radiation effects on the solvent, as well as complete separation and recovery of the three
 separate relatively clean phases. The organic phase will be stripped of U by solvent de-extraction;
 therefore, additives to the solvent or the process conditions must not impact stripping. The program will
 define the operating conditions that consistently achieve the dissolution targets and develop prototype
 equipment that enables eventual scale-up to industrial equipment. Key processing requirements include
 controlled addition of fuel powder, rapid dissolution, efficient phase separation, and material transfers that
 can be done remotely at scale.

 7.1.3 Priority Area 3 – Mature U/TRU Recovery in the Direct Extraction Process
 The second strike of the direct dissolution process dissolves the uranium, plutonium, and possibly
 neptunium that were not dissolved in the first strike in a different organic solvent using an extractant
 selective for U and Pu, and possibly Np, such as a nitrate-loaded solvent consisting of the extractant
 MOPPA [mono-(1,1,3,3-tetramethylbutyl)phenyl phosphoric acid] dissolved in dodecane or kerosene.
 The goal of the process is to consistently dissolve all of the residual U and Pu remaining in the
 undissolved solids in the solvent, leaving the fission products and minor actinides as solid nitrates and
 oxides. The dissolution process generates three immiscible phases: U- and Pu-loaded solvent, water
 released upon extraction of U and Pu, and the undissolved TRU and fission products as mixed oxides and
 nitrates. It is likely that some nitrate will dissolve in the water phase. As with the uranium extraction, the
 process equipment must allow for rapid dissolution to minimize radiation effects on the solvent, and
 efficient separation and the recovery of the three separate relatively clean phases. The collected organic
 phase will be stripped of U, Pu, and Np by solvent de-extraction. The program will define the processing
 conditions that consistently achieve the dissolution targets and design the prototype equipment that
 enables eventual scale-up to industrial equipment. Operations include controlled addition of feed powder,
 rapid dissolution, efficient phase separation, and material transfers. The solvent must be readily stripped
 of uranium and recycled, so additives, such as hold-back agents, and processing conditions must not
 impact dissolution or stripping.

 7.1.4 Priority Area 4 – Np and Tc Selective Ligands for Direct Extraction
 In the first strike of the direct dissolution process, Tc is coextracted with the uranium into the solvent
 phase and stripped from the solvent together with the U. Np is also extracted if it is present as Np(VI), so
 it must be reduced to Np(IV) or Np(V), which are not extracted by DEHiBA; otherwise, it must be
 selectively stripped from the solvent after dissolution. Separation of the Tc from U requires an additional
 ion exchange step. In the second strike of the direct dissolution process, both Np and Tc will dissolve to
 an extent.

 In either process, the extraction of these species may be mitigated by the addition of complexants or hold-
 back agents that selectively bind to Np and/or Tc, preventing their extraction by DEHiBA or MOPPA. In
 stripping, these complexants may enable selective stripping of Tc and Np from the primary U or U/Np
 products, if desirable. The campaign is identifying candidate sulfonamide ligands with desired attributes
 for complexes with either Tc or Np and developing synthesis schemes for these ligands. The identified
 ligands will then be tested to verify that they display the desired selectivity for Tc or Np in these solvent
 systems. Finally, they will be tested with process feeds to verify the observed selectivity under process

 33

 conditions to determine if there are any detrimental impacts to the dissolution or stripping processes
 where they may be used.

 7.1.5 Priority Area 5 – Direct Extraction Modeling
 Process models are critical to the efficient design of process flowsheets for testing and implementation
 with process equipment. Computer codes for predicting distribution coefficients for solvent extraction and
 for designing detailed liquid-liquid extraction separations, and dissolution of UNF in nitric acid are
 established. However, codes that model the direct dissolution and extraction of UNF into an organic
 solvent do not yet exist, nor does a model for voloxidation.

 The program plan is to adapt existing models and data for used fuel dissolution and solvent extraction,
 such as the AMUSE code, to the direct dissolution process as the chemistries are related. However, as a
 three-phase process, the new code will be developed specifically for direct extraction. By developing an
 extensible platform, additional processes can be integrated with the dissolution processes, including
 voloxidation, ion exchange, and off-gas capture. The final code will enable a complete mass balance for
 the facility as implemented.

 7.1.6 Priority Area 6 – Fluoride Volatility
 Fluoride volatility is used to separate different volatile fluoride species according to relative differences in
 volatilities and reactivities with surfaces. The process first separates volatile components of used fuel that
 has been fluorinated from non-volatile components. In used fuel, UF6, NpF6, PuF6, and several transition
 metal fission product fluorides are volatile at low pressures and temperatures sufficiently above ambient.
 Because NpF6 and PuF6 are less volatile and more reactive than UF6, they can be captured on a solid
 adsorbent composed of UF4 or UF5. Several of the volatile transition metal fission product fluorides are
 more volatile than UF6 and are not captured on the adsorbent.

 The programmatic plan is to identify conditions under which UF6 remains bound by the UF5 adsorbent
 while fission product fluorides are not captured, and conditions under which UF6 can be effectively
 separated from NpF6 and PuF6 by selective adsorption of the latter two species on UF5. The program will
 identify the appropriate conditions that achieve these separations at small lab-scale, and will then design
 equipment to enable scale-up of the process as required to recover reactor-grade UF6 from used fuel.

 7.2 Electrochemical Processes and Innovative Salt Systems
 Electrochemical processing is a high-temperature, non-aqueous technology for recycling UNF, primarily
 through electrorefining in a molten salt bath. The molten salt provides benefits with an absence of neutron
 moderators and immunity to radiation and thermal damage. The campaign is bridging technical gaps to
 deployment and optimizing key process steps. Although ideal for metallic fuels, the campaign is
 expanding the processes with head-end and process treatment steps to accommodate diverse advanced
 reactor fuels. Key technology developments include a new U/TRU co-deposition process providing
 improved separation factors from challenging fission products, advances in predictive modeling, recovery
 of high value materials from UNF, and technologies to reduce waste. Near term ambitions for
 electrochemical processing will be to assess off-normal conditions to derisk commercial deployment.

 34

 7.2.1 Priority Area 1 – Bridging Technical Gaps
 This area addresses immediate and near-term operational challenges. This includes managing the liquidus
 temperature of the Mk-IV electrorefiner salt as sodium and fission products accumulate, developing
 sacrificial electrodes to protect salt systems from moisture and oxygen ingression, optimizing
 voloxidation for finer particle sizes to improve separations efficiency, establishing salt cleanup and
 recycle capability to extend salt life and reduce waste, clarifying the iodine mass balance in
 electrochemical systems, improving corrosion control through better understanding of chloride and
 fluoride potentials, and developing experimental approaches to characterize off-normal and accident
 conditions.

 7.2.2 Priority Area 2 – Maintaining Leadership Through Process Optimization
 Improving the performance and understanding of existing electrochemical processes is the focus of this
 area. Key activities include developing optimized salt-metal separation systems (bottom pour, distillation,
 centrifugation), building working models of electrochemical test systems, demonstrating cadmium-free
 solid U/TRU co-deposition in LiCl-KCl, developing mitigations for oxygen electrode fouling in oxide
 reduction, establishing predictive modeling of uranium dendrite morphology, and developing an
 “electrochemical processing toolkit” for monitoring and process control in molten salt systems.

 7.2.3 Priority Area 3 – Championing New Electrochemical Technologies
 This area pursues longer-range innovations to expand the range of fuel types and processing approaches
 available. This includes evaluating innovative oxide reduction and electrorefining systems, evaluating
 LiCl-only electrochemical processes, establishing the feasibility of oxide dissolution as a replacement for
 oxide reduction and electrorefining, addressing R&D gaps for MSR fuel cycles through liquid-liquid
 extraction and fluoride salt technologies, establishing competencies in fluoride-based electrochemical
 reprocessing including fluoride distillation processes, and exploring innovative approaches for UAlx,
 TRISO, and other non-LWR fuel types.

 7.2.4 Priority Area 4 – Cultivating Cross-Cutting Technologies
 This priority area provides the sustained infrastructure and collaborative framework needed to support the
 other three goals on an ongoing basis. This includes proactive stewardship of the nation's electrochemical
 testbeds, pursuing high-value engineering-scale technology demonstrations useful to industry,
 determining licensing needs for commercial electrochemical processing facilities to inform R&D
 priorities, coordinating with notice of funding opportunity (NOFO) awardees to avoid duplication,
 continuing small high-risk/high-reward innovation proposals, maintaining active industry collaboration,
 and integrating with other DOE-NE campaigns to identify cross-cutting opportunities.

 7.3 Off-Gas and Waste Forms
 The Off-Gas and Waste Forms Technical area focuses on increasing the technical readiness of key
 solutions to achieve U.S. regulatory needs, such as EPA 40CFR190 regulations for iodine, and waste
 forms which can successfully encapsulate salt wastes. Notably, the campaign is leading international
 research in copper-based sorbents for iodine, potentially offering a much less expensive, non-regulated
 under the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act, and ten-fold increase in iodine loading solution
 versus the current silver-based state of the art sorbent. The campaign is also developing a glass waste

 35

 form capable to directly dissolve and encapsulate salts with a simple melting and mixing process,
 potentially dramatically simplifying salt waste disposal.

 7.3.1 Priority Area 1 – Establishing Options for Salt Waste Forms
 Long-term solutions are being studied for treatment and immobilization of salt wastes generated from
 electrochemical processing of EBR-II fuel for disposal. The two general pathways are being explored:
 (a) full salt immobilization options and (b) partitioned salt options.

 The baseline process for full-salt immobilization is the glass-bonded sodalite ceramic waste form (GBS-
 CWF) process, but other options are being considered, including a single-step vitrification using a glass
 frit. For the GBS-CWF process, work is ongoing to dramatically simplify the waste form production
 baseline approach by eliminating powder preparation steps (i.e., using larger-sized reagents and salt),
 eliminating the salt occlusion step, and reducing processing temperatures and times. Additionally, scale-
 up of this simplified approach is planned for fiscal year 2026.

 For partitioning salt, the most promising pathway is halide removal (dehalogenation), considering that the
 halogen content limits solubility in all known candidate waste form systems. The current work for
 dehalogenation evaluation is ongoing with organic acid reagents, and the properties of the resulting
 borosilicate glass products after mixing with glass-forming additives and performing high-temperature
 vitrification are being studied, including chemical durability, phase distribution, and microstructural
 analyses. The waste form products resulting from organic acid dehalogenation have the potential to
 increase salt cation loading in the final waste form by more than 10× compared to that of the GBS-CWF.

 7.3.2 Priority Area 2 – Developing Options for Iodine and Noble Gas
 Management
 Off-gas scrubbing is needed for aqueous and electrochemical reprocessing and during operation of MSRs
 to meet regulatory and environmental release requirements for these facilities. Three of the primary off-
 gas elements/radionuclides of concern in all these processes are 85Kr, Xe, and 129I.

 The baseline sorbent system for radioiodine is silver mordenite (reduced IONEX Ag-900, AgZ), but
 additional technologies and materials are being explored that include solid sorbents with higher
 decontamination factors and higher overall loading capacities. Testing and evaluation of new materials
 includes static and dynamic iodine testing on (a) sorbents with higher Ag-content than Ag-900 (9-13
 mass% Ag) [e.g., Ag-faujasite (26 mass% Ag), Ag-chabazite (18 mass% Ag), Clariant AC6120 (12
 mass% Ag)], (b) sorbents without silver due to its complications with disposal and high associated costs
 (e.g., Bi-based sorbents, Cu-based sorbents), and (c) liquid scrubbers (e.g., caustic).

 Additional options are being explored to improve sorbent performances by increasing bed lifetimes and
 bed capacities by including NOx removal upstream of the iodine solid sorbent bed since NOx exposure
 degrades all chemisorption-based active sorbents.

 36

 For noble gas (85Kr, Xe) capture, the baseline capture process is cryogenic distillation, but solid sorbents
 with higher operating temperatures (target: near room temperature) are being explored. For solid sorbent
 development, ongoing efforts include materials exploration, materials production, engineered form
 production of promising candidate materials, and extensive testing on these materials. The goals of the
 experimental testing are to compare breakthrough times, loading capacities, selectivities, regeneration,
 specific surface area, as well as other properties.

 7.4 Chemical Decladding
 Chemical decladding is a group of technologies aimed at removing zirconium or aluminum cladding from
 UNF. Chemically decladding zirconium cladding could facilitate its recycle for fabricating fuel hardware.
 Chemically decladding aluminum has benefits for waste management because established processes
 typically dissolve the aluminum and fuel together such that the aluminum contributes to the HLW
 volume. The primary approach under development uses a chlorinating liquid to effect decladding through
 the formation of dissolved ZrCl4 or AlCl3 at low temperature and atmospheric pressure. Another process
 using molten lithium to dissolve aluminum and is potentially compatible with processing aluminum-clad
 fuel at INL. RD&D is progressing to simulated UNF before demonstration with representative actual
 UNF.

 7.4.1 Priority Area 1 – Establish Sulfur Monochloride (S2Cl2) Process
 Technologies
 Foundational S₂Cl₂ process technologies needed to support both aluminum- and zirconium-based systems
 will be developed. All activities will be conducted to identify and solve the practical problems associated
 with advancing S2Cl2 processes to engineering scale. This includes identifying compatible materials of
 construction, producing surrogate fuel plates to support testing, and creating processes for solvent
 regeneration and UCl₃ recovery. The effort also emphasizes developing and implementing on-line
 monitoring to track solvent condition, understand the effects of radiation on chlorination behavior, and
 identify reliable pumps and seals, and integrating these elements into functional bench- and engineering-
 scale systems. Together, these activities provide the underlying scaffold for a viable, scalable chlorination
 process with the necessary materials, controls, and performance data to support further development.

 7.4.2 Priority Area 2 – Demonstrate S2Cl2 for Decladding Aluminum Fuel
 A viable pathway for reprocessing of aluminum-clad fuels, such as those from the Advanced Test Reactor
 (ATR) located at INL, will be developed and demonstrated. This includes developing a reliable method to
 extract AlCl₃ from the solvent and recovery of uranium from the fuel in a form compatible with further
 purification for reuse. An integrated half-scale process capable of recovering uranium fuel particles from
 depleted UAlx-bearing fuel plates will be established to develop and optimize operating parameters. In
 addition, the objective includes advancing this capability to a hot-cell environment by demonstrating the
 integrated system with half-length irradiated fuel plates. Together, these efforts aim to validate the
 technical feasibility and scalability of the S₂Cl₂ process for aluminum decladding under both surrogate
 and radiological conditions.

 7.4.3 Priority Area 3 – Demonstrate S2Cl2 for Decladding Zirconium Fuel
 Priority Area 3 focuses on a low-temperature approach for decladding zirconium fuels using S₂Cl₂. This
 includes developing a fundamental understanding of and a reliable method for extracting ZrCl₄ from the

 37

 solvent, as well as establishing an integrated half-scale process to recover uranium from zirconium-clad
 fuel materials. The objective also advances to hot-cell demonstration by enabling testing of the integrated
 system with half-length irradiated fuel plates. Collectively, these efforts aim to validate the effectiveness
 and scalability of the S₂Cl₂ process for zirconium decladding under both surrogate and radiological
 conditions.

 7.4.4 Priority Area 4 – Demonstrate UAlx Processes Compatible with the Fuel
 Cycle Facility
 UAlx processing technologies that are compatible with the Fuel Cycle Facility will be developed to
 provide a viable pathway to ATR fuel reprocessing and ensure continued efficient use of available hot-
 cell space. Several promising technologies have been identified and are at various stages of development.
 Activities include developing and demonstrating lithium-based aluminum decladding with associated
 lithium recovery, evaluating the feasibility of uranium electrorefining from solid or molten aluminum,
 and investigating gallium-based decladding processes including aluminum dissolution, uranium recovery,
 and gallium recycle. In parallel, viable technologies will be compared and assessed based on Fuel Cycle
 Facility compatibility, implementation cost, and processing timelines for ATR fuel. Any new
 technologies will have to compare favorably to the best of these to be considered further. The most
 promising approaches will be demonstrated at bench scale using irradiated fuel to support down-selection
 and future deployment.

 8.0 MRWFD CAMPAIGN RISKS AND OPPORTUNITIES

 8.1 Funding and Budget Risks
 The most immediate risk to the MRWFD campaign is funding instability. The campaign requires
 sustained, multi-year investment to advance technologies through successive scales of demonstration.
 Interruptions in funding can disrupt long-running experimental campaigns, result in loss of specialized
 staff, and delay milestone completion in ways that are difficult and costly to recover from. Changes in
 Congressional appropriations, shifts in DOE-NE budget priorities, or competition for resources from
 other campaigns could compress or redirect the planned work scope.

 • Maintain a clearly prioritized, tiered work scope that identifies which activities can absorb budget
 reductions with minimum programmatic impact and which can be deferred, enabling rapid and
 coherent responses to budget changes.
 • Maintain a portfolio of activities spanning short-term operational priorities and longer-range
 technology development, ensuring that some near-term, high-visibility deliverables are always in
 progress to demonstrate value and sustain sponsor confidence even in constrained budget
 environments.
 • Coordinate with other DOE-NE campaigns to identify shared priorities and cost-sharing opportunities
 that can sustain critical activities even when individual campaign budgets are constrained.

 8.2 Facility and Infrastructure Risks
 The MRWFD campaign depends heavily on several unique but aging national laboratory facilities.
 Equipment failures, facility maintenance outages, safety-driven operational restrictions, or loss of facility
 authorization could significantly disrupt experimental programs.

 • Advocate for equipment maintenance and inspection programs for all critical electrochemical testbed
 infrastructure, with particular attention to high-risk components.
 • Develop contingency experimental plans that identify alternative facilities or scaled-down
 experimental approaches that can be substituted if a primary testbed becomes unavailable, minimizing
 the impact of facility outages on milestone completion.
 • Invest in design improvements and materials selection for new and replacement equipment to extend
 operational lifespans.
 • Proactively invest in testbed stewardship as a continual cross-cutting activity, ensuring that facility
 condition assessments, safety basis updates, and infrastructure investments are addressed before they
 become critical path issues.

 39

 8.3 Workforce and Talent Risks
 The MRWFD campaign requires a highly specialized technical workforce with broad expertise. This
 expertise is concentrated in a relatively small number of individuals at the national laboratories who carry
 institutional knowledge accumulated over decades of fundamental and applied research activity.
 Retirements, attrition, or difficulty recruiting and retaining qualified personnel, particularly given
 competition from the growing commercial nuclear sector, could create capability gaps that are difficult to
 fill quickly. The university talent pipeline supported through NEUP awards and partially mitigates this
 risk but cannot fully replace experienced practitioners in the near term.

 • Actively support the university talent pipeline through NEUP awards, graduate student internships,
 and postdoctoral appointments to ensure a steady flow of early-career researchers into the campaign.
 • Implement structured knowledge capture practices to reduce dependence on tacit knowledge held by
 individual experts and facilitate onboarding of new personnel.
 • Develop formal mentoring and succession planning processes that pair early-career researchers with
 experienced practitioners in critical technical areas before retirements or departures create gaps.
 • Coordinate across the labs to share specialized expertise, reducing the risk that the departure of a
 single individual creates a critical capability gap at either institution.

 8.4 Regulatory and Licensing Risks
 The near-term technical area strategies assume that regulatory engagement with the NRC progresses in a
 manner that allows licensing-relevant data to be generated in a timely and targeted way. However, the
 absence of well-exercised regulatory framework for commercial reprocessing facilities in the U.S.
 represents a significant source of uncertainty. If NRC develops new or unexpected data requirements, if
 the regulatory pathway for reprocessing proves more complex than anticipated, or if safeguards and
 nonproliferation concerns lead to additional restrictions on certain process chemistries or fuel types, the
 MRWFD campaign’s scope and timeline may need to be substantially revised.

 • Engage with NRC to build a shared understanding of the licensing pathway, identify data needs as
 early as possible, and reduce the risk of late-stage surprises requiring costly additional work.
 • When necessary, conduct a regulatory gap analysis to identify where current data does and does not
 address anticipated licensing requirements, and use this to prioritize activities that fill the most critical
 gaps.
 • Engage the safeguards and nonproliferation community, including the MPACT program to ensure
 process designs incorporate safeguards and proliferation resistance by design, reducing the risk of
 late-stage regulatory objections.

 40

 8.5 Industry and Commercial Landscape Risks
 The relevance and prioritization of specific technical activities in the near-term is partly predicated on
 assumptions about which advanced reactor and fuel recycling concepts will gain commercial traction over
 the strategy period. If the commercial landscape shifts – for example, if MSR deployment timelines
 accelerate or decelerate significantly, if certain fuel types gain or lose industry interest, or if NOFO
 awardees pursue technical directions that overlap more than expected with MRWFD activities – the
 campaign may need to rebalance its portfolio to remain aligned with actual industry needs and avoid
 duplication.

 • Maintain active engagement with industry partners and NOFO awardees through semi-annual
 coordination meetings and annual industry workshops to stay current with evolving commercial
 priorities and adjust the MRWFD campaign’s portfolio before misalignment becomes significant.
 • Structure the work scope around broadly applicable technology platforms and unit operations rather
 than optimizing narrowly for a single commercial concept that may not ultimately be deployed.
 • Maintain an agile work scope and be able to pivot to new technologies if necessary.
 • Conduct annual portfolio reviews that explicitly assess the continued relevance of each major
 technical activity against the current commercial landscape, with clear criteria for adding, modifying,
 or discontinuing activities.

 8.6 Geopolitical and Policy Risks
 Broader geopolitical developments and shifts in U.S. energy and nonproliferation policy could alter the
 strategic context for the MRWFD campaign’s work. Changes in U.S. policy on UNF reprocessing, shifts
 in international safeguards requirements, or disruptions to uranium supply chains that change the
 economic case for recycling could all affect program priorities and resource levels. Conversely, increased
 urgency around domestic fuel supply security, as has been seen with HALEU availability concerns, could
 accelerate certain aspects of the program, particularly those tied to near-term material recovery missions.

 Mitigations:

 • Monitor evolving U.S. energy and nonproliferation policy through active engagement with DOE-NE
 policy offices, ensuring the program is positioned to respond quickly to shifts that affect the strategic
 case for recycling.
 • Coordinate with the DOE Office of Environmental Management and NNSA to identify synergies
 between electrochemical R&D and used fuel management, waste disposition, and nonproliferation
 missions, broadening the program’s policy relevance and potential funding base.

 8.7 Supply Chain and Materials Risks
 Several aspects of the MRWFD campaign’s work scope depend on access to specific materials and
 equipment. Delays in acquiring surrogate materials, restrictions on the transfer of irradiated fuel between
 facilities, or supply chain disruptions affecting specialty equipment and materials could delay
 experimental campaigns and push milestones beyond planned completion dates.

 41

 • Initiate material acquisition activities well in advance of experimental needs, pursuing multiple
 parallel procurement sources for critical materials and equipment where possible.
 • Identify and qualify non-radioactive surrogate materials that can advance process development in
 non-radiological facilities while irradiated fuel acquisition is pending, maximizing productive use of
 available experimental time.
 • Build schedule contingency into campaigns that depend on irradiated or TRU materials, recognizing
 that regulatory approvals and inter-facility transfers routinely introduce unpredictable delays.
 • Maintain an inventory of commonly used consumables to buffer against supply chain disruptions that
 might otherwise cause immediate experimental interruptions.

 8.8 Commercial and Political Opportunities
 There has recently been a resurgence in commercial interest for reprocessing and recycling UNF, with
 individual vendors expressing interest in pursuing aqueous, electrochemical, and fluoride volatility
 technologies. Additionally, the President’s Executive Order “Reinvigorating the Nuclear Industrial Base,”
 signed on May 23, 2025, says:

 It is the policy of the United States to… maximize the efficiency and effectiveness of nuclear fuel
 through recycling, reprocessing, and reinvigorating the commercial sector.

 Thus, there are both commercial and political drivers toward reprocessing and recycling UNF that present
 an opportunity for the MRWFD campaign to expand its technical activities and influence. The
 opportunity can be realized with the following measures:

 • Aligning technical program priority areas to commercially viable options.
 • Increasing vendor participation in campaign reviews and planning activities by invitations to program
 reviews or under contract for consultancy, for example.
 • Active participation in national and international forums and conferences.

 This appendix describes the criteria that should be assessed to determine the Technology Readiness Level
 (TRL) of a technology. The TRL title is followed by the overall question to be answered, followed by the
 criteria in terms of concept development, testing, and modeling and simulation.

 TRL-4: Complete Technology and/or its Components Validated in the
 Laboratory
 Has laboratory-scale testing of the technology, like full-scale, been completed in the laboratory?

 Concept Development

 1. Key process variables/parameters fully identified and preliminary hazard evaluations have been
 completed and documented.

 2. Metrics for measuring technology performance against requirements have been established.

 3. Analysis completed to establish technology’s component compatibility (do components work
 together).

 4. Scalable equipment prototypes have been produced (components made larger than laboratory
 scale).

 5. Scaling documents and designs of equipment have been completed.

 Testing

 1. Tested laboratory-scale technology components are surrogates for full-scale components.

 2. Individual technology components tested in laboratory or by supplier.

 3. Multiple technology components tested at laboratory scale using performance-based simulants.

 4. Laboratory test requirements, derived from technology requirements, are established.

 5. Available technology components assembled into the laboratory-scale test arrangement.

 6. Planning for prototypical laboratory-scale tests completed.

 7. Laboratory experiments with available components show that they work together.

 8. Technology demonstrates basic functionality in the laboratory, to include test and validation of
 safety functions.

 9. Controlled laboratory environment used in testing.

 10. Low-fidelity (approximate) technology integration and engineering completed in the laboratory.

 11. Process/parameter limits and safety control strategies are being explored.

 49

 12. Key physical and chemical properties have been characterized for a range of feeds.

 13. A limited number of simulants have been developed that approximate the range of used nuclear
 fuel (UNF) feed properties.

 14. Laboratory-scale tests on a limited range of simulants and real UNF have been completed.

 15. Scale-up relationships are understood/accounted for in technology development program.

 16. Manufacturing processes for key equipment and chemicals have been identified.

 17. Developmental manufacturing processes for key equipment and chemicals assessed in laboratory.

 Modeling and Simulation

 1. Modeling and simulation used to simulate some components and interfaces between components.

 TRL-3: Analytical and Experimental Critical Function and/or Characteristic
 Proof of Concept
 Has the technology’s concept been proven in a laboratory environment?

 Conceptual Development

 1. Key process parameters/variables and associated hazards have begun to be identified.

 2. Paper studies indicate that the technology components ought to work together.

 3. Performance metrics for the technology are established (what must it do).

 4. Scaling studies have been started.

 5. Analysis of present state of the art shows that technology fills a need.

 Testing

 1. Testing completed with a simple simulant that includes the principal chemical and physical
 characteristics affecting technology performance.

 2. A comprehensive performance-based simulant has been developed that approximates key feed
 properties.

 3. Predictions of elements of technology’s capability validated by analytical studies.

 4. The basic science has been validated at the laboratory scale.

 50

 5. No technology components, only basic laboratory research equipment to verify physical
 principles.

 6. Laboratory experiments verify feasibility of application.

 7. Sources of key hardware and chemicals for laboratory testing identified.

 8. Scientific feasibility fully demonstrated.

 9. Laboratory-scale tests on a simulant have been completed.

 10. The individual technology components have been tested at the laboratory scale.

 Modeling and Simulation

 1. Science known to the extent that mathematical and/or computer models and simulations are
 possible.

 2. Predictions of elements of technology’s capability validated by modeling and simulation.

 TRL-2: Formulation of Technology Concept
 Has the technology concept and/or application formulated?

 Concept Development

 1. Potential need of the application has been identified.

 2. Basic components of the technology have been identified.

 3. The scope and scale of the problem to be solved with the technology has been determined.

 4. Preliminary strategy to obtain TRL-4 developed (e.g., scope, schedule, cost).

 5. Capabilities and limitations of researchers and research facilities are known.

 6. The experiments required (research approach) to achieve TRL-3 are known.

 Testing

 1. Paper studies show that application of the technology is feasible.

 2. A performance-based simulant has been conceptualized.

 3. Technology components have been partially characterized for performance.

 4. Rigorous analytical studies confirm basic principles.

 51

 5. Analytical studies reported in scientific journals/conference proceedings/technical reports.

 6. Individual components of the technology work (no real attempt at integration).

 Modeling and Simulation

 1. Performance predictions made for each element.

 2. Modeling and simulation only used to verify physical principles.

 TRL-1: Observation and Reporting of Basic Principles
 Have the basic technology principles been observed and reported?

 1. “Back of envelope” performance-based simulant defined.

 2. Physical laws and assumptions used in new technologies defined.

 3. Paper studies confirm basic principles.

 4. Initial scientific observations reported in journals/conference proceedings/technical reports.

 5. Basic scientific principles observed and understood.

 6. Know where, why, and how the technology is beneficial.

 7. Research hypothesis formulated.

 8. Basic UNF characterization data exists to proceed.

 9. Know who would perform research and where it would be done.

 Yearly Work Package Planning

 Scope - The work package scope is the foundation of the PICS entry and should clearly communicate
 what work will be performed, why it is important, and what outcomes will be achieved. The scope should
 be written at a level that provides DOE and program management with a clear understanding of the

 55

 planned work while allowing flexibility for some adaptation as the project evolves. The scope should
 focus on the technical objectives, major work areas, and expected deliverables, rather than detailed
 experimental procedures or day-to-day activities. A well-written scope should describe the work for the
 entire period of performance and demonstrate how it supports DOE program goals.

 Objective - The Objectives section should clearly define the technical outcomes the work package is
 intended to achieve during the fiscal year. Objectives should be written as high-level, outcome-focused
 statements that describe the capabilities, knowledge, products, or technologies that will be developed or
 demonstrated. Avoid listing detailed tasks, experimental procedures, or routine activities. A well-written
 objective should allow a reviewer to quickly understand what success looks like at the completion of the
 work package.

 Assumptions & Prerequisites - The Assumptions and Prerequisites section should identify the key
 assumptions and dependencies upon which successful execution of the work package relies. These
 statements should focus on conditions that are outside the direct control of the WPM and could
 significantly impact the project's ability to meet its objectives if they are not satisfied. This section is not
 intended to describe project risks or routine project management activities.

 Activities & Milestones - While no work package is the same, it is recommended that you break up your
 activities into logical tasks which are the right size to be reasonably planned and make sense for your
 milestones. M4s are generally planning activities or the conclusion of minor research. M3s are to report
 on significant activities, funded at ~$300k or greater. M2s are pre-defined and will be pre-loaded into the
 applicable WP. Always review these carefully and communicate early if there are major issues. Any
 milestone delays should be socialized in advance. It is expected that M3’s and M4’s should be
 appropriately included leading up to M2 milestones. We plan activities for a fiscal year, do not plan
 activities or milestone into the next fiscal year unless discussed with the CAM or NTD in advance.

 Activities - Activities define the major technical work areas required to achieve the work package
 objectives. They should provide a logical framework for organizing the work throughout the fiscal
 year without becoming a detailed project schedule. Activities should describe what major work will
 be performed, not every experiment, meeting, or individual task.

 Milestones - Milestones are the measurable technical accomplishments that demonstrate progress
 toward completing the work package objectives. They should represent meaningful outcomes, not
 routine activities, and provide clear evidence that the project is advancing as planned. Milestones
 should represent meaningful accomplishments that demonstrate measurable progress toward the work
 package objectives. In most DOE-NE work packages these are technical accomplishments, but
 milestones may also represent key decision points, technology demonstrations, or major deliverables.
 Each milestone should have a clear completion criterion and, whenever possible, produce a tangible
 outcome that can be objectively verified.

 Within PICS, milestones should be a separate item subordinate to the from the associated “activity”
 containing the funding spread. The “criteria for completion” tab of the milestone deliverable form should
 be used to provide additional detail on technical objectives of the milestone necessary for the intent to
 have been met, not to state administrative plans such as “a report will be uploaded into PICS.”

 Budget Planning - The work package budget should support successful execution of the planned scope,
 activities, milestones, and deliverables. While the total work package budget is pre-populated in PICS,
 WPMs are responsible for developing a realistic monthly spending plan that aligns with the technical
 schedule and laboratory financial systems. Budget plans should accurately reflect the work expected to be
 performed and be actively managed throughout the fiscal year to minimize significant variances.

 • When developing the budget, WPMs should:

 • Recognize that the overall work package budget is pre-populated in PICS; only the monthly budget
 distribution is planned by the WPM.

 • For INL work packages, ensure the final monthly budget spread is consistent with the INL COBRA
 financial system.

 • Align planned expenditures with the work package scope, activities, milestones, and schedule.

 • Include all anticipated costs, including labor, materials, analytical support, facility usage,
 procurements, travel, and subcontracts, as applicable.

 57

 • Plan approximately 20% carryover into the first quarter of the following fiscal year, when
 appropriate, to mitigate potential continuing resolution delays.

 • If a larger carryover is anticipated, plan it realistically and budget only for the work that can
 reasonably be executed during the fiscal year.

 • Monitor spending throughout the year and update forecasts as project conditions change.

 • Minimize significant budget variances, as large underruns or overruns require explanation during
 DOE-NE monthly program reviews.

 • Best Practice: Budget to execute the planned work—not to spend the available funding. A realistic
 budget accurately reflects when work will occur, supports successful completion of milestones,
 and demonstrates effective planning and execution. Avoid creating a budget spread that will show
 dramatic underspending toward the end of the year.

 Follow your individual laboratory expectations to plan the monthly budget distribution. For INL work
 packages, final PICS monthly budget spreads must be based on data from INL’s COBRA financial
 system. Each WP budget is reported to NE-4 in a monthly DOE-HQ program review meeting. Significant
 variances are flagged and must have an explanation.

 Note - When entering Q3 and your WP is 75% underspent, this is poor messaging to program
 leadership about your ability to plan and execute research.

 Carryover - Plan ~20% of your budget as carryover into the next fiscal year to mitigate funding delays
 associated with continuing resolution. This is easily done by having an Activity titled, “Carryover”

 Monthly Reporting - The second week of the month, WP managers will be notified by the system to
 enter a bulleted text summary of accomplishments in PICS. Take a few minutes to think about “Look
 Aheads,” which might be interesting or notable experiments, hosting of conferences/workshops, or
 similar interesting items that could be used to help highlight our program to DOE-HQ.

 Your input is harvested for DOE’s monthly review meetings to highlight the value gained for the
 expenditures. Short summary paragraphs of the accomplishment and its value to the program are ideal. If
 you enter vague explanations of accomplishments just to fill in the text box, this is not a productive use of
 anyone’s time. We all recognize that not every WP has substantive accomplishments every month; in that
 case, a few sentence reminder of working on topic X in preparation for the upcoming milestone would be
 appropriate.)

 Missed Milestones - Missed or delayed level 2 and level 3 milestones are tracked by DOE and reported
 on their own slide in the monthly program review. These should be identified as far in advance as
 achievable. The important thing is to help provide information to help the program articulate to DOE the
 significant efforts made to accomplish the milestone (although possibly ultimately unsuccessful), and how
 we will recover.

 Milestones and Reporting - Generally, milestone reports can completed with your laboratory’s template
 and document tracking numbers, but it is preferable to use MRWD’s template, especially when multiple
 institutions are involved. In this case, the report number is still from the lead institution, as DOE-NE no
 longer has its own document tracking system. Reports uploaded into PICS must have been cleared for
 public release through your laboratory’s clearance process. If the milestone was not appropriate for a
 report, notice of milestone completion can be done as a memorandum to the NTD, with copies to CAM
 and federal program manager. It is required that the memorandum reference the work package, milestone
 numbers, report title, and lab identifier number.

 Quality Level - The Quality Rigor Level (QRL) of most of our research is 3. Paper studies and literature
 surveys are QRL 4. For QRL 3, we are no longer required to do QA cover pages and are instead covered
 by DOE Order 414.1 (this is a check box on the milestone submittal form). Below this checkbox you will
 simply list your technical reviewer (usually internal to your lab).

 Export Control - PICS is not suitable for export controlled or similar information. In the event your
 deliverable report is controlled unclassified information (CUI), a memorandum should be uploaded to
 PICS which provides a suitable short summary, references the milestone, and directs the reader to an
 appropriate point of contact and backup point of contact at your laboratory to receive the full CUI report.
 Memoranda uploaded into PICS must have been cleared for public release through your laboratory’s
 clearance process. You must email the CAM, NTD, Deputy NTD and federal program manager with a
 copy of the milestone report.

 BCP

 A Baseline Change Proposal (BCP) is the formal process used to request approval for significant changes
 to an approved work package baseline. The project baseline consists of the approved scope, schedule,
 budget, milestones, and deliverables. A BCP helps ensure that significant changes are properly
 documented, reviewed, and approved before implementation, maintaining an accurate and traceable
 project baseline. Not every change requires a BCP; routine technical adjustments that do not affect the
 approved baseline can typically be managed through normal project execution. When in doubt, WPMs
 should consult program leadership to determine whether a BCP is required.

 A BCP should be considered when:

 • The approved technical scope changes significantly, or in a way that wouldn’t be clear to program
 leadership.

 • Milestones or deliverables are added, deleted, or significantly modified.

 • The project schedule changes in a way that affects the approved baseline.

 • The approved work package budget changes beyond established laboratory or program thresholds.

 • Additional funding results in new scope, additional milestones, or expanded project objectives.

 • Budget reductions require descoping work or modifying planned deliverables.

 • DOE or the NTD directs a significant change in project priorities or objectives.

 59

 A BCP should include:

 • A clear description of the proposed change.

 • The reason for the change.

 • The impact on scope, schedule, budget, milestones, and deliverables.

 • The benefits, risks, and justification for the proposed change.

 • Any impacts to other work packages or projects.

 Acknowledgments - For all milestone document, acknowledgments must be given to all team members
 that were not included as authors. Acknowledge facilities and include the acknowledgment of the
 MRWFD campaign.

 Deliverable Preparation for Archiving - It is expected that all the authors of the milestone document
 have had an opportunity to read and provide feedback. Milestones should have been peer-reviewed as
 well as gone though technical editing. If AI was used to help write the document it should be noted.
 Technical reports should include basic information to clarify the background, problem statement and
 equipment used. Raw and curated datasets including uncertainty bands (where applicable) should be
 uploaded to PICS with the report. Datasets should be uploaded as Excel or delimited (CSV) spreadsheets.
