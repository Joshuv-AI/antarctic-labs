The censored stochastic six-vertex model and parabolic Kazhdan–Lusztig R R -polynomials
Hindy Drillick, Levi Haunschmid-Sibitz

Abstract
. We introduce a censored version of the stochastic six-vertex model. We show that for parameters b 1 < b 2 b_{1}<b_{2} , this model started from the initial condition 𝟏 x > 0 \bm{1}_{x>0} is stochastically dominated at any time by the blocking measure. This is a partial analog of the censoring inequality for monotone spin systems. In particular, this result allows us to control the behavior of second-class particles. The proof uses parabolic Kazhdan–Lusztig R R -polynomials, whose appearance is explained using a connection between the stochastic six-vertex model and the Iwahori–Hecke algebras of symmetric groups. Furthermore, we find an intertwining relation for this process using normalized parabolic Kazhdan–Lusztig R R -polynomials as an intertwining kernel.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Preface

The stochastic six-vertex model was first introduced by
[
GS92
]
as a specialization of the six-vertex model, which is a classical model from equilibrium statistical mechanics going back to
[
Pau35
]
.
With this specialization, the model has a Markovian structure, which enables it to be seen as a discrete-time interacting particle system. It is connected via a suitable limit degeneration to the asymmetric simple exclusion process (ASEP)
[
Agg17
]
, the Kardar–Parisi–Zhang (KPZ) equation
[
CT17
,
CGST20
,
Lin20a
]
, the stochastic telegraph equation
[
BG19
,
ST19
,
Lin20b
]
, and lies in the (one-dimensional) KPZ universality class
[
GS92
,
BCG16
,
Dim23
,
ACH24a
,
ACH24b
]
.
Furthermore, it can be put into the more general setting of higher-spin vertex models, see
[
CP16
,
BP18
,
BW22
]
.

In this paper, our focus is on certain monotonicity properties of the stochastic six-vertex process, which are much more subtle than their counterparts for other exclusion processes such as ASEP.
The stochastic six-vertex process has a censored version, where at certain censored vertices, particles and holes cannot pass each other. Our main result, Theorem
1.8
, shows that the censored stochastic six-vertex process is still dominated by its stationary measure at any fixed time.
This can be used to bound the behavior of higher-class particles, see Theorem
4.7
, which was the original motivation for this work.

This result can also be seen as a partial
censoring inequality
. Censoring inequalities originate in
[
PW13
]
for monotone spin systems. A censoring inequality states that censoring certain vertex updates can only increase the (total variation) distance to stationarity, and that the censored process will, at any fixed time, be dominated by the stationary measure.
We call our result a
partial
censoring inequality since, in our setting, it is no longer true that censoring always increases the distance to stationarity.

In the proof of the main results, we introduce a family of measures which we call
parabolic Kazhdan–Lusztig R-measures
, as they are given by normalized parabolic Kazhdan–Lusztig R-polynomials. The appearance of Kazhdan–Lusztig R-polynomials in the context of the stochastic six-vertex model can be understood by considering the connection between the colored stochastic six-vertex model and Iwahori–Hecke algebras as described in
[
Buf20
,
Gal21
,
BW22
]
.
Our result gives a new avenue to interpret properties of the Kazhdan–Lusztig R-polynomials probabilistically.

We define the stochastic six-vertex model as a measure on collections of up-right paths in the upper half plane
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

All paths start on the horizontal line

Paths are allowed to share vertices, but not edges. This leads to six possible configurations at each vertex, each receiving a weight according to the two model parameters
b_{1},b_{2}\in(0,1)

1

We take
b_{1},b_{2}
such that
0<b_{1}<b_{2}<1

The measure will further depend on an initial condition
\eta_{0}:\mathbb{Z}\to\{0,1\}

\eta_{0}(x)=1

(x,0)

\eta_{0}(x)=0

Given an initial configuration
\eta_{0}

\eta_{0}(x)=0

We define the occupation variables
(\eta_{t}(x))_{(x,t)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
where
\eta_{t}(x)=1

(x+t,t)

\eta_{t}(x)=0

Definition 1.1
.

The process
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is called the (shifted) stochastic six-vertex process on the line with initial condition
\eta_{0}
and parameters
b_{1},b_{2}
.

Remark 1.2
.

The reason for including the shift

(1,1)
.
One way to see this is that only with this shift will the process possess non-translation-invariant stationary measures, while without it only translation-invariant measures will remain, see
[
Lin23
]
.
Note also that in the scaling limit
b_{1},b_{2}\to 0
with
\frac{b_{1}}{b_{2}}=q
fixed it is the shifted process that converges to ASEP, see
[
Agg17
]
.

This process has the same stationary measures as the asymmetric simple exclusion process, see
[

]
.
In particular, these stationary measures are the blocking measures first defined in
[

]
, which depend on
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
through
q\coloneqq\frac{b_{1}}{b_{2}}\in(0,1)

Definition 1.3

Let

1.3.
Main theorem

Consider the dynamics of the stochastic six-vertex model modified as follows.

Definition 1.7

(Censored stochastic six-vertex model)
.

Given a set
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
of vertices, which we call censored vertices, we define the
\mathcal{C}
-censored stochastic six-vertex process
\eta_{t}
as follows. The dynamics of
\eta_{t}
are defined as in Definition
1.1
, except that whenever one updates at a vertex in
\mathcal{C}
, configurations III and V in Figure
1
are never chosen, i.e., at the vertices in
\mathcal{C}
, paths can never go straight, they must always turn.
See Figure
2
for a visual representation of the censored vertices.

Our main theorem is the following:

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
0<b_{1}<b_{2}<1
and let
\mathcal{C}
be any subset of
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Then the
\mathcal{C}
-censored stochastic six-vertex model
\eta
started from
\eta_{0}=\bm{1}_{x>0}
satisfies that the law of
\eta_{t}
is dominated by the blocking measure
\pi
for any time
t
.

Figure 2.
A piece of
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
where some of the vertices have been censored.

Remark 1.9
.

The uncensored case of Theorem
1.8
(i.e., where
\mathcal{C}=\emptyset
) is also interesting and nontrivial. This case can also be proven using the monotone coupling in
[
Agg20
, Proposition 2.6]
.

Let us compare Theorem
1.8
to the censoring inequality from
[
PW13
]
.
Informally, the censoring inequality can be stated as follows.

Theorem 1.10

(Theorem 1.1 in
[
PW13
]
)
.

Consider Glauber dynamics on a monotone spin system.
Let
\nu
and
\tilde{\nu}
both be obtained by starting from the minimal configuration of the spin system and running a censored Glauber dynamics where the set of transitions censored for
\tilde{\nu}
contains the set censored for
\nu
.
Then we have that

(2)

\tilde{\nu}\preceq\nu\preceq\pi\,,

where
\pi
is the stationary measure of the Glauber chain.

It is not surprising that the full version of this theorem does not hold for the stochastic six-vertex model, since the stochastic six-vertex model lacks a form of monotonicity which is a key ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.8
.
Indeed one can easily see that the reverse inequality can hold between
\nu
and
\tilde{\nu}
, see Example
A.1
.
Considering this, it is perhaps surprising that the second part of the inequality in (
2
), namely domination by the stationary measure, does still hold.

The proof of Theorem
1.8
proceeds by showing that at any time
t
the law of
\eta_{t}
is given by a convex combination of
v_{\lambda}
, where
(v_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}
is a family of probability measures indexed by partitions that we define in Definition
3.6
. The measures
v_{\lambda}
are normalized versions of the
parabolic Kazhdan–Lusztig
R
-polynomials
, a family of polynomials appearing in the study of representations of Hecke algebras. They were introduced in
[
Deo87
]
as a projection of the (non-parabolic) Kazhdan–Lusztig
R
-polynomials
[
KL79
]
onto a parabolic quotient.
Theorem
1.8
follows from the key observation that all the measures
v_{\lambda}
are stochastically dominated by
\pi
.
The measures
v_{\lambda}
arose naturally in the proof, and their intriguing connection to parabolic Kazhdan–Lusztig
R
-polynomials will be illuminated in Section
5
.

When expanding
\eta_{t}
as a convex combination of the measures
v_{\lambda}
, the coefficients in this expansion are themselves given by a stochastic six-vertex process with the parameters
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
flipped. This gives rise to an intertwining relation for the stochastic six-vertex model, which we state as Theorem
4.1
.

For ASEP the corresponding statement to Theorem
1.8
follows easily from the ideas used in
[
PW13
]
, see
[
Lac16
]
for SEP and
[
FS25
]
for ASEP (see also
[
GNS23
]
).
For completeness we write out the steps of this proof in Appendix
A
.
For the stochastic six-vertex process this is only possible if
b_{1}+b_{2}\leq 1
, as otherwise the process lacks the kinds of monotonicity properties necessary for the proof, i.e., the ones in Lemmas
A.3
and
A.4
.
One should note that even in the case of
b_{1}+b_{2}\leq 1
where Theorem
A.2
yields a stronger result than Theorem
1.8
, the proof of Theorem
1.8
still gives new insight into the structure of the process.

One useful application of Theorem
1.8
is to control the behavior of second-class particles in the multi-class (or colored) stochastic six-vertex process. We can do this by applying Theorem
1.8
to a censoring scheme where we censor vertices corresponding to first-class particles and holes such that the resulting graph describes the behavior of second-class particles with respect to third-class particles, i.e., where all positions containing first-class particles and holes have been deleted. This is stated precisely in Theorem
4.7
. A stochastic domination result of this type allows for greater control over these higher-class particles starting from non-stationary initial conditions. Such coupling techniques to control second-class particles are widely utilized in the study of interacting particle systems, see e.g.
[
BBHM05
,
BS10
,
BS09
,
BKS12
,
DHS25
,
BBB+25
,
BN25
]
, but have not yet been fully developed for the stochastic six-vertex model.

2.
Proof of the main theorem

In this section we prove the main theorem by reducing it to Theorem
2.6
below, which will be proved in Section
3
.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a particle configuration
\eta
, let
\eta^{k}
denote the configuration obtained from
\eta
by swapping the values of
\eta(k)
and
\eta(k+1)
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
P_{k}
be the propagator of the following dynamics on particle configurations
\eta\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\{0,1\}
.
Exchange the values of
\eta(k)
and
\eta(k+1)
with probability
b_{1}
if
(\eta(k),\eta(k+1))=(0,1)
and with probability
b_{2}
if
(\eta(k),\eta(k+1))=(1,0)
.
In other words, if
\nu
is a measure on particle configurations and
\eta
is a particle configuration, then

P_{k}\nu(\eta)=\begin{cases}b_{2}\nu(\eta^{k})+(1-b_{1})\nu(\eta),&\text{ if }(\eta(k),\eta(k+1))=(0,1)\\
b_{1}\nu(\eta^{k})+(1-b_{2})\nu(\eta),&\text{ if }(\eta(k),\eta(k+1))=(1,0)\\
\nu(\eta),&\text{ if }\eta(k)=\eta(k+1)\,.\end{cases}

Remark 2.3
.

Note that the
P_{k}
act on Dirac measures in the following way:

(3)

P_{k}\delta_{\eta}=\begin{cases}(1-b_{1})\delta_{\eta}+b_{1}\delta_{\eta^{k}},&\text{ if }(\eta(k),\eta(k+1))=(0,1)\\
(1-b_{2})\delta_{\eta}+b_{2}\delta_{\eta^{k}}&\text{ if }(\eta(k),\eta(k+1))=(1,0)\\
\delta_{\eta},&\text{ if }\eta(k)=\eta(k+1)\,,\end{cases}

as can be easily checked from the definition.

Applying these operators in the correct order to the initial condition gives exactly the law of the stochastic six-vertex process.

Proposition 2.4
.

The law of the
\mathcal{C}
-censored stochastic six-vertex process
\eta_{t}
started from the initial condition
\bm{1}_{x>0}
is given by

\lim_{N\to\infty}\prod_{s=0}^{t-1}\prod_{k=-N}^{t-1-s}{P}_{-k,t-1-s}\delta_{\bm{1}_{x>0}}

where

{P}_{k,t}=\begin{cases}P_{k}\,\text{, if }(k+t,t)\notin\mathcal{C}\\
\mathrm{Id}\,\text{, else.}\end{cases}

Remark 2.5
.

Note that we are following the standard notation of applying the operators in the product from right to left, i.e., the first operator applied to
\delta_{\bm{1}_{x>0}}
is
P_{0,0}
.
Unfortunately, this necessitates the reversal in the indices in the formula above.

Proof.

Consider the intermediate states
\eta_{t,k}
defined by

\eta_{t,k}(x)=\begin{cases}\eta_{t+1}(x)\text{, if }x<k\\
0\text{, if }x=k\text{ and the edge between $(k-1+t,t)$ and $(k+t,t)$ is empty}\\
1\text{, if }x=k\text{ and the edge between $(k-1+t,t)$ and $(k+t,t)$ is occupied}\\
\eta_{t}(x)\text{, if }x>k\,.\end{cases}

Figure 3.
The edges whose occupation variables are the
\eta_{t,k}
are shown as arrows.

See Figure
3
for a diagram of this definition.
Note the following properties of the
\eta_{t,k}
.
First, note that for
k\leq-t
, we have
\eta_{t,k}=\eta_{t}
, since
\eta_{t+1}(x)=\eta_{t}(x)=0
for
x\leq-t
(because all particles initially started to the right of
0
) and therefore also
\eta_{t,k}(k)=0
.
Furthermore
\lim_{k\to\infty}\eta_{t,k}=\eta_{t+1}
, with the sequence being almost surely constant for large enough
k
, because almost surely for
x
large enough
\eta_{t+1}(x)=\eta_{t}(x)=1
.
Note that this implies that the law of
\eta_{t,k}
converges weakly to the law of
\eta_{t+1}
, even though this law will not be constant in
k
.
Finally note that

(4)

\mathcal{L}(\eta_{t,{k+1}})=P_{k,t}\mathcal{L}(\eta_{t,k})\,,

where
\mathcal{L}
denotes the law of a random variable.
Indeed,
\eta_{t,k}(k)
and
\eta_{t,k}(k+1)
are the occupation variables of the incoming edges of the vertex
(k+t,t)
and
\eta_{t,k+1}(k)
and
\eta_{t,k+1}(k+1)
are the occupation variables of the outgoing edges at this vertex.
Therefore, if the vertex
(k+t,t)
is not censored,
\eta_{t,k+1}
is obtained from
\eta_{t,k}
exactly by swapping the values
\eta_{t,k}(k)
and
\eta_{t,k}(k+1)
with the probabilities just as in Definition

, while if it is censored
\eta_{t,k+1}=\eta_{t,k}
.

Iterating (

) together with the behavior of
\eta_{t,k}
for

discussed above gives

\mathcal{L}(\eta_{t+1})=\lim_{N\to\infty}\prod_{k=-N}^{t}{P}_{-k,t}\mathcal{L}(\eta_{t})\,.

Iterating over

yields

\prod_{s=0}^{t-1}\lim_{N\to\infty}\prod_{k=-N}^{t-1-s}{P}_{-k,t-1-s}\delta_{\bm{1}_{x>0}}

by noting that
\mathcal{L}(\eta_{0})=\delta_{\bm{1}_{x>0}}
.
Since
\eta_{t,k}(x)
only depends on

one can take the limit outside of the outer product.
∎

Theorem
1.8
will now follow easily from the following theorem, which is the main technical result of the paper.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\delta_{\bm{1}_{x>0}}
be the Dirac delta on the configuration
\eta(x)=\bm{1}_{x>0}
.
For any sequence

, the measure

\prod_{i=1}^{n}P_{k_{i}}\delta_{\bm{1}_{x>0}}

is stochastically dominated by the blocking measure
\pi
.

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

By Proposition
2.4
, the law of
\eta_{t}
is the limit of measures that are each dominated by
\pi
by Theorem

.
This property is preserved under weak convergence, and thus the law of
\eta_{t}
is also dominated by
\pi
.
∎

Note that Proposition

actually shows the equivalence of Theorem

, since any product of

3.
Parabolic Kazhdan–Lusztig
R

3.1.
Partitions

Throughout the paper, it will be convenient to identify particle configurations with partitions. We denote by
\mathcal{P}
the set of all partitions, i.e.,

\mathcal{P}=\{\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{k}):\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{k}\geq 0\}\,,

where we identify partitions of different lengths if one can be obtained from the other by removing trailing zeros.
We define the size of a partition to be
|\lambda|=\sum_{i\geq 1}\lambda_{i}
.

We further identify a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots)
with its Young diagram, i.e., the set

\{(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{2}:1\leq j\leq\lambda_{i}\}\,.

We draw Young diagrams as unions of
1\times 1
squares using the English convention, i.e., we index the rows
i
from top to bottom and columns
j
from left to right.

We identify particle configurations in
A
with partitions by letting
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
denote the ordered positions of the particles and then setting
\lambda_{i}=i-X_{i}
. In other words,
\lambda_{i}
records the distance
X_{i}
has traveled from its starting position. Note that increasing
\lambda_{i}
by one is equivalent to
X_{i}
jumping one step to the left. The configuration
\bm{1}_{x>0}
is identified with the empty partition, which we denote by
()
.

The partial ordering of particle configurations by their height function corresponds to the partial ordering of partitions given by

(5)

\lambda\leq\mu:\!\iff\lambda_{i}\leq\mu_{i}\text{, for all }i\geq 1\,.

In terms of Young diagrams, this means that
\lambda\subseteq\mu
, so we can call this the
containment ordering
.

Finally, we will also denote the set of partitions contained in a box of height
i
and width
n-i
by

\mathcal{P}(n,i):=\left\{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}:\lambda\subseteq(n-i)^{i}\right\}.

From now on, we will abuse notation and identify measures on particle configurations with measures on partitions, e.g., we will write things like
\pi(\lambda)
.

3.2.
Properties of the blocking measure

Proposition 3.1

(q-Exchangeability)
.

The measure
\pi
is
q
-exchangeable, meaning that if
\tilde{\eta}
is obtained from
\eta
by moving a particle one step to the left, then
\pi(\tilde{\eta})=q\pi(\eta)
.

Proof.

This follows from the
q
-exchangeability of the product Bernoulli measure
\hat{\pi}
.
∎

This allows us to compute the probability of a partition
\lambda
under
\pi
.

Proposition 3.2
.

We have that

(6)

\pi(\lambda)=q^{|\lambda|}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{i}).

Proof.

By
q
-exchangeability, the proof follows from showing that
\pi(())=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{i})
.
We have that

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\pi(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\pi(())\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}q^{|\lambda|}

\displaystyle=\pi(())\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{i})^{-1}.

The second line follows from
q
-exchangeability and the fact that to obtain the configuration
\lambda
starting from the step configuration requires moving the
i
th leftmost particle
\lambda_{i}
steps to the left.
∎

3.3.
Definition of parabolic Kazhdan–Lusztig
R
-measures

Definition 3.3
.

We define the
k
th diagonal
of a partition to consist of all cells
(i,j)
such that
i-j=k
, where
i
denotes the row number and
j
denotes the column number.

Definition 3.4
.

For
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we define
\lambda^{k}
as the partition obtained by flipping a corner in the
k
-th diagonal of
\lambda
, i.e., by adding or removing a box with coordinates
(i,j)
that satisfies
i-j=k
. If this is not possible, then we just take
\lambda^{k}=\lambda
.

For example,

The stochastic six-vertex model is also sometimes studied on the half line or on the strip, which are the domains

Configurations

(Half line)

Consider the censored stochastic six-vertex process

Then for any

and the space-time history of the first-class particles, is stochastically dominated by the blocking measure

Note that the choice of

among the second- and third-class particles is the same for all possible realizations of

Remark 4.8

An analogous result is already known in the setting of ASEP where the proof follows from the monotonicity of the graphical construction of ASEP. This was first stated in

Proof of Theorem
4.7
.

First note that the law of
\xi_{0}
is given by
\delta_{\bm{1}_{x>0}}
. Once we condition on
\bm{Z}
, the labeling process can be understood as the stochastic six-vertex process on the subgraph of

given by the edges of the second- and third-class particles.
Indeed, whether particles turn or go straight at a vertex of this subgraph at which a second-class and a third-class particle meet is independent of the rest of
\bm{Z}
, since second and third-class particles both interact the same way with the first-class particles and the holes. For a more formal description of this independence, see the proof of Theorem 1.6 in
[

The graph can be described as a sequence of directed paths, which touch at the vertices of degree four.
Whenever the paths touch the path on the left will be the horizontal incoming edge and the path on the right will be the vertical incoming edge of the vertex at which they touch.
What matters for the law of the labeling process is only the sequence of degree four vertices where the paths touch.
Any such sequence can be replicated by censoring sufficiently many vertices.
∎

5.
Hecke algebra point of view

While working on this project, we discovered that the measures
v_{\lambda}
are actually a special case of the
parabolic Kazhdan–Lusztig
R
-polynomials
defined by Deodhar
[
Deo87
]
and further studied by Brenti in
[
Bre02
]
. This connection arises when viewing the stochastic six-vertex model as a
random walk on a Hecke algebra
. We review the relevant definitions and discuss this connection in more detail.

5.1.
Hecke algebra setup

We first review the connection between the colored stochastic six-vertex model and random walks on Hecke algebras, following the notation in
[
Buf20
]
. We will restrict ourselves to the colored stochastic six-vertex model defined on an
i\times(n-i)
rectangle. We view the colored stochastic six-vertex model as a permutation-valued particle process, i.e.,
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
such that
\eta_{t}\in S_{n}
is a permutation of the
n
particles with colors
1,2,\ldots,n.

Note that
S_{n}
is a Coxeter group generated by the set
S
of adjacent transpositions of the form
(k,k+1)
for some
1\leq k\leq n-1
. Each permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
can be assigned a length
\ell(\sigma):=\text{inv}(\sigma)
which is given by the number of inversions in the permutation
\sigma
. Our convention is to define the product of two permutations as
\tau\sigma=\sigma\circ\tau.

We will make use of the following partial order called the
Bruhat order
on
S_{n}
.

Definition 5.1

(Bruhat ordering on permutations)
.

For permutations
u,v\in S_{n}
, we say that
u\to v
if there exists a transposition
w
such that
v=wu
and
\ell(v)>\ell(u)
. We say that
u\leq v
if there is a finite sequence of permutations
u_{i}\in S_{n}
such that
u=u_{1}\to u_{2}\to\ldots\to u_{m}=v
.

Let
0<q<1
. We define the
Hecke algebra

\mathcal{H}(S_{n})
to be the algebra with linear basis
\{T_{\sigma}\}_{\sigma\in S_{n}}
where we can define
T_{\sigma}
using the following multiplication rule for all
\sigma\in S_{n},\tau\in S
.

(19)

T_{\tau}T_{\sigma}=\begin{cases}T_{\tau\sigma},&\text{ if }\ell(\tau\sigma)=\ell(\sigma)+1\\
(1-q^{-1})T_{\sigma}+q^{-1}T_{\tau\sigma},&\text{ if }\ell(\tau\sigma)=\ell(\sigma)-1.\end{cases}

An equivalent way to write this is that the
T_{\sigma}
satisfy

\displaystyle T_{\sigma}T_{\sigma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=T_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\text{ if }\ell(\sigma\sigma^{\prime})=\ell(\sigma)+\ell(\sigma^{\prime})

(20)

\displaystyle(T_{\tau}-1)(T_{\tau}+q^{-1})

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ if }\tau\in S.

Remark 5.2
.

We note here that the choice of the multiplication rule in (
5.1
) is somewhat non-standard, and is made in order to better match the probabilistic interpretation of the model. However, if we define

\widetilde{T}_{\sigma}:=q^{\ell(\sigma)}T_{\sigma}
then we can see that the
\widetilde{T}_{\sigma}
instead satisfy

(21)

\displaystyle(\widetilde{T}_{\tau}-q)(\widetilde{T}_{\tau}+1)=0.

This matches the more standard convention found e.g. in
[
BB05
]
3
3

3

Note that in
[
Buf20
]
a third convention is used:
(T_{\tau}-1)(T_{\tau}+q)=0
. This matches our convention up to swapping
q
and
q^{-1}
.
.

Next, we define the set

\mathcal{H}_{\text{prob}}(S_{n})=\{h\in\mathcal{H}(S_{n}):h=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\kappa_{\sigma}T_{\sigma},\sum_{\sigma}\kappa_{\sigma}=1,\kappa_{\sigma}\geq 0\}.

Each of the elements
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{prob}}
can be thought of as defining a probability distribution on
S_{n}
.

Let
e\in S_{n}
denote the identity permutation. Then for
\tau=(k,k+1)\in S
, we can define the element
Y_{\tau}\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{prob}}(S_{n})
as follows:

Y_{\tau}:=b_{1}T_{\tau}+(1-b_{1})T_{e}.

Note that

(22)

Y_{\tau}T_{\sigma}=\begin{cases}b_{1}T_{\tau\sigma}+(1-b_{1})T_{\sigma}\text{, if }\sigma(k)<\sigma(k+1)\\
b_{2}T_{\tau\sigma}+(1-b_{2})T_{\sigma}\text{, if }\sigma(k)>\sigma(k+1)\,,\end{cases}

which we can check using (
19
). It follows that
\mathcal{H}_{\text{prob}}
is closed under left multiplication by
Y_{\tau}
.

Finally, to define the stochastic six-vertex model at time
1
, we multiply together these elements in a deterministic fashion as follows. Define

W_{i,n}:=Y_{(n-1,n)}\cdots Y_{(i+1,i+2)}Y_{(i,i+1)}.

Then the colored stochastic six-vertex model in the rectangle
i\times(n-i)
is given by the element

W_{1,n-i+1}\cdots W_{i-1,n-1}W_{i,n}.

We call this viewpoint of defining the stochastic six-vertex model through a product of elements in
\mathcal{H}_{\text{prob}}(S_{n})
a
random walk on the Hecke algebra
\mathcal{H}(S_{n})
.

We now explain how to go from the colored stochastic six-vertex model defined above back to the uncolored stochastic six-vertex model via projection. Given
\sigma\in S_{n}
, we can obtain a particle-hole configuration by taking
(\mathbf{1}_{\sigma(j)>i})_{j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}
. In other words, we map all particles with color less than or equal
i
to holes and all particles with colors greater than
i
to particles. We can then define the extended configuration

\eta(x)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }x\leq-i\\
\mathbf{1}_{\sigma(x+i)>i},&\text{if }-i+1\leq x\leq n-i\\
1&\text{if }x\geq n-i+1\,.\end{cases}

The particle configuration
\eta(x)
belongs to the set
A
defined in (
1
) and is therefore associated to a partition
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n,n-i)
. We define the map
\phi:S_{n}\to\mathcal{P}(n,n-i)
by setting
\phi(\sigma)=\lambda
.

By abuse of notation, we also set
\phi(T_{\sigma})=\delta_{\phi(\sigma)}
and extend linearly to a function
\phi:\mathcal{H}_{\text{prob}}(S_{n})\to\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathcal{P}(n,n-i))
so that we can also project probability measures on permutations to probability measures on particle configurations/partitions. Note that if
\sigma\leq\sigma^{\prime}
under the Bruhat ordering, then
\phi(\sigma)\leq\phi(\sigma^{\prime})
under the containment ordering in (
5
).

In the language of Coxeter groups,
\phi
is the projection of
S_{n}
onto a parabolic quotient. Let
\tau_{i}=(i,i+1)
and let
J=S\setminus\{\tau_{i}\}.
We can then define the
parabolic subgroup

\left(S_{n}\right)_{J}
as the subgroup of
S_{n}
generated by
J
. We can see that

\left(S_{n}\right)_{J}=\operatorname{Stab}(\{1,\ldots i\}).
We also define the
parabolic quotient

\displaystyle\quad\left(S_{n}\right)^{J}:

\displaystyle=\left\{\sigma\in S_{n}:\ell(\sigma s)>\ell(\sigma)\text{ for all }s\in J\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\sigma\in S_{n}:\sigma^{-1}(1)<\cdots<\sigma^{-1}(i)\text{ and }\sigma^{-1}(i+1)<\cdots<\sigma^{-1}(n)\right\}.

We call this a quotient because the minimal-length representatives of the left cosets of
(S_{n})_{J}
are given by
(S_{n})^{J}
. In particular the representative of the coset
\sigma\cdot(S_{n})_{J}
is obtained by placing the colors
1,\ldots,i
in the positions
\sigma^{-1}(1),\ldots,\sigma^{-1}(i)
and the colors
i+1,\ldots n
in positions
\sigma^{-1}(i+1),\ldots,\sigma^{-1}(n)
. Note that
\sigma\in\left(S_{n}\right)^{J}
is completely determined by specifying
\sigma^{-1}(1),\ldots,\sigma^{-1}(i)
and therefore can be identified with the particle configuration
(\mathbf{1}_{\sigma(j)>i})_{j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}
. It follows that we can identify
\left(S_{n}\right)^{J}
with
\mathcal{P}(n,n-i)
.

We will now define a second basis for the Hecke algebra that projects down to the basis
v_{\lambda}
defined in Definition
3.6
. This new basis consists of the elements
\overline{T}_{\sigma}:=T_{\sigma^{-1}}^{-1}
, which we first need to check are well-defined.

For
\tau\in S
, we can check directly that

(23)

\displaystyle T_{\tau}^{-1}=qT_{\tau}+(1-q)T_{e}.

It then follows from the fact that any
T_{\sigma}
can be written as a product of factors
T_{s_{j}}
where
s_{j}\in S
that we can also invert
T_{\sigma}
for all
\sigma\in S_{n}
.

The following proposition demonstrates that
Y_{\tau}\overline{T}_{\sigma}
can be written as a convex combination of
\overline{T}_{\tau\sigma}
and
\overline{T}_{\sigma}
. This property is the analogue of Proposition
3.11
and will show that
\phi(\overline{T}_{\sigma})=v_{\phi(\sigma)}

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\tau=(k,k+1)
. Then the elements
\overline{T}_{\sigma}:=T_{\sigma^{-1}}^{-1}
satisfy

(24)

Y_{\tau}\overline{T}_{\sigma}=\begin{cases}(1-b_{2})\overline{T}_{\sigma}+b_{2}\overline{T}_{\tau\sigma}\text{, if }\sigma(k)<\sigma(k+1)\\
(1-b_{1})\overline{T}_{\sigma}+b_{1}\overline{T}_{\tau\sigma}\text{, if }\sigma(k)>\sigma(k+1)\,.\end{cases}

Proof.

If
\sigma(k)<\sigma(k+1)
then
\ell(\tau\sigma)=\ell(\sigma)+1
.
Then

\overline{T}_{\tau\sigma}=T_{\tau}^{-1}\overline{T}_{\sigma}=qT_{\tau}\overline{T}_{\sigma}+(1-q)\overline{T}_{\sigma}.

Rearranging terms gives

T_{\tau}\overline{T}_{\sigma}=q^{-1}\overline{T}_{\tau\sigma}+(1-q^{-1})\overline{T}_{\sigma}

and finally

Y_{\tau}\overline{T}_{\sigma}=(b_{1}T_{\tau}+(1-b_{1})T_{e})\overline{T}_{\sigma}=b_{2}\overline{T}_{\tau\sigma}+(1-b_{2})\overline{T}_{\sigma}.

The other case proceeds similarly.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

For all
\sigma\in S_{n}

\phi(\overline{T}_{\sigma})=v_{\phi(\sigma)}\,.

Proof.

The key observation is that for any

and any transposition

\phi(Y_{\tau_{k}}h)=P_{k}\phi(h)\,.

Considering first

for some
\sigma\in S_{n}

) and (
3
) using the definition of
\phi
.
The statement for general

follows by linearity of
\phi
on
\mathcal{H}_{\text{prob}}

Applying
\phi
to both sides of (
24

\sigma\in S_{n}
and all
k\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}

P_{k}\phi(\overline{T}_{\sigma})=\begin{cases}(1-b_{2})\phi(\overline{T}_{\sigma})+b_{2}\phi(\overline{T}_{\tau_{k}\sigma}),&\text{ if $\sigma(k)<\sigma(k+1)$}\\
(1-b_{1})\phi(\overline{T}_{\sigma})+b_{1}\phi(\overline{T}_{\tau_{k}\sigma}),&\text{ if $\sigma(k)>\sigma(k+1)$}\,.\end{cases}

Using this we can iteratively prove that
\phi(\overline{T}_{\sigma})=v_{\phi(\sigma)}

First note that
\phi(\overline{T}_{e})=\delta_{()}=v_{()}

Now assume that the statement has already been proven for some

\phi(\overline{T}_{\tau_{k}\sigma})=\frac{1}{b_{2}}P_{k}\phi(\overline{T}_{\sigma})+\frac{(b_{2}-1)}{b_{2}}\phi(\overline{T}_{\sigma})=\frac{1}{b_{2}}P_{k}v_{\phi(\sigma)}+\frac{(b_{2}-1)}{b_{2}}v_{\phi(\sigma)}.

Using Proposition

Definition 5.5

(
[

The Kazhdan–Lusztig

\left\{R_{u,v}(q)\right\}_{u,v\in S_{n}}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[q]
defined through the following recurrence relations: Let
R_{u,v}(q)=0
if

and
R_{u,v}(q)=1
if

u<v

\displaystyle R_{u,v}(q)=\begin{cases}R_{su,sv}(q)&\text{if $su<u$}\\

It is straightforward to check that the following identity holds:

(25)

(-q)^{\ell(v)-\ell(u)}R_{u,v}\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)=R_{u,v}(q).

We can now express the elements

Proposition 5.6

(
[

)

Equivalently, using (

\displaystyle{T}_{w^{-1}}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\leq w}(-1)^{\ell(y)-\ell(w)}R_{y,w}(q)q^{\ell(y)}{T}_{y}

5.2.
Lack of monotonicity for the Kazhdan–Lusztig
R
-polynomials

It is natural to ask whether the monotonicity properties we showed for the
parabolic
Kazhdan–Lusztig
R
-polynomials in Proposition
3.15
are just projections of some more general monotonicity properties for the Kazhdan–Lusztig
R
-polynomials. We give a partial negative answer below.

We show through a counterexample that the measures
\mathbb{P}_{v}(u)
do not exhibit the desired stochastic monotonicity relative to the Bruhat ordering on
v
.

Example 5.11
.

Consider the symmetric group
S_{3}
. We will show that even though
321>231
in the Bruhat ordering, the measure
\mathbb{P}_{321}
does not stochastically dominate
\mathbb{P}_{231}
. We first compute the polynomials
R_{\bullet,231}
and
R_{\bullet,321}
to obtain

\displaystyle R_{123,231}(q)

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{2};

\displaystyle R_{132,231}(q)=q-1;

\displaystyle R_{213,231}(q)

\displaystyle=q-1;

\displaystyle R_{231,231}(q)=1,

and

\displaystyle R_{123,321}(q)

\displaystyle=(q-1)(q^{2}-q+1);

\displaystyle R_{132,321}(q)=(q-1)^{2};

\displaystyle R_{213,321}(q)

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{2};

\displaystyle R_{231,321}(q)=q-1;

\displaystyle R_{312,321}(q)

\displaystyle=q-1;

\displaystyle R_{321,321}(q)=1.

It follows that for all
0<q<1
,

\mathbb{P}_{321}(123)=q^{3}(q^{-1}-1)(q^{-2}-q^{-1}+1)>\mathbb{P}_{231}(123)=q^{2}(q^{-1}-1)^{2}.

This implies that
\mathbb{P}_{321}
does not stochastically dominate
\mathbb{P}_{231}
.

Appendix A
Full censoring inequality for
b_{1}+b_{2}\leq 1

In this appendix we will outline how one can prove the full censoring inequality in the case of
b_{1}+b_{2}\leq 1
. We first note that for parameters
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
satisfying
b_{1}+b_{2}>1
the full analogue of Theorem
1.10
does not hold, since additional updates can move one further away from stationarity, as the following example shows.

Example A.1
.

Let
b_{1}+b_{2}>1
.
Then, for the censoring schemes

\mathcal{C}_{1}=\{(-1,0),(1,0),(0,1),(2,1)\},\quad\mathcal{C}_{2}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\{(1,1)\}\,,

the law of
\eta_{2}
under the censoring of
\mathcal{C}_{1}
is strictly dominated by the law of
\eta_{2}
under the censoring of
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, even though
\mathcal{C}_{1}\subsetneq\mathcal{C}_{2}
.
Indeed, the first one has probability
(1-b_{1})^{2}+b_{1}b_{2}
to be in minimal state
\bm{1}_{x>0}
, while the second has probability
1-b_{1}
to be in this state.
For
b_{1}+b_{2}>1
it holds that
(1-b_{1})<(1-b_{1})^{2}+b_{1}b_{2}
.

The following theorem is the censoring inequality stated for the stochastic six-vertex model.
Its proof will closely follow
[
Lac16
]
, except for the proof of the monotonicity property Lemma
A.4
, which proceeds via a coupling particular to the stochastic six-vertex model.

Theorem A.2
.

Let
b_{1}\leq b_{2}
satisfy
b_{1}+b_{2}\leq 1
and let
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\tilde{\eta}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the
\mathcal{C}
-censored and the
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
-censored stochastic six-vertex processes respectively, where
\mathcal{C}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
.
Then

\mathcal{L}(\tilde{\eta}_{t})\preceq\mathcal{L}(\eta_{t})\preceq\pi

and

\|\mathcal{L}(\tilde{\eta}_{t})-\pi\|_{\textrm{TV}}\geq\|\mathcal{L}(\eta_{t})-\pi\|_{\textrm{TV}}\,.

The proof of this will follow from the following two properties of the
P_{k}
.

Lemma A.3
.

If
b_{1}+b_{2}\leq 1
the following holds.
Let
\nu
be a measure on
A
such that the Radon–Nikodym derivative
\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}
of
\nu
with respect to
\pi
is a decreasing function on
A
. Then, for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
the Radon–Nikodym derivative
\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{k}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}
is also a decreasing function and
\nu\preceq P_{k}\nu
.

Proof.

Note that since
\pi
is proportional to
q^{|\lambda|}
,
\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}
being decreasing means that for any
\lambda
such that
\lambda^{k}>\lambda
we have

\nu(\lambda^{k})\leq q\nu(\lambda)\,.

After applying
P_{k}
we have

\displaystyle P_{k}\nu(\lambda^{k})-qP_{k}\nu(\lambda)

\displaystyle=b_{1}\nu(\lambda)+(1-b_{2})\nu(\lambda^{k})-q(b_{2}\nu(\lambda^{k})+(1-b_{1})\nu(\lambda))

\displaystyle=(1-b_{1}-b_{2})(\nu(\lambda^{k})-q\nu(\lambda))\leq 0\,.

Notice that the same calculation shows that
P_{k}\nu(\lambda^{k})\geq\nu(\lambda^{k})
, since
P_{k}\nu(\lambda)+P_{k}\nu(\lambda^{k})=\nu(\lambda)+\nu(\lambda^{k})
.
Note that
P_{k}\nu
and
\nu
only differ at partitions
\lambda
such that
\lambda^{k}\neq\lambda
and at these points
P_{k}\nu
always assigns more mass to the bigger partition, so we also get
\nu\preceq P_{k}\nu
.

∎

Lemma A.4
.

If
b_{1}+b_{2}\leq 1
and
\nu_{1}\preceq\nu_{2}
then
P_{k}\nu_{1}\preceq P_{k}\nu_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\chi
be a coupling of
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
such that for
\eta^{1}_{0},\eta^{2}_{0}
sampled according to
\chi
we have that
\eta^{1}_{0}\leq\eta^{2}_{0}
.
Let
\eta^{1}_{1}
and
\eta^{2}_{1}
be obtained from
\eta^{1}_{0}
and
\eta^{2}_{0}
respectively by applying the dynamics described in Definition
2.2
, i.e., the values
(\eta^{1}_{0}(k),\eta^{1}_{0}(k+1))
are swapped with probability
b_{1}
if they are
(0,1)
and with probability
b_{2}
if they are
(1,0)
.
We will now construct a coupling of
\eta^{1}_{1}
and
\eta^{2}_{1}
such that
\eta^{1}_{1}\leq\eta^{2}_{1}
holds, which shows that
P_{k}\nu_{1}\preceq P_{k}\nu_{2}
.
Firstly, if
(\eta^{1}_{0}(k),\eta^{1}_{0}(k+1))=(\eta^{2}_{0}(k),\eta^{2}_{0}(k+1))
, sample
\eta^{1}_{1}
and
\eta^{2}_{1}
such that
(\eta^{1}_{1}(k),\eta^{1}_{1}(k+1))=(\eta^{2}_{1}(k),\eta^{2}_{1}(k+1))
as well.
Further if
(\eta^{1}_{0}(k),\eta^{1}_{0}(k+1),\eta^{2}_{0}(k),\eta^{2}_{0}(k+1))=(0,1,1,0)
sample
(\eta^{1}_{1}(k),\eta^{1}_{1}(k+1),\eta^{2}_{1}(k),\eta^{2}_{1}(k+1))
according to

\mathbb{P}\big((\eta^{1}_{1}(k),\eta^{1}_{1}(k+1),\eta^{2}_{1}(k),\eta^{2}_{1}(k+1))=x\big)=\begin{cases}b_{1}\text{, if }x=(1,0,1,0)\\
b_{2}\text{, if }x=(0,1,0,1)\\
1-b_{1}-b_{2}\text{, if }x=(0,1,1,0)\\
0\text{, if }x=(1,0,0,1)\,.\end{cases}

One can easily check that this has the correct marginals.
Importantly the event that a particle moves to the left in
\eta^{1}
, while a particle moves to the right in
\eta^{2}
at the same time is
0
, which would be the only case in which the ordering between
\eta^{1}
and
\eta^{2}
could be broken.
∎

Given these two lemmas Theorem
A.2
now follows as in
[
Lac16
, Section A.2]
.
We repeat the argument here for completeness.

Proof of Theorem
A.2
.

By Proposition
2.4
, what we need to show is that for all
N

(26)

\prod_{n=1}^{N}\hat{P}_{k_{n}}\delta_{\bm{1}_{x>0}}\preceq\prod_{n=1}^{N}P_{k_{n}}\delta_{\bm{1}_{x>0}}\,,

where

\hat{P}_{k_{n}}=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Id}&\text{for $k_{n}$ corresponding to vertices censored in $\tilde{\mathcal{C}}$ but not in $\mathcal{C}$.}\\
P_{k_{n}}&\text{else}.\end{cases}

By Lemma
A.3
all partial products are measures with decreasing Radon–Nikodym derivatives with respect to
\pi
, since
\delta_{\bm{1}_{x>0}}
is such a measure.
In particular, this shows they are all dominated by
\pi
.

We will now prove (
26
) by induction on
N
.
Assume it has been proven for some
N
, and denote the product until
N
on the left and right hand side by
\tilde{\nu}_{N}
and
\nu_{N}
respectively.
If
\hat{P}_{k_{N+1}}
is the identity we have

\tilde{\nu}_{N+1}=\tilde{\nu}_{N}\preceq\nu_{N}\preceq P_{k_{N+1}}\nu_{N}=\nu_{N+1}\,,

where we used the induction hypothesis and Lemma
A.3
.
If on the other hand
\hat{P}_{k_{N+1}}=P_{k_{N+1}}
then

\tilde{\nu}_{N+1}\preceq\nu_{N+1}
follows from
\tilde{\nu}_{N}\preceq\nu_{N}
and Lemma
A.4
.

To obtain the statement on the total variation distance note that for decreasing measures
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
, the inequality
\nu_{1}\preceq\nu_{2}
implies that
\|\nu_{1}-\pi\|_{\textrm{TV}}\geq\|\nu_{2}-\pi\|_{\textrm{TV}}
, see
[
Lac16
, Lemma A.4]
.
∎

To compare with the proof for general
b_{1}<b_{2}
, note particularly that the parabolic Kazhdan–Lusztig R-measures
v_{\lambda}
do not generally have decreasing Radon–Nikodym derivative with respect to
\pi
.

Remark A.5
.

With the appropriate (very minor) modifications, the same proof works for the permutation-valued stochastic six-vertex model on a strip.
