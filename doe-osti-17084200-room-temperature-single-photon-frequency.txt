ABSTRACT
 Full control of photon spectral properties vital for the development of quantum devices. In
particular, transducing both the frequency and bandwidth of single photons is critical for
entanglement distribution across a hybridized quantum network. To interface with large-scale
quantum technology, photonic integrated circuit devices are beneficial with a small-footprint and
complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) compatibility.
 In this work, we propose a novel single photon frequency and bandwidth transducer using a
Raman-based quantum memory of interstitial nitrogen molecule defects in silicon nitride waveguides
(N2:SiN). Using confocal Raman spectroscopy, we show nitrogen molecule defects in CMOS-
fabricated silicon nitride. We calculate quantum memory efficiency and discuss frequency and
bandwidth transduction. Finally, we use finite-element modeling of optical waveguides to calculate
phase matching for frequency transduction between visible and telecom wavelengths. In conclusion,
we show the possibility of a high-efficiency N2:SiN quantum memory which could be used for
broadband quantum processing or networking.

1. OVERARCHING GOALS
 Full control of photon spectral properties is vital for the development of quantum devices in
areas such as distributed quantum computing, quantum networks, and quantum sensing. The ability
to convert single photon frequency and spectral bandwidth is required to fully integrate hybridized
quantum systems. For example, efficient photon generation mechanisms may have optical
frequencies in the telecom regime for low-loss transmission through optical fiber. These telecom
optical frequencies are not compatible with components such as quantum memories or qubits at a
given quantum node which often require visible frequency photons. Furthermore, photons from a
photon generation source and different species of quantum memories or qubits all have different
spectral bandwidths. For efficient entanglement distribution between quantum nodes, photons are
required to be indistinguishable in frequency and optical bandwidth. Therefore, efficiency
transduction of both frequency and bandwidth is paramount to developing hybridized quantum
networks. Furthermore, as quantum systems grow in scale and complexity, they require integrated
photonics systems making the ability to manipulate photon properties in an integrated photonic
platform essential.
 The overarching goals of this work were to understand the capabilities of a Raman-based
quantum memory for single-photon frequency and bandwidth conversion. In particular, we studied
the feasibility of using interstitial nitrogen molecules in silicon nitride waveguides (N2:SiN) in
Raman-based quantum memory architecture for on-chip tunable frequency and bandwidth
conversion of spectrally broadband single photons. In this work our goals were to:
 (1) show the possibility of high efficiency storage and retrieval of single photons in a
 N2:SiN device using computational simulations,
 (2) demonstrate frequency and bandwidth transduction capabilities and determine
 fundamental limitations in a Raman-based quantum memory,
 (3) use finite-element simulations to design N2:SiN waveguide devices, demonstrate
 wavelength flexibility of these devices, and explore the effect of fabrication
 imperfections, and
 (4) develop theory to understand the spectral purity of photons generated via Raman-
 mediated interactions, which will determine the fidelity of Raman-based quantum
 memories in N2:SiN and other solid-state systems.
 Preliminary results of goals (1)-(3) were disseminated through various presentations and
conference papers throughout the duration of this project [1-7]. The rest of this document will
supplement the information found in these documents. The results of goal (4) can be found in its
entirety in the submitted paper, Ref. [8], and will not be included in this report.

2. OVERVIEW OF APPROACH
 Plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD) grown silicon nitride (Si3N4, referred
to as SiN) waveguides are often used in integrated photonic platforms on account of their low
optical losses spanning the visible to near-infrared wavelengths and complementary metal-oxide-
semiconductor (CMOS)-compatible fabrication procedures [9][10]. During the PECVD growth
process, some nitrogen (N2) molecules used as a precursor gas are trapped in the SiN matrix [11].
The interstitial N2 molecules possess a sharp Raman-mediated emission corresponding to the
vibrational stretching mode of the N2 molecules. These N2 molecules trapped in the SiN films create
a Raman-active ensemble that can be used to store and retrieve photons with variable frequency and
bandwidth.
 The N2-defect Raman-active ensemble can be described by a Λ-style quantum memory (Fig.
1). An input signal photon (𝜔𝑖) and strong control field (𝜔𝑐) are temporally overlapped with a
frequency difference resonant (Δ𝑖) with the Raman transition of the first N2 vibrational state. In
doing so, the input signal photon is annihilated creating a collective vibrational excitation in the N2-
defect Raman-active ensemble. A subsequent retrieval field (𝜔𝑟) then annihilates the collective
excitation with the creation of an output photon (𝜔𝑜) with frequency and bandwidth determined by
the retrieval field spectral properties. The bandwidth of storage is limited by the energy splitting
between ground and storage states (up to the energy splitting of Ω = 70 THz), allowing for the
storage and conversion of large bandwidth photons. The efficiency of this protocol is proportional
to the optical depth, control field power, and inversely proportional to the frequency detuning from
the excited state [12].

 Figure 1. Energy diagram of N2:SiN quantum memory transducer. An input photon with center
frequency (𝝎𝒊) and bandwidth (𝝈𝒊) is mapped to a collective excitation of nitrogen molecule defects by a
write control pulse. The center frequency of the control pulse (𝝎𝒄) is set to satisfy the two-photon Raman

  collective excitation can be annihilated by a read control pulse to generate a single output photon with
 frequency (𝝎𝒐) and bandwidth (𝝈𝒐) determined by the properties of the read control pulse.

 By being inherently incorporated in silicon nitride, N2:SiN can by easily fabricated into
waveguides with standard CMOS-fabrication procedures. The waveguide structure allows for long
interaction lengths which in turn increases the optical depth. Furthermore, the optical mode of the
control field allows for larger control field powers without damaging the silicon nitride waveguides.
This allows for simple waveguide structures to be used instead of an optical cavity which maintains

 11

the broadband spectral bandwidth of the input and output photons. With long interaction lengths
and high control field optical powers, optical dispersion in the integrated waveguides becomes non-
negligible and must be considered when designing the quantum memory transducer. Finite-element
modeling is used to simulate wavelength-dependent dispersion parameters to find phase matching
conditions and higher-order dispersion effects that limit the efficiency of an integrated quantum
memory.
 Previous work has shown frequency and bandwidth single-photon conversion from a Raman
Λ-style memory in hydrogen gas–filled photonic crystal fibers [13] and bulk diamond [14], both of
which can be challenging to implement in on-chip integrated photonics. While bulk diamond could
in theory be fabricated into waveguides for this purpose, N2:SiN waveguides offer additional
advantages in higher optical depths and lower thermal noise. The efficiency of a diamond memory is
limited by a low optical depth, which is a fixed parameter of the diamond crystal. Conversely, the N2
number density in N2:SiN is controlled by the fabrication procedure which could be optimized to
increase the optical depth, and thus memory efficiency. Additionally, N2:SiN quantum memories will
have lower noise from thermally populated phonons arising from a larger energy splitting between
the ground state and phonon mode (70 THz for N2 molecules compared to 40 THz for diamond).
This difference results in more than an order of magnitude reduction in thermally populated
phonons at room temperature.
 Quantum frequency conversion has been routinely demonstrated in integrated photonics
chips mainly via second- and third-order nonlinear optical processes including sum and difference
frequency generation [15], [16] and four-wave-mixing Bragg scattering [17]. Similarly, photon
spectral bandwidth conversion has been demonstrated via chirped-pulse upconversion [18], alkali
atoms coupled in an optical cavity [19], rare-earth ion atoms coupled to a resonator [20], χ2
nonlinearities in periodically poled lithium niobate (PPLN) waveguides [21], and proposed in χ3
nonlinear materials by four-wave mixing [22]. A N2:SiN Raman memory could provide an alternate
method of frequency and spectral bandwidth conversion with advantages arising from: easy
integration into CMOS-compatible fabrication processes; frequency and bandwidth conversion
tunability controlled by retrieval field optical properties; stronger interactions and therefore lower
pulse energy requirements arising from a controllable optical depth; and built in time-delay capability
to reduce noise arising from four-wave mixing.
 In this report, we show the feasibility of a N2:SiN quantum memory for frequency and
bandwidth transduction of single photons. We first use confocal Raman spectroscopy to observe
and characterize CMOS-fabricated N2:SiN thin films and show possible advantages to annealing
these films. Then, we use numerical integration to solve the optical Maxwell-Bloch equations in the
Raman limit to calculate the potential efficiency of an N2:SiN quantum memory. We use these
results to discuss the potential, and limitations, of frequency and bandwidth transduction. Finally,
using finite-element modeling, we discuss the effect of long interaction length through optical
waveguides by simulating optical dispersion in this system. These results show the potential of a
N2:SiN quantum memory for on-chip transduction of broadband single-photons.

3. KEY RESULTS

3.1. Confocal Raman spectroscopy of silicon nitride thin films
 The silicon nitride studied in this work is commonly used as a waveguide routing material for
visible wavelengths for various photonic integrated circuit (PIC) applications because of the ultra-
low-loss and low autofluorescence [10], [23]. Previous work has used Fourier transform infrared
spectroscopy (FTIR) to understand hydrogen defect concentration in silicon nitride thin films [24].
Since nitrogen molecules do not possess a dipole moment, FTIR cannot measure the presence of
interstitial nitrogen molecule defects. Raman spectroscopy, however, is sensitive to interstitial
nitrogen molecule defects and is used in this study to qualitatively detect nitrogen molecule defects
along with the more routinely studies hydrogen defects.
 Amorphous silicon nitride thin films of nominal 300 nm thickness were grown in a 200 mm
wafer CMOS foundry using plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD) on a silicon
substrate both with and without a silicon dioxide (SiO2) under-cladding. Confocal Raman
spectroscopy was used to determine defects in the silicon nitride which arise from incomplete
reaction of the precursor gases [24]. Raman Stokes scattering in silicon nitride samples were
measured using a WITec alpha300 series confocal Raman microscope. Approximately 100 uW CW
laser light was used as the pump at 532 nm wavelength and focused onto the sample surface with a
100x microscope objective. Scattered Stokes light was collected using the same microscope
objective.

broadened peak suggests the nitrogen molecule ensemble will have a longer T2 coherence time than

 We now look at the influence of a SiO2 layer between the silicon wafer and PECVD
deposited silicon nitride in Figure 3. The silicon nitride grown on top on the oxide layer shows
additional peaks at 2920 cm-1 and 3680 cm-1 which could be from a mixing of the silicon nitrogen
and silicon dioxide at the interface with the initial growth of the nitride. This SiOxNy interface layer
has been seen with other silicon nitride growth and is believed to contribute to the creation of single
photon emitters in silicon nitride upon high temperature annealing [25]. The added silicon dioxide
layer also adds a broad background to the Raman spectra, especially for wavenumbers of less than
2600 cm-1. We attribute this background to a slight autofluorescence in the SiOxNy interface layer or
the SiO2 under-cladding. The additional autofluorescence and SiOxNy interface layer are not fully
understood and should be the subject of additional study.

Finally, we study the effect of annealing on silicon nitride defects. Samples were subjected to rapid
thermal annealing (RTA) bakes in argon. We ramped the temperature of the RTA to 250°C for a
minute before baking at the final annealing temperature for 30 seconds. The Raman spectroscopy
results of the annealed samples are shown in Fig. 4. We observe the N2 vibrational peak at 2323 cm-1
remains with a potential slight narrowing of the linewidth. We also observe a slight decrease in Si-H
defects concentration as shown previously [26]. This shows that annealing the silicon nitride film
could be a way to remove interference from Si-H defects in a Raman-based quantum memory which
would increase the decoherence of the system. However, the small variation we see could be a result
of heterogenous variation across the wafer, so further investigation on the annealing effects needs to
be completed.

3.1.2. Spectroscopy conclusions
 We performed Raman spectroscopy measurements in CMOS-fabricated PECVD thin film
silicon nitride. We observe a strong, narrow peak at 2323 cm-1 corresponding to high density
interstitial nitrogen molecules present in the as-grown silicon nitride thin film. The defects are
present in as-grown silicon nitride films without additional processing steps such as annealing.
Therefore, the N2:SiN quantum memory device can be easily integrated into a CMOS-foundry
without adding any additional processing steps.
 We also look at other hydrogen defects in silicon nitride thin films. As shown previously, Si-
H and N-H defects are present in our CMOS-fabricated silicon nitride. The overlap of the Si-H peak
and N2 peak will likely negatively affect the coherence lifetime of the quantum memory device. This
can potentially be mitigated by annealing the films at the expense of CMOS-compatibility of any
additional metal process layers deposited before the silicon nitride layer.

3.2. Analytic simulations of quantum memory efficiency
 In this section, we use analytic simulations to solve Raman-based quantum memory
efficiency for the N2:SiN system. Using numeric integration, we solve for storage and total efficiency
of the quantum memory across input/output photon wavelength, temporal bandwidth, interaction
length, and control pulse energy. We discuss the efficiency of using the system for frequency and
bandwidth transduction of single photons.

3.2.2. Simulated efficiency results
 We numerically simulate storage and total efficiency for broadband photon for an N2:SiN
quantum memory. In these simulations, the input and write control pulses have a 200 fs FWHM
Gaussian temporal shape corresponding to approximately 1.1 THz bandwidth. In Section 3.2.2.1, we
discuss overall efficiency and frequency conversion, so we have a 200 fs read control pulse width. In
Section 3.2.2.2, we discuss the effect of changing the output bandwidth via the read control pulse
properties.
 The atomic ensemble is comprised of interstitial nitrogen molecules discussed in Section 3.1.
The storage state |𝑠⟩ is the N2 vibrational mode with |𝑔⟩⟷|𝑠⟩ energy of 0.2895 eV (70 THz). The |
𝑔⟩⟷|𝑒⟩ transition energy is ~7.351 eV which sets our two-photon detuning (Δ) and has
spontaneous decay rate, 𝛾 = 8.33e4 Hz [30]. From our Raman spectroscopy measurements of
N2:SiN thin films, we estimate an order of magnitude interstitial N2 concentration of 1018 cm-3 which
is used along with the waveguide interaction length to calculate the optical depth [31]. This leads to
exceptionally large optical depths from both a N2 number concentration and interaction lengths
orders of magnitude larger than more traditional alkali atomic-vapor cell ensembles used for Raman-
based quantum memories [32].

3.2.2.1. Frequency conversion results ignoring spin wave decay
 By numerically integrating Equations 3 and 5, we determine the storage and total efficiency
of our quantum memory. We calculate both the total efficiency for retrieval in the forward and
backward direction. In the forward retrieval configuration, the signal, write control, read control, and
output all propagate in the same +𝑧 direction. The backward retrieval configuration reverses
direction of the read process, where the read control and output photon instead propagate in the ―
𝑧 direction. In Raman-based quantum memories, retrieval in the forward direction is often limited
by reabsorption of the output photon as it is created and propagates out of the quantum memory
[12], [27], [28]. This reabsorption limitation is mitigated with a backward retrieval scheme. However,
phase matching and excitation lifetime considerations can be a considerable hurdle toward
implementing a backward retrieval scheme. In this section, we assume perfect phase matching and
no spin state decoherence in both forward and backward total efficiency calculations.
 The efficiency and fidelity of a Raman-based quantum memory depend strongly on the
Raman coupling coefficient 𝐶 which is proportional to optical depth, 𝑑, control pulse energy, 𝑊,
exciting state spontaneous decay, 𝛾, and inversely proportional to the two-photon detuning, Δ [27],
[29]. Since 𝛾 is fixed by the N2 molecule, we look at the three remaining parameters in context of
our quantum memory.

 Since the Raman protocol relies on far off-resonant two-photon absorption, a N2:SiN
quantum memory can interact with a wide range of photon wavelengths. With the large nitrogen
molecule excited-state energy, the N2 ensemble can store and retrieve photons into ultraviolet
wavelength regime while maintaining the Δ ≫ 𝛾 adiabatic condition. However, since nitrogen
molecules are trapped in silicon nitride, the quantum memory is limited to wavelengths ≳ 400 nm
due to absorption of the silicon nitride waveguide host [9]. The transparency window of silicon
nitride extends into the mid-infrared, so hypothetically, the quantum memory can store and/or
retrieve photons with wavelengths extending throughout the visible to mid-infrared region.
 Figure 5 shows simulated quantum memory efficiency when interfacing with photons from
visible to telecom wavelengths. Here, the stored and retrieved photons have identical wavelengths.
As the wavelength increases, both storage and retrieval efficiency decreases corresponding to a larger
two-photon detuning from the excited state. Because the N2 excited state energy is much larger than
the bandgap of silicon nitride, the detuning is substantially large at all visible to telecom wavelengths.
Therefore, the decrease in efficiency is not catastrophic to the quantum memory performance and
can be mitigated by increasing the interaction length or increasing the control field power.
 The storage and retrieval efficiencies are largely the same if we are storing and retrieving
photons of different wavelengths up to a small change in Δ between the write and read process. This
opens the possibility of frequency transduction where the wavelength of the stored photon differs
from the retrieved photon. Of particular interest is frequency transduction from visible to telecom
wavelengths to interface qubits or long-lifetime quantum memories to long-distance communication
channels using optical fiber. In Figure 6, we simulate transduction efficiency where we store an input
visible photon with wavelength 𝜆 = 670 nm and retrieve an output telecom photon with wavelength
𝜆 = 1310 nm. The input and output photon wavelengths are not unique, as discussed above, but are
chosen out of convenience for future experimental demonstration using a standard Ti:Sapphire laser
with an optical parametric oscillator (OPO).

 When looking at bandwidth transduction, it becomes essential to include the effect of spin

the output photon to have any different spectral bandwidth than the input photon [27], [29].

will generally reduce the efficiency if the temporal bandwidth is longer than the coherence lifetime.

3.2.3. Simulated efficiency conclusions
 We solved the optical Maxwell-Bloch equations in the Raman-limit and simulated storage
and total efficiency of a N2:SiN quantum memory. The efficiency strongly depends on the Raman
coupling coefficient which is a function of the input/output photon wavelength, interaction length,
and optical control power. We discussed each one of these parameters in turn and showed how we
can get large storage and frequency transduction efficiency of single photons for single photons
spanning the transparency window of silicon nitride including visible to telecom transduction.
Finally, by including spin wave dephasing in the Maxwell-Bloch solution, we discussed limits of
bandwidth transduction using this system.

Dispersion is considered normal when 𝐷 < 0 and anomalous when 𝐷 > 0. Normal dispersion is
the most common situation for bulk materials and occurs when the group velocity decreases with
increasing optical frequency. By confining the optical mode in certain waveguide geometries,
materials can exhibit anomalous dispersion across various spectral regions. Anomalous dispersion is
required for one of the optical pulse center wavelengths to satisfy the phase matching condition (Eq.
6). Ideally, we want to find conditions where the high intensity control pulses experience normal
dispersion with either the input or output photon wavelength in anomalous dispersion regime. That
configuration would limit the effect of spectral broadening and supercontinuum generation while
still allowing the phase matching condition to be satisfied.

3.3.2. Simulation results with air cladding
 We first look at the simulated results across waveguide width and height for an air clad
waveguide. Figure 10 plots the dispersion parameter for waveguides of a constant width, 𝑤 = 1000
nm, and the constant height, ℎ = 520 nm. All the waveguide geometries shown exhibit anomalous
dispersion (𝐷 > 0) for some wavelength region between 𝜆 = 700 nm and 𝜆 = 1600 nm. This
implies that satisfying the phase matching condition is possible for some combination of input and
output photon wavelengths. We observe larger anomalous dispersion across a larger wavelength
region with increased waveguide height. When waveguide width increases, the anomalous dispersion
region red shifts to longer wavelengths and decreases in amplitude. More narrow waveguides do not
support optical modes with telecom wavelengths. Additionally in waveguide widths greater than
1300 nm, the anomalous dispersion region disappears. Therefore, careful waveguide simulation and
choice of waveguide geometry parameters are essential to satisfy phase matching for frequency
transduction.

3.3.4. Finite-element modeling conclusions
 High storage and retrieval efficiency is possible in this quantum memory since N2:SiN can
easily be integrated into waveguides. The waveguide architecture and long interaction lengths require
dispersion engineering to satisfy the phase matching condition for efficiency retrieval. We have
shown this is possible by carefully designing waveguide geometry to achieve phase matching for
frequency conversion between input and output photons. We showed devices that are designed for
large-scale frequency conversion between visible and telecom wavelengths and how waveguide
geometry and cladding affects phase matched wavelengths. For these devices, we discussed GVM
and showed how long ~1 cm effective interaction lengths are possible for these devices.
 Waveguide geometry is an essential first step to satisfying phase matching. We found thick
waveguides show more anomalous dispersion, which is essential for phase matching. The thickness
of waveguides and silicon nitride stress and etching capabilities need to be balanced and very thick
waveguides may not be easily fabricated. Waveguide width can also be used to generate anomalous
dispersion but can have multiple modes supported in wide waveguides. However, both parameters
can be adjusted to manipulate dispersion and satisfy the phase matching condition for desired
wavelengths. Similar dispersion manipulations are likely by heat or strain tuning of the waveguides
but was not explored in this work. Additional phase matched conditions can be found by
considering mode or polarization transduction, with potential loss in efficiency from reducing mode
overlaps between the storage and retrieval pulses and spin wave mode.

4. FUTURE NEEDS
 First and foremost, an experimental demonstration of a N2:SiN quantum memory device
needs to be shown. As a proof-of-principle demonstration, a Ti:Sapphire laser with attached optical
parametric oscillator (OPO) can be used to create time-synchronous THz bandwidth beams which
can be used for the input beam, write control pulse, and read control pulse (Fig. 18). A pulse shaper
on the read control beam path can be inserted to demonstrate small-scale bandwidth conversion
capability of the quantum memory. In this setup, the input pulse will be a weak coherent state with
on average one photon per pulse, which does not change the underlying mechanism of the quantum
memory. Follow-up experiments can replace the weak coherent state with a true single photon
source.

 Figure 17. Proposed experimental diagram. A weak coherent state is used as the input photon to
 demonstrate wavelength transduction from visible to telecom wavelengths.

Future work will need to be done to understand the lifetime and decoherence mechanism of the
nitrogen collective vibrational mode to optimize the utility of this device. The lifetime of this
excitation determines how long a single photon can be stored while maintaining the quantum
properties with high fidelity. We have also shown how the excitation lifetime limits the ability of the
quantum memory to transduce the bandwidth of single photons and its ability to interface with
narrowband state-of-the-art quantum nodes.
 Once the lifetime and decoherence mechanisms are known for the collective excitation,
mechanisms can be developed, simulated, and tested to mitigate decoherence and increase the
excitation lifetime. The mitigation techniques could potentially look like phonon engineering to
reduce phonon relaxation pathways in the silicon nitride, operating a low-temperature to reduce
thermal occupation of low-energy phononic states, or engineering integrated cavity structures.
 Finally, we will need to integrate our quantum memory with other PIC devices. These
devices include on-chip lasers of appropriate wavelengths, photonic control devices to set the timing
and phase optical pumps, wavelength division multiplexing devices to connect three colored optical
pulses into the quantum memory, and pump suppression devices to remove unwanted pump beams
from the final output. Integrating these devices onto one chip will allow this device to be deployed
into quantum networks used for distributed quantum computing, fielded quantum sensors, and
quantum communication platforms.

5. CONCLUSIONS
 This report demonstrated our progress toward developing a new method of single photon
frequency and bandwidth conversion using a Raman-based quantum memory of interstitial nitrogen
molecule defects in silicon nitride waveguides. In this approach, a single input photon is mapped
into the N2 ensemble via an off-resonant two-photon transition with a strong write optical control
pulse. The excitation is retrieved by a read control pulse generating an output photon by annihilating
the collective excitation. The frequency and bandwidth of the output photon depend on the
characteristics of the read control pulse, not the spectral characteristics of the input pulse, allowing
for spectral manipulation of the single photon. By developing a Raman-based quantum memory out
of defects in silicon nitride, we can control the optical depth, have long interaction lengths, and
implement high control pulse energies which will increase the efficiency of our device.
 We used confocal Raman spectroscopy to find and characterize N2 defects in CMOS-
fabricated silicon nitride without any additional processing steps required. We found high
concentration of N2 defects along with hydrogen defects in the form of Si-H and N-H bonds. When
annealing the silicon nitride films, we saw a decrease in Si-H bonding without a significant change in
the N2 peak, which provides a possible mechanism to reduce any detrimental effects from an
overlap of the Si-H and N2 peaks. Finally, we looked at deposition with and without a bottom SiO2
layer and found a potential SiOxNy layer forming at the interface with small autofluorescence. The
effect of this interfacial layer is still unknown and will be the subject of future study.
 Next, we used numerical integration to solve the optical Maxell-Bloch equations in the
Raman-limit to calculate quantum memory efficiency. We found high storage and retrieval efficiency
is possible with centimeter-long waveguides and nanojoule control pulse energies. The effect of
input and output photon wavelength, interaction length, and control pulse energy was discussed.
Our simulations showed the potential of a N2:SiN quantum memory to be used for wavelength
transduction between visible and telecom wavelengths with bandwidth transduction limited by the
lifetime of the N2 collective excitation.
 In our last section, we used finite-element modeling to calculate the wavelength-dependent
dispersion of various waveguide geometry and claddings. We looked at the difference between air
cladding and air polishing, where the anomalous dispersion region is larger with an air cladding. We
then looked at a taper etch and silicon nitride over etch into the oxide under-cladding and how that
affects the dispersion. We showed how with careful waveguide dispersion engineering, we can
develop phase matched N2:SiN waveguides for efficient wavelength transduction between visible
and telecom wavelengths with low group velocity mismatch.
 Overall, we demonstrated that an N2:SiN quantum memory transducer can be developed
using CMOS-compatible fabrication techniques. Such a device has flexibility in the wavelengths used
for the input and output photons, and with certain waveguide geometry, can have wavelength
tunability. Wide-range wavelength transduction is possible in this system with limited bandwidth
transduction, with potential improvement possible in future work. This device provides a low-cost
mechanism to efficiently convert the frequency and bandwidth of single photons in an integrated
photonic platform which will have significant impact on the development large-scale quantum
networking technologies.
