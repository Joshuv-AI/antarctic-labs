65 TOPS optoelectronic multi-core computing unlocking multi-feature fusion enhancement
Authors: Xiangyan Meng, Junshen Li, Menghan Yang, Kangwei Fei, Yanzhen Li, Wei Li, Jianping Yao, Ninghua Zhu, Nuannuan Shi, Ming Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid progress of artificial intelligence demands scalable, energy-efficient hardware with massive parallelism and high throughput. Although optical computing offers a promising post-Moore solution, current implementations face speed limitations and multi-core integration challenges. Here, we propose a high-throughput optical processing unit (OPU) that simultaneously exploits coherent interference, wavelength-division multiplexing, and spatial parallelism to achieve revolutionary performance gains. Integrating four optical analog cores on a monolithic chip, the OPU supports 124-channel parallel task processing, achieving 65.04 trillion operations per second (TOPS) computational speed and 5.16 TOPS/mm2 compute density. Leveraging this OPU platform, an optoelectronic convolutional neural network (OE-CNN) is constructed that fuses the OPU’s parallel 4-kernel convolution and average pooling operations with electronic nonlinear activation and fully connection operations. This OE-CNN, empowered by multi-feature fusion through 4-kernel parallel convolution, achieves a 95.08% MNIST classification accuracy—representing a 9.20% improvement over its single-core counterpart. The OPU demonstration achieves multi-core parallel operation and accelerated computational speed, establishing a scalable hardware foundation for optoelectronic many-core intelligent computing.Subject terms: Microwave photonics, Integrated optics

Introduction

In recent years, the rapid advancement of large-scale artificial intelligence (AI) has led to an exponential surge in data processing throughput, imposing high demands on computational speed and energy efficiency1,2. In particular, many-core processors, which are designed to deliver massive parallelism, have emerged as pivotal components in addressing these demands. However, they are presently limited by both the architectural bottlenecks of digital computing—such as the memory wall and power walls—and the physical slowdown of Moore’s Law3–5. As a result, their performance improvements have gradually approached the physical limits. Consequently, optical analog computing, with its high parallelism, low transmission latency, and natural suitability for linear operations, is regarded as a promising solution to overcome electronic digital computational bottlenecks in the “post-Moore era”6–14. Recent developments in photonic integration technology have accelerated research progress in optical analog computing chips, with notable achievements in the architectures of microring resonators (MRRs)15,16, Mach‒Zehnder interferometers (MZIs)17–19, phase-change materials (PCMs)20,21 and multimode interferometers (MMIs)22. These optical analog computing chips have demonstrated continuous improvements in computational speed, computational precision, and energy efficiency.

While optical analog computing demonstrates promising prospects, substantial improvements in computational speed remain necessary, and its computational capabilities in neural networks still need to be further expanded to enable widespread adoption. On the one hand, advancements in single-core computational speed remain imperative. Several approaches have adopted wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) to increase computational parallelism21,23–28. However, relying on multiple wavelengths for a single matrix operation, these methods still underutilize light’s natural parallelism, restricting their potential for high-speed optical computation. On the other hand, exploration of photonics multi-core spatial parallel computing to improve computational speed is vital. Current optical analog computing chips predominantly adopt single-core verification29–32, lacking comprehensive exploration of scalable on-chip many-core parallel computing architectures, which are fundamental in electronic processors. Further efforts are needed to unlock the potential of multi-core spatial parallel computing. In addition to computational speed, the absence of optical pooling operations significantly hampers the advancement of optoelectronic convolutional neural networks (OE-CNNs). As a critical function of convolutional neural networks, pooling operations serve multiple roles, including reducing computational complexity, enhancing model robustness, preventing overfitting, and expanding the receptive field33. However, most existing optical OE-CNNs have yet to effectively integrate this function34, which significantly limits the scalability of OE-CNNs.

Here, we present a monolithic optical processing unit (OPU) integrating four optical analog cores on a silicon-on-insulator (SOI) platform. Each core implements wavelength-specific convolution operations through the interference effect, utilizing on-chip coherent superposition of transmission and interference phases for precise optical weighting. Furthermore, each core enables synchronous parallel operation across 31 WDM channels, implementing parallel data distribution and concurrent multi-task processing. The designed four-core OPU, which harnesses coherent interference, spectral multiplexing, and spatial parallelism to improve computation throughput, supports 124-channel parallel task processing, achieving a peak computational speed of 65.04 trillion operations per second (TOPS), a compute density of 5.16 TOPS/mm2 and an energy efficiency of 1.50 pJ/OPS. Moreover, an OE-CNN is experimentally demonstrated that combines the OPU’s four-kernel parallel convolution and average pooling operations with electronic nonlinear activation and fully connected operations, enabling seamless hybrid computation. Benefiting from multi-feature fusion enhancement through four-kernel parallel convolutional operations, the network achieves an accuracy of 95.08% on the MNIST handwritten digit classification, outperforming single-core implementations by 9.20% (85.88%). It marks a significant step forward in the exploration of high-throughput, multi-core parallel optical computing chips, paving the way for the realization of next-generation high-performance AI computing platforms.

Results

Principle

Due to its inherent massive parallelism, optical analog computing exhibits highly attractive advantages over electronic digital computing. The comparison between electronic digital multi-core computing and optical analog multi-core computing, both employing the same number of computing cores, is shown in Fig. 1a. In electronic digital computing, a single weight element and a single data element are fed into one digital computing core within one data cycle, where they undergo one addition or multiplication operation. As a result, with m electronic cores, m operations can be completed in a single data cycle. In optical analog computing, a weight matrix (containing n elements) and a data matrix (containing n elements) are simultaneously passed into one optical analog core to perform multiplication and accumulation within one data cycle. This process enables the execution of 2n operations per data cycle via single-wavelength operation. Leveraging parallel processing with k independent wavelengths and m optical analog cores to simultaneously handle multiple data streams achieves a peak computational speed of 2nkm operations per data cycle. Consequently, when operating with the same number of computing cores, the optical analog computing core achieves a 2nk folds enhancement in computational speed for linear matrix operations compared to its electronic digital counterpart.

Built upon the multi-core optical analog computing architecture, the principle of optical linear matrix computing implemented by the proposed OPU is shown in Fig. 1b. The proposed OPU function is presented by explaining matrix operation with a single wavelength in one optical analog core. Initially, data X1=[x11x12⋯x1n] (using optical analog core 1 in Fig. 1b as an example) are temporally serialized and loaded onto the intensity of an optical carrier with a data rate of f Baud, which is then fed into the optical analog core.

The computational workflow of vector multiplication within one optical analog core is shown in Fig. 1c. The data-loaded optical carrier is first split into n equal-power branches via an MMI. These branches then pass through varying lengths of optical delay lines, introducing delays of T0,Δt+T0,...,(n−1)Δt+T0. T0 is the transmission delay of the shortest branch, and Δt is the delay difference between adjacent branches, which corresponds to one data cycle, i.e., Δt=1/f. After the time delay, the temporally serialized adjacent n data elements in X1 are simultaneously fed into n weight units within the same data cycle (the n-th data cycle).

Each weight unit is implemented using an MZI and represents one element in W1. The MZI is implemented with two optical couplers (OCs) and two-phase shifters (PSs). The inner PS, located between two OCs, is used to adjust the weight vector W1=(w11w21...wn1) through the tuning of the interference phase. Meanwhile, the outer PS located after the second OC is used to adjust the transmission phase, achieving in-phase alignment between different branches. By leveraging the interference effect of light, each MZI attenuates the optical intensity of one branch according to one element in the weight vector. The interference phase Δφ between the two arms of an MZI ranges from 0 to π, corresponding to a weight value in W1 varying from 1 to 0 in a nonlinear manner of wi1∝cos(Δφ/2). After transmission through the second OC in MZIs, element-wise multiplications are performed, yielding the values of x11w11,x12w21,...,x1nwn1. After tuning the transmission phases in each branch using the outer PS for in-phase alignment, the optical signals are coherently superimposed via the next MMI, summing the multiplied data to obtain the vector multiplication result. Multi-core OPU fabrication and experimental setup

The multi-core OPU is fabricated on a 200 mm SOI wafer with a top Si thickness of 220 nm and a buried oxide thickness of 3 μm. The microscope image of OPU is shown in Fig. 2a with a size of 2.8 mm × 4.5 mm = 12.60 mm2. The proposed OPU monolithically integrates four spatially parallel analog cores, with each one realizing a 1 × 9 weight vector. Within each core, eight helically structured optical waveguides of progressively increasing lengths introduce a one-data-cycle temporal delay between adjacent branches. The magnified microscope images of the optical delay lines and MZIs are shown in Fig. 2b. The PS is thermo-optically tuned and utilizes TiN as the heating electrode. It employs the substrate undercut technique for heat insulation, improving thermal tuning efficiency while reducing thermal crosstalk. The packaged chip is shown in Fig. 2c. Optical input and output coupling is achieved via fiber arrays aligned to edge couplers, with an insertion loss of approximately 2 dB/facet. The DC electrodes are connected via PCB adapters, and a thermoelectric cooler (TEC) is integrated below the chip, with a temperature control accuracy of 0.001 °C, ensuring that the chip operates at a constant temperature.

The thermal response of the MZI is shown in Fig. 2d, with an efficiency of 1.66 mW/π. Figure 2e shows the measured C-band spectral response of optical analog core 1 when programmed with a uniform weight vector (all elements set to 1), exhibiting a minimum insertion loss of 16.34 dB. The optical response exhibits periodic spectral notches with a frequency spacing of 29.14 GHz between adjacent peaks, corresponding to a temporal delay of 34.32 ps between adjacent branches (details about delay measurement can be seen in Supplementary Note 2). This translates to a data input rate of 29.14 GBaud. Accordingly, the wavelength interval is chosen as 0.94 nm (i.e., four times of 29.14 GHz), which ensures spectral alignment between the WDM channels and the OPU’s spectral response and avoids crosstalk between WDM channels. The higher insertion loss around 1542 nm and 1558 nm (Fig. 2e) is caused by the unequal arm lengths of the MZIs used for weight tuning, which is discussed in detail in Supplementary Note 3. Additionally, the spectral response of the four cores can be seen in Supplementary Note 4.

The experimental setup for linear matrix operation using the packaged four-core OPU is shown in Fig. 3. For each core, an optical carrier is generated from a laser diode and fed into an intensity modulator (IM) through a polarization controller. Digital data are transformed into a high-speed electronic analog signal through an arbitrary waveform generator (AWG). Then, the electric analog signal is fed into an IM, encoding the data onto the optical carrier via amplitude modulation. After that, the data-loaded optical carrier is sent to one optical analog core of the OPU. Within the OPU, weight matrices are dynamically reconfigurable via a computer-controlled multi-channel voltage source, which delivers precisely tuned electrical signals to on-chip microelectrodes, thereby modulating the refractive indices of waveguides through the thermo-optic effect. After transmitting through the OPU core, the process of vector multiplication of data and weight is accomplished. Then, the optical signal is fed to a semiconductor optical amplifier (SOA) to compensate for the optical power loss and converted to an electrical signal through an optical receiver. Finally, the electrical signal is recorded using an oscilloscope (OSC) and transferred to a computer, where a square-root operation is first performed to recover the linear convolution result (Supplementary Eq. (8)), after which the final computational output is extracted. The experimental data flow is controlled and processed by a computer, where the AWG and OSC communicate with the computer via the VISA protocol, while the multi-channel voltage source connects through serial communication. Additionally, clock synchronization is required between the AWG and OSC.

WDM-based multi-task optical convolutional operation

When conducting convolution operations to extract the features of images with the proposed OPU, the 2-D input images are flattened into 1-D data (details about data preprocessing can be found in Supplementary Note 5) and input to the OPU. In the experiment, the images from the USC-SIPI image database (which also includes two images from the DAVIS dataset35) are utilized for experimental demonstration. Limited by the data record length of AWG, images are down-sampled to 224 × 224 pixels. Since each core in the OPU supports the vector multiplication of 1 × 9, which is functionally equivalent to 3 × 3 convolution operations, the input image is partitioned into 222 sub-matrices with a size of 3 × 224. Each 3 × 224 sub-matrix is temporally flattened into a 1 × 672 sub-vector. These 222 sub-vectors are sequentially concatenated to form a continuous 1 × 149,184 data vector. This vector stream is processed by the OPU at 29.14 GBaud, completing the optical convolution operations in 5.12 μs. After calculating within the OPU, the significance computing values are extracted to obtain a 222 × 222 pixels feature image (details about significance value extraction can be found in Supplementary Note 5).

Figure 4 presents the parallel multi-task convolution results using optical analog core 1 in the OPU, where the convolution kernel is set as −101−101−101 to extract the right edge of the image. Due to the periodic notch characteristics of the unbalanced MZI, 24 wavelengths in the C-band exhibit low insertion loss, supporting parallel real-valued convolution operations of 24 images. Given that the input data are represented by the intensity of the optical carrier, and the convolution kernel is implemented through optical attenuation using MZIs, real-valued convolution kernels are realized by performing two data acquisitions and computing their difference. For example, to implement the real-valued convolution kernel of −101−101−101, two separate data acquisitions are performed to realize convolution kernels 001001001 and 100100100, and then the difference between the two acquired data is computed to obtain the real-valued kernel. Figure 4a shows the temporal waveforms of 24 images acquired via 24 wavelengths, with blue and orange lines denoting the theoretical and experimental results, respectively. Figure 4b presents a magnified view of the waveform at 1530.758 nm alongside its corresponding feature map. Supplementary Note 6 provides the individual waveforms obtained at 1547.324 nm from the two separate data acquisitions, along with the differential waveform used to realize the convolution kernel. To mitigate the impact of noise in the analog computing links, each computation is averaged over five consecutive acquisitions (a discussion of alternative noise-reduction methods is provided in Supplementary Note 11). The computational precision was calibrated using 49,284 results extracted at a wavelength of 1547.324 nm. The standard deviation of the error between the theoretical and experimental values is 0.0779, corresponding to a computational precision of 4.68 bits (see Supplementary Note 7 for detailed calculations and precision-improvement discussion).

The convolution operation is conducted with a convolution kernel of 001001001, and the resulting 31 temporal waveforms are provided in Supplementary Note 6. Regarding the computational speed, each optical analog core in the proposed OPU performs 2 × 9 = 18 operations in one data cycle. For a single wavelength, this yields a processing speed of 29.14 GBaud × 18 = 524.52 giga-operations per second (GOPS). Through a synergistic combination of wavelength-division multiplexing (31 wavelengths) and multi-core processing (4 cores), the OPU achieves 124-channel parallel multiplexing, and the total computational speed reaches 524.52 GOPS × 31 × 4 = 65.04 TOPS. Accordingly, the compute density of the OPU reaches 65.04 TOPS ÷ 12.60 mm2 ≈ 5.16 TOPS/mm2, and the energy efficiency is 1.50 pJ/OPS (details can be seen in Supplementary Note 8).

OE-CNN with multi-feature fusion enhancement for digit classification

Based on the proposed four-core OPU to multiply feature fusion enhancement, an OE-CNN is implemented for handwritten digit classification on the MNIST dataset36. This network integrates an optical convolution layer with an electronic fully connected layer, as depicted in Fig. 6a. The optical convolution and optical average pooling operation in the optical convolutional layer are implemented by reusing the OPU, and a four-core spatial parallel architecture is adopted to enable simultaneous multi-feature fusion processing.

During the optical convolution operations, four convolution kernels (identical to those in Fig. 5) are implemented using four optical analog cores within the OPU. Following the data preprocessing methods introduced in Supplementary Note 5, the theoretical and experimental temporal waveforms for the flattened 28 × 28 input image and the convolution results of kernel 10−110−110−1 can be seen in Fig. 6b-1, b-2. After the optical convolution operation, the significant computing values are extracted, and a 26 × 26 pixels feature image is obtained. Then, an electronic nonlinear activation is conducted with the ReLU function.

The proposed OPU also supports optical average pooling to reduce the computational amount in the electronic fully connected layer. When conducting 2 × 2 optical average pooling with the proposed OPU, the 26 × 26 pixels nonlineared image is divided into 13 sub-matrices with a size of 2 × 26. Then, each sub-matrix is flattened to a 1 × 52 sub-vector. A total of 13 sub-vectors are sequentially concatenated to form a 1 × 676 data vector and experimentally fed into the OPU again. To realize a 2 × 2 optical average pooling operation with a 1 × 9 optical analog core, the convolution kernels are set to 001001011 (details for data preprocessing and significant data extraction for the optical average pooling operation can be found in Supplementary Note 9). The experimental and theoretical temporal waveforms are shown in Fig. 6b-3, and the 13 × 13 image obtained after the average pooling operation is shown in Fig. 6b-4. For completeness, the results of the remaining three cores are provided in Supplementary Note 10.

After the optical convolution layer, one electronic fully connected layer is used for the classification task. Using 60,000 images from the MNIST training set for training and 10,000 images from the testing set for evaluation, the classification accuracy and cross-entropy loss on the MNIST test dataset over 400 training epochs are shown in Fig. 7a. Through multi-feature fusion enhancement with four spatial parallel cores, the proposed OE-CNN achieves a classification accuracy of 95.08% (the maximum value among 10 consecutive training runs). The confusion matrix for the ten-class classification of the MNIST test dataset is shown in Fig. 7b, where the horizontal axis represents the true label, while the vertical axis represents the predicted label. The values in the grid cells indicate the percentage of predictions for each class.

Benefiting from the multi-core parallel architecture of the OPU, multiple convolutional kernels can be processed concurrently, leading to a substantial improvement in OE-CNN classification accuracy. Using the network structure in Fig. 6a, we trained models with 1 to 4 convolutional kernels (corresponding to 1 to 4 optical analog cores). While keeping the number of fully connected layers unchanged, we adjusted the size of the fully connected layer to match the outputs of the convolutional layers and then evaluated the corresponding maximum classification accuracies. Similarly, we computed the maximum classification accuracies of electronic convolutional neural networks with an identical architecture serving as the theoretical reference, where the only difference lies in whether the convolution and pooling operations are performed optically. The results are shown in Fig. 7c, where the classification accuracy increases with the number of kernels. We also calculated the discrepancy between the experimental and theoretical accuracy, which decreases as the number of convolutional kernels increases. This highlights the significant advantage of optical multi-core parallel computing, enabling multi-feature fusion to enhance the recognition accuracy of OE-CNNs.

Benefiting from a coherent computing architecture that integrates on-chip delay lines for serial computation and synergizes transmission-phase and interference-phase modulation, the proposed design eliminates the extra computational overhead incurred by singular value decomposition (SVD)-based coherent computing architectures17,37. Moreover, the proposed coherent computing architecture supports multi-wavelength parallelism for concurrent multi-task processing, thereby significantly enhancing the computational speed of a single core. Furthermore, the proposed OPU adopts a four-core architecture, which not only validates the feasibility of multi-core optical computing but also further enhances the overall computational speed. To evaluate the performance of the proposed OPU, we compare it with a broad range of state-of-the-art processors, including general-purpose optical neural network chips based on MZI, MRR, PCM, and MMI, representative electronic digital processors such as the Nvidia H100, and compute-in-memory (CIM) architectures that enable single-cycle MAC operations38. The comparison, presented in Table 1, considers multiple aspects, including the single-core matrix dimension, the number of wavelengths, the number of cores, data rate, computational speed, energy efficiency, computational precision, compute density, network architecture, and classification accuracy on the MNIST handwritten digit dataset. The proposed OPU demonstrates a substantial computational speed advantage and enhanced energy efficiency compared with existing optical computing chips, highlighting the feasibility of optical multi-core acceleration. Furthermore, the OPU achieves a higher compute density than conventional electronic digital processors and outperforms CIM architectures in terms of computational speed. These results underscore its potential in high-performance computing. Although current implementations show limitations in energy efficiency and computational precision, future full on-chip integration, combined with techniques adapted from CIM architectures39–41—such as dynamic range scaling multi-cycle accumulation—could significantly enhance both metrics.

To further enhance the computational speed of OPU, two main directions can be explored: (1) increasing the number of wavelengths in WDM. The current design suffers from spectral notch issues due to the unequal-arm MZI structure, which limits the number of usable wavelengths. Adopting equal-arm MZIs can substantially increase the number of available wavelengths to 37 channels, thereby improving the computational speed of the four-core OPU to 77.63 TOPS. (2) Expanding the chip area for large-scale parallelism. The OPU presented in this work is a small-scale prototype with only four optical analog cores, occupying an area of only 12.60 mm2. In contrast, modern electronic computing chips typically have an area of more than 800 mm2, which could theoretically accommodate approximately 254 optical analog cores, potentially boosting the computational speed to over 4900 TOPS. This provides a promising and scalable solution for optoelectronic many-core computing.

The realization of optoelectronic computing systems via co-packaged optoelectronic design constitutes a pivotal step in unlocking the full computational capability of optical computing. First, for optical computing chips, the current OPU is fabricated on a passive photonic platform and only integrates the optical analog core on-chip. By shifting to an active photonic platform, it becomes possible to integrate additional components such as modulators and photodetectors on-chip and to employ an optical frequency comb as the light source. This paves the way for full on-chip integration of all optical components except the laser source. Second, in terms of optoelectronic co-packaging, the current OPU operates with separate modules for the optical computing chip, electronic control circuits, and electronic computing units. Through co-packaging of the optical computing chip, driving and control circuits, and electronic computing units, a tightly coupled optoelectronic hybrid computing system can be established. Such integration would improve device-level noise characteristics, reduce interconnect loss, and enhance thermal stability, thereby fundamentally improving computational precision. Moreover, close integration of optics and electronics enables on-chip noise-aware learning and in situ training, which can further mitigate the influence of analog computational precision on overall neural network performance. Collectively, these advances will pave the way for deep optical convolutional neural networks in practical artificial neural network applications and enable the ultrahigh computational speed of optical processors to be fully utilized.

Discussion

We demonstrated a monolithic SOI-based high-throughput OPU with four parallel optical analog cores. In the proposed OPU, optical delay lines with varying lengths are used for temporal coherent vector multiplication computing. WDM technology is employed to increase parallel data throughput, and four optical analog cores are spatially integrated in a single chip for parallel convolution operation. By harnessing coherent interference, spectral multiplexing, and spatial parallelism, the OPU supports 124-channel parallel task processing and experimentally demonstrates a computational speed of 65.04 TOPS, achieving a compute density of 5.16 TOPS/mm2 with an energy efficiency of 1.50 pJ/OPS. An OE-CNN that seamlessly contains optical convolution, optical average pooling, electronic nonlinear activation and electronic fully connected operations is applied to MNIST handwritten digit classification, achieving an accuracy of 95.08%. By employing multi-feature fusion enhancement, the four-core parallel architecture achieves a 9.20% improvement in classification accuracy over its single-core counterpart. By unifying coherent computing, WDM, and spatial parallelism, this work delivers a breakthrough in optical computing, fulfilling the scalability and performance demands of many-core computing systems.

Methods

Configuration

The proposed OPU chip was fabricated at the Advanced Micro Foundry (AMF) in Singapore. Optical computing with the proposed OPU was implemented using commercially available optoelectronic components. The laser diode is an IDPHOTONICS CoBrite-DX laser source with tunable polarization-maintaining output ports. An arbitrary waveform generator (M8196A) from Keysight is used to generate an analog modulation signal with a sample rate of 87.42 GSa/s, corresponding to a data rate of 29.14 GBaud, with each data point sampled three times. Electro-optical intensity modulators from EOSPACE (AX-0MVS-40-PFA-PFA-LV) follow the laser diode to load the image data onto the intensity of the optical carriers. A custom-built multi-channel voltage source is used to adjust the OPU. The voltage source features 96 independent channels with a voltage adjustment precision of 2 mV within a range of 0–10 V. After computing in the OPU, a semiconductor optical amplifier from THORLABS (SOA1013S) is inserted into the computing link to compensate for the optical loss. Then, the optical signal is converted to an electrical signal with a THORLABS optical receiver (RXM38AF), which has a bandwidth of 38 GHz. Finally, a high-speed oscilloscope from Keysight (UXR0402A) with a sample rate of 256 GSa/s was utilized for data recording. When increasing the data input dimensionality of optical convolution operations via WDM, the chosen wavelength spacing of 116.56 GHz does not conform to standard WDM channel spacing, making it impossible to use commercially available standard components for the experiment. To address this, we utilized a tunable laser to adjust the optical carrier wavelength and conducted measurements individually, thereby verifying the feasibility of WDM for expanding the data input dimensionality.
