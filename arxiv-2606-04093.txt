The Origin of Da Scaling: Suppressed Cooling in Fast-Cooling Mixing Layers Journal: ApJ scipy ( 44 ) , numpy ( 19 ) , matplotlib ( 20 ) , adstex ( https://github.com/yymao/adstex )
Lachlan Lancaster, Drummond Fielding, Rajsekhar Mohapatra, Greg L. Bryan

Abstract
In numerical experiments simulating Turbulent Radiative Mixing Layers (TRMLs) it is observed that as the cooling time in the mixed gas, t cool t_{\rm cool} , becomes very short compared to the dynamical time of the turbulence, t eddy / t cool ≫ 1 t_{\rm eddy}/t_{\rm cool}\gg 1 , there is a change in the scaling behavior of the total energy radiated in the TRML as a function of this ratio, also known as the Damköhler number, Da ≡ t eddy / t cool {\rm Da}\equiv t_{\rm eddy}/t_{\rm cool} , from E ˙ cool ∝ Da 1 / 2 \dot{E}_{\rm cool}\propto{\rm Da}^{1/2} to E ˙ cool ∝ Da 1 / 4 \dot{E}_{\rm cool}\propto{\rm Da}^{1/4} . The latter, so-called “fast-cooling,” regime is of particular interest as many astrophysical mixing layers lie in this regime. We demonstrate that the origin of this change is the suppression of turbulent folding of the surface by the ram-pressure of the inflowing gas, which becomes much greater than the turbulent pressure in this regime. We present an argument that reproduces the E ˙ cool ∝ Da 1 / 4 \dot{E}_{\rm cool}\propto{\rm Da}^{1/4} behavior by appealing to the suppression of the fractal structure of the interface by the ram-pressure of the inflowing gas.

These systems are characterized by (i) the turbulence in the layer, (ii) the hydrodynamic properties of the two phases, such as their temperature and density, and (iii) the details of the radiative processes that take place in the mixed material. In the

A_{\rm int}\approx L^{2}
(the actual interface area, on the smallest scales, should in fact be larger e.g.

v_{\rm diff}=\sqrt{\frac{v_{t}(L_{\rm int})L_{\rm int}}{t_{\rm cool}}}\,.

In the

The interface is then not able to be smoothed out by the turbulence but remains thin while the turbulence gives it multi-scale, ‘fractal’ structure by advecting the thin surface with the flow. The first mechanism, first proposed by
18

{\rm Da}<1

\tilde{t}_{\rm cool}=\sqrt{t_{\rm cool}t_{\rm eddy}(L_{\rm int})}\,,

(8)

18

\lambda_{T}

The second mechanism to explain the scaling in the
{\rm Da}>1
regime is provided by

\lambda_{\rm F,turb}

(10)

A_{\rm int}(\lambda_{\rm F,turb})=L^{2}\left(\frac{\lambda_{\rm F,turb}}{L_{\rm int}}\right)^{-d}\,.

\frac{\dot{E}_{\rm cool}}{v_{t}(L_{\rm int})}=\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}PL^{2}{\rm Da}^{\frac{d-p}{1-p}}\,.

(13)

d=1/2
does seem to apply over a broad range, it does not work everywhere. Additionally, the appeal to Kolmogorov scaling to describe multiphase, compressible turbulence is questionable and we will see that it does not apply in our simulations.

II
Simulation Description

A more detailed description of our simulations is given in Paper 1, but we review the essential aspects here for clarity. We perform a set of hydrodynamical simulations of TRMLs using the GPU-accelerated
AthenaK
code
(
39
)
. The simulations are initialized within a box with side lengths
(L_{x},L_{y},L_{z})=(L_{\rm box},L_{\rm box},1.5\times L_{\rm box})
at uniform resolution
\Delta x\equiv L_{\rm box}/N_{\rm res}
. The initial conditions consist of two states that are transitioned between at
z_{\rm trans}=L_{\rm box}/2
with the “upper” (
z>z_{\rm trans}
) state consisting of hot, low-density gas and the “lower” (
z<z_{\rm trans}
) state consisting of cold, high-density gas. These phases are initially in pressure equilibrium at pressure
P_{0}
with the hot gas at density
\rho_{\rm hot}=\rho_{0}
and the cold gas at density
\rho_{\rm cold}=\chi\rho_{0}
. We set temperature simply as
T=P/\rho
so that
T_{\rm hot}=P_{0}/\rho_{0}=\chi T_{\rm cold}
(for pressure equilibrium). The adiabatic sound speed in the hot gas is
c_{s,{\rm hot}}=\sqrt{\gamma P_{0}/\rho_{0}}
with
\gamma=5/3
. The two phases are set up in relative motion in the
x
-direction with
v_{x,{\rm hot}}=v_{\rm rel}/2
and
v_{x,{\rm cold}}=-v_{\rm rel}/2
. Noise is introduced near the interface in order to excite the Kelvin-Helmholtz instability.

We employ a cooling function similar to that in
4
in which the two phases are thermally stable (heating and cooling balance) but cooling dominates at intermediate temperatures. The cooling function is a piece-wise power law with the volumetric cooling rate proportional to
\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm cool}\propto T^{-\beta(T)}
with
(\beta_{\rm lo},\,\beta_{\rm hi})=(-2,\,3)\times 2/\log_{10}(\chi)
for
\beta_{\rm lo}
at
T<T_{\rm pk}
and
\beta_{\rm hi}
at
T>T_{\rm pk}
. The
\chi
dependence is included to assure that
\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm cool}(T_{\rm hot})
and
\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm cool}(T_{\rm cold})
are the same across simulations with varying
\chi
. The cooling rate at the peak cooling temperature
T_{\rm pk}\equiv\left(T_{\rm cold}^{2}T_{\rm hot}\right)^{1/3}
is determined by the minimum cooling time,
t_{\rm cool,min}
, which is itself set by a choice of the parameter
(
15
)

\xi\equiv\frac{t_{\rm sh}}{t_{\rm cool,min}}

(14)

where
t_{\rm sh}\equiv L_{\rm box}/v_{\rm rel}
is the shear-time of the layer. This parameter is very similar to
{\rm Da}
and we explore their relationship in
Appendix A
. In
Appendix B
we briefly explore the impact of the variation in the shape of the cooling function on integrated quantities (
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}
). While we expect this shape to have an impact on the phase structure of the gas (explored in more depth in Paper 1) we do not expect it to impact any of the main conclusions of this work, though this would be important to check in future studies.

We consider
P_{0}
and
\rho_{0}
to be fixed across the simulations (at unity in code units) and therefore the parameters that determine a simulation are the Mach number of the shear flow
\mathcal{M}\equiv v_{\rm rel}/c_{s,{\rm hot}}
(which sets
v_{\rm rel}
), the density contrast
\chi\equiv\rho_{\rm cold}/\rho_{\rm hot}
(which sets
\rho_{\rm cold}
), and
\xi
(which sets
t_{\rm cool,min}
). We run simulations at
\mathcal{M}=1/2
,
\xi=1,\,3,\,10,\,30,\,100,\,300,\,\&\,1000
, and
\chi=30,\,10^{2},\,\&\,10^{3}
, though we do not discuss the
\xi=1

\mathcal{M}=1/8
for the
\chi=10^{2}
runs. Each parameter choice above is run at resolutions of
N_{\rm res}=64,\,128,\,256,\,\&\,512
. The simulations are run for
30\,t_{\rm sh}
and all analysis is performed on
t>10\,t_{\rm sh}
in order to assure the layer has reached an approximate steady-state. In Paper 1 we primarily explored the resolution dependence of certain properties of TRMLs. Here we will focus on how scaling behavior in TRMLs vary as a function of
{\rm Da}
and
\chi
and will only explore the resolution dependence when necessary. In

we show slices in

through the

plane of several of our high-resolution simulations with varying

and

III.2
What Suppresses
A_{\rm int}({\rm Da})
?

What are the driving factors behind the value of
A_{\rm int}
? As noted above, we see from the bottom panel of
Figure 2
that
A_{\rm int}
has a roughly constant value below the transition and that this value is smaller for larger
\chi
. Given that each mixing layer has roughly the same level of turbulence,
v_{t}(L_{\rm int})
, this lower
A_{\rm int}
at higher
\chi
is likely caused by the inertia of the cold gas: it is harder to “mix-up” (and therefore fold, and enhance the interface area of) gas with a larger inertia. This effect manifests not only as lower
A_{\rm int}
but a smaller vertical extent of the mixing layer, as is visually apparent in
Figure 1
.

As we already noted in
Section III.1
, in the high
{\rm Da}
regime the bulk inflow velocity to the layer becomes comparable to the integral scale turbulent velocity,
v_{t}(L_{\rm int})
. We might expect this to significantly impact the turbulent folding of the interface once the ram pressure of the inflow (
\rho_{\rm hot}v_{\rm bulk}^{2}
) becomes comparable to or greater than the turbulent pressure (
\approx\rho_{\rm cold}v_{t}(L_{\rm int})^{2}
, assuming the dynamical pressure is dominated by the cold phase), so that the suppression of interface growth occurs when
v_{\rm bulk}\gtrsim\sqrt{\chi}v_{t}(L_{\rm int})
.

The above argument is somewhat maximal in that it compares the ram-pressure of the low density inflow to the turbulent pressure of the cold, high density gas. In reality, turbulent motions in the hot gas should be significantly disrupted once the inflow’s ram-pressure is comparable to the turbulent pressure
in the hot phase

\rho_{\rm hot}v_{\rm bulk}^{2}\gtrsim\rho_{\rm hot}v_{t}(L_{\rm int})^{2}
. Since the flow is subsonic, disruption of turbulence in the hot gas likely suppresses turbulence in the cold gas, as cold-phase motion that is strongly countervailing to motion in the hot-phase would result in compression and pressure gradients that would counter this motion. In this interpretation, we should have a suppression of structure when

v_{\rm bulk}\gtrsim v_{t}(L_{\rm int})\,,

(19)

which is exactly what is observed in
Figure 2
.

In
Figure 3
we further illustrate the way in which the inflow suppresses
A_{\rm int}
in the
\chi=10^{2}
simulation. In the top panels we show the turbulent SFs,
v_{t}(\ell)
, in comparison to
v_{\rm bulk}
(horizontal lines) over the range of simulations where the change in
A_{\rm int}({\rm Da})
scaling behavior occurs (
{\rm Da}\approx\xi=30-300
). We include the full set of simulation resolutions here in order to illustrate what part of
v_{t}(\ell)
is well-resolved. In particular, we indicate all scales
\ell\leq 8\,\Delta x
with dashed-lines as these seem to roughly correspond to the range where motions are damped due to numerical viscosity. Consistent with the resolution independence of
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}
discussed in Paper 1,
v_{\rm bulk}
is also relatively independent of resolution across these simulations.

In the middle panels of
Figure 3
we show the ratio of the 2nd-order structure functions in the
z
- and
y
-components of the velocity field. As discussed in Paper 1,
v_{y}
is considered the independent tracer of turbulence as it is not associated with the shear or inflow directions. Therefore, the ratio we show in the middle panels of
Figure 3
is a tracer of the degree of anisotropy in the turbulence as a function of scale. It is clear that all simulations shown in
Figure 3
are anisotropic on large scales, indicating the importance of the inflow, while they are closer to isotropy on small scales, especially at low
\xi
.

Finally, in the bottom panels of
Figure 3
we show how the interface surface area,
A_{\rm int}
, varies as you measure it on different scales (details in
Section II
). We indicate the expected scaling for fractals with
d=1/4

A_{\rm int}\propto\ell^{-1/2}

{\rm SF}_{2}(v_{z})/{\rm SF}_{2}(v_{y})\gtrsim 1.5

.

The return to more volume-filling fractal scaling on the smallest scales is then natural given that the turbulence becomes more isotropic on these scales. This can be intuitively explained by the fact that the smallest scale structures are somewhat shielded from the inflow suppression, as they lie deeper within the turbulent structure of the interface (see

IV
Discussion

IV.1
Past Works

In
Section III
we demonstrated that the origin of the change in scaling behavior of the cooling as a function of
{\rm Da}
is a suppression of the surface area of the mixing interface caused by the ram-pressure of the inflow disrupting the turbulent folding of the surface. Furthermore, from
Figure 3
, it seems that the nature of this suppression in area, which occurs mostly on large scales, is well-resolved in the simulations.

It is fair to interrogate if this finding is congruent with the theoretical models of
40
and
15
discussed in
Section I
. In
Section III.2
we have directly measured key parameters of the
15
model: the fractal dimension of the interface,
d
, and the power-law scaling of the turbulent structure function,
p
. We find that the
p=1/3
, Kolmogorov-like scaling that is assumed in the
15

does not
apply in this regime
7
7

7

There is of course the possibility that our inferred
p
is affected by our finite resolution
(
24
)
.
. This is not necessarily surprising given that the flow is multi-phase and compressible. Further, while the excess fractal dimension of
d=1/2
that is assumed in
15
does apply broadly in our simulations, it is precisely in the high
{\rm Da}
limit that it ceases to apply at all scales (
Figure 3
). While the exact argument of
15
does not seem to hold, we will see in
Section IV.2
that the general approach of balancing the inflow of hot gas against the fractal structure and turbulent diffusivity can still lead to a prediction consistent with the data presented here.

It is harder to confront the phenomenological argument of
40
with our available measurements. As discussed in
Section I
, this argument picks out a specific time-scale for parcels of gas that are cooling in the mixing layer:
\tilde{t}_{\rm cool}
(
Equation 8
). The best test of this model would be to track the cooling of Lagrangian tracer particles in the flow and see if their cooling histories, as an ensemble, had a characteristic time-scale for evolution of
\tilde{t}_{\rm cool}
. However, given that the argument of
40
makes no reference to the suppression of
A_{\rm int}
in the high
{\rm Da}
regime, it is not immediately obvious that their picture can be reconciled with the evidence presented here.

Finally, the work of
38
finds (see Figure 5 of their supplementary material) that when increasing
\xi
by increasing the transverse dimension of the box,
L_{\rm box}
, (and thereby increasing
t_{\rm sh}
) one does not find the
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}\propto\xi^{1/4}
scaling behavior expected (if
\xi\approx{\rm Da}
) but instead
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}
independent of
\xi
. It is our suspicion that this occurs exactly because of the effect that we discuss here: the suppression of growth in the turbulent structure due to the ram-pressure of the inflowing gas. In particular, in this regime, the vertical extent of the layer is suppressed and therefore the outer scale of the turbulence ceases to track the size of the box and instead tracks the thickness of the layer.
38
also explain this result by appealing to the suppressed vertical extent of the layer but extend this to infer that the
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}\propto\xi^{1/4}
scaling regime does not exist. We would suggest that if the turbulent structure was measured in these simulations and
{\rm Da}
as defined by
Equation 5
were used in place of
\xi
that one would find that
{\rm Da}
did not change across this range of experiments that increase
L_{\rm box}
, consistent with the lack of changes in integrated cooling observed in that work. Though we do not explore the
{\rm Da}<1
regime here, one may imagine that this same point may become important in this regime as the layer is expected to thicken vertically in this regime. In this case it is important to make sure that one is using a consistent definition of
{\rm Da}
.

IV.2
A New Interpretation

We now appeal to the phenomena presented in this work, namely the suppression of the fractal surface area, to explain the
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}/v_{t}(L_{\rm int})\propto{\rm Da}^{1/4}
. In analogy with
Equation 3
, we imagine a coarse-graining on scale
\ell
of the dynamics across the layer and write the total cooling in the layer as a function of scale,
\ell
, as

\dot{E}_{\rm cool}(\ell)=\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}P_{0}v_{\rm diff}(\ell)A_{\rm int}(\ell)\,,

where
v_{\rm diff}(\ell)
is some
effective
diffusivity on scale
\ell
, and
A_{\rm int}(\ell)
is the interface area measured on scale
\ell
. Here we are imagining a “coarse-graining” of the surface on scale
\ell
, where the properties of the interface are smoothed on this scale and we imagine measuring the interface area and diffusive velocities similarly to how it is done in the simulations. We will assume that the effective diffusivity is mediated by turbulent motions on scale
\ell
so that, in analogy with
Equation 6
,
v_{\rm diff}(\ell)=\sqrt{v_{t}(\ell)\ell/t_{\rm cool,min}}
. However, differing from the argument of
15
, we will now assume that the fractal scaling of the interface only applies below a scale
L_{\rm frac}
, so that

A_{\rm int}(\ell)=L^{2}\left(\frac{\ell}{L_{\rm frac}}\right)^{-d}\,,

(20)

where
L
is still the large-scale dimension of the interface (equivalent to
L_{\rm box}
in our simulations) and we have assumed a scale hierarchy
\lambda_{\rm F}\ll\ell<L_{\rm frac}<L_{\rm int}<L
, where
\lambda_{\rm F}
is the true Field-length corresponding to the balance of micro-physical thermal conduction and cooling
(
13
)
. This lower bound is instilled here to guarantee that the
effective
turbulent diffusivity is the principal means of energy transport across the layer.

Combining the coarse-grained energy flux relation and
Equation 20
with a power-law scaling of the turbulent structure (
Equation 12
) we arrive at

\frac{\dot{E}_{\rm cool}(\ell)}{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}P_{0}L^{2}v_{t}(L_{\rm int})}={\rm Da}^{1/2}\left(\frac{\ell}{L}\right)^{\frac{1+p}{2}-d}\left(\frac{L}{L_{\rm frac}}\right)^{-d}\,,

(21)

where
p
is the power-law scaling of the turbulent velocity with
\ell
.

Let us imagine running a series of experiments, like those presented in the work, where we vary the cooling time,
t_{\rm cool,min}
, and hence
{\rm Da}
, while keeping the integral scale turbulent velocities,
v_{t}(L_{\rm int})
, fixed
8
8

8

While
v_{t}(L_{\rm int})
increases mildly as one moves further in to the fast-cooling regime,
{\rm Da}\gg 1
, this dependence is very mild
v_{t}(L_{\rm int})\propto{\rm Da}^{1/10}
, (see also
40
Figure 12).
. Focusing now only on terms dependent on
{\rm Da}
, we can ignore the
(\ell/L)
term.

As we showed in
Section III.2
, at high
{\rm Da}
the fractal structure becomes suppressed by the ram-pressure of the inflow disrupting the turbulence in the hot phase. It is natural to expect that the scale on which fractal structure is realized would then become suppressed in proportion to the ratio of the ram-pressure of the inflow to the turbulent pressure in the hot phase. So that, when
v_{\rm bulk}>v_{t}(L_{\rm int})
we have:

\frac{L}{L_{\rm frac}}\propto\frac{v_{\rm bulk}^{2}}{v_{t}(L_{\rm int})^{2}}\,.

(22)

We show the relationship between these two quantities

9

9

Ideally we would measure
L_{\rm frac}
in our simulations as the scale at which the fractal dimension of the interface,
d
(
Equation 15
), went from zero on large-scales to a non-zero value. In practice there is some level of structure on all scales and the transition is not as immediate as the relation shown in
Equation 20
implies. Therefore we measure
L_{\rm frac}
in the simulations as the scale at which
d(L_{\rm frac})>1/3
for a given snapshot and, in keeping with other measurements, take

as the median value over all snapshots with
t/t_{\rm sh}>10
.

Equation 22

Given the definition of
v_{\rm bulk}
in
Equation 4

Equation 21

Equation 22

Equation 21

\frac{\dot{E}_{\rm cool}(\ell)}{v_{t}(L_{\rm int})}\propto{\rm Da}^{\frac{1}{2(1+2d)}}\,,

Based on the results presented in the top panel of

V
Conclusion

Our main conclusions are as follows:

1.

We demonstrate (
Figure 2
) that the change in behavior of
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}/v_{t}(L_{\rm int})
with
{\rm Da}
occurs near the point where the velocity of gas inflowing to the layer (to balance cooling occurring in the layer),
v_{\rm bulk}
, becomes greater than the integral-scale turbulent velocity in the layer,
v_{t}(L_{\rm int})
.

2.

At this same transition, the area of the interface between hot and cold gas,
A_{\rm int}
, becomes suppressed in comparison to its low-
{\rm Da}
(roughly constant) value (bottom panel of
Figure 2
).

3.

The suppression of
A_{\rm int}
tracks, as a function of physical scale, the anisotropy of turbulent structure in the velocity field (
Figure 3
and
Figure 4
), caused by the inflow of gas to the TRML.

4.

In
Section IV
we argue that both previously proposed models to explain the
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}/v_{t}(L_{\rm int})\propto{\rm Da}^{1/4}
regime are inconsistent with the above observations. We then use the above observations to derive a new relation (
Equation 23
) which self-consistently recovers
\dot{E}_{\rm cool}/v_{t}(L_{\rm int})\propto{\rm Da}^{1/4}
assuming the excess fractal dimension of the interface is
d=1/2
on small scales and with clear predictions of deviations from that scaling if
d\neq 1/2
.

Appendix A
On the Definition of
{\rm Da}
and its Relation to
\xi

In
Section I
we define the Damköhler number as

{\rm Da}\equiv\frac{t_{\rm eddy}(L_{\rm int})}{t_{\rm react}}=\frac{L_{\rm int}}{v_{t}(L_{\rm int})t_{\rm react}}\,,

(A1)

where
L_{\rm int}
is the integral scale of the turbulence. For a given system, there is some freedom to choose exactly how to measure each of these quantities, and this is not done consistently across the literature. Different choices in how this measurement is done can result in large differences in
{\rm Da}
, to the extent that one set of choices would imply a simulation is in the fast cooling regime (
{\rm Da}>1
) while another would imply that it is in the slow cooling regime (
{\rm Da}<1
). In this appendix, we demonstrate this by comparing different choices from the literature and attempting to justify our choices, as well as relating
{\rm Da}
to
\xi
for the simulations presented here. These comparisons are summarized in
Figure 6
.

{\rm Da}
is a quantification of the importance of turbulence relative to cooling while
\xi
compares the time for a parcel of gas to cross the box to the minimum cooling time in our simulations. We can control
\xi
in our simulations by directly setting the shear velocity (
v_{\rm rel}
) and cooling time (
t_{\rm cool,min}
), whereas
{\rm Da}
arises naturally from the turbulence that develops in the system. Nevertheless, on comparing our measured
{\rm Da}_{\rm sim}
to
\xi
in the top left panel of
Figure 6
we see that they are nearly equal, especially in the
\mathcal{M}=1/2
simulations (circles). At high
\xi
in the
\mathcal{M}=1/8
simulations, a larger fraction of the turbulent driving is provided by the inflow to the layer (bottom panel of
Figure 2
) so that
{\rm Da}_{\rm sim}
becomes somewhat smaller than
\xi
.

Appendix B
On the correct choice of
t_{\rm cool}

The choice of
T_{\rm mix}
for the temperature at which to measure the cooling time is based on the following argument from
2
. In a TRML with turbulent velocity
v_{t}(L_{\rm int})
at integral scale
L_{\rm int}
, the mass flux of hot gas into the mixing layer can be written as
\dot{m}_{h}\approx\eta_{h}\rho_{\rm hot}v_{t}(L_{\rm int})
for dimensionless efficiency factor,
\eta_{h}
. If one assumes that the mass flux of cold gas into the mixing layer is determined by the Kelvin-Helmholtz instability on scale
l_{c}
then the mass flux of cold gas to the layer can be written as
\dot{m}_{c}\approx\eta_{c}\rho_{c}l_{c}/t_{\rm KH}(l_{c})
. Since
11
11

11

Truly, we should use
v_{t}(l_{c})
in this relation, but that is not how it is used in the original argument.

t_{\rm KH}(l_{c})=\sqrt{\rho_{c}/\rho_{h}}\,l_{c}/v_{t}(L_{\rm int})
the cold gas mass flux in this argument becomes
\dot{m}_{c}\approx\eta_{c}\sqrt{\rho_{c}\rho_{h}}v_{t}(L_{\rm int})
, independent of
l_{c}
. One can then get an estimate of the temperature of the mixed gas by computing a mass-flux-weighted average:

\overline{T}=\frac{\dot{m}_{c}T_{\rm cold}+\dot{m}_{h}T_{\rm hot}}{\dot{m}_{c}+\dot{m}_{h}}\approx\left[\frac{\eta_{c}+\eta_{h}\sqrt{\chi}}{\eta_{h}+\eta_{c}\sqrt{\chi}}\right]T_{\rm mix}\,,

(B1)

so that
\overline{T}\approx T_{\rm mix}
assuming the term in brackets is order unity.

There are several issues with the above argument: (i) there is no reason
a priori
that
t_{\rm KH}
should be used to estimate the flux of gas from the cold phase and not the hot phase, switching this assumption leads to
\overline{T}\approx T_{\rm cold}
(in the limit
\chi\gg 1
) which is quite different, (ii) it is not clear why the time-scale for a linear instability (
t_{\rm KH}
) should be applied in the context of fully non-linear turbulent mixing, (iii) indeed, if one assumed a different time-scale associated with a different instability that may be more relevant in a given scenario (such as the Darrieus-Landau
(
23
)
, Vishniac
(
45
)
, or Rayleigh-Taylor
(
3
)
instabilities) one would arrive at a different formula for
\overline{T}
, and (iv) this argument only considers the role of turbulent diffusion (mixing) in estimating what the ‘typical’ temperature of mixed gas is, while cooling is also generally very important in changing the temperature of a fluid element.

In order to test whether or not cooling at
T_{\rm mix}
is truly indicative of cooling in the layer in the fully non-linear setting, we run a series of additional simulations with
\chi=10^{2}
and
\mathcal{M}=1/2
but with modified cooling functions. These cooling functions are shown in the left panel of
Figure 7
. In particular, we compare the
\xi=3
simulations with the fiducial cooling function (blue) with simulations which are identical except for
\beta_{\rm hi}=4.5
(orange dashed), and
\xi=1
and
\beta_{\rm hi}=5/3
(green dotted). We refer to the first modification as
mc
as it modifies the mixed temperature (
T_{\rm mix}
) cooling time by a factor of 3, and the second modification as
pc
as it changes the peak temperature (
T_{\rm pk}
) cooling time by a factor of 3 but keeps the cooling time at
T_{\rm mix}
unchanged.

The total cooling rates for simulations at resolution of
N_{\rm res}=64\,\,\&\,\,128
are shown in the right panel of
Figure 7
. As we can see, large changes in the cooling at
T_{\rm mix}
result in relatively small changes in the total cooling rate (comparing orange to blue points, they are consistent within errors) whereas a change to the peak cooling temperature that keeps the cooling at
T_{\rm mix}
unchanged results in significant differences (comparing blue to green points). We conclude that
t_{\rm cool,min}
is a more meaningful time-scale on which to measure the rate of cooling in the layer than
t_{\rm cool,mix}
.
