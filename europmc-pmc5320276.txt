A brief account of the anatomy and development of the
intersigmoid fossa will perhaps not be out of place.
Anatomy of the Fossa.?The fossa was first described by
Hensing in 1742. It is best displayed by lifting the pelvic
colon upwards and to the right, and putting the mesocolon on
the stretch. The orifice of the fossa is then seen under the
mesocolon to the left of the vertebral column.
The fossa is present in 70 to 80 per cent, of bodies
(Moynihan 70 per cent., Bruce and Ross 80 per cent., Waldeyer
84 per cent.). It is found very consistently in the infant, but
its frequency becomes less with increasing years.
In the adult the pelvic mesocolon has an inverted V-shaped
attachment to the bony pelvis. This attachment passes along
the left brim of the pelvis to the bifurcation of the common
iliac artery or higher, then turns sharply downwards over the
promontory of the sacrum to its third piece. From the ape*
of this attachment, a finger-like process of the under layer oi
the mesocolon is prolonged upwards. This finger-like process
passes under the attachment of the mesocolon and comes to he
between the parietal peritoneum in front and the psoas muscle

In the earlier months of development, the distal half of
the colon occupies a mid-line position, and possesses a long
mesentery (mesocolon) attached to the posterior abdominal wall
near the middle line. The pressure of the developing loop of
small bowel pushes the proximal part of the distal colon with
its mesocolon over to the left, after the fashion of the turning
over of a page of a book. The colon with its mesocolon thus
comes against the posterior abdominal wall and the left iliac
fossa (Fig. 2). By the seventh month the descending and
iliac parts of the colon have lost their mesocolon and have
become fixed to the posterior abdominal wall.
The disappearance of the descending mesocolon was
originally attributed by Treitz, Hyrtl and Waldeyer to
spreading of the mesocolon, resultant upon the growth of the
abdominal wall and the growing forward of the kidney. This
was shown by Langer, Toldt and Symington to be incorrect.
What really happens, is that the left layer of the mesocolon
undergoes what Toldt has referred to as " physiological
adhesion " to the parietal peritoneum of posterior abdominal
wall. The presence of the cavity of the pelvis prevents this
process of " physiological adhesion " passing further downwards
than the iliac colon, and allows the pelvic colon to retain its
mesocolon. While the proximal half of this pelvic mesocolon
receives an attachment to the left brim of the pelvis, the distal
half retains its original mid-line attachment. It is in this way
that the pelvic mesocolon acquires its inverted V-shaped
attachment to the walls of the pelvis.
To the medial side of the kidney between it and the bodies
of the vertebrae there is a gutter. In the region of this gutter,
the process of " physiological adhesion " is frequently deficient,
with the result that a tubular recess is formed, the lower
opening of which is at the apex of the inverted V-shaped
attachment of the pelvic mesocolon. This recess is the inter-
sigmoid fossa and may extend up as far as the duodeno-jejunal
junction.
This process of " physiological adhesion " begins first of all
at the splenic flexure, and spreads downwards. Symington
demonstrated a transition stage in a six months' foetus, in which
the upper part of the descending colon was placed lateral to
the kidney, but as it passed downwards the colon gradually
inclined medially in front of the kidney. It had no mesentery
at its upper end, but possessed one at its lower end.

Summary of Published Cases.?In a recent paper Rendle
Short reviews the reported cases of retro-peritoneal hernia.
He only finds eight authentic cases of intersigmoid hernia in
the literature, but has overlooked at least six others. These,
along with the present case, bring the total of reported cases
to fifteen.
Two cases which one has not accepted as typical are those
?f Black and Kljutscharew. On reading the published report
?f Black's case, one is not left with a clear impression of its
nature. Black diagnosed the condition as an intersigmoid hernia
prior to operation, and subsequently excised the hernial sac from
the mesocolon, a procedure which was followed by recovery.
Lambret's case was not accepted by Moynihan, because he
was unable to obtain the original paper, but this case has been
verified by Coley, and has been included in the present series.

Ten of the series of fifteen cases presented the signs and
symptoms of acute obstruction, two, including the present one,
showed recurrent subacute strangulation, although the present
one finally became acute. None of the cases showed palpable
tumour formation, and three first came to light on the post-
mortem table, unoperated upon.
The content of the hernia has, except in two instances,
been small intestine only. The exceptions are Krall's which
contained part of the sigmoid colon itself, and the case of Bruce
and Ross which contained sigmoid colon in addition to 5 feet
of small intestine. The amount of small intestine involved has
varied from 4 inches to the entire small gut.
Mortality.?In the series there have been five recoveries and ten
deaths, although two of the latter were not directly attributable to
the hernia. Only twelve of the fifteen cases were operated upon.
Intersigmoid hernia is the rarest of all the retroperitoneal
herniae. The extreme rarity of the hernia contrasted with the
frequent occurrence of the fossa has been attributed to the very
sheltered position the orifice of the fossa enjoys beneath the
pelvic mesocolon.
It is suggested that in order to get a hernia, another factor
is necessary. This latter may be either a lengthening of the
mesentery of the small gut resulting from an inguinal hernia
(Eccles' case); the imprisoning of the small gut in the pelvis by
adhesions (present case) ; or, as in Eve's case, the turning up and
fixation of the sigmoid loop, rendering the orifice of the fossa
more accessible. In Eve's case the sigmoid colon was bound to
the left iliac fossa by three definite bands. In only four cases
has the presence of an additional factor been mentioned.
