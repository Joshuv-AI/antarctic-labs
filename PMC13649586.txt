A human single-cell atlas identifies OLR1+ scar-associated macrophages as a potential therapeutic target for chronic liver disease

Abstract

Chronic liver disease (CLD) results in liver fibrosis, leading to adverse clinical outcomes. Macrophages regulate fibrosis and represent an attractive therapeutic target. However, the specific macrophage subpopulation that drives fibrosis in CLD remains unclear. Here we generate a liver single-cell atlas comprising 649,295 cells from 42 healthy and 35 CLD patients, identifying 125 transcriptionally distinct cellular states. We resolve two types of disease-expanded TREM2+ scar-associated macrophages (SAMac), including a pro-inflammatory subpopulation that expresses the scavenger receptor OLR1 and spatially accumulates in the CLD fibrotic niche. High hepatic OLR1 expression was associated with increased fibrosis across multiple CLD etiologies and adverse patient outcomes in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease. Corollary pro-inflammatory OLR1+ SAMac expanded in mouse CLD, while targeting of macrophage OLR1 attenuated fibrogenic activity in human coculture and multilineage spheroid models. Overall, our atlas provides a reference to study disease-associated cell states in CLD and identifies OLR1 as a potential therapeutic target to attenuate liver fibrosis.
This study provides a single-cell RNA-sequencing atlas of 77 healthy and chronic liver disease livers and identifies a subpopulation of pro-inflammatory scar-associated macrophages enriched for OLR1 expression with therapeutic potential.

Main

Chronic liver disease (CLD) is a major global healthcare problem resulting in approximately 2 million deaths per year. Irrespective of cause, chronic liver damage causes progressive scarring (fibrosis) that can ultimately advance to cirrhosis, leading to clinical complications. Higher levels of liver fibrosis are associated with poor clinical outcomes,, whereas improvements in fibrosis correlate with a better prognosis. Due to this close relationship between fibrosis and outcomes, there is significant interest in developing new antifibrotic therapies for CLD patients,

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) is transforming our understanding of human CLD pathogenesis and the identification of therapeutic targets,. However, most existing human liver scRNA-seq studies include relatively small cell numbers from limited patient cohorts, constraining their ability to resolve subtle transcriptional states relevant to CLD pathogenesis. Large-scale single-cell atlases have started to address this issue in other human organs–, but a comprehensive human liver scRNA-seq atlas spanning healthy and diseased states remains unavailable.

Immune cell accumulation is a feature of human CLD and regulates tissue injury responses. Specifically, cells of the mononuclear phagocyte (MP) lineage have been shown to have a crucial role in regulating liver fibrosis–. Previous human scRNA-seq studies identified disease-expanded TREM2+ CD9+ scar-associated macrophages (SAMacs) as drivers of scar-producing myofibroblast activation in the liver,, making them a potential antifibrotic target. However, hepatic macrophages also regulate fibrosis regression and tissue repair–, with SAMac-expressed molecules such as TREM2 and SPP1 that actually show anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic properties–. It remains unclear whether distinct SAMac subpopulations mediate divergent functions and how pro-inflammatory, pro-fibrotic SAMacs might be selectively targeted, without compromising reparative macrophage activity.

To address this, we generate a human liver scRNA-seq atlas from healthy and CLD livers. We reveal previously unrecognized SAMac heterogeneity, identifying a distinct subpopulation of OLR1+ SAMacs that expand in CLD and show pro-inflammatory properties. High hepatic expression of OLR1 is predictive of adverse clinical outcomes in patients with CLD, while selective targeting of OLR1 reduces fibrogenic activity in human in vitro models. Our work provides a foundational resource to study disease-associated cell states in CLD and highlights OLR1 inhibition as a potential antifibrotic therapeutic target.

Results

Establishing a human CLD single-cell atlas

We conducted integrated analysis of seven published human scRNA-seq studies (Fig. and Supplementary Table ),–, combining data from 77 (42 healthy and 35 CLD) patients, derived from 38 (21 healthy and 17 CLD) males and 39 (21 healthy and 18 CLD) females. To facilitate downstream applications, three levels of atlas annotation were established, based on the transcriptional similarity across level 3 clusters (Supplementary Fig. ). Broad level 1 clusters consisted of 16 cell types (Fig. and Supplementary Table ). All studies contributed to level 1 annotation, with expected study-specific differences based on cell isolation methods from the original publications, such as more epithelial cells in the studies from refs. ,, and only T cells from the study discussed in ref. (Fig. ). Apart from the study discussed in ref. , all studies contained a mixture of healthy individuals and patients with CLD. All studies contributed both male and female donors to the atlas (Fig. ).

Intermediate level 2 annotation resulted in 34 cell types (Fig. and Supplementary Table ). Fine-grained unsupervised level 3 clustering resulted in a total of 125 final cell states (Supplementary Fig. and Supplementary Table ). Overall, atlas structure and hierarchy are summarized in Extended Data Fig. . Atlas gene expression profiles can be browsed at https://shiny.igc.ed.ac.uk/Human_Liver_scRNA-seq_Atlasv2/.

Notably, our approach identified rare cell types such as neurons, mast cells, basophils, neutrophils and hematopoietic progenitor cells (Fig. ), which are not well described in individual scRNA-seq studies. Level 3 clusters from each cell lineage were then used to assess changes in cell-type composition and gene expression between healthy and CLD liver tissue.

Differential abundance analysis using the Milo package demonstrated that both SAMac1 and SAMac2 expanded in CLD livers compared to healthy livers (Fig. ), a finding we confirmed using an alternative statistical approach (Extended Data Fig. ). No differences were observed in MP composition between male and female patients (Extended Data Fig. ). SAMac2 expansion was consistent across all etiologies of CLD, while SAMac1 expansion was more notable in patients with the biliary disorders primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) and primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC; Extended Data Fig. ). These data suggest that expansion of the SAMac2 population is a common feature of CLD.

Annotation of other hepatic cell types

To identify other CLD-associated cell states, we replicated the level 3 analysis for other major cell lineages. A total of 7,301 mesenchymal cells were partitioned into eight clusters (Fig. and Supplementary Table ). We resolved four distinct subpopulations of MYH11+ vascular smooth muscle cells, LHX2+
NR1H4+ quiescent and activated hepatic stellate cells (HSC), THY1+
FBLN1+
IGF1+ portal fibroblasts and MSLN+ mesothelial cells (Fig. ). Differential abundance analysis demonstrated expansion of portal fibroblasts in CLD (Fig. ). This population, along with activated HSCs, expressed the highest levels of fibrillar collagens (COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1; Fig. ), suggesting that they are the main scar-producing populations in the CLD liver.

Vascular changes are a characteristic feature of advanced CLD and regulate fibrosis,,. A total of 36,591 liver endothelial cells were partitioned into ten clusters (Fig. ). Distinguishing marker genes (Fig. and Supplementary Table ) enabled annotation of liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, RSPO3+ central vein endothelia, FBLN5+ hepatic artery endothelia, CCL21+
PDPN+ lymphatic endothelia and three additional subpopulations of vascular endothelial cells (Vasc. endo 1–3). We also confirmed the presence of PLVAPhi and ACKR1hi disease-associated endothelial cells, as previously described. Milo analysis demonstrated expansion of arterial, lymphatic, PLVAPhi and ACKR1hi subpopulations in CLD, as well as a reduction in homeostatic sinusoidal and vascular subpopulations (Fig. ).

We resolved 19 subpopulations of T/NK cells (Extended Data Fig. and Supplementary Table ). Identified T cell subpopulations included naive, memory, tissue resident memory and regulatory (Treg) CD4+ T cells (Extended Data Fig. ). Similar heterogeneity was identified in the CD8+ T cell compartment, with subpopulations of naive, effector memory (TEM), resident memory and cytotoxic cells (Extended Data Fig. ). We also defined subpopulations GNLY+ CD56dim NK cells, CD160+
EOMES+ CD56bright NK cells and three clusters of SLC4A10+
IL23R+ mucosal-associated invariant T cells (Extended Data Fig. ). Differential abundance analysis showed a loss of NK and cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and shift toward a CD4-rich environment with expansion of CD4+ naive, CD4+ tissue resident memory and Tregs in CLD (Extended Data Fig. ).

Heterogeneity of the hepatic B cell compartment was also observed, with distinct subpopulations of TCL1A+
IL4R+ naive B cells, NR4A2+
TNFRSF13B+ memory B cells and two clusters of MZB1+
SDC1+ plasma cells (Extended Data Fig. and Supplementary Table ). All naive and memory B cell subpopulations expanded in CLD livers, most strikingly IL6+
MAST4+ activated memory B cells (Extended Data Fig. ).

Finally, we annotated epithelial (hepatocyte and cholangiocyte) subpopulations and identified gene expression patterns indicative of hepatocyte zonation (Extended Data Fig. and Supplementary Table ). Hepatocytes were mainly derived from the studies in refs. , (Extended Data Fig. ), so conclusions about CLD-associated changes in hepatocyte composition or transcription cannot be drawn. Cholangiocytes were captured from all the included studies (Extended Data Fig. ).

OLR1+ SAMac2 has a more pro-inflammatory pro-fibrotic profile

Having identified two subpopulations of TREM2+
CD9+ SAMac, we defined the transcriptional differences between SAMac1 and SAMac2, showing 903 genes significantly enriched in SAMac1 and 1,176 genes in SAMac2 (Fig. and Supplementary Table ). SAMac1 expressed higher levels of GPNMB as well as several anti-inflammatory antifibrotic factors such as MMP9, IL10, GDF15, CD36 and PPARG (Fig. ). SAMac1 is therefore transcriptionally similar to lipid-associated macrophages described in mice, and humans. In contrast, SAMac2 demonstrated higher expression of pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic mediators (IL1B, TNF, IL18, NLRP3, AREG, PDGFB), and scavenger receptors including OLR1 and CLEC5A (Fig. ). No differences in TREM2 or CD9 expression were observed between SAMac1 and SAMac2 (Extended Data Fig. ), but SAMac2 showed coexpression of TREM2/OLR1 or TREM2/CLEC5A, while SAMac1 coexpressed TREM2/GPNMB or TREM2/FABP5 (Extended Data Fig. ). Flow cytometry confirmed expansion of TREM2+ OLR1+ macrophages in CLD livers (Extended Data Fig. ). Immunofluorescence staining indicated that OLR1+ CD68+ cells were spatially located in areas of fibrosis in CLD and represented a subpopulation of CD68+ SAMacs (Extended Data Fig. ). To define the spatial distribution of SAMac subpopulations, we performed spatial proteomics on 46 patient liver samples (10 healthy (8 resection and 2 biopsy) and 36 CLD (9 metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) explant, 4 MASLD biopsy, 9 alcohol-related liver disease (ARLD) explant, 8 PBC explant and 6 PSC explant)) through a 36-plex antibody panel on the PhenoCycler-Fusion (PCF). We annotated major liver cell types including OLR1+ and GPNMB+ macrophages (Fig. ). Assessment of cellular composition across patients demonstrated loss of hepatocytes and expansion of fibroinflammatory cells in CLD (Fig. ). Both OLR1+ and GPNMB+ macrophages accumulated significantly in CLD (Fig. ), with no differences in abundance observed between different etiologies (Fig. and Extended Data Fig. ). Neighborhood analysis identified three cellular niches, including a fibrotic niche that was enriched in fibroinflammatory cells (Fig. ), fibrillar collagen deposition (Fig. ) and expanded in CLD (Fig. ). Examination of OLR1 and GPNMB staining showed occasional double-positive cells but predominantly discrete expression, mirroring our scRNA-seq data (Fig. ). Both OLR1+ and GPNMB+ macrophages preferentially accumulated within the fibrotic niche (Fig. ). Hence, OLR1 and GPNMB label distinct macrophage subpopulations, both of which spatially accumulate in the CLD fibrotic niche.

High OLR1 expression is associated with adverse clinical outcomes

OLR1 (LOX-1), a class E scavenger receptor, has been suggested as a therapeutic target for cardiovascular disease,. OLR1 expression, alongside CLEC5A, was specific to MPs in the human liver and highly enriched in SAMacs (Extended Data Fig. ), suggesting that it may offer a route to selective targeting of SAMacs.

To determine whether OLR1 could represent a therapeutic target across the CLD severity spectrum, we performed scRNA-seq on liver needle biopsies from patients with MASLD compared to those with histologically normal liver. A total of 71,062 cells from 13 patients (7 MASLD and 6 healthy; Supplementary Table ) were annotated using our level 2 atlas (Fig. ). SAMacs expanded in MASLD biopsies (Fig. ) and upregulated OLR1 (Fig. ). OLR1 expression remained specific to macrophages (Fig. ). To assess OLR1 expression across disease stages in a larger patient cohort, we analyzed bulk RNA-seq data from MASLD samples in SteatoSITE. Hepatic OLR1 expression correlated positively with histological fibrosis stage (Fig. ) and other fibroinflammatory genes (Fig. ), and negatively with genes expressed by homeostatic nonparenchymal cell populations such as KCs (TIMD4, MARCO) or liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (CLEC4M, CLEC4G; Fig. ). To confirm SAMac2 expansion across fibrosis stages, we performed deconvolution analysis of SteatoSITE bulk RNA-seq data using MP scRNA-seq data. SAMac2 and SAMac1 proportions both increased with fibrosis stage in MASLD (Fig. ). We confirmed these findings in bulk liver RNA-seq data from additional histologically staged CLD cohorts. OLR1 upregulation and SAMac2 expansion were associated with increasing fibrosis stage in additional patients with MASLD (Extended Data Fig. ), PSC (Extended Data Fig. ), ARLD (Extended Data Fig. ) and PBC (Extended Data Fig. ).

The SteatoSITE cohort includes linked longitudinal clinical outcome data. Strikingly, high hepatic OLR1 expression and expansion of the SAMac2 population were both associated with higher overall mortality (Fig. ) and increased rates of liver disease progression (Fig. ) in MASLD. Overall, these data indicate that OLR1+ SAMacs may promote hepatic inflammation and fibrogenesis across the full spectrum of severity in different CLD etiologies.

OLR1+ SAMacs are conserved across species

To assess cross-species conservation, we used the mouse carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) mouse model of liver fibrosis progression and regression, performing scRNA-seq on blood and liver CD45+ immune cells from uninjured, liver fibrosis (24 and 96 h) and fibrosis regression (4 weeks) groups (Fig. ). After QC and lineage annotation (Extended Data Fig. ), 18,177 MPs partitioned grouped into 17 cell clusters, revealing both the expected subpopulations of monocytes, macrophages and DCs and previously unreported mouse MP subpopulations, such as an Acod1+
Cxcl10+ liver macrophage cluster (MDM Acod1+; Fig. and Supplementary Table ). We identified two subpopulations of Trem2+
Cd9+
Cd63+
Spp1+ SAMacs, termed SAMac1 and SAMac2 (Fig. ). Both mouse SAMac subpopulations were liver derived (Fig. ) and expanded at fibrotic time points (24 and 96 h), but had returned to baseline following regression (4 weeks; Fig. ). As with human SAMacs, differential expression between mouse SAMac1 and SAMac2 revealed transcriptional heterogeneity (Fig. and Supplementary Table ), with SAMac1 being enriched for Gpnmb, Slamf7 and other anti-inflammatory mediators (for example, Il18bp and Pparg), while mouse SAMac2 showed elevated expression of Olr1 and pro-inflammatory factors including inflammasome components Il1b and Nlrp3 (Fig. ). We confirmed our findings using scRNA-seq of blood and liver CD45+ cells from bile duct ligation (BDL) mouse model, demonstrating expansion of SAMac1 and SAMac2 in BDL livers (Extended Data Fig. ) and highlighting a similar MP marker gene profile across models (Extended Data Fig. ), including the utility of Olr1 and Gpnmb as markers to distinguish SAMac2 and SAMac1, respectively (Extended Data Fig. ). To study mouse SAMac1 and SAMac2 kinetics, we conducted flow cytometry to distinguish CD319/Slamf7+ SAMac1 and Olr1+ SAMac2 in the CCl4 model (Extended Data Fig. and Fig. ). OLR1+ SAMacs show pro-inflammatory interactions with mesenchyme

To understand how SAMac subpopulations regulate fibrosis, we used the LIANA+ package to perform interactome analysis between the two human SAMac subpopulations (as senders) and mesenchymal cell clusters (as receivers). Focusing on HSCs, activated HSCs and portal fibroblasts as the main mesenchymal cells that express extracellular matrix and regulate liver fibrosis, we identified three groups (factors) of ligand–receptor pairs where ligands were expressed by both SAMac1 and SAMac2 (Fig. and Supplementary Table ). Common SAMac-derived ligands included galectins and TIMPs between SAMac1/SAMac2 and HSCs (Extended Data Fig. ), calmodulins and osteopontin (SPP1) between SAMac1/SAMac2 and activated HSCs (Extended Data Fig. ), and proganulin (GRN) and macrophage migration inhibitory factor between SAMac1/SAMac2 and portal fibroblasts (Extended Data Fig. ). We also identified two factors that were specific to SAMac1 and SAMac2 as senders, factor 8 and factor 9, respectively (Fig. and Supplementary Table ). Factor 8 (SAMac1) ligands contained several reported antifibrotic factors, including HGF, IGF1 (ref. ), MMP9 (ref. ) and GDF15 (ref. ; Fig. ). In contrast, factor 9 (SAMac2) ligands included TNF, TGFB1, IL1B–, IL18, C3 and PDGFB (Fig. ), mediators that have been associated with promoting fibrogenesis in the liver and/or other organs. Hence, SAMac heterogeneity has potential consequences for signaling to mesenchymal cells, with OLR1+ SAMac2 predicted to promote activation of scar-producing activated HSCs and portal fibroblasts.

To determine whether OLR1+ monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) have a functional role in liver fibrosis, we used a human multilineage spheroid culture system–, supplemented with the addition of primary human monocytes from patients with CLD. This model demonstrated that human OLR1+ MDMs have a pro-fibrotic function (Supplementary Note and Supplementary Fig. ).

We then sought to assess drivers of OLR1 expression and SAMac differentiation. OLR1 expression was upregulated in THP-1 MDMs in response to phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (Extended Data Fig. ), and in primary human MDMs in response to lipopolysaccharide (Extended Data Fig. ). To understand conditions promoting SAMac1 and SAMac2 differentiation, we interrogated a transcriptomic dataset where human MDMs were polarized using 67 different conditions. The TPP model mimics chronic inflammation and more closely resembles human macrophages from chronic inflammatory diseases. We proceeded to investigate whether targeting OLR1 itself could modulate pro-fibrotic macrophage functions. OLR1 knockdown using siRNA in THP-1 MDMs reduced IL1B gene expression (Extended Data Fig. ) and attenuated IL-1β protein secretion (Extended Data Fig. ). Similarly, treatment of primary human MDMs from patients with CLD with OLR1 siRNA decreased OLR1 and IL1B gene expression (Extended Data Fig. ). Conditioned media from primary MDMs with OLR1 knockdown was less able to induce fibrogenic activity in LX2 HSCs than control media (Fig. ). Furthermore, treatment of primary human MDMs with MEDI6570, a clinically tested OLR1 blocking antibody, attenuated fibrillar collagen expression in LX2 cells after conditioned media transfer (Fig. ). No effect was observed on LX2 survival with MDM OLR1 knockdown or antibody blockade (Extended Data Fig. ).

To determine the impact of OLR1 modulation in a more complex primary human CLD model, we used a 3D-MASH model comprising multilineage liver spheroids containing primary hepatocytes, macrophages and HSCs with the addition of lipotoxic free fatty acids (FFA; Fig. ). OLR1 knockdown in these spheroids reduced OLR1 expression (Fig. ), attenuated fibrogenic activity (Fig. ) and abrogated fibrillar collagen protein production in response to FFA treatment (Fig. ). Hence, OLR1 inhibition is a potential new therapeutic approach to selectively modulate SAMac phenotype and inhibit liver fibrogenesis.

Discussion

Our human liver atlas, comprising 649,295 cells from 77 patients, is a valuable resource to dissect the pathobiology of liver fibrosis. We focused on the immune compartment, identifying a subpopulation of OLR1+ SAMacs that expands across different etiologies of human CLD, is conserved across species and has a pro-inflammatory phenotype. Attenuating macrophage OLR1 expression reduces fibrogenic activity, highlighting OLR1 as a potential therapeutic target for liver inflammation and fibrosis in CLD.

SAMacs have been shown to expand in fibrotic human liver in previous studies, but data on their function in fibrosis are conflicting. Some studies demonstrated a pro-fibrotic role for SAMacs through interactions with HSCs,. In contrast, others have suggested that TREM2+ SAMacs have an antifibrotic function in CLD, promoting scar resolution and the restitution of normal tissue architecture–. Our human scRNA-seq atlas has identified SAMac heterogeneity that could explain this functional dichotomy. In particular, the OLR1+ SAMac2 population displays a more pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrogenic phenotype, suggesting that this may be the optimal target population to inhibit pathogenic SAMac properties.

Targeting OLR1 itself represents a potential approach to selectively modulate SAMac2, reinforced by strong associations between elevated liver OLR1 expression and adverse clinical outcomes in SteatoSITE, and functional proof that attenuating OLR1 signaling reduces pro-fibrogenic macrophage properties. OLR1 has been implicated in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis, cardiac fibrosis and cancer. Mechanistically, OLR1 signaling in atherosclerosis can induce pro-inflammatory responses, including activation of the NF-κB pathway and/or NLRP3 inflammasome in endothelial cells, and macrophages,. Indeed, OLR1 blockade has been proposed as an atherosclerosis therapy, with the monoclonal antibody MEDI6570 having been administered to patients in phase 1 (ref. ) and phase 2 clinical trials (ClinicalTrials.gov registration: NCT04610892). Exploration of OLR1 inhibition as a therapy for CLD is warranted, either through repurposing of MEDI6570 or through developing small-molecule OLR1 inhibitors. Further work should also interrogate OLR1 biology in CLD, developing a mechanistic understanding of the antifibrotic effects of OLR1 inhibition, defining activating ligands in the CLD liver and understanding the utility of OLR1 as predictive biomarker (). In addition, other SAMac2-expressed candidate targets should be functionally explored, for example, CLEC5A that has been reported to mediate pro-inflammatory activity in myeloid cells.

Our finding of SAMac heterogeneity raises new questions about SAMac biology. TREM2+ GPNMB+ SAMac1 is transcriptionally analogous to lipid-associated macrophages defined in mice,, and MetMacs in humans, while TREM2+ OLR1+ SAMac2 reflects a discrete liver macrophage subpopulation. Here we annotated these macrophages as SAMacs due to their temporal and spatial association with scar deposition. However, TREM2+ macrophages expand in other diseases such as acute liver injury or liver cancer. It remains unclear whether TREM2+ macrophage phenotypes differ according to the nature or duration of injury. This will be important to ascertain, as macrophage nomenclature may require refinement according to the disease contexts in which they emerge. Additional data on the functional roles of SAMac1 and SAMac2 in CLD pathogenesis are also crucial. While our data suggest that OLR1+ and GPNMB+ SAMacs represent distinct macrophage polarization states, targeted manipulation of these subpopulations, potentially through selective in vivo depletion, will be important in determining how they regulate local tissue responses. Macrophages are also capable of switching phenotype according to microenvironmental cues. It is unknown whether macrophages can switch between SAMac2 and SAMac1 phenotypes and which signals might regulate this. Further work using a time-controlled fate mapping system is needed to dissect potential SAMac plasticity. Intriguingly, our spatial data suggest that OLR1+ and GPNMB+ macrophages accumulate in the same niche, suggesting that local reprogramming rather than differential recruitment may be responsible for distinct macrophage phenotypes. Expanded spatial studies, using larger panels of RNA and/or protein markers, will enable improved annotation of cellular phenotypes and facilitate a more nuanced analysis of interactions between SAMac subpopulations and other pathogenic cell types within this niche.

Beyond macrophages, our atlas has facilitated the distinction of rare cell types and identified other CLD-associated cellular states. These findings can be followed up more mechanistically to identify approaches to modulate other pathogenic populations. It should be noted that our atlas is based on scRNA-seq data, enabling detailed analysis of immune cells. However, cell types such as hepatocytes and HSCs are better captured using single-nucleus RNA-seq,,– or using our fixed cell profiling on frozen biopsies (Fig. ). Future atlases should integrate scRNA-seq, single-nucleus RNA-seq and expanded fixed cell profiling data from patient biopsies at different CLD stages, to fully dissect cellular heterogeneity. Notably, ‘healthy’ scRNA-seq samples may have confounders such as occult liver disease, field effects of disease elsewhere in the liver (for example, colorectal metastases) or technical artifacts such as tissue ischemia or dissociation. It is therefore crucial that any pathogenic cell types or candidate therapeutic targets identified from single-cell analyses are validated in independent, histologically staged biopsy cohorts, as we have done for OLR1+ SAMacs.

In conclusion, by generating a fully annotated human liver scRNA-seq atlas, we have resolved the previously poorly studied heterogeneity in SAMacs and identified OLR1 as a potential target for liver fibrosis. This work forms the basis for a more refined approach to module SAMacs and provides a resource for the identification of additional therapeutic strategies in CLD.

scRNA-seq analysis

Human data processing, QC and integration

Human scRNA-seq data from eight previously published studies,–,, were downloaded from the European Nucleotide Archive as paired FASTQ files (210 samples from 81 patients; Supplementary Table ). Sequence alignment and initial sample processing were performed using Cell Ranger (v.7.0.0) and the GRCh38 reference human genome (v.GRCh38-2020-A downloaded from 10x Genomics) to generate feature barcode matrices. Subsequent processing and analysis were performed in R (v.4.3.1) using the R package Seurat (v.5.0.0), and in Python (v.3.10.16). All visualizations were produced using Seurat functions in conjunction with the ggplot2 (v.3.5.1) and grid (v.4.3.2; R Core Team (2020)) R packages.

Samples were adjusted for ambient RNA contamination using the R package DecontX (v.1.14.2), and cells failing quality thresholds (mitochondrial gene percentage >15% or total unique features <100) were removed. Patients with <500 cells passing these thresholds were excluded. As a result, all patients mentioned in ref. were excluded, leaving 77 patients from seven studies for downstream analysis.

For patients whose genetic sex was not reported in the original publications, sex was inferred based on the expression of 20 Y chromosome-specific genes—RPS4Y1, DDX3Y, KDM5D, UTY, USP9Y, ZFY, EIF1AY, TSPY1, PRKY, NLGN4Y, AMELY, PCDH11Y, CDY1, CDY2, VCY, BPY2, DAZ, HIST1H2AY, RBMY and SRY. A total of 50 cells were randomly selected from each patient, and, if any of these genes had nonzero expression in the selected cells, the patient was classified as male; otherwise, as female.

Patient disease status was obtained either from the provided metadata or from patient characteristics reported in each study. Liver samples from patients annotated as healthy or those with no fibrosis reported on histology were considered healthy controls, whereas those annotated as diseased or with documented fibrosis on histology were classified as CLD. Where available, the etiologies of CLD were extracted from the original metadata or were inferred from published histological findings. All other patients with unspecified etiology were annotated as unknown.

To facilitate efficient integrative analysis of all cells from the 77 remaining patients, 1,000 representative cells were first selected from each sample using the Seurat function ‘SketchData’ based on a method described in ref. . Normalization, scaling, variable feature identification and principal component analysis (PCA) were performed on the ‘sketched’ data using default Seurat methods, followed by integration using Harmony and clustering using Seurat. Using the sketched integration as reference, the Seurat function ‘ProjectIntegration’ was then used to integrate the entire dataset. Cluster labels from the sketched cells were projected onto the whole dataset using the function ‘ProjectData’ from Seurat.

Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were performed using the ‘FindAllMarkers’ function from Seurat, identifying marker genes from each cluster. Clusters were then manually annotated using previously published literature, identifying 11 cell lineages.

Clustering, annotation and atlas construction

Cell lineages were subset individually and the previous preprocessing steps repeated (normalization, scaling, variable feature identification and PCA). As before, cells were clustered and then annotated based on the expression of known marker genes. Doublet clusters were identified manually based on their expression of multiple lineage markers and removed. Low-quality clusters (median nFeature < 800) were also removed. After QC for each lineage, the final atlas included 649,295 cells.

Annotations from each cell lineage (subsequently referred to as ‘level 3’ annotation) were mapped back to the complete atlas. Cluster similarities were assessed using the function ‘buildSNNGraph’ from R package scran (v.1.30.2) and the function ‘pariwiseModularity’ from R package bluster (v.1.12.0). Based on the results, clusters from level 3 annotation were manually merged into 34 grouped clusters, defined as the ‘level 2’ annotation. The same similarity testing and merging procedures were then applied to the level 2 clusters, resulting in the manual identification of 16 ‘level 1’ clusters.

For atlas visualization, a ShinyApp was built using R package ShinyCell (v.2.1.0) and a Loupe file was generated using LoupeR (v.1.1.4; https://github.com/10XGenomics/loupeR).

Differential abundance and expression testing

Differential composition analysis was performed on level 3 annotations of selected lineages using MiloR (v1.8.1). First, cellular states were modeled as neighborhoods based on the k-nearest neighbor graph. Differential neighborhood abundance testing was then performed comparing disease status (healthy versus CLD). For the MP lineage, additional differential abundance tests were also performed for each level 3 MP cell type, comparing healthy versus CLD, male versus female and healthy versus each annotated disease etiology. Analysis was restricted to patients with more than 50 MPs. The relative abundance of each cell type was calculated for each patient, and relative abundances among groups were tested using unpaired two-sample t tests, implemented through the ‘stat_compare_means’ function from the ggpubr R package. Differential gene expression analysis was performed comparing SAMac1 and SAMac2 clusters using the ‘model-based analysis of single-cell transcriptomics’ framework, deployed using the Seurat function ‘FindMarkers’.

Cell–cell interaction analysis

Ligand–receptor analysis was performed on the level 3 atlas using Python packages liana-py (v.1.4.0) and cell2cell (v.0.7.4). To accommodate Python analysis, the dataset was transformed from a Seurat object to H5AD format using R packages sceasy (v.0.0.7) and reticulate (v.1.35.0). Analysis was limited to the MP clusters SAMac1 and SAMac2 (as senders) and level 3 mesenchymal cell clusters (as receivers). Ligand–receptor score calculations were iteratively run across individual patient samples using the liana-py function ‘rank_aggregate.by_sample’. Ligand–receptor scores were input to cell2cell as a four-dimensional tensor (sample × sender cell × receiver cell × ligand–receptor pair). Using the Tensor–cell2cell pipeline, tensor decomposition was performed, extracting unique context-dependent patterns of intracellular communication in the form of ten factors. Before decomposition, elbow analysis was performed to select the optimal number of factors. Ligand–receptor pairs associated with each factor were retrieved using the cell2cell function ‘tensor_downstream.get_lr_by_cell_pairs’.

Human biopsy scRNA-seq analysis

Analysis was performed on 13 human liver needle biopsy samples (n = 6 healthy and n = 7 MASLD). Sequence alignment was performed using Cell Ranger (v.7.0.0) and the GRCh38 reference human genome (version GRCh38-2020-A downloaded from 10x Genomics). Subsequent processing was performed in R (v.4.4.1) using R package Seurat (v.5.3.1.0000). Visualizations were produced using Seurat functions in conjunction with the R package ggplot2 (v.3.5.2). Cells with total unique features of <500 were removed. Normalization, scaling, variable feature identification and PCA were performed using default Seurat methods. Samples were integrated using R package Harmony (v.1.2.3). Clustering was performed iteratively, identifying and removing doublet clusters (based on their expression of multiple lineage markers), low-quality clusters (based on their high proportion of mitochondrial gene expression and low unique feature count), as well as proliferating cells. The final dataset included 71,062 liver cells. Ambient RNA removal was performed using SoupX, provided with clustering from Seurat and manual cell-type annotations, using ALB as a marker of the hepatocyte lineage. Label transfer was performed on the full dataset using Seurat functions ‘FindTransferAnchors’ and ‘TransferData’, and the human liver cell atlas as reference.

Mouse CCl4 scRNA-seq analysis

Analysis was performed on 12 mouse scRNA-seq datasets (n = 6 CD45+ cells from liver and n = 6 CD45+ cells from blood). Sequence alignment and initial sample processing were performed using Cell Ranger (v.3.0.2) and the mm10 reference mouse genome (version mm10-3.0.0 downloaded from 10x Genomics). Subsequent processing was performed in R (v.4.2.2) using the R package Seurat (v.4.1.3). Visualizations were produced using Seurat functions in conjunction with the ggplot2 (v.3.2.1) and grid (v.3.6.3; R Core Team (2020)) R packages. Cells failing quality thresholds (mitochondrial gene percentage >30% or total unique features <300) were removed. Normalization, scaling, variable feature identification and PCA were performed using default Seurat methods. After merging the 12 datasets, dimensional reduction was performed (uniform manifold approximation and projection). Clustering was performed iteratively, identifying and removing doublet clusters based on their expression of multiple lineage markers. After QC, the final dataset included 27,070 cells. Clustering of the final dataset was then performed using default methods, and marker genes for each cluster were identified by Wilcoxon rank-sum test, facilitating manual annotation of cell types. The MP lineage was isolated and re-analyzed as described above (normalization, scaling, variable feature identification and PCA). Clustering was then performed using the first 17 principal components and a resolution of 0.7. Annotation was performed manually based on the expression of known marker genes. Differentially expressed genes between SAMac1 and SAMac2 clusters were identified using the ‘model-based analysis of single-cell transcriptomics’ approach. Gene lists were filtered to include only genes that are expressed in >10% cells with an absolute log-transformed fold change >0.5 and Bonferroni-adjusted P value <0.01.

Mouse BDL scRNA-seq analysis

Analysis was performed on eight mouse scRNA-seq datasets (n = 4 CD45+ cells from liver, and n = 4 CD45+ Ly-6G− cells from blood). Sequence alignment was performed using Cell Ranger (v.7.0.0) and the mm10 reference mouse genome (version mm10-2020-A downloaded from 10x Genomics). Subsequent processing was performed in R (v.4.4.1) using R package Seurat (v.5.3.1.0000). All visualizations were produced using Seurat functions in conjunction with the R package ggplot2 (v.3.5.2).

Processing was performed as described for human biopsy scRNA-seq. In brief, samples were integrated using Harmony, clustered and filtered, including removal of proliferating and nonimmune cell populations. The final dataset included 27,789 immune cells. Annotation was performed manually, based on the expression of known marker genes. Ambient RNA removal was performed using SoupX (v.1.6.2), provided with cell-type annotations, and Csf1r as a marker of the MP lineage. Integration using Harmony was repeated after ambient RNA removal, followed by clustering. Clusters expressing MP lineage markers were subsetted and reintegrated, creating a final MP dataset of 17,240 cells. Label transfer was performed using the Seurat functions ‘FindTransferAnchors’ and ‘TransferData’, and the annotated mouse CCl4 scRNA-seq dataset as reference.

Human liver bulk RNA-seq analysis

SteatoSITE RNA-seq and histology data were generated as described in detail in the original paper. For correlation between OLR1 gene expression and time-to-event analysis, biopsy cases with available RNA-seq from the SteatoSITE multimodal database were used in R (v.4.4.3). Normalized counts for OLR1 were available as part of the dataset. Decompensation events in the clinical data extract were defined by a combination of ICD codes and UK OPCS-4 codes identifying procedures relating to cirrhosis-related hospital admissions activity as previously described. Decompensation event analysis was only conducted on biopsy cases for which the first decompensation-related coding was present in the clinical data extract after the biopsy date and analysis were conducted using death as a competing risk. The cutpoint for OLR1 gene activity or SAMac2 proportion was calculated using surv_cutpoint() from the ‘survminer’ (v.0.4.9) package, applying maximally selected rank statistics of the ‘maxstat’ package (v.0.7.25) with a minimum proportion of 0.25. Kaplan–Meier estimator curves of all-cause mortality or decompensation events for assigned high/low risk groups were compared by log-rank testing in ‘survminer’.

The second MASLD dataset was downloaded from NCBI (GSE135251). The PBC dataset was downloaded from Zenodo. The ARLD dataset was provided by J.A. and R.B. The PSC dataset was provided by Gilead and analyzed by J.D.R. Raw count matrices and sample metadata table of each dataset were imported and analyzed using R (v.4.3.2). Raw count data were variance stabilized using a variance-stabilizing transformation (VST) with the vst() function in the sctransform (v.0.4.2) package. Next, PCA was performed on the VST-transformed expression values, and sample clustering patterns were examined. Samples that segregated separately in PCA space (PC1/PC2) were considered potential outliers and were excluded from downstream analyses. Gene expression levels were normalized using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method by calcNormFactors() function (method = ‘TMM’) from the edgeR (v.3.42.4) package. Normalized expression values were expressed as counts per million (CPM) using TMM-adjusted library sizes through the cpm() function (normalized.lib.sizes = TRUE, log = FALSE). Statistical analysis of expression differences in TMM-normalized CPM values for OLR1 across samples grouped by fibrosis stage was calculated by Spearman correlation tests (MASLD and PSC) performed using the cor.test() function (method = ‘spearman’, exact = FALSE) from the stats (v.4.3.2) R package, or group-wise comparisons (for ARLD and PBC) using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method, implemented through the wilcox_test() function in the rstatix (v.0.7.2) R package.

Deconvolution of bulk RNA-seq data

Bulk RNA-seq deconvolution was performed using the BayesPrism (v.2.2) R package. To generate the single-cell reference, raw count matrices were reconstructed for the MP Seurat object. Seven cell types were used as cell.type.labels, which include tissue monocytes, STAB1+ macrophages, SAMac1, SAMac2, cDCs, KCs and transitioning macrophages. Deconvolution was performed using new.prism() with default settings for model fitting and outlier handling, and final cell-type fractions were obtained from the posterior distribution. Estimated proportions across fibrosis score or fibrosis stage groups were visualized using box plots generated with ggplot2 package. Statistical testing for differences across fibrosis stages was performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests adjusted for multiple comparisons using the BH method.

Human MDM microarray analysis

Human monocyte/macrophage gene expression data were processed as described. In brief, microarray data from GEO accession GSE47189 (ref. ) were downloaded using R package GEOquery. Gene set variation analysis was performed using the R package, and the top 100 differentially expressed genes between SAMac1 and SAMac2 were used as the gene sets. The expression of pairs of genes was correlated across all conditions using Pearson correlation.

Spatial proteomic analysis

Qptiff files from the PCF were registered into spatial data zarr (v.2) using Sopa (v.2.0.8). Cell segmentation was performed using Cellpose (v.4.0.7). A cell segmentation layer was generated using DRAQ5 as nucleus channel and a composite channel of HLA-A, ACTB, ECAD, CD45 and CD68 for the membrane channel through 99th percentile rescaling and Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization of each channel, followed by merging. Mean pixel intensity was extracted from each cell segmentation mask, yielding a cell × protein matrix that was analyzed in Seurat (v.5.2.1). Areas with artifactual staining and cells with low raw DAPI expression or area of <120 px2 or >4,000 px2 were removed (pixel to μm = 1:0.5050). Each sample was individually normalized using center log ratio for each feature independently. Samples were then integrated, scaled and PCA computed. Batch correction was done using reciprocal PCA with FindIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData functions using default parameters. Clustering and cell-type annotation were then performed similarly to a previously described method. In brief, clustering was done at a coarse lineage level followed by iterative cell subtype clustering. Clusters were manually annotated based on marker expression. Clusters that could not be easily annotated were subjected to manual gating of known markers (for example, T cell subtype markers). To address the issue of lateral spillover (that is, bright expression from one cell type may ‘spill’ into its neighbor, thereby causing that cell to be mislabeled), we visualized the markers on the images using Napari. Annotations of the clusters that represented lateral spillover artifacts were corrected to the appropriate cell type. Finally, for clusters that had no reliable annotation after multiple rounds of gating, we performed a final round of clustering and label transfer using FindTransferAnchors and MapQuery on default parameters from Seurat. Any remaining cells without a reliable label following this process were annotated as unknown. To confirm annotation accuracy, all cluster labels were then manually examined and confirmed on the staining images. Cell density was calculated as the number of cells per mm2 tissue area by converting pixel to mm2 using conversion factor (0.5050 μm pixel−1). Cell-type proportions were computed for each patient and condition by dividing the number of cells of each type by the total number of cells within the grouping unit (patient or condition). Cellular niche analysis on annotated spatial object was conducted using the scNiche package (v.1.1.1), using suggested parameters at kcutoff = 30, target_ k (clusters) = 3. Cell-type enrichment within spatial niches was assessed by dividing observed proportion of each cell type by the expected proportion. Then, enrichment was quantified by log2(O/E) = log2((mean observed + ε) / (mean expected + ε)), where ε = 10−6. Niche area was calculated by the sum of all cells from the niche as a percentage of total sample area per patient. Patient-level mean intensity values were computed for COL1 and COL3 across all cells assigned to a particular niche. Statistical analysis was performed in R. For comparisons of cell-type density across multiple etiology groups, one-way analysis of variance was used followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons against the control group using the multcomp package (v.1.4-29). For comparisons across spatial niches, Kruskal–Wallis test was applied followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with BH adjustment. Two group comparisons (control versus CLD) were performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.

Statistics and reproducibility

All nonbioinformatics data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 software. All graphs are shown as mean ± s.e.m. Normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and subsequent parametric and nonparametric statistical analyses were chosen accordingly. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample sizes. Sample sizes for in vivo mouse work are similar to those previously reported in mouse CLD. Computational data excluded from analyses (for example, cells failing QC thresholds) are described in subsections in . Human experiments were not randomized but mice were randomly allocated to different treatment groups. Data collection and analysis were not performed blindly to the conditions of the experiments. Statistically significant comparisons are highlighted as *P ≤ 0.05, **P ≤ 0.01, ***P ≤ 0.001, ****P ≤ 0.0001.
