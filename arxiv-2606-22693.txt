Abstract
. The paper offers the first systematic study of ordinary and essential numerical ranges of operators on ℓ p \ell_{p} , 1 < p < ∞ 1<p<\infty , as an atomic picture within a broader L p L^{p} project. The paper begins with Banach-space foundations, including the finite-codimensional description of the essential numerical range and a Banach-space convex-hull inclusion for the essential spectrum. It then turns to finite-dimensional ℓ p \ell_{p} geometry, where one finds both positive star-shapedness phenomena and explicit 2 × 2 2\times 2 counterexamples. On ℓ p \ell_{p} , we prove that the essential numerical range is compact and convex, identify it with the algebraic numerical range of the Calkin image, obtain a compact-perturbation formula, and show that, moreover, the closure of the numerical range is star-shaped, while points in the interior of the essential numerical range are exact star-centres of the numerical range. The paper illustrates the developed theory with sequence-space examples, covering tridiagonal Toeplitz operators and the discrete Hilbert transform, and, after relating our study to a variant of the Crouzeix inequality, closes with a brief discussion of extensions to spaces of class ( P ) (P) and to joint essential numerical ranges.

Introduction

The numerical range
W(T)
of a linear operator
T
on a Hilbert space
H
, and its generalisations, are of fundamental importance throughout operator theory, including spectral theory, functional calculi, dilation theory and operator-norm estimates.
A good contemporary account of most of the important features of
W(T)
can be found in
[
23
]
, and recent papers
[
1
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
27
]

and
[
30
]
can serve as pertinent illustrations.
The applications of numerical ranges also extend to PDE theory, where they are used as spectral enclosures, to control theory and engineering, where they appear in the study of structured eigenvalue problems and convex and non-convex optimisation, and to mathematical physics and quantum information theory. A fundamental property of
W(T)
is its convexity, discovered by Toeplitz and Hausdorff. This observation led to a surprisingly fruitful theory, to many useful generalisations, such as higher-rank, infinite, essential and joint numerical ranges, and to convexity phenomena in related contexts, see, in particular,
[
23
]
and
[
24
]
. At the same time, topological properties of
W(T)
remain rather mysterious and are still not well understood. The situation is even more difficult on non-Hilbert spaces, where very little information is available.

Outside Hilbert space the situation changes drastically: the spatial numerical range is typically non-convex, duality becomes less rigid, and even basic geometric conclusions require additional arguments.
This is already visible on
\ell_{p}
for
p\neq 2
, see Section
2
.

Numerical ranges on non-Hilbert spaces have remained a rather obscure subject, and only very limited results seem to be available in this direction. One reason is that the study of numerical ranges on such spaces is technically demanding even in low-dimensional situations: already the case of rank-two operators presents substantial difficulties. This stands in sharp contrast with the Hilbert space setting, where many basic structural facts about numerical ranges can be reduced to the analysis of numerical ranges of
2\times 2
matrices.

The purpose of the present paper is to isolate the atomic
\ell_{p}
part of a broader
L^{p}
project. We adopt the view that the atomic case should be treated first. It is rich enough to display the main non-Hilbertian phenomena, but still concrete enough to allow explicit calculations, exact examples, and a reasonably complete atomic picture. The non-atomic
L^{p}
theory, with its genuinely different mechanisms, is better treated separately.

Since the paper is concerned mainly with
\ell_{p}:=\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N})
, we record already here how the two numerical ranges should be read in that setting. Throughout this introductory discussion
1<p<\infty
and
q
is the conjugate exponent. We identify
(\ell_{p})^{*}
with
\ell_{q}
by means of the pairing

\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_{j\geq 1}x_{j}\overline{y_{j}},\qquad x=(x_{j})\in\ell_{p},\qquad y=(y_{j})\in\ell_{q}.

For
x=(x_{j})
on the unit sphere of
\ell_{p}
, the unique norming functional is represented by

J(x)=(x_{j}|x_{j}|^{p-2})_{j\geq 1}\in\ell_{q},\qquad\|J(x)\|_{q}=1=\langle x,J(x)\rangle.

Thus
W(T)
, for
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})
, is obtained by testing
T
on pairs
(x,J(x))
with
x
of norm one:

W(T)=\bigl\{\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle:\ \|x\|_{p}=1\bigr\}.

In the same concrete
\ell_{p}
setting, the essential numerical range may be described sequentially: a complex number
\lambda
belongs to
W_{e}(T)
precisely when there are vectors
x_{n}\in\ell_{p}
such that

\|x_{n}\|_{p}=1,\qquad x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}}0,\qquad\langle Tx_{n},J(x_{n})\rangle\to\lambda.

The formal Banach-space definitions, including the general net version, are recalled in Section
1
.

In this work, we analyse a number of examples showing that the ordinary numerical range on
\ell_{p}
,
p\neq 2
, retains several features familiar from its Hilbert-space counterpart, corresponding to
p=2
. At the same time, some of these examples reveal substantial differences between the Hilbertian and non-Hilbertian cases. In particular, for
p\neq 2
, convexity already fails in dimension two.
Against this background it is natural to ask whether the essential
numerical range retains any of the Hilbert-space features. We show that, on

\ell_{p}
, it does:
W_{e}(T)
is convex, coincides with the algebraic numerical
range of the Calkin image
\pi(T)
, contains
\operatorname{conv}\sigma_{e}(T)
,
and has the expected compact-perturbation and star-shapedness consequences,
including its relation to
W(T).

The ordinary numerical range on
\ell_{p}
,
p\neq 2
, appears to be genuinely non-Hilbertian. Nevertheless, it retains some vestiges of convexity. In the infinite-dimensional
\ell_{p}
setting,
W(T)
is governed to a considerable extent by
W_{e}(T)
, and
\overline{W(T)}
is star-shaped with every point of
W_{e}(T)
as a star-centre. It remains open whether
W(T)
itself is always star-shaped, even for operators on finite-dimensional
\ell_{p}
,
p\neq 2
.

This is close in spirit to the philosophy of
L^{p}
-operator algebras, where one
asks which Hilbert-space phenomena survive after replacing Hilbert spaces by

L^{p}
-spaces, see e.g.
[
9
,
10
]
. Here the same question is pursued for
numerical ranges, with the essential numerical range playing the role of the
stable, Calkin-level object.

Along the way we also obtain several results on numerical ranges on general Banach spaces that may be of independent interest, and we identify large natural subsets of numerical ranges on
\ell_{p}
. These sets again point to traces of convexity in the geometry of general
\ell_{p}
-spaces.

A non-atomic counterpart is developed separately in
[
37
]
. Some weaker convexity phenomena survive there as well, but the arguments are considerably more delicate and rely on methods that have no atomic analogue, most notably disjoint-support and shrinking-support constructions on non-atomic measure spaces.

The paper is organised as follows. Section
1
motivates and elaborates the
background used throughout the paper. After a short discussion of
Hilbert space numerical ranges, we recall the ordinary and essential numerical
ranges on Banach spaces, obtain a new characterisation of

W_{e}(T)
in terms of approximately biorthogonal nets, and
prove the Banach-space spectral inclusion

\conv\sigma_{e}(T)\subset W_{e}(T)

for the essential spectrum
\sigma_{e}(T).

Section
2
turns to finite-dimensional
\ell_{p}
-spaces. The
main purpose of this section is to isolate the rigid
2\times 2
geometry that
underlies the later atomic theory. We obtain an explicit parametrisation of the
numerical range, derive a non-convexity example which extends to
every finite-dimensional
\ell_{p}
-space of dimension at least two, prove that the convex hull of the diagonal entries
is always contained in the numerical range, establish star-shapedness for
triangular
2\times 2
matrices, and present an explicit counterexample
showing that the trace centre need not be a star-centre for general

2\times 2
matrices.

Section
3
contains the atomic
\ell_{p}
core of the
paper. After recalling the continuity properties of the duality map
J
, we
show that the non-convex finite-dimensional geometry persists on
\ell_{p}
.
We then prove that the numerical range contains all countable convex
combinations of the diagonal values
\langle Te_{j},J(e_{j})\rangle
, with respect to the
standard unit basis
(e_{j})_{j\geq 1}
in
\ell_{p}
. For the essential numerical
range we obtain a tail-space characterisation, a finite-codimensional
annihilator form, convexity, a Calkin-algebra description, and the
compact-perturbation formula. For

\lambda\in\Int W_{e}(T)
, we obtain a
\lambda
-diagonal compression statement,
thus matching its Hilbert space counterpart.
Finally, we prove that

\Int W_{e}(T)\subset W(T),

that
\overline{W(T)}
is star-shaped with every point of
W_{e}(T)
as a
star-centre, and that, when
\Int W_{e}(T)\neq\varnothing
, every point of

\Int W_{e}(T)
is a star-centre of
W(T)
.

Section
4
collects sequence-space examples that still belong
naturally to the atomic side of the theory. We treat unilateral Toeplitz
operators and explicit tridiagonal examples on
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, the
discrete Hilbert transform on
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z})
, and the unilateral shift.
The section ends with a discussion of the failure of Crouzeix-type estimates
on
\ell_{p}
and with finite-dimensional shift examples leading to a natural
question about the correct order of finite-dimensional Crouzeix-type bounds.

Section
5
indicates two natural extensions of the present work:
spaces of class
(P)
and joint essential numerical ranges. The appendix
contains the computational proof of the
\ell_{3}^{2}
counterexample used in
Section
2
.

To keep the paper within reasonable limits, we do not elaborate here
numerical ranges on
\ell_{p}
for special classes of operators, such as
essentially Hermitian operators and related lifting questions, cf.

[
2
]
, nor several further structural questions.

1.
Numerical and essential numerical ranges on Banach spaces

1.1.
Notation and conventions

We collect the notation used throughout the paper. We write

\mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\},\qquad\mathbb{T}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}.

Thus
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
denotes the closed unit disc. For a subset
E\subset\mathbb{C}
we write
\overline{E}
for its closure,
\partial E
for its boundary,
\Int E
for its interior, and
\conv E
for its convex hull.

If
X
is a Banach space, then
\mathcal{L}(X)
denotes the algebra of all bounded linear operators on
X
, and
X^{*}
denotes the dual space. The duality pairing is written as
\langle x,x^{*}\rangle
. For a subset
A\subset X
we put

A^{\perp}:=\{x^{*}\in X^{*}:\langle a,x^{*}\rangle=0\text{ for all }a\in A\}.

We write
x_{\alpha}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}}x
for weak convergence in
X
, and
x_{\alpha}^{*}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}^{*}}x^{*}
for weak-* convergence in
X^{*}
. We also set

\Pi(X):=\{(x,x^{*})\in X\times X^{*}:\|x\|=\|x^{*}\|=1=\langle x,x^{*}\rangle\}.

Throughout, finite-codimensional subspaces are understood to be closed.
In expressions like “
x_{n}\to x
as
n\to\infty
” we omit the range of the parameter
whenever the nature of the limit is clear.

If
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, then
\sigma(T)
denotes its spectrum. We write
\mathcal{K}(X)
for the ideal of compact operators on
X
.

Throughout the paper,
\ell_{p}
,
1<p<\infty
, stands for the usual complex sequence space

\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N})
.
When the underlying index set is relevant, we write it explicitly, for example

\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
or
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z})
, where

\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. We denote by
\ell_{p}^{n}
the space

\mathbb{C}^{n}
equipped with the canonical
\|\cdot\|_{p}
-norm.

On these sequence spaces we use the convention

\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_{j}x_{j}\overline{y_{j}},\qquad x=(x_{j})\in\ell_{p},\quad y=(y_{j})\in\ell_{q},

under the standard identification
(\ell_{p})^{*}=\ell_{q}
, where
q
is the conjugate exponent.

1.2.
Hilbert-space theory

For orientation and to put our results into proper context, we recall the classical Hilbert-space picture.
It will be a pattern for the rest of the paper.
Let
H
be a complex Hilbert space and let
T\in\mathcal{L}(H)
. Then

W(T):=\{\langle Tx,x\rangle:x\in H,\ \|x\|=1\}

denotes the numerical range of
T
, and

w(T):=\sup\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in W(T)\}

denotes the numerical radius.

The numerical range is a fundamental concept in Hilbert-space operator
theory, with numerous applications. A concise account of its basic properties
can be found in
[
24
]
. For a more recent and comprehensive treatment,
see
[
23
]
. We now recall the facts from the Hilbert-space theory that
will serve as a reference point for the rest of the paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(H)
. Then:

(i)

W(T)
is a convex subset of
\mathbb{C}
;

(ii)

\frac{\|T\|}{2}\leq w(T)\leq\|T\|
;

(iii)

\sigma(T)\subset\overline{W(T)}
;

(iv)

T
is selfadjoint if and only if
W(T)\subset\mathbb{R}
. In this case,
\overline{W(T)}=\conv\sigma(T)
and
w(T)=\|T\|
.

There are many variants of the numerical range of Hilbert space operators
which also exhibit convexity, see, for instance,
[
23
, Sections 4 and 8]

and also
[
35
, Section 5]
,
[
36
]
and the references therein.
The most important of these variants for the present paper, and also for
applications, is the essential numerical range. In the Hilbert-space setting
it was systematically studied in the foundational paper
[
21
]
.

On a Hilbert space
H
, the essential numerical range of
T\in\mathcal{L}(H)
is defined by

\begin{split}W_{e}(T):=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:&\ \exists\ (x_{n})\subset H,\ \|x_{n}\|=1\ \text{for all }n,\\
&\ x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}}0,\ \langle Tx_{n},x_{n}\rangle\to\lambda\}.\end{split}

Equivalently, one may require only that

(x_{n})\subset H
is an orthonormal sequence
and
\langle Tx_{n},x_{n}\rangle\to\lambda
.

We also use the corresponding essential spectrum, which is easy to describe in terms of the Calkin algebra
\mathcal{L}(H)/\mathcal{K}(H).
Let
\mathcal{K}(H)
denote the ideal of all compact operators on
H
, and let

\pi:\mathcal{L}(H)\to\mathcal{L}(H)/\mathcal{K}(H)

be the canonical quotient map. For
T\in\mathcal{L}(H)
we then define

\sigma_{e}(T):=\sigma\bigl(\pi(T),\mathcal{L}(H)/\mathcal{K}(H)\bigr).

The basic Hilbert-space properties of the essential numerical range may be summarised as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(H)
.
Then:

(i)

W_{e}(T)
is a compact convex subset of
\mathbb{C}
;

(ii)

\sigma_{e}(T)\subset W_{e}(T)
;

(iii)

W_{e}(T)=\bigcap\{\overline{W(T+L)}:L\in\mathcal{K}(H)\};

(iv)

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, one has
\lambda\in W_{e}(T)
if and only if for every subspace
M\subset H
of finite codimension and every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a unit vector
x\in M
such that
|\langle Tx,x\rangle-\lambda|<\varepsilon
;

(v)

If
\lambda\in\operatorname{Int}W_{e}(T)
, then
\lambda\in W(T)
. Moreover, for every subspace
M\subset H
of finite codimension there exists a unit vector
x\in M
such that
\langle Tx,x\rangle=\lambda
.

The properties (i)–(iii) are standard and can be found, for instance, in

[
23
, Chapter 4.2]
. For the proof of (iv), see e.g.

[
34
, Lemma 4.1]
, while the proof of (v) can be found in

[
35
, Corollary 4.2]
. The latter two properties are established
there in the more general setting of operator tuples.

Both the numerical range and the essential numerical range are intimately related to the notion of algebraic numerical range, and in particular to the numerical range in Calkin algebras. Recall that if
\mathcal{A}
is a unital Banach algebra with unit
1_{\mathcal{A}}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
then the algebraic numerical range
V(a,\mathcal{A})
of
a
is given by

V(a,\mathcal{A})=\{f(a):f\in\mathcal{A}^{*},\|f\|=1=f(1_{\mathcal{A}})\},

where
\mathcal{A}^{*}
denotes the dual of
\mathcal{A}
, i.e., the space of all bounded linear functionals on
\mathcal{A}
. The algebraic numerical range in any unital Banach algebra is a closed convex set, see e.g.
[
12
, Chapter 1.2]
or
[
23
, Chapter 4.1]
.

The next statement records the Hilbert-space identities for the algebraic
numerical range, including the Calkin-algebra identity generalised below, see e.g.

[
23
, Chapters 4.1 and 4.2]
. More general theory is discussed in

[
12
, Chapter 3, §9]
and
[
13
, Chapter 7, §34]
, see also
Theorem
1.5
below.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(H)
. Then:

(i)

V(T,\mathcal{L}(H))=\overline{W(T)}
;

(ii)

V\bigl(\pi(T),\mathcal{L}(H)/\mathcal{K}(H)\bigr)=W_{e}(T),

where
\pi:\mathcal{L}(H)\to\mathcal{L}(H)/\mathcal{K}(H)
is the quotient map.

The rest of the paper studies, in particular, which parts of this Hilbertian picture survive, and in what form, on
\ell_{p}
for
p\neq 2
.

1.3.
Numerical ranges in Banach spaces

Given a complex Banach space
X,
the numerical range of an operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
is defined by

(1.1)

W(T):=\{\langle Tx,x^{*}\rangle:(x,x^{*})\in\Pi(X)\},

where
\Pi(X)
is as in the notation above.
Then its numerical radius is given by

w(T):=\sup\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in W(T)\}.

Standard references for the general theory of numerical ranges include

[
12
]
and
[
13
]
. The numerical range of a Banach-space operator
is much less rigid than in the Hilbert setting, and its topology is not
understood even on Banach spaces with very simple structure.
However, the following counterparts of Hilbertian properties remain true.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. Then:

(i)

\conv\sigma(T)\subset\overline{W(T)}
;

(ii)

\exp(-1)\|T\|\leq w(T)\leq\|T\|
;

(iii)

\overline{\conv}\,W(T)=V(T,\mathcal{L}(X))
.

The properties stated in Theorem
1.4
can be found in
[
12
,
13
]
.
In particular, the result in (i) is due to Crabb, and is presented in
[
13
, Theorem 3, p.22]
.

In general, the numerical range is not convex in the Banach space setting, and thus to relate it to algebraic numerical range one has to pass to its convex hull. Moreover, the theory of Calkin algebras on Banach spaces is more involved
and its interplay with the theory of (essential) numerical ranges depends on the geometric properties of the underlying Banach space. See Subsection
3.4
for more on that.
The next statement records the properties of algebraic numerical range which can be formulated for general Banach spaces,
and can be found in
[
12
, Chapter 3, §9]
and
[
13
, Chapter 7, §34]
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(X).
Then the following hold.

(i)

V(T,\mathcal{L}(X))=\overline{\conv}\,W(T)
;

(ii)

V\bigl(\pi(T),\mathcal{L}(X)/\mathcal{K}(X)\bigr)=\bigcap\{V(T+K,\mathcal{L}(X)):K\in\mathcal{K}(X)\},

where
\pi:\mathcal{L}(X)\to\mathcal{L}(X)/\mathcal{K}(X)
is the quotient map.

1.4.
Essential numerical range

We now pass from the ordinary numerical range to its essential counterpart.
The first goal is to record the general Banach-space framework in a form suited to the later
\ell_{p}
arguments.

We now define the main object of our study.

Definition 1.6
.

Let
X
be a complex Banach space and
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. The essential numerical range
W_{e}(T)
is the set of all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
such that there exists a net
(x_{\alpha},x^{*}_{\alpha})\subset\Pi(X)
with

x_{\alpha}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}}0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\langle Tx_{\alpha},x^{*}_{\alpha}\rangle\to\lambda.

Remark 1.7
.

If
X
is finite-dimensional, then
W_{e}(T)=\varnothing
. If
X
is infinite-dimensional, the non-emptiness of
W_{e}(T)
is immediate from the net definition. Let
\mathcal{U}
be the directed set of weak neighbourhoods of
0
, ordered by reverse inclusion. For each
U\in\mathcal{U}
, choose
(x_{U},x_{U}^{*})\in\Pi(X)
with
x_{U}\in U
. Then
x_{U}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}}0
, and the scalar net
\langle Tx_{U},x_{U}^{*}\rangle
is bounded. Hence it has a convergent subnet, whose limit belongs to
W_{e}(T)
.
Moreover, by
[
6
, Proposition 5]
, the set
W_{e}(T)
is closed, and is thus compact for arbitrary Banach-space operators.

Note that
[
6
, Theorems 11 and 13]
shows that in reflexive spaces, and more generally in Asplund spaces, the net definition may be replaced by an equivalent sequential one.
On
\ell_{p},

1<p<\infty
, one may therefore deal with sequences when convenient. In that setting closedness also follows by a standard diagonal argument.

A basic perturbation tool for the study of numerical ranges on Banach spaces is the following Bishop-Phelps-Bollobás (BPB) theorem, see e.g.
[
13
, Theorem 16.1]
.

Theorem 1.8
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
, let
x\in X
and
x^{*}\in X^{*}
satisfy
\|x\|\leq 1
,
\|x^{*}\|=1
, and

|\langle x,x^{*}\rangle-1|<\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}.

Then there exists
(y,y^{*})\in\Pi(X)
such that

\|y-x\|<\varepsilon,\qquad\|y^{*}-x^{*}\|<\varepsilon.

We shall use the following Banach-space characterisation of the essential numerical range. The equivalence between the net definition and the finite-codimensional formulation is
[
6
, Proposition 6]
. The intermediate approximate-normalisation form is included for later reference.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Then the following are equivalent.

(i)

\lambda\in W_{e}(T)
.

(ii)

There exists a net
(x_{\alpha},x^{*}_{\alpha})\in X\times X^{*}
such that

x_{\alpha}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}}0,\quad\|x_{\alpha}\|\to 1,\quad\|x_{\alpha}^{*}\|\to 1,\quad\langle x_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}^{*}\rangle\to 1,\quad\langle Tx_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}^{*}\rangle\to\lambda.

(iii)

For every subspace
M\subset X
with
\codim M<\infty
, and for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
(x,x^{*})\in\Pi(X)
with
x\in M
and

|\langle Tx,x^{*}\rangle-\lambda|<\varepsilon.

Proof.

The equivalence of (i) and (iii) is the finite-codimensional characterisation of the essential numerical range from
[
6
, Proposition 6]
. The implication (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) is immediate from the definition of
W_{e}(T)
.

It remains only to justify that (ii) implies (iii). Let
M\subset X
be finite-codimensional and let
\varepsilon>0
. Put

L:=M\cap T^{-1}(M).

Then
L
is finite-codimensional. Choose a closed finite-dimensional complement
G
of
L
and let
Q:X\to G
be the corresponding projection with
\Ker Q=L
. Since
x_{\alpha}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}}0
and
Q
has finite rank,
Qx_{\alpha}\to 0
in norm. Hence, for

u_{\alpha}:=x_{\alpha}-Qx_{\alpha}\in L,

we have

\|u_{\alpha}-x_{\alpha}\|\to 0,\qquad\|u_{\alpha}\|\to 1.

Restrict
x_{\alpha}^{*}
to
M
. Since
u_{\alpha}\in L\subset M
and
Tu_{\alpha}\in M
, the assumptions in (ii) imply

\|x_{\alpha}^{*}|_{M}\|\to 1,\qquad\langle u_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}^{*}|_{M}\rangle\to 1,\qquad\langle Tu_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}^{*}|_{M}\rangle\to\lambda.

For large
\alpha
, set

v_{\alpha}:=\frac{u_{\alpha}}{\|u_{\alpha}\|},\qquad f_{\alpha}:=\frac{x_{\alpha}^{*}|_{M}}{\|x_{\alpha}^{*}|_{M}\|}\in M^{*}.

Then
\|v_{\alpha}\|=\|f_{\alpha}\|=1
and

\langle v_{\alpha},f_{\alpha}\rangle\to 1.

Next, apply the Bishop–Phelps–Bollobás theorem in the Banach space
M
with an error tending to zero. We obtain pairs
(y_{\alpha},g_{\alpha})\in\Pi(M)
such that

\|y_{\alpha}-v_{\alpha}\|\to 0,\qquad\|g_{\alpha}-f_{\alpha}\|\to 0.

Let
y_{\alpha}^{*}\in X^{*}
be a Hahn–Banach extension of
g_{\alpha}
. Then
(y_{\alpha},y_{\alpha}^{*})\in\Pi(X)
and
y_{\alpha}\in M
. Since
u_{\alpha}\in L
, we have
Tu_{\alpha}\in M
. Hence

\displaystyle\langle Ty_{\alpha},y_{\alpha}^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle=y_{\alpha}^{*}(T(y_{\alpha}-u_{\alpha}))+g_{\alpha}(Tu_{\alpha}).

The first term tends to
0
. Moreover,

|g_{\alpha}(Tu_{\alpha})-f_{\alpha}(Tu_{\alpha})|\leq\|g_{\alpha}-f_{\alpha}\|\,\|Tu_{\alpha}\|\to 0,

and, since
\|x_{\alpha}^{*}|_{M}\|\to 1
,

f_{\alpha}(Tu_{\alpha})=\frac{\langle Tu_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}^{*}|_{M}\rangle}{\|x_{\alpha}^{*}|_{M}\|}\to\lambda.

Thus
\langle Ty_{\alpha},y_{\alpha}^{*}\rangle\to\lambda
, and choosing
\alpha
sufficiently large gives the pair required in (iii).
∎

1.5.
A Banach-space convex-hull inclusion for
W_{e}(T)

We next relate the essential numerical range to spectral data. The resulting convex-hull inclusion is one of the Banach-space inputs that later reappears in the atomic
\ell_{p}
setting.

For a Banach space
X
, we define the essential spectrum as in the Hilbert-space case, using the Calkin algebra. Thus, if
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
and
\pi:\mathcal{L}(X)\to\mathcal{L}(X)/\mathcal{K}(X)
is the quotient map, then

\sigma_{e}(T):=\sigma\bigl(\pi(T),\mathcal{L}(X)/\mathcal{K}(X)\bigr).

The essential approximate point spectrum
\sigma_{\pi e}(T)
often arises in the study of numerical ranges in Hilbert spaces,
and we will also need this notion for developing the Banach space theory. It can be defined in several equivalent ways, and for the purposes of this paper
we set

\sigma_{\pi e}(T):=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\begin{array}[]{l}\forall M\subset X\text{ finite-codimensional},\ \forall\varepsilon>0,\\[2.84526pt]
\exists x\in M\text{ with }\|x\|=1\text{ and }\|(T-\lambda I)x\|<\varepsilon.\end{array}\right\}

Alternatively one may use approximate eigenvectors converging weakly to zero, resembling equivalent descriptions of
W_{e}(T)
in
Theorem
1.9
.

We shall use the standard inclusions

\partial\sigma_{e}(T)\subset\sigma_{\pi e}(T)\subset\sigma_{e}(T),

see e.g.
[
33
, Chapter III.19, Proposition 1]
.

Consequently, using the elementary fact that

\operatorname{conv}K=\operatorname{conv}\partial K
for compact
sets
K\subset\mathbb{C}
, we have

(1.2)

\operatorname{conv}\sigma_{e}(T)=\operatorname{conv}\sigma_{\pi e}(T).

The proof of the following theorem uses two standard auxiliary facts from
Banach space geometry. The first is a finite-codimensional separation device,
and the second is a classical convex-combination selection lemma due to
Zenger. Both are standard, see, for example,
[
33
, p. 329]

and
[
13
, p. 20]
, respectively.

Lemma 1.10
.

Let
N\subset X
be a finite-dimensional subspace and let
0<\delta<1
. Then there exists a finite-codimensional subspace
M\subset X
such that

\|u+v\|\geq(1-\delta)\|u\|\qquad(u\in N,\ v\in M).

Lemma 1.11
.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
be linearly independent vectors in
X,

and let
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}>0
satisfy
\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}=1
. Then there exist complex numbers
t_{1},\dots,t_{m}
, a vector

x=\sum_{k=1}^{m}t_{k}x_{k}\in X,

and a functional
f\in X^{*}
such that

\|x\|=\|f\|=\langle x,f\rangle=1,\qquad\langle t_{k}x_{k},f\rangle=a_{k}\quad(1\leq k\leq m).

We now combine these two lemmas with the definition of
\sigma_{\pi e}(T)
to obtain an inclusion result for the convex hull of the essential spectrum,
which seems to have been missing from the literature.
It is the essential-spectrum counterpart of Crabb’s Theorem
1.4
,(i).

Theorem 1.12
.

Let
X
be a complex Banach space and let
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. Then

\conv\sigma_{e}(T)\subset W_{e}(T).

Proof.

By (
1.2
), it is enough to show that

\operatorname{conv}\sigma_{\pi e}(T)\subset W_{e}(T).

Let

\lambda=\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\lambda_{k},\qquad a_{k}>0,\quad\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}=1,\quad\lambda_{k}\in\sigma_{\pi e}(T),

and let
M\subset X
be a finite-codimensional subspace. We prove that,
for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
(x,x^{*})\in\Pi(X)
such that

x\in M
and

|\langle Tx,x^{*}\rangle-\lambda|<\varepsilon.

The conclusion will then follow from
Theorem
1.9
.

Choose
0<\delta<1/2
, and put

C:=(1-\delta)^{-1},\qquad\eta:=\frac{\varepsilon}{2mC}.

We construct unit vectors
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in M
. Set
L_{0}:=M
. Since

\lambda_{1}\in\sigma_{\pi e}(T)
, choose
x_{1}\in L_{0}
such that

\|x_{1}\|=1,\qquad\|(T-\lambda_{1}I)x_{1}\|<\eta.

Suppose that
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
have been chosen, where
1\leq r<m
, and
let

F_{r}:=\operatorname{span}\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\}.

By Lemma
1.10
, there is a finite-codimensional
subspace
N_{r}\subset X
such that

\|u+v\|\geq(1-\delta)\|u\|\qquad(u\in F_{r},\ v\in N_{r}).

In particular,
F_{r}\cap N_{r}=\{0\}
. Define

L_{r}:=M\cap N_{1}\cap\cdots\cap N_{r}.

Then
L_{r}
is finite-codimensional. Since

\lambda_{r+1}\in\sigma_{\pi e}(T)
, choose
x_{r+1}\in L_{r}
such that

\|x_{r+1}\|=1,\qquad\|(T-\lambda_{r+1}I)x_{r+1}\|<\eta.

This completes the recursive construction.

Since
x_{r+1}\in N_{r}
and
F_{r}\cap N_{r}=\{0\}
for
1\leq r<m
, the
vectors
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
are linearly independent. Let

Y:=\operatorname{span}\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\}.

For
0\leq r\leq m
, let
P_{r}:Y\to Y
be the projection onto

\operatorname{span}\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\}
along

\operatorname{span}\{x_{r+1},\ldots,x_{m}\}
, with
P_{0}=0
and
P_{m}=I_{Y}
.
We claim that

\|P_{r}\|\leq C\qquad(0\leq r\leq m).

For
r=0
and
r=m
this is clear. Let
1\leq r<m
, and write

y=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\beta_{k}x_{k}\in Y

with complex scalars
\beta_{k}
. Then

P_{r}y\in F_{r},\qquad y-P_{r}y=\sum_{k=r+1}^{m}\beta_{k}x_{k}\in N_{r},

because
x_{k}\in L_{k-1}\subset N_{r}
for
k>r
. Hence

\|y\|\geq(1-\delta)\|P_{r}y\|,

and the claim follows.

Let
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}\in Y^{*}
be the coordinate functionals
associated with the basis
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
. Since

(P_{k}-P_{k-1})y=\varphi_{k}(y)x_{k},\qquad\|x_{k}\|=1,

we have

|\varphi_{k}(y)|\leq\bigl(\|P_{k}\|+\|P_{k-1}\|\bigr)\|y\|\leq 2C\|y\|\qquad(1\leq k\leq m),

and therefore

\|\varphi_{k}\|\leq 2C\qquad(1\leq k\leq m).

By Lemma
1.11
, there exist complex scalars
t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}
, a
vector

x:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}t_{k}x_{k}\in Y,

and a functional
g\in Y^{*}
such that

\|x\|=\|g\|=\langle x,g\rangle=1,\qquad\langle t_{k}x_{k},g\rangle=a_{k}\quad(1\leq k\leq m).

Let
x^{*}\in X^{*}
be a Hahn–Banach extension of
g
with

\|x^{*}\|=1
. Then

(x,x^{*})\in\Pi(X),\qquad x\in Y\subset M.

Moreover, since
t_{k}=\varphi_{k}(x)
,

|t_{k}|\leq 2C\qquad(1\leq k\leq m).

Using

\langle t_{k}x_{k},x^{*}\rangle=\langle t_{k}x_{k},g\rangle=a_{k}\qquad(1\leq k\leq m),

we obtain

\displaystyle\langle Tx,x^{*}\rangle-\lambda

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\langle t_{k}(T-\lambda_{k}I)x_{k},x^{*}\rangle.

Consequently,

\displaystyle|\langle Tx,x^{*}\rangle-\lambda|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}|t_{k}|\,\|(T-\lambda_{k}I)x_{k}\|\,\|x^{*}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}2C\eta=\varepsilon.

Since
M
and
\varepsilon
were arbitrary,
Theorem
1.9
gives

\lambda\in W_{e}(T)
.
∎

As an immediate corollary, we are able to identify the essential numerical range of isometries with large peripheral spectrum,
in particular, of non-invertible isometries.

Corollary 1.13
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
be an isometry such that

\mathbb{T}\subset\sigma(T).

Then

(1.3)

W_{e}(T)=\overline{\mathbb{D}}.

In particular, the equality (
1.3
) holds if
T
is a non-invertible isometry.

Proof.

For every
(x,x^{*})\in\Pi(X)
one has

|\langle Tx,x^{*}\rangle|\leq\|Tx\|\,\|x^{*}\|=1,

so

W_{e}(T)\subset\overline{W(T)}\subset\overline{\mathbb{D}}.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}
. Since
\sigma(T)\subset\overline{\mathbb{D}}
for every isometry and
\mathbb{T}\subset\sigma(T)
by assumption, the point
\lambda
belongs to
\partial\sigma(T)
and is not isolated in
\sigma(T)
. Hence
\lambda\in\sigma_{e}(T)
, see, for example,
[
33
, Theorem III.18.4]
and
[
28
, Proposition 3.7.8]
. Therefore

\mathbb{T}\subset\sigma_{e}(T).

By Theorem
1.12
,

\overline{\mathbb{D}}=\conv\mathbb{T}\subset\conv\sigma_{e}(T)\subset W_{e}(T).

Thus
W_{e}(T)=\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

Finally, if
T
is a non-invertible isometry, then
\sigma(T)=\overline{\mathbb{D}}
by, for example,
[
40
, Corollary 6.14]
. Hence the last claim follows.
∎

This concludes the Banach-space preparation used in the sequel. We now turn to the explicit
2\times 2
geometry on finite-dimensional
\ell_{p}
-spaces, which forms the first finite-dimensional layer of the atomic theory.

2.
Numerical ranges on finite-dimensional
\ell_{p}
-spaces

This section gives the first finite-dimensional layer of the atomic theory. We work throughout with a fixed exponent
1<p<\infty
, and we write
q
for the conjugate exponent, so that
1/p+1/q=1
. We keep the scalar spatial numerical range on finite-dimensional
\ell_{p}
and isolate the concrete
2\times 2
geometry that later underlies the atomic theory.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we write
\ell_{p}^{n}
for the
n
-dimensional
\ell_{p}
-space.
Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
be the standard basis of
\ell_{p}^{n}
and
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
the standard basis of
\ell_{q}^{n}
.
For

x=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}e_{j}\in\ell_{p}^{n},\qquad x^{*}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}f_{j}\in\ell_{q}^{n},

we have

(2.1)

\langle x,x^{*}\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\overline{\beta_{j}}.

Throughout this section, matrices are identified with the corresponding
operators with respect to the standard bases.

If
x\in\ell_{p}^{n}
is a unit vector, then

J(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}|\alpha_{j}|^{p-2}f_{j}\qquad\text{for }x=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}e_{j},

is the unique norming functional of
x
. Hence, for
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{n})
,

W(T)=\{\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle:x\in\ell_{p}^{n},\ \|x\|=1\}.

2.1.
The
2\times 2
parametrisation

This parametrisation is the basic computational tool for the finite-dimensional
part of the paper.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{2})
be represented as

T=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}

with
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{C}.
Set

\tau:=\frac{a+d}{2},\qquad\alpha:=\frac{a-d}{2},

\beta(r):=r^{1/q}(1-r)^{1/p},\qquad\gamma(r):=r^{1/p}(1-r)^{1/q}.

For
0\leq r\leq 1
put

(2.2)

E_{r}:=\Bigl\{(2r-1)\alpha+\beta(r)be^{i\theta}+\gamma(r)ce^{-i\theta}:\theta\in[0,2\pi]\Bigr\}.

Then

W(T)=\tau+\bigcup_{0\leq r\leq 1}E_{r}.

Proof.

Let
x=(u,v)\in\ell_{p}^{2}
be a unit vector. Multiplying
x
by a scalar of modulus one does not change the value of

\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle
, because
J(\omega x)=\omega J(x)
for
|\omega|=1
. Hence, after such a rotation, we may write

u=r^{1/p},\qquad v=e^{i\theta}(1-r)^{1/p}

with
0\leq r\leq 1
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi].
For this vector,

J(x)=\bigl(r^{1/q},e^{i\theta}(1-r)^{1/q}\bigr).

Using (
2.1
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=\bigl(ar^{1/p}+be^{i\theta}(1-r)^{1/p}\bigr)r^{1/q}

\displaystyle\quad+\bigl(cr^{1/p}+de^{i\theta}(1-r)^{1/p}\bigr)e^{-i\theta}(1-r)^{1/q}

\displaystyle=ar+d(1-r)+b\,r^{1/q}(1-r)^{1/p}e^{i\theta}+c\,r^{1/p}(1-r)^{1/q}e^{-i\theta}

\displaystyle=\tau+(2r-1)\alpha+\beta(r)be^{i\theta}+\gamma(r)ce^{-i\theta}.

This proves the formula for
W(T)
.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

For fixed
r
, the set
E_{r}
is a possibly degenerate ellipse with centre

(2r-1)\alpha
. More precisely, write

b=Be^{i\phi},\qquad c=Ce^{i\psi},\qquad B:=|b|,\qquad C:=|c|,\qquad\sigma:=\frac{\phi+\psi}{2}.

Then

\displaystyle E_{r}=(2r-1)\alpha+e^{i\sigma}\Bigl\{

\displaystyle\bigl(B\beta(r)+C\gamma(r)\bigr)\cos\eta

\displaystyle+i\bigl(B\beta(r)-C\gamma(r)\bigr)\sin\eta:\eta\in[0,2\pi]\Bigr\}.

Indeed, this follows from the change of variable

\eta=\theta+(\phi-\psi)/2
. After the rotation by
e^{-i\sigma}
and
translation by
-(2r-1)\alpha
, the curve has the form

A_{r}\cos\eta+iD_{r}\sin\eta
, with
A_{r},D_{r}\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence it is an ellipse,
with the usual degeneracies allowed.

Remark 2.3
.

In the Hilbertian case
p=2
, the preceding parametrisation reduces to the usual elliptical-range picture. Indeed, then

\beta(r)=\gamma(r)=\sqrt{r(1-r)}.

Writing
t=2r-1
, we have

\sqrt{r(1-r)}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1-t^{2}}.

Using the notation of Remark
2.2
, after subtracting
\tau
we obtain

W(T)-\tau=\left\{t\alpha+\frac{1}{2}e^{i\sigma}\bigl((B+C)u+i(B-C)v\bigr):t^{2}+u^{2}+v^{2}=1\right\}.

Thus, letting
S^{2}
be the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3},
we have
W(T)-\tau=L(S^{2})
for a real-linear map
L:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}
.
Since
L
has non-trivial kernel,
L(S^{2})
is the same as the image under

L
of the closed Euclidean unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Indeed, if

y=Lx
with
\|x\|_{2}<1
, one may add a suitable multiple of a non-zero
vector in
\ker L
to reach the unit sphere without changing
Lx
.
Therefore this image is an elliptical disc, possibly degenerate.
This recovers the classical elliptic range theorem for
2\times 2
Hilbert-space operators.

Using Proposition
2.1
it is easy to produce a plethora of operators on
\ell_{p}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
with non-convex numerical range, and we give a sample below. Another example
of such an operator was given in
[
12
, Section 11]
, and similar examples were provided later in
[
3
, Example 11.5]
and
[
31
]
.

Example 2.4
.

Let
p\neq 2
. Then there exists an operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{2})
such that
W(T)
is not convex.

Proof.

Let
T
be defined as

T:=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}.

For this matrix, in the notation of Proposition
2.1
, we have

\tau=0,\qquad\alpha=1,\qquad b=c=1.

Thus Proposition
2.1
gives

W(T)=\bigcup_{0\leq r\leq 1}E_{r},

where

E_{r}=\left\{(2r-1)+(\beta(r)+\gamma(r))\cos\theta+i(\beta(r)-\gamma(r))\sin\theta:\theta\in[0,2\pi]\right\}.

Let

M:=\max\{\beta(r)-\gamma(r):0\leq r\leq 1\}.

Since

\beta(r)-\gamma(r)=r^{1/p}(1-r)^{1/p}\bigl(r^{1-2/p}-(1-r)^{1-2/p}\bigr),

and
p\neq 2
, we have
M>0
. Moreover,

\beta(1-r)-\gamma(1-r)=-(\beta(r)-\gamma(r)),

and therefore

M=\max_{0\leq r\leq 1}|\beta(r)-\gamma(r)|.

Choose
r_{0}\in[0,1]
such that

M=\beta(r_{0})-\gamma(r_{0}).

Then, for
\theta=\pi/2
,

(2r_{0}-1)+iM\in E_{r_{0}}\subset W(T).

Also,

\beta(1-r_{0})-\gamma(1-r_{0})=-M,

so for
\theta=3\pi/2
we get

1-2r_{0}+iM\in E_{1-r_{0}}\subset W(T).

We claim that
iM\notin W(T)
. More generally, if
is\in W(T)
for some
s\in\mathbb{R}
, then
|s|<M
.
Suppose on the contrary that there exist
r_{1}\in[0,1]
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
with
|s|\geq M
and
is\in E_{r_{1}}
. Then

s=(\beta(r_{1})-\gamma(r_{1}))\sin\theta

for some
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
. Hence
|s|=M
and
\sin\theta=\pm 1
, so
\cos\theta=0
. Therefore

is=2r_{1}-1+i(\beta(r_{1})-\gamma(r_{1}))\sin\theta.

It follows that
r_{1}=1/2
, and hence
\beta(r_{1})-\gamma(r_{1})=0
, so
s=0
, a contradiction.
Thus
W(T)
is not convex.
∎

2.2.
Convex hull of diagonal entries

Having identified the geometry of numerical ranges of operators on
\ell_{p}^{2}
explicitly, we turn to the first positive structural feature. The next results show that diagonal data already enforce nontrivial convexity inside the numerical range.

We start with a lemma supplying the ingredients needed for the study of diagonals in the
\ell_{p}
-setting.
It is based on an elementary homotopy argument, and
may be of independent interest.

For a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{C}
, we write
\widehat{K}
for its polynomial hull. In the plane this is the union of
K
with all bounded components of
\mathbb{C}\setminus K
. The concept enters here because the ellipses
E_{s}
are boundary ellipses, whereas the diagonal segment argument requires access to the filled region enclosed by such an ellipse.

With
E_{r}
as in (
2.2
), the following lemma says that the filled
ellipse bounded by
E_{s}
is swept out by the later ellipses
E_{r}
,

s\leq r\leq 1
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{2})
be given by

T=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix},

and let
s\in(0,1)
. Set
\tau:=(a+d)/2
. If

\lambda\in\tau+\widehat{E_{s}},

then

\lambda\in\tau+\bigcup_{s\leq r\leq 1}E_{r}\subset W(T).

Proof.

We use the description of
W(T)
given by Proposition
2.1
,
and assume without loss of generality that
\tau=0
.

The statement is clear if
\lambda\in\bigcup_{s\leq r\leq 1}E_{r}
. Assume that
\lambda\in\Int\widehat{E_{s}}
and suppose, towards a contradiction, that

\lambda\notin\bigcup_{s\leq r\leq 1}E_{r}.

For
r\in[s,1]
define a curve
f_{r}:[0,2\pi]\to\mathbb{C}
by

f_{r}(\theta)=(2r-1)\alpha+\beta(r)be^{i\theta}+\gamma(r)ce^{-i\theta}.

Then
f_{r}([0,2\pi])=E_{r}
, so
f_{r}(\theta)\neq\lambda
for all
r\in[s,1]
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
.
Moreover,
f_{1}
is a constant curve, whereas

\operatorname{ind}(f_{s},\lambda)\neq 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{ind}(f_{1},\lambda)=0.

But
(r,\theta)\mapsto f_{r}(\theta)
is a homotopy in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{\lambda\}
between
f_{s}
and
f_{1}
, a contradiction.
Therefore

\lambda\in\bigcup_{s\leq r\leq 1}E_{r},

and Proposition
2.1
implies that
\lambda\in W(T).

∎

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{2})
be represented as

T=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix},

where
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{C}.
Then

\conv\{a,d\}\subset W(T).

Proof.

The endpoints
a
and
d
are attained at the basis vectors. Let

\tau=(a+d)/2
and
\alpha=(a-d)/2
. Let

0<\varepsilon<1
. Since

(1-\varepsilon)a+\varepsilon d=\tau+(1-2\varepsilon)\alpha\in\tau+\widehat{E}_{1-\varepsilon},

Lemma
2.5
gives

(1-\varepsilon)a+\varepsilon d\in W(T)
. Hence the whole segment

\operatorname{conv}\{a,d\}
is contained in
W(T)
.
∎

We shall also use the following elementary observation. Let
X
be a finite
or infinite coordinate
\ell_{p}
-space, and let

u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\in X
be unit vectors with pairwise disjoint supports.
Put

E:=\operatorname{span}\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\},

and let
V:\ell_{p}^{m}\to E
be the isometry

V(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{j}u_{j}.

For
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, define
T_{E}\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{m})
by

T_{E}e_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle Tu_{j},J(u_{i})\rangle e_{i},\qquad j=1,\ldots,m.

We call
T_{E}
the
block compression
of
T
associated with

u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}
.
Observe that

(2.3)

W(T_{E})\subset W(T).

Indeed, if
\xi=(\xi_{j})_{j=1}^{m}
is a unit vector in
\ell_{p}^{m}
and

x=V\xi
, then
\|x\|=1
and, because the supports of the
u_{j}
’s are
pairwise disjoint,

J(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{j}|\xi_{j}|^{p-2}J(u_{j}).

Consequently,

\langle T_{E}\xi,J(\xi)\rangle=\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle\in W(T),

which proves (
2.3
).

We can now identify subsets of numerical ranges in terms of convex hulls of
diagonal entries, although the
\ell_{p}
-numerical range is not convex in general.
The result given below will also be useful for obtaining its countable convex-combination counterpart proved in Section
3
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{n})
. Then

\conv\bigl\{\langle Te_{j},J(e_{j})\rangle:j=1,\dots,n\bigr\}\subset W(T).

Proof.

The statement is clear if
n=1
. For
n=2
it is precisely Corollary
2.6
.

Assume now that
n\geq 3
and that the statement has been proved for
n-1
.
Let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\geq 0
satisfy
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}=1
.
We must show that

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\langle Te_{j},J(e_{j})\rangle\in W(T).

If
\alpha_{n}=1
, there is nothing to prove. Set

\beta:=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\alpha_{j}=1-\alpha_{n}>0.

By the induction hypothesis, there exists a unit vector

x\in\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1}\}

such that

\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{\beta}\,\langle Te_{j},J(e_{j})\rangle.

Let

X:=\operatorname{span}\{x,e_{n}\}.

Since
x
and
e_{n}
have disjoint supports,
X
is isometrically isomorphic to
\ell_{p}^{2}
. Let
R
be the block compression of
T
to
X
with respect to the ordered basis
(x,e_{n})
in the sense of the preceding observation. Its diagonal entries are

\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle\qquad\text{and}\qquad\langle Te_{n},J(e_{n})\rangle.

Applying Corollary
2.6
to
R
, and then using the inclusion (
2.3
),
we obtain a unit vector
y\in X
such that

\langle Ty,J(y)\rangle=\beta\,\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle+\alpha_{n}\,\langle Te_{n},J(e_{n})\rangle.

Hence

\langle Ty,J(y)\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\alpha_{j}\langle Te_{j},J(e_{j})\rangle+\alpha_{n}\langle Te_{n},J(e_{n})\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\langle Te_{j},J(e_{j})\rangle,

as required.
∎

2.3.
Positive and negative
2\times 2
geometry

The remaining two-dimensional statements illustrate both sides of the finite-dimensional picture: there are positive star-shapedness results for special matrices, but
there is also a counterexample to a general trace-centre theorem
for
2\times 2
matrices.

Although finite-dimensional numerical ranges need not be convex, numerical ranges of some natural families in
\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{2})
remain star-shaped, while the trace centre need not be a star-centre, already on
\ell_{3}^{2}
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{2}(\mathbb{C}))
have matrix representation

T=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
0&d\end{pmatrix}

with
\{a,b,d\}\subset\mathbb{C}.

Then
W(T)
is star-shaped with centre

\frac{\operatorname{tr}T}{2}=\frac{a+d}{2}.

Proof.

Set

\tau:=\frac{a+d}{2},\qquad\alpha:=\frac{a-d}{2},\qquad S:=T-\tau I=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&b\\
0&-\alpha\end{pmatrix}.

Then
W(T)=\tau+W(S)
, so it suffices to prove that
W(S)
is star-shaped with centre
0
.

If
\alpha=0
, then by Proposition
2.1
,

W(S)=\{\rho(r)e^{i\theta}:0\leq r\leq 1,\ \theta\in[0,2\pi]\},\qquad\rho(r):=|b|\,r^{1/q}(1-r)^{1/p}.

Since
\rho
is continuous on
[0,1]
with
\rho(0)=\rho(1)=0
, its range is an interval
[0,\rho_{\max}]
. Hence

W(S)=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:|\zeta|\leq\rho_{\max}\},

a closed disc centred at
0
.

Assume now that
\alpha\neq 0
. Multiplying
S
by a unimodular scalar only rotates
W(S)
, so without loss of generality we may assume that
\alpha>0
is real. By Proposition
2.1
,

W(S)=\{c(r)+\rho(r)e^{i\theta}:0\leq r\leq 1,\ \theta\in[0,2\pi]\},

where

c(r):=(2r-1)\alpha,\qquad\rho(r):=|b|\,r^{1/q}(1-r)^{1/p}.

Thus, for each fixed
r
, the corresponding curve is the circle with centre

c(r)
and radius
\rho(r)
.

The function
\rho
is concave on
[0,1]
: the map
(u,v)\mapsto u^{1/q}v^{1/p}
is concave on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
because the exponents
1/q
and
1/p
are positive and sum to
1
, hence
r\mapsto r^{1/q}(1-r)^{1/p}
is concave on
[0,1]
.

Let

z=c(r)+\rho(r)e^{i\theta}\in W(S)

and let
t\in[0,1]
. Define

r_{t}:=tr+\frac{1-t}{2}.

Then
c(r_{t})=t\,c(r)
. By concavity of
\rho
,

\rho(r_{t})\geq t\rho(r)+(1-t)\rho(1/2)\geq t\rho(r).

Consider the continuous function
F:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

F(s):=|tz-c(s)|-\rho(s),\qquad s\in[0,1].

At
s=r_{t}
we have

F(r_{t})=|tz-c(r_{t})|-\rho(r_{t})=t\rho(r)-\rho(r_{t})\leq 0,

whereas

F(0)=|tz+\alpha|\geq 0.

Hence, by continuity, there exists
s_{0}\in[0,r_{t}]
such that
F(s_{0})=0
. Thus

|tz-c(s_{0})|=\rho(s_{0}),

so for some
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}
,

tz=c(s_{0})+\rho(s_{0})e^{i\varphi}\in W(S).

Therefore
tW(S)\subset W(S)
, and
W(S)
is star-shaped with centre
0
.
Consequently,
W(T)=\tau+W(S)
is star-shaped with centre
\tau=(a+d)/2
.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

By symmetry, the same conclusion holds for lower triangular matrices

T=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}.

We do not know whether
W(T)
is star-shaped for every
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{2}).

We can only show that some natural choices of its points
may not be star-centres, and even that task can be demanding as the next proposition shows.

Proposition 2.10
.

Consider
S\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{3}^{2})
represented as

S=\begin{pmatrix}8+\frac{i}{5}&1\\[2.84526pt]
\frac{6}{5}&-8-\frac{i}{5}\end{pmatrix}.

Then

\operatorname{tr}S=0,\qquad 0\in W(S),

but
W(S)
is
not
star-shaped with centre
0
. More precisely,

6\in W(S),\qquad 3\notin W(S).

Hence

W(S)\cap[0,\infty)

is not an interval, so
W(S)
cannot be star-shaped with centre
0
.

Proof.

The full computation is rather technical and is deferred to
Appendix
A
. There we analyse the positive
real ray in the
t
-parametrisation of
W(S)
and prove that

3\notin W(S)
, while
0,6\in W(S)
. Since
3
is the midpoint of
0
and

6
, the conclusion follows.
∎

Remark 2.11
.

Let
T_{0}\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{2})
, and let
m\geq 1
. Consider the block operator

T:=T_{0}\oplus 0_{m}

acting on
\ell_{p}^{2+m}=\ell_{p}^{2}\oplus_{p}\ell_{p}^{m}
. Then

W(T)=\bigcup_{s\in[0,1]}sW(T_{0}).

In particular, if
0\in W(T_{0})
, then
W(T)
is automatically star-shaped with centre
0
, regardless of the geometry of
W(T_{0})
. More generally, for a scalar
c\in\mathbb{C}
,

W(T_{0}\oplus cI_{m})=c+\bigcup_{s\in[0,1]}s\bigl(W(T_{0})-c\bigr).

Hence padding by zero blocks or scalar blocks radialises the
2\times 2
geometry and destroys the mechanism of Proposition
2.10
. Extending that proposition to arbitrary dimension therefore requires a genuinely nontrivial construction rather than a simple block embedding.

The finite-dimensional picture is therefore mixed: explicit non-convexity and
failure of trace-centred star-shapedness coexist with convexity phenomena coming
from diagonal data. The next section shows that, on
\ell_{p}
, the tail
version of this diagonal mechanism feeds into the essential numerical range
and the star-centre geometry of
W(T)
.

3.
Numerical and essential numerical ranges on
\ell_{p}

In this section we pass from finite-dimensional geometry of
\ell_{p}^{n}
to
the geometry of
\ell_{p}
. In particular, the new feature is the
essential numerical range: it is described by tail subspaces, and this tail
description will be the basis for the convexity and Calkin-algebra results
below.

We continue to work with the same fixed exponent
1<p<\infty
, and we write
q
for the conjugate exponent. We first record the basic continuity properties of the duality map and the persistence of non-convexity, then prove the countable diagonal convex-combination theorem, and finally develop the structure theory of the essential numerical range and its star-centre consequences.

Let
(e_{j})_{j\geq 1}
and
(f_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be the standard bases in
\ell_{p}
and
\ell_{q}
, respectively.
For

x=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}e_{j}\in\ell_{p},

write

J(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}|\alpha_{j}|^{p-2}f_{j}.

Then

\|J(x)\|_{q}^{q}=\|x\|_{p}^{p}.

In particular, if
x\in\ell_{p}
is a unit vector, then
J(x)
is the unique functional satisfying

\|J(x)\|_{q}=1=\langle x,J(x)\rangle.

In the concrete sequence-space formulas below, the coordinate adjoint is taken with respect to this sesquilinear pairing; thus it is represented by the conjugate-transpose matrix acting on
\ell_{q}
.
For
m\in\mathbb{N}
let
P_{m}
be the canonical projection onto
\bigvee_{j=1}^{m}e_{j}
defined by
P_{m}e_{j}=e_{j}\quad(j\leq m)
and
P_{m}e_{j}=0\quad(j>m)
.

3.1.
Continuity of
J
and persistence of non-convexity

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
x,x_{n}\in\ell_{p}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then:

(i)

if
\|x_{n}-x\|_{p}\to 0
, then
\|J(x_{n})-J(x)\|_{q}\to 0
;

(ii)

if
x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}}x
in
\ell_{p}
, then
J(x_{n})\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}}J(x)
in
\ell_{q}
.

Proof.

Both assertions are immediate from the coordinate formula for
J
, see also

[
14
, Theorems 2.16 and 4.14]
.
∎

In bigger spaces, one might hope for better convexity properties of the numerical range, but non-convexity still persists on
\ell_{p}
.

Example 3.2
.

Let
p\neq 2
and let
T:\ell_{p}\to\ell_{p}
be defined by

Te_{1}=e_{1}+e_{2},\qquad Te_{2}=e_{1}-e_{2},\qquad Te_{j}=0\quad(j\geq 3).

Then
W(T)
is not convex.

Proof.

Let
S\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{2})
be given by

S=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}.

As in Example
2.4
, one obtains two points
a+iM
and
-a+iM
in
W(S)
, for some
a\in\mathbb{R}
, whose midpoint
iM
does not belong to
W(S)
.
For
x\in\ell_{p}
with
\|x\|=1
, put

r:=\bigl(|x_{1}|^{p}+|x_{2}|^{p}\bigr)^{1/p}.

If
r>0
, then
u:=r^{-1}(x_{1},x_{2})
is a unit vector in
\ell_{p}^{2}
, and

\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle=r^{p}\langle Su,J(u)\rangle.

The case
r=0
gives the value
0
. Conversely, every value

t\langle Su,J(u)\rangle
, where
0\leq t\leq 1
and
u
is a unit vector in

\ell_{p}^{2}
, is obtained by taking the first two coordinates equal to

t^{1/p}u
and adding a tail of norm
(1-t)^{1/p}
. Hence

W(T)=\{tz:z\in W(S),\ 0\leq t\leq 1\}.

Hence
a+iM,-a+iM\in W(S)\subset W(T)
. It was shown in Example
2.4
that
no point
is\in W(S)
satisfies
|s|\geq M.

So
iM\notin W(T)
and
W(T)
is not convex.
∎

This is compatible with Remark
2.11
: padding radialises
the two-dimensional numerical range, but radialisation need not restore
convexity; the particular two-dimensional gap used here survives after
radialisation.

3.2.
Countable convex combinations of diagonal entries

The next surprising theorem is a characteristic atomic feature: the numerical range contains all countable convex combinations of the diagonal entries.

We shall use the following elementary fact, in the planar form recorded in
[
38
]
. If
C
is a convex subset of
\mathbb{C}
,
z_{j}\in C
,
\alpha_{j}\geq 0
,
\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}=1
, and the series
\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}z_{j}
converges, then
\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}z_{j}\in C
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})
, let
\alpha_{j}\geq 0
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
, and assume that

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}=1.

Then

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}\langle Te_{j},J(e_{j})\rangle\in W(T).

Proof.

Put

d_{j}:=\langle Te_{j},J(e_{j})\rangle,\qquad j\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
|d_{j}|\leq\|T\|
, the series
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}d_{j}
converges absolutely. Let

C:=\conv\{d_{j}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset\mathbb{C}.

By the preceding fact,

d:=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}d_{j}\in C.

Thus
d
belongs to the convex hull of the set
\{d_{j}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Hence there are indices
j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}
and numbers
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m}\geq 0
such that

\sum_{k=1}^{m}\beta_{k}=1

and

d=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\beta_{k}d_{j_{k}}.

Let

F:=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}\},\qquad E_{F}:=\operatorname{span}\{e_{j}:j\in F\}.

Let
T_{F}
be the coordinate compression of
T
to
E_{F}
, written as an operator on
\ell_{p}^{|F|}
after the natural identification of
E_{F}
with
\ell_{p}^{|F|}
. By Theorem
2.7
, applied to
T_{F}
,

d\in W(T_{F}).

The coordinate case of the block-compression observation gives
W(T_{F})\subset W(T)
. Hence
d\in W(T)
, which proves the theorem.
∎

3.3.
The essential numerical range on
\ell_{p}

We now pass from the ordinary numerical range on
\ell_{p}
to its essential counterpart. In the atomic setting the abstract Banach-space descriptions become considerably more concrete, because finite-codimensional conditions may be expressed in terms of tails and finite supports.

For later use we spell out how the preceding Banach-space definition reads on
\ell_{p}
. Since
\ell_{q}
is separable, the net in the definition of
W_{e}(T)
may be replaced by a sequence. Moreover, the norming functional of a unit vector
x\in\ell_{p}
is unique and equals
J(x)
. Hence

\lambda\in W_{e}(T)

is equivalent, in the present setting, to the existence of a sequence of unit vectors
(x_{k})\subset\ell_{p}
such that

x_{k}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}}0,\qquad\langle Tx_{k},J(x_{k})\rangle\to\lambda.

No new definition is being introduced here. The next theorem sharpens this sequential form by using coordinate tails and finite supports.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})
and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Then the following conditions are equivalent.

(i)

\lambda\in W_{e}(T)
.

(ii)

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a unit vector
x
with finite support such that

\supp x\subset\{k+1,k+2,\dots\},\qquad|\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle-\lambda|<\varepsilon.

(iii)

For all subspaces
M,F\subset\ell_{p}
with
\codim M<\infty
and
\dim F<\infty
, and for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a unit vector
x\in M
with finite support such that

|\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle-\lambda|<\varepsilon,\qquad\sup\{|\langle f,J(x)\rangle|:f\in F,\ \|f\|=1\}<\varepsilon.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii). By
Theorem
1.9
, there exists a unit vector
x\in(I-P_{k})\ell_{p}
such that
|\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle-\lambda|<\varepsilon
.
For
m\geq k
large enough,
P_{m}x\neq 0
. For such
m
, set

x_{m}:=\frac{P_{m}x}{\|P_{m}x\|}.

Then
x_{m}\to x
in norm, and Lemma
3.1
(i) gives
J(x_{m})\to J(x)
in norm. Hence for
m
large enough,

|\langle Tx_{m},J(x_{m})\rangle-\lambda|<\varepsilon,

and
x_{m}
has finite support contained in
\{k+1,k+2,\dots\}
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii). Let
M,F\subset\ell_{p}
with
\codim M<\infty
and
\dim F<\infty
, and let
\varepsilon>0
.
Choose a sequence
(x_{k})
as in (ii), with

\supp x_{k}\subset\{k+1,k+2,\dots\},\qquad|\langle Tx_{k},J(x_{k})\rangle-\lambda|<k^{-1}\qquad(k\in\mathbb{N}).

Then
x_{k}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}}0
.

Let
Q:\ell_{p}\to\ell_{p}/M
be the quotient map. Since
\ell_{p}/M
is finite-dimensional and
Qe_{1},Qe_{2},\dots
span
\ell_{p}/M
, there exist indices
j_{1},\dots,j_{m}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
Qe_{j_{1}},\dots,Qe_{j_{m}}
form a basis of
\ell_{p}/M
. Set

E:=\operatorname{span}\{e_{j_{1}},\dots,e_{j_{m}}\}.

Then
Q|_{E}:E\to\ell_{p}/M
is an isomorphism. Let

R:=(Q|_{E})^{-1}Q\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p},E).

Thus
R
has finite rank and

x-Rx\in M\qquad(x\in\ell_{p}).

For each
k
, put

y_{k}:=x_{k}-Rx_{k}\in M.

Since
R
has finite rank and
x_{k}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}}0
, we have
\|Rx_{k}\|\to 0
. Hence

\|y_{k}-x_{k}\|=\|Rx_{k}\|\to 0.

Moreover,
y_{k}
has finite support because both
x_{k}
and
Rx_{k}\in E
do. For
k
large enough we have
y_{k}\neq 0
. For such
k
, put

z_{k}:=\frac{y_{k}}{\|y_{k}\|}.

Then
z_{k}\in M
,
\|z_{k}\|=1
,
z_{k}
has finite support, and
\|z_{k}-x_{k}\|\to 0
. Since
x_{k}
and
z_{k}
are unit vectors, Lemma
3.1
(i) yields

\|J(z_{k})-J(x_{k})\|\to 0.

Consequently,

\langle Tz_{k},J(z_{k})\rangle\to\lambda.

Also
z_{k}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}}0
, because
x_{k}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}}0
and
\|z_{k}-x_{k}\|\to 0
. By Lemma
3.1
(ii),

J(z_{k})\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}}0\qquad\text{in }\ell_{q}.

Since
F
is finite-dimensional, this implies

\sup\{|\langle f,J(z_{k})\rangle|:f\in F,\ \|f\|=1\}\to 0.

Choosing
k
large enough gives the required vector.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i). Take
F=\{0\}
. Then Theorem
1.9
yields
\lambda\in W_{e}(T)
.
∎

The next corollary converts the smallness of
J(x)
on a prescribed finite-dimensional subspace into exact annihilation of that subspace. This is a finite-dimensional correction of the canonical norming functional supplied by Theorem
3.4
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})
and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Then

\lambda\in W_{e}(T)
if and only if, for every finite-codimensional subspace

M\subset\ell_{p}
, every finite-dimensional subspace
F\subset\ell_{p}
, and
every
\varepsilon>0
, there are
x\in M
and
x^{*}\in F^{\perp}
such that

\|x\|=1,\qquad\langle x,x^{*}\rangle=1,\qquad\|x^{*}\|<1+\varepsilon,

and

|\langle Tx,x^{*}\rangle-\lambda|<\varepsilon.

In the implication from
\lambda\in W_{e}(T)
to this condition,
x
may moreover
be chosen with finite support.

Proof.

Let
R:\ell_{q}\to F^{*}
be the restriction map. Since
F
is finite-dimensional,
R
has a bounded right inverse
L:F^{*}\to\ell_{q}
. Put
C:=\|L\|
.
Choose
0<\delta<1
so small that

C\delta<\frac{1}{2},\qquad\frac{1+C\delta}{1-C\delta}<1+\varepsilon,

and

\frac{\delta+\|T\|C\delta}{1-C\delta}+\frac{|\lambda|C\delta}{1-C\delta}<\varepsilon.

By Theorem
3.4
, choose a unit vector
x\in M
with finite support such that

|\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle-\lambda|<\delta\quad\hbox{and}\quad\|RJ(x)\|_{F^{*}}<\delta.

Define

c_{F}:=L(RJ(x)),\qquad g:=J(x)-c_{F}.

Then
g\in F^{\perp}
and

\|c_{F}\|\leq C\delta.

Consequently, if

a:=\langle x,g\rangle,

then

|a-1|\leq C\delta,\qquad|a|\geq 1-C\delta,

and

\|g\|\leq 1+C\delta.

Set

x^{*}:=\frac{1}{\overline{a}}\,g,

and note that

x^{*}\in F^{\perp},\qquad\langle x,x^{*}\rangle=1,

and

\|x^{*}\|\leq\frac{1+C\delta}{1-C\delta}<1+\varepsilon.

Moreover

|\langle Tx,g\rangle-\lambda|\leq|\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle-\lambda|+\|T\|\|c_{F}\|<\delta+\|T\|C\delta.

Since

\langle Tx,x^{*}\rangle=\frac{1}{a}\langle Tx,g\rangle,

we get

\displaystyle|\langle Tx,x^{*}\rangle-\lambda|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|\langle Tx,g\rangle-\lambda|}{|a|}+|\lambda|\left|\frac{1}{a}-1\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta+\|T\|C\delta}{1-C\delta}+\frac{|\lambda|C\delta}{1-C\delta}<\varepsilon.

This proves the forward implication. Conversely, take
F=\{0\}
and let the
finite-codimensional subspace
M
and
\varepsilon>0
vary. The
approximate-normalisation part of Theorem
1.9
gives

\lambda\in W_{e}(T)
.
∎

The next two statements are the
\ell_{p}
analogues of a Hilbert-space interior-point construction. Compare
[
34
, Corollary 4.3]
, where an orthonormal sequence plays the role taken below by the biorthogonal pairs
(x_{n},x_{n}^{*})
. In the present atomic setting the required finite-dimensional annihilation is supplied by Corollary
3.5
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})
, and
\lambda\in\Int W_{e}(T)
. Let
F,M\subset\ell_{p}
satisfy
\dim F<\infty
and
\codim M<\infty
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a pair
(x,x^{*})\in M\times F^{\perp}
such that

\begin{gathered}\bigl|\|x\|-1\bigr|\leq\varepsilon,\qquad\bigl|\|x^{*}\|-1\bigr|\leq\varepsilon,\\
|\langle x,x^{*}\rangle-1|\leq\varepsilon,\qquad\langle(T-\lambda I)x,x^{*}\rangle=0.\end{gathered}

Proof.

Replacing
T
by
T-\lambda I
, we may assume that
\lambda=0
. Choose

r>0
such that

\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq r\}\subset W_{e}(T).

Choose
0<\delta<r/2
so small that, with

\gamma:=\sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{r}},

one has

\gamma<\varepsilon,\qquad\frac{2\delta}{r}<\varepsilon,

(1+\delta)(1+\gamma)<1+\varepsilon,\qquad\frac{1-2\delta/r}{1+\gamma}>1-\varepsilon.

By Corollary
3.5
, applied to
0\in W_{e}(T)
, choose

(u,u^{*})\in M\times F^{\perp}
such that

\|u\|=1,\qquad\langle u,u^{*}\rangle=1,\qquad\|u^{*}\|<1+\delta,

and, putting

c:=\langle Tu,u^{*}\rangle,

one has

|c|<\delta.

If
c=0
, the pair
(u,u^{*})
has all the required properties, provided

\delta<\varepsilon
. Assume therefore that
c\neq 0
and set

\eta:=-r\frac{c}{|c|}.

Then
|\eta|=r
, hence
\eta\in W_{e}(T)
. Define

M_{0}:=M\cap\Ker u^{*}\cap\Ker T^{*}u^{*},\qquad F_{0}:=F+\operatorname{span}\{u,Tu\}.

Applying Corollary
3.5
to
\eta
,
M_{0}
and
F_{0}
, choose

(v,v^{*})\in M_{0}\times F_{0}^{\perp}
such that

\|v\|=1,\qquad\langle v,v^{*}\rangle=1,\qquad\|v^{*}\|<1+\delta,

and, with

d:=\langle Tv,v^{*}\rangle,

one has

|d-\eta|<\delta.

In particular
|d|>r/2
. Put

\tau:=-\frac{c}{d}.

Then

|\tau|\leq\frac{2\delta}{r}.

Choose
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
such that

a\overline{b}=\tau,\qquad|a|=|b|=|\tau|^{1/2}\leq\gamma.

Finally, set

x:=u+av,\qquad x^{*}:=u^{*}+bv^{*}.

Then
x\in M
and
x^{*}\in F^{\perp}
. The definitions of
M_{0}
and
F_{0}
give

\langle v,u^{*}\rangle=0,\quad\langle Tv,u^{*}\rangle=0,\quad\langle u,v^{*}\rangle=0,\quad\langle Tu,v^{*}\rangle=0.

Therefore

\langle Tx,x^{*}\rangle=c+a\overline{b}\,d=c+\tau d=0,

and

\langle x,x^{*}\rangle=1+\tau.

The norm estimates follow directly from the choice of
\delta
:

|\|x\|-1|\leq|a|\leq\gamma<\varepsilon,

and

\|x^{*}\|\leq(1+\delta)(1+\gamma)<1+\varepsilon.

On the other hand,

\|x^{*}\|\geq\frac{|\langle x,x^{*}\rangle|}{\|x\|}\geq\frac{1-|\tau|}{1+\gamma}\geq\frac{1-2\delta/r}{1+\gamma}>1-\varepsilon.

Also

|\langle x,x^{*}\rangle-1|=|\tau|<\varepsilon.

Thus
(x,x^{*})
has the required properties in the case
\lambda=0
.
Returning to the original operator gives

\langle(T-\lambda I)x,x^{*}\rangle=0.

∎

Iterating the preceding lemma gives the following biorthogonal consequence.
For a prescribed
\lambda\in\Int W_{e}(T)
, the result may be viewed as a

\lambda
-diagonal compression statement, in the spirit of
Theorem
1.2
.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})
and
\lambda\in\Int W_{e}(T).
Then for every
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,\infty)
there exists a biorthogonal system

(x_{n},x_{n}^{*})\in\ell_{p}\times\ell_{q}\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N})

such that

\|x_{n}\|=1,\qquad\|x_{n}^{*}\|<1+\varepsilon_{n},\qquad\langle x_{n},x_{m}^{*}\rangle=\delta_{n,m},\qquad\langle Tx_{n},x_{m}^{*}\rangle=\lambda\,\delta_{n,m}

for all
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, choose
\delta_{n}>0
so small that

\frac{(1+\delta_{n})^{2}}{1-\delta_{n}}<1+\varepsilon_{n}.

We repeat the earlier recursive construction, now using Lemma
3.6
as the
\ell_{p}
input. By Lemma
3.6
, applied with
\delta_{1}
, there exist
x_{1}\in\ell_{p}
and
x_{1}^{*}\in\ell_{q}
such that

\begin{gathered}|\|x_{1}\|-1|<\delta_{1},\qquad|\|x_{1}^{*}\|-1|<\delta_{1},\qquad|\langle x_{1},x_{1}^{*}\rangle-1|<\delta_{1},\\
\langle(T-\lambda I)x_{1},x_{1}^{*}\rangle=0.\end{gathered}

After replacing
(x_{1},x_{1}^{*})
by

\left(\frac{x_{1}}{\|x_{1}\|},\frac{\|x_{1}\|}{\overline{\langle x_{1},x_{1}^{*}\rangle}}x_{1}^{*}\right),

we may assume that

\|x_{1}\|=1=\langle x_{1},x_{1}^{*}\rangle,\qquad\|x_{1}^{*}\|<1+\varepsilon_{1},\qquad\langle Tx_{1},x_{1}^{*}\rangle=\lambda.

Assume that
(x_{1},x_{1}^{*}),\dots,(x_{n},x_{n}^{*})
have already been constructed so that

\|x_{j}\|=1,\qquad\|x_{j}^{*}\|<1+\varepsilon_{j},\qquad\langle x_{j},x_{r}^{*}\rangle=\delta_{j,r},\qquad\langle Tx_{j},x_{r}^{*}\rangle=\lambda\,\delta_{j,r}

for
1\leq j,r\leq n
. Apply Lemma
3.6
with

M:=\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}\Ker x_{j}^{*}\cap\Ker T^{*}x_{j}^{*},\qquad F:=\operatorname{span}\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n},Tx_{1},\dots,Tx_{n}\}.

We obtain
(u,u^{*})\in M\times F^{\perp}
such that

\begin{gathered}|\|u\|-1|<\delta_{n+1},\qquad|\|u^{*}\|-1|<\delta_{n+1},\\
|\langle u,u^{*}\rangle-1|<\delta_{n+1},\qquad\langle(T-\lambda I)u,u^{*}\rangle=0.\end{gathered}

Set

x_{n+1}:=\frac{u}{\|u\|},\qquad x_{n+1}^{*}:=\frac{\|u\|}{\overline{\langle u,u^{*}\rangle}}u^{*}.

Then

\|x_{n+1}\|=1=\langle x_{n+1},x_{n+1}^{*}\rangle,\qquad\|x_{n+1}^{*}\|\leq\frac{(1+\delta_{n+1})^{2}}{1-\delta_{n+1}}<1+\varepsilon_{n+1},

and

\langle Tx_{n+1},x_{n+1}^{*}\rangle=\lambda.

Since
x_{n+1}\in M
and
x_{n+1}^{*}\in F^{\perp}
, the enlarged family remains biorthogonal and satisfies the required diagonal identities for
T
. Iterating the procedure completes the proof.
∎

3.4.
General properties of
W_{e}(T)
on
\ell_{p}

Theorem
3.4
yields in particular convexity of
W_{e}(T)
for every

T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}),
thus establishing one of the main results of this paper.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})
. Then
W_{e}(T)
is a compact convex set.

Proof.

Compactness follows from Remark
1.7
,
so only convexity must be shown.
Let
\lambda,\mu\in W_{e}(T)
. It is enough to prove that
(\lambda+\mu)/2\in W_{e}(T)
, since closedness then gives all convex combinations.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
. By Theorem
3.4
, there exists a unit vector
x\in\ell_{p}
with finite support such that

\supp x\subset\{k_{0}+1,k_{0}+2,\dots\},\qquad|\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle-\lambda|<\varepsilon.

Choose
k_{1}>k_{0}
so that

\supp x\subset\{k_{0}+1,\dots,k_{1}\},\qquad\|(I-P_{k_{1}})Tx\|<\varepsilon.

Set

M:=(I-P_{k_{1}})\ell_{p}\cap\Ker(P_{k_{1}}T).

Since
(I-P_{k_{1}})\ell_{p}
and
\Ker(P_{k_{1}}T)
are both finite-codimensional, so is
M
.
By Theorem
3.4
, applied to
M
and
F=\{0\}
, there exists a unit vector
y\in M
with finite support such that

|\langle Ty,J(y)\rangle-\mu|<\varepsilon.

Because
y\in(I-P_{k_{1}})\ell_{p}
, the supports of
x
and
y
are disjoint. Moreover,

\langle Ty,J(x)\rangle=\langle P_{k_{1}}Ty,J(x)\rangle=0,

while

|\langle Tx,J(y)\rangle|=|\langle(I-P_{k_{1}})Tx,J(y)\rangle|\leq\|(I-P_{k_{1}})Tx\|<\varepsilon.

Let

z:=\frac{x+y}{2^{1/p}}.

Since
x
and
y
have disjoint supports,
\|z\|=1
and

J(z)=\frac{J(x)+J(y)}{2^{1/q}}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\Bigl|\langle Tz,J(z)\rangle-\frac{\lambda+\mu}{2}\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(|\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle-\lambda|+|\langle Ty,J(y)\rangle-\mu|\Bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(|\langle Tx,J(y)\rangle|+|\langle Ty,J(x)\rangle|\Bigr)

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{2}(\varepsilon+\varepsilon)+\frac{1}{2}(\varepsilon+0)=\frac{3\varepsilon}{2}.

Since
k_{0}
and
\varepsilon
were arbitrary,
(\lambda+\mu)/2\in W_{e}(T)
, and hence
W_{e}(T)
is convex.
∎

After the tail characterisation and convexity of
W_{e}(T)
for operators on

\ell_{p}
have been established, we identify
W_{e}(T)
with the algebraic
numerical range of the Calkin image. We first record that, on
\ell_{p}
, the
operator quantity used in
[
6
]
gives the usual Calkin norm.

For a Banach space
X
and
S\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, set

(3.1)

\|S\|_{\mu}:=\inf\{\|S|_{M}\|:\ M\subset X,\ \operatorname{codim}M<\infty\}.

This is one of the standard measures of non-compactness for operators, see,
for example,
[
33
, Chapter 24]
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
1<p<\infty
. For every
S\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})
, one has

\|S\|_{\mu}=\|S+\mathcal{K}(\ell_{p})\|.

Proof.

Let
q=p/(p-1)
, and let
P_{n}^{(q)}
denote the coordinate projection
onto
\operatorname{span}\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}
in
\ell_{q}
. Combining the
standard identities for operator measures of non-compactness from

[
33
, Chapter 24]
, we have

\|S\|_{\mu}=\chi(S^{*}B_{\ell_{q}}),

where
B_{\ell_{q}}
denotes the closed unit ball of
\ell_{q}
, and

\chi
stands for the Hausdorff measure of non-compactness. By the tail
formula for bounded subsets of
\ell_{q}
, see

[
5
, Theorem 5.18(a)]
,

\chi(S^{*}B_{\ell_{q}})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|(I-P_{n}^{(q)})S^{*}\|.

The last limit is the essential norm of
S^{*}
. Indeed,
P_{n}^{(q)}S^{*}
is
finite-rank, and hence

\|S^{*}+\mathcal{K}(\ell_{q})\|\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\|(I-P_{n}^{(q)})S^{*}\|.

Conversely, if
K\in\mathcal{K}(\ell_{q})
, then

(I-P_{n}^{(q)})K\to 0

in norm. Therefore

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|(I-P_{n}^{(q)})S^{*}\|\leq\|S^{*}-K\|.

Taking the infimum over
K\in\mathcal{K}(\ell_{q})
gives the reverse
inequality. Thus

\|S\|_{\mu}=\|S^{*}+\mathcal{K}(\ell_{q})\|.

Since
\ell_{p}
is reflexive, passage to the adjoint preserves the essential
norm. Consequently,

\|S\|_{\mu}=\|S+\mathcal{K}(\ell_{p})\|.

∎

For a Banach space
X
and
T\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, let
V_{\mu}(T)

denote the algebraic numerical range of
\pi(T)
in

\mathcal{L}(X)/\mathcal{K}(X)
, equipped with the quotient seminorm

\|\cdot\|_{\mu}
.
It was shown in
[
6
, Theorem 8]
that

(3.2)

V_{\mu}(T)=\overline{\conv}\,W_{e}(T),\qquad T\in\mathcal{L}(X),

for every Banach space
X
. Applying this to

X=\ell_{p}
, and using Lemma
3.9
together with
Theorem
3.8
, we obtain the following Calkin-algebra
identification of the essential numerical range on
\ell_{p}
.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})
. Then

W_{e}(T)=V\bigl(\pi(T),\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})/\mathcal{K}(\ell_{p})\bigr)

(where the Calkin algebra is equipped with the usual quotient norm).

Proof.

By (
3.2
) and Theorem
3.8
,

V_{\mu}(T)=W_{e}(T).

By Lemma
3.9
, the quotient seminorm defined by

\|\cdot\|_{\mu}
on

\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})/\mathcal{K}(\ell_{p})
coincides with the usual quotient
norm. Hence
V_{\mu}(T)
is precisely

V\bigl(\pi(T),\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})/\mathcal{K}(\ell_{p})\bigr),

computed with respect to the usual quotient norm. This proves the assertion.
∎

Theorem
3.10
allows us to describe
W_{e}(T)
in terms of compact perturbations, extending the corresponding Hilbert-space formula to the whole range
1<p<\infty
.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})
. Then

W_{e}(T)=\bigcap\{\overline{W(T+K)}:K\in\mathcal{K}(\ell_{p})\}.

Proof.

For each compact operator
K
, one has
\overline{W(T+K)}\supset W_{e}(T+K)=W_{e}(T)
. Hence

\bigcap\{\overline{W(T+K)}:K\in\mathcal{K}(\ell_{p})\}\supset W_{e}(T).

On the other hand, by Theorem
3.10
and Theorem
1.5
,

W_{e}(T)=V\bigl(\pi(T),\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})/\mathcal{K}(\ell_{p})\bigr)=\bigcap\{V(T+K,\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})):K\in\mathcal{K}(\ell_{p})\}.

Since by Theorem
1.5
we have
V(T+K,\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}))=\overline{\conv}\,W(T+K)
,

W_{e}(T)=\bigcap\{\overline{\conv}\,W(T+K):K\in\mathcal{K}(\ell_{p})\}\supset\bigcap\{\overline{W(T+K)}:K\in\mathcal{K}(\ell_{p})\}.

Thus equality holds.
∎

The compact-perturbation formula in Theorem
3.11
represents
W_{e}(T)
as an intersection of
\overline{W(T+K)}
over all compact perturbations
K
. Under the additional assumption
\operatorname{Int}W_{e}(T)\neq\varnothing
, it can be strengthened to a single compact perturbation.

Corollary 3.12
.

Let
1<p<\infty
, and let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})
. If

\operatorname{Int}W_{e}(T)\neq\varnothing,

then there exists
K\in\mathcal{K}(\ell_{p})
such that

W_{e}(T)=\overline{W(T+K)}.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.10
,

W_{e}(T)=V\bigl(\pi(T),\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})/\mathcal{K}(\ell_{p})\bigr).

Since
W_{e}(T)
has non-empty interior, the compact-perturbation
realisation result in
[
15
, Theorem 3.1, Remark 2]

yields
K\in\mathcal{K}(\ell_{p})
such that

V(T+K,\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}))=V\bigl(\pi(T),\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})/\mathcal{K}(\ell_{p})\bigr).

Hence

V(T+K,\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}))=W_{e}(T).

On the other hand, in view of Theorem
1.5
,

W_{e}(T)=W_{e}(T+K)\subset\overline{W(T+K)}\subset V(T+K,\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})).

Therefore
\overline{W(T+K)}=W_{e}(T)
, as claimed.
∎

Finally, the formula for the numerical range of adjoint
T^{*}
in our
\ell_{p}
setting is immediate from the definition and the identity
J_{q}(J_{p}(x))=x
for
\|x\|_{p}=1
(with
T^{*}
understood in the sense of duality fixed at the beginning of this section).

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})
. Then

W_{e}(T^{*})=\{\bar{z}:z\in W_{e}(T)\}.

Remark 3.14
.

The convexity phenomenon for
W_{e}(T)
is not a general
feature of Banach-space essential numerical ranges. In fact, there exists an
operator on a space isometric to
\ell_{\infty}
whose spatial essential numerical
range is not convex. More precisely, for every
\eta>0
one can construct an
operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{\infty})
such that

i+\eta,\,-i+\eta\in W_{e}(T),\qquad\eta\notin W_{e}(T).

Thus the midpoint of two points of
W_{e}(T)
may fail to belong to
W_{e}(T)
.

The construction starts with an operator on
\ell_{\infty}^{2}
whose numerical
range has an exposed gap: the values
i
and
-i
are attained on the
boundary line
\operatorname{Re}z=0
, while their midpoint
0
is not.
This operator is then lifted coordinatewise to

\ell_{\infty}(\mathbb{Z};\ell_{\infty}^{2})
. One adds to it a perturbation
supported on a sparse set of coordinates, with coefficients defined by
oscillatory functionals coming from a shift-invariant mean on

\ell_{\infty}(\mathbb{Z})
. The finite-codimensional interpolation built into
these functionals makes the two translated endpoint values
i+\eta
and

-i+\eta
visible in the spatial essential numerical range. On the other
hand, if the translated midpoint
\eta
were approached by numerical-range
values, the Bessel estimate for the oscillatory coefficients would force the
corresponding norming functionals to concentrate asymptotically at a single
sparse coordinate. At that coordinate one is brought back to the original
two-dimensional exposed gap, which excludes the midpoint. Since

\ell_{\infty}(\mathbb{Z};\ell_{\infty}^{2})
is isometric to the scalar space

\ell_{\infty}
, this gives a genuine counterexample on
\ell_{\infty}
.

We do not include the details here, since they would lead away from the main
theme of the present paper, which is already rather long. They will be presented
elsewhere. The example shows, however, that the structural assumptions used above
are not merely technical: without some tail or shrinking mechanism, the spatial
essential numerical range may lose convexity even on a classical sequence space.

Remark 3.15
.

The appearance of the Calkin algebra in Theorem
3.10
is also consistent with recent work
[
10
]
, where it was shown that the
\ell_{p}
-Calkin algebra
\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})/\mathcal{K}(\ell_{p})
,
1<p<\infty
, contains isomorphic copies of all separable
C^{*}
-algebras. This gives another indication that, after passing to the Calkin algebra, part of the Hilbert-space picture survives in the non-Hilbertian
\ell_{p}
-setting.

3.5.
Interior points and star-shapedness

Once the structure of
W_{e}(T)
on
\ell_{p}
is in place, one can return to the ordinary numerical range
W(T)
and ask what geometric information is forced by the essential one. The next results answer this question for interior points and star-shapedness. We first show that interior points of the essential numerical range yield exact points of the ordinary numerical range. The proof uses the following tail-vector realisation lemma.

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})
, assume that
0\in\Int W_{e}(T)
, and set

\varepsilon:=\dist\bigl(0,\partial W_{e}(T)\bigr).

Let
k\geq 0
, and let
x\in\ell_{p}
be a unit vector with finite support contained in
\{k+1,k+2,\dots\}
such that

\eta:=|\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle|\leq\varepsilon.

Then there exists a unit vector
y
with finite support contained in
\{k+1,k+2,\dots\}
such that

|\langle Ty,J(y)\rangle|\leq\eta/2\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|y-x\|\leq 3\Bigl(\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon}\Bigr)^{1/p}.

Proof.

If
\eta=0
, take
y=x
. Hence assume
\eta>0
.
Find
k_{1}>k
such that

\supp x\subset\{k+1,\dots,k_{1}\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|(I-P_{k_{1}})Tx\|<\eta/4.

Let
c:=\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle
, so
|c|=\eta
. Set

c^{\prime}:=-\frac{c\varepsilon}{\eta}.

Then
|c^{\prime}|=\varepsilon
, hence
c^{\prime}\in W_{e}(T)
.
Choose a unit vector
u\in\ell_{p}
with finite support, satisfying

u\in(I-P_{k_{1}})\ell_{p}\cap T^{-1}(I-P_{k_{1}})\ell_{p}

and

|\langle Tu,J(u)\rangle-c^{\prime}|<\eta/4.

Now set

y:=\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+\eta}\Bigr)^{1/p}x+\Bigl(\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon+\eta}\Bigr)^{1/p}u.

Since
x
and
u
have disjoint supports,
\|y\|=1
and

J(y)=\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+\eta}\Bigr)^{1/q}J(x)+\Bigl(\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon+\eta}\Bigr)^{1/q}J(u).

Therefore

\displaystyle|\langle Ty,J(y)\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl|\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+\eta}c+\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon+\eta}c^{\prime}\Bigr|+\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon+\eta}|\langle Tu,J(u)\rangle-c^{\prime}|+|\langle Tx,J(u)\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq 0+\frac{\eta}{4}+\frac{\eta}{4}=\frac{\eta}{2}.

Finally,

\|y-x\|\leq 1-\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+\eta}\Bigr)^{1/p}+\Bigl(\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon+\eta}\Bigr)^{1/p}.

Writing

\alpha:=\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon+\eta},

we have
0\leq\alpha\leq 1/2
, and therefore

\|y-x\|\leq 1-(1-\alpha)^{1/p}+\alpha^{1/p}.

Moreover, since
r\mapsto r^{1/p}
is concave on
[0,\infty)
, we infer that

(1-\alpha)^{1/p}\geq 1-2\alpha.

Thus

\|y-x\|\leq 2\alpha+\alpha^{1/p}\leq 3\alpha^{1/p}\leq 3\left(\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/p}.

∎

Now the statement on interior points follows by iterative use of Lemma
3.16
.

Theorem 3.17
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})
. Then

\Int W_{e}(T)\subset W(T).

Moreover, for every
\lambda\in\Int W_{e}(T)
and every
k\geq 0
, there exists a unit vector
x
with

\supp x\subset\{k+1,k+2,\dots\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle=\lambda.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\Int W_{e}(T)
and
k\geq 0
. Without loss of generality we may assume that
\lambda=0
.
Choose
\varepsilon>0
such that

\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq\varepsilon\}\subset W_{e}(T).

Choose a unit vector
x_{0}\in\ell_{p}
with finite support contained in
\{k+1,k+2,\dots\}
and satisfying

|\langle Tx_{0},J(x_{0})\rangle|\leq\varepsilon.

Using Lemma
3.16
inductively, construct unit vectors
x_{n}\in\ell_{p}
with finite support in
\{k+1,k+2,\dots\}
such that

|\langle Tx_{n},J(x_{n})\rangle|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{n}}

and

\|x_{n}-x_{n-1}\|\leq 3\cdot 2^{(-n+1)/p}.

Thus
(x_{n})
is a Cauchy sequence. Let
x=\lim_{n}x_{n}
. Then
\|x\|=1
, the support of
x
is still contained in
\{k+1,k+2,\dots\}
, and Lemma
3.1
(i) gives
J(x_{n})\to J(x)
. Hence

\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle=\lim_{n}\langle Tx_{n},J(x_{n})\rangle=0.

This proves the theorem.
∎

While the interior of
W_{e}(T)
produces points in
W(T)
, the whole of

W_{e}(T)
provides star-centres for
\overline{W(T)}
, thus in particular making
\overline{W(T)}
star-shaped.

Theorem 3.18
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})
. Then
\overline{W(T)}
is star-shaped, and every point of
W_{e}(T)
is a star-centre of
\overline{W(T)}
.

Proof.

Let
\mu\in W_{e}(T)
,
\lambda\in\overline{W(T)}
,
\varepsilon>0
, and
t\in(0,1)
.
Choose a unit vector
x\in\ell_{p}
such that

|\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle-\lambda|<\varepsilon.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, set

x_{k}:=\frac{P_{k}x}{\|P_{k}x\|}.

For
k
large enough,
x_{k}
is defined,
x_{k}\to x
in norm, and hence
J(x_{k})\to J(x)
by Lemma
3.1
(i). Thus there exists
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

|\langle Tx_{k_{0}},J(x_{k_{0}})\rangle-\lambda|<\varepsilon,\qquad\|(I-P_{k_{0}})Tx_{k_{0}}\|<\varepsilon.

Write
x^{\prime}:=x_{k_{0}}
. Since
\mu\in W_{e}(T)
, Theorem
3.4
(iii), applied to

M:=(I-P_{k_{0}})\ell_{p}\cap\Ker(P_{k_{0}}T)\qquad\text{and}\qquad F=\{0\},

yields a unit vector
y\in M
with finite support such that

|\langle Ty,J(y)\rangle-\mu|<\varepsilon.

In particular,

\supp y\subset\{k_{0}+1,k_{0}+2,\dots\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad P_{k_{0}}Ty=0.

Set

z:=t^{1/p}x^{\prime}+(1-t)^{1/p}y.

Then
\|z\|=1
and

J(z)=t^{1/q}J(x^{\prime})+(1-t)^{1/q}J(y).

Therefore

\displaystyle\bigl|\langle Tz,J(z)\rangle-(t\lambda+(1-t)\mu)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq t|\langle Tx^{\prime},J(x^{\prime})\rangle-\lambda|+(1-t)|\langle Ty,J(y)\rangle-\mu|

\displaystyle\qquad+|\langle Tx^{\prime},J(y)\rangle|+|\langle Ty,J(x^{\prime})\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq t\varepsilon+(1-t)\varepsilon+\varepsilon+0=2\varepsilon,

where

|\langle Tx^{\prime},J(y)\rangle|=|\langle(I-P_{k_{0}})Tx^{\prime},J(y)\rangle|\leq\|(I-P_{k_{0}})Tx^{\prime}\|<\varepsilon,

while

\langle Ty,J(x^{\prime})\rangle=\langle P_{k_{0}}Ty,J(x^{\prime})\rangle=0.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary,
t\lambda+(1-t)\mu\in\overline{W(T)}
.
∎

If
\Int W_{e}(T)\neq\varnothing
, one can strengthen the preceding result from
\overline{W(T)}
to
W(T),

thus showing that on
\ell_{p}
the numerical range is often star-shaped. To this end, we need the next auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 3.19
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})
, let

0\in\operatorname{Int}W_{e}(T)
, and choose
\varepsilon>0
such that

\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq\varepsilon\}\subset W_{e}(T).

Let
0<s^{\prime}<s\leq 2s^{\prime}
, and suppose that
x\in\ell_{p}
is a unit vector
with finite support satisfying

|\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle-s|\leq\frac{(s-s^{\prime})\varepsilon}{s^{\prime}}.

Then, for every
\delta>0
, there exists a unit vector
y\in\ell_{p}
with
finite support such that

|\langle Ty,J(y)\rangle-s^{\prime}|<\delta

and

\|y-x\|\leq 3\left(\frac{s-s^{\prime}}{s}\right)^{1/p}.

Proof.

Set

c^{\prime}:=\frac{s^{\prime}\bigl(s-\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle\bigr)}{s-s^{\prime}}.

By the hypothesis,

|c^{\prime}|\leq\frac{s^{\prime}}{s-s^{\prime}}|\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle-s|\leq\varepsilon.

Hence
c^{\prime}\in W_{e}(T)
.

Choose
k_{1}
so large that

\operatorname{supp}x\subset\{1,\ldots,k_{1}\},\qquad\|(I-P_{k_{1}})Tx\|<\delta/2.

By Theorem
3.4
, choose a unit vector
u\in\ell_{p}
with finite support such that

u\in(I-P_{k_{1}})\ell_{p}\cap T^{-1}(I-P_{k_{1}})\ell_{p}

and

|\langle Tu,J(u)\rangle-c^{\prime}|<\delta/2.

Set

y:=\left(\frac{s^{\prime}}{s}\right)^{1/p}x+\left(\frac{s-s^{\prime}}{s}\right)^{1/p}u.

Then
\|y\|=1
,
y
has finite support, and

J(y)=\left(\frac{s^{\prime}}{s}\right)^{1/q}J(x)+\left(\frac{s-s^{\prime}}{s}\right)^{1/q}J(u).

Since
Tu\in(I-P_{k_{1}})\ell_{p}
and
J(x)
is supported in

\{1,\ldots,k_{1}\}
, we have

\langle Tu,J(x)\rangle=0.

Moreover,

|\langle Tx,J(u)\rangle|=|\langle(I-P_{k_{1}})Tx,J(u)\rangle|<\delta/2.

Therefore

\displaystyle|\langle Ty,J(y)\rangle-s^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{s^{\prime}}{s}\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle+\frac{s-s^{\prime}}{s}c^{\prime}-s^{\prime}\right|

\displaystyle+\frac{s-s^{\prime}}{s}|\langle Tu,J(u)\rangle-c^{\prime}|+|\langle Tx,J(u)\rangle|.

The first term is zero by the definition of
c^{\prime}
. Hence

|\langle Ty,J(y)\rangle-s^{\prime}|<\delta.

Finally,

\|y-x\|\leq 1-\left(\frac{s^{\prime}}{s}\right)^{1/p}+\left(\frac{s-s^{\prime}}{s}\right)^{1/p}.

Since
s\leq 2s^{\prime}
, the number

\alpha:=\frac{s-s^{\prime}}{s}

belongs to
[0,1/2]
. Then, arguing as in the end of the proof of Lemma
3.16
, we obtain

\|y-x\|\leq 3\left(\frac{s-s^{\prime}}{s}\right)^{1/p}.

∎

Now using the above lemma iteratively, we deduce star-shapedness of
W(T)
under very mild assumptions.

Theorem 3.20
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})
. If
\Int W_{e}(T)\neq\varnothing
, then
W(T)
is star-shaped. More precisely, every point of
\Int W_{e}(T)
is a star-centre of
W(T)
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\operatorname{Int}W_{e}(T)
, let
\mu\in W(T)
, and let

t\in(0,1)
. We show that

(1-t)\lambda+t\mu\in W(T).

If
\lambda=\mu
, there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, replacing
T
by

\frac{T-\lambda I}{\mu-\lambda},

we may assume that

\lambda=0,\qquad\mu=1.

Let

\varepsilon:=\operatorname{dist}(0,\partial W_{e}(T)).

For
n\geq 0
, set

s_{n}:=t+\frac{1-t}{2^{n}}.

Then
s_{0}=1
and
s_{n}\downarrow t
.

Choose a unit vector
u\in\ell_{p}
such that

\langle Tu,J(u)\rangle=1.

For
m
large enough, the normalised truncations

u_{m}:=\frac{P_{m}u}{\|P_{m}u\|}

are well defined and satisfy

\langle Tu_{m},J(u_{m})\rangle\to 1

by Lemma
3.1
(i). Hence we may choose a unit vector
x_{0}\in\ell_{p}
with
finite support such that

|\langle Tx_{0},J(x_{0})\rangle-s_{0}|<\frac{(1-s_{1})\varepsilon}{s_{1}}.

We construct inductively unit vectors
x_{n}\in\ell_{p},n\in\mathbb{N},
with finite support
such that

(3.3)

|\langle Tx_{n},J(x_{n})\rangle-s_{n}|<\frac{(1-s_{1})\varepsilon}{2^{n}s_{1}}

and

(3.4)

\|x_{n}-x_{n-1}\|\leq 3\left(\frac{s_{n-1}-s_{n}}{s_{n-1}}\right)^{1/p}\qquad(n\geq 1).

Suppose that
x_{n-1}
has already been constructed. Since
s_{n}\leq s_{1}
,
we have

\frac{(1-s_{1})\varepsilon}{2^{n-1}s_{1}}=\frac{(1-t)\varepsilon}{2^{n}s_{1}}\leq\frac{(1-t)\varepsilon}{2^{n}s_{n}}=\frac{(s_{n-1}-s_{n})\varepsilon}{s_{n}}.

Thus the hypothesis of Lemma
3.19
is satisfied with
s=s_{n-1}
and

s^{\prime}=s_{n}
. Applying Lemma
3.19
with

\delta:=\frac{(1-s_{1})\varepsilon}{2^{n}s_{1}}

gives a unit vector
x_{n}\in\ell_{p}
with finite support such that
(
3.3
) and (
3.4
) hold.
This completes the induction step.

Since
s_{n-1}\geq t
and

s_{n-1}-s_{n}=\frac{1-t}{2^{n}},

we have

\|x_{n}-x_{n-1}\|\leq 3\left(\frac{1-t}{2^{n}t}\right)^{1/p}.

The right-hand side is summable in
n
, and hence
(x_{n})
is a Cauchy
sequence. Let

x:=\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}.

Then
\|x\|=1
, and Lemma
3.1
(i) gives
J(x_{n})\to J(x)
in
\ell_{q}
.
Since

s_{n}\to t\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{(1-s_{1})\varepsilon}{2^{n}s_{1}}\to 0,

we obtain

\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle Tx_{n},J(x_{n})\rangle=t.

Thus
t\in W(T)
for the normalised operator. If
T_{0}
denotes the
operator before the affine reduction, the preceding conclusion applied to

(T_{0}-\lambda I)/(\mu-\lambda)
gives a unit vector
x
such that

\left\langle\frac{T_{0}-\lambda I}{\mu-\lambda}x,J(x)\right\rangle=t.

Equivalently,

\langle T_{0}x,J(x)\rangle=(1-t)\lambda+t\mu,

and hence
(1-t)\lambda+t\mu\in W(T_{0})
.
Therefore every point of
\operatorname{Int}W_{e}(T)
is a star-centre of

W(T)
, and
W(T)
is star-shaped.
∎

The structure theory developed in this section suggests that concrete matrix examples on sequence spaces should display both Hilbert-space features and genuinely non-Hilbertian behaviour. We therefore turn next to Toeplitz-type operators and to the discrete Hilbert transform.

4.
Discrete Toeplitz and related sequence-space examples

The atomic theory on
\ell_{p}
already yields explicit sequence-space examples which serve as a natural bridge to the later
L^{p}
questions. We record here only those parts of the discrete Toeplitz discussion that are used below and that fit the present scope. Topological properties of
W(T)
and
W_{e}(T)
are invariant under isometric conjugacy, so one may pass freely between isometric examples such as
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N})
and
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, where
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, or between different enumerations of
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z})
.
In particular, we use
\mathbb{N}_{0}
in the one-sided shift examples only for
notational convenience; of course
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
is canonically
isometric to
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N})
.
By contrast, the operator classes discussed below have natural domains of their own, and it is convenient to state the results directly for those examples.

4.1.
The unilateral sequence-space example

We begin the example section with one-sided sequence-space Toeplitz operators. As above, let

1<p<\infty
, and let
(e_{j})_{j\geq 0}
be the standard basis of

\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
. We denote by
S_{R}
and
S_{L}
the right and left shifts on

\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, given by

(4.1)

S_{R}e_{j}=e_{j+1}\quad(j\geq 0),\qquad S_{L}e_{0}=0,\qquad S_{L}e_{j}=e_{j-1}\quad(j\geq 1).

We continue to write
J
for the duality map on
\ell_{p}
. These operators provide the simplest discrete analogues of Hardy-space Toeplitz operators and already exhibit the main essential-range mechanism.
For operator-algebraic aspects of the
\ell_{p}
-Toeplitz algebra on

\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, related to
S_{R}
and
S_{L}
, see e.g.
[
41
]
.
Here we use only the concrete Toeplitz examples as test objects for ordinary
and essential numerical ranges.

Sequence-space Toeplitz operators are described primarily by their coefficient sequences. Thus a one-sided Toeplitz operator on
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
is given by a matrix
\bigl(a_{j-k}\bigr)_{j,k\geq 0}
which is constant along diagonals, while the corresponding bilateral Laurent operator on
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z})
is given by convolution with the same coefficient sequence. When the coefficients arise as Fourier coefficients of a bounded function
\varphi
on
\mathbb{T}
, it is natural to regard
\varphi
as a symbol. In the Hilbert-space Hardy setting, by contrast, analytic symbols have a direct numerical-range interpretation. For
\varphi\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, let
T_{\varphi}
be the Toeplitz operator on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, given by
T_{\varphi}f=P_{+}(\varphi f)
, where
P_{+}
denotes the Riesz projection from
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
onto
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Then

\overline{W(T_{\varphi})}=\overline{\conv}\,\varphi(\mathbb{D})

by
[
26
]
. The discussion below retains the Toeplitz pattern on
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
while keeping the geometry completely explicit and illustrating how differently numerical ranges behave outside the Hilbert setting.

Now let
a=(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z})
and define the unilateral Toeplitz operator
T_{a}
on
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})

by

(4.2)

(T_{a}x)_{n}:=\sum_{m\geq 0}a_{n-m}x_{m},\qquad n\geq 0.

The next simple proposition records a special feature of Toeplitz numerical
ranges on
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
,
1<p<\infty
, resembling the one for
p=2
.

Proposition 4.1
.

For every
T_{a}\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0}))
defined by (
4.2
) one has

W_{e}(T_{a})=\overline{W(T_{a})}.

As a consequence,

\overline{W(T_{a})}
is a convex set.

Proof.

For every unit vector
x\in\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
and every
m\geq 0
,
the corresponding numerical-range value is invariant under the right shift

S_{R}^{m}
. Indeed, for
n\geq m
one has

(T_{a}(S_{R}^{m}x))_{n}=(T_{a}x)_{n-m},

whereas
(S_{R}^{m}x)_{n}=0
for
n<m
. Moreover,

J(S_{R}^{m}x)=S_{R}^{m}J(x)
. Therefore

\displaystyle\langle T_{a}(S_{R}^{m}x),J(S_{R}^{m}x)\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq m}(T_{a}(S_{R}^{m}x))_{n}\,(J(S_{R}^{m}x))_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq m}(T_{a}x)_{n-m}\,J(x)_{n-m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}(T_{a}x)_{k}\,J(x)_{k}=\langle T_{a}x,J(x)\rangle.

Since
S_{R}^{m}x\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}}0
, this yields

W(T_{a})\subset W_{e}(T_{a}).

The reverse inclusion

W_{e}(T_{a})\subset\overline{W(T_{a})}

is tautological from the definition of
W_{e}(T_{a})
. Hence

W_{e}(T_{a})=\overline{W(T_{a})}.

∎

4.2.
Explicit tridiagonal Toeplitz examples on
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})

We next specialise the general Toeplitz discussion to tridiagonal examples. This is the first place where one can write down explicit formulas and compare upper bounds with exact descriptions. The first theorem gives a general enclosure for
W_{e}(T_{a})=\overline{W(T_{a})}.

Let

(4.3)

T=a_{-1}S_{L}+a_{0}I+a_{1}S_{R}\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})),\qquad a_{-1},a_{0},a_{1}\in\mathbb{C},

where
S_{R}
and
S_{L}
are given by (
4.1
).
Define

\mathcal{E}_{p}(a_{1},a_{-1}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}a_{1}rs^{p-1}e^{-i\theta}+a_{-1}sr^{p-1}e^{i\theta}:\\[2.84526pt]
r,s\geq 0,\ \ r^{p}+s^{p}=1,\ \ \theta\in[0,2\pi]\end{array}\right\}.

For fixed
r,s\geq 0
with
r^{p}+s^{p}=1
, the curve in the parameter
\theta

which occurs in the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{p}(a_{1},a_{-1})
is an ellipse,
possibly degenerate.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0}))
be given by (
4.3
).
Then

W_{e}(T)=\overline{W(T)}\subset a_{0}+2\,\overline{\conv}\,\mathcal{E}_{p}(a_{1},a_{-1}).

Proof.

Let
x\in\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
with
\|x\|_{p}=1.

A direct expansion gives

\displaystyle\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=a_{0}\sum_{n\geq 0}|x_{n}|^{p}+a_{1}\sum_{n\geq 1}x_{n-1}|x_{n}|^{p-2}\overline{x_{n}}+a_{-1}\sum_{n\geq 0}x_{n+1}|x_{n}|^{p-2}\overline{x_{n}}

\displaystyle=a_{0}+\sum_{n\geq 0}\Bigl(a_{1}x_{n}|x_{n+1}|^{p-2}\overline{x_{n+1}}+a_{-1}x_{n+1}|x_{n}|^{p-2}\overline{x_{n}}\Bigr).

For each
n\geq 0
, write

x_{n}=\rho_{n}e^{i\varphi_{n}},\qquad\rho_{n}\geq 0,\quad\varphi_{n}\in[0,2\pi),

and set
d_{n}:=\rho_{n}^{p}+\rho_{n+1}^{p}
. If
d_{n}>0
, define

r_{n}:=\rho_{n}/d_{n}^{1/p},\qquad s_{n}:=\rho_{n+1}/d_{n}^{1/p},\qquad\theta_{n}:=\varphi_{n+1}-\varphi_{n}.

Then
r_{n}^{p}+s_{n}^{p}=1
, and the
n
th bracket equals

d_{n}\Bigl(a_{1}r_{n}s_{n}^{p-1}e^{-i\theta_{n}}+a_{-1}s_{n}r_{n}^{p-1}e^{i\theta_{n}}\Bigr)\in d_{n}\,\mathcal{E}_{p}(a_{1},a_{-1}).

Since

\sum_{n\geq 0}d_{n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(\rho_{n}^{p}+\rho_{n+1}^{p})=2-\rho_{0}^{p}\leq 2,

and
\mathcal{E}_{p}(a_{1},a_{-1})
is centrally symmetric (replace
\theta
by
\theta+\pi
), one has

0\in\conv\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{p}(a_{1},a_{-1})\bigr).

Let

C:=\conv\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{p}(a_{1},a_{-1})\bigr).

For each
n
with
d_{n}>0
, choose
\eta_{n}\in\mathcal{E}_{p}(a_{1},a_{-1})
so that the
n
th bracket equals
d_{n}\eta_{n}
. Since
0\in C
and
\sum_{n\geq 0}d_{n}\leq 2
, we have

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\geq 0}d_{n}\eta_{n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{d_{n}}{2}\eta_{n}+\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\geq 0}d_{n}\right)0\in C.

Thus

\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle-a_{0}=\sum_{n\geq 0}d_{n}\eta_{n}\in 2C=2\,\conv\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{p}(a_{1},a_{-1})\bigr).

Taking closures yields

\overline{W(T)}\subset a_{0}+2\,\overline{\conv}\,\mathcal{E}_{p}(a_{1},a_{-1}).

Now apply Proposition
4.1
.
∎

The above inclusion can be complemented as follows.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0}))
be defined by (
4.3
),
and put

\Gamma_{T}:=a_{0}+\{a_{1}e^{-it}+a_{-1}e^{it}:0\leq t\leq 2\pi\},\qquad\Delta_{T}:=\conv\Gamma_{T}.

Then

\sigma_{e}(T)=\Gamma_{T},\qquad\Delta_{T}\subset W_{e}(T)=\overline{W(T)}.

Moreover, the set
\Delta_{T}
has the following explicit form. If

a_{1}=a_{-1}=0
, then
\Delta_{T}=\{a_{0}\}
. If

|a_{1}|\neq|a_{-1}|
, then
\Delta_{T}
is the closed ellipse centred at

a_{0}
with
semiaxis lengths

|a_{1}|+|a_{-1}|,\qquad\bigl||a_{-1}|-|a_{1}|\bigr|,

with the usual interpretation as a disc when one of
a_{1},a_{-1}
is zero.
If
|a_{1}|=|a_{-1}|=\rho>0
, then
\Delta_{T}
is a line segment centred at

a_{0}
and of length
4\rho
.

Proof.

The case
a_{1}=a_{-1}=0
is immediate, so suppose that at least one of

a_{1},a_{-1}
is non-zero. Choose arguments
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{-1}
so
that

a_{1}=|a_{1}|e^{i\phi_{1}},\qquad a_{-1}=|a_{-1}|e^{i\phi_{-1}},

choosing the argument of a zero coefficient arbitrarily, and put

\omega=e^{i(\phi_{1}+\phi_{-1})/2}.

Then

a_{1}e^{-it}+a_{-1}e^{it}=\omega\Bigl((|a_{1}|+|a_{-1}|)\cos\eta+i(|a_{-1}|-|a_{1}|)\sin\eta\Bigr),

where

\eta=t+\frac{\phi_{-1}-\phi_{1}}{2}.

This gives the asserted geometric description of
\Delta_{T}
.
The essential-spectrum identity
\sigma_{e}(T)=\Gamma_{T}
is the classical
fact from the theory of finite-band Toeplitz operators on
\ell_{p}.
See e.g.
[
20
, Theorem 1]
.
The lower inclusion follows from Theorem
1.12
and
Proposition
4.1
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

The ellipse
\Delta_{T}
is already visible in the upper estimate of
Theorem
4.2
. Indeed, taking

r=s=2^{-1/p}
in the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{p}(a_{1},a_{-1})
gives

2\bigl(a_{1}rs^{p-1}e^{-i\theta}+a_{-1}sr^{p-1}e^{i\theta}\bigr)=a_{1}e^{-i\theta}+a_{-1}e^{i\theta},

and hence

\Delta_{T}\subset a_{0}+2\conv\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{p}(a_{1},a_{-1})\bigr).

In the symmetric case,
W_{e}(T_{a})
admits an exact description. The arguments rest on convexity of
W_{e}(T)

and the properties of
W_{e}(T_{a})
proved above.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0}))
be given by

T:=a_{0}I+c(S_{R}+S_{L})\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})),\qquad a_{0},c\in\mathbb{C}.

Define

E_{p}:=\Bigl\{rs^{p-1}e^{-i\theta}+sr^{p-1}e^{i\theta}:\ r,s\geq 0,\ r^{p}+s^{p}=1,\ \theta\in[0,2\pi]\Bigr\}.

Then

W_{e}(T)=\overline{W(T)}=a_{0}+2c\,\overline{\conv}\,E_{p}.

In particular, for
T_{1}:=S_{R}+S_{L}
, one has

W_{e}(T_{1})=\overline{W(T_{1})}=2\,\overline{\conv}\,E_{p}.

Proof.

The upper inclusion follows directly from Theorem
4.2
with

a_{-1}=a_{1}=c.

Thus it remains to prove

a_{0}+2c\,\overline{\conv}\,E_{p}\subset W_{e}(T).

By Theorem
3.8
and Proposition
4.1
, the set
W_{e}(T)=\overline{W(T)}
is closed and convex.
It is therefore enough to prove that

a_{0}+2c\eta_{p}\in W_{e}(T)\qquad(\eta_{p}\in E_{p}).

Fix
\eta_{p}\in E_{p}
. By definition, there exist
r,s\geq 0
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
such that

r^{p}+s^{p}=1,\qquad\eta_{p}=rs^{p-1}e^{-i\theta}+sr^{p-1}e^{i\theta}.

Set

\alpha:=r,\qquad\beta:=se^{i\theta}.

For each
M\in\mathbb{N}
, define
x^{(M)}\in\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
by

x^{(M)}:=M^{-1/p}(\alpha,\beta,\alpha,\beta,\dots,\alpha,\beta,0,0,\dots),

where the alternating block
(\alpha,\beta)
is repeated
M
times. Then

\|x^{(M)}\|_{p}^{p}=M^{-1}\,M\,(|\alpha|^{p}+|\beta|^{p})=r^{p}+s^{p}=1,

so
\|x^{(M)}\|_{p}=1
.
Now

(4.4)

\langle Tx^{(M)},J(x^{(M)})\rangle=a_{0}+c\sum_{n=0}^{2M-2}\Bigl(x^{(M)}_{n}|x^{(M)}_{n+1}|^{p-2}\overline{x^{(M)}_{n+1}}+x^{(M)}_{n+1}|x^{(M)}_{n}|^{p-2}\overline{x^{(M)}_{n}}\Bigr).

For each
n\in\{0,\dots,2M-2\}
, the pair
(x^{(M)}_{n},x^{(M)}_{n+1})
is either

(M^{-1/p}\alpha,M^{-1/p}\beta)\qquad\text{or}\qquad(M^{-1/p}\beta,M^{-1/p}\alpha).

In both cases the corresponding bracket equals
M^{-1}\eta_{p}
. Hence every one of the
2M-1
brackets in (
4.4
) contributes exactly
M^{-1}\eta_{p}
, and therefore

\langle Tx^{(M)},J(x^{(M)})\rangle=a_{0}+c\,\frac{2M-1}{M}\,\eta_{p}\xrightarrow[M\to\infty]{}a_{0}+2c\eta_{p}.

Thus
a_{0}+2c\eta_{p}\in\overline{W(T)}=W_{e}(T)
. Since
\eta_{p}\in E_{p}
was arbitrary, we obtain

a_{0}+2cE_{p}\subset W_{e}(T).

By convexity and closedness of
W_{e}(T)
, this gives

a_{0}+2c\,\overline{\conv}\,E_{p}\subset W_{e}(T).

Combining this with the upper inclusion completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.6
.

For
T_{1}=S_{R}+S_{L}
, as in Theorem
4.5
, one has

\sup\{\operatorname{Re}z:z\in W_{e}(T_{1})\}=2,\qquad\sup\{|\operatorname{Im}z|:z\in W_{e}(T_{1})\}=2\beta_{p},

where

\beta_{p}:=\max\{|sr^{p-1}-rs^{p-1}|:r,s\geq 0,\ r^{p}+s^{p}=1\}.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.5
,

W_{e}(T_{1})=2\,\overline{\conv}\,E_{p}.

For fixed
r,s
, varying
\theta
gives

rs^{p-1}e^{-i\theta}+sr^{p-1}e^{i\theta}=\bigl(rs^{p-1}+sr^{p-1}\bigr)\cos\theta+i\bigl(sr^{p-1}-rs^{p-1}\bigr)\sin\theta.

Thus the corresponding ellipse, possibly degenerate, has semiaxes

rs^{p-1}+sr^{p-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|sr^{p-1}-rs^{p-1}|.

By Hölder’s inequality,

rs^{p-1}+sr^{p-1}\leq(r^{p}+s^{p})^{1/p}(s^{p}+r^{p})^{1/q}=1,

and equality is attained at
r=s=2^{-1/p}
. This gives

\sup\{\operatorname{Re}z:z\in W_{e}(T_{1})\}=2.

The formula for the maximal imaginary part is immediate from the same ellipse description and the definition of
\beta_{p}
.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

For
T_{1}
, Proposition
4.3
gives

\conv\sigma(T_{1})=\conv\sigma_{e}(T_{1})=[-2,2].

Corollary
4.6
shows that the exact essential numerical range keeps this horizontal semiaxis equal to
2
, but has transverse semiaxis
2\beta_{p}
. Thus for
p=2
the set collapses to the spectral segment, while for
p\neq 2
the numerical range is a genuine two-dimensional thickening of that segment. The phase reduction in Remark
4.8
gives the same conclusion, after rotation and translation, whenever
|a_{-1}|=|a_{1}|
.

Remark 4.8
.

The same exact formula extends to

T=a_{-1}S_{L}+a_{0}I+a_{1}S_{R}\qquad\text{whenever }|a_{-1}|=|a_{1}|.

Indeed, if
a_{-1}=\rho e^{i\beta}
and
a_{1}=\rho e^{i\alpha}
, choose

\omega:=e^{i(\alpha-\beta)/2}\qquad(|\omega|=1),

and let
D:\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})\to\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
be the diagonal surjective isometry given by

(Dx)_{n}:=\omega^{n}x_{n},\qquad(n\geq 0).

Then

D^{-1}S_{R}D=\overline{\omega}\,S_{R},\qquad D^{-1}S_{L}D=\omega\,S_{L},

so

D^{-1}TD=a_{0}I+c(S_{R}+S_{L}),\qquad c:=\rho e^{i(\alpha+\beta)/2}.

Since both the numerical range and the essential numerical range are invariant under the mapping
T\to D^{-1}TD
,
Theorem
4.5
applies. For general unequal coefficients, however, the alternating-block argument above no longer produces a single local value on every edge, and the exact formula remains open.

Remark 4.9
.

The failure of the Hilbertian segment picture is already visible
in the ordinary numerical range. Let
p\neq 2
and let

T_{0}=S_{R}+S_{L}
on
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, as above. If

x=(r,is,0,0,\ldots),\qquad r,s>0,\quad r^{p}+s^{p}=1,

then a direct computation gives

\langle T_{0}x,J(x)\rangle=i\bigl(sr^{p-1}-rs^{p-1}\bigr).

Thus, whenever
r\neq s
, this is a nonzero purely imaginary point of

W(T_{0})
. In particular, for
p\neq 2
, the ordinary numerical range of

T_{0}
is not contained in the real line.

Remark 4.10
.

Even in the simplest symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz case, the ordinary numerical range is already genuinely two-dimensional on
\ell_{p}
when
p\neq 2
, although the Hilbertian symbol is the real interval
[-2,2]
. This contrasts with the classical Hardy-space theory, where Toeplitz numerical ranges are convex and, for analytic symbols, are described by the convex hull of the symbol image on
\mathbb{D}

[
26
]
. Thus the discrete Toeplitz example already exhibits the breakdown of the Hilbert-space Toeplitz picture for numerical ranges. Theorem
4.5
gives an exact formula in the symmetric case and, by Remark
4.8
, in the balanced case
|a_{-1}|=|a_{1}|
, whereas Theorem
4.2
remains only an upper-bound result for general unequal coefficients.

4.3.
The discrete Hilbert transform on
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z})

We now turn to bilateral sequence-space operators. Before introducing the
discrete Hilbert transform, we record the bilateral analogue of the shift
argument used above for one-sided Toeplitz operators.

Let
(e_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
be the standard basis of
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z})
, and let

U
be the bilateral shift given by

Ue_{j}=e_{j+1},\qquad j\in\mathbb{Z}.

Equivalently,
(Ux)_{n}=x_{n-1}
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Again we write
J
for the duality map on
\ell_{p}
.

As before, all general results from Section 3 apply here after transporting them through any fixed surjective isometry
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z})\cong\ell_{p}
.

We start with the bilateral analogue of Proposition
4.1
.

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z}))
satisfy

TU=UT.

Then

W_{e}(T)=\overline{W(T)}.

Proof.

Let
x\in\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z})
be a unit vector. Since
T
commutes with
U
and
J(U^{m}x)=U^{m}J(x),

one has

\langle T(U^{m}x),J(U^{m}x)\rangle=\langle U^{m}Tx,U^{m}J(x)\rangle=\langle Tx,J(x)\rangle\qquad(m\geq 0).

Moreover, the sequence
(U^{m}x)
converges weakly to
0
. Hence every point of
W(T)
belongs to
W_{e}(T)
, so

W(T)\subset W_{e}(T).

The reverse inclusion

W_{e}(T)\subset\overline{W(T)}

is immediate from the definition of
W_{e}(T)
. Therefore

W_{e}(T)=\overline{W(T)}.

∎

For finitely supported sequences
a=(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
, define

(Ha)_{n}:=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{a_{n-m}}{m},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

By the discrete M. Riesz theorem,
H
extends uniquely to a bounded operator on

\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z})
. We denote this extension by
H_{\mathbb{Z}}
and call it
the discrete Hilbert transform.

Equivalently, one may view
H
as the
Fourier multiplier on
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z})
acting on finitely supported sequences
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
as

\widehat{Ha}(\theta)=m(\theta)\,\widehat{a}(\theta),\qquad\widehat{a}(\theta):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{n}e^{-in\theta},\qquad\theta\in\mathbb{T},

with symbol

m(\theta)=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{e^{-ik\theta}}{k}=i\Bigl(\frac{\theta}{\pi}-1\Bigr),\qquad 0<\theta<2\pi.

Since
H_{\mathbb{Z}}
commutes with the bilateral shift,
Proposition
4.11
gives

W_{e}(H_{\mathbb{Z}})=\overline{W(H_{\mathbb{Z}})}.

If
p=2
, then
H_{\mathbb{Z}}
is unitarily equivalent to the
multiplication operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
with symbol

m(\theta)=i\left(\frac{\theta}{\pi}-1\right),\qquad 0<\theta<2\pi.

Hence

\sigma(H_{\mathbb{Z}})=W_{e}(H_{\mathbb{Z}})=i[-1,1],\qquad W(H_{\mathbb{Z}})=i(-1,1).

For
p\neq 2
, this one-dimensional picture breaks down: the ordinary and
essential numerical ranges are no longer contained in
i\mathbb{R}
, as the
next proposition shows.

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
H_{\mathbb{Z}}
be the Hilbert transform on
\ell_{4}(\mathbb{Z})
. Then

\frac{6}{17\pi}\,\overline{\mathbb{D}}\subset W_{e}(H_{\mathbb{Z}})=\overline{W(H_{\mathbb{Z}})}.

In particular,
W_{e}(H_{\mathbb{Z}})
has nonempty interior and is not contained in the imaginary axis.

Proof.

Set

x_{\psi}:=\frac{\frac{1}{2}e^{i\psi}e_{0}+e_{1}}{(1+2^{-4})^{1/4}}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}e^{i\psi}e_{0}+e_{1}}{(17/16)^{1/4}}\qquad(\psi\in\mathbb{R}).

Since
p=4
, the duality map is given by

J(x)=\bigl(|x_{n}|^{2}x_{n}\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}.

Hence

J(x_{\psi})=\frac{\frac{1}{8}e^{i\psi}e_{0}+e_{1}}{(17/16)^{3/4}}.

Now
H_{\mathbb{Z}}x_{\psi}
is only needed at the coordinates
0
and
1
:

(H_{\mathbb{Z}}x_{\psi})_{0}=-\frac{1}{\pi(17/16)^{1/4}},\qquad(H_{\mathbb{Z}}x_{\psi})_{1}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}e^{i\psi}}{\pi(17/16)^{1/4}}.

Therefore

\langle H_{\mathbb{Z}}x_{\psi},J(x_{\psi})\rangle=\frac{-\frac{1}{8}e^{-i\psi}+\frac{1}{2}e^{i\psi}}{\pi(17/16)}=\frac{6\cos\psi+10i\sin\psi}{17\pi}.

As
\psi
varies, this traces the boundary of the ellipse

E:=\left\{\frac{6\cos\psi+10i\sin\psi}{17\pi}:\ \psi\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

For every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the shifted vector
U^{N}x_{\psi}
has the same numerical value, since
H_{\mathbb{Z}}
commutes with
U
.
Since
U^{N}x_{\psi}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}}0
as
N\to\infty,
we have

E\subset W_{e}(H_{\mathbb{Z}}).

By Theorem
3.8
, the set
W_{e}(H_{\mathbb{Z}})
is convex, so it contains the convex hull of
E
, that is, the filled ellipse bounded by
E
. This ellipse contains the disc of radius
\frac{6}{17\pi}
centred at the origin. Hence

\frac{6}{17\pi}\,\overline{\mathbb{D}}\subset W_{e}(H_{\mathbb{Z}}).

This proves, in particular, that
W_{e}(H_{\mathbb{Z}})
is not contained in

i\mathbb{R}
.
∎

Remark 4.13
.

The discrete Hilbert transform already shows, within the sequence-space setting of the present paper, that the exact Hilbert-space segment picture is unstable away from
p=2
: on
\ell_{4}(\mathbb{Z})
the essential numerical range contains a genuine disc.

4.4.
The unilateral shift and the disc picture

We conclude the example section with the unilateral shift itself. This returns to the simplest one-sided example and shows how the abstract Banach-space disc theorem and the atomic arguments combine in a particularly transparent form.
The unilateral shift provides the simplest sequence-space example in which the geometry of the numerical range can be described almost completely. We describe this first and then draw the Crouzeix-type consequence.
The proof below could have been based on
Theorem
3.17
,
but we preferred a direct argument.

Proposition 4.14
.

Let
1<p<\infty
, and let
S=S_{R}
be the unilateral shift on

\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, given by
Se_{j}=e_{j+1}
for the standard basis

(e_{j})_{j\geq 0}
. Then

W_{e}(S)=\overline{\mathbb{D}},\qquad W(S)=\mathbb{D}.

In particular,

W(S)\cap\mathbb{T}=\varnothing,\qquad\overline{W(S)}=W_{e}(S)=\overline{\mathbb{D}}.

Proof.

Let
|\lambda|<1
.
The case
\lambda=0
is obtained from
e_{0}
. Let now
0<|\lambda|<1
.
Choose
\alpha\neq 0
,
|\alpha|<1
, such that

\overline{\alpha}|\alpha|^{p-2}=\lambda.

Put

x^{(\alpha)}:=(1-|\alpha|^{p})^{1/p}(1,\alpha,\alpha^{2},\ldots).

Then
\|x^{(\alpha)}\|=1
, and a direct computation gives

\langle Sx^{(\alpha)},J(x^{(\alpha)})\rangle=\lambda.

Thus
\mathbb{D}\subset W(S)
.

For
m\geq 0
, set
x_{m}:=S^{m}x^{(\alpha)}
. Then
x_{m}\to 0
weakly and

\langle Sx_{m},J(x_{m})\rangle=\lambda.

Hence
\mathbb{D}\subset W_{e}(S)
. Since
W_{e}(S)
is closed and
S
is an
isometry, it follows that

W_{e}(S)=\overline{\mathbb{D}}.

It remains to exclude boundary points from
W(S)
. Let
x=(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}

be a unit vector. Then

\langle Sx,J(x)\rangle=\sum_{n\geq 1}x_{n-1}\overline{x_{n}}|x_{n}|^{p-2}.

By the triangle inequality and Hölder’s inequality,

|\langle Sx,J(x)\rangle|\leq\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}|x_{n-1}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}|x_{n}|^{p}\right)^{1/q}=(1-|x_{0}|^{p})^{1/q}\leq 1.

If equality were attained, then
x_{0}=0
, and equality in Hölder’s inequality
would give a constant
c\geq 0
such that

|x_{n-1}|^{p}=c|x_{n}|^{p},\qquad n\geq 1.

Since both sums in Hölder’s inequality are then equal to
1
, one has

c=1
. Thus
|x_{n-1}|=|x_{n}|
for all
n\geq 1
. Together with
x_{0}=0

this forces
x=0
, a contradiction. Hence

|\langle Sx,J(x)\rangle|<1

for every unit vector
x
, and therefore
W(S)\subset\mathbb{D}
. Since

\mathbb{D}\subset W(S)
, we get
W(S)=\mathbb{D}
.
∎

4.5.
The unilateral shift and Crouzeix-type estimates

We conclude the sequence-space examples by combining the disc behaviour of the unilateral shift with the polynomial estimates behind Crouzeix-type inequalities. The result is a counterexample on
\ell_{p}
. After recording it, we isolate its finite-dimensional analogue, which leads to the scale

n^{|1/p-1/2|}.

Recall first the famous Hilbert-space Crouzeix inequality: if
T
is a Hilbert-space operator, then one has, for every polynomial
f
,

(4.5)

\|f(T)\|\leq(1+\sqrt{2})\sup_{z\in\overline{W(T)}}|f(z)|.

For background on numerical-range spectral-set questions and related functional calculi, see
[
17
,
18
,
4
,
23
,
19
]
. Note that no analogue of (
4.5
), with
1+\sqrt{2}
replaced by an absolute constant
C>0
, can hold for all operators on
\ell_{p}
when
1<p<\infty
and
p\neq 2
.

Indeed, let
S=S_{R}
be the unilateral shift on
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
. In view of Proposition
4.14
,
for every polynomial
f
, we have

\sup_{z\in W(S)}|f(z)|=\sup_{|z|\leq 1}|f(z)|.

If such an analogue held for
S
, then the polynomial functional calculus of

S
would be bounded with respect to the disc-algebra norm. We show that this
would imply the same boundedness for the bilateral shift.

Let
U
be the bilateral shift on
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z})
, given by
Ue_{j}=e_{j+1}
for the standard basis
(e_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Fix an analytic polynomial

q
. It is enough to estimate
q(U)
on finitely supported vectors. If

x\in\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z})
has finite support, choose
N
so large that
U^{N}x

is supported in
\mathbb{N}_{0}
. On this subspace the action of
U
agrees with
the action of the unilateral shift
S
. Hence

q(U)U^{N}x=q(S)U^{N}x.

Since
U^{N}
is an isometry and commutes with
q(U)
, we obtain

\|q(U)x\|=\|U^{N}q(U)x\|=\|q(U)U^{N}x\|=\|q(S)U^{N}x\|\leq\|q(S)\|\|x\|.

By density of finitely supported vectors in
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z})
, it follows that

\|q(U)\|\leq\|q(S)\|.

Thus polynomial boundedness of
S
would imply polynomial boundedness of

U
. This contradicts the known fact that the bilateral shift on

\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z})
,
p\neq 2
, is not polynomially bounded, see, for instance,

[
22
, Theorem 5.7]
or
[
16
, Proposition 4.1]
.
Therefore the required dimension-free Crouzeix-type estimate cannot hold on

\ell_{p}
.

This proves the following consequence.

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
1<p<\infty
and
p\neq 2
. Then there is no constant
C\geq 1
such that

(4.6)

\|f(T)\|\leq C\sup_{z\in\overline{W(T)}}|f(z)|

for every polynomial
f
and every operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})
.

This conclusion agrees with
[
8
, Theorem 6.1]
, formulated there for the algebraic numerical range. Although the algebraic-range version is formally stronger in general, in the shift case considered above
\overline{W(S)}
and
V(S,\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}))
both coincide with the closed unit disc.

4.6.
Finite-dimensional shift tests and the scale
n^{|1/p-1/2|}

The preceding argument only rules out a dimension-free
Crouzeix-type estimate on
\ell_{p}
,
p\neq 2
. It leaves open whether, on

\ell_{p}^{n}
, such an estimate holds with a constant
C_{p,n}
depending only
on
p
and
n
, and what the possible growth of
C_{p,n}
is as

n\to\infty
. However, the constants
C_{p,n}
, if finite, cannot remain
bounded as
n\to\infty
: otherwise, applying the corresponding uniform
finite-dimensional estimate to the coordinate compressions
P_{n}T|_{\ell_{p}^{n}}
,
using (
2.3
) with
u_{j}=e_{j}
, and passing to the strong
limit
P_{n}TP_{n}\to T
, would give the estimate on
\ell_{p}
. The finite shifts
considered below point to polynomial asymptotics of order
n^{\alpha_{p}}
, where

\alpha_{p}:=\left|\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}\right|=\max\left\{\frac{1}{p},\frac{1}{q}\right\}-\frac{1}{2},\qquad q=\frac{p}{p-1}.

If
E\subset\mathbb{C}
is compact and
f
is a polynomial, set

\|f\|_{E}:=\sup_{z\in E}|f(z)|,

and let
e_{0},\ldots,e_{n-1}
be the standard basis of
\ell_{p}^{n}
. Let

S_{n,p}\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{n})
be the truncated unilateral shift given by

S_{n,p}e_{j}=e_{j+1}\quad(0\leq j\leq n-2),\qquad S_{n,p}e_{n-1}=0.

Thus
S_{n,p}^{n}=0
and
\|S_{n,p}\|=1
.

Proposition 4.16
.

Let
1<p<\infty
and
n\geq 2
. Then there is an absolute constant

c>0
such that

c\,n^{\alpha_{p}}\leq\sup_{f\neq 0}\frac{\|f(S_{n,p})\|}{\|f\|_{W(S_{n,p})}}\leq e\,n^{\alpha_{p}},

and

c\,n^{\alpha_{p}}\leq\sup_{f\neq 0}\frac{\|f(S_{n,p})\|}{\|f\|_{V_{n,p}(S_{n,p})}}\leq e\,n^{\alpha_{p}},

where, for
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{n})
,

V_{n,p}(T):=V\bigl(T,\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{n})\bigr).

Proof.

We first record the elementary disc information. Fix
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}
and put

u_{\lambda,n}:=n^{-1/p}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\overline{\lambda}^{\,j}e_{j}.

Then
\|u_{\lambda,n}\|_{p}=1
, and a direct computation with the duality map gives

\langle S_{n,p}u_{\lambda,n},J(u_{\lambda,n})\rangle=\frac{n-1}{n}\lambda.

Thus, if

r_{n}:=\frac{n-1}{n},

then

r_{n}\mathbb{T}\subset W(S_{n,p}),\qquad r_{n}\overline{\mathbb{D}}\subset V_{n,p}(S_{n,p}).

Since
S_{n,p}
is a contraction, we also have

W(S_{n,p})\subset\overline{\mathbb{D}},\qquad V_{n,p}(S_{n,p})\subset\overline{\mathbb{D}}.

For the lower estimate we use flat polynomials, as in

[
8
, Theorem 4.3]
. Here the classical Rudin–Shapiro construction
suffices: there is an absolute constant
C_{\rm RS}
such that, whenever

m=2^{s}
, one can choose signs

\varepsilon_{0},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m-1}\in\{-1,1\}

so that

f_{m}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\varepsilon_{k}z^{k}

satisfies

\|f_{m}\|_{\overline{\mathbb{D}}}\leq C_{\rm RS}\sqrt{m}.

See, e.g.,
[
39
]
.

Fix
n\geq 2
, and choose
m=2^{s}
such that
n/2<m\leq n
. We regard
f_{m}

as a polynomial of degree at most
n-1
, with zero coefficients in the remaining
positions. Since

f_{m}(S_{n,p})e_{0}=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\varepsilon_{k}e_{k},

we get

\|f_{m}(S_{n,p})\|\geq m^{1/p}.

Applying the same argument to the adjoint gives the complementary estimate.
Indeed,
S_{n,p}^{*}
is the backward shift on
\ell_{q}^{n}
, and

f_{m}(S_{n,p})^{*}e_{m-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\varepsilon_{k}e_{m-1-k}.

This vector has
\ell_{q}^{n}
-norm
m^{1/q}
. Hence

\|f_{m}(S_{n,p})\|=\|f_{m}(S_{n,p})^{*}\|\geq m^{1/q}.

Therefore

\|f_{m}(S_{n,p})\|\geq m^{\max\{1/p,1/q\}}.

Because
W(S_{n,p})\subset V_{n,p}(S_{n,p})\subset\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, it follows that

\frac{\|f_{m}(S_{n,p})\|}{\|f_{m}\|_{W(S_{n,p})}}\geq\frac{\|f_{m}(S_{n,p})\|}{\|f_{m}\|_{V_{n,p}(S_{n,p})}}\geq\frac{\|f_{m}(S_{n,p})\|}{\|f_{m}\|_{\overline{\mathbb{D}}}}\geq C_{\rm RS}^{-1}m^{\alpha_{p}}.

Since
m>n/2
and
0\leq\alpha_{p}\leq 1/2
, the last quantity is bounded below
by
cn^{\alpha_{p}}
with an absolute constant
c>0
.

We turn to the upper estimate. Note that for every operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{n})
,

(4.7)

\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{n})}\leq n^{\alpha_{p}}\|T\|_{\mathcal{L}(\ell_{2}^{n})}.

This follows immediately from the standard norm comparisons between
\ell_{p}^{n}
and
\ell_{2}^{n}
.

Let
r=r_{n}=(n-1)/n
and define the diagonal operator
D_{r,2}
on
\ell_{2}^{n}
by

D_{r,2}e_{j}=r^{-j}e_{j},\qquad 0\leq j\leq n-1.

Then

D_{r,2}^{-1}S_{n,2}D_{r,2}=rS_{n,2}.

Hence, for every polynomial
f
,

f(S_{n,2})=D_{r,2}f(rS_{n,2})D_{r,2}^{-1}.

Since
S_{n,2}
is a Hilbert-space contraction, von Neumann’s inequality applied to
z\mapsto f(rz)
gives

\|f(rS_{n,2})\|\leq\|f\|_{r\overline{\mathbb{D}}}.

Moreover,

\|D_{r,2}\|\|D_{r,2}^{-1}\|=r^{-(n-1)}=\left(\frac{n}{n-1}\right)^{n-1}\leq e.

Therefore

\|f(S_{n,2})\|\leq e\,\|f\|_{r\overline{\mathbb{D}}}.

Combining this with (
4.7
), we obtain

\|f(S_{n,p})\|\leq e\,n^{\alpha_{p}}\|f\|_{r\overline{\mathbb{D}}}.

Finally,
r\overline{\mathbb{D}}\subset V_{n,p}(S_{n,p})
, while
r\mathbb{T}\subset W(S_{n,p})
and the maximum principle gives

\|f\|_{r\overline{\mathbb{D}}}=\sup_{|z|=r}|f(z)|\leq\|f\|_{W(S_{n,p})}\leq\|f\|_{V_{n,p}(S_{n,p})}.

This proves both upper estimates.
∎

The same exponent is not specific to truncated shifts. It also appears for cyclic shifts.
Let
e_{0},\ldots,e_{n-1}
be the standard basis of
\ell_{p}^{n}
, and let

U_{n,p}\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{n})
be the cyclic shift given by

U_{n,p}e_{j}=e_{j+1}\quad(0\leq j\leq n-2),\qquad U_{n,p}e_{n-1}=e_{0}.

Proposition 4.17
.

Let
1<p<\infty
and
n\geq 2
. Then there is an absolute constant
c>0
such that

c\,n^{\alpha_{p}}\leq\sup_{f\neq 0}\frac{\|f(U_{n,p})\|}{\|f\|_{W(U_{n,p})}}\leq n^{\alpha_{p}},

and

c\,n^{\alpha_{p}}\leq\sup_{f\neq 0}\frac{\|f(U_{n,p})\|}{\|f\|_{V_{n,p}(U_{n,p})}}\leq n^{\alpha_{p}},

where

V_{n,p}(U_{n,p}):=V\bigl(U_{n,p},\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{n})\bigr).

Proof.

For the lower bound, choose again
m=2^{s}
with
n/2<m\leq n
and use
the polynomial
f_{m}
from the proof of
Proposition
4.16
; in particular,

\|f_{m}\|_{\overline{\mathbb{D}}}\leq C_{\rm RS}\sqrt{m}
. Since
m\leq n
, the
vectors
e_{0},U_{n,p}e_{0},\dots,U_{n,p}^{m-1}e_{0}
are distinct standard basis
vectors. Hence

\|f_{m}(U_{n,p})e_{0}\|=m^{1/p},

and the adjoint argument gives the complementary lower bound
m^{1/q}
. Thus

\|f_{m}(U_{n,p})\|\geq m^{\max\{1/p,1/q\}}.

As
U_{n,p}
is an isometry on
\ell_{p}^{n}
, both
W(U_{n,p})
and

V_{n,p}(U_{n,p})
are contained in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. Hence

\frac{\|f_{m}(U_{n,p})\|}{\|f_{m}\|_{W(U_{n,p})}}\geq\frac{\|f_{m}(U_{n,p})\|}{\|f_{m}\|_{\overline{\mathbb{D}}}}\geq C_{\rm RS}^{-1}m^{\alpha_{p}}\geq cn^{\alpha_{p}},

and the same estimate holds with
W(U_{n,p})
replaced by

V_{n,p}(U_{n,p})
.

For the upper bound, use (
4.7
). Since
U_{n,2}
is unitary on
\ell_{2}^{n}
,

\|f(U_{n,2})\|=\max_{\omega^{n}=1}|f(\omega)|.

The points
\omega
with
\omega^{n}=1
are eigenvalues of
U_{n,p}
and are realised by their unimodular eigenvectors, hence they belong to
W(U_{n,p})
. Therefore

\|f(U_{n,p})\|\leq n^{\alpha_{p}}\max_{\omega^{n}=1}|f(\omega)|\leq n^{\alpha_{p}}\|f\|_{W(U_{n,p})}\leq n^{\alpha_{p}}\|f\|_{V_{n,p}(U_{n,p})}.

This proves the asserted estimates.
∎

We may now formulate the finite-dimensional Crouzeix-type inequality in the
\ell_{p}
setting, which looks natural in view of the above observations. Define

\mathcal{C}^{W}_{n,p}:=\sup_{T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{n})}\sup_{f\neq 0}\frac{\|f(T)\|}{\|f\|_{W(T)}},

and

\mathcal{C}^{V}_{n,p}:=\sup_{T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p}^{n})}\sup_{f\neq 0}\frac{\|f(T)\|}{\|f\|_{V(T)}}.

Since
W(T)\subset V(T)
, one has

\mathcal{C}^{V}_{n,p}\leq\mathcal{C}^{W}_{n,p}.

Proposition
4.16
gives

\mathcal{C}^{V}_{n,p}\geq cn^{\alpha_{p}},\qquad\mathcal{C}^{W}_{n,p}\geq cn^{\alpha_{p}}

with an absolute constant
c>0
.
Thus we arrive at the next curious question.

Question 4.18
.

For fixed
1<p<\infty
, is there a constant
C_{p}>0
such that

\mathcal{C}^{V}_{n,p}\leq C_{p}n^{\alpha_{p}}\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N})?

More strongly, is there a constant
C^{\prime}_{p}>0
such that

\mathcal{C}^{W}_{n,p}\leq C^{\prime}_{p}n^{\alpha_{p}}\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N})?

The
V
-version is algebraically more natural, but
V(T)
is difficult to determine explicitly. The
W
-version is closer to the original Crouzeix formulation, but it is technically harder because
W(T)
is typically non-convex.
There are several natural ways to test Question
4.18
, but we omit their discussion.

The examples discussed in this section complete the atomic example part of the paper. They illustrate that the general theory from Sections
3
and
4
is not only formal: even for very concrete sequence-space operators, the essential numerical range on
\ell_{p}
can already display geometry with no Hilbert-space analogue, and the unilateral shift shows that this geometry does not support a Hilbert-space-type Crouzeix theory on
\ell_{p}
. Nevertheless, the finite-dimensional shift examples suggest that some vestiges of such a theory may remain at the finite-dimensional level.

5.
Possible extensions

5.1.
Spaces of class
(P)

We close the paper by indicating two directions in which the preceding arguments extend beyond the atomic
\ell_{p}
setting. The first one concerns Banach spaces of class
(P)
.

The methods developed above are not tied exclusively to
\ell_{p}
.
They extend, with only minor changes in notation, to Banach spaces carrying a compatible
exhaustion by finite-rank projections. We say that a Banach space
X
belongs to class
(P)
if there exists a sequence
(P_{n})_{n\geq 1}

of non-zero finite-rank projections such that:

(i)

P_{m}P_{n}=P_{n}P_{m}=P_{\min\{m,n\}}
for all
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
P_{n}x\to x
for every
x\in X
;

(ii)

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, whenever
x,y\in X
satisfy

\|P_{n}x\|\leq\|P_{n}y\|,\qquad\|(I-P_{n})x\|\leq\|(I-P_{n})y\|,

one has
\|x\|\leq\|y\|
;

(iii)

for every
x^{*}\in X^{*}
one has

\bigl\|x^{*}|_{(I-P_{n})X}\bigr\|\to 0\qquad(n\to\infty),

or, equivalently,
\|(I-P_{n})^{*}x^{*}\|\to 0
.

If
X
is of class
(P)
, then
P_{n}X
is finite-dimensional and

(I-P_{n})X
is finite-codimensional for every
n
. Moreover,
condition (ii) implies

\|P_{n}\|=1,\qquad\|I-P_{n}\|=1,\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},

provided the corresponding projection is non-zero. Indeed, apply
(ii) first to
P_{n}x
and
x
, and then to
(I-P_{n})x
and
x
.
Also,
X
is separable, because
\bigcup_{n}P_{n}X
is dense in
X
, while

X^{*}
is separable by (iii). Thus class
(P)
isolates spaces with
finite-dimensional initial parts, finite-codimensional tails and a compatible
dual tail approximation.

If, in addition,

\dim(P_{n}-P_{n-1})X=1,\qquad n\geq 1,\quad P_{0}:=0,

then, after choosing normalised vectors

e_{n}\in(P_{n}-P_{n-1})X,\qquad n\geq 1,

the sequence
(e_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a shrinking
1
-unconditional basis of

X
. Thus class
(P)
may be viewed as a finite-dimensional-decomposition
version of the shrinking
1
-unconditional basis setting.

Thus, in the setting of Banach spaces of class
(P)
, one again has finite-dimensional initial blocks and
finite-codimensional tails, and the arguments based on block decomposition,
tail approximation, and weak convergence go through with only minor changes
in the proofs. In particular, the description of the essential numerical range, its convexity,
and the star-centre mechanism admit analogues for spaces of class
(P)
.

Besides
\ell_{p}
, natural examples are provided by
\ell_{p}
- or
c_{0}
-sums of
a sequence of finite-dimensional Banach spaces
(E_{n})
:

\Big(\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\Big)_{\ell_{p}},\qquad 1<p<\infty,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Big(\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\Big)_{c_{0}}.

In the
c_{0}
-case the numerical range is understood in the general Banach-space
sense of (
1.1
), not through a duality map.
We do not pursue this extension here. The point of the formulation above is
rather to indicate that the present arguments depend only on a small number
of structural features of
\ell_{p}
.
A related way of isolating sequence-space arguments through assumptions on a
decomposition of the underlying Banach space appears in
[
25
]
, where

c_{0}
and
\ell_{p}
,
1<p<\infty
, are obtained as applications of a more
general theorem.

5.2.
Joint essential numerical ranges

A second extension concerns several operators simultaneously. The same ideas lead naturally to several new properties of the joint essential numerical range on
\ell_{p}
-spaces. Let
1<p<\infty
. For an
r
-tuple
(T_{1},\dots,T_{r})\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})^{r}
, define the joint numerical range by

W(T_{1},\dots,T_{r}):=\bigl\{(\langle T_{1}x,J(x)\rangle,\dots,\langle T_{r}x,J(x)\rangle):x\in\ell_{p},\ \|x\|=1\bigr\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{r}.

The joint essential numerical range
W_{e}(T_{1},\dots,T_{r})
is defined as the set of all
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r})\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
such that there exists a weakly-null sequence

(x_{n})\subset\ell_{p}
of unit vectors with

\langle T_{j}x_{n},J(x_{n})\rangle\to\lambda_{j}\qquad(j=1,\dots,r).

The arguments of the preceding section extend almost literally to finite tuples, where, in particular, the Calkin-algebra formulation is understood in the usual joint algebraic numerical range sense.
We record the resulting formulation and omit the proof.
Here the symbol
V
in the quotient-algebra formula denotes the joint algebraic numerical range.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(T_{1},\dots,T_{r})\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})^{r}
. Then:

(i)

W_{e}(T_{1},\dots,T_{r})
is a nonempty compact convex subset of
\mathbb{C}^{r}
;

(ii)

W_{e}(T_{1},\dots,T_{r})=V\bigl(\pi(T_{1}),\dots,\pi(T_{r}),\mathcal{L}(\ell_{p})/\mathcal{K}(\ell_{p})\bigr)
;

(iii)

W_{e}(T_{1},\dots,T_{r})=\bigcap\{\overline{W(T_{1}+L_{1},\dots,T_{r}+L_{r})}:L_{1},\dots,L_{r}\in\mathcal{K}(\ell_{p})\}
;

(iv)

\overline{W(T_{1},\dots,T_{r})}
is star-shaped, and every point of
W_{e}(T_{1},\dots,T_{r})
is a star-centre of this set.

(v)

\operatorname{Int}W_{e}(T_{1},\dots,T_{r})\subset W(T_{1},\dots,T_{r})
. If this interior is nonempty, then
W(T_{1},\dots,T_{r})
is star-shaped, with every point of the interior as a star-centre.

Hilbert-space counterparts of the tuple statements above can be found in

[
29
,
32
,
34
,
35
]
. See also
[
23
, Chapter 8.1]
.

Appendix A
A computational proof of the
\ell_{3}^{2}
counterexample

We supply here the full computation for Proposition
2.10
.

Proof of Proposition
2.10
.

For
p=3
and
q=3/2
, write

r=\frac{t^{3}}{1+t^{3}},\qquad t>0,

and introduce the shorthand

s_{t}:=2r-1=\frac{t^{3}-1}{1+t^{3}}.

Then

\beta(r)=\frac{t^{2}}{1+t^{3}},\qquad\gamma(r)=\frac{t}{1+t^{3}}.

Applying Proposition
2.1
to the matrix
S
, one obtains ellipses

E_{t}=\left\{s_{t}\!\left(8+\frac{i}{5}\right)+a_{t}\cos\eta+i\,\varepsilon_{t}b_{t}\sin\eta:\eta\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

where
\varepsilon_{t}\in\{\pm 1\}
is irrelevant for the underlying ellipse and

a_{t}=\frac{t^{2}+\frac{6}{5}t}{1+t^{3}}=\frac{5t^{2}+6t}{5(1+t^{3})},\qquad b_{t}=\frac{\bigl|t^{2}-\frac{6}{5}t\bigr|}{1+t^{3}}=\frac{|5t^{2}-6t|}{5(1+t^{3})}.

Thus

W(S)=\bigcup_{t>0}E_{t}.

We first determine which parameters can contribute points on the positive real ray.
If
\rho\in E_{t}\cap[0,\infty)
, then comparing imaginary parts gives

0=\frac{s_{t}}{5}+\varepsilon_{t}b_{t}\sin\eta,

hence necessarily

\left|\frac{s_{t}}{5}\right|\leq b_{t}.

Equivalently,

(A.1)

|t^{3}-1|\leq|5t^{2}-6t|.

If
1\leq t\leq 6/5
, then (
A.1
) becomes

Q(t):=t^{3}+5t^{2}-6t-1\leq 0.

Note that

Q
is strictly increasing on
[1,\infty)
, and

Q\!\left(\frac{9}{8}\right)=\frac{1}{512}>0.

Hence no
t\in[9/8,6/5]
satisfies (
A.1
).
If
t\geq 6/5
, then (
A.1
) becomes

P(t):=t^{3}-5t^{2}+6t-1\leq 0.

Observe that

P
is strictly decreasing on
[6/5,31/20]
, while

P\!\left(\frac{31}{20}\right)=\frac{91}{8000}>0.

Therefore no
t\in[6/5,31/20]
satisfies (
A.1
). Consequently, if
E_{t}
meets the positive real ray, then necessarily

(A.2)

t<\frac{9}{8}\qquad\text{or}\qquad t>\frac{31}{20}.

Next we obtain a uniform bound for the horizontal semiaxis.
For all
t>0
one has

a_{t}\leq\frac{6}{5}.

Indeed,

\frac{6}{5}-a_{t}=\frac{6t^{3}-5t^{2}-6t+6}{5(1+t^{3})}.

This positivity can be checked without using the discriminant. Indeed, with

h(t):=6t^{3}-5t^{2}-6t+6
, one has the elementary decomposition

h(t)=\frac{(6t-5)^{2}(12t+5)}{108}+2(t-1)^{2}(t+2)+\frac{91}{108}.

All terms on the right-hand side are non-negative for
t>0
, and the last one
is strictly positive. Hence
h(t)>0
for all
t>0
.

We now show that the point
3
is missing from
W(S)
.
If
\rho\in E_{t}\cap[0,\infty)
, then

\rho=8s_{t}+a_{t}\cos\eta,

hence

(A.3)

8s_{t}-a_{t}\leq\rho\leq 8s_{t}+a_{t}.

If
t<9/8
, then by monotonicity of
s_{t}
, (
A.3
), and the uniform bound on the horizontal semiaxis obtained above,

\rho\leq 8s_{9/8}+\frac{6}{5}=\frac{16126}{6205}<3.

If
t>31/20
, then again by monotonicity of
s_{t}
,

\rho\geq 8s_{31/20}-\frac{6}{5}=\frac{644894}{188955}>3.

Together with (
A.2
), this shows that no positive real point of
W(S)
can equal
3
. Hence
3\notin W(S)
.

It remains to prove that the points
0
and
6
do belong to
W(S)
.
Since the diagonal entries of
S
are
8+i/5
and
-8-i/5
, Corollary
2.6
gives

0=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(8+\frac{i}{5}\Bigr)+\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(-8-\frac{i}{5}\Bigr)\in W(S).

At
t=2
one has

s_{2}=\frac{7}{9},\qquad a_{2}=\frac{32}{45},\qquad b_{2}=\frac{8}{45},

so

E_{2}=\left\{\frac{56}{9}+\frac{7i}{45}+\frac{32}{45}\cos\eta+\frac{8i}{45}\sin\eta:\eta\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

Its intersection with the real axis is obtained from

\frac{7}{45}+\frac{8}{45}\sin\eta=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sin\eta=-\frac{7}{8},

hence

E_{2}\cap\mathbb{R}=\left[\frac{56}{9}-\frac{4\sqrt{15}}{45},\,\frac{56}{9}+\frac{4\sqrt{15}}{45}\right].

Since
4\sqrt{15}>10
, one has

\frac{56}{9}-\frac{4\sqrt{15}}{45}<6<\frac{56}{9}+\frac{4\sqrt{15}}{45},

so
6\in E_{2}\subset W(S)
.

Thus, we have proved that
0
and
6
belong to
W(S)
while
3\notin W(S),
so that
W(S)
is not star-shaped with centre
0
.
∎
