Frequency-dependent alterations of degree centrality in patients with poststroke depression: a resting-state functional MRI study
Authors: Shixin Wu, Sangsang Chen, Hao Chen, Xiulin Huang, Lishan Xu, Yanlan Huang, Qiuhong Lu, Jie Liu, Zhijian Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPoststroke depression (PSD) severely impedes functional recovery among stroke survivors. However, the neural mechanisms underlying PSD remain poorly understood. This study aimed to investigate aberrant intrinsic brain activity in PSD patients using multiband degree centrality analysis.MethodsA total of 84 patients with acute cerebral infarction were enrolled, including 35 patients with PSD and 49 non-PSD patients. All participants underwent resting-state functional MRI. Degree centrality values across the conventional, slow-4, and slow-5 frequency bands were compared between groups. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the relationships between regional degree centrality alterations and depressive symptoms.ResultsRelative to the non-PSD group, patients with PSD exhibited consistently decreased degree centrality in the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) across all three frequency bands, while reduced degree centrality in the left DLPFC was specifically identified in the slow-4 band. In the slow-4 band, the PSD group showed significantly elevated degree centrality in the left pallidum, left middle temporal gyrus, left fusiform gyrus, and corpus callosum, whereas no brain regions displayed increased degree centrality in the slow-5 band. Correlation analyses revealed that slow-4 degree centrality values in the left DLPFC were negatively correlated with Self-rating Depression Scale scores, whereas degree centrality values in the left pallidum were positively correlated with depressive severity.ConclusionPSD patients exhibited distinct frequency-dependent patterns of abnormal intrinsic brain connectivity, with the slow-4 band showing the most prominent functional alterations. These findings provide novel insights into the neural mechanisms underlying PSD and may facilitate the identification of frequency-specific functional biomarkers for PSD.

1. Introduction

Poststroke depression (PSD) is a frequent psychiatric complication after stroke [1], affecting nearly one-third of stroke survivors [2]. PSD has a negative impact on stroke rehabilitation, physical activity, and cognitive function, and increases the risk of recurrent cerebrovascular events [3]. To date, the exact pathogenesis of PSD remains unclear. Therefore, investigating the underlying neural mechanisms of PSD is essential for improving stroke recovery.

PSD is presumed to arise from complex interactions between the psychosocial stress induced by stroke and the biological damage resulting from focal brain lesions. The widely accepted fronto-striatal-limbic circuit dysfunction model provides a robust framework for elucidating PSD pathogenesis [4]. This model proposes that depressive manifestations originate from disrupted connectivity across a distributed network encompassing the prefrontal cortex (notably its dorsolateral and medial subregions), anterior cingulate gyrus, and striatum. The location of ischemic stroke can directly disrupt these specific circuits. In addition, the phenomenon of diaschisis – where a focal cerebral lesion triggers remote functional deficits in anatomically intact brain areas through interrupted neural pathways – represents a critical mechanistic pathway connecting primary stroke injury to widespread network dysfunction [5]. Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) captures spontaneous neuronal activity and has advantages over task-based fMRI in characterizing neurological function in patients with PSD, as no external stimulation is required [6,7]. To date, studies of PSD in the subacute phase after ischemic infarction have highlighted the insula and frontal lobe as core implicated brain regions [8–10]. Traditional views attributed PSD to direct lesion-induced damage to emotion-related circuits, such as the limbic-cortical-striatal-pallidal-thalamic (LCSPT) circuit [11]. However, growing evidence suggests that stroke impairs large-scale brain network integrity, triggering widespread dysregulation that contributes to depressive symptoms [12,13]. In recent years, graph theory-based brain network metrics, including degree centrality, have become crucial tools for characterizing poststroke brain functional reorganization and compensatory mechanisms. Specifically, degree centrality quantifies the number of direct functional connections a voxel has with others in the network, reflecting local topological properties. It exhibits high sensitivity, specificity, and test reliability [14], making it well-suited to reveal the complexity of the brain functional connectome and uncover compensatory mechanisms in PSD. However, previous rs-fMRI network analyses have predominantly relied on classical frequency bands, potentially obscuring distinct information encoded by different neural oscillations.

rs-fMRI signals can be decomposed into four discrete frequency bands: slow-5 band (0.01–0.027 Hz), slow-4 band (0.027–0.073 Hz), slow-3 band (0.073–0.198 Hz), and slow-2 band (0.198–0.25 Hz) [15]. In addition, slow-4 and slow-5 are the most widely studied frequency bands because of their associations with disease-related brain function alterations. Evidence suggests that these bands reflect distinct physiological processes. Existing research indicates that slow-5 oscillations may relate to frontal cortex signals and sustained network integration, whereas slow-4 oscillations tend to be associated with local neural processing and basal ganglia function [16]. Meanwhile, Gong and Zuo [17] further highlighted the specificity of these bands to higher-order functions, such as attention and executive control. Previous Parkinson’s disease research has noted this phenomenon, potentially suggesting that distinct pathophysiological processes manifest at different oscillatory frequencies [18]. Similarly, in patients with basal ganglia ischemic stroke, degree centrality alterations show frequency-dependent patterns, suggesting that oscillatory frequencies may serve as proxies for dissociable mechanisms [19]. Collectively, these cross-disease findings indicate that PSD-related network changes may also display frequency-dependent characteristics.

Based on the fronto-striatal dysfunction and diaschisis pathogenic models of PSD, alongside evidence for distinct physiological roles of neural oscillations and frequency-specific pathological profiles in other neurological disorders, we hypothesize that PSD-related degree centrality alterations exhibit frequency-dependent properties. Specifically, the activated brain regions in the slow-4 and slow-5 bands may exhibit distinct spatial distributions. Therefore, the present study aims to investigate frequency-dependent alterations in degree centrality in patients with PSD using rs-fMRI. We suggest that a multifrequency degree centrality approach may better characterize the network pathophysiology of PSD and uncover insights overlooked by single-band analysis.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Participants

A total of 88 patients with first-ever stroke were prospectively recruited from the Department of Neurology, The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University, between July 2020 and December 2024. All enrolled patients were categorized into two groups based on the presence of PSD: 38 patients in the PSD group and 50 nondepressed stroke patients in the non-PSD group. None of the participants had taken any antidepressant medications before the PSD assessment. All patients diagnosed with PSD received standardized psychiatric management after formal diagnosis by the psychiatry department. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) age ranging from 18 to 80 years; (b) right-handedness; (c) first-ever stroke with an interval of at least 1 month before PSD diagnosis, and PSD was confirmed by senior neurologists according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition; (d) absence of visual or hearing impairment, aphasia, and severe cognitive dysfunction. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) presence of any MRI contraindications; (b) a lifetime history of psychiatric disorders; (c) incomplete MRI data, poor image quality, or excessive head motion during scanning; (d) poor behavioral compliance with the study protocol. In total, four patients were excluded because of excessive head motion during rs-fMRI scanning, with detailed head motion parameters summarized in Table S1, Supplemental digital content 1, https://links.lww.com/WNR/A858. The final analytical cohort consisted of 35 PSD patients and 49 non-PSD stroke patients.

2.2. Demographic and clinical data

All participants completed a battery of assessments, including the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) and Self-rating Depression Scale (SDS). Meanwhile, cognitive function was evaluated using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), and daily living ability was assessed via the Barthel Index (BI). Potential confounding factors, such as anxiety, sleep quality, vascular burden, and medication effects, were considered in this study.

2.3. MRI data acquisition

All neuroimaging data were acquired using a 3.0 T Siemens Prisma scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University. rs-fMRI images were collected utilizing a standard echo-planar imaging sequence with the following scanning parameters: echo time = 35 ms, repetition time = 2000 ms, 40 axial slices (thickness/gap = 3/0 mm), flip angle = 90°, field of view (FOV) = 240 × 240 mm2, matrix = 64 × 64, voxel size = 2.6 × 2.6 × 3 mm3. Total duration = 6 min 12 s. The structural T1-weighted images were recorded by magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo: inversion time = 900 ms, echo time = 2.98 ms, repetition time = 2300 ms, 176 sagittal slices (thickness/gap = 1/0 mm), FOV = 256 × 256 mm2, matrix = 256 × 256, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3. Total duration = 5 min 21 s. Participants were instructed to remain still with their heads fixed and eyes closed while staying awake during scanning. Foam padding and headphones were utilized to reduce motion artifacts and scanner noise.

2.4. MRI data processing

All data preprocessing and statistical analyses were implemented based on MATLAB R2022b (https://uk.mathworks.com/products/matlab). rs-fMRI data were processed and analyzed using Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm) and the rs-fMRI Data Analysis Toolkit plus (RESTplus, V1.25; http://restfmri.net/forum/restplus) [20]. The detailed preprocessing pipeline was performed as follows: (a) the first 10 functional volumes were discarded to eliminate unstable initial scanning signals; (b) slice timing correction was conducted to compensate for inter-slice acquisition delays; (c) realignment was performed using the first volume as the reference, and datasets with excessive head motion (translation >3 mm or rotation >3°) were excluded; (d) all functional images were spatially normalized to the standard Montreal Neurological Institute brain template and resampled into 3 mm isotropic voxels; (e) linear drift removal was applied to correct for low-frequency signal drift; (f) nuisance signal regression was performed, including white matter signal, cerebrospinal fluid signal, and Friston-24 head motion parameters; (g) band-pass filtering was separately conducted for three frequency bands: conventional band (0.01–0.1 Hz), slow-4 band (0.027–0.073 Hz), and slow-5 band (0.01–0.027 Hz). Notably, spatial smoothing was not performed before subsequent degree centrality calculation.

2.5. Degree centrality calculation

Voxel-wise degree centrality quantifies the number of functional connections between a given voxel and all other voxels across the whole brain. Degree centrality calculation was performed using RESTplus V1.25. Specifically, functional connectivity was determined by computing Pearson correlation coefficients between each pair of voxels, with a predefined correlation threshold applied to define valid functional connections [21]. In the present study, the correlation threshold was set at r > 0.25 [8]. Voxel-wise degree centrality values were initially computed for each frequency band, followed by Fisher z-transformation to normalize the degree centrality maps. Before statistical analysis, all degree centrality maps were spatially smoothed using a Gaussian kernel with a full-width at half-maximum of 4 mm. Finally, band-specific degree centrality metrics derived from the conventional, slow-4, and slow-5 frequency bands were separately generated for subsequent statistical analyses.

2.6. Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, USA). Continuous variables, including age, SDS scores, and NIHSS scores, were presented as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range according to their distribution characteristics. Demographic indicators (age and education level) and neuropsychological scale scores were compared between groups using independent sample t-tests or nonparametric rank-sum tests. Categorical variables were analyzed via the chi-square test. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P value <0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

Voxel-wise two-sample t-tests were conducted to compare whole-brain degree centrality maps across the conventional, slow-4, and slow-5 frequency bands between the PSD and non-PSD groups. Head motion, education level, and gender were treated as confounding covariates. To mitigate the influence of lesion heterogeneity, lesion location and lesion laterality were additionally incorporated into the statistical model. Notably, all patients with multiple infarcts were excluded to reduce inter-individual variability in brain functional activity. Gaussian random field correction was applied for multiple comparisons, with a voxel-level threshold of P < 0.005 and a cluster-level threshold of P < 0.05 (cluster size >50 voxels) to identify brain regions with significant intergroup degree centrality alterations. Brain anatomical segmentation was performed based on the automated anatomical labeling atlas.

Degree centrality values of significantly altered brain clusters were extracted for subsequent correlation analyses. The normality of data distribution was preliminarily tested, and Spearman correlation analysis was adopted to evaluate the associations between regional degree centrality values and clinical indicators, including SDS, NIHSS, MMSE, and BI scores. Lesion location and laterality were controlled as covariates during correlation analyses. Of note, this study did not perform subgroup stratification based on lesion volume, nor did it quantitatively assess the potential impacts of stroke lesions on depression-related LCSPT neural circuits, which should be further explored in future research.

3. Results

3.1. Demographic and clinical characteristics

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the final cohort, including 35 patients with PSD (mean age: 56.857 ± 12.040 years; 9 females) and 49 nondepressed stroke patients (non-PSD; mean age: 55.940 ± 8.883 years; 12 females), are summarized in Table 1. Baseline demographic variables, including age, gender, and education level, were well-matched between the two groups. No significant intergroup differences were found in MMSE scores (P = 0.937), BI scores (P = 0.708), or NIHSS scores (P = 0.901). As expected, the PSD group exhibited significantly higher SDS scores than the non-PSD group (P < 0.001). Furthermore, the two groups were comparable in vascular burden, medication usage, sleep quality, and anxiety status. In terms of lesion characteristics, no significant group differences were observed in lesion location, laterality, or lesion volume, indicating balanced lesion features across the enrolled participants (Table 1).

3.2. Degree centrality alterations in multifrequency bands

In the conventional frequency band, patients in the PSD group showed significantly decreased degree centrality values in the right angular gyrus and right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) relative to the non-PSD group. In contrast, significantly higher degree centrality values were detected in the left fusiform gyrus (FFG), left thalamus, left lingual gyrus (LG), and corpus callosum (CC) in the PSD group (Table 2 and Fig. 1).

In the slow-4 frequency band, the PSD group exhibited reduced degree centrality in the bilateral rectus gyrus, bilateral DLPFC, orbital part of the left middle frontal gyrus (MFG), right precentral gyrus (PrCG), and opercular part of the right inferior frontal gyrus. Meanwhile, increased degree centrality values were observed in the left pallidum, left middle temporal gyrus, left FFG, and CC in the PSD group compared with the non-PSD group (Table 2 and Fig. 1).

Notably, the slow-5 band displayed a distinct degree centrality alteration pattern relative to the other two frequency bands, with significant differences confined solely to cortical regions and characterized exclusively by decreased degree centrality signals. Specifically, the PSD group showed significantly lower degree centrality values in the bilateral supplementary motor area, left inferior temporal gyrus, and right DLPFC than the non-PSD group (Table 2 and Fig. 1).

3.3. Correlation analysis between degree centrality values and clinical scores

Correlation analyses revealed significant clinical associations exclusively within the slow-4 frequency band in PSD patients. Specifically, degree centrality values in the left DLPFC were negatively correlated with SDS scores (r = −0.357, uncorrected P = 0.036; Fig. 2). In contrast, degree centrality values in the left pallidum showed a significant positive correlation with SDS scores (r = 0.376, uncorrected P = 0.026; Fig. 2). No significant correlations were detected between degree centrality values and other clinical indicators across the remaining frequency bands.

4. Discussion

4.1. Specific alterations in degree centrality values across different frequency bands

The current study adopted a degree centrality analytical approach to identify critical brain nodes associated with PSD and to explore the characteristics and clinical relevance of brain functional reorganization in patients with PSD across multiple frequency bands (conventional, slow-4, and slow-5). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in lesion distribution, infarct volume, lesion laterality, NIHSS score, and BI score. Infarcts in both groups were predominantly located in the basal ganglia. The balanced baseline characteristics minimized the confounding effects of lesion heterogeneity on PSD and ensured good comparability between the two groups. Compared with the non-PSD group, we found that the degree centrality value of right DLPFC declined across all three bands in the PSD group. By contrast, the left DLPFC exhibited lower degree centrality values exclusively within the slow-4 band. Meanwhile, several brain regions displayed a significant degree centrality differences between the slow-4 and slow-5 bands. No brain regions with increased degree centrality values were found in the slow-5 band. These findings indicated a potential trend of frequency-dependent degree centrality changes in PSD. Collectively, the slow-4 band showed relatively greater differentiation and more extensive degree centrality alterations. Therefore, subsequent discussion focuses primarily on degree centrality alterations within the slow‑4 band in patients with PSD.

In the present study, we detected significantly reduced degree centrality values in the bilateral DLPFC, specifically within the slow‑4 frequency band. Accumulating evidence over recent decades has established the prefrontal depression circuit as a critical contributor to the pathogenesis of depressive disorders. This circuit, centered primarily on the left DLPFC, was first validated via cross‑validation in a previous investigation by Padmanabhan et al. [22]. Reduced degree centrality values in the prefrontal lobe may weaken interregional neural connectivity, thereby disrupting the transmission and processing of emotional information [23,24]. Furthermore, the prefrontal cortex is tightly coupled to the reward pathway that governs dopamine release [25]. Reduced degree centrality within the left DLPFC may suppress the functional activity of the reward circuit and exacerbate depressive symptoms in stroke survivors. Accumulating clinical studies have repeatedly reported diminished spontaneous neural activity in the MFG across patients with bipolar disorder, anxiety disorders, and major depressive disorder (MDD) [26,27]. Meanwhile, significant reductions in DLPFC gray matter volume have also been documented among patients with MDD [28]. Conversely, several investigations have detected elevated degree centrality values within the left DLPFC [8–10,29,30]. These findings imply that enhanced DLPFC activity may counterbalance stroke-related lesions within the depressive circuit in PSD. Notably, whereas most prior fMRI research focused primarily on left DLPFC abnormalities in PSD, our data revealed diminished activity in the right DLPFC across all tested frequency bands among PSD patients, indicating that the right DLPFC also participates in PSD-associated brain reorganization. Tassy et al. [31] reported that the right DLPFC may be involved in integrating emotions related to contextual information processing. Consistent with previous findings, bilateral DLPFC (particularly the left DLPFC) has been commonly implicated in depression-related brain changes in stroke patients.

Meanwhile, we identified significantly elevated degree centrality values in the left LG, left pallidum, temporal lobe, and CC within the slow‑4 band. By contrast, the PrCG, left orbital MFG, left frontal operculum, and bilateral RG displayed reduced degree centrality values. Several of these highlighted brain regions (the left DLPFC and left pallidum) showed significant correlations with SDS scores, whose detailed findings will be elaborated subsequently. Shen et al. [32] reported associations between PSD and functional alterations in the temporal lobe as well as the genu of the CC using diffusion kurtosis imaging. Human emotion and mental processing rely on coordinated activity across an integrated neural network comprising the prefrontal cortex and temporal lobe; the latter features extensive anatomical interconnections with distant brain regions via white-matter fiber tracts. Additional work has confirmed the temporal lobe’s central role in regulating emotional responses, and suppressed temporal-lobe activity has been linked to improved emotional stability in stroke cohorts [33]. The temporal lobe may play a critical role in depressive symptomatology [34]. Accordingly, we detected statistically elevated degree centrality values across the temporal lobe, an observation that may mechanistically account for irritability manifested in patients with PSD. Regarding the CC, this transverse white-matter fiber bundle represents the largest interhemispheric commissure of the human brain. In addition, all other brain regions showing reduced degree centrality were confined to the frontal lobe. Hypoactivity within these frontal territories is closely linked to core depressive manifestations such as emotional blunting and hopelessness [35].

4.2. Correlation analysis

Degree centrality values in the left pallidum and left DLPFC were both positively correlated with SDS scores among patients with PSD. Notably, these correlations lost statistical significance following FDR correction, precluding definitive causal inferences. Nevertheless, their moderate uncorrected correlation coefficients, together with sound neuroanatomical rationales, suggest these two regions may contribute to PSD pathophysiology and merit further investigation. The ventral pallidum (VP) has been associated with depressive behaviors in prior preclinical and clinical research [36–38]. Philipsson et al. [39] reported that pallidal hyperactivation triggers acute severe depressive symptoms, supporting a tight link between pallidal dysfunction and the neurophysiological underpinnings of PSD [40]. Preliminary findings from this study indicate that abnormalities in the left pallidum may be associated with neurophysiological processes related to PSD, suggesting that this brain region holds promise as one of the potential key neural nodes for PSD. On the contrary, a tentative negative correlation was observed between the degree centrality value of the left DLPFC and the SDS score. Earlier work has indicated that damage to the left DLPFC is closely linked to depressive symptoms [41], and this region has been widely adopted as a target for repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation therapy for depression in relevant research [42,43]. Our observations may hint at a potential key node involved in PSD and offer preliminary clues regarding early brain functional reorganization in affected patients, which may help to elucidate the potential neural mechanisms of PSD. Further large-sample studies are still needed to verify these preliminary results and enhance statistical reliability.

4.3. Frequency-dependent degree centrality alterations specific to poststroke depression

In the present study, we found that degree centrality alterations exhibited distinct patterns across conventional, slow-4, and slow-5 frequency bands, indicating that degree centrality abnormalities in PSD may be frequency-dependent. Furthermore, correlations between degree centrality values and clinical indicators were specific to the slow-4 frequency band and were not observed in the conventional or slow-5 bands. These findings support the existence of frequency-dependent mechanisms. This phenomenon may be related to the inherent characteristics of low-frequency oscillations (LFOs), since the slow-4 band has a stronger capacity to detect neural oscillatory signals in the basal ganglia and adjacent white matter. Meanwhile, stroke lesions of the enrolled subjects were concentrated in the basal ganglia, a region rich in blood vessels. Damage to the basal ganglia can induce functional changes in the cortices of multiple closely connected brain regions. The basal ganglia act as a critical convergence hub for extensive neural pathways. Infarction in this region can disrupt the integrity of the frontal cortico-basal ganglia-thalamic circuit, which is significantly associated with the onset of PSD [44]. Anatomically, the basal ganglia comprise multiple substructures and complex neural projections, among which the VP acts as a core constituent of the limbic circuit [45]. Moreover, accumulating evidence indicates that different frequency bands subserve distinct higher-order cognitive functions and support varying rates of brain network information integration, collectively enabling flexible brain responses to complex cognitive demands [17]. Notably, although rigorous preprocessing procedures – including motion artifact correction, hemodynamic noise separation, and global cerebrospinal fluid signal regression – were performed in the present study, the potential confounding effects of hemodynamic filtering cannot be completely eliminated. Future studies should simultaneously acquire auxiliary neurophysiological indicators to quantitatively evaluate hemodynamic confounding influences and further validate the present findings. Overall, our findings suggest that the slow‑4 band enables more robust detection of regional brain alterations relative to the slow‑5 band, a finding that aligns with prior investigations [19,46,47]. However, several prior studies have reported conflicting findings highlighting higher detection sensitivity within the slow‑5 band [48,49]. This discrepancy may reflect intrinsic frequency-dependent physiological patterns, and inconsistent findings across studies can be ascribed to multiple confounding factors: variable lesion topography, divergent analytical pipelines, and heterogeneous clinical characteristics of enrolled patients. The low-frequency oscillatory signals adopted in this study are susceptible to various physiological noises, including cardiac pulsation, respiratory fluctuation, vascular oscillation, and scanner-related artifacts. Therefore, to achieve a more objective and accurate interpretation of the biological implications of resting-state oscillations in distinct frequency bands, future studies should adopt a larger sample size while rigorously controlling for all confounding variables.

4.4. Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although laterality was controlled for in our statistical analyses, lesion heterogeneity was not comprehensively accounted for in the present study. In particular, subgroup stratification based on lesion volume and anatomical location was absent, and the quantitative contribution of lesions involving LCSPT-associated circuits remained unassessed. Future investigations incorporating larger cohorts and refined lesion mapping are required to resolve these unresolved associations. Second, the present investigation adopted a cross‑sectional design. Such a study design cannot clarify whether degree centrality abnormalities precede the onset of PSD or arise secondary to depressive pathology, precluding robust causal inferences and predictive clinical translation. Future longitudinal research is warranted to delineate the causal contribution of dynamic degree centrality shifts to PSD pathogenesis. Third, this study inherits inherent methodological limitations of rs-fMRI. First, the adopted degree centrality threshold (r > 0.25) lacks rigorous standardized criteria. In addition, LFOs are susceptible to physiological noise, scanner artifacts, and residual head motion, with such confounding effects being particularly prominent in stroke populations. Stroke-induced vascular damage may further disrupt neurovascular coupling, making it difficult to distinguish true neuronal network dysfunction from secondary vascular artifacts. Moreover, heterogeneous preprocessing pipelines across studies substantially influence rs-fMRI results and impair cross-study reproducibility, while the biological implications of distinct frequency bands remain incompletely understood and lack unified interpretative standards. Finally, resting-state brain activity is inevitably affected by uncontrolled variations in participants’ mental states and the constrained scanning environment.

5. Conclusion

The present study systematically characterized aberrant local intrinsic neural activity across three frequency bands in patients with PSD, advancing our understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying PSD. Importantly, our findings revealed evident frequency-dependent patterns of degree centrality alterations in PSD, highlighting the necessity of adopting frequency-specific analyses to capture subtle brain functional changes in this population. Future fMRI studies of PSD should fully consider frequency-specific effects to further elucidate the pathological mechanisms and refine brain functional biomarkers for PSD.
