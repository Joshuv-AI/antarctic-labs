Project Narrative – Track 3: Accelerator Stewardship Test Facility Program
1.0 Background/Introduction
 Photoinjectors capable of producing high spin-polarized electron beams are essential to current and
next-generation accelerator facilities in the United States and worldwide, from CEBAF and the EIC to
MAMI, ILC, and KEK. Beyond large machines, spin-polarized photocathodes also underpin smaller-scale
instruments such as spin-polarized low-energy electron microscopes (SPLEEM) for basic materials
research. Today’s state of the art relies almost exclusively on III–V semiconductors, especially multilayer
strained-layer superlattices (SLS) coupled with distributed Bragg reflectors (DBR) and Fabry–Pérot (FP)
resonators to boost quantum efficiency (QE) via enhanced absorption. GaAs, grown by MBE or MOCVD,
remains the incumbent platform; however, the combination of growth complexity, uniformity/yield
challenges, and rising gallium cost and supply risk [1] motivate a serious search for alternative material
systems.
 Recognizing both the strategic need and the opportunity for simplification and resilience, RMD is
developing cadmium telluride (CdTe) as a promising II–VI alternative and applying atomic layer deposition
(ALD) as the growth method. The hypothesis is that ALD’s monolayer-level control over thickness, strain,
and composition can deliver high-quality emitters and ultrathin surface stacks with a manufacturing profile
that is difficult to realize with conventional III–V routes. Partnership with BNL provides the UHV
activation environment and polarimetry needed to evaluate QE and spin polarization on realistic timelines.
In Phase I we used this framework to reduce uncertainty around CdTe’s viability: although a stable NEA,
emissive state was not realized during the period of performance, the data converged on a clear, addressable
near-surface bottleneck. Thus the program has advanced from “Can CdTe work?” to “Here is the specific
process path that unlocks it,” positioning CdTe as a credible candidate to complement or, in select use-
cases, replace GaAs while mitigating single-supply vulnerabilities.
2.0 Proposed Research, Innovative Claims, Technical Rationale, and Approach
 This project explored ALD as an alternative growth technique for II–VI photocathodes and assessed
CdTe for spin-polarized electron production. Working with BNL (Cultrera group), we established the
workflow to measure QE and spin polarization using BNL’s Mott polarimeter, and we prepared both
conventional Cs/O NEA activation and a heterostructure NEA route leveraging an ultrathin Cs2Te
overlayer. The latter provides a pragmatic activation alternative while maintaining photoexcitation
primarily in CdTe.

3.0 Final Remarks

 This Phase-I effort advanced CdTe from a speculative alternative to GaAs toward a defined,
testable engineering program. While a stable NEA, emissive surface was not realized within the
period of performance, the measurements we executed, converged on a clear, process-
addressable root cause: a recombination-active near-surface region (“dead layer”) compounded
by limited effective collection length in the present polycrystalline films. That convergence is the
key outcome: it transforms an open-ended feasibility question into a decision-oriented plan with
quantitative gates.

 Technically, we established the diagnostics and analysis framework needed for rapid
iteration. QE(λ) proved to be a sensitive, low-latency indicator of both surface and transport
quality, and our two-parameter model (dead-layer thickness 𝑑𝑑0 and collection length 𝐿𝐿𝐶𝐶 ) provides
numerical targets to judge each process change. These results explain the low absolute QE and
the blue-dip/green-peak/red-roll-off spectral shape, and they point directly to remedies that are
standard, practical, and compatible with scale.

 Programmatically, Phase I also built the infrastructure to execute: an ALD growth
baseline for CdTe; a documented surface-prep and activation workflow; and a ready path to Mott
polarimetry at BNL. These capabilities, combined with the lessons on surface handling,
substantially de-risk a continuation. The path forward is clear: (1) stand up a dedicated CdTe
ALD line to eliminate contamination and accelerate testing; (2) introduce Sb p-type control to
tune band bending and reduce surface depletion; (3) apply remote hydrogen plasma plus ultrathin
passivation/NEA caps to collapse the dead layer; and (4) proceed to NEA and first polarization,
with success measured by changes in 𝑑𝑑0 and 𝐿𝐿𝑐𝑐 .

 The broader impact remains compelling. A credible CdTe platform, realized via ALD,
would diversify the nation’s photocathode supply chain, reduce reliance on complex III–V SLS
stacks, and align with manufacturing-forward approaches that favor uniformity and conformality.
Even in the absence of an emissive demonstration in Phase I, the evidence points to process, and
not physics limits, and the remedies are within reach. Success would open a new lane for
scalable, robust, spin-polarized electron sources, with downstream benefits for accelerators and
spin-sensitive instrumentation.
