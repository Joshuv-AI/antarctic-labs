Impact of government health expenditure on health outcomes in China: a quantitative analysis of mediation and threshold effects
Authors: Mei Gui, Xianming Kuang, Caifa Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo investigate the impact of government health expenditure on health outcomes in China, including its direct effects, underlying pathways and how these effects vary under different conditions of expenditure scale, health resource availability and economic development.DesignA quantitative study using provincial panel data from 2008 to 2023. Analysis was conducted using a fixed-effects model, with instrumental variables employed to address potential endogeneity. A mediation model was used to examine indirect pathways and a threshold regression model was applied to explore heterogeneous effects across different levels of key variables.SettingThe study was conducted using data from Chinese provinces over the period 2008–2023, within the context of China’s national health policy and the ‘Healthy China’ strategy.Participants31 provinces, autonomous regions and municipalities in China were included, with data extracted from official statistical yearbooks and health reports over the 16-year period.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcomes: maternal mortality and perinatal mortality. Secondary outcome: life expectancy.ResultsGovernment health expenditure significantly reduced maternal mortality and perinatal mortality but its effect on life expectancy was not statistically significant. The number of health institutions showed a negative mediating effect. Threshold analysis revealed that the impact of government health expenditure varied across levels of expenditure scale, health resources and economic development. In low-income contexts, government health expenditure could even produce adverse effects; however, with improved economic development and greater health resource availability, its positive impact on health outcomes became increasingly evident.ConclusionsThe health effects of government health expenditure are highly context-dependent. Optimal policy effectiveness requires coordinated interaction among multiple factors, including expenditure scale, health resource endowment and regional economic development.

Introduction

Against the dual backdrop of the deepening implementation of the Healthy China 2030 strategy and the accelerating demographic ageing, government health expenditure has emerged as a central institutional mechanism for safeguarding population health at the macro level in China. The effectiveness of the entire expenditure chain—from central fiscal transfers to the actual provision of local health services—directly determines the achievement of core national health objectives, including improvements in life expectancy and the prevention and control of major diseases. The ‘stress test’ of the COVID-19 pandemic exposed the urgent need to strengthen the resilience of the public health system, while widening regional health disparities and the increasing burden of chronic diseases have become salient challenges for high-quality development. Consequently, re-examining the mechanisms through which government health expenditure shapes health outcomes has become a critical entry point for understanding China’s health development model.

Government health expenditure has generally been found to exert positive effects on population health outcomes.1 A substantial body of cross-country and country-specific studies indicates that increasing public health spending effectively reduces infant and child mortality rates.2 3 Moreover, higher public health expenditure has been shown to improve life expectancy.4 5 Targeted public health investment also contributes to reducing the burden of specific diseases. For example, evidence from Chinese provincial panel data suggests that a 10% increase in per capita public health expenditure is associated with a 0.0019% decrease in tuberculosis incidence.6

However, the magnitude of this positive effect is far from uniform; substantial heterogeneity exists depending on a country’s or region’s level of economic development, fiscal capacity and baseline health conditions. The health-enhancing impact of public health spending tends to be stronger in low-income countries than in high-income countries.3 For high-income countries, increases in public health expenditure may yield diminishing marginal improvements in health outcomes. Within a single country, regional poverty levels7 and local government incentives shaped by fiscal decentralisation8 significantly moderate the health returns to government health expenditure.

Furthermore, the health effects of government health expenditure are influenced by several mediating and moderating factors. First, environmental quality interacts with health spending: for example, air pollution exacerbates disease burden and raises medical expenditures,9 whereas investment in green technological innovation can indirectly promote health improvements by enhancing environmental conditions.10 11 Second, governance and institutional quality serve as critical moderators, with sound governance substantially amplifying the health benefits of public expenditure.12 13 Third, health expenditure needs to operate synergistically with other forms of social spending—such as education and social protection—to generate optimal health outcomes.14 15 Lastly, technological innovation and digital transformation enhance accessibility and efficiency in health service delivery, thereby improving health outcomes.16 17

Despite meaningful progress in existing research, several important gaps remain. First, limited systematic evidence exists regarding the specific transmission pathways through which government health expenditure affects health outcomes—such as whether the impact operates primarily through ‘economic empowerment’ or ‘resource provision’ mechanisms. Second, few studies have examined potential non-linear features of expenditure effects, particularly whether structural changes in the magnitude or direction of impact arise once expenditure scale, economic development or health resource levels cross critical thresholds. To address these gaps, this study constructs an analytical framework that integrates both mediation pathways and non-linear conditions. Specifically, it systematically examines the mediating roles of income level and health resource availability in the relationship between government health expenditure and health outcomes, and employs a panel threshold model to identify threshold effects associated with expenditure scale, health resource endowment and economic development. The aim is to provide refined empirical evidence to support optimised allocation and coordinated regional development of China’s health expenditures, thereby contributing a more comprehensive and policy-relevant analytical perspective to the field.

Variable design and data sources

Dependent variables

The dependent variables in this study are measures of population health outcomes. Internationally, life expectancy, maternal mortality rate and under-five mortality rate are widely recognised as three comprehensive indicators of national health and social progress. In this study, we employ life expectancy, maternal mortality rate and perinatal mortality rate as indicators of health outcomes. Due to the lack of internationally comparable data on under-five mortality at the provincial level in China, perinatal mortality is used as a valid substitute. Together, these indicators provide a comprehensive reflection of population health.

Key explanatory variable

The key explanatory variable is government health expenditure, measured as the proportion of government health expenditure in total health expenditure. This measure effectively captures the relative intensity of government investment in the health system and reflects a ‘government-led’ policy orientation. While per capita government health expenditure is intuitively appealing from a budget perspective, it primarily reflects absolute spending levels and is highly correlated with regional economic development. In contrast, the proportion of government health expenditure in total health expenditure (THE) (GHE/THE) captures the relative intensity of government financing within the overall health sector—a metric closely tied to the government’s strategic priority and the reduction of household out-of-pocket burden. This indicator is less confounded by gross domestic product (GDP) and better reflects the ‘government-led’ policy orientation. Nevertheless, we fully recognise the policy relevance of absolute expenditure; therefore, we include per capita government health expenditure as a robustness check.

Mediating and threshold variables

Mediating variables include per capita disposable income and the number of primary healthcare institutions. Threshold variables include the proportion of government health expenditure in total health expenditure, the logarithm of per capita GDP and the number of practising physicians per 1000 population.

Control variables

To mitigate potential omitted variable bias, and based on existing literature, the following control variables are included: economic development (per capita GDP), resident income (per capita disposable income), education level (number of higher education students per 100 000 population), social protection (per capita social security expenditure), urbanisation (urbanisation rate) and population structure (child and elderly dependency ratios). These variables comprehensively cover socio-economic and demographic factors that influence population health. Descriptive statistics for the main variables are presented in table 1.

Instrumental variable

Fiscal decentralisation—measured as the ratio of provincial per capita fiscal expenditure to central per capita fiscal expenditure—is used as an instrumental variable for government health expenditure. The rationale is twofold. First, relevance: fiscal decentralisation directly affects the share of government health expenditure because provinces with greater fiscal autonomy have more discretion to allocate budgets toward health, especially under China’s decentralised health administration. Second, exogeneity: fiscal decentralisation is largely determined by long-standing administrative arrangements and national policies, and is unlikely to be directly influenced by short-term fluctuations in provincial health outcomes such as maternal mortality or life expectancy.

Results

Baseline regression results

The baseline regression results examining the impact of government health expenditure on health outcomes are presented in table 3. The results indicate that the effects of government health expenditure vary significantly across different health indicators. Specifically, an increase in the proportion of government health expenditure in total health expenditure does not have a statistically significant effect on life expectancy, with a coefficient of −0.001 and a p value greater than 0.1. In contrast, this variable exhibits a significant negative effect on both maternal mortality and perinatal mortality. A one percentage-point increase in the proportion of government health expenditure in total health expenditure is associated with a reduction of 0.992 in maternal mortality (per 100 000 live births) and 0.051 in perinatal mortality (per 1000 live births). Overall, the results suggest that increasing government health expenditure has a significant and positive effect in reducing maternal and perinatal mortality, while its impact on improving overall life expectancy is not statistically significant.

Endogeneity treatment

In the baseline regressions, the panel fixed-effects model may be subject to endogeneity. To address potential endogeneity issues, this study constructs a fiscal decentralisation index as an instrumental variable and performs a two-stage least squares regression based on the baseline model (1). The results are presented in table 4. In the first stage, the instrumental variable is significantly associated with the proportion of government health expenditure in total health expenditure at the 1% level. The Wald F-statistics in all specifications exceed 10, indicating that weak instrument problems are unlikely. In the second stage, the key explanatory variable—government health expenditure as a proportion of total health expenditure—remains statistically insignificant for life expectancy. In contrast, it exhibits a significant negative effect on both maternal mortality and perinatal mortality at the 1% significance level, consistent with the baseline regression results.

Robustness test

To verify the reliability of the baseline regression results, robustness checks were conducted from two perspectives: ‘adjusting the sample period’ and ‘replacing the key explanatory variable’.

Adjusting the sample period: Since 2008 marked a critical stage in China’s healthcare system reform, the data better reflect the actual effects of government health expenditure post-reform. In addition, in 2020, the government increased health expenditure and implemented tax reduction and fee exemption policies to respond to the COVID-19 pandemic. Excluding data from 2020 onward mitigates the influence of such extraordinary external shocks, allowing a more focused assessment of the intrinsic relationship between variables. Accordingly, the original sample period of 2008–2023 was adjusted to 2008–2019, forming a balanced panel of 31 provinces (including autonomous regions and municipalities) for re-estimation. Columns (1)–(3) in online supplemental table 1 report the effects of government health expenditure on health outcomes for this adjusted sample. The results indicate that the proportion of government health expenditure in total health expenditure remains statistically insignificant for life expectancy, while its coefficients for maternal mortality and perinatal mortality remain negative and significant at the 1% level (−0.962 and −0.033, respectively). Compared with the baseline regression coefficients in table 3 (columns 2 and 3: −0.992 and −0.051), the changes are minor (<5%) and not statistically significant. This suggests that even after excluding the post-2020 external shocks, the core relationship—‘an increase in the relative intensity of government health expenditure promotes better health outcomes’—remains robust.

Threshold effect analysis

Based on the baseline regression results, government health expenditure has a significant negative impact on maternal and perinatal mortality. This section conducts a panel threshold effect analysis for these two indicators. Given the multidimensional nature of government health expenditure’s impact on population health, its effect may vary across different ranges of government health expenditure, the number of physicians per 1000 population and per capita GDP. The panel threshold regression model proposed by Hansen (1999) is employed to test whether threshold effects exist for these variables.

To validate the threshold regression results, the existence of a statistically significant threshold effect is examined using the bootstrap method. When maternal mortality is the dependent variable, all three proposed threshold variables—government health expenditure as a proportion of total health expenditure, physicians per 1000 population and per capita GDP—pass the double-threshold test, indicating that the relationship between government health expenditure and maternal mortality exhibits threshold effects based on these variables. Likelihood ratio (LR) function plots further illustrate the estimated threshold values and corresponding 95% CIs. The LR statistic reaches zero when the thresholds are 66.87% and 67.99% for government health expenditure proportion, 1.56 and 2.4085 for physicians per 1000 population, and 9.5368 and 10.2085 for the logarithm of per capita GDP (see online supplemental table 2-4 and online supplemental figure 1-3).

When perinatal mortality is the dependent variable, the threshold variables of government health expenditure proportion and physicians per 1000 population do not pass the test, indicating no threshold effects. Only per capita GDP passes the double-threshold test, suggesting a threshold effect for government health expenditure on perinatal mortality based on economic development level. The LR function plots show that the LR statistic reaches zero at per capita GDP logarithm values of 9.5368 and 10.128 (see online supplemental table 2-4 and online supplemental figure 4).

In regions with low physician density (Doc. <1.56), expenditure increases significantly reduce maternal mortality (coefficient=−0.565, p<0.001). In medium-density regions (1.56≤Doc.<2.41), the negative effect strengthens (coefficient=−1.001, p<0.001) and in high-density regions (Doc. ≥2.41), the negative effect reaches its maximum (coefficient=−1.317, p<0.001). These results suggest that adequate medical resources can amplify the health benefits of government health expenditure.

Logarithm of per capita GDP as threshold variable (Model 3): At low per capita GDP levels (LnGDP <9.54), the effect of government health expenditure on maternal mortality is not significant (coefficient=−0.017, p>0.05). At medium levels (9.54≤LnGDP<10.21), the negative effect becomes significant (coefficient=−0.668, p<0.001) and at high levels (LnGDP ≥10.21), it further strengthens (coefficient=−1.037, p<0.001). This indicates that economic development is an important condition for government health expenditure to effectively reduce maternal mortality.

Logarithm of per capita GDP as threshold variable for perinatal mortality (Model 4): At low GDP levels (LnGDP <9.54), increases in government health expenditure are associated with higher perinatal mortality (coefficient=0.028, p<0.01), possibly reflecting inefficiencies in converting expenditure into service quality under low-income conditions. At medium and high GDP levels (9.54≤LnGDP<10.13 and LnGDP ≥10.13), government health expenditure significantly reduces perinatal mortality (coefficients=−0.030 and −0.061, p<0.001), with stronger effects as economic development increases.

Overall, the impact of government health expenditure on maternal and perinatal mortality exhibits significant threshold effects, depending on the level of expenditure, healthcare resources and economic development. These findings emphasise the importance of optimising health expenditure structure and coordinating it with medical resources and economic conditions to improve health outcomes effectively. Policymakers should consider these threshold effects and adopt differentiated strategies across varying threshold ranges.

Discussion

Government health expenditure in China has been effective in reducing maternal and perinatal mortality, but its impact on overall life expectancy is relatively limited. First, increased government health expenditure significantly lowers maternal and perinatal mortality, consistent with existing international theories and empirical evidence.4 23–28 Between 2008 and 2023, maternal mortality in China declined from 34.2 per 100 000 to 15.1 per 100 000, a cumulative reduction of approximately 55.8%. This decline is closely linked to sustained government investment in healthcare and targeted maternal and child health policies, such as ‘Reducing Consumption’ initiatives, indicating that targeted health expenditures can effectively improve health outcomes in specific populations. Second, the effect of government health expenditure on overall life expectancy is relatively limited, which differs from some previous findings.23 For example, studies on Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries suggest a positive correlation between per capita health expenditure and life expectancy, though the marginal effect diminishes as expenditure increases.29 Li Mengdie et al27 found that a 1% increase in government health expenditure is associated with an average increase of 0.075% in life expectancy. An apparent paradox arises: reductions in government health expenditure significantly reduce perinatal and maternal mortality—which should, in theory, increase life expectancy—yet we find no statistically significant effect on life expectancy. One possible explanation is that our data span 2008–2023, during which China’s life expectancy had already reached a high baseline (72.95 years in 2005 to 78.6 years in 2023). Further gains require addressing chronic diseases in middle and old age, which are less responsive to the types of government health expenditure that primarily target maternal and child health. Thus, the mortality reductions achieved in early life may be offset by stagnation in adult mortality decline, or simply be too small in magnitude relative to the large baseline life expectancy to produce a statistically detectable effect. This is an important area for future research using cause-specific mortality data.

The positive association between per capita disposable income and maternal/perinatal mortality observed in our mediation analysis is counterintuitive. We believe this finding likely reflects residual confounding rather than a genuine harmful effect of income. Wealthier provinces may have more complete vital registration systems, leading to higher recorded mortality and women in these regions tend to delay childbearing, which is independently associated with elevated maternal risk. These factors could generate a spurious positive coefficient even if the true income-mortality relationship is protective. We therefore conclude that per capita disposable income is not a reliable mediator in this context, and the observed indirect effect via income is likely attributable to model misspecification. Future studies using individual-level data and stronger causal designs are needed to disentangle these relationships. The number of healthcare institutions has a negative mediating effect in the pathway from government health expenditure to maternal and perinatal mortality. Rana et al3 found that in high- and upper-middle-income countries, the number of medical institutions has a positive mediating effect. However, in low-income countries, the effect is insignificant, suggesting that institutional quantity may not be the main channel through which government expenditure affects health. A possible explanation is that expansion of primary healthcare institutions may lead to resource fragmentation, insufficient professional staffing and reduced service quality.30 Therefore, health policy design should consider regional development stages and resource endowment, shifting focus from quantitative expansion to quality improvement to maximise the health benefits of government expenditure.

This study confirms the existence of threshold effects for government health expenditure on maternal and perinatal mortality. The inhibitory effect on maternal mortality exhibits a ‘strong–weak–strong’ pattern relative to expenditure levels and is strengthened by higher physician density and per capita GDP. At low per capita GDP, government health expenditure is positively associated with perinatal mortality, while at medium and high per capita GDP, it significantly reduces perinatal mortality, with effects increasing as economic development improves. These findings theoretically corroborate previous studies while providing novel insights in specific dimensions. Existing research remains inconclusive on whether the effects of health expenditure are linear. Our findings are consistent with Mao Wenlin and Longbao30 on threshold effects and extend Li Mengdie et al’s27 linear perspective. Specifically, health expenditure shows a ‘strong–weak–strong’ pattern for maternal mortality, and physician density reinforces this inhibitory effect. Yin Ming and Mingyue31 suggested that effects in low-income areas are insignificant; however, this study found that government health expenditure has a negative impact on perinatal mortality at low per capita GDP, which reflects hard constraints in the health production function of these poorer areas. Among these, the availability of health human resources is arguably the most critical. Empirical studies on China’s regional health systems have shown significant and non-convergent efficiency gaps between eastern, central and western provinces.32 These gaps are driven not only by financial inputs but also by core human resource indicators such as the number of physicians per 1000 population.33 In western provinces such as Qinghai, research has documented substantial spatial disparities in travel time to healthcare facilities, directly affecting service accessibility and utilisation.34 Consequently, increasing government health expenditure without simultaneously addressing workforce shortages and geographical barriers is likely to yield diminishing marginal health gains in low-income regions.

Therefore, ‘higher-quality spending’ should be focused on three aspects: First, spending should be directed towards health workforce development—including targeted training, retention incentives and continuing education for primary care providers—to relax the binding human resource constraint. Second, investment should improve service accessibility and system efficiency in underserved areas, such as township health centres and telemedicine infrastructure, rather than simply expanding bed capacity or high-technology equipment.34 Third, a greater share of expenditure should be allocated to preventive and primary care, given that lower-income populations are more price-sensitive and bear a larger burden of preventable diseases.

To maximise the health benefits of government health expenditure, our threshold and mediation results suggest several specific, actionable strategies. First, based on the strong-weak-strong pattern of expenditure effects on maternal mortality (Model 1), we recommend that policymakers in low-expenditure regions prioritise increasing government health expenditure to at least 67%, as the marginal return is high. In contrast, regions already near 67%–68% should avoid simply expanding expenditure further without improving complementary conditions; instead, they should focus on increasing physician density or per capita GDP. Second, the negative mediating effect of the number of healthcare institutions implies that simply building more primary care facilities—without ensuring adequate staffing and service quality—can be counterproductive. Therefore, we suggest a moratorium on further quantitative expansion in provinces with already high institutional density, redirecting funds toward workforce training, telemedicine infrastructure and performance-based quality incentives. Third, a notable finding is that in low-income regions (LnGDP <9.54), increasing government health expenditure is associated with higher perinatal mortality (coefficient=0.028, p<0.05). This is not an argument against investing in poor regions; rather, it indicates that health financing alone may be insufficient or even counterproductive without complementary workforce policies. In such contexts, we recommend that governments implement direct human resource measures before or alongside expenditure increases. These include: contractually required rural service rotations for medical graduates (linked to postgraduate training or licensure), salary subsidies for physicians in poor counties and task-shifting to community health workers. These recommendations extend the work of Chen et al,35 which emphasised overall funding increases and supply-side improvements (eg, hospital beds and skilled birth attendance). Our threshold analysis adds that the effectiveness of such measures critically depends on local economic and human resource baselines, and that in the poorest regions, untargeted spending may even backfire unless accompanied by targeted workforce policies. Finally, in high-income regions (LnGDP ≥10.21), where government health expenditure significantly reduces mortality, high-income regions should continue to increase fiscal investment in maternal and child health, including expanding high-quality obstetric medical resources, improving the tiered diagnosis, treatment and rapid referral system for high-risk pregnant women, strengthening community-based postpartum follow-up services, enhancing the remuneration and protection of obstetric healthcare workers and reducing the risk of maternal and perinatal mortality in all aspects.

This study also has some limitations. First, while focusing on mediating variables such as income and the number of healthcare institutions, other important factors influencing health outcomes, including service quality, physician competence and social security systems, may not be fully incorporated, limiting the comprehensiveness of the pathway analysis. Second, the study posits that ‘resource fragmentation’ and ‘reduced service quality’ may explain the negative effects of institutional quantity, but this is largely theoretical, lacking microlevel empirical evidence on the actual operation of primary healthcare institutions (eg, workload, equipment utilisation, patient satisfaction) to directly support this mechanism. Third, certain findings, such as the negative mediating effect of institution number, may heavily depend on China’s specific development stage and institutional context; thus, generalisation to other countries, especially those with different development levels or healthcare systems, should be approached cautiously.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that increased government health expenditure significantly reduces maternal and perinatal mortality. However, its impact on overall population life expectancy is not statistically significant, and this conclusion remains robust after addressing endogeneity and conducting robustness checks. The study finds that the number of health institutions is the only significant and robust negative mediating factor between government health expenditure and maternal/perinatal mortality. The role of per capita income could not be reliably established. Moreover, the health benefits of government health expenditure exhibit clear threshold effects, with the magnitude and direction of the effect significantly depending on regional expenditure scale, economic development level and healthcare human resources. These findings indicate that the effectiveness of government health expenditure in China is not determined solely by the scale of spending, but is jointly constrained by expenditure structure, resource allocation efficiency and socio-economic context. Future policy optimisation should shift focus from merely increasing ‘spending scale’ to addressing binding constraints on health returns—particularly health workforce availability and geographical accessibility—while enhancing spending efficiency and system coordination. In lower-income regions, this requires moving beyond undifferentiated expenditure growth towards structurally smarter investments that prioritise primary care workforce development, preventive services and accessibility improvements. Implementing region-specific, targeted strategies according to local development stages will more effectively improve population health outcomes and advance the ‘Healthy China’ initiative.
