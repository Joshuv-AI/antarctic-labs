The geometry of the giant component of random geometric graphs
Karoline Dubin, Christian Gorski, Marcus Michelen

Abstract
. Consider a random geometric graph G M ​ ( n , r ) G_{M}(n;r) whose vertex set consists of n n points chosen independently and uniformly from a Riemannian manifold M M , with edges joining pairs of vertices whose distance in the metric d M d_{M} is at most r r . Let Δ \Delta denote the expected average degree of the graph. As is the case for Erdős-Rényi graphs, there is a critical value Δ c \Delta_{c} , depending only on the dimension of M M , such that if Δ > Δ c \Delta>\Delta_{c} then G M ​ ( n , r ) G_{M}(n;r) has a giant component. We show that whenever Δ > Δ c \Delta>\Delta_{c} , the giant component of G M ​ ( n , r ) G_{M}(n;r) , equipped with the graph distance, converges to the underlying manifold M M in the Gromov-Hausdorff distance after rescaling by an appropriate deterministic factor. Our result holds for Δ \Delta depending on n n as well, provided Δ = o ⁡ ( n ) \Delta=o(n) and Δ ≥ Δ c + ε \Delta\geq\Delta_{c}+\varepsilon for any fixed ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 . As a consequence, we show that for any pair of non-isometric compact Riemannian manifolds M 1 M_{1} and M 2 M_{2} , there is a polynomial-time algorithm that distinguishes random geometric graphs on M 1 M_{1} and M 2 M_{2} throughout this regime of Δ . \Delta. In the thermodynamic regime—i.e. when Δ \Delta is constant—our results appear to be new even in the classical cases where M M is a sphere or a torus. Our proof makes use of techniques from first-passage percolation which allow us to understand the long-range behavior of the graph distance on small, approximately Euclidean patches of M M , together with global arguments that glue these local estimates into a global description.

1.1.
Algorithmic implications

There has recently been much attention on statistical and algorithmic problems related to the geometry of random geometric graphs. A natural question is when can one detect the difference between random geometric graphs and the Erdős-Rényi random graph. An influential work of Bubeck, Ding, Eldan, and Rácz
[
6
]
considers the case of constant edge density
p=\Omega(1)
and the regime where the dimension
d
and the number of points
n
simultaneously grow. They show that for
d\gg n^{3}
a random geometric graph of constant edge density is indistinguishable from the Erdős-Rényi graph while for
d\ll n^{3}
the graphs can be distinguished by counting signed triangles. Further work in this direction for the case of sparse
p
was considered by Liu, Mohanty, Schramm and Yang
[
18
]
. In both cases, the underlying manifold of the geometric random graph is the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

A question closer to our own is whether one can detect the underlying geometry itself. An influential work by Bernstein, De Silva, Langford, and Tenenbaum
[
3
]
from 2000 from the literature of
manifold learning
analyzes a closely related problem; they seek understand an algorithm “Isomap” which has a crucial stage where one approximates the graph distance in a random geometric graph on a
d
-dimension manifold
M
embedded in a higher dimension Euclidean stage with the intrinsic geodesic distance. At the core of
[
3
]
is an estimate of distances that is analogous to case of large
\Delta
, i.e.
3
. There has been much work on manifold learning since the work of
[
3
]
, although none appears to hold in the regime where the graph fails to be connected, e.g. when
\Delta=\Theta_{d}(1).

We highlight a recent sequence of works by Huang, Jiradilok, and Mossel
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
seeks to reconstruct the underlying geometry of noisy versions of random geometric graphs in an algorithmic fashion. Throughout their series of papers, rather than simply adding edges deterministically for those of distance at most
r
, they add edges between points
x,y
with some probability
p(d(x,y))
for some function
p
. This introduces a challenge to their problem that does not appear in our setting: the presence of additional edges—for instance edges that can be long in the
d_{M}
metric—leads to a noisier and denser setting. In particular, the graphs they consider need
not
be approximately isometric to the underlying manifold, and one of their central challenges is to infer the “signal” of the underlying geometry in spite of this “noise.”

The first work in their series
[
12
]
considers an embedded manifold
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and gives a
O(n^{3})
time algorithm for reconstructing the underlying metric in the dense regime (i.e.
r
is a constant, or equivalently when
\Delta=\Theta(n)
). Their next work
[
13
]
extends their work to smaller average degree, in particular down to
\Delta\gg n^{1/2}\mathrm{polylog}(n)
. Their very recent work
[
14
]
introduces a different technique for denoising and improves their results in higher dimensions. We refer the reader to their papers and the references therein for more context on manifold learning.

As a consequence of our techniques, we will show that one can detect geometry in polynomial time for all average degree bounded away from the critical threshold
\Delta_{c}(d)
. Since the distribution of the random geometric graph of average degree
\Delta
is invariant under rescaling the manifold, we will normalize so our manifolds have volume
1
.
We phrase our algorithmic result in the general setting of distinguishing between two manifolds that are not isometric:

Theorem 4
.

Let
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
be connected compact Riemannian manifolds of dimension
d_{1},d_{2}\geq 2
and volume
1
so that
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are not isometric. Let
\Delta_{c}(d_{j})
denote the critical average degree for having a giant component. There is some
K=K(M_{1},M_{2})<\infty
so that there is a deterministic algorithm depending on
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
with run time at most
n^{K}
with the following properties. There is
\alpha=\alpha(M_{1},M_{2})
so that for each
\Delta_{0}>\max\{\Delta_{c}(d_{1}),\Delta_{c}(d_{2})\}
there is
c=c(M_{1},M_{2},\Delta_{0})
so for all
\Delta\in[\Delta_{0},\alpha n]
, given the adjacency matrices of the random geometric graphs
G_{M_{1}}(n;r_{1})
and
G_{M_{2}}(n;r_{2})
of expected average degree
\Delta
, the algorithm can correctly identify which came from
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}n)\,.

In the case of constant
\Delta>\Delta_{c}(d)
this appears to be new even in the case of distinguishing a sphere and a torus. The main idea is that if two compact manifolds are not isometric, then there is some finite collection of points in one so that the array of pairwise distances between those points is never achieved in the other (see
37
) and subsequently applying
1
.

Remark 5
.

If
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
have different
diameter
, then the runtime in
4
can be simplified. In particular, the proof shows that we need only find the diameter of the graphs, and with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}n)
the graph coming from the manifold of larger diameter will have larger graph diameter. One can create the array of pairwise distances by running a breadth-first search starting from each of the
n
vertices in each graph. Since a breadth-first search has run-time
|E|
where
|E|=\Theta(n\Delta)
is the number of edges in each graph, this implies that we can distinguish between manifolds of different diameter in time
O(n^{2}\Delta)
in the setting of

Example 6

(Sphere versus torus)
.

If
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
have the same volume but different diameter then one example of a distinguishing statistic is the diameter of the giant components. In particular, in the case of a sphere versus a flat torus, we can let
M_{1}=\alpha_{d}\mathbb{S}^{d}
where
\alpha_{d}
is the normalization so that
M_{1}
has volume
1
and
M_{2}=\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d}

\mathrm{diam}(M_{1})=\pi\alpha_{d}
and
\mathrm{diam}(M_{2})=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{2}
. Since we have

\alpha_{d}=\left(\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}{2\pi^{(d+1)/2}}\right)^{1/d}

one can check that
\pi\alpha_{d}=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{2}
if and only if
d=1
. In particular, for all
d\geq 2
we have that
\mathrm{diam}(M_{1})>\mathrm{diam}(M_{2})
and so choosing the graph of larger graph metric diameter distinguishes a sphere from a torus with high probability.

We next give an example where the manifolds have equal volume and diameter but are not isometric.

Example 7
.

It is possible for
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
to have the same volume and diameter and still fail to be isometric. For instance, if we consider
M_{1}=\alpha_{2}\mathbb{S}^{2}

\mathrm{Vol}(M_{2})=1

Since the case of

1.2.
Proof outline and structure of the paper

Before describing the giant component of random geometric graphs, we recall some intuition for the emergence of the giant component in the Erdős-Rényi random graph
G(n,p)
. Recall that if
G=G(n,p)
for
p\in[0,1]
then
G
has
n
vertices and each possible edge is added independently with probability
p
. If we parameterize
p=\Delta/n
where we think of
\Delta
as fixed, then the average degree of
G
is approximately
\Delta
. Further, if we pick a random vertex
v\in G
, then it will have roughly
\mathrm{Poisson}(\Delta)
many neighbors, each of which will have approximately
\mathrm{Poisson}(\Delta)
neighbors and so on. When
\Delta
is fixed and
n
tends to infinity, the depth
k
ball around a random vertex
v
will be indistinguishable from a branching process with offspring distribution
\mathrm{Poisson}(\Delta)
. In the language of random graphs, this says that the
local weak limit
of
G(n,\Delta/n)
is the branching process with offspring distribution
\mathrm{Poisson}(\Delta)
. Synonymously, one says that
G(n,\Delta/n)
Benjamini-Schramm converges to the branching process with offspring distribution
\mathrm{Poisson}(\Delta)
.

The classical theory of branching processes tells us that when
\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Poisson}(\Delta)=\Delta\leq 1
we have that the branching process is finite almost surely, while if
\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Poisson}(\Delta)=\Delta>1
there is a positive probability that it is infinite. When thinking about
G(n,\Delta/n)
for
\Delta\leq 1
, this means that for a typical vertex its connected component has
O(1)
many vertices. This immediately implies that the largest component has
o(n)
many vertices; more refined work shows that the largest component is size
O(\log n)
for
\Delta<1
and
O(n^{1/3})
for
\Delta=1
. Conversely, for
\Delta>1
, there is a positive probability that the underlying branching process is infinite. Since
G(n,\Delta/n)
has only finitely many nodes, this means that two nodes whose local limits are quite large must eventually meet. This shows that for
\Delta>1
the largest connected component has size
\Omega(n).
We refer the reader to
[
4
]
for more context on
G(n,p)
and this phase transition.

One can understand the case of random geometric graphs in analogy with the Erdős-Rényi case by similarly thinking of Benjamini-Schramm convergence. If we set
M
to be a connected, compact Riemannian manifold of dimension
d
, then a small neighborhood of each point of
M
is locally Euclidean. Further, the volume form near a point will also be approximately Lebesgue when rescaled. More formally, for a point
x\in M
, if we take
\delta>0
small enough, then there is a map
u_{x}:B_{M}(x,\delta)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
that maps the ball of radius
\delta
centered at
x
in
M
that approximately preserves the measure and metric on
M
(see
8
for a formal statement). The image of the component of
G_{M}(n;r)
in
B_{M}(x,\delta)
will have vertex set given by an (approximately) homogeneous Poisson process of intensity
n
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and whose edges consist of points within Euclidean distance (approximately) at most
r
. In the case where
\Delta
is fixed, we can rescale
\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that points are connected if they are within distance
1
and the intensity
\lambda
is so that the average degree is
\Delta
(one may use
9
to show that
\lambda=\Delta/\mathrm{Vol}(B_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1))
). This limiting process is a model of
continuum percolation
that goes by various names, such as the Poisson-Boolean model or the Gilbert model, after being introduced by Gilbert in 1961
[
10
]
.

In analogy with Erdős-Rényi graphs, the relevant question is whether this continuum percolation model has an infinite component. Classical results show that for
d\geq 2
there is indeed a non-trivial phase transition, and there is some
\lambda_{c}=\lambda_{c}(d)
so that if
\lambda<\lambda_{c}(d)
there is almost-surely no infinite component while if
\lambda>\lambda_{c}(d)
then there is almost-surely a unique infinite component. We refer the reader to the classic book of Meester and Roy
[
19
]
on continuum percolation for more history and theory related to this transition.

This argument shows that the local weak limit of
G_{M}(n;r)
is given by continuum percolation; this can be turned into an argument that shows a giant component phase transition occurs precisely as the average degree
\Delta
crosses the critical value for continuum percolation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
where
d
is the dimension of
M
. Letting
\mathring{G}_{M}(n;r)
denote the giant component of
G_{M}(n;r)
, this local weak limit in fact captures the
geometry
of the giant component. Graph geodesics in
\mathring{G}_{M}(n;r)
between nearby points translate to graph geodesics in some approximate local weak limit, i.e. they are fairly close to graph geodesics in the infinite continuum percolation cluster in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The graph distance in a supercritical percolation cluster is often referred to as the
chemical distance
, and is typically analyzed using tools from
first-passage percolation
.

The most classical setting of first-passage percolation consists of the underlying graph being the infinite lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for
d\geq 2
with i.i.d. non-negative random variables
\tau_{e}
assigned to each edge referred to as its weight. The weight of a path is given by the sum of the weights along the path, and the distance
T(x,y)
between two points
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is the smallest weight achievable by a path between the points. Among the most classical statements of first-passage percolation is that for far away points, the random distance
T(x,y)
is roughly deterministic: for each unit vector
\widehat{v}
and edge distribution
\tau
there is a constant
\mu(\widehat{v})
known as the
time constant
so that if
x-y
is roughly parallel to
\widehat{v}
, then
T(x,y)\approx\mu(\widehat{v})\|x-y\|_{2}
. A stronger version of this that is uniform in the direction
\widehat{v}
is referred to as the
shape theorem
and was proven by Cox and Durrett
[
8
]
using a subadditive ergodic theorem by Kingman
[
15
]
which immediately proves the case for fixed
\widehat{v}
. We refer the reader to
[
2
]
for more context on first-passage percolation.

In our setting, the chemical distances on the infinite percolation cluster in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
also may be seen to follow a shape theorem. We will use a quantitative version of this by Yao-Chen-Guo
[
24
]
which we reproduce as
10
. This will show that not only do small patches of
\mathring{G}_{M}(n;\Delta)
look like the infinite cluster of continuum percolation from the perspective of connectivity, but also from the perspective of geometry. Namely, in each small patch of
M
, we will show using
10
that distances within that patch are approximately the time constant multiplied by the (rescaled) Euclidean distance.

In
Section

4
—which is the most technical section of the paper—we show that one can in fact move from the giant component of the finite random geometric graph
G_{M}(n;r)
to the infinite component of continuum percolation that is its local weak limit. From there, we apply the quantitative shape theorem of Yao-Chen-Guo in
Section

5
to show that on patches of
M
, we have that graph distances in
\mathring{G}_{M}(n;r)
behave in a predictable fashion. We then need to boost these patch estimates into global estimates, and do so by applying our results from
Sections

4
and
5
to show that not only does a finite collection of patches capture much of the geometry involved, but that graph geodesics do not wander far enough to escape any of these patches.
Section

6
is split into two sections, the first of which
Section

6.1
performs this gluing of patches to show that two-point distances in
\mathring{G}_{M}(n;r)
are close to the rescaled version of
d_{M}
while
Section

6.2
uses this estimate to build approximate embeddings that witness a bound on the Gromov-Hausdorff distance. These three sections complete the proof of
2
.

The proof of the denser case
3
is simpler and requires no percolation input. The proof proceeds in
Section

7
by showing an analogous two-point estimate by simply showing that in small patches along a
d_{M}
geodesic in
M
there are enough points so that the graph distance is roughly close to its minimum possible value. We end with
Section

8
where we deduce
4
from
1
. The main work there is to prove an elementary and likely standard fact about compact metric spaces
37
which shows that if two compact metric spaces are not isometric then there is a finite collection of points in one whose pairwise distances are never achieved in the other.

To begin with,
Section

2
consists of relevant preliminaries on Riemannian manifolds, Poisson processes, and first-passage percolation. Some of these facts are more standard—such as the Palm theory for Poisson processes—while some are tailored to our setting, such as the continuity of the time constant in continuum percolation (
12
, recently proven by the first two authors). We continue with a bookkeeping section,
Section

3
, where we specify choices of parameters and trap our approximately Euclidean patch between two copies of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
where we have only slightly rescaled the metric and measure.

Preliminaries

Throughout,
G_{M}^{n}(r)
will be the geometric random graph on
M
whose vertex set is given by a Poisson process of intensity
n
and we connect vertices
v
and

if

to Euclidean space. There we will rescale space by

and so we will define
\lambda:=nr^{d}
. While in the introduction we parameterize based on the average degree

instead since it will be more convenient when comparing to Euclidean space. The relationship between

and

We first describe a basic tool to compare our Riemannian manifold

with

2.1.
Riemannian manifolds

Throughout,
M
is a connected compact Riemannian manifold of volume
1
. We write
d_{M}
for the metric induced by the Riemannian metric on
M
and
dM
for the Riemannian volume measure. Central to our approach will be to compare a patch of
M
with a set in Euclidean space. We write
\mathcal{L}
for Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The following basic lemma will be our main tool:

Lemma 8

(Microscopic balls on
M
are almost flat)
.

For each
z\in M
and
\eta>0
there exists
\delta_{0}\in(0,1)

such that for every
0<\delta\leq\delta_{0}
,
any ball
U=B_{M}(z,\delta)
in
M
of radius
\delta
has the following properties.
Let

u:U\to B_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,\delta)

be exponential normal coordinates.
Then
u

is a diffeomorphism with

e^{-\eta}d_{M}(x,y)\leq d_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(u(x),u(y))\leq e^{\eta}d_{M}(x,y)

for all
x,y\in U
.
Moreover if
\mathcal{L}
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
then the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the pushforward
u_{\ast}dM
of the Riemannian volume measure
dM
satisfies

e^{-\eta}\leq\frac{u_{\ast}dM}{d\mathcal{L}}\leq e^{\eta}

on
u(U)
.

Proof.

Set
\phi=\log_{z}=(\exp_{z})^{-1}:U\to T_{z}M\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
where we use an orthonormal basis for
T_{z}M
to
identify
T_{z}M
with
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and

\log_{z}
is the inverse of
the exponential map
\exp_{z}:T_{z}M\to M

restricted to a small enough neighborhood
of
0
that it is a diffeomorphism. For a point
y\in U
we set
u=\phi(y)
and note that in these coordinates the metric satisfies

(1)

g_{ij}(u)=\delta_{ij}+O(|u|^{2})

(see, e.g.,
[
17
, Proposition 5.24]
). Taking
\delta
sufficiently small ensures that
\phi
is
e^{\eta}
-bi-Lipschitz. The Riemannian volume element
dM
is

(2)

dM=\sqrt{\det(g_{ij})}du^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge du^{d}

(see
[
17
, Proposition 2.41]
) and so combining with (
1
) shows
dM=(1+O(|u|^{2}))du^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge du^{d}
. Taking
\delta
small enough forces the
(1+O(|u|^{2}))
coefficient to lie in
[e^{-\eta},e^{\eta}]
which then completes the proof.
∎

2.2.
Poisson processes and first-passage percolation

Throughout we will make use of various aspects of Poisson processes. A useful tool is the Palm theory for Poisson processes, certain versions of which are often called Mecke formulas; this will allow us to compute the expectation of various statistics of Poisson processes in terms of deterministic integrals. We will often make use of these for estimating certain quantities and so we present a general version of this property:

Theorem 9

(Theorem 4.1
[
16
]
)
.

[Palm theory for Poisson processes.]

Let
\nu
be a
\sigma
-finite measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
X_{\nu}
a Poisson process with intensity
\nu
. Then for all non-negative, bounded and measurable functions
f
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{x\in X_{\nu}}f(x,X_{\nu})\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[f(x,X_{\nu}\cup\{x\})\right]\,d\nu(x)\,.

We note that iterating
9
yields a multivariate version, also known as the multivariate Mecke equation or the factorial Mecke equation (see
[
16
, Theorem 4.4]
for a precise statement).

For any
r>0
we let
G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\lambda}(r)
be the random geometric graph on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
where the vertex set is a Poisson process of intensity
\lambda
and points are connected by an edge if they are within distance at most
r
. Note that for any
r>0
we have that the pushforward
of
G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\lambda}(r)

under the map

\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},x\mapsto\frac{1}{r}x

has distribution equal to that of
G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\lambda/r^{d}}(1)
, and so we can always rescale Euclidean space so the connection radius is
r=1
.

For each
d\geq 2
, there exists
\lambda_{c}(d)<\infty
such that

\mathbb{P}(G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\lambda}(1)\mbox{ has an infinite connected component})=\begin{cases}0&\lambda<\lambda_{c}(d)\\
1&\lambda>\lambda_{c}(d)\end{cases}

and in fact there is always at most one infinite connected component
[
20
, Theorem 9.19]
.
We write
C_{\infty}(\lambda)
for the almost-surely unique infinite connected component of
G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\lambda}(1)
for
\lambda>\lambda_{c}(d)
.

By
9
, the average degree
\Delta
of
G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\lambda}(1)
satisfies
\Delta=\lambda\alpha_{d}
where
\alpha_{d}:=\mathcal{L}(B_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,1))
is the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The critical average degree
\Delta_{c}(d)
can thus be compared to
\lambda_{c}(d)
via
\Delta_{c}(d)=\alpha_{d}\lambda_{c}(d).

For
\lambda>\lambda_{c}(d)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we may write
\mathring{x}=\mathring{x}(\lambda)\in C_{\infty}(\lambda)
to be the closest point in
C_{\infty}(\lambda)
to
x
. Writing
d_{\lambda}
for the graph distance, we are interested in understanding the behavior of
d_{\lambda}(\mathring{x},\mathring{y})
for distant points
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A quantitative version of the shape theorem will say that these large distances behave in deterministic ways. We require a bit more notation to set this up. For events concerning
G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\lambda}
, we let
\mathbb{P}_{x,y}
denote the probability measure conditioned on
x
and
y
lying in the vertex set. For a graph
G
and vertices
x,y\in V(G)
, write
x{\mathrel{\to}^{G}}y
for the event that
x
and
y
are connected in the graph. The deterministic function
\mu:(\lambda_{c},\infty)\to(1,\infty)
is referred to as the
time constant
and is dependent only on the dimension; we may view the following as a definition of
\mu(\lambda)
:

Theorem 10

(Yao-Chen-Guo,
[
24
]
)
.

Fix
\lambda>\lambda_{c}(d)
. Let
G^{\lambda}=G^{\lambda}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1)
and let
d_{\lambda}
denote the graph distance
on
G^{\lambda}
. Then, for any
\xi>0
there exists

c(\lambda,\xi,d)>0
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

\mathbb{P}_{x,y}\left(x{\mathrel{\to}^{G^{\lambda}}}y,\frac{d_{\lambda}(x,y)}{\mu(\lambda)\|x-y\|}\notin[e^{-\xi},e^{\xi}]\right)\leq\exp(-c\|x-y\|).

We note that
c
may depend on
\lambda
, and so we will see that we will only apply
10
for a finite collection of
\lambda
which we will specify in
Section

3
. Using the relationship
\Delta=\alpha_{d}\lambda
, we may write
\overline{\mu}(\Delta):=\mu(\Delta/\alpha_{d})
.

Since in
G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\lambda}(1)
we always have
d_{\lambda}(x,y)\geq\|x-y\|
, we immediately have that
\mu(\lambda)\geq 1
for all
\lambda>\lambda_{c}
. A quick consequence of
10
will be that
\mu(\lambda)
is non-increasing. We need one more simple property, namely that
\mu(\lambda)\downarrow 1
as
\lambda\to\infty
.

Lemma 11
.

The time constant
\mu(\lambda)
is non-increasing with
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\mu(\lambda)=1\,.

Proof.

We note that the fact that
\mu(\lambda)
is non-increasing is a consequence of
10
. To see that
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\mu(\lambda)=1
, let
C_{\infty}(\lambda)
denote the unique infinite cluster of
G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\lambda}(1)
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
let
\mathring{x}(\lambda)\in C_{\infty}(\lambda)
denote the closest point
C_{\infty}(\lambda)
to
x
. Let
d_{\lambda}
denote the graph distance on
C_{\infty}(\lambda)
. The constant
\mu(\lambda)
is defined as the almost-sure limit

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{d_{\lambda}(\mathring{0}(\lambda),\mathring{n}(\lambda))}{n}=\mu(\lambda)

where we write
0=(0,0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
n=(n,0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This limit is shown to exist by a subadditive ergodic theorem argument in
[
24
]
and so in fact we also have
\mu(\lambda)\leq\mathbb{E}d_{\lambda}(\mathring{0}(\lambda),\mathring{1}(\lambda))
. Note that if we take
\lambda\to\infty
we almost surely have
d_{\lambda}(\mathring{0}(\lambda),\mathring{1}(\lambda))\to 1.
By the dominated convergence theorem we see that
\mathbb{E}d_{\lambda}(\mathring{0}(\lambda),\mathring{1}(\lambda))\to 1
completing the proof.
∎

We also need a non-trivial property of the time constant, namely that in each dimension
d
the function
\lambda\mapsto\mu(\lambda)
is continuous. Recently, the first two authors proved Lipschitz continuity, and we refer to
[
9
]
and the references therein for more context:

Theorem 12

(Theorem 1, Dubin and Gorski
[
9
]
)
.

For each
d\geq 2
and
\lambda_{0}>\lambda_{c}(d)
, there exists
C(\lambda_{0},d)
such that

|\mu({\lambda})-\mu({\lambda^{\prime}})|\leq C(\lambda_{0})|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|

for each
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in[\lambda_{0},\infty)
.

2.3.
Uniqueness of the giant component

For a random geometric graph
G
we will write
\mathring{G}
for the component of largest diameter in the underlying metric.
2
2

2

In the regimes we consider,
this will with high probability coincide
with the component of largest diameter in the
graph
metric; for the most important case, see
15
.
Throughout, we will make use of the fact that there is only one component of large diameter. We start with a version of this statement in Euclidean space for possibly distorted intensity measure and metric:

Lemma 13
.

Fix
\lambda_{0}>\lambda_{c}(d)
and let
\phi_{s}\leq s
be an increasing
function of
s
.
Then there exist
c(d,\lambda_{0})>0,C=C(d)>0

such that the following holds.

Let
\sigma
be a metric on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

which satisfies

\sigma(x,y)\leq\|x-y\|\leq 2\sigma(x,y)

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let

\nu
be a measure on

\mathbb{R}^{d}
which satisfies

\frac{d\nu}{d\mathcal{L}}\geq\lambda_{0}
.
Let
G_{s}
be the random geometric
graph
whose vertices are the
points of a Poisson process on
[-s,s]^{d}

with intensity measure
\nu

and whose edge set consists
of pairs of vertices with

\sigma
-distance at most 1.

Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{Exactly one component of }G_{s}\\
\mbox{ has Euclidean diameter at least }\phi_{s}\end{array}\right)\geq 1-Cs^{d}\exp(-c\phi_{s}).

This is a slightly adapted version of
[
20
, Prop. 10.13]
. In
[
20
]
, there is no distortion and so we indicate how to adapt the proof of
[
20
, Prop. 10.13]
to our setting in
Appendix

A
.

From here we will deduce that in our setting the giant component is unique with high probability.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\lambda_{0}>\lambda_{c}
. Then there are constants
C=C(\lambda_{0},M)>0,c=c(\lambda_{0},M)>0
so that for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
and
r\phi\leq c
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{Exactly one component of }G_{M}^{n}(r)\\
\mbox{ has }d_{M}\mbox{-diameter at least }r\phi\end{array}\right)\geq 1-Cr^{-d}\exp(-c\phi)\,.

Proof.

Let
\eta>0
be small enough so that
\lambda_{0}e^{-\eta}>\lambda_{c}.
For each
z\in M
let
\delta_{z}\in(0,1)
and
u_{z}
be guaranteed from
8
; we may further reduce
\delta_{z}
so that
[-\delta_{z},\delta_{z}]^{d}
lies in the image of
u_{z}
. Define
V_{z}=u_{z}^{-1}((-\delta_{z},\delta_{z})^{d})
and note that
\{V_{z}\}_{z\in M}
forms an open cover of
M
. By compactness of
M
there is a finite set
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\}
so that
\mathcal{U}=\{V_{z_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{k}
is an open cover of
M
.

Define the graph
H
with vertex set
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
with edges defined by
E(H)=\{\{i,j\}:V_{z_{i}}\cap V_{z_{j}}\neq\emptyset\}.
Note that since
M
is connected, the graph
H
is connected too. For each pair
\{i,j\}\in E(H)
we may find a point
w_{i,j}\in V_{z_{i}}\cap V_{z_{j}}
and
\varepsilon_{i,j}
so that
B_{M}(w_{i,j},\varepsilon_{i,j})\subset V_{z_{i}}\cap V_{z_{j}}.
By potentially further shrinking
\varepsilon_{i,j}
we may define
U_{i,j}=u_{z_{i}}^{-1}(u_{z_{i}}(w_{i,j})+[-\varepsilon_{i,j},\varepsilon_{i,j}]^{d})
. Let
G_{i}
and
G_{i,j}
denote the induced subgraph of
G_{M}^{n}(r)
whose vertices lie in
V_{z_{i}}
and
U_{i,j}
respectively. Let
\mathcal{E}_{i}
denote the event that
G_{i}
contains exactly one component of
d_{M}
-diameter at least
r\phi
; define
\mathcal{E}_{i,j}
analogously. Note that by
13
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{j\in[k]}\mathcal{E}_{j}\cap\bigcap_{\{i,j\}\in E(H)}\mathcal{E}_{i,j}\right)\geq 1-Cr^{-d}\exp(-c\phi)

for some
C,c>0
depending on
\eta,\lambda_{0},M
. We will intersect with the event
\mathcal{E}=\bigcap_{j\in[k]}\mathcal{E}_{j}\cap\bigcap_{\{i,j\}\in E(H)}\mathcal{E}_{i,j}
and let
\mathring{G}_{i}
and
\mathring{G}_{i,j}
denote the component of largest diameter.

Note that for each
\{i,j\}\in E(H)
we have that
U_{i,j}\subset V_{z_{i}}\cap V_{z_{j}}
and so
\mathring{G}_{i,j}\subset\mathring{G}_{i}\cap\mathring{G}_{j}
. In particular, for each edge
\{i,j\}\in E(H)
, the largest components in
V_{z_{i}}
and
V_{z_{j}}
are in fact the same component of
G_{M}^{n}(r)
. Since
H
is connected, this shows that the components
\mathring{G}_{i}
all lie in the same component of
G_{M}^{n}(r)
which we may call
\kappa
.

To see that
\kappa
is the only component of large diameter, let
\alpha>0
be the Lebesgue covering number of
\mathcal{U}=\{V_{z_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{k}
meaning that if
d_{M}(x,y)<\alpha
then there is some
i\in[k]
so that
x,y\in V_{z_{i}}
. Note that if there is a component
\kappa^{\prime}
of
G_{M}^{n}(r)
of
d_{M}
-diameter
\geq r\phi
, then there are points
x,y\in\kappa^{\prime}
so that
d_{M}(x,y)\in[r\phi,r(\phi+1)]
. By restricting
c
, we have that
r(\phi+1)\leq\alpha/2
and so there is some
i\in[k]
so that
x,y\in V_{z_{i}}
. We note that either
x,y
are connected in
G_{i}
or otherwise the path from
x,y
exits
V_{z_{i}}
which would require having two points of distance,
\alpha/2
apart. On event
\mathcal{E}
, this implies that
\kappa^{\prime}\cap G_{i}
has metric diameter at least
r\phi
implying that
\kappa^{\prime}\subset\mathring{G}_{i}
. This shows that
\kappa^{\prime}\subset\kappa
, completing the proof.
∎

Lastly, we note that, with high probability,
\mathring{G}^{n}_{M}(r)
is also
the component of largest diameter
in the graph metric
.
Thus, in our statements of
1
and
2
,
we may interpret the phrase “component of largest diameter”
as referring to either

or the graph metric, which
will be important for algorithmic applications.

Corollary 15
.

Let
\lambda_{0}>\lambda_{c}
. There exist constants
C=C(\lambda_{0},M),c=c(\lambda,M)>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathring{G}_{M}^{n}(r)\mbox{ is the largest component}\\
\mbox{ of }G_{M}^{n}(r)\mbox{ in graph diameter}\end{array}\right)\geq 1-C\exp(-c\log^{2}n)

Proof.

First, if
r\geq n^{-1/d+\beta}
for some
\beta>0
then

shows the graph is connected. Otherwise, applying

twice shows that there is one component of
d_{M}
diameter at least

,
and all other components have

-diameter larger at most

. Each component of
d_{M}
-diameter at most

has graph diameter at most
(\log n)^{O_{d}(1)}
, while
the giant component has graph diameter
\Omega(1/r)
.
∎

3.
Squeezing between homogeneous processes in Euclidean space

For each point
x\in M
, we will use
8
to identify a small

-ball that can be identified with a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
up to small measure and metric distortion. This will show that when we pushforward the measure
\lambda\cdot dM
into
\mathbb{R}^{d}
on this patch, we may trap this measure between
\underline{\lambda}d\mathcal{L}
and
\overline{\lambda}d\mathcal{L}
for
\underline{\lambda}
and
\overline{\lambda}
close to
\lambda
. For technical reasons, we will want that among all
\lambda\in[\lambda_{0},\infty)
and
x\in M
we have only a finite collection of choices for
\underline{\lambda}
and
\overline{\lambda}
. We begin by specifying these points and then define homogeneous random graphs in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose vertex sets are Poisson with these intensities.

3.1.
Picking upper and lower intensities

Throughout this subsection we will fix
\varepsilon>0
and
\lambda_{0}>\lambda_{c}
.
Note that by
11
, there is some
\lambda_{+}
so that

(3)

\mu(\lambda_{+})\leq e^{\varepsilon/10}\,.

For
\eta>0
to be chosen later as a function of
\varepsilon,\lambda_{0}
, define
\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{0}e^{(d+1)\eta k}
and define
K=\min\{k:\lambda_{k}\geq\lambda_{+}\}
. By taking
\eta
small enough, we may assume that
\lambda_{-1}>\lambda_{c}
. Define the (multiplicative) net
\mathcal{N}=\{\lambda_{k}:k\in\{-1,0,\ldots,K+2\}\}
. Now, for
\lambda\in[\lambda_{0},\lambda_{+}e^{(d+1)\eta}]
define

\underline{\lambda}=\underline{\lambda}(\lambda)=\max\{\lambda_{k}\in\mathcal{N}:\lambda_{k}\leq e^{-(d+1)\eta}\lambda\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\overline{\lambda}=\overline{\lambda}(\lambda)=\min\{\lambda_{k}\in\mathcal{N}:\lambda_{k}\geq e^{(d+1)\eta}\lambda\}\,.

We will require two main features from these choices of
\underline{\lambda}
and
\overline{\lambda}
; first, we have

(4)

\underline{\lambda}e^{{(d+1)\eta}}\leq\lambda\leq\overline{\lambda}e^{(d+1)\eta}

by construction. We also have
\overline{\lambda}e^{-2(d+1)\eta}\leq\lambda\leq\underline{\lambda}e^{2(d+1)\eta}
. Thus, we may use the uniform continuity of
\lambda\mapsto\mu(\lambda)
on the compact interval
[\lambda_{-1},\lambda_{K+2}]\subset(\lambda_{c},\infty)
guaranteed by
12
so that if we take
\eta
