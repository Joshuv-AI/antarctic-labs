Rapid and Scalable
Synthesis, Structure, and Physical
Properties of Solid-Solution Layered Titanium Niobium Carbosulfide
“Ti
x
Nb2–x
SC”

Ahmad Majed, Asaf Pesach, Anna Eyal, Amit Keren, Mina Saleh, Kaitlyn Prenger, El’ad N. Caspi, Maxim Avdeev, Michael Naguib

Abstract

We demonstrate rapid,
scalable synthesis of solid-solution
layered
transition metal carbosulfides, Ti
x
Nb2–x
SC (0 ≤ x ≤ 2). Thermogravimetric analysis confirmed superior thermal
stability of Ti2SC vs Nb2SC; oxidation of Nb2SC in air (750 °C) produced an accordion-like sulfur-bearing
niobium oxide. Room-temperature X-ray diffraction and temperature-dependent
neutron diffraction revealed linear contraction of lattice parameters
with increasing Ti content. Strong anisotropic thermal expansion behavior
arises from metallic basal planes vs nonmetallic M–S bonds,
with Nb2SC showing larger volumetric expansion than Ti2SC. Specific heat measurments confirm that Ti2SC
is stiffer than Nb2SC. In both thermal expansion and specific
heat, nonmonotonic behaviors are linked to the critical role of Ti/Nb
mixing. SQUID measurements show diamagnetic shielding in high-Nb samples,
which, lacking an anomaly in specific heat, is ascribed to trace,
percolating superconducting impurities rather than bulk superconductivity.

Introduction

Layered ternary transition
metal carbides, initially known as Hagg
phases, were first reported in 1960 by Rohde and Kudielka, followed by the discovery of a substantial number
of new materials belonging to this phase by Hans Nowotny and co-workers.
−

 In 1998, Barsoum and El-Raghy presented their research on Ti3SiC2. Barsoum later
introduced the MAX phases in 2000, describing them as hexagonal layered
ternary transition metal carbides and nitrides with space group D
6h

4–P63
/mmc (a). These compounds have the chemical formula M
n+1AX
n

transition metal, A represents elements from group IIIA (Al, Ga, In),
group IVA (Si, Ge, Sn), group VA (P, As), and group VIA (S), and X
denotes C and/or N. Through selective
etching of the A layer from MAX phases, M
n+1X

T
x

x

Layered carbosulfide
MAX phases have sulfur as
the A element (group
VIA), which has a higher electronegativity compared to elements from
group IIIA (Al, Ga, In) commonly found in other MAX phases. The c-lattice constant of Nb2SC is significantly
lower than that of Nb2AlC, Nb2GaC, and Nb2InC, indicating a stronger bond between Nb and S. This stronger M–A bond explains why MXenes
have not been synthesized from carbosulfide MAX phases thus far. However,
metastable Nb2S2C has been reported, with weak
out-of-plane S–S bonds in its atomic stacking of S–Nb–C–Nb–S.
−

 Nb2S2C demonstrates potential exfoliability
with a predicted cleavage energy of 0.38 J·m–2. Its synthesis involved selective etching
of Cu from Cu0.67Nb2S2C, which was
obtained by heating Nb2SC+S+Cu.

Layered transition metal carbosulfides offer intriguing applications.
Notably, superconducting behavior was reported for Nb2SC0.9 at T

Nb2S2C at T

≈60 W/m K, surpassing most of the reported MAX phases, while

revealed that increasing the Nb content in Ti
x

Young’s modulus and Debye temperature.

Historically, the low boiling point of chalcogens relative
to the
formation temperatures of transition metal carbo-chalcogenides has
necessitated a closed heating environment, like vacuum-sealed quartz
tubes. This has limited the scalability (<1 g), heating temperature
(<1200 °C), and required extended heating durations (>6
days). However, in 2015, Ti2SC was successfully
synthesized using iron sulfide instead of elemental sulfur, followed
by acid treatment to remove the iron byproduct. The higher melting point of transition metal sulfides allowed
for (1) heating in an ambient-pressure inert environment, (2) higher-temperature
heating (up to 1700 °C), (3) significantly shorter required heating
durations, and (4) substantial scaling of the reaction. Previous reports
on carbo-chalcogenides include Nb2SC,

M2AX transition metal carbo-chalcogenides, but iron sulfide

Nb2S2C has been synthesized using Nb2SC, suggesting the possibility of other M2AX phases producing
M2A2X. To the best of our knowledge, scalable
synthesis of Ti

Nb2–x

a comprehensive study of the temperature-dependent structural and
physical properties, as well as the stability of the Ti

Nb2–x

Synthesis
and Characterization

Solid-State Synthesis

The precursors
for this study were mixed in various molar ratios listed in Table S1 to generate different stoichiometries
of Ti
x
Nb2–x
SC. Titanium (Ti, 99.5%, <45 μm), titanium carbide
(TiC, 99.5%, ∼2 μm), niobium (Nb, 99.8%, <45 μm),
and niobium carbide (NbC, 99%, <10 μm) were purchased from
Alfa Aesar and used as received. Iron­(II) sulfide (FeS, 99%, 60–200
μm) was purchased from Strem. The chemicals were combined in
a high-density polyethylene jar inside an argon-filled glovebox, sealed
with parafilm, and then mixed in a Turbula T2F mixer at 56 rpm for
3 h. The resulting powders were transferred in the glovebox and pressed
into 1-in. diameter pellets (up to 50 g each), placed in an alumina
crucible, and heated from room temperature to 1550 °C at a ramp
of 10 °C/min in an alumina tube furnace operated within a chemical
fume hood for safety. The temperature was held for 2 h before allowing
the samples to cool to room temperature. The heating, holding, and
cooling steps were carried out under a continuous flow of argon gas.

Iron Removal

To eliminate iron byproduct
after synthesis from the samples, the sintered pellets were ground
to particles smaller than 44 μm and added to aqueous hydrochloric
acid (12 M HCl) in a ratio of 1 g of powder per 20 mL of HCl solution.
The solution was stirred using a Teflon-coated magnetic stir bar at
50 °C for 24 h. The sample was subsequently washed with deionized
(DI) water to remove impurities and neutralize the solution to pH
∼ 7. The solution was vacuum-filtered overnight using a Whatman
filter paper and a vacuum pump. Residual moisture was removed by heating
it at 110 °C under vacuum for 4 h.

Measurements

The crystal structures
of the investigated samples were analyzed using a Rigaku D/Max-2200
X-ray diffractometer with a Cu Kα source. Scans were performed
with a 2θ step size of 0.02° and a sweep rate of 1°/min.
Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images were obtained using a Hitachi
S-4800 microscope at an accelerating voltage of 3 kV. Chemical compositions
were confirmed through energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS)
analysis with an INCA-Hitachi S-3400 microscope at an accelerating
voltage of 30 kV. These measurements were done at Tulane University,
USA.

Low intensity neutron powder diffraction (NPD) measurements

High-resolution NPD measurements on Nb2SC were conducted

High-intensity NPD measurements on Nb2SC were conducted

Structural Properties at
Elevated Temperatures

The thermal stability of Ti
x
Nb2–x
SC in air
was investigated using
TGA, as shown in a. Since the reaction path occurred over a range of temperatures,
the temperature values in this study were recorded in reference to
the midpoints of their reactions. Nb2SC and Ti0.5Nb1.5SC witnessed mass gains of 15.2% and 14.6% at ∼475
°C and ∼480 °C, respectively. On the other hand,
Ti1.5Nb0.5SC and Ti2SC exhibited
higher oxidation temperatures of ∼657 °C and ∼650
°C with mass gains of 10.9% and 10.6%, respectively. TiNbSC showed
an intermediate reaction temperature at ∼570 °C with a
mass gain of 14.3%. To understand the resulting structure after heating
in air, Ti
x
Nb2–x
SC samples were heated in air to 750 °C for 30 min. XRD
patterns were collected and plotted in b. The crystalline oxidation products are
transition metal oxides: anatase TiO2 for Ti2SC, Nb2O5 for Nb2SC, and a mixture
of TiNb2O7, anatase TiO2, and Nb2O5 for the solid solutions Ti
x
Nb2–x
SC (x = 0.5–1.5), with no sulfur-containing crystalline phases
detected by XRD. It is therefore reasonable to assume that most of
the S and C are released as SO2 and CO2, respectively.
However, as evidenced by the localized EDS mapping (see below), the
rapid oxidation kinetics of the physical system permit the partial
retention of sulfur within the resultant oxide matrices. Interestingly,
the color of Nb2SC powder after oxidation was orange, as
shown in the inset of a. Given that Nb2O5 is white in color,
this suggests sulfur might be entrapped in the oxidized material.
Sulfur-doped transition metal oxides can potentially be used in interesting
applications, such as enhanced electrocatalytic activity. EDS analysis revealed no sulfur for all samples
at low magnification. However, at high magnification, sulfur was detected
in the Nb2SC sample, with a Nb:S atomic ratio of 2.00:0.16.
While oxygen vacancies in pure niobium oxides typically induce gray,
blue, or brown color centers,
,
 the oxidized Nb2SC powder exhibited a distinct orange hue. This macroscopic
optical shift, coupled with the localized EDS signal, supports the
retention of sulfur within the oxidized matrix. Because EDS cannot
differentiate between atomic sulfur doping within the Nb2O5 lattice and the entrapment of unreacted nanoscale sulfide/sulfate
phases, further high-resolution chemical analysis (e.g., X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy) is required to determine the precise chemical coordination
of the retained sulfur. The SEM images of the oxidized samples in c depict an interesting
accordion-like layered morphology for Nb2SC and TiNbSC
samples. Ti/Nb oxides with layered morphology were previously reported
for energy storage applications with a relatively longer procedure
beginning with the synthesis of the TiNbAlC MAX phase, followed by
HF-etching into MXene, and finally controlled oxidation. This oxidation pathway may provide a simple
route to sulfur-modified layered mixed oxides directly from carbosulfide
MAX precursors.

Physical and Structural Properties at Low
Temperatures

The temperature-dependent ZFC-FC magnetic susceptibility
of Ti
x
Nb2–x
SC (x = 0, 0.5, 1, 2) shows a general increase
upon cooling, with various anomalies indicative of magnetic or electronic
transitions (). Coalescence of the ZFC and FC curves for x =
0, 0.5, and 1 in the temperature range of 180–200 K suggests
the existence of ferromagnetic interactions in these samples below
room temperature. For x = 2, this probably happens
only above the highest measurement point of ∼400 K. The ZFC
curves of x = 0 and 0.5 show a sharp decrease at
temperatures of 3.6 and 2.9 K, respectively, reminiscent of superconductivity
(, inset).
However, no absolute negative susceptibility is observed. Crucially,
our low-temperature specific heat measurements (see below) reveal
no corresponding lambda anomaly at these temperatures. The absence
of a bulk thermodynamic phase transition confirms that the main MAX
phase is not a bulk superconductor. Instead, this partial diamagnetic
shielding likely arises from trace, interconnected superconducting
impurities (such as the Nb2S2C phase detected
in our NPD analysis) whose actual volume fraction is too small to
be detected in specific heat.

From the totality of
the bulk magnetic and room-temperature
diffraction
results, it appears that although the existence of iron was not observed
in the EDS measurements, small amounts of ferromagnetic-containing
impurities remain in all samples. This is further supported by the
field-dependent magnetization measurements (c.f. Figure S3), where all samples at all temperatures show soft
ferromagnetic behavior. Due to its ferromagnetic
characteristics even at elevated temperatures (T
c = 1043 K), a minute amount can be responsible for a large
magnetic signal. In this sense, the magnetization measurements serve
as a very sensitive probe for the ability to wash off the iron used
for the samples’ synthesis, as well as its byproducts.

The thermal expansion results support
the strong anisotropy
of
the Ti
x
Nb2–x
SC system. As mentioned above, the expansion in the basal plane
is governed by the electronic pressure of the metallic layer. In contrast,
the thermal expansion across the layers through the nonmetallic Ti–S
bonds can be excellently explained with no electronic term at all.
Moreover, these results can be explained mostly by Einstein’s
optical modes. The nature of the lattice vibrations along the nonmetallic
gap varies dramatically with mass. In pure Nb2SC, the extreme
mass contrast between the heavy Nb2C block and the light
S atoms creates a large acoustic impedance mismatch. Long-range acoustic
waves cannot efficiently couple across this gap, forcing the S atoms
to vibrate independently in localized optical (Einstein’s)
modes. In pure Ti2SC, substituting
the lighter Ti atoms reduces this acoustic impedance mismatch, allowing
a small fraction of low-frequency acoustic phonons to couple across
the gap [yielding w
D = 0.17(5); ]. However, in the
TiNbSC solid solution, the mass-variance and static strain fields
within the alloyed M-layer reintroduce intense point-defect scattering
of phonons. This scattering destroys
the acoustic wave translation seen in Ti2SC, causing the
phonons to localize into flat-band vibrations. As a result, the TiNbSC c-axis expansion
collapses back entirely to localized rattling modes, and can be modeled
using w

D
 = 0.

The
physical behavior of thermal expansion can help understand
the anisotropic relative thermal strain observed in the studied samples
(). Cooling
from room temperature (296 K) down to 2 K, opposite trends are observed
when comparing the three phases. Nb2SC contracts ∼50%
more than Ti2SC and TiNbSC along the hexagonal plane, while
out of the hexagonal plane it is the equiatomic solid-solution (x = 1) that contracts ∼35% more than both end compounds
(x = 0, 2). These opposite trends resulted in a total
volume contraction of ∼30% more for Nb2SC compared
to Ti2SC and TiNbSC. Interestingly, and probably counterintuitive,
TiNbSC’s volumetric contraction as a function of temperature
is the smallest of the three that were studied by temperature-dependent
NPD. These macroscopic observations could be explained by the microscopic
thermodynamic decoupling modeled in this work. First, the bulk thermodynamic
dilation of an anisotropic crystal is governed by the trace of its
thermal expansion tensor. For a hexagonal
lattice, this geometric trace yields a total volumetric expansion
that is mathematically dominated by the basal plane, since β­(T)
 = 2αa(T) + αc(T), where β­(T) is the volumetric thermal expansion and αa (αc) is the a (c) axis thermal expansion. Therefore, the a-axis
behavior primarily dictates the bulk volume. Second, it is hypothesized
that the ∼50% a-axis overcontraction in Nb2SC occurs because the thermal expansion/contraction in this
direction is driven by both electronic and vibrational inflation/deflation
( and ). In contrast, the
TiNbSC basal plane is governed by positive chemical pressure, which
elastically hardens the M2C block. Combined with the complete
structural scattering of acoustic phonons, discussed in the previous
paragraph, TiNbSC shrinks solely via the loss of 2D electronic pressure.
Because the mathematically dominant a-axis is relatively
anchored in TiNbSC, its total volumetric contraction is heavily restricted.
Conversely, the anomalous ∼35% overcontraction of the TiNbSC c-axis highlights severe solid-solution frustration. As
mentioned above, expansion across this nonmetallic gap is driven entirely
by S-layer rattling. The mismatched Ti and Nb atomic radii probably
create large static strain fields, forcing the compliant S atoms into
highly asymmetric, anharmonic regions of their potential wells. High anharmonicity directly yields a massive
localized Grüneisen parameter. When this highly anharmonic system is cooled to 2 K, the thermal
rattling pressure vanishes rapidly, causing the nonmetallic gap to
collapse drastically compared to the symmetric pure phases. However,
since this c-axis collapse contributes to the volume
linearly, it is mathematically overpowered by the rigid a-axis, with good agreement with TiNbSC being the least contractive
material overall.

The refined parameters () demonstrate significant
deviation from the Kopp-Neumann
rule. This rule predicts monotonous evolution of θD for the solid solutions between the two end compounds, following
the inverse square root of the average mass. We propose that this
deviation is driven by a synergistic mechanism involving both localized
static strain and alterations to the valence electron concentration
(VEC). Ti (Group IV) and Nb (Group V) differ significantly in both
atomic radius and valence electron count. In the M-X rocksalt blocks
of MAX phases, altering the transition metal VEC fundamentally modifies
the macroscopic elasticity. It is established that increasing the
VEC in transition metal carbides populates the d-t2g orbitals,
which overlap upon shear and degrade the shear modulus (C44), leading to macroscopic lattice softening.

On the Nb-rich side, in Ti0.5Nb1.5SC,
the
substitution of smaller Ti atoms into the larger Nb matrix induces
positive internal chemical pressure. Concurrently, the substitution
of a Group 4 element into a Group 5 matrix decreases the overall VEC,
thereby depleting the destabilizing d-t2g orbitals and
enhancing the shear resistance of the M–C bonds. The combination
of local steric contraction and electronic bond stiffening steepens
the interatomic potential well, substantially increasing the effective
macroscopic spring constant (k). Coupled with the
reduction in average atomic mass, these synergistic structural and
electronic effects provide a physical interpretation for the pronounced
elevation of θD to 445 K.

Conversely, on the
Ti-rich side, in Ti1.5Nb0.5SC, the substitution
of larger Nb atoms into the tightly packed Ti
matrix induces significant localized static expansion. Simultaneously,
the introduction of the Group 5 element increases the overall VEC,
populating the d-t2g orbitals and electronically degrading
the shear modulus. In the framework of anharmonic lattice dynamics,
the localized static expansion forces the interatomic bonds outward
into the shallower regions of their asymmetric potential wells. This
static displacement degrades the effective macroscopic spring constant. When it operates in tandem with the electronic
softening induced by the altered d-band filling, this suggested mechanism
accounts for the pronounced, anomalous softening of the lattice (dropping
θD to 386 K), completely overriding the Kopp-Neumann
mass-averaging rule.

When comparing the Debye and Einstein temperatures
extracted from
the bulk specific heat to those derived from the directional thermal
expansion ( and ), it cannot be assumed that the c
p results are a simple spatial average of the
directional fits. This discrepancy arises from the fundamentally different
ways these two macroscopic properties sample the microscopic phonon
density of states across the Brillouin zone. Specific heat is a purely
scalar thermodynamic quantity. It integrates the internal energy of
all vibrational modes purely based on their frequency distribution,
regardless of their structural anharmonicity. In contrast, directional
thermal expansion acts as a highly selective physical filter. It isolates only the subset of phonon modes
that possess a nonzero, direction-specific mode-Gruneisen parameter.
For example, an in-plane vibrational mode may possess high energy
(significantly contributing to the bulk c
p and raising the bulk θD) but possesses a near-zero
mode-Gruneisen parameter (contributing nothing to the a-axis thermal expansion, as seen above for TiNbSC). Consequently,
the directional thermal expansion fits map structurally active anharmonicity,
while the c
p fits map the total harmonic
energy of the lattice, ruling out a simple 2:1 mathematical averaging
between the axes.

Since specific heat is an extensive thermodynamic
property, the
measured c
p(T) profiles
represent a mass-weighted sum of the main phase and the secondary
impurity phases. Notably, Nb2SC contains the largest impurity
fraction among the studied samples (∼10 wt % combined impurities,
primarily Fe2Nb and NbC). Consequently, it was analyzed
as a limiting case to estimate the maximum deviation in specific heat
induced by secondary phases. The extracted parameters for the Nb2SC composite [θD = 341.2(4) K and γ
= 12.56(3) mJ/(mol K2)] represent effective macroscopic
values. To establish the thermodynamic bounds of the pristine phase,
the specific heat parameters of the primary impurities were utilized.
The literature values for NbC0.93 are θD = 496(10) K and γ = 3.2 mJ/(mol K2). Furthermore, recent thermodynamic modeling of
the C14 Laves phase Fe2Nb establishes its parameters as
θD = 532 K and γ = 19.8 mJ/(mol K2). Applying a mass-weighted Kopp-Neumann
correction to the low-temperature regime demonstrates that subtracting
these structurally stiffer impurity contributions further lowers the
calculated intrinsic Debye temperature of the pristine Nb2SC phase to ∼333 K, while the Sommerfeld coefficient is marginally
adjusted to ∼12.7 mJ/(mol K2). Therefore, the presence
of impurities does not artificially induce the observed lattice softening.
Rather, the low θD driven by the anharmonicity of
the nonmetallic S-layers is robust against impurity-induced inflation.
Nevertheless, further thermodynamic investigations on higher-purity
samples are required.

Conclusion

Nb2SC, Ti2SC, and their solid solutions Ti
x
Nb2–x
SC
(x = 0.5, 1, 1.5) were successfully synthesized by
a one-step reaction under Ar using FeS as an effective sulfur source,
enabling the rapid and scalable synthesis of S-based MAX phases. Although
most of the Fe was removed through HCl treatment, magnetization measurements,
supported by X-ray and neutron diffraction, revealed trace Fe-based
impurities that remain detectable.

TGA showed that Nb-rich phases,
i.e., Nb2SC and Ti0.5Nb1.5SC, oxidize
near ∼480 °C, whereas
Ti-rich phases, i.e., TiNbSC, Ti1.5Nb0.5SC,
and Ti2SC, exhibit substantially higher stability. Oxidation
of Nb2SC at 750 °C in air produced a sulfur-bearing
niobium oxide with a layered morphology, suggesting a simple pathway
to sulfur-modified layered mixed oxides directly from carbosulfide
MAX precursors.

Low-temperature magnetic measurements of Nb2SC and Ti0.5Nb1.5SC revealed a sharp
ZFC downturn, which
hints at superconductivity. However, an absolute negative magnetic
response was never observed down to 2 K, and the lack of a corresponding
λ anomaly in the specific heat data confirms the absence of
bulk superconductivity. This partial diamagnetic shielding is attributed
to trace, interconnected superconducting impurities (such as Nb2S2C) whose bulk thermodynamic signal is negligible,
together with the masking effects of the highly magnetic Fe-based
impurities. It is important to keep in mind that using FeS as an agent
for rapid, large-scale S-based MAX phase manufacturing may affect
properties sensitive to ferromagnetic impurities.

Substituting
Nb atoms with Ti led to a linear decrease in both
lattice parameters, with slightly stronger contraction along the c axis (∼3.1%) than the a axis (∼2.8%).
This anisotropy in lattice dimensions decreases between Nb2SC and Ti2SC corroborating the strong anisotropy observed
in the thermal expansion. The latter is explained in the framework
of the difference between the primarily metallic in-plane bonds and
nonmetallic out-of-plane ones. In the solid solutions, when mixing
effects are involved as well, a much more complex behavior is observed,
involving significant phonon scattering and high anharmonicity that
act as competing mechanisms in the directional thermal expansion,
resulting in TiNbSC having smaller volumetric thermal expansion compared
to the end members.

Specific heat measurements as a function
of temperature indicate
that bulk Ti2SC is stiffer than bulk Nb2SC.
The specific heat curves of the solid solutions lie between those
of the end members but do not follow the Kopp-Neumann rule owing
to substantial localized strain fields introduced by Ti/Nb mixing.
For the Ti-poor solid solutions, this effect further adds to the stiffness
associated with the reduced average atomic mass upon substitution
of Nb with Ti. In contrast, for the Ti-rich solid solutions, it substantially
suppresses the mass-driven increase in the stiffness.

Furthermore,
the ability to tune the valence electron concentration
in the Ti
x
Nb2–x
SC system holds significant promise for their 2D derivatives.
When MAX phases are exfoliated into 2D MXenes, the ’A’
element is etched away, leaving transition metal carbide layers with
highly electronegative surface terminations. As highlighted by previous
studies on 2D transition metal compounds with strongly electronegative
surfaces, such architectures possess
diverse charge-donating and accepting abilities that are highly advantageous
for charge transfer, adsorption, catalysis, and energy storage applications.
The capacity to fine-tune the Ti/Nb ratio, and consequently the d-band
filling, provides a fundamental lever to optimize the core electronic
properties of these future 2D MXenes for targeted nanoscale applications.

Overall, layered carbosulfide MAX phases exhibit pronounced directional
anisotropy arising from distinct and sometimes competing physical
mechanisms. Understanding these mechanisms is the key to fine-tuning
macroscopic properties of interest in solid solutions of this family
of compounds.
