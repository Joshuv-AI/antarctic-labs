Executive Summary
Current cybersecurity threats outpace the development rate of defense capabilities. Rapid
advancement in artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled cyber operations—including the use of
autonomous agents to plan, build, and execute large-scale cyber espionage campaigns—exposes
vulnerabilities in U.S. critical infrastructure where existing security tools designed for
human‑directed attacks cannot yet match the speed, adaptability, or autonomy of AI‑enabled
adversaries. 1 As the energy sector continues to modernize and integrate emerging wireless
technologies across systems, this capability/response gap opens a new threat vector for advanced
adversaries. However, it also represents a new opportunity for proactive integration of AI-
assisted workflows and operations. This report offers an approach to countering machine‑speed
adversarial operations by bolstering AI-supported workflows within cybersecurity research and
development to accelerate the delivery of resilient, intelligent network defenses for critical
energy systems.

The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) is advancing this next-generation approach to
securing critical energy infrastructure in alignment with The White House Executive Order
14363: Launching the Genesis Mission and America’s AI Action Plan. In the face of advanced
AI cyber threats, NLR is using AI tools to streamline and accelerate work on the development of
an AI‑native, autonomous defensive architecture within fifth-generation (5G)/next-generation
(NextG)‑enabled critical energy systems that can detect, contain, and learn from emerging threats
in real time.

At the core of the proposed architecture is an artificial intelligence radio access network (AI-
RAN)-enabled dynamic honeypot system that detects, isolates, diverts, and contains
sophisticated attacks in real time. Operating across all RAN timescales—from
sub-10-millisecond radio-layer reactions to strategic, multi-second policy decisions—the system
systematically migrates threats from production environments to a controlled “caged zone” for
safe analysis. This approach seeks to bring unprecedented automation, speed, and reliability to
NextG-based grid protection and directly addresses the challenges of AI-orchestrated cyber
operations. 2

To build this complex and adaptive architecture, the Broadband Automation for Distributed Grid
Efficiency and Resilience (BADGER) project adopts AI-supported development workflows to
determine how automation impacts productivity, quality, and risk. Traditional workflows
executed without AI assistance, though familiar, are constrained by manual analysis and
developer cognitive load. In the category of AI integrated development environment workflows,
GitHub Copilot provides coding acceleration but lacks broader architectural context, whereas
Model Context Protocol (MCP)-assisted workflows enable a fundamentally different approach:

an AI “orchestrator” that is capable of reasoning across the entire codebase, specifications, logs,

potential for reducing development time, lowering defect rates, and enabling continuous,

The BADGER project will evaluate the efficacy of development workflows and build upon
metrics to generate an actionable phased path for advancing AI-orchestrated processes. Early
phases will leverage mature tools, like GitHub Copilot, to achieve immediate productivity gains,
followed by proof-of-concept efforts to integrate richer, project-specific context using MCP

Through this staged approach, the BADGER project team will develop and refine AI workflows

purpose AI models with software mechanisms to enable autonomous action or manipulation of a
closed system to achieve a desired effect. 3 This dual-layered adoption of AI-supported security
directly supports U.S. national priorities with the potential to strengthen the security and
resilience of the energy systems that power the nation.

1 Introduction
The Broadband Automation for Distributed Grid Efficiency and Resilience (BADGER) project
aligns with national strategic priorities for integrating emerging wireless technologies into
critical infrastructure and advancing artificial intelligence (AI)‑driven security. As energy
infrastructure modernizes with increasingly software‑defined and interconnected systems, the
ability to leverage fifth-generation (5G)/next-generation (NextG) wireless technology networks
and AI‑driven cybersecurity countermeasures becomes essential for defending critical
infrastructure against machine-speed cyberattacks, such as GTG-1002 (U.S. House Committee
on Homeland Security 2025). This report outlines work at the National Laboratory of the
Rockies (NLR) to develop a 5G/NextG‑native security architecture powered by AI-integrated
systems, methods, and workflows that enable efficient and reliable architectures and
development. Together, these efforts position the laboratory to accelerate innovation while
directly supporting national security and energy system resilience objectives.

1.1 Strategic Context: The Genesis Mission and America’s Artificial
 Intelligence Action Plan
Federal policy and planning around AI adoption and strategic development impact technological
integration across U.S. critical infrastructure. The White House Executive Order 14363:
Launching the Genesis Mission and America’s AI Action Plan encourage rapid innovation in AI,
telecommunications, and grid resilience technologies (The White House 2025a; The White
House 2025b), The BADGER project responds to these priorities by exploring how 5G/NextG
networks, open radio access network (O‑RAN) intelligence, and AI‑enabled automation can
enhance threat detection and response for energy systems. By aligning the project’s research with
these initiatives, BADGER ensures that the outcomes are relevant, impactful, and positioned to
inform future national‑scale deployments.

1.2 The Rise of the Artificial Intelligence Radio Access Network
Artificial intelligence radio access network (AI‑RAN) systems represent a major shift in how
wireless networks operate, embedding high‑performance AI models into RAN to enable
real‑time cyber optimization, anomaly detection, and security actions (AI-RAN Alliance n.d).
Industry leaders are rapidly developing strategies and playbooks for using AI to advance from
5G to 6G by integrating graphics processing unit (GPU)‑accelerated inference and intelligent
control loops directly into RAN infrastructure (Atri, 2026; Chowdhury et al. n.d.). In the context
of BADGER research, AI‑RAN provides the foundation for a dynamic honeypot system capable
of detecting, migrating, and containing threats across multiple timescales. By leveraging this
paradigm, the project transforms the RAN from a passive transport layer into an active security
participant with millisecond‑level responsiveness.

1.3 Purpose of This Report
This report serves two purposes. First, it outlines the work proposed for the BADGER project’s
proposed AI‑enabled dynamic honeypot architecture for securing critical energy systems using

 1

NextG networks. This project strives to create AI-enabled cybersecurity tools that can combat
the current landscape of cyber threats. Second, it discusses three software development
workflows—traditional, GitHub Copilot‑assisted, and Model Context Protocol (MCP)-
orchestrated—to outline approaches for integrating automated and potentially autonomous
processes into cybersecurity research and development in the context of next-generation energy
technologies. The discussion of these workflows demonstrates potential advantages of increased
automation in building and validating a complex AI-integrated system architecture (GitHub n.d.-
c; Model Context Protocol n.d.-a). The analysis offers a foundation for researchers to
select AI‑assisted methodologies that balance productivity, quality, and long‑term scalability.
NLR’s BADGER team will test such methodologies in building, testing, and executing
development of the proposed honeypot architecture.

2 Artificial Intelligence Radio Access Network-
  Enabled Dynamic Honeypot System Architecture
The BADGER project proposes a NextG‑native security architecture that uses “AI-enabled cyber
tools to detect, divert, and deceive threats” as they emerge within a 5G/NextG environment (The
White House 2026). The architecture, presented in Figure 1, embeds an intelligent control loop
federation that plugs directly into the RAN, enabling the system to detect attackers and migrate a
threat to an isolated area within the wireless fabric itself.

 Figure 1. Conceptual diagram showing a threat being moved from a “healthy zone” to a “caged
 zone.”

 5G/NextG network-native dynamic honeypot isolation. Illustration by Joshua Rivera

As demonstrated in Figure 1, researchers at NLR will use AI‑driven intelligence capabilities
across the RAN intelligent controller (RIC) (dApp, xApp, rApp) fifth-generation core (5G-Core)
network data analytics function (NWDAF) and an emerging multi-access edge computing-based
grid controller on the user plane to automate the system transition of malicious actors from the
live “healthy zone” (shown in green) to an isolated “caged zone” (shown in red). This enables
safe, high‑fidelity analysis of adversarial behavior without disrupting critical infrastructure
operations.

 3

Operating across multiple RAN control loop timescales—from sub‑10-millisecond radio‑layer
reflexive actions to multisecond strategic decisions—the architecture enables continuous,
adaptive, and automated threat response suitable for protecting mission‑critical energy systems.

The following subsections describe the architecture’s components, their coordination
mechanisms, the threat‑mitigation workflow, evaluation criteria, and resulting operational
impacts.

2.1 Architecture Maturity, Assumptions, and Limitations
The AI‑RAN dynamic honeypot system described in this report is currently at a
conceptual‑to‑prototype maturity level. Early-stage components—including preliminary RIC
inference logic, candidate xApp/rApp policies, and experimental MEC‑layer coordination
strategies—are under active development within the Advanced Research on Integrated Energy
Systems (ARIES) Cyber Range. This section outlines key assumptions and known limitations to
provide clear boundaries for interpretation.

Key Assumptions

 • GPU‑accelerated inference is available at RAN timescales to support dApp‑level
 reflexive responses.
 • OAI‑based emulation fidelity is sufficient to model realistic cross‑layer interactions
 between RAN, core, and MEC components.
 • Reliable telemetry exchange exists across RAN, 5GC, and MEC layers, enabling
 coherent multi‑layer policy generation and enforcement.
Known Limitations

 • Latency coupling: Multi‑layer policy evaluation may introduce coordination overhead,
 especially during rapid migration events.
 • Cross‑layer complexity: Ensuring non‑interference between dApp, xApp/rApp, and MEC
 controllers remains an active research challenge requiring careful arbitration.
 • Detection fidelity: Early-stage AI models may produce uncertainty that necessitates
 multi‑source correlation and validation before high‑confidence mitigation.
Clarifying these boundaries sets realistic expectations and establishes a transparent foundation
for the experimental validation described in later sections of this report.

2.2 Component Roles and Responsibilities
Each component within the AI-driven system architecture contributes specialized capabilities
that collectively support a coordinated defense strategy for isolating cyber threats and mitigating
adverse outcomes. In addition to AI‑enabled RAN controllers, the architecture includes a
multi-access edge computing (MEC)‑hosted grid‑aware controller operating within the user
plane to support cross‑layer hybrid decision making.

 4

 1. dApp—millisecond-scale reflexive response: The dApp performs GPU-accelerated
 inference to detect radio frequency anomalies (e.g., jamming or spoofing) and applies
 immediate localized mitigation. Operating at the radio layer, it forms the ultra-low-
 latency first line of defense (Lacava et al. 2025).

 2. xApp—near-real-time traffic steering: The xApp executes policy-driven actions
 produced by the rApp and implements near-real-time RAN control. It orchestrates Next
 Generation NodeB (gNodeB) behavior to seamlessly reroute suspicious user equipment
 into the caged zone without disrupting services.

 3. rApp—strategic policy engine: Operating within the non-real-time RIC, the rApp
 consolidates system intelligence and NWDAF analytics to produce high-confidence
 mitigation and recovery policies. It initiates the migration of attackers when
 threat-confidence thresholds are met.

 4. NWDAF—network-wide analytics: The NWDAF processes telemetry from across the
 5GC to correlate anomalies and identify coordinated threats. Its analytics enable the rApp
 to distinguish sophisticated attacks from normal network variation (OpenAirInterface
 n.d.). (Mekrache & Boutiba n.d.).

 5. MEC grid controller—user plane decision engine: The MEC grid controller introduces
 a grid-aware user plane intelligence layer that participates directly in the AI federation
 (Rivera, Peterson, & Sahu 2026). Positioned close to operational energy system
 workloads, it provides Its responsibilities include:

 • User-plane situational awareness: Monitoring UE behavior, traffic patterns, and
 grid-relevant workloads.
 • Local enforcement: Applying near-real-time traffic-level mitigation that
 complements xApp actions.
 • Cross-layer coordination: Sharing constraints, telemetry, and intent with rApp and
 xApp to maintain policy consistency.
 • Timescale bridging: Operating between sub-second reactions and slower
 multi-second application-state transitions, forming a middle layer in the hybrid
 hierarchy.
 • Workload-aware policy refinement: Ensuring that mitigation maintains
 grid-stability and safety requirements.
 This multi-layered arrangement enables rapid detection, coordinated RAN-core-MEC
 response, and stable transition of suspicious entities from the healthy to the caged zone.

2.2.1 Cross‑Layer Policy Coordination Challenge

A central challenge, identified in the NLR provisional patent “AI‑Driven Hybrid Policy
Enforcement System,” is ensuring coherence and non‑interference between user‑plane and
control‑plane policy decisions during threat migration and mitigation (Rivera, Peterson, & Sahu
2026). As the dApp, xApp/rApp, and MEC grid controller each operate on different timescales

 5

and with distinct objectives, they may attempt to influence the same user equipment or traffic
flow simultaneously. Without explicit coordination, this can lead to conflicting actions, unstable
mitigation cycles, or unintended impacts on grid‑relevant workloads.

To address this hybrid‑policy environment, the BADGER architecture incorporates several
complementary mechanisms:

 • Hierarchical arbitration: A layered policy stack where high-level intent guides
 downstream actions and prevents conflicting enforcement.
 • Constraint-aware coordination: Exchange of intent, telemetry, and safety constraints to
 ensure mitigation actions preserve grid stability.
 • Synchronized state sharing: Shared context buffers that minimize misalignment during
 rapid threat migration or state transitions.
Together, these mechanisms enable coherent decision-making across layers and maintain system
stability during adversarial events.

2.3 End-to-End Artificial Intelligence-Driven Threat Mitigation and
 Validated Decision Flow
The dynamic progression into the honeypot system is a coordinated, multi-timescale sequence
that integrates intelligence from the RIC-based applications (dApp, rApp, xApp), 5GC NWDAF,
and multi-access edge computing grid controller to detect, migrate, isolate, and remediate threats.
Figure 2 shows a basic representation of an AI-driven policy enforcement systems methodology
(Rivera, Peterson, & Sahu 2026).

 Figure 2. AI-driven threat detection, migration, and validated decision flow.

 6

 Hybrid decision policy enforcement for threat isolation. Illustration by Joshua Rivera

 1. Immediate reaction (dApp—<10 ms): The dApp performs GPU‑accelerated radio
 frequency anomaly detection and applies rapid localized mitigation to stabilize the radio
 link activity, such as jamming (D’Oro et al. 2022).
 2. Network-wide detection (NWDAF—minutes): The NWDAF correlates anomalies
 across the healthy zone using 5GC telemetry, identifying patterns consistent with
 coordinated or evolving threats.
 3. Strategic decision (rApp—>1 s): The rApp synthesizes NWDAF insights and the
 system state to generate migration policies instructing the RAN to move the suspicious
 user equipment into the caged zone.
 4. Near-real-time enforcement (xApp—10 ms to 1 s): The xApp executes the rApp’s
 mitigation policy, commanding the gNodeB to reroute the user equipment into the caged
 zone with no service disruption.
 5. MEC grid controller user plane validation and enforcement (subsecond to
 multisecond): Before and during migration, the MEC controller evaluates grid‑relevant
 workload impacts, applies application‑layer safeguards, and ensures that the user plane
 actions remain aligned with the control plane decisions. This step is essential for
 maintaining operational stability during adversarial transitions.
 6. Isolation and quarantine: The attacker enters the caged zone, a high-fidelity, isolated
 environment emulating sensitive energy systems, where activity is fully recorded for
 forensic analysis while protecting production systems.
 7. Recovery (rApp): Once the threat is neutralized, the rApp issues recovery policies
 restoring normal RAN and 5GC operation. This completes the automated, closed‑loop
 mitigation sequence.
This layered workflow will demonstrate how AI-RAN transforms threat handling from a
reactive, human-driven process into an automated, continuous, and adaptive security loop. By
embedding intelligence directly into the RAN, the system architecture will achieve the desired
responsiveness, containment fidelity, and system awareness required for modern critical
infrastructure.

2.4 Decision Flow for the Evaluation of Threat Detection, Mitigation,
 and Isolated Deception
Dynamic honeypots will be evaluated based on threat detection, mitigation, and deception
through the following metrics of evaluation:

 1. Detection confidence: The AI models will strive to reduce the false positives and false
 negatives. False positives may divert legitimate user equipment into the caged zone,
 potentially degrading service or increasing operator workload, while false negatives may
 allow adversarial behavior to persist longer in the healthy zone.

 7

 2. Mitigation evaluation: Mitigation policies will incorporate dynamic thresholds,
 multi-source correlation (e.g., NWDAF + MEC cross-checks), and validation logic to
 reduce misclassification.
 3. Isolated deception: This will be evaluated based on multiple metrics, including:
 a. Attack-based metrics like engagement time per session or successful and failed
 attacks.
 b. Defense-based metrics like block delay or mean time to block.
 c. Tactics, techniques, and procedures coverage depth, such as percentage of
 attacker's activities that can be mapped to MITRE ATT&CK for ICS framework
 (MITRE ATT&CK n.d.).
Prior work has evaluated the efficacy of cyber deception by modeling the objective to increase
the network packets from the adversary to target the caged network (Sahu, A., Paul, S., &
Macwan, R. 2026). BADGER will extend that work to consider more realistic evaluation criteria.

2.5 Operational Impact on Energy Systems
The AI‑RAN dynamic honeypot is designed not only as a cybersecurity mechanism but also as
an enabler of energy system resilience. Effective cyber mitigation at machine speed has direct
implications for grid stability, reliability, and operational continuity in 5G/NextG‑enabled
environments.

Key impacts include:

 • Stability Preservation: Rapid migration of malicious traffic into the caged zone prevents
 adversaries from interacting with latency‑sensitive grid control applications running on
 MEC infrastructure. Maintaining uninterrupted performance of these control loops is
 essential for system stability.
 • Reliability and Continuity: Automated isolation of compromised user equipment
 reduces the risk of cascading degradation across distributed grid assets. By containing
 threats before they propagate, the system supports resilient operation of both edge and
 centralized energy functions.
 • Accelerated Recovery: The closed‑loop cycle of detection, migration, validation, and
 restoration shortens operator response times and limits disruption. This reduces cognitive
 burden on human defenders while restoring normal system behavior more quickly.
By linking autonomous cyber actions to measurable energy system outcomes, the architecture
demonstrates clear operational benefit aligned with DOE and national security objectives.

To rigorously evaluate these detection, mitigation, and operational impacts under realistic
network and grid conditions, the BADGER project relies on the ARIES Cyber Range as its
high‑fidelity emulation environment. Section 3 describes this environment and the associated
development tools that enable controlled, repeatable experimentation of the architecture
introduced in Section 2.

3 Emulation and Development Environment
A system as dynamic and multilayered as the BADGER AI‑RAN-driven honeypot architecture
requires an equally sophisticated environment for development, testing, and validation. To
support this complexity, the NLR research team is using an ARIES Cyber Range advanced
wireless capability designed to emulate full 5G/NextG network behavior, security events, and
system interactions with high fidelity (NLR n.d.). The ARIES Cyber Range environment enables
researchers to model the complete threat migration path from the initial anomaly in the healthy
zone to full isolation within the caged zone without risking production systems.

Figure 3 and Figure 4 illustrate the integrated layout of the emulation and hardware capabilities
within the ARIES Cyber Range’s advanced wireless capability, highlighting its modular design
and ability to orchestrate large, tightly controlled experiments. The environment integrates key
NextG components—such as OpenAirInterface (OAI)-based 5GC and RAN, open radio access
network (O‑RAN) controllers (dApps, xApps, rApps), NWDAF analytics functions, and
GPU‑accelerated workloads—all deployed through repeatable continuous integration/continuous
deployment DevOps pipelines with virtualized and containerized infrastructure.

This range environment enables deterministic, reproducible evaluations of AI-driven RAN

4 Artificial Intelligence-Assisted Development Life
  Cycle
Building the BADGER project at the pace required by national initiatives demands a shift from
traditional development practices toward workflows that incorporate intelligent, context‑aware
AI systems. This section outlines how emerging AI capabilities, especially those enabled by the

 o It can be deployed and configured in a manner to operate locally or within the

4.4 ARIES Cyber Range for the 5G Honeypot Automation
In a cyber range environment that emulates 5G RAN, 5GC, and MEC architectures, MCP
enables a shift from fragmented, manually coordinated development to a unified, semantically
driven workflow. In this setting, the MCP assistant acts as the central intelligence layer. It
preemptively ingests OAI repositories, third-generation partnership project (3GPP) and O‑RAN
specifications, application programming interface schemas, configuration files, and historical test
artifacts. Using these sources, it constructs a cross‑referenced knowledge graph that links
normative requirements, software components, interfaces, and runtime behaviors (3GPP n.d., O-
RAN n.d., Ouyang et al. 2025).

This knowledge graph provides the formal alignment needed to ensure consistency between
specification clauses and the corresponding implementation within the cyber range. Instead of
manually correlating PDF requirements with C++ handlers, configuration files, or state
machines, the MCP environment provides semantic retrieval and constraint‑aware reasoning to
surface the appropriate code regions, messages, timers, and data structures. The developer no
longer performs this correlation manually; the MCP assistant automatically retrieves and
interprets it using structured context queries over the graph (Tomicevic 2026).

Within this paradigm, the developer’s role transitions from executing tasks to directing them.
High‑level prompts, such as generating an xApp that complies with a particular O‑RAN
procedure or diagnosing an attach failure based on log traces, trigger multistep synthesis by the
MCP assistant. The assistant then produces architecturally consistent code artifacts, end‑to‑end
test scenarios across emulated slices, and post‑execution analyses against specification‑derived
invariants. As test executions run in the cyber range, logs, events, and state transitions stream
back into the MCP context engine, enabling automated causal‑trace reconstruction and intelligent
detection of protocol violations. This elevates debugging from a manual, exploratory activity to a
structured, system‑aware reasoning loop.

The proposed MCP client-server architecture also extends beyond core SDLC tasks to support
the design, deployment, and operation of the dynamic honeypot system. For example:

 • Phase 1—design and threat modeling: Without MCP, engineers must manually review

 topologies aligned with the MEC workloads and the RAN control traffic. It can also
 validate isolation rules against rApp policy logic.
 • Phase 2—Honeypot implementation: MCP‑enabled clients can detect exposure points

 insert honeypot services into the appropriate parts of the emulated network. This enables
 rapid iteration of dynamic honeypot designs without requiring extensive manual
 integration.
 • Phase 3—Continuous Integration/Continuous Delivery (CI/CD) integration (lint →

Overall, MCP will transform the ARIES Cyber Range into an intelligent experimentation

5.4 Clarifying Present and Future AI Capabilities
To prevent overinterpretation of the AI capabilities referenced throughout this section, it is
important to distinguish clearly between (1) what today’s tools can reliably perform, (2) what
near-term MCP-enabled prototypes may enable within the ARIES Cyber Range, and (3) what
longer-term, autonomous workflows represent as research objectives for the BADGER project.

Current Capabilities

Modern tools such as GitHub Copilot provide meaningful automation in well-bounded tasks—
particularly code generation, document parsing, pattern extraction, and basic test synthesis.
These capabilities accelerate coding activity and reduce certain classes of syntactic or local logic
defects, but they remain dependent on human oversight and lack system-level awareness. They
do not autonomously orchestrate multistep workflows, correlate cross-component telemetry, or
reason across the entire RAN–Core–MEC architecture.

Near-Term Capabilities

The MCP-assisted workflows described in this report reflect a near-term experimental trajectory.
Within the ARIES Cyber Range, MCP-connected assistants can ingest heterogeneous project
artifacts—including code repositories, 3GPP/O-RAN specifications, logs, topologies, and
experiment outputs—and use this context to perform structured reasoning. Early prototypes may
support capabilities such as:

 • Multifile architectural reasoning and refactoring
 • Generating cross-layer test plans and experiment scaffolding
 • System-aware debugging grounded in logs, specifications, and telemetry
 • Semi-autonomous orchestration of emulated experiments under human review
These functions represent feasible research prototypes but are not production ready features.

Long-Term Research-Phase Capabilities

References in this document to “autonomous debugging,” “multi-step orchestration,” or
“system-aware reasoning” describe future implementations the BADGER project aims to
evaluate. These research capabilities may include:

 • Closed-loop coordination between dApp, xApp/rApp, and MEC policies
 • Automatic detection-to-mitigation pipelines that evaluate grid-stability constraints
 • Autonomous experiment management within ARIES Cyber Range.
 • Integrated, machine-speed reasoning across the full wireless-and-grid control stack
These capabilities define the project’s long-term vision and serve as guiding research objectives
rather than descriptions of current system maturity.

6 Quantitative Framework for Workflow Analysis
This section presents a conceptual and illustrative quantitative framework designed to support
engineering decision-making, rather than to provide predictive or empirically calibrated results.
The objective is to formalize the relationships between workflow complexity, developer effort,
defect generation, project risk, and lifecycle cost across three paradigms: traditional, Copilot-
assisted, and MCP-assisted development. This approach is inspired by foundational work in
software engineering economics, such as Boehm’s Constructive Cost Model (COCOMO) (Boehm
1983), but it adapts these principles to the modern context of AI-assisted software development.

The framework emphasizes directional insights—such as how system-level AI assistance reduces
integration defects or compresses delivery timelines—without asserting strict numerical accuracy,
hence the framework is not calibrated with empirical constants and should not be used for absolute
forecasting. It is intended to guide comparative evaluation, trade-off analysis, and investment
prioritization within the BADGER project and similar environments that will be developed in
future.

Modeling Assumptions

To ensure interpretability, the framework adopts the following simplifying assumptions:

 • Linear complexity-to-effort relationship: Development time scales proportionally with
 task complexity normalized by expertise.
 • Separable task domains: Coding and non-coding activities (analysis, integration, testing)
 are treated independently where appropriate.
 • Abstract AI multipliers: AI assistance is represented through multiplicative efficiency
 factors rather than task-specific micro-models.
 • Defect reduction as aggregate effect: Defect-reduction parameters (Pcopilot, Pmcp)
 represent broad classes of prevented errors rather than specific defect taxonomies.
 • Risk linearity: Project risk is modeled as a weighted linear combination of schedule and
 defect exposure.
 • Cost decomposition: Lifecycle cost is driven by development effort, tooling overhead, and
 post-deployment defect remediation.
 These assumptions intentionally trade fidelity for clarity and extensibility, allowing the
 model to remain adaptable across different system architectures and development contexts.
For evaluation purposes this framework will consider three workflows assessed in the BADGER
project:

 • Traditional (human centric)
 • GitHub Copilot assisted (augmented)
 • MCP assisted (agentic/orchestrated)

6.4 Project Life Cycle Cost Analysis
Total cost ($) is a function of development hours, tooling costs, and long-term maintenance costs
(fixing bugs discovered post-deployment):

 $ = (𝑇𝑇 ⋅ Ratedev ) + $tools + (𝐵𝐵prod ⋅ $fix )

The project costs across each workflow are given by:

 • Traditional: long development time, no AI tool costs, high defect remediation cost:

 $trad = (Ttrad.Ratedev) + $tools_trad + (Btrad.$bugfix)

 • Copilot-assisted: reduced coding effort, moderate tool cost, moderate defect reduction:

 $copilot = (Tcopilot .Ratedev) + $tools_copilot + (Bcopilot.$bugfix)

 • MCP-assisted: higher tool cost but dramatically reduced development time and lower
 post-deployment defect costs:

 26

 $mcp = (Tmcp.Ratedev) + $tools_mcp + (Bmcp.$bugfix)

Given that post-deployment bug fixes are typically orders of magnitude more expensive than
development-time corrections, MCP’s reduction in defects has an outsized impact. As a result:

 $mcp < $copilot < $trad

MCP therefore yields the most favorable life cycle cost profile, particularly for complex,
integration-heavy 5G/NextG systems involving heterogeneous RAN, core, and MEC

Using equal weights for illustration (𝛼𝛼 𝑇𝑇 = 𝛼𝛼𝐵𝐵 = 𝛼𝛼𝑅𝑅 = 𝛼𝛼$ = 0.25), the traditional workflow
produces a baseline Pain Index of 1.00. Incorporating GitHub Copilot reduces the development
time (100 → 86.7 units) and defect rates (100 → 75), yielding a Pain Index of 0.805, which is
equivalent to a 19.5% reduction in aggregate pain. In contrast, the MCP-assisted workflow—
accelerating analysis, coding, debugging, integration, and system reasoning—reduces time to 40
units and defects to 45, achieving a Pain Index of 0.428, or a 57.2% reduction.

This simple example demonstrates how the Pain Index complements the detailed quantitative
model by offering a consolidated view of workflow efficiency. Rather than evaluating
improvements separately (shorter time, fewer bugs, lower cost), the Pain Index combines them
into a single interpretable metric. As BADGER transitions toward MCP enabled, AI orchestrated
development, the Pain Index provides a practical decision tool for assessing workflow upgrades,
prioritizing engineering investments, and communicating the return on effort across stakeholders.

The framework primarily encodes four key relationships:

 1. AI assistance reduces effective complexity, leading to lower T, with MCP extending

 3. Risk increases with both schedule delays and defect exposure, reinforcing the importance
 of joint optimization.

 4. Lifecycle cost is highly sensitive to post-deployment defects, making defect reduction
 disproportionately valuable.

7 Next Steps: Transitioning to an Artificial
  Intelligence-Orchestrated Development Workflow
The BADGER project is poised to begin at an inflection point. The complexity of NextG‑based
security systems and the national urgency behind the Genesis Mission demands faster iteration,
tighter integration, and development workflows that can scale beyond human‑centric limits.
Today’s AI tools already accelerate coding tasks, but the future of BADGER requires something
broader: an ecosystem where AI actively interprets, coordinates, and acts across the entire
development environment. This section outlines a focused strategy to move from today’s
AI‑augmented development toward tomorrow’s fully orchestrated, MCP‑enabled workflows.

7.1 Why a Hybrid Approach Is Needed
NLR research demonstrates how current tools, such as GitHub Copilot, deliver meaningful,
immediate gains. They speed up code generation, help reduce boilerplate code, and support
developers inside the integrated development environment; however, these tools operate on a
local, file‑level context. Their impact, though valuable, remains narrow and does not consider the
whole scope of varying contexts.

By contrast, an MCP‑enabled system provides global visibility. It can be designed to access
repositories, logs, experiments, specifications, and deployment artifacts and to synthesize these
sources to reason about the entire project. This turns the AI from a reactive helper into a
proactive collaborator capable of executing full observe → decide → act loops (Krishnan 2025).

The near‑term path forward is not to abandon today’s tools but to combine them by using Copilot
to accelerate local development tasks now and developing MCP‑driven context engines that
unlock system‑level reasoning.

Together, these tools will establish a hybrid workflow that delivers immediate productivity
improvements while laying the foundation for full AI orchestration.

7.1.1 Using Artificial Intelligence Augmentation to Build 5G NWDAF and RIC
 Apps

AI-based development tools, such as GitHub Copilot and MCP-assisted methods, will
significantly accelerate the creation of 5G NWDAF components and RIC applications. In this
workflow, the AI assistant acts as an advanced engineering partner that will help ARIES Cyber
Range research engineers more effectively design, develop, and validate software code.

 • AI-enhanced code development: During this process, AI will generate boilerplate code
 structures, configuration files, container service assets, and complex functions by
 leveraging the full project repository context. Example: The research will promote
 Copilot to automatically generate a C function that extracts metrics from an OAI network
 function.

 29

 • Clarifying complex standards and architectures: The assistant will explain
 interactions across the OAI codebase or decode intricate 3GPP/O-RAN specifications.
 Example: The researcher will promote copilot to describe the detailed protocol data unit
 session establishment sequence using OAI’s session management function
 implementation.

 • AI-supported testing and validation: AI will streamline testing by producing unit tests,
 mock objects, and scenario-based test frameworks. This reduces manual effort and speeds
 up verification across NWDAF, dApps, xApps, and rApps.

 • MCP as a development force multiplier: When paired with the MCP, the AI assistant
 becomes context aware across an entire environment. It functions like a superpowered
 pair programmer capable of generating application logic and configuration targeting
 kernel-based, virtual machine-based, and containerized deployments.

7.1.1.1 How Artificial Intelligence Helps Build Key Components

 • RIC dApp: AI supports the creation of ultraefficient real-time code appropriate for
 constrained distributed unit/centralized unit environments. It can recommend safe
 patterns that avoid high-latency operations or dynamic memory allocation.
 • NWDAF machine learning models: AI generates data-processing pipelines,
 model-training scripts, and integration scaffolding for analytics workflows, such as
 anomaly detection.
 • Security rApp: The assistant can create foundational code for policy generation,
 NWDAF alert ingestion, and representational state transfer-based interfaces for
 orchestration.
 • Traffic-steering xApp: Because of the complexity of the E2 interface, AI assistance
 helps generate subscription logic, control message structures, and code patterns aligned
 with E2 Application Protocol (E2AP) specifications.
 • Dynamic honeypot configuration: AI can produce virtualized and container-based
 manifests and network policies to support secure honeypot deployment within a NextG
 system.

7.1.1.2 Artificial Intelligence-Augmented Testing Across Layers

 1. Unit testing: AI generates mock servers, fixtures, and automated checks tailored to
 each component (dApp, xApp, rApp).

 2. Integration testing: AI can build application programming interface-driven test
 harnesses that validate communication flows—for example, by ensuring a policy
 generated by an rApp is parsed correctly by an xApp.

 3. End-to-end scenario testing: AI can script attack simulations, log-monitoring
 routines, and verification tasks that confirm whether the entire threat-response chain
 behaves as expected.

7.2 A Phased Path Toward Artificial Intelligence-Orchestrated
 Development
Transitioning to an AI‑orchestrated, closed‑loop development workflow requires disciplined
experimentation and staged adoption. The BADGER project will pursue a three‑phase
progression designed to reduce risk, validate performance, and gradually expand AI
responsibilities across code generation, testing, analysis, and ARIES Cyber Range orchestration.
This phased plan supports the development of advanced security and resilience methods within a
5G/NextG capability while aligning the workflow with the urgency and complexity of
mission‑driven research.

7.2.1 Phase 1: Establish the Baseline (Artificial Intelligence Augmentation)

This initial phase focuses on deploying mature AI tools, primarily GitHub Copilot, to quantify
immediate productivity gains and establish a baseline for comparison. During this phase, the
developers will:

 • Use GitHub Copilot to accelerate local development tasks, such as writing boilerplate
 code, generating configuration files, and assisting with OAI and O‑RAN code navigation.

 • Document time savings, error patterns, iteration counts, and developer feedback.

 • Identify workflow bottlenecks that Copilot cannot address, especially those requiring
 cross‑document reasoning (e.g., correlating logs, specifications, and multicomponent
 code interactions).

These data will define the baseline against which system‑aware MCP workflows will be
evaluated.

7.2.2 Phase 2: Build a Context-Aware Proof of Concept (Artificial
 Intelligence-Enhanced Context)

This phase will develop a “context engine,” an MCP‑enabled system that integrates
heterogeneous project artifacts (e.g., code, documents, logs, specifications, and test results) into a
unified and machine‑navigable knowledge space. The objective is to explore how system‑aware
reasoning improves accuracy, reliability, and development speed compared to augmentation
alone.

7.2.2.1 Context Engine: Prototype Architecture—Multi-App Context Engine
 Investigation

Researchers will investigate the feasibility of building an architecture that indexes the National
Institute of Standards and Technology’s 5G automation framework and tool, O-RAN Split, and
3GPP security frameworks (NIST 2026, GitHub n.d.-b., The Art of Service n.d.). Key research
areas will include:

 31

 • Unified indexer: Evaluate how to ingest Abstract Syntax Notation 1 (ASN.1) definitions
 for E2/A1 interfaces and E2 service models to ensure that the generated xApps or rApps
 remain syntactically compatible with the OAI gNodeB.

 • Relational storage: Explore models to map “control loop” dependencies, specifically
 how an rApp policy triggers an xApp action within the OAI RAN.

 • NWDAF integration: Incorporate 3GPP TS 33.501 (security architecture) and MITRE
 FiGHT for 5G and MITRE ATT&CK for ICS to determine if an LLM can accurately
 correlate OAI signaling anomalies with known threat vectors (ESTI 2024, The Art of
 Service. n.d. MITRE n.d., MITRE ATT&CK n.d.).

7.2.2.2 Evaluation Framework: System-Aware Reasoning vs. Augmentation

This framework compares an LLM’s reasoning when supported by the context engine versus
standard retrieval‑augmented generation (Google Cloud. n.d.). The study will document:

 • Augmentation alone: Identify where retrieval‑augmented generation or Copilot‑style
 approaches fail—for example, generating xApps that violate OAI real‑time constraints or
 misuse ASN.1

 • Conflict analysis: Evaluate system‑aware reasoning by determining whether an LLM
 can detect when an rApp “energy-saving” policy (A1 interface) conflicts with a
 distributed unit‑level “low-latency” requirement enforced by a dApp.

 • Logic integrity: Ensure that generated xApps/rApps do not trigger signaling storms,
 violate O‑RAN/E2AP timing, or cause OAI stack overflows. This is a primary research
 metric.

7.2.2.3 Implementation Steps

 1. Ingestion research: Evaluate methods to index RIC/NWDAF software development
 kits alongside core OAI C-code and documentation.

 2. Cross-domain linking study: Map NWDAF analytics identifications (e.g., user
 mobility) to specific OAI variables and C‑code paths that generate these metrics.

 3. App generation prototyping: Test LLM‑generated xApps/rApps using the context
 engine to verify that calls and data structures align with E2 interface constraints.

 4. Threat simulation analysis: Feed simulated attack logs into the engine to assess
 NWDAF anomaly‑detection accuracy and risk‑scoring calibration.

7.2.2.4 Capable Large Language Models

To ensure that the engine is fairly evaluated, the LLM must meet high standards for calibration
(knowing its limits), robustness (stability under noise), reasoning (logical depth), and
performance (latency/accuracy) (Walsh et al. 2025).

 32

 • Calibration (confidence vs. correctness): Models such as GPT-5.4 will be investigated
 to ensure that NWDAF risk scores are statistically reliable, preventing false positive
 security actions that could drop legitimate traffic (OpenAI 2026).

 • Robustness (stability under noise): Evaluate the ability of models such as Gemini 3.1
 Pro to handle massive, “noisy” OAI network logs and thousands of pages of 3GPP
 standards without performance degradation (Google DeepMind n.d.).

 • Reasoning (logical depth): Test models such as Claude Opus 4.6 on the ability to
 maintain state across complex E2/A1 protocol logic to ensure that the high-level intent
 (A1) correctly translates to execution (E2) (Anthropic 2026).

 • Performance (optimization): Research high-efficiency models such as DeepSeek-R1 to
 generate real-time radio resource management algorithms for constrained OAI distributed
 unit/centralized unit environments (Guo et al. 2026).

7.2.3 Phase 3: Select the Strategic Path (Artificial Intelligence Orchestration)

The final phase will use empirical data from phases 1 and 2 to define a sustainable strategy for
adopting agentic, MCP‑enabled workflows across the BADGER project. Activities will include:

 • Identify the highest‑value opportunities for AI orchestration (e.g., automated experiment
 deployment, test generation, anomaly analysis, cross‑layer policy reasoning).

 • Establish governance around AI‑generated artifacts to ensure safety, reproducibility, and
 traceability.

 • Design an adoption roadmap for integrating MCP‑based tools into daily development,
 continuous integration/continuous delivery automation, cyber range experiment
 orchestration, and RAN logic validation.

In this phase, AI will transition from a passive assistant to an active collaborator capable of
executing observe → decide → act loops over code, runtime environments, and experiment
outcomes.

8 Conclusion
The BADGER project will operate at a pivotal moment in the national push toward AI‑driven,
NextG‑native security innovation. This report outlines both a novel AI‑RAN‑enabled dynamic
honeypot system architecture and the development methodologies that are required to build such
a system at the pace demanded by the Genesis Mission and America’s AI Action Plan. Together,
these architectural and workflow advancements would position the NLR to deliver
mission‑critical security capabilities for modern energy infrastructure, while accelerating
subsequent cybersecurity research and development practices through AI workflows.

A central insight from this work is that advanced AI must accelerate two parallel
transformations:

 1. The systems researchers build will include intelligent RAN‑embedded detection,
 migration, and containment capabilities.

 2. The workflows that researchers will use to build such capabilities will shift from
 traditional human‑centric development to AI‑augmented and ultimately AI‑orchestrated
 engineering.

The comparative analysis of traditional, Copilot‑assisted, and MCP‑orchestrated workflows
demonstrates a clear trajectory. As development processes evolve toward deeper AI
involvement, supported by the MCP, engineering becomes faster, more reliable, and better
aligned with the complexity of emerging NextG systems. These workflow changes will reduce
cognitive load, integrate cross‑domain reasoning, and enable continuous system‑aware
refinement throughout the development life cycle.

To translate these insights into action, this report presents a deliberate, data‑driven roadmap for
staged AI adoption. Near‑term efforts will leverage proven tools, such as GitHub Copilot, to
deliver immediate productivity gains. Concurrently, the project will advance toward a fully
agentic, MCP‑enabled environment where AI plays an active role in planning, testing,
debugging, and orchestrating experiments within the ARIES Cyber Range. This hybrid approach
balances practical progress with strategic modernization, ensuring readiness for a future where
AI is a first‑class engineering partner.

Ultimately, this dual‑track strategy—advancing both the AI‑RAN security architecture and the
AI‑enabled development workflows—offers a forward‑looking blueprint for how NextG security
systems should be engineered. By adopting the phased plan outlined in this report, the BADGER
project will aim to strengthen national critical infrastructure resilience while establishing a
scalable, repeatable model for future AI‑driven research and development initiatives.
