Beating the Ahlswede–Khachatrian bound for the Erdős–Frankl–Pach problem
Tuan Tran, Zixiang Xu

Abstract
In the 1980s, Erdős and, independently, Frankl and Pach conjectured that, for sufficiently large n n , every ( d + 1 ) (d+1) -uniform family on { 1 , … , n } \{1,\ldots,n\} with VC-dimension d d has size at most ( n − 1 d ) \binom{n-1}{d} , the size of a star. Ahlswede and Khachatrian disproved this conjecture in 1997 by giving a family of size ( n − 1 d ) + ( n − 4 d − 2 ) \binom{n-1}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2} . This value has since been widely believed to be best possible, and Mubayi and Zhao explicitly conjectured its optimality in 2007. Very recently, Wang, Xu and Zhang proved their conjecture for d = 2 d=2 and n ≥ 7 n\geq 7 , providing further support for this belief. Surprisingly, we show that the Mubayi-Zhao conjecture is false for every d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 by constructing families larger than the Ahlswede–Khachatrian bound. Our constructions suggest that the answer to the Erdős–Frankl–Pach problem depends delicately on both n n and d d .

Introduction

Let

Over 40 years ago, Erdős
[
3
]
and, independently, Frankl and Pach
[

]
initiated the study of the uniform analogue of the Sauer–Shelah theorem. The problem has long attracted interest
[

,

]
, but substantial progress has come only in recent years
[

,

,

.
For
d\geq 0
, let
M_{d}(n)
denote the maximum size of a family

\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
with
\operatorname{VC}(\mathcal{F})\leq d
.
It is not hard to see that
M_{0}(n)=1
and
M_{1}(n)=n-1
. For
d\geq 2
, however, much less is known.

Question 1.1

(Erdős–Frankl–Pach)
.

For given integers
d\geq 2
and
n\geq 2d+2
, determine
M_{d}(n)
.

This problem was originally motivated by a possible
generalization of the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem. Indeed, the canonical star

\{F\in\binom{[n]}{d+1}:1\in F\}
has size
\binom{n-1}{d}
and
VC-dimension at most
d
. Erdős
[
3
]
, as well Frankl and
Pach
[
4
]
, conjectured that this construction is optimal whenever

n
is sufficiently large compared to
d
. Frankl and Pach
[
4
]
also proved
the elegant upper bound
M_{d}(n)\leq\binom{n}{d}
, which remains a
fundamental benchmark. Later improvements include the
\Omega_{d}(\log n)

saving of Mubayi and Zhao
[
6
]
for prime power
d
and sufficiently large
n
, and the result of Ge, Xu, Yip, Zhang and Zhao
[
5
]
that the Frankl–Pach bound is never attained. More recently, Chao, Xu, Yip and Zhang
[
2
]
gave a purely combinatorial proof that
M_{d}(n)\leq\binom{n-1}{d}+O_{d}(n^{d-1-\frac{1}{4d-2}})
, which Yang and Yu
[
11
]
subsequently sharpened to
M_{d}(n)\leq\binom{n-1}{d}+O_{d}(n^{d-2})
.

On the lower bound side, the star conjecture was disproved by Ahlswede and
Khachatrian
[
1
]
, who constructed a
(d+1)
-uniform family
with VC-dimension at most
d
and size

\operatorname{AK}_{d}(n):=\binom{n-1}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2}
. Mubayi and Zhao
[
6
]
later found infinitely many non-isomorphic constructions of the same size and conjectured that
M_{d}(n)=\operatorname{AK}_{d}(n)
for all sufficiently large
n
.
For convenience, we recall their construction. Let

V=[n]\setminus\{1,2\}
, and define

\mathcal{G}_{1}=\binom{V}{d}\setminus\bigg\{G\in\binom{V}{d}:4\in G,\ 3\notin G\bigg\}\ \textup{and}\ \mathcal{G}_{2}=\left\{G\in\binom{V}{d}:4\in G\right\}.

Set

\mathcal{A}=\bigg\{F\in\binom{[n]}{d+1}:\{1,2\}\subseteq F\bigg\}\cup\big\{\{1\}\cup G:G\in\mathcal{G}_{1}\big\}\cup\big\{\{2\}\cup G:G\in\mathcal{G}_{2}\big\}.

Ahlswede and Khachatrian
[
1
]
proved that
\operatorname{VC}(\mathcal{A})\leq d
, and a direct calculation gives

|\mathcal{A}|=\binom{n-1}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2}=\operatorname{AK}_{d}(n).

The case
d=2
provides strong evidence for this conjecture: Wang, Xu and Zhang
[
10
]
proved that

M_{2}(n)=\operatorname{AK}_{2}(n)
for all
n\geq 7
. Thus, for 3-uniform families with VC-dimension at most
2
, the Ahlswede–Khachatrian bound is optimal.

Our main result disproves the Mubayi–Zhao conjecture for every
d\geq 3
.

Theorem 1.2
.

The following statements hold.

(i)

For
n\geq 6
,

M_{3}(n)\geq\binom{n-1}{3}+n-3.

(ii)

For every
d\geq 4
and
n\geq 2d
,

M_{d}(n)\geq\binom{n-1}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2}+\binom{n-6}{d-3}+2M_{d-4}(n-6).

Thus,
Theorem

1.2
improves the Ahlswede–Khachatrian bound by
by at least

\binom{n-6}{d-3}
. The exact result for
d=2
can also be used as a seed in the recursive term. For
example, for
n\geq 13
, we have
M_{6}(n)\geq\operatorname{AK}_{6}(n)+\binom{n-6}{3}+2\binom{n-7}{2}+2
.

We find the phenomenon behind this improvement quite striking.
For
d=2
, the Ahlswede–Khachatrian construction is sharp because it leaves no lower dimensional room to fill.
In higher dimensions, however, missing patterns can be filled
recursively. This suggests that the Erdős–Frankl–Pach problem may not admit a uniform answer across all dimensions: the correct formula may genuinely
change with
d
.

3
Some remarks

Our constructions should not be viewed as the end of the story. The underlying idea is to use a carefully chosen 6-point core with two missing
4
-patterns that can
be filled recursively. We believe that if one replaces this 6-point core by a larger finite core, it might be possible
to further improve the coefficients of the
lower order error terms in our main result. This is a finite problem in
principle, but it becomes complicated very quickly: a missing pattern that is
usable at one level might be destroyed by traces coming from higher levels, and
the certificate table has to be checked simultaneously with all recursive
insertions.

Thus, the present construction points to a sharp increase in the complexity of
the Erdős–Frankl–Pach problem once
d\geq 3
. Accordingly, we should not expect the exact values in higher dimensions to be governed by the same rigidity phenomenon as in the case
d=2
. The first natural test case is

d=3
. Part (i) of Theorem
1.2
gives

M_{3}(n)\geq\binom{n-1}{3}+n-3,

for every
n\geq 6
, and we suspect that this bound is sharp for all sufficiently large
n
.
The restriction to sufficiently large
n
is necessary: it was shown in
[
10
]
that
M_{3}(8)\geq 45,
whereas
\binom{7}{3}+5=40
.

Conjecture 3.1
.

For all sufficiently large
n
,

M_{3}(n)=\binom{n-1}{3}+n-3.
