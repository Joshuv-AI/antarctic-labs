On the Parameterized Approximability of (Mergeable) Sum of Radii Clustering
Ameet Gadekar

Abstract
The sum of radii problem ( k k -MSR) asks, given a metric space on n n points, to place k k balls covering all points so as to minimize the sum of their radii. Despite extensive study from the perspectives of approximation and parameterized algorithms, the exact parameterized complexity of the problem and the existence of efficient parameterized approximation schemes remained open. We advance this understanding on both the hardness and algorithmic fronts. We begin by showing that k k -MSR is W ​ [ 2 ] \textsc{W}[2] -hard parameterized by k k , thereby pinpointing its location in the W -hierarchy. Moreover, via our reduction, we rule out efficient parameterized approximation schemes (EPAS)—that is, ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximations running in time f ⁡ ( k , ε ) ⋅ poly ⁡ ( n ) f(k,\varepsilon)\cdot\mathrm{poly}(n) —unless W ​ [ 2 ] = FPT \textsc{W}[2]=\textsc{FPT} . Assuming the Exponential Time Hypothesis, we further rule out such algorithms running in time f ⁡ ( k , ε ) ⋅ n o ⁡ ( k ) f(k,\varepsilon)\cdot n^{o(k)} , strengthening recent lower bounds for the problem. On the algorithmic side, we study k k -MSR under the framework of mergeable constraints , which captures a broad class of clustering constraints, including fairness, diversity, and lower bounds. We obtain an FPT ( 8 3 + ε ) (\frac{8}{3}+\varepsilon) -approximation, improving upon the previous best guarantee of ( 4 + ε ) (4+\varepsilon) . Moreover, given access to a suitable assignment subroutine, we achieve a ( 2 + ε ) (2+\varepsilon) -approximation, matching the best known bound for the unconstrained problem. This, in turn, yields ( 2 + ε ) (2+\varepsilon) FPT -approximations for several important settings, including ( t , k ) (t,k) -fair, ( α , β ) (\alpha,\beta) -fair, ℓ \ell -diversity, and private clustering.

Clustering with the sum of radii objective has emerged as a natural alternative to classical center-based formulations such as

From a computational perspective, the approximability landscape of

Over the past two decades,

While these constraints are well motivated by real-world applications, they introduce significant challenges in enforcing feasibility. More importantly, the rapidly growing variety of such constraints makes it increasingly difficult to design algorithms tailored to each individual setting. Mergeable constraints.

To address this challenge, Arutyunova and Schmidt
2
introduced a clean and general framework of
mergeable constraints
. Informally, a constraint is said to be mergeable if merging any two clusters of a feasible solution results in another feasible (possibly larger) cluster. This abstraction captures a broad range of commonly studied constraints
14
, including the following:

•

Balanced Clustering

12
: Points are colored either red or blue, and each cluster must contain equal numbers of red and blue points.

•

Pairwise Fair Clustering

17
: Points are colored red or blue, and each cluster must maintain a bounded ratio between the number of blue and red points, lying within
[1/t,t]
. This is also known as
(t,k)
-fair clustering.

•

Pairwise Exact with Multiple Colors

42
: Points have multiple colors, and each cluster must preserve the same proportion of each color.

•

\ell
-Diversity Clustering

21
;
5
: Points have multiple colors, and no color constitute more than a
1/\ell
fraction of any cluster.

•

Fair Representational

9
;
10
: Points have multiple colors, and each color must lie within specified lower and upper bounds in every cluster.

•

(\alpha,\beta)
-Fair Clustering

10
;
5
: A generalization of fair representational clustering in which a point may have multiple colors simultaneously. It captures all of the above constraints.

•

Private Clustering

42
: Each cluster must contain at least
L
points; this is also known as clustering with a uniform lower bound.

In their work, Carta et al.
14
studied mergeable constraints for
k
-MSR and designed a
(6+\varepsilon)

FPT
-approximation for
k
-MSR under mergeable constraints, yielding a unified guarantee across all these settings. They also obtained improved
(3+\varepsilon)
approximations for special cases such as balanced clustering and uniform lower bounds. Subsequently, Bandyapadhyay and Chen
4
improved the general factor to
(4+\varepsilon)
, which is the current best known bound.

Our second main result significantly improves this factor to
(\tfrac{8}{3}+\varepsilon)
.

Theorem 2
.

There is an algorithm for
k
-MSR under a mergeable constraint that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, computes a
(\tfrac{8}{3}+\varepsilon)
-approximation in time
2^{O(k\log(k/\varepsilon))}\cdot{\rm poly}(n)
.

At a high level, our algorithm follows the blueprint of Bandyapadhyay and Chen
4
, but introduces key new ingredients. Their approach first computes an unconstrained clustering, then expands the clusters, and finally merges them to obtain feasibility. In other words, it constructs an unconstrained solution and subsequently
projects
it onto a feasible one, incurring losses in both steps.
In contrast, our algorithm operates entirely within the mergeable-constraints framework, thereby avoiding this projection step altogether. This structural difference, together with a more refined charging scheme and a careful analysis, leads to an improved approximation factor.

Our final result further improves the approximation to
(2+\varepsilon)
, assuming access to an assignment subroutine for the underlying constraint. Such a subroutine takes a set of at most
k
balls and assigns each point to the center of a ball containing it, while respecting the constraint, provided such an assignment exists.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose there exists an assignment subroutine
\mathcal{A}_{A}
with running time
T(\mathcal{A}_{A})
.
Then there is an algorithm that computes a
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation for
k
-MSR under a mergeable constraint in time
2^{O(k\log(k/\varepsilon))}\cdot n^{2}\cdot T(\mathcal{A}_{A})

This result matches the best known
FPT

k
-MSR

(\alpha,\beta)
-fair

Corollary 4

There is an algorithm for

Hardness implications and limitations of prior reductions.

Both of our hardness results—
\textsc{W}[2]
-hardness and the non-existence of an EPAS—extend to the mergeable-constraint setting, since the unconstrained version trivially satisfies the mergeability property. A result of
4
claims to rule out algorithms with running time
f(k)\,n^{o(k)}
for fair-representational
k
-MSR, which would in turn imply such hardness for the broader mergeable-constraint setting.
However, we observe that the reduction in
4
contains a subtle flaw that invalidates the claimed hardness. In this light, our result not only restores the intended hardness conclusion but also strengthens it by ruling out an EPAS. See

Further Related Work

Polynomial-time regime.

Behsaz and Salavatipour
8
showed that
k
-MSR can be solved exactly in polynomial time on unweighted graphs, provided that zero-radius clusters are disallowed. In Euclidean metrics of constant dimension,
30
gave a polynomial-time exact algorithm, and showed that the problem is
NP
-hard in metrics of constant doubling dimension. They further established
NP
-hardness for shortest-path metrics induced by weighted planar graphs, while also presenting a QPTAS for general metrics. Moreover, even when the locations of the optimal centers are known, computing a valid assignment of points to centers remains
NP
-hard
30
;
23
. For
k
-MSR under pairwise fair constraints,
41
recently obtained an
O(1)
-approximation in polynomial time.

FPT
regime.

For (unconstrained)
k
-MSR,
6
showed an EPAS in Euclidean metrics. When parameterizing by the treewidth of the underlying metric graph,
22
obtained an
XP
-time algorithm.

Chen et al.
16
obtained a
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation for
k
-MSR in general metrics. Their approach can be viewed as an extension of the classical algorithm of
35
for
k
-center: repeatedly selecting a point among the remaining points, guessing the radius of its optimal cluster, and removing all points within twice that radius. They also extend this approach to fair and matroid constraints, incurring an additional loss in the approximation factor.

Under capacity constraints, the best known
FPT
-approximation factor is due to
25
. Liu et al.
40
studied an extension of
k
-MSR with outliers, called colorful
k
-MSR, which imposes separate budgets on the number of outliers for each color class, and obtained a
(7+\varepsilon)

FPT
-approximation. Recently,
29
considered
k
-MSR under simultaneous fairness (on centers) and outlier constraints, and presented a
(3+\varepsilon)

FPT
-approximation that extends to objectives defined by monotone symmetric norms of radii, beyond the sum-of-radii objective.

For mergeable constraints,
22
designed an EPAS for
k
-MSR in Euclidean metrics of arbitrary dimension. This was subsequently extended to metrics of constant doubling dimension by
7
.

4.1
Limitations of Previous Hardness Result

We briefly explain the issue with the lower-bound construction of Bandyapadhyay and Chen
4
. Their reduction is from Dominating Set. Given a graph
G
, the construction augments
G
by adding two sets of
k
vertices and connecting each such vertex to every vertex of
G
. We refer to these as
auxiliary vertex sets
, and to their elements as
auxiliary vertices
.
The groups are defined as follows: one group for each auxiliary vertex set and one group corresponding to the original vertex set of
G
. The proportions are chosen so that, for each auxiliary group,
\alpha_{i}=1/(n+3)
and
\beta_{i}=1
, while for the original group,
\alpha=1/3
and
\beta=1
, where
n
denotes the total number of vertices in the constructed
k
-MSR instance. The metric is given by the shortest-path distances in the resulting graph.

In the yes-instance, there is a feasible
k
-MSR solution of cost
k
, obtained by selecting the vertices corresponding to a dominating set of
G
. For the no-instance, the argument in
4
relies on the observation that singleton clusters violate the fairness constraints, and therefore concludes that zero-radius clusters cannot occur. This is then used to argue that any feasible solution to
k
-MSR can be used to construct a dominating set of size
k
.

The issue is that, while singleton clusters are indeed infeasible, this does not preclude the existence of zero-radius clusters. In particular, empty clusters satisfy the fairness constraints and have zero radius. Exploiting this, one can construct a low-cost solution as follows: choose a center from one of the auxiliary sets, which—via the added edges—covers all points within distance
2
. This forms a large cluster that satisfies the fairness constraints. The remaining
k-1
clusters can then be opened at arbitrary vertices but left empty (their centers are assigned to the large cluster). The resulting solution remains feasible and has total cost at most
2
, contradicting the intended hardness gap.

More generally, this highlights a limitation of reductions that introduce auxiliary vertices enabling
shortcuts
in the metric. Under mergeable constraints, such constructions can collapse into a single large cluster, while the remaining clusters can be left empty without violating feasibility, thereby yielding artificially low-cost solutions.

d(p_{C},q)\leq R_{i^{*}}+2{\rm cost}(\mu^{\prime}-\mu_{i^{*}})\leq\mathcal{R}_{C},

as desired.
∎
Now we use
4
to finish the proof of the lemma as follows. For every component
C
, pick the center
s_{C}
as the point that minimizes its maximum distance to any other point in
X_{C}
, and assign all the points in
X_{C}
to
s_{C}
. First note that due to the choice of
s_{C}
, we have that,
\max_{q\in X_{C}}d(s_{C},q)\leq\max_{q\in X_{C}}d(p_{C},q)\leq\tfrac{4}{3}\mathcal{R}_{C}
, due to
4
. Let
(S,\sigma)
denote the resulting solution.
Moreover, since the balls across the components are disjoint, we have that

{\rm cost}((S,\sigma))=\sum_{\text{component }C}{\rm rad}(c_{C})\leq\sum_{\text{component }C}\tfrac{4}{3}\mathcal{R}_{C}=\tfrac{4}{3}{\rm cost}(\mathcal{F}).

Now, suppose
\mathcal{F}
is a
\Pi^{*}
-feasible cover for some optimal clustering
\Pi^{*}
to
\mathcal{I}
. Then there exists a ball
B_{j}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
\Pi_{i}^{*}\subseteq B_{j}
. Now consider any other ball
B_{j^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{F}
that intersects
\Pi_{i}^{*}
. Since
\Pi_{i}^{*}\subseteq B_{j}
, we have
B_{j^{\prime}}\cap B_{j}\neq\emptyset
, and hence
B_{j^{\prime}}
is adjacent to
B_{j}
in
G
. Therefore, all balls that intersect
\Pi_{i}^{*}
lie in the same connected component of
G
. In particular, all points of
\Pi_{i}^{*}
are contained in the union of balls of a single component.
This implies that no optimal cluster is split across multiple components. Hence, merging all points within a component corresponds to merging entire optimal clusters, and by mergeability of the constraint, this yields a feasible cluster.
