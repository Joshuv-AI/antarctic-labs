Mehler formula for Wronskians of Hermite polynomials
Alexey Kuznetsov, Minjian Yuan

Abstract
We prove that the bilinear generating function for Wronskians of Hermite polynomials can be expressed as the classical Mehler kernel multiplied by a polynomial, thereby extending the result of Pupasov-Maksimov [ 16 ] for exceptional Hermite polynomials. We establish several properties of the polynomials appearing in this extended version of the Mehler formula and present four conjectures about them.



Keywords
: Wronskian, Hermite polynomials, Mehler formula, heat kernel, Darboux transformation

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
: Primary 26C05, Secondary 33C45

1
Introduction and main results

Let
H_{n}(x)
denote the Hermite polynomials.
It is known (see
[
5
, formula (18.18.28)]
) that for all
|z|<1
and
x,y\in{\mathbb{C}}

E(z,x,y):=\sum\limits_{n\geqslant 0}z^{n}\frac{H_{n}(x)H_{n}(y)}{2^{n}n!}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-z^{2}}}\exp\bigg(\frac{2xzy-z^{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})}{(1-z^{2})}\bigg).

The expression on the right-hand side is called the Mehler kernel, and it plays a fundamental role in many applications. For example, it is used to construct the kernel of the fractional Fourier transform
[
14
]
, the propagator for the harmonic oscillator in quantum mechanics
[
16
]
, and the transition probability density of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
[
3
]
.

Before stating our results, we introduce some notation. We denote by
{\mathbb{N}}
the set of positive integers and by
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}
the set of nonnegative integers. The Wronskian of smooth functions
\{f_{j}(x)\}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m}
is defined as

\textnormal{Wr}[f_{1},\dots,f_{m}]:=\det\,[\partial_{x}^{i-1}f_{j}(x)]_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant m}.

For an
m
-tuple
\textbf{{j}}=(j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{m})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}^{m}
we denote

H_{\textbf{{j}}}(x):=\textnormal{Wr}[H_{j_{1}}(x),\dots,H_{j_{m}}(x)].

We set
H_{\textbf{{j}}}(x)\equiv 1
if
{\mathbf{j}}=\varnothing
(the empty tuple).
In what follows,
\textbf{{k}}=(k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{l})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{l}
will always denote a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers, and
l
will denote the length of the sequence
k
. For
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}
we denote
H_{\textbf{{k}},n}(x):=H_{\textbf{{j}}}(x)
where
\textbf{{j}}=(k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{l},n)
.

Wronskians of Hermite polynomials are important objects that arise in several contexts. They play a fundamental role in the classification of rational monodromy-free Schrödinger operators with rational potentials having quadratic growth at infinity
[
15
]
. They appear as rational solutions of the fourth Painlevé equation
[
10
]
. Zeros of Wronskians of Hermite polynomials were studied in
[
6
]
. It is known
[
1
]
that
H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)
has no real zeros if and only if
k
is a
Krein-Adler sequence
, that is, a strictly increasing sequence
\textbf{{k}}=(k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{l})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{l}
such that
l
is even and
k_{2i}=k_{2i-1}+1
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant l/2
.

We now define our main object of interest:

E_{\textbf{{k}}}(z,x,y):=\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}\setminus\textbf{{k}}}z^{n}\frac{H_{\textbf{{k}},n}(x)H_{\textbf{{k}},n}(y)}{2^{n+l}n!\prod\limits_{j=1}^{l}(n-k_{j})}.

(1)

As we will show later in Lemma
1
, the series in (
1
) converges for
|z|<1
,
x,y\in{\mathbb{C}}
and defines an analytic function of three variables in that domain. Our main result is the following

Theorem 1
.

For any strictly increasing sequence
\textbf{{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{l}
there exist polynomials
Q^{\textbf{{k}}}_{n}(x,y)
,
0\leqslant n\leqslant k_{l}+1
such that

E_{\textbf{{k}}}(z,x,y)=E(z,x,y)\sum\limits_{n=0}^{k_{l}+1}z^{n}Q^{\textbf{{k}}}_{n}(x,y),

(2)

for all
|z|<1
,
x,y\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

Theorem
1
was established by A. M. Pupasov-Maksimov
[
16
]
in the important case where
k
is a Krein-Adler sequence. To explain the significance of this result and the role that Krein-Adler sequences play, let us consider the functions

f_{\textbf{{k}},n}(x):=e^{-x^{2}/2}\frac{H_{\textbf{{k}},n}(x)}{H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)}.

(3)

It is known that for every strictly increasing sequence
\textbf{{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{l}
these functions
are solutions of the second-order linear differential equation

{\mathcal{L}}f(x):=-f^{\prime\prime}(x)+U_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)f(x)=(2n+1-2l)f(x),

(4)

where
U_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)
is a rational function defined by

U_{\textbf{{k}}}(x):=x^{2}-2\partial_{x}^{2}\ln(H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x))=x^{2}-2\frac{H_{\textbf{{k}}}^{\prime\prime}(x)}{H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)}+2\frac{H_{\textbf{{k}}}^{\prime}(x)^{2}}{H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)^{2}}.

This result follows from
[
8
, Proposition 5.2]
and it is stated in this exact form in the proof of
[
8
, Proposition 5.5]
. The differential operator in (
4
) and the solutions of equation (
4
) can be obtained by a sequence of Darboux transformations, see
[
15
, Theorem 3]
and
[
8
]
.

As mentioned above, when
k
is a Krein-Adler sequence, the polynomial
H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)
has no real zeros (see
[
8
, Theorem 4.2]
) and the polynomials
H_{\textbf{{k}},n}(x)
are called
exceptional Hermite polynomials
. In this case, the functions
f_{\textbf{{k}},n}(x)
belong to the Schwartz class (the class of smooth rapidly decreasing functions on
{\mathbb{R}}
) and satisfy the orthogonality condition

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f_{\textbf{{k}},n}(x)f_{\textbf{{k}},m}(x){\textnormal{d}}x=\delta_{n,m}\sqrt{\pi}2^{n+l}n!\prod_{j=1}^{l}(n-k_{j}),\;\;\;n,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}\setminus\textbf{{k}}.

Moreover, the functions
\{f_{\textbf{{k}},n}(x)\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}\setminus\textbf{{k}}}
form a complete orthogonal basis of
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\textnormal{d}}x)
. These results can be found in
[
8
,
11
]
. Thus, when
k
is a Krein-Adler sequence, the functions
\{f_{\textbf{{k}},n}(x)\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}\setminus\textbf{{k}}}
form a complete eigenbasis for the operator
{\mathcal{L}}
in
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\textnormal{d}}x)
. This allows one to write down the spectral expansion of the corresponding heat kernel
[
2
,
19
]
(the integral kernel of the operator
\exp(-t{\mathcal{L}})
) in the form

p(t,x,y)=\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}\setminus\textbf{{k}}}e^{-(2n+1-2l)t}\;\frac{f_{\textbf{{k}},n}(x)f_{\textbf{{k}},n}(y)}{\lVert f_{\textbf{{k}},n}\rVert^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{(2l-1)t-(x^{2}+y^{2})/2}\;\frac{E_{\textbf{{k}}}(e^{-2t},x,y)}{H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)H_{\textbf{{k}}}(y)}.

(5)

Thus, the significance of Theorem
1
in the Krein-Adler case is that it gives an explicit expression for the heat kernel of the Schrödinger operator
{\mathcal{L}}
.

Remark 1:
Pupasov-Maksimov
[
16
]
states his results using the probabilists’ version of Hermite polynomials, defined by
{\textnormal{He}}_{n}(x):=2^{-n/2}H_{n}(x/\sqrt{2})
. Denoting the polynomials appearing in
[
16
]
by
\widetilde{Q}_{n}^{\textbf{{k}}}(x,y)
, the relation between these two families of polynomials is

\widetilde{Q}_{n}^{\textbf{{k}}}(x,y)=C\times Q_{n}^{\textbf{{k}}}(x/\sqrt{2},y/\sqrt{2}),

where
C
is a normalization constant that depends only on
k
.

In the following three propositions, we state several properties of the polynomials
Q_{n}^{\textbf{{k}}}(x,y)
.

Proposition 1
.

(i)

For
n=0,1,\dots,k_{l}+1
the polynomials
Q_{n}^{\textbf{{k}}}(x,y)
satisfy

Q^{\textbf{{k}}}_{n}(x,y)=Q_{n}^{\textbf{{k}}}(y,x)=Q^{\textbf{{k}}}_{n}(-x,-y)=(-1)^{\kappa+n}Q^{\textbf{{k}}}_{n}(-x,y),

where

\kappa:=k_{1}+k_{2}+\dots+k_{l}-l(l+1)/2
.

(ii)

We have

Q^{\textbf{{k}}}_{0}(x,y)=(-2)^{l}\bigg[\prod\limits_{j=1}^{l}k_{j}\bigg]\times H_{\textbf{{k}}-1}(x)H_{\textbf{{k}}-1}(y),

(6)

where
\textbf{{k}}-1:=(k_{1}-1,\dots,k_{l}-1)
.

(iii)

For
n=1,2,\dots,k_{l}+1

the polynomials
Q^{\textbf{{k}}}_{n}(x,y)
can be computed recursively via

Q^{\textbf{{k}}}_{n}(x,y)={\mathbf{1}}_{\{n\notin\textbf{{k}}\}}\frac{H_{\textbf{{k}},n}(x)H_{\textbf{{k}},n}(y)}{2^{n+l}n!\prod_{j=1}^{l}(n-k_{j})}-\sum\limits_{m=1}^{n}\frac{H_{m}(x)H_{m}(y)}{2^{m}m!}Q^{\textbf{{k}}}_{n-m}(x,y).

(7)

The parity conditions and formula
(
7
) were established in
[
16
]
for Krein-Adler sequences
k
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\textbf{{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{l}
be a Krein-Adler sequence. Denote

\displaystyle\Omega(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{y-x}\Big(\frac{H^{\prime}_{\textbf{{k}}}(y)}{H_{\textbf{{k}}}(y)}-\frac{H^{\prime}_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)}{H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)}\Big),

\displaystyle\omega(x)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{y\to x}\Omega(x,y)=\frac{H_{\textbf{{k}}}^{\prime\prime}(x)}{H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)}-\frac{H_{\textbf{{k}}}^{\prime}(x)^{2}}{H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)^{2}}.

For every
x,y\in{\mathbb{C}}
, the following identities hold:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=0}^{k_{l}+1}Q^{\textbf{{k}}}_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)H_{\textbf{{k}}}(y),

(8)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=0}^{k_{l}+1}nQ_{n}^{\textbf{{k}}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=-H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)H_{\textbf{{k}}}(y)\big(\Omega(x,y)-l\big),

(9)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=0}^{k_{l}+1}n^{2}Q_{n}^{\textbf{{k}}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)H_{\textbf{{k}}}(y)\times\bigg((\Omega(x,y)-l)^{2}+\frac{\omega(x)+\omega(y)-2\Omega(x,y)}{(y-x)^{2}}\bigg).

(10)

Formula (
8
) for general
x
and
y
and formula (
9
) for the special case
x=y
were first established in
[
16
]
.

Proposition 3
.

Let
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\textbf{{k}}=(k)
, so that
l=1
. Then

Q_{n}^{\textbf{{k}}}(x,y)={\mathbf{1}}_{\{n\geqslant 1\}}2^{n-1}(k)_{n-1}H_{k+1-n}(x)H_{k+1-n}(y)-2^{n+1}(k)_{n+1}H_{k-1-n}(x)H_{k-1-n}(y),

(11)

where
(r)_{n}:=r(r-1)\dots(r-n+1)
denotes the falling factorial.

We also state several conjectures, which we have verified numerically for many cases of strictly increasing sequences
\textbf{{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{l}
.

Conjecture 1:
Identities (
8
)-(
10
) hold for any strictly increasing sequence
\textbf{{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{l}
, not only for Krein-Adler sequences.

Conjecture 2:

For
l\geqslant 2
and any strictly increasing sequence
\textbf{{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{l}
, the following identity holds:

Q^{\textbf{{k}}}_{k_{l}+1}(x,y)=2^{k_{l}+l-1}(k_{l})!\bigg[\prod\limits_{j=1}^{l-1}(k_{l}-k_{j})\bigg]\times H_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(x)H_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(y),

where
\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}=(k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{l-1})
.

Conjecture 3:
When
l=2
and
k
is a Krein-Adler sequence
\textbf{{k}}=[k,k+1]
(with
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
), then

\displaystyle Q^{\textbf{{k}}}_{k+1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=2^{k+1}k!k\Big(H_{k+1}(x)H_{k+1}(y)+2(k+1)\big[H_{k+1}(x)H_{k-1}(y)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+H_{k+1}(y)H_{k-1}(x)\big]+4k(k+1)H_{k-1}(x)H_{k-1}(y)\Big).

Conjecture 4:

The polynomials
Q^{\textbf{{k}}}_{n}(x,y)
have integer coefficients. A stronger conjecture is that the coefficients of the polynomials
Q^{\textbf{{k}}}_{n}(x/2,y/2)
are integers that are divisible by

\prod\limits_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant l}(k_{i}-k_{j})^{2}.

Foata
[
7
]
gave a combinatorial proof of the classical Mehler formula. If true, Conjecture 4 may indicate that Theorem
1
could be proved by combinatorial methods and may suggest a potential combinatorial interpretation of the polynomials
Q_{n}^{\textbf{{k}}}(x,y)
.

We present the proofs of all our results in the next section.
Our proof of Theorem
1
follows the same general strategy as the proof of Pupasov-Maksimov
[
16
]
in the case of Krein-Adler sequences. However, the Krein-Adler condition, which implies the orthogonality and completeness of

in

[

Proofs

We denote by
\psi_{n}(x):=e^{-x^{2}/2}H_{n}(x)
the non-normalized Hermite functions. If

H_{n}(x)\equiv 0

\psi_{n}(x)\equiv 0

\textbf{{j}}=(j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{m})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}^{m}

|\,\textbf{{j}}\,|:=j_{1}+\dots+j_{m}.

For a strictly increasing sequence
\textbf{{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{l}

\textbf{{j}}=(k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{l},n)

\textnormal{Wr}[f(x)g_{1}(x),f(x)g_{2}(x)\dots,f(x)g_{m}(x)]=f(x)^{m}\textnormal{Wr}[g_{1}(x),g_{2}(x)\dots,g_{m}(x)],

(12)

\psi_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)=e^{-lx^{2}/2}H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x),\;\;\;\psi_{\textbf{{k}},n}(x)=e^{-(l+1)x^{2}/2}H_{\textbf{{k}},n}(x).

(13)

In what follows, we work with the bilinear generating functions of
\psi_{n}
and
\psi_{\textbf{{k}},n}

{\mathcal{E}}(z,x,y):=\sum\limits_{n\geqslant 0}z^{n}\frac{\psi_{n}(x)\psi_{n}(y)}{2^{n}n!}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-z^{2}}}\exp\bigg(\frac{4xyz-(1+z^{2})(x^{2}+y^{2})}{2(1-z^{2})}\bigg),

(14)

and

{\mathcal{E}}_{\textbf{{k}}}(z,x,y):=\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}\setminus\textbf{{k}}}z^{n}\frac{\psi_{\textbf{{k}},n}(x)\psi_{\textbf{{k}},n}(y)}{2^{n+l}n!\prod\limits_{j=1}^{l}(n-k_{j})}.

(15)

From (
13
), we have

{\mathcal{E}}(z,x,y)=e^{-(x^{2}+y^{2})/2}E(z,x,y),\;\;\;{\mathcal{E}}_{\textbf{{k}}}(z,x,y)=e^{-(l+1)(x^{2}+y^{2})/2}E_{\textbf{{k}}}(z,x,y).

(16)

Lemma 1
.

(i)

There exists
C=C(\textbf{{k}})>0

(17)

(ii)

The function

(iii)

Proof.

To prove (i), we start with the formula

\psi_{\textbf{{k}},n}(x)=e^{-(l+1)x^{2}/2}\textnormal{Wr}[H_{k_{1}}(x),\dots,H_{k_{l}}(x),H_{n}(x)],

:

\psi_{\textbf{{k}},n}(x)=e^{-(l+1)x^{2}/2}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l}p_{j}(x)\partial_{x}^{j}H_{n}(x),

where

p_{j}(x)=(-1)^{j+l}{\textnormal{det}}\Big[\partial_{x}^{m}H_{k_{i}}(x)\Big]_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant l\\

m\neq j\end{subarray}}.

It is clear from the above determinant expression that

is a polynomial of degree not exceeding
|\,\textbf{{k}}\,|
. This, combined with the formula

\partial_{x}^{j}H_{m}(x)=2^{j}m(m-1)\dots(m-j+1)H_{m-j}(x),

The proof of item (ii) requires the following asymptotic result (see

]

and therefore

\int_{-\infty}^{y}{\mathcal{E}}_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(z,x,u)\frac{\psi_{\textbf{{k}}}(u)}{\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(u)^{2}}{\textnormal{d}}u=-\frac{1}{\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(y)}\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}\setminus\textbf{{k}}}z^{n}\frac{\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}},n}(x)\psi_{\textbf{{k}},n}(y)}{2^{n+l}n!\prod\limits_{j=1}^{l}(n-k_{j})}+z^{k_{l}}\psi_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)G(y),

for some function
G(y)
.
Finally, applying identity (
22
) once again and noting that
{\mathcal{L}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}\psi_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)\equiv 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle-\frac{\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(y)}{\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(x)}{\mathcal{L}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}\int_{-\infty}^{y}{\mathcal{E}}_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(z,x,u)\frac{\psi_{\textbf{{k}}}(u)}{\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(u)^{2}}{\textnormal{d}}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(x)}\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}\setminus\textbf{{k}}}z^{n}\frac{{\mathcal{L}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}},n}(x)\psi_{\textbf{{k}},n}(y)}{2^{n+l}n!\prod\limits_{j=1}^{l}(n-k_{j})}

(25)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}\setminus\textbf{{k}}}z^{n}\frac{\psi_{\textbf{{k}},n}(x)\psi_{\textbf{{k}},n}(y)}{2^{n+l}n!\prod\limits_{j=1}^{l}(n-k_{j})}={\mathcal{E}}_{\textbf{{k}}}(z,x,y).

In the above derivation of formula (
23
), we interchanged summation and differentiation/integration. Let us first justify the interchange of summation and integration in the first step of (
24
). Consider the function
f_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}},k_{l}}(u)f_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}},n}(u)
, which we rewrite in the form

f_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}},k_{l}}(u)f_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}},n}(u)=\Big[e^{-u^{2}/2}\frac{H_{\textbf{{k}}}(u)}{H_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(u)^{2}}\Big]\times\Big[e^{(l-1)u^{2}/2}\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}},n}(u)\Big].

The above factorization follows from (
3
) and (
13
).
The function
e^{-u^{2}/4}H_{\textbf{{k}}}(u)/H_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(u)^{2}
is bounded by some constant
A=A(\textbf{{k}})
on
(-\infty,c]
(since
H_{\textbf{{k}}}(u)
and
H_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(u)
are polynomials and
H_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(u)
is non-zero on
(-\infty,c]
). Thus,

g(u):=e^{-u^{2}/2}H_{\textbf{{k}}}(u)/H_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(u)^{2}
satisfies
|g(u)|<Ae^{-u^{2}/4}
for
u\in(-\infty,c]
.
From (
17
) we conclude that there exists a constant
B=B(\tilde{\textbf{{k}}})
such that

|e^{(l-1)u^{2}/2}\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}},n}(u)|<Bn^{l-1}(1+|u|^{M})\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l-1}|\psi_{n-j}(u)|,\;\;\;u\in{\mathbb{R}},

where
M=|\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}|
.
Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and using the fact that
\lVert\psi_{n}\rVert^{2}=\sqrt{\pi}2^{n}n!
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{y}|f_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}},k_{l}}(u)f_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}},n}(u)|{\textnormal{d}}u

\displaystyle\leqslant ABn^{l-1}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l-1}\int_{-\infty}^{c}(1+|u|^{M})e^{-u^{2}/4}\;|\psi_{n-j}(u)|\,{\textnormal{d}}u

\displaystyle\leqslant ABn^{l-1}\Big[\int_{-\infty}^{c}(1+|u|^{M})^{2}e^{-u^{2}/2}{\textnormal{d}}u\Big]^{1/2}\times\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l-1}\lVert\psi_{n-j}\rVert\leqslant Cln^{l-1}\sqrt{2^{n}n!},

for some
C=C(\textbf{{k}})>0
.
Using the above estimate and the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
1
, we conclude that the series

\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}\setminus\textbf{{k}}}r^{n}\frac{|\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}},n}(x)|}{2^{n+l-1}n!\prod\limits_{j=1}^{l-1}(n-k_{j})}\int_{-\infty}^{y}|f_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}},k_{l}}(u)f_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}},n}(u)|{\textnormal{d}}u

converges for all
r\in(0,1)
,
x\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
y\in(-\infty,c]
. Thus, we can apply Fubini’s Theorem and interchange summation and integration in (
24
).

To justify the interchange of summation and differentiation in the first step of (
25
), we use the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
1
and conclude that the series

\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}\setminus\textbf{{k}}}z^{n}\frac{\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}},n}(x)\psi_{\textbf{{k}},n}(y)}{2^{n+l}n!\prod\limits_{j=1}^{l}(n-k_{j})}

converges uniformly on compact subsets of
\{(z,x,y)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{3}\;:\;|z|<1\}
. Hence, it defines an analytic function on this set, and the infinite series can be differentiated term-by-term.

\sqcap\kern-8.0pt\hbox{$\sqcup$}

For

|z|<1

Lemma 4
.

The function
I_{n}(z,x,y)
extends to an analytic function in the domain
\{(z,x,y)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{3}\;:\;|z|<1\}
and it satisfies

I_{n}(z,x,y)=z^{n}\psi_{n}(x)\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\big(1+{\textnormal{erf}}(w)\big)-e^{-x^{2}/2-w^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\zeta^{i}z^{n-i}H_{i-1}(w)H_{n-i}(x),

(27)

where
\zeta:=\sqrt{1-z^{2}}
and
w:=(y-xz)/\zeta
.

Proof.

We first observe that
{\mathcal{E}}(z,x,y)
defined in (
14
) can be written in the form

{\mathcal{E}}(z,x,y)=\zeta^{-1}e^{y^{2}/2-x^{2}/2-(y-xz)^{2}/\zeta^{2}}.

Assume that
z\in(-1,1)
and
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Using the above expression for
{\mathcal{E}}(z,x,y)
, we rewrite (
26

I_{n}(z,x,y)=\zeta^{-1}e^{-x^{2}/2}\int_{-\infty}^{y}e^{-(u-xz)^{2}/\zeta^{2}}H_{n}(u){\textnormal{d}}u.

We change the variable of integration to
v=(u-xz)/\zeta
and obtain

I_{n}(z,x,y)=e^{-x^{2}/2}\int_{-\infty}^{w}e^{-v^{2}}H_{n}(\zeta v+xz){\textnormal{d}}v.

Noting that
\zeta^{2}+z^{2}=1
, we apply the addition formula for Hermite polynomials (see
[
5
, formula 18.18.11]
)

H_{n}(\zeta v+xz)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\zeta^{i}z^{n-i}H_{i}(v)H_{n-i}(x).

The desired result (

) follows from the above two equations and the following formulas:

\int_{-\infty}^{w}e^{-v^{2}}{\textnormal{d}}v=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\big(1+{\textnormal{erf}}(w)\big),\;\;\;\int_{-\infty}^{w}e^{-v^{2}}H_{i}(v){\textnormal{d}}v=-e^{-w^{2}}H_{i-1}(w),

(28)

which can be found in
[
5
, §7.2]
and
[
12
, eqn. (7.373)]
.

Now that we have established (
27
) for
z\in(-1,1)
,
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}

\{(z,x,y)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{3}\;:\;|z|<1\}

can be extended in this domain by analytic continuation.

\sqcap\kern-8.0pt\hbox{$\sqcup$}

We introduce the
l
-th order linear differential operator

{\mathcal{M}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}
, acting on the
x

{\mathcal{M}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}h(x):=\textnormal{Wr}[\psi_{k_{1}},\dots,\psi_{k_{l}},h](x),

(29)

[

[

k
.

Lemma 5
.

For
|z|<1
,
x,y\in{\mathbb{C}}
we have

{\mathcal{E}}_{\textbf{{k}}}(z,x,y)={\mathcal{M}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}(-1)^{j+l-1}\psi_{\textbf{{k}}\setminus k_{j}}(y)I_{k_{j}}(z,x,y).

(30)

Proof.

The proof proceeds by induction on
l
. The case
l=1
and
\textbf{{k}}=(k)
is covered by Lemma
3
, followed by analytic continuation in
x
and
y
.
Assume now that
l\geqslant 2
and that for
\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}=(k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{l-1})
we have

{\mathcal{E}}_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(z,x,y)={\mathcal{M}}_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}},x}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l-1}(-1)^{j+l-2}\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}\setminus k_{j}}(y)I_{k_{j}}(z,x,y),

with the above identity valid for all
z\in(-1,1)
and
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
We substitute the above expression for
{\mathcal{E}}_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(z,x,y)
into the right-hand side of (
23
) and conclude that there exists
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that for
z\in(-1,1)
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and

{\mathcal{E}}_{\textbf{{k}}}(z,x,y)=\frac{\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(y)}{\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(x)}{\mathcal{L}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}{\mathcal{M}}_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}},x}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l-1}(-1)^{j+l-1}\int_{-\infty}^{y}\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}\setminus k_{j}}(u)I_{k_{j}}(z,x,u)\frac{\psi_{\textbf{{k}}}(u)}{\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(u)^{2}}{\textnormal{d}}u.

Using the Wronskian identity (
21
), we check that

\frac{{\textnormal{d}}}{{\textnormal{d}}u}\frac{\psi_{\textbf{{k}}\setminus k_{j}}(u)}{\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(u)^{2}}=\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}\setminus k_{j}}(u)\frac{\psi_{\textbf{{k}}}(u)}{\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(u)^{2}}.

Note that
\psi_{\textbf{{k}}\setminus k_{j}}(u)/\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(u)
is a rational function of

(this follows from (
12

z\in(-1,1)

\zeta>0

27

\lim\limits_{u\to-\infty}I_{k_{j}}(z,x,u)\frac{\psi_{\textbf{{k}}\setminus k_{j}}(u)}{\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(u)}=0.

Using the above two results and integrating by parts, we obtain

\int_{-\infty}^{y}\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}\setminus k_{j}}(u)I_{k_{j}}(z,x,u)\frac{\psi_{\textbf{{k}}}(u)}{\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(u)^{2}}{\textnormal{d}}u=I_{k_{j}}(z,x,y)\frac{\psi_{\textbf{{k}}\setminus k_{j}}(y)}{\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(y)}-\int_{-\infty}^{y}{\mathcal{E}}(z,x,u)\psi_{k_{j}}(u)\frac{\psi_{\textbf{{k}}\setminus k_{j}}(u)}{\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(u)}{\textnormal{d}}u.

(32)

Next, we use the identity

0=\det\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{k_{1}}(u)&\psi_{k_{2}}(u)&\cdots&\psi_{k_{l}}(u)\\
\psi_{k_{1}}(u)&\psi_{k_{2}}(u)&\cdots&\psi_{k_{l}}(u)\\
\psi_{k_{1}}^{\prime}(u)&\psi_{k_{2}}^{\prime}(u)&\cdots&\psi_{k_{l}}^{\prime}(u)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\psi_{k_{1}}^{(l-2)}(u)&\psi_{k_{2}}^{(l-2)}(u)&\cdots&\psi_{k_{l}}^{(l-2)}(u)\end{bmatrix}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}(-1)^{j-1}\psi_{k_{j}}(u)\times\psi_{\textbf{{k}}\setminus k_{j}}(u),

where we have expanded the above determinant along the first row.
Therefore,

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l-1}(-1)^{j-1}\psi_{k_{j}}(u)\psi_{\textbf{{k}}\setminus k_{j}}(u)=(-1)^{l}\psi_{k_{l}}(u)\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(u).

Combining the above result with (
31
) and (

)
we arrive at

{\mathcal{E}}_{\textbf{{k}}}(z,x,y)=\frac{1}{\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(x)}{\mathcal{L}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}{\mathcal{M}}_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}},x}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}(-1)^{j+l-1}\psi_{\textbf{{k}}\setminus k_{j}}(y)I_{k_{j}}(z,x,y).

From the Wronskian identity (
21
) it follows that

{\mathcal{L}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}{\mathcal{M}}_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}},x}h(x)=\psi_{\tilde{\textbf{{k}}}}(x){\mathcal{M}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}h(x),

for any entire function
h
. Thus, we have proved that for
z\in(-1,1)
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and

we have

{\mathcal{E}}_{\textbf{{k}}}(z,x,y)={\mathcal{M}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}(-1)^{j+l-1}\psi_{\textbf{{k}}\setminus k_{j}}(y)I_{k_{j}}(z,x,y).

By analytic continuation, the above identity holds everywhere in the domain
\{(z,x,y)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{3}\;:\;|z|<1\}
.
This completes the induction step.

Corollary 1
.

For
l\geqslant 2
,
|z|<1
,
x\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
y\in{\mathbb{R}}

{\mathcal{E}}_{\textbf{{k}}}(z,x,y)={\mathcal{M}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}\int_{-\infty}^{y}{\mathcal{E}}(z,x,u)e^{-u^{2}/2}P_{\textbf{{k}}}(u,y){\textnormal{d}}u,

where
P_{\textbf{{k}}}(u,y)
is a polynomial in
u
that satisfies
\partial_{u}^{j}P_{\textbf{{k}}}(u,y)|_{u=y}=0
for all
j=0,1,\dots,l-2
.

Proof.

Formula (
33
) is equivalent to (
30
) with
P_{\textbf{{k}}}(u,y)
defined by

e^{-u^{2}/2}P_{\textbf{{k}}}(u,y)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}(-1)^{j+l-1}\psi_{k_{j}}(u)\psi_{\textbf{{k}}\setminus k_{j}}(y)=(-1)^{l}\det\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{k_{1}}(u)&\psi_{k_{2}}(u)&\cdots&\psi_{k_{l}}(u)\\

\psi_{k_{1}}^{\prime}(y)&\psi_{k_{2}}^{\prime}(y)&\cdots&\psi_{k_{l}}^{\prime}(y)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\psi_{k_{1}}^{(l-2)}(y)&\psi_{k_{2}}^{(l-2)}(y)&\cdots&\psi_{k_{l}}^{(l-2)}(y)\end{bmatrix}.

The above equation can be written in the form

P_{\textbf{{k}}}(u,y)=e^{-(l-1)y^{2}/2}(-1)^{l}\det\begin{bmatrix}H_{k_{1}}(u)&H_{k_{2}}(u)&\cdots&H_{k_{l}}(u)\\
H_{k_{1}}(y)&H_{k_{2}}(y)&\cdots&H_{k_{l}}(y)\\

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
H_{k_{1}}^{(l-2)}(y)&H_{k_{2}}^{(l-2)}(y)&\cdots&H_{k_{l}}^{(l-2)}(y)\end{bmatrix},

which immediately implies

\partial_{u}^{j}P_{\textbf{{k}}}(u,y)|_{u=y}=0
for all
j=0,1,\dots,l-2
.

\sqcap\kern-8.0pt\hbox{$\sqcup$}

Lemma 6
.

Let
m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}
and let
x,y
be fixed real numbers satisfying
x>y
. Let
P(u)
be a polynomial such that
P^{(i)}(y)=0
for
i=0,1,\dots,n
. If
m\leqslant n+1
then

\lim\limits_{z\to 1-}\frac{\partial_{x}^{m}\int_{-\infty}^{y}{\mathcal{E}}(z,x,u)e^{-u^{2}/2}P(u){\textnormal{d}}u}{{\mathcal{E}}(z,x,y)}=0.

(34)

If
m=n+2
, the above limit exists and is finite.

Proof.

First, we check that formula (
14
) can be rewritten in the equivalent form

{\mathcal{E}}(z,x,y)=e^{(x^{2}+y^{2})/2-2xy/(1+z)}\times\zeta^{-1}e^{-(y-x)^{2}/\zeta^{2}},

(35)

where
\zeta=\zeta(z)=\sqrt{1-z^{2}}
. For the rest of this proof, we assume that
1/\sqrt{2}<z<1
, so that
0<\zeta<1/\sqrt{2}
.

We claim that, for any
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}
and any fixed
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
x>y
, we have

\zeta^{-2}e^{(y-x)^{2}/\zeta^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{y}e^{-2xu/(1+z)-(u-x)^{2}/\zeta^{2}}u^{m}{\textnormal{d}}u\to\frac{e^{-xy}y^{m}}{2(x-y)},

(36)

as
z\to 1-
(note that
\zeta\to 0+
as
z\to 1-
).
Indeed, changing the variable of integration
u=y-\zeta^{2}v
, we obtain

\zeta^{-2}e^{(y-x)^{2}/\zeta^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{y}e^{-2xu/(1+z)-(u-x)^{2}/\zeta^{2}}u^{m}{\textnormal{d}}u=e^{-2xy/(1+z)}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2(x-y)v-\zeta^{2}(v^{2}-2xv/(1+z))}(y-\zeta^{2}v)^{m}{\textnormal{d}}v,

and the limit in (
36
) follows by the dominated convergence theorem.

Next, let
q(z,u)
be a polynomial in
u
of the form

q(z,u)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{M}a_{i}(z)u^{i},

(37)

where
M
does not depend on
z
and the coefficients
a_{i}(z)
are continuous in some neighborhood of
z=1
. For
z\in(0,1)
and
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}
we consider

{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}(z,x,y):=\zeta^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{y}\Big[\partial_{u}^{j}e^{-(u-x)^{2}/\zeta^{2}}\Big]\times e^{-2xu/(1+z)}q(z,u)(u-y)^{n+1}{\textnormal{d}}u.

(38)

From (
35
) and (
36
) we conclude that

\frac{{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}(z,x,y)}{{\mathcal{E}}(z,x,y)}\to 0

as
z\to 1-
. When
0<j\leqslant n+1
, we
integrate by parts
j
times
and obtain

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}(z,x,y)

\displaystyle=\zeta^{-1}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{j-1}(-1)^{i}\Big[\partial_{u}^{j-1-i}e^{-(u-x)^{2}/\zeta^{2}}\Big]_{u=y}\times\Big[\partial_{u}^{i}\Big(e^{-2xu/(1+z)}q(z,u)(u-y)^{n+1}\Big)\Big]_{u=y}

(39)

\displaystyle+(-1)^{j}\zeta^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{y}e^{-(u-x)^{2}/\zeta^{2}}\partial_{u}^{j}\Big(e^{-2xu/(1+z)}q(z,u)(u-y)^{n+1}\Big){\textnormal{d}}u.

In the finite sum in the above formula, we have

, and therefore

\Big[\partial_{u}^{i}e^{-2xu/(1+z)}q(z,u)(u-y)^{n+1}\Big]_{u=y}=0.

Thus, the finite sum in (
39
) is equal to zero, and
it follows from (
35
) and (
36
) that, for
0<j\leqslant n+1
,

\frac{{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}(z,x,y)}{{\mathcal{E}}(z,x,y)}\to 0,

as
z\to 1-
.

When
j=n+2
, the preceding argument shows that the terms with
0\leqslant i\leqslant j-2
in the finite sum in (
39
) are all equal to zero, and when
i=j-1=n+1
we have

\Big[\partial_{u}^{n+1}\Big(e^{-2xu/(1+z)}q(z,u)(u-y)^{n+1}\Big)\Big]_{u=y}=e^{-2xy/(1+z)}q(z,y)(n+1)!.

Thus, it follows from
(
35
), (
36
) and
(
39
) that, as
z\to 1-
,

\frac{{\mathcal{Q}}_{n+2}(z,x,y)}{{\mathcal{E}}(z,x,y)}\to(-1)^{n+1}e^{-(x^{2}+y^{2})/2}q(1,y)(n+1)!.

Now we are ready to complete the proof of Lemma

. For any fixed
z\in(1/\sqrt{2},1)
, we interchange the integration and differentiation in the numerator in (
34
),
use the factorization (
35
), and obtain

\partial_{x}^{m}\int_{-\infty}^{y}{\mathcal{E}}(z,x,u)e^{-u^{2}/2}P(u){\textnormal{d}}u=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{j}\binom{m}{j}\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}(z,x,y),

(40)

where we have defined

(41)

When deriving the above formula, we used the fact that

\partial_{x}^{j}e^{-(u-x)^{2}/\zeta^{2}}=(-1)^{j}\partial_{u}^{j}e^{-(u-x)^{2}/\zeta^{2}}.

The interchange of integration and differentiation is justified since, for
z\in(1/\sqrt{2},1)
, we have

; thus, for all
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
the integrand in (

) is dominated by
C\exp(-u^{2})
for some
C>0
, uniformly in
x
on compact subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}
. We note that the conditions

for

imply that

for some polynomial

, which in turn implies

\Big[\partial_{x}^{m-j}e^{x^{2}/2-2xu/(1+z)}\Big]\times P(u)=e^{x^{2}/2-2xu/(1+z)}q_{2}(x,u/(1+z),u)(u-y)^{n+1},

is some polynomial in three variables. The important conclusion is that

) (recall that everywhere in this proof

is fixed, so dependence of the coefficients of the polynomial

on

\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}(z,x,y)
is an integral of the form (

), and using the results about

\lim\limits_{z\to 1-}\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}(z,x,y)}{{\mathcal{E}}(z,x,y)}

exists and is equal to zero for all

, while for

the limit exists and is finite. The desired result now follows from (
40
).

\sqcap\kern-8.0pt\hbox{$\sqcup$}

whose degree in
z
is not greater than
n
.
An expression such as

g(z,x,y)\in H(z,x,y)\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{R}}[x]h_{i}(z,x,y)

should be interpreted as stating that there exist polynomials
P_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]
such that

g(z,x,y)=H(z,x,y)\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}(x)h_{i}(z,x,y)

for all
z,x,y
in the domain of
g
,
H
and

. For example, with this notation we can express (

) in the form

{\mathcal{M}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}h(x)\in e^{-lx^{2}/2}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l}{\mathbb{R}}[x]\,\partial_{x}^{j}h(x).

(43)

As before, we denote
\zeta:=\sqrt{1-z^{2}}
and
w:=(y-xz)/\zeta
. We collect some preliminary facts.

(i)

For all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}

H_{n}(w)=n!\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{j}(2w)^{n-2j}}{j!(n-2j)!}\in\zeta^{-n}{\mathbb{R}}_{n}[z,x,y].

(44)

(ii)

For all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}

\partial_{x}^{n}\,{\textnormal{erf}}(w)=-(z/\zeta)^{n}\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-w^{2}}H_{n-1}(w)\in e^{-w^{2}}\zeta^{1-2n}{\mathbb{R}}_{2n-1}[z,x,y].

(45)

(iii)

For all
n,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0}

\partial_{x}^{m}\Big[e^{-w^{2}}H_{n}(w)\Big]=(z/\zeta)^{m}e^{-w^{2}}H_{n+m}(w).

(46)

The expression for
H_{n}(w)
in item (i) can be found in
[
5
, formula 18.5.13]
. The results in items (ii) and (iii) follow from (
28
) and the fact that
\partial_{x}=(-z/\zeta)\partial_{w}
.

We denote

\displaystyle I^{(1)}_{n}(z,x,y)

\displaystyle:=z^{n}\psi_{n}(x)\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\big(1+{\textnormal{erf}}(w)\big),

\displaystyle I_{n}^{(2)}(z,x,y)

\displaystyle:=-z^{n}e^{-w^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\binom{n}{i}(z/\zeta)^{-i}H_{i-1}(w)\psi_{n-i}(x).

By (
30
), we have

{\mathcal{E}}_{\textbf{{k}}}(z,x,y)\in e^{-(l-1)y^{2}/2}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}{\mathbb{R}}[y]\Big[{\mathcal{M}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}I^{(1)}_{k_{j}}(z,x,y)+{\mathcal{M}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}I^{(2)}_{k_{j}}(z,x,y)\Big].

Using (

) we obtain

{\mathcal{M}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}I^{(1)}_{k_{j}}(z,x,y)\in z^{k_{j}}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\big(1+{\textnormal{erf}}(w)\big){\mathcal{M}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}\psi_{k_{j}}(x)+z^{k_{j}}e^{-lx^{2}/2}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geqslant 0,m\geqslant 1\\

{\mathcal{M}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}
(see (

. We use (

to conclude that

In the last step, we used the fact that for all

which is true since

Similarly, using (

) and (

{\mathcal{M}}_{\textbf{{k}},x}I^{(2)}_{k_{j}}(z,x,y)\in z^{k_{j}}e^{-(l+1)x^{2}/2-w^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k_{j}}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{l}{\mathbb{R}}[x](z/\zeta)^{m-i}H_{m+i-1}(w)\subset e^{-(l+1)x^{2}/2-w^{2}}\zeta^{1-2l}{\mathbb{R}}_{2l-1+k_{j}}[z,x,y].

Thus,

{\mathcal{E}}_{\textbf{{k}}}(z,x,y)\in e^{-(l+1)x^{2}/2-(l-1)y^{2}/2-w^{2}}\zeta^{1-2l}{\mathbb{R}}_{2l-1+k_{l}}[z,x,y].

Using the expression (
35
) and
the above two equations we arrive at

\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{\textbf{{k}}}(z,x,y)}{{\mathcal{E}}(z,x,y)}\in e^{-l(x^{2}+y^{2})/2}(1-z^{2})^{1-l}\,{\mathbb{R}}_{2l-1+k_{l}}[z,x,y].

Thus, we have established that

R(z,x,y):=e^{l(x^{2}+y^{2})/2}\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{\textbf{{k}}}(z,x,y)}{{\mathcal{E}}(z,x,y)}=\frac{P(z,x,y)}{(1-z^{2})^{l-1}},

(47)

where
P(z,x,y)
is a real polynomial in three variables, whose degree in the
z
-variable is at most
2l-1+k_{l}
. We see that
R(z,x,y)
is a rational function of
z
with possible poles only at
z=\pm 1
. Now assume that
x,y
are real and
x>y
. Letting
z\to 1-
and applying Corollary
2
, we conclude that
R(z,x,y)
has a finite limit at
z=1
. This implies that, when
x,y
are real and
x>y
, the polynomial
P(z,x,y)
is divisible by
(1-z)^{l-1}
. This divisibility property holds for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Indeed, consider the Taylor coefficients of the numerator
P(z,x,y)
at
z=1
. These Taylor coefficients are polynomials in
x,y
and the fact that
P(z,x,y)
is divisible by
(1-z)^{l-1}
implies that the first
l-1
coefficients are equal to zero. It is clear that if a polynomial in two variables
x,y
is zero for all real values
x>y
, then it must be equal to zero for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

By the symmetry conditions (
18
), the rational function
R
satisfies
R(z,x,y)=(-1)^{\kappa}R(-z,-x,y)
, which implies that it also has a finite limit as
z\to-1
. Thus, the polynomial
P(z,x,y)
is divisible by
(1-z^{2})^{l-1}
. Since
(1-z^{2})^{l-1}
has degree
2l-2
, and the numerator
P(z,x,y)
has degree in
z
not greater than
2l-1+k_{l}
, we conclude that the function
R(z,x,y)
must be a polynomial in the three variables
z,x,y
, with degree in
z
not greater than
k_{l}+1
.

Thus, we have established that there exists a polynomial
R(z,x,y)
, with degree in
z
not greater than
k_{l}+1
, such that

{\mathcal{E}}_{\textbf{{k}}}(z,x,y)=e^{-l(x^{2}+y^{2})/2}\,{\mathcal{E}}(z,x,y)R(z,x,y),

which is equivalent to the desired result (
2
) by (
16
).

Proof of Proposition
2
:

The proof is based on the following short-time expansion of the heat kernel. This result can be found in
[
2
, Section 5.9]
, see also
[
13
,
19
]
. Let
p(t,x,y)
be the heat kernel of the operator
L=-\partial_{x}^{2}+V(x)
, where
V:{\mathbb{R}}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
is a smooth function. As
t\to 0^{+}
, we have the asymptotic expansion

p(t,x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(y-x)^{2}}{4t}\Big)\big[1+u_{1}(x,y)t+u_{2}(x,y)t^{2}+O(t^{3})\big],

(49)

where

u_{1}(x,y)=-\int_{0}^{1}V(x+s(y-x)){\textnormal{d}}s,

(50)

and

u_{2}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}u_{1}(x,y)^{2}-\int_{0}^{1}s(1-s)V^{\prime\prime}(x+s(y-x)){\textnormal{d}}s.

(51)

The heat kernel for the operator
{\mathcal{L}}^{(1)}=-\partial_{x}^{2}+x^{2}
is

p^{(1)}(t,x,y)=\frac{e^{-t}}{\sqrt{\pi}}{\mathcal{E}}(e^{-2t},x,y),

since
{\mathcal{L}}^{(1)}\psi_{n}=(2n+1)\psi_{n}
and the functions
\psi_{n}
form an orthogonal basis for
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\textnormal{d}}x)
with
\lVert\psi_{n}\rVert^{2}=\sqrt{\pi}2^{n}n!
.
Let
\omega(x):=\partial_{x}^{2}\ln(H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x))
. From (
5
) we find that the heat kernel for the operator

{\mathcal{L}}^{(2)}=-\partial_{x}^{2}+U_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)+2l=-\partial_{x}^{2}+x^{2}-2\omega(x)+2l,

is

p^{(2)}(t,x,y)=\frac{e^{-t}{\mathcal{E}}_{\textbf{{k}}}(e^{-2t},x,y)}{\sqrt{\pi}\psi_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)\psi_{\textbf{{k}}}(y)}.

From (
2
) and (
16
) we conclude that

\frac{p^{(2)}(t,x,y)}{p^{(1)}(t,x,y)}=\frac{1}{H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)H_{\textbf{{k}}}(y)}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{k_{l}+1}e^{-2nt}Q_{n}^{\textbf{{k}}}(x,y).

(52)

Let
\alpha_{1}(x,y)
and
\alpha_{2}(x,y)
(respectively,
\beta_{1}(x,y)
and
\beta_{2}(x,y)
) be the coefficients
u_{1}(x,y)
and
u_{2}(x,y)
defined by equations (
50
) and (
51
) with
V(x)=x^{2}+2l-2\omega(x)
(respectively,
V(x)=x^{2}
). For simplicity, we write
\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{i}(x,y)
and similarly for
\beta_{i}
. According to (
49
), we have

\frac{p^{(2)}(t,x,y)}{p^{(1)}(t,x,y)}=\frac{1+\alpha_{1}t+\alpha_{2}t^{2}+O(t^{3})}{1+\beta_{1}t+\beta_{2}t^{2}+O(t^{3})}.

(53)

We compute
\beta_{i}
using formulas (
50
) and (
51
) with
V(x)=x^{2}
:

\beta_{1}=-\frac{1}{3}(x^{2}+xy+y^{2}),\;\;\;\beta_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\beta_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{3}.

Next, we use the fact that
\omega(x)=\partial_{x}^{2}\ln(H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x))
and compute

\Omega(x,y):=\int_{0}^{1}\omega(x+s(y-x)){\textnormal{d}}s=\frac{1}{y-x}\Big(\frac{H^{\prime}_{\textbf{{k}}}(y)}{H_{\textbf{{k}}}(y)}-\frac{H^{\prime}_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)}{H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)}\Big).

After integrating by parts twice, we arrive at

\Theta(x,y):=\int_{0}^{1}s(1-s)\omega^{\prime\prime}(x+s(y-x)){\textnormal{d}}s=\frac{1}{(y-x)^{2}}\Big[\omega(x)+\omega(y)-2\Omega(x,y)\Big].

Again, in what follows, we will write
\Omega=\Omega(x,y)
and
\Theta=\Theta(x,y)
.
Using formulas (
50
) and (
51
) with
V(x)=x^{2}+2l-2\omega(x)
we find

\alpha_{1}=\beta_{1}-2l+2\Omega,\;\;\;\alpha_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{3}+2\Theta.

We check that

\frac{1+\alpha_{1}t+\alpha_{2}t^{2}+O(t^{3})}{1+\beta_{1}t+\beta_{2}t^{2}+O(t^{3})}=1+\gamma_{1}t+\gamma_{2}t^{2}+O(t^{3}),

where

\gamma_{1}=\alpha_{1}-\beta_{1}=2(\Omega-l),

and

\gamma_{2}=\alpha_{2}-\beta_{2}-\gamma_{1}\beta_{1}=2(\Omega-l)^{2}+2\Theta.

Combining the above formulas with (
52
) and (
53

\frac{1}{H_{\textbf{{k}}}(x)H_{\textbf{{k}}}(y)}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{k_{l}+1}e^{-2nt}Q_{n}^{\textbf{{k}}}(x,y)=1+2(\Omega(x,y)-l)t+2\Big[(\Omega(x,y)-l)^{2}+\Theta(x,y)\Big]t^{2}+O(t^{3}).

Comparing the first three coefficients in the Taylor expansions at
t=0
on both sides of the above equation gives us the desired formulas (

We denote
