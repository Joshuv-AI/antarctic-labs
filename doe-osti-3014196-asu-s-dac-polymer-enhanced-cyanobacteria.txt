Executive Summary
ASU’s DAC polymer-enhanced cyanobacterial
bioproductivity (AUDACity) project aims to
demonstrate a novel, scalable method for removing
carbon dioxide (CO2) directly from ambient air and
delivering it to cyanobacterial cultures to produce
commodity biofuel, mid-value protein for supplements,
and high value phycocyanin (PC), a natural blue
colorant (Figure A). This approach uses low-cost,
reusable anion exchange polymers embedded in
modular mesh packets, which capture CO2 during
drying cycles when exposed to ambient air, and release
concentrated CO2 into aqueous cultivation systems. The
project addresses a critical challenge in energy research
needed for developing sustainable, economically viable Figure A. Illustration of the AUDACity concept
methods of Direct Air Capture (DAC) that can be using moisture-driven CO2 sorbents to deliver CO2
integrated with bio-based systems for fuel and chemical from the air to liquid medium for cultivating
production. cyanobacteria to produce sustainable fuels,
 chemicals and high-value products.
AUDACity contributes to scientific understanding by
integrating materials chemistry, cyanobacterial biology, and system engineering to create a distributed
CO2 delivery platform. Key insights have emerged around the design of biocompatible sorbents,
optimization of CO2 capture-release cycles, and durability of packet-based delivery systems under
outdoor conditions. Notably, the team has synthesized and tested a range of polymer sorbents, identified
mechanisms of material degradation and fouling, and advanced both lab- and pilot-scale cultivation
systems to evaluate performance.
From a technical and economic standpoint, AUDACity shows promise for achieving cost-effective CO2
capture and delivery into aqueous media and biofuel production. Preliminary techno-economic analysis
(TEA) indicates that the DAC system based on current performance can reach $680/tonne CO2 delivered
into aqueous solution; with reasonable improvements to sorbent lifetime, sorbent capacity, reducing water
uptake the approach could reach $66/tonne by avoiding the need for energy-intensive sorbent
regeneration and CO2 compression, making it more feasible for decentralized deployment. With these
costs for CO2 and by extracting and selling high-value PC ($50/kg) and mid-value protein supplement
($6/kg), the remaining biomass can be hydrothermally treated into biofuel for $2.50/gallon, and would
support a small first-of-a-kind biorefinery capable of producing 500 barrels per day of biofuel.
The project offers meaningful public benefits by advancing carbon removal technologies that are low-
energy, modular, and adaptable to non-arable land and brackish water use. It aligns with national goals to
develop advanced biotechnology and supports future pathways for bio-based fuels and products. By
enabling direct coupling of CO2 transfer into aqueous medium and biological carbon utilization,
AUDACity lays the groundwork for effective algae cultivation without wasteful CO2 delivery and is a
promising and innovative solution for low-carbon fuel and bioproduct generation contributing to a
vigorous bioeconomy.

Background
Algae-based biofuel and bioproduct systems face three central challenges addressed in the AUDACity
project: the high cost of sourcing carbon dioxide, low efficiency in delivering and utilizing CO2 during
cultivation, and the high cost of producing solely biofuel from algal biomass.
Large-scale microalgae cultivation requires substantial CO2 inputs to maintain high productivity.
However, optimal cultivation sites, such as those with abundant sunlight and access to water, are often far
from concentrated CO2 sources like industrial facilities. In such scenarios, CO2 is typically compressed
and transported via pipeline to the cultivation site, an approach that is both capital- and energy-intensive,
requiring significant infrastructure for compression, transportation, and distribution. These logistical
challenges raise costs and limit the feasibility of algae-based systems in many good growing locations.
While DAC technologies present an alternative source of CO2 that can be generated at the cultivation
facility, most current DAC systems rely on fans to move large volumes of air consuming 40‒60% of the
total energy for capturing CO2 and require significant capital investment that accounts for 50‒70% of the
total system cost.1 Many DAC processes also depend on energy-intensive thermal or vacuum-driven
regeneration, or operate at high temperatures typically achieved by burning fossil fuels.2–4 Some use
caustic materials that pose environmental health and safety challenges, further complicating deployment. 3
Additionally, established methods for delivering CO2 for large-scale microalgae cultivation involve
sparging or bubbling concentrated CO2 into shallow ponds. Unfortunately, this approach loses the
majority (>70%) of the CO2 to the atmosphere.5 Sump sparging systems have been developed to address
some of these inefficiencies by injecting CO2-enriched air into a sump tank before recirculating the
dissolved carbonate species to the pond. While this method improves CO2 retention relative to direct
sparging, it still relies on the availability of concentrated CO2 sources and pressurized gas handling
infrastructure, careful reactor design to maintain the necessary gas and liquid flows and can increase
energy use due to changes to hydrodynamic flows that deviate from efficient raceway flows. Additionally,
maintaining optimal carbonate speciation and pH during recirculation adds operational complexity.
Another major barrier is the high cost of producing biofuel alone from algal biomass, which remains
uncompetitive with petroleum-based fuels at >$7.00/gasoline gallon equivalent (GGE).6,7 The challenge is
compounded by the relatively low value of the bulk biomass, particularly when cultivation strains
produce limited quantities of high-value co-products. Conventional processing methods often require
energy-intensive harvesting, dewatering, and conversion steps, which further increase production costs.
Without the ability to extract valuable co-products such as pigments, proteins, or lipids prior to biofuel
conversion, the economic case for algal biofuels is difficult to justify. Improving the value proposition of
algal biomass through integrated bioproduct pathways is essential to achieving cost-effective and scalable
biofuel production.
One way to increase the value of the biomass is to isolate valuable components for sale into higher-value
markets before converting the residual biomass into biofuel.8 For example, protein supplements have a
market value of $6/kg (the value of whey protein used as a human health supplement), and the
phycocyanin protein pigment complex can be used as a natural blue pigment with market valuations over
$150/kg for analytic grade materials to $1/kg animal feed supplements. However, natural phycocyanin is
susceptible to degradation by heat, pH extremes and light,9–11 and rapidly degrades above 50-60 ᵒC,10,12
making it less suitable for some food and beverage applications that could increase its market size. Also,
existing phycocyanin isolation methods are complex and costly, making them impractical to address
larger food and beverage markets.13–16 Finding ways to increase the markets for the additional high-value
co-products is essential to support large-scale fuel production.

Project Objectives
Project Goal
This AUDACity project addresses key challenges in CO2 sourcing for cultivating algae and cyanobacteria
by combining DAC polymer technology with engineered cyanobacteria in a closed-loop, renewable
biofuel and bioproduct platform. AUDACity eliminates dependence on point-source CO2 and compressed
gas infrastructure by enabling ambient air capture and in situ carbon delivery using solid-supported
alkaline sorbents. The overarching project goal is to demonstrate the AUDACity concept, advance the
technology from technology readiness level (TRL) 2 to 5, and demonstrate AUDACity is a compelling
and economic technology strategy that leads to excellent algal performance and productivity.
Innovation
AUDACity introduces a novel, integrated platform for DAC and algal cultivation, designed to overcome
critical limitations in existing CO2 supply and biomass value chains. Its innovations span CO2 capture,
delivery, cultivation biology, and bioproduct recovery:
 1. Ambient CO2 Sourcing at Cultivation Sites: Traditional microalgae systems require
 concentrated CO2 from industrial sources or pipelines, limiting site flexibility. AUDACity
 overcomes this by capturing CO2 directly from ambient air using passive, moisture-swing
 sorbents, enabling cultivation in remote or underutilized areas without CO2 infrastructure.
 2. Efficient Carbon Delivery without Sparging Losses: In conventional systems, >70% of
 sparged CO2 escapes to the atmosphere. AUDACity embeds alkaline-compatible sorbents directly
 into the culture system or medium recycle stream, allowing CO2 to convert into non-outgassable
 bicarbonate (HCO3–), improving retention and uptake efficiency without gas bubbling.
 3. Modular, Low-Energy Passive DAC with Moisture-driven CO2 release: AUDACity offers an
 alternative to high-energy DAC systems through a modular, moisture-swing DAC platform built
 around anion exchange resins (AERs) that reversibly bind CO2 in dry air and release it into
 alkaline medium. Unlike traditional systems requiring thermal regeneration, compression, or gas
 delivery, AUDACity releases CO2 directly into aqueous cultivation environments. The system
 also uses passive air flow for capturing CO2 to eliminate fan cost and energy use and a slow-
 moving motorized belt system to significantly reduce energy demand.
 4. Multi-Product Biomanufacturing with Engineered Strains: AUDACity deploys genetically
 engineered Synechocystis strains that secrete methyl laurate directly into the culture medium,
 simplifying downstream fuel harvesting. Simultaneously, high-protein biomass (up to 60%) and
 high-value co-products like phycocyanin enable diversified revenue streams.
 5. Efficient Isolation of High-Value Products: A simple procedure was developed to isolate
 phycocyanin from a number of cyanobacterial strains with superior efficiency and yield, thus
 removing a critical barrier in cost-efficient isolation of important cyanobacterial products.
 6. Thermally-Stable Natural Pigments: Native phycocyanin degrades rapidly at elevated
 temperatures. We developed a protocol to encapsulate dried phycocyanin in milk fat globule
 membranes, preserving >90% of its coloration at 100°C, expanding market potential in food,
 beverage and other applications that require higher thermal stability.
 7. Cost-Competitive Biorefinery Economics: While conventional algae-based biofuel costs exceed
 $7/GGE, technoeconomic analyses show that AUDACity can support fuel production at $2.50 per
 GGE, assuming biomass protein is sold at $6.29/kg. This model enables production at the 500
 barrels per day scale without saturating co-product markets, supporting first-of-a-kind
 deployment. Developing a thermostable phycocyanin as done in this project will expand the range
 of desired uses of phycocyanin tremendously.

Significance
AUDACity addresses foundational challenges in algal biomanufacturing by transforming the economics
and logistics of carbon sourcing, cultivation, and product diversification. Its innovations enable
sustainable, decentralized fuel and protein production in regions previously limited by CO2 access or
processing costs.
 • CO2 Sourcing Freedom: By enabling on-site CO2 capture, AUDACity expands algal cultivation
 beyond industrial clusters, opening opportunities in locations with ideal algal cultivation
 conditions but that are far from CO2 sources.
 • Modular Scalability: The passive, belt-driven DAC design allows modular systems to be
 tailored to the biofuel or co-product market needs of specific locations and regions, reducing
 transport costs and increasing opportunities with local manufacturing and economic benefits.
 • Global Protein Access: AUDACity’s low-cost protein extraction from cyanobacterial biomass
 offers a scalable pathway to meet rising global protein demands, especially in food-insecure
 regions.
 • Market-Aligned Multi-Product Streams: With strains capable of producing both fuel and an
 array of bioproducts, the system enables economic resilience through multiple revenue channels -
 critical for commercial viability.
 • Alignment with U.S. Clean Energy Goals: AUDACity supports DOE goals by advancing low-
 carbon biofuels, enabling scalable DAC, and fostering innovation in biomass conversion,
 contributing to both carbon dioxide conversion and domestic energy security.
Impact on US Department of Energy (DOE) Goals
AUDACity helps advance the Department of Energy’s goal of bringing biofuel products closer to
economic parity with fossil fuels by developing a transformative, high-productivity advanced algal
system that gets its inorganic carbon from air at high efficiency and low cost. Indeed, AUDACity meets
and exceeds the Topic Area 3 requirements of the funding opportunity announcement (FOA) by 1)
capturing ~100% of its CO2 from the air (well above the required 20%), 2) growing high-quality biomass
with valuable bioproducts (PC, methyl laurate), 3) directly excreting biofuel from the cell, thereby
eliminating significant dewatering costs, and 4) delivering comprehensive life cycle and techno-economic
analyses (LCA and TEA), showing a pathway to reduced cultivation costs and increased revenues from
bioproducts and biofuels that meet the renewable fuels standard. Preliminary TEA results indicate a
potential path to algal fuels at $2.50/GGE and CO2 delivery costs below $50/tonne, with coproduct
valorization improving economic viability. This positions AUDACity to contribute meaningfully to
national goals to increase energy production and rural job creation through distributed renewable fuel
production.

Budget Period 2 [Months 4–21]: Lab-scale AUDACity demonstration and evaluation
Summary of Activities: BP2 activities included (Task 1) further developing polymers with improved
CO2 capture and release kinetics, fouling resistance, decreasing inactive anion binding, and reducing
drying times, and processing these polymers into flexible, porous capture materials; (Task 2) testing
biocompatibility of DAC polymers and using AUDACity systems to cultivate biofuel- and high-value
product-producing Synechocystis at lab scale; (Task 3) developing AUDACity systems to rapidly screen
DAC polymer performance, measuring performance of a continuous belt design in a wind tunnel, and
collecting and delivering CO2 to cultivate Synechocystis in the lab; (Task 5) developing preliminary
TEA/LCA to guide engineering design decisions. (Note that Task 4 does not start until Budget Period 3.)
A Go/No-Go Decision occurred at month 18 with lab-scale demonstration of the AUDACity concept.
Anticipated Outcome: DAC polymers synthesized and evaluated for CO2 capture performance and
biocompatibility and then used within an AUDACity system to deliver CO2 from air to cultivate
Synechocystis at a biomass productivity of ≥ 50 mg L–1 d–1 at lab scale. Preliminary TEA demonstrates a
path to $2.5/GGE.
Realized Results: Polystyrene-based DAC polymers with high ion exchange capacities (4.0-4.7 mmol/g)
were synthesized and tested, including variants more hydrophobic than the commercial A501 resin. These
demonstrated improved drying behavior and biocompatibility with Synechocystis under alkaline
conditions. The AUDACity 1g system, equipped with Excellion and A501 sorbents, delivered CO2 to
support Synechocystis cultivation, achieving biomass productivities averaging ~80-100 mg L–1 d–1 over
multiple-day periods. Wind tunnel experiments confirmed full CO2 adsorption capacity of A501 (670–
678 µmol CO2/g) under optimal conditions, and elongated tubular mesh packets improved sorbent
drying/loading rates by 4.3-fold over prior configurations. Preliminary TEA demonstrated that sorbent
drying rate and ion exchange capacity significantly affect the cost of CO2 delivery, supporting a pathway
to $50/tonne CO2 and $2.50/GGE fuel cost when including high-value co-products like phycocyanin.
Additionally, a genetically adapted Synechocystis strain maintained robust growth with CO2 delivered by
the DAC polymer.
Expected Outcomes: 1) DAC polymer capture performance goal (≥ 2.0 µmol CO2 s⁻¹ g⁻¹ polymer and
≤ 1 h cycle time in lab) was not achieved. 2) Two DAC polymers were identified for cost-effective
cyanobacterial production – achieved. 3) AUDACity 1g systems capable of delivering ~1 g CO2 per day
were developed – achieved. 4) AUDACity delivered CO2 from air to cultivate Synechocystis at lab scale
with biomass productivity ≥ 50 mg L⁻¹ d⁻¹ – achieved. 5) Economic evaluation demonstrated a viable
pathway to $2.5/GGE – achieved.
Budget Period 3 [Months 22–36]: AUDACity optimization for outdoor evaluation
Summary of Activities: BP3 activities included: (Task 1) optimizing polymers and processing them into
DAC materials for outdoor evaluation; (Task 2) maximizing growth and bioproducts with AUDACity
and evaluating yield and quality of biomass, biofuels and bioproducts; (Task 3) scaling up AUDACity for
outdoor cultivation and evaluating AUDACity outdoors (without photosynthetic microbes) over the four
seasons; (Task 4) performing outdoor cultivation trials using AUDACity and biofuel-producing
Synechocystis; (Task 5) revising TEA and LCA models to account for data collected during outdoor
testing; using the revised models to informed decision-making to move AUDACity beyond TRL5.
Anticipated Outcome: DAC polymers optimized for outdoor conditions and used within a 100g CO2 /
day AUDACity system to deliver CO2 from air to cultivate Synechocystis with a productivity of ≥ 12.9 g
m–2 d–1 or ≥ 10% over 2018 SOT in 4.2 m2 outdoor, covered ponds. TEA shows a pathway to ≤ $50/tonne
CO2 delivered to the algal system. LCA of the proposed biorefinery is completed.

packet density, and commissioned for outdoor operation at AzCATI. Outdoor operation in April 2024

Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions by Task
The AUDACity project integrates six main tasks: (0) Process and data validation; (1) DAC polymer
design and synthesis; (2) polymer biocompatibility and lab-scale cultivation with Synechocystis sp. PCC
6803; (3) development and scaling of the AUDACity delivery platform; (4) outdoor cultivation using
AUDACity; and (5) technoeconomic and life cycle analysis of the integrated system.
Task 0 - Process and Data Validation
The project team met with the verification team from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL)
and the Department of Energy (DOE) Bioenergy Technology Office (BETO) on Nov. 3rd, 2020. NREL
provided a series of recommendations to strengthen the statement of project objectives (SOPO) and
technical datasheet key performance parameters. Some highlights included adding Task 4.0 to establish a
baseline productivity and biomass composition for wild-type Synechocystis in 4 m2 outdoor raceways;
based on the baseline productivity a 10% productivity increase was targeted using AUDACity sorbents.
Revisions to the SOPO and tech datasheet were approved and the project received a “go”
recommendation to advance to budget period 2 to meet Milestone 0.1.

Task 1 - Polymer design and synthesis (M4-36)
Summary. We synthesized a ~200 different anion-exchange-resin-type polymer sorbents, including
polymers made with a hydrophilic ethylene glycol backbone that could be synthesized rapidly (~5 min)
with UV light, and polymers with fluorine to increase hydrophobicity, reduce water uptake and reduce
drying times, while also resisting fouling. When the polymer membranes were ground into powders and
attached to adhesives, they achieved DAC performance to meet SMART Milestone 1.1.1 and 1.2.1 of ≥
2.0 and ≥ 4.0 µmol CO2 s–1 m–2 polymer, respectively. However, these powders were not stable on the
adhesive, so future work contained sorbent powders and beads within mesh bags. The methacrylate-based
polymers were not stable under alkaline conditions. Since a commercial sorbent (A501) with a
polystyrene backbone was found to be stable under alkaline cultivation conditions and biocompatible, a
third sorbent class was made with a polystyrene backbone. This section summarizes some of the work
synthesizing polymer-based sorbents with a focus on the most promising ones that were analyzed most
extensively.
Synthesis of ethylene glycol- and methacrylate-based polymers
Polymer membranes were designed and synthesized using the following two strategies: 1) inclusion of
quaternary ammonium monomers for CO2 capture capacity, and 2) incorporation of monomers that
provide desired properties (e.g., hydrophobicity, pliability). Variations of hydrophilic and hydrophobic
direct air capture (DAC) membranes were prepared using combinations of a quaternary ammonium
monomer, [2-(methacryloyloxy)ethyl] trimethylammonium (trimethyl aminoethyl methacrylate;
TMAEMA) chloride, and a crosslinker. Several crosslinking monomers were explored including ethylene
glycol dimethacrylate (EGDMA), diethylene glycol dimethacrylate (DEGDMA), poly(ethylene glycol)
diacrylate (PEGDA), and poly(ethylene glycol) methacrylate (PEGMA). In addition, 2,2,2-
trifluoromethyl methacrylate (TFEMA) was used to investigate the hydrophobic characteristics of the
membranes. The chemical structure of all chemicals used in the synthesis reactions are shown in Figure
1.1. The membranes were synthesized through a photo-initiated free-radical polymerization reaction,
using 2,2-dimethoxy-2-phenylacetophenone (DMPA) as the photoinitiator. The materials were chosen
based on the previous work by BL Technologies, Inc., where they synthesized anion-exchange
membranes with IECs of about 2.7 mmol/g.17 We chose to use a photo-initiated polymerization
mechanism using DMPA (a commonly used photoinitiator) because of its simplicity and shorter time
scales. The preparation of these membranes is visually shown in Figure 1.2. Monomers were all added to
a vial and then stirred and sonicated for 10 minutes. The mixture was then added to a silicon mold and
exposed to UV light (365 nm, 2 mW cm–2) for three minutes. Subsequently, the solidified membrane was
inverted within the mold and exposed to UV for an additional three minutes.
Ion Exchange.
The quaternary-ammonium-containing monomer, TMAEMA, initially contained a chloride (Cl–)
counterion, which had to be exchanged with either carbonate (CO32–), bicarbonate (HCO3–), or hydroxide
(OH−) to enable DAC functionality. Therefore, samples such as T/M-1 (Table 1.1) were soaked in a 1 M
potassium bicarbonate (KHCO3) solution for approximately 24 hours. The KHCO3 concentration
corresponded to a 50-fold molar excess of HCO3– to Cl– based on the membrane’s theoretical IEC.

Additional methacrylate polymer synthesis and alkaline instability:
We synthesized and evaluated over 20 different methacrylate polymers that included fluorinated groups to
increase hydrophobicity. However, they were toxic to cyanobacteria, displayed reduced CO2 delivery
after multiple uses and absorbed too much water to be efficient in repeated dry/wet cycles for CO2 capture
and delivery. A variety of methacrylamide polymers were made to increase stability at high pH, but they
did not achieve sufficient CO2 capacity and were also toxic to the cyanobacteria. Since promising results
were obtained from a commercially available resin (A501) this work was concluded.
Polystyrene-based sorbents:
Commercial ion-exchange resins typically have exceptional selectivity, stability, and durability. For our
project, we selected two commercial strong-base anion-exchange resins, HPR-4800 and IRA-900, from
the Amberlite series manufactured by DuPont, and Purolite A501P resin from Purolite Corporation. These
resins are widely used in the pharmaceutical, food and beverage, water treatment, and chemical
processing industries, are available in diverse forms such as beads, powders, and membranes, and can
thus be tailored to meet specific application requirements. Although both companies do not disclose the
exact molecular structure of these materials, general information about them can be found on their
websites. These polymers typically are comprised of polystyrene (PS)-divinylbenzene (DVB) copolymers
with quaternary ammonium (QA) functional groups, which make them suitable choices for our project.

Quantifying ion-exchange capacity by titration.
The ion-exchange capacity sets the upper limit of the CO2-binding capacity for a given polymer. A high
ion-exchange capacity is critical for achieving cost-effective CO2 capture and delivery to the
cyanobacteria. Table 1.2 summarizes the titration data collected for a variety of synthesized styrene-
based polymers used to quantify their ion-exchange capacity. Moreover, we normalized our titrations
using the dry mass of the resin. Given the high water content of the A501 resin, we initially stabilized its
weight by drying the sample in ambient air for 24 hours. Our observations show that the resin undergoes a
mass loss of approximately 60% after drying for this period. The dry mass of these samples was then used
to calculate the experimental IEC. Furthermore, our data indicate that this commercial resin displayed a
significantly lower capacity compared to all the synthesized polymers.

Water uptake in commercial ion exchange resins, meshes and use of hydrophobic coatings
Considering the significant impact long drying times can have on reducing the total CO2 delivered per
mass of sorbent per day, we explored several hydrophobic coatings with the goal of reducing water
uptake and subsequent necessary drying time without significantly reducing the CO2 delivery
performance. Hydrophobic coatings still permitted exchange of water vapor from liquid medium into
sorbent materials, facilitating release of CO2 into the cultivation medium. In addition to water uptake in
the sorbent, the nylon mesh material used to contain the sorbent absorbed additional water, slowing
drying and overall cycle times. We investigated water uptake in A501 contained within A25 nylon mesh
packets, both with and without waterproofing spray (Star brite), as well as coated packets containing
waterproofing-coated A501 resin. Starbrite hydrophobic coating was found to be effective at reducing
water uptake in the mesh, but not the sorbent beads.

Task 2 - Polymer biocompatibility and lab-scale DAC cultivation of Synechocystis (M4-36)
Summary. Commercial and synthesized anion-exchange resins were evaluated for moisture-driven CO₂
delivery, biocompatibility and the ability to support cyanobacterial growth. Milestone 2.1.1 to evaluate
five polymers for biocompability was achieved by testing three commercial sorbents and dozens of
synthesized sorbents. At the 50-mL scale, Purolite A501 demonstrated superior biocompatibility and CO₂
release capacity, supporting higher Synechocystis growth than bicarbonate controls. At the 12-L scale, a
bench-scale the 1g system using 16 g of A501 delivered ~2 g CO2/day, enabling vigorous Synechocystis
growth with ~1.5 g/day of CO2 fixed by the cells, or about 62.5 mg dry biomass L–1 d–1 to meet Milestone
2.2.1 of > 50 mg dry biomass L–1 d–1. These data also satisfy Milestone 3.2.1 to demonstrate at least one
sorbent (A501) for delivering CO2 to cultivate Synechocystis in a laminar flow hood. Polymer
performance declined with repeated use due to biofouling and ion exchange with medium components. A
modified growth medium and cleaning protocol partially restored sorbent capacity. These results
confirmed the feasibility of moisture-driven DAC sorbents for microbial cultivation and highlight the
need for strategies to reduce fouling and degradation. The bioproducts laurate and phycocyanin (PC) were
also evaluated over a range of cultivation conditions to meet Milestone 2.2.2. A simple protocol for
efficiently isolating PC from several cyanobacterial strains was determined, and a protocol for
encapsulating PC with milk fat globule membranes was shown to dramatically increase thermal stability;
encapsulated PC was able to retain ~75% of its color after 10 minutes at 100 ᵒC, whereas natural PC lost
all its color.
Biocompatibility of commercial sorbents and 50-mL scale cultivation:
Details. Four commercially available strong-base anion exchange resins (AER) were evaluated: three
beaded ones (HPR-4800 (DuPont), Amberlite IRA-900 (DuPont) and A501 (Purolite)) and one sheet
AER, Excellion (Snopure). These sorbents were assessed based on their ion-exchange capacity (IEC),
CO₂ delivery kinetics into aqueous medium, and biocompatibility. The measured IEC, which is
theoretically twice the maximum CO2-capture capacity, was similar across all beaded AERs, ranging
from 2.1 to 2.3 mmol/g (Table 2.1). The CO2 delivery capacity and kinetics were evaluated by
submerging dry, air-loaded sorbents into a 10 mM Na2CO3 solution and calculating CO2 release from the
observed pH change, following the methodology outlined by Shesh et al.18 As shown in Figure 2.1 A,
one gram of each beaded sorbent exhibited comparable CO2 profiles, while the Excellion sheets displayed
CO2 release rates that were 3-4 times slower, consistent with prior observations.19 Notably, the HPR-
4800, which initially showed CO2 release capacity comparable to that of A501, demonstrated a marked
reduction in CO2 release capacity after exposure to alkaline conditions after the second run (Figure
2.1 B). This behavior suggests that HPR-4800 may possess weakly bound quaternary ammonium
functional groups that are lost under extended exposure (several hours) to alkaline conditions (pH 10–11),
thus making this polymer less suitable for our applications.

For this procedure, the cyanobacterial culture (OD730 ~4) was left in the dark for 24 – 48 hours to settle;
the biomass settled at the bottom of flask and the clear culture medium (~80%) was slowly poured out.
The biomass suspension (1 mL) was pipetted into a 2-mL screw-cap tube. 45 mM CaCl2 was added along
with an equal volume of 0.1 mm glass beads. The biomass was homogenized in a bead beater (four cycles

stirrer. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C (12,000 g for 15 min) and the supernatant was
discarded. The phycocyanin precipitate was resuspended in 22.5 mM CaCl2 and centrifuged at 4 °C

Task 3 - Design, develop and evaluate AUDACity systems (M4-36)
Summary. A bench-scale system capable called the AUDACity 1g was designed and built for
continuously circulating a sorbent belt made of either 16 mesh tube packets each containing 1 g of A501
AER beads or 40 Excellion AER sheets and delivered ~1.3 g CO2/d limited by the drying rate within the
laminar flow hood (~0.4 m/s). The mesh tube packets with A501 were found to dry and load CO2 ~4x
faster than mesh bags and deliver CO2 3-4 times faster than Excellion sheets. Three AUDACity 100g
systems were constructed to satisfy Milestone 3.4.1, 1) a design with a single belt with a “V” path to
increase air contact within the confined footprint of the raceway pond used in winter 2023, 2) an
improved design with two parallel loops to provide redundancy and chain guides to prevent chain
derailment and increased packet capacity from 256 to 376 used in spring/summer 2024, and 3) a rebuild
of the second system with new packets to support a trial in the fall/winter 2025/2025. A model was
developed based on sorbent drying and CO2 loading vs wind speed measured in a wind tunnel that
predicted outdoor performance within ~3% when temperature and relative humidity were similar to wind
tunnel measurements. Over 302 days of use, mesh packets and sorbent beads experienced significant
wear, which we hypothesize was due to prolonged exposure to intense UV radiation and wet/dry cycle-
induced mechanical stresses.
Mesh tube and hanging packet design:
Summary: A key innovation in the AUDACity system was the development and evaluation of elongated
mesh tube packets for sorbent containment, which significantly enhanced system performance. Compared
to traditional mesh bags, these mesh tube packets, fabricated from nylon with 25 µm pores and with 0.75
cm diameter, demonstrated 4x faster drying and CO2 loading times in wind tunnel experiments due to
improved surface area exposure and reduced clumping of sorbent beads. Optimized packet spacing and
fill (2 g A501 per tube with ~30% void volume) balanced ease of manual filling with sorbent swelling
during wet-dry cycles. These design improvements not only improved the CO2 adsorption and desorption
rates but also facilitated better integration into continuous-operation DAC systems by maintaining high
surface area exposure, enhancing drying efficiency and supporting modular scalability.
Sorbent selection and mesh packet design. The most critical component of the DAC system is the
sorbent. A501 anion exchange resin (AER) beads, with a diameter range of 420–1200 µm, were selected
for their microporous structure, which enhanced both gas and liquid transport through the particles during
CO2 capture and release. Additionally, they demonstrated stability in alkaline conditions (pH 9–11),
which are necessary for retaining and concentrating CO2 in solution.
Due to its high charge density, A501 resin packets can absorb approximately 330 wt.% water (as a
percentage of dry sorbent mass) (Figure 1.8 under Task 1). While this hydration facilitates rapid CO2
release, it delays drying and CO2 uptake and increases water demand and environmental impact. Drying
and CO2 loading performance were assessed in a closed-loop wind tunnel using dry air (15% RH) at 25
°C flowing at 5 m/s. As shown in Figure 3.1 the mesh tube packets dried in ~30 minutes and achieved
90% of their CO2 uptake capacity within ~90 minutes; this is approximately four times faster than the
same sorbent beads placed in a single mesh bag, which enabled the sorbent to clump. CO2 release from
the dry-loaded sorbent was evaluated by immersing the tube packet in 1 L of 10 mM Na2CO3 solution and
tracking pH changes. As shown in Figure 3.2, approximately 90% of the sorbed CO2 was delivered
within 15 minutes, which is 3-4 times faster than release from an Excellion SnowPure AER sheet.

Task 5 - Techno-Economic and Life Cycle Analyses (TEA / LCA)
Summary: A modular engineering process model was developed, integrating moisture-driven DAC with
algal cultivation and biorefining. This included a novel DAC subprocess model developed and validated
against the literature to meet Milestone 5.1.1. The techno-economic analysis (TEA) employed a
discounted cash flow rate of return (DCFROR) method and showed that with reasonable improvements to
sorbent lifetime, sorbent capacity, and reduce water uptake AUDACity could reach $66/tonne CO2 by
avoiding the need for energy-intensive sorbent regeneration and CO2 compression and a best-case
scenario of $51/tonne CO2, which was the goal to meet Milestone 5.2.2. Life cycle analysis (LCA)
indicated that the carbon intensity of fuels derived from cyanobacterial biomass using this system is
substantially lower than fossil-based diesel to meet Milestone 5.3.1; however, new sorbent materials with
~10-fold lower water uptake than A501 are needed to meet the target of 10:1 mass ratio of H2O:CO2 to
reduce water use. Importantly, by extracting high-value coproducts like phycocyanin and protein from
biomass prior to hydrothermal liquefaction, biofuel could be produced at a cost of $2.50 per gasoline
gallon equivalent (GGE), supporting the feasibility of a small first-of-a-kind biorefinery producing 500
barrels per day of fuel. This analysis highlights the commercial viability and environmental advantages of
integrating moisture-driven DAC with bioproduct and biofuel pathways.
Methods
Process Models. This analysis is based on a modular engineering process model that integrates the mass
and energy flows across a co-located algal cultivation and biorefining system. The model simulates the
entire process chain from biomass production in open raceway ponds, integrating the novel carbon
delivery system described through downstream conversion that includes the recovery of high-value PC
with remaining biomass converted to fuels via hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL). The DAC and protein
extraction subprocess models were developed as a part of this study. The cultivation subprocess model is
described in Davis et al.7 and the hydrothermal liquefaction subprocess model is described in Chen et al.30
The foundational modeling provides the infrastructure for concurrent TEA and LCA.
Integrated Biorefinery Model. The costs and greenhouse gas emissions associated with an nth-of-a-kind
biorefinery composed of moisture-driven DAC for use in cultivating microalgae and cyanobacteria were
assessed. This assessment assumes the performance of the sorbent measured in the laboratory and
outdoors prior to direct contact with microalgae or cyanobacteria to achieve a material lifetime of five
years, which may be achieved by interfacing the sorbent with the media recycle stream to reduce
biofouling-related sorbent performance degradation, and UV-stabilized mesh materials. The engineering
process model is shown in Figure 5.1. The DAC and protein extraction subprocess models are described
later as a part of this study. The cultivation subprocess model is described in Davis et al.7 and the
hydrothermal liquefaction subprocess model is described by Chen and Quinn.30 The protein fraction of
the cyanobacterial biomass is a value adding co-product to be sold as protein supplements and animal
feed and the phycocyanin protein fraction extracted and sold as a natural blue pigment.
DAC Subprocess Model. The model is based on current system performance to capture CO2 and deliver
it into an alkaline brine, which can be used for algae cultivation. An engineering process model of the
system was developed and is depicted by the process flow diagram in Figure 5.2. AER was cycled
between the air and an alkaline aqueous solution by an overhead zig-zag conveyor transferring CO2 to the
solution that is inspired by designs from Lackner.31 The DAC subprocess design was validated through
experimental data and descriptions by Lackner on solid sorbent moisture swing sorption.32 This CO2-
enriched solution is then used for cultivating the microalgae. These parameters form the basis of two
scenarios, a practical scenario representative of experimental results to date and an aspirational scenario
representative of improved performance by future development.

TEA Methodology. Economic performance was assessed using a discounted cash flow rate of return
(DCFROR) method to determine the minimum fuel selling price (MFSP) that achieves a net present value
of zero over a 30-year facility lifetime. The analysis assumes "nth-of-a-kind" economics, including a 10%
internal rate of return and a 35% tax rate. Additional financial assumptions, such as depreciation
schedules, debt structure, and plant start-up periods, follow established conventions.
Capital and operating costs were derived by scaling unit operations based on process flow rates and
system throughput using exponential scaling factors. Equipment sizing for cultivation and dewatering
systems was based on maximum volumetric and mass flow requirements. Conversion infrastructure,
including HTL and fuel upgrading systems, was sized using annual average flow and energy demand,
with seasonal variability addressed through intermediate biomass storage. Cost data were sourced from
literature and normalized to 2021 dollars using the Chemical Engineering Plant Cost Index. Variable
costs, including nutrients, hydrogen, and natural gas, were similarly adjusted. Process model outputs were
used to calculate annual production volumes and determine the MFSP.
LCA Methodology. An attributional life cycle assessment was performed to quantify the environmental
impacts of the modeled system. The system boundary spans from algal cultivation, dewatering, biomass
storage, conversion, product distribution, and end use. Results are presented for global warming potential
(GWP) using a 100-year time horizon. All impacts were normalized to a functional unit of 1 MJ of liquid
fuel, accounting for the combined energy content of renewable diesel and naphtha products. Life cycle
inventory (LCI) data were derived from Ecoinvent 3.7 and analyzed using OpenLCA 1.11. LCA utilized
the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) standards ISO 14040 and ISO 14044 to provide
the framework for conducting the assessment, ensuring methodological rigor and consistency. The 100-
year global warming potential values from the International Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) AR637 were
used: 1 for CO2, 28.9 for CH4, and 273 for N2O. Emissions factors for indirect inputs were sourced from
the GREET model, Ecoinvent 3.7, and supporting literature.
Sensitivity Methods. For the DAC subprocess, a global sensitivity analysis was conducted based on the
methods of Morris38 to identify important areas to focus further research effort. For the global sensitivity
analysis 10 random and unique designs were selected by varying the values of each variable. The
elementary effect of each variable in each design was calculated as defined in Morris.38 The mean and
standard deviations of these elementary effects of a variable across all designs were recorded. Then
Monte Carlo simulation was performed, which is a method of defining the impact of uncertainty in input
variables on MSP. Monte Carlo simulation uses random sampling, running the model with inputs selected
from a pre-define distribution, to predict outcomes based on probability distributions. We used uniform
distributions for the variables not experimentally defined in the Monte Carlo simulation while the other
variables were held constant.

Pathway to $2.50/GGE. Microalgae cultivation with CO2 supplied by conventional DAC is currently
cost prohibitive. Therefore, the cost of cultivating microalgae is typically assessed assuming CO2 will be
supplied from a point source at a very low cost (~$50/tonne). This, however, severely limits the
production capacity of microalgae cultivated in this manner given the mismatch between CO2 sources and
good growing regions for microalgae. Previous studies have predicted microalgae to cost $510/tonne ash
free dry weight (AFDW) but this is assuming a point source supplied CO2 is delivered with a high
utilization efficiency (Figure 5.6).6,7 In reality, CO2 utilization efficiencies are still lower (40%) than the
90% target, increasing cost to $650/tonne AFDW. To achieve the large capacity of algae cultivation
required to satisfy aviation fuel demand or to achieve food security, DAC CO2 sources are needed, but
current costs for DAC would increase costs further to >$1,100/tonne AFDW. However, TEA shows that
moisture-driven DAC and CO2 delivery for cultivating cyanobacteria can be used to reduce the cost to
$500/tonne AFDW through increased CO2 utilization efficiency and the potential for CO2 costs to be
close to $50/tonne using the AUDACity DAC strategy.

Path Forward
TEA identified several improvements to AUDACity needed to make it commercially viable, including
1) increasing sorbent lifetime, which could be achieved by reducing fouling and sorbent degradation,
2) increasing sorbent CO2 binding capacity that would increase the amount of captured per unit sorbent
mass, 3) reducing water uptake that would reduce the mass of water transported by the belt and reduce
drying and CO2 loading time to enable more cycles per day. LCA noted high water use that could be
addressed by improvement #3, but could also be realized with sorbents that resist ion exchange in salt or
brackish water to enable lower-quality water sources to be used. Improvement #2 could also be addressed
via indirect cultivation strategies where the sorbent interfaces the media recycle stream after the biomass
has been removed to minimize fouling. Finally, future work is recommended to evaluate AUDACity with
additional strains of fast-growing or biofuel-producing cyanobacteria and alkaliphilic microalgae.
Increasing Sorbent Lifetime by Resisting Fouling and Degradation
Sorbent lifetime in AUDACity system is challenged by fouling from biomass and biofilms, especially
during prolonged submersion in outdoor cultivation ponds. Past trials showed sorbent CO2 capacity could
decrease by ~75% with as little as 35 days of cultivation, although 70% of its capacity could be restored
with a cleaning protocol. One possible path forward involves shifting to a system where sorbents are
exposed primarily in the post-harvest medium recycle stream, which minimizes exposure to biomass. This
design reduces the risk of biofouling while still enabling effective CO2 transfer. Another possible path
forward is to increase the hydrophobicity of the polymer surface to reduce fouling; such an increase in
hydrophobicity is also desired from a water use and drying time perspective. Additionally, enhanced
cleaning protocols using sequential washes (bleach, acid, and bicarbonate) can be optimized and routinely
implemented to maintain sorbent performance. Structural degradation of polymer packets from UV
exposure and extended wetting cycles can also be mitigated through improved materials and mechanical
redesign, including use of UV-stabilized sorbent mesh and upgrading system components.
Improved Sorbents that Reduce Water Uptake and Tolerate Salt or Brackish Water
The A501 sorbent takes up 330 wt.% of is dry mass in water, which is not only a significant waste, but
also adds weight that must be transported with significant electricity use and also increases the drying
time of the sorbent. New sorbents are needed that are more hydrophobic to only absorb sufficient water to
release CO2. We believe 50 wt% water uptake is a reasonable scenario. Further, hydrophobic materials
could also be helpful for enabling use with lower-quality salt or brackish water. Many potential
deployment sites in the Southwest U.S. have access only to brackish water sources, which contain high
concentrations of anions (e.g., Cl⁻, NO3⁻, SO42⁻) that can suppress DAC sorbent function. Future work
should validate AUDACity’s performance under these conditions by running outdoor cultivation trials
with water sources containing 1–3 g/L total dissolved solids. To mitigate performance losses, bicarbonate
and carbonate can be added to increase buffer capacity and maintain sorbent CO2-loading efficiency
above 50–60% of baseline capacity. These trials would help establish practical guidelines for site
selection and water treatment strategies, allowing AUDACity to operate effectively on low-quality water
resources unsuitable for agriculture or drinking.
Indirect Cultivation Strategies
To reduce biofouling and maximize CO2 utilization, AUDACity could also be integrated with the medium
recycle loop rather than directly submerged in cultivation ponds. This configuration allows CO2 released
from sorbents to react with carbonate in the recycle stream, forming bicarbonate, which is then
reintroduced into the growth medium. This strategy not only shields sorbents from biomass contact but
also enhances carbon retention, since bicarbonate is more stable in water than dissolved CO2 gas.
Additional systems, such as hanging packet designs, where packets dip into the medium while mechanical
parts remain dry, could be used to reduce exposure of sensitive components to water-induced corrosion.

Strain and System Optimization
Future trials should evaluate AUDACity’s performance in triplicate in 4.2 m2 raceway ponds (or larger)
with multiple cyanobacterial and algal strains, including:
• Synechococcus sp. PCC 11901, a fast-growing strain with a doubling time of ~2 hours,44
• Methyl-laurate-producing Synechocystis, engineered for biofuel production,20 and
• Chlorella sorokiniana str. SLA-04, a robust microalga that thrives at pH > 10.45
Strain selection and cultivation protocols should be tailored to optimize carbon uptake and co-product
yield (e.g., protein, phycocyanin, biofuels), with performance metrics informing the TEA and LCA
models. The ability to tune operating parameters, such as loading/desorption cycles and wind exposure,
makes the platform adaptable for diverse deployment environments, including saline and brackish waters.
