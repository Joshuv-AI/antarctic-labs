Constructive interpolation and generalization rates for neural ODEs: a control perspective
Antonio Álvarez-López, Lorenzo Liverani, Enrique Zuazua

Abstract
We study supervised regression with neural ODEs (NODEs) from a control-theoretic perspective to derive explicit population-risk bounds. We focus on a widely used class of non-autonomous models with constant parameters and explicit time dependence, which we call semi-autonomous NODEs (SA-NODEs). We constructively prove that SA-NODEs are capable of exact interpolation of admissible finite datasets, and even satisfy a stronger property that we call simultaneous cell controllability (SCC): their flows can map prescribed disjoint cells into arbitrarily small target balls. This property is the mechanism that upgrades interpolation into quantitative generalization, by allowing SA-NODEs to emulate piecewise-constant nonparametric estimators. Consequently, our risk bounds recover the rates of histogram and nearest-neighbor estimators, provided the network width satisfies a conservative scaling with the sample size. Numerical experiments show that trained SA-NODEs achieve competitive—often lower—test errors than these baselines. Finally, we show that the explicit time dependence is essential. Although two-layer autonomous NODEs can interpolate geometrically nondegenerate datasets, structural obstructions prevent them from achieving SCC. These limitations, further confirmed numerically, support the view that SA-NODEs provide a minimal effective architecture for learning.

Introduction

1.1
Expressivity and generalization

Supervised learning is one of the central paradigms in machine learning, with successful applications across a wide range of problems in science and engineering
[
35
]
. The standard framework relies on a dataset of size
N\geqslant 1
, consisting of pairwise distinct inputs
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
and corresponding labels
\mathbf{y}_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
,

\mathcal{D}_{N}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N},\qquad\text{with }\mathbf{x}_{i}\neq\mathbf{x}_{j}\text{ for }i\neq j.

(1)

The goal is to approximate the unknown relationship between inputs and labels by fitting a predictor to the sample
\mathcal{D}_{N}
, typically via a parametric family
\{F_{\theta}\}_{\theta}
, where
\theta
is the learnable parameter. The choice of
\mathcal{Y}
determines the task. In this work, we focus on regression in a deterministic, noiseless setting. Accordingly, we assume throughout that
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\geqslant 2
, and that there exists a ground-truth map
y:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
such that
y(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\mathbf{y}_{i}
for all
i
.

Of course, fitting
\mathcal{D}_{N}
alone does not constitute learning: the true goal is to approximate
y
accurately across
\mathcal{X}
. To assess performance beyond the training data, we fix a test probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, often viewed as the (unknown) data-generating distribution, and define the population (true) risk by

\mathcal{R}(\theta)\coloneqq\int\ell\left(F_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}),y(\mathbf{x})\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x}),

(2)

where
\ell\colon\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
is a chosen loss function. Since
\mu
is unknown, the population risk
\mathcal{R}(\theta)
cannot be computed, let alone minimized, directly from
\mathcal{D}_{N}
. One therefore introduces the empirical risk

\mathcal{R}_{N}(\theta)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell\left(F_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\mathbf{y}_{i}\right)=\int\ell\left(F_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}),y(\mathbf{x})\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{N}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\mu_{N}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}).

(3)

Training then amounts to selecting parameters
\theta
that minimize the empirical risk. This approach introduces a fundamental tension, which is revealed by the following error decomposition:

\mathcal{R}(\theta)\leqslant\mathcal{R}_{N}(\theta)+\left|\mathcal{R}(\theta)-\mathcal{R}_{N}(\theta)\right|.

(4)

The first term measures the fit on the dataset; driving it to zero (exact interpolation) requires enough model
expressivity
. The second term is the
generalization gap
, measuring the discrepancy between the expected risk under
\mu
and its empirical proxy
\mu_{N}
. Standard approaches usually control this gap by constraining the parameter space. This strategy is not designed for the interpolation regime, where models are inherently overparameterized but may still generalize, in tension with classical capacity-based theory
[
57
,
60
,
11
,
9
]
. Our purpose is to exhibit a different mechanism, based on controllability of macroscopic regions, by which interpolation and generalization can coexist.

Controlled flows and neural ODEs

We study expressivity and generalization within a class of predictors derived from dynamical systems. More precisely, we cast supervised learning under the guise of
controlled flows
, modeled by the ODE

\begin{cases}\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=v_{\theta(t)}(\mathbf{x}(t)),\qquad t\in(0,T],\\
\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{cases}

(5)

with
T>0
fixed, and the vector field
v_{\theta(\cdot)}(\cdot):[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
that we assume to be measurable in
t
and uniformly Lipschitz in
\mathbf{x}
. Under these conditions, (
5
) induces a unique flow

\Phi_{t}^{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad t\in[0,T],

and the predictor is
F_{\theta}=\Phi_{T}^{\theta}
. This formulation offers a useful control-theoretic interpretation of deep learning
[
25
,
31
,
40
]
. The parameters
\theta(\cdot)
act as
controls
steering the dynamics, population risk minimization translates into an optimal control problem
[
24
,
36
,
27
,
17
]
, and empirical risk minimization becomes, in essence, a problem of
simultaneous controllability
of finitely many data points.

From a machine learning perspective, it is natural to parameterize
v_{\theta}
as a neural network. With this choice, (
5
) is termed a neural ODE
[
15
]
, with many variants differing in parameterizations, readouts or discretizations
[
22
,
47
,
20
,
33
,
43
,
14
]
. For a single-layer ReLU network of constant width
p\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain:

\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbf{w}_{i}(t)\big(\mathbf{a}_{i}(t)\cdot\mathbf{x}(t)+b_{i}(t)\big)_{+},\qquad t\in[0,T].

(
node
)

Here,
\theta=(\mathbf{w}_{i},\mathbf{a}_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{p}:[0,T]\to(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R})^{p}
are the controls, and
z_{+}\coloneqq\max\{z,0\}
is the ReLU.

While (
node
) is highly flexible, its effective parameter count scales with temporal discretization. Rather than mitigating this by prescribing flexible temporal parameterizations for all controls
[
20
,
43
]
, we seek simpler architectures that preserve expressivity. We focus on the
semi-autonomous neural ODE
:

\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbf{w}_{i}\big(\mathbf{a}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}(t)+b_{i}t+c_{i}\big)_{+},\qquad t\in[0,T],

(
sanode
)

derived from (
node
) by restricting
\mathbf{w}_{i}
and
\mathbf{a}_{i}
to be time-independent, and
b_{i}(t)=b_{i}t+c_{i}
. Equivalently, this corresponds to parameterizing
v_{\theta}
via a time-invariant network applied to the augmented state
(\mathbf{x},t)
. This design, standard in practice
[
15
,
29
,
47
]
, makes (
sanode
) an appealing middle ground: its relative simplicity eases implementation compared to (
node
) while retaining high expressivity
[
39
]
.

1.2
Main results

Our goal is to establish expressivity and generalization guarantees for neural ODEs. We demonstrate that (
sanode
) achieves both, avoiding structural limitations of purely autonomous models.

1.2.1
Expressivity.

The study of approximation and controllability of neural ODEs has garnered significant attention
[
53
,
19
,
2
,
37
,
55
,
49
,
38
,
5
,
16
]
. A central concept is the
universal interpolation property
(
UIP
,
[
19
,
16
]
): given finitely many pairwise distinct inputs
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and targets
\mathbf{y}_{i}
, does there exist a control
\theta(\cdot)
such that
\Phi^{\theta}_{T}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\approx\mathbf{y}_{i}
for all
i
? While
approximate
interpolation (up to arbitrary precision) holds for various models, the exact
UIP
(
\Phi^{\theta}_{T}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\mathbf{y}_{i}
for all
i
) is much more rigid. For time-dependent controls as in (
node
), constructive proofs typically introduce temporal discontinuities
[
5
,
49
]
. However, this machinery breaks down for (semi-)autonomous systems, leaving their exact
UIP
as a challenging open question.

Beyond exact interpolation of points, we introduce the notion of
simultaneous cell controllability
(SCC) to control entire regions of the input space. As illustrated in
Figure

1
, this requires the flow to map disjoint convex cells (e.g., a Voronoi partition) entirely into target balls
B(\mathbf{r}_{k},\upeta)
. Conceptually, this requirement sits between the
UIP
and the more rigid notion of continuous ensemble controllability
[
1
,
52
]
, and related distributional questions arise in the study of normalizing flows

[
26
,
50
,
3
,
28
]
.

With these notions in place, our expressivity results for (
sanode
) establish the following:

•

We prove the exact
UIP
for (
sanode
) in
Theorem

2.3
. In the spirit of
[
49
,
48
,
50
,
5
,
4
,
3
,
28
]
, our approach is constructive and yields explicit controls rather than merely proving their existence.

•

We formalize simultaneous cell controllability and prove that (
sanode
) exhibits this property in
Theorem

2.5
. Via Barron constant estimation
[
8
,
34
]
, we derive explicit bounds on the required width.

1.2.2
Generalization.

Under the setting of (
5
) and the squared Euclidean loss, the population and empirical risks (
2
)–(
3
) become:

\mathcal{R}(\theta)=\int\left\|\Phi_{T}^{\theta}(\mathbf{x})-y(\mathbf{x})\right\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\mathcal{R}_{N}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\Phi_{T}^{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-\mathbf{y}_{i}\right\|^{2}.

(6)

The central challenge remains bounding the decomposition (
4
). Existing theoretical approaches typically bound the generalization gap uniformly over a restricted parameter space
\Theta
. For instance,
[
41
]
proves that for the linear-in-control version of (
node
) under bounded Lipschitz controls, with probability
1-\delta
:

\mathcal{R}(\theta)\leqslant\mathcal{R}_{N}(\theta)+\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}|\mathcal{R}(\theta)-\mathcal{R}_{N}(\theta)|\lesssim\mathcal{R}_{N}(\theta)+\sqrt{\frac{(m+1)\log(R_{\Theta}mN)}{N}}+\frac{m\sqrt{R_{\Theta}}}{N^{1/4}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}

where
m
is the number of scalar control functions and
R_{\Theta}\geq 0
encapsulates the bounds on
\Theta
. If the controls are time-independent, this bound improves by removing the
O(N^{-1/4})
term.

While informative, such bounds highlight an inherent tension: keeping the complexity (
m
and
R_{\Theta}
) fixed as
N\to\infty
shrinks the generalization gap but may preclude exact interpolation, leaving a large empirical risk. Conversely, driving
\mathcal{R}_{N}(\theta)
to zero requires increasing complexity when
N
grows large, which deteriorates the bound. This motivates a natural
question
:

Can we design data-dependent controls
\theta_{N}
, while appropriately scaling the network width, to guarantee that the population risk
\mathcal{R}(\theta_{N})
vanishes at a quantifiable rate as
N\to\infty
?

We provide an affirmative answer by connecting (
sanode
) to nonparametric statistics. Rather than assuming a fixed parametric form, nonparametric methods—such as kernel estimators
[
44
,
59
]
, histograms or
k
-nearest-neighbors
[
30
,
56
]
—construct local approximations directly from the data. For Hölder spaces, these procedures yield explicit, minimax-optimal convergence rates
[
54
]
. This establishes a natural benchmark: any learning method claiming to generalize should ideally match these rates. We show that (
sanode
) achieves this benchmark by using simultaneous cell controllability to emulate these estimators.

Informal statement
(
Propositions

4.5
and
4.6
). Given a dataset
\mathcal{D}_{N}
, a compactly supported probability measure
\mu
, and an
\alpha
-Hölder continuous target
y
, there exists a control
\theta_{N}
such that

\mathcal{R}(\theta_{N})\lesssim\mathsf{Err}_{\mathrm{np}}+\mathsf{Err}_{\mathrm{node}},

where
\mathsf{Err}_{\mathrm{np}}
is the statistical error of a piecewise-constant nonparametric estimator built from
\mathcal{D}_{N}
, and
\mathsf{Err}_{\mathrm{node}}
measures how accurately the flow of (
sanode
) realizes this estimator. Provided the network width
p
grows sufficiently fast with
N
(
p\geq p_{N}
), the realization error is absorbed. Taking the expectation over the random draw of
\mathcal{D}_{N}
(if the inputs
\mathbf{x}_{i}
are sampled i.i.d. from
\mu
), we recover the classical rates:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{N}}\big[\mathcal{R}(\theta_{N})\big]\lesssim\begin{cases}N^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+d}}&\quad\text{for histogram estimators},\\[6.0pt]
\left(\frac{\log N}{N}\right)^{\frac{2\alpha}{d}}&\quad\text{for nearest-neighbor estimators}.\end{cases}

Our width requirement
p_{N}
—which depends on
N
and the geometry of the data partition—acts as an achievability guarantee rather than a tight bound.
It shows that the architecture can support generalization: given sufficient width, there exists a control whose true risk vanishes at an explicit rate. This circumvents the inherent tension of uniform bounds: exact interpolation and optimal statistical consistency can mathematically coexist.

Related work.

Recent literature has explored generalization across various continuous-time models. Building on the uniform bounds of
[
41
]
for the linear-in-control version of (
node
),
[
58
]
extended this capacity-constrained approach to broader nonlinear neural ODEs. In parallel, other works have studied complementary mechanisms:
[
32
]
showed that incorporating feedback enhances robustness, while
[
13
]
derived generalization bounds for neural controlled differential equations driven by irregular time series. Complementary to our viewpoint,
[
42
]
develops a
nonparametric statistical theory for likelihood-based distribution learning
with neural ODEs. Closest in spirit is
[
51
]
, which studies the
interpolation–generalization trade-off for learning diffeomorphisms via an
L^{2}
-regularized optimal control formulation.

1.2.3
The limits of autonomous models

Having established the capabilities of (
sanode
), it is natural to ask whether explicit time dependence is truly necessary. We may remove it entirely, leading to the
autonomous neural ODE
:

\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbf{w}_{i}\big(\mathbf{a}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}(t)+b_{i}\big)_{+},\qquad t\in[0,T].

(
anode
)

To compensate for the resulting structural constraints, we also consider increasing the depth of the neural network, yielding the
two-layer autonomous neural ODE
(
2anode
):

\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{p_{2}}\mathbf{w}_{j}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p_{1}}u_{ji}\left(\mathbf{a}_{ij}\cdot\mathbf{x}(t)+b_{ij}\right)_{+}+c_{j}\right)_{+},\qquad t\in[0,T],

(
2anode
)

with
\mathbf{w}_{j},\mathbf{a}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
u_{ji},b_{ij},c_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
for
p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
.

While we prove in
Theorem

2.9
that (
2anode
) can achieve the exact
UIP
(provided the data satisfy the geometric condition (
13
)), we also discuss obstructions to simultaneous cell controllability in autonomous models like (
anode
) and (
2anode
). Topological constraints prevent autonomous flows from being universal approximators of arbitrary continuous maps
[
22
]
; however, our study reveals a more precise gap: they can satisfy the
UIP
but fail at cell routing. This distinction, illustrated experimentally in
Section

5.2
, confirms that among the models we consider, (
sanode
) provides the simplest setting in which both properties coexist.

2
Controllability of neural ODEs

2.1
Simultaneous point controllability

We begin with our central notion of expressivity. Consider the general model (
5
) with flow map
\Phi_{t}^{\theta}
. Since
\Phi_{T}^{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a homeomorphism, and hence injective, exact interpolation is impossible if distinct inputs
\mathbf{x}_{i}\neq\mathbf{x}_{j}
are assigned the same target. To avoid this, we restrict our attention to datasets satisfying

\mathbf{x}_{i}\neq\mathbf{x}_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{y}_{i}\neq\mathbf{y}_{j}\qquad\text{for all }i\neq j.

(8)

Any dataset satisfying (
8
) will be called
admissible
.

Definition 1
.

We say that the control system associated with (
5
) possesses the
approximate universal interpolation property (
UIP
)
if for every
\varepsilon>0
, every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and every dataset
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
admissible in the sense of (
8
), there exist
T>0
and an admissible control
\theta
such that the flow map
\Phi^{\theta}_{T}
generated by (
5
) satisfies

\|\Phi^{\theta}_{T}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-\mathbf{y}_{i}\|\leq\varepsilon,\qquad\text{for all }i\in[N].

(9)

If (
9
) holds with
\varepsilon=0
, we say that the system possesses the
(exact)
UIP

.

When the vector field is a neural network, the admissible controls in
Definition

1
are understood to range over all widths. Thus, the
UIP
is a property of the whole family, not of a fixed-width model.

Remark 2.1

(Time rescaling)
.

In the definition above, the time horizon
T
is allowed to be chosen freely. However, this is equivalent to a fixed-horizon formulation whenever the family of vector fields under consideration is closed under positive scalar multiplication and time reparameterization. Indeed, for any
T_{\ast}>0
, the rescaled curve
\mathbf{y}(s)\coloneqq\mathbf{x}(sT/T_{\ast})
solves an analogous system on
[0,T_{\ast}]
driven by the vector field
v_{\theta(sT/T_{\ast})}T/T_{\ast}
, which remains in the same class. All models considered in this work satisfy this property, provided there are no uniform bounds imposed on the parameter space.

Before detailing our contributions, we briefly review known results for the general model (
node
). The
UIP
was proved in
[
49
]
using
p=1
neurons. We state its generalization to arbitrary
p\geq 1
from
[
5
]
.

Theorem 2.2

(Simultaneous point controllability for (
node
)
[
5
, Theorem 1]
)
.

For every
d\geq 2
,
T>0
and
p\in\mathbb{N}
, the system (
node
) possesses the exact
UIP
. Furthermore, for any admissible dataset of size
N
(in the sense of (
8
)), the control can be chosen piecewise constant in time with
2\left\lceil N/p\right\rceil-1
jumps.

The constructive proof of this theorem exploits the freedom afforded by time-dependent parameters. The key device is the orthogonality constraint
\mathbf{w}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{a}_{i}=0
: geometrically, this forces the flow generated by each neuron to act as a shear transformation parallel to its own activation hyperplane. This decoupling makes the dynamics tractable and allows the control strategy to split naturally into two phases separated by a single switch. In the first interval,
N
parallel hyperplanes steer
d-1
coordinates of the data simultaneously; after the switch, the hyperplanes reorient to handle the remaining coordinate.

By contrast, for (
sanode
) the vectors
\mathbf{w}_{i}
and
\mathbf{a}_{i}
are time-independent, so the reorientation mechanism described above is no longer available. The compensating source of flexibility is the explicit time dependence in the argument
\mathbf{a}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b_{i}t+c_{i}
, which makes the activation hyperplanes translate at a constant velocity given by
b_{i}
. Our first new result—whose proof is deferred to
Section

7.1.1
—shows that this single degree of freedom is sufficient to recover exact controllability without requiring any temporal switching.

Theorem 2.3

(Simultaneous point controllability for (
sanode
))
.

For every
d\geq 2
and
T>0
, the system (
sanode
) possesses the exact
UIP
. Moreover, for any admissible dataset of size
N
(in the sense of (
8
)), exact interpolation can be achieved with width
p=2N
.

2.2
Simultaneous cell controllability

The
UIP
captures the capacity to interpolate any admissible finite dataset. To control the population risk, however, one needs the flow to behave well across entire regions of the input space, rather than only at finitely many points. This leads to the following stronger controllability notion.

Definition 2
.

We say that the control system associated with (
5
) possesses the property of
simultaneous cell controllability
(
SCC
) if, for every finite family of pairwise disjoint compact convex sets
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, every collection of target points
\{\mathbf{r}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and every
\upeta>0
, there exist
T>0
and an admissible control
\theta
such that the flow map
\Phi^{\theta}_{T}
generated by (
5
) satisfies

\Phi_{T}^{\theta}(A_{k})\subset B(\mathbf{r}_{k},\upeta)\qquad\text{for all }k\in[K].

(10)

Remark 2.4
.

Although framed here in the context of flows, simultaneous cell controllability is an architecture-independent notion. It applies to any parametric map
F_{\theta}
, from neural networks to classical regression models.

The property of
SCC
is stronger than the approximate
UIP
, as setting
A_{k}=\{\mathbf{x}_{k}\}
in (
10
) directly recovers condition (
9
). The fundamental shift is that the flow must now simultaneously route whole macroscopic regions rather than isolated points. Despite this added complexity, the following theorem establishes that (
sanode
) exhibits this property, and provides a sufficient width threshold. This is derived in
Section

7.1.3
by constructing a reference vector field and applying quantitative Barron-type estimates (with the geometric constants detailed in
Remark

2.6
).

Theorem 2.5

(Simultaneous cell controllability for (
sanode
))
.

Let
d,K\geq 2
and
R,T>0
. Let
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
be a family of pairwise disjoint, compact, and convex subsets of
I_{R}\coloneqq[-R,R]^{d}
, and let
\{\mathbf{r}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be pairwise distinct target vectors. Then there exist

\mathfrak{C}=\mathfrak{C}\left(d,T,R,(\mathbf{r}_{k})_{k},(A_{k})_{k}\right)>0,\qquad\mathfrak{M}=\mathfrak{M}\left(d,T,R,(\mathbf{r}_{k})_{k}\right)>0

and
\upeta_{0}\in(0,1]
such that for every
\upeta\in(0,\upeta_{0}]
and every integer

p\geq\mathfrak{C}\,\upeta^{-2-\mathfrak{C}}\left(1-\log\upeta\right)^{2},

(11)

there exists a control
\theta
of width
p
for (
sanode
) such that

1.

\Phi_{T}^{\theta}(A_{k})\subset B(\mathbf{r}_{k},\upeta)
for all
k\in[K]
;

2.

\|\Phi_{T}^{\theta}\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{R})}\leq\mathfrak{M}
.

If the targets are not necessarily pairwise distinct, the same conclusions hold for each fixed
\upeta>0
; however, the constant
\mathfrak{C}
is no longer uniform in
\upeta
.

Remark 2.6

(Explicit geometric constants)
.

The constants
\mathfrak{C}
,
\mathfrak{M}
, and
\upeta_{0}
in

2.5
may be chosen explicitly. A possible choice, obtained by combining

7.6
with

, is governed by the geometry of the cells and their targets. Assume that the target vectors
\mathbf{r}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{r}_{K}
are pairwise distinct, let

D_{*}\coloneqq\max_{k\in[K]}\operatorname{diam}(A_{k}),\qquad s_{*}\coloneqq\min_{i\neq j}\operatorname{dist}(A_{i},A_{j}),

and let
\gamma_{k}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be smooth pairwise disjoint curves such that
\gamma_{k}(0)\in A_{k}
,
\gamma_{k}(T)=\mathbf{r}_{k}
,
\gamma_{k}
is constant on
[0,2T/3]
, and
\|\gamma_{k}(t)\|<1+\max_{k\in[K]}\|\mathbf{r}_{k}\|+\sqrt{d}\,R
for all

—their existence is guaranteed for

, see

. Define

m\coloneqq\min_{t\in[0,T]}\min_{i\neq j}\|\gamma_{i}(t)-\gamma_{j}(t)\|,\qquad G\coloneqq 1+\max_{k\in[K]}\max_{1\leq j\leq d+5}\left\|\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{j}\gamma_{k}}{\,\mathrm{d}t^{j}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)},

and let

L\coloneqq C_{d,T}\left[\frac{G}{m}+\frac{1+D_{*}/s_{*}}{T}\left(1+\log_{+}(4D_{*})\right)\right],

and

B\coloneqq C_{d,T}\,K\left[TG^{d+5}(m^{d}+m^{-4})+\frac{(D_{*}+s_{*})^{d+1}(1+s_{*}^{-(d+4)})}{T}\left(1+\log_{+}(4D_{*})\right)\right].

Exploiting these quantities—see the proofs of

7.6
and
7.7
for a derivation—one may take

\displaystyle\upeta_{0}\coloneqq\min\left\{1,\frac{s_{*}}{2},\frac{m}{2}\right\},\quad\mathfrak{M}\coloneqq 2+\max\left\{T,\max_{k\in[K]}\|\mathbf{r}_{k}\|+\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{d}\,R\right\},

(12)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\coloneqq\max\left\{3,\,2TL,\,4C_{d}^{2}\,\mathfrak{M}^{4}\,T^{2}\,B^{2}e^{2TL}\right\}.

Thus, while
\mathfrak{M}
depends solely on the domain size and target locations,

captures the geometric bottleneck of the partition: the term

in

\exp(c/s_{*})
in the factor

as

.

Remark 2.7

(Width requirement and sharpness)
.

The width condition (
11
) provides a sufficient condition rather than a sharp complexity estimate. As noted in
Remark

2.6
, the geometric constant
\mathfrak{C}
incurs an exponential penalty as the minimum cell separation

shrinks. Consequently, for the refined partitions used later to establish statistical consistency, this geometric bottleneck can force a super-polynomial threshold

as the dataset size

We emphasize that this scaling acts purely as an achievability guarantee. The specific bound arises from our chosen proof strategy—specifically, applying the uniform approximation results of
[

. Employing alternative tools (e.g.,
[

2.3
Autonomous models

Having established the
UIP
and
SCC
for (
sanode
), it is natural to ask whether explicit time dependence is truly necessary. To answer this, we remove it entirely and analyze the autonomous model (
anode
) and its two-layer variant (
2anode
).

While
[
5
]
establishes the approximate
UIP
for (
anode
)—including explicit error decay rates as the width grows—bridging the gap from approximate to exact interpolation remains unresolved:

Open Problem.

Does the exact
UIP
hold for the single-layer autonomous system (
anode
)?

The difficulty is structural: unlike time-dependent models, which can concatenate flow stages by switching controls, autonomous single-layer fields are highly rigid. Specifically, they cannot be spatially localized. Because they are finite superpositions of ridge functions—whose Fourier transforms are supported on one-dimensional sets—such nontrivial vector fields can never belong to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Though elementary, this fact seems absent from standard references, so we provide a proof.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
d\geq 2
and let
\sigma\in\mathscr{C}^{0}(\mathbb{R})
have at most polynomial growth.
For any
p\geq 1
, define

g(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}w_{j}\,\sigma(\mathbf{a}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b_{j}),\qquad(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}),\qquad\text{with }\mathbf{a}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad w_{j},b_{j}\in\mathbb{R}.

If
g\not\equiv 0
, then
g\notin L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In particular,
g
cannot have compact support unless
g\equiv 0
.

Proof.

For each
j
, the Fourier transform of the ridge function
\mathbf{x}\mapsto\sigma(\mathbf{a}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b_{j})
is supported, in the sense of tempered distributions, on the line
L_{j}=\{\lambda\mathbf{a}_{j}:\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.
By linearity,
\widehat{g}
is then supported in the finite union
\bigcup_{j=1}^{p}L_{j}
, which has Lebesgue measure zero in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
since
d\geq 2
by hypothesis.

Suppose
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then, by Plancherel,
\widehat{g}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as well. But
\widehat{g}=0
a.e. because an
L^{2}
–function supported on a null set must vanish almost everywhere, and therefore
g\equiv 0
.
∎

While
Lemma

2.8
does not strictly rule out exact interpolation, it explains why “pointwise” steering strategies are exceptionally hard to implement with (
anode
) alone. A minimal way to restore localization without reintroducing time dependence is to add one compositional layer. By composing ReLU units, the two-layer field (
2anode
) can generate vector fields supported on convex polytopes (see
Lemma

7.3
below), enabling the construction of localized controls.

For (
2anode
), we require the stronger assumption that input-output segments are mutually disjoint:

[\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i}]\cap[\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{y}_{j}]=\varnothing\qquad\text{for all }i\neq j,

(13)

where
[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]
denotes the line segment between
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
. Note that this naturally implies
\mathbf{x}_{i}\neq\mathbf{y}_{j}
for
i\neq j
.

Theorem 2.9

(Simultaneous point controllability for (
2anode
))
.

Let
N\geq 1
,
d\geq 2
, and
T>0
be fixed.
Consider any dataset
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
admissible in the sense of (

), and additionally satisfying (

\Phi_{T}^{\theta}
generated by (

), with

The proof is deferred to

.
Although (

Remark 2.10

(Dimensional constraints)
.

Condition (

) depends strongly on the dimension

[\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i}]
, these paths must not cross. In

Nevertheless, the

without requiring (

At this point, the distinction between point interpolation and cell routing becomes essential. While autonomous systems can map finite point clouds arbitrarily close to their targets by letting isolated trajectories bypass each other in

(
[

), routing macroscopic cells

introduces structural obstructions. Since autonomous trajectories cannot cross, models like (
anode

lies behind

Nonparametric estimation

In this section, we recall the construction and fundamental properties of piecewise-constant nonparametric estimators. Our analysis focuses on two canonical approaches:

1.

Histogram estimators
, which partition the input domain using a fixed uniform grid.

2.

Nearest-neighbor estimators
, which rely on Voronoi tessellations
[
6
,
12
]
.

While the results in this section are standard, proofs are collected in
Section

7.2
, as the precise statements we need do not appear verbatim in the literature. We refer the interested reader to
[
30
,
56
]
and references therein for a deeper dive into the theory of nonparametric estimators.

3.1
Histogram estimator

We first analyze the histogram approach. Fix
R>0
, a resolution
h\in(0,2R]
and let

\mathcal{K}\coloneqq\left\{0,\;\dots,\;\left\lceil 2R/h\right\rceil-1\right\}^{d}

(14)

be the corresponding multi-index set. For each
k\in\mathcal{K}
, we define the cube

Q_{k}\coloneqq I_{R}\cap\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[-R+k_{j}h,\,-R+(k_{j}+1)h\right),

(15)

ensuring that the collection
\{Q_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
forms a partition of
I_{R}
up to a
\mu
-null boundary set. The total number of cells is given by

K_{h}\coloneqq|\mathcal{K}|=\left\lceil\frac{2R}{h}\right\rceil^{d}\lesssim h^{-d},

(16)

and the diameter of each cell satisfies
\operatorname{diam}(Q_{k})\leqslant\sqrt{d}\,h
. We approximate
y(\cdot)
by averaging its values within each cell. This leads to the definition of both a population-level and an empirical estimator:

•

The population average
y_{h}\colon I_{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the best piecewise-constant approximation of
y
in
L^{2}(\mu)
:

y_{h}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\mathbf{s}_{k}\,\mathbf{1}_{Q_{k}}(\mathbf{x}),

(17)

where the coefficients
\mathbf{s}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are defined by

\mathbf{s}_{k}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu(Q_{k})}\int_{Q_{k}}y(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x}),\qquad&\text{if }\mu(Q_{k})>0,\\[8.0pt]
0,\qquad&\text{if }\mu(Q_{k})=0.\end{cases}

(18)

Note that
y_{h}(\cdot)
is in fact the best piecewise-constant approximation of
y(\cdot)
in
L^{2}(\mu)
, as each
\mathbf{s}_{k}
minimizes the local
L^{2}(\mu)
-error over
Q_{k}
.

•

The empirical average
y_{N,h}:I_{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, instead, is computed directly from the dataset:

y_{N,h}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\mathbf{S}_{k}\,\mathbf{1}_{Q_{k}}(\mathbf{x}).

(19)

Letting
N_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{Q_{k}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
denote the number of samples falling into
Q_{k}
, the coefficients
\mathbf{S}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are now defined as

\mathbf{S}_{k}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{i:\mathbf{x}_{i}\in Q_{k}}y(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\qquad&\text{if }N_{k}\geqslant 1,\\[8.0pt]
0,\qquad&\text{if }N_{k}=0.\end{cases}

(20)

A schematic representation of
y_{N,h}(\cdot)
when
d=2
can be seen in
Figure

3
(central column). We now establish the convergence rate for the empirical histogram estimator.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{N}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y(\mathbf{x}_{i}))\}_{i=1}^{N}
with
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{N}
drawn i.i.d. from
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{ac}}(I_{R})
, and
y\in\mathscr{C}^{0}(I_{R};\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then, for any
h\in(0,2R]
, the estimator
y_{N,h}(\cdot)
defined in (
19
) satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left[\left\|y-y_{N,h}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\right]\;\lesssim\;\omega_{y}(\sqrt{d}\,h)^{2}\;+\;\frac{\|y\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{R})}^{2}}{Nh^{d}},

(21)

where
\omega_{y}(t)\coloneqq\sup\left\{\|y(\mathbf{x})-y(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\|\colon\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in I_{R},\,\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|\leq t\right\}
is the modulus of continuity of
y(\cdot)
.

Figure 3
:

y:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, represented in both 3D (height and color) and 2D (color only).

Center:

gray
cells indicate regions with no training data.

The bound (
21
) captures the standard bias-variance trade-off in nonparametric estimation
[
30
]
. The bias term
\omega_{y}(\sqrt{d}\,h)^{2}
measures the spatial approximation error and vanishes as
h\to 0
. Conversely, the statistical variance scales as
1/(Nh^{d})
; as the grid becomes finer, fewer samples fall into each cell. Driving the total error to zero therefore requires the dataset size
N
to grow strictly faster than the number of cells.

The continuity assumption on
y
can be relaxed at the cost of losing an explicit algebraic decay rate for the bias. If
y\in L^{\infty}(I_{R};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the bound (
21
) holds with the bias replaced by
\|y-y_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}
, which still vanishes as
h\to 0
(e.g., via standard martingale convergence on nested grids
[
23
, Theorem 4.4.6]
). For
y\in L^{2}(\mu;\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the bias similarly vanishes, though extending the
(Nh^{d})^{-1}
variance bound generally requires further assumptions. We do not pursue this level of generality here.

Conversely, by imposing stronger regularity on
y(\cdot)
one can quantify the modulus of continuity. We focus on the Hölder case, characterized by

\omega_{y}(t)\leqslant L_{y}t^{\alpha}\qquad\text{for some }\alpha\in(0,1]\text{ and }L_{y}>0.

Optimizing the grid resolution
h
yields the following minimal statistical rate.

Corollary 3.2
.

Suppose
y\in\mathscr{C}^{0,\alpha}(I_{R};\mathbb{R}^{d})
for some
\alpha\in(0,1]
. Then, the estimator
y_{h}
from (
17
) satisfies

\|y-y_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\;\lesssim\;h^{2\alpha}.

(22)

Moreover,
h=(2R)\wedge(N^{-\frac{1}{2\alpha+d}})\eqqcolon h_{N}
yields the minimal convergence rate in (
21
),

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left[\left\|y-y_{N,h_{N}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\right]\;\lesssim\;N^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+d}}.

(23)

Remark 3.3

(Sample complexity)
.

The approximation by Riemann sums on a fixed grid of size
h
yields (
22
). To ensure a squared error below
\varepsilon>0
, the number of cells must scale as
K_{h}\gtrsim\varepsilon^{-\frac{d}{2\alpha}}
. Consequently, the sample complexity to guarantee an expected risk below
\varepsilon
in (
23
) scales as:

N\;\gtrsim\;\varepsilon^{-\frac{2\alpha+d}{2\alpha}}\;=\;\varepsilon^{-1}\cdot\varepsilon^{-\frac{d}{2\alpha}}.

(24)

We observe that the sample complexity naturally decomposes into the product of the Monte Carlo rate
\varepsilon^{-1}
(associated with estimating a mean with fixed variance) and a geometric factor
\varepsilon^{-d/(2\alpha)}
. This reflects the curse of dimensionality: whereas numerical integration computes a single global average, function recovery requires estimating
K_{h}
distinct local averages simultaneously to resolve the spatial structure of
y(\cdot)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Remark 3.4

(Minimax optimality)
.

In terms of the number of cells
K_{h}\asymp h^{-d}
, the bias estimate (
22
) yields

\|y-y_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\lesssim K_{h}^{-\alpha/d}.

This matches the optimal rate among all
linear
approximation spaces of dimension
K_{h}
, as captured by the Kolmogorov
n
-width

d_{n}(\mathcal{F})\;\coloneqq\;\inf_{\dim(V)=n}\ \sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\ \inf_{g\in V}\|f-g\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}.

For the isotropic
\mathscr{C}^{0,\alpha}
-Hölder ball
\mathcal{F}
, one has
d_{n}(\mathcal{F})\asymp n^{-\alpha/d}

[
45
]
, an order attained by uniform piecewise-constant grids such as
y_{h}
. Moreover, this exponent cannot be improved even by
adaptive
(nonlinear) methods with continuous parameter selection: the corresponding manifold
n
-widths exhibit the exact same decay rate on Besov (and hence Hölder) balls
[
21
, Eq. (9.4)]
. Adaptivity can only yield strictly faster rates on smaller, spatially inhomogeneous target classes
[
18
]
.

The rate
N^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+d}}
matches the minimax lower bound for nonparametric regression under additive label noise
[
54
]
. When
\mathbf{y}_{i}=y(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\text{noise}
, individual data points are unreliable, making local spatial averaging the optimal strategy to cancel out stochastic fluctuations.

In contrast, our setting is strictly
noiseless
because
\mathbf{y}_{i}=y(\mathbf{x}_{i})
. Here, spatial averaging is overly conservative and wastes the precision of perfect data. Enforcing
strict interpolation
bypasses the statistical variance penalty, yielding faster rates. For instance, assuming
y\in\mathscr{C}^{k,\alpha}(I_{R};\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
k\geq 0
integer and
\alpha\in(0,1]
, spline interpolants
m_{N}
achieve
[
10
]

\|y-m_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\;\lesssim\;\|y-m_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{R})}^{2}\;\lesssim\;\left(\frac{\log N}{N}\right)^{\frac{2(k+\alpha)}{d}}.

(25)

To exploit the lack of noise, we turn to the simplest interpolation scheme: the nearest-neighbor estimator.

3.2
Nearest-neighbor estimator

We now partition
I_{R}
by assigning each point to its nearest neighbor within
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Unlike the histogram approach, where the grid is fixed and independent of the data, this partition naturally adapts to the local density of
\mathcal{D}_{N}
: dense regions produce smaller cells, while sparse regions yield larger ones. This adaptivity is exactly what yields faster approximation rates.

Formally, let
\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
denote the covering of
I_{R}
by the closed Voronoi cells generated by
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
:

V_{i}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{x}\in I_{R}:\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|\leq\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|\text{ for all }j\neq i\}.

(26)

For any
\mathbf{x}\in I_{R}
, we define its nearest neighbor within the dataset by

x_{\mathrm{NN}}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\argmin_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{N}\}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|.

(27)

where any ties on the cell boundaries are broken arbitrarily. Using this mapping, we define the nearest-neighbor interpolant of
y
, denoted by
y_{N}^{V}
, as

y_{N}^{V}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq y\left(x_{\mathrm{NN}}(\mathbf{x})\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}y(\mathbf{x}_{i})\,\mathbf{1}_{V_{i}}(\mathbf{x}).

(28)

A schematic representation of
y_{N}^{V}
for
d=2
can be seen in the rightmost column of
Figure

3
. The accuracy of
y_{N}^{V}
is governed purely by the covering radius of
\mathcal{D}_{N}
, defined by

R_{N}\coloneqq\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in I_{R}}\min_{i\in[N]}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in I_{R}}\|\mathbf{x}-x_{\mathrm{NN}}(\mathbf{x})\|.

(29)

The smaller
R_{N}
, the closer every point in
I_{R}
is to some input
\mathbf{x}_{i}
. For i.i.d. samples drawn from a density bounded below on
I_{R}
, the expected scaling of
R_{N}
is characterized by
[
46
, Theorem 2.1]
, which yields

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{N}}[R_{N}^{q}]\;\lesssim\;\left(\frac{\log N}{N}\right)^{q/d}\quad\text{for any }q>0.

(30)

The following proposition uses this rate to quantify the
L^{2}
-error for Hölder targets:

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
N\geq 2
and
\mathcal{D}_{N}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y(\mathbf{x}_{i}))\}_{i=1}^{N}
with

\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{N}
drawn i.i.d. from
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ac}}(I_{R})
.
Assume that
\mu
admits a density
\rho
with

\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in I_{R}}\rho(\mathbf{x})>0
, and let

y\in\mathscr{C}^{0,\alpha}(I_{R};\mathbb{R}^{d})
for some
\alpha\in(0,1]
.
Then the nearest-neighbor interpolant
y_{N}^{V}
defined in
(
28
) satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{N}}\Big[\|y-y_{N}^{V}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\Big]\lesssim\left(\frac{\log N}{N}\right)^{\frac{2\alpha}{d}}.

(31)

Note that the rate in (
31
) is strictly faster than the corresponding rate of
O\left(N^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+d}}\right)
derived in (
23
).

4
Generalization via simultaneous cell controllability

The previous two sections provide the ingredients needed for our subsequent analysis. On the one hand,
Section

2
shows that (
sanode
) can map prescribed convex cells into small target balls. On the other hand,
Section

3
provides nonparametric estimators with explicit statistical risks. We now combine both ingredients to derive generalization bounds.

4.1
General bounds

To derive quantitative rates, we assume that
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{N}
are drawn i.i.d. from
\mu
.
1
1

1

The deterministic core of the argument below, however, is purely geometric and applies to any fixed dataset.
This induces the expected population risk over all realizations of the training sample:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left[\mathcal{R}(\theta_{N})\right]\coloneqq\int_{I_{R}}\cdots\int_{I_{R}}\mathcal{R}\left(\theta_{N}(\mathcal{D}_{N})\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x}_{1})\cdots\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x}_{N}).

(32)

Our approach proceeds in two steps. First, we construct from
\mathcal{D}_{N}
a piecewise-constant proxy
y_{N}(\cdot)
that estimates
y(\cdot)
. Second, we design a control
\theta_{N}
such that
\Phi_{T}^{\theta_{N}}
approximates
y_{N}
on most of the domain.

Let
\{P_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
be a partition of
I_{R}
into convex cells, let
\{\mathbf{r}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be target values computed from
\mathcal{D}_{N}
(e.g., via cell averages), and define the piecewise-constant estimator

5.2
Necessity of time-dependence

As discussed in

\begin{cases}\text{\eqref{eq:saNODE} params}=(2d+2)p+d\\
\text{\eqref{eq:aNODE} params}=(2d+1)p+d\end{cases}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_{\mathrm{aNODE}}=\left\lceil\frac{2d+2}{2d+1}\,p_{\mathrm{saNODE}}\right\rceil.

6
Conclusions and perspectives

We have developed a controllability-based framework to study generalization in neural ODEs. A central insight of our work is that point interpolation, while necessary for expressivity, cannot fully explain generalization. To bridge this gap, we introduced
simultaneous cell controllability
—the ability of the flow to compress entire input regions toward prescribed targets. We proved that the semi-autonomous model (
sanode
) satisfies this property, enabling it to approximate piecewise-constant nonparametric estimators.

This connection yields explicit population-risk bounds. By constructing a single, data-dependent flow that realizes a statistically meaningful proxy, we demonstrate that exact interpolation and quantitative generalization can coexist. Our results thus establish that overparameterized neural ODEs can emulate nonparametric procedures, providing an achievability guarantee rather than an efficient scaling law. This approach departs from standard uniform generalization bounds, which evaluate the worst-case error over an entire hypothesis class and force a rigid trade-off between expressivity and global complexity.

Finally, we find that explicit time dependence is essential for our generalization mechanism. Without it, topological obstructions break simultaneous cell controllability, preventing autonomous architectures from routing macroscopic regions even when point interpolation is preserved.

We conclude by outlining natural directions opened for future work.

Saturation of regularity and higher-order flows.

The bounds established here rely on piecewise-constant (order-
0
) approximations, whose statistical bias saturates at Lipschitz continuity (
\alpha=1
). We deliberately favor this baseline because it naturally extends the exact
UIP
. Nevertheless, if the ground truth is smoother (
y\in\mathscr{C}^{m,\alpha}
), replacing the order-
0
proxy with a degree-
m
cell-wise polynomial would yield faster statistical rates. Transferring this higher-order rate to
\Phi_{T}^{\theta}
, however, would require a complex analogue of
Definition

2
capable of realizing local higher-degree polynomials up to tolerance
\upeta
. Formulating and proving such higher-order controllability remains an interesting open problem.

Quantitative sharpness.

While our proofs establish the vanishing of population risk, the required network width grows super-polynomially with
N
due to worst-case geometric bottlenecks in the partition. Closing the gap between these theoretical thresholds and the much smaller widths observed in practice requires sharper quantitative controllability estimates.

From static maps to trajectories.

Finally, our controllability viewpoint suggests a natural extension from static regression to dynamical-system learning. While this work uses neural ODEs to approximate fixed terminal maps, applications often involve continuous sequences. Extending simultaneous cell controllability to steer entire trajectory tubes—rather than just terminal cells—would provide a framework for trajectory-level generalization bounds in sequence modeling.

Remark 7.2
.

Theorem

extends to any neural ODE of the form

\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbf{w}_{j}\left(\mathbf{a}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{x}(t)+f_{j}(t)+c_{j}\right)_{+},\qquad t\in[0,T],

provided each

is strictly monotone. Geometrically, the hyperplane velocities then vary over time rather than remain constant. We emphasize, however, that the linear choice

For instance, one may take

, where

rescales time and

bounds the hyperplane speed. In this case, the evolution splits into two phases:

1. Control:

and the exact-control objective is attained in this phase.

2. Stationary:
As

Proof of

.

Let

Let

\|\Phi_{T}^{\theta}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{r}_{k}\|\leq\|\Phi_{T}^{\theta}(\mathbf{x})-\Psi_{T}(\mathbf{x})\|+\|\Psi_{T}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{r}_{k}\|\leq\frac{\upeta}{2}+\frac{\upeta}{4}<\upeta.

Furthermore, using (
83
), we can easily verify (ii):

\|\Phi_{T}^{\theta}\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{R})}\leq\|\Psi_{T}\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{R})}+\|\Phi_{T}^{\theta}-\Psi_{T}\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{R})}\leq(\mathfrak{M}-1)+\frac{\upeta}{2}<\mathfrak{M},

which holds because
\upeta\leq 1
.

Finally, consider the case where the targets
\mathbf{r}_{k}
are not necessarily pairwise distinct. We can slightly perturb them to choose auxiliary targets
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{k}
that are pairwise distinct and satisfy:

\|\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{k}-\mathbf{r}_{k}\|<\upeta/4\qquad\text{for all }k.

Applying the previous argument to these auxiliary targets
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{K}
with a tighter tolerance of
\upeta/2
, we obtain, for sufficiently large
p
, a control
\theta
such that
\Phi_{T}^{\theta}(A_{k})\subset B(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{k},\upeta/2)
. By the triangle inequality, it immediately follows that
\Phi_{T}^{\theta}(A_{k})\subset B(\mathbf{r}_{k},\upeta)
. Since the mutual separation of the auxiliary targets may be of order
\upeta
,
the path-separation parameter
m
, and hence the constants produced by the previous argument, need not remain uniform as
\upeta\downarrow 0
.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

3.1
.

By the squared-sum inequality, the expected error can be decomposed into a bias and a variance component:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left[\left\|y-y_{N,h}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\right]\leq 2\left\|y-y_{h}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}+2\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left[\left\|y_{h}-y_{N,h}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\right],

where
y_{h}
and
y_{N,h}
are the population and empirical estimators defined in (
17
) and (
19
), respectively. We bound each term separately.

Bias.
Fix a cell
Q_{k}
with
p_{k}\coloneqq\mu(Q_{k})>0
. The population coefficient
\mathbf{s}_{k}
is the conditional expectation of
y(\cdot)
on
Q_{k}
. By Jensen’s inequality, for any
\mathbf{x}\in Q_{k}
:

\|y(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{s}_{k}\|=\Biggl\|\frac{1}{p_{k}}\int_{Q_{k}}\left(y(\mathbf{x})-y(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\Biggr\|\leq\frac{1}{p_{k}}\int_{Q_{k}}\|y(\mathbf{x})-y(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

For any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in Q_{k}
, the distance satisfies:
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|\leq\operatorname{diam}(Q_{k})\leq\sqrt{d}\,h.

Consequently,
\|y(\mathbf{x})-y(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\|\leq\omega_{y}(\sqrt{d}\,h)
, which implies
\|y(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{s}_{k}\|\leq\omega_{y}(\sqrt{d}\,h)
. Since cells with
p_{k}=0
do not contribute to the
L^{2}(\mu)
norm, integrating over the partition yields:

\|y-y_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\int_{Q_{k}}\|y(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{s}_{k}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})\leq\omega_{y}(\sqrt{d}\,h)^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\mu(Q_{k})=\omega_{y}(\sqrt{d}\,h)^{2},

(84)

where
\mathcal{K}
is the index set defined in (
14
).

Variance.
Since
y_{h}|_{Q_{k}}=\mathbf{s}_{k}
and
y_{N,h}|_{Q_{k}}=\mathbf{S}_{k}
, the variance term becomes:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left[\left\|y_{h}-y_{N,h}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\right]=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}p_{k}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left[\|\mathbf{s}_{k}-\mathbf{S}_{k}\|^{2}\right].

(85)

Fix an index
k
and let
N_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{Q_{k}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
denote the number of samples falling into
Q_{k}
. Because the inputs are drawn i.i.d. from
\mu
,
N_{k}
follows a binomial distribution,
N_{k}\sim\mathrm{Binom}(N,p_{k})
.

Conditionally on
N_{k}=n\geq 1
, the coefficient
\mathbf{S}_{k}
is the empirical average of
n
i.i.d. random variables
\mathbf{Y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{Y}_{n}
distributed as
y(\mathbf{X})
for
\mathbf{X}\sim\mu(\cdot\mid Q_{k})
. By definition,
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Y}_{1}]=\mathbf{s}_{k}
. The variance of this empirical average is exactly:

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{S}_{k}-\mathbf{s}_{k}\|^{2}\mid N_{k}=n\right]=\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\Biggl\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\mathbf{Y}_{j}-\mathbf{s}_{k}\right)\Biggr\|^{2}\Biggr]=\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{Y}_{1}-\mathbf{s}_{k}\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{\|y\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{R})}^{2}}{n}.

If
N_{k}=0
, we defined
\mathbf{S}_{k}=0
, which gives
\|\mathbf{S}_{k}-\mathbf{s}_{k}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{s}_{k}\|^{2}\leq\|y\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{R})}^{2}
. Taking the unconditional expectation over the binomial variable
N_{k}
, we obtain:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left[\|\mathbf{S}_{k}-\mathbf{s}_{k}\|^{2}\right]\leq\|y\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{R})}^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\{N_{k}>0\}}}{N_{k}}\right]+\mathbb{P}(N_{k}=0)\right).

We bound the two terms in the parenthesis. First,
\mathbb{P}(N_{k}=0)=(1-p_{k})^{N}\leq e^{-Np_{k}}
. Second, utilizing the elementary inequality
\frac{1}{n}\leq\frac{2}{n+1}
for
n\geq 1
, we evaluate the expectation via an integral identity for the binomial generating function:

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\{N_{k}>0\}}}{N_{k}}\right]\leq 2\,\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N_{k}+1}\right]=2\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}[t^{N_{k}}]\,\mathrm{d}t=2\int_{0}^{1}(1-p_{k}+p_{k}t)^{N}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{2}{p_{k}(N+1)}.

Substituting these estimates into (
85
) yields:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left[\left\|y_{h}-y_{N,h}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\right]\leq\|y\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{R})}^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\left(\frac{2}{N+1}+p_{k}e^{-Np_{k}}\right).

Recall from (
16
) that the total number of cells is
K_{h}\lesssim h^{-d}
. Moreover, using
\sup_{p\geq 0}pe^{-Np}=(eN)^{-1}
, we can bound the sum uniformly:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left[\left\|y_{h}-y_{N,h}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\right]\leq\|y\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{R})}^{2}\left(\frac{2K_{h}}{N+1}+\frac{K_{h}}{eN}\right)\lesssim\frac{\|y\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{R})}^{2}K_{h}}{N}\lesssim\frac{\|y\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{R})}^{2}}{Nh^{d}}.

Combining the bias and variance upper bounds directly gives (
21
).
∎

Proof of
Corollary

3.2
.

Hölder regularity gives
\omega_{y}(t)\leqslant L_{y}t^{\alpha}
where
L_{y}>0
is the Hölder constant of
y(\cdot)
on
I_{R}
. Hence (

) gives

\|y-y_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\leq\omega_{y}(\sqrt{d}\,h)^{2}=L_{y}^{2}(\sqrt{d}\,h)^{2\alpha}=L_{y}^{2}d^{\alpha}h^{2\alpha}\lesssim h^{2\alpha}.

Substituting this into (

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{N}}\|y-y_{N,h}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\lesssim h^{2\alpha}+(Nh^{d})^{-1}.

The choice

Proof of

.

For a single cell
Q_{k}

. Summing over the

To prove
Lemma

4.3
, we first isolate a preliminary estimate controlling the probability that the gap between the first two nearest-neighbor distances is small.

Lemma 7.8
.

Assume
N\geq 2
, fix
\mathbf{x}\in I_{R}
, and let
D_{1}(\mathbf{x})\leq D_{2}(\mathbf{x})
be the distances from
\mathbf{x}
to its nearest and second-nearest points within
\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{N}\}
. For
r>0
, denote
F_{\mathbf{x}}(r)\coloneqq\mu(B(\mathbf{x},r))
. Then, for every
\delta>0
,

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left(D_{2}(\mathbf{x})-D_{1}(\mathbf{x})\leq 2\delta\right)\leq N(N-1)\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[F_{\mathbf{x}}(r+2\delta)-F_{\mathbf{x}}(r)\right]\left(1-F_{\mathbf{x}}(r)\right)^{N-2}\,\mathrm{d}F_{\mathbf{x}}(r).

Proof.

For each
i\in[N]
, set
Z_{i}\coloneqq\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|
. Writing the order statistics as
Z_{[1]}\leq Z_{[2]}\leq\cdots\leq Z_{[N]},

we have
D_{1}(\mathbf{x})=Z_{[1]}
and
D_{2}(\mathbf{x})=Z_{[2]}
. By symmetry,

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{2}(\mathbf{x})-D_{1}(\mathbf{x})\leq 2\delta\right)\leq N\,\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{1}=Z_{[1]},\ Z_{[2]}\leq Z_{1}+2\delta\right).

Conditioning with respect to
Z_{1}
, we see that on the event
\{Z_{1}=r,\ Z_{1}=Z_{[1]},\ Z_{[2]}\leq r+2\delta\}
, at least one of the remaining
N-1
distances must belong to
[r,r+2\delta]
, while all the others must be at least
r
. For a fixed index
j\in\{2,\dots,N\}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(r\leq Z_{j}\leq r+2\delta\right)=F_{\mathbf{x}}(r+2\delta)-F_{\mathbf{x}}(r),

and for each
k\notin\{1,j\}
,
\mathbb{P}(Z_{k}\geq r)=1-F_{\mathbf{x}}(r).

By independence, these probabilities multiply, and a union bound over the
N-1
possible choices of
j
yields

\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{1}=Z_{[1]},\ Z_{[2]}\leq r+2\delta\mid Z_{1}=r\right)\leq(N-1)\left[F_{\mathbf{x}}(r+2\delta)-F_{\mathbf{x}}(r)\right]\left(1-F_{\mathbf{x}}(r)\right)^{N-2}.

Integrating with respect to the law of
Z_{1}
, whose distribution function is
F_{\mathbf{x}}
, proves the claim.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

4.3
.

We first treat the case
N=1
. Then
V_{1}=I_{R}
, so
\Omega_{\delta}=\{\mathbf{x}\in I_{R}:\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{x},\partial I_{R})<\delta\}
, and since
\mu
has density bounded above,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{N}}[\mu(\Omega_{\delta})]=\mu(\Omega_{\delta})\lesssim\delta\leq\delta N^{1/d}
.

We may therefore assume
N\geq 2
. Let
\mathbf{x}\sim\mu
be an independent test point; by Fubini’s theorem,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{N}}\left[\mu(\Omega_{\delta})\right]=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{\delta}(\mathcal{D}_{N})\right),

where the probability is taken jointly over
\mathcal{D}_{N}
and
\mathbf{x}
. The bound is trivial whenever
\delta N^{1/d}\geq 1
since probabilities are at most
1
, so we restrict to
\delta N^{1/d}\leq 1.

Let
D_{1}(\mathbf{x})
and
D_{2}(\mathbf{x})
denote the distances from
\mathbf{x}
to its nearest and second-nearest sample points among
\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{N}\}
, respectively. We claim that

\{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{\delta},\ \operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{x},\partial I_{R})>\delta\}\subset\{D_{2}(\mathbf{x})-D_{1}(\mathbf{x})\leq 2\delta\}.

(87)

Suppose that
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{\delta}
and
\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{x},\partial I_{R})>\delta
, and let
V_{i}
be the Voronoi cell containing
\mathbf{x}
. Since
\mathbf{x}\notin V_{i}^{\delta}
, there exists
\mathbf{z}\in\partial V_{i}
with
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\|\leq\delta
. The constraint
\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{x},\partial I_{R})>\delta
excludes
\mathbf{z}\in\partial I_{R}
, so
\mathbf{z}
lies on an internal Voronoi boundary and is equidistant from at least two sample points
\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}
. The triangle inequality gives

\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|\leq\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\|+\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|\leq\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|+2\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\|.

Taking
\mathbf{x}_{i}
as a nearest neighbor and
\mathbf{x}_{j}
as a second-nearest neighbor of
\mathbf{x}
, we obtain
D_{2}(\mathbf{x})-D_{1}(\mathbf{x})\leq 2\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\|\leq 2\delta
, which proves (
87
). Consequently,

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{\delta}(\mathcal{D}_{N})\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{x},\partial I_{R})\leq\delta\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(D_{2}(\mathbf{x})-D_{1}(\mathbf{x})\leq 2\delta\right).

(88)

Since
\mu
has density bounded above and the Lebesgue measure of the
\delta
-strip near
\partial I_{R}
is
O(\delta)
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{x},\partial I_{R})\leq\delta\right)\lesssim\delta.

(89)

For the second term, fix
\mathbf{x}\in I_{R}
and define
F_{\mathbf{x}}(r)\coloneqq\mu(B(\mathbf{x},r))
. The density bound on
\mu
controls the
\mu
-mass of a spherical annulus by its Euclidean volume:

F_{\mathbf{x}}(r+2\delta)-F_{\mathbf{x}}(r)\lesssim\delta\,(r+2\delta)^{d-1}\lesssim\delta\,r^{d-1}+\delta^{d}.

(90)

Substituting (
90
) into
Lemma

7.8
and using the identity
(N-1)\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-F_{\mathbf{x}})^{N-2}\,\mathrm{d}F_{\mathbf{x}}=1
(immediate from the change of variable
s=F_{\mathbf{x}}(r)
),

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{2}(\mathbf{x})-D_{1}(\mathbf{x})\leq 2\delta\mid\mathbf{x}\right)\lesssim N\delta\,I_{\mathbf{x}}+N\delta^{d},\qquad I_{\mathbf{x}}\coloneqq(N-1)\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d-1}\left(1-F_{\mathbf{x}}(r)\right)^{N-2}\,\mathrm{d}F_{\mathbf{x}}(r).

(91)

It remains to bound
I_{\mathbf{x}}
uniformly in
\mathbf{x}\in I_{R}
. Stieltjes integration by parts, whose boundary terms vanish (at
r=0
since
d\geq 2
, at
r=\infty
since
\operatorname{supp}\mu\subset I_{R}
), gives

I_{\mathbf{x}}=(d-1)\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d-2}(1-F_{\mathbf{x}}(r))^{N-1}\,\mathrm{d}r.

Let
\rho_{\min}>0
be a uniform lower bound for the density of
\mu
. Since
I_{R}
is a cube, there exist constants
c>0
and
r_{0}>0
such that
|B(\mathbf{x},r)\cap I_{R}|\geq c\,r^{d}
for all
\mathbf{x}\in I_{R}
and
0<r\leq r_{0}
, hence
F_{\mathbf{x}}(r)\geq c\,r^{d}
on the same range. Using
1-z\leq e^{-z}
, we get
(1-F_{\mathbf{x}}(r))^{N-1}\leq e^{-c(N-1)r^{d}}
for
r\leq r_{0}
, while for
r\geq r_{0}
,
1-F_{\mathbf{x}}(r)\leq 1-F_{\mathbf{x}}(r_{0})
is uniformly bounded away from
1
. Setting
D_{R}\coloneqq\operatorname{diam}(I_{R})
and splitting at
r_{0}
,

I_{\mathbf{x}}\lesssim\int_{0}^{r_{0}}r^{d-2}e^{-c(N-1)r^{d}}\,\mathrm{d}r+\int_{r_{0}}^{D_{R}}r^{d-2}(1-F_{\mathbf{x}}(r_{0}))^{N-1}\,\mathrm{d}r.

The change of variables
u=(N-1)r^{d}
in the first integral yields a bound

, and the second is exponentially small in
N
and absorbed into the same bound. Hence

I_{\mathbf{x}}\lesssim N^{-(d-1)/d}\qquad\text{uniformly in }\mathbf{x}\in I_{R}.

(92)

Substituting (
92
) into (
91

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{2}(\mathbf{x})-D_{1}(\mathbf{x})\leq 2\delta\mid\mathbf{x}\right)\lesssim\delta N^{1/d}+N\delta^{d}\lesssim\delta N^{1/d},

and

. Taking expectations and combining with (

.
