1 Introduction
When a shock wave reaches a material’s free surface, surface imperfections and machined pertur-
bations become unstable and grow via Richtmyer–Meshkov instability [10, 8]. This growth ejects a
cloud of solid or liquid particles from the surface [1]. This phase change comes hand-in-hand with
a scientiﬁc discipline change. Once the particles separate from the surface, the problem changes
from solid mechanics to rapidly expanding multiphase ﬂow. The lagging particles of a material
density much larger than the ﬂow, of varying sizes and diameters, increase in velocity until catch-
ing up to the ﬂow’s speed. Characterizing and quantifying the size, position, and velocity of these
ejecta particles is challenging.

 Figure 1: Illustration of micro-jet mechanism formation in shock ejecta problems [6].

To date, many ejecta measuring diagnostics produce indirect measurements of aggregate ejecta
cloud properties (Asay foils, Asay windows, piezoelectric pins, radiography). Photon Doppler Ve-
locimetry (PDV) is one diagnostic that can directly detect ejecta signals, but has largely remained
qualitative due to the complexity of the physics of the collected apparent signals. PDV measures
the Doppler shift of light scattered from moving particles, yielding to a time-resolved spectrogram
that contains information about velocity distributions, cloud evolution, attenuation effects, and
more. Interpreting the generated spectrograms quantitatively remains challenging. PDV diagnos-
tics have improved in sensitivity, the opportunities and feasibility has increased to transform this
previously qualitative ejecta sensing diagnostic into a quantitative measurement.

Current research direction is developing forward models that generate synthetic PDV spectrograms
directly from simulation data for the goals of (1) allowing simulated spectrograms to be directly
compared with experiments, and (2) supporting pre-shot prediction and enabling inference on the
data to be measured [5, 7, 6].

The objective of this internship work was to develop a numerical framework that includes two for-
ward scattering models: (I) a localized Beer-Lambert deterministic approach, and (II) a Monte-Carlo
photon transport stochastic approach. A synthetic PDV spectrogram is then generated from the
particle scattered ﬁeld. A comparison can then be drawn between the synthetic and experimental
spectrograms. This proposed framework is more clearly illustrated in ﬁg. 3.

The overarching goal of this framework is to make PDV spectrogram interpretation quantitative

The equation describes how the intensity I(x, ŝ) - power per unit area pe runit solid angle traveling
in direction ŝ) at point x - changes as you move along the beam.
The term on the left-hand side is the directional derivative of the intensity along the propagation
direction ŝ). It’s the rate at which I changes as a photon travels through space.
All the terms on the right-hand (RHS) side serve as a source or a sink that drives this change. If the
RHS were zero, ŝ · ∇I = 0 means intensity is constant along the ray (free propagation, nothing is
happening).
The ﬁrst term on the right-hand side describes the loss of intensity due to the beam interacting with
matter leading to extinction. The β coefﬁcient is a summation of how the photons get absorbed
and scattered out of direction into some other direction by the particles (β = κ + σs ).
The second term is emission gain from the medium’s own thermal (blackbody) emission. The
same κ that removes light in the extinction term also adds light through self-emission.
The last term is the effect of light traveling in other directions, multi-scattering, with I(ŝ′ ) being the
intensity coming from every other direction ŝ. The probability that light heading in ŝ′ gets redirected
into ŝ is denoted by P (ŝ′ , ŝ), integrated over all incoming directions. This is the term that makes
the equation challenging to solve since now I in direction ŝ depends on I in every other direction.
Three assumptions were adopted in order to simplify eq. (1)
 1. Ignore emission, second term on right-hand side. At a 1550 nm wavelength, particles do not
 have an emission that would affect the PDV light.
 2. Ignore multi-scattering, third term on right-hand side. This is a simpliﬁcation for now, but a
 future expansion target.
 3. Non-uniform particle cloud (particle density changes along laser beam).

3 Localized Beer–Lambert Algorithm
The localized Beer–Lambert model attenuates each illuminated particle by the upstream particles
lying within a narrow tube around its line of sight to the laser, as shown in ﬁg. 5. Unlike the photon
method (detailed in the next section), no rays are traced: the optical depth is accumulated directly
from a neighbor count.

j (closer to the laser along the beam axis, d∥,k < d∥,j ) and within the local tube of radius rtube = ζ dp

The transmission depends directly on the neighbor-search geometry. Two slicing strategies are

4 Photon Transport Algorithm
The photon transport algorithm launches photons uniformly across the beam cross section, marches
them along the beam axis, and extinguishes each photon at the ﬁrst particle it strikes. The surviving
fraction deﬁnes the per-particle transmission.

8 Limitations and Future Work
There were some assumptions taken for ease of ﬁrst steps of modeling that are important to high-
light as they are part of future work to improve accuracy of modeling prediction:
 1. the Beer-Lambert transmission algorithm cannot differentiate how far a particle’s center is
 with respect to the local tube ray being evaluated. As long as the particle’s center is within
 it, the same attenuation is applied. This can lead to always overestimating the attenuation
 for the case of a particle falling on the edge of the ray tube, compared to one located at the
 center of it.
 2. the photon transport extinction march assumes the photons to be always traveling in a straight
 line. This assumption fails when the spot size is small and the particle falls outside the
 Rayleigh range in which the spot size is now diverging. A mitigation to that is the adoption of
 a Wigner distribution.
 3. the algorithms are currently implemented for monodisperse particles. There is the need to
 expand this for polydisperse cases.
