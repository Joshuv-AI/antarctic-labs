Familial caudal regression syndrome with complex spinal dysraphism presenting as recurrent cutaneous infection
Authors: Umm E. Abiha, Mehar Masroor, Anam Ghauri, Altaf Ali Laghari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Caudal regression syndrome (CRS) is a rare congenital disorder characterized by varying degrees of sacral agenesis and spinal dysraphism. Although most cases are sporadic and associated with maternal diabetes, familial occurrence with variable phenotypic expressivity across generations has rarely been described.Case Description: We report a 12-year-old boy with CRS presenting with recurrent midline pustular lesions since birth, intermittent urinary incontinence, and lower-limb pain. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed sacral agenesis with preservation of S1, a low-lying conus medullaris consistent with tethered cord, and features of lipomyelocele. He underwent L4–S1 laminectomy and detethering under intraoperative neuromonitoring. Intraoperatively, a cystic lesion adherent to the cauda equina nerve roots was identified and excised. Histopathology confirmed an epidermoid cyst. Postoperatively, the patient remained neurologically stable. Evaluation of his mother revealed partial sacral agenesis on imaging despite minimal neurological symptoms, suggesting familial CRS with variable expressivity.Conclusion: This case highlights the importance of considering underlying spinal dysraphism in children with recurrent midline cutaneous infections and supports targeted imaging of first-degree relatives in suspected familial CRS. Careful surgical technique with intraoperative neuromonitoring is valuable in managing complex dysraphic anatomy.

INTRODUCTION

Caudal regression syndrome (CRS) is a rare poly-malformative congenital condition resulting from abnormal gastrulation during early embryonic development, with an estimated incidence of approximately 1 in 100,000 live births.[7,8] The phenotypic spectrum is broad, ranging from partial sacral agenesis to more extensive caudal dysgenesis, often involving neurological, urological, and orthopedic systems. Spinal dysraphism, including tethered cord and lipomatous malformations, is commonly associated with these anomalies.[12]

Although CRS is classically described as sporadic and strongly associated with maternal diabetes, familial cases have been infrequently reported.[16] The genetic basis of CRS remains incompletely understood, with only a limited number of genes implicated in early embryonic patterning, such as VANGL planar cell polarity protein 1 (VANGL 1) and, Homeobox HB9 (HLXB9).[9]

Consequently, familial CRS may be underrecognized, limiting opportunities for targeted surveillance, genetic counseling, and early intervention in at-risk relatives.

Complex dysraphic anomalies associated with CRS may also harbor inclusion cysts, such as epidermoid cysts, which arise from ectodermal entrapment during neurulation.[2] These lesions can present later in childhood and can manifest with subtle cutaneous findings or recurrent infection, potentially obscuring the underlying spinal pathology.[4]

We report a case of a 12-year-old boy with CRS, tethered cord, lipomyelocele, and an epidermoid cyst presenting as recurrent pustular lesions over the lower back, with radiological evidence of sacral agenesis in his mother, suggesting a possible familial predisposition.

CASE REPORT

A 12-year-old boy presented to our clinic with a history of recurrent pustular lesions over his lower back since birth, along with several months of intermittent urinary incontinence and bilateral lower-limb pain. On physical examination, muscle tone, strength, and range of motion were normal in all four limbs. No sensory deficits were observed, and bowel/bladder dysfunction was largely preserved, apart from intermittent urinary incontinence. Representative preoperative appearance of the lumbosacral cutaneous lesion is shown in Figure 1.

Preoperative gross image of the pustular lesion on the lower back of the patient. Clinical image of the lumbosacral region showing bilateral dermal sinus openings (yellow arrow) with an adjacent erythematous pustular swelling over the right lumbosacral area (blue arrow), suggestive of local infection.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the spine demonstrated sacral agenesis with preservation of only the S1 vertebra, consistent with CRS [Figure 2]. A thin fibrous tract was seen within the subcutaneous plane at the L5 level without any definite communication with the spinal canal. A small cystic focus along this tract was also noted, representing a localized collection secondary to recent cutaneous infection. The conus medullaris was elongated and low-lying, terminating at the level of the upper endplate of S1 vertebra, indicative of a tethered cord. The cauda equina nerve roots protruded posteriorly through the absent posterior elements at the S1 level and below, extending into the subcutaneous tissue and intermingled with some fatty tissue. These findings were consistent with a lipomyelocele.

Following discussion of the imaging findings, the patient’s family was counseled regarding surgical intervention. During this consultation, it was revealed that the patient’s mother also had a history of chronic low back pain without radicular symptoms, neurogenic claudication, or sphincter dysfunction. Given the presence of chronic low back pain in a first-degree relative and the possibility of an underlying congenital developmental anomaly, targeted evaluation for a potential familial etiology was undertaken. Maternal MRI was reviewed to assess for occult sacral or caudal segmentation abnormalities that might support a familial association. Review of her MRI lumbosacral spine demonstrated partial sacral agenesis with absent S3-5 vertebrae [Figure 3], and she remained neurologically intact. These findings suggested a possible familial occurrence of CRS. Additional family screening was not pursued beyond the mother, as there was no clinical suspicion of caudal anomalies in other family members, and broader screening was also limited by resource constraints.

Magnetic resonance imaging lumbosacral spine T2-weighted sagittal image of mother. (a and b) Partial sacral agenesis with truncation of the distal sacrum (yellow arrows), whereas conus medullaris appears to terminate at an appropriate level for an adult (blue arrow).

Preoperative nerve conduction studies and electromyography were unremarkable. The patient subsequently underwent L4-S1 laminectomy with detethering of the spinal cord under multimodality intraoperative neuro-monitoring (IONM), including somatosensory evoked potentials, motor evoked potentials, and free-running electromyography. Baseline somatosensory and motor evoked potentials were present in the monitored lower-limb and sphincter muscles. However, during detethering, a transient reduction of >50% in motor evoked potentials from bilateral lower-limb and sphincter muscles was observed, prompting modification of surgical manipulation. By wound closure, motor evoked potentials showed partial recovery, and no new postoperative neurological deficits were noted.

Intraoperatively, a thickened filum terminale, a lipoma adherent to the cauda equina, and a dysmorphic sacrum were identified [Figure 4]. A cystic lesion encountered during detethering was completely excised and sent for histopathological evaluation. The histopathology revealed a cyst lined by stratified squamous epithelium with an intact granular layer and a lumen filled with acellular, lamellated keratin, consistent with an epidermoid cyst [Figure 5].

Postoperatively, the patient experienced transient lower-limb pain and voiding difficulty, which were resolved by the 4th postoperative week. At the 6-month follow-up, he remained neurologically stable with no residual symptoms.

DISCUSSION

CRS represents a wide spectrum of congenital malformations involving abnormal development of the caudal portion of the embryo, with clinical manifestations ranging from isolated sacral agenesis to severe multisystem involvement.[13] While most cases are sporadic, rare familial occurrences have been described, suggesting potential genetic susceptibility that may be variably expressed across individuals and generations.[9,16]

In this case, radiological evidence of sacral agenesis was identified in both the affected child and his mother, despite markedly different clinical severity. This finding supports a possible familial predisposition and illustrates the concept of variable expressivity in caudal developmental anomalies. Similar intrafamilial variability has been described previously, including a recent report by Hameed and Qureshi, which documented increasing severity of CRS among three affected siblings.[8] Together, these observations suggest that mildly affected or clinically silent individuals may harbor developmental anomalies that manifest more severely in offspring, likely reflecting complex interactions between genetic susceptibility and modifying environmental or epigenetic factors.

Although additional family screening was not considered, no other family members demonstrated symptoms or clinical histories warranting targeted imaging. Resource limitations further restricted broader evaluation. Nevertheless, the identification of sacral agenesis in both mother and child suggests that mildly affected relatives may remain clinically unrecognized, supporting consideration of selective screening in suspected familial cases.

Several genes involved in early embryonic patterning and caudal morphogenesis, including HLXB9, VANGL1, and the brachyury (T) gene, have been implicated in familial or syndromic forms of CRS.[6,9,11] However, definitive inheritance patterns remain poorly defined, and genetic testing is not routinely available in many clinical settings. In the present case, formal genetic consultation and molecular evaluation were not feasible because of resource limitations. Consequently, a hereditary basis could not be genetically confirmed. Nevertheless, the radiological findings in both mother and child underscore the importance of considering genetic counseling, molecular testing when available, and targeted evaluation in suspected familial cases.

Maternal diabetes is a well-established risk factor for CRS, reported in approximately 20–25% of affected pregnancies.[17] Proposed mechanisms include altered expression of developmental genes involved in caudal morphogenesis, such as HLXB9.[3] Notably, the mother in our case was nondiabetic with no history of teratogenic exposure, consistent with several reported cases of CRS occurring in the absence of identifiable maternal risk factors, which supports intrinsic developmental susceptibility rather than an environmental insult alone.[3,5,8,11,15]

CRS is frequently associated with spinal dysraphism, including tethered cord, lipomyelocele, and abnormal positioning of the cauda equina. These anomalies may remain clinically silent in early childhood and present later with progressive neurological or urological symptoms, as observed in our patient. The distorted caudal anatomy encountered in CRS poses surgical challenges and increases the risk of neurological injury during detethering procedures. In this case, the use of multimodality IONM proved valuable, as transient signal changes prompted immediate modification of surgical manipulation and contributed to a favorable neurological outcome.

A particularly notable aspect of this case is the coexistence of an epidermoid cyst in association with CRS and lipomyelocele, presenting clinically as recurrent cutaneous infection. Spinal epidermoid cysts are rare inclusion lesions thought to arise from ectodermal entrapment during defective neurulation and are frequently associated with spinal dysraphism.[1,10] These lesions may remain occult for prolonged periods and can manifest through infection, retethering, or subtle cutaneous findings.[2,14] In our patient, recurrent pustular lesions over the lower back likely represented intermittent infection of the underlying inclusion cyst, serving as an important clinical clue to deeper spinal pathology. This emphasizes the need for heightened clinical suspicion and thorough neuroimaging in children presenting with recurrent midline cutaneous lesions, particularly in the lumbosacral region.

Experimental therapeutic approaches aimed at improving neurological outcomes in CRS have been reported. Devesa et al. described functional improvement in a child with CRS and sacral agenesis following growth hormone therapy combined with intensive rehabilitation, hypothesizing a neurotrophic effect mediated through activation of neural stem or ependymal cells.[7] However, these findings remain exploratory, and current management of CRS continues to rely on timely surgical intervention for associated spinal dysraphism and multidisciplinary rehabilitation.

The importance of the present case lies not in the surgical technique itself, which follows established principles of tethered cord release, but in the unusual constellation of clinical and radiological findings. To our knowledge, reports describing radiologically confirmed sacral agenesis in two generations, together with complex spinal dysraphism and an epidermoid cyst presenting as recurrent cutaneous infection, remain exceedingly rare. This case expands the phenotypic spectrum of familial CRS and highlights a diagnostic challenge in which superficial recurrent lumbosacral infection may represent an underlying dysraphic anomaly. Furthermore, it underscores the potential value of targeted evaluation of first-degree relatives when familial CRS is suspected, even in the absence of significant neurological symptoms, as mild or subclinical anomalies may otherwise remain undetected, particularly in resource-limited settings.

The limitations of this report include the absence of genetic testing and the inherent constraints of a single-case observation. Nevertheless, this case contributes to the limited literature on familial CRS and expands the spectrum of associated dysraphic and inclusion anomalies. Further studies incorporating larger cohorts and genetic analysis are needed to better elucidate hereditary patterns and optimize counseling and management strategies for affected families.

CONCLUSION

This case underscores several important clinical implications. First, familial occurrence of CRS, although rare, should prompt consideration of imaging for first-degree relatives when feasible, as this can guide targeted surveillance, counseling, and timely intervention for at-risk relatives. Second, recurrent midline cutaneous lesions should raise suspicion for underlying spinal dysraphism or an inclusion cyst. Finally, surgical management of CRS-associated tethered cord requires careful planning, meticulous technique, and IONM due to distorted anatomy and heightened neurological risk.
