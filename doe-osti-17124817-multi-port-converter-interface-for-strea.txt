Executive Summary

GE Research, in collaboration with Clarkson University and funded by the U.S. Department
of Energy (Award No. DE-EE0010279), was working to develop a Multi-Port Converter
(MPC) to streamline the integration of distributed energy resources within District Energy
systems, with a ﬁeld demonstration centered on a 375 kVA prototype at a school in Tok,
Alaska — integrating 51 kW solar PV, 375 kWh battery storage, and a 125 kVA biomass CHP
system. Economic analysis against a New York hospital benchmark conﬁrmed the MPC
delivers a 16.23% annualized ROI, approximately 33% higher than conventional baseline
systems, driven by a ~17% reduction in CAPEX and the elimination of interconnection
delays. The hardware leveraged a modular Silicon Carbide (SiC) MOSFET-based power
electronics architecture scalable from prototype to multi-MW deployments, while a Grid
Forming control scheme — validated across a comprehensive suite of grid stress events —
enabled black-start and islanded operation in compliance with IEEE 1547. Clarkson
University further contributed probabilistic load forecasting and robust optimization
algorithms to minimize operational costs under uncertainty. With the Tok solar array
commissioned and controls validated in software-in-the-loop testing, the project was on
track to offering a compelling, scalable pathway for clean energy integration in district
energy systems nationwide.

Statement of Objectives

The objective of this project is to design, build, and test in the ﬁeld a multi-port converter
(MPC) for distributed energy resource (DER) aggregation in District Energy (DE) systems.
The project incorporates several scopes including control and hardware design, prototype
manufacturing and testing, economic dispatch algorithms development, and ﬁeld
performance validation tests.

A 375kVA prototype will be designed and built for deployment in the Tok school, AK. A 51kW
solar photovoltaic (PV) system and a 375kWh battery energy storage (BESS) will be
installed along with the existing 125kVA biomass combined heat and power (CHP) system
to form a fully renewable powered district energy system with the school buildings as
electric and heat loads. In addition to the hardware, controls algorithms such as grid
forming control will be developed to allow the multi-port converter to comply to emerging
industry standards for inverter-based resources and be able to provide grid services and
perform reliably in islanded mode. Economic dispatch algorithms including resources
forecasting such as electric, heat loads, PV generation and energy prices will be also
developed and implemented in a microgrid controller that will be deployed along the multi-
port converter. At the end, the project will demonstrate the economic and technical
performance of a multi-port converter in optimizing district energy system operation while
reducing its energy costs.

Task 2—Develop Technical Speciﬁcations of a Multi-Port Interface Converter for DE

Task 2.1 and 2.3 Speciﬁcations for Full Scale and Prototype Converters

The MPC is made up of modular power electronic building blocks. These building blocks
are intended to be re-used in many applications, such as PV Solar, microgrids, and BESS
Therefore, a block is required with a speciﬁcation that can span this wide application
space. The core power board (shown left in Figure 2) is built around the 2kV, 400A SiC
MOSFET power module (FF3MR20W3M1H_H11) from Inﬁneon. Use of this module enables
a 1500Vdc bus, a requirement in utility-scale PV solar, as well as power ratings in the 100’s
of kW (dependent on selected switching frequency). This power rating is consistent with
the ratings of modern PV solar and battery strings.

 Figure 2. In-progress power and gate drive boards that serve multiple platforms

Each power converter module (containing four power boards) can be utilized to build
several different useful circuit topologies. These variants include: the two level three phase
DC/AC inverter (e.g., low voltage AC interfaces such as electric machines), the interleaved
DC/DC converter (e.g., battery interfaces as shown for example in Figure 3), the capacitor-
inductor-inductor-capacitor (CLLC) isolated DC/DC converter (e.g., creating galvanically
isolated, ﬂoating voltage sources), and ﬂoating H-Bridges for building the modular
multilevel converter (MMC) (e.g., medium voltage AC interface). This ﬂexibility further
expands the reach of the common power hardware into multiple applications.

Lastly, since the MPC is constructed of modular converters, the same modules may be
used for both the scaled prototype and the full multi-MW product vision MPC. The only
practical difference is the number of converter modules applied. Therefore, the
speciﬁcations are identical between the two.

Task 3—Design and Develop Controls of the Multi-Port Interface Converter

Task 3.1 Architecture Selection

An overall system diagram of the Tok, Alaska demonstration site is shown in Figure 5. The
Tok school’s electrical system consists of backup diesel generation, the biomass plant,
and the newly operational PV solar array. The diesel backup only comes online during APT
grid outages. The biomass plant operates in an “open loop” heat load-following fashion.
Note that the shredded wood steam boiler feeds both a steam engine generator and a heat
exchanger. That same heat exchanger is also fed from the biomass generator’s waste
exhaust (i.e., the CHP part of the system). The heat is exchanged to the school’s thermal
loads through the exchanger from steam to a water-glycol radiant heating system. A
human-operated valve to the CHP system is adjusted to dial in the desired thermal load,
with the electricity produced being a byproduct of the speciﬁc steam power setpoint. In
this way, the biomass system “follows the weather” in a manner conceptually like PV solar.
The PV Solar operates in a maximum power point tracking (MPPT) mode to extract as much
energy as is available. Even once the multiport converter is introduced, the original
biomass and PV solar feeds will be maintained as backup paths should the multiport
converter go offline.

The team has selected the architecture shown in Figure 5 for the Tok, Alaska demonstration
site version of the multiport converter (as opposed to the MW-scale vision of the multiport

converter). Each port of the converter is built from a common module design (shown as
white blocks), where the main difference between modules is how their inputs/outputs are
connected and their version of software. The DERs are each controlled from an individual
interfacing converter connected to a common low voltage DC bus (e.g., 1000V). A BESS is
added to add a dispatchable element to the microgrid; the optimization of its
charging/discharging is the goal of the dispatch algorithms under development in Task 5.
The grid interface shown in Figure 7 is designed to test the vision multiport converter’s
solid-state transformer (SST)-isolated interface. This interface enables the cascading of
converters for direct interfacing to a medium voltage (MV) (4160V to 13800V) grid with no
transformer in between. Even with this functionality, which also provides galvanic isolation
between the microgrid and the AC loads, a 400kVA step down transformer has been
included in the design since directly interfacing to 208VAC drives the current rating of the
converter ports too high.

Figure 5. Selected architecture for integration of DERs in the Tok, Alaska prototype

solar, CHP, and BESS and testing them in software-in-the-loop and hardware-in-the-loop
setups. The major components to model included: the PV solar, BESS, CHP generator,
208V grid, DC/AC inverter, DC/DC converter, and the CLLC converter.

Major effort was focused on developing an averaged model of the CLLC converter for the
MPC due to its important dynamics that are difficult to model. This sort of a model runs

averaged model uses a linear state-space model that captures the ﬁrst-harmonic
component of power transfer between the two bridges. PLECS can model state-space
networks as a block which can be integrated with an electrical model via controlled current
sources as shown in Figure 7.

Task 3.3 Grid Forming Algorithms

The detailed Grid Forming (GFM) control scheme of the MPC is shown in Figure 8. Overall,
multi-loop is employed in the control, which includes power, voltage and current loops. The
point-of-common-coupling (PCC) voltage and current are measured and used for all the
power and positive and negative sequence (PNS) component calculations. A virtual
synchronous machine (VSM)-based active power loop is used to obtain the phase angle θ
and implement the grid synchronization. Var-volt (Q-V) droop control is added before the
voltage loop to prevent large reactive power circulating between the grid and MPC. The
output of the Q-V droop provides the voltage magnitude reference v*qpos.

The PNS voltage control is included in the control scheme to enhance the MPC to adapt for
unbalanced conditions. The PNS voltage references v*dpos, v*qneg, and v*qneg could be
determined based on the speciﬁc control goals. The virtual impedance zv=rv+jxv is added
in the voltage control loop to emulate the MPC as a voltage source behind an impedance.
Proportional-integral (PI) regulators are used to control all the PNS voltages. The output

signals of the voltage control are PNS current references i* qpos, i*dpos, i*qneg, and
i*qneg, respectively.

Per the current control, the modiﬁed double synchronous reference frame (DSRF) current
controllers are used to achieve accurate current regulation as well as excellent transient
response. Rather than controlling the decoupled DSRF currents, the coupled DSRF
currents including the cross-coupling terms from the other synchronous reference frame
are regulated as shown in Figure 8. Likewise, PI regulators are used to control all the PNS
currents. The output signals of the current controllers are used to generate PWM gate
signals for the MPC.

 Figure 8. Grid forming controller block diagram

The GFM controller, along with simpliﬁed models of the MPC, was put through a rigorous
test regime as detailed in Table 3. The left-most column of the table lists grid events, such
as low voltage ride through (LVRT) and rate-of-change-of-frequency (ROCOF), that are
utilized to perturb the AC port of the MPC. The columns represent performing these events
at different “strengths” of grid, with the strongest on the left and the weakest on the right.
All tested scenarios resulted in asymptotically stable behavior, indicating the robustness of
the GFM controller to the stresses of operating on a real power grid.

Task 4—Develop Hardware Design of the Multi-Port Interface Converter

Task 4.1 – Preliminary Assembly

Figure 8 shows the initial schematic layout of a converter module. This module is meant to
be inserted into a standard 19” rack in the same manner as a computer server (as shown in
the Figure 9 preliminary module cabinet layout). It would plug into the low voltage DC bus
via a copper bus bar backplane via copper stabs on the backside of the unit. Because of
this modular design, the same conceptual components developed for the Tok, Alaska
demonstration would also be equally applicable to the MW-scale product vision design;
the MW-scale unit would be built by paralleling the needed number of modules to achieve
the power rating.

The layout is designed to facilitate easy manufacturing and high performance. From back
to front, ferrite cores are placed on the input/output of the module to block
electromagnetic interference (EMI) from exiting the box on the power leads. Cooling fans
provide air-cooling for both the controls compartment and the main power components.
Contactors are placed at the input and output sections to enable disconnection of the unit
during maintenance intervals or following faults. As discussed, four SiC modules are
arranged across a cold plate (to be determined if air or water-cooled), and the associated
gate driver boards are stacked on top and connected via board-to-board connectors. DC
link capacitors and a ﬁlter board remove switching harmonic content at the input and

output to ensure high quality DC and AC waveforms. Lastly, a control interface board plugs
into the input/output/gate driver sections for control and monitoring. The “sandwich
stack”, zero-wiring harness design allows for automated manufacturing of the unit in the
product vision.

Clarkson University made progress towards a robust dispatch of the microgrid with
heterogeneous resources comprised of the Combined Heat Power (CHP), Battery Energy
Storage Systems (BESS), and solar farms to serve both the electric and thermal load with
forecasting uncertainties. To this end, 1) Quantile CatBoost methodology has been
developed to improve the forecasting accuracy of the electric and thermal load demand in
rural areas; 2) a set of Gaussian Copula based technologies have been developed to
accurately model the spatial and temporal correlation of forecasting uncertainties from
both electric and thermal load; and 3) a robust optimization framework was established to
explicitly incorporate forecasting uncertainties into optimized asset dispatch for improved
energy efficiency and reduced operational cost for the microgrid.

The project planned to integrate, test and validate the proposed technology on the Alaska
microgrid with real measurement data and investigate the adaptive protection if the budget
allows.
