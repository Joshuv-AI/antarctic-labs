Minimax optimal submatrix detection: Sharp non-asymptotic rates
Parker Knight, Julien Chhor

Abstract
Given an observation 𝐘 ∈ ℝ d 1 × d 2 \mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}} from the model 𝐘 = 𝐗 + 𝐄 \mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{E} where 𝐗 \mathbf{X} is constant and 𝐄 \mathbf{E} has i.i.d. N ⁡ ( 0 , 1 ) N(0,1) entries, we consider the problem of detecting a planted submatrix in the mean matrix 𝐗 \mathbf{X} . Specifically, we aim to distinguish the null hypothesis 𝐗 = 0 \mathbf{X}=0 from the alternative hypothesis in which 𝐗 \mathbf{X} is non-zero only on a submatrix of size s 1 × s 2 s_{1}\times s_{2} with elevated entries bounded below by μ > 0 \mu>0 . We establish a minimax lower bound characterizing how large μ \mu must be to ensure that the two hypotheses are distinguishable with high probability. Furthermore, we derive novel minimax-optimal tests achieving the lower bound, and describe extensions of these tests that are adaptive to unknown sparsity levels s 1 s_{1} and s 2 s_{2} . In contrast with previous work, which required restrictive assumptions on s 1 , s 2 , d 1 s_{1},s_{2},d_{1} and d 2 d_{2} , our non-asymptotic upper and lower bounds match for any configuration of these parameters.

Introduction

Since the groundbreaking work of Ingster
(
35
;
31
;
33
;
34
;
36
)
, a vast literature has emerged aiming to establish fundamental statistical limits of testing hypotheses under structured alternatives
(
6
;
1
;
2
;
3
;
50
;
22
;
32
;
7
;
45
;
40
)
. A notable line of research within this literature focuses on the
submatrix detection problem
, in which in the statistician observes a noisy matrix and seeks to determine, by way of statistical hypothesis testing, whether the matrix admits a potentially sparse submatrix of entries with elevated mean. Submatrix detection arises naturally in practical high-dimensional data analysis, in particular as the biclustering problem in genomics
(
17
;
49
;
51
;
4
)
and as community detection in statistical network analysis
(
24
;
3
;
26
)
. The rigorous study of submatrix detection was famously initiated by Butucea and Ingster
(
11
)
, in which the authors derive the precise minimax detection boundary for detecting a sparse submatrix in homoscedastic Gaussian noise in a particular high-dimensional asymptotic regime. The precision and tightness of the results of
11
, as well as those of subsequent works
(
3
;
50
;
23
;
41
)
, explicitly rely on the form of the asymptotic regime considered by the authors. To our knowledge, there are no existing results establishing the non-asymptotic minimax rate of submatrix detection.
Moreover, prior work has relied on restrictive assumptions on the shape of the observed matrix and the planted submatrix, which severely hampers the statistical community’s understanding of this problem in regimes not captured by such assumptions.

We aim to close this gap by studying the submatrix detection problem in an entirely non-asymptotic setting. We observe a matrix
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
drawn from the model

\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{E}

(1)

where
\mathbf{X}
is a constant mean matrix and the entries of the noise matrix
\mathbf{E}
satisfy
\mathbf{E}_{ij}\overset{\textrm{iid}}{\sim}N(0,1)
for
(i,j)\in[d_{1}]\times[d_{2}]
. Our goal is to test for whether or not
\mathbf{X}
admits a submatrix of elevated entries. We formalize this problem in the language of minimax hypothesis testing.

Let
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
with
s_{1}\leq d_{1}
and
s_{2}\leq d_{2}
. For any
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{P}_{k}(n)
denote the set of all subsets of
[n]
with cardinality
k
.
For any
S_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{s_{1}}(d_{1})
,
S_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{s_{2}}(d_{2})
and any
\mu>0
, we define

\displaystyle\Theta(S_{1},S_{2},\mu)=\left\{\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}~\text{ s.t. }~\Bigg\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\!\forall(i,j)\in S_{1}\!\times\!S_{2}:\,\mathbf{M}_{ij}\geq\mu\\
\!\forall(i,j)\notin S_{1}\!\times\!S_{2}:\,\mathbf{M}_{ij}=0\end{array}\right\}.

For any
\mu>0
, let

\displaystyle\Theta(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu)=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{s_{1}}(d_{1})\\
S_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{s_{2}}(d_{2})\end{subarray}}\Theta(S_{1},S_{2},\mu).

For any matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\Theta(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu)
, the quantities
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
denote the row and column sparsity levels of
\mathbf{X}
respectively, and
\mu
represents the strength of the signal contained in the nonzero entries of
\mathbf{X}
. We consider the testing problem

\displaystyle H_{0}:\forall(i,j)\in[d_{1}]\times[d_{2}]:\mathbf{X}_{ij}=0\qquad\text{ against }\qquad H_{1}(\mu):\mathbf{X}\in\Theta(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu).

(2)

A
test
is a measurable function of the data
\mathbf{Y}
taking its values in
\{0,1\}
. We measure the quality of a test
\Delta
by its
risk
, defined as the sum of its type I and type II errors. Here and throughout the paper, we denote by
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}
the probability distribution of
\mathbf{Y}
under the mean parameter
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
and let
\mathbb{P}_{0}
denote the distribution of
\mathbf{Y}
under the null hypothesis.

Definition 1
.

The risk of a test
\Delta
is defined as

\mathcal{R}(\Delta,\mu)=\mathbb{P}_{0}(\Delta=1)+\sup_{\mathbf{X}\in\Theta(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu)}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}(\Delta=0).

(3)

Furthermore, we define the
minimax risk
as the infimal risk over all tests for the problem (
2
).

Definition 2
.

The minimax risk associated with (
2
) is defined as

\mathcal{R}^{*}(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu)=\inf_{\Delta}\mathcal{R}(\Delta,\mu),

(4)

where the infimum is taken over all tests
\Delta
.

We measure the difficulty of the testing problem (
2
) via the
minimax separation rate

\mu^{*}
defined as follows.

Definition 3
.

Let
\eta\in(0,1)
be given. The minimax separation rate of problem (
2
) at the desired level of risk
\eta
is defined as

\mu^{*}(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2})=\inf\left\{\mu>0:\mathcal{R}^{*}(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu)<\eta\right\}.

(5)

We will consider
\eta\in(0,1)
as a fixed constant throughout the paper. We will also write
\mu^{*}
instead of
\mu^{*}(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2})
whenever no ambiguity arises, but we emphasize that our goal is to obtain a sharp expression of this quantity of interest as a function of
s_{1},s_{2},d_{1}
and
d_{2}
. The minimax separation rate
\mu^{*}
is a non-asymptotic function of the tuple
(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu)
that provides us with necessary and sufficient conditions on the signal strength
\mu
such that consistent detection is possible with risk controlled to be at most
\eta
. Whenever
\mu
satisfies
\mu>\mu^{*}
, then a test
\Delta^{*}
exists such that
\mathcal{R}(\Delta^{*},\mu)\leq\eta
. Conversely, if
\mu<\mu^{*}
, no such test exists. In the present work, we aim to derive the value of
\mu^{*}
up to absolute multiplicative constants, which will grant us a complete characterization of the statistical hardness of the testing problem (
2
) across all values of
s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2}
.

1.1
Prior work

11
established the first formal decision-theoretic results pertaining to submatrix detection in Gaussian noise. They consider the asymptotic setting with
s_{1},s_{2},d_{1}
, and
d_{2}
all tending to infinity with
s_{1}=o(d_{1})
and
s_{2}=o(d_{2})
, and impose that

1.

s_{1}\log(d_{1}/s_{1})\asymp s_{2}\log(d_{2}/s_{2})

2.

\log\log(d_{1}/s_{1})=o(\log(d_{2}/s_{2}))
and
\log\log(d_{2}/s_{2})=o(\log(d_{1}/s_{1}))
.

In this regime,
11
show that the asymptotic minimax rate of separation in this regime satisfies

\big(\mu^{*}\big)^{2}=\left[\frac{d_{1}d_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}s_{2}^{2}}\land 2\Bigg(\frac{1}{s_{2}}\log\left(\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}}\right)+\frac{1}{s_{1}}\log\left(\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}}\right)\Bigg)\right](1+o(1)).

(6)

This expression establishes a clear phase transition in the form of
\mu^{*}
, which we elucidate by temporarily restricting our attention to the asymptotically square submatrix setting
s_{1}\asymp s_{2},d_{j}\asymp s_{j}^{A_{j}}
where
A_{j}>1
for
j\in\{1,2\}
. The minimax rate in this setting simplifies to:

\displaystyle\mu^{*}

\displaystyle\asymp s_{1}^{-2+(A_{1}+A_{2})/2}\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\;\;\;\text{if $A_{1}+A_{2}\leq 3$},

\displaystyle\mu^{*}

\displaystyle\asymp\sqrt{\frac{2(A_{1}+A_{2}-2)\log(s_{1})}{s_{1}}}\quad\text{otherwise.}

The former expression corresponds to the
dense regime
, in which
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
grow quickly relative to
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
respectively, and the latter to the
sparse regime
. This phase transition in
\mu^{*}
is analogous to classical results in the sparse vector testing literature
(
33
;
25
)
, but the result of Butucea and Ingster carries with it the additional complexity introduced by the product-structure of the alternative hypothesis in (
2
) (namely, the decoupling of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
). Furthermore, (
6
) is exact, and establishes the precise minimax constant in the asymptotic regime that they consider.

Following this seminal result of
11
, a wave of results were published establishing the statistical properties of submatrix detection beyond the homoscedastic Gaussian setting. Arias-Castro and Verzelen considered submatrix detection in Bernoulli noise
(
3
;
50
)
, motivated by the problem of community detection in Erdos-Renyi graphs, and establish the minimax rates of detecting a square submatrix in the adjacency matrix of a random graph with elevated connection probability in the dense
(
3
)
and sparse
(
50
)
graph settings respectively. The work of
41
extended the results of
11
to the multi-modal tensor setting, and prove the minimax rate of detecting a subtensor in a tensor of standard Gaussian noise. More recently,
23
provided upper and lower bounds on the minimax rate of detecting multiple submatrices in a square Gaussian matrix that are tight up to log factors.
44
extended these results to the inhomogeneous setting, in which the planted submatrices may have heterogeneous means or covariance matrices.

A parallel line of research investigates computational aspects of the submatrix detection problem; namely, whether or not the information-theoretic lower bounds establishes by
11
are attainable by (possibly randomized) polynomial time algorithms. Under the planted clique hypothesis,
42
established that no randomized polynomial time test can detect a square submatrix of size
s\times s
in a
d\times d
Gaussian matrix if
\mu=o(ds^{-2-\delta})
for
\delta>0
and
\sqrt{\log d}\lesssim\mu^{-1}
. These conditions on
\mu
are strictly stronger than the lower bound of
11
when
\sqrt{s}\gg\log d
, revealing a regime in which detection is statistically possible, but computationally intractable.
27
established an analogous statistical-computational gap in the community detection problem. These results were generalized by
9
, who derived computational lower bounds for detecting a submatrix of
\mathcal{Q}
-distributed entries in a matrix of
\mathcal{P}
-distributed data which hold for any
(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
pair with a finite KL divergence. Computational barriers to submatrix localization, defined as the problem of finding a planted submatrix rather than merely detecting its presence, has also received widespread attention in the statistics community over the past several years; we refer interested readers to
4
;
16
;
14
;
29
;
30
;
28
.

Our work also builds on and contributes to recent developments in the sparse Gaussian sequence model (GSM) testing literature, which dates back to
6
.
22
established non-asymptotic minimax rates for estimating linear and quadratic functionals on the sparse GSM, which they used to derive the corresponding minimax rates of signal detection under sparsity. The concurrent work
21
presented optimal rates for adaptive estimation of linear functions when the sparsity and noise variance are unknown.
20
presented minimax rates for estimating general
\ell_{p}
norms (i.e., nonlinear functionals) of the mean parameter under the same sparse GSM.
15
considered the estimation of the degree of sparsity
s
given draws from a
d
-dimensional GSM. Minimax rates for sparse changepoint detection in the GSM where established by
40
. Recently,
38
described minimax rates of signal detection in the sparse GSM with correlated observations, and
39
;
19
derived analogous results under heteroskedastic noise profiles.

Finally, we remark that the results in this paper complement those of our concurrent work
18
, which considers the problem of detecting a planted community in a bipartite graph. This corresponds to the submatrix detection problem under Bernoulli noise, which presents entirely different behavior from the Gaussian setting. Moreover, the results of
18
are limited by restrictive assumptions on the observed graph’s baseline connection probability, and thus fail to completely characterize the statistical hardness of community detection in bipartite graphs.
These restrictive assumptions also cause difficulty in obtaining adaptation to the size of the hidden communities. In the present paper, we completely resolve the minimax rate of submatrix detection in Gaussian noise and describe optimal adaptive tests, which constitutes a significant advance over our own companion work. Furthermore, we emphasize that since we place ourselves in the Gaussian setting, the optimal testing procedure described in Section
3
relies on fundamentally different test statistics from those used in the bipartite graph setting.

1.2
Our contributions

In this paper, we reopen the investigation into the statistical limits of submatrix detection in Gaussian noise, and make the following contributions.

1.

We fully resolve the non-asymptotic minimax rate
\mu^{*}
defined in (
5
), and show that it satisfies

\displaystyle\big(\mu^{*}\big)^{2}\asymp

\displaystyle\Bigg[\frac{1}{s_{1}}\log\!\Big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\log\!\Big(\!e\scalebox{.92}{$\displaystyle{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}$}\Big)\,\Big)+\frac{1}{s_{2}}\log\!\Big(1+\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\log\!\Big(\!e\scalebox{.92}{$\displaystyle{d_{2}\choose s_{2}}$}\Big)\,\Big)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\land\,\Bigg[\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\log\left(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\right)\Bigg]\,\land\,\Bigg[\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\log\left(1+\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\right)\Bigg].

Our results generalize the rates of sparse signal detection in the vector case
(
22
)
and also encompass the seminal results of Butucea and Ingster up to constants (Corollary
1
), which required the stringent balancedness assumption
s_{1}\log(d_{1}/s_{1})\asymp s_{2}\log(d_{2}/s_{2})
.
In contrast to the previous literature, our bounds always match up to multiplicative constants for any configuration of
d_{1},d_{2},s_{1}
and
s_{2}
.
This allows us to provide a comprehensive picture of all possible regimes and phase transitions, especially in highly imbalanced settings
s_{1}\ll s_{2}
or
d_{1}\ll d_{2}
, which, to the best of our knowledge, were previously absent from the literature.

2.

Our proof of the lower bound on
\mu^{*}
in Theorem
1
relies on a refined use of the second moment method. However, our argument departs significantly from the lower bound techniques developed in
11
and
3
. Rather than analyze a truncated likelihood ratio statistic, we directly control the variance of the likelihood ratio under all possible regimes of sparsity. This requires several auxiliary results for controlling the moment-generating function of the product of binomial random variables which may be of independent interest.

3.

Our upper bound on the minimax rate requires the development of new testing procedures. We use a combination of linear tests and truncated
\chi^{2}
-based tests, first studied by
22
for optimal testing in the sparse Gaussian sequence model, and carefully designed scanning procedures to achieve the optimal submatrix detection rates across all values of
s_{1},s_{2},d_{1}
, and
d_{2}
. To our knowledge, the tests described in this paper (as well as in
18
) constitute the first multi-dimensional extensions of the truncated
\chi^{2}
method now widely-used in the testing literature
(
22
;
38
;
40
;
19
)
, and we anticipate that the fundamental ideas behind the construction of our optimal tests will inspire the development of new methods for optimal sparse signal detection in matrix- or tensor-structured data. Furthermore, we describe an adaptive extension of our testing procedure that is minimax optimal without knowledge of the size of the planted submatrix
(s_{1},s_{2})
.

The manuscript is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we present our main theorem. Section
3
presents the construction of our optimal testing procedure and gives an expanded statement of the upper bound included in Theorem
1
(Proposition
1
). We then describe the adaptive extension of our optimal test and present a guarantee on its risk (Proposition
2
). In Section
4
, we provide a detailed comparison of our results to those in the existing literature. Finally, Section
5
summarizes our contributions and outlines the many promising avenues for future work implied by our results.

Main result

We begin this section by introducing notation that will serve us in the statements of the results that follow. For any
s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the following quantities:

\displaystyle\psi(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s_{1}}\log\left(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\log\left(e{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\right)\right),

(7)

\displaystyle\phi(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\log\left(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\right)

(8)

To make our notation concise, we write

\displaystyle\phi_{12}=\phi(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2}),

\displaystyle\phi_{21}=\phi(s_{2},s_{1},d_{2},d_{1}),\,

\displaystyle\psi_{12}=\psi(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2}),

\displaystyle\psi_{21}=\psi(s_{2},s_{1},d_{2},d_{1}).

The following theorem is our main result, which describes the precise form of the minimax rate of separation
\mu^{*}
as defined in (
5
) up to constants.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\eta\in(0,1)
be given. Then, the following claims hold.

1.

There exists a constant
c>0
that depends only on
\eta
such that

(\mu^{*})^{2}\geq c\cdot\big[\left(\psi_{12}+\psi_{21}\right)\land\phi_{12}\land\phi_{21}\big].

(9)

2.

There exists a constant
C^{\prime}>0
that depends only on
\eta
such that if
\mu^{2}\geq C^{\prime}\cdot[(\psi_{12}+\psi_{21})\land\phi_{12}\land\phi_{21}]
, then the test
\Delta^{*}
defined in (
25
) satisfies

\mathcal{R}(\Delta^{*},\mu)\leq\eta
.
This implies that

(\mu^{*})^{2}\leq C^{\prime}\cdot\big[\left(\psi_{12}+\psi_{21}\right)\land\phi_{12}\land\phi_{21}\big].

(10)

Theorem
1
establishes that the minimax rate satisfies

(\mu^{*})^{2}\asymp\left(\psi_{12}+\psi_{21}\right)\land\phi_{12}\land\phi_{21}.

(11)

The upper bound of Theorem
1
is achieved by an entirely novel testing procedure constructed from a combination of carefully aggregated truncated
\chi^{2}
and linear test statistics. We defer the details to Section

. The proof of the lower bound in Theorem

relies on a precise application of the second moment method
(
35
)
. We lower bound the minimax risk with the risk of a simple versus simple testing problem, in which the alternative hypothesis is defined by integrating over our parameter space with a uniform prior. We then invoke the Neyman-Pearson lemma and control the second moment of the resulting likelihood ratio statistic to show that the testing risk can be made arbitrarily large for
\mu\leq c\mu^{*}
, where
c>0
is a small constant that depends on
\eta
. Notably, our proof substantially departs from that of
11
. Rather than employ the well-established truncated second moment method
(
11
;
3
;
50
)
, we perform an elementary but extremely detailed analysis of the second moment of the likelihood ratio test across all possible regimes of
(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2})
. We provide the proof in detail in the supplement.

5
Discussion

5.1
Discussion on the proposed tests

Our test
\Delta^{*}
is a careful combination of several testing procedures, which substantially depart from test statistics proposed in the literature for submatrix detection problems.

11
(resp.
6
) use a
scan
test statistic, which which consists of scanning over all possible submatrices (resp. subvectors) of size
s_{1}\times s_{2}
(resp. of size
s
) and rejecting if one of them has entries summing to an unusually large value.
Analogous procedures have been used in related community detection settings
(
3
;
48
)
.
Notably, our results never make use of the scan test, and highlight that it can always be successfully replaced by a Bonferroni corrected linear or truncated
\chi^{2}
test, with lower time complexity
1
1

1

Computing
\Delta^{h_{3}}_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$},1}
requires
O(s_{1}d_{2}{d_{1}\choose s_{1}})
operations, rather than
O(s_{1}s_{2}{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}{d_{2}\choose s_{2}})
for the scan test.
.

Our two novel tests
\Delta_{\text{trunc-$\chi^{2}$},1}^{h}
and
\Delta^{h_{3}}_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$},1}
build on the truncated
\chi^{2}
test recently developed in the Gaussian sequence model.
Given a vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the truncated
\chi^{2}
test statistic is given by

\displaystyle T=\sum_{j=1}^{d}(X_{j}^{2}-\nu_{a})\mathds{1}{\big(|X_{j}|\geq a\big)}

for some
a>0
and where
\nu_{a}=\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\big|\,|Z|\geq a\right)
.
The corresponding test that rejects for large values of
T
is known to be optimal for detecting sparse alternatives in the Gaussian sequence model
(
22
)
.
To adapt this test to the matrix case where
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
the truncated
\chi^{2}
test, we use

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\Big(\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{d_{2}}\mathbf{Y}_{ij}\Big)^{2}-\nu_{a}\Big)\mathds{1}{\big(\sum_{j=1}^{d_{2}}\mathbf{Y}_{ij}\geq a\big)}

for some suitable truncation threshold
a>0
and re-centering parameter
\nu_{a}
.
This test statistic can be successfully applied to achieve optimality in regimes where
\tilde{R}\asymp\phi_{21}
and
s_{1}^{2}<d_{1}
(see (
25
) and (
21
)).
However, this test can also become suboptimal in other important sparse regimes.
Specifically, its main limitation is due to the terms
\sum_{j=1}^{d_{2}}\mathbf{Y}_{ij}
for
i\in[d_{1}]
, which completely eliminate the sparse structure of the corresponding vectors
(Y_{ij})_{j}
, and thereby prevent us from leveraging sparsity along the columns.
We overcome this difficulty by proposing a Bonferroni correction of tests statistics of the form

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\Big(\Big(\sum_{j\in S}\mathbf{Y}_{ij}\Big)^{2}-\nu_{a}\Big)\mathds{1}{\big(\sum_{j\in S}\mathbf{Y}_{ij}\geq a\big)},\qquad\forall S\in\mathcal{P}_{s_{2}}(d_{2}),

which preserve the sparse structure along the columns.
The rest of this section is devoted to discussing the differences between these two truncated
\chi^{2}
test statistics.

Heuristically, our truncated
\chi^{2}
test statistic
t_{\text{trunc-$\chi^{2}$},1}
defined in (
13
) is optimal when the submatrix has a large number of rows and a small number of columns.
In contrast, the Bonferroni-corrected truncated
\chi^{2}
test statistic
t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$},1}
defined in (
15
) is optimal when the number of rows and of columns are both small.
To see this, assume first that
s_{2}=d_{2}
and
s_{1}\ll d_{1}
for the sake of discussion.
We have justified in Section
4.4
that the column vector obtained as the row-wise sum of
\mathbf{Y}
is a sufficient statistic of the data for the mean matrix
\mathbf{X}
.
Therefore, when the number of rows or columns is large enough, the problem can be reduced to a vector-based problem, for which the linear test or truncated chi-square test are known to be optimal depending on whether
s_{1}\lessgtr\sqrt{d_{1}}

(
22
)
. This justifies the construction of our test statistic
t_{\text{trunc-$\chi^{2}$},1}
, which consists of summing row-wise or column-wise, and applying the linear test if
s_{1}\geq\sqrt{d_{1}}
or the vector-based truncated
\chi^{2}
test otherwise.

When the hidden submatrix has few rows and columns, collapsing the observation matrix into a single vector (by summing over rows or columns) destroys too much information from the data.
In this regime, a natural idea would be to apply the scan test proposed in
11
. Unfortunately, this procedure is not optimal in most regimes, generalizing the suboptimality reported in
6
(see Proposition
3
).
Instead, we propose a non-trivial refinement based on a Bonferroni correction of carefully selected truncated chi-square test statistics.
Our procedure, the Bonferroni corrected truncated
\chi^{2}
test as formally defined in equation (
15
), is obtained by scanning and summing over subsets along
one
dimension of the matrix and truncating along the other dimension.
In some regimes, this procedure fails and needs to be applied with the dimensions flipped, that is, by scanning over columns and truncating along rows.
This procedure captures subtle concentration effects of elevated submatrices with extremely small number of rows and columns, achieving minimax optimality in such regimes.

5.2
Conclusion and future work

In this paper, we have completely resolved the minimax rate of separation for detecting a planted submatrix in homoscedastic Gaussian noise. Our results reveal new phase transitions in the minimax rate that were previously unknown to the literature, and allow us to describe regimes in which the previous state-of-the-art results are in fact suboptimal. Our upper bound relies on the construction of new testing procedures, the ideas behind which we anticipate will lead to the development of new optimal procedures for similar detection and estimation problems in matrix- and tensor-structured data.

Our results present many opportunities for future research. We briefly discuss a few of these opportunities below.

•

Computational gaps
: The submatrix detection admits a well-known statistical-computation gap; that is, there exists a regime where detection is statistically possible but computationally infeasible
(
42
)
. However, the results of
42
and follow-up work
(
9
;
8
)
hold only in the square matrix and submatrix regime (with
d_{1}=d_{2},s_{1}=s_{2}
), and it is naturally of interest to investigate the existence of such gaps without this restriction. We employ the Bonferroni corrected linear and truncated
\chi^{2}
tests whenever
(\mu^{*})\asymp\psi_{12}+\psi_{21}
, which require maximizing over all subsets of
[d_{1}]
(resp.
[d_{2}]
) of size
s_{1}
(resp.
s_{2}
). These procedures can be intractable when
{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}
or
{d_{2}\choose s_{2}}
is exponential in one of the parameters, and we conjecture that constraining ourselves to the use of polynomial time testing procedures will lead to suboptimal statistical performance in this regime. Establishing this phenomenon formally is an important future research direction.

•

Extension to tensors
: The work of
41
in part extends the results of
11
to the tensor setting, in which the statistician observes a multi-modal tensor in Gaussian noise and attempts to discern whether there exists a planted sub-tensor of elevated mean. Their results similarly require balancedness conditions on the size of the planted sub-tensor, adapted from
11
to the multi-modal setting (see Theorem 9 in
41
and its assumptions). Extending our Theorem
1
to the tensor setting is of great interest, and we conjecture that such a result would reveal new phenomena in the sub-tensor detection problem, in particular new phase transitions according to the relative shapes of the observed tensor and planted sub-tensor.

•

Submatrix recovery
: In this paper, we have focused our attention on the submatrix detection problem, in which the statistician aims to detect the presence of a planted submatrix in noise. Submatrix recovery, where the goal is to estimate the support of the planted submatrix, is a statistically harder task that has also seen great research activity over the past decade
(
30
;
14
;
10
;
5
;
28
;
23
;
44
)
. Resolving the non-asymptotic minimax rate of submatrix support recovery for any configuration of
s_{1},s_{2},d_{1}
, and
d_{2}
constitutes a valuable research direction, and any such result will likely rely on recent developments in the variable selection literature for the Gaussian sequence model
(
13
;
43
;
12
)
. We leave these investigations to future work.

Appendix A
Proof of lower bound

In this section, we prove the lower bound on the minimax rate of separation
\mu^{*}
. Our general strategy is to lower bound the minimax risk of testing with the Bayes risk defined with respect to a well-chosen prior over the parameter space. We then invoke the Neyman-Pearson lemma and carefully control the second moment of the resulting likelihood ratio statistic. Throughout this section of the supplement, we will denote our desired minimax rate of separation as

R:=(\psi_{12}+\psi_{21})\land\phi_{12}\land\phi_{21}.

Let
\eta\in(0,1)
be given. Recall that the minimax risk is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{*}(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\Delta}\mathcal{R}(\Delta,\mu)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\Delta}\Big\{\mathbb{P}_{0}(\Delta=1)+\sup_{\mathbf{X}\in\Theta(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu)}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}(\Delta=0)\Big\}.

Define the reduced parameter space
\overline{\Theta}(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu)
as

\overline{\Theta}(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu)=\{\mathbf{X}\in\Theta(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu):X_{ij}\neq 0\implies X_{ij}=\mu\}.

We let
\pi
denote the uniform distribution over
\overline{\Theta}(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu)
, meaning that for any
\mathbf{M}\in\overline{\Theta}(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu)
it holds
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\pi}(\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{M})=\frac{1}{{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}{d_{2}\choose s_{2}}}
. Using
\pi
as our prior on
\overline{\Theta}(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu)
, we define the mixture distribution
\mathbf{P}_{\pi}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
as

\mathbf{P}_{\pi}(A)=\int_{\mathbf{X}\in\overline{\Theta}(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu)}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}(A)\pi(\textrm{d}\mathbf{X}),

where
A
is a measurable set. With this infrastructure in hand, we can lower bound the minimax risk as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{*}(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\Delta}\Big\{\mathbb{P}_{0}(\Delta=1)+\sup_{\mathbf{X}\in\Theta(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu)}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}(\Delta=0)\Big\}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\Delta}\Big\{\mathbb{P}_{0}(\Delta=1)+\sup_{\mathbf{X}\in\overline{\Theta}(s_{1},s_{2},d_{1},d_{2},\mu)}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}(\Delta=0)\Big\}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\Delta}\Big\{\mathbb{P}_{0}(\Delta=1)+\mathbf{P}_{\pi}(\Delta=0)\Big\}.

The final expression above is the minimax risk of a simple versus simple hypothesis testing problem, and we can characterize it precisely using the Neyman-Pearson lemma (
46

46

\displaystyle\inf_{\Delta}\Big\{\mathbb{P}_{0}(\Delta=1)+\mathbf{P}_{\pi}(\Delta=0)\Big\}

\displaystyle=1-\text{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{0},\mathbf{P}_{\pi})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}}|\textrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{0}(\mathbf{Y})-\textrm{d}\mathbf{P}_{\pi}(\mathbf{Y})|

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}}\Big|\frac{\textrm{d}\mathbf{P}_{\pi}(\mathbf{Y})}{\textrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{0}(\mathbf{Y})}-1\Big|\textrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{0}(\mathbf{Y}),

where
\textrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{0}
and
\textrm{d}\mathbf{P}_{\pi}
denote the Radon-Nikodym derivatives with respect to Lebesgue measure of
\mathbb{P}_{0}
and
\mathbf{P}_{\pi}
respectively. Letting

denote the likelihood ratio, we apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}}\Big|\frac{\textrm{d}\mathbf{P}_{\pi}(\mathbf{Y})}{\textrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{0}(\mathbf{Y})}-1\Big|\textrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{0}(\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}}\Big|L_{\pi}(\mathbf{Y})-1\Big|\textrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{0}(\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\int_{\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}}\Big(L_{\pi}(\mathbf{Y})-1\Big)^{2}\textrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{0}(\mathbf{Y})}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{0}\big[L_{\pi}^{2}(\mathbf{Y})\big]-1},

\mathbb{E}_{0}[L_{\pi}(\mathbf{Y})]=1

c_{1,\mu}>0
be the constants obtained from applying Lemma
8

\alpha=\varepsilon/2
. We can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)\mathds{1}(X\leq C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}})]+\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)\mathds{1}(X>C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}})]

By Lemma
8
, if
\mu^{2}\leq c_{1,\mu}R
, then
\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)\mathds{1}(X>C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}})]<\varepsilon/2
.
Now we turn our attention to the first term. Suppose that
C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}<1
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)\mathds{1}(X\leq C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X=0)

\displaystyle\leq 1,

and it holds that
\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)]\leq 1+\varepsilon/2
and the proof is complete. Otherwise, suppose that
C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}\geq 1
. Then by Lemma
1
, there exists a constant
c_{2,\mu}>0
such that if
\mu^{2}\leq c_{2,\mu}R
, it holds
\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)\mathds{1}(X\leq C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}})]<1+\varepsilon/2
. Letting

, it follows that if
\mu^{2}\leq c_{\mu}R
, then

\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)]\leq 1+\varepsilon.

Case 2:
Suppose that there exists a constant
\bar{c}\in(0,(2e)^{-4})
such that
s_{1}^{2}\leq\bar{c}d_{1}s_{2}
. We split our analysis into two sub-cases. Assume first that
s_{2}<s_{1}\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big)
. For a constant
C_{*}\geq 1
whose value will be determined later, we form the partition

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)\mathds{1}(X\leq C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}})]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)\mathds{1}(C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}<X<s_{2}^{-1}\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big))]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)\mathds{1}(X\geq C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}\lor s_{2}^{-1}\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big))]

\displaystyle=\text{I}(C_{*})+\text{II}(C_{*})+\text{III}(C_{*}).

By Lemma
12
, there exist constants
C_{1,*}\geq 1
and
c_{1,\mu}>0
such that if
\mu^{2}\leq c_{1,\mu}R
, then
\text{III}(C_{1,*})<\varepsilon/3
. Suppose that
\lceil C_{1,*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}\rceil\geq\lfloor s_{2}^{-1}\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big)\rfloor
. In this case, we take
C_{*}=C_{1,*}
, which gives us
\text{II}(C_{*})=0
. If
C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}<1
, it immediately follows that
\text{I}(C_{*})\leq 1
from an elementary calculation in the proof of Case 1, and thus
\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)]\leq 1+\varepsilon/3
which completes the proof. Otherwise, if
C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}\geq 1
, then by Lemma
1
there exists a constant
c_{2,\mu}>0
such that if
\mu^{2}\leq c_{2,\mu}R
, then
\text{I}(C_{*})\leq 1+\frac{2}{3}\varepsilon
. In this case, we take
c_{\mu}=\min(c_{1,\mu},c_{2,\mu})
and for
\mu^{2}\leq c_{\mu}R
it holds that
\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)]\leq 1+\varepsilon
. This completes the proof in the case
\lceil C_{1,*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}\rceil\geq\lfloor s_{2}^{-1}\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big)\rfloor
.

Now suppose that
\lceil C_{1,*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}\rceil\leq\lfloor s_{2}^{-1}\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big)\rfloor
. From here, we consider two further subcases. First, suppose that
2e\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}\geq\frac{s_{2}^{2}}{d_{2}}
. Then, by Lemma
9
, there exist constants
C_{2,*}\geq 1
and
c_{2,\mu}

\mu^{2}\leq c_{2,\mu}R

\text{II}(C_{2,*})<\varepsilon/3
. Now we take
C_{*}=\max(C_{1,*},C_{2,*})
. If
C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}<1
, it immediately follows that
\text{I}(C_{*})\leq 1
, and thus
\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)]\leq 1+\frac{2}{3}\varepsilon
which completes the proof. Otherwise, if
C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}\geq 1
, then by Lemma
1
there exists a constant
c_{3,\mu}>0
such that if
\mu^{2}\leq c_{3,\mu}R
, then
\text{I}(C_{*})\leq 1+\varepsilon/3
. We then take
c_{\mu}=\min(c_{1,\mu},c_{2,\mu},c_{3,\mu})
and for
\mu^{2}\leq c_{\mu}R
it holds that
\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)]\leq 1+\varepsilon
. Next, suppose that
2e\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}\leq\frac{s_{2}^{2}}{d_{2}}
. In this case, we partition
\text{II}(C_{*})
as follows:

\displaystyle\text{II}(C_{*})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)\mathds{1}(C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}<X<s_{2}^{-1}\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big))]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\big(\mu^{2}XY\big)\mathds{1}\bigg(1\vee C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}\leq X\leq\frac{C_{*}s_{2}^{2}/d_{2}}{\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}^{2}}{s_{1}^{2}d_{2}}\big)}\wedge s_{1}\bigg)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)\mathds{1}\bigg(1\vee C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}\vee\frac{C_{*}s_{2}^{2}/d_{2}}{\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}^{2}}{s_{1}^{2}d_{2}}\big)}\leq X\leq\frac{s_{2}}{\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big)}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\text{II}^{(a)}(C_{*})+\text{II}^{(b)}(C_{*}).

By Lemmas
10
and
11
, there exist constants
C_{2,*},C_{3,*}\geq 1
and
c_{2,\mu},c_{3,\mu}>0
such that if

. We take
C_{*}=\max(C_{1,*},C_{2,*},C_{3,*})
. If
C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}<1
, then
\text{I}(C_{*})\leq 1
and thus
\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)]\leq 1+\frac{2}{3}\varepsilon
which completes the proof. Otherwise, if
C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}\geq 1
, then by Lemma
1

c_{4,\mu}>0
such that if
\mu^{2}\leq c_{4,\mu}R
, then
\text{I}(C_{*})\leq 1+\varepsilon/3
. We then take

and for
\mu^{2}\leq c_{\mu}R

\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)]\leq 1+\varepsilon
. This completes the proof in the case
s_{2}<s_{1}\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big)
.

We now turn our attention to the case
s_{2}\geq s_{1}\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big)
. In this case, we perform the partition

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)\mathds{1}(X\leq C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}})]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)\mathds{1}(C_{*}\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}<X\leq s_{1}\big))]

\displaystyle=\text{I}(C_{*})+\text{II}(C_{*})

The remainder of the proof follows exactly as in the

10

1

A.2
Proof of lower bound in the very dense setting

Now suppose that there exists a constant
c>0
such that
s_{1}>cd_{1}
. Suppose that
\mu^{2}\leq c\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\log\big(1+c^{\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)
for
c^{\prime}=\log(1+\varepsilon)
. By the definition of
c
, it immediately holds that

\mu^{2}\leq c\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\log\big(1+c^{\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)\leq\frac{1}{s_{1}}\log\big(1+c^{\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)

We divide our analysis into two simple cases. First, suppose that there exists a constant
\bar{c}<1
such that
s_{2}<\bar{c}d_{2}
, which grants that
\frac{s_{2}}{d_{2}-s_{2}}\asymp\frac{s_{2}}{d_{2}}
, and we may consider
Y\sim\text{Bin}(s_{2},\frac{s_{2}}{d_{2}})
instead of
Y\sim\text{Bin}(s_{2},\frac{s_{2}}{d_{2}-s_{2}})
. By direct calculation, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}s_{1}Y)]\quad\text{(since $X\leq s_{1}$ almost surely)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big(\log\big(1+c^{\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)Y\Big)\Big]\quad\text{(since $\mu^{2}\leq\frac{1}{s_{1}}\log\big(1+c^{\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)$)}

\displaystyle=\Big(1+\frac{s_{2}}{d_{2}}\big(e^{\log\big(1+c^{\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)}-1\big)\Big)^{s_{2}}

\displaystyle=\big(1+\frac{c^{\prime}}{s_{2}}\big)^{s_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{c^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=1+\varepsilon.

If no such
\bar{c}
exists, then
s_{2}\geq cd_{2}
for a constant
1\geq c>0
, and we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)]

\displaystyle\leq\exp(\mu^{2}s_{1}s_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(\log\big(1+c^{\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)s_{2}\Big)\quad\text{(since $\mu^{2}\leq\frac{1}{s_{1}}\log\big(1+c^{\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)$)}

\displaystyle=\big(1+\frac{c^{\prime}d_{2}}{s^{2}_{2}}\big)^{s_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{c^{\prime}\cdot c}

\displaystyle=1+\varepsilon

where we adjust the constant
c^{\prime}
to satisfy
c^{\prime}=c^{-1}\log(1+\varepsilon)
. It remains to show that
\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\log\big(1+c^{\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)\asymp R
. Let
C^{\prime}=\frac{1}{c}
. First, note that
\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{s_{1}}\leq C^{\prime}
, so hence if
R
is defined with
C\geq C^{\prime}
, it holds
R\leq\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\log\big(1+c^{\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)
by definition. It remains to show that
\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\log\big(1+c^{\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)\lesssim R
. Since
\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}}\leq C^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\log\big(1+c^{\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{s_{1}}\log\big(1+c^{\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{s_{1}}\log\left(1+c^{\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\log\left(e{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\psi_{12}

\displaystyle\leq\psi_{12}+\psi_{21}.

Furthermore, using the inequality
\log(1+x)\leq x
for any
x>0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\log\big(1+c^{\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq c^{\prime}\frac{d_{1}d_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}s_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq c^{\prime}C^{\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{1}s_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\log\Big(1+\frac{1}{s_{1}}\Big)\quad\text{(since $x\lesssim\log(1+x)$ for $x<1$)}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\log\Big(1+\frac{d_{1}}{k^{2}_{1}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime\prime}\phi_{21}.

Clearly,
\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\log\big(1+c^{\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)\leq\phi_{12}
. Therefore we have
\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\log\big(1+c^{\prime}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)\lesssim R
, and the proof is complete.

A.3
Lemmas for proof of lower bound

Lemma 1
.

Let
C>0
be a constant such that that
s_{1}^{2}\geq d_{1}/C
. Then for any
\alpha>0
, there exists a constant
c_{\mu}>0
such that if
\mu^{2}\leq c_{\mu}\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\log\big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)
, then

\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)\mathds{1}\big(X\leq C\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}\big)\Big]<1+\alpha.

Proof.

By direct calculation, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)\mathds{1}\big(X\leq C\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp(\mu^{2}C\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}Y)\Big]

\displaystyle=\Big(1+\frac{s_{2}}{d_{2}}\big(e^{\mu^{2}C(s_{1}^{2}/d_{1})}-1\big)\Big)^{s_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(\frac{s_{2}^{2}}{d_{2}}\big(e^{\mu^{2}C(s_{1}^{2}/d_{1})}-1\big)\Big).

Now since
\mu^{2}\leq c_{\mu}\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\log\big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)
, we have

\displaystyle\exp\Big(\frac{s_{2}^{2}}{d_{2}}\big(e^{\mu^{2}C(s_{1}^{2}/d_{1})}-1\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(\frac{s_{2}^{2}}{d_{2}}\big(e^{c_{\mu}\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\log\big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)C(s_{1}^{2}/d_{1})}-1\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(\frac{s_{2}^{2}}{d_{2}}\big(\big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)^{c_{\mu}C}-1\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(\frac{s_{2}^{2}}{d_{2}}\big(c_{\mu}C\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\exp\big(c_{\mu}C\big)

where the second inequality holds for
c_{\mu}<C^{-1}
, by the inequality
(1+x)^{y}\leq 1+yx
which holds for any
x\geq 0
and
y\in[0,1]
. This final expression is at most
1+\alpha
for
c_{\mu}
taken sufficiently small.
∎

For any
C\geq 1
, we define the set of indices

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{C}=\left\{\Big\lceil C\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}\Big\rceil,\dots,s_{1}\right\}

(44)

The results in this section will depend largely on the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that
s_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}d_{1}
. Then for any
\alpha>0
, there exist constants
C_{*}\geq 1
and
c_{\mu}>0
such that for any
\mathcal{A}\subset\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{C_{*}}
, if

\mu^{2}\leq\min_{k\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{k}\log\Bigg(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}}\bigg(\exp\Big[\frac{c_{\mu}k}{s_{2}}\log\big(\frac{kd_{1}}{2es_{1}^{2}}\big)\Big]-1\bigg)\Bigg)

then

\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)\mathds{1}\Big(X\in\mathcal{A}\Big)\Big]<\alpha.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)\mathds{1}\Big(X\in\mathcal{A}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp(\mu^{2}kY)\Big]\Pr(X=k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}}\Big(1+\frac{s_{2}}{d_{2}}\big(e^{k\mu^{2}}-1\big)\Big)^{s_{2}}\Pr(X=k)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}}\Big(1+\frac{s_{2}}{d_{2}}\big(e^{k\mu^{2}}-1\big)\Big)^{s_{2}}\Big(\frac{s^{2}_{1}2e}{kd_{1}}\Big)^{k}e^{-s_{1}^{2}/d_{1}}

where the inequality follows from Lemma
22
. Since we have assumed that

\mu^{2}\leq\min_{k\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{k}\log\Bigg(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}}\bigg(\exp\Big[\frac{c_{\mu}k}{s_{2}}\log\big(\frac{kd_{1}}{2es_{1}^{2}}\big)\Big]-1\bigg)\Bigg),

we can substitute this into the above calculation to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}}\Big(1+\frac{s_{2}}{d_{2}}\big(e^{k\mu^{2}}-1\big)\Big)^{s_{2}}\Big(\frac{s^{2}_{1}2e}{kd_{1}}\Big)^{k}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}}\Bigg(\Big(\frac{kd_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}2e}\Big)^{c_{\mu}}\Bigg)^{k}\Bigg(\frac{s^{2}_{1}2e}{kd_{1}}\Bigg)^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}}\Bigg(\Big(\frac{s^{2}_{1}2e}{kd_{1}}\Big)^{1-c_{\mu}}\Bigg)^{k}.

Since
\mathcal{A}\subset\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{C_{*}}
, for every
k\in\mathcal{A}
we have

\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{kd_{1}}\leq\frac{1}{C_{*}}.

Therefore, by taking
C_{*}
sufficiently large, we can control the above sum with a geometric series

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}}\Bigg(\Big(\frac{s^{2}_{1}2e}{kd_{1}}\Big)^{1-c_{\mu}}\Bigg)^{k}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}}\Bigg(\Big(\frac{2e}{C_{*}}\Big)^{1-c_{\mu}}\Bigg)^{k}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tilde{c}}{1-\tilde{c}}

where
\tilde{c}=(2e/C_{*})^{1-c_{\mu}}
. This quantity can be made arbitrarily small by taking
c_{\mu}
sufficiently small and
C_{*}
sufficiently large. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3
.

Suppose that there exists a constant
\bar{c}\in(0,(2e)^{-4})
such that
s_{1}^{2}\leq\bar{c}d_{1}s_{2}
, and
s_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}d_{1}
.
Furthermore, suppose that
s_{2}<s_{1}\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big)
. Then for any
\alpha>0
, there exist constants
c_{\mu}>0
and
C_{*}\geq 1
such that if

\mu^{2}\leq c_{\mu}\frac{1}{s_{2}}\log\left(\frac{s_{2}d_{1}}{2es_{1}^{2}}\log\left(\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}}\right)\right),

then

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)\mathds{1}\Big(X\geq\frac{s_{2}}{\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big)}\vee\frac{C_{*}s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}\Big)\right]<\alpha.

If we further assume that
\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}}\geq 2(1-e^{-c_{\mu}})^{-1}
and that
s_{1}<s_{2}\log(d_{2}/s_{2})
, then if
\mu^{2}\leq\frac{c_{\mu}}{s_{2}}\log\big(\frac{d_{1}}{2es_{1}}\big)+\frac{1}{s_{1}}\log\big((1-e^{-c_{\mu}})\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}}\big)
it holds

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\mu^{2}XY)\mathds{1}\Big(X>\frac{s_{2}}{\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big)}\vee\frac{C_{*}s_{1}^{2}}{d_{1}}\Big)\right]<\alpha.

Proof of Lemma
3
.

Note that the set
\mathcal{A}=\{1\vee\big\lceil\frac{s_{2}}{\log\big((d_{1}s_{2})/s_{1}^{2}\big)}\big\rceil\vee\big\lceil C_{*}\frac{s^{2}_{1}}{d_{1}}\big\rceil,...,s_{1}\}
is a subset of
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{C_{*}}
, and to prove the claim it suffices to show

\mu^{2}\leq\min_{k\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{k}\log\Bigg(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}}\bigg(\exp\Big[\frac{c_{\mu}k}{s_{2}}\log\big(\frac{kd_{1}}{2es_{1}^{2}}\big)\Big]-1\bigg)\Bigg)

and invoke Lemma
2
. To this end, for
k\in\mathcal{A}
we define

g(k)=\frac{1}{k}\log\Bigg(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}}\bigg(\exp\Big[\frac{c_{\mu}k}{s_{2}}\log\big(\frac{kd_{1}}{2es_{1}^{2}}\big)\Big]-1\bigg)\Bigg).

(45)

Notice that for
k\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{c_{\mu}k}{s_{2}}\log\big(\frac{kd_{1}}{2es_{1}^{2}}\big)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c_{\mu}}{\log\big(\frac{s_{2}d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big)}\log\Big(\frac{s_{2}d_{1}}{2es_{1}^{2}\log\big(\frac{s_{2}d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big)}\Big)

\displaystyle=c_{\mu}\Big(1-\frac{\log\big(2e\log(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}})\big)}{\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big)}\Big).

Recall that there exists a constant
\bar{c}\in(0,(2e)^{-4})
such that
s_{1}^{2}\leq\bar{c}d_{1}s_{2}
. In particular, this implies that
d_{1}s_{2}/s_{1}^{2}\geq 16e^{4}
. Using this, as well as the bound
\log(x)/x\leq 1/2
for
x>1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\log\big(2e\log(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}})\big)}{\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log(2e)}{\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big)}+\frac{\log\big(\log(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}})\big)}{\log\big(\frac{d_{1}s_{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}\big)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log(2e)}{4\log(2e)}+\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{4}.

Combining this with our calculation above, we have that, for any
k\in\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\frac{c_{\mu}k}{s_{2}}\log\big(\frac{kd_{1}}{2es_{1}^{2}}\big)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c_{\mu}}{4}

\displaystyle>0.

Thus,
\exp\big(\frac{c_{\mu}k}{s_{2}}\log\big(\frac{kd_{1}}{2es_{1}^{2}}\big)\big)
is bounded away from 1, and hence for
c=1-e^{-c_{\mu}}
we have

f(k)=:\frac{1}{k}\log\Bigg(c\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}}\exp\Big[\frac{c_{\mu}k}{s_{2}}\log\big(\frac{kd_{1}}{2es_{1}^{2}}\big)\Big]\Bigg)\leq g(k)

for each
k\in\mathcal{A}
. So the prove the claim, it suffices to show that
\mu^{2}
is at most the minimum value of
f(k)
over
\mathcal{A}
. We can write
f(k)
as

B.1
Analysis of the linear test

Lemma 14
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
be given. There exist constants
C_{\mu},C_{h}>0
such that if

\mu^{2}\geq C_{\mu}\frac{d_{1}d_{2}}{s^{2}_{1}s_{2}^{2}},

then
\Delta^{h}_{\text{lin}}
with
h=C_{h}
satisfies

\mathcal{R}(\Delta^{h}_{\text{lin}},\mu)\leq\alpha.

Proof.

We begin by controlling the Type I error. Recall that
t_{\text{lin}}\sim N(0,1)
under the null hypothesis. Then by an elementary calculation, for
C_{h}\geq\sqrt{2\log(2/\alpha)}
it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}(\Delta^{h}_{\text{lin}}=1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{Z\sim N(0,1)}(Z>h)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-h^{2}/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}.

Now we consider the Type 2 error. Suppose that the mean matrix
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Y}]=\mathbf{X}
is an arbitrary element of
\Theta(s_{1},s_{2},\mu)
. In this case, we have
t_{\text{lin}}\sim N(\frac{s_{1}s_{2}}{\sqrt{d_{1}d_{2}}}\mu,1)
. Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{\text{lin}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{s_{1}s_{2}}{\sqrt{d_{1}d_{2}}}\mu

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{C_{\mu}}\quad\text{(by our assumption on $\mu$)}

\displaystyle\geq 2h,

where the final inequality holds for
C_{\mu}
taken sufficiently large. With this inequality in hand, we can control the Type II error as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}(\Delta^{h}_{\text{lin}}=0)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}(t_{\text{lin}}\leq h)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}(t_{\text{lin}}-\mathbb{E}_{X}[t_{\text{lin}}]\leq h-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{\text{lin}}])

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}((t_{\text{lin}}-\mathbb{E}_{X}[t_{\text{lin}}])^{2}\geq(h-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{\text{lin}}])^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}((t_{\text{lin}}-\mathbb{E}_{X}[t_{\text{lin}}])^{2}\geq(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{\text{lin}}]/2)^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{\text{lin}}])^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{C_{\mu}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{2},

where the final inequality holds for
C_{\mu}
taken sufficiently large. Combining our bounds on the Type I and Type II errors yields

\mathcal{R}(\Delta^{h}_{\text{lin}},\mu)\leq\alpha,

and the proof is complete.
∎

B.2
Analysis of the Bonferroni corrected linear test

Lemma 15
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
be given. There exists a constant
C_{\mu}>0
such that if

\mu^{2}\geq C_{\mu}\frac{d_{2}}{s_{1}s_{2}^{2}}\log\left(e{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\right),

then the max-linear test with
h=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha}}\vee\sqrt{2\log\Big(\frac{2e}{\alpha}{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\Big)}
satisfies

\mathcal{R}(\Delta_{\text{max-lin},1}^{h},\mu)\leq\alpha

Proof.

We begin with the Type I error.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}(\Delta_{\text{max-lin},1}^{h}=1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{0}(t_{\text{max-lin},1}>h)

\displaystyle\leq{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\mathbb{P}_{Z\sim N(0,1)}(Z>h)\quad\text{(union bound)}

\displaystyle\leq{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}e^{-h^{2}/2}\quad\text{(Chernoff bound)}

\displaystyle\leq{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\exp\left(-\log\left(\frac{2e}{\alpha}{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\right)\right)\quad\text{(by our choice of $h$)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}

Now suppose that
\mathbf{X}
is an arbitrary matrix in
\Theta(s_{1},s_{2},\mu)
with support
S_{1}\times S_{2}
. Define the statistic

t_{S_{1}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{2}}\bar{Y}_{S_{1},j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{1}d_{2}}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{2}}\sum_{i\in S_{1}}Y_{ij}.

Under the alternative hypothesis,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{S_{1}}]=\mu s_{2}\sqrt{s_{1}/d_{2}}
and
\text{var}(t_{S_{1}})=1
. By our assumption on
\mu
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{S_{1}}]

\displaystyle=\mu s_{2}\sqrt{\frac{s_{1}}{d_{2}}}

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{C_{\mu}\log\left(e{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\right)}

\displaystyle\geq 2h,

where the final inequality holds for
C_{\mu}
taken sufficiently large. We then have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}(\Delta_{\text{max-lin}}^{h}=0)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}(t_{\text{max-lin},1}\leq h)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}(t_{S_{1}}\leq h)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}(t_{S_{1}}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{S_{1}}]\leq h-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{S_{1}}])

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}((t_{S_{1}}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{S_{1}}])^{2}\geq(h-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{S_{1}}])^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}((t_{S_{1}}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{S_{1}}])^{2}\geq h^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{h^{2}}\quad\text{(Markov's inequality)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}\quad\text{(by our choice of $h$)}

Combining these two bounds gives us

\mathcal{R}(\Delta_{\text{max-lin},1}^{h},\mu)\leq\alpha

and the proof is complete.
∎

\chi^{2}

Lemma 16
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)

x

x\geq b

b>0

B>0

C_{h}

40

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Y}]=\mathbf{X}
is an arbitrary element of
\Theta(s_{1},s_{2},\mu)

\bar{Y}_{j}\sim N(\mu\frac{s_{1}}{\sqrt{d_{1}}},1)

\displaystyle\leq{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\mathbb{P}_{Z_{j}\overset{\textrm{iid}}{\sim}N(0,1)}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{d_{2}}(Z_{j}^{2}-\beta_{\tau^{*}})\mathds{1}(|Z_{j}|>\tau^{*})>h)\Big)\quad\text{(by union bound)}

\displaystyle\leq{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\mathbb{P}_{Z_{j}\overset{\textrm{iid}}{\sim}N(0,1)}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{d_{2}}(Z_{j}^{2}-\beta_{\tau^{*}})\mathds{1}(|Z_{j}|>\tau^{*})>\bar{h})\Big)

\displaystyle\leq{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}e^{-\log(\frac{2}{\alpha}{d_{1}\choose s_{1}})}\quad\text{(using Lemma 5 of \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{liu2021minimax}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}.

Now we consider the Type 2 error. Let the mean matrix
\mathbf{X}
be an arbitrary element of
\Theta(s_{1},s_{2},\mu)
with support
S_{1}\times S_{2}
. Note that
\tau^{*}\geq 1
by definition. Therefore, for
C_{\mu}\geq 8
, we apply Lemmas 6 and 7 of
40
to conclude

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}\big[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}\big]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{s_{1}s_{2}}{2}\mu^{2}

\displaystyle\textrm{var}_{\mathbf{X}}(t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}})

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}s_{1}s_{2}\mu^{2}+C_{1}(\tau^{*})^{3}e^{-(\tau^{*})^{2}/2}(d_{2}-s_{2}),

where
C_{1}
is the absolute constant from Lemma 7 of
40
. For
C_{\mu}\geq 4C_{h}
, it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}\big[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}\big]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{s_{1}s_{2}}{2}\mu^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C_{\mu}}{2}\Big(s_{2}\log\Big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\Big)+\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq 2h.

Thus, we can control the Type 2 error as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}\big(t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}\leq h\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}\big(t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}]\leq h-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}]\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}\big((t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}])^{2}\geq(h-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}])^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}\big((t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}])^{2}\geq(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}]/2)^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leq 4\frac{\textrm{var}_{\mathbf{X}}(t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}})}{(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}])^{2}}\quad\text{(Markov's inequality)}

\displaystyle\leq 4\frac{C_{1}s_{2}s_{1}\mu^{2}+C_{1}(\tau^{*})^{3}e^{-(\tau^{*})^{2}/2}(d_{2}-s_{2})}{\big(\frac{s_{2}s_{1}}{2}\mu^{2}\big)^{2}}

\displaystyle=4(\text{I}+\text{II}).

We now control these two terms separately.

I

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{1}s_{2}s_{1}\mu^{2}}{\big(\frac{s_{2}s_{1}}{2}\mu^{2}\big)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4C_{1}}{C_{\mu}^{2}s_{2}\log\big(1+c\big)+C_{\mu}^{2}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{16},

where the final inequality holds for
C_{\mu}
taken sufficiently large. Finally, we have

II

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{1}(\tau^{*})^{3}e^{-(\tau^{*})^{2}/2}(d_{2}-s_{2})}{\big(\frac{s_{2}s_{1}}{2}\mu^{2}\big)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{1}(\tau^{*})^{3}e^{-(\tau^{*})^{2}/2}d_{2}}{\big(\frac{s_{2}s_{1}}{2}\mu^{2}\big)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{1}(C\log(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}))^{3/2}d_{2}}{\big(\frac{s_{2}s_{1}}{2}\mu^{2}\big)^{2}(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\log{d_{1}\choose{}_{1}})}\quad\text{(by our choice of $\tau^{*}$)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{\prime}\big(\log(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}})\big)^{3/2}}{C_{\mu}^{2}\big(\log(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}})\big)^{2}}\frac{\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}}{1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}}\quad\text{(by our lower bound on $\mu^{2}$)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{C_{\mu}^{2}\sqrt{\log(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}})}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{C_{\mu}^{2}\sqrt{\log(1+c)}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{16},

where
C^{\prime}=8C_{1}C^{3/2}
and again the final inequality holds for
C_{\mu}
sufficiently large. Combining these bounds on the Type I and Type II errors gives us

\mathcal{R}(\Delta_{\text{trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}^{h},\mu)\leq\alpha

and the proof is complete.
∎

B.6
Analysis of adaptive tests

B.6.1
Adaptive Bonferroni corrected linear test

Lemma 18
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
be given. Define

\Omega_{1}=\left\{\frac{d_{1}}{2^{m}}:m\in\{0,\ldots,\lfloor\log_{2}(d_{1})\rfloor\}\right\}.

The adaptive Bonferroni corrected linear test is defined as

\Delta^{\text{ada}}_{\text{max-lin},1}=\max_{s_{1}\in\Omega_{1}}\mathds{1}\big(t_{\text{max-lin},1}(s_{1})>h(s_{1})\big),

for
h(s_{1})
to be specified later. Let
s_{1}^{*}
and
s_{2}^{*}
denote the true row and column sparsity under the alternative hypothesis. Suppose that
d_{1}\geq 3
and
s_{1}^{*}\leq d_{1}-1
. Then there exist constants
C_{h},C_{\mu}>0
such that if

\mu^{2}\geq C_{\mu}\frac{d_{2}}{s^{*}_{1}(s^{*}_{2})^{2}}\log\left(e{d_{1}\choose s^{*}_{1}}\right),

then the adaptive Bonferroni corrected linear test with cutoff
h(s_{1})=C_{h}\sqrt{2\log\left(e{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\log_{2}(d_{1})\right)}
satisfies

\mathcal{R}(\Delta^{\text{ada}}_{\text{max-lin},1},\mu)\leq\alpha.

Proof.

We consider the Type I error first. Under the null hypothesis, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(\Delta^{\text{ada}}_{\text{max-lin},1}=1\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(\max_{s_{1}\in\Omega_{1}}\max_{J_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{s_{1}}\!(d_{1})}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{2}}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{2}}\bar{Y}_{J_{1},j}>h(s_{1})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s_{1}\in\Omega_{1}}{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\mathbb{P}_{Z\sim N(0,1)}\big(Z>h(s_{1})\big)\quad\text{(union bound)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s_{1}\in\Omega_{1}}{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}e^{-h^{2}(s_{1})/2}\quad\text{(Chernoff bound)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{1}\in\Omega_{1}}{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\exp\left[-C_{h}^{2}\log\left(e{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\log_{2}(d_{1})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s_{1}\in\Omega_{1}}{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\log_{2}(d_{1})}\right)^{C_{h}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\log_{2}(d_{1})}\right)^{C_{h}^{2}-1}\quad\text{(for $C_{h}>1$)}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{\log_{2}(3)}\right)^{C_{h}^{2}-1}\quad\text{(for $d_{1}\geq 3,{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\geq 1$)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{2},

where the final inequality holds for
C_{h}
taken sufficiently large. Now we turn our attention to the Type II error. Let the mean matrix
\mathbf{X}
be an arbitrary element of
\Theta(s^{*}_{1},s^{*}_{2},\mu)
where
s^{*}_{1}
and
s^{*}_{2}
denote the true row and column sparsity levels respectively with support
S^{*}_{1}\times S^{*}_{2}
. We have not assumed that
s_{1}^{*}\in\Omega_{1}
. However, it is true that
s_{1}^{+}:=d_{1}2^{-\lceil\log_{2}(d_{1}/s_{1})\rceil}
belongs to
\Omega_{1}
. Since
s_{1}^{*}\leq s_{1}^{+}
, there exists a set
S_{1}^{+}\in\mathcal{P}_{s_{1}^{+}}(d_{1})
such that
S_{1}^{*}\subset S_{1}^{+}
. Define the statistic

t_{S^{+}_{1}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{2}}\bar{Y}_{S^{+}_{1},j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{s^{+}_{1}d_{2}}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{2}}\sum_{i\in S^{+}_{1}}Y_{ij}.

Under the alternative hypothesis,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{S^{+}_{1}}]=(\mu s_{2}s^{*}_{1})/\sqrt{s_{1}^{+}d_{2}}
and
\text{var}(t_{S_{1}})=1
. By our assumption on
\mu
and the construction of
s_{1}^{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t_{S_{1}}]

\displaystyle=\mu\frac{s_{2}s^{*}_{1}}{\sqrt{s_{1}^{+}d_{2}}}

\displaystyle\geq\mu s_{2}\sqrt{\frac{s^{*}_{1}}{2d_{2}}}

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mu}}{2}\log\left(e{d_{1}\choose s^{*}_{1}}\right)}

\displaystyle\geq 2h,

where the final inequality holds for
C_{\mu}
taken sufficiently large, and relies on
s_{1}^{*}\leq d_{1}-1
to obtain the inequality
\log\left(e{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}\right)\geq\log\log_{2}(d_{1})
. The remainder of the proof follows exactly as that of Lemma
15
; we omit the details. The proof is complete.
∎

B.6.2
Adaptive truncated
\chi^{2}
test

Lemma 19
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
be given. Suppose that there exists a constant
c>0
such that
\frac{d_{2}}{(s_{2}^{*})^{2}}\geq c
and that
s_{2}^{*}\geq 3
, where
s_{2}^{*}
denotes the true column sparsity under
H_{1}
. Let
S_{2}=\{s_{2}:cs_{2}^{2}\leq d_{2},s_{2}\geq 3\}
, and let

\Omega_{2}=\left\{\frac{d_{2}}{2^{m}}\in S_{2}:m\in\{0,\ldots,\lfloor\log_{2}(d_{2})\rfloor\}\right\}

Given
s_{2}\in\Omega_{2}
, we define

\tau(s_{2})=\sqrt{C\log\Big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\Big)},

where
C=\big(\log(1+c)\big)^{-1}
. Letting
h(s_{2})=C_{h}s_{2}\log\big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)
for
C_{h}
to be specified later, the adaptive truncated
\chi^{2}
test is defined as

\Delta^{\text{ada}}_{\text{trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}=\max_{s_{2}\in\Omega_{2}}\mathds{1}(t^{\tau(s_{2})}_{\text{trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}\geq h(s_{2})).

There exist constants
C_{h},C_{\mu}>0
such that if

\mu^{2}\geq C_{\mu}\frac{d_{1}}{(s^{*}_{1})^{2}}\log\big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{(s^{*}_{2})^{2}}\big),

then
\Delta^{\text{ada}}_{\text{trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}
with satisfies

\mathcal{R}(\Delta^{\text{ada}}_{\text{trunc-$\chi^{2}$}})\leq\alpha.

Proof.

We begin by controlling the Type I error. This proof follows the structure of the proof of Theorem 23 in
38
. For every
s_{2}\in S_{2}
, we define
u(s_{2})=\frac{C^{2}_{h}}{81}\log^{2}(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}})\land\frac{C_{h}}{9}s_{2}\log(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}})
. By direct calculation, we have

\displaystyle 9\Big(\sqrt{d_{2}e^{-\tau(s_{2})^{2}}u(s_{2})}+u(s_{2})\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 9\Big(\sqrt{d_{2}\frac{s_{2}^{2}}{s_{2}^{2}+d_{2}}e^{-C}u(s_{2})}+u(s_{2})\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 9\Big(s_{2}e^{-C}\frac{C_{h}}{9}\log\big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)+\frac{C_{h}}{9}\log\big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C_{h}s_{2}\log\big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)

\displaystyle=h(s_{2}).

Then it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}\Big(\Delta^{\text{ada}}_{\text{trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}=1\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s_{2}\in S_{2}}\mathbb{P}_{0}\Big(t^{\tau(s_{2})}_{\text{trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}\geq h(s_{2})\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s_{2}\in S_{2}}\mathbb{P}_{0}\Big(t^{\tau(s_{2})}_{\text{trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}\geq 9\Big(\sqrt{d_{2}e^{-\tau(s_{2})^{2}}u(s_{2})}+u(s_{2})\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s_{2}\in S_{2}}e^{-u(s_{2})}\quad\text{(by Lemma 5 of \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{liu2021minimax}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s_{2}\in S_{2}}e^{-\frac{C^{2}_{h}}{81}\log^{2}\big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)}+\sum_{s_{2}\in S_{2}}e^{-\frac{C_{h}}{9}s_{2}\log\big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)}.

We control the second sum first. Note that for
s_{2}\geq 3
and
C_{h}
taken sufficiently large, it holds

\frac{C_{h}}{9}\log\big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)\geq\frac{C_{h}}{9}\log\big(1+c\big)\geq\frac{\log(s_{2}^{2})}{s_{2}}+\log\big(\frac{8\pi^{2}}{6\alpha}\big).

Then by taking
C_{h}
sufficiently large, it holds

\displaystyle\sum_{s_{2}\in S_{2}}e^{-\frac{C_{h}}{9}s_{2}\log\big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{16}+\frac{\alpha}{16}+\sum_{s_{2}\in S_{2}/\{1,2\}}e^{-\frac{C_{h}}{9}s_{2}\log\big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{8}+\sum_{s_{2}\in S_{2}/\{1,2\}}e^{-\log(s_{2}^{2})-\log(8\pi^{2}/6\alpha)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{8}+\frac{6\alpha}{8\pi^{2}}\sum_{s_{2}=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{4}.

To control the first sum, we have

\sum_{s_{2}\in S_{2}}e^{-\frac{C^{2}_{h}}{81}\log^{2}\big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\big)}\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\Big(-\frac{C_{h}^{2}}{81}x^{2}\Big)\text{d}x\leq\sqrt{\frac{81\pi}{C^{2}_{h}}}\leq\frac{\alpha}{4},

where the final inequality holds for
C_{h}
taken sufficiently large. Therefore, we have shown

\mathbb{P}_{0}(\Delta^{\text{ada}}_{\text{trunc-$\chi^{2}$}})\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}.

We now turn our attention to the Type II error. Let the mean matrix
\mathbf{X}
be an arbitrary element of
\Theta(s^{*}_{1},s^{*}_{2},\mu)
where
s^{*}_{1}
and
s^{*}_{2}
denote the true row and column sparsity levels respectively. While we have assumed that
s_{2}^{*}\in S_{2}
, we have not assumed that
s_{2}^{*}\in\Omega_{2}
. However, since
s_{2}\mapsto d_{2}/s_{2}^{2}
is decreasing in
s_{2}
, it is true that
s_{2}^{-}:=d_{2}2^{-\lceil\log_{2}(d_{2}/s_{2})\rceil}
belongs to
\Omega_{2}
. Note that
\tau(s_{2}^{-})\geq 1
by construction. Therefore, to apply Lemmas 6 and 7 of
40
to the statistic
t^{\tau(s^{-}_{2})}_{\mathsf{trunc}-\chi^{2}}
, we first to verify that
\mathbb{E}[\bar{Y}_{j}]\geq 8\tau(s_{2}^{-})
. Observe

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bar{Y}_{j}]

\displaystyle=\frac{s_{1}^{*}}{\sqrt{d_{1}}}\mu

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{C_{\mu}\log\left(1+\frac{d_{2}}{(s_{2}^{*})^{2}}\right)}

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{C_{\mu}\log\left(1+\frac{d_{2}}{2(s_{2}^{-})^{2}}\right)}

\displaystyle\geq 8\tau(s_{2}^{-}),

where the final inequality holds for
C_{\mu}
taken sufficiently large. We may therefore invoke Lemmas 6 and 7 of
40
to lower and upper bound the mean and variance of
t^{\tau(s^{-}_{2})}_{\mathsf{trunc}-\chi^{2}}
respectively, and then proceed according to the proof of Lemma
16
. We omit the details for brevity. The proof is complete.
∎

s_{k}^{+}=d_{k}2^{-\lfloor\log_{2}(d_{k}/s_{k})\rfloor}
so that
s_{k}^{-}\leq s_{k}\leq s_{k}^{+}
. Since
(s_{1},s_{2})\mapsto d_{2}s_{2}^{-2}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}
is decreasing in
s_{2}
, increasing in
s_{1}
for
s_{1}\leq d_{1}/2
, and decreasing in
s_{1}
for
s_{1}\geq d_{1}/2
, it always holds that either
(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-})
or
(s_{1}^{-},s_{2}^{-})
belong to
\bar{\Omega}
depending on the value of
s_{1}
. Since we have assumed that
d_{1}/s_{1}^{*}\geq e
, we need only consider the case
s_{1}\leq d_{1}/2
. We first verify that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t^{(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-})}_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}]\geq 2u(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-})
. We aim to apply Lemma 6 of
40
. To do so, we first need to establish that
s_{1}^{*}s_{2}^{-}\mu^{2}\geq\tau^{2}(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-})
. By our assumption on the size of
\mu
, it is sufficient to show that
s_{1}^{*}s_{2}^{-}\mu^{2}\geq C(\log\log d_{1}+\log\log d_{2})
to establish this fact, where
C
is the constant specified in the definition of
\tau(s_{1},s_{2})
. Note that

\displaystyle\frac{(s_{1}^{*})\cdot(s_{2}^{-})}{2}\mu^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{s_{1}^{*}s_{2}^{-}}{2}C_{\mu}\Bigg(\frac{1}{s^{*}_{1}}\log\Big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{(s^{*}_{2})^{2}}\log{d_{1}\choose s^{*}_{1}}\Big)+\frac{1}{s^{*}_{2}s^{*}_{1}}\log{d_{1}\choose s^{*}_{1}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq C^{\prime}_{\mu}s_{2}^{-}+\frac{C_{\mu}}{2}\frac{s_{2}^{-}}{s_{2}^{*}}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}^{*}}

\displaystyle=C^{\prime}_{\mu}s_{2}^{*}\frac{s_{2}^{-}}{s_{2}^{*}}+\frac{C_{\mu}}{2}\frac{s_{2}^{-}}{s_{2}^{*}}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}^{*}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C^{\prime}_{\mu}}{4}s_{2}^{*}+\frac{C_{\mu}}{4}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}^{*}},

where
C^{\prime}_{\mu}=\frac{C_{\mu}}{8}\log(1+c)
and the final inequality uses
2s_{2}^{-}\geq s_{2}^{*}
which holds by the construction of
s_{2}^{-}
. Since
d_{1}/s_{1}^{*}\geq e
by assumption, it is clear that

\displaystyle\frac{C_{\mu}}{4}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}^{*}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C_{\mu}}{4}s_{1}^{*}\log\left(\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{*}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq C\log\log_{2}d_{1},

Furthermore, invoking this logic again and the fact that
\log_{2}(x)\geq\log(x)
for
x\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{C^{\prime}_{\mu}}{4}s_{2}^{*}+\frac{C_{\mu}}{4}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}^{*}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C^{\prime}_{\mu}}{4}s_{2}^{*}+\frac{C_{\mu}}{4}\log\log d_{1}

\displaystyle\geq C^{\prime\prime}_{\mu}\log\log d_{2}\quad\text{(by hypothesis, with $C^{\prime\prime}_{\mu}=4^{-1}(C_{\mu}\land C^{\prime}_{\mu})\cdot C^{\prime}$)}

\displaystyle\geq C\log\log d_{2},

where the final inequality holds for
C_{\mu}
taken sufficiently large. Thus, it holds that
s_{1}^{*}s_{2}^{-}\mu^{2}\geq\tau^{2}(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-})
and we may invoke Lemma 6 of
40
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t^{(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-})}_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{s_{1}^{*}s_{2}^{-}}{2}\mu^{2}.

Additionally, it holds

\displaystyle u(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})

\displaystyle=9\Big(\sqrt{d_{2}e^{-(\tau(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2}))^{2}/2}\log(\frac{2}{\alpha}{d_{1}\choose s^{+}_{1}}\log_{2}(d_{1})\log_{2}(d_{1}))}+\log\Big(\frac{2}{\alpha}{d_{1}\choose s^{+}_{1}}\log_{2}(d_{1})\log_{2}(d_{1})\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9}{2}Cs^{*}_{2}+9\log\Big(\frac{2}{\alpha}{d_{1}\choose s^{+}_{1}}\log_{2}(d_{1})\log_{2}(d_{2})\Big)\Big)\quad\text{(by the form of $\tau(s_{1},s_{2})$ and using $s_{2}^{-}\leq s_{2}^{*}$)}

\displaystyle=\frac{9}{2}Cs^{*}_{2}+9\log\Big(\frac{2}{\alpha}{d_{1}\choose s^{+}_{1}}\Big)+9\log\log_{2}d_{1}+9\log\log_{2}d_{2}.

By our construction of
s_{1}^{+}
, it holds

\displaystyle\frac{\log{d_{1}\choose s^{+}_{1}}}{\log{d_{1}\choose s^{*}_{1}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{s_{1}^{+}\log(ed_{1}/s_{1}^{+})}{s_{1}^{*}\log(d_{1}/s_{1}^{*})}

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{\log(ed_{1}/s_{1}^{*})}{\log(d_{1}/s_{1}^{*})}

\displaystyle=2\Big(1+\frac{e}{\log(d_{1}/s_{1}^{*})}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2(1+e)\leq 8,

where the second to final inequality uses
d_{1}/s_{1}^{*}\geq e
. Therefore,
u(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-})
satisfies

u(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-})\leq\frac{9}{2}Cs_{2}^{*}+9\log(2/\alpha)+72\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}^{*}}+9\log\log_{2}d_{1}+9\log\log_{2}d_{2}.

Using our previously established lower bound on
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t^{(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-})}_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}]
and the assumption
\mu
, it is clear that

\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t^{(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-})}_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}]\geq\frac{9}{2}Cs_{2}^{*}+9\log(2/\alpha)+72\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}^{*}}+9\log\log_{2}d_{1}+9\log\log_{2}d_{2},

for
C_{\mu}
taken sufficiently large. We have thus shown that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[t^{(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-})}_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}]\geq 2u(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-})
. With this result in hand, we control the Type 2 error using Chebyshev’s inequality.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}\left(\Delta^{\text{ada}}_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}=0\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{(s_{1},s_{2}\in\bar{\Omega})}\bigg\{t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}^{(s_{1},s_{2})}\leq u(s_{1},s_{2})\bigg\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}^{(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})}\leq u(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}^{(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})}-\mathbb{E}[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}^{(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})}]\leq u(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})-\mathbb{E}[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}^{(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})}]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigg(t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}^{(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})}-\mathbb{E}[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}^{(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})}]\bigg)^{2}\geq\bigg(u(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})-\mathbb{E}[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}^{(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})}]\bigg)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\bigg(t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}^{(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})}-\mathbb{E}[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}^{(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})}]\bigg)^{2}\geq\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}^{(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})}]\bigg)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\frac{\textrm{var}\Big(t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}^{(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})}\Big)}{\Big(\mathbb{E}[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}^{(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})}]\Big)^{2}}.

By Lemma 7 of
40
, it holds

\textrm{var}_{\mathbf{X}}(t^{(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-})}_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}})\leq\frac{C_{1}}{4}s^{*}_{1}s^{-}_{2}\mu^{2}+d_{2}C_{1}(\tau(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-}))^{3}e^{-(\tau(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-}))^{2}/2},

where
C_{1}\geq 1
is universal constant. With this inequality in hand, alongside the established fact that
\mathbb{E}[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}^{(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})}]\geq 2^{-1}s_{1}^{*}s_{2}^{-}\mu^{2}
, we can bound the Type 2 error as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}\left(\Delta^{\text{ada}}_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}=0\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\frac{\textrm{var}\Big(t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}^{(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})}\Big)}{\Big(\mathbb{E}[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}^{(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})}]\Big)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 4\frac{\frac{C_{1}}{4}s^{*}_{1}s^{-}_{2}\mu^{2}+d_{2}C_{1}(\tau(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-}))^{3}e^{-(\tau(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-}))^{2}/2}}{\Big(\mathbb{E}[t_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}^{(s^{+}_{1},s^{-}_{2})}]\Big)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4C_{1}}{s_{1}^{*}s_{2}^{-}\mu^{2}}+4\sqrt{2}C_{1}\frac{d_{2}(\tau(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-}))^{3}e^{-(\tau(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-}))^{2}/2}}{\mu^{4}(s_{1}^{*}s_{2}^{-})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\text{I}+\text{II}.

We control each of these terms separately.

I

\displaystyle=\frac{4C_{1}}{s_{1}^{*}s_{2}^{-}\mu^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4C_{1}}{C_{\mu}s_{2}^{-}\log\Big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{(s_{2}^{*})^{2}}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}^{*}}\Big)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4C_{1}}{C_{\mu}s_{2}^{-}\log(1+c)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{4},

with the final inequality holding for
C_{\mu}
taken sufficiently large. Turning our attention to II, it holds

II

\displaystyle=4\sqrt{2}C_{1}\frac{d_{2}(\tau(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-}))^{3}e^{-(\tau(s_{1}^{+},s_{2}^{-}))^{2}/2}}{\mu^{4}(s_{1}^{*}s_{2}^{-})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{2}C_{1}C^{3/2}\frac{d_{2}\big(\log(1+\frac{d_{2}}{(s_{2}^{-})^{2}}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}^{+}}\log_{2}(d_{1})\log_{2}(d_{2}))\big)^{3/2}}{\mu^{4}(s_{1}^{*}s_{2}^{-})^{2}\big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{(s_{2}^{-})^{2}}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}^{+}}\log_{2}(d_{1})\log_{2}(d_{2})\big)}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{2}C_{1}C^{3/2}\frac{\big(\log(1+\frac{d_{2}}{(s_{2}^{-})^{2}}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}^{+}}\log_{2}(d_{1})\log_{2}(d_{2}))\big)^{3/2}}{\mu^{4}(s_{1}^{*})^{2}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}^{+}}\log_{2}(d_{1})\log_{2}(d_{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\sqrt{2}C_{1}C^{3/2}}{C^{4}_{\mu}}\frac{\big(\log(1+\frac{d_{2}}{(s_{2}^{-})^{2}}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}^{+}}\log_{2}(d_{1})\log_{2}(d_{2}))\big)^{3/2}}{\big(\log(1+\frac{d_{2}}{(s_{2}^{*})^{2}}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}^{*}})\big)^{2}\log_{2}(d_{1})\log_{2}(d_{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\sqrt{2}C_{1}C^{3/2}}{C^{4}_{\mu}}\frac{\big(\log(1+\frac{d_{2}}{(s_{2}^{-})^{2}}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}^{+}}\log_{2}(d_{1})\log_{2}(d_{2}))\big)^{3/2}}{\Big(\log\big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{(s_{2}^{*})^{2}}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}^{*}}\log_{2}(d_{1})\log_{2}(d_{2})\big)\Big)^{2}}\quad\text{(since $d_{1}\land d_{2}\geq 8\geq 2^{e}$)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\sqrt{2}C_{1}C^{3/2}}{C^{4}_{\mu}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log\big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{(s_{2}^{*})^{2}}\log{d_{1}\choose s_{1}^{*}}\log_{2}(d_{1})\log_{2}(d_{2})\big)}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\sqrt{2}C_{1}C^{3/2}}{C^{4}_{\mu}\sqrt{\log(1+c)}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{4},

where the final inequality holds with
C_{\mu}
taken sufficiently large. Assembling these results, we conclude

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{X}}\left(\Delta^{\text{ada}}_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}=0\right)\leq\text{I}+\text{II}\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}.

Combining this result with our Type 1 error bound, we have shown that

\mathcal{R}\left(\Delta^{\text{ada}}_{\text{max-trunc-$\chi^{2}$}}\right)\leq\alpha,

and the proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 21
.

For any two real numbers
x,y>1
, at least one of the two inequalities
x\geq e\log(y)
or
y\geq e\log(x)
holds.

Proof of Lemma
21
.

We first prove a preliminary result: For any
x>1
, it holds that
x\geq e\log(e\log(x))
.
To see this, define the function
f:(1,\infty)\to\mathbb{R};~x\mapsto x-e\log(e\log(x))
. We have for any
x>1

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)=1-\frac{e}{x\log(x)}.

The only value
x^{*}
for which
f(x^{*})=0
is
x^{*}=e
, which implies that
f
is decreasing over
(1,e)
and increasing over
(e,\infty)
. Hence,
f
is minimized at
e
and its corresponding minimum value is

\displaystyle f(e)=e-e\log(e\log(e))=0.

This fact being established, assume now for the sake of contradiction that there exist two real numbers
x,y
such that
x<e\log(y)
and
y<e\log(x)
.
Then we obtain
x<e\log(e\log(x))
which is a contradiction. This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 22
.

Let
X\sim\text{Bin}(n,p)
with
p\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Then for any
k\in\{1,...,n\}
, we have

\Pr(X=k)\leq\Big(\frac{2enp}{k}\Big)^{k}\exp\big(-np\big)

Proof.

Using the bound
{n\choose k}\leq\big(\frac{ne}{k}\big)^{k}
(Appendix A in
47
), we have

\displaystyle\Pr(X=k)

\displaystyle={n\choose k}p^{k}\big(1-p\big)^{n-k}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{npe}{k}\Big)^{k}\big(1-p\big)^{n-k}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{npe}{k}\Big)^{k}\big(1-p\big)^{n}\big(1-p\big)^{-k}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{npe}{k}\Big)^{k}\big(1-p\big)^{n}\big(1/2\big)^{-k}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{2enp}{k}\Big)^{k}\Big(1-p\Big)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{2enp}{k}\Big)^{k}\exp\big(-np\big)

where the final inequality uses
(1-x)^{b}\leq\exp(-xb)
for any

.

Lemma 23
.

Let
f:S\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be differentiable, where
S\subseteq(0,\infty)
is an interval. For
x\in S
, define
g(x)=\frac{1}{x}\log\big(f(x)\big)
and
g^{(1)}(x)=\frac{1}{x}\log\big(1+f(x)\big)
. If
g
is decreasing on
S
, then
g^{(1)}
is decreasing on
S
as well.

Proof.

Let
f^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\textrm{d}f}{\textrm{d}x}(x)
for any
x\in S
. By direct calculation, we have

\frac{\textrm{d}g}{\textrm{d}x}(x)=\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{xf(x)}-\frac{\log\big(f(x)\big)}{x^{2}}.

If
g
is decreasing on
S
, then for each
x\in S
it holds

\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{xf(x)}<\frac{\log\big(f(x)\big)}{x^{2}}.

Now calculating the derivative of
g^{(1)}
at any
x\in S
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\textrm{d}g^{(1)}}{\textrm{d}x}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{x\big(1+f(x)\big)}-\frac{\log\big(1+f(x)\big)}{x^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{x\big(1+f(x)\big)}\frac{f(x)}{f(x)}-\frac{\log\big(1+f(x)\big)}{x^{2}}

\displaystyle<\frac{\log\big(f(x)\big)}{x^{2}}\frac{f(x)}{\big(1+f(x)\big)}-\frac{\log\big(1+f(x)\big)}{x^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 0

where the final inequality uses
f(x)\leq 1+f(x)
and
\log\big(f(x)\big)\leq\log\big(1+f(x)\big)
. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 24
.

Let
x,y>e
.

(i)

If
x/\log(x)\leq y/2
, then we have
x\leq y\log(y)
.

(ii)

If
x/\log(x)\geq y
, then we have
x\geq y\log(y)
.

Proof.

(i)

We prove the statement by contrapositive. Suppose that
x>y\log(y)
. Then since
t\mapsto\frac{t}{\log(t)}
is increasing over
(e,\infty)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{x}{\log(x)}\geq\frac{y\log(y)}{\log(y\log(y))}=\frac{y}{1+\frac{\log\log(y)}{\log(y)}}>\frac{y}{2}.

Again, we prove the statement by contrapositive.
Suppose that
x/\log(x)<y.
Then since
t\mapsto\frac{t}{\log(t)}
is increasing over
(e,\infty)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{x}{\log(x)}<\frac{y\log(y)}{\log(y\log(y))}=\frac{y}{1+\frac{\log\log(y)}{\log(y)}}<y.

This completes the proof.

Lemma 25
.

The following two properties hold.

(i)

For any
x\geq 0
and
c\in[0,1]
, we have
\log(1+cx)\geq c\log(1+x)
.

(ii)

For any
x\geq 0

25
.

Applying this with

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s_{1}}\log\!\Big(1+\frac{d_{2}}{s_{2}^{2}}\log\!\Big(\!e\scalebox{.92}{$\displaystyle{d_{1}\choose s_{1}}$}\Big)\,\Big)+\frac{1}{s_{2}}\log\!\Big(1+\frac{d_{1}}{s_{1}^{2}}\log\!\Big(\!e\scalebox{.92}{$\displaystyle{d_{2}\choose s_{2}}$}\Big)\,\Big)

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{1}{s_{1}}\log(d_{1})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{1}}}\log(d_{1})

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{1}}}\log(d_{1})

where the second-to-last expression uses

to simplify the second summand. It thus remains to show that

The proof that
