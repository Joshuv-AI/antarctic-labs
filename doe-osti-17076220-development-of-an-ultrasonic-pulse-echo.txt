 ABSTRACT

Molten salt reactors (MSRs) require reliable thermophysical and thermochemical property data for reactor
design, safety analysis, licensing, and operational assessment. In particular, the speed of sound is
important for describing molten salt compressibility and for completing equations of state for
multiphysics analyses. This work presents the design, validation, and initial application of a high-
temperature ultrasonic pulse-echo system for measuring the speed of sound in molten salts. The system
uses a quartz rod as a guided wave transmitter and receiver. An ultrasonic pulse propagates through the
rod, enters the molten salt, reflects off the bottom of the salt vessel, and returns to the transducer. The
sound speed is calculated from the measured round-trip travel time and the propagation distance between
the rod tip and container bottom. The method and system were first validated using room-temperature
water (22 °C), for which a measured velocity of 1519 ± 38 m/s was compared with the accepted value of
1488 m/s. The system was then used to measure a LiCl–KCl mixture (60/40 mol %) at temperatures of
435°C, 520°C, and 644°C, yielding velocities of 2039 m/s, 1937 m/s, and 1829 m/s, respectively. The
corresponding estimated standard uncertainties were 52 m/s, 49 m/s, and 47 m/s. The measured velocity
decreased with increasing temperature and showed good agreement with available literature data. These
results demonstrate the feasibility of the compact ultrasonic pulse-echo approach for high-temperature
molten salt measurements and establish an infrastructure for future measurements of fluoride salts as well
as actinide- and/or beryllium-containing salts.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Molten salt reactors (MSRs) require high-fidelity descriptions of the thermophysical and thermochemical
properties of their working fluids to support reactor design, safety analysis, licensing, and operational
assessment. These properties (e.g., density, viscosity, thermal conductivity, heat capacity, and
compressibility) directly influence the prediction of fluid flow, heat transfer, pressure response, and
reactor dynamics [1]. Although significant efforts are underway to characterize many molten salt
properties [2], measurements of the speed of sound and the associated compressibility remain limited for
salts of interest to the nuclear industry [3, 4].

The speed of sound is an important parameter for describing the compressibility and equation of state
(EOS) for liquids in general [5]. Accurate EOS information is necessary for modeling reactor conditions
that involve nonuniform pressure and temperature fields as well as transient events such as reactor startup,
shutdown, and accident progression [6]. In addition, the speed of sound can affect predictions of neutronic
and thermal-hydraulic behavior, including prompt-criticality response, fluid pressure dynamics, and
fluid–structure interactions. Experimental measurements of the speed of sound in high-temperature
molten salts are therefore needed to improve the fidelity and validation of multiphysics simulations used
in MSR analysis.

An example of an analysis tool is MELCOR, an engineering-level computer code developed for the US
Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC). MELCOR models the progression of severe accident scenarios
and calculates associated source terms. To adequately represent the behavior of molten salts during these
scenarios, the code requires appropriate thermophysical properties and an EOS [7, 8]. Researchers at Oak
Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) are working to establish an experimental capability for measuring the
speed of sound in high-temperature molten salts. The initial target salt was LiCl–KCl eutectic, and
measurements obtained were compared with available literature data.

In addition to generating data for LiCl–KCl, the goal was to establish a flexible speed of sound
measurement approach that can be applied to other molten salt systems of interest to researchers and the

nuclear industry. Future applications include fluoride-based eutectics, actinide halides, and beryllium-
containing salts. The resulting capability provides a foundation for expanding the available database of
molten salt acoustic and compressibility properties while improving the fidelity of reactor safety, thermal-
hydraulic, and neutronic analyses.

 2. METHOD FOR MEASURING SPEED OF SOUND

When measuring the speed of sound of any given molten salt, the following are common challenges that
need to be addressed:

 • Any material or component in the salt or in the furnace must be suitable for high temperatures
 (approximately 600 °C or greater).
 • Wetted materials must be corrosion resistant and compatible with the salts.
 • An inert environment must be maintained for feedthroughs such as thermocouples or probes.
 • Temperature limits of sensors and transducers need to be considered.
 • The apparatus should be small enough to fit inside a glovebox for testing of actinide- or
 beryllium-bearing salts.

Based on the previous considerations for measuring the speed of sound in a molten salt system, an
ultrasonic pulse-echo guided wave approach was selected as the most suitable method. The following
design requirements were added to this method to ensure successful wave transmission in the molten salt:

 • Thermal expansion should be minimized due to the transducer and probe terminal end being at
 two different temperatures (ambient temperature and salt temperature).
 • Length of the waveguide should be evaluated for large attenuation due to transmission efficiency
 and thermal gradients.
 • The waveguide should be able to vertically move to allow for the probe to be retracted in/out of
 the molten salt and adjust the wave propagation distance in molten salt.
 • The waveguide should have a flat bottom surface that is parallel to the salt container to maximize
 the reflection from the container’s bottom surface.

Initial tests were conducted in water to verify the methodology and conduct apparatus shakedowns before
performing tests with LiCl–KCl (melting point 353 °C).

2.1 MEASURING WAVE VELOCITY USING A WAVEGUIDE

A schematic of the guided wave method is provided in Figure 1. As shown in the figure, this method
measures the speed of sound in a fluid contained in a vessel. An ultrasonic transducer is mounted on the
top end of a rod. The ultrasonic pulser/receiver excites the transducer, thereby generating an ultrasonic
wave that propagates through the rod in the form of a guided wave. When the wave reaches the lower end
of the rod, it enters and passes through the fluid, reflects off the bottom of the container, and then travels
back into the rod. The returning wave is detected by the same single transducer. The received signal is
then filtered and amplified by the pulser/receiver before being digitized by an oscilloscope and computer.

Also illustrated in Figure 1 is an example of the received signal containing multiple reflection echoes. The
early echoes (shown in black) are reflections from the bottom of the quartz rod, while the later echoes
(shown in green) originate from reflections at the bottom of the container. The time difference, ∆t,
between the first rod bottom echo (black) and the first container bottom echo (green) corresponds to the
round-trip travel time of the wave through the fluid. The speed of sound in the fluid is calculated using
Eq. (1):

Because the initial target salt for demonstration was LiCl–KCl eutectic, quartz was selected as a suitable
and compatible waveguide and delay line. Dispersion curves for guided waves in the quartz rod were
analyzed using the open-source toolbox PCDISP [9]. Figure 2(a) and (b) show both the phase velocity
and group velocity dispersion curves. To obtain clear reflections within the quartz rod, a 2.25 MHz
broadband transducer (Evident V106-RM) was used. This transducer usually is used for room-
temperature testing and has a temperature limit of 50°C. Because of the long delay line, the transducer
temperature should be low even when the bottom of the delay line is inside the high-temperature salt.
Because the transducer operates over a broad frequency range, multiple peaks appear in the received
signal. These peaks arise from slight velocity differences among the various guided wave modes, as
indicated by the group velocity dispersion curve. Figure 2(c) presents the time-domain signal collected
from the 13 mm diameter quartz rod. The signal contains two main reflection packets, and each packet
includes multiple echoes corresponding to different longitudinal modes.

2.2 METHOD VALIDATION IN WATER

First, the method was validated using water. Figure 3 shows the benchtop setup for measuring the
acoustic wave velocity in water at room temperature. A Panametrics NDT-5800 pulser/receiver was used
to excite the 2.25 MHz longitudinal ultrasonic transducer mounted on top of the quartz rod, and the
signals were collected using an oscilloscope (PicoScope 5444D). The quartz rod was attached to a vertical
manual positioning slide above a stainless-steel container. Ultrasonic signals were acquired at seven
different rod height positions. The distance between the rod tip and the bottom of the container was
arbitrarily adjusted to a range of 0–76.2 mm in increments of 12.7 mm.

 4

 Figure 3. Benchtop configuration for method confirmation.

During testing, the rod was raised to different heights, creating varying propagation distances for waves
traveling through the water. At each height, a waveform was recorded, as shown in Figure 4a. Each signal
contains a large reflection packet with multiple echoes between approximately 110 and 160 μs, followed
by another packet extending from about 260 μs onward. These reflections originate from the rod bottom
and are not useful for determining the wave velocity in water. When these small echoes overlap with the
main reflection packets, they become difficult to identify. To isolate the water path reflections, the signal
collected at 0 mm was used as a reference. All other signals were subtracted from this reference within
the time window of 0–210 μs. The resulting signals, shown in Figure 4b, exhibit much clearer reflection
echoing from the container bottom. The reflections of interest (i.e., those from the container bottom
propagating through the water) appear as small echoes depicted inside the red circles.

 3. DESIGN OF PULSE-ECHO SYSTEM FOR MOLTEN SALT

Following the initial method validation in water and component configuration on a benchtop, an
apparatus was designed and constructed to be well suited for molten salt applications as shown in

 5

Figure 5. The apparatus consists of a flanged salt pot with a flat bottom that connects to a compact linear
shift mechanism (CLSM), which is a motorized ball screw and bellows mechanism that allows for motion
along a single axis (50 mm stroke). Lastly, a custom flange with appropriate feedthroughs for the
waveguide and K-type thermocouple creates a seal to maintain an inert environment. The system was
purged using ultra-high-purity argon controlled by a mass flow controller. Aside from the quartz being
compatible with LiCl–KCl, an additional feature is its small linear thermal expansion coefficient, α = 0.5
×10−6 / °C, which results in a negligible length change of 0.03% (i.e., 0.09 mm when the temperature is
increased to 600 °C from room temperature). Quartz also has low thermal conductivity compared to
SS304, which insulates the transducer from the heat. The initial benchtop testing supported that
encountering dissimilar materials will cause the pulse to be attenuated, thereby decreasing the signal-to-
noise ratio. Therefore, a liner or crucible might pose additional challenges. A decision was made to not
use a liner but rather load the salt directly into the vessel. After considering the available budget and time
constraints, a second decision was made to use SS304 as the material of construction for the pot rather
than a high nickel alloy. The salt constituents, acquired from MilliporeSigma, were prepared in the
glovebox before being loaded directly into the vessel. For this configuration, a different pulser/receiver
(the BYK DPR300) was paired with the PicoScope 5244D. A LabVIEW program was used to acquire
ultrasonic signal data at various locations along the quartz rod using a sampling rate of 31.25 MS/s. A
picture of the complete measurement system is shown in Figure 6.

 4. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Prior to the waveguide being fixed in place, the CLSM was lowered to its minimum vertical position, at
which point the quartz rod was manually lowered to be in contact with the bottom of the pot. This served
as the level reference. The vessel was removed and filled with 43 mL of water, which corresponded to a
liquid height of 38 mm. This allowed the waveguide and the thermocouple to start from the uppermost
vertical position of the CLSM without being in contact with the liquid. By starting above the liquid, the
waveguide does not have to experience melting/freezing when switched to salt. An initial measurement
was done with water (similar to section 2.2) to verify that the system (transducer, pulse generator,
oscilloscope, CLSM, and temperature log shown in Figure 6) functioned adequately.

Upon completion of the water test, the pot was removed, drained, cleaned, and dried at 120 °C for 5 h
before being allowed to cool to room temperature. The salt pot was then loaded with a total of 74 g of
LiCl–KCl mixture (60/40 mol %). Measurements of the wave propagation velocity (U) in molten LiCl–
KCl were evaluated at three distinct temperatures: 435 °C, 520 °C, and 644 °C. The waveguide was
lowered into the molten LiCl–KCl while recording the time-domain signal. For each measurement point,
the measured travel distance (L) and transit time (Δt) were related using the linear model, where the slope
is the wave velocity. This relationship can be expressed by Eq. (2):

 5. SUMMARY

A high-temperature ultrasonic pulse-echo system for measuring the speed of sound in molten salts was
successfully designed, tested, and validated. This initial iteration of the system uses a quartz rod as a
guided wave delay line and a conventional ultrasonic probe (50 °C maximum). An ultrasonic pulse travels
through the rod, enters the molten salt, reflects from the vessel bottom, and returns to the transducer. This
system was applied to a LiCl–KCl (60/40 mol %) eutectic at average temperatures of 435 °C, 520 °C, and
644 °C. The measured velocities were 2039 m/s, 1937 m/s, and 1829 m/s, respectively, demonstrating a
decrease in sound speed with increasing temperature and very good agreement with available literature
data. Measurements of the speed of sound allow for calculation of isobaric (Cp) and isochoric (Cv) heat
capacities because the two can be related. By extension, measurements of the speed of sound allow for
calculation of isothermal and adiabatic compressibility.

For application to fluoride salts, the system will be improved by ensuring the waveguide is chemically
compatible. Quartz may be suitable for short-duration testing in fluoride salts, but alternative waveguide
materials, such as nickel-based alloys or other compatible ceramics, will be evaluated. The use of a
different material for the waveguide will also increase thermal expansion and conduction. This will
require a more in-depth calibration of the rod probe over the relevant temperature range to correct for any
alignment and positioning errors. The current configuration requires the user to move the rod using the
CLSM controller. The data collection process will also need to be automated to conduct multiple sweeps
(downward travel, upward travel) while continuously recording transducer signals. Additional tests need
to be conducted using fluids with known speed of sound values at higher temperatures (> 300 °C). By
improving the system with the previously mentioned tasks, a more rigorous calibration and uncertainty
quantification can be performed.
