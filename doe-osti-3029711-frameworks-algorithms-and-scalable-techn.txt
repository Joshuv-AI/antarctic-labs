Abstract
SMU personnel formed a portion of the overall “Time Integration” team within the FASTMath
SciDAC-5 Institute, and we interacted very closely with team members from collaborating
institutions. The major goals of our team within the FASTMath institute may be categorized into
two groups: the development of advanced and application-aware time integration methods and
software, and close interactions with DOE application scientists to facilitate their use of these new
methods and software. The two goals are intimately linked, since our research and development
of novel tools is informed by the needs of our application partners, who in turn benefit from
subsequent mathematical and software advances.

While the SMU personnel collaborated with the rest of the Time Integration FASTMath team on
most of our shared deliverables within the larger FASTMath institute, SMU personnel primarily
contributed to the following subset of those goals:

 ●​ expanding capabilities for higher-order and solve-decoupled multirate methods,
 ●​ enhancing support for temporal adaptivity within multirate methods, and
 ●​ adding structure-aware time integration methods.

Each of these goals focused both on intellectual contributions through journal articles or research
presentations, and on enhancements to the open-source SUNDIALS library of time integrators
and nonlinear solvers, of which the SMU PI Reynolds is a core developer.

This report is structured as follows. The next section describes our accomplishments toward the
above objectives, as well as other related work that was funded by this project. We then describe
the training and professional development opportunities that were funded by this project,
followed by a section outlining how we disseminated our results to communities of interest.

Project Accomplishments
We group our accomplishments under this project topically within the following subsections. In
general, we organize these so that our intellectual contributions (via publications and research
presentations) are first, followed by software contributions to the SUNDIALS library, and ending
with contributions in collaboration with both applications scientists and with other sub-teams
within the FASTMath institute. We note that many of the accomplishments below do not fit
neatly within this organizational structure, in that some accomplishments combine both
publications and software contributions, while other publications were with outside collaborators,
and so we group these thematically in lieu of overly dissecting each effort into separate areas.

Higher-order and solve-decoupled implicit multirate methods
Under previous FASTMath funding, we completed an initial implementation of both third- and
fourth-order solve-decoupled implicit multirate infinitesimal (MRI) methods within the

SUNDIALS ARKODE library. These developments built off of two previously-existing
components in ARKODE: (1) the ARKStep module that supports implicit, explicit and mixed
implicit-explicit (IMEX) time integration using single-rate additive Runge--Kutta (ARK) methods
[Cooper and Sayfy, Math. Comp., 1983], and (2) the MRIStep module that supported up to
third-order explicit multirate infinitesimal step (MIS) methods [Wensch, Knoth & Galant, BIT
Numer. Math., 2009]. Under that previous FASTMath funding, we began extending the MRIStep
module to support a new class of multirate infinitesimal GARK (MRI-GARK) methods [Sandu,
SIAM J. Numer. Anal., 2019], that allow up to fourth-order temporal accuracy, as well as either
explicit or solve-decoupled implicit treatment of the slow time scale. Under the current project,
we completed implementation of the MRI-GARK, and have released them in SUNDIALS v5.7.0
(January 2021).

We then extended MRIStep to support a novel class of MRI-GARK methods that allow IMEX
treatment of the slow time scale, with so-called implicit-explicit MRI-GARK (IMEX-MRI-GARK)
methods. These methods combine the strengths of ARK IMEX methods and MRI-GARK
methods, allowing either third- or fourth-order multirate methods with extreme flexibility for
large-scale scientific applications. In addition to releasing these methods as part of SUNDIALS
v6.0.0 in December 2021, we published an article describing these methods [Chinomona and
Reynolds, SISC, 2021]

Lastly, under funding under both the previous FASTMath institute and this project, we have
created a new class of very high order multirate methods. These approaches are rooted in
exponential Rosenbrock method theory, and as such we name them Multirate Exponential
Rosenbrock (MERB) methods. Notably, these constitute the first-ever sixth order accurate
infinitesimal multirate methods. We published an article describing these methods and
demonstrating their performance on a suite of benchmark applications [Luan, Chinomona and
Reynolds, SISC, 2022].

Higher-order and time-step adaptive multirate methods
Our most significant contributions under the current project have focused on extensions to allow
temporal adaptivity within MRI methods. While temporal adaptivity for single-rate time
integration methods has been well studied, prior to this project there existed no published work on
related techniques to adapt the multiple nested step sizes that arise within MRI time integration
methods. However, historically all of the time integration methods released as part of
SUNDIALS provided this feature, ensuring that initial-value problems were solved to requisite
accuracy instead of merely with a given number of time steps using fixed step sizes.

Under this project, we first identified a range of candidate multirate methods that supply both a
time-evolved solution and an embedded estimate of the local temporal error, however the quality
of these embedded error estimates can vary widely between methods. At that time, we could only
identify two families: multirate GARK (MrGARK) methods [Sarshar, Roberts and Sandu, SISC,
2019], MRI-GARK methods [Sandu, SINUM, 2019], and multirate exponential Runge--Kutta
(MERK) methods [Luan, Chinomona & Reynolds, SISC, 2020], and of those, only MRI-GARK
methods supported an “infinitesmal” structure as expected within SUNDIALS. Notably, none of
the IMEX-MRI-GARK methods from our previous publication included embeddings to estimate
temporal error, and as such, there did not exist IMEX MRI methods that could be used for time
adaptivity. To fill this gap, we derived a new class of up to fourth order IMEX MRI methods that

include embeddings. The mathematical theory for this family, called IMEX-MRI-SR, also
provided much-needed understanding of the better-than-expected behavior of our previous
“MERK” multirate methods for nonlinear applications. This work was published in [Fish,
Reynolds, and Roberts, JCAM, 2024].

In addition to identifying candidate base methods, we derived a range of algorithms for adaptively
selecting the various time steps that comprise a multirate method. These methods seek to choose
both the “slow” step size H and the “fast” step size h << H such that both are as large as possible
(to shorten the total integration time) while simultaneously satisfying user-provided accuracy
requirements. During this reporting period, we completed derivations of novel “simultaneous”
controllers for both H and h that are rooted in optimal feedback control theory, and that rely on
piecewise polynomial (constant, linear and quadratic) approximations of the feedback control.
Our proposed methods show considerable promise in meeting the dual goals of maximal
efficiency and acceptable accuracy, and was published in [Fish and Reynolds, SISC, 2023].

Temporally adaptive MRI methods require propagation of information between the time
integrator used at the fast time scale and the enclosing MRI method; however, the initial
implementation of MRIStep did not provide sufficient flexibility to support this use case. We
thus thoroughly overhauled the entire time adaptivity infrastructure within ARKODE to utilize an
object-oriented approach. The new “SUNAdaptController” infrastructure is available to all
SUNDIALS integrators. It supports all ARKODE’s previous temporal controllers, facilitates the
development of new multirate controllers, and allows user-supplied adaptivity algorithms. This
was released to users in Fall 2023 in SUNDIALS v6.7.0. With this infrastructure in place, we
examined the performance of our existing suite of single-rate adaptivity controllers in
SUNDIALS, and found that although they worked well for challenging, very stiff, problems, they
under-performed when tasked with simpler, non-stiff applications. We thus developed a suite of
test problems with a wide range of difficulty, and ran exhaustive testing to compare ARKODE’s
existing single-rate controllers with others from the literature. Through this investigation, we
identified a number of published single-rate controllers that performed remarkably well. We thus
added new SUNAdaptController modules to implement these, and updated the default ARKODE
controller and its parameters to the combination that performed best across the testing suite.
These were released with SUNDIALS v7.3.0 in April 2025.

Also with this updated adaptivity infrastructure in place, we extended the MRIStep module to
support a wide range of embedded MRI methods. These include extensions in our support for
MRI-GARK methods to correctly handle their final “embedding” stage, as well as new support
for both IMEX-MRI-SR and MERK methods. Of these, we note that our previous work on
MERK methods did not include embeddings for temporal error estimation, so as part of this work
we derived novel embedding coefficients to enhance our previously-introduced MERK methods,
and implemented them within MRIStep.

Following our publication on “simultaneous” MRI adaptivity controllers above, and their
implementation in MRIStep, we tested them on a range of target applications. Those results
showed that although these simultaneous controllers could effectively control multirate temporal
error, they did not achieve as significant a time scale separation as expected for the underlying
applications (i.e., the ratio H/h that came from the controller was significantly smaller than the
applications warranted, thus resulting in less computational efficiency than expected. We thus
developed two new classes of MRI adaptivity controllers that should be usable within

“telescopic” multirate applications (i.e., applications with 3 or more time scales). We included
these controllers in the SUNDIALS v7.2.0 release (December 2024), and submitted a journal
article detailing their derivation and performance in October 2025 [Reynolds, Amihere, Mitchell,
and Luan, arXiv, 2025].

Solve-coupled multirate methods
Although solve-coupled infinitesimal multirate methods have been theoretically viable for some
time, and offer the hope of absolute linear stability for any combination of slow and fast time step
sizes (H and h) within a multirate method, none have been proposed in the literature due to a
perception of overly high computational cost. This cost arises due to the “solve-coupled” nature:
the slow stage solution must serve dual roles as a forcing term to, and the eventual solution of, a
modified differential equation at the fast time scale. In order to compute a slow stage solution
that satisfies both of these competing criteria, one must solve a highly challenging system of
nonlinear equations. Under this project, we benchmarked novel approaches that could be used to
solve this system of equations, and developed the mathematical theory for the achievable linear
stability that could be afforded by solve-coupled infinitesimal multirate methods. Unfortunately,
all approaches that we devised required very significant computational costs. After discussing
these options with our scientific collaborators, we have found that they have little/no “appetite”
for these costlier (but potentially more stable) multirate algorithms. A summary of their feedback
is that they only consider using multirate methods for problems where they can perform as much
of the application as possible using explicit methods, and that they would reserve any use of
fully-coupled implicit algorithms for a single time scale, choosing to “step over” faster dynamics
using an L-stable implicit integrator. We thus halted work on this topic.

ML tools for optimizing solver parameters
Adaptive time integration methods, particularly those that include implicit algebraic solvers,
include a multitude of parameters that may be tuned to optimize performance for a given
application. Traditionally, these options are modified manually by experts, who leverage
extensive experience in solving similar applications. In order to help remove this “expert”
bottleneck for new applications, we wanted to expose these SUNDIALS parameters so that they
could be modified at the command-line, enabling application-specific optimization through
automated parameter tuning software (e.g., GPTune). Thus under this project, we added utilities
to all SUNDIALS integrators (ARKODE, CVODE, CVODES, IDA, IDAS, KINSOL) and shared
modules (SUNLinearSolver, SUNNonlinearSolver, SUNAdaptController, etc.) to support
command-line options for all scalar-valued “set” routines. By adding a small number of function
calls to a user code to enable command-line control, this infrastructure allows users to adjust
solver parameters without modifying source code or recompiling their applications. This support
was released as part of SUNDIALS v7.5.0 in September 2025.

General SUNDIALS support
SUNDIALS has undergone numerous improvements since the first “method” paper was
published in 2005. In this project, we published a follow-on method paper that describes its new
flexibility for supporting application-specific data structures and algebraic solvers, as well as
numerous new options [Gardner, Reynolds, Woodward and Balos, ACM TOMS, 2022].
Additionally, we published a second method paper that focused exclusively on the new ARKODE

solver module in SUNDIALS, that has been primarily authored by the SMU PI Reynolds
[Reynolds, Gardner, Woodward, and Chinomona, ACM TOMS, 2023]. This publication
described ARKODE’s role as an infrastructure to support one-step methods for initial-value
problems, the current time-stepping algorithms that it includes, and examples highlighting both its
flexibility and parallel scalability. We contributed to a final method paper that describes more
recent updates to SUNDIALS from releases 6.2.0 through 7.3.0 (i.e., April 2022 through April
2025); which we submitted for publication in Spring 2025 [Roberts, Aggul, Reynolds, Balos,
Gardner, and Woodward, arXiv, 2025].

Also within this project, we worked with our LLNL collaborators to completely rewrite the
SUNDIALS documentation so that it could be hosted online
(https://sundials.readthedocs.io/en/latest/). This web documentation is at least as complete and
well-curated as our previous PDF-based documentation, and has proven to be an invaluable
resource for our research collaborators.

To improve support for applications that use complex arithmetic within time-adaptive simulations
or nonlinear root-finding problems, we have worked to upgrade the entire SUNDIALS library to
natively support complex solution data. While it has always been possible for users to solve
problems posed in complex arithmetic, users could only accomplish this by providing custom
vector interfaces and/or packing both real and imaginary coefficients into a double-length
real-valued array, and for implicit calculations they also had to provide custom algebraic solver
modules. Partially in support of a SciDAC-FES partnership project (Frontiers in leadership
gyrokinetic simulation), that funded extension of our Krylov linear solver modules (PCG,
GMRES, FGMRES, BiCGStab, and TFQMR) to complex arithmetic, under this project we
implemented additional infrastructure to support complex arithmetic throughout the rest of
SUNDIALS. While this implementation is now complete, and is accessible in the public GitHub
repository, it has not yet been released.

Collaborations with FES SciDAC projects
In anticipation of future SciDAC partnership proposals, we dedicated a portion of our effort on
this project to initiate collaborations with other SciDAC projects, particularly those funded under
FES. To that end, we collaborated closely with a team from the Partnership for Multiscale
Gyrokinetic Turbulence (MGK) SciDAC project, on the construction of a novel reduced-order
code for studies of coupled electron and ion scale turbulence in magnetic fusion devices. Our
MGK collaborators created a reduced model (named MuSHrooM) to simulate interacting
electron-scale and ion-scale turbulence, and we worked to explore optimal time integration
methods for such simulations. To this end, we have guided our collaborators through the
construction of a fully featured but lightweight interoperability layer between MuSHrooM and
ARKODE, and explored the use of single-rate, adaptive, IMEX time integration methods for the
problem. These results were highly promising, resulting in ion-scale simulations that reproduce
plasma instabilities at less than 1/18th the cost of full-order 5D simulations. We then constructed
a MuSHrooM interface to MRIStep, to enable multirate simulations that could subcycle the faster
electron scale components. The most challenging aspect of this, however, was determination of
an effective splitting of the application into “fast” and “slow” components. This task was not
resolved before the MGK project ended, at which time the project splintered and work on
MuSHrooM on their end was halted. However, the connections forged through this collaboration
resulted in our inclusion in multiple FES SciDAC partnership project proposals, and thus the

FASTMath-funded efforts planted strong seeds for subsequent collaborations.

Collaborations with AMReX team on reactive flow applications
In addition to inter-project collaboration, we collaborated extensively with the AMReX team in
FASTMath throughout the duration of the project, resulting in two publications and multiple
training opportunities.

We worked with members of the AMReX team to explore the benefits from using explicit and
mixed implicit-explicit multirate methods from MRIStep for structured grid simulations of
reactive flow. In previous work, the AMReX team achieved significant efficiency gains through
using multirate spectral deferred correction (MRSDC) methods on such applications [Emmett,
Zhang and Bell, Combustion Theory and Modeling, 2014]. Under previous FASTMath funding,
we converted their MRSDC codes for reactive flow to instead utilize the MIS, MRI-GARK and
IMEX-MRI-GARK infinitesimal multirate methods in ARKODE. Initial results showed that
these MRI methods resulted in dramatic performance gains over MRSDC for such applications.
Our article detailing this research was published in [Loffeld, Nonaka, Reynolds, Gardner, and
Woodward, Int. J. High Perf. Comput. Appl., 2024].

We additionally worked with members of the AMReX team who contribute to the Pele and Nyx
codes, that perform large-scale simulations of combustion and primordial cosmology,
respectively. Both codes use SUNDIALS similarly, to perform time integration of stiff reaction
networks within each finite-volume cell, but where these cells are decoupled from one another.
We published an article describing our collaborations with these teams on using SUNDIALS in
these contexts within exascale applications in [Balos, Day, Esclapez, Felden, Gardner, Hassanaly,
Reynolds, Rood, Sexton, Wimer, & Woodward, Int. J. High Perf. Comput. Appl., 2024].

Training and Development Opportunities
This project directly funded the efforts of one full time graduate student, and the partial efforts of
two postdoctoral researchers. Through this support, it provided professional development of the
skills necessary to pursue leading computational and applied mathematics research. In addition to
institutionally-provided training in these areas, the PI provides one-on-one mentorship in a variety
of skills related to high performance mathematical computing. Specific activities include
research-related “reading courses” focusing on the development of modern mathematical
software libraries, the use of such libraries within scientific research codes, techniques for
running these codes on large-scale supercomputing resources, grantsmanship (including reporting
and presentations), and vertical mentoring of junior researchers in the group.

In addition to directly training the SMU graduate student under this grant, the SMU PI regularly
represents the FASTMath SciDAC Institute as a lecturer in the Argonne Training Program for
Extreme Scale Computing (ATPESC). This is an intensive, two-week program where students
(primarily graduate students and postdoctoral researchers throughout STEM fields) learn “skills,
approaches, and tools to design, implement, and execute computational science and engineering
applications on current high-end computing systems and the leadership-class computing systems
of the future.” (from https://extremecomputingtraining.anl.gov).
