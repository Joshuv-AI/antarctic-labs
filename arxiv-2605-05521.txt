Abstract
Counterfactual utilities evaluate decisions not only by the realized outcome under a given decision, but also by the counterfactual outcomes that would arise under alternative decisions. By generalizing standard utility frameworks, they allow decision-makers to encode asymmetric criteria, such as avoiding harm and anticipating regret. Recent work, however, has raised fundamental concerns about the coherence and transitivity of counterfactual utilities. We address these concerns by extending the von Neumann–Morgenstern (vNM) framework to preferences defined on the extended space of all potential outcomes rather than realized outcomes alone. We show that expected counterfactual utility satisfies the vNM axioms on this extended domain, thereby admitting a coherent preference representation. We further examine how counterfactual preferences map onto the realized outcome space through menu-dependent and context-dependent projections. This axiomatic framework reconciles apparent inconsistencies highlighted by the Russian roulette example in the statistics literature and resolves the well-known Allais paradox from behavioral economics. We also derive an additional axiom required to reduce counterfactual utilities to standard utilities on the same potential outcome space, and establish an axiomatic foundation for additive counterfactual utilities, which satisfy a necessary and sufficient condition for point identification. Finally, we show that our results hold regardless of whether individual potential outcomes are deterministic or stochastic. Keywords: causal inference, decision theory, stochastic potential outcomes, counterfactual harm, behavioral economics

Introduction

Suppose a physician must decide whether to administer a new,
experimental drug to a patient. Let
D_{i}\in\{0,1\}

D_{i}=1

An oracle policy would administer the experimental drug only to patients

In practice, however, such a policies are not directly implementable because of the

(

.

Given this limitation, scholars typically base decisions solely on
realized outcomes and seeks to maximize average, rather than
individual-level, causal effects. Formally, a decision-maker specifies
a utility function

that depends on a given action

and
its realized outcome
y_{d}
. We refer to such utilities as

V(d;u):=\mathbb{E}[u(d;Y(d))]

(
30
, e.g.,)
.

Recently, a number of scholars have considered

;

;
33
;
31
;
5
;
10
;
11
;
24

. In this

that depends on both potential outcomes.
Evaluation is then based on the expected counterfactual utility

V(d;\tilde{u})=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{u}(d;Y(0),Y(1))]

(

;
24
)
. Counterfactual utility fits naturally within Wald’s statistical decision framework
(

)
, where the state of nature is the joint distribution of the full potential outcome vector.

This formulation allows utility to be defined at the individual level

principle of “do no harm” by assigning greater disutility to causing

(

;
33
;
5
;

)
.

Recently, however, some scholars have raised fundamental questions
about the coherence and transitivity of counterfactual utilities. For
example,

of asymmetric counterfactual utilities but raises the concern that
decisions based on them need not maximize survival. Others have voiced related criticisms

(

survival probability over one with a higher survival probability.
Based on this observation, GM concludes that counterfactual utilities
can yield nonsensical recommendations, such as money pumps, because
they “violate the axioms of classical ([von] Neumann–Morgenstern)
decision theory” (page 2). In
particular,

argues
that decisions based on counterfactual utilities can violate

In this paper, we establish an axiomatic foundation for counterfactual
statistical decision theory
(Section
3
).
Specifically, we show that decision making based on counterfactual utilities
constitutes a coherent framework by generalizing the von
Neumann–Morgenstern (vNM) axioms to the extended space where preferences are defined over all potential outcomes

(
41
)
. We further show that
counterfactual utilities can violate the vNM axioms where preferences
are instead defined over realized outcomes. This resolves the aforementioned controversy in the literature by distinguishing the space over which preferences are defined.
We connect counterfactual and standard decision theory by introducing an additional axiom under which counterfactual utilities collapse to standard utilities on this potential outcome space.

Using our theoretical results, we demonstrate that our axiomatization
reconciles the apparent inconsistency illustrated by GM’s Russian
roulette example. We also show that counterfactual utility can also be
used descriptively and resolve the well-known Allais paradox in the
behavioral economics literature
(
1
)
. This is achieved by generalizing regret theory
(
4
;
29
;
26
, e.g.,)
, while yielding transitive preferences when defined on the potential outcome space.

Furthermore, we derive an axiomatic characterization of
additive

counterfactual utilities (Section
4
). A central
criticism of counterfactual utilities is that their expectations are
generally not identifiable, as they depend on the joint distribution
of potential outcomes. In contrast, additive counterfactual utilities
depend only on the marginal distributions of potential outcomes and
constitute the largest class of counterfactual utilities whose
expectations are identifiable

(
24
)
.

To connect counterfactual utilities to the behavioral economics literature, we
formalize two projections through which counterfactual utilities can
induce preferences on the realized outcome space
(Section
5
). The
first is the
menu-dependent
projection, which nests regret
theory. This is the mapping considered by the aforementioned critics
of counterfactual utility. We show that under this mapping,
counterfactual utilities can fail to satisfy transitivity. An
alternative is the
context-dependent
projection, which yields
a transitive order on a finite domain.

Finally, we show that our axiomatic results hold regardless of whether
potential outcomes are treated as deterministic or stochastic
(Section
6
). We then examine GM’s proposal to extend
counterfactual utilities by taking expectations over stochastic
potential outcomes prior to evaluating decisions. We first demonstrate that the extension is not
unique, leading to ambiguity. In addition, under mild conditions, the
resulting utilities collapse to standard utilities that depend solely
on realized outcomes, thereby eliminating the asymmetry that is a key
feature of counterfactual utilities. We further show that this
extended utility violates the vNM independence axiom on the potential
outcome space and the same vNM violations arise after projecting the
induced preferences onto the realized outcome space. Fortunately,
under our framework, these inconsistencies can be resolved only by
imposing additivity, which yields additive counterfactual utilities.

2
Examples of Asymmetric Counterfactual Utilities

To motivate our theoretical development, we begin by revisiting GM’s Russian roulette example and show that, when placed in a medical context, its conclusion no longer appears nonsensical. This suggests that, like standard utilities, counterfactual utilities are inherently context-dependent. Furthermore, we illustrate the descriptive power of counterfactual utilities using the well-known Allais paradox from behavioral economics. We show that asymmetric counterfactual utilities can account for a common pattern of decision-making observed in practice rather than serving a purely normative role.

2.1
Russian Roulette

GM considers the game of Russian roulette, in which one must choose between two lotteries:

•

R_{1/6}
: survive with probability
5/6
and die with probability
1/6
,

•

R_{1/7}
: survive with probability
6/7
and die with probability
1/7
.

In this case,
R_{1/7}
is clearly preferred, as it offers a higher probability of survival. Let
d=0
and
d=1
denote
R_{1/6}
and
R_{1/7}
, respectively, and let
Y=1
represent survival and
Y=0
death. Under the standard utility function
u(d;y_{d})=y_{d}
, expected utility therefore ranks
R_{1/7}
above
R_{1/6}
, i.e.,
V(1;u)>V(0;u)
.

GM introduces the following asymmetric counterfactual utility function, which implies that survival under
R_{1/7}
yields only half the gain relative to survival under
R_{1/6}
:

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(0;y_{0},y_{1})=\mathds{1}{\left\{y_{0}>y_{1}\right\}},\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{u}(1;y_{0},y_{1})=0.5\,\mathds{1}{\left\{y_{0}<y_{1}\right\}}.

(1)

The decision is determined by the expected utility difference
V(1;\tilde{u})-V(0;\tilde{u})
.
To evaluate this, GM assumes independence of the two potential outcomes:

Assumption 1
(Independent Potential Outcomes)

Y(0)
\perp\!\!\!\perp
Y(1).

Under this assumption, GM shows that

V(1;\tilde{u})-V(0;\tilde{u})=-\frac{1}{21}<0
,
which leads to the nonsensical conclusion that
R_{1/6}
should be preferred to
R_{1/7}
.

We now show that this seemingly nonsensical result can be reasonable in a different setting.
Recall the example briefly described in Section
1
, in which a physician must decide whether to administer an experimental drug (
d=1
) or provide standard care (
d=0
), with the outcome of interest being survival (
Y=1
) versus death (
Y=0
).
Under the same numerical specification as in the Russian roulette example,
6/7\approx 86\%
of patients would survive under the experimental drug, compared to
5/6\approx 83\%
under standard care.

However, under Assumption
1
, only
1/7\approx 14\%
of patients
benefit
from the experimental drug, that is,
(Y(0),Y(1))=(0,1)
, while
5/42\approx 12\%
of patients are
harmed
, that is,
(Y(0),Y(1))=(1,0)
. Thus, although the average survival rate is higher under the experimental drug, a non-negligible fraction of patients would be made worse off. In such a setting, it may be reasonable for a physician to prefer standard care, prioritizing the avoidance of adverse counterfactual outcomes over improvements in expected benefits. Indeed, such a decision reflects the Hippocratic principle of “do no harm”. Such a decision may also be reflective of the physician’s fear of a malpractice lawsuit, whereby evidence may be easier to gather if
d=1
means admitting the patient to the hospital.

This example underscores the fact that a utility function represents the preferences of the decision-maker and is therefore inherently subjective and context-dependent. A utility specification that appears reasonable in one setting may seem nonsensical in another.
The same point applies to standard utilities as well. For example, one could use standard utilities such as
u(d;y_{d})=-y_{d}
and
u(d;y_{d})=y_{d}-c\cdot d
with
c
being a cost parameter, which would recommend
R_{1/6}
for a sufficiently large value of
c
. However, such utilities, which may be reasonable in other contexts, do not make much sense in the Russian roulette example.
Moreover, different decision-makers may adopt distinct utility functions even in the same context, reflecting heterogeneous ethical, clinical, or practical considerations.

2.2
The Allais Paradox

Next, through the well-known Allais paradox

(
1
)
, we illustrate that asymmetric
counterfactual utility can be used descriptively.

22
report the results of two experiments. In
the first experiment, subjects are asked to choose one of the
following two lotteries.

a_{1}
.

Receive $4000 with
0.8
probability.

b_{1}
.

Receive $3000 with certainty.

The authors show that most people choose Option (
b_{1}
) over Option (
a_{1}
). In the second experiment, subjects are asked to choose between the following slightly modified options,

a_{2}
.

Receive $4000 with
0.2
probability.

b_{2}
.

Receive $3000 with
0.25
probability.

The authors find that in this experiment, most people select Option (
a_{2}
) instead of Option (
b_{2}
).

This empirical finding is inconsistent with the standard utility.
To see this, assume that the utility depends only on the monetary value they receive, regardless of which option one chooses, i.e.,
u(d;y_{d})=u(y_{d})
. Without loss of generality, we normalize
u(0)=0
. Then, the result of the first experiment implies
V(b_{1};u)>V(a_{1};u)\Leftrightarrow u(3000)>0.8u(4000)
. In contrast, the result of the second experiment implies
V(a_{2};u)>V(b_{2};u)\Leftrightarrow u(3000)<0.8u(4000)
. This leads to a contradiction regardless of what values
u(4000)
and
u(3000)
take.

The behavioral economics literature has proposed many models to
explain the Allais paradox and other related phenomena (e.g., reflection effect, probabilistic insurance, and preference reversals) documented by

22
. One prominent approach is regret theory
(
4
;
29
;
7
;
26
, e.g.,)
. Suppose that a subject experiences “regret” when forgoing the option that would have yielded a better outcome, and has “rejoicing” when the chosen option is better than the alternative. This idea can be formalized through the following asymmetric counterfactual utility
(
4
)

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{\mathrm{Bell}}(d;y_{0},y_{1})=y_{d}+f_{\lambda}(y_{d}-y_{1-d})\quad\text{where}\quad f_{\lambda}(r)=1-\exp{(-\lambda r)},\lambda>0.

(2)

If
r>0
, then
f_{\lambda}(r)>0
and captures rejoicing. If
r<0
, then
f_{\lambda}(r)<0
and captures regret. Because
|f_{\lambda}(-r)|>|f_{\lambda}(r)|
for all
r>0
, the utility encodes that a loss is evaluated more severely than a gain of the same magnitude. Under the assumption of independent lotteries, i.e., Assumption
1
, this asymmetric counterfactual utility can lead to the decisions consistent with the above experimental results (e.g.,
\lambda\geq 0.003
).
In Section
5
, we show that counterfactual decision theory nests regret theory as a special case.

3
Axiomatizing Counterfactual Decision Theory

The examples presented above suggest that, contrary to some recent claims in the literature, counterfactual utilities can provide a coherent decision-theoretic framework. In this section, we formalize this perspective by generalizing the axioms of
41
to the
potential outcome space
,
establishing that counterfactual decision theory constitutes a coherent decision-theoretic framework. We then introduce an additional axiom under which the framework reduces to standard utilities.

3.1
Setup

Consider a setting in which a decision
D\in\mathcal{D}:=\{0,1,\ldots,K-1\}
is chosen for a unit with potential outcomes
(Y(0),\ldots,Y(K-1))\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}
and covariates
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{X}
, where
\mathcal{Y}:=\{0,1,\ldots,M-1\}
and
K,M\geq 2
. We assume
\mathcal{X}
is finite. Let
\mathcal{Z}:=\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X}
denote the
potential outcome space
.

Our aim is to evaluate the quality of a (possibly randomized)
policy

\pi:\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{D})
, where
\Delta(\mathcal{D})
denotes the set of probability distributions on
\mathcal{D}
. Our framework asks the decision maker to evaluate all policies, including oracle policies that depends on the full vector of potential outcomes, though in practice a policy will only depend on covariates, i.e.,
\pi(d;y_{0},\ldots,y_{K-1},\bm{x})=\pi(d;\bm{x})
. We use the extended evaluative domain to because it allows us to fully express the decision maker’s preferences. Each policy
\pi
induces a decision defined by

D\mid Y(0)=y_{0},\ldots,Y(K-1)=y_{K-1},\bm{X}=\bm{x}\sim\pi(\cdot;y_{0},\ldots,y_{K-1},\bm{x}).

To quantify the consequence of choosing
d
for a unit with potential outcomes
\bm{y}=(y_{0},\ldots,y_{K-1})
and covariates
\bm{x}
, we specify a
counterfactual utility
function
\tilde{u}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
and evaluate a policy based on its expectation,

\displaystyle V_{P}(\pi;\tilde{u})

\displaystyle:=\ \mathbb{E}_{P^{\pi}}[\tilde{u}(D;Y(0),\ldots,Y(K-1),\bm{X})]

\displaystyle\ =\ \sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{u}(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})\cdot P^{\pi}(D=d,Y(0)=y_{0},\ldots,Y(K-1)=y_{K-1},\bm{X}=\bm{x})

\displaystyle\ =\ \sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}}\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{u}(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})\cdot\pi(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})\cdot P(Y(0)=y_{0},\ldots,Y(K-1)=y_{K-1},\bm{X}=\bm{x}),

where
P^{\pi}(d,\bm{y},\bm{x})=\pi(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})\cdot P(\bm{y},\bm{x})
is a joint law of
Z=(D,Y(0),\ldots,Y(K-1),\bm{X})\in\mathcal{Z}
induced by the policy
\pi
and the probability measure
P
. The policy
\pi
determines the conditional distribution of the decision given
(\bm{y},\bm{x})
, while
P
specifies the joint distribution of potential outcomes and covariates. We treat
P
as the unknown
state of nature

(
42
;
6
;
30
;
24
, e.g.,)
. In the terminology of classical decision theory, a policy
\pi
corresponds to an
act
that maps a state of the world
P
to an outcome distribution
P^{\pi}
, i.e.,
\pi(P)=P^{\pi}

(
39
, e.g.,)
.

Let
\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
denote the set of probability measures on
\mathcal{Z}
. Because we allow for an arbitrary oracle policy, every element of
\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
can be written as
P^{\pi}
for some pair
(\pi,P)
. This is interpreted as the distribution induced by deploying policy
\pi
in state
P
. Given a utility function
\tilde{u}
, we evaluate
P^{\pi}
by its value
V_{P}(\pi;\tilde{u})
, which induces a preference relation on
\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
.

Definition 1
(Preference Relation)

Let
P^{\pi},Q^{\rho}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
be probability distributions on
\mathcal{Z}
where
\pi
and
\rho
denote policies and
P
and
Q
represent the state of nature. Then, a binary preference relation
\succsim
on
\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
is represented by a counterfactual utility
\tilde{u}
if the value of policy
\pi
in state
P
is no less than that of policy
\rho
in state
Q
, i.e.,

P^{\pi}\succsim Q^{\rho}\iff V_{P}(\pi;\tilde{u})\geq V_{Q}(\rho;\tilde{u}).

For a given utility
\tilde{u}
, Definition
1
states that one (weakly) prefers deploying
\pi
in state
P
over deploying
\rho
in state
Q
if and only if the value of the former is no less than that of the latter. In other words,
\tilde{u}
ranks policy–state pairs
(\pi,P)
via
P^{\pi}
.

As a special case, we can define preferences over (non-oracle) policies within a fixed state of nature
P\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\pi\succsim_{(\tilde{u},P)}\rho\iff P^{\pi}\succsim_{\tilde{u}}P^{\rho}.

(3)

However, allowing
P
and
Q
to differ is useful when comparing policies across distinct populations with different covariate and causal effect distributions, as emphasized in the external-validity and generalization literature
(
3
;
14
, e.g.,)
. For example, one may compare policy implementations across different cities or demographic groups.

Although some comparisons across states are immediate (e.g., a decision maker will typically prefer a healthier population over a sicker one), preferences across states carry additional information. If a preference relation is defined within a fixed state, many utility-state pairs
(\tilde{u},P)
can rationalize the same ordering in Equation (
3
). Indeed, if we treat both utility and beliefs as subjective, they may become confounded; a high value may reflect either a high perceived likelihood of certain states or a high utility assigned to them
(
2
, e.g.,)
. By requiring the decision maker to rank elements in
\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
, Theorem
1
can recover a unique counterfactual utility
\tilde{u}
(up to a positive affine transformation) that represents these preferences. This identifies not only an induced ordering of decisions, but also the decision maker’s tradeoffs across decisions and states.

We emphasize that counterfactual utility ranks distributions on the potential outcome space
\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
, rather than labels
d\in\mathcal{D}
themselves. This is consistent with standard vNM decision theory, where decisions carry no intrinsic value apart from the distribution of realized outcomes they induce.

3.2
Axioms

Expected counterfactual utility represents expected utility on the potential outcome space
\mathcal{Z}
. Hence, it follows that the induced preferences are characterized by the von Neumann–Morgenstern (vNM) axioms
(
41
)
. We review these axioms and show how they apply to counterfactual utility.

Let
\succsim
denote a preference relation on
\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
. For
p,q\in\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
, write
p\succ q
if
p\succsim q
but not
q\succsim p
, and write
p\sim q
if both
p\succsim q
and
q\succsim p
.

Axiom 1
(Completeness)

For all
p,q\in\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
, either
p\succsim q
or
q\succsim p
.

Axiom
1
requires that one can compare any pair of distributions. In our setting, this means that we can rank any two policy–state pairs,
(\pi,P)
and
(\rho,Q)
.

Axiom 2
(Transitivity)

For all
p,q,r\in\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
, if
p\succsim q
and
q\succsim r
, then
p\succsim r
.

Axiom
2
requires that preferences are consistent across multiple options and rules out preference cycles. In our context, if the policy–state pair
(\pi,P)
is preferred to
(\rho,Q)
and
(\rho,Q)
is preferred to
(\sigma,R)
, then
(\pi,P)
must be preferred to
(\sigma,R)
. Together with Axiom
1
, this axiom implies that
\succsim
is a weak order.

For the next set of axioms, fix
\alpha\in[0,1]
. For
p,q\in\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
, define their convex combination by
\{\alpha p+(1-\alpha)q\}(z):=\alpha p(z)+(1-\alpha)q(z)
,
z\in\mathcal{Z}
. That is, we draw
Z
from
p
with probability
\alpha
and from
q
with probability
1-\alpha
. This mixture is again a probability measure on
\mathcal{Z}
, and therefore is an element of
\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
.

Axiom 3
(Independence)

For all
p,q,r\in\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
, if
p\succ q
then
\alpha p+(1-\alpha)r\succ\alpha q+(1-\alpha)r
.

Axiom
3
requires that strict preferences are preserved under mixing with a common third option. In our setting,
(\pi,P)
is strictly preferred to
(\rho,Q)
, then mixing
(\pi,P)
and
(\sigma,R)
with with probabilities
\alpha
and
1-\alpha
, respectively, is strictly preferred to the mixture that selects
(\rho,Q)
with probability
\alpha
and
(\sigma,R)
with probability
1-\alpha
.

Axiom 4
(Continuity)

For all
p,q,r\in\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
, if
p\succ q\succ r
then there exists
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)
such that
\alpha p+(1-\alpha)r\succ q\succ\beta p+(1-\beta)r
.

Axiom
4
assumes that preferences are continuous so that they can be separated by sufficiently small deviations in probability.

The following theorem is an immediate application of the vNM theorem, applied to the potential outcome space
\mathcal{Z}
rather than the space of realized outcome
Y=Y(D)\in\mathcal{Y}
. It implies that preferences defined over both realized and counterfactual outcomes can be coherent. Modern proofs can be found in
25
and
18
.

Theorem 1
(von Neumann and Morgenstern for Counterfactual Utility)

The following are equivalent:

1.

\succsim
satisfies Axioms
1
–
4
;

2.

there exists a counterfactual utility
\tilde{u}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
that represents
\succsim
in the sense of Definition
1
.

Moreover, any such utility function
\tilde{u}
is unique up to a positive affine transformation.

Remark 1
(Realized and Potential Outcome Spaces)

At first glance, Theorem
1
appears to contradict claims
that counterfactual utility can result in nonsensical decision
recommendations such as money pumps because “it violates the axioms
of classical (von Neumann-Morgenstern) decision theory”

(
17
, page 2)
, or that they can be intransitive

(
37
)
. This apparent
conflict is resolved by distinguishing the space on which
preferences are defined. As we formally show in
Section
5
, the
violation of vNM axioms can occur when one interprets the induced
preferences only over the realized outcome
Y=Y(D)
on the

realized outcome space

\mathcal{Y}
.

By contrast, the counterfactual decision theory framework considered
here and elsewhere
(
28
;
24
, e.g.,)
defines preferences
on the
potential outcome space

\mathcal{Z}
. That is, decisions are
evaluated with respect to both realized and counterfactual outcomes

\{Y(k)\}_{k\in\mathcal{D}}
. Theorem
1
implies that on this extended space, preferences induced by expected counterfactual utility satisfy the vNM axioms.

3.3
Relation to Standard Decision Theory

Theorem
1
shows that preferences induced by counterfactual utilities satisfy the generalized vNM axioms on the potential outcome space. We now connect this result to the standard decision theory. Specifically, we characterize preferences induced by standard utilities, which depend only on
(D,Y(D),\bm{X})\in\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{X}
, when viewed as preferences on the potential outcome space
\mathcal{Z}
. We show that such utilities assume indifference to the
counterfactual
outcomes under alternative decisions. We introduce an axiom that formally characterizes the irrelevance of counterfactual outcomes.

Axiom 5
(Irrelevance of Counterfactual Outcomes)

Let
\delta_{z}(\cdot)
denote the Dirac measure at
z
. For all
p,q\in\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
,
d\in\mathcal{D}
and
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, if

1.

p(D=k)=q(D=k)=\delta_{d}(k),\quad\forall k\in\mathcal{D}
,

2.

p(\bm{X}=\bm{x}^{\prime})=q(\bm{X}=\bm{x}^{\prime})=\delta_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime}),\quad\forall\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
,

3.

p(Y(d)=y)=q(Y(d)=y),\quad\forall y\in\mathcal{Y}
,

then
p\sim q
.

In our setting, Axiom
5

implies that a decision maker is indifferent between
(\pi,P)
and

(\rho,Q)
if (1) the two policies always choose the same action
d
,
(2) both states are degenerate at the same covariate value
\bm{X}=\bm{x}

such that all units have the same covariate values, and (3) the induced marginal distribution of the realized outcome
Y(d)
coincides. Differences in the distribution of counterfactual outcomes
\{Y(k)\}_{k\neq d}
are irrelevant under this axiom.
Axiom
5
is weaker than requiring indifference between distributions that have the same joint distribution
(D,Y(D),\bm{X})
, although, under Axioms
1
–
4
, the two conditions are equivalent.

The next result shows that Axioms
1
–
5
characterize the preferences induced by standard utilities on the potential outcome space
\mathcal{Z}
.

Theorem 2
(Preferences of Standard Utilities on the potential outcome Space)

The following are equivalent:

1.

\succsim
satisfies Axioms
1
–
5
;

2.

there exists a standard utility
u(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})=u(d;y_{d},\bm{x})
that represents
\succsim
in the sense of Definition
1
.

Moreover, any such utility function
u
is unique up to a positive affine transformation.

The proof is given in Appendix
S3.1
.
Note that in standard decision theory, a utility often depends only on the realized outcome
Y(D)\in\mathcal{Y}
(but not the decision), i.e.,
\tilde{u}(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})=u(y_{d})
.
Appendix
S1
shows that, even in this special case, standard decision theory can be axiomatized using the potential outcome space
\mathcal{Z}
, yielding an axiom that is stronger than Axiom
5
. In particular, such a utility implies indifference whenever the distribution of the realized outcome is identical, even if it arises under different decisions.

3.4
Revisiting the Allais Paradox

Armed with the formal axiomatic properties derived above, we now
revisit the Allais paradox in Section
2
.
The Allais paradox violates the independence axiom when preferences are defined on the realized outcome space
\mathcal{Y}
. The first experiment corresponds to the distributions
p_{a_{1}}=0.8\delta_{4000}+0.2\delta_{0}
and
p_{b_{1}}=\delta_{3000}
, whereas the second experiment corresponds to
p_{a_{2}}=0.2\delta_{4000}+0.8\delta_{0}
and
p_{b_{2}}=0.25\delta_{3000}+0.75\delta_{0}
. Note that

p_{a_{2}}\ =\ 0.25p_{a_{1}}+0.75\delta_{0},\quad p_{b_{2}}\ =\ 0.25p_{b_{1}}+0.75\delta_{0}.

According to Axiom
3
(if restricted to
\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
),
p_{b_{1}}\succ p_{a_{1}}
would imply
p_{b_{2}}\succ p_{a_{2}}
. Yet, the experiment shows the opposite, suggesting that subjects’ behavior violates independence over
\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
.

However, as we now show, this does not violate independence over
\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
. Let
\mathcal{D}=\{0,1\}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be finite such that
\{0,3000,4000\}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
. Further, let
\delta_{(y_{0},y_{1})}
denote the Dirac measure at
(y_{0},y_{1})\in\mathcal{Y}^{2}
. Associate
a_{1},a_{2}
with
d=0
and
b_{1},b_{2}
with
d=1
. Under the independent coupling of Assumption
1
(other couplings are also possible), the two states of nature over
(Y(0),Y(1))
are given by,

P_{1}:=P_{a_{1}b_{1}}=0.8\,\delta_{(4000,3000)}+0.2\,\delta_{(0,3000)},

for the first experiment, and

P_{2}:=P_{a_{2}b_{2}}=0.05\,\delta_{(4000,3000)}+0.15\,\delta_{(4000,0)}+0.2\,\delta_{(0,3000)}+0.6\,\delta_{(0,0)},

for the second experiment. The induced measures on
\Delta(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}^{2})
are

q_{a_{1}}=P_{1}^{0}=\delta_{(d=0)}P_{1}
,
q_{b_{1}}=P_{1}^{1}=\delta_{(d=1)}P_{1}
,
q_{a_{2}}=P_{2}^{0}=\delta_{(d=0)}P_{2}
, and
q_{b_{2}}=P_{2}^{1}=\delta_{(d=1)}P_{2}
.
Note that

\displaystyle q_{a_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16}q_{a_{1}}+\frac{15}{16}\left(0.16\delta_{(d=0)}\delta_{(4000,0)}+0.2\delta_{(d=0)}\delta_{(0,3000)}+0.64\delta_{(d=0)}\delta_{(0,0)}\right),

\displaystyle q_{b_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16}q_{b_{1}}+\frac{15}{16}\left(0.16\delta_{(d=1)}\delta_{(4000,0)}+0.2\delta_{(d=1)}\delta_{(0,3000)}+0.64\delta_{(d=1)}\delta_{(0,0)}\right).

Unlike in the standard case, the remainder terms are not identical because
\delta_{(d=0)}\neq\delta_{(d=1)}
. Therefore, Axiom
3
(when applied to
\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
) does not imply
q_{b_{2}}\succ q_{a_{2}}
.

Under the utility function
\tilde{u}_{\mathrm{Bell}}
introduced in Equation (
2
), for any
\lambda\geq 0.003
, the induced policy values satisfy the following inequalities,

V_{P_{1}}(1;\tilde{u}_{\mathrm{Bell}})>V_{P_{1}}(0;\tilde{u}_{\mathrm{Bell}})>V_{P_{2}}(0;\tilde{u}_{\mathrm{Bell}})>V_{P_{2}}(1;\tilde{u}_{\mathrm{Bell}}),

implying
q_{b_{1}}\succ q_{a_{1}}\succ q_{a_{2}}\succ q_{b_{2}}
, which matches the observed choice pattern in the Allais paradox. This ranking indicates a preference of choosing
b_{1}
in state
P_{1}
to choosing
a_{1}
in
P_{1}
, to choosing
a_{2}
in
P_{2}
, to choosing
b_{2}
in
P_{2}
. In particular, each choice is evaluated conditional on the relevant correlation structure encoded by the state.

In the presentation above, subjects face binary menus, so we model the experiment as two separate binary decisions with
\mathcal{D}=\{0,1\}
. One can alternatively model the experiment as a single choice with
\mathcal{D}=\{0,1,2,3\}
, comparing all lotteries simultaneously. Under this interpretation, the paradox can also be resolved, for example with

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(d;y_{0},y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})=y_{d}+\sum_{d^{\prime}\neq d}f_{\lambda}(y_{d}-y_{d^{\prime}}),

(4)

where
f_{\lambda}
is defined in Equation (
2
). Under Assumption
1
(with independence among
\{Y(d)\}_{d\in\mathcal{D}}
), for
\lambda\geq 0.002
, the induced ranking on
\Delta(\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}^{4})
again matches the observed pattern with
b_{1}\succ a_{1}\succ a_{2}\succ b_{2}
given
P_{a_{1}b_{1}a_{2}b_{2}}
consistent with the marginals.

4
Identification of Counterfactual Utilities

A central criticism of counterfactual utilities is that their expectations are typically not point identified
(
13
;
12
;
35
;
36
, e.g.,)
. While in general
V_{P}(\pi;\tilde{u})
depends on the joint distribution of the full potential outcome vector, we only observe one potential outcome per unit. We discuss two remedies considered in the literature: partial identification, which derives sharp bounds, and additive counterfactual utilities, which are point-identifiable. We briefly summarize the former approach and then focus on the latter by characterizing the axioms that are implied by additivity.

4.1
Partial Identification

Under a partial identification approach, one derives sharp bounds on the unobserved joint distribution of potential outcomes consistent with the observed marginals, and then evaluates each policy by its worst-case value over the resulting identified set
(
5
)
.
Imposing additional assumptions (e.g., monotonicity) or leveraging external observational data, can tighten these bounds
(
27
;
28
)
.
While always applicable, partial identification can be conservative and may fail to deliver a policy recommendation, a difficulty that is often exacerbated when there are more treatments under consideration.

4.2
Additive Counterfactual Utilities

An alternative to partial identification is to consider a class of counterfactual utilities whose expectation is identifiable.
24
proves that
V_{P}(\pi,\tilde{u})
is identifiable for non-oracle policies for every state of nature
P
if and only if
\tilde{u}
is additive in potential outcomes, i.e., there exist
u_{k}:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
for
k\in\mathcal{D}
such that

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}u_{k}(d;y_{k},\bm{x}).

Due to the additive structure,
V_{P}(\pi;\tilde{u})
depends only on the marginal distributions of the potential outcomes, and is therefore identifiable under the unconfoundedness of the decision. No other class of utility functions yields an identifiable expectation for every state of nature.

Additivity breaks the dependence structure of potential outcomes and
is thus less expressive (or less general) than fully counterfactual utilities. Unlike
standard utilities, however, it still incorporates counterfactual
outcomes. The next axiom formalizes this point.

Axiom 6
(Irrelevance of Counterfactual Correlation)

For all
p,q\in\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
,
d\in\mathcal{D}
and
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, if

1.

p(D=k)=q(D=k)=\delta_{d}(k),\quad\forall k\in\mathcal{D}
,

2.

p(\bm{X}=\bm{x}^{\prime})=q(\bm{X}=\bm{x}^{\prime})=\delta_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime}),\quad\forall\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
,

3.

p(Y(k)=y)=q(Y(k)=y),\quad\forall k\in\mathcal{D},\forall y\in\mathcal{Y}
,

then
p\sim q
.

Axiom
6
weakens Axiom
5
by strengthening its premise. For the decision maker to be indifferent between distributions, the marginal distributions of
all
potential outcomes, realized or counterfactual, must coincide. In other words, the decision maker cares about counterfactual outcomes, but is indifferent to their dependence structure.

The next theorem establishes that Axioms
1
–
4
and
6
characterize the preferences induced by additive utilities on the potential outcome space
\mathcal{Z}
. The proof is given in Appendix
S3.2
.

Theorem 3
(Preferences of Additive Utilities on the potential outcome Space)

The following are equivalent:

1.

\succsim
satisfies Axioms
1
–
4
and
6
;

2.

there exists an additive utility
\tilde{u}(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}u_{k}(d;y_{k},\bm{x})
that represents
\succsim
in the sense of Definition
1
.

Moreover, the utility function
\tilde{u}
is unique up to a positive affine transformation.

Proposition 1 of
24
shows that when the decision is binary (i.e.,
K=2
), for every additive counterfactual utility
\tilde{u}
, there exist a standard utility
u
and a decision-independent function
h
such that

\tilde{u}(d;\bm{y},\bm{x})=u(d;y_{d},\bm{x})+h(\bm{y},\bm{x}).

Consequently, for any fixed state of nature
P
,
\tilde{u}
and
u
induce the same ranking over policies
\pi
. However, they need not induce the same preference relation on
\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
, since
\mathbb{E}_{P}[h]
may vary with
P
and thus affects comparison across states. This means that even in the binary decision case and under Axioms
1
–
4
, Axiom
6
is strictly weaker than Axiom
5
. Too see this, consider the following additive utility,

\tilde{u}(d;\bm{y})=y_{0}+y_{1},

where
h=\tilde{u}
and
u=0
in the above formulation. Consider the two states
P((Y(0),Y(1))=(0,1))=1
and
Q((Y(0),Y(1))=(0,0))=1
. Let
d=0
, then
P^{0}\succ_{\tilde{u}}Q^{0}
. However, since
P^{0}(Y(0))=Q^{0}(Y(0))
, Axiom
5
implies
P^{0}\sim Q^{0}
.

Furthermore,
24
shows that no such equivalence holds when
K\geq 3
(Proposition 2). In these cases, additive counterfactual utilities are strictly more expressive than standard utilities, and their decision-making cannot, in general, be replicated by any standard utility.

5
Projecting Counterfactual Preferences onto Realized Outcome Space

In
Sections
3
and
4
,
we characterized preferences induced by counterfactual utilities on
the potential outcome space
\mathcal{Z}
. In behavioral economics and
psychology, preferences are often expressed over (monetary) lotteries
on the
realized
outcome space
\mathcal{Y}
. We therefore consider how
to “project” preferences from
\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
onto
\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
as
briefly mentioned in Remark
1
. In what follows, we discuss two such projections, one is menu-dependent and the other context-dependent.

5.1
Setup

Let
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and let
\mathcal{D}
be a nonempty index set, where
each
d\in\mathcal{D}
corresponds to a lottery
p_{d}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
. A

menu
is a nonempty, finite subset
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
from
which an individual chooses. Note that the individual is presented
with marginal distributions, but the correlation is not given. Choices
can be summarized by the following
choice function

\chi:2^{\mathcal{D}}\setminus\{\emptyset\}\to 2^{\mathcal{D}}\setminus\{\emptyset\},

with
\chi(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{A}
for all
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
. For example, if an individual chooses lottery
p_{a}
from menu
\mathcal{A}=\{a,b\}
, then
\chi(\mathcal{A})=\{a\}
. For simplicity of exposition, we suppress the covariates, though one could allow
\chi
to depend on them.

A special role is played by choice functions that are induced by maximizing a
value
function
V:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
:

\chi(\mathcal{A})=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{d\in\mathcal{A}}V(d).

(5)

Such choice behavior can be characterized by two axioms
(
38
)
.

Axiom 7
(Sen’s
\alpha
condition)

If
d\in\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
and
d\in\chi(\mathcal{B})
, then
d\in\chi(\mathcal{A})
.

Axiom
7
is implied by value maximization in
Equation (
5
), since a choice
d
selected from menu
\mathcal{B}
must have a higher value than any other element of
\mathcal{B}
, and therefore any element of
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
. This axiom is also known as independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) or Chernoff’s condition
(
9
)
.

The second condition is technical and concerns tie-breaking.

Axiom 8
(Sen’s
\beta
condition)

If
d,d^{\prime}\in\chi(\mathcal{A})
,
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
and
d^{\prime}\in\chi(\mathcal{B})
, then
d\in\chi(\mathcal{B})
.

Theorem 4
(Value Maximization)

The following are equivalent:

1.

\chi
satisfies Axioms
7
and
8
;

2.

there exists a value function
V:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
that induces
\chi
in the sense of Equation (
5
).

A proof can be found in
25
. Axioms
7
and
8
together are known as the Weak Axiom of Revealed Preferences (WARP). Under WARP, the revealed preference relation

d\succsim^{\textsc{R}}d^{\prime}\iff d\in\chi(\{d,d^{\prime}\})

is complete and transitive and is represented by a value function
V
.

5.2
Menu-Dependent Projection

We first consider the menu-dependent projection of counterfactual
preferences onto
\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
. This projection can capture regret
behavior
4
;
29
;
26
and connects to the confusion about
counterfactual utility discussed in
Remark
1
. This projection may not satisfy WARP and can induce intransitive revealed preferences.

For each menu
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
, choose a counterfactual utility
\tilde{u}_{\mathcal{A}}:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathbb{R}
and a state of nature
P_{\mathcal{A}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{A}})
such that each lottery
d\in\mathcal{A}
is a marginal of
P_{\mathcal{A}}
, i.e.,
P(Y(d))=p_{d}
. Define

\chi^{\textsc{M}}(\mathcal{A})=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{d\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mathcal{A}}}[\tilde{u}_{\mathcal{A}}(d;(Y(k))_{k\in\mathcal{A}})].

Because
(\tilde{u}_{\mathcal{A}},P_{\mathcal{A}})
may vary with
\mathcal{A}
,
\chi^{\textsc{M}}
can violate Sen’s
\alpha
condition (Axiom
7
). As a consequence, the induced pairwise revealed preference relation on
\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
, which is defined as,

\displaystyle d\succsim^{\textsc{M}}d^{\prime}

\displaystyle\iff d\in\chi^{\textsc{M}}(\{d,d^{\prime}\})

\displaystyle\iff\mathbb{E}_{P_{\{d,d^{\prime}\}}}\left[\tilde{u}(d;Y(d),Y(d^{\prime}))\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{P_{\{d,d^{\prime}\}}}\left[\tilde{u}(d^{\prime};Y(d^{\prime}),Y(d)\right],

(6)

may not be transitive.

Moreover, intransitivity can persist even by fixing menu size (so a single, menu-independent utility can be chosen) and imposing a common correlation structure in the joint probability measure (e.g., independence). The following example illustrates this.

Example 1

Let
\mathcal{Y}=\{1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9\}
and
\{a,b,c\}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
such that

p_{a}=\frac{1}{3}(\delta_{2}+\delta_{4}+\delta_{9}),\qquad p_{b}=\frac{1}{3}(\delta_{1}+\delta_{6}+\delta_{8}),\qquad p_{c}=\frac{1}{3}(\delta_{3}+\delta_{5}+\delta_{7}).

For every binary menu, assume independence between lotteries and let
\tilde{u}(d;y_{d},y_{d^{\prime}})=\mathds{1}{\left\{y_{d}>y_{d^{\prime}}\right\}}
. Then

P(Y(a)>Y(b))=\frac{5}{9}>\frac{4}{9}=P(Y(b)>Y(a)),

P(Y(b)>Y(c))=\frac{5}{9}>\frac{4}{9}=P(Y(c)>Y(b)),

P(Y(c)>Y(a))=\frac{5}{9}>\frac{4}{9}=P(Y(a)>Y(c)).

Hence

a\succ^{\textsc{M}}b,\qquad b\succ^{\textsc{M}}c,\qquad c\succ^{\textsc{M}}a,

Thus, the pairwise revealed preference relation induced by the menu-dependent projection is not transitive.

However, the preference relation is transitive on the potential outcome space. Facing three separate binary choice problems we can model them with
\mathcal{D}=\{0,1\}
, states of nature (under the same independent coupling)
P_{ab}
,
P_{bc}
, and
P_{ac}
, and utility
\tilde{u}
. Choosing
a
over
b
corresponds to
P_{ab}^{a}=\delta_{(d=0)}P_{ab}\succ\delta_{(d=1)}P_{ab}=P_{ab}^{b}
, choosing
b
over
c
to
P_{bc}^{b}=\delta_{(d=0)}P_{bc}\succ\delta_{(d=1)}P_{bc}=P_{bc}^{c}
, and choosing
c
over
a
to
P_{ac}^{c}=\delta_{(d=1)}P_{ac}\succ\delta_{(d=0)}P_{ac}=P_{ac}^{a}
. Since each comparison is made within a different state, transitivity is not violated. In fact, we obtain the global ranking
P_{ab}^{a}\sim P_{bc}^{b}\sim P_{ac}^{c}\succ P_{ab}^{b}\sim P_{bc}^{c}\sim P_{ac}^{a}
.

Example 2

The same setup as in Example
1
also yields a violation of Sen’s
\alpha
condition (Axiom
7
). Let
\mathcal{A}=\{a,c\}
and
\mathcal{B}=\{a,b,c\}
with
\tilde{u}_{\mathcal{A}}(d,y_{d},y_{d^{\prime}})=\mathds{1}{\left\{y_{d}>y_{d^{\prime}}\right\}}
as before and
\tilde{u}_{\mathcal{B}}(d,y_{d},y_{d^{\prime}},y_{d^{\prime\prime}})=(\mathds{1}{\left\{d=a\right\}}+1)\mathds{1}{\left\{y_{d}>y_{d^{\prime}}\right\}}+\mathds{1}{\left\{y_{d}>y_{d^{\prime\prime}}\right\}}
. Then,
\chi^{\textsc{M}}(\mathcal{A})=\{c\}
, but
\chi^{\textsc{M}}(\mathcal{B})=\{a\}
. Since
a\in\mathcal{A}
, this violates Sen’s
\alpha
condition.

The intransitivity result of
37
was derived using the same utility as in Example
1
and is discussed further in Example
5
.
The next result, taken from
26
, characterizes when
\succsim^{\textsc{M}}
is transitive.

Proposition 1
(Transitivity under Menu-dependent Projection)

Let
\tilde{u}:\{0,1\}\times\mathcal{Y}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a counterfactual utility with
\tilde{u}(0;y_{0},y_{1})=\tilde{u}(1,y_{1},y_{0}),\,\forall y_{0},y_{1}\in\mathcal{Y}
. Then, the following are equivalent:

1.

\succsim^{\textsc{M}}
is transitive for every
P\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{2})
;

2.

there exists
u:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
and a symmetric function
h:\mathcal{Y}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,
h(x,y)=h(y,x)
, such that

\tilde{u}(d;y_{d},y_{d^{\prime}})=u(y_{d})+h(y_{d},y_{d^{\prime}}).

Proposition
1
shows that, under the menu-dependent projection, transitivity can be recovered only by restricting to utilities that effectively ignore correlation across lotteries. This contrasts with counterfactual decision making on the potential outcome space, which remains transitive even under correlation because the dependence structure is part of the state being evaluated. The condition

\tilde{u}(0;y_{0},y_{1})=\tilde{u}(1,y_{1},y_{0}),\,\forall y_{0},y_{1}\in\mathcal{Y}
can be interpreted as the utility not assigning labels to either lottery. It implies skew symmetry on the induced utility contrast.

For binary menus, the menu-dependent projection subsumes expected-regret models
(
4
;
29
, e.g.,)
, since it allows arbitrary utility–state pairs. It is closely related to
26
, though he works with utility contrasts and preferences on
\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{2})
. For non-binary menus, however, the menu-dependent projection does not pin down a unique way to rank suboptimal choices within a menu.

5.3
Context-Dependent Projection

We next consider the menu-independent (but context-dependent) projection that incorporates the full set of available lotteries directly into the utility, inducing preferences only on a finite set but satisfying WARP.

Let
\mathcal{D}=\{0,1,\ldots,K-1\}
index the set of available lotteries. Fix a counterfactual utility
\tilde{u}:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\to\mathbb{R}
and a state of nature
P\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}})
such that each lottery
p_{d}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
is a marginal of
P
, i.e.,
P(Y(d))=p_{d}
for all
d\in\mathcal{D}
. Define the choice function,

\chi^{\textsc{C}}_{(\tilde{u},P)}(\mathcal{A})=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{d\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[\tilde{u}(d;Y(0),\ldots,Y(K-1))].

Its associated value function is
V_{P}(d;\tilde{u})=\mathbb{E}_{P}[\tilde{u}(d;Y(0),\ldots,Y(K-1))]
. Hence, by Theorem
4
,
\chi^{\textsc{C}}_{(\tilde{u},P)}
satisfies Sen’s
\alpha
and
\beta
conditions (Axioms
7
and
8
). The induced revealed preference relation, which is given by,

d\succsim^{\textsc{C}}_{(\tilde{u},P)}d^{\prime}\iff d\in\chi^{\textsc{C}}_{(\tilde{u},P)}(\{d,d^{\prime}\}),

is therefore complete and transitive. However, this preference relation might still be context dependent because a preference between
d
and
d^{\prime}
may depend on what other lotteries are in
\mathcal{D}
. The following example highlights this.

Example 3

Let
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
and consider the lotteries

p_{a}=\delta_{1},\qquad p_{b}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{1},\qquad p_{c_{1}}=\frac{3}{4}\delta_{0}+\frac{1}{4}\delta_{1},\qquad p_{c_{2}}=\frac{1}{4}\delta_{0}+\frac{3}{4}\delta_{1}.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{1}=\{a,b,{c_{1}}\}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}=\{a,b,{c_{2}}\}
, and consider the same menu
\mathcal{A}=\{a,b\}
in both cases. Define an additive counterfactual utility,

\tilde{u}(a;y_{a},y_{b},y_{c})=1-y_{c},\qquad\tilde{u}(b;y_{a},y_{b},y_{c})=y_{b}.

Then, we have,

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{u}(a;Y(a),Y(b),Y(c))]=P(Y(c)=0),\qquad\mathbb{E}[\tilde{u}(b;Y(a),Y(b),Y(c))]=\mathbb{E}[Y(b)]=\frac{1}{2}.

Under
\mathcal{D}_{1}
,
P(Y(c_{1})=0)=3/4
, so
a\succ^{\textsc{C}}_{(\tilde{u},P)}b
. Under
\mathcal{D}_{2}
,
P(Y(c_{2})=0)=1/4
, so
b\succ^{\textsc{C}}_{(\tilde{u},P)}a
. As this counterfactual utility is additive, the conclusion does not depend on a particular choice of
P
. However, if we modify the additive counterfactual utility to

\bar{u}(a;y_{a},y_{b},y_{c})=2+y_{c},\qquad\bar{u}(b;y_{a},y_{b},y_{c})=y_{b},

then, we have
\tilde{u}(a;y_{a},y_{b},y_{c})-\tilde{u}(b;y_{a},y_{b},y_{c})\geq 1
for all
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{3}
. Thus,
a\succ^{\textsc{C}}_{(\bar{u},P)}b
for every
P
and
\succsim^{\textsc{C}}_{(\bar{u},P)}
is context-independent for the pair
(a,b)
.

While standard utilities
u(d;y_{d})
are inherently context-independent, the example above shows that additive counterfactual utilities may or may not be, depending on their structure. Thus, standard utilities are sufficient for context independence, but not necessary, whereas additive counterfactual utilities are not sufficient.

Moreover, the induced relation
\succsim^{\textsc{C}}_{(\tilde{u},P)}
generally does not satisfy the vNM independence (Axiom
3
) or continuity (Axiom
4
) on
\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
. The next example demonstrates the violation of vNM independence.

Example 4

Assume the setup of Example
3
and focus on
\mathcal{D}_{1}=\{a,b,c_{1}\}
. Extend
\tilde{u}
by

\tilde{u}(c_{1};y_{a},y_{b},y_{c_{1}})=\frac{5}{8}.

Then,
V_{P}(c_{1};\tilde{u})=5/8
, such that

a\succ^{\textsc{C}}_{(\tilde{u},P)}c_{1}\succ^{\textsc{C}}_{(\tilde{u},P)}b,

for any
P
consistent with the marginals.
Now take
\alpha=1/3
. If the induced preference on
\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
satisfied vNM independence,
a\succ^{\textsc{C}}_{(\tilde{u},P)}c_{1}
would imply

p_{b}=\frac{1}{3}p_{a}+\frac{2}{3}p_{c_{1}}\succ^{\textsc{C}}_{(\tilde{u},P)}\frac{1}{3}p_{c_{1}}+\frac{2}{3}p_{c_{1}}=p_{c_{1}},

contradicting
c_{1}\succ^{\textsc{C}}_{(\tilde{u},P)}b
.

However, the same example does not violate independence on the potential outcome space. To see this, associate
\mathcal{D}
with
\{0,1,2\}
and fix a state
P
consistent with the marginals. Then
a=\delta_{(d=0)}P,b=\delta_{(d=1)}P,c_{1}=\delta_{(d=2)}P
. Now
\frac{1}{3}a+\frac{2}{3}c_{1}=\frac{1}{3}\delta_{(d=0)}P+\frac{2}{3}\delta_{(d=2)}P\neq\delta_{(d=1)}P=b
due to the difference in the decision indicator. Thus independence does not force a preference reversal.

In general, vNM independence and continuity are not well defined for the context-dependent projection on the realized outcome space. The preference relation is defined only on the finite set
\mathcal{D}
(otherwise
\tilde{u}
would require an infinite-dimensional input), and
\mathcal{D}
is typically not convex unless it is a singleton. For example, if
\mathcal{D}=\{p,q\}
with
p\succ q
, then for
\alpha\in(0,1)
the mixture
\alpha p+(1-\alpha)q
is not in
\mathcal{D}
, and hence cannot be ranked with
\tilde{u}
.

5.4
Discussion

Each projection models preferences on
\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
differently. The menu-dependent projection assumes that only lotteries available in the current menu affect choice. In contrast, the context-dependent projection assumes that choice depends on all lotteries, including those not currently available.

For example, in the Allais paradox of Section
3.4
, both methods take

\mathcal{D}=\{a_{1},b_{1},a_{2},b_{2}\}
. The menu-dependent projection models the data as two binary menus,
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\{a_{1},b_{1}\}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}=\{a_{2},b_{2}\}
, and may choose the utility given in Equation (
2
). On the potential outcome space, this corresponds to treating each menu as a separate binary decision problem with
\mathcal{D}=\{0,1\}
, associating
a_{1},a_{2}
with
d=0
and
b_{1},b_{2}
with
d=1
, as in Section
3.4
.

On the other hand, the context-dependent projection might use the same menus but evaluates choices directly on
\mathcal{D}=\{a_{1},b_{1},a_{2},b_{2}\}
and may use the utility given in Equation (
4
). On the potential-outcome space, this corresponds to modeling the setting as a single four-option decision problem.

As shown above, both projections are compatible with expected counterfactual utility and yield transitive preferences on the potential outcome space. However, on the marginal space
\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
, only the context-dependent projection is always transitive.

The next example, taken from
37
, illustrates this further.

Example 5

37
consider a setting, in which a decision maker decides among three medical treatments
\{a,b,c\}
and observes an ordered health outcome in
\mathcal{Y}=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}
(greater means better health). The authors are interested in preferences on the realized outcome space
\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
and wish to select the single best option from
\{a,b,c\}
. A natural approach is the context-dependent projection introduced in Section
5.3
that satisfies transitivity. This corresponds to specifying a utility
\tilde{u}:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
and choosing according to

\chi^{\textsc{C}}_{(\tilde{u},P)}(\{a,b,c\})=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{d\in\{a,b,c\}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[\tilde{u}(d;Y(a),Y(b),Y(c)))].

Instead, the authors model the setting as facing three separate binary choice problems, along the lines of Section
5.2
. They motivate this approach by viewing the decision as a choice among three (binary) randomized controlled trials. They consider the utility
\tilde{u}(d;y_{d},y_{d^{\prime}})=\mathds{1}{\left\{y_{d}>y_{d^{\prime}}\right\}}
with the following marginal distributions

p_{a}=\frac{1}{6}\delta_{1}+\frac{5}{6}\delta_{4},\qquad p_{b}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{5},\qquad p_{c}=\frac{5}{6}\delta_{3}+\frac{1}{6}\delta_{6}.

Under this setup, the authors show that under any coupling
P\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{3})

P(Y(b)>Y(a))\geq P(Y(a)>Y(b)),

P(Y(a)>Y(c))>P(Y(c)>Y(a)),

P(Y(c)>Y(b))\geq P(Y(b)>Y(c)),

which implies,

b\succsim^{\textsc{M}}a,\qquad a\succ^{\textsc{M}}c,\qquad c\succsim^{\textsc{M}}b.

Thus, this pairwise revealed preference relation induced by the menu-dependent projection is not transitive.

However, the preference relation is transitive on the potential outcome space. Following
37
, consider three separate binary choice problems. For any state of nature
P
with binary joints
P_{ab}
,
P_{bc}
, and
P_{ac}
, and utility
\tilde{u}
, choosing
b
over
a
corresponds to
P_{ab}^{b}=\delta_{(d=1)}P_{ab}\succsim\delta_{(d=0)}P_{ab}=P_{ab}^{a}
, choosing
a
over
c
to
P_{ac}^{a}=\delta_{(d=0)}P_{ac}\succ\delta_{(d=1)}P_{ac}=P_{ac}^{c}
and
choosing
c
over
b
to
P_{bc}^{c}=\delta_{(d=1)}P_{bc}\succsim\delta_{(d=0)}P_{bc}=P_{bc}^{b}
.
Since each comparison is made within a different state, transitivity is not violated.

6
Counterfactual Utilities with Stochastic Potential Outcomes

Finally, we examine the recent proposal by
17
(GM) that counterfactual utilities be defined in terms of stochastic, rather than deterministic, potential outcomes. As discussed in Remark
1
, the original motivation for this proposal is no longer relevant if preferences are modeled over the potential outcome space. Nevertheless, we consider it on its own terms as an alternative framework. Below, we first explain why the use of stochastic potential outcomes does not alter any of the axiomatic results derived above. We then show that the key aspect of GM’s proposal is the use of
extended
counterfactual utilities. We establish that these extended counterfactual utilities are not unique, reduce to standard utilities under a set of mild assumptions, and violate the vNM independence axiom. Our results imply that while the use of stochastic potential outcomes is valid, extended counterfactual utilities represent an incoherent framework.

6.1
Use of Stochastic Potential Outcomes

Our analysis so far has considered potential outcomes drawn from a super-population. In the causal inference literature, potential outcomes are commonly assumed to be
fixed
for a given unit, with randomness arising only from sampling
(
32
;
34
)
. This contrasts with
stochastic
potential outcomes, which have an additional source of randomness for each unit
(
19
;
40
)
.
As GM correctly recognizes, both frameworks “imply the same joint population distribution” (page 5). Since our axiomatic results only require the existence of probability distribution over
Z=(D,Y(0),\ldots,Y(K-1),\bm{X})
, they directly apply to counterfactual decision theory under stochastic potential outcomes. In other words, the use of stochastic potential outcomes does not alter the results derived in Sections
3
and
4
.

6.2
Extended Counterfactual Utilities

GM’s proposal not only uses stochastic potential outcomes but also depends on an extension of counterfactual utilities. Counterfactual utilities discussed so far are mappings from the the decision, the set of potential outcomes, and covariates, i.e.,

\tilde{u}:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}.

Given a state of nature
P\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X})
decisions are evaluated by their expected utility
V_{P}(d;\tilde{u})
.

Instead, GM proposes to assign the value
p_{d}(P)=\mathbb{E}_{P}[Y(d)]

to each stochastic potential outcome and then evaluate a decision using an
extended
counterfactual utility that incorporates decision, i.e.,
\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}(d;p_{0}(P),\ldots,p_{K-1}(P))
.
1
1

1

GM does not discuss randomized policies or covariates.
Note that even when the outcome is discrete, we have
p_{d}(P)\in\mathbb{R}
, implying the need to extend the domain of utility function. Because this construction depends only on marginal means, the extended counterfactual utility
\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}
is identifiable.

Specifically, GM proposes to extend the original utility in Equation (
1
) using the function

\displaystyle\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}(0;p_{0},p_{1})=\mathds{1}{\left\{p_{0}>p_{1}\right\}},\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}(1;p_{0},p_{1})=0.5\,\mathds{1}{\left\{p_{0}<p_{1}\right\}}.

As shown by GM, applying this extended counterfactual utility to the Russian roulette example
discussed in Section
2.1
yields the recommendation that the lottery with a higher survival probability
R_{1/7}
should be chosen,

\displaystyle\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}(1;p_{0},p_{1})-\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}(0;p_{0},p_{1})=0.5\,\mathds{1}{\left\{p_{0}<p_{1}\right\}}-\mathds{1}{\left\{p_{0}>p_{1}\right\}}=0.5>0.

(7)

Since only marginals enter Equation (
7
), Assumption
1
is not required for this result.

We can generalizes GM’s proposal to the mapping

\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}:\mathcal{D}\times\left(\Delta(\mathcal{Y})\right)^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}.

Given a distribution
\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
and a conditional distribution of potential outcomes
\zeta:\mathcal{X}\to\left(\Delta(\mathcal{Y})\right)^{\mathcal{D}}
we can compute the expected utility of each decision.

In general, the expectations of
\tilde{u}
and of
\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}
will be different, even if
\mu
and
\zeta
are consistent with
P
. In particular, given a fixed
\tilde{u}
there are many ways to define
\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}
so that it coincides with
\tilde{u}
on Dirac measures. We will discuss non-uniqueness of the extension in Section
6.3
. Before conducting our analysis, we point out that stochastic potential outcomes are not essential for this procedure. Indeed, the same exact setup can be applied to deterministic potential outcomes, which have the same marginal distributions, yielding the identical recommendation.

6.3
Non-Uniqueness

As noted above, GM’s procedure requires extending the domain of the utility function from binary inputs
y_{d}\in\{0,1\}
to real-valued quantities
p_{d}=\mathbb{E}[Y(d)]\in[0,1]
. However, this extension of counterfactual utility is not unique, and alternative extensions may yield different recommendations.

To illustrate this in the Russian roulette example, note that the utility contrast induced by Equation (
1
) can be written in two ways,

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(1;y_{0},y_{1})-\tilde{u}(0;y_{0},y_{1})=0.5\,\mathds{1}{\left\{y_{0}<y_{1}\right\}}-\mathds{1}{\left\{y_{0}>y_{1}\right\}}=0.5\,(1-y_{0})y_{1}-y_{0}(1-y_{1}).

Although these representations are equivalent on the binary domain, they lead to different extensions and hence can yield opposite recommendations. Specifically, the first representation yields the
asymmetric
extension, recommending
R_{1/7}
, as shown in Equation (
7
).
In contrast, the second representation leads to the following
product
extension, recommending
R_{1/6}
,

\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}_{\mathrm{Prd}}(1;p_{0},p_{1})-\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}_{\mathrm{Prd}}(0;p_{0},p_{1})=0.5\,(1-p_{0})p_{1}-p_{0}(1-p_{1})=-\frac{1}{21}<0.

In fact, there are infinitely many such extensions from
\tilde{u}:\{0,1\}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
to
\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}:\{0,1\}\times[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
that are equivalent on the original domain. Each extension implicitly encodes an additional structure. For instance, the product extension is equivalent to making the independence assumption (Assumption
1
) within the original framework of Section
3.1
, which is restrictive in many applications.

This issue becomes especially apparent when utilities are specified as payoff
tables rather than as closed-form expressions. In fact,
mathematically, a utility function
\tilde{u}:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
can be represented by a vector of real numbers. For
example, the utility in Equation (
1
) might only be
given in the following matrix form:

\tilde{u}(0;\,y_{0},y_{1})=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|cc}&y_{1}=0&y_{1}=1\\
\hline\cr y_{0}=0&0&0\\
y_{0}=1&1&0\end{array}\right],\qquad\tilde{u}(1;\,y_{0},y_{1})=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|cc}&y_{1}=0&y_{1}=1\\
\hline\cr y_{0}=0&0&\tfrac{1}{2}\\
y_{0}=1&0&0\end{array}\right].

GM’s procedure cannot be applied to payoff tables directly. One must first choose a functional form representation consistent with the table. This step can be nontrivial and, as we have seen, is generally non-unique.

6.4
Reduction to Standard Utility

The difference in the extended counterfactual utility given in
Equation (
7
) reduces to a comparison of
marginal means, i.e., whether
p_{1}>p_{0}
. It therefore yields the
same recommendation as the standard utility
u(d;y_{d})=y_{d}
under
the framework introduced in Section
3.1
. In particular,
the comparison no longer depends on the joint distribution of
potential outcomes, thereby eliminating the asymmetry that is present in the original counterfactual utility. In Appendix
S2
, we formalize and further generalize this result to arbitrary discrete outcomes. Specifically, according to Theorem
S2
in the appendix, under mild conditions and in the case of binary decisions, for every extended counterfactual utility, there exists an extended standard utility that yields the same recommendation in every state of nature.

6.5
Violation of the Independence Axiom

Finally, we show that the extended counterfactual utilities proposed by GM do not satisfy the generalized vNM independence axiom (Axiom
3
) over the potential outcome space. Indeed, only those
\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}
that admit a representation that is affine in the probability measure will lead to preferences consistent with Axiom
3
.
This result does not contradict the claim given in
Section
6.4
that, for a given
extended counterfactual utility
\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}
, there exists an
equivalent, extended standard utility that yields the same optimal
policy for any state of nature
P
. The reason is that here we focus on the preference relation over all (possibly non-optimal) policies and allow comparisons across states.

We begin by noting that an extended counterfactual utility induces the following preference relation over
\Delta(\mathcal{Z})
,

\displaystyle P^{d}\succsim Q^{k}\iff\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}\!\left(d;p_{0}(P),\ldots,p_{K-1}(P)\right)\geq\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}\!\left(k;p_{0}(Q),\ldots,p_{K-1}(Q)\right).

(8)

Like GM, we focus on deterministic decisions.
Consider the extension given in Equation (
7
). Let
P,Q\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{2})
satisfy

P((Y(0),Y(1))=(0,1))=1,\qquad Q((Y(0),Y(1))=(1,0))=1,

with marginal means
(p_{0},p_{1})=(0,1)
and
(q_{0},q_{1})=(1,0)
. For the fixed decision
d=1
,

\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}(1;p_{0},p_{1})=0.5,\qquad\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}(1;q_{0},q_{1})=0,

and hence
P^{1}\succ Q^{1}
.
Now, set
R=Q
and consider the following mixtures with the same weight
\alpha=0.4
,

L^{1}:=\alpha P^{1}+(1-\alpha)R^{1}=0.4P^{1}+0.6Q^{1},\qquad M^{1}:=\alpha Q^{1}+(1-\alpha)R^{1}=Q^{1},

where marginal means are
(l_{0},l_{1})=(0.6,0.4)
and
(m_{0},m_{1})=(1,0)
. Thus, the extended counterfactual utilities are given by,

\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}(1;l_{0},l_{1})=0,\qquad\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}(1;m_{0},m_{1})=0,

and therefore
L^{1}\sim M^{1}
. This contradicts Axiom
3
, which would require
L^{1}\succ M^{1}
because
P^{1}\succ Q^{1}
and
L^{1}
and
M^{1}
are mixtures of
P^{1}
and
Q^{1}
with the same
R^{1}
and the same weight
\alpha
. Thus, the preference relation induced by extended counterfactual utility does not, in general, define a coherent vNM preference relation on the potential outcome space.
Moreover, projecting these preferences onto the realized outcome space, with either the menu-dependent or menu-independent projection, leads to the same respective vNM violations as discussed in Section
5
.

6.6
Extended Counterfactual Utilities under Additivity

We can recover coherence on the potential outcome space by imposing additivity on the extended utility. Assume that the extended utility takes the additive form,

\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}(d;p_{0},\ldots,p_{K-1})=\alpha_{d}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\beta_{d,k}\,p_{k},

for constants
\alpha_{d},\beta_{d,k}\in\mathbb{R}
. This can be viewed as a
natural extension of the additive counterfactual utility
\tilde{u}(d;\bm{y})=\alpha_{d}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\beta_{d,k}\,y_{k}
from Section
4.2
, though this
transformation is not one-to-one. We have
\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}(d;p_{0}(P),\ldots,p_{K-1}(P))=V_{P}(d;\tilde{u})
because additive
utilities also depend only on marginal distributions alone. Thus, as
shown earlier, the preference relation induced by
\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}
satisfies the vNM independence axiom.

We emphasize that this result does not contradict the conclusions of Sections
4.2
and
6.4
. As shown above, under binary decisions, additivity reduces the extended counterfactual utilities to standard utilities. However, when there are more than two decision categories, additive counterfactual utilities no longer reduce to standard utilities and are more expressive.

7
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we establish an axiomatic foundation for counterfactual statistical decision theory, which has recently gained popularity but attracted criticisms at the same time. These results imply that counterfactual statistical decision theory constitutes a coherent decision-making framework, which enables decision-makers to encode a variety of ethical and other subjective considerations.
Our axiomatic results also reconcile apparent inconsistencies and paradoxes that have been discussed in the literature.

Our analysis complements the broader literature on causal decision theory, which studies preference representations over interventions induced by structural causal models and utilities
(
20
, e.g.,)
. In contrast, we characterize preferences over policy–state pairs induced by counterfactual utilities within the given potential outcome framework. Our framework can nest common models in behavioral economics, such as regret theory
(
4
;
29
;
7
;
26
)
, while preserving the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms on the potential outcome space.

Finally, some scholars have contended that counterfactual utilities should never be used in decision-making and practitioners should use standard utilities instead
(
12
;
35
;
37
, e.g.,)
.
We argue that the role of statisticians is not to prescribe which
utility functions decision-makers ought to adopt, or on which outcome
space preferences should be defined. These choices embody the
subjective preferences and ethical (and other) judgments of
decision-makers. Rather, the statistician’s task is to identify optimal decisions and quantify the associated uncertainty under a given utility specification.

\tau(p_{0})\in(\underline{p}_{1},\overline{p}_{1})
such that
\Gamma\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}(p_{0},\tau(p_{0}))=0
.

Assumption
2
holds whenever outcomes are bounded, whereas Assumption
3
requires that a utility contrast is more likely to recommend a decision

when
p_{d}
is greater.
Lastly, Assumption
4
is a mild regularity condition. If the contrast can recommend each decision for some values of
(p_{0},p_{1})
, it must cross zero exactly once.

The following theorem shows that by switching the order of evaluation, for a given counterfactual utility under Assumptions
2
–
4
,, one can always find a standard utility with the same optimal policy recommendation for any state of nature
P
. In particular, the problem reduces to comparing
(possibly non-linear transformation of) marginal means, which does not respect the original asymmetric structure.

Theorem S2
(Equivalent standard utility)

Assume
\mathcal{D}=\{0,1\}
. Suppose that decisions are evaluated as proposed in
17
. Let
\tilde{u}
be a counterfactual utility and
\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}
be a chosen extension. Under Assumptions
2
–
4
, there exists an extended standard utility of the form
u^{\mathrm{Ext}}(d;p_{d})=\phi_{d}(p_{d})
that induces the same optimal decision as
\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}
. That is, for all state of natures
(p_{0},p_{1})\in I^{2}
,

\displaystyle\Gamma\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}(p_{0},p_{1})>0\iff\Gamma u^{\mathrm{Ext}}(p_{0},p_{1})>0,

and
\Gamma\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}(p_{0},p_{1})=0\iff\Gamma u^{\mathrm{Ext}}(p_{0},p_{1})=0
. Moreover, one may choose
\phi_{1}
to be the identity,
\phi_{1}(p)=p
, and
\phi_{0}
to be non-decreasing.

The proof is given in Appendix
S3.5
.
We illustrate this result with two examples.

Example 6
(Asymmetric Extension)

Assume
Y\in\{0,1\}
. Then any (non-extended) counterfactual utility contrast can be written as

\displaystyle\Gamma u(y_{0},y_{1})=u_{10}\,\mathds{1}{\left\{y_{0}>y_{1}\right\}}+u_{01}\,\mathds{1}{\left\{y_{0}<y_{1}\right\}}+u_{00}\,\mathds{1}{\left\{y_{0}=y_{1}=0\right\}}+u_{11}\,\mathds{1}{\left\{y_{0}=y_{1}=1\right\}}.

\displaystyle\Gamma\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}_{\mathrm{Asm}}(p_{0},p_{1})=\tilde{u}_{0}\,\mathds{1}{\left\{p_{0}>p_{1}\right\}}+\tilde{u}_{1}\,\mathds{1}{\left\{p_{0}<p_{1}\right\}}+\tilde{u}_{01}\,\mathds{1}{\left\{p_{0}=p_{1}\right\}}.

.
Assumption
2
is immediate. Assumptions

and

either require that

\tilde{u}_{0}<0<\tilde{u}_{1}

\tilde{u}_{01}=0

\tilde{u}_{01}=0

Example 7
(Product extension)

In the setting of Example

, the product extension of Section
6.3
yields the contrast

\displaystyle\Gamma\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}_{\mathrm{Prod}}(p_{0},p_{1})=\tilde{\lambda}+\tilde{\lambda}_{0}p_{0}+\tilde{\lambda}_{1}p_{1}+\tilde{\lambda}_{01}p_{0}p_{1}.

As discussed in Section

the contrast is equivalent to assuming Assumption

in the context of Section

If

\tilde{\lambda}_{01}\neq 0

3

4

\max\{0,-\tilde{\lambda}_{01}\}\leq\tilde{\lambda}_{1}

\phi_{0}(p_{0})=\begin{cases}-1,&\text{if }\tilde{\lambda}+\tilde{\lambda}_{0}p_{0}+\tilde{\lambda}_{1}p_{1}+\tilde{\lambda}_{01}p_{0}p_{1}>0\text{ for all }p_{1}\in[0,1],\\[6.0pt]

Lemma S2
(Additive Utilities)

Consider a utility function

Fix a baseline
\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}

(S3)

For each
k\in\mathcal{D}
and

, define the function

\displaystyle u_{k}(a):=\tilde{u}(y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,y_{k-1}^{\prime},a,y_{k+1}^{\prime},\ldots,y_{K-1}^{\prime})-\sum_{l\neq k}u_{l}(y_{l}^{\prime}).

(S4)

Now, fix any
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle P_{k}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\;\delta_{(y_{0},\ldots,y_{k-1},y_{k}^{\prime},\ldots,y_{K-1}^{\prime})}+\frac{1}{2}\;\delta_{(y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,y_{k-1}^{\prime},y_{k},y_{k+1}^{\prime},\ldots,y_{K-1}^{\prime})},

\displaystyle Q_{k}

\displaystyle\Gamma\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}(p_{0},p_{1})>0\iff\phi_{1}(p_{1})-\phi_{0}(p_{0})>0,\quad\Gamma\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}(p_{0},p_{1})=0\iff\phi_{1}(p_{1})-\phi_{0}(p_{0})=0.

Moreover, one can choose
\phi_{1}
to be the identity, i.e.,
\phi_{1}(p)=p
.

Proof.

For the ease of notation, we denote
\Gamma\tilde{u}^{\mathrm{Ext}}(p_{0},p_{1})
by
\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0},p_{1})
. Let
L<U
be the bounds from Assumption
2
.
Set
\phi_{1}(p_{1})=p_{1}
and define the function
\phi_{0}:I\to[L-1,U+1]
by

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(p_{0})=\begin{cases}L-1\ \ &\text{if }\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0},p_{1})>0\text{ for all }p_{1}\in I,\\
U+1\ \ &\text{if }\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0},p_{1})<0\text{ for all }p_{1}\in I,\\
\text{unique }\tau(p_{0})\in I\text{ such that }\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0},\tau(p_{0}))=0\ \ &\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that
\phi_{0}
is well defined by Assumptions
2
–
4
. Since
\phi_{1}
is non-decreasing, it remains to show that
\phi_{0}
is non-decreasing and that
\mathrm{sign}(\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0},p_{1}))=\mathrm{sign}(\phi_{1}(p_{1})-\phi_{0}(p_{0}))
.

We first show that
\phi_{0}
is non-decreasing. Take
p_{0}^{\prime}<p_{0}^{\prime\prime}
, we need to show that
\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime})\leq\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime})
. We consider the four cases.

1.

Suppose
\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime})=U+1
.
Then, by definition
\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0}^{\prime},p_{1})<0
for all
p_{1}\in I
. By Assumption
3
,
p_{0}\to\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0},p_{1})
is non-increasing, implying
\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime},p_{1})\leq\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0}^{\prime},p_{1})<0
. Hence,
\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime})=U+1
and thus
\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime})=\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime})
.

2.

Suppose
\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime})=L-1
.
Since
\phi_{0}(p_{0})\geq L-1
for all
p_{0}\in I
, it follows that
\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime})\leq\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime})
.

3.

Suppose
\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime})\in[L,U],\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime})\in\{L-1,U+1\}
.
If
\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime})=U+1
then trivially
\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime})\leq\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime})
. Now suppose
\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime})=L-1
, then by definition
\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime},p_{1})>0
for all
p_{1}\in I
. By Assumption
3
and
p_{0}^{\prime}<p_{0}^{\prime\prime}
, we must have
\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0}^{\prime},p_{1})\geq\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime},p_{1})>0
. Hence,
\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0}^{\prime},p_{1})>0
for all
p_{1}\in I
which implies
\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime})=L-1
, contradicting
\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime})\in[L,U]
.

4.

Suppose
\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime})
and
\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime})\in[L,U]
.
Note that since
\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime}),\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime})\in[L,U]
, we have
\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0}^{\prime},\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime}))=0
and
\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime},\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime}))=0
. Suppose for contradiction that
\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime})>\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime})
. Assumption
3
further implies

0=\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime},\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime}))\leq\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime},\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime}))\leq\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0}^{\prime},\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime}))=0

such that
\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime},\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime}))=0
. By Assumption
4
(a) this imposes
\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime})=\phi_{0}(p_{0}^{\prime\prime})
, yielding a contradiction.

This proves that
\phi_{0}
is non-decreasing.
Finally, we show that
\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0},p_{1})
and
\phi_{1}(p_{1})-\phi_{0}(p_{0})
have the same sign. Fix
p_{0}\in I
. We consider the following cases.

1.

Suppose
\phi_{0}(p_{0})=L-1
.
This directly implies
\phi_{1}(p_{1})-\phi_{0}(p_{0})=p_{1}-(L-1)>0
for all
p_{1}\in I
. Moreover, by construction of
\phi_{0}
, we must have
\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0},p_{1})>0
for all
p_{1}\in I
.

2.

Suppose
\phi_{0}(p_{0})=U+1
.
In this case
\phi_{1}(p_{1})-\phi_{0}(p_{0})=p_{1}-(U+1)<0
for all
p_{1}\in I
. As in the previous case, the construction of
\phi_{0}
implies
\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0},p_{1})<0
for all
p_{1}\in I
.

3.

Suppose
\phi_{0}(p_{0})\in[L,U]
.
By construction,
\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0},\phi_{0}(p_{0}))=0
. If
p_{1}<\phi_{0}(p_{0})
, then
\phi_{1}(p_{1})-\phi_{0}(p_{0})<0
. By Assumption
3
, we must have
\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0},p_{1})\leq\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0},\phi_{0}(p_{0}))=0
. If
\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0},p_{1})=0
, this would give a second root, which violates Assumption
4
. Hence,
\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0},p_{1})<0
. The same argument applies to the case
p_{1}>\phi_{0}(p_{0})
. If
p_{1}=\phi_{0}(p_{0})
, we trivially have
\phi_{1}(p_{1})-\phi_{0}(p_{0})=p_{1}-\phi_{0}(p_{0})=0
and
\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0},p_{1})=\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0},\phi_{0}(p_{0}))=0
.

We have shown that regardless of the value of
\phi_{0}(p_{0})
, the signs of
\Gamma\tilde{u}(p_{0},p_{1})
and
\phi_{1}(p_{1})-\phi_{0}(p_{0})
agree. This concludes the proof.
∎
