Executive Summary
Overview
This project focused on developing a high-fidelity numerical framework to simulate the multiphysics
environment within Liquid Argon Time Projection Chamber (LArTPC) detectors. The primary ob-
jective was to characterize the complex interplay between ion transport, background fluid dynamics,
and electric field distortions—a critical factor for the calibration and sensitivity of next-generation
High Energy Physics experiments, such as DUNE.

Technical Achievements
The research successfully yielded a hybrid numerical space-charge solver utilizing a Cell-Centered
Finite Volume Method (FVM) for ion transport coupled with a Finite Element Method (FEM) for
electric potential. Key accomplishments include:

 • Verification & Validation: The 3-D solver was rigorously verified against 1-D analytical solu-
 tions, demonstrating high numerical accuracy in predicting space-charge-induced field devia-
 tions.

 • Field Distortion Analysis: 3D simulations revealed that space charge eﬀects introduce signifi-
 cant non-uniformities in the electric field. Critically, the research identified that background
 LAr flow velocities, when comparable to ion drift velocities, markedly exacerbate these dis-
 tortions.

 • Technology Transfer: The resulting source code and comprehensive user manuals were suc-
 cessfully transferred to collaborators at Fermilab, providing a portable computational tool for
 the broader scientific community.

Challenges and Future Directions
While the space-charge solver achieved all performance metrics, the integrated fluid dynamics mod-
eling encountered convergence challenges stemming from the extreme 200-fold disparity in length
scales between the detector’s 37 mm inlet pipes and the 8-meter global domain. To address this,
the project has identified a clear technical pivot toward Hierarchical Geometric Adaptive Mesh
Refinement (HG-AMR).
 By implementing an h-type refinement strategy with hanging nodes, future iterations of this
solver will be capable of resolving localized high-gradient inlet flows without the prohibitive compu-
tational costs of regular grids. This advancement, combined with data-driven uncertainty quantifi-
cation based on MicroBooNE-style calibration, will enable the precise modeling of detector responses
in large-scale cryogenic environments where direct measurement remains diﬃcult.

Impact
The computational tools developed under this award provide a foundation for enhancing the energy
resolution and spatial reconstruction of noble liquid detectors. By bridging the gap between theo-
retical fluid dynamics and experimental field calibration, this work supports the DOE’s mission to
advance the frontiers of neutrino physics and dark matter detection.

1 Background/Introduction
The Deep Underground Neutrino Experiment (DUNE) [2, 5] will employ several particle detectors
in a beam of neutrinos produced at Fermi National Accelerator Laboratory in Batavia, IL. The
near detectors will be located on the Fermilab site, and the far detectors will be located at the
Sanford Underground Research Facility in Lead, SD. One of the the three primary purposes of the
experiment is to measure the properties of neutrinos as they travel 1300 km through the earth. They
are expected to oscillate from one flavor of neutrino to another and back as they travel. Measuring
the flavor transition probabilities will tell us about the masses, mixings, and charge-parity (CP)
symmetry violating properties of neutrinos. CP violation is a necessary ingredient to explain why
there is more matter in the universe than there is antimatter. The second primary purpose is to
detect neutrinos from supernova bursts within the Milky Way galaxy and nearby dwarf galaxies
such as the Magellanic Clouds. The third purpose is to search for nucleon decay, which is predicted
by some grand unified theories. Before building the large far detector modules, each of which will
contain 10 kt of fiducial mass of liquid argon, prototypes have been built and additional prototypes
will be built in order to test the design and the robustness of the components. Two large prototypes,
ProtoDUNE-SP and ProtoDUNE-DP [2] are located at CERN in a test beam.

1.1 Liquid Argon Time Projection Chamber
The primary particle detection technology chosen for the large far detector modules is the liquid
argon time projection chamber (LArTPC). For this reason, one of the three near detector technolo-
gies is also planned to be a LArTPC, so that similar interactions of neutrinos with the detectors
can cancel some systematic uncertainties in the comparison of the near and far detectors data. The
other two technologies for the near detector are a gaseous argon TPC and a 3D plastic scintillator
detector. Calorimetry and muon detection systems are also planned for the near detectors. This
proposal will focus on the LArTPCs. However, the computational tool developed in this project
will be applicable to other detectors as well.
 A LArTPC contains a volume of highly purified liquid argon in a strong electric field, provided by
a planar cathode and a planar anode. High-speed charged particles such as cosmic rays or particles
produced in neutrino interactions will ionize argon atoms along the particle trajectories. Some of
the electrons that are freed in the ionization drift in the electric field, scattering from the argon
atoms but they are not absorbed. Other electrons freed by ionization recombine with the positive
argon ions, producing flashes of scintillation light. If the argon is suﬃciently free of electronegative
impurities such as oxygen and water, then the drifting electrons will travel to the anode.
 Two anode planes are on the outside faces of the module, facing the cryostat, and one vertically
hung cathode plane is in the middle. The distance between the cathode plane and the anode plane
is 3.6 m in both the far detector and in ProtoDUNE-SP. The nominal electric field strength is 500
V/cm. The potential on the cathode is -180 kV, and the second induction plane of the APA is
grounded. A field cage consisting of an array of metal profiles that are held at electrical potentials
that linearly vary with their position between the cathode and the anode shape the field, providing
as close to a uniform field as possible within the volume of liquid argon (cf. Fig. 1). Ten photon
detectors are located between the grounded mesh planes inside each APA frame.
 The anode plane is made up of charge-sensing elements. In a single-phase LArTPC, it consists
of four planes of wires – a grid plane, two induction planes (U and V), and a collection plane (X).
A grounded mesh lies behind the collection plane. The potentials are set on the wire planes so
that charge drifts past the grid wires and the wires in the two induction planes but collects on
the collection plane. Sensitive amplifiers and digitizers are attached to each induction-plane wire

 The general operating principle of the single phase LArTPC is as follows. Negatively charged
ionization electrons from the neutrino interaction drift horizontally opposite to the electric field in
the LAr and are collected on the anode. Photon detectors will provide the t0 of the interaction. The
drifting charges induce bipolar signals on the U and V wires and unipolar signals on the X wires
(cf. Fig. 2). The arrival time of the drifting charge, together with knowledge of the drift velocity of
the electrons in the applied field, allows the determination of the distance from the ionization point
to the anode, provided that the time of the original ionization is known. This time can be known
either from the timing of the beam pulse from Fermilab, or, in the case of natural sources such as
comic rays, the time of the scintillation light flashes.

1.2 The Eﬀect of Space Charge
For LArTPCs on the surface, such as ProtoDUNE-SP, cosmic rays constantly impinge on the detec-
tor. While the drifting electrons are quickly swept away by the applied electric field, the remaining
positively charged argon ions drift very slowly, on the order of 1 cm/sec. This speed is comparable
to the fluid flow speed. Positive ions therefore accumulate in the active volume of the LArTPC, and
cause distortions of the electric potential from its nominal form (see Fig. 3) [11]. Negatively-charged
ions created when electrons attach to impurities (oxygen or water molecules) also are expected to
drift slowly. The resulting distortions in the electric field cause drifting electrons to arrive at diﬀer-
ent places and at diﬀerent times on the anode plane than they would had there been no distortions.
The distorted electric field also changes the fraction of electrons that recombine with the argon ions.
Measurements of the positions of track endpoints can help constrain space-charge distortion eﬀects
[4].

1.3 Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) Simulation of the LAr Flow Field
Since ions move with a speed comparable to the speed of the bulk fluid, the motion of ions is
aﬀected by not only the electric field but also the flow field. Therefore, we need to first solve
the LAr flow equations to obtain the accurate fluid velocity distribution. Various commercial
Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) packages including ANSYS CFX and Star-CCM+ have
been used to calculate the flow motion [18, 10, 9] with various levels of success.
 In this project, we tried to use our in-house incompressible flow solver for the flow field simula-
tion. Since we have full control of the CFD code, we are able to make any modifications as needed
to suit the needs of LArTPC flow simulation. Our CFD solver is based on the pressure correction
or projection method on staggered meshes using a hybrid finite volume/element method [14, 16]. In
our nontraditional staggered-grid scheme, the velocity variables are placed at the cell centers while
a pressure-related auxiliary variable is placed at the mesh vertices. A fractional step approach is

used to obtain an intermediate velocity field by solving the original momentum equations with a
matrix-free implicit cell-centered finite volume method. The pressure Poisson equation derived from
the fractional step approach is solved by the node-based continuous Galerkin finite element method
for the auxiliary variable. The auxiliary variable is used to correct the velocity field and the pressure
field. The one-equation Spalart-Allmaras Detached Eddy Simulation (SA-DES) turbulence model is
employed to provide the eddy viscosity if the flow is turbulent. The turbulence equation is solved by
the same finite volume method as the one for the momentum equation. Our numerical solver will be
parallelized using the Message Passing Interface (MPI) library. Since our cell-centered finite volume
and continuous finite element solvers are constructed on compact computational stencils, the inter-
processor communication involves only nodes, faces and elements on the partition boundaries. This
compactness makes it trivial to attain high parallelizability using MPI for fixed-topology meshes.
Very eﬃcient non-blocking MPI functions can be called to set up the inter-processor “scatter” and
“gather” routines in the pre-processing stage [7, 15].

1.4 Preliminary 2D Work
In the summer of 2021, the PI participated in the DOE’s Visiting Faculty Program (VFP) hosted
at Fermilab. This work was aimed to help answer the question about the space charge eﬀect
on the detector electric field. In this 10-week period, we modified our CFD code to implement
several new capabilities needed for the simulation of the ProtoDUNE-SP LArTPC flow field. One
is the Boussinesq thermal model for simulating buoyancy driven convection flows, the second is the
capability to handle porous material within the flow field, and the last is the capability to handle
the spatially varying heat flux boundary conditions along specified boundaries. Due to the limited
time, we were only able to test our code on a simplified two-dimensional geometry (cf. Fig. 4)
to verify the capabilities of our solver. Numerical tests (cf. Fig. 5) demonstrated that our CFD
solver is able to produce qualitatively meaningful velocity and temperature distributions within the
detector. Though the results shown are 2D only, the computational domain contains all types of
structures seen in the true 3D model. We were pretty confident we had built a firm foundation for
future 3D simulations.

2.2.1 Nondimensionalization of Equations
The actual values of the physical quantities in Eqs. (1) and (5) vary a lot in magnitude in SI
units. It is desirable to use an appropriate set of reference values to nondimensionalize and scale
the dimensional values such that all dimensionless values are within similar magnitude range close
to 1. Independent primary reference values are as follows:

 • ⇢ref : reference charge density.

 • Lref : reference length.

 • uref : reference velocity.

 • Vref : reference electric potential.

Other derived reference values include

 • tref = Lref /uref : reference time scale,

 • Eref = Vref /Lref : reference electric field,

 • µref = uref /Eref : reference mobility,

 • Dref = Lref uref : reference diﬀusivity,

 • Sref = ⇢ref uref /Lref : reference source term and

 • "ref = ⇢ref L2ref /Vref : reference permittivity.

It can be easily shown that the resulting dimensionless equations are mathematically identical to
the original ones.

2.2.4 Parallelization of the Numerical Solver
To solve large scale problems, the current numerical solver is parallelized using the Message Passing
Interface (MPI) library to access multiple processors simultaneously and speed up computation.
The choice of the MPI parallel paradigm is due to its standardization, excellent platform inde-
pendent portability and flexibility on both distributed memory and shared memory machines. The
parallelism is achieved via the Single Program Multiple Data (SPMD) principle. The computational
mesh is first partitioned across certain number of processes using the ParMETIS library [8] (cf. Fig.
7). The partitioning ensures the number of elements is roughly the same on each of the processes for
the load balancing purpose. In addition, ParMETIS also minimizes the inter-process communica-
tion overhead. The same numerical solver program is then executed on each of the processes on its
portion of the mesh simultaneously. Inter-process communication occurs to synchronize the compu-
tation. Since the current cell-centered finite volume and nodal finite element solvers are constructed
on compact computational stencils, the inter-processor communication involves only nodes, faces
and elements on the partition boundaries (cf. Fig. 8). This compactness makes it trivial to attain
high parallelizability using MPI for fixed-topology meshes. Very eﬃcient non-blocking MPI func-
tions can be called to set up the inter-processor “gather” and “scatter” routines in the pre-processing
stage [7, 15]. The communication overhead has been minimized thanks to these routines.

3.1 Geometric Modeling and Mesh Generation
The original CAD model of the protoDUNE-SP geometry, provided by FermiLab, is shown in
Fig. 19 (Left). The internal architecture of the detector is characterized by a high density of
complex components, including Anode Plane Assemblies (APA), field cage walls, ground plates, and
electronic housings. Resolving these features directly within the mesh would result in a prohibitively
large cell count, exceeding current computational resource limits.
 Consequently, these structures are modeled as embedded porous media, following established
methodologies in the field [10, 18]. This approach represents the physical obstruction of the flow

This reduction in the number of discrete porous regions significantly streamlines the simulation
setup and improves the stability of the pressure-velocity coupling.

3.3 Numerical Tests and Discussions
We ran the simulation hoping to obtain the realistic velocity field that can be fed into our space-
charge solver. The primary objective of the fluid dynamics phase was to simulate the evolution of
the velocity, pressure, and temperature fields within the LArTPC detector volume. However, the
simulation encountered significant numerical instability, specifically characterized by poor conver-
gence of the nonlinear iterations within each temporal step. Consequently, the resulting numerical
solutions could not be validated to the degree of precision required for high-fidelity physics anal-
ysis.Extensive sensitivity studies and solver tuning were performed to mitigate these instabilities.
We hypothesize that the lack of convergence originates from the extreme separation of length scales
between the inlet geometry and the global computational domain. Specifically, the driving force of
the interior flow is governed by inlet pipes with a diameter of 37 mm, while the primary domain
spans a volume of approximately 8m3 . This 200-fold diﬀerence in scale imposes a severe constraint
on the numerical stiﬀness of the system. While the flow solver utilizes an implicit time integration
scheme—theoretically allowing for larger CFL numbers—the geometric disparity necessitated a pro-
hibitively small time step to resolve the high-gradient gradients at the inlets. Despite these eﬀorts,
the computational overhead required to achieve full convergence remained beyond the feasible limits
of the current project’s performance period.
 To overcome the multi-scale constraints identified in Objective 1, future iterations of this research
should implement Hierarchical Geometric Adaptive Mesh Refinement (HG-AMR). This approach
utilizes a nested hierarchy of grid levels, allowing for localized refinement with hanging nodes at
the 37 mm inlet boundaries while maintaining a coarse base mesh throughout the 8-meter detector
volume.
 By employing a hierarchical structure, the solver can dynamically manage the resolution ratio
between the driving inlet flow and the bulk fluid, significantly improving the condition number of
the nonlinear system. This targeted refinement strategy is expected to mitigate the numerical stiﬀ-
ness encountered in the current performance period, enabling stable convergence and high-fidelity
temporal tracking of LArTPC thermal evolution. Ensuring flux conservation across the hierarchical

interfaces will be critical for maintaining the physical integrity of the mass and momentum transport
equations.

4 Conclusion/Future Work
4.1 Conclusion and Summary of Accomplishments
In this project, a multiphysics hybrid numerical framework was successfully developed to simulate
the complex interaction between space charge distributions and electric field distortions in Liquid
Argon Time Projection Chamber (LArTPC) detectors. The solver employs a cell-centered Finite
Volume Method (FVM) to resolve the ion transport equations, coupled with a Finite Element
Method (FEM) to solve the electric potential equation. This framework enables the high-fidelity
simulation of the iterative coupling between charge density and field strength within each temporal
step until a steady-state equilibrium is achieved.
 The 3-D solver was rigorously verified against 1-D analytical solutions, confirming its numeri-
cal accuracy. Subsequent 3-D testing demonstrated that space charge eﬀects introduce significant
non-uniformity into the electric field, deviating from the idealized design. Crucially, the research
highlighted that when background fluid flow magnitudes are comparable to ion drift velocities,
field distortions become markedly more pronounced. This integrated solver now serves as a robust
computational tool for investigating space charge dynamics across a wide range of noble liquid or
gaseous detector geometries.

4.2 Uncertainty Quantification and Impact
The fidelity of these simulations is intrinsically linked to the precision of input parameters, including
source term magnitudes, recombination rates, and ion mobilities for Ar+ and electronegative con-
taminants O2 and H2 O. Furthermore, the background flow field—governed by thermal gradients,
LAr flow rates, and detector element porosities—introduces additional variables.Future iterations
of this model will employ a data-driven calibration procedure, similar to the methodology utilized
by MicroBooNE [11], to maximize agreement between drifting electron data and model predic-
tions. The resulting reduced uncertainties will be critical for predicting space charge impacts in
environments where direct measurement is challenging, such as the DUNE Far Detector.

4.3 Future Work: Hierarchical AMR and Multiphysics Coupling
A primary objective for future development is the integration of Hierarchical Geometric Adaptive
Mesh Refinement (HG-AMR) with support for hanging nodes into the in-house flow solver. This
h-type refinement capability is essential for eﬃciently resolving the multi-scale disparity between
the 37 mm inlet geometries and the 8-meter global computational domain. By dynamically allo-
cating mesh density, the solver will be able to produce realistic, converged flow fields without the
prohibitive computational overhead currently imposed by regular unstructured grids. Additionally,
future research will investigate the coupled convergence of the incompressible Navier-Stokes, ther-
mal conduction, and turbulence transport equations. Once these realistic flow fields are available,
they will be integrated into the space charge solver to provide a fully coupled, calibrated model of
detector response.
