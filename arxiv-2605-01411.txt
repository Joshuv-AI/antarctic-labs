Quantum jump trajectories, hybrid systems, non-Hermitian evolutions, quantum/classical walks
Alberto Barchielli

Abstract
Stochastic differential equations have been used to give the evolution of quantum open system and to formulate a theory of quantum measurements in continuous time; often the name of quantum trajectory theory is used. Here we develop the general theory of stochastic master equations of jump type, when also memory is involved. The quantum system together to the counting processes involved in the stochastic master equation constitute an hybrid quantum/classical system. The construction of the physical probability law is given; it turns out to depend on the full dynamics. The role of conditional states and mean states is discussed. By introducing the notion of “typical trajectory”, we show how to recursively construct the solution of the non-linear stochastic master equation (the conditional state). Moreover, by the notion of “exclusive probability densities” we can describe all the probabilities related to the jumps, in particular, the waiting times of the jumps and their probability distributions. This general formulation and the idea of hybrid system allow to unify and generalize different fields: evolutions under non-Hermitian Hamiltonians, piecewise dynamics, various types of quantum/classical random walks. Non-Hermitian effective Hamiltonians naturally appear in the time evolution in between two jumps; they give in a consistent way the quantum evolution and the probability distribution of the associated survival time. We show how we can have a large variety of probability densities for the survival time when we are at an “exceptional point” or near to it. Models given by a unitary dynamics interrupted at random times by a quantum channel have been introduced in solid state physics and statistical mechanics to describe dissipative evolutions; these models have been also used to give non-Markovian evolutions for quantum open systems. Here we show how all these models can have a dynamical formulation by means of suitable jump stochastic master equations and how to use the exclusive probability densities in this context. Finally, we restrict our approach to the case in which the dynamics of the full hybrid system is Markovian, but not the evolution of the quantum component alone. In this way we include other classes of models which were proposed as examples of evolutions with memory, such as the “Lindblad rate equation”. By restricting the possible values of the classical component to the vertices of a graph, we include also the so called “continuous time open quantum walks”. Again, in the formulation by means of stochastic master equations, also the probability distributions of the waiting times of the walk are included. A two-dimensional example is discussed to show the variety of possible behaviours.



Keywords
: stochastic master equations, hybrid dynamics, quantum counting processes, non-Hermitian Hamiltonian,
continuous time open quantum walks, non-Markovian quantum open systems, quantum renewal processes, Lidblad rate equation, exclusive probability densities

2.5
The classical component

The dynamical system described by the SMEs (
7
), (
16
) can be seen as an hybrid quantum/classical system because classical stochastic processes are involved, whose probability law depends on the quantum dynamics itself. These processes are classical, which implies that they can be observed without perturbing their dynamics. However, some of these stochastic processes could represent pure noise, not observed nor controlled by the experimenter. Various choices of the
classical observables
are possible, depending on the physical situation.

A first choice of observables is to take discrete counting processes. We could take for
\mathscr{U}
a discrete space or to consider as monitored output the processes

N_{k}(t)=\Pi(A_{k};(0,t]),\qquad A_{k}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{U}),\qquad A_{k}\cap A_{l}=\emptyset\ \ \text{for}\ \ k\neq l.

The typical application in quantum optics is direct detection;
N_{k}(t)
can represent the counts at a photon detector and the associated jump operator describes the emission process due to the jump between two discrete levels
[
46
]
. The observed output could be also a smoothed version of these counting processes. For instance, we could have detection with time delay and smoothing; in this case, the output
X(t)
is

X_{j}(t)=\sum_{k}\int_{(0,t]}a_{jk}(t-s)\,\mathrm{d}N_{k}(s)+e_{j}(t).

(17)

The terms
e_{j}(t)
represent additive extra-noise due to the detection apparatus; the quantities
a_{jk}(t-s)
are non-random and represent the detector response functions. A dependence on
X(s)
,
s<t
, can be introduced in the Hamiltonian
H(t)
or in other operators appearing in
\mathcal{L}(t)
. In this way we can realize feedback and closed loop control
[
34
,
14
,
19
,
40
]
. By controlling the quantum system we have also a (partial) control of the properties of the output
X(t)
, for instance the squeezing in the output spectrum. Also the statistical properties of the counting processes
N_{k}(t)
can be influenced, for instance by going from super-Poissonian to sub-Poissonian statistics
[
34
,
40
,
97
]
.

Another example of observables based on the counting processes
N_{k}
is given in
[
19
,
40
]
. They consider linear combinations, such as
\sum_{k}c_{k}N_{k}(t)
, which are used to represent entropies and other thermodynamical quantities. Also feedback is explicitly introduced by taking the Hamiltonian dependent on the last jump.

Similarly to the case of discrete levels, we could use a continuous counting measure
\Pi
to describe emission of photons by jumps involving energy bands. In any case, as observed classical component, we could take also the full counting measure
\Pi
, whose trajectories are described in Sec.
3
, where also possible applications are presented.

The classical component can have also its own dynamics, as in the Markovian case studied in
[
32
]
.
The classical component of the hybrid system is the stochastic process
X(t)
taking values in the phase space
\mathbb{R}^{s}
and satisfying the stochastic differential equation (restricted to the purely jump case)
[
32
, (2)]

\mathrm{d}X_{i}(t)=c_{i}\big(X(t_{-})\big)\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\mathscr{U}}g_{i}\big(X(t_{-}),u\big)\Pi(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}t),\qquad i=1,\ldots,s.

(18)

Also
H(t),\;L_{k}(t),\;J_{j}(u,t)
are taken to depend on the past only through
X(t_{-})
. The dynamics of the full quantum/classical system can be represented by a semigroup on a suitable hybrid state space and this gives the “Markovian” character of the dynamics
[
31
,
32
]
. A class of Markovian hybrid evolutions is developed in Sec.
6
.

In this presentation we have introduced a quantum system coupled to classical processes and classical observables; however, also approaches using only coupled quantum systems are possible. The quantum theory of measurements in continuous time can be formulated by representing the classical observables by commuting operators of a Bose field, used as “ancilla”
[
1
,
11
,
9
,
47
]
. Similar structures are obtained by starting with measurements at discrete times and, then, by taking the continuous limit, inside the formalism of “repeated interactions” or “collision models”
[
98
,
99
,
39
]
.

2.5.1
Connection with instruments and positive operator valued measures

The equation (
16
) for the conditional states is non linear, but this non-linearity is due only to the normalization in the definition of
\rho(t)
(
9
); underlying this non-linear equation there is the linear SME (
6
). This allows to show that the linear structure of quantum mechanics is respected and that probabilities and conditional states can be obtained from the standard notions of quantum measurement theory:
positive operator valued measures
(POVM) and
instruments

[
9
,
14
,
15
,
13
]
.

The map
\rho_{0}\mapsto\sigma(t)\in\mathscr{T}(\mathscr{H})
is linear in
\rho_{0}
and positive (Proposition
1
); so, it can be extended to the whole trace class. By writing
\sigma(t;\rho)
for the solution of (
7
) with initial condition
\rho\in\mathscr{T}(\mathscr{H})
, we can define the propagator of the linear SME by

\Sigma(t):\mathscr{T}(\mathscr{H})\to\mathscr{T}(\mathscr{H}),\qquad\Sigma(t)[\rho]:=\sigma(t;\rho).

(19)

This map is linear and completely positive (CP); complete positivity follows from the form of the involved operators: Lindblad-type generators and CP jump operators. By taking the mean and the trace of the SME (
6
) and by using linearity, we get the trace preserving property

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\operatorname{Tr}\left\{\Sigma(t)[\rho]\right\}\big]=\operatorname{Tr}\{\rho\},\qquad\forall\rho\geq 0,\quad\rho\in\mathscr{T}(\mathscr{H}).

As discussed in the first part of Sec.
2.5
some of the involved stochastic processes are pure noise and only some relevant classical observables are monitored. Then, we can consider the filtration
\mathscr{E}_{t}
,
t\geq 0
, generated by the monitored processes. We have
\mathscr{E}_{t}\subset\mathscr{F}_{t}
; the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathscr{F}_{t}
, containing the events up to time
t
, has been introduced in Assumption
1
.
For every
t>0
, we can now define an
instrument

\mathcal{I}_{t}
on the measurable space
(\Omega,\mathscr{E}_{t})
. For every
\mathsf{E}\in\mathscr{E}_{t}
, we define the map on
\mathscr{T}(\mathscr{H})
by

\forall a\in\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H}),\quad\forall\rho\in\mathscr{T}(\mathscr{H}),\qquad\operatorname{Tr}\left\{a\mathcal{I}_{t}(\mathsf{E})[\rho]\right\}:=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\mathtt{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\operatorname{Tr}\left\{a\Sigma(t)[\rho]\right\}\big].

(20)

Then,
\mathcal{I}_{t}
turns out to be an instrument, which means that the following properties hold

1.

\forall\mathsf{E}\in\mathscr{E}_{t}
,
\mathcal{I}_{t}(\mathsf{E})
is a CP, linear map on
\mathscr{T}(\mathscr{H})
and it is trace decreasing, i.e.
\operatorname{Tr}\big\{\mathcal{I}_{t}(\mathsf{E})[\rho]\big\}\leq\operatorname{Tr}\{\rho\}
,
\forall\rho\geq 0
.

2.

(normalization)
\operatorname{Tr}\big\{\mathcal{I}_{t}(\Omega)[\rho]\big\}=\operatorname{Tr}\{\rho\}
.

3.

(
\sigma
-additivity) For every countable family
\{\mathsf{E}_{i}\}
of disjoint sets in
\mathscr{E}_{t}

\mathcal{I}_{t}\Big(\bigcup_{i}\mathsf{E}_{i}\Big)[\rho]=\sum_{i}\mathcal{I}_{t}(\mathsf{E}_{i})[\rho].

These properties follow easily from the properties of the map
\Sigma(t)
and of the indicator function
\mathtt{1}_{\mathsf{E}}
. Given the instrument and the pre-measurement state
\rho\in\mathscr{S}(\mathscr{H})
, the probability of the result
\mathsf{E}
is
\operatorname{Tr}\{\mathcal{I}_{t}(\mathsf{E})[\rho]\}
and the post-measurement state, knowing that the result is in
\mathsf{E}
, is given by the mapping
\mathcal{I}_{t}(\mathsf{E})
and normalization:

\rho(t|\mathsf{E}):=\frac{\mathcal{I}_{t}(\mathsf{E})[\rho]}{\operatorname{Tr}\{\mathcal{I}_{t}(\mathsf{E})[\rho]\}},\qquad\mathsf{E}\in\mathscr{E}_{t}.

(21)

When the result of the observation is not known or not taken into account the post-measurement state reduces to
\mathcal{I}_{t}(\Omega)[\rho]
. Finally, the operator valued function
\mathsf{E}\mapsto\mathcal{I}_{t}(\mathsf{E})^{*}[\mathds{1}]\in\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})
is the POVM associated with the instrument
\mathcal{I}_{t}
and represents a generalized quantum observable.

Note that the probabilities given by this family of instruments coincide with the physical probability introduced in Sec.
2.3
. Now the initial state at time zero is
\rho_{0}
fixed in (
6
). Then, it is easy to see that the physical probability, defined by (
8
) and (
11
), and the mean state
\eta(t)
(
14
) can be expressed as

\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{E})=\operatorname{Tr}\big\{\mathcal{I}_{t}(\mathsf{E})^{*}[\mathds{1}]\rho_{0}\big\},\quad\forall\mathsf{E}\in\mathscr{E}_{t},\qquad\eta(t)=\mathcal{I}_{t}(\Omega)[\rho_{0}].

When the set
\mathsf{E}
in (
21
) practically shrinks to a single trajectory of the relevant observables, from the definition of the instruments (
20
) and the related construction of post-measurement states, we get the
\mathscr{E}
-conditional states

\rho(t|\mathscr{E}_{t})=\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\Sigma(t)[\rho_{0}]\big|\mathscr{E}_{t}\big]}{\operatorname{Tr}\big\{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\Sigma(t)[\rho_{0}]\big|\mathscr{E}_{t}\big]\big\}}.

Apart from simple cases, it is not possible to find an explicit expression for the conditional mean defined above and a closed evolution equation for the
\mathscr{E}
-conditional states. We can say that we are in a case of “partial observation”. When
\mathscr{E}_{t}=\mathscr{F}_{t}
,
\forall t\geq 0
, the states
\rho(t|\mathscr{E}_{t})
coincides with the conditional states introduced in the definition (
9
).

2.6
Non-uniqueness of the construction

The physical law of the point process
\Pi(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}t)
is determined by its compensator
I(u,t)\nu_{t}(\mathrm{d}u)\mathrm{d}t
(Remark
3
); only the product counts. We allowed the measure
\nu_{t}
and the operators involved in the definition of
I(u,t)
to be random in order to include some extra randomness, but this randomness could be shifted from one ingredient to the other without changing the counting process. So, we can have an equivalent construction by making the replacements

\Pi(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}t)\to\Pi^{\prime}(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}t),\qquad\nu_{t}(\mathrm{d}u)\to\nu^{\prime}_{t}(\mathrm{d}u),\qquad\mathcal{J}(u,t)\to\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(u,t),

with the constraint

\mathcal{J}(u,t)\nu_{t}(\mathrm{d}u)=\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(u,t)\nu^{\prime}_{t}(\mathrm{d}u);

(22)

no other element is changed, so that
\mathcal{L}(t)
remains unchanged. Then, under the physical probability, the stochastic intensities (
12
) and (
13
) do not change and
\Pi
and
\Pi^{\prime}
have the same law. Moreover, no change appears in the non-linear SME (
16
).

As only the product (
22
) is relevant, we can use this freedom to simplify the choice of the
\mathbb{Q}
-probability law of the point measure
\Pi(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}t)
and we take the following assumption, which turns out to be only a mild restriction.

Assumption 4
.

We take the measure
\nu_{t}(\mathrm{d}u)
to be deterministic and independent of time:
\nu_{t}(A)\to\nu(A)
,
\forall A\in\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{U})
. We take also
\nu(\mathscr{U})>0
.

All the other properties contained in Assumption
2
continue to hold. Under this Assumption, the point measure
\Pi(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}t)
turns out to be, under
\mathbb{Q}
, a time homogeneous, Poisson point process; in particular, the process
N(t)
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-Poisson process of intensity
\nu(\mathscr{U})
. If we want to change the distribution of the counting process, the needed random contribution are included into the definition of the jump operators
J(u,t)
as done in Sec.
5
.

3
Typical trajectories

The times of jumps are determined by the process
N(t)
(
2
), the sum of all the counting processes represented by
\Pi
(Assumption
2
). Under
\mathbb{Q}
,
N(t)
is a Poisson process (Assumption
4
), while, under
\mathbb{P}
, it is a counting process of stochastic intensity
\lambda(t)
(
13
). Heuristically, the expressions

\mathbb{Q}[\mathrm{d}N(t)=1|\mathscr{F}_{t_{-}}]=\nu(\mathscr{U})\mathrm{d}t,\qquad\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{d}N(t)=1|\mathscr{F}_{t_{-}}]=\lambda(t)\mathrm{d}t

(23)

represent the conditional probabilities of a jump in an infinitesimal time interval, given the whole past. Under
\mathbb{P}
,
when
\lambda(t)=0
, no jump is possible at time
t
.

Then, the type of jump is determined by the full point measure
\Pi
. If there is a jump at time
t
, the jump is of type
u\in A
,
A\subset\mathscr{U}
, with probabilities

\mathbb{Q}\big[\Delta\Pi(A;t)=1\big|\Delta N(t)=1,\mathscr{F}_{t_{-}}\big]=\frac{\nu(A)}{\nu(\mathscr{U})},

(24a)

\mathbb{P}\big[\Delta\Pi(A;t)=1\big|\Delta N(t)=1,\mathscr{F}_{t_{-}}\big]=\int_{A}\frac{I(u,t)}{\lambda(t)}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}u),

(24b)

where

\Delta N(t):=N(t_{+})-N(t_{-}),\qquad\Delta\Pi(A,t):=\Pi(A,[t_{-},t_{+}]).

(24c)

So, under
\mathbb{P}
, the conditional probabilities of the type of jump depend on the ratio
\frac{I(u,t)}{\lambda(t)}
; if
I(u,t)=0
, but
\lambda(t)>0
, we can have a jump, but not of type
u
.

Due to the assumptions of a finite measure
\nu(\mathscr{U})
(
1
) and of the existence of a finite operator
R(t)
(
3
), the probabilities of next jump (
23
) have a smooth behaviour in time. Indeed, these assumptions eliminate the possibility of having “infinitely many small jumps”, a typical possibility in the theory of Lévy processes
[
92
]
, which has its counterpart in the theory of measurements in continuous time
[
100
,
101
]
. Then,
a typical trajectory in
[0,T)
of the point process
\Pi
is

\tau(t;m)\equiv\{(u_{j},t_{j}),\;j=1,2,\ldots,m,\;u_{j}\in\mathscr{U},\;0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{m}<t\},\quad m\in\mathbb{N};

(25)

the case
m=0
means that there is no jump in
[0,t)
.

3.1
The solution of the SME

The non-linear SME (
16
) says that at a jump
(u_{j},t_{j})
the quantum state is transformed according to

\rho(t_{j-})\mapsto\rho(t_{j})=I(u_{j},t_{j})^{-1}\mathcal{J}(u_{j},t_{j})[\rho(t_{j-})],\qquad I(u_{j},t_{j})=\operatorname{Tr}\left\{\mathcal{J}(u_{j},t_{j})[\rho(t_{j-})]\right\},

(26)

where
\mathcal{J}(u,t)
was introduced in (
4a
) and
I(u,t)
in (
8
). Instead, in between two jumps the quantum state evolves according to the (non linear, CP preserving, trace preserving) equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathcal{L}_{0}(t)\big[\rho(t)\big]-\frac{1}{2}\left\{R(t)-\lambda(t)\mathds{1},\,\rho(t)\right\}.

(27)

In (
27
), the presence of
\lambda(t)
(
13
) gives the trace preservation property.

In an analogous way we have that the linear SME (
7
) gives

\sigma(t_{j-})\mapsto\sigma(t_{j})=\mathcal{J}(u_{j},t_{j})[\sigma(t_{j-})]

(28)

at a jump, and

\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathcal{L}_{0}(t)\big[\sigma(t)\big]-\frac{1}{2}\left\{R(t)-\nu(\mathscr{U})\mathds{1},\,\sigma(t)\right\}

(29)

in between two jumps. By (
8
) and (
9
) the quantities
\rho(t)
and
\sigma(t)
differ only by the normalization.

The operators
\mathcal{L}_{0}(t)
and
R(t)
are defined in (
4b
) and (
3
); they can depend on the trajectory (
25
) up to time
t
and eventually on some extra noise. Equations (
27
) and (
29
) hold in between two jumps; so,
\mathcal{L}_{0}(t)
and
R(t)
must to be computed under the condition of no jump in the considered time interval. To construct the solution of (
4b
) and (
3
) we introduce an explicit notation.

Definition 1

(No jump propagator)
.

Let us denote by
\mathcal{L}_{0}(t|t_{0})
and
R(t|t_{0})
the operators
\mathcal{L}_{0}(t)
and
R(t)
given under the condition of no jump in
(t_{0},t)
:
N(t)-N(t_{0})=0
.
Then, we define the (random)
no jump propagator

\mathcal{S}(t,t_{0})
by

\frac{\mathrm{d}\ }{\mathrm{d}t}\,\mathcal{S}(t,t_{0})[\rho]=\mathcal{A}(t|t_{0})\circ\mathcal{S}(t,t_{0})[\rho],

(30a)

\mathcal{A}(t|t_{0})[\rho]=\mathcal{L}_{0}(t|t_{0})[\rho]-\frac{1}{2}\left\{R(t|t_{0}),\rho\right\},\qquad\mathcal{S}(t_{0},t_{0})=\mathrm{Id}.

(30b)

When there is no jump in
(t_{0},t)
, from Eqs. (
27
), (
29
) we get

\rho(t_{-})=\exp\left\{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\lambda(s)\mathrm{d}s\right\}\mathcal{S}(t,t_{0})\big[\rho(t_{0})\big],\qquad\sigma(t_{-})=\exp\left\{\nu(\mathscr{U})\left(t-t_{0}\right)\right\}\mathcal{S}(t,t_{0})\big[\sigma(t_{0})\big].

By these results, the solution of the linear SME (
6
), in the typical trajectory
\tau(t,m)
, can be written in the following form:

\sigma(t_{-})=\exp\left\{\nu(\mathscr{U})t\right\}\mathcal{S}(t,0)[\rho_{0}],\qquad\text{for}\ m=0,

(31a)

\sigma(t_{-})=\exp\left\{\nu(\mathscr{U})t\right\}\mathcal{S}(t,t_{m})\circ\mathcal{J}(u_{m},t_{m})\circ\mathcal{S}(t_{m},t_{m-1})\circ\\
{}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{J}(u_{2},t_{2})\circ\mathcal{S}(t_{2},t_{1})\circ\mathcal{J}(u_{1},t_{1})\circ\mathcal{S}(t_{1},0)[\rho_{0}],\qquad\text{for}\ m\geq 1.

(31b)

Analogously, the solution of the non-linear SME (
16
) takes the expression

\rho(t_{-})=\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(s)\mathrm{d}s\right\}\mathcal{S}(t,0)\big[\rho_{0}\big]=\frac{\mathcal{S}(t,0)\big[\rho_{0}\big]}{\operatorname{Tr}\left\{\mathcal{S}(t,0)\big[\rho_{0}\big]\right\}},\qquad\text{for}\ m=0,

(32a)

\rho(t_{-})=\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(s)\mathrm{d}s\right\}\mathcal{S}(t,t_{m})\circ\frac{\mathcal{J}(u_{m},t_{m})}{I(u_{m},t_{m})}\circ\mathcal{S}(t_{m},t_{m-1})\circ\\
{}\circ\cdots\circ\frac{\mathcal{J}(u_{2},t_{2})}{I(u_{2},t_{2})}\circ\mathcal{S}(t_{2},t_{1})\circ\frac{\mathcal{J}(u_{1},t_{1})}{I(u_{1},t_{1})}\circ\mathcal{S}(t_{1},0)[\rho_{0}],\qquad\text{for}\ m\geq 1.

(32b)

Equations (
32
) give the structure of the statistical operator
\rho(t)
, the state of the system given the whole past. In spite of the simple recursive aspect, quantum jump trajectories can show very complex behaviours when applied to problems in quantum statistical mechanics
[
102
,
103
,
104
,
105
]
.

3.2
No jump probability and waiting times

Let us consider now the probabilities of no jump in a given time interval. Under the reference probability,
N
is a Poisson process and the no-jump probability has the simple expression

\mathbb{Q}[N(t)-N(t_{0})=0|\mathscr{F}_{t_{0}}]=\mathbb{Q}[N(t)-N(t_{0})=0]=\exp\left\{-\nu(\mathscr{U})\left(t-t_{0}\right)\right\}.

(33)

Under the physical probability
\mathbb{P}
, these probabilities become

\mathbb{P}[N(t)=0]=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\operatorname{Tr}\{\mathcal{S}(t,0)[\rho_{0}]\}\big],

(34a)

\mathbb{P}[N(t)-N(t_{0})=0|\mathscr{F}_{t_{0}}]=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\operatorname{Tr}\left\{\mathcal{S}(t,t_{0})[\rho(t_{0})]\right\}\big|\mathscr{F}_{t_{0}}\}\big].

(34b)

Computations.

Being (
27
) trace preserving, in between two jumps, by (
8
), (
30
), (
31a
) we get

\operatorname{Tr}\left\{\mathcal{S}(t,t_{0})\big[\rho(t_{0})\big]\right\}=\exp\left\{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\lambda(s)\mathrm{d}s\right\},\qquad p(t_{-})=\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\left(\nu(\mathscr{U})-\lambda(s)\right)\mathrm{d}s\right\}.

(35)

Then, the no jump probability is given by

\mathbb{P}[N(t)=0]=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[p(t)\mathtt{1}_{\{N(t)=0\}}\big]=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\exp\Big\{\int_{0}^{t}\left(\nu(\mathscr{U})-\lambda(s)\right)\mathrm{d}s\Big\}\mathtt{1}_{\{N(t)=0\}}\Big]\\
{}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{Q}[N(t)=0]}\int_{\{N(t)=0\}}\mathbb{Q}(\mathrm{d}\omega)\,\operatorname{Tr}\{\mathcal{S}(t,0)[\rho_{0}]\}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\operatorname{Tr}\{\mathcal{S}(t,0)[\rho_{0}]\}\big].

We introduce now a notation for the event “no jump in
(t_{0},t)
”:
\mathsf{E}(t,t_{0}):=\{N(t)-N(t_{0})=0\}
. Similarly to the previous computations we get:
\forall\mathsf{F}\in\mathscr{F}_{t_{0}}
,

\frac{\mathbb{P}[\mathsf{E}(t,t_{0})\cap\mathsf{F}]}{\mathbb{P}[\mathsf{F}]}=\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}[p(t)\mathtt{1}_{\mathsf{F}}\mathtt{1}_{\mathsf{E}(t,t_{0})}]}{\mathbb{P}[\mathsf{F}]}=\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}[p(t_{0})\mathtt{1}_{\mathsf{F}}\mathtt{1}_{\mathsf{E}(t,t_{0})}\operatorname{Tr}\left\{\mathcal{S}(t,t_{0})[\rho(t_{0})]\right\}]}{\mathbb{Q}[\mathsf{E}(t,t_{0})]\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\mathtt{1}_{\mathsf{F}}p_{t_{0}}]}.

When
\mathsf{F}
shrinks to a single trajectory in
(0,t_{0})
we get the final result in (
34b
).
∎

Let us take
t=0
as initial time or as time of a jump; we are interested in the waiting time
T
of the next jump. By its meaning we have the identification

\mathbb{P}[T\leq t]=1-\mathbb{P}[N(t)=0].

(36a)

We can obtain the probability density by derivation, using (
34
), (
35
):

p_{T}(t)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}[T\leq t]}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}[N(t)=0]}{\mathrm{d}t}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\lambda(t)\operatorname{Tr}\{\mathcal{S}(t,0)[\rho_{0}]\}\big].

(36b)

By (
13
), the positivity of the operator
R
gives the positivity of
\lambda(t)
and this gives the positivity of the probability density of the waiting time. It must be remarked that it is possible to have
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}[N(t)=0]>0
. In this case
\mathbb{P}[T=+\infty]>0
and the distribution has also a discrete point at infinity; some examples are given in Secs.
4.2.2
and
6.5.2
.

The identification (
36a
) opens the possibility of a systematic approach to the notion of waiting times for quantum systems, working in continuous time
[
56
,
55
,
54
,
63
]
. Here we have considered only the waiting times related to the process
N(t)
, but we could consider also the ones related to the point measure
\Pi(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}t)
; in this case the events for which one can introduce the waiting time are labelled by elements (or subsets) of the space
\mathscr{U}
.

3.3
Exclusive probability densities

All the probabilities for the counts can be reduced to the
exclusive probability densities
, quantities which were at the basis of the first consistent formulation of the quantum theory of measurements in continuous time
[
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
(see also
[
4
,
6
,
8
,
11
,
106
]
and references therein).

The exclusive probability densities give the probabilities of a count of type
u_{1}
around
t_{1}
, a count of type
u_{2}
around
t_{2}
, …, a count of type
u_{m}
around
t_{m}
, and no other count in between two counts and up to
t
; they are densities with respect to the measure
\nu_{t_{1}}(\mathrm{d}u_{1})\cdots\nu_{t_{m}}(\mathrm{d}u_{m})

4
Evolutions under non-Hermitian Hamiltonians

The study of evolutions under non-Hermitian (n-H) Hamiltonians is an active field of theoretical and experimental research, see
[
57
,
58
,
59
,
60
,
61
,
62
,
64
,
65
,
67
,
68
,
66
,
63
]
and references therein. In the jump trajectory theory, the n-H Hamiltonians naturally appear in between jumps.

Here we consider the case no extra randomness, as in Remark
5
, and no time dependence in the operators giving the dynamics; moreover, we take
\mathcal{L}_{0}
of purely Hamiltonian type, i.e. in (
4b
) we take
L_{k}(t)=0
and
R_{0}(t)=0
.
Then, equations (
30
) for the no-jump propagator reduce to

\frac{\mathrm{d}\ }{\mathrm{d}t}\,\mathcal{S}(t,t_{0})[\rho]=\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{S}(t,t_{0})[\rho],\qquad\qquad\mathcal{A}[\rho]=-\mathrm{i}\left(H-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\,R\right)\rho+\mathrm{i}\,\rho\left(H+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\,R\right).

(45)

We can say that the evolution between two jumps is given by the n-H Hamiltonian
H-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\,R
, with
R\geq 0
.

Viceversa, when we want to include any given effective n-H Hamiltonian
H_{\rm eff}
, we can construct
H
and
R
by

H:=\frac{1}{2}\left(H_{\rm eff}+H_{\rm eff}^{\dagger}\right),\qquad R:=c\mathds{1}+\mathrm{i}\left(H_{\rm eff}-H_{\rm eff}^{\dagger}\right),\qquad\text{with}\quad c\in\mathbb{R}:\ R\geq 0.

(46)

The choice of
c
is always possible because we are considering only bounded operators (Assumption
3
); more in general, we could ask
{}\>\mathrm{i}H_{\rm eff}-\mathrm{i}H_{\rm eff}^{\dagger}
to be bounded from below. The positivity of
R
is essential for the consistency of the theory. In particular, the condition
R\geq 0
guaranties the decreasing of the survival probability
\mathbb{P}[N(t)=0]
and the positivity of the probability density (
36
) of the waiting time
T
of the next jump; this point was recognized also in the literature on n-H evolutions
[
59
, Sec. III.A]
.

In this section we are interested only to the evolution in between two jumps; so, we can simplify by introducing a single counting process
N(t)
, which means that the type of jump is not observed or that there is a single type of jump (
\mathscr{U}
reduces to one point). Under the reference probability,
N(t)
is a Poisson process of intensity
\nu
; moreover, we denote by
\mathcal{J}
the CP jump operator. Then, the linear SME (
7
) reduces to

\mathrm{d}\sigma(t)=\Bigl(\mathcal{A}_{0}[\sigma(t_{-})]-(c-\nu)\sigma(t_{-})\Bigr)\mathrm{d}t+\Bigl(\mathcal{J}[\sigma(t_{-})]-\sigma(t_{-})\Bigr)\mathrm{d}N(t),

(47)

\mathcal{A}_{0}[\rho]=-\mathrm{i}H_{\rm eff}\rho+\mathrm{i}\rho H_{\rm eff}^{\dagger}\,.

(48)

Moreover, equations (
4a
), (
5
), (
45
), (
46
), (
38
) give

\mathcal{G}=\nu\mathcal{J},\qquad\mathcal{G}^{*}[\mathds{1}]=R,\qquad\mathcal{A}[\rho]=\mathcal{A}_{0}[\rho]-c\rho,\qquad\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{G},\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}\eta(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathcal{L}[\eta(t)].

(49)

A possible choice for the jump operator is to take
\mathcal{G}[\rho]=R^{1/2}\rho R^{1/2}
. Once
H_{\rm eff}
has been fixed, there are infinitely many choices for
c
and
G
. These choices change the jump operator in (
47
) and, so, they are physically inequivalent, as they change what happens at a jump. However, these choices do not change the dynamics in between two jumps. Being
\mathcal{G}
CP, the mean state
\eta(t)
satisfies an usual quantum master equation.

4.1
The non-linear SME and the survival probability

Under the physical probability
\mathbb{P}
,
N(t)
is a counting process of stochastic intensity
\lambda(t)
(
13
) and the a posteriori states (
9
) satisfy the non-linear SME (
16
), which becomes

\mathrm{d}\rho(t)=\Bigl(\mathcal{A}_{0}[\rho(t_{-})]+\tilde{\lambda}(t)\rho(t_{-})\Bigr)\mathrm{d}t+\Bigl(I(t)^{-1}\mathcal{J}[\rho(t_{-})]-\rho(t_{-})\Bigr)\mathrm{d}N(t),

(50)

with

\tilde{\lambda}(t)=\mathrm{i}\operatorname{Tr}\left\{\left(H_{\rm eff}-H_{\rm eff}^{\dagger}\right)\rho(t_{-})\right\},\qquad I(t)=\operatorname{Tr}\left\{\mathcal{J}\big[\rho(t_{-})\big]\right\},\qquad\lambda(t)=I(t)\nu=\tilde{\lambda}(t)+c.

(51)

By (
45
), (
46
), (
48
), the no-jump propagator (
30
) can be written as

\mathcal{S}(t,t_{0})=\mathrm{e}^{-c(t-t_{0})}\mathcal{S}_{0}(t-t_{0}),\qquad\mathcal{S}_{0}(t)[\rho]:=\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{A}_{0}t}[\rho]\equiv\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}H_{\rm eff}t}\rho\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}H_{\rm eff}^{\dagger}t}.

(52)

Then, the two SMEs (
47
) and (
50
) give that, in between two jumps, the evolution is given by

\sigma(t)=\mathrm{e}^{(\nu-c)(t-t_{0})}\mathcal{S}_{0}(t-t_{0})[\sigma(t_{0})],\qquad\rho(t)=\frac{\sigma(t)}{\operatorname{Tr}\{\sigma(t)\}}=\frac{\mathcal{S}_{0}(t-t_{0})[\rho(t_{0})]}{\operatorname{Tr}\{\mathcal{S}_{0}(t-t_{0})[\rho(t_{0})]\}}\,.

(53)

Let us stress that the evolution of the conditional state does not depend on the choice of
c
and that it is determined only by
H_{\rm eff}
. Being the effective Hamiltonian non-Hermitian, the evolution of
\rho(t)
includes the normalization factor. In the theory of non-Hermitian evolutions, such a normalization appeared, for instance, in
[
57
]
.

Instead, at a jump the state change is given by

\sigma(t_{0})=\mathcal{J}[\sigma({t_{0}}_{-})],\qquad\rho(t_{0})=\frac{\mathcal{J}[\sigma({t_{0}}_{-})]}{I(t_{0})}=\frac{\mathcal{G}[\rho({t_{0}}_{-})]}{\lambda(t_{0})}=\frac{\mathcal{G}[\rho({t_{0}}_{-})]}{c+\mathrm{i}\operatorname{Tr}\left\{\left(H_{\rm eff}-H_{\rm eff}^{\dagger}\right)\rho({t_{0}}_{-})\right\}}\,;

(54)

recall that, under
\mathbb{P}
, when
\lambda(t_{0})=0
there is no jump.

From (
39
), the survival probability is

\mathbb{P}[N(t)=0]=\mathrm{e}^{-ct}\operatorname{Tr}\{\mathcal{S}_{0}(t)[\rho_{0}].

(55)

Then, the distribution of the waiting time (
36
) is given by

\mathbb{P}[T\leq t]=1-\mathbb{P}[N(t)=0],\qquad p_{T}(t)=\lambda(t)\mathbb{P}[N(t)=0]=\bigl(c+\tilde{\lambda}(t)\bigr)\mathbb{P}[N(t)=0];

(56)

\tilde{\lambda}(t)
is defined in (
51
) and
c
is needed to guarantee the positivity of this probability density. As we shall see in the examples in Sec.
4.2.2
,
it is possible to have
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}[N(t)=0]>0
; in this case we have
\mathbb{P}[T=+\infty]>0
, and the distribution of
T
is a mixture of a continuous one and a discrete one.

Summarizing, the whole behaviour of the system before the first jump (or in between two jumps) depends on the constant
c
and on the effective Hamiltonian
H_{\rm eff}
, both introduced in (
46
). The no-jump propagator
\mathcal{S}_{0}(t)
(
52
), generated by
H_{\rm eff}
, gives the dynamics of the conditional state (the second equation in (
53
)). Finally,
\mathcal{S}_{0}(t)
and the constant
c
give the survival probability (
55
) and the waiting time distribution (
56
).

4.2
Examples in
\mathbb{C}^{2}

In the literature on n-H evolutions a relevant role is played by the
exceptional points
(EPs). The evolution under an effective n-H Hamiltonian can be studied by starting from its eigenvalues and right/left eigenvectors
[
62
]
; the EPs in the parameter space are singular points at which two eigenvectors and eigenvalues collapse. As stated in many articles, the physical behaviour of the system near an EP is well represented by the two-level case; so, we illustrate the theory by some examples involving
\mathscr{H}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
. We do not consider explicitly the jump operator; it could determine a jump inside the two-level space, but it could be a jump to some other levels; in this last case, at the jump the system disappears. In
[
61
,
64
,
65
]
it is explicitly stated that the n-H dynamics stays in a two dimensional subspace up to a jump to a third fundamental state. The experimental realizations of systems with n-H evolutions are complicated apparatuses involving excitons, polaritons, control of gain and losses…
[
58
,
61
,
67
]
.

So, let us consider a n-H dynamics in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, before the first jump (or in between two jumps). The generic effective Hamiltonian can be parametrized as

H_{\rm eff}=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{1}&\beta\\
\delta&\epsilon_{0}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}}{2}\,\mathds{1}+K,\qquad\epsilon_{j},\;\beta,\;\delta\in\mathbb{C},

(57a)

K=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\delta&-\alpha\end{pmatrix}=\frac{\beta+\delta}{2}\,\sigma_{x}+\frac{\mathrm{i}(\beta-\delta)}{2}\,\sigma_{y}+\alpha\sigma_{z},\qquad\alpha:=\frac{\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{0}}{2};

(57b)

\sigma_{x}
,
\sigma_{y}
,
\sigma_{z}
are the usual Pauli matrices. Then, the
R
-operator and the positivity condition (
46
) become

R=\tilde{c}\,\mathds{1}+\mathrm{i}\left(K-K^{\dagger}\right)=\tilde{c}\,\mathds{1}+\begin{pmatrix}-2\mathrm{\,Im\,}\alpha&\mathrm{i}\left(\beta-\overline{\delta}\right)\\
\mathrm{i}\left(\delta-\overline{\beta}\right)&2\mathrm{\,Im\,}\alpha\end{pmatrix},\qquad\tilde{c}:=c-\mathrm{\,Im\,}\left(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{0}\right),

(58a)

R\geq 0\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\tilde{c}\geq k:=\sqrt{\left|\beta-\overline{\delta}\right|^{2}+(2\mathrm{\,Im\,}\alpha)^{2}}\geq 0.

(58b)

A relevant point of the
\mathbb{C}^{2}
-case is that the square of the
K
-operator (
57b
) is proportional to the identity:

K^{2}=\varkappa\mathds{1},\qquad\varkappa:=\alpha^{2}+\beta\delta.

(59)

By using the quantity
\varkappa
, the bound
k
in (
58b
) can be rewritten as

\qquad k^{2}=2\left|\alpha\right|^{2}+\left|\beta\right|^{2}+\left|\delta\right|^{2}-2\mathrm{\,Re\,}\varkappa=2\left(\left|\alpha\right|^{2}+\left|\beta\right|\left|\delta\right|-\mathrm{\,Re\,}\varkappa\right)+\left(\left|\beta\right|-\left|\delta\right|\right)^{2}.

(60)

From the parametrization (
57b
) of
K
and the expressions of
k
(
58b
) and (
60
), we have

K=K^{\dagger}\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad k=0\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\left|\beta\right|=\left|\delta\right|,\quad\varkappa=\left|\alpha\right|^{2}+\left|\beta\right|^{2}.

(61)

Now, the no jump probability (
55
) takes the expression

\mathbb{P}[N(t)=0]=\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{c}\,t}\operatorname{Tr}\{\zeta(t)\},\qquad\zeta(t):=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}Kt}\rho_{0}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}K^{\dagger}t};

(62)

the associated probability density of the waiting time of the first jump is given by (
56
). Let us remark that the survival probability (
62
) has been obtained consistently with the usual formulation of a quantum theory based on POVMs and “instruments”, see Secs.
2.5.1
and
3.2
. Then, the conditional state before the first jump (
53
) has the expression

\rho(t)=\frac{\zeta(t)}{\operatorname{Tr}\{\zeta(t)\}};

(63)

note that this evolution is non linear in the initial state because of the normalization: it is the state attributed to the system knowing that no jump occurred up to time
t
. Also the name of post-selected state is used
[
60
,
64
,
65
]
.

According to the choices of the parameters in (
57a
) there are different behaviours of the quantum system.

•

R=0
: equivalent to
\beta=\overline{\delta}
,
\mathrm{\,Im\,}\epsilon_{1}=\mathrm{\,Im\,}\epsilon_{0}
,
\tilde{c}=0
. In this case there are no jumps, because
\lambda(t)=0
, and the evolution is unitary, because
K=K^{\dagger}
.

•

R\propto\mathds{1}
,
R\neq 0
: equivalent to
\beta=\overline{\delta}
,
\mathrm{\,Im\,}\epsilon_{1}=\mathrm{\,Im\,}\epsilon_{0}
,
\tilde{c}>0
. We have a unitary evolution with Hamiltonian
K=K^{\dagger}
interspersed by quantum channels; the jumps are at a rate
\lambda=c-\mathrm{\,Im\,}(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{0})>0
. We shall treat this case in a more general set up in Sec.
5
.

•

K\neq K^{\dagger}
,
K^{2}=0
: equivalent to
\alpha^{2}+\beta\delta=0
and
\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|\neq 0
. This is the case of an exceptional point (EP), discussed in Sec.
4.2.1
.

•

K^{2}\neq 0
,
K\neq K^{\dagger}
: equivalent to
\alpha^{2}+\beta\delta\neq 0
and
k>0
. This is the typical case of a n-H evolution, when two left eigenvector (and two right eigenvectors) can be found, see Sec.
4.2.2
.

In the literature there are various proposals of effective Hamiltonians in the reduced case of
\mathscr{H}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and in some cases also the related experiments are performed. By using the parametrization given in (
57
), the proposals of
[
58
,
59
,
60
,
61
,
67
,
68
]
can be unified by taking

\epsilon_{j}=E_{j}-\mathrm{i}\Gamma_{j},\quad E_{j},\;\Gamma_{j}\in\mathbb{R},\qquad\delta=\overline{\beta}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta},\quad\theta\in\mathbb{R},\qquad\alpha=\frac{E_{1}-E_{2}}{2}-\mathrm{i}\,\frac{\Gamma_{1}-\Gamma_{2}}{2}.

(64a)

The parameters
E_{j}
represent the energy levels and the
\Gamma_{j}
represent gain and losses. Then, in (
58
), (
59
), we have

\begin{split}\frac{\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{0}}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left[E_{1}+E_{0}-\mathrm{i}\left(\Gamma_{1}+\Gamma_{0}\right)\right],\qquad\qquad&\tilde{c}=c-\frac{\Gamma_{1}+\Gamma_{0}}{2},\\
k=\sqrt{4\left|\beta\right|^{2}\left(\sin\theta/2\right)^{2}+(\Gamma_{1}-\Gamma_{0})^{2}},\qquad\qquad&\varkappa=\alpha^{2}+\left|\beta\right|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}.\end{split}

(64b)

Here below we study the behaviour of a n-H evolution in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with a particular attention to the distribution of the waiting time of the first jump, an argument not usually considered in the literature.

4.2.1
The behaviour in an exceptional point

The EP point in the parameter space is when we have

\varkappa\equiv\alpha^{2}+\beta\delta=0,\qquad\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|\neq 0\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad K\neq 0,\qquad K^{2}=0;

(65)

the equivalence follows immediately from the expression of
K
(
57b
) and the property (
59
). In this case we have not two distinct eigenvectors; indeed, we have

K=({\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|}){|\phi_{0}\rangle\langle\phi_{1}|},\qquad\langle\phi_{j}|\phi_{i}\rangle=\delta_{ij},

(66)

\displaystyle\text{for}\ \beta\neq 0:\qquad\phi_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|}}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\left|\beta\right|}\\
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\left(\arg\delta-\arg\alpha\right)}\sqrt{\left|\delta\right|}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\phi_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\arg\alpha}\sqrt{\left|\delta\right|}\\
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\arg\beta}\sqrt{\left|\beta\right|}\end{pmatrix},

(67a)

\displaystyle\text{for}\ \beta=0:\qquad\qquad\phi_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\arg\delta}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\phi_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(67b)

Computations.

From the vanishing of
\varkappa
we get

\left|\alpha\right|^{2}=\left|\beta\right|\left|\delta\right|,\qquad\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\arg\alpha}=-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\arg\beta+\arg\delta)},\qquad\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\arg\alpha-\arg\delta)}=-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\arg\alpha-\arg\beta)}.

When
\beta=0
, the quantities
\arg\alpha
and
\arg\beta
are not defined; similarly,
\arg\alpha
and
\arg\delta
are not defined when
\delta=0
.

Then, we search for two vectors such that
K\phi_{0}=0
and
K\phi_{1}\propto\phi_{0}
; it is easy to verify that the vectors given in (
67
) satisfy this requirement, they are orthogonal and give the expression (
66
) for
K
. However, the expression
|\phi_{0}\rangle\langle\phi_{1}|
is independent from a common phase of the two vectors, and one can check that it is not possible to choose this phase in a way independent from any value of the parameters. The choice (
67a
) works well when all the parameters are not vanishing; it works also for
\delta=0
, because in this case the dependence on
\arg\delta
and
\arg\alpha
disappears and the two vectors become

\text{for}\ \delta=0:\qquad\phi_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\phi_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\arg\beta}\end{pmatrix}.

In the case of
\beta=0
, the choice (
67a
) looses its meaning because the dependence on
\arg\alpha
survives; in this case, one can check that the choice (
67b
) works well.
∎

Being

a nilpotent operator (
65
), the associated evolution operator
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}Kt}
turns out to be linear in

and we have

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}Kt}=\mathds{1}-\mathrm{i}Kt=\mathds{1}-\mathrm{i}t\left(\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|\right)|\phi_{0}\rangle\langle\phi_{1}|.

(68)

Moreover, by (
60
) and (
65
), the positivity condition (

) becomes

R\geq 0\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\tilde{c}\equiv c-\mathrm{\,Im\,}\left(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{0}\right)\geq k\equiv\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|>0.

(69)

Let us consider a generic initial state

\rho_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}|\psi_{i}\rangle r_{i}\langle\psi_{i}|,\qquad\langle\psi_{i}|\psi_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij},\qquad 0\leq r_{i}\leq 1,\qquad r_{2}=1-r_{1}.

By (
62
), (
68
) we get

\zeta(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{2}|\psi_{i}(t)\rangle r_{i}\langle\psi_{i}(t)|,\qquad|\psi_{i}(t)\rangle=\left(\mathds{1}-\mathrm{i}Kt\right)|\psi_{i}\rangle=|\psi_{i}\rangle-\mathrm{i}t\left({\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|}\right)|\phi_{0}\rangle\langle\phi_{1}|\psi_{i}\rangle.

Then, the conditional state up to the first jump (
63
) takes the form

\rho(t)=\frac{\zeta(t)}{\sum_{j}r_{j}\left\|\psi_{j}(t)\right\|^{2}},\qquad\left\|\psi_{i}(t)\right\|^{2}=\left|\langle\phi_{1}|\psi_{i}\rangle\right|^{2}+\left|\langle\phi_{0}|\psi_{i}\rangle-\mathrm{i}t\left(\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|\right)\langle\phi_{1}|\psi_{i}\rangle\right|^{2},

(70a)

and the survival probability (
62
) becomes

\mathbb{P}[N(t)=0]=\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{c}t}\sum_{i=1}^{2}r_{i}\left\|\psi_{i}(t)\right\|^{2}.

(70b)

Finally, by direct computation of the derivative in (
36
), we get the probability density of the waiting time of the first jump:

p_{T}(t)=-\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}[N(t)=0]}{\mathrm{d}t}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}r_{i}p_{i}(t),

(71a)

p_{i}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{c}t}\Bigl\{\left(\tilde{c}-\left|\beta\right|-\left|\delta\right|\right)\left\|\psi_{i}(t)\right\|^{2}+\left(\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|\right)\left|\langle\phi_{0}|\psi_{i}\rangle+\mathrm{i}\langle\phi_{1}|\psi_{i}\rangle\left[1-t(\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|)\right]\right|^{2}\Bigr\}.

(71b)

As it appears, the positivity condition (
69
) is necessary to guarantee the positivity of these probability densities.
To illustrate this point, we consider two simple examples in the case of a pure initial state
\rho_{0}=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
. By taking
\psi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\phi_{0}+\mathrm{i}\phi_{1}\right)
we get

p_{T}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{c}t}\left\{\left(\tilde{c}-\left|\beta\right|-\left|\delta\right|\right)\left[1+\left(\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|\right)t\right]+\frac{1}{2}\,\tilde{c}\left(\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|\right)^{2}t^{2}\right\};

the positivity condition (
69
) is needed to have
p_{T}(t)\geq 0
for small times. Instead, for
\psi=\phi_{1}
we get

p_{T}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{c}t}\Big[\tilde{c}\left(1+t^{2}(\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|)^{2}\right)-2t(\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|)^{2}\Big]

and the positivity condition (
69
) is needed to guarantee
p_{T}(t)\geq 0
at intermediate times, as one sees by taking
t=1/(\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|)
:

p_{T}(t)\Big|_{t=1/(\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|)}=\exp\left\{\frac{-\tilde{c}}{\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|}\right\}2\left(\tilde{c}-\left|\beta\right|-\left|\delta\right|\right).

As these examples show, there is a variety of behaviours of the probability densities (
71b
), depending on the initial state. This variety of behaviours is apparent also by taking only the mean and the variance of the survival time. Let us consider a pure initial state and the smallest possible value for the parameter
\tilde{c}
:

\rho_{0}=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|,\qquad\tilde{c}=\left|\beta\right|+\left|\delta\right|=:\lambda_{0}.

Then, by direct computations from the density

, we get some examples:

•

for
\psi=\phi_{0}
:
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[T]=1/\lambda_{0}
,
\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb{P}}[T]=1/\lambda_{0}^{2}
;

•

for
\psi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\phi_{0}-\mathrm{i}\phi_{1}\right)
:
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[T]=1/\lambda_{0}
,
\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb{P}}[T]=3/\lambda_{0}^{2}
;

•

for
\psi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\phi_{0}+\mathrm{i}\phi_{1}\right)
:
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[T]=3/\lambda_{0}
,
\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb{P}}[T]=3/\lambda_{0}^{2}
;

•

for
\psi=\phi_{1}
:
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[T]=3/\lambda_{0}
,
\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb{P}}[T]=5/\lambda_{0}^{2}
.

4.2.2
Out of the exceptional point

Now, we consider the case of a n-H evolution out of the EP:

\varkappa\equiv\alpha^{2}+\beta\delta\neq 0,\qquad k\equiv\sqrt{\left|\beta-\overline{\delta}\right|^{2}+(2\mathrm{\,Im\,}\alpha)^{2}}>0\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad K\neq K^{\dagger},\qquad K^{2}\neq 0;

(72)

Also in this case it is possible to get the evolution operator generated by
K
directly from the property (
59
), without introducing its eigenvectors. The computations needed in proving the following statements are given in Appendix
B.1
.

Remark 7
.

The propagator
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}Kt}
can be expressed as

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}Kt}=g_{+}(\varkappa t^{2})\mathds{1}-g_{-}(\varkappa t^{2})\mathrm{i}Kt,\qquad g_{+}(\xi):=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(-\xi)^{n}}{(2n)!},\qquad g_{-}(\xi):=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(-\xi)^{n}}{(2n+1)!}.

(73)

The radius of convergence of the two series is
+\infty
;
\varkappa=0
is not a singular point. For
\varkappa\to 0
we have the right evolution in the EP.

It is possible to rewrite the propagator (
73
) in terms of eigenvalues and right eigenvectors of
K
; this procedure involves the choice the square root of
\varkappa
.
By defining

\chi:=\arg\,\frac{\varkappa}{\left|\varkappa\right|},\qquad z:=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\chi/2}\sqrt{\left|\varkappa\right|},

(74)

the two series appearing in (
73
) can be expressed by means of
z
as

g_{+}(\varkappa t^{2})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}zt}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}zt}\right),\qquad g_{-}(\varkappa t^{2})=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}zt}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}zt}-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}zt}\right).

(75)

By inserting (
75
) into (
73
), we get the expression of the propagator in terms of eigenvalues and eigenvectors:

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}Kt}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}zt}V_{+}+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}zt}V_{-},\qquad V_{\pm}:=\frac{\mathds{1}}{2}\pm\frac{K}{2z}\quad\Rightarrow\quad{V_{\pm}}^{2}=V_{\pm},\quad V_{\mp}V_{\pm}=0,\quad V_{+}+V_{-}=\mathds{1}.

(76)

By the definition of
V_{\pm}
and its properties, we obtain

K=zV_{+}-zV_{-},\qquad KV_{\pm}\psi=\pm zV_{\pm}\psi.

(77)

So, the quantities
V_{\pm}\psi
are the right eigenvectors of the n-H operator
K
(if
\psi
is such that both are different from zero). However,
V_{-}^{\dagger}V_{+}\neq 0
and the two eigenvectors are not mutually orthogonal.

Remark 8
.

In (
74
) the choice of the sign of the square root of
\varkappa
is arbitrary. If we take a shift of
2\pi
in the phase
\chi
, we get an exchange of the two eigenvalues:

\chi\to\chi+2\pi\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\pm z\to\mp z,\qquad V_{\pm}\to V_{\mp}.

(78)

In any case this exchange leaves invariant
K
and
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}Kt}
, see (
73
), (
75
), (
77
).

Let us denote by
u_{\pm}
the normalized right eigenvectors of
K
:

Ku_{\pm}=\pm zu_{\pm},\qquad\left\|u_{\pm}\right\|=1.

(79)

By using the expression of
K
we can find explicitly these two eigenvectors, but we have to distinguish some subcases. The proof of the following proposition is given in Appendix
B.2
.

Proposition 2
.

We take
\varkappa\neq 0
.

•

For
\beta\delta\neq 0
we get

u_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left|\alpha\pm z\right|^{2}+\left|\delta\right|^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\pm z\\
\delta\end{pmatrix}=\frac{\Phi}{\sqrt{\left|\alpha\mp z\right|^{2}+\left|\beta\right|^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}-\beta\\
\alpha\mp z\end{pmatrix},\qquad\Phi:=\frac{\left(\overline{\alpha}\mp\overline{z}\right)\delta}{\left|\alpha\mp z\right|\left|\delta\right|};

(80)

•

For
\beta\delta=0
we have
\alpha\neq 0
and we chose
z=\alpha
. Then, we get
u_{+}
from the first expression in (
80
) and
u_{-}
from the second one, without the phase factor
\Phi
.

Moreover, we have the following equivalencies:

\langle u_{+}|u_{-}\rangle=0\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad(K/z)^{\dagger}=K/z\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad V_{\pm}={V_{\pm}}^{\dagger}.

(81)

In (
80
)
\Phi
is only a phase factor needed to have the equality. Equation (
81
) says that
u_{+}
and
u_{-}
are mutually orthogonal only when
K
is proportional to a self-adjoint operator.

When
\varkappa\to 0
also
z\to 0
and the two right eigenvectors
u_{\pm}
collapse in a single one, proportional to
\phi_{0}
(
67
). It is possible to introduce also the left eigenvectors
{v_{\pm}}^{\dagger}\propto\left(\alpha\pm z,\;\beta\right)
; for
\varkappa\to 0
they collapse to
\phi_{1}
.

By (
60
) and (
74
), the positivity condition (
58
) can be written as

\tilde{c}\geq k\equiv\sqrt{2\left(\left|\alpha\right|^{2}+\left|\beta\delta\right|-\left|\varkappa\right|\right)+\left(\left|\beta\right|-\left|\delta\right|\right)^{2}+4(\mathrm{\,Im\,}z)^{2}}\geq 2\left|\mathrm{\,Im\,}z\right|.

(82)

Having the explicit expression of the propagator, by (
62
) and (
63
), one can compute the conditional state up to the first jump and the survival probability. Let us consider the survival probability in the case of a pure initial state
\psi
:

\psi=x_{+}u_{+}+x_{-}u_{-},\qquad x_{\pm}\in\mathbb{C},\qquad\left|x_{+}\right|^{2}+\left|x_{-}\right|^{2}+2\mathrm{\,Re\,}(\overline{x}_{+}x_{-}\langle u_{+}|u_{-}\rangle)\equiv\left\|\psi\right\|^{2}=1.

(83)

Then, we have

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}[N(t)=0]=\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{c}t}\left\|\psi(t)\right\|^{2},\qquad\psi(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}Kt}\psi=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}zt}x_{+}u_{+}+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}zt}x_{-}u_{-},\\
&\left\|\psi(t)\right\|^{2}=\left|x_{+}\right|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2t\mathrm{\,Im\,}z}+\left|x_{-}\right|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2t\mathrm{\,Im\,}z}+2\mathrm{\,Re\,}(\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}t\mathrm{\,Re\,}z}\;\overline{x}_{+}x_{-}\langle u_{+}|u_{-}\rangle).\end{split}

(84)

By the condition (
82
) the survival probability cannot increase. However, for certain choices of the parameters, we could have a not vanishing survival probability for long times:
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}[N(t)=0]>0
. To have this case (but with
K\neq K^{\dagger}
), we need
\tilde{c}=k
and
k=2\left|\mathrm{\,Im\,}z\right|>0
, which means
\left|\beta\right|=\left|\delta\right|
,
\left|\alpha\right|^{2}+\left|\beta\delta\right|=\left|\varkappa\right|
.
So, we are in the class of models parameterized by (
64
), and we have to take

\left|\beta\right|=\left|\delta\right|,\qquad\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\arg\alpha}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta},\qquad\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\neq 1,\qquad\tilde{c}=2\left|\mathrm{\,Im\,}z\right|>0.

This gives

\varkappa=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\left(\left|\alpha\right|^{2}+\left|\beta\right|^{2}\right),\qquad\mathrm{\,Im\,}z\neq 0,

\mathbb{P}[N(t)=0]=\mathrm{e}^{-2t\left|\mathrm{\,Im\,}z\right|}\left[\left|x_{+}\right|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2t\mathrm{\,Im\,}z}+\left|x_{-}\right|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2t\mathrm{\,Im\,}z}+2\mathrm{\,Re\,}(\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}t\mathrm{\,Re\,}z}\;\overline{x}_{+}x_{-}\langle u_{+}|u_{-}\rangle)\right];

then, one of the first two contributions survives for long times.

A special case of interest is when
K\neq K^{\dagger}
, but its eigenvalues are real, see for instance
[
59
]
. This case is equivalent to
k>0
and
\varkappa>0
, i.e.

\varkappa\equiv\alpha^{2}+\beta\delta>0,\qquad k^{2}\equiv 2\left(\left|\alpha\right|^{2}+\left|\beta\delta\right|-\varkappa\right)+\left(\left|\beta\right|-\left|\delta\right|\right)^{2}>0.

By taking
z:=\sqrt{\varkappa}>0
and using (
83
), (
84
), (
36
), we have

\mathbb{P}[N(t)=0]=\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{c}t}\left\|\psi(t)\right\|^{2},\qquad\left\|\psi(t)\right\|^{2}=1+2\mathrm{\,Re\,}\left[\left(\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}zt}-1\right)\overline{x}_{+}x_{-}\langle u_{+}|u_{-}\rangle\right],

p_{T}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{c}t}\left\{\tilde{c}\left\|\psi(t)\right\|^{2}+4z\mathrm{\,Im\,}(\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}zt}\,\overline{x}_{+}x_{-}\langle u_{+}|u_{-}\rangle)\right\}.

By
k>0
we have that
\tilde{c}>0
and, so,
p_{T}(t)
is a true probability density. If we exclude the case of
K
proportional to a self-adjoint operator, this probability density has a very peculiar oscillating behaviour.

An example with real eigenvalues is given
[
59
]
; the proposed model is

K=s\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{i}\sin\phi&1\\
1&-\mathrm{i}\sin\phi\end{pmatrix},\quad s\in\mathbb{R}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\varkappa=s^{2}(\cos\phi)^{2},\quad z=\left|s\cos\phi\right|,\quad k=2\left|s\sin\phi\right|;

K
is Hermitian only for
s\sin\phi=0
. As example we take

s>0,\qquad\phi=\pi/4,\qquad\tilde{c}=k,\qquad x_{+}=\frac{1+\mathrm{i}}{2},\qquad x_{-}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\sqrt{2}};

then, we have

\varkappa=\frac{s^{2}}{2}\,\qquad z=\frac{s}{\sqrt{2}},\qquad k=\sqrt{2}\,s=2z,\qquad u_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{i}\pm 1\\
\sqrt{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\langle u_{+}|u_{-}\rangle=\frac{1+\mathrm{i}}{2},

\overline{x}_{+}x_{-}\langle u_{+}|u_{-}\rangle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\sqrt{2}},\qquad\psi=x_{+}u_{+}+x_{-}u_{-},\qquad\left\|\psi\right\|^{2}=1,

\left\|\psi(t)\right\|^{2}=1-\frac{\sin\sqrt{2}st}{\sqrt{2}},\qquad p_{T}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2}st}s\left\{\sqrt{2}-\sin\sqrt{2}st+\cos\sqrt{2}st\right\}.

With this choice of the parameters, we have not only a probability density with oscillations, but also with a sequence of zeros:
p_{T}(t)=0
for
\sqrt{2}st=2n\pi+3\pi/4
.

Sometimes, the case of n-H Hamiltonian with real eigenvalues is considered of interest when it is possible to modify the internal product in such a way that the evolution becomes unitary. However, as stated in
[
59
]
, this means to go back to an Hermitian Hamiltonian. Here we have studied the n-H case without changing the internal product and we have shown that we get very unusual probability densities for the survival time.

Another point of interest is to see what happens when the parameters are modified along a path which goes around an EP. This procedure exchanges the two eigenvectors and the two eigenvalues; as discussed in Remark
8
, there is no change in the dynamics. On the contrary, if this continuous modification of the parameters is applied to the preparation, there is an exchange
u_{+}\leftrightarrow u_{-}
and the properties of the preparation change; about this point there are also experimental results
[
58
]
.

A piecewise dynamics interspersed by jump operators at random times

Now we consider the case of a (unitary or dissipative) dynamics interrupted at random times by some action (typically given by a quantum channel). It can represent a smooth dynamics modified by interactions with external environments or measuring apparatuses, and it has been used to model dissipative evolutions, mainly in solid state physics and in statistical mechanics
[

,

,
71

]

We start from the counting process

satisfies Assumptions

(85)

with

assigned once for all. The operator

Now, we can introduce all the operators on

Firstly, we define

It is possible to check that

note that we have

5.1

We take a sequence of distributions on

In (

6.1.1
The Pauli rate equation

Under the reference law
\mathbb{Q}
, the probabilities at time

Let us introduce the probability of being in the position

Computations.

By differentiating the Kronecker delta in (

\mathrm{d}\delta_{X(t),x(k)}=\sum_{u=1}^{r}\mathtt{1}_{F_{u}}\big(X(t_{-})\big)\mathrm{d}N_{u}(t)\left(\delta_{y^{u}(X(t_{-})),x(k)}-\delta_{X(t_{-}),x(k)}\right).

Then, by taking the
\mathbb{Q}
-mean we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}q_{k}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\sum_{u=1}^{r}\nu_{u}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\mathtt{1}_{F_{u}}\big(X(t_{-})\big)\delta_{y^{u}(X(t_{-})),x(k)}-\mathtt{1}_{F_{u}}\big(x(k)\big)\delta_{X(t_{-}),x(k)}\right]\\
{}=\sum_{u=1}^{r}\nu_{u}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\sum_{i}\mathtt{1}_{F_{u}}\big(x(i)\big)\delta_{y^{u}(x(i)),x(k)}\delta_{x(i),X(t_{-})}-\mathtt{1}_{F_{u}}\big(x(k)\big)\delta_{X(t_{-}),x(k)}\right]\\
{}=\sum_{u=1}^{r}\nu_{u}\left[\sum_{i}\mathtt{1}_{F_{u}}\big(x(i)\big)\delta_{y^{u}(x(i)),x(k)}q_{i}(t)-\mathtt{1}_{F_{u}}\big(x(k)\big)q_{k}(t)\right].

This gives (

∎

6.4
Typical trajectories

Once again, we can give the recursive structure of the solutions of the evolution equations along the typical trajectories (
25
). Let us start by the classical random walk (
136
), which is constant in time up to the first jump and between two jumps. At the jump
(u_{j},t_{j})
we have

X(t_{j-1})\quad\to\quad X(t_{j})=\begin{cases}y^{u_{j}}\big(X(t_{j-1})\big)\quad&\text{if}\ X(t_{j-1})\in F_{u_{j}}\\
X(t_{j-1})&\text{if}\ X(t_{j-1})\notin F_{u_{j}},\end{cases}\qquad t_{0}=0,

(152)

where we have taken into account that
X({t_{j}}_{-})=X(t_{j-1})
by the absence of evolution between two jumps.

Regarding the SMEs, recall that all the operators involved are independent of time in between two jumps, while at a jump all the operators can change together with
X(t_{j})
according to the dependence (
141

Firstly, we introduce the no jump propagator as in Definition
1
: in between two jumps

\mathcal{S}(t,t_{0})=\exp\left\{(t-t_{0})\mathcal{A}(t_{0})\right\},\qquad\mathcal{A}(t_{0})[\rho]=\mathcal{L}_{0}\big(X(t_{0})\big)[\rho]-\frac{1}{2}\left\{R\big(X(t_{0})\big),\,\rho\right\}.

(153)

Then, the solution of the linear SME (

31

\nu(\mathscr{U})\equiv\nu

the solution goes to zero because of the indicator function inside the jump operator (
141b
).
Analogously, the solution of the non-linear SME (

32

vanishes have to be eliminated, because they have probability zero. Then, we have

(154)

in between two jumps, while at a jump (with
X(t_{j-1})\in F_{u_{j}}

\rho({t_{j}}_{-})\quad\to\quad\rho(t_{j})=I(u_{j},t_{j})^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{1}}J_{i}\big(X(t_{j-1}),u_{j}\big)\rho({t_{j}}_{-})J_{i}\big(X(t_{j-1}),u_{j}\big)^{\dagger};

(155)

Finally, the exclusive probability densities have the structure (

Summarizing, we can say that the jumps happen at random times determined by the stochastic intensities
\nu_{u}I(u,t)
(

(

(

\rho(t)
according to equation (

The whole construction gives explicitly the hybrid dynamics as a classical random walk on a graph (

\big(X(t_{0}),\rho(t_{0})\big)

(156)

t_{0}

6.5.2
Dependence on the Hamiltonian contribution

For the model introduced in this section, the behaviour at a jump is completely fixed (
162
), but the waiting time distribution (
166
) and the quantum evolution (
163
) depend on the no-jump propagator (
164
) and, so, they depend on the choice of the Hamiltonian (
158
). As in Sec.
4.2
, we have again a non-Hermitian evolution in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and the choice of the Hamiltonian (
158
) has a strong influence on the behaviour of the system.

(a)
H_{k}\propto\sigma_{z}
.

The first choice is to take the Hamiltonian proportional to
\sigma_{z}
:

H_{k}=\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}\,\sigma_{z},\qquad\omega_{k}\in\mathbb{R},\quad k=0,\,1.

(167)

Then, the selfadjoint part of the effective Hamiltonian (
160
) commutes with the anti-selfadjoint contribution, and the no-jump propagator (
164
) has the expression

\mathcal{S}_{0}(t)[\rho]=\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{0}t/2}P_{1}+\mathrm{e}^{(\mathrm{i}\omega_{0}-\nu_{0})t/2}P_{0}\right)\rho\left(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{0}t/2}P_{1}+\mathrm{e}^{(-\mathrm{i}\omega_{0}-\nu_{0})t/2}P_{0}\right),

\mathcal{S}_{1}(t)[\rho]=\left(\mathrm{e}^{(-\mathrm{i}\omega_{1}-\nu_{1})t/2}P_{1}+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{1}t/2}P_{0}\right)\rho\left(\mathrm{e}^{(\mathrm{i}\omega_{1}-\nu_{1})t/2}P_{1}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{1}t/2}P_{0}\right).

So, the no-jump probability (
166
) turns out to be

\mathbb{P}[N(t)-N(t_{0})=0|\rho(t_{0}),x(k)]=\operatorname{Tr}\{P_{k}\rho(t_{0})\}\mathrm{e}^{-\nu_{k}t}+\operatorname{Tr}\{(\mathds{1}-P_{k})\rho(t_{0})\}.

This is a second example in which the waiting time of the next jump can be infinity with a non vanishing probability, as discussed after eqs. (
36
). Note that the initial state
X(0)=x(0)
and
\rho(0)=P_{1}
turns out to be a stationary state, because it is not possible to have jumps; similarly, also
X(0)=x(1)
and
\rho(0)=P_{0}
is a stationary state.
Analogously, the Lindblad rate equations (
161
) have not a unique stationary state, as was found in
[
79
,
80
]
.

(b)
H_{k}\propto\sigma_{x}
.

The second choice for the Hamiltonian is to take it proportional to a spin component orthogonal to
\sigma_{z}
, say

H_{k}=\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}\,\sigma_{x}\qquad\omega_{k}\in\mathbb{R},\quad\omega_{k}\neq 0,\quad k=0,\,1.

(168)

Then, the effective n-H Hamiltonian defined in (
160

H_{\rm eff}^{(k)}=-\frac{\mathrm{i}\nu_{k}}{4}\,\mathds{1}+K_{k},\qquad K_{k}=\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}\,\sigma_{x}-\frac{\mathrm{i}\tilde{\nu}_{k}}{4}\,\sigma_{z},\qquad\tilde{\nu}_{1}=\nu_{1},\quad\tilde{\nu}_{0}=-\nu_{0}.

(169)

The evolution generated by this effective Hamiltonian can be written as

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}H_{\rm eff}^{(k)}t}=\mathrm{e}^{-\nu_{k}t/4}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}K_{k}t}

and we can get an explicit expression for it by the techniques developed in Sec.
4.2
. Firstly, we have

K_{k}^{2}=\varkappa_{k}\mathds{1},\qquad\varkappa_{k}=\frac{1}{4}\left(\omega_{k}^{2}-\frac{\nu_{k}^{2}}{4}\right).

(170)

Remark 11
.

According to the value of
\varkappa_{k}
we have three cases:

1.

\left|\omega_{k}\right|>\nu_{k}/2
:
K_{k}
has two real eigenvalues
\pm z_{k}
,

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}K_{k}t}=\mathds{1}\cos(z_{k}t)-\frac{\mathrm{i}K_{k}}{z_{k}}\,\sin(z_{k}t);

(171)

2.

\left|\omega_{k}\right|=\nu_{k}/2

has two imaginary eigenvalues

7
Conclusions

By using the idea of quantum/classical hybrid systems, we have given a general formulation of quantum stochastic master equations of jump type (Sec.
2
). This formulation allows to include not only detection in continuous time, feedback, some forms of memory, but also other fields of research: non-Hermitian evolutions (Sec.
4
), piecewise evolutions interspersed by jump operators (Sec.
5
), Lindblad rate equation and continuous time open quantum walks (Sec.
6
). The key ingredients are the notions of typical trajectories and of waiting time of the next jump (Sec.
3
).

The notion of typical trajectory allows to introduce the “exclusive probability densities” (Sec.
3.3
), from which all the probability distributions involved in the theory can be obtained. Moreover, typical trajectories are involved also in the recursive construction of the solution of the quantum dynamics (Secs.
3.1
,
5.2
,
5.3
).

The notion of waiting time of the next jump is typical of a formulation based on counts (Sec.
3.2
) and we have used it as a unifying feature in all the applications. As for all probabilities involved in the theory, the waiting time distribution can be obtained by using positive operator-valued measures, as required in the general formulation of a quantum theory (Sec.
2.5.1
). These distributions appear as survival probabilities in the theory of non-Hermitian evolutions (Sec.
4.1
) and as starting point in the theory of a piecewise dynamics (Sec.
5.1
). In the final application to quantum/classical walks, we show how very general waiting time distributions can be obtained (Secs.
6.4.1
,
6.5
), despite the Markovian character of the composed hybrid system.
