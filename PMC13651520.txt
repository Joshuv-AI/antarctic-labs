Linking Oxygen Evolution
Selectivity to Water Oxidation
Reaction Order on Hematite Photoanodes by Operando Photoelectrochemical
Mass Spectrometry

Daniele Benetti, Soren B Scott, Shijie Yu, James Murawski, Caiwu Liang, Anna Winiwarter, Guangmeimei Yang, Andreas Kafizas, Anna Hankin, Ifan E L Stephens, James R Durrant, Flurin Eisner

Abstract

Water oxidation on metal-oxide (photo)­anodes is often
assumed to
yield molecular oxygen. Here, we combine operando photoelectrochemical
mass-spectrometry oxygen detection with spectroelectrochemical rate
law analyses to link oxygen selectivity to surface-hole density on
hematite photoanodes. At low current and hole densities, where first-order
kinetics dominate, a near-zero Faradaic efficiency for oxygen evolution
is observed, revealing that isolated single-hole water oxidation is
poorly coupled to molecular oxygen formation. Increasing surface-hole
density sharply increases O2 Faradaic efficiency, showing
that the kinetic crossover to cooperative third-order water oxidation
is also a selectivity switch toward O2 evolution. Equivalent
behavior under dark electrochemical operation shows that multihole
chemistry is intrinsic to hematite and governs selective oxygen evolution,
likely representing a general feature of semiconducting metaloxides.

Electrochemical (EC) and photoelectrochemical
(PEC) water splitting offer promising routes to green hydrogen, but
are limited by the sluggish water oxidation reaction.
,
 Based on electrochemical measurements alone, anodic current during
water oxidation is often assigned to molecular oxygen formation; indeed,
the reaction is conventionally referred to as the oxygen evolution
reaction (OER). However, direct operando quantification
of O2 and Faradaic efficiency (FE) remains relatively limited
for semiconducting metal-oxide photoanodes, especially at low reaction
rates where limited detection sensitivity can obscure the true oxygen-evolution
response.
,
 In these systems, water oxidation is governed
by the accumulation of photogenerated surface holes, with the reaction
rate often determined by the density of oxidizing equivalents rather
than applied potential alone.
,−

 As surface-hole density increases, distinct kinetic regimes have
been reported, from first-order behavior at low hole density to higher-order
kinetics at larger hole accumulation.
,,
 However, it remains unclear whether this change in
reaction order correlates with a change in oxygen evolution efficiency,
with most studies to date continuing to assume that the water oxidation
reaction is synonymous with oxygen evolution.

Hematite (α-Fe2O3) provides a model
case for this population-driven behavior. Spectroelectrochemical studies
have shown that increasing surface-hole accumulation drives a transition
from first-order kinetics to a third-order regime associated with
cooperative multihole catalysis.
,,,
 Under typical solar illumination,
hematite operates predominantly in the third-order regime, where the
rate scales with accumulated surface-hole density. However, direct molecular evidence linking this kinetic
transition to the actual formation of molecular oxygen is lacking.
It is not yet clear whether the low-order regime contributes significantly
to O2 production or instead corresponds predominantly to
alternative oxidation pathways such as hydroxyl-radical-like chemistry.
,,,

Previous studies have relied on optical or transient measurements
of hole populations to infer the mechanistic change, but these approaches
cannot determine whether the accumulated charge effectively drives
O–O bond formation and oxygen release, or drives alternative
reactions. Investigating this potential change in selectivity experimentally
has been challenging because photocurrent measurements alone cannot
distinguish between partial and complete oxidation processes.
,

Here, we address this challenge by undertaking operando photoelectrochemical mass-spectrometry (PEC-MS) on α-Fe2O3 photoanodes, which enables direct, real-time
quantification of molecular O2 with pmol·s–1 sensitivity under operating photoelectrochemical conditions, while preserving optical access, electrochemical
control, and illumination of the photoelectrode (a). By combining this new PEC-MS
capability with spectroscopic measurements of surface-hole accumulation,
we correlate FE with independently determined hole-density rate laws,
establishing a direct experimental link between surface charge accumulation,
reaction order, and molecular oxygen formation under both photoelectrochemical
and electrochemical conditions.

We identify a sharp threshold separating a regime
with minimal
oxygen formation from one in which oxygen evolution becomes the dominant
anodic process. This selectivity switch coincides with the spectroscopic
crossover from first- to third-order hole kinetics. The first-order
regime contributes little to molecular oxygen production, indicating
that it is dominated by alternative oxidation pathways, whereas efficient
oxygen evolution emerges only in the cooperative multihole regime.

To establish our operando approach, we couple
a newly designed light-compatible cell with an established EC-MS membrane-chip
platform, previously limited only to electrochemical systems (a). This configuration enables simultaneous monitoring of
applied potential, photocurrent, and molecular oxygen evolution from
an illuminated photoanode with subsecond (∼300 ms) response
time. b shows
a representative measurement at 1.4 VRHE under modulated
illumination of increasing intensity. The O2 signal (m/z = 32) increases systematically with
photon flux and follows the photocurrent response, demonstrating direct,
real-time detection of molecular oxygen during photoelectrochemical
operation. Potential-sweep measurements further validate the PEC-MS
response (Figure S1).

To investigate
how molecular oxygen evolution depends on the accumulated
surface-hole population, we tuned the operating conditions (i.e.,
light intensity and bias) to access different kinetic regimes (Figures S2–S3). Increasing photon flux
results in a proportional increase in both current and O2 production (Figure S3c), consistent with
operation in a population-controlled (band-edge-pinned) regime.

Figure S1), where holes accumulate predominantly in surface-localized states

∼80%, suggesting a change in the dominant surface reaction

measurement conditions, rather than an intrinsic limitation (see Supplementary Note 3).

To determine whether the relationship between multihole kinetics
and O2 selectivity is intrinsic to hematite surface chemistry
rather than specific to photoexcitation, we performed analogous measurements
under dark electrochemical conditions, where surface holes are generated
by anodic bias.
,
 The rate laws measured under
illumination and in the dark overlap closely across the full surface-hole-density
range (a),
indicating that the same cooperative multihole mechanism governs both
photoelectrochemical and electrochemical water oxidation.

Similar kinetic behavior has previously been reported
for other
oxide electrodes,
,
 in which valenceband holes are
generated in the dark via field-assisted band-to-band tunnelling (i.e.,
Zener mechanism) to drive water oxidation, indicating that this mechanism
may be general across metal-oxide photoelectrodes.
,,

The O2 FE in dark is then
measured as a function of
current density (Figure S6a). As observed
under illumination, the FE shows a transition from low to high efficiency,
indicating that the fraction of charge producing molecular oxygen
rises as the surface-hole population increases. Comparison with the
spectroscopically derived kinetic fractions shows that this increase
in O2 selectivity again follows the f
3 pathway (Figure S6b-c), demonstrating
that the same relationship between reaction order and oxygen formation
is preserved under dark electrochemical conditions. We then applied
the same FE correction used for the light condition to isolate the
oxygen-producing current density under dark conditions, J
OER_dark. The resulting rate law follows only the superlinear
dependence expected for cooperative multihole oxygen evolution, with
an approximately third-order dependence (b). Thus, under dark electrochemical conditions
as well, the disappearance of the low-order component in J
OER_dark shows that the first-order pathway contributes
little to molecular oxygen formation.

The mechanistic picture
emerging from these results is summarized
schematically in . Overall, the low FE observed in the first-order regime suggests
that this pathway corresponds to oxidation processes that do not efficiently
produce molecular oxygen. We have previously shown that at low surface-hole
densities, holes accumulate predominantly in surface trap states, where oxidation proceeds through isolated single-hole
reactions. Under these conditions, the reaction is likely limited
to early oxidation steps, where oxidation of water by Fe­(IV)O
sites forms hydroxyl-radical-like intermediates that may desorb or
undergo further oxidation in solution, diverting charge away from
direct O2 formation.
,,,
 These processes are characteristic
of the first-order regime and are consistent with the formation of
reactive radical intermediates observed in recent measurements under
similar regime.
,
 Because O–O bond formation
does not occur directly through these isolated single-hole pathways,
only a small fraction of the extracted charge results in molecular
oxygen, leading to the low FE measured at low current densities.

As the surface-hole population increases, these
trap states become
progressively filled (i.e., oxidized), allowing additional holes to accumulate in valence-band surface
states capable of participating directly in the OER. In this regime,
neighboring Fe–OH groups can interact cooperatively, enabling
multihole chemistry characteristic of the third-order kinetic regime.
Lateral coupling between adjacent surface intermediates facilitates
the formation of μ-oxo-bridged species, allowing concerted O–O
bond formation and efficient oxygen evolution.
,
 This cooperative mechanism is consistent with the spectroscopically
observed transition to third-order kinetics and coincides with the
region where the FE approaches its plateau value.

In summary,
operando PEC-MS and the FE analysis introduced here
provide the molecular counterpart to spectroscopic measurements of
hole accumulation, enabling direct, real-time O2 quantification
from a working photoanode. Combined with rate-law spectroscopy, this
links charge accumulation dynamics to catalytic oxygen formation.

The close correspondence between the emergence of the cooperative
multihole pathway and the increase in oxygen selectivity demonstrates
that efficient O2 evolution on hematite arises from cooperative
multihole chemistry at the oxide surface. In contrast, the first-order
regime contributes substantially to the measured anodic current, especially
at low light intensities/biases, but produces little molecular O2, consistent with competing pathways involving surface intermediates
or radical species without directly forming O2. The FE
analysis herein therefore reveals that the transition from first-
to third-order water-oxidation kinetics on hematite is also a transition
in oxygen-evolution selectivity. The observation that comparable kinetic
behavior is preserved under photoelectrochemical and electrochemical
conditions indicates that this mechanism is intrinsic to hematite
surface chemistry. Similar water-oxidation rate laws reported for
other metal oxides suggest that this O2-selectivity relationship
may extend beyond hematite, although further direct measurements are
required.
