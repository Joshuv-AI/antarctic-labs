 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 The project objective was to utilize two-phase heat transfer to enable compact and power-dense
 inverters. The high heat transfer capacity and near isothermal characteristics of two-phase
 cooling can enable higher power densities, reducing the size and cost of the power electronics
 system. This project focused on developing efficient, passive (no pump or compressor) two-
 phase cooling strategies that consist of an air-cooled condenser and evaporator.

 This report describes the modeling work conducted to design the evaporator and air-cooled
 condenser for a prototype inverter development work underway in the John Deere. Prototypes of
 the final evaporator and condenser designs were then fabricated via 3D printing for testing.
 Experiments on the evaporator confirmed that this component can meet the heat load and
 junction temperatures requirements for Si IGBT (insulated-gate bipolar transistor) inverter.
 Experiments with the air-cooled condenser were not possible due to leaks through the condenser
 walls.

 Task 1: Evaluate two-phase cooling of JDES’s planar inverter via modeling

 The National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) conducted finite element analysis (FEA) to
 model two-phase cooling of the Si IGBTs to be used in the prototype of JDES’s inverter. The
 objective was to evaluate the feasibility of using boiling heat transfer (two-phase cooling) to cool
 the inverter’s power devices, e.g. Si IGBTs. The FEA modeled one switch (due to symmetry)
 and included all the package/ substrate layers, including the interface layers (solders and thermal
 grease), and imposed heat transfer coefficients (HTCs) on the boiling surface to simulate boiling
 heat transfer. The boiling HTCs were varied to evaluate the effect of the two-phase cooling
 performance on the total heat dissipated. At each HTC value, the IGBT and diode heat values
 were adjusted to reach a maximum junction temperature of 150°C. The thermal modeling results
 were used to compute the HTC requirements to achieve the heat dissipation target value.

 Figure 1 shows the normalized heat dissipated (heat / target heat) at various boiling HTC values.
 Normalized heat values greater than one indicate that the condition exceeds the target heat
 dissipation. The results show that HTC values ≥45,000 W/m2-K can achieve the desired heat
 dissipation. This level of performance is well within the measured HTCs for boiling heat transfer
 (see Task 2 results). Moreover, the use of a copper substrate enables slightly higher heat
 dissipation as compared to using aluminum.

 Task 2: Select the refrigerant for the two-phase cooling system

 Refrigerants HFC-134a, HFC-245fa, HFO-1234yf, HFE-7100, and FC-72 were evaluated to
 determine the best coolant for this application. Boiling heat transfer performance (HTC and
 critical heat flux [CHF]), pressure versus temperature relationship, and refrigerant safety were
 factors considered when selecting the refrigerant. Figure 2 shows the CHF versus temperature
 curves for the five refrigerants. The graph on the left is the performance on smooth (plain)
 copper surfaces. The graph on the right uses a microporous boiling enhancement coating. As
 shown, the use of the coating significantly increases the CHF (i.e., the maximum heat flux that
 can be dissipated in the nucleate boiling regime) by 50%-140%. HFC-245fa was selected due to
 its relatively lower pressure and higher CHF at the expected operating temperature ranges (80°C-
 100°C).

 Task 3: Design and optimize the thermal performance via modeling

 Evaporator Design

 The evaporator (boiling component) was designed using FEA thermal modeling to dissipate the
 target heat load while keeping junction temperatures ≤150°C. Designing the evaporator to
 operate below the CHF was a challenge for this project due to the device’s high heat flux.
 Various evaporator concepts were developed to spread heat and decrease the heat flux at the
 boiling surface to operate below the CHF limit. The various concepts listed in Tables 1 and 2
 were modeled using FEA. Table 1 shows the results for the plain evaporator while Table 2 shows
 the results for the microporous-coated evaporator. Representative HTC values from Figure 3
 were imposed as boundary conditions to simulate boiling for plain and microporous-enhanced
 evaporators. Maximum heat loads were imposed to the devices (volumetric heat generation) to
 compute the maximum junction temperatures and boiling surface heat flux values. The boiling
 surface heat flux values were computed as the directional heat flux values normal to the boiling
 surface and were compared against experimentally measured CHF values (Figure 2). As shown
 in Table 1, the initial baseline case was predicted to produce heat flux values that were ~4 times
 greater than the CHF target value (well above the CHF limit, thus not a viable design).

 Task 4: Experimentally characterize the thermal performance of the two-phase cooling
 system
 Evaporator Experiments

 Prototypes of the final evaporator design were 3D printed to experimentally confirm the
 modeling results. Two evaporator prototypes were 3D printed out of copper using a selective
 laser sintering technique (Figure 6). A copper microporous boiling enhancement coating was
 applied to one prototype, and the other was left uncoated (plain). The coating was fabricated by
 Celsia Inc. and was applied by sintering micrometer-sized copper particles to the boiling surface.

 Figure 6. 3D printed evaporator prototypes (Photo Credit: Gilbert Moreno, NREL)

 An apparatus was designed and fabricated to test the performance of the two evaporators (Figure
 7). A liquid (water-ethylene glycol [WEG])-cooled aluminum condenser was fabricated and used
 to evaluate the evaporator performance. The condenser was used to condense vapor to a fluid
 that drained back to the evaporator via gravity. An aluminum manifold was fabricated and
 located between the evaporator and condenser. The manifold connected the evaporator to the
 condenser and contained multiple threaded ports. The threaded ports were used to install
 thermocouples to measure the refrigerant vapor and liquid temperatures, an absolute pressure
 transducer to measure vapor pressure, a pressure relief device, and a charging port.

 Condenser Experiments

 The air-cooled condenser design (Concept 3, Table 3) was fabricated out of aluminum via 3D
 printing (Figure 11). The condenser and the evaporators were to be assembled together to test the
 thermal performance of the entire two-phase cooling system (evaporator and air-cooled
 condenser). However, several leaks were detected on the condenser during initial hydrostatic
 pressure qualifications that prevented any further tests. The leaks are associated with 3D metal
 printing limitations that can form porous structures. NREL’s air test bench would have been used
 to provide the air flow across the air-cooled condenser (Figure 11). Both parties agreed it was
 acceptable to discontinue work on this element due to time and funding limits.
