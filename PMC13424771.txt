Death and grief literacy in the context of loneliness: Insights from an international symposium
Authors: Kerstin Kremeike, Bianca Neumann, David Blum, Allan Kellehear, Kerrie Noonan, Lilith Raza, Francis Seeck, Raymond Voltz, Els Van Wijngaarden, Julia Strupp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Death and grief literacy are increasingly recognised as crucial public health resources for responding to serious illness, dying, death, and bereavement. This critical essay summarises key discussions from a three-day international symposium, “Fostering Connection in Existential Phases of Life: How Do We Enhance Death and Grief Literacy?” (July 2025), which brought together experts from palliative care, caring communities, LGBTQ+ advocacy, migration studies, social work, volunteering, and psychosocial support. The symposium did not include members of the general public but focused on academic, clinical, and practice-based expertise. The symposium explored how enhancing death and grief literacy can improve individual and collective responses to loneliness, particularly among marginalised and vulnerable populations who are at higher risk of experiencing loneliness and poorer outcomes in the context of serious illness, dying, and bereavement, and foster deeper connections during life’s existential phases. Discussions addressed the relational, communal, and practical dimensions of death and grief literacy; the entanglement of loneliness and desire to die in severe illness; experiences of grief-related loneliness; and the potential of compassionate communities. Outcomes included concrete recommendations for policy, practice, and education, emphasising community-based support, cultural humility, public engagement, and the integration of death and grief literacy into curricula across multiple educational and professional settings, including school curricula, university education, and relevant workplace training programmes. Overall, we argue that strengthening death and grief literacy can shift societies from pathologising loneliness towards shared responsibility, solidarity, and compassionate care at the end of life and during bereavement.

Introduction

Despite growing international interest there is a lack of comprehensive comparative research on how cultural contexts shape death and grief literacy, including differences between Western and non-Western societies. Moreover, limited attention has been paid to the influence of factors such as religion, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and education on these forms of literacy. At the same time, loneliness at the end of life is highly prevalent among seriously ill and dying patients, and their carers, and social support and connection are key to alleviating it.1,2 Serious illness, dying, death, and grief often trigger fear, uncertainty, loneliness, and disconnection, pointing to gaps in how individuals and communities understand and respond to these experiences. Importantly, loneliness in these contexts has been shown to worsen physical symptoms, mental health, and overall well-being, 3 yet it remains under-researched. 4 We argue that these gaps are closely linked to limited levels of death and grief literacy, which shape how people recognise, communicate about, and respond to experiences of dying, loss, and social disconnection. In response to these intersecting gaps in research and practice, we developed an international and interdisciplinary symposium to explore how strengthening death and grief literacy might improve responses to loneliness in the context of serious illness, dying, and grief.

Methods

Following the symposium, a structured synthesis process was undertaken to translate the workshop and plenary discussions into the themes, research agenda, and policy recommendations presented in this essay. Detailed notes were taken during keynote sessions, World Café workshops, and plenary discussions by members of the organising and writing group. These notes were then collated and discussed in iterative writing meetings, where recurring ideas and cross-cutting issues were identified. Through a process of comparative discussion and refinement, these were organised into overarching themes that reflected the central dimensions of loneliness and death and grief literacy addressed during the symposium. The resulting research questions and policy recommendations were derived from this thematic synthesis and agreed upon by the author group.

Thematic sections

Compassionate communities initiatives shift the focus of end-of-life and bereavement care from purely clinical settings to community spaces, emphasising relational and local bonds. 5 Initiatives such as death cafés can support the development of these literacies by facilitating open conversations about end-of-life experiences and loss.6,7 The concepts of death and grief literacy are increasingly recognised as important tools for fostering such engagement. They provide a foundation for strengthening community-based support and collective responses, enabling more open conversations about death and enhancing support for individuals facing serious illness, dying, death, and bereavement.5,8

Death literacy refers to the knowledge, skills, and experiences that enable individuals and communities to understand and act on end-of-life and death care options. 8 It encompasses practical, experiential, factual, and community knowledge 7 and is considered a collective public health resource that builds capacity for caring. 8 Developing death literacy can enhance the ability of individuals and communities to comprehend and respond to end-of-life choices. Being death literate involves “living life well with death in mind and being equipped and prepared for when this becomes an acute part of our lives—to be ready for the when and not the if.” 9

Grief literacy refers to the capacity to access, process, and use knowledge about loss, encompassing knowledge, skills, and values. 5 While grief literacy broadly encompasses a wide range of significant losses beyond death and bereavement, this article focuses specifically on grief in the context of serious illness, dying, and bereavement. Bereaved individuals benefit from access to information about the grieving process and to compassionate, non-judgemental social support. 10

Together, these concepts informed discussions on our symposium questions and the formulation of political demands aimed at fostering greater solidarity in existential phases of life. The results of the four sessions are presented below.

Conceptual and experiential perspectives on loneliness and death literacy

This section introduces loneliness as a central theme and explores its conceptual and experiential dimensions in relation to death literacy.

Vulnerable populations and loneliness at the end of life

Building on the conceptual framing of loneliness and its relationship to death and grief literacy, the following section highlights how these dynamics are particularly pronounced among structurally vulnerable populations.

Loneliness is a significant public health concern, affecting roughly one in six people worldwide across all ages and social strata. 11 It becomes particularly pronounced in older age, during severe illness, and at the end of life, where it negatively impacts quality of life, health outcomes, and symptom burden. A substantial body of research has demonstrated these associations across palliative and geriatric care settings. 12 Vulnerable populations—including people with disabilities, refugees and migrants, LGBTQ+ individuals, and indigenous or ethnic minority groups—face structural and social disadvantages that heighten the risk of profound loneliness. These inequities are increasingly understood as being shaped by intersecting social, cultural, and structural determinants of health. 13 Loneliness can be understood as “a story of losses,” encompassing the loss of health, functional roles, and meaningful relationships. It is also relationally and socially constructed, reflecting disruptions in identity, belonging, and participation in social worlds. Social networks play a critical role: intact connections can support increased interaction, their absence or fragmentation may increase the risk of sustained and multidimensional forms of loneliness, with potential negative consequences for well-being and care outcomes. 14 Family caregivers also experience social, emotional, and existential loneliness, often hidden or stigmatised, which has received comparatively less attention in research and practice, highlighting the interplay of structural, social, and individual factors in shaping experiences of loneliness and informing research and policy priorities.

These findings underscore that addressing loneliness in these contexts requires not only structural and social interventions but also strengthening death and grief literacy, which can enable more inclusive, culturally sensitive, and responsive forms of support.

Enhancing death literacy through witnessing and a perceiving eye

Building on loneliness and its structural, social, and individual dimensions, Els van Wijngaarden examined more closely the concept of death literacy. She emphasised that death literacy involves not only access to knowledge about end-of-life care and dying processes but also the capacity to engage meaningfully with death and dying as both a personal and shared reality. Despite growing awareness, many individuals struggle to articulate their own mortality, dying wishes, or suffering, particularly when these experiences are emotionally or existentially complex. Similarly, it can be difficult for people to listen to and explore the dying experiences of their family members, friends, or other significant persons. A striking illustration of this challenge is provided by the story of Mrs. de Beaufort, a 99-year-old woman interviewed as part of a study on death wishes. 15 Her narrative, shown in Box 1, exemplifies the silent erosion of the self under the cumulative weight of loss, grief, and existential loneliness.

Box 1.Narrative on the erosion of the self under the weight of cumulative loss, grief, and existential loneliness.Mrs. de Beaufort, formerly a successful interior fabric designer, lives in a nursing home that reflects her lifelong aesthetic sensibility. Yet despite these markers of identity, she experiences her environment as alienating. Her story is not merely one of physical decline, but of relational and existential displacement. Her days are marked by the loss of independence, sensory deterioration, and chronic, untreatable pain. These are not isolated losses, but part of a cumulative and deeply destabilising process: she can no longer read, travel, or shop; her connection with her children, who live abroad, has weakened; and she feels estranged from her own body, which she describes as a source of constant discomfort and betrayal. Even seemingly minor disruptions—such as the inability to maintain the order and beauty she once valued in her home—become poignant reminders of who she used to be and what she can no longer control. She remarks, “This isn’t me anymore. I’m no longer myself.” What remains is a blurred version of a once-vivid life, where her sense of personhood is no longer mirrored in daily routines or recognised by others. Her nights are dominated by a wish to die: “Every night I say, ‘Dear God, please take me’.” Yet these experiences are described as rarely met with understanding among the health professionals around her, highlighting perceived gaps in communication and relational care at the end of life. 16

Narrative on the erosion of the self under the weight of cumulative loss, grief, and existential loneliness.

This case illustrates what might be termed death illiteracy: a cultural and clinical difficulty or even inability to recognise or respond adequately to the existential suffering expressed by older adults at the end of life. The tendency in care contexts to view such suffering through the lens of diagnostic frameworks (e.g. distinguishing “normal” from “complicated” grief) often misses the lived texture of the experience. In Mrs. de Beaufort’s case, grief is not a discrete event but an ongoing, multi-layered experience: it is cumulative, anticipatory, and ambiguous. Her physical suffering is accompanied by a loss of recognition—by others and of self—and a sense of being disconnected from the world.

To enhance death literacy, it is important that professionals develop not only clinical competencies but also an explorative and phenomenological attitude. 17 Drawing on the distinction made by nurse philosopher Kari Martinsen, this requires cultivating what she calls the “perceiving eye”: a form of attention that listens without rushing to interpret, that is receptive to the other’s world rather than diagnostic. 18 In contrast to the “recording eye,” which categorises and explains, the perceiving eye allows for witnessing; a mode of relational presence that affirms the other’s suffering as real, shared, and meaningful. 19

Mrs. de Beaufort’s experience turns out not totally devoid of consolation. She is in contact with a nun who assists her with groceries and laundry and also calls weekly, asking the same question again and again: “Tell me, how are you?” and then listens attentively to her response. This simple act of presence and repetition offers more than just support: it restores a sense of being seen. In this gesture lies the ethical heart of care at the end of life.

This illustrates that enhancing death literacy entails more than public education or policy reform; it also calls for a cultural and relational reorientation; towards openness, attentiveness, and the willingness to witness. Stories like Mrs. de Beaufort’s are not only accounts of suffering; they are invitations to a deeper kind of engagement, and ultimately, to more humane care. 19

In this sense, death literacy is not only an individual competence, but a relational and cultural practice that can mitigate experiences of loneliness by fostering recognition, communication, and shared meaning-making.

Structural and social inequalities

Building on these perspectives, the following contributions highlight how structural and social inequalities shape experiences of loneliness, grief, and end-of-life care.

Classism, poverty, and the politics of griefability

Building on the focus on death literacy and the personal and relational challenges of engaging with mortality, Francis Seeck shifted attention to the structural dimensions of loneliness and grief. In their keynote, “Classism, Poverty, and the Politics of Griefability,” Seeck highlighted how social inequality shapes experiences of loss, bereavement, and isolation. It was emphasised that factors such as socioeconomic status, class, and poverty not only influence access to care and support but also affect how grief is perceived, acknowledged, and legitimised within society. By linking individual experiences of loneliness and grief to broader structural conditions, Seeck’s contribution complemented earlier discussions and underscored the need for public health strategies that address both personal and systemic determinants of social isolation and vulnerability.

Social inequality permeates all areas of life and extend beyond death itself. From the perspective of classism and poverty research, burial and mourning practices not only reflect but actively reproduce structural inequalities. Classism refers to discrimination based on class origin or class position.20 –22 It operates through exclusion, and marginalisation, denying people access to resources, participation, respect, and recognition.23,24 Classism targets people from working-class or poor backgrounds, their descendants, including those who are low-income, unemployed, or homeless. It limits access to housing, education, healthcare, power, recognition, and money. 25

The effects are starkly visible in mortality data. In Germany, the life expectancy gap at birth between the lowest and highest income groups is 4.4 years for women and 8.6 years for men. 26 A Danish study found that homelessness reduces life expectancy by 21.6 years for men and 17.4 years for women. 27 Classism also shapes the conditions under which people are buried and mourned. In Germany, burial is legally required and typically the responsibility of heirs or legally obligated relatives. When no relatives can be located, municipal authorities arrange statutory burials. These are often anonymous, without ceremony, flowers, or a named grave as described in Box 2.

Box 2.Statutory burials in Germany.Sörries describes dawn mass interments of urns attended only by a gravedigger, sometimes conducted far from the deceased’s last place of residence to save costs. 28 If relatives are located but unable to pay for a funeral, social welfare burials are arranged. Since the abolition of the statutory funeral grant in 2000, the number of social welfare burials has risen sharply. Funeral research anticipates that such numbers will continue to rise alongside growing poverty rates. 29 These burial arrangements exemplify what Schneider terms the neoliberalisation of dying, in which individuals are increasingly expected to plan and finance their own funerals as part of a “life-end project.” 30 Municipalities tender burial contracts to the lowest bidder, with requirements varying from a minimal ceremony and floral decoration to what critics call a “disposal mentality.” Such conditions, Sörries argues, have led to “an ever-growing number of the dead who are no longer mourned.” 28

Historically and today, social movements have resisted these exclusions and claimed the right to be mourned. During the AIDS crisis, AIDS Coalition to Unleash Power (ACT UP) activists in the United States and Germany challenged stigma by making dying and mourning public through die-ins and symbolic funeral marches. 31 The hospice movement sought to de-bureaucratise dying, driven by civil society actors confronting what Möllers calls the “expropriated death.” 32 Since the 1990s, initiatives in Germany have emerged to ensure dignified funerals for marginalised people. Hospices and homeless services also raise funds for named burials and hold their own mourning rituals. Central to these counter-practices is the insistence on naming the dead. Names are spoken at funerals, engraved on headstones, and published on institutional websites. For those deemed “unmournable” in life, naming becomes an act of reclamation. As Butler notes: “Even the utterance of the name can come as the most extraordinary form of recognition, especially when one has become nameless or when one’s name has been replaced by a number.” 33 In the context of classism, such practices are more than gestures of compassion; they are acts of resistance against structural erasure. They challenge funeral systems that replicate social hierarchies and make visible the enduring inequality of who is remembered, how, and by whom. The politics of mournability thus illuminate the intersection of economic deprivation, social exclusion, and the struggle for dignity in death.

Living between borders: Diversity, transgender loneliness, and end-of-life care

Building on the discussion of structural inequalities and the politics of grief, Lilith Raza turned the focus to the intersection of cultural and gender diversity with experiences of loneliness at the end of life. In her keynote, “Living Between Borders: Cultural and Gender Diversity, Loneliness, and End-of-Life Care,” Raza highlighted how social, cultural, and gendered factors shape access to support, the experience of isolation, and engagement with end-of-life care. Drawing on her perspective as a transgender woman from Punjab, Pakistan, she emphasised the unique vulnerabilities faced by marginalised communities, and how culturally sensitive approaches are crucial for fostering connection, recognising grief, and providing compassionate care. Her insights complemented previous discussions by linking structural, cultural, and personal dimensions of loneliness and grief.

The narrative begins not at the end of life, but amidst survival. At 16, Lilith Raza became aware that her identity differed from that of her male peers. Lacking the language to describe herself, she found the term “transgender” insufficient. In Punjab, Pakistan, this difference carried profound social stigma: members of the hijra community were perceived as cursed, unnatural, and destined to die in isolation. Hijra and Khwaja Sira communities are culturally specific gender-diverse communities in South Asia, often recognised as a third gender, with long-standing social and cultural roles but also facing significant stigma and marginalisation. This early experience shaped her understanding of loneliness not as a mere emotion, but as an existential reality.

Her life story, like that of many transgender people, is marked by paradoxes—visibility and erasure, resilience and vulnerability, belonging and exile. It illustrates loneliness as structurally produced, influenced by legal frameworks, cultural norms, and migration processes. Community resilience, expressed through chosen families and kinship networks, emerges as a vital remedy to social death, while displacement and border regimes exacerbate isolation, leaving traces of political erasure even beyond death.

For many transgender individuals, loneliness is experienced from early life as a seemingly unchangeable destiny, intensified by family and community rejection and persisting even after death. 34 This reflects the structural violence described by Farmer, in which laws, healthcare systems, and cultural norms fail to affirm gender identity, producing systemic harm rather than individual deficiency. 35 Historically, Hijra and Khwaja Sira communities established care practices to ensure that no individual died alone, highlighting the protective role of chosen families and communal solidarity.36,37 However, migration and displacement fracture these networks, and in exile, transgender individuals may die unacknowledged, with misrecorded identities and without recognition from family or state—a form of political erasure that underscores the need for recognition both in life and death.34,38,39

The findings highlight that loneliness among transgender individuals, particularly those displaced across borders, is a structural rather than merely emotional phenomenon. While communities provide resilience through chosen families and kinship networks, displacement and migration can dismantle these support systems, producing social and political erasure. For palliative care, this underscores that affirming identity and supporting chosen families is as vital as managing physical symptoms. Cultural, gendered, and migratory factors—including social rejection, structural violence, and contested recognition—shape experiences of loneliness, while community solidarity offers protection against social death. These insights call for culturally and gender-sensitive palliative care models that acknowledge transgender migrants’ expertise over their own lives and deaths. Theoretically, this aligns with intersectionality, the politics of recognition, and necropolitics, as well as relational and sociological accounts of loneliness that conceptualise it as a deficit of meaningful recognition rather than mere social contact.34,40 –42

These structural conditions highlight that enhancing death and grief literacy must also involve critical engagement with social inequalities, ensuring that literacy is accessible and relevant across diverse social and cultural contexts.

Implications for care, community, and practice

The following contributions focus on practical implications, illustrating how loneliness can be addressed through clinical care, community-based approaches, and public health strategies.

Loneliness and the desire to die

Building on the public health framing of loneliness, the symposium explored its existential and clinical dimensions, with Raymond Voltz highlighting the strong interplay between loneliness and the desire to die in the context of severe illness, dying, death, and grief. He emphasised that loneliness intensifies vulnerability, shapes meaning-making at the end of life, and can transform existential distress into a wish to escape suffering. The desire to die is understood as a spectrum of experiences, ranging from passive thoughts of not wanting to wake up to acute suicidal ideation, with multifaceted psychosocial causes and functions. 43 Loneliness magnifies feelings of disconnection, burdensomeness, and existential despair, underscoring the importance of relational and community support alongside medical care. Voltz highlighted that open, empathetic conversations are crucial first interventions, allowing individuals to articulate and reflect on their feelings while helping clinicians, volunteers, and caregivers understand underlying needs. 44 Concrete community-based strategies, such as the Buddy Project—which pairs trained volunteers with seriously ill or isolated individuals to provide regular social contact, emotional support, and a stable relational anchor—demonstrate how low-threshold interventions can alleviate loneliness, strengthen social connectedness, and enhance subjective well-being, particularly for those lacking strong social networks or fearing to burden their families. 45 These insights bridge clinical, relational, and societal perspectives, illustrating how addressing loneliness is key to reducing desire-to-die experiences and supporting holistic end-of-life care.

Loneliness in the digital age at end of life

Building on discussions of structural, cultural, and gendered dimensions of loneliness, the symposium then explored how technological contexts shape social connection at the end of life. David Blum highlighted the role of digital technologies in addressing loneliness, noting that their impact varies depending on platform and mode of engagement. 46 While passive consumption, such as on YouTube, may exacerbate isolation, interactive tools like WhatsApp can foster meaningful connection. 47 Emerging digital health interventions—including smartphone apps, wearable devices, and adaptations of dignity therapy—show promise for supporting patients’ sense of safety and connectedness, but should complement rather than replace personal interaction. 46 This perspective underscores the multifactorial nature of loneliness and the potential of technology to enhance, rather than substitute, relational care at the end of life.

Compassionate communities and loneliness

Building on the discussion of loneliness and the desire to die, Allan Kellehear explored the role of compassionate communities in addressing loneliness at the end of life, while also highlighting common misconceptions in public discourse. He noted that “dying alone” is often interpreted as a sign of abandonment or systemic failure, although in reality, only a small fraction of deaths occur in complete isolation. 48 Loneliness, he argued, cannot be reduced to mere solitude or lack of social contact; it is experienced in relation to meaningful connections, autonomy, and the quality of relationships. 48 Many individuals, particularly older adults, value selective companionship, privacy, and agency, and may perceive institutional interventions as intrusive or even threatening. 49 Kellehear emphasised that compassionate community initiatives should be designed as reciprocal and participatory systems, where support is co-shaped by individuals and communities rather than imposed unilaterally. This approach recognises that solitude can represent choice, dissent, or resistance, rather than deprivation, and stresses that public health strategies cannot guarantee well-being but can provide supportive frameworks for those seeking connection. By situating loneliness within social, relational, and structural contexts, Kellehear’s insights underscore the need for nuanced, dignity-respecting interventions that foster meaningful engagement at the end of life, complementing clinical care and community-based support.

Fostering social connection and collective action

Can death and grief literacy be viewed through a lens of connection and action? Kerrie Noonan explored these concepts by grounding them in lived experience and research, showing how end-of-life and bereavement experiences can be shaped by social connections. This presentation highlighted common structural barriers that can impact connection at end of life. This included the distance between the family home and hospital, physical strain on older family members, restrictive visiting hours, reduced privacy and family comforts, and fragmented care. These common institutional care experiences expose how easily care can be undermined. Connection, however, is frequently actively created by family caregivers through social networks external to the hospital or health system. Noonan shared a personal experience of a funeral director providing transport for dying family member, when the hospital was unable to respond in a timely way. This funeral director, provided additional support and care, and then subsequently provided the funeral service. Caring at home both supported and facilitated connection. Friends, family, pets, meals, facetime calls all happened around the acts of end-of-life and death care at home. This facilitated relational supports and social connections. Informed by social network research at end of Noonan posits that social acts of caring, can enhance connectedness and support the development of death and grief literacy. 50 In fully integrated community responses to end of life, people can draw upon shared knowledge and co-produce care and share knowledge and skills. Where such integration does not exist naturally, public health palliative care approaches such as compassionate communities can have a role in facilitating connection, collective action, and more compassionate care.

Policy recommendations and practical implications.

The symposium’s contributions highlight loneliness as a multidimensional, structurally shaped experience across serious illness, dying, and bereavement. It emerges at the intersection of social, cultural, healthcare, and political conditions. The following discussion examines these findings and their implications for public health palliative care, death and grief literacy, and compassionate community initiatives.

Discussion

Loneliness at the end of life is often framed as an individual psychological state, yet the symposium highlighted its profound social, cultural, and structural dimensions. What appears private frequently reflects broader societal arrangements described in the literature: fragmented care systems, precarious living conditions, migration histories, unrecognised identities, and eroded communal ties. 51 Treating loneliness solely as an emotional-deficit risks overlooking these political and relational landscapes.

Participants emphasised that loneliness intensifies when people feel unseen—when identities, relationships, or forms of belonging are unacknowledged by institutions or communities. For migrants, trans people, and those living in poverty, this invisibility is structurally produced. End-of-life loneliness, therefore, is not simply “being without others” but experiencing the withdrawal of recognition. At the same time, solitude is not inherently harmful. Many value autonomy, quietness, or selective contact, which requires compassionate communities to cultivate support that respects agency while facilitating meaningful connection. This distinction between loneliness and chosen solitude is well established, yet remains insufficiently integrated into end-of-life care practices. 52 This raises an ongoing tension: how can practitioners distinguish between harmful isolation and meaningful aloneness without imposing normative assumptions?

Death and grief literacy emerged as a key pathway for enabling supportive environments. Literacy—understood as encompassing shared knowledge, skills, values, language, and cultural competence—reduces stigma, encourages earlier conversations, and strengthens collective responsibility. Yet literacy alone is insufficient without humility, reciprocity, and active witnessing. Enhancing literacy also means addressing disparities in access shaped by culture, education, and socioeconomic status. Public dialogue, professional training, and valuing lived experience are critical to normalising conversations about loneliness, dying, and grief.

Workshop participants described loneliness as feeling “broken,” “alien,” or “invisible,” highlighting a paradox of being “together but lonely.” The metaphor of a “journey from loneliness to hope” captures this trajectory: the Valley of Loneliness (social stigma, neglect), the River of Loss (grief, erosion of support), the Bridge of Connection (trust, community care), and the Hill of Hope (dignity, recognition, belonging). Such imagery reframes loneliness as a collective concern and opens space for empathy and action.

A distinction between loneliness and aloneness emerged as essential. While loneliness carries distress and disconnection, aloneness can be reflective, supportive of integration, and meaningful, particularly during end-of-life phases. Recognising this prevents pathologising necessary or chosen solitude. Nevertheless, this distinction is fluid and context-dependent, requiring ongoing relational interpretation rather than fixed categorisation.

Participants stressed the influence of structural positioning on loneliness. Micro-level dynamics (family, identity), meso-level environments (community, care systems), and macro-level forces (policy, culture, inequality) interact to shape experiences of connection and marginalisation. Understanding loneliness as a spectrum—from introspective solitude to harmful isolation—underscores the need for context-sensitive responses. While such multi-level frameworks are well established in public health and social theory, their application to end-of-life loneliness remains underdeveloped and warrants further empirical and conceptual work.

Symposium discussions also highlighted the centrality of reciprocity. Genuine connection thrives when care is mutual: shared vulnerability and recognition foster dignity, agency, and belonging. Professional and informal carers alike benefit from these reciprocal dynamics, yet current systems often overlook them. This reflects broader critiques of care systems that prioritise efficiency over relationality. 53 Embedding reciprocity into education, community practice, and policy shifts the focus from deficits to relational growth.

Finally, the symposium urged a move from individual responsibility to structural compassion. Loneliness must be recognised as a societal issue requiring collective action. This involves reframing palliative care as a shared task, integrating cultural and community-based approaches, and fostering cross-sector collaboration. By recognising loneliness as a shared human experience—particularly during life’s most vulnerable phases—society can work towards ensuring that no one feels unseen, unheard, or abandoned. However, translating this call for collective responsibility into practice remains a key challenge, requiring clearer pathways for implementation, accountability, and evaluation.

Implications for clinical practice and research

The findings of this symposium also have important implications for clinical practice. Addressing loneliness in the context of serious illness, dying, and bereavement requires clinicians to move beyond purely symptom-focused approaches and to recognise relational and existential dimensions of care. This includes fostering open communication about loneliness and desire to die, acknowledging diverse social contexts, and engaging with patients’ social networks and communities.

From a research perspective, further work is needed to better understand the mechanisms, trajectories, and interventions related to loneliness in end-of-life contexts. In particular, there is a need for interdisciplinary and participatory approaches that integrate perspectives from marginalised populations and examine the role of death and grief literacy in shaping care practices and outcomes.

Conclusion

The symposium underscores that loneliness at the end of life is a multifaceted phenomenon shaped by structural, cultural, and relational factors. Future research must adopt intersectional, cross-cultural, and participatory approaches to better understand the trajectories, mechanisms, and protective factors of loneliness, particularly for marginalised populations such as migrants, transgender individuals, and those experiencing poverty. Policy efforts should recognise loneliness as a public health priority, strengthen community-based and culturally sensitive support systems, and integrate death and grief literacy across societal institutions.

Ultimately, addressing loneliness requires a shift from individualised solutions towards collective responsibility: building societies that affirm belonging, nurture relational connection, and uphold the humanity of all in life’s most vulnerable phases.
