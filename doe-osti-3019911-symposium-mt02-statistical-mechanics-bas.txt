1. Symposium Overview and Objectives
Symposium MT02 brought together a diverse and interdisciplinary community of scientists
specializing in Statistical Mechanics-based computational modeling to investigate phase
transformations in materials exhibiting complex disordered structures. As the demand for
materials with extreme performance metrics grows—from aerospace components to
next-generation optical fibers—the ability to predict microstructural evolution under
non-equilibrium conditions has become paramount. The primary goal of this symposium was
to identify, evaluate, and discuss advanced computational tools capable of designing precise
manufacturing conditions to tailor material properties efficiently. By fostering a dialogue
between computational theorists and experimentalists, the symposium sought to establish
new protocols for predicting how processing history—such as cooling rates or strain
paths—dictates the final microstructure.

The scientific scope of the symposium was meticulously categorized into two main areas to
address different manifestations of disorder:
 1.​ Complex Materials with Intrinsic Disorder: This category focused on systems where
 disordered structures occur naturally under ambient thermodynamic conditions due to
 their chemical complexity. Key examples discussed included high-entropy alloys (HEAs),
 where configurational entropy stabilizes solid solution phases against intermetallic
 compound formation, and ZBLAN glasses, which require careful suppression of
 crystallization to maintain their optical purity.
 2.​ Imposed Glassy Transitions: This category examined materials driven into disordered
 states via overdriven, non-equilibrium conditions. These include systems subjected to
 rapid cooling rates (as seen in additive manufacturing processes like laser powder bed
 fusion) or material deformation at the wake of a shock wave. In these scenarios, the
 material is kinetically trapped in a high-energy state, leading to unique properties not
 accessible through conventional processing.
A central theme of the symposium was the accommodation of deformation through
mechanisms such as displacive phase transformation and deformation twinning. Particular
attention was paid to the role of strain rate and temperature history in inducing these

mechanisms, highlighting the critical need for multiscale models that can bridge the gap
between atomic fluctuations and macroscopic continuum behavior.

Key Scientific Topics
The technical program focused on a series of advanced topics that represent the frontier of
computational materials science:
 ●​ Disordered Phase Evolution: Detailed studies on crystallization pathways from the melt,
 covering the kinetics of rapid solidification and the devitrification stability of fragile
 glasses.
 ●​ Advanced Theoretical Frameworks: The application and extension of rigorous
 frameworks such as Dynamical Density Functional Theory (DDFT), Phase-Field Crystal
 (PFC) models, and Phase-Field (PF) models specifically adapted for systems operating
 far from equilibrium.
 ●​ Nucleation Dynamics: Investigations into the stochastic nature of homogeneous and
 heterogeneous nucleation within glassy disordered media, which is critical for controlling
 grain structure and defect density.
 ●​ Thermodynamics of Complexity: Fundamental discussions on the role of entropy in
 complex, multi-component materials, challenging traditional Gibbsian thermodynamics in
 favor of statistical approaches better suited for rough energy landscapes.
 ●​ AI-Driven Modeling: The integration of Artificial Intelligence to enable multiscale
 modeling for phase transformation in overdriven systems, moving from simple data-fitting
 to physics-informed predictive agents that respect conservation laws.
 ●​ Extreme Environments: The development of robust atomistic models for shock-induced
 phase transitions and the kinetics of interfacial structural transformation under
 high-pressure and high-strain-rate regimes.

2. Summary of Presentations and Program Highlights
The symposium featured a robust technical program spanning multiple days, organized into
distinct sessions. Invited talks anchored the sessions, providing high-level overviews of critical
advancements in the field while setting the stage for contributed talks and posters.

Invited Speaker Highlights
 ●​ MT02.01: Extreme Conditions I​
 Avinash Dongare (University of Connecticut) opened the discussion on extreme
 environments with his presentation, "Understanding the Links Between Phase
 Transformation and Twinnability in Fe-Based BCC Metal Microstructures." His work
 offered critical insights into the atomic-level failure mechanisms of armor and structural
 materials. By correlating shock conditions with specific microstructural responses, his
 team provided a roadmap for designing alloys that can better withstand high strain rates
 through controlled twinning and phase transformation.
 ●​ MT02.02: Extreme Conditions II​

 Leonid Zhigilei (University of Virginia) presented "Dynamics of Laser-Induced Nanoscale
 Phase Decomposition," a talk that bridged the gap between simulation and experiment.
 He demonstrated the validation of atomistic modeling using time-resolved optical and
 X-ray probing, showing how short-pulse lasers can induce unique phase decomposition
 pathways not accessible through thermal processing. Paulo Branicio (University of
 Southern California) followed with "Atomistic Design Maps for Growth-Twinning and
 Amorphization in Ni-Based and High-Entropy Alloy Thin Films," providing quantitative
 "design maps" that allow engineers to predict whether a film will fail via amorphization or
 deform plastically via twinning based on composition and deposition conditions.
●​ MT02.03: Amorphization & Glass Transition (ZBLAN Focus)​
 This session featured a comprehensive, multi-perspective look at the processing of
 ZBLAN (ZrF4-BaF2-LaF3-AlF3-NaF) glasses, effectively bridging fundamental theory and
 space-based manufacturing:
 ○​ Heike Ebendorff-Heidepriem (University of Adelaide) delivered a keynote on
 "ZBLAN Glass Processing in Microgravity." She reviewed recent breakthroughs
 achieved on the International Space Station (ISS) in collaboration with Flawless
 Photonics, where an impressive 11.9 km of fiber was drawn. Her talk provided a
 compelling demonstration of how the unique environment of microgravity suppresses
 convection-induced ion mobility. On Earth, these convection currents drive
 crystallization defects that act as scattering centers, severely limiting the optical
 transmission of ZBLAN fibers. The space-based experiments confirmed that
 eliminating these convective flows preserves the amorphous state, enabling the
 production of fibers with near-theoretical transmission limits.
 ○​ Tomorr Haxhimali (Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory) complemented the
 experimental results with "Crystal Nucleation and Growth in FluoroZirconate (ZBLAN)
 Glasses," presenting mesoscopic phase-field studies. He detailed how inertial effects
 and convection flow reduce local viscosity at high shear rates, thereby expediting
 nucleation events and narrowing the working temperature range—a theoretical
 finding that strongly supports the rationale for space-based manufacturing.
 ○​ Damien Tourret (IMDEA Materials Institute) broadened the scope with "Phase-Field
 Insights into Microstructure Selection During Rapid Solidification of Metallic Alloys,"
 discussing how competitive growth between phases determines the final
 microstructure in additive manufacturing scenarios.
●​ MT02.04: Entropy & Thermodynamics
 ○​ Peter Voorhees (Northwestern University) discussed "Nonequilibrium Interfaces:
 Thermodynamics and Phase Field Methods," focusing on the mathematical treatment
 of moving boundaries in multi-component alloys.
 ○​ Mogadalai Gururajan (IIT Bombay) presented "Phase-Field Modeling of Domain
 Self-Organization in Ferroelectric Thin Films." Using a Ginzburg-Landau-Devonshire
 free energy formalism, he demonstrated how the competition between long-range
 electrostatic interactions and elastic constraints leads to the spontaneous
 subdivision of domains into fine stripes and fractals to minimize depolarization
 energy, a critical factor for next-generation memory devices.

 ●​ MT02.05: Data-Driven & AI Models​
 Akinori Yamanaka (Tokyo University of Agriculture and Technology) presented "Bayesian
 Data Assimilation-Integrated Phase-Field Simulation of Solid-State Sintering." This
 approach represents a shift towards "digital twins," where experimental data is fed into
 the simulation in real-time to correct trajectory. Tapio Ala-Nissila (Aalto University)
 discussed "Multiscale Modeling of Atomistic Materials: From Machine-Learned Molecular
 Dynamics to Phase-Field Crystal Models," showcasing how ML can generate interatomic
 potentials with DFT-level accuracy at a fraction of the cost. Zhi-Feng Huang (Wayne
 State University) presented "Grain Dynamics in Polycrystalline Systems with Even and
 Odd Elasticity," exploring novel mechanical responses in active matter systems.
 ●​ MT02.06: Solid-Solid Phase Transformation​
 Katsuyo Thornton (University of Michigan) presented "Phase-Field Modeling of
 Microstructural Evolution in Highly Deformed Polycrystals," elucidating how stored energy
 in grain boundaries drives recrystallization. Moneesh Upmanyu (Northeastern University)
 closed the invited sessions with "Restructuring of Emergent Grain Boundaries as a
 Non-Conservative Deformation Mechanism in Polycrystalline Thin Films," proposing that
 grain boundaries act as active thermodynamic defects that can absorb or emit vacancies
 during deformation.

Highlights of Student Contributions
The symposium also highlighted the work of promising early-career scientists:
 ●​ Daniel Xiao (MIT) presented "From Atoms to Alloys: Direct Atomistic Prediction of
 Complex Phase Diagrams using Hybrid MD/MC." His hybrid approach offers a new
 computational pathway to predict phase stability across vast compositional spaces
 without relying on extensive experimental databases.
 ●​ Jason Ogbebor (MIT) discussed "Efficient Configurational Entropy Calculation Via Monte
 Carlo Thermodynamic Integration," addressing a key challenge in the thermodynamic
 modeling of complex alloys: accurately quantifying the entropic contribution to free
 energy.

3. Contributions to the Discipline
The symposium successfully highlighted several critical advancements that represent a
paradigm shift in the field of computational materials science:
 1.​ Integration of Machine Learning with Physics-Based Models: A recurring and
 dominant theme was the move beyond pure data mining toward "physics-informed" AI.
 The community is actively developing workflows where Machine Learning potentials are
 trained "on-the-fly" to handle complex phase transformations. This allows simulations to
 access time and length scales previously unattainable, bridging the gap between
 atomistic accuracy and mesoscale phenomena.
 2.​ Understanding Metastability for Manufacturing: Several talks provided new strategies
 for the kinetic stabilization of metastable phases. This contribution is crucial for the field

 of additive manufacturing, where rapid cooling rates lock in non-equilibrium structures.
 Understanding the stability maps of these phases allows engineers to design heat
 treatments that retain superior mechanical properties (such as high strength and
 ductility) that would be lost at equilibrium.
 3.​ Advancements in Entropy Calculation: The presentation of new methods for efficient
 configurational entropy calculation allows for better prediction of phase diagrams for
 multi-component alloys. This solves a longstanding bottleneck in the design of
 High-Entropy Alloys, where the sheer number of possible element combinations makes
 experimental exploration impossible.

4. Future Directions
Discussions throughout the symposium pointed toward several key future research directions
that will likely define the field in the coming years:
 ●​ Real-Time Data Assimilation: There is a strong push to move simulations from being
 purely "predictive" to "adaptive." Future work will focus on integrating in-situ
 experimental data (e.g., from synchrotron light sources or electron microscopy) directly
 into running simulations. This "data assimilation" will allow models to self-correct in
 real-time, greatly increasing their accuracy for complex manufacturing processes.
 ●​ Bridging Scales in Extreme Environments: Developing robust workflows that
 seamlessly pass information from quantum mechanical calculations to mesoscale
 phase-field models remains a priority. This is particularly needed for predicting failure
 mechanisms in materials subjected to extreme radiation or shock, where quantum effects
 at the crack tip influence macroscopic fracture.
 ●​ Generative Material Design: The ultimate goal is to use the developed statistical
 mechanics tools not just to analyze existing materials, but to "inversely design"
 microstructures. Researchers aim to define a desired performance metric (e.g., specific
 conductivity or strength) and use generative algorithms to identify the required starting
 composition and processing path.
 ●​ A Need for a Deeper Theoretical Understanding of Glassy Transition: Reaching out
 to the community of Statistical Physics in the field of Spin Glasses and more generally in
 the Complexity studies.
To further enhance this collaboration, we intend to organize additional symposiums soon and
publish a bulletin featuring the invited and contributed talks.

Conclusion:
The ARO funding was instrumental in the success of Symposium MT02. It enabled the
participation of high-caliber invited speakers and industry experts while supporting students
in presenting cutting-edge research. The event successfully identified the current state of the

art and charted a course for future research in statistical mechanics-based tools for complex
materials, ensuring that the community remains at the forefront of materials design and
discovery.
