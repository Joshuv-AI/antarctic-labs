Suspected carotid stent-graft infection presenting as recurrent pseudomonas aeruginosa bacteremia after oncologic surgery: a case report
Authors: Xiaoqin Ji, Ziyan Zhang, Qiqi Yuan, Huiling Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarotid blowout syndrome (CBS) is a catastrophic complication associated with head-and-neck surgery and other conditions involving exposure or erosion of major cervical vessels. Covered stent-graft placement can provide rapid hemostasis while preserving arterial patency. However, implantation in a contaminated field adjacent to a tracheostoma may predispose patients to devastating device-related infection, particularly in immunocompromised hosts.Case presentationWe report the case of a 35-year-old man with stage IVB thymic carcinoma (T4N2M0) who received induction chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy followed by extended resection, including segmental tracheal resection, right thyroid lobectomy with isthmusectomy, supraclavicular island flap tracheal reconstruction, and tracheostomy. Thirteen days after discharge, he developed massive hemoptysis consistent with CBS and underwent emergency neck exploration, angiography, and placement of covered stent grafts in the right common carotid and right subclavian arteries. During the subsequent course, he developed recurrent fever, and repeated blood cultures grew Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Despite culture-directed antipseudomonal therapy and removal of indwelling central venous catheters, bacteremia recurred. Flexible fiberoptic laryngoscopy later demonstrated exposure of the vascular stent graft into the airway, strongly suggesting stent-graft infection or bacterial colonization of the exposed device. The patient subsequently developed recurrent bleeding through the tracheostoma and persistent bacteremia, followed by massive hemorrhage and progressive clinical deterioration. Because definitive surgical source control was judged to offer no acceptable risk-benefit profile after multidisciplinary reassessment, the family elected to withdraw active treatment.ConclusionsThis case suggests that covered stent placement in a contaminated head-and-neck field should be undertaken with great caution, as subsequent bacterial colonization or stent-graft infection may occur and may further lead to recurrent or even catastrophic hemorrhage. When stent implantation is unavoidable, early multidisciplinary evaluation is essential, and every effort should be made to prevent wound infection, bacterial colonization of the device, and related bleeding complications.

Introduction

Thymic carcinoma is a rare but aggressive thymic epithelial tumor, and advanced disease may invade adjacent aerodigestive and cervical vascular structures (1–4). Carotid blowout syndrome (CBS), defined as rupture or impending rupture of the carotid artery or its major branches, is a life-threatening surgical emergency with high mortality(5). Although thymic carcinoma-specific incidence data are unavailable, reported CBS incidence after oncologic surgery involving cervical vessels is 2.9%−4.5% (6), and mortality may exceed 50% or approach 80% after re-irradiation (7–9). Emergency endovascular covered stent implantation has become an important salvage option because it can achieve rapid hemostasis while preserving arterial patency.

However, covered stent placement in contaminated head-and-neck fields, especially near a tracheostoma or infected tissue, may predispose to bacterial colonization and stent-graft infection (10–12), especially in immunocompromised oncology patients with extensive soft-tissue disruption and prolonged hospitalization (13). Though arterial stent infection is rarely reported, it can be fatal; recognized risk factors include procedural contamination, inadequate antibiotic prophylaxis, repeated vascular puncture, immunosuppression, diabetes, and concurrent infection, with reported mortality of approximately 12.5% (14).

Pseudomonas aeruginosa can form biofilms on synthetic implants, contributing to antibiotic tolerance and persistent infection (15). In contaminated or necrotic cervical wounds, P. aeruginosa may also promote persistent inflammation, impaired wound healing, and local tissue destruction through virulence factors such as proteases, elastases, and exotoxins, thereby potentially aggravating vascular wall exposure or erosion (16–20). Most published reports of CBS treated with covered stents involve patients with head-and-neck cancer, particularly after radiotherapy, recurrent disease, or extensive cervical surgery (21–24), however, reports of recurrent P. aeruginosa bacteremia with suspected stent-graft infection remain limited. Here, we report a fatal case after covered stent placement for CBS following extended resection of advanced thymic carcinoma, highlighting the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in an airway-adjacent contaminated field with major soft-tissue disruption and prosthetic vascular material implantation.

Case report

A 35-year-old man was diagnosed with stage IVB thymic carcinoma (T4N2M0). He had no significant past medical history or known comorbidities, no prior surgery or blood transfusion, and no history of smoking, alcohol use, or illicit drug use. His family history was negative for hereditary or familial diseases. Approximately four months before admission to our center, he developed progressive dysphagia without obvious cough or other respiratory symptoms. He initially sought medical attention at another hospital, where thyroid ultrasonography revealed a mass in the right thyroid lobe, and ultrasound-guided biopsy of the right thyroid lesion was performed; however, the initial pathological diagnosis was inconclusive.

On admission to our center, his general condition was stable, with no hoarseness, dyspnea, chills, or fever. Preoperative MRI and contrast-enhanced CT showed a cervicothoracic mass involving the right thyroid region and superior mediastinum, with invasion of adjacent tracheoesophageal structures and multiple cervical lymphadenopathies. Further staging workup showed no pulmonary, abdominal visceral, or bone metastases. The biopsy specimen from the right thyroid lesion was reviewed at our institution. Histopathology showed poorly differentiated squamoid epithelial cell nests within thyroid tissue. Immunohistochemistry showed TTF-1 (–), Tg (–), calcitonin (–), PCK (+), CK5/6 (+), p40 (+), CK19 (+), CD5 partial (+), and CD117 partial (+), supporting thymic squamous cell carcinoma involving the thyroid rather than primary thyroid carcinoma.

The patient received two cycles of induction chemo-immunotherapy consisting of albumin-bound paclitaxel 400 mg on day 1, cisplatin 40 mg on days 1–3, and tislelizumab 240 mg every 3 weeks. He subsequently underwent extended resection including upper mediastinal tumor resection, segmental tracheal resection for tracheal malignant involvement, right thyroid lobectomy with isthmusectomy, supraclavicular island flap tracheal reconstruction, and tracheostomy. Final pathology confirmed thymic squamous cell carcinoma invading the thyroid and tracheal adventitia, with metastasis in 5 of 26 right cervical lymph nodes. He was discharged on postoperative day 8 with a tracheostomy tube and a nasogastric tube.

Thirteen days after discharge, the patient presented to the emergency department with massive hemoptysis, consistent with acute CBS in the context of prior tumor encasement of the right common carotid artery and intraoperative adventitial injury. Emergency neck exploration revealed active bleeding from an approximately 2-mm defect at the origin of the right common carotid artery. Vascular surgery was consulted intraoperatively, and subsequent femoral angiography further defined its proximity to the brachiocephalic bifurcation and guided endovascular repair. Because this deeply located defect could not be safely exposed through a standard cervical incision, open repair was considered unsuitable, and endovascular repair was selected for rapid hemorrhage control. Two heparin-bonded covered stent grafts were implanted: a 6 mm × 5 cm graft in the right common carotid artery and a 7 mm × 5 cm graft in the right subclavian artery. Because the defect was located at the origin of the right common carotid artery, complete exclusion required proximal extension of the covered stent into the brachiocephalic artery, which would have compromised the origin of the right subclavian artery. Therefore, a parallel covered stent-graft technique was used to exclude the defect while preserving right subclavian arterial flow. Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit. Antibiotics were discontinued on day 14, and antithrombotic therapy was adjusted during hospitalization as clinically indicated.

On day 21, he developed fever with a maximum temperature of 39.2 °C. Two sets of peripheral blood cultures were obtained on day 22 from the left and right antecubital veins, and both sets yielded Pseudomonas aeruginosa on day 24. No catheter-drawn blood cultures were obtained. Because catheter-related bloodstream infection could not be excluded, the right upper-arm inserted central catheter (PICC) inserted in the emergency department on day 0 and the right femoral central venous catheter inserted intraoperatively on day 0 were both removed on day 25. Cultures of both catheter tips were negative; therefore, this episode did not fulfill the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) criteria for definite catheter-related bloodstream infection, and recurrent bacteremia was considered more likely related to hematogenous seeding of the stent graft and/or local contamination through the exposed graft surface. The nasogastric tube was also removed after oral intake was tolerated. Antimicrobial therapy was adjusted according to clinical tolerance and susceptibility results. With antimicrobial therapy and supportive care, the fever resolved by day 41, and the patient was discharged on day 42. On day 55, the patient was readmitted because of recurrent fever of more than 12 days' duration. Two sets of blood cultures were obtained from the same sites and again yielded P. aeruginosa (reported on day 59), indicating recurrent bacteremia. Multidisciplinary evaluation considered stent-graft infection or colonization a probable source of sepsis because of recurrent P. aeruginosa bacteremia, negative catheter-tip cultures, laryngoscopic and computed tomography evidence of stent-graft exposure, and relapse after antimicrobial discontinuation. Because of the patient's poor general condition, large vascular defect, arterial high-pressure system, and high operative risk, vascular replacement or bypass surgery was considered infeasible. Conservative management with culture-directed antimicrobial therapy was therefore pursued.

Flexible fiberoptic laryngoscopy demonstrated exposed vascular stent graft material within the airway (Figure 2), strongly suggesting stent-graft infection or bacterial colonization of the exposed device. From day 75 onward, he intermittently expectorated blood-tinged sputum and blood clots via the tracheostoma, representing sentinel bleeding with gradually increasing volume. Two sets of blood cultures obtained again from the same sites on day 84 remained positive for P. aeruginosa (reported on day 88). On day 85, the patient experienced two episodes of massive hemorrhage via the tracheostoma. Recurrent bleeding persisted on day 86 and required mechanical ventilation. Given progressive clinical deterioration with persistent bacteremia, exposed stent graft, and recurrent life-threatening hemorrhage, the family elected to withdraw active treatment, and the patient was discharged against medical advice. In brief, antimicrobial therapy after CBS consisted of empirical imipenem/cilastatin followed by piperacillin/tazobactam and cefoperazone/sulbactam, which was discontinued on day 14. After the first episode of P. aeruginosa bacteremia, imipenem/cilastatin was restarted and then changed to cefepime according to susceptibility results. During recurrent bacteremia, treatment was adjusted to meropenem plus levofloxacin, with meropenem restarted after the final positive blood culture. Discussion

This case describes recurrent Pseudomonas aeruginosa bacteremia and clinically suspected stent-graft infection after covered stent placement for CBS following extended resection of advanced thymic carcinoma. The clinical course was characterized by recurrent fever, repeated positive blood cultures, endoscopically confirmed exposure of the stent graft into the airway, and progressive hemorrhage, ultimately resulting in treatment withdrawal. The key clinical message is that persistent or recurrent bacteremia with sentinel bleeding after covered stent placement, especially when stent exposure is present, should prompt urgent concern for stent-graft infection and impending catastrophic rebleeding. From a practical standpoint, such findings should not be regarded as isolated infectious or minor bleeding events, but rather as warning signs requiring immediate diagnostic reassessment and escalation of multidisciplinary management.

Several factors likely contributed to infection in this patient. First, advanced malignancy and prior systemic treatment may have impaired host immunity (4). Second, extensive oncologic resection with tracheal reconstruction and tracheostomy created a compromised local environment with major soft-tissue disruption (25). Third, the covered stent grafts were implanted in a contaminated field adjacent to the airway, which likely increased the risk of microbial adherence and colonization. Finally, repeated invasive procedures and prolonged hospitalization may also have contributed to infectious risk (26).

Despite the recognized infection risk, covered stenting was selected as emergency salvage therapy to rapidly control life-threatening CBS while preserving carotid flow. Open repair was considered unsafe in the contaminated, previously reconstructed field, and carotid sacrifice was not pursued because occlusion tolerance could not be assured (27, 28).

The diagnosis of stent-graft infection in this case should be interpreted with caution. A definitive microbiological diagnosis was not established because P. aeruginosa was not isolated directly from the stent graft or surrounding tissue, and device explantation or perigraft sampling was considered prohibitively hazardous. Therefore, the evidence supported a clinically suspected, rather than microbiologically proven, stent-graft infection or colonization. Vascular stent-graft exposure into the airway may theoretically occur from mechanical erosion alone. However, in this case, recurrent P. aeruginosa bacteremia despite catheter removal and culture-directed antibiotics, relapse after antimicrobial discontinuation, and endoscopic/CT evidence of graft exposure to the nonsterile airway favored suspected stent-graft infection or colonization rather than isolated mechanical exposure. Although direct graft culture was unavailable, these findings met multiple MAGIC minor criteria and supported the clinical diagnosis. Previous reports have linked P. aeruginosa to vascular graft and device-related infections, and its ability to adhere to prosthetic material and form biofilms may allow infection to persist despite antimicrobial therapy (16, 17). Therefore, antimicrobial therapy alone may fail to eradicate infection when colonized prosthetic material remains in place(19, 20). Recurrent P. aeruginosa bacteremia after covered stent placement, especially with stent exposure, should therefore prompt suspicion of stent-graft colonization or infection (18).

In similar cases, management should distinguish definitive source control from temporary stabilization. Device explantation with vascular reconstruction should be considered when feasible, whereas vessel sacrifice requires assessment of carotid occlusion tolerance whenever possible. If neither option is feasible, suppressive antimicrobial therapy remains a temporizing rather than curative approach, with persistent risk of rebleeding. Although successful surgical explantation of infected stent grafts has been reported, our patient was considered a poor candidate for such intervention because removal of the stent from the origin of the right common carotid artery in an infected superior mediastinal field would have required highly invasive reoperation with complex arterial reconstruction. After multidisciplinary assessment, the anticipated risks of catastrophic hemorrhage, anastomotic failure, reinfection, sepsis, and perioperative death were judged to outweigh the potential benefit of definitive surgery. Carotid ligation or sacrifice was also considered high risk because tolerance to permanent carotid occlusion could not be assured. Conservative antimicrobial therapy was therefore used as a temporizing rather than curative strategy.

This case also underscores the importance of close surveillance after salvage covered stent placement in contaminated head-and-neck fields. Recurrent fever, persistent bacteremia, new airway bleeding, or endoscopic evidence of exposed vascular material should all be regarded as warning signs requiring urgent multidisciplinary reassessment involving head-and-neck surgery, vascular surgery or interventional radiology, infectious diseases, and critical care. Such reassessment should explicitly address whether the patient remains a candidate for definitive source control, vascular reconstruction, or vessel sacrifice, because delayed recognition may leave clinicians with only noncurative conservative options. Therefore, covered stents placed in contaminated airway-adjacent fields require close clinical, microbiological, endoscopic, and imaging surveillance. Recurrent bacteremia, sentinel bleeding, or exposed vascular material should prompt urgent repeat imaging, reassessment of source control, and multidisciplinary discussion of surgical, endovascular, or palliative options.

Conclusion

We report a rare fatal case of recurrent Pseudomonas aeruginosa bacteremia and suspected covered stent-graft infection after salvage treatment of carotid blowout following extended resection of advanced thymic carcinoma. In contaminated head-and-neck settings, especially in patients with tracheostomy and endoscopically confirmed stent exposure, persistent bacteremia and recurrent airway bleeding should raise strong suspicion for device-related infection and impending catastrophic rebleeding. The key take-home message is that recurrent bacteremia, sentinel bleeding, or exposed stent material after covered stent placement should prompt urgent microbiological reassessment, vascular imaging, endoscopic evaluation when appropriate, and early multidisciplinary discussion of source control, vascular reconstruction, vessel sacrifice, or suppressive antimicrobial therapy.
