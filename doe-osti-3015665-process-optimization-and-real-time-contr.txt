3. Executive Summary: Provide a one-page summary of the motivation, importance,
 goals, and major accomplishments of the project. The summary should include: (1)
 how the research or project adds to the understanding of the area investigated; (2) the
 technical effectiveness and economic feasibility of the methods or techniques
 investigated or demonstrated; and (3) how the project is beneficial to the public. The
 discussion should be a minimum of one paragraph and written in terms
 understandable by an educated layperson.

 The overarching goal of this work was to accelerate the commercialization of high-
 productivity, mixed community microalgal treatment technologies for the synergistic
 treatment of wastewater and the production of biofuel feedstocks. This project
 addressed a critical barrier to the financial viability and energy efficiency of algal
 wastewater treatment: an inability to design and operate high-rate processes that
 reliably achieve target effluent qualities, areal productivities, and biochemical
 compositions (lipid, protein, carbohydrate content) despite fluctuations in wastewater
 composition, climate, and microbial communities.
 This project overcame this barrier by focusing on the EcoRecover process which
 leverages Advanced Biological Nutrient Recovery (ABNRTM) – a technology which has
 the potential to achieve reliable and efficient recovery of phosphorus from
 wastewaters to concentrations below the current limit of technology (generally
 regarded as 0.1 mg-P·L-1 and 3 mg-N·L-1). The ABNR process filled a critical need for
 wastewater treatment facilities with pending nutrient permits by reducing effluent total
 phosphorus to below 0.04 mg-P·L-1 year-round, even at high latitudes. This project
 aimed to expedite the market penetration of this technology by improving effluent
 quality, consistent biomass yield and quality, and energy efficiency, all while reducing
 the physical footprint, capital, and operating costs.
 The specific objectives of this work were (i) to develop a mechanistic process
 simulator to predict effluent nutrient concentration and process sustainability, (ii) to
 adapt and train a low-cost monitoring system for real-time characterization of microbial
 community structure, and (iii) to leverage the validated simulator and monitoring
 capabilities to advance the financial viability and environmental sustainability of algal
 nutrient recovery from wastewaters. These objectives were accomplished by
 integrating long-term characterization of a 0.15 million gallon per day (MGD) ABNR
 treatment system at the Village of Roberts (VoR, Wisconsin), comprehensive meta-
 genomic/-transcriptomic characterization, novel real-time microbial monitoring, and
 process modeling to develop optimized process designs and process controls to
 improve the financial viability and environmental sustainability of wastewater
 integration with the microalgae-to-biofuel value chain.
 Key outcomes from this work include an optimized and controlled ABNR design as
 well as a suite of open-source tools that include a calibrated and validated algae
 process simulator in QSDsan and a novel low-cost, real-time microbial monitoring tool.
 These tools can be leveraged by other algal cultivation and wastewater treatment
 technology developers in future work.

5. Background: Provide up-to-date references to peer-reviewed journal publications,
 conference proceedings, and other technical publications on the latest developments
 in similar technology fields as the work undertaken in the project. Include a technical
 discussion that shows how the published work is: 1) similar to this project; 2) different
 from this project; and 3) being fully leveraged to accelerate the timeline and improve
 the quality of the current project. This should place the project in living context
 alongside the current state of the art in the literature and ongoing R&D.

 Are photobioreactors (PBRs) and membranes too costly? PBRs and membranes
 make ABNR a “bolt-on” tertiary treatment process capable of unparalleled
 treatment efficacy, establishing a clear value proposition for utilities. The
 conventional wisdom is that PBRs are prohibitively expensive. It is true that the system
 will have a higher capital intensity on a footprint basis ($·m-2) relative to open ponds,
 but the financial implications of this are mitigated: (i) by reducing the areal footprint of
 the cultivation system by more than 20x relative to past PBR studies through
 separation of hydraulic retention time (~1-2 hours; which governs reactor sizing) and
 solids residence time (~1.5-2.5 days); (ii) through the availability of low-interest
 government loans for capital improvements at wastewater utilities (e.g., VoR received
 a loan at <2% interest with $1.5M in loan forgiveness for ABNR construction); and (iii)
 by providing an engineered process that satisfies a pressing need for wastewater
 utilities to meet pending N/P permits with lower effluent concentrations, lower life cycle
 cost (due to revenue from algal biomass sales), and comparable energy consumption
 relative to conventional nutrient removal technologies, including at high latitudes.
 Further, the modular ABNR design can be integrated into existing WWTFs irrespective
 of facility size (e.g., urban vs. rural), which provides a distinct advantage over open
 ponds or other low-rate cultivation systems.
 Why focus on process modeling and control? Mechanistic modeling and dynamic
 control of microbial ecology and physiology are critical for the optimization of
 high-performance, mixed community algal bioprocesses. Intensification and
 optimization of algal mass culture must balance the tradeoffs between cellular growth
 and carbon accumulation that are difficult to predict across dynamic environmental
 conditions and microbial communities. Systems must efficiently grow phototrophs to
 uptake nutrients and create biomass, but increasing the energetic content of biomass
 (e.g., higher lipid content) comes at the expense of growth:1 balancing these factors
 is key for biofuel feedstock production. In addition to environmental/operational
 parameters that select for metabolic function on a cellular level, species-level
 interactions and stochastic conditions drive complex microbial community dynamics
 that further alter carbon fixation and partitioning. To maximize carbon productivity, it
 is necessary to model, predict, and control the metabolic processes and the underlying
 species-level dynamics that shape community structure and function.
 Why use ’omics tools? The integration of ’omics tools (metagenomics and
 transcriptomics) with process modeling will enable the optimization of high-
 performance cultivation by linking process conditions with community structure,
 function, metabolic responses, and process performance. PI Guest has developed a
 process model for mixed algal communities to predict biomass growth, nutrient

 Page 5 of 54

uptake, and the dynamic storage/consumption of carbohydrates and lipids (the
phototrophic process model, PPM).2–5 The integration of ’omics level understanding
will represent a transformative step forward in process simulation by elucidating
species- and metabolism-level processes governing how water chemistry,
design/operational parameters, and environmental conditions influence community
structure and carbon uptake and partitioning among algal community members.
Specifically, metagenomics will allow the robust examination of community dynamics
over long-term, field-scale trials, and transcriptomics will be used to infer community
function and the activation of specific carbon accumulation pathways in response to
process conditions. When linked with dynamic influent composition, irradiance,
temperature, algal biochemical composition and physiology, these data will enable us
to mechanistically link locality-specific factors (climate, wastewater) and process
design/operational decisions (SRT, HRT, pH, etc.) with community structure, carbon
fixation and partitioning, and functional gene response.
Why use miniaturized flow imaging microscopy (mFIM)? Low-cost mFIM is ideal for
rapid, automated microalgal species identification. Mixed communities have the
potential to outperform monocultures through increased stability and productivity,6 but
their successful implementation requires dynamic control of selective pressures to
drive natural selection in our engineered reactors enabling robust, resilient, and
predictable performance. To develop these selective pressures and to manage
cultivation ecology for long-term operation, we need monitoring tools that will track
community structure to enable near real-time process control (adjustment of internal
recycles, SRT, pH, etc.). Traditionally, flow cytometry7 and FIM8 have been used for
monitoring of microalgal species in mixed microbial communities. While these
approaches demonstrate excellent performance for species identification and
enumeration, current market-ready devices are prohibitively expensive and require
specialized training and infrastructure (e.g., FlowCam® costs >$37,000). An
alternative approach would be to leverage recent progress in low-cost miniaturized
microscopy. Co-PI Pinto’s group has developed a novel, low-cost (<$300)
miniaturized FIM (mFIM) – the Autonomous Real-Time Microbial Scope (i.e.,
ARTiMiS)9 – that provides sub-micron resolution and performs all necessary image
processing on an onboard Raspberry Pi. This is the first device of its kind at a low-
price point for microbial monitoring,9 and could make near real-time monitoring of
cultivation ecology broadly accessible to industry.

 Project Goal
 The overarching goal of this work was to accelerate the commercialization of high
 productivity, mixed community microalgal treatment technology – the Advanced
 Biological Nutrient Recovery (ABNRTM) process – which enables reliable and efficient
 recovery of phosphorus from wastewaters beyond the current limit of technology
 (LOT). This project addressed a critical barrier to the financial viability and energy
 efficiency of algal wastewater treatment and mixed community cultivation: an inability
 to design and operate high-rate processes that reliably achieve target effluent qualities,
 areal productivities, and biochemical compositions (lipid, protein, carbohydrate
 content) despite fluctuations in wastewater composition, climate, and microbial
 communities. This barrier stems from a lack of understanding of how process design
 and operation coupled with environmental factors influence the net rates of nutrient
 uptake, inorganic carbon uptake, and partitioning (into growth, storage lipids and
 carbohydrates) by mixed algal communities.

Innovation & Impact
The proposed set of advancements (Table 1) represents (1) a low-risk, high-reward
approach to achieve reproducibility and optimization of high-performance cultivation
through the development of a mechanistic process simulator, and (2) a high-risk, high-
reward approach to improve process stability by integrating low-cost imaging,
sequencing, and modeling to control ecology and performance in near real-time. In
particular, innovations from this project addressed four key challenges outlined in the
DOE’s National Algal Biofuels Technology Review10 (Figure 2):

Figure 2. Current state-of-the-art and innovations of this project.

Impact 1. Increased productivity across geographical locations and
wastewaters. By focusing on mechanistic drivers of system performance, predictive
modeling enabled the development of selective pressures that achieve reproducible
performance and stable ecology. Furthermore, the ARTiMiS system enabled near
real-time process monitoring and control to improve year-round cultivation outcomes.
The proposed ABNR system particularly has high performance across the nation,
even at high latitudes in winter. This project demonstrated the potential to expand the
range of U.S. cultivation sites and total potential algal biomass and biofuel yields in
the U.S.
Impact 2. An open-source process simulator to translate cultivation concepts
to successful full-scale systems. Mechanistic modeling enabled researchers and
technology developers to evaluate cultivation innovations computationally and identify
pitfalls in the translation of bench-scale technologies to field-scale implementation.
This work established links between climate, wastewater composition, and process
design to facilitate process optimization of nutrient recovery, energy consumption,
biomass yield/quality, and process economics.
Impact 3. Financially viable biofuel feedstock production. Improvements in
process kinetics and energy consumption along with increasingly reliable TP removal
(below the current LOT) increased market opportunities for microalgae cultivation on
wastewaters.

Summary of Tasks
The specific targets for this project were: Target #1 to achieve effluent TP
concentrations ≤ 0.04 mg-P·L-1 for 90% of operating time; Target #2 to reduce
process energy consumption by 40% to 126 kWh·kg-P-1; and Target #3 to
increase biomass yield by 40% to 79 g-AFDW·g-P-1.

Task 1: Verification. The team worked with DOE to refine the technology readiness
level. All data were provided to support the claims in the original application.
Task 2: Dynamic Process Characterization. Monitoring of field-scale ABNR system.
The existing ABNR system owned and operated by the Village of Roberts (VoR) was
continuously operated and monitored. Biomass, aqueous, and kinetic characterization.
Sensors tracked dissolved oxygen, pH, orthophosphate, temperature, and irradiance.
Samples were analyzed for ammonia, TP, orthophosphate, total N (TN), nitrate, nitrite,
dissolved inorganic carbon, dissolved organic carbon, and alkalinity. Biomass
characterization included elemental composition (C, H, N, P), amino acid residuals,
carbohydrate content, total lipid, and fatty acid content. Kinetic and stoichiometric
parameters were also measured for process model calibration and validation in short-
term studies using lab-scale photobioreactors. ’Omics characterization of community
structure and function. Metagenome assembled genomes (MAGs) were obtained and
used to develop qPCR primers for key organisms and genes involved. qPCR and
amplicon sequencing were used to estimate the absolute abundance of key organisms
(gDNA) and their gene activities (cDNA).
Task 3: Process Simulator Development. Process model calibration/validation. The
initial model structure (phototrophic process model; PPM) was expanded into the
phototrophic-mixotrophic process model (PM2). The updated model was then
calibrated and validated by leveraging kinetics and ABNR operation data. Process
model enhancement with ’omics data. Based on ’omics data, an alternate model
structure was developed to better represent system dynamics. Integrate PPM, TEA,
& LCA into QSDsan. Internal cost, energy, and consumable baseline were estimated.
QSDsan was updated with the improved PPM and additional operational and
monitoring data. The calibrated and validated process simulator was built into QSDsan
and publicly shared.
Task 4: Miniaturized Flow Imaging Microscopy (mFIM) Customization and
Training. Software development and algorithm training. Customization included (1)
optimization of object detection computer vision algorithms to accurately isolate both
single-cell and colonial microalgae in morphologically diverse communities, (2)
development of tailored classification algorithms to distinguish both taxonomic groups
and morphological variants within taxonomic classes, and (3) develop an efficient
database structure to manage time series image data and aid in model training and
development. Samples were analyzed in parallel using FlowCam at VoR to
benchmark ARTiMiS results. Hardware customization. Customization included
modifications to device design and durability to withstand deployment to wastewater
treatment facilities, optimization of fluidic pathing and control to reduce maintenance
requirements, and adaptations to optical components and device drivers to improve
image quality, thus increasing identification accuracy. Nanopore sequencing

                                   Page 10 of 54

integration. Nanopore sequencing was used to enhance the annotation of ARTiMiS-
based cell classification with selective metagenomics and 18S rRNA gene sequencing.
Task 5: mFIM Field Validation. Field testing of mFIM. The mFIM platform was
deployed at field-scale ABNR systems for validation coupled with all analyses outlined
in Task 2. Field deployments highlighted blind spots in hardware, analysis software,
user interface, and user experience that were addressed in response. In-field use
informed sampling cadence for mFIM-nanopore sequencing integration to optimize
frequency of sequencing calibration of field-deployed classification models.
Laboratory refinement of mFIM. Cycling between field tests and laboratory-based
testing/modifications enabled iterative design improvements and the development of
a generalized protocol for mFIM implementation for a variety of microalgal systems
with minimal user intervention required.
Task 6: Design Optimization and Process Control. Design optimization and
controls plans via QSDsan. The ABNR system was optimized to meet target effluent
quality at the VoR facility. The project team developed detailed recommendations to
improve the design of the ABNR system. Execute ABNR modifications. An optimized
ABNR system and a set of potential operational controls were established. Validation
of simulator and real-time process control. The experimental evaluation of process
controls was achieved through the integration of data from online analyzers, the mFIM
system, and the range of control strategies established by the project team.

 The ABNR system utilizes a PBR to optimize microalgal energy metabolism during
peak light hours. Gene expression analysis from the metatranscriptomics sequencing
reveals that, during these periods, microalgae in the PBR prioritize starch biosynthesis
over immediate glucose utilization, storing energy for nighttime use (Figure 14). This
pattern aligns with prior research indicating that carbohydrate content in PBRs
fluctuates with the light cycle, supporting balanced energy needs and growth.
Additionally, lipid metabolism shows a trend toward lipid synthesis, particularly
triacylglycerol, which can serve as biofuel precursors. The system also enhances
stress response mechanisms, as evidenced by the upregulation of heat shock proteins
like HSP70, which help mitigate stresses such as pH fluctuations, oxygen level
changes, and pest presence. Conversely, microalgae in the mix tank focus on
immediate energy production from available nutrients, driven by continuous glycolysis
and the TCA cycle, ensuring sustained metabolic activity. This differential gene
expression between the PBR and mix tank illustrates the adaptive strategies of
microalgae for efficient nutrient removal and biomass production, highlighting the
system's ability to balance energy storage and growth.

metabolism and microalgae on phosphorus metabolism, ensures efficient nutrient

Task 3: Process Simulator Development
Subtask 3.1: Process model calibration/validation
Milestone 3.1.1: PPM calibration and validation on 1 monitoring period.
The original phototrophic process model (PPM) only included the photoautotrophic
metabolism of algae. In order (1) to improve the accuracy of the algae process model
itself and also (2) to make the model more compatible with other wastewater models,
the PPM was expanded into the phototrophic-mixotrophic process model (PM2).
Specifically, the growth of algae on each of acetate and glucose (heterotrophic
metabolism) was newly added (Figure 15).

Figure 15. Schematic representation of the lumped metabolic pathways included in the Phototrophic-
Mixotrophic Process Model (PM2).

reproducible simulation, and transparent implementation.

5/26/2022). A total of three datasets (two kinetic assays & one unit process experiment)
were used to calibrate and validate the model, and the simulation results for algae

Subtask 4.3: Nanopore sequencing integration
Milestone 4.3: Optimization and integration of nanopore sequencing with ARTiMiS.
We initially tested three long-read primer sets: ss5-ss316, ITS9munngs-ITS4ngsUni17,
and V4_Balzano_F-D11_3143R17. The ITS9munngs-ITS4ngsUni primer set failed to
generate specific products. Using the two effective primer sets, our Oxford Nanopore
sequencing yielded 658.89 Mb of data, with 440.86 Mb meeting quality filtering criteria
(minimum score: 7). Although gel electrophoresis revealed clear bands, the
V4_Balzano_F-D11_3143R products showed varying read lengths and smaller data
sizes (1.34-69.69 Mb) compared to ss5-ss3 products (85.15-119.70 Mb). After
analyzing the ss5-ss3 amplification results and comparing them with V8-V9 Illumina
sequencing data, we discovered inconsistencies between Nanopore and Illumina
platforms. This led us to conduct a systematic comparison of short-read and long-read
sequencing approaches to evaluate their effectiveness in microalgal community
characterization.
 We began with in-silico analysis of V418, V8V918, and ss5ss3 primer pairs using
SILVA19 and PR220 reference databases. This analysis revealed important limitations
in both databases, including partial 18S rRNA genes and occasional taxonomic
misclassifications (Figure 26). These findings emphasized the need to interpret in-
silico results cautiously and validate them through in-vitro experiments for robust
comparison.

                                      Page 34 of 54

Figure 26. (Left) Number of reference sequences and (Right) Proportion of reference sequences from
the four main taxonomic groups (e.g. Archaea, Bacteria, Eukaryota, and Chlorophyta) captured from
each database by single primer or primer pairs. The V8V9 reverse primer captures substantially fewer
reference sequences compared to the V8V9 forward primer, likely due to many partial 18S rRNA gene
sequences present in the reference databases. Additionally, some eukaryotic sequences appear

 Based on these in-silico findings, we proceeded with in-vitro validation using
samples collected between August 2022 and July 2023. We sequenced V8V9
amplicons from 48 samples on the Illumina MiSeq platform and ss5ss3 amplicons
from 47 of these samples on the PacBio Sequel II platform (one sample was excluded
due to low concentration). While we initially planned to use Oxford Nanopore
sequencing, we ultimately selected PacBio technology for its superior accuracy in full-
length 18S rRNA gene sequencing. Our final analysis focused on 30 common samples
across platforms after rarefying, examining three datasets: Illumina MiSeq V8V9
sequences, PacBio full-length sequences, and computationally extracted V8V9
regions from PacBio data.
 The results revealed that both approaches effectively monitor operational
performance, with distinct advantages for each method. The V8V9 primers provided
broader taxonomic coverage compared to ss5ss3 primers, while long-read
sequencing offered enhanced capabilities for detailed Chlorophyta taxonomic studies.
These findings have been documented in a manuscript titled "Systematic comparisons
between long-read and short-read based amplicon sequencing to characterizing
microalgal community". All sequence data has been uploaded to the shared Box folder
to facilitate future improvements in microalgal community characterization methods.

Subtask 5.1: Field testing of mFIM
Milestone 5.1.1: mFIM within 10% of accuracy of FlowCam in terms of abundance and

The extensive sampling campaign of this project yielded a rich dataset to statistically
evaluate the total system biomass from both traditional and FIM-based methods.
Comparison of ARTiMiS biomass estimates to FlowCam and total suspended solids
(TSS) data over a two-year period were evaluated and indicated that, when taking
manually measured TSS as the ground truth biomass measurement, ARTiMiS mFIM

                                         Page 35 of 54

outperformed estimates by both FlowCam and a calibrated and error-corrected TSS
probe (Figure 27). This result exceeded expectations for this milestone.

Figure 27. Evaluation of biomass prediction accuracy taking manually measured total suspended solids
(TSS) as ground truth. ARTiMiS biomass estimates outperform both FlowCam and a calibrated TSS
probe, both in terms of lower estimation error and greater estimate linearity.

 When attempting to evaluate ARTiMiS’ classification capabilities relative to
FlowCam, it was determined that FlowCam’s native classification software was unable
to accurately and reliably classify objects to the level of detail required for this project,
primarily due to the intra-class heterogeneity observed in the ABNR microalgal
community. In brief, FlowCam’s built-in classification system relied on a cascading
series of feature thresholds that, in practice, proved too inflexible to identify all classes
evenly; classes given priority in the classifier hierarchy had high sensitivity but low
specificity, and thus misclassification rates were extremely high.
 To circumvent this, a common classification approach was taken for data collected
on both FlowCam and ARTiMiS systems (ARTiMiS’ classification was implemented
using FlowCam data), and thus the two were not evaluated on their classification
algorithm accuracy but rather the quality of data captured on each instrument to inform
a classification model, which was identical for both data streams. In testing, the overall
accuracy of deep learning classification models trained on FlowCam data and
ARTiMiS data differed by less than 3%, with several per-class accuracies within 1-2%
difference (Figure 28).
 Thus, it was demonstrated that (1) FlowCam’s native classification technique could
not be used as benchmark, as it would have trivialized the milestone, and (2) with both

Milestone 5.1.2: Installation of final mFIM-nanopore units at VoR.
At project-start, it was not clear how accurate ARTiMiS mFIM would be at identifying
microalgae and other microorganisms in the ABNR community, nor was it clear how
stable the community composition would be over short (weeks) and long (seasonal)
time spans. Thus it was proposed to incorporate nanopore sequencing with mFIM to
provide a means to continually update the onboard classification models and
disambiguate potentially frequent incidence of unclassified organisms.
 However, due to the combination of the discovery of ABNR’s relative community
stability (inclusion of new classes was infrequent and occurred on a yearly basis), and
the highly accurate classification performance, it was determined that ARTiMiS mFIM
would not require direct integration with nanopore sequencing for routine operation;
rather, sequencing could be performed on an as-needed basis in parallel to
continuous mFIM monitoring.
 Thus, when ARTiMiS mFIM was deployed to field sites (Figure 29), nanopore
sequencing was not directly integrated. ARTiMiS saw two field deployments during
this project, in November 2021 at the Village of Roberts ABNR facility in Roberts, WI,
for a two-week field test. Following hardware and software refinement to improve
device reliability and user experience, the updated ARTiMiS device was deployed to
the Waupun ABNR facility in Waupun, WI in 2024 for four months from March to July.
ARTiMiS was recalled to the laboratory for further iterative development to improve
on new information learned from the extended field deployment, which also coincided
with the withdrawal of CLEARAS Water Recovery from managing the site.

 accomplishments and explain why they are significant. Both positive and negative

 Dynamic Process Characterization
 Drivers of stable and unstable performance (including algal and pests populations)
 were characterized. Stable operational periods were characterized by low eukaryotic
 diversity and a dominant Scenedesmus population (55-80%), while upset periods had
 higher algal diversity and were dominated by Chlorella and Monoraphidium. Long-
 term cultures contained highly diverse bacterial communities across both upset and
 stable periods. A balanced nitrifying community did not positively or negatively impact
 microalgae, but partial nitrification resulting in NO2- accumulation, insufficient alkalinity
 (<100 mg·L-1 as CaCO3), and low pH (<6) were associated with deteriorating process
 performance and culture turnover. The stable algal community was positively
 correlated with several key abiotic factors, including system pH, alkalinity, and influent
 ammonium (NH4+) which promoted increased algal biomass and nutrient assimilation.
 Pest temporal dynamics followed host-specific dynamics and occurred during
 elevated summer temperature (22-25 °C) after the loss of system performance and
 community turnover, revealing the importance of maintaining healthy algal cultures
 and high biomass productivity. Specific gene pathways were identified during both
 mixed tank and PBR operational units, allowing future design to allow optimization of
 carbon storage for downstream processing. Full metagenome and transcriptomes of
 algal species were also annotated to add to our inventory and understanding of algal
 genomes.

 Process Simulator Development
 This study demonstrated the potential of PM2 to predict the long-term performance of
 a full-scale, mixed microalgal wastewater resource recovery process under real-world
 conditions. In comparison to other conventional microalgae process models, PM2 had
 the distinct advantage of being able to account for changes in stored carbohydrate
 and lipid concentrations according to nutrient availability and diel light-dark cycles.
 Due to its application at treatment plants with stringent effluent phosphorus permits,
 the ABNR system tended to treat secondary effluents with <1 mg-P·L-1 of phosphorus,
 making it important to understand the storage and mobilization (consumption) of
 biopolymers that help microalgae balance growth across nutrient deplete and replete
 conditions. In addition, the ABNR system had different light availability in the mix tank
 and PBR and an internal recirculation between reactors, all of which will influence
 carbon storage dynamics and overall system performance. In this regard, PM2 was
 particularly suited to elucidate a mechanistic understanding of the factors governing
 phosphorus recovery across unit operations and over 24-hour cycles, thereby
 supporting the optimization of system design and operational controls.

Real-time Monitoring of Community Structure
Real-time monitoring via Flow Imaging Microscopy (FIM) enabled responsive action
to be taken by facility operators in response to daily changes observed in
microorganism community composition. After the development of hypotheses of
classes or attributes (e.g., larger particle size) associated with degraded system
performance, operators quickly adopted a strategy of consulting daily FIM data to
make process decisions. For example, operating with the hypothesis that larger
microalgae and bacterial flocs were associated with decreased orthophosphate
uptake, operators monitored the abundance of larger taxa/morphotypes and related
aggregate cell features (e.g., average cell size) and adjusted harvest rates through a
size-selective screen accordingly in order to decrease average particle size in the
system. Extended periods of stable system performance over the course of this study
can be, in part, attributed to these efforts to size-select microalgal constituents.
Retrospective analysis of the correlations between mFIM data and system
performance supported the hypothesis that smaller morphotypes of Scenedesmus
and Chlorella were positively associated with high orthophosphate removal rates,
meanwhile larger morphotypes of the same taxa were associated with decreased
removal rates.
 With high temporal frequency monitoring enabled by mFIM, in combination with
single-cell resolution and high-accuracy classification, a highly detailed time series of
microalgal community composition was able to be constructed. This dataset provided
comparable taxonomic resolution to sequencing data, with the addition of
morphological attribute information, and was available with daily time resolution. Thus,
it was able to be used for detailed time series analysis to determine critical
relationships between system parameters and community composition which would
otherwise not be possible with sparse sequencing data alone. This form of analysis
led to the identification of key taxonomic and morphological traits associated with high
and low system performance, as well as the impacts of key water chemistry and
operational parameters on these traits. In such a variable and open system,
correlations were challenging to determine with statistical certainty, and causal
relationships were further out of reach; however, this methodology demonstrated a
blueprint for further studies of both uncontrolled and controlled systems to assemble
highly detailed datasets that can be mined for universal insights.

mFIM Technology Readiness Advancement
This study directly contributed to advancing the technology readiness level of the
ARTiMiS mFIM system, significantly improving both its hardware and software
capabilities. After successful demonstration of the technology during field trials, and
with a wealth of new insights from overcome deployment challenges and user
feedback, it is hoped that one of the key outcomes of this project is that ARTiMiS
mFIM is now near commercial readiness for pilot adoption by future industrial partners.
This technology may significantly impact research and development, process
optimization, and full-scale process stability in other microalgal application areas of
critical importance to the broader industry.

 Building on the lessons learned from this project (DE-EE0009270), the project team
 was awarded another project (DE-EE0011058) from the Department of Energy's
 Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy (EERE).
 The overarching goal of the new project is to advance the stability of high-
 productivity, mixed community microalgal cultivation systems by developing holistic
 crop protection strategies that minimize culture upset events due to pests. This project
 will tackle a critical barrier to scaling and intensification of mixed community algal
 biomass production - the inability to consistently maintain stable cultures with high
 areal productivity due to invasive and deleterious bacterial, fungal, and zooplankton
 pests. This barrier stems from a lack of understanding of how pests upset algal
 processes through microalgal community turnover which undercuts areal productivity
 and process stability. The objectives of the proposed research plan are (1) to identify
 pre-emergence factors that predict culture crash (i.e., upset) trajectories, (2) to enable
 real-time monitoring of pest-induced algal phenotypic changes, and (3) to develop and
 implement a pre-emergent pest detection and control system to guide culture
 trajectories and maintain process performance (Figure 33).
