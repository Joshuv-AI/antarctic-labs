Abstract
This paper proposes a conservative focused extension of Martin-Löf Dependent Type Theory (MLTT), integrating three contributions with substantial technical content, connected by an explicit integration result. Proof status (important): The paper contains two categories of results: (A) Complete mathematical proofs : Proposition 3.2 , Lemma 3.12 , Lemma 3.13 , Algorithm 6.3 with full correctness (Lemma 6.5 ), all eight AGM postulates R1–R8 (Theorem 6.9 – 6.12 ), the Disjunctive Entrenchment Lemma (Lemma 6.7 ), the BP-comp Failure Theorem (Theorem 7.1 ), and SSRS Coherence (Theorem 7.4 ); (B) Framework theorems (pending mechanisation) : the RC-elim step of the Canonicity Theorem (Theorem 3.14 ) is currently supported only by a careful hand-verified argument; its full mechanisation is left as future work (Section 12 , Appendix B ). The paper is accompanied by a Coq mechanisation comprising 34 complete proofs with zero admit s for the BP-comp Failure Theorem and SSRS Coherence (Appendix B ). (I) Trace types. We introduce the inductive family 𝖥𝗂𝗇𝖳𝗋𝖺𝖼𝖾 ⁡ ( σ 0 , σ n ) \mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n}) of finite execution traces with typed transition witnesses. Proposition 3.2 proves: 𝖥𝗂𝗇𝖳𝗋𝖺𝖼𝖾 \mathsf{FinTrace} and 𝖲𝗍𝖺𝗋 ⁡ ( 𝖲𝗍𝖾𝗉 ) \mathsf{Star}(\mathsf{Step}) (Kleene closure) are isomorphic as path types but not judgementally equal as MLTT type families; when 𝖲𝗍𝖾𝗉 \mathsf{Step} is viewed as a binary relation, the step function of 𝖳𝗋𝖺𝖼𝖾𝖤𝗅𝗂𝗆 \mathsf{TraceElim} takes the event label e : 𝖤𝗏𝖾𝗇𝗍 e:\mathsf{Event} as an explicit parameter, providing an interface better suited to event-driven induction than 𝖲𝗍𝖺𝗋𝖤𝗅𝗂𝗆 \mathsf{StarElim} (interface design choice, not new expressive power). We give the structural eliminator TraceElim with β ​ η \beta\eta -rules, prove the Trace–Reachability Correspondence (Theorem 3.8 ), the Deterministic Replay Theorem (Theorem 3.10 ), and a full Canonicity framework via logically stratified reducibility candidates with an explicit Transport Lemma (Theorem 3.14 , Lemma 3.12 ; RC-elim mechanisation is future work; all other Core-layer results are Coq-verified). (II) Sheaf semantics. Trace-indexed proposition families are interpreted as contravariant sheaves over the free trace partial-order category T ^ f \widehat{T}_{\!f} . We prove the Separation Theorem distinguishing proof-theoretic monotonicity from semantic non-monotonicity with an explicit countermodel (Theorem 4.5 ), and establish the term model as an initial CwF (Theorem 4.8 ; “completeness” denotes a syntactic universal property, not classical semantic completeness). (III) AGM belief revision. We give a constructive type-theoretic treatment of full AGM revision. Algorithm 6.3 provides an explicit constructive algorithm for partial meet contraction; correctness conditions (C1)–(C4) are fully verified (Lemma 6.5 ). All eight AGM postulates (R1)–(R8) are established as theorems (Theorems 6.9 – 6.12 ). Proofs of R7 and R8 use the Disjunctive Entrenchment Lemma (Lemma 6.7 ), a derivable property of Gärdenfors–Grove entrenchment ordering given here with a fully self-contained constructive proof. Throughout, the core proof calculus is conservative: no structural rules beyond MLTT are added. The categorical model is a CwF extended by the sheaf category T ^ f \widehat{T}_{\!f} , with complete soundness and consistency proofs.

2
Syntax and Judgements

ZX-Calculus is a
trace-indexed epistemic extension built on top of MLTT
:
it does not rebuild a new foundational type theory, but adds new type structures
expressing historical traces, dynamic knowledge, non-monotone revision, and
event-driven state evolution, while keeping all MLTT core rules intact.

2.1
Base Type-Theoretic Framework

We work in MLTT with cumulative universes
\mathsf{U}_{0}:\mathsf{U}_{1}:\mathsf{U}_{2}:\cdots
and
assume
\mathsf{Prop}:\mathsf{U}_{0}
denotes the impredicative universe of propositions with
proof irrelevance. Proof irrelevance distinguishes “whether a proposition holds”
from “what computational content it carries”, preventing unnecessary inflation
of proof objects when only propositional truth is needed.

The system uses standard MLTT judgement forms:

\vdash\Gamma\ \mathsf{ctx}
(well-formed context),

\Gamma\vdash A:\mathsf{U}_{i}
(type),

\Gamma\vdash t:A
(term),

\Gamma\vdash t\equiv u:A
(judgemental term equality),

\Gamma\vdash A\equiv B:\mathsf{U}_{i}
(judgemental type equality).

These judgements are inherited from standard MLTT, so ZX-Calculus preserves:
substitution stability, context extension, type preservation, and constructive
interpretation.
Standard structural rules
Ctx-Empty
,
Ctx-Ext
,
T-Var
,

Conv
,
Weaken
,
Subst
are used unchanged;
the complete rule system is in Appendix
A
.

ZX-Calculus does not modify the core structural rules of MLTT;
all extensions are realised by adding new type structures and semantic
interpretations.

2.2
Dependent Type Structure

\Pi
- and
\Sigma
-types satisfy standard
\beta\eta
-rules.

A\to B
abbreviates
\Pi(\_:A).B
;
A\times B
abbreviates
\Sigma(\_:A).B

\Pi
-types describe parametrised knowledge, event-dependent reasoning, and
trace induction rules.

\Sigma
-types play a particularly central role:

They are used not only for ordinary dependent-pair constructions but also
to express the “constructive selection witness” in AGM revision.

This means that in ZX-Calculus, logical correctness becomes part of the
constructed object itself—a key feature of dependent type theory.

Three base types:

3
Trace Types and Their Meta-Theory

3.1
The Inductive Family FinTrace

In ZX-Calculus, traces are elevated to first-class constructive objects in
dependent type theory—a key distinction from classical operational semantics.

Definition 3.1

(FinTrace as a positive inductive family)
.

Define
\mathsf{FinTrace}:\mathsf{State}\to\mathsf{State}\to\mathsf{U}_{0}
as the inductive family of finite
traces between states, generated by:

\displaystyle\mathsf{nil}

\displaystyle:\Pi(\sigma:\mathsf{State}).\,\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma,\sigma),

\displaystyle\mathsf{step}

\displaystyle:\Pi(\sigma_{0}\,\sigma_{1}\,\sigma_{2}:\mathsf{State}).\,\Pi(e:\mathsf{Event}).\,\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1})\to\mathsf{Step}(\sigma_{1},e,\sigma_{2})\to\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{2}).

Trace length:

\mathsf{len}(\mathsf{nil}(\sigma)):=0
;

\mathsf{len}(\mathsf{step}(\ldots,\tau,\pi)):=\mathsf{len}(\tau)+1
.

\mathsf{FinTrace}
is a strictly positive inductive family (Dybjer–Setzer positivity
criterion): all constructor parameters mention
\mathsf{FinTrace}
only in positive
positions, with no negative nesting.
This guarantees: well-foundedness of the inductive definition, consistency
of the elimination rule, termination of recursion, and preservation of the
constructive character of the type system.
In other words, although traces can express complex historical structures,
they do not disrupt MLTT’s core meta-theoretic properties.

The traditional concern of epistemic logic is:

“What is true in the current state?”

ZX-Calculus is more concerned with:

“How did the system reach its current state?”

\mathsf{FinTrace}
internalises this historical dependency into the type system.

3.2
Comparison with
\mathsf{Star}(\mathsf{Step})

\mathsf{Star}(\mathsf{Step})
is the reflexive-transitive closure of
\mathsf{Step}
, whose
eliminator
\mathsf{StarElim}
performs induction on path length.

\mathsf{FinTrace}
is an inductive family indexed by end-states, whose eliminator

\mathsf{TraceElim}
takes
e:\mathsf{Event}
as an explicit argument.

Proposition 3.2

(Precise comparison)
.

(1)

(Isomorphism)
For any states
\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n}
, there is a
canonical bijection
\iota:\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})\simeq\mathsf{Star}(\mathsf{Step})(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})
.
The two types are equivalent as path collections.

(2)

(Not judgementally equal)
There is no context
\Gamma
in which

\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{FinTrace}\equiv\mathsf{Star}(\mathsf{Step}):\mathsf{State}\to\mathsf{State}\to\mathsf{U}_{0}
.

\mathsf{FinTrace}
has independent constructor signatures and computational behaviour.

(3)

(Interface advantage)
When
\mathsf{Step}
is viewed as a binary
relation,
\mathsf{TraceElim}
’s step function takes
e:\mathsf{Event}
explicitly; any motive
requiring case analysis on the event label benefits directly.
The conclusion is an
interface-level advantage
, not an expressive-power
gain:
\mathsf{StarElim}
can recover event information via

R:=\Sigma(e:\mathsf{Event}).\mathsf{Step}(-,e,-)
, but this requires extra projections

\pi_{1}(\pi)
, reducing readability and naturalness.

Proof.

(1) Bijection.

Define
\iota:\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})\to\mathsf{Star}(\mathsf{Step})(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})

using
\mathsf{TraceElim}
:

\displaystyle\iota(\mathsf{nil}(\sigma))

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{refl}(\sigma),

\displaystyle\iota(\mathsf{step}(\ldots,\tau,\pi))

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{trans}(\iota(\tau),\pi).

The inverse
\iota^{-1}
is defined by induction on
\mathsf{Star}(\mathsf{Step})
:

\displaystyle\iota^{-1}(\mathsf{refl}(\sigma))

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{nil}(\sigma),

\displaystyle\iota^{-1}(\mathsf{trans}(s,\pi))

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{step}(\ldots,\iota^{-1}(s),\pi).

Note: the event
e
can be recovered from
\pi:\mathsf{Step}(\sigma_{1},e,\sigma_{2})
.
By induction,
\iota\circ\iota^{-1}
and
\iota^{-1}\circ\iota
are both
identities.

(2) Not judgementally equal.

The constructor
\mathsf{step}
carries
\Pi(e:\mathsf{Event}).\ldots
as an independent
parameter; in
\mathsf{trans}
, event information is implicit in the type of

\pi
and not an independent constructor argument.
There is no finite
\beta\eta
-reduction sequence unifying the two constructors.
Hence they are not judgementally equal.

(3) Interface gap.

\mathsf{TraceElim}
’s step function
s
receives
e:\mathsf{Event}
explicitly, enabling direct
case analysis on event labels.
When
\mathsf{Step}
is a binary relation,
\mathsf{StarElim}
does not expose events
directly; recovering them via
\Sigma
-encoding requires additional projection
and unpacking.
The primary distinction is therefore:

Whether the event-causal structure becomes a native component of the
trace induction interface.

∎

3.3
TraceElim: The Structural Eliminator

Definition 3.4

(TraceElim)
.

Given a dependent motive

P:\Pi(\sigma_{0}\,\sigma_{n}:\mathsf{State}).\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})\to\mathsf{U}_{i}
,
a base case

b:\Pi(\sigma:\mathsf{State}).P(\sigma,\sigma,\mathsf{nil}(\sigma))
, and a step function

s:\Pi(\sigma_{0}\,\sigma_{1}\,\sigma_{2}:\mathsf{State})(e:\mathsf{Event})(\pi:\mathsf{Step}(\sigma_{0},e,\sigma_{1}))(\tau:\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})).P(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\tau)\to P(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{2},\mathsf{step}(\ldots,\tau,\pi)),

the eliminator
\mathsf{TraceElim}(P,b,s,\tau):P(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n},\tau)
satisfies the

\beta
-rules:

\displaystyle\mathsf{TraceElim}(P,b,s,\sigma,\sigma,\mathsf{nil}(\sigma))

\displaystyle\equiv b(\sigma),

\displaystyle\mathsf{TraceElim}(P,b,s,\sigma_{0},\sigma_{2},\mathsf{step}(\ldots,\tau,\pi))

\displaystyle\equiv s(\ldots,\tau,\pi,\mathsf{TraceElim}(P,b,s,\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\tau)).

The
\eta
-rule (uniqueness): if
f,g
agree on
\mathsf{nil}
and agree on
\mathsf{step}

whenever they agree on the sub-trace, then
f\equiv g
pointwise.
(Coq:
trace_eta
.)

The meaning of
\mathsf{TraceElim}
goes far beyond ordinary recursion.
It expresses the constructive mechanism of knowledge evolving stepwise along
history.

In traditional temporal logic, history is typically an external meta-level
object; the logic can only describe “what holds at a given moment”.
In ZX-Calculus, by contrast,
\tau:\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})
is an
internal object, so
P(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n},\tau)
can depend on:
the start state; the end state; the entire historical path; and the specific
event sequence.
This means we can reason constructively about “how the history was formed”
itself.
For example: whether a piece of knowledge was triggered by a specific event;
at which step a belief was retracted; which histories cause knowledge to fail;
which class of events preserves a given invariant—all can be established
constructively via
\mathsf{TraceElim}
.

A core feature of
\mathsf{TraceElim}
is that
e:\mathsf{Event}
is passed explicitly to the
step function
s
.
This may appear to be just an interface detail, but it is an important
distinction between ZX-Calculus and traditional path induction: knowledge
evolution typically depends on
which event occurred
, not merely

that the state changed
.
ZX-Calculus trace induction is therefore not only induction on path length,
but induction on event-driven knowledge evolution.

The
\eta
-rule is the universal property that makes
\widehat{T}_{\!f}
a free category:
any functor
F:\widehat{T}_{\!f}\to\mathcal{C}
is uniquely determined by its values on
objects and generating steps.
The
\beta
-rules show how to compute; the
\eta
-rule shows that no
non-trivial trace identities exist beyond what the category axioms impose.
This is why all of the subsequent constructions—deterministic replay,
presheaf restriction, SSRS transitions, dynamic knowledge semantics—can
be unified on top of the trace structure.

3.4
Concatenation Algebra

Traces must not only be constructible but also composable.
In a dynamic system, complex histories arise from sequentially concatenating
multiple local histories.
ZX-Calculus needs to define: how to connect traces; how to express a
single-step history; and how to ensure the composition satisfies algebraic laws.

Definition 3.5

(Trace concatenation and single-step trace)
.

Define the trace concatenation operation:

\mathsf{cat}:\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1})\to\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\to\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{2})

by
\mathsf{TraceElim}
-recursion on the first argument:

\mathsf{cat}(\mathsf{nil}(\sigma),\tau_{2}):=\tau_{2},\qquad\mathsf{cat}(\mathsf{step}(\pi,\tau_{1}),\tau_{2}):=\mathsf{step}(\pi,\mathsf{cat}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})).

Intuitively,
\mathsf{cat}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
is the history obtained by first executing
all events in
\tau_{1}
and then all events in
\tau_{2}
.

The
single-step trace
:

\mathsf{single}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},e,\pi):=\mathsf{step}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{1},e,\mathsf{nil}(\sigma_{0}),\pi).

\mathsf{single}
represents the minimal unit of causal history: a single event step.
Any complex trace can therefore be viewed as an iterated concatenation of
single steps, making
\mathsf{single}
the “generator” of the whole trace structure.

Without a stable concatenation structure, none of the dynamic knowledge
semantics that follow can be established:
knowledge depends on traces; AGM revision depends on history extension;
restriction maps depend on the prefix structure; replay depends on trace
composition.

Lemma 3.6

(Concatenation algebra)
.

(1)

(Left unit)

\mathsf{cat}(\mathsf{nil}(\sigma_{0}),\tau)\equiv\tau
.

(2)

(Right unit)

\mathsf{cat}(\tau,\mathsf{nil}(\sigma_{n}))\equiv\tau
.

(3)

(Associativity)

\mathsf{cat}(\mathsf{cat}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2}),\tau_{3})\equiv\mathsf{cat}(\tau_{1},\mathsf{cat}(\tau_{2},\tau_{3}))
.

(4)

(Length additivity)

\mathsf{len}(\mathsf{cat}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))=\mathsf{len}(\tau_{1})+\mathsf{len}(\tau_{2})
.

Proof.

All four identities are proved by
\mathsf{TraceElim}
-induction on the first argument.
In the step case, the
\beta
-step rule unfolds the concatenation definition;
the
Conv
rule completes the judgemental equality transformation.
All proofs are fully mechanised in Coq (
ft_cat_nil_l
,

ft_cat_nil_r
,
ft_cat_assoc
,
ft_len_cat
),
with zero
admit
s.
∎

These three laws show that
\mathsf{FinTrace}
forms a category
\widehat{T}_{\!f}
:

•

The empty trace
\mathsf{nil}(\sigma)
is the identity morphism;

•

Trace concatenation
\mathsf{cat}
is morphism composition;

•

Associativity ensures composition order is unambiguous.

Therefore
\widehat{T}_{\!f}
can be organised as a category.
This categorical structure is why all the subsequent constructions—presheaf
semantics, restriction maps, replay, SSRS transitions, and dynamic knowledge
semantics—can be built on top of
\widehat{T}_{\!f}
.

The single-step trace
\mathsf{single}
may appear to be only a technical notation,
but it is in fact crucial.
It is the
atomic unit
of the whole dynamic structure:

•

Replay only needs to define behaviour at a single step;

•

Presheaf restrictions only need to handle single-step extension;

•

AGM revision only needs to specify how an event changes knowledge;

•

Behaviour over complex histories is obtained recursively by concatenation.

Thus the entire dynamic structure of ZX-Calculus is ultimately built on

\mathsf{single}
and
\mathsf{cat}
.

3.5
Trace–Reachability Correspondence

Definition 3.7

(Reflexive-transitive reachability
\leadsto^{*}
)
.

Define
\leadsto^{*}
by two rules:

•

(Reflexive)

\mathsf{rfl}(\sigma):\sigma\leadsto^{*}\sigma
.

•

(Extension)
if
\pi:\sigma_{0}\xrightarrow{e}\sigma_{1}
and

r:\sigma_{1}\leadsto^{*}\sigma_{2}
, then

\mathsf{ext}(\pi,r):\sigma_{0}\leadsto^{*}\sigma_{2}
.

While
\sigma_{0}\leadsto^{*}\sigma_{n}
is merely a proposition (“there exists
a path”), an element
\tau:\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})
is a piece of
data—a specific, fully recorded causal history.
This is the essential distinction.

Theorem 3.8

(Trace–Reachability Correspondence)
.

For any
\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n}:\mathsf{State}
, there is a canonical isomorphism

\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})\simeq(\sigma_{0}\leadsto^{*}\sigma_{n})
.

Proof.

Define
f:\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})\to(\sigma_{0}\leadsto^{*}\sigma_{n})
by

\mathsf{TraceElim}
:
f(\mathsf{nil}(\sigma)):=\mathsf{rfl}
; each
\mathsf{step}
appends an

\mathsf{ext}
.
Define
g:(\sigma_{0}\leadsto^{*}\sigma_{n})\to\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})
by
induction on
\leadsto^{*}
:
g(\mathsf{rfl}):=\mathsf{nil}
;
g(\mathsf{ext}(\pi,r)):=\mathsf{step}(\ldots,g(r),\pi)
.
By induction,
f\circ g=\mathrm{id}
and
g\circ f=\mathrm{id}
.
∎

ZX-Calculus does not abandon traditional operational semantics; it

internalises
it: the data of how the system arrived at its current state
becomes a first-class object in the type system.

3.6
Deterministic Replay

Definition 3.9

(Replay function)
.

\mathsf{replay}:\Pi(\sigma_{0}\,\sigma_{n}:\mathsf{State}).\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})\to\mathsf{State}

is defined by
\mathsf{TraceElim}
:

\mathsf{replay}(\sigma,\sigma,\mathsf{nil}(\sigma)):=\sigma,\qquad\mathsf{replay}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{2},\mathsf{step}(\ldots,\tau,\pi)):=\sigma_{2}.

Theorem 3.10

(Deterministic Replay)
.

For every
\tau:\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})
:

\mathsf{replay}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n},\tau)\equiv\sigma_{n}:\mathsf{State}
.
Moreover,
\mathsf{replay}(\mathsf{cat}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))\equiv\mathsf{replay}(\tau_{2})
.

Proof.

By
\mathsf{TraceElim}
-induction with motive

Q(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n},\tau):=(\mathsf{replay}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n},\tau)\equiv\sigma_{n})
.

Base case:

\mathsf{replay}(\sigma,\sigma,\mathsf{nil}(\sigma))\equiv\sigma
,
so
\sigma\equiv\sigma
holds by
\mathsf{refl}
.

Step case:

\mathsf{replay}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{2},\mathsf{step}(\ldots,\tau,\pi))\equiv\sigma_{2}

by definition, so
\sigma_{2}\equiv\sigma_{2}
holds by
\mathsf{refl}
.
(In the step case no induction hypothesis is needed: replay returns the
endpoint directly from the constructor.)
The concatenation result follows similarly.
Both results are fully Coq-proved (
deterministic_replay
,

replay_cat
).
∎

Deterministic replay guarantees that the same history always produces the
same final state—a prerequisite for consistent restriction maps and AGM
revision semantics.

The proof is worth noting for a specific detail: in the step case, the
induction hypothesis is
not needed
.
This is because replay returns the endpoint directly from the constructor

\mathsf{step}(\ldots,\tau,\pi)
: the terminal state
\sigma_{2}
is already
embedded in the constructor itself.
This reveals a key design principle:

The trace object carries its own endpoint information inside the
constructor.

Traces are not opaque path objects; they are transparent structured data
from which all relevant semantic information can be directly extracted.

This property has important consequences:

•

If replay were non-deterministic, the knowledge state would also be
non-deterministic;

•

If the same history could produce different endpoints, restriction maps
and revision semantics would lose coherence.

Deterministic replay therefore guarantees that the entire ZX-Calculus dynamic
semantics rests on a stable, unambiguous foundation.

3.7
Canonicity

In type theory, “canonicity” is one of the most central meta-theoretic
properties.
Its intuitive content: every well-typed closed term eventually reduces to a

canonical form
—for natural numbers, canonical forms are

0,\mathsf{succ}(0),\mathsf{succ}(\mathsf{succ}(0)),\ldots
;
for booleans,
\mathsf{true}
or
\mathsf{false}
.
For ZX-Calculus, canonicity means every closed trace term reduces to a form
built from
\mathsf{nil}
and
\mathsf{step}
.

This is critical.
If traces had “spurious canonical forms”, then replay could fail,
presheaf restriction could be unstable, AGM revision could fail to compute
along history, and trace induction could be unreliable.
Canonicity guarantees the entire dynamic knowledge semantics rests on a solid
foundation.

Canonicity proofs typically cannot be carried out by direct syntactic induction,
because: the induction hypothesis refers to subterms of a different type
(dependent motive), and after applying
\mathsf{TraceElim}
, the goal type changes.
We therefore introduce reducibility candidates—a technique tracing back to
Tait, Girard, and Martin-Löf’s strong normalisation proofs.
The core idea: define a family of “well-behaved” sets at each type, and show
every well-typed term belongs to the appropriate set.

Definition 3.11

(Reducibility candidates)
.

For each pair of states
(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})
, define
\mathcal{R}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})

as the minimal set of terms closed under:

•

(RC-nil)

\mathsf{nil}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{R}(\sigma,\sigma)
.

•

(RC-step)
If
\tau\in\mathcal{R}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1})
and

\pi:\mathsf{Step}(\sigma_{1},e,\sigma_{2})
is in canonical form, then

\mathsf{step}(\ldots,\tau,\pi)\in\mathcal{R}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{2})
.

•

(RC-elim)
If base and step arguments lie in their reducibility
families, then
\mathsf{TraceElim}(P,b,s,\ldots,t)\in\mathcal{R}_{P}
.
(This step is left as future work; see Remark
3.15
.)

•

(RC-SN)
Every
t\in\mathcal{R}
is strongly normalising.

For function types
\Pi(x:A).B
,
\mathcal{R}_{\Pi}
consists of strongly normalising
closed terms
f
such that
fa\in\mathcal{R}_{B[a/x]}
for all
a\in\mathcal{R}_{A}
.

Lemma 3.12

(Transport Lemma)
.

If
\Gamma\vdash t:P(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n},\tau)
and

\Gamma\vdash P(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n},\tau)\equiv Q:\mathsf{U}_{i}
, then
t\in\mathcal{R}_{Q}
.

Proof.

By the
Conv
rule,
\Gamma\vdash t:Q
.
Reducibility depends only on the type up to judgemental equality.
Judgemental equality preserves canonical forms, so
\mathcal{R}_{P(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n},\tau)}=\mathcal{R}_{Q}
.
The conclusion follows.
∎

The Transport Lemma fills a critical gap specific to trace-dependent types:
after
\mathsf{TraceElim}
reduces, the goal type typically does not match syntactically.
Without transport of reducibility, the induction step breaks.

Lemma 3.13

(Reducibility closed under
\mathsf{TraceElim}
)
.

Suppose:

(1)

b(\sigma)\in\mathcal{R}_{P(\sigma,\sigma,\mathsf{nil}(\sigma))}
;

(2)

for all
\tau\in\mathcal{R}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1})
, canonical
\pi
, and

r\in\mathcal{R}_{P(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\tau)}
:

s(\ldots,\tau,\pi,r)\in\mathcal{R}_{P(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{2},\mathsf{step}(\ldots,\tau,\pi))}
.

Then
\mathsf{TraceElim}(P,b,s,\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n},\tau)\in\mathcal{R}_{P(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n},\tau)}
.

Proof.

By induction on
\mathsf{len}(\tau)
.

Base case:
By
\beta
-nil and closure under reverse reduction.

Step case:
For
\tau=\mathsf{step}(\ldots,\tau^{\prime},\pi)
, by
\beta
-step:

\mathsf{TraceElim}(\ldots,\mathsf{step}(\ldots))\to_{\beta}s(\ldots,\tau^{\prime},\pi,\mathsf{TraceElim}(\ldots,\tau^{\prime})).

By the induction hypothesis,
\mathsf{TraceElim}(\ldots,\tau^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}_{P(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\tau^{\prime})}
.
By hypothesis (2),
s(\ldots)\in\mathcal{R}_{P(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{2},\mathsf{step}(\ldots))}
.
Finally, apply the Transport Lemma under the
\beta
-step equality to complete
the type transport.
∎

Theorem 3.14

(Canonicity – framework)
.

Every closed term
\vdash t:\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})
reduces to a canonical
form: either
\mathsf{nil}(\sigma)
(with
\sigma_{0}\equiv\sigma\equiv\sigma_{n}

\mathsf{step}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{n},e,\tau^{\prime},\pi^{\prime})
where
\tau^{\prime}
and
\pi^{\prime}
are

\mathcal{R}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})

+(RC-elim).
Step 2: By (RC-SN), every term in
\mathcal{R}
is strongly normalising.
Step 3: The only canonical forms of closed terms of type
\mathsf{FinTrace}
are

\mathsf{nil}
and
\mathsf{step}
, since there are no free variables and no remaining

∎

4.1
The Trace Partial-Order Category
\widehat{T}_{\!f}

Definition 4.1

(Trace category
\widehat{T}_{\!f}
)
.

Define the category
\widehat{T}_{\!f}
as follows:

•

Objects
: states in
\mathsf{State}
.

•

Morphisms
:
\widehat{T}_{\!f}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n}):=\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})

(a trace
\tau
is a morphism from
\sigma_{0}
to
\sigma_{n}
).

•

Identity
:
\mathsf{nil}(\sigma)
(empty trace).

•

Composition
:
\mathsf{cat}
.

Lemma
3.6
supplies the left/right unit laws and associativity,
confirming
\widehat{T}_{\!f}
satisfies the category axioms.

A
prefix morphism

\epsilon:\tau\hookrightarrow\tau^{\prime}
holds iff

\tau^{\prime}=\mathsf{cat}(\tau,\tau^{\prime\prime})
for some
\tau^{\prime\prime}
; this characterises the partial

Proposition 4.2

(Free category)
.

\widehat{T}_{\!f}
is the free category generated by the directed graph
(\mathsf{State},\mathsf{Event},\mathsf{Step})
:
any functor
F:\widehat{T}_{\!f}\to\mathcal{C}
is uniquely determined by its values on objects
and generating steps.
Uniqueness is guaranteed by the
\eta
-rule of
\mathsf{TraceElim}
.

\widehat{T}_{\!f}
is not a groupoid: there is no
\mathsf{sym}
, no invertibility.
Historical causality is one-directional, unlike HoTT paths which are
invertible and composable with higher-dimensional structure.
For example, the two-step path

\sigma_{0}\xrightarrow{e_{1}}\sigma_{1}\xrightarrow{e_{2}}\sigma_{2}

and the direct path

\sigma_{0}\xrightarrow{e_{3}}\sigma_{2}

are
different
morphisms in
\widehat{T}_{\!f}
even if they share endpoints,
because ZX-Calculus tracks specific event sequences, not just reachability.
This is why replay, belief revision, presheaf restriction, and non-monotone
knowledge can all depend on the “specific history” rather than only on the
terminal state.

The “free” in “free category” means: beyond the category axioms themselves,
no additional relations are imposed between traces.
The
\eta
-rule of
\mathsf{TraceElim}
provides the universal property that enforces this:
any functor
F:\widehat{T}_{\!f}\to\mathcal{C}
is uniquely determined by its values on
objects and generating steps.
The
\beta
-rules show how to
compute
with traces; the
\eta
-rule shows
that traces have
no non-trivial identities
beyond those imposed by the
category axioms.

4.2
Knowledge Presheaves and Non-Monotonicity

Definition 4.3

(Knowledge presheaf and softness)
.

A
knowledge presheaf
is a functor
\mathcal{F}:\widehat{T}_{\!f}^{\mathsf{op}}\to\mathbf{Set}

mapping each trace
\tau
to the knowledge state
\mathcal{F}(\tau)
at that
history.
For each prefix extension
\epsilon:\tau\hookrightarrow\tau^{\prime}
, the

restriction map

\mathsf{res}_{\epsilon}:\mathcal{F}(\tau^{\prime})\to\mathcal{F}(\tau)

projects from future history back to past history.
Functoriality requires
\mathsf{res}_{\mathsf{id}}=\mathsf{id}
and

\mathsf{res}_{\epsilon_{1}}\circ\mathsf{res}_{\epsilon_{2}}=\mathsf{res}_{\epsilon_{2}\circ\epsilon_{1}}
.

\mathcal{F}
is
soft
at
\epsilon
iff
\mathsf{res}_{\epsilon}
is surjective
(all past knowledge recoverable from future history).

\mathcal{F}
is
non-monotone
at
\epsilon
iff
\mathsf{res}_{\epsilon}
is
not surjective (some knowledge is lost after extension).

Theorem 4.4

(Non-monotone characterisation)
.

For any
\mathcal{F}
and
\epsilon:\tau\hookrightarrow\tau^{\prime}
, the following
are equivalent: (1)
\mathcal{F}
is non-monotone at
\epsilon
;
(2)
\mathsf{res}_{\epsilon}
is not surjective;
(3)
\mathcal{F}
is not soft at
\epsilon
.

Existence of non-monotone presheaves:

For any
\tau\hookrightarrow\tau^{\prime}
with
\tau\neq\tau^{\prime}
, define

\mathcal{F}_{\epsilon}(\tau^{\prime\prime}):=\begin{cases}\emptyset&\text{if }\tau^{\prime\prime}=\tau^{\prime},\\
\{*\}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then
\mathsf{res}_{\epsilon}:\emptyset\to\{*\}
is not surjective.

Theorem 4.5

(Separation Theorem with explicit countermodel)
.

ZX-Calculus simultaneously exhibits:

(1)

(Proof-theoretic monotonicity)
Weakening, substitution, and
subject reduction hold unconditionally;

(2)

(Semantic non-monotonicity)
There exists a presheaf

\mathcal{F}_{\epsilon}
that is non-soft at
\epsilon
, characterising
non-monotone knowledge;

(3)

(Independence)
One can construct a constantly-soft presheaf

\mathcal{G}(\tau):=\{*\}
(proof-theoretic monotonicity preserved) and a
non-soft presheaf
\mathcal{F}_{\epsilon}
(semantic non-monotonicity present),
both in the same framework.
Hence proof-theoretic monotonicity and semantic non-monotonicity are
independent.

(Coq:
restriction_defined
,
constant_is_soft
,

separation
.)

Remark 4.6
.

This theorem clarifies a long-standing confusion: “knowledge retraction” does
not mean the type theory loses logical stability.
Non-monotonicity arises from the structural properties of restriction maps
under trace extension, not from proof rules.

4.3
Term Model and Initial CwF

We have now constructed: trace type
\mathsf{FinTrace}
, trace category
\widehat{T}_{\!f}
,
presheaf knowledge semantics, AGM revision structure, and CwF semantic
interpretation.
A natural question is: do the syntactic system of ZX-Calculus and these
semantic structures truly correspond to each other?
More concretely: do the types, terms, and proofs we write down receive
consistent interpretations in all models?

“Term model initiality” answers precisely this question.
The
term model

\mathcal{M}_{\mathit{term}}
is the model constructed
directly from ZX-Calculus syntax itself:
types are syntactic types; terms are syntactic terms; substitution is syntactic
substitution; judgemental equality is derivable equality.
The term model is therefore “syntax itself as a model”—a central idea in
type theory that truly unifies the proof system with the semantic structure.

Remark 4.7

(Scope clarification: initiality vs. semantic completeness)
.

The following result is
not
the traditional semantic completeness
theorem of classical logic.
It is a categorical universal property of the ZX-Calculus syntax system.
More precisely: the term model is the initial object among all CwF models.
This means every semantic model must receive its interpretation uniquely from
the syntactic term model.

This differs fundamentally from the traditional assertion
“every semantically valid formula is provable”, which requires: formulas
holding in some class of external semantic models are derivable in the proof
system.

What this result establishes is: ZX-Calculus syntax itself already constitutes
the most basic, most general CwF model.
The “initiality” of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathit{term}}
is a syntax–semantics
unification property, not classical model-theoretic completeness.

Genuine semantic completeness over set-valued presheaf models remains an open
problem (Section
12
).

In category theory, an initial object is the “most free, most general”
object: free groups, free categories, and initial algebras all express the
idea that beyond the structural rules themselves, no additional constraints
are imposed.
Here
\mathcal{M}_{\mathit{term}}
is the “syntactic source” of all
ZX-Calculus semantic models: any CwF-model interpretation must respect the
syntactic structure of ZX-Calculus.

Theorem 4.8

(Initial CwF)
.

The term model
\mathcal{M}_{\mathit{term}}
is the initial CwF model:
for any CwF model
\mathcal{M}
, there exists a unique CwF morphism

!:\mathcal{M}_{\mathit{term}}\to\mathcal{M}
.

The core meaning of Theorem
4.8
is that the syntactic
interpretation of ZX-Calculus is unique.
That is, any model satisfying ZX-Calculus rules must interpret the syntax
in the same way.
Everything—how traces are concatenated, how replay is computed, how presheaf
restriction works, how AGM revision operates—is already determined by the
syntactic structure itself.
Models are not free to assign arbitrary meanings; they are strongly constrained
by ZX-Calculus syntax.

“Existence of the morphism” means syntax is interpretable;
“uniqueness” means this interpretation is essentially unambiguous.
Thus ZX-Calculus syntax is complete enough to uniquely determine its semantic
behaviour—a precise mathematical guarantee of the “syntax-determines-semantics”
principle.

A corollary of the theorem: for any closed type
A
and closed term
t
,

\llbracket t\rrbracket\in\llbracket A\rrbracket
holds in every CwF model if and only if
\vdash t:A
is
derivable in ZX-Calculus.
Note: here “every model” is limited to CwF models; this is “type-theoretic
semantic correctness”, not Gödel completeness in classical first-order logic.

ZX-Calculus is not ordinary MLTT: it simultaneously introduces trace indexing,
presheaf historical semantics, non-monotone knowledge, AGM revision, SSRS,
and dynamic knowledge evolution.
These structures are highly interrelated.
The term model initiality therefore establishes that the entire system remains
unified, coherent, and interpretable—excluding the danger that different
models interpret traces, restriction maps, revision, and replay in mutually
incompatible ways.

Since
\widehat{T}_{\!f}
is a free category (Proposition
4.2
), the term
model is similarly a free CwF model:
ZX-Calculus has not only a “free historical structure” but also a
“free semantic structure”.
This gives the theory strong extensibility, modularity, mechanisation potential,
and formal-verification friendliness.
It is also the important foundation for subsequent mechanisation; see
Appendix
B
for the Coq proofs.

AGM Belief Revision: A Constructive Type-Theoretic Treatment

6.1
Belief States and Epistemic Entrenchment

Definition 6.1

(Belief state and epistemic entrenchment ordering)
.

The
belief state
at trace
\tau
is

\mathsf{B}_{\tau}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
with
\mathsf{B}_{\tau}=\mathsf{Cn}(\mathsf{B}_{\tau})
(deductive closure).

\mathsf{B}_{\tau}
is
consistent
iff
\bot\notin\mathsf{B}_{\tau}
.

The
epistemic entrenchment ordering

(\leq_{\tau})
is a total pre-order on

\mathcal{L}
organised as:

\mathsf{Ent}(\mathsf{B}_{\tau}):=\Sigma(\leq_{\tau}).\mathsf{E1}\times\mathsf{E2}\times\mathsf{E3}\times\mathsf{E4}\times\mathsf{E5},

i.e., “a total pre-order”
+
“proofs that it satisfies the AGM postulates”.

The epistemic entrenchment intuition:
\phi_{1}\leq_{\tau}\phi_{2}
means
“
\phi_{1}
is at least as hard to abandon as
\phi_{2}
”, or equivalently,

\phi_{1}
is more deeply entrenched than
\phi_{2}
.
For example: mathematical axioms are typically maximally entrenched; temporary
observations are weakly entrenched; long-standing regularities are more stable
than one-off perceptions.
When new information conflicts with existing beliefs, the system preferentially
retracts the least-entrenched content.

This
\Sigma
-type is the key feature: ZX-Calculus does not merely assert
“there exists an ordering” but demands “a constructively verified ordering”.

\mathsf{Ent}(\mathsf{B}_{\tau})
is not just an ordering—it is “an ordering”
+

“proofs that it satisfies the AGM postulates”.
This is an important characteristic of dependent type theory: logical
properties themselves become part of the constructed object.

The five postulates and their intuitive meaning:

(E1) Transitivity.

\phi_{1}\leq_{\tau}\phi_{2}
and
\phi_{2}\leq_{\tau}\phi_{3}
imply
\phi_{1}\leq_{\tau}\phi_{3}
.
The comparison of belief stability must be consistent; otherwise the system
exhibits circular preferences.

(E2) Dominance.

If
\phi_{1}\vdash\phi_{2}
then
\phi_{1}\leq_{\tau}\phi_{2}
.
Stronger propositions are at least as stable as their consequences: abandoning
a strong proposition typically means abandoning its conclusions too.

(E3) Conjunctiveness.

For
\phi_{1}\wedge\phi_{2}
, at least one of

\phi_{1}\leq_{\tau}(\phi_{1}\wedge\phi_{2})
or
\phi_{2}\leq_{\tau}(\phi_{1}\wedge\phi_{2})

holds.
The stability of a conjunction comes from at least one of its conjuncts;
otherwise a conjunction would become “spuriously more stable”.

(E4) Minimality.

If the system is consistent and
\psi\notin\mathsf{B}_{\tau}
, then

\neg(\psi\leq_{\tau}\bot)
.
An unbelieved proposition cannot be more stable than a contradiction;
otherwise the system would prefer to retain unbelieved content.

(E5) Maximality.

\phi\leq_{\tau}\top
for all
\phi
.
The tautology
\top
is the least retractable item: logical truths should never
be revoked by a revision system.

The ordering is a total pre-order: any two formulas are comparable, and equal
entrenchment is permitted.
This ensures the system can always decide which belief to retract when conflict
arises—a prerequisite for the contraction algorithm to be executable.

The distinction from the epistemic operator
\mathbf{K}_{a}
:
\mathbf{K}_{a}
discusses what
an agent
knows
(truth-oriented), while
\mathsf{B}_{\tau}
discusses what an
agent is
willing to retain
(revisability-oriented).
ZX-Calculus is therefore not a static epistemic logic but a dynamic
epistemic–doxastic evolution system.

6.2
Partial Meet Contraction: Explicit Algorithm

Definition 6.2

(Selection
\Sigma
-type)
.

Algorithm 6.3

(Entrenchment-ordering contraction)
.

Given

(\leq_{\tau}):\mathsf{Ent}(\mathsf{B}_{\tau})

Step (2): Iterative removal.

S_{i-1}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Intuition: if

those beliefs are non-essential and may be retracted; the system always removes
the least-entrenched sentences first.

Step (3): Deductive closure.

S:=\mathsf{Cn}(S_{m})
.

6.3
Disjunctive Entrenchment Lemma

Lemma 6.7

(Disjunctive Entrenchment Lemma)
.

Let
\leq_{\tau}
be a total pre-order on
\mathcal{L}
satisfying
(E1)–(E3)
.
For any
\alpha,\beta:\mathcal{L}
, if
\alpha\leq_{\tau}\beta
, then

\alpha\vee\beta\equiv_{\leq_{\tau}}\beta

(i.e.,
\alpha\vee\beta
and
\beta
are in the same entrenchment level).

Proof.

Suppose
\alpha\leq_{\tau}\beta
.
We show
\beta\leq_{\tau}\alpha\vee\beta
and
\alpha\vee\beta\leq_{\tau}\beta
.

Step 1:
\beta\leq_{\tau}\alpha\vee\beta
.

By propositional logic,
\beta\vdash\alpha\vee\beta
.
By dominance (E2),
\beta\leq_{\tau}\alpha\vee\beta
.

Step 2:
\alpha\vee\beta\leq_{\tau}\beta
.

Note the propositional identity
(\alpha\vee\beta)\wedge\beta\equiv_{\mathit{prop}}\beta
.
By (E2):
\beta\leq_{\tau}(\alpha\vee\beta)\wedge\beta
and

(\alpha\vee\beta)\wedge\beta\leq_{\tau}\beta
.

Apply conjunctiveness (E3) to the pair
(\alpha\vee\beta,\beta)
.
One of the following holds:

(1)

\alpha\vee\beta\leq_{\tau}(\alpha\vee\beta)\wedge\beta
.
By transitivity (E1) and
(\alpha\vee\beta)\wedge\beta\leq_{\tau}\beta
:

\alpha\vee\beta\leq_{\tau}\beta
.

(2)

\beta\leq_{\tau}(\alpha\vee\beta)\wedge\beta
.
Then
\beta\equiv_{\leq_{\tau}}(\alpha\vee\beta)\wedge\beta
.
By totality,
\alpha\vee\beta
and
\beta
are comparable.
If
\beta<_{\tau}\alpha\vee\beta
, apply (E3) to
(\alpha\vee\beta,\beta)
again;
using
(\alpha\vee\beta)\wedge\beta\equiv_{\mathit{prop}}\beta
, we can only
get
\alpha\vee\beta\leq_{\tau}(\alpha\vee\beta)\wedge\beta\leq_{\tau}\beta
,
contradicting
\beta<_{\tau}\alpha\vee\beta
.
Hence
\alpha\vee\beta\leq_{\tau}\beta
.

Combining Steps 1 and 2:
\alpha\vee\beta\equiv_{\leq_{\tau}}\beta
.
Since
\alpha\leq_{\tau}\beta
,
\beta=\max_{\leq_{\tau}}(\alpha,\beta)
, so

\alpha\vee\beta
and
\max_{\leq_{\tau}}(\alpha,\beta)
are at the same
entrenchment level.
∎

Remark 6.8

(“Maximum entrenchment” of disjunctions)
.

Lemma
6.7
shows that in an AGM-type entrenchment ordering,
the entrenchment of
\alpha\vee\beta
does not exceed its most stable disjunct:

\mathrm{rank}(\alpha\vee\beta)=\max(\mathrm{rank}(\alpha),\mathrm{rank}(\beta)).

This is consistent with the “conservative expansion” philosophy: adding
weaker information (a disjunction) does not elevate its epistemic priority.
The property is crucial in the partial meet contraction algorithm and Grove
sphere-model representation, ensuring “weaker information is not harder to
retract than its supporting disjuncts”.

This Disjunctive Entrenchment Lemma is the key that unlocks the proofs of
R7 (Theorem
6.11
) and R8 (Theorem
6.12
).
In particular, the key step
(**)
in the proof of R8 relies essentially on
this lemma: the fact that
\neg\psi\vee\neg\chi
and
\neg\chi
are at the
same entrenchment level (when
\neg\psi\leq_{\tau}\neg\chi
) ensures that
the contraction algorithm treats them identically, establishing the crucial
equality
\mathsf{B}_{\tau}\mathbin{\div}(\neg\psi\vee\neg\chi)=\mathsf{B}_{\tau}\mathbin{\div}\neg\chi
.

6.4
All Eight AGM Postulates as Theorems

Classical AGM theory (Alchourrón–Gärdenfors–Makinson) answers:
when a rational agent receives new information
\psi
, how should it minimally
change its belief set?
AGM decomposes this into expansion, contraction, and revision, connected by
the Levi identity
K*\psi=(K\div\neg\psi)+\psi
.

Theorem 6.9

(AGM postulates R1–R8)
.

Under the Levi revision
\mathsf{B}_{\tau}*\psi=\mathsf{Cn}((\mathsf{B}_{\tau}\mathbin{\div}\neg\psi)\cup\{\psi\})

the following hold as theorems:

The six basic AGM postulates (R1)–(R6) characterise local rationality of
the revision operation:
(R1) and (R5) ensure the revised knowledge system remains a rational theory;
(R2) confirms revision genuinely accepts new information;
(R3) establishes minimal-change character;
(R4) shows revision degenerates to ordinary expansion when no conflict exists;
(R6) ensures revision depends only on logical content, not syntactic form.

Theorem 6.10

(R1–R6)
.

x

(R1)

(R2)

(R1).

(R3).

(R5).
Follows from (R1).

(R6).
If

•

Superexpansion (R7): simultaneous revision is no weaker than sequential
revision;

•

Subexpansion (R8): if the new information is consistent with the result
of the first revision, then sequential and simultaneous revision agree.

Together they control how the revision operation should coordinate multiple
pieces of new information entering the system simultaneously.

AGM postulates (R7) and (R8) encode the “minimal change principle” for
simultaneous revision by conjunctions—the deepest rationality constraints.

7
Coherence: Integrating Traces, Sheaves, and Belief Revision

This section answers a structural question: can AGM belief revision be lifted
to a strict “trace functor semantics”? If not, what is the essential nature
of the failure?

7.1
Structural Tension Between Sheaf Structure and AGM

In categorical semantics, a “sheaf-like structure” expresses a strong local
consistency principle: the composition of local updates must equal the direct
global update.
This is precisely the content of (BP-comp):

\mathsf{res}_{\epsilon_{1}}\circ\mathsf{res}_{\epsilon_{2}}=\mathsf{res}_{\epsilon_{2}\circ\epsilon_{1}}.

However, AGM revision’s “minimal change principle” is
semantically
sensitive
, not path-invariant.
Belief revision depends on: the current belief set’s structure; the entrenchment
ordering
\leq_{\tau}
; the “substitute influence” of removed formulas; and
whether subsequent information changes the conflict-resolution interpretation.

Revision is not function application, but a “structural rebalancing
process”.

Traces, knowledge, and revision are uniformly characterised in a
single categorical semantics.

The goal of this semantic lifting is:

•

To interpret “event sequences” as chains of morphisms in a category;

•

To interpret “belief types” as type families dependent on context;

•

To interpret “AGM revision” as a structure-preserving semantic
transformation.

ZX-Calculus’s core characteristic is its three-layer dependency structure:

\text{Trace}\;\to\;\text{Type Dependency}\;\to\;\text{Knowledge Update.}

The CwF model provides a standard semantic framework for exactly this pattern:
objects are contexts; morphisms are context extensions; type families depend on
context; terms are evidence or constructions in context.
ZX-Calculus is therefore interpreted as a

trace-indexed dependent type system
.

Definition 8.1

(ZX-Calculus CwF model and interpretation)
.

The CwF model consists of a category
\mathcal{C}
with terminal object,
type functor
\mathbf{Ty}:\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{op}}\to\mathbf{Set}
,
term functor
\mathbf{Tm}
, and projection structure.

\mathbf{Ty}
is extended to interpret:
trace type
\mathsf{FinTrace}
, knowledge operator
\mathbf{K}_{a}
, and AGM
\Sigma
-structure.

The interpretation mapping:

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})\rrbracket

\displaystyle:=\widehat{T}_{\!f}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n}),

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathsf{nil}(\sigma)\rrbracket

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{id}_{\sigma},

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathsf{step}(\ldots,\tau,\pi)\rrbracket

\displaystyle:=\llbracket\tau\rrbracket\cdot\llbracket\pi\rrbracket,

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathbf{K}_{a}(\phi,\tau)\rrbracket

\displaystyle:=\prod_{\tau^{\prime}:\tau\sim_{a}\tau^{\prime}}\llbracket\phi(\tau^{\prime})\rrbracket.

The interpretation of
\mathbf{K}_{a}
has key significance:

\mathbf{K}_{a}
is interpreted as a global quantification over all reachable future
states—a dependent product ensuring knowledge consistency across all reachable
branches.

\prod
denotes the dependent product;
\tau\sim_{a}\tau^{\prime}
denotes the
state transition induced by action
a
.
Thus
\mathbf{K}_{a}
is essentially a global constraint operator ensuring knowledge
consistency over all reachable branches.

8.2
Key Structural Properties

(1)

Compositionality:

\llbracket\tau_{1}\cdot\tau_{2}\rrbracket=\llbracket\tau_{1}\rrbracket\circ\llbracket\tau_{2}\rrbracket
.
Knowledge evolution corresponds to categorical composition.

(2)

Context stability:

Type interpretation depends only on the current trace context, not global
history.
Types are localised structures, not globally-dependent entities.

(3)

Dependent closure:

\mathbf{Ty}
is closed under trace extension.

These three properties together guarantee that ZX-Calculus’s semantics is not
externally imposed but endogenous to the categorical structure.
The CwF model reveals three essential characteristics:

•

Traces are morphisms
: knowledge evolution corresponds to
categorical composition.

•

Knowledge is a dependent type
: beliefs are context-dependent
structures.

•

Revision is a semantic transformation
: AGM revision corresponds
to structural rewriting on morphisms.

8.3
Soundness and Consistency

Theorem 8.2

(Soundness and consistency)
.

(1)

(Soundness)
If
\Gamma\vdash t:A
is derivable in ZX-Calculus,
then in any CwF model:
\llbracket\Gamma\rrbracket\vdash\llbracket t\rrbracket\in\llbracket A\rrbracket
.

(2)

(Consistency)
There is no closed term
t
with
\vdash t:\bot
.

Proof.

(1)
By induction on derivation structure:
variable rules are preserved by projection morphisms;
abstraction rules by exponential objects;
application rules by morphism composition;
dependent-type rules by functoriality of
\mathbf{Ty}
.
All syntactic derivations are therefore preserved at the semantic level.

(2)
If
\vdash t:\bot
existed, then
\llbracket t\rrbracket\in\llbracket\bot\rrbracket=\emptyset
,
which is impossible. Hence the system is consistent.
∎

9.1
Structural Interpretation of Meta-Properties

Under the CwF semantics:

•

Weakening
corresponds to context projection;

•

Substitution
corresponds to functoriality of morphisms;

•

Subject reduction
corresponds to sound reduction of computation
rules in the semantics.

These meta-properties are therefore not essentially syntactic properties but:

Direct reflections of structure-preservation in the categorical
semantics.

Theorem 9.1

(Weakening, substitution, and subject reduction)
.

(1)

(Weakening)
If
\Gamma\vdash t:A
and
\Gamma\vdash B:\mathsf{U}_{i}
,
then
\Gamma,x:B\vdash t:A
.

(2)

(Substitution)
If
\Gamma,x:A\vdash t:B
and
\Gamma\vdash s:A
,
then
\Gamma\vdash t[s/x]:B[s/x]
.

(3)

(Subject reduction)
If
\Gamma\vdash t:A
and
t\to_{\beta}t^{\prime}
,
then
\Gamma\vdash t^{\prime}:A
.

Proof.

(1)
In CwF,
\Gamma\vdash A
corresponds to
\mathbf{Ty}(\Gamma)
.
Context extension
\Gamma,x:B
corresponds to a projection morphism

\pi:\Gamma.B\to\Gamma
.
Since
\mathbf{Ty}
is a contravariant functor, any type judgement holding at

\Gamma
can be pulled back along
\pi
to
\Gamma.B
.

(2)
Substitution corresponds to the key CwF structure

\mathbf{Tm}(\Gamma.A)\cong\mathbf{Tm}(\Gamma)\circ A
.
Given
\Gamma,x:A\vdash t:B
and
\Gamma\vdash s:A
,
the term
s:\Gamma\to A
is a morphism at the semantic level.
The substituted term
t[s/x]
corresponds to
\llbracket t\rrbracket\circ s
;
since
\mathbf{Tm}
is a natural family over context changes,
substitution preserves type consistency and
B[s/x]
is the pullback of
B

along
s
.

(3)
If
t\to_{\beta}t^{\prime}
, the reduction corresponds to a computation
rule in CwF, specifically
\Pi
- and
\Sigma
-type
\beta
-rules, and the
deductive closure stability of AGM structures.
All new constructors (
\mathsf{FinTrace}
,
\mathsf{TraceElim}
,
\mathbf{K}_{a}
) are designed as
semantics-preserving structural operators, so
\llbracket t\rrbracket=\llbracket t^{\prime}\rrbracket
.
Combining with soundness:
\Gamma\vdash t^{\prime}:A
.
∎

10.2
Distributed Systems: SSRS in a Restricted Model

Remark 10.1

(Relationship with FLP impossibility)
.

An earlier draft claimed to characterise the FLP impossibility theorem.
After review: the FLP theorem concerns
asynchronous
systems with
process crashes and message delays, while the current SSRS framework is based
on a
synchronous
event-driven model.
The model assumptions are fundamentally different and a formal equivalence
cannot be established through informal analogy.
This section is revised to the following precisely scoped weaker proposition.

Proposition 10.2

(Belief retraction under synchronous partition model)
.

Let
G
be a group of agents,
\tau
the current global trace, and

e_{\mathit{part}}
a partition event.
Assume: (H1) the system is modelled by a synchronous trace model;
(H2)
e_{\mathit{part}}
is modelled as a single event step in the trace;
(H3) there exists
a\in G
with
\neg\psi_{e_{\mathit{part}}}\in\mathsf{B}_{\tau}
.
Then by Theorem
7.4
(RS-surj):

\mathsf{B}_{\tau}\not\subseteq\mathsf{B}_{\tau^{\prime}}
,
i.e., agent
a
’s belief set strictly shrinks after trace extension, and its
assumption about global consistency is structurally retracted.

Proof.

By (H3) and SSRS Definition
7.2
,

\neg\psi_{e_{\mathit{part}}}\in\mathsf{B}_{\tau}
triggers belief retraction;
by Theorem
7.4
(RS-surj),

\mathsf{B}_{\tau^{\prime}}=\mathsf{B}_{\tau}*\psi_{e_{\mathit{part}}}
, so

\mathsf{B}_{\tau}\not\subseteq\mathsf{B}_{\tau^{\prime}}
.
∎

Limitations.

Proposition
10.2
depends on assumptions (H1)–(H3),
especially the synchrony assumption (H1).
The FLP impossibility theorem treats asynchronous systems, which is beyond the
current framework’s modelling capability.
Extending SSRS to asynchronous models is left as future work
(Section
12
).
