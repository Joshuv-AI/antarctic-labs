 1. Executive Summary

The overall objective of this work is to perform a pre-Front End Engineering Design (pre-FEED)
study of the conversion of associated gas produced at an active oil pad of a Bakken oil field
producer, to high value carbon black and hydrogen using the Microwave Plasma Pyrolysis of
Associated Gas (MPP-AG) process developed by H Quest Vanguard, Inc (HQV).

The University of North Dakota’s Center for Process Engineering Research (CPER) collaborated
with HQV to complete a design and techno-economic analysis for the MPP-AG deployed at an
active Bakken well site flaring an average of 54,000 standard cubic feet per day of AG. The
proposed design consists of:

 • A Microwave Pyrolysis Unit (MPU) which consists of H Quest’s proprietary MPP
 technology
 • A patent-pending Gas Conditioning Unit (GCU) designed by the University of North
 Dakota to condition intermittent and variable flow gas prior to pyrolysis.
 • An auxiliary unit running on the associated gas to power the GCU and MPU in an “island
 mode” configuration.

The completed technical objectives of the project include:

 • Develop a techno-economic assessment (TEA) of the process that includes experimental
 testing with Bakken-simulated associated gas to determine site-specific energy
 requirements and conversion metrics,
 • Perform a technology gap assessment,
 • Develop a technology maturation plan targeting a technology readiness level (TRL) of 9
 by 2030,
 • Develop a commercialization plan at a specific host site,
 • Develop an environmental information volume for the process and target site.

Two designs of the MPP-AG were completed:

 • A single train system optimized for the selected well site and equipped with a 133 kWe
 microwave system,
 • A four-train, commercial-scale system equipped with four 133 kWe microwave modules.

The four-train design which corresponds to the commercial system was oversized for the host site,
requiring the scale-down of the single train.

 1

The evaluated technology offers significant market advantage for commercialization, including
positive revenue based only on revenue from the sale of carbon black. The cost results for the
proposed technology corresponds to a class 3 estimate cost (AACE International Recommended
Practice No. 18R 97) with a range accuracy of -15%/+30%.

 2. Concept Description

 Flaring in Bakken (North Dakota)

Approximately 95% of the gas produced in the Bakken is sold following successful
implementation of effective gas flaring capture regulation. 1 However, with an estimated 3 BCF
per day of associated gas production anticipated in 2025, 2 flaring volumes in the Bakken are
expected to exceed 62 BCF in 2025.

Animated density heatmaps were generated to understand geographic and temporal flaring trends
in the Bakken. These visualizations mapped monthly flaring volumes by geographic coordinates,
allowing for dynamic inspection of flaring “hotspots” over time, see Section 7. This method also
enabled the identification of persistently high-flaring regions with significant variability observed.
Only one region consistent flaring across the period evaluated, attributed to a collection of wells.

The results suggest any viable flaring mitigation solution needs to be flexible and mobile so it can
be quickly mobilized and installed between flaring locations.

 Proposed Solution

The MPP-AG system is designed as a fully modular system consisting of three trailers that could
be mobilized/de-mobilized within 24 hours and requires only power (electric or associated gas)
for its operation.

The proposed commercial design targets the production of up to 2 metric ton per day of carbon
black from 360 thousand standard cubic feet per day (MSCFD) with the “island” mode
configuration, or, 140 MCFD for a grid powered design. This is achieved with four microwave
trains in the MPU system.

A scaled-down, unoptimized design was proposed for the identified host site equipped with a
single microwave train in the MPU with all other major equipment scaled down to meet the lower
capacity. This scaled-down system produces 250 - 500 kg/day of carbon black from 90 MSCFD
with the “island” mode configuration, or, 34 MSCFD for a grid powered design. The footprint of
the smaller unit is identical to the scale of the commercial system. Figure 2-1 is a 3D rendering of
the MPP-AG system including the carbon black storage trailer for the scaled-down design.

Two key benefits are achieved with adoption of the MPP-AG technology for mitigating flaring
of associated gas at well sites:

 1) flare gas valorization, made possible thanks to H Quest’s unique process that produces a
 high value carbon black , which satisfies requirements for tread-grade (N2XX and N3XX)
 commercial carbon black grades, providing up to fourty-fold increase in revenue (assuming
 80% conversion carbon in associated gas to carbon black and $2.0/kg carbon black sale
 price), see Figure 2-2.

 2) up to 68% Reduction in CO2 emissions. CO2 reductions were estimated for a first-of-a-
 kind (FOAK) and an Nth-of-a-kind (NOAK) system. The FOAK design adopts
 commercially available electric generators with a maximum H2 blend of 20 – 25%. In this
 design, the MPP-AG cannot operate on only the H2-rich gas and requires associated gas
 from the well pad. The combustion of associated gas to power the system limits the avoided
 CO2 emissions to under 50%. The NOAK design assumes integration of a genset operating
 with the H2-rich gas from the MPU system, and achieves up to 68% avoided CO2 at the
 well site. Several equipment manufacturers report future plans to produce an electric
 generator which can be powered by over 50% H2.

The MPP-AG process is ready for commercial-scale field demonstration to advance technology to
market.

The development status of the two main components that require field demonstration are:
 • GCU: A detailed design and control scheme has been completed by the CPER. All major
 and minor equipment proposed in the design are commercially available with no research
 and development activities required. A patent filing has been initiated and discussions are

 Performance Definition via Experimental Work

 3.2.1. Product Gas Composition (Experimental)

Pure methane and pure propane were converted using H Quest’s 36-kWh microwave pyrolysis
system and the conversion data used to develop an Aspen Plus process model. The 36-kWh system
supports fast (sub 100 milliseconds) residence times and rapid product gas quenching from
1,100°C with no heat recovery. The lack of heat recovery to pre-heat inlet gases increases the
energy consumed per kg carbon black produced and limits the conversion of the feed products to
carbon black.

To accurately estimate the performance of the system, the product gas composition obtained during
experimental work was used to develop a heat and mass balance in the Aspen Plus. For conversion
of methane and propane, the experimental results were adopted. For conversion of larger
hydrocarbons (4+, not tested experimentally), conversion was assumed to be 100%.

 3.2.2. Physical and Chemical Characteristics of Produced Carbon

Carbon black produced from by the 36 kWh MPP system from pipeline natural gas and recovered
directly from the system’s carbon capture and handling unit before any further processing was
delivered to UND for analysis of physical properties – density, particle size, heat capacity, and
elementary composition.

               3.2.2.1. Density

The apparent density was measured at 1.45 g/cc by gas Pycnometry using a Micromeritics AccPyc
II 1345. The bulk density was also measured at 0.02 g/cc. The material will require compaction
before storage to reduce storage requirements. Preliminary testing showed up to tenfold increase
in bulk density using a hand-press compression die. H Quest has developed and is testing a
compacting process for the carbon black that will be integrated in the MPU system.

               3.2.2.2. Particle Size

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a FEI Quanta 400F SEM at a high voltage (HV) of
30KV and working distance of ~12mm was used to determine the particle size distribution, see
Figure 3-3. The particle size ranged from 5-25µm, with the majority of particles falling between 5
and 10µm.

The three case studies evaluated using Aspen modelling are summarized below:
 (1) Modelling of experimental conditions “as-is” (no heat recovery). This model set the
 baseline for the heat mass balance of the process and estimated the heat lost to the
 environment.
 (2) Model update to include heat recovery from the product gas. Key aspects of this case
 include:
 o The feed was preheated by recovering heat from the product gas stream.
 o The composition of the product gas was kept identical to experimental.
 o 10% of the incoming methane, ethane and propane was not converted and leaves in
 the product gas.
 o 100% of heavier hydrocarbons (C4+) were converted

 This model simulates the scenario where the plasma is not quenched by the cold feed
 gas achieving higher temperatures in the reactor and improving carbon black yields
 significantly.
 (3) Model update to simulate the commercial performance target of 80% conversion of the
 carbon atoms in the feed gas being converted to carbon black. To achieve the higher
 conversion, an equilibrium reactor (RGibbs) was adopted with the following key
 assumptions:
 o Only 80% of the carbon in the feed can be converted to carbon black
 o 10% of the incoming methane, ethane and propane was not converted and leaves in
 the product gas.

 12

 o 100% of heavier hydrocarbons (C4+) were converted
 o Balance of carbon was equimolarly distributed between hydrocarbon species
 identified experimentally
 o Heat recovery was assumed the same as for case 2.

 This model is a preliminary simulation of H Quest’s commercial system which will be
 equipped with 100-kWth microwaves, longer gas residence times and the heat recovery
 system.

 3.4.1.1. Gas Conditioning Unit (GCU)

The GCU is a patent-pending process developed at the University to handle intermittent flare gas
at the well site. The operating philosophy includes receiving intermittent and variable flow gas
from the well pad, conditioning the gas to remove natural gas liquids and providing a consistent

 14

gas composition and flow to the MPU unit. The system comprises a compressor block, piping and
instrumentation to facilitate operation.

 3.4.1.2. Microwave Pyrolysis Unit (MPU)

The MPU system consists of a microwave pyrolysis conversion unit, a two-stage heat exchanger
to preheat inlet gases with the product stream, solids separation and storage, and hydrogen
discharge to the auxiliary unit and the flare. The entire process is designed by H quest to be a drop-
in, stand-alone system with one inlet (AG), two outlets (Carbon black and hydrogen) and electric
power hookups. The baseline design for the system assumes a 62% MPU efficiency; 25%
efficiency losses from the microwave and 13% thermal losses from the design of the MPU reactor.
The design basis was determined from experimental and modelling data in Section 3.3.

Current research and development (R&D) by H Quest seeks to optimize performance of the MPU
primarily by recovering heat from the product gas and adopting four 133-kWe microwave
generators for the commercial containerized system. Figure 3-8 summarizes current and projected
performance for the MPU carbon production capacity once optimization is completed. Sensitivity
analyses on the impacts of these optimizations on final costs are discussed below.

 3.4.1.3. Auxiliary System

The auxiliary system consists of an off-the-shelf electric generator and chiller, and an
instrumentation control panel. A 175 kWe generator was selected to provide power for the MPU
and balance of plant at the host site. This commercially-ready generator operates at maximum
hydrogen concentrations of 20 vol.%, which corresponds to ~16 MSCFD of H2-rich product gas
blended with 42 MSCFD of associated gas.

 15

Following outreach to manufacturers of more efficient emerging hydrogen power generation
technologies that can operate with over 50 vol.% hydrogen 3,4 determined these new systems were
either not commercially available at the desired scale or were not currently offered in the US.
When these systems become available, a 39% or higher generation efficiency will be ensure the
MPP-AG is powered entirely by the hydrogen-rich product gas.

A 17-ton chiller (57 kW thermal) provides cooling for the process and the product streams to under
60°C. The nominal power consumption of the chiller is approximately 15 kWe.

An Instrumentation and controls panel ensures automated operation of the GCU, MPU and
auxiliaries. A 2 kWe is assumed for powering the controls.

 3.4.1.4. Hydrogen Flare

A hydrogen flare is required for the unused product gas stream and the existing flare at the well
pad was assumed to be suitable.

 3.4.2. Operation Philosophy

To handle flare volume intermittency, an automated flow control valve installed on the flare gas
piping diverts associated gas from the flare to a compressor (Blackmer HDL172C, capacity range;
10-17 MSCFD) and storage in a tube trailer at 40 bara (FIBA Technologies 8 tubes Jumbo Trailer,
12 MSCF capacity). Condensed liquids are recovered during the compression step and returned
to the oil tanks. The storage tank is used to deliver a steady state flow to the generator and the
MPU unit and therefore avoid hard stops during operation. A process scheme was developed to
operate the compressor smoothly with intermittent and variable associated gas flow from the host
site. This design results in two operating modes:

 1. A batch mode where the volume of flared gas from the host site is less than 85 MSCFD.
 In this scenario, the storage tank pressure decreases during operation until a minimum
 acceptable pressure in the storage tank at which the MPU and electric generator are turned
 off. With the main electric generator off, the compressor is powered from grid energy or
 an optional, smaller, auxiliary generator to re-pressurize the storage tank to 40 bars, see
      Figure 3-9.

2. A continuous mode where the volume of flared gas is equal to or greater than 85 MSCFD

 4.1.3. Process and Project Contingency

Project contingency generally compensates for cost uncertainties and construction risk associated
with final design and construction of the project until completion. However, deployment of the
MPP-AG does not require on-site construction, therefore the primary source of cost uncertainties
is inflation. Given the small and modular design of the MPP-AG, the manufacturing period is
anticipated to not exceed three months, minimizing inflation-related cost uncertainties. Therefore,
no project contingency is applied to the MPP-AG system.

Process contingency generally compensates for unforeseen process modifications required during
demonstration. The most expensive component of all systems in the MPP-AG – compressor (for
GCU), microwave (for MPU) and generator (power island) are commercially mature systems with

 18

performance guarantees from vendors. Therefore, process risks arise from all other components
required to integrate these systems. Hence, process contingency is taken as 100% of the current
procurement cost of all the minor equipment, piping and instrumentation; calculated as 15% of
total equipment cost.

5. Technology Gap Assessment (TGA)

We identify three technology gaps with all currently being addressed by H Quest:

 • MPU Performance: the lack of experimental data developed with the specific gas
 composition of the host site. Range of performance was approached from a single design
 point of experimental data conducted on a smaller scale system and using pure methane
 and propane gas. Future demonstration work utilizing the commercial-scale microwave
 modules (133 kWe) is recommended to update the heat and mass balance and validate final
 product gas composition and feed carbon to carbon black conversion (carbon black yield).

 • Product Heat Recovery: this technical gap and risk is around the deployment of heat
 exchangers to improve the efficiency of the process.

 • Carbon Recovery: This technology gap and risk is focused on the automated recovery and
 compaction of the carbon black.

 • GCU: this is a patent-pending concept that utilizes equipment that are commercially
 available with the only identified technical risks and gap around the operation of the
 process at a relevant scale. No R&D related risks are expected.

6. Technology Maturation Plan

 Project Progress and Impact on Technology Readiness Level

The MPP-AG consists of three components – the Gas Conditioning Unit (GCU), the Methane
Pyrolysis Unit (MPU) and the Auxiliary Unit. The TRL at the end of the project is a 6 for the MPU
and at a 4 for the GCU. The Auxiliary unit comprises commercial support systems and process
controls and is not included in the TRL evaluation, since all its key components are at a TRL of 9.

Figure 3-7 depicts the updated First-of-a-Kind (FOAK) design with key changes:
 ● The design of the gas conditioning process was completed and adopts off-the-shelf
 commercial equipment that require no significant research and development.
 ● A heat recovery system integrated with the solid separation system was designed by H
 Quest with testing ongoing. This activity was not part of the current project scope but will
 continue beyond project schedule.
 ● A commercially available generator with a 20 vol.% limit on amount of Hydrogen (H2)
 blended was adopted for “island-mode” operation of the system, requiring the addition of
 a flare system with this FOAK design. This decision was made since commercially
 available H2-ready electric generators can only accept 20 – 30 vol.% H2 blends.

 6.2.1. Gas Conditioning Unit (GCU)

A patent-pending gas conditioning process was designed as part of this project to handle the
intermittency of flaring observed at the select host site. The process is designed for well sites with
significant variability in flare gas volumes that make deploying the MPP unit remotely
challenging.
 ● Scope and Level of Integration: The proposed conditioning system adopts commercial
 equipment all at TRL 9, however, the integration required to handle intermittency has not
 been demonstrated in a laboratory or field setting.
 ● Fidelity: The fidelity of respective components is high, while the fidelity of the proposed
 process is low and requires demonstration to achieve a TRL of 6 or greater.
 ● Environment and Scale of Testing: No testing has been performed on the process as
 designed.

7. Commercialization Plan

 Background – Flaring in The United States

The United States hosts several productive oil and gas basins, each contributing significantly to
national energy production. Among these, the Permian Basin, Bakken, Eagle Ford Shale, and
Niobrara Basin stand out due to their substantial hydrocarbon outputs and associated flaring
activities. Figure 7-1 illustrates the geographical locations of these major basins.

 Figure 7-1 Geographical location of major U.S. Oil and Gas basins 6

 7.1.1. Permian Basin (Texas & New Mexico)

The Permian Basin is the largest oil and natural gas producing area in the U.S., with production at
around 5.5 million barrels of oil and 23 billion cubic feet (BCF) of natural gas reported per day in
2023 7. In 2023, Over 156 and 24.6 BCF of natural gas was reported flared in Texas and New
Mexico respectively. 8 In the same year, 54,860 producing wells were reported in Texas and 22,681
in New Mexico. 9 Lower regulatory constraints account for the higher flaring volumes per well

 33

observed in Texas meanwhile New Mexico is implementing an aggressive 98% capture target by
the end of 2026. 10

 7.1.2. Niobrara Basin (Colorado & Wyoming)

The Niobrara Basin, situated in eastern Wyoming and northeastern Colorado, is another major oil
and gas-producing area. In 2023, flaring volumes of 0.641 BCF and 59.0 BCF were reported in
Colorado and Wyoming, with over 9,800 active oil wells and 23,000 active oil wells reported
respectively. 11 Colorado flares significantly less natural gas per well than Wyoming due to stricter
regulations and more developed gas capture infrastructure. 12

 7.1.3. Texas (Eagle Ford Shale and Permian)

The Eagle Ford Shale accounted for 8.8% of U.S. oil production in 2022 with natural gas
production averaging 7.1 BCF/d reported in 2023. 13 Texas in 2023 produced about 156 BCF of
flared gas.11 Flaring data for the Eagle Ford are not broken out specifically, but the large oil and
gas production from the basin likely accounts for a significant share of the flaring volume within
the state 14. Many of the oil and gas producing wells are in the Eagle Ford Shale, mostly in Railroad
Commission districts 1 through 6.3 Over 37,000 active wells were reported in 2024. 15

 7.1.4. Bakken (North Dakota)

Approximately 95% of the gas produced in the Bakken is sold following implementation of
effective capture regulation. 16 However, with an estimated 3 BCF per day of associated gas
production anticipated in 2025, 17 flaring volumes in the Bakken are expected to exceed 62 BCF in
2025. A detailed evaluation of flaring in the Bakken is presented further in below.

 Proposed Business Models

The preferred commercialization pathway would maximize revenues per unit by minimizing de-
mobilization events (cost) while maximizing carbon production (capacity factor/revenues). Based
on the market potential evaluation, two business models are proposed to capture these two
objectives.

 7.3.1. Carbon Black Production Model

In this business model, the primary commercialization plan is the production of carbon black from
flare gas. The potential market are wells with high volumes of associated gas available consistently
so that the annual capacity factor of the MPP-AG is greater than 80%. For successful
commercialization following this method, proposed pathway will include:
 • Identifying Oil & Gas companies with wells that flare consistently
 • Secure long-term site and flare gas access for installation and operation of the MPP-AG
 system.
 • To ensure, consistent operation when flare gas volumes are intermittent, the option to
 convert associated gas captured and sold on site, i.e. behind the gas sales meter, should be
 included in negotiations.

In this business model, the carbon black produced is the primary revenue source so negotiations
with the O&G producers should focus on receiving the flared gas for free or at a significant
discount. Ownership and maintenance of the fleet of MPP-AG systems will likely be the
responsibility of the business entity.

The market size evaluation performed from 2022 to 2024 identified only 63 wells would fit this
business model assuming no access to gas behind the sales meter, implying this method though
effective at reduce flaring at specific wells, will not be effective at reducing flaring in the greater
Bakken region.

 42

 7.3.2. Total Flare Elimination-as-a-Service

This commercialization pathway targets a large deployment of the MPP-AG solution across a large
number of wells within a specific region, county or township. This approach recognizes the
majority of wells in the Bakken flare less than 7,600 mcf/month and would require a fee for no-
flare operation at the well site.

To reduce the cost of deploying per well, the MPP-AG system could be decoupled and for wells
with low and intermittent flare volumes (< 25 thousand cubic feet per day), only the GCU system
with enhanced storage capabilities is installed. A mobile MPU system would periodically be
deployed to the well to convert the stored gas into carbon black. For wells with low but consistent
flare volumes and not requiring gas conditioning, only the mobile MPU system would be deployed
to reduce investments per well and increase affordability.

In this business model, both the carbon black and a fee for eliminating flaring will be the revenue
source. However, a joint venture with the well operator and business entity might be required to
achieve a significant deployment of the units across the large number of wells to achieve
profitability at low utilization. This business model has the biggest potential to reduce flared gas
volumes in the Bakken.

8. Environmental Information Volume

 Emission Sources

Two primary emission sources exist for the process – emissions from the auxiliary unit and
emissions from the flare. For this project, it was assumed that the existing flare at the well site will
be used for all flaring activities. This implies all emission sources are covered by either the existing
well site permit for the flare or the exhaust emissions of the commercial generator. No additional
environmental permits are expected.

 Carbon Dioxide Emissions

CO2 reductions were estimated for a first-of-a-kind (FOAK) and an Nth-of-a-kind (NOAK) system.
The FOAK design adopts commercially available electric generators with a maximum H2 blend of
20 – 25%. In this design, the MPP-AG cannot operate on only the H2-rich gas and requires
associated gas from the well pad. The combustion of associated gas to power the system limits the
avoided CO2 emissions, see Table 8-1.. The NOAK design assumes availability of a genset
operating with the H2-rich gas from the MPU system, and achieves up to 68% avoided CO2 at the
well site. Several equipment manufacturers report future plans to produce an electric generator
which can be powered by over 50% H2. Potential reduction in CO2 emissions is anticipated to be
more than 36% for all design cases.
