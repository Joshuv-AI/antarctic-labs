Budgeted Act-or-Defer Multi-Agent LLM Deliberation with Local Reliability Bounds
Mengdie Flora Wang, Haochen Xie, Guanghui Wang, Devin Zhang, Jae Oh Woo

Abstract
Multi-agent deliberation among LLMs can improve reasoning, but deployment requires deciding when the current answer is reliable enough to act on and when it should be escalated to human review. We formulate this as budgeted act-or-defer decision making. At each round, the system maps the debate prefix to a low-dimensional state, computes a k k -nearest-neighbor lower confidence bound on state-conditional correctness using calibration data, and acts only when the bound exceeds a user-specified reliability threshold. The certificate controls wrong actions through the decomposition β = δ + α + ε act \beta=\delta+\alpha+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}} , separating calibration failure, residual action risk, and representation gap. The guarantee is conditional, not distribution-free: it relies on a valid local bias envelope and an action-region representation-gap bound, and each assumption is paired with falsification-style diagnostics. Because the same absolute wrong-action budget has different meanings across tasks of different difficulty, we set budgets relative to each task’s final-round error using training data only, and evaluate safety by normalized budget usage WA / β \mathrm{WA}/\beta . On six benchmarks against nine baselines, the method uses 9–12% of the pre-declared budget on activated datasets, reaching up to 84% automation and 96% acted-on accuracy; on stress-test datasets, it defers rather than forcing unreliable automation. Rather than relying on per-task post-hoc threshold search, the method prospectively converts a user-declared wrong-action budget into an auditable act-or-defer operating point before deployment, under explicitly stated assumptions.



1
Introduction

Multi-agent debate, where multiple LLM instances exchange responses over several rounds, has been shown to improve reasoning
(
21
;
51
)
. To deploy such systems in practice, one needs a stopping criterion with principled control over the probability of acting on a wrong answer. In human-in-the-loop deployments this stopping decision is also an escalation decision: the system should act autonomously only when the local reliability bound supports action, and otherwise defer the instance to human review. Existing approaches either operate at a fixed final round with only marginal coverage (e.g., split conformal prediction
(
78
;
4
)
) or rely on heuristic consensus thresholds without explicit wrong-action budgets. Neither provides per-instance, per-round reliability bounds that enable adaptive stopping.

We address this gap by constructing local lower confidence bounds on the correctness probability using
k
-nearest-neighbor (
k
-NN) neighborhoods in a debate-state metric space. The debate prefix at round
t
is mapped to a state
U_{t}
in a metric space
(\mathcal{U},d)
. The local correctness function
q_{t}(u)=\Pr(Y=\hat{Y}_{t}\mid U_{t}=u)
is bounded from below via a
local bias envelope

b_{t,k}(u)
that controls the
k
-NN approximation error. For each candidate neighborhood size
k
in a pre-specified family
\mathcal{K}
, we subtract the bias envelope and a Hoeffding concentration term from the
k
-NN empirical mean, then take the maximum over
\mathcal{K}
:

L_{t}=\max_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\left[\widehat{q}_{t,k}(U_{t})-b_{t,k}(U_{t})-\sqrt{\frac{\log(T|\mathcal{K}|/\delta)}{2k}}\right],

(1)

where the
\log|\mathcal{K}|
penalty absorbs the adaptivity in
k
. The bias envelope
b_{t,k}(u)
is a generic primitive (Lipschitz, Hölder, bounded-oscillation models are special cases). The first correction controls approximation bias, the second controls sampling variance, and the maximization over
\mathcal{K}
selects the tightest local scale per instance.

A central design choice is
budget alignment
. The user specifies a risk budget
\beta
and the method decomposes it as

\beta\;=\;\underbrace{\delta}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{statistical}\\
\text{failure}\end{subarray}}\;+\;\underbrace{\alpha}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{residual}\\
\text{action risk}\end{subarray}}\;+\;\underbrace{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{representation}\\
\text{gap}\end{subarray}}.

(2)

The residual
\alpha=\beta-\delta-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}
directly sets the stopping threshold
1-\alpha
. The stopping rule
\tau=\inf\{t:L_{t}\geq 1-\alpha\}
then satisfies
\Pr(\text{act and wrong})\leq\beta
conditional on local assumptions (Theorem
3
). The contribution is not the mathematical tools (Hoeffding + union bound, which are classical) but a risk-decomposition framework for adaptive deliberation stopping under auditable local assumptions.

2
Problem Setup

Consider a multiple-choice task with input space
\mathcal{X}
and label space
\mathcal{Y}=\{1,\ldots,C\}
. An ensemble of
N
LLM agents debates for at most
T
rounds. At round
t
, the debate has produced a filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
containing all agent responses and per-agent belief distributions
\{P^{(t)}_{j}(y\mid x)\}_{j=1}^{N}
up to round
t
. We aggregate these into a
social belief

P_{\mathrm{social}}^{(t)}(y\mid x)\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}P^{(t)}_{j}(y\mid x),

(3)

and take
\hat{Y}_{t}=\arg\max_{y}P_{\mathrm{social}}^{(t)}(y\mid x)
as the round-
t
prediction (Appendix
B
specifies how each
P^{(t)}_{j}
is elicited).

State embedding.

Each debate prefix is mapped to a state
U_{t}=\phi_{t}(\mathcal{F}_{t})
in a metric space
(\mathcal{U},d)
via a deterministic,
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable
\phi_{t}
. Typical components include the top social probability
p_{t}^{(1)}=\max_{y}P_{\mathrm{social}}^{(t)}(y\mid x)
, the margin
\Delta_{t}=p_{t}^{(1)}-p_{t}^{(2)}
, inter-agent disagreement, and top-answer stability; the metric
d
reflects correctness relevance (e.g., Euclidean on a normalized feature vector).

Geometry–sufficiency trade-off.

The choice of
\phi_{t}
jointly controls three quantities central to the framework:

(a)

smoothness of
q_{t}
, which governs the bias envelope
b_{t,k}
;

(b)

representation gap
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}
, the information lost in compressing
\mathcal{F}_{t}
to
U_{t}
;

(c)

intrinsic dimension
D
, which enters the certification rate
O(n^{-1/(2+D)})
.
Richer states reduce
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}
but raise
D
and may roughen
q_{t}
; minimal states keep
D
small and
q_{t}
smooth but risk larger
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}
. We investigate this trade-off empirically in Section
H.5
.

Local correctness and goal.

The target is the
local correctness function

q_{t}(u):=\Pr(Y=\hat{Y}_{t}\mid U_{t}=u),

(4)

a pointwise quantity conditioning on the exact state rather than averaging over a region. We seek a stopping rule
\tau=\inf\{t\leq T:L_{t}\geq 1-\alpha\}
with a data-driven lower bound
L_{t}
for
q_{t}(U_{t})
, so that
\Pr(\tau\leq T,\;\hat{Y}_{\tau}\neq Y)\leq\beta
. The budget decomposition (
2
) controls each term separately:
\delta
by Hoeffding with a
(t,k)
union bound,
\alpha
by the stopping threshold, and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}
by the action-region representation gap.

3
Method

The method turns
\beta
into a stopping rule in two steps: fix the action threshold
1-\alpha
with
\alpha=\beta-\delta-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}
from the budget decomposition (
2
), then build a lower bound
L_{t}
certifying when the threshold is met. The main guarantee (Theorem
3
) follows by summing worst cases per component.

Offline: calibration.

On
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we run the full
T
-round debate once per instance and record, for every
(t,i)
, the state
U_{i,t}=\phi_{t}(\mathcal{F}_{i,t})
and correctness indicator
Z_{i,t}=\mathbf{1}[Y_{i}=\hat{Y}_{i,t}]
. We split
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}
into
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mod}}
(20%, for bias-envelope estimation) and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{loc}}
(80%, the database searched at test time).

Online:
k
-NN lookup.

At test round
t
with state
U_{t}
, and for each
k
in a pre-specified family
\mathcal{K}=\{k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}\}
, let
\mathcal{N}_{t,k}(U_{t})\subset\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{loc}}
denote the
k
nearest calibration instances and
h_{t,k}(U_{t})=\max_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{t,k}(U_{t})}d(U_{i,t},U_{t})
the
k
-NN radius. The raw empirical local correctness is
\widehat{q}_{t,k}(U_{t})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{t,k}(U_{t})}Z_{i,t}
.

Adaptive lower bound.

The raw estimate
\widehat{q}_{t,k}
is optimistic on two counts: neighbors are
near

U_{t}
but not
at

U_{t}
(local bias), and with only
k
samples the empirical mean may exceed the truth (sampling variance). We subtract a correction for each and take the tightest bound across
k
:

\displaystyle L_{t,k}(U_{t})

\displaystyle\;=\;\widehat{q}_{t,k}(U_{t})\;-\;\underbrace{b_{t,k}(U_{t})}_{\text{bias}}\;-\;\underbrace{\sqrt{\tfrac{\log(T|\mathcal{K}|/\delta)}{2k}}}_{\text{variance}},

(5)

\displaystyle L_{t}

\displaystyle\;=\;\max_{k\in\mathcal{K}}L_{t,k}(U_{t}).

(6)

Each test instance automatically picks the
k
with the best bias–variance trade-off at its location; the
\log|\mathcal{K}|
term pays for adaptivity via a union bound. By construction
L_{t,k}\leq q_{t}(U_{t})
with high probability (Theorem
1
).

Stopping rule.

The system acts at
\tau=\inf\{t\leq T:L_{t}\geq 1-\alpha\}
and defers otherwise; pseudocode is in Algorithm
1
(Appendix
B
).

Bias envelope instantiations.

The bias correction
b_{t,k}(u)
is a plug-in interface: any conservative bound on how much
q_{t}
can vary within the
k
-NN neighborhood is valid.

•

Lipschitz
:
b_{t,k}(u)=L_{t}^{\star}\cdot h_{t,k}(u)
— global slope bound.

•

Hölder
:
b_{t,k}(u)=C_{t}\cdot h_{t,k}(u)^{s}
— sub-linear growth for smoother functions.

•

Empirical modulus
(default): fit a smoothed pilot
\tilde{q}_{t}
on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mod}}
(logistic GAM; Appendix
C
), then measure how much
\tilde{q}_{t}
varies within radius
r
:

b_{t,k}(u)=\hat{\omega}_{t}(h_{t,k}(u)),\quad\hat{\omega}_{t}(r)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mod}}\\
d(U_{i,t},U_{j,t})\leq r\end{subarray}}|\tilde{q}_{t}(U_{i,t})-\tilde{q}_{t}(U_{j,t})|.

(7)

The empirical modulus avoids the pathology of estimating a global Lipschitz constant from binary labels (where pairwise slopes diverge at short distances). Its estimation failure is absorbed into
\delta
(Corollary
8
), keeping the three-way budget intact. We stress-test validity by
\rho
-inflation (
b_{t,k}\to\rho\,b_{t,k}
,
\rho\in\{1,1.5,2\}
); Proposition
4
gives
\Pr(\text{wrong act})\leq\beta+\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{env}}^{c}(\rho))
, and Table
11
shows
2\times
inflation reduces Act by only 1–3 pp.

4
Assumptions

The guarantees in Section
5
rest on four assumptions. The first two are standard; the last two are specific to the local approach and constitute the price for pointwise guarantees. Each is paired with a diagnostic (Table
11
).

Assumption 1

(Across-instance exchangeability)
.

The calibration examples
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. (or exchangeable) and independent of the test example
(X,Y)
. Within each instance, debate rounds may be arbitrarily dependent.

Assumption 2

(State measurability)
.

For each round
t
, the prediction
\hat{Y}_{t}
and state
U_{t}=\phi_{t}(\mathcal{F}_{t})
are
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable.

Assumption 3

(Local bias envelope — plug-in interface)
.

For each round
t
and candidate
k\in\mathcal{K}
, there exists a measurable function
b_{t,k}:\mathcal{U}\to[0,\infty)
such that

\big|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{t,k}(u)}q_{t}(U_{i,t})-q_{t}(u)\big|\leq b_{t,k}(u)
a.s. for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
, where
\mathcal{N}_{t,k}(u)
is the
k
-NN of
u
among calibration states.

This is an
interface
, not a specific smoothness model: any user-supplied conservative envelope can be plugged into (
5
), and the theorems hold as long as the envelope is valid. The estimation error decomposes as sampling fluctuation (controlled by Hoeffding) plus local bias (controlled by
b_{t,k}
):

\widehat{q}_{t,k}(u)-q_{t}(u)=\underbrace{\widehat{q}_{t,k}(u)-\tfrac{1}{k}\textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{t,k}(u)}q_{t}(U_{i,t})}_{\text{sampling fluctuation}}+\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{k}\textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{t,k}(u)}q_{t}(U_{i,t})-q_{t}(u)}_{\text{local bias}}.

(8)

Lipschitz, Hölder, and bounded-oscillation models are all special cases (Section
3
). The guarantee is only as good as the envelope: if
b_{t,k}
underestimates the true bias, validity degrades by exactly
\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{env}}^{c})
(Proposition
4
).

Assumption 4

(Action-region representation gap (one-sided))
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}\geq 0
such that for every round
t
and every realized debate history
f_{t}
with
u=\phi_{t}(f_{t})
satisfying
q_{t}(u)\geq 1-\alpha
:

\big[q_{t}(u)-\Pr(Y=\hat{Y}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=f_{t})\big]^{+}\;\leq\;\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}\quad\text{a.s.}

(9)

where
[x]^{+}=\max(x,0)
.

This is weaker than a global two-sided gap: it is
one-sided
(only overestimation matters) and
restricted to the action region

\{(t,u):q_{t}(u)\geq 1-\alpha\}
. Lipschitz smoothness and local support are
not
required by the main theorems—they are optional instantiations of
b_{t,k}
, formalized in Appendix
A
.

5
Theoretical Guarantees

All proofs are in Appendix
A
. We build from pointwise validity to the main wrong-action guarantee. Define the bad event
E_{t,k}:=\{L_{t,k}(U_{t})>q_{t}(U_{t})\}
.

Theorem 1

(Pointwise validity)
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
3
, for any fixed
t
,
k\in\mathcal{K}
, and
\delta_{t,k}\in(0,1)
:

\Pr(E_{t,k})\leq\delta_{t,k}
.

Theorem 2

(Simultaneous validity)
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
3
, set
\delta_{t,k}=\delta/(T|\mathcal{K}|)
. Then
\Pr(\bigcup_{t,k}E_{t,k})\leq\delta
; equivalently,
L_{t}=\max_{k}L_{t,k}(U_{t})\leq q_{t}(U_{t})
for all
t
with probability
\geq 1-\delta
.

Theorem 3

(Budget-aligned wrong-action control)
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
4
, the stopping rule
\tau=\inf\{t\leq T:L_{t}(U_{t})\geq 1-\alpha\}
satisfies

\Pr\big(\tau\leq T,\;\hat{Y}_{\tau}\neq Y\big)\leq\delta+\alpha+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}.

(10)

In particular, if
\delta+\alpha+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}\leq\beta
, then
\Pr(\tau\leq T,\;\hat{Y}_{\tau}\neq Y)\leq\beta
.

Scope.
Theorem
3
does not provide distribution-free conditional correctness, nor does it verify the state representation automatically. It states that, given a valid local bias envelope and an action-region representation-gap bound, the stopping rule converts these quantities into a wrong-action guarantee. Assumption violations add explicitly to the risk (Proposition
4
below).

Each term controls a distinct error source (Figure
4
):
\delta
covers calibration failure,
\alpha
is the residual wrong-answer probability when
q_{t}\geq 1-\alpha
, and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}
captures representation loss. We make the cost of assumption failure explicit:

Proposition 4

(Cost of assumption failure)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{env}}
be the event that Assumption
3
holds in the action region, and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{rep}}
the event that Assumption
4
holds. Then:

\Pr(\tau\leq T,\;\hat{Y}_{\tau}\neq Y)\leq\delta+\alpha+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}+\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{env}}^{c})+\Pr(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{rep}}^{c})
.

The guarantee degrades gracefully: each assumption violation adds its failure probability to the bound.

Corollary 5

(Acted-on error rate)
.

Under the same conditions as Theorem
3
, if
p_{\mathrm{act}}:=\Pr(\tau\leq T)>0
:

\Pr\!\big(\hat{Y}_{\tau}\neq Y\mid\tau\leq T\big)\;\leq\;\alpha+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}+\frac{\delta}{p_{\mathrm{act}}}.

(11)

This connects the unconditional wrong-action bound (Theorem
3
) to Acc
|
Act
=1-\Pr(\hat{Y}_{\tau}\neq Y\mid\tau\leq T)
reported in experiments. When Act is large (e.g.,
p_{\mathrm{act}}=0.77
on BBH), the
\delta/p_{\mathrm{act}}
overhead is small (
0.03/0.77\approx 0.04
).

B
illustrates the budget decomposition and its empirical envelope.

Difficulty-normalized deployment budgets.

A fixed absolute wrong-action budget is not a task-invariant deployment requirement: it corresponds to very different levels of tolerance depending on the task’s final-round error. For ARC (
\widehat{e}_{T}\approx 5\%
),
\beta=0.10
is permissive relative to the final-round error; for MuSR (
\widehat{e}_{T}\approx 34\%
), the same
\beta
is stringent. Absolute
\beta
therefore conflates task difficulty with stopping quality. We use
\lambda
to
parameterize deployment tolerance
relative to the task’s final-round error—the fraction of
\widehat{e}_{T,d}
the user is willing to allocate as autonomous wrong-action budget—and select the operating multiplier
\lambda^{\star}_{d}
from training data only, inducing

\beta_{d}\;=\;\lambda^{\star}_{d}\;\widehat{e}_{T,d}^{\,\mathrm{train}},

(12)

where
\widehat{e}_{T,d}^{\,\mathrm{train}}=\Pr_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}}(\widehat{Y}_{T}\neq Y)
is the final-round error on the training split. The operating budget is selected before deployment using only training data: we sub-split the training set, sweep
\lambda\in\{0.25,0.50,\ldots,5.00\}
, and pick the
\lambda
that maximizes automation subject to
\widehat{\mathrm{WA}}/\beta\leq 0.10
on the held-out training sub-split (Appendix
B.5
). We fix
\delta=0.03
and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}=0.02
, giving action threshold
\alpha=\beta-0.05
. The formal guarantee (Theorem
3
) holds for
any
fixed
\beta
; training-only selection simply chooses the operating point before deployment without compromising test-time validity. No test-set quantity is used to choose
\lambda^{\star}_{d}
,
\beta_{d}
,
k
, or the stopping threshold; the held-out test split is used only for evaluation, averaged over 10 random splits. Because
\beta_{d}
is task-dependent by design, the primary cross-dataset safety metric is the normalized budget usage

7
Discussion and Limitations

The paper does not provide distribution-free conditional correctness; the contribution is to expose assumptions, budget their cost (Proposition
4
), and stress-test them with diagnostic proxies (Table
11
). The representation gap (Assumption
4
) is the unavoidable price of a deployable low-dimensional stopping state: any practical system commits to a finite summary of
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}
quantifies the one-sided cost of that commitment in the action region; our diagnostics do not prove this gap is small but are designed to falsify obviously insufficient representations and to guide conservative inflation of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}
(Appendix
I
). Accordingly
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}
enters the wrong-action bound additively, and the certificate degrades gracefully if Assumption
4
fails (Proposition
4
). The 2D default state
(p_{t}^{(1)},\Delta_{t})
empirically balances statistical efficiency and representation loss (Table
16
). Low activation on GPQA and MuSR is consistent with the statistical nature of local certification: small
n
and high base error enlarge both the
k
-nearest-neighbor approximation radius and the concentration slack, so the lower bound rarely crosses threshold under moderate budgets; the per-component decomposition is in Appendix
N
. We view this deferral as intended behavior rather than a failure.

9
Conclusion

We reframe multi-agent deliberation as budgeted act-or-defer for human-in-the-loop deployment: local
k
-NN lower bounds on state-conditional correctness trigger autonomous action when reliable and defer otherwise, with
\beta=\delta+\alpha+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}
. The guarantee is conditional on two auditable structural assumptions (bias envelope and representation gap), each paired with a diagnostic proxy rather than a formal verifier; assumption failures enter the wrong-action bound additively (Proposition
4
), making the cost of each violation explicit rather than hidden behind a global correctness claim. Under training-selected difficulty-normalized budgets, the framework uses 9–12% of the allowed budget on activated datasets (BBH 84%/96%, MMLU-Pro 71%/94%, ARC 75%/97%, LogiQA 28%/89%), while baselines at risk-calibrated defaults consume 18–82%; on stress tests (GPQA, MuSR) the method defers rather than forcing unreliable automation, the intended behavior under insufficient calibration evidence. The contribution is not better subset quality at a matched automation level—tuned scalar thresholds can recover the same operating point after per-task search—but the prospective conversion of a declared wrong-action budget into an auditable act-or-defer policy before deployment, under explicitly exposed assumptions; we view this as a template for other inference-time compute pipelines where repeated autonomous action is cheap but occasional wrong action is costly. The central open challenge is the geometry–sufficiency trade-off governing
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}
, motivating future work on learned representations and extensions to open-ended generation.

Proof of Theorem
1

Proof.

Fix a round
t
and a candidate
k\in\mathcal{K}
. Define the sigma-field
\Sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}=\sigma\!\big(\{U_{i,t}\}_{i=1}^{n},\,U_{t}\big)
, generated by all calibration states at round
t
and the test state. Because calibration and test instances are i.i.d. (Assumption
1
),
\Sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}
is independent of the correctness labels
\{Z_{i,t}\}_{i=1}^{n}

conditional on the states
—that is, each
Z_{i,t}
is conditionally independent given
U_{i,t}
with
\mathbb{E}[Z_{i,t}\mid U_{i,t}]=q_{t}(U_{i,t})
.

Step 1: neighborhood is
\Sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}
-measurable.
Conditional on
\Sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}
, the neighborhood index set
\mathcal{N}_{t,k}(U_{t})\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
and the
k
-NN radius
h_{t,k}(U_{t})
are deterministic, since they depend only on the distances
\{d(U_{i,t},U_{t})\}
.

Step 2: bias envelope controls the conditional mean.
By the bias envelope (Assumption
3
):

\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{t,k}(U_{t})}q_{t}(U_{i,t})\;\geq\;q_{t}(U_{t})-b_{t,k}(U_{t}).

(13)

Therefore, the conditional mean of the
k
-NN estimator satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{q}_{t,k}(U_{t})\mid\Sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}]=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{t,k}(U_{t})}q_{t}(U_{i,t})\geq q_{t}(U_{t})-b_{t,k}(U_{t})
.

Step 3: upper-tail Hoeffding conditional on
\Sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}
.
Since
Z_{i,t}\in[0,1]
are conditionally independent given
\Sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}
(each
Z_{i,t}
depends on its own instance, and cross-instance independence follows from Assumption
1
), the upper-tail Hoeffding inequality
[
37
]
gives:

\Pr\!\left(\widehat{q}_{t,k}(U_{t})>\mu_{t,k}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta_{t,k})}{2k}}\;\middle|\;\Sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}\right)\leq\delta_{t,k},

(14)

where
\mu_{t,k}:=\mathbb{E}[\widehat{q}_{t,k}(U_{t})\mid\Sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}]=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{t,k}(U_{t})}q_{t}(U_{i,t})
.

Step 4: combine and integrate.
Write
c_{k}=\sqrt{\log(1/\delta_{t,k})/(2k)}
. On the event
\{\widehat{q}_{t,k}\leq\mu_{t,k}+c_{k}\}
:

L_{t,k}(U_{t})=\widehat{q}_{t,k}(U_{t})-b_{t,k}(U_{t})-c_{k}\leq\mu_{t,k}+c_{k}-b_{t,k}(U_{t})-c_{k}=\mu_{t,k}-b_{t,k}(U_{t})\leq q_{t}(U_{t}),

(15)

where the last step uses
\mu_{t,k}\leq q_{t}(U_{t})+b_{t,k}(U_{t})
from the bias envelope (Assumption
3
). Hence
\Pr(E_{t,k}\mid\Sigma_{\mathrm{loc}})\leq\delta_{t,k}
almost surely. Integrating over
\Sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}
:

\Pr(E_{t,k})=\mathbb{E}\big[\Pr(E_{t,k}\mid\Sigma_{\mathrm{loc}})\big]\leq\delta_{t,k}.\qed

).

Theorem 6

(Lipschitz envelope).

Remark 2

(Bernstein refinement)
.

The Hoeffding term
c_{k}=\sqrt{\log(T|\mathcal{K}|/\delta)/(2k)}
can be tightened using the empirical Bernstein inequality
[
56
]
. Let
\widehat{\sigma}_{t,k}^{2}=\widehat{q}_{t,k}(1-\widehat{q}_{t,k})
be the plug-in variance estimate. The Bernstein bound replaces
c_{k}
with:

c_{k}^{\mathrm{Bern}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\widehat{\sigma}_{t,k}^{2}\log(2T|\mathcal{K}|/\delta)}{k}}+\frac{7\log(2T|\mathcal{K}|/\delta)}{3(k-1)}.

(46)

When the local correctness is near 0 or 1 (i.e.,
q_{t}(U_{t})
is close to the boundary),
\widehat{\sigma}_{t,k}^{2}
is small and
c_{k}^{\mathrm{Bern}}\ll c_{k}
. This does not change the formal theory—any valid concentration inequality can be substituted—but can improve practical automation in the high-confidence regime. We use Hoeffding as the default for simplicity and because the Bernstein gain is modest in our setting (most acted-on instances have
q_{t}\in[0.85,0.99]
where
\sigma^{2}
is not negligibly small).

Remark 3

(Sharpness of the union bound)
.

The Bonferroni correction
\delta_{t,k}=\delta/(T|\mathcal{K}|)
is worst-case tight: an adversary can construct a distribution where the bad events
\{E_{t,k}\}
are disjoint, making the union bound an equality. In practice, the
L_{t,k}
values are positively correlated across
k
(larger
k
neighborhoods contain smaller ones), so the effective number of independent tests is less than
T|\mathcal{K}|
. A Holm-type sequential correction could exploit this, but at the cost of requiring ordered
p
-values and sequential computation. For our setting with
T|\mathcal{K}|=4\times 3=12
, the
\log(12/\delta)
penalty is mild and the Bonferroni correction is practical.

Remark 4

(Relationship between Assumptions
3
and
4
)
.

The bias envelope (Assumption
3
) and the representation gap (Assumption
4
) control different error sources and are not redundant. To see this, consider two extreme state embeddings. If
\phi_{t}=\mathcal{F}_{t}
(the full debate transcript), then
q_{t}(\phi_{t}(\mathcal{F}_{t}))=\Pr(Y=\hat{Y}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})
and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}=0
, but
\mathcal{U}
is infinite-dimensional and the bias envelope
b_{t,k}
is uncontrollable (the
k
-NN radius does not shrink). Conversely, if
\phi_{t}\equiv c
(a constant embedding), then
D=0
and
b_{t,k}=0
trivially, but
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}
equals the full variation of
\Pr(Y=\hat{Y}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})
in the action region. Our 2D embedding
(p_{t}^{(1)},\Delta_{t})
balances these extremes (Table
16
).

B.1
Debate protocol and social probability

Debates use a structured prompt with
<reasoning>
and
<answer>
tags; at round
t>1
, agents see all round-
(t{-}1)
responses. Social probability uses uniform weights
w_{i}=1/N
over the
N
agents:
P_{\mathrm{social}}^{(t)}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}[\text{agent }i\text{ answers }y\text{ at round }t]
. The prediction is
\hat{Y}_{t}=\arg\max_{y}P_{\mathrm{social}}^{(t)}(y\mid x)
, with ties broken by random selection.

B.2
State normalization

The default state is
U_{t}=(p_{t}^{(1)},\Delta_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
p_{t}^{(1)}=\max_{y}P_{\mathrm{social}}^{(t)}(y)
and
\Delta_{t}=p_{t}^{(1)}-p_{t}^{(2)}
. Both components are normalized to
[0,1]
via calibration quantile transform: for each coordinate
j\in\{1,2\}
, we set
U_{t}^{(j,\mathrm{norm})}=\widehat{F}_{j}(U_{t}^{(j)})
, where
\widehat{F}_{j}
is the empirical CDF computed on the training split. Quantile normalization makes the Euclidean distance in the normalized space correspond to a rank-based distance, which is robust to the marginal distribution shape (e.g., heavy concentration of
p_{t}^{(1)}
near
1/C
or
1
). All
k
-NN distances and the empirical modulus are computed in this normalized space.

B.3
k
-NN search and bias envelope

k
-NN search is exact via brute-force (Euclidean distance in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
), which is fast at our scale (
n\leq 4{,}339
per split,
D=2
). We use the empirical modulus instantiation of the bias envelope:
b_{t,k}(u)=\hat{\omega}_{t}(h_{t,k}(u))
, with
\hat{\omega}_{t}
estimated on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mod}}
(
{\approx}\,830
instances for MMLU-Pro) via (
7
); see Appendix
C
for smoother and modulus computation details.

B.4
Budget allocation

Default budget split:
\delta=0.03
,
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}=0.02
, giving
\alpha=\beta-0.05
. When
\beta\leq\delta+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}+0.001=0.051
, the method cannot certify (negative
\alpha
) and defers on all instances. Results are averaged over 10 random 50/50 train/test splits; standard errors are below 0.5% for wrong-action rates.

B.5
Training-selected
\lambda^{\star}
procedure

The risk budget
\beta_{d}=\lambda^{\star}_{d}\cdot\widehat{e}_{T,d}^{\,\mathrm{train}}
(§
6
, eq. (
12
)) is selected as follows. Each 50/50 train/test split is further sub-split: the training half is divided 50/50 into
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}_{1}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}_{2}}
. For each candidate
\lambda\in\{0.25,0.50,\ldots,5.00\}
:

1.

Compute the final-round error on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}_{1}}
:
\hat{e}_{T}^{(1)}=1-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}_{1}}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}_{1}}}Z_{i,T}
.

2.

Set
\beta=\lambda\cdot\hat{e}_{T}^{(1)}
. If
\beta>0.99
, skip.

3.

Run our method with budget
\beta
on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}_{1}}
(calibration)
\to

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}_{2}}
(evaluation).

4.

Record automation rate
\widehat{\mathrm{Act}}(\lambda)
and wrong-action ratio
\widehat{\mathrm{WA}}(\lambda)/\beta
.

Select
\lambda^{\star}=\arg\max\{\widehat{\mathrm{Act}}(\lambda):\widehat{\mathrm{WA}}(\lambda)/\beta\leq 0.10,\;\widehat{\mathrm{Act}}(\lambda)>0\}
. If no
\lambda
satisfies the constraint, the method defers on all test instances. This is standard hyperparameter selection on held-out training data; the formal guarantee (Theorem
3
) holds for any fixed
\beta
, so selecting
\beta
on training data does not compromise test-time validity.

B.6
Computational complexity

Let
n
be the calibration size,

the number of debate rounds,
|\mathcal{K}|
the number of candidate

the state dimension.

Precomputation (one-time per split):
Running all

Appendix C
Smoother Details and Modulus Estimation

The empirical modulus
\hat{\omega}_{t}
in (
7
) depends on the choice of pilot smoother
\tilde{q}_{t}
. We describe the default (logistic GAM) and an alternative (isotonic regression), then compare them experimentally.

C.1
Default: logistic GAM

A logistic generalized additive model (GAM) models the correctness probability as

\log\frac{\tilde{q}_{t}(u)}{1-\tilde{q}_{t}(u)}=f_{1}(p_{t}^{(1)})+f_{2}(\Delta_{t}),

(47)

where
f_{1},f_{2}
are smooth spline functions estimated via penalized maximum likelihood on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mod}}
. The spline penalty controls overfitting, and the logistic link guarantees
\tilde{q}_{t}\in(0,1)
. We use cubic B-splines with 8 knots per feature and
\ell_{2}
regularization (
C=1.0
). The GAM naturally exploits the joint dependence on both state dimensions, producing a tighter modulus than univariate smoothers.

Given
\tilde{q}_{t}
, the empirical modulus is computed on the full 2D state space:

\hat{\omega}_{t}(r)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mod}}\\
d(U_{i,t},U_{j,t})\leq r\end{subarray}}|\tilde{q}_{t}(U_{i,t})-\tilde{q}_{t}(U_{j,t})|.

(48)

In practice, we tabulate
\hat{\omega}_{t}
on a grid of 200 radius values from
0
to the 99th percentile of pairwise distances, enforce monotonicity (the modulus must be non-decreasing), and use linear interpolation for intermediate radii.

C.2
Alternative: isotonic regression

Isotonic regression fits
\tilde{q}_{t}
as the best
L^{2}
monotone approximation to
Z_{t}
:

\tilde{q}_{t}=\arg\min_{f\text{ non-decreasing}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mod}}}(Z_{i,t}-f(p_{i,t}^{(1)}))^{2}.

(49)

This yields a monotone step function
\tilde{q}_{t}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
. It is non-parametric, hyperparameter-free, and natural when higher social probability indicates higher correctness. However, it is univariate (uses only
p_{t}^{(1)}
, ignoring
\Delta_{t}
) and cannot capture non-monotone relationships.

C.3

Table

compares the two smoothers on pooled MMLU-Pro. Both achieve valid coverage (WA
\leq\beta=0.15
). The logistic GAM produces a tighter modulus (0.122 vs. 0.285 at
r=0.05
) because its spline basis exploits the joint dependence on both state dimensions, leading to higher automation (29.8% vs. 21.8%). We use the logistic GAM as the default throughout the paper.

C.4
Formal analysis: when does
\hat{\omega}_{t}\geq\omega_{t}
?

The validity of the empirical modulus envelope rests on
\hat{\omega}_{t}(r)\geq\omega_{t}(r)
for all relevant radii
r
. We decompose the gap between the true and estimated modulus to identify sufficient conditions.

Let
\omega_{t}(r)=\sup_{d(u,v)\leq r}|q_{t}(u)-q_{t}(v)|
be the true modulus of
q_{t}
, and
\omega_{\tilde{q}}(r)=\sup_{d(u,v)\leq r}|\tilde{q}_{t}(u)-\tilde{q}_{t}(v)|
the modulus of the pilot smoother. The empirical modulus
\hat{\omega}_{t}(r)
approximates
\omega_{\tilde{q}}(r)
via a finite maximum over
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mod}}
points. The error decomposes as:

\omega_{t}(r)-\hat{\omega}_{t}(r)\leq\underbrace{[\omega_{t}(r)-\omega_{\tilde{q}}(r)]}_{\text{smoother error}}+\underbrace{[\omega_{\tilde{q}}(r)-\hat{\omega}_{t}(r)]}_{\text{discretization error}}.

(50)

Smoother error.
By the triangle inequality on the supremum:

|\omega_{t}(r)-\omega_{\tilde{q}}(r)|\leq 2\,\|q_{t}-\tilde{q}_{t}\|_{\infty},

(51)

where
\|q_{t}-\tilde{q}_{t}\|_{\infty}=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}|q_{t}(u)-\tilde{q}_{t}(u)|
is the uniform approximation error of the pilot smoother. If
\tilde{q}_{t}
is a consistent estimator of
q_{t}
(e.g., a logistic GAM with penalty selected by BIC), then
\|q_{t}-\tilde{q}_{t}\|_{\infty}\to 0
as
|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mod}}|\to\infty
.

Discretization error.
The modulus
\omega_{\tilde{q}}(r)
is a supremum over all pairs
(u,v)
with
d(u,v)\leq r
, while
\hat{\omega}_{t}(r)
maximizes only over pairs in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mod}}
. If
\tilde{q}_{t}
is
L_{\tilde{q}}
-Lipschitz (which holds for logistic GAMs with bounded coefficients), and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mod}}
has covering number
\mathcal{N}(\epsilon)
at scale
\epsilon
, then
\omega_{\tilde{q}}(r)-\hat{\omega}_{t}(r)\leq 2L_{\tilde{q}}\cdot\epsilon_{\mathrm{cover}}
, where
\epsilon_{\mathrm{cover}}
is the covering radius. For
|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mod}}|=m
points in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
,
\epsilon_{\mathrm{cover}}=O(m^{-1/D})
.

Sufficient condition for validity.
Combining,
\hat{\omega}_{t}(r)\geq\omega_{t}(r)
holds when:

2\,\|q_{t}-\tilde{q}_{t}\|_{\infty}+2L_{\tilde{q}}\cdot O(m^{-1/D})\leq 0,

(52)

which is not guaranteed in finite samples. In practice, two factors provide a safety margin: (i) penalized splines
under
-approximate variation, making
\omega_{\tilde{q}}(r)\leq\omega_{t}(r)
unlikely but
\tilde{q}_{t}
smoother than
q_{t}
; (ii) when
\tilde{q}_{t}
under-smooths (tracks noise),
\omega_{\tilde{q}}(r)>\omega_{t}(r)
,
inflating
the envelope conservatively. The stress test (Table
4
) validates this empirically: even
2\times
inflation of
\hat{\omega}_{t}
leaves WA unchanged, confirming that the estimated modulus is already above
\omega_{t}
.

Impact on validity.

The theoretical guarantee (Corollary
8
) holds for any smoother, provided
\hat{\omega}_{t}(r)\geq\omega_{t}(r)
with high probability. The choice of smoother affects the
tightness
of the bound, not its validity. An undersmoother (too flexible) will track noise in
Z
, inflating
\hat{\omega}_{t}
and making the bias envelope conservative. An oversmoother (too rigid) will underestimate the true variation of
q_{t}
, potentially violating the envelope. The logistic GAM strikes a good balance: the spline penalty prevents overfitting while allowing multivariate dependence, producing a tighter modulus than univariate isotonic regression.

C.5
Envelope stress test

A central concern is whether
\hat{\omega}_{t}\geq\omega_{t}
holds in practice—i.e., whether the empirical modulus overestimates the true modulus of continuity. We test robustness by inflating
\hat{\omega}_{t}
by multiplicative factors
\{1.0,1.25,1.5,2.0\}
and measuring the impact on Act and WA (Table
4
). If the baseline modulus were a tight upper bound on
\omega_{t}
, inflating it would shrink the bias correction
b_{t,k}(u)=\hat{\omega}_{t}(h_{t,k}(u))
, lowering
L_{t}
and reducing automation. The results show that even at
2\times
inflation, Act degrades only modestly (MMLU-Pro: 29.8%
\to
27.8%; BBH: 74.9%
\to
72.3%) while WA remains unchanged. This insensitivity confirms that the empirical modulus is already conservative (substantially above
\omega_{t}
), providing a safety margin against modulus misspecification. ARC remains at 0% Act at all factors because it does not certify at
\beta=0.15
.

Appendix G
Budget Sensitivity, Ablations, and Robustness

This section examines how our method behaves as the risk budget
\beta
varies, verifies that each design choice contributes meaningfully, and confirms robustness to fixed-budget settings. We organize the analysis into four parts: (§
G.1
) the risk–automation frontier under error-normalized budgets, (§
G.2
) empirical budget alignment showing WA
\leq\beta
holds uniformly, (§
G.3
) ablations isolating the contributions of adaptive
k
and calibration size, and (§

) a fixed-budget robustness check applying the same
\beta
across all datasets.

G.1
Risk–automation frontier

Table
1
reports results under training-selected
\lambda^{\star}
, which determines a single
\beta
per dataset. Here we trace the full frontier by sweeping
\rho=\beta/e_{T}
(the error-normalized multiplier) across

Appendix I
Representation-gap stress tests

Assumption
4
is not statistically verified by these diagnostics; rather, the diagnostics are designed to falsify obviously insufficient state representations in the action region and to guide conservative inflation of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}
. We report three falsification proxies.

State-residual proxy.

Let
\widehat{q}^{\mathrm{state}}
be a correctness predictor trained on the default 2D state
U_{t}=(p_{t}^{(1)},\Delta_{t})
and
\widehat{q}^{\mathrm{rich}}
a richer predictor trained on the 4D state
(p_{t}^{(1)},\Delta_{t},p_{t}^{(2)},H_{t})
. For each dataset, we estimate the one-sided residual gap

\widehat{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}}^{\,\mathrm{proxy}}\;=\;\sup_{(t,u)\,:\,L_{t}\geq 1-\alpha}\,[\widehat{q}^{\mathrm{rich}}(\mathcal{F}_{t})-\widehat{q}^{\mathrm{state}}(U_{t})]^{+}

over the action region. Because the richer predictor is itself a compressed summary of
\mathcal{F}_{t}
—not
\Pr(Y\!=\!\hat{Y}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})
—the proxy is a falsification check, not a verifier. Table
13
in Appendix
H.3
reports the 90th/95th/maximum percentiles on pooled MMLU-Pro: mean gap
\approx 0.000
, max 0.021 in the acted-on region, which does not robustly exceed the operating
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}=0.02
.

Action-region subgroup check.

We partition acted-on instances by benchmark subtask (e.g., BBH’s 10 reasoning tasks, MuSR’s 3 subtasks) and by round at which stopping occurred, and verify that acted-on accuracy and
\mathrm{WA}/\beta
remain close to the pooled values. For BBH, per-subtask acted-on accuracy ranges 94.6–99.2% at pooled 96.4%; for MMLU-Pro, per-domain acted-on accuracy ranges 91.5–96.8% at pooled 93.9%. No subtask exhibits acted-on accuracy below
1-\alpha-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{act}}
, providing no falsification of the action-region assumption on activated datasets.

k
-NN heterogeneity check.

For each acted-on test instance we compute the label entropy within its
k^{\star}
-NN calibration neighborhood,
H_{k}(U_{t})=-\widehat{q}_{t,k}\log\widehat{q}_{t,k}-(1-\widehat{q}_{t,k})\log(1-\widehat{q}_{t,k})
. On activated datasets, median
H_{k}
is 0.14–0.27 nats (near the 0 of a pure neighborhood), confirming that the
k
-NN neighborhoods used for certification are locally near-homogeneous. On MuSR, median
H_{k}
exceeds 0.55 nats in the deferred region, consistent with the certificate’s refusal to act.

Interpretation.

These three proxies do not replace Assumption
4
. Their role is to surface states and action regions where the representation is implausibly insufficient. Across activated benchmarks (MMLU-Pro, BBH, LogiQA, ARC), none of the three falsification checks fires; on MuSR and GPQA the checks fire in the deferred region, consistent with the intended deferral behavior.
