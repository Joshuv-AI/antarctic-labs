 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Powder metallurgy (PM)–hot isostatic pressing (PM-HIP) has long been recognized as a powerful route
for producing fully dense, near net shape metallic components. By consolidating powders under high
temperature and pressure, HIP provides isotropic properties, uniform microstructures, and scalability to
complex geometries that are vital for sectors such as aerospace, energy, and nuclear power. Yet despite
these advantages, the technology has remained constrained by costly trial and error canister fabrication,
limitations of conventional forging, and incomplete knowledge about how the canister design influences
final part properties. Additive manufacturing (AM), by contrast, thrives on design freedom and geometric
flexibility but struggles with speed, scalability, and cost when applied to very large structures. The
research presented in this report investigated how a convergent manufacturing approach, combining AM
with PM-HIP, can merge the strengths of both technologies, leveraging AM’s flexibility for canister
design and HIP’s consolidation capability to deliver reliable, large, and complex parts.

The work progressed through three case studies that built on one another in scale and complexity. Small
cylindrical canisters fabricated by conventional methods, laser powder bed fusion, and directed energy
deposition were filled with stainless steel powders and subjected to HIP. The resulting parts demonstrated
near-full density and mechanical properties on par with wrought stainless steel, showing for the first time
that AM canisters can be a direct substitute for conventional ones without sacrificing quality. The next
step involved a medium-scale, noncentrosymmetric T-valve, which is an enclosed, multibranch geometry
that tested the limits of AM + PM-HIP integration. The T-valve achieved predictable shrinkage and
uniform densification, confirming feasibility for enclosed designs. However, this study also revealed
oxide inclusions and interfacial challenges at the AM + HIP boundary, underscoring the critical
importance of controlling interface chemistry and employing robust, in situ strategies, such as melt pool
monitoring and thermal monitoring, coupled with nondestructive evaluation techniques such as x-ray
computed tomography.

Finally, the effort culminated in fabricating a large-scale impeller weighing nearly 2000 lb and spanning
5 ft in diameter. Produced via multirobot wire arc AM and hot isostatic pressed to near-full density, the
impeller validated industrial-scale feasibility. Predictive models closely matched experimental shrinkage,
tensile properties were spatially uniform across the component, and the AM + PM-HIP interface proved
mechanically sound despite the presence of oxide-decorated prior particle boundaries. This large-scale
demonstration is a major milestone, showing that hybrid AM + PM‑HIP can reliably deliver components
at reactor-relevant scales.

Collectively, these studies charted a logical pathway: small-scale work built scientific confidence,
medium-scale work highlighted opportunities and challenges, and large-scale work proved
industrial impact.

The overarching conclusion of this report is that AM + PM-HIP should not be seen as a replacement for
forging but as a complementary pathway that provides the US with flexibility, resilience, and new options
for manufacturing nuclear-grade components. Looking ahead, several directions emerge as critical to
sustaining progress. Predictive modeling must become faster, more accessible, and more accurate, with
digital twins and machine learning reducing reliance on trial and error. Powders and alloys must be
optimized for HIP, with improved cleanliness, reduced oxides, and tailored chemistries that enhance
creep, fatigue, and irradiation resistance. Interfaces between AM and HIP regions must be better
engineered through coatings, machining strategies, and surface treatments to mitigate oxide formation and
ensure reliable bonding to explore opportunities for HIP of targeted compositional parts, as well as
multimaterial HIP cladding applications. Monitoring and nondestructive evaluation need to expand,
incorporating multimodal sensors, x-ray computed tomography, and real-time data integration through
platforms such as Pelican. At the same time, the pathway to industrial adoption requires techno-economic

 vii

analysis, machinability studies, and qualification frameworks aligned with industry and regulatory
standards. Finally, workforce and academic engagement must be strengthened. Programs that train
technicians and engineers for US Navy and US Department of Energy manufacturing challenges should
be paired with academic partnerships to support fundamental research, with open sharing of non-export-
controlled data to accelerate innovation and build the next generation of experts.

In conclusion, this report demonstrates that hybrid AM + PM-HIP is scientifically viable and strategically
important. By combining the design agility of AM with the consolidation strength of HIP and embedding
modeling, monitoring, and workforce development, this approach provided a transformative new
capability for US manufacturing. The path forward is clear: hybrid AM + PM-HIP is not just a promising
research direction but is also potentially an industrially relevant pathway that can reshape how nuclear-
grade components are designed, qualified, and deployed.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Hot isostatic pressing (HIP) for powder metallurgy (PM) applications involves consolidation of metal
powder at high temperature (80% of solidus temperature) and isostatic pressure (100–200 MPa) to
manufacture near net shape (NNS) components [1]. In the recent past, this technique was also adopted to
eliminate closed porosity in bulk components fabricated by additive manufacturing (AM) [2-5]. PM-HIP
is a mature technology that has the ability to process a multitude of alloy systems into complex NNS parts
for automotive, tooling, energy, and aerospace applications [6-8]. The nuclear industry has also been
exploring PM-HIP as an advanced manufacturing technology with the potential to supersede forging,
which is the current modality of manufacturing used to fabricate nuclear reactor components [7, 9-12]. In
spite of PM-HIP having the ability to fabricate large-scale components with uniform grain structure,
isotropic mechanical properties, and limited internal defects, it still suffers from multiple challenges that
are not often discussed or fully understood [7].

The quality and success of post-HIP components depends on various parameters, among which geometric
design of the pre-HIP canisters plays a crucial role in achieving a defect-free part with desired
dimensional accuracy and mechanical properties [13]. The PM-HIP process generally includes a thin-
walled pre-HIP canister filled with metal powder, which is then degassed using a vacuum pump and
sealed before subjecting it to HIP temperatures and pressures to achieve dense NNS components. This
process is known to be extremely sensitive to the canister geometry, and the design of this canister
currently involves several trial and error iterations that contribute to the cost of manufacture components
via PM-HIP [13]. Techno-economic analysis conducted by Mariarz et al. indicated container
manufacturing and consolidation steps to be significant cost contributors to PM-HIP technology [14].

Interestingly, in spite of the high costs for canister fabrication, PM-HIP still turns out to be more
economical, especially for complex-shaped component fabrication in low volumes, because of lower
tooling costs when compared with forging [14]. However, ironically, the HIP industry still relies on
forging for fabricating pre-HIP canisters. This results in the foundry capabilities of the country/region
limiting the maximum size of an HIP canister that can be fabricated. Furthermore, the inability of forging
technology to manufacture intricately shaped, complex HIP canisters would prove to be a bottleneck in
achieving NNS parts via HIP. Currently, most HIP canisters are typically made by sheet metal forming.
This technology can be used for ductile materials with thin sheets of constant thickness and limited
forming radius and bend angles. It is reasonably versatile but has certain material constraints, geometric
limitations, and process limitations. It is labor-intensive and requires specialized equipment such as dies
and rollers that need to be custom built for each profile, which is expensive and time-consuming,
especially for custom-designed complex geometries. On the other hand, AM as a business model thrives
on complexly designed parts for custom applications. Although demonstrations have shown large-scale
components being fabricated to NNS with AM technologies alone, notably, as much as AM is
customization-friendly, it is also expensive. Alternatively, not only are fabrication rates (kilograms per
hour) for PM much higher than those for AM, but PM-HIP is also considered to be more economical than
AM technologies [14]. Especially with the advent of large-scale AM techniques, this offers a perfect
opportunity for the use of AM in HIP canister manufacturing. Therefore, a combination of AM + PM-HIP
technology becomes more viable for bigger, custom-designed parts with lower production volume and
high complexity, as shown in Figure 1.

Incorporating AM modalities to fabricate HIP canisters is proposed as a potential solution to overcome
these limitations imposed by forging. This would, however, require a thorough understanding of how
different canister fabrication techniques influence post-HIP part properties on the macro- and microscales.
Although a few studies in the past were directed toward understanding powder compaction behavior and
densification mechanism and developing computational models to predict the post-HIP component’s
densities and dimensions [13, 15-17], a large segment of trial and error experimental results and
computational models are safeguarded by HIP industries as trade secrets. Moreover, information available
in open literature focuses mainly on density and dimensional shrinkages of post-HIP components but fails
to discuss microstructural differences and ensuing mechanical property variations. Figure 2 provides a
visual summary of the progression of HIP canisters fabricated at the Oak Ridge National Laboratory
(ORNL) Manufacturing Demonstration Facility (MDF), ranging from small, simple cylinders to larger,
more geometrically complex designs. This emphasizes scalability in component size and complexity and
highlighs the flexibility in pairing different material systems and processing modalities for the canister
and the powder core. Figure 2 also conveys the overarching theme of this report by delineating how the
convergent AM + PM-HIP strategy is not limited to laboratory-scale parts but is instead a versatile
pathway for fabricating increasingly large and complex nuclear-relevant components.

The results from this research highlight the potential to explore a variety of AM modalities for designing
and fabricating HIP canisters. This design freedom, customization capabilities, and scalability associated
with different AM techniques could be leveraged to push the boundaries of canister fabrication for
PM‑HIP technologies. Additionally, computational models for the PM-HIP process are explored in this
report to aid in rapid canister design and avoid traditional trial and error approaches.

 2. CASE STUDY 1: SMALL-SCALE, GEOMETRICALLY SIMPLE PARTS

The first case study in this report investigated the role of canister fabrication routes such as conventional
forming, laser powder bed fusion (LPBF), and blown powder directed energy deposition (BP-DED). The
effect of different canister fabrication techniques on the densification, microstructure, and mechanical
properties of PM-HIP 316L stainless steel (SS) powders was studied. Despite clear microstructural and
thermal conductivity differences between canister types, all post-HIP samples exhibited near-full
densification (>99.5%) and mechanical properties on par with wrought 316L SS. These results reinstate
that canister fabrication techniques (conventional or AM) had little effect on the final HIP material
quality, particularly for thin-walled canisters. For the PM-HIP community, these results underscore that
AM canisters can be a viable replacement for conventional cans without compromising microstructure or
strength and can provide cost and geometric flexibility. The experimental matrix included powder
characterization, thermal property measurements, electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), and tensile
testing across all canister types, designed to isolate the effects of canister manufacturing on HIP
consolidation. Overall, this case study established a critical baseline showing that AM-fabricated canisters
are suitable for HIP, setting the stage for exploring more complex geometries in subsequent case studies.

Thermal properties of the as-received conventional canisters and as-printed LPBF and directed energy
deposition (DED) canisters were characterized. Thermal diffusivity measurements were made using a
laser flash technique in accordance with the ASTM E1461 standard. A light pulse from a xenon flash
lamp went through apertures and uniformly heated up one surface of a disk-shaped sample, and the
infrared (IR) detector collected temperature data from the other side. Five shots were taken on each
sample at each set temperature in 50°C intervals between room temperature and 1,000°C to measure the
average thermal diffusivity of the bulk specimens. Thermal conductivity values were calculated based on
thermal diffusivity measurements using Eq. (1):

                                          𝑘 = α × ρ × 𝐶p,#(1)

 4

where k is thermal conductivity (W/[m·K]), α is thermal diffusivity (m2/s), ρ is material density (kg/m3),
and Cp is specific heat capacity (J/[kg·K]). The specific heat capacity of 316L SS at different
temperatures was obtained from published literature based on the studies conducted by Kim et al. [18].

Furthermore, each canister was filled with 316L SS powder through a fill-stem while being vibrated to
attain high packing density. Subsequently, the capsule was subjected to degassing under vacuum for 12 h
at 300°C, followed by gas-tight crimping and welding of the fill-stem. The six powder-filled, degassed,
and hermetically sealed canisters were then subjected to HIP. They were placed in a hot isostatic press
(American Isostatic Presses Inc., Columbus, Ohio, USA) and heated to 1,160°C at a rate of 10°C/min.
While heating, the hydrostatic pressure of the argon was raised at a rate of 0.7 MPa/min up to 103 MPa.
Once the maximum temperature (1,160°C) and isostatic pressure (103 MPa) were reached, the HIP cans
were held at these conditions for 4 h. Figure 3a shows the temperature and pressure recorded during a
typical HIP cycle. After HIP, each canister was analyzed for shrinkages using a blue light scanner. The
scanned 3D surface mesh of each canister was converted to a point cloud and compared using
CloudCompare software. Furthermore, metallography specimens were extracted from each of the HIP
cans at a radius of 11.8 mm from the canister center. Six cylindrical specimens (10 mm diameter and 15
mm height) were extracted along the height of the canister (see Figure 3b). The bulk density of all six
specimens was calculated by measuring the weight and volume of these samples before sectioning,
mounting, and polishing the sample at the center for metallography. Notably, the locations of the
specimens extracted for metallography, microstructure analysis, and comparison were the same for all
cans.

Specimens extracted from each canister were subjected to sectioning, grinding, and polishing in
accordance with standard metallographic procedures. Final polishing was performed on a vibratory
polisher using colloidal silica solution for 6 h. Secondary electron (SE) imaging, energy-dispersive x-ray
spectroscopy (EDS), and EBSD data were acquired on a field emission scanning electron microscope
(ZEISS Gemini450 FESEM) equipped with a Bruker EBSD detector. Data were acquired at 25 kV with a
0.5 µm step size. The hardness of the 316L SS compacts was measured by using a micro-Vickers
hardness tester (LECO AMH 55 Automatic Hardness Tester, LM110 series, St. Joseph, Michigan, USA)
with a 500 gram-force load and 15 s holding time. Tensile properties of post-HIP samples were performed
at a strain rate of 2.5 × 10−4 s−1 with an Instru-Met Instron electromechanical testing system (Union, New
Jersey, USA).

2.2 NONDESTRUCTIVE EVALUATION: BLUE LIGHT SCANS

To explore differences between the AM and conventional canisters at the macroscale, the linear and
volumetric shrinkages of canisters were compared post-HIP. Figure 4 shows the cross-sectional
comparison of point cloud data extracted from blue light scans of HIP canisters made via different
fabrication techniques. No significant differences in linear shrinkages were observed between the
canisters. Furthermore, volumetric shrinkage in each canister was calculated by subtracting the volume of
post-HIP cans from their initial volume based on the dimensions of the can. Figure 4 shows the outlines
of conventional, DED, and LPBF cans in pre-HIP (black) and post-HIP (red) states, along with a
quantitative measure of the volume of the canister pre- and post-HIP. The canisters (conventional, DED,
and LPBF) had an average initial diameter of 76 mm with a standard deviation of 0.2 mm. These
variations in canister dimensions were attributed to the dimensional tolerances associated with LPBF and
DED techniques. Furthermore, the change in volume between the pre- and post-HIP conditions was

2.3 PREDICTIVE MODELING

PM-HIP is frequently used for producing smaller NNS parts with high precision and quality, but its
application to bigger parts is limited because of large, unpredictable post-HIP shrinkages and shape
distortions. The shrinkages and distortions can be caused by several factors, such as canister stiffness,
temperature gradients, and powder characteristics [19-21]. Among these factors, the canister stiffness and
temperature gradients are directly affected by the canister design [22]. Therefore, it is crucial to
understand the deformation mechanisms in the PM-HIP process for a suitable canister design that can
limit shrinkage and shape distortion.

 6

A mechanics-based model can be a viable alternative to experimental trial and error approaches to
understand deformation mechanisms because it can significantly reduce developmental costs and lead
times [20, 23]. Most of the computational models used for PM-HIP simulations can be categorized into
plastic, viscoplastic, and combined (plastic + viscoplastic) models. The plastic models for PM-HIP are
based on the classical J2 plasticity theory with modified yield function and hardening law to account for
density and temperature dependence during the densification process [20, 24]. Similarly, the viscoplastic
models for PM-HIP use a modified form of the Norton power law that accounts for the variations in the
density and temperature during the densification process [19, 22]. A combined model has both plastic and
viscoplastic models that are either active in parallel or serially [25, 26]. Among these, the plastic models
are preferred because of their simpler calibration approach that requires less experimental data. However,
plastic models sometimes produce incorrect results when subject to slight variations in HIP conditions
during densification, such as reduced applied pressure caused by canister stiffness or a variation in
powder density at the beginning of densification. Therefore, a comparison of plastic and viscoplastic
models was performed to demonstrate their predictive capabilities [27, 28]. The models used for
simulations were first calibrated with the compression testing data using a single element model [28].

Four conventional cylindrical cans with different sizes and wall thicknesses were considered in this
problem. The goal was to evaluate the effect of a can’s size and wall thickness on the model predictions.
The cans and their finite element mesh used in simulations are shown in Figure 5a and Figure 5b. The
canister and powder material were 316L SS, and the HIP cycle was 7 h, with a 4 h hold after 2 h of ramp-
up. The peak pressure and temperature used were 106 MPa and 1,060°C, respectively. The obtained
relative density predictions for the cans using plastic and viscoplastic models are shown in Figure 5c and
Figure 5d for the four different cases named small thick canister (SMTHK), small thin canister
(SMTHN), large thick canister (LGTHK), and large thin canister (LGTHN). Some underdensification was
observed in plastic model cases, but all the cases in the viscoplastic model attained full relative density.
Except for the SMTHK, the underdensification in the plastic model was mostly confined to the fill stems,
with the rest of the canister having homogenous relative density.

powder pressure (SMTHK, Figure 6d). Therefore, stress shielding tended to affect the transient
densification behavior in a viscoplastic model rather than the final relative density as in the plastic model.

2.4 MICROSTRUCTURE CHARACTERIZATION

2.4.1 Powder Characterization

Powder size and morphology play a crucial role in determining the packing density, which influences
uniform densification and homogenous microstructure development during HIP [29]. Figure 8a shows an
SE image of as-received 316L SS powders. As-received powders had a clear spherical morphology—
characteristic of gas atomized powders—in addition to the presence of finer powder particles, which are
commonly referred to as satellites (marked with red arrows). Satellites are typically a cause of concern
because they lead to agglomeration of the powder and are also known to affect powder uniformity and
flowability [30]. However, in technologies such as HIP, powder packing plays a significant role in
determining distortions and final part density. Satellites in powders increase interlocking and cause
internal friction between powder particles, leading to inhomogeneous powder distributions [31].

Figure 8b depicts the particle size distribution of as-received powders. Here, number fraction is the
proportion of particles within a specific size range, where each particle is counted as an individual particle
and has equal weight in the calculation, whereas volume fraction is the proportion of the total volume
occupied by particles within that same size range [32]. The number fraction data indicated that most of

 10

the powder particles had a size distribution of 15–45 µm. Volume fraction analysis revealed that 90% of
the particles were less than 53 µm, and 50% of the particles had a diameter of less than 44 µm.

 study.

2.4.2 Canister Characterization

The microstructures of canisters fabricated via various manufacturing methods were compared. Figure 9a
shows an inverse pole figure (IPF) map of a conventional canister in its as-received state, and Figure 9b
and Figure 9c show IPF maps of DED and LPBF canisters, respectively, in their as-printed conditions.
The figure evidently shows that canisters fabricated via DED had the largest grain size distributions
among all three canisters, whereas LPBF and conventional cans had finer grain size distribution, with the
conventional cans showing equiaxed microstructure. Moreover, it should be noted that the presence of
twins, characteristic to annealing and recrystallization in austenitic SSs, was only observed in the
conventional cans and was not evident in the microstructures of DED and LPBF cans. Such
microstructural transition from columnar to equiaxed grains in LPBF and DED processing of Inconel 718
was explained in the past by Dehoff et al. as a function of thermal gradient and solidification velocity
[33]. Although it is known that the cooling rates LPBF (on the order of 105–107 K/s) are significantly
higher those observed in DED (102–104 K/s) [34, 35], the difference in grain size between the
microstructure of DED and LPBF canisters also arises from differences in the size of the powder
feedstock used in each of these processes. The LPBF process used a finer powder size distribution of 15–
45 µm, and the BP-DED technique used 316L SS powders in the size range of 45–106 µm.

To understand how the microstructural differences between canisters fabricated using conventional and
AM techniques affect the thermal properties, thermal conductivity values were calculated for each
canister across a range of temperatures. Figure 10 shows the thermal conductivity as a function of
temperature for DED, LPBF, and conventional canisters. This figure shows that the thermal conductivity
of DED canisters was highest, followed by that of LPBF canisters, with the conventional canisters
exhibiting the lowest thermal conductivity. The differences in thermal conductivity were found to be
more apparent at higher temperatures (beyond 100°C). The shape of the thermal conductivity curves with
an increasing trend is characteristic of alloys in which the electronic contribution to thermal conductivity
dominates. Additionally, differences in microstructure and grain are known to affect thermal conductivity
of materials, where smaller grains generally decrease thermal conductivity due to phonon scattering at the
grain boundaries [36, 37].

2.5 MECHANICAL TESTING

Room-temperature tensile testing was performed on samples extracted from consolidated powders hot
isostatic pressed in conventional, LPBF, and DED canisters. The stress–strain curves of PM-HIP samples
are shown in Figure 14. The effect of sample orientation on tensile properties was ignored because of the
equiaxed grain structure in the microstructure, as seen in Figure 12. The 0.2% yield strength (YS) and
ultimate tensile strength (UTS) were evaluated for samples from each of the canisters (conventional,
LPBF, and DED). The mean YSs of post-HIP specimens from conventional, DED, and LPBF canisters
were calculated to be 294, 298, and 301 MPa, respectively. The maximum percentage total elongation
was evaluated as 60%, 59%, and 60% for samples from conventional, DED, and LPBF canisters,
respectively. Thus, the post-HIP YS, UTS, and percent elongation were nearly the same for all specimens,
regardless of which canister the 316L SS powders were hot isostatic pressed in. Moreover, the mechanical
properties were compared with test results from the previous literature to establish consistency in data.
Prior work by Byun et al. [39] reported mechanical properties of wrought 316L SS with a YS of 261.1 ±
14.3 MPa, UTS of 562.2 ± 32.5 MPa, and total elongation of 72.8% ± 9%. Furthermore, Cooper et al.
[40] observed an average YS, UTS, and total elongation of 279 MPa, 611 MPa, and 74%, respectively, in
forged 316L SS, whereas in HIP 316L SS, YS, UTS, and total elongation were in the range of 249–334
MPa, 561–658 MPa, and 61%–73%, respectively. This wide variation in tensile properties of HIP 316L
SS in the work of Cooper et al. was attributed to differences in chemical composition of the starting
powder feedstock—more specifically, the oxygen and nitrogen content of the powders.

To identify fracture mode and determine the presence of any detrimental second phase particles, tensile
tests were followed by characterizing the fracture surfaces of spent tensile samples. The fracture surfaces
of samples from all the canisters were similar, as shown in Figure 15. Post-HIP specimens from
conventional, DED, and LPBF canisters showed a distinct cup-cone fracture surface with dimples of
varying sizes. The larger dimples are thought to be sites where the smaller pores coalesced during plastic
deformation before the initiation of fracture at this interface. A closer look at the dimples in the fracture
surface revealed embedded particles that were hypothesized to be unsintered powder.

2.6 SIMPLE CANS: CONCLUDING REMARKS

This case study confirmed that simple cylindrical canisters fabricated via conventional forming, LPBF, or
DED canister all successfully consolidate 316L SS powder to near-full density, producing equiaxed
microstructures and mechanical properties comparable with wrought benchmarks. Importantly, the study
showed that the choice of canister fabrication method has minimal effect on the final HIP material
properties when powders are hot isostatic pressed in thin-walled canisters. This work demonstrated that
AM canisters are a viable replacement for conventional cans of thin cross sections and offer greater
flexibility and design freedom. Additionally, accurate predictions from PM-HIP process models
demonstrated their potential use for canister design. Looking ahead, future work should explore the
interactions between powder compaction behavior, canister wall thickness, and stress shielding effects
across varied HIP cycles, including intermittent HIP. Extending this understanding to more demanding
alloys such as nickel-based superalloys and titanium alloys will broaden applicability to aerospace and
energy sectors. Finally, translating insights from simple cylindrical geometries to more complex canister
shapes will provide a critical bridge toward scaling hybrid AM + PM-HIP for larger, mission-relevant
components. Building on these results, the next case study explored a medium-scale, geometrically
complex, noncentrosymmetric component that has application in nuclear reactor pressure vessels: the
T‑valve.

  3. CASE STUDY 2: MEDIUM-SCALE, NONCENTROSYMMETRIC, GEOMETRICALLY
 COMPLEX PART—T-VALVE

The second case study in this report demonstrated the fabrication, HIP consolidation, and characterization
of a medium-scale, geometrically complex, noncentrosymmetric structure using a convergent approach in
which the AM canister and PM-HIP core were 316L SS. This component was a subsized (50% scaled
down) version of a T-valve that has application in small modular reactor pressure vessels. Key findings of
this case study included successful fabrication of a fully enclosed, leak-tight canister with predictable
isotropic shrinkage validated by blue-light scans and modeling. Although PM-HIP of the powders
achieved high-density equiaxed microstructures in the core, the AM + PM-HIP interface was found to
contain oxide inclusions that degraded tensile ductility, highlighting a challenge for long-term reliability
without proper measures to improve interface characteristics. This study showed the importance of
interface chemistry control, validated in situ monitoring, and post-HIP nondestructive testing as viable
quality assurance approaches for complex AM + PM-HIP components. The experimental matrix,
including predictive modeling, density mapping, EBSD/EDS interface analysis, and mechanical testing of
AM, PM-HIP, and interface regions, was designed to evaluate structural integrity and interface
performance. This case study connected simple geometries to more complex, enclosed components,
providing lessons on the strengths and challenges associated with a convergent AM + PM-HIP approach
for real-world structural parts.

3.1 CANISTER FABRICATION

The T-valve canister was fabricated at the ORNL MDF using a Mazak VTC-800G SR hybrid AM system
(Takeda, Japan), which integrates laser hot wire deposition with multiaxis machining. The canister was
built from 316L SS wire in a fully enclosed, hollow geometry with approximately 7 mm wall thickness.
The CAD rendering in Figure 16 shows the hollow, multibranch T-valve design. The geometry
demonstrated the complexity of enclosed features achievable with AM-built HIP canisters. This case
study contextualized the application of AM + HIP for medium-scale, nuclear-relevant components. The
part featured multiple right-angle overhangs and branch intersections, which were successfully deposited
without the need for internal supports by employing optimized bead placement, deposition angles, and
alternating build strategies at the flange intersections. Machining was integrated during the build to ensure
net shape accuracy of critical features and to maintain smooth surfaces for subsequent powder filling.

 Figure 16. CAD model of T-valve canister.

 18

One challenge by the DED industry has been manufacturing fully enclosed hollow structures; typically,
machine limits and tool pathing are challenges to overcome. A common alternative strategy is to curve
edges and print nonright angles to account for overhangs [41, 42]. The part detailed in this report was
completed with full right-angle overhangs within one machine tool and is fully enclosed. The creation of
this part was enabled by a method developed through using distinct stepovers, print angles, and process
parameters. With these new methods, challenges arose, which required additional steps to mitigate. First,
the HIP valve was deconstructed into basic forms, which ultimately consist of cylinders. For the major
cylinder, traditional AM would require complex deposition angles or support structures to form the hole
subsection in the part. As a result of having subtractive capabilities, the major cylinder was programmed
as a solid tube with no hole, then subsequently programmed to have the cutout section machined.

The VTC-800G SR AM has a traditional xyz coordinate system, as well as two rotational axes designated
A and C. Rotational axes allow for consistent printing of cylindrical features; as a result, concentric ring
paths in the vertical orientation were preferred. The major cylinder was set up such that the offshoot
cylinder canister be prepared in the same orientation. The printing took approximately 7 h. The challenges
faced related to the toolpath of keeping the deposition head perpendicular to the printed surface. The outer
wall flanges were 45°, which prompted some unique solutions. As opposed to a continuous build, the
inner and outer walls at the angled flanges were printed in alternating paths as opposed to printed
simultaneously. The straight wall sections were all completed in as few toolpaths as possible while being
printed in a simultaneous motion. Ultimately, no major challenges were posed by the completion of the
major cylinder. The top lid face, as depicted in Figure 17, was completed using a unique stepover, layer
height, and toolpath compared with the typical parameters. The final AM-fabricated T‑valve shell is
shown. The sharp overhangs and enclosed sections highlight the successful deposition strategy. This
established the feasibility of producing fully enclosed HIP canisters without support.

The as-printed canister geometry was analyzed using blue-light scanning to confirm dimensional
tolerances, and x-ray computed tomography (XCT) scans were performed postbuild to evaluate print
integrity and identify regions prone to irregular deposition. Geometric features, particularly at sharp
corners and complex transitions, were analyzed to identify porosity and defects that can be detrimental to
the pressure holding capability of the canisters. After fabrication, the canister was filled with 316L SS

powder through a fill stem, vibrated to improve packing density, and subsequently degassed and welded

part. This fabrication sequence demonstrated the feasibility of producing fully enclosed, medium-scale
geometries via hybrid AM with integrated inspection and powder handling workflows that are critical to
successful HIP consolidation of complex components such as the T-valve.

3.2 IN SITU MONITORING: T-VALVE

On the Mazak VTC-800 laser hot wire AM system, multiple sensor modalities were used to monitor and
capture process data. Optical cameras were mounted to provide visual recordings of the build process.
Additionally, a weld pool camera was off axis to deposition and helped observe melt pool stability, wire
feed alignment, and general process conditions, making it possible to identify irregularities during
deposition. A FLIR A700 IR camera was also integrated, enabling thermal imaging of the part during the

understanding heat flow, potential hot spots, and cooling behavior throughout the AM process.
Thermocouples were attached to the baseplate to record precise, localized temperature histories. Unlike
the broader thermal field captured by the FLIR camera, thermocouples provided accurate point
measurements of the substrate, giving direct feedback on heat accumulation and dissipation over time.
Together, these modalities—optical, IR, and thermocouples—delivered complementary insights into the
thermal and physical behavior of the system, enabling better control, validation, and understanding of AM
on the VTC-800. Cameras, thermography, and thermocouples are shown monitoring the T-valve build in

3.3 NONDESTRUCTIVE EVALUATION: X-RAY COMPUTED TOMOGRAPHY

The as-printed T-valve canister was subjected to XCT using a ZEISS Volumax9 Titan system operating at
450 kV and 1,500 W to assess print integrity before powder filling (Figure 19). This nondestructive
evaluation helped identify regions of irregular deposition during printing and geometric transitions in the
component that could pose higher risks of defect formation. In parallel, the as-built canister was helium
leak tested to evaluate weld quality; this allowed for identification and manual repair of any weld
discontinuities before powder consolidation.

 Figure 19. XCT analysis of the as-printed T-valve canister before powder filling. Following HIP, the
 consolidated T-valve was reexamined via XCT to quantify defect distribution (Figure 20). The scans
 revealed a clear demarcation between the AM canister and the HIP core, with a higher concentration of
 defects localized along the AM–PM interface. These defects can be classified into two primary
 categories: (1) porosity, arising from incomplete densification or packing variations, and (2) oxide
 inclusions, associated with laser hot wire deposition of 316L SS during canister fabrication. Qualitative
 observations suggested that porosity is preferentially concentrated at the interface between the AM
 canister and the PM-HIP core. Porosity quantification studies are underway to provide a more complete
 statistical distribution.

XCT analysis also highlighted the significance of design and process control in complex hybrid
AM + PM-HIP builds. Variations in powder packing fraction, particularly in intricate geometries, can
drive localized densification challenges. Similarly, careful design of fill stems and powder filling
procedures is necessary to minimize irregularities and avoid defect clustering at geometric transitions.
Together, these results reinforced the importance of integrating XCT and leak testing into the
qualification workflow, ensuring that pre- and post-HIP states were well-understood before proceeding to
metallographic validation.

Further, artificial intelligent (AI) enabled X-ray computed tomography (XCT) reconstruction was
implemented to analyze the post-HIPed T-valve. Simurgh is an AI-enabled XCT reconstruction
framework that combines physics-based modeling, CAD-informed priors, and deep learning algorithms to
overcome longstanding challenges in industrial CT [43-48]. The framework has already demonstrated
substantial benefits, including reconstructions up to twelve times faster and flaw detection improvements
of more than six-fold compared to standard methods. Its ability to suppress artifacts such as beam
hardening and scattering while preserving fine structural detail has made it a versatile tool for non-
destructive evaluation across diverse materials and geometries. Building on these successes, we have
recently extended Simurgh to address the extreme demands of high-energy XCT of large-scale metallic
components. In this regime, conventional algorithms are hampered by scattering, streaking, and
incomplete penetration, which obscure internal features and limit flaw detectability. Using Simurgh,
however, we have been addressing these limitations producing cleaner reconstructions from significantly
faster scans [49].

As part of non-destructive inspection of the T-Valve printed and PM-HIPed under this AMMT work
package, we evaluated recent developments to examine the part before and after PM-HIP. At 450 kV, the
pre-HIP scan produced little usable information, as the size and thickness of the part resulted in severe
beam blocking and loss of defect sensitivity. Following HIP, the same valve was imaged using a 9 MeV
system, at North-Star Imaging (NSI), where conventional reconstructions still exhibited significant noise
and beam hardening. Applying the pre-trained Simurgh model developed in [49], we obtained high-
quality reconstructions that substantially reduced artifacts and improved interpretability. The
reconstructed volumes were analyzed with our deep learning segmentation framework [50], which
automatically isolated flaws and defects. Post-processing was performed using connected component
analysis and morphological operations to highlight ring-like features located along the AM–PM
boundary. To ensure accuracy, regions identified automatically were refined through manual expert
tuning. For each flaw, morphological features were extracted in three dimensions, including the Feret
diameter (the maximum caliper distance across a flaw), equivalent area diameter (the diameter of a sphere
of equal volume), elongation (ratio of longest to shortest axis), and sphericity (deviation from an ideal
spherical shape).

3.5 MICROSTRUCTURE CHARACTERIZATION

The AM HIP canister and the powder filled inside the canister for the T-valve were both of the same
material system - SS 316L. For microstructural evaluation of the T-valve, samples were extracted from
both the AM canister wall and the PM-HIP consolidated powder core. Because both regions were
fabricated using 316L SS, direct comparisons between the microstructure of the AM and PM-HIP
sections provided insight into the influence of processing route on grain morphology, grain boundary
character distribution, and ensuing mechanical properties.

To begin, blue light 3D scanning was employed to quantify the dimensional changes in the T-valve before
and after HIP. The as-built geometry of the AM T-valve canister was scanned immediately after AM
fabrication, and the same procedure was repeated after HIP of the powder filled canister to capture the net
shrinkage and distortion associated with densification. The overlay of pre-HIP (as-built) and post-HIP
scans (Figure 24) clearly shows uniform dimensional shrinkage across the entire component. The overlay
maps also confirm that the shrinkage was isotropic, with no significant warpage or distortion at the
flanges and only a slight noticeable distortion at the branch arms. In the cross-sectional comparisons, the
post-HIP geometry (red outline) aligns closely with the as-built geometry (white outline), demonstrating
that the HIP process resulted in predictable and homogeneous densification. The geometric deviations
between as-built and post-HIP specimens were primarily confined to concave corners and thick-walled
sections, consistent with finite element model predictions of densification gradients in these regions.
More results on the finite element model predictions are discussed in Section 3.4. Overall, the blue light
scan comparison validated that HIP processing produced a dimensional reduction without introducing
large distortions or shape irregularities.

 Figure 24. Blue light scan comparison of as-built and post-HIP T-valve depicting shrinkages post-HIP.

As a first step toward characterizing macroscale porosity in the PM-HIP regions of the T-valve, samples
were extracted from various sections of the post-HIP component and subjected to metallographic analysis.
Samples were strategically extracted from key geometric features in the T-valve such as the stem, branch-
arm, and the flange, as well as the intersection of each of these geometric features. A montage of optical

images was analyzed (Figure 25) for each of these geometric features, which showed a distinct
delineation between the AM canister wall and the PM-HIP core, particularly in the flange region of the
T‑valve. Such distinct differentiation at the can–core interface was assumed to arise from the formation of
an oxide layer on the AM build, inhibiting seamless diffusion bonding at the interface.

 Figure 25. Optical montage of samples extracted from different locations in the T-valve.

To further analyze the grain/grain boundary characteristics as well as to characterize the chemical
composition along the can–core interface, EBSD and EDS analysis were performed at the AM + PM-HIP
interface. EBSD mapping demonstrated a highly textured microstructure with large, elongated grains in
the AM canister compared with equiaxed grains with random orientation distribution in the HIP-
consolidated core, as shown in Figure 26. Scanning electron microscopy combined with EDS revealed the
AM + PM-HIP interface was fully bonded. However, a distinct interfacial layer of inclusions or oxides
was observed, with evidence of intermittent interfacial porosity. These discrete oxide inclusions were
found to be rich in manganese, chromium, and silicon, as detected by EDS analysis. In most PM-HIP
applications, the HIP canister is machined or leached post-HIP. However, if the HIP canister was to be
used as a cladding, these oxides could degrade local bonding and have the potential to be detrimental to
mechanical properties, especially at the can–core interface.

3.6 MECHANICAL TESTING

Room temperature uniaxial tensile testing was conducted on specimens extracted from the AM canister,
the PM-HIP powder core, and the AM + PM-HIP interface region of the T-valve. The resulting stress–
strain curves (Figure 27) revealed clear distinctions in strength and ductility between the different regions,
highlighting the influence of processing history on mechanical response. Samples taken from the AM
canister exhibited a YS of 202 ± 3 MPa and UTS of 434 ± 7 MPa. In contrast, the PM-HIP 316L SS
samples demonstrated a higher YS and UTS of 284 ± 11 MPa and 585 ± 12 MPa, respectively. The
samples extracted from the AM + PM-HIP interface samples failed prematurely and were found to be
extremely brittle. Characterization of fracture surfaces from these specimens was performed. EDS
mapping of the fracture surface (inset, Figure 27) identified a high density of nickel- and manganese-rich
oxide inclusions decorating the fracture surface. These oxides were assumed to have originated from the
as-deposited AM builds, which inhibited any diffusion bonding at the AM and PM-HIP interface, thereby
deteriorating the mechanical properties in this region. ASTM A182 specifies minimum YS and UTS of
170 and 485 MPa, respectively, for forged 316L SS. The results from the PM-HIP samples in this study
exceeded these requirements. This work demonstrated that the integrated AM + PM-HIP approach can
deliver mechanical properties on par with or superior to conventionally forged SSs.

3.7 T-VALVE: CONCLUDING REMARKS

The T-valve case study demonstrated successful fabrication and PM-HIP consolidation of a medium-
scale, fully enclosed AM canister filled with 316L SS powder. This study showed that predictable
densification and shrinkage could be achieved even in complex geometries such as a T-valve. Although
PM-HIP produced a dense and equiaxed powder core, microstructural characterization revealed interfacial
oxides at the AM + PM-HIP interface that locally affected tensile performance. This result emphasizes
the critical role of interface chemistry control. For the PM-HIP community, this case study underscores
the promise of hybrid manufacturing (AM + machining) for complex, hollow parts and the advantage of
using convergent AM + PM-HIP approaches for complex part fabrication. Future work will target scaling
hybrid AM + PM-HIP to full-size industrial parts, integrating hybrid machining strategies to clean and
finish internal canister surfaces before powder filling, and systematically comparing similar versus
dissimilar can–core material combinations. These steps will ensure that interface issues are understood
and mitigated, enabling reliable deployment of hybrid-manufactured components in safety-critical
applications. With this foundation established at the medium scale, the final case study in this report
transitions to a large-scale impeller. Case study 3, described in the next section, demonstrated the
feasibility of hybrid AM + PM-HIP for reactor-relevant and industrially significant components.

 4. CASE STUDY 3: LARGE-SCALE, GEOMETRICALLY COMPLEX PART—HYDRO-
 IMPELLER

The third case study in this report pushed the convergent AM + PM-HIP approach to large-scale, high-
complexity geometries such as the hydro-impeller. This component, fabricated by WAAM 410 SS
canister, which encapsulates SA508 Grade 4N powder core, was hot isostatic pressed together. The work
successfully produced an approximately 2000 lb, 5 ft diameter, 2.5 ft tall impeller, validated through
multirobot WAAM fabrication, predictive HIP modeling, and post-HIP microstructural and mechanical
characterization. Key findings included strong agreement between model predictions and experimental
shrinkage, fully dense HIP cores with <2% porosity, microstructural characterization, and tensile
properties across the blades, hub, and blade–hub interface. For the PM-HIP community, this case study
demonstrated the opportunities and challenges of scaling to reactor-relevant component sizes, with due
consideration for powder cleanliness and interface chemistry. The experimental design included WAAM
process optimization, Pelican-enabled in situ monitoring, finite element model–based HIP simulations,

 28

and mechanical sampling across geometric features, ensuring results were directly tied to qualification
needs. This case study thus provided a critical demonstration of using a convergent AM + PM-HIP
approach as a pathway to manufacture large, complex, nuclear-grade components.

4.1 CANISTER FABRICATION

MedUSA (Figure 28) is multiagent state-of-the-art WAAM system. It was designed to be able to
manufacture large metallic parts with a horizontal diameter of approximately 6 ft and maximum height of
approximately 7 ft. The introduction of a workpiece positioner allowed access from any robot to any
location in the workspace. The unique control architecture with artificial intelligence task management
decided on the proper assignments of atomic tasks independently for each of the three arms as well as the
positioner coordination. The optimized velocities and accelerations for all four robots (including the
positioner) were calculated in real time, along with temporal feedback-based corrections at 250 Hz to
ensure a high quality of deposition and uniform growth of the part. Additionally, the control system had
built-in collision avoidance and automated task creation for fault recovery. This unique approach resulted
in an extremely resilient system to power or hardware failures. In case of a major malfunction, as long as
one deposition head functioned and the power was restored, the system could automatically recover and
finish the manufacturing job. Other than the controls and reliability, the system effectively tripled the
productivity of a single deposition head, especially for parts with large diameters. Currently, the
commercially available welding torches have been proven in manufacturing parts at a rate of 12–18 lb/h
depending on the type of alloys. This puts the system maximum deposition rate at near approximately
50 lb/h. Prototype torches tested with Inconel 625 showed a combined deposition rate of over 100 lb/h,
and a single unit was tested up to 46 lb/h in creating complex geometries. The WAAM system at ORNL
was tested and manufactured a variety of components with many different steel alloys: low alloy,
stainless, ferritic-martensitic, maraging, invar, tool, nickel-based, or copper-based steels [51, 52].
Additionally, the system collected over 50 different variables in real time, providing valuable data for
analysis and maintaining a digital twin.

The complex geometries of AM components challenge many traditional quality inspection paradigms, a
complication that is only exacerbated by the large-scale structures made possible by WAAM. In situ
monitoring of AM processes promises to alleviate some of these burdens by providing continuous
monitoring of process stability and component quality during manufacturing, allowing for a scalable,
concurrent inspection technique. However, analysis of in situ monitoring data streams produced during
WAAM processes is still in its infancy. To this end, ORNL has been developing Pelican, a data-driven

 recording and viewing machine and operational meta- and log data;
 exploring and visualizing multimodal, spatiotemporal process monitoring data streams;
 spatially and temporally registering ex situ characterization results with in situ sensing data for
 comparative analyses;
 training machine learning models to detect process anomalies and predict part quality; and
 benchmarking trained models to quantify performance.

Pelican is a flexible analysis tool designed for spatiotemporal datasets of arbitrary origin, structure, and
resolution. Available with both a GUI and a companion API, Pelican is built first for DED processes, in

spatial quality (e.g., XCT, dimensional conformance scans, microstructural data). In addition to its suite
of spatiotemporal visualizations, Pelican provides a flexible architecture for developing and deploying
advanced analytical tools (e.g., computer vision algorithms, machine learning models) aimed at assessing
process and part quality using the multimodal datasets. Figure 33 provides a summary of the Pelican

4.4 NON-DESTRUCTIVE EVALUATION BY ULTRASONIC PULSE-ECHO
 MEASUREMENT

The ultrasonic pulse-echo method for nondestructive examination (NDE) transmits pulses of high-
frequency sound into the material under test. Internal features, such as cracks or inclusions, are
discontinuities in the material properties, and result in a large proportion of the applied acoustic energy
being reflected back to the source (ultrasonic transducer). When the applied ultrasonic pulse encounters a
boundary between well-bonded regions in materials, most of the energy continues to propagate with only
a small amount of energy reflected. However, if a delamination or debonded interface exists, the
significant acoustic impedance mismatch produces a strong reflection. By measuring the time-of-flight of
these echoes, the location of the delamination can be determined.
The ultrasonic pulse-echo NDE technique can be effective for parts fabricated with hot isostatic pressing
(HIP) because it can penetrate thick, dense structures while providing sensitivity to planar defects such as
separation between bonded layers (particularly between the internal material and the external shell). To
evaluate the feasibility of ultrasonic pulse-echo measurements for debonding detection, several
measurements were carried out with portion of an impeller that was fabricated using HIP. In this case, the
shell and the internal fill material were dissimilar, increasing the potential for debonding. The preliminary
measurements were performed on the specimen with as-fabricated surface roughness.

 35

Figure 38 presents the test setup and the measurement locations on both the exterior and interior surfaces
of the HIP specimen. The setup [Figure 38(a)] consists of an ultrasonic pulser/receiver connected to a
2.25 MHz longitudinal transducer operating in pulse-echo mode. Due to the rough specimen surface,
honey was used as the couplant to ensure that the applied acoustic signal could be reliably coupled into
the HIP specimen. The ultrasonic signals were digitized and recorded using an oscilloscope. As shown in
Figure 38(b) and Figure 38(c), measurements were performed at nine locations—eight on the exterior
surface and one on the interior surface.

4.7 IMPELLER: CONCLUDING REMARKS

The impeller case study demonstrated the first successful fabrication and HIP consolidation of a very
large (approximately 2,000 lb and 60 in. diameter) WAAM-built canister filled with SA508 4N powder,
showing excellent agreement between predictive modeling and experimental shrinkage, with near-full
densification. These findings validated the feasibility of scaling the convergent AM + PM-HIP approach
to reactor-relevant component sizes and highlighted powder cleanliness and interface chemistry as key
challenges. For the PM-HIP community, this study establishes a proof of concept that large-scale,
complex geometries can be reliably consolidated, thereby setting the stage for industrial adoption. Future
directions should include exploration of multimaterial HIP for functional cladding and thermal
management, enabling tailored designs based on thermal expansion, chemical compatibility, and diffusion
kinetics. Additional studies should focus on site-specific property variations within large parts (near vs.
far from the canister wall), machinability of HIP components, and a techno-economic analysis to quantify
cost advantages at the industrial scale. Together, these efforts will transform the hybrid AM + PM-HIP
process into a mainstream pathway for manufacturing large, safety-critical components.

 5. SUMMARY OF REPORT

This report presents a systematic exploration of hybrid AM and PM-HIP as a convergent pathway for
fabricating NNS metallic components at scales and levels of complexity not easily achievable by
traditional methods. The overarching aim was to establish a framework that is both scientifically rigorous
and practically viable, demonstrating how hybrid AM + PM-HIP can complement forging and
conventional fabrication to expand the nation’s manufacturing toolkit for critical nuclear reactor pressure
vessel components. By introducing greater design flexibility, responsiveness, and scalability, this work
supports the US Department of Energy’s mission to accelerate the qualification, demonstration, and
deployment of advanced materials and manufacturing technologies for nuclear energy systems.

 42

The study progressed through three case studies, each providing insights as the size and complexity
increased. The first case focused on small-scale cylindrical canisters, comparing conventional canister
fabrication with those produced using LPBF and DED. The results showed that AM-fabricated canisters
achieved the same densification, microstructures, and tensile properties as their conventional counterparts.
This confirmed that AM canister serve as a practical substitute, offering comparable material quality and
new flexibility and resilience in the supply chain.

The second case study moved to a medium-scale T-valve, representing a more geometrically complex,
enclosed, and multibranch structure. The component was successfully fabricated and hot isostatic pressed
to full density, validating that hybrid AM + PM-HIP can handle enclosed geometries. At the same time,
this study revealed a key challenge; oxide inclusions and interface chemistry played an important role in
local ductility and fracture behavior, especially at the AM can–PM-HIP core interface. These findings
highlighted the dual nature of hybrid AM + PM-HIP. The method holds immense promise for enabling
complex geometries but also requires careful attention to interface control and monitoring.

The third and final case study demonstrated industrial-scale feasibility through the fabrication of an
approximately 2,000 lb, 5 ft diameter, and 2.5 ft tall impeller. Built using multirobot WAAM and
consolidated by HIP, the impeller reached near-full density across its blades and the hub. Predictive
modeling of shrinkage and densification closely matched experimental results, thus instilling confidence
in assessments inferred from digital twins. This large-scale achievement marks a milestone for reactor-
relevant components, proving that hybrid AM + PM-HIP can operate at industrial scales with
performance on par with established methods.

Taken together, these studies provide a coherent progression: small-scale work established scientific
parity with conventional canisters; medium-scale work demonstrated feasibility for complex, enclosed
geometries and exposed interface challenges; and large-scale work validated industrial viability by
achieving uniform properties and structural integrity at reactor-relevant scales. The body of evidence
shows that AM + PM-HIP is not meant to replace conventional manufacturing modalities but to
complement and extend it, adding valuable flexibility for complex geometries, reducing lead times, and
enhancing resilience across critical supply chains.

The significance of this work is broad. For the US Department of Energy, it directly supports the
Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies program’s goals by providing a proven pathway for
advanced manufacturing in nuclear systems. For the PM-HIP community, it delivers a rare and openly
available dataset connecting canister fabrication routes, geometry, and interface chemistry to resulting
microstructure and performance. For the US manufacturing base, and especially those producing nuclear
reactor pressure vessel components, it offers a credible, complementary approach that strengthens the
nation’s ability to design and deliver safety-critical parts with confidence. In summary, this report
demonstrates that hybrid AM + PM-HIP is scientifically sound and industrially significant. By merging
the design agility of AM with the consolidation strength of HIP, this approach created a new capability
for US manufacturing—one that offers flexibility, reliability, and innovation for the future of nuclear-
grade component production.

 6. FUTURE WORK

The progression of case studies in this report—spanning small cylindrical canisters, a medium-scale
T‑valve, and a large-scale impeller—demonstrated that hybrid AM + PM-HIP can deliver NNS
components with high density, structural integrity, and mechanical performance. Together, these studies
provide a proof of concept that AM-fabricated canisters are viable replacements for conventional cans,
complex enclosed geometries can be consolidated without catastrophic distortion, and reactor-relevant
scale components can be produced with uniform properties. The next phase of research must build on this

 43

foundation to transform AM + PM-HIP from a demonstration technology into a mainstream
manufacturing solution for nuclear, defense, and aerospace applications.

Powder quality and supply represent a central challenge. HIP-grade powders remain expensive, with
limited suppliers and long lead times, particularly for specialized alloys. Scaling this technology to large-
scale deployment will require significant investment in domestic powder production capacity and
improvements in powder cleanliness. Reducing oxide satellites, controlling oxygen and nitrogen content,
and ensuring consistent size distributions are critical for achieving reliable toughness and ductility. New
approaches to producing ferrous and SS powders at scale, as well as strategies for designing novel alloy
compositions, must be pursued in tandem with component demonstrations. Without reliable and
affordable powders, the promise of HIP for large structural components cannot be fully realized.

The next priority is developing more robust and accessible modeling frameworks. Current HIP
simulations are limited to a handful of experts and require long run times, slowing the pace of design
iteration. Establishing shared material property datasets and constitutive models for widely used alloys
will allow predictive modeling to be deployed more broadly across industry. Incorporating plastic and
viscoplastic models into user-friendly software tools will make it possible to anticipate capsule
deformation, shrinkage, and densification in days or hours rather than weeks. As part of this, integrating
HIP modeling into an Integrated Computational Materials Engineering framework will accelerate alloy
discovery, enabling the design of powders and components that are optimized for HIP processing from
the outset. These advances will help reduce trial and error experimentation and shorten the time to
qualification.

Future work must also continue to advance AM for canister design. The ability to print leak-tight, weld-
free canisters introduces new design flexibility but requires rigorous process control. Hybrid approaches
that combine AM with subtractive finishing will ensure that internal surfaces are clean and free of defects
before powder filling. In situ monitoring during AM builds, coupled with integrated inspection techniques
such as blue light scanning, XCT, and leak testing, will be essential to establish repeatability. The next
step is to extend these strategies to dissimilar and multimaterial canisters, where functional cladding,
thermal management, or graded structures can be achieved. Such capabilities would allow PM-HIP
components to be tailored for specific service conditions, from corrosion resistance to thermal expansion
matching.

Scaling to larger components introduces a different set of scientific and engineering problems. The
successful demonstration of a 5 ft diameter impeller confirms that reactor-relevant geometries can be
produced, but further work is required to ensure through-thickness microstructural uniformity, consistent
grain size, and reproducible mechanical properties across large sections. The construction of larger HIP
units—exceeding 4 m in diameter—will be essential to consolidate full-scale reactor pressure vessel
heads and other massive components. In parallel, research must develop improved NDE techniques for
thick-walled parts and refine process models to predict distortions and property gradients that arise in
large geometries.

Advanced process controls will be another cornerstone of future research. The qualification of HIP
components for safety-critical service requires consistency and repeatability across builds. Real-time
monitoring of temperature, pressure, and densification, paired with machine learning–enabled feedback
loops, will allow anomalies to be detected and corrected during the process itself. Digital twin
frameworks that integrate in situ sensing with predictive models will further enhance confidence,
allowing designers and regulators to trace every step of the process and connect it to final part
performance. These capabilities will reduce variability, accelerate qualification, and lower the costs of
certification.

 44

Finally, the long-term performance of HIP alloys in extreme environments must be benchmarked.
Although tensile properties of HIP components have already been shown to match or exceed those of
wrought benchmarks, questions remain about creep-fatigue behavior, radiation tolerance, hydrogen
embrittlement, and corrosion resistance under aggressive conditions such as supercritical CO2, molten
salts, or high-temperature steam. Large components may also exhibit microstructural gradients through
thickness that affect stability and performance. Addressing these challenges will require systematic
studies of phase stability, carbide and precipitate evolution, fracture toughness, and defect sensitivity
under service-relevant conditions. Coating and cladding strategies may also be needed to extend
durability in highly corrosive or irradiated environments.

In summary, the future of AM + PM-HIP lies in aligning technical advancements with industrial scale-up
and qualification frameworks. Modeling tools must become faster and more accessible, powders must be
produced in greater volumes with higher quality, AM canisters must be manufactured with precision and
reliability, and HIP systems must grow to reactor-scale dimensions. At the same time, advanced process
controls and digital twins will reduce variability and increase confidence, and benchmarking under
extreme conditions will establish credibility for nuclear, defense, and aerospace deployment. By pursuing
these avenues, AM + PM-HIP can transition from a promising complement to forging and casting into a
mainstream manufacturing pathway—one capable of meeting the urgent needs of energy security,
national defense, and aerospace innovation.
