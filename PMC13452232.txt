Impact of green tea powder on the flavor and functional properties of fermented glutinous rice sweet wine☆
Authors: Deyi Yang, Dajuan Shi, Xueyue Deng, Linyao Song, Xuan Dong, Yichen Zhao, Su Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study developed a novel tea glutinous rice sweet wine (TRW) by co-fermenting glutinous rice with tea powder, enhancing flavor and functionality. Using HS-SPME-GC–MS and E-nose, we analyzed volatile organic compounds (VOCs), microbial activity, and enzyme dynamics during fermentation. HS-SPME-GC–MS identified 135 VOCs; ROAV analysis highlighted 37 key aroma-active compounds. Critically, 4-hydroxy-3-methoxystyrene and phenylacetaldehyde imparted unique clove and honey aromas, generated via microbial/enzyme-driven amino acid metabolism. Compared to traditional sweet wine (TSW), TRW exhibited significantly higher in vitro antioxidant capacity and, in vitro assays, inhibited rises in blood pressure, glucose, and blood lipid (triglyceride) levels. TRW also contained lower alcohol and sugar content, benefiting health-conscious consumers. This demonstrates tea powder's potential to improve the flavor profile and nutritional value of traditional rice wines, enabling functional beverages with reduced alcohol and sugar.

Introduction

Traditional sweet wine occupies a distinguished position in Chinese culinary history. Its unique combination of sweet and sour tastes, along with its rich nutritional profile, has made it a staple in both everyday meals and celebratory occasions (Jiang et al., 2020). However, conventional sweet wine often fails to meet the needs of modern consumers due to its high alcohol and sugar content, which may pose health risks for certain populations, such as individuals with diabetes or those seeking to limit their alcohol intake (Niu et al., 2019). Furthermore, traditional formulations have seen limited innovation regarding functionality and flavor diversity, resulting in a growing demand for healthier and more dynamic alternatives.

Tea is a beverage renowned for its health benefits, being rich in polyphenols and flavonoids. It has long been recognized for its ability to improve metabolic health, including blood sugar regulation, antioxidant defense, and anti-inflammatory properties (Fu et al., 2017). Due to its catechin content, tea possesses antibacterial properties and has garnered increasing attention from researchers regarding its influence on enhancing the flavor and nutritional value of co-fermented foods (Tian et al., 2024). For instance, Xu et al. (2020) co-fermented Fu brick tea with glutinous rice to produce sweet beer, which enhanced microbial biotransformation and altered flavor metabolites. Similarly, Chen et al. (2023) co-fermented jasmine tea extract with beer to amplify floral and fresh aromas, reduce bitterness, and improve overall beer quality. The antioxidant capacity of these co-fermented samples significantly exceeds that of traditional beer, with an inhibition rate of amylase activity that is 30.5% higher than that of conventional beer. Ma et al. (2026) co-fermented dark tea infusion with Debaryomyces hansenii and Bacillus subtilis, and demonstrated that co-fermentation significantly reduced total tea polyphenols (by 45.12%) and ester catechins (by 92.63%) while promoting the accumulation of gallic acid, thereby improving the overall flavor quality of fermented tea beverages. More directly related to the present study, Liang et al. (2016) and Lin et al. (2016) investigated the effects of green tea or its extracts on the fermentation of Hongqu glutinous rice wine, demonstrating that green tea addition improved flavor, inhibited acidification, and increased total phenol content, while green tea extracts at different concentrations differentially regulated the biomass and enzyme production of monascus and yeasts. Wu et al. (2024) reported that green tea beer (GTB) exhibited superior antioxidant properties and desirable aroma profiles, with ethyl caprylate, linalool, and phenethyl alcohol identified as key contributors. Preliminary laboratory work has also demonstrated that the co-fermentation of Eucommia ulmoides tea powder with glutinous rice yielded improved oxidation performance, which could mitigate the ‘three highs’ (hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and hyperglycemia) associated with mixed sweet wine (Ren et al., 2022). This trend reflects the growing interest in maximizing tea's nutritional benefits and exploring its potential as an innovative functional ingredient in food. Green tea, in particular, is non-fermented and retains the highest levels of unoxidized catechins (Ma et al., 2014), providing abundant flavor precursors for microbial/enzymatic conversion, while its minimal processing preserves a fresh, vegetal aroma (Zhang et al., 2023); thus, green tea powder is an ideal ingredient for developing a novel tea-infused rice wine.

During the co-fermentation of sweet wine, the microbial community often undergoes dynamic changes (Huang et al., 2019). To develop new sweet rice wines, numerous researchers have optimized the fermentation process, monitored microbial activities during brewing, and analyzed nutritional components, thereby providing insights into enhancing the flavor quality of sweet rice wine (Wang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2020). For instance, alcohol is ultimately produced through the hydrolysis of rice starch into sugars by brewer's yeast, facilitated by liquefaction from Aspergillus and certain bacteria, as well as saccharification enzymes (Chen et al., 2023). Additionally, Aspergillus, brewer's yeast, and bacteria in the koji produce various extracellular enzymes that enhance cell metabolism and subsequent small molecule production, thereby contributing to the flavor profile of the final product. Sakandar et al. (2020) also demonstrated that the types and quantities of microorganisms present in the JiuQu (JQ), along with their interactions, determined the yield and sensory quality of sweet rice wine, highlighting their essential role in fermentation. Building on the known effects of tea on microorganisms during co-fermentation, this study examines the influence of tea powder on microbial dynamics and extracellular enzyme activities in glutinous rice co-fermentation, providing insights into the mechanisms of aroma formation.

This study aimed to investigate the effect of green tea powder on the flavor and functional properties of glutinous rice sweet wine, with the goal of developing a novel product: tea glutinous rice wine (TRW). Additionally, the study analyzed the characteristics of flavor changes during the fermentation process. A variety of methods were employed to assess the flavor profile, including sensory evaluation, electronic nose (E-nose) technique, and headspace solid-phase microextraction gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS). These methods provided a comprehensive overview of changes in the flavor profile during TRW fermentation (Heo et al., 2026). Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) and Relative odor activity value (ROAV) were utilized to differentiate and characterize the various stages of fermentation, aiding in the identification of important volatile organic compounds (VOCs) following the addition of tea powder. Furthermore, fluctuations in microbial and extracellular enzyme activities during TRW fermentation were observed, and their relationship with key VOCs and sensory evaluation was explored. The effects of tea powder incorporation on enhancing the functional properties and flavor of traditional fermented foods were elucidated through flavor, physicochemical properties, and in vitro enzyme inhibition experiments comparing TRW with traditional sweet wine (TSW). This study is significant for the comprehensive investigation of functional and flavor changes in traditional sweet wine with the addition of tea powder, as well as the mechanisms of aroma formation during TRW fermentation.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Tea powder was sourced from Anshun Yucha Village Tea Industry Co., Ltd. (Guizhou, China). JiuQu was sourced by Guizhou Xiaoxian Yeast Co., Ltd. (Guizhou, China). The glutinous rice used in this study was provided by the Key Laboratory of Plant Resource Conservation and Germplasm Innovation in Mountainous Areas, Guizhou University. Potassium sodium tartrate, CuSO₄·5H₂O, sodium hydroxide, glucose standard solution, and concentrated HCl were produced by Hubio Trading Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). C₇–C₄₀ normal alkanes and phenyl ethyl acetate were manufactured by Shanghai Ampel Standard Technical Service Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). PVPP, TritonX-100, Catechol, salicin, DNS, DTT, TPP, pyruvic acid, and other chemical products were produced by Sigma Aldrich Trading Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Glass beads for yeast spread-plating were purchased from Coolaber, China. Methyl Red-Methylene Blue (DI0421) was obtained from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Furthermore, all other chemicals and reagents utilized were of analytical grade or chromatographic purity.

Processing of samples

First, an appropriate amount of glutinous rice was thoroughly rinsed and soaked in water at room temperature (25 °C) for 12 h, using a volume of water twice that of the rice, or until the rice could be easily crushed by hand. After draining, the rice was steamed for 35 min and then cooled to approximately 30 °C in pure water. It was subsequently mixed with 2.5% ultra-fine tea powder and 0.4% angel sweet liquor, stirred thoroughly, and placed in a fermenter. Following the addition of 20% pure water and the creation of a central hole, the mixture was placed in an electric constant temperature incubator (DHP-9162CP, Changzhou Jintan Liangyou Instrument Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) to ferment at 32 °C for 56 h. The fermented product was subsequently collected and sterilized in a 75 °C water bath for 15 min to yield the final product (CS100, ThermoFisher Scientific Co., Ltd., USA). During the fermentation process, TRW samples were collected at 0, 12, 24, 40, and 56 h, and were stored in a − 20 °C refrigerator after halting fermentation. The TSW sample is an original traditional sweet wine brew that is fermented normally for 56 h without tea powder.

The fermentation parameters (tea powder addition, JQ addition, fermentation time, and temperature) were initially optimized using single-factor experiments with scoring conducted on three sensory attributes: color and appearance, aroma, and taste. This was followed by an orthogonal array design (L9(34)) to identify the optimal fermentation conditions.

Sensory characteristics evaluation for TRW

A trained sensory panel (5 males and 5 females, aged 20 to 40) evaluated samples at five fermentation time points (0, 12, 24, 40, 56 h) of TRW and samples fermented for 56 h of TSW. Each sample was scored from 0 to 10 for six attributes: color, fragrance, sweetness, astringency, alcohol aroma, and viscosity (Song et al., 2025). All attributes were rated on an intensity scale from 0 (none or extremely weak) to 10 (extremely strong); higher scores thus indicate greater intensity of the attribute, regardless of its hedonic valence. For example, a higher score for astringency means a stronger astringent taste, while a higher score for sweetness means a sweeter taste. The scores from ten panelists were averaged and analyzed using a t-test. Detailed scoring criteria are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Objective color measurement

To quantitatively evaluate the color evolution of TRW during fermentation, a photographic method combined with a standard color checker was employed. At each TRW fermentation time point (0, 12, 24, 40, 56 h), about 5.00 g of TRW sample and TSW sample fermented for 56 h (rice grains and liquid) were placed in a white ceramic dish under fixed lighting conditions (natural daylight, diffuse lighting). A X-Rite Color Checker Classic (or a standard gray/color scale card) was placed next to the sample in each photograph. All images were captured using the same smartphone (iPhone 16, main camera) with locked white balance, exposure, and focus settings.

The captured images were imported into ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). The RGB values were measured from three randomly selected regions of the liquid phase of each sample. These RGB values were then converted to CIELAB (L*a*b*) color space using the standard sRGB-to-XYZ-to-L*a*b* transformation formulas (Illuminant D65, 2° standard observer). The reported L* (lightness), a* (red-green), and b* (yellow-blue) values are the average of triplicate measurements. The method was based on the procedure of Zhu et al. (2026).

E-nose analysis

The E-nose method was based on previous studies but was slightly modified to accommodate the specific samples used (Xu et al., 2025). The flammable fingerprints of TRW samples at different time points (0 h, 12 h, 24 h, 40 h, and 56 h) and TSW fermentation 56 h samples were analyzed using the portable electronic headset system PEN3 (Win Muster Airsense Analytics, Inc., Schwering, Germany), equipped with a sensor array composed of ten metal oxide semiconductors (see Table S2). A 1.00 g sample of TRW and TSW was placed in a 20.00 mL headspace sample bottle and immediately sealed, respectively. The sample was then introduced onto the sensor surface at a flow rate of 300.00 mL/min. Detection occurred over a period of 100 s, during which the sensor signal was recorded. The E-nose settings were as follows: detection time was set to 120 s, cleaning time to 100 s, pre-injection time to 5 s, injection flow rate to 200.00 mL/min, and the carrier gas flow rate was also maintained at 200.00 mL/min. Each sample was measured in triplicate.

Qualitative and quantitative analysis of volatile compounds detected by HS-SPME-GC–MS

Samples of TRW (0, 12, 24, 40, 56 h) and TSW (56 h) were analyzed for volatile organic compounds (VOCs) using headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with HS-SPME-GC–MS. For each analysis, 4.00 g of sample was placed in a 20.00 mL headspace vial and sealed with a polytetrafluoroethylene/butyl rubber septum. Then, 10.00 μL of an internal standard solution (ethyl phenylacetate, 8.21 mg/L) was added to each vial. The absolute mass of the internal standard (referred to as “internal standard solvent quality” in the quantification formula) was calculated as follows: the volume of internal standard solution added is 10.00 μL, which equals 10.00 μL, and its concentration is 8.21 μg/mL; thus, the internal standard solvent quality is 10.00 μL × 8.21 μg/mL = 0.0821 μg. The VOCs were extracted using a solid-phase microextraction fiber for 30 min, followed by an additional 3 min of extraction in the gas chromatography injection port. GC–MS separation was performed on an Agilent 7890-5975C system equipped with an HP-5 column. The oven temperature program was: 40 °C for 3 min, increased to 80 °C at 5 °C /min, then to 160 °C at 10 °C/min (hold 0.5 min), then to 175 °C at 2 °C/min, and finally to 230 °C at 10 °C/min for 7 min. The analysis was conducted in split less mode with helium as carrier gas at 103 kPa. The MS was operated in electron impact mode at 70 eV, with ion source temperature at 230 °C, quadrupole at 150 °C, and a solvent delay of 7 min. Full-scan mass spectra were recorded in the range of m/z 45–500. VOCs were identified by comparison of mass spectra with the NIST Chemistry Webbook database, and retention index (RI) values were calculated using C₇–C₄₀ n-alkane standards. The relative content of VOCs was calculated using the following formula:(1)The relative content of VOCsμg/g=Peak area of volatile compounds×Internal standard solvent qualityμgPeak area of internal standard solution×Quality of samplegwhere the “Internal standard solvent quality” is 0.0821 μg (calculated above), and the “Quality of sample” is 4.00 g. All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

Dynamic changes of microorganisms and enzymes during TRW fermentation

The measurement of dynamic changes in Saccharomyces and Aspergillus in TRW was conducted by the China National Standard Method (GB 4789.15–2016), titled ‘Food Microbiological Examination - Counting of Moulds and Yeasts.’ Amylase activity was assayed as described by Wang et al. (2023). Polyphenol oxidase (PPO) was quantified as described by Adeseko et al. (2021). β-glucosidase activity was determined using the method described by Xiao et al. (2021). Pectinase activity was assessed by quantifying galacturonic acid in the sample, which reacted with 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid at 540 nm (Kc et al., 2020). Pyruvate decarboxylase (PDC) activity was assayed as modified by Tian et al. (2017). The activity of alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) was determined according to the method of Gao (2012) with slight modifications. One unit of β-glucosidase, amylase, and pectinase activity was the enzyme releasing 1.00 μg of glucose from its respective substrate per minute under the assay conditions.

Determination of functional components in TRW and TSW

The content of free amino acids was determined using the China National Standard Method (GB/T 8314–2013, ‘Determination of Total Free Amino Acids in Tea’). The polyphenol content was assessed according to the China National Standard Method (GB/T 21733–2008, ‘Tea Beverage’). The HPLC method employed for the determination of rutin and epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) in TRW and TSW was consistent with the protocol established by Ren et al. (2022).

Determination of alcohol content and total sugar by TRW and TSW

The measurement of alcohol content was conducted in accordance with the China National Standard Method for Food Safety (GB 5009.225–2016, ‘Determination of Ethanol Content in Wine’), while the total sugar measurement adhered to the China National Standard Method for Food Safety (GB/T 13662–2018, ‘Huangjiu’).

Evaluation on the antioxidant properties

The activity of superoxide dismutase (O2-·), the clearance rate of hydroxyl radicals (·OH), and the antioxidant capacities determined by DPPH and ABTS+ were measured according to the method described by Ren et al. (2022).

Enzyme inhibition ability in vitro

In vitro α-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibitory activity analysis

Following the recommendations of Ren et al., 2022, a slight modification was implemented in the α-glucosidase and α-amylase inhibition experiments.

In the α-glucosidase inhibition assay, 5.00 μL of the sample solution, 25.00 μL of α-glucosidase (0.10 U/mL), and 140.00 μL of 0.10 mol/L phosphate buffer solution (pH 6.8) were combined in a centrifuge tube. Following a 5-min reaction at 37 °C, 25.00 μL of a 24.00 mmol/L p-nitrophenyl-β-D-glucopyranoside (PNPG) solution was added, and the reaction was continued at 37 °C for an additional 15 min. The reaction was then terminated by adding 100.00 μL of 0.20 mol/L Na2CO3 solution, and the absorbance was measured at 405 nm. Distilled water served as a blank control in place of the sample solution, and distilled water was also utilized as a control in place of the α-glucosidase solution. The experiment was conducted in triplicate.(3)Inhibition rate%=1−Asample−AcontrolAblank×100%

In the α-amylase inhibition assay, 500.00 μL of the sample solution was mixed with 500.00 μL of α-amylase solution (1 U/mL) at 25 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of DNS reagent was added to the sample solution, which was then boiled for 5 min. After boiling, the solution was cooled to room temperature and diluted with distilled water to a final volume of 10.00 mL. Determination of digestive enzyme activity

The experimental procedure was adapted from Ren et al. (2022) with modifications. For the Standard Bile Acid Curve, 2.00 mL of each concentration was placed in a stoppered test tube, followed by the addition of 6.00 mL of 60% H2SO4. The tube was incubated at 70 °C for 20 min. After incubation, the tube was removed from the ice bath and allowed to stand for 5 min, after which the absorbance was measured at 387 nm. The bile acid content was plotted on the x-axis, while the absorbance was plotted on the y-axis. Subsequently, standard curves for thiouracil-conjugated and glycine-conjugated taurine bile acids were generated.

Subsequently, 3.00 mL of the sample solution was withdrawn from a 10.00 mL batchvial. To each sample, 3.00 mL of a 10.00 mg/mL pepsin solution and 1.00 mL of a 0.01 mol/L HCl solution were added, followed by incubation for 1 h at 37 °C on a shaker. The pH was then adjusted to 6.3 using a 0.10 mol/L sodium hydroxide solution, and 4.00 mL of a 10.00 mg/mL trypsin solution was added. The samples were further incubated on a shaker at 37 °C for an additional hour. Next, either 4.00 mL of a 0.40 mmol/L sodium glycylcholate solution or 4.00 mL of a 0.5 mmol/L sodium taurocholate solution was added to each sample, followed by incubation on an orbital shaker at 4000 rpm for 1 h. The supernatant was then removed, and the absorbance was measured at 387 nm. The residual sodium glycylcholate and sodium taurocholate were quantified using a standard curve, and the binding capacity was calculated as the difference between the total amount added and the residual amount.

ACE inhibition capacity

The inhibitory capacity of TRW and TSW on angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) was assessed using N-(3-(2-furyl)acrylamide)-L-phenylalanine glycine-glutamic acid (FAPGG) (ACE substrate). This method was modified based on the approach described by Ren et al. (2022). The reaction components were added to the sample as outlined in Table S3. Specifically, 40.00 μL of the sample and 10.00 μL of an 80.00 mmol/L HEPES buffer (pH 8.3) were added to a 96-well plate, followed by the addition of 10.00 μL of a 0.10 U/mL ACE solution mix. Subsequently, 50.00 μL of the FAPGG solution was introduced and incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. The absorbance was measured at 340 nm. Data analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student's t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. SPSS software (version 23.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for these analyses.

For multivariate analysis of VOCs and electronic nose data, principal component analysis (PCA) and OPLS-DA were performed using SIMCA software (version 14.10, Umetrics, Umeå, Sweden). Prior to PCA and OPLS-DA, the data matrix was mean-centered and scaled to unit variance (UV scaling) to give each variable equal weight regardless of its absolute magnitude. The OPLS-DA model was validated by 200 permutation tests to assess overfitting; a model was considered valid if the R2 and Q2 values of the permuted models were lower than those of the original model and the Q2 intercept was negative. The quality of the OPLS-DA model was evaluated by the goodness-of-fit parameter (R2Y, with values close to 1 indicating high explanatory power) and the predictive ability parameter (Q2, with values >0.5 indicating good predictability). Variable importance in projection (VIP) scores was calculated to identify VOCs that contributed significantly to group discrimination (VIP > 1).

For the ROAV analysis, a compound with ROAV >1 was considered a key aroma-active contributor. The correlation heatmap between microorganisms, enzyme activities, sensory attributes, and VOCs was generated using ChiPlot online platform (https://www.chiplot.online), with Pearson correlation coefficients calculated and visualized.

Results and discussion

Sensory properties evaluation and electronic nose analysis

Prior to the dynamic analysis of flavor changes, the fermentation parameters of TRW were optimized using single-factor experiments and an orthogonal array design (L₉(34)). The effects of green tea powder addition (1.0–3.0% w/w), JQ addition (0.1–0.9% w/w), fermentation time (24–72 h), and fermentation temperature (20–40 °C) on sensory score were systematically evaluated. The results (detailed in Supplementary Tables S4–S8) showed that the optimal combination was: green tea powder 2.5%, JQ 0.4%, fermentation time 56 h, and fermentation temperature 32 °C. Under these conditions, the sensory score of TRW reached 93/100, and the product exhibited a harmonious balance of tea and rice wine aromas, a clear tea-green color, moderate sweetness and acidity, and a pleasant mouthfeel. All subsequent analyses of TRW were performed using this optimized fermentation protocol.

As illustrated in Fig. 1A, the color and liquid exudation of TRW underwent significant alterations throughout the fermentation process. The color of the fermented rice grains transitioned from purple with greenish-yellow hues to purple with yellowish-brown hues. This chromatic shift could be attributed to the dissolution of certain pigment compounds, such as catechins, coupled with the aggregation and precipitation of sample components (Zhang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025). Concurrently, the increase in liquid exudation was a consequence of sustained ethanol production driven by ongoing yeast metabolic activity as fermentation progressed. In addition to visual observation, objective color measurements were performed using a smartphone-based photographic method combined with a standard color checker and ImageJ analysis. As shown in Fig. S1, the CIELAB parameters revealed a progressive darkening (L* value decreased from 52.6 ± 1.3 to 39.2 ± 1.5), an initial increase followed by a slight decrease in redness (a* value peaked at 13.9 ± 0.6 at 40 h and then slightly dropped to 13.2 ± 0.5), and a continuous increase in yellowness (b* value increased from 12.1 ± 0.7 to 23.5 ± 0.4). Sensory evaluation analysis was conducted at distinct fermentation stages of TRW (0, 12, 24, 40, and 56 h). The trained panel assessed the samples based on color, fragrance, sweetness, astringency, alcohol aroma, and viscosity. Quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA) results revealed significant changes in the sensory profiles of TRW during fermentation (Fig. 1B). During the initial phase (0 h to 12 h), lower scores for sweetness, viscosity, and fragrance were observed, potentially due to incomplete fermentation where Aspergillus had not yet initiated significant saccharification. The intensity of alcohol aroma peaked at the mid-fermentation stage (24 h), likely resulting from increased ethanol conversion associated with heightened Aspergillus activity (Wang et al., 2023). In the later fermentation stages (40 h to 56 h), scores for both alcohol aroma and astringency diminished compared to earlier time points. This reduction might stem from the antimicrobial effects of catechins, which can compromise cell membrane integrity or interfere with cellular metabolic processes, thereby suppressing the ethanol-producing activity of Aspergillus (Chen et al., 2019; Ikigai et al., 1993). In summary, prolonged fermentation time enhanced the attributes of TRW, including color, fragrance, sweetness, and viscosity, while simultaneously reducing the alcohol aroma and astringency. The optimal sensory score was achieved at 56 h. These findings indicated that incorporating appropriate amounts of tea residue powder during the traditional sweet rice wine fermentation process could effectively mitigate both the characteristic astringency of tea and the typical alcohol aroma of sweet rice wine. This approach held significant potential for developing novel alcoholic beverages with unique sensory characteristics.

E-nose functions by mimicking the human olfactory system to identify odor molecules (Wu et al., 2022). In this study, an E-nose was employed to evaluate aroma changes during TRW fermentation as a rapid detection and analysis tool. Radar plots and PCA profiles were constructed using data from the instrument's 10 metal-oxide-semiconductor sensors (Table S2). As depicted in Fig. 1C, the 0 h sample (initial fermentation stage) exhibited low response values for the W5S and W1W sensors, which were particularly sensitive to nitrogen oxides and organic sulfides, respectively. However, the response values for these sensors increased as fermentation progressed, with the 56 h TRW sample showing the highest responses in both W1W and W5S. PCA, a dimensionality reduction technique, was utilized in this experiment to differentiate the overall aroma profiles of TRW samples across distinct fermentation stages (Song et al., 2025). Fig. 1D demonstrated that the PCA model effectively discriminated samples from the five fermentation stages. PC1 and PC2 accounted for 99.73% and 0.25% of the total variance, respectively, yielding a cumulative explained variance of 99.98%. This indicated that the model captured the overall aroma differences among the five fermentation stage samples. The odor profiles of TRW evolved over time. During fermentation, the aromas of the 0 h and 12 h samples were similar, as were those of the 40 h and 56 h samples. While distinct odor differences between samples from different fermentation stages were evident, the specific volatile compounds responsible for distinguishing these stages could not be definitively identified based solely on E-nose data. Therefore, further analysis of volatile compounds in the samples was conducted to elucidate the underlying causes of the observed aroma changes during fermentation.

Changes in VOCs during fermentation

As illustrated in Fig. 2 and Table S9, the volatile profiles of TRW exhibited systematic changes across three distinct fermentation phases. During the initial fermentation stage (0−12h), hydrocarbons predominated, constituting 82.43% to 65.64% of the total VOCs. Ethylbenzene and P-xylene emerged as key aromatic markers. Both compounds have been reported as typical volatile products generated during thermal processing of starch-based materials (Żymankowska-Kumon et al., 2017), and their occurrence in TRW may be related to the thermal degradation of starch and associated components during steaming (Fig. 2A and B). Previous studies indicated that hydrocarbons detected in alcoholic beverages typically contributed minimally to aromatic profiles. This observation might explain why the 0 h sample, despite its higher VOC content, registered the lowest aroma scores in sensory evaluation. At the mid-fermentation stage (24 h), alcohol concentrations increased significantly to 654.55 ± 22.7 μg/g (31.59%) (p < 0.05), substantially exceeding initial levels. This shift was characterized by signature metabolites such as 3-methyl-1-butanol and linalool, consistent with findings reported by Yang et al. (2024). During the later fermentation phase (40–56 h), ester concentrations rose steadily to become the dominant aroma compounds, increasing from 0.23% to 34.29%. This phenomenon resulted from the esterification of acids with alcohols generated mid-fermentation, leading to increased formation of larger ester molecules (Tian et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Concurrently, phenolic compounds increased significantly, reaching 14.22% of total VOCs (p < 0.05). This elevation was attributed to the oxidation of flavonoids present in various phenolic substances derived from tea powder, synthesized during fermentation (Liu et al., 2021). As shown in Fig. 2C, compared with the baseline (0 h), 46 VOCs decreased, and 39 VOCs (mainly hydrocarbons) were eliminated after 56 h of fermentation. In contrast, 45 new VOCs emerged during this period, with esters accounting for a large proportion. Analysis of VOCs with ROAV >1 in TRW during fermentation

Previous research has demonstrated that not all VOCs contribute equally to the formation of aroma characteristics. The ROAV is a widely utilized metric for assessing the impact of specific aroma compounds; substances with a ROAV greater than 1 are considered active and significantly influential to the overall aroma (Cui et al., 2024). VOCs with an ROAV exceeding 1 were further screened to enhance the understanding of dynamic changes in aroma compounds that provided unique aromas during TRW fermentation. By prioritizing aroma compounds with ROAV greater than 1, the dynamic evolution of VOCs during TRW fermentation was systematically analyzed, resulting in the identification of 37 key odor substances (ROAV >1) that influenced sensory transitions. These included 6 esters, 7 alcohols, 13 aldehydes, 5 ketones, 3 phenols, 1 acid, and 2 alkenes (Fig. 2D). Together, these compounds coordinated the gradual refinement of TRW aroma characteristics, transitioning from unprocessed grain aromas to complex aromas with high sensory scores and consumer preferences (Table S10). In the initial stage (0–12 h), hexanal and (E)-2-hexenal were predominant, resulting in a characteristic fatty aroma, consistent with the amylase-mediated decomposition of rice starch that produced this aroma. At the mid-fermentation stage (24 h), alcohols such as 3-methyl-1-butanol (ROAV: 0.00 ± 0.00 to 351.63 ± 10.05) exhibited a pronounced upward trend, contributing a distinct whiskey aroma to TRW. This indicated activation of the PDC and ADH pathways associated with ethanol production in Saccharomyces, consistent with the elevated alcoholic smell recorded in sensory evaluation. By the later fermentation stage (40–56 h), TRW's aroma profile was characterized by increased abundance of esters, aldehydes, and alkenes. Enhanced concentrations of ethyl palmitate and ethyl octanoate imparted wax and fruity aromas, respectively. This increase was attributed to non-enzymatic esterification, as established in prior research (Qiu et al., 2024). Among aldehydes, phenylacetaldehyde emerged, delivering a unique honey aroma to TRW. This was likely mediated by enzymatic reactions involving extracellular enzymes (including PPO) acting on free amino acids (e.g., phenylalanine) during tea powder fermentation (Chen et al., 2020) (Fig. S2A). And phenylacetaldehyde has also been identified as a common key volatile marker in various fermented teas, including dark tea fermented with different Bacillus species (Yang et al., 2025), further supporting its microbial/enzymatic origin in TRW. Notably, the concentration of 4-hydroxy-3-methoxystyrene, responsible for a characteristic clove note, increased significantly in the 56 h sample (p < 0.05). The concentration of this compound in TRW (13.39 ± 0.52 μg/g, i.e., 13,390 μg/L) is substantially higher than its reported odor thresholds in water (∼300 μg/L) (Dong, 2014) and Chinese rice wine (77 μg/L detection threshold) (Jing et al., 2012), reaching levels 44 to 174 times above the threshold values. This explains why it imparts a distinct and intense clove-like aroma to TRW. This rise might be attributed to the greater availability of free amino acids (e.g., tyrosine) during tea fermentation, mediated by extracellular enzymes such as β-glucosidase. These precursors were subsequently converted to 4-hydroxy-3-methoxystyrene via a series of enzymatic steps (Chen et al., 2020; Kang et al., 2015) (Fig. S2B). To sum up, the aroma profile of TRW underwent a significant transformation following tea powder co-fermentation, transitioning from the aroma of raw glutinous rice to a complex profile featuring distinctive honey and clove aromas associated with high sensory scores. This unique aroma signature, a hallmark of TRW fermented for 56 h, reflected the dynamic evolution of diverse VOCs. It was further determined that key aroma compounds were generated through microbial and enzymatic mediation. The integration of microbial ecology with sensory science thus provided a deeper analytical framework for understanding VOC generation and sensory impact in fermented foods.

OPLS-DA of flavor metabolites in TRW fermentation

To better elucidate the trend of VOCs during TRW fermentation and to limit overfitting while reducing grouping-independent system noise, we further analyzed the differences in flavor metabolites among the various sample groups using the OPLS-DA method (Qiu et al., 2024). This shift could be attributed to the initial increase in secondary metabolites produced by yeast fermentation (Wang et al., 2023). This trend further confirmed that the TRW sample fermented for 56 h would possess distinctive honey and clove aroma characteristics.

Correlation analysis between microorganisms and enzyme activities of TRW and VOCs during fermentation

Microorganisms play pivotal biocatalytic roles in alcoholic beverage fermentation. Previous research has established the significant functional contributions of Saccharomyces and Aspergillus to key processes, including fermentation, saccharification, and aroma generation in alcoholic beverages (Liu et al., 2022). Furthermore, these microbes mediate the production of a diverse array of extracellular enzymes. The sustained enzymatic reactions they catalyze not only facilitate the development of complex sensory attributes but also directly participate in the formation of aroma-active compounds (Hu et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2020). This study specifically investigated the temporal dynamics of microbial viability (focusing on Saccharomyces and Aspergillus) and the activities of key enzymes, including amylase, PPO, β-glucosidase, pectinase, PDC, and ADH, across five fermentation time points. As shown in Fig. 3A, both Saccharomyces and Aspergillus genera entered their logarithmic growth phases within the first 24 h of fermentation, resulting in increased biomass. Saccharomyces emerged as the dominant genus driving alcoholic fermentation. Subsequently, microbial colony counts stabilized during later stages, likely attributable to the gradual depletion of macromolecular nutrients in TRW, rendering the substrate insufficient to sustain continued microbial growth and proliferation (Wu et al., 2022). Fig. 3B and C demonstrated that the activities of amylase, PPO, pectinase, and β-glucosidase all increased with fermentation time, reaching peak levels at 24 h (31.75 ± 1.27 U/g, 37.22 ± 1.17 U/g, 23899.31 ± 2061.27 μg/h·g, and 12,333.33 ± 611.01 μg/h·g, respectively). Between 24 h and 56 h of fermentation, enzyme activities initially declined and subsequently stabilized. Amylase and β-glucosidase represented the primary enzymes secreted by Aspergillus in TRW, consistent with previous findings that Aspergillus and related genera (e.g., Rhizopus) are key producers of starch-hydrolyzing and cell-wall-degrading enzymes during rice wine fermentation (Shen et al., 2024). Consequently, the initial rise in their activities corresponded with the peak growth phase of Aspergillus. However, the subsequent decline in amylase and β-glucosidase activities could be attributed to increased osmotic pressure within the fermenting matrix resulting from ethanol accumulation (Wang et al., 2023a; Wang et al., 2023b). This highlighted the interplay between enzyme activity and metabolite production in shaping the final sensory profile. PPO activity, crucial for color and flavor development via phenolic oxidation, increased during the initial fermentation phase. However, the sustained decline in PPO activity later in fermentation may be partly attributed to thermal denaturation at the elevated fermentation temperature. This observation aligned with findings by Kumar et al. (2016) on enzyme activity during rice wine fermentation, indicating thermal denaturation as a contributing factor to the late-stage activity reduction. Pectinase activity, essential for pectin degradation and juice yield, increased sharply during the initial stage coinciding with the peak growth of Saccharomyces. However, pectinase activity was susceptible to inhibition by catechins and their oxidation products, leading to relative stabilization in the later fermentation stages. As shown in Fig. 3D, PDC and ADH activities exhibited a continuous increase throughout the fermentation period. This study further elucidated systematic associations among Saccharomyces, Aspergillus, six extracellular enzymes, six sensory attributes, and five types of aroma-active compounds (ROAV >1) through correlation network analysis (Fig. 3E). The results demonstrated that the vast majority of microorganisms and enzyme activities (including Saccharomyces, Aspergillus, β-glucosidase, pectinase, amylase, PPO, PDC, and ADH) exhibited significant positive correlations with favorable sensory attributes (color, fragrance, sweetness, viscosity) (r > 0.7, p < 0.05). Conversely, amylase and PPO activities showed significant negative correlations with undesirable sensory attributes (astringency, alcohol aroma) (r < −0.7, p < 0.05). Specifically, β-glucosidase activity displayed a strong positive correlation with the biomass of Saccharomyces and Aspergillus, simultaneously driving the substantial accumulation of alcohol flavors (such as linalool oxide, linalool, and geraniol) and ester flavor compounds. Additionally, ADH activity was highly positively correlated with alcohol content. PPO activity was positively correlated with the color attribute but negatively correlated with the intensity of astringency. These correlations suggested that β-glucosidase, synergistically secreted by Saccharomyces and Aspergillus, acted as a key catalyst of aroma formation, releasing volatile aglycones (including free alcohols and terpenes) via the hydrolysis of glycosidic bonds, and thereby providing alcohol precursors for subsequent esterification reactions that ultimately constructed the aroma framework of TRW (Luo et al., 2024; Muradova et al., 2023; Tian et al., 2024). ADH imparted an alcoholic aroma characteristic to TRW by efficiently converting acetaldehyde to ethanol. The dual regulatory role of PPO was evident in its ability to catalyze the oxidation and condensation of phenolic compounds, leading to the formation of tea pigments such as theaflavins and thearubigins, which enhanced color (Abudureheman et al., 2022). Concurrently, PPO consumed astringency precursors like catechins (Hu et al., 2024), thereby significantly improving the taste profile. Additionally, amylase facilitated the degradation of starch into soluble sugars, which not only neutralized astringency but also reduced viscosity, aligning with the mechanisms that enhanced juice clarity (Zhao et al., 2020). This interconnected network elucidates a cascade pathway of “microbial proliferation → enzyme activity expression → substrate conversion → flavor generation → sensory response”, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for the targeted regulation of tea quality. Based on this framework, potential interventions could include the specific activation of β-glucosidase to enhance aroma, as well as the optimization of PPO conditions to achieve a balance between color and astringency. The influence of microbial communities on VOC formation is well documented in tea fermentation. Xiao et al. (2022) showed that solid-state fermentation of dark tea with Eurotium cristatum dramatically altered VOCs, with mid-fermentation stage having the greatest impact; hydrolytic enzymes secreted by the fungus catalyzed reactions that generated key flavor metabolites. This supports our observation that Aspergillus and its enzymes shape TRW's VOC profile, especially during 24–40 h. Yang et al. (2025) identified phenylacetaldehyde as a common key marker in Bacillus-fermented dark teas, consistent with our finding that phenylacetaldehyde is a major honey aroma contributor in TRW. Xiao et al. (2024) reported that different E. cristatum strains increased alcohols and aldehydes in Fu brick tea, affecting microbial diversity. These findings align with our correlation analysis showing that Saccharomyces and Aspergillus synergistically secrete β-glucosidase and other enzymes to release terpene alcohols and provide ester precursors, thereby building TRW's aroma framework. Collectively, these studies confirm that fungal/bacterial communities drive VOC formation via secreted enzymes and that markers like phenylacetaldehyde and linalool are reliable indicators of microbial/enzymatic activity in tea-based fermentations.

In addition, it also studied the improvement of traditional product functions through the addition of tea powder during co-fermentation.

Notably, TSW exhibited higher scores for sweetness and alcohol aroma. The lower sweetness and alcohol intensity in TRW, may appeal to health-conscious consumers seeking reduced sugar and alcohol intake. These sensory differences were closely linked to the distinct chemical compositions of the two products, prompting further investigation into the non-volatile components that influence both flavor and functionality.

The addition of tea powder not only resulted in distinct VOCs between TSW and TRW but also significantly influenced the non-volatile functional components. Further analysis focused on the components within TRW that influenced aroma, taste, and functionality, specifically free amino acids, polyphenols, flavonoids, and catechins. Tea is known to be rich in free amino acids, with some varieties containing over 20 types. The content and composition of these amino acids directly impact the aroma and taste of the tea, while also possessing both edible and medicinal properties. These factors play a crucial role in determining the quality and nutritional value of tea (Cui et al., 2024). The free amino acid content in TRW was measured (Fig. 4B) and found to be significantly higher than that in TSW (p < 0.05). This finding suggested that the free amino acids derived from the tea powder effectively enhanced the nutritional value of TRW, indicating that TRW provided greater nutritional benefits compared to TSW.

Polyphenols in tea are a class of phenolic compounds recognized for their bio-oxidative properties. These tea polyphenols play a crucial role in antioxidation, anti-tumor activity, antibacterial effects, anti-obesity mechanisms, as well as in the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. Measurements of the mass concentration of polyphenols in TSW and TRW yielded calculated contents of 70.21 ± 0.08 μg/g and 199.0 ± 0.06 μg/g (Fig. 4B), respectively, indicating that the polyphenol content in TRW was significantly higher than that in TSW (p < 0.05).

Among the catechins, epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) is the most abundant in green tea and possesses significant antioxidant properties (Wu et al., 2022). The mass concentrations of EGCG in TSW and TRW were found to be 0.00 ± 0.00 μg/g and 10.8 ± 0.08 μg/g, respectively. These results suggested that TRW contained potent constituents such as EGCG and rutin, which were not present in TSW, because of the addition of tea powder.

Total sugar and total alcohol contents are regarded as critical parameters for assessing the quality of rice wine during fermentation (Suo et al., 2024). Consequently, we analyzed the total sugar and alcohol contents in TRW to determine the influence of tea powder on these metrics. As illustrated in Fig. 4C, the total sugar content in TSW was measured at 28.8 ± 0.5 g, whereas TRW exhibited a total sugar content of 23.4 ± 0.3 g/100 g. TRW antioxidant assessment

Oxygen free radicals (including O2-·, ·OH, etc.) are produced during metabolism and can be rapidly neutralized by antioxidant enzymes or antioxidants in the body under physiologically healthy conditions, thereby maintaining a balance that prevents cellular damage. However, with aging, the activity of antioxidant enzymes declines. When oxygen free radicals undergo peroxidation, they compromise the structure and function of bio membranes, leading to cellular senescence and death (Chang et al., 2018). Numerous studies have confirmed that polyphenols and catechins found in tea effectively scavenge free radicals, delay the aging process, and inhibit cancer cell proliferation.

In this study, we assessed the antioxidant properties of TRW by determining its scavenging capacity against various free radicals, including DPPH, ABTS+, superoxide anion (O2-·), and hydroxyl radical (·OH). The results were compared with those of TSW, vitamin C (Vc), and tea soup. In the O2-· scavenging assay, for the sample volume of 1.00 mL, Vc exhibited the highest scavenging rate at 95.42 ± 0.04%. In comparison, the scavenging rate of the TRW was 80.90 ± 0.01%, which surpassed that of the tea soup at 70.35 ± 0.05% and the TSW at 63.16 ± 0.03%. These findings indicated that the TRW demonstrated superior antioxidant properties in terms of superoxide anion scavenging capacity, approaching the efficacy of Vc (see Fig. 5A). In the -OH scavenging experiments, as illustrated in Fig. 5B, for the sample volume of 1.00 mL, the scavenging rate of TRW was measured at 79.43 ± 0.01%, which surpassed that of TSW (64.91%) and was comparable to that of tea soup (70.46 ± 0.05%). Although TRW did not fully match the hydroxyl radical scavenging efficacy of Vc (98.87 ± 0.05%), its antioxidant capacity remained significantly superior to that of TSW (p < 0.05). This finding suggested that certain components of TRW, particularly polyphenols and catechins, exerted a pronounced effect on ·OH scavenging. As illustrated in Fig. 5C, the DPPH radical scavenging assay demonstrated that TRW exhibited a superior antioxidant capacity compared to the other samples, achieving a scavenging rate of 97.10 ± 0.04%. This rate was significantly higher than that of TSW (p < 0.05), which recorded a scavenging rate of 87.23 ± 0.02%, representing an improvement of 9.87%. These findings suggested that the antioxidant components present in TRW, such as polyphenols and catechins, were effective in scavenging DPPH free radicals (Hu et al., 2020). As illustrated in Fig. 5D, the terms of ABTS+ free radical scavenging capacity, TRW exhibited a scavenging rate of 78.69 ± 0.02%, which was significantly higher than that of TSW, recorded at 43.52 ± 0.05% (p < 0.01). Furthermore, its antioxidant activity surpassed that of tea soup, which demonstrated a scavenging rate of 65.69 ± 0.01%. These results indicated that the antioxidants present in TRW possessed a robust capacity for scavenging ABTS+ radicals. Combining the results of the four antioxidant capacity measurements, TRW demonstrated superior antioxidant effects compared to TSW in scavenging free radicals. In certain instances, TRW's antioxidant properties approached or even matched those of Vc. The preliminary screening results from our study suggest that TRW may mimic the inhibitory activity of captopril on ACE. As illustrated in Fig. 5E, both captopril and TRW demonstrated strong ACE inhibition, with rates of 94.28 ± 1.67% and 90.20 ± 3.87%, respectively. In contrast, TSW exhibited an inhibition rate of only 40.55 ± 6.01%, which was significantly lower than that of TRW (p < 0.01). It should be noted that these experiments were performed at a single concentration (0.10 mL/mL (v/v)), and the positive control (captopril) was compared at the same mass concentration (1.00 mg/mL) rather than on an equimolar basis, because TRW is a complex mixture whose exact molar concentration of active components cannot be determined. Therefore, these results are preliminary and suggest a potential hypotensive effect of TRW; further studies with multiple concentration gradients, dose-response modeling (e.g., IC₅₀ determination), and in vivo validation are required.

In conclusion, tea phenols exhibit various pharmacological effects, including antioxidation, anti-mutagenesis, anti-inflammation, and anti-cancer properties, as well as the ability to chelate digestive enzymes. Additionally, rutin has been found to reduce the risk of diabetes (Xing et al., 2019). Several studies have demonstrated that tea can lower triglyceride levels and blood pressure, with flavonoids in tea shown to be effective in reducing hypertension. As illustrated in Fig. 6B, the contents of polyphenols and rutin in TRW were significantly higher than those in TSW (p < 0.05), suggesting that the increased levels of polyphenols and rutin in TRW might contribute to its potential in regulating blood sugar and blood pressure. This inference is supported by previous findings that high-concentration tea polyphenols exhibit moderate α-amylase inhibitory activity (He et al., 2007), and that EGCG and EC effectively inhibit α-glucosidase in vitro, thereby modulating postprandial blood glucose levels. Moreover, classic studies have shown that galloylated catechins (ECG, EGCG) and theaflavins possess notable ACE inhibitory activity, with a binding mode possibly similar to that of captopril (Hara et al., 1987; Actis-Goretta et al., 2006; Kurita et al., 2016). Pear juice kombucha studies have also demonstrated that phenolic content increases proportionally with fruit juice addition, accompanied by enhanced α-glucosidase inhibition (Kuraj & Kolniak-Ostek, 2026). Furthermore, studies indicated that hyperlipidemia was associated with weight gain and elevated serum hormone levels, leading to abnormalities in lipid metabolism. However, these metabolic irregularities could be significantly improved by tea polyphenols and catechins (Wen et al., 2023). Consistently, fermentation with Eurotium cristatum has been reported to increase total phenolic content by up to 40% and significantly enhance antioxidant activity (Fan et al., 2025; Yi et al., 2025). TRW contained EGCG, a component absent in TSW, which may have contributed to its superior ability to lower blood lipid levels, although the exact causal relationship requires further validation.

Conclusions

This study successfully developed a novel rice wine (TRW) through the co-fermentation of glutinous rice with tea powder. Detailed analysis of the fermentation process revealed significant alterations in flavor and functional properties. Dynamic VOC analysis during fermentation highlighted a transition in aroma profiles from simple to complex notes, including clove and honey aromas, with key compounds identified as responsible for these transformations. Furthermore, the incorporation of tea powder enhanced the antioxidant capacity of TRW while reducing total sugar content (from 28.8 ± 0.5 to 23.4 ± 0.3 g/100 g) and alcohol content (from 0.49 ± 0.02% to 0.33 ± 0.01% vol) compared to TSW. In vitro tests confirmed that TRW exhibited superior antioxidant capacity (DPPH scavenging rate 97.10 ± 0.04%, ABTS+ scavenging rate 78.69 ± 0.02%), inhibited elevations in blood pressure and glucose levels, and reduced blood lipid levels. The VOCs and functional components in TRW, such as catechins and polyphenols, significantly contributed to its enhanced nutritional and therapeutic potential. Correlation analysis confirmed that Saccharomyces and Aspergillus synergistically secreted key enzymes (β-glucosidase, amylase, PPO), which were significantly correlated (r > 0.7) with the formation of desirable sensory attributes.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The functional activities reported are based exclusively on in vitro assays, and therefore require in vivo validation using animal models or clinical trials. Furthermore, microbial analysis was restricted to the enumeration of Saccharomyces and Aspergillus, which limits our understanding of the dynamic microbial community and its intricate relationship with flavor development. Future studies integrating metagenomics, metabolomics, and in vivo experiments are warranted to comprehensively elucidate the health benefits and flavor formation mechanisms of TRW. Despite these limitations, TRW represents a promising low-alcohol, low-sugar, antioxidant-rich fermented beverage with a distinctive flavor profile. Further investigations into process optimization, storage stability, and consumer acceptance will pave the way for its commercial application.
