Circadian Blood Pressure at Hospital Discharge: A Physiological Window Into the Vulnerable Phase of Heart Failure
Authors: Dae Young Cheon
Published: 2026

Hospital discharge after acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF) marks a beginning, not an end. The following 60 to 90 days—the vulnerable phase—carry a burden of death and readmission out of proportion to any other period in the disease course.1) Tools that identify risk at this precise moment are scarce. In this issue of the Journal, Boob and colleagues2) test one such tool: 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) performed before discharge in 300 patients with ADHF and related to all-cause mortality and rehospitalization at three months. Their premise is worth stating plainly. Discharge ABPM is unlikely to be simply another blood pressure measurement. It may instead offer a physiological snapshot of residual autonomic vulnerability at the transition from acute illness to chronic heart failure.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

The novelty of this study lies less in what was measured than in when it was measured. Most ABPM data in heart failure come from chronic, stable outpatients; here, monitoring is anchored to the acute-to-chronic transition. A smaller single-center study had already linked lower ambulatory pressures and blunted dipping to reduced survival in advanced heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF); the present work extends that signal to a larger, phenotypically broader cohort assessed specifically at discharge.3) Reverse and extreme dipping were independently associated with adverse outcomes, whereas higher average real variability appeared to reflect disease severity rather than providing independent prognostic information, losing significance after adjustment. The distinction matters, and the authors are right to draw it.

BLOOD PRESSURE AND ITS RHYTHM IN HEART FAILURE

Blood pressure behaves unusually in heart failure. Unlike most cardiovascular diseases, lower rather than higher blood pressure predicts worse outcomes in heart failure—a reverse epidemiology seen across heart failure registries, including the Korean Acute Heart Failure registry, in which mortality rose as on-treatment pressure fell below a nadir of roughly 132/74 mmHg.4) Ambulatory central blood pressure has separately been associated with left ventricular reverse remodeling in patients with HFrEF.5) ADHERE identified low admission systolic pressure as one of the strongest predictors of in-hospital death.6) The present finding that low daytime systolic pressure predicted mortality sits squarely within this literature. Circadian pattern adds a further layer. Loss of nocturnal dipping and its extremes index the autonomic dysregulation—driven in part by sympathetic overactivation and impaired baroreflex sensitivity—together with the neurohormonal activation and residual congestion that define advanced disease7); reverse and extreme dipping have consistently been associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes.8) Read this way, an abnormal dipping profile at discharge is less a blood pressure abnormality than a readout of a nervous system that has not recovered.

THE MORNING-SURGE INVERSION

The most provocative observation concerns the morning blood pressure surge. In hypertension, an exaggerated surge predicts stroke.9) In HFrEF, a high surge predicts worsening heart failure.10) Here the relationship inverts: a blunted surge, not an exaggerated one, marked higher mortality. A more plausible interpretation is that a blunted morning surge reflects impaired autonomic reserve rather than a protective effect of a higher surge. This finding is consistent with the reverse epidemiology frequently observed in advanced heart failure. The association also weakened after adjustment, reinforcing severity rather than independence. This finding should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

READING THE STATISTICS WITH CAUTION

Several features constrain interpretation. The first is arithmetic. Statistical significance should not be mistaken for statistical stability. Sixteen deaths occurred over three months. A multivariable Cox model built on so few events can support one, perhaps two, covariates with confidence; this one carries more. The adjusted hazard ratios for reverse and extreme dipping are correspondingly large—7.5 and 9.3—with confidence intervals reaching 46, and the extreme-dipper group numbered six patients. These estimates should be interpreted cautiously, because sparse-event bias almost certainly inflated the observed effect sizes. A C-statistic of 0.83, reported without internal validation or optimism correction, is likely optimistic. Confounding compounds the problem. Abnormal dipping and variability travel with low ejection fraction, low pressure, and age, and severe left ventricular dysfunction proved too unstable to retain in the final model. Whether circadian abnormalities are causal, actionable, or simply the shadow of advanced disease cannot be settled here.

WHAT SHOULD THE CLINICIAN DO—AND WHAT COMES NEXT

For now, little. The signal is strong enough to pursue but too fragile to guide management, and the study is single-center, short in follow-up, and reliant on a single measurement that cannot separate a fixed trait from the turbulence of the transition period. The therapeutic corollary is equally unsettled. Targeting nocturnal or morning blood pressure through bedtime dosing remains an attractive but unproven concept,11) and the neutral TIME trial has tempered earlier enthusiasm for chronotherapy.12) Importantly, however, neither study specifically addressed patients recovering from acute heart failure. The path forward is familiar but non-negotiable: multicenter cohorts with adequate events, serial ABPM to capture autonomic recovery rather than a single snapshot, longer follow-up, and integration with natriuretic peptides and imaging. The key question is therefore no longer whether circadian blood pressure abnormalities identify high-risk patients—they do—but whether acting on them changes outcomes. The challenge for the next generation of studies is therefore not to confirm that circadian blood pressure abnormalities exist, but to determine whether modifying them changes the natural history of heart failure.
