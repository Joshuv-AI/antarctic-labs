The diagnostic yield of endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle biopsy in autoimmune pancreatitis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Huiling Yu, Jingwei Kou, Qijin He, Dongxuan Zhang, Yalan Chen, Jie Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimAutoimmune pancreatitis (AIP) is a rare chronic form of pancreatitis mediated by autoimmune mechanisms. Evidence regarding the diagnostic performance of endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle biopsy (EUS-FNB) in AIP remains limited. This study aimed to estimate the pooled diagnostic accuracy of EUS-FNB in patients with AIP.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, CNKI, and Wanfang Database were systematically searched to identify fully published studies evaluating EUS-FNB for tissue sampling in AIP. Pooled estimates were generated using a random-effects model. The assessed outcomes included diagnostic accuracy, sample adequacy, contribution to AIP diagnosis, and adverse events.ResultsSixteen studies, including one randomized controlled trial and four prospective studies, comprising 732 patients, were included. The overall diagnostic accuracy was 79% (73%–83%). Diagnostic accuracy was higher for the Franseen needle (83%, 75%–90%) and Fork-tip needle (90%, 80%–99%) compared with the reverse/forward‑bevel (51%, 31%–71%), with a statistically significant difference (P < 0.001). The 22G needle also demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy (85%, 79%–91%) compared with 19G/20G needles (70%, 42%–98%) (P < 0.001). The pooled sample adequacy rate was 97% (95%–98%). EUS-FNB contributed to the diagnosis in 69% (50%–88%) of cases where diagnosis could not be established using imaging, serology, or involvement of other organs. The overall adverse event rate was 4% (1%–8%).ConclusionsEUS-FNB demonstrates excellent histological tissue acquisition and moderate diagnostic accuracy in AIP, with a low rate of adverse events. The 22G Franseen or Fork-tip needles may be a reasonable option, though direct comparative evidence is lacking.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-05164-y.

Introduction

Autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP) is a rare form of chronic pancreatitis mediated by autoimmune mechanisms. Although pancreatitis related to immune dysfunction has long been recognized, the term “AIP” was first introduced by Yoshida et al. in 1995 [1]. Based on histopathological characteristics, AIP is classified into two subtypes. Type 1 AIP, also referred to as lymphoplasmacytic sclerosing pancreatitis (LPSP), is associated with IgG4-related disease, often involves other organs, and is typically associated with elevated serum IgG4 levels. Type 2 AIP, also known as idiopathic duct-centric pancreatitis (IDCP), is usually characterized by normal serum IgG4 levels, is commonly associated with inflammatory bowel disease, and may present with acute-like pancreatitis symptoms. In Asian countries such as China and Japan, Type 1 AIP accounts for the predominant subtype. An epidemiological study conducted in Japan reported a prevalence of 10.1 per 100,000 and an annual incidence of approximately 3.1 per 100,000 [2]. However, epidemiological data on AIP in China remain limited. A systematic review [3] that analyzed 26 studies on AIP in China published between January 2006 and June 2014 included 706 patients with AIP. Type 2 AIP accounted for approximately 4.7% of cases, while 29.7% of patients with AIP underwent surgical intervention due to a suspected diagnosis of malignancy.

The clinical features of AIP are non-specific, and diagnosis relies on an integrated assessment of imaging findings, serological markers, involvement of other organs, pancreatic histopathology, and response to corticosteroid therapy. When imaging results are characteristic, and serum IgG4 levels are significantly elevated, AIP can usually be diagnosed with confidence, and histological confirmation is not required. When non-invasive imaging and serological findings are inconclusive, particularly in cases of focal or segmental pancreatic enlargement, histopathological evaluation becomes important. According to the International Consensus Diagnostic Criteria (ICDC) [4], a definitive pathological diagnosis of AIP requires core biopsy tissue or surgical specimens. Although endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) can obtain pancreatic specimens for cytological assessment, it has limited capability to retrieve core tissue samples, and therefore is not recommended by the ICDC. In contrast, endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine needle biopsy (EUS-FNB) is designed to obtain larger tissue cores while better preserving histological structure, making it more appropriate for the evaluation of AIP. Two previous meta-analyses [5, 6] have also reported that EUS-FNB achieves a higher diagnostic yield for AIP compared with EUS-FNA. However, the analysis by Yoon et al. [5] included a substantial number of case reports in the EUS-FNB group, while the study by Facciorusso et al. [6] incorporated EUS-FNA cases within the EUS-FNB cohort. In addition, some of the included studies were available only as conference abstracts. Furthermore, both meta-analyses [5, 6] included studies using the Trucut needle, which is now obsolete and no longer in clinical use. In recent years, EUS-FNB has been increasingly adopted for the diagnosis of AIP, and with the wider availability of newer-generation biopsy needles, a comprehensive evaluation of the diagnostic performance and safety of EUS-FNB in patients with AIP is required.

Materials and methods

Literature search

A comprehensive literature search was performed in three English databases (PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library) and two Chinese databases (China National Knowledge Infrastructure and Wanfang Database). The search strategy in English used the terms: (“autoimmune pancreatitis”) AND (“endosonography” OR “endoscopic ultrasound” OR “fine needle biopsy” OR “EUS – FNB”), while the Chinese search strategy was: (自身免疫性胰腺炎) and ((超声内镜) or (穿刺)). There was no restriction on publication date, and the final search was conducted on November 30, 2025. Fully published studies on the use of EUS-FNB for tissue sampling in AIP were collected. In addition, the reference lists of recently published AIP reviews and commentaries were screened to minimize the risk of missing relevant studies. This systematic review and meta-analysis were performed and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines. The completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in the supplementary materials.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion Criteria: (1) The ICDC established in 2011 [4] was used for the diagnosis of AIP; (2) studies included at least 10 patients who underwent EUS-FNB; and (3) each study reported at least one measure of diagnostic performance of EUS-FNB, such as accuracy or sensitivity.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Duplicate studies; (2) reviews, commentaries, guidelines, meta-analyses, and conference abstracts; (3) case reports or case series involving fewer than 10 patients; (4) studies that used the obsolete Trucut biopsy needle; and (5) publications for which only abstracts were available and the full text could not be retrieved.

Literature screening and data extraction

Two gastrointestinal endoscopists independently conducted the literature search. First, studies such as reviews, case reports, and conference abstracts were excluded based on their titles. Subsequently, the abstracts were reviewed to identify potentially eligible studies that met the inclusion criteria. Finally, full-text articles were reviewed to confirm the final set of eligible studies. After study selection, the two endoscopists independently extracted the relevant data and performed cross-checking. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third investigator until a consensus was reached. The extracted data comprised: (1) basic information, including the first author, publication year, study design, patient characteristics, type of biopsy needle used, and number of needle punctures; (2) outcome measures, including diagnostic accuracy, sample adequacy, contribution to AIP diagnosis, and adverse events.

Outcome assessment criteria

(1) Diagnostic accuracy was calculated as (true positives + true negatives obtained by EUS-FNB)/total number of patients. True positives were defined as histological findings meeting ICDC Level 1 or Level 2 criteria. (2) For type 1 AIP, Level 1 histological evidence required the presence of at least three of the following four features: (i) diffuse lymphoplasmacytic infiltration; (ii) storiform fibrosis; (iii) obliterative phlebitis; and (iv) a large number of IgG4 + plasma cells, defined as > 10 per high-power field (HPF). Level 2 was defined as the presence of any two of these features. (3) For type 2 AIP, Level 1 was defined by granulocytic epithelial lesions (GELs) in the interlobular ducts, with < 10 IgG4 + plasma cells/HPF. Level 2 was defined by granulocytic and lymphoplasmacytic acinar infiltration, also with < 10 IgG4 + plasma cells/HPF; (4) Sample adequacy referred to whether the tissue obtained by EUS-FNB was sufficient for histological evaluation; (5)Contribution to AIP diagnosis: The proportion of cases where the histological results obtained via EUS-FNB confirm AIP diagnosis when imaging, serological, and other organ involvement indicators fail to establish the diagnosis; (6) Adverse Events: Defined as various complications directly related to the EUS-FNB procedure that occur during or after the intervention, including abdominal pain, hemorrhage, acute pancreatitis, perforation, infection, and fever. The incidence rates and severity grades of each adverse event were statistically analyzed.

Quality assessment of the literature

Since this study evaluated the diagnostic performance of a single intervention, EUS-FNB, in AIP, the collected data were considered as diagnostic accuracy studies. The Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) tool [7], which is the recommended standard for assessing risk of bias in diagnostic test meta-analyses, was used to evaluate both risk of bias and applicability concerns in the included studies. The QUADAS-2 tool assesses four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. Each domain was rated as having a low, high, or unclear risk of bias according to predefined criteria. Two investigators independently performed the quality assessment, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. Overall, no study was judged to have a high risk of bias in any domain. Most prospective multicenter studies were rated as having a low risk of bias across all four domains. Some retrospective cohort studies were classified as having an unclear risk of bias, mainly due to insufficient reporting of consecutive patient enrollment and blinding of index test interpretation. No significant concerns regarding applicability were identified across the included studies, indicating generally acceptable methodological quality.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi version 2.6.44. Pooled estimates were calculated using a random-effects model based on the DerSimonian-Laird method, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) reported. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using Cochrane’s Q test and the I2 statistic. An I2 > 50% indicated substantial heterogeneity, an I2 < 20% indicated low heterogeneity, and an I2 between 20% and 50% indicated moderate heterogeneity. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s test. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Literature screening

Based on the predefined search strategy, 386 English and 97 Chinese articles were initially identified. After the screening process, 16 studies (15 in English and 1 in Chinese) [8–23], involving 732 patients in total, were included in the final meta-analysis. Characteristics and quality evaluation of the included studies

Of the 16 included studies, one [9] was a randomized controlled trial, four [10, 16, 17, 19] were prospective studies, and the remaining studies [8, 11–15, 18, 20–23] were retrospective in design. Two studies [8, 12] assessed EUS-FNA/FNB collectively, and only the data related to EUS-FNB were extracted and included in this study. Two studies [15, 16] investigated the diagnostic performance of EUS-FNB across multiple diseases, and only the data related to AIP were extracted for this analysis. Most of the patients were diagnosed with type 1 AIP, while only five studies [8, 10, 15, 18, 19] included a small proportion of type 2 AIP cases. Meta - analysis results

Diagnostic accuracy

All 16 included studies [8–23] reported the diagnostic performance of EUS–FNB. The pooled diagnostic accuracy for AIP was 79% (95% CI: 73%–83%, I² = 89.9%, indicating substantial between-study heterogeneity assessed by Cochrane’s Q test), as illustrated in Fig. 3. Heterogeneity was preliminarily correlated with needle type, needle gauge and study design.

Fig. 3Pooled analysis for the diagnostic accuracy of EUS-FNB in patients with AIP

Pooled analysis for the diagnostic accuracy of EUS-FNB in patients with AIP

A subgroup analysis based on the type of puncture needle demonstrated that the diagnostic accuracy of the Franseen needle (83%, 95% CI: 75%–90%, I2 = 81.8%) and the Fork-tip needle (90%, 95% CI: 80% − 99%, I2 = 57.8%) was significantly higher than that of the reverse/forward-bevel needle (51%, 95% CI: 31%–71%, I2 = 34.2%) (all P < 0.001). No significant difference was observed between the Franseen and Fork-tip needles (P > 0.05), as illustrated in Fig. 4. A subgroup analysis according to the diameter of the puncture needle showed that the 22G puncture needle achieved a higher diagnostic accuracy (85%, 95% CI: 79%–91%, I2 = 73.3%) compared with the 19G/20G puncture needles (70%, 95% CI: 42%–98%, I2 = 92.0%), with a statistically significant difference (P < 0.001), as illustrated in Fig. 5. Notably, only one included randomized controlled trial directly performed head-to-head comparisons of different biopsy needle types and gauges. Most studies only reported the diagnostic performance of a single needle type; therefore, direct head-to-head comparative forest plots could not be constructed. Accordingly, the above results reflect separate pooled diagnostic accuracy estimates for each needle subgroup rather than direct comparative analyses.

The diagnostic yield of EUS-FNB for achieving Level 1 histological criteria was 49% (95% CI: 38%–61%, I2 = 75.9%), while that for Level 2 criteria was 30% (95% CI: 23%–37%, I2 = 0). For specific histopathological features of type 1 AIP, the detection rates of EUS-FNB were as follows: diffuse lymphoplasmacytic infiltration 86% (95% CI: 81%–90%, I2 = 93.3%), storiform fibrosis 57% (95% CI: 46%–69%, I2 = 78.4%), obliterative phlebitis 32% (95% CI: 22%–42%, I2 = 37.8%), and > 10 IgG4 + plasma cells per HPF 64% (95% CI: 54%–74%, I2 = 88.5%).

Sample adequacy

A total of 11 studies [9, 10, 13–20, 22] reported the performance of EUS-FNB in obtaining histological tissue samples. The pooled sample adequacy rate was 97% (95% CI: 95%–98%, I² = 87.6%), as illustrated in Fig. 6.

Contribution to AIP diagnosis

Seven studies [9–11, 17, 19, 21, 22] reported the use of EUS-FNB in establishing a definitive diagnosis of AIP in cases where other diagnostic modalities were inconclusive. The pooled diagnostic contribution rate of EUS-FNB for AIP was 69% (95% CI: 50%–88%, I² = 82.3%), as illustrated in Fig. 7.

Adverse events

Nine studies [9, 10, 12, 14, 17, 19–22] reported 17 adverse events, including 8 cases of mild pancreatitis, 3 cases of mild abdominal pain, 2 cases of minor bleeding, 2 cases of pancreatic fistula, and 2 cases of difficulty during instrument manipulation. The pooled incidence of adverse events was 4% (95% CI: 1%–8%, I² = 0%), as illustrated in Fig. 8. Note: Difficulty during instrument manipulation was classified as device-related adverse events in the original included study, and we extracted data strictly in accordance with the definitions of original literatures.

Publication bias

Assessment of publication bias using funnel plots and Egger’s test for diagnostic accuracy, sample adequacy, contribution to AIP diagnosis, and adverse events indicated the presence of statistically significant publication bias across all evaluated outcomes (P < 0.001; P < 0.001; P = 0.019; P = 0.038), as illustrated in Fig. 9. Some studies distributed outside the funnel plot were identified as outlier literatures, which were important contributors to overall heterogeneity and publication bias.

Discussion

AIP may present with clinical features such as obstructive jaundice, abdominal pain, and weight loss. Imaging findings on CT or MRI often include diffuse or focal enlargement of the pancreas and narrowing of the pancreatic or biliary ducts, which can closely mimic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). The consequences of misdiagnosis are considerable. If AIP is incorrectly identified as PDAC, patients may undergo unnecessary and invasive pancreatic resection. Conversely, if PDAC is misdiagnosed as AIP, inappropriate corticosteroid therapy may be initiated, leading to delays in surgical or chemotherapeutic intervention and ultimately worsening patient outcomes. Dickerson et al. [24] reported that 57.8% of patients with AIP underwent surgical resection because malignancy was suspected. Similarly, Karamya et al. [25] found that 1.6% of patients who underwent pancreatic resection for presumed pancreatic cancer were ultimately diagnosed with AIP postoperatively, with AIP accounting for 26% of unnecessary surgeries attributed to benign conditions. To exclude malignancy and provide supportive evidence for AIP, histopathological evaluation of pancreatic tissue is often necessary. This study demonstrates that EUS-FNB has an overall diagnostic accuracy of 79%, indicating that it offers good diagnostic performance for AIP.

Over the past two decades, the FNB biopsy needle has continuously evolved to improve ease of use, enable acquisition of larger tissue samples, and better preserve tissue structure. The 19G Trucut puncture needle was among the earliest devices used for endoscopic ultrasound-guided biopsy; however, due to its limited flexibility and technical difficulty in handling, it has been gradually phased out. Subsequently, the reverse bevel needle was introduced into clinical use. It features a side-opening hollow reverse bevel design and obtains tissue through the lateral cutting edge during needle withdrawal. However, a meta-analysis demonstrated that there was no significant difference between the reverse bevel needle and the conventional FNA needle in terms of diagnostic accuracy and sample adequacy for solid pancreatic lesions [26]. Since 2016, a new generation of biopsy needles, including the Franseen, Fork-tip needle, forward-bevel needle, and Menghini needle, has been introduced into clinical practice. The forward-bevel needle retains a side opening but incorporates a forward-angled design. The Franseen features three symmetrically arranged cutting edges of equal length at its tip, while the Fork-tip needle has two symmetrical but slightly unequal sharp prongs. In contrast, the Menghini needle is characterized by an inwardly beveled cutting surface at its tip. A network meta-analysis evaluating the Franseen, Fork-tip needle, Menghini needle, forward-bevel needle, reverse-bevel needle, and conventional FNA needle for solid pancreatic lesions found that the Franseen and Fork-tip needles performed better than the other needle types in terms of diagnostic accuracy and sample adequacy. In contrast, the Menghini needle showed superiority over the reverse-bevel needle only with respect to sample adequacy [27]. The pooled analysis in this study indicates that the diagnostic accuracy of the Franseen and Fork-tip needle (83% and 90%, respectively) is significantly higher than that of the bevel-type needles (51%). No significant difference was observed between the Franseen and Fork-tip needles, which is consistent with the findings from previous studies. This study included only one report using the Menghini needle [14], which showed a diagnostic accuracy of 64%, falling between that of the Franseen/Fork-tip needles and the bevel-type needles.

The Franseen and Fork-tip needles are available in three sizes, namely 19G, 22G, and 25G, while the bevel-type needle also includes a 20G model. A meta-analysis by Facciorusso et al. [26] reported that there was no significant difference in diagnostic accuracy among needles of different gauges. Oh et al. [28] and Tomoda et al. [29] both reported that there was no significant difference in diagnostic accuracy between 22G and 25G Franseens for pancreatic lesions. In addition, Iwata et al. [20] found that the 19G Franseen had a higher rate of obtaining Level 1 histological findings compared with the 22G Franseen; however, it did not show any advantage in the diagnosis of AIP. Ishikawa et al. [17] reported that in the diagnosis of AIP, the 19G Franseen demonstrated a diagnostic accuracy comparable to that of the 22G Franseen in the historical control group. In general, 22G needles are the most commonly used needle tip size in pancreatic biopsy procedures. Currently, no direct comparative studies have evaluated the diagnostic performance of different-sized needle tips. The pooled analysis in this study indicates that the 22G puncture needle achieves a significantly higher diagnostic accuracy (85%) than the 19G/20G puncture needles (70%). This difference may be explained by the fact that the 22G group mainly consisted of Franseen and Fork-tip needles, whereas more than half of the 19G/20G cases involved bevel-type needles, which may have influenced and confounded the overall results. Although larger-sized needles (19G/20G) may theoretically yield more tissue, their increased rigidity can make manipulation more challenging, particularly during angled approaches such as transduodenal puncture. In contrast, smaller-sized needles (25G) tend to retrieve limited tissue samples and may necessitate more punctures. Overall, the observed superior performance of 22G needles was largely driven by findings from a single high-quality randomized controlled trial rather than pooled evidence from multiple head-to-head comparative meta-analyses. The 22G puncture needle shows a relatively balanced performance in clinical practice; however, this conclusion requires further validation through large-scale, multicenter comparative studies.

Adequate tissue acquisition is essential for reliable histological diagnosis. The findings of this study demonstrate that EUS-FNB achieves an overall sample adequacy of 97%, indicating excellent tissue procurement capability in patients with AIP. Chalhoub et al. [30] suggested that two punctures using a Franseen or Fork-tip needle, or three punctures with other FNB needles, are generally adequate to obtain sufficient tissue for diagnosing solid pancreatic lesions. This recommendation is broadly consistent with the number of needle punctures reported in the studies included in this analysis. Despite the high sample adequacy, the rates of specific histopathological diagnoses remain relatively low, with a Level 1 diagnosis rate of 49%, storiform fibrosis detected in 57%, and obliterative venous inflammation identified in only 32% of cases. Several factors may account for this discrepancy: (1) storiform fibrosis and obliterative venous inflammation are often focally distributed in AIP, making their detection in biopsy samples largely dependent on chance; (2) small tissue samples often lack sufficient vascular structures, which limits reliable identification of obliterative venous inflammation and may complicate differentiation from venous scarring; (3) current diagnostic criteria are primarily based on surgical specimens, and the interpretation of storiform fibrosis and obliterative venous inflammation in biopsy material can be ambiguous, suggesting a need for revised criteria tailored to biopsy samples.

When imaging findings, serological tests, and evidence of involvement of other organs are insufficient to establish a diagnosis of AIP, EUS-FNB becomes particularly important. The pooled analysis in this study shows that EUS-FNB enabled a definitive diagnosis in 69% of such cases. This suggests that EUS-FNB plays a significant role in the diagnostic workup of AIP, and it should be actively considered when the diagnosis of AIP remains unclear.

The pooled analysis in this study indicates that the overall adverse event rate associated with EUS-FNB is 4%, with no severe complications reported, and all events resolving with conservative management. These findings are consistent with large-scale studies by Thomsen et al. [15] and Pan et al. [31], further supporting that EUS-FNB is a safe procedural technique.All classifications of adverse events in this meta-analysis followed the original definitions of included studies, and no secondary modification was made to ensure data objectivity.

High heterogeneity and potential publication bias are important limitations of this study. The observed heterogeneity may be attributed to the use of different needle types and sizes across studies, as well as variations in the definitions of “sample adequacy.” The presence of publication bias is likely related to the predominance of retrospective study designs in the included literature, which may be associated with selective outcome reporting.

Most critically, all stratified comparisons of diagnostic accuracy by needle type and gauge rely on indirect cross-study single-arm pooled analyses rather than synchronized within-cohort head-to-head trials, which constitutes a major methodological limitation of this work. Multiple unadjustable confounding variables across independent studies may distort observed subgroup accuracy differences, including divergent patient baseline characteristics, variable endoscopist expertise, inconsistent biopsy protocols, heterogeneous pathological adjudication standards for AIP histology, and uneven proportions of Type 1 versus Type 2 AIP patients across cohorts. Without unified internal control groups within primary studies, we cannot isolate the independent effect of needle design on diagnostic performance. Readers must avoid drawing definitive causal inferences or rigid clinical preference guidelines solely based on these between-study subgroup comparisons; prospective multicenter randomized controlled trials with direct head-to-head needle comparisons are urgently needed to validate these preliminary observational trends.Although needle specification and type are potential sources of high heterogeneity, the available data for each subgroup were extremely limited and unevenly distributed among the 16 included studies. To avoid statistical false positives and result bias caused by small-sample subgroup analysis, we did not perform further quantitative pooled subgroup analysis. We only qualitatively analyzed the potential influencing factors of heterogeneity.After accumulating more research data in the future, targeted stratified analysis and subgroup verification will be carried out to further explore the sources of heterogeneity.

This study has several additional limitations. First, most of the included studies focused on type 1 AIP, with very few cases of type 2 AIP. In some reports, such as Zhou et al. [18], type 2 AIP was included, but no separate diagnostic performance analysis was performed; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to type 2 AIP. Second, the inclusion of relatively few non-AIP diseases limits the assessment of true negative results for EUS-FNB, and in some studies, the reported “accuracy” may reflect sensitivity rather than the true diagnostic accuracy. Third, the long-time span of the included studies introduces variability, as endoscopic techniques have evolved, and interobserver differences among pathologists may also have influenced the results. Collectively, these issues highlight the need for prospective, multicenter randomized controlled trials, along with standardized procedural protocols and needle selection strategies, to more accurately evaluate the diagnostic value of EUS-FNB in AIP.In addition, there is a lack of sufficient head-to-head comparative studies in the included literature evaluating different biopsy needle types, which limits direct quantitative comparison between different needle types and gauges. Future research should prioritize well-designed paired or controlled comparative trials to address this evidence gap.

In conclusion, the available evidence suggests that EUS-FNB can provide adequate histological samples with satisfactory diagnostic accuracy in patients with AIP, while maintaining a low rate of adverse events. It is particularly valuable in cases where imaging findings, serological markers, and evidence of involvement of other organs are insufficient for diagnosis. In such situations, 22G Franseen or Fork-tip needles may be a reasonable option, though direct comparative evidence is lacking.
