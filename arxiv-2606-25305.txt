1.
Introduction

The middle-third Cantor set
C
consists of all real numbers in
[0,1]
whose ternary expansions have only digits
0
and
2
. That is,

C:=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{j}}{3^{j}}:a_{j}\in\{0,2\},j\geq 1\right\}.

In 1984, Mahler
[
17
]
proposed to study the problem of how well elements in
C
can be
approximated by rational numbers in
C
(intrinsic approximation) and by rational numbers outside
C
(extrinsic approximation). Mahler’s problem pioneered the study of Diophantine approximation on fractals, leading to a substantial amount of research; see, for instance,
[
15
,
5
,
19
,
22
,
3
,
8
,
9
,
4
]
and references therein. We do not attempt to give an exhaustive review. Instead, we highlight the results most directly relevant to our work.

In
[
15
]
Levesley, Salp and Velani studied approximation on
C
by triadic rationals; that is, rationals whose denominators are powers of
3
. More precisely, let
\mu
be the Cantor-Lebesgue measure on
C
. Equivalently,
\mu
is exactly the
\gamma
-dimensional Hausdorff measure restricted to
C
, where

\gamma:=\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}

is the Hausdorff dimension of
C
. Given
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
, set

W_{3}(\psi):=\left\{x\in[0,1]:\|3^{n}x\|<\psi(n)\text{ for infinitely many }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

Here and throughout,
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean distance to the nearest integer. Levesley, Salp and Velani
[
15
]
proved a Khintchine-type result for
\mu(W_{3}(\psi))
stating that
\mu(W_{3}(\psi))
is either null or full according to the convergence or divergence of a certain series. This result was generalized by Li, Li and Wu
[
16
]
to the case of multiplicatively dependent bases.

For approximation on
C
with dyadic rationals, the situation is substantially different and much more difficult. Given
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
let

(1.1)

W_{2}(\psi):=\left\{x\in[0,1]:\|2^{n}x\|<\psi(n)\text{ for infinitely many }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

The following conjecture was attributed to Velani in
[
2
]
.

Conjecture 1.1
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
be monotonic. Then

\mu(W_{2}(\psi))=\begin{cases}0\quad\text{ if }\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)<\infty,\\
1\quad\text{ if }\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)=\infty.\end{cases}

For approximation functions of the form
\psi_{\tau}(n)=n^{-\tau}
(
\tau>0
), write
W_{2}(\psi_{\tau})
as
W_{2}(\tau)
for short. In this case, Velani’s conjecture reduces to the following.

Conjecture 1.1
′
.

We have

\mu(W_{2}(\tau))=\begin{cases}0\quad\text{ if }\tau>1,\\
1\quad\text{ if }0<\tau\leq 1.\end{cases}

Although there have been several deep results, Conjectures
1.1
and
1.1′
are widely open. Allen, Chow, and Yu
[
2
]
made the first progress in both the convergence and divergence parts of Conjecture
1.1
. They proved that
\mu(W_{2}(\psi))=0
if

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(2^{-\log n/\log\log n\cdot\log\log\log n}\psi(n)^{\log 2/\log 3}+\psi(n)\right)<\infty,

and
\mu(W_{2}(\psi))=1
if
\psi(n)=2^{-\log\log n/\log\log\log n}
; see
[
2
, Theorems 1.5, 1.9]
.
A key ingredient in
[
2
]
is to relate Velani’s conjecture to the principle that the base-
2
and base-
3
expansions of a number are not both structured. However, the current known results supporting this principle are much weaker than conjectured, which prevents the method of
[
2
]
from fully resolving Velani’s conjecture; see
[
2
, Section 5]
for some conditional results. This indicates the difficulty of the conjecture.

The above principle falls within the broader context of Furstenberg’s
\times 2,\times 3
principle from dynamical systems. Broadly speaking, Furstenberg’s
\times 2,\times 3
principle describes the independence of the maps
T_{2}:x\mapsto 2x\bmod 1
and
T_{3}:x\mapsto 3x\bmod 1
on the unit circle at various levels. This principle has given rise to many open problems and deep results, see, for instance
[
11
,
13
,
12
,
20
,
23
,
7
]
. We refer the reader to
[
3
, Section 1]
for a more detailed discussion of related results.

The connection between Furstenberg’s
\times 2,\times 3
principle and Velani’s conjecture was also emphasized by Baker
[
3
]
.
To study this principle in the context of shrinking targets problems,
Baker
[
3
]
proposed a conjecture which generalizes Velani’s conjecture to the broader framework of shrinking targets, without assuming monotonicity of
\psi
. More precisely,
given a sequence
\mathbf{x}=(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
[0,1]
and a function
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
, set

W_{2}(\psi,\mathbf{x}):=\left\{x\in[0,1]:\|2^{n}x-x_{n}\|<\psi(n)\text{ for infinitely many }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

Note that when
x_{n}=0
for all
n
,
W_{2}(\psi,\mathbf{x})
coincides with
W_{2}(\psi)
defined in (
1.1
).
Baker conjectured that

(1.2)

\mu(W_{2}(\psi,\mathbf{x}))=\begin{cases}0\quad\text{ if }\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)<\infty,\\
1\quad\text{ if }\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)=\infty.\end{cases}

Regarding this conjecture, Baker
[
3
]
confirmed the divergent part for approximation functions
\psi_{\tau}(n)=n^{-\tau}
with
0<\tau\leq 0.01
. Indeed, he proved a more general counting result stating that for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in C
,

(1.3)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\#\left\{1\leq n\leq N:\|2^{n}x-x_{n}\|<n^{-0.01}\right\}}{2\sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-0.01}}=1.

In particular, this implies that

(1.4)

\mu(W_{2}(\tau))=1,\qquad\forall 0<\tau\leq 0.01,

which represents a step towards the full measure part of Velani’s Conjecture
1.1′
.

By applying a key lemma from
[
3
]
(cf. Lemma
2.1
) and some other techniques,
Allen, Baker, Chow, and Yu
[
1
]
made further progress on the null part of Conjecture
1.1′
. They considered the more general inhomogeneous setting. More precisely, let
y\in\mathbb{R}
be fixed.
For
\tau>0
, denote
\psi_{\tau}(n)=n^{-\tau}
and

(1.5)

W_{2}(\psi_{\tau},y):=\left\{x\in[0,1]:\|2^{n}x-y\|<n^{-\tau}\text{ for infinitely many }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

Allen, Baker, Chow, and Yu
[
1
]
proved that

(1.6)

\mu(W_{2}(\psi_{\tau},y))=0,\qquad\forall\tau>\frac{1}{\gamma}-\frac{0.078(1-\gamma)}{\gamma(2-\gamma)}.

In this paper, we further explore Velani’s Conjecture
1.1′
. Our first main result is the following theorem, which improves the known results on both the null and full measure parts on Conjecture
1.1′
.

Theorem 1.2
.

We have

\mu(W_{2}(\tau))=\begin{cases}0\quad\text{ if }\tau>\frac{1}{\gamma}-\frac{1-\gamma}{3-\gamma},\\
1\quad\text{ if }0<\tau<\frac{\gamma}{12}.\end{cases}

Remark 1.3
.

Note that

\frac{\gamma}{12}\approx 0.052,\quad\frac{1}{\gamma}-\frac{1-\gamma}{3-\gamma}\approx 1.429\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{\gamma}-\frac{0.078(1-\gamma)}{\gamma(2-\gamma)}\approx 1.552.

Hence Theorem
1.2
improves both Baker’s result (
1.4
) and the result (
1.6
) due to Allen, Baker, Chow and Yu.

For the full measure part of Theorem
1.2
, similar to
[
3
]
, we indeed prove the following more general counting result, which generalizes Baker’s result (
1.3
).

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of real numbers in
[0,1]
. Let
0<\tau<\frac{\gamma}{12}
. Then for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in C
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\#\left\{1\leq n\leq N:\|2^{n}x-x_{n}\|<n^{-\tau}\right\}}{2\sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-\tau}}=1.

Let
W_{2}(\psi_{\tau},y)
be defined as in (
1.5
). The null part of Theorem
1.2
follows from the following.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\tau>\frac{1}{\gamma}-\frac{1-\gamma}{3-\gamma}
. Then

\mu(W_{2}(\psi_{\tau},y))=0
.

Remark 1.6
.

The proof of Theorem
1.5
still works if
y
is replaced by a sequence
(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
[0,1]
.
That is, we have
\mu(W_{2}(\psi_{\tau},\mathbf{x}))=0
for any
\tau>\frac{1}{\gamma}-\frac{1-\gamma}{3-\gamma}
. Therefore, Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
also make progress towards Baker’ conjecture (
1.2
).

Finally, we remark that our approach to prove Theorem
1.4
can also be extended to give a counting result with respect to general self-similar measures for approximation on some missing-digit sets. More exactly, given an integer
b\geq 3
and a set
D\subseteq\{0,1,\ldots,b-1\}
with cardinality
2\leq\#D<b
, the
missing-digit set
with base
b
and digit set
D
, denoted by
K_{b,D}
, is defined as

K_{b,D}:=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\varepsilon_{j}}{b^{j}}:\varepsilon_{j}\in D,j\geq 1\right\}.

Clearly,
K_{3,\{0,2\}}
is the middle-third Cantor set
C
. Let
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{\#D})
be a probability vector with strictly positive entries, i.e.,
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{\#D}\in(0,1)
and
\sum_{i=1}^{\#D}p_{i}=1
. Let
\mu_{\mathbf{p}}
be the self-similar measure supported on
K_{b,D}
associated with probability vector
p
. That is,
\mu_{\textbf{p}}
is the unique Borel probability measure supported on
K_{b,D}
satisfying

\mu_{\mathbf{p}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\#D}p_{j}\mu_{\mathbf{p}}\circ\phi_{j}^{-1},

where

\phi_{j}(x)=\frac{x+k_{j}}{b},\quad D=\{k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{\#D}\}\ {\rm and}\ k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots<k_{\#D}.

We have the following extension of Theorem
1.4
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of real numbers in
[0,1]
. Suppose
b\geq 3
is a prime number and
t\geq 2
is an integer satisfying that
b\nmid t
. Let

\kappa:=\frac{-\log\left(\min\limits_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq\#D}(1-2p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}})\right)}{12\log b}.

Then for any
\tau\in(0,\kappa)
, we have for
\mu_{\textbf{p}}
-a.e.
x\in K_{b,D}
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\#\{1\leq n\leq N:\|t^{n}x-x_{n}\|<n^{-\tau}\}}{2\sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-\tau}}=1.

Remark 1.8
.

Note that when
K_{b,D}
is the middle-third Cantor set and
\mu_{\textbf{p}}
is the Cantor-Lebesgue measure,
\kappa
in Theorem
1.7
coincides with the threshold
\frac{\gamma}{12}
in Theorem
1.4
.

At the end of this section, we briefly describe the key ideas in our proofs of the main results.
Similar to previous works
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, our method is Fourier-analytic. A main obstruction is that
\mu
has no global Fourier decay (i.e.,
|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|\not\to 0
as
|\xi|\to\infty
). To get around this, Baker
[
3
, Lemma 2.2]
proved that for a fixed non-zero integer
h
,
\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})
decays to zero polynomially fast outside a relatively small set of
n
’s; see also Lemma
2.1
. This result underpins the development in
[
3
]
and is also crucial in
[
1
]
. In this paper, our key new ingredient is establishing the following estimates

\sum_{n=1}^{N}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|^{2}\ll N^{1-\gamma},\quad\sum_{n=1}^{N}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|\ll N^{1-\frac{\gamma}{2}},\quad\sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-\sigma}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|\ll_{\sigma}N^{1-\frac{\gamma}{2}-\sigma},

which are uniform in
h\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
; see Lemmas
2.2
and
2.4
. These estimates refine Baker’s lemma and are of importance in their own right. Then our general strategy is to take these as the Fourier input in the mechanism in
[
3
]
and
[
1
]
to establish Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
. Certainly, several other techniques are also necessary and will be apparent in the course of the proofs.

Since the proof of Theorem
1.7
is analogous to that of Theorem
1.4
, we give the full details only for the latter. For Theorem
1.7
, we shall merely indicate the necessary modifications to the key lemmas.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we establish several estimates on the Fourier transform of
\mu
; the main objects are Lemmas
2.2
and
2.4
. Then in Section
3
, we apply these estimates (especially Lemma
2.4
) to derive integral estimates by following the mechanism of
[
3
]
. We then prove Theorem
1.4
in Section
4
. Section
5
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.5
. Finally, in the last section, we present necessary modifications to the key lemmas needed to prove Theorem
1.7
.

Notation.
For complex-valued functions
f
and
g
, we write
f\ll g
or
f=O(g)
if there exist a constant
M>0
such
|f|\leq M|g|
pointwise. Throughout,
\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{N}
denote respectively the set of integers and the set of positive integers.

2.
Preliminary lemmas

Given a Borel probability measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}
, the Fourier transform of
\nu
is defined as

\widehat{\nu}(\xi)=\int e^{-2\pi i\xi y}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y).

Recall that
\mu
is the Cantor-Lebesgue measure on
C
. Equivalently,
\mu
is the self-similar measure on
C
defined by

\mu=\frac{1}{2}\mu\circ S_{1}^{-1}+\frac{1}{2}\mu\circ S_{2}^{-1},\qquad\text{where }S_{1}(x)=\frac{x}{3},\,S_{2}(x)=\frac{x+2}{3}.

It is well-known that (see
[
21
, page 342]
)

\widehat{\mu}(x)=\prod_{q=1}^{\infty}\frac{1+e(-2x/3^{q})}{2},\qquad|\widehat{\mu}(x)|=\prod_{q=1}^{\infty}\left|\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{3^{q}}\right)\right|.

Here and throughout, for convenience, we write

e(x)=e^{2\pi ix}.

For more information on self-similar measures, we refer the reader to
[
10
]
.

In
[
3
]
, Baker proved Theorem
1.3
based on the following result, which is also a key ingredient in
[
1
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
3
, Lemma 2.2]

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
h\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Then there exist
C_{1},C_{2}>0
independent of
N
and
h
such that

\#\left\{0\leq n<N:\left|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})\right|>C_{1}N^{-0.078}\right\}\leq C_{2}N^{0.922}.

Lemma
2.1
establishes that, despite
\mu
lacking global Fourier decay,
\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})
decays polynomially to zero for all but a relatively small set of
n
. In this section, instead, we directly estimate the partial sums of the Fourier coefficients of
\mu
along the sequence
h2^{n}
(
n\in\mathbb{N}
) for any fixed
h\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.
The main results are the following two lemmas, which are crucial in the subsequent development of this paper. Recall that

\gamma=\frac{\log 2}{\log 3},

which is the Hausdorff dimension of
C
.

Lemma 2.2
.

There exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that, for every non-zero integer
h
, every integer
a\geq 0
, and every integer
N\geq 1
,

(2.1)

\sum_{n=a}^{a+N-1}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|^{2}\leq c^{2}N^{1-\gamma}.

Consequently,

(2.2)

\sum_{n=a}^{a+N-1}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|\leq cN^{1-\frac{\gamma}{2}}.

In Lemma
2.2
,
the case where
a=0
is of particular interest, and we state it below.

Corollary 2.3
.

For any
h\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|^{2}\ll N^{1-\gamma}\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|\ll N^{1-\frac{\gamma}{2}},

where the underlying constants are independent of
N
and
h
.

A direct consequence of Corollary
2.3
is the following result, which is particularly important in proving Theorem
1.4
; see the proofs of Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
in Section
3
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
0<\sigma<1-\frac{\gamma}{2}
. Then for any
h\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\sum_{1\leq n\leq N}n^{-\sigma}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|\ll_{\sigma}N^{1-\frac{\gamma}{2}-\sigma},

where the implicit constant is independent of
N
and
h
.

Proof.

Fix
h\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. For
X\geq 1
, let

A_{h}(X):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}:1\leq n\leq X}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|.

Then by Corollary
2.3
,
A_{h}(X)\ll X^{1-\frac{\gamma}{2}}
. Moreover, Abel’s Summation Formula (cf.
[
18
, Theorem 4.10]
) gives

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq n\leq N}n^{-\sigma}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|

\displaystyle=N^{-\sigma}A_{h}(N)+\sigma\int_{1}^{N}x^{-\sigma-1}A_{h}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\ll_{\sigma}N^{1-\frac{\gamma}{2}-\sigma}+\int_{1}^{N}x^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}-\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\ll_{\sigma}N^{1-\frac{\gamma}{2}-\sigma}.

This proves the lemma.
∎

It remains to prove Lemma
2.2
.
For this purpose, we establish several auxiliary lemmas.

We first give some notation. For
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
,
\nu_{3}(m)
denotes the
3
-adic valuation
of
m
, i.e.,
\nu_{3}(m)
is the largest integer
n\geq 0
such that
3^{n}\mid m
. For an integer
q\geq 2
and an integer
a
coprime to
q
, let
\ord_{q}(a)

be the
multiplicative order
of
a
modulo
q
. That is,
\ord_{q}(a)
is the smallest
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

a^{k}\equiv 1\bmod q.

The following number-theoretic lemma is due to Cassels
[
6
, Lemma 2]
, which we include a proof for completeness.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
b\geq 2
satisfy
b\equiv 1\bmod{3}
. Set
\ell:=\nu_{3}(b-1)
.
Then, for every integer
r\geq 0
,

(2.3)

\{b^{n}\bmod 3^{\ell+r}:0\leq n<3^{r}\}=\{(1+3^{\ell}k)\bmod 3^{\ell+r}:0\leq k<3^{r}\}.

Equivalently, the powers
b^{n}
,
0\leq n<3^{r}
, run exactly once through all residue classes modulo
3^{\ell+r}
that are congruent to
1
modulo
3^{\ell}
.

Proof.

By the Lifting the Exponent Lemma
[
18
, Theorem 1.37]
,

(2.4)

\nu_{3}(b^{n}-1)=\ell+\nu_{3}(n),\qquad n\geq 1.

Letting
n=3^{r}
in (
2.4
), we obtain

b^{3^{r}}\equiv 1\bmod{3^{\ell+r}}.

If
0<n<3^{r}
, then
\nu_{3}(n)\leq r-1
. Hence

\nu_{3}(b^{n}-1)\leq\ell+r-1,

and so
b^{n}\not\equiv 1\bmod{3^{\ell+r}}
for
0<n<3^{r}
. Therefore,

\ord_{3^{\ell+r}}(b)=3^{r}.

The
3^{r}
residues
b^{0},b^{1},\ldots,b^{3^{r}-1}
are therefore distinct modulo
3^{\ell+r}
. They all lie in the set of residue classes congruent to
1
modulo
3^{\ell}
, and that set has exactly
3^{r}
elements. Hence the two sets in (
2.3
) coincide.
∎

Next, we extend Lemma
2.5
to non-zero multiples of
b^{n}
.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
b,\ell
be as in Lemma
2.5
. Let
h\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and write
h=3^{m}u
, where
m\geq 0
and
u\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
with
\gcd(u,3)=1
. Then, for every integer
r\geq 0
,

(2.5)

\{hb^{n}\bmod{3^{m+\ell+r}}:0\leq n<3^{r}\}=\{(h+3^{m+\ell}k)\bmod{3^{m+\ell+r}}:0\leq k<3^{r}\}.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.5
, we have

b^{n}\equiv(1+3^{\ell}t_{n})\bmod{3^{\ell+r}},

where
t_{n}
(
0\leq n<3^{r}
) run through all residue classes modulo
3^{r}
.
Multiplying by
h=3^{m}u
gives

hb^{n}\equiv(h+3^{m+\ell}ut_{n})\bmod{3^{m+\ell+r}}.

Since
\gcd(u,3)=1
, the values
ut_{n}
(
0\leq n<3^{r}
) run through all residue classes modulo
3^{r}
. This proves (
2.5
).
∎

Remark 2.7
.

For
b=4
, we have
\ell=\nu_{3}(4-1)=1
. Therefore, for any
h\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
with
\gcd(h,3)=1
,

\{h4^{n}\bmod 3^{r+1}:0\leq n<3^{r}\}=\{a\bmod{3^{r+1}}:a\equiv h\bmod{3}\}.

We also need the following elementary lemma.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\gcd(u,3)=1
. Then for every integer
r\geq 0
,

\frac{1}{3^{r}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\bmod 3^{r+1}\\
y\equiv u\bmod 3\end{subarray}}\prod_{q=1}^{r+1}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi y}{3^{q}}\right)=2^{-r-2}.

Proof.

The case
r=0
is immediate, since for
u\in\{1,2\}
,

(2.6)

\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi u}{3}\right)=\frac{1}{4}=2^{-2}.

Assume now that
r\geq 1
.

By (
2.6
) we also have

\prod_{q=1}^{r+1}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi y}{3^{q}}\right)=2^{-2}\prod_{q=2}^{r+1}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi y}{3^{q}}\right).

Write

y=u+3s,\qquad 0\leq s<3^{r}.

Then it suffices to show that

(2.7)

\frac{1}{3^{r}}\sum_{s=0}^{3^{r}-1}\prod_{q=2}^{r+1}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(u+3s)}{3^{q}}\right)=2^{-r}.

Using the identity

\cos^{2}(\pi x)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}e(x)+\frac{1}{4}e(-x),

we expand

\prod_{q=2}^{r+1}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi y}{3^{q}}\right).

A typical term is of the form

c_{\bm{\varepsilon}}e\left(y\sum_{q=2}^{r+1}\frac{\varepsilon_{q}}{3^{q}}\right),\qquad\varepsilon_{q}\in\{-1,0,1\}.

After substituting
y=u+3s
, its average over
s
is equal to
c_{\bm{\varepsilon}}e\left(u\sum_{q=2}^{r+1}\frac{\varepsilon_{q}}{3^{q}}\right)
times

(2.8)

\frac{1}{3^{r}}\sum_{s=0}^{3^{r}-1}e\left(s\sum_{q=2}^{r+1}\varepsilon_{q}3^{1-q}\right).

Put

(2.9)

B=\sum_{q=2}^{r+1}\varepsilon_{q}3^{r+1-q}.

Then (
2.8
) is rewritten as

\frac{1}{3^{r}}\sum_{s=0}^{3^{r}-1}e\left(\frac{sB}{3^{r}}\right),

which equals
1
when
B\equiv 0\bmod{3^{r}}
and vanishes otherwise. But

|B|\leq 1+3+\cdots+3^{r-1}=\frac{3^{r}-1}{2}<3^{r}.

Thus
B\equiv 0\bmod{3^{r}}
implies
B=0
. This in turn forces that

(2.10)

\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon_{3}=\cdots=\varepsilon_{r+1}=0.

Indeed, assume
B=0
. Then modulo
3
in (
2.9
), we get

\varepsilon_{r+1}\equiv 0\bmod 3
. Since

\varepsilon_{r+1}\in\{-1,0,1\}
, this gives

\varepsilon_{r+1}=0
. Dividing by
3
and repeating the argument,
we obtain (
2.10
).
Therefore,

\frac{1}{3^{r}}\sum_{s=0}^{3^{r}-1}\prod_{q=2}^{r+1}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(u+3s)}{3^{q}}\right)=\frac{1}{3^{r}}\sum_{s=0}^{3^{r}-1}\prod_{q=2}^{r+1}\frac{1}{2}=2^{-r}.

This proves (
2.7
) and thus completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Now we are ready to give the proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

For convenience, define

P(x):=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|\cos\left(\frac{\pi x}{3^{j}}\right)\right|.

Then

|\widehat{\mu}(x)|=P(2x).

It is enough to prove the corresponding estimates for
P(h2^{n})
.

We first show that for every
h\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and every
r\geq 0
,

(2.11)

\sum_{n=0}^{3^{r}-1}P(h4^{n})^{2}\leq 3^{r}2^{-r-2}.

Write

h=3^{m}u,

where
m\geq 0,u\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
with
\gcd(u,3)=1
.
By the definition of
P
,

P(h4^{n})^{2}=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi 3^{m}u4^{n}}{3^{j}}\right).

The factors with
j\leq m
are equal to
1
, so after relabelling
q=j-m
we get

P(h4^{n})^{2}=\prod_{q=1}^{\infty}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi u4^{n}}{3^{q}}\right).

Since every factor is at most
1
, we have

P(h4^{n})^{2}\leq\prod_{q=1}^{r+1}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi u4^{n}}{3^{q}}\right).

Applying Lemma
2.5
to
b=4
(see Remark
2.7
), the residues

u4^{n}\bmod{3^{r+1}},\qquad 0\leq n<3^{r},

run exactly once through all residue classes
y\bmod{3^{r+1}}
with
y\equiv u\bmod 3
. Combining this with Lemma
2.5
gives

\sum_{n=0}^{3^{r}-1}P(h4^{n})^{2}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\bmod 3^{r+1}\\
y\equiv u\bmod 3\end{subarray}}\prod_{q=1}^{r+1}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi y}{3^{q}}\right)=3^{r}2^{-r-2}.

This proves (
2.11
).

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, set

S_{4}(a,N;h):=\sum_{n=a}^{a+N-1}P(h4^{n})^{2}.

Let
R\geq 0
be the integer such that

3^{R}\leq N<3^{R+1}.

Then by (
2.11
) (in which we take
h
to be
h4^{a}
), we have

S_{4}(a,N;h)\leq\sum_{n=0}^{3^{R+1}-1}P(h4^{a}\cdot 4^{n})^{2}\leq 3^{R+1}2^{-(R+1)-2}\ll\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{R}.

Since
3^{R}\leq N<3^{R+1}
,

\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{R}=(3^{R})^{1-\log 2/\log 3}\leq N^{1-\gamma}.

Thus

(2.12)

\sum_{n=a}^{a+N-1}P(h4^{n})^{2}\ll N^{1-\gamma}.

Next we deduce from (
2.12
) the desired estimate (
2.1
) by splitting
[a,a+N)\cap\mathbb{Z}
into even and odd
n
’s. Since

P(h2^{n})^{2}=\begin{cases}P(h4^{k})^{2}&\text{ if }n=2k,\\
P(2h4^{k})^{2}&\text{ if }n=2k+1,\end{cases}

applying (
2.12
) to
h
and to
2h
gives

\sum_{n=a}^{a+N-1}P(h2^{n})^{2}\ll N^{1-\gamma}.

This proves (
2.1
).

Finally, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=a}^{a+N-1}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|

\displaystyle\leq N^{1/2}\left(\sum_{n=a}^{a+N-1}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\ll N^{(1+1-\gamma)/2}=N^{1-\frac{\gamma}{2}}.

This proves (
2.2
) and thus completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

3.
Smoothing and Integral estimates

In this section, based on the estimate established in Lemma
2.4
, we follow the method of
[
3
, Section 2.2]
to derive integral estimates for smooth approximations of the counting function appearing in Theorem
1.4
.

For a
\mathbb{Z}
periodic function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\int_{0}^{1}|f(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty
, the Fourier coefficients of
f
are given by

\widehat{f}(k):=\int_{0}^{1}f(x)e^{-2\pi ikx}\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

For bounded
f
, it’s modulus of continuity is defined as

\omega_{f}(x):=\sup\{|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})|:|x_{1}-x_{2}|\leq x\},\quad x\geq 0.

Without causing confusion, we just write
\omega_{f}(x)
as
\omega(x)
to ease notation.

Similar to
[
3
]
, we will make use of the following Jackson’s inequality.

Lemma 3.1
.

[
14
, Theorem IV, Chapter 1]

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
\mathbb{Z}
periodic
C^{1}
function such that
f^{\prime}
has a modulus of continuity
\omega
. Then for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

\left|f(x)-\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\widehat{f}(k)e^{2\pi ikx}\right|\ll\frac{\log N}{N}\omega(1/N),\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R},

where the implicit constant is independent of
f
or
N
.

Throughout this section, fix

0<\tau\leq\frac{\log 2}{6\log 3}.

In the following,
we apply the technique in
[
3
]
to construct smooth approximations to the indicator functions for the sets

\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\|2^{n}x-x_{n}\|<n^{-\tau}\},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

To this end, let
(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of real numbers in
[0,1]
. Given constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
with
c_{1}<c_{2}
, we let
(f_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of
C^{2}
real-valued functions on
\mathbb{R}
satisfying the following properties:

(1)

Each
f_{n}
is
\mathbb{Z}
periodic.

(2)

0\leq f_{n}(x)\leq 1
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(3)

f_{n}(x)=1
for
x\in[x_{n}-c_{1}n^{-\tau},x_{n}+c_{1}n^{-\tau}]+\mathbb{Z}.

(4)

f_{n}(x)=0
for
x\notin[x_{n}-c_{2}n^{-\tau},x_{n}+c_{2}n^{-\tau}]+\mathbb{Z}.

(5)

\|f^{\prime}_{n}\|_{\infty}\ll n^{\tau}.

(6)

\|f^{\prime\prime}_{n}\|_{\infty}\ll n^{2\tau}.

Here
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denotes the supremum norm. We emphasise that the underlying constants appearing in (5) and (6) do depend upon
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, but this will not affect the result so we suppress it from our notation.

By the property (6) and the mean value theorem,
f^{\prime}_{n}
has a modulus of continuity
\omega
such that
\omega(x)\ll n^{2\tau}|x|
. Then it follows from Lemma
3.1
that for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(3.1)

\sum_{\ell\notin[-N,N]}c_{\ell,n}e(\ell x)=O\left(\frac{n^{2\tau}\log N}{N^{2}}\right).

Here and afterwards, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
(c_{\ell,n})_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}
denote the Fourier coefficients of
f_{n}
. Note that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.2)

|c_{0,n}|\asymp n^{-\tau},\quad|c_{\ell,n}|\ll n^{-\tau},\quad\forall\ell\in\mathbb{Z}.

Our purpose in this section is to prove the following two lemmas, which respectively strengthen Lemmas 2.3 and 2.4 in
[
3
]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(f_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of functions satisfying properties (1)-(6) for some
c_{1},c_{2}
and
(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
. Then for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(3.3)

\int\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(2^{n}x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{0,n}+O\left(N^{1-\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3}}\log N\right)

and

\int\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(2^{n}x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\asymp N^{1-\tau}.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(f_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of functions satisfying properties (1)-(6) for some
c_{1},c_{2}
and
(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
. For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\int\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(2^{n}x)-\int\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(2^{n}y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=O\left(N^{2-\frac{\log 2}{6\log 3}}\log N\right).

We prove Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
by following the strategy in
[
3
]
. The key differences is that we invoke Lemmas
2.4
rather than Baker’s Lemma ( see Lemma
2.1
) as the Fourier input. Since the require modifications are substantial, we provide detailed proofs for the sake of completeness and clarity.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

Put

\rho=\tau+\frac{\log 2}{6\log 3}.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
. By (
3.1
) and the fact that each
f_{n}
coincides with its Fourier series, we have

\int\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(2^{n}x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{0,n}+\int\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}c_{\ell,n}e(\ell 2^{n}x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu+O\left(N^{1+2\tau-2\rho}\log N\right).

Note that

1+2\tau-2\rho=1-\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3}.

So to prove (
3.3
), it suffices to show that

(3.4)

\int\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}c_{\ell,n}e(\ell 2^{n}x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu=O\left(N^{1-\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3}}\log N\right).

We have

(3.5)

\int\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}c_{\ell,n}e(\ell 2^{n}x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{\ell,n}\int e(\ell 2^{n}x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Hence by Lemma
2.4
and the fact that
|c_{\ell,n}|\ll n^{-\tau}
,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{\ell,n}\int e(\ell 2^{n}x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right|\ll\sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-\tau}|\widehat{\mu}(\ell 2^{n})|\ll N^{1-\frac{\gamma}{2}-\tau}.

Plugging this upper bound into (
3.5
) and noting that
1-\frac{\gamma}{2}-\tau+\rho=1-\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3}
, we obtain (
3.4
) and so complete the proof of the first part of the lemma. The second part follows from the first part, (
3.2
) and the fact that
\tau<\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

Let
\rho
be as in Lemma
3.2
. Multiplying out the bracket we have

(3.6)

\displaystyle\int\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(2^{n}x)-\int\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(2^{n}y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(2^{n}x)\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu-\left(\int\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(2^{n}x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right)^{2}.

To bound the first term on the right hand side of (
3.6
), we use (
3.1
) to get

\displaystyle\int\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(2^{n}x)\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu

(3.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{0,n}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}c_{\ell,n}e(\ell 2^{n}x)+O\left(N^{1-\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3}}\log N\right)\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Since

(3.8)

\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{0,n}\asymp N^{1-\tau}

and

\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}c_{\ell,n}e(\ell 2^{n}x)\right|\ll\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-\tau}\ll N^{1-\tau+\rho},

we expand the bracket in (
3
) to obtain that

\displaystyle\int\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(2^{n}x)\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{0,n}\right)^{2}}_{A}+\underbrace{2\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{0,n}\int\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}c_{\ell,n}e(\ell 2^{n}x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu}_{B}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sum_{j\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}c_{\ell,n}c_{j,m}e((\ell 2^{n}+j2^{m})x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu}_{C}

\displaystyle+O\left(N^{2-\tau-\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3}}\log N\right)+O\left(N^{2(1-\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3})}(\log N)^{2}\right)+O\left(N^{2-\tau+\rho-\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3}}\log N\right).

These final three error terms are all

O\left(N^{2-\tau+\rho-\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3}}\log N\right)=O\left(N^{2-\frac{\log 2}{6\log 3}}\log N\right).

Therefore it remains to consider terms
A
,
B
, and
C
. By Lemma
3.2
we know that

\left(\int\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(2^{n}x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right)^{2}=\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{0,n}\right)^{2}+O\left(N^{2-\tau-\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3}}\log N\right).

Hence,

\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{0,n}\right)^{2}-\left(\int\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(2^{n}x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right)^{2}=O\left(N^{2-\tau-\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3}}\log N\right)=O\left(N^{2-\frac{\log 2}{6\log 3}}\log N\right).

Therefore, it remains to show that
B
and
C
are both
O\left(N^{2-\frac{\log 2}{6\log 3}}\log N\right)
. This we do below.

From the proof of (
3.4
) in Lemma
3.2
, we see that

\left|\int\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}c_{\ell,n}e(\ell 2^{n}x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right|=O\left(N^{1-\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3}}\right).

So by (
3.8
),

\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{0,n}\int\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}c_{\ell,n}e(\ell 2^{n}x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right|=O\left(N^{2-\tau-\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3}}\right).

Therefore the term
B
is
O\left(N^{2-\frac{\log 2}{6\log 3}}\log N\right).

To bound the term
C
, we rewrite it as follows:

\displaystyle\int\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sum_{j\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}c_{\ell,n}c_{j,m}e((\ell 2^{n}+j2^{m})x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\int\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{j\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}c_{\ell,n}c_{j,n}e((\ell 2^{n}+j2^{n})x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu}_{D}

\displaystyle+2\underbrace{\int\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{m=n+1}^{N}\sum_{j\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}c_{\ell,n}c_{j,m}e((\ell 2^{n}+j2^{m})x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu}_{E}.

By (
3.2
),

\displaystyle\int\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{j\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}c_{\ell,n}c_{j,n}e((\ell 2^{n}+j2^{n})x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu=O\left(N^{1-2\tau+2\rho}\right).

Since
1-2\tau+2\rho=1+\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3}
, it follows that
D
is
O\left(N^{2-\frac{\log 2}{6\log 3}}\log N\right)
. Below we bound term
E
.

We first bound
E
from above by an expression to which we can apply Lemma
2.4
:

\displaystyle\left|\int\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{m=n+1}^{N}\sum_{j\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}c_{\ell,n}c_{j,m}e((\ell 2^{n}+j2^{m})x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\int\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{m=n+1}^{N}\sum_{j\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}c_{\ell,n}c_{j,m}e(2^{n}(\ell+j2^{m-n})x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right|

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{k=1}^{N-n}\sum_{j\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{n^{2\tau}}\left|\int e(2^{n}(\ell+j2^{k})x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right|

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{n^{2\tau}}\left|\int e(2^{n}(\ell+j2^{k})x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{j\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{n^{2\tau}}\left|\int e(2^{n}(\ell+j2^{k})x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right|.

In the third line in the above we have used (
3.2
). To proceed, we split the last term above according to
\ell+j2^{k}
equals
0
or not. Notice that if
k
is such that there exist
\ell,j\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}
for which
\ell+j2^{k}=0,
then
k\leq\lfloor\rho\log_{2}N\rfloor
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{j\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{n^{2\tau}}\left|\int e(2^{n}(\ell+j2^{k})x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\rho\log_{2}N\rfloor}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{j\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{n^{2\tau}}\left|\int e(2^{n}(\ell+j2^{k})x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right|}_{F}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\underbrace{\sum_{k=\lfloor\rho\log_{2}N\rfloor+1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{j\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{n^{2\tau}}\left|\int e(2^{n}(\ell+j2^{k})x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right|}_{G}.

To complete the proof, it suffices to show that
F
and
G
are both
O\left(N^{2-\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3}}\log N\right).

The term
F
is
O\left(N^{2-\frac{\log 2}{6\log 3}}\log N\right)
, since

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\rho\log_{2}N\rfloor}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\!\sum_{j\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\!\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{n^{2\tau}}\left|\int e(2^{n}(\ell+j2^{k})x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right|

\displaystyle=O(N^{1-2\tau+2\rho}\log N)=O\left(N^{1+\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3}}\log N\right)=O\left(N^{2-\frac{\log 2}{6\log 3}}\log N\right).

Now we focus on
G
. If
\ell+j2^{k}\neq 0
, since
2\tau\leq\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3}<1-\frac{\gamma}{2}
, we can apply Lemma
2.4
to yield that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{n^{2\tau}}\left|\int e(2^{n}(l+j2^{k})x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right|\ll N^{1-\frac{\gamma}{2}-2\tau}

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=\lfloor\rho\log_{2}N\rfloor+1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{j\in[-\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor,\lfloor N^{\rho}\rfloor]\setminus\{0\}}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{n^{2\tau}}\left|\int e(2^{n}(\ell+j2^{k})x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right|

\displaystyle\ll N^{1+\rho+\rho+1-\frac{\gamma}{2}-2\tau}=N^{2-\frac{\log 2}{6\log 3}}.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

Equipped with Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
we are now in a position to prove Theorem
1.4
. We follow the strategy in
[
3
, Section 2.3]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

For convenience, write

\tau_{0}=\frac{\log 2}{12\log 3},\qquad\theta=2-\frac{\log 2}{6\log 3}.

Recall that
\theta
is the exponent appearing in Lemma
3.3
. Fix
0<\tau<\tau_{0}
. Since
\frac{\theta}{2}<1-\tau
,
we can find a large positive integer
Q
such that

\frac{Q\theta+1}{2}<M<Q(1-\tau)

for some positive integer
M
. Let such
Q
and
M
be fixed.

Let
\epsilon>0
be arbitrary and
(f_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of
C^{2}
functions satisfying properties
(1)-(6)
for
c_{1}=1,

c_{2}=1+\epsilon
and
(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
. It follows from these properties and this choice of parameters that for any
x\in C
,

(4.1)

\#\{1\leq n\leq N:\|2^{n}x-x_{n}\|<n^{-\tau}\}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(2^{n}x).

By our choice of
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
it is also clear that

(4.2)

\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{0,n}\leq 2(1+\epsilon)\sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-\tau}.

By Lemma
3.3
and Markov’s inequality, we have the following for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\quad\mu\left(x\in C:\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N^{Q}}f_{n}(2^{n}x)-\int\sum_{n=1}^{N^{Q}}f_{n}(2^{n}y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right|\geq N^{M}\right)

\displaystyle=\mu\left(x\in C:\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N^{Q}}f_{n}(2^{n}x)-\int\sum_{n=1}^{N^{Q}}f_{n}(2^{n}y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right)^{2}\geq N^{2M}\right)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{N^{Q\theta}\log N}{N^{2M}}=\frac{\log N}{N^{2M-Q\theta}}.

Since
2M-Q\theta>1
, it follows that

\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\mu\left(x\in C:\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N^{Q}}f_{n}(2^{n}x)-\int\sum_{n=1}^{N^{Q}}f_{n}(2^{n}y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right|\geq N^{M}\right)<\infty.

Therefore by the Borel-Cantelli lemma, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in C
, we have

(4.3)

\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N^{Q}}f_{n}(2^{n}x)-\int\sum_{n=1}^{N^{Q}}f_{n}(2^{n}y)\,d\mu(y)\right|<N^{M}

for all large
N
. For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
K_{N}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(4.4)

K_{N}^{Q}\leq N<(K_{N}+1)^{Q}.

Notice that

(4.5)

\lim_{K\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{(K+1)^{Q}}n^{-\tau}}{\sum_{n=1}^{K^{Q}}n^{-\tau}}=1\quad{\rm and}\quad\sum_{n=1}^{K^{Q}}n^{-\tau}\asymp K^{(1-\tau)Q}.

Now let
x
belong to the full
\mu
measure set for which (
4.3
) holds for all sufficiently large
N
. Then we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\#\{1\leq n\leq N:\|2^{n}x-x_{n}\|<n^{-\tau}\}}{2\sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-\tau}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Counting upper bound}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(2^{n}x)}{2\sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-\tau}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{defKN}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{(K_{N}+1)^{Q}}f_{n}(2^{n}x)}{2\sum_{n=1}^{K_{N}^{Q}}n^{-\tau}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eventually}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\int\sum_{n=1}^{(K_{N}+1)^{Q}}f_{n}(2^{n}y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+(K_{N}+1)^{M}}{2\sum_{n=1}^{K_{N}^{Q}}n^{-\tau}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Lemma }\ref{Lemma1}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{(K_{N}+1)^{Q}}c_{0,n}+(K_{N}+1)^{M}+O\left(K_{N}^{(1-\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3})Q}\log K_{N}\right)}{2\sum_{n=1}^{K_{N}^{Q}}n^{-\tau}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{abequation}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{2(1+\epsilon)\sum_{n=1}^{(K_{N}+1)^{Q}}n^{-\tau}+(K_{N}+1)^{M}+O\left(K_{N}^{(1-\frac{\log 2}{3\log 3})Q}\log K_{N}\right)}{2\sum_{n=1}^{K_{N}^{Q}}n^{-\tau}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Kasymptotics}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle 1+\epsilon.

It follows that for
\mu
-a.e.
x
, we have

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\#\{1\leq n\leq N:\|2^{n}x-x_{n}\|<n^{-\tau}\}}{2\sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-\tau}}\leq 1+\epsilon.

Since
\epsilon
is arbitrary, we conclude that for
\mu
-a.e.
x
,

(4.6)

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\#\{1\leq n\leq N:\|2^{n}x-x_{n}\|<n^{-\tau}\}}{2\sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-\tau}}\leq 1.

By an analogous argument, this time taking a sequence of
C^{2}
functions
(f_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
satisfying properties
(1)-(6)
for
c_{1}=1-\epsilon
,
c_{2}=1
and
(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
, we can show that for
\mu
-a.e.
x
,

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{\#\{1\leq n\leq N:\|2^{n}x-x_{n}\|<n^{-\tau}\}}{2\sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-\tau}}\geq 1-\epsilon.

Again by the arbitraness of
\epsilon
, we have that for
\mu
-a.e.
x
,

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{\#\{1\leq n\leq N:\|2^{n}x-x_{n}\|<n^{-\tau}\}}{2\sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-\tau}}\geq 1.

This combining with (
4.6
) completes the proof.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

Recall that
\mu
is the Cantor-Lebesgue measure and
\gamma=\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}
.

We prove Theorem
1.5
by combining Lemma
2.2
with a modification of the method from
[
1
, Theorem 2]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Recall that our aim is to show that for any
\tau>\frac{1}{\gamma}-\frac{1-\gamma}{3-\gamma}
,
\mu(W_{2}(\psi_{\tau},y))=0
. Fix such a
\tau
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, put

\sigma_{n}=n^{-\tau},\qquad\delta_{n}=n^{-\varrho},

where
0<\varrho<\min(\tau,\frac{\gamma}{2})
is to be determined. Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be large such that
N^{\tau-\varrho}\geq 150
.

Write
G_{N}
for the set of integers
n\in[N,2N]
such that

\sum_{1\leq|h|\leq 2/\delta_{2N}}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|\leq 1

and let
B_{N}
be its complement in
[N,2N]\cap\mathbb{Z}
. Then we have

(5.1)

\sum_{n\in B_{N}}\sum_{1\leq|h|\leq 2/\delta_{2N}}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|\geq\sum_{n\in B_{N}}1=\#B_{N}.

On the other hand, it follows from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Lemma
2.2
that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in B_{N}}\sum_{1\leq|h|\leq 2/\delta_{2N}}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq|h|\leq 2/\delta_{2N}}\sum_{n\in B_{N}}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq|h|\leq 2/\delta_{2N}}(\#B_{N})^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{n\in B_{N}}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq|h|\leq 2/\delta_{2N}}(\#B_{N})^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{n=N}^{2N}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\ll(\#B_{N})^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{1\leq|h|\leq 2/\delta_{2N}}N^{\frac{1-\gamma}{2}}

\displaystyle\ll(\#B_{N})^{\frac{1}{2}}N^{\varrho+\frac{1-\gamma}{2}}.

This together with (
5.1
) gives that

\#B_{N}\ll N^{2\varrho+1-\gamma}.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\sigma>0
, denote

A_{n}^{y}(\sigma)=\{x\in[0,1]:\|2^{n}x-y\|<\sigma\}.

Then

W_{2}(\psi_{\tau},y)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}A_{n}^{y}(\sigma_{n}).

Hence by the Borel-Cantelli lemma, to prove
\mu(W_{2}(\psi_{\tau},y))=0
, it suffices to show that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(A_{n}^{y}(\sigma_{n}))<+\infty.

We need the following estimate from
[
2
, Section 2.1]
:

(5.2)

\mu(A_{n}^{y}(\sigma_{n}))\ll\sigma_{n}^{\gamma}\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N}).

Moreover, by
[
24
, Theorem 4.1]
, we have

(5.3)

\mu(A_{n}^{y}(\delta_{n}))\ll\delta_{n}\left(1+\sum_{1\leqslant|h|\leqslant 2/\delta_{n}}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|\right)\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N}).

Since for
n\in G_{N}
,

\sum_{1\leq|h|\leq 2/\delta_{n}}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|\leq\sum_{1\leq|h|\leq 2/\delta_{2N}}|\widehat{\mu}(h2^{n})|\leq 1,

we see from (
5.3
) that

(5.4)

\mu(A_{n}^{y}(\delta_{n}))\ll\delta_{n}=n^{-\varrho},\qquad\forall n\in G_{N}.

It was also proved in
[
1
]
that for all
n\in[N,2N]\cap\mathbb{Z}
,

(5.5)

\mu(A_{n}^{y}(\sigma_{n}))\ll\frac{(\sigma_{n}/2^{n})^{\gamma}}{(\delta_{n}/2^{n})^{\gamma}}\,\mu(A_{n}^{y}(\delta_{n}))=\sigma_{n}^{\gamma}\delta_{n}^{-\gamma}\mu(A_{n}^{y}(\delta_{n}))=n^{-\gamma(\tau-\varrho)}\mu(A_{n}^{y}(\delta_{n})).

Now, combing the above estimates, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=N}^{2N}\mu(A_{n}^{y}(\sigma_{n}))=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in G_{N}}\mu(A_{n}^{y}(\sigma_{n}))+\sum_{n\in B_{N}}\mu(A_{n}^{y}(\sigma_{n}))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Ahlforsregular}}}{{\ll}}

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in G_{N}}\mu(A_{n}^{y}(\sigma_{n}))+\sum_{n\in B_{N}}\sigma_{n}^{\gamma}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eqsigmadeltan}}}{{\ll}}

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in G_{N}}n^{-\gamma(\tau-\varrho)}\mu(A_{n}^{y}(\delta_{n}))+\sum_{n\in B_{N}}\sigma_{N}^{\gamma}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Good}}}{{\ll}}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=N}^{2N}n^{-\gamma(\tau-\varrho)-\varrho}+N^{2\varrho+1-\gamma-\tau\gamma}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle N^{1-\gamma(\tau-\varrho)-\varrho}+N^{2\varrho+1-\gamma-\tau\gamma}.

Write

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(A_{n}^{y}(\sigma_{n}))\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=2^{k}}^{2^{k+1}}\mu(A_{n}^{y}(\sigma_{n}))\ll\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2^{k(1-\gamma(\tau-\varrho)-\varrho)}+2^{k(2\varrho+1-\gamma-\tau\gamma)}),

which converges when

1-\gamma(\tau-\varrho)-\varrho<0\quad\text{ and }\quad 2\varrho+1-\gamma-\tau\gamma<0.

Equivalently,

\tau>\max\left\{\frac{1-(1-\gamma)\varrho}{\gamma},\frac{2\varrho+1-\gamma}{\gamma}\right\}.

The minimum of the right-hand side is attained when

\frac{1-(1-\gamma)\varrho}{\gamma}=\frac{2\varrho+1-\gamma}{\gamma},

which gives

\varrho=\frac{\gamma}{3-\gamma}\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{2\varrho+1-\gamma}{\gamma}=\frac{1}{\gamma}-\frac{1-\gamma}{3-\gamma}.

Note that
\varrho<\frac{\gamma}{2}
and

\varrho<\frac{2\varrho+1-\gamma}{\gamma}
. Therefore, letting
\varrho=\frac{\gamma}{3-\gamma}
we see from the above proof that
\mu(W_{2}(\psi_{\tau},y))=0
whenever

\tau>\frac{1}{\gamma}-\frac{1-\gamma}{3-\gamma}\approx 1.4292.

This completes the proof.
∎

6.
Key Lemmas for Theorem
1.7

The general strategy to prove Theorem
1.7
is similar to that of Theorem
1.4
. However, some modifications of key lemmas are necessary, which we detail in this section.

Recall that
\mu_{\mathbf{p}}
is the self-similar measure on
K_{b,D}
satisfying

\mu_{\mathbf{p}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\#D}p_{j}\mu_{\mathbf{p}}\circ\phi_{j}^{-1},

where

\phi_{j}(x)=\frac{x+k_{j}}{b},\quad D=\{k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{\#D}\}\ {\rm and}\ k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots<k_{\#D}.

It is well-known that (see
[
21
, page 342]
)

\widehat{\mu}_{\mathbf{p}}(x)=\prod_{q=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\#D}p_{j}e\left(-\frac{k_{j}x}{b^{q}}\right),\qquad|\widehat{\mu}_{\mathbf{p}}(x)|=\prod_{q=1}^{\infty}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{\#D}p_{j}e\left(-\frac{k_{j}x}{b^{q}}\right)\right|.

Similar to Theorem
1.4
,
the key to prove Theorem
1.7
is to estimate the following weighted sum

\sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-\sigma}|\widehat{\mu}_{\mathbf{p}}(ht^{n})|,

where
\sigma>0
,
h\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, and
t\geq 2
is an integer coprime to
b
; see Lemma
6.4
.

We first give an elementary lemma, which is an extension of Lemma
2.8
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Suppose
b
is a prime number,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
u\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
,

(6.1)

\frac{1}{b^{r}}\sum_{y=b^{\ell}s+u,\,0\leq s<b^{r}}\prod_{q=\ell+1}^{\ell+r}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{\#D}p_{j}e\left(-\frac{k_{j}y}{b^{q}}\right)\right|^{2}\leq\left(\min_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq\#D}(1-2p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}})\right)^{r}.

In particular, if
\#D=2
, then

(6.2)

\frac{1}{b^{r}}\sum_{y=b^{\ell}s+u,\,0\leq s<b^{r}}\prod_{q=\ell+1}^{\ell+r}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{2}p_{j}e\left(-\frac{k_{j}y}{b^{q}}\right)\right|^{2}=(1-2p_{1}p_{2})^{r}.

Proof.

Note that to prove (
6.1
), it is equivalent to show that for every pair
(j_{1},j_{2})
with
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq\#D
,

\frac{1}{b^{r}}\sum_{y=b^{\ell}s+u,0\leq s\leq b^{r}-1}\prod_{q=\ell+1}^{\ell+r}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{\#D}p_{j}e\left(-\frac{k_{j}y}{b^{q}}\right)\right|^{2}\leq\left(1-2p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}\right)^{r}.

Fix
(j_{1},j_{2})
satisfying
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq\#D
. Since

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=1}^{\#D}p_{j}e\left(-\frac{k_{j}y}{b^{q}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|p_{j_{1}}e\left(-\frac{k_{j_{1}}y}{b^{q}}\right)+p_{j_{2}}e\left(-\frac{k_{j_{2}}y}{b^{q}}\right)+\sum_{1\leq j\leq\#D,j\notin\{j_{1},j_{2}\}}p_{j}e\left(-\frac{k_{j}y}{b^{q}}\right)\right|,

it follows from the triangle inequality that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=1}^{\#D}p_{j}e\left(-\frac{k_{j}y}{b^{q}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|p_{j_{1}}e\left(-\frac{k_{j_{1}}y}{vb^{q}}\right)+p_{j_{2}}e\left(-\frac{k_{j_{2}}y}{b^{q}}\right)\right|+\left|\sum_{1\leq j\leq\#D,j\notin\{j_{1},j_{2}\}}p_{j}e\left(-\frac{k_{j}y}{b^{q}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|p_{j_{1}}+p_{j_{2}}e\left(-\frac{(k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y}{b^{q}}\right)\right|+1-p_{j_{1}}-p_{j_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(p_{j_{1}}-p_{j_{2}})^{2}+4p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y}{b^{q}}\right)}+1-p_{j_{1}}-p_{j_{2}}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=1}^{\#D}p_{j}e\left(-\frac{k_{j}y}{b^{q}}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(p_{j_{1}}-p_{j_{2}})^{2}+4p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y}{b^{q}}\right)+(1-p_{j_{1}}-p_{j_{2}})^{2}

\displaystyle+2(1-p_{j_{1}}-p_{j_{2}})\sqrt{(p_{j_{1}}-p_{j_{2}})^{2}+4p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y}{b^{q}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq(p_{j_{1}}-p_{j_{2}})^{2}+4p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y}{b^{q}}\right)+(1-p_{j_{1}}-p_{j_{2}})^{2}

\displaystyle+2(1-p_{j_{1}}-p_{j_{2}})(p_{j_{1}}+p_{j_{2}})

\displaystyle=(p_{j_{1}}-p_{j_{2}})^{2}+(1-p_{j_{1}}-p_{j_{2}})(1+p_{j_{1}}+p_{j_{2}})+4p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y}{b^{q}}\right)

\displaystyle=1-4p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}+4p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi(k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y}{b^{q}}\right).

Using

\cos^{2}(\pi x)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}e(x)+\frac{1}{4}e(-x),\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R},

we obtain that

\left|\sum_{j=1}^{\#D}p_{j}e\left(-\frac{k_{j}y}{b^{q}}\right)\right|^{2}\leq 1-2p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}+p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}e\left(\frac{(k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y}{b^{q}}\right)+p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}e\left(\frac{(k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y}{b^{q}}\right).

It follows that

(6.3)

\displaystyle\prod_{q=\ell+1}^{\ell+r}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{\#D}p_{j}e\left(-\frac{k_{j}y}{b^{q}}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{q=\ell+1}^{\ell+r}\left(1-2p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}+p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}e\left(\frac{(k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y}{b^{q}}\right)+p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}e\left(-\frac{(k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y}{b^{q}}\right)\right).

Denote

c_{0}=1-2p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}},\quad c_{-1}=c_{1}=p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}.

Expanding the right side of (
6.3
), a typical term is of the form

c_{\bm{\varepsilon}}e\left((k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y\sum_{q=\ell+1}^{\ell+r}\frac{\varepsilon_{q}}{b^{q}}\right),

where

\bm{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{\ell+1},\varepsilon_{\ell+2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{\ell+r})\in\{-1,0,1\}^{r}\quad{\rm and}\quad c_{\bm{\varepsilon}}=\prod_{q=\ell+1}^{\ell+r}c_{\varepsilon_{q}}.

Hence the right side of (
6.3
) is equal to

\sum_{\bm{\varepsilon}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{r}}c_{\bm{\varepsilon}}e\left((k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y\sum_{q=\ell+1}^{\ell+r}\frac{\varepsilon_{q}}{b^{q}}\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{y=b^{\ell}s+u,\,0\leq s<b^{r}}\prod_{q=\ell+1}^{\ell+r}\left(1-2p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}+p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}e\left(\frac{(k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y}{b^{q}}\right)+p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}e\left(-\frac{(k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y}{b^{q}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y=b^{\ell}s+u,\,0\leq s<b^{r}}\sum_{\bm{\varepsilon}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{r}}c_{\bm{\varepsilon}}e\left((k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y\sum_{q=\ell+1}^{\ell+r}\frac{\varepsilon_{q}}{b^{q}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\varepsilon}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{r}}\sum_{y=b^{\ell}s+u,\,0\leq s<b^{r}}c_{\bm{\varepsilon}}e\left((k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y\sum_{q=\ell+1}^{\ell+r}\frac{\varepsilon_{q}}{b^{q}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\varepsilon}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{r}}\left(c_{\bm{\varepsilon}}e\left((k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})u\sum_{q=\ell+1}^{\ell+r}\frac{\varepsilon_{q}}{b^{q}}\right)\sum_{s=0}^{b^{r}-1}e\left((k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})s\frac{\sum_{q=\ell+1}^{\ell+r}\varepsilon_{q}b^{r+\ell-q}}{b^{r}}\right)\right).

Since
b
is a prime number and
\bm{\varepsilon}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{r}
, an argument analogous to the proof of Lemma
2.8
yields that

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{b^{r}-1}e\left((k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})s\frac{\sum_{q=\ell+1}^{\ell+r}\varepsilon_{q}b^{r+\ell-q}}{b^{r}}\right)=\begin{cases}b^{r},\ &{\rm if}\ \bm{\varepsilon}=\bm{0},\\
0,\ &{\rm if}\ \bm{\varepsilon}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{r}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}.\end{cases}

Consequently, we have

(6.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{y=b^{\ell}s+u,0\leq s\leq b^{r}-1}\prod_{q=\ell+1}^{\ell+r}\left(1-2p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}+p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}e\left(\frac{(k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y}{b^{q}}\right)+p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}}e\left(-\frac{(k_{j_{2}}-k_{j_{1}})y}{b^{q}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(1-2p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}})^{r}b^{r}.

This together with (
6.3
) gives (
6.1
). Finally, we prove the “In particular” part. Note that if
\#D=2
, then

\left|\sum_{j=1}^{2}p_{j}e\left(-\frac{k_{j}y}{b^{q}}\right)\right|^{2}=1-2p_{1}p_{2}+p_{1}p_{2}e\left(\frac{(k_{2}-k_{1})y}{b^{q}}\right)+p_{1}p_{2}e\left(\frac{(k_{2}-k_{1})y}{b^{q}}\right).

Combing this with (
6.4
) gives (
6.2
).
∎

Since
b\geq 3
is a prime number and
t\geq 2
is an integer satisfying that
b\nmid t
, the following lemma follows from the identical proof of Lemma
2.5
and Corollary
2.6
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Denote
\ell=\nu_{b}(t^{b-1}-1)
. Let
h\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and write
h=b^{m}u
, where
m\geq 0
and
u\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
with
\gcd(u,b)=1
. Then, for every integer
r\geq 0
,

\left\{ht^{(b-1)n}\bmod b^{m+\ell+r}:0\leq n<b^{r}\}=\{(h+b^{m+\ell}k)\bmod b^{m+\ell+r}:0\leq k<b^{r}\right\}.

Based on Lemmas
6.1
and
6.2
, we can establish analogues to Lemmas
2.2
and
2.4
as follows.

Let

(6.5)

\alpha:=1+\frac{\log\left(\min\limits_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq\#D}(1-2p_{j_{1}}p_{j_{2}})\right)}{\log b}\quad\text{ and }\quad\beta:=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}.

Lemma 6.3
.

Suppose
b\geq 3
is a prime number and
t\geq 2
is an integer satisfying that
b\nmid t
. Then there exists an absolute constant
c>0
, such that for every integer
a\geq 0
, every positive integer
N
and every non-zero integer
h
,

\sum_{n=a}^{a+N-1}|\widehat{\mu}_{\mathbf{p}}(ht^{n})|^{2}\leq c^{2}N^{\alpha}.

Consequently,

\sum_{n=a}^{a+N-1}|\widehat{\mu}_{\mathbf{p}}(ht^{n})|\leq cN^{\beta}.

The constant
c
is independent of
a
,
N
and
h
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Suppose
b\geq 3
is a prime number and
t\geq 2
is an integer satisfying that
b\nmid t
. Let
\beta
be defined in (
6.5
) and

\sigma\in(0,\beta)
. Then for every non-zero integer
h
,

\sum_{n=1}^{N}n^{-\sigma}|\widehat{\mu}_{\mathbf{p}}(ht^{n})|\ll_{\sigma}N^{\beta-\sigma},

where the implicit constant is independent of
N
and
h
.

With Lemma
6.4
in hand, we just need to repeat the proof of Theorem
1.4
(in which we replace
\mu
by
\mu_{\mathbf{p}}
and
2
by

1.7
, we omit the details.
