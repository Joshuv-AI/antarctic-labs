Quantum fractional revival on zero-divisor graphs over ℤ n \mathbb{Z}_{n} Thanks: *Corresponding Author
Bui Phuoc Minh 1,2 and Songpon Sriwongsa ∗

Abstract
. In this paper, we characterize the existence of perfect state transfer (PST) and fractional revival in continuous-time quantum walks on the zero-divisor graph Γ ⁡ ( ℤ n ) \Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}) . By using the canonical equitable partition of Γ ⁡ ( ℤ n ) \Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}) induced by the proper divisors of n n , we derive a sufficient condition on n n for PST to occur between a pair of vertices. We show that fractional revival is restricted to cells of size 2 2 within the equitable partition. Furthermore, assuming − 1 -1 is not an eigenvalue of the quotient spectrum, we establish that two vertices in Γ ⁡ ( ℤ n ) \Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}) are strongly cospectral if and only if they form a cell of size 2 2 within the equitable partition that is either a set of false twins or true twins. Finally, we provide a characterization of fractional revival on bipartite Γ ⁡ ( ℤ n ) \Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}) and prove the non-existence of fractional revival on Γ ⁡ ( ℤ p 2 ​ q ) \Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}q}) .

1.
Introduction

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite, undirected, simple graph with the adjacency matrix
A(G)
.
The
transition matrix

H(t)
associated with
A(G)
is defined as

H(t):=\exp(itA(G))=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(it)^{k}}{k!}(A(G))^{k},\quad t\geq 0,

where
i=\sqrt{-1}
.
Since
A(G)
is real and symmetric,
H(t)
is also symmetric and unitary.
In particular,

\overline{H(t)}=H(t)^{-1},

where
\overline{\ \cdot\ }
denotes complex conjugation, and hence

(\overline{H(t)})^{T}=H(t)^{-1}.

The matrix
H(t)
governs a continuous-time quantum walk on
G
,
a fundamental concept in quantum computation and quantum information theory.
Quantum walks serve as algorithmic tools in various contexts, including
Grover-type search procedures
[
24
]
and the decision-tree algorithm of Farhi
and Gutmann
[
19
]
.
Moreover, Bose
[
8
]
proposed quantum walks on graphs as models for quantum state transfer in quantum communication networks.

We say that
(\alpha,\beta)
-
fractional revival
(FR) occurs from a
vertex
u
to a vertex
v
at time
\tau
if

|H(\tau)_{u,u}|^{2}+|H(\tau)_{u,v}|^{2}=1,

where
\alpha=H(\tau)_{u,u}
and
\beta=H(\tau)_{u,v}
denote the complex
amplitudes at
u
and
v
, respectively
[
29
]
. The case
\beta\neq 0
is called

proper
FR, while the case
\beta=0
is called
periodicity

relative to
u
. In particular, when
\alpha=0
(and hence
\beta=1
), we say that the graph
G

exhibits
perfect state transfer
(PST) from
u
to
v
. Note that, FR is
a generalization of both PST (
\alpha=0
) and periodicity (
\beta=0
).
Furthermore, since proper
(\alpha,\beta)
-FR occurs from
u
to
v
if and
only if proper
(-\frac{\bar{\alpha}\beta}{\bar{\beta}},\beta)
-FR occurs from

v
to
u

[
12
, Proposition 4.1]
, we shall often say proper FR
occurs
between

u
and
v
. We also say the graph
G
itself is periodic
if there is time
\tau
such that
|H(\tau)_{u,u}|=1
for all vertices
u

[
22
]
.

FR was introduced in
[
30
]

as a physical mechanism in quantum state transfer.
Unlike PST, where the quantum state is transferred entirely from one vertex to another,
FR describes a phenomenon in which only a portion of the state is transferred,
while the remaining amplitude may persist or reappear at other vertices.
In quantum physics, fractional revival arises naturally in systems such as the infinite potential well,
where the time-evolved wave function can be expressed as a superposition of translated copies
of the initial state arranged in a parity-preserving manner

[
2
]
.
Dooley and Spiller
[
17
]
further investigated FR in the context of qubit interactions,
relating it to multiple Schrödinger-cat states and quantum carpets.
In recent years, FR has developed into a topic at the interface of mathematics and physics,
particularly within spectral graph theory and algebraic combinatorics.

The mathematical theory of quantum state transfer, encompassing both PST and
FR, has developed rapidly. PST was first introduced by Christandl et al.

[
16
]
and has since been extensively studied. Godsil
[
22
]
provided a comprehensive survey of PST in graphs and its associated spectral conditions. Several works on PST have focused on graphs arising from groups, see for examples
[
9
,
10
]
. PST has also been investigated in graphs derived from rings.
Integral circulant graphs were characterized spectrally in
[
6
]
,
while unitary Cayley graphs admit PST only in the cases
K_{2}
and
C_{4}

[
5
]
.
Eigenvalue methods were extended to unitary Cayley graphs over finite local
rings
[
27
]
,
and later generalized to finite commutative rings and gcd-graphs

[
32
]
,
with additional refinements in
[
15
,
26
]
.

Subsequent research has focused on FR.
Fractional revival in XX quantum spin chains was studied in
[
20
]
,
where models based on isospectral deformations and Para-Krawtchouk polynomials
were shown to admit both PST and FR.
Balanced fractional revival and its connection with quantum walks on hypercubes
were examined in
[
7
]
.
Symmetry of FR between vertex pairs was established in
[
12
]
,
while conditions for FR in paths and cycles were analyzed in

[
13
]
.
A general framework for FR in spin networks, extending the notion of cospectral vertices,
was developed in
[
11
]
, and it was shown that FR may occur between non-cospectral vertices in certain infinite graphs
[
21
]
.
Characterizations of FR in weighted graphs, including twin vertices and double cones,
were obtained in
[
29
]
.
Further studies investigated FR in semi-Cayley and Cayley graphs over finite
abelian groups
[
34
,
33
]
,
and in unitary Cayley graphs over
\mathbb{Z}_{n}

[
31
,
25
]
.

Consider the ring
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
of integers modulo
n
.
Recall that a nonzero element
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is called a zero divisor
if there exists a nonzero element
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
such that
ab=0
.
The
zero-divisor graph

\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
is a simple undirected graph
whose vertex set consists of all nonzero zero divisors of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
,
where two distinct vertices
u
and
v
are adjacent if and only if

uv=0
.

Zero-divisor graphs have been extensively studied and play an important role
in algebraic graph theory. Motivated by the preceding discussion,
we investigate PST and FR on the zero-divisor graph
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
in this paper.
In Section
3
, we present the structural properties of
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
and survey the graph adjacency spectrum.
A sufficient condition for
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
to admit PST
is established in Section
4
.
In Section
5
, we derive necessary and sufficient conditions for two
vertices of
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
to be strongly copsectral. Furthermore, we
study FR on bipartite
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
and prove the non-existence of

\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}q})
.

We denote the

. A graph is

The

,

,

A partition

of any vertex

refines the unique

In a connected graph

, let

For a partition

3.
The Zero-Divisor Graph
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})

For an integer

, let
Z(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
denote the set of all zero
divisors of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and
Z^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{n})=Z(\mathbb{Z}_{n})\setminus\{0\}

The zero-divisor graph
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
has the vertex set
V(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}))=Z^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{n})

u
and
v
are
adjacent if and only if
uv=0
in
\mathbb{Z}_{n}

3

[
1
]

Following the notation in
[
4

Because the vertices of

. Since every nonzero element

V_{d_{i}}=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}:\gcd(x,n)=d_{i}\}.

4.
Perfect state transfer

We investigate perfect state transfer on
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
using the theory
of equitable partitions. By
[
29
, Corollary 2]
, provided that

|V(G)|\geq 3
, proper FR (and thus PST) cannot occur within twin sets of size
3

or greater, nor between a twin and a non-twin. Because each cell
V_{d_{i}}
of size
at least
2
forms a set of twins, these general properties immediately rule out
PST between distinct cells, and within any cell where
|V_{d_{i}}|=\varphi(n/d_{i})\geq 3
.

While these twin bounds effectively filter out non-candidate pairs, the theory
of equitable partitions provides a framework that independently recovers these
non-existence results and more importantly, establish conditions under which PST
occurs. We begin by showing that vertices grouped within the same cell of any
equitable partition of
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
must share the same greatest
common divisor with
n
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
be the zero-divisor graph of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, and
let
\pi
be any equitable partition of its vertex set. If
u
and
v
are
vertices belonging to the same cell of
\pi
, then
\gcd(u,n)=\gcd(v,n)
.

Proof.

Let
u,v\in V(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}))
belong to the same cell of an equitable
partition
\pi
. By definition,
u
and
v
must have the same degree. For
any vertex
x\in V(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}))
with
d=\gcd(x,n)
, its degree
is determined by the number of nonzero multiples of
\frac{n}{d}
in

\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. There are exactly
d-1
such multiples. To avoid self-loops
in the simple graph
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
, we must subtract
1
from this
count if
x^{2}\equiv 0\pmod{n}
, a condition equivalent to
n\mid d^{2}
.
Thus, the degree of
x
is given by:

\deg(x)=\begin{cases}d-1&\text{if }n\nmid d^{2}\\
d-2&\text{if }n\mid d^{2}.\end{cases}

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
\gcd(u,n)\neq\gcd(v,n)
.
Let
d_{1}=\gcd(u,n)
and
d_{2}=\gcd(v,n)
, and assume without loss of
generality that
d_{1}<d_{2}
. From the piecewise function above, the maximum
possible degree of
u
is
d_{1}-1
, and the minimum possible degree of

v
is
d_{2}-2
. For
\deg(u)=\deg(v)
to hold, it is necessary that:

d_{2}-2\leq d_{1}-1\implies d_{2}\leq d_{1}+1.

Since
d_{1}<d_{2}
, we must have
d_{2}=d_{1}+1
. This strict equality forces

\deg(u)=d_{1}-1
and
\deg(v)=d_{2}-2
, which in turn requires
n\nmid d_{1}^{2}
and
n\mid d_{2}^{2}
. Note that consecutive integers are coprime, so

\gcd(d_{1},d_{2})=1
. Since
d_{1}\mid n
and
d_{2}\mid n
, their coprimality
ensures that
d_{1}d_{2}\mid n
. Substituting
d_{2}=d_{1}+1
yields:

d_{1}(d_{1}+1)\mid n.

Furthermore, since
n\mid d_{2}^{2}
, it follows that
n\mid(d_{1}+1)^{2}
. By
the transitivity of divisibility:

d_{1}(d_{1}+1)\mid(d_{1}+1)^{2}.

Canceling the nonzero factor
(d_{1}+1)
implies
d_{1}\mid(d_{1}+1)
, and
consequently,
d_{1}\mid 1
. This forces
d_{1}=1
, contradicting the
requirement that any zero divisor
u
in
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
must satisfy

\gcd(u,n)>1
. This is a contradiction, and we conclude that
\gcd(u,n)=\gcd(v,n)
.
∎

In the proof of
[
22
, Corollary 9.3]
, Godsil showed that if

and
\pi_{v}

.
Consequently, PST from

to

,

in

fails to admit PST.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose that
|V(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}))|>2

.

Proof.

Let

and

, and let

\pi_{v}

denote the CEPs refining the partitions

,
any cell of an equitable partition of

\pi_{u}=\left\{\{u\},V_{d_{s}}\setminus\{u\}\right\}\displaystyle\bigcup_{i\neq s}V_{d_{i}}

∎

Theorem 4.3
.

Let

By an argument similar to the proof of

Remark 4.4
.

The results in
4.2
and
4.3
are consistent with the
general properties of twin sets from
[
29
, Corollary 2]
.

It can be deduced from
4.2
and
4.3
that PST cannot occur
between any vertex pair in
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
whenever

is not a multiple
of
3
or
4

[
3
]
.

Theorem 4.5
.

[
3
, Theorem 1]

Let
G
be a graph with an equitable partition
\pi

and

\pi

and

Using this result, the following theorem determines the values of

for which
PST occurs and explicitly identifies the vertex pairs exhibiting this phenomenon.

Theorem 4.6
.

The zero-divisor graph
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
admits perfect state transfer
whenever

, or

and

For

\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{8})

and

; similarly, for

\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})

is isomorphic to

, which admits PST between its end vertices at

. Now, suppose that

is isomorphic to the complete bipartite

. Let
\pi=\left\{\{p\},\{2p\},V(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{3p}))\setminus\{p,2p\}\right\}

be the

Since

∎

Remark 4.7
.

We note a subtle distinction regarding the necessary conditions for PST

23

For example, consider the Cartesian product

, where

V(P_{2})=\{0,1\}
and
V(K_{1,4})=\{c,l_{1},l_{2},l_{3},l_{4}\}
(see
Fig.

2

Figure 2
.

It is well known that

has PST between
0
and
1
at time

with a transition magnitude of
|-i|=1
. Furthermore,
since

consists of even integers,

with the transition

\left[H_{A(K_{1,4})}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\right]_{c,c}=-1

, we have

However, the distance partitions for these vertices are not identical.

\displaystyle\Delta_{(0,c)}

5.
Fractional revival

As discussed in
Section

4
, the only candidates for proper FR are the twin sets
of size
2
, where PST occurs if
n=8
,
n=9
or
n=3p
. However, if
n\notin\{8,9,3p\}
, it remains unclear whether the vertices in the twin sets of
size
2
exhibit the phenomenon. For instance, if
n=18
, it is not
immediately apparent whether the pairs
\{3,15\}
and
\{6,12\}
exhibit PST.
Although the general case for
n
is not fully determined, the structure of
such pairs allows for a partial characterization.

Let
A(G)
have the spectral decomposition
\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}E_{j}
. The

eigenvalue support
of a vertex
u
is defined as the set
\Phi_{u}=\{\lambda_{j}:E_{j}\mathbf{e}_{u}\neq\mathbf{0}\}
. Vertices
u
and
v
are
cospectral

if
(E_{j})_{u,u}=(E_{j})_{v,v}
for each
j
, and
parallel
if

E_{j}\mathbf{e}_{u}=c_{j}E_{j}\mathbf{e}_{v}
for some constants
c_{j}
. In the stronger
case where
E_{j}\mathbf{e}_{u}=\pm E_{j}\mathbf{e}_{v}
for each
j
, the vertices
are said to be
strongly cospectral
. Under this condition, the support

\Phi_{u}=\Phi_{v}
can be written as
\Phi_{u}=\Phi_{u,v}^{+}\cup\Phi_{u,v}^{-}
, where

\Phi_{u,v}^{+}=\{\lambda_{j}:E_{j}\mathbf{e}_{u}=E_{j}\mathbf{e}_{v}\}\;\text{and}\;\Phi_{u,v}^{-}=\{\lambda_{j}:E_{j}\mathbf{e}_{u}=-E_{j}\mathbf{e}_{v}\}

By
[
29
, Lemma 4]
, proper FR between twin vertices
u

and
v
requires them to be strongly cospectral. Since a twin pair is strongly
cospectral if and only if they are parallel
[
28
, Corollary 3.8]
,
the occurrence of proper FR between twins requires parallelism. Therefore, our
task reduces to establishing the specific conditions under which these twin
pairs are parallel. To characterize parallel vertices in
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
,
we first establish the following lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
A
be the adjacency matrix of
G
, and let
Q
be the adjacency matrix
of the quotient graph
G/\pi
for some equitable partition
\pi
. If

\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
A
that is not an eigenvalue of
Q
, then any
eigenvector
\mathbf{v}
associated with
\lambda
sums to zero on every
cell of
\pi
.

Proof.

Let
S
be the characteristic matrix of
\pi
. Since
AS=SQ
, the column
space of
S
, denoted by
\mathcal{C}(S)
, is
A
-invariant. Since
A
is
symmetric, its orthogonal complement
\mathcal{C}(S)^{\perp}
is also

A
-invariant. We uniquely decompose the eigenvector
\mathbf{v}
as

\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}
, where
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{C}(S)

and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}(S)^{\perp}
.

The eigenvalue equation
A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}
yields
A\mathbf{x}+A\mathbf{y}=\lambda\mathbf{x}+\lambda\mathbf{y}
. By the

A
-invariance of the subspaces and the uniqueness of the orthogonal
decomposition, it follows that
A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}
. Since

\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{C}(S)
, we may write
\mathbf{x}=S\mathbf{w}

for some
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Then,

SQ\mathbf{w}=AS\mathbf{w}=A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}=\lambda S\mathbf{w}.

Because
S
has full column rank,
SQ\mathbf{w}=\lambda S\mathbf{w}

implies
Q\mathbf{w}=\lambda\mathbf{w}
. However,
\lambda
is not an
eigenvalue of
Q
by hypothesis, so we must have
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{0}
,
which implies
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
. Thus,
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}(S)^{\perp}
, completing the proof.
∎

Monterde’s work provides a general bound on the size of twin sets that can admit
parallel vertices. Specifically,
[
28
, Corollary 3.10]
shows
that parallelism cannot occur when
T\geq 3
. Our next results demonstrate that
for the graph
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
, when
|T|=2
and the vertices are false
twins, they are parallel.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
V_{d}\in\pi_{\mathcal{D}}
be a cell of size
|V_{d}|\geq 2
such that
the induced subgraph
\Gamma(V_{d})\cong\overline{K}_{|V_{d}|}
. Then, any
two distinct vertices in
V_{d}
are parallel if and only if
|V_{d}|=2
.

Proof.

Let
u,v\in V_{d}
be distinct false twins. First, we consider the case

\lambda\neq 0
. Let
\mathbf{x}
be any eigenvector associated with

\lambda
. Because false twins share identical neighborhoods, their
corresponding rows in the adjacency matrix
A=A(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}))

are equal. Evaluating the
u
-th and
v
-th entries of
A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}
yields:

\lambda(\mathbf{e}_{u}^{T}\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{e}_{u}^{T}(A\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{e}_{u}^{T}A)\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{e}_{v}^{T}A)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{e}_{v}^{T}(A\mathbf{x})=\lambda(\mathbf{e}_{v}^{T}\mathbf{x}).

Since
\lambda\neq 0
, dividing by
\lambda
yields
\mathbf{e}_{u}^{T}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{e}_{v}^{T}\mathbf{x}
. Because this equality holds for every
eigenvector in the
\lambda
-eigenspace
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
, the projections
of
\mathbf{e}_{u}
and
\mathbf{e}_{v}
onto
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
are identical.
Thus,
E_{\lambda}\mathbf{e}_{u}=E_{\lambda}\mathbf{e}_{v}
.

Consider the case
\lambda=0
. Let
S
be the characteristic matrix of the
equitable partition
\pi_{\mathcal{D}}
. Following the argument in

5.1
, we decompose
\mathbf{e}_{u}
as follows:

\mathbf{e}_{u}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{|V_{d}|}\mathbf{1}_{V_{d}}}_{\mathbf{m}}+\underbrace{\left(\mathbf{e}_{u}-\frac{1}{|V_{d}|}\mathbf{1}_{V_{d}}\right)}_{\mathbf{z}},

where
\mathbf{1}_{V_{d}}
denotes the characteristic vector of the cell
V_{d}
.

Let
E_{0}
denote the orthogonal projector onto the null space
\mathcal{N}(A)
.
Applying
E_{0}
to both sides of our decomposition yields:

E_{0}\mathbf{e}_{u}=E_{0}\mathbf{m}+E_{0}\mathbf{z}.

Because all vertices in
V_{d}
share identical columns in
A
, we know

A\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{0}
, meaning
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{N}(A)
and

E_{0}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{z}
. Meanwhile,
\mathbf{m}
is constant on the
cells of
\pi_{\mathcal{D}}
and therefore it lies in
\mathcal{C}(S)
.
By
5.1
, the absence of
0
as an eigenvalue of the
quotient matrix guarantees
\mathcal{N}(A)\perp\mathcal{C}(S)
. Thus,

E_{0}\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{0}
. Applying
E_{0}
to our decomposition yields

(5.1)

E_{0}\mathbf{e}_{u}=\mathbf{e}_{u}-\frac{1}{|V_{d}|}\mathbf{1}_{V_{d}}

For
u
and
v
to be parallel in the null space, we require

E_{0}\mathbf{e}_{u}=cE_{0}\mathbf{e}_{v}
for some
c\neq 0
. Substituting
our projection formula yields

\mathbf{e}_{u}-\frac{1}{|V_{d}|}\mathbf{1}_{V_{d}}=c\left(\mathbf{e}_{v}-\frac{1}{|V_{d}|}\mathbf{1}_{V_{d}}\right).

Evaluating this equation at the
u
-th coordinate gives
1-\frac{1}{|V_{d}|}=c\left(0-\frac{1}{|V_{d}|}\right)
, which simplifies to
c=1-|V_{d}|
. Conversely, evaluating at the
v
-th coordinate gives

0-\frac{1}{|V_{d}|}=c\left(1-\frac{1}{|V_{d}|}\right)
, which simplifies
to
c=\frac{-1}{|V_{d}|-1}
.

Equating these two required values for
c
yields
(|V_{d}|-1)^{2}=1
. Since

|V_{d}|\geq 2
, this forces
|V_{d}|=2
(yielding
c=-1
). Therefore,

u
and
v
are parallel in the null space, and consequently parallel, if
and only if
|V_{d}|=2
.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
V_{d}\in\pi_{\mathcal{D}}
be a cell of size
|V_{d}|\geq 2
such that
the induced subgraph
\Gamma(V_{d})\cong K_{|V_{d}|}
. If
-1
is not an
eigenvalue of the associated quotient matrix, then any two distinct vertices
in
V_{d}
are parallel if and only if

.

Proof.

Proceeding analogously, the adjacency rows of true twins satisfy

(\mathbf{e}_{u}-\mathbf{e}_{v})^{T}A=-(\mathbf{e}_{u}-\mathbf{e}_{v})^{T}
.
Right-multiplying by any eigenvector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}

yields
\lambda(\mathbf{e}_{u}-\mathbf{e}_{v})^{T}\mathbf{x}=-(\mathbf{e}_{u}-\mathbf{e}_{v})^{T}\mathbf{x}
, which simplifies to

for all
\lambda\neq-1
, we have
\mathbf{e}_{u}^{T}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{e}_{v}^{T}\mathbf{x}

E_{\lambda}\mathbf{e}_{u}=E_{\lambda}\mathbf{e}_{v}

For

\mathbf{e}_{u}=\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{z}
, where
\mathbf{m}=\frac{1}{|V_{d}|}\mathbf{1}_{V_{d}}
and

Let
E_{-1}
be the orthogonal projector onto the eigenspace
\mathcal{E}_{-1}
.
Because
V_{d}
forms a complete graph, we have

,

and only if

.
∎

w^{2}+4ul(w-l)=w^{2}-4ul(l-w)\geq 1.

This inequality implies
4ul(l-w)<w^{2}
. Since
l-w\geq 1
, it
follows that
4ul<w^{2}
, and consequently
2\sqrt{ul}<w
. Given
our assumption that

, we obtain the bound:

(5.18)

2\sqrt{ul}<l\implies 4ul<l^{2}\implies 4u<l.

Recalling
Eq.

5.15
, the positivity of the factors on the

(u+2)^{2}>4ul+w>4ul
. Substituting
l>4u

then yields
(u+2)^{2}>16u^{2}
, or equivalently
15u^{2}-4u-4<0
.
This inequality has no integer solution for
u\geq 1
. Therefore, the
case

is impossible.

Case 3:

z>0.
As before, let
d=\gcd{(z,A)}
and write
z=kd
and

, where
\gcd{(k,l)}=1
. Since the right-hand side of

is positive, it follows that

, and thus

. Proceeding analogously to the case
z<0
, the same
divisibility argument yields

for some

. Substituting these expressions into

,
we obtain:

(5.19)

\displaystyle nk(k^{2}-4pl(k-l))

\displaystyle=(p-2)^{2}

Let
\Delta=k-l
. Substituting

into

yields:

\displaystyle(l+\Delta)^{2}-4pl\Delta

\displaystyle=\frac{(p-2)^{2}}{nk}

(5.20)

\displaystyle l^{2}-(4p-2)l\Delta+\Delta^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(p-2)^{2}}{nk}

Let
f(l,\Delta)=l^{2}-(4p-2)l\Delta+\Delta^{2}
denote the
left-hand side of
Item

Case 3:
. Since the right-hand side
is positive, we must have
f(l,\Delta)>0
. Because
f(l,\Delta)

is symmetric in its arguments, we may assume without loss of generality
that
l\geq\Delta
. Dividing the condition
f>0
by
\Delta^{2}

and setting
r=\frac{l}{\Delta}\geq 1
, it follows that
r
must
lie outside the interval
[r_{2},r_{1}]
formed by the roots of the

r^{2}-(4p-2)r+1=0

where
r_{1}
and

denote the larger and smaller roots, respectively.

Because

and

, we have

. Therefore,

.
Since

, we have

. Substituting the

into the right-hand side of

Item

Case 3:
and noting that

, we obtain:

(5.21)

\frac{(p-2)^{2}}{nk}\leq\frac{(p-2)^{2}}{4p-1}=\frac{p^{2}-4p+4}{4p-1}<\frac{p}{4}.

To analyze the left-hand side, recall that
l>(4p-3)\Delta
. We
may write

for some integer

.
Substituting this into
f(l,\Delta)
yields :

(5.22)

f(l,\Delta)=u^{2}+(4p-2)u\Delta+\Delta^{2}

We now consider the possible values for the integer

.

Subcase 3.1:
u\geq 1.
For
u\geq 1
, the

\displaystyle f(l,\Delta)

\displaystyle=u^{2}+(4p-2)u\Delta+\Delta^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 1^{2}+(4p-2)\Delta+\Delta^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 4p\Delta+(\Delta-1)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 4p,

where the last inequality holds for all

. Since

for any

in

.

Subcase 3.2:
u=0
. If
u=0
, then

. It follows that

, and

requires

n(4p-1)\Delta^{3}=(p-2)^{2}

Theorem 5.20
.

Let
p
and
q
be distinct primes. Then the graph
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}q})

does not admit fractional revival between any pair of distinct vertices.

Proof.

Consider a set of twins
V_{d}=\{u,v\}
in
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}q})
. Since
neither
0
nor
-1
is an eigenvalue of
C(\Upsilon_{p^{2}q})

(see
5.21
),
u
and
v
are strongly cospectral by

5.4
.
Because
|V(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}q})|\geq 3
, the eigenvalue support
satisfies
\Phi_{u,v}^{+}\geq 2
by
[
28
, Theorem 3.4]
.
By
5.19
, the eigenvalues in
\Phi_{u,v}^{+}

are either roots of an irreducible cubic or quartic, or they consist of an
integer and a root of an irreducible cubic. In either case, the support fails
to satisfy both
5.11
and

. Thus,
the claim follows.
∎

Some of our results thus far require that
-1\notin\sigma(\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})/\pi_{\mathcal{D}})
. While we conjecture this
holds broadly, a general proof remains challenging due to the fast growth of
the quotient matrix as the graph size
n
increases. We next show that
-1

is not in the spectrum of the symmetrized quotient matrix
C(\Upsilon_{n})

for
n\in\{pq,p^{2}q,p_{1}p_{2}p_{3},p^{k}\}
. We note that the case
n=pq
is
straightforward, as
\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pq})
is a complete bipartite graph

K_{\varphi(p),\varphi(q)}
with a known spectrum that not having
-1
for
distinct primes
p,q
.

Theorem 5.21
.

Let
n=p^{2}q
, where
p
and
q
are distinct primes. Then
-1\notin\sigma(C(\Upsilon_{n}))
.

Proof.

Let
\phi_{C}(x)
be the characteristic polynomial of the
quotient matrix, as derived in
[
4
, Example 3.1]
. Evaluating

\phi_{C}(-1)
, we find
\phi_{C}(-1)=1+(p-2)-2p(p-1)(q-1)-p(p-1)(p-2)(q-1)+p(p-1)^{3}(q-1)^{2}.

Factoring out the common term
(p-1)
, we obtain:

\phi_{C}(-1)=(p-1)\left[1-p^{2}(q-1)+p(p-1)^{2}(q-1)^{2}\right].

Since
p
is prime,
(p-1)\geq 1
. Let
f(p,q)=p(p-1)^{2}(q-1)^{2}-p^{2}(q-1)+1
. It suffices to show
f(p,q)\neq 0
for all primes
p,q
.
We write
f(p,q)
as:

f(p,q)=p(q-1)\left[(p-1)^{2}(q-1)-p\right]+1.

Given
q\geq 2
, we note
(p-1)^{2}(q-1)-p\geq p^{2}-3p+1
. For
p\geq 3
, this quadratic is strictly positive, implying
f(p,q)>1
; for

p=2
, the expression becomes
2(q-1)(q-3)+1
, which is nonzero for all
prime
q
. It follows that
\phi_{C}(-1)
never vanishes, and thus

-1\notin\sigma(C(\Upsilon_{n}))
.
∎

Theorem 5.22
.

Let
n=p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}
, where
p_{1},p_{2}
, and
p_{3}
are distinct primes. Then

-1\notin\sigma(C(\Upsilon_{n}))
.

Proof.

Let
Q=\varphi(p_{1}p_{2}p_{3})
and
S=\varphi(p_{1}p_{2})+\varphi(p_{2}p_{3})+\varphi(p_{3}p_{1})
. From
[
4
, Theorem 3.7]
,
\phi_{C}(-1)
is
given by:

(Q-1)^{3}-S(Q-1)-2Q=0,

which can be written as:

(Q-1)[(Q-1)^{2}-S-2]=2.

Since
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
are distinct primes, the smallest value for
Q
is

8
. For any other set of distinct primes,
Q>8
. Consequently,
Q-1\geq 7
, which cannot divide
2
. Thus,
x=-1
is not a root.
∎

Theorem 5.23
.

Let
n=p^{k}
where
p
is a prime and
k
is odd. Then
-1\notin\sigma(C(\Upsilon_{n}))
.

Proof.

Let
k=2m+1
for some
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, and let
\phi_{C}(x)

denote the characteristic polynomial of
C(\Upsilon_{n})
. Following

[
4
, Theorem 4.1]
, the polynomial is given by:

\phi_{C}(x)=(-x)^{m}\prod_{t=1}^{m}\varphi(p^{t})\det(X),

where
X
is the
m\times m
tridiagonal matrix as defined in the theorem.

Evaluating the expression at
x=-1
, the pre-factors
(-x)^{m}\prod\varphi(p^{t})
are strictly positive for
p\geq 2
. Thus,
-1
is a
root if and only if
\det(X)=0
at
x=-1
.

Under the substitution
x=-1
, all terms in the definitions of the
off-diagonal entries
b_{j}
containing
(1+x)
vanish, thus
X
is
a diagonal matrix. The diagonal entries are
a_{1}=1-\varphi(p^{m+1})
and

a_{i}=-\varphi(p^{m+i})
for
i=2,\dots,m
. Since
\varphi(p^{m+1})\geq 2

for all primes
p
and
m\geq 1
, it follows that
a_{i}<0
for all
i
.
Consequently,
\det(X)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\neq 0
, which confirms that
-1

is not an eigenvalue.
∎

Theorem 5.24
.

Let
n=p^{k}
where
p
is a prime and
k
is even. Then
-1\notin\sigma(C(\Upsilon_{n}))
.

Proof.

For
n=p^{2m}
, the characteristic polynomial is defined as:

\phi_{C}(x)=(-x)^{m-1}\prod_{t=1}^{m}\varphi(p^{t})\det(X),

where
X
is the
m\times m
tridiagonal matrix as defined in

[
4
, Theorem 4.2]
.

Substituting
x=-1
, the off-diagonal entries
b_{j}
vanish for

j\geq 2
, while
b_{1}=1
remains constant. This results in a block
diagonal structure for
X
, comprising a
2\times 2
leading principal
submatrix
M
and a diagonal trailing part. The diagonal entries are
a_{1}=1
,
a_{2}=1-\varphi(p^{m+1})
, and
a_{i}=-\varphi(p^{m-1+i})
for

i\geq 3
.

The determinant of the
2\times 2
block is given by:

\det(M)=a_{1}a_{2}-b_{1}^{2}=1(1-\varphi(p^{m+1}))-1^{2}=-\varphi(p^{m+1}).

Since
\varphi(p^{m+1})\neq 0
and all remaining diagonal entries
a_{i}
are
strictly negative, the total determinant
\det(X)=\det(M)\prod_{i=3}^{m}a_{i}

is nonzero. Thus,
\phi_{C}(-1)\neq 0
, and
-1
is not an
eigenvalue.
∎
