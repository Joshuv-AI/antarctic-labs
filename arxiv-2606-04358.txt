Gauss Circle Lattices with Geometric Convolutions for Synthesizing High Dimensional Image-Source Room Impulse Responses


Abstract
The image-source model (ISM) is a widely adopted method for efficiently simulating acoustic room impulse responses (RIRs) under specular reflection assumptions. Acoustic paths between source and receiver are traced to lattice points computed from successive reflections over bounding planes of the room. Rectangular rooms bound the total number of image-sources to be polynomial in the RIR’s duration or distance k k equivalent, with degree equal the number of room dimensions N N . Direct ISM simulations are therefore compute upper-bound by O ​ ( k N ) \textrm{O}\left({k^{N}}\right) , and consider only cases of N ≤ 3 N\leq 3 for tractability and real-world applications. This work proposes an alternative computational method that lowers the asymptotic compute bound to O ​ ( N ​ k 2 ​ log ⁡ k ) \textrm{O}\left({Nk^{2}\log k}\right) for integer coordinates and room dimensions via reducing ISM lattice point counting to the classic Gauss circle problem (GCP). We extend the lattice counting model to frequency-dependent and reflection weighted image-sources in higher dimensions, relating solutions between successive dimensions via the convolution operator. Two constructions for realizing RIRs are presented, along with time-frequency controls, error and run-time analysis, and RIR statistics.

The classic image method

2
Image-Source Gauss Circle Problem

The reflected room boundaries in ISM are defined by a lattice of cuboids, and generalized to be
N
-dimensional orthotopes over a set of lattice coordinates. Let a lattice coordinate be given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{{\nu}}=\left[{\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{N}}\right]^{T},\hskip 9.24994pt\nu_{n}\in\mathbb{Z},\end{split}

(1)

where the lattice coordinate origin
\bm{{\nu}}=\bm{{0}}
indexes the orthotope of size
\bm{{\ell}}=\left[{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{N}}\right]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
containing an omni-directional sound source at
\bm{{s}}=\left[{s_{1},\ldots,s_{N}}\right]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and receiver at
\bm{{r}}=\left[{r_{1},\ldots,r_{N}}\right]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
in meters. Specular acoustic paths between source, boundaries, and receivers are modeled by successive reflections or mirroring of the source across the orthotope’s planes. For brevity, we can express the image-sources’ coordinates as a function of the lattice coordinates
\bm{{p}}(\bm{{\nu}})=\left[{p_{1}(\nu_{1}),\ldots,p_{N}(\nu_{N})}\right]^{T}
as shown in Fig.
1
, where the
n^{th}
coordinate is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{n}(\nu_{n})=\ell_{n}\nu_{n}+(-1)^{\nu_{n}}s_{n},\end{split}

(2)

and where the acoustic path-length equates to the image-source to receiver distance
d(\bm{{\nu}})=\left\|{\bm{{p}}(\bm{{\nu}})-\bm{{r}}}\right\|_{2}
. Therefore, the set of all lattice coordinates with image-sources within time
T
(seconds) is given by
\Xi=\left\{{\bm{{\nu}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}\,|\,\left\|{d(\bm{{\nu}})}\right\|_{2}\leq cT}\right\}
, where
c
is the speed of sound in meters / second.

Figure 1:
Reflecting the source across the orthotope planes generates the image-sources. Image-sources between

contribute to the RIR at time

with delay and attenuation proportionate to their distances
\left\|{\bm{{p}}(\bm{{\nu}})-\bm{{r}}}\right\|_{2}

The image-source model’s acoustic room transfer function (RTF)
H(\omega)
at angular frequency
\omega
between source and receiver integrates over all image-sources and is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}H(\omega)&=\sum_{\bm{{\nu}}\in\Xi}A(\bm{{\nu}})R(\omega,\bm{{\nu}})e^{\frac{\minus j\omega d(\bm{{\nu}})}{cT_{s}}},\hskip 9.24994ptA(\bm{{\nu}})=\frac{1}{d^{\frac{N\minus 1}{2}}(\bm{{\nu}})},\\
R(\omega,\bm{{\nu}})&=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\Gamma_{+n}^{\left|{\left\lfloor{\frac{\nu_{n}+1}{2}}\right\rfloor}\right|}(\omega)\,\Gamma_{\minus n}^{\left|{\left\lfloor{\frac{1\minus\nu_{n}}{2}}\right\rfloor}\right|}(\omega),\end{split}

(3)

where
T_{s}
is the sampling period (seconds), and
\left\lfloor{x}\right\rfloor
,
\left\lceil{x}\right\rceil
are the floor and ceiling functions respectively. The distance attenuation term
A(\bm{{\nu}})
normalizes for total energy scattered over the surface of a
N
-dimensional sphere. The wall-material transfer functions
\Gamma_{+n}(\omega)
,
\Gamma_{\minus n}(\omega)
model reflections between positive and negative facing walls in the
n^{th}
dimension respectively;
R(\omega,\bm{{\nu}})
models the contribution of all wall-reflections for the image-source at
p(\bm{{\nu}})
. Lastly, the complex exponential in the frequency domain accounts for the acoustic delay. Thus, the direct cost of computing Eq. (
3
) containing up to the
K^{th}
order reflection is polynomial
\textrm{O}\left({K^{N}}\right)
, which is prohibitive for computing both the RTF’s reverberation tail, and generalizing into higher-dimensional rooms (
N>3
). Fortunately, we can show the integration to be separable across successive dimensions as follows:

Let us consider integrating image-source contributions over radial slices of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
consisting of contributions from the lower dimensional slice in
\mathbb{R}^{N\minus 1}
. To motivate and then illustrate this approach, we introduce the Gauss circle problem, which counts the number of integer lattice coordinates within a
k
-radius ball under the Euclidean norm
\left\|{\bm{{\nu}}}\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nu^{2}_{n}}
as shown in Fig.
2
. Source and receiver coordinates are coincident to the origin within a unit sized orthotope; image-source coordinates are coincident to integer lattice coordinates. Thus, discretizing GCP’s radius
k
over integer multiples of the sampling distance
cT_{s}
and taking the difference between consecutive samples yields the number of image-sources in the sampling interval. Taking weighted finite differences of GCP w.r.t.
k
therefore approximates the RIR of Eq. (
3
). We formulate the discretized GCP volume function as follows:

Let
C(k,N)=\left|{\left\{{\bm{{\nu}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}\,\left|\,\left\|{\bm{{\nu}}}\right\|_{2}\leq k\right.}\right\}}\right|
be the cardinality of the set of lattice coordinates, upper bounded by
k
Euclidean norm. Squaring and subtracting the
N^{th}
coordinate
\nu_{N}
from both sides of the inequality yields the equivalent set and size given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}C(k,N)&=\left|{\left\{{\bm{{\nu}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}\,\left|\,\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nu_{n}^{2}\leq k^{2}\right.}\right\}}\right|\\
&=\sum_{\nu_{N}=\minus\infty}^{\infty}\left|{\left\{{\bm{{\nu}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N\minus 1}\,\left|\,\sum_{n=1}^{N\minus 1}\nu_{n}^{2}\leq k^{2}-\nu_{N}^{2}\right.}\right\}}\right|\\
&=\sum_{\nu_{N}=\minus\infty}^{\infty}C\left({\sqrt{k^{2}-\nu_{N}^{2}},\,N-1}\right).\end{split}

(4)

The set size therefore has a recurrence relation that integrates over the cardinality of the set of lattice points
\bm{{\nu}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N\minus 1}
in the lower preceding dimensions as shown in Fig.
2
. Computing GCP’s base-case (
N=1
) and higher dimension cases (
N>1
) gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}C(k,N)&=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1+2\left\lfloor{k}\right\rfloor,&N=1\\
\sum\limits_{m=\minus\left\lfloor{k}\right\rfloor}^{\left\lfloor{k}\right\rfloor}C\left({\sqrt{k^{2}-m^{2}},\,N-1}\right),&N>1\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(5)

by which the summation bounds are tightened.

Observe that for integer distances

C\left({\sqrt{q},N}\right)=\hat{C}(q,N)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{C}(q,N)&=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1+2\left\lfloor{\sqrt{q}}\right\rfloor,&N=1\\[2.0pt]
\sum\limits_{m=\minus\left\lfloor{\sqrt{q}}\right\rfloor}^{\left\lfloor{\sqrt{q}}\right\rfloor}\hat{C}\left({{q-m^{2}},N-1}\right),&N>1\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(6)

where
q=k^{2}

\hat{C}(q,N)

\textrm{O}\left({Nk^{2}}\right)
memory and
\textrm{O}\left({Nk^{3}}\right)
compute costs under direct evaluation. We can reduce the computation costs of the summation in Eq. (
6

k
for
N>1
by expressing the recurrence relation in terms of the equivalent geometric convolution operation
\hat{C}_{N}=f*\hat{C}_{N\minus 1}
given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{C}(q,N)&=\sum_{\tau=0}^{k^{2}}f(\tau)\,\hat{C}\left({q-\tau,N-1}\right),\\
f(\tau)&=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1,&\tau=0\\
2,&\tau\in\left\{{n^{2}\,|\,n\in\mathbb{N}}\right\}\\
0,&\textrm{Otherwise}\end{array}\right.,\end{split}

(7)

where
f(\tau)
is a sparse convolution kernel with non-zero values at indices that are perfect squares. Using the Fast Fourier transform (FFT)
\mathcal{F}
with zero-padding to
Nk^{2}
elements, and element-wise product
\odot
operators, the recurrence relation simplifies to multiplication in the frequency domain given as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{F}\left[{{\hat{C}_{N}}}\right]&=\mathcal{F}\left[{{f}}\right]\odot\mathcal{F}\left[{{\hat{C}_{N\minus 1}}}\right]=\mathcal{F}^{N\minus 1}\left[{f}\right]\odot\mathcal{F}\left[{{\hat{C}_{1}}}\right],\end{split}

(8)

whereby

in the frequency domain undergoes element-wise exponentiation, and
\hat{C}_{N}
is recovered via inverse FFT. Thus, the computation costs are further reduced to
\textrm{O}\left({Nk^{2}\log k}\right)
. Sample run-time performances across the methods in Fig.

2.1
GCP with Image-Source Extensions

(9)

2.2
GCP-ISM Forward and Inverse RIR Constructions

For the forward construction of RIR

T_{s}i
from the finite-difference of the GCP-ISM volume function in Eq. (

\displaystyle\begin{split}h(i)&=\frac{\ddot{S}\left({k_{i+1}^{2},\,N}\right)-\ddot{S}\left({k_{i}^{2},\,N}\right)}{k_{i}^{\frac{N\minus 1}{2}}},\hskip 9.24994ptk_{i}=cT_{s}i,\end{split}

(16)

where

is proportional to the sample time and sound speed, and the denominator component normalizes for spherical scattering energy. Most sample distances

are not integers, and the non-uniform distance resolution of values stored in

given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}S^{\left\{{\lambda}\right\}}(\lambda k,N)=\left|{\left\{{\bm{{\nu}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}\,\left|\,\left\|{\lambda\left({\bm{{p}}(\bm{{\nu}})-\bm{{r}}}\right)}\right\|_{2}\leq\lambda k\right.}\right\}}\right|,\end{split}

4
Conclusions

We presented a fast computational method for high-dimensional ISM in integer coordinates
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
that reduces its computational complexity class to
\textrm{O}\left({Nk^{2}\log k}\right)
. Our method extended the classic 2D lattice counting GCP into higher dimensions by expressing sub-solutions across dimensions via recurrence relations and fast geometric convolution. We then extended GCP to ISM, which included coordinate translation, scaling, and weighting to model varied source, receiver locations, and room reflections. RIRs were realized via forward and inverse finite difference methods of a GCP-ISM volume function, which can be made more accurate via coordinate scaling. Later experiments showed the trade-offs between error-reduction and run-time per doubling of coordinate dimensions. We supported frequency-dependent reflections via complex reflection coefficients and RIR assembly via the inverse Fourier transform and time-shifted summation. Experiments showed that RIR in high-dimensions with only positive-real reflection coefficients had excessive echo density, which was addressed via the introduction of phase-inverted reflection coefficients. Long duration and high-dimension RIRs were shown with metrics for posterity.
