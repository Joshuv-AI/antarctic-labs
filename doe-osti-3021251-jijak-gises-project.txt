1.0 Executive Summary
The Match-E-Be-Nash-She-Wish Band of Pottawatomi Indians, d.b.a. Gun Lake Tribe (Gun Lake Tribe/Tribe),
installed a grid connected 69 kW solar photovoltaic array at the Tribe’s Jijak Property located in Hopkins, MI, as
originally planned. The array generated 95,070 kWh in its first year of operation, 7,014 kWh more than
projected. Achieved savings were $12,214 during the first year of production, including $6,022 in generation
credit and $6,192 in offset electrical cost. This array will save the Tribe more than $312,000 with increasing
electric rates during the 20-year life of the system.

Competitive pricing allowed the Tribe to install a second smaller array to power the Jijak Garden. The 4.85 kW
Jijak Garden Array produced 6,644 kWh during its first year of production, 476 kWh more than projected, saving
$1,126 during year one of production (including invoice credits and offset energy consumption). The system is
expected to produce 132,879 kWh and save over $26,000 over 20 years.

The two arrays resulted in 74 kW of solar PV, which produced 100,327 kWh in the first year and offset 85% of
the electrical load of seventeen main campus buildings plus the Garden Office and greenhouse. Savings in the
first year were $13,340 and are expected to be $338,000 over the 20-year life of the arrays.

2.0 Background
The Gun Lake Tribe is a federally recognized Indian Tribe located in southwest Michigan. The Tribe has an
enrolled citizenship of 604 individuals. Tribal lands include 1,007 acres held in trust by the United States
Government for the community. The Tribe holds an additional 677 acres in agricultural, residential, and
industrial lands not held in trust.

The Jijak Gises Project progressed toward goals outlined within the Tribe’s Climate Change Adaptation Plan. The
Tribal Council Strategic Plan includes a long-term goal to enhance infrastructure and strengthen Tribal self-
sufficiency. Prior to the Jijak Gises project, the Tribe completed photovoltaic feasibility studies for four Tribally
owned properties, including the Jijak Property, and installed small solar arrays at the Tribal housing
development and Luella Collins Community Center. The Tribe also retrofitted the government campus and the
Gun Lake Casino Resort with LED lights.

The Tribe’s energy vision stated within the Strategic Energy Plan is, “The Gun Lake Tribe will honor those who
walked before us and the next seven generations by strengthening our sovereignty through self-sufficiency with
thoughtful, responsible energy and conservation practices.”

3.0 Project Objectives
The Match-E-Be-Nash-She-Wish Band of Pottawatomi Indians (Gun Lake Tribe/Tribe) originally proposed to
install approximately 69 kW of solar photovoltaic (PV) at the Tribe’s Jijak property located in Hopkins, MI. This
project was estimated to meet up to 100% of the pre-project electrical consumption of seventeen (17) buildings

4.0 Description of Activities Performed
 • Through a public Request for Proposals (RFP) competitive bid process adhering to the Tribe’s
 procurement policy, Jijak Gises Project, Harvest Solar, was selected as the vendor from three received
 project bids.
 • Due to the cost savings on the Jijak Gises Solar array, funding was available for a second array. An RFP
 for a smaller second array at the Jijak Property was publicly advertised. Only one vendor bid on the
 project and was selected.
 • Necessary project reviews and permits applications were completed including National Historic
 Preservation Act, National Environmental Policy Act, Building, Electrical and Mechanical. Related surveys
 and inspections occurred. When questions arose regarding compliance, questions were raised to and
 answered by appropriate sources.
 • Applications and reviews for the Consumers Energy Distributed Generation program were submitted
 and conducted.
 • The Jijak Gises Solar array, a 142 panel 68.87 kW system was installed.
 • The Jijak Garden solar array, a 10 panel 4.85 kW system was installed.
 • The vendor reviewed each system with Tribal staff and provided the log in information regarding the
 portal to access energy production data.

5.0 Conclusions and Recommendations
This project progressed towards the Tribe’s strategic energy goals and provided proof of concept for larger
photovoltaic systems to power Tribal operations. The systems installed exceeded the energy production
estimate during the first year. Due to increase in use of the property for almost daily events requiring the use of
lighting and other electric use increased post solar array installation.

In conclusion, the Tribe considers the Jijak Gises Project a success. The Tribe is harvesting energy from the sun
and using less electricity from traditional sources. It paved the way for future solar projects, including one that is
currently underway at the Government Campus. The lessons learned along the way improved planning
efficiency and implementation of energy projects.

6.0 Lessons Learned
Obstacle 1: The Tribe modified the path of the electrical line from the panel to the array for one of the systems.
The most efficient route of the electrical line was not as shown in the original proposal which crossed a paved
driveway multiple times and near a potential bat roost tree.
 • Additional time was required to make an inquiry regarding whether the tree could be removed. Due to
 the timeline to get an official response, the Tribe opted to slightly reroute the pathway, though still
 nearby the tree which required more hand digging around the roots.

 • Due to the driveways and the change in pathway from the proposal, the Tribe decided to bore under the
 driveways which required the Tribal Historic Preservation Department (THPO) to be reengaged due to
 additional ground disturbance.
 • The Michigan Utility Notification Center (MISSDIG) does not provide utility information within
 properties, only to the panel. The Tribe needed to contract ground penetrating radar reports along the
 route both for potential utilities lines and to provide the THPO with potential interest areas to monitor.
 • The Tribe’s IT Department recommended hardwiring a line from the array to the internet rather than
 relying on Wi-Fi for communication with the portal as originally proposed.

 Lesson 1: Actions and time that had not been considered in the proposal were necessary to make this
 project successful. In future projects, the Tribe will include the following considerations in the project
 timeline.

Obstacle 2: How to best protect the array from vandalism.
 • Staff considered fencing the system to protect the controls and the actual panels from acts of vandalism.
 Fencing would be obtrusive, potential eyesore and costly. Also, fencing may not stop or prevent vandals.
 • Staff considered physically locking the actual controls, but this would not be allowed for safety reasons –
 affecting the ability to turn off the system from the control box.
 • The vendor had not experienced vandalism of any of their solar installations and relayed that very few
 are fenced.
 • The Tribe has had very frequent use of the Jijak Property and not experienced any vandalism of the solar
 array.
 Lesson 2: Expect people to respect the Tribe’s property. No vandalism was experienced related to the array.

Obstacle 3: The staff taking care of the electric bill are not the staff monitoring the solar array production.
 • Previously, accessing electrical bills involved Environmental Staff emailing Finance Staff, who would then
 locate and return the relevant documents.
 • An efficient process for Environmental Staff to track electricity generation sold to the utility was not in
 place.

 Lesson 3: Shared access to both (1) the online utility billing account, and (2) the online energy dashboard
 (where the data on consumption and electrical generation sold to the utility can be reviewed) is critical.

Obstacle 4: Staff noticed a huge gap between production of the solar array and the generation sold to the utility.
The electrical bills in terms of cost and electric consumption remained mostly similar compared to pre project.
 • The Jijak Garden array was offline for several weeks due to a break in the electrical line during an
 unrelated construction project.
 • Staff plan to complete an energy audit of the property. They expect the increase in consumption to be
 the increased use, rather than inefficiencies.
 Lesson 4: While the solar array produced is much more energy than expected, due to increase use of the
 Jijak Property year-round, lights were on more often and for longer duration, energy consuming electronics
 and appliances were run more frequently consuming increasing the energy use considerably during the pre-
 project period.
