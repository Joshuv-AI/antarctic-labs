The “Tie Stitch”: An Arthroscopic Stich for Long Head of Biceps Tenodesis Associated With Rotator Cuff Repair or as a Superior Capsule Reconstruction. Surgical Step‐by‐Step Technique
Authors: Simone Cerciello, Dario Candura, Lorenzo Proietti, Katia Corona, Michele Mercurio, Fabrizio Mocini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong head of biceps (LHB) tenodesis is a common procedure. Historically, it has been recommended in the setting of rotator cuff reconstruction in young and active patients when the LHB is degenerated or severely inflamed to reduce postoperative pain. More recently, LHB has been proposed as an alternative to fascia lata to reconstruct the superior capsule in young patients with massive and irreparable rotator cuff tears. In such a situation, strong and solid initial fixation of the LHB is mandatory. Several arthroscopic stitches have been described: they should be easy to perform and strong enough to avoid slackening of a degenerated LHB. We describe an arthroscopic stitch for the tenodesis of the LHB that is easy to perform and avoids LHB slackening.

Long head of biceps (LHB) pathology is frequently encountered in patients with rotator cuff tears (RCT). Studies report that its integrity is compromised in 36.1% to 88% of patients who undergo RCT repair. 1 , 2

The two surgical options to address LHB pathology are tenotomy and tenodesis. 3 , 4 , 5 Tenotomy is a simple and reproducible procedure with reduced healing time after surgery and no requirement for specific postoperative rehabilitation program. 6 Tenodesis is a more demanding procedure with longer operative and recovery times but offers some theoretical advantages over tenotomy, including lack of cosmetic problems such as Popeye sign, maintaining supination and elbow flexion power, avoiding cramping pain, and preventing muscle atrophy. 7 Several surgical options have been described including open subpectoral tenodesis, arthroscopic tenodesis at the groove with interference screws, proximal tenodesis with anchors and fixation of the LHB at the interval with sutures. All of them have pros and cons; however, tenodesis should be strong enough to ensure primary resistance of the construct to allow soft tissue healing.

More recently, the interest in LHB has increased since it has also been proposed as an autograft in the treatment of massive RCTs, which is extremely challenging especially in young and active patients. 8 The reconstruction of the superior capsule has been proposed in the past to decrease retear rate of cuff repair in massive tears and to protect the repaired cuff. At the same time, it has been described as a reliable option in irreparable tears to recenter the humeral head in the glenoid fossa and to improve shoulder function. 9 Moreover, a recent systematic review showed that patients undergoing superior capsule reconstruction report better functional outcomes, greater acromiohumeral distance, and a lower infection rate than those undergoing latissimus dorsi tendon transfer. 10 However, some concerns still exist regarding this technique because of its complexity, high cost, potential of graft healing at the glenoid and donor side morbidity. The use of the long head of the biceps tendon (LHB) has emerged as a reliable graft with several potential advantages such as lower cost, less surgical complexity, lower morbidity at the donor site, and greater healing potential and a strong attachment at the glenoid side. With the LHB as a solid graft, the key point of superior capsule reconstruction has become its fixation to the humeral head. The aim of this technical note is to describe an arthroscopic stitch to achieve a secure fixation of the LHB on the humeral head both in case of its tenodesis and in case of superior capsule reconstruction.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Patient Positioning and Preparation

The procedure can either be performed under interscalene block or under general anesthesia or both. The patient is positioned in a beach chair position. The surgical field is prepared with Chlorhexidine, and the arm is placed into a traction of 3 kg.

Arthroscopic Procedure

Drawing of the anatomic landmarks is performed. The posterior soft spot is palpated, and a posterior portal is created. A diagnostic intra‐articular arthroscopy is performed with a 30° arthroscope. An anterior working portal is created under direct visualization with an outside‐in technique using a spinal needle to verify the location of the portal. This portal is just lateral to the coracoid process and enables clear and direct access to all intra articular structures. No cannula is used at this step. A systematic inspection is performed to assess rotator cuff and any additional pathology. A wide release of the rotator interval including sectioning of the middle glenohumeral ligament is performed. The anatomical involvement of the long head of the biceps is assessed by pulling it outside of the groove with a probe. The deep layer of the supra and infraspinatus tendons are checked. If the deep layer is damaged indicating a partial articular‐side tear avulsion (PASTA), a spinal needle is percutaneously inserted in the tear to make the subacromial recognition of the tear easier. If a medium to large size cuff tear is encountered, it is addressed during the subacromial step. However, the preparation of the greater tuberosity with the removal of scar tissue and tendon remnants can be performed with an intra‐articular view, which enables clear visualization of the whole footprint.

Subacromial Step Part 1: Long Head of Biceps Tenodesis in Association With Rotator Cuff Repair

The subacromial investigation always starts at the anterolateral portion just below the coracoacromial ligament, where the subacromial bursa is less abundant with the posterior viewing portal. The subacromial bursa is removed laterally to clearly expose the supra and infraspinatus tendons and preserved medially, at the musculotendinous junction to maintain its blood supply to the cuff. The deep aspect of the coracoacromial ligament is released as a part of the decompression. The viewing portal is then shifted from posterior to lateral and, if necessary, an additional anterosuperolateral portal is established. If a PASTA lesion has been encountered during the intra‐articular inspections, the spinal needle is found, and the situation of the cuff is assessed through a blunt device. This maneuver always confirms the poor quality of the subacromial layer allowing the instrument to penetrate the cuff and reaching the glenohumeral joint. The tear is then completed to obtain a small complete crescent tear. If a complete tear of the cuff has been detected during the intra‐articular inspection, it is addressed at this time. The quality of the tendon, the shape and extension of the tear, and its reduction to the footprint are carefully evaluated. The greater tuberosity is then refreshed with a Burrell burr to enhance the healing potential of the repaired cuff. In case of a small complete tear or a PASTA lesion which has been then completed to a full thickness tear, a 2.8 mm all‐suture anchor with three #2 HI‐FI strands (CONMED, Utica, NY, USA) is placed slightly anterior to the center of the cuff tear with the aid of a spinal needle to choose the perfect location. The repair of the cuff is performed with 3 single stitches. A 7 mm cannula is then placed in the posterior portal. The anterior suture of the anchor is used to perform the LHB tenodesis using the Tie Stitch technique. The biceps is pierced with a penetrator and the suture is retrieved according to the lasso‐loop technique (Figure 1). The free hand of the suture is then pushed medially until the subscapularis tendon passing posteriorly to the LHB with an arthroscopic ring grasper (Figure 2). The grasper is the passed below the two strands of the suture (Figure 3) and over the LHB to reach the subscapularis tendon through the rotator interval (Figure 4). The suture is retrieved laterally (Figure 5) to “tie” around the LHB (Figure 6). The step is repeated a second time to have a double loop around the LHB (Figure 7). The 2 stitches are retrieved from the posterior cannula and tied over the stitch of the anchor with nonsliding half stitches. The tenotomy is performed from the anterior portal under direct visualization (from the lateral portal into the cuff tear to reach the glenohumeral space). The two strands are then retrieved from the anterosuperolateral portal. The anterior part of the RCT is pierced with a penetrator using the anterior portal and one of the strands of the previous tenodesis is retrieved. The posterior part of the cuff is repaired with a simple stitch using a penetrator from the posterior cannula. The middle part is repaired using a dedicated Neviaser portal and an angled‐up penetrator. The 3 stitches are then tied from posterior to anterior. If the tear is medium to large size, an additional posterior anchor is used to repair the posterior part of the supraspinatus and the infraspinatus. A minimal acromioplasty is performed to increase bleeding and potential for cuff healing at the end of the procedure.

Subacromial Step Part 2: Long Head of Biceps Rerouting/Superior Capsule Reconstruction

The procedure has the same intra‐articular steps; however, the subacromial part has some peculiar aspects. The first difference is bursectomy. In massive/irreparable tears, the amount of bursal tissue is less abundant than is case of small cuff tears. Therefore, the necessity for extensive bursectomy is less relevant. Once the visualization of the supra and infraspinatus tendons margins is achieved and the reduction to the footprint is checked, the reconstruction of the superior capsule/LHB rerouting is performed. A gentle freshening of the greater tuberosity is performed with a Burrell burr. An all‐suture anchor is placed on the greater tuberosity, in the center of the cuff tear as lateral as possible to have a wide contact between the LHB and bone. Through the anterosuperolateral portal, 3 Tie Stitches are performed in the LHB from lateral to medial trying to be as lateral as possible. Usually performing the subsequent tenotomy may pose the most lateral stitch at risk of iatrogenic damage from the electrocautery. The risk can be minimized by retrieving the most lateral stitch from the posterior cannula and pulling it. This brings the proximal part of the LHB in the groove more posterior, making the tenotomy from the anterosuperolateral portal much safer. The three stitches from the LHB are tied from lateral to medial alternating the post to have the best compressive effect on the LHB. The reconstruction of the superior capsule with the LHB can be associated with advancement of the infraspinatus (partial repair) on an additional anchor in case of partial tear of its tendon (loss of strength in external rotation 1 but not clear lag sign) or tendon transfer (latissimus dorsi or lower trapezius) in case of its complete tear (clear lag sign in external rotation 1). In these situations, acromioplasty is generally not indicated (Video 1).

The procedure can either be performed under interscalene block or under general anesthesia or both. The patient is positioned in a beach chair position. The surgical field is prepared with Chlorhexidine, and the left arm is placed into a traction of 3 kg. Drawing of the anatomic landmarks is performed. The view is obtained by inserting the arthroscope through the lateral portal. An anterior working portal is created under direct visualization with an outside‐in technique. A 2.8 mm all‐suture anchor with three strands is placed slightly anterior to the center of the cuff tear as laterally as possible. The biceps is pierced with a penetrator and the suture is retrieved according to the lasso‐loop technique. The free hand of the suture is then pushed medially until the subscapularis tendon passing posteriorly to the long head of biceps (LHB) with an arthroscopic ring grasper. The grasper is the passed below the two strands of the suture and over the LHB to reach the subscapularis tendon through the rotator interval. The suture is retrieved laterally to “tie” around the LHB. The step is repeated a second time to have a double loop around the LHB. The second loop is performed more laterally than the first one. In this way, you obtain the “Tie Stitch” knot. The same maneuver is repeated to perform a second “Tie Stitch” knot. Finally, a lasso loop knot is performed between the two previously placed “Tie Stitch” knots. Then, you retrieved the suture from the posterior cannula and pull posteriorly the LHB to performer the tenotomy. The tenotomy of the LHB is performed distal to the most lateral suture point. In the end, the LHB can be seen firmly anchored to the humeral head and the supraglenoid tuberosity of the scapula, ensuring proper centering of the humeral head within the glenoid cavity as a superior capsule. Postoperative Rehabilitation

The rehabilitation protocol widely changes according to the 2 surgical options. If the LHB tenodesis is associated with small to medium size tendon tears, the patient is put in a sling for 4 weeks. The sling is discontinued from the first day to allow passive flexion of the elbow and active motion of the wrist and hand keeping the arm at side. Passive forward elevation begins 15 days after surgery in case of small tears and 4 weeks after surgery in case of medium size tears. The stretching exercises continue until the complete passive elevation is reached. Active elevation with no weightlifting begins once the complete passive elevation is achieved. In case of massive tears or when the superior capsule reconstruction with the LHB is associate with advancement of the infraspinatus, a sling is used for 5 weeks. Passive motion of the elbow and active motion of the wrist and hand with the arm at side begins from day 1. Passive exercises to recover forward elevation begin at 5 weeks and last until the complete elevation is reached; thereafter active elevation begins. When the superior capsule reconstruction with the LHB is associated with tendon transfer (latissimus dorsi or lower trapezius), the protocol is the same as for the massive tears, but the arm is held in 45° abduction sling rather than in a sling. In all the previous conditions, unrestricted weight lifting, demanding activities, and sports are not allowed before 6 months.

DISCUSSION

The LHB is a common source of shoulder pain and pathologic degeneration is routinely encountered in case of RCTs and the larger the RCT the more severe the damage to the LHB. 11 LHB tendinopathy has been recognized as the primary reason for anterior shoulder pain, and in about 40% of patients, relevant anatomic changes are encountered, including tendinitis, tendon wear, SLAP lesions, and partial or complete ruptures of the LHB. 11 , 12 , 13

Therefore, it is widely accepted to address the LHB either with tenotomy or tenodesis is widely recommended. Tenodesis has several advantages over tenotomy but usually requires a dedicated anchor to achieve strong bone to tendon contact. In addition, the technique should have an initial intrinsic strength to avoid the wire to plow into the degenerated tissue of the LHB. The present technique ensures strong LHB fixation through a lasso loop stitch reinforced with a double modified loop around the LHB. This new stich resembles the configuration the traditional “tie,” and therefore, it has been named “Tie Stitch.” Moreover, once the tenodesis has been performed, the same wire can be used to fix the anterior aspect of the supraspinatus tendon. This allows fewer anchors to be used, reducing the economic costs of the repair which is a crucial aspect in several countries (Tables 1 and 2). 14

The potential drawback of superimposition of the repaired anterior supraspinatus over the LHB tenodesis does not seem to be a major issue because the anterior aspect of the supraspinatus, which is in continuity with the posterior pulley, is usually looser and of worse quality and strength than the middle and posterior parts of the supraspinatus tendon.

In case of massive cuff tears in young adults presenting with pain and functional impairment, treatment options include partial/complete cuff repair, tendon transfers, superior capsule reconstruction, and reverse shoulder arthroplasty. Shoulder arthroplasty should be avoided whenever possible. Arthroscopic repair of these massive lesions yields significant initial functional improvement and patient satisfaction even in case of severe fatty infiltration, the rate of structural failures is high, ranging from 20% to 94%. 15 , 16 Superior capsule reconstruction, isolated or in combination with partial rotator cuff repair or tendon transfer aims at recentering the humeral head in the glenoid fossa restoring the lever arm of the deltoid and protecting the partial cuff repair or tendon transfer. 17 , 18 Several techniques based on the LHB have been described. 19

The advantages of our technique include the use of a high‐strength arthroscopic knot that can be employed both for LHB tenodesis, preventing suture cut‐off and for superior capsule reconstruction, ensuring proper centering of the humeral head within the glenoid cavity. Furthermore, the sutures from the same anchor used for the tenodesis can also be utilized for rotator cuff repair, potentially reducing the number of anchors required and, consequently, the overall surgical costs.

In cases of superior capsule reconstruction, using the LHB with the present techniques offers several potential advantages over fascia lata. They include lower costs, no donor site morbidity, and a stronger attachment on the glenoid side compared with the use of an allograft or fascia lata. In addition, when the quality of the LHB is poor, simple or lasso loop stitches may not be strong enough to ensure a strong construct. The present technique with three Tie Stitches should guarantee strong construct even if the LHB is degenerated. There are no real disadvantages of the present technique, except that it is more technically demanding compared with a simple stitch; this may lead to increased operative time, particularly for surgeons with less experience in shoulder arthroscopy.

The triple “Tie Stitch” construct ensures the strong and wide contact of the LHB tendon to the greater tuberosity avoiding possible ploughing of stitches in the degenerated LHB tissue.
