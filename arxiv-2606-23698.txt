FP8 is All You Need (Part 2): Efficient Ozaki–Bailey Style FFT Through Tensor-core Garner Reformulation and Kulisch Escape Route A γ \gamma -Roof Analysis and Four-Floor Codesign Rule for the Post-FP64 Era
Satoshi Matsuoka

Abstract
NVIDIA’s Blackwell Ultra (B300) cuts FP64 vector throughput to ∼ 1.3 \sim 1.3 TFLOPS— ∼ 30 × \sim 30\times below B200 and well below the level at which bandwidth-limited fp64 workloads stay memory-bound. The Ozaki Scheme II framework [ 2 , 3 ] recovers fp64 -equivalent throughput by routing matrix-multiply emulation through fp8 tensor cores; a companion paper [ 1 ] covers dense GEMM, batched GEMV, stencils, and SpMV. This paper adds the canonical missing primitive, the 3-D FFT. We present Ozaki-Bailey FFT , an emulated 3-D FFT via the Bailey six-step decomposition [ 6 ] with both 1-D FFT GEMMs on fp8 tensor cores. Its small Bailey inner dimension k ≈ N k\approx\sqrt{N} makes a third roofline—the γ \gamma -roof of the TME model, reconstruction latency—binding. We split Garner reconstruction into a Phase A ( fp8 tensor cores, ∼ 1 \sim 1 ms for 1024 3 1024^{3} ) and a Phase B reduction, and identify a Kulisch fixed-point accumulator [ 16 ] as a Phase B reformulation whose accumulation is exact —a full 53-bit fp64 -mantissa result, more accurate than naive fp64 summation—running entirely on the integer vector pipe, untouched by the FP64 collapse. Closed-form bandwidth-parity floors follow ( 1.56 1.56 , 8.25 8.25 , 170 ​ B HBM 170\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}} ); B300 misses the native FP64 floor by ∼ 10 × \sim 10\times but meets both Kulisch floors, giving a projected ∼ 18 \sim 18 ms for 1024 3 1024^{3} at full fp64 , essentially the 12.9 12.9 ms memory roof. The figures are model upper bounds, not measurements. A final result targets the title’s one qualifier—the integer pipe, whose presence on B300 is incidental to the AI design pivot and not guaranteed to persist. Assertively , a width-parameterised Kulisch accumulator runs the same exact reduction on any integer pipe of width ≥ 8 \geq 8 bits at no cost (INT16/INT8 both exact); native FP64 is certainly unnecessary. And more aggressively but realistically , reformulating Phase B as a tall-skinny fp8 tensor-core GEMM removes the integer pipe entirely, leaving fp8 tensor cores and HBM as the only silicon a full- fp64 FFT touches—a true and total “ fp8 is all you need.” While this last step is a theoretical upper bound pending implementation, it is a realistic one: an inverse analysis shows it keeps the memory roof as long as the small-inner-dimension tall-skinny GEMM sustains only ∼ 11 \sim 11 – 15 % 15\% efficiency—far below the ∼ 69 % \sim 69\% tile-utilisation ceiling, so even a heavily penalised kernel clears the bar. The title thus reads: fp8 is all you need, certainly with a narrow integer pipe, and—on this evidence—very likely without one, pending implementation. Keywords: FP8 tensor-core emulation; Ozaki–Bailey FFT; tensor-core Garner reformulation; Kulisch accumulator; bandwidth-parity floor; double-precision FFT emulation; NVIDIA Blackwell Ultra (B300); post-FP64 GPU architecture; AI for Science (AI4S).

Status of the results: a model, not a measurement.

We state plainly at the outset what these results are and are not. The
proposed Ozaki-Bailey-Kulisch FFT has
not
been implemented on
the target hardware; the wall-time figures (the
\sim 18
ms headline
included) are derived from operation counts and advertised peak rates,
not from measurement. Every central ingredient remains unvalidated on
real silicon: the
fp8
residue decomposition and the emulated matrix
operations; the fusion of decomposition, Bailey transforms,
reconstruction, and transpositions; the tensor-core formulation of
forward CRT; the five-word Kulisch accumulator with its carry
propagation and final conversion; and—most consequentially—the
assumed overlap of memory traffic, tensor work, and integer-vector
work, together with the achievable occupancy and instruction
throughput. Accordingly, where this paper says a path “is projected
to reach,” “would, under the stated assumptions, reach,” or “might
reach” the memory roof, those conditional phrasings are deliberate and
load-bearing: the roofline analysis establishes an
upper bound

on achievable performance, and which fraction of it a real kernel
attains is exactly what the measurement programme of
§
8
is designed to determine. Like Part 1, this is a
codesign argument about what the hardware makes possible, not a report
of delivered performance.

On “precision” and “accuracy”.

We use these terms in their standard, distinct senses, and the
distinction matters for what is and is not claimed.
Precision
is
a property of the number format—the
fp64
format carries a 53-bit
mantissa—whereas
accuracy
is a property of a computed result,
namely its closeness to the exact value. The Kulisch Phase B is

exact
in a precise and provable sense: the accumulation
introduces no rounding error whatsoever, with a single correctly-rounded
integer
\to
fp64
conversion at readout (this is a property of the
fixed-point accumulator, independent of any hardware claim). The output
is therefore an ordinary
fp64
number—it carries the full 53-bit
mantissa, no more and no less—computed to an accuracy strictly better
than naive
fp64
summation, which loses bits to the
\sim S\approx 11

intermediate roundings the exact accumulator avoids. When we write
“full
fp64
” we mean exactly this: an output of full
fp64

mantissa width whose error is bounded by the single final rounding,
hence at least as accurate as, and generically more accurate than, a
naive
fp64
evaluation of the same reduction. We do
not
claim
more-than-
fp64
precision (the result is an
fp64
value), and we
reserve “
fp64
-equivalent” for the throughput of the emulated path
(its rate matches what a native
fp64
unit would deliver), never for
its accuracy. We have aimed to use this vocabulary consistently
throughout; the Phase B accuracy analysis is in
Appendix
C

3.1
Native Roofline

For a kernel performing
W

fp64
flops on
Q
bytes of memory
traffic, with operational intensity
\mathrm{OI}=W/Q
, the
native (un-emulated) wall time is bounded below by

T_{\text{nat}}=\max\!\left(\frac{W}{P_{\mathrm{fp64}}},\ \frac{Q}{B_{\mathrm{HBM}}}\right)+L_{\text{mem}},

(2)

where
P_{\mathrm{fp64}}
is the peak native
fp64
throughput and

L_{\text{mem}}
is fixed kernel-launch and latency overhead. The
ridge point of the Roofline lies at

\mathrm{OI}=P_{\mathrm{fp64}}/B_{\mathrm{HBM}}
; below it the kernel is
memory-bound, above it compute-bound.

3.2
Emulated Roofline: definitions of
\alpha
,
\beta
,

\gamma

Under Ozaki-II emulation the same fp64-equivalent work is performed,
but on
fp8
/
int8
tensor cores at peak throughput

P_{\mathrm{low}}\gg P_{\mathrm{fp64}}

model parameterises the overhead by three multipliers:

T_{\text{emu}}=\max\!\left(\frac{\alpha W}{P_{\mathrm{low}}},\ \frac{\beta Q}{B_{\mathrm{HBM}}}\right)+\gamma\,n_{\text{out}}.

(3)

Concretely:

•

: the number of

fp8

(3r+1)

fp8

4.5
Classical exact-accumulation schemes for Phase B

The Phase B reduction is, per output, a sum of
S
small
non-negative integers each scaled by a fixed positional weight:

\text{result}=(s_{A}s_{B})^{-1}\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}P_{s}\cdot 256^{s}
,
with
\log_{2}P_{s}\lesssim 20
bits. This is the canonical
multiply-accumulate-with-positional-weights structure studied
extensively in the floating-point summation literature. We surveyed
the classical options, focusing on their fit to B300 where the FP64
vector pipe is the binding bottleneck. The full survey is in
Appendix
C
; the qualitative finding is direct:

All classical methods other than Kulisch trade speed
against precision in the wrong direction.

Kahan, Neumaier, and
Ogita-Rump-Oishi Sum2/SumK compensation all run on the same FP64
vector pipe with
4
–
6\times
overhead and so are strictly slower
than naive fp64 sum on B300 (without gaining precision-vs-cost on a
collapsed pipe). Double-double on fp32 substrate yields
\sim 48

bits at
\sim 30
ms—fast but not full fp64. Quad-double on fp32
yields
\sim 72
bits at higher cost than full fp64 needs.
Reproducible BLAS (ReproBLAS) uses a bin-based signed-integer scheme
but still bottlenecks on the fp64 vector pipe for its bin floats.
Tall-skinny structured DGEMV on cuBLAS does not invoke tensor cores
for fp64 reductions on Blackwell/Rubin. None of these routes Phase B
around the FP64 vector collapse.

The Kulisch route.

Kulisch’s complete-arithmetic
proposal
[
16

17

maintains a wide fixed-point
register that accumulates products of floating-point values

exactly

fp64

clean. Recall the per-output reduction:

y=(s_{A}s_{B})^{-1}\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}P_{s}\cdot 256^{s},\qquad P_{s}\in\mathbb{Z},\ \log_{2}P_{s}\lesssim 20\text{ bits},\quad S=11.

Each term

is an INT32 value shifted to a known bit
position—specifically bit
8s

A

No floating-point arithmetic

\to

Two things to verify.

Implementation considerations.

Three practical notes about CUDA realisation:

•

Register pressure.
The 5 INT32 accumulator registers are
held thread-local. Combined with the inputs
P_{s}
(which can stream
in from shared memory, not be register-resident), this fits within
the per-thread register budget on Blackwell SMs without spilling.

•

Warp-level reduction.
If multiple threads in a warp
compute outputs sharing the same accumulator (e.g., when several
outputs are reduced across a Bailey GEMM column), the per-thread
160-bit accumulators must be combined via a 5-word

warp.reduce.add
, costing
\sim 25
ops per warp once per
output. This overhead is included in the 50–70% efficiency
estimate above.

•

Double-buffering for overlap.
Because Phase B uses
INT32 SIMT and HBM traffic uses neither the FP64 nor INT32 vector
pipe, the Phase B reduction of one tile can be scheduled concurrently
with the HBM load of the next tile. In the ideal-overlap limit
analysed in Appendix
D.2
, this halves the effective
Phase B wall time and the corresponding sub-floor relaxes from

8.25\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}
to
4.125\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}
—comfortably within B300’s INT32
spec rather than just at its margin.

fp8

tensor cores—so a future GPU that narrowed or dropped INT32 would
not close the escape route. Hence the cautious
architectural statement: the native parity floor remains the safe
procurement target, and Kulisch is a credible rescue for
already-deployed sub-floor architectures but should not be used to
justify further FP64 cuts on future generations.

4.6
Bandwidth-parity floors

The wall-time picture motivates a clean architectural-design rule.
The Ozaki-Bailey-Kulisch path activates three independent compute
pipes:
fp8
tensor cores (Phase A), the
fp64
vector pipe
(naive Phase B, if used), and the INT32 SIMT pipe (Kulisch Phase B).
Each pipe carries its own bandwidth-parity floor below which it
becomes the binding bottleneck. The native FP64 path provides a
fourth, independent floor on the
fp64
vector pipe.

For memory-roof FFT parity at full
fp64
, a GPU must satisfy

either
: (i) the native FP64 floor alone (no emulation
needed),
or
(ii) both the
fp8
tensor-core floor (so that
Phase A doesn’t bottleneck)
and
the Kulisch INT32 sub-floor
(so that Phase B doesn’t bottleneck), which together enable the
Ozaki-Bailey-Kulisch path. We derive each floor in turn.

Floor 1: Native FFT parity (FP64 vector).

Setting the native FFT compute-bound time
T_{\text{nat}}=W/\eta

equal to the memory-roof time
T_{\text{mem}}=Q/B_{\mathrm{HBM}}
and solving
for

\boxed{\;\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\mathrm{native}}=\mathrm{OI}_{\mathrm{FFT}}\cdot B_{\mathrm{HBM}}\approx 1.56\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}.\;}

(15)

Floor 2: Naive Ozaki-Bailey Phase B parity
(FP64 vector, informational).

For the Ozaki-Bailey FFT kernel with the naive fp64 Phase B reduction:

\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\mathrm{naive\text{-}OBFFT}}=\frac{3S}{16}\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}\approx 2.06\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}\quad(r=12,\ S=11).

(16)

This is strictly stronger than (
15

demands less FP64-vector work per byte than Ozaki-Bailey FFT Phase B does. At
any

its strictly higher Phase B demand is moot.
Equation (
16

threshold at which the naive Ozaki path
also
reaches the memory
roof—useful for sanity-checking architectures but not the binding
floor.

Floor 3: Kulisch Phase B sub-floor (INT32 SIMT pipe).

For the Kulisch fixed-point Phase B of §
4.5

18N^{3}\cdot c\,S

\boxed{\;\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\mathrm{Kulisch}}=\frac{3cS}{16}\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}\approx 8.25\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}\quad(c\!=\!4,\ S\!=\!11).\;}

The full derivation is in Appendix

4.7
Architectural implications

Three implications follow:

1.

B300 is the architectural outlier, with a software
escape route.
The 1.3 TFLOPS FP64 vector spec is
\sim 10\times

below the native-FFT parity floor of 12.5 TF at 8 TB/s. Among
naive reductions, no kernel engineering recovers the memory roof
at full
fp64
. However, the Kulisch fixed-point Phase B of
§
4.5
routes the reduction onto the
abundant INT32 SIMT pipe and projects to
\sim 18
ms at full

fp64
precision—near memory-roof parity. This rescue depends
on engineering quality (50–70% INT32 SIMT efficiency assumed)
and is not yet in any production library, but it suggests B300
need not be a dead end for FFT-heavy codes if the kernel work is
done.

2.

Rubin is at native FFT parity by design or by
coincidence.
The 33 TFLOPS FP64 vector spec at 22 TB/s sits 4%
below the 34.3 TF native floor—essentially at parity. For
spectral codes, Rubin is balanced; Ozaki-II provides no further
speedup for FFT (and indeed
\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}^{Ozaki-BaileyFFT}=57.75

Rubin’s 33 TF, so the Ozaki-Bailey FFT path would be
\sim 1.8\times
slower
than native by Phase B bound).

3.

The FP64 floor for future GPUs is set by FFT, not by
HPL.
Designers tempted to cut FP64 vector silicon further on
post-Rubin generations should treat
\eta\geq 1.56\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}
as a
hard floor. The Kulisch escape route exists today because
INT32 SIMT survived the FP64 collapse—but it does not depend on
INT32 specifically: §
5
shows it runs equally on any

\geq 8
bits (INT16/INT8, exact), and
can in principle be carried on the
fp8

not necessarily close the route. The safe

\eta\geq 1.56\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}

plus
an
integer-vector pipe of width
\geq 8
bits at
\gtrsim 8\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}
as
the fallback—no longer specifically INT32 (see §
5

for the substrate-width relaxation and the
fp8
-only route).

5
FP8 as the Sole Primitive: Eliminating the Integer Pipe

The results so far carry one qualifier, and it is the qualifier on the
title. The Ozaki-Bailey-Kulisch path is projected to reach the memory
roof at full
fp64
accuracy on a
fp64
-collapsed GPU, but Phase B
routes its per-output
reduction onto the INT32 SIMT pipe, so the parity analysis of
§
4.6
demands an INT32 vector throughput of at least

8.25\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}
. This is the
single
place in the entire post-FP64
stack—across Phase A tensor work, Phase B reduction, and BLAS-1
compensation—where a
non-tensor, non-FP8
hardware capability
is required.

We should be candid that the INT32 escape route works on B300 by

accident
, not by design. B300 retains a wide INT32 SIMT pipe
not because anyone provisioned it for double-precision science, but
because INT32 happens to remain useful for address arithmetic,
indexing, and integer-heavy AI preprocessing; its throughput relative
to FP64—which makes the Kulisch rescue viable—is an incidental
by-product of the AI-driven design pivot, not a guaranteed property of
the architecture class. The next turn of the same pivot could narrow
it as readily as it narrowed FP64: an inference-oriented processor has
no structural reason to keep INT32 wide, and there is no guarantee that
future silicon will. A rescue that leans on an accidental
throughput ratio is fragile. What is needed, therefore, is not a better
exploitation of INT32 but a Phase B formulation that does
not
depend on the presence of any particular integer pipe
—ideally none
at all. As stated so far the thesis is not quite “
fp8

is all you need” but “
fp8
plus a healthy INT32 vector pipe is all
you need”; this section removes that contingency, so the title’s
robustness does not rest on an accident of the current generation.
Discharging this qualifier is therefore not a footnote—it is what
determines whether the title is literally true, and whether the
architectural recommendation is robust against the next turn of the
AI-hardware ratchet.

This section establishes that the qualifier can be discharged, in two
escalating steps. First (§
5.2
) we show the integer
requirement is not specifically about
INT32
: a
width-parameterised Kulisch accumulator runs the exact same reduction
on any narrow integer pipe—INT16 at a slight net gain, INT8 at
break-even—so the demand relaxes to “some integer-vector pipe,”
a far weaker and more future-proof condition. Second
(§
5.3
) we show the integer pipe can be removed

entirely
: by reformulating Phase B itself as a tall-skinny

fp8
tensor-core GEMM, the reduction moves onto the same FP8 tensor
units that already carry Phase A, leaving a machine of
fp8
tensor
cores and HBM as the only silicon the whole
fp64
FFT touches. That
is the literal, unqualified content of the title: a
true and
total
“
fp8
is all you need.”

We are explicit about the epistemic status, because the two steps
differ in it. The integer-width relaxation is robust and largely
hardware-independent—it is exact arithmetic re-tiled across lanes,
and INT16/INT8 vector pipes are ubiquitous. The FP8-tensor elimination
is, by contrast, a
theoretical upper bound
: it is correct on the
throughput accounting, but its realisation is
highly dependent on
the hardware implementation of the tensor cores
—above all on how
efficiently a given tensor core executes the small-inner-dimension
tall-skinny GEMM the reformulation produces. As in Part 1

[
1
]
and throughout this paper, we establish the
upper bound here and hand the efficiency constant to the measurement
programme of §
8
. The claim is that “
fp8
is all you
need” is achievable in principle with no native FP64 and no integer
vector pipe whatsoever—and that confirming it on real silicon is now
a concrete, bounded engineering question rather than an open conceptual
one.

5.1
The accumulator is substrate-agnostic: a width-parameterised
sub-floor

The Kulisch accumulator is a fixed-point bin; nothing in its definition
requires the bin to be tiled by 32-bit words. The running sum of
§
4.5
occupies
\approx 104
bits and is currently
held in
5\times
INT32 words. It can equally be held in
w
-bit words for
any
w
, provided (i) enough words are used to span the
104
-bit range
and (ii) carry propagation between words is handled. This is the
classical
reduced-radix
(redundant) representation long used in
multiprecision and modular arithmetic on SIMD units: a wide integer is
stored as an array of words each carrying fewer than
w

so carries accumulate in the spare guard bits and explicit
carry-propagation is deferred to the end
[
34
]
. The
Kulisch accumulator in a high-radix carry-save form has been realised on
GPUs before, for reproducible
summation
[
33

32
]
; what is new here is
the deliberate use of word width as a

on a pipe
whose width is at a premium.

The lever is the following identity. A vector datapath of fixed bit

16

at INT16,

at INT8.

, but the
104

20
-bit slice payload

spans more words on deposit. Writing

for the guard bits
reserved per word and

for the usable radix, the
width-parameterised Kulisch sub-floor generalises (

)
to

\boxed{\;\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\text{Kulisch}}(w)=c(w)\cdot\mathrm{OI}_{\text{red}}\cdot B_{\mathrm{HBM}},\qquad c(w)\approx\frac{S\,\lceil b_{P}/\rho\rceil+\lceil W_{\text{acc}}/\rho\rceil}{V/w},\;}

(19)

where

is the slice payload width,
W_{\text{acc}}\approx 104
the accumulator width,

the slice count, and the numerator
counts word-level operations per output (deposit

INT32 floor at

—and hence the achievable

5.2
INT16 and INT8: narrowing the integer word

Two strategies present themselves, and they differ sharply.

Strategy A: keep the moduli, split slices across narrow words.

Retain the existing large (
\sim\!28

\approx 20
-bit slices, and simply deposit each slice

\lceil b_{P}/\rho\rceil

32/w
while the per-slice deposit work rises by

\lceil b_{P}/\rho\rceil

Shrink the moduli so each slice payload fits a single

5.3
FP8: ruling out vector arithmetic, and the tall-skinny
tensor escape

FP8 must be treated separately from the integer substrates, because the
mechanism that makes Kulisch exact—accumulation with no rounding until
readout—is incompatible with FP8
floating-point
arithmetic.

FP8 vector arithmetic is ruled out.

E4M3 carries a
3{+}1
-bit significand. Every FP8 add rounds to four
significant bits, so a literal fixed-point accumulation in FP8 is a
contradiction in terms. Even error-compensated summation
(Kahan/2Sum/Dekker) recovers at best of order twice the significand,

\sim\!8
bits, against the
53
required. No amount of compensation
closes a
45
-bit gap; FP8 vector arithmetic cannot carry Phase B.

FP8 as an exact integer carrier: the tall-skinny tensor
route.

There is, however, a second way to use FP8 that sidesteps the FP8 adder
entirely. E4M3 represents integers up to
\pm 448

exactly
—this
is the very property Phase A already exploits to run modular GEMMs on FP8
tensor cores. We can apply it again in Phase B by reformulating the
per-output reduction itself as a matrix multiply and accumulating in the

tensor core’s
FP32 accumulator rather than on the integer vector
pipe. Writing the reduction
y_{i}=\sum_{s}P_{is}\,2^{8s}
as a
contraction over the slice index
s
, the slices
P_{is}
become the
inner (contraction) dimension of a GEMM whose outer dimension is the
(very large) output count. Because the positional weights
2^{8s}
span

80
bits, the contraction is split into
\lceil 104/24\rceil\approx 5

positional
bands
, each accumulated within the
24
-bit-safe range
of an FP32 tensor accumulator, with a second-order error-free transform
stitching the band boundaries so no bits are lost where bands meet.

This route is structurally a
tall-skinny GEMM
: the contraction
depth is only
S=11
, well below the native MMA
k
-dimension of
16
, so
each band runs at
\sim\!11/16\approx 69\%
tile utilisation with a

31\%
padding waste, and the five bands multiply the work. The net
Phase B tensor cost is on the order of
0.3
–
0.4\times
the Phase A
tensor cost—a real but bounded addition. Crucially, it

eliminates the integer vector pipe from Phase B altogether
, at
the price of
dual-using the FP8 tensor cores
: Phase A and
Phase B now contend for the same silicon. The correct accounting is
therefore not that Phase B is free, but that the FP8 tensor-core floor

rises
to carry both phases. Adding the
0.3
–
0.4\times

Phase B load to the
170\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}
Phase A floor gives a combined
dual-use FP8 floor of

\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\texttt{fp8},\text{dual}}\;\approx\;(1.3\text{--}1.4)\times 170\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}\;\approx\;220\text{--}240\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}\quad(\sim 1.8\text{--}1.9~\text{PFLOPS at }8~\text{TB/s}),

(20)

replacing the
8.25\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}
INT32 sub-floor of §
4.6
with
a higher
FP8
demand rather than a vector-pipe demand. B300’s

\sim 5
PFLOPS dense FP8 still clears this raised floor with

\sim\!2.6
–
2.8\times
margin (down from the
3.7\times
Phase-A-only
margin, but comfortably positive). The FP8-only path is thus feasible
on the throughput accounting
after
charging Phase B against the
same FP8 budget—the dual-use cost is real but does not break the
floor on B300.

This is an upper bound, and it is tensor-core-dependent.

The throughput accounting above is exactly that: an upper bound. Whether
it is
achievable
hinges on how efficiently a given tensor core
executes an inner-dimension-
11
tall-skinny GEMM—a regime tensor
cores are not optimised for, where realised efficiency can fall well
below the
69\%
tile-utilisation figure once memory-staging and
small-
k
launch overheads are included. It also requires that the FP8
tensor path expose its FP32 accumulator with the integer-exactness
guarantees Phase A relies on, and that the band-boundary error-free
transform add no rounding. These are precisely the hardware-characteristic
questions that only measurement settles. We therefore present the

Parallel FP32 Analysis

Many spectral scientific codes operate at FP32 precision, either
because the underlying physics tolerates it (turbulence
simulations using fp32 for the bulk and fp64 for sensitive

6.1

The FFT operation count and memory traffic are precision-independent

and

, respectively); only the

(8 B per complex element vs. 16 B), and the OI is therefore the
same as in fp64 if one counts “flops” uniformly. Concretely:

(23)

identical to FP64 native. The architectural data is shown in

Ozaki-II for FP32

An Ozaki-II variant targeting fp32 precision requires roughly

6.3
The FP32 floor for future architectures

The FP32 floor at 8 TB/s is 12.5 TF (native) / 18 TF (Ozaki). At

FP32 vector—a

floor, which B300 has dropped below natively (rescued in software by
Kulisch Phase B) and which Rubin reaches within 4%, the FP32 floor
is comfortably above current spec—so an architect could plausibly
trade FP32 vector area for additional AI4S tensor area on a future
generation while keeping spectral codes memory-bound. The natural
target for any post-Rubin GPU intended to serve mixed FP64/FP32
spectral codes is therefore
\eta_{\text{fp32}}\geq 1.56\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}
and

\eta_{\text{fp64-vec}}\geq 1.56\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}
, with

\eta_{\text{int32-vec}}\geq 8.25\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}
as the Kulisch fallback
in case the FP64 vector is further reduced.

7.3
Limitations

Several caveats apply:

•

The Phase B bound assumes the per-output reduction has no
useful tensor-core mapping at small inner dimension
r=12
. This
is true for naive matrix-vector formulations but could in
principle be circumvented by recursive Ozaki-II of Phase B
itself, which would introduce its own (smaller)
\gamma

We have not investigated this.

•

The double-double Phase B path of §
4.4
is
projected from arithmetic op counts but has not been measured. Its
actual achievable performance depends on FP32 vector pipeline
efficiency under the dependency pattern of DD additions, which can
degrade throughput by up to
2\times
in some realisations.

•

The Phase A formulation requires that the slicing GEMM be
fully fused into the surrounding Bailey kernel; if Phase A
materialises slice results to shared memory, the bandwidth
multiplier
\beta
rises and the memory roof shifts. We assume

\beta=1
throughout, consistent with the discipline of

[
1
]

established by measurement rather than a settled constant; the
register-fusion realism discussion of §
3

(

set by tile shape and register budget, with the
fixed shared-memory DFT
32
matrix making the FFT case more
favourable than streaming GEMM) and the delivered-

measurement of §
8
qualify this assumption directly.

•

The precision results in Table

GEMMs; cumulative
twiddle round-off across

Bailey stages may behave
differently at production sizes. Real-hardware measurement is

•

The Tensor–Memory Equilibrium model of

\gamma

The principal architectural results are three closed-form

\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\text{FP64}}\approx 1.56\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}
is the binding one: at

The Kulisch sub-floor

A fourth result—the strongest—then strips the escape route of its

Appendix A
Derivation of the FP64 Bandwidth-Parity Formula

We derive the parity formula
\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}=\mathrm{OI}\cdot B_{\mathrm{HBM}}

and its FFT-specific instantiation
\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}\approx 1.56\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}

(native) and
\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}\approx 2.06\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}
(Ozaki-Bailey FFT,
r=12
).

Setup.

Consider a 3-D FFT of size
N^{3}
with
N=pq
(Bailey factorisation
with
p\approx q\approx\sqrt{N}
). The total work in complex-flop
units is

W_{\text{FFT}}=3\cdot 5N^{3}\log_{2}N

(25)

flops (counted as multiply-adds), where the factor of 3 accounts
for the three axes and the factor of 5 is the standard FFT
flop count per butterfly (1 complex mul + 2 complex adds expanded
as 6 real flops, conventionally rounded to 5 per Cooley–Tukey
convention).

The total HBM traffic is

Q_{\text{FFT}}=6N^{3}\cdot 16\text{ bytes}=96N^{3}\text{ bytes},

(26)

counting one read and one write per axis (3 axes), each of
N^{3}

complex-fp64 elements (16 bytes per element).

Hence the operational intensity is

\mathrm{OI}_{\text{FFT}}=\frac{W_{\text{FFT}}}{Q_{\text{FFT}}}=\frac{15N^{3}\log_{2}N}{96N^{3}}=\frac{15\log_{2}N}{96}.

(27)

For
N=1024
,
\log_{2}N=10
, giving

\mathrm{OI}_{\text{FFT}}=150/96=1.5625
FLOPS/byte,
which we round to
1.56
in the main text.

Native parity.

The native execution time is bounded below by the larger of the
compute-bound and memory-bound times:

T_{\text{nat}}=\max\!\left(\frac{W_{\text{FFT}}}{\eta},\ \frac{Q_{\text{FFT}}}{B_{\mathrm{HBM}}}\right).

(28)

Setting these equal gives the parity point:

\frac{W_{\text{FFT}}}{\eta}=\frac{Q_{\text{FFT}}}{B_{\mathrm{HBM}}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\eta=\frac{W_{\text{FFT}}}{Q_{\text{FFT}}}\cdot B_{\mathrm{HBM}}=\mathrm{OI}\cdot B_{\mathrm{HBM}}.

(29)

Thus
\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\text{nat}}=1.56\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}
.
This is the binding
floor.

Ozaki-Bailey FFT parity (Phase B-bound).

For the Ozaki-Bailey FFT kernel, Phase A on tensor cores is fast enough
(
\sim 1
ms for
1024^{3}
) to be ignored. The Phase B time is

Appendix B
The Bernstein Fractional CRT Reconstruction

The forward CRT formula (
11
) can be written in
fractional form. Let
w_{k}=y_{k}/m_{k}\in[0,1)
. Then

\sum_{k}v^{\prime}_{k}\cdot u_{k}=M\cdot\sum_{k}v^{\prime}_{k}\cdot w_{k}

(34)

and hence

C\equiv\sum_{k}v^{\prime}_{k}\cdot u_{k}\pmod{M}=M\cdot\left(\sum_{k}v^{\prime}_{k}\cdot w_{k}\bmod 1\right).

(35)

The sum
s=\sum_{k}v^{\prime}_{k}\cdot w_{k}\in[0,r)
has bounded range,
so its fractional part
f=s-\lfloor s\rfloor\in[0,1)
can
be computed in fp arithmetic. The reconstructed value is

C/(s_{A}s_{B})=(M/(s_{A}s_{B}))\cdot f.

(36)

Precision analysis.

With
r=12
moduli of size
\sim 128
,
M\approx 2^{112}
and

M/(s_{A}s_{B})\approx 64
(since
s_{A}s_{B}\approx 2^{106}
). The
fractional part
f
must therefore be accurate to

\sim 1/M\cdot s_{A}s_{B}\approx 2^{-6}
relative precision for the
result to be fp64-accurate.

The sum
s\in[0,r)=[0,16)
has dynamic range
\log_{2}r=4

bits at the top. In fp64 (53-bit mantissa),
s
is computed to
relative precision
r\cdot 2^{-53}\approx 2^{-49}
, i.e.,
absolute precision
\sim 2^{-49}
. Hence
f
has absolute
precision
2^{-49}
and the final result has absolute precision

64\cdot 2^{-49}\approx 2^{-43}
, equivalent to
\sim 43
-bit
precision—about 10 bits below fp64.

The discrepancy with the recursive Garner’s
\sim 47
-bit
empirical precision (relative error
\sim 10^{-14}
) is exactly
the
\sim 9
-bit loss measured in Table
4
.

In fp32+Kahan compensated sum.

With fp32 (24-bit mantissa),
s
has relative precision
r\cdot 2^{-24}\approx 2^{-20}
. Kahan compensation gives
\sim 2^{-44}
for
benign sums, but the fractional-part extraction amplifies the
absolute error by
M/(s_{A}s_{B})\approx 64
, yielding result
precision
\sim 2^{-38}\approx 10^{-12}
in the best case. The
measured
10^{-3}
in Table

indicates
non-benign accumulation (terms are not aligned in magnitude),
giving only
\sim 14
-bit precision. Iterative refinement closes

\sim 3\times
runtime.

D.1.2

is elsewhere

A natural expectation is that CG, GMRES, and other Krylov solvers
benefit from Kulisch because they involve dot products. They typically
do not. In a CG iteration the cost is dominated by one SpMV (which is
memory-bound for typical sparse matrices) plus two dot products

The SpMV

implemented as banded GEMV with

become FP64-vector-bound, in which case the Kulisch rescue applies:
this is the “bandwidth-bound multi-term stencils” case in

The earlier draft of this paper double-counted by listing “iterative

D.2.3
The overlap loosening

If Kulisch Phase B on the INT32 SIMT pipe can be scheduled
concurrently with the HBM traffic for the next tile—which uses
neither the FP64 nor the INT32 vector pipe—the effective time
becomes
\max(T_{\text{Kulisch}},T_{\text{memory}})
rather than

T_{\text{Kulisch}}+T_{\text{memory}}
. In the perfect-overlap
limit, the Kulisch sub-floor (
37
) loosens by
a factor of 2:

\eta_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\text{Kulisch, overlap}}\approx\frac{c\,W}{2\,Q_{\text{out}}}\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}\approx 4.125\,B_{\mathrm{HBM}}\quad(\text{Ozaki-Bailey case}).

(39)

At
B_{\mathrm{HBM}}=8
TB/s this drops the INT32 requirement from

\sim 66
TOPS to
\sim 33
TOPS—well within B300’s
\sim 75
TOPS
spec, with margin.

The achievable overlap factor depends on the kernel’s ability to
double-buffer the Phase B accumulator across tiles. For the
Ozaki-Bailey kernel this is feasible because Phase A (tensor-core
GEMMs) and Phase B (INT32 reductions) use disjoint hardware pipes and
disjoint register-bank classes. The benchmark of
§
8
(1) measures the achievable factor empirically;
the prediction is
1.5\times
–
2\times
realised overlap, leaving
B300 comfortably above the relaxed Kulisch sub-floor for the
Ozaki-Bailey case.

For algorithms beyond Ozaki-Bailey: fully data-parallel reductions
(per-row
spmv
, batched quadrature) should achieve
near-perfect overlap; algorithms with sequential dependency chains
(Horner polynomial evaluation, recursive sums) will not. The overlap
loosening therefore applies to most of the
Table
5
beneficiaries but not uniformly.

D.3
Implementation notes for libKulisch

This subsection documents the implementation specifics referenced
in §
5.6
.

D.3.1
Library components

libKulisch
offers four components:

1.

A templated wide-accumulator primitive parameterised on output
bit-width (typically 128–256 bits, packed into 4–8 INT32 words) and
on the source operand format (INT8/INT16/INT32, with compile-time
positional weights).

2.

Warp-level shuffle primitives for carry propagation across
word boundaries, optimised for 32-thread warps (Blackwell) and
for the wider warps expected on the next generation.

3.

Drop-in replacements for the per-output reduction loops in
GEMMul8
[
2
]
, cuBLAS Ozaki, SpMV libraries (cuSPARSE,
PETSc), and ReproBLAS—matching the beneficiary list of
Table
5
.

4.

A benchmark harness measuring wall time and achieved INT32
throughput across
B_{\mathrm{HBM}}
-bound and
\eta_{\text{INT32}}
-bound
regimes, validating (
37
) empirically.

The goal of
libKulisch
is to deliver an empirical answer to a
single question: within the narrowed beneficiary class of
§
5.6
, how often does Kulisch actually provide
meaningful end-to-end speedup, and how often is the predicted Amdahl
fraction
f
accurate to within a factor of 2? The model predicts

2
–
10\times
end-to-end speedup across the five beneficiary classes
of Table
5
; empirical confirmation would
establish Kulisch as a standard tool in the post-FP64-collapse
arsenal, complementary to the tensor-core Phase A acceleration that
already serves the high-OI half of the Ozaki-II family.

D.3.2
Caveats and limitations

Three implementation caveats apply:

1.

Kulisch requires bounded dynamic range. Reductions with truly
unbounded inputs (e.g., quantum-chemistry codes with extreme condition
numbers) require either application-level rescaling or adaptive-precision
extensions
[
21
]
.

2.

The wide accumulator consumes register pressure (
\sim 5
INT32
registers per output for the Ozaki-Bailey case). Kernels already at
the register-pressure limit may need to spill, degrading throughput.
The benchmark harness reports per-kernel register use to flag
spill-prone cases.

3.

The achievable overlap factor (Appendix
D.2
) depends
on the surrounding kernel’s dependency structure; algorithms with
sequential reductions cannot benefit from the factor-of-2 sub-floor
loosening. This is automatically detected by the benchmark harness.
