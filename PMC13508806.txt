Early Detection of Parkinson’s Disease Using Automatic Classification of Single-photon Emission Computed Tomography Images
Authors: Jihad Boucherouite, Abdelilah Jilbab, Atman Jbari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesParkinson’s disease (PD) is a chronic neurodegenerative disorder characterized by central nervous system dysfunction. Early identification may enable prompt treatment and help slow the progression of disabling symptoms. Previous studies have reported that clinical assessments based on visual interpretation may be insufficiently accurate and often miss early PD. Therefore, this study aimed to develop an automated single-photon emission computed tomography-based model for binary classification of healthy controls and patients with early PD.MethodsWe analyzed 514 DaTSCAN images from the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative database, using one unique scan per individual. The workflow comprised three main stages: image processing; computation of 23 features, including radial, threshold, boundary, and striatal binding ratio features; and image classification using machine learning algorithms.ResultsThe medium Gaussian support vector machine achieved an accuracy of 97.09% ± 1.53% (95% confidence interval, 95.11%–99.07%), sensitivity of 98.18% ± 1.48%, specificity of 93.85% ± 3.44%, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 98.49% ± 1.31%. This performance was significantly higher than that of the three-dimensional convolutional neural network baseline model (accuracy, 94.55% ± 2.98%; p = 0.045), while requiring substantially less training time.ConclusionsIn this dataset, carefully designed feature engineering combined with classical machine learning outperformed the deep-learning baseline when the training data were limited and the selected features aligned with clinical diagnostic criteria. This approach achieved high accuracy for early PD detection and may provide computational efficiency and interpretability suitable for clinical implementation.Keywords: Parkinson Disease, Tomography, Early Diagnosis, Image Processing, Computer-Assisted, Support Vector Machine

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a chronic neurodegenerative disorder that affects the central nervous system. Its symptoms include tremor, bradykinesia, freezing of gait, postural instability, postural deformity, and rigidity [1]. PD primarily involves degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the striatum, including the caudate and putamen, resulting in dopamine depletion [2]. Brain imaging modalities used in PD assessment include magnetic resonance imaging, functional magnetic resonance imaging, positron emission tomography, and single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) [3]. Positron emission tomography and SPECT are particularly useful for detecting PD at an early stage [4]. SPECT is commonly used in European clinics for early PD diagnosis [2] and can help distinguish PD from drug-induced parkinsonism, multiple system atrophy, essential tremor, and progressive supranuclear palsy [5]. However, early-stage PD remains difficult to diagnose and requires specialized expertise. Moreover, the accuracy of clinical diagnosis has remained limited over the past 25 years, with pooled accuracy not exceeding 82.7% [6].

Various machine learning (ML) approaches have been explored for early PD detection [7], and automated image analysis methods may support more objective diagnosis. Image processing of brain scans is a promising approach because it enables biomarkers to be extracted directly from SPECT images. Staff et al. [8] and Palumbo et al. [9] classified DaTSCAN images as PD or healthy control (HC) by segmenting the striatum, applying shape analysis, and using probabilistic neural networks, classification trees, Naive Bayes methods, or singular value decomposition of voxels in the striatal region; these approaches achieved accuracy of up to 94.8%. Castillo-Barnes et al. [10] used striatal voxel intensity values as support vector machine (SVM) classifier features and obtained an accuracy of 97.04%. Segovia et al. [11] analyzed the right and left striatum separately using partial least squares to evaluate the maximum information encoded in the striatal region, achieving an accuracy of 94.7%. Several studies have also used feature engineering approaches, in which manually or semi-automatically extracted features provide interpretable markers of striatal degeneration. Prashanth et al. [12] used striatal binding ratios (SBRs) for both caudate nuclei and putamina and incorporated shapebased features as inputs to a diagnostic model, whereas Bhalchandra et al. [13] proposed radial and gradient features. In recent years, deep-learning models have also been applied to DaTSCAN SPECT imaging [14,15]. These models enable automated feature extraction from raw images but typically require larger datasets and offer limited interpretability. Feature engineering, by contrast, preserves explicit clinical meaning while requiring fewer samples; therefore, the present work sought to retain interpretability while automating the feature extraction pipeline.

Building on our previous work [16], this study aimed to develop an automated SPECT image analysis model to support early objective diagnosis of PD, reduce observer variability, and aid clinical decision-making. Using SPECT images from the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) database, the workflow comprised image processing; extraction of radial, threshold, boundary, and SBR features; and ML classification.

Dataset Image Preprocessing

The PPMI database [17] includes patients with PD from different countries and at different stages of disease. The dataset used in this study comprised 514 unique individuals categorized into two groups: 385 patients with PD (251 male and 134 female) and 129 HCs (89 male and 40 female). Because the database contains longitudinal data with multiple scans per participant, one unique scan per individual was used to avoid data leakage [18]. The demographic distribution is presented in Table 1. The three-dimensional (3D) SPECT scans first underwent preprocessing of the raw projection data. Chang 0 attenuation correction was applied, followed by a standard 3D Gaussian filter with a radius of 6.0 mm. Final normalization ensured that all scans were anatomically aligned with the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard coordinate system [19]. The preprocessed SPECT images consisted of 91 two-dimensional slices, each measuring 109 × 91 pixels. Slices 39–42 were selected because the putamina and caudate nuclei were clearly visible in this range; the slice with the largest aggregated striatal surface area was then retained for further analysis. This selection was based on methods used in previous studies [16,20], careful examination of the striatal region, and Society of Nuclear Medicine (SNM) recommendations, which specify (i) use of at least three consecutive slices in the region of interest and (ii) consistency in slice selection.

Image Processing

Bhalchandra et al. [13] transformed SPECT images into binary images using a fixed threshold in the range of 0.8–0.9, selected by observing the highest activity in the caudate nuclei and putamina. The effectiveness of these preprocessing steps was evaluated by radiologists and neurologists. Prashanth et al. [12] used binarization with a fixed threshold selected through experimentation and observation of high-uptake regions. Rumman et al. [20] segmented SPECT images using unsharp masking, adaptive thresholding (0.52–0.71), and edge detection with a sequential grassfire algorithm. In the present study, several image-processing procedures were applied to improve visual quality and facilitate subsequent analysis. The first procedure was designed to reduce noise, enhance structural detail, and emphasize the striatal region. Each slice was first converted from RGB to grayscale, resized by a factor of two to increase spatial clarity, and sharpened using a Gaussian kernel with a width of 4 and a strength of 2. In this study, λ was set to 2 because preliminary experiments indicated that this value provided the most stable and visually consistent enhancement. z(n, m) is the correction signal computed as the output of a linear high-pass filter.

The second procedure used thresholding to segment the striatum from the extracted slices and select the appropriate slice for each patient. The main challenge in grayscale images is determining a threshold that enables slice segmentation and subsequent calculation of radial and boundary features. Previous studies used several approaches: one study relied on an expert physician to localize the striatal region [19], another used the average of bimodal image intensity values from the histogram as the threshold [22], and two studies selected a value from a predetermined range based on a fixed standard deviation [12,13]. In this study, a fully automated process was developed using a global thresholding algorithm that converges on the interclass difference between the main region and the background [23]. This approach is based on images with a bimodally distributed histogram and produces a personalized threshold, T. Figure 2 shows the histogram of a PD SPECT image. The initial threshold, T, was selected by calculating the weighted mean intensity of all pixels, after which the image was segmented using T. This process divided the image pixels into two regions. F1 comprised pixels with grayscale values >T, and F2 consisted of pixels with grayscale values ≤T. Average intensity was then calculated for both regions. The threshold T was updated as the mean of both averages, as shown in Equation (2), and the process was repeated until the threshold value no longer changed.

The third procedure applied Canny edge detection [13], a de facto method for detecting object boundaries in images based on intensity gradients, to extract shape-descriptive features.

Feature Extraction and Classification Models

For each SPECT image, three types of features were extracted: boundary features (10-dimensional), radial features (8-dimensional), and SBR values (4-dimensional). The threshold feature T (1-dimensional), extracted during image processing, was then added, resulting in a 23-dimensional feature vector.

1) Boundary features

Boundary features were computed separately for the left and right striatal regions. These features included area, circularity, major axis length, equivalent diameter, and perimeter, yielding 10 values in total (5 features × 2 sides). The features were calculated using the following equations:

In addition to boundary features, radial features were extracted to capture shape irregularities in the striatal regions [24,13]. The data file contained a four-dimensional vector representing the corresponding SBR measures. SBR values for all PPMI images were extracted from the PPMI database. In this study, each SBR measurement was assigned to the closest available SPECT image, minimizing the temporal difference between the two acquisitions. To ensure temporal consistency between imaging data and SBR measurements, the time difference was consistently quantified as Δt = |AgeSBR – AgeScan | in years. The median Δt was 0.10 years, or approximately 36 days; 95.9% of scans were paired within 0.25 years, or approximately 3 months, and 99.6% were paired within 1 year. These values support the temporal proximity of the assigned SBRs and imaging sessions, reducing potential bias due to longitudinal disease progression. Combining quantitative SBR measures with imaging data for early PD detection is consistent with established practices in the field [12,25]. Because the striatal dopaminergic signal declines by approximately 5%–12% annually in early PD [26], the expected SBR change within the median temporal window of 36 days, with 95.9% of pairs within 3 months, is <3%, which is within measurement precision and supports the validity of this strategy.

4) Model classification

This study implemented 24 classifiers using the classification learner application, including SVM, naive Bayes, logistic regression, decision tree, discriminant analysis, k-nearest neighbor (KNN), and ensemble classifiers. Table 2 provides an overview of the hyperparameter settings for the best-performing classifiers. A 3D convolutional neural network (CNN) was also implemented, comprising three convolutional blocks with progressively increasing feature maps (8 → 16 → 32 filters). Each block contained a 3 × 3 × 3 convolutional layer, rectified linear unit activation, and 2 × 2 × 2 max-pooling with a stride of 2. The model was trained using the Adam optimizer with cross-entropy loss for 50 epochs per fold. All experiments used five-fold cross-validation and were executed on an Intel Core i7-10750H CPU at 2.60 GHz without graphics processing unit acceleration.

Table 5 presents a performance comparison across 24 classical ML classifiers and the 3D CNN, with all metrics reported as mean ± standard deviation across folds. The medium Gaussian SVM achieved the highest performance, with an accuracy of 97.09% ± 1.53% (95% confidence interval: 95.11%–99.07%), sensitivity of 98.18% ± 1.48%, specificity of 93.85% ± 3.44%, and an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 98.49% ± 1.31%. The low standard deviation indicated consistent performance across folds. The misclassification cost was 15, the prediction speed was approximately 27,000 observations/second, and the training time was 2.73 seconds. The linear SVM obtained an accuracy of 96.30% ± 0.81%, whereas the cosine KNN and cubic KNN achieved accuracies of 96.69% ± 1.49% and 96.30% ± 0.83%, respectively. Ensemble methods, particularly the RUSBoosted trees ensemble (96.30% ± 0.81%) and bagged trees ensemble (95.91% ± 2.51%), also demonstrated strong performance.

To compare this approach with a representative deep learning method, a 3D CNN was implemented to operate directly on the full volumetric SPECT scans. The model achieved an accuracy of 94.55% ± 2.98%, sensitivity of 96.90% ± 3.00%, specificity of 87.60% ± 8.30%, and AUC of 98.80% ± 0.70%. Although the 3D CNN achieved competitive overall performance and a similarly high area under the curve, several performance differences were observed (Figure 5). Its accuracy varied more across folds than that of the medium Gaussian SVM, as reflected by the higher standard deviation (2.98% vs. 1.53%). Its specificity was also lower and less stable (87.60% ± 8.30% vs. 93.85% ± 3.44%). Training time was considerably longer, at 206 minutes. A paired t-test applied to fold-wise accuracies showed that the medium Gaussian SVM significantly outperformed the 3D CNN (p = 0.045), suggesting a consistent advantage for the classical model despite the use of a modern deep learning architecture.

Discussion

This study presents an automated SPECT-based classification system for early PD detection that integrates image processing, feature extraction, and ML. The approach combined boundary, radial, and SBR features with adaptive global thresholding and achieved diagnostic accuracy of 97.09%, which was higher than the 94.55% accuracy of the 3D CNN baseline. Compared with the 3D CNN, the feature engineering approach produced higher and more stable accuracy, required fewer computational resources, remained robust with limited training data, and provided greater interpretability through clinically meaningful anatomical and functional features.

This approach performed comparably to previously reported feature engineering and deep-learning methods. Prashanth et al. [12] manually selected the segmentation threshold for each patient and obtained an accuracy of 97.29% using SVM classifiers; the mean ± standard deviation values of the selected thresholds for HC and PD were 0.63 ± 0.04 and 0.69 ± 0.05, respectively. In a related study, Prashanth et al. [27] used only SBR features in SVM classifiers with a radial basis function kernel and obtained an accuracy of 96%. The present model achieved numerically higher performance than several existing methods that used other techniques, including CNNs [28], feed-forward neural networks [29], and artificial neural networks [20]. Magesh et al. [30] reported 95.2% accuracy using a VGG16-based model with transfer learning and incorporated local interpretable model-agnostic explanations to enhance interpretability. However, these studies used different datasets, database sizes, PD stages, and classification methods, which limits direct comparison.

Future research will prioritize external validation using independent, multi-institutional datasets to assess generalizability across diverse clinical settings. The classification framework will also be extended from binary to multiclass classification to distinguish HCs, patients with Hoehn and Yahr stage 1 PD, and patients with Hoehn and Yahr stage 2 PD, thereby enabling disease severity assessment. In addition, future work will incorporate scans without evidence of dopaminergic deficit in patients with a clinical diagnosis of PD. Complementary feature types will also be explored to improve diagnostic accuracy.
