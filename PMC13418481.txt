Jasmonic acid and engineered dog manure biochar improve salinity tolerance in Phaseolus vulgaris through coordinated hormonal, antioxidant, and root responses
Authors: Ghulam Murtaza, Muhammad Usman, Zeeshan Ahmed, Mohammed S Alotaibi, Asqer S Aloteeb, Sajad Ali, Sajid Ullah, Rashid Iqbal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalinity is a major abiotic stress that disrupts plant growth by altering hormone balance, oxidative homeostasis, and root development. However, the combined effects of jasmonic acid and engineered biochar on these responses remain poorly understood. A two-year glasshouse pot experiment was conducted during the 2023 and 2024 growing seasons using a randomized complete block design with four replications to investigate the effects of JA (0, 1, and 2 mM) and dog-manure-derived biochar (raw, hydrochloric acid-modified, and hydrogen peroxide-modified) on the morphological, root anatomical, physiological, biochemical, and hormonal responses of Phaseolus vulgaris under three salinity levels (0, 3, and 6 dS m−1 NaCl). Salinity significantly inhibited shoot and root growth, root cortical parenchyma and vascular cylinder development, relative water content, and endogenous indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), while promoting oxidative stress. In contrast, JA and biochar jointly improved salinity acclimation, as shown by changes in endogenous ABA, IAA, and ACC concentrations, antioxidant enzyme activities, oxidative damage markers, osmotic adjustment, and root traits. Under saline conditions, biochar treatments reduced the ACC and ABA concentrations while increasing the IAA and the IAA to ABA and IAA to ACC ratios, indicating a shift in hormone balance associated with improved root growth and plant water status. The combined treatments also strengthened the antioxidant capacity through increased catalase activity and reduced oxidative damage. Root anatomical traits remained largely unaffected by biochar under non-saline conditions, suggesting that biochar-induced structural remodeling primarily occurred during stress adaptation. The strongest osmoprotective response was observed with raw biochar plus 2 mM JA under moderate salinity, whereas hydrochloric acid-modified biochar combined with 1 mM JA produced the greatest catalase activity. To our knowledge, this study provides the first integrated evaluation of dog manure-derived biochar and JA across hormonal, antioxidant, oxidative, and root anatomical, and growth responses in salt-stressed common bean. These findings show that the combined treatments are associated with coordinated changes in hormone balance, antioxidant capacity, oxidative damage, and root traits, while direct signaling mechanisms require molecular and pathway-based validation. These findings identify a potential treatment approach for improving salinity tolerance in P. vulgaris under controlled glasshouse conditions and provide a basis for future field evaluation.Keywords: jasmonic acid, hormone balance, oxidative homeostasis, antioxidant defense, salinity tolerance, reactive oxygen species, root plasticity

Introduction

Phaseolus vulgaris is a crucial legume crop that accounts for 85% of the worldwide bean production. 1 It is cultivated on a large scale globally and yields an annual production of 27 million metric tons across 29 million hectares. 2 Soil salinity is a major obstacle that greatly reduces its productivity, as found by Zhang et al. 3 Salinity of the soil is a widespread agricultural issue that has a detrimental effect on the growth and yield of several crops. 4 It impacts more than 6% of the Earth's total land area and 30% of the land that is suitable for farming. Furthermore, the situation is anticipated to worsen due to global warming caused by climate change. It is projected that by 2050, soil salinization will result in the depletion of over 50% of arable land, resulting in an approximate yearly financial loss of $12 billion for farmers. 5

Addressing the effects of salinity is an urgent issue to protect food security for the increasing world population. Acquiring knowledge about salinity stress, its influence on plant functions, and methods to alleviate its consequences is of utmost importance. 6 Numerous crops cultivated in saline environments can suffer from osmotic stress, which frequently results in insufficient nutrient supply. 7 Salt stress decreases the soil's water potential, impairs the ability of plants to absorb water, and leads to ionic toxicity and hyperosmotic stress as a result of increased levels of chloride and sodium ions. 8 Plants respond to salinity stress by enhancing osmolytes such as soluble carbohydrates and proline in their vacuoles and cytoplasm, partitioning sodium ions in vacuoles, and exporting hazardous ions. 9 Under conditions of elevated salt levels, plants experience an increase in the accumulation of proline. In response, plants activate both non-enzymatic and enzymatic antioxidant systems to neutralize ROS and mitigate oxidative damage. 10 Plants require the enhanced activity of antioxidant enzymes and elevated levels of non-enzymatic chemicals to effectively endure stress. 11 Antioxidant enzymes such as peroxidases and catalase play a crucial role in eliminating ROS. 12 Oxidative stress is commonly indicated by the peroxidation of unsaturated fatty acids, which results in the generation of malondialdehyde. 13 In their study, Chen et al. 14 discovered that soil salinity leads to an elevation in the activity of glutathione reductase, ascorbate peroxidase, superoxide dismutase, and catalase in common bean leaves. Additionally, they detected a rise in malondialdehyde levels. Liu et al. 15 found that salinity causes membrane and oxidative damage, increasing malondialdehyde levels. Malviya et al. 16 and Zhou et al. 17 observed that soil salinity diminishes the growth of plants and impacts various aspects, including dry and fresh root and shoot weight, carotenoids and chlorophyll, as well as potassium concentration. Additionally, it increases hydrogen peroxide, sodium levels, proline levels, and antioxidant enzymes such as catalase, superoxide dismutase, and peroxidase.

In recent decades, jasmonic acid has become recognized as a crucial phytohormone in response to adverse conditions and the growth of plants. 18 Jasmonic acid is a class of hormone molecules that regulate both the growth and defense mechanisms of plants. 19 Jasmonic acid is a stress hormone that regulates how plants respond to various environmental stressors, including heavy metal toxicity, temperature changes, drought, and salinity. 20 Extensive research has demonstrated the functional effects of jasmonic acid during that period. Moreover, recent findings in the field of plant physiology have revealed that plant growth hormones and regulators, including jasmonic acid, have provided a significant new understanding of the signalling pathways employed by plants when faced with adverse environmental conditions. 21 Jasmonic acid plays a key role in a wide range of biological and cellular processes, including molecular, physiological, and biochemical pathways. It has a substantial impact on resisting phytopathogenic bacteria and insect outbreaks, as well as on different stages of plant growth. 22 Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that the exogenous administration of jasmonic acid can be efficacious in promoting the germination of seeds in the presence of salinity, and it enhances plant growth in non-saline and saline conditions. 23 In addition, jasmonic acid can effectively combat abiotic stressors in plants by regulating several phytohormones. It is also effective in scavenging free radicals, facilitating nutrient uptake, maintaining osmotic balance, regulating mineral homeostasis, and promoting the formation of suitable secondary metabolites. 24

Biochar is a carbonaceous and porous substance created by heating biomass, including plant and agricultural residues or woody biomass, to regulate oxygen-deprived conditions, resulting in thermal decomposition. Pyrolysis is a process in which biomass is heated to different temperatures without oxygen, which prevents complete combustion. 25 Biochar has emerged as a highly effective substance for mitigating several stresses, including salinity and drought stress, in recent years. 26 One widely recognized method for mitigating the harmful effects of salt stress on agricultural plants, as stated by Sultan et al. 27 Biochar raises soil pH, enhancing the absorption of vital minerals such as calcium, magnesium, iron, zinc, manganese, and potassium. It also enhances the soil porosity, organic matter level, and aggregate stability while reducing the levels of toxic ions. As a result, plant growth and productivity are increased. 28 According to reports, biochar can adsorb several noxious ions, including sodium and chlorine, owing to its large surface area and cation exchange capacity. This leads to a reduction in the negative impacts of salt stress. 29 This substance has a positive impact on the soil by improving its microbial activity through increased water retention and aggregate stability. As a result, there is a higher release of dissolved organic nitrogen and carbon from the roots while also reducing salt stress. 30 In addition, the biochar used in acidic soils aids in maintaining pH levels while also improving nutrient retention and cation exchange. This ultimately fosters the stimulation of growth-enhancing the growth of plants by boosting soil microorganisms, such as arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi and plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria. 31

Furthermore, the application of biochar decreases the formation of malondialdehyde and reactive oxygen species while enhancing plant development in saline soils through the acceleration of photosynthesis and improvement of plant water relations. 32 Biochar enhances the efficacy of the antioxidant system of plants by augmenting the availability of various vital components. 33 In the research conducted by Eghlima et al. 34 , it was found that biochar had a substantial impact on reducing the accumulation of hydrogen peroxide, proline, peroxidase, and malondialdehyde in Nigella sativa. Biochar enhances CEC and SOC in saline soils. It improves the water retention capacity, availability of nutrients, soil aeration, and porosity. Additionally, biochar facilitates soluble salt leaching by improving water movement in soil. It also creates a favorable environment for soil microbial communities, thereby mitigating the adverse effects of salt stress on soil biological properties. 35 Moreover, the utilization of biochar enhances the levels of unsaturated fatty acids, leading to enhanced membrane stability. It also enhances the effectiveness of glutathione reductase, superoxide dismutase, catalase, and ascorbate peroxidase, which safeguard plants against oxidative damage caused by salinity. 36

The individual effects of different biochars and plant growth regulators, such as salicylic acid, along with other materials, have been studied on salt stress mitigation in P. vulgaris crops. Before this study, there was no existing data on the combined impact of dog poop-derived biochar and jasmonic acid on reducing soil salinity in agricultural systems and even P. vulgaris plants. This study evaluated the individual and combined effects of dog manure-derived biochar and foliar JA on the growth, physiological, biochemical, hormonal, root anatomical, and grain yield responses of P. vulgaris exposed to salinity under controlled glasshouse conditions. These findings provide a basis for subsequent validation under field conditions. We hypothesize that the application of jasmonic acid and dog-poop-derived biochar, both combined and individually, can alleviate salt stress in P. vulgaris. In addition, this study attempted to show improved plant productivity and soil health, providing important new information on sustainable farming practices for managing salinity in semi-arid areas. Salinity limits plant performance through interconnected osmotic, ionic, and oxidative effects. Excess soluble salts lower the soil water potential, restrict water uptake, and can cause ion toxicity. Plants may respond through osmotic adjustment, antioxidant defense, hormone regulation, and changes in root development. The present study focused on these measurable physiological, biochemical, hormonal, and root responses. Individual sodium, chloride, and potassium concentrations and photosynthetic traits were not assessed.

Material and methods

A two-year glasshouse pot experiment was conducted during the 2023 and 2024 growing seasons. The experiment followed a randomized complete block design with four replications for each treatment combination. The P. vulgaris seeds, specifically the white star variety, were obtained from the Regional Agricultural Research Institute, Bahawalpur, Pakistan. In the first year, the seeds were soaked on the 13th of April, while in the second year, they were soaked on April 29th. The seeds were sown in pots, with each pot comprising 12 plants and 10 kg of loamy sand soil (equal to the weight of the soil when it was dry). Three different levels of Jasmonic acid (JA) were examined: JA0, JA1, and JA2, which were supplied at concentrations of 0 mM/L, 1 mM/L, and 2 mM/L, respectively. In addition, dog poop-biochar was utilized at four different levels: without biochar (control), with biochar (DMB, 2.6% per soil weight), modified biochar with hydrochloric acid (HCl, 1.30% per soil weight), and with hydrogen peroxide-modified biochar (HP, 1.30% per soil weight). Salinity stress (SS) was induced through the use of sodium chloride (NaCl) at three different levels: SS0 (control, without salinity), SS3 (3 dS m−1), and SS6 (6 dS m−1), respectively. The SALTCALC program was used to determine and implement the target salinity level. The soil's electrical conductivity in the pots was measured using a conductivity meter to control the salinity. Plastic covers were employed to inhibit salt leaching and facilitate its return to the pots. The salt treatment was administered in two phases: at the initial planting and once the seedlings had become fully developed in the soil. Foliar application of jasmonic acid was conducted at two specific periods of the plant's growth cycle: early flowering and late flowering, at predefined levels. The temperatures glasshouse was maintained within the range of 19 °C to 24 °C, with a relative humidity of 50%. The glasshouse was exposed to natural light conditions and followed a day/night cycle of 14 h of day and 10 h of night. The biochar utilized in the study was derived from a dog-poop that underwent pyrolysis at 450 °C. The biochar derived from dog poop exhibited the following properties: volatile matter content of 17.9%, ash content of 7.51%, CEC of 39.94 cmol kg−1, a specific surface area of 432.11 m2 g, as well as WHC of 49%. In terms of the particle size distribution, 56% were between 0 and 0.5 mm, 19% measured between 0.5 and 2 mm, and 18.9% measured between 2 and 5 mm. The modified/treated biochar was created by adding 10 g of biochar to a solution containing 100 mL of hydrochloric acid and hydrogen peroxide. After the mixture had been thoroughly mixed, it was agitated for 24 h. The biochar was separated from the solution using a centrifuge and subsequently rinsed three times with DI water. The mixture was subjected to a drying process at a temperature of 60 °C and thereafter placed in airtight containers for storage. The soil utilized in the experiment was obtained from the University Research Centre located in Bahawalpur, Pakistan. The soil samples were dried in air and then passed through a 2-mm sieve. After that, they were thoroughly mixed, and their properties were measured using the standard methods described by Page and Keeney 37 and Lindsay and Norvell 38 (Tables 1–3).

Anatomical and morphological studies

After being sown at the full flowering stage, the plants were harvested for anatomical and morphological determination. The samples were preserved by placing them in 0.2-mL centrifuge tubes and covering them with liquid paraffin to prevent evaporation. The tubes were stored at a temperature of 4°C for 72 h. The total measurements of root length, area, diameter, and volume were determined by scanning the roots and subsequently analyzing the images using WinRHIZO Pro software developed by Regent Instruments in Quebec, Canada. Root fragments from the absorption area, located 1.5 cm from the tip, were collected for anatomical study. The tissues were preserved in a solution of acetic acid, water, ethanol, and formaldehyde for 4‒6 d. After that, they were dehydrated using butyl alcohol and then embedded in paraffin. The Cell-o-Tape V 0.7.7 program (Center for Plant Integrative Biology, University of Nottingham, UK) was used to examine digital images obtained from the absorption area. The software was used to assess the areas of the cortical parenchyma (CP) and vascular cylinder (VC). The root density was determined by dividing the weight of the roots by the volume they occupied.

Over the flowering stage, the fresh weights of the roots and leaves were measured. ACC, ABA, and IAA content

The levels of endogenous ABA and IAA were measured using the ELISA technique 39 The root samples (1 g fresh weight) were subjected to extraction in 10 mL of 80% methanol at a low temperature of 4°C in the absence of light for 1 d. Butyl-hydroxy-toluene was added to the extraction mixture. After preparation, the samples were subjected to centrifugation at a force of 7000 times the acceleration due to gravity for 15 min. The resulting liquid above the sediment was collected and treated with Sep-Pak C18 cartridges to remove color. The methanol was eliminated using a rotary evaporator at a temperature of 40°C. The remaining substance from each sample was then dissolved in a buffer containing 1 mM Tris, 1 mM magnesium chloride, 150 mM sodium chloride, 0.1% Tween 20, and 0.1% gelatin. The concentrations of ABA and IAA were quantified using the ELISA method. 39 The antibodies and antigens were acquired from Remark Chemicals, located in Lahore, Pakistan. To measure the ACC concentration in the root, 0.5 g of root samples were blended in 70% ethanol and then subjected to centrifugation for 30 min at a force of 2500 times the acceleration due to gravity at a temperature of 4°C. The ethanol was removed through evaporation under vacuum at a temperature of 40°C. The remaining substance was then diluted in 1 mL of water and made ready for the ACC test, following the procedure described by Concepcion et al. 40 using a gas chromatograph (CP-3800 Varian, California, USA).

Quantification of proline levels

The concentration of proline in fresh leaves was quantified following the methodology described by Bates et al. 41 Concisely, a 0.5 g freeze-dried sample was pulverized using liquid nitrogen and subsequently placed into a 15 ml test tube. A 3.3 ml solution containing 3% sulfosalicylic acid was added to each tube. The mixture was then centrifuged for 15 min at 300 rpm. 4 mL of toluene was added after 2 mL of glacial acetic acid and ninhydrin. An ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer was utilized to quantify the absorbance at a wavelength of 520 nm. The proline level was determined as milligrams per gram of fresh weight using a standard curve. The concentration of proline was quantified in the filtered samples 48 h after filtration with filter paper.

Membrane lipid peroxidation quantification (MDA concentrations)

The quantification of lipid peroxidation was conducted using the methodology described in a recent study by Kanwal et al. 42 A spectrophotometer that operates in the ultraviolet and visible range was employed to quantify the absorbance of the extracted solutions at wavelengths 532 nm and 600 nm. Subsequently, the concentration of MDA was quantified in μM g−1 FW, employing a molar extinction value of 155 × 105 mM−1 cm−1. Using the methodology outlined by Han et al. 43 , we quantified total soluble sugar accumulation. In brief, 0.2 g of freeze-dried powdered leaf was combined with three milliliters of anthrone solution, which consisted of 150 mg of anthrone and 100 ml of 72% H2SO4. The solution was subjected to boiling, and thereafter, the samples were analyzed at a specific wavelength of 625 nm. Subsequently, the standard curve was employed to determine the precise quantity of soluble sugar.

Peroxidase and catalase

At first, plant tissue was removed to ascertain its enzymatic activity. A cooled mortar completely dissolves 50 milligrams of plant tissue in two milliliters of monosodium phosphate buffer with a pH of 8.6. The mixture was thereafter subjected to centrifugation at 4 °C and 15,000 rpm for 10 min. The uppermost stratum of the acquired extract was gathered and examined for enzymatic activity measurements utilizing a spectrophotometer. The spectrophotometer determines peroxidase and catalase activity by measuring the absorption of light. 44

100 µL of enzyme extract and 100 µL of 15 mM H2O2 were added to 8.2 mL of 50 mM NaH2PO4 buffer (pH 8.6). 8.2 mL of a 50-millimolar solution of NaH2PO4 buffer with a pH of 8.6, 100 mL of enzyme extract, and 100 microliters of a 15-millimolar solution of H2O2 were added. The reaction mixture was supplemented with H2O2 to initiate the activation of the enzymes. The blank sample consisted of 100 microliters of enzyme extract dissolved in 9.2 mL of 50 mM NaH2PO4 buffer with a pH of 8.6. The activity of the enzyme was determined by quantifying the reduction in light absorption at a wavelength of 240 nm within a time frame of 15–75 s after the addition of H2O2 45 .

Peroxidase

To assess the enzymatic activity of peroxidase, a solution containing 25 mM NaH2PO4 buffer (pH 8.5), 20 mM C7H8O2 (100 μL), an extract of enzyme (10 μL), and 40 mL of H2O2 (100 mL) was prepared and added. H2O2 was added to the reaction mixture to activate the enzymatic activity. A blank sample solution was created by diluting a 25 mM NaH2PO4 buffer with 20 mM C7H8O2 in a 100 mL extract enzyme. The time interval of 15 to 75 s following the addition of H2O2 was employed to quantify the rise in light absorption at 470 nm, which was then utilized to determine the enzyme activity. 46

Polyphenol oxidase

The initial stage in determining the concentration of H2O2 was extraction. A total of 50 mg of powdered plant tissue were completely dissolved in 1.5 mL of a phosphate buffer solution with a concentration of 50 millimolars and a pH value of 6.8. The buffer solution also contained 1 millimolar of H3NO. The resultant mixture was thereafter subjected to centrifugation at a speed of 6000 rpm for 20 min at a temperature of 4°C. The top layer of the extract was utilized for the quantification of H2O2 47 . To determine the concentration of H2O2, 900 mL of extract was mixed with 300 mL of a reagent containing 1% TicC2 in 20% H2SO4. The mixture was then subjected to centrifugation at a temperature of 4°C for 10 min. The quantity of H2O2 was determined by measuring the amount of light absorbed at a wavelength of 410 nm, using the molar absorption coefficient of 28,000 mol− 1 cm−1μ for the TiO2 complex. Enzyme activities were determined by calculating the rate of variation in absorbance per min/kg of protein, as described by Mirzaei et al. 48

Yield of grain

After the experimental research, the grain yield of P. vulgaris was determined by weighing all grains in P. vulgaris plants from each treatment and replication, and the results were reported in grams per plant.

Statistical analyses

The study utilized a four-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to examine the effects of different treatments, such as salt stress, jasmonic acid, and biochar, as well as the time (measured in years), on the biochemical and physiological characteristics and the grain yield. The analysis was conducted using SAS software version 9.4. In addition, the mean data comparison was conducted by Duncan's multiple range tests, and the results were visualized with Excel software.

Results

Root growth

The salinity had adverse effect on the diameter, length, dry weight, and density of P. vulgaris roots, as evidenced by the significantly decreased levels of these parameters observed under moderate and higher levels of salinity. However, the specific root length showed an increase under similar conditions (Table 3). The application of biochar at 3 and 6 dS m−1 significantly improved the root length, density, diameter, dry weight, and specific root length of the treated plants. The utilization of biochar did not demonstrate a noteworthy impact on the density and diameter of roots in non-saline environments. However, it did enhance the length of the roots, as well as the dry weight and specific length of the roots. Under salinity conditions, all the biochar treatments exhibited a comparable impact on the growth of roots.

Root anatomy

Table 4 shows that the level of salt rise decreased the total area, CP area, VC area, and VC/CP of P. vulgaris roots. Our findings indicate that the use of biochar enhanced the morphological features of P. vulgaris roots when exposed to 3 and 6 dSm−1 salinity levels. However, the biochar treatments did not affect these parameters in without-saline conditions. The area of the CP was expanded only through the application of biochar; however, this expansion occurred only in the presence of high salt stress. Furthermore, the addition of jasmonic acid with biochar treatment did not result in any changes to the anatomical parameters of P. vulgaris roots.

Shoot weight, shoot/root ratio, and RWC

Plants exposed to salt stress showed a decrease in shoot dry weight (Figure S1). When applied to moderately and severely salinized soils, biochar enhanced shoot dry weight (DW); however, in non-saline circumstances, it had no discernible effect. Both biochar applications and salt stress had an impact on the shoot/root ratio; salinity led to a considerable drop in this ratio. Similarly, in non-saline environments, biochar application did not alter the shoot/root ratio. However, the shoot-to-root ratio was noticeably enhanced under 3 and 6 dSm−1 in response to biochar treatment. There was no significant difference observed in the P. vulgaris shoot dry weight and the shoot/root ratio when comparing the biochar and jasmonic acid usage. The relative water content (RWC) of roots and leaves was notably affected by the combined influence of biochar and salinity, resulting in significant interaction impacts. The utilization of biochar under non-saline conditions had no impact on the root and leaf relative water content (Figure S1). However, when biochar was applied at both moderate and severe salinity, it significantly enhanced the RWC.

ACC, ABA, and IAA contents

Under salinity stress, the levels of ACC and ABA were elevated, but the content of IAA and the ratios of IAA/ABA and IAA/ACC were reduced. The levels of ACC and ABA were not impacted by the application of biochar in the without-saline condition. However, the use of biochar resulted in a rise in the content of IAA, as well as the ratios of IAA/ACC and IAA/ABA. In addition, biochar significantly decreased the levels of ACC and ABA under saline circumstances. Nevertheless, the content of IAA and the ratios of IAA/ACC and IAA/ABA exhibited an increase, as shown in Figure S2.

Biochemical traits

Based on the analysis of variance (ANOVA) results presented in Table 5, it can be concluded that the treatments had significant effects on the parameters being evaluated. While the year merely impacted the amount of grain yield, the presence of salt stress had a significant impact on the levels of proline level, polyphenol oxidase, and catalase activity. Furthermore, biochar had significant effects on the levels of proline level, catalase, hydrogen peroxide activity, polyphenol oxidase, and accumulation of MDA. The addition of jasmonic acid to the leaves did not have a significant effect on the parameters that were investigated. In addition, the interactions of year, salt stress, jasmonic acid, and dog poop-derived biochar in two, three, and four-way interactions had significant impacts on the parameters being evaluated (p < 0.05, Table 5).

Proline level

The salt stress had a significant impact on the level of proline. Moderate salinity caused enhancement in proline accumulation in the leaves, while high salinity decreased it compared to the control group (p < 0.05, as shown in Table 5). The proline level increased by 41% and 12.3% at moderate salinity (3 dS m−1) and high salinity (6 dS m−1), respectively, compared to the control. In addition, applying biochar to the soil and spraying jasmonic acid, both in combination and individually, effectively mitigated the negative effects of salt, resulting in an increased concentration of proline. The leaf proline concentration was maximum at 0.8 mg g−1 FW when regular-raw biochar (DMB) and 1 mM jasmonic acid (JA) were mixed under a moderate salinity medium. This value was 110.2% in comparison to the control. On the contrary, P. vulgaris had the lowest leaf proline concentration, measuring 0.149 mg/g FW, when raw biochar was administered without jasmonic acid in non-stress conditions (Figure S3).

MDA accumulation

The combined impact of jasmonic acid and biochar, along with the four-way interaction of year, biochar, salinity, and jasmonic acid, significantly influenced the concentration of MDA in plants affected by salt (p < 0.05, as shown in Table 5). Both moderate and severe salinity stress in both years led to an upward trend in MDA accumulation in this study. During the first year of the study, moderate salt resulted in a 46% increase in the MDA levels, whereas severe salinity led to a 56.4% increase. In the 2nd year, it enhanced by 80.2% and 28%. The maximum concentration of MDA was reported in the first year when HCl and JA2 were applied together, reaching 90.1 μM g−1 FW. This concentration was 480.1% greater than that in the control group. The minimal accumulation was seen when HP and JA0 were applied together under SS6, with a 9.2 μM g−1 FW. In the 2nd year, the plants treated with SS3 and a combination of HCl and JA3 showed the highest concentration, which was 218% greater than that of the control group (Figure 1). Nevertheless, no significant disparity was observed when HCl-modified was administered without JA3 and the control group in the presence of elevated salinity levels. In the 2nd year, the combination of HCl and JA2 applied to the soil with severe/high salinity (SS6) led to the maximum MDA accumulation in P. vulgaris leaves. Conversely, the lowest MDA levels were seen when HP and JA1 were applied to soil with moderate salinity (SS3).

As the statistical significance indicates, high salt levels significantly impacted the catalase enzyme's activity (p < 0.05, as shown in Table 5). Irrespective of the severity, the catalase activity was enhanced than that of plants (P. vulgaris) in non-saline soils/conditions. The leaf catalase activity reached its highest level when HCl-treated biochar was combined with 1 mM jasmonic acid (JA1) in a non-saline soil environment (0.130 μmol H2O2 min−1 mg−1 protein). Conversely, the plants treated with jasmonic acid and cultivated in non-saline soils without biochar had the lowest leaf catalase activity, measuring 0.030 μmol H2O2 min−1 mg−1 protein (Figure 2).

Peroxidase's enzymatic activity (POX)

The administered treatments significantly enhanced the Peroxidase's enzymatic activity (p < 0.05, as shown in Table 5). Overall, the presence of salt led to an increase in peroxidase activity over both years. Neither biochar nor jasmonic acid did not have a notable effect on peroxidase enzyme activity. Nevertheless, jasmonic acid increased it. Furthermore, the application of hydrogen peroxide to modified biochar resulted in an augmentation of peroxidase activity under mild stresses in both years. When HCl-modified biochar was combined with 2 mM jasmonic acid (JA1), it exhibited the maximum activity of peroxidase (1.160 µM min−1 mg−1 protein) under severe salt stress conditions (SS6) in both years. In addition, the pristine biochar (DMB) with JA2 for plants in non-saline environment/soil exhibited a maximum peroxidase activity than the other applied combinations, measuring at 1.040 µM min−1 mg−1 protein (Figure S4).

Polyphenol oxidase enzymatic activity (PPO)

The biochar and soil salinity had a significant impact on the PPO activity in the leaves. However, foliar treatment with jasmonic acid alone did not have a significant effect on this property (p < 0.05, as indicated in Table 5). The findings demonstrated a significant rise in polyphenol oxidase activity as a result of salinity in both years of the study. Specifically, in the initial year of the research, the presence of severe and moderate salt stress led to a respective increase of 10.3% and 24.1% in polyphenol oxidase. In the 2nd year, moderate salinity stress resulted in a 16.1% increase in this parameter. However, high salinity did not have a greater impact on this property in the 2nd year. Surprisingly, although using jasmonic acid alone had no effect when it was used combined with biochar; it significantly increased polyphenol oxidase activity. The administration of biochar alone resulted in an increase in polyphenol oxidase activity, both under salinity and under normal stress. Significantly, the application of HCl-modified biochar along with 1 mM jasmonic acid led to the maximum level of polyphenol oxidase activity in leaves under a moderate salinity environment (SS3), achieving 43.1 µM min−1 mg−1 protein in both trial years (Figure S5).

Hydrogen peroxide concentration

The application of the treatments and their combinations had significant effects on this attribute (p < 0.01, as indicated in Table 5). The moderate saline led to a 76% rise in the hydrogen peroxide concentration, but the severe saline did not have a significant impact on this property (Figure S6). The plants that grew under severe salinity (SS6) showed the highest concentration of hydrogen peroxide, especially when HCl-modified biochar (HCl) was combined with 1 and 2 mM jasmonic acid. In contrast, the activity of hydrogen peroxide was at its lowest when hydrogen peroxide-treated biochar (HP) was utilized with 0 and 2 mM jasmonic acid (0.080 and 0.084 µM g−1FW, respectively) in highly saline soil conditions (Figure S6).

Grain yield

Under controlled glasshouse pot conditions, salinity, foliar JA, biochar application, and their interactions significantly affected the grain yield per plant. Salinity at moderate and severe levels reduced the grain yield per plant by 18.0% and 34.61%, respectively, compared with that of the non-saline control. Biochar and JA, applied individually or together, improved the grain yield per plant under both saline and non-saline conditions (Table 5). The highest grain yield per plant was 33 g under nonsaline conditions with HCl-modified biochar and 2 mM JA, whereas the lowest recorded treatment value was 18.4 g per plant under severe salinity with hydrogen peroxide-modified biochar and 2 mM JA (Figure S7).

Discussion

Biochar and jasmonic acid play a vital role in reducing the negative effects of salinity stress by regulating crucial biochemical and physiological processes as well as anatomical aspects. 20 , 49 The findings of this study demonstrated that the growth of P. vulgaris plants was significantly affected by the presence of high levels of salt in the environment. The response was comparable to that detailed by Parreira et al. 50 in beans. Plants commonly exhibit growth loss as a general reaction to salinity. Salinity hindered root development by increasing the production of ABA and ACC and decreasing the levels of growth hormones such as IAA. The decrease in root density was attributable to reduced root dry weight under salinity. Under salt stress conditions, the use of biochar enhanced root growth by reducing oxidative damage (and strengthening the ratios of IAA/ACC and IAA/ABA. Under severe salt stress, the shoot/root ratio of P. vulgaris was much greater, indicating that the root dry weight was more influenced by salt than the shoot. The shoot/root ratio may have increased as a result of the adaptation of root processes. The results of our study demonstrated that P. vulgaris plants exhibited an increase in specific root length in response to salinity, indicating their ability to adapt to the challenging conditions imposed by salt stress. Evelin et al. 51 identified this adaptive system as resulting from root expansion and growth. The introduction of biochar into the soil reduced the negative impact of salinity and enhanced the specific root length and the shoot/root ratio. These findings indicated that the application of biochar under conditions of salinity had a greater beneficial impact on the growth of shoots compared to roots. Biochar boosted shoot growth and elevated the specific root length and shoot/root ratio under salinity. This was achieved by offering essential nutrients for the growth of shoots and reducing osmotic and oxidative stress in roots (Mehmood et al. 52 ). Biochar enhances the growth of plants by directly providing essential nutrients and indirectly enhancing the soil's physicochemical and biological properties (Ondrasek et al. 53 ). In addition, the present study has not evaluated the impact of biochar on soil pH, ion exchange capacity, availability of nutrients, nutrient cycling, as well as buffering capacity. A significant outcome of the toxicity of salt is the heightened production of ethylene, which, when present in excessive amounts, hinders the growth of roots and shoots and restricts the total growth of plants (Riyazuddin et al. 54 ). Ethylene production in plants is directly linked to the levels of endogenous ACC. The application of biochar to the plants resulted in a decrease in the levels of ACC and ABA, but the IAA levels increased. To clarify, the utilization of biochar resulted in a reduction in hormones, including ACC and ABA, which play a role in the stress response. Conversely, the growth of the plant stimulant IAA boosted Na2+ uptake (El Moukhtari et al. 55 ).

Salt stress had an adverse impact on several root anatomical attributes and morphological traits, as previously observed in cotton (Kumar et al. 56 ). Salt stress leads to decreased root growth and the formation of xylem due to alterations in ion toxicity and osmotic pressure (Saberi Riseh et al. 57 ). The width and characteristics of xylem constituents in the root's terminal cell walls are the primary factors that determine xylem hydraulic function (Otlewska et al. 58 ). Salinity reduced the total root area, vascular cylinder area, and cortical parenchyma area in P. vulgaris. The biochar treatments partially maintained these anatomical traits under saline conditions. The improvement in root anatomical development occurred together with changes in root growth and relative water content, suggesting that the amendments helped maintain root structure and hydration under stress. However, sodium, chloride, and potassium concentrations were not measured in root tissues or xylem sap. The anatomical responses therefore cannot be attributed directly to reduced sodium uptake or altered ion transport. The observed expansion of the vascular cylinder (VC) and cortical parenchyma (CP) in response to salt stress following biochar treatment can be attributed to the promotion of root growth and the mitigation of sodium ion toxicity in the root. The results of our study align with the findings from the application of biochar, which led to a considerable reduction in sodium levels in the xylem sap of Solanum tuberosum plants (Datta et al. 59 ). In addition, the presence of biochar reduced the absorption of sodium by lettuce plants when they were exposed to high levels of salt (Datta et al. 60 ). The decrease in root water content may be linked to reduced root growth, plant vitality, xylem component regions, and root density in response to increased salt (El-Dakak et al. 61 ). The rise in the root water content in both the roots and leaves caused by biochar was strongly linked to enhanced root growth in the presence of salt stress. This was attributed to the augmentation of the overall root area and the area of the xylem component.

According to our findings, salinity significantly decreased various physiological and biochemical characteristics. Nevertheless, incorporating biochar and jasmonic acid, either in combination or individually, enhanced all traits, as well as grain yield. Multiple studies have indicated that jasmonic acid and biochar play a vital role in mitigating plant salt stress by regulating essential biochemical and physiological processes. Biochar and jasmonic acid, for instance, mitigate osmotic stress and water loss, promote root development, and enhance salinity tolerance (Oyebamiji et al. 62 ). Additionally, they can enhance the uptake of vital nutrients such as phosphorus and nitrogen, which are crucial for promoting growth. Their addition has been proven to effectively reduce the negative impact of salt, therefore improving the ability of plants to withstand and survive under stressful conditions (Lalay et al. 63 ). The positive impacts of biochar and jasmonic acid arise from their potential to enhance the structure of soils, decrease erosion of soil, and enhance minerals and water availability. 64

Plants have a well-known adaptation response to salinity stress, which involves modifying osmotic pressure via osmolytes such as proline (Singh et al. 65 ). Proline is a helpful solute in both stressful and non-stressful conditions. 66 Proline plays a crucial role in maintaining water balance, stabilizing membrane structure and proteins, regulating the pH of cells, and neutralizing hydroxyl radicals. 67 Salinity-induced stressful events lead to a rise in proline levels as a result of increased pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthase activity and the lowered activity of proline oxidase. 68 Furthermore, data from research have demonstrated that salt leads to an elevation in the amount of proline by decreasing the activity of proline oxidase within cells, thus causing the breakdown of proline. 69 It functions as a signaling molecule that triggers plant recovery in response to stressful conditions. 70 Multiple studies have documented that when plants are exposed to high levels of salt, they respond by increasing the concentration of osmotic chemicals, such as proline. This serves to maintain metabolic balance and enhance the plant's ability to absorb nutrients and water. 71 Our study found that salinity led to an increase in the accumulation of proline, while greater levels of salinity resulted in a decrease in proline content. Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies that have documented an elevation in proline levels in response to salt-induced stress in various species, including chickpeas, lentils, and rice. 72 Proline accumulation can be favorable since it plays a protective function against oxidative damage generated by NaCl in some plant species. 73 The increase in proline concentration in plants cultivated under salinity may result from the decrease in proline oxidase activity, which breaks down proline to decrease its accumulation. 74 Another possible cause is an elevation of Pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthase, resulting in an accumulation of proline. 75 However, our findings demonstrated that biochar decreased the level of proline. Nevertheless, the combined use of jasmonic acid and biochar resulted in an increased accumulation of proline, which ultimately reduced the effects of salinity. Additional scientists have documented the combined effect of biochar in reducing the accumulation of proline in leaf cells. 76 According to reports, biochar is a useful substance for mitigating challenging conditions such as salinity and drought. This is because it helps to increase the soil's ability to hold water, improves the characteristics of soil microbes, and supplies essential nutrients for the growth of plants in stressful environments. 4 Furthermore, reports indicate that the application of jasmonic acid in the presence of salt can enhance the accumulation of proline, resulting in increased resistance to reactive oxygen species (ROS) and improved osmotic regulation. 77 Research has revealed that jasmonic acid enhances proline levels in cells during periods of stress by upregulating gene expression involved in proline synthesis. 78 Consequently, the application of jasmonic acid enhances the probability of effective plant growth and development in challenging/stressful environments, leading to improved economic production.

Multiple studies have demonstrated that the oxidative stress induced by salt in plants is a result of reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydrogen peroxide and superoxide. This can result in phytotoxicity. 79 Plants can endure salt-induced stress by enhancing their enzymatic activity and minimizing cell damage caused by oxidative stress. 80 Plants can counteract the negative impacts of stressful circumstances such as salt and drought by enhancing antioxidant enzymatic activity such as superoxide dismutase, peroxidase, and catalase, along with other non-enzymatic substances including proline. 81 Our study demonstrated that exposure to salinity stress resulted in augmented enzymatic activity, specifically of the enzymes PPO, POX, and CAT, compared to the control group. Moreover, the combined use of biochar and jasmonic acid had a good impact on the activity of these enzymes, hence improving their metabolic activities. 82 Consistent with our research, Farag et al. 83 revealed elevated levels of peroxidase and catalase enzymes in P. vulgaris leaves when exposed to salinity stress. Furthermore, Dawood et al. 84 observed similar findings in P. vulgaris, where the examined plants exhibited increased enzyme activity in response to saline conditions. The increased enzyme activity observed under stressful conditions indicates the plant's capacity to oxidize toxic compounds that accumulate under environmental stress. 85 However, it has been found that the addition of biochar to the soil can effectively alleviate stress situations. For example, it has been asserted that in an experiment, the application of biochar enhanced catalase activity in plants, both normal and salinity stress conditions, hence improving plant growth and development. 86 Therefore, Rady et al. 87 found that the addition of biochar did not have an impact on both peroxidase and catalase activities when plants were exposed to low salt stress. However, it did decrease the activities of these enzymes when the plants were subjected to severe salinity. The incorporation of biochar into the soil enhances nutrient solubility by promoting the activity of soil microbes, improving the availability of vital elements such as potassium and nitrogen. Consequently, this leads to improved growth and productivity, particularly under stressful conditions. 88 Furthermore, it is asserted that the biochar porous structure leads to increased activity of enzymes, stabilizes the function of the plant plasma membrane, decreases the malondialdehyde concentration, and reduces electrolyte leakage. 89 According to reports, jasmonic acid has an impact on catalase activity, leading to the inhibition of reactive oxygen species. 90 Research has demonstrated that, under stressful situations, the application of jasmonic acid can have a substantial positive impact on plant growth-related parameters. It can also promote the production of suitable solutes and activate antioxidant pathways that effectively suppress the activity of reactive oxygen species, both through non-enzymatic and enzymatic processes. 91 Wang et al. 18 highlighted the significance of jasmonic acid for modulating reactive oxygen species during stressful circumstances. They explained that jasmonic acid promotes the gene expression associated with antioxidant enzymes, triggers the antioxidant defense system, and improves plant resilience to saline conditions by sustaining higher water content in the leaves.

Our results show that plants cultivated under non-saline conditions had decreased PPO activity. But when all forms of biochar and jasmonic acid were used, it increased. Moreover, in the control group (CL and JA0), PPO activity enhanced as the salinity levels increased. When biochar was combined with jasmonic acid, PPO activity increased most noticeably, especially when HCl-treated biochar was administered. This result demonstrated an interactive response to jasmonic acid and biochar, particularly for PPO activity, under the tested treatment conditions. By modifying soil properties such as porosity, pH, activity of enzymes, O: C level, CEC, WHC, and microbial population, adding biochar improves nutrient availability. 92 It has a significant function in decreasing the uptake of sodium while enhancing the uptake of vital nutrients such as phosphorus, potassium, manganese, iron, and calcium. These nutrients are crucial for plant growth, especially under stressful conditions. 93 In addition, the use of biochar can alleviate the impacts of salt by reducing the soil EC, exchangeable Na percentage, and sodium uptake ratio. 94 Nevertheless, the positive impacts of biochar are contingent upon variables, including the dosage of biochar applied, the biochar type, the characteristics of the soil, and the intensity of the plant's stress.

Salinity stress is recognized for its ability to cause membrane and oxidative damage, resulting in elevated levels of reactive oxygen species, including hydrogen peroxide and malondialdehyde. The results of our study demonstrated a gradual rise in the MDA levels as the salt levels increased, which is in line with the results of previous research. Salinity stress led to an increase in MDA levels in P. vulgaris plants. MDA, a byproduct of unsaturated fatty acids, acts as a biomarker for the process of lipid peroxidation. Superoxide radicals produced under salt stress induce peroxidation of the membrane, resulting in elevated levels of MDA and subsequent damage to the cellular membrane. 95 Similarly, under different stress circumstances, such as salinity stress, hydrogen peroxide, which is produced as a result of several metabolic pathways, tends to accumulate. 96 In our research, we found that salt had a significant impact on increasing the concentration of hydrogen peroxide in leaves. However, when biochar and jasmonic acid were used in combined form, they effectively decreased the accumulation of hydrogen peroxide. Jasmonic acid interacts with H2O2, resulting in an elevation of its content through the inhibition of ascorbate peroxidase and catalase enzymes. Therefore, jasmonic acid can function as a reactive oxygen species inhibitor and strengthen the plant's defensive mechanisms against environmental stress. 97 The external application of jasmonic acid under stressful conditions positively affects plant biochemical characteristics. It acts as a scavenger of reactive oxygen species and improves the plant's defensive capabilities. 98 Studies have demonstrated that the use of biochar can decrease the activity of MDA by 29% in Peruvian lily flowers and minimize the formation of active oxygen species in fenugreek. 99

Multiple researchers have observed that salinity significantly affects the yield and quality of various crops. 100 Adverse conditions, such as high salinity, impede nutrient uptake and use, leading to nutritional imbalances or deficiencies. 101 Reduced nutrient availability can directly reduce grain yield, as nutrients are crucial for photosynthesis and the formation of photosynthetic pigments. 102 Moreover, salinity increases the cellular Cl− and Na+ concentrations in leaves, interfering with enzymes that break down nitrogen and inhibiting crops' ability to assimilate nitrogen. 103 Elevated sodium and chloride concentrations can impede cell growth, diminish photosynthetic function, and accelerate leaf senescence, ultimately reducing grain yield. 104 In addition, high salinity levels might delay flowering, which can negatively affect productivity Our research supports the findings of Nasiri et al. 105 , which demonstrated that incorporating biochar into soil improves bean grain yield under high salinity. The study found that biochar-enhanced enzymatic activity increased the accumulation of soluble proline and carbohydrates and decreased the level of malondialdehyde. These effects helped minimize membrane damage, reduce the impairment of physiological processes, and ultimately decrease grain yield. Zheng et al. 22 observed that biochar mitigated the adverse impacts of salt on beans, resulting in higher grain production than in plants that were not treated. Biochar treatment can enhance stomatal conductance and the photosynthetic rate under both normal and stressful conditions. This improvement is attributed to increased availability of nitrogen and other nutrients in the soil. 106 In addition, it controls soil pH levels, thereby enhancing crop yield. 107

Additionally, our findings demonstrated that the application of jasmonic acid had a substantial positive impact on the production of grains in our study, regardless of whether the plants were cultivated in saline or non-saline soils. The increase in grain yield per plant following JA and biochar application demonstrated a treatment response under the defined glasshouse pot conditions. The improved water status, root development, osmotic adjustment, and antioxidant activity may have contributed to the maintenance of reproductive performance under salinity. However, the measured values represent yield per plant in pots and should not be interpreted as field yield or commercial productivity. Pot volume, controlled irrigation, limited soil variation, and the glasshouse environment may influence both salinity exposure and plant responses. Field experiments are therefore required to determine whether these treatment effects remain consistent at larger spatial scales and under variable environmental conditions. The coordinated changes in ABA, IAA, ACC, hormone ratios, antioxidant enzyme activities, hydrogen peroxide, MDA, proline, and root traits indicate an integrated physiological and biochemical response to JA and biochar application. However, these measurements represent hormone pools and biochemical end points rather than direct assessments of signaling pathway activity. Therefore, the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of causal hormonal crosstalk or redox signaling. Analyses of hormone receptors, signaling genes, transcription factors, protein abundance, phosphorylation events, and targeted pathway perturbations will be required to establish the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Conclusion

Our research demonstrated that the use of jasmonic acid and biochar can substantially alleviate the detrimental effects of salt stress on P. vulgaris. Salinity diminished the growth of roots and shoots, along with the total root area, chlorophyll content, vascular cambium area, relative water content, and indole-3-acetic acid concentration in the roots of P. vulgaris plants. The utilization of biochar enhanced the shoot/root ratio, specific root length, and production of ACC and ABA. Under non-saline conditions, the use of biochar did not significantly affect root anatomy. Nonetheless, it improved root weight and length by increasing the ratios of IAA to ABA and IAA to ACC. The results of our study indicate that the incorporation of biochar enhanced water availability by improving hormonal and root anatomical characteristics during salt stress, leading to increased development of P. vulgaris. Soil salinity adversely affects the biochemical characteristics and grain yield of P. vulgaris. The incorporation of biochar into the soil and the application of jasmonic acid, individually or synergistically, have demonstrated an improvement in enzymatic activity, plant development, and grain yield under conditions of high salt. The elevated salinity adversely affected the biochemical properties of P. vulgaris, resulting in increased levels of MDA, PPO, POX, CAT, and PC. Furthermore, it diminished the grain yield, which is the principal variable in all agricultural operations. Under controlled glasshouse pot conditions, salinity reduced plant growth, root anatomical development, relative water content, hormone balance, and grain yield per plant in P. vulgaris. Biochar and JA, applied individually or together, alleviated several of these effects by improving root traits, plant water status, antioxidant activity, osmotic adjustment, and grain yield per plant. These results demonstrate the potential of the treatments under the conditions tested. However, this study does not establish field-scale agronomic effectiveness because the pot volume, controlled irrigation, soil uniformity, and glasshouse environment differ from those of commercial production conditions. Multilocation field experiments using different soil types, natural salinity conditions, and crop management systems are required before recommending specific JA concentrations or biochar formulations for agricultural use. The combined treatments improved salinity tolerance through associated changes in hormone concentrations and ratios, antioxidant enzyme activities, oxidative damage, water status, and root traits. These results define an integrated physiological and biochemical response but do not establish direct hormonal or redox signaling mechanisms. The research on jasmonic acid and biochar effects on P. vulgaris under salt stress appears to offer interesting insights; however, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. For instance, the research was carried out in a controlled glasshouse, which might not properly reflect actual farming conditions in the real world. 108
