Distinguishing Perception from Evidence in Psychiatrists’ Attitudes Toward Second-Generation Long-Acting Injectable Antipsychotics [Letter]
Authors: Yoshiyo Oguchi
Published: 2026

Dear editor

I read with great interest a recent article in your journal by Ifteni et al titled “Second-Generation Long-Acting Injectable Antipsychotics: A Comprehensive Assessment of Effectiveness, Safety, and Cost”, in which 80 Romanian psychiatrists rated the four available second-generation long-acting injectable antipsychotics (SG-LAIs) across a range of domains.1 Aripiprazole was rated most favorably on nearly every domain and in terms of overall preference. Lack of patient trust, limited hospital supply, and inexperience were listed among the principal barriers to SG-LAI prescribing.1 This is a welcome addition to the sparse literature on prescriber attitudes in Central and Eastern Europe, and I would like to offer three observations that may help to further frame the study’s findings, as well as suggest some potential future directions worth pursuing in the field.

The study measured what Romanian clinicians believed regarding the effectiveness, safety, and cost of these agents, rather than their properties themselves. As the authors stated, no patient-level data were collected.1 This distinction is crucial when interpreting the study. Cost is the clearest case: in a system that, as the authors describe, fully reimburses LAIs for insured patients, a rated cost does not reflect real economic burden and is best interpreted as simply another opinion. Understood this way, as a study of perception rather than of the drugs themselves, the survey gains importance rather than losing it. Once reimbursement and supply no longer determine which agent a patient receives, the remaining variation in prescriptions falls largely to each clinician’s individual discretion. This is precisely what the survey in question captured. It may be worthwhile to state this explicitly when interpreting the study, so that the survey’s thorough attitudinal data are considered as such, rather than as comparative clinical or pharmacoeconomic evidence.

The efficacy ratings illustrate the same point. Aripiprazole was rated clearly the most effective agent; however, the maintenance network meta-analysis the authors cited found no clear differences in efficacy among these drugs, and the authors themselves described the efficacy levels of the studied SG-LAIs as broadly comparable.1,2 When the available evidence draws no efficacy-related distinctions, but the ratings draw a clear one, the most conservative interpretation is that the perceived efficacy score measured something other than efficacy. This is most likely the tolerability, ease of administration, and freedom from post-injection monitoring that genuinely set aripiprazole apart. Consistent with this, aripiprazole was rated most favorably on almost every attribute concurrently, suggesting a single overall impression that biased respondents’ individual ratings, rather than an independent appraisal of each attribute. The same tolerability-led ordering appears in the 2025 Japanese expert consensus, in which the D2 partial agonists aripiprazole and brexpiprazole scored higher on tolerability, rather than on any efficacy-related advantages.3 The fact that prescriber communities from regions as disparate as Romania and Japan converged on the same agent, and for similar reasons, argues for a shared tolerability-first prescribing culture rather than a real efficacy gap. This important caveat is worth keeping in mind when the drug rankings are considered alongside their clinical trial evidence.

My third observation concerns a gap inherent to the survey itself. Among the measures that the respondents believed would increase appropriate LAI use, 38.8% endorsed standardized tools for assessing the need for initiation, and the authors noted the absence of nation-level guidance concerning how LAIs should be prescribed.1 This is a question of when to initiate an LAI, which is distinct from which agent to choose, and is not unique to Romania. In our research team’s recent survey of Japanese psychiatrists, more than half were found to hold negative attitudes toward atypical LAIs, and reluctance to use them in cases of first episodes increased with age and clinical experience, with several leading concerns (ie, cost, injection pain, and confidence in oral adherence) contradicting the available evidence.4 The Japanese expert consensus illustrated the practical cost of this gap: when asked when an LAI should be initiated, it could offer only expert opinion, as no validated indicator exists to anchor that decision. It therefore placed first-episode initiation no higher than second-line, despite meta-analytic evidence of a mortality benefit, against a background in which only an estimated 5–10% of Japanese patients are prescribed an LAI.3 The authors’ own research team has proposed one response to this gap in the Rating Opportunity for LAI Initiation Index.5 Whether this type of instrument remains robust across diverse healthcare systems whose LAI cultures differ as much as Romania’s and Japan’s, and how it might best be adapted and validated for each, is a question the authors are particularly well positioned to address.

In short, the value of this survey lies less in ranking the agents and more in exposing the disparity between what clinicians perceive and what the evidence supports—as well as in identifying a clinician-developed tool that might reduce it. I would be grateful to learn how the authors view these questions. I am particularly curious to learn whether they read aripiprazole’s consistent advantage as being indicative of genuine efficacy or as the signature of a tolerability-first prescribing culture. I also commend and thank them for their stimulating contribution to the literature.
