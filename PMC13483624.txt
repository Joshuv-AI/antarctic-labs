SALGINTR: development and early evaluation of a low-cost cloud-based digital participatory surveillance system for influenza-like Illness among physicians in Türkiye — a proof of concept
Authors: Eray Ontas, Hasan Güçlü, Yeşim Aydın Son
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParticipatory surveillance complements sentinel influenza monitoring in high-income settings, yet no such platform has targeted physicians as both participants and clinical sentinels. We established SALGINTR, a digital, physician-driven participatory surveillance system for influenza-like illness (ILI), assembling an automated data collection and analysis pipeline from freely available cloud-based tools at a total infrastructure cost of approximately US $100.MethodsPhysicians across Türkiye were recruited via snowball sampling to provide weekly self-reports of ILI symptoms through an online platform (www.salgin.com.tr) during epidemiological weeks 40–52 of the 2025–26 influenza season. ECDC ILI case definition was applied, and independent ILI episodes were ascertained using a clinical-episode rule whereby consecutive symptomatic weeks attributed to the same agent were counted as a single episode. Temporal concordance with national sentinel surveillance was assessed by Pearson and Spearman correlation with moving-block bootstrap confidence intervals, lead–lag cross-correlation, and Bland–Altman agreement. Epidemic detection was evaluated using a panel of six aberration detection algorithms (EARS C1–C3, negative-binomial CUSUM, EWMA, empirical 95th percentile).ResultsOf 243 registered physicians, 197 (analytical cohort) contributed 1,484 respondent-weeks of observation. Cumulative ILI incidence was 50.8% (100/197), with 156 independent episodes identified from 190 symptomatic person-weeks using a clinical-episode definition. Season-level viral positivity was similar in the two streams (SALGINTR 17.3%, 95% CI 11.7–24.2, 27/156; sentinel 14.5%, 95% CI 12.8–16.2, 247/1,709). Weekly SALGINTR viral-systemic positivity was temporally concordant with sentinel surveillance under the pre-specified causal three-week moving average (Pearson r = 0.575, 95% CI 0.35 to 0.90; Spearman ρ = 0.747; pre-epidemic W40–W49 r = 0.886, 95% CI 0.38 to 0.94). Bland–Altman analysis indicated no significant systematic bias (mean difference + 4.6 pp; 95% limits of agreement − 18.3 to + 27.4 pp). Neither system produced any alarm during the ten pre-epidemic weeks, and alarm agreement across the window was substantial (Cohen’s κ = 0.755; specification-curve median κ = 0.639 across 72 analytical choices). Lead–lag analysis identified no timing advantage in either direction (peak lag − 1 week; bootstrap CI − 2 to + 2 weeks).ConclusionsSALGINTR demonstrates that a physician-driven digital participatory surveillance system can generate ILI signals concordant with established sentinel surveillance, at minimal cost. This proof-of-concept supports integration of participatory surveillance as a complementary component within mosaic respiratory disease surveillance frameworks in middle-income settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-14153-1.

Background

Seasonal influenza remains a leading cause of acute respiratory morbidity and mortality worldwide, responsible for an estimated 290,000–650,000 respiratory deaths annually [1]. Timely detection of influenza activity is essential for initiating public health responses, yet conventional sentinel surveillance systems—which rely on clinician-reported consultations at designated healthcare facilities—face inherent limitations in geographic coverage, reporting latency, and the exclusion of illness episodes that never reach medical attention [2, 3]. These constraints have motivated a search for complementary surveillance approaches capable of capturing community-level respiratory illness signals in near-real time.

Participatory surveillance, in which volunteers self-report symptoms through digital platforms, has emerged as a promising complement to traditional systems. Since 2003, platforms such as Influenzanet (ten European countries), FluTracking (Australia and New Zealand), and Flu Near You (United States) have demonstrated moderate-to-strong temporal concordance with sentinel networks [4–8]. A recent systematic review encompassing 26 systems from 18 countries confirmed that most participatory platforms achieve moderate-to-strong concordance with sentinel surveillance, although representativeness remains a persistent challenge [9].

Despite this proliferation, nearly all existing systems recruit volunteers from the general public. Only one published study (Martin et al. in Switzerland) has implemented online influenza surveillance specifically targeting primary care workers [10]. Yet physicians constitute a particularly informative sentinel population: they are occupationally exposed to respiratory pathogens at significantly elevated rates compared to the general community [11, 12], they frequently continue working while symptomatic [13, 14], and they possess the clinical expertise to apply standardized case definitions with high fidelity. A large Italian study found that 47.5% of medical residents experienced at least one ILI episode in a single season—substantially exceeding general population estimates—and reported that ILI among residents anticipated peaks in the broader community [15].

The national sentinel ILI network covers only 23 of 81 provinces [16], reports are published with a delay of several weeks, and the system’s reliance on laboratory confirmation may underrepresent community-level disease burden. Influenza vaccination coverage among healthcare workers remains critically low at below 7% [17], and no participatory surveillance system for respiratory illness has previously been implemented in the country. The WHO “Crafting the Mosaic” framework explicitly positions participatory surveillance as an essential component of resilient respiratory virus monitoring [18], and the 2024 WHO Best Practices guideline provides standardized methodological recommendations for such systems [19].

Against this backdrop, we established SALGINTR, a proof-of-concept, physician-driven digital participatory surveillance system for ILI in Türkiye, assembled entirely from freely available cloud-based tools at a total infrastructure cost of approximately US $100. This study reports its design, implementation, and epidemiological evaluation across the first 13 epidemiological weeks of the 2025–26 influenza season, with a specific focus on temporal concordance with the national sentinel system, epidemic detection performance, and ILI burden among physicians.

Methods

Context and setting

Türkiye’s national influenza surveillance is conducted through a sentinel network coordinated by the Ministry of Health (MoH) and integrated within the WHO/ECDC European Respiratory Virus Surveillance Summary (ERVISS) [16]. The sentinel system targets 250 family physicians across 23 provinces (20 each in İstanbul and Ankara; 10 in remaining provinces), who randomly sample one specimen per week from patients presenting with ILI. During the study period (epidemiological weeks 40–52; 29 September 2025–28 December 2025), a median of 129 specimens per week (range: 76–220; total: 1,709) were tested through this network. Despite its established role, the sentinel system covers only 23 of 81 provinces, relies on laboratory confirmation excluding mild-to-moderate community-managed cases, and is subject to inherent facility-based reporting delays.

System design and architecture

SALGINTR was designed as a “syndromic-sentinel-targeted high-risk” model that bridges two established surveillance paradigms (Table 1). As of 2024, Türkiye has over 220,000 active physicians, averaging approximately 21 outpatient consultations per day, positioning physicians as uniquely sensitive sentinels for respiratory illness. Unlike classic sentinel systems (facility-based, laboratory-confirmed, geographically limited) and general-population participatory systems (self-selected volunteers, variable diagnostic specificity), SALGINTR targets a defined high-risk occupational cohort whose frequent patient contact creates elevated exposure and whose clinical training enhances the specificity of self-reported syndromic data. Three design principles guided development: (a) ultra-low-cost infrastructure using freely available cloud-based tools, ensuring feasibility and replicability in resource-constrained settings; (b) alignment with international participatory protocols (Influenzanet, FluTracking) for comparability; and (c) contextual adaptation for Türkiye’s epidemiological and health system landscape.

The entire platform was built within the Google ecosystem at a total operational cost of approximately US$100. Data collection was performed via Google Forms; data integration and longitudinal linkage were managed through Google Sheets combined with Google Apps Script for pseudonymised unique ID generation and prefilled URL creation. Automated weekly reminders and survey distribution were handled by Apps Script email functions. The public-facing interactive dashboard (www.salgin.com.tr) was built using Google Looker Studio. No dedicated servers, commercial software licences, or external API services were required. The complete technology stack, data flow architecture, and surveillance cycle are detailed in Supplement S1.

Physicians completed an online registration form, after which an pseudonymised unique participant ID and a prefilled weekly survey URL were automatically generated and sent to the participant’s registered email. Automated reminders were sent every Saturday, with a response window through Tuesday and public dashboard updates every Wednesday. The intake questionnaire (~ 3 min) covered demographics, professional information, and risk factors. The weekly questionnaire (~ 1 min if asymptomatic) employed conditional branching: symptomatic participants were directed to a clinical impression question, and those attributing symptoms to a viral/systemic cause (influenza, SARS-CoV-2) received additional items on symptom duration, severity, healthcare utilisation, absenteeism, and testing. This clinical attribution served as the surveillance proxy for laboratory confirmation.

Study design and participants

This was a prospective, community-based participatory surveillance study conducted over 13 epidemiological weeks (W40–52, 29 September – 28 December 2025), preceded by a 3-week pilot phase (W37–39). Physicians actively employed in Türkiye with internet access were eligible; no exclusion criteria were applied beyond non-physician status. Recruitment used snowball sampling via specialist medical associations and physician professional communication networks, commencing during the pilot phase with recruitment remaining open throughout the surveillance period. Of 243 registered physicians from 49 provinces, 197 (81.1%) submitted at least one weekly report. Because current-season vaccination status was complete for all reporting physicians, no participants were excluded on this basis: the analytical cohort comprised all 197 reporters contributing 1,484 person-weeks (67 vaccinated [34.0%], 130 unvaccinated [66.0%]). Case definitions and outcomes

ILI was defined according to the ECDC case definition [20], the standard used in Türkiye’s national surveillance: sudden onset of symptoms, with at least one systemic symptom (fever or feverishness, malaise, headache, myalgia) AND at least one respiratory symptom (cough, sore throat, shortness of breath). Independent ILI episodes were ascertained using a clinical-episode rule: an episode begins at a symptomatic week and continues across consecutive symptomatic weeks attributed to the same agent, so that continuation weeks are not counted as new events (190 symptomatic person-weeks yielded 156 independent episodes). This clinical-episode definition replaces the fixed two-week mechanical washout used at initial submission; it better reflects the natural duration of an illness episode and is unaffected by intervening non-reported weeks. Non-reporting weeks were treated as non-contributing rather than imputed as asymptomatic: weekly denominators are the observed respondent-weeks, and incidence and survival models count only observed person-weeks (an open-cohort design). An observation-process check indicated that being symptomatic in a given week did not predict reporting in the following week (odds ratio ≈ 0.98, non-significant), suggesting that intermittent non-response was largely non-informative for symptom status, although the reporting panel differed from non-reporters on vaccination, sex and age (a differential-selection limitation addressed in the Discussion). Two outcome definitions were applied at different analytical levels: the broad ECDC ILI case definition for individual-level analyses (risk factors, vaccine effectiveness), and the clinically attributed viral-systemic positivity rate—viral-systemic new episodes divided by all new episodes each week—for system-level concordance analysis, paralleling the sentinel system’s metric of test positivity. This dual-definition approach was pre-specified to align each analytical objective with the most appropriate outcome. Individual-level risk factor and exploratory vaccine effectiveness methods and results are reported in full in Supplement S7, including logistic regression, time-varying Cox proportional hazards models, Poisson regression, and propensity score approaches; supplementary methods for derived variables and time-varying vaccination assignment are provided in Supplement S2.

Statistical analysis

The primary analytical framework for system-level evaluation comprised temporal concordance assessment and computational epidemic detection performance. The estimand was identical in the two streams: the probability of a viral agent given influenza-like illness. For SALGINTR stream this was the number of viral-systemic (A-cluster) new episodes divided by all new ILI episodes in the same week; for MoH’s ERVISS sentinel data stream it was positive specimens divided by specimens tested. Both series were smoothed with a causal three-week moving average computed from weeks t − 2, t − 1 and t only. Concordance with ERVISS sentinel test positivity was assessed by Pearson and Spearman correlation with 95% confidence intervals from a moving-block bootstrap (block length 4 weeks, 2,000 replicates), both across the whole window and within the pre-epidemic phase (W40–W49) defined a priori by the MEM historical threshold. Because the causal moving average induces serial correlation, the nominal significance test for a correlation coefficient is not valid in this setting: the lag-1 autocorrelations of 0.63 and 0.43 reduce the effective sample size from 13 to approximately 7. We therefore do not report p values for correlations, and inference rests on the bootstrap intervals alone. To separate agreement on the seasonal trend from agreement on week-to-week movement, correlations were additionally computed on the first differences of both series. The MEM epidemic threshold used to define the phases was derived from national sentinel data in parallel unpublished work by one of the authors (E.Ö.), and the threshold is accordingly used only to label phases descriptively and never as an outcome. Lead–lag structure was examined by cross-correlation at lags of ± 1–3 weeks, with the peak lag declared identifiable only if its bootstrap confidence interval spanned no more than two lags. Bland–Altman agreement was computed on the unsmoothed series (Supplement S6).

To evaluate the system’s capacity for automated epidemic detection, a panel of six aberration detection algorithms was applied independently to both data streams: three control-chart methods (EARS C1, C2 and C3), a negative-binomial CUSUM based on a generalised likelihood ratio, an exponentially weighted moving average (λ = 0.30), and an empirical 95th-percentile threshold. Each estimates expected baseline activity from a rolling seven-week window and flags observations exceeding a three-standard-deviation threshold; the decision interval of the negative-binomial CUSUM was calibrated by Monte Carlo simulation to a common false-alarm rate rather than assumed. A week was classified as an alarm week if at least one detector fired. Agreement between the two streams was quantified by Cohen’s κ with the sentinel as reference, together with percentage agreement, sensitivity, positive predictive value and the φ coefficient. Because any single κ depends on arbitrary analytical choices, the analysis was repeated across a specification curve of 72 defensible combinations of measured quantity, smoothing, baseline length, threshold and consensus rule (Supplement S6).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was approved by the Middle East Technical University Human Research Ethics Committee (26.09.2025-0436-ODTUIAEK-2025) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants. All participating physicians provided informed digital consent prior to enrolment via the SALGINTR platform (www.salgin.com.tr), covering study objectives, data handling procedures, and the right to withdraw. No patients were enrolled; all data were self-reported by physicians regarding their own health status.

Data collection followed a privacy-by-design pipeline that separated identifiers from health data. Registration data (contact details and baseline characteristics) and weekly symptom reports were held in two separate, access-controlled Google Workspace accounts, each protected by two-factor authentication. On enrolment, a Google Apps Script generated a pseudonymous participant identifier from a randomly generated token (not derived from any personal identifier) and delivered a pre-filled weekly-survey link keyed to that token. Weekly questionnaires collected no direct identifiers (no name or e-mail); longitudinal follow-up relied solely on the pseudonymous token embedded in the pre-filled link. The token-to-identity mapping was stored separately from the symptom data and was combined only transiently, on the investigator’s local device, during analysis. Only aggregated indicators are published on the public dashboard (no individual-level data, and no cell counts below five). The individual-level research dataset is therefore de-identified (pseudonymised) rather than anonymised, since the mapping is retained under restricted access. Processing complied with Türkiye’s Personal Data Protection Law; participants gave explicit consent covering the processing of special-category health data and cloud-based processing that may involve cross-border transfer.

Results

System implementation and cohort

Over the study period, 243 physicians from 49 provinces registered on the SALGINTR platform (www.salgin.com.tr), which provided weekly updated ILI indicators accessible to both participants and the public. The three most represented provinces—Ankara (28.0%), İzmir (11.1%), and İstanbul (10.7%)—collectively account for 30.5% of Türkiye’s population. Of registered physicians, 197 (81.1%) were active participants, submitting a median of 7 weekly reports (IQR 4–12; out of 13 possible). The weekly respondent count grew from 44 (W40) to 149 (W52), reflecting ongoing snowball recruitment (Table 2). Because current-season vaccination status was complete for all reporting physicians in the finalized dataset, no participants were excluded for unknown vaccination status; the analytical cohort therefore comprised all 197 reporters (60.9% female; mean age 40.3 ± 10.5 years) contributing 1,484 person-weeks. Of these, 67 (34.0%) were vaccinated in the current season and 73 (37.1%) in the previous season. Just over one-third (38.1%) were engaged in primary face-to-face patient examination. By registered title, participants comprised specialists/sub-specialists (36.3%), physicians with an academic title (35.9%), residents (17.7%) and general practitioners (10.1%); granular clinical specialty (e.g., emergency medicine, infectious diseases, respiratory medicine) was not separately coded. ILI Incidence

Over the 13-week surveillance period (W40–W52), 197 physicians contributed 1,484 respondent-weeks and reported 190 symptomatic person-weeks. Applying the clinical-episode definition—whereby consecutive symptomatic weeks attributed to the same agent are counted as a single ongoing episode—yielded 156 independent ILI episodes, a 17.9% reduction reflecting consolidation of 34 continuation weeks into ongoing episodes. The cumulative incidence was 50.8% (100/197), with 32.5% experiencing a single episode, 11.7% two episodes, and 6.6% three or more. The overall ILI incidence rate was 10.5 episodes per 100 person-weeks (12.8 symptomatic person-weeks per 100 before episode consolidation). Weekly ILI rates among respondents ranged from 6.7% (W45) to 18.2% (W40), with a season mean of 11.1% (Table 2, Supplement S5).

Risk factor analysis and exploratory vaccine effectiveness

Risk factor analysis identified self-reported prior-season ILI severity as the only factor significantly associated with ILI occurrence (OR 1.64, 95% CI: 1.11–2.43, p = 0.014); vaccination status was consistently non-significant across logistic regression, time-varying Cox proportional hazards, and Poisson models. Exploratory vaccine effectiveness estimates were uniformly null to negative regardless of analytical approach (VE − 10.4% crude to − 16.5% fully adjusted; propensity-score approaches likewise null to negative), most parsimoniously explained by residual confounding from prior-season vaccination behaviour (standardised mean difference 1.30 after propensity score adjustment) and limited statistical power with only 67 vaccinated physicians contributing 34 vaccinated-person-week events. These estimates should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Full risk factor analyses, nested multivariable models, and vaccine effectiveness results with sensitivity analyses are reported in Supplement S7.

System-level concordance with sentinel surveillance

Over the 13-week window the two systems agreed closely on the season-level burden of viral infection among ILI: 27 of 156 incident SALGINTR episodes were viral-systemic (17.3%, 95% CI 11.7–24.2), against 247 of 1,709 sentinel specimens testing positive (14.5%, 95% CI 12.8–16.2), a difference of 2.9% points with overlapping confidence intervals. Weekly dynamics were likewise concordant. Under the pre-specified causal three-week moving average the Pearson correlation between the two positivity series was r = 0.575 (95% bootstrap CI 0.35 to 0.90; Spearman ρ = 0.747), and concordance was strongest during the pre-epidemic phase W40–W49, when both series were tracking low-level background activity (r = 0.886, 95% CI 0.38 to 0.94). The epidemic phase could not be evaluated as a separate stratum: the MEM epidemic window opening at W50 is censored at W52 by the end of the proof-of-concept period, leaving only three epidemic weeks. Both series declined through the autumn and rose again from W48, and this shared trajectory accounts for most of the correlation between them: on first differences, which remove any common trend, agreement was weaker and no longer distinguishable from zero (full window r = 0.384, 95% CI − 0.19 to 0.67; unsmoothed r = 0.038, 95% CI − 0.72 to 0.44). The pre-epidemic association was the more robust of the two, persisting on first differences (r = 0.669, 95% CI − 0.17 to 0.82). In sensitivity analyses the unsmoothed weekly series gave r = 0.492 (95% CI − 0.29 to 0.70) and a symmetric three-week moving average gave r = 0.722; because the symmetric filter borrows information from week t + 1 it overstates what a real-time system could achieve (Supplement Tables S6.1 and S6.3).

Bland–Altman analysis revealed no significant systematic bias between the two systems (mean difference: +4.6% points; 95% limits of agreement: −18.3 to + 27.4% points; t = 1.42, p = 0.182). The proportional bias test was likewise non-significant (r = − 0.11, p = 0.72), indicating that agreement did not deteriorate at higher or lower positivity levels (Supplement S6). The wide limits of agreement reflect expected variability between a self-reported participatory system and a laboratory-based sentinel network measuring fundamentally different indicators.

The precision of these estimates is bounded by the reliability of each weekly series. With a median of 12 incident episodes per week, the between-week variance of SALGINTR positivity did not exceed the variance expected from binomial sampling alone (reliability estimate ≈ 0; the variance-components estimator returned − 0.02 and was truncated at zero), whereas the sentinel series, based on a median of 129 specimens per week, was highly reliable (0.950). Smoothing recovers part of the signal, because a three-week average divides the sampling-error variance by three: on the causal moving-average series, which is the series actually correlated, reliability rises to 0.116 for SALGINTR and 0.936 for the sentinel, implying an attenuation ceiling of about 0.33. The observed correlation of 0.575 sits above that ceiling, which should be read not as a strong result but as an indication that the variance-components estimator is downward-biased at this sample size and that the level correlation is inflated by the shared seasonal trend documented above. At this cohort size the weekly participatory signal is therefore noise-dominated, and the concordance reported above should be read as agreement on the seasonal trend rather than agreement at the level of individual weeks. Using the season-wide signal variance, weekly reliabilities of 0.5, 0.7 and 0.8 would require approximately 33, 78 and 134 incident episodes per week respectively, corresponding to roughly a three- to eleven-fold increase in the active weekly respondent pool (Supplement Table S6.2).

A panel of six aberration detection algorithms applied independently to both streams identified two alarm weeks in SALGINTR (W51 and W52) and three in the sentinel (W50, W51 and W52). Neither system produced a single alarm during the ten pre-epidemic weeks W40–W49. Across the 13-week window agreement between the two alarm series was substantial (Cohen’s κ = 0.755; percentage agreement 92.3%; sensitivity 0.67 and positive predictive value 1.00 with the sentinel as reference; φ = 0.778) (Table 3). Requiring at least two concordant detectors rather than one yielded perfect agreement, both systems alarming only at W52 (κ = 1.00). Because any single κ reflects one set of analytical choices, the analysis was repeated across 72 defensible specifications: the median κ was 0.639 (range 0.409 to 1.000; interquartile range 0.567 to 0.755), with 67% of specifications in the substantial range or above and none below 0.40 (Supplement Tables S6.5, S6.6).

Timeliness was assessed by cross-correlation at lags of ± 1–3 weeks. The cross-correlation function peaked at lag − 1 (r = 0.641), indicating that SALGINTR followed rather than led the sentinel, but the moving-block bootstrap confidence interval for the peak lag spanned − 2 to + 2 weeks. Because this interval covers five candidate lags and exceeds the pre-specified identifiability limit of two, no lead or lag can be established from these data in either direction. Consistent with this, the first SALGINTR alarm week varied between W47 and W52 across the 72 specifications (modal value W51), whereas the sentinel first alarmed at W50 under the defined specification (Supplement Table S6.4 and Figure S6.3).

The phase structure of these results is informative. Weekly participation rose from 44 respondents at W40 to 149 by W52. The ten pre-epidemic weeks, during which participation was lowest, produced no alarms in either stream yet yielded the strongest correlation between the two positivity series (r = 0.886), whereas detection agreement was carried entirely by the three epidemic weeks. This dissociation indicates that the participatory series tracked the seasonal trend from the outset but acquired sufficient weekly precision to trigger formal aberration detection only once the epidemic signal became large relative to sampling noise. Both observations identify weekly participant volume, rather than the choice of detection algorithm, as the binding constraint on first-season participatory surveillance.

Discussion

SALGINTR represents, to our knowledge, the first physician-targeted participatory surveillance system for ILI implemented in Türkiye and one of only two such systems described in the published literature, alongside the Swiss pilot by Martin et al. [10] Its deployment during thirteen epidemiological weeks of the 2025–26 influenza season provides a proof-of-concept for leveraging clinicians as both sentinels and subjects for respiratory illness monitoring in a middle-income country with limited sentinel coverage.

The causal three-week moving-average Pearson correlation of r = 0.575 (95% CI 0.35 to 0.90) between SALGINTR’s weekly positivity rates and the national ERVISS sentinel data of the order reported by established European participatory systems, although the raw weekly correlation was weaker and its interval included zero (r = 0.492, 95% CI − 0.29 to 0.70) given only 13 time points. Any such comparison is approximate: the published estimates were most probably computed on unsmoothed or symmetrically smoothed series, whereas the value reported here comes from a causal filter, which is the more conservative choice. Perrotta et al. documented correlations of 0.472–0.699 for Italy’s Influweb across three consecutive seasons [21], while Kalimeri et al. reported values of 0.69–0.88 across nine Influenzanet countries [22]. When the smoothing filter was made symmetric, as in the sensitivity analysis, the correlation rose to r = 0.722; because a symmetric filter borrows information from the following week, that estimate is not attainable in real time and the causal value is reported here instead. Concordance was strongest in the pre-epidemic weeks (r = 0.886), when both systems were tracking low-level background activity, and this was the only estimate to survive first differencing. For the window as a whole, the first-difference analysis shows that agreement is carried by the shared seasonal trajectory rather than by week-to-week co-movement, which is precisely the ecological interpretation a reader should adopt. These estimates remain imprecise, and a single short season from a cohort of 197 physicians — against platforms enrolling tens of thousands of public volunteers — provides only preliminary evidence of concordance. Both systems alarmed at W52, which is consistent with the near-synchronous timing between participatory and sentinel systems reported in other European settings [7, 8]. Bland-Altman analysis confirmed no systematic bias, though the wide limits of agreement reflect expected variability between fundamentally different measurement approaches.

A critical practical advantage not captured by concordance metrics is reporting timeliness. MoH sentinel reports are published with a delay of approximately three to four weeks, characteristic of laboratory-based systems requiring specimen collection, transport, testing, and data aggregation. By contrast, SALGINTR data were available within the same epidemiological week of reporting. In operational terms, SALGINTR’s signal—the detected aberration—would have been available approximately three weeks before equivalent sentinel data became public. This distinction between analytical concordance and operational timeliness is crucial and aligns with the core rationale for participatory surveillance.

The convergence of both systems on their alarm weeks at the epidemic peak, together with the complete absence of pre-epidemic alarms in either stream once a detector panel appropriate to a first season was used, supports the value of multi-algorithm approaches for first-season systems lacking historical baselines [23, 24]. Alarm agreement across the observation window was substantial (Cohen’s κ = 0.755) and remained so across the great majority of 72 alternative analytical specifications (median κ = 0.639).

The cumulative ILI incidence of 50.8% among participating physicians is notably high but consistent with the 47.5% reported among Italian medical residents [15]. Serologically confirmed influenza incidence among healthcare workers is substantially lower (10.6–22.3%) [25, 26], as expected given the broad ECDC syndromic definition. This high burden reinforces the rationale for monitoring physicians as a sentinel population and underscores occupational risk documented in meta-analytic evidence [11]. The exploratory vaccine effectiveness estimates were uniformly null to negative across all analytical approaches (Supplement S7), most likely reflecting structural confounding and limited statistical power rather than true absence of vaccine effect.

The ultra-low-cost architecture (~ US$100) contrasts markedly with published alternatives, with the next lowest reported system costing approximately US$1,200 [27]. While the Google-based approach introduces ecosystem dependencies, it dramatically lowers the barrier to entry for participatory surveillance in resource-constrained settings.

Future directions - toward AI-enhanced participatory surveillance: The six independent aberration detection algorithms deployed in SALGINTR represent a foundational analytical layer upon which more advanced capabilities can be built. As multi-season data accumulate, algorithm outputs could be formalised into a supervised meta-learner where individual alarm signals serve as input features for a classifier trained on confirmed epidemic periods, moving from independent parallel evaluation to a true ensemble approach. Integrating participatory data streams with environmental covariates and digital data sources through predictive modelling frameworks could enable short-term ILI forecasting, shifting the system from reactive detection to proactive prediction. The low-cost, modular design of the SALGINTR platform facilitates iterative development, allowing future integration of such components as the system’s data depth and analytical requirements evolve.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the 13-week observation period precludes assessment of inter-seasonal stability and performance across varying influenza subtypes. Second, the physician-only cohort with voluntary enrolment limits generalisability and may inflate ILI rates through selection bias—a challenge well-documented across participatory systems [28, 29]. Finally, the MoH sentinel system used as reference is itself imperfect, with limited geographic coverage and multi-week reporting delays. Several further limitations warrant emphasis. Non-reporting was frequent (median 7 of 13 weeks, IQR 4–12); although an observation-process check suggested it was largely non-informative for symptom status, differential non-response by symptomatic physicians could bias ILI rates in either direction. Because recruitment remained open, the weekly respondent pool grew substantially (44 to 149), changing cohort composition over the window and destabilising early-week estimates; a consistent-reporter sensitivity analysis is therefore advisable and planned. Participants were aware of taking part in a surveillance study, so a Hawthorne effect on symptom reporting cannot be excluded. The temporal concordance may also partly reflect shared seasonal forcing—both systems track winter respiratory viruses—rather than genuine sentinel sensitivity, and the two systems measure different constructs (self-reported syndrome vs. laboratory positivity) in different populations (physicians vs. patients), so the comparison should be read as trend concordance rather than measurement agreement. With only 14 provinces represented by a single physician, re-identification risk is non-trivial and is mitigated by publishing only aggregated indicators (no names, institutions, or sub-cell counts below five) and pseudonymised participant identifiers. Given the short first-season window and few weekly time points, all epidemic-detection and early-warning findings should be regarded as preliminary. Most importantly, at a median of 12 incident episodes per week the weekly participatory positivity series carried no variance in excess of binomial sampling noise, so week-level estimates are noise-dominated and the concordance reported here reflects the seasonal trend rather than agreement at the level of individual weeks; the epidemic phase comprised only three weeks, which precludes any formal assessment of discriminative accuracy or of lead time. Approaches to mitigate selection bias in future waves include inverse-probability-of-participation or post-stratification weighting to national physician demographics, negative-control outcomes, and self-swab validation subsets.

Conclusion

SALGINTR demonstrates that a physician-driven, digitally enabled participatory surveillance system can generate ILI trend data that are temporally concordant with established sentinel surveillance and can reproduce the sentinel’s epidemic alarm weeks without generating false alarms in the pre-epidemic period, at a fraction of the cost of traditional systems. Within this first 13-week window no timing advantage over the sentinel could be demonstrated and weekly estimates remained noise-limited, so the proof-of-concept establishes participatory surveillance as a viable complement to existing infrastructure in Türkiye rather than a substitute for it, with particular value for geographic coverage expansion, real-time situational awareness, and capacity strengthening during periods of high demand. Future work should prioritise a larger weekly respondent pool to lift week-level precision above sampling noise, multi-season continuity to establish stable baselines, integration of self-collected respiratory specimens for pathogen-specific monitoring, and expansion beyond the physician cohort to the general population, guided by the WHO mosaic surveillance framework. The parallel application of six independent computational detectors, each grounded in distinct anomaly detection principles, and their evaluation across 72 analytical specifications, demonstrated that algorithmically diverse surveillance methods can converge on consistent epidemic signals from participatory data, providing a foundation for developing integrated predictive tools as multi-season baselines accumulate.
