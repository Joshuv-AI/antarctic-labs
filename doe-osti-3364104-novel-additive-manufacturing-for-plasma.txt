1. Project Overview and Objectives
The primary objective of this project was to advance innovative manufacturing approaches for plasma-
facing materials relevant to fusion energy applications. A particular focus was given to developing flashing
sintering technology for the processing of tungsten and related refractory materials. The work was intended
to be carried out through collaborative engagement between Florida International University (FIU), Miami
Dade College (MDC), and Purdue University, each contributing complementary expertise across materials
science, advanced manufacturing, and nuclear engineering. A secondary component of the project involved
providing undergraduate students from FIU and MDC with educational and experiential opportunities in
fusion energy science and engineering—equipping the next generation of engineers with awareness and
skills in this important field.

2. Project History and Contextual Challenges
2.1 Project Initiation Delays
Although the award period officially commenced on February 1, 2023, the actual activation of the project
was staggered across institutions due to contract processing timelines. Purdue's subcontract was finalized in
late April 2023, the FIU project was cleared for theoretical and simulation work only in late May 2023, and
MDC's sub-award was not executed until mid-July 2023. Laboratory access at FIU was further deferred
pending completion of safety-mandated renovations required by FIU's Environmental Health and Safety
(EHS) office, with an original target of January 2024 for lab clearance.

2.2 PI Transition
The project was originally led at FIU by PI Zhe Cheng (Associate Professor, Mechanical and Materials
Engineering), who championed the project's vision and established its early framework. Following Dr.
Cheng's departure from FIU, PI Bilal El-Zahab assumed responsibility for the project for the remaining
approximately five months of the award period. Upon taking over, PI El-Zahab encountered the same
constraints that had impeded progress under the prior PI.
2.3 No-Cost Extension and Residual Constraints
Recognizing that the compounding delays had left a substantial portion of the planned experimental work
incomplete, PI El-Zahab submitted a no-cost extension request, which was granted. However, effective
fund availability within the extension window was limited to approximately four months due to
administrative and institutional processing timelines, further constraining the ability to execute the full
scope of planned research.
2.4 Modification of the Student Engagement Component
A portion of the originally proposed work included structured academic engagement activities with
students from MDC in support of the project's educational objectives. This component was discontinued
during the project period as a result of broader institutional policy changes that came into effect, making it

no longer feasible to execute these activities as originally designed. The project team navigated this change
in good faith and redirected available efforts toward the technical research components.
2.5 Decision Not to Seek Further Extension
Given the departure of the original PI who championed this project, the effective funding window even
after the no-cost extension, and the completion of the activities that remained viable under the modified
scope, PI El-Zahab has determined that seeking a further extension would not be productive or in the best
interest of the project or the sponsoring agency. This final report therefore serves as the formal conclusion
of project activities under Award No. DE-SC0023647.

3. Accomplishments
Despite the considerable challenges outlined above, meaningful progress was achieved across several areas
of the project. The accomplishments are organized below by institution and activity type.
3.1 Purdue University – Fusion Engineering Summer Camp
One of the most substantive and well-executed activities of the entire project period was the inaugural
Purdue Fusion Energy Summer Camp, held July 31–August 4, 2023, at Purdue University's West Lafayette
campus. The camp was organized and led by PI Xiaoyuan Lou and the Department of Nuclear Engineering,
with strong faculty and staff participation.
The curriculum was structured to provide undergraduate students with a rigorous introduction to fusion
energy science and engineering across a broad range of topics. The full agenda covered:
 • Basics of radiation and biological effects
 • Introduction to fusion energy, health science applications, and space propulsion
 • Fusion materials science and plasma physics
 • Reactor types, reactor physics, and detector fundamentals
 • Nuclear materials and reactor manufacturing
 • Flash sintering and its application to advanced manufacturing
 • PUR-1 research reactor operations, startup, and safety procedures
 • Hands-on radiation measurement laboratory using Geiger counters
Students were given facility tours of the PUMA (Purdue University Multi-dimensional Integral Test
Assembly) facility and the PUR-1 research reactor. The camp concluded with an off-campus field trip to
the Clinton Nuclear Power Plant in Clinton, Illinois, providing students with a firsthand perspective on the
scale and complexity of commercial nuclear infrastructure.

The camp was received with exceptional enthusiasm by participating students. Feedback letters conveyed
that the experience meaningfully deepened students' interest in nuclear and fusion engineering, provided a
rare opportunity to observe an operating research reactor, and created lasting academic and personal
connections. The camp was also featured in the Purdue Nuclear Engineering Newsletter, extending its reach
and visibility.
A second iteration of the camp was planned for the following year based on the success and feedback from
this first cohort; however, execution of that activity was contingent on project continuity and the
institutional factors described in Section 2.

3.2 FIU – Undergraduate Research Training and Mini-Projects
At FIU, PI Cheng and co-PIs Drozd and Durygin engaged a cohort of undergraduate students from the
Mechanical Engineering, Civil Engineering, and Chemistry programs in structured research training
activities. Given the unavailability of the laboratory for experimental work, these activities were
appropriately scoped to design, simulation, and theoretical work. Students were organized into three
subgroups, each working on a project directly relevant to advanced manufacturing and flash sintering:

Subgroup 1 – Design and Implementation of a Single-Step Ceramic 3D Printing System
This team undertook the mechanical design of a selective laser sintering (SLS)-based 3D printer capable of
processing ceramic powders. Students conducted a preliminary review of required components
(microcontrollers, frames, power supplies, laser systems), disassembled an existing SLS unit to build
familiarity with its architecture, and designed a dual-chamber powder feeding and build mechanism. The
central engineering challenge identified was the synchronization of the two-chamber z-axis motion system,
and students developed schematics and a parts specification list toward resolving this. The team's work
represented meaningful design practice at the interface of mechanical systems, materials processing, and
additive manufacturing.

Figure 2. Conceptual schematic developed by Subgroup 1 illustrating the dual-chamber powder handling
mechanism for the ceramic SLS 3D printer. The build chamber descends incrementally along the z-axis
with each layer, while the feeding chamber simultaneously rises to supply fresh ceramic powder. A roller
then redistributes the powder across the build surface, enabling successive layer-by-layer densification.

Subgroup 2 – 3D Modeling and Printing of the Flash Sintering Sample Assembly
This team focused on the dimensional documentation and replica fabrication of the flash sintering sample
assembly currently in use at FIU's laboratory. Using SolidWorks, the students prepared detailed 3D
drawings of the multi-component assembly, which includes outer graphite sleeves, hexagonal boron nitride
(h-BN) insulation tubes, inner graphite electrodes, and the sample die set. The plastic-replicated assembly
was intended to serve as a hands-on demonstration and training tool for future users of the flash sintering
system. The team also explored potential component modifications to improve sintering efficiency and
support more complex sample geometries.

Figure 3. (a) Photograph of the flash sintering sample assembly components at FIU, including the outer
graphite sleeve, graphite outer electrodes, inner h-BN tube with inner graphite electrodes, and steel die set
for pressing the 3 mm diameter powder compact. (b) Cross-sectional schematic of the assembled
configuration, showing the spatial arrangement of graphite, h-BN insulation, powder compact, and
thermocouple (TC) placement. This assembly was the subject of Subgroup 2's SolidWorks modeling and
3D printing replication effort.

Subgroup 3 – Resistance Measurement System for Flash Sintering
This team worked on the implementation of an electromechanical system for measuring the electrical
resistivity of powder compacts under varying applied pressures—a measurement capability directly
relevant to understanding densification mechanisms and optimizing the flash sintering process. The system
design, originally conceived by co-PI Dr. Durygin, integrates a linear actuator, load cell, linear scale
encoder, electrical current probes, and a milli-ohmmeter controlled via microcontroller-based electronics.
Students gained proficiency in Arduino microcontroller programming through a series of practical exercises
and began the integration of the linear motion and sensing components into a functional prototype frame.
Weekly team meetings were held with all subgroups and the PI/co-PI team at FIU, maintaining project
coherence, enabling cross-subgroup learning, and ensuring steady progress tracking across activities.

Figure 4. SolidWorks FEA simulation result for the CZL-601AC load cell under an applied compressive
load of 75 kN. The von Mises stress distribution (color scale in N/m²) reveals regions of concentrated
compression and tension near the strain gauge locations, consistent with the sensor's intended operating
principle. This simulation informed the structural integrity assessment of the measurement system design.

3.3 MDC – Equipment Procurement and Student Engagement
At MDC, Dr. Cristian Penciu coordinated early-stage activities including student identification and
engagement for the Purdue summer camp. Three MDC students were selected, supported, and accompanied
by Dr. Penciu to attend the Purdue Fusion Energy Summer Camp. MDC also procured a metallurgical
microscope for sample characterization in support of the project's planned experimental activities, and
initiated the specification process for a sample polisher and associated consumables.

Figure 5. SolidWorks drawings of the structural frame designed to house the resistivity measurement
system, including front, side, and isometric views with critical dimensions annotated (all dimensions in
inches). The frame was redesigned by Subgroup 3 to accommodate the load cell, linear actuator, and
encoder while maintaining mechanical rigidity under the anticipated loading conditions.

4. Products and Dissemination
4.1 Publications
No peer-reviewed publications resulted from this project at FIU. The combination of factors including PI
transition did not provide sufficient time to conduct and report experimental research at a publishable level.

4.2 Intellectual Property
No patentable inventions or intellectual property disclosures were generated under this award.
4.3 Technologies and Techniques
The design work for the SLS-based ceramic 3D printer and the resistance measurement system for flash
sintering constitute early-stage technical developments that, while not yet reduced to practice in full
experimental form, represent foundational engineering work for continued development under future
efforts.
4.4 Other Dissemination
Activities were disseminated through the Purdue Nuclear Engineering Newsletter, which featured coverage
of the Purdue Fusion Energy Summer Camp and its outcomes.

6. Budget Utilization
Due to the convergence of factors described in Section 2, the majority of the project budget was not
expended. The project team made good-faith efforts to execute meaningful work within the constraints that
emerged. Expenditures were concentrated in areas where progress was most feasible: student support for
the Purdue summer camp, equipment procurement at MDC, and personnel time for design and training
activities at FIU and Purdue.
PI El-Zahab does not consider it appropriate or beneficial to the agency to request further extensions for
this project. The scientific and programmatic rationale that motivated the original scope has been
substantially affected by the PI transition and the operational limitations encountered throughout the award
period, and the most productive path forward is a clean and transparent close-out of this award.

7. Conclusions
This project made meaningful early contributions to two distinct but complementary goals: advancing the
technical understanding and development of flash sintering for plasma-facing material fabrication, and
building awareness and foundational skills in fusion energy science among undergraduate engineering
students. The Purdue Fusion Energy Summer Camp stands as a particularly successful and high-impact
outcome of this collaboration—one that generated genuinely enthusiastic responses from student
participants and helped establish early institutional connections between Purdue, FIU, and MDC in the
domain of fusion energy science.
The project ultimately fell short of its full research objectives, due to circumstances that were largely
outside the direct control of the research team. Laboratory access delays, the administrative processes
governing multi-institution awards, the mid-project transition in project leadership, and the need to
discontinue certain originally planned activities collectively prevented the experimental research program
from reaching maturity. These are acknowledged candidly here so that the sponsoring agency has an
accurate accounting of what was and was not accomplished.
The research team expresses sincere appreciation to DOE's Office of Science and the FES-RENEW
program for the opportunity and support provided under this award, and to Dr Shaw throughout the project
period.
