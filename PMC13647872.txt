Hydroxyethylcellulose-
and Chitosan-Modified 3D-Printed
Carbon Electrodes for Indole-3-Acetic Acid and Salicylic Acid Determination

Pablo H. S. Martins, Hugo L. S. L. Leão, Polyana F. Pereira

Abstract

This study describes
the development of a 3D-printed carbon black
(3D-CB) electrode modified with hydroxyethylcellulose (HEC) and chitosan
(CH) layers, applied to the simultaneous quantification of indole-3-acetic
acid (IAA) and salicylic acid (SA) in plant samples. Measurements
were performed by square-wave voltammetry (SWV) under optimized conditions,
enabling rapid and resolved detection of phytohormones. The method
exhibited linear responses for both analytes, with correlation coefficients
(r) of 0.999. Limits of detection (LOD) were 0.446 μM for IAA
and 0.335 μM for SA. Good precision was obtained, with RSD values
for both intraday and interday analyses not exceeding 4.70%. The applicability
of the method was evaluated in real plant samples, including roots
and leaves of soybean and maize, as well as roots, leaves, and green
fruits of strawberry. For IAA, mean recoveries for the three extracts
ranged from 96.82% to 101.37%, with RSD values between 2.74% and 5.27%.
For SA, recoveries ranged from 98.13% to 102.98%, with RSD values
between 1.92% and 4.01%. The absence of a statistically significant
difference between added and found concentrations confirmed the accuracy
and reliability of the proposed method. Owing to its simple fabrication,
rapid SWV response, low operational cost, and use of environmentally
friendly materials, the proposed 3D-CB/HEC–CH platform represents
an alternative for efficient monitoring of IAA and SA in agricultural
matrices.

Introduction

Plant physiological processes
are regulated by organic molecules
known as phytohormones, with indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and salicylic
acid (SA) standing out as important regulators due to their central,
and often antagonistic, roles in coordinating plant growth and defense
responses. IAA mainly regulates growth
and developmental processes, including cell elongation, organogenesis,
and root–shoot morphogenesis. Beyond
these classical roles, recent studies have revealed that IAA contributes
to plant adaptation to abiotic stresses such as salinity, drought, and nutrient
deficiency. SA is an indispensable molecule
that modulates growth and development by enhancing photosynthetic
efficiency, activating antioxidant defenses, and promoting osmoprotectant
accumulation.

While IAA and SA are
commonly studied separately, recent studies
have shown that both act in a coordinated manner in plant stress responses,
with SA signaling directly modulating auxin transport and distribution.
This interaction between these phytohormones reinforces the need for
analytical approaches capable of simultaneously determining IAA and
SA under complex physiological conditions. Although chromatographic techniques dominate the literature on simultaneous
IAA and SA determination,
−

 nonchromatographic methods such
as colorimetric, spectrofluorimetric, and immunoassays
,
 are predominantly
employed for isolated quantification. However, challenges regarding
selectivity, sensitivity, and matrix effects limit their applicability
for reliable simultaneous analysis in complex plant matrices. An overview of the physiological roles of IAA
and SA, current analytical techniques, their limitations, and emerging
trends in electrochemical sensing is presented in .

Voltammetric techniques offer
a compelling alternative for IAA
and SA determination due to their operational simplicity, strong analytical
performance, rapid response, and ease of implementation.
−

 Several limitations remain in the current literature. The simultaneous
detection of IAA and SA requires correction procedures for voltammetric
signals to resolve overlapping signals. The electrochemical response
is influenced by analyte diffusion, which compromises measurement
accuracy and reproducibility. Certain electrodes exhibited variations
of 10–13%, attributed to reproducibility limitations. These
challenges highlight the need for electrochemical platforms with enhanced
selectivity, stability, and analytical performance.
,

In recent years, particular attention has been given to carbon-based
electrodes, especially those with three-dimensional (3D) architectures,
which have emerged as frontrunners for enabling low-cost and customizable
platforms.
,
 The use of biopolymers such as
chitosan (CH) has further enhanced the applicability of these 3D platforms,
providing analyte affinity while maintaining compatibility with advanced
voltammetric techniques for phytohormone analysis.
,,

The use of CH as the sole matrix material
is associated with inherent
limitations, including film swelling, pH-dependent solubility, limited
mechanical stability, and difficulties in achieving homogeneous and
reproducible coatings in aqueous and complex sample conditions.
,
 Nanoparticles and conductive materials have been incorporated into
CH films to improve strength, conductivity, and stability. Another approach is the addition of hydroxyethylcellulose
(HEC). Previous work by these authors has shown that CH films with
HEC are more uniform and more resistant, and they produce more stable
signals, thereby supporting more reliable
electrochemical detection of phytohormones such as IAA and SA.

In this work, a novel method comprising a 3D-printed carbon black
(3D-CB) electrode functionalized with a hydroxyethylcellulose/chitosan
(HEC–CH) bilayer for the rapid and simultaneous electrochemical
determination of IAA and SA by square-wave voltammetry (SWV) was proposed.
Although progress has been made in electrochemical sensors for IAA
and SA determination, most of the previously reported approaches involve
multistep fabrication protocols and elaborate surface modifications,
which raise production costs. 3D printing, by contrast, offers a rapid,
low-cost, and customizable route to electrode fabrication, and the
HEC–CH bilayer can be deposited through a straightforward modification
step that eliminates the need for sophisticated functionalization
or nanostructured materials, yielding a simple, reproducible, cost-effective,
and environmentally friendly platform for phytohormone analysis. No
previous studies have described the integration of 3D-printed carbon
electrodes with cellulose-derived biopolymers for the simultaneous
voltammetric detection of these phytohormones. The applicability was
demonstrated through the analysis of soybean (Glycine
max), maize (Zea mays) and strawberry (Fragaria × ananassa) extracts,
highlighting its suitability for complex plant matrices.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of 3D-CB/HEC–CH

Electrochemical characterization of the 3D-CB/HEC–CH electrode
by CV in the [Fe­(CN)6]3–/4– redox
system revealed anodic and cathodic peaks with a peak-to-peak separation
(ΔEp) larger than expected for a reversible process, indicating
quasi-reversible/irreversible electron-transfer kinetics at the electrode
surface. The anodic peak current showed a linear relationship with
the square root of the scan rate between 10 and 100 mV s–1 (r > 0.998), confirming diffusion control, whereas the anodic
peak
potential (Ep) shifted linearly with log­(scan rate) (r = 0.980), consistent
with the Nicholson–Shain treatment for irreversible systems. The slope of this relationship was used to determine
the transfer coefficient (αna).

Considering
the irreversible redox behavior, a modified Randles–Ševčík
equation incorporating the transfer coefficient was applied to calculate
the electroactive surface area. A diffusion
coefficient of 7.6 × 10–6 cm2 s–1 was used for the calculations, yielding an electroactive
surface area of 0.37 cm2 for the modified electrode, consistent
with values reported for carbon black 3D-printed electrodes. Considering a geometric area of 0.070 cm2, the corresponding roughness factor was estimated as 5.3,
reflecting a 5.3-fold increase in active area relative to the geometric
surface. These results indicate that surface modification led to a
greater availability of electroactive sites at the electrode surface.
The cyclic voltammograms, linear fitting, and calculation details
are presented in Figure S1 (ESI).

The influence of surface modification
on the charge-transfer properties
of the electrodes was evaluated by EIS. presents the Nyquist plots for the (a) 3D-CB,
(b) 3D-CB/HEC, (c) 3D-CB/CH, and (d) 3D-CB/HEC–CH electrodes.
For the bare 3D-CB electrode, the semicircle diameter corresponded
to a charge-transfer resistance (Rct) of 362.27 Ω.
After modification with HEC, the Rct increased to 495.98
Ω, indicating that this polymer layer introduces a barrier effect
that hinders electron transfer. Modification with CH alone decreased
the Rct to 311.18 Ω, suggesting that chitosan provides
a more favorable interface for charge transfer than HEC while maintaining
electrode conductivity. This behavior can be attributed to the free
amino groups of CH, which undergo protonation and establish electrostatic
interactions with the negatively charged [Fe­(CN)6]3–/4– redox probe, facilitating interfacial charge
transfer.

The 3D-CB/HEC–CH electrode, in turn, exhibited
a further
decrease in Rct to 132.82 Ω, indicating faster electron
transfer than either polymer alone. This result suggests that HEC
does not impair the electroactive function of CH within the composite
film. This enhancement can be attributed to an improved organization
of the polymeric matrix and electrostatic interactions between protonated
CH amino groups and the negatively charged [Fe­(CN)6]3–/4– species, facilitating charge transport.
The higher CPE-Y0 value observed for 3D-CB/HEC–CH
further indicates increased capacitance and charge accumulation. These
findings are consistent with the electroactive area enhancement determined
by CV. This behavior corroborates previous studies reporting enhanced
charge-transfer properties for CH-modified electrodes.

The actual contribution of HEC was further
confirmed by comparing
the signal stability of the 3D-CB/HEC–CH and 3D-CB/CH electrodes
under prolonged exposure (). Over five consecutive measurements with the same
electrode continuously immersed in 0.50 M NaOH, the HEC–CH
electrode exhibited stability (RSD of 4.77% for IAA and 4.34% for
SA), whereas the CH-only electrode showed signal decline over the
same period (RSD of 22.99% for IAA and 51.77% for SA), corresponding
to a decrease of ∼61% in the SA signal between the first and
last measurements, accompanied by a shift in the peak potential of
both analytes. This is consistent with the hydrophilic, film-forming
character of HEC, which promotes a more homogeneous matrix and may
favor ion permeability, thereby stabilizing the CH film under prolonged
exposure.

Surface morphological features of the electrodes
were examined
by SEM, with micrographs presented in a and c. SEM micrographs recorded at a 10
μm scale revealed that the 3D-CB electrode (a) showed a granular morphology
with interparticle voids, characteristic of the agglomerated structure
of 3D-CB. The 3D-CB/HEC–CH-modified electrode (c) showed a film-like structure
covering the carbon particles. The blend was distributed over regions
with surface relief, indicating successful deposition of the polymeric
film. These morphological changes are consistent with the electrochemical
results, including the increased electroactive surface area and lower
Rct values.

EDS analysis was performed to
assess the surface elemental profile,
with the corresponding spectra presented in b and d. Although the SEM analysis suggested
accumulation of the polymeric blend in regions with higher surface
relief, the EDS spectra indicated a uniform coating distribution over
the electrode surface. The spectrum of the unmodified 3D-CB electrode
(b) showed major signals corresponding to carbon (C) and oxygen (O),
along with trace amounts of aluminum (Al) and silicon (Si), which
are most likely attributable to inorganic additives or residual mineral
fillers present in the commercial CB/PLA filament used for electrode
fabrication, as previously reported for conductive PLA-based filaments. These impurity signals were absent from the
spectrum of the 3D-CB/HEC–CH-modified electrode (d).

To further assess compositional uniformity, EDS spectra were acquired
from three different regions of each surface (Figures S2 and S3, ESI). The consistent
C and O signals across all analyzed areas, together with the absence
of additional elemental signals on the modified electrode, confirm
the homogeneous distribution of the HEC–CH film over the electrode
surface.

Optimization of Conditions for the 3D-CB/HEC–CH
Electrode

The electrochemical responses of IAA and SA at
the 3D-CB/HEC–CH electrode were evaluated as a function of
pH using Britton–Robinson (BR) buffer over the pH range of
7–13. As shown in Figure S4 (ESI), the oxidation response of IAA was observed
throughout the investigated pH range, with a progressive shift of
the anodic peak toward less positive potentials as the pH increased.
In contrast, SA exhibited well-defined oxidation peaks only at pH
7, 12, and 13, with no significant response observed at the remaining
pH values, indicating that alkaline media were more favorable for
the electrochemical response of both analytes. Overall, pH 13 was
selected as the optimal condition since it provided the highest oxidation
current for both analytes and a more effective separation between
the anodic peaks of IAA and SA.

Based on the results obtained
from the BR buffer study, the electrochemical behavior of IAA and
SA was subsequently investigated in different supporting electrolytes,
including 0.10 M PBS (pH 7), 0.10 M borate buffer (pH 9), and 0.50
M sodium hydroxide solution (pH 13). CVs were recorded at 50 mV s–1 between −1.50 and +1.00 V vs Ag|AgCl (sat.)
at room temperature. Figure S5 (ESI) shows the cyclic voltammograms obtained
for IAA and SA in the different supporting electrolytes evaluated.

Based on the cyclic voltammograms displayed in Figure S5 (ESI), recorded in 0.50
M sodium hydroxide solution (pH 13), a marked enhancement in the electrochemical
response was observed for both IAA and SA when compared with the other
investigated media. In this electrolyte, improved peak definition
was achieved, indicating superior electrochemical sensitivity. Accordingly,
the sodium hydroxide solution (pH 13) was selected as the supporting
electrolyte for subsequent electrochemical analysis. Although forced
degradation studies have reported that salicylic acid and related
compounds can undergo alkaline hydrolysis under elevated temperatures
and prolonged exposure, the electrochemical
measurements in this study were performed on freshly prepared solutions
over a short timescale at room temperature, conditions considerably
milder than those employed in such degradation protocols.

The
cyclic voltammograms displayed in show oxidation processes for both IAA and
SA under the selected alkaline conditions. For IAA, oxidation begins
at ∼+0.25 V, with a pronounced anodic response observed around
+0.35 V vs Ag|AgCl (sat.). In contrast, SA exhibits an oxidation response
at more positive potentials, with an anodic peak observed at approximately
+0.65 V. The separation between the oxidation peak potentials enables
the electrochemical discrimination of the two analytes during simultaneous
analysis.

The scan-rate data shown in Figure S6 (ESI) were analyzed to clarify the oxidation mechanism.
For both
analytes, the anodic peak current exhibited a linear dependence on
the square root of the scan rate (r = 0.997 for IAA and r = 0.998
for SA). In addition, the plots of log Ip vs log ν (Figure S6a3 and b3 (ESI)) yielded slopes of 0.68 for IAA and 0.52 for SA. These results
corroborate the finding that the oxidation of SA is predominantly
diffusion-controlled, whereas the oxidation of IAA is mainly governed
by diffusion with a minor contribution from adsorption.

The
oxidation of IAA involves the transfer of two electrons and
one proton, with formation of a 3-methyleneindolenine carboxylic acid
cation as the principal oxidized species (). The dependence
of the anodic peak potential on pH, evaluated using Britton–Robinson
buffer (Figure S4b (ESI)), exhibited a slope of −56.3 mV pH–1, which supports the participation of protons in the oxidation process
and is consistent with the proton-coupled electron-transfer mechanism
previously reported for IAA. In turn, SA undergoes an electrochemical
oxidation involving two electrons and two protons, leading to a quinone-like
oxidized product (). As shown in Figure S4
c (ESI), SA exhibited a well-defined oxidation peak only under strongly
alkaline conditions (pH ≥ 12); therefore, a systematic pH-dependent
mechanistic evaluation could not be established for this analyte.
Consequently, the proposed proton and electron stoichiometry for SA
was assigned based on previous literature reports. These oxidation
pathways account for the well-separated anodic peaks observed during
simultaneous voltammetric measurements.

Optimization
of SWV Parameters and Analytical
Signal Selection

SWV was employed for the simultaneous determination
of IAA and SA in sodium hydroxide solution (pH 13), with the oxidation
responses monitored between +0.10 and +0.80 V vs Ag|AgCl (sat.). The
SWV parameters were optimized by evaluating modulation amplitude,
frequency, and step potential using standard solutions containing
50 μM each of IAA and SA. The optimization results, including
the evaluation of peak current, minimal full width at half-maximum
(fwhm), and peak height (IP), are presented in Figures S7 and S8 (ESI). Based on the best compromise between
peak intensity, peak definition, and simultaneous resolution of both
analytes, the optimized SWV parameters were set to a modulation amplitude
of 50 mV, a frequency of 1 Hz, and a step potential of 0.5 mV, which
were used in the subsequent analytical studies.

Detection Performance of the 3D-CB/HEC–CH
Electrode

0.335 μM for SA, and limits of quantification (LOQs) of 1.359

The stability
of the SWV response obtained with the 3D-CB/HEC–CH
electrode was assessed at two concentration levels within the calibration
range, namely 12 and 62 μM for both IAA and SA. At each concentration,
eight consecutive measurements (n = 8) were performed in a single
day to assess intraday precision, whereas interday precision was evaluated
using measurements collected after a 48 h interval (raw data are provided
in Table S2, ESI). The intra- and interday mean responses were compared at each concentration
using one-way ANOVA (α = 0.05). The results are summarized in .

For IAA and SA, the 3D-CB/HEC–CH electrode
exhibited satisfactory
intraday and interday precision, with RSD values below 4.70% (). The obtained values
were consistent with the criteria established by the Association of
Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC). The
low variability observed across the measurements indicates good response
stability and suggests the absence of a memory effect at the electrode
surface, including during alternating injections of low- and high-concentration
solutions.

Figure S10 (ESI) shows the voltammetric responses recorded
in triplicate for a standard
solution containing 25 μM IAA and SA, using three independently
prepared electrodes, as an additional assessment of intra- and interday
precision. The RSD values obtained were 4.55% for IAA and 4.49% for
SA. One-way ANOVA revealed a statistically significant difference
among electrodes for IAA (F = 9.51, p = 0.0138), whereas no significant
difference was observed for SA (F = 1.37, p = 0.323). Nevertheless,
each electrode individually exhibited good precision, with RSD values
within acceptable limits across the triplicate measurements, indicating
that the observed variability arose from small differences between
electrodes rather than from inconsistent performance of any single
electrode.

A study was conducted to evaluate the mutual influence
of IAA and
SA within the working range of the electrode. In this experiment,
the maximum concentration of each compound (87 μM) was introduced
into the system, followed by progressive additions to achieve a 1:1
ratio of the analytes. The goal was to determine whether the presence
of one analyte would significantly affect the anodic signal of the
other (Figure S11 (ESI)).

The mutual influence of IAA and SA on each other’s
anodic
signals was evaluated within the working range up to a 1:1 ratio of
the analytes (Figure S11, ESI). Signal recovery remained within ±10% throughout
the tested range; above 87 μM, however, larger deviations were
observed, supporting the selection of this concentration as the upper
limit of the working range and confirming accurate simultaneous determination
of IAA and SA without significant mutual interference. These results
demonstrate that the 3D-CB/HEC–CH electrode can reliably measure
IAA and SA when both are present, within the established working range,
including up to the upper limit of 87 μM, confirming that simultaneous
detection of both compounds is feasible in complex plant samples (data
reported in Table S3, ESI).

In addition, tests were conducted with other interfering
compounds
to evaluate the reliability of the 3D-CB/HEC–CH electrode in
plant samples. The influence of potential interferents on the SWV
responses of IAA and SA (87 μM) was evaluated under optimized
conditions. Each species was analyzed in a 1:1 ratio with IAA and
SA: phenolic compounds (gallic acid and catechol), organic acids (benzoic
acid and citric acid), antioxidant compounds (ascorbic acid and rutin),
sugars (sucrose), an amino acid (l-phenylalanine (Phe)),
and phytohormones (kinetin, indole-3-butyric acid (IBA), and abscisic
acid (ABA)). In all cases, the anodic peak current (IAA, ∼+0.28
V; SA, ∼+0.45 V) varied by ≤5% relative to the signals
of the pure compounds (). The raw triplicate data used for these analyses are presented
in Table S4 (ESI).

The assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity
were satisfied
for all groups, with no significant deviations observed based on the
corresponding p-values. One-way ANOVA revealed no statistically significant
differences among the group means at a significance level of α
= 0.05.

The robustness of the method was examined by independently
varying
temperature, operator, and supporting electrolyte concentration while
maintaining the remaining experimental conditions unchanged. None
of these factors produced a statistically significant effect on the
analytical response, based on triplicate measurements (Table S5, ESI).

Overall, the 3D-CB/HEC–CH-based SWV method exhibited satisfactory
linearity, selectivity, precision, and robustness, supporting its
applicability to the analysis of real plant samples. The main analytical

Real Sample Analysis

The applicability
of the proposed SWV method, using the modified electrode, was evaluated
for the quantification of IAA and SA in real plant matrices. Root
and leaf samples of soybean and maize, as well as root, leaf, and
green fruit samples of strawberry, were analyzed under the optimized
experimental conditions.

The plant samples were subjected to
the established extraction procedure, and the extracts were analyzed
before and after fortification using the standard-addition method.
The additions were performed within the linear concentration range
established for each analyte, and the net peak currents are presented
in Table S6 (ESI). Calibration plots were generated by applying least-squares linear
regression to the complete set of addition levels. Each determination
was carried out in triplicate. The corresponding calibration equations
and correlation coefficients are summarized in , and the recorded SWV voltammograms () show well-defined
oxidation peaks for both IAA and SA in the plant extracts.

The proposed method enabled the quantification
of endogenous phytohormones
in plant tissue extracts, yielding concentrations of 8.99 μM
of IAA and 1.08 μM of SA in soybean, 11.57 μM of IAA and
6.06 μM of SA in maize, and 41.53 μM of IAA and 17.35
μM of SA in strawberry extracts, which exhibited the highest
phytohormone levels among the analyzed samples. The endogenous concentrations
of IAA and SA in plant extracts were determined by the standard-addition
method to minimize matrix effects.

Spike-recovery assays were
used to assess the accuracy of the proposed
method in real plant extracts. The samples were fortified with known
amounts of IAA and SA, followed by electrochemical measurements under
the optimized conditions. The concentrations of the fortified samples
were calculated using the corresponding standard-addition calibration
equations for each extract. Recovery (%) was calculated as the ratio
of the increase in the measured concentration after fortification
to the added concentration of each analyte.

The accuracy of
IAA and SA determinations was assessed using soybean,
maize, and strawberry extracts fortified with 25 and 75 μM for
each analyte (n = 3). Electrochemical measurements were conducted
under the optimized SWV/3D-CB/HEC–CH conditions, with the results
presented in .

For IAA, recoveries ranged from 96.82% to 101.37%,
with RSD values
between 2.74% and 5.27%. For SA, recoveries ranged from 98.13% to
102.98%, with RSD values between 1.92% and 4.01% (). Considering all fortification
levels and plant matrices, the overall mean recovery was 99.27 ±
1.71%, with an RSD of 1.72%. These results demonstrate adequate precision
and accuracy and indicate that the sample matrices did not significantly
interfere with the determination of either analyte. The raw triplicate
data used for the recovery calculations are provided in the Supporting Information (Table S7, ESI).

A two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied
to evaluate the trueness of the results by comparing theoretical and
experimentally determined concentrations. At the 95% confidence level,
all calculated values were lower than the critical value, indicating
no statistically significant difference between added and found concentrations.
The recovery results indicate that the proposed approach provides
reliable determination of IAA and SA in soybean, maize, and strawberry
samples.

a comparison between the proposed approach and analytical methods
previously described for the determination of IAA and SA. Although
many electrochemical sensors described in the literature exhibit high
sensitivity, they rely on elaborate architectures and nanomaterials,
which may hinder routine application. As shown in , these platforms commonly employ

 MXenes, molecularly imprinted polymers, gold nanoparticles, and other nanostructured
materials that require multistep fabrication
and surface modification procedures. In contrast, the proposed 3D-CB/HEC–CH
sensor employs a simple HEC–CH biopolymer bilayer deposited
onto a 3D-printed carbon electrode through a straightforward modification
process, eliminating the need for nanostructured materials while enabling
reliable simultaneous determination of IAA and SA.

Although chromatographic techniques
−

 generally offer
wider
linear ranges and lower detection limits compared with electrochemical
methods, as demonstrated by the LODs and linear ranges presented in , they require expensive
instrumentation, specialized infrastructure, and laborious sample
preparation, limiting large-scale monitoring. Electrochemical sensors,
in turn, offer the advantages of faster analysis, simpler instrumentation,
and lower operational costs, making them attractive for large-scale
and field-based applications.

The proposed method employs biodegradable
polymeric materials and
a simple 3D-CB electrode architecture, using low-impact solvents in
accordance with green analytical principles. The simultaneous determination of IAA and SA was achieved using
SWV, a rapid and sensitive technique that remains scarcely explored
for the simultaneous detection of these phytohormones. The fast signal
acquisition and reduced analysis time, inherent to SWV, further enhance
the practical applicability of the proposed method. Together with
the straightforward fabrication of both the 3D-printed electrode and
the HEC–CH surface modification, as well as reduced operational
cost, the proposed platform is easily scalable, offering a practical,
sustainable, reproducible, and time-efficient alternative for the
routine monitoring of IAA and SA in plant matrices.

Accordingly,
the proposed sensor is best regarded as a complementary
tool for rapid, low-cost screening applications, whereas chromatographic
and mass spectrometry-based techniques remain more suitable for confirmatory
analysis or scenarios requiring detection at submicromolar concentrations.

Conclusion

In this work, a 3D-CB/HEC–CH
electrode was successfully
developed for the simultaneous voltammetric detection of IAA and SA,
combining low-cost 3D-printed electrodes with a biodegradable HEC–CH
bilayer, without the need for nanomaterials or multistep surface modifications.
The method exhibited excellent linearity (r = 0.999), low detection
limits (0.446 μM for IAA and 0.335 μM for SA), and good
precision (RSD < 4.70%) and was successfully validated in soybean,
maize, and strawberry matrices, with recoveries between 96.82 and
101.37% for IAA and 98.13 and 102.98% for SA, confirming its reliability
across tissues with different compositions.

Compared with chromatographic
techniques, the proposed method offers
faster analysis, simpler instrumentation, and lower operational costs,
making it particularly well-suited for rapid screening applications,
whereas trace-level confirmatory analysis remains more appropriate
for chromatographic and mass spectrometry-based approaches. Future
work should focus on testing the electrode under field conditions
and with a broader range of plant species, as well as exploring its
integration into portable or automated platforms. Overall, the 3D-CB/HEC–CH
electrode constitutes a sustainable, low-cost, and field-deployable
alternative for decentralized phytohormone monitoring, with promising
potential for application in precision agriculture.

Methods

Reagents and Samples

The chemicals
used in this study were purchased from the following suppliers: HEC
from Quimisul (São Paulo, SP, Brazil); boric acid, sodium hydroxide,
and ascorbic acid from Alphatec (São Paulo, SP, Brazil); potassium
ferricyanide, potassium chloride, CH, IAA, abscisic acid (ABA), indole-3-butyric
acid (IBA), and kinetin from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA); potassium
ferrocyanide, SA, and benzoic acid from Exodo Científica (Hortolándia,
SP, Brazil); ethyl alcohol from ACS Científica (São
Paulo, SP, Brazil); sodium phosphate monobasic, sodium phosphate dibasic,
phosphoric acid, citric acid, and catechol from Neon (Suzano, SP,
Brazil); acetic acid and gallic acid from Dinâmica (Indaiatuba,
SP, Brazil); and l-phenylalanine from Synth (Diadema, SP,
Brazil). All solutions were prepared using distilled water (resistivity
≥ 18 MΩ·cm), except for the IAA, SA, ABA, IBA, and
kinetin standard solutions, which were prepared in ethanol. All chemicals
were of analytical grade and were used as received, without further
treatment.

The influence of pH on the electrochemical response
of IAA and SA was first evaluated using a 0.1 M Britton–Robinson
(BR) buffer solution, prepared from acetic, boric, and phosphoric
acids mixed in equimolar proportions, which covers a pH range of 7
to 13. Reported structural alterations in CH under acidic conditions
precluded the evaluation of lower pH values. BR buffer, PBS, and borate buffer solutions were selected for subsequent
evaluation as supporting electrolytes, with their pH adjusted using
0.5 M sodium hydroxide. These pH values were selected based on the
acid–base properties of IAA and SA, since their protonation
state directly affects their oxidation mechanism and electron-transfer
kinetics. Under alkaline conditions, progressive deprotonation of
the carboxylic groups in both analytes, as well as the phenolic group
in SA, alters their voltammetric response, which justifies exploring
alkaline media to characterize their electrochemical behavior.

Stock standard solutions of IAA and SA
were prepared at a concentration
of 0.025 M in ethanol and used to construct separate analytical calibration
curves for each analyte. For the simultaneous determination of both
analytes and for the standard addition experiments, mixed standard
solutions were prepared at a concentration of 0.0125 M for each compound
in the same medium.

To evaluate the applicability of the proposed
sensor, plant samples
were analyzed using the standard addition method to minimize potential
matrix effects. Soybean and maize roots and leaves, as well as strawberry
roots, leaves, and fruits, were collected from a rural property located
in Rio Verde, Goiás, Brazil. Approximately 300 g of each plant
tissue was macerated overnight in 30 mL of ethanol. The mixture was
subjected to ultrasonic extraction (40 kHz) for 30 min at room temperature.
Following extraction, the samples underwent centrifugation at 5000
× g for 30 min. The resulting supernatant was
then collected for electrochemical analysis. For the electrochemical
assays, 0.1 mL of the obtained extract was added to the electrochemical
cell containing the supporting electrolyte.

Instrumentation
and Equipment

All
electrochemical experiments were conducted at ambient temperature
under dissolved oxygen conditions with a PGSTAT302N potentiostat/galvanostat
(Eco Chemie, Metrohm, Utrecht, Netherlands) operated via NOVA 2.1.7.
Both cyclic voltammetry (CV) and SWV were performed using a three-electrode
configuration comprising a 3D-CB/HEC–CH working electrode,
a platinum wire auxiliary electrode (Metrohm, Utrecht, Netherlands),
and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode (sat. KCl; Metrohm, Utrecht, Netherlands).

CB/PLA conductive filament (Protopasta, Vancouver, WA, USA) was
used to produce the 3D-CB electrodes with a Creality Ender 2 Pro printer
(Shenzhen, Guangdong, China). Each electrode measured 50 mm in length,
with outer and inner diameters of 4.60 and 3.31 mm, respectively.
The electrode geometry was designed in Autodesk Fusion and converted
into printable files using Creality Slicer. The design file is included
in the Supporting Information (Figure S12, ESI) to facilitate reproduction of the
fabrication procedure. Surface preparation consisted of sequential
polishing with 600- and 1200-grit sandpaper using ultrapure water.

Preparation and Characterization of 3D-CB/HEC–CH

Prior to each measurement, the 3D-printed carbon electrode was
mechanically polished and electrochemically activated, following a
protocol adapted from the literature. For film preparation, 1% (w/v) stock solutions of HEC and CH were
used. The CH solution was prepared by dissolving CH in 0.10 M acetic
acid, whereas water was used as the solvent for HEC. The bilayer was
deposited by drop-casting: 10 μL of HEC solution was first applied
onto the electrode surface and dried at 40 °C for 24 h, after
which 10 μL of CH solution was applied and dried under the same
conditions. This renewal protocol, comprising
polishing, activation, and fresh HEC–CH deposition, was performed
daily prior to each set of measurements, ensuring reproducible electrode
surfaces while minimizing degradation of the biopolymer film upon
prolonged exposure to the alkaline supporting electrolyte.

Electrode
characterization was performed by CV using a 0.020 M [Fe­(CN)6]3–/4– solution prepared in 0.10 M KCl.
Voltammograms were recorded between −0.2 and +0.7 V at scan
rates ranging from 10 to 100 mV s–1, with a potential
step of 1 mV. The resulting data were used to estimate the electroactive
surface area using a modified Randles–Ševčík
equation incorporating the transfer coefficient, considering the observed
irreversible electron-transfer behavior.

Electrochemical impedance
spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted in the
same redox probe medium at the formal potential, using an AC amplitude
of 10 mV and frequencies from 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz.

Scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) was used to examine the electrode
morphology using a JSM-6610 microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). The surface
elemental composition was determined by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDS) using a detector coupled to the microscope and a Thermo Scientific
NSS Spectral Imaging system.

SWV was used for the simultaneous
detection of IAA and SA under
optimized experimental conditions.
