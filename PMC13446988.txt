Combined transcatheter mitral and tricuspid edge-to-edge repair or tricuspid edge-to-edge repair alone in moderate mitral regurgitation: a propensity-matched analysis
Authors: Mohammad Kassar, Fabien Praz, Muhammed Gerçek, Vera Fortmeier, Karl-Patrik Kresoja, Jennifer von Stein, Christoph Pauschinger, Wolfgang Rottbauer, Bjoern Goebel, Paolo Denti, Paul Achouh, Tienush Rassaf, Manuel Barreiro-Perez, Peter Boekstegers, Andreas Rück, Monika Zdanyte, Marianna Adamo, Flavien Vincent, Philipp Schlegel, Sebastian Rosch, Mirjam G Wild, Christian Besler, Stefan Toggweiler, Stephanie Brunner, Julia Grapsa, Tiffany Patterson, Holger Thiele, Tobias Kister, Giuseppe Tarantini, Giulia Masiero, Marco De Carlo, Alessandro Sticchi, Fabian Voss, Amin Polzin, Antonio Popolo Rubbio, Francesco Bedogni, Mathias H Konstandin, Eric Van Belle, Marco Metra, Tobias Geisler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsThe coexistence of moderate mitral regurgitation (MR) and severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is common, yet evidence guiding optimal management remains limited. Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) of both valves—performed either sequentially or in combination—has emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy. This study aimed to assess the prognostic impact of moderate MR in patients undergoing tricuspid TEER (T-TEER) for severe TR and to evaluate whether concomitant mitral TEER (M-TEER) improves clinical outcomes.MethodsData from the EuroTR registry (2016–25) were analysed, including patients with severe TR treated with T-TEER. Outcomes were compared between patients with untreated moderate MR and those who underwent concomitant M-TEER using propensity score matching (PSM). The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality at 2 years. Secondary endpoints included New York Heart Association (NYHA) class, 6 min walk distance (6MWD), TR severity, and heart failure rehospitalizations.ResultsAmong 3100 patients, 30% had moderate MR, which was associated with higher 2-year mortality (23% vs 37%, p<0.0001). After PSM, 217 matched patients treated with concomitant M-TEER had greater TR reduction (−1.9 vs −1.6 grades, P = .001), better NYHA improvement, and increased 6MWD at follow-up. Survival was higher in the combined treatment group (87% vs 76% at 1 year; 81% vs 70% at 2 years, P = .005). In a multivariable analysis, moderate MR predicted increased mortality [hazard ratio (HR) 1.81, P = .005), while combined M-TEER predicted better survival (HR 0.46, P < .0001).ConclusionsModerate MR predicts impaired prognosis in patients undergoing T-TEER for treatment of severe TR. Concomitant M-TEER is associated with improved survival and functional outcomes in this population with multivalve disease. These findings are hypothesis-generating and need to be tested in a dedicated randomized controlled trial.

Introduction

The prevalence of multiple valvular heart disease increases in high- and middle-income countries due to population ageing and longer life expectancy of patients with heart failure due to improved management and medical treatment.1 Patients with multiple valvular heart disease have a poor prognosis, particularly in case of concomitant severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR), an often-complex condition largely underdiagnosed and undertreated.2 Secondary TR, which accounts for 85% of TR, is related to left-sided heart disease in 60% of the cases3 and a marker of impaired survival.4 Similarly, a large UK registry found that combined mitral regurgitation (MR) and TR have a synergistic negative effect on long-term outcomes compared with isolated MR or TR.5

According to the classically described pathophysiological pathway linking TR to MR, long-standing severe MR with secondary pulmonary hypertension leads to relevant ventricular TR.6 However, simultaneous apparition of MR and TR due to biatrial dilatation principally (but not exclusively) linked to permanent atrial fibrillation may lead to severe atrial TR accompanied by moderate MR due to faster dilatation of the less fibrous tricuspid annulus7; this mechanism account for about 25% of the cases. As TR worsens, the right ventricle eventually fails, right ventricular diastolic pressure rises, and the interventricular septum shifts towards the left ventricle during diastole (diastolic D-shaping). As a result of ventricular interdependence, left ventricular (LV) end-diastolic volume decreases and pressure increases, finally reducing cardiac output (CO) and possibly masking actual MR severity.8,9 Interventional TR treatment could therefore uncover severe MR by improving LV filling and CO.

Although concomitant treatment of both valvular diseases (≥3+ MR and ≥moderate TR) is indicated during surgery,10 in case of transcatheter approaches, the benefit and optimal timing have not been defined yet. Early modelling studies showed that TR correction increased LV fibre stress by 26%, due to increased filling.11–13 These findings were supported by clinical data, which showed that treating TR improved right ventricular performance, LV filling, and CO, thereby alleviating patient symptoms,14 though this improvement may be partially hindered in the presence of significant MR which mitigates the positive impact of TR treatment on LV filling and CO.

Moderate MR was associated with worse clinical outcomes after transcatheter aortic valve implantation.15–17 However, because of the limitations of the data hitherto available, it is still not clear whether this association is related to MR or simply a marker of worse prognosis.

Thus, using data of patients with severe TR who underwent tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER) included into the EuroTR registry, the aim of this study was to first assess the functional and prognostic impact of moderate MR and to explore whether concomitant mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) modifies prognosis.

Methods

Study cohorts and procedural techniques

Patients from tertiary European Heart Valves Centers who underwent T-TEER for treatment of ≥2+ TR between 2016 and 2025 were retrospectively included into the EuroTR registry (NCT06307262). Given the retrospective design and the very limited data available, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed; instead, the adequacy of the sample size was assessed based on the precision of effect estimates, with hazard ratios (HRs) interpreted in conjunction with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All eligible patients enrolled in the EuroTR registry, which represents one of the largest T-TEER registries worldwide, were considered for inclusion into the present study.

For the primary analysis of the impact of moderate or moderate-to-severe MR on outcomes, patients with 4+ MR and patients who underwent concomitant M-TEER were excluded. The rest of the cohort was separated into two groups: patients with untreated moderate MR grade 2+ or 3+ (MR+ group) were compared with patients with MR grade 0 or 1+ (MR− group) (Figure 1A).

MR, mitral regurgitation; TR, tricuspid regurgitation; M-TEER, mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge-repair, T-TEER, tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair

Patients were selected for treatment according to an individual decision made by the local interdisciplinary Heart Team. The T-TEER procedure was conducted using either the PASCAL (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA, USA) or the TriClip system (Abbott, Santa Clara, CA, USA), as described elsewhere.18

For the secondary analysis, a subgroup of patients with moderate or moderate-to-severe MR (grade 2+ or 3+) treated during the same procedure was compared with matched patients with untreated MR grade 2+ or 3+ (Figure 1B); staged M-TEER procedures performed after index T-TEER were not considered this analysis. Heart failure symptoms and exercise capacity were assessed using the New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class and the 6 min walk distance (6MWD). Echocardiographic evaluations were performed by experienced cardiologist at each centre in line with current guidelines.19–22 Patients were followed for 2 years according to each centre’s standard of care. The study was approved by each centre’s local ethics committee and adheres to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study outcomes

The association between moderate or moderate-to-severe MR and all-cause mortality within 2 years after T-TEER was tested in patients who underwent T-TEER only, using a Kaplan–Meier analysis.

As a second study analysis, a propensity score matching was used to assess whether M-TEER performed in addition to T-TEER during the same procedure modified patient prognosis. For this analysis, all-cause mortality was chosen as a primary endpoint. Functional improvement according to NYHA class, 6MWD, as well as TR reduction were assessed as secondary endpoints. Mitral regurgitation was graded on a scale from 1+ to 4+ according to the guidelines of the American Society of Echocardiography,22 and TR was graded on a scale from I to V according to Hahn and Zamorano.19 Vital status at the time of the inclusion was evaluated through review of charts and follow-up records.

Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q-Q plots. In case of symmetrical distribution, quantitative values were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using independent t-test for independent samples; otherwise, values appeared as median and 25th–75th percentiles and were compared by the Mann–Whitney U test. Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test across all numeric variables. Uni- and multivariable Cox regression analysis of continuous variables were performed to determine relevant predictors of all-cause mortality. Multivariable linear regression models were used for continuous secondary outcomes and were constructed using clinically plausible covariates; assumptions of normality and homogeneity of residuals, as well as linearity of continuous predictors, were assessed using residual diagnostics and Levene’s test, with no substantial violations detected. Propensity score was modelled using a multivariable logistic regression model based on the following baseline variables: any signs of right heart failure, age, diabetes, sex, European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE) II score, Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) score, MR and TR grades, LV ejection fraction (LVEF), atrial fibrillation, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, mean pulmonary arterial pressure, and NYHA functional class. These variables were selected based on their demonstrated or presumed association with treatment strategy selection and/or mortality, as well as clinical relevance. Propensity score matching was performed using 1:1 greedy nearest-neighbour matching with a calliper width of 0.2 SD of the logit of the propensity score. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated for all baseline variables in the matched cohort to assess covariate balance, using a threshold of <0.1.23 All subsequent outcome analyses were conducted in the propensity score–matched (PSM) cohorts. As a sensitivity analysis, an inverse probability-of-treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied using the same baseline covariates. Results from the IPTW model were compared with those from the primary PSM analysis to evaluate robustness.

Associations with all-cause mortality were assessed using univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression. For multivariable Cox models, variables associated with the outcome at P < .10 in univariable analyses were considered for inclusion, while accounting for collinearity using a variance inflation factor threshold > 2.5. No automated stepwise selection procedures were applied. In addition, a prespecified multivariable Cox model based on a literature- and clinically informed covariate set was fitted as a sensitivity analysis, including age, sex, diabetes, EuroSCORE II, STS score, mitral and TR grades, LVEF, atrial fibrillation, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, mean pulmonary arterial pressure, NYHA functional class, and signs of right heart failure.

Event rates were based on Kaplan–Meier estimates in time-to-first-event analyses, and HRs with 95% CIs were reported. A P < .05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Time zero for all survival analyses was defined as the date of the T-TEER procedure. Follow-up time was calculated from the procedure date until the occurrence of the outcome of interest or until 2 years after the procedure, with patients censored at the last known follow-up or at 2 years, whichever occurred first. Missing baseline covariates were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations. Missingness overview is shown in Supplemental Table 1. Continuous variables were imputed using predictive mean matching and categorical variables using logistic regression. Ten imputed datasets were generated, and estimates were pooled using Rubin’s rules. The imputation model was performed under the assumption that data were missing at random, meaning that the probability of missingness could be explained by observed patient characteristics included in the model. Analyses were performed with XLSTAT Premium, version 2022.1.2 (Addinsoft, New York, NY, USA), IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), or R.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the entire cohort

Among the 3100 patients of the EuroTR, 2762 were included in the primary analysis. The reason for exclusion was concomitant M-TEER in 217 patients and untreated severe MR in 121 (Figure 1A). The mean age of the MR+ and MR− groups was comparable (78.7 ± 6.8 vs 78 ± 7.3 years, P = .12) with similar proportion of female patients (55% vs 54%, P = .59). The mean surgical risk was high in both groups (EuroSCORE II 6.3 ± 6.9 for the MR+ group and 6.2 ± 5.5 for the MR− group, P = .65). TR severity in the MR+ group was classified as torrential in 23%, massive in 33%, and severe in 43%, while in the MR− group TR severity was torrential in 20%, massive in 33%, and severe in 44% of the cases.

Notably, 90% of the MR+ group had grade 2+ MR. The majority of patients experienced NYHA class III-IV dyspnoea at baseline (83% for the MR+ group and 82% for the MR− group, respectively, P = .028) and had elevated N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide levels [4787.5 (3297–8954) pg/mL for the MR+ group and 4292 (3145–8354) pg/mL for the MR− group, P = .100).

Before treatment, about 70% of patients presented with signs of right heart failure in both groups and renal function was impaired in most of the subjects (estimated glomerular filtration rate < 45 mL/min in 60% of both groups). Left ventricular function was preserved in both groups (Table 1), while patients in the MR+ group had significantly larger left ventricles (49.2 ± 8.4 mm vs 47.8 ± 7.6 mm, P < .0001) and a lower LVEF (52.2 ± 11.6% vs 53.3 ± 10.8%, P = .028) (Table 1).

Propensity score–matched cohort

The 217 patients excluded from the primary analysis who underwent M-TEER during the same procedure (concomitant M-TEER group) were included into the PSM cohort resulting in 217 pairs (Figure 1B). After matching, age (79.7 ± 7.2 vs 79.6 ± 7.4, SMD 0.014), sex (60% vs 59% female, SMD 0.020), symptoms (i.e. NYHA class, symptoms of right heart failure), surgical risk [EuroSCORE II: 6.6 (3.9–7.4) vs 6.7 (3.7–7.7), SMD −0.041], and most cardiovascular risk factors or medical treatment (Table 2) did not differ significantly when comparing concomitant M-TEER vs isolated T-TEER patients. However, the concomitant M-TEER group showed slightly higher mean (mPAP) and diastolic pulmonary arterial pressures (dPAP) (mPAP 31.5 ± 7.5 vs 32.6 ± 7.4 mmHg; SMD −0.147, and dPAP 19.9 ± 8.3 vs 20.9 ± 9.9 mmHg; SMD −0.109). Alanine aminotransferase values were also modestly higher in the concomitant group [21 (13–27) vs 23 (13–30) U/L; SMD −0.157]. Additional minor residual imbalances included a higher fractional area change (FAC 36.8 ± 8.5 vs 35.6 ± 11.9; SMD 0.116) and a higher tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (16.6 ± 4.1 vs 16.1 ± 3.8; SMD 0.126) in the T-TEER only group.

Post-procedural outcomes

At hospital discharge, higher TR reduction (mean TR reduction 1.9 ± 1.0 vs1.6 ± 1.2 grade, P = .001) and lower residual TR (mean TR grade 2.0 ± 1.0 vs 1.5 ± 0.9, P = .0001) were achieved in the concomitant M-TEER group compared with the isolated T-TEER group: 83% of patients having moderate or less TR, compared with only 72% after T-TEER only (P = .0001) (Table 3). After a median follow-up of 9 months, TR severity was assessed in 177 (82%) patients in the isolated T-TEER group and 189 (87%) patients in the concomitant M-TEER group. There was a more sustained TR reduction in the concomitant M-TEER group, with 76% of patients having moderate or less TR, compared with 67% in the isolated T-TEER group (P = .028) (Figure 2). Although the baseline NYHA functional class was similar between both groups (SMD 0.016), the concomitant M-TEER group experienced better symptom relief with 60% achieving class ≤II, compared with 42% in the isolated T-TEER group (P = .02; Figure 3 and Table 3) at a median follow-up of 7 months (IQR 135–460 days). In addition, only the concomitant M-TEER group showed a significant increase in the 6MWD at follow-up (231 ± 90 m at baseline vs 282 ± 141 m at follow-up, P = .001) compared with the isolated T-TEER group (238 ± 75 m at baseline vs 258 ± 112 m at follow-up, P = .120) [median follow-up of 200 days (IQR 88–390); Table 3].

Survival at 30 days, 1 and 2 years, and rehospitalizations

Among the 2762 patients treated for severe TR using T-TEER included in the primary analysis, the survival rate of the MR+ patients was 63% (95% CI 0.61;0.69), whereas 77% of MR− patients were alive (P < .0001 according to log-rank testing; 95% CI 0.74;0.79) (Figure 4) at 2 years (median follow-up: 510 days). When patients were further stratified into MR grade 2+, MR grade 3+, and those without relevant MR (MR−), Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed a markedly reduced 2-year survival for the MR grade 2+ group compared with the MR− group (log-rank P < .0001) (see Supplementary data online, Figure S1).

In the PSM cohort, 8 patients (4.0%) in the isolated T-TEER group and 10 patients (5%) in the concomitant M-TEER group had died (P = .841) at 30 days. After 1 year, 76% of patients in the isolated T-TEER group were alive [53 deaths (95% CI 0.62–0.76)], compared with 87% in the concomitant M-TEER group [28 deaths (95% CI 0.80–0.90), P = .002]. Similarly, at 2 years, the survival rate was 70% in the isolated T-TEER group [66 deaths (95% CI 0.48–0.65)] and 81% of the concomitant M-TEER group [41 deaths (95% CI 0.70–0.83), P = .007] (Table 3 and Figure 5). Within 2 years, 22% of patients in the isolated T-TEER group experienced cardiac rehospitalization (95% CI 0.66–0.77), compared with 13% in the concomitant M-TEER group (95% CI 0.83–0.93, P = .011) (Table 3). In a subgroup analysis including only patients with MR grade 2+, concomitant M-TEER was superior to T-TEER only according to Kaplan–Meier estimates of survival at 2 years (log-rank P < .0001) (see Supplementary data online, Figure S2).

Discussion

This study showed the negative impact of associated moderate (2+) or moderate-to-severe MR (3+) on mortality in a large population of patients undergoing T-TEER at experienced tertiary European Heart Valve Centres. After propensity score matching, concomitant M-TEER was found to improve prognosis and functional status, as well as to promote sustained TR reduction (Structured Graphical Abstract).

Haemodynamic consequences and clinical impact of moderate mitral regurgitation in the context of severe tricuspid regurgitation

Moderate MR has been historically associated with worse prognosis in the Framingham Heart Study,24 as well as after transcatheter aortic and tricuspid interventions.15,16,25 Our study confirms the deleterious impact on mortality of untreated moderate or moderate-to-severe MR 2 years after isolated T-TEER in a large contemporary population of patients. The high prevalence of moderate MR of around 30% in our cohort undergoing T-TEER underscores the necessity of understanding the clinical impact of this entity to optimize treatment strategies. Concomitant M-TEER was associated with more frequent TR reduction to ≤2+ (83% of patients), better symptom control, and a reduced risk of hospital readmissions for heart failure. Furthermore, achieving TR reduction to ≤2+ was directly associated with improved 1-year survival (87%), highlighting the potential therapeutic advantage of a combined procedure addressing MR and TR at the same time over standard of care.

This study reiterates the complexity of the haemodynamic interactions between TR and MR. The impact of moderate MR and its treatment on the outcomes of patients with severe TR treated with T-TEER could have several explanations. On one hand, MR defined as moderate could have been underestimated due to the impact of TR on LV preload (low CO and interventricular interaction11) and become severe after TR treatment.25 This could be of particular significance in patients with elevated left atrial pressure and heart failure with preserved ejection faction.12,13 On the other hand, MR increases left atrial and post-capillary pressures26 and could in turn mitigate long-term TR reduction: studies show that reduction of pulmonary pressure following treatment of severe MR also reduces TR in up to 30% of the cases.27 Our data suggest that moderate MR should therefore not be looked as an innocent bystander in T-TEER candidates and concomitant treatment considered upfront.

Concomitant mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair: a potential therapeutic strategy

Among patients presenting with both moderate MR and severe TR, concomitant TEER was safe and did not result in a statistically relevant difference in mortality at 30 days (8 deaths in the T-TEER group vs 10 in the concomitant group). Potential risks include longer procedural duration with prolonged general anaesthesia, greater haemodynamic shifts through two valve treatment potentially increasing the risk of afterload mismatch, as well as rare complications not captured in the registry. The PSM analysis reveals that concomitant M-TEER resulted in improved clinical outcomes compared with T-TEER alone with or without delayed M-TEER (not captured in this study). Importantly, the concomitant procedure led to a greater reduction in TR severity, better NYHA functional class improvement, and longer 6MWD, potentially resulting in better mobility and quality of life. Moreover, TR reduction was more durable in the concomitant group. The combined approach also demonstrated an independent survival benefit consistent across different statistical methods (PSM analysis, IPTW, and multivariable Cox regression) at 1 and 2 years with lower incidence of cardiac rehospitalizations. These findings extend on the study of Mehr et al.28 focusing on patients with both severe MR and TR from the international TriValve registry (n = 122; T-TEER only) and from the German TRAMI registry (n = 106; concomitant T-TEER and M-TEER). However, these analyses were limited by the absence of matching leaving uncorrected differences in baseline characteristics.

Our findings suggest that addressing both moderate MR and severe TR concomitantly might mitigate the pathological cycle perpetuated by these interdependent valvular conditions with a relative risk reduction in clinical events of 39% to 46%, depending on the model used (see Supplementary data online, Table S2, Table S3 and Table 5), which corresponds to a number needed to treat of 9–10 patients.

Although our study focuses on the impact of concomitant procedures, an important consideration in the management of patients with concomitant TR and MR is whether to perform valve interventions simultaneously or in a staged fashion. A staged approach—performing T-TEER first and reassessing MR severity before considering M-TEER—may reduce procedural complexity, minimize immediate haemodynamic shifts, and allow time for MR improvement following TR reduction. Indeed, prior studies have reported that a minority of patients experienced a decrease in MR severity after isolated TR intervention, potentially obviating the need for mitral repair in some cases.29,30 However, delaying M-TEER may also postpone symptomatic relief, increase the likelihood of persistent pulmonary hypertension, and expose patients to the risks of a second intervention, including repeated anaesthesia and vascular access. Prospective randomized data are needed to definitively clarify the sequence of treatment (same procedure vs staged).

Limitations

Despite the strengths of a large multicentre cohort and the use of propensity score matching, several limitations must be acknowledged. Propensity score matching was imperfect, and some residual differences between groups persisted, even though residual disbalances were in favour of the control group (T-TEER only). Patients were treated according to local heart failure management practices and no standardized treatment protocol was prespecified. Mitral regurgitation and TR severity were assessed locally at each participating centre without adjudication by a central core laboratory, which may introduce inter-operator variability in imaging interpretation and severity grading. Detailed information on mitral and tricuspid valve anatomy and precise MR aetiology were not available and may represent a possible confounder. Furthermore, no data were collected regarding potential crossover to M-TEER after the index procedure; however, this does not alter the principal message that combined treatment appears superior to no or delayed treatment. In addition, information on prior M-TEER or other previous transcatheter or surgical valvular interventions was not systematically collected in the EuroTR registry and therefore could not be reported in the baseline characteristics or used for adjustment in the analyses.

Procedural experience varied considerably among participating centres, and the proportion of patients undergoing concomitant M-TEER differed across sites, representing an additional potential source of selection bias. However, the direction and statistical significance of the treatment effect were consistent across methods, supporting the robustness of the association between concomitant M-TEER and improved outcomes. Finally, the interpretation of HRs warrants caution, as they may be influenced by inherent selection bias due to conditioning on event-free survival over time. Randomized trials are needed to validate these findings and elucidate the mechanisms underlying the observed benefits of concomitant M-TEER.

Conclusions

This study provides evidence that moderate MR is associated with worse clinical outcomes in patients with severe TR undergoing T-TEER. In this observational analysis, concomitant M-TEER in patients with MR grade 2+ or 3+ was associated with improved survival, symptomatic relief, and functional capacity compared with T-TEER alone. While these findings suggest a potential benefit of simultaneous percutaneous interventions for multivalvular heart disease, they should be considered hypothesis-generating, and prospective randomized trials are warranted to confirm whether a combined approach confers a causal benefit.
