Intravenous placenta-derived mesenchymal stem cells enhance early survival as an adjunct to supportive care in severe burn: a preclinical study
Authors: Eng-Kean Yeong, Houng-Chi Liou, Yan Ting Liou, Chin-Hao Chang, Yu-Xiu Lin, Thai-Yen Ling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSevere burn injury induces a rapid systemic inflammatory response, and early survival depends heavily on timely fluid resuscitation and thermal support. However, optimal supportive care may be difficult to deliver in austere or resource-limited environments. Placenta-derived choriodecidual mesenchymal stem cells (pcMSCs) possess immunomodulatory properties and may improve outcomes when supportive care is suboptimal. This study investigated whether intravenous pcMSC therapy improves early survival after severe burn injury in a preclinical model.MethodsSixty adult male Sprague–Dawley rats underwent standardized 40% total body surface area full-thickness burns and were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 10/group) differing in fluid resuscitation (1× vs. 3× Ringer’s lactate), thermal support (heating lamp vs. incubator warming), and intravenous pcMSC therapy. Survival was monitored for 14 days and analyzed using Kaplan–Meier methods with log-rank testing. Serum TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels, body weight, water intake, and core body temperature were assessed longitudinally in selected groups.ResultsSurvival differed significantly among groups, with 93% of deaths occurring within the first 3 days post-burn. Under low-volume resuscitation, survival declined to 30% in untreated animals and improved to 50% with pcMSC therapy, indicating a survival benefit under insufficient resuscitation conditions. With adequate fluid resuscitation and thermal support, survival was high without pcMSCs (80%) and numerically higher with pcMSCs (90%), although this difference was not statistically significant after adjustment. Thermal support exerted a marked effect on outcomes, with warmed animals demonstrating substantially higher survival despite similar or lower fluid volumes. PcMSC treatment attenuated post-burn hypothermia, reduced weight loss and dehydration, and significantly decreased circulating TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels.ConclusionsPlacenta-derived choriodecidual mesenchymal stem cells may confer an additive survival benefit after severe burn injury, particularly under inadequate fluid resuscitation and thermal support, likely through stabilization of body temperature, improved hydration, and attenuation of systemic inflammation, supporting their potential role as an adjunctive therapy in austere or resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Previous studies have reported that burns extending over 40% of the total body surface area (TBSA) can trigger systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), resulting in massive fluid loss in the first few hours after injury, affecting various functions of systemic organs [1, 2]. In recent decades, although there have been great advancements in the concepts of burn care [3–5], clinical treatments to attenuate SIRS have always been neglected, leading to complications or failure of resuscitation, especially in truly deep burns > 70% TBSA. The mortality can reach as high as 33%-100% and mostly occurs in the early stage of burn, during which the storm is always intense [6–9].

In the literature, high-dose vitamin C infusions, resveratrol, metallothionein, glucocorticoids, ulinastatin, etc., have been explored as anti-inflammatory interventions [10–18], yet none have demonstrated consistent clinical benefit or achieved standard-of care status. In contrast, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have emerged as promising candidates due to their immunomodulatory, regenerative, and cytoprotective properties [19–36]. Preclinical studies have shown that MSCs can attenuate inflammation, reduce burn-related mortality, and mitigate endotoxin-induced acute lung injury [37, 38]. In 2014, the IL-6R (interleukin 6 receptor) antibody has also been proposed as a strategy to temper the cytokine storm, but comparative data on its efficacy and safety relative to MSC-based therapies remain limited [39].

Although the clinical application of stem cells in the acute phase of severe burns is still unclear, accumulating evidence suggests that MSCs may blunt the early inflammatory surge and improve survival. Placenta-derived choriodecidual MSCs (pcMSCs) are particularly attractive because they are readily obtainable from medical waste, can be isolated from healthy donors, and can be reliably expanded and cryopreserved.

In this study, we hypothesized that early pcMSC infusion would modulate the acute inflammatory response and improve early post-burn survival, and investigated whether intravenous administration of human pcMSCs enhanced survival in a rat model of 40% TBSA burns. The rat burn model was selected for its reproducibility, exhibiting comparable pathophysiological responses to thermal injury as humans., allowing relatively precise control over burn size, depth and location, enabling standardized and reproducible experiments and repeated blood sampling and cell injections. This platform enables rigorous evaluation of stem cell–based interventions prior to translation into human clinical trials.

Methods

This study has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

pcMSC isolation and expansion

Human placentas were obtained from healthy donors undergoing cesarean delivery with written informed consent and approval from the Taipei Medical University Hospital Ethics Committee (TMU-JIRB 201703081). Choriodecidual membranes were dissected, washed with Hank’s buffer, minced, and digested overnight at 4 °C in serum-free medium containing protease (0.5 mg/mL), collagenase B (0.5 mg/mL), and DNase I (1 mg/mL). Following filtration (100-µm strainer) and centrifugation, cells were washed and cultured in MCDB201 medium supplemented with insulin–transferrin–selenium, epidermal growth factor (10 ng/mL), and penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were plated on collagen type IV–coated dishes, nonadherent cells removed after 24 h, and adherent pcMSCs expanded with medium changes every 3–4 days.

Experiment design of a rat burn model

All animal procedures were approved by the National Taiwan University College of Medicine and Public Health IACUC (IACUC 20200094).

Animals, housing and husbandry conditions

Male Sprague–Dawley rats (6–8 weeks old, 326–350 g) were obtained from the National Taiwan University Laboratory Animal Center (BioLASCO Taiwan Co., Ltd.). Animals were specific pathogen-free (SPF), wild-type, and had not undergone any prior experimental procedures. Animals were acclimatized for 7 days before experimentation and housed 2–3 per polycarbonate cage with autoclaved bedding and environmental enrichment under a 12:12 h light–dark cycle, at 22 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 10% relative humidity, with filtered air ventilation. Standard rodent chow and filtered water were provided ad libitum. Animals were monitored twice daily for general health, hydration status, body weight, and signs of distress.

Burn induction model

Rats were fasted for 4–6 h and anesthetized with intraperitoneal Zoletil 50 (tiletamine–zolazepam, 20 mg/kg). After shaving the dorsum and positioning the animals in a custom mold, 40% TBSA full-thickness burns were induced by immersing the exposed skin in 100 °C water for 10 s, a method chosen for its reproducibility and consistency in inducing deep dermal to full-thickness injury. Burn size was quantified using ImageJ, and TBSA calculated as described by Gouma et al. [40]. The burn depth was confirmed in tissue samples collected on day 0 (n = 3).

Burned rats were housed individually during recovery to prevent wound interference and maintained under thermal support using an incubator (25–30 °C) in Groups C, D, and F to minimize post-burn hypothermia. Carprofen (0.5 mg/100 g, SC) was administered for analgesia, and wounds were treated with silver sulfadiazine to reduce the risk of infection.

Treatment protocol

Immediately after burn induction, rats received intraperitoneal Ringer’s lactate (40 mL/kg) to simulate early fluid resuscitation following severe burn injury, with or without pcMSCs (2 × 10⁶ cells in 0.5 mL) administered intravenously via the tail vein. The pcMSC dose was selected based on prior studies demonstrating immunomodulatory efficacy in acute inflammatory injury models. For groups receiving multiple doses, Ringer’s lactate was administered at 0, 8, and 16 h post-injury to mimic repeated early resuscitation in severe burns.

Animals, experimental groups, sample size, and randomization

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined a priori. Animals were excluded if they died within 3 h post-burn or did not receive the full experimental intervention. Animals were included if they received the full intervention and had complete data for the outcomes assessed at each time point. A total of 62 animals underwent burn injury induction; following the early exclusions, 60 animals were randomly allocated to six experimental groups using a predefined sequential allocation scheme. Sample size estimation was based on a two-sided log-rank test for survival analysis. Based on prior experimental data, anticipated survival probabilities were 25% in the control group and 85% in the treatment group, corresponding to an approximate hazard ratio of 8.5 under an exponential survival assumption. With a two-sided α of 0.05 and 80% power, fewer than 10 animals per group were required to detect this difference. To account for biological variability, potential non–study-related attrition, and to ensure robust estimation across experimental conditions, we included 10 animals per group. This sample size was selected to balance adequate statistical power with ethical considerations regarding animal use. The experimental groups were defined as follows:

In all applicable groups, fluid resuscitation was initiated prior to pcMSC administration. All animals were housed under identical conditions. The experimental unit was a single rat. The primary outcome measure was 14-day survival. For survival analysis, all remaining animals were included (n = 10 per group). For secondary outcomes, including body temperature, body weight, water intake, and circulating cytokine levels, data from all animals that were alive at each time point were included in the analysis. The investigator administering treatments (Yan-Ting Liou) was aware of group allocation; no blinding was applied during treatment administration or survival assessment.

Measurements and monitoring

Outcomes

The prespecified outcome measures were survival rate, serum cytokine concentrations, rectal temperature, weight lost rate and water intake.

Data collection

Wound images, body weight, water intake, rectal temperature, and blood samples were collected on post-burn days 1, 3, 7, and 14. Blood samples (1 mL) were obtained via the tail vein for quantification of interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

Study endpoints and humane endpoints

The study was terminated on post-burn day 14. Survival rate was the primary outcome measure and was assessed on post-burn days 7 and 14.

Predefined humane endpoints were established to minimize animal suffering. Animals were euthanized before the scheduled study endpoint if they exhibited any of the following signs of severe distress or moribund condition: persistent recumbency or inability to access food or water, unresponsiveness to external stimuli, labored or agonal breathing, severe hypothermia, or marked dehydration or body weight loss exceeding 20% of baseline. Animals meeting humane endpoint criteria were humanely euthanized and recorded as non-survivors for survival analyses.

Animals were monitored at least twice daily throughout the study period, with increased monitoring frequency during the first 72 h post-burn, when mortality risk was highest.

Animal euthanasia

Rats were euthanized using carbon dioxide inhalation. Animals were placed in a transparent ventilated chamber connected to a carbon dioxide source. Carbon dioxide was introduced at a flow rate of 6 L/min for 1–2 min and increased to 10 L/min after loss of posture. Death was confirmed by cessation of respiration and pupillary dilation.

Statistical analysis

Daily survival over a 14-day period was assessed across six experimental groups. Pairwise comparisons of survival proportions at day 14 were performed using Fisher’s exact test, with corresponding odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) estimated. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed, and overall differences in survival among groups were evaluated using the log-rank test.

Continuous outcomes, including body temperature, weight loss rate, water intake, and circulating levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for each experimental group. Because the data were not assumed to follow a normal distribution, prespecified pairwise comparisons between Groups A and B, and between Groups C and D, at each time point (baseline and days 1, 3, 7, and 14) were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Sixty rats were included in the analysis after exclusion of two animals that died within 3 h of burn injury. Baseline characteristics of the animals included a mean body weight of 258 ± 23 g and a mean burn size of 49.6 ± 12.9 cm², corresponding to approximately 38 ± 4% total body surface area. Histological examination confirmed deep-dermal to full-thickness injury.

Overall survival differed significantly among experimental groups (log-rank test; Fig. 1; Table 1), with 93% of deaths occurring within the first three days following burn injury. Survival trends over time (Table 1)

Under conditions of low-volume fluid resuscitation (1× Ringer’s lactate), survival in Group A declined rapidly to 30% by Day 3 and remained unchanged through Day 14. Adjunctive pcMSC therapy (Group B) improved early survival to 50%, which persisted throughout the observation period, indicating a survival advantage under insufficient resuscitation conditions. However, this improvement did not reach statistical significance after adjustment for multiple comparisons.

Pairwise comparison of 14-day survival (day 14) (Table 2)

With adequate fluid resuscitation and thermal support (3× Ringer’s lactate with incubator warming), survival was substantially improved. Group C achieved an 80% survival rate, while Group D, which additionally received pcMSC therapy, demonstrated the highest survival at 90%. Although survival was numerically greater in Group D, the difference between Groups C and D was not statistically significant after Holm–Bonferroni adjustment, indicating that optimized supportive care was the dominant determinant of outcome, with pcMSC therapy providing a modest additive effect. In fact, comparisons evaluating stem cell therapy showed consistent trends toward benefit but did not reach statistical significance after adjustment.

Thermal support exerted a pronounced influence on survival across resuscitation strategies. Despite receiving higher fluid volumes, Group E (3× Ringer’s lactate without warming) experienced progressive mortality, with survival declining to 20% by Day 14. In contrast, Group F (1× Ringer’s lactate with incubator warming) maintained a 60% survival rate, demonstrating that active thermal support significantly improves survival and may outweigh the benefit of increased fluid volume alone.

Attenuation of burn-induced physiological derangements by pcMSCs (Tables 3 and 4)

When comparing Group A and B, pcMSC treatment significantly attenuated hypothermia, reduced post-burn weight loss, and improved hydration status following burn injury, which was otherwise characterized by hypothermia, weight loss, and reduced water intake. Serum TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels were markedly elevated after burn injury and were significantly reduced by pcMSC therapy, consistent with suppression of systemic and inflammasome-related inflammatory responses. No significant physiological or biochemical differences were observed between Groups C and D.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that placenta-derived choriodecidual mesenchymal stem cells (pcMSCs) confer an additive survival benefit following severe burn injury, particularly under conditions of suboptimal fluid resuscitation. This effect appears to be mediated through early immunomodulation and attenuation of the systemic inflammatory response. Overall survival in the study was 62.5%, with all deaths occurring within 14 days and 93% occurring within the first 3 days.

Early mortality was strongly associated with poor hydration, progressive weight loss, hypothermia, and inadequate resuscitation—particularly in rats receiving only a single intraperitoneal dose of lactated Ringer’s solution. In parallel, serum TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels were markedly elevated following burn injury, with peak levels typically occurring between days 1 and 7, coinciding with the period of highest mortality. These findings are consistent with established literature describing early cytokine surges after major burn injury [12, 41]. Notably, IL-6 has been increasingly recognized as a central mediator of the acute inflammatory storm and early vascular injury [39].

PcMSC treatment was associated with stabilization of body temperature, reduced weight loss, and improved water intake (Table 3). These physiological benefits were consistent with significant reductions in circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines (Table 4), indicating effective attenuation of the systemic inflammatory response and confirming the robust immunomodulatory properties of pcMSCs. Collectively, these findings align with previous reports demonstrating substantially lower mortality in MSC-treated burn models compared with untreated controls [41]. Similar anti-inflammatory effects have also been reported with human umbilical cord–derived MSCs and other MSC sources [32, 42].

In this study, a scenario of suboptimal fluid resuscitation and inadequate post-burn thermal support was modeled in Groups A and B, in which rats received a single dose of lactated Ringer’s solution and were maintained under a heating lamp in room air following burn injury. Under these conditions, pcMSC treatment improved survival from 30% (Group A) to 50% (Group B). Although the 14-day survival difference did not reach statistical significance—likely due to limited sample size—this finding should be interpreted in a real-world clinical context. In clinical practice, optimal fluid resuscitation and thermal support are often difficult to achieve, particularly during mass casualty incidents, in field medical units, or in resource-limited settings with constrained intensive care capacity [43, 44]. Importantly, while not statistically significant, the magnitude of survival improvement is clinically meaningful and underscores the potential value of pcMSCs as an adjunctive therapy when standard resuscitative care cannot be reliably delivered.

The efficacy of pcMSC treatment was further enhanced by adequate fluid resuscitation combined with thermal support, with survival increasing from 50% to 90%, the highest survival rate observed in the study. These findings suggest that increased-volume hydration and thermal support act synergistically to improve survival. Rats receiving adequate fluid resuscitation and thermal support—regardless of pcMSC administration—demonstrated substantially better outcomes than those receiving minimal hydration without warming. We speculate that higher fluid volumes may mitigate cytokine storms through dilutional effects, enhanced renal clearance, and reduced microvascular obstruction by infused cells. Notably, no significant survival difference was observed between rats receiving three doses of lactated Ringer’s solution with thermal support with or without pcMSCs (Group C vs. Group D), suggesting that the critical role of hydration and thermal support in the early treatment. Indeed, post-burn peripheral vasoconstriction and reduced circulating vascular volume may impede effective intravenous stem cell delivery.

The importance of thermal support was further underscored by comparisons between Groups E and F. Animals receiving three doses of lactated Ringer’s solution without appropriate warming exhibited progressive mortality, with survival declining to 20% by day 14, whereas animals receiving a single dose of fluid with active warming maintained a 60% survival rate at day 14. These findings indicate that effective warming (28 °C for 4 h) is an independent and critical determinant of survival following severe burn injury in rats. This effect is likely attributable to the loss of skin barrier function after burns, which results in rapid heat loss and impaired thermoregulation. Post-burn hypothermia exacerbates burn shock and systemic inflammation, leading to impaired tissue perfusion and increased early mortality, thereby underscoring the essential role of active thermal support during early resuscitation [45, 46].

The survival rate observed in the untreated group was comparable to that of our prior unpublished study (40%, n = 10). Similarly, survival in rats receiving pcMSCs alone (80%) was consistent with our earlier findings (National Taiwan University College of Medicine and Public Health IACUC 2013033: Human choriodecidual mesenchymal stem cells decrease burn mortality in a rodent model). This reproducibility across independent experiments strengthens the evidence supporting a protective role of pcMSCs during the early post-burn period.

Although a clearer understanding of MSC biodistribution is essential for clinical translation, the ultimate fate of infused MSCs remains incompletely understood. Previous studies have shown that intravenously administered MSCs are predominantly sequestered within the pulmonary circulation shortly after infusion, with an estimated half-life of approximately 24 h [47, 48]. Large-animal tracking studies have similarly demonstrated sustained pulmonary retention of pcMSCs for up to one week [49]. This pulmonary localization may be advantageous in burns associated with inhalation injury as MSCs can locally suppress neutrophil and macrophage infiltration, reduce cytokine production, promote tissue remodeling, and enhance expression of the antimicrobial peptide LL-37 [50–54]. However, peripheral venous infusion carries a potential risk of embolic events prior to pulmonary sequestration, underscoring the importance of adequate fluid resuscitation before cell infusion and meticulous delivery techniques.

Despite the known regenerative and pro-angiogenic properties of MSCs, we did not observe a reduction in burn size by the study endpoint, consistent with prior reports [42, 55]. Because pcMSC localization was not tracked following tail-vein injection, it remains unclear whether the cells reached burn wound sites. Previous studies have demonstrated MSC localization to wound tissue by day 4, with declining numbers thereafter [56], while large-animal models report prolonged healing times following extensive burns [57]. The discrepancy between histological severity and limited gross wound improvement warrants further investigation.

In this study, the rat burn model was selected for its reproducibility and its ability to recapitulate key pathophysiological features of severe thermal injury observed in humans, including early systemic inflammation, fluid shifts, hypothermia, and high early mortality. While species-specific differences in immune responses and burn physiology limit direct extrapolation, the observed immunomodulatory and survival benefits of pcMSC therapy are likely to be relevant to human burn injury, particularly in the early resuscitation phase. These findings may therefore inform the development of adjunctive cell-based therapies for severe burns in clinical settings, although confirmation in large-animal models and human studies will be required.

Several limitations and potential confounders should be acknowledged. Uniform burn depth across animals is inherently difficult to achieve with manual scalding techniques; however, this was partially mitigated by having all burn procedures performed by a single experienced operator using a standardized protocol, thereby reducing inter-operator and inter-animal variability. Animals were treated and assessed according to predefined group assignments, and outcome measurements followed the same procedures across groups to minimize systematic measurement bias.

Despite these measures, several confounders were not fully controlled. The relatively small sample size in each experimental group, further reduced by progressive mortality, may have limited statistical power. In addition, environmental temperature varied among heat lamp–treated groups due to technical constraints, and procedural difficulties with tail blood sampling and tail-vein injections in some severely burned animals led to variability in sampling success and timing. These uncontrolled sources of variability may have influenced cytokine kinetics, cell viability, and overall outcome measures and should be considered when interpreting the study findings.

Conclusions

Placenta-derived choriodecidual mesenchymal stem cells (pcMSCs) confer a clinically meaningful additive survival benefit following severe burn injury, particularly under conditions of inadequate fluid resuscitation and insufficient thermal support—circumstances commonly encountered in austere environments. This survival advantage appears to be mediated by stabilization of body temperature, attenuation of post-burn weight loss, and improved hydration, consistent with observed reductions in circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines. While timely fluid resuscitation and active warming remain the primary determinants of burn survival, pcMSC therapy represents a promising adjunctive strategy for early burn management in resource-limited settings.
