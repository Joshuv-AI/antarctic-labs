 PREFACE
 It is our pleasure to introduce this technical report of contributions arising from the 2026 R-matrix
Workshop on Methods and Applications, held July 13–17, 2026, in the DJ Nelson Auditorium at Oak Ridge
National Laboratory (ORNL) in Oak Ridge, Tennessee. The workshop brought together students, early-career
researchers, experimentalists, theorists, nuclear data evaluators, code developers, and specialists who apply
R-matrix methods across a broad range of problems in nuclear science and technology. The principal emphasis
of the workshop was phenomenological R-matrix analysis, particularly for neutron-induced reactions. It also
focused on the wider perspective needed to connect nuclear reaction theory, experimental measurements,
evaluated nuclear data, and applications.
 Since its foundational development, R-matrix theory has provided a rigorous and versatile framework
for describing nuclear reactions in terms of resonance structure, reaction channels, and experimentally
observable quantities. Its enduring value lies both in its theoretical foundation and in its ability to support
the simultaneous interpretation of diverse measurements and the construction of evaluated nuclear data files.
As experimental capabilities, computational methods, and application requirements continue to advance,
the R-matrix formalism must also evolve. Modern analyses increasingly require improved optimization
methods, statistically meaningful uncertainty quantification, efficient computational implementations, and
careful treatment of correlations, model deficiencies, and the transition between the resolved and unresolved
resonance regions.
 The R-matrix Workshop on Methods and Applications series was initiated in Santa Fe, New Mexico, in
2016, with Los Alamos National Laboratory playing a central organizing role. That inaugural meeting, held
June 27–July 1, established the character that has continued to distinguish the series: extended technical
presentations, substantial time for discussion, and close interaction among researchers working in theory,
experiment, nuclear astrophysics, and nuclear data evaluation. Its program began with historical and
introductory lectures on R-matrix theory and continued with presentations and tutorials on the SAMMY,
EDA, and AZURE2 codes. Subsequent sessions addressed scattering and breakup reactions, neutron-induced
fission, nuclear astrophysics, experimental and evaluated covariances, uncertainty propagation, connections
between phenomenological and ab initio approaches, and the implementation of R-matrix methods in transport
and nuclear data applications. The meeting therefore established the workshop not simply as a venue for
reporting results but as a forum for comparing formalisms, computational implementations, analysis practices,
and unresolved methodological questions.
 An additional virtual R-matrix workshop was held June 21–24, 2021, maintaining a forum for exchange
between the principal in-person meetings. The series returned to an in-person format at Ohio University in
Athens, Ohio, on June 20–23, 2023. The stated objectives of the 2023 workshop were to educate young
researchers in the use of R-matrix techniques, discuss current theoretical and applied developments, identify
capabilities that were still needed, and highlight recent accomplishments. Its program combined introductory
presentations and practical demonstrations of SAMMY, EDA, and AZURE2 with contributions on nuclear
data evaluations, Trojan Horse method analyses, charged-particle and neutron-induced reactions, sensitivity
calculations, deep learning applications, and uncertainty-aware extrapolation of astrophysical reaction rates.
In this way, the 2023 meeting reaffirmed the educational mission of the series as well as introduced a new
generation of computational and statistical methods.
 The 2026 workshop at ORNL was the third in-person meeting of the series and continued its progression
toward closer integration of foundational theory, experimental analysis, evaluated nuclear data, computational
tools, and application-driven validation. The workshop placed particular emphasis on neutron-induced
reactions, theoretical advances, experimental techniques, resonance evaluation, statistical descriptions of the

 vii

unresolved resonance region, uncertainty quantification, and computational approaches intended to improve
the efficiency and reliability of nuclear-data evaluation. The 2026 event met the defining purpose of the
workshop series by bringing students and early-career researchers into sustained technical discussions with
experienced specialists in the field.
 The opening day established a common foundation for the discussions that followed. Introductory lectures
addressed phenomenological R-matrix methods and the underlying theory, followed by a presentation on
experimental techniques and the measured data required for reliable R-matrix analyses. The program then
connected these foundations directly to nuclear data evaluation through reviews of the 14 N and 63,65 Cu
evaluations, AMPX processing, and ongoing R-matrix analysis activities at ORNL. The accompanying
working discussions gave participants an opportunity to examine assumptions, conventions, and practical
analysis choices that are often difficult to explore fully in conventional conference presentations.
 The subsequent sessions illustrated the breadth and continuing evolution of the field. Presentations
addressed automated fitting in the resolved resonance region, automated uncertainty quantification, parametric
matrix models for nuclear data evaluation, measurements of samarium isotopes at the Los Alamos Neutron
Science Center, crystalline effects in thermal and epithermal neutron analyses, relativistic R-matrix theory,
statistical uncertainties in the unresolved resonance region, and the determination of the resolved-to-unresolved
resonance boundary. Sessions on specific compound nuclear systems—including 15 N and 7 Be—examined
practical issues such as distant poles, multichannel evaluations, and the relationship between physical
constraints and fitted resonance parameters. Updates to the Los Alamos Energy Dependent Analysis code and
a “Riccati flow” approach to optimizing R-function parameters provided further examples of new theoretical
and numerical developments.
 The later sessions expanded the discussion to multistep nuclear reactions and to the continuing development
of the software infrastructure required by the community. Contributions included a multistep R-matrix
formalism, a new Python-based R-matrix code, recent developments in AZURE2 and SAMMY, and nuclear
data uncertainty quantification with SCALE. The presentation of VENUS, the neutron time-of-flight facility
at the Spallation Neutron Source, emphasized the importance of linking modern analysis methods with new
experimental capabilities. The final day extended the scope to thermal scattering evaluations, measurements
and R-matrix analysis of 10,11 B(𝛼, 𝑛) reactions, and energy-dependent neutron transmission measurements
for fluorine nuclear data validation. The workshop concluded with an extended discussion of future R-matrix
developments, allowing the participants to identify common priorities extending beyond individual codes,
reactions, or application areas.
 A particularly valuable feature of the 2026 workshop was its emphasis on discussion. Working lunches,
focused question-and-answer periods, daily summaries, and the extended closing session were incorporated
deliberately into the agenda. These activities encouraged participants to engage outside formal presentations
and examine issues such as parameter conventions, experimental normalization, covariance construction,
model dependence, optimization strategy, code verification, and the physical interpretation of fitted results.
Visits to the historic X-10 Graphite Reactor and the Spallation Neutron Source further connected the scientific
program with ORNL’s history and its present experimental capabilities. Presentations on SAMMY, SCALE,
and VENUS, illustrated the full path from measurement and resonance analysis to evaluated data and their
use in nuclear applications.
 Viewed across the history of the series, the 2026 meeting demonstrated both continuity and change.
The fundamental questions concerning reaction channels, resonance interference, boundary conditions,
parameter transformations, and the physical interpretation of R-matrix parameters remain central. At the same
time, the field is increasingly shaped by automated optimization, statistically rigorous uncertainty analysis,
new experimental facilities, modern programming environments, and computational techniques capable of
handling larger and more complex evaluation problems. The workshop reaffirmed that progress in these areas

requires sustained interaction among theorists, experimentalists, evaluators, software developers, and users of
evaluated nuclear data. It also underscored the importance of transferring both theoretical understanding and
practical analysis experience to students and early-career scientists.

 ABSTRACT
 This technical memorandum compiles the contributions presented at the 2026 R-Matrix Workshop
on Methods and Applications, held July 13–17, 2026, at Oak Ridge National Laboratory. The workshop
brought together researchers working in phenomenological R-matrix theory, experimental nuclear physics,
nuclear data evaluation, uncertainty quantification, and computational methods, with particular emphasis on
neutron-induced reactions. The collected contributions cover foundational R-matrix theory and experimental
techniques; resolved and unresolved resonance region analyses; automated resonance fitting and uncertainty
quantification; statistical and parametric approaches to nuclear data evaluation; and relativistic, multistep, and
distant-pole formulations. Applications to specific compound nuclear systems, charged-particle reactions,
thermal scattering, neutron transmission, and nuclear data validation are also presented. The memorandum
documents recent developments in widely used analysis and processing tools, including Energy Dependent
Analysis, AZURE2, SAMMY, AMPX, and SCALE, as well as emerging Python-based implementations and
new optimization methods. Additional contributions relate to experimental capabilities and measurements at
facilities including the Los Alamos Neutron Science Center and the Spallation Neutron Source, including the
VENUS neutron time-of-flight instrument. These contributions provide a representative account of current
R-matrix methods, practical evaluation challenges, and emerging research directions. This memorandum is
intended to preserve the technical content of the workshop and to serve as a reference for experimentalists,
theorists, nuclear data evaluators, software developers, students, and users of evaluated nuclear data in
applications ranging from nuclear astrophysics to criticality safety.

AMPX AND GNDS
 Once an R-matrix analysis is incorporated into an
evaluation file, the cross sections may be exercised in
simulations of various nuclear engineering systems via neutron
transport codes. The AMPX nuclear data processing code [9]
prepares cross sections and other relevant nuclear data for use
in the SCALE code system [10].
 For many years, the ENDF/B evaluated nuclear data

1.1 PHENOMENOLOGICAL R-MATRIX METHODS — CARL BRUNE
Boundary-condition independence and the ANC test
    Transcript time: approximately 02:47:56–02:49:46.
Question. Can the 𝛼 + 𝑑 ab-initio test of the 6 Li asymptotic normalization coefficient (ANC) also be used to
demonstrate explicitly that the extracted result is independent of the R-matrix boundary condition and that the
background poles have been included correctly?
Answer. Formally, the different boundary-condition representations can be transformed into one another, so
observables should not depend on that choice. In the specific ANC study, however, the 6 Li separation energy
was fixed to the known ab-initio value, which limits the usefulness of that fit as a direct boundary-condition
test. The channel radius was varied over a reasonable range and the conclusions were found to be insensitive
to that variation. The ab-initio overlap function also makes visible the “renormalization” that occurs between
the true overlap at the channel radius and the asymptotic Whittaker-function description.
What was fitted in the ab-initio benchmark?
    Transcript time: approximately 02:50:03–02:52:06.
Question. Was the fit performed to a clean theoretical cross section, or was simulated experimental noise
added? What exactly constituted the “data” in the ab-initio benchmark?
Answer. The fits used calculated phase shifts and a 𝜒2 -minimization procedure. Randomized uncertainties
were assigned to the phase-shift points. This was not intended to be a fully realistic experimental uncertainty
model: systematic uncertainties were not included, and the simplified study did not include the other
partial-wave components of the physical scattering problem.
Follow-up question. If the assumed uncertainty were taken to zero, should the extracted ANC agree exactly
with the ab-initio value, or does the residual difference indicate a model deficiency?
Follow-up answer. The next place to investigate is the physical coupled 𝑆- and 𝐷-wave structure of 𝛼 + 𝑑
scattering, which was simplified in the test. It is also possible that residual truncations in the ab-initio
calculation itself contribute. The approximately percent-level difference should therefore not be attributed
immediately to a single source.

Question. Is the calculation of angular distributions of outgoing photons actually implemented in an R-matrix
code, or was it only derived theoretically?
Answer. The capability has been implemented in AZURE2, at least in versions used by the developers, and

Meaning of reduced-width “renormalization”
 Questioner: Devin Barry.
Transcript time: approximately 02:53:19–02:55:07.
Question. When the reduced-width amplitude is described as being renormalized, does that simply mean that
its numerical value is updated, or is something more physical being implied?
Answer. For the 𝛼 + 𝑑 channel in 6 Li, the exact ab-initio overlap function has not yet reached its pure
asymptotic Whittaker form at a typical phenomenological channel radius. Consequently, the phenomenological
reduced-width amplitude does not equal the exact value of the overlap function at that radius. Instead,
it corresponds to the value obtained by extrapolating the asymptotic Whittaker behavior inward to the
chosen radius. That is the sense in which the quantity is “renormalized.” Despite this distinction, the
phenomenological description reproduces the correct ANC and the positive-energy phase shift.

Identifying missing background poles from channel-radius sensitivity
    Transcript time: approximately 02:55:21–02:57:44.
Question. If the extrapolated result depends strongly on the channel radius, this is often interpreted as
evidence that background poles are missing. Is there a systematic way to determine the spin and parity of the
background pole that should be added?
Answer. A useful diagnostic is to vary the channel radius one partial wave at a time. If the observable or
extrapolation is particularly sensitive to the radius in one 𝐽 𝜋 block, that identifies the spin-parity sector in
which an additional background level should be investigated. Although one often uses a common radius for
all partial waves of a particle pair, varying them individually is useful as a diagnostic.
Follow-up question. How large must the change be before the channel-radius dependence should be
considered significant?
Follow-up answer. There is no universal numerical threshold. The change should be judged relative to the
uncertainty of the observable of interest and to the quality and amount of data in the fit. For example, if a
modest radius variation changes an extrapolated quantity by an amount comparable with its total uncertainty,
that dependence should be considered important.

Value of polarization measurements
    Transcript time: approximately 02:57:55–02:59:14.
Question. How useful are polarization data for R-matrix analyses, and should the community invest in new
polarized-beam measurements?
Answer. Polarization data can be extremely valuable because they add constraints that are not available from
unpolarized cross sections alone. Historic high-quality polarization measurements are particularly useful
for systems such as 𝛼 + 𝑑 scattering, and polarized-proton elastic-scattering measurements could provide
strong additional information. The limitation is practical: rebuilding the required polarized-beam and target
infrastructure is expensive, so the scientific benefit must be weighed against the required resources.

1.2 R-MATRIX THEORY AND THE GREEN–BLOCH PERSPECTIVE — MARK PARIS
Self-consistency and data requirements for breakup reactions
    Transcript time: approximately 04:15:21–04:21:39.

 15

Question. In the breakup formalism based on a Lippmann–Schwinger/Faddeev-like treatment, are the breakup
amplitudes implicitly dependent on other amplitudes, requiring a larger and coupled set of experimental
information?
Answer. Yes. The amplitudes must be solved self-consistently. After the first ejectile is emitted, a second
resonating subsystem remains, so the computational method must be capable of treating more than one
compound system. The quasi-two-body Green function also requires integrations over the spectator degree of
freedom in order to preserve three-body unitarity. Experimentally, the challenge is the much larger kinematic
space of a 2 → 3 reaction. Complete or near-complete event reconstruction, such as Dalitz-plot information
from large-solid-angle detectors, would be especially useful. For heavier systems a reduced description may
ultimately be unavoidable, but for light systems the goal is to retain the explicit channels because reduced
treatments weaken the direct connection to the ab-initio problem.

Exact channel-radius dependence and the Riccati flow
 Questioner: Jakub (surname not stated in this exchange).
Transcript time: approximately 05:00:03–05:04:58.
Question. Can the dependence of an R-matrix calculation on channel radius be calculated explicitly, for
example through a derivative of the cross section with respect to the channel radius?
Answer. For an exact representation, the derivative of any observable with respect to the channel radii should
be zero: observables cannot depend on the arbitrary channel surface. The difficulty appears after truncating
the infinite pole expansion. The Riccati-flow equations describe how R-matrix elements and parameters
must change as the channel radius is changed. In principle, even if only a finite set of reduced widths is
nonzero initially, an infinitesimal change in radius generates contributions from the omitted space. Therefore
a strictly finite pole expansion cannot preserve exact channel-radius independence. Current work is aimed at
projecting the flow into a useful finite subspace. In practical phenomenological fits, residual radius sensitivity
is therefore expected, and background poles can be viewed as an effective way of representing the omitted
levels.

𝑅∞ , external backgrounds, and direct capture
    Transcript time: approximately 05:16:20–05:22:40.
Question. How should the contribution of omitted levels, often represented by 𝑅∞ or external/background
terms, be treated in SAMMY and in an ENDF evaluation? Can such a term also account for direct-capture-like
behavior?
Answer. The discussion distinguished the formal mathematical constraints from the practical SAMMY
implementation. From the 𝑅-function point of view, the energy-dependent background that preserves the
relevant 𝑅-function structure has a restricted form; a linear energy dependence was specifically noted.
SAMMY, however, contains external-matrix parameterizations described in the Fröhner documentation
that can represent 𝑅∞ and additional energy dependence. These terms can approximate omitted negative-
and high-energy levels, although edge effects and unitarity considerations must be handled carefully. The
discussion cited an existing evaluated-data example in which an external R-matrix term is used.

1.3 EXPERIMENTAL CONSTRAINTS AND MULTICHANNEL ANALYSES — JAMES DEBOER
Choice of reference angle for yield ratios
    Transcript time: approximately 06:25:41–06:26:34.
Question. When fitting ratios of excitation functions measured at different angles, how should the reference
angle in the denominator be chosen?
Answer. Experimentally, a sensible reference is an angle for which the signal is clean and can be separated
reliably over the full energy range. The analysis should also be repeated using different denominator angles to
assess sensitivity to that choice. A poor reference angle can propagate problems into all of the ratios.
Alternative to using ratios: fitted run normalizations
    Transcript time: approximately 06:26:39–06:28:17.
Question. Is it preferable to avoid choosing a reference angle and instead treat the beam–target normalization
for each run as an additional fit parameter? Would that introduce too many nuisance parameters for an MCMC
calculation?
Answer. Treating the common beam–target factor as a nuisance parameter can remove the bias associated
with selecting one angle as the denominator. Although this appears to introduce one parameter per energy or
run, the normalization is strongly constrained by the data and, for the formulation discussed, its best value can
be calculated analytically rather than sampled as a fully independent MCMC parameter. This also avoids the
complicated correlated uncertainties created by forming ratios.

Where should experimental resolution be applied?
    Transcript time: approximately 06:30:16–06:32:52.
Question. Should a resolution function be applied directly to the calculated cross section, or should it be
applied to the actual measured observable, such as transmission or capture yield, before conversion to a cross

Answer. Applying the experimental response to the measured observable is the preferred treatment.
Transmission, for example, is nonlinearly related to total cross section, so broadening a reconstructed cross
section is not generally equivalent to broadening the transmission. The cross-section-space treatment shown
in the presentation was acknowledged as a simplified starting point used because only processed cross-section
data were available. The plan is to move toward the more rigorous SAMMY-style observable-space treatment,
including effects such as self-shielding and multiple scattering where relevant.

Normalization uncertainties and evaluated covariances
 Questioner: Ian Thompson.
Transcript time: approximately 07:48:25–07:54:49.
Question. When experimental normalizations (for example, target-thickness or overall scale uncertainties) are
adjusted in a resonance fit, how should those normalization uncertainties and their correlations be separated
from the resonance-parameter covariance when producing an ENDF evaluation?
Answer. Ideally, the evaluation format would preserve the normalization parameters, their uncertainties, and
their correlations with the resonance parameters in the same way that SAMMY can retain them internally.
ENDF does not presently provide a straightforward way to carry all of that information. A practical strategy
used in previous evaluations is to provide the resonance-parameter covariance in File 32 and supplement it
with energy-dependent cross-section uncertainties in File 33. The discussion emphasized that this is not a
fully rigorous separation, but it is workable with current formats. It was also noted that current fitting practice
can assign independent normalization parameters to individual data sets, while correlations among those
normalizations are often neglected.

Copper angular distributions and the upper energy used in reconstruction
    Transcript time: approximately 07:51:37–07:53:18.
Question. Would the disagreement between reconstructed and measured natural-copper angular distributions
improve if the Blatt–Biedenharn reconstruction were restricted to a lower energy, rather than including
resonance parameters far above the formal resolved-resonance boundary?

Model used when relaxing the Reich–Moore approximation
    Transcript time: approximately 00:55:05–00:57:30.
Question. When the Reich–Moore approximation is relaxed in the synthetic-data study, how are the individual
capture widths to the many final states generated? Are the widths sampled statistically and constrained to
reproduce a prescribed total capture width?
Answer. The synthetic model explicitly contains many final states rather than one aggregated capture
channel. In the implementation discussed, approximately 50 relevant levels were retained, with higher-lying
contributions truncated once they became sufficiently weak. Consequently, the aggregate capture-width
distribution has a large effective number of degrees of freedom. The speaker noted that this part of the
work had been developed primarily by collaborators and that a more detailed description of the sampling
prescription should be taken from that work.

Meaning of an “unbiased” number of resonances
    Transcript time: approximately 00:57:41–01:00:46.
Question. What is meant by the conclusion that an evaluator should strive for an “unbiased number of
resonances”? Does this imply deliberately inserting fictitious or background resonances when the experiment
is known to miss weak levels?
Answer. In this context, an unbiased resonance count means that the reported ladder should reproduce the
expected mean level spacing, rather than systematically under-counting weak levels. The synthetic-data
tests showed that some resonances will be missed regardless of the fitting procedure. If no compensating
resonances are introduced, the mean capture cross section can be biased because the average cross section
depends on the resonance density together with the average neutron and capture widths. The interpretation of
the study was therefore that inserting statistically motivated fictitious resonances can be appropriate when
needed to restore the expected average level density.

Using bound coherent and incoherent scattering as an additional test
    Transcript time: approximately 01:00:46–01:02:06.
Question. Could the testing framework include low-energy constraints from measured bound coherent and
incoherent scattering quantities, for example values tabulated by NIST, as a quasi-benchmark for the resonance
parameters?
Answer. The suggestion was viewed as useful and complementary to the existing synthetic-data tests. The
resonance parameters can, in principle, be used to calculate the corresponding scattering quantities and
compared with measured reference values. The development team had not yet incorporated this type of
constraint and expressed interest in discussing how to add it to the toolchain.

Discrepant real data sets and the role of synthetic data
    Transcript time: approximately 01:02:11–01:05:35.
Question. Can the automated framework be applied to collections of real experimental data containing
discrepant data sets or outliers, and is the long-term goal only to validate evaluation methodologies or also to
perform new evaluations?
Answer. The fitting machinery can be applied to real data. The principal advantage of synthetic data is not
that real data are unusable, but that the true cross section is known, which makes objective performance
measures possible. With experimental data, the same automated fitting machinery can be used, but one
loses the ability to compare directly with a known truth. The discussion made clear that the methodology
is intended to be useful both as a testing framework and as a practical aid to evaluators; the code was also
described as open source and applicable to real-data analyses.

How the algorithm decides whether a small structure is a resonance
    Transcript time: approximately 01:06:12–01:11:09.
Question. What makes the automated procedure identify one small dip or fluctuation as a resonance
while rejecting another nearby structure? How robust is the “bump-hunting” procedure against statistical
fluctuations?
Answer. Resonance identification is treated statistically and controlled by a continuous hyperparameter
governing how aggressively small structures are accepted. Increasing the parameter causes smaller features to
be labeled as resonances, but many of those become false positives when compared with the known synthetic
truth. Decreasing it suppresses false positives but begins to miss genuine resonances. The synthetic studies
provide a way to tune this trade-off. For real data, the proposed practical criterion is to choose the setting that
produces a resonance density consistent with the expected mean level spacing and statistical information
about missing levels.

Meaning of the “other” resonance-classification category
    Transcript time: approximately 01:11:16–01:12:09.
Question. What does the “other” curve represent in the comparison between true and fitted resonance ladders?
Answer. It represents ambiguous many-to-one or one-to-many matches. For example, two fitted resonances
may correspond to one true resonance, or one fitted resonance may represent two true levels. In such cases the
algorithm has identified that structure is present, but it has not reproduced the correct number of resonances.

Extension to higher partial waves and additional reaction channels
    Transcript time: approximately 01:14:24–01:16:36.
Question. Is the automated fitting method limited to 𝑠-wave neutron resonances and capture, or can it
accommodate 𝑝- and 𝑑-waves and additional reaction channels?
Answer. The tantalum-181 demonstration was dominated by two 𝑠-wave spin groups, so higher partial
waves were not important in that test. The algorithm itself is not restricted to those cases and can be
extended to additional spin-parity groups. Zirconium was identified as a useful next test because the richer
spin structure makes reassignment of spin groups more consequential. At the time of the workshop, the
implementation primarily treated neutron elastic and radiative-capture channels; adding further channels,
with fission mentioned as a possible next step, remained future work.

2.2 AUTOMATED UNCERTAINTY QUANTIFICATION IN THE RESOLVED RESONANCE
 REGION — ELAN PARK-BERNSTEIN
Including normalization and other systematic uncertainties in File 33
    Transcript time: approximately 01:45:49–01:47:20.
Question. If normalization factors and other experimental systematic uncertainties are not propagated into
an ENDF File 32 resonance-parameter covariance, can the new energy-space method incorporate those
contributions into a File 33 cross-section covariance?
Answer. The initial demonstration focused on uncertainty associated with the fitting model itself and did not
yet include all experimental systematic terms. However, such effects can be added as separate covariance
contributions. For example, a normalization uncertainty could be represented by an additional covariance
matrix and combined with the model-derived covariance. Thus, the method does not fundamentally preclude
systematic uncertainties; they were simply outside the first implementation.
Information used by the learned covariance models
    Transcript time: approximately 01:47:28–01:50:10.
Question. Do the random-forest, elastic-net, and related covariance predictors make use of information from
the automated resonance-identification study, such as the probability that a weak resonance has been missed?
Answer. Yes. The machine-learning models are mappings from several physically motivated predictors to an
empirical covariance obtained from repeated synthetic-data fits. One important predictor is a higher-cardinality
fit in which additional possible resonances are retained, thereby carrying information about structures that may
have been missed in the preferred fit. The fitted cross section itself and a cross-section-dependent variance
estimate are also used as predictors. These quantities allow the learned model to increase uncertainty in
regions where the conventional GLS covariance can be overconfident.
Computational scaling and reporting of large covariance matrices
    Transcript time: approximately 01:50:28–01:53:16.
Discussion question. If energy-space covariances can be estimated more realistically, how can the method be
made computationally tractable and how should very large cross-section covariance matrices be reported for
nuclear-data applications?
Answer. This was identified as a principal open problem. Automation reduces manpower requirements, but
the calculation remains computationally demanding, and a pointwise energy-space covariance can become
far larger than a resonance-parameter covariance. Principal-component or other reduced representations
were discussed as possible compression strategies. The workshop discussion also noted that ensemble-based
uncertainty calculations already require cluster computing for realistic problems, so both efficient generation
and compact representation of the covariance are important future tasks.

Meaning of the 𝑉𝑆𝐹 interaction in the Trojan-Horse amplitude
    Transcript time: approximately 02:36:10–02:37:45.
Question. What is the potential 𝑉𝑆𝐹 that appears in the reaction-amplitude matrix element?
Answer. It represents the interaction between the spectator particle 𝑆 and the compound system 𝐹. In the
reaction amplitude it participates in connecting the entrance configuration, including the virtual transferred
particle, to the exit configuration containing the compound system and spectator. The speaker emphasized the
physical role of the term rather than giving an explicit potential model during the discussion.

Suppression of direct-reaction contributions
    Transcript time: approximately 02:37:47–02:40:04.
Question. Does the approximation used in the Trojan-Horse analysis assume that direct reaction mechanisms
are negligible, leaving only resonant contributions?
Answer. Yes, for the selected events. The analysis imposes stringent kinematic and dynamical conditions,
including constraints associated with the quasi-free mechanism and spectator momentum. Background is
then subtracted from the reconstructed three-body cross section so that the retained signal is interpreted as the
resonant contribution. Under those conditions the two-body spectrum has a baseline near zero away from the
resonances.

Range of applicability of the Trojan-Horse approach
    Transcript time: approximately 02:40:05–02:41:10.
Question. Is there an energy or reaction-mechanism limit beyond which the method can no longer be applied,
for example when direct reactions become important?
Answer. Yes. The method is not universally applicable. Before using it one must establish that the selected
reaction is dominated by the quasi-free, resonant mechanism and that the required kinematic conditions can be
imposed. Cases with negative 𝑄 values were mentioned as particularly difficult. The applicability therefore
has to be established reaction by reaction.

How strongly should Wigner statistics be enforced for sparse spectra?
    Transcript time: approximately 03:05:49–03:08:43.
Question. For a nucleus such as 90 Zr, where the level spacing is large and the number of observed resonances
may be modest, should the Wigner nearest-neighbor distribution be imposed rigidly, or should the statistical
prior include additional flexibility?
Answer. The discussion emphasized that tantalum and zirconium are qualitatively different test cases. For
tantalum, changing the spin assignment often has little effect on the inferred cross section, whereas for
zirconium the resonance shapes associated with different spin groups can be much more distinct. The exact
strength of the statistical prior therefore should not be assumed to transfer unchanged from one isotope
to another. Introducing flexibility in how strongly the Wigner distribution is enforced was considered a
reasonable question for the zirconium study.

Using angular distributions to determine spin groups
    Transcript time: approximately 03:08:49–03:09:31.
Question. Could angular-distribution data be included in the automated fit to help distinguish spin groups?
Answer. Yes. This had already been considered, but tantalum-181 was a poor test case because the relevant
resonances were predominantly 𝑠-wave and the scattering was essentially isotropic. Zirconium provides a
more suitable system in which angular distributions could carry useful information about partial waves and
spin assignments.

Two neutron channels and the Porter–Thomas distribution
    Transcript time: approximately 03:09:31–03:11:24.
Question. For zirconium spin groups that contain two neutron channels with the same 𝐽 𝜋 , can the two widths
simply be aggregated, and what statistical distribution should describe the aggregate width?
Answer. The discussion clarified that two independent neutron-width amplitudes lead to a chi-squared
distribution with two degrees of freedom for the summed width. Strictly speaking, the Porter–Thomas
distribution refers to the one-degree-of-freedom case. Whether the two physical channels can be replaced by
a single effective width without losing important interference information was left as a testable question for
the synthetic-data framework.

How are resonance spins determined experimentally?
    Transcript time: approximately 03:11:30–03:16:30.
Question. For the zirconium resonances under discussion, how are the 𝐽 values actually determined
experimentally? Are they obtained from line shapes, angular distributions, or some other observable?
Answer. Angular-momentum coupling determines which 𝐽 𝜋 values and channel spins are allowed, but the
experimental assignment of an individual resonance can remain ambiguous. For a strong isolated resonance

 41

that approaches the unitarity limit, the peak height provides information proportional to 2𝐽 + 1. Angular
distributions can constrain the orbital angular momentum, but different 𝐽 𝜋 values can share the same 𝑙.
Definitive separation of channel-spin contributions may require spin-dependent or polarization measurements,
which are often unavailable. The group therefore characterized some assignments as effectively informed
guesswork and proposed quantifying the consequence of assigning them incorrectly.

Should capture widths be fixed or allowed to fluctuate?
    Transcript time: approximately 03:20:04–03:26:24.
Question. For nuclei where the aggregate capture width is commonly held constant, when is that approximation
justified, and when should resonance-to-resonance capture-width fluctuations be fitted?
Answer. The common argument for a constant capture width is that many independent gamma channels
produce a large effective number of degrees of freedom and a narrow aggregate-width distribution. The
discussion cautioned that even a few tens of degrees of freedom are not literally an infinite-degree-of-freedom
delta function. A practical criterion is the quality of the experimental data: if the expected physical variance
is smaller than what the data can constrain, fixing the width may be reasonable; if the measurement precision
is competitive with the expected variance, the widths should probably be allowed to vary. Possible departures
from ideal statistical assumptions due to nuclear-structure effects were also noted. Initial average capture
widths are typically taken from previous evaluations or the Atlas of Neutron Resonances, but those values
should themselves be treated as uncertain input.

2.5 NUCLEAR-DATA EVALUATION WITH PARAMETRIC MATRIX MODELS — DEAN LEE
Relation to Ericson fluctuation theory
    Transcript time: approximately 05:28:06–05:28:40.
Question. How does the proposed parametric-matrix-model treatment of the unresolved resonance region
differ from the traditional Ericson fluctuation-theory analysis?
Answer. The speaker expected some conceptual overlap but anticipated that the parametric-matrix-model
approach would develop in a substantially different direction. The issue was not resolved in detail during the
session and was identified as a useful comparison for follow-up discussion.

What quantity is actually fitted in the unresolved resonance region?
    Transcript time: approximately 05:28:47–05:30:49.
Question. Does the parametric matrix model calculate a pointwise cross section, and if so, what is actually
minimized when fitting unresolved-resonance data?
Answer. The model does generate pointwise cross sections, but it is deliberately not fitted by requiring
point-by-point agreement with the measured spectrum. Instead, a local spectral-power measure, implemented
through a short-time Fourier transform, is compared between model and data. The purpose is to identify a
random-matrix model whose local averages, fluctuation scale, and correlation structure reproduce the data
even though a particular realization of the resonance ladder may have individual peaks at different energies.

Relationship to sampling Wigner and Porter–Thomas resonance ladders
    Transcript time: approximately 05:29:54–05:30:49.
Question. Is the procedure essentially equivalent to sampling a random resonance ladder from Wigner level
spacings and Porter–Thomas widths, but allowing the model to determine the average parameters rather than
choosing them by hand?

 42

Answer. That interpretation is close to the simplest demonstration, which used a Gaussian orthogonal
ensemble as the reference model. The intended method is more general, however. The matrix ensemble itself
can be parameterized and learned from the data, allowing systematic departures from GOE behavior when
those departures are required to reproduce the observed local spectral power.

Application to partially known nonlinear dynamical systems
    Transcript time: approximately 05:32:03–05:33:19.
Question. Could the same parametric-matrix strategy help with nonlinear dynamical systems in which the
local structure of the governing equations is known but a complete high-fidelity model is unavailable?
Answer. Yes, that was described as a natural use case. If one knows structural information—for example,
that a system obeys a differential equation of a certain order and form—that information can be built into a
reduced-order matrix model. The remaining unknown pieces can then be learned from data. The method is
intended precisely for problems where some of the physics is known but a complete microscopic calculation
is unavailable or too expensive.

GOE edge effects and truncation
    Transcript time: approximately 05:33:33–05:34:17.
Question. When level spacings are generated from the eigenvalues of a GOE random matrix, does the Wigner
semicircle behavior near the edges require the spectrum to be truncated before extracting level statistics?
Answer. Yes. Edge effects are important and the analysis should use an interior region where the desired
stationary level statistics apply. The discussion also mentioned rescaling procedures that flatten the semicircle
density before sampling, although some residual edge trimming can still be useful.

Including experimental resolution in the PMM analysis
    Transcript time: approximately 05:34:29–05:37:25.
Question. Since the unresolved resonance region is partly defined by finite experimental resolution, is the
detector resolution included in the parametric-matrix-model analysis?
Answer. It can be included explicitly in the forward model. Because finite resolution allows many different
underlying resonance realizations to produce similar measured spectra, the analysis can sample multiple
statistically acceptable models and assess them probabilistically. A related observation from the discussion
was that convolution becomes multiplication after Fourier transformation, so a well-characterized resolution
function may be especially convenient to incorporate or deconvolve in a spectral-power analysis.

Recovering average URR parameters for evaluated data
    Transcript time: approximately 05:37:34–05:38:18.
Question. ENDF unresolved-resonance evaluations ultimately require average resonance parameters. Can
those average quantities be extracted from the parametric matrix model?
Answer. Yes. The underlying matrix model contains the statistical information from which average resonance
quantities can be obtained. The method is not intended only to reproduce the visible fluctuation pattern; the
goal is to infer the model parameters and statistical properties needed for evaluated-data use.

2.7 CRYSTALLINE EFFECTS IN LOW-ENERGY R-MATRIX EVALUATIONS — CHRIS

Room temperature versus effective temperature
    Transcript time: approximately 06:41:29–06:42:27.
Question. Was the “free-gas” plutonium-dioxide comparison calculated at the material’s effective temperature
or simply at room temperature?
Answer. The plot used an ordinary room-temperature free-gas treatment. At room temperature, the difference
between the physical temperature and the effective temperature for PuO2 was expected to be relatively small.
The difference becomes much larger at cryogenic temperatures; an example was given in which a material
temperature near 20 K corresponds to an effective temperature closer to 90 K. Consequently, low-temperature
transmission measurements provide a much more sensitive validation test of the crystalline treatment.

Simple rescaling of a TSL cross section versus the CNR treatment
    Transcript time: approximately 06:42:28–06:44:05.
Question. Could one obtain an adequate answer simply by dividing a thermal-scattering cross section by its
potential-scattering contribution and multiplying by the non-TSL resonance cross section, rather than using
the coupled CNR formalism?
Answer. Such a rescaling may give reasonably similar one-dimensional cross sections in some cases, but the
speaker had not performed a systematic comparison. More importantly, a scalar rescaling does not obviously
reproduce the altered double-differential scattering distribution or the physical coupling between thermal and
resonant scattering. It should therefore be viewed as a different approximation rather than as an established
replacement for the coupled formalism.

Why a resonance can enhance upscattering
    Transcript time: approximately 06:44:07–06:48:07.
Question. Why does inclusion of the resonance contribution increase the apparent upscattering probability
just below the low-energy 239 Pu resonance?
Answer. A simple physical picture is that a neutron incident just below the resonance can exchange energy
with the moving or vibrating lattice and thereby move closer to the resonance energy, where the interaction
probability is larger. That mechanism favors energy transfer toward the resonance and competes with the
usual thermal tendency toward downscattering. The same qualitative idea already exists for a free-gas Doppler
treatment; phonon dynamics modify the magnitude and detailed structure rather than introducing an entirely
unrelated effect.

Could the external neutron state be treated as a Bloch wave?
    Transcript time: approximately 06:48:48–06:57:43.
Question. For very-low-energy neutrons propagating through a crystal, could one formulate the external
region of R-matrix theory using periodic Bloch-like wave functions rather than free-particle exterior solutions,
thereby modifying the shift and penetrability functions?
Answer. The idea was not part of the implementation presented, which still treats the asymptotic incident and
outgoing neutron states in the conventional manner and introduces lattice physics through the thermal-scattering
correlations. However, the question led to a broader discussion that the R-matrix formalism can accommodate a
known non-free exterior solution through modified logarithmic derivatives, shift functions, and penetrabilities.
Gerry Hale noted an analogous strategy used in muon-catalyzed fusion, where nuclear short-range dynamics
were matched to muonic-molecular exterior solutions rather than free waves, with good agreement with
experiment. The idea was therefore identified as a potentially complementary way to formulate low-energy
scattering in a medium.
The uncoupled-phonon approximation
    Transcript time: approximately 06:57:59–07:00:55.
Question. What is the physical and computational content of the uncoupled-phonon approximation?
Answer. The underlying thermal correlation function is expanded so that, at an appropriate truncation
order, the time variable associated with lattice dynamics can be separated from the variables associated
with formation and decay of the compound nucleus. This decoupling allows the potential, resonant, and
interference terms to be evaluated separately. The resulting expressions are still mathematically complicated,
but they are substantially more tractable computationally and easier to debug and interpret than the fully
coupled correlation-function expression.

2.9 CLOSING TECHNICAL DISCUSSION: PHOTON CHANNELS, EDA, AZURE2, AND SAMMY
Fission channels in EDA
   Transcript time: approximately 08:12:17–08:14:28.
Question. How does EDA treat fission channels, given that most of its historical applications have involved
light nuclei?
Answer. The participants were not aware of an EDA application that treats fission in the way needed for
actinide evaluations, and no eliminated-channel fission formalism had been developed for EDA. This reflects
the code’s historical emphasis on light systems, for which a fission channel is not present. The discussion
contrasted this with SAMMY’s Reich–Moore treatment and identified the extension to other eliminated
channels as an area requiring further work.
Direct versus resonant radiative capture in EDA and AZURE2
   Transcript time: approximately 08:16:06–08:23:17.
Question. Does EDA contain an explicit direct-capture contribution, and how does its treatment compare
with the internal/external capture decomposition used in AZURE2?
Answer. EDA does not contain a separately labeled direct-capture term in the form discussed. Smooth
direct-capture-like behavior can instead be generated by distant poles. This may reproduce an energy
dependence but does not automatically guarantee the correct direct-capture angular distribution. AZURE2,
by contrast, contains an explicit internal plus external capture decomposition: the external contribution is
calculated from the scattering state, bound-state properties, and electromagnetic multipolarity, while the
internal reduced gamma width is fitted. For low-𝑄-value capture reactions, the external contribution can
dominate and is often what is meant by direct capture. The group agreed that comparing both descriptions on
a reaction with good differential gamma data would be valuable.
Can direct reactions be incorporated through external unitary matrices?
   Transcript time: approximately 08:23:45–08:27:00.
Discussion question. Is there a more general scattering-matrix way to incorporate direct reactions, including
direct capture, alongside the resonance R-matrix contribution?
Answer. One possibility discussed was to multiply the resonance scattering matrix by energy-dependent
unitary matrices that parameterize direct-reaction contributions. Capture channels could, in principle, be
represented through matrix elements of those external factors. Eliminating capture channels would then
introduce nonunitarity and complex quantities analogous to the imaginary capture widths of a Reich–Moore
description. The discussion emphasized that this is, at present, more a formal route than an established
evaluated-data implementation, particularly because the angular dependence of direct capture must still be
treated correctly.
Eliminating photon channels in the relativistic EDA formalism
   Transcript time: approximately 08:27:38–08:30:44.
Question. If photon channels were eliminated in the relativistic EDA formalism, would the result reduce to
the usual Reich–Moore form with only complex resonance energies?
Answer. Gerry Hale suggested that the elimination would likely be based on a reduced R-matrix construction
in which the retained matrix becomes complex. He did not expect the effect necessarily to be identical to
standard Reich–Moore, because quantities beyond the resonance energies could be modified. The group
had not implemented or tested this reduction. The discussion closed with the broader goal of finding a
unified treatment in which direct and compound radiative capture can be fitted together and the different
Lane–Thomas/AZURE2 and EDA photon-channel formulations can be compared quantitatively.

    Transcript time: approximately 00:45:49–00:46:49.
Question. Were the Wang 11 B+𝛼 data part of Bruce Vogelaar’s thesis work, and is there additional information

Dominant resolution effect in the thick-target 11 B(𝛼, 𝑝) data
    Transcript time: approximately 00:47:01–00:47:52.
Question. In the reconstructed broadening of the Wang 11 B(𝛼, 𝑝) data, is the dominant experimental-resolution
effect caused mainly by alpha energy loss and straggling in the target, or by the outgoing proton?
Answer. The dominant effect is the incident alpha beam losing energy and straggling through the relatively
thick boron target. The outgoing proton contributes less to the observed broadening. Modeling that target
response allows the underlying, much narrower R-matrix cross section to reproduce the measured thick-target
excitation function.

    Transcript time: approximately 00:47:01–00:49:33.

shared for comparison with the existing Los Alamos evaluation? Are all of the data already available through
EXFOR?
Answer. The speaker agreed to share the calculated cross sections and input information. Not all of the data
used in the analysis are necessarily available in EXFOR; some older measurements had to be digitized directly
from the literature. The participants therefore proposed a direct data exchange before publication so that the
independent analyses could be compared and remaining inconsistencies identified.

Physical meaning of the autocorrelation length 𝜌(𝐸)
    Transcript time: approximately 01:22:44–01:26:00.
Question. What is the autocorrelation quantity 𝜌(𝐸) that appears when the discrete covariance expression is
converted to a continuous-energy formula?
Answer. It characterizes the energy scale over which random cross-section fluctuations remain correlated. If
the cross section at one energy is known, nearby energies can be predicted rather well because a resonance
line shape is continuous; sufficiently far away, that information is lost. In the continuous limit, the integral of
the cross-section autocorrelation enters as the effective correlation length that converts a local variance into
its contribution over an extended energy range. It can be computed from ensembles of synthetic resonance
ladders generated from the average resonance parameters.

Statistical URR fluctuations versus uncertainty in the average parameters
    Transcript time: approximately 01:26:12–01:28:43.
Question. How does the proposed “statistical uncertainty” from not knowing the exact URR resonance
realization relate to the conventional uncertainty in the URR average parameters and probability tables?
Answer. They are distinct contributions. The calculation presented deliberately assumed that the average
resonance parameters are known exactly and quantified only the uncertainty arising because the actual
resonance realization is unknown. Uncertainty in the average spacings and widths would add another
component on top of this statistical contribution. Likewise, missing resonances or model deficiencies in the
resolved region add separate uncertainties. In principle, the corresponding variances can be combined to
form a more complete uncertainty budget.

Does the result imply that the RRR should always be extended as far as possible?
    Transcript time: approximately 01:28:58–01:32:33.
Question. Since the predicted URR contribution to application uncertainty decreases as the RRR boundary is
pushed upward, does the study simply support extending the resolved resonance region as high in energy as
possible?
Answer. Only if the resolved-region model remains reliable. Moving the boundary upward reduces uncertainty
associated with replacing explicit resonances by average parameters, but it may introduce a different uncertainty
if unresolved groups of levels are represented incorrectly as individual fitted resonances. The long-term goal
is therefore to estimate both contributions: a decreasing URR uncertainty and an increasing or otherwise
changing RRR-model uncertainty. Their combination should have a minimum that provides a more defensible
choice of the RRR–URR boundary.

3.3 INDEPENDENT DISTANT POLES IN THE 7 Be COMPOUND SYSTEM — HIBIKI NAKADA
Difference between an independent distant pole and a conventional background pole
 Questioner: Jakub.
Transcript time: approximately 00:29:39–00:31:19 in the second recording segment.
Question. What exactly is an independent distant pole (IDP), and how does it differ from the conventional
distant/background pole used in an R-matrix fit?
Answer. The essential distinction is in the channel structure. A conventional distant pole contributes the usual
channel-to-channel 𝑅𝑐𝑐′ matrix structure, whereas the IDP construction introduces background contributions
associated with individual entrance-channel components independently. The IDPs were placed well above the
fitted energy range so that they act as slowly varying background terms rather than as physical resonances in
the region of interest.

    Transcript time: approximately 00:31:30–00:33:28.

also be refitted without the IDPs to determine whether the physical resonance parameters can compensate?
Answer. The speaker agreed that the plotted “IDP off” curves were not refits. The motivation for introducing
the IDPs came from the earlier AMUR analysis, in which the simultaneous fit did not converge satisfactorily
without them. That non-convergent or poor-fit case was not shown in the presentation. The discussion
therefore identified a direct no-IDP refit comparison as useful supporting information.

Why multiple background poles may be required in a multichannel 𝐽 𝜋 block
    Transcript time: approximately 00:34:38–00:36:16.
Discussion question. Why can one distant pole be insufficient when several physical channels exist within
the same 𝐽 𝜋 block?
Answer. A single R-matrix pole generates an outer product of reduced-width amplitudes and therefore
contributes a rank-one matrix in channel space. A general symmetric background matrix has more independent
degrees of freedom. For example, a two-channel symmetric matrix has three independent elements, whereas
one pole supplies only two independent reduced widths, with the off-diagonal element constrained to their
product. More than one distant pole, or an equivalent structured background parameterization, is therefore
needed to span the full range of possible multichannel background behavior.

3.5 RICCATI FLOW AND CHANNEL-RADIUS EVOLUTION OF R-MATRIX POLES — JOSHUA

Does the Riccati flow remove practical channel-radius dependence?
    Transcript time: approximately 01:47:21–01:48:18.
Question. If fitted R-matrix parameters change when the channel radius is changed, what does the Riccati-flow
analysis allow one to do in practice? Does it mean that channel-radius dependence is no longer a problem?
Answer. For the exact theory, the physical scattering matrix is invariant under a change of channel radius
provided the 𝑅 matrix evolves according to the Riccati equation. The fitted pole energies and reduced widths
therefore move with the boundary even though the observable does not. The subsequent discussion refined
this point: in a finite, truncated pole expansion the invariance is only approximate, so residual channel-radius
dependence remains a practical diagnostic of truncation.

sensitivity decrease systematically as one goes from one pole to two poles, three poles, and so on, approaching
exact Riccati-flow invariance in the large-𝑁 limit?
Answer. That was identified as exactly the kind of numerical and analytic test that should be done next. The
current examples used simple one- and few-pole toy models. Extending the study to known potentials and
tracking convergence as the number of retained poles increases is planned.

What remains radius dependent in a finite pole expansion?

available experimental information is reported as total capture. The discussion emphasized that the physical
12 C(𝑛, 𝛾) problem is more complicated: several final states can contribute, a subthreshold state can influence

the ground-state transition, direct capture may be important, and interference between those components can
alter the energy dependence. Some experimental publications provide partial capture information, but many
EXFOR entries contain only the total. A more complete evaluation should therefore distinguish the important
final-state transitions when the data support it and sum them to obtain the total capture cross section.

Do retained reduced widths become complex after channel elimination?
    Transcript time: approximately 00:44:34–00:47:02.
Question. In the reduced R-matrix formalism, elimination of capture channels gives complex resonance
energies. Should the retained reduced-width amplitudes also be allowed to acquire imaginary components?
Answer. The discussion indicated that this can occur in the more general channel-elimination problem.
Once the eliminated-channel logarithmic derivative contributes a complex term, diagonalizing the resulting
complex symmetric level-space matrix produces complex eigenvalues and complex eigenvectors. Absorbing
those eigenvectors into the retained-channel width vectors can therefore generate complex retained widths.
Standard Reich–Moore does not normally require those additional degrees of freedom, but the more general
breakup problem may. It was suggested that the fitting procedure itself could test whether the imaginary
components are needed.

What information survives when breakup channels are eliminated?
    Transcript time: approximately 00:47:07–00:49:19.
Question. If several breakup channels are eliminated in a reduced R-matrix description, can the calculation
recover the contribution of each individual breakup channel, or only their sum?
Answer. Once a group of channels has been eliminated, their individual identities are lost from the reduced
problem. The reduced scattering matrix can provide the total cross section associated with the eliminated
space, but it cannot separate that total back into the original individual channels without additional information
or an explicit-channel calculation.

Relationship of the new breakup code to EDA
    Transcript time: approximately 00:49:21–00:49:55.
Question. Is the new code being developed for the reduced R-matrix/breakup study a new component of the
EDA modernization, or an independent code?
Answer. It is a separate code, developed independently of EDA. The implementation uses some numerical
and reaction-model infrastructure associated with the COH code and is intended as a flexible test bed for the
reduced-R-matrix formalism. Code-to-code comparisons with EDA remain an important validation step.

4.2 MULTISTEP DIRECT AND COMPOUND REACTIONS IN AN R-MATRIX FRAMEWORK —
 GORAN ARBANAS
Historical connection between R-matrix and Feshbach projection formalisms
 Transcript time: approximately 00:21:32–00:25:19 in the second recording segment.
Question/Comment. A Lane–Robson paper from the 1960s used the Bloch-operator/R-matrix framework in
a way that was closely connected with Feshbach projection operators. Does this reinforce the view that the
R-matrix and projection operator approaches are alternative, compatible descriptions rather than mutually
exclusive theories?
Answer. Yes. The speaker agreed and noted that preparation of the talk had uncovered a substantial historical
literature making similar connections among R-matrix theory, doorway states, shell-model descriptions, and
projection operators. Modern methods such as the Gamow shell model and shell model embedded in the
continuum continue this effort to connect structure and reaction degrees of freedom. The historical work
therefore supports the idea that different formalisms can often be transformed into, or interpreted as, different
representations of the same underlying reaction dynamics.

Can multistep direct and multistep compound processes be unified?
   Transcript time: approximately 00:25:22–00:26:39.
Question. Multistep direct and multistep compound reactions were presented as two different chains of
processes. Is there a more unified formalism in which the distinction does not have to be imposed explicitly?
Answer. The speaker pointed to modern continuum shell-model approaches as natural examples. In the
Gamow shell model, shell model embedded in the continuum, and related coupled-channel developments, the
calculation can treat particle-hole structure and continuum coupling within a common many-body framework
rather than labeling every path as “direct” or “compound.” Such approaches may be particularly important for
loosely bound systems or cases with more than one particle in the continuum, where the older one-particle
projection formalism becomes restrictive.

Overlap functions as a bridge to R-matrix theory
   Transcript time: approximately 00:26:40–00:28:04.
Question/Comment. Can overlap functions provide a practical connection between modern many-body/continuum
calculations and phenomenological R-matrix descriptions?
Answer. Yes. By projecting a complicated many-body wave function onto a particular two-cluster channel,
one obtains an overlap function that can be evaluated at a chosen channel radius. The value and derivative
of that overlap provide a natural bridge between the microscopic many-body description and the R-matrix
boundary description. The participants noted that this idea had already appeared in related earlier work and
could be developed further.

What distinguishes the frequentist and Bayesian uncertainty treatments?
    Transcript time: approximately 02:18:04–02:20:49.

4.5 WORKING-LUNCH DISCUSSION: GENERALIZED REICH–MOORE AND ELIMINATED
 CAPTURE CHANNELS
Can the real shift term be retained when eliminating channels?
    Transcript time: approximately 03:15:16–03:16:12.
Question. The standard Reich–Moore approximation replaces the eliminated capture-channel logarithmic
derivative by an imaginary penetrability term. In a more general eliminated-channel treatment, can the real
shift component also be retained?
Answer. Yes. The purely imaginary treatment is a simplification appropriate to the standard Reich–Moore
approximation. The more general Lane–Thomas channel-elimination expression can retain a full complex
logarithmic derivative with both real and imaginary components. The discussion noted that this greater
generality is likely to be necessary when eliminating breakup channels rather than a very large set of weak
gamma channels.

Generalized Reich–Moore through complex eigenvalues and widths
    Transcript time: approximately 03:16:13–03:23:09.
Discussion question. What additional parameters arise if one performs the eliminated-channel problem
without dropping the off-diagonal terms that are neglected in standard Reich–Moore?
Answer. The eliminated channels contribute a complex symmetric matrix in level space. Diagonalizing the
combination of the original level-energy matrix and this complex term produces complex eigenvalues and
complex eigenvectors. The complex eigenvalues generalize the familiar Reich–Moore complex resonance
energies, while the transformed retained-channel reduced-width amplitudes can also become complex. For
ordinary radiative capture the off-diagonal terms may be small enough that standard Reich–Moore is an
excellent approximation. For other eliminated channels, particularly when there are fewer of them or their
couplings are stronger, the additional imaginary width components could be physically relevant fitting
parameters.

How might direct capture be eliminated consistently?
    Transcript time: approximately 03:23:15–03:33:06.
Discussion question. If resonant capture channels are eliminated from the R-matrix level matrix, how should
a direct-capture contribution be treated so that direct and resonant amplitudes can still interfere consistently?
Answer. The proposed route uses the direct-process matrices that multiply the resonance scattering matrix.
When all channels are retained, those matrices are unitary. Eliminating gamma channels from the direct sector
should make them nonunitary in a way analogous to the nonunitarity introduced into the resonant sector. An
exploratory simple parameterization discussed during the session was to allow a quantity associated with
the external/direct scattering, even the effective channel radius in a simplified representation, to acquire an
imaginary component. The total capture would then be obtained from the deviation from unitarity of the
complete reduced scattering matrix, allowing direct and resonant effects to be combined at the amplitude
level. This was presented as a research direction, not as an established production formalism.

What information is lost when gamma channels are eliminated?
    Transcript time: approximately 03:34:19–03:37:47.
Question. After summing over and eliminating the individual gamma channels, can the effective widths still
be associated with specific final states or multipolarities?

 84

Answer. No. The effective eliminated-channel matrix contains sums over many gamma channels, and the
subsequent reduced parameters no longer retain a unique association with individual final states, multipolarities,
or gamma rays. The reduced calculation preserves the total cross section represented by those eliminated
channels, obtained through the loss of unitarity, but not their individual identities. An intermediate strategy
is possible in which important strong capture channels are retained explicitly while the large remainder is
eliminated.

    Transcript time: approximately 00:38:00–00:39:49.

Smallest practical sample for a VENUS transmission measurement
    Transcript time: approximately 00:39:49–00:43:13.
Question. Given the very high neutron intensity at VENUS, how small a radioactive or rare-isotope sample
could be measured in the resolved resonance region?
Answer. A definitive minimum mass had not yet been calculated. The relevant quantity is not mass alone
but the desired macroscopic areal density relative to the cross section and target transmission. A useful
design procedure is to select a target transmission, for example roughly 20–30% through a resonance, and
derive the required areal density from the cross section. For very small samples, beam collimation and
spatial uniformity can become as important as neutron statistics. Moving the sample closer to the source and
improving collimation were among the proposed beamline modifications. For actinides, facility safety limits
and encapsulation requirements also constrain the allowable sample design.

4.8 NUCLEAR-DATA UNCERTAINTY QUANTIFICATION WITH SCALE — RIKE BOSTELMANN
Sensitivities to geometry and nuclide concentrations
    Transcript time: approximately 01:39:27–01:43:30.
Question. Would it be useful to extend SCALE/TSUNAMI-style sensitivity coefficients beyond nuclear cross
sections to geometry dimensions and nuclide concentrations, both for complete uncertainty propagation and
for design optimization?
Answer. The speaker had not seen geometry or concentration sensitivities used as systematically as
cross-section sensitivities in the SCALE community. Geometry and composition uncertainties are more
commonly treated by direct parameter variation or random sampling because the number of variables
and correlations is usually modest. Nevertheless, extending formal sensitivity methods to those inputs
was considered interesting. The speaker emphasized that an even broader need is to extend sensitivity
analysis to additional output quantities beyond 𝑘 eff , such as power, flux, depletion inventories, and other

Why can evaluated nuclear-data uncertainties increase over time?
    Transcript time: approximately 01:43:37–01:48:07.
Question/Discussion. Why do some evaluated-data uncertainties become larger in newer library releases
even though additional experiments and evaluations have been performed?
Answer. A larger uncertainty does not necessarily mean that the knowledge has become worse. Older
evaluations may have omitted systematic effects, inherited overly optimistic standards uncertainties, or
represented only the formal resonance-parameter covariance. New evaluations can reveal previously
unrecognized discrepancies among experiments and therefore adopt a more realistic uncertainty. The discussion
emphasized the need to distinguish parameter covariance from experimental/systematic contributions and to
carry both into evaluated-data formats wherever possible.

Prioritizing which cross sections should be improved
 Questioner: Jesse Brown.
Transcript time: approximately 01:48:08–01:53:23.
Question. A ranking of contributors to uncertainty in 𝑘 eff combines the system sensitivity with the nuclear-data
covariance. How can one distinguish a reaction that is important because its sensitivity is large but its data are
already precise from one where an achievable reduction in the cross-section uncertainty would genuinely
improve the application?
Answer. The two ingredients should be examined separately as well as together. Energy-dependent sensitivity
profiles show where the application responds to a reaction, while the evaluated relative uncertainty shows
how poorly that reaction is known. A high-priority measurement is typically one that is both important
to the application and sufficiently uncertain that an experiment could materially reduce the propagated
uncertainty. The ranking is also application dependent: reactions that are unimportant in conventional
light-water systems can become major contributors in advanced fast-spectrum reactors, safeguards, depletion,
or other applications.

Including experimental cost in measurement prioritization
    Transcript time: approximately 01:53:46–01:56:24.
Question/Discussion. Can the prioritization of new measurements include not only sensitivity and uncertainty
reduction but also the expected experimental cost of achieving a given precision?

 87

Answer. The discussion recalled an earlier ORNL effort that treated this as an inverse sensitivity/uncertainty
optimization problem. The concept was to assign a cost to reducing particular nuclear-data uncertainties and
select the combination of measurements that most efficiently reduced uncertainty in an application such as
𝑘 eff . An older code and publication were said to exist, and the participants suggested that the approach could
be revisited if there is sufficient application and sponsor interest.

How should application requirements drive nuclear-data targets?
 Transcript time: approximately 01:56:14–end of technical discussion.
Question. What information from analysts and application designers would most help evaluators and
experimentalists decide what nuclear-data accuracy is actually needed?
Answer. The discussion emphasized application-specific tolerances. There is no single uncertainty target that
is optimal for every use of a nuclear-data evaluation. A reactor design, criticality benchmark, safeguards
observable, isotopic inventory prediction, or forensic application may weight the same reaction very differently.
Ideally, simulation and sensitivity tools would translate design or performance requirements into quantitative
nuclear-data accuracy needs. Evaluators could then judge whether an existing uncertainty is already adequate
or whether a new measurement would have a meaningful impact.

5.1 THERMAL NEUTRON SCATTERING LAW AND THE IRMA CODE — IYAD AL-QASIR AND
 KEMAL RAMIĆ
Why does one machine-learning phonon model predict softer spectra?
    Transcript time: approximately 01:00:13–01:01:29.
Question. Why does the DPA/DeepMD-based global machine-learning potential appear to predict
systematically softer phonon spectra than several of the other models?
Answer. No definitive physical explanation was offered. The speaker noted that a similar softening trend had
also appeared when training a model on a single material, suggesting that the behavior may arise from the
details of the model architecture or training rather than from the IRMA implementation. The purpose of
providing several global potentials is therefore partly to allow users to compare model spread rather than rely
on a single prediction.

Can the spread of machine-learning potentials be used as a thermal-scattering uncertainty estimate?
    Transcript time: approximately 01:02:22–01:04:01.
Question. How should the set of different machine-learning interatomic potentials be used to quantify
uncertainty in a thermal-scattering-law calculation?
Answer. The speaker stressed that the code does not yet claim a formally rigorous uncertainty quantification
method. Instead, the ensemble of available potentials can be used as an exploratory sensitivity study. A user
can generate phonon spectra and corresponding thermal-scattering data from several models, propagate those
variants through a transport calculation, and see whether a quantity such as 𝑘 eff changes appreciably. If the
spread is important, that is evidence that a full material-specific calculation or evaluation is warranted.

Is IRMA written in Python and when will it be open source?
 Questioner: Devin Barry.
Transcript time: approximately 01:04:14–01:05:26.
Question. Is IRMA primarily a Python code, and when is an open-source release expected?
Answer. Yes, the code is primarily Python-based. The release process had already been initiated at the time of
the workshop, with an open-source release hoped for within roughly one or two months. A paper describing
the code and its validation was also in preparation. Considerable effort had been devoted to validating the
implemented physics against independent codes and neutron scattering measurements.

5.2 (𝛼, 𝑛) MEASUREMENTS ON LIGHT NUCLEI AND MULTICHANNEL R-MATRIX ANALYSIS
 — JAMES DEBOER
Why measure differential and partial (𝛼, 𝑛) cross sections rather than only totals?
    Transcript time: approximately 01:11:19–01:12:30.
Question. Are the campaign measurements limited to angle-integrated (𝛼, 𝑛) cross sections, or are angular
distributions and partial cross sections also being measured?
Answer. The goal is to measure double-differential neutron cross sections, angular distributions, and partial
cross sections to individual final states. Total neutron yields alone do not identify the populated final state and
therefore do not uniquely determine the outgoing neutron energy spectrum. The angular information is also
important for applications in which forward–backward asymmetries matter and is essential for constraining
an R-matrix analysis.

What does the simultaneous multi-detector measurement buy experimentally?
    Transcript time: approximately 01:21:43–01:22:56.
Question/Discussion. Why was the experiment designed to measure neutrons, gamma rays, and charged
particles simultaneously?
Answer. Simultaneous acquisition provides several independent reaction channels under the same beam
and target conditions. This creates shared systematic uncertainties that can cancel in ratios or common fits
and provides internal cross-checks on normalization and target characterization. It also gives the R-matrix
analysis a much richer set of constraints than a neutron-only measurement.

Why are the measured 10 B(𝛼, 𝑛) angular distributions important?
    Transcript time: approximately 01:31:29–01:32:44.
Question/Discussion. What new information is provided by the angular-distribution measurements for
10 B(𝛼, 𝑛)?

Answer. The existing evaluation effectively assumed isotropic neutron emission in regions where little angular
information was available. The new measurements showed substantial departures from isotropy. These data
therefore provide direct constraints on the partial-wave and spin-parity content of the reaction and should
improve the evaluated neutron energy-angle distributions.

Can the multichannel data resolve ambiguous spin-parity assignments?
    Transcript time: approximately 01:34:41–01:35:33.
Question/Discussion. Can simultaneous fits to several reaction channels distinguish between competing
literature spin-parity assignments for individual levels?
Answer. Yes. One example discussed was a state that had been tentatively assigned 2− in the literature. By
fitting angular distributions and several simultaneously measured exit channels—including neutron, proton,
and deuteron channels—the analysis favored a 3+ assignment. This was presented as an example of how
multichannel data can constrain compound-nucleus level structure more strongly than a single reaction
channel.

How constrained is the low-energy 12 C(𝑛, 𝛾) ground-state capture?

Question. The capture plot showed relatively sparse ground-state capture data, while the total capture cross

Answer. Yes, effectively. The quoted thermal value is for total capture rather than the ground-state branch

What controls the importance of the subthreshold-state contribution?
    Transcript time: approximately 01:48:49–01:50:16.
Question/Comment. How is the importance of a subthreshold state connected to the low-energy scattering
behavior?
Answer. The discussion connected the effect qualitatively with the scattering length and the strength of the
bound-state tail in the entrance channel. For 12 C+𝑛, the positive scattering length and the strong 𝑠-wave
bound-state contribution make the subthreshold term important. The speaker contrasted this with another
system under study where the scattering length is negative and the analogous subthreshold contribution
appears much smaller.

When will the new boron data be submitted to EXFOR?

Question. What is the expected timeline for making the new boron (𝛼, 𝑛) measurements available through

Answer. The boron data are expected to be the first campaign data set released. A project report containing
the data had already been prepared, while the journal paper was being written. The expectation expressed at
the workshop was that the paper would be submitted in the fall and that the data would be placed in EXFOR
after publication, with early 2027 mentioned as a plausible time frame.

Are significant breakup channels present in the boron systems?
    Transcript time: approximately 01:51:58–01:52:30.
Question. Do important three-body or multiparticle breakup channels open over the energy region of the
boron measurements?
Answer. They are expected to be weak over most of the measured range, which is one reason the boron
systems were attractive for the campaign. A breakup contribution may begin to appear near the highest
energies, but it was not expected to dominate. The experimental treatment of strong breakup channels was
acknowledged as substantially more difficult.

5.3 19 F EVALUATION AND ENERGY-DEPENDENT ATTENUATION MEASUREMENTS —
 MARCO PIGNI
Why do the historical 19 F total-cross-section measurements disagree by so much?
    Transcript time: approximately 02:29:47–02:31:17.
Question. Is there a known reason for the roughly several-percent discrepancy among the historical fluorine
total-cross-section measurements?
Answer. No firm explanation had yet been established because the project had only recently started. Sample
characterization was suggested as one plausible source, precisely because transmission measurements are
normally expected to be comparatively robust. The large discrepancy was therefore one motivation for
performing an independent attenuation experiment. The broader value of the project is the development of a
reusable measurement capability that could later be applied to other materials such as chromium and iron.
What neutron-energy range can the attenuation experiment cover?
    Transcript time: approximately 02:31:33–02:32:07.
Question. What neutron-energy range is expected to be accessible with the energy-dependent attenuation
measurement?
Answer. The reactor beam is expected to provide useful information from low neutron energies into the
MeV region. For the fluorine problem, the primary region of interest is the few-MeV range where the
differential-data discrepancies are most evident.
Can the new fluorine work be formulated as an R-matrix evaluation?
    Transcript time: approximately 02:32:27–02:33:31.
Question. Can the new 19 F evaluation be carried out directly in an R-matrix formalism rather than only as
reconstructed pointwise cross sections?
Answer. That is the intended direction. Earlier file-format or processing limitations appear to have contributed
to the use of reconstructed cross sections instead of an explicit resonance-parameter representation. The new
work aims to provide resonance parameters and include the relevant inelastic channels explicitly as particle
pairs in the R-matrix analysis, subject to the energy range and available data.
5.4 CLOSING DISCUSSION: FUTURE DIRECTIONS FOR R-MATRIX ANALYSIS
Which developments would most improve evaluator efficiency?
    Transcript time: approximately 02:34:08–02:36:00.
Discussion question. What future developments would most improve the practical use of R-matrix methods?
Answer. A recurring theme was better use of evaluator time. Participants highlighted faster and more reliable
parameter-search methods, easier access to prior evaluations and experimental databases, and the ability to
exchange resonance parameters among codes and formats. The discussion emphasized that many technical
developments are driven by concrete physics problems, but that better software infrastructure can make those
developments reusable across applications.

 96

Would a common repository of R-matrix evaluations be useful?
    Transcript time: approximately 02:36:03–02:38:49.
Question/Discussion. Would nuclear astrophysics benefit from an open repository containing R-matrix
evaluations, resonance parameters, and reconstructed cross sections in a directly usable form?
Answer. Yes. Participants noted that published resonance parameters are often easier to locate than complete
reconstructed cross-section curves, and that the community lacks a single convenient repository for many
charged-particle evaluations. At the same time, ENDF and eventually GNDS were identified as the natural
long-term common formats. The preferred solution is therefore not to create an isolated parallel standard, but
to make it much easier for codes such as AZURE2 to read from and write to ENDF/GNDS and to provide
user-friendly tools for processing those files.

Why is ENDF/GNDS adoption difficult in nuclear astrophysics?
    Transcript time: approximately 02:38:51–02:43:03.
Question/Discussion. If ENDF already provides a common nuclear-data framework, why is it not more
widely used directly by the nuclear-astrophysics R-matrix community?
Answer. Accessibility is a major barrier. An evaluator unfamiliar with ENDF can find the file format
and processing chain difficult to learn, even when the underlying resonance parameters are conceptually
simple. The discussion suggested that a user-facing processor or conversion layer—for example, through
AZURE2, Ferdinand, or future GNDS tools—could hide much of the format complexity. GNDS was viewed
as potentially more flexible for quantities such as ANCs and charged-particle resonance information.

Can AI perform parts of an R-matrix evaluation?
    Transcript time: approximately 02:43:03–02:46:20.
Question/Discussion. How far could artificial intelligence be used to automate nuclear-data evaluation?
Answer. Participants considered AI likely to become increasingly important in code development, literature
and database searches, initial level-scheme construction, parameter exploration, and possibly automated
fitting. A plausible future workflow would allow an AI system to query evaluated nuclear structure databases,
locate relevant experiments, assemble initial resonance parameters, run fitting codes, and compare candidate
models. The discussion also emphasized the need for strict provenance and review checkpoints: automation
should increase throughput without turning the evaluation process into an uncontrolled sequence of opaque
changes. Reviewed intermediate states of an evaluation should therefore be preserved as explicit milestones.

Why is breakup a priority for future R-matrix development?
    Transcript time: approximately 02:46:29–02:47:03.
Question/Discussion. Which new reaction mechanism is particularly important for extending current R-matrix
capabilities?
Answer. Three-body and multibody breakup was repeatedly identified as a major challenge. It is especially
relevant for systems such as beryllium and lies outside the simplest two-body channel picture used by most
production R-matrix codes. Reduced-channel treatments may provide useful approximations in some cases,
but a more complete treatment remains an important research direction.

How important are graphical interfaces and “quality-of-life” features?
    Transcript time: approximately 02:47:03–03:01:03.
Question/Discussion. Should development effort prioritize modern graphical interfaces and interactive tools,
or should it remain focused on the physics engine?
Answer. Both viewpoints were represented. Experienced users often prefer direct control of text input
files, while newer users benefit greatly from interactive interfaces that make resonance energies, widths, and
interference signs easy to inspect and modify. The broader consensus was that lowering the entry barrier is
valuable because it can attract new evaluators and experimentalists to the field, provided the interface does
not hide the underlying physics. The modern interfaces demonstrated in the workshop were therefore viewed
as complementary to, not replacements for, robust physics engines.

Can new calculated observables drive new experiments?
    Transcript time: approximately 03:01:03–03:04:02.
Question/Discussion. Should R-matrix codes add observables such as polarization and multi-particle decay
spectra even when little experimental data currently exist?
Answer. Yes. The discussion emphasized a “chicken-and-egg” relationship between theory tools and
experiments. If a code can predict a polarization observable or a decay spectrum and demonstrate that it
is highly sensitive to an ambiguous resonance parameter, that prediction can motivate the substantial effort
required to build a new experiment. Examples mentioned included polarization observables, beta-delayed
particle spectra, inelastic electron scattering followed by particle decay, and transfer reactions populating
unbound states.

Can undergraduate institutions contribute meaningfully to R-matrix and nuclear-data work?
    Transcript time: approximately 02:49:37–02:53:44.
Question. Is there interest in engaging primarily undergraduate institutions in R-matrix analysis and
nuclear-data work?
Answer. Yes, provided projects are carefully scoped. Full R-matrix evaluation has a substantial learning
curve, but open tools such as AZURE2 and public data from EXFOR make the field accessible. Repetitive but
meaningful tasks—for example, preparing EXFOR entries, digitizing or validating data sets, or performing
families of similar calculations—can be well suited to course-based undergraduate research experiences.
Such projects can simultaneously reduce data backlogs and build a future workforce.

Gerry Hale’s view of the most important future directions
    Transcript time: approximately 02:54:02–02:56:51.
Discussion summary. Gerry Hale highlighted several directions that he considered especially important:
treatment of three-body and multibody breakup, extension toward relativistic R-matrix theory at higher
energies, improved treatment of gamma-ray channels, modernization of user interfaces, and greater use
of AI for code development, experimental-data discovery, and exploration of possible level structures. He
emphasized that the underlying R-matrix engines can remain powerful while benefiting substantially from
modern interfaces and automation.
