What is portal monitoring?
Portal monitoring is one method in a suite of options for treaty verification and can assist
in verification of treaty accountable items (TAIs)2. Portal monitor technology spans a
wide range of sensors, including radiation detectors, break-beams, or weight sensors.
For radiation-detecting portal monitors, they are able to confirm absence / presence of a
radiological signature and to track TAI direction of motion. Portal monitoring uses
measurements from strategically placed sensors (e.g., radiation detectors) to record the
entry or egress of TAIs. The implementation of portal monitoring relies on three key
elements: perimeter definition, portal locations, and sensor technology, and can be
implemented in either time-bound or continuous operations depending on what is
permitted by the Treaty.

Portal Monitoring Methods and Techniques
Perimeter Definition – The first step in establishing portal monitoring capabilities is
defining a perimeter of interest. The perimeter is reliant on the inspecting party’s goals,
as defined in the treaty or agreement, and the physical landscape of the area. For
example, a perimeter can be drawn at the outermost site boundary, around a building,
or around a room. Determining an appropriate perimeter for useful portal monitoring is
similar to determining a material balance area (MBA) in regards to nuclear material
accountancy and control (NMAC), such as that employed by the International Atomic
Energy Agency. Perimeter boundaries may or may not be visible or tangible (e.g., an
infrared boundary, a boundary drawn on paper). The inspector may want to consider
methods for confirmation of the perimeter boundary’s integrity to ensure there are no
openings for diversion of TAIs. In the interest of time, money, and rapport with the host
country, it could be useful to employ existing host country infrastructure, such as a
facility fence line, when possible.

Portal Location – After determining the perimeter of interest, portal locations can be
selected. A portal is an intentional, declared opening in the perimeter boundary. The
portal is monitored, with fixed or mobile sensors, that can take measurements of any
items, people, or vehicles that pass through the portal. It is ideal to have as few portals
as necessary to reduce the burden on both parties, and to also use existing host

Sensor Technology and Item Attributes– The final step in establishing portal monitoring
capabilities is to consider the sensor technology and what TAI attributes it is able to
measure and verify. Portal monitoring technology can be unattended or attended by
inspectors, and also range in size. The size range for radiation detectors spans from
hand-held detectors to portals large enough to monitor whole vehicles and shipping
containers. Other sensors could include break-beam technology, video cameras,
radiography, radiofrequency identification tag scanners, and weight sensors. The
inspector may also decide to forego technology in an attended monitoring scenario, and
simply visually confirm TAI serial numbers as they pass through the portal.

To select portal monitor technology, the inspecting country should determine their
priorities and needs, account for what is allowable in the treaty or agreement, and
consider the operations, items, and activities occurring within the perimeter. For
example, if there is no vehicle traffic through the perimeter, then large vehicle monitors
would be unnecessary. Consideration should also be given to the amount, frequency,
and type of data generated; data post-processing plans; and data transfer from the
technology to the inspector (for example, does the host need to review the data first or
should an information barrier be mandated).

An additional concern for sensor selection is how the signal collected by a sensor(s) is
mapped to an attribute of a TAI or the absence of an attribute. For example, if TAIs
were defined as containing plutonium or uranium, a spectroscopic gamma detector
could analyze the spectrum of an item passing through the monitor to determine if that
item contains that given attribute. In this case, if the spectrum did not have the
characteristics of a plutonium or uranium source, despite its radioactivity, the portal
monitor would not record the passage of a TAI through the portal. Some other TAI
attributes that could be considered for use in portal monitoring can include when the
mass of an item exceeds a designated threshold, item shape (perhaps determined
behind an information barrier), and serial numbers.

What information can portal monitoring provide?
Portal monitoring is a useful chain of custody (CoC) approach for TAIs, where “CoC”
refers to the systems, processes, and procedures put in place by the inspector to
maintain confidence during the inspector’s absence. CoC may be needed during an
onsite inspection, such as when the inspectors leave at the end of the day, or in
between onsite inspections or reporting periods. Portal monitoring can therefore be
useful to track TAI movements, even in the inspector’s absence.

What are the advantages of portal monitoring?
The greatest advantage of portal monitoring is the ability to hold prohibited host
activities at risk of detection when inspectors cannot be there in person. Portal
monitoring can also be scalable and its technology diverse, making it a flexible and
customizable solution to meet inspector needs. Lastly, its level of intrusiveness can also
be varied. The inspecting party and the host country can work together to identify when,
how, and how often data from the portals should be transmitted. In some situations, only
locally stored data may be permitted (for operational security reasons), requiring
inspectors to verify sealed data during each inspection of the site.

What kind of technologies are used for portal monitoring?
The types of technologies that can be used for portal monitoring are vast and diverse,
and they will depend on the goals of the treaty. However, a commonly considered
approach for TAI verification is radiation detection equipment. This equipment can
detect and collect count rates, counts above a threshold, and radiation spectra.
Additional technology can include vehicle scanners that use radiography to inspect
spatial details of the contents of vehicles; break beams to indicate an occupancy within
a portal, and weight or motion sensors to identify vehicles driving by. It may be
important to verify the number of TAIs on a shipment, but this is challenging to do
without inspecting the contents of the shipment. One method to account for the
number of TAIs in a vehicle at a portal monitor could be applying emitting unique
identifier tags (UIDs) to the TAIs and automatically register these UID tags at the portal.
However, these UIDs would likely be applied to the TAI container rather than being
affixed directly to the TAI. The automatic registration of UIDs may serve as an
automated declaration of TAIs passing through a portal. Additional CoC, challenge
inspections, or portal monitor sensors may be used to increase confidence that each
TAI container with a registered UID truly contains a TAI.

Aside from technology types, it is also important to identify how the technology will be
used. An autonomously operated portal monitoring system would be advantageous
because it can provide a remote monitoring option that is persistent. However
autonomous operation adds significant complexity to false alarm handling, and
establishing data and equipment trust would be more challenging. Another option could
be a portal monitor that is part of an inspector check point. For example, having an
inspector at a portal where contents are unloaded for inspection could allow for the
visual confirmation of serial numbers or number of items traveling through a portal.

Portal Monitoring Applications for Ipindovia
For Ipindovia3, implementing portal monitoring as part of the overall treaty verification
approach is dependent on the goal of a particular treaty, which may be a limitation on
the total number of NWs, or a defined reduction in NWs over time. In either treaty type,
IPNDV have identified four potential situations where inspector confidence could be
improved through the implementation of portal monitoring. Of the four verification

 Objective #1: Verify the total number of TAIs in Ipindovia.
 Given that all nuclear weapons (NWs) must pass through maintenance activities
 during their lifetime, the LADDU production site is the location of greatest interest for
 portal monitoring. LADDU consists of three main areas: (1) central storage of NWs;
 (2) NW production/refurbishment facility and storage; and (3) the broader LADDU
 site. Only the first two locations should contain NWs. Therefore, it is recommended
 to establish portal monitoring around the perimeter of these two areas of LADDU.
 The portal monitors would verify each movement notification associated with
 LADDU. The verification of movements provide information about the change in the
 number of TAIs at the site, which supports verification of the total declared number
 of TAIs.

 Objective #2: Verify the distribution of NWs at each deployment site.
 In addition to Objective 1, the four active delivery sites with declared NWs could also
 be monitored. This includes Seastar Naval Base, Northern Light ICBM Base, Altitude
 AFB, and Westend ICBM Base. Portal monitoring around each base can be used to
 verify the count of TAIs arriving at and departing from each one. Then, the
 distribution of TAIs within Ipindovia could be determined. However, for operational
 deployment bases, locally-held data produced by portal monitoring equipment would
 likely be preferred as the Host is unlikely to permit an ongoing inspector presence at
 the control point (the Intermediate Nuclear Forces treaty verification measures offer
 an exemption to this principle). This method could also help confirm that the correct
 number of monthly TAI movements to/from the bases are occurring.

 Objective #3: Verify the absence of undeclared NWs.
 In addition to Objectives 1 and 2, portal monitoring or inspections can be used to
 verify that the four inactive delivery sites are not receiving undeclared NWs. These
 sites include Eastside ICBM Base, Velocity AFB, Southern Cross ICBM Base, and
 Saltstar Naval Base. Given that these were previously active bases, it could be
 destabilizing if the host is building up an undeclared inventory at these four
 locations. Unattended portal monitoring at these four sites could be a cost-effective
 way to verify NWs do not enter these bases, and reduce or eliminate the need for
 inspectors to visit those locations. Due to their inactive nuclear weapon status, Host
 countries may be willing to accept remote, networked radiation portal monitoring as
 long as the Host’s military security concerns can be managed.

 Objective #4: Localized maintenance of the continuity of knowledge of TAI
 components.
 During the dismantlement of TAIs, temporary and strategically placed portal
 monitoring can provide confidence that all nuclear material from the TAI remains

 within the perimeter of the facility. This form of temporary active perimeter maintains
 the ‘continuity of knowledge’ of all parts of the TAI until a new chain of custody is
 established through, documentation, template matching and/or the application of
 seals on the fissile material storage container. The use of portal monitoring was
 exercised in German-French disarmament verification exercises NuDiVe, in 2019
 and 2022.

It is important to acknowledge that some sites are too cumbersome to monitor for very
little gain. For example, the Arendy Research Station, where the diversity and dual-use
nature of the facility’s research would generate so much nuclear data on non-treaty-
relevant research activities that it would be difficult to process in a timely and helpful
manner and could actually decrease confidence through false alarms.

Challenges and Considerations for Implementation
What are the limitations of portal monitoring?
Portal monitoring can only be applied to declared facilities, so to address the concern of
potential undeclared facilities in the host country, other verification methods would need
to be used in tandem. In addition, when establishing a portal monitoring system, it may
be limited by existing host infrastructure (or lack thereof) in the country, potentially
including access to utilities such as power and internet. The agreed-upon level of
intrusiveness as defined in the treaty can also affect the placement of the perimeter and
portals.

What are some of technical challenges of portal monitoring?
While perimeter portal monitoring is a promising concept for verification scenarios,
several key limitations should be understood. First, when collecting data at portals, one
must avoid collecting too much or too little data. A balance between what is useful and
what can reasonably be analyzed will need to be identified. Second, false alarms from
the detection equipment can be generated, such as a count rate alarm from unrelated
radioactive material (e.g., naturally occurring radioactive material, individuals treated
with medical radionuclides) passing through a portal; both parties should establish a
process for handling false alarms so they do not greatly undermine confidence. Third,
the impact of shielding material or container type on detection probabilities should be
considered. A final challenge for portal monitoring technology is defining characteristic
differences in the detection signatures between TAIs and non-TAIs or different TAI
types without giving away sensitive information about the TAI. These and other
limitations need to be dually considered when developing a portal monitoring strategy or
solution.

General Questions to Consider When Negotiating the Use of Portal Monitoring
  • How do differing refurbishment timelines in Ipindovia impact portal monitoring?
  • What are the trade-offs for drawing certain perimeters at certain facilities?
 o How does perimeter size, number, and location of portals affect inspector
 and host confidence in compliance?

• What are the trade-offs for using different portal monitor technologies/detection
 methods?
 o How does the efficiency of detection affect inspector confidence?
• How will portal monitoring data be transferred to the inspecting party?
 o How much data is expected?
 o When and at what frequency should data be released, processed, and
 evaluated?
 o What tools may be necessary to transmit large data sets?
• What additional information is required to support portal monitoring use?
 o How does portal monitoring fit into the larger monitoring and verification
 strategy?
• Can the portal monitoring system distinguish between TAIs and non-TAIs that
 also have a radiological signature?
• How should both parties handle false positive alarms?
• How should both parties address portal monitoring technology that is broken?
 o How do inspectors verify the portal monitoring is working correctly?
• For vehicle portal, how important is it to determine the number of TAIs in a
 shipment? How feasible is this?
