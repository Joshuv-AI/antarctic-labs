 Executive Summary
This project purchased equipment to advance hydrogen technology research. These additions to
the University at Buffalo (UB) laboratories in Chemical and Biological Engineering (CBE), and
Materials Design and Innovation (MDI) are expanding the research enterprise at UB, supporting
the creation of new clean energy, clean industry infrastructure, and the associated stable, good-
paying jobs across the economic spectrum. A Dynamic Vapor Sorption (DVS) Carbon CO2 and
H2O gravimetric sorption analyzer was deployed in CBE. A tensile module was deployed in
MDI, to be installed in a scanning electron microscope.

The resulting growth of leading-edge hydrogen infrastructure in New York will help to position
UB, its academic and research partners, and Western New York industry at the forefront of the
energy transition and help meet state and national energy decarbonization targets.

 1. Introduction
This project purchased equipment to advance hydrogen technology research at the University at
Buffalo. The equipment is installed in laboratories in the Departments of Chemical and
Biological Engineering (CBE) and Materials Design and Innovation (MDI) at the University at
Buffalo (UB).

The equipment, to be shared across UB researchers and with regional technology partners, is
being used to address future hydrogen challenges and opportunities. The equipment
complements existing facilities at UB and is intended to support a wide range of research on the
use of hydrogen as a synthetic fuel. No specific problem or challenge was identified at the time
the proposal was prepared.

The new equipment will expand the research enterprise at UB, support the creation of new clean
energy and clean industry infrastructure and the associated stable, good-paying jobs across the
economic spectrum. The resulting growth of leading-edge hydrogen infrastructure in New York
will help to position UB, its academic and research partners, and Western New York industry at
the forefront of the energy transition and help meet state and federal energy targets.

There is no plan for commercialization. The University at Buffalo’s research and
commercialization partners will have ready access to the new equipment.

This report is organized using the template provided by DOE. Section 2 provides background
information. Section 3 identifies the key achievements of the project. Sections 4 and 5 address
the planned benefits of the purchases and the key achievements to date, respectively. Sections 6
and 7 present the lessons learned and the conclusions of the project, respectively. Section 8
addresses possible future work. There are no publications, inventions, or patents to report now
but future research using the purchased equipment will generate such products. Similarly,
because the project involved the purchase of two pieces of equipment, there is neither a list of
references for appendices containing data nor calculations.

 2. Background
The purpose of the project was to augment existing UB laboratories with equipment to support
new hydrogen-related research, with the goal of helping DOE and US industry deploy hydrogen
at scale as a synthetic fuel.

The equipment was chosen based on the available funding of $250,000 USD, with two $125,000
purchases: one for deployment in the Department of Chemical and Biological Engineering
(CBE) and the other in the Department of Materials Design and Innovation (MDI).

The project technical team comprised

• SUNY Distinguished Professor Andrew Whittaker, Ph.D., P.E., S.E., NAE, F.ACI, F.SEI,
 Dist.M.ASCE
• SUNY Distinguished Professor Mark Swihart, Ph.D., F.AIChE, F.AAAS
• SUNY Distinguished Professor Krishna Rajan, ScD, F.ASM

Professor Whittaker is a civil engineer; Professor Swihart is a chemical engineer; and Professor
Rajan is a materials scientist.

 3. Results and Discussion
3.1 Introduction

The entire scope of the project was the purchase of two pieces of equipment and their
deployment in existing laboratories in the School of Engineering and Applied Science (SEAS) at
UB. One piece of equipment was deployed in the Department of Chemical and Biological
Engineering (CBE) under the leadership of then department chair, Professor Swihart. The other
piece of equipment was deployed in the Department of Materials, Design, and Innovation (MDI)
under the leadership of then department chair, Professor Rajan. Each deployment is briefly
described below.

No data and calculations were generated by the project. No assumptions were made. No
technical problems were encountered and no changes in direction were required. There was no
research and development, and no computer modeling was performed. The only problem
encountered in the project was the long lead time for the piece of equipment deployed in MDI.

Because the scope of the project involved the purchase of two pieces of equipment, tasks
numbers and milestones were not assigned. The target completion date for each purchase was as
soon as possible. Accordingly, task and milestone tables are not provided but delivery dates for
the CBE and MDI equipment are identified in Sections 3.2 and 3.3, respectively.

3.2 CBE deployment

The CBE component of the project was to acquire and deploy a piece of analytical equipment
that can simultaneously measure the adsorption of CO2 and H2O. Separation of hydrogen from
CO2 and H2O is a pervasive need in hydrogen production. The ability to monitor the
simultaneous, competitive uptake of both CO2 and H2O from mixtures with H2 is thus a key need
in hydrogen research. Thus, this acquisition strengthens research on hydrogen production,
especially blue hydrogen production process in which hydrogen is invariably mixed with CO2
and H2O. The Dynamic Vapor Sorption (DVS) Carbon CO2 and H2O gravimetric sorption
analyzer was delivered to UB in mid-2024 and installed shortly thereafter. Figure 3.1 is a
photograph of the analyzer installed in 322 Furnas Hall on the University at Buffalo, North
Campus, in late July 2024.

 Figure 3.1 DVS gravimetric sorption analyzer

The DVS sorption analyzer has been extensively used by three research groups at UB, including
those of Haiqing Lin and Miao Yu of the Department of Chemical and Biological Engineering
and Hyeongyun Cha of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering. The analyzer is critical for
measuring the uptake of CO2 in the presence of humidity.

3.3 MDI deployment

The MDI component of the project was to acquire and deploy specialized specimen-stage
equipment to enable in-situ mechanical deformation and temperature-controlled experiments
within an ultra-high resolution scanning electron microscope (SEM). This capability will
strengthen research on hydrogen–materials interactions at UB, particularly hydrogen
embrittlement and deformation behavior, and to provide shared experimental infrastructure for
researchers.

The components required for the construction and operation of the in-situ specimen stages were
delivered to UB late in CY 2025, including the deformation module, temperature control system,
and cryogenic stage. Photographs of the components are presented in Figure 3.2, taken in late
2025.

 a) deformation module b) temperature control system c) cryogenic stage

 Figure 3.2 Specimen-stage components
Assembly and functional testing of these components have demonstrated their mechanical
stability, electrical safety, and compatibility with SEM operations.

The deformation stage has been evaluated for mechanical alignment, load transmission, and
actuation reliability. The temperature control system has been configured, and calibration studies
of temperature measurement and regulation are well advanced. These studies are essential for
ensuring reliable operation across the temperature ranges relevant to hydrogen-related
deformation experiments. These efforts establish operational readiness of the specimen-stage
system prior to microscope installation.

 4. Key Achievements
The key achievements of the project were the deployment of two pieces of equipment in existing

As noted in Section 1, the two pieces of equipment will, in the near term, expand the research

industry infrastructure and the associated stable, good-paying jobs across the economic
spectrum.

The resulting growth of leading-edge hydrogen infrastructure in New York will help to position
UB, its academic and research partners, and Western New York industry at the forefront of the
energy transition and help meet state and federal energy targets.

 6. Lessons Learned
Because the entire scope of the project was the purchase of two pieces of equipment and their
deployment in existing UB laboratories, there are no lessons learned that would be useful to

 7. Conclusions
Because the entire scope of the project was the purchase of two pieces of equipment, and their
deployment in existing UB laboratories is recent, there are no scientific and technical findings to

purchasing the equipment.
