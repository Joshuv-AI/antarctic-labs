Ancient Bengal numismatic dataset of Vanga Janapada, Gauda, Samatata, and Harikela coinage
Authors: Ishtiak Al Mamoon, Ariful Islam, Wafial Hasnat Laisa, Abu Sufian, Golam Moula, Saikat Kumar Gain, Theophil Dango, Alif Ahmed, Saddat Kabir, Noman Nasir, AKM Muzahidul Islam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAncient Bengal coinage represents one of the significant pieces of evidence for understanding the political, economic, and cultural development of Eastern South Asia. The study of coin analysis remains challenging because many types are rare, visually complex, dispersed across private collections, and difficult to identify without any specialized numismatics knowledge. Even though interest in applying machine learning and computer vision to numismatics research is growing, the absence of well-structured datasets has limited the computation research on the early coinage of Bengal. This study presents a novel image dataset along with metadata annotations of ancient Bengal coins, which cover a broad historical period from the 5th century BC to the 10th century AD. The dataset includes 2704 annotated coin images across 52 classes of four major numismatic traditions of Bengal: Vanga Janapada, Gauda, Harikela, and Samatata. Our collection captures the evolution of Bengal’s monetary system from punch-marked coinage to the regional gold and silver coinage of the post-Gupta and early medieval periods. The images were collected from authenticated private collections and five permission granted auction houses. To ensure reliable class identification, each coin was carefully examined and annotated using historical classification, inscription, motifs, symbols, variety, and coin types. Additionally, all coins were checked and confirmed using numismatic sources, including SK Bose and Noman Nasir's numismatic books and auction house records. The dataset contains a wide range of rare and historically significant collections that provide a valuable resource for machine learning-based coin collection, feature extraction, image retrieval, and archaeological research. Moreover, the combination of modern computational methods with the ancient Bengal numismatics dataset offers a standardized benchmark for future interdisciplinary research in archaeology, numismatics, history, cultural heritage, and artificial intelligence.Keywords: Numismatics, Ancient Bengal coin, Cultural heritage, Archaeology, Image processing, Computer vision

Background

Ancient Bengal consisted of present-day Bangladesh and the Indian state of West Bengal, which developed through a long process of political transmission, economic growth, and cultural heritage [1,2]. The strategic location of Bengal between the Ganges-Brahmaputra river system and the Bay of Bengal has enabled extensive inland and maritime trade, which makes the region an important centre of interaction between South Asia and South-east Asia [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Additionally, agriculture, fishing, river transport, and overseas trade became the foundation of the ancient Bengal economy. These geographical conditions strongly reflected the early coinage development through displaying motifs such as boats, fish, birds, wheels, and solar symbols that represent the everyday life and economic activities of river-based communities [3]. The Bengal monetary history extended from the early punch-marked coinage of the Vanga Janapada periods to the gold and silver coinages of the post-Gupta and early mediaeval eras [[1], [2], [3]]. According to the S. K. Bose and Noman Nasir [3] proposed standard classification, the documented coinage of Ancient Bengal can be grouped into four principal numismatic traditions: Vanga Janapada, Gauda, Samatata, and Harikela. These four traditions represent the major monetary systems that developed across Bengal between the 5th century BC and the 10th century AD. Even though several rulers, local issues, and typological varieties exist under each tradition, they are treated as subdivisions of these four principal traditions [3]. Among the earliest known Vanga local coinages, punch-marked coins were discovered at Wari-Bateshwar (WB), Mahasthangarh (MGH), and Chandraketugarh (CKG). These coins are distinguished by different regional symbols such as boats, fish, birds, trees, wheels, and solar motifs, which reflect the ancient Bengal riverine economy, maritime trade, and agricultural foundations [3,5,6]. Wari-Bateshwar coins commonly represent four major symbols: the sun, a boat, a six-armed symbol and a fish or bird. Moreover, Mahasthangarh coins include some Mauryan features with local agricultural symbols such as ploughs, hooks, and trees. Chandraketugarh coins are mainly made of copper or billon and are categorized by three symbol compositions dominated by ship motifs, highlighting the region's strong maritime connections [3]. By following the decline of imperial authority in northern India, Bengal witnessed the rise of several regional diplomacies that include Gauda, Samatata, and Harikela [2,3,5,7,8]. Additionally, these kingdoms developed their own political identities, traditions, and monetary systems. Among them, Gauda emerged as a major political power during the period of King Shashanka (c. 603–637 CE) and produced some early gold coins, which were associated with an independent Bengali ruler [3,4]. After the fall of Shashanka, Jayanaga briefly occupied Karnasuvarna, the first capital of independent Bengal, and introduced his coinage. Even though there is a debate about the first independent Bengal ruler between Shashanka and Jayanaga, most historians claim that Shashanka was the first independent ruler of Bengal [3,4]. Moreover, between the 5th and 8th centuries, Samatata became an important centre of political and commercial activity in South Eastern Bengal and produced a series of gold coins influenced by Gupta and post-Gupta monetary traditions [3,5,6]. The Gupta Empire promoted classical Sanskritic culture and made trade and city life in the area more active [3]. After the fall of Gupta authority, local rulers began issuing independent coinage, starting with the anonymous type and later beginning to bear the name of regional rulers, including Samachar, Gopa, Varavandra, Bala, Prithubhata, Jayanaga, Sri Kumara, Sridharanaratha, Rajabhata and Manjusri [3,8]. This sequence demonstrates the gradual political and monetary evolution of Samatata, transitioning from external influence to regional independence [3,7,8]. Interestingly, few rulers, including Shashanka, Jayanaga and Samachar deva, appear in both the Gauda and Samatata coin traditions, which doesn’t indicate duplicate coinage rather reflects changing political control during the 7th century [3,8]. Additionally, historical evidence suggests that territorial expansion allowed different Gauda rulers to extend their influence into parts of Samatata for certain periods of time [3]. As a result, coins bearing the name of these particular rulers circulated in both regions, and Samatata continued to preserve many of its local monetary traditions [8]. Later on, between the seventh and tenth centuries, Harikela developed as a significant coastal kingdom and produced silver coinage across south-eastern Bengal, present Chittagong, Cumilla, southern Tripura, Sylhet, and parts of adjoining Arakan, which reflects the growing importance of maritime trade networks across the Bay of Bengal [3,7]. Additionally, Harikela coinage was developed following several typological stages, such as Candra dynasty issues, early Harikela coins, Akara ruler coins, later Harikela issues, city-state coinage, and thin bracteates coins [3,7]. Most of the coins carry a recumbent humped bull on the face and a tripartite symbol flanked by the sun and a crescent on the other. The design grew out of the bull-and-symbol coinage of the Candra rulers of Arakan and was taken up by successive communities along the coast and inland river routes [3]. Early heavy pieces with garlanded bulls and clear Brahmi legends represent one stylistic phase. Later strikes show lighter flans, retrograde or blundered lettering, and a much wider spread of die varieties. Named Akara rulers from the Chittagong region and bracteates hoards from southern Tripura add further layers to the series.

These kingdoms' coinage provides direct evidence of artistic development and regional interaction. Additionally, these coins were produced continuously and circulated widely, unlike literary sources, which makes them valuable primary historical records [3]. However, many coin types from ancient Bengal remain very rare, scattered across private collections, museums, auction archives and specialized numismatics publications [3,7]. As a result, their limited accessibility became constrained in both historical research and the development of computational approaches for coin identification and classification. With machine learning and computer vision advancements, new opportunities are created for the automated study of historical artefacts [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]], and several coin image datasets are developed for different historical regions' coin classification tasks, including Roman [14], Ghana [15], Indian [16,17], Gupta [18], and Bangladesh coins [19]. However, there is no publicly available documented dataset that comprehensively covers the major coin traditions of ancient Bengal, spanning the period from the 5th century BC to the 10th century AD. Addressing this gap, our present work introduces a well-structured and annotated coin image dataset, which consists of coin types from Vanga Janapada, Gauda, Harikela, and Samatata traditions. The dataset supports future research in archaeology, history, cultural heritage preservation, computer vision, and artificial intelligence through combining historical numismatic knowledge with modern data-driven methodologies.

Data Description

The dataset represents four major numismatic traditions of Ancient Bengal and covers a long historical period from the 5th century BC to the 10th century AD [3]. The collection contains a total of 2704 coin images along with their metadata annotation and is distributed across 52 classes under four principal groups: Vanga Janapada, Gauda, Harikela, and Samatata of Ancient Bengal. The dataset follows the standard numismatic classification of Ancient Bengal proposed by S K Bose and Noman Nasir [3]. Additionally, the dataset includes both early punch-marked coinage and later gold and silver coinage issued by different regional kingdoms of ancient Bengal.

The Vanga Janapada section represents the earliest local monetary traditions of Bengal that contain punch-marked coins from the major three distributive subsections: Wari-Bateshwar (WB), Mahasthangarh (MGH), and Chandraketugarh (CKG) [2,3,5]. These coins preserve the symbols associated with trade, agriculture, riverine transportation, and local political authority and become a part of the earliest monetary issues of the Bengal regions. Afterward, the Gauda section includes coinage from three major ruling periods: Samachar Deva, Jayanaga, and several varieties of Shashanka coinage. Additionally, Shashanka was one of the earliest independent rulers of Bengal [3]. Subsequently, the Harikela section represents the silver coinage traditions of southeastern Bengal, southern Tripura, and Sylhet and includes different phases of Harikela monetary development through covering six major typological series (Type A – Type F) of Harikela coinage [3,7]. The Samatata section contributes the largest component of the dataset and contains 37 classes, which is derived from the 22 major types of Samatata coinage [3,8]. Moreover, the Samatata coinage represents rare, precious, and rich gold coinage traditions of south-eastern Bengal during the post-Gupta and early medieval periods [3].

The complete dataset contains 52 classes, where Vanga Janapada contributes 3 classes, Gauda contributes 6 classes, Harikela contributes 6 classes, and Samatata contributes 37 classes. Their classes are specified based on the coin types, variety, sub-variety, and rulers. The Samatata sections originally consist of 22 major coin types with numerous sub-varieties [3]. Those sub-varieties represent only a limited number of the surviving specimen’s coin collection. To create a balanced and practical dataset for machine learning-based applications, we grouped closely related sub-varieties into unified classes. For example, under Samatata Type-1, multiple varieties such as SAM 1A.1 to SAM 1A.5 were merged into a single unified class, where they shared common iconographic and typological characteristics. This approach provides sufficient image representation for computational analysis and preserves the historical classification system [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]].

Several coin-image datasets, such as Roman [14], Ghana [15], Indian banknote currency [16], northeastern Indian [17], Gupta archer type [18], and Bangladesh [19], have been introduced for modern currency classification tasks. However, there is no publicly available documented dataset that comprehensively covers the major coin tradition of Ancient Bengal at this scale. The present study addresses this gap through combining rare and historically significant coin images from the multiple collections and numismatic sources into a unified resource for modern machine learning-based computation and archaeological research.

The coinage collection included in this dataset exhibits substantial variation in artistic style, inscription quality, preservation condition, and manufacturing techniques [20]. From the dataset some coins contain clear ruler legends and names, while others preserve only partial symbolic elements or inscriptions due to circulation wear and age-related deterioration. Moreover, this variability reflects the authentic characteristics of ancient coinage and provides a more realistic environment for archaeological research, numismatic heritage studies, and machine learning applications.

The coins are primarily composed of silver, copper, or mixed metal issues and are characterized by distinctive punch-marked symbols such as solar motifs, six-armed symbols, boats, birds, fish, trees, and geometric designs that represent the riverine economy and trade networks of early Bengal. Variations in surface wear, corrosion, mineral deposits, and patina developed during centuries of burial and circulation are common natural characteristics of ancient Bengal coins. Moreover, these features preserve the authenticity and historical integrity of the dataset rather than producing artificially enhanced specimens.

The coins of Chandraketugarh are particularly notable for their maritime imagery that includes boat and ship motifs, which reflects the importance of coastal trade in the lower Ganges Delta region. Subsequently, Mahasthangarh coins display both local and Mauryan influences and include symbols that are related to administrative and economic activities. Moreover, the coins from Wari-Bateshwar commonly contain symbols such as solar motifs, six-armed symbols, birds, boats, fish, ploughs, and other river-based associated symbols that reflect trade and agricultural activities. The obverse side commonly represents multiple punch-marked symbols that are arranged in different combinations, and the reverse side may contain additional symbols, banker marks, or countermarks or remain blank based on the specific coin type [[3], [4], [5], [6]].

Gauda coin dataset

The Gauda section represents the earliest independent coinage traditions of Bengal during the medieval period. This collection includes three rulers of ancient Bengal: Jayanaga, Samachar Deva, and Shashanka, who played important roles in political history during the fifth and sixth centuries CE [2,3]: Among these kings, Shashanka was widely renowned as the first independent ruler of Bengal. The Gauda sections include a total of 262 coin images distributed across six classes, where Jayanaga and Samachar Deva have one class each, and Shashanka coinage has four major varieties (A-D) of coins. Each of the varieties of Shashanka coinage was considered an individual class. Gauda coins are primarily gold and display a distinctive combination of royal, religious, and inscriptional elements. To separate the coins into different classes, variations in iconography, inscriptions, motifs, and symbols were used. Harikela coin dataset

The Harikela section represents one of the most important coinage traditions of southern Bengal between the seventh and tenth centuries CE [3,7]. and played an impactful role in the regional trade network connected to the Bay of Bengal. This part is organized into six major series categorized as Types A-F. Each of the states represents a different stage of the monetary development system. Additionally, they contain several catalog varieties based on the differences in their motif, inscription, and features [3]. The Harikela part contains 1088 annotated coin images, which are distributed among six different classes and 13 catalog varieties. Moreover, we stored each image in TIFF format and linked it with the corresponding annotation record through a unique identifier. Samatata coin dataset

The Samatata section represents the largest and most comprehensive component of the ancient Bengal coin collection, which covers a long historical period from the 5th to the 10th century AD [3,8]. Additionally, this section preserves the most diverse monetary traditions in ancient Bengal and covers several ruling families and political phases [2,[4], [5], [6]]. Moreover, these coins provide important evidence of royal authority, religious symbolism, artistic development, and regional trade networks. The Samatata collection contains 1213 annotated images across 22 types and is distributed among 37 classes, where Samatata types begin from Kushan copies and Gupta copies and follow a series of ruler-based types and sub varieties. Moreover, each of the sub-varieties represents very limited numbers of coins because of the rarity and historical availability.

The minor close sub-varieties were grouped into unified classes to create a practical dataset for machine learning-based applications and image classification tasks. Additionally, the group was formed based on the similar visual and typological characteristics of each coin. For example, several sub varieties, such as SAM 1A.1 to SAM 1A.5 under Type 1, represent similar visual characteristics and were merged into a single class. This approach ensures each class contains a sufficient number of images and preserves the historical classification system. Following this distribution, the original 22 types of Samatata coinage were organized within 37 classes. The Ancient Bengal coin dataset represents a naturally non-uniform class distribution, where some classes contain substantially more images than others, which makes a moderately imbalanced dataset. Overall class distribution of Ancient Bengal coin dataset.

This imbalance is mostly visible in Harikela and Samatata traditions. They particularly demonstrating a more imbalanced distribution, where many classes contain more than a hundred images and several rare classes contain around twenty images. Among the 52 classes of Ancient Bengal, the largest class is the Harikela Type-D with 278 images, which is basically the later Harikela series. In contrast, many Samatata and Gauda classes contain around 21–25 images.

This imbalance does not arise from the annotation procedure or sampling decisions; rather, it reflects actual historical distribution, minting pattern, ruling period, archaeological recovery, and existence of surviving coinage [3]. Additionally, the distribution also reflects the political history of ancient Bengal, where Harikela and Samatata had long-lasting kingdoms with a higher number of surviving coinages compared to short-lived rulers or transitional political periods [3]. For example, the Harikela Type-D, later the Harikela series, covers an extended period from the 8th century to the 10th century AD and produced a large number of coins [3,7].

On the other hand, multiple Samatata classes such as Vara Chandra, Sridharanaratha, Prithubhata, and the very late series are known for a limited number of rare surviving coin records [3,8]. Similarly, in the Gauda kingdom, Shashanka types B and C represent very rare varieties of Gauda coinage, which reflect the class distribution of this dataset [20].

During class distribution, extremely rare sub-varieties were merged into larger classes where they shared common typological and iconographic characteristics [20]. This strategy was particularly applied in the Samatata collection because it contains numerous rare varieties and sub-varieties that reduce severe class imbalance.

Researchers should consider the effect of class imbalance during the model development and apply strategies such as class weighting, data augmentation, oversampling in minority classes, and focal loss. This strategy may improve model performance on underrepresented classes. Additionally, a stratified train-validation-test split is recommended during evaluation to preserve the original class distribution. These approaches can produce more reliable and unbiased classification through maintaining the historical integrity of the dataset.

Materials and Methods

The Ancient Bengal coin images were produced using different minting techniques, metals, and artistic styles, and they show different variations in appearance. Moreover, among the collected images, several differences such as corrosion, wear, lighting conditions, and image backgrounds also created variations. Additionally, different sources of our data collection, such as private and five different auction houses, also created data variations with different resolutions. To ensure consistency and preserve the original characteristics of each coin, we used a dedicated preprocessing pipeline using Python with OpenCV, NumPy, and Pillow (PIL) libraries.

Data cleaning and preprocessing

During preprocessing, a 5 × 5 median filtering technique was used to denoise each of the images to remove small sensor noise, dust particles, and minor background artifacts. Additionally, it preserves important visual details, including inscriptions, symbols, decorative motifs, and coin boundaries. Compared to other smooth filtering techniques, median filtering effectively reduces isolated noise without affecting the structures, which is important for numismatic images. Moreover, more advanced enhancement techniques, including bilateral filtering, CLAHE, or deep learning-based restoration, were intentionally not applied because they may alter historical surface characteristics and reduce the authenticity of the original coin. Our objective was to preserve the original appearance while performing only minimal preprocessing for dataset standardization. After noise removal, we performed background normalization, where the coin was separated from the background using an automatic segmentation process and temporarily converted to grayscale images. To distinguish the foreground coin from the background, Otsu’s thresholding method was applied. The resulting binary mask was used to isolate the coin, and the background was replaced by a uniform white color (RGB: 255, 255, 255). This process preserves the original appearance of the coin and reduces the differences of varying lighting conditions, camera setups, and background objects. Moreover, morphological opening and closing operations were applied using a 3 × 3 structuring element, which removed small background artifacts, filled minor gaps, refined coin boundaries, and improved the image quality. Furthermore, all the images were resized using bicubic interpolation (INTER_CUBIC) to a standard resolution of 512 × 512 pixels. During resizing images, this interpolation method preserves fine surface details and produces smooth edge transitions. Finally, the processed images were saved in TIFF format that preserves image quality without compression artifacts. It is important to clarify that no additional enhancement techniques such as color correction, illumination normalization, synthetic image generation, or data augmentation were applied during preprocessing. All the preprocessing operations were performed in a standardized computing environment using Python 3.12, OpenCV 4.10, NumPy 2.1, and Pillow (PIL) 11.0.

This preprocessing workflow effectively addresses the unique challenges of ancient Bengal coin images. The combination of noise reduction, automatic background subtraction, contour refinement, and image standardization improves visual consistency and preserves important numismatic features such as inscriptions, symbols, decorative motifs, and surface textures.

Feature extraction and segmentation procedure

Ancient Bengal coins include several important features such as inscriptions, motifs, structural patterns, and symbols. A feature extraction and segmentation workflow was used to visually represent the important features of different Ancient Bengal coins. This workflow was implemented using Python 3.12 and OpenCV 4.10 for image processing and, for deep feature extraction, PyTorch 2.2 with Torch Vision. The representative feature extraction results are demonstrated in Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5,Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Fig. 9,Fig. 11, Fig. 12, Fig. 13, and Fig. 16, Fig. 17, Fig. 18, Fig. 19, Fig. 20, Fig. 21. Initially, the coin region was detected using the Hough circle transform. Each image was converted to a grayscale image and filtered using a median filter to reduce high-frequency noise. The parameters of the detector were experimentally optimized for this dataset (dp = 1.3, minDist = 120, param1 = 60, param2 = 35, minRadius = 45, and maxRadius = 220) that provides reliable coin detection across different image sizes and preservation conditions. Moreover, some of the ancient coins have worn edges, corrosion, or incomplete circular boundaries. In these cases, counter-based segmentation was used as an alternative approach. Additionally, to identify the largest foreground region to the corresponding coin, we applied adaptive thresholding followed by connected component analysis that produced more reliable segmentation for damaged and irregular specimens. After background removal, the coin edges were enhanced using the Canny edge detector with a lower threshold value of 60 and an upper threshold value of 160. Additionally, a minimum connected-component area of approximately 150–250 pixels was used to remove small noisy regions. This process retained meaningful inscriptions, symbols, and decorative motifs for further analysis.

Furthermore, deep feature extraction was implemented using the ResNet-50 [21] model (Feature_Ex_part 3.py), which is pre-trained on the ImageNet-1k dataset [22]. Following that, the final classification layer was removed, and the output of the global average pooling layer was used to generate a 2048-dimensional feature vector for each coin image. Before the feature extraction process, all the images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels and normalized using the standard ImageNet mean and standard deviation. The complete feature extraction was designed as a modular pipeline that makes it easy to reproduce and extend future research. Additionally, all the feature extraction scripts (Feature_Ex_part 1.py, Feature_Ex_part 2.py, and Feature_Ex_part 3.py) are included in the supplementary materials.

Throughout the manuscript representative examples from all four coin traditions of Ancient Bengal are presented to illustrate the preprocessing, segmentation, and feature extraction workflow under different preservation conditions. This approach supports the reproducibility and future reuse of the dataset.

This generation workflow ensures every included image passes through a consistent sequence of verification, processing, annotation, validation, and repository organization. Limitations

The Ancient Bengal dataset covers major traditions including Vanga Janapada, Gauda, Harikela, and Samatata. Since, many ancient coin types from those traditions are extremely rare and have limited number surviving records, some of the classes contain fewer images compare to others. Additionally, the dataset reflects the historical availability of surviving records rather than equal class distribution across all classes. Moreover, natural variations were introduced due to the differences in preservation, wear, corrosion, and strike quality. Although, the dataset provides an organized resource for archeological and machine learning research, it doesn’t cover every known variety of Ancient Bengal coinage. Therefore, researcher should consider these limitations whenever using the dataset for broader numismatic studies.
