 Abstract
 This report documents the validation of various 235 U prompt fission neutron spectrum (PFNS)
 evaluations with recently measured PFUNS reaction rates. PFUNS is an integral experiment cam-
 paign conducted at Los Alamos National Laboratory’s National Criticality Experiments Research
 Center (NCERC). This campaign focused on measuring various reaction rates using activation foils
 in a 235 U critical assembly with the explicit aim to reduce uncertainties in the 235 U PFNS above
 outgoing neutron energies of 8 MeV. When one simulates these reaction rates with various evalu-
 ated 235 U PFNS and compares to experimental values, it becomes evident that a harder spectrum
 with increasing outgoing neutron energy would lead to better experiment-simulation agreement.
 In addition to that, the simulated 92 Mo(n,p) and 93 Nb(n,2n) reaction rates differ distinctly and
 more strongly from experimental data than any of the other reaction rates. This outlying trend
 could either point to the experimental data or nuclear data of the studied reaction rates being
 biased.

1 Introduction
This report is in answer to the FY26 Nuclear Criticality Safety Program (NCSP) milestone: “LANL
will be assessing PFUNS data in the context of the current U-235 evaluated PFNS.”
 PFUNS is an NCSP-supported critical assembly experiment performed at NCERC using the
Planet [1] critical assembly machine with highly enriched uranium (HEU) metal hemishells (108 kg
critical mass) [2, 3, 4]. The experiment aims to reduce uncertainties in the 235 U prompt fission neutron
spectrum (PFNS), particularly above 8 MeV outgoing neutron energy. At these energies, differential
measurements supporting the PFNS evaluations are very scarce and highly uncertain due to the fact
that the PFNS falls off many orders of magnitude [5, 6, 7] from its peak to 20 MeV leading to a low
outgoing neutron flux. On the contrary, critical experiments have a very high neutron flux from the
assembly. Hence, the PFUNS work complements earlier differential measurements at LANSCE by the
Chi-Nu and CEA teams [8, 9].
 Conducted in 2024, PFUNS involved four critical configurations and two irradiations: a short 2-
hour irradiation focusing on fission foils and short-lived activation foils, and a long 12-hour irradiation
targeting activation foils with high thresholds and long half-lives. The activation foils were strategically
placed in a central void region. The reactions were chosen to ensure coverage of different threshold
energies for subsequent studies of specific energy regions of the PFNS.
 In this report, we simulate various PFUNS reaction rates with six different 235 U PFNS evaluations
and compare to experimental data. This comparison allows us to gain an understanding of the high
outgoing neutron energy tail of the PFNS. Section 2 describes and shows the various nuclear data eval-
uations that were investigated. The simulated results are compared to experimental ones in Section 3.
A summary and outlook to future work is given in Section 4.

2 Evaluations studied
The impact of six different PFNS evaluations was studied on simulated PFUNS reaction rate ratios:

  2. An evaluation with CEA [9], Chi-Nu [8] and historical experimental PFNS data specified in [10]
 (“Evaluated with All Data”).
  3. An evaluation with CEA until 5.5 MeV incident neutron energy, Chi-Nu and historical data

  4. An evaluation with CEA until 5.5 MeV incident neutron energy and historical data (“Historical

These different evaluations are shown in Figs. 1–3 in comparison to differential experimental data used
as input for the evaluation. For the rest of this analysis, we follow the nomenclature in the legend of
these plots.
 All studied evaluations above thermal incident neutron energies started from the same prior infor-
mation described in Ref. [10] and were executed with generalized least squares. The only difference is
which experimental data were used for the evaluation.
 At thermal for ENDF/B-VIII.1, PFNS are adopted from an IAEA evaluation that was only based
on differential experimental PFNS and spectrum averaged cross sections. These spectrum averaged
cross sections were used to inform the high-outgoing neutron energy tail (above 8 MeV) similarly to
our use of PFUNS experiments here. Contrary to this study, the spectrum-averaged cross sections
inform PFNS at thermal incident neutron energies, while the PFUNS spectrum would inform PFNS
for incident neutron energies of 0.5–5 MeV with the peak of the criticality spectrum being around
0.5–1 MeV (with a mean of 2 MeV). It is noteworthy that the ENDF/B-VIII.1 PFNS at thermal
incident neutron energies increases significantly above 8 MeV due to the usage of spectrum-averaged
cross section (see Fig. 1 top left), while all other evaluated PFNS have a clear downward trend. This
clear downward trend stems largely from the Los Alamos model [13]; differential experimental data in
this energy range are highly uncertain and prone to bias (neutron and fission detector responses are
difficult to quantify, competing backgrounds can lead to issues [14, 15, 5]). However, the Los Alamos
model is not a first-principle model. Approximations are made (like assuming one average fission
fragment pair that emits neutrons rather than all fission yields) that could very well impact the PFNS
predicted at high outgoing neutron energies. Therefore, PFUNS data are of high importance to clarify
if the trend of the thermal ENDF/B-VIII.1 PFNS is corroborated by integral data at higher incident
neutron energies or not.
 For 0.5–5 MeV incident neutron energies, most of the evaluations are close together with the
exception of the evaluation “CEA only”. This evaluation does not at all follow experimental data
above 2 MeV outgoing neutron energies in Figs. 1 and 2. It does not even follow the only experimental
data set it was based on (CEA experimental data). This evaluation is biased by tension between a
strongly correlated prior and experimental data. It is clearly unphysical. However, because most other
evaluations are close together, we decided to take into account the biased “CEA only” evaluation to
test if this drastic difference in evaluated PFNS would equally bias simulated PFUNS reaction rates.
If this is not the case, PFUNS reaction rates are an insufficient diagnostic of evaluated 235 U PFNS.
Also, it let’s one explore whether an increase of the PFNS at high outgoing neutron energies would be
beneficial or not.
 One other issue that should be noted is that the most recent and very comprehensive CEA and
Chi-Nu differential experimental data do not agree very well at high outgoing neutron energies. CEA
trends lower on average than Chi-Nu PFNS at high outgoing neutron energies. This difference is
noteworthy because both measurements used the same neutron detector equipment in the same exper-
iment location; there was even overlap in team members. This systematic difference was attributed in
Ref. [16] to a different approach for obtaining the neutron detector response (measurement relative to
252
 Cf(sf) PFNS for CEA data versus the ratio-to-ratio method for Chi-Nu data). This lack of clarity on
the true PFNS at high outgoing neutron energies points again for the need of the PFUNS experiment.
 The mean energy computed from all the different PFNS is shown in Fig. 4. Most PFNS have similar
mean energies until 5 MeV incident neutron energies (the range of interest for PFUNS) except for the
evaluation “CEA only”. This trend was expected as the evaluated PFNS are close as well except for
the “CEA only” evaluation.

3 Validation results and discussion
Validation of the various PFNS with the PFUNS reaction rates is shown in Fig. 5. To this end,
the new evaluated PFNS values were cut into ENDF/B-VIII.1 files from thermal to 20 MeV incident
neutron energies. All these files were incorporated into ENDF/B-VIII.1 in ENDF-6 format and then
processed into ACE format with NJOY [17]. Many of the ENDF/B-VIII.1 dosimetry reactions were
imported from IRDFF-II [18]. The reaction rate values shown in Fig. 5 were calculated otherwise with
ENDF/B-VIII.1 using MCNP-6.3 [19].
 In Fig. 5, the C/E values are shown—that is the calculated, C, over experimental, E, values—for
the PFUNS reaction rates. These C/E values are ordered along the horizontal axes keeping in mind
their mean energy (E50%) of the integral of the PFNS and reaction. That means reactions closer to
the left of the plot have lower E50% than those on the right. Exact E50% were not calculated but
estimated by looking at plots and literature as this should only assess a qualitative tendency of C/E
value with increasing outgoing neutron energy of the PFNS.
 First of all, it should be mentioned that PFUNS has a leakage spectrum with a peak in the 0.5–1
MeV region with a mean around 2 MeV. Due to that, PFNS of 0.5–2 MeV incident neutron energy
will influence the simulation the most.
 Second, it is evident that there is a clear trend with respect to outgoing neutron energy. The larger
the E50% is (and the more we only query the high-energy part of the PFNS) the more strongly C/E
falls off. That indicates that it would be beneficial if the PFNS has a larger contribution above 8 MeV
than it has in ENDF/B-VIII.1 and all evaluations other than “CEA only”.
 The “CEA only” evaluation, which has an unphysically high PFNS at high outgoing neutron
energies, is the only one with C/E values larger than 1 on average. While this evaluation is by no
means correct, it indicates that “Eval. with All Data” should ideally have a higher tail at high outgoing
neutron energies than it currently has when comparing C/E values of this and other evaluations with
increasing E50% of the reaction rates. For this evaluation, there is a systematic trend in as far as the
C/E becomes larger with increasing E50% of the reaction. Starting with the 48 Ti(n,p) reaction rate,
the bias between C and E becomes distinctly larger than the experimental uncertainties. Therefore,
validation with the “CEA only” evaluation showcases that C/E validation with PFUNS reaction rates
can correctly identify biased PFNS.

associated with it. This evaluation has a somewhat higher tail compared to most other reasonable

 It is noteworthy that PFUNS supports differential data having a trend to higher PFNS values
above 8 MeV outgoing neutron energies, because this also gives an indication whether one should
trust more the trend of Chi-Nu or CEA experimental data. CEA experimental data would trend to
lower values at high outgoing neutron energies, Chi-Nu to higher ones. PFUNS seems more in line
with Chi-Nu data which the ENDF/B-VIII.1 evaluation was largely based on above 0.5 MeV incident
neutron energy. Still the evaluated PFNS tails are currently not high enough to give good agreement
with PFUNS experiments. However, one should investigate if that is truly only caused by PFNS or if
the fission cross section or, inelastic and/ or elastic reactions could also play a role.
 Finally, there are two reaction rates that are strongly outlying: 92 Mo(n,p) has a C/E value of 1.5,
which is far outside its 1-σ bound and does not fit within the trend of other reaction rates with similar
E50%. Also, 93 Nb(n,2n) has a suspiciously high C/E value far outside any trend seen in other reaction
rates. For both reaction rates, the experimental and evaluated data of the reaction in question should
be investigated.

4 Summary and outlook
This report documents that we completed the FY26 Nuclear Criticality Safety Program (NCSP) mile-
stone: “LANL will be assessing PFUNS data in the context of the current U-235 evaluated PFNS.”
Here, we validated six different evaluated 235 U PFNS with respect to PFUNS reaction rates. This
validation demonstrated that PFUNS data are accurate enough that they can correctly identify biased
PFNS evaluations. Furthermore, validation with PFUNS data indicates that the high-outgoing neu-
tron energy tail of the ENDF/B-VIII.1 evaluation is too low and should be increased. This conclusion,
however, only holds if it is shown that the PFUNS reaction rates are mostly sensitive to PFNS and
the studied reaction cross section. If the PFUNS reaction rates are also significantly sensitive to other
nuclear data (e.g., elastic and/ or inelastic cross sections and angular distributions, or the fission cross
section), then one would need to investigate if changes in these observables are also necessary before
attributing the poor C/E with increasing threshold of the reaction to the PFNS only.
 Unfortunately, we do not currently have a model that supports a high tail at high outgoing neutron
energies. Also, differential experimental data do not reach far enough to correct the high-energy tail.
Adjustment of the PFNS to PFUNS reaction rates would be one way forward to include the knowledge
gained through PFUNS reaction rates on the PFNS. For this adjustment, it would be necessary to
have sensitivities of the reaction rates to evaluated PFNS. As part of the PARADIGM LDRD-DR
project, Brain et al. [20] have demonstrated that the FSEN MCNP capability can be used to compute
these sensitivities. Hence, there would be a path forward for this adjustment, but the computation of
sensitivities and code development for this adjustment would be necessary.
 It would also be necessary to investigate if the PFNS is the only observable these reaction rates
are mostly sensitive to. It is conceivable that not only the PFNS has a high sensitivity, but also that
there is a non-negligible sensitivity of the PFUNS reaction rates to the fission, inelastic and elastic
cross sections or angular distributions of the latter two. If that is the case, then one would need to
adjust all these nuclear data at once.
 Finally, it should be noted that 92 Mo(n,p) and 93 Nb(n,2n) reaction rates had suspiciously high
C/E values compared to other reaction rates. These reaction rates should be further investigated for
potential biases in their experimental or evaluated nuclear data.
