Enhancing Spectral Embedding through Robust and Flexible Knowledge Transfer in Electronic Health Records
Feiqing Huang, Zongqi Xia, Rong Ma, Tianxi Cai

Abstract
We propose a spectral-based, unsupervised representation learning framework to derive low-dimensional embeddings for clinical concepts and patients in rare disease cohorts from electronic health records, where data are high-dimensional but sample sizes are limited. To overcome this challenge, we incorporate a knowledge matrix extracted from a broader population that shares a partially overlapping subspace with the rare-disease cohort. Our method departs from existing approaches by relaxing restrictive one-to-one signal-alignment assumptions between the latent data matrix and knowledge matrix, allowing more flexible and realistic forms of structured sharing. We introduce a novel two-step spectral embedding procedure: first, we identify and remove irrelevant components from the knowledge matrix; then, we apply a projection-based method to separately recover shared and heterogeneous components. Simulations and an analysis of a real-world multiple sclerosis cohort show that the proposed method outperforms competing approaches, particularly in challenging scenarios where shared signals are weak and only partially aligned, as is common in rare-disease data.



Keywords:
transfer learning, unsupervised representation learning, spectral methods, auxiliary knowledge, electronic health records

Introduction

Rare diseases collectively affect millions of people worldwide, yet they are often difficult to study because individual conditions are uncommon and clinically heterogeneous. Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a representative example: although its global prevalence is only about 35.9 per 100,000 individuals
(
37
)
, it remains a major focus of clinical research because patients vary widely in symptoms, disease severity, and care patterns. This combination of low prevalence and substantial heterogeneity makes MS difficult to study systematically, as researchers must draw insight from limited cohorts while accounting for between-patient variation.

Electronic Health Records (EHRs) provide a rich and longitudinal repository of clinical data, capturing patient diagnoses, treatments, laboratory results, and clinical notes. This wealth of information has supported unsupervised representation learning methods that estimate low-dimensional embeddings without labels, enabling tasks such as patient profiling
(
23
;
21
)
and critical event identification
(
38
)
. However, the inherently limited sample size of rare disease cohorts presents a major challenge for unsupervised representation learning methods, which typically require large datasets to learn robust representations. General biomedical knowledge graphs offer a potential solution by providing external information to enrich learning from scarce data. Yet, these graphs are often constructed with a focus on common diseases, which can lead to misalignment or irrelevant associations when directly applied to rare disease populations.

To state our problem, let
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
denote the latent data matrix underlying EHR observations, where
n
is the number of patients and
p
is the number of clinical concepts (diagnoses, medications, procedures, laboratory tests, and related records). Because clinical concepts are often disease-specific and tend to co-occur in meaningful concept clusters and patient subgroups
(
35
)
, we model
\boldsymbol{X}
as low-rank. In practice, we observe a noisy version
\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}=\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{Z}
, where
\boldsymbol{Z}
captures measurement noise and nuisance variation. Entries in
\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}
can be binary indicators, code counts, or continuous laboratory values. Our goal is to learn low-dimensional embeddings for both clinical concepts and patients in rare disease cohorts, where
n
can be much smaller than
p
.
In many cases, prior knowledge about relationships among clinical concepts, derived from broader populations or external cohorts, is available and encoded as a reference concept embedding matrix
\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
with embedding dimension
q
different from
p
; we refer to this as the knowledge matrix. We expect
\boldsymbol{W}
to partially overlap with the latent concept structure in
\boldsymbol{X}
. Therefore, the central challenge is how to leverage
\boldsymbol{W}
to improve learning of the low-rank structure of
\boldsymbol{X}
.

Numerous methods have been proposed to learn low-rank representations of
\boldsymbol{X}
with auxiliary knowledge. They can be broadly grouped into three families: spectral, manifold-based, and deep methods (Section 1.1). In the rare-disease setting, with limited sample size, high dimensionality, and strong interpretability requirements, we center our analysis on spectral methods and assume the latent data and auxiliary knowledge share partially overlapping subspaces. Within this setting, existing methods, including JIVE
(
25
)
, AJIVE
(
16
)
, CJIVE
(
30
)
, and others
(
26
)
, face two key limitations. First, many existing spectral transfer methods assume
one-to-one signal alignment
: each principal component (PC) direction in
\boldsymbol{X}
perfectly aligns with one PC direction in
\boldsymbol{W}
; this yields the diagonal pattern in Figure
1
(a). This assumption is often overly restrictive in real-world clinical data, where a PC direction of
\boldsymbol{X}
aligns with multiple directions across
\boldsymbol{W}
and
\boldsymbol{W}_{\perp}
(mixed alignment; Figure
1
(b)).

Second, existing methods typically rely on sufficiently strong shared signals between
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{W}
and provide limited safeguards against
negative transfer
, meaning that incorporating external knowledge leads to worse embedding estimation than using
\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}
alone (e.g., PCA). In rare-disease applications, clinically meaningful shared components are often subtle and can be overwhelmed by stronger heterogeneous variation (e.g., demographics or common comorbidities)
(
42
)
, which makes transfer unstable. Although
24

through subspace-distance minimization, it does not explicitly address this weak-shared-signal negative-transfer regime. The main contributions of this work can be summarized as follows:

•

Flexible transfer beyond one-to-one alignment.

We relax the restrictive one-to-one signal-alignment assumption underlying many existing knowledge-transfer methods and develop a general subspace-based transfer framework. The framework is built on subspace-distance characterization and a newly introduced nonseparability parameter, which jointly capture mixed cross-component correspondence between
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{W}
and support a more refined theoretical analysis.

•

A two-step design to mitigate negative transfer under weak shared signal.

We introduce a two-step estimator tailored to rare-disease settings where shared signal is weak relative to strong heterogeneous variation: Step I preprocesses the raw knowledge matrix to remove non-transferable directions, and Step II estimates shared and heterogeneous components through projection onto
\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{W})
and
\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\perp})
, respectively. This design improves recovery of weak shared structure and provides robustness against negative transfer where direct transfer is unstable (Figure
2
).

•

Non-asymptotic guarantees for concept and patient embeddings.

We provide rigorous theory for both preprocessing and estimation, including consistency of the preprocessing step and non-asymptotic error bounds for concept and patient embeddings. In particular, Theorem
4.4
shows that informative concept-level transfer improves not only concept embedding but also patient embedding estimation.

To establish these results, we develop technical tools that align with the three contributions above. We formalize mixed sharing through subspace-distance and nonseparability quantities, characterize when preprocessing avoids negative transfer, and derive non-asymptotic guarantees for both concept and patient embeddings. In particular, we establish deterministic upper and lower bounds that track strong and weak signal components individually, rather than analyzing only their aggregate linear span as in prior work
(
24
;
26
)
. This yields a sharper and more interpretable decomposition of subspace estimation error into deterministic bias and stochastic fluctuation. See Supplementary Section S1 for details.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 1.1 reviews related work. Section 2 introduces the model setup and geometric quantities for knowledge transfer. Section 3 presents the proposed two-step SENT procedure, including knowledge preprocessing and embedding estimation. Section 4 establishes deterministic and statistical guarantees for concept and patient embeddings. Sections 5 and 6 report simulation and real-data results, respectively. Section 7 concludes with a discussion.

Related work

Unsupervised representation learning methods can be broadly grouped into three families: spectral methods (e.g., PCA and factor models)
(
4
;
14
)
, manifold-based methods (e.g., tSNE and UMAP)
(
27
;
29
)
, and deep representation methods (e.g., generative and contrastive approaches)
(
28
;
8
)
. In this paper, our focus is the rare-disease regime with limited sample size, high dimensionality, and a strong need for interpretability. In such settings, manifold and deep methods can be less stable and harder to interpret geometrically, especially when sample size is small
(
3
;
9
)
. We therefore focus on spectral approaches, which provide a transparent and statistically tractable foundation for embedding estimation
(
2
)
.

Given this spectral focus, methods that incorporate auxiliary knowledge can be grouped into four classes: linear subspace alignment
(
25
;
16
;
26
)
, manifold-geometry transfer
(
12
;
20
)
, distributional alignment
(
33
;
11
)
, and deep transfer methods such as adversarial or discrepancy-based alignment
(

)
. These approaches provide useful transfer mechanisms when geometric and distributional assumptions are matched across domains. However, in clinical transfer settings with substantial cohort heterogeneity, manifold correspondence may be unstable, distributional alignment can be sensitive to covariate shift, and deep transfer methods often require larger sample sizes in both source and target domains. For these reasons, subspace-based spectral transfer methods are the most directly relevant comparison class for our problem.

Matrix Denoising Model with Subspace Knowledge

2.1
Model setup

In this subsection, we introduce the matrix denoising model, define alignment between auxiliary knowledge and the latent data subspace via principal angles, and use this alignment to decompose the latent subspace into a shared (knowledge-driven) component and a heterogeneous (purely data-driven) component.

Suppose we observe
\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
, generated from a rank-
r
latent data matrix
\boldsymbol{X}
plus perturbation
\boldsymbol{Z}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}=\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{Z},\quad\text{where}\quad\boldsymbol{X}\overset{\mathrm{SVD}}{=}\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{F}^{\top}.

(2.1)

Here
\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathcal{O}_{p,r}
and
\boldsymbol{V}\in\mathcal{O}_{n,r}
are the left and right singular vector matrices, and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\diag(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{r}>0
. For EHR applications, we interpret
\boldsymbol{U}
as latent concept embeddings and
\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1/2}
as patient embeddings.

Let
\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathcal{O}_{p,r_{W}}
be a transferable knowledge matrix, where
r_{W}\leq r
. The integer
r_{W}
is the transferable knowledge rank (estimated in Section 3), and in Section 2 we treat
\boldsymbol{W}
as the ideal informative knowledge matrix. To characterize how
\boldsymbol{W}
aligns with the latent subspace
\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{U})
, consider the SVD of
\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{W}
:

\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{W}=[\underset{r\times r_{W}}{\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}}\ \underset{r\times(r-r_{W})}{\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}}]\begin{bmatrix}\diag(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{r_{W}})\\
\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{O}^{\top},

(2.2)

where
[\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\ \boldsymbol{Q}_{2}]\in\mathcal{O}_{r}
,
\boldsymbol{O}\in\mathcal{O}_{r_{W}}
, and
1\geq\sigma_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{r_{W}}>0
.
Define the principal angles by

\displaystyle\theta_{j}:=\arccos(\sigma_{j}),\quad 1\leq j\leq r_{W}.

(2.3)

Smaller
\theta_{j}
indicates stronger alignment between the knowledge subspace and the latent data subspace. The matrix
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}
captures directions in
\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{U})
shared with
\boldsymbol{W}
, while
\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}
captures heterogeneous directions orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{W}
.

The angles
\theta_{j}
are uniquely defined. For fixed
\boldsymbol{U}
,
\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}
is identifiable up to right multiplication by an orthogonal matrix in
\mathcal{O}_{r-r_{W}}
. The identifiability of
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}
depends on multiplicities of
\sigma_{j}
: it is unique up to sign when the
\sigma_{j}
are distinct, and up to blockwise orthogonal rotation within groups of repeated singular values otherwise.

This decomposition gives the shared-versus-heterogeneous split used by our estimator in Section
3
and by the nonseparability analysis in Section
2.2
.

2.2
Subspace distance, nonseparability, and flexible sharing

To formalize flexible knowledge transfer and support later theory, we introduce subspace-distance and nonseparability quantities. For orthonormal basis matrices
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathcal{O}_{p,q}
and
\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathcal{O}_{p,k}
with principal angles
\phi_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\phi_{q\wedge k}
and
q\wedge k=\min(q,k)
, define

\displaystyle\|\sin\Theta(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B})\|:=\max_{1\leq j\leq q\wedge k}\sin\phi_{j},\qquad\|\sin\Theta(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B})\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}:=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{q\wedge k}\sin^{2}\phi_{j}\Big)^{1/2}.

(2.4)

Applying this to
\boldsymbol{U}
and
\boldsymbol{W}
, we define

\displaystyle\gamma:=\|\sin\Theta(\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{W})\|=\sin\theta_{r_{W}},\qquad\gamma_{{\mathrm{F}}}:=\|\sin\Theta(\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{W})\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{r_{W}}\sin^{2}\theta_{j}\Big)^{1/2},

(2.5)

where
\theta_{j}
are the principal angles in (
2.3
) and
\theta_{r_{W}}
is the largest.

Let
\mathcal{U}=\text{span}(\boldsymbol{U})
and
\mathcal{W}=\text{span}(\boldsymbol{W})
. Since
\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}
, rotating the column vectors of
\boldsymbol{U}
by
[\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\ \boldsymbol{Q}_{2}]
from (
2.2
) gives

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}=\underbrace{[\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\ \boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}]}_{\text{rotated eigenbasis}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}&\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}\\
(\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Q}_{2})^{\top}&\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{rotated eigenvalue blocks}}[\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\ \boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}]^{\top}.

Geometrically, this decomposition splits
\mathcal{U}
into two orthogonal components:
\text{span}(\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{Q}_{1})
, which overlaps with
\mathcal{W}
, and
\text{span}(\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{Q}_{2})
, which is orthogonal to
\mathcal{W}
.
The middle block matrix shows how signal is allocated across the two components: the diagonal blocks
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}
describe signal variation within each component, while the off-diagonal block
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}
measures how strongly the two components are mixed.

If the two components are not mixed,
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}=\boldsymbol{0}
and we then have
perfect separation
. The one-to-one alignment setting underlies many multi-matrix learning approaches, e.g., JIVE
(
25
)
, is perfectly separated (see Example
1
).

However, this setting is often unrealistic. In practice,
\mathcal{U}
typically overlaps with both
\mathcal{W}
and its orthogonal subspace, leading to the mixed alignment setting where
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
(see Example
2
). To quantify this, we define the signal
nonseparability
parameter

\delta:=\|\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}\|.

(2.6)

Larger
\delta
indicates stronger mixing between the shared and heterogeneous components. When all eigenvalues are identical,
\delta=0
because
\boldsymbol{X}
can be represented using
[\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{Q}_{1},\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}]
without changing
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, which is effectively another perfectly separated case. When eigenvalues differ,
\delta
is generally nonzero and captures the extent of cross-component mixing.

Example 1

(One-to-one alignment)
.

Consider
\boldsymbol{W}=[\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{w}_{r_{W}}]\in\mathcal{O}_{p,r_{W}}
and
\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1/2}
, where
\boldsymbol{U}=[\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{u}_{r}]\in\mathcal{O}_{p,r}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\textup{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r})
with distinct eigenvalues. Suppose each
\boldsymbol{w}_{j}
forms a small angle
\theta_{j}
with
\boldsymbol{u}_{j}
and is orthogonal to all
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
for
i\neq j
. Then

\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}=[\boldsymbol{I}_{r_{W}},\boldsymbol{0}_{r_{W},r-r_{W}}]^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}=[\boldsymbol{0}_{r-r_{W},r_{W}},\boldsymbol{O}_{r-r_{W}}]^{\top}
,
where
\boldsymbol{O}_{r-r_{W}}\in\mathcal{O}_{r-r_{W}}
. Hence
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}=\boldsymbol{0}
, so
\delta=0
.

Example 2

(Mixed alignment)
.

Consider
r=2
and
r_{W}=1
. Write
\boldsymbol{W}=[\boldsymbol{w}]
with
\|\boldsymbol{w}\|=1
, and let
\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1/2}
, where
\boldsymbol{U}=[\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2}]\in\mathcal{O}_{p,2}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\textup{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
with
0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}
. Since
r_{W}=r-r_{W}=1
, write
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}=[\boldsymbol{q}_{1}]
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}=[\boldsymbol{q}_{2}]
for unit vectors
\boldsymbol{q}_{1},\boldsymbol{q}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let
\boldsymbol{u}_{\perp}
be a unit vector orthogonal to
\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{U})
, and set
\boldsymbol{w}=\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}\big(\gamma\boldsymbol{u}_{1}+\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\big)+\gamma\boldsymbol{u}_{\perp}
for
0<\gamma<0.5
. Then
\|\sin\Theta(\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{W})\|=\gamma
. Moreover, the normalized projection of
\boldsymbol{w}
onto
\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{U})
is
\gamma\boldsymbol{u}_{1}+\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
, so
\boldsymbol{q}_{1}=[\gamma,\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}]^{\top}
,
\boldsymbol{q}_{2}=[-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}},\gamma]^{\top}
, and hence
\delta=\|\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}\|=|\boldsymbol{q}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{q}_{2}|=\gamma\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})>0
.

As shown in Section 4, the subspace-distance quantities
\gamma
and
\gamma_{{\mathrm{F}}}
, together with the nonseparability parameter
\delta
, are central to characterizing the relationship between the latent data and auxiliary knowledge. They appear explicitly in both upper and lower bounds for embedding estimation error (see Theorem 4.1).

Section 2 provides the geometric setup under a transferable knowledge matrix
\boldsymbol{W}
. In practice, we start from a raw matrix
\boldsymbol{W}_{0}
, which may combine transferable and non-transferable directions. Section 3 introduces a preprocessing step to construct a transferable matrix from
\boldsymbol{W}_{0}
and gives its sample algorithm. Section 4 then develops theory using Section 2 quantities for the transferable knowledge matrix
\boldsymbol{W}
constructed in Section 3.

3.1
Step I: Knowledge preprocessing

Section 2 introduced the population notion of transferable knowledge. In Step I, we map a raw knowledge matrix
\boldsymbol{W}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}

\boldsymbol{W}
and its rank
r_{W}

\boldsymbol{W}_{0}

We compute the SVD of
\boldsymbol{W}_{0}

\tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}\in\mathcal{O}_{p,d}

[\boldsymbol{o}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{o}_{d}]\in\mathcal{O}_{d}

\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}

\boldsymbol{O}_{1,k}=[\boldsymbol{o}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{o}_{k}]\in\mathcal{O}_{d,k}
for

Then define candidate knowledge matrices

\boldsymbol{W}_{k}:=\tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}\boldsymbol{O}_{1,k}

Each is a rotated and truncated version of the knowledge basis
\tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}

While larger

Hence, a candidate is transferable if its subspace distance to
\boldsymbol{U}
is at most the error order

for the non-transfer baseline estimator

\mathbb{W}_{U,k}=\left\{\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathcal{O}_{p,k}:\ \exists c_{W}>0\ \text{(independent of $n,p$) s.t.}\ \|\sin\Theta(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{U})\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}\leq c_{W}\varepsilon_{0}\right\},

Among transferable candidates, we choose the largest rank as the

\displaystyle r_{W}=\max\!\left(\{0\}\cup\left\{1\leq k\leq r\wedge d:\ \boldsymbol{W}_{k}\in\mathbb{W}_{U,k}\right\}\right).

(3.2)

The

If

Embedding estimation with knowledge transfer

Theoretical Results

4.1
Deterministic bounds for subspace analysis

In Section 4, all quantities are defined with respect to the transferable knowledge matrix
\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r_{W}}
in (
3.3
) with rank
r_{W}
, rather than the raw knowledge matrix
\boldsymbol{W}_{0}
. Proposition
3.1
shows that the sample estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}
from Step I consistently recovers
\boldsymbol{W}
.
In this subsection, we study deterministic error bounds for
\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}
from Algorithm
2
; full technical forms are given in Theorems S.1 and S.2 in the Supplementary.

In (
2.2
),
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}
split
\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{U})
into shared and heterogeneous directions. Recall that
\gamma=\|\sin\Theta(\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{W})\|
and
\gamma_{{\mathrm{F}}}=\|\sin\Theta(\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{W})\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}
measure subspace distance (see (
2.5
)), while
\delta=\|\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}\|
measures cross-component mixing (see (
2.6
)). Define

D:=\lambda_{Q_{2},\min}-\gamma^{2}\lambda_{Q_{1},\max},\qquad e_{\delta}:=\frac{\delta\gamma_{{\mathrm{F}}}}{D},

where
\lambda_{Q_{1},\max}
and
\lambda_{Q_{2},\min}
are the largest and smallest eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}
, respectively. Here,
D
measures the gap between heterogeneous and shared signals, and
e_{\delta}
is the resulting deterministic mixing bias.
Let
e_{\text{rand}}
denote the stochastic error from finite-sample noise after projection onto
\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\perp})
, as defined in
Theorem S.1.

Theorem 4.1

(Deterministic subspace estimation bounds (informal))
.

Under the conditions of Theorems S.1 and S.2 in the Supplementary, if
D>2\delta\gamma
, then

\displaystyle\|\sin\Theta(\hat{\boldsymbol{U}},\boldsymbol{U})\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}\leq\min\!\left(\gamma_{{\mathrm{F}}}+\frac{4}{3}e_{\delta}+e_{\text{rand}},\sqrt{r}\right).

(4.1)

In addition, the corresponding minimax lower bound satisfies

\displaystyle\inf_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\in\mathcal{O}_{p,r}}\sup_{(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Z},\boldsymbol{W})\in\mathcal{G}}\|\sin\Theta(\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}},\boldsymbol{U})\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}\gtrsim\max\!\left\{\gamma_{{\mathrm{F}}},\ \min\!\left(\frac{e_{\delta}+e_{\text{rand}}}{\sqrt{5}},\sqrt{r-r_{W}}\right)\right\},

(4.2)

where
\mathcal{G}
is the model class defined in Theorem S.2.

Theorem
4.1
gives a three-part decomposition:

\underbrace{\gamma_{{\mathrm{F}}}}_{\text{subspace distance}}\;+\;\underbrace{e_{\delta}}_{\text{mixing bias}}\;+\;\underbrace{e_{\text{rand}}}_{\text{finite-sample noise}}.

The first term
\gamma_{{\mathrm{F}}}
is the deterministic distance between
\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{U})
and
\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{W})
. The second term
e_{\delta}
is additional deterministic bias from cross-component mixing, and it becomes larger when mixing is stronger or gap
D
is
smaller. The third term
e_{\text{rand}}
is the stochastic error from projected finite-sample perturbation.
When
r_{W}=0
, there is no shared knowledge component, so the estimator reduces to the non-transfer baseline estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}^{(0)}
. In this edge case, the subspace-distance term vanishes (
\gamma_{{\mathrm{F}}}=0
), the mixing term is absent, and
e_{\text{rand}}
is interpreted as the estimation error of
\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}^{(0)}
.

Remark 3
.

The condition
D\gtrsim\gamma\delta
means that the heterogeneous signal
\lambda_{Q_{2},\min}
must be large enough relative to both the misalignment-induced shared contribution
\gamma^{2}\lambda_{Q_{1},\max}
and the cross-component mixing term
\gamma\delta
.
It can be necessary in mixed-alignment cases with
\delta\neq 0
. In Example
2
, let
\boldsymbol{u}_{q_{1}}:=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{q}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{u}_{q_{2}}:=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{q}_{2}
, and
\boldsymbol{z}:=\gamma\boldsymbol{u}_{q_{1}}-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}\,\boldsymbol{u}_{\perp}
. Then
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{q_{2}},\boldsymbol{z}\}
is an orthonormal basis for a two-dimensional subspace of
\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\perp})
. In the noiseless setting, the heterogeneous signal direction is recovered from the leading eigendirection of
\boldsymbol{W}_{\perp}\boldsymbol{W}_{\perp}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{W}_{\perp}\boldsymbol{W}_{\perp}^{\top}
, i.e., the projection of
\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}
onto
\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\perp})
. The restriction of this operator to
\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{u}_{q_{2}},\boldsymbol{z}\}
has matrix representation

\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{Q_{2},\min}&\gamma\delta\\
\gamma\delta&\gamma^{2}\lambda_{Q_{1},\max}\end{bmatrix}.

Thus
D=\lambda_{Q_{2},\min}-\gamma^{2}\lambda_{Q_{1},\max}
is the diagonal separation, while
\gamma\delta
is the off-diagonal mixing term. If
D
does not dominate
\gamma\delta
, the leading eigendirection of this noiseless projected operator cannot stay close to the target heterogeneous direction
\boldsymbol{u}_{q_{2}}
, so a small deterministic error bound is impossible even before sampling noise is added.
When
\delta=0
, the condition reduces to
\lambda_{Q_{2},\min}/\lambda_{Q_{1},\max}>\gamma^{2}
, the form of a standard population eigengap condition; see, for example, the switch-point gap requirement in
26
.

The upper bound in Theorem
4.1
is the basis for the statistical convergence results in Section 4.2, while the lower bound shows that this decomposition is sharp up to constants.

4.2
Statistical convergence for embedding estimation

We now derive statistical convergence guarantees for both the concept subspace estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}
and the patient embedding estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}
.
We first establish a subspace error bound, then extend the result to patient embeddings.

Assumption 1

(Sub-Gaussian error)
.

The perturbation matrix
\boldsymbol{Z}
is given by
\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{z}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{E}
. Here,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{z}
is a
p\times p
positive definite matrix with
\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{z}\|\leq\sigma^{2}
, and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{z}^{1/2}
is its symmetric square root;
\boldsymbol{E}=(E_{i,j})_{1\leq i\leq p,1\leq j\leq n}
is
p\times n
with independent sub-Gaussian entries satisfying
\mathbb{E}(E_{i,j})=0,\hskip 5.69054pt\mathbb{E}(E_{i,j}^{2})=1,\hskip 5.69054pt\|E_{i,j}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\phi^{2}

for
1\leq i\leq p,1\leq j\leq n
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\psi_{2}}
is the sub-Gaussian norm and
\sigma^{2},\phi^{2}
are absolute positive constants.

This assumption allows cross-sectional dependence through
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{z}
and is standard in weak-factor analysis
(
15
;
31
;
32
)
.

To obtain sharper bounds, we control the heterogeneous basis vectors (the columns of
\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}
) individually and then aggregate them. Let
\lambda_{Q_{1},1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{Q_{1},r_{W}}
denote the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}
, and let
\lambda_{Q_{2},1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{Q_{2},r-r_{W}}
denote the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}
.

Assumption 2

(Identification of
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}
)
.

Let
K
be the number of distinct principal angles between
\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{U})
and
\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{W})
, and partition
\{1,\dots,r_{W}\}
into groups
S_{1},\dots,S_{K}
such that angles are equal within each group.
Assume that (i) the eigenvalues
\lambda_{Q_{1},j}
are distinct across different groups
S_{k}
, and (ii) there exists some fixed constant
c_{0}\in(0,1)
such that
\max\!\left\{\lambda_{Q_{2},j+1},\ \gamma^{2}\lambda_{Q_{1},\max}\right\}\leq(1-c_{0})\lambda_{Q_{2},j}
for
1\leq j\leq r-r_{W}
with
\lambda_{Q_{2},r-r_{W}+1}=0
.

Part (i) removes rotational ambiguity in the SVD-based decomposition. Part (ii) is a heterogeneous eigengap condition: each
\lambda_{Q_{2},j}
must dominate both the next heterogeneous eigenvalue and the misalignment-induced shared contribution
\gamma^{2}\lambda_{Q_{1},\max}
. This condition is only needed for the sharper eigenvector-level bound, not for a coarser block-level bound.

Since our target application is rare-disease cohorts with limited sample size, we focus on the regime
n\leq p
. Let
\lambda_{Q_{2},\max}=\max\lambda_{Q_{2},j}
and recall that
\lambda_{Q_{2},\min}=\min\lambda_{Q_{2},j},\lambda_{Q_{1},\max}=\max\lambda_{Q_{1},j}
. Define

\varepsilon_{W^{\perp}}^{2}:=\sum_{j=1}^{r-r_{W}}\frac{p\lambda_{Q_{2},\max}+np}{\lambda_{Q_{2},j}^{2}},

(4.3)

which captures the stochastic error from projecting sub-Gaussian noise onto
\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\perp})
. The following theorem gives a statistical upper bound for subspace estimation.

Theorem 4.2

(Subspace estimation error)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. If

\displaystyle\lambda_{Q_{2},j}\gtrsim\sqrt{np}+p,\qquad\lambda_{Q_{2},\min}-\gamma^{2}\lambda_{Q_{1},\max}\gtrsim\delta,\qquad\gamma_{{\mathrm{F}}}\asymp\sqrt{r}\gamma,\qquad\varepsilon_{W^{\perp}}^{2}\lesssim\varepsilon_{0}^{2},

then

\inf_{\boldsymbol{O}\in\mathcal{O}_{r}}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}-\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{O}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\lesssim\min\!\left(\underbrace{\gamma_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}+\varepsilon_{W^{\perp}}^{2}}_{\text{with knowledge}},\underbrace{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}_{\text{without knowledge}}\right),

with probability at least

1-C\exp\!\big(-cp+\log((r-r_{W})\vee 1)\big).

Here
\varepsilon_{0}
is the error order of non-transfer baseline estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}^{(0)}
without auxiliary knowledge.

We now briefly interpret the assumptions. First, only the heterogeneous eigenvalues
\lambda_{Q_{2},j}
in
\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\perp})
need to be lower bounded. The condition
\lambda_{Q_{2},j}\gtrsim\sqrt{np}+p
is a minimum signal-strength requirement. Compared with classical factor settings that often require signals of order
np

(
5
;
36
)
, this allows weaker heterogeneous signals; for example, when
n\asymp p

satisfied by
\lambda_{Q_{2},j}\gtrsim np^{\alpha}
with
0<\alpha<1

(
6
;
32
)
. Second, the condition

4.1

3

Finally, Remark
4

that, under standard rates for

Remark 5

. Thus, smaller
\theta_{j}
implies stronger alignment with transferable directions. The resulting matrix
\boldsymbol{W}=[\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{w}_{r_{W}}]
has subspace distance

\gamma_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{r_{W}}\sin^{2}\theta_{j}=1.5\,r_{W}\,\bar{\gamma}^{2}
.

Thus, the last
r_{W}
latent directions are the transferable components aligned with
\boldsymbol{W}
, while the first
r-r_{W}
directions remain heterogeneous. We set
\boldsymbol{W}_{\mathrm{NT}}
to have orthonormal columns in the orthogonal complement of
\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{U})+\mathrm{span}(\boldsymbol{W})
.

Real Data Analysis

We analyzed EHR data from patients with multiple sclerosis (MS) in the Comprehensive Longitudinal Investigation of MS (CLIMB) registry at Mass General Brigham (MGB). MS is a relatively rare disease, with a reported global prevalence of approximately 35.9 per 100,000 individuals
(
37
)
, and it exhibits substantial heterogeneity in disease progression and disability outcomes. Quantifying disability severity is central to MS research and clinical management, most commonly through the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) and the Patient-Determined Disease Steps (PDDS)
(
22
)
. In routine clinical data, however, these outcomes are available only for a subset of patients. This makes MS a natural setting for studying whether external knowledge can improve patient and concept representations learned from limited EHR samples.

The observed data matrix
\hat{\boldsymbol{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
is a concept-by-patient matrix, where each entry
\hat{x}_{ij}
is the observed occurrence count of concept
i
in patient
j
. Rows correspond to retained clinical concepts (e.g., diagnoses, procedures, medications), and columns correspond to sampled patient windows. To construct the columns, we segment each patient’s timeline from MS diagnosis into six-month windows, count concept occurrences within each window, and randomly sample one window per patient. To stress-test limited-sample regimes, we considered two training sizes,
n=100
and
500
. For each size, we repeatedly sampled without replacement 100 MS subcohorts, applied the full estimation and evaluation pipeline to each subcohort, and summarized performance over the sampled subcohorts.

As external knowledge, we used ONCE embeddings
(
39
)
, which are derived from large-population EHR corpora and further augmented with contextual biomedical text embeddings. In our framework, these embeddings provide external concept knowledge learned from broader EHR populations. After filtering concepts by ONCE cosine similarity to MS
\geq 0.1
, we formed the raw knowledge matrix
\boldsymbol{W}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
with
p=4{,}268
concepts and
d=768
embedding dimensions.

7
Conclusion and Discussion

SENT addresses a central challenge in rare disease research: learning meaningful patient and concept representations from high-dimensional noisy data when the target cohort is small. Our framework combines knowledge preprocessing with projection-based spectral estimation, allowing transfer from broad-population concept knowledge while reducing the risk of negative transfer from irrelevant directions. Both the simulation study and the MS application show that this preprocessing step is especially valuable when shared structure is weak or the target sample is limited.

More broadly, SENT provides an interpretable and statistically tractable way to incorporate external concept knowledge into unsupervised EHR representation learning. Although we focus on a single knowledge source, the two-step strategy can be extended to multiple knowledge matrices
(
17
)
. Future work includes combining this framework with nonlinear or semi-supervised spectral methods, and extending it to longitudinal settings where the relationship between data and knowledge may evolve over time.
