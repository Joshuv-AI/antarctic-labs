Best Evidence Summary for Evidence-Based Nursing Self-Management in Patients with Neurogenic Bladder Following Spinal Cord Injury
Authors: XiaoMei Li, XiXi Li, ZhongLei Zhao, Chun Li, Wei Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo synthesize available evidence on self-management in patients with neurogenic bladder following spinal cord injury using evidence-based methods, and to provide a reference for evidence-based nursing interventions.MethodsWe conducted a best evidence summary following the methodological standards of the Fudan University Center for Evidence-based Nursing and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI). We searched CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, BMJ Best Practice, UpToDate, RNAO, NGC, GIN, and Medlive from inception to October 2025. We included clinical guidelines, expert consensuses, systematic reviews, and evidence summaries. Quality was appraised using AGREE II for guidelines and JBI checklists for other types. Evidence was synthesized thematically and graded using JBI levels (1–5). Recommendation strength (A/B) was determined based on FAME (Feasibility, Appropriateness, Meaningfulness, Effectiveness) attributes. No meta-analysis was performed.ResultsA total of 18 articles were included: 8 clinical guidelines, 2 expert consensuses, 4 systematic reviews, and 4 evidence summaries. The evidence was synthesized into 9 themes: comprehensive assessment, structured education, fluid management, device empowerment, catheter management, assisted voiding, complication monitoring, perineal skin care, and follow-up. A total of 26 pieces of evidence were formulated, with recommendations graded A or B based on the FAME framework.ConclusionThis study systematically synthesizes the available evidence on self-management in patients with neurogenic bladder following spinal cord injury. The 26 evidence items provide a practical, evidence-based framework for clinical nursing decisions, particularly in the areas of intermittent catheterization training, fluid management, complication monitoring, and patient education. The application of evidence should be carefully adapted to the local clinical context, taking into account regional and economic differences, as well as individual patient characteristics such as upper limb function and cognitive status.Keywords: spinal cord injury, neurogenic bladder, self-management, evidence summary, evidence-based nursing

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) refers to a group of clinical syndromes caused by damage to the neural tissues within the spinal canal (including the spinal cord and nerve roots) and their functions due to various pathogenic factors, resulting in sensory, motor, reflex, and bladder and bowel dysfunctions below the level of injury.1 According to statistics, approximately 70% to 84% of patients with SCI develop neurogenic bladder (NB). NB is clinically characterized by urinary retention, urinary incontinence, and voiding difficulties, and can lead to urinary tract infections, bladder stones, hydronephrosis, and even renal failure, severely affecting patients’ quality of life.2,3 The treatment goals for NB are not only to improve urinary control and voiding function, but also to consider the patient’s overall health status, treatment costs, and potential complications.4 Therefore, treatment is primarily non-surgical, aiming to preserve upper urinary tract function, restore lower urinary tract function, alleviate symptoms of urinary incontinence, and improve quality of life.5 As a chronic condition, most patients with NB require lifelong bladder management.6 During the rehabilitation phase, management largely depends on self-management in community or home settings, including bladder training, assisted voiding, behavioral therapy, and intermittent catheterization.6,7

However, self-management is particularly challenging in this population. Patients with cervical or high thoracic SCI often have impaired hand function, making clean intermittent catheterization difficult or impossible without assistance. Cognitive impairments, including executive dysfunction and memory deficits, can affect the ability to maintain fluid intake schedules, recognize signs of urinary tract infection, and adhere to catheterization protocols.8 Many patients rely on family caregivers for catheterization, but caregiver burden, lack of training, and caregiver availability can compromise the quality of care. Socioeconomic barriers, including the cost of disposable catheters, transportation to follow-up appointments, and limited health literacy, further compound these challenges. Poor adherence to intermittent catheterization—due to pain, discomfort, inconvenience, or lack of understanding—is a common cause of treatment failure and complications.9

Providing standardized guidance on bladder self-management can help patients return more quickly to family and community life. Current research on self-management in patients with NB following SCI, both domestically and internationally, is mostly qualitative or survey-based, with a primary focus on the psychological experience and current status of intermittent catheterization. Due to limitations in healthcare professionals’ rehabilitation knowledge and clinical conditions, intervention strategies tend to be relatively singular, and management lacks precision and comprehensiveness.10

While several clinical guidelines and systematic reviews have addressed neurogenic bladder management, most focus on diagnostic evaluation, pharmacological treatment, and surgical interventions, with limited attention to patient self-management and nursing care. Existing evidence summaries have addressed specific aspects of NB management, such as upper urinary tract risk management, volume management, and intermittent catheterization, but no single summary has comprehensively synthesized evidence across all domains of self-management. In view of this, this study aims to systematically synthesize the best evidence on self-management in patients with NB following SCI using evidence-based methods, so as to provide a scientific basis for enhancing patients’ self-management capabilities and improving their health outcomes.

Methods

Study Design

This is a best evidence summary conducted according to the methodological standards of the Fudan University Center for Evidence-based Nursing and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) for evidence summary development. Evidence summaries systematically search for, appraise, and synthesize the highest available evidence from multiple types of literature to provide comprehensive recommendations for clinical practice.

Literature Search Strategy

A systematic search was conducted from the top down using the “6S” pyramid evidence resource model. Both Chinese and English keywords were defined. The Chinese search terms (used for Chinese databases) included: spinal cord injury, spinal fracture, spinal cord trauma, neurogenic bladder, neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction, neurogenic incontinence, neurogenic bladder dysfunction, self-management, self-care, self-control, self-efficacy, bladder management, and clean intermittent self-catheterization. The English search terms included: “Spinal Cord Injury”, “Spinal Cord Transection”, “Spinal Cord Trauma”, “Neurogenic Bladder”, “Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction”, “Neurogenic bladder dysfunction”, “Neurogenic incontinence”, “self management”, “self administration”, “self care”, “self control”, “self efficacy”, “bladder management”, and “clean intermittent self-catheterization”.

Search techniques such as MeSH terms, truncation, and Boolean logic were applied. The following databases and resources were searched: CNKI, Wanfang Database, VIP Database, The Cochrane Library, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, BMJ Best Practice, UpToDate, the Registered Nurses’ Association of Ontario (RNAO), the National Guideline Clearinghouse (NGC), the Guidelines International Network (GIN), and Medlive. The “Limit” function was used to restrict the search scope, “Filters” were applied for literature screening, and results were sorted by “Relevance”. The search period covered from the inception of each database to October 2025.

The search was conducted from October 2024 to October 2025. A complete search strategy was developed for each database, with detailed search combinations, search fields, and filters documented in Supplementary Table S1. The following is an example of the PubMed search strategy:

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

① Studies focusing on patients with NB following SCI, aged 18 years or older;

② Research content covering topics related to bladder management or self-care;

③ Literature types including guidelines, expert consensuses, evidence summaries, systematic reviews, and recommended practices

④ Publications in Chinese or English.

Exclusion Criteria

① Literature consisting of guideline interpretations and obsolete versions of guidelines that have been updated;

② Studies with incomplete evidence information or those for which the full text could not be obtained;

③ Studies that failed to meet the inclusion criteria after quality assessment.

Quality Appraisal Criteria

Quality appraisal was conducted according to the type of literature using a tiered approach:

① For guidelines, the AGREE II instrument was used, with independent assessments performed by four researchers trained in evidence-based methodology;

② For systematic reviews, expert consensuses, and randomized controlled trials, the corresponding tools from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Evidence-Based Healthcare Center (2016) were applied, with independent assessments conducted by two researchers who had received the same training;

③ For evidence summaries and best practices, the appraisal was based on the category of their original source literature, aligning with the corresponding evaluation system described above.

All appraisals followed the principle of dual independent review. In the event of disagreement, a third member of the evidence-based team was consulted to reach a consensus, ensuring the objectivity and consistency of the evaluation results.

Evidence Synthesis and Grading

The level of evidence was determined according to the evidence recommendation standards of the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Evidence-Based Healthcare Center (2016). Evidence was graded from Level 1 to Level 5. Two researchers who had received systematic and standardized training in evidence-based methodology collaboratively discussed and formulated recommendations based on the FAME (Feasibility, Appropriateness, Meaningfulness, Effectiveness) attributes of the evidence: Grade A indicated a strong recommendation, and Grade B indicated a weak recommendation.

Management of Evidence Overlap

In accordance with evidence summary methodology, multiple types of evidence (guidelines, systematic reviews, and evidence summaries) were included because each type provides complementary information. Guidelines provide the highest-level recommendations but often focus on clinical diagnosis and treatment, with less attention to self-management and nursing care. Systematic reviews provide synthesized evidence on specific questions but may not cover the full scope of self-management. Existing evidence summaries provide pre-appraised evidence on specific topics but may have different scope or currency. We managed potential overlap by: (1) extracting non-overlapping evidence from each source; (2) prioritizing guidelines for evidence related to clinical diagnosis, assessment, and treatment; (3) using systematic reviews and evidence summaries for evidence related to patient experience, self-management strategies, and nursing care; and (4) cross-checking all extracted evidence to avoid duplication.

Discussion

Comparison with Existing Guidelines and Evidence Summaries

This evidence summary adds value beyond existing guidelines and previous evidence summaries in several ways. First, while existing guidelines focus primarily on clinical diagnosis, urodynamic evaluation, and treatment algorithms, our summary emphasizes patient-centered self-management, including practical guidance on fluid management, catheterization techniques, complication monitoring, and lifestyle adaptation. Second, our synthesis organizes evidence into nine themes that correspond to the clinical workflow of NB management, making it more accessible to nurses and healthcare providers. Third, we integrate evidence from both international and Chinese sources, providing a more comprehensive perspective that considers cultural and healthcare system differences. Fourth, our evidence items are graded according to the FAME framework, which explicitly considers feasibility, appropriateness, meaningfulness, and effectiveness, making the recommendations more applicable to real-world clinical practice.

Comprehensive Assessment: From Qualitative to Quantitative Precision in Understanding Patient Status

Evidence items 1–4 summarize the main components of comprehensive assessment for patients with NB following SCI. Before patients can achieve self-management, a systematic comprehensive assessment must be completed. Such assessment should not only answer “what type” of condition is present, but also address “how severe it is” and “how high the risk is”. Video-urodynamics is the gold standard for assessment and serves as the foundation for all clinical decisions. Neurogenic bladder management without urodynamic guidance is considered blind.4 Urodynamic studies determine bladder safe capacity, detrusor leak point pressure (DLPP), bladder compliance, and detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia. Only by understanding “under what pressure the bladder works” and “what capacity is safe” can specific fluid intake plans and catheterization frequency instructions be provided. For example, if the DLPP is found to be 40 cmH2O, the upper limit for intermittent catheterization volume must be set below this pressure threshold.27

The greatest hazard of NB is not urinary incontinence, but hydronephrosis and renal failure resulting from high-pressure reflux. The goal of upper urinary tract imaging and renal function assessment is kidney preservation. If a patient already has mild hydronephrosis, the red lines for self-management are “strict avoidance of bladder overdistension” and “strict management of urinary tract infections”.28

The voiding diary and bladder diary are among the most cost-effective yet powerful tools for bladder management, serving as a bridge between “hospital assessment” and “self-management”. Through continuous recording over 3 to 7 days, deviations between actual patient practice and the prescribed regimen can be identified.

In addition, assessment of comorbidities and functional status in spinal cord injury is equally important. The ability to perform intermittent catheterization differs significantly between patients with tetraplegia and those with paraplegia. Psychological states such as depression or denial can directly affect patient adherence. Severe constipation can compress the bladder and increase voiding pressure.29 Therefore, upper limb function, cognitive and psychological status, and bowel function are all essential components of comprehensive assessment for patients with NB following SCI.

Strengthening Structured Health Education to Enhance Patients’ Self-Management Ability

Evidence items 5–10 summarize that self-management for patients with NB following SCI is not merely about following instructions, but rather about empowering patients to become their own “bladder managers”. Starting with the question of “why,” structured health education can be delivered through methods such as one-on-one bedside instruction, peer support groups, health lectures, educational brochures, and visual feedback. This education may include showing patients hydronephrosis via ultrasound imaging or using urodynamic reports to explain the harm of high pressure to the kidneys, as well as the necessity of self-bladder management. Adherence occurs when patients establish a causal link between their “invisible kidneys” and daily fluid intake and catheterization.

It is essential to emphasize the distinction between symptoms and risks. High-pressure bladders and renal damage are often silent; the absence of symptoms does not indicate the absence of risk. The most common reason for failure in self-management is overly general medical advice that lacks precise prescriptions and actionable plans. Fluid prescriptions should be quantified and scheduled, for example: “total daily intake of 1500–2000 mL, 200 mL at 8:00 AM, 200 mL at 10:00 AM, 150 mL every two hours in the afternoon, and no fluid intake after 6:00 PM”.

For patients with appropriate conditions, a portable bladder scanner can precisely determine whether catheterization is needed, greatly reducing patient anxiety. Recording a voiding diary and using mobile applications for management enables automatic generation of trend charts, allowing early warning of abnormalities (such as increased nighttime urine volume).30,31

Intermittent Catheterization: From Passive Execution to Active Mastery

Reducing intravesical pressure and achieving timely bladder emptying are essential strategies in the management of NB. Evidence items 11–20 summarize the key steps and precautions for intermittent catheterization, reflecting the concept of “from passive execution to active mastery”. Self-management of intermittent catheterization involves not only mastering the technique of catheter insertion, but also establishing a comprehensive management system that encompasses timing judgment, aseptic principles, fluid control, and integration into daily life.

In the home environment, clean intermittent catheterization is generally sufficient; however, in hospital settings or during infectious episodes, a near-sterile approach is required. The use of hydrophilic-coated catheters can significantly reduce friction-induced trauma and pain, thereby improving acceptance of intermittent catheterization.

Training for those performing catheterization should include handwashing techniques, urethral anatomy, catheterization procedures, and key precautions. The core of active mastery lies in “catheterization on demand,” with the “demand” determined by bladder capacity. The general interval is 4 to 6 hours, based on the safe bladder capacity provided by urodynamic reports (typically recommended not to exceed 400–500 mL, with a lower threshold for high-pressure bladders).32 Therefore, the catheterization interval and volume should be dynamically adjusted according to the patient’s specific condition, with the core principle being maintenance of safe bladder capacity.33

When using non-disposable, non-hydrophilic-coated catheters (traditional rubber or silicone catheters),34 proper cleaning and storage are critical. Furthermore, intermittent catheterization should not confine patients to their homes. Active mastery means having the ability to go out, engage in social activities, and travel, which requires preparation of a catheterization kit, identification of accessible restroom locations, and adjustment of fluid intake.

Selecting Appropriate Assisted Voiding Methods Based on Patient Condition

Evidence items 21–22 summarize assisted voiding methods. Assisted voiding specifically refers to methods that facilitate urine expulsion through physical stimulation or maneuvers that increase intra-abdominal pressure, without the insertion of a catheter. Improper assisted voiding (such as forceful manual compression) is a leading cause of renal injury and vesicoureteral reflux.35 Before initiating any assisted voiding, safety must be confirmed through urodynamic studies. Assisted voiding should not be regarded as a substitute for “spontaneous voiding,” but rather as a means of bladder training or a voiding method under specific conditions.36 If assisted voiding significantly improves the patient’s quality of life and no risks are identified under close monitoring, it may be used with caution. However, it must be combined with intermittent catheterization (eg, assisted voiding followed by catheterization to measure residual volume and achieve complete emptying) to ensure the dual guarantees of “low-pressure voiding” and “complete emptying”.37

Managing Infection: Differentiating Between “Odor” and “Infection”

It is common for patients with NB to have bacteria in their urine, but this does not always necessitate treatment for infection. Evidence items 23–26 summarize the evidence-based recommendations for reducing urinary tract infections, emphasizing prevention and avoiding overuse of antibiotics to prevent the development of drug-resistant bacteria.38 For isolated findings such as cloudy urine, sediment, or mild odor in the absence of fever, flank pain, or increased spasticity, there is no need for concern. These typically indicate colonization, which can often be managed by increasing fluid intake to dilute the urine.39

If signs of systemic infection develop—such as elevated body temperature (exceeding 38°C), chills, nausea, marked worsening of baseline spasticity, sudden increase in episodes of incontinence, or a strong, foul odor of urine—urine culture should be performed and sensitive antibiotics administered.40

Routine perineal skin care should be maintained, keeping the perineal area clean and dry. Effective and complete voiding is an important measure for preventing urinary tract infections. Intermittent catheterization is the gold standard; even during the acute phase of SCI, indwelling catheters should be removed as early as possible and intermittent catheterization initiated.

Implementation Barriers and Facilitators

The translation of this evidence into clinical practice faces several barriers. At the patient level, barriers include limited health literacy, cognitive impairment, impaired hand function, caregiver dependence, and the psychological burden of lifelong catheterization. At the healthcare system level, barriers include the lack of standardized training for nurses in NB self-management education, limited access to urodynamic equipment and bladder scanners, high costs of hydrophilic-coated catheters, and insufficient follow-up resources. At the policy level, lack of reimbursement for catheter costs in many regions and limited integration of NB management into primary care settings are significant barriers.

Limitation

This review has several limitations. First, we only searched literature published in English and Chinese, which may have introduced language bias and excluded relevant evidence published in other languages. Second, grey literature (eg, unpublished studies, conference abstracts, ongoing trials) was not searched, potentially missing relevant evidence. Third, this evidence summary was not prospectively registered in a public registry, which may affect the transparency and reproducibility of the review process. Fourth, there is potential overlap among the included evidence sources (guidelines, systematic reviews, and evidence summaries), which may have resulted in some evidence being extracted from multiple sources, although we attempted to manage this by cross-checking and prioritizing guideline evidence. Fifth, the heterogeneity in evidence quality among the included studies, particularly the methodological limitations of some systematic reviews (as noted in Table 4), may affect the robustness of the evidence synthesis. Sixth, economic evaluations and cost-effectiveness analyses were not included, limiting the ability to assess the feasibility of implementing these recommendations in resource-limited settings. Seventh, most of the included evidence originated from Western countries, and the applicability of these findings to the Chinese healthcare context requires careful consideration. Finally, the lack of primary data from the Chinese population in the included studies limits our ability to assess the cultural appropriateness and acceptability of the recommendations for Chinese patients.

Conclusion

This study synthesized a total of 26 pieces of evidence on self-management in patients with neurogenic bladder following spinal cord injury, summarizing the best evidence across nine themes: comprehensive assessment, structured education, fluid management, device empowerment, catheter management, assisted voiding, complication monitoring, perineal skin care, and follow-up. These findings provide an evidence-based basis for personalized self-management interventions in clinical practice.

The implementation of this evidence has the potential to significantly improve patient outcomes. By enhancing patients’ self-management ability through structured education and practical skills training, patients can achieve more effective bladder emptying, reducing the risk of urinary tract infections, bladder overdistension, and upper urinary tract damage. Effective self-management also reduces the burden on caregivers and healthcare systems, and may improve patients’ quality of life by enabling greater independence, social participation, and psychological well-being. The evidence-based recommendations on complication monitoring and perineal skin care can help prevent secondary complications that are major causes of morbidity and healthcare utilization in this population.

As some of the evidence in this study was derived from international sources, barriers to evidence implementation should be considered during clinical translation, including patient cultural background, national healthcare policies, patient preferences, and regional economic differences. Individualized strategies should be adopted based on the specific circumstances of each patient to improve their quality of life. Future research should validate these recommendations in different healthcare settings, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, and should evaluate the cost-effectiveness of the recommended interventions. Implementation science research is needed to identify effective strategies for translating this evidence into routine clinical practice and to overcome the barriers identified in this review.
