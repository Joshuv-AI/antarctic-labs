Executive Summary
This final report for FEW0277 summarizes the work performed over the project performance
period of October 2021 – March 2025.
This project was funded under the “Reactive Capture and Conversion R&D” lab call released in
FY2021. The goal of the project was to develop dual-function materials and process for capturing
CO2 from the atmosphere and converting it into CH4. The work was organized into four parallel
tracks in 1) direct air capture materials synthesis and characterization, 2) catalysts for CO 2
conversion, 3) mechanistic investigations via ab initio simulations, and 4) process modeling,
technoeconomic analysis, and lifecycle assessment.
The project was split into two budget periods. The first budget period focused on development of
amine-based materials, due to their known performance for CO2 direct air capture and their
potential to act synergistically with metal catalysts to enable a low-temperature methanation
pathway. The second budget period focused on development of alkali-based materials and a
simulated-moving-bed process for high conversion catalytic reduction of captured CO 2 to CH4. All
project milestones were completed during the project performance period and are summarized in
this report.
Our work resulted in publication of eight peer-reviewed manuscripts, one patent application, and
numerous presentations given at domestic and international conferences and invited academic
department seminars.

Accomplishments
Task 1: Synthesize hybrid adsorbent-catalyst materials
We synthesized a series of (2-aminoethyl)-3-aminopropyltrimethoxysilane (herein “diamine” or
“diaminosilane”)-grafted oxides on oxide supports that varied in terms of their composition (SiO 2,
Al2O3, TiO2, CeO2, ZrO2), porosity (ordered mesoporous vs. random pore structure), and form
factor (powders, pellets, and microspheres). A grafting method utilizing gentle mixing on an orbital
shake plate, rather than magnetic stirring, was developed to allow retention of the pellet and
microsphere form factors. These materials were analyzed for their silane grafting efficiency and
thermal stability using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). Depending on the grafting conditions,
the mass loading (mmol silane/g support) and the silane surface loading (µmol/m 2 support) could
be controlled, providing a degree of tunability.
Using down-selected materials and methods, we synthesized several hybrid adsorbent-catalyst
materials, first depositing 1% and 5% nominal Ru loading and then grafting diaminosilane. The
silane loading could be controlled by varying the amount of water used during synthesis, as
indicated in Table 1. Additionally, we found that bare oxides had greater silane grafting yields
compared to metal-loaded oxides. The grafting yields on catalysts with 1% or 5% Ru were
approximately equal, indicating that within this range the metal loading did not strongly control the
grafting yield. Materials with a lower metal loading had greater metal dispersion, so both may
have similar reductions in surface area for functionalization with silane.

The grafting results indicated that Ru catalysts may be readily functionalized with aminosilanes
via the process developed for bare oxide supports. This represents a facile route to produce hybrid
materials with both CO2 adsorption (diaminosilane) and CO2 methanation (Ru) functionalities.

We compared the thermal stability of the organic diaminosilane functionality in the TGA and
compared them to sorbent-only materials (Figure 1). In both SiO2- and TiO2-supported sorbent-
only materials, there were three broad mass loss peaks during oxidative burnoff after an initial
drying step at 100 °C. In metal-containing hybrid materials, the degradation profile changed fairly
drastically. TiO2 hybrid materials showed a single sharp mass loss peak, whereas in SiO 2 hybrid
materials there was a similar sharp peak following one small peak at lower temperatures. This
new decomposition profile suggested that the existence of the metal on the surface affected the
diaminosilane stability. At temperatures below 300 °C, the hybrid materials appeared more stable,
but the organic content of the silane rapidly degraded between 300 and 400 °C in all materials.
The sharp peak and this change may indicate a metal-catalyzed oxidative degradation of the
diaminosilane moieties.

Task 2: Evaluate adsorption performance with dilute CO 2
We evaluated the adsorption performance of adsorbent and hybrid adsorbent-catalyst materials
primarily using TGA to precisely control the gas and temperature environment. The materials
were degassed at 100 °C under a flow of inert gas to remove any adsorbed atmospheric water
and CO2. Following cooling to the adsorption temperature of 30 °C, the materials were exposed
to a gas environment of 410 ppm CO2 in N2 to simulate the concentration of atmospheric CO2,
and the mass increase was measured.
All materials showed reasonable direct air capture performance, although the SiO 2-based
materials were generally superior to the TiO2 materials (Table 2). The amine efficiencies for each
of the SiO2 materials, the sorbent-only material and with 1% and 5% Ru loading, were identical,

suggesting that the sorbent functionality of the hybrid materials is just as effective as that of the
sorbent-only material in SiO2. However, the same cannot be said for the TiO 2 materials. The
amine efficiency decreased with increasing Ru content in these materials, indicating that the Ru
may be somehow hindering the sorbent performance. This is likely a result of the materials having
relatively low silane loadings. The amine efficiency tends to increase with amine loading due to
ability to form proximal amine–amine capture pairs, and the two hybrid TiO 2 materials have low
silane loadings. We hypothesize this is more likely to be the case, given that the hybrid SiO 2
materials did not see a hindrance of sorbent performance with metal content.

Milestone 1: Measured DAC adsorption capacity >0.25 mol CO2/kg
We also examined the change in adsorption performance following thermal treatment to assess
the potential impact of methanation reaction temperature on the stability of the material (Table 3).
Notably, many of the adsorbents synthesized on SiO 2, Al2O3, and TiO2 powders surpassed
the Milestone 1 target DAC adsorption capacity of 0.25 mol CO2/kg in their as-synthesized
state and many maintain their capacity up to thermal treatment temperatures of 250 °C.
This temperature is a reasonable minimum temperature of interest for methanation reactions. At
300 °C more drastic decreases in capacity were seen as more of the grafted mass on the oxide
supports was lost and/or possibly deactivated. In particular, materials based on Al 2O3 displayed
almost complete capacity loss at 300 ° C, suggesting a weaker or less stable bond with the silane
compared to SiO2 or TiO2.

Task 3: Characterize catalysts and perform methanation with dilute CO 2
An experimental comparison of two well-established synthesis techniques, strong electrostatic
adsorption (SEA) and incipient-wetness impregnation (IWI), was conducted to compare the
performance of Ru/TiO2 materials created via these two techniques. While both methods
produced materials wherein the Ru was well-dispersed on the TiO2 support (measured via powder
X-ray diffraction, indicated by absence of reflections attributable to large Ru particles), materials
created with SEA were more readily reduced (measured via H2 temperature-programmed
reduction), displayed higher dispersion (measured via pulse CO chemisorption), and had more
available surface area for catalysis (measured via cryogenic N2 physisorption), leading us to
select this technique for further studies.

Milestone 2: Achieved >25% CO2 single-pass conversion from dilute CO2
Catalyst-only and hybrid adsorbent-catalyst materials were tested for steady-state single-pass
catalytic activity using an Altamira-300 reactor system. Materials were first exposed to metal
reduction conditions at 200 °C in a flow of 4% H2/Ar, followed by exposure to a mixture of CO2
and H2 to evaluate steady-state conversion (Table 4 and Figure 3). Additionally, the CO2 capacity
of materials was measured before and after exposure to reaction conditions to evaluate the impact
of catalysis on adsorption.

The higher metal loading Ru/TiO2-5 had moderate activity, converting a maximum of ~23% CO2
but showed a remarkable improvement in activity on adding the diamine. Ru/TiO2-5-diamine had
a maximum CO2 conversion of ~30% and 100% selectivity for CH4, meeting our Milestone

To summarize, we demonstrated sorbent-catalyst combinations capable of capturing CO 2 in low
concentration streams. These materials were proficient for steady-state CO2 conversion up to 200
°C with ~100% selectivity for CH4. Amines are stable under these conditions and depending on
the oxide support may experience improvement in CO 2 capture capacity following thermal
treatment. Conversion of greater than 25% were obtained on Ru/TiO 2-5-diamine, which entirely
maintained CO2 capture capacity after the reaction.

Task 4: Simulate interaction between captured CO2 and single-atom catalyst site
We developed a series of simulations, based on density functional theory (DFT) to capture the
interactions between CO2, amine, and Ru metal sites. This is a complex system, which can be
described by three primary components: the grafted aminosilanes, the oxide support, and the
metal catalysts. A primary challenge in modeling this system is that in the experimental system,
there are many possible ways for the aminosilanes to graft and for the metal catalyst atoms to
disperse on the oxide supports, due to the heterogeneity of the oxide surface. Furthermore, the
distance and orientations between the grafted aminosilanes and metal catalyst sites are likely to
be broadly distributed. Thus, to efficiently model the effect of the amine binding on the catalytic
conversion of CO2 to methane, we developed a simplified model wherein CO2 bound to
methylamine was used to simulate CO2 bound to the full aminopropylsilane, due to the similarity
in binding energy for these species. While the full aminopropylsilane structure may be important
in modeling other aspects of the system, such as the effect of the support on the grafted polymer
dynamics, or the effect of the polymer degrees of freedom on the CO2 capture, we can be
confident that the simplified model captures the chemical reaction energy effects most dominant
in the surface catalytic conversion itself.

Milestone 3: Established energetics for conversion of captured CO2 to CH4
We proposed a series of elementary reaction steps for the catalytic conversion of amine-bound
CO2 and conventional CO2 methanation for comparison (Figure 4). From these proposed steps,
we identified all unique adsorbed intermediates. We then set up models of all these intermediates
and proceeded to calculate their energies using DFT calculations, using the harmonic
approximation to calculate free energy corrections (zero-point energy, heat capacity, and entropy
corrections, at several temperatures). From these total energies, we calculated the binding
energies, which are essentially formation energies, such that reaction energies for any possible
elementary step involving the included intermediates could be readily calculated. Using this
approach allowed us to study a variety of resulting pathways, without having to calculate each
reaction energy explicitly any time a new elementary step is to be included, unless that step
involves an adsorbate not previously considered.

Figure 4. Proposed reaction mechanism for amine-assisted (amine-bound; top) and conventional pathway
(non-amine-bound; bottom) toward CO2 methanation on Ru(0001) surface. [A]H refers to the gas phase
methylamine(CH3NH2). The methylamine in the figure (and in our DFT model) is a computational model
that approximates the local chemical environment/bonding of the full surface-bound/grafted amine species
on the Ru catalyst surface. Both pathways converge toward different modes of amine unbinding (indicated

From the reaction energies, we constructed free energy diagrams to show the major
mechanistic differences between amine-assisted and conventional CO 2 methanation
pathways, meeting our Milestone 3 goal (Figure 5). For the amine-assisted pathway, we also
calculated the energetics for various stages at which the amine could unbind from the C-species,
which is necessary at some point to be able to form CH4.
We calculated the reaction free energy barriers for selected reactions that are known to be rate-
limiting for CO2 methanation on different metal surfaces, specifically C–O bond dissociation and
C hydrogenation. Without the amine ligand, C–O bond dissociation has been previously shown
to have a very high barrier, above 2 eV, in agreement with our calculations. In contrast, with the
amine ligand, the barrier is lowered by more than 1 eV due to stabilization of the undercoordinated
C that is produced by the reaction. This stabilization is evident in the reaction energies as well,
with the amine-assisted pathway being slightly exergonic compared with the endergonic
conventional dissociation reaction step.
In contrast, for C hydrogenation, the reaction energy trend is flipped—hydrogenation of the amine-
bound C is endergonic by about 0.6 eV, whereas hydrogenation of unbound surface C is
exergonic by about 0.2 eV. Surprisinglythe free energy barriers for these two reactions, regardless
of the presence of the amine, are about the same. Subsequent hydrogenation steps have similar
energetics and the amine unbinds from the CHx species prior to the final hydrogenation steps.

Task 7: Synthesize second-generation materials
In budget period 2, we switched from amine-based adsorbent materials to alkali-based adsorbent
materials. Though the amine-based materials demonstrated some promise, preliminary
experiments performed for cyclic adsorption-methanation resulted in a high fraction of CO2
desorbing from the material, unreacted, with minimal (<5%) CH4 formation. We hypothesize that,
though our simulations and initial steady-state methanation experiments suggested the presence

of the amine should alter the energetics of CO2 methanation, that this impact is not sufficient to
enable significant CO2 methanation at temperatures below the typical desorption temperature of
CO2. To aid other researchers, we published a Perspective article in collaboration with another
FECM-funded project on the prospects and considerations of using amines for thermocatalytic
reactive capture (see W.W. McNeary et al., ACS Omega 2025).
Alkali-based adsorbent materials were selected due to their slightly higher binding energy for CO 2,
potentially allowing the adsorbed CO2 to remain on the surface long enough to be converted to a
stable intermediate that would not desorb prior to methanation. While reactive capture and
methanation had been demonstrated in the literature for Na-based materials on Al2O3 prior to our
work, we hypothesized that changing the support to TiO2 could help to improve the CO2
conversion due to the reducibility of the support.

Milestone 4: Downselected material compositions
We developed a series of Na-based materials on the same oxide supports discussed previously.
We also procured an additional higher surface area TiO2 support, noted as TiO2 Hombikat M311,
to complement work on the lower surface area Aeroxide P25 and P90 supports. From the
original support oxide materials, we selected Al2O3 and the three TiO2 supports after initial
testing for further investigation. The SiO2 materials appeared to be unstable in the high pH
conditions required for Na-impregnation, and the CeO2 had too low surface area to effectively
disperse the Na, leading to low CO2 uptake performance. Thus, our downselection resulted in
selection of alkali-based adsorbents over amine-based adsorbents and alkaline-stable high
surface area supports that retained porosity through the material preparation procedure.
Task 8: Evaluate adsorption performance and material durability with humidity

Milestone 5: Measured DAC adsorption capacity >0.40 mol CO2/kg
We measured the CO2 capacity for the alkali-based adsorbents using our standard TGA-based
screening for CO2 uptake (Figure 6). We identified multiple adsorbent materials with DAC
capacity exceeding our Milestone 5 goal of 0.40 mol CO2/kg by using higher surface area
titania (P90 and M311) with adequate Na loading (>5 wt%). These materials were identified by
screening different Na loadings, loading procedures, and pre-treatment procedures. We found
that high calcination temperature (>500 °C) resulted in Na intercalation into the TiO 2 support, with
evidence from powder X-ray diffraction of formation of sodium titanate-like phases that had little-
to-no CO2 adsorption capacity. A longer calcination (10 h) at 400 °C tended to produce the highest
performing materials. From these observations, we concluded that a long calcination time at 400
°C was the optimal calcination approach for activating the materials and maintaining material
integrity. We additionally prepared dual-functional materials containing both Na and Ru to
examine the impact of Ru on the CO2 adsorption performance, since we observed a slight
decrease for the amine-based materials.
For the Na-only materials, the adsorption capacity decreased with increasing adsorption
temperature, as would be thermodynamically expected for chemisorbing materials. For the Ru/Na
materials, the adsorption capacity exhibited a peak at 50 °C. These materials appeared to
experience some additional kinetic barriers to CO2 adsorption at 30 °C from the addition of Ru to
the sorbent since there is a difference in uptake between the Na-only and Ru/Na materials. At 50
°C and above, the uptake capacities are nearly identical between the Na-only and Ru/Na
sorbents. A benchmark alumina-supported Ru/Na material exhibited the highest CO 2 uptake, at

nearly 40% more capacity than the Hombikat M311 TiO 2 supported material. This may partially
be attributed to the higher surface area of the Al2O3 support compared to the various TiO2 used.

With some modification to our systems, we were able to investigate the impact of humidity on CO 2
adsorption performance for the TiO2-based materials (Figure 7). These adsorption experiments
were performed on a fixed bed breakthrough analyzer, rather than the TGA, to allow accurate
determination of the CO2 capacity in the presence of humidity. Experiments with humidity were
performed in two ways—in one experiment, noted as pre-saturated, the hybrid adsorbent-catalyst
material was exposed to a gas stream of N2 saturated with humidity. Following saturation with
water, the gas stream was switched to a mixture of CO2 and N2 with humidity. In another
experiment, noted as co-exposure, the dry, regenerated material was simultaneously exposed to
CO2 and humidity. The pre-saturated experiment is important for understanding the maximum
potential CO2 uptake under humid conditions, as the amount of water adsorbed on these materials
is not expected to change during the CO2 exposure. On the other hand, the co-exposure
experiment more accurately reflects the candidate operational cycle; that is, regenerated material
(following CO2 methanation) would be exposed to the ambient air, and hence, CO 2 and humidity
simultaneously. However, as the amount of adsorbed water changes during this experiment, the
quantitative results can be impacted non-material factors like the design of the fixed bed.
In both experimental cases and for all TiO2-based materials, the CO2 capacity increased by ~25%.
This is in agreement with expectations from literature results and from initial understanding of
adsorption mechanisms. Water may help form stable adsorbed species, likely some form of
carbonate or bicarbonate, in homogeneous reaction in the air or surface reactions. An
improvement in adsorption capacity with humidity is promising for air capture applications, as any
ambient environment will have some amount of humidity and will preclude the need for pre-drying
of inlet streams.

Task 9: Develop cyclic air capture-methanation process and test performance
We developed two different cyclic air capture-methanation processes. In the standard cycle
(termed “cold load”), the materials were exposed to dilute CO 2 at 50 °C, then heated to 300 °C in
the presence of H2. In the novel process (termed “hot load”), the materials were cooled from 300
°C to 50 °C in the presence of dilute CO2 over the course of 100 min, then heated to 300 °C in
the presence of H2. These are shown schematically in Figure 8. He is used to avoid mixing air
and H2 at elevated temperature.

Figure 8. Schematics showing the gas flows and temperature profile during (A) “cold load” and (B) “hot
load” (right) reactive capture and conversion (RCC) cycle. Reproduced from M. J. Rasmussen et al., Chem.
Eng. J. 2025.

These reactive capture and conversion (RCC) cycles rely on the ability for the adsorbent
component of the material to bind CO2 strongly enough that it can be converted either all the way
to CH4 or into a stable intermediate before it desorbs from the surface. As previously discussed,
we pivoted away from the amine-based materials because initial cold load experiments performed
for these materials suggested that only ~5% of the CO2 was able to be converted to CH4, with the
majority of CO2 desorbing unreacted.

Milestone 6: Converted >50% of captured CO2 into CH4
Initial experiments were performed with 5 cold load and 3 hot load cycles to examine the
performance of Ru-Na/oxide materials (Figure 9). The Al2O3 and TiO2 materials stood out in terms
of achieving high CO2 uptake and good CH4 production. Additionally, the calculated CO2
conversion for both materials, with both RCC protocols, surpassed our Milestone 6 target.
As previously discussed as part of Milestone 4, the SiO2 and CeO2-based materials fared poorly
due to pore collapse and low surface area, respectively, leading to low initial CO 2 uptake and poor
conversion, and were not carried forward for significant additional testing.

for a series of Ru-Na DFMs on various supports over the course of 5 “cold load” RCC cycles and 3 “hot
load” RCC cycles.

The reactor effluent profiles (Figure 10) for several different TiO2 supports confirmed the
importance of support surface area not only in impacting the CO 2 capacity (and hence, maximum
CH4 production), but also the CO2 conversion – the high surface area TiO2 (Hombikat M311) had
the highest CO2 capacity, CH4 production, and CO2 conversion out of all TiO2 materials. While the

Al2O3 material had higher surface area, and hence higher CO 2 capacity and CH4 production, more
CO2 desorbed from this material unreacted.

Milestone 7: Retained >75% performance after extended cyclic operation
We performed a series of cold load and hot load cycles with a single material: the 1 wt% Ru – 10
wt% Na/TiO2(M311), which was the highest performing material for CO2 conversion that also had
reasonable CO2 uptake and CH4 production (Figure 11). During the series of hot load cycles,
parametric tests were conducted to examine the impact of process parameters (not shown).

Figure 11. RCC performance of Ru-Na/TiO2(M311) during all hot and cold load cycles from the 77-cycle
campaign. CO2 uptake (black circles, left axis), CO2 desorption (blue bars, left axis), CH4 production (red
bars, left axis), and CH4 desorption purity (purple squares, right axis) are reported for each cycle.
Reproduced from M. J. Rasmussen et al., Chem. Eng. J. 2025.

The amount of mass-normalized CO2 uptake and CH4 production decreased minimally over the
course of the durability campaign. In fact, the CH4 production for the first hot load cycle (Cycle
14) and last hot load cycle (Cycle 77) was measured as 0.31 mmol/g and 0.29 mmol/g
respectively, representing a roughly 5% decrease in mass normalized methane production over
the course of >60 RCC cycles. The CO2 uptake for the first and last hot load cycles was measured
as 0.34 mmol/g and 0.31 mmol/g respectively, representing roughly 9% reduction over the course
of >60 RCC cycles. In combination, these performance metrics demonstrate that we achieved
>90% retention in both CO2 uptake and conversion over the course of >60 cycles,
exceeding our Milestone 7 target. Moreover, CH4 desorption purity improved throughout the
campaign, with each of the last 4 hot load cycles (74–77) exceeding the minimum pipeline-quality
specification for natural gas of 97%.

Task 10: Simulate adsorption and conversion processes
We conducted ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) to investigate the structure of Na2O/TiO2
materials to better understand the impact of Na dispersion on CO 2 binding and calculate the
electronic structure of the materials. We executed a well-structured, temperature-controlled
annealing process as a metaheuristic approach to achieve a more precise representation of the
catalyst under real experimental reaction conditions. This resulted in the creation of two distinct
catalyst models that aim to mimic the effects of dispersed metal particles as opposed to clustered
ones, and the effect on CO2 binding (Figure 12).

The nanoparticle (NP) and dispersed models resulted in multiple pockets of interfacial Na/TiO 2
sites available for CO2 adsorption, further studied through density functional theory calculations.
Over the NP model, the CO2 binding energy ranged between 0.044 and -0.166 eV, while it ranged
between -0.70 and -0.82 eV over the atomically dispersed model. In contrast, benchmark
calculations for CO2 adsorption on a well-defined crystalline sodium titanate revealed significantly
weaker and almost negligible adsorption energies over the Na (0.0197 eV) and Ti sites (-0.074
eV). These DFT results indicate that atomic dispersion of Na atoms enhances CO 2 binding energy
and uptake, but formation of sodium titanate decreases CO 2 binding, consistent with experimental
observations.
Exploring the electronic charge distribution within the TiO2 support across the dispersed and NP
models, Bader charge density difference plots illustrate electronic interactions between the Na
particle and TiO2 support. The charge density analysis shows a higher degree of electronic
interaction over the dispersed model, where electrons from Na atoms transfer to the TiO 2
substrate, leaving Na as cationic sites. In contrast, the NP model shows no electronic
interaction/charge transfer with the support. These electronic distinctions help explain why the
dispersed model exhibits stronger CO2 adsorption, aligning with experimental findings where
higher Na dispersion correlates with increased CO2 uptake.

Task 11: Refine technoeconomic and life cycle analyses
Throughout the project, we developed and refined process and technoeconomic analysis (TEA)
models for separated CO2 capture and conversion processes and the reactive CO 2 capture
process (Figure 13). In general, we used 1 MMBTU of natural gas as our functional unit; here,
we describe it as synthetic renewable natural gas (SRNG) due to the use of hydrogen from
electrolysis, assuming use of solar or wind electricity. The process model included a complete
mass and energy balance and allowed us to track all inputs and outputs of the system. The TEA
model included all equipment related to the process and accounted for the indirect capital costs
(land cost, legal cost, and project contingency) and fixed operating costs (labor, insurance,
maintenance, taxes, and others) as a function of the direct capital cost. Some general
assumptions in the TEA model included a plant life of 25 years, an annual operating capacity of
90%, and interest rates of 10%, which resulted in a calculated capital recovery factor of 11%. We
also assumed the electricity price to be $0.06/kWh, waste heat at no cost, hydrogen price of
$3.00/kg (produced from low-temperature electrolysis), and sorbent/catalyst cost of $50/kg. All
equipment that makes up the direct capital cost is currently scaled accordingly to appropriate
capacities or gas flow rates depending on the function of the equipment (e.g., the fan in the DAC
system is scaled according to CO2 input) to a common exponential factor of 0.6 to represent
economies of scale.

Figure 13. Simplified process flow diagrams for (A) a separated CO2 capture and conversion process, with
and without heat integration, and (B) a reactive CO2 capture process. Reproduced from A. Aui et al., Cell
Reports Sustainability 2025.

We estimated via TEA that producing SRNG using CO2 from the air and renewable hydrogen
costs approximately $45–52/MMBTU (Figure 14). The variable operating costs, including the cost
of the hydrogen and the replacement cost of the sorbent/catalyst, constitute most of the total cost,
while fixed operating and capital costs make only a minor contribution. Comparing the separated
and reactive capture processes, there are only minor differences in the minimum SRNG selling
price a result of the overall cost being dominated by the fixed hydrogen input, minimizing the
impact of potential energy and capital efficiency improvements.

separated and reactive CO2 capture process. The inset shows the contribution of energy and other variable
operating costs (catalyst/material replacement, CO2 compression, revenue from electricity), capital cost,
and fixed operating cost. MSPs in the figure are rounded to the nearest dollar. The TEA assumes a

hydrogen price of $3/kg, electricity price of $0.06/kWh, plant lifetime of 25 years with a capital recovery

Milestone 8: Demonstrated impact of CO2 adsorption capacity and fractional conversion on RNG
MFSP
We examined the impact of several important economic and performance parameters on the MSP
of SRNG (Figure 15). In general, the lifetime of the sorbent/catalyst and the cost of hydrogen had
the strongest impact on the MSP, as expected from the cost breakdown. Additionally, the cost of
the sorbent/catalyst could help to reduce the MSP, though this was coupled with the lifetime (i.e.
a longer lifetime reduced the impact of material cost). In contrast, the capital cost of the RCC
system had minimal impact on the overall MSP cost. We assumed that the RCC reactor cost the
same as the standalone methanation reactor due to the lack of publicly available data, though it
may be less expensive due to operation near ambient pressure or more expensive due to the
larger expected volume; the sensitivity analysis shows that this assumption is not critical for
predicting the MSP.

Figure 15. (A) Parametric sensitivity analysis on the minimum SRNG selling price. The ranges of each
parameter are noted on the vertical axis as [low:base:high]; numbers overlaid on the figure represent the
resulting minimum SRNG selling price. (B) Variation of minimum SRNG selling price vs. conversion rate
and comparison with today’s benchmark processes, demonstrating the reactive CO 2 capture conversion
efficiency required, assuming FOAK DACS cost ($820/tCO2) and current hydrogen price ($3/kg).
Reproduced from A. Aui et al., Cell Reports Sustainability 2025.

The CO2 adsorption capacity has a small, but meaningful impact on the MSP. The baseline value
assumed in this study, approximately 1 mol CO2/kg material, is higher than that achieved for the
alkali materials in our experimental work, suggesting that there is additional work to be done to
improve this parameter. The fractional CO2 conversion of the RCC process also has moderate
impacts on the MSP. Our baseline scenario assumed 100% conversion of CO 2 to CH4; incomplete
conversion requires additional CO2/CH4 gas separation (modeled here with an MDEA capture
process) to make the SRNG pipeline ready. The cost increases by only 5% if the single-pass
conversion drops to 50% but increases more sharply as the conversion decreases below 20%.
This further validates the decision to pursue the alkali-based materials, which are able to achieve
acceptable single-pass conversion, whereas the amine-based materials are not. At these ultra-
low single-pass conversions, the system essentially acts as a separated CO 2 capture and
conversion process, but with two CO2 separation steps, driving the high cost. This sensitivity
analysis satisfies Milestone 8.

To analyze the business case for formation of SRNG using an RCC process, we considered
alternative pathways to producing low-carbon natural gas (not shown). Producing synthetic
natural gas is significantly more expensive than business-as-usual operation and is also more
expensive than capturing the emissions from conventional natural gas use (e.g. capturing the
emissions of a natural gas power plant) and offsetting other emissions with dedicated CO 2 direct
air capture. Synthetic natural gas is cost-competitive with methane produced by anaerobic
digestion.
Where SRNG from RCC may provide value is as a long-duration energy storage technology
(Figure 16). As an energy storage technology, the cost of SRNG from RCC is $0.26/kWh and is
generally cheaper than most technologies, except for diabatic compressed-air energy storage
(CAES). CAES is designed for long durations, but its scale is limited by the available geology and
cavern design. Pumped storage hydropower, which has a similar cost, also faces the same terrain
challenge and high capital cost. Batteries (lithium-ion, vanadium redox flow, lead-acid, etc.) have
the highest cost but typically serve a shorter duration market. Some battery technologies suffer
from other challenges such as significant use of critical materials, low energy density, and high
self-discharge rates when used for long durations. Hydrogen energy storage systems have been
investigated for medium-to-long term energy storage due to low self-discharge rate and high
energy density. However, there remains compatibility and integration challenges for hydrogen at
large scales with current distribution and storage infrastructure, such as structural degradation
risks due to hydrogen embrittlement and a possibility of leakage and ensuing safety issues.
Producing SRNG is favorable because it is compatible with existing storage, transmission, and
distribution infrastructure, i.e., natural gas pipelines and storage reservoirs. This is in addition to
its long-term energy storage capability and ability to complement the intermittency of renewable
energy from solar or wind, thus, allowing the grid to be more reliable even under a high proportion
of renewable energy.
