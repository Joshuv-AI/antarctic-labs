Private Learning in Bilateral Trade
Simone Di Gregorio, Federico Fusco, Stefano Leonardi, Chris Schwiegelshohn

Abstract
Bilateral trade models one of the most fundamental economic interactions: the intermediation between two strategic agents, a seller and a buyer, willing to trade a good. We consider the learning version of the problem, where the goal is to learn a mechanism from a sampled dataset of agents’ valuations to maximize either profit or economic efficiency. While known learning algorithms are characterized by high sensitivity to the input dataset, we specifically study this problem through the lens of differential privacy, ensuring that each data point does not significantly affect the probability of learning any specific mechanism. For our results, we adopt the PAC-learning framework: with high probability, the learning algorithm should output a mechanism that is at most an additive α \alpha away from optimal, in a ε \varepsilon -differentially private way. As a first result, we show that differential privacy and (near)-optimality are not achievable for general distributions. Surprisingly, assuming that the distribution underlying the agents’ valuations is σ \sigma -smooth, we recover nearly optimal sample-complexity bounds for both economic efficiency and profit. For profit, we show how to construct in polynomial time an α \alpha -optimal and ε \varepsilon -differentially private mechanism using Θ ~ ​ ( 1 / σ ​ ε ​ α 2 ) \tilde{\Theta}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sigma\varepsilon\alpha^{2}}}) samples. For efficiency, measured by the gain from trade, we achieve the same result using Θ ~ ​ ( 1 / ε ​ α + 1 / α 2 ) \tilde{\Theta}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon\alpha}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha^{2}}}) samples. Notably, these bounds are essentially tight in the precision parameter α \alpha , since achieving α \alpha -optimality (ignoring differential privacy) requires at least 1 / α 2 \nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha^{2}}} samples.

Introduction

The bilateral trade problem captures a fundamental economic primitive: how should a broker intermediate between a seller and a buyer wishing to trade a specific good? The crucial tension underlying this task arises from the competing interests of the two rational agents —high vs. low price— who, however, need to agree to make the trade happen. Bilateral trade has been investigated intensively in the economic literature, starting from the seminal works of
58
and
49
, and has attracted a considerable attention by computer scientists
(
25
;
13
;
5
;
12
;
41
;
43

, mainly due to its natural applicability in online markets (e.g., Online Marketplaces, Online Advertising Markets, Ride-sharing Platforms).

The seller and the buyer are characterized by their private valuations for the good, which are drawn from some underlying distribution, and the goal of the broker is to design a “good” mechanism, which is robust to the strategic manipulations of the two agents. In this paper, we investigate mechanisms that are dominant-strategy incentive compatible (DSIC), ensuring agents optimize their utility by reporting their true valuations, and individual rational (IR), guaranteeing that participating in the mechanism yields non-negative utility. To assess the quality of the outcome of the mechanism, we consider the two most natural quantities:

•

Economic Efficiency
, measured in terms of the increase in social welfare induced by the trade (the so-called gain-from-trade).

•

Profit
, which is simply the net utility that the broker extracts from the agents.

When dealing with maximizing gain from trade, we require the mechanism to enforce a third property: budget balance (BB), so that the payment made to the seller does not exceed the payment collected from the buyer. Budget balance is needed to avoid the undesirable situation in which the broker
subsidizes
the market. The addition of this constraint has the surprising effect of
collapsing
the family of DSIC, IR and BB mechanisms for gain-from-trade maximization to the simple sub-family of fixed-price mechanisms, where the broker posts one price to both agents and the trade happens if they both accept

A fruitful line of work has investigated the quality of such mechanisms under the assumption that the distribution underlying agents’ valuation

A critical challenge that remains unaddressed

Neglecting this aspect poses a significant risk, as the optimized mechanism itself may leak sensitive information about the valuations contained in the training data set. Indeed, the state-of-the-art learning algorithms for bilateral trade are highly data dependent, so that it is easy to recover some of the points used to train them

Moreover, this sensitivity makes them vulnerable to adversarial manipulation of the underlying dataset, as a single seller-buyer pair in the training set may drastically change the output mechanism. In this work, we tackle this problem through the lens of differential privacy (DP), the gold standard for algorithmic privacy

A possibly randomized algorithm

1.2
Technical Challenges

We start by reviewing the state-of-the-art learning algorithms for bilateral trade and observe that they are highly “non-private”, so that we need fresh ideas to attain private learning.

For gain-from-trade maximization, it is possible to get an
\alpha
-optimal posted-price mechanism by running empirical risk minimization on
\tilde{\Theta}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha^{2}}})
samples (or follow-the-leader, in the online learning jargon), via the uniform convergence property mentioned above. Clearly such an approach is inherently data-dependent. First, if all the samples are concentrated in one point, then the output mechanism will also heavily depend on that point. Second, and most crucially for differential privacy, if there are two prices that are equally good on the sampled dataset, then changing one single valuation may change completely the identity of the price output. Moving to profit maximization, the learning algorithm Simplify-the-Best-Mechanism used in
31
is even more drastically data dependent. It works as follows: first, it computes the empirical risk minimizing mechanism
\mathcal{M}^{\star}
on the sampled dataset, then, it “simplifies” it, by adapting the allocation region of
\mathcal{M}^{\star}
to a geometric tessellation of the
[0,1]^{2}
square whose tiles
are induced by the observed data points
. Both these two steps are inherently non private.

Private Learning Frameworks via Exponential Sampling

A popular method to obtain differentially-private algorithms is to combine the exponential mechanism
(
47
)
with a
fixed
and
finite
subfamily of candidate mechanisms
(
8
;
45
)
. Succinctly, it entails sampling a mechanism
M
with probability proportionate to
\exp\left(\varepsilon\cdot f(M)\right)
, where the score function
f
denotes either the empirical profit or the gain from trade, depending on the problem at hand. This exponential sampling procedure guarantees
\varepsilon
differential privacy, while preferring mechanisms with high empirical score. The applicability of such approach is limited by three factors. (i) The best mechanism on
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
should be comparable with the best
overall
mechanism, so that a good mechanism gets picked with some probability. (ii) The empirical scores (i.e., profit or gain from trade) are tightly concentrated around the true scores, as otherwise the distribution might output low-value mechanisms. (iii) A naive implementation requires enumerating over all candidate mechanisms
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
, which may lead to an infeasible running time.

A Fixed
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
does not work for general distributions

Unfortunately, both the gain from trade and the profit objectives
are not
approximable with fixed discretizations! This is a well-known feature of bilateral trade, for both profit
(
31
)
and gain from trade
(
4
, e.g.,)
which makes its learning typically challenging. We use the construction underlying this fact to formally argue that it is not possible to privately learn either objective in bilateral trade, without making some assumption on the underlying distribution. To overcome these impossibilities, we follow a consolidated beyond-worst-case approach
(
40
)
, which has already been successfully employed for bilateral trade
(
4
)
, and study smooth distributions. We detail how smoothness helps recovering suitable
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
, achieving the three desiderata sketched above; the argument for gain from trade is more streamlined than that for profit, so we focus on the latter.

Smoothness Implies Fixed Discretizations

For profit maximization, we consider a fixed discretization of the mechanism space which relies on a suitable tiling of the
[0,1]^{2}
square (see the family of simple mechanisms introduced in
Section

3.2
). We argue that the best mechanism in such
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
is nearly optimal, under the smoothness assumption. Note, while this discretization is reminiscent of the ones used in
1
;
31
, we derive original bounds for the generic
p^{\textnormal{th}}
moment of the approximation error (see
Lemma

3
) which are also used to prove the novel uniform convergence bound for profit-maximization with smooth distributions (see Appendix
B
).

Smoothness Implies Fast Concentration

The second, and technically most involved challenge is to show that all mechanisms in
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
have their profit well estimated, up to the
\alpha
tolerated error. Ignoring lower-order terms, the cardinality of
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
is exponential in
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}
, so that combining Hoeffding inequality with a standard union bound would entail a (suboptimal) bound on the sample complexity bound of order
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha^{2}}}\times\log|\hat{\mathcal{M}}|\approx\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha^{3}}}
. We improve on this bound via probabilistic chaining
(
57
)
: for each mechanism
M\in\hat{\mathcal{M}}
we define a sequence of approximating mechanisms of increasing “complexity” and then perform a careful union bound along these chains (we refer to
Figure

2
for a visualization of approximating sequences). We mention that probabilistic chaining is also used in
31
to achieve tight sample complexity bounds for general distributions, however, our use of the mathematical tool is essentially different: there, the authors design a “filtering procedure” to ensure higher moments bounds for a distribution-and-data-dependent chaining decomposition, while here we exploit smoothness to make sure that the chaining analysis carries over on a fixed discretization, via uniform bounds on the errors’
p^{\textnormal{th}}
moments.

Computational Efficiency

The last challenge we need to overcome arises from the need to sample efficiently from the exponential distribution on
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
, whose cardinality is exponential in
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}
. Indeed, in general exponential sampling leads to a running time linear in the support of the distribution. We exploit the geometric structure of the mechanisms to associate each simple mechanism with a path on a suitable graph supported in the
[0,1]^{2}
. We show how to associate weights to its (polynomially many) edges, so that we can simulate perfectly the exponential sampling on mechanisms/paths via random walks on the graphs, by sampling one edge after the other according to these weights. As already mentioned, this approach is inspired from an efficient sampling technique developed in the online learning literature
(
56
)
.

Differential Privacy and Machine Learning.

If the privacy guarantee only extends to the labels, the VC dimension characterizes private learnability in binary classification

Differential Privacy in Mechanism Design.

The interplay of differential privacy and mechanism design has been studied from various angles
(
53
, see, e.g.,)
. A fundamental connection exists between differentially private mechanisms and approximately truthful ones, as both require stability against changes in a single agent’s private type.

Preliminaries

In the bilateral trade problem, a seller holds a single good that a buyer wants to purchase. The seller’s and buyer’s valuations for the good are
v_{s}\in[0,1]
and
v_{b}\in[0,1]
, respectively, and are private information. Seller and buyer submit bids
b_{s},b_{b}\in[0,1]
(not necessarily truthful) to a broker running mechanism
M
, characterized by an allocation region
A\subseteq[0,1]^{2}
, and pricing rules
p,q:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
.
The trade happens if and only if the bids
(b_{s},b_{b})
belong to the allocation region
A
, and payments are made according to
p
and
q
.
That is, the seller receives
p(b_{s},b_{b})
,
while the buyer pays
q(b_{s},b_{b})
.
Without loss of generality, we require that
p(b_{s},b_{b})=q(b_{s},b_{b})=0
whenever
(b_{s},b_{b})\not\in A
, i.e., there is no trade.

The seller and the buyer are rational agents with quasi-linear utility. Namely, when they submit bids
(b_{s},b_{b})\in[0,1]^{2}
but have valuations
v_{b}
and
v_{s}
, the utilities are, respectively:

U_{s}\left(b_{s},b_{b}\right)=\mathds{1}_{\{(b_{s},b_{b})\not\in A\}}\cdot v_{s}+p(b_{s},b_{b}),\quad U_{b}\left(b_{s},b_{b}\right)=\mathds{1}_{\{(b_{s},b_{b})\in A\}}\cdot v_{b}-q(b_{s},b_{b}).

We focus on dominant-strategy incentive compatible (DSIC) and individually rational (IR) mechanisms: each player maximizes his utility by a truthful bid regardless of the other player’s bid, and the utility from participating in the mechanism is at least as high as that from not participating in the mechanism (i.e., it is at least
v_{s}
for the seller and at least
0
for the buyer).
In formulas,

\displaystyle\text{DSIC:}\quad U_{s}\left(v_{s},b_{b}\right)

\displaystyle\geq U_{s}\left(b_{s},b_{b}\right)

\displaystyle\forall\ v_{s}\in[0,1],(b_{s},b_{b})\in[0,1]^{2}

\displaystyle U_{b}\left(b_{s},v_{b}\right)

\displaystyle\geq U_{b}\left(b_{s},b_{b}\right)

\displaystyle\forall\ v_{b}\in[0,1],(b_{s},b_{b})\in[0,1]^{2}

\displaystyle\text{IR}:\quad\;\;\;U_{s}\left(v_{s},b_{b}\right)

\displaystyle\geq v_{s},\,\,U_{b}\left(b_{s},v_{b}\right)\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall\ (v_{s},v_{b})\in[0,1]^{2},(b_{s},b_{b})\in[0,1]^{2}

We denote the class of all DSIC and IR mechanisms by
\mathcal{M}
. Since we focus on such class, in the rest of the paper, we assume that the players’ bids are equal to their respective valuations. We consider two natural objectives: gain-from-trade and profit. The gain-from-trade measures the increase in social welfare resulting from the mechanism, and is a measure of economic efficiency:

\mathrm{GFT}(M,(v_{s},v_{b}))=(v_{b}-v_{s})\cdot\mathds{1}_{\{(v_{s},v_{b})\in A\}}

The profit earned by the broker running mechanism
M

is the difference between the payment the mechanism collects from the buyer and the payment that it makes to the seller:

\textnormal{{profit}}(M,(v_{s},v_{b}))=q(v_{s},v_{b})-p(v_{s},v_{b}).

A mechanism enforces
budget balance
if the profit is always non-negative. For brevity, when it is clear from the context, we may adopt the vectorial notation and replace
(v_{s},v_{b})
with
v.

2.1
Characterization of DSIC Mechanisms

The allocation regions and the pricing functions of DSIC and IR mechanisms have a distinctive structure: the allocation region respects a certain monotonicity property, while the allocation region uniquely induces the payments.

Definition 1

(Monotone Allocation Region)
.

An allocation region
A\subseteq[0,1]^{2}
is monotone if for any
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in A
and
y=(y_{1},y_{2})\in[0,1]^{2}
with
x_{1}\geq y_{1}
and
x_{2}\leq y_{2},
it holds that
y\in A
. A mechanism is monotone if its allocation region is monotone.

Definition 2

(Myerson Payments)
.

Let
A
be a monotone allocation region, then the associated Myerson payments are defined as follows:

\displaystyle p(b_{s},b_{b})=

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{\{(b_{s},b_{b})\in A\}}\cdot\max\{x\in[0,1]\mid(x,b_{b})\in A\}

\displaystyle\forall\;(b_{s},b_{b})\in[0,1]^{2}

\displaystyle q(b_{s},b_{b})=

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{\{(b_{s},b_{b})\in A\}}\cdot\min\{y\in[0,1]\mid(b_{s},y)\in A\}

\displaystyle\forall\;(b_{s},b_{b})\in[0,1]^{2}

In words, for an allocation region to be monotone it should be “closed” in a north-west direction. That is, if a point
(b_{s},b_{b})
is in
A
then any point
(b_{s}^{\prime},b_{b})
with
b_{s}^{\prime}\leq b_{s}
and any point
(b_{s},b_{b}^{\prime})
with
b_{b}^{\prime}\geq b_{b}
should be in
A
as well. The payments of a point
(b_{s},b_{b})\in A
, in turn, correspond to the “east” projection minus the “south” one onto the allocation boundary. See
Figure

1
for an illustration. Note that for the above definition to be well-posed, we require (without loss of generality) that all allocation regions are topologically closed.

2.2
The Learning Task

The goal of this paper is to design learning algorithms for profit and gain-from-trade maximization in bilateral trade that are differentially private. Given sample access to a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
[0,1]^{2}
, we want to find mechanisms that maximize the expected profit or gain-from-trade, when valuations are drawn according to
\mathcal{D}
. Note that the agents’ valuations may be correlated. In the following and henceforth, we explicitly mention the variables and/or distributions with respect to which probability statements are made only when there is any ambiguity. If the context is clear, we omit this information. In general, the sources of randomness we consider are limited to three: the randomness inherent in the sample
S
, the randomness of the fresh sample
v
drawn from the same distribution, and the internal randomness of the differentially private algorithm.

Definition 3
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
\beta\in[0,1]
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a possibly randomized learning algorithm that takes as input i.i.d. samples
S
from
\mathcal{D}
and outputs a mechanism in
\mathcal{M}
. We say that
\mathcal{A}
is
(\alpha,\beta)
-optimal with respect to metric
f
if

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A},S}\!\left(\sup_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}_{v\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\left[f(M,v)-f(\mathcal{A}(S),v)\right]\leq\alpha\right)\geq 1-\beta.

This definition is a standard “PAC-learning” style one: with probability
1-\beta
with respect to the sampling phase and its internal randomization, the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
finds a mechanism
\mathcal{A}(S)
that is at most an additive
\alpha
apart from the best mechanism,
on a fresh sample
.
An important family that we study in this paper is that of the
\sigma
-smooth distributions
(
40
, e.g.,)
, characterized by density functions that are not “too peaked”.

Definition 4

(Smooth Distributions)
.

A distribution
\mathcal{D}
over

2.3
Differential Privacy

We now recall some notions from the differential privacy literature

4

4

Definition 5

For simplicity, we state the definition of differential privacy for

\mathcal{Y}

5

5

Given a

\mathcal{Y}

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}(S)\in\tilde{\mathcal{M}}\right)\leq e^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\in\tilde{\mathcal{M}}\right)

. In this paper, our goal is to learn (good) mechanisms for bilateral trade; hence, the data sets are i.i.d. valuations of the type

. A crucial ingredient in our approach is the

Definition 6

(Sensitivity)

Let

In this paper, we consider finite target space

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}^{\textnormal{exp}}_{f}(S)=M\right)=\frac{\exp\left({\varepsilon\cdot\frac{f(S,M)}{2\Delta_{f}}}\right)}{\sum_{M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp\left({\varepsilon\cdot\frac{f(S,M^{\prime})}{2\Delta_{f}}}\right)}.

The exponential mechanism is

2.4
A Meta Theorem

We present a Meta-Theorem that spells out requirements to achieve, at the same time, approximate optimality and differential privacy. Our approach consists in finding a suitable subfamily
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
of mechanisms, and then running the exponential mechanism on it, using as score function the empirical performance on samples. More precisely, we require a “uniform convergence” bound on
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
, and that such a family is representative enough of the whole mechanism space, i.e., that is not too far from the optimal mechanism.
Since the same argument works for both gain-from-trade and profit, we use
f
as a proxy for both objectives, and
f(\cdot,S)
as its empirical estimate on dataset
S
:

f(M,S)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in S}f(M,v).

Given a finite sub-family
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
of mechanisms, we consider the exponential mechanism supported on
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
that uses
f(\cdot,S)
as its score function. Since both profit and gain from trade have values in
[-1,1]
, then the sensitivity of
f
is upper bounded by
\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}.
In the following meta-theorem we formalize the requirements on
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
to achieve
(\alpha,\beta)
-optimality and
\varepsilon
-differential privacy, along with the corresponding sample complexity required.

Theorem 1
.

(Meta-Theorem)

Fix any
\alpha>0
,
\beta\in[0,1]
, and
\varepsilon>0
, and consider the objective
f
. Let
S
be a random i.i.d. sample of size
n
, with
v
independently drawn from the same distribution.
Assume that there exists a finite family
\hat{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
that respects the following properties:

(i)

It holds that

\sup_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}_{v}\!\left[f(M,v)\right]-\max_{M\in\hat{\mathcal{M}}}\mathbb{E}_{v}\!\left[f(M,v)\right]\leq\frac{\alpha}{4}

(ii)

There exists
n_{0}=n_{0}(\alpha,\beta)
such that when
|S|\geq n_{0}
, the following inequality holds with probability at least
1-\nicefrac{{\beta}}{{2}}
over the sampling of
S

\max_{M\in\hat{\mathcal{M}}}|\mathbb{E}_{v}\!\left[f(M,v)\right]-f(M,S)|\leq\frac{\alpha}{4}.

If
|S|\geq n_{0}+\frac{16\log(\nicefrac{{2\lvert\hat{\mathcal{M}}\rvert}}{{\beta}})}{\alpha\varepsilon}
, then there exists a randomized learning algorithm that is
(\alpha,\beta)
-optimal and
\varepsilon
-differentially private.

The proof of the theorem is deferred to Appendix
C.2
. We conclude with some comments on the two properties required by the meta-theorem. Property (i) states that the subfamily
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
contains a mechanism whose performance is
\alpha
-close to the optimal value that
f
may achieve, i.e., that
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
has a small
discretization error
with respect to
f
. On the other hand, property (ii) requires that we have enough samples to obtain
uniform convergence
in the subfamily; stated differently, the objective
f
is well estimated over all the mechanisms in
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
.

Theorem 3

Fix any

Proof.

We slightly abuse notation and denote with

\phi_{h}\left(\cdot\right)

As a first step, we relate the uniform convergence error in

Claim 2

Let

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S}\!\left[\max_{M\in\hat{\mathcal{M}}}\Bigl\lvert\textnormal{{profit}}(M,S)-\mathbb{E}_{v}\!\left[\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v)\right]\Bigr\rvert\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\left(\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}_{S,\textbf{g}}\!\left[\max_{M\in\hat{\mathcal{M}}}\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h+1}(M),v_{i})-\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h}(M),v_{i}))\cdot g_{i}\right\rvert\right]+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi n}}\right)

Given this result, we have to control the expected maximum appearing inside the term above, across all levels of approximation. To do this, we define a target clean event

and for every mechanism

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h+1}(M),v_{i})-\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h}(M),v_{i})\right)^{2}<35n\cdot 2^{-h}\cdot\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sigma}}

As long as

.

5

where, for brevity, we omit the dependence on

Similarly, we can bound the second moment of

As a last ingredient to apply Bernstein, we observe that

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{3n\cdot 2^{-h}\cdot\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sigma}}}{788}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{3n\cdot\alpha}{6304}\right)

(As

)

\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{i}\right]\leq 34\cdot\nicefrac{{2^{-h}}}{{\sigma}}.
At this point, we union bound over all the levels

We can go back to studying the right-hand-side term appearing in

Claim 4

(Bounding the links in the chain)
.

The following inequality holds:

\mathbb{E}_{S,\textbf{g}}\!\left[\max_{M\in\hat{\mathcal{M}}}\Bigl\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h+1}(M),v_{i})-\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h}(M),v_{i}))\cdot g_{i}\Bigr\rvert\,\Big|\,\mathcal{E}\right]\leq\frac{22}{\sqrt{{\sigma n}}}

.

Since we are conditioning on

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h+1}(M),v_{i})-\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h}(M),v_{i})\right)^{2}<35\cdot 2^{-h}\cdot\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sigma}}

(3)

For any fixed realization of the sample

\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_{\boldsymbol{g}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h+1}(M),v_{i})-\textnormal{{profit}}(\phi_{h}(M),v_{i}))\cdot g_{i}\right)\leq\nicefrac{{35}}{{n}}\cdot 2^{-h}\cdot\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sigma}}.

Let’s now take a step back: in

Claim 5
.

If

, then the following uniform convergence bound holds:

\mathbb{E}_{S}\!\left[\max_{M\in\hat{\mathcal{M}}}\Bigl\lvert\textnormal{{profit}}(M,S)-\mathbb{E}_{v}\!\left[\textnormal{{profit}}(M,v)\right]\Bigr\rvert\right]\leq\log\left(\frac{n}{\sigma}\right)\frac{145}{\sqrt{n\sigma}}

5

6
in Appendix

). To this end, we only need to argue that the uniform convergence error

\mathbb{P}\left(U(S)\geq\mathbb{E}\left[U(S)\right]+t\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{nt^{2}}{2}\right).

\mathbb{P}\left(U(S)\geq\nicefrac{{\alpha}}{{4}}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(U(S)-\mathbb{E}\left[U(S)\right]\geq\sqrt{\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}\log\nicefrac{{2}}{{\beta}}}\right)\leq\nicefrac{{\beta}}{{2}},

(5)

We have all the ingredients to invoke

which uses as score function the empirical profit verifies the following theorem.

Theorem 4
.

Fix any
\alpha>0,\beta\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon>0
. Let
S
be a
\sigma
-smooth i.i.d. sample of size
n
. There exists a polynomial-time randomized learning algorithm which is
(\alpha,\beta)
-optimal and
\varepsilon
-differentially private for

n\geq\frac{40^{5}\cdot\log^{2}(\nicefrac{{50}}{{\alpha\sigma}})}{\alpha^{2}\sigma}+\frac{128\log\nicefrac{{2}}{{\beta}}}{\alpha^{2}}+\frac{256\log(\nicefrac{{2}}{{\beta}})}{\alpha^{2}\sigma\varepsilon}\in\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}\sigma}\left[\log^{2}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha\sigma}})+\frac{\log\nicefrac{{1}}{{\beta}}}{\varepsilon}\right]\right)

The only part of the Theorem that we still need to prove concerns how to run the exponential mechanism on the exponentially large
\hat{\mathcal{M}}
. We present here a computationally efficient sampling routine, deferring the proofs to Appendix
C.6
. Before proceeding, recall the definition of the directed graph
G_{\eta}=(V_{\eta},E_{\eta})
as in
Section

3.2
:
V_{\eta}
is given by the points in the uniform grid of step size
\eta
, while the edges
E_{\eta}
are the natural ones from the grid, oriented either from left to right or bottom-up.

Definition 8
.

A path
\pi
on
G_{\eta}
is complete if it starts in
(0,0)
and ends in
(1,1)
. A complete path
\pi
identifies a mechanism
M_{\pi}\!\in\!\mathcal{M}_{\eta}
of allocation region

A_{\pi}\!=\!\{y\in[0,1]^{2}\!:\!\exists x\in\pi\text{ {s.t.} }x_{1}\geq y_{1},x_{2}\leq y_{2}\}.

From now on, we thus consider only the set of complete paths
in
G_{\eta}
, which we denote as
\mathcal{P}_{\eta}
. There is a natural bijection between mechanisms in
\mathcal{M}_{\eta}
and paths
\mathcal{P}_{\eta}
, so if we consider the exponential mechanism over
\mathcal{M}_{\eta}
, we can equivalently consider a suitable distribution
\mu
over
\mathcal{P}_{\eta}
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
S
be a sample of
n
valuations, and let
\mu
be the distribution on
\mathcal{P}_{\eta}
:

\mu(\pi)=\frac{\exp(n\cdot\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{4}}\cdot\textnormal{{profit}}(M_{\pi},S))}{\sum_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{\eta}}\exp(n\cdot\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{4}}\cdot\textnormal{{profit}}(M_{\pi^{\prime}},S))}\quad\forall\,\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\eta}

(6)

There exist edge probabilities
\{q_{e}\}_{e\in E_{\eta}}
, computable in time
O(\nicefrac{{n}}{{\eta^{2}}})
, such that:

(i)

q_{e}\in[0,1]\ \forall\,e\in E_{\eta}
.

(ii)

\sum_{e\in E^{+}_{\eta}(u)}q_{e}=1\ \forall\,u\in V_{\eta}
, where
E^{+}_{\eta}(u)
is the set of outgoing edges from node
u
in
E_{\eta}
.

(iii)

\prod_{e\in\pi}q_{e}=\mu(\pi)\ \forall\,\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\eta}
, almost surely with respect to the sampling of
S
.

The theorem implies we can
simulate
the distribution
\mu
supported over paths with a random walk on
V_{\eta}
, starting at
(0,0)
and ending in
(1,1)
, which builds the sampled path “one edge at a time”. Note, the number of weights to keep in memory is then polynomial (as
|E_{\eta}|\in O(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\eta^{2}}})
).

We conclude the section by explaining how to compute these
q_{e}
, in polynomial time. As a first step, we introduce a notion of edge-weights
w_{e}
, in such a way that the profit of a generic mechanism on a sample set can be decomposed as the sum of the logarithm of the weights of its edges:
\textnormal{{profit}}(M_{\pi},S)\propto\sum_{e\in\pi}\log w_{e}
.

Definition 9

(Weighting the edges in
G_{\eta}
)
.

Consider an edge
e
in
G_{\eta}
. We define its interest region
A_{e}
:

•

A_{e}=[0,{k}{\eta}]\times[{j}{\eta},{(j+1)}{\eta}]
if
e
is a vertical edge connecting
({k}{\eta},{j}{\eta})
to
({k}{\eta},{(j+1)}{\eta})
.

•

A_{e}=[{k}{\eta},{(k+1)}{\eta}]\times[{j}{\eta},1]
if
e
is a horizontal edge connecting
({k}{\eta},{j}{\eta})
to
({(k+1)}{\eta},{j}{\eta})
.

Let
S=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
be a sample on
n
valuations. We assign weights
w_{e}
to the edges of
G_{\eta}
:

w_{e}=\begin{cases}\exp\left(-\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{4}}\cdot{k}{\eta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\{v_{i}\in A_{e}\}}\right)\ &\text{if $e$ connects $({k}{\eta},{j}{\eta})$ to $({k}{\eta},{(j+1)}{\eta})$.}\\
\exp\left(\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{4}}\cdot{j}{\eta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\{v_{i}\in A_{e}\}}\right)\ &\text{if $e$ connects $({k}{\eta},{j}{\eta})$ to $({(k+1)}{\eta},{j}{\eta})$.}\end{cases}

The second step of the construction consists in deriving the right “transition probabilities”.
Since each step in a random walk on
G_{\eta}
presents a choice between at most two possible next nodes, or equivalently two edges, we define these transitions by balancing the immediate edge weight with the
profit
of the paths that follow. This leads to our second intuition: weighting nodes recursively by summing the
profit
contributions of all possible
future
paths stemming from them.

Definition 10

(Weighting the nodes in
G_{\eta}
)
.

Let
u
be a generic vertex of
G_{\eta}
and let
\mathcal{N}^{+}_{\eta}(u)
be its out-neighborhood. Let
w_{(1,1)}=1
and recursively define the weight of
u
as
w_{u}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{N}^{+}_{\eta}(u)}w_{(u,s)}w_{s}
.

:
To the left, a path (in red) and its induced allocation region (in blue) are illustrated. To the right, four interest regions (as defined in
Definition

9
) are shown for four different edges: two vertical and two horizontal.

Due to the recursive definition, we stress that
\{w_{u}\}_{u\in V_{\eta}}
can be computed by dynamic programming in polynomial time. At any point in the random walk, given
\{w_{e}\}_{e\in E_{\eta}}
and
\{w_{u}\}_{u\in V_{\eta}}
, we can now account for both the immediate contribution of the next edge to
profit
and the aggregated contribution of all potential future paths. The natural idea is then multiplying the two and normalizing to get probabilities. More specifically, for any node
u\neq(1,1)
in
V_{\eta}
, if
s\notin\mathcal{N}^{+}_{\eta}(u)
, then we define
q_{(u,s)}=0
; if
\mathcal{N}^{+}_{\eta}(u)=\{s\}
, then we define
q_{(u,s)}=1
; if
\mathcal{N}^{+}_{\eta}(u)=\{s,r\}
:

q_{(u,s)}=\frac{w_{(u,s)}w_{s}}{w_{(u,s)}w_{s}+w_{(u,r)}w_{r}}\quad\quad q_{(u,r)}=\frac{w_{(u,r)}w_{r}}{w_{(u,s)}w_{s}+w_{(u,r)}w_{r}}.

(7)

This concludes the presentation of the efficient sampling routine. We conclude this section with a remark on the implementability of our approach. Namely, we comment on what the algorithm can do if it does not know the smoothness parameter
\sigma
(or a constant upper bound of it).

Remark 1

(On not knowing
\sigma
)
.

We can run the exponential mechanism on
\mathcal{M}_{\eta}
with
\eta=\nicefrac{{\alpha}}{{8}}
, irrespective of

. With minor adjustments to the previous analysis, it can be shown that

samples are sufficient to (privately) obtain a
(\nicefrac{{\alpha}}{{\sigma}},\beta)
-optimal mechanism. The latter bound is of the same order as that in

4
, modulo rescaling accordingly the desired precision parameter.

4.1
Private Learning is Impossible for General Distributions

In this section, we argue that, in general, it is not possible to privately learn the best fixed-price mechanism for bilateral trade. We report the statement of the impossibility result, and a proof sketch, while the complete argument is deferred to Appendix
C.7
.

Theorem 6
.

For any learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for gain from trade maximization, precision parameter
\alpha\in(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{80}})
, privacy parameter
\varepsilon>0
and number of samples
N
, there exists a hard distribution
\mathcal{D}
for which the learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on
N
i.i.d. samples cannot be
\varepsilon
-differentially private and satisfy:

\sup_{p\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{GFT}(p,v)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{GFT}(\mathcal{A}(S),v)\right]+\alpha,

where the expectation is with respect to
S
and
v
drawn from
\mathcal{D}
, and the randomization of
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof Sketch.

We construct a “hard” family of distributions, indexed by the uniform grid
\Gamma
over the interval
[\nicefrac{{9}}{{20}},\nicefrac{{11}}{{20}}]
, with step-size
\xi
, to set later. To each such
\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma
, we associate a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{i}
with the following two properties: (i) the optimal price given
\mathcal{D}_{i}
is
\gamma_{i}
and (ii) any price “far” from
\gamma_{i}
results in a gain from trade that is suboptimal by more than
\alpha
.

Consider now any learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
. By point (ii), for each
\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma
there should exist a sample
S_{i}
such that
\mathcal{A}(S_{i})
posts a price close to
\gamma_{i}
, let’s say in some neighborhood
I_{i}
of it, but far from
all the other

\gamma_{j}\in\Gamma
, so that
I_{i}\cap I_{j}=\emptyset.
For the generic pair
\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\in\Gamma
, the corresponding samples
S_{i}
and
S_{j}
may be different, but
for sure
, they differ by at most
N
points. Therefore, we can construct a sequence of sets
S_{i}=S^{0}_{i,j},S^{1}_{i,j},\dots,S^{N}_{i,j}=S_{j}
such that
S^{k}_{i,j}
and
S^{k+1}_{i,j}
differ by at most one point. We can then repeatedly apply the definition of
\varepsilon
-differentially private algorithm on
\mathcal{A}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}\!\left(\mathcal{A}(S_{i})\in I_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}\!\left(\mathcal{A}(S^{1}_{i,j})\in I_{j}\right)

(
S_{i}
and
S^{1}_{i,j}
differ in one point)

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\varepsilon}\left[e^{-\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}\!\left(\mathcal{A}(S^{2}_{i,j})\in I_{j}\right)\right]

(
S^{1}_{i,j}
and
S^{2}_{i,j}
differ in one point)

\displaystyle\geq e^{-N\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}\!\left(\mathcal{A}(S_{j})\in I_{j}\right)

(iterating
N
times)

Now, in order to be
\alpha
-optimal, the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
should respect that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}(S_{i})\in I_{i}\right)
is greater than some constant, let’s say
\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}
, for all
i
. Since the
I_{i}
are all disjoint, we have

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}\!\left(\mathcal{A}(S_{i})\notin I_{i}\right)\geq\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}\!\left(\mathcal{A}(S_{i})\in I_{j}\right)\geq\left(\frac{1}{\xi}-2\right)e^{-N\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}\!\left(\mathcal{A}(S_{i})\in I_{i}\right)\geq\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{1}{\xi}-2\right)e^{-N\varepsilon}.

We can take
\xi
as small as possible, while
\varepsilon
and
N
are fixed, thus arriving to the contradiction that the mechanism proposed on sample
S_{i}
prefers posting away from
\gamma_{i}
, thus violating (i) and (ii).
∎

4.2

Given accuracy
