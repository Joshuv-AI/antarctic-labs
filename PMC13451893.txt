Light thinning promotes large-diameter tree growth and stand volume in Eucalyptus–Castanopsis hystrix mixed forests
Authors: Junmo Xu, Qiuhai Chen, Yongjie Huang, Shaohong Deng, Jinlei Chen, Dongjing Sun, Yuanguang Wen, Zhi Nong, Junting Wu, Jichao Li, Xiaoguo Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryShort-rotation Eucalyptus plantations struggle to produce large-diameter timber. Mixed plantations of Eucalyptus with the native climax tree Castanopsis hystrix under a multi-cycle management regime offer a strategy to balance short-term wood production with long-term timber goals. In a long-term field experiment, a gradient of strip-thinning intensities (0%–100% Eucalyptus removal) was applied to a mixed stand in southern China. Five years later, neighborhood-based spatial indices showed that light thinning created a random tree distribution, moderate species mingling, and competitive dominance of Eucalyptus. This spatial configuration maximized total stand volume (+12.1% vs. unthinned control) and promoted the development of large-diameter Eucalyptus trees, whereas heavy thinning favored large-diameter C. hystrix. Large-diameter trees of both species consistently occupied microsites with random distribution and dominant status but differed in their mingling needs. These findings provide a mechanistic, neighborhood-based rationale for thinning prescriptions that reconcile high productivity with large-diameter timber cultivation in mixed-species plantations.

Introduction

Eucalyptus is one of the most economically important fast-growing timber genera worldwide, known for its broad adaptability and high economic value.1,2 Eucalyptus plantations in China have increased to more than 5.4 million hectares by the end of 2018, have an indelible contribution to the world timber supply and ecological balance.3 However, driven by rising timber demand, the rotation cycle of Eucalyptus plantations has been reduced to merely 5–7 years. This “clear-cut” or “whole-stand” management regime yields only small-to medium-diameter logs in the short term and has inevitably triggered ecological issues including soil degradation and diminished biodiversity.4,5 More critically, this ultra-short-rotation practice fundamentally conflicts with China’s strategic long-term goal of cultivating large-diameter, high-quality timber.6

To overcome the drawbacks associated with monoculture plantations, establishing mixed-species stands of Eucalyptus with precious native trees is considered a highly prospective sustainable silvicultural strategy.7,8 C. hystrix, for instance, is a high-value climax species with considerable ecosystem stability. Its large-scale monoculture development, however, is hindered by biological traits such as slow initial growth and a requirement for lateral shade in the early stages.9,10 A Eucalyptus-C. hystrix mixture can improve stand stability via species complementarity, enhance soil fertility, and boost biodiversity. Moreover, such a system offers the potential to generate short-term income while establishing the basis for medium-to long-term production of large-diameter timber.11 This mixed-plantation model facilitates the synergistic realization of ecological and economic objectives.12

However, simple species mixing alone does not fundamentally address the inherent conflict in wood harvest cycles. To concurrently address the market’s pressing need for short-term wood and the national strategic requirement for large-diameter timber, we propose a multi-cycle (short-, medium-, and long-term) integrated management model termed “eco-silviculture”.11,13 The model’s essence involves three phases: initially, leveraging fast-growing Eucalyptus to quickly establish an overstory canopy, which creates a suitable micro-environment for C. hystrix while generating early income. In the intermediate phase, regulated reduction of Eucalyptus density meets ongoing demand for Eucalyptus wood while progressively liberating the growth potential of C. hystrix. The long-term objective is to develop a stable ecosystem yielding high-value, large-diameter timber from both Eucalyptus and C. hystrix.11 Through deliberate structural design, the stand is engineered to deliver multiple outputs concurrently within the same space: short-term commodity wood, intermediate-term revenue, and long-term large-diameter timber. This necessitates management interventions that can fine-tune the competition for spatial resources among species and across tree cohorts. Thinning is a pivotal technical operation to achieve this objective.14

As the core anthropogenic measure in forest management, the essence of thinning lies in adjusting tree density to free up growing space for the remaining trees.15 Thinning operations of varying intensity mitigate competition among trees for resources like water, nutrients, and light to different extents.16,17 In the context of large-diameter timber cultivation, a certain degree of canopy and root competition is essential for stimulating radial growth and achieving superior stem form.18 Consequently, scientifically based and appropriate thinning is an indispensable pathway for guiding the transition of forest stands from “short-rotation plantations” to “large-diameter timber cultivation”.19 Nonetheless, conventional thinning studies have predominantly concentrated on pure stands, with a focus on stock volume responses. In the complex system of uneven-aged, multi-layered mixed forests, identifying the thinning intensity that optimally balances short-term yield and long-term large-diameter timber production remains an open scientific question. Therefore, a deep comprehension of the underlying mechanisms of thinning necessitates an investigation at the stand spatial structure level.

Stand spatial structure reflects the internal characteristics of a stand, including interspecific relationships and the spatial arrangement of individual trees relative to their neighbors.20 Numerous methods are available for characterizing stand spatial structure, including Ripley’s K(r) function, pairwise correlation function and O-ring function.21,22,23 Regrettably, a limitation is that these methods operate at the stand level and lack the precision to capture the fine-scale spatial structural characteristics of the microenvironment surrounding individual trees.24,25 By comparison, certain neighborhood-based metrics are capable of quantifying the spatial structure for each individual tree: specifically, the uniform angle index (W, characterizing spatial distribution patterns), the mingling index (M, gauging degree of species mixing), and the dominance index (U, indicating the competitive status between trees).26 So far, despite the theoretical understanding of the linkages between thinning, spatial structure, and tree growth,27 an empirical study situating these relationships within the complex silvicultural system of “uneven-aged, multi-storied mixed forests of Eucalyptus and C. hystrix” is still lacking. More specifically, a critical process that needs to be quantified is how varying intensities of strip thinning modify stand spatial structure parameters (W, M, and U indices), which in turn affect the regeneration growth of the second Eucalyptus rotation, the development of large-diameter trees from the first generation of Eucalyptus and C. hystrix, and finally govern the dynamics of total stand volume. We posit that low-intensity thinning, rather than high-intensity intervention, offers the greatest potential to optimize the overall spatial structure of the stand while partially harvesting Eucalyptus (for immediate yield). This optimization would involve achieving a more desirable tree spatial pattern, fostering facilitative species interactions, and enhancing the competitive position of target large-diameter trees, consequently leading to the maximization of total stand volume (encompassing both immediate harvest and long-term residual volume).

Consequently, this study utilized a long-term permanent plot established in 2012 (Figure 1), where gradient strip thinning (0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%) of Eucalyptus (E. urophylla × E. grandis) was implemented in 2019 (Figure 2), with stump coppice regeneration promoted across all thinned treatments. A complete stand inventory was carried out in 2024, five years after the thinning intervention. The objective was to systematically explore the integrated impact of varying thinning intensities on the growth and spatial structure of this uneven-aged, multi-storied mixed forest of Eucalyptus and C. hystrix, with a focus on resolving these key issues: (1) how different thinning intensities influence the growth dynamics of both Eucalyptus and C. hystrix and the resulting stand volume structure; (2) how thinning remodels the stand spatial pattern by modifying key structural parameters including the uniform angle index (W), mingling index (M), and dominance index (U); (3) how the development and spatial distribution patterns of large-diameter trees respond to thinning-induced structural alterations; (4) which thinning intensity most effectively balances the dual goals of achieving immediate timber yield and fostering long-term large-diameter timber production. By analyzing the cause-and-effect pathway of “thinning intensity—spatial structure—large-diameter tree development—stand volume increase,” the results will offer key theoretical support and technical specifications for the integrated management of Eucalyptus-based mixed forests. Results

Growth responses to thinning among Eucalyptus generations and C. hystrix

In order to understand how thinning affects the productivity of the multi-cycle management system in the Eucalyptus and C. hystrix mixed forest, the growth responses of both tree species were examined. Results revealed that the influence of thinning on tree growth differed depending on the species and tree generation (Figure 3). For Eucalyptus at the stand level, the mean diameter at breast height (DBH), tree height, and individual tree volume followed a pattern of initial increase followed by a decline as thinning intensity increased. The moderate thinning (MT) and light thinning (LT) stands exhibited significantly greater values compared to other treatments (p < 0.05; Figures 3A–3C). For C. hystrix, no statistically significant differences were found in these growth indicators across thinning treatments (p = 0.878), yet the mean values tended to be comparatively higher in the heavy thinning (HT) and MT stands (Figures 3A–3C).Figure 3Effects of different thinning intensities on mean DBH, tree height, ITV and stand volume of Eucalyptus-C. hystrix mixed forests(A) Mean DBH, (B) mean tree height, (C) mean individual tree volume, and (D) stand volume. A more detailed analysis of the different Eucalyptus generations uncovered even more intricate competition dynamics. For the first-generation Eucalyptus (12 a), mean DBH, tree height, and ITV showed significant increases as thinning intensity rose from 0% to 75% (Figures 3A–3C), demonstrating that the growth of established trees is highly responsive to reduced competition. Conversely, the growth metrics for the coppice-regenerated second-generation Eucalyptus (5 a) displayed a “V”-shaped pattern (Figures 3A–3C), where their growth was inhibited in the HT and MT stands.

These divergent growth responses directly shaped the stand volume structure. The standing stock volume of Eucalyptus declined significantly as thinning intensity increased (p < 0.05; Figure 3D). In contrast, the volume of C. hystrix showed no significant variation across treatments (p = 0.339; Figure 3D). Specifically, in the LT stand, the present standing volume was chiefly comprised of first-generation Eucalyptus, with the second generation maintaining considerable volume potential. Markedly different was the clear-thinning (CT) stand, where the standing volume consisted solely of second-generation Eucalyptus, reflecting a complete reorientation of management goals toward short-rotation production.

Effects of thinning intensity on total stand volume and its components

The response of total stand volume (i.e., the sum of harvested volume removed during thinning and standing stock volume at final inventory) to thinning intensity revealed a distinct optimal operational range. As illustrated in Figure 4A, the LT treatment yielded the greatest total stock volume, representing a 12.1% increase over the control (CK). Thinning intensity concurrently governed the proportional contribution from different volume sources. In stands under high-intensity thinning (HT, CT), the proportion of harvested volume was higher, while the share of standing volume was lower. In contrast, the LT stand demonstrated a distinct pattern, achieving a substantial harvest volume while preserving a high level of standing stock (Figure 4A).

Analysis of cumulative standing stock volume across diameter classes provided mechanistic insight into the superiority of the LT treatment (Figure 4B). Prior to the 16 cm diameter class, the cumulative stock volume in the LT stand trailed behind that of the MT, HT, and CT stands. However, after the 18 cm diameter class, the LT stand’s cumulative volume overtook the others and consistently exceeded that of the MT, HT, and CK stands. This dynamic pattern suggests that the advantage in total stock volume for the LT stand is largely attributable to the volume accumulated within larger-diameter trees.

Influence of thinning intensity on the development of large-diameter trees

Analysis of the number and cumulative volume of large-diameter trees for both Eucalyptus and C. hystrix indicated that the LT treatment yielded the greatest number and total volume of large-diameter trees (combined species) (Figure 5C). Thinning intensity differentially influenced the cultivation of large-diameter trees for the two species. These species-specific responses create a pronounced trade-off in thinning management. The HT treatment, although optimal for maximizing large-diameter C. hystrix development, substantially reduced both the number of large-diameter Eucalyptus trees and the total stand volume. The MT treatment provided intermediate outcomes for both species but did not maximize either. In contrast, the LT treatment exhibited a superior balance: it simultaneously achieved the highest density and volume of large-diameter Eucalyptus, attained the greatest total stand volume among all treatments, and sustained an intermediate level of large-diameter C. hystrix development. This balanced outcome positions LT as the thinning intensity that most effectively accommodates the dual management objectives of producing large-diameter Eucalyptus timber while maintaining viable C. hystrix development within the same stand.

Concerning the spatial association of species, the stand-level mingling index (W¯) showed a unimodal response to thinning intensity, increasing from CK to MT and declining thereafter (Figure 6B). Mingling was highest in the MT stand, where the balanced retention of Eucalyptus and C. hystrix maximized interspecific adjacency. The CT stand, by contrast, exhibited the lowest mingling, consistent with the near-complete dominance of a single species (C. hystrix) in the overstory.

With respect to inter-tree competition, Eucalyptus was dominant over C. hystrix in the CK and LT stands (Figure 6C). With higher thinning intensities, Eucalyptus and C. hystrix exhibited roughly equal dominance in the MT stand, whereas dominance shifted to C. hystrix in the HT and CT stands. Thus, thinning intensity resulted in differentiated pathways for modulating spatial structure: LT promoted a structure characterized by Eucalyptus dominance, random tree distribution, and intermediate mingling. MT maximized species mingling but concurrently heightened competition among the remaining trees, resulting in a merely marginal advantage for Eucalyptus over C. hystrix. The high-intensity treatment (HT and CT) simplified the structure: the HT stand retained a random distribution and elevated mingling but shifted competitive dominance to C. hystrix; the CT stand reverted to a regular, monospecific-dominated configuration with low mingling and full C. hystrix dominance. These structural trajectories provide the mechanistic context for interpreting the species-specific growth and large-diameter tree responses reported above.

Spatial microenvironment of large-diameter trees

To identify the microenvironments that support large-diameter tree development, we shifted the analysis from stand-level mean indices to the individual tree scale, examining the spatial structure of the four nearest neighbors (W, M, U at the single-tree level) for all large-diameter trees. Data were pooled across thinning treatments to reveal species-specific associations that are robust across the experimental gradient.

The frequency distributions of the three spatial parameters revealed both shared microhabitat requirements and a key species-specific contrast between large-diameter Eucalyptus and C. hystrix trees (Figure 7). Regarding competitive status, the overwhelming majority of large-diameter trees of both species occupied dominant or subdominant positions (U ≤ 0.25; Eucalyptus 94%, C. hystrix 96%; Figures 7A and 7D). Eucalyptus exhibited a stronger tendency toward absolute dominance (85% at U = 0.00), whereas C. hystrix showed a more balanced distribution between dominant (70% at U = 0.00) and subdominant individuals. For horizontal spatial pattern, a random distribution of neighbors (W = 0.50) was the most frequent category for both species (Eucalyptus 43%, C. hystrix 44%; Figures 7B and 7E). Taken together, large-diameter trees of both species typically occupy microhabitats characterized by low competitive pressure and a random spatial arrangement. The contrasting mingling requirements—low to intermediate for Eucalyptus vs. high to complete for C. hystrix—represent a fundamental ecological distinction that underpins the species-specific optimal spatial structure units defined in below.

Response of optimal spatial structure units to thinning intensity

In order to determine which thinning intensity promotes large-diameter tree development, we established species-specific “optimal spatial structure units” based on the earlier results: for Eucalyptus (W = 0.50, U = 0, M = 0.25 or 0.50) and for C. hystrix (W = 0.50, U = 0, M = 0.75 or 1.00). The percentage of trees conforming to these units was then quantified for each thinning treatment. The results distinctly illustrate the differential impact of thinning (Figure 8). For Eucalyptus, the proportion of trees occupying its optimal spatial unit was highest in the LT stand (36.4%), exceeding values in other treatments (CK: 31.8%; MT: 20.5%; HT: 6.8%; CT: 4.5%) (Figure 8A). This suggests that light thinning is most effective at generating the ideal microenvironment for Eucalyptus to develop into large-diameter trees. For C. hystrix, the percentage of trees meeting its optimal unit criteria increased with thinning intensity, reaching 28.0% under the CT treatment (Figure 8B). Discussion

Generation-specific growth responses of Eucalyptus and C. hystrix to thinning intensity

Our results demonstrate that light to moderate thinning intensities (25%–50%) optimally enhance the average DBH and height growth of Eucalyptus. This is consistent with established silvicultural principles and a body of prior research, which posit that appropriate thinning lowers stand density, mitigates inter-tree competition for resources like light, moisture, and nutrients, and consequently stimulates the growth of remaining trees.28,29 However, the generation-specific analysis revealed more complex dynamics. First-generation Eucalyptus (12-year old), which formed the dominant overstory, showed a continuous positive growth response to increasing thinning intensity, with the greatest gains observed under HT. This implies that the growth of these nearly mature Eucalyptus trees (according to fast-rotation timber production criteria) has been chronically constrained by strong within-species competition. The growing space made available even by intensive thinning can be quickly captured and converted into substantial increases in both DBH and height.30 In marked contrast, second-generation (5-year old) coppice displayed a V-shaped response: growth was suppressed under moderate to heavy thinning but maximized after clear thinning. This reinforces the pivotal importance of light competition in vertical stand structure.31 Clear thinning created an extensive canopy opening, offering light-demanding Eucalyptus coppice a near-optimal high-light environment that activates rapid growth.32

For C. hystrix, growth was numerically highest under HT, though not statistically significant, suggesting that shade-tolerant, late-successional species may need stronger disturbance to overcome competitive limitations.33 High-intensity thinning provides more crown space, converting larger photosynthetic area into growth benefit.34 Under clear thinning, C. hystrix had maximum growing space but slightly lower DBH and height than under HT, possibly due to pronounced intraspecific competition, since facilitative effects of species mixture appear more beneficial for C. hystrix stand development.35 Its long-term performance warrants continued observation. Therefore, the divergent responses exhibited by different species and tree generations co-existing in the same mixed stand highlight the intricacy of managing uneven-aged, multi-storied forests. This necessitates forest management interventions that are highly targeted and precise.

Thinning shifts stand spatial pattern from uniform to random and alters species dominance

Stand spatial structure is a fundamental determinant of tree growth, regeneration, and successional dynamics within forest community.36 It regulates variations in soil temperature, nutrient availability, water content, and microbial communities, thereby influencing the structural stability of forest ecosystems.37 In our study, light to heavy thinning shifted the stand spatial pattern from the uniform distribution (CK) to a random distribution (LT, MT, and HT). This transition carries ecological significance: random distribution is regarded as more akin to the pattern found in natural forests, allowing trees to exploit spatial resources more efficiently, minimize direct competition between individuals, and consequently promote stand stability and productivity.38 A thinning experiment on Pinus massoniana by Wen et al.39 similarly found that thinned stands became randomly distributed with improved light conditions, while unthinned stands remained uniform with fierce competition.

Moreover, high species mingling underpins the ability of mixed-species stands to deliver ecological services such as biodiversity maintenance and pest regulation.40,41,42 Moderate thinning resulted in the greatest stand mingling, suggesting it was most effective in fostering spatial mixture of Eucalyptus, C. hystrix, and native species. This aligns with the intermediate disturbance hypothesis, where moderate disturbance prevents niche monopolization while preserving favorable site conditions.43 The dominance index revealed roughly equal dominance between the two species under MT. As thinning intensity increased further, dominance shifted from Eucalyptus to C. hystrix in the HT and CT (Figure 6C). Intensive Eucalyptus removal liberated growing space for C. hystrix crown expansion; however, as the C. hystrix canopy closed in these heavily thinned stands, understory light availability was reduced, potentially limiting the recruitment of subordinate vegetation. This interpretation is consistent with Deng et al.44 who found that moderate thinning suitably reduced stand density, enhanced the growth of residual plants, and increased understory species richness, while HT markedly decreased species diversity. Notably, peak species mingling (MT) did not coincide with maximum stand volume (achieved under LT), indicating a trade-off between ecological (high mingling) and economic (high volume) goals. By sustaining intermediate mingling while creating spatial structure most conducive to Eucalyptus growth, LT offers an optimal compromise for timber-oriented management.

Large-diameter trees share common spatial prerequisites but differ in mingling needs

Growing evidence suggests that structural diversity may exert a stronger influence on forest productivity than species diversity,45,46,47 yet most studies rely on stand-level averages that obscure individual-scale heterogeneity.45,48 Our results provide empirical support for this emerging paradigm at the individual-tree scale. Large-diameter trees of both Eucalyptus and C. hystrix share two spatial features: a random distribution (W = 0.50) and dominant competitive status (U = 0). This suggests that what matters for large-size development is not species richness but the specific spatial arrangement of neighbors. The key ingredients are low competitive pressure and asymmetric access to growing space. By decoding the spatial structure of large-diameter Eucalyptus and C. hystrix trees at the individual level, our study reveals their “niche signature”. Zhang et al.49 noted that optimal spatial structure units maximize productivity and ecosystem service potential. Consistent with this concept, our findings show that large-diameter trees of both species overwhelmingly occupied a dominant competitive status (U = 0) and random spatial pattern (W = 0.50) (Figure 7). Thus, “competitive superiority” and “access to asymmetric growing space” are universal preconditions for large-diameter development. Random distribution enables light from multiple sides, fostering asymmetric competition and consistent productivity.50 In contrast, uniform patterns in CK and CT promote symmetric competition, canopy closure, and high stand density, hindering large-diameter formation.51

Despite these shared prerequisites, the two species diverged fundamentally in their mingling requirements. Large-diameter Eucalyptus trees preferred low-to-intermediate mingling (M = 0.25–0.5, 66%; Figure 7A). This aligns with its traits as a fast-growing pioneer species characterized by high light demand and high transpiration.52 Eucalyptus trees generally develop narrow crowns and exhibit fast vertical growth, allocating biomass preferentially to the main stem to rapidly dominate the canopy layer. By alleviating competition, moderate thinning enables the remaining trees to acquire more light, thus sustaining enhanced photosynthetic rates and resource utilization.52 Additionally, in young unthinned (closed canopy) stands, lower branches are shed rapidly, but in thinned stands, lower branches remain well lit and contribute more to tree growth.53 Consistent with this, Eucalyptus benefits from moderate interspecific competition (low-to-intermediate mingling), whereas excessive mingling could limit growth via heightened competition.54

In marked contrast, large-diameter C. hystrix trees were concentrated in high-to-complete mingling (M = 0.75–1.0, 59%; Figure 7D), consistent with its biology as a shade-tolerant, late-successional climax species. C. hystrix functions as a dominant edificator in the climax communities of the south subtropical zone and typically forms a wide, spreading crown. While seedlings are sensitive to high light intensity and grow optimally under moderate shade,55 progressing into the main canopy and reaching the overstory to develop a large bole necessitates ample overhead light.56 Intensive thinning substantially modifies canopy architecture and the sub-canopy light regime, markedly increasing direct beam radiation in the forest interior.57 This “canopy release” effect likely triggers photosynthetic acclimation and a strategic shift in growth of C. hystrix—moving from a conservative pattern of resource allocation (e.g., more investment in defense) toward aggressive resource capture and accelerated growth, thereby releasing its chronically constrained growth potential.9,58 Its development thus relies on intimate spatial intermingling with companion trees such as Eucalyptus to obtain suitable side shade and a stable microsite.59

Light thinning balances short-term harvest with long-term large-diameter timber production

The superiority of light thinning (25%) in maximizing total stand volume arises from a balanced trade-off between tree density and individual tree growth. Stand productivity can be enhanced either by maintaining a sufficiently high number of stems or by promoting the development of larger, high-quality trees.60,61 Heavy thinning increases per-tree volume but reduces residual density too drastically, failing to fully utilize site resources and compromising future stand stocking.62,63 In contrast, light thinning strikes an optimal compromise between removing a subset of trees (for immediate revenue) and keeping an adequate stock of superior trees (to secure future growth). This approach yields moderate current income while maintaining the greatest residual volume, thereby establishing the basis for sustained growth and the development of large-diameter timber. Therefore, thinning is more than a simple harvesting operation; it is a pivotal intervention that reshapes canopy architecture, redistributes light resources, and reconfigures belowground competitive space.64 Consistent with these insights, our results indicate that high-intensity thinning (e.g., HT, CT) accelerates early economic returns, aligning with short-rotation forestry objectives.65 Light thinning, however, prioritizes the retention of standing biomass as a form of capital accumulation, which is essential for long-term goals such as large-diameter timber cultivation. Thus, for the composite management of Eucalyptus and C. hystrix, LT most effectively realizes synergistic gains across short, medium, and long horizons. Neither simply prolonging the rotation without intervention (CK) nor implementing a short-rotation clear-cut system (CT) is conducive to maximizing total stand volume.

For management strategies that combine short- and medium-term timber production with overall stand productivity, light to moderate thinning (25%–50%) is optimal. It promotes large-diameter Eucalyptus growth while preserving substantial growth potential of C. hystrix, thus maximizing the overall stand productivity. If the primary objective shifts to long-term cultivation of C. hystrix as a climax valuable species, heavier thinning (e.g., 75%) may be warranted to hasten its progression from the understory into the dominant canopy, accepting a potential trade-off in reduced short-term Eucalyptus output. This exemplifies the inherent trade-offs in multi-objective forest management: varying thinning intensities establish different pathways balancing immediate timber yield, mid-term stand structural adjustment, and long-term large-diameter tree production.66 Placing our findings in a broader context, they support the global transition of Eucalyptus plantations from short-rotation, single-purpose systems toward multi-objective integrated management.67,68 Contemporary frameworks emphasize that balancing timber production, carbon sequestration, and biodiversity requires fine-scale structural regulation rather than simplistic density control.69,70 Our demonstration that a single moderate thinning intervention can simultaneously deliver immediate income, maintain high residual volume, and promote large-diameter timber formation provides empirical evidence for the “closer-to-nature” paradigm that prioritizes structural diversity and continuous cover.

Limitations of the study

Our study provides valuable insights into the effects of gradient thinning on stand spatial structure, large-diameter tree formation, and total stand volume in Eucalyptus-C. hystrix mixed forests. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, our findings are based on a single experimental site in Guangxi, southern China. While this site is representative of the main Eucalyptus-growing region of subtropical China, the optimal thinning intensity (25%) and the observed species-specific spatial responses may not be directly generalizable to other climatic zones, soil types, or mixed-species assemblages. Multi-site replication across the geographic range of Eucalyptus-C. hystrix plantations is needed to assess the broader applicability of our thinning prescriptions. Second, the post-thinning observation period spans only five years (2019–2024). Thinning effects—particularly for late-successional species such as C. hystrix—may take longer to fully manifest. Longer-term monitoring is essential to determine whether the benefits of light thinning persist beyond the first post-thinning decade and at what point a second thinning intervention becomes necessary to maintain optimal stand structure. We have scheduled a re-measurement of all 15 plots in autumn 2026, which will enable the calculation of annual growth increments over the 2024–2026 period and the analysis of temporal dynamics in spatial structure that could not be captured in the current single-inventory design. This repeated measurement will substantially strengthen the capacity to distinguish thinning-induced responses from background stand development trends. Third, the optimal thinning intensity is contingent on management objectives: 25% thinning maximized total stand volume and Eucalyptus large-diameter production, while heavier thinning (50%–75%) may be preferable for promoting C. hystrix large-diameter timber or understory biodiversity. Thus, thinning regimes should be tailored to specific multi-objective priorities, and our recommendations apply primarily to management contexts in which Eucalyptus large-diameter timber and total stand productivity are the primary goals.

Future research should extend this work to multiple sites, include a wider range of ecosystem services (e.g., biodiversity, carbon sequestration, water yield), and integrate physiological measurements to clarify the mechanisms underlying the contrasting mingling responses of Eucalyptus and C. hystrix. Process-based forest models combined with long-term monitoring will further advance the sustainable, multi-objective management of Eucalyptus-based mixed plantations.

Method details

Study area and field measurements

This study site was located in the Qing Mountain Station of Guangxi Youyiguan Forest Ecosystem Observation and Research Station (22°9′33″-22°9′56″ N, 106°41′43″-106°42′23″ E) in Pingxiang City, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, China (Figure 1). The climate of this area is a typical South Asian subtropical monsoon climate, with an average annual temperature of 20.5°C–21.7 °C; the average annual precipitation is 1200–1500 mm, and the relative humidity is 80%–84%, showing distinct dry and wet seasons. The main plant species include E. urophylla × E. grandis, C. hystrix, Rhodomyrtus tomentosa, Microstegium fasciculatum, Dicranopteris pedata and Miscanthus floridulu.

The experimental site was originally occupied by a Pinus massoniana plantation, which was clear-felled in late 2011. After manually cleaning the logging slash, the Eucalyptus (E. urophylla × E. grandis) and C. hystrix mixed plantation was established in March 2012. The plantation employed a wide-and-narrow row design: narrow rows were spaced 2 m apart, wide rows were spaced 5 m apart. Eucalyptus was planted on both sides of each narrow rows, while C. hystrix was planted in the middle of each wide rows. The spacing between trees within all rows was 2 m (Figure 1A).

In autumn 2019, a randomized block design was implemented with three blocks established in areas of comparable site quality. Within each block, the five thinning treatments were randomly assigned to the plots: no thinning of Eucalyptus (Control, CK), and removal of 25% (Light Thinning, LT), 50% (Moderate Thinning, MT), 75% (Heavy Thinning, HT), and 100% (Clear Thinning, CT) of Eucalyptus stems (Figures 2A–2E). Before any thinning operation was conducted, we carried out a pre-treatment inventory of all Eucalyptus and C. hystrix trees across all treatments, measuring DBH and total tree height for both species. One-way ANOVA revealed no significant differences in mean DBH, mean tree height, or stand density among the five treatment assignments within each block (p > 0.05; Table S1), confirming that the pre-thinning stand structure was statistically homogeneous across the experimental area. Subsequently, thinning was applied as whole-row removal along the original narrow Eucalyptus rows, while the adjacent wide rows containing C. hystrix were left intact. No selective thinning (e.g., removing only suppressed or diseased trees) was performed within retained rows, ensuring operational consistency across replicates.

Upon completion of the thinning operations, the felled Eucalyptus trees were immediately removed from the plots. To promote vigorous and uniform stump sprouting, the ground cover (litter and herbaceous vegetation) in an approximately 0.5 m radius around each Eucalyptus stump was manually cleared. This practice was intended to increase irradiance and temperature at the stump surface, reduce competition for water and nutrients from neighboring vegetation, and thereby break physiological dormancy of epicormic buds. During the spring of 2020, sprout thinning was performed when the primary sprout on 90% of the Eucalyptus stumps had attained a height of 1.5 m for all treatments. A single, most vigorous and straight-growing sprout (typically the largest in diameter and most centrally located on the stump) was retained per stump, and all remaining sprouts were removed at their base. In the treatments with thinning intensities from 25% to 75%, these operations generated an uneven-aged, multi-layered mixed forest consisting of both the original (first-generation, planted 2012; 12 years old at final inventory) and the coppice-regenerated (second-generation, sprouted in 2019–2020; 5 years old at final inventory) Eucalyptus trees, together with the C. hystrix (planted 2012; 12 years old at final inventory). In the CT treatment, an uneven-aged mixed forest composed of second-generation Eucalyptus coppice and 12-year-old C. hystrix was formed.

A full inventory was carried out in early 2024 across all 15 plots (5 treatments × 3 blocks). Each plot covered an area of 25 m × 25 m. All trees reaching a height of 4 m or more, including Eucalyptus, C. hystrix, and indigenous species, were inventoried for their species identity, spatial coordinates (x, y), diameter at breast height (DBH), and total tree height.

Stand volume measurements

Stand volume was calculated by summing the individual tree volume (ITV) of each plot. ITV was estimated using the experimental form factor method (Equation 1), a standardized approach widely adopted in Chinese forest inventory:71(Equation 1)ITV=D2×(1/4)×π×(H+3)×f∃where D represented the diameter at breast height (cm), H represented the tree height (m) and f∃ represented the experimental form factor. According to the experimental form-factor classification for Chinese tree species, both Eucalyptus and C. hystrix belong to Class V (broadleaved species with average stem taper), for which f∃ = 0.4.71,72 This approach has been validated in similar mixed-species stands in the study region.11 While we acknowledge that species-specific allometric equations might yield marginally different absolute volume estimates, the same equation was applied consistently to all trees and all treatments, ensuring that relative comparisons among thinning intensities remain unbiased.

Definition of large-diameter trees

To identify large-diameter trees for subsequent analyses of spatial structure and contribution to stand volume, we employed a relative threshold based on ITV. For each tree species separately, we pooled all measured trees from all 15 plots (843 Eucalyptus and 323 C. hystrix individuals) and ranked them by ITV. Trees whose volume fell within the top 10% of this pooled, experiment-wide ranking for their species were defined as large-diameter trees (yielding 84 Eucalyptus and 32 C. hystrix individuals). This experiment-wide percentile procedure generates a single, fixed absolute volume threshold for each species that is independent of treatment; consequently, individuals designated as large-diameter are directly comparable across all thinning intensities. The absolute DBH and ITV ranges of these large-diameter trees under each thinning intensity are provided in Table 1.

The use of a relative (experiment-wide percentile) rather than an absolute DBH (e.g., DBH ≥ 20 cm) is deliberate and aligns closely with our study objective. We aim to compare the probability of a tree attaining a position of competitive dominance under different thinning regimes, not to assess compliance with industrial timber-grade specifications. A fixed absolute DBH threshold would be inherently biased against younger stands (e.g., the 5-year-old second-generation coppice in the CT treatment), systematically excluding vigorous young trees that are performing well for their age. The experiment-wide percentile procedure provides a standardized, internally consistent index of relative competitive success that is sensitive to thinning effects across the full range of thinning intensities and stand age classes present in our experiment.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (mean ± standard deviation) were calculated using Microsoft Excel 2024.All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.0.1. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to assess the effects of thinning intensity (CK, LT, MT, HT, CT) on tree-level growth variables (mean DBH, tree height, ITV), and stand-level variables (stand volume, mean spatial structure indices W¯,M¯). Prior to ANOVA, data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. All variables met the assumptions (p > 0.05). When a significant treatment effect was detected (p < 0.05), Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test was applied for all pairwise multiple comparisons. To compare the mean dominance index (U¯) between the two tree species (Eucalyptus and C. hystrix) under the same thinning intensity, independent two-sample t-tests were performed for each treatment level (CK, LT, MT, HT, CT). The significance level was set at α = 0.05. Data visualization was carried out using Origin 2024.
