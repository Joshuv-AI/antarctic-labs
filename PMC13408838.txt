Macrophage adenylyl cyclase 7 protects against myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury in male mice
Authors: Guofang Xia, Simeng Zhu, Yujia Liu, Yifan Shi, Jiaxin Chen, Jingwei Pan, Zhong Chen, Peng Wei, Chengxing Shen, Ailian Du, Congfeng Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury undermines the clinical benefit of percutaneous coronary intervention, with cardiac macrophages playing critical roles. Here, using spatial transcriptomics and flow cytometry, we identified adenylyl cyclase 7 (ADCY7) as a macrophage-specific regulator and potential therapeutic target in myocardial I/R injury, and validated its expression in patient samples. By establishing a macrophage depletion/reconstitution model, we demonstrate that macrophage Adcy7 deficiency significantly exacerbates myocardial I/R injury and cardiac dysfunction in male mice, whereas Adcy7 overexpression attenuates these effects. Macrophage Adcy7 deficiency also increases leukocyte infiltration and pro-inflammatory cytokine production. Mechanistically, transcriptomic and phosphoproteomic analyses reveal that ADCY7 activates cAMP-protein kinase A signaling, thereby inhibiting nuclear translocation of NF-κB and restraining the pro-inflammatory response. Combined with the macrophage depletion/reconstitution approach, we developed a photoactivated adenylyl cyclase system that alleviated cardiac inflammation and I/R injury. Our study identifies ADCY7 as a macrophage-intrinsic anti-inflammatory regulator and a promising therapeutic target for myocardial I/R injury.

Introduction

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is widely used to salvage the ischemic myocardium after myocardial infarction1. However, the restoration of blood flow post-PCI paradoxically causes substantial myocardial damage, termed ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury. This injury includes infarct area expansion and systolic dysfunction, ultimately contributing to 25–40% of the final infarct size in patients receiving reperfusion therapy2. Additionally, no cardioprotective intervention has demonstrated pronounced clinical benefits3. The restoration of blood flow and oxygen delivery exacerbates coronary microvascular injury, including activation of endothelial cells as well as impairment of endothelial integrity, and augments cardiomyocyte injury, especially Ca2+ overload, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial injury2. These injuries facilitate leukocyte infiltration and trigger danger-associated molecular pattern (DAMP)-induced sterile inflammation4. Sterile inflammation activates a cascade of immune effectors, including immune cells and cytokines5. While controlled activation of immune cells such as macrophages and neutrophils is essential for clearing dead cardiomyocytes and promoting scar formation to replace infarcted myocardium6, excessive production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species during I/R injury can become maladaptive, further aggravating cardiomyocyte damage and cardiac dysfunction7.

Emerging evidence highlights the indispensable role of cardiac macrophages in myocardial I/R injury, as they orchestrate the inflammatory cascade throughout the entire pathological process8. Early after myocardial I/R injury, multiple danger signals, such as high-mobility group box 1 protein, mitochondrial DNA and reactive oxygen species9–11, are released from injured and dead cells. This prompts the recruitment of circulating monocytes into myocardium, where they differentiate into C-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2)+ macrophages. These CCR2+ macrophages, major effectors of cardiac inflammation, rapidly phagocytose dead cells, secrete proteases, reactive oxygen species, as well as pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby initiating a sterile inflammatory response that supports wound healing and tissue repair7. However, overactivation of these macrophages drives an excessive or prolonged inflammatory response, which impedes cardiac repair and promotes adverse ventricular remodeling12. Therefore, regulation of CCR2+ macrophages and resolution of inflammation represent promising therapeutic targets for modulating I/R injury13.

Adenylyl cyclases comprise a family of nine transmembrane isoforms with distinct expression patterns across tissues and cell types. These enzymes convert ATP to cyclic AMP (cAMP), a key intracellular second messenger. Adenylyl cyclases exert potent regulatory effects on immune cells, including T cells, B cells, and neutrophils, through multiple pathways14. Growing evidence indicates that adenylyl cyclases and downstream cAMP signaling dampen lipopolysaccharide-induced inflammation in macrophages, for example, by suppressing Toll-like receptor 4-mediated signaling cascade15,16. However, the specific role of adenylyl cyclases in cardiac macrophages during myocardial I/R injury remains unclear.

In this study, we identified adenylyl cyclase 7 (ADCY7) as a key player in the inflammatory network using spatial transcriptomics in a murine model of myocardial I/R injury. Taking advantage of macrophage depletion and reconstitution strategy with Adcy7-knockout or -overexpressing macrophages in I/R mice, we validated the protective effect against myocardial I/R injury. Integrating transcriptomic and phosphoproteomic analyses, we found that ADCY7 activates protein kinase A (PKA), which inhibits nuclear translocation of P65 and thereby restrains NF-κB signaling and pro-inflammatory cytokine production. We further developed a photoactivated adenylyl cyclase-based system to control I/R-induced inflammation, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting ADCY7.

Results

Elevated ADCY7 expression in cardiac macrophages post-ischemia/reperfusion

To screen for key molecules regulating inflammatory homeostasis after myocardial I/R, we conducted bioinformatic analysis of a spatial transcriptomic dataset from murine hearts subjected to myocardial I/R (Supplementary Fig. 1A). DESeq2-based differential expression analysis ( | log2FC | > 1, adjusted p < 0.05) identified 1536 upregulated genes and 940 downregulated genes in I/R hearts compared with sham-operated controls (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed that upregulated genes were predominantly associated with inflammatory response, cell adhesion and tumor necrosis factor production, while the down-regulated genes were enriched in oxidative phosphorylation and aerobic respiration (Supplementary Fig. 1C). To narrow the candidate list, we further performed network topological analysis using Cytoscape with the ClueGO algorithm and identified Adcy7 as a potential regulator for inflammatory resolution (Supplementary Fig. 1D). Spatial analysis displayed that Adcy7 expression was significantly elevated in murine I/R model and was mainly concentrated in the infarct and border zone in I/R myocardium (Supplementary Fig. 1E).

To investigate ADCY7 expression during myocardial I/R process, we employed a temporary coronary artery ligation murine model. Quantitative PCR and immunoblotting assay showed that ADCY7 expression increased after reperfusion, peaking from day 3 to day 7 post-reperfusion (Fig. 1A–C). This timing coincided with the early reparative phase, suggesting a potential role for ADCY7 in inflammation regulation and cardiac repair. Single-cell RNA-sequencing analysis indicated that Adcy7 was highly enriched in immune cells, with negligible expression in other cardiac cell types (Supplementary Fig. 1F). Given that macrophages are the predominant immune cell population in the heart8, we performed immunofluorescence staining for CD68 (a pan-macrophage marker) and ADCY7 on myocardial sections, and observed that ADCY7 strongly colocalized with CD68⁺ signals, particularly in the infarct and border zone at day 3 post-I/R (Fig. 1D, E), indicating that upregulated ADCY7 is mainly expressed in macrophages. Cardiac macrophages are heterogeneous and can be subdivided based on major histocompatibility complex II (MHC-II) and CCR2 expression17. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that ADCY7 was significantly elevated in MHC-II−CCR2+ macrophages post-I/R (Fig. 1F, G), a subset likely derived from newly recruited monocytes18,19. Quantitative PCR confirmed elevated Adcy7 mRNA in sorted cardiac macrophages post-I/R (Fig. 1H, I). Adcy7 deficiency aggravates myocardial inflammation and I/R injury

To evaluate the functional impact of ADCY7 on myocardial I/R injury, we measured cardiac inflammation and dysfunction in WT and Adcy7 KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B). Baseline echocardiography showed no detectable cardiac abnormalities in Adcy7 KO mice (Fig. 3A, B). However, on day 3 post-I/R, Adcy7 KO mice exhibited significantly worse left ventricular systolic function (Fig. 3A, B) and increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis (TUNEL+/cTNT+ cells) compared with WT mice (Fig. 3C, D). Given the critical role of inflammation in I/R injury, we then investigated the effects of Adcy7 deficiency on inflammation during I/R injury, and flow cytometric analysis revealed increased CD45+ immune cell infiltration, particularly CCR2+ macrophages, in Adcy7 KO mice (Fig. 3E, F), indicating enhanced recruitment of pro-inflammatory macrophages into ischemic myocardium in Adcy7 KO mice. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed higher CD68+ macrophage numbers in heart sections from Adcy7 KO mice (Fig. 3G, H), accompanied by upregulated pro-inflammatory genes, such as Il1, Tnfa, Cxcl1 and Nos2 (Fig. 3I). Macrophage Adcy7 overexpression protects against myocardial I/R injury

As we observed above, we reasoned that Adcy7 overexpression in macrophages may be beneficial for myocardial I/R injury. To overexpress Adcy7 in BMDMs, we employed a lentiviral system to infect BMDMs and confirmed successful overexpression based on RT-qPCR quantification and GFP⁺ cell enumeration (Supplementary Fig. 2D, E). With the same D/R system as mentioned previously, we established macrophage-depleted mice reconstituted with Adcy7-overexpressing BMDMs (D/R-MϕAdcy7 mice, Supplementary Fig. 2F, G). Compared with control-reconstituted mice, D/R-MϕAdcy7 mice exhibited improved left ventricular ejection fraction (Fig. 6A, B), reduced infarct-to-AAR ratio (Fig. 6C, D), decreased apoptosis (Fig. 6E, F), fewer CD45+ cells and CCR2+ macrophage infiltration (Fig. 6G, H), reduced CD68+ macrophage density (Fig. 6I, J), and downregulated pro-inflammatory genes, including Il1, Tnfa, Cxcl1 and Nos2 (Fig. 6K). ADCY7 regulates NF-κB-mediated pro-inflammatory gene transcription via inhibiting NF-κB nuclear translocation

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying ADCY7-mediated regulation of macrophage function, we conducted RNA-sequencing on Adcy7-deficient BMDMs. Principal component analysis showed clear segregation between Adcy7 knockout and control BMDMs (Supplementary Fig. 3A). DESeq2 analysis ( | log2fold change| > 1 and p < 0.05) identified 950 upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and 605 downregulated DEGs. Pro-inflammatory cytokines (Cfb, Il1a, Il12b, Il6 and Il22) and chemokines (Ccl22, Cxcl1, Cxcl3 and Ccrl2) were among the most strongly upregulated genes (Fig. 7A, B). GO and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) analyses confirmed robust enrichment of pro-inflammatory pathways, including chemokine receptor binding, leukocyte chemotaxis, and IL-1 signaling (Fig. 7C, D; Supplementary Fig. 3B). Adcy7 deficiency markedly reduced intracellular cAMP concentration in BMDMs (Supplementary Fig. 5A). We therefore engineered BMDMs with bPAC overexpression (BMDMbPAC) (Fig. 10A). Discussion

Being highly heterogeneous and functionally diverse, cardiac macrophages have been the subject of intense investigation in an array of cardiovascular diseases26. Simply put, CCR2- cardiac macrophages are embryonically derived and participate in heart development and homeostasis maintenance, while CCR2+ cardiac macrophages are haematopoietically derived and recruited to the heart with contribution to inflammation, fibrosis and age-related disorders8. Given the critical role of inflammation in myocardial I/R injury, it is not surprising that we identified ADCY7 as a key regulator in CCR2+ cardiac macrophages during myocardial I/R injury. Macrophage depletion followed by reconstitution with Adcy7-deficient or -overexpressing macrophages in mice demonstrates its prominent role in restraining inflammation and protecting against myocardial I/R injury. We further showed that ADCY7 inhibits the NF-κB activation via suppression of P65 nuclear translocation. To assess the translational potential of ADCY7, we developed a photoactivatable adenylyl cyclase-based strategy that effectively regulates inflammation post-I/R, highlighting its therapeutic potential for myocardial I/R injury.

Primarily based on a spatial transcriptomic dataset, we screened Adcy7 as a potential candidate for regulating inflammation post-myocardial I/R injury27. However, other candidate genes, such as Ppbp, Arrb2 and Inhba, also deserve to be explored further in I/R injury. Indeed, Arrb2 has been shown to be protective in I/R injury28,29. In our study, ADCY7 expression began to rise at day 3 post-myocardial I/R and remained elevated until day 7, a temporal pattern consistent with the inflammation resolution post-I/R, which typically occurs from ~4 days post-I/R until 7 days30. Multiparametric flow cytometry coupled with intracellular ADCY7 staining revealed predominant induction of ADCY7 in the CCR2+ macrophage subset, corresponding to the monocyte-derived recruited population, although we cannot completely exclude its expression in other cell types such as cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells. As CCR2+ cardiac macrophages are usually haematopoietically derived and recruited post injury, we employed a macrophage depletion/reconstitution model31–33 to replace the endogenous circulating monocytes/macrophages with genetically modified BMDMs expressing either Adcy7 deficiency or overexpression. With this model, we showed Adcy7 suppresses leukocyte infiltration and pro-inflammatory mediator production, including pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, reactive oxygen species and matrix metalloproteinases, thereby protecting against infarct expansion and systolic dysfunction5. Because clodronate can also affect dendritic cells and impair neutrophil function, our results cannot fully exclude the effect from other immune cells34,35.

Adenylyl cyclases constitute an evolutionarily conserved enzyme family that catalyzes the conversion of ATP to cAMP, a ubiquitous second messenger that mediates diverse physiological and pathological effects36. Although cAMP is generally considered anti-inflammatory, the functions of individual adenylyl cyclase isoforms vary across inflammatory contexts37. For example, ADCY6 is cardioprotective in cardiac injury, while ADCY5 is not38. In this study, we confirmed the cardioprotective effect of ADCY7. Further, taking advantage of computational prediction of transcription factor activity and kinase-substrate network analysis, we identified two critical nodal effectors–PKA Cα and NF-κB1- that provide mechanistic insights into ADCY7-mediated anti-inflammatory effects. PKA activation downstream of ADCY7 limits NF-κB nuclear translocation. This mechanism of inflammation suppression is not unique to ADCY7, as inhibition of the cAMP-PKA pathway has been shown to promote NF-κB activation and nuclear translocation39. Notably, our phosphoproteomic analysis was performed with only a single biological replicate, which may limit the robustness of that particular finding. Therefore, we employed both PKA activator and inhibitor to validate the function of PKA downstream of ADCY7. It has been reported that activated PKA binds to the scaffold protein AKAP95 and phosphorylates the NF-κB precursor p105, thereby inhibiting NF-κB activity and pro-inflammatory cytokine production40. cAMP could activate diverse effector proteins, such as protein kinase A (PKA), exchange protein activated by cAMP (EPAC), popeye domain containing (POPDC) proteins or cAMP-gated ion channels in a cell type-dependent manner14. With 9 transmembrane isoforms of adenylyl cyclase, 2 types of PKA, 2 isoforms of Epac, more than 50 isoforms of PDEs and hundreds of GPCRs related to the regulation of adenylyl cyclase-cAMP signaling, it is challenging to target cAMP with sufficient specificity, while targeting adenylyl cyclase-coupled GPCRs and PDEs or downstream signaling effectors could be the potential strategies for regulating adenylyl cyclase-cAMP signaling41. For instance, ADCY6 suppresses TLR4-mediated inflammation by altering receptor endocytosis and promoting degradation via lipid raft42. Further research on subcellular compartmentalization and plasma membrane microdomain distribution of ADCY7 may shed more light on its regulation of inflammation.

Although pharmacological activators and inhibitors of ACs are available, they often lack sufficient isoform selectivity, limiting their therapeutic application43. Therefore, we developed an optogenetic intervention platform to evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting adenylyl cyclases in myocardial I/R injury. This approach enables spatiotemporal precision through light-controlled modulation of macrophage activity. By locally suppressing macrophage-mediated inflammation, it reduces immune cell infiltration and pro-inflammatory cytokine production, thus attenuating myocardial I/R injury.

In conclusion, we identify ADCY7 in CCR2+ cardiac macrophages as a critical regulator of inflammation post-myocardial I/R, supported by both loss- and gain-of-function of Adcy7 in macrophages based on a macrophage depletion/reconstitution mouse model. Mechanistically, ADCY7 suppresses inflammation through PKA-mediated inhibition of NF-κB nuclear translocation and downstream transcription of target genes. We further validated the cardioprotective effect of a photoactivated adenylyl cyclase-based approach, underscoring the promising translational potential of targeting ADCY7 for the treatment of myocardial I/R injury.

Methods

Clinical study participants

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Affiliated Sixth People’s Hospital (2017-KY-003(K)). All patients provided written informed consent before enrollment. Participants were recruited consecutively from patients with STEMI at Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital. Control subjects were enrolled from individuals undergoing coronary angiography for non-ischemic indications with angiographically normal coronary arteries. Patients diagnosed with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) at Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital were enrolled in this study. The diagnosis of myocardial infarction was based upon the Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction1, including a typical clinical history, characteristic electrocardiographic findings, and serum enzyme levels at least 50% higher than the upper limit of normal. Additionally, stable patients with angiographically normal coronary arteries were included as control group. These control subjects had no evidence of coronary artery stenosis, valvular or structural heart disease, or cardiomyopathy. Patients with a recent history of stroke or significant comorbidities, such as cancer, serious infections, or autoimmune diseases, were excluded from the study. Peripheral blood was drawn by venipuncture into tubes containing sodium heparin (BD Biosciences). Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were then isolated from the blood samples using density gradient centrifugation with Lymphoprep Solution (Axis-Shield, Dundee, Scotland) for further analysis.

Animals

C57BL/6J mice (substrain C57BL/6J, genetic background: C57BL/6J, species: Mus musculus) were obtained from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, CN). C57BL/6JCya-Adcy7em1/Cya (Adcy7-/-) mice (substrain: C57BL/6JCya, genetic background: C57BL/6J, species: Mus musculus) were generated by Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou, China). Six- to eight-week-old male mice were purchased and acclimated in our facility for at least 7 days before any experiments. Mice were group-housed in individually ventilated cages under specific pathogen-free conditions, with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, controlled temperature (22 ± 1 °C) and humidity (50 ± 10%), and ad libitum access to standard rodent chow and autoclaved water. Animal welfare and health status were monitored daily by trained personnel. Euthanasia was performed via pentobarbital overdose followed by cervical dislocation. All experimental procedures involving mice were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Sixth People’s Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine and were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. Sample size was determined a priori using G*Power software. Computer-generated randomization with allocation concealment ensured group assignment objectivity. Blinding protocols were maintained during animal experiments.

Myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury mouse model

Mice were anesthetized using 1.5% isoflurane with a controlled delivery system (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, CN). After making a small incision on the skin of the left chest, the major and minor pectoral muscles were dissected and retracted. With a mosquito clamp, a small hole was made at the fourth intercostal space and kept slightly open. The first three fingers of the left hand worked together to delicately squeeze the heart out. The left anterior descending artery (LAD) was sutured with a 6-0 silk suture using a slipknot, and the heart was placed back into the thoracic space. After evacuation of air in the thorax, the major and minor pectoral muscles were repositioned, and the incision was closed. Following a period of 45 minutes of ischemia, the slipknot was released by means of a gentle and smooth pull on the long end of the slipknot suture.

Macrophage depletion/reconstitution procedure

Male C57BL/6 mice were intraperitoneally injected with 20 mg/kg busulfan 2 days before operation. One day before operation, one dose of clodronate-containing liposomes (0.2 mL/mouse at 5 mg/mL, SunLipo, CN) was intravenously injected to deplete the macrophages. Elimination of the macrophages was confirmed by flow cytometry pre- and post-I/R (Supplementary Fig. 2C). To perform macrophage reconstitution, 1 × 106 BMDMs were injected into macrophage-depleted mice intravenously 1 hour pre-I/R.

Tissue optical clearing

The optical clearing agent was made by mixing PEG-400 with thiazone at a volume ratio of 9:1 and then mixing with saturated sucrose solution at a volume ratio of ~1:123. Mice were anesthetized using 1.5% isoflurane gas with an isoflurane delivery system. The mice were depilated in front thorax with depilatory creams (Watsons, CN), and the skin was stripped 20 times with an adhesive tape for optical clearing agent treatment. Following the chest wall optical clearing procedure, the mice were habituated in transparent polypropylene cages with adjustable light-emitting diodes array (blue light, λ = 460 ± 10 nm; red light, λ = 650 ± 10 nm, AIJIA Electronics) underneath the cage.

Cell culture and treatment

C57BL/6 mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation and sterilized. The femur and tibia were integrally cut at the knee joint, and the bone marrow was flushed out. Cell concentration was adjusted to 2 × 106 cells/mL and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 10% CMG14-12 cell (M-CSF producing cell line) supernatant44,45. The bone marrow cells were differentiated into BMDMs after 7 days. For LPS treatment, BMDMs were incubated with LPS at 100 ng/mL for the indicated times and analyzed with the indicated assays. For 6-Bnz-cAMP (MCE, US) and H-89 (MCE, US) treatment, BMDMs were treated with 2 μM 6-Bnz-cAMP or 10 μM H-89 for 45 minutes and analyzed with the indicated assays.

To produce lentivirus, 293FT cells were co-transfected with lentiCRISPRv2/pLenti-puro/pLenti-bPAC/pLenti-Adcy7, psPAX2 and pMD2.G using polyethylenimine (YEASEN, CN). For lentivirus infection, the bone marrow cells were incubated with lentivirus at an MOI of 30 on day 1 post-isolation and differentiated into BMDMs as indicated.

Detection of cellular cAMP production in BMDMs

cAMP production was detected using cAMP-Glo kit (Promega, USA). Briefly, 5000 isolated BMDMs/well were plated in 96-well plates. One day later, BMDMs were transfected with the indicated lentivirus. 5 days post-transfection, BMDMs were subjected to cAMP assay or illumination for 1 hour to activate bPAC before cAMP assay. After lysis with cAMP-Glo lysis buffer to release cAMP, PKA was added, and the PKA reaction was measured with a luciferase assay. The cellular cAMP concentration was calculated following a standard curve and normalized to the control group.

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting and flow cytometry

Mice were anesthetized with 2.5% isoflurane and intracardially perfused with pre-cooled PBS. Left ventricles were harvested, minced, and digested in PBS containing 450 U/mL type I collagenase (Worthington Biochemical Corporation, Lakewood, NJ, US), 450 U/mL type II collagenase, 150 U/mL type IV collagenase and 60 U/mL DNase I for 60 minutes at 37 °C with gentle agitation. The suspension was pipetted repeatedly, passed through a 70-μm cell strainer, and washed with PBS. Single-cell suspensions were blocked with CD16/32 antibody (clone 93, eBioscience) on ice for 15 minutes. For cardiac macrophages sorted to measure adenylyl cyclase mRNA transcription, the cardiac cells were stained with the following antibodies on ice for 30 minutes: CD11b-BV605 (M1/70, BD Bioscience) and CX3CR1-PE-cy7 (SA011F11, BioLegend), followed by 50 μg/mL propidium iodide and sorted by FACS Melody cell sorter (BD Biosciences, NJ, US). For ADCY7 indirect staining, the cardiac cells were stained with ADCY7 primary antibody (Servicebio, CN) and followed by Alexa-488-conjugated secondary antibody (Sigma Aldrich, US). The cells were then incubated with CD45-APC-cy7 (30-F11, BD Bioscience), Ly6G-BV510 (1A8, BioLegend), CD11b-BV605 (clone M1/70, BioLegend), CCR2- BV421(SA203G11, BioLegend) and MHC-II-PE antibody (AF6-120.1, BD Bioscience). For immune cells infiltration analysis, the cardiac cells were stained with the following antibodies: CD45-APC-cy7 (30-F11, BD Bioscience), Ly6G-BV510 (1A8, BioLegend), CD11b-BV605 (M1/70, BioLegend), CCR2-BV421(SA203G11, BioLegend) and F4/80-APC (BM8, BioLegend). Flow cytometric analysis was performed on FACSCanto plus (BD Biosciences) with BD FACSDiva Software_v8.0.2 and analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star, US).

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRI reagent (Sigma Aldrich). Reverse transcription was performed using HiScript II Reverse Transcriptase (Vazyme, Jiangsu, CN). Quantitative PCR analysis was performed on a Light Cycler 96 Real-Time System (Roche, Indianapolis, IN, US) for 45 cycles using a SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme). The relative transcriptional levels of target genes were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt with LightCycler96 software. The primer pairs used for qPCR were listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunofluorescence staining

For immunofluorescence staining, frozen heart sections or cultured cells were fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100 and blocked with 5% donkey serum, and stained with the indicated primary antibodies in blocking buffer at 4 °C overnight. Samples were then incubated with Alexa488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit antibody (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), Alexa594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit antibody (ThermoFisher Scientific) or Alexa488-conjugated goat anti-rat antibody (ThermoFisher Scientific). The sections were imaged using Olympus BX53 fluorescence microscopes with OLYMPUS cellSens Standard software and quantified using ImageJ software (Version 1.54, NIH). For colocalization experiments, fluorescent intensities of P65 and DAPI were analyzed in separate channels across nuclear transects within BMDMs, where synchronous intensity changes demonstrated colocalization.

Immunoblotting

Immunoblotting was performed with the following protocol: cell lysates were prepared using pre-cold RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotech, CN) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) and phosphatase inhibitor (Roche). Protein samples were separated by electrophoresis with SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were probed with the indicated primary antibodies. Subsequently, the HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. The immunoreactive bands were detected with West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) using the ECL detection system (Tanon, CN) with Tanon AllDoc_x software.

Plasmid construction and lentivirus production

The Adcy7-targeted gRNA was designed using CRISPOR46. Oligonucleotides were synthesized from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, CN) and the sequences are shown in the Supplementary Table 1. sgRNA was cloned into lentiCRISPRv2 (Addgene #52961). Full-length murine Adcy7 cDNAs were PCR amplified from first-strand cDNA libraries and subcloned into pLenti-puro vector. The bPAC (Beggiatoa sp. photoactive adenylyl cyclase) cDNA (Genbank™ accession number GU461307) was subcloned into pLenti-puro vector.

Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed using Vevo 3100 system (VisualSonics, Toronto, ON, Canada) and analyzed with Vevo Lab v5.7.1 software. In detail, mice were shaved on the chest, anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and fixed on the echo pad in the supine position. Two-dimensional (2D) images were acquired in the left ventricular long-axis planes while maintaining a heart rate of 500–550 bpm, and echocardiographic measurements were recorded in M-mode. All measurements were averaged over three consecutive cardiac cycles.

Infarct size assessment

Infarct size was assessed using double staining with Evans blue (Sigma Aldrich) and 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC, Sigma Aldrich). On day 3 post-I/R, after the aorta was clamped, 1% Evans blue was injected into the left cardiac chamber, and the heart was excised integrally, frozen at −20 °C, and sliced at a thickness of 1 mm. The heart sections were incubated in 1% TTC at 37 °C for 5 minutes, photographed by Stereo Microscope (Jiangnan, CN) with ScopeImage v9.0 software and analyzed using ImageJ.

Myocardial apoptosis

Myocardial apoptosis was determined using the TUNEL apoptosis assay kit (Beyotime Biotech). In brief, the heart sections were fixed with 4% PFA at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 45 minutes. The heart sections were stained with mouse anti-cTNT antibody (ab8295, Abcam, UK) followed by Alexa-488-conjugated secondary antibody (Sigma Aldrich, US). The heart sections were then incubated with 50 μL TUNEL reaction mixture for 1 hour at 37 °C kept in the dark, and labeled with DAPI before observation. The images were acquired by Olympus BX53 microscopy (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and analyzed with ImageJ. Apoptotic cardiomyocytes are designated only when their nuclei are completely enclosed within the cTNT+ cytoplasm. Nuclei merely adjacent to cardiomyocytes were excluded from quantification.

RNA-sequencing

The integrity of the RNA was confirmed using a Bioanalyzer 2100 (RIN > 7.0) and agarose gel electrophoresis. Poly (A) RNA was purified using Dynabeads Oligo (dT) 25 and fragmented with the Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module. Reverse transcription was then performed with SuperScript™ II. Adapter ligation to A-tailed cDNA fragments was performed following end repair, and size selection was conducted using AMPure XP beads. The ligated products were then amplified via PCR, and the final cDNA library had an average insert size of 300 ± 50 bp. The library was subjected to sequencing on an Illumina Novaseq™ 6000 instrument, utilizing 2 × 150 bp paired-end sequencing.

The fastp software was utilized for quality filtering, and HISAT2 was utilized to align the reads to the Mus musculus GRCm39 reference genome. Mapped reads were assembled with StringTie. The assembly of all transcriptomes was undertaken using GffCompare. Expression levels of transcripts were estimated using StringTie and FPKM calculation. The identification of differentially expressed mRNAs was conducted using the DESeq2 R package, with |log2fold change| > 1 and a p value < 0.05.

Phosphoproteomic analysis

BMDM extracts were sonicated, reduced with DTT, digested with trypsin, and blocked with IAA at 20 mM. Peptides were desalted on Empore™SPE MCX columns and freeze-dried. Phosphopeptides were enriched with the High-Select™ Fe-NTA Kit, freeze-dried, and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid. iRT peptides were added, and DIA analysis was performed on a nanoElute 1 system coupled with timsTOF MS Pro 2(Bruker). The LC separation used buffer A (0.1% formic acid in water) and buffer B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile/water, 99.9% acetonitrile). A C18 reverse-phase analytical column (Thermo Scientific EASY column, 25 cm length, 75 μm inner diameter, 1.9 μm particles) was employed. The flow rate was set to 300 nL/min. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0.00–3.00 min, linear gradient from 5% to 8% B; 3.00–43.00 min, linear gradient from 8% to 26% B; 43.00-48.00 min, linear gradient from 26% to 40% B; 48.00–50.00 min, linear gradient from 40% to 90% B; 50.00–60.00 min, held at 90% B. The dia-PASEF method was configured with an m/z range of 100–1700, an ion mobility range of 1.5–0.6 V s/cm², 12 dia-PASEF scans, and a cycle time of 1.4 seconds (Supplementary Fig. 4A)47. MS2 used DIA with four TIMSScan windows, 100 ms each. In PASEF mode, collision energy varied with ion mobility 1/K0 (20-59 eV, 0.85-1.30 V s/cm²). DIA data were processed using Spectronaut (version 17) using directDIA with the match between runs feature enabled and retention time calibration performed using the iRT peptides (Supplementary Fig. 4B, C). Trypsin was specified as the enzyme with up to 2 missed cleavages allowed. Carbamidomethyl (C) as a fixed mod, and dynamic mods including Oxidation (M), Acetyl (Protein N-term), Phospho (STY). The false discovery rate (FDR) at the peptide precursor level was set to <1% for protein and peptide identification. Peptide grouping considered modified peptides as distinct entities. The quantification used for all downstream bioinformatic analyses was based on the phosphosite-level quantification results. For phosphosite localization, Class 1 sites (localization probability ≥ 0.99) were used for all downstream analyses. The phosphosite-level intensity data were normalized to the total peak intensity of each sample. No imputation was performed for missing values in the downstream analysis. This streamlined process ensures comprehensive analysis while maintaining experimental integrity. The GO enrichment and network analysis were performed with OmicStudio platform and ClueGO. The kinase analysis was conducted using PhosphoSitePlus system with differentially phosphorylated substrates (Fig. 9B).

Bioinformatic analysis of single-cell and spatial transcriptomic sequences

The single-cell RNA-sequencing dataset analyzed in this study was retrieved from the ArrayExpress (E-MTAB-7895), and the spatial transcriptomics dataset was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE269054). Raw count matrices were subjected to standard quality-control filtering. Cells with 2000–5000 detected unique molecular identifiers and <15% mitochondrial gene content were retained to exclude low-quality or low-complexity cells. Following quality control, gene expression values were normalized using the LogNormalize method in Seurat v5 with a scale factor of 10,000, followed by data scaling. The 2000 most variable genes were selected for principal component analysis. The first 15 principal components were subsequently used for dimensionality reduction by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection and for graph-based Leiden clustering. Cluster-enriched marker genes were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with a minimum expression fraction of 0.25 and a log-fold-change threshold of 0.25. For pseudo-bulk transcriptomic analysis, gene-level counts from cardiac resident macrophages within each sample were aggregated to generate a sample-level pseudo-bulk count matrix, and differential gene expression was assessed using DESeq2.

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as mean ± SD. Statistical comparisons were performed using GraphPad Prism v8.3.0 software. One-way ANOVA or Student’s t test was used for all statistical analyses in this study. Bonferroni or Tukey multiple comparisons test was applied for comparing each mean with every other mean (all-pairwise comparisons), and Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test for comparing each mean with the control mean (multiple-to-one comparisons). p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
