 Abstract
 Calibration of a reactive burn model for detonation of high explosive is an important step towards predictive hy-
 drodynamic simulations of detonation. A typical calibration consists of varying model parameters (e.g., rate constants,
 activation energies) until results of hydrodynamic simulations match the experimental data for a certain set of ex-
 periments. Hydrodynamic simulations of the dependence of steady detonation velocity on the radius of a cylindrical
 high-explosive charge - often used in such calibrations - can be computationally expensive. In this work, we propose a
 method where such expensive simulations are performed infrequently, and only to parameterize and refine a surrogate
 model for the dependence of the detonation velocity on calibrated parameters. The method is developed, implemented
 and applied to an example problem - calibration of the AWSD reactive burn model for important high explosive PBX
 9502. Two different flavors of the surrogate model are investigated, and the calibration is performed successfully.

1 Introduction
A reactive burn model (RBM) describes the rate of decomposition of high explosive (HE) in detonation. A typical rate law
produces the rate of decomposition, in the Lagrangian hydrodynamic frame, as a function of constant model parameters
(e.g., rate constants, activation energies), local state variables (e.g., pressure, temperature, extent of reaction), as well as
such non-local variables as the leading shock pressure and temperature [1, 2]. At present, it is impossible to fully set all
the RBM parameters (e.g., rate constants, activation energies, threshold pressures for switching between burn regimes etc)
for a specific HE from just theoretical considerations or modeling results (e.g., atomistic simulations). Because of this,
the RBM calibration is typically performed by varying RBM parameters until the sufficient agreement is reached between
simulations that use the RBM and experimental data for a certain suite of detonation experiments. A de facto standard
suite of such experiments consists of:
 1. Shock-to-detonation transition (SDT) - an experiment where an HE charge is hit by a flyer, and the transformation
 of the resulting non-reactive shock wave into the fully developed detonation is monitored in a time-resolved manner.
 2. Diameter effect (DE) - an experiment where a detonation wave is launched along an HE cylinder, a rate stick (RS),
 and what is studied is the dependence of the steady detonation velocity on the RS radius.
The combination of these two classes of experiments has been widely used to calibrate RBM [3, 2, 4, 5, 6] at LANL and
elsewhere. Of the corresponding computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations, SDT is significantly less expensive
computationally, as the actual shock-to-detonation transition problem, as typically formulated, is one-dimensional. On
the other hand, an intrinsically two-dimensional DE simulation can be rather expensive. A combination of the need to
(i) reach the steady detonation (requires a long RS, e.g., L ∼ 40 cm), and (ii) calculate DE for large-radius sticks (e.g.,
R ∼ 20-50 mm [7]) with the grid resolution of ∼ 5- 20 µm, both along and perpendicular to the RS axis, can result in a
single simulation taking up to ~1-2 hours, even if heavily parallelized. A typical calibration involves numerical minimization
of a loss function that relates values of RBM parameters to the loss - a dimensionless scalar value that quantifies the
discrepancy between simulation results and experimental data. Single evaluation of the loss function involves SDT and
DE CFD simulations, and since hundreds of evaluations might be required for minimization, the RBM calibration can
quickly become prohibitively expensive computationally. To circumvent this problem, two routes have been explored in
literature. The first one is to basically use the “brute-force” approach just described, but drastically reduce the number of
RBM parameters that are actually varied in calibration, e.g., from ∼ 10 for a typical RBM to ∼ 1-2 [8, 9]. This decreases
the dimensionality of the parameter space and so a smaller number of loss function evaluations is required for successful
minimization. This approach can be justified if, for example, a new HE formulation, an RBM is calibrated for, is expected
to be similar, from the detonation performance perspective, to some previously calibrated HE. This similarity can be
expected, for example, if the new and old HE formulations are two different lots of the same HE. The similarity implies
that the RBM parameter values should be similar for the new and old RBMs, and we choose to adjust just a couple of
them to adapt the old RBM to the new HE formulation without performing a comprehensive from-scratch calibration.

 1 INTRODUCTION

 The second route, a de facto standard for the SDT+DE calibration, is to approximately split the minimization problem
into several lower-dimensional ones by observing that even though all the RBM parameters affect the both SDT and DE
behaviors, some parameter might affect more SDT than DE, or the other way around. Typically, this happens when an
RBM is not a completely empirical model (e.g., a generic polynomial function, with no a priori connection to physics, fit to
the rate law), but a semi-empirical one where the total rate comprises several contributions, each representing a physically
distinct regime of detonation. For example, an Ignition & Growth (IG) RBM does have at least two contributions to the
decomposition rate, the first (ignition) is more important at the very onset of SDT [10]. The growth, being more important
towards the end of SDT, can in turn also have multiple terms, describing the low and high pressure/temperature behaviors
[3, 11, 2]. Finally, additional terms can be introduced to describe post-growth behavior such as carbon clustering [11, 3, 2].
It can be observed, for example, that the IG model parameters describing the kinetics of high pressure/temperature growth
and, especially of carbon clustering, affect the SDT performance only weakly. This allows one to approximately decouple
the minimization problem to have the following two-step procedure:
 • Step 1: Ignition and low temperature/pressure growth parameters are calibrated against the loss function that
 is based on SDT simulations only. This is computationally rather inexpensive, and so a significant number of
 parameters can be calibrated.
 • Step 2: Parameters corresponding to high temperature/pressure growth and carbon clustering are calibrated against
 DE simulations. This is computationally expensive, but the small number of model parameters describing these
 contributions makes it feasible.
Since the decoupling is only approximate, adjusting parameters in Step 2 also affects SDT. However, since the coupling
between steps is expected to not be very strong, several iterations of the two-step procedure are expected to result in an
overall good calibration. This approach has been widely used [3, 2, 4, 6], and a variation of this approach, where Step
1 is done not just for SDT, but for SDT combined with the DE for the lowest-radius RS, has been proposed recently
[5]. Possible problems with this “decoupled” approach include insufficient decoupling between detonation regimes on the
RBM level, which might require many iteration of the two-step procedure to converge. Furthermore, even if converged,
the calibration result will not correspond to the minimum of the total loss function. A trivial example of this is provided
in App. A.
 In this work, we propose yet another iterative approach to RBM calibration. The main motivation comes from the
desire to decrease the number of expensive DE CFD simulations. The idea comes from an observation that even though
each specific DE simulation might be very computationally expensive, variations of RBM parameter might not result in
large variations of the resulting detonation velocities. Indeed, the most expensive DE simulations are those for the largest
RS radii, where the resulting sensitivity to RBM parameters is weak since the detonation velocity is already very close to
the CJ detonation velocity that is determined solely by thermodynamics and not by RBM. Under these conditions, we
propose the following new two-step procedure:
 • Step 1: Use DE simulations to construct an approximation - a surrogate - to the dependence of the DE detonation
 velocity on some small variations of RBM parameters.
 • Step 2: Use the surrogate DE model within the total loss function to calibrate the RBM parameters.
Step 1 can be rather computationally demanding, but once the surrogate model (e.g., a local linear approximation to the
DE dependence on RBM parameters) is constructed, evaluation of the DE with the resulting surrogate model is practically
instantaneous. This means that Step 2 can involve (i) calibration with respect to all the relevant RBM parameters, and
(ii) the total, albeit approximate because of the surrogate DE, loss function. The combination of these two factors can
be expected to remedy the problems with the “brute-force” and “decoupled” approaches discussed above. On the other
hand, since the surrogate is only approximate, several iterations of the just proposed two-step procedure can be required,
where a surrogate is regenerated on each iteration to describe the DE in the area of the parameter space Step 2 led to.
In this work, we develop, implement and study the feasibility of the proposed RBM calibration method. As a system to
apply the method to we choose the AWSD reactive burn model to describe detonation of important high explosive PBX
9502 [2]. We emphasize that this is not a goal of this work to come up with a new better AWSD calibration than that
obtained in Ref. [2]. Instead, we use the problem setting from Ref. [2] to study the feasibility of the new proposed RBM
calibration method. The setting includes:
  1. A specific HE, PBX 9502.
  2. Selection of experimental results for this HE. We further limit the experimental data used in Ref. [2] to only that
 at the ambient temperature.
  3. Calibrated reactants/products EOS.

5 Sequential SDT+LsDE Calibration
Section 3 describes the construction of the linear surrogate DE (LsDE) model for the diameter effect. In this section, we
utilize this procedure to perform AWSD calibration. The proposed algorithm for such a calibration, briefly stated at the
end of Sec. 1, is elaborated on in Tab. 2.

 #0. Set the current values of AWSD parameters to be the corresponding reference values, i.e., those in Eq. (2).
 #1. As described in Sec. 3, generate an LsDE for each of the four RS radii considered in this work. The base node of
 each simplex correspond to the current values of AWSD parameters.
 #2. Construct the total loss function, Sec. 4, from (i) the SDT loss function based on SDT simulations and (ii) the
 DE loss function based on computationally extremely cheap evaluation of LsDE. Each evaluation of the loss function
 does thus involve four (number of shots) SDT simulations (run in parallel, ~5-10 seconds in total), and four (number
 of RS radii) almost instantaneous evaluations of LsDE models.
 #3. Minimize the total loss function with respect to variation of “active” (i.e., those that are varied) AWSD
 parameters. Minimization is performed with the Nelder-Mead algorithm.
 #4. The set of current AWSD parameters is updated with the values resulting from minimization in #3. Go to #1,
 unless the active set is deemed converged.

6 Sequential SDT+GPsDE Calibration
In Sec. 5, we introduce an approach to RBM calibration where a single simplex in the active parameter space is used
to produce an LsDE model. The LsDE model is then sequentially regenerated from scratch at each external iteration
of algorithm 2. The obvious deficiency of this approach is that once the LsDE is regenerated, previous simplexes are
discarded and not used anyhow even though they contain very valuable information - the exact DE at the simplexes’
nodes, calculated via computationally expensive shock-fitting simulations. It would be great to come up with a method to
construct a surrogate DE model using all the simplexes calculated up to a point point in time through multiple external
iterations of the algorithm in Tab. 2. Already for two simplexes, such a surrogate model would be non-linear, i.e., not
given by a hyperplane. Generally, a process of fitting a functional form through an available set of points is referred to as
regression. In this work, we employ the so called Gaussian process regression [16] to generate a non-linear surrogate DE
model.

 1. For at least a few first iterations of (2), the GPsDE model is used in the extrapolative regime, since a single
 SDT+GPsDE calibration run will likely result in the parameter values that sit outside the area of the parameter
 space explored by simplexes. The smoothness of µ∗ (x), critical for extrapolation, is determined by the kernel
 parameters:
 • Length scales σi define the scale on which µ∗ (x) changes.
 • Order ν determines the smoothness class of µ∗ (x) - the larger ν, the smoother µ∗ (x) [16].
 2. We observed that if σi and/or ν are too large, µ∗ (x), and, therefore, the resulting GPsDE model, “trusts” the
 simplex points (i.e., the slope/curvature they “suggest”) too much, producing a situation akin to fitting points with
 a high-degree polynomial where interpolation might be reasonable but extrapolation often becomes unbound and
 chaotic.
 3. On the other hand, small σi and/or ν produce very short-lived “memory” of local derivatives suggested by a simplex.
 Since a function sampled from a posterior GP, f (x) ∼ GP ∗ (0, k ∗ (x, x′ )), converges to 0 away from the training
 set points, this short-lived memory results in µ∗ (x) being equal to Eq. (11) almost everywhere, except for a small
 neighborhood of each simplex point. This results in a very non-smooth behavior of the GPsDE model.

7 Conclusion & Outlook
This work explores the feasibility of an RBM calibration approach where the computationally expensive DE simulations
are performed infrequently and only to generate a surrogate DE model. This surrogate model is then used to perform
the actual underlying (internal) calibration. Since the surrogate model is inherently approximate, it is updated once
the internal calibration run converges, and then the new iteration of the internal calibration starts, thus giving rise to
sequential calibration approach. For the specific problem setting, described at the end of Sec. 1, the proposed calibration
algorithm demonstrates successful convergence in just two iterations, Fig. 4. Most importantly, the proposed method is
expected to converge to the true minimum of the total exact loss function, as discussed in Sec. 1. Two flavors of the
surrogate DE model are developed, linear and Gaussian process-based. The both flavors demonstrate essentially identical
convergence under the problem setting in this work, but the latter one, being non-linear, is potentially more powerful.
 In what follows, we give a few rather disconnected commentaries on the performance of the proposed calibration
method and its potential use:
 1. The non-linear Gaussian process-based model, GPsDE, being more powerful, also has more “moving parts” than the
 linear surrogate model, LsDE. This concerns both the code base that needs to be maintained and tested, as well as
 dealing with hyperparameters. The latter can be especially critical, since very non-optimal values of hyperparameters
 can potentially result in non-convergence of the calibration algorithm. Under these conditions, the choice between
 LsDE and GPsDE in a specific setting remains a non-trivial problem and the relative performance of the two
 surrogate model flavors needs to be studied further.
 2. Because the surrogate DE model substitutes the expensive exact one in the loss evaluation, the total loss function,
 even though approximate, is rather cheap to evaluate. This should allow for various uncertainty quantification
 (UQ) tools and methods to become practical. For example, one can attempt to extract uncertainties for values of
 calibrated RBM parameters.
