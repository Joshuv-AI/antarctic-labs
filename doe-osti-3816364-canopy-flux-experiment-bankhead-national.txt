1.0 Summary
 The Canopy Flux Experiment Bankhead National Forest (CAFE-BNF; AFC10212) is a collaborative field
 campaign led by Washington State University (WSU) in partnership with the National Center for
 Atmospheric Research at the U.S. Department of Energy Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM)
 third Mobile Facility (AMF3) site in Bankhead National Forest, Alabama. The campaign examines how
 momentum, heat, water vapor, and carbon dioxide are exchanged from the forest floor through the canopy
 and roughness sublayer into the overlying atmospheric boundary layer. Its central focus is the morning
 transition, when changes in canopy coupling and the timing of heat and moisture release can influence
 boundary-layer development and the conditions supporting shallow convection.

 The observational system has expanded substantially since the previous interim report. Six WSU
 eddy-covariance (EC) systems now operate at 3.5, 8, 16, 26, 32, and 42 m on the approximately 43 m S10
 tower, complementing three ARM EC systems at 20, 36, and 43 m. In April 2026, an additional WSU EC
 system was installed with a sonic anemometer at 2 m on a 3 m tripod approximately 10 m from the tower.
 The tripod installation also includes the principal surface-energy-balance measurements and profiles of
 soil temperature, soil moisture, and soil heat flux. The combined 10-level array spans the forest floor,
 lower and upper canopy, canopy top, roughness sublayer, and air above the canopy.

1.1 Campaign Progress and Operational Status
The WSU EC systems have generally operated reliably and transmit high-frequency measurements to the
Principal Investigator laboratory at WSU for daily performance monitoring. The team conducts routine
processing, quality control, and archiving and meets weekly to review instrument status and preliminary
analyses. These workflows have produced an internally consistent multilevel data set suitable for
examining flux profiles, scalar coupling, coherent events, and the downward reach of canopy-top
motions.

Lightning-related disruptions and prolonged rainfall during summer 2026 caused gaps during an
important part of the growing season. A complete annual cycle in 2027 is therefore needed to recover
seasonal coverage and to distinguish recurring responses to phenology and soil moisture from behavior
associated with individual storms or short sampling periods.

1.2 Requested Renewal and Scientific Scope
The original authorization ended in August 2026. We request renewal through December 31, 2027, to
maintain the existing measurement array and obtain a complete 2027 annual cycle. The requested renewal
is limited to continued field observations, instrument operation, data quality control, and analysis of the
resulting measurements. Numerical simulations are not part of the CAFE-BNF extension request.

The extended observations will support the following objectives:
 • Quantify seasonal and diurnal changes in the vertical partitioning of momentum, sensible heat, latent
 heat, and carbon dioxide exchange.

 2

  • Determine how canopy phenology, soil moisture, and atmospheric stability regulate coupling from
 the forest floor through the canopy and roughness sublayer.
  • Identify the coherent motions that carry momentum and scalar transport during the morning transition
 and determine how far those motions penetrate the canopy.
  • Provide a quality-controlled multi-season data set that complements ARM surface, boundary-layer,
 and cloud observations and supports future community analyses.

 The renewal would preserve a functioning observational capability, maximize the scientific return from
 the deployment, and contribute process-level evidence relevant to ARM and U.S. Department of Energy
 Atmospheric System Research (ASR) priorities in land-atmosphere coupling and boundary-layer
 development.

2.0 Preliminary Results
 The results below use the five simultaneous WSU tower levels available during summer 2025 (3.5, 8, 16,
 26, and 32 m). They therefore characterize the initial canopy-to-roughness-sublayer observational
 column; the 42 m tower system and 2 m forest-floor system were added later and are not included in these
 analyses. The examples establish the value of vertical resolution and motivate continued sampling across
 seasons.

 2.1 Persistent Vertical Organization of Turbulent Fluxes
 Mean diurnal composites for June-August 2025 show systematic height dependence in sensible heat,
 latent heat, and carbon dioxide fluxes (Figure 2). Daytime sensible and latent heat fluxes generally
 increase from the lower canopy toward the canopy top and roughness sublayer, while carbon dioxide
 fluxes differ in both magnitude and sign among levels. The scalar-specific profiles indicate that a single
 above-canopy measurement cannot identify the source, sink, and transport contributions within the
 canopy. Latent heat flux at 3.5 m can reach roughly one-third of the value at 32 m during daytime,
 indicating appreciable turbulent water-vapor exchange deep within the canopy.

 structure from 3.5 to 31.5 m.

2.2 Morning Transition Development and Scalar Coupling
Five-minute examples on June 4, 2025, show that temperature and water-vapor fluctuations change across
neutral, transition, and weakly unstable periods (Figure 3). Ramp-like structures become more evident
after sunrise, particularly near and above the canopy top, while temperature-humidity correspondence is
weaker at some within-canopy levels. Companion time-height analyses show that momentum and scalar
exchange strengthen first near the canopy top and then extend downward. These patterns support a layer-
and regime-dependent view of coupling rather than an assumption that the full canopy responds
simultaneously to the morning transition.

 4

Figure 3. Examples of air-temperature and water-vapor-density fluctuations at five WSU levels during
 neutral, morning-transition, and weakly unstable periods on June 4, 2025. The examples
 illustrate changing scalar organization across height and stability regime.

Quadrant analyses further indicate that strong sweep and ejection events make substantial contributions to
canopy transport, but their relative contributions vary with height, time, and transported quantity. After
sunrise, momentum sweeps can contribute more strongly than momentum ejections despite occupying a
smaller fraction of the sampling time. Heat- and moisture-transport events also become more pronounced
during the transition. Because momentum and scalar quadrants are defined from different fluctuation
pairs, the analyses are interpreted separately rather than assuming that a momentum sweep predetermines
the sign of heat or water-vapor transport.

2.3 Wind Regime Controls on Sweep Intensity and Downward Reach
A preliminary event-tracking analysis follows momentum sweeps downward from 32 m through
successively lower tower levels. For June 2025 daytime conditions, stronger shear and larger vertical
momentum-flux gradients are associated with greater sweep intensity and sweep propagation speed
increases with shear (Figure 4a). Wind-speed regimes occupy distinct dynamical environments, showing
that background forcing must be considered when comparing coherent events.

Event intensity and downward reach are nevertheless distinct properties. The strongest
momentum-transport events do not consistently penetrate to the deepest observed levels (Figure 4b).
Thus, vertical reach is useful evidence of cross-canopy connectivity, but transport-effective coupling also
requires cross-height coherence and a measurable flux contribution. This result motivates the full-year
measurements needed to evaluate how canopy state, atmospheric stability, and wind regime jointly
control event penetration and transport.

 5

 Figure 4. Preliminary June 2025 daytime sweep statistics. Upper panels show sweep intensity and
 propagation speed in relation to canopy-top shear and the vertical momentum-flux gradient,
 colored by upper-level wind regime. Lower panels compare sweep intensity with estimated
 downward reach and propagation speed. Event strength does not map one to one onto
 penetration depth.

3.0 Publications and Research Products
 No peer-reviewed publications have yet resulted from CAFE-BNF. The team is currently developing two
 manuscripts based on the observational analyses summarized above. One manuscript examines the
 vertical organization of momentum and scalar fluxes, coherent-event contributions, and canopy coupling
 during the morning transition. The second examines how wind regime and canopy-top forcing organize
 sweep intensity, propagation, and downward penetration. Both manuscripts are in preparation; no
 publication status is claimed in this report.

 6

 Preliminary findings have also been communicated through scientific presentations, including an
 ARM/ASR plenary presentation in August 2026 and a research presentation at Pacific Northwest National
 Laboratory. Continued observations through 2027 will allow the manuscripts to test whether the initial
 relationships persist across a complete range of seasonal and hydrometeorological conditions.

4.0 Lessons Learned
 The multilevel systems demonstrate the scientific and operational value of real-time data transmission,
 daily instrument monitoring, and routine cross-level quality checks. The summer 2026 lightning and
 prolonged rainfall also demonstrate that a single growing season may not provide continuous coverage of
 all key regimes. Maintaining the deployed array through a complete 2027 annual cycle is the most direct
 way to recover this coverage and evaluate seasonal repeatability.
