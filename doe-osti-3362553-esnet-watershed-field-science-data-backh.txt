1.0 Summary
 ESnet’s Watershed Field Science Data Backhaul Campaign is intended to improve the ability to gather
 data from sensors deployed across the East River and Trail Creek watersheds, with a special focus on the
 Snodgrass distributed sensor area, the Pumphouse instruments with co-located U.S. Department of
 Energy (DOE) Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) User Facility Surface Atmosphere Integrated
 Field Laboratory (SAIL) tethered balloon setup, and the former ARM SAIL campaign’s second ARM
 Mobile Facility (AMF2) site by the Watershed Function Science Focus Area (WFSFA) program. This
 program is not funded by ARM, but collaborates with ARM as part of the SAIL campaign, collocated
 with the WFSFA field sites in Colorado’s East River Watershed.

 This project supports collection and movement of WFSFA data.

 Purposes of this effort:
  • To improve data backhaul capabilities for WFSFA and ESnet’s ability to serve remote-site sensor
 systems
  • To identify how science use of advanced wireless data backhaul capabilities will impact workflows
 served by ESnet and other DOE user facilities.

 Three main lines of effort (see Figure 1):
 1. Evaluation of non-terrestrial networks for science, and exploring how to improve ESnet and WFSFA
 field data capabilities using Starlink to support scientific data backhaul.
 2. Testing scientific use-cases for 5G and terrestrial shared-spectrum capabilities like Citizens
 Broadband Radio Service (CBRS), directional WiFi, WiFi HaLow, LoRa (a sensor radio protocol,
 short for Long Range), mmWave point-to-point transmission, etc. and implications of these use cases
 for ESnet’s mission and capabilities.
 3. Assessing opportunities to use Internet of Things (IoT) technologies to develop new services to
 support science users. Examples of these services might include CBRS performance prediction and
 technology consulting, inclusion of wireless capacity in reservation services like the On-demand
 Secure Circuits and Advance Reservation System (OSCARS), or other ways of improving the ability
 of science teams to use wireless data backhaul when needed.

 The effort was motivated by discussions between ESnet and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory
 (LBNL)’s Earth and Environmental Sciences Area (EESA), on self-driving field laboratories and future
 networking and data mobility services needed to support this vision.

2.0 Results

 2.1 5G Base Station
 We deployed a tower for private cellular (5G Stand Alone in CBRS spectrum) on Crested Butte
 Mountain, (i.e., a ski resort with limited power access) to provide coverage in surrounding environmental
 research field sites. This was a very early use of 5G SA Citizen Band Radio Service deployment, and one
 of a very few such deployments attempted in a remote site setting, using Starlink backhaul to create a
 private cellular service area for scientific IoT. The purpose of this effort was to assess the suitability of
 private cellular to support earth and environmental research and included:
  • Radio frequency (RF) modeling of the area to assess the possible performance and antenna/beam
 forming needed to support the Snodgrass sensor field
  • Identification, configuration, and testing of current off-the-shelf equipment (as of 2022/23) for this
 new technology
  • Design of a tower, leveraging resources from the ARM AMF2 deployment to Crested Butte, along
 with a Snodgrass solar-powered site local-area distribution station, which provided WiFi, LoRA,
 WiFi HaLow, and Ethernet and experimental support
  • Remote operation and testing of reliability and ability to manage the system from California
  • Learning and assessment of CBRS capabilities in this mission-space and environment.

2.2 Cellular Tower Physical and Electrical Setup
Figure 3 presents the electrical setup of the installed base station. Backhaul connectivity is provided
through Starlink, and 5G connectivity is provided by a Baicells Aurora 243 gNodeB with a directional
antenna (L-com HG-1742DP-17EG-NM). A Raspberry Pi provides remote access and control over all
instruments in this local network. A Cradlepoint R1900 router is included for testing and evaluation
purposes. It is remotely configurable and can connect to commercial cellular networks, our private 5G
network, WiFi, and the Starlink LAN.

Figure 2. Instruments deployed on the top of the tower (5G gNodeB with directional antenna, camera,
 Starlink dish).

The power and cost requirements for the deployed system do not allow off-grid installations, so the
system was installed in a ski resort at a location with line power. The system was built with commercial
hardware, limiting the amount of electrical and regulatory work. The only electrical work involved in the
installation was the installation of ground rods and a power outlet by a local licensed electrician. The
power is distributed using a power strip with circuit breaker, surge suppression, and isolated outputs to
maximally protect hardware in case of failures and lightning strikes. In this context, proper ground
potential distribution to all devices on the tower is critical. All tower legs (and/or guy wires) should be
grounded and conform to regulations, and no sharp turns should be present in ground cables.
Furthermore, one should take into account subsurface conditions when planning for ground rod
installation. When installing hardware on the tower, one should make sure to install all hardware below
the lightning “cone of safety”. To further reduce electrical hazards, no AC voltage was supplied to the
instruments mounted outside the electrical enclosure. Only low DC voltage and data signals were
distributed to the top of the tower.

2.3 Mechanical Setup
For the construction of the 20’ tower on a concrete foundation, we selected the Rohn 25G tower series
hardware. This series has a configuration for deployments without guy wires, which is a critical safety
requirement for ski areas. Furthermore, short tower sections (5’ long) are available, which simplifies
logistics. In order to facilitate a safe and simple deployment, we made sure to build a hinged tower that
can fully be assembled on the ground, eliminating the need to climb the tower. After assembly, the tower
can be erected in a safe controlled way by using ratcheting ropes. To allow for such a hinged deployment
in a tower without guy wires, we developed a novel hinging mechanism for the ROHN 25GSSB base
plate, as depicted in Figure 4. For all connections to the concrete foundation, chemical anchors were used.
Furthermore, all bolts exposed to (wind induced) vibrations were locked with threadlock, locknuts,
locking washers, or double nuts. A smooth installation requires appropriate battery powered tools
(including a hammer drill, reciprocating saw, and screwdrivers) and rebar-cutting drill bits.

The tower is equipped with a reinforced glass fiber enclosure, which allows for wireless devices in the
box. The enclosure should be over-dimensioned, and take heat dispensing and cable storage into account
(Figure 5).

2.4 Logistics and Regulations
In order to obtain and maintain a safe, effective, and compliant installation, several precautions must be
taken. Manufacturer instructions and data sheets provide important guidelines that should not be omitted.
Furthermore, installers should familiarize themselves with local laws and regulations in terms of electrical
installations, antenna towers, and building requirements. In our deployment, electrical work was
performed by a local licensed electrician. Furthermore, permits for the installation of the tower were
obtained from the U.S. Forest Service, Gunnison County, and Vail Ski Resorts. The latter also provided

land access permits. The administrative burden and timeline to obtain permits from all involved
regulating entities should be accounted for when planning a deployment.

From a more practical and safety point of view (especially given its deployment in a ski resort), the tower
was equipped with safety markings (paint and signage), guy wires were omitted, and padding was
installed around the tower.

2.6 Field Hubs
In order to provide local wireless coverage at environmental sensor sites and support a variety of network
technologies, we developed low-power wireless connectivity hubs, as depicted in Figure 8. These hubs

 6

 are equipped with a Cradlepoint R1900 router that connects to the 5G network and provides local
 connectivity through WiFi, Ethernet, LoRa, and several sensor technologies, as well as limited edge
 computing capabilities and remote monitoring and control. The entire system consumes less than 15 W,
 which allows off-grid 24/7 operation on a small (400 Wp) solar installation. We selected a 400 W Renogy
 solar panel + charge controller system with three sealed batteries (12V, 125 Ah).

 Other low-power user equipment (UE) that can be installed in outdoor configurations, includes a
 Raspberry Pi with an RM502x 5G HAT (available through WaveShare).

 Figure 8. A low-power wireless connectivity hub.

 2.7 Multi-Area Starlink Use and Remote Management Testing
 In addition to the terrestrial RF field hubs, we also deployed and evaluated Starlink as a remote site option
 for data mobility. Starlink units were placed at Trail Creek, at the Fish Hatchery, and the Crested Butte
 Pumphouse. These were operated remotely from California and their performance was evaluated. Overall,
 and as anticipated, uplink performance was adequate for many applications, reliability was quite good
 despite inclement weather and conditions, and no units needed to be replaced. As part of this effort, we
 supported radar, hydrology, and weather measurement science efforts, and this effort also allowed us to
 develop improved methods to provision and manage distributed systems in remote locations supporting
 LBNL science efforts.

4.0 Lessons Learned
 This deployment and collaboration between WFSFA and ESnet was extremely beneficial for helping us
 build out a Wireless Edge program and set of services. Based on this collaboration, ESnet has gone on to
 deploy similar Wireless Edge systems supporting other projects in California, Tasmania, Nevada, and
 Illinois. We are also including Wireless Edge capabilities in our plans for ESnet7, our next-generation
 network for DOE science data.
