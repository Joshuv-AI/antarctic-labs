Nonparametric Riemannian Empirical Bayes, and Denoising Measurements on Manifolds
Adam Quinn Jaffe, Leonardo V. Santoro, Bodhisattva Sen

Abstract
We initiate the study of nonparametric empirical Bayes denoising methods in the setting where both the latent variables and their measurements lie on a compact Riemannian manifold, and where the likelihood is a Riemannian Gaussian distribution. Our starting point is a novel Tweedie-Eddington formula for Riemannian Gaussian mixture models which identifies a certain surrogate oracle denoiser in terms of the marginal distribution of the measurements; it avoids the explicit computation of the posterior Fréchet mean (as required by the Bayes denoiser) via a first-order approximation, hence we refer to it as the “tangential” Bayes denoiser. We show that this surrogate oracle achieves nearly the Bayes risk in a low-noise regime, we construct a fully data-driven approximation of it using the spectral theory of the Laplace-Beltrami operator, and we establish finite-sample rates of convergence for the distance between the the surrogate oracle and its approximation. Contrasting the nearly-parametric rates from the Euclidean setting, the rates in the Riemannian setting are slower due to the singularities of the Riemannian Gaussian density at the cut locus of its Fréchet mean; in the special case of the circle we establish matching lower bounds which show that our proposed denoiser is minimax-optimal, and that the denoising problem exhibits a genuinely nonparametric rate of convergence. Our theory and simulations reveal that pooling information across different measurements—namely, by shrinking towards high-density regions and stretching away from low-density regions—can substantially outperform the naïve denoiser that treats each measurement separately. Lastly, we implement our methodology in two scientific applications: in astronomy, the sphere-valued problem of denoising the locations of gamma ray bursts; in structural biology, the torus-valued problem of denoising pairs of torsion angles of adjacent amino acids in a protein (i.e., the Ramachandran plot).

Introduction

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a compact connected Riemannian manifold with geodesic distance
d
, and consider the problem of estimating a
latent variable

\Theta\in\mathcal{M}
given a
measurement

X\in\mathcal{M}
thereof. This problem arises naturally in many scientific settings, and in this paper we are particularly motivated by two applications:
the first takes place on the sphere
\mathcal{M}:=\mathbb{S}^{2}
and requires denoising the positions of a catalog of observed gamma ray bursts, which is a long-standing problem in astronomy known as
gamma-ray burst localization

[
41
,
18
,
8
]
;
the second takes place on the torus
\mathcal{M}:=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
and requires denoising the pairs of torsion angles formed by adjacent amino acids along a protein, usually referred to as a
Ramachandran plot

[
69
,
36
,
64
,
60
]
.
We are also motivated by potential applications to the
orientation estimation
problem from cryogenic electron microscopy
[
32
,
58
,
86
]
which takes place on the Lie group of rotations,
\mathcal{M}=\SO(3)
.

To formulate our problem more precisely, suppose that
G
is a fixed but unknown probability distribution on
\mathcal{M}
, and consider the following model for a pair of random variables
(\Theta,X)\in\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}
:

\Theta\sim G,\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad(X\mid\Theta=\theta)\sim P_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}.

(1)

Here,
\{P_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}\}_{\theta\in\mathcal{M}}
is the family of
Riemannian Gaussian distributions

[
62
]
on
\mathcal{M}
, defined so that
P_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}
has a density with respect to the volume form
\textnormal{d}x
of
\mathcal{M}
given by

p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}(x,\theta)}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)

(2)

for all
\theta,x\in\mathcal{M}
, where
\sigma^{2}>0
is a fixed and known quantity representing the scale of the noise of the measurement
X
of
\Theta
.
We emphasize that both
\Theta
and
X
are elements of the Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
.
We also write

f(x):=\int_{\mathcal{M}}p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x)\,\textnormal{d}G(\theta),\qquad\mbox{for }x\in\mathcal{M}

for the marginal density of
X
. Further, we consider the setting that
(\Theta_{1},X_{1}),\ldots,(\Theta_{n},X_{n})
are i.i.d. from model (
1
), meaning
\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{n}
is an ensemble of unobserved latent variables and
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are measurements thereof.
Our goal is to estimate (or predict) the latent variables
\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{n}
from the measurements
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, which can be seen as the analog of a standard
denoising
problem but for data living on a Riemannian manifold.

Mathematically, we pose the denoising problem as aiming to find a function
\delta:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
, called a
denoiser
, achieving a small value of the following risk, which is the average expected squared geodesic distance when integrated over the joint distribution of
(\Theta_{1},X_{1}),\ldots,(\Theta_{n},X_{n})
:

\begin{cases}\textnormal{minimize}&\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d^{2}(\delta(X_{i}),\Theta_{i})\right]\\
\textnormal{over}&\delta:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}.\end{cases}

(3)

By exchangeability, problem (
3
) is equivalent to the following, in which we integrate over the joint distribution of
(\Theta,X)
:

\begin{cases}\textnormal{minimize}&\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(\delta(X),\Theta)]\\
\textnormal{over}&\delta:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M},\end{cases}

(4)

and standard Bayesian arguments show that the solution to (
4
) is the minimizer of the posterior loss, namely the
posterior Fréchet mean

\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(x)\;\in\;\underset{\vartheta\in\mathcal{M}}{\arg\min}\;\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(\vartheta,\Theta)\,|\,X=x].

(5)

(In fact,
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
is also optimal in a generalization of problem (
3
) in which a different denoiser
\delta_{i}
can be applied to each coordinate and each
\delta_{i}
can depend on all of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.)
We refer to
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
as the
oracle Bayes denoiser
, since it depends on the unknown distribution
G
of
\Theta
and hence cannot be implemented in most statistical settings.

Nonetheless, the
empirical Bayes
approach to denoising is a collection of methods for constructing a fully data-driven denoiser
\hat{\delta}
which achieves nearly the same risk as the oracle denoiser
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
; such
\hat{\delta}
often have a substantially smaller risk than the
naïve denoiser

\delta_{\mathcal{N}}(x):=x

(6)

which is implicitly used in works that do not employ techniques from empirical Bayes.
In the standard Euclidean setting, there is a large literature on statistical theory and methodology for empirical Bayes approaches to denoising, e.g.,
[
89
,
47
,
73
,
78
,
70
,
71
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
.

In this paper, we develop a nonparametric empirical Bayes approach to denoising on compact Riemannian manifolds.
For the Riemannian Gaussian mixture model in (
1
) and (
2
), we derive an intrinsic version of the Tweedie-Eddington formula
[
20
,
71
,
43
]
which does not directly characterize the oracle Bayes denoiser
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
but rather characterizes a surrogate denoiser that we call the
oracle tangential Bayes denoiser

\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
.
This suggests denoising by a plug-in procedure, leading to a fully data-driven denoiser that we call the
empirical tangential Bayes denoiser

\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
: first, estimate the marginal density
f
of
X
by
\hat{f}_{n}
, then compute its score
\nabla\log\hat{f}_{n}
, and finally adjust each data point along the resulting vector field (hence shrinking the data towards high-density regions and stretching the data away from low-density regions).
In the terminology of
[
24
,
25
]
, this is an instance of
f
-modeling
, and the resulting estimator is simple to implement and computationally tractable.
It turns out that
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
is provably close to
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
when
\sigma^{2}
is small, and that
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
is provably close to
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
when
n
is large, so our proposed denoiser can achieve nearly the Bayes risk in the asymptotic regime of interest.

There are two reasons for using
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
instead of
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
in this work.
The first is that
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
is directly identifiable in terms of the marginal density
f
of
X
via our Tweedie-Eddington formula (see Lemma
3.1
), while
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
is not.
The second is that
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
is much easier to compute than
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
, which typically must be numerically approximated using iterative procedures from Riemannian optimization (e.g.,
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
26
,
59
,
77
]
); even worse, such computations must be performed separately for each evaluation
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(X_{1}),\ldots,\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(X_{n})
, which can be expensive in practice.
However, in order to reasonably change our target from
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
to
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
, we must establish that
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
are indeed close in some sense, and this is a technical challenge; the oracle Bayes denoiser
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
is exactly a (posterior) Fréchet mean, and existing analyses of Fréchet means require strict assumptions that exclude our setting of interest.
For example, some results (e.g.,
[
11
,
75
,
88
]
) restrict to nonpositively curved Riemannian manifolds
\mathcal{M}
, but this excludes the basic setting of the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
which is fundamental in applications of directional statistics.
Other results (e.g.,
[
29
,
10
,
27
,
40
,
74
]
) allow general Riemannian manifolds
\mathcal{M}
and instead require the distribution of the data to satisfy some small-support condition, with respect to the curvature of
\mathcal{M}
; however, this excludes the empirical Bayes setting when
G
can be fully-supported.
Thus, in order to establish quantitative results on the closeness of
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
for general compact Riemannian manifolds, we develop a new technique for analyzing the posterior Frećhet mean in the small-noise asymptotic regime,
\sigma^{2}\to 0
.

Another technical difficulty in this work is that Riemannian Gaussian mixture densities have worse smoothness than Euclidean Gaussian mixture densities, owing to the fact that the squared geodesic distance in (
2
) can be nonsmooth at its cut locus.
Thus, in the Riemannian setting our denoiser
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
exhibits genuinely nonparametric rates of convergence depending on the smoothness of the density
g
of
G
, rather than the nearly-parametric behavior familiar from Euclidean empirical Bayes
[
47
,
9
,
73
]
.
While we can explicitly describe these rates of convergence in several special cases of interest (and provide matching lower bounds in the case of the circle
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{1}
), a complete analysis would require precise quantitative control on the smoothing properties of Riemannian Gaussian mixture densities and their singular behavior near cut loci.
Such a theory is not yet available in full generality, except in some special cases.

points on the sphere,

.
Here,

is a mixture of 5 Riemannian Gaussian distributions, at roughly equally-spaced positions where the squared scale parameter of each component is
\tau^{2}=0.05
, and the likelihood is Riemannian Gaussian with squared scale parameter equal to
\sigma^{2}=0.25
.
Concretely, we note that the Bayes risk is
0.211
while our empirical Bayes denoiser
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}

0.216
and the naïve denoiser

; in other words, the risk of

In this work we chose the likelihood (
2
) to be a particular generalization of Gaussian distributions to Riemannian manifolds that is often attributed to Pennec
[
62
]
, but it is worth noting that there are multiple possible generalizations, e.g., wrapped Gaussians
[

]
and the heat-kernel Gaussians
[

]
.
Our choice of Pennec’s Riemannian Gaussian (

.

1.1
Summary of Results

We recall some basic elements of Riemannian manifolds needed to state our results, but we direct the reader to Section
2
for further detail.
Recall that each
x\in\mathcal{M}
admits a Hilbert space
\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})
called the
tangent space
of
\mathcal{M}
at
x
which represents the best linear approximation of
\mathcal{M}
, and
\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})
is endowed with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{x}
and a norm
\|\cdot\|_{x}
.
Further, the
exponential map

\Exp_{x}:\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})\to\mathcal{M}
for each
x\in\mathcal{M}
is a smooth map sending each tangent vector
v\in\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})
to the point on
\mathcal{M}
which results from “shooting” distance
\|v\|_{x}
in the direction
v/\|v\|_{x}
from
x
.
Conversely, the
logarithmic map

\Log_{x}:\mathcal{M}\to\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})
for each
x\in\mathcal{M}
sends a point
\theta\in\mathcal{M}
to a vector
v\in\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})
satisfying
\Exp_{x}(v)=\theta
; in other words,
\Log_{x}
is a smooth right inverse of
\Exp_{x}
, wherever it is well-defined.
When
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the exponential and logarithmic maps are simply given by
\Exp_{x}(v)=v+x
and
\Log_{x}\theta=\theta-x
, hence
\Log_{x}\theta
can be regarded as a natural, intrinsic notion of displacement of
\theta
from
x
in
\mathcal{M}
.

The first main result of this paper, as is common in most works on nonparametric empirical Bayes
[
20
,
71
,
43
]
, is to establish a Tweedie-Eddington formula which identifies a suitable oracle denoiser in terms of the marginal density
f
of
X
.
In the Riemannian setting, we use the simple geometric fact
\nabla_{x}d^{2}(x,\theta)=-2\Log_{x}\theta
to derive (Lemma
3.1
) the following Tweedie-Eddington formula

\mathbb{E}[\Log_{x}\Theta\,|\,X=x]=\sigma^{2}\nabla_{x}\log f(x).

(7)

While this does not directly characterize the Bayes denoiser
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
in terms of
f
, it suggests considering the following oracle-level denoiser as a surrogate:

\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x):=\Exp_{x}\left(\sigma^{2}\nabla_{x}\log f(x)\right)=\Exp_{x}\left(\mathbb{E}[\Log_{x}\Theta\,|\,X=x]\right).

(8)

To gain some intuition for this denoiser, note for each
x\in\mathcal{M}
that
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x)
is precisely the result of approximately solving the optimization problem (
5
) with a single step of Riemannian gradient descent, with stepsize
1/2
and with initialization
\delta_{\mathcal{N}}(x)=x
.
Indeed, at the point
\vartheta\in\mathcal{M}
, the objective function
\Phi_{x}(\vartheta):=\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(\vartheta,\Theta)\,|\,X=x]
satisfies
\nabla_{\vartheta}\Phi_{x}(\vartheta)=-2\mathbb{E}[\Log_{\vartheta}\Theta\,|\,X=x]
, hence we have

\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x)=\Exp_{x}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\vartheta}\Phi_{x}(x)\right).

(9)

(As explained in
[
62
, Section 4.6]
on general numerical algorithms for Fréchet means, this can also be seen as a single step of an approximate Riemannian Newton’s method, where the Hessian of
\Phi_{x}
is approximated by ignoring certain curvature effects.)
Since
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
is thus a first-order approximation of
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
, we refer to
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
as the
oracle tangential Bayes denoiser
.
Note in the Euclidean case of
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
that (
8
) coincides with the usual Tweedie-Eddington formula and yields
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}=\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
.

The second main result of the paper establishes that
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
has nearly the same risk as
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
as
\sigma^{2}\to 0
, which is the regime of interest in our scientific applications where
\sigma^{2}>0
is known to be small.
(The regime
\sigma^{2}\to 0
has been studied in some recent empirical Bayes works, e.g.,
[
52
,
53
]
.)
To state this, let us define the risk of any denoiser
\delta:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
as

R(\delta)=R(\delta,\sigma^{2}):=\mathbb{E}\left[d^{2}(\delta(X),\Theta)\right]=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathcal{M}}d^{2}(\delta(x),\theta)p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x)\textnormal{d}x\,\textnormal{d}G(\theta)

(10)

where we emphasize the (squared) scale
\sigma^{2}>0
of the likelihood in (
2
) when necessary.
Then, our main oracle-level result (Theorem
3.8
) states that for any compact Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
we have

0\leq R(\delta_{\mathcal{T}},\sigma^{2})-R(\delta_{\mathcal{B}},\sigma^{2})\leq O(\sigma^{5}),

(11)

meaning
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
have approximately the same risk as each other (relative to the usual scaling of
\sigma^{2}
).
This result may be expected in a heuristic sense since, if
\sigma^{2}>0
is small enough so that the conditional distribution of
\Theta
given
\{X=x\}
is highly concentrated near
x
where
\mathcal{M}
is well-approximated by
\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})
, then we have

\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x)=\Exp_{x}(\mathbb{E}[\Log_{x}\Theta\,|\,X=x])\approx\mathbb{E}[\Exp_{x}(\Log_{x}\Theta)\,|\,X=x])=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\,|\,X=x]\approx\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(x).

(12)

However, rigorously establishing (
11
) is difficult, because existing techniques for quantitatively analyzing Fréchet means do not apply in our setting where the conditional distribution of
\Theta
given
\{X=x\}
has full support for every
\sigma^{2}>0
.
To resolve this, we introduce a new technique which uses the fixed-point equation for Fréchet means (i.e., the first-order optimality conditions for (
5
)) to recursively bound
\Log_{x}(\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x))-\Log_{x}(\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(x))
in terms of
\Log_{x}(\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(x))
; in order to prevent this recursive bound from diverging, we need very sharp quantitative control on
\Log_{x}(\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x))
and related quantities, uniformly in
x\in\mathcal{M}
.
We also point out that, in the simulation studies of Section
4
, the denoisers
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
have extremely close risks for even moderate values of
\sigma^{2}>0
, to the point that the resulting curves are nearly indistinguishable.

Our last contribution is to develop an empirical Bayes approximation
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
of
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
and provide statistical guarantees for it; the approximation is based on an estimate
\hat{f}_{n}
of the density
f
which we then plug-in for
f
in expression (
8
).
Following the works
[
34
,
48
]
, we form a density estimate by utilizing the eigendecomposition of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta:L^{2}(\mathcal{M})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
, which is a useful method for adapting Fourier analysis to Riemannian manifolds.
More precisely, we consider the density estimator defined, for
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
being a countable basis of eigenfunctions of
\Delta
, via

\hat{f}_{n}(x):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K(x,X_{i},\ell_{n}),\qquad\textnormal{ where}\qquad K(x,y,\ell)=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\phi_{j}(x)\overline{\phi_{j}(y)}

(13)

for all
x\in\mathcal{M}
.
Then, we define the
empirical tangential Bayes denoiser
as

\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}(x):=\Exp_{x}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}\nabla_{x}\hat{f}_{n}(x)}{\max\{\hat{f}_{n}(x),\rho\}}\right)

(14)

for some carefully chosen
\ell_{n}\geq 0
and
\rho>0
.
We emphasize that
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}(x)
is very easy to compute; after computing the density estimate
\hat{f}_{n}
and its gradient
\nabla_{x}\hat{f}_{n}(x)
, all subsequent evaluations of
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}(x)
require only computing the exponential map
\Exp_{x}(\cdot)
, which relies on the local geometry of
\mathcal{M}
near
x
.

We establish explicit rates of convergence for the empirical tangential Bayes denoiser
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
.
To state this, let us define the distance of an arbitrary denoiser
\delta:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
from
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
as

D_{\mathcal{T}}(\delta):=\mathbb{E}\left[d^{2}(\delta(X),\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(X))\right]=\int_{\mathcal{M}}d^{2}(\delta(x),\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x))f(x)\textnormal{d}x.

(15)

While in the Euclidean setting of
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
we have that
D_{\mathcal{T}}(\delta)
exactly equals the excess risk relative to the tangential Bayes (equivalently, Bayes) denoiser, i.e.,
R(\delta)=R(\delta_{\mathcal{T}})+D_{\mathcal{T}}(\delta)
, in the Riemannian setting we have only

R(\delta)\leq(1+\varepsilon)R(\delta_{\mathcal{T}})+C_{\varepsilon}D_{\mathcal{T}}(\delta)
for all
\varepsilon>0
(Lemma
3.18
); as such, bounding
D_{\mathcal{T}}
leads to a non-sharp oracle inequality for the risk, but which can be made arbitrarily close to sharp.
Using this natural notion of distance, we show (Theorem
3.17
) that, under suitable conditions, there exists
0<\gamma<1
, such that choosing
\ell_{n}
and
\rho
appropriately leads to the bound

\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}d^{2}\left(\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}(x),\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x)\right)f(x)\textnormal{d}x\right]\lesssim_{\mathcal{M},G,\sigma^{2},\gamma}\frac{1}{n^{\gamma}}

(16)

with constants that do not depend on
n
.
Along the way, we also develop (Proposition
3.16
) some results on the rate of convergence of density estimation and gradient density estimation for Riemannian Gaussian mixtures, which slightly extend and sharpen some results from the earlier work
[
34
]
.

It turns out that the rate of convergence of
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
(i.e., the exponent
0<\gamma<1
above) depends directly on the smoothness of the marginal density
f
of
X
, which can suffer from the fact that the squared geodesic distance functional is not globally smooth and its Hessian exhibits singular behaviors near the cut locus (Example
3.11
).
This is a phenomenon not seen in the Euclidean setting, where the density of a Euclidean Gaussian mixture is always infinitely differentiable (in fact, analytic) irrespective of the regularity of the mixing measure.
So, while our theory holds under direct assumptions on the smoothness of
f
, it is natural to ask whether smoothness of
f
can be instead guaranteed from suitable smoothness of
g
. It turns out that this is a hard question which depends on the geometry of
\mathcal{M}
and must be worked out on a case-by-case basis; we address this problem in detail (Subsection
3.3
) for several geometries of interest (e.g.,
\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{2},\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and
\SO(3)
).

Consequently, our rates of convergence for
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
are polynomial but slower than parametric.
In the case of the circle
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{1}
, we show (Example
3.21
and Theorem
3.22
) matching upper and lower bounds; if
\mathcal{G}_{s}(\alpha,\beta,L)
denotes the set of densities
g:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\alpha\leq g\leq\beta
and
\|g^{(s)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\leq L
and we infimize over all measurable functions
\hat{\delta}:\mathbb{S}^{1}\times(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{n}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
, then (including the dependence on
g
for emphasis) we have

\inf_{\hat{\delta}}\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{s}(\alpha,\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}_{g}\left[\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}d^{2}\left(\hat{\delta}(x;X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}),\delta_{\mathcal{T},g}(x)\right)f_{g}(x)\textnormal{d}x\right]\asymp_{\sigma^{2},s,\alpha,\beta,L}\frac{1}{n^{\frac{2s+2}{2s+5}}}

(17)

with constants that do not depend on
n
, and where
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
achieves the infimal rate up to constants.
In other words, our proposed empirical tangential Bayes denoiser is minimax-optimal (with no additional logarithmic factors), and the minimax rate is genuinely nonparametric.
The rate should be understood as
n^{-2p/(2p+1)}
for
p=(s+2)-1
which reflects the fact that the difficulty of denoising reduces to the difficulty of estimating the gradient of
f
, which is
(s+2)
-smooth whenever
g
is
s
-smooth (see Lemma
3.14
); our precise control of this smoothing effect in the special case of
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{1}
comes from the fact that
f
is given by a convolution of
g
with a kernel that is differentiable but not continuously differentiable.

These main results highlight the practical value of the Riemannian empirical Bayes approach to denoising.
That is, we have shown
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
has comparable risk to
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
when
\sigma^{2}
is small and that
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
has comparable risk to
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
when
n
is large; hence, we conclude that
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
has comparable risk to
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
when both
\sigma^{2}
is small and
n
is large.
Moreover, the empirical Bayes denoiser
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
is easy to compute.
While our theory determines an oracle choice of the hyperparameters
\ell_{n}
and
\rho
, we also emphasize that they can be selected in a fully data-driven way via cross-validation with an adaptation of Hyvärinen’s score-matching objective
[
42
,
84
]
to the Riemannian setting

[
19
]
.
We further explore these practical considerations in several detailed simulations in Section
4
, and in two scientific applications in Section
5
, which highlight the remarkable ability of empirical Bayes denoisers to adapt to latent structure in the data.
The proofs of all results (and the statements and proofs of some auxiliary results) are given in Appendix
A
, detail on the implementation of our denoisers is given in Appendix
B
, and additional figures are given in Appendix
C
.
All figures in this paper can be reproduced with the code available at
https://github.com/aqjaffe/RiemannianEB
, which also contains an easy-to-use Python package integrated with the
Geomstats
paradigm
[
59
]
.

Related Literature

The primary context for our work is the large and growing literature on nonparametric empirical Bayes, which has been summarized in the recent tutorials
[
43
,
50
]
.
In particular, we provide the first extension to the Riemannian setting of the principle that there exist fully data-driven denoisers that can be computed efficiently and achieve a risk that is nearly equal to the Bayes risk, even in the setting where the distribution of the latent variables is not assumed to have any particular parametric form; analogous results in the Euclidean setting have been established for Gaussian
[
89
,
47
,
73
,
78
]
and Poisson
[
46
,
76
]
likelihoods.
Such empirical Bayes methods are often traced back to the work of Robbins
[
71
]
, although they are known to have a longer history in astronomy
[
20
,
21
]
; recent interest in nonparametric empirical Bayes (and much of our modern terminology) was popularized by Efron
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
.

There is also a large literature on parametric empirical Bayes methods, where, typically, the assumption of a conjugate prior allows one to directly approximate the Bayes denoiser by generalized method of moments.
In the Euclidean case, this was initiated by the empirical Bayes interpretation of the James-Stein shrinkage estimator due to Efron and Morris
[
22
]
, and the very many subsequent developments can be found in textbook treatments
[
12
,
55
]
.
In the Riemannian case, we are only aware of one previous work developing empirical Bayes methodology, namely
[
87
]
which provides an empirical Bayes interpretation for a particular shrinkage estimator on the manifold of positive semi-definite matrices.
There is also the closely-related is work
[
57
]
which shows, in the setting of metric spaces with non-positive (Alexandrov) curvature, that a certain geodesic James-Stein estimator strictly dominates the Fréchet mean, although without utilizing any conjugate prior structure directly.

Another context for our work is the field of statistics on Riemannian manifolds, which includes theory and methodology in the general setting
[
5
,
6
,
59
]
and in specific geometries of interest; of particular importance are the Riemannian manifolds studied in directional statistics, outlined in
[
54
]
.
The phenomena in this work that the singularity of a Riemannian Gaussian density at the cut locus of its Fréchet mean can deteriorate the rate of convergence of empirical Bayes denoising is closely related to the “smeariness” for Fréchet mean estimation (which has been established in spheres
[
37
]
and in other geometries, e.g.,
[
27
,
40
]
).
There is some literature on Bayesian statistics on Riemannian manifolds
[
3
,
4
,
51
]
, which is typically focused on particular models tailored to each geometry of interest.
Overall, we emphasize that our assumptions on the geometry of
\mathcal{M}
are much more general than what is typically assumed in this field; we require only that
\mathcal{M}
is a compact connected Riemannian manifold, while other works occasionally require much further structure that can be limiting in some applications (e.g., non-positive curvature, constant curvature, homogeneity, Lie group structure).

Many aspects of this paper are closely related to existing literature on density estimation, deconvolution, and score matching on Riemannian manifolds.
Regarding density estimation, two popular methods are based on kernel smoothing using kernels that are made either from the geodesic distance
[
61
,
35
]
or the eigenfunctions of Laplace-Beltrami operator
[
34
]
.
Regarding deconvolution, a few methods have been developed in particular geometries of interest, e.g., hyperspheres
[
33
]
, the special orthogonal group
[
49
]
, and the hyperbolic plane
[
39
]
(but all with likelihoods that are not Riemannian Gaussian); these works develop methods based on Fourier analysis (or generalization thereof), and they exhibit slow rates of convergence also seen in the Euclidean setting
[
31
]
.
There is also a recent interest in score-matching on Riemannian manifolds
[
19
]
which studies Riemannian versions of the generative modeling procedures that have become popular in the Euclidean setting
[

,

]
.

Basics.
Throughout, we assume that
\mathcal{M}
is a Riemannian manifold of dimension
m\in\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
.
This means each point
x\in\mathcal{M}
has a
tangent space

\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})
, which is an
m
-dimensional vector space representing the best linear approximation of
\mathcal{M}
near
x
, as well as an inner product
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{x}
(hence also a norm
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{x}
) on
\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})
; we refer to
\{\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})\}_{x\in\mathcal{M}}
as the
tangent bundle
of
\mathcal{M}
and
\{\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{M}}
as the
metric tensor
of
\mathcal{M}
.
There also exists a
\sigma
-finite measure on
\mathcal{M}
called the
volume form
which is a generalization of the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and we write
\textnormal{d}x
for integration against the volume form (e.g.,
\int_{\mathcal{M}}h(x)\textnormal{d}x
for any bounded, measurable function
h:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
).
When
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
the tangent bundle is
\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})=\mathbb{R}^{m}
and the volume form is just the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
; when
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{2}
the tangent bundle is
\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})=(\textnormal{span}(x))^{\bot}
and the volume form is the uniform measure on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
; when
\mathcal{M}=\SO(3)
, the tangent bundle is
\Tan_{X}(\mathcal{M})=\{XA\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}:A=-A^{\top}\}
is the space of
3\times 3
antisymmetric matrices multiplied on the left by
X
, the metric tensor is
\langle V,W\rangle_{X}=-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}(X^{-1}VX^{-1}W)
, and the volume form is the Haar measure.

Metric.
One may also view
\mathcal{M}
as a metric space
(\mathcal{M},d)
, where
d
is a metric on
\mathcal{M}
called the
geodesic distance
defined as
d(x,y):=\inf_{\gamma(0)=x,\gamma(1)=y}\mathcal{L}(\gamma)
where the infimum is taken over all curves
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{M}
which are infinitely differentiable in the locally Euclidean structure of
\mathcal{M}
and where
\mathcal{L}(\gamma):=\int_{0}^{1}\|\gamma^{\prime}(t)\|_{\gamma(t)}\textnormal{d}t
; we always have
\gamma^{\prime}(0)\in\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})
, and
\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})
can in fact be defined as the closure of the span of all
\gamma^{\prime}(0)
, i.e., tangent vectors at
x
exactly correspond to velocities of curves starting at
x
.
Note that
\mathcal{L}(\gamma)
is the
length
of the curve
\gamma
, so that the geodesic distance
d(x,y)
is just the length of the shortest smooth curve connecting
x
to
y
.
A curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{M}
is called a
constant-speed geodesic
if it satisfies
d(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))=(t-s)d(\gamma(0),\gamma(1))
for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq 1
; if
\gamma
is a constant-speed geodesic then it is infinitely differentiable and we have
\|\gamma^{\prime}(t)\|_{\gamma(t)}=d(\gamma(0),\gamma(1))
, for all
t\in[0,1]
.
We say that
\mathcal{M}
is
compact
if the metric space
(\mathcal{M},d)
is compact, and that
\mathcal{M}
is
connected
if
(\mathcal{M},d)
is connected.
We define the
diameter
of
\mathcal{M}
as
\diam(\mathcal{M}):=\sup_{x,y\in\mathcal{M}}d(x,y)
.

Exponential and Logarithm.

For each point
x\in\mathcal{M}
, there is a natural correspondence between
\mathcal{M}
and its tangent bundle given as follows.
The
exponential map

\Exp_{x}:\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})\to\mathcal{M}
is defined via
\Exp_{x}(v):=\gamma(1)
for sufficiently small
\|v\|_{x}
where
\gamma
is the unique constant-speed geodesic with
\gamma(0)=x
and
\gamma^{\prime}(0)=v
(which is guaranteed to exist and be unique under suitable assumptions on
\mathcal{M}
, e.g. compactness) and then extended to all
v\in\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})
; in other words,
\Exp_{x}(v)
is the result of “shooting” from
x
in direction
v/\|v\|_{x}
for length
\|v\|_{x}.
The
logarithmic map
is defined as
\Log_{x}(y)=\gamma^{\prime}(0)
where
\gamma
is the constant-speed geodesic with
\gamma(0)=x
and
\gamma(1)=y
, if it exists and is unique; consequently, we have
\|\Log_{x}(y)\|_{x}=d(x,y)
whenever the left side is well-defined. Also, it is known that the exponential map is infinitely-differentiable on all of
\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})
; in fact the map
(x,v)\mapsto\Exp_{x}(v)
is smooth (i.e., infinitely-differentiable) as a function of both arguments
[
65
, Chapter 5.5.1]
.
In the Euclidean case
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we simply have
\Log_{x}(y)=y-x
and
\Exp_{x}(v)=v+x
, so the logarithmic map in general provides a natural notion of displacement in
\mathcal{M}
; in
\mathcal{M}=\SO(3)
we have
\Exp_{X}(V)=X\exp(X^{-1}V)
where
\exp(\cdot)
denotes the matrix exponential (defined via the usual power series) which explains the notation.
See Figure
2
for an illustration of the logarithmic and exponential maps.

Cut Locus.

For
x\in\mathcal{M}
, the
cut locus
of
x
, denoted
C_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
, is (roughly speaking) given by the closure of the set of all
y\in\mathcal{M}
such that there is more than one constant-speed geodesic from
x
to
y
; see
[
1
, Definition 1.3.9]
for a precise definition.
Then, we may view the logarithmic map as a function
\Log_{x}:\mathcal{M}\setminus C_{x}\to\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})
, and this is in fact its maximal domain of well-definition.
It is also known that the cut locus
C_{x}
has measure zero under the volume form, for each
x\in\mathcal{M}
.
If
\mathcal{M}
is compact, then the cut locus
C_{x}
is non-empty for all
x\in\mathcal{M}
.
For example, if
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{m}
is a hypersphere then the cut locus of any point
x
is exactly the antipode of
x
.
See Figure
2
for an illustration of the cut locus, which need not be a single point.

Gradients.
If
h:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
is a sufficiently regular function, its gradient
\nabla h
is a vector field on
\mathcal{M}
, meaning it assigns a vector
\nabla h(x)\in\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})
for each
x\in\mathcal{M}
.
More precisely, we say that
h
is differentiable if we have

\frac{h(\gamma(t))-h(\gamma(0))}{t}\to\langle\nabla h(x),\gamma^{\prime}(0)\rangle_{x}

(18)

as
t\to 0
, for all sufficiently regular paths
\gamma
with
\gamma(0)=x
.
For example, it is known that the squared-distance function has gradient

\nabla_{x}d^{2}(x,y)=-2\Log_{x}(y)

(19)

for all
x\in\mathcal{M}\setminus C_{y}
; additionally, it is known that
x\mapsto d(x,y)
is Lipschitz on all
\mathcal{M}
, but it may fail to be differentiable on
C_{y}\cup\{y\}
.
Higher-order derivatives of
h
are defined similarly, namely for
r\in\mathbb{N}
the derivative
\nabla^{r}h(x)
is an
r
-linear function from
\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})
to
\mathbb{R}
, meaning it is a linear functional of
r
many vectors in
\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})
; the space of all
r
-linear functions is a finite-dimensional vector space that can be endowed with the norm
\|H\|_{x,r}:=\max_{\|v_{1}\|_{x}=\cdots=\|v_{r}\|_{x}=1}|H(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})|
(or, alternatively, many other norms since they are all equivalent up to constants).

Fréchet Means.
For a probability measure
P
on a Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
, its
Fréchet variance
is the infimal value of the functional
y\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{M}}d^{2}(x,y)\textnormal{d}P(x)
, and a
Fréchet mean
is any minimizer, if it exists.
It is known
[
30
, Lemma 2.13]
that a Fréchet mean always exists if
\int_{\mathcal{M}}d^{2}(x,y)\textnormal{d}P(x)<\infty
for some (equivalently, all)
y\in\mathcal{M}
(but it may be non-unique if
P
is not sufficiently concentrated); in particular, on a compact and connected Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
, every probability measure admits a Fréchet mean.
If
P
possesses a density with respect to the volume form, then the first-order optimality conditions for
y
to be a Fréchet mean of
P
are exactly
\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Log_{y}(x)\textnormal{d}P(x)=0
, but this condition is not sufficient in general.
If
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
, then the Fréchet mean of
P
is unique and equals the expectation
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}x\textnormal{d}P(x)
of
P
, but in general there is no closed-form expression for Fréchet means.

Laplace-Beltrami Operator.

We write
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
for the space of square-integrable (complex) functions with respect to the volume form of
\mathcal{M}
, and write
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
for the space of essentially bounded functions with respect to the volume form of
\mathcal{M}
.
There exists a unique nonnegative linear operator
\Delta:L^{2}(\mathcal{M})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
called the
Laplace-Beltrami operator
which acts like a second-order differential operator when restricted to smooth functions; in other words, it is a generalization of the usual Laplace operator
\Delta=-(\partial^{2}/\partial x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\partial^{2}/\partial x_{m}^{2})
from
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where, for smooth
f
, the value
\Delta f(x)
represents the difference between the value of
f(x)
compared to the average value of
f
on infinitesimally small balls around
x
.
If
\mathcal{M}
is compact, then
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
admits a countable basis of eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
of
\Delta
and corresponding eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
, which are infinitely-differentiable functions.
The smallest eigenvalue is always
\lambda_{0}=0
and corresponds to a (suitably normalized) constant function
\phi_{0}
.
As
j\to\infty
, it is known (see
[
16
, Section VI.4]
) that the eigenvalues satisfy Weyl’s law
\lambda_{j}\sim 4\pi^{2}(\omega_{m}\textnormal{vol}(\mathcal{M}))^{-2/m}j^{2/m}
where
\omega_{m}
denotes the Lebesgue measure of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\textnormal{vol}(\mathcal{M}):=\int_{\mathcal{M}}1\textnormal{d}x
denotes the volume of
\mathcal{M}
; in particular, the asymptotic behavior of the eigenvalues depends only on coarse geometric features of
\mathcal{M}
, namely its dimension and volume.
The spectral theory of the Laplace-Beltrami operator provides a natural generalization of Fourier analysis to functions on compact Riemannian manifolds; for example, if
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{1}
then
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
are the complex exponentials and the resulting basis is the Fourier series, and if
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{2}
then
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
are the spherical harmonics.

Homogeneity.
An
isometry
of a Riemannian manifold is a map
\psi:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
such that for all
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
we have
d(\psi(x),\psi(y))=d(x,y)
, and the set of all isometries of
\mathcal{M}
is a group under composition denoted
\textnormal{Iso}(\mathcal{M})
and called the
isometry group
.
We say that a Riemannian manifold is
homogeneous
if for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
there exists
\psi\in\textnormal{Iso}(\mathcal{M})
such that
\psi(x)=x^{\prime}
(i.e., the natural action of
\textnormal{Iso}(\mathcal{M})
on
\mathcal{M}
is transitive).
None of the results in this paper require homogeneity; however, most examples in applications are indeed homogeneous (e.g.,
\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{2},\SO(3)
) and many of our proofs could be slightly simplified under the additional assumption of homogeneity.

Lie Groups.

Many of the Riemannian manifolds in this paper can also be endowed with a group
structure so that the multiplication map
\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
and inversion
map
\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
are smooth; examples include
\mathbb{S}^{1}
,

\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and
\SO(3)
, but not
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
When a Lie group is equipped with a bi-invariant Riemannian metric tensor, many of these geometric notions simplify; the
Riemannian volume form is proportional to Haar measure and is invariant under
left and right translations, the tangent space
\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})
can be identified with the Lie algebra

\Tan_{e}(\mathcal{M})
by left or right translation where
e
is the identity element of
\mathcal{M}
, and the geodesic distance between
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
can be
written as a function of the group-theoretic difference
d(x,y)=d(e,x^{-1}y)=d(e,y^{-1}x)
.
In general there are many possible Riemannian metric tensors on a Lie group; whenever we use Lie-group structure in this paper, we assume that the group has been equipped with a fixed bi-invariant Riemannian metric tensor.
In particular, such a Lie group is homogeneous.

In this section we state and prove the main results of the paper.

contains preliminary results on Riemannian Gaussian mixture models (including our version of the Tweedie-Eddington formula), Subsection

provides a comparison of the risks of
\delta_{\mathcal{N}},\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
at the oracle level, Subsection

3.1
Riemannian Gaussian Mixtures

We first give some introductory results related to Riemannian Gaussian distributions and mixtures of Riemannian Gaussian distributions.
The proofs of all results in this subsection appear in Appendix
A.1
, except that we give a sketch of proof of the Tweedie-Eddington formula (Lemma
3.1
) since it is simple and instructive.
Throughout this section we will assume that
\mathcal{M}
is a compact connected Riemannian manifold.

We begin by defining a particular notion of Gaussian distributions on Riemannian manifolds, which is usually attributed to Pennec
[
62
]
.
That is, for
\theta\in\mathcal{M}
and
\sigma^{2}>0
, the
Riemannian Gaussian distribution with location parameter

\theta
and scale parameter
\sigma
is the probability measure
P_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}
on
\mathcal{M}
whose density with respect to the volume form
\textnormal{d}x
is

p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x):=\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}(x,\theta)}{2\sigma^{2}}-A(\sigma^{2},\theta)\right)\qquad\textnormal{where}\qquad A(\sigma^{2},\theta):=\log\int_{\mathcal{M}}\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}(x,\theta)}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\textnormal{d}x.

(20)

As explained in
[
62
]
, the Riemannian Gaussian distribution
P_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}
is centered in the sense that
\theta
is always a local minimizer (but not necessarily a global minimizer) of the Fréchet functional
y\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{M}}d^{2}(x,y)\textnormal{d}P_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x)
.
If
\mathcal{M}
is homogeneous, then (see
[
56
, Theorem 2]
) the point
\theta
is the unique global minimizer (i.e, the Fréchet mean) of
P_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}
, and
A
does not depend on
\theta
.
We also emphasize that it is not a restriction to assume that
\mathcal{M}
is connected;
P_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}
assigns zero probability to all connected components of
\mathcal{M}
which do not contain
\theta
.

Now we recall the setting from the Introduction, where
\Theta
is an
\mathcal{M}
-valued random variable with distribution
G
, and the conditional distribution of
X
given
\{\Theta=\theta\}
is
P_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}
; in this setting, the marginal distribution of
X
is a mixture of Riemannian Gaussian distributions, hence its density is
f(x)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x)\textnormal{d}G(\theta)
, for
x\in\mathcal{M}
.
The starting point for this entire paper is the following version of a Tweedie-Eddington formula for Riemannian Gaussian mixture models.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is a compact connected Riemannian manifold and the cut locus
C_{x}
of
x\in\mathcal{M}
satisfies
G(C_{x})=0
, then
f
is differentiable at
x\in\mathcal{M}
and we have

\mathbb{E}[\Log_{x}\Theta\,|\,X=x]=\sigma^{2}\nabla_{x}\log f(x).

(21)

Consequently, if
G
has a density with respect to the volume form, then
G(C_{x})=0
for every
x\in\mathcal{M}
, hence
f
is differentiable, and (
21
) holds for all
x\in\mathcal{M}
.

Sketch of proof of Lemma
3.1
.

For any constant-speed geodesic
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{M}
with
\gamma(0)=x
and
\gamma^{\prime}(0)=v
, differentiate under the integral and use the chain rule to obtain (see (
18
) and (
19
))

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{f(\gamma(t))-f(x)}{t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\frac{p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(\gamma(t))-p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x)}{t}\,\textnormal{d}G(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left\langle\sigma^{-2}\Log_{x}(\theta)\,p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x),\,v\right\rangle_{x}\textnormal{d}G(\theta)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\sigma^{-2}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Log_{x}(\theta)\,p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x)\,\textnormal{d}G(\theta),\,v\right\rangle_{x}.

As this holds for all
v\in\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})
, we see that
f
is differentiable at
x
, with gradient equal to

\nabla f(x)=\sigma^{-2}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Log_{x}(\theta)\,p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x)\,\textnormal{d}G(\theta).

Multiplying this expression by
\sigma^{2}/f(x)
gives

\sigma^{2}\nabla_{x}\log f(x)=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Log_{x}(\theta)\,p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x)\,\textnormal{d}G(\theta)}{f(x)},

and we recognize on the right side that
q_{x}(\theta):=p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x)/f(x)
is the density, with respect to
G
, of the conditional distribution of
\Theta
given
\{X=x\}
; this finishes the proof.
∎

Although
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
is non-compact and hence does not satisfy our hypotheses, it is useful to note that the conclusion of Lemma
3.1
in this Euclidean setting coincides with the usual Tweedie-Eddington formula

\mathbb{E}[\Theta\,|\,X=x]=x+\sigma^{2}\nabla_{x}\log f(x),

since we have
\Log_{x}(\theta)=\theta-x
.

One key difference between Riemannian Gaussian mixture densities and Euclidean Gaussian mixture densities is that the former can be uniformly lower bounded; we show this in the following result, where ess inf and ess sup denote the essential infimum and essential supremum, with respect to the volume form of
\mathcal{M}
, respectively.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is a compact connected Riemannian manifold and
G
has a density
g
with respect to the volume form, then we have

\inf_{x\in\mathcal{M}}f(x)\geq c_{\mathcal{M}}\,\underset{\theta\in\mathcal{M}}{\textnormal{ess}\inf}\,g(\theta)\qquad\textnormal{ and }\qquad\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}f(x)\leq C_{\mathcal{M}}\,\underset{\theta\in\mathcal{M}}{\textnormal{ess}\sup}\,g(\theta)

for all
\sigma^{2}\leq(\diam(\mathcal{M}))^{2}
, where
c_{\mathcal{M}},C_{\mathcal{M}}>0
are constants depending only on
\mathcal{M}
.

We will also need the following uniform bound on the gradient of a Riemannian mixture density.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is a compact connected Riemannian manifold and
G
has a density
g
with respect to the volume form, we have

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\|\nabla f(x)\|_{x}\leq C_{\mathcal{M}}\sigma^{-1}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

for all
\sigma^{2}\leq(\diam(\mathcal{M}))^{2}
, where
C_{\mathcal{M}}>0
depends only on
\mathcal{M}
.

3.2
Oracle-Level Denoising

In this subsection we analyze the risks of the oracle-level denoisers
\delta_{\mathcal{N}},\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
, and
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
, in the asymptotic regime where
\sigma^{2}\to 0
, for any compact connected Riemannian manifold.
Our main results show (Proposition
3.4
) that the risk of
\delta_{\mathcal{N}}
is asymptotically equivalent to
m\sigma^{2}
, and (Theorem
3.8
) that the risks of
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
differ by at most
O(\sigma^{5})
.
The proofs of all results (and some auxiliary results needed along the way) are provided in Appendix
A.2
.

To begin, we consider the naïve denoiser
\delta_{\mathcal{N}}(x)=x
which is implicitly used in works that do not use techniques from empirical Bayes.
In the general setting, we can describe the risk of
\delta_{\mathcal{N}}
as follows.

Proposition 3.4
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is a compact connected Riemannian manifold, then
R(\delta_{\mathcal{N}},\sigma^{2})\sim m\sigma^{2}
as
\sigma^{2}\to 0
.

The preceding result shows that the risk of
\delta_{\mathcal{N}}
is equivalent to its risk in the Euclidean setting
\mathbb{R}^{m}
; intuitively speaking, this comes from the fact that, for small
\sigma^{2}>0
, a Riemannian Gaussian distribution is well-approximated by a Euclidean Gaussian distribution in the tangent space.

Next we introduce the oracle-level denoisers of interest whose analysis will occupy the remainder of this subsection.
That is, we define the
oracle Bayes denoiser
as

\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(x)\in\underset{\vartheta\in\mathcal{M}}{\arg\min}\,\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(\vartheta,\Theta)\,|\,X=x]

(22)

and the
oracle tangential Bayes denoiser
as

\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x):=\Exp_{x}\left(\mathbb{E}[\Log_{x}\Theta\,|\,X=x]\right)

(23)

for all
x\in\mathcal{M}
.
As we explained in the Introduction, one can view
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x)
as a one-step approximation of
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(x)
, by initializing Riemannian gradient descent at the output of the naïve denoiser
\delta_{\mathcal{N}}(x)=x
; also, by Lemma
3.1
, the oracle tangential Bayes denoiser can be written as
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x)=\Exp_{x}(\sigma^{2}\nabla_{x}\log f(x))
, which depends only on the marginal density of
X
and hence can be approximated in a fully data-driven manner.

Example 3.5

(homogeneous
\mathcal{M}
, uniform
G
)
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is a compact connected homogeneous Riemannian manifold and
G
is proportional to the volume form (i.e., the uniform distribution), then the denoisers
\delta_{\mathcal{N}},\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
, and
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
are all equal.
To see
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}=\delta_{\mathcal{N}}
, note that the conditional distribution of
\Theta
given
\{X=x\}
is a Riemannian Gaussian distribution with location parameter
x
and scale parameter
\sigma
, hence
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(x)=x
by definition.
To see
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}=\delta_{\mathcal{N}}
, note that the marginal distribution of
X
is uniform, hence
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x)=\Exp_{x}(\sigma^{2}\cdot 0)=x
.
This behavior is distinct from the Euclidean case, where the naïve denoiser cannot be written as the Bayes denoiser with respect to any
G
.
In fact, this argument shows that
\delta_{\mathcal{N}}
, being the minimizer of a posterior expected loss, is admissible; this somewhat contrasts the recent work
[
57
]
on the James-Stein estimator in spaces of non-positive curvature.

Next we turn to a comparison of the risks of
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
and
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
, in the regime where
\sigma^{2}\to 0
.
Since
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
directly involves the random variable
\mathbb{E}[\Log_{X}\Theta\,|\,X]
, we must first develop some auxiliary results for conditional distributions of this random variable and some related quantities.

Proposition 3.6
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}
is a compact connected Riemannian manifold and that
G
has a strictly positive, continuously differentiable density with respect to the volume form. Then we have

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\big\|\mathbb{E}\left[\Log_{x}\Theta\,|\,X=x\right]\big\|_{x}=O(\sigma^{2})\qquad\textnormal{ and }\qquad\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\big\|\textnormal{Cov}\left(\Log_{x}\Theta\,|\,X=x\right)\big\|_{x,2}=O(\sigma^{2})

as
\sigma^{2}\to 0
.

Although
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}
is non-compact and hence does not satisfy our hypotheses, it is useful to note that we can do explicit calculations in this Euclidean setting in order to confirm that the rates in Proposition
3.6
are correct.
That is, suppose
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}
and
G=\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^{2})
, so that the conjugate prior structure allows us to directly calculate

\mathbb{E}[\Theta-x\,|\,X=x]=-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau^{2}+\sigma^{2}}x=O(\sigma^{2})\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad\textnormal{Cov}(\Theta-x\,|\,X=x)=\frac{\tau^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\tau^{2}+\sigma^{2}}=O(\sigma^{2}).

If we restrict
\mathcal{M}
to be a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}
, then these bounds also hold uniformly over
x
.

In the previous result we established that
\mathbb{E}[\Log_{X}\Theta\,|\,X]
is of order
O(\sigma^{2})
; the next result shows that
\Log_{X}(\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(X))
is also of order
O(\sigma^{2})
, but that the difference
\mathbb{E}[\Log_{X}\Theta\,|\,X]-\Log_{X}(\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(X))
is of the smaller order
O(\sigma^{4})
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}
is a compact connected Riemannian manifold and that
G
has a strictly positive, continuously differentiable density with respect to the volume form. Then we have

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\big\|\Log_{x}(\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(x))\big\|_{x}=O(\sigma^{2})\qquad\textnormal{ and }\qquad\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\big\|\mathbb{E}[\Log_{x}\Theta\,|\,X=x]-\Log_{x}(\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(x))\big\|_{x}=O(\sigma^{4})

as
\sigma^{2}\to 0
.

While Proposition
3.6
is a relatively straightforward calculation using normal coordinates (see (
32
) in Appendix
A.2
), Proposition
3.7
is more difficult because
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(x)
, for
x\in\mathcal{M}
, is only implicitly defined via (
22
).
Nonetheless, the proof proceeds by observing that the first-order optimality conditions for
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(x)
provide a recursive bound on the distance between
\Log_{x}(\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(x))
and
\mathbb{E}[\Log_{x}\Theta\,|\,X=x]
, which upon iterating and combining with Proposition
3.6
implies the desired estimates.

Now we combine Proposition
3.6
and Proposition
3.7
to prove the following, which is our main result comparing the risks of
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
.
The proof (given in Appendix
A.2
) reveals that the Taylor expansions of the squared geodesic distances
d^{2}(\theta,\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(x))
and
d^{2}(\theta,\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x))
agree up to second-order, i.e., the denoiser
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
captures precisely the impact of the sectional curvature of
\mathcal{M}
on
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
.

Theorem 3.8
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}
is a compact connected Riemannian manifold and that
G
has a strictly positive, continuously differentiable density with respect to the volume form. Then we have
0\leq R(\delta_{\mathcal{T}},\sigma^{2})-R(\delta_{\mathcal{B}},\sigma^{2})=O(\sigma^{5})
as
\sigma^{2}\to 0
.

3.3
Sobolev Norm Bounds

In the Euclidean setting, the key to the fast rates of convergence of empirical Bayes denoising is that the density
f
of a Gaussian mixture is always infinitely differentiable (in fact, analytic), regardless of
G
.
In the Riemannian setting, it turns out that smoothness of the density
f
of a Riemannian Gaussian mixture is more delicate. In this subsection, we will develop some sufficient conditions on the geometry of
\mathcal{M}
and the density
g
of
G
which imply a bound on a suitable Sobolev norm of
f
.
The proofs of all results in this subsection are contained in Appendix
A.3
.

To state the desired estimates precisely, let
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
and
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
denote the eigenfunctions and eigenvalues of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta
on
\mathcal{M}
.
For any function
h:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{C}
, we define
h^{\ast}(j):=\int_{\mathcal{M}}h(x)\overline{\phi_{j}(x)}\textnormal{d}x
for all
j=0,1,\ldots
, which are just its coefficients in the basis
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
, i.e., we have
h=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}h^{\ast}(j)\phi_{j}
with the sum convergent in
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
.
For
r\geq 0
, we also define

\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}:=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(1+\lambda_{j})^{r}|h^{\ast}(j)|^{2},

(24)

called the
Sobolev norm of order
r
of
h
.

To understand these Sobolev norms, it is helpful to consider some special cases.
For example, for
r=0
we see from Parseval’s identity that
\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}(\mathcal{M})}=\|h\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}
.
A more interesting example is
r=1
, where integration by parts allows us to compute

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|\nabla h(x)\|_{x}^{2}\textnormal{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\overline{h(x)}\Delta h(x)\textnormal{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\overline{h^{\ast}(j)}\,\overline{\phi_{j}(x)}\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}h^{\ast}(j)\lambda_{j}\phi_{j}(x)\right)\textnormal{d}x=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}|h^{\ast}(j)|^{2};

hence

\|h\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathcal{M})}=\|h\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}+\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|\nabla h(x)\|^{2}_{x}\textnormal{d}x.

(25)

More generally, for integer
r
, the spectral norm in (
24
)
is equivalent to the usual Sobolev norm involving weak derivatives up to order

r
; in particular,
\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}<\infty

if and only if all weak derivatives of
h
up to order
r
belong to the
corresponding tensor-valued
L^{2}
spaces.
The definition in (
24
), however, makes sense for all

r\geq 0
, including non-integer values; see
[
1
, Chapter 2]
.

Our empirical Bayes results will require the assumption that we have finite Sobolev norm
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}<\infty
for some
r>1
, and the resulting rates of convergence will depend explicitly on
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}
and
r
; we will see that large values of
r
are desirable, since the resulting rates approach the parametric rate of convergence
n^{-1}
as
r\to\infty
.
To understand the assumption
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}<\infty
for some
r>1
, we give the following result (which is an immediate consequence of Lemma
3.3
) that states that this condition is nearly satisfied in general.

Lemma 3.9
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is a compact connected Riemannian manifold and
G
has a density
g\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
with respect to the volume form, then we have
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathcal{M})}\leq C_{\mathcal{M}}\sigma^{-1}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}
, for all
\sigma^{2}\leq(\diam(\mathcal{M}))^{2}
, where
C_{\mathcal{M}}>0
is a constant depending only on
\mathcal{M}
.

In the Riemannian setting, we will require further assumptions on
\mathcal{M}
and
g
in order to upgrade the finite Sobolev norm of order 1 into a finite Sobolev norm of order
r>1
.
This is a distinct behavior from the Euclidean setting, where Gaussian location mixture models always have finite Sobolev norms of all orders.
For instance, we give the following result in the Euclidean setting which implies that
f
has finite Sobolev norms of all orders, although where the Sobolev space
\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
needs to be defined slightly differently since
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
is non-compact (and its Laplace-Beltrami operator does not have a discrete spectrum).

Lemma 3.10
.

If
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
G
has a density
g\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
with respect to Lebesgue measure, then for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\|\nabla^{r}f(x)\|_{r}\leq C_{m,r}\sigma^{-r}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}

for all
\sigma^{2}>0
where
C_{m,r}>0
is a constant that depends only on
m
and
r
.

The crucial aspect of Euclidean spaces
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
which permits a straightforward argument for the preceding result is that the functional
x\mapsto\|x-\theta\|^{2}
has uniformly bounded
r
th order derivatives for all
r\geq 2
.
In the Riemannian setting such bounds can fail dramatically, because the
r
th order derivatives of the functional
x\mapsto d^{2}(x,\theta)
for
r\geq 2
may explode as
x
approaches the cut locus
C_{\theta}
of
\theta
.
We illustrate this with the following basic calculation.

Example 3.11

(Hessian of distance squared on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
)
.

Fix
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
\sigma^{2}=1
and use
[
63
]
to calculate
\nabla_{x}^{2}d^{2}(x,\theta)
, (where we emphasize the differentiation with respect to the
x
variable); to write the expression explicitly, we represent
\mathbb{S}^{2}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\|x\|=1\}
and we have

\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{x}^{2}d^{2}(x,\theta)=\frac{\Log_{x}\theta(\Log_{x}\theta)^{\top}}{d^{2}(x,\theta)}+h(d(x,\theta))\left(I-xx^{\top}-\frac{\Log_{x}\theta(\Log_{x}\theta)^{\top}}{d^{2}(x,\theta)}\right)

(26)

where
h(t)=t\cos(t)/\sin(t)
for
0<t\leq\pi
with
h(0):=1
by continuity.
Observe that, as
x\to C_{\theta}=\{-\theta\}
, we have
h(d(x,\theta))\to-\infty
and hence
\|\nabla_{x}^{2}d^{2}(x,\theta)\|_{x,2}\to\infty
.

Consequently, in the Riemannian setting, the Sobolev smoothness of
f
relies more delicately on both the geometry of
\mathcal{M}
and the properties of
g
; in the rest of this subsection we give some results of this form.

For instance, most of the examples of interest in this work are Lie groups (e.g.,
\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and
\SO(3)
), where the convolution structure allows us to differentiate
f
without directly differentiating the Riemannian logarithm.
This allows us to show the following, namely that
f
inherits the same smoothness as
g
; we write
C^{r}(\mathcal{M})
for the set of real functions on
\mathcal{M}
which are
r
-times continuously differentiable.

Lemma 3.12
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is a compact connected Lie group and
G
possesses a density
g\in C^{r}(\mathcal{M})
with respect to the volume form, then we have
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}\leq C_{\mathcal{M},r}(\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}+\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{M}}\|\nabla^{r}g(\theta)\|_{\theta,r})
for all
\sigma^{2}>0
, where
C_{\mathcal{M},r}>0
is a constant depending only on
\mathcal{M}
and
r
.

The sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is not a Lie group so the previous result does not apply, but we can nonetheless use the explicit calculations from Example
3.11
to say something general in the following.

Lemma 3.13
.

If
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
G
possesses a density
g\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
with respect to the volume form, then
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathcal{M})}\leq C_{\mathcal{M}}\sigma^{-2}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}
for all
\sigma^{2}\leq(\diam(\mathcal{M}))^{2}
, where
C_{\mathcal{M}}>0
is a constant depending only on
\mathcal{M}
.

Note the qualitative difference between Lemma
3.12
and Lemma
3.13
.
In Lemma
3.12
we show that
f
is at least as smooth as
g
, while in Lemma
3.13
we show that
f
gains some smoothness over that of
g
.
We do not expect either of these results to be sharp, as we show next in the special case of the circle
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{1}
.
In this special case, we show that
f
gains two derivatives over that of
g
, which can be understood via standard Fourier analysis since the density
f
of the Riemannian Gaussian mixture is exactly a convolution of
g
with a kernel that is differentiable but not continuously differentiable (because of its behavior at the cut locus).

Lemma 3.14
.

If
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{1}
and
G
possesses a density
g\in\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
with respect to the volume form, then we have
c_{\sigma^{2}}\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\leq\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s+2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\leq C\sigma^{-2}\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}
for all
s\geq 0
, where
C>0
is a universal constant and
c_{\sigma^{2}}>0
is a constant depending only on
\sigma^{2}
.

Another special case where we can make our results precise is the following.

Lemma 3.15
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is a compact connected homogeneous Riemannian manifold and
G
is proportional to the volume form (i.e.,
G
is the uniform distribution), then
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}=(\textnormal{vol}(\mathcal{M}))^{-1/2}
for all
r\geq 1
.

As the preceding examples show, obtaining sharp Sobolev regularity bounds for the marginal density
f
in terms of the Sobolev regularity of the mixing density
g
is a difficult problem which depends on the geometry of
\mathcal{M}
.
One obstruction is the behavior of higher-order derivatives of the squared-distance function
x\mapsto d^{2}(x,\theta)
near the cut locus
C_{\theta}
; already at second order, the Hessian
\nabla_{x}^{2}d^{2}(x,\theta)
can exhibit singular behavior near
C_{\theta}
(cf. Example
3.11
).
Furthermore, the integrability of these singularities also depends on the (co)dimension and local structure of the cut loci
C_{\theta}
themselves (cf. the proof of Lemma
3.13
).
In the case of compact Lie groups, the convolution structure allows us to avoid differentiating the Riemannian logarithm directly and gives partial results in this direction (cf. Lemma
3.12
), but a sharp analysis appears to require further tools from representation theory.
A detailed study of Sobolev bounds for densities of Riemannian Gaussian mixtures is therefore an interesting direction for future work, and lies beyond the scope of this paper.

3.4
Empirical-Level Denoising

In this final subsection we establish (Theorem
3.17
) a rate of convergence for the empirical tangential Bayes denoiser
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
to the oracle tangential Bayes denoiser
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
.
The proofs of all results in this section are given in Appendix
A.4
.
Combined with the results of Subsection
3.2
, this shows that the empirical tangential Bayes denoiser
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
can achieve nearly the same risk as the oracle Bayes denoiser
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
, when
\sigma^{2}
is small and
n
is large.

To begin, let
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
and
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
denote the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta
on
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
, and define

K(x,y,\ell):=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\phi_{j}(x)\overline{\phi_{j}(y)},

for
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
, which is just a finite-rank projection of the identity operator, with respect to the basis
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
of
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
.
The function
K
plays the role of the kernel in our methodology, in the sense that we define our estimator of the density
f
of
X
to be

\hat{f}_{n}(x):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K(x,X_{i},\ell_{n}),

(27)

where
\ell_{n}
is some sequence that will be determined later.

Before we use this density estimator in our empirical Bayes approximation of
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
, we give the following result which provides simultaneous control on the
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
estimation error of
\hat{f}_{n}
and its gradient
\nabla_{x}\hat{f}_{n}
.

Proposition 3.16
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}
is a compact connected Riemannian manifold, that
G
has a density
g\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
with respect to the volume form, and that we have
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}<\infty
for some
r>0
.
Then setting

\ell_{n}:=\left\lceil\left(\frac{n\|f\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}}{\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}}\right)^{\frac{m}{2r+m}}\right\rceil

yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left|\hat{f}_{n}(x)-f(x)\right|^{2}\textnormal{d}x\right]\leq\frac{C_{\mathcal{M},r}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}^{\frac{2m}{2r+m}}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}^{\frac{2r}{2r+m}}}{n^{\frac{2r}{2r+m}}}

and, for
r>1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left\|\nabla_{x}\hat{f}_{n}(x)-\nabla_{x}f(x)\right\|_{x}^{2}\textnormal{d}x\right]\leq\frac{C_{\mathcal{M},r}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}^{\frac{2m+4}{2r+m}}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}^{\frac{2r-2}{2r+m}}}{n^{\frac{2r-2}{2r+m}}},

for all
n
and
\sigma^{2}\leq(\diam(\mathcal{M}))^{2}
, where
C_{\mathcal{M},r}>0
is a constant depending only on
\mathcal{M}
and
r
.

The work
[
34
]
initiated the study of estimating a nonparametric density
f
from a sample of i.i.d. random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
on a compact Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
, using a kernel made from the eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator;
our Proposition
3.16
is limited to the setting that
f
is the density of a Riemannian Gaussian mixture, but our results are more precise in that the rate of convergence is more explicit and that we also establish a rate of convergence for the estimation of the gradient
\nabla f
.
These minor extensions are needed for the analysis of our empirical Bayes denoiser in Subsection
3.4
, but the proofs are conceptually similar to the methods used in
[
34
]
; see also the discussion in
[
34
, Section 4.6]
.
Nonetheless, they may be of independent interest in the literature on density estimation on Riemannian manifolds.
Also, note that this result provides a non-trivial rate of convergence for estimating
\nabla f
only if
r>1
; this is precisely what requires us to develop the finer results of Subsection
3.3
.

Now we define the denoiser of interest, namely

\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}(x):=\Exp_{x}\left(\sigma^{2}\frac{\nabla_{x}\hat{f}_{n}(x)}{\max\{\hat{f}_{n}(x),\rho\}}\right),

(28)

where
\hat{f}_{n}
is chosen with
\ell_{n}
according to the previous result and
\rho>0
will be chosen later.
By comparing with (
8
), we see that this is like a plug-in form of
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
, unless
\hat{f}_{n}(x)
provides a very low estimate for
f(x)
in which case we regularize by replacing
\hat{f}_{n}(x)
with
\rho
.

Our main result on the empirical Bayes denoiser
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
is the following, which provides an explicit rate of convergence for the expected distance
\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{T}}(\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}})]
defined in (
15
).

Theorem 3.17
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}
is a compact connected Riemannian manifold, that
G
has a density
g\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
with respect to the volume form, and that we have
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}<\infty
for some
r>1
.
Then setting
0<\rho\leq\min_{x\in\mathcal{M}}f(x)
and

\ell_{n}:=\left\lceil\left(\frac{n\|f\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}}{\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}}\right)^{\frac{m}{2r+m}}\right\rceil

yields

\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\mathcal{T}}(\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}d^{2}\left(\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}(x),\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x)\right)f(x)\textnormal{d}x\right]\leq\frac{C_{\mathcal{M},r}\sigma^{2}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}^{\frac{2m+4}{2r+m}}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}^{\frac{8r+3m}{2r+m}}}{\rho^{4}n^{\frac{2r-2}{2r+m}}}

for all
n
and
\sigma^{2}\leq(\diam(\mathcal{M}))^{2}
, where
C_{\mathcal{M},r}>0
is a constant depending only on
\mathcal{M}
and
r
.

It is easy to upgrade this result to a bound on the expected risk of
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
, although we obtain a non-sharp oracle inequality rather than a sharp one.

Lemma 3.18
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that we have
R(\delta)\leq(1+\varepsilon)R(\delta_{\mathcal{T}})+C_{\varepsilon}D_{\mathcal{T}}(\delta)
for all denoisers
\delta:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
, where
C_{\varepsilon}\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

The above result, combined with Theorem
3.17
, immediately yields

\mathbb{E}\left[R\left(\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}},\sigma^{2}\right)\right]\leq(1+\varepsilon)R(\delta_{\mathcal{T}},\sigma^{2})+\frac{C_{\mathcal{M},r,\varepsilon}\sigma^{2}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}^{\frac{2m+4}{2r+m}}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}^{\frac{8r+3m}{2r+m}}}{\rho^{4}n^{\frac{2r-2}{2r+m}}}

for all
n
and
\sigma^{2}\leq(\diam(\mathcal{M}))^{2}
, where
C_{\mathcal{M},r,\varepsilon}>0
is a constant depending only on
\mathcal{M}
,
r
, and
\varepsilon
.

In the remainder of this subsection, we interpret the rate of convergence appearing in the previous result, in terms of
n
.
We note that
g
is unknown but appears only in the constant, and that by Lemma
3.2
we can choose
\rho
to depend only on
\mathcal{M}
and
g
, provided that
g
has a strictly positive lower bound.
In some cases it is also desirable to understand the dependence of the rate on
\sigma^{2}
, since the oracle-level results from Subsection
3.2
are asymptotic results in the regime where
\sigma^{2}\to 0
; however, this is a hard problem even in the Euclidean setting, and most existing rates of convergence for nonparametric empirical Bayes (e.g.,
[
89
,
73
,
78
]
) either do not depend explicitly on
\sigma^{2}
or in fact deteriorate as
\sigma^{2}\to 0
.
In the following, we use results of Subsection
3.3
to explicitly describe the resulting rates of convergence in various settings.

First we consider Lie groups, where the rate of convergence depends explicitly on the smoothness of
g
.

Example 3.19

(Lie group
\mathcal{M}
, smooth
G
)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{M}
is a compact connected Lie group (e.g., the circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, the torus
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
, or the space of orientation-preserving rotations
\SO(3)
) and that the density
g
of
G
satisfies
g\in C^{r}(\mathcal{M})
.
Then, Lemma
3.12
implies
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}<\infty
.
If
r\geq 2
, then, in terms of constants depending only on
\mathcal{M},g
, and
\sigma^{2}
, we can set
\ell_{n}\asymp n^{m/(2r+m)}
and achieve the rate of convergence
n^{-(2r-2)/(2r+m)}
.
Note that this can be made arbitrarily close to the parametric rate
n^{-1}
as
r\to\infty
, although the pre-factor may explode.
To be concrete, consider the case that
g
is twice-continuously differentiable so that we take
r=2
above; if
m=1
(e.g., the circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
) then setting
\ell_{n}\asymp n^{1/5}
leads to the rate of convergence
n^{-2/5}
, if
m=2
(e.g., the torus
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
) then setting
\ell_{n}\asymp n^{1/3}
leads to the rate of convergence
n^{-1/3}
, and if
m=3
(e.g., rotations
\SO(3)
) then setting
\ell_{n}\asymp n^{3/7}
leads to the rate of convergence
n^{-2/7}
.

Second, we consider the sphere, where our result holds for all
g
but with a slower rate of convergence.

Example 3.20

(
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{2}
, general
G
)
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
g\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
has a strictly positive lower bound, then Lemma
3.13
guarantees
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathcal{M})}\lesssim\sigma^{-2}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}
hence we can take
r=m=2
in the main result.
Consequently, and again ignoring constants that depend on
\mathcal{M},g
, and
\sigma^{2}
, we see that setting
\ell_{n}\asymp n^{1/3}
leads to the rate of convergence
n^{-1/3}
.

Last is the case of the circle, in which we can precisely identify the smoothness of
f
in terms of the smoothness of
g
; note that the following result is sharper than what we get by treating
\mathbb{S}^{1}
as a Lie group above.

Example 3.21

(
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{1}
, smooth
G
)
.

If
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{1}
and
G
has a density
g
with some smoothness, then we can obtain an explicit rate of convergence in terms of
\sigma^{2}
and
n
.
Indeed, if we have
\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})}<\infty
for some
s\geq 0
, then Lemma
3.14
shows that we may apply Theorem
3.17
for
r=s+2
; then, ignoring factors that depend only on
\mathcal{M}
and
g
, it follows that setting
\ell_{n}\asymp\sigma^{-4/(2s+5)}n^{1/(2s+5)}
leads to a rate of convergence of
\sigma^{(4s-2)/(2s+5)}n^{-(2s+2)/(2s+5)}
.
We can understand this rate of convergence in a few cases of interest.
For
s=0
, it coincides with the rate
\sigma^{-2/5}n^{-2/5}
in Example
3.19
, but with an explicit dependence on
\sigma^{2}>0
which deteriorates as
\sigma^{2}\to 0
.
For
s=1
, the rate is
\sigma^{2/7}n^{-4/7}
which is faster in
n
and vanishes as
\sigma^{2}\to 0
.
(More generally, with respect to
\sigma^{2}\to 0
, the rate is non-deteriorating if and only if
s\geq 1/2
and vanishing if and only if
s>1/2
.)
Lastly, we note that the rate can be made arbitrarily close to parametric rate
\sigma^{2}n^{-1}
if
\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})}<\infty
for all
s\geq 0
; indeed, for any
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
, set
s:=(3-5\varepsilon)/(2\varepsilon)
and
\ell_{n}\asymp\sigma^{-4/(2s+5)}n^{1/(2s+5)}\approx\sigma^{-4\varepsilon/3}n^{\varepsilon/3}
to achieve the rate of convergence
\sigma^{2-4\varepsilon}n^{-(1-\varepsilon)}
.
(However, the constant prefactor may explode as
\varepsilon\to 0
.)

In the special case of the circle
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{1}
, we complement the rate of convergence in Example
3.21
by providing the following matching lower bound, uniformly over all densities
g
in a suitable subset of a Sobolev ball of order
s\geq 0
.
In particular, this establishes that
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
is minimax-optimal and that the minimax rate is nonparametric,
n^{-(2s+2)/(2s+5)}
.
For convenience, we emphasize the dependence of
\mathbb{E},f
, and
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
on
g
by writing
\mathbb{E}_{g},f_{g}
, and
\delta_{\mathcal{T},g}
, respectively.

Theorem 3.22
.

Suppose
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{1}
, fix
0<\alpha<(2\pi)^{-1}<\beta
,
s\geq 0
, and
L>(2\pi)^{-1/2}
, and define

\mathcal{G}_{s}(\alpha,\beta,L):=\left\{g:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}:\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}g(\theta)\textnormal{d}\theta=1,\alpha\leq g(\theta)\leq\beta\textnormal{ for all }\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\textnormal{, and }\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\leq L\right\}.

Then, for all
0<\sigma^{2}<\pi^{2}
, we have

\inf_{\hat{\delta}}\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{s}(\alpha,\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}_{g}\left[\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}d^{2}\left(\hat{\delta}(x;X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}),\delta_{\mathcal{T},g}(x)\right)f_{g}(x)\textnormal{d}x\right]\geq\frac{C_{\sigma^{2},s,\alpha,\beta,L}}{n^{\frac{2s+2}{2s+5}}}

for all
n
, where
C_{\sigma^{2},s,\alpha,\beta,L}>0
is a constant depending only on
\sigma^{2},s,\alpha,\beta
, and
L
, and the infimum is taken over all measurable functions
\hat{\delta}:\mathbb{S}^{1}\times(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{n}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

The proof of this result reveals that the hardness of denoising coincides with the hardness of estimating the derivative of
f
, and the rate of convergence reflects the fact that
f
inherits 2 more degrees of smoothness than
g
because of the convolution structure; indeed, the rate of convergence should be understood as
n^{-2p/(2p+1)}
for
p=(s+2)-1
.
The obstruction to extending this analysis to general
\mathcal{M}
is that we do not have a precise understanding of the smoothing properties of Riemannian Gaussian mixture models in cases other than
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{1}
, which is the same difficulty we encountered in Subsection
3.3
.
In some sense, these non-parametric rates are a manifestation of the well-known “smeariness” phenomenon in non-Euclidean statistics, whereby the rates of convergence can suffer in problems where the likelihood is not differentiable at the cut locus of its Fréchet mean
[
37
,
27
,
28
,
40
]
.

4
Numerical Simulations

In this section we give some numerical simulations to illustrate the theory of Section
3
, and discuss some computational aspects of the denoising procedures that will be used in the applications of Section
5
.
The simulations are designed with two goals in mind.
First, we are interested in comparing the risks of
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
with those of
\delta_{\mathcal{N}}
and
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
as
\sigma^{2}\to 0
(as in Subsection
3.2
).
Second, we are interested in comparing the distance between
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
and
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
(as in Subsection
3.4
).

As in the rest of the paper, the latent variables
\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{n}
are i.i.d. samples from some distribution
G
, and the measurements
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are conditionally independent Riemannian Gaussian random variables given
\{\Theta_{1}=\theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{n}=\theta_{n}\}
where
X_{i}
has location parameter
\theta_{i}
and some scale parameter
\sigma>0
, for all
1\leq i\leq n
.
Also recall that for any denoiser
\delta
we define the risk
R(\delta,\sigma^{2})
via (
10
) and the distance
D_{\mathcal{T}}(\delta)
from the oracle
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
via (
15
).

In Appendix
B
we give a detailed description of the implementation of our denoisers and our simulations, but we briefly summarize a few important points here.
First, we note that, even with oracle knowledge of the latent variable distribution
G
, evaluating the oracle tangential Bayes denoiser
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
requires numerical integration because evaluating the marginal density
f(x)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x)\textnormal{d}G(\theta)
already requires numerical integration; indeed, we use a Monte Carlo approximation of
f
by drawing additional oracle samples of
\Theta
from
G
, and then we use the identity
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x)=\Exp_{x}(\sigma^{2}\nabla_{x}f(x)/f(x))
.
Second, we compute the oracle Bayes denoiser
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
by first approximating the conditional Fréchet mean of
\Theta
given
\{X=x\}
via Nadaraya-Watson smoothing (see (
5
) and Appendix
B
) using additional oracle samples of
(\Theta,X)
from (
1
), and then by numerically computing the Fréchet mean of the resulting distribution using the built-in methods from
Geomstats

[
59
]
.
Lastly, we note that choosing the optimal parameters
\ell_{n}
and
\rho
(recall (
27
) and (
28
)) requires some oracle knowledge of
G
; for
\ell_{n}
, in this section we follow the oracle choices suggested in Subsection
3.4
, since our goal is simply to verify our theoretical developments, while
\rho
is set to the 1st percentile of the positive values taken on by the density
\hat{f}_{n}
.
Later, in the applications section, we will choose both parameters in a fully data-driven way with cross-validation of the Riemannian version of Hyvärinen’s score-matching objective
[
42
,
84
,
19
]
.

All of our simulations have the same form.
That is, for various structured distributions
G
on a Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
, we compute the following.
First, we compute the oracle-level naïve, Bayes, and tangential Bayes denoisers on the basis of 10,000 oracle samples and we let
\sigma^{2}
vary over the interval
(0,0.25]
in 6 linearly-spaced increments, then we plot the risks
R(\delta_{\mathcal{N}},\sigma^{2})
,
R(\delta_{\mathcal{B}},\sigma^{2})
and
R(\delta_{\mathcal{T}},\sigma^{2})
as a function of
\sigma^{2}
.
Second, we fix
\sigma^{2}=0.15
and we let
n
vary over the interval
\{100,\ldots,10,000\}
in 5 logarithmically-spaced increments, then we plot
\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{T}}(\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}})]
as a function of
n
, on a log-log scale; in this way, the slope of the resulting line reflects the rate of convergence of the empirical tangential Bayes denoiser
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
, as predicted by Theorem
3.17
.
In the rest of this section, we discuss several simulations in the circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
and the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

4.1
Circle,
\mathbb{S}^{1}

The first setting of interest is the circle,
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{1}
, in which our methodology and results already reveal some interesting new phenomena.
Throughout this subsection, we represent
\mathbb{S}^{1}=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
for convenience in some expressions.

In order to compute the empirical Bayes denoiser
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
, we need to be able to compute sums of eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta
, and derivatives thereof.
To do this, we recall from standard Fourier analysis that the eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}=0
occurs with multiplicity 1 and that
\lambda_{m}=m^{2}
occurs with multiplicity 2 for
m\geq 1
.
As such, it is natural to index our eigenfunctions by integers
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, so that the eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}=k^{2}
has corresponding eigenfunction
\phi_{k}(x)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{ikx}
, where we recall that we have chosen to represent
\mathbb{S}^{1}=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
.
Thus if we construct the kernel
K
by fixing
M>0
and summing over
|k|\leq M
, we have

K(x,y,M)=\sum_{|k|\leq M}\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{ik(x-y)}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}D_{M}(x-y)

where
\{D_{M}\}_{M\in\mathbb{N}}
is the
Dirichlet kernel
(see
[
81
, p. 206]
) defined by

D_{M}(z):=\frac{\sin\left(\left(M+\frac{1}{2}\right)z\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}z\right)},

(29)

for
z\neq 0
, and
D_{M}(0)=2M+1
by continuity.
Recall that the Dirichlet kernel is responsible for the “ringing” phenomenon whereby high-density regions close to low-density regions can create the appearance of waves (e.g., in the density estimate) which are not truly present in the data; this phenomenon will be seen in the application to protein structure in Subsection
5.2
.
Note that
M
is related to
\ell_{n}
via
\ell_{n}=2M+1
, i.e.,
M\asymp\ell_{n}
.

We consider the following examples for the latent variable distribution
G
and corresponding hyperparameter choices.
The first is the uniform distribution (i.e.,
\textnormal{d}G(\theta)\propto\textnormal{d}\theta
), in which we set
M\propto\log n
. The second is the uniform distribution on a cap (i.e.,
\textnormal{d}G(\theta)\propto\mathbbold{1}\{a\leq\theta\leq b\}\textnormal{d}\theta
), in which (Example
3.21
) we set
M\asymp n^{1/6}
since direct calculation reveals
\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}<\infty
if and only if
s<1/2
.
The remaining distributions are themselves Riemannian Gaussian mixture models with varying numbers of equally-spaced components and varying component scale parameters
\tau>0
, i.e.,
in the 2-mode case (i.e., antipodal components) as
\tau^{2}=0.1
, in the 3-mode case as
\tau^{2}=0.05
, and in the 5-mode case as
\tau^{2}=0.0005
; in these cases, we set (Example
3.21
)
M\asymp\log n
since the theory suggests letting
M
grow slower than
n^{\varepsilon}
for all
0<\varepsilon<1
.
We always set
\rho>0
to the first percentile of the empirical density
\hat{f}_{n}
.

D_{\mathcal{T}}(\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}})

Next we make some remarks about these plots and their relationship with the theory of Section
3
.
At the oracle level, we observe that the risks of
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
are nearly indistinguishable (Theorem
3.8
) while the risk of
\delta_{\mathcal{N}}
is approximately equal to
\sigma^{2}
(Proposition
3.4
); the only exception is the uniform distribution in which case all of the risks coincide (Example
3.5
).
At the empirical level, we note that the slope of the line in the case of the cap example is
-0.5
which corresponds to a rate of convergence of
n^{-1/2}
(Example
3.21
), and in all other cases the slope is approximately
-1
which corresponds to the nearly-parametric rate of convergence
n^{-1}
(Example
3.21
).

4.2
Sphere,
\mathbb{S}^{2}

In this next example, we consider the sphere,
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
This is one of the fundamental examples in directional statistics, and will be the focus of our application to astronomy in Subsection
5.1
.
We emphasize that visualizing data on the sphere requires choosing a particular two-dimensional projection of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
onto the plane; in this work we always use the
Mollweide projection
, which is an equal-area projection, hence it is well-suited to visualizing scatter plots and other statistical objects, and is commonly used in astronomy
[
82
]
.
However, we emphasize that our denoising procedure takes place in the intrinsic geometry of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, not in the projected geometry; in this regard, other projections can be chosen for the visualization step without changing the underlying methodology.

In order to apply our method we need to recall some aspects of the eigendecomposition of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
It turns out that the eigenvalues occur with multiplicities, so the eigendecomposition can be conveniently parameterized by integer pairs
(m,k)
with
|k|\leq m
;
more precisely, the eigenvalue
\lambda_{m}=m(m+1)
occurs with multiplicity
2m+1
, and its corresponding eigenfunctions are the so-called
spherical harmonics

\phi_{m,-m},\ldots,\phi_{m,m}:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
.
We can also simplify the calculation of our kernel by choosing coordinates for
\mathbb{S}^{2}
by representing it as
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\|x\|=1\}
; then, by fixing
M
and summing over all
(m,k)
with
m\leq M
, the addition formula for spherical harmonics yields

K(x,y,M):=\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{|k|\leq m}\phi_{m,k}(x)\overline{\phi_{m,k}(y)}=\sum_{m=0}^{M}\frac{2m+1}{4\pi}P_{m}\left(x^{\top}y\right)

where
\{P_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
are the so-called
Legendre polynomials
.
Note that
M
is related to
\ell_{n}
via
\ell_{n}=\sum_{m=0}^{M}(2m+1)=(M+1)^{2}
, i.e.,
M\asymp\ell_{n}^{1/2}
.

The examples of our latent variable distribution
G
and hyperparameter choices are as follows.
As before, we consider 5 choices for
G
; the first is the uniform distribution (i.e.,
\textnormal{d}G(\theta)\propto\textnormal{d}\theta
), and the second is the uniform distribution on a cap (i.e.,
\textnormal{d}G(\theta)\propto\mathbbold{1}\{\theta\in C\}\textnormal{d}\theta
for suitable
C\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{2}
).
The third is approximately supported on the equator; more precisely it has a density given by
g(\theta)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}p_{\eta,\tau^{2}}(\theta)\textnormal{d}\nu(\eta)
where
\nu
is the uniform distribution on the equator and
\tau^{2}=0.0001
.
The remaining are Riemannian Gaussian mixture models with varying numbers of components and varying component scale parameters
\tau^{2}>0
.
While the locations of the components cannot be exactly equally-spaced in this setting, we make them approximately so by placing them at the locations of a Fibonacci spiral.
The component scale parameters are chosen in the 5-mode case as
\tau^{2}=0.05
, and in the 10-mode case as
\tau^{2}=0.001
.
In all cases we set
M
according to the conservative estimate
M\asymp n^{1/6}
, i.e.,
\ell_{n}=n^{1/3}
(Example
3.20
).

is fixed and

We show the results of these simulations in Figure

At the oracle level, we note as before that
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}

5
Scientific Applications

In this section we apply our methodology to two denoising problems from concrete scientific applications, where the goal is to estimate some latent manifold-valued random variables
\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{n}
from noisy manifold-valued measurements
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
thereof; that is, we compute the empirical tangential Bayes denoiser
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
and then the denoised data set
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}(X_{1}),\ldots,\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}(X_{n})
.
In each application we also display our estimate
\nabla\hat{f}_{n}/\max\{\rho,\hat{f}_{n}\}
of the score field, which (by slight abuse of notation) we denote
\nabla\log\hat{f}_{n}
; this helps to visualize the fine properties of the empirical tangential Bayes denoiser.

Since the distribution
G
is not known in practice, we cannot use the oracle choice of
M
and
\rho
described in Theorem
3.17
for hyperparameter selection; instead, we use cross-validation of the Riemannian version of Hyvärinen’s score-matching objective
[
19
]
, i.e. for a candidate density
\tilde{f}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
computed on some training data we compute

\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\tilde{f})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\|\nabla\log\tilde{f}(X_{i})\|_{X_{i}}^{2}+2\,\Delta\log\tilde{f}(X_{i})\right)

(30)

on a test set of hold-out data.
We also use an Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) penalty to prevent overfitting.
See Appendix
B
for further detail on computation and implementation.
(Recall the sign convention for
\Delta
in the Riemannian setting, which may cause confusion about the
+
sign in (
30
).)

5.1
Localization of Gamma Ray Bursts

Our first application concerns denoising the locations of a catalog of gamma ray bursts (GRBs), which are short-lived celestial objects consisting of an extremely powerful emission of light; see
[
66
]
for a detailed introduction.
Because of their transient nature, estimating the positions of GRBs, a task referred to as
localization
, is known to be difficult and has attracted a great deal of research attention
[
41
,
18
,
8
]
.
Moreover, GRBs are situated at an extremely large distance away from Earth, so their positions are often recorded as points on a sphere, in the so-called
celestial coordinates
of declination and right-ascension.
Thus, we naturally regard GRB localization as a sphere-valued denoising problem.
We use the data from the BATSE-4Br catalog
1
1

1

Available at
https://gammaray.nsstc.nasa.gov/batse/grb/catalog/4b/4br_basic.html
, which includes the positions of GRBs observed during 1991-1996.

To cast this problem in our framework, we set
\mathcal{M}:=\mathbb{S}^{2}
to be the two-dimensional sphere, we write
\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{n}
for the latent positions of
n=2702
GRBs, which we assume are i.i.d. samples from some
G\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
which possesses a density with respect to the volume form, and we write
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
for measurements of
\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{n}
, respectively.
We follow the conservative estimates from
[
8
]
that the error of the measurements is roughly 10 degrees, yielding
\sigma^{2}=(10\pi/180)^{2}\approx 3.046\times 10^{-2}
.

Implementing the empirical tangential Bayes denoiser
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
requires some computations with the eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator, which in this case are the spherical harmonics; to this end, we follow the set-up in Subsection
4.2
.

We also note that the positions of GRBs are not typically modeled with Riemannian Gaussian distributions but rather with von Mises-Fisher distributions
[
18
, Section 5]
, but that these distributions share many qualitative features (unimodality, squared-exponential decay away from the location parameter, etc.) and are extremely similar when
\sigma^{2}
is small.

The results are shown in Figure
5
.
As in Section
4
, we use the Mollweide projection to visualize the data, but the denoising takes place in the intrinsic geometry of the sphere and hence does not rely on this choice of visualization.
The selected hyperparameters are
\ell_{n}=576
(i.e.,
M=23
) and
\rho\approx 0.017
.
While the marginal distribution of the measurements appears to be close to uniform, we observe that the denoised data set has many small clusters which themselves seem to be approximately uniformly distributed over the sphere.

5.2
Protein Structure from Ramachandran Plots

Our second application concerns denoising the pairs of torsion angles formed by adjacent amino acids along the backbone of a given protein; the plot of all such pairs, referred to as a
Ramachandran plot
, is a scatter plot on the torus which contains a great deal of information regarding the structure of a protein
[
69
,
36
,
64
,
60
]
.
We focus on the ten enzymes of the glycolytic pathway (hexokinase, phosphoglucose isomerase, phosphofructokinase, aldolase, triosephosphate isomerase, GAPDH, phosphoglycerate kinase, phosphoglycerate mutase, enolase, and pyruvate kinase), obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB)
2
2

2

Available at:
https://www.rcsb.org/
via 1HKG, 1IRI, 1PFK, 1ADO, 2YPI, 1GD1, 3PGK, 1E58, 1ENO, and 1A3W
.
Analyzing a functionally related set of enzymes from the same metabolic pathway allows us to study shared structural features in the Ramachandran plot across proteins with diverse sequences.

To put this in the framework above, we set
\mathcal{M}:=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
to be the two-dimensional flat torus, and for each enzyme
1\leq j\leq 10
we write
\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{n_{j}}
for the pairs of latent adjacent torsion angles, which we assume are i.i.d. samples from some
G\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1})
possessing a density with respect to the volume form, and
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n_{j}}
for measurements thereof.
We pool all
n=\sum_{j}n_{j}
torsion-angle pairs and fit a single density
\hat{f}_{n}
to the pooled data, then apply the resulting denoiser to each enzyme separately.
In order to determine
\sigma^{2}
, we note that dihedral angles are not measured directly but rather are computed from measurements of atomic positions; as such, we take, as a crude estimate,
\sigma:=\arctan(\tau/b)
where
\tau^{2}=\bar{B}/(8\pi^{2})
is the mean-square atomic displacement derived from the pooled mean B-factor
\bar{B}\approx 26.42
Å
2
(as in
[
72
,
80
]
) and
b\approx 1.5
Å is a typical bond length, leading to the pooled estimate
\sigma^{2}\approx 0.136
.
Although our assumptions of independence need not hold in this setting, we believe that correlations among
\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{n}
(and conditional correlations among
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
given
\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{n}
) are weak and hence the methodology still provides a reasonable application of the method in practice.

Implementing our results requires an explicit form of the eigendecomposition of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
; this is easy since we studied the eigendecomposition of
\Delta
on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
in Subsection
4.1
, and the Laplace-Beltrami operator tensorizes on product manifolds.
More precisely, if we coordinatize
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
by representing
x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
as
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})\times(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})
, we may fix
M>0
and take our kernel to be

K(x,y,M)=\sum_{|k_{1}|,|k_{2}|\leq M}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{ik_{1}(x_{1}-y_{1})+ik_{2}(x_{2}-y_{2})}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}D_{M}(x_{1}-y_{1})D_{M}(x_{2}-y_{2})

where
\{D_{M}\}_{M\in\mathbb{N}}
is the Dirichlet kernel defined in (
29
).

We show the results in Figures
6
(and Figure
11
in Appendix
C
), where the selected hyperparameters are
\ell=38
(i.e.,
M=2
) and
\rho\approx 0.006
.
We observe that the denoiser exhibits some shrinkage near the upper-left, center-left, and center-right regions of the torus, which are known to correspond to the so-called
\beta
-sheets
,
right-handed
\alpha
-helices
, and
left-handed
\alpha
-helices
, respectively.
Overall, the empirical Bayes denoiser exhibits the same qualitative behavior as in the Euclidean setting: shrinkage towards high-density regions and stretching away from low-density regions of
\hat{f}_{n}
.
However, we notice some “ringing” phenomenon near the coordinates
(\frac{\pi}{2},-\frac{\pi}{4})
, whereby the shrinkage is not due to concentration of left-handed
\alpha
-helix torsion angles but rather due to nearby concentration of
\beta
-sheet torsion angles.

We make one last remark on the statistical interpretation of the denoised Ramachandran plot.
In many settings the Ramachandran plot is meant to be compared with a known decomposition of the torus
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
into “favorable”, “allowed”, and “disallowed” regions, in which case the denoised data set may not be a good surrogate for the latent variables; indeed, it is a manifestation of the shrinkage phenomenon that
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}(X_{1}),\ldots,\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}(X_{n})
and
\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{n}
can be close in the sense that their mean squared error is small while being far in the sense that their distributions differ.
Performing empirical Bayes denoising subject to some constraints that the distributions match, would require adapting methodologies like those in
[
45
]
to data on the torus or more general Riemannian manifolds.

6
Future Work

In this paper we have developed an empirical Bayes methodology for denoising, which (in the terminology of Efron
[
24
]
) is based on
f
-modeling, but it is natural to also consider strategies based on
g
-modeling.
That is, we may first estimate the density
g
of the distribution of
\Theta
(or, we may estimate the distribution
G
itself), and then we plug this into the definition of
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
or
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
to form our empirical Bayes denoiser.
Although
g
-modeling does not involve the Tweedie-Eddington formula in the construction of the denoiser, it is still used crucially in the development of statistical theory; as such, our Tweedie-Eddington formula (Lemma
3.1
) can provide the first step in future work on
g
-modeling approaches to nonparametric Riemannian empirical Bayes.
It is generally known that
g
-modeling is preferable to
f
-modeling (see
[
76
]
for a detailed discussion), and allows one to accommodate heteroskedasticity in the likelihood, as in our applications in Section
5
.

Another direction for future work is to extend our theory from the empirical Bayes setting to the compound decisions setting, namely the setting where
\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
are fixed (i.e., non-random) but unknown
[
89
,
78
,
67
]
.
This is closely related to our empirical Bayes setting but where the distribution
G
is replaced by the empirical measure of
\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{n}
, denoted
\bar{G}_{n}
, which does not even have a density.
While our Tweedie-Eddington formula (Lemma
3.1
) fails at some points since
\bar{G}_{n}
has atoms, it is still well-defined almost everywhere (with respect to the volume form).
The compound decisions setting thus introduces further technical difficulties beyond what we have addressed in this work.

Lastly, we discuss the potential application of the methodology in this work to the mathematical theory of cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM).
In cryo-EM, many identical copies of a particle of interest are suspended in an aqueous solution, rapidly frozen, and then imaged with an electron microscope; in this process, each particle leads to exactly one image since the imaging procedure destroys the particle.
While the random orientations are sometimes viewed as a nuisance parameter, several recent works
[
32
,
58
,
86
]
have argued that the subroutine of estimating the latent orientations is of independent interest, e.g., since it is known empirically that particles do not experience re-orientations uniformly at random.
The work
[
86
]
proposes a Bayesian methodology for the orientation estimation problem, where priors are carefully designed to reflect expected symmetries in the unknown molecule.
However, as the molecule and its symmetries are unknown in practice, it is of interest to develop methods that can borrow strength across particles while making minimal assumptions about the structure of the latent rotations; we believe that our empirical Bayes methodology balances these practical considerations.
We view this as an interesting future direction.

Lemma A.3
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is a compact connected Riemannian manifold, then for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\int_{\mathcal{M}}d^{k}(x,\theta)\,p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x)\textnormal{d}x\sim\mathbb{E}[\|Z\|^{k}]

as
\sigma^{2}\to 0
, uniformly in
\theta\in\mathcal{M}
, where

Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{m})
.

Proof.

First, recall from
[
62
, Theorem 5]
that we have

\lim_{\sigma^{2}\to 0}\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{\exp(A(\sigma^{2},\theta))}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{m}}=1,

(33)

which is similar to Lemma
A.2
with the sharp constant 1, but only asymptotically as
\sigma^{2}\to 0
.
Now fix
0<\varepsilon<1
, and use the uniform convergence
\lim_{v\to 0}\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{M}}|j_{\theta}(v)-1|=0
to get some
r>0
such that we have
|j_{\theta}(v)-1|<\varepsilon
for all
\theta\in\mathcal{M}
and
v\in B_{r}(0)\subseteq\Tan_{\theta}(\mathcal{M})
; by taking
r>0
smaller if necessary, using the fact that
\mathcal{M}
being compact implies a positive injectivity radius, we may also assume
B_{r}(0)\subseteq D_{\theta}
for all
\theta\in\mathcal{M}
.
Now, convert to normal coordinates (
32
) at
\theta
and use
d(x,\theta)=\|\Log_{x}\theta\|_{x}
to get

\begin{split}\int_{\mathcal{M}}d^{k}(x,\theta)p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x)\textnormal{d}x&=\int_{\mathcal{M}}d^{k}(x,\theta)\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}(x,\theta)}{2\sigma^{2}}-A(\sigma^{2},\theta)\right)\textnormal{d}x\\
&\sim\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{m}}\int_{\mathcal{M}}d^{k}(x,\theta)\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}(x,\theta)}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\textnormal{d}x\\
&=\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{m}}\int_{D_{\theta}}\|v\|^{k}_{\theta}\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|^{2}_{\theta}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)j_{\theta}(v)\textnormal{d}v\\
&=\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{m}}\int_{B_{r}(0)}\|v\|^{k}_{\theta}\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|^{2}_{\theta}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)j_{\theta}(v)\textnormal{d}v\\
&\qquad+\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{m}}\int_{D_{\theta}\setminus B_{r}(0)}\|v\|^{k}_{\theta}\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|^{2}_{\theta}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)j_{\theta}(v)\textnormal{d}v\end{split}

(34)

uniformly in
\theta
.
Next, note

\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{m}}\int_{\Tan_{\theta}(\mathcal{M})\setminus B_{r}(0)}\|v\|^{k}_{\theta}\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|^{2}_{\theta}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\textnormal{d}v\lesssim\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)

which vanishes as
\sigma^{2}\to 0
.
In particular, combining with Lemma
A.1
implies that the second term of (
34
) vanishes, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{m}}\int_{D_{\theta}\setminus B_{r}(0)}\|v\|^{k}_{\theta}\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|^{2}_{\theta}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)j_{\theta}(v)\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\mathcal{M}}\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{m}}\int_{\Tan_{\theta}(\mathcal{M})\setminus B_{r}(0)}\|v\|^{k}_{\theta}\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|^{2}_{\theta}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\textnormal{d}v

For the first term of (
34
), note that we have the upper bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{m}}

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}(0)}\|v\|^{k}_{\theta}\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|^{2}_{\theta}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)j_{\theta}(v)\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{m}}\int_{B_{r}(0)}\|v\|^{k}_{\theta}\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|^{2}_{\theta}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1+\varepsilon}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{m}}\int_{\Tan_{\theta}(\mathcal{M})\setminus B_{r}(0)}\|v\|^{k}_{\theta}\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|^{2}_{\theta}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle=(1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}\left[\|Z\|^{k}\right]-(1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}\left[\|Z\|^{k}\mathbbold{1}\{\|Z\|\geq r\}\right].

Since

Similarly, the first term of (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{m}}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{m}}\int_{\Tan_{\theta}(\mathcal{M})\setminus B_{r}(0)}\|v\|^{k}_{\theta}\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|^{2}_{\theta}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\textnormal{d}v

hence

as claimed.

Proof of Proposition

.

For convenience, we introduce the notation

V_{x}:=\Log_{x}\Theta,\qquad\mu_{x}:=\mathbb{E}[V_{x}\mid X=x],\qquad\Sigma_{x}:=\textnormal{Cov}(V_{x}\mid X=x),

(35)

for all
x\in\mathcal{M}
.
Also, we introduce normal coordinates in order to make some explicit calculations.
Let us write
D_{x}
for an exponential domain at

, and recall that the compactness of
\mathcal{M}
implies that there exists
r>0
such that we have
B_{r}(0)\subseteq D_{x}
for all
x\in\mathcal{M}
.

First, let us rewrite the integral expression for
\mu_{x}
in normal coordinates using (
32
) and
\|\Log_{x}\theta\|_{x}=d(x,\theta)
, then use Lemma
A.2
to bound:

\begin{split}\mu_{x}&=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Log_{x}\theta\;p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x)\textnormal{d}G(\theta)}{\int_{\mathcal{M}}p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x)\textnormal{d}G(\theta)}\\

(36)

where
\asymp_{\mathcal{M}}
denotes equality up to constants that depend only on
\mathcal{M}
and where
j_{x}
is the usual Jacobian determinant. Now let us control the numerator and denominator of the right side separately.

For the numerator, the idea is that the fast decay of the integrand allows us to replace the domain
D_{x}
with the smaller domain
B_{r}(0)
while only incurring an exponentially small error.
More precisely, we write

\begin{split}&(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{-m}\int_{D_{x}}v\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|_{x}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)g(\Exp_{x}(v))j_{x}(v)\textnormal{d}v\\

(37)

and we will show that the first term is
O(\sigma^{2})
and the second term is exponentially small hence also
O(\sigma^{2})
.
For the first term, develop a Taylor expansion with a first-order remainder

g(\Exp_{x}(v))\,j_{x}(v)=g(x)+r(x,v),

where
r
is continuous and satisfies the uniform bound

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\sup_{\|v\|_{x}\leq r}\frac{|r(x,v)|}{\|v\|_{x}}<\infty,

which holds since
g
is continuously differentiable,
j_{x}
is smooth in
(x,v)
on
\{\|v\|_{x}\leq r\}
, and
\mathcal{M}
is compact.
Then compute:

\displaystyle(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{-m}\int_{B_{r}(0)}v\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|_{x}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)g(\Exp_{x}(v))j_{x}(v)\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle=g(x)(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{-m}\int_{B_{r}(0)}v\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|_{x}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\textnormal{d}v+(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{-m}\int_{B_{r}(0)}vr(x,v)\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|_{x}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\textnormal{d}v.

Note that the first term vanishes by symmetry while the second term is
O(\sigma^{2})
uniformly in
x\in\mathcal{M}
.
For the second term of (
37
), use compactness of
\mathcal{M}

\begin{split}&(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{-m}\left\|\int_{D_{x}\setminus B_{r}(0)}v\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|_{x}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)g(\Exp_{x}(v))j_{x}(v)\textnormal{d}v\right\|\\

which is certainly
O(\sigma^{2})
, uniformly in
x\in\mathcal{M}
.

Lastly, we turn to the denominator of (
36
).
To control this, use continuity of

Then, compute:

\begin{split}(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{-m}\int_{D_{x}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|_{x}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)g(\Exp_{x}(v))j_{x}(v)\textnormal{d}v&\geq c(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{-m}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(0)}\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|_{x}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\textnormal{d}v\\
&=c\cdot\mathbb{P}(\|\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{m})\|\leq\varepsilon).\end{split}

(39)

Note that the probability converges to 1 as
\sigma^{2}\to 0
, hence the right hand side is
\Omega(1)
, uniformly in
x\in\mathcal{M}
.
By combining this calculation with that of the numerator, we have shown

32

\begin{split}\Sigma_{x}&=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Log_{x}\theta(\Log_{x}\theta)^{\top}\;p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x)\textnormal{d}G(\theta)}{\int_{\mathcal{M}}p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x)\textnormal{d}G(\theta)}-\mu_{x}\mu_{x}^{\top}\\

40
) establishes that the subtracted term is

\frac{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{-m}\int_{D_{x}}vv^{\top}\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|_{x}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)g(\Exp_{x}(v))j_{x}(v)\textnormal{d}v}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{-m}\int_{D_{x}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|_{x}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)g(\Exp_{x}(v))j_{x}(v)\textnormal{d}v}=O(\sigma^{2}).

Proof of Proposition
3.7
.

Let us write
\mathcal{W}

We observe that any denoiser
\delta:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}

w_{x}:=\Log_{x}(\delta(x))

Consequently, we may parameterize all such denoisers by elements of

In fact, if we write

Now let

.
For convenience, we adopt the notation from the proof of Proposition

Recall that

is, by definition a minimizer of the conditional risk which we can easily calculate; fix

, and use the Taylor series (

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(\Theta,\delta_{w}(X))\,|\,X=x]\\

(44)

In the last equality, we simply expanded all squared norms and plugged in the definitions in (

Because on a Riemannian manifold all squared distances and Taylor expansions thereof are smooth away from their cut loci, we can take the gradient of the expression above with respect to

.
Thus we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=2(w_{\mathcal{B}x}-\mu_{x})-\frac{2\mathbb{E}[K_{x}(V_{x},w_{\mathcal{B}x})\,|\,X=x]}{3}\left(w_{\mathcal{B}x}\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{x})-\Sigma_{x}w_{\mathcal{B}x}+w_{\mathcal{B}x}\|\mu_{x}\|^{2}-\mu_{x}\langle w_{\mathcal{B}x},\mu_{x}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle+O\!\left(\lVert w_{\mathcal{B}x}\rVert^{4}\right)-\frac{2\mathbb{E}[\nabla K_{x}(V_{x},w_{\mathcal{B}x})\,|\,X=x]}{3}\left(\|w_{x}\|^{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{x})-\langle w_{x},\Sigma_{x}w_{x}\rangle+\|w_{x}\|^{2}\|\mu_{x}\|^{2}-\langle w_{x},\mu_{x}\rangle^{2}\right),

Now apply the elementary bounds

\displaystyle\bigg\|w_{x}\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{x})-\Sigma_{x}w_{x}\bigg\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|w_{x}\|\|\Sigma_{x}\|,

\displaystyle\bigg\|w_{x}\|\mu_{x}\|^{2}-\mu_{x}\langle w_{x},\mu_{x}\rangle\bigg\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|w_{x}\|\|\mu_{x}\|\|w_{x}-\mu_{x}\|,

\displaystyle\bigg\|\|w_{x}\|^{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{x})-\langle w_{x},\Sigma_{x}w_{x}\rangle\bigg\|

\displaystyle\bigg\|\|w_{x}\|^{2}\|\mu\|^{2}-\langle w_{x},\mu_{x}\rangle^{2}\bigg\|

along with the fact that

\begin{split}&\|w_{\mathcal{B}x}-\mu_{x}\|\\

for sufficiently small

Thus, we get

(47)

by applying the estimates of Proposition
3.6
.

Next, we use this to control the size of

.
First, we show the qualitative statement that

as
\sigma^{2}\to 0
.
To see this, note that

\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(x)
is the Fréchet mean of the conditional distribution of
\Theta

\{X=x\}

\Theta

Since

is compact, weak convergence implies

Second, we use (

By combining (

\|\mu_{x}\|=O(\sigma^{2})

Now write

.
Finally, plugging the first claim into (

A.3
Proofs from Subsection
3.3

In this subsection, we prove some results establishing Sobolev bounds on the density of Riemannian Gaussian mixtures.

In some cases we bound the Sobolev norm of a function by first uniformly bounding its derivatives, and then using the following result to upgrade this to a bound on the Sobolev norm.

Lemma A.4
.

For integer
r\geq 1
and sufficiently smooth
h:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\|h\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}\asymp_{\mathcal{M},r}\|h\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}+\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|\nabla^{r}h(x)\|_{x,r}^{2}\textnormal{d}x

Proof.

It is standard (see
[
1
, Chapter 2]
) that we have

\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}\asymp_{\mathcal{M},r}\|h\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}+\|\Delta^{r/2}h\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}

so it suffices to show

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|\nabla^{r}h(x)\|_{x,r}^{2}\textnormal{d}x\asymp_{\mathcal{M},r}\|\Delta^{r/2}h\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}.

To do this, note that if
r
is even then we can iteratively apply the following elliptic regularity bound (which follows, e.g., from Gårding’s inequality
[
81
, Chapter 7.6, Vol II]
):

\|\nabla^{2}h\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}\lesssim_{\mathcal{M}}\|h\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}+\|\Delta h\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}.

If
r
is odd, then we combine this with the identity in (
25
).
∎

Now we turn to the proofs of the main results from the body.

Proof of Lemma
3.9
.

By (
25
),

\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}=\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}+\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|\nabla f(x)\|_{x}^{2}\textnormal{d}x.

By Lemma
3.2
, we have

\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}\leq\textnormal{vol}(\mathcal{M})\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}\lesssim_{\mathcal{M}}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}.

By Lemma
3.3
,

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|\nabla f(x)\|_{x}^{2}\textnormal{d}x\leq\textnormal{vol}(\mathcal{M})\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\|\nabla f(x)\|_{x}^{2}\lesssim_{\mathcal{M}}\sigma^{-2}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}.

Since
\sigma^{2}\leq(\diam(\mathcal{M}))^{2}
, the first term is absorbed into the second term, up to a constant depending only on
\mathcal{M}
.
Taking square roots gives the result.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.10
.

Write
H_{r}
for the
r
th multivariate Hermite polynomial in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, i.e., the
r
-linear form defined by the relation

\nabla^{r}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}\right)=(-1)^{r}H_{r}(x)\exp\left(-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}\right).

Then we can compute

r

denotes any norm on the finite-dimensional vector space of
r
-linear forms:

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{r}f(x)\|_{r}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\nabla^{r}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-\theta\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)g(\theta)\textnormal{d}\theta\right\|_{r}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{\sigma^{-r}}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}H_{r}\left(\frac{x-\theta}{\sigma}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-\theta\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)g(\theta)\textnormal{d}\theta\right\|_{r}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{\sigma^{-r}}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}H_{r}\left(-\frac{v}{\sigma}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\|v\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)g(x+v)\textnormal{d}v\right\|_{r}

Now write
Z
for a standard Gaussian random variable in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
(i.e., with mean 0 and covariance matrix

), so that the expression above is just

\|\nabla^{r}f(x)\|_{r}\leq\sigma^{-r}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|H_{r}\left(Z\right)\right\|_{r}\right].

Since the expectation depends only on
m
and

, this finishes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma

.

Directly compute
f^{\ast}(0)=(\textnormal{vol}(\mathcal{M}))^{-1/2}
and
f^{\ast}(j)=0
for all
j\geq 1
.

Proof of Lemma
3.14
.

First, we define the kernel
K_{\sigma}:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
by

K_{\sigma}(x):=\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}(x,0)}{2\sigma^{2}}-A(\sigma^{2})\right),

which, by representing
\mathbb{S}^{1}=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
can be written as

K_{\sigma}(t):=\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}-A(\sigma^{2})\right)

which extending periodically from
t\in[-\pi,\pi]
to
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Note that
A(\sigma^{2})
does not depend on
\theta
since
\mathcal{M}
is homogeneous.
Then note that

f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}K_{\sigma}(x-\theta)\,g(\theta)\,\textnormal{d}\theta,

which is exactly the convolution of
K_{\sigma}
and
g
on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

Next we bound the Fourier series coefficients of
K_{\sigma}
.
For convenience, we index the eigenvalues as
\lambda_{k}=k^{2}
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, and we claim that

|K^{\ast}_{\sigma}(k)|\asymp\sigma^{-2}(1+k^{2})^{-1}

(50)

holds uniformly over
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
0<\sigma^{2}\leq\pi^{2}
.
The claim is obvious for
k=0
, since
K_{\sigma}
being a probability density implies
K_{\sigma}^{\ast}(0)\lesssim 1\lesssim\sigma^{-2}
for
\sigma^{2}\leq\pi^{2}
.
So, fix
k\neq 0
, and use integration by parts to get

K_{\sigma}^{\ast}(k)=\frac{1}{2\pi ik}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}K_{\sigma}^{\prime}(t)e^{-ikt}\,\textnormal{d}t.

For
-\pi<t<\pi
we have

K_{\sigma}^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{t}{\sigma^{2}}K_{\sigma}(t),

so
K_{\sigma}^{\prime}
is piecewise continuous and has a jump at
\pm\pi
.
Integrating by parts again gives

K_{\sigma}^{\ast}(k)\leq\frac{1}{2\pi k^{2}}\left(\bigl|K_{\sigma}^{\prime}(\pi^{-})-K_{\sigma}^{\prime}(-\pi^{+})\bigr|+\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|K_{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}(t)|\,\textnormal{d}t\right),

(51)

and we can analyze the two terms in parentheses separately.
The first term of (
51
) is exactly

|K_{\sigma}^{\prime}(\pi^{-})-K_{\sigma}^{\prime}(-\pi^{+})|=2\pi\sigma^{-2}K_{\sigma}(\pi)=2\pi\sigma^{-2}\exp\left(-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}-A(\sigma^{2})\right),

and Lemma
A.2
implies

\exp(-A(\sigma^{2}))\lesssim\sigma^{-1}
for
0<\sigma^{2}\leq\pi^{2}
, hence
|K_{\sigma}^{\prime}(\pi^{-})-K_{\sigma}^{\prime}(-\pi^{+})|\lesssim\sigma^{-3}\exp(-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}})\lesssim\sigma^{-2}
.
For the second term of (
51
), we compute
K_{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}(t)=(\sigma^{-4}t^{2}-\sigma^{-2})K_{\sigma}(t)
for
-\pi<t<\pi
,
hence

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|K_{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}(t)|\,\textnormal{d}t\leq\sigma^{-4}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}t^{2}K_{\sigma}(t)\,\textnormal{d}t+\sigma^{-2}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}K_{\sigma}(t)\,\textnormal{d}t.

The second integral is exactly
1
, and the first integral is bounded by
\sigma^{-2}
, which follows from Lemma
A.2
and the
Gaussian moment bound
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}t^{2}e^{-t^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}\,\textnormal{d}t\lesssim\sigma^{3}
, hence we have shown
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|K_{\sigma}^{\prime\prime}(t)|\,\textnormal{d}t\asymp\sigma^{-2}.

Substituting these bounds into (
51
) establishes

|K_{\sigma}^{\ast}(k)|\asymp\sigma^{-2}k^{-2}

for all
k\neq 0
, whence (
50
).

Lastly, we use the convolution identity for Fourier coefficients, the bound
(
50
), and the definition of Sobolev norms to obtain

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s+2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(1+k^{2})^{s+2}|f^{\ast}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(1+k^{2})^{s+2}|K_{\sigma}^{\ast}(k)|^{2}|g^{\ast}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\asymp\sigma^{-4}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(1+k^{2})^{s}|g^{\ast}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-4}\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{2}.

Taking square roots completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.16
.

First, note that we have
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
because of
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}<\infty
for some
r>0
, and also that
\hat{f}_{n}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
because it is written as a finite linear combination of
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}\subseteq L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
.
Now observe that
f^{\ast},\hat{f}_{n}^{\ast}:\{0,1,\ldots,\}\to\mathbb{C}
are given by

f^{\ast}(j):=\int_{\mathcal{M}}f(x)\overline{\phi_{j}(x)}\textnormal{d}x

and

\hat{f}_{n}^{\ast}(j):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\overline{\phi_{j}(X_{i})}&\textnormal{ if }j\leq\ell\\
0&\textnormal{ if }j>\ell,\end{cases}

so that we have

\hat{f}_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\hat{f}_{n}^{\ast}(j)\phi_{j}(x)

and also that
\hat{f}_{n}^{\ast}(j)
is an unbiased estimator of
f^{\ast}(j)
for all
0\leq j\leq\ell
.

The first claim is based on the following calculations.
First, expand

\begin{split}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left|\hat{f}_{n}(x)-f(x)\right|^{2}\textnormal{d}x&=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\left|\hat{f}_{n}^{\ast}(j)-f^{\ast}(j)\right|^{2}+\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{\infty}|f^{\ast}(j)|^{2}\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{j}(X_{i})-f^{\ast}(j)\right|^{2}+\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{\infty}|f^{\ast}(j)|^{2}.\end{split}

(52)

and let us now handle the two terms on the right side separately.
For the first term (which represents the variance), we take the expectation over
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
then use Lemma
3.2
to get:

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{j}(X_{i})-f^{\ast}(j)\right|^{2}\right]&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\,\textnormal{Var}(\phi_{j}(X_{1}))\\
&\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\,\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\phi_{j}(x)|^{2}f(x)\textnormal{d}x\\
&\leq\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}\frac{\ell}{n}\\
&\lesssim_{\mathcal{M}}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}\frac{\ell}{n}\\
\end{split}

(53)

for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.
For the second term (which is non-random, and can be interpreted as the squared bias), use the assumption of
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}<\infty
, and Weyl’s law
[
16
, Section VI.4]
, to get

\begin{split}\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{\infty}|f^{\ast}(j)|^{2}&\leq\lambda_{\ell}^{-r}\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{r}|f^{\ast}(j)|^{2}\\
&\leq\lambda_{\ell}^{-r}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{r}|f^{\ast}(j)|^{2}\\
&\leq\lambda_{\ell}^{-r}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(1+\lambda_{j})^{r}|f^{\ast}(j)|^{2}\\
&=\lambda_{\ell}^{-r}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}\\
&\lesssim_{\mathcal{M},r}\ell^{-\frac{2r}{m}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}\\
\end{split}

(54)

for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.
Consequently, plugging in (
54
) and (
53
) into (
52
) yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left|\hat{f}_{n}(x)-f(x)\right|^{2}\textnormal{d}x\right]\lesssim_{\mathcal{M}}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}\frac{\ell}{n}+\ell^{-\frac{2r}{m}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}

(55)

for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.

The second claim is based on similar calculations, as follows.
First, use (
25
) to expand

\begin{split}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left\|\nabla\hat{f}_{n}(x)-\nabla f(x)\right\|_{x}^{2}\textnormal{d}x&=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\lambda_{j}\left|\hat{f}_{n}^{\ast}(j)-f^{\ast}(j)\right|^{2}+\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}|f^{\ast}(j)|^{2}\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\lambda_{j}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{j}(X_{i})-f^{\ast}(j)\right|^{2}+\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}|f^{\ast}(j)|^{2},\end{split}

(56)

and we control these terms as before.
For the first term, take expectation and use Lemma
3.2
and Weyl’s law:

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\lambda_{j}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{j}(X_{i})-f^{\ast}(j)\right|^{2}\right]&=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\lambda_{j}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{j}(X_{i})-f^{\ast}(j)\right|^{2}\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\,\lambda_{j}\textnormal{Var}(\phi_{j}(X_{1}))\\
&\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\,\lambda_{j}\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\phi_{j}(x)|^{2}f(x)\textnormal{d}x\\
&\leq\frac{\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\,\lambda_{j}\\
&\lesssim_{\mathcal{M}}\frac{\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\,\lambda_{j}\\
&\leq\frac{\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}}{n}\ell\lambda_{\ell}\\
&\lesssim_{\mathcal{M}}\frac{\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}}{n}\ell^{1+\frac{2}{m}}\\
\end{split}

(57)

for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.
For the second term, use
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}<\infty
, and Weyl’s law:

\begin{split}\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}|f^{\ast}(j)|^{2}&=\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{1-r}\lambda_{j}^{r}|f^{\ast}(j)|^{2}\\
&\leq\lambda_{\ell}^{1-r}\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{r}|f^{\ast}(j)|^{2}\\
&\leq\lambda_{\ell}^{1-r}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{r}|f^{\ast}(j)|^{2}\\
&\leq\lambda_{\ell}^{1-r}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(1+\lambda_{j})^{r}|f^{\ast}(j)|^{2}\\
&=\lambda_{\ell}^{1-r}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}\\
&\lesssim_{\mathcal{M},r}\ell^{\frac{2(1-r)}{m}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}\\
\end{split}

(58)

for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.
Now plug (
58
) and (
57
) into (
56
) to obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left\|\nabla\hat{f}_{n}(x)-\nabla f(x)\right\|_{x}^{2}\textnormal{d}x\right]\lesssim_{\mathcal{M}}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}\frac{\ell^{1+\frac{2}{m}}}{n}+\ell^{\frac{2(1-r)}{m}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}

(59)

for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.

Note that we can capture both (
55
) and (
59
) in the following expression for
k\in\{0,1\}
, where we use the norm
\|\cdot\|
to denote
|\cdot|
in the
k=0
case and to denote
\|\cdot\|_{x}
in the
k=1
case:

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left\|\nabla^{k}\hat{f}_{n}(x)-\nabla^{k}f(x)\right\|^{2}\textnormal{d}x\right]\lesssim_{\mathcal{M}}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}\frac{\ell^{1+\frac{2k}{m}}}{n}+\ell^{\frac{2(k-r)}{m}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}.

Thus, we complete the proof by plugging in the given form of

to obtain

\begin{split}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla^{k}\hat{f}_{n}(x)-\nabla^{k}f(x)\right\|^{2}\right]\textnormal{d}x&\lesssim_{\mathcal{M},r}\frac{\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}\ell^{1+\frac{2k}{m}}}{n}+\ell^{\frac{2(k-r)}{m}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}\\
&=\left(\frac{\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}\ell}{n}+\frac{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}}{\ell^{\frac{2r}{m}}}\right)\ell^{\frac{2k}{m}}\\
&\asymp\frac{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathcal{M})}^{\frac{2m}{2r+m}}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}^{\frac{2r}{2r+m}}}{n^{\frac{2r}{2r+m}}}\ell^{\frac{2k}{m}}\\

(60)

This implies both claims by taking
k=0
and
k=1
, respectively.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

For convenience, write

\hat{w}_{\mathcal{T}x}:=\sigma^{2}\nabla_{x}\hat{f}_{n}(x)/\max\{\hat{f}_{n}(x),\rho\}

(61)

for all

so that we can expand the norm appearing on the right side above as

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\nabla_{x}\hat{f}_{n}(x)}{\max\{\hat{f}_{n}(x),\rho\}}-\frac{\nabla_{x}f(x)}{f(x)}\right\|^{2}_{x}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|\frac{\nabla_{x}\hat{f}_{n}(x)-\nabla_{x}f(x)}{\max\{\hat{f}_{n}(x),\rho\}}\right\|_{x}^{2}+\left\|\frac{\nabla_{x}f(x)}{f(x)}\right\|^{2}\left|\frac{f(x)-\max\{\hat{f}_{n}(x),\rho\}}{\max\{\hat{f}_{n}(x),\rho\}}\right|^{2}.

3.2

3.16

\end{split}

(62)

For the second term, use

3.3
and Lemma
3.2
to get

Proof of Theorem
3.22
.

Before the main part of the proof, we briefly introduce, for each
p\in\mathbb{N}
, a collection of functions
\psi_{p,0},\ldots,\psi_{p,2^{p}-1}:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
that will feature in our construction.
The existence of these functions involves standard properties of wavelets on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
which can be found in
[
85
, Theorem 7.2]
or
[
2
, Section 2]
.
Specifically, we may construct such functions so that, for all
a_{0},\ldots a_{2^{p}-1}\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\left\|\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}a_{z}\psi_{p,z}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{2}\asymp\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}a_{z}^{2}

(64)

and, for all
\varepsilon_{0},\ldots\varepsilon_{2^{p}-1}\in\{0,1\}
, we have

\left\|\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}\varepsilon_{z}\psi_{p,z}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\lesssim 2^{p/2}.

(65)

In fact, we further have the property that, for all
z
we have
\psi_{p,z}^{\ast}(k)\neq 0
only if
|k|\asymp 2^{p}
, which allows us to generalize (
64
) as follows; for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
and all
a_{0},\ldots a_{2^{p}-1}\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\begin{split}\left\|\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}a_{z}\psi_{p,z}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(1+k^{2})^{r}\left|\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}a_{z}\psi_{p,z}^{\ast}(k)\right|^{2}&\asymp_{r}2^{2pr}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}a_{z}\psi_{p,z}^{\ast}(k)\right|^{2}\\
&=2^{2pr}\left\|\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}a_{z}\psi_{p,z}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\asymp 2^{2pr}\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}a_{z}^{2},\end{split}

(66)

for all
p
.

Next we define the functions
\zeta_{p,0},\ldots,\zeta_{p,2^{p}-1}:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
as follows, for each
z
:

\zeta_{p,z}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}p_{\theta,\sigma^{2}}(x)\psi_{p,z}(\theta)\textnormal{d}\theta=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}(x,\theta)}{2\sigma^{2}}-A(\sigma^{2})\right)\psi_{p,z}(\theta)\textnormal{d}\theta.

Note that
\zeta_{p,z}
has the interpretation of the “density” of
X
when
\psi_{p,z}
is the “density” of
\Theta
in (
1
), although these functions can take negative values so are not bona fide densities.
As we showed in the proof of Lemma
3.14
, we have
\zeta_{p,z}^{\ast}(k)\asymp_{\sigma^{2}}(1+k^{2})^{-1}\psi_{p,z}^{\ast}(k)
for all
|k|\asymp 2^{p}
, from which we can deduce bounds similar to (
66
).
Indeed, just compute

\left\|\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}a_{z}\zeta_{p,z}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{2}=\left\|\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}a_{z}\zeta_{p,z}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{2}\asymp_{\sigma^{2}}\left\|\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}a_{z}\psi_{p,z}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{-2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{2}\asymp 2^{-4p}\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}a_{z}^{2}

(67)

and

\begin{split}\left\|\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}a_{z}\nabla\zeta_{p,z}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k^{2}\left|\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}a_{z}\zeta_{p,z}^{\ast}(k)\right|^{2}&\asymp 2^{2p}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}a_{z}\zeta_{p,z}^{\ast}(k)\right|^{2}\\
&=2^{2p}\left\|\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}a_{z}\zeta_{p,z}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\asymp 2^{-2p}\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}a_{z}^{2}\end{split}

(68)

for all
p
.

Now we set up the proof using Assouad’s method.
To do this, fix
p\in\mathbb{N}
such that
2^{p}\asymp n^{1/(2s+5)}
and fix
\gamma>0
such that
\gamma\asymp L2^{-p(s+\frac{1}{2})}=Ln^{-(s+\frac{1}{2})/(2s+5)}
.
Then, for each
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{0},\ldots,\varepsilon_{2^{p}-1})\in\{0,1\}^{2^{p}}
, set

g_{\varepsilon}(\theta):=\frac{1}{2\pi}+\gamma\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}\varepsilon_{z}\psi_{p,z}(\theta).

In order to make sure that
g_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{G}_{s}(\alpha,\beta,L)
for all
\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}^{2^{p}}
, note from (
65
) and
\gamma\lesssim L2^{-p\left(s+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\lesssim 2^{-p/2}
that we have
\alpha\leq g_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\leq\beta
for all
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and similarly that from (
66
) and
\gamma\leq L2^{-p\left(s+\frac{1}{2}\right)}
we have
\|g_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\leq L
.
Now for convenience, write
f_{\varepsilon}
for the marginal density of
X
when
\Theta
has density
g_{\varepsilon}
in (
1
), so that
f_{\varepsilon}=(2\pi)^{-1}+\gamma\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}\varepsilon_{z}\zeta_{p,z}
.
The core of the rest of the proof is to show that
\{f_{\varepsilon}:\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}^{2^{p}}\}
are close in terms of their KL divergence but far in terms of the displacement of their denoisers.
Throughout the rest of the proof, we write
d_{\mathrm{H}}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
for the Hamming distance between
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{2^{p}}
.

For the KL computation, suppose
d_{\mathrm{H}}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})=1
, hence without loss of generality
f_{\varepsilon}-f_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}=\gamma\zeta_{p,z_{0}}
for some
z_{0}
.
Then, use Lemma
3.2
to get
f_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x)\gtrsim\alpha
for all
x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and use (
67
) to compute

\KL(f_{\varepsilon}\,|\,f_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\lesssim_{\alpha}\|f_{\varepsilon}-f_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}=\gamma^{2}\left\|\zeta_{p,z_{0}}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\asymp\gamma^{2}2^{-4p}

Further, if we let
P_{\varepsilon}
be the joint distribution of i.i.d. samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
from
f_{\varepsilon}
, and similarly for
P_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
, we have

\KL(P_{\varepsilon}\,|\,P_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})=n\KL(f_{\varepsilon}\,|\,f_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\lesssim_{\alpha}n\gamma^{2}2^{-4p}

(69)

for all
n
.
(Note that we chose
\gamma\asymp L2^{-p(s+\frac{1}{2})}
and
2^{p}\asymp n^{1/(2s+5)}
precisely to guarantee that these KL divergences on adjacent hypothesis are bounded by a small universal constant).

For the displacement computation, we claim

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}d^{2}(\delta_{\mathcal{T}\varepsilon}(x),\delta_{\mathcal{T}\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x))\textnormal{d}x\gtrsim_{\sigma^{2},\alpha,\beta}\gamma^{2}2^{-2p}d_{\mathrm{H}}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})

(70)

for all
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{2^{p}}
, where
\delta_{\mathcal{T}\varepsilon}(x)=\Exp_{x}(\sigma^{2}\nabla\log f_{\varepsilon}(x))
and
\delta_{\mathcal{T}\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x)=\Exp_{x}(\sigma^{2}\nabla\log f_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x))
denote the oracle tangential Bayes denoisers when
\Theta
has distribution with density given by
g_{\varepsilon}
and
g_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
, respectively.
To do this, we first show that for all
0<\sigma^{2}<\pi^{2}
there exists a constant
0<C_{\alpha,\sigma^{2}}<\pi
such that we have

\sup_{\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}^{2^{p}}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}\|\Log_{x}(\delta_{\mathcal{T}\varepsilon}(x))\|_{x}\leq C_{\alpha,\sigma^{2}}

(71)

To see this, use the Tweedie-Eddington formula (Lemma
3.1
), Jensen’s inequality, and the identity,
\|\Log_{x}\theta\|_{x}=d(x,\theta)
to obtain

\displaystyle\|\Log_{x}(\delta_{\mathcal{T}\varepsilon}(x))\|_{x}=\left\|\mathbb{E}[\Log_{x}\Theta\,|\,X=x]\right\|_{x}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\|\Log_{x}\Theta\|_{x}\,|\,X=x]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[d(x,\Theta)\,|\,X=x]

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}d(x,\theta)\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}(x,\theta)}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)g_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\textnormal{d}\theta}{\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}(x,\theta)}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)g_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\textnormal{d}\theta}

\displaystyle\leq\pi-(\pi-\sigma)2\alpha\sigma\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)=:C_{\alpha,\sigma^{2}},

where the last line follows by direct calculation after splitting the integral into
[-\sigma,\sigma]
and its complement
[-\pi,\pi]\setminus[-\sigma,\sigma]
.
Next note that (
71
) implies that there exists a constant
c_{\alpha,\sigma^{2}}>0
such that we have

d\left(\delta_{\mathcal{T}\varepsilon}(x),\delta_{\mathcal{T}\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x)\right)\geq c_{\alpha,\sigma^{2}}\|\nabla\log f_{\varepsilon}(x)-\nabla\log f_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x)\|_{x},

for all
x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, hence

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}d^{2}(\delta_{\mathcal{T}\varepsilon}(x),\delta_{\mathcal{T}\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x))\textnormal{d}x\right]\gtrsim_{\alpha,\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\|\nabla\log f_{\varepsilon}(x)-\nabla\log f_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x)\|^{2}_{x}\textnormal{d}x\right].

To further this lower bound, use Lemma
3.2
to get
\alpha\lesssim f_{\varepsilon}(x)\lesssim\beta
for all
x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
and all
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{2^{p}}
, hence

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\log f_{\varepsilon}-\nabla\log f_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{f_{\varepsilon}\nabla f_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-f_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\nabla f_{\varepsilon}}{f_{\varepsilon}f_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{2}

\displaystyle\gtrsim_{\beta}\left\|f_{\varepsilon}\nabla f_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-f_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\nabla f_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|f_{\varepsilon}\left(\nabla f_{\varepsilon}+\gamma\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{z}-\varepsilon_{z})\nabla\zeta_{p,z}\right)-\left(f_{\varepsilon}+\gamma\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{z}-\varepsilon_{z})\zeta_{p,z}\right)\nabla f_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}\left\|f_{\varepsilon}\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{z}-\varepsilon_{z})\nabla\zeta_{p,z}-\left(\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{z}-\varepsilon_{z})\zeta_{p,z}\right)\nabla f_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\gamma^{2}\left(\left\|f_{\varepsilon}\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{z}-\varepsilon_{z})\nabla\zeta_{p,z}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}-\left\|\left(\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{z}-\varepsilon_{z})\zeta_{p,z}\right)\nabla f_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\right)^{2}.

Now bound both of the terms on the right side as follows.
For the first term, use
f_{\varepsilon}(x)\gtrsim\alpha
then (
68
) to obtain

\left\|f_{\varepsilon}\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{z}-\varepsilon_{z})\nabla\zeta_{p,z}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\gtrsim_{\alpha}\left\|\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{z}-\varepsilon_{z})\nabla\zeta_{p,z}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\asymp 2^{-2p}\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}(\varepsilon_{z}^{\prime}-\varepsilon_{z})^{2}.

For the second term, use Lemma
3.3
and (
67
) to get

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{z}-\varepsilon_{z})\zeta_{p,z}\right)\nabla f_{\varepsilon}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{z}-\varepsilon_{z})\zeta_{p,z}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\|\nabla f_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\sigma^{2}}\left\|\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{z}-\varepsilon_{z})\zeta_{p,z}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\|g_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{2}

\displaystyle\asymp_{\beta}2^{-4p}\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{z}-\varepsilon_{z})^{2}.

Now combine these displays and note that
p\to\infty
, so the dominant term between
2^{-2p}
and
2^{-4p}
is the slower one, i.e.,
2^{-2p}
.
Thus,

\left\|\nabla\log f_{\varepsilon}-\nabla\log f_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{2}\gtrsim_{\sigma^{2},\alpha,\beta}\gamma^{2}\sum_{z=0}^{2^{p}-1}(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{z}-\varepsilon_{z})^{2}\left(2^{-2p}-2^{-4p}\right)^{2}\gtrsim\gamma^{2}2^{-2p}d_{\mathrm{H}}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})

for all
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{2^{p}}
, which is exactly (
70
).

To put the pieces together, we use Assouad’s lemma (e.g.,
[
68
, Theorem 31.2]
) and Pinsker’s inequality. Using the bounds in (
69
) and (
70
) and the specification
\gamma\asymp L2^{-p(s+\frac{1}{2})}
and
2^{p}\asymp n^{1/(2s+5)}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\delta}}\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{s}(\alpha,\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}d^{2}(\hat{\delta}(x),\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x))\textnormal{d}x\right]

\displaystyle\gtrsim_{\sigma^{2},\alpha,\beta}\gamma^{2}2^{-2p}\cdot 2^{p}\left(1-\max_{d_{\mathrm{H}}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})=1}\sqrt{\KL(P_{\varepsilon}|P_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})}\right)

\displaystyle\asymp_{L}n^{-\frac{2s+1}{2s+5}}2^{-p}\left(1-n^{1/2}\gamma 2^{-2p}\right)

\displaystyle\asymp n^{-\frac{2s+2}{2s+5}}.

Finally, use Lemma
3.2
to get
f(x)\gtrsim\alpha
for all
x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
and all
g\in\mathcal{G}_{s}(\alpha,\beta,L)
, hence

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\delta}}\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{s}(\alpha,\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}d^{2}(\hat{\delta}(x),\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x))f(x)\textnormal{d}x\right]

\displaystyle\gtrsim_{\alpha}\inf_{\hat{\delta}}\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{s}(\alpha,\beta,L)}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}d^{2}(\hat{\delta}(x),\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x))\textnormal{d}x\right]\gtrsim_{\sigma^{2},\alpha,\beta,L}n^{-\frac{2s+2}{2s+5}}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.18
.

Recall
[
30
, Lemma 2.3]
that for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that we have
d^{2}(x,z)\leq(1+\varepsilon)d^{2}(x,y)+C_{\varepsilon}d^{2}(y,z)
for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{M}
.
Thus, for each
x,\theta\in\mathcal{M}
we have

d^{2}\left(\delta(x),\theta\right)\leq(1+\varepsilon)d^{2}(\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x),\theta)+C_{\varepsilon}d^{2}\left(\delta(x),\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x)\right).

Now integrate this over the joint distribution of
(\Theta,X)
to get the result.

B
Implementation Details

In this section, we describe the details of implementing our denoising procedures.
Specifically, in Subsection
B.1
we discuss approximately computing the oracle denoisers
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
, in Subsection
B.2
we discuss computing the empirical tangential Bayes denoiser
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
, and in Subsection
B.3
we discuss the hyperparameter selection via the Riemannian score-matching objective.

B.1
Approximating the Oracle Denoisers

In the simulations of the main body, both oracle denoisers
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}
are approximated by utilizing additional oracle samples from
G
.
In this subsection we explain this approximation and its computation in more detail.

B.1.1
Oracle Bayes Denoiser

First, we consider computing the oracle Bayes denoiser
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}(x)
for fixed
x\in\mathcal{M}
, defined implicitly via the optimization problem in (
22
).
To approximate it, we sample i.i.d pairs
(\Theta_{1},X_{1}),\ldots,(\Theta_{n},X_{n})
from the model (
1
), and we estimate the conditional Fréchet mean of
\Theta
given
\{X=x\}
via Fréchet Nadaraya-Watson regression; that is, we use
Geomstats
to compute the Fréchet mean of the probability distribution

\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(x)\delta_{\Theta_{i}}

where

w_{i}(x)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{d^{2}(X_{i},\,x)}{2h^{2}}\right),

(72)

for some bandwidth parameter
h>0
selected by cross-validation.

B.1.2
Oracle Tangential Bayes Denoiser

Second, we consider computing the oracle tangential Bayes denoiser
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x)
for fixed
x\in\mathcal{M}
, defined in (
23
).
Since Lemma
3.1
allows us to write

\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x)=\Exp_{x}\left(\sigma^{2}\frac{\nabla f(x)}{f(x)}\right),

it follows that a straightforward approximation of
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}(x)
is given as follows; sample i.i.d random variables
\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{n}
from
G
, and then compute

\Exp_{x}\left(\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Log_{x}(\Theta_{i})p_{\Theta_{i},\sigma^{2}}(x)}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{\Theta_{i},\sigma^{2}}(x)}\right).

If
\mathcal{M}
is homogeneous, then the normalizing constants above all cancel and we can simply compute

\Exp_{x}\left(\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Log_{x}(\Theta_{i})\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}(\Theta_{i},x)}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}(\Theta_{i},x)}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)}\right)

In order to reduce the variance of this approximation in practice (recall that all our applications in practice are homogeneous), we fix a fine grid
S\subseteq\mathcal{M}
and we compute

\Exp_{x}\left(\frac{\sum_{\theta\in S}\Log_{x}(\theta)w(\theta)}{\sum_{\theta\in S}w(\theta)}\right)

where

w(\theta):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{d^{2}(\theta,x)}{2\sigma^{2}}\right).

B.2
Computing Empirical Tangential Bayes Denoiser

Even computing the empirical tangential Bayes denoiser
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
, as in the simulations and applications in the main body, requires some work.
The complication is that the expression (
28
) for
\hat{\delta}_{\mathcal{T}}
involves gradients of
\hat{f}_{n}
which by (
27
) involve gradients of the eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta
.
While there is no closed-form expression for such gradients in general, they can be computed by embedding
\mathcal{M}
into
\mathbb{R}^{k}
for some
k
and then applying the well-known result (see
[
7
, Chapter 3]
) that the Riemannian gradient of a function
h:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
at
x\in\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
is just the projection of the ambient (Euclidean) gradient
\nabla h(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
onto the (affine) tangent space
x+\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{M})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

In the case of the circle
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{1}
, we may directly differentiate the Dirichlet kernel (
29
), which is equivalent to embedding
\mathbb{S}^{1}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
as the unit circle.

In the case of the sphere
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{2}
, we embed
\mathbb{S}^{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
via
\mathbb{S}^{2}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\|x\|=1\}
.
Then use the summation formula to get that suitable sums of eigenfunctions are equal to suitable Legendre polynomials; we then differentiate these polynomials and multiply the result by the matrix
I_{3}-xx^{\top}
which corresponds to the projection onto
\Tan_{x}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
.

B.3
Hyperparameter Selection

In the applications of Section
5
where we do not have oracle information to guide our choices of
\ell_{n}
and
\rho
, we instead selected these hyperparameters with the data-driven method of cross-validation, where the objective is an adaptation of Hyvärinen’s score matching objective
[
42
]
to the Riemannian setting
[
19
]
.
In this subsection we explain this in further detail.

B.3.1
Derivation of Riemannian Score Matching

First, we explain how to derive the objective
\hat{\mathcal{J}}
from (
30
).
Note that our empirical tangential Bayes denoiser directly involves the vector field
\nabla\log f(x)
on
\mathcal{M}
, where
f:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
is the marginal density of
X
, hence a natural loss function for a candidate density
\tilde{f}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
is

\mathcal{L}(\tilde{f})=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla\log\tilde{f}(X)-\nabla\log f(X)\|_{X}^{2}\big].

(73)

Of course, this cannot be computed in practice since it involves the unknown
f
.
Nonetheless, we can expand the square and then use integration by parts to get

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\tilde{f})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla\log\tilde{f}(X)\|_{X}^{2}\big]-2\,\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\nabla\log\tilde{f}(X),\nabla\log f(X)\rangle_{X}\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla\log f(X)\|_{X}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla\log\tilde{f}(X)\|_{X}^{2}\big]+2\,\mathbb{E}\big[\Delta\log\tilde{f}(X)\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla\log f(X)\|_{X}^{2}\big].

Notice that the last term does not depend on
\tilde{f}
, hence optimizing
\mathcal{L}
is equivalent to optimizing
\mathcal{J}
.

Next note that
\mathcal{J}(\tilde{f})
still depends on
f
, but only implicitly through the integral over the marginal distribution of
X
.
By estimating this integral via the natural Monte Carlo estimate built from the measurements
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, we are led to consider

\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\tilde{f})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\|\nabla\log\tilde{f}(X_{i})\|_{X_{i}}^{2}+2\Delta\log\tilde{f}(X_{i})\right),

which is an unbiased estimator of
\mathcal{J}(\tilde{f})
for each fixed
\tilde{f}
.
This is precisely the objective we defined in (
30
) in the main body.

B.3.2
Computing Riemannian Score Matching Objective

For general
\tilde{f}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
we cannot easily compute
\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\tilde{f})
, but it turns out that the specific form (
27
) of our candidate densities
\hat{f}_{n}
allows us to do this computation exactly.

First, we re-write the objective in terms of
\tilde{f}
instead of
\log\tilde{f}
:

\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\tilde{f})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\|\nabla\log\tilde{f}(X_{i})\|_{X_{i}}^{2}+2\Delta\log\tilde{f}(X_{i})\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\|\nabla\tilde{f}(X_{i})\|_{X_{i}}^{2}}{\tilde{f}(X_{i})}+\frac{2\Delta\tilde{f}(X_{i})}{\tilde{f}(X_{i})}\right),

As such, we need to evaluate derivatives of
\tilde{f}
of order up to 2.

Now consider
\tilde{f}=\hat{f}_{n}
.
We know that computing
\hat{f}_{n}
only requires computing sums of eigenfunctions of
\Delta
, and we showed in Subsection
B.2
that in concrete cases of interest one can also exactly compute gradients of sums of eigenfunctions of
\Delta
.
In order to compute the second derivative, we simply note that
\Delta
acts diagonally on the span of
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
, i.e., we have

\Delta_{x}K(x,y,\ell)=\sum_{j\leq\ell}\lambda_{j}\phi_{j}(x)\overline{\phi_{j}(y)},

hence

\Delta\hat{f}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\leq\ell}\lambda_{j}\phi_{j}(x)\overline{\phi_{j}(X_{i})}.

B.3.3
Cross-Validation

We perform
K
-fold cross-validation of the Riemannian score-matching objective in the usual way, namely by splitting the data into
K
folds and for each fold computing the density estimate on the resulting training set and evaluating the score-matching objective
\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\hat{f}_{n})
on the complementary test set.
In the applications of Section
5
we chose
K=5
.

We also include a penalty in our cross-validation procedure, in order to prevent over-fitting.
We follow the usual Akaike information criterion (AIC) where we penalize proportional to the effective dimension of the model, which in our setting corresponds to the number of eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator.
More precisely, we minimize

\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\hat{f}_{n})+\frac{2\ell}{n}

which encourages more parsimonious models.

Lastly, we comment on the hyperparameter ranges over which we search.
For
\ell
, we set its upper bound for
\ell
to be equal to the conservative oracle choice from Theorem
3.17
.
For
\rho
, we parameterize it in terms of a percentile of the positive values taken by the density
\hat{f}_{n}
, and we set its upper bound as the 10 percentile.
