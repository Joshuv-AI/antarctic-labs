 Modular Autonomous Experimentation for Biological Applications
 Diego I. Oyarzun, Aldair E. Gongora, and Dante Ricci
                                         23-ERD-043
Abstract
The Modular Autonomous Research System (MARS) was developed to address the pressing need
for faster, more reliable, and more adaptable scientific discovery. Traditional experimentation is
limited by manual labor, long cycle times, and fragmented data streams, which constrain the
ability to explore complex chemical and materials design spaces. To overcome these limitations,
we created an integrated, modular platform that combines laboratory robotics, diverse
measurement instruments, and a central data infrastructure with artificial intelligence–driven
decision-making. The system links liquid handling robots, robotic arms, and optical plate readers
into a closed loop where experiments are executed automatically, data is analyzed in real time,
and subsequent experimental conditions are adaptively chosen to maximize information gain.
Over the course of the project, MARS was validated on two primary test cases—spectroscopic
metal–ligand binding assays and peptide-directed mineralization—which highlighted the
system’s ability to handle uncertainty and variability in experimental measurements. To further
demonstrate modularity and extensibility, we also established additional testbeds in
electrochemistry for catalyst discovery and electrolyte formulation for advanced batteries. The
results show that MARS can reliably conduct autonomous campaigns with minimal human
intervention, adapt to distinct scientific domains, and provide a scalable model for future self-
driving laboratories. This work establishes new capabilities for modular, uncertainty-aware
automation and directly supports the need for advanced, data-driven research platforms capable
of accelerating discovery across a wide range of scientific and national security missions.
1.1 Background and Research Objectives
Scientific discovery in chemistry, materials science, and molecular biology is increasingly
constrained by the traditional “slow loop” of hypothesis → experiment → analysis → insight.
While individual tools—robotic liquid handlers, high-throughput instruments, machine learning,
and data infrastructure—are mature in many cases, their integration into self-driving laboratories
(SDLs) or autonomous experimentation platforms remains nascent. Recent reviews underscore
both the promise and the challenges: SDLs have the potential to compress discovery timelines by
orders of magnitude, but existing systems are often monolithic, brittle, or narrowly tailored to
one class of experiments (Tobias et al. 2025). A remaining barrier is the absence of standardized
interfaces and modular architectures, meaning that swapping or upgrading hardware or software
often requires rewriting control logic rather than plug-and-play reconfiguration (NIST 2025).
In this context, our central research question was: How can one build a flexible, modular
autonomous experimentation system that is robust to measurement uncertainty, adaptable
across multiple scientific domains, and capable of closed-loop decision making? The motivation
was to overcome the limitations of rigid systems, reduce human intervention, and accelerate
exploration of complex chemical and materials design spaces. From that, we derived several
concrete research objectives:

1. Design a modular hardware/software architecture so that instruments and robotic modules
 can be added, removed, or reconfigured easily without rewriting the entire control stack.
2. Develop an uncertainty-aware decision engine (or planning layer) to select experiments
 adaptively in the presence of noise, failures, or model uncertainty.
3. Integrate with multiple instrument modalities (e.g., spectroscopic binding assays, peptide
 mineralization, and electrochemistry and electrolyte mixing) to test generality.
4. Validate autonomous operation by running closed-loop campaigns with minimal human
 oversight and demonstrate error recovery, calibration, and fallback strategies.
5. Demonstrate extensibility beyond the first use cases, such as introducing new testbeds for
 catalyst screening or battery electrolyte formulation, to show that the system is not
 hardwired to one domain.
As the project progressed, ancillary objectives also emerged. For instance, it was necessary to
implement fallback heuristics when the predictive model was uncertain or failed outright, and to
develop inter-module calibration protocols and communication fault-recovery routines to
maintain system robustness. Another extension was designing meta-monitoring diagnostics that
track performance degradation over time (e.g., drift in sensors or actuator reliability) and trigger
corrective recalibration.
Overall, the original objectives were largely met. The modular architecture was successfully
demonstrated: robotic modules and measurement instruments could be swapped or
reconfigured with relatively low integration overhead. The uncertainty-aware decision engine
allowed the system to choose experiments that improved model prediction quality faster than
naive or random strategies. The two core experimental domains—spectroscopic metal–ligand
binding and peptide-directed mineralization—were executed in autonomous closed-loop
campaigns, including handling measurement noise or occasional failures. The fallback heuristics
and calibration routines proved essential in practice, enhancing resilience beyond initial
expectations. Moreover, by developing driver modules for electrochemistry and electrolyte-
mixing testbeds, we validated that the architecture is extensible to new domains. Not all
challenges have been resolved—scaling to very large networks of instruments, formalizing
standards across external platforms, and guaranteeing reliability at scale remain open areas for
future work (Tom et al. 2024; Vescovi et al. 2023; Seifrid et al. 2022).
1.2 Scientific Approach and Accomplishments

Our technical approach combined theoretical methods, experimental methods, and systems
engineering to design, implement, and validate the Modular Autonomous Research System
(MARS). On the theoretical side, we developed an uncertainty-aware Bayesian optimization (BO)
framework to guide experimental design. The algorithm was initially trained on a previously
acquired dataset, which made it domain-agnostic and portable across different scientific
applications. As a result, the same BO framework was applied not only to spectroscopic metal–
ligand binding assays, but also to peptide-directed mineralization studies and to a heat-transfer
problem involving triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) structures, where parameters such as
Reynolds and Prandtl numbers of two fluids were varied. This cross-domain generality

demonstrates that our optimization layer is not tied to one experimental modality but can
accelerate exploration wherever complex parameter spaces are present. Importantly, the BO
implementation was resilient to uncertainty, explicitly handling measurement variability and
enabling adaptive repetition of key conditions to prevent spurious convergence.

Experimentally, we integrated liquid handling robots, robotic arms, and optical microplate
readers into a closed-loop workflow coordinated by a central database and decision engine. The
primary campaigns focused on high-uncertainty problems such as spectroscopic metal–ligand
binding assays and peptide-directed mineralization. To validate throughput and robustness,
more than 600 microplate reader measurements were performed under automated control,
yielding statistically rich datasets and confirming reproducibility across replicates. To further
demonstrate modularity, the system was extended to electrochemistry for catalyst discovery and
to electrolyte formulation for advanced batteries.

A key innovation was the development of abstraction layers that map experimental ideas into
executable laboratory protocols and ultimately into physical experimental actions. These layers
separated high-level planning (“what to test”), mid-level protocol generation (“how to test it”),
and low-level device execution (“carry out pipetting, mixing, and reading”). This multi-layered
architecture allowed MARS to efficiently transform scientific questions into working
experimental results. Protocols were realistic and complex, combining both single-channel and
eight-channel pipettes across a wide range of volumes. This not only added realism to the
workflows but also increased the effective parameter space that could be explored, in contrast
to many existing autonomous systems that are constrained and require significant re-engineering
to expand their scope.

To ensure reproducibility and reliability, we implemented unit tests across hardware interfaces,
data pipelines, and optimization modules. These revealed areas for improvement such as
calibration drift in pipetting volumes and communication latency between devices. Addressing
these issues improved reproducibility, reduced variability, and enhanced robustness during long
autonomous runs.

The most significant accomplishments included: (1) demonstrating a fully autonomous closed-
loop workflow capable of running hundreds of experiments with minimal human oversight, (2)
showing that Bayesian optimization efficiently guided exploration of metal–binder parameter
spaces and extended seamlessly to peptides and TPMS fluid-flow optimization, (3) validating that
abstraction layers enabled realistic, complex liquid handling routines beyond the capabilities of
other systems, (4) proving resilience to noise and variability in experimental data, and (5)
establishing modular extensions into electrochemistry and electrolyte discovery. Collectively,
these outcomes fulfilled the project objectives and established a robust foundation for scalable,
cross-domain self-driving laboratories.

1.3 Mission Impact

This research significantly advances Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory’s (LLNL) mission by
delivering a new capability for autonomous experimentation that integrates robotics, artificial
intelligence, and data-driven decision-making into a modular and extensible framework. The
Modular Autonomous Research System (MARS) directly addresses longstanding bottlenecks in
experimental science by reducing human labor, increasing reproducibility, and accelerating the
pace of discovery across chemistry, materials science, and biotechnology. For LLNL, this creates
an enduring capability that can be applied to diverse programmatic needs, from the development
of advanced materials to high-throughput screening in chemical and biological systems. The work
also fostered new collaborations across disciplines and brought in staff expertise in machine
learning, robotics, and data infrastructure, expanding the Laboratory’s technical base in areas
central to its future.

The impact on Department of Energy (DOE) and National Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA)
missions is equally significant. MARS provides an adaptable platform for advancing science-based
autonomous experimentation systems through rapid exploration of material chemistries and
processing pathways, supporting mission agility in materials qualification and lifecycle analysis.
In addition, the system’s ability to handle uncertain and noisy measurements has clear
applications for energy security and climate resilience, including the discovery of improved
catalysts, electrolytes, and functional coatings. Beyond DOE/NNSA, this work aligns with the
missions of other federal agencies such as the National Science Foundation (NSF), where
autonomous and modular experimentation accelerates fundamental research, and the National
Institutes of Health (NIH), where adaptive, high-throughput assays can be applied to biomedical
discovery. By demonstrating a robust, generalizable platform that spans chemistry, biology, and
physics, LLNL is positioned as a leader in the emerging field of self-driving laboratories, shaping a
new paradigm of data-driven, uncertainty-aware experimental science that will influence both
federal research agendas and industrial practice.

1.4 Conclusion

The development of the Modular Autonomous Research System (MARS) establishes a foundation
for both continued research within Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory and potential
transfer to external partners. The immediate next step is to expand the system’s capabilities by
scaling to more complex experimental workflows, integrating additional robotic modules, and
extending the optimization engine to higher-dimensional parameter spaces. These advances will
allow MARS to tackle increasingly challenging problems in materials design, electrochemistry,
and biomolecular discovery.

There is strong potential for collaboration and technology transfer to both government and
industry. Within the DOE and NNSA ecosystem, MARS can be applied to mission-critical programs
such as accelerated qualification of new materials, high-throughput discovery of electrolytes and
catalysts for clean energy technologies, and adaptive testing strategies for advanced
manufacturing with autonomous experimentation systems. Beyond DOE/NNSA, the platform
aligns with priorities at the National Science Foundation, the National Institutes of Health, and
the Department of Defense, where autonomous, modular experimentation can reduce costs,
shorten timelines, and open new avenues for discovery. Industry partners in pharmaceuticals,
energy storage, and advanced manufacturing are also natural collaborators, given the growing
demand for automation that combines flexibility with intelligent decision-making.

Future work will focus on deepening collaborations with external laboratories, universities, and
industrial partners while seeking new support from competitive funding programs. The vision is
to evolve MARS into a broadly applicable “science factory” model—an adaptable, uncertainty-
aware platform that can be reconfigured to address diverse scientific and technological
challenges. In doing so, this work will extend LLNL’s leadership in autonomous experimentation
and ensure that the Laboratory remains at the forefront of national and global efforts to
accelerate discovery through artificial intelligence and robotics.
