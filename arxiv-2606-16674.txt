Effects on a metal that is proximately coupled to hyperbolic photon modes
Zhiyu Dong, Patrick A. Lee

Abstract
The hyperbolic mode (HM) refers to a polariton mode in a polar insulator where the dielectric function is negative in some direction of propagation. Within a frequency window the light occupies a greatly expanded region in momentum space. The HM in hexagonal Boron Nitride (hBN) has been under intense study and we consider placing a metal directly on top of hBN and ask whether its physical properties can be strongly affected. While the problem resembles superficially the electron phonon coupling problem, there are important differences. Due to the longitudinal nature of the HM mode the coupling is driven by time dependent charge fluctuations which results in a coupling that strongly increases with the energy difference of the initial and final states. We find a significant frequency and momentum dependence of the self energy which allows us to identify the dimensionless coupling constant λ 0 \lambda_{0} that controls this effect. There is a suppression of the quasi-particle weight but it turns out that the leading correction to the velocity renormalization is canceled. We compute the single particle spectral function which shows a side band that can be measured experimentally. The virtual exchange of HM leads to a repulsive interaction which is ineffective to leading order because of the energy dependence. We are motivated to seek pairing by going to second order. Unfortunately we find that the leading contributions exactly cancel. This cancellation is not an accident and we give an explanation of why this cancellation would take place.



I
Introduction

The notion that sub-wavelength cavity confinement of light can lead to enhanced light matter interaction has a long history, as seen in recent reviews
4
;
5
and references therein. We are particularly interested in the question of whether the confined light can affect the electronic properties of materials nearby. Much of the work on this topic has been theoretical but recently some experimental successes have emerged that have energized the field. For example, it has been shown that cavity confined light can affect the localization properties and therefore the quantum Hall effect in a proximate GaAs layer.
2
. Recently, Keren et al.
8
reported that by placing a thin slab of hBN (with thickness of 25-100 nm) on top of an organic superconductor (SC) the superconductivity is suppressed in a region just under the hBN, as detected by a reduction of the Meissner screening current. Inspired by this work, one of us studied the zero point fluctuation of the photon just outside the hBN, due to the presence of hyperbolic mode (HM) in hBN within a certain frequency window and considered whether this greatly enhanced zero point fluctuation can affect physical properties of a nearby material.
10
. As pointed out by Ashida et al.
3
, the use of HM has advantages that can overcome limitations of the use of micro-cavity or plasmonics effect due to the finite cavity size that is achievable as well as dissipation effects. Ref.
10
focused on the effect of HM on the self energy correction to a charged relativistic boson and applied the result to systems near the Mott transition. In this work we consider the effect of HM photons on the self energy and effective interaction in a Fermionic metal. The leading interaction is repulsive but is zero on the Fermi surface. This motivates us to seek pairing by going to second order, in the spirit of Kohn and Luttinger.
9
Unfortunately we find that the leading order exactly cancels. We give a description of our attempt and provide a reason for this cancellation.

A similar discussion pertains to the type I HM except that now it is the coefficient of the

In this paper we will work out the geometry where a thin metallic layer is placed between two hBN layers with a gap

Next we consider the effective interaction between electrons via the exchange of HM photons and describe our attempt to find pairing by going to second order. We note that the self energy correction as well as the possibility of pairing using polariton modes were recently discussed by Riolo et al.

\xi_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}+{{\boldsymbol{q}}}}-\xi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\simeq v_{F}q_{\perp}
, and

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\prime\prime}({{\boldsymbol{k}}},\omega>0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{C\alpha z}{2}\int dq_{\perp}dq_{\parallel}\,\frac{(v_{F}q_{\perp})^{2}}{q\cosh^{2}(qz)}

\displaystyle\quad\times\Theta(q_{\perp})\delta(\omega-v_{F}q_{\perp}-\bar{\omega}).

(23)

The delta function fixes
q_{\perp}=(\omega-\bar{\omega})/v_{F}
, and the Fermi factor gives
\Theta(\omega-\bar{\omega})
. Defining
\nu=\omega/\bar{\omega}
, one obtains

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\prime\prime}({{\boldsymbol{k}}},\omega>0)

\displaystyle=-C\alpha^{2}\bar{\omega}\,\left(\frac{\nu-1}{\alpha}\right)^{2}\mathcal{J}\!\left(\frac{\nu-1}{\alpha}\right)\Theta(\nu-1),

(24)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\!\left(\frac{\nu-1}{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{z\,dq_{\parallel}}{\sqrt{z^{2}q_{\parallel}^{2}+(\nu-1)^{2}/\alpha^{2}}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}\!\left[\sqrt{z^{2}q_{\parallel}^{2}+(\nu-1)^{2}/\alpha^{2}}\right]}.

(25)

For
|\nu-1|/\alpha\ll 1
,
\mathcal{J}[(\nu-1)/\alpha]\simeq\ln[\alpha/|\nu-1|]
, while for
|\nu-1|/\alpha\gg 1
,
\mathcal{J}[(\nu-1)/\alpha]\simeq 2\sqrt{\pi\alpha/|\nu-1|}\exp[-2|\nu-1|/\alpha]
, so it is exponentially small in
|\nu-1|/\alpha
.

With this
\Sigma^{\prime\prime}
we calculate the spectral function. Results are shown in Fig.
1
. The continuum sideband starts above
\bar{\omega}
and spreads over a frequency range
\sim v_{F}/z=\alpha\bar{\omega}
. We can verify that in this plot the width is given by
\approx\alpha
. For
\alpha>1
, the area under the sideband is given by
1-Z=C\alpha/(1+C\alpha)
. This fixes the peak height to be proportional to
\bar{\omega}
which is the scaling factor of the vertical axis. This explains why the peak height is slowly varying in Fig.
1
a. For
\alpha<1
,
1-Z
departs from
C\alpha/(1+C\alpha)
(see discussion after Eq.
15
) and becomes progressively smaller. This explains the decrease of the peak height and the area in Fig.
1
b. The aim of these calculations is to give an idea of how the sideband evolves with parameter changes such as
z
. For simplicity we have ignored the dispersion of the mode. This is valid for
\alpha>1
, but the peak is expected to be broadened by
\omega_{L}-\omega_{T}
. This approximation breaks down for the case
\alpha<1
shown in Fig.
1
b. There the dispersion will further smear out the already weak peak, making detection difficult.

We remind the reader that these calculations are done in a geometry where the metal is encapsulated on both sides by HM materials. To perform ARPES or STS we need an open surface. In this case the
\cosh^{-2}(qz)
factor in Eq. (
22
) is replaced by
e^{-2qz}
. As a good approximation, we can redefine the parameter
\alpha
to be
v_{F}/(2z\bar{\omega})
and multiply the vertical axis in Fig.
1
by
1/2
.

As mentioned earlier, the HM mode side-band was recently observed in an empty band in a semiconductor placed on top of hBN.
7
. These experiments should be feasible in a metal. The possibility of tuning
z
, and
\bar{\omega}
by using different HM materials, should allow us to explore the range of behavior shown in Fig.
1
. This topic will be further discussed in the concluding section.

IV
The search for pairing

In this section we describe our attempt to use the exchange of HM mode to induce superconductivity. While the effort was not successful, the procedure and the reason for the failure may be of interest to the community. In the past, the vector nature of the
{\boldsymbol{A}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{j}}
coupling has led to the proposal of Amperean pairing between electrons moving in the same direction to take advantage of the Amperean attraction.
12
;
11
. Amperean pairing was proposed for the case of cavity confinement
15
but was criticized in ref.
1
which pointed out that the interaction is not via magnetic field fluctuations, as mentioned earlier. Indeed, finite momentum pairing between electrons with parallel velocities is not favorable: the coupling vanishes for pairs of electrons on the Fermi surface as seen in Eq.
8
and one is forced to pair highly excited states. We have found that an artificially strong coupling is needed to create an instability for the onset of this state. Instead, we turn our attention to seek pairing in the traditional zero momentum pairing channel and we try to take advantage of the repulsive interaction that has an unusual dependence on the electron energy
\xi
in that it grows monotonically as
\xi^{2}
.

To start, we project the HM-mediated interaction to the Cooper channel
({{\boldsymbol{p}}},-{{\boldsymbol{p}}})\to({{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\prime},-{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\prime})
. In this section
\xi=\xi_{{{\boldsymbol{p}}}}
and
\xi^{\prime}=\xi_{{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\prime}}
denote the incoming and outgoing single-particle energies measured from the Fermi surface. The dimensional Cooper-channel interaction is

\displaystyle V_{\rm pair}

\displaystyle(\xi,\xi^{\prime};i\omega_{n}-i\omega_{m},q)=e^{2}\frac{\eta^{2}}{2\sqrt{2}\omega_{L}\omega_{T}}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{T}}{\omega_{L}}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{(\xi-\xi^{\prime})^{2}}{q\cosh^{2}(qz)}\frac{1/\epsilon_{\infty}}{(\omega_{n}-\omega_{m})^{2}+\bar{\omega}^{2}}.

(26)

where the last term is the
A
field propagator with Matsubara frequency.

Here,
V_{\rm pair}>0
, so the interaction is repulsive. The factor
(\xi-\xi^{\prime})^{2}
comes from the longitudinal current vertices derived above; in the Cooper channel the two electrons carry opposite momenta, so the two current factors have opposite signs. This is the origin of the repulsive sign in Eq. (
26
). The other factors come from integration over
\kappa
and are the same as in the self energy calculations in the last section.

The presence of
\xi-\xi^{\prime}
factor at each vertex implies that the first-order irreducible pairing interaction (Fig.
2
a)) vanishes for electrons on Fermi surface
\xi=\xi^{\prime}=0
. Therefore, below we look into the second-order diagrams.

Four irreducible second-order processes, like in Kohn-Luttinger theory, are illustrated in Fig.
2
(b). Usually, with repulsion, the bubble diagram is attractive whereas the vertex corrections are repulsive. For contact interaction, these diagrams cancel leaving the cross diagram which is overall repulsive. Kohn and Luttinger managed to show that for a high enough angular momentum channel the logarithmic singularity gives rise to an attractive channel which dominates the first order term, but the resulting
T_{c}
is very small. In contrast, in our case the first order term automatically vanishes. Furthermore three of the four diagrams involving bubble and vertex correction all vanish on the Fermi surface, and the only non-vanishing one is the cross diagram.
In addition to this diagram, we have two extra diagrams that also contribute at the second order of
\lambda_{0}
to effective interaction on Fermi surface: one from integrating out the high-energy Cooper ladder, which is shown in Fig.
2
c), the other from the
e^{2}A^{2}/2m
vertex that arises from Peierls substitution
-\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2m}\rightarrow\frac{(i\nabla+eA)^{2}}{2m}
as shown in Fig
2
d). Below we consider their total contribution to the effective interaction of a Cooper pair on the Fermi surface. We focus on the processes with initial and final Cooper pair
{\boldsymbol{p}}
and
{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}
both on the Fermi surface, and with two HM carrying momentum
{\boldsymbol{q}}
and
-{\boldsymbol{q}}-{\boldsymbol{p}}+{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}
.
The effective interaction generated through cross diagram is

\displaystyle V_{\rm pair;{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}}^{(2,\rm cross)}({\boldsymbol{q}})\sim\mathcal{D}(\nu)\mathcal{D}(-\nu-\omega_{n}+\omega_{n^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{\xi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}^{\,2}\,\xi_{-{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}^{\,2}/\left[q^{2}(-q-p+p^{\prime})^{2}\right]}{\left(i(\omega_{n}+\nu)-\xi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}\right)\left[i(-\omega_{n}^{\prime}+\nu)-\xi_{-{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}\right]},

(27)

where
\mathcal{D}(\Omega)=\lambda_{0}\bar{\omega}/[\Lambda(\Omega^{2}+\bar{\omega}^{2})]
. By power-counting, we see that after integrating over
q
this will yield a UV divergent interaction
\nu_{0}\int\frac{d^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}V^{(2,\rm irr)}_{\rm pair;{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}}(q)\sim O(\lambda_{0}^{2}\frac{\Lambda}{\bar{\omega}})
.

The effective interaction generated through the ladder diagram shown in Fig.
2
c is given by

\displaystyle V_{\rm pair;{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}}^{(2,\rm ladder)}({\boldsymbol{q}})

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{D}(\nu)\mathcal{D}(-\nu-\omega_{n}+\omega_{n^{\prime}})\frac{\xi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}^{\,4}\,/\left[q^{2}(-q-p+p^{\prime})^{2}\right]}{(\omega_{n}+\nu)^{2}+\xi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}^{2}},

(28)

The effective interaction generated through the double-HM vertex diagram (Fig.
2
d)) is

\displaystyle V_{\rm pair;{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}}^{(2,A^{2})}({\boldsymbol{q}})\sim-\frac{2}{(2m)^{2}}\mathcal{D}(\nu)\mathcal{D}(-\nu-\omega_{n}+\omega_{n^{\prime}}),

(29)

Here the factor of
2
arises from swapping the two HM lines’ endpoints. By power counting after ignoring the frequency term in the energy denominator, we can see the three diagrams have similar magnitude for a parabolic band.
Below we show that the three contributions above cancel each other. We consider two regimes:
q_{z}>k_{F}
and
q_{z}<k_{F}
.

First let us consider the case
q_{z}>k_{F}
. This is the regime of small Fermi surface area such that
\epsilon_{F}\ll\Lambda
. In this case the HM mode can scatter an initial state near the Fermi surface to an excited state
{\boldsymbol{p}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}
with a large momentum of order
q_{z}
, so that
{\boldsymbol{p}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}
is typically outside the Fermi surface, i.e.
\xi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}
is positive. By the same reasoning,
\xi_{-{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}
is also likely to be positive, because the volume of the Fermi sea is relatively small compared with the entire accessible phase space. In Eq.
27
the frequencies
\omega_{n}
etc. in the denominator can be ignored because they are limited by the photon propagator to be of order
\bar{\omega}
which is small compared with electronic energy. As a result the denominator is approximated by
\xi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}\xi_{-{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}
which is positive. In this way
V_{\rm pair;{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}^{(2,\rm cross)}
is attractive and contributes to pairing. The same sign and result also applies to ladder diagram
V_{\rm pair;{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}^{(2,\rm ladder)}
.

\displaystyle V_{\rm pair;{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}}^{(2,\rm cross)}({\boldsymbol{q}}\gg k_{F})\simeq V_{\rm pair;{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}}^{(2,\rm ladder)}({\boldsymbol{q}}\gg k_{F})

\displaystyle\simeq\mathcal{D}(\nu)\mathcal{D}(-\nu-\omega_{n}+\omega_{n^{\prime}})\xi_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}^{\,2}/q^{4}

(30)

While these two diagrams give an attractive contribution, they are canceled by the diagram involving the
A^{2}
vertex shown in Fig.
2
d) after using the fact that
\xi_{q}^{2}/q^{4}=\frac{1}{(2m)^{2}}
for parabolic band. We conclude that the sum of the leading large-
q
contribution from all three diagrams cancels.

On the other hand, in the second regime
q_{z}<k_{F}
the three types of processes behave completely differently. The intermediate state momentum
{\boldsymbol{p}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}
must be close to both

{\boldsymbol{p}}
and
-{\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}}
, and the latter two must be close to each other. As a result
{\boldsymbol{p}}+{\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}}
is near
2k_{F}
. If ignoring Fermi surface curvature, when
{\boldsymbol{p}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}
is outside the Fermi surface,
-{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}+{\boldsymbol{q}}
is likely to be inside. In this case the denominator in Eq.
27
is negative so that the cross diagram gives rise to repulsion. In fact this repulsion almost cancels the contribution from ladder diagram,
1
1

1

We thank Taige Wang for pointing out this cancellation, which was overlooked in our first draft.
and the residual is exactly canceled by the
A^{2}
contribution. To see this, we consider the sum of the ladder and cross diagrams:

\displaystyle V_{\rm pair;{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}}^{(2,\rm cross)}({\boldsymbol{q}})+V_{\rm pair;{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}}^{(2,\rm ladder)}({\boldsymbol{q}})

\displaystyle\propto\xi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}(\xi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}+\xi_{-{\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}+{\boldsymbol{q}}})/q^{4}=\left(\frac{{\boldsymbol{p}}\cdot q}{m}+\frac{q^{2}}{2m}\right)\frac{2q^{2}}{2m}/q^{4}

(31)

After angular averaging over
{\boldsymbol{p}}
, the first term vanishes, so the last line becomes
\frac{2}{(2m)^{2}}
, which exactly cancels with the
A^{2}
vertex contribution in Eq.(
29
).

This cancellation of leading contribution of HM-mediated interaction is not accidental. The physics reason is that the HM couples to electron through its longitudinal component of A, which can be written as
{\boldsymbol{A}}=\nabla\chi({\boldsymbol{r}},t)
. In the static limit, this becomes a pure gauge and has no impact on electrons. However, in our analysis above, to track the leading contribution, we have dropped the frequency dependence in the energy denominator, which amounts to focusing on the static (zero-frequency) component of the HM propagator. Nonzero contribution due to dynamics can arise from the frequency dependence that we dropped in the analysis above, which should contain at least one more power of
\bar{\omega}/\Lambda
.

V
Discussion

In this paper we calculated the frequency and momentum dependence of the self energy and use these results to
identify the dimensionless coupling constant
\lambda_{0}
that characterizes this process. It turns out that the correction to the Fermi velocity cancels in leading order in
\Lambda/\bar{\omega}
. Sub-leading correction may give a small correction. The correction to the
Z
factor of the quasi-particle weight is given by
\lambda_{0}
. The missing spectral weight goes to a side band. We calculated this side band which should be experimentally measurable by ARPES. The case of the empty band was reported by Gatti et al.
7
. Below we show some estimates of the expected magnitude for
\lambda_{0}
.
Note that
\lambda_{0}
is proportional to

\frac{1}{z}
and is independent of any other material parameters of the metal such as
v_{F}
and
k_{F}
. It is inversely proportional to the average HM frequency and can be greatly enhanced in materials with low
\bar{\omega}
as we shall see in the example below.

We first fix the geometric parameters. A realistic hBN-electron-layer separation is
z=6\,\text{\AA}
. We take the dielectric constant of hBN to be
\epsilon_{\infty}/\epsilon_{0}=4
. The upper HM mode in hBN is type II and has an energy of
\bar{\omega}=170\,{\rm meV}
, with
\eta^{2}/(\omega_{L}\omega_{T})=0.3
and
\sqrt{\omega_{T}/\omega_{L}}=0.92
. This yields a coupling strength
\lambda_{0}^{\rm II}\simeq
0.344. Carrying out the same estimate for the lower type I HM mode in hBN, which has energy
\bar{\omega}=96\,{\rm meV}
, with
\eta^{2}/(\omega_{L}\omega_{T})=0.1
and
\sqrt{\omega_{T}/\omega_{L}}=0.97
, we find
\lambda_{0}^{\rm I}\simeq

0.214. The contributions from the two modes to self energy add directly, therefore the total effective
\lambda_{0}
is given by
\lambda_{0}^{\rm total}\simeq

0.559. This result is for a metal encapsulated by hBN on both sides. For a single sided surface, we expect the coupling strength to be smaller, but still significant.

We repeat the same estimate for Bi
2
Se
3
, using the dielectric-function parameters of Ref.
16
. For the type-II HM in Bi
2
Se
3
, the parameters are
\epsilon_{\perp,\infty}=29
,
\omega_{T}=64~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}=7.94~\mathrm{meV}
, and
\omega_{L}\simeq 146~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}=18.1~\mathrm{meV}
. Here
\omega_{L}
is the zero of the in-plane dielectric function used in the same one-oscillator estimate as above; the actual type-II hyperbolic window is cut off near
135~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}
by the
z
-axis phonon. These parameters give
\eta^{2}/(\omega_{L}\omega_{T})\simeq 1.84
and
\sqrt{\omega_{T}/\omega_{L}}\simeq 0.66
. The mode frequency is about a factor 10 smaller than in hBN. Partly due to this small number we need to take a larger interface distance
z=30~\text{\AA}
to keep the coupling strength in the controllable regime. Even with this large distance we find
\lambda_{0}^{\rm II}\simeq 0.90
. For the type-I HM in Bi
2
Se
3
, the
\eta
parameter is very small and the coupling is negligible. The strength of the coupling to the type-II HM in Bi
2
Se
3
suggests that this is a good material to explore strong-coupling effects experimentally in a regime not accessible by theory. It also gives a strong coupling for relatively large
z
of a few tens of angstroms. It may be possible to study the
z
dependence by inserting insulating spacer layers.

Appendix A
velocity renormalization

In this appendix we show that the velocity renormalization due to HM vanishes for a parabolic band.
We start by analyzing
F_{k}=\left.d\Sigma^{\prime}(\mathbf{k},0)/d\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\right|_{k=k_{F}}
. At zero temperature and in the dominant high-energy region
\lvert\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}}\rvert\gg\bar{\omega}
, the electronic part of the static self-energy becomes

\frac{\Theta(\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}})}{-\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}}-\omega_{\mathbf{q}\kappa}}+\frac{\Theta(-\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}})}{-\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}}+\omega_{\mathbf{q}\kappa}}\simeq-\frac{1}{\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}}}.

(32)

The static self-energy can therefore be written as

\Sigma^{\prime}(\mathbf{k},0)\simeq-\frac{e^{2}}{2\epsilon_{\infty}}\int\frac{d^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\kappa}{2\pi}\frac{f_{\mathbf{q}\kappa}^{2}}{\omega_{\mathbf{q}\kappa}q^{2}\cosh^{2}(qz)}\frac{\left(\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\right)^{2}}{\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}}}.

(33)

The
\xi_{k}
-derivative comes from
\frac{\left(\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\right)^{2}}{\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}}}
, so we focus on this quantity.
Taking the derivative of this quantity with respect to
\xi_{k}
while noting that both
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}
and
\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}}
vary when the external momentum is changed, we find

\left.\frac{d}{d\xi_{\mathbf{k}}}\left[-\frac{\left(\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\right)^{2}}{\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}}}\right]\right|_{\xi_{\mathbf{k}}=0}=2-\left.\frac{d\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}}}{d\xi_{\mathbf{k}}}\right|_{k=k_{F}}.

(34)

For the quadratic dispersion used here,

\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}}=\xi_{\mathbf{k}}+q^{2}/(2m)+(kq/m)\cos\phi
, where
\phi
is the angle between
{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
and
{{\boldsymbol{q}}}
. Therefore,

\left.\frac{d\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}}}{d\xi_{\mathbf{k}}}\right|_{k=k_{F}}=\frac{d\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}}/dk}{d\xi_{\mathbf{k}}/dk}=1+\frac{q}{k_{F}}\cos\phi.

(35)

As the remaining q-dependence in
\Sigma^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{k}},0)
is isotropic, the
\cos\phi
term vanishes after integrating over
q
. Therefore,

\left\langle\frac{d}{d\xi_{\mathbf{k}}}\left[-\frac{\left(\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\right)^{2}}{\xi_{\mathbf{k+q}}}\right]\Bigg|_{\xi_{\mathbf{k}}=0}\right\rangle_{\phi}=1.

(36)

Using the same
q
and
\kappa
integrations that define
\lambda_{0}
, we obtain

F_{k}=\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{\infty}\bar{\omega}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dq}{\cosh^{2}(qz)}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\frac{\eta^{2}}{\omega_{T}\omega_{L}}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{T}}{\omega_{L}}}=\lambda_{0}.

(37)

Together with
F_{\omega}=-\lambda_{0}
, this gives the quasiparticle residue
Z=1/(1-F_{\omega})=1/(1+\lambda_{0})
. The renormalized Fermi velocity is consequently

\frac{v_{F}^{\prime}}{v_{F}}=Z(1+F_{k})=\frac{1+\lambda_{0}}{1+\lambda_{0}}=1.

(38)

Thus, within the leading high-energy approximation, the frequency and momentum contributions cancel in the Fermi velocity. Any residual velocity renormalization is determined by the sub-leading terms in
\bar{\omega}/\Lambda_{z}
.

Appendix B
Angular average near Fermi surface

In the main text, we studied the scattering of a Cooper pair from
\xi
near the Fermi surface to some other energy
\xi^{\prime}
through exchanging HM, with a momentum transfer
q
. In this appendix, our goal is to calculate the projection of the scattering amplitude
\sim 1/(q\cosh^{2}(qz))
on the pairing channel of interest (i.e. s-wave), which is essentially to calculate the following angular average:

I_{\theta}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\frac{1}{q\cosh^{2}(qz)/k_{F}}.

(39)

The momentum transfer can be expressed in terms of the scattering angle and the initial and final energies as

q=2k_{F}\sqrt{\sin^{2}(\theta/2)+a^{2}},\qquad a=\frac{|\xi^{\prime}-\xi|}{2v_{F}k_{F}}.

(40)

We will assume
\xi/v_{F}k_{F}
and
\xi^{\prime}/v_{F}k_{F}
to be small. We shall see from the derivation below that, for
z>1/k_{F}
, the relevant excitation energies are indeed less than the Fermi energy.

Substituting this expression for
q
into
I_{\theta}
, we find

I_{\theta}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\sin^{2}(\theta/2)+a^{2}}\cosh^{2}\!\left[2k_{F}z\sqrt{\sin^{2}(\theta/2)+a^{2}}\right]}.

(41)

Using the symmetry of the integrand and changing variable to
x=\sin(\theta/2)
, this becomes

I_{\theta}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}dx\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^{2}+a^{2}}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\cosh^{2}\!\left[2k_{F}z\sqrt{x^{2}+a^{2}}\right]}.

(42)

For
k_{F}z\gtrsim 1
, the factor
\cosh^{-2}[2k_{F}z\sqrt{x^{2}+a^{2}}]
restricts the main contribution to the small-
x
region. Therefore we can set
\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\simeq 1
. With

u=2k_{F}zx,\qquad\beta=2k_{F}za=\frac{z|\xi^{\prime}-\xi|}{v_{F}},

we obtain

\displaystyle I_{\theta}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2k_{F}z}du\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{u^{2}+\beta^{2}}\cosh^{2}\!\sqrt{u^{2}+\beta^{2}}}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{u^{2}+\beta^{2}}\cosh^{2}\!\sqrt{u^{2}+\beta^{2}}}.

(43)

In the last step, we have extended the upper limit to infinity, since the integrand is already strongly suppressed once
u
is of order one. We therefore define

\mathcal{F}(\beta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{u^{2}+\beta^{2}}\cosh^{2}\!\sqrt{u^{2}+\beta^{2}}},\qquad I_{\theta}\simeq\frac{1}{\pi}\mathcal{F}(\beta).

(44)

The function
\mathcal{F}(\beta)
gives the angular cutoff produced by the finite distance
z
. When
\beta\ll 1
, the logarithmic contribution comes from the region
\beta\ll u\ll 1
, where the integrand is approximately
1/u
. Thus

\mathcal{F}(\beta)\simeq\int_{\beta}^{1}\frac{du}{u}=\ln\frac{1}{\beta},\qquad\beta\ll 1.

(45)

When
\beta\gtrsim 1
, the argument of the
\cosh
is already of order one or larger at the lower limit, so the kernel is rapidly suppressed. Thus
\mathcal{F}(\beta)
is a logarithmic kernel cut off at
\beta\sim 1
.

For the analytical estimate in the main text, we replace this smooth cutoff by a sharp cutoff with the same total weight. This weight is

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\,\mathcal{F}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{u^{2}+\beta^{2}}\cosh^{2}\!\sqrt{u^{2}+\beta^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}d\varphi\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\,\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}r}=\frac{\pi}{2},

(46)

where in the second line we used polar coordinates in the
(u,\beta)
plane. Therefore the area-preserving sharp-cutoff approximation is

\mathcal{F}(\beta)\approx\frac{\pi}{2}\Theta(1-\beta).

(47)

Since
\beta=\frac{z|\xi^{\prime}-\xi|}{v_{F}},
the condition
\beta<1
is equivalent to
|\xi^{\prime}-\xi|<\frac{v_{F}}{z}.
Thus the Theta function corresponds to an energy cutoff at
\Lambda=\frac{v_{F}}{z}
. Collecting everything, we conclude that

\left\langle\frac{1}{q\cosh^{2}(qz)}\right\rangle_{\theta}\approx\frac{1}{2k_{F}}\Theta\!\left(\Lambda-|\xi^{\prime}-\xi|\right),\quad\Lambda=\frac{v_{F}}{z}.

(48)

Appendix C
Band-edge mass renormalization in a semiconducting TMD/hBN interface

Recent ARPES measurements on monolayer WS
2
on insulating hBN report an hBN-related side band near the local
\Gamma
-valley valence-band maximum, while the observed band-edge mass renormalization is very small
7
. In this appendix, we show that the longitudinal HM coupling used in our paper is not in conflict with this observation. A closely related problem was treated in ref.
6
using the same
A.j
coupling. They consider a surface mode at an interface rather than the HM mode, but the problem is very similar. Ref.
6
treated the case where the virtually excited electronic states has much less energy than the mode frequency and can be ignored in the energy denominator. (They refer to this as the dipole approximation.) In this appendix we relax this approximation. Where our work overlap, we are in agreement. A major result is that we find a cancellation between the
Z
factor coming from
\frac{\partial\Sigma^{\prime}({{\boldsymbol{k}}}=0,\omega)}{\partial\omega}
and the
k^{2}
dependence of the self energy for the mass renormalization. As a result the mass renormalization is very small. Interestingly the same cancellation is found in Ref.

7
which considers the Coulomb coupling to the lattice dipole instead of the
A.j
coupling. Due to the large value of the mode frequency in hBN, the experiment is in the regime where the dipole approximation is appropriate, but we think it is useful to work out the more general case which may be applicable to systems with smaller mode frequencies.

Consider a filled valence band near a local maximum at
\Gamma
like in Ref.
7
,

\xi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}=E_{\Gamma}-\frac{k^{2}}{2m},\qquad\xi_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}+{{\boldsymbol{q}}}}-\xi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}=-\left(\frac{q^{2}}{2m}+\frac{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\cdot{{\boldsymbol{q}}}}{m}\right),

(49)

where
E_{\Gamma}
is the local band-edge energy. The longitudinal HM vertex is the same as Eq. (
8
),

g_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},{{\boldsymbol{q}}}\kappa}=\frac{e}{\sqrt{2\epsilon_{\infty}\omega_{{{\boldsymbol{q}}}\kappa}}}\frac{\xi_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}+{{\boldsymbol{q}}}}-\xi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}{q}f({\boldsymbol{q}},\kappa,z).

(50)

At zero temperature the valence band is filled, and the retarded self-energy contribution relevant for the ARPES hole side is

\Sigma^{R}({{\boldsymbol{k}}},\omega)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{\kappa}\int\frac{d^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{|g_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},{{\boldsymbol{q}}}\kappa}|^{2}}{\omega-\xi_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}+{{\boldsymbol{q}}}}+\omega_{{{\boldsymbol{q}}}\kappa}+i0^{+}}.

(51)

The real part of Eq. (
51
) is therefore

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\prime}({{\boldsymbol{k}}},\omega)

\displaystyle=2\pi z\lambda_{0}\bar{\omega}\int\frac{d^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{1}{q\cosh^{2}(qz)}\frac{(\xi_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}+{{\boldsymbol{q}}}}-\xi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}})^{2}}{\bar{\omega}\left(\omega-\xi_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}+{{\boldsymbol{q}}}}+\bar{\omega}\right)},

(52)

Here
\lambda_{0}
is the same coupling constant defined in Eq. (
15
). The coefficient in Eq. (
52
) is obtained by solving Eq. (
15
) for the microscopic prefactor,

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{\infty}}\frac{\pi\eta^{2}}{\sqrt{2}\,\omega_{L}\omega_{T}}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{T}}{\omega_{L}}}

\displaystyle=2\pi z\lambda_{0}\bar{\omega}.

(53)

The constant
\Sigma^{\prime}(0,E_{\Gamma})
only shifts the local band edge and is absorbed into
E_{\Gamma}
below.

Expanding the Green’s function near the band edge gives

G^{R}({{\boldsymbol{k}}},\omega)\simeq\frac{Z}{\omega-E_{\Gamma}-Z\left[\xi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}-E_{\Gamma}+\Sigma^{\prime}({{\boldsymbol{k}}},E_{\Gamma})\right]+i0^{+}},

(54)

where we have defined the quasiparticle residue
Z=1/(1+\lambda_{\Gamma})
, and

\lambda_{\Gamma}=-\left.\frac{\partial\Sigma^{\prime}({{\boldsymbol{k}}}=0,\omega)}{\partial\omega}\right|_{\omega=E_{\Gamma}}
.

The quasiparticle pole is

E_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}=E_{\Gamma}+Z\left[\xi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}-E_{\Gamma}+\Sigma^{\prime}({{\boldsymbol{k}}},E_{\Gamma})\right]+O(k^{4}).

(55)

For a valence-band maximum, the positive effective mass is defined by

E_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}=E_{\Gamma}-k^{2}/(2m^{*})+O(k^{4})
. Taking the downward curvature of Eq. (
55
) gives

\frac{1}{m^{*}}=Z\left[\frac{1}{m}-\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\Sigma^{\prime}({{\boldsymbol{k}}},\omega)}{\partial k_{x}^{2}}\right|_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}=0,\omega=E_{\Gamma}}\right].

(56)

It is useful to define

\rho_{\Gamma}=-m\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\Sigma^{\prime}({{\boldsymbol{k}}},\omega)}{\partial k_{x}^{2}}\right|_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}=0,\omega=E_{\Gamma}}.

(57)

Then

\frac{m^{*}}{m}=\frac{1+\lambda_{\Gamma}}{1+\rho_{\Gamma}}.

(58)

The frequency derivative gives

\displaystyle\lambda_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=2\pi z\lambda_{0}\bar{\omega}\int\frac{d^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{1}{q\cosh^{2}(qz)}\frac{1}{\bar{\omega}}\frac{(q^{2}/2m)^{2}}{(q^{2}/2m+\bar{\omega})^{2}}.

(59)

The curvature derivative has an additional contribution from the explicit
{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
dependence of the longitudinal vertex. Expanding Eq. (
52
) to second order in
k_{x}
, doing the angular average, and then setting
{{\boldsymbol{k}}}=0
gives

\displaystyle\rho_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=2\pi z\lambda_{0}\bar{\omega}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dq}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(qz)}\frac{1}{\bar{\omega}}\left[\frac{(q^{2}/2m)^{2}}{(q^{2}/2m+\bar{\omega})^{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 62.59596pt\left.-\frac{2(q^{2}/2m)\bar{\omega}^{2}}{(q^{2}/2m+\bar{\omega})^{3}}\right]=\lambda_{\Gamma}-\beta_{\Gamma},

(60)

where

\displaystyle\beta_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=2\pi z\lambda_{0}\bar{\omega}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dq}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(qz)}\frac{1}{\bar{\omega}}\frac{2(q^{2}/2m)\bar{\omega}^{2}}{(q^{2}/2m+\bar{\omega})^{3}}.

(61)

Therefore

\frac{m^{*}}{m}-1=\frac{1+\lambda_{\Gamma}}{1+\lambda_{\Gamma}-\beta_{\Gamma}}-1=\frac{\beta_{\Gamma}}{1+\lambda_{\Gamma}-\beta_{\Gamma}}\simeq\beta_{\Gamma}.

(62)

From Eq. (
62
) we see that the mass correction is not controlled directly by
\lambda_{\Gamma}
, but by
\beta_{\Gamma}
, which is the residual part that does not cancel between the frequency dependence and the momentum dependence of the self-energy.

We now estimate both quantities in the limit
\bar{\omega}\ll 1/(2mz^{2})
. For
\lambda_{\Gamma}
, we find Eq. (
59
) is identical to frequency renormalization calculated in the main text, therefore

\lambda_{\Gamma}=\lambda_{0}.

(63)

For
\beta_{\Gamma}
, the integral in Eq. (
61
) is dominated by
q\sim\sqrt{2m\bar{\omega}}
, so
\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(qz)}\simeq 1
in the same limit. This gives

\displaystyle\beta_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\simeq 2\pi z\lambda_{0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dq}{2\pi}\frac{2(q^{2}/2m)\bar{\omega}^{2}}{(q^{2}/2m+\bar{\omega})^{3}}

\displaystyle=2\pi z\lambda_{0}\frac{\sqrt{2m\bar{\omega}}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,\frac{2x^{2}}{(1+x^{2})^{3}}

\displaystyle=2\pi z\lambda_{0}\frac{\sqrt{2m\bar{\omega}}}{2\pi}\frac{\pi}{8}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{4\sqrt{2}}\lambda_{0}z\sqrt{m\bar{\omega}},\quad\bar{\omega}\ll\frac{1}{2mz^{2}}

(64)

Note that according to Eq.(
53
) the product
z\lambda_{0}
is independent of
z
.
Combining Eqs. (
62
), (
63
), and (
64
) gives the final mass correction

\frac{m^{*}}{m}-1\simeq\frac{\pi}{4\sqrt{2}}z\sqrt{m\bar{\omega}}\,\lambda_{0},\quad\bar{\omega}\ll\frac{1}{2mz^{2}}

(65)

Equivalently, defining the electronic scale
\Lambda=1/(2mz^{2})
, the right-hand side of Eq. (
65
) can be written as
(\pi/8)\sqrt{\bar{\omega}/\Lambda}\,\lambda_{0}
. This form makes explicit that the mass correction is smaller than
\lambda_{0}
by a factor of
\sqrt{\bar{\omega}/\Lambda}
when
\bar{\omega}\ll\Lambda
.
In sum, we find that the mass renormalization produced by this mechanism is small. The reason is that the frequency dependence of the self-energy gives the usual
\lambda_{\Gamma}
contribution, but the momentum dependence of the self-energy gives an almost identical contribution to the band curvature. These two pieces cancel in the effective mass. Interestingly, the same cancellation happened in the case treated in ref.
7
which considered the usual Coulomb coupling to the optical polar phonon.
In reality both couplings are present and we conclude that our longitudinal HM mode scattering mechanism is consistent with the small mass enhancement observed in WS
2
/hBN.

In the discussion starting from Eq.(
63
), we are treating the regime of
\bar{\omega}\ll\Lambda
. We now note that the opposite regime can be realized in systems with larger mass, such as in itinerant electrons on hBN where
m\sim m_{e}
. In this system,
the dipole approximation is valid due to the large HM frequency. In this case
\bar{\omega}/\Lambda
is large and
\beta_{\Gamma}
is the leading order term, while
\lambda_{\Gamma}
and
\rho_{\Gamma}
are sub-leading with higher power of
\Lambda/\bar{\omega}
. Therefore we are in agreement with ref.
6
which did not treat
\lambda_{\Gamma}
.

The same experiment also observes a side band near the
\Gamma
-valley band edge
7
. The longitudinal HM coupling does not necessarily produce a distinct side band, because it scatters electrons everywhere in q-space, rather than concentrating near
\mathbf{q}=0
as in the coupling to polar phonon.
7
. However, for finite
z
,
q
is restricted to
\approx 1/z
and becomes mainly forward scattering as well. Therefore both coupling mechanisms can contribute to the sideband.
