Equivalent-circuit-based multi-objective parameter design and Pareto analysis of an LCC-S wireless power transfer system

Gang Zhou, Shishi Liao, Qi Jiang

ABSTRACT
Parameter selection in an LCC-S compensated wireless power transfer system is difficult because the circuit variables are coupled and an increase in output power does not necessarily lead to higher transmission efficiency. In this study, the primary compensation inductance, equivalent load resistance, and mutual inductance were treated as three design variables, and their selection was formulated as a two-objective optimization problem. The search was performed using an archive-based particle swarm optimization method that combines dynamic inertia adjustment, probabilistic velocity pausing, and stagnation reset. MOPSO, NSGA-II, and MOGWO were used as comparison methods in 10 independent runs. Hypervolume, Spacing, the number of nondominated solutions, and runtime were recorded. Relative to MOPSO, the proposed method increased mean hypervolume by 8.45%, reduced Spacing by 41.14%, and reduced the observed MATLAB runtime by 26.30%. The obtained Pareto front contained candidates corresponding to a high-output-power boundary, an intermediate power–efficiency trade-off, and high-efficiency operation. Three representative candidates were further examined using a switching-level Simulink model together with model-calculated current and loss indicators. These results are intended to support preliminary parameter selection for LCC-S systems; hardware calibration is still required before the reported candidates can be used as practical operating points.

1. Introduction Wireless power transfer (WPT) enables contactless energy delivery through electromagnetic coupling and resonant energy conversion [ 1 , 2 ]. It has been investigated for electric-vehicle charging [ 3 – 5 ], implantable medical devices [ 6 – 8 ], and consumer, mobile, or aerial applications [ 9 – 11 ]. Recent studies have further examined dynamic wireless charging for electric vehicles [ 12 ] and intermittent charging methods for cardiac pacemakers [ 13 ]. WPT research also encompasses radiative energy-transfer technologies [ 14 ], while artificial-intelligence methods are increasingly being explored for WPT system analysis and design [ 15 ]. In resonant WPT systems, output power and transmission efficiency depend jointly on the compensation topology, magnetic coupling, load condition, and parasitic losses. For LCC-S compensated systems, these factors are strongly coupled, and independent adjustment of individual circuit parameters may shift the operating point away from the desired resonance condition or produce an unfavorable power–efficiency trade-off. Joint parameter selection can therefore be formulated as a constrained multi-objective design problem. Several optimization methods have been used in WPT system design. Genetic algorithms have been applied to component selection in LCC-S systems [ 16 ]. Particle swarm optimization (PSO) [ 17 ] has also been used because of its relatively simple implementation and population-based search capability, although premature convergence remains a known limitation [ 18 ]. Multi-objective methods are useful when output power and transmission efficiency must be considered together [ 19 , 20 ]. For the problem studied here, the main difficulty is that the compensation parameter, load condition, and magnetic coupling condition affect both objectives simultaneously. Existing PSO variants address different parts of this problem. Adaptive or dynamic PSO changes algorithm parameters during the iteration process [ 21 ]. VPPSO [ 22 ] reduces the frequency of velocity updates, whereas MOPSO [ 23 ] uses an external archive to retain nondominated solutions. The quality of a multi-objective solution set can be evaluated using indicators such as hypervolume (HV) [ 24 ], which considers both convergence and the extent of the obtained Pareto front. In this study, these established PSO mechanisms are integrated into an archive-based Pareto parameter-design framework tailored to the LCC-S WPT problem, and the resulting procedure is referred to as Velocity-Pausing and Stagnation-Reset Particle Swarm Optimization (VPSR-PSO). The methodological contribution is therefore the coordinated application and evaluation of these mechanisms for the coupled power–efficiency design problem rather than the introduction of fundamentally new general-purpose optimization operators. The aim of this study was to develop and evaluate an equivalent-circuit-based multi-objective parameter-design procedure for an LCC-S compensated WPT system. The primary compensation inductance, equivalent load resistance, and mutual inductance were jointly optimized, with output power and transmission efficiency treated as competing objectives. An archive-based particle swarm procedure integrating dynamic inertia adjustment, velocity pausing, and stagnation reset was used as the search method. The procedure was evaluated over 10 independent runs against representative multi-objective optimizers, and selected Pareto-front candidates were further examined using time-domain switching simulations and model-level electrical-stress indicators. The results show that the proposed framework can identify candidate parameter sets corresponding to different power–efficiency design preferences under the tested model assumptions. 2. System modeling and transmission characteristic analysis This section establishes the equivalent-circuit model and transmission-characteristic analysis of the LCC-S compensated WPT system, providing the computational basis for the subsequent multi-objective optimization. 2.1. System modeling Among various compensation topologies, the Series-Series (SS) topology is simple but sensitive to load variations [ 25 ]. The double-sided LCC topology offers excellent misalignment tolerance but requires more passive components, increasing system size and cost [ 26 ]. In contrast, the LCC-S topology combines the benefits of constant current output and a compact secondary side, making it highly suitable for EV charging applications [ 27 , 28 ]. The two-coil Magnetically-Coupled Resonant Wireless Power Transfer (MCRWPT) system is illustrated in Fig 1 . The AC mains are first rectified through an AC–DC converter, converting the electrical energy into a DC supply. Subsequently, a DC–AC inverter transforms the DC power into an AC signal tuned to the system’s resonant frequency. This AC signal excites the primary compensation network and transmitting coil to achieve resonance, thereby enabling efficient energy transfer to the secondary side. On the receiving side, the secondary coil resonates with its own compensation network, and the induced AC power is then rectified by an AC–DC converter to supply DC power to the load. 10.1371/journal.pone.0355130.g001 Fig 1 Schematic of the two-coil MCRWPT system. System parameter optimization design VPSR-PSO uses MOPSO as the multi-objective framework and introduces three search controls: nonlinear dynamic inertia adjustment, probabilistic velocity pausing, and stagnation reset. These mechanisms are used to modify different stages of the search process while retaining the external Pareto archive of MOPSO. The three mechanisms were selected because L p , M , and R L form a strongly coupled search space. Dynamic inertia changes the search from broader exploration in the early iterations to local refinement later. Velocity pausing limits repeated changes in search direction, while stagnation reset reinitializes particles that stop improving for a specified number of iterations. These mechanisms are not presented as new general-purpose optimization operators. The contribution of the present method lies in their coordinated integration within an archive-based LCC-S Pareto parameter-design procedure and in the quantitative evaluation of their individual and combined roles in this application-specific optimization problem. 3.1. Standard PSO algorithm In the standard PSO algorithm, a number of particles are initialized to search within a defined domain D , where each particle represents a potential solution to the optimization problem. Each particle has two attributes—position and velocity, which correspond to the current solution and the rate of change used to update the position in the next iteration, respectively, and can be expressed as follows: { @ l @ @ l x i ( t ) = [ x i 1 ( t ) , x i 2 ( t ) , x i 3 ( t ) … x i d ( t ) ] v i ( t ) = [ v i 1 ( t ) , v i 2 ( t ) , v i 3 ( t ) … v i d ( t ) ] (8) where x i ( t ) denotes the position of the i -th particle at iteration t , d is the dimensionality of the problem, and v i ( t ) deno t es the velocity of the i -th particle at iteration t . In the optimization process, the algorithm updates particle positions by moving them through the search space and tracking two best positions: the personal best and the global best. The personal best represents the optimal solution found by an individual particle, while the global best corresponds to the optimal solution discovered by the entire swarm. Let p B i denote the personal best position of the i -th particle and g B denote the global best position of the swarm. The velocity of each particle is updated as follows: v i ( t + 1 ) = w v i ( t ) + c 1 r 1 [ p B i ( t ) − x i ( t ) ] + c 2 r 2 [ g B ( t ) − x i ( t ) ] (9) where w is the inertia weight, which controls the momentum of particle movement. A larger value of w enhances the global exploration ability of the swarm, while a smaller value promotes local exploitation. c 1 and c 2 are the acceleration coefficients, representing the cognitive (individual) and social (group) learning factors, respectively. r 1 and r 2 are random numbers uniformly distributed in [0,1], which introduce stochasticity into the search process, enabling particles to perform random exploration. In the standard PSO algorithm, the inertia weight w is constant. However, in practice, it should be set larger at the beginning of the search to enhance global exploration, and gradually decreased later to strengthen local exploitation. In addition, since each particle updates its velocity at every iteration, it may oscillate between two good solutions or accelerate excessively toward an optimum, thereby missing potential solutions in intermediate regions [ 31 ]. In this case, a smaller value of f ( • ) indicates a better solution, and the personal best position is updated when the current particle position gives a smaller objective value than the previous personal best. In VPSR-PSO, candidate solutions are therefore compared by Pareto dominance instead of by a single scalar objective. For two candidates x a and x b , x a dominates x b when η ( x a ) ≥ η ( x b ) and P out ( x a ) ≥ P out ( x b ), with at least one strict inequality. In the following discussion, “objective values” refer to η and P out , whereas the fitness used by the multi-objective search is the corresponding two-dimensional objective vector. No scalar aggregation of the two objectives is used. 3.2. VPSR-PSO algorithm It should be emphasized that, in Section 3.1 , the particle position x i denotes a general particle position in the standard PSO framework. Its specific physical meaning depends on the selected design variables of the optimization problem. For the VPSR-PSO implementation in this work, x i is specifically defined as a parameter vector composed of the three selected optimization variables. In the proposed VPSR-PSO implementation, each particle represents a complete candidate parameter configuration of the LCC-S WPT system rather than a single circuit variable. Specifically, the position vector of the i -th particle is defined as x i = [ L P , i , M i , R L , i ], where L P , i , M i , and R L , i denote the primary compensation inductance, mutual inductance, and equivalent load resistance encoded by this particle, respectively. The corresponding velocity vector is defined as v i = [ v L P , v M , v R L ]. Therefore, each particle updates the three design variables L P , M , and R L simultaneously within the bounds defined in Eq. (7) . The resulting candidate parameter combination is evaluated using the objective vector [ η , P out ]. From Equation (9) , it can be observed that the inertia weight w controls the influence of the particle’s previous velocity on its current update speed, which is crucial for balancing the global and local search abilities of the algorithm. In this study, the use of fixed or linearly varying inertia weights is abandoned, and a nonlinear dynamic decay strategy is adopted in combination with the approach proposed in [ 22 ]: w ( t ) = e − ( b t / T ) b (13) where T denotes the total number of iterations, and b is a constant, typically set to 2.5. The inertia weight determines how strongly the previous particle velocity contributes to the next update. In this study, the fixed inertia weight is replaced by the nonlinear decay in Eq. (13) . The larger value during the early iterations allows wider movement in the search space, whereas the lower value in later iterations favors local refinement. The exponential schedule also avoids abrupt changes between different search stages. At the same time, it is not necessary to update the particle velocity at every iteration, since excessive velocity updates may cause the particles to fall into local optima or miss potential global solutions [ 32 ]. Therefore, a velocity pause strategy is introduced in this paper. In each iteration, the velocity of each particle is updated probabilistically according to the following rule: v i ( t + 1 ) = { @ l @ v i ( t ) i f r a n d < α | v i ( t ) | r 1 w ( t ) + c 1 r 1 [ p B i ( t ) − x i ( t ) ] + c 2 r 2 [ g B ( t ) − x i ( t ) ] o t h e r w i s e (14) where α denotes the velocity pause probability and is defined within the range [ 0 , 1 ] . r a n d is a random number uniformly distributed in [ 0 , 1 ] . When r a n d ≥ α , the particle velocity is updated according to the PSO velocity-update rule. When r a n d < α , the velocity update is paused, and the particle retains its previous velocity. Therefore, the velocity update probability is 1 − α , while the velocity pause probability is α . This probabilistic velocity-pausing strategy prevents particles from changing their search direction too frequently and allows them to further explore promising local regions. In particular, when the particle velocity is relatively small, retaining the previous velocity helps the particle hover around potential Pareto-optimal regions, thereby improving local exploitation and maintaining population diversity. Subsequently, the particle position is updated according to Eq. (10) . In addition, some particles may still fall into local optima and stagnate, which can mislead the entire population into converging to a suboptimal region far from the global optimum. To address this issue, this paper introduces a stagnation reset mechanism. A stagnation counter, Stall i , is assigned to each particle and updated according to the following equation: Stall i ( t + 1 ) = { @ l @ 0 i f x i ( t + 1 ) d o m i n a t e s p B i ( t ) Stall i ( t ) + 1 o t h e r w i s e (15) Eq. (15) indicates that, during each iteration, if the new position of a particle fails to dominate its individual historical best solution p B i , its stagnation counter Stall i is incremented by one. Conversely, if p B i is successfully updated, the counter is reset to zero. The stagnation condition is determined as follows: Stall i ( t ) ≥ Stall m a x (16) where Stall m a x denotes the predefined stagnation threshold. Once this condition is met, the particle undergoes a “reset” operation as defined by the following equation: { @ l @ x i ( t + 1 ) = reset ( D ) v i ( t + 1 ) = 0 Stall i ( t + 1 ) = 0 (17) where reset ( D ) denotes the operation of generating a random position within the search domain D . After the reset, the particle is assigned a completely new position within the entire search space, and both its velocity and stagnation counter are reinitialized. A stagnation counter records consecutive iterations in which the current particle does not improve its personal best under Pareto dominance. When the counter reaches Stall m a x , the particle is assigned a new random position in the feasible domain, and its velocity and stagnation counter are reset. This step reintroduces search diversity after prolonged stagnation. The VPSR-PSO algorithm can be applied to single-objective optimization problems; however, in this study, the parameter optimization task is formulated as a multi-objective optimization problem. According to the literature [ 23 ], the main distinction between multi-objective and single-objective optimization lies in the introduction of an external archive used to store a set of non-dominated solutions, which collectively form the Pareto front [ 33 ]. This set of solutions represents a trade-off between transmission efficiency and output power, providing a compromise set of optimal parameter configurations. Different from the standard single-objective PSO described in Section 3.1 , the proposed VPSR-PSO evaluates particles using a multi-objective fitness vector rather than a single scalar fitness value. The fitness vector is defined by the two physical objectives, namely transmission efficiency η and output power P out . Since both objectives are to be maximized, the comparison among particles is conducted according to Pareto dominance. A newly generated solution updates the personal best only when it dominates the previous personal best, while all nondominated solutions are stored and updated in the external Pareto archive. The optimization process of the VPSR-PSO algorithm proceeds as follows. First, the algorithm initializes all parameters and randomly generates the positions and velocities of the particles. The initial individual best solutions p B i and the initial Pareto archive are then established. During the iterative process, the particle velocities are updated using Eq. (13) and Eq. (14) according to the dynamic inertia weight and velocity pause strategies, while the positions are updated using Equation (10) . Instead of using a single global best as in single-objective PSO, each particle selects a leader g i from the Pareto archive to guide its velocity update. The personal best positions are updated according to Eq. (11) , and the Pareto archive is continuously refined based on the dominance relationship among solutions. Meanwhile, the stagnation counter of each particle is updated following Eq. (15) ; once this counter exceeds the threshold defined in Eq. (16) , the particle is reinitialized according to Eq. (17) . Finally, when the maximum number of iterations is reached, the algorithm outputs the final Pareto-optimal solution set. The Pseudo-code of the VPSR-PSO algorithm is illustrated in Algorithm 1. In Algorithm 1, the personal best is updated according to Pareto dominance, the external archive is maintained by retaining nondominated solutions and removing dominated ones, and the leader used for velocity updating is selected from the current Pareto archive. By combining nonlinear dynamic inertia, velocity pausing, and stagnation reset, VPSR-PSO modifies the search trajectory, velocity-update frequency, and recovery from particle stagnation within the MOPSO framework. The individual contributions of these mechanisms are evaluated separately through the ablation analysis in Section 4.3 . For clarity, the external Pareto archive used by VPSR-PSO accumulates historical nondominated solutions throughout the search. It is initialized from the nondominated feasible solutions in the initial population. After each objective evaluation, a candidate is added to the archive if it is not dominated by any archived solution, while archived solutions dominated by the new candidate are removed. No fixed archive-capacity limit or archive-pruning operation is imposed during the VPSR-PSO search. Because equality of the two objective values is not treated as Pareto dominance, identical objective vectors may temporarily coexist in the search archive. Before the reported performance indicators are calculated, however, the final objective vectors are subjected to a common nondominance filter and duplicate removal. The leader used for particle updating is selected uniformly at random from the current archive. The computational complexity of VPSR-PSO can be analyzed from Algorithm 1. Let n denote the population size, MaxIt the maximum number of iterations, D v the number of decision variables, m the number of objectives, and A the size of the external Pareto archive. Position and velocity updating requires O( D v ) operations per particle, whereas dominance checking and archive maintenance may require up to O( m A). The resulting search complexity can therefore be expressed approximately as O( MaxItn ( D v + m A)). Dynamic-inertia calculation, the velocity-pause decision, and stagnation-counter updating do not change this asymptotic order relative to the underlying MOPSO framework. However, this complexity expression should not be interpreted as implying a general runtime advantage, because practical computation time also depends on archive growth, dominance checking, data structures, implementation details, and the number of stagnation-triggered reset evaluations. The runtime comparisons reported in Section 4.2 therefore refer specifically to the present MATLAB implementation and computing environment. To provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the Pareto-front quality under different parameter settings, HV and Spacing are introduced in addition to the number of nondominated solutions and total computation time. The reported solutions refer to the nondominated solutions retained in the external Pareto archive after dominance filtering, while dominated solutions are removed during the archive update process. Therefore, the number of nondominated solutions is used only as an auxiliary indicator. HV is used to evaluate the convergence and coverage of the obtained Pareto front, where a larger value indicates better overall Pareto-front quality. Spacing is used to evaluate the distribution uniformity of the nondominated solutions, where a smaller value indicates a more uniformly distributed Pareto front. Since output power and transmission efficiency have different numerical scales, both objectives are normalized before calculating HV and Spacing. Specifically, within each parameter comparison in this subsection, both objectives were min–max normalized to [0,1] using common bounds determined from all nondominated solutions of the settings included in that comparison. Consequently, the normalized HV and Spacing values are intended for within-table comparison only. Because Tables 4–6 involve different comparison sets and therefore different normalization bounds, the repeated reference configuration n =100, α =0.7, and Stall max = 10 may have different normalized metric values across the three tables. And because both objectives are maximized, HV was calculated in the normalized objective space with the reference point r =(0,0), representing the worst normalized objective vector. The effect of the particle population size n on the algorithm is first discussed. The population size determines how many particles are available to search for the optimal solution during the optimization process, which inevitably affects both the computation time and the quality of the obtained results. Table 4 presents the number of nondominated solutions, HV, Spacing, and the computation time required to find the optimal solutions for n = [50, 100, 200], with α = 0.7 and Stall m a x = 10. The corresponding Pareto-front distributions under the same parameter settings are illustrated in Fig 5 . The representative fronts in Fig 5 illustrate the corresponding distribution patterns, while the repeated-run quantitative results are summarized in Table 4 . The repeated-run results in Table 4 show that increasing the population size improves Pareto-front coverage but also increases computational cost substantially. The highest mean HV is obtained with n =200 (0.7582), compared with 0.7545 for n =100; however, this approximately 0.49% HV increase is accompanied by an increase in mean runtime from 25.67 s to 82.87 s. Although n =50 provides the shortest runtime and the lowest mean Spacing, its mean HV is lower and the resulting nondominated set is substantially smaller. Therefore, n =100 is retained as a compromise between Pareto-front quality, solution-set coverage, and observed implementation-level runtime rather than as the setting that optimizes every individual metric. The velocity pause parameter α plays a crucial role in the VPSR-PSO algorithm. It controls the rate at which particles adjust their velocity during iterations, thereby influencing the balance between local exploitation and global exploration. A smaller α leads to more frequent velocity updates and stronger global exploration, whereas a larger α results in fewer updates and more meticulous searches around promising regions. In the experiments for this parameter, the population size was set to n = 100 and Stall m a x = 10. Table 5 reports the number of nondominated solutions, HV, Spacing, and the computation time when α = [0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7], while Fig 6 illustrates the corresponding Pareto-front distributions under the same parameter settings. When α = 0, the velocity pause mechanism is disabled, meaning that all particles continuously update their velocities during the iterations. The repeated-run results provide a consistent preference for α =0.7. Its mean HV of 0.7582 is the highest among the tested settings, while its mean Spacing of 0.0141 and runtime of 25.67 s are the lowest. In addition, the relatively small HV standard deviation indicates stable performance across independent initializations. In contrast, disabling velocity pausing α =0 substantially reduces mean HV and increases both Spacing and runtime. These results indicate that, for the present multi-objective LCC-S problem, a relatively high velocity-pause probability reduces unnecessary changes in particle search direction while the stagnation-reset mechanism maintains exploration when particles cease to improve. Although Reference [ 22 ] used α =0.3 for different single-objective test problems, the repeated-run results here favor α =0.7 for the present multi-objective LCC-S design problem. Accordingly, α =0.7 is retained for the subsequent experiments. The third parameter considered is the stagnation detection threshold, Stall m a x , which determines how many consecutive iterations a particle is allowed to show no improvement before being reset. Experiments were conducted with n = 100 and α = 0.7. Table 6 reports the number of nondominated solutions, HV, Spacing and the algorithm runtime under different Stall m a x settings of [ 5 , 10 , 20 ]. The corresponding Pareto front distributions are illustrated in Fig 7 . As shown in Fig 7 and Table 6 , the stagnation threshold affects the balance between search continuation and random reinitialization. The repeated-run results show that the stagnation threshold controls a clear trade-off between reset frequency, Pareto-front coverage, and computational cost. A threshold of 5 produces the shortest runtime and the lowest mean Spacing, but frequent resets limit historical front coverage and yield the lowest mean HV among the three tested settings. Increasing the threshold to 20 allows substantially more nondominated solutions to accumulate but increases the mean runtime to 104.57 s without improving HV. A threshold of 10 produces the highest mean HV (0.7432) and the smallest HV standard deviation, while maintaining substantially lower runtime than the threshold of 20. Therefore, Stall m a x =10 is retained as a balanced setting that favors overall Pareto quality and run-to-run stability rather than the minimum Spacing alone. Based on the repeated-run parameter analysis, n = 100, α =0.7, and Stall m a x =10 are retained for the subsequent benchmark comparison. These values are selected as robust compromise settings across Pareto-front quality, run-to-run variability, solution-set coverage, and computational cost rather than as settings that independently optimize every reported metric. 4.2. Benchmark comparison with representative optimization algorithms To further evaluate the performance of the proposed VPSR-PSO framework, MOPSO [ 23 ], NSGA-II [ 34 ], and MOGWO [ 35 ] were included as representative multi-objective comparison algorithms. NSGA-II represents a population-based evolutionary algorithm, whereas MOGWO provides an additional swarm-intelligence-based comparison. Recent WPT studies have also considered multi-objective optimization. Wu et al. [ 36 ] applied NSGA-II to a detuned LCC-S dynamic WPT system under mutual- and self-inductance drift, while Wu et al. [ 37 ] combined an adaptive DNN model with improved PSO for multi-objective coil design. More recent studies have further explored multi-objective optimization in LCC-S WPT systems. Liu et al. [ 38 ] employed MOPSO for data-efficient multi-objective design of LCC-S auxiliary localization coils under misalignment conditions, whereas Zhao et al. [ 39 ] used NSGA-II for LCC-S parameter optimization under coupling and load variations. Because their optimization variables, objectives, and system configurations differ from those considered here, their numerical results are not directly comparable with the present study. The four algorithms were therefore evaluated under a controlled computational protocol using the same LCC-S analytical model, objective functions, decision-variable bounds, population size, maximum number of iterations, and computing platform. The stochastic guidance rules used in the comparison were also implemented consistently with the random-control versions adopted in this study: VPSR-PSO, MOPSO, and MOGWO select archive leaders randomly, while equal-rank selection in the implemented NSGA-II comparison is resolved randomly. Algorithm-specific search and archive mechanisms were otherwise retained rather than replacing them with a newly imposed common archive structure. It therefore includes the algorithmic search operations performed during the run, including objective evaluation, particle or population updating, dominance checking, and archive maintenance. The subsequent cross-algorithm normalization, statistical testing, figure generation, and other reporting-oriented post-processing were not included in the reported runtime. Accordingly, the runtime values should be interpreted as implementation-level measurements under the reported software and hardware conditions rather than as hardware-independent measures of algorithmic complexity. The comparison was evaluated using HV, Spacing, the number of unique nondominated solutions, and runtime. HV reflects both the convergence and coverage of the obtained Pareto front, with a larger value indicating better overall Pareto-front quality. Spacing reflects the distribution uniformity of the nondominated solutions, with a smaller value indicating a more even distribution. The number of unique nondominated solutions is reported only as an auxiliary indicator because a larger solution count does not necessarily imply better Pareto-front quality. Runtime represents the observed computation time of the corresponding MATLAB implementation under the reported computing environment. To quantify the uncertainty associated with stochastic optimization, 95% confidence intervals were calculated across the 10 independent runs. The second and third solutions represent intermediate and high-efficiency model-based candidates, respectively, and are retained for subsequent prototype-level evaluation rather than direct practical deployment. Model limitations and practical feasibility discussion All results reported in this study are model-based. The analytical optimization kernel employs the FHA and does not explicitly model several hardware-dependent effects, including inverter dead time and device voltage drops, semiconductor conduction and switching losses, tolerances and temperature dependence of compensation components and winding resistances, magnetic-core and frequency-dependent winding losses, or measurement uncertainty [ 25 , 27 , 29 ]. In addition, coil displacement or angular misalignment changes the mutual inductance and may also alter the coil self-inductances and loss characteristics. These effects can shift both output power and efficiency relative to the model-derived Pareto candidates and should be included when the framework is applied to a specific prototype. FHA was retained because it makes repeated objective evaluation practical during optimization. The Simulink model keeps the complete square-wave excitation and provides a more detailed time-domain representation, but it is still a simulation model. As quantified in Section 4.5 , the output-power differences between the analytical and switching-level models range from 8.08% to 11.01%, while the corresponding efficiency differences range from 0.30 to 7.00 percentage points. These discrepancies reflect the different levels of waveform representation used by the FHA-based analytical model and the complete switching model. A recent comparison of lumped-parameter and distributed electromagnetic WPT models also reported that computationally inexpensive lumped models can lose accuracy when distributed effects become important [ 40 ]. The occurrence of M = 60 μH in two representative solutions further indicates that part of the obtained Pareto front is influenced by the prescribed magnetic-coupling boundary. Extending the numerical upper bound alone would not necessarily constitute a physically meaningful sensitivity test because the attainable mutual inductance is determined by a specific coil geometry, air gap, alignment condition, and magnetic structure. Since these hardware quantities are not independently parameterized in the present equivalent-circuit optimization, the current results are deliberately interpreted only within the specified M = 1–60μH domain. Future prototype-oriented optimization should first determine an experimentally or electromagnetically validated coupling interval for the selected coupler and then repeat the Pareto analysis within that hardware-specific range. The Pareto points should therefore be treated as preliminary model-based design candidates rather than directly deployable operating points. For a specific prototype, the ranges of L p , R L and particularly M should first be restricted according to component ratings, coupler geometry, nominal coil spacing, allowable misalignment, and thermal and electromagnetic constraints. A candidate can then be selected according to the required power–efficiency trade-off and subsequently verified using a hardware prototype. Such validation should evaluate component tolerances, coil displacement, temperature-dependent losses, inverter non-idealities, semiconductor losses, and magnetic losses that are not fully represented in the present optimization model. 5. Conclusion We formulated the joint selection of L p , R L , and M in an LCC-S WPT system as a two-objective parameter-design problem involving output power and transmission efficiency. The optimization framework integrates dynamic inertia adjustment, velocity pausing, and stagnation reset within an archive-based PSO rather than introducing fundamentally new general-purpose optimization operators. Across 10 independent benchmark runs, VPSR-PSO increased mean HV by 8.45%, reduced Spacing by 41.14%, and reduced the observed MATLAB runtime by 26.30% relative to the baseline MOPSO. Statistical testing confirmed the improvement over MOPSO, whereas NSGA-II and MOGWO achieved better final Pareto-quality indicators but showed longer observed runtimes under the reported implementation. The ablation analysis further showed that velocity pausing and stagnation reset provide the main performance improvements, while nonlinear dynamic inertia primarily contributes to search stability rather than a clear independent improvement in final Pareto-front quality. The resulting Pareto front provides model-based candidates corresponding to high-output-power, intermediate power–efficiency, and high-efficiency regions. Switching-level Simulink evaluation showed consistent trade-off trends while also revealing quantitative discrepancies from the FHA-based analytical predictions. The proposed framework should therefore be interpreted as an application-specific tool for preliminary LCC-S parameter screening and power–efficiency trade-off analysis. The reported candidates are not experimentally validated operating points, and prototype-level verification remains necessary before hardware implementation. 5.1.
