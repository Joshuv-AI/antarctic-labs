Integrated digit‐in‐noise testing for hearing and cognitive screening
Authors: Shangqiguo Wang, Lena L. N. Wong, June Tung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONHearing loss and cognitive decline often coexist in aging, and reduced audibility can bias cognitive screening. We evaluated integrated digit‐in‐noise (iDIN) testing for paired screening.METHODSAmong 962 community‐screened adults 55 years of age or older, 495 had linked audiometry, iDIN, and the Hong Kong version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (HK‐MoCA). Outcomes were better‐ear four‐frequency pure‐tone average (PTA4) >35 dB hearing level (HL) and HK‐MoCA <22.RESULTSIn linked participants, 294 (59.4%) had PTA4 >35 dB HL and 195 (39.4%) were HK‐MoCA screen‐positive. Backward‐recall speech reception threshold (SRT) difference scores discriminated HK‐MoCA <22 (SRT3b‐3/SRT3b‐2 area under the curve [AUC] = 0.807/0.800). SRT3b‐3 correlated with HK‐MoCA (Spearman rs = –0.520), not PTA4 (rs = 0.018). SRT2/SRT3 similarly classified hearing loss (AUC = 0.878/0.874).DISCUSSIONiDIN may support brief prediagnostic triage by pairing hearing characterization with cognitive‐load screening, rather than replacing multidomain assessment or diagnosis.

BACKGROUND

Hearing loss and cognitive decline are major public‐health problems in aging societies. More than 1.5 billion people live with some degree of hearing loss worldwide, mild cognitive impairment affects more than 15% of community‐dwelling adults 50 years of age or older, and dementia prevalence is projected to rise substantially by 2050. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 These conditions carry large individual and societal costs and are clinically intertwined: hearing loss is associated with cognitive decline and dementia, hearing intervention may reduce decline in higher‐risk older adults, and dementia‐prevention frameworks treat hearing loss as a modifiable risk factor. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9

Cognitive screening remains constrained by sensory, linguistic, and educational assumptions. The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) is widely used for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) screening, and the Hong Kong version (HK‐MoCA) has local validation and education‐adjusted cutoff evidence. 10 , 11 , 12 MoCA administration commonly requires ≈15 to 20 min and may take longer when repeated instruction, cognitive decline, or sensory impairment is present. Item‐level work in low‐education Hong Kong older adults suggests that education can influence some MoCA items, and reduced audibility can lower spoken cognitive test scores. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Hearing‐adapted cognitive tools reduce sensory bias but do not provide one scalable workflow for paired hearing and cognitive screening. 17 , 18

Care pathways for hearing and cognition are often separated across audiology, otolaryngology, geriatrics, psychiatry, and neurology services. Hearing screening often requires calibrated equipment and controlled listening conditions, whereas cognitive screening commonly uses paper‐and‐pencil or interview tools that assume adequate audibility. This separation is inefficient for community outreach, where older adults often require referral decisions before specialist assessment. A task that anchors audibility and then adds a controlled cognitive‐load manipulation could address this practical gap.

Integrated digit‐in‐noise (iDIN) testing was developed as a digit‐based extension of speech‐in‐noise testing that varies digit length and recall direction so the same task family can provide hearing and cognitive information. 19 , 20 Participants hear digit sequences in background noise and repeat them in the instructed order. Because digits carry little semantic content, iDIN may reduce vocabulary and schooling demands. The outcome is the speech reception threshold (SRT), the signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) at which ≈50% of digit sequences are repeated correctly. Conventional 3‐digit DIN tests are established hearing‐screening tools, and Cantonese iDIN validation supports 2‐ and 3‐digit forward SRTs (SRT2 and SRT3) as hearing‐screening indicators. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26

The cognitive rationale can be framed as a two‐step demand model. First, digits must be recognized in noise. This effortful‐listening demand is described by the Ease of Language Understanding model and the Framework for Understanding Effortful Listening, is expected to increase with digit‐sequence length, and remains hearing dependent. 20 , 27 , 28 , 29 Second, the recognized sequence must be recalled. Forward recall mainly requires serial reproduction, whereas backward recall requires storage and reverse‐order manipulation beyond simple repetition. 30 This structure motivates SRT difference scores. SRT3b denotes the 3‐digit backward‐recall SRT and SRT5 the 5‐digit forward SRT. SRT3b‐3, defined as SRT3b minus SRT3, is intended to reduce the shared 3‐digit recognition and audibility component and emphasize backward‐recall cost. SRT3b‐2 is also expected to emphasize recall manipulation because 2‐ and 3‐digit forward sequences impose low and similar recognition demands. By contrast, SRT5‐2 and SRT5‐3 may include both longer‐sequence recognition load and memory load. This conceptual framework is summarized in Figure 1.

In high‐demand conditions, such as 3‐digit backward recall, task demand may exceed available resources, and measured SRT may reflect both auditory function and cognitive‐resource limitation. SRT3b‐3 is calculated as SRT3b minus SRT3. Because both conditions use the same 3‐digit recognition‐in‐noise framework but differ in recall rule, this contrast is intended to reduce shared auditory‐recognition demands and emphasize added backward‐recall load. The current study builds on prior controlled iDIN work by evaluating hearing and cognitive screening in a real‐world community‐to‐clinic pathway across five local community centers and the University of Hong Kong Speech, Language and Hearing Clinic. A preliminary study in 107 older adults suggested that SRT2 and SRT3 can screen hearing loss and that SRT3b‐3 may classify cognitive‐screen‐positive status. 20 Whether this promise persists in a larger real‐world pathway remains unclear. The primary aim was to determine whether SRT3b‐3 and SRT3b‐2 discriminate HK‐MoCA screen‐positive status beyond hearing level. Secondary aims were to evaluate community screening yield, community‐clinic agreement, and whether SRT2 and SRT3 provide comparable hearing screening despite cognitive variation.

METHODS

Study design and setting

This was a two‐stage community‐to‐clinic screening study of adults 55 years of age or older. Community screening was conducted by trained social workers from March to November 2024 across five local community centers. Clinic follow‐up was conducted from May 2024 to May 2025 at the University of Hong Kong Speech, Language and Hearing Clinic, where assessments were administered by clinical audiologists or trained research staff in a controlled clinic environment.

Recruitment and eligibility

Participants were recruited through community‐center outreach activities and voluntary participation. The community stage prioritized older adults who were interested in hearing screening or further hearing assessment. Participants were not recruited through random sampling, household sampling, or consecutive population‐based sampling, and no population denominator was available.

Eligible community participants were adults 55 years of age or older who could follow Cantonese test instructions, provide written informed consent, and give oral or otherwise scorable responses for digit repetition. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Practical exclusion criteria were age <55 years, inability to understand Cantonese test instructions, inability to provide a scorable response because of severe communication or medical constraints, missing age or sex, and missing or invalid community 3‐digit SRT data. Clinic‐level analyses were restricted to participants with a linked community record and complete clinic assessment, defined as available pure‐tone audiometry, clinic iDIN forward SRTs, 3‐digit backward completion status or SRT, and HK‐MoCA total/domain data.

Community screening pathway

The community measure was the 3‐digit forward DIN SRT. It was administered at five local community centers by trained social workers using the iDIN setup described below. Before community screening, researchers and/or an audiologist provided detailed training to all social workers who administered the test. Social workers used standardized verbal instructions and were asked to administer the test with the calibrated equipment in the quietest feasible room at each center. The community stage used only the 3‐digit forward condition to minimize outreach testing time and because the optimal iDIN cognitive‐screening combination had not yet been established.

The operational referral criterion at the community stage was 3‐digit SRT > –7.7 dB SNR, a cutoff previously estimated in a preliminary iDIN study conducted under more controlled hearing‐clinic conditions. 20 Participants whose community SRT exceeded this cutoff were invited for comprehensive assessment at the University of Hong Kong Speech, Language and Hearing Clinic. Some participants whose community SRT was at or below the cutoff but who wanted further hearing assessment also attended the clinic. Clinic records were therefore available only for participants who attended and completed the linked follow‐up assessment.

Community testing was not assumed to be equivalent to clinic testing. The operational cutoff was evaluated as a referral rule rather than as a definitive diagnostic boundary. Additional thresholds were explored to show how community referral yield and verified hearing‐screening accuracy would change under alternative local operating points; these thresholds require external validation. Differences between community and clinic performance were expected because community testing was administered by trained social workers, and could be affected by residual background noise, outreach‐room constraints, or less consistent understanding of instructions, and was completed by older adults whose motivation to perform a hearing screen may vary.

Data linkage and analysis cohorts

Community and clinic records were linked by participant identification. Throughout the manuscript, community SRT3 refers to the outreach 3‐digit forward SRT, and clinic SRT2 or SRT3 refers to the corresponding forward iDIN condition administered at the University of Hong Kong Speech, Language and Hearing Clinic. Three analytic cohorts were specified before manuscript analysis. The community cohort (N = 962) was used to describe screening yield and community SRT3 distribution. The linked clinic cohort (N = 495) was used for community‐clinic agreement, audiometric hearing‐screening accuracy, and HK‐MoCA analyses.

The 3‐digit backward condition was attempted after backward‐recall practice items. A numeric 3‐digit backward‐recall SRT was available for 476 linked clinic participants. For 19 participants, no valid numeric backward‐recall SRT was available because the participant was unable to complete the condition or a valid adaptive threshold could not be obtained. These 19 participants were excluded from analyses requiring observed continuous backward‐recall SRT values but were retained in screening‐style binary analyses as an unable/worst category.

Restricting clinic‐level analyses to linked participants preserves the pathway question: how the community screen relates to full follow‐up assessment among participants who actually entered the staged screening pathway. Clinic‐only participants without valid community screening were not included in the main clinic analyses.

iDIN test administration and scoring

The Cantonese iDIN was administered using the same testing configuration, custom MATLAB software, and hardware as prior iDIN work: a laptop connected to an external sound card and circumaural headphones (TASCAM US‐2 × 2 sound card and Sennheiser HDA 300 headphones). 20 , 25 The same complete equipment configuration was used for community and clinic testing. Community test units were calibrated at the University of Hong Kong Speech, Language and Hearing Clinic before distribution to the community centers. Test materials were spoken digit sequences presented in background noise. Participants repeated the heard digits orally, and responses were scored by the tester during administration rather than entered by participants using a keyboard.

In each sequence, a 200‐ms silent interval separated adjacent digits. Digit sequences were embedded in long‐term average speech spectrum‐shaped noise, generated to approximate the long‐term average spectrum of the speech materials and fixed at 65 dB sound pressure level (SPL). The noise started 500 ms before the digit sequence and stopped 500 ms after the sequence.

Each iDIN condition started at 0 dB SNR and used a one‐up one‐down adaptive procedure with a 2‐dB step size. The background noise level was fixed, and the SNR changed adaptively according to whether the previous sequence was repeated correctly. Each formal condition consisted of 24 digit sequences. The SRT was calculated as the mean SNR of the final 20 sequences and represents the SNR at which ≈50% of digit sequences are repeated correctly; higher SRT values indicate poorer speech‐in‐noise performance. A sequence was scored as correct only when all digits in the sequence were reproduced accurately and in the required order.

In forward‐recall conditions, participants repeated the digits in the presented order; in backward‐recall conditions, they repeated the digits in reverse order. The community stage used the 3‐digit forward condition as a brief hearing‐screening test. At the hearing clinic, participants completed 2‐digit forward, 3‐digit forward, 5‐digit forward, and 3‐digit backward conditions in that fixed order. There was no practice list before the forward conditions. Immediately before the 3‐digit backward condition, a researcher asked each participant to complete three backward‐recall practice items administered through the custom test platform with gradually increasing difficulty to ensure that the participant understood the reverse‐order response requirement.

Conceptual basis for iDIN screening indicators

The iDIN cognitive‐screening rationale depends on how an adaptive SRT is interpreted. In each condition, the adaptive procedure estimates the SNR at which the participant can correctly repeat approximately half of the digit sequences under the specified task rule. The SRT is therefore a task‐level threshold, not a direct measure of either hearing or cognition alone. A poorer SRT can occur because the acoustic signal is less accessible; because the task places greater demands on attention, encoding, storage, or manipulation; or because both processes are limiting performance.

We conceptualized each iDIN condition as containing two partially separable demand components. The first component is digit recognition in noise. This component includes audibility, speech‐in‐noise perception, attention to the acoustic stream, and encoding of the heard digits. It is hearing‐dependent and is explained by effortful‐listening frameworks such as the Ease of Language Understanding model and the Framework for Understanding Effortful Listening. 27 , 28 , 29 The second component is recall of the recognized digit sequence. Forward recall mainly requires serial reproduction, whereas backward recall requires maintaining and manipulating the sequence in reverse order. 30

Low‐demand forward conditions were not assumed to be cognition‐free. Even 2‐digit and 3‐digit forward SRTs require attention, auditory recognition, short‐term retention, and an oral response. The key assumption is instead a resource‐sufficiency assumption: for most older adults, including many with screen‐positive cognitive vulnerability, the cognitive demand of short forward digit repetition is usually below available cognitive capacity. Under this condition, variation in SRT should be governed primarily by auditory access and speech‐in‐noise recognition. When sequence length or recall manipulation increases task demand beyond available cognitive resources, the adaptive SRT can become poorer even when auditory function is unchanged.

This framework distinguishes same‐length backward‐forward contrasts from length‐based contrasts. SRT3b‐3 compares 3‐digit backward recall with 3‐digit forward recall. Because both conditions use the same digit length, masker, oral response mode, scoring rule, and adaptive SRT framework, the contrast is intended to reduce shared digit‐recognition‐in‐noise and audibility demands and emphasize the added cost of reverse‐order recall. The contrast is not interpreted as a pure cognition measure; hearing influence is reduced, not eliminated. By contrast, SRT5‐2 and SRT5‐3 compare different digit lengths and therefore combine recall demand with additional digit‐recognition‐in‐noise demand. Because recognition load may increase disproportionately as digit sequences become longer, length‐based contrasts may retain stronger associations with hearing level.

Audiometric reference standard

Pure‐tone audiometry was completed by clinical audiologists in a sound‐treated booth using standard clinical procedures and calibrated clinical equipment. Air‐conduction thresholds were obtained for each ear at standard audiometric frequencies. The present analyses used the four‐frequency pure‐tone average (PTA4), defined as the average of air‐conduction thresholds at 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz. Better‐ear PTA4 was defined as the lower PTA4 across ears.

The primary hearing reference was better‐ear PTA4 >35 dB hearing level (HL), consistent with the World Health Organization (WHO) adult disabling‐hearing‐loss threshold. 1 , 31 Better‐ear PTA4 >25 dB HL was analyzed as an any‐hearing‐loss sensitivity definition. Thirty‐two linked clinic participants had better‐ear PTA4 >65 dB HL. They were retained in the primary analyses because severe hearing loss is part of the target older‐adult screening population, and the influence of severe hearing loss was evaluated in sensitivity analyses excluding these participants.

HK‐MoCA cognitive reference standard

Clinic cognitive assessment used the Hong Kong version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (or HK‐MoCA). HK‐MoCA administrators had completed the official MoCA online certification and administered the test according to standardized instructions. MoCA was originally developed as a brief screen for MCI, and the Hong Kong version has local validity and cutoff evidence. 10 , 11 , 12 , 14

To reduce the influence of hearing loss on verbally administered cognitive assessment, all participants completing the HK‐MoCA used a personal sound‐amplification device (Pocketalker‐type amplifier) during administration. The device was used throughout verbally administered HK‐MoCA instructions and spoken test items. This procedure was intended to improve audibility and reduce the likelihood that poor hearing might lower HK‐MoCA performance.

The HK‐MoCA domain sum was calculated from visuospatial/executive, naming, attention, language, abstraction, delayed recall, and orientation subdomains. Clock drawing was additionally analyzed as a separate task score. The primary cognitive‐screening outcome used the locally validated HK‐MoCA education adjustment: one point added for participants with 6 years of education or less, capped at 30, with adjusted score <22 corresponding to the 21/22 cutoff for cognitive impairment/MCI screening in Hong Kong Chinese older adults. Adjusted score <19 was treated only as an exploratory lower‐score range rather than a clinical dementia diagnosis.

Assessment masking and order

Examiners were not formally blinded to prior screening or assessment results because the study was implemented as a staged community‐to‐clinic screening pathway rather than as a fully blinded diagnostic‐accuracy experiment. Measurement variability was reduced by using objective audiometric thresholds obtained in a sound‐treated booth, automated adaptive iDIN SNR changes, a prespecified rule requiring all digits in a sequence to be correct, certified HK‐MoCA administration, and Pocketalker‐supported verbal instructions during HK‐MoCA testing. These procedures did not eliminate all potential expectation bias, but they limited tester discretion in the main index‐test and reference‐standard measurements.

The fixed clinic iDIN order was chosen to approximate the intended future community screening workflow, in which a short forward condition would first screen hearing and provide an audibility anchor before backward recall is used for cognitive screening. The order was not randomized because the intended use case is a staged screening sequence rather than an experimental comparison of counterbalanced conditions.

iDIN screening indicators

Forward iDIN SRTs were interpreted primarily as hearing‐screening indicators because they estimate the SNR required to identify digit sequences. The primary hearing‐screening indicators were the 2‐digit forward SRT and 3‐digit forward SRT. The 5‐digit forward condition was interpreted as increasing sequence‐length recognition and memory load, whereas the 3‐digit backward condition was interpreted as increasing recall manipulation while holding digit length constant relative to the 3‐digit forward condition. This distinction motivated separate reporting of forward SRT hearing‐screening accuracy and SRT difference‐score cognitive‐screening accuracy.

The abbreviation system was prespecified for reporting. SRT2 denotes the 2‐digit forward SRT, SRT3 denotes the 3‐digit forward SRT, SRT3b denotes the 3‐digit backward‐recall SRT, and SRT5 denotes the 5‐digit forward SRT. Difference‐score labels indicate subtraction: SRT3b‐3 = SRT3b minus SRT3, SRT3b‐2 = SRT3b minus SRT2, SRT5‐2 = SRT5 minus SRT2, and SRT5‐3 = SRT5 minus SRT3.

Cognitive‐screening indicators were calculated from SRT differences. The primary indicators were SRT3b‐3 and SRT3b‐2. Two length‐based SRT difference scores, SRT5‐2 and SRT5‐3, were analyzed as secondary cognitive‐load indicators. Participants unable to complete the 3‐digit backward condition were retained as unable/missing and, in the screening‐style sensitivity analysis, assigned worse‐than‐worst observed backward SRT difference‐score values.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were planned and reported with attention to the Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) principles for diagnostic‐accuracy studies, including transparent description of participants, index tests, reference standards, thresholds, and applicability limits. 32 , 33 Analyses were performed using reproducible Python scripts (Python 3.12 with pandas and NumPy). Descriptive statistics are reported as mean (SD), median (interquartile range [IQR]), range, or n (%), as appropriate. Unless otherwise stated, uncertainty is reported using 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Areas under the curve (AUCs) were calculated using rank‐based Mann–Whitney formulations, and bootstrap CIs and paired AUC differences used nonparametric percentile resampling with fixed random seeds. Primary tables used 1000 bootstrap resamples unless otherwise stated. All p values are two‐sided and are reported exactly unless p < 0.001.

Community‐to‐clinic SRT3 agreement was summarized using paired mean difference with 95% CI, median difference, mean absolute difference, Spearman correlation, cutoff classification agreement, and Bland–Altman limits of agreement. The original community cutoff was evaluated as an operational referral threshold rather than as a definitive diagnostic boundary.

Hearing‐screening accuracy was estimated in the linked clinic verification subset using receiver operator characteristic (ROC) AUC with 95% CI, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and Youden index. Candidate cutoffs were internally derived by maximizing the Youden index in the verified clinic subset unless a prespecified operational cutoff was being evaluated. Sensitivity and specificity CIs were estimated for the main PTA4 >35 dB HL table. PTA4 >25 dB HL was used as a sensitivity definition. Clinic SRT2 and SRT3 were compared using paired bootstrap AUC differences to address the hypothesis that 2‐digit testing might reduce cognitive demand while preserving hearing‐screening accuracy.

The primary cognitive‐screening analysis compared AUCs with 95% CIs for HK‐MoCA adjusted <22 across SRT3b‐3, SRT3b‐2, SRT5‐2, and SRT5‐3. Complete‐case data were used for analyses requiring observed continuous scores. Backward unable/missing records were analyzed two ways: numeric‐only analyses used participants with observed backward SRT values, whereas screening‐style analyses retained unable/missing participants as worse‐than‐worst performance because inability to complete the backward condition is clinically meaningful in a cognitive screen. No other imputation was performed.

Spearman correlations were calculated between iDIN cognitive‐load SRT difference scores and HK‐MoCA adjusted total, MoCA subdomains, and clock drawing to examine convergent and domain‐level cognitive associations. For the displayed correlation matrix, significance markers were based on Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment across the 24 displayed Spearman correlations and are reported as q‐value thresholds. Partial correlations adjusted for PTA4, age, and education were used to evaluate whether each iDIN cognitive‐load SRT difference score retained cognitive information beyond hearing level and core demographic covariates.

For hearing‐screening indicators, education effects were evaluated by comparing ROC AUCs, candidate cutoffs, sensitivity, and specificity across education strata. PTA4 was not included as an adjustment covariate in these education‐stratified hearing‐screening analyses because PTA4 was the reference standard, and conditioning on the reference standard would change the meaning of diagnostic‐accuracy estimation. In a complementary descriptive model, community SRT3, clinic SRT2, and clinic SRT3 were regressed on age, sex, education, and raw HK‐MoCA score; PTA4 was not included in this model because the forward SRTs were intended to represent PTA‐defined hearing status.

Verification enrichment was examined because only a subset of community‐screened participants entered clinic testing. The linked clinic cohort was compared with the community‐only cohort using standardized mean differences for age, sex, community SRT3, and exceeding the original community cutoff. An inverse‐probability‐weighted sensitivity analysis reweighted the verified clinic subset toward the observed community cohort using age, sex, and community SRT3 as verification predictors.

Additional sensitivity analyses examined exclusion of participants with better‐ear PTA4 >65 dB HL, exclusion of extreme forward SRT values, lower HK‐MoCA score ranges, MoCA‐stratified hearing‐screening performance, bootstrap stability of candidate cutoffs, subgroup robustness by sex, age, education, and HK‐MoCA status, and the clinical profile of participants unable to complete 3‐digit backward testing. No formal multiplicity adjustment was applied to secondary and sensitivity analyses; these analyses were interpreted as supportive and hypothesis‐generating. All AUCs and cutoffs are interpreted as internally estimated values requiring external validation.

RESULTS

Cohort flow and baseline characteristics

The community cohort included 962 participants 55 years of age or older with valid age, sex, and community 3‐digit forward SRT (SRT3) data. Mean age was 75.5 years (SD, 8.5; median, 75; IQR, 70–81), and 744 participants (77.3%) were female. Community SRT3 had a mean of –4.43 dB SNR (SD, 6.36) and a median of –6.65 dB SNR (IQR, –8.40 to –3.00).

The linked clinic analysis cohort included 495 participants, and 467 participants remained in the community‐only cohort without complete clinic follow‐up. The linked clinic cohort had poorer community SRT3 than the community‐only cohort (–3.37 vs –5.55 dB SNR; standardized mean difference [SMD], 0.35) and was more likely to exceed the original community cutoff (85.3% vs 43.0%; SMD, 0.98).

Within the linked clinic cohort, better‐ear PTA4 averaged 41.0 dB HL (SD, 15.4), and median education was 6 years (IQR, 2–10). Overall, 402 participants (81.2%) met PTA4 >25 dB HL and 294 (59.4%) met PTA4 >35 dB HL. HK‐MoCA adjusted score averaged 21.6 (SD, 6.3), 195 participants (39.4%) screened positive using adjusted <22, 51 (10.3%) were in the adjusted score band 19–21, and 144 (29.1%) were in the lower‐score range <19. Community screening yield and verification enrichment

At the original community cutoff of SRT >–7.7 dB SNR, 623 of 962 community participants (64.8%) exceeded the referral threshold. The number exceeding the threshold decreased as the criterion was shifted upward: 536 participants (55.7%) at >–7.2 dB SNR, 481 (50.0%) at >–6.7 dB SNR, and 400 (41.6%) at >–5.8 dB SNR. These results show how referral volume would change under different operational policies.

Among the 495 linked clinic participants, 422 (85.3%) had exceeded the original community cutoff, compared with 201 of 467 community‐only participants (43.0%). Thus, the clinic cohort was intentionally verification‐enriched rather than population‐representative. Verification‐enrichment and inverse‐probability weighting checks are reported in Tables S1 and S2 and Figure S1.

Community‐to‐clinic agreement

Among the 495 linked participants, community SRT3 was on average poorer than clinic SRT3. Mean community SRT3 was –3.37 dB SNR compared with –4.77 dB SNR at the hearing clinic, yielding a mean paired difference of 1.40 dB SNR (95% CI, 0.98–1.82). The paired association was moderate to strong (Spearman rs = 0.700; p < 0.001; Figure 3B). The mean absolute difference was 3.34 dB SNR; 49.1% of paired values were within 2 dB SNR, 65.3% within 3 dB SNR, and 79.4% within 5 dB SNR. Bland–Altman limits of agreement were –7.92 to 10.71 dB SNR (Figure 3C), indicating meaningful individual‐level variability.

These paired results are best interpreted as cross‐setting agreement rather than test–retest reliability, because the community and clinic administrations differed in setting, administrator training and experience, environmental control, and likely participant motivation. The mean shift and wide limits of agreement support the community test as a first‐stage triage tool, but they do not support treating the community and clinic SRT values as interchangeable for individual diagnostic decisions.

At the original –7.7 dB SNR cutoff, community and clinic SRT3 classifications agreed in 347 participants (70.1%): 289 exceeded the cutoff in both settings, 58 were below the cutoff in both settings, 133 exceeded the cutoff only in the community setting, and 15 exceeded the cutoff only in the clinic setting.

Hearing‐screening performance

For the primary hearing reference of better‐ear PTA4 >35 dB HL, community SRT3 showed good discrimination (AUC, 0.800; 95% CI, 0.760–0.839). In this community implementation, the clinic‐derived original cutoff >–7.7 dB SNR was sensitive (95.6%) but had limited specificity (29.9%), consistent with a first‐stage referral rule in a less controlled outreach environment. The candidate sensitivity‐prioritized cutoff >–7.2 dB SNR maintained high sensitivity (91.8%) with improved specificity (48.3%), whereas the internally balanced Youden cutoff >–5.8 dB SNR had a sensitivity of 75.2% and specificity of 73.1%.

In the hearing clinic, SRT2 and SRT3 had similar hearing‐screening performance for PTA4 >35 dB HL. Clinic SRT2 had AUC 0.878 (95% CI, 0.848–0.906), and clinic SRT3 had AUC 0.874 (95% CI, 0.843–0.902). The paired AUC difference for SRT2 minus SRT3 was 0.004 (95% CI, –0.018 to 0.026). Results were similar in MoCA‐stratified analyses and in lower HK‐MoCA score ranges, giving no evidence that SRT3 was materially more cognitively affected than SRT2 for hearing screening.

Using PTA4 >25 dB HL as an any‐hearing‐loss definition increased prevalence to 81.2% but did not change the overall ranking of the hearing‐screening tests. Community SRT3 AUC was 0.801, clinic SRT2 AUC was 0.894, and clinic SRT3 AUC was 0.908. Predictive values changed because most linked clinic participants met the less stringent definition; detailed PTA4 >25 dB HL threshold performance is reported in Table S3 and Figure S2.

MoCA‐stratified hearing‐screening operating characteristics for HK‐MoCA adjusted <22 are reported in Table S4.

Continuous associations with better‐ear PTA4 supported the interpretation of forward SRTs as hearing‐screening measures. Community SRT3 was moderately associated with PTA4 (Spearman rs = 0.617; p < 0.001), whereas clinic SRT2 and SRT3 showed stronger associations (Spearman rs = 0.784 and 0.789, respectively; both p’s < 0.001).

Education‐stratified analyses did not suggest that SRT3 was more education‐dependent than SRT2. For PTA4 >35 dB HL, clinic SRT2 and SRT3 had similar AUCs in both education strata, and community SRT3 had similar AUCs across education strata (Tables S5 and S6).

Cognitive screening with iDIN

The central cognitive‐screening question was whether memory‐loaded iDIN SRT difference scores discriminated HK‐MoCA screen‐positive status beyond the participant's hearing level. The primary backward‐recall indicators, SRT3b‐3 and SRT3b‐2, outperformed the forward length‐based indicators SRT5‐2 and SRT5‐3 for HK‐MoCA adjusted <22.

Observed numeric 3‐digit backward‐recall SRT values were available for 476 participants. For 19 participants, no valid numeric backward‐recall SRT was available because they were unable to complete the condition or a valid adaptive threshold could not be obtained. This unable/worst group had lower HK‐MoCA adjusted scores (mean, 10.6 vs 22.1), a much higher HK‐MoCA screen‐positive rate (94.7% vs 37.2%), and poorer hearing (better‐ear PTA4 mean, 48.0 vs 40.7 dB HL), supporting their retention as a clinically meaningful unable/worst category in screening analyses.

Among participants with observed numeric values, backward‐recall SRT difference scores were larger and more variable than forward length‐based SRT difference scores. SRT3b‐3 had a mean of 5.00 dB SNR (SD, 7.43; median, 2.20; IQR, 0.00–8.20), and SRT3b‐2 had a mean of 4.28 dB SNR (SD, 7.41; median, 1.50; IQR, –0.45 to 7.25). Forward length‐based scores were smaller: SRT5‐2 had a mean of 1.64 dB SNR (SD, 3.99; median, 0.80), and SRT5‐3 had a mean of 2.40 dB SNR (SD, 3.90; median, 1.40).

For HK‐MoCA adjusted <22, backward‐recall SRT difference scores outperformed forward length‐based SRT difference scores. With backward unable/missing treated as worst, SRT3b‐3 had AUC 0.807 (95% CI, 0.760–0.850), and SRT3b‐2 had AUC 0.800 (95% CI, 0.755–0.842). Forward length‐based scores were lower: SRT5‐2 AUC 0.626 and SRT5‐3 AUC 0.676. The AUC difference between SRT3b‐3 and SRT3b‐2 was small (0.007; 95% CI, –0.014 to 0.031), whereas both backward‐recall scores had higher AUCs than SRT5‐2 and SRT5‐3.

The numeric‐only analysis produced the same ranking with slightly lower AUCs because the unable/missing group was excluded: SRT3b‐3 AUC 0.790 (95% CI, 0.741–0.839) and SRT3b‐2 AUC 0.783 (95% CI, 0.737–0.830). Pairwise comparisons in the screening‐style analysis confirmed higher AUCs for backward‐recall SRT difference scores than for length‐based SRT difference scores; detailed cognitive‐marker results are reported in Tables S7 through S10. Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted after the primary hearing and cognitive analyses. Defining hearing loss as PTA4 >25 dB HL increased the apparent hearing‐loss burden from 59.4% to 81.2%, but the relative performance pattern remained stable: clinic SRT2 and SRT3 had the highest AUCs among evaluated hearing‐screening conditions, and community SRT3 remained suitable as a first‐stage referral test.

MoCA‐stratified analyses did not show evidence that SRT3 was more cognitively affected than SRT2. Among participants with HK‐MoCA adjusted <22, AUCs for PTA4 >35 dB HL were 0.880 for clinic SRT2 and 0.873 for clinic SRT3. Among participants with HK‐MoCA adjusted ≥22, the corresponding AUCs were 0.872 and 0.870. Lower HK‐MoCA score‐range checks led to the same interpretation; within HK‐MoCA adjusted <19, clinic SRT2 had AUC 0.856, and clinic SRT3 had AUC 0.838, with a paired difference of 0.019 (95% CI, –0.029 to 0.074).

Descriptive model‐based checks that did not adjust for PTA4 were interpreted cautiously because forward SRTs are designed to represent PTA‐defined hearing status. These models supported the same practical interpretation as the stratified ROC analyses: education‐related variation in SRT2 and SRT3 was small and did not translate into materially different hearing‐screening accuracy.

Verification analyses confirmed that the clinic subset was enriched for poorer community SRT and for exceeding the original community cutoff, as expected in a staged referral pathway. Inverse‐probability weighting toward the observed community cohort did not materially alter the community SRT3 pattern: the unweighted AUC for PTA4 >35 dB HL was 0.800 and the weighted AUC was 0.800. Bootstrap analyses suggested stable AUCs but wider uncertainty in exact optimal cutoffs, supporting presentation of candidate thresholds as locally derived rather than universal values.

Additional robustness checks, including exclusion of the 32 participants with better‐ear PTA4 >65 dB HL, exclusion of extreme forward SRT values, subgroup analyses by sex, age, education, and HK‐MoCA status, and lower HK‐MoCA score‐range analyses did not materially alter the primary interpretation. Additional supporting results, including lower‐score‐range hearing‐screening checks, the backward unable/missing profile, and forward SRT‐PTA4 correlation estimates, are provided in Tables S11 through S13 and Figures S3 through S5.

DISCUSSION

Principal findings

The key contribution of this study is that iDIN separated hearing characterization from cognitive‐load screening within the same digit‐in‐noise task family. Forward SRT2 and SRT3 characterized PTA4‐defined hearing loss with comparable accuracy, including among participants with HK‐MoCA screen‐positive status and lower HK‐MoCA score ranges. Backward‐recall SRT difference scores, especially SRT3b‐3, discriminated HK‐MoCA screen‐positive status and retained a cognitive association after adjustment for PTA4, age, and education. iDIN should therefore be viewed as an audibility‐anchored screening architecture, not a stand‐alone diagnostic test.

This architecture addresses a central measurement problem in dementia early‐detection pathways. Hearing loss and cognitive decline frequently coexist, and each can compromise assessment of the other: spoken cognitive tests can underestimate cognition when audibility is poor, whereas speech‐in‐noise tests can become partly cognitive if they require excessive memory, response selection, or technology use. iDIN makes these demands explicit. Short forward conditions characterize speech‐in‐noise audibility, and backward recall adds defined manipulation demand to a similar digit‐recognition task. Same‐length SRT3b‐3 may, therefore, reduce recognition and audibility contributions, whereas length‐based contrasts retain hearing‐related recognition‐load differences. This distinction matters because poor performance on a difficult iDIN condition is ambiguous unless recognition load and recall manipulation are separated analytically.

The community findings are best interpreted as pathway evidence rather than population‐prevalence evidence. Screening was directed toward older adults interested in hearing screening or further assessment, and clinic attendance was enriched for participants exceeding the original community cutoff. This resembles outreach practice, but the accuracy estimates and candidate thresholds require external validation before use as population‐level performance estimates or universal referral criteria.

Relation to prior work

Conventional DIN tests are established hearing‐screening tools because they use simple closed‐set digits, can be automated, and have been deployed through telephone, smartphone, and online platforms. 21 , 23 , 24 Prior Cantonese iDIN work suggests that simple digit materials remain useful across language‐exposure and accent‐related variation, supporting scalable screening. 25 , 26 Recent work has begun to test whether this logic extends to people with cognitive impairment. In a Thai memory‐clinic study of older adults with MCI or dementia, a smartphone‐based 3‐digit DIN test showed high sensitivity but modest accuracy for moderate hearing loss (AUC, 0.746) and a weak SRT‐PTA association. 34 That result may partly reflect self‐administered touchscreen demands beyond listening. The present oral‐response design is lower demand, and forward 2‐ and 3‐digit SRTs retained hearing‐screening performance in HK‐MoCA screen‐positive and lower‐score subgroups.

The cognitive findings build on the preliminary iDIN dual‐screening study, which proposed SRT3b‐3 as a candidate cognitive‐screening marker but was limited by sample size. 20 The present larger linked cohort replicated and clarified that pattern. Backward‐recall SRT difference scores outperformed 5‐digit forward length‐based scores, were more strongly related to HK‐MoCA total and relevant domains, and showed negligible direct association with PTA4. This is theoretically coherent. Five‐digit forward recall is difficult partly because longer sequences increase digit‐recognition demand in noise, a hearing‐dependent component that may rise disproportionately with sequence length. Three‐digit backward recall adds recall manipulation while keeping sequence length short. The negligible PTA4 association for SRT3b‐3 supports the interpretation that subtracting SRT3 reduces the recognition component; the similar SRT3b‐2 pattern may reflect the low and similar demands of SRT2 and SRT3.

Implications for dementia early detection

For dementia early‐detection research, the value of iDIN is not that it restricts cognitive screening to people with hearing loss, but that it places hearing and cognitive screening in the same brief pathway for older adults. This framing aligns with dementia‐prevention evidence emphasizing hearing loss as a prevention‐relevant risk factor, including attributable‐fraction analyses and the Aging and Cognitive Health Evaluation in Elders (ACHIEVE) trial. 8 , 9 , 35 A universal older‐adult pathway is important because hearing loss, cognitive vulnerability, communication barriers, and cognitive‐test bias can intersect before a person reaches a memory clinic.

Clinically, iDIN should be positioned as a front‐end triage tool rather than as a replacement for MoCA, neuropsychological assessment, or clinical diagnosis. A positive cognitive‐load iDIN result would indicate that the person may need fuller cognitive screening, such as HK‐MoCA or another locally validated multidomain instrument, and, when indicated, specialist diagnostic assessment. A positive forward iDIN result would indicate the need for audiologic follow‐up. The proposed role is therefore pre‐MoCA and pre‐specialist triage in community or outreach settings, where the practical question is who should be referred for more complete hearing, cognitive, or integrated assessment.

The screening advantages of iDIN are central to this pathway. Digits are simple speech materials, require limited semantic and educational knowledge, and can be administered quickly. The task first estimates speech‐in‐noise audibility and then, through longer or backward recall in noise, estimates the added cognitive cost of storage, updating, and manipulation of degraded auditory information. These properties reduce language and administration burden and may support deployment by trained non‐audiologist personnel, especially in low‐literacy or low‐education settings where conventional cognitive items may partly measure schooling exposure.

A practical pathway can use community 3‐digit DIN as a short triage step, with thresholds locally calibrated according to referral capacity and public‐health objective. The present community stage used only 3‐digit forward DIN to minimize outreach burden and because the best iDIN cognitive‐screening combination had not yet been established. The next implementation step is a streamlined community version, likely 3‐digit forward followed by 3‐digit backward iDIN, that could provide paired hearing and cognitive screening in ≈5 min. In clinic or follow‐up settings, 2‐digit and 3‐digit forward SRTs appear comparably suitable for hearing characterization, while backward‐recall difference scores provide the more informative cognitive‐screening signal.

Future implementation should preserve accessibility for people with cognitive impairment. Self‐administered iDIN may suit lower‐risk screening, but keypad or touchscreen input may add cognitive, motor, and technology‐use demands. Oral‐response iDIN may be preferable when suspected MCI or dementia makes manual input less suitable. Automated speech recognition could retain oral responding while reducing staffing burden; early automated speech‐audiometry and proof‐of‐concept DIN systems support this direction, although Cantonese‐speaking older adults require direct validation. 36 , 37 Large‐language‐model and multimodal artificial‐intelligence systems may support instructions, quality checks, adaptive prompting, and referral summaries, but should remain operational aids until prospectively validated. 38

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include the linked community‐to‐clinic design, formal audiometric reference testing, multiple iDIN conditions, HK‐MoCA domain‐level data, and explicit comparison of hearing and cognitive screening targets. These features allowed forward digit SRTs to be evaluated as hearing‐screening measures and backward‐recall or length‐based SRT difference scores as candidate cognitive‐screening measures anchored to audibility.

The most important limitation is the cognitive reference standard. HK‐MoCA is itself a cognitive screening test, not a clinical diagnosis of MCI or dementia. The present study therefore evaluates whether iDIN discriminates HK‐MoCA screen‐positive status but cannot establish diagnostic accuracy for clinically adjudicated MCI or dementia. Future studies should involve geriatric cognitive or memory‐clinic teams and use standard clinical diagnostic procedures, ideally supplemented by longitudinal cognitive outcomes and biomarkers when available.

A second limitation is selection and verification enrichment. Only approximately half of the community cohort had complete clinic assessment, and clinic participants were selected partly because they exceeded the original community cutoff or requested clinic follow‐up. This reflects the intended staged screening pathway but means that clinic diagnostic‐accuracy estimates describe the verified subset rather than the full screened community cohort.

Several implementation features also limit causal or condition‐level interpretation. Community testing was less controlled than clinic testing, so community SRTs may reflect environmental and administration variability as well as participant hearing and cognition. Examiners were not formally blinded, and clinic iDIN conditions were completed in a fixed order to approximate the intended workflow rather than as a counterbalanced experiment. Cutoffs are internally derived and should be presented as candidate thresholds requiring external validation, consistent with diagnostic‐accuracy reporting guidance. 32 , 33

The study used tester‐scored oral responses rather than self‐testing or automatic speech recognition, so fully automated iDIN, including AI‐assisted response scoring, remains to be validated in Cantonese‐speaking older adults and clinically diagnosed MCI or dementia cohorts.

Conclusion

A staged iDIN pathway may support integrated hearing and cognitive screening in older adults by separating forward hearing characterization from backward cognitive‐load screening. Community 3‐digit DIN is best framed as an outreach referral tool requiring local calibration. In clinic testing, backward‐recall SRT difference scores provided the most consistent HK‐MoCA screen‐positive signal with minimal direct dependence on PTA4. This audibility‐anchored architecture warrants validation against clinical diagnosis, longitudinal dementia‐related outcomes, and automated response‐scoring workflows.
