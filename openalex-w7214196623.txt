BATS, VECTORS AND EMERGING DISEASES: A ONE HEALTH PERSPECTIVE
Danilo Alves de França1
Nássarah Jabur Lot Rodrigues2
Artur Bibiano de Vasconcelos3
Helio Langoni4
ABSTRACT: This review explores the fundamental role of bats as reservoirs of infectious agents,
emphasizing the One Health approach to understanding and mitigating the emergence of diseases. Bats
harbor several zoonotic pathogens and facilitate their transmission between humans, domestic animals
and wildlife. The study highlights the interconnectedness of ecosystems and the evolutionary dynamics
of pathogens, highlighting factors such as habitat loss, climate change and human behavior that
contribute to disease spread events. Bats, with their ancient evolutionary history and vast geographic
distribution, serve as long-standing reservoirs for infectious agents around the world, posing risks when
pathogens jump to new hosts. In addition, the review discusses the fundamental role of vectors
associated with bats such as ticks, fleas and mosquitoes, and presents the main microorganisms detected
in bats to date. Strategies to mitigate disease cycles without harming bat populations are crucial,
considering their ecological importance and the complexity of wildlife-related disease dynamics. In
conclusion, understanding the intricate relationships between bats and the emergence of diseases is
essential for effective public health strategies. Preserving the habitat and life of bats, and at the same
time monitoring the first signs of disease in these animals, can help prevent future outbreaks and protect
human health.
Keywords: Spillover. wildlife reservoirs. Zoonoses. emerging diseases emergence.

INTRODUCTION

Zoonoses are diseases that can be transmitted between animals and humans. Many
diseases now known to be restricted to humans have already been studied and identified as
having an animal origin. This is the case with AIDS, Ebola and numerous viral diseases that
cause flu, such as influenza. Thinking of living beings as a large group reaffirms the Darwinian
logic that humanity is a type of animal intrinsically linked to other animals, in its origin, in
health and in disease (Quammen 2012).
With advances in the field of molecular biology, it has been possible to see through
analysis of the genetic profile of microorganisms and the development of phylogenetic trees
that infectious diseases have appeared and reappeared over periods of time, and such
resurgences are motivated by a wide range of influences, such as: gene adaptation of the agent,
polymicrobial diseases, susceptibility of the human host, demographic aspects and human
behavior, cross-border trade and travel, wars and bioterrorism, occupational exposure,
inappropriate use of antimicrobials, climate change, land use, poverty and social inequality,
absence of public health services, animal populations, and famine. Around sixty to eighty
percent of recent infections in humans have originated from animals, with a greater emphasis
on rodents and bats (Morens and Fauci 2013).
Infectious disease is a kind of natural mortar that connects one animal to another within
the elaborate biophysical buildings we call ecosystems. Pathogens are like predators that eat
their prey, being as natural as a lion attacking a zebra. Just as predators have their usual prey,
their preferred targets, so do pathogens. And just as a lion can occasionally stray from its
natural behavior and attack a cow that has invaded its habitat, a pathogen can also switch to a
new target host. Circumstances change and, with them, so do the demands and opportunities.
When a pathogen moves from a non-human animal to a person and manages to establish itself
there, we have what we call spillover and the emergence of a zoonosis (Quammen 2012).
This process has occurred in the history of countless diseases, including some
pandemics, such as the plague, Spanish flu, monkey pox, bovine tuberculosis, Nile fever, rabies,
hantavirus, COVID-19, among others. Their animal origins were diverse, ranging from wild
pigs and waterfowl to non-human primates and bats, as well as their territorial origins, passing
through America and Eurasia as well as Africa and Oceania. This form of interspecies jumping
is common, not rare. Around sixty percent of emerging infectious diseases affecting humans
are zoonotic, originating in animals, and more than two thirds of them originate in wildlife.

According to the International Livestock Research Institute, two million people are killed each
year by diseases that spread to humans from wild and domestic animals (Robbins 2012). Some
diseases can be more complex to combat than others, such as yellow fever, which not only has
non-human primates and humans involved in the epidemiological chain, but also mosquitoes,
which can give rise to countless new types of new species that are sources of infection for the
agent, since controlling mosquitoes is extremely more difficult than controlling wild or
domestic outbreaks of disease (Morens and Fauci 2013).
Microorganisms often "hide" in what we call a reservoir host. A reservoir is a living
organism that carries the pathogen, chronically harboring it while suffering little or no disease.
In situations where infectious agents no longer seem to occur in certain territories, they may
actually be residing in a place where biological diversity is high and the ecosystem is relatively
undisturbed. When ecological disturbance occurs, these agents reveal themselves (Shaheen et
al. 2022). Emerging diseases consist of pathogens present in a territory that have mutated,
resulting in variants that behave differently in the new target. An example of this is influenza,
which mutates regularly, leading to the need for annual vaccine updates (Morens and Fauci
2013).
This understanding of how zoonoses arise and how spillover occurs enables international
agencies to make progress in combating new diseases with pandemic and epidemic potential,
identifying them in places before they even reach humans and spread (Shaheen et al. 2022).
While it has been feasible to eradicate some infectious diseases and effectively control many
others in recent decades, it seems unlikely that we will be able to eliminate the vast majority of
emerging infectious diseases in the near future. This is due to the fact that pathogenic
microorganisms have the ability to undergo rapid genetic changes, resulting in new phenotypic
characteristics that take advantage of changes in the host and the available environmental
conditions (Robbins 2012).
The advantages conferred by microorganisms can only be tackled and overcome through
rigorous surveillance, continuous and dedicated research, as well as the rapid development and
implementation of countermeasures, such as monitoring tools, diagnostics, drugs and vaccines.
Identifying, diagnosing, sequencing and typing new microorganisms in new locations are
important tools in the fight against new outbreaks. The fight against emerging infectious
diseases needs to be a constant effort (Morens and Fauci 2013).

THE IMPORTANCE OF WILDLIFE IN THE EMERGENCE OF INFECTIOUS
DISEASES

Diseases have historically originated in forests and wildlife, before spreading to human
communities. Two notable examples are plague and malária. Experts claim that in the last fifty
years, the number of emerging diseases has increased fourfold, largely due to the constant
expansion of areas inhabited by humans into animal habitats, especially in tropical regions
identified as hotspots for the spread of disease around the world. In addition, with global
mobility facilitated by modern air travel and the illicit wildlife trade, there is significant
potential for serious outbreaks to occur in densely populated areas (Quammen 2012).
Changes in ecosystems, such as deforestation, urbanization, climate change and
environmental degradation, can lead to an increase in encounters between wildlife and humans.
This can result in an increased risk of infectious disease transmission. Wildlife, due to its wide
genetic diversity, can be a favorable environment for the mutation and evolution of pathogens,
and these genetic changes can give rise to more virulent or more transmissible variants that
pose a threat to global health. The detection of diseases in wild animals can serve as an early
warning indicator of the presence of dangerous pathogens. This can provide valuable
information for the prevention and control of infectious disease outbreaks in humans. Wildlife
conservation is intrinsically linked to human health, and therefore maintaining healthy
ecosystems and biodiversity can reduce the risk of these diseases emerging (Daszak et al. 2000).
The dynamics of wildlife populations and their seasonal migrations or long-distance
movements can play an important role in the spread of pathogens (Shaheen et al. 2022). For
example, the migration of birds can transport pathogens from one region to another, or the
migration of marine mammals to coastal areas, contributing to the geographical dispersal of
diseases. Habitat degradation and deforestation often lead to greater interaction between
wildlife, domestic animals and humans, increasing the risk of infectious disease transmission.
As humans expand into previously untouched areas, more frequent encounters with wildlife
can occur, increasing the chances of exposure to pathogens. This same exposure can occur in
recurring ecotourism habits or the migration of indigenous peoples. The illegal wildlife trade
is also a significant concern, as it can introduce pathogens to new regions (Quammen 2012).
According to experts, the key to predicting and preventing the next pandemic lies in
understanding the protective effects provided by untouched nature. An example of this is
evidenced in the Amazon, where a study revealed that an increase of approximately four

Ecological imbalance undoubtedly leads to the emergence of new infection conditions,

Many of the pathogens that move between wildlife, domestic animals and humans have
existed in wild animals for a long period of time (Daszak et al. 2000). Originally, these
pathogens did not necessarily cause disease, they evolved together with their natural hosts over
millions of years (Shaheen et al. 2022). Over this time, they have established a kind of
equilibrium, replicating slowly and steadily, passing discreetly through the host population.
This has allowed them to ensure long-term security by avoiding short-term success in the form
of maximum replication in each host individual. This strategy has been successful, however,
when humans disturb this balance, whether by invading host populations, hunting for meat,
displacing or destroying ecosystems, their actions increase the risk, providing opportunities for
these pathogens to jump from their natural hosts to new hosts. These new hosts can be any
animal, but are often humans, due to their intrusive and abundant presence in environments,
offering a rich place of opportunity for these pathogens (Quammen 2012).
The study of diseases in wild animals is fundamental to understanding and preventing
the emergence of infectious diseases. Research into the ecology of pathogens and the dynamics
of wildlife populations can lead to more effective prevention and control strategies. According
to experts, the most effective approach to preventing future outbreaks in humans is the socalled One Health approach. This is a global program involving a community of more than six

The order Chiroptera is the most diverse and geographically distributed mammal taxon.
It has been proven that bats are part of our ancient native fauna. Fossil records from
Queensland, Australia, show that small bats have existed there for at least 55 million years,
while human presence is much more recent, dating back only tens of millennia (Quammen
2012).
Bats are considered to be ancient reservoirs of infectious diseases. As animals with an
evolutionary history spanning millions of years, they have undergone a process of coevolution
with various microorganisms, making them resistant to most of the diseases they cause. This
is different from what happens when these microorganisms reach species that did not evolve
with them, humans for example, and the impact will undoubtedly be severe since this new host
does not have specific immune defenses to fight them (Quammen 2012). An example of this
negative impact was what happened in Malaysia with the emergence of the henipahvirus,
which infected fruit bats, and at some point, due to the constant contact of domestic pigs with
the saliva of bats present in fallen fruit from trees, the virus jumped to a new species, and then
reached humans, causing 106 deaths from neurological and disabling disorders in the rural
region of the country (Chua et al. 2003). Something similar happened with the hendravirus in
Australia, but in an urban environment. With urbanization, bats changed their habitat in search
of food, and began to transmit the virus to horses and humans, with a mortality rate of
approximately 70% (Field et al. 2010).
SARS-CoV-2 is also believed to have arisen in this way, through interaction between
humans, pigs and bats in China, however more studies are needed as SARS viruses are complex
in their natural history. Two other epidemics, one in China and one in Saudi Arabia, were
caused by coronaviruses that closely resemble SARS-CoV-2, SARS-CoV and MERS-CoV.
SARS emerged in Guangdong province in southern China and was marked by fever, headache
and the subsequent appearance of respiratory symptoms, including cough, dyspnea and
pneumonia. The disease caused by this virus was highly transmissible between humans,
spreading throughout China and to 28 other countries, causing 774 fatal cases of the disease.
MERS emerged as a respiratory syndrome similar to that caused by SARS, and although less

originating from bats (Hu et al. 2015).
The transmission of microorganisms from bats to humans and other animals requires
the alignment of various factors, including the ecological, epidemiological and behavioral
determinants of exposure to pathogens, and human factors that can affect susceptibility to
infection. It is believed, for example, that urban bats have a greater potential to transmit
diseases than other bats, because they tend to stay in the same place and experience a reduction
in regular exposure to microorganisms that they used to acquire in the wild. This previous
exposure helped keep infection to a minimum. As a result, more bats, due to factors such as
poor nutrition, loss of habitat and other elements, end up being infected and consequently
spread a greater amount of microorganisms in urban areas (França et al. 2024, França et al. 2025a,
França et al. 2025b, França et al. 2025c, França et al. 2025d). Another example is feeding habits,
as hematophagous bats are very likely to transmit viruses through their saliva, such as the rabies
virus, while insectivorous bats are more likely to carry infectious agents transmitted by
arthropods. In addition, the physiology of bats, evolved for flight conditions, allows for a
compatible and favorable blood regulation for the development and maintenance of several
families of pathogens (Altringham et al. 2011).

The flight abilities of this mammal influence the occurrence of new diseases not only
physiologically, but also geographically, as they are able to fly long distances in search of food,
depending on the relief conditions of the region, and consequently act as a migratory agent.
Bats are known for their exceptional flying abilities, and their flight capacity can vary
depending on the species. On average, most bat species can fly distances ranging from 20 to 50
kilometers in a single night. Some migratory bat species can fly much further in their seasonal
migrations. A notable example is the free-tailed bat, Tadarida brasiliensis, which is widely found
in the Americas. This species is known for its long migrations and can fly hundreds of
kilometers in a single night during its spring and autumn migrations. In some regions, these
bats have been recorded flying up to 1,600 kilometers in seasonal migrations (Altringham et al.
2011). Some bats, such as Desmodus rotundus, are known to be hematophagous and transmit
rabies to livestock and river-dwelling populations. Because they feed exceptionally on blood,
they are much less resistant to fasting, due to the absence of pancreatic β-cells that is
characteristic of these types of bat. This absence prevents the bat's liver from producing
glycogen reserves and means that it has to search for food every day, making its contact with
other animals even greater, and also increasing the chances of interaction between animals and
new pathogens. The potential to fly long distances, migratory habits, gregarious habits and
longevity make them a vehicle with more potential for transmitting zoonotic viruses than other
animals (Di Tosto et al. 2006).
The relationship between bats and the maintenance of infectious diseases in populations
is a complex topic that requires a comprehensive One Health approach (Quammen 2012). Bats
play several roles in this context, because in addition to acting as reservoirs and amplifiers of
infectious diseases, they play a fundamental role for the ecosystem of the places they inhabit.
One of the most important roles of bats is in seed dispersal, contributing to biodiversity and
the restoration of ecosystems. Two other fundamental roles are in the biological control of
insects and small birds and in pollination, where these types of animals are needed in certain
places to prevent pests from appearing and green areas from shrinking (Altringham et al. 2011).
Environmental disturbances such as deforestation, climate change, dam construction
and urbanization of forest areas can lead to bat migration and closer interaction with humans
and domestic animals, increasing the risk of disease transmission and genetic mutation
processes. Natural changes in the environment, as well as those produced by human activities,

THE ROLE OF VECTORS IN CONTACT WITH BATS

The importance of bat vectors in disease transmission is a topic of great relevance in
public health research and epidemiology. Vectors, such as ticks, fleas and mosquitoes, play a
key role in spreading pathogens from bats to other animals, including humans. Vectors can
transport bat pathogens to distant areas, extending the geography of diseases. This can be
particularly relevant in contexts of climate change, which can expand the distribution areas of
vectors.
Ticks (Ixodoidea)

Ticks are arthropods that necessarily feed on blood. They are found all over the world
and comprise more than nine hundred species known to parasitize terrestrial vertebrates. The
evolutionary history of this group dates back around a hundred million years, when there was
widespread genetic diversification and dispersal across territories. Ticks are divided into two
main categories: hard ticks, represented by the Ixodidae family, which is the most numerous,
and soft ticks, belonging to the Argasidae family (Sándor et al. 2021).
Bats, on the other hand, have a wide geographical distribution, a great diversity of
species and also show various evolutionary adaptations, making them ideal hosts for the
parasitism of these arthropods. Soft ticks, although a minority, have a worldwide distribution
with a greater focus on tropical regions, and are related to most human diseases. In addition,
they are highly resistant to extreme fasting and are easier to transfer pathogens to other
animals, making them great potential vectors. Some other points favor this parasitism, such as
morphological adaptations for flight, making it difficult for the bat to eliminate ticks; resting

no detections of Bartonella, Borrelia and C. burnetii. However, although these agents have not
been described in ticks in Brazil, this interaction is not excluded, since there are almost no
studies with this focus of investigation and since bats have been shown to harbor these agents
in their blood and organs (Tahir et al. 2016).
Fleas (Siphonaptera)

Fleas are obligate hematophagous parasites, most abundant and diverse in small and
medium-sized mammals, with more than two thousand species currently known. Most flea
species parasitize rodents, but they are not host-specific and generally exploit other groups of
mammals, digesting the blood of bats very well. On the other hand, bats are often parasitized
by fleas from the Ischnopsyllidae family, which do not exploit hosts other than bats. Thus, bats
are seen as potential targets for fleas in general. This means that, although there are no reports
of bats infested by cat fleas, this is possible, especially in urban environments (Krasnov et al.
2007).

intermedius fleas, respectively. In the United States, Sternopsylla texanus infest bats, and a
genotype of Bartonella has already been described (Stuckey et al. 2017). A large-scale
investigation on the territory of Russia identified several species of the Ischnopsyllidae family
in bats (Orlova et al. 2022). Flea parasitism of prevalent bat species, as well as the presence of

Mosquitoes (Culicidae)

Mosquitoes are hematophagous insects that are widely recognized for their importance
in both natural ecosystems and human health. When a mosquito feeds on an infected bat, it
can acquire the microorganism and subsequently transmit it to other animals or humans. There
are thousands of species around the world, each with specific adaptations to its environment
and life cycle. Tropical regions tend to have a greater diversity of this type of insect (Lampo et
al. 2000).
Studies have reviewed cases in the literature in which it is possible to identify
mosquitoes that have fed on bat blood. However, there is still little information on the primary
mosquito vectors that feed on bats. Some flaviviruses, such as dengue virus (DENV), Koyose
virus (YOKV), Tamana virus (TABV), Rio Bravo virus (RBV), Japanese encephalitis virus
(JEV) and West Nile virus (WNV), have already been detected in bats, but viremia rates and
their role in pathogen transmission are still poorly defined. These infections are believed to
have occurred due to transmission by mosquito vectors, which play an important role in
spreading these viruses among bats and potentially to other species. In relation to
phlebotomines, the vectors of Leishmaniasis, bats prove to be a natural source of food for them,
and this is because they share the same habitats in caves and shelters, in addition to bats being
seen as excellent reservoirs of trypanosomatid (Lampo et al. 2000).

ONE HEALTH APPROACH TO MITIGATE THE RISKS OF PATHOGEN
SPILLOVER

The One Health approach requires integrated strategies that consider human, animal
and environmental health in an interconnected way. Innovative solutions can combine
technological advances, real-time monitoring and community engagement to prevent future
outbreaks.
A central proposal is the use of monitoring systems based on artificial intelligence and
machine learning to detect emerging patterns of interaction between bats, their pathogens and
the environment. Ecological sensors placed in bat habitats could collect data on movement,
population density and the general health of the animals, while algorithms analyze this data in
real time to predict areas at high risk of overflow. This technology could be integrated into
national surveillance programs, providing early warnings about ecological changes that favor
the emergence of diseases (Davenport and Kalakota 2019).
Another solution is to develop ecological buffer zones in areas at high risk of contact
between humans and bats, such as degraded forests and peri-urban regions. These zones,
planned on the basis of epidemiological risk maps, could include planting fruit tree species that
attract bats to locations far from human populations, reducing the likelihood of direct contact.
At the same time, these areas could serve as controlled spaces for studying the dynamics of
pathogens in bats, with systematic collection of biological samples (Davenport and Kalakota
2019).
In addition, community-based educational interventions can be powerful tools for
reducing risky interactions. Environmental education programs involving local communities
can help deconstruct myths about bats and foster safe behaviors, such as avoiding the
consumption of bushmeat or contact with bat colonies. These actions could be strengthened by

In the field of biotechnology, research into preventive zoonotic vaccines aimed at
pathogens with pandemic potential found in bats, such as the Nipah virus or coronavirus,
should be expanded. Such vaccines could be applied selectively to populations of animals at
risk, reducing their viral load and limiting the likelihood of spillover. This approach requires
international and ethical collaboration, respecting biodiversity and avoiding negative impacts
on ecosystems (Anthony et al. 2013).
One promising strategy involves the creation of regional biobanks of biological samples
from bats and their associated ecosystems. These biobanks would allow for the systematic
storage of samples of bats' blood, tissues and ectoparasites, as well as environmental samples
such as soil and water from their habitats. In parallel, microbiome studies could identify
microbial patterns that indicate the state of health of bat populations and possible changes
associated with the emergence of zoonotic pathogens. This approach would allow continuous
monitoring of microbial diversity and early identification of imbalances that could favor
spillover. In addition, biobanks would be crucial resources for future investigations of outbreaks
and the development of countermeasures, such as vaccines and therapies (Anthony et al. 2013).
Engaging local communities in participatory surveillance efforts can be a powerful tool
for mitigating risks. Through the use of mobile apps and digital platforms, residents of areas
close to bat habitats could report observations of colonies, behavioral changes, bat mortality or
the emergence of new urban shelters. These tools could be gamified to encourage continued
use, offering virtual or educational rewards for the most engaged users. The data collected
would be analyzed by systems based on artificial intelligence, allowing for dynamic and
collaborative risk mapping. This approach not only increases vigilance, but also raises
awareness and empowers communities as active agents in disease prevention (Newman et al.
2012).
Finally, it is essential to implement integrated and collaborative governance, connecting
researchers, health professionals, environmental agencies and local communities. Global One
Health programs could benefit from the creation of specialized regional hubs, where data and
solutions can be shared in an agile and effective manner, aligned with global zoonosis
surveillance and response strategies (Newman et al. 2012).

ERADICATE NO KILLING: ETHICAL STRATEGIES TO MITIGATE THE
DISEASES

It is important to note that most bat species do not pose a significant threat to public
health and play an important role in balancing the ecosystem. In general, they have a
remarkable diversity of feeding habits and play important ecological roles (Mikich and
Bianconi 2005).
Some families and species specialize in different types of food. Some families, such as
Vespertilionidae and Molossidae, consist of bats that feed exclusively on insects, playing a
crucial role in controlling insect populations and contributing to pest regulation. Bats of the
Phyllostomidae family exhibit a wide diversity in their diets, some are frugivorous, while
others are nectarivorous,. These bats play an essential role in seed dispersal and pollination of
various plant species, contributing to the regeneration of forested areas (Reis 2007).
There are only three species of hematophagous bats, and all of them are found in
neotropical regions, including Brazil. They feed on the blood of vertebrates and act to control
the populations of their prey, a role that is also important for ecological balance. Some bats
hunt small vertebrates, such as rodents, birds, amphibians, reptiles and even other bats, and are
therefore recognized as carnivorous bats. An example of a carnivorous bat is the Chrotopterus
auritus, which plays the same population control role as hematophagous bats. Bats from the
Noctilionidae family, such as the fishing bat Noctilio leporinus, are piscivores and have a diet
consisting mainly of fish and other marine animals. They play an important role in the ecology
of aquatic environments (Reis 2007).
Popular myths make people see bats as dangerous pests to our health, and this has led to
the death of several important species. Much of this is due to the alarming period that the world
experienced with rabies in the 1990s and the impact that bats still have on the occurrence of
human cases described in recent years. In Brazil, for example, there are currently around 178
species of bat and only three feed on blood, and yet the idea still prevails that all bats are
vampires and capable of transmitting rabies, even though most hematophagous bats live in
rural environments near livestock farms and are a minority among bats. University outreach
and scientific discussion about the role of bats in the occurrence of infectious diseases should
be done with caution and responsibility. In addition to bat hunting, forest destruction has also
had an impact on the decline of bat populations and their migration to urban areas. Of course,
most species adapted to the urban environment migrate for food and favorable conditions for

Non-destructive strategies for managing bat populations are essential to mitigate
zoonotic disease risks while preserving their ecological roles. These approaches can be adapted
to various contexts, including urban and rural environments. One effective strategy is physical
exclusion, which involves identifying entry and exit points in buildings and using barriers such
as nets or exclusion devices to prevent bats from accessing these spaces. Modifying habitats by
removing nearby food sources, such as fruit trees, and dense vegetation can discourage bats
from settling in urban areas. Similarly, increasing lighting in roosting areas can deter bats, as
they prefer darker environments (Kotait 2003).
Providing alternative shelters, such as installing nest boxes in appropriate locations,
offers a humane solution by redirecting bats to safer areas. Collaboration with wildlife experts
or environmental agencies is vital to ensure best practices and compliance with region-specific
regulations. Additionally, sealing cracks and openings in buildings, once bats have vacated, can
prevent their return. In some cases, sound and visual repellents, such as high-frequency devices
or flashing lights, can be used to discourage bats, although these methods may vary in
effectiveness depending on the species. Public education plays a critical role in fostering
coexistence, raising awareness about bats’ ecological importance, and promoting non-lethal
management practices. Continuous monitoring of implemented measures is necessary to adapt
strategies as required, ensuring long-term success in managing bat populations ethically and
sustainably (Kotait 2003).
Population control must be carried out with sensitivity and respect for the role of these
animals in the ecosystem. Seeking guidance from qualified professionals is essential to ensure
that the practices adopted comply with laws and ethical standards.
