Prevalence of depression, anxiety and post-traumatic stress disorder among international medical students from conflict zones versus non-conflict areas: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Manar M Ellaban, Sally M Bashandy, Ziad Y El-Yamanay, Reham O Alebiary, Salma S Osman, Yosra S Abd El-Ghaffar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInternational medical students face elevated risks of depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD; characterized by re-experiencing, avoidance, and a sense of threat per ICD-11), and complex PTSD (C-PTSD; encompassing core PTSD symptoms alongside disturbances in self-organization, including emotion dysregulation, negative self-concept, and relationship difficulties). This study aims to determine the prevalence of these conditions among students at Ain Shams University (n = 310) and to compare those from conflict-affected regions with those from non-conflict regions.MethodsThis analytical cross-sectional survey (October 2024–January 2025) employed the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress 21 Scale (DASS-21) and the International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsStudents experienced a high symptom burden: 36.5% had extremely severe depression, 42.3% had extremely severe anxiety, and 18.4% had extremely severe stress. PTSD was identified in 18.4% of students, while C-PTSD was identified in 21.5%. Students from conflict-affected regions exhibited significantly higher rates across all outcomes compared with non-conflict peers (depression, anxiety, stress, PTSD, and C-PTSD: all p < 0.05).ConclusionInternational medical students, particularly from conflict-affected areas, exhibit substantial mental health problems. The prevalence of C-PTSD (21.5%) exceeded that of PTSD (18.4%), underscoring chronic trauma impact. Targeted interventions—trauma-informed counseling, peer support, and resilience training—are urgently needed. Findings provide baseline data for the development of culturally sensitive mental health programs at Ain Shams University and similar institutions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42506-026-00224-3.Keywords: Ain Shams University, Depression, Anxiety, PTSD, CPTSD, International medical students, Conflict-affected areas, Mental health

Introduction

Depression, anxiety, stress, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and complex PTSD (C-PTSD) are significant mental health challenges among international medical students, posing serious obstacles to their academic achievement and emotional well-being [1].

Depression is characterized by a persistent low mood and loss of interest in previously enjoyable activities, and according to DSM-5 and ICD-11, its diagnosis requires a constellation of symptoms such as diminished pleasure, appetite or weight changes, sleep disturbances, psychomotor changes, fatigue, impaired concentration, guilt or worthlessness, and recurrent thoughts of death. Anxiety involves excessive and persistent worry or fear; in ICD-11, generalized anxiety disorder is defined by uncontrollable worry across multiple life domains, heightened apprehension, and associated physical symptoms, including restlessness and agitation. Anxiety disorders impair students’ ability to study and perform on assessments by inducing severe fear or worry. Stress, on the other hand, refers to a state of psychological or emotional strain arising from demanding circumstances; although it can precipitate or exacerbate mental disorders, it is not classified as a standalone psychiatric condition in either DSM-5 or ICD-11, both of which conceptualize stress as an external factor contributing to other disorders [2–6].

Medical students in particular commonly manifest depression and anxiety through a combination of emotional, cognitive, and somatic symptoms driven by intense academic pressure, prolonged working hours, and lifestyle changes. Emotionally, they may experience persistent sadness, irritability, and anxiety. Cognitively, they may struggle with concentration and negative thought patterns. Somatically, these disorders may present as physical complaints like fatigue and sleep disturbances [7, 8].

PTSD is a condition that is marked by challenges with emotional regulation, consciousness, and self-perception and is brought on by experiencing or witnessing traumatic events, as defined by the American Psychiatric Association (2013). C-PTSD results from prolonged or repeated trauma and is characterized by intrusive memories, avoidance behaviors, and altered emotional responses [2, 9]. Studies highlight the heightened susceptibility of international medical students to both disorders [10].

The mental health consequences faced by individuals residing in conflict zones are well-documented in the literature. These include elevated vulnerability to anxiety, depression, and PTSD, among other forms of psychological distress [11, 12]. The ICD-11 distinguishes PTSD from C-PTSD, identifying the latter as a separate condition defined by the core PTSD symptom clusters alongside additional disturbances in emotion regulation, self-concept, and interpersonal functioning that typically arise from sustained or repeated trauma. Evidence shows that C-PTSD is especially prevalent among individuals exposed to chronic conflict, forced displacement, or prolonged violence—experiences that closely reflect the backgrounds of many international students from war-affected regions. Research further indicates that populations enduring continuous conflict exhibit higher rates of C-PTSD compared with PTSD alone [13, 14]. Burnout among international medical students is a significant concern; studies show that they often face higher risks of burnout due to factors such as social isolation from family and the pressure to maintain academic performance and professionalism [15].

Experiences of violence, loss, and displacement can leave people with psychological wounds that inflict lasting psychological harm that affects individuals’ long-term functioning within their communities [16, 17]. The demanding nature of medical training compounds these difficulties and may exacerbate existing mental health problems [1, 18]. According to Kartal et al., international medical students from war-torn areas may experience distinct stressors, including cultural adjustment, discrimination, and the challenge of adapting to a new academic environment while managing the aftereffects of trauma [19]. Moreover, the stigma surrounding mental health disorders may deter individuals from seeking care, leaving many without adequate support [20, 21]. International medical students represent a high-risk group due to academic demands, cross-cultural adjustment, and prior exposure to conflict and displacement, making them a population well suited for the assessment of trauma-related and stress-related disorders [22].

Many studies suggest that symptoms of depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress are widespread among university students. A global review 2021 reported pooled prevalence estimates of mild depressive symptoms of 35.4% and severe depression of 13.4%; for anxiety, mild symptoms were 40.2% and severe anxiety were 16.8%; and for post-traumatic stress symptoms, a pooled prevalence of 25.1% was identified [23]. Medical students, exposed to heightened academic and clinical stress, show even higher rates, with 45% experiencing anxiety and 48% depression. International or migrant students face additional acculturative and social stressors, resulting in mental health burdens that can reach 56–60%, highlighting their heightened vulnerability compared with other student populations [24, 25].

Exposure to armed conflict poses serious risks to the mental health and educational prospects of students. Systematic reviews among adolescents living in conflict or post-conflict settings report high pooled prevalence of psychiatric disorders, including PTSD (47%), depression (43%), and anxiety (27%)—substantially higher than in non-conflict populations [26]. In higher education settings, recent data from conflict-affected regions reveal that students exposed to war report markedly elevated symptoms of depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress—often accompanied by disrupted sleep, diminished motivation, and decreased academic performance. Together, these findings suggest that war exposure not only heightens mental health burden but also undermines learning outcomes, underlining the urgent need for trauma-informed support strategies for students from conflict zones [26, 27].

Recent studies among war-affected university students confirm these patterns. Guo et al. [28] in China reported a high prevalence of depression symptoms (36–48%) among college students from socioeconomic stressors, while Haq et al. linked war-related PTSD to major depressive episodes [28, 29]. Urra-López and Araneda-Flores found strong conflict-depression correlations, and a study from Banha University (2022) documented elevated anxiety (45%) among Egyptian medical students from social/academic pressures [30, 31]. Kaur et al. showed that violence-exposed students had 41 % extreme anxiety/stress, moderated by resilience [32]. Among conflict-displaced populations, the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) (2023) highlights displacement patterns in Sudan and Yemen consistent with the nationality distribution observed in our sample, in which Sudanese students constituted the majority (51.3%) [33]. A study conducted in Gaza reported elevated levels of depression and anxiety among conflict-affected populations [27]. An association between C-PTSD and psychosis has been reported among conflict-affected populations [34, 35]. A Syrian refugee study has demonstrated a correlation between conflict intensity and PTSD prevalence, with rates of 27% for PTSD and 36% for C-PTSD reported among those exposed to high-intensity conflict [36]. Furthermore, a systematic review and meta-analysis of populations exposed to mass conflict and displacement documented long-term associations between traumatic events and post-traumatic mental health disorders [12].

The current study aims to address this gap in the existing literature by specifically focusing on depression, anxiety, and stress among international medical students from countries suffering from wars or conflicts. Moreover, it aims to determine how often post-traumatic stress disorder occurs among these students. By identifying the magnitude of these issues, this research will examine and compare the burden of depression, anxiety, stress, PTSD, and C-PTSD among international medical students from conflict-affected versus non-conflict regions to inform evidence-based mental health interventions tailored to this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study design and setting

This analytical cross-sectional study was carried out at the Faculty of Medicine, Ain Shams University, located in the Cairo Governorate, Egypt, which is one of the largest medical schools in Africa and the Arab world. According to the official faculty website for the 2024–2025 academic year, the number of undergraduate students exceeds 8,000 [37]. This substantial student body reflects the faculty’s capacity to provide comprehensive medical education and training to a diverse and large group of future healthcare professionals.

Study participants

The research was carried out from October 2024 to January 2025. Inclusion criteria included international students enrolled at Ain Shams University’s Faculty of Medicine from all academic years (the first three preclinical years, the subsequent two clinical years (4th and 5th years), and the last two training years as house officers). Until the necessary sample size was attained, all international students of both sexes who agreed to take part in our study and fulfilled the inclusion requirements were recruited.

Participants were recruited via university email lists and in-person announcements in the classes. After excluding 20 students who did not meet the eligibility criteria, a total of 325 students were invited to participate in the study; 15 declined, resulting in 310 completed surveys (response rate: 95%). Students were excluded from participation if they were currently receiving psychiatric inpatient care or intensive outpatient treatment at the time of recruitment, as these circumstances could significantly influence survey responses. To ensure adequate exposure to the academic environment relevant to the study, students who had resided in Egypt for less than one month were excluded. Students who were on academic leave or not actively enrolled during the data collection period were also excluded, as were those who had previously participated in a similar mental health survey within the past six months.

In this research, countries of origin were classified into four categories based on conflict exposure using the Armed Conflict Location and Event Data (ACLED) Conflict Index: extreme conflict, high conflict, turbulent, and no conflict. This index compiles systematic data on political violence and conflict events worldwide. While primarily designed for political and humanitarian research, previous studies have applied ACLED data in psychological research to assess associations between conflict exposure and mental health outcomes in refugee and displaced populations [38, 39].

The ACLED Conflict Index ranks countries based on four criteria: deadliness (political violence fatalities), danger to civilians, geographic spread of violence, and armed group fragmentation, which collectively assess each nation’s conflict risk and intensity. From recent ACLED data, countries like Sudan, Palestine, Syria, and Nigeria qualified as “extreme conflict zones” because they met all indicators. Countries like Yemen, Iraq, India, South Sudan, Afghanistan, and Bangladesh were ranked under “high conflict areas,” meaning there was considerable violence, however, at slightly lower levels than the most intense areas. Nations such as Chad and Indonesia were categorized as “turbulent areas,” reflecting periods of limited but substantial conflict risk regions. The others included Jordan, Saudi Arabia, Oman, Djibouti, Bahrain, Tunisia, and Eritrea, which were referred to as “no conflict” because they did not reach the required benchmark of enduring political violence within the timeframe set by the ACLED Index for this study. This categorization allowed a systematic assessment of mental health outcomes in relation to varying levels of conflict exposure across students’ countries of origin [39].

With a 95% confidence level, a design effect (DE) of 2, and a margin of error of 8%, the sample size was determined using the 64% proportion of depression in a study on medical students at Ain Shams University’s Faculty of Medicine [40]. A sample size of at least 280 was required in accordance with these parameters. The OpenEpi software, version 7, was used to compute the sample.

Burnout was referenced conceptually in the introduction as contextual background for understanding medical student distress, but was not measured in this study, which focused on depression/anxiety/stress (DASS-21) and PTSD/C-PTSD (ITQ).

A pilot study was conducted with 20 international medical students to evaluate the survey instruments’ clarity, length, cultural relevance, and overall comprehensibility. Feedback indicated that several items in the DASS-21 and ITQ required minor wording adjustments to enhance understanding, and the survey layout was optimized for online completion. This pilot testing phase provided valuable insights that informed subsequent revisions. To prevent any bias and ensure the robustness of the study’s methodology, data collected during the pilot were not included in the main analyses.

Statistical analysis

IBM’s SPSS statistical program, version 26.0 for PC, was used to analyze the data. The K-S test was used to determine whether the data were normally distributed. Means ± standard deviations were used to present quantitative data. Numbers and percentages were used to report categorical data. Using a χ² test with two-tailed p < 0.05 as the cutoff value for statistically significant results, the proportions of students who experienced psychological distress (depression, anxiety, stress, and PTSD) were calculated, and then the factors most significantly associated with them were identified.

Results

More than half were females (54.2%) compared with males (45.8%). This distribution reflects a reasonably balanced representation across sexes. Approximately two-thirds of students (59.7%) resided in shared housing. A smaller fraction (22.9%) lived with family or relatives, while 12.6% lived alone. A small portion (4.8%) resided on campus, which could be a sign of limited on-campus availability or a preference for off-campus housing.

Depression levels by gender showed that the distributions of male and female students were comparable across all categories. A greater proportion of female students (41.7%) than male students (30.3%) fell into the extremely severe category. Conversely, a greater proportion of male students (21.8%) than female students (19.0%) were classified as normal. These differences were not statistically significant (χ² = 4.99, p = 0.29).

The table also presents the distribution of depression severity among students from nations with varied levels of conflict. revealing notable differences across conflict exposure categories. Students from nations with extreme levels of conflict had the highest proportion in the extremely severe category (41.0%), whereas those from nations with no conflict were more evenly distributed across severity levels. This difference was not statistically significant (χ² = 15.63, p = 0.21).

One important finding was observed among students who reported experiencing the negative effects of conflict. Of these students, nearly half (48.7%) fell into the category of extremely severe depression, compared with only 23.7% among those who did not report conflict-related harm. This difference is statistically significant (p = < 0.001), underscoring the significant impact that war-related trauma has on mental health.

Discussion

The results of this study provide a comprehensive picture of the prevalence of anxiety, depression, PTSD, and C-PTSD among Ain Shams University’s international medical students. The study demonstrates that students from conflict-affected areas experience significant mental health challenges, underscoring the severe effects of war-related trauma on their psychological well-being.

Summary of key findings (depression, anxiety, stress, PTSD/C-PTSD)

The sociodemographic characteristics of the students in this study offer important information about the diversity and composition of the student body. Female students (54.2%) outnumber male students (45.8%) in the gender distribution, suggesting a balanced representation that is consistent with international trends in higher education. Gender distributions have been similar in comparable research, such as Castillo and Reyes’ study, which revealed that gender representation in higher education has become more balanced [47].

Regarding living arrangements, the majority of students (59.7%) shared housing with other students, reflecting a strong preference for communal living. Only 4.8% of students live on campus, 12.6% live alone, and a smaller percentage (22.9%) live with family or relatives. Scarcity of on-campus housing may limit protective factors [48], while shared housing (59.7%) aligns with Yasmin, showing peer support buffers stress in medical students [49].

First-year students make up the largest group (35.2%), followed by third-year students (20.0%) and fourth-year students (18.7%), according to the distribution of educational levels. The decline in numbers in the upper years may be attributable to various factors, including academic attrition, transfers, or program completion [50]. This pattern is consistent with findings from other studies, including that of the Institute of Education Sciences (2008), which observed comparable trends in educational progression [47, 51]. Students’ smoking status reveals that the majority (78.1%) do not smoke, followed by current smokers (21.3%) and past smokers (0.6%). This relatively low smoking prevalence aligns with anti-smoking programs and global health trends [52]. Global Citizen (2023) reported similar findings, highlighting the effectiveness of anti-smoking campaigns in reducing smoking rates among students [52, 53]. With the largest percentages of Sudanese (32.9%), Palestinians (17.1%), and Indians (12.9%), the nationality data reflects the diverse composition of the student body. The inclusivity of the educational institution and the global movement of students are reflected in this diversity [53]. Similar research has highlighted the growing diversity of student populations, as demonstrated by Castillo and Reyes’ study. More than half (51.3%) of the students who reported experiencing the effects of conflict were Sudanese, followed by Palestinians (26.6%), Yemenis (13.3%), and Iraqis (5.1%) [47].

The results of this study demonstrate a high prevalence of depression, with 36.5% reporting extremely severe depression, followed by moderate depression (25.2%) and normal levels (20.3%). In terms of depression by gender, the percentage of female students who experienced extremely severe depression was higher (41.7%) than that of male students (30.3%). Female students reported higher levels of extremely severe anxiety (45.2%) than male students (38.7%) [29]. When comparing levels of stress by gender, female students had higher levels of extremely severe stress (22.6%) than male students (13.4%). The impact of war-related damage was significant, with 48.7% reporting extremely severe depression (vs. 23.7% without, p = 0.00, 49.4% extremely severe anxiety (vs. 34.9%), and 24.7% extremely severe stress (vs. 11.8%). Among those experiencing war-related harm, PTSD was diagnosed in 18.4% and C-PTSD in 21.5%, with symptoms including re-experiencing (55.1%), avoidance (61.1%), affective dysregulation (79.7%), and relationship disturbances (82.9%). War harm primarily affected psychological impairment (97.1%), lack of shelter (63.9%), death (56.3%), financial loss (30.4%), incarceration (22.8%), torture (21.5%), and physical impairment (13.3%). Affected people included friends (53.8%), first-degree relatives (41%), and participants themselves (23%).

Comparison with previous literature

Depression findings align with Guo et al. Who found equally high levels of depression among college students. According to that study, socioeconomic pressures and academic stress were major contributors to depression symptoms [28]. Research by Haq et al. Also demonstrates this trend, finding that female students had a tendency toward greater rates of depression attributed to hormonal changes and social expectations [29]. According to Guo et al. Female students frequently report higher levels of emotional distress, which may be a result of coping strategy variations [28]. In contrast, Urra-López and Araneda-Flores identified a strong correlation between conflict exposure and elevated levels of depression, indicating that environmental instability is a significant indicator of mental health deterioration [30]. This result corroborates the findings of Haq et al. Who found that one of the main causes of major depressive episodes in students was post-traumatic stress disorder related to war [29]. The psychological effects of war-related trauma were also emphasized by Urra-López and Araneda-Flores, who linked extended exposure to instability with long-term mental health consequences [30]. Similar to findings in other studies, a 2022 study of medical students at Benha University in Egypt found that female students scored significantly higher on anxiety than male students as a result of increased social pressure and academic strain [31]. These results are consistent with Kaur et al. Who found that students in areas with persistent violence had significantly higher anxiety levels attributable to constant stress and instability [32]. A study conducted in Pakistan similarly found that university students’ anxiety levels were significantly elevated by exposure to combat trauma [29]. According to research by Urra-López and Araneda-Flores, extended exposure to violent environments also increases the risk of developing severe anxiety and stress-related disorders [30].

When comparing levels of stress by gender, findings are in line with an earlier study conducted in 2022 in Banha [31]. A study conducted in India found that students exposed to violence and instability had significantly elevated stress levels [32]. Furthermore, a study conducted in Pakistan found that experiencing war trauma caused extreme stress reactions, which were frequently associated with PTSD. Haq [29]. This distribution is consistent with the patterns of violence globally, where nations like Yemen, Iraq, Sudan, and Palestine have experienced protracted conflicts and humanitarian crises. UNHCR notes that Yemen and Sudan rank among the nations with the greatest numbers of displaced people as a result of ongoing hostilities [54, 55].

A study on the psychological consequences of the war in Gaza revealed significant levels of depression and anxiety in affected populations [56]. Studies document long-term health consequences of conflict-related injuries [57, 58]. Torture and imprisonment are also common in conflict zones, with significant ethical, legal, and psychological implications [59, 60].

Students from conflict-affected regions may experience compounded trauma effects through shared narratives as conflict exposure directly contributes to elevated PTSD/C-PTSD rates (18.4% and 21.5%, respectively). The current findings align with the incidence of PTSD symptoms among first-degree relatives [61]. Research on Syrian refugees showed that the degree of conflict correlated with PTSD/C-PTSD severity [11]. The present study found that avoidance behaviors are significantly more common in regions with extreme conflict (61.6%) than in areas with high conflict (10.7%) and no conflict (0%). Nonetheless, there are variations in the frequency of specific symptoms. Compared to the current study’s findings of 79.7% overall, previous research, including studies that focused on the aftermath of the Iraq War, indicated lower rates of affective dysregulation [62]. Panayi et al. Demonstrated that complex PTSD symptoms predict positive symptoms of psychosis in high-conflict exposure [34]. Hyland et al. Presented a memory and identity theory of ICD-11 C-PTSD arising from repeated trauma [36]. A JAMA Network 2025 study found a high likelihood of anxiety, depression, and PTSD three years post-trauma [63].

Explanations for the findings (conflict exposure, gender, social factors)

While stress represents a normal physiological/psychological response to challenges that can enhance resilience when adaptive, trauma involves extreme events (e.g., war-related death, 56.3%, psychological impairment 97.1%) that overwhelm coping capacity, causing lasting functional disruptions manifested as PTSD (18.4%) and C-PTSD (21.5%). This distinction explains why students experiencing war-related damage showed significantly elevated ‘extremely severe stress’ (24.7% vs. 11.8% among those without war-related damage; χ² significant), reflecting trauma-induced pathology rather than routine academic stressors [63].

The gender-based differences in this study were not statistically significant, suggesting that although the pattern is consistent with previous research, other factors might influence these findings [28]. Gender trends (female higher depression/anxiety) attributable to hormones/social factors are moderated by non-significant χ² results, suggesting protective buffers such as shared housing (59.7%) providing peer support and a sense of community belonging—known to reduce psychological distress in healthcare students [28]. This explains why conflict-exposed females did not show significantly worse outcomes despite trauma exposure.

The variation in coping strategies or resilience among students from conflict-affected areas could be one explanation for this study’s lack of statistical significance. Students from extreme conflict nations showed 41.0% extremely severe depression. The pattern was apparent but not significant. Despite these apparent patterns, the study’s gender-based anxiety differences were not statistically significant, indicating that although gender might be a factor, other psychological and environmental factors might also influence the intensity of anxiety. The largest percentage of students with extremely severe anxiety (41.5%) came from nations with extreme conflicts.

Non-significant results suggest varying resilience and coping among conflict-exposed students. Individual coping strategies may reduce anxiety intensity despite conflict exposure [28]. This study’s gender-based variations in stress intensity were not statistically significant, suggesting that other factors—such as academic pressure and personal coping strategies—may have a greater impact on stress responses than gender alone. Students from countries experiencing acute conflict had the greatest proportion of extremely severe stress (21.0%). This study found no statistically significant difference in stress levels according to the level of conflict exposure, indicating that stress responses vary according to socioeconomic circumstances, individual resilience, and access to mental health services [28]. The high rate of psychological impairment and displacement emphasizes how urgently conflict-affected areas need housing solutions and mental health support. The findings also underscore the critical importance of addressing the financial, material, and legal consequences of conflict in order to support the recovery and resilience of affected populations.

There are some significant distinctions. The nature of combat and the lack of advanced medical care at the time often resulted in a more direct impact on participants in earlier research, such as studies on the Vietnam War [61, 64]. The current study demonstrates a reduced percentage of direct impact on participants, which could be explained by the availability of better medical interventions and support networks in modern wars. Prescott investigated distinctions between traumatic events related to combat and civilian life and how they affect PTSD symptoms. PTSD symptom intensity and type are greatly influenced by the context and nature of war-related incidents [65]. Previous research has repeatedly demonstrated that the degree of conflict is correlated with the severity of PTSD and C-PTSD symptoms. People from high-conflict areas were more likely to exhibit avoidance and re-experiencing behaviors [66]. There are variations in the frequency of specific symptoms. Compared to the current study’s findings of 79.7% overall, previous research indicated lower rates of affective dysregulation. Differences in the types of conflicts and the accessibility of mental health support services could be the cause of this discrepancy. Furthermore, compared to earlier studies, the current study reveals a higher prevalence of relationship disturbances (82.9%), which may reflect the protracted nature of modern conflicts and their effects on social structures.

Contrary to expectation, students from high-conflict areas demonstrated higher rates of PTSD and C-PTSD compared to those from extreme-conflict areas. This unexpected pattern may be explained by several factors. Students from extreme-conflict areas may have developed greater psychological resilience through prolonged exposure to adversity, or may have benefited from stronger community and social support networks. Furthermore, the small sample size in the high-conflict subgroup (n = 11) limits the reliability of these percentages and may have contributed to this unexpected result. This finding should therefore be interpreted with caution and warrants further investigation in future studies with larger sample sizes. Some nationality-based subgroup analyses were based on very small cell frequencies, which limits the reliability of the derived percentages and the validity of the statistical comparisons. These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Previous research had demonstrated a relationship between the intensity of conflict and the prevalence of PTSD and C-PTSD [67]. Those who lived in high-conflict areas had higher rates of PTSD (27.3%) and C-PTSD (36.4%) than people who lived in extreme conflict areas (18.1% and 20.8%, respectively) or no conflict areas (0%). There are variations in the frequency of specific diagnoses. Compared to the current study’s findings of 21.5% overall, previous research [26]indicated lower rates of C-PTSD. Furthermore, contrary to some earlier research [28]suggesting higher PTSD rates among females, the current study reveals a higher prevalence of PTSD diagnoses among males (24.2%) compared with females (14.1%). According to this interpretation, there may be diagnostic variability as a result of the functional independence between PTSD and symptoms of disturbances in self-organization (DSO), which could account for the differing prevalence rates seen in various conflict contexts [28]. This study emphasizes how trauma can have long-term effects on mental health, which could explain why various war settings have differing rates of PTSD and C-PTSD diagnoses [28]. By physically damaging schools, displacing students and educators, and producing psychological stress that impairs learning, war and conflict disrupt education. The negative effects of conflict on education and the significance of resilience-building techniques are also highlighted in studies by Concern Worldwide 2023 and Lunov and Rozhkova [49, 50]. Overall, the sociodemographic attributes demonstrate the study’s diversity and resilience. Findings highlight the significance of offering adequate resources and support to meet the diverse needs of students, especially those affected by conflict.

Implications for medical education and mental health interventions

Overall, the study emphasizes how critical it is to provide targeted mental health interventions and support networks for international medical students, especially those from areas affected by conflict. The study also provides essential baseline data for universities to develop evidence-based mental health support programs, including targeted services for students from conflict-affected regions and early screening protocols for high-risk students. Given the high rates of anxiety, depression, PTSD, and C-PTSD among these students, their specific psychological needs must be met. To improve this population’s well-being and academic performance, future research should focus on developing comprehensive mental health programs that include cultural sensitivity, resilience-building techniques, and readily accessible support services.

Study limitations and future directions

Despite its strengths, it is important to recognize the study’s limitations. The use of self-reported data could introduce response bias (social desirability, recall inaccuracy) and compromise the accuracy of findings, while reliance on screening tools reflects symptom burden and probable diagnoses rather than definitive clinical assessments derived from clinician-administered interviews. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causality between conflict exposure and mental health outcomes, as temporal relationships cannot be established. Findings may not generalize to all international medical students, particularly those from more diverse educational or cultural backgrounds.

Additionally, cultural differences in mental health stigma, particularly pronounced among students from collectivistic backgrounds (e.g., Sudanese, Palestinian, and Indian), may have led to underreporting of symptoms, potentially affecting the accuracy of the findings. We also acknowledge the potential for sampling bias, as students experiencing higher psychological distress may have been more likely to participate (self-selection bias). Furthermore, the timeframe of questionnaire items (past week or month) and the influence of stigma may have affected recall accuracy and willingness to disclose mental health symptoms. Moreover, some nationality-based subgroup analyses were based on very small cell frequencies, which limits the reliability of the derived percentages and the validity of the statistical comparisons. These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution.

These limitations highlight the need for future longitudinal research tracking the effects of conflict exposure over time with validated diagnostic tools, larger and more diverse samples, and intervention trials to confirm causality and test tailored programs, such as trauma-informed counseling and peer mentoring for conflict-affected students. Future studies should specifically examine symptom trajectories and chronic C-PTSD progression, conduct deeper gender analyses exploring female students’ higher depression rates and protective factors such as shared housing, and evaluate randomized intervention trials of resilience training and culturally adapted programs for Sudanese, Palestinian, and similar cohorts.

Conclusions

International medical students at Ain Shams University, especially those from conflict-affected areas, exhibit significant rates of depression, anxiety, PTSD, and C-PTSD. These results demonstrate the substantial psychological strain these students endure and the necessity of targeted mental health interventions. Educational institutions can better promote the academic achievement and general well-being of international students by addressing the specific difficulties arising from trauma associated with conflict.

Several recommendations for enhancing the mental health support for international medical students can be offered in light of the study’s findings. First and foremost, it is critical to create targeted mental health programs that cater to the specific needs of students from areas affected by conflict. Comprehensive support frameworks that are accessible and culturally sensitive should be incorporated into these programs. Second, improving mental health services’ accessibility is essential to promoting their use by international students. Thirdly, students can be supported in managing stress and trauma by being trained in resilience-building techniques through seminars and workshops. Finally, to better understand the long-term effects of conflict exposure on mental health and academic achievement, future research should prioritize longitudinal studies.
