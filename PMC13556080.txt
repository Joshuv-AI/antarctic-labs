Ecosystem-matched reprogramming of the tumor–immune microenvironment to overcome cancer immunotherapy resistance
Authors: Xiaodong Wang, Qianqian Wang, Wei Gao, Songjiang He, Di Xiong, Yiping Huang, Bingwen Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune checkpoint blockade has redefined cancer therapy, yet durable responses remain constrained by resistance states that arise from coordinated tumor-cell, immune, stromal, vascular, lymphatic, and metabolic programs rather than from a single defective pathway. This review develops a translational framework in which tumor-intrinsic immune invisibility, myeloid and regulatory lymphocyte suppression, defective dendritic-cell priming, stromal and vascular exclusion, lymphatic control of antigen drainage, metabolic stress, and ILC2/type 2 immune plasticity are interpreted as interdependent ecosystem states. We discuss therapeutic strategies that reprogram these states, including myeloid and Treg modulation, stromal and vascular remodeling, preservation or restoration of productive lymphatic communication, cytokine and metabolic interventions, alternative checkpoint blockade, oncolytic viruses, vaccines, engineered cell therapies, and nanomedicine-enabled local delivery. Emphasis is placed on the lessons of failed or modestly effective trials, which show that biologically plausible interventions often fail when the dominant resistance bottleneck is not defined, tissue target engagement is not verified, or treatment sequence is not matched to the immune architecture of the tumor. We propose that future progress will depend on ecosystem-matched combinations guided by spatial biomarkers, on-treatment pharmacodynamics, and adaptive trial designs capable of linking mechanism to clinical decision-making.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Tumor–immune ecosystem, Immunotherapy resistance, Translational immuno-oncology, Lymphatic vessels, Type 2 immunity

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors have fundamentally transformed clinical oncology by demonstrating that durable tumor regression can arise from the restoration of antitumor immune responses rather than from direct tumor cytotoxicity [1, 2]. Antibodies targeting PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA-4 have established new standards of care in melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, renal cell carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, and additional malignancies [1, 3, 4]. Nevertheless, a major limitation endures: durable clinical benefit from single-agent PD-1 or PD-L1 blockade remains confined to a minority of patients even in relatively responsive solid tumors, while objective response rates are markedly lower in immunologically cold malignancies; moreover, a substantial fraction of initial responders ultimately acquire resistance through tumor cell-intrinsic lesions such as neoantigen loss and interferon signaling defects [5–7]. The pivotal clinical question has thus shifted from the feasibility of therapeutically engaging antitumor immunity to the identification of factors that determine intermittent success and the mechanisms that drive primary and acquired resistance.

Tumor cell-intrinsic mechanisms, such as neoantigen loss, impaired antigen processing and presentation, reduced interferon sensitivity, and cellular plasticity, contribute substantially to resistance phenotypes. However, these alterations unfold within a broader tissue context. Cancer cells actively reshape adjacent immune, stromal, vascular, and metabolic environments, generating niches in which T-cell priming is compromised, lymphocyte recruitment and infiltration are restricted, effector functions are suppressed, and immunosuppressive populations are maintained. Resistance is therefore best understood not as an isolated tumor-cell property but as an integrated ecosystem state arising from dynamic, reciprocal interactions between malignant cells and their host tissue milieu [8–10]. This conceptual framework explains why tumors with comparable mutational burdens exhibit heterogeneous responses, why systemic antitumor lymphocytes may fail to mediate effective local tumor control, and why numerous combination strategies underperform when they do not address the principal local ecological barriers [7, 10, 11].

Although many syntheses in immuno-oncology present tumor-intrinsic and extrinsic resistance mechanisms in parallel or catalog emerging therapeutic agents, they frequently stop short of explicitly delineating the specific resistant tissue states that these agents are intended to modulate. Such syntheses are valuable yet increasingly inadequate for advancing the field. A critical unmet need is the explicit articulation of translational linkages between defined resistance mechanisms and matched interventional strategies [12–14]. The clinical relevance of this linkage is evident in the requirement for distinct approaches to tumors with defective priming due to poor dendritic cell recruitment and lymphatic dysfunction versus those characterized by dense infiltration accompanied by TGF-β dominance, myeloid suppression, and stromal exclusion [11, 15, 16].

This article accords particular attention to lymphatic vessels as modulators of immune surveillance and immunotherapy outcomes, to innate lymphoid cells with emphasis on ILC2 populations, and to the context-dependent roles of type 2 immunity, which may exert protumor or antitumor effects depending on the prevailing microenvironmental conditions.

The review advances the thesis that surmounting resistance in solid tumors demands intentional reprogramming of the tumor–immune ecosystem rather than exclusive reliance on augmenting T cell activity. Its first section dissects the construction of resistant ecosystem states via tumor-intrinsic signaling programs, suppressive immune networks, stromal-vascular remodeling, lymphatic control, and metabolic constraints. The second section appraises reprogramming interventions through a translational lens, underscoring clinical findings that corroborate mechanistic models, instructive negative results that have clarified biological principles, and the continuing necessity for validated biomarkers to enable precise patient selection. By adopting this structure, the review aims to move beyond descriptive summaries toward a framework that directly supports therapeutic decision-making in contemporary translational immuno-oncology.

Biological foundations of resistance within the tumor–immune ecosystem

Tumor-cell-intrinsic programs that lower immune visibility

Tumor-intrinsic resistance originates in failures of immune recognition. Clonal selection during tumor progression and under therapeutic pressure enriches for subpopulations with reduced clonal neoantigen burden or defects in antigen presentation, contributing to acquired resistance even in tumors that initially appeared immunogenic [5, 17, 18]. Defects at multiple levels of antigen processing and presentation, including β2-microglobulin loss, HLA class I downregulation, and JAK–STAT pathway disruption impairing IFN-γ signaling, can abolish tumor cell recognition by cytotoxic lymphocytes even when antigenic peptides are present [17, 18]. These lesions assume particular importance because they can generate resistance even in tumors that appear inflamed on routine histology or transcriptomic profiling. Tumors can harbor substantial CD8 + T-cell infiltration yet remain resistant because of defects in antigen presentation or tumor-cell visibility to effector lymphocytes; this provides a direct mechanistic basis for clinical progression despite immune-cell presence and demonstrates that infiltration alone does not ensure therapeutic efficacy [17–19].

Recognition failure is reinforced by adaptive inhibitory signaling. Resistant tumors can expand their inhibitory repertoire beyond the PD-1 axis, with enrichment of programs and ligands associated with LAG-3, TIM-3, TIGIT, and VISTA pathways under therapeutic pressure [20, 21]. Simultaneously, oncogenic signaling networks—including Wnt/β-catenin, PI3K–AKT, MYC, and STAT3—suppress production of chemokines required for dendritic-cell recruitment and CD8 + T-cell trafficking, thereby establishing immune-desert or immune-excluded phenotypes. Oncogenic signaling such as Wnt/β-catenin can suppress chemokine production required for dendritic-cell recruitment, thereby contributing to immune-desert or immune-excluded phenotypes and impaired T-cell priming [6, 17, 22]. In parallel, epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity and associated cell-state transitions can further diminish immune recognition while concurrently increasing resistance to cytotoxic effector mechanisms.

Epigenetic mechanisms, including chromatin remodeling and regulation by demethylases such as KDM1B, can stabilize resistant cell states characterized by stemness and immune escape, rendering evasion more persistent under therapeutic pressure [17, 22, 23]. Such locking of resistant states carries direct clinical implications, because it explains why tumors that were once immunologically accessible can become refractory after initial therapy. Tumor-intrinsic resistance typically arises from the convergence of antigen presentation defects, oncogenic signaling rewiring, and epigenetic/transcriptional reprogramming rather than single mutations; these programs further reshape myeloid and stromal compartments to reinforce ecosystem-level suppression [17, 18, 22] (Fig. 1).

Myeloid, regulatory lymphocyte, and dendritic-cell circuits of suppression

When tumor-intrinsic lesions compromise visibility, suppressive immune circuits undermine the operability of effector responses. Myeloid populations occupy a central coordinating role. Tumor-associated macrophages, polarized by factors including CSF-1, hypoxia, apoptotic-cell uptake, IL-4/IL-13, and tumor-derived lactate, adopt suppressive M2-like phenotypes that produce IL-10, TGF-β, VEGF, arginase, and nitric oxide, thereby inhibiting effector T-cell and NK-cell function while promoting angiogenesis, matrix remodeling, and tumor progression [24–26]. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells suppress T-cell responses locally and systemically through amino-acid depletion (e.g., arginine), production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, and interference with T-cell receptor signaling. These cells interact reciprocally with tumor-intrinsic oncogenic programs that reduce chemokine production, thereby promoting myeloid recruitment and stabilization of suppressive phenotypes [27, 28].

Regulatory T cells accumulate in tumors via chemokine recruitment, local expansion, and mediators such as TGF-β and adenosine, then suppress effector responses through nonredundant mechanisms including IL-2 consumption, CTLA-4-dependent dendritic-cell inhibition, IL-10/TGF-β secretion, and adenosine generation. Tregs actively impair antigen presentation quality and effector-cell survival; consequently, tumors with substantial CD8 + infiltration can remain clinically resistant when Tregs numerically or functionally dominate [29–31]. The spatial and functional dominance of Tregs further compounds defects in antigen presentation arising from tumor-intrinsic lesions, creating layered barriers to effective immunity.

Failure of conventional type 1 dendritic cells (cDC1s) or tolerization of dendritic cells by VEGF, IL-6, IL-10, or metabolic stress prevents effective cross-presentation and sustained priming of antitumor T cells. This accounts for the frequent observation that therapies releasing antigen or inducing local inflammation fail to restore productive immunity when the dendritic-cell compartment is absent or dysfunctional, integrating lymphatic, stromal, and myeloid defects into a unified failure of the cancer-immunity cycle [32–34]. What appears histologically as a cold tumor is often a lesion in which antigen transport, dendritic-cell migration, and T-cell priming have failed in concert. These dendritic-cell defects are reinforced by tumor-intrinsic oncogenic programs and by suppressive myeloid and Treg networks that limit dendritic-cell costimulation and function [6, 33].

These suppressive compartments interact reciprocally rather than functioning as isolated lineages. Tumor-associated macrophages recruit and stabilize regulatory T cells that limit dendritic-cell costimulation; myeloid-derived suppressor cells exacerbate metabolic deprivation; and chronic antigen exposure drives T-cell exhaustion. The resulting resilient suppressive network means that productive antitumor immunity requires destabilization of the network as a whole [27, 29, 34] (Fig. 2). Translationally, this means that measuring only one compartment is often misleading. A decrease in tumor-associated macrophage frequency without recovery of dendritic-cell function or effector-cell entry may have little therapeutic consequence. What matters is whether the suppressive network as a whole has been destabilized sufficiently for productive immunity to resume. Such network-level resilience explains why single-agent interventions frequently prove insufficient and why ecosystem-matched combination strategies are required to restore functional antitumor responses [33, 34] (Fig. 3).

Stromal, vascular, and lymphatic control of immune access

Resistance is architectural as well as immunologic. Cancer-associated fibroblasts, extracellular-matrix accumulation, and vascular dysfunction control physical entry, migration, and viability of immune cells within tumor tissue. Desmoplastic stroma elevates interstitial pressure and creates tortuous paths that exclude lymphocytes from malignant nests; CAF-derived TGF-β and CXCL12 generate chemotactic and mechanical barriers that trap immune cells peripherally, preventing deep parenchymal access [11, 34, 35]. These stromal processes are clinically important because they explain why tumors may exhibit peritumoral inflammation yet remain refractory to checkpoint blockade. In such lesions, the problem is not absence of immunity but failure of immune cells to occupy the appropriate spatial niche. Stromal architecture interacts with tumor-intrinsic programs by limiting dendritic-cell and effector-lymphocyte access, thereby amplifying consequences of defective antigen presentation [6, 8, 33].

Abnormal vasculature magnifies architectural exclusion. VEGF-driven endothelial dysfunction suppresses adhesion molecules for leukocyte rolling and transmigration, compromises oxygenation, and promotes hypoxia-dependent myeloid recruitment; endothelial cells act as selective barriers disfavoring cytotoxic-cell entry or survival [36, 37]. Angiogenesis therefore functions as an immune-regulatory circuit in addition to a nutrient pathway. Vascular normalization reframes anti-angiogenic therapy as an immunologic intervention by restoring vessel functionality, improving dendritic-cell and T-cell trafficking, and reducing hypoxia that favors suppressive myeloid and stromal programs, thereby addressing both physical exclusion and metabolic/myeloid alterations sustaining resistance [37, 38].

Lymphatic vessels are not passive conduits for metastasis. Functional lymphatic networks actively regulate antigen drainage to tumor-draining lymph nodes, dendritic-cell trafficking, immune priming, and the balance between tolerance and activation. Preserved or induced lymphatic function is indispensable for effective antitumor responses, whereas blockade may favor immunosuppression [10, 39]. Lymphatics can support immune activation or dampening depending on context and must be treated as immune-regulatory tissue. This explains why some inflamed tumors generate weak systemic responses despite local activation—failure of antigen transport and nodal communication is part of the problem. Lymphatic dysfunction integrates with stromal exclusion and vascular abnormalities to restrict initiation and dissemination of productive immunity [39, 40].

Once vascular and lymphatic biology are brought into the same framework with stromal dynamics, a more coherent view of resistant ecosystems emerges. Stromal exclusion, endothelial dysfunction, and altered lymphatic signaling are not three separate topics. They are interconnected regulators of where antitumor immunity can be initiated, where it can travel, and whether it can reach the tumor bed in sufficient quantity and functional state [8, 33, 34]. This is why therapies that normalize vessels, modulate fibroblast activity, or preserve immunologically productive lymphatic function should be considered core immunotherapeutic strategies rather than ancillary measures. These architectural elements collectively determine the physical and biochemical accessibility of the tumor bed, thereby shaping the efficacy of any intervention directed at immune reactivation.

Metabolic stress, ILC2s, and the evolving view of type 2 immunity

Metabolic competition remains a powerful driver of resistance. Tumor cells and suppressive myeloid populations compete with effector lymphocytes for glucose, amino acids, and oxygen while generating lactate, adenosine, and other suppressive metabolites that weaken cytotoxic function [41–43]. In acidic, nutrient-restricted environments, T cells lose proliferative capacity and cytokine productivity, NK-cell function declines, and macrophage polarization is skewed toward tumor-supportive states [42, 44]. These changes are not merely epiphenomena of rapid tumor growth; they actively stabilize immune dysfunction. Lactate is especially important because it links malignant-cell metabolism to a broad immunologic reconfiguration, influencing myeloid phenotype, cytotoxic lymphocyte fitness, and tissue pH [24, 42]. Adenosine-rich environments similarly act as local biochemical checkpoints, dampening both T-cell and NK-cell responses even when antigen recognition remains intact [43, 45]. Metabolic stress further interconnects with stromal and vascular abnormalities by exacerbating hypoxia and with suppressive immune circuits by reinforcing myeloid and Treg dominance.

A second area that has been insufficiently integrated into classical resistance narratives concerns innate lymphoid cells and the wider type 2 immune axis. Earlier descriptions have tended to present type 2-associated pathways predominantly as tumor-promoting. That perspective captures an important dimension of the biology but is increasingly recognized as incomplete. ILC2s do not occupy a fixed suppressive role across all tumors. Depending on tissue context, timing, and crosstalk with eosinophils, dendritic cells, macrophages, and lymphatic structures, ILC2s may contribute to antitumor activity by recruiting or sustaining other effector populations, or they may instead reinforce tissue-remodeling and suppressive programs [44, 46, 47]. In melanoma and related systems, recent investigations have drawn attention to both their tumoricidal potential and the distinct mechanisms through which tumors can metabolically or cytokinally suppress that potential [47, 48]. The functional plasticity of ILC2s exemplifies how innate lymphoid populations can either counteract or reinforce the suppressive networks established by myeloid cells and Tregs.

This broader reassessment extends beyond ILC2s to type 2 immunity as a whole. IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, eosinophil recruitment, alternative macrophage activation, and tissue-repair programs cannot be assigned uniform functional meaning across all cancers; their effects are strictly context- and tissue-dependent, sometimes favoring fibrosis or suppressive myeloid states and in other settings supporting effector recruitment or antitumor cooperation [44, 46, 47]. The key translational lesson is that type 2 immunity should be interpreted spatially and temporally, not ideologically (Fig. 2). Mechanisms that appear suppressive in one tissue or disease phase may be permissive or even beneficial in another. For high-level narrative synthesis in contemporary immuno-oncology, this context dependence is no longer a peripheral nuance; it forms part of the field’s evolving conceptual framework. Such context dependence also intersects with metabolic signals, as lactate and other metabolites can skew type 2 programs toward nonproductive outcomes [42, 46].

Bringing metabolism and type 2 immunity into the same analytical section is therefore deliberate. Metabolites such as lactate are not merely biochemical waste products; they are ecological signals that shape which immune lineages survive and what functions they perform. When tumor metabolism suppresses ILC2 activity or redirects type 2 programs toward nonproductive tissue repair, metabolic resistance and immune-polarization resistance become two sides of the same ecosystem-level phenomenon [42, 44, 46]. This integrated view exemplifies the kind of mechanistic synthesis required to identify interventions capable of reprogramming resistant tumor–immune ecosystems rather than merely targeting individual pathways in isolation. Only by addressing these interconnected foundations—tumor-intrinsic visibility, suppressive cellular networks, architectural access, and metabolic–type 2 polarization—can therapeutic strategies be matched to the dominant constraints operating within a given resistant tumor.

Translational reprogramming strategies

Rewiring myeloid and regulatory lymphocyte compartments

Suppressive myeloid populations constitute recurrent bottlenecks in resistant tumors, rendering macrophage- and MDSC-directed strategies among the most actively investigated reprogramming approaches [49–51]. Two complementary therapeutic logics have developed. The first centers on depletion or recruitment blockade, illustrated by CSF-1R inhibition to impair TAM survival and by chemokine-receptor antagonists to limit influx of suppressive granulocytic populations [52, 53]. The second emphasizes functional repolarization, shifting myeloid cells from suppressive or tissue-repair states toward inflammatory and antigen-presenting phenotypes through CD40 agonism, innate agonist delivery platforms, PI3Kγ inhibition, and combinations with checkpoint blockade [54, 55]. Successful myeloid interventions exert substantial leverage because they can simultaneously improve antigen presentation, reshape cytokine tone, influence vascular behavior, and enhance T-cell fitness [56, 57].

Clinical experience has shown that myeloid-targeted agents more consistently achieve pharmacodynamic remodeling than durable tumor control when deployed as unselected monotherapies or without a matched immune-activating partner [58, 59]. Early programs demonstrated pharmacodynamic activity and occasional synergy, yet meaningful responses remained infrequent when a second constraint, such as defective priming or profound stromal exclusion, persisted. Myeloid rewiring must therefore be framed in ecosystem rather than lineage-specific terms. Depletion or reprogramming of TAMs creates a permissive niche only when paired with an intervention capable of exploiting it—checkpoint blockade, vaccines, localized innate agonists, or radiation-induced antigen release [55, 57, 60]. This perspective supplants the earlier conception of myeloid cells as uniformly detrimental populations that should be eliminated wherever possible.

Treg-targeted strategies require comparable precision. Anti-CTLA-4 antibodies provided initial evidence that intratumoral Treg reduction can contribute to antitumor efficacy, yet broad interference with regulatory compartments carries clear toxicity risk [3, 61, 62]. Subsequent strategies have therefore moved toward context-restricted regulatory modulation, including Fc-dependent or Fc-optimized depletion of intratumoral regulatory cells, CCR4-directed Treg targeting, local checkpoint delivery, and cytokine designs that reduce preferential engagement of high-affinity IL-2 receptors on Tregs [63, 64]. The limited success of first-generation systemic cytokines reinforced the principle that immunologic potency without tissue selectivity produces unacceptable risk [65, 66]. The translational frontier now favors site-weighted immunomodulation, concentrating regulatory disruption within the tumor or tumor-draining nodal axis rather than throughout the patient.

For both myeloid and Treg strategies, the decisive endpoint is not numeric depletion but whether the suppressive network has been destabilized sufficiently to restore antigen presentation, permit effector-cell entry, and sustain function under checkpoint release [11, 33, 34]. Accordingly, translational assessment should move beyond lineage depletion and determine whether treatment restores a functionally permissive immune architecture—one capable of antigen presentation, effector-cell access, and sustained cytotoxic activity after checkpoint release. This distinction supplies a more realistic basis for evaluating translational success than lineage-specific cell counts alone.

Remodeling stroma, vasculature, lymphatics, and suppressive soluble networks

Therapies directed at stromal and vascular compartments address a defining feature of resistant solid tumors: immune cells and therapeutic agents frequently cannot penetrate or function efficiently within diseased tissue [67–69]. Matrix depletion, CAF modulation, TGF-β blockade, and anti-angiogenic agents arose from this recognition [11, 68]. Their collective purpose is to render immune recruitment and effector function physically feasible rather than merely to soften tumor tissue. Clinical data have nevertheless demonstrated that barrier removal does not automatically generate productive immunity [35, 70, 71]. The phase III failure of pegvorhyaluronidase alfa in hyaluronan-high metastatic pancreatic cancer remains instructive: stromal modification can improve a plausible physical barrier yet fail clinically when it is not coupled to a regimen that establishes productive antitumor immunity [71, 72]. Stroma-targeted approaches are therefore necessary in selected contexts but rarely sufficient when used alone.

TGF-β occupies a particularly complex position. The pathway links fibroblast activation, immune exclusion, epithelial plasticity, and suppression of cytotoxicity, rendering it mechanistically attractive [11, 73, 74]. Broad systemic TGF-β inhibition, however, carries context-dependent safety and efficacy uncertainties because TGF-β signaling can mediate both tumor-promoting immune exclusion and tissue-protective homeostatic functions [11, 73, 75]. Early interest in bifunctional PD-L1/TGF-β traps reflected strong preclinical rationale, yet later-stage results in heterogeneous populations indicated that pathway centrality cannot be assumed across tumor types or patient subsets [75, 76]. TGF-β blockade therefore requires matching to a demonstrable ecosystem phenotype, such as immune exclusion or fibroblast-rich signaling, if it is to carry a credible prospect of success [11, 73].

Anti-angiogenic combinations provide a more established success model [77, 78]. The approval of atezolizumab plus bevacizumab in unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma demonstrated that correction of a vascularly immunosuppressive niche can enhance checkpoint efficacy and alter clinical practice [77, 79, 80]. This example carries conceptual weight because it validates aberrant vascular architecture as a dominant determinant of immunotherapy response. Comparable logic extends to other malignancies in which VEGF-driven endothelial dysfunction and hypoxia organize a hostile immune geography [81, 82].

Lymphatic modulation remains less clinically mature but conceptually central. Lymphatic vessels actively regulate antigen drainage to tumor-draining lymph nodes, dendritic-cell trafficking, immune priming, and the balance between activation and tolerance [10, 83]. Strategies that preserve or restore productive lymphatic communication may be particularly relevant in tumors where antigen drainage, dendritic-cell migration, or tumor-draining nodal priming is limiting (Fig. 3). Soluble mediators including IL-6, IL-8, CSF-1, chemokine axes, and adenosine-generating pathways should therefore be interpreted as ecosystem-specific liabilities rather than generic targets, favoring biomarker-defined intervention over empiric pathway blockade [45, 52, 84].

Supplying or engineering immunity

Certain suppressive ecosystems cannot be rescued by blockade or remodeling alone. Tumors that fail to generate robust endogenous T-cell responses may require active immune supplementation through cytokine engineering, intratumoral innate agonists, oncolytic viruses, vaccines, or adoptive cellular therapy [85–87]. These approaches share the premise that when the cancer-immunity cycle is deficient at initiation or expansion phases, the system must be supplied with inflammatory signals, tumor-reactive cells, or both [88, 89].

Cytokine engineering exemplifies both promise and constraint. Contemporary IL-2 and IL-15 platforms aim to expand effectors while limiting capillary leak, off-target exposure, and preferential Treg engagement [90, 91]. Biologic refinement has not, however, ensured clinical translation. Although bempegaldesleukin produced encouraging early-phase immune-pharmacodynamic signals, subsequent late-stage development did not establish a practice-changing benefit, underscoring that engineered cytokines should be judged by tissue pharmacodynamics, schedule, and biomarker-defined disease context rather than by receptor bias alone [66, 90, 92]. Cytokines must therefore be evaluated in relation to receptor selectivity, tissue localization, dosing schedule, and the dominant ecosystem defect present in the trial population [90, 91].

Oncolytic viruses and therapeutic vaccines can bridge antigen release, local inflammation, and T-cell priming, offering particular value in poorly inflamed tumors that require conversion from immune indifference to immune engagement [93–95]. Their clinical development has nevertheless been uneven: early evidence that oncolytic therapy can inflame tumors has not consistently translated into definitive late-stage benefit, and recent regulatory scrutiny of newer melanoma oncolytic platforms reinforces the need for randomized evidence and tissue-level pharmacodynamic confirmation [93, 96]. Personalized mRNA vaccine strategies combined with PD-1 blockade, by contrast, continue to indicate that vaccine-primed immunity can confer clinically meaningful benefit in appropriately selected settings [87, 97]. These platforms function best when matched to tumors lacking priming, intratumoral amplification, or systemic breadth [15, 98].

Adoptive cell therapy constitutes the most direct form of immune supplementation [89, 99]. Regulatory approval of lifileucel for advanced melanoma validates that expanded tumor-reactive lymphocytes can deliver benefit after prior checkpoint exposure [100, 101]. Standard adoptive platforms nevertheless remain constrained in solid tumors by the same ecosystem barriers discussed earlier—poor trafficking, suppressive cytokines, hypoxia, myeloid clearance, metabolic deprivation, and checkpoint-rich tissue [8, 11, 102]. Next-generation designs therefore incorporate ecology-aware features: armored CAR-T cells, TGF-β-resistant constructs, locally secreting checkpoint antagonists, CAR-NK platforms, and engineered macrophages that either withstand or actively recondition the tissue environment into which they are deployed [103, 104].

Combination design, biomarkers, and lessons from failed trials

The field has moved from single-agent optimism toward ecosystem-matched combination design. Combination therapy must not, however, be equated with indiscriminate escalation. High-profile disappointments—including the phase III failure of epacadostat despite strong enthusiasm around IDO1 biology, uneven results with broad anti-TIGIT programs, mixed outcomes with TGF-β-targeting strategies, the failure of stromal depletion alone in pancreatic cancer, and recent setbacks for intratumoral and oncolytic approaches—demonstrate that plausible mechanisms can fail when the wrong population is enrolled, tissue target engagement is inadequately verified, or the regimen does not correct the ecosystem defect that actually limits response [71, 105, 106].

Negative-trial literacy is therefore essential to any serious translational synthesis. Failed trials function as experiments that expose incomplete assumptions: whether the targeted pathway was truly dominant in the enrolled tumors, whether inhibition was demonstrated in tissue or only inferred from blood biomarkers, and whether the regimen addressed the rate-limiting step of priming, trafficking, or effector function. The conditional biology revealed by anti-TIGIT setbacks, for example, cautions against pathway-agnostic immune stimulation or combinations assembled on theoretical synergy without spatial or temporal biomarker support [20, 107]. Field maturation depends as much on what has not succeeded as on what has.

The practical implication is biomarker-guided stratification anchored in tissue context. Bulk PD-L1 expression alone is inadequate for ecosystem matching [9, 108]. More informative approaches integrate transcriptomic signatures of interferon activity, TGF-β signaling, angiogenic tone, and myeloid infiltration with spatial methods that map immune-cell location relative to tumor, stroma, and vasculature [8, 11, 109]. Multiplex immunohistochemistry, single-cell analysis, and spatial transcriptomics prove particularly valuable because they situate cells within anatomical relationships rather than merely enumerating them [8, 110]. These tools inform whether a patient requires priming, decompression of stromal or myeloid barriers, adoptive supplementation, or multi-layer combinations [9, 11].

Sequencing further refines this logic. Some tumors benefit from priming before checkpoint release, while others require vascular or myeloid decompression before endogenous or transferred lymphocytes can function effectively [38, 57, 93]. Neoadjuvant trials are especially informative because serial tissue sampling reveals whether a regimen genuinely alters the ecosystem rather than simply reducing radiographic burden [111, 112]. The future of combination immunotherapy therefore lies less in larger drug cocktails than in smaller, mechanistically disciplined, biomarker-informed regimens tailored to the resistant tissue state confronting the clinician [8, 105]. Conclusion

Resistance to cancer immunotherapy is best understood as an emergent property of the tumor–immune ecosystem rather than as a defect confined to any single cellular or molecular compartment. Tumor-cell-intrinsic lesions that impair antigen presentation, suppressive myeloid and regulatory lymphocyte networks, fibroblast-rich desmoplastic stroma, abnormal vasculature that restricts trafficking, context-dependent lymphatic programs that govern priming and tolerance, metabolic deprivation that impairs effector function, and exhausted or excluded effector populations cooperate to generate immune-refractory states. This ecosystem perspective accounts for the observation that many patients fail to benefit from checkpoint blockade despite apparently promising biomarkers and explains why interventions directed at one pathway frequently disappoint when the dominant constraint lies elsewhere within the tissue.

A translationally useful framework must therefore connect mechanistic insight, tissue architecture, and rational therapy selection. Tumors dominated by defective priming will not be reliably rescued by additional checkpoint blockade alone. Myeloid-rich or fibrotic tumors may require prior decompression of suppressive niches before cytotoxic lymphocytes can function. Immune-excluded lesions may depend on vascular normalization, stromal modulation, or restoration of productive lymphatic drainage to permit effective infiltration and antigen transport. Within this framework, innate lymphoid cells, particularly ILC2s, and type 2 immune programs can no longer be treated as peripheral curiosities or assumed to be uniformly tumor-promoting; they form part of the broader organization of tissue immunity in cancer and must be integrated into contemporary accounts of resistance.

The field has now entered a more disciplined phase in which negative trials are recognized as equally informative as positive ones. The path forward is unlikely to derive from increasingly broad empiric combinations. It is more likely to emerge from ecosystem-matched reprogramming strategies supported by spatial biomarkers that define dominant constraints, on-treatment tissue pharmacodynamics that verify target engagement, and adaptive trial designs that distinguish incomplete mechanistic hypotheses from genuine biologic effect. When resistance is framed in these terms, deliberate reprogramming of the tumor–immune ecosystem becomes not merely an aspirational slogan but a practical translational agenda. On that agenda rests the next stage of meaningful progress in cancer immunotherapy.
