In situ kidney perfusion: a novel technique for managing intra-operative vascular complications in kidney transplantation – two case reports
Authors: Vu N. Le, Anh V. Nguyen, Dang H. Do, Huy Q. Tran, Hong S. Duy Phung, Dung T.K. Dao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Vascular complications during kidney transplantation, such as thrombosis, can impair graft function and often require graft removal and external perfusion.Case presentation:We describe an in situ kidney perfusion technique to manage intraoperative arterial and venous thrombosis without removing the graft, which was applied at Viet Duc University Hospital. Two female patients, aged 20 and 58 years, demonstrated successful application of in situ perfusion, with restored graft function and no postoperative complications.Discussion:Timely treatment always produces positive outcomes without interfering with the functioning of the transplanted kidney. It is an ideal, quick, and effective solution that eliminates the need to remove the kidney, re-prepare the perfusion table, and increase the use of both staff and supplies.Conclusion:In situ perfusion is a safe, effective method to address vascular complications, reducing operative time and preserving graft integrity.

Introduction

Currently, kidney transplantation is routinely performed worldwide, including in Vietnam. This procedure yields favorable outcomes that contribute to improving the quality of life of patients with end-stage renal disease, also known as chronic kidney failure. The success of a kidney transplant depends on a variety of factors[1,2]. Among these, vascular complications are reported to occur in between 3% and 15% of transplant procedures, representing a critical challenge that can lead to early graft loss if not managed promptly[3]. Normally, a transplanted kidney begins to produce urine shortly after reperfusion. However, if there is no urine production after 5 minutes and the kidney appears contracted, with collapsed veins, there may be issues such as a narrowed anastomosis or thrombus formation. Traditionally, when complications arise with the vascular anastomosis, the transplanted kidney can be removed, perfused externally, and then retransplanted[4,5]. This approach is time-consuming and carries an increased risk of vascular damage to the transplanted kidney. In this situation, we adopted the kidney perfusion technique at the Organ Transplantation Center – Viet Duc University Hospital. The objective of this report is to describe our modified in situ perfusion technique for managing arterial thrombosis during kidney transplantation. Methods

Principle of the technique: The technique isolates the external iliac artery, performs an anastomosis with the renal artery, and injects cold heparinized lactated Ringer’s solution to perfuse the kidney in situ, flushing thrombi and preventing ischemia.

Required supplies: Three vascular clamps, one 14- or 16-gauge (G) intravenous (IV) cannula, one IV tubing set, 500 mL of cold Ringer’s lactate (2–8 °C), 2500 IU of heparin, and one pressure infuser bag.

Procedure: Mix 0.5 mL heparin (2500 IU) with 500 mL Ringer’s lactate. Connect the solution to the IV tubing, remove air from the line, and attach a 14 or 16 G cannula. Clamp the external iliac artery above and below the renal artery anastomosis. Insert the cannula into the iliac artery below the anastomosis, connect to the tubing, and infuse at 70–100 mmHg using the pressure infuser bag. Clamp the renal vein to prevent backflow. If systemic heparinization is undesirable, create a small incision in the renal vein to drain the perfusate. The solution flows through the renal artery, kidney parenchyma, and vein, clearing thrombi and restoring perfusion (Fig. 1). Case report

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Viet Duc University Hospital (IRB No. 01.2023.NCVD). Consent for publication was obtained and noted in the patient’s medical record.

Case 1

A 20-year-old female with ESRD due to glomerulonephritis, on dialysis for 2 months, received a kidney from her 49-year-old mother. The patient’s examination indicated a lean body mass index (BMI) of 16.65, a blood pressure of 140/90 mmHg, and a creatinine level of 830 µmol/L. Examination of the donor’s kidneys showed good renal function. Renal scintigraphy revealed good function of the left kidney (57%) and the right kidney (43%). Crossmatch test was negative, and HLA immunocompatibility was high. The donor’s right kidney had two arteries (main artery 4.8 mm, no sclerosis) and a 16-mm renal vein. The length from the aortic division to the bifurcation of the two branches was 18.7 mm; the right renal vein was 16 mm in diameter; and the length from the confluence to the vena cava was 12.2 mm.

Surgical procedure

During transplantation, the renal vein was anastomosed to the external iliac vein (6/0 Prolene, 8 minutes), and the two renal arteries were connected to the external iliac artery (6/0 and 7/0 Prolene, 12 minutes). After reperfusion, the lower third of the kidney became ischemic with no urine output after 10 minutes, suggesting arterial thrombosis. Five to ten minutes later, the kidney became soft and purple in color, and there was still no urine. After rechecking the entire anastomosis, no bleeding points and no twisting were found. Examination revealed that the small artery was beating poorly; the patient was suspected of having internal thrombosis; the major arterial branch was still beating well; and the blood pressure was maintained at 160/100 mmHg. Despite raising the blood pressure, repositioning the graft and administering a furosemide challenge, the kidney still did not produce urine. The cause was suspected to be arterial thrombosis. A Fogarty balloon was used to remove a small thrombus from the smaller artery, but urine output remained absent after 10 minutes. The surgical staff consulted and decided to reperfuse the kidney in situ without removing the vascular anastomosis.

The superior and inferior external iliac arteries were isolated by clamping, and a 16-G IV cannula was placed into the lumen of the external iliac artery through the thrombectomy site. Then, 500 mL of cold Ringer’s lactate solution (6 °C) mixed with 2500 IU of heparin was injected through the IV cannula into the grafted renal artery at a pressure of 130 mmHg to dissolve the thrombus and prevent venous backflow, and the perfusing fluid passed through the renal vein into the inferior vena cava (Fig. 2). After performing in situ kidney perfusion, the kidney parenchyma was rosy and tight. The anastomosis was sutured, the blood vessel clamp was released, and urine returned after 2 minutes. Since the kidney had started excreting urine and the arterial pulse remained stable, Doppler ultrasonography showed good patency. A ureteral stent was inserted according to the Lich–Gregoire method, with a JJ stent as the internal tube.

Postoperative follow-up

On the first day after surgery, urine output, 2780 mL/12 h; appearance, clear yellow; abdominal drainage, 60–90 mL; CVP, 10 cmH2O; fluid in, 3650 mL; fluid out, 3240 mL; fluid balance, +410 mL; Hct, 28.5%; urea/creatinine, 11/463 µmol/L; CRP, 27; and Na/K/Cl, 130/4.8/96. On the second day after surgery, creatinine decreased to 260 µmol/L. The abdominal ultrasound revealed no narrowing or occlusion of the grafted kidney. The patient was maintained on heparin at 50 IU/kg/day for 10 days and was then switched to aspirin 100 mg/day.

After 7 days, the patient’s normal urine flow remained stable between 200 and 250 mL/h without the use of Lasix. Serum creatinine levels gradually decreased, and there was no evidence of slow graft function or acute tubular necrosis. The patient was discharged after 2 weeks without any complications. After 1 year of follow-up, the serum creatinine was 98 µmol/L and the eGFR was 62.4 mL/min.

Case 2

A 58-year-old woman with ESRD due to glomerulonephritis, on dialysis for 24 months, received a deceased-donor kidney (HLA mismatch 3/6). The renal vein was anastomosed to the external iliac vein (12 minutes), and the renal artery was anastomosed to the external iliac artery (end-to-side, 10 minutes). Upon releasing the arterial clamp, the vein was found to be twisted due to excessive length. The surgical team decided to perform in situ kidney perfusion without dismantling the vascular anastomoses. The external iliac artery above and below the renal artery anastomosis was isolated using clamps. In situ perfusion was performed: the iliac artery was clamped, a 16 G cannula was inserted, and 500 mL of cold Ringer’s lactate with 2500 IU heparin was infused at 130 mmHg by pump for 5 minutes. The perfusion fluid flowed through the renal artery, renal vein, and into the inferior vena cava (Fig. 3). The patient’s intraoperative blood pressure was maintained at a stable 130/70 mmHg. After perfusion, the renal parenchyma appeared clear again. The arterial anastomosis was left intact, while the venous anastomosis was dismantled, shortened, and re-anastomosed. The perfusion process took 5 minutes, and the re-anastomosis of the vein took 8 minutes. Upon the second release of the vascular clamps, the kidney appeared uniformly well-perfused. Urine output was observed 2 minutes after clamp release. Postoperative laboratory results were as follows (Table 1). Ultrasound showed no thrombosis (renal artery: RI 0.72, PSV 66.7 cm/s). The patient was discharged with stable creatinine (100 µmol/L) and eGFR (80.6 mL/min) at the 1-year follow-up. Discussion

The incidence of transplant renal artery thrombosis is 1%, typically at the anastomosis site[7]. If undetected for 72 hours to 2 weeks post-transplantation, the kidney may develop necrosis, requiring removal[8]. Sudden anuria indicates graft arterial thrombosis, but late detection complicates salvage. Intraoperative Doppler ultrasonography aids early thrombus identification. In our first case, suspecting arterial thrombosis, we opted for a Fogarty balloon over mechanical thrombectomy to minimize ischemic time. Traditionally, anuria from arterial occlusion requires kidney removal and in situ perfusion before reanastomosis[4,5]. In our case, we decided to perfuse the kidney in situ rather than remove it to reduce further damage to the kidney and shorten the operative time. The segment of the iliac artery corresponding to the location of the transplanted kidney was isolated during in situ kidney perfusion. The WIT was higher than 30 minutes but still lower than 60 minutes, thresholds associated with a 23% increase in death and graft failure[9].

To reduce the possibility of cardioplegia, we used lactated Ringer’s solution containing 5000 IU of heparin in place of the Custodiol solution for in situ kidney perfusion. In Nerli’s study, cold perfusion was also used[7]. This allows us to avoid isolating the corresponding iliac vein segment while achieving the required effect. Excessive kidney perfusion dissolves and flushes away small, deep-seated thrombi that cannot be removed mechanically with instruments.

The management of post-transplantation renal vein thrombosis (RVT) often necessitates urgent intervention to salvage the graft, with cold perfusion playing a critical role in some cases. In the case report by Mahmoud (2017), successful management of early RVT involved surgical thrombectomy followed by perfusion with heparinized Ringer’s solution to restore graft viability and remove residual thrombus[10]. The use of cold perfusion helps minimize ischemic damage during thrombectomy. Similarly, Joana highlights a case where cold perfusion was employed post-thrombectomy to clear thrombus and improve graft perfusion, contributing to unexpected graft salvage[11]. Cold perfusion aids in flushing out thrombi while protecting the graft from warm ischemia, which is vital given the time-sensitive nature of RVT management.

At Viet Duc University Hospital, in situ kidney perfusion is used when arterial anastomosis needs revision, such as for long renal arteries, large kidneys, arterial kinking, or venous twisting upon clamp release. This allows venous re-anastomosis without graft removal. Postoperatively, kidneys produce urine without acute tubular necrosis or slow graft function, indicating no ischemic damage[5,12].

Timely treatment always produces positive outcomes without interfering with the functioning of the transplanted kidney. It is an ideal, quick, and effective solution that eliminates the need to remove the kidney, re-prepare the perfusion table, and increase the consumption of both staff time and supplies.

Conclusion

In situ kidney perfusion offers a safe and effective method for managing intraoperative vascular complications during kidney transplantation. By avoiding graft removal and external perfusion, this technique reduces vascular damage, shortens operative time, and preserves graft function. It represents a promising approach in kidney transplant surgery and should be further studied.
