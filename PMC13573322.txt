Peri-knee soft tissue reconstruction using retrograde pedicled anterolateral thigh flaps with selective vascular supercharging: a retrospective study
Authors: Aierbanjiang Shali, Xiong Debin, Cheng Erlin, Xiriaili Kadier, Alimu Keremu, Yusufu Aihemaitijiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSoft tissue defects around the knee create significant reconstructive difficulties owing to complex anatomy, high functional demands, and exposure of vital structures. The retrograde pedicled anterolateral thigh flap (RPALT), supplied via the descending branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery, provides an alternative to free tissue transfer. Retrograde perfusion can compromise distal flap viability, especially when larger flaps undergo extensive rotation. We evaluated the clinical outcomes of peri-knee reconstruction using RPALT flaps with standardized intraoperative perfusion assessment followed by selective vascular augmentation if indicated.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective review of 23 consecutive patients who underwent peri-knee reconstruction with RPALT flaps (January 2015–August 2025). Following flap rotation, distal perfusion was assessed during a mandatory 40-minute observation period using color, temperature, capillary refill, and distal bleeding (CTCD criteria). Persistent perfusion compromise prompted mechanism-specific augmentation (venous, arterial, or combined). The primary outcome was flap survival; secondary outcomes included Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS) knee score, Vancouver Scar Scale (VSS), Scar Cosmesis Assessment and Rating (SCAR) scale, follow-up duration, and complications.ResultsPatients (mean age 38.48 ± 11.03 years) had defects in the prepatellar region (n = 9), lateral knee (n = 8), and popliteal fossa (n = 6), predominantly from trauma. Eight patients (34.8%) required vascular augmentation: venous in 4, arterial in 2, and combined in 2. All flaps survived with no total flap loss (mean follow-up 35.26 ± 22.25 months). One patient experienced a postoperative vascular crisis, which was successfully salvaged with timely intervention; another had marginal proximal partial skin loss/necrosis managed with limited skin grafting. Mean HSS score was 79.57 ± 7.87, mean VSS was 3.13 ± 0.81, and mean SCAR was 5.74 ± 1.05. The augmentation group had significantly larger defect and flap areas (P = 0.034 and P = 0.015), whereas functional and scar outcomes were comparable between groups.ConclusionsIn this selected cohort, RPALT flap reconstruction guided by standardized preoperative ultrasonography mapping and structured intraoperative perfusion assessment achieved excellent flap viability with satisfactory functional and cosmetic outcomes. Selective vascular augmentation was more frequently required for larger defects and flaps and may enhance reliability in higher-demand peri-knee reconstruction.

Soft tissue defects around the knee pose substantial reconstructive challenges. The region has a limited soft tissue envelope, and injuries frequently expose critical structures including the patella, tendons, joint capsule, and internal fixation hardware [1]. These defects typically arise from high-energy trauma, deep infections following surgery, or oncologic resection. Optimal reconstruction should provide durable coverage while facilitating early rehabilitation to minimize functional impairment.

Traditional reconstructive options include local fasciocutaneous flaps and muscle flaps, particularly the gastrocnemius flap [2]. Although the gastrocnemius flap remains useful for selected posterior or proximal defects, its rotation arc and surface area may be insufficient for some anterior or lateral peri-knee defects. Free tissue transfer can address extensive or composite defects but requires microsurgical expertise, prolongs operative time, depends on suitable recipient vessels, and carries the inherent risk of complete flap loss [3, 4].

The retrograde pedicled anterolateral thigh (RPALT) flap, based on the descending branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery (LCFA), offers a pedicled alternative for moderate-to-large peri-knee defects while preserving major axial vessels of the lower limb [5]. This flap depends on retrograde perfusion through peri-knee vascular communications linking the genicular arterial network and the LCFA descending branch. However, retrograde hemodynamics provide less physiologic reserve than antegrade flow, potentially resulting in venous congestion or arterial insufficiency, particularly with larger flaps or extensive rotation [6].

Selective vascular supercharging—mechanism-directed augmentation of venous and/or arterial perfusion—can address these limitations without converting to free tissue transfer [7]. Recent evidence has emphasized venous congestion as the dominant mechanism of compromise in distally based/reverse-flow ALT designs and proposed practical classification-based surgical guidelines, reinforcing the need for standardized intraoperative decision-making [8]. Rather than routine supercharging, a selective approach based on intraoperative perfusion assessment may enhance reliability while maintaining the advantages of pedicled reconstruction. This study reports clinical outcomes of RPALT flap reconstruction guided by a structured intraoperative perfusion assessment algorithm with selective supercharging, focusing on larger or higher-demand peri-knee defects.

Methods

Study design

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Department of Trauma and Microreconstructive Surgery, First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University, Urumqi, China. The institutional review board approved the study protocol. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants for surgery and use of anonymized clinical data for research. This manuscript is reported in accordance with the STROBE statement for observational studies.

Consecutive patients undergoing peri-knee soft tissue reconstruction with RPALT flaps between January 2015 and August 2025 were evaluated. Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) peri-knee soft tissue defect requiring flap coverage; (2) reconstruction using RPALT flap; (3) complete perioperative records; and (4) minimum 6-month follow-up. Exclusion criteria were: (1) incomplete baseline or outcome data; (2) loss to follow-up; (3) major ipsilateral thigh vascular injury or previous thigh surgery compromising RPALT flap perfusion; and (4) severe peripheral vascular disease precluding reliable pedicled perfusion.

Preoperative planning

All patients underwent standardized preoperative vascular assessment. Handheld Doppler ultrasound identified cutaneous perforators and estimated the course of the LCFA descending branch and relevant peri-knee vessels. B-mode ultrasonography mapped the LCFA descending branch, superior lateral genicular artery (SLGA), their peri-knee communicating branches, and corresponding perfusion territories, and this information was used to refine flap-axis design, pivot-point selection, and potential recipient-vessel planning.

For flap design (Fig. 1), a reference line (AP line) was drawn from the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS, point A) to the superolateral patellar border (point P). The flap was designed according to defect dimensions and dominant perforators identified on ultrasonography. The pivot point (point R) was marked on the AP line at least 3 cm proximal to point P, corresponding to the peri-knee communication zone between the LCFA descending branch and SLGA. The proximal flap border (point F) was positioned at least 10 cm distal to the ASIS, consistent with the proximal origin of the LCFA system. To ensure adequate reach after rotation, the distance from point R to the most distant defect margin was planned to equal the distance from R to F. The flap was designed to be approximately 1–2 cm larger than the defect in both dimensions to minimize inset tension.

Following thorough debridement and definitive defect measurement, the flap was elevated in a subfascial plane from distal to proximal, preserving dominant perforators. Excessive pedicle dissection was avoided to reduce vasospasm and mechanical injury. The flap was then islanded and rotated (typically 120°–180°) to the recipient site. Before rotation, all flaps demonstrated satisfactory antegrade perfusion after harvest, with acceptable color, temperature, capillary refill, and distal bleeding, and no clinically evident vascular compromise was observed at baseline. The structured CTCD-based assessment described below was therefore intended to detect perfusion changes arising after distal transposition and rotation. Before inset, the pedicle course was optimized to prevent kinking, twisting, compression at the pivot point, or excessive traction. When necessary, additional soft tissue release created a tension-free tunnel or open pedicle route. To allow for potential selective supercharging, we routinely preserved and dissected free for approximately 2–3 cm (i) a sizable anatomically proximal flap draining vein within the pedicle system (typically a vena comitans) and (ii) an anatomically proximal arterial branch/perforator suitable for microanastomosis. After distal transposition/rotation, these preserved vessels became the most distal accessible flap vessels in the inset position and were therefore used for selective supercharging when indicated.

Perfusion assessment algorithm

After flap rotation and provisional positioning, a uniformly applied mandatory 40-minute observation period was implemented to allow hemodynamic equilibration. This waiting period allowed transient vasospasm or perfusion shifts to resolve, preventing premature intervention. During this period, flap perfusion was systematically evaluated using the CTCD criteria, which consist of color, temperature, capillary refill, and distal bleeding—four commonly accepted clinical parameters for intraoperative flap perfusion assessment.

During observation periods, we focused on (1) pedicle geometry optimization, (2) warming and avoiding compression, (3) hemostasis and recipient-vessel preparation, and (4) selective use of topical vasodilator irrigation per surgeon preference. Tourniquet release/deflation monitoring followed contemporary safety recommendations and evidence summaries [9].

Postoperative management

Postoperatively, the limb was elevated and the knee initially immobilized in extension using a brace or splint to protect the pedicle and inset. Flap monitoring included assessment of color, temperature, capillary refill, and bleeding response. Following wound stabilization, rehabilitation progressed stepwise from passive to active range of motion based on wound condition and patient tolerance.

Primary outcome was flap survival, categorized as complete survival, partial necrosis, or total loss. Secondary outcomes included knee function (HSS score) at final follow-up, scar quality (VSS and SCAR scale), follow-up duration, and postoperative complications. HSS scores were classified as excellent (≥ 85), good (70–84), fair (60–69), or poor (< 60). HSS, VSS, and SCAR assessments were performed by the same surgeon.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) with range, and categorical variables as number and percentage. Prior to parametric comparisons, continuous data were tested for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) to justify use of t-tests. For exploratory between-group comparisons, continuous variables (defect area, flap area, HSS, VSS, SCAR) were compared using Welch’s t-test, which accommodates unequal variances and small sample sizes. Categorical variables were compared descriptively due to limited sample sizes. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test when appropriate; otherwise, they were described descriptively due to small cell counts. Given the retrospective design and modest sample size (n = 23), analyses were exploratory rather than confirmatory. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons. Two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 31.0 (IBM Corp.).

Results

Demographics

Twenty-three patients met inclusion criteria: 17 male (73.9%), 6 female (26.1%), with mean age 38.48 ± 11.03 years. Defects were located in the prepatellar region (n = 9, 39.1%), lateral knee (n = 8, 34.8%), and popliteal fossa (n = 6, 26.1%). Etiologies included trauma (n = 17, 73.9%), infection (n = 5, 21.7%), and tumor resection (n = 1, 4.3%). Mean follow-up was 35.26 ± 22.25 months. Demographic data and clinical outcomes are summarized in Table 1. Selective supercharging was required in 8 patients (34.8%); 15 patients (65.2%) underwent reconstruction without vascular augmentation. Lateral knee defects accounted for the plurality of supercharged cases (4/8, 50.0%), consistent with Table 1.

Flap survival

All 23 flaps remained viable, with no total flap losses. Two complications were observed: one patient developed a postoperative vascular crisis and underwent emergent re-exploration; pedicle torsion was identified and corrected (detorsion), after which perfusion returned and the flap survived. Another patient had a small area of marginal proximal partial skin loss/necrosis at the proximal flap border (corresponding to point F in the flap design, i.e., the region farthest from the pivot point after rotation), managed with limited skin grafting. No cases of wound dehiscence, recurrent infection requiring reoperation, or delayed vascular compromise occurred.

Functional outcomes

At final follow-up, mean HSS score was 79.57 ± 7.87. Based on established HSS criteria (excellent ≥ 85, good 70–84, fair 60–69), 5 patients achieved excellent outcomes, 15 good, and 3 fair, yielding an excellent-to-good rate of 87.0% (20/23). Cosmetic outcomes were satisfactory. Mean VSS score was 3.13 ± 0.81, indicating mild scar hypertrophy and pigmentation—generally acceptable in lower extremity reconstruction. Mean SCAR score was 5.74 ± 1.05, reflecting favorable patient- and observer-rated scar appearance. Exploratory comparisons showed no significant differences in HSS, VSS, or SCAR scores between supercharging and no-supercharging groups (all P > 0.05; Table 2), suggesting selective supercharging effectively mitigated potential adverse effects of larger defect and flap sizes on functional and cosmetic outcomes.

Typical cases

Case 1

A 34-year-old woman sustained extensive soft-tissue defects of the right knee and proximal lower leg following a traffic accident. After temporary external fixation and serial debridement, a defect measuring 20 × 6 cm remained with exposure of underlying structures. An RPALT flap measuring 25 × 10 cm was designed on the ipsilateral thigh. After flap elevation and rotation, distal flap congestion manifested as purplish discoloration, suggesting venous outflow insufficiency. Venous supercharging was therefore performed by anastomosing a distal flap vein to the great saphenous vein. The flap was then inset without tension. At follow-up, the flap survived completely, and no wound-related or vascular complications were observed (Fig. 3).

A 61-year-old man presented with an open prepatellar soft-tissue defect complicated by knee joint infection. Following thorough debridement, the defect measured 8 × 8 cm. Discussion

In this retrospective cohort, peri-knee reconstruction using RPALT flaps guided by a structured intraoperative perfusion assessment yielded excellent flap viability with satisfactory functional and cosmetic outcomes. A clinically relevant finding was that selective supercharging proved necessary in approximately one-third of cases (34.8%) and was significantly associated with larger defect and flap dimensions. Importantly, HSS, VSS, and SCAR outcomes remained comparable between supercharged and non-supercharged groups. These findings support a pragmatic, mechanism-oriented strategy: even larger reconstructions can remain within a pedicled paradigm if surgical teams are prepared to augment perfusion selectively after confirming persistent compromise.

Arterial-predominant compromise was observed in 2 patients, and combined inflow/outflow compromise was observed in 2 additional patients. These cases presented after flap rotation with distal pallor, coolness, poor or absent capillary refill, and minimal distal bleeding. After exclusion of mechanical causes during the mandatory observation period, arterial supercharging was performed by anastomosing a preserved flap arterial branch—which became the accessible distal vessel after rotation—to a healthy peri-knee recipient artery outside the zone of injury. Although a representative arterial supercharging photo series was unavailable, the operative indication and decision-making process were consistent across these cases. All flaps with an arterial component of compromise were successfully salvaged and ultimately survived, underscoring the value of standardized assessment and timely mechanism-directed intervention.

Anatomical and hemodynamic considerations in retrograde perfusion

The RPALT flap depends on retrograde perfusion through peri-knee vascular communications between the genicular arterial network (including the SLGA system) and the LCFA descending branch territory [10]. Compared with conventional antegrade ALT perfusion, retrograde circulation may offer less physiologic reserve and appears more vulnerable to anatomic variation, small-caliber communicating vessels, vasospasm, and increased resistance following long-arc rotation [11]. Venous drainage presents even greater vulnerability because outflow may be compromised by compression or kinking at the pivot point and is often limited by the retrograde venous pathway [12, 13]. This hemodynamic profile explains why distal cyanosis or congestion may develop after rotation, and why initially acceptable appearance does not always predict stable long-term perfusion.

Why defect and flap size increases the likelihood of supercharging

Our data demonstrated significantly larger defect and flap areas in the supercharging group (P = 0.034 and P = 0.015). This association has physiologic basis: as flap size increases, distal tissue lies farther from the effective inflow source, perfusion pressure must overcome greater pathway length and resistance, and venous return must drain expanded capillary beds [14]. Additionally, very large skin paddles may extend beyond the reliable perfusion territory of the dominant perforator–LCFA descending branch axis in some patients, increasing distal hypoperfusion risk. Critically, the greater supercharging requirement for larger reconstructions does not imply that large RPALT flaps are inherently unsafe; rather, it indicates that larger flaps more often require intraoperative perfusion optimization to match tissue demand.

Value of a mandatory post-rotation observation window and stepwise optimization

A central protocol feature is the uniformly applied mandatory 40-minute post-rotation observation window. Immediately after transfer, flap physiology is influenced by temperature shifts, transient spasm, residual pre-equilibration perfusion, and pedicle geometry. Premature intervention risks unnecessary supercharging, whereas delayed intervention may permit progressive congestion or ischemia. A defined observation period improves reproducibility and decision discipline, particularly when coupled with stepwise mechanical correction (addressing malposition, twisting, tunnel compression, traction) before declaring persistent compromise. During observation, we reassess CTCD criteria after mechanical optimization. If supercharging is required, the additional microanastomosis typically added approximately 1.0–1.5 h to operative time in our practice.

Mechanism-directed selective supercharging and recipient vessel choice

Selective supercharging should be conceptualized as a planned extension of the reconstructive strategy in higher-risk scenarios rather than as failure rescue. The critical principle is matching augmentation to dominant mechanism. Venous-predominant congestion is typically addressed by venous supercharging to the GSV system or suitable tributaries, establishing an additional low-resistance outflow pathway and reducing distal venous hypertension. Arterial-predominant insufficiency can be addressed through arterial supercharging using reliable peri-knee recipient branches [15]. When combined compromise is evident, both inflow and outflow augmentation may be required. In the present cohort, timely mechanism-directed augmentation likely contributed to perfusion stabilization in larger reconstructions, which is consistent with the observation that final functional and scar outcomes remained similar between groups despite larger defect/flap burdens in supercharged cases.

In our setting, many patients had severe local soft-tissue injury around the knee, and the popliteal vessels could be compromised or located within the zone of injury. For these patients, converting to a free flap relying entirely on popliteal inflow/outflow may increase the risk of catastrophic total flap loss. Selective supercharging allows us to preserve the advantages of a pedicled flap while adding targeted microanastomosis to undamaged peri-knee vessels (e.g., GSV/tributaries for venous outflow and reliable peri-knee arterial branches for inflow) when persistent compromise is demonstrated.

Technical considerations: flap design, rotation, and pedicle handling

Several technical principles may reduce the risk of vascular compromise in retrograde pedicled designs. First, designing the flap slightly larger than the defect (approximately 1–2 cm in both dimensions) helps minimize inset tension and pedicle traction. Second, limiting rotation when possible and ensuring a tension-free pedicle course reduces the risk of pedicle twisting or compression at the pivot point. Third, avoiding excessive pedicle dissection may reduce spasm and mechanical injury to the LCFA descending branch and its communicating vessels. Finally, preoperative ultrasonography mapping deserves particular emphasis in this setting. In our protocol, handheld Doppler and B-mode ultrasonography were used not only to identify dominant perforators, but also to delineate the LCFA descending branch, the SLGA, their peri-knee communication zone, and potential recipient vessels, thereby facilitating flap-axis design, pivot-point selection, and contingency planning for selective supercharging in the presence of recognized anatomic variability [5, 16].

All RPALT flaps in this cohort were elevated in a subfascial plane. While flap thinning may improve contour and knee range of motion in antegrade ALT designs, thinning a reverse-flow/retrograde pedicled flap may jeopardize the subdermal plexus and choke vessels that contribute to distal perfusion. Therefore, we did not routinely thin the flap in this series. Future studies may evaluate carefully selected thinning strategies (e.g., staged debulking) to balance perfusion reliability and functional contour.

Positioning RPALT among reconstructive options

From a reconstructive algorithm perspective, RPALT flap with selective supercharging occupies a practical intermediate position between local muscle flaps and free tissue transfer [17]. Compared with gastrocnemius flaps, the RPALT flap can provide larger skin paddles and reach anterior/lateral peri-knee defects while preserving calf musculature. Compared with free flaps, it avoids full microvascular transfer and may reduce procedural burden, while still permitting limited microsurgical augmentation when truly required [18]. Based on our experience and this cohort, RPALT flaps reliably covered peri-knee defects and the proximal third of the leg; extension toward the mid-third may be feasible in selected cases but is more likely to require augmentation in large/long-arc rotations.

Our outcomes appear favorable compared to prior reports of reverse-flow ALT flaps without supercharging. For example, one series noted that a distally based ALT flap (skin paddle 12 × 20 cm) suffered ~ 2 cm of distal flap loss without augmentation [19]. In the present study, by using selective augmentation, we observed no total flap losses and only a minor distal edge necrosis in one case, suggesting that supercharging helped mitigate the known perfusion limitations of the reverse-flow ALT design.Our outcomes compare favorably with published distally based/reverse-flow ALT experiences, where venous congestion and partial distal necrosis remain common concerns—particularly for larger flaps—supporting the rationale for a selective, standardized supercharging algorithm in high-risk cases [8, 20].

Objective perfusion assessment (ICG-FA) versus clinical judgement

This study relied on standardized clinical CTCD assessment. Indocyanine green fluorescence angiography (ICG-FA) is inc

reasingly used to support intraoperative perfusion assessment, and recent work has advanced quantitative/algorithmic interpretation approaches, although access and cost vary across institutions [21–23]. When available, combining standardized clinical evaluation with ICG-FA may further refine supercharging indications.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective single-center design and modest sample size limit generalizability and allow only exploratory comparisons. Second, intraoperative perfusion assessments (CTCD criteria) and postoperative outcome scoring were performed by the treating surgeon, and assessors were not blinded to whether supercharging was performed; this could introduce observer and assessment bias. Third, we did not routinely use objective perfusion imaging such as indocyanine green fluorescence angiography (ICG-FA), which is increasingly used to support intraoperative perfusion assessment[24–26]; availability varies across institutions. Fourth, because patients with major ipsilateral thigh vascular injury, previous thigh surgery compromising RPALT perfusion, or severe peripheral vascular disease were excluded, our cohort represented a relatively selected population with more favorable pedicled perfusion conditions. Therefore, the present findings should not be extrapolated directly to these higher-risk settings, in which the feasibility of preserving a pedicled reconstructive strategy with selective supercharging rather than converting to free tissue transfer remains uncertain. Future multicenter prospective studies incorporating objective perfusion tools (e.g., ICG-FA) and broader patient inclusion may refine selection criteria for selective supercharging and strengthen external validity.

Conclusions

In this selected cohort, RPALT flap reconstruction guided by structured intraoperative perfusion assessment achieved complete flap survival with satisfactory functional and cosmetic outcomes for peri-knee soft tissue defects. Selective supercharging was more frequently required in larger defects and flaps, and may enhance reconstructive reliability in higher-demand cases. This approach represents a pragmatic intermediate strategy between local flaps and free tissue transfer, although its applicability to patients with major ipsilateral thigh vascular compromise or severe peripheral vascular disease remains uncertain.
