 Introduction
The Rock Valley Direct Comparison (RV/DC) is the third phase of the Source Physics Experiment (SPE;
Snelson et al., 2014), a project aimed at improving seismic discrimination between explosive sources and
natural earthquakes. Phases I and II of SPE focused on studying the generation of shear waves in
endmember geologies (granite and alluvium, respectively), while Phase III aims to detonate a chemical
explosion co-located with the source region of the 1993 Rock Valley earthquake sequence, located in the
Rock Valley Fault Zone (RVFZ), in the southern portion of the Nevada Test Site (now the Nevada
National Security Site, or NNSS).

The Rock Valley earthquake sequence comprises ~200 recorded earthquakes between May and
November 1993, with the largest being Mw 3.8 (Shields, 1999). These earthquakes are unusually shallow
for natural events – early analysis, including the observation of S minus P arrival times of 0.5 s or less at
nearby station RTPP, puts the depths of the earthquakes to be generally shallower than 2 km below the
ground surface (Shields, 1999), and recent re-analysis of nine of the largest of these earthquakes has
constrained their source depths to within 3 km of the surface (Pyle et al., 2023a). As shallow source depth
may be used to discriminate a seismic event as an explosion, RV/DC seeks to record and explore the
differences between these 1993 earthquakes and a controlled-source chemical explosion in a similar
location.

In this study, we seek to use near-regional seismic data to study the 3D Vp and Vs structure, seismicity
distributions, and fault motions of the RVFZ and surrounding area. We leverage the extensive seismic
catalogs developed by the Nevada Seismological Laboratory (NSL), active source data, and continuous
seismic data acquired throughout the NNSS and surroundings to build a well-constrained model for the
region.

The resulting velocity model and relocated hypocenters are available from the author by request.

 Data and joint inversion for 3D velocity structure and location
We use JointTomoDD (Syracuse et al., 2017) to develop a 3D Vp and Vs model and seismicity locations
for the RVFZ and southern NNSS region. JointTomoDD uses any combination of P-wave arrival, S-wave
arrival, surface wave dispersion, and gravity data to jointly invert for 3D velocity structure, and optionally
density and seismicity locations, depending on the dataset(s) used. In JointTomoDD, all datasets are
included in an iterative least-squares inversion of a Gm=d equation containing partial derivatives, model
perturbations, and data for all included datasets, ensuring consistency among the results. The combination
of multiple data types utilizes the varying sensitivity of each, providing a single Earth model that is
consistent will all datasets and often minimally sacrifices the fit of one in order to fit the other(s). In the
work presented here, P-wave arrival, S-wave arrival, and Rayleigh wave group velocity measurements are
used.

 Earthquake travel time data

The earthquake-based body wave dataset consists of P- and S-wave first arrival picks from the NSL for
earthquakes from 2000 – 2024, with data for some additional earthquakes from the 1993 series (Figure 2).
Some of the 1993 events have been manually repicked to ensure consistency of picking waveform
features among the events, but a more extensive repicking of the data is not yet available at this time. We
include arrivals from 69 stations, including two in Rock Valley (RTPP and RVEE) that have both surface
and borehole instruments.

Reported pick time uncertainties are translated from time to a relative weight between 0 and 1. Because
we are particularly interested in recovering the depths of the shallow earthquakes in Rock Valley, P- and
S-wave arrivals times that are within 1 s of each other and recorded at the same station for a given event
(indicative of a relatively shallow source depth) are multiplied by a factor of 10, thereby placing a greater
weight on fitting observations for these close-in stations than more distant ones.

Differential P-wave and differential S-wave travel times are also calculated, using ph2dt from the
HYPODD package (Waldhauser, 2001). These differential times are calculated for pairs of earthquakes
that are within 3 km of each other and recorded by at least 10 stations in common. Inclusion of these as a
constraint distinct from recorded arrival times improves inter-event locations and can improve the
resolution of near-source velocity structure (Zhang and Thurber, 2003, 2006).

A variety of measures are taken to ensure the quality of the body wave dataset. First, earthquake sources
with an azimuthal gap greater than 180° are removed. As some catalog locations are placed at the nearest
recording station location, earthquakes must first be relocated in the initial 1D velocity model to properly
calculate this azimuthal gap. Additionally, an earthquake must have at least 10 recorded arrivals,
including 1 S arrival, in order to be included in the velocity inversion. Because Little Skull Mountain has
a high rate of seismicity compared to the rest of the study region (more than 50% of the earthquakes in the
study region from the NSL catalog are around Little Skull Mountain), earthquakes in this area must meet
a higher threshold of having least 15 recorded arrivals to be included in the inversion, in order to make
sampling across the study area more even.

To further ensure the quality of the body wave dataset, preliminary bootstrapping uncertainty analysis is
conducted for earthquake locations. One hundred realizations of earthquake observations are created, in
which arrival times are perturbed randomly in a normal distribution using their reported arrival time
uncertainties as the standard deviation for the distribution, and starting location is perturbed randomly
with a 0.5 km standard deviation for the distribution. Earthquakes are relocated using these 100
realizations and error ellipse are calculated; those with a value over 0.1 km2 for product of the semi-major
and semi-minor axis lengths are omitted from use in the velocity inversion, removing about 10% of the
earthquake population.

While the NSL omits arrival time data that their analysts suspect to be affected by data timing issues,
some affected arrival times may still exist in their catalog. Through review of travel time residuals
obtained in preliminary 3D inversions of the dataset, a small number of arrivals with such suspected
issues are removed (fewer than 100 total). These quality constraints resulted in a dataset of over 6400
earthquakes, 70000 P-wave arrival times, and 50000 S-wave arrival times to be used in the velocity
inversion.

For the final set of earthquakes shown in Figure 2, we used an expanded version of the body wave dataset
to include more earthquakes, with the aim of better illuminating fault structures through the inclusion of
smaller seismicity. This relocation-focused dataset was relocated in the final 3D velocity model obtained
using the previously described dataset. All the same quality-control measures were taken for the
relocation-focused dataset, with the exception of relaxing the minimum number of arrivals required to
include an earthquake within 10 km of the 1993 series to at least 4 arrivals, including 1 S arrival. For
relocations of all earthquakes in the final 3D velocity model, cross-correlation times between similar
waveforms recorded at a station for a pair of earthquakes were calculated and included in the relocation
inversion, in order to further constrain inter-earthquake locations.

 Active-source body wave data

A recent accelerated weight drop (AWD) survey of Rock Valley (Harding et al., 2021; 2023) provides
additional constraints on the shallow velocity structure near the 1993 earthquakes. The survey consists of
a total of 553 source locations recorded by 188 three-component geophones. At each source location, both
vertical and angled ground impacts were performed, producing both P- and S-wave signals observable at
the geophones. Due to the broader focus of our study in comparison to the AWD work and the lack of
ability to refine grid node spacing in the survey area, the AWD dataset was down-selected to include data
from 17 evenly spaced source locations recorded at up to 90 geophones, resulting in the inclusion of 303
arrival times from this dataset (Figure 3). Due to the well-known source locations and times of the sources
and quality of the data, the travel times from this dataset were weighted by a factor of 5 in comparison to
the earthquake-based dataset.

 Ambient-noise interferometry for surface wave dispersion

Ambient-noise-based Rayleigh wave group velocities provide complementary constraints to the body
wave dataset, providing additional coverage of the area at a range of depths, including at the near-surface,
where body wave raypaths are subvertical, and potentially below the deepest seismicity.

To calculate interferometric ambient-noise-based velocities, vertical-channel waveforms are first
preprocessed, correcting for response, converting to displacement, downsampling to 10 samples per
second, and broadly filtered (0.5-50 s). Thirty-minute windows of data are then cross-correlated in the
frequency domain, converted back to the time domain, and stacked over a year.

Stacking over less than a year also provides good-quality results, but workflows in place allow this
process to be continued for longer durations with little effort, and they allow the capture of station pairs
that may not be available during a selected shorter-duration period within the year. The station
distributions in and around the NNSS have varied considerably since 2000. With some stations

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 5

operational only for shorter durations due to targeted experiments (e.g., SPE Phase I or II), it is wise to
utilize the varying station distributions over time to maximize coverage. As such, this process is repeated
over 7 unique time periods (2004, 2008, 2012, 2014, 2016, 2018, and 2023) to optimize coverage (Figure
4).

Each year-long cross correlation was visually assessed for Rayleigh wave content. Those for which
Rayleigh wave signals were observed were analyzed using the pgswmfa software (Ammon,
http://eqseis.geosc.psu.edu/cammon/). Measurements of Rayleigh wave group velocity and period were
limited to within 5-29 s period, where signal was observable from the primary microseism and potentially
the secondary microseism, and are sampled at 1 s increments.

This strategy resulted in many repeated measurements. In the vast majority of the cases, measurements
were replicated within uncertainties for all years considered; in these cases, the repeated results were
averaged over all measurements for a station pair. In a minority of cases, differences were observed.
While temporal changes in surface wave velocity can be caused by changes in material properties, two
instrument-related issues are presumed to be the cause of the changes observed here. In some cases, a
subtle difference in observed Rayleigh wave frequency content can be correlated with changes in
instrument type of one of the station pair, and the observed change in signal may be due to incomplete
response information. In these cases, the difference was subtle enough that all data were considered in the
averaging. In other cases, a distinct shift in timing between Rayleigh waves was observed, indicating that
this is likely a station timing issue. In these cases, the timing issue could usually be isolated to a single
station among several station pairs and a short time period, up to several months, between two durations
of the station being out of operation. Additionally, during many of these suspected timing issues, the NSL
reported no arrival times for the station with the suspected issue, indicating that their analysts likely also
suspected a timing issue, possibly identified by large travel time residuals. In cases where we were able to
identify a likely station timing issue, the issue was isolated in time and cross correlations using that
station were removed. The final surface wave dataset is weighted at 20 times that of the body wave data
in the inversion (see Section 2.4).

 Joint Inversion for 3D Velocity Structure and Location

The 3D model is defined at grid nodes that are regularly spaced horizontally at 1 km intervals, with the
origin of the grid centered on RV/DC surface ground zero (SGZ) and in depth ranging from 0.2 km
intervals near the surface to 10 km intervals at the bottom of the model at 100 km depth (far below where
the model is well-constrained by the data, but required in order to properly predict surface wave
dispersion). Two additional horizontal rings of nodes are spaced 20 and 120 km from the main part of the
inversion region, which allows ambient-noise-based dispersion measurements to be included for station
pairs where one of the station pair is outside of the main inversion region. All depths referred to here are
relative to mean sea level, so negative depths exist for near-surface features within the topography.

Prior to the 3D inversion, body wave arrival times and surface wave dispersion measurements are jointly
inverted for a 1D Vp and Vs model, serving as starting point for the 3D inversion (Figure 5). This
inversion served mainly to improve the fit to the surface wave dispersion (Table 1). For the full 3D
inversion, a large range of damping, smoothing, and relative data weights are explored (Figure 6), as well
as the total number of iterations for the inversion. From these results, improvements in fit to each data
type and Vp and Vs model lengths are compared in a multi-dimensional L-curve approach (Menke, 1984;
Syracuse et al., 2017) to empirically determine the optimal combination of parameters that fit the data
well and limit the overall model lengths from 1D (Figure 5). The inversion is carried out for two
iterations, with earthquakes relocated after each. In each iteration, Vp is allowed to perturb by up to 0.3
km/s and Vs by up to 0.15 km/s. Allowing the inversion to continue for additional iterations or with larger

perturbations causes instabilities in the model and data misfits. Data misfits before and after 1D and 3D
inversion are shown in Table 1.

Several advancements have been applied to the inversion technique since it was presented in Syracuse et
al. (2017). For local regions with significant topography variations, grid nodes lying above the ground
surface are given an arbitrarily low velocity, to ensure that rays are not traced through the air. These
nodes are also omitted from the surface wave dispersion measurements, improving consistency among the
constraints from the datasets. Surface wave dispersion measurements are now directly included and traced
in straight-line paths through the model, whereas they previously needed to be inverted for a series of
period-specific 2D surface wave velocity maps, and those maps were then included as a constraint in the
joint inversion. This removal of an intermediate inversion step aims to improve fit to the raw observations
and allow the recovery of finer-scale features in the velocity structure.

While the influence of both P- and S-wave velocities to Rayleigh wave velocities is accounted for in their
forward calculation, this sensitivity is only considered in the inversion for S-wave velocities, not P-wave
velocities. Preliminary inversion results indicated that their inclusion caused large, poorly resolved
perturbations in deeper portions of the model where there were no body wave raypaths to additionally
constrain velocities.

 Velocity results
The southern NNSS is geologically complex, and there is limited information linking surficial features to
the subsurface. The Vp and Vs models recovered here provide additional constraints on the subsurface
expressions of a variety of features. This report focuses on presenting the results of the inversions, and
further geologic interpretation is planned to be presented in a forthcoming manuscript.

Before interpreting velocity results, we must be able to evaluate in what areas features of a given size are
likely to be reliably recovered. Checkerboard tests are often used for this purpose, although there are
several limitations to their use, e.g., sensitivity of results to dimensions and positions of anomalies
horizontally and in depth; regular pattern of anomalies do not represent geology; susceptibility to
smearing. Instead of using such checkerboard tests to assess velocity recovery, we evaluate velocity
recovery using a suite of 100 random velocity models. Length scales and amplitudes of features within
these random models roughly represent those observed in the actual recovered velocity models.

From the 100 random models tested here, semblance (Zelt, 1998) is calculated and used to demarcate
areas of the study region that are likely to have more reliable velocity patterns recovered. Semblance is a
measure of the fit between the recovered velocity model and the target model used to calculate the
synthetic body and surface wave data used in the inversion, with values ranging between 0 to 1, with 1
indicating perfect fit. For misfit at a given node, it considers velocity recovery at neighboring nodes

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 10

within in a small distance, here within 8 km laterally and within 4 nodes vertically (ranging between 1
and 40 km, depending on node depth). The semblance results for the 100 models tested are averaged, and
outlines of areas with a semblance value of 0.7 or greater are overlain on velocity maps and cross sections
in Figures 7 through 10, outlining areas of relatively confident velocity recovery.

The velocity results show significant 3D structure throughout the region. Beneath Rock Valley, near-
surface low velocities extend to slightly deeper depths than a few kilometers north or south, indicating
some possibility of fault offset of geologic structures. A velocity and seismicity profile below RV/DC
SGZ is highlighted in Figure 11. A distinct transition from near-surface low velocities to greater velocities
at depths occurs between 0 and 0.5 km depth, corresponding to between approximately 1.2 and 1.7 km
below the surface. Recent Corehole drilling 0.12 km W of this location indicates that top of the Paleozoic
layer is at 1.18 km below the surface, corresponding well with the top of this velocity transition in depth
(Freimuth et al., 2025). Based on this, we may be able to use a Vp contour of somewhere between 5.2 and
5.5 km/s and a Vs contour of between 2.8 and 3.0 km/s as an indication of the top of the Paleozoic layer,
which would allow this point measurement to be extended laterally through the region. However, caution
must be exercised when doing this far beyond Rock Valley, as other areas of the study region, discussed
below, show additional complexity. Additionally, the AWD survey and dense station coverage within
Rock Valley provide greater resolution in the shallowest layers here compared to other areas of the
model, which make comparisons of shallow structure across the entire study region difficult.

Looking outside of Rock Valley at a broad scale, low-velocity features observed at 0-5 km depth relate to
low-velocity features nearer to the surface such as Frenchman Flat or Little Skull Mountain, although they
are not always continuous in depth. Beneath Frenchman Flat (12 ≤ x ≤ 18, -13 ≤ y ≤ 2; Figures 9, 10), Vp
below 5.5 km/s persists to 4 km depth, while Vs reaches above 3.0 km/s as shallow as 0.5 km depth,
indicating that the possible criteria to designate the top of the Paleozoic that fits Rock Valley may not be
applicable here. Other studies (Slate at al. 1999; Wagoner and Prothro, 2020) show that alluvial layers are
on the order of a few hundred meters thick or less, with volcanics and Paleozoic layers below that.
Beneath Frenchman Flat, the region of Vp<5.5 km/s to 4 km depth may indicate the depth extent of
Miocene volcanics.

Velocities surrounding Little Skull Mountain vary laterally, with generally low Vp (<5.5 km/s) to 4 km
depth to the NW of the surface fault expression (-20 ≤ x ≤ -6, -15 ≤ y ≤ -5; Figures 9,10) and variable to
high Vp (>6 km/s) at the same depth to the SE of the fault (-20 ≤ x ≤ -14, -5 ≤ y ≤ 2; Figures 9,10). These
two regions appear to be underlain by dipping high- and low-velocity structure at 5-10 km depth, possibly
aligned with the Little Skull Mountain seismicity. Assuming that these regions are both within the
Paleozoic, this points to evidence of possible extensive faulting and thrusting within the Paleozoic, of
which there is evidence for elsewhere in the broader geologic region (e.g., Death Valley; Niemi, 2002)
but geologic maps of the study region do not suggest (e.g., Slate et al., 1999).

 Earthquake locations, focal mechanisms, and implications for
 faulting
Following 3D velocity inversion, a final bootstrapping analysis is conducted for uncertainty in earthquake
locations using the expanded body wave dataset that includes earthquakes with as few as 4 arrivals. As
with the preliminary bootstrapping analysis used to determine which earthquakes were included in the
velocity inversion, 100 realizations of earthquake observations are created, in which arrival times are
perturbed randomly in a normal distribution using their reported arrival time uncertainties as the standard
deviation for the distribution. Starting locations are perturbed randomly with a 0.5 km standard deviation
for the distribution. Cross-correlation times between similar waveforms are included in the inversion but
are not perturbed. Earthquakes are relocated using these 100 realizations and one-standard-deviation
epicentral confidence ellipses are calculated. Confidence in depth is assessed as the standard deviation of
depth resulting from the 100 realizations. Results are shown in Figures 12, 13, and 14. Only earthquakes
with epicentral confidence ellipses with an area of less than 0.314 km2 are shown here and throughout this
document; this removes about 7% of the 8400 earthquakes in the dataset (Figure 15).

The general orientations of confidence ellipses indicate that uncertainties are largest in the NNW-SSE
direction. Since this is the across-strike direction for most faults with surface expressions in and around
Rock Valley, this means that errors in earthquake locations will have an impact on any interpretations for
subsurface fault locations. But as epicentral and depth confidence are correlated, careful selection of

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 24

earthquakes in Rock Valley would produce a further downselected dataset that could be used to better
constrain subsurface fault locations. However, as there is scatter in earthquake locations beyond
calculated confidences, it must be noted that this analysis does not capture the full uncertainties in
locations.

In comparison to original locations from the NSL catalog, relocation moves earthquake epicenters 0.94
km laterally and depths move an average of 0.96 km, with no overall trend in epicentral or depth shift. In
many areas, seismicity that is scattered in the NSL catalog relocates to a collapsed feature; this occurs
mainly for clusters outside of Rock Valley (Figures 16, 17).

Areas of relocated seismicity that align into fault-like features are investigated further, and focal
mechanisms are calculated where waveforms are available and azimuthal coverage is sufficient. P-wave
first motions are evaluated manually, and earthquakes occurring in similar locations and with similar
patterns of first motions are grouped. From these groups, composite events are built, leveraging variable
station coverage over time to provide better azimuthal sampling of each group. The polarity direction
must agree for at least 2/3 of the measurements made at a station in order for it to be included in
calculating a composite focal mechanism. Mechanisms are then calculated using HASH (Hardebeck and
Shearer, 2002) using a variety of 1D velocity models appropriate for the region (e.g., Anderson and
Myers, 2010; K. Gao, personal comm., 2025; J. Gochenour, personal comm., 2025; Hoffman and
Mooney, 1983; Li et al., 2024; T. Luckie, personal comm., 2025; Preston et al., 2019; Pyle et al., 2023b),
including a 1D average of the final 3D model recovered in this study. The results are shown in Figures 18
and 19, and they are generally consistent with those determined in Chatterjee et al. (2025).

Interpreting the earthquake locations and focal mechanism together provides constraints on subsurface
faulting. While the focal mechanisms are not constrained by dense enough sampling to provide precise
fault orientation information, they do provide insight into senses of motion on faults. In Rock Valley,
seismicity indicates that faults are steeply dipping to the SE (dip of ~80°), shallower than 5 km depth, and
with multiple fault strands likely active since 1993 (Figure 9 -4 ≤ x ≤ 4 and Figure 16, features K, L, M).
Assuming generally WSW-ENE fault strikes, seismicity along Rock Valley faults is generally left-lateral
strike slip, with some component of extension (Figure 18 K, L, M). Seismicity in western Rock Valley
(Figure 9 -8 ≤ x ≤ -6 and Figure 16, area I) does not show clear fault orientations or dips, but focal
mechanisms agree with those seen in central Rock Valley (Figure 18 I). Similarly, seismicity in
northeastern Rock Valley (Figure 9 2 ≤ x ≤ 6 and Figure 16, feature N) appears diffuse, although one
near-vertically dipping WSW-ENE lineation is identified, with a similar focal mechanism to others in
Rock Valley (Figure 18 N). Note that because the majority of shallow seismicity in Rock Valley used in
this study is from 1993, limited station coverage and waveform availability for those earthquakes prevents
the calculation of focal mechanisms in greater detail.

West of Rock Valley and south of Little Skull Mountain, seismicity on and around the Specter Range
faults is generally oriented N-S, similar to the orientation of the surface expressions of the faults (Figure 9
-18 ≤ x ≤ -10 and Figure 16, features A, F, G). Seismicity is clustered into three N-S features, which are
not clearly visible in the NSL seismicity, but they are visible in the relocations derived in this study.
Moving from south to north, cluster A forms a sheet that dips steeply (70°) to the NNE, cluster F deepens
to the NNW at 30°, and cluster G forms a vertically oriented sheet with a slight deepening to the north.
While cluster A may be a southern extension of a Specter Range fault seen at the surface, the other two
features may indicate additional faults present below the surface. Although focal mechanisms for these
clusters (Figure 18 A, F, G), are largely similar to those seen elsewhere throughout Rock Valley, the
generally N-S orientations of the seismicity lineations indicates that motion on these faults may actually
be right-lateral, in comparison to the left-lateral motion seen elsewhere in the region (Figure 18 I, L, M).

Seismicity at Little Skull Mountain highlights activity along a dominant SE-dipping fault (dip of ~30-40°,
Figure 9 -18 ≤ x ≤ -16 and Figure 16, features B, D, E) between 7-11 km depth, with some activity on a
NW-dipping conjugate fault near the southern end of the dominant fault (5-7 km depth, dip of ~60°;
Figure 9 -18 ≤ x ≤ -14 and Figure 16, feature C), as observed previously (e.g., Smith et al., 2001). In
contrast to Smith et al. (2001) who found extensional fault motion on the dominant fault, we find
primarily left-lateral strike-slip motion on the dominant fault (Figure 16 B, E). We also find some
earthquakes that show extensional motion, albeit at a different orientation than Smith et al. (2001) (Figure
18 D). The focal mechanism obtained for earthquakes in the conjugate fault is also left-lateral strike slip
(Figure 18 C), consistent with Smith et al. (2001). Additional deeper conjugate fault seismicity at the
northeastern end of the dominant fault is newly identified here (7-10 km depth, Figure 9 x=-10 and Figure
16, feature H), which shows some extensional motion (Figure 18 H). Seismicity along both the dominant
and conjugate faults are more tightly clustered in the relocations derived here than in the NSL catalog.

Other outlying features include a steeply dipping SW-NE lineation at 6-7 km depth beneath Skull
Mountain (Figure 9 x = 6 and Figure 16, feature O). Its focal mechanism suggests dip-slip motion on a
vertical N-S fault, but its pattern of first motions combined with the limited coverage to the south and east
suggests that left-lateral strike-slip motion on a SW-NE fault would produce the same first motions as
those observed and be more consistent with other observations in the area. In the NSL catalog, this feature
appears as a diffuse area of seismicity.

North of Little Skull Mountain is another steeply dipping lineation of seismicity between 4-7 km depth,
with an unclear orientation in map view (Figure 9 x = -6 and Figure 16, feature J). Assuming a SW-NE
orientation to the faults, its focal mechanism is left-lateral strike-slip and is well-constrained azimuthally
(Figure 18 J).

 Summary
The velocity model and earthquake relocations resulting from this study provide additional constraints on
the subsurface structure in Rock Valley and surrounding areas. Relocated seismicity both refines features
observable in the NSL catalog and illuminates new ones not previously seen, while improving fit to the P-
wave arrival time dataset by 70% and the S-wave arrival time dataset by 68%, relative to a 1D velocity
model.

The velocity model recovered here can be used both to better understand the geology and history of the
region and as input to modeling and simulation work. It can also be used to help constrain future updates
to the Rock Valley Geologic Framework Model (Downs and Gochenour, 2025).

Future work will focus on

 • comparison of relocation results produced by the velocity model obtained here to those produced
 using other velocity models,

 • the incorporation of additional seismic data sources in Rock Valley, including arrival times from
 the LASSO array and Corehole instruments, and

 • the incorporation of gravity data into the inversion, to produce an updated version of the velocity
 model and an accompanying density model.
