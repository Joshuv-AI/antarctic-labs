 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 Hazen Research, Inc., in collaboration with University of Colorado, Boulder (CU-B) and
 National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL), will develop and demonstrate a ceramic-
 polymer hybrid membrane based on garnet (Li7La3Zr2O12) and PVDF (Poly Vinylidene
 Fluoride)-HFP (Hexafluoropropylene) adaptable to Li-metal anodes. Under this CRADA,
 NREL will test the developed membranes providing feedback and advice to demonstrate
 hybrid membranes with Li-ion conductivity >10-4 S/cm at room temperature that are stable
 with Li-metal, followed by demonstration of full cells with Li-metal anode.

 Summary of Research Results:

 This report contains Protected CRADA Information, which was produced on 01/26/2021 under
 CRADA No. CRD-19-00807 and is not to be further disclosed for a period of five (5) years from
 the date it was produced except as expressly provided for in the CRADA.

 Hazen Research, Inc., in collaboration with the University of Colorado, Boulder (CU-B) and
 NREL, demonstrated the fabrication of the proposed compositionally gradient, cubic
 Li6.25Al0.25La3Zr2O12 (LiLZO)–polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF)–hexafluoropropylene (HFP)
 hybrid membranes for potential use in Li-metal batteries. We demonstrated novel inorganic–
 organic composite and combustible precursors for the synthesis of LiLZO through solid state
 reactions (SSR) in a muffle furnace and used a spray pyrolysis reactor (SPR) and fluidized-bed
 reactor (FBR). Pure LiLZO and the hybrid membranes were fabricated, characterized, and
 evaluated for Li-ion conductivity. Unfortunately, the highest Li-ion conductivity determined was
 approximately 0.35 x 10-6 S/cm, 1/30 of our target of 10-6 S/cm for a garnet-type solid state
 electrolyte. If higher Li-ionic conductivity can be demonstrated, the methods and the membrane
 architecture may have the potential for Li-metal battery applications.

 The order of the tasks was reshuffled during the course of the project to do the measurement last
 (i.e., Task 1 from Measurement of Li-Ion conductivity to Stability checking, Task 2 from Stability
 checking with Li-metal anodes to Fabrication and Task 3 from Fabrication and testing of full cell
 to the Measurement of the conductivity).

 Approach: The original hypothesis and technical approach of the Hazen–CU–NREL team was to
 use novel inorganic–organic composite precursor chemistries to facilitate a self-propagating
 combustion reaction and use spray pyrolysis combined with fluidized-bed reactions for the
 synthesis of submicron sized LiLZO powders. We proposed developing inorganic–organic
 composite precursors to produce cubic Al-doped LiLZO using Li-, Al-, La-nitrates and acetates,
 and Zr-propionate (Zr-OPr) as the starting materials. We hypothesized a combustion reaction
 below 500°C and formation of submicron LiLZO powders above 750°C during SSR in an SPR
 and FBR. If successful, these powders would be made into a slurry using dimethylformamide
 (DMF) as the solvent and tape-casted onto a porous substrate. DMF has been shown1 to chemically
 interact with LiLZO and to improve the Li-ion conductivity when incorporated into a PVDF-based
 polymer matrix. The PVDF–HFP copolymer containing LiLZO in DMF then will be tape-casted
 onto the first LiLZO layer. This composite membrane will be released from the substrate and
 thermally processed to remove the solvents. A very thin layer of LiF then will be deposited and
 processed on the LiLZO surface using a novel metal organic chemistry. The final product will be
 a compositionally gradient LiLZO–(PVDF–HFP) composite with a LiF passivated and dense
 LiLZO surface layer as the interface with the Li-metal anode.

 Results: Our technical approach was to fabricate the proposed membrane architecture through a
 tape-casting method involving interfacial polymerization, which is self-limiting with respect to the
 thickness of the layer formed and can be controlled from 10 nm thick layers. We proposed a
 method involving suspension of the submicron-sized particles of LiLZO in dimethylacetamide
 (DMAC), a dipolar aprotic solvent, using combined mechanical stirring and sonication. The
 suspension will be divided in thirds, with two parts continuing the mechanical mixing processes.
 The remaining one part of the premixed LiLZO–DMAC suspension will be roll-coated onto a

 2

 microporous, organic solvent-resistant, high porosity casting web, and the excess DMAC solvent
 will be vacuum filtered away from the LiLZO particles to form a 3–5 μm thin dense particle layer
 after filtering. The other two parts of the premixed LiLZO suspension will be mixed with PVDF–
 HFP co-polymer to create LiLZO–PVDF–HFP volume ratios of 2:1 and 1:1 when the solvent is
 removed in the subsequent steps. These two suspension mixtures will be applied atop the dense
 particle layer in the order of 2:1 and 1:1 (inorganic/polymer) ratios. The thickness of each
 suspension applied controls the overall and gradient thicknesses of the final membrane structure
 created. The multilayer structure then will be immersed into a nonsolvent bath to complete the
 phase inversion (vitrification) of the LiLZO-containing polymer phase. The vitrified membrane
 structure is removed from the immersion bath and the robust, casting web is peeled away leaving
 a multilayer LiLZO-gradient composite membrane. Upon drying, the composite structure will have
 a dense LiLZO-rich layer on one side and a PVDF–HFP composite on the other side. We
 formulated, synthesized, and characterized inorganic–metalorganic precursor solutions using Li-,
 Al-, La-nitrates and acetates and Zr-propionate (Zr-OPr) to produce cubic Al-doped LiLZO with
 the composition Li6.75Al0.25La3Zr2O12. High-purity starting materials were procured from
 commercial vendors.

 Task 1: Stability Checking of LiLZO Material

 Figure 1 shows the thermogravimetric analysis–differential scanning calorimetry (TGA–DSC)
 data of the precursor LiLZO-PRS-001 developed. Two exothermic events < 400°C correspond to
 La-nitrate decomposition and Zr-OPr combustion reactions near 290 and 340°C, respectively.
 Based on these data, we chose the initial processing conditions in the FBR at 750°C for 3 h. Figure
 2 shows the typical XRD patterns of LiLZO powders produced in a muffle furnace at 750°C,
 LiLZO produced in the SPR at 750°C and FBR at 900°C, and a purchased LiLZO sample used by
 CU to synthesize hybrid membranes. Unlike the data from the muffle furnace, reasonably pure
 LiLZO powders only formed in the FBR at 900°C. Figure 3 shows the scanning electron
 microscopy (SEM) images of the LiLZO powder and pellet produced using the baseline precursor
 chemistry.

 Task 2: Fabrication and Testing of the LiLZO-(PVdF-HFP) Composite Membrane:

 The composite membranes were made in a glove box under a dry N2 purge with the commercial
 LiLZO powders because the Li-ion conductivity of the samples we produced was lower than that
 of the powder. In this chamber, individual aliquots were weighed into glass vials under dry N2 and
 sealed for later use in the various experiments listed in Table 3.

 We varied the following five parameters to identify the most promising formulation to meet the
 program goals:

 • Volume fraction of LiLZO versus PVDF–HFP
 • Casting recipe and mixing protocols (suspension solvent, methods and proportions of
 addition, mixing time, and degassing time)
 • Thickness of the layer before vitrification and the casting surface (e.g., glass, PTFE, and
 PDMS)
 • Vitrification methodology (dry evaporation under N2/wet immersion in a poor solvent-like
 toluene-phase inversion, proportions of each step with respect to time and temperature)
 • Post-vitrification drying and heat treatment

 Task 3: Ionic Conductivity Measurement of Pure LiLZO Pellets and LiLZO-(PVdF-HFP)
 Composite Membranes

 During the Phase I effort, 18 samples of the composite membranes and 6 sintered pellets of LiLZO
 powders were submitted to NREL to measure the Li-ion conductivity. The samples were placed in
 a vacuum chamber and, upon arrival at NREL, they were cut into the required size and shape in a
 glove box and kept under Ar atmosphere just before the Li-ion conductivity measurement. Li-ion
 conductivities were measured using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) using silver
 paint as the blocking electrode. EIS measurements were conducted under a single frequency of
 1000 Hz and an AC potential amplitude of 100 mV. Figure 10 shows the sandwich cell used for
 EIS and the stacking sequence of the electrodes and membranes in the sandwich cell. More than

 Summary and Conclusions: Our Phase I efforts identified three promising mixed inorganic–
 organic precursors for the synthesis of LiLZO at 750°C. We demonstrated SPR and FBR
 processing methods for the formation of LiLZO powders. We also were able to demonstrate the
 fabrication of the proposed compositionally gradient LiLZO–(PVDF–HFP) hybrid membranes.
 However, the best Li-ion conductivity of the hybrid membranes was approximately 0.35x10-6
 S/cm and needs to be improved. Based on the literature, three reasons for the lower than expected
 Li-ion conductivity in pure LiLZO and/or LiLZO–(PVDF–HFP) hybrid membranes are as follows:

 • Larger LiLZO particle size
 • Li-carbonate surface impurities on LiLZO particles surface or in the grain boundaries of
 the pellets
 • Limited Li-ion percolation paths in the hybrid membrane due to LiLZO particle
 aggregation

 Low ionic conductivity in the compositionally gradient LiLZO–(PVDF–HFP) hybrid membranes
 also can be attributed to exposure to moisture and CO2-containing atmosphere during or after the
 synthesis of the LiLZO powders, fabrication of the membranes, subsequent handling, and surface
 contact roughness.
