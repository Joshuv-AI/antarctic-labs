Spectral Properties of Dense Barabási–Albert Graphs
Steven J. Miller, Arman Rysmakhanov

Abstract




Abstract

Preferential attachment graphs model networks whose growth produces highly uneven degree distributions, describing many real-world systems. Their adjacency spectra are important because they allow graph-theoretic questions to be studied through the eigenvalues of matrices. We analyze the adjacency matrix of a dense Barabási–Albert (B–A) multigraph, where the number of edges added at each step is proportional to the final number of vertices. First, we compute the large-
n
limit of the expected adjacency matrix and show that it is described by a rank-one limiting kernel, viewed as a continuous analogue of the adjacency matrix. After centering and scaling, the fluctuations form a random matrix with a computable variance profile. Using the quadratic vector equation approach, we derive the limiting bulk spectral distribution. We also determine the asymptotic location of the leading eigenvalue generated by the rank-one mean component.

1
Introduction

1.1
Background and related work

Spectral properties of adjacency matrices of graphs, such as eigenvalue distributions and leading eigenvalues/eigenvectors, provide compact summaries of key graph properties, including degree-related information, walk counts, and connectivity patterns
[
8
]
.

The Barabási–Albert (B–A) model
[
4
]
is a canonical random graph model for networks with heavy-tailed degree distributions. In this model, vertices arrive sequentially and attach to existing vertices with probability proportional to their current degree, producing the well-known scale-free degree distribution observed in many real-world networks, such as the network of scientific collaborations
[
3
]
, the functional connections in the human brain
[
12
]
, and the network of Wikipedia hyperlinks
[
9
]
. In its classical form, the process begins with a complete graph of
m_{0}
vertices, and each new vertex attaches to
m\leq m_{0}
existing vertices with probability proportional to their degrees, as described in
[
25
, Section 3.1.1]
.

Empirical and theoretical investigations of spectral properties of scale-free networks have shown significant differences from classical random matrix behavior. Numerical and heuristic studies indicate that preferential attachment networks exhibit non-semicircular spectral densities and structural features strongly influenced by degree heterogeneity
[
11
,
13
]
.

Rigorous spectral results for preferential attachment graphs have largely focused on the sparse regime, where each vertex attaches to a fixed number of existing vertices. In this setting, high-degree vertices play a dominant role in determining extreme eigenvalues, and leading eigenvectors often localize around hubs
[
14
]
. Similar results exist for a wide variety of random trees
[
6
]
.

The dense preferential attachment regime, in which the number of edges added at each step grows proportionally with the network size, is qualitatively different from the sparse setting. In the dense regime, adjacency matrices become dense weighted objects, and graph limit theory provides a natural framework for describing their asymptotic structure. Dense graph sequences can converge to graphons, which are measurable symmetric kernels encoding limiting edge densities
[
19
]
.

Several works have established convergence of dense preferential attachment models to deterministic limits and have connected these limits to random graph constructions
[
18
,
23
,
22
]
. These results describe the limiting mean structure of dense preferential attachment networks. However, deterministic kernel convergence alone does not determine the limiting eigenvalue distribution of centered adjacency matrices, which typically requires random matrix analysis of fluctuations around the mean
[
26
]
. For such matrices, the limiting spectral distribution is determined by a self-consistent resolvent equation rather than by the semicircle law
[
15
,
17
]
. The quadratic vector equation (QVE) framework developed by Ajanki, Erdős, and Krüger provides a general method for determining limiting spectral densities of Wigner-type matrices with arbitrary variance profiles under suitable moment and irreducibility conditions
[
2
]
.

Additionally, finite-rank perturbation theory shows that adding a low-rank deterministic component to a random matrix can produce isolated eigenvalues separated from the bulk spectrum. Such outliers are well understood in the context of spiked random matrix models and can often be characterized explicitly
[
5
]
.

Motivated by these developments, we study the spectral properties of dense preferential attachment multigraphs in a regime where the number of attachments grows linearly with the network size. We show that the expected adjacency matrix converges to an explicit kernel that generates a rank-one component. After centering and scaling, the fluctuation matrix forms a Wigner-type ensemble with an explicit variance kernel suitable for analysis under the QVE framework. This approach results in a deterministic limiting bulk spectral distribution and an explicit asymptotic description of the leading eigenvalue generated by the rank-one mean structure. Our results complement existing work on sparse preferential attachment spectra and connect dense preferential attachment graph limits with modern random matrix theory.

Finding the expected adjacency matrix.

2.1
Expected degrees of individual vertices.

Fix a vertex
j\leq t
. Define the random variable
\Delta d_{j}\left(t\right)
to be the number of new edges from
t
to
j
at time
t
. Then,

\Delta d_{j}\left(t\right)\ \sim\ \mathrm{Binomial}\left(m,q_{t\to j}\right),\quad\mathbb{E}[\Delta d_{j}\left(t\right)]\ =\ mq_{t\to j}\ =\ m\frac{d_{j}\left(t-1\right)}{D_{t-1}}.

Using (
1.1
), this gives a linear recursion for
\mu_{j}\left(t\right):=\mathbb{E}[d_{j}\left(t\right)]
. For
t>j
,

\mu_{j}\left(t\right)\ =\ \mu_{j}\left(t-1\right)\left(1+\frac{m}{2m\left(t-1\right)-m\left(m+1\right)}\right)\ =\ \mu_{j}\left(t-1\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{2\left(t-1\right)-\left(m+1\right)}\right).

(2.1)

At the time of arrival, each vertex has degree
m
, so
\mu_{j}\left(j\right)=m
.

Thus, with
s_{0}:=\max\left\{j,t_{0}\right\}
,

\mu_{j}\left(t\right)\ =\ m\prod_{s=s_{0}+1}^{t}\left(1+\frac{1}{2\left(s-1\right)-\left(m+1\right)}\right)\ =\ m\prod_{s=s_{0}+1}^{t}\frac{s-\frac{m+2}{2}}{s-\frac{m+3}{2}}.

(2.2)

This product can be represented using Gamma-functions,

\mu_{j}\left(t\right)\ =\ m\frac{\Gamma\left(t-\frac{m}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(s_{0}-\frac{m}{2}\right)}\frac{\Gamma\left(s_{0}-\frac{m+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(t-\frac{m+1}{2}\right)},\qquad t\geq s_{0},

(2.3)

which follows from Lemma

.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
r_{0}<r_{1}
be integers and

such that the Gamma terms below are defined. Then

\prod_{r=r_{0}+1}^{r_{1}}\frac{r-a}{r-b}\ =\ \frac{\Gamma\left(r_{1}+1-a\right)}{\Gamma\left(r_{0}+1-a\right)}\frac{\Gamma\left(r_{0}+1-b\right)}{\Gamma\left(r_{1}+1-b\right)}.

Proof.

Using the standard result
\Gamma\left(z+1\right)=z\Gamma\left(z\right)
,

\displaystyle\Gamma\left(r_{1}+1-a\right)

\displaystyle\ =\ \left(r_{1}-a\right)\Gamma\left(r_{1}-a\right)

\displaystyle\ =\ \left(r_{1}-a\right)\left(r_{1}-1-a\right)\Gamma\left(r_{1}-1-a\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \,\vdots

\displaystyle\ =\ \left(\prod_{r=r_{0}+1}^{r_{1}}\left(r-a\right)\right)\Gamma\left(r_{0}+1-a\right).

Hence

\prod_{r=r_{0}+1}^{r_{1}}\left(r-a\right)\ =\ \frac{\Gamma\left(r_{1}+1-a\right)}{\Gamma\left(r_{0}+1-a\right)}.

The same argument applies to
b
and taking their ratio proves the lemma.

Kernel Approach to
\mathbb{E}[M]

3.1
Definition of a kernel

Following Lovász
[
19
]
, who introduced the concept to spectral graph theory, we define a
kernel
to be any bounded, symmetric, measurable function

W:\left[0,1\right]^{2}\ \to\ \mathbb{R},

which we can use to generalize adjacency matrices of graphs.

Given a finite graph
H
with adjacency matrix
A=\left(A_{kj}\right)
, the associated step-function kernel
W_{H}
is defined by partitioning
\left[0,1\right]
into
\left|V\left(H\right)\right|
equal intervals
I_{1},\dots,I_{\left|V\left(H\right)\right|}
and setting
W_{H}\left(x,y\right)=A_{kj}
whenever
x\in I_{k},\ y\in I_{j}
.

A graphon is a kernel whose values lie in the interval
\left[0,1\right]
, corresponding to simple graphs
[
19
]
. In general, kernels serve as the limit objects for sequences of weighted or multigraph adjacency matrices.

The kernel of
\mathbb{E}[M]

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
c\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
and
m=cn
. For indices

k/n\to y\in\left(0,1\right]

s_{0}=\max\left\{j,t_{0}\right\}

\mathbb{E}[M_{kj}]\ =\ m\frac{\mu_{j}\left(k-1\right)}{D_{k-1}}\ \to\ \frac{c}{\sqrt{\left(2\max\left\{x,c\right\}-c\right)\left(2y-c\right)}},\qquad n\to\infty,

where
D_{t}=2mt-m\left(m+1\right)
and
\mu_{j}\left(\cdot\right)=\mathbb{E}[d_{j}\left(\cdot\right)]
. Moreover,
\mathbb{E}[M_{kj}]=O(1)
uniformly in

Proof.

We first prove the uniform bound. Set

s_{0}\ :=\ \max\left\{j,t_{0}\right\}.

From the degree evolution, for
t>s_{0}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\Delta d_{j}(t)}\left[d_{j}\left(t\right)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\ =\ \left(1+\frac{m}{D_{t-1}}\right)d_{j}\left(t-1\right).

Writing

\mu_{j}\left(t\right)\ :=\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}\left[d_{j}\left(t\right)\right],

we get

\displaystyle\mu_{j}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}\left[d_{j}\left(t\right)\right]

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\Delta d_{j}(t)}\left[d_{j}\left(t\right)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}\left[\left(1+\frac{m}{D_{t-1}}\right)d_{j}\left(t-1\right)\right]

\displaystyle\ =\ \left(1+\frac{m}{D_{t-1}}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}\left[d_{j}\left(t-1\right)\right]

\displaystyle\ =\ \left(1+\frac{m}{D_{t-1}}\right)\mu_{j}\left(t-1\right).

Since

D_{t-1}\ \geq\ D_{t_{0}}\ =\ m\left(m+1\right),

we have

\frac{m}{D_{t-1}}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{m+1}\ \leq\ \frac{C_{1}}{n}

for some constant
C_{1}>0
. Since
\mu_{j}\left(s_{0}\right)=m
, iterating gives

\displaystyle\mu_{j}\left(k-1\right)

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \left(1+\frac{C_{1}}{n}\right)\mu_{j}\left(k-2\right)

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \left(1+\frac{C_{1}}{n}\right)^{2}\mu_{j}\left(k-3\right)

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \cdots

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \left(1+\frac{C_{1}}{n}\right)^{k-1-s_{0}}\mu_{j}\left(s_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \left(1+\frac{C_{1}}{n}\right)^{n}m

\displaystyle\ \leq\ C_{2}n

for some constant
C_{2}>0
. Therefore

\mathbb{E}[M_{kj}]\ =\ m\frac{\mu_{j}\left(k-1\right)}{D_{k-1}}\ \leq\ m\frac{C_{2}n}{m\left(m+1\right)}\ \leq\ C,

which proves the uniform bound.

From the Gamma-function representation (
2.3
),

\frac{\mu_{j}\left(k-1\right)}{n}\ =\ \frac{m}{n}\cdot\frac{\Gamma\left(k-1-\frac{m}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(k-1-\frac{m+1}{2}\right)}\cdot\frac{\Gamma\left(s_{0}-\frac{m+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(s_{0}-\frac{m}{2}\right)}.

Set

For fixed

\frac{\Gamma\left(z+a\right)}{\Gamma\left(z+b\right)}\ \to\ z^{a-b}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)\right),\qquad z\to\infty.

Therefore we obtain

\frac{\Gamma\left(z_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(z_{1}\right)}\ \to\ z_{1}^{1/2}\left(1+O\left(z_{1}^{-1}\right)\right),\qquad\frac{\Gamma\left(z_{0}\right)}{\Gamma\left(z_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\ \to\ z_{0}^{-1/2}\left(1+O\left(z_{0}^{-1}\right)\right).

Finally, we derive

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{kj}]

\displaystyle\ =\ m\frac{\mu_{j}\left(k-1\right)}{D_{k-1}}\ =\ \frac{m}{n}\frac{\mu_{j}\left(k-1\right)}{n}\frac{n^{2}}{D_{k-1}}

\displaystyle\ =\ c\left(c\sqrt{\frac{z_{1}}{z_{0}}}+O\left(n^{-1}\right)\right)\left(\frac{1}{c\left(2y-c\right)}+O\left(n^{-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\ =\ c\frac{c\sqrt{z_{1}/z_{0}}}{c\left(2y-c\right)}+O\left(n^{-1}\right)\ =\ c\frac{\sqrt{z_{1}/z_{0}}}{2y-c}+O\left(n^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle\ =\ c\frac{1}{2y-c}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n\left(y-\frac{c}{2}\right)+O\left(1\right)}{n\left(\max\left\{x,c\right\}-\frac{c}{2}\right)+O\left(1\right)}}+O\left(n^{-1}\right)\right)+O\left(n^{-1}\right)

4.1
Centering and isolation of the bulk

Definition.

Let
M
be the
\left(\text{symmetric}\right)
adjacency matrix of the B–A multigraph and set

Y\ :=\ \frac{M-\mathbb{E}[M]}{\sqrt{m}},\qquad m\ =\ cn,\qquad 0<c<\frac{1}{2}.

We use
Y
to focus on the bulk of the spectrum.

Lemma 4.1

(Rank–one structure of
\mathbb{E}{[}M{]}
)
.

Let
A=\left[0,c\right]
,
B=\left(c,1\right]
, and
u\left(t\right)=\left(2t-c\right)^{-1/2}
. Define the function
\phi:\left[0,1\right]\to\mathbb{R}
by

\phi\left(x\right)\ :=\ \mathbf{1}_{\left\{x\in A\right\}}+\sqrt{c}\,\mathbf{1}_{\left\{x\in B\right\}}u\left(x\right),

where
\mathbf{1}_{x\in S}
is the indicator function defined as

\mathbf{1}_{x\in S}\ =\ \begin{cases}1,&x\in S,\\
0,&x\notin S,\end{cases}

for some set
S
. Then the limiting mean kernel
F
from (
3.1
) satisfies

F\left(x,y\right)\ =\ \phi\left(x\right)\phi\left(y\right).

Consequently, if one forms the
n\times n
matrix

F^{\left(n\right)}_{kj}\ :=\ F\left(\frac{k}{n},\frac{j}{n}\right),\qquad 1\leq k,j\leq n,

then
F^{\left(n\right)}
is rank one, since

F^{\left(n\right)}\ =\ v^{\left(n\right)}\left(v^{\left(n\right)}\right)^{\top}\!,\qquad v^{\left(n\right)}_{k}\ :=\ \phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right).

In particular, by Theorem
3.1
, the expectation matrix
\mathbb{E}[M]
is asymptotically a rank–one matrix.

Proof.

By definition of
F
and of
\phi
,

\phi\left(x\right)\phi\left(y\right)\ =\ \begin{cases}1,&x,y\in A,\\
\sqrt{c}\,u\left(y\right),&x\in A,\ y\in B,\\
\sqrt{c}\,u\left(x\right),&x\in B,\ y\in A,\\
cu\left(x\right)u\left(y\right),&x,y\in B,\end{cases}

which matches
F\left(x,y\right)
case-by-case (the diagonal
x=y
is negligible for our purposes and can be set to 0). Hence

F\left(x,y\right)\ =\ \phi\left(x\right)\phi\left(y\right)

for all
\left(x,y\right)\in\left[0,1\right]^{2}
.

Now define the vector
v^{\left(n\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

v^{\left(n\right)}_{k}\ :=\ \phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right),\qquad 1\leq k\leq n.

Then

F\left(\frac{k}{n},\frac{j}{n}\right)\ =\ \phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\phi\left(\frac{j}{n}\right)\ =\ v^{\left(n\right)}_{k}v^{\left(n\right)}_{j},

so

F\left(\frac{k}{n},\frac{j}{n}\right)\ =\ v^{\left(n\right)}\left(v^{\left(n\right)}\right)^{\top},

and therefore
F^{\left(n\right)}
has rank one.

Finally, Theorem
3.1
gives

\mathbb{E}[M_{kj}]\ =\ F\left(\frac{k}{n},\frac{j}{n}\right)+O\left(n^{-1}\right),

so
\mathbb{E}[M]
is asymptotically rank–one, with leading term
F\left(\frac{k}{n},\frac{j}{n}\right)=v^{\left(n\right)}\left(v^{\left(n\right)}\right)^{\top}
.
∎

Lemma 4.2

(From Tao
[
24
]
)
.

Let
\left\{X_{n}\right\}
be a sequence of random Hermitian matrix ensembles such that
\left\{\mu_{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}X_{n}}\right\}
converges almost surely to a limit
\mu
. Let
\left\{Z_{n}\right\}
be another sequence of random Hermitian matrix ensembles such that
\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{rank}\left(Z_{n}\right)
converges almost surely to zero. Then
\left\{\mu_{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(X_{n}+Z_{n}\right)}\right\}
converges almost surely to
\mu
.

Lemma
4.2
applies almost immediately to our case, where scaling by
\sqrt{n}
is equivalent to scaling by
\sqrt{c}\sqrt{m}
, where
\sqrt{c}
is constant, so
Y
and
M/\sqrt{m}
have the same limiting spectral distribution. Since
\mathbb{E}[M]
is rank–one by Lemma
4.1
, the Corollary 4.3.9 from
[
16
]
applies:
M/\sqrt{m}
has at most one outlier. Lemma
4.12
shows further why
Y
has no other outlier eigenvalues.

4.2
Poissonization

Now we use standard results (Lemma
4.3
, Propositions
4.4
,
4.5
) to construct a matrix
\widetilde{M}
whose entries are conditionally independent Poisson random variables.

Lemma 4.3

(Multinomial as conditional Poisson)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}
be independent random variables with
X_{j}\sim\mathrm{Poisson}\left(\lambda_{j}\right)
for
j=1,\dots,k
, and set
\Lambda:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}
. Then, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
and any
\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
satisfying
\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}=m
,

\mathbb{P}\left[X_{1}\ =\ x_{1},\dots,X_{k}\ =\ x_{k}\,\middle|\,\sum_{j=1}^{k}X_{j}\ =\ m\right]\ =\ \frac{m!}{x_{1}!\cdots x_{k}!}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\Lambda}\right)^{x_{j}}.

More concisely, conditional on
\sum_{j=1}^{k}X_{j}=m
, the random vector
\left(X_{1},\dots,X_{k}\right)
has the probability distribution described by
\mathrm{Multinomial}\left(m;\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\Lambda},\dots,\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\Lambda}\right)
.

Proposition 4.4

(Perturbation bound for Stieltjes transforms; Lu and Yao
[
20
, Lemma 18]
)
.

Let
A,B
be
n\times n
Hermitian matrices, and let
z=x+i\eta\in\mathbb{C}
with
\eta>0
. Denote their resolvents by
G_{A}\left(z\right)=\left(A-zI\right)^{-1}
and
G_{B}\left(z\right)=\left(B-zI\right)^{-1}
, and their Stieltjes transforms by

s_{A}\left(z\right)\ =\ \frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}G_{A}\left(z\right),\qquad s_{B}\left(z\right)\ =\ \frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}G_{B}\left(z\right).

Then

\left|s_{A}\left(z\right)-s_{B}\left(z\right)\right|\ \leq\ \frac{1}{\left(\Im z\right)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\left\|A-B\right\|_{F}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
S\sim\mathrm{Poisson}\left(\mu\right)
. Then

\mathbb{E}[\left|S-\mu\right|]\ \leq\ \sqrt{\mu}.

Proof.

For any random variable
X
with mean
\mu
, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have

\mathbb{E}[\left|X-\mu\right|]\ =\ \mathbb{E}[\left|X-\mu\right|\cdot 1]\ \leq\ \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\left(X-\mu\right)^{2}]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[1^{2}]}\ =\ \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}\left(X\right)}.

Since
\operatorname{Var}\left(S\right)=\mu
for a Poisson random variable, the claim follows.
∎

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
n\to\infty
and
m=cn
with fixed
c\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
. Let
M
denote the symmetric B–A multigraph adjacency matrix, and for each
k>t_{0}
let

\mathbf{d}\left(k-1\right)\ :=\ \left(d_{1}\left(k-1\right),\dots,d_{k-1}\left(k-1\right)\right),\qquad p_{kj}\ :=\ \frac{d_{j}\left(k-1\right)}{D_{k-1}},\qquad j<k.

Conditionally on
\mathbf{d}\left(k-1\right)
, the row-vector
\left(M_{k1},\dots,M_{k,k-1}\right)
is multinomial with
m
trials and probabilities
\left(p_{k1},\dots,p_{k,k-1}\right)
. Define the symmetric matrix
\widetilde{M}
by

\widetilde{M}_{kj}\mid\mathbf{d}\left(k-1\right)\ \sim\ \mathrm{Poisson}\left(\lambda_{kj}\right),\qquad\lambda_{kj}\ :=\ mp_{kj},\qquad j<k,

and set
\widetilde{M}_{jk}=\widetilde{M}_{kj}
,
\widetilde{M}_{kk}=M_{kk}=0
.

Let

Y\ :=\ \frac{M-\mathbb{E}[M]}{\sqrt{m}},\qquad\widetilde{Y}\ :=\ \frac{\widetilde{M}-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{M}]}{\sqrt{m}}.

Then, for each fixed
z\in\mathbb{H}
, as
n\to\infty

\left|\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Tr}\left(Y-zI\right)^{-1}]-\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Tr}\left(\widetilde{Y}-zI\right)^{-1}]\right|\ =\ o\left(1\right).

Consequently,
Y
and
\widetilde{Y}
have the same limiting spectral measure via Stieltjes inversion.

Proof.

Fix
k>t_{0}
and let
\mathbf{d}\left(k-1\right)
denote the vector of degrees at time
k-1
. In the multigraph model, conditional on
\mathbf{d}\left(k-1\right)
the
m
attachments of vertex
k
are i.i.d. draws to
\left\{1,\dots,k-1\right\}
with probabilities

p_{kj}\ :=\ \frac{d_{j}\left(k-1\right)}{D_{k-1}},\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}p_{kj}\ =\ 1,

hence

\left(M_{k1},\dots,M_{k,k-1}\right)\ \big|\ \mathbf{d}\left(k-1\right)\ \sim\ \mathrm{Multinomial}\left(m;p_{k1},\dots,p_{k,k-1}\right).

By Lemma
4.3
, there exist independent random variables

\widetilde{M}_{kj}\ \big|\ \mathbf{d}\left(k-1\right)\ \sim\ \mathrm{Poisson}\left(\lambda_{kj}\right),\qquad\lambda_{kj}\ :=\ mp_{kj},\qquad j<k,

such that, with
S_{k}:=\sum_{j<k}\widetilde{M}_{kj}
,

\left(\widetilde{M}_{k1},\dots,\widetilde{M}_{k,k-1}\right)\ \Big|\ \left(\mathbf{d}\left(k-1\right),S_{k}\ =\ m\right)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\ \left(M_{k1},\dots,M_{k,k-1}\right)\ \big|\ \mathbf{d}\left(k-1\right).

Here
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
denotes equality in distribution, that is, two random objects
X
and
Y
satisfy
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Y
if they have the same probability law.

Conditionally on
\mathbf{d}
, define
\overline{M}
row-wise by

\left(\overline{M}_{k1},\dots,\overline{M}_{k,k-1}\right)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\ \left(\widetilde{M}_{k1},\dots,\widetilde{M}_{k,k-1}\right)\ \Big|\ \left(S_{k}\ =\ m,\mathbf{d}\left(k-1\right)\right),

and set
\overline{M}_{jk}=\overline{M}_{kj}
,
\overline{M}_{kk}=0
. Then
\left(\overline{M}\mid\mathbf{d}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\left(M\mid\mathbf{d}\right)
, and

\mathbb{E}[\overline{M}_{kj}\mid\mathbf{d}]\ =\ \mathbb{E}[\widetilde{M}_{kj}\mid\mathbf{d}]\ =\ mp_{kj},\qquad k\neq j.

We introduce
\overline{M}
because it conditionally matches the original matrix in distribution,
\left(\overline{M}\mid\mathbf{d}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\left(M\mid\mathbf{d}\right)
, while being constructed from
\widetilde{M}
in the same probability space, so that their difference is an explicit small perturbation we can bound.

Unconditionally,
\mathbb{E}[M]=\mathbb{E}[\overline{M}]=\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{M}]
by the tower property.

Let
\overline{Y}:=\left(\overline{M}-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{M}]\right)/\sqrt{m}
and
\widetilde{Y}:=\left(\widetilde{M}-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{M}]\right)/\sqrt{m}
. Since
\mathbb{E}[M]=\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{M}]
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}\left(Y-zI\right)^{-1}\ \big|\ \mathbf{d}\right]\ =\ \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\overline{Y}-zI\right)^{-1}\ \big|\ \mathbf{d}\right].

Define the “edit” difference

\Delta\ :=\ \overline{Y}-\widetilde{Y}\ =\ \frac{\overline{M}-\widetilde{M}}{\sqrt{m}}.

Denote
s_{\overline{Y}}\left(z\right):=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\overline{Y}-zI\right)^{-1}
and
s_{\widetilde{Y}}\left(z\right):=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\widetilde{Y}-zI\right)^{-1}
. By the perturbation bound for Stieltjes transforms (Proposition
4.4
),

\left|s_{\overline{Y}}\left(z\right)-s_{\widetilde{Y}}\left(z\right)\right|\ \leq\ \frac{1}{\left(\Im z\right)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\left\|\Delta\right\|_{F}}{\sqrt{n}}.

(4.1)

We only have to bound
\left\|\Delta\right\|_{F}
. Let
K_{k}:=S_{k}-m
. Enforcing the constraint
S_{k}=m
in row
k
can be achieved by at most
\left|K_{k}\right|
“edits”, where each changes a single off-diagonal pair
\left(k,j\right),\left(j,k\right)
by
\pm 1
, which adjusts the row sum by
\pm 1
while preserving symmetry.

Under the scaling by
\sqrt{m}
, one “edit” contributes

\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\right)^{2}\ \leq\ \frac{2}{m}

to
\left\|\Delta\right\|_{F}^{2}
. Summing over all rows
k>t_{0}
and their at most
\left|K_{k}\right|
“edits” gives

\left\|\Delta\right\|_{F}^{2}\ \leq\ \frac{2}{m}\sum_{k>t_{0}}\left|K_{k}\right|.

Taking expectations, we first note that
\sum_{j<k}\lambda_{kj}=m
and that

S_{k}\mid\mathbf{d}\left(k-1\right)\ \sim\ \mathrm{Poisson}\left(m\right)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S_{k}\ \sim\ \mathrm{Poisson}\left(m\right),

since

\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}\ =\ k\right)\ =\ \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}\ =\ k\mid\mathbf{d}\left(k-1\right)\right)]\ =\ \mathbb{E}\left[e^{-m}\frac{m^{k}}{k!}\right]\ =\ e^{-m}\frac{m^{k}}{k!}.

With
K_{k}:=S_{k}-m
and applying Proposition
4.5
,

\mathbb{E}[\left|K_{k}\right|]\ =\ \mathbb{E}[\left|S_{k}-m\right|]\ \leq\ \sqrt{m}.

Therefore

\mathbb{E}[\left\|\Delta\right\|_{F}^{2}]\ \leq\ \frac{2}{m}\sum_{k>t_{0}}\mathbb{E}[\left|K_{k}\right|]\ \leq\ \frac{2n}{\sqrt{m}}\ =\ \frac{2}{\sqrt{c}}\sqrt{n},

and thus
\mathbb{E}[\left\|\Delta\right\|_{F}]=O\left(n^{1/4}\right)
. For fixed
z\in\mathbb{H}
, taking expectations in (
4.1
) results in

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|s_{\overline{Y}}\left(z\right)-s_{\widetilde{Y}}\left(z\right)\right|\right]\ \leq\ \frac{1}{\left(\Im z\right)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}[\left\|\Delta\right\|_{F}]}{\sqrt{n}}\ =\ O\left(n^{-1/4}\right)\ \to\ 0,\qquad n\to\infty.

Since
Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\overline{Y}
, we conclude

\mathbb{E}[\left|s_{Y}\left(z\right)-s_{\widetilde{Y}}\left(z\right)\right|]\ =\ \mathbb{E}[\left|s_{\overline{Y}}\left(z\right)-s_{\widetilde{Y}}\left(z\right)\right|]\ \to\ 0,\qquad n\to\infty.

Moreover, from

\left\|\Delta\right\|_{F}^{2}\ \leq\ \frac{2}{m}\sum_{k>t_{0}}\left|K_{k}\right|,\qquad\mathbb{E}[\left\|\Delta\right\|_{F}^{2}]\ =\ O\left(\sqrt{n}\right),

we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\|\Delta\right\|_{F}^{2}}{n}\right]\ =\ O\left(n^{-1/2}\right),

and hence by Markov’s inequality, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\left\|\Delta\right\|_{F}}{\sqrt{n}}\geq\varepsilon\right)\ =\ \mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\left\|\Delta\right\|_{F}^{2}}{n}\geq\varepsilon^{2}\right)\ \leq\ \frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\|\Delta\right\|_{F}^{2}}{n}\right]\ =\ O\left(n^{-1/2}\right)\ \to\ 0,\qquad n\to\infty.

Combining this with the perturbation bound (
4.1
) gives that, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|s_{\overline{Y}}\left(z\right)-s_{\widetilde{Y}}\left(z\right)\right|>\varepsilon\right)\ \to\ 0,\qquad n\to\infty.

Since
Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\overline{Y}
, it follows likewise that for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|s_{Y}\left(z\right)-s_{\widetilde{Y}}\left(z\right)\right|>\varepsilon\right)\ \to\ 0,\qquad n\to\infty.

By definition,
s_{Y}\left(z\right)
and
s_{\widetilde{Y}}\left(z\right)
are the Stieltjes transforms of the empirical eigenvalue distributions of
Y
and
\widetilde{Y}
, respectively. Since
\widetilde{Y}
has a deterministic limiting Stieltjes transform (given by Proposition
4.10
), the convergence in probability of their Stieltjes transforms implies that
Y
and
\widetilde{Y}
have the same limiting spectral measure.
∎

Proposition 4.7

(Independent Poisson comparison)
.

For
k>t_{0}
and
j<k
, define

\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\ :=\ \mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{M}_{kj}\right]\ =\ \mathbb{E}\left[M_{kj}\right].

Let
P_{kj}
be independent random variables with

P_{kj}\ \sim\ \mathrm{Poisson}\left(\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\right),\qquad k>t_{0},\ j<k.

Define the symmetric random matrix
H
as

H_{kj}\ :=\ \frac{P_{kj}-\bar{\lambda}_{kj}}{\sqrt{m}},\qquad H_{jk}\ :=\ H_{kj},\qquad k>t_{0},\ j<k,

and set
H_{kj}=0
for entries in the initial clique, i.e. when
k\leq t_{0}
and
j\leq t_{0}
. Then, for every fixed
z\in\mathbb{H}
,

\left|s_{\widetilde{Y}}\left(z\right)-s_{H}\left(z\right)\right|\ \to\ 0

in probability. Consequently,
\widetilde{Y}
and
H
have the same limiting spectral measure.

Proof.

For
k>t_{0}
and
j<k
, write

\lambda_{kj}\ :=\ mp_{kj}\ =\ m\frac{d_{j}\left(k-1\right)}{D_{k-1}},\qquad\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\ :=\ \mathbb{E}\left[\lambda_{kj}\right].

It is enough to compare
H
with a random matrix with entries having the same distribution as the entries of
\widetilde{Y}
, because the empirical spectral distribution depends only on the law of the matrix. Let

\widehat{Y}\ :=\ \frac{\widehat{M}-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{M}]}{\sqrt{m}}.

We construct
\widehat{M}
and
P
jointly so that
\widehat{M}
has the same law as
\widetilde{M}
, while
P
has independent Poisson entries.

Note that if
A
and
B
are independent Poisson random variables with means
\alpha
and
\beta
, then
A+B
is Poisson with mean
\alpha+\beta
.

Fix the collection
\left(\lambda_{ab}\right)
. For all
k,j
, defein

\alpha_{kj}\ :=\ \min\left\{\lambda_{kj},\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\right\},\qquad\beta_{kj}\ :=\ \left|\lambda_{kj}-\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\right|.

Let
A_{kj}
and
B_{kj}
be independent Poisson random variables with means
\alpha_{kj}
and
\beta_{kj}
, respectively, independently over all
k,j
.

If
\lambda_{kj}\geq\bar{\lambda}_{kj}
, define

P_{kj}\ :=\ A_{kj},\qquad\widehat{M}_{kj}\ :=\ A_{kj}+B_{kj}.

If
\lambda_{kj}<\bar{\lambda}_{kj}
, define

\widehat{M}_{kj}\ :=\ A_{kj},\qquad P_{kj}\ :=\ A_{kj}+B_{kj}.

In either case, conditional on
\left(\lambda_{ab}\right)
,

\widehat{M}_{kj}\ \sim\ \mathrm{Poisson}\left(\lambda_{kj}\right),\qquad P_{kj}\ \sim\ \mathrm{Poisson}\left(\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\right).

Since
\bar{\lambda}_{kj}
is deterministic, the law of
P_{kj}
does not depend on
\left(\lambda_{ab}\right)
, so
P_{kj}
are independent Poisson random variables.

Also, conditionally on
\left(\lambda_{ab}\right)
, the variables
\widehat{M}_{kj}
have the same law as the Poissonized entries
\widetilde{M}_{kj}
, so
\widehat{Y}
has the same law as
\widetilde{Y}
.

Now consider that

\widehat{M}_{kj}-P_{kj}\ =\ \pm B_{kj},

so

\left(\widehat{M}_{kj}-P_{kj}\right)^{2}\ =\ B_{kj}^{2}.

Since
B_{kj}\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\beta_{kj})
after conditioning on the collection
\left(\lambda_{ab}\right)
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{M}_{kj},P_{kj}}\left[\left(\widehat{M}_{kj}-P_{kj}\right)^{2}\mid\left(\lambda_{ab}\right)\right]\ =\ \beta_{kj}+\beta_{kj}^{2}.

Therefore

\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{M}_{kj},P_{kj}}\left[\left(\widehat{M}_{kj}-P_{kj}\right)^{2}\mid\left(\lambda_{ab}\right)\right]\ =\ \left|\lambda_{kj}-\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\right|+\left|\lambda_{kj}-\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\right|^{2}.

Take expectations of both sides with respect to
\left(\lambda_{ab}\right)
to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\left(\lambda_{ab}\right)}\left[\left(\widehat{M}_{kj}-P_{kj}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{E}_{\left(\lambda_{ab}\right)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{M}_{kj},P_{kj}}\left[\left(\widehat{M}_{kj}-P_{kj}\right)^{2}\mid\left(\lambda_{ab}\right)\right]\right]

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{E}_{\left(\lambda_{ab}\right)}\left[\left|\lambda_{kj}-\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\right|+\left|\lambda_{kj}-\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{E}_{\left(\lambda_{ab}\right)}\left[\left|\lambda_{kj}-\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\right|\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\left(\lambda_{ab}\right)}\left[\left|\lambda_{kj}-\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\right|^{2}\right].

From the proof of Proposition
4.8
,

\operatorname{Var}\left(d_{j}\left(k-1\right)\right)\ =\ O\left(n\right).

Since
D_{k-1}
is deterministic and
D_{k-1}=\Theta\left(n^{2}\right)
,

\operatorname{Var}\left(\lambda_{kj}\right)\ =\ \operatorname{Var}\left(m\frac{d_{j}\left(k-1\right)}{D_{k-1}}\right)\ =\ \frac{(cn)^{2}}{D_{k-1}^{2}}\operatorname{Var}\left(d_{j}\left(k-1\right)\right)\ =\ O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

Therefore

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\lambda_{kj}-\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\right|^{2}\right]\ =\ \operatorname{Var}\left(\lambda_{kj}\right)\ =\ O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right),

and by Cauchy-Schwarz,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\lambda_{kj}-\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\right|\right]\ \leq\ \sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\lambda_{kj}-\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\right|^{2}\right]}\ =\ O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Hence

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{M}_{kj}-P_{kj}\right)^{2}\right]\ =\ O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Since

\widehat{Y}-H\ =\ \frac{\widehat{M}-P}{\sqrt{m}}

on the non-deterministic off-diagonal entries, and since deterministic entries contribute zero, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{Y}-H\right\|_{F}^{2}\right]\ \leq\ \frac{2}{m}\sum_{k>t_{0}}\sum_{j<k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{M}_{kj}-P_{kj}\right)^{2}\right]\ \leq\ \frac{2}{cn}\cdot n^{2}\cdot O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\ =\ O\left(\sqrt{n}\right).

Therefore

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{Y}-H\right\|_{F}\right]\ \leq\ \sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{Y}-H\right\|_{F}^{2}\right]}\ =\ O\left(n^{1/4}\right).

By Proposition
4.4
,

\left|s_{\widehat{Y}}\left(z\right)-s_{H}\left(z\right)\right|\ \leq\ \frac{1}{\left(\Im z\right)^{2}}\frac{\left\|\widehat{Y}-H\right\|_{F}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Taking expectations gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|s_{\widehat{Y}}\left(z\right)-s_{H}\left(z\right)\right|\right]\ \leq\ \frac{1}{\left(\Im z\right)^{2}}\frac{O\left(n^{1/4}\right)}{\sqrt{n}}\ =\ O\left(n^{-1/4}\right)\ \to\ 0.

Hence

\left|s_{\widehat{Y}}\left(z\right)-s_{H}\left(z\right)\right|\ \to\ 0

in probability. Since
\widehat{Y}
has the same law as
\widetilde{Y}
, we have that
\widetilde{Y}
and
H
have the same limiting spectral distribution.
∎

4.3
Variance for the centered matrix

Proposition 4.8

(Variance kernel for the independent centered matrix)
.

Let
H
be the independent Poisson matrix from Proposition
4.7
, with
m=cn
and fixed
c\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
. For
k>t_{0}:=m+1
and
j<k
, set

p_{kj}\ :=\ \frac{d_{j}\left(k-1\right)}{D_{k-1}},\qquad D_{k-1}\ :=\ \sum_{v=1}^{k-1}d_{v}\left(k-1\right).

Then

\operatorname{Var}\left(H_{kj}\right)\ =\ \mathbb{E}[p_{kj}],\qquad n\operatorname{Var}\left(H_{kj}\right)\ =\ n\mathbb{E}[p_{kj}].

Consequently, if
j/n\to x
and
k/n\to y
, then

\sigma^{2}\left(x,y\right)\ :=\ \lim_{n\to\infty}n\operatorname{Var}\left(H_{kj}\right)\ =\ \frac{1}{c}F\left(x,y\right).

(4.2)

Equivalently, with
A=\left[0,c\right]
,
B=\left(c,1\right]
, and
u\left(t\right)=\left(2t-c\right)^{-1/2}
,

\sigma^{2}\left(x,y\right)\ =\ \begin{cases}0,&x,y\in A,\\[4.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{c}}u\left(y\right),&x\in A,\ y\in B,\\[8.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{c}}u\left(x\right),&x\in B,\ y\in A,\\[8.0pt]
u\left(x\right)u\left(y\right),&x,y\in B,\end{cases}\qquad u\left(t\right)\ =\ \left(2t-c\right)^{-1/2}.

(4.3)

Proof.

Let
H
be the independent Poisson matrix from Proposition
4.7
. For
k>t_{0}
and
j<k
, write

\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\ :=\ \mathbb{E}\left[M_{kj}\right].

By definition,

H_{kj}\ =\ \frac{P_{kj}-\bar{\lambda}_{kj}}{\sqrt{m}},\qquad P_{kj}\ \sim\ \mathrm{Poisson}\left(\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\right).

Therefore

\operatorname{Var}\left(H_{kj}\right)\ =\ \frac{1}{m}\operatorname{Var}\left(P_{kj}\right)\ =\ \frac{\bar{\lambda}_{kj}}{m}\ =\ \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{kj}\right]}{m}.

Since
m=cn
, this gives

n\operatorname{Var}\left(H_{kj}\right)\ =\ \frac{n}{m}\mathbb{E}\left[M_{kj}\right]\ =\ \frac{1}{c}\mathbb{E}\left[M_{kj}\right].

If
k,j\leq t_{0}
, then
H_{kj}
is deterministic and equal to
0
, so

\operatorname{Var}\left(H_{kj}\right)\ =\ 0.

Then the variance kernel is zero on
A\times A
.

Now assume that the vertices are not in the initial clique. By symmetry, it is sufficient to consider
k>t_{0}
and
j<k
. By Theorem
3.1
, with
x=j/n
and
y=k/n
,

\mathbb{E}\left[M_{kj}\right]\ \to\ F\left(x,y\right).

Therefore

n\operatorname{Var}\left(H_{kj}\right)\ =\ \frac{1}{c}\mathbb{E}\left[M_{kj}\right]\ \to\ \frac{1}{c}F\left(x,y\right).

Hence

\lim_{n\to\infty}n\operatorname{Var}\left(H_{kj}\right)\ =\ \frac{1}{c}F\left(x,y\right)

outside
A\times A
.

So the variance kernel is

\sigma^{2}\left(x,y\right)\ =\ \begin{cases}0,&x,y\in A,\\[4.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{c}F\left(x,y\right),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then (
4.3
) follows from the definition of
F
in (
3.1
).
∎

Proposition 4.9
.

We have
\sigma^{2}
is rank-two.

Proof.

Define

\phi_{1}\left(x\right)\ =\ \mathbf{1}_{x\in A},\qquad\phi_{2}\left(x\right)\ =\ u\left(x\right)\mathbf{1}_{x\in B}.

Then
\sigma^{2}
from (
4.3
) can be expressed using only
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
:

\sigma^{2}\left(x,y\right)\ =\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\phi_{1}\left(x\right)\phi_{2}\left(y\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\phi_{2}\left(x\right)\phi_{1}\left(y\right)+\phi_{2}\left(x\right)\phi_{2}\left(y\right).

Thus
\sigma^{2}\left(x,y\right)
lies in the span of the two functions
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
, so it has rank at most
2
. If it were rank-one, where
\sigma^{2}\left(x,y\right)=\psi\left(x\right)\psi\left(y\right)
for some
\psi
, then
\sigma^{2}=0
on
A\times A
forces
\psi=0
on
A
, contradicting
\sigma^{2}\left(x,y\right)>0
on
A\times B
, so the operator has to be rank-two.
∎

(From

)

4.5
Reduction of the continuum QVE

Recall that the limiting variance kernel
\sigma^{2}
from (
4.3
) is of the form

\sigma^{2}\left(x,y\right)\ =\ \begin{cases}0,&x,y\in A=\left[0,c\right],\\[4.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{c}}u\left(y\right),&x\in A,\ y\in B,\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{c}}u\left(x\right),&x\in B,\ y\in A,\\[6.0pt]
u\left(x\right)u\left(y\right),&x,y\in B,\end{cases}\qquad u\left(t\right)\ =\ \left(2t-c\right)^{-1/2}.

By Proposition
4.13
, the continuum QVE associated with
\sigma^{2}
is

-\frac{1}{m\left(x;z\right)}\ =\ z+\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{2}\left(x,y\right)m\left(y;z\right)\,dy,\qquad x\in\left[0,1\right],\ z\in\mathbb{H}.

(4.7)

Since
\sigma^{2}
is rank-two by Proposition
4.9
, the integral equation collapses to a closed system of two scalar quantities. We carry out this reduction explicitly.

Define

M_{A}\left(z\right)\ :=\ \int_{0}^{c}m\left(y;z\right)\,dy,\qquad L\left(z\right)\ :=\ \int_{c}^{1}u\left(y\right)m\left(y;z\right)\,dy.

These two scalar quantities capture all dependence of (
4.7
) on
m\left(\cdot;z\right)
, because
\sigma^{2}
only couples
m
through the functions
\mathbf{1}_{A}
and
\mathbf{1}_{B}u
. We can now prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof.

For
x\in A
,
\sigma^{2}\left(x,y\right)=0
for
y\in A
and
\sigma^{2}\left(x,y\right)=\left(1/\sqrt{c}\right)u\left(y\right)
for
y\in B
. Hence

\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{2}\left(x,y\right)m\left(y;z\right)\,dy\ =\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\int_{c}^{1}u\left(y\right)m\left(y;z\right)\,dy\ =\ \frac{L\left(z\right)}{\sqrt{c}}.

Substituting into (
4.7
) gives

-\frac{1}{m_{A}\left(z\right)}\ =\ z+\frac{L\left(z\right)}{\sqrt{c}},\qquad m_{A}\left(z\right)\ =\ -\frac{1}{z+L\left(z\right)/\sqrt{c}}.

Integrating over
A=\left[0,c\right]
yields
M_{A}\left(z\right)=cm_{A}\left(z\right)
.

For
x\in B
,

\sigma^{2}\left(x,y\right)\ =\ \begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{c}}u\left(x\right),&y\in A,\\[4.0pt]
u\left(x\right)u\left(y\right),&y\in B,\end{cases}

so that

\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{2}\left(x,y\right)m\left(y;z\right)\,dy\ =\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}u\left(x\right)M_{A}\left(z\right)+u\left(x\right)L\left(z\right)\ =\ u\left(x\right)\left(L\left(z\right)+\frac{M_{A}\left(z\right)}{\sqrt{c}}\right).

The QVE becomes

-\frac{1}{m\left(x;z\right)}\ =\ z+u\left(x\right)\left(L\left(z\right)+\frac{M_{A}\left(z\right)}{\sqrt{c}}\right),

hence

m\left(x;z\right)\ =\ -\frac{1}{z+u\left(x\right)K\left(z\right)},\qquad K\left(z\right):=L\left(z\right)+\frac{M_{A}\left(z\right)}{\sqrt{c}}.

Using
M_{A}\left(z\right)=cm_{A}\left(z\right)=-c/\left(z+L\left(z\right)/\sqrt{c}\right)
, we obtain

K\left(z\right)\ =\ L\left(z\right)+\frac{c}{\sqrt{c}}\left(-\frac{1}{z+L\left(z\right)/\sqrt{c}}\right)\ =\ L\left(z\right)-\frac{\sqrt{c}}{z+L\left(z\right)/\sqrt{c}}.

By definition of
L
,

L\left(z\right)\ =\ \int_{c}^{1}u\left(y\right)m\left(y;z\right)\,dy\ =\ -\int_{c}^{1}\frac{u\left(y\right)}{z+u\left(y\right)K\left(z\right)}\,dy.

This proves the reduced system (
1.2
).
∎

The system (
1.2
) is a pair of equations that implicitly defines the pair
\left(L\left(z\right),K\left(z\right)\right)
, which uniquely determines the solution
m\left(\cdot;z\right)
of the continuum QVE. In particular, once
L\left(z\right)
and
K\left(z\right)
are known, the Stieltjes transform of the limiting eigenvalue distribution is

G_{c}\left(z\right)\ =\ \int_{0}^{1}m\left(x;z\right)\,dx\ =\ cm_{A}\left(z\right)+\int_{c}^{1}-\frac{1}{z+u\left(x\right)K\left(z\right)}\,dx.

Stieltjes inversion then yields the limiting spectral density.

The outlier eigenvalue

In this section we describe the leading eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix.

Proposition 5.1

(Weyl’s perturbation theorem;
[
7
, III.2.6]
)
.

Let
A
and
B
be Hermitian
n\times n
matrices, and let
\lambda_{1}\left(\cdot\right)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}\left(\cdot\right)
denote their eigenvalues. Then

\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\left|\lambda_{j}\left(A\right)-\lambda_{j}\left(B\right)\right|\ \leq\ \left\|A-B\right\|.

5.1
Top eigenvalue of
\mathbb{E}[M]

Recall from Lemma
4.1
that the limiting mean kernel satisfies
F\left(x,y\right)=\phi\left(x\right)\phi\left(y\right)
, where

\phi\left(x\right)\ =\ \begin{cases}1,&x\in\left[0,c\right],\\[4.0pt]
\sqrt{c}\left(2x-c\right)^{-1/2},&x\in\left(c,1\right].\end{cases}

Define the vector
v^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

v^{(n)}_{i}:=\phi\left(\frac{i}{n}\right),\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{E}[M]\right)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}\left(\mathbb{E}[M]\right)
be the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{E}[M]
. Then

\lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{E}[M]\right)\ =\ \alpha\left(c\right)n+O\left(1\right),

where

\alpha\left(c\right):=\int_{0}^{1}\phi\left(x\right)^{2}\,dx\ =\ c+\frac{c}{2}\log\frac{2-c}{c}.

(5.1)

Proof.

By Theorem
3.1
, for each fixed
k,j
,

\mathbb{E}[M_{kj}]\ =\ F\left(\frac{k}{n},\frac{j}{n}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\ =\ v^{(n)}_{k}v^{(n)}_{j}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

Set

R^{(n)}_{kj}:=\mathbb{E}[M_{kj}]-v^{(n)}_{k}v^{(n)}_{j}.

Then

\left\|R^{(n)}\right\|_{F}^{2}\ =\ \sum_{k,j=1}^{n}O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\ =\ O\left(1\right),

hence
\left\|R^{(n)}\right\|\leq\left\|R^{(n)}\right\|_{F}=O\left(1\right)
.

Write

\mathbb{E}[M]\ =\ v^{(n)}\left(v^{(n)}\right)^{\top}\!+R^{(n)}.

The rank–one matrix
v^{(n)}\left(v^{(n)}\right)^{\top}
has eigenvalues
\left\|v^{(n)}\right\|_{2}^{2}
and
0
with multiplicity
n-1
. Applying Proposition
5.1
results in

\left|\lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{E}[M]\right)-\left\|v^{(n)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right|\ \leq\ \left\|\mathbb{E}[M]-v^{(n)}\left(v^{(n)}\right)^{\top}\right\|\ =\ \left\|R^{(n)}\right\|\ =\ O\left(1\right).

Thus

\lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{E}[M]\right)\ =\ \left\|v^{(n)}\right\|_{2}^{2}+O\left(1\right).

(5.2)

Next we compute
\left\|v^{(n)}\right\|_{2}^{2}
. By definition,

\left\|v^{(n)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{n}\phi\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{2}\ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{cn}1+\sum_{k=cn+1}^{n}\frac{c}{\frac{2k}{n}-c}.

The first sum equals
cn+O\left(1\right)
. For the second, write it as a Riemann sum:

\sum_{k=cn+1}^{n}\frac{c}{\frac{2k}{n}-c}\ =\ n\int_{c}^{1}\frac{c}{2x-c}\,dx+O\left(1\right)\ =\ n\cdot\frac{c}{2}\log\frac{2-c}{c}+O\left(1\right).

Therefore

\left\|v^{(n)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\ =\ n\left(c+\frac{c}{2}\log\frac{2-c}{c}\right)+O\left(1\right)\ =\ \alpha\left(c\right)n+O\left(1\right).

Substituting into (
5.2
) yields

\lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{E}[M]\right)\ =\ \alpha\left(c\right)n+O\left(1\right),

as claimed.
∎

5.2
Outlier eigenvalue of
M

Proposition 5.3

(Asymptotic outlier eigenvalue)
.

Let
M
be the adjacency matrix of the B–A multigraph with
m=cn
and fixed
c\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
. Then

\lambda_{\max}\left(M\right)\ =\ \alpha\left(c\right)n+o_{\mathbb{P}}\left(n\right),

where
\alpha\left(c\right)
is given by (
5.1
).

Proof.

Set

Y\ :=\ \frac{M-\mathbb{E}[M]}{\sqrt{m}},\qquad m\ =\ cn.

By Proposition
4.6
, there exists a matrix
\overline{Y}
such that
\overline{Y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Y

and

\frac{\left\|\overline{Y}-\widetilde{Y}\right\|_{F}}{\sqrt{n}}\ \to\ 0

in probability. By Proposition
4.7
, there is a matrix
\widehat{Y}
such that
\widehat{Y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\widetilde{Y}

and

\frac{\left\|\widehat{Y}-H\right\|_{F}}{\sqrt{n}}\ \to\ 0

in probability.

Since
\widehat{Y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\widetilde{Y}
,

\frac{\left\|\overline{Y}-H\right\|_{F}}{\sqrt{n}}\ \leq\ \frac{\left\|\overline{Y}-\widehat{Y}\right\|_{F}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{\left\|\widehat{Y}-H\right\|_{F}}{\sqrt{n}}\ \to\ 0

in probability. Hence

\left\|\overline{Y}-H\right\|\ \leq\ \left\|\overline{Y}-H\right\|_{F}\ =\ o_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sqrt{n}\right).

By Lemma
4.12
, applied to the independent matrix
H
, the limiting measure obtained from the QVE is supported on a finite union of compact intervals, hence has compact support. Write

\operatorname{supp}\mu\ \subset\ \left[-C,C\right]

for some constant
C<\infty
.

Since
H
is a real symmetric matrix, all of its eigenvalues are real, and

\left\|H\right\|\ =\ \max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left|\lambda_{i}\left(H\right)\right|.

By Lemma
4.12
, asymptotically with overwhelming probability all eigenvalues of
H
lie in
\operatorname{supp}\mu
. Hence, asymptotically with overwhelming probability,

\lambda_{i}\left(H\right)\in\left[-C,C\right]

for all
i=1,\dots,n
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\|H\right\|>C\right]\ =\ \mathbb{P}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left|\lambda_{i}\left(H\right)\right|>C\right]\ \to\ 0

and

\left\|H\right\|\ =\ O_{\mathbb{P}}\left(1\right).

It follows that

\left\|\overline{Y}\right\|\ \leq\ \left\|H\right\|+\left\|\overline{Y}-H\right\|\ =\ O_{\mathbb{P}}\left(1\right)+o_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sqrt{n}\right)\ =\ o_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sqrt{n}\right).

Since
\overline{Y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Y
, we also have

\left\|Y\right\|\ =\ o_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sqrt{n}\right).

Therefore

\left\|M-\mathbb{E}[M]\right\|\ =\ \sqrt{m}\left\|Y\right\|\ =\ O\left(\sqrt{n}\right)o_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sqrt{n}\right)\ =\ o_{\mathbb{P}}\left(n\right).

Now apply Proposition
5.1
. Since both
M
and
\mathbb{E}[M]
are Hermitian,

\left|\lambda_{\max}\left(M\right)-\lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{E}[M]\right)\right|\ \leq\ \left\|M-\mathbb{E}[M]\right\|\ =\ o_{\mathbb{P}}\left(n\right).

By Lemma
5.2
,

\lambda_{1}\left(\mathbb{E}[M]\right)\ =\ \alpha\left(c\right)n+O\left(1\right).

Combining the last two expressions gives

\lambda_{\max}\left(M\right)\ =\ \alpha\left(c\right)n+O\left(1\right)+o_{\mathbb{P}}\left(n\right).

Finally, since
O\left(1\right)=o\left(n\right)
deterministically, it follows that

O\left(1\right)+o_{\mathbb{P}}\left(n\right)\ =\ o_{\mathbb{P}}\left(n\right).

Hence

\lambda_{\max}\left(M\right)\ =\ \alpha\left(c\right)n+o_{\mathbb{P}}\left(n\right).

∎

Theorem
1.2
is a corollary of Proposition
5.3
.

Appendix A
QVE conditions verification

We verify (A)-(D) in order.

(A) Flatness.

First note that diagonal entries and entries inside the initial clique are deterministic after centering, so their variances are zero. Thus it remains to consider entries with
k>t_{0}
and
j<k
; by symmetry this covers all off-diagonal entries.

For the independent Poisson matrix
H
from Proposition
4.7
, we have

H_{kj}\ =\ \frac{P_{kj}-\bar{\lambda}_{kj}}{\sqrt{m}},\qquad P_{kj}\ \sim\ \mathrm{Poisson}\left(\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\right),\qquad\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\ =\ \mathbb{E}\left[M_{kj}\right].

Hence

s^{(n)}_{kj}\ :=\ \mathbb{E}\left[\left|H_{kj}\right|^{2}\right]\ =\ \operatorname{Var}\left(H_{kj}\right)\ =\ \frac{1}{m}\operatorname{Var}\left(P_{kj}\right)\ =\ \frac{\bar{\lambda}_{kj}}{m}\ =\ \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{kj}\right]}{m}.

By Theorem
3.1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[M_{kj}\right]\ =\ O\left(1\right)

uniformly in
j,k
.
Since
m=cn
, it follows that

s^{(n)}_{kj}\ =\ \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{kj}\right]}{m}\ =\ O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)

uniformly in
j,k
. Hence, for all
k,j
,

0\ \leq\ s^{(n)}_{kj}\ \leq\ \frac{C}{n}

for some constant
C
independent of
n
.

(B) Uniform primitivity.

Fix any
\delta\in\left(0,1-c\right)
and set

J\ :=\ \left[c+\delta,1\right],\qquad\mathcal{J}_{n}\ :=\ \left\{\ell\in\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}:\frac{\ell}{n}\in J\right\}.

Then
\left|\mathcal{J}_{n}\right|\geq n\left|J\right|/2
for all sufficiently large
n
.

We claim that there is a constant
a>0
, independent of
n
, such that

s^{(n)}_{i\ell}\ \geq\ \frac{a}{n}

for all sufficiently large
n
, all
i
, and all
\ell\in\mathcal{J}_{n}
with
i\neq\ell
.

Let
r=\max\left\{i,\ell\right\}
and
q=\min\left\{i,\ell\right\}
.
Since
\ell/n\in J\subset\left(c,1\right]
, we have
r>t_{0}
for all sufficiently large
n
.
For
i,\ell
, the corresponding independent Poisson entry in
H
has variance

s^{(n)}_{i\ell}\ =\ \operatorname{Var}\left(H_{i\ell}\right)\ =\ \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{rq}\right]}{m}.

Since

\mathbb{E}\left[M_{rq}\right]\ =\ m\mathbb{E}\left[p_{rq}\right],\qquad p_{rq}\ =\ \frac{d_{q}\left(r-1\right)}{D_{r-1}},

we have

s^{(n)}_{i\ell}\ =\ \mathbb{E}\left[p_{rq}\right].

Every existing vertex has degree at least
m
, so
d_{q}\left(r-1\right)\geq m
.
Also
D_{r-1}\leq D_{n}\leq 2mn
. Hence

p_{rq}\ \geq\ \frac{m}{D_{n}}\ \geq\ \frac{1}{2n}.

Therefore

s^{(n)}_{i\ell}\ =\ \mathbb{E}\left[p_{rq}\right]\ \geq\ \frac{1}{2n}.

So the claim holds with
a=1/2
.

Therefore, for
L=2
,

\left(S^{(n)}\right)^{2}_{ij}\ =\ \sum_{\ell=1}^{n}s^{(n)}_{i\ell}s^{(n)}_{\ell j}\ \geq\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in\mathcal{J}_{n}\\
\ell\neq i,j\end{subarray}}s^{(n)}_{i\ell}s^{(n)}_{\ell j}.

For all sufficiently large
n
,

\left|\left\{\ell\in\mathcal{J}_{n}:\ell\neq i,j\right\}\right|\ \geq\ \frac{n\left|J\right|}{4}.

Hence

\left(S^{(n)}\right)^{2}_{ij}\ \geq\ \frac{n\left|J\right|}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\ =\ \frac{\left|J\right|}{16n}\qquad\forall\,i,j

for all sufficiently large
n
. Thus (B) holds with
L=2
and
p=\left|J\right|/16
.

(C) Bounded solution of the QVE.

Since boundedness can be checked only after solving for
m\left(\cdot,z\right)
, another approach is necessary. By Remark 1.1 of
[
2
]
, condition (C) is equivalent to the conditions of Theorem 6.1 of
[
1
]
, namely

A1 (Symmetry and positivity preserving).

Note that
S
is an integral operator with a symmetric, non-negative kernel
S_{xy}
, i.e.,

S_{xy}\ =\ S_{yx}\ \geq\ 0.

Equivalently,

\left\langle u,Sw\right\rangle\ =\ \left\langle Su,w\right\rangle,\qquad\left(Sp\right)\left(x\right)\ \geq\ 0

whenever

p\left(y\right)\geq 0.

A2 (Smoothing /
L^{2}\to L^{\infty}
boundedness).

The operator
S
acts by

\left(Sw\right)\left(x\right)\ =\ \int_{0}^{1}S_{xy}w\left(y\right)\,dy,

and for each fixed
x
the function
y\mapsto S_{xy}
lies in
L^{2}
. To see the concrete form of this condition, apply Cauchy-Schwarz at every
x
:

\left|\left(Sw\right)\left(x\right)\right|\ =\ \left|\int_{0}^{1}S_{xy}w\left(y\right)\,dy\right|\ \leq\ \left(\int_{0}^{1}S_{xy}^{\,2}\,dy\right)^{1/2}\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}}.

Taking the supremum over
x
yields

\left\|Sw\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\ \leq\ \left(\sup_{x\in\left[0,1\right]}\left(\int_{0}^{1}S_{xy}^{\,2}dy\right)^{1/2}\right)\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}}.

Thus the requirement that
S
be a bounded operator
L^{2}\to L^{\infty}
is equivalent to demanding

\sup_{x\in\left[0,1\right]}\left(\int_{0}^{1}S_{xy}^{\,2}\,dy\right)^{1/2}\ <\ \infty.

B1 (Quantitative block fully indecomposability).

There exist constants
\phi>0
and
K\in\mathbb{N}
, a fully
indecomposable matrix
Z=\left(Z_{kj}\right)_{k,j=1}^{K}
with
Z_{kj}\in\left\{0,1\right\}
, and a measurable partition
\mathcal{I}:=\left\{I_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{K}
of
\left[0,1\right]
such that for every
1\leq i,j\leq K
the following hold:

\left|I_{j}\right|\ =\ \frac{1}{K},\qquad\sigma^{2}\left(x,y\right)\ \geq\ \phi Z_{ij}

whenever

\left(x,y\right)\in I_{i}\times I_{j}.

Note that the original condition (C) is framed in terms of a real-valued matrix, while the alternative conditions A1, A2, and B1 apply to a possibly infinite-dimensional operator. We then write
S^{(n)}
using the conventions of
[
1
]
, starting with defining

X_{n}\ :=\ \{1,\dots,n\},\qquad\pi_{n}(\{j\})\ :=\ \frac{1}{n},\qquad B_{n}\ :=\ \ell^{\infty}(X_{n}),\qquad\|w\|_{B_{n}}\ :=\ \max_{1\leq j\leq n}|w_{j}|,

and

\langle u,w\rangle_{n}\ :=\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{j}\overline{w_{j}},\qquad\langle w\rangle_{n}\ :=\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}.

The operator
S
in the notation of
[
1
]
is the operator induced by
the variance matrix
S^{(n)}=(s^{(n)}_{kj})
, namely

(Sw)_{k}\ :=\ \sum_{j=1}^{n}s^{(n)}_{kj}w_{j},\qquad 1\leq k\leq n.

Equivalently,

S_{kj}\ :=\ ns^{(n)}_{kj}.

A1.

Since
S^{(n)}
is a variance matrix,

s^{(n)}_{kj}\ =\ s^{(n)}_{jk}\ \geq\ 0.

Therefore, for
u,w\in B_{n}
,

\left\langle u,Sw\right\rangle_{n}\ =\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}u_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n}s^{(n)}_{kj}w_{j}\ =\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{n}s^{(n)}_{jk}u_{k}\ =\ \left\langle Su,w\right\rangle_{n}.

Thus
S
is symmetric. If
p_{j}\geq 0
for all
j
, then

(Sp)_{k}\ =\ \sum_{j=1}^{n}s^{(n)}_{kj}p_{j}\ \geq\ 0

for all
k
. Hence A1 holds uniformly in
n
.

A2.

In the finite setting, A2 requires

\sup_{1\leq k\leq n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{kj}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\ <\ \infty

uniformly in
n
, with
S_{kj}=ns^{(n)}_{kj}
.
So it is enough to show

\sup_{1\leq k\leq n}\left(n\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(s^{(n)}_{kj}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\ <\ \infty.

By (A), there is a constant
C_{1}>0
, independent of
n
, such that

0\ \leq\ s^{(n)}_{kj}\ \leq\ \frac{C_{1}}{n}

for all
k,j
. Therefore

n\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(s^{(n)}_{kj}\right)^{2}\ \leq\ n\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{C_{1}^{2}}{n^{2}}\ =\ C_{1}^{2}.

Hence

\sup_{1\leq k\leq n}\left(n\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(s^{(n)}_{kj}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\ \leq\ C_{1}.

Thus A2 holds uniformly in
n
.

B1.

Set
A=\left[0,c\right]
,
B=\left(c,1\right]
, and
u\left(t\right)=\left(2t-c\right)^{-1/2}
. Since
c<1/2
, fix
\delta
such that

0\ <\ \delta\ <\ \frac{1}{2}-c,

and let

B_{\delta}\ :=\ \left(c+\delta,1\right]\subset B.

Then

\left|B_{\delta}\right|\ =\ 1-c-\delta\ >\ \frac{1}{2}.

Choose a sufficiently large
K\in\mathbb{N}
and define

r\ :=\ \left\lfloor K\left|B_{\delta}\right|\right\rfloor

such that

\frac{K}{2}\ <\ r\ \leq\ K\left|B_{\delta}\right|.

Now choose disjoint measurable sets

I_{K-r+1},\dots,I_{K}\subset B_{\delta}

with

\left|I_{j}\right|\ =\ \frac{1}{K},\qquad K-r+1\leq j\leq K.

Partition the complement

\left[0,1\right]\setminus\bigcup_{j=K-r+1}^{K}I_{j}

arbitrarily into measurable sets
I_{1},\dots,I_{K-r}
of measure
1/K
each. Thus
\left\{I_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{K}
is a measurable partition of
\left[0,1\right]
with

\left|I_{j}\right|\ =\ \frac{1}{K}

for all
j
.

Let

P\ :=\ \left\{K-r+1,\dots,K\right\}.

Define
Z\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{K\times K}
as

Z_{ij}\ =\ \begin{cases}1,&i\neq j\ \text{and}\ \left(i\in P\ \text{or}\ j\in P\right),\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We first show that
Z
is fully indecomposable. The zero entries of
Z
lie either on the diagonal or in the submatrix indexed by
P^{c}\times P^{c}
. Since

\left|P^{c}\right|\ =\ K-r\ <\ \frac{K}{2},

any zero submatrix contained in
P^{c}\times P^{c}
has size at most
\left(K-r\right)\times\left(K-r\right)
.
If such a zero submatrix has size
a\times b
, then

a+b\ \leq\ 2\left(K-r\right)\ <\ K.

The remaining zero submatrices are single diagonal entries, and these also satisfy
a+b<K
for
K\geq 3
. Therefore
Z
has no zero submatrix of size
a\times b
with
a+b\geq K
, so
Z
is fully indecomposable.

Now define

m_{1}\ :=\ \min_{x\in\left[c+\delta,1\right]}u\left(x\right)\ >\ 0,\qquad m_{2}\ :=\ \min_{y\in\left[c,1\right]}u\left(y\right)\ >\ 0.

For
x\in B_{\delta}
and
y\in\left[0,1\right]
, we have from (
4.3
) that

\sigma^{2}\left(x,y\right)\ \geq\ \min\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}m_{1},\ m_{1}m_{2}\right\}\ =:\ \phi\ >\ 0.

By symmetry, the same bound holds if
y\in B_{\delta}
and
x\in\left[0,1\right]
.

If
Z_{ij}=1
and
x\in I_{i}
,
y\in I_{j}
, then either
i\in P
or
j\in P
. Hence either
I_{i}\subset B_{\delta}
or
I_{j}\subset B_{\delta}
. Therefore

\sigma^{2}\left(x,y\right)\ \geq\ \phi.

Since
H
has variance profile

s^{(n)}_{kj}\ =\ \frac{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{kj}\right]}{m},

Theorem
3.1
gives

ns^{(n)}_{kj}\ =\ \frac{n}{m}\mathbb{E}\left[M_{kj}\right]\ \to\ \frac{1}{c}F\left(\frac{k}{n},\frac{j}{n}\right)\ =\ \sigma^{2}\left(\frac{k}{n},\frac{j}{n}\right).

Hence, for all sufficiently large
n
,

ns^{(n)}_{kj}\ \geq\ \frac{\phi}{2}

when
\frac{k}{n}\in I_{i}
,
\frac{j}{n}\in I_{j}
, and
Z_{ij}=1
.

Then the partition
\left\{I_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{K}
and the fully indecomposable
K\times K
matrix
Z
satisfy

\left|I_{j}\right|\ =\ \frac{1}{K},\qquad ns^{(n)}_{kj}\ \geq\ \frac{\phi}{2}Z_{ij}

whenever
\frac{k}{n}\in I_{i}
and
\frac{j}{n}\in I_{j}
,
satisfying B1.

(D) Bounded moments.

Let
b\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed. If
s^{(n)}_{kj}=0
, then
H_{kj}=0
, so

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|H_{kj}\right|^{b}\right]\ =\ 0\ \leq\ \mu_{b}\left(s^{(n)}_{kj}\right)^{b/2}.

Thus, by symmetry, it is sufficient to consider entries with
k>t_{0}
and
j<k
. Write

\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\ :=\ \mathbb{E}\left[M_{kj}\right].

By definition of
H
,

H_{kj}\ =\ \frac{P_{kj}-\bar{\lambda}_{kj}}{\sqrt{m}},\qquad P_{kj}\ \sim\ {\rm Poisson}\left(\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\right),

and

s^{(n)}_{kj}\ =\ \operatorname{Var}\left(H_{kj}\right)\ =\ \frac{\bar{\lambda}_{kj}}{m}.

We first note that
\bar{\lambda}_{kj}
is bounded above and below uniformly. The upper bound

\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\ =\ \mathbb{E}\left[M_{kj}\right]\ =\ O\left(1\right)

follows from the uniform bound in Theorem
3.1
. For the lower bound, since every existing vertex has degree at least
m
and
D_{k-1}\leq D_{n}\leq 2mn
, we have

p_{kj}\ =\ \frac{d_{j}\left(k-1\right)}{D_{k-1}}\ \geq\ \frac{m}{D_{n}}\ \geq\ \frac{1}{2n}.

Therefore

\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\ =\ \mathbb{E}\left[M_{kj}\right]\ =\ m\mathbb{E}\left[p_{kj}\right]\ \geq\ \frac{m}{2n}\ =\ \frac{c}{2}.

Hence there are constants
0<a<A<\infty
such that

a\ \leq\ \bar{\lambda}_{kj}\ \leq\ A

uniformly in
j,k,n
for all non-deterministic entries.

Now let
X\sim{\rm Poisson}\left(\lambda\right)
. First,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X-\lambda\right|^{b}\right]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\left[\left(X+\lambda\right)^{b}\right]\ =\ \sum_{a=0}^{b}\binom{b}{a}\lambda^{b-a}\mathbb{E}\left[X^{a}\right].

The moments of a Poisson random variable satisfy

\mathbb{E}\left[X^{a}\right]\ =\ \sum_{r=0}^{a}S\left(a,r\right)\lambda^{r},

where
S\left(a,r\right)
are the Stirling numbers of the second kind
(from
[
10
, p.160]
). Thus

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X-\lambda\right|^{b}\right]\ \leq\ \sum_{a=0}^{b}\binom{b}{a}\lambda^{b-a}\sum_{r=0}^{a}S\left(a,r\right)\lambda^{r}\ =\ P_{b}\left(\lambda\right),

where
P_{b}
is a polynomial with positive coefficients depending only on
b
. Since
\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\in\left[a,A\right]
, we get

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|P_{kj}-\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\right|^{b}\right]\ \leq\ C_{b}\ \leq\ \mu_{b}\bar{\lambda}_{kj}^{b/2}

for some constant
\mu_{b}
depending only on
b
.

Therefore

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|H_{kj}\right|^{b}\right]\ =\ m^{-b/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|P_{kj}-\bar{\lambda}_{kj}\right|^{b}\right]\ \leq\ \mu_{b}m^{-b/2}\bar{\lambda}_{kj}^{b/2}\ =\ \mu_{b}\left(\frac{\bar{\lambda}_{kj}}{m}\right)^{b/2}\ =\ \mu_{b}\left(s^{(n)}_{kj}\right)^{b/2}.

This proves (D).

Combining (A)–(D), we conclude that the variance profile
S^{(n)}
associated with the independent Poisson matrix
H
satisfies all assumptions of Proposition
4.10
, and the limiting variance kernel is
\sigma^{2}
from (
4.3
).
