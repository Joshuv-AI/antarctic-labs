Integrative WGBS and ATAC-seq profiling reveals epigenetic and chromatin accessibility signatures associated with clutch length in goose ovaries
Authors: Hechuan Wang, Jiaxin Yin, Ke Jiang, Kexin Cong, Yunuo Liu, Weiran Yang, Xinyi Miao, Zhifeng Chen, Ying Zhang, Shengjun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clutch length is an important reproductive trait in geese, but its epigenetic basis remains poorly characterized. Daily egg production was recorded for 280 individually housed Zi geese, and clutch-related indices were calculated as described in our previous study. Based on these records, six geese with contrasting clutch-length phenotypes were selected and assigned to the long-clutch (LC) and short-clutch (SC) groups. Ovarian tissues from three geese per group were subjected to whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) and assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) to identify candidate epigenetic signatures associated with clutch length. WGBS identified 630,909 differentially methylated regions (DMRs), whereas ATAC-seq identified 902 differentially accessible regions (DARs). Integrated analysis revealed distinct patterns of ovarian DNA methylation and chromatin accessibility between the two groups, suggesting that clutch length variation may be accompanied by epigenomic differences in ovarian tissue. Genes associated with DMRs and/or DARs were enriched in biological processes related to granulosa cell differentiation and endocrine competence, follicular fate regulation, and periovulatory cytoskeletal and signaling remodeling. RERE was prioritized as a candidate locus because it was supported by changes in both DNA methylation and chromatin accessibility, whereas FOXL2, STAR, BAK1, FGF17, PRSS35, ACTR3, and AXIN1 were supported mainly by evidence from a single omics layer. RT-qPCR analysis of selected genes showed expression trends broadly consistent with the corresponding epigenomic differences, providing additional supportive evidence for these candidate associations. Collectively, this study provides an exploratory ovarian epigenomic resource and identifies candidate epigenetic signatures, genes, and biological processes associated with clutch length variation in geese.

Introduction

Egg-laying efficiency is a cornerstone of modern poultry production. Beyond total egg number, increasing attention has focused on laying patterns, which reflect the temporal persistence of ovulation throughout the production cycle. Clutch length, commonly defined as the number of eggs laid consecutively, summarizes an individual’s laying rhythm and is widely used to evaluate breed-level performance and monitor genetic improvement over time (Shi et al., 2023). In geese, a clutch is typically defined as a series of consecutive ovipositions separated by inter-egg intervals of no more than 48 h (Brun et al., 2003). This pattern likely reflects the interplay between the ovarian circadian clock and the pace of follicular development (Qin et al., 2013). In the avian ovary, follicle selection and the transition from prehierarchical to preovulatory follicles depend on coordinated granulosa cell (GC) differentiation and the acquisition of steroidogenic capacity (Johnson, 2015). Increasing evidence indicates that these processes are regulated not only at the transcriptional level but also through epigenetic mechanisms, particularly DNA methylation and chromatin accessibility. In chicken follicles, stage-dependent DNA methylation changes in the proximal promoters of key steroidogenic genes, such as STAR and CYP11A1, are associated with altered gene expression and follicular steroidogenic activity (Zhu et al., 2015). In parallel, ATAC-seq–based studies of avian ovaries have shown that dynamic changes in chromatin accessibility accompany ovarian development and egg-laying status, with accessible regulatory regions linked to genes involved in follicle development, hormone responsiveness, and reproductive performance (Hu et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2025). Moreover, chromatin architecture remodeling in chicken GCs is coordinated with transcriptomic divergence across prehierarchical, preovulatory, and postovulatory follicular stages (Li et al., 2022). Together, these findings suggest that DNA methylation and chromatin accessibility act as complementary regulatory layers that link GC differentiation, steroidogenic activation, follicle maturation, and reproductive performance in birds.

DNA methylation is a central component of epigenetic regulation in avian species and influences reproductive phenotypes, including egg number, eggshell quality, and sperm motility, by modulating the transcriptional potential of promoters, enhancers, and gene bodies (Ju et al., 2023). Moreover, because promoters are key regulatory regions that govern transcription initiation, differential promoter methylation has been associated with egg production in laying hens, suggesting that methylation changes at specific loci may contribute to phenotypic variation (Zhang et al., 2022). Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) provides base-resolution maps of DNA methylation across the genome, enabling the identification and annotation of epigenetic features associated with phenotypic variation (Lister and Ecker, 2009). Chromatin accessibility profiling delineates active regulatory regions across the genome and provides a complementary layer of information for interpreting gene regulatory programs (Klemm et al., 2019). The assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) enables rapid profiling of chromatin accessibility and facilitates the systematic identification of putative cis-regulatory elements.

To date, WGBS and ATAC-seq have been increasingly used to dissect the regulatory mechanisms underlying laying performance in avians. In geese, combined WGBS and RNA-seq analyses have been used to identify candidate genes underlying differences in laying rate, suggesting that variation in this trait is closely associated with differential methylation patterns (Ma et al., 2025). Integrative methylome analyses along the hypothalamus–ovary axis further suggest that ovarian methylation landscapes shift in a physiology-dependent manner and may mirror corresponding changes in gene expression, supporting the view that methylation acts as a context-dependent regulator rather than a static background mark (Zhang et al., 2023). Integrated analyses of the ovarian transcriptome and chromatin accessibility in laying hens indicate that the open-chromatin landscape is dynamic during ovarian development, enabling the identification of regulatory genes and putative transcription factor (TF) binding events associated with egg-laying performance (Zhang et al., 2025). However, the application of WGBS and ATAC-seq to reproductive traits in geese remains limited. In the present study, ovarian WGBS and ATAC-seq were used as the primary approaches to characterize epigenetic changes associated with clutch length, whereas reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) served as a complementary method to determine whether the mRNA expression patterns of selected genes were consistent with these epigenetic alterations. Together, these findings provide a foundation for further elucidating the epigenetic mechanisms underlying laying persistence in geese.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The Ethics Committee of Science and Technology of the Heilongjiang Bayi Agricultural University approved this study (License No.: DWKJXY2025069). All experimental operations were carried out under the relevant guidelines and supervision. Written informed consent was obtained from all animal owners prior to their animals’ participation in this study.

Animals, clutch length assessment, and sample collection

The study was conducted from March to June 2025 at the Branch of Animal Husbandry and Veterinary, Heilongjiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences (47.26°N, 123.68°E, altitude 150 m). A total of 280 healthy 7-month-old female Zi geese (Anser cygnoides) were housed individually in cages in a ventilated poultry house, with 1 m² of floor space per goose. Geese were maintained under a natural photoperiod of 12–16 h of light at ambient temperatures of 9–29°C. All geese received the same standardized diet and had ad libitum access to clean drinking water; the diet composition and nutrient content were described previously (Liu et al., 2025). During the four-month experimental period from March to June 2025, egg number (EN) and egg weight (EW) were recorded for each goose twice daily at 09:00 and 16:00. Clutch-related traits, including large clutch length (LCL), large clutch number (LCN), average clutch length (ACL), and average clutch number (ACN), were calculated using the Poultry Breeding Information Intelligent Management and Analysis System (Hand Era Technology, Beijing, China) according to the definitions described by Bao et al. (2022). Phenotypic characterization of this experimental population was reported in our previous multi-omics study integrating ovarian transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic analyses (Wang et al., 2026). Briefly, EN, ACN, ACL, LCN, and LCL were significantly higher in the LC group than in the SC group, whereas EW did not differ significantly between the groups. The present study used ovarian tissues from the same phenotypically characterized individuals and applied the same LC/SC classification framework, but focused on distinct epigenomic layers, namely DNA methylation and chromatin accessibility, to further investigate the regulatory mechanisms associated with clutch length.

Geese in the upper (top 25%) and lower (bottom 25%) quartiles of ACL were first identified as candidate individuals, and three geese from each quartile were subsequently assigned to the long-clutch (LC) and short-clutch (SC) groups. For these selected individuals, ACN, LCL, and LCN were used only as supplementary descriptive indices and were not used for group assignment. After a 12-h fast, the geese were transferred to the dissection facility and anesthetized with 1.5–3.0% isoflurane by inhalation, after which they were immediately humanely euthanized via carotid artery transection. Ovarian tissues were collected immediately after euthanasia. To reduce sample heterogeneity caused by differences in follicle size and yolk content, visible large yolk-filled hierarchical follicles were avoided, and ovarian cortical tissue containing small follicles and adjacent ovarian stromal tissue was collected from each individual using the same sampling strategy. The samples were transferred to sterile cryovials, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until processing for WGBS and ATAC-seq.

DNA extraction, bisulfite conversion, and WGBS library preparation

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from ovarian tissue using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Unmethylated λDNA (Promega, WI, USA) was spiked into the gDNA at 0.5% (w/w), and the resulting mixture was diluted with nuclease-free water for subsequent steps. Bisulfite conversion and desulfonation of the gDNA–λDNA mixture were performed using the EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit (Zymo Research, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The eluted DNA was used for single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) library preparation. The ssDNA underwent adapter ligation followed by bead-based purification. Library amplification and indexing were performed using i7 and i5 dual-index primers (Illumina, CA, USA), followed by another round of bead-based purification. Library quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA), and sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, CA, USA).

WGBS data processing, read alignment, and methylation calling

Raw sequencing reads were processed with fastp (version 0.25.0) (Chen et al., 2018) to remove adapter sequences, low-quality bases, and reads containing ambiguous nucleotides. Read quality was assessed using FastQC (version 0.12.0). Quality-filtered reads were aligned to the goose reference genome (GCF_040182565.1) (Zhao et al., 2024) using Bismark (version 0.24.2) (Krueger and Andrews, 2011), and PCR duplicate reads were removed using the markdup function in SAMtools (version 1.22) (Li and Durbin, 2010). DNA methylation levels were quantified using MethPipe (version 5.0.0) (Song et al., 2013) as the proportion of reads supporting methylated cytosines relative to the total coverage at each cytosine site and its corresponding guanine on the opposite strand of the goose reference genome. Differential methylation analysis was subsequently performed as described in the bioinformatics and statistical analysis section.

Nuclei preparation, Tn5 tagmentation, and ATAC-seq library construction

Ovarian tissues were minced and enzymatically dissociated to generate a single-cell suspension. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer, pelleted by centrifugation, and counted using an automated cell counter (BodBoge Technology, Guangdong, China). Cells were lysed for 5 min to isolate nuclei. Tn5 transposition was performed by incubating isolated nuclei with the Tagment DNA TDE1 Enzyme and Buffer Kit (Illumina, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After purification of tagmented DNA, libraries were PCR-amplified and indexed with sample-specific barcodes using dual-index i7 and i5 primers from the Nextera XT Index Kit v2 (Illumina, CA, USA). Library concentration was quantified using a Qubit 4 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), and library quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Libraries were sequenced in PE150 mode on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using the Xp workflow (Illumina, CA, USA).

ATAC-seq data processing, read alignment, and peak calling

Raw sequencing reads were processed with fastp (version 0.25.0) (Chen et al., 2018) to remove adapter sequences, low-quality bases, and short fragments. Reads were aligned to the goose reference genome (GCF_040182565.1) (Zhao et al., 2024) using Bowtie2 (version 2.5.4) (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012), and peaks representing accessible chromatin regions were called using MACS2 (version 3.0.3) (Zhang et al., 2008). Motif enrichment within peak regions was performed using HOMER (version 5.1) (Heinz et al., 2010), and transcription factor (TF) footprints were inferred using TOBIAS (0.14.0) (Bentsen et al., 2020). Differential chromatin accessibility analysis was subsequently performed as described in the bioinformatics and statistical analysis section.

Reverse transcription–quantitative PCR validation of expression patterns of candidate genes associated with DMRs and DARs

Total RNA was isolated using AG RNAex Pro Reagent (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, tissue samples were placed into RNase-free tubes containing 1.2 mL of AG RNAex Pro Reagent and grinding beads and were then homogenized using a KZ II high-speed tissue homogenizer (Servicebio Technology, Hubei, China). The homogenates were incubated at room temperature for 5 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatants were transferred to fresh tubes, mixed thoroughly with 240 μL of chloroform, incubated at room temperature for 5 min, and centrifuged again at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. The aqueous phase was then transferred to a new tube, mixed thoroughly with 600 μL of isopropanol, incubated at room temperature for 10 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. After removal of the supernatant, the RNA pellets were washed twice with pre-chilled 80% ethanol at −20°C, air-dried at room temperature, and finally dissolved in RNase-free water.

First-strand cDNA synthesis was conducted using the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR Ver. 2 (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 1 μg of total RNA was added to a 20-μL reverse transcription reaction containing RNase-free water, 2 μL of gDNA Clean Reaction Mix, and 4 μL of 5 × Evo M-MLV RT Reaction Mix. Reverse transcription was performed in a Biometra TOne 96 G thermal cycler (Analytik Jena, Thuringia, Germany) under the following conditions: 37°C for 15 min, 85°C for 5 s, and then held at 4°C.

Gene-specific primers for selected candidate genes associated with differentially methylated regions (DMRs) and differentially accessible regions (DARs) were designed using Primer3Plus (version 3.3.0) (Table 1). qPCR was carried out using the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit III (Low Rox Plus) (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each 20-μL reaction mixture contained 10 μL of 2 × SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix III, 2 μL of cDNA template, 0.4 μL of forward primer (10 μM), 0.4 μL of reverse primer (10 μM), and 7.2 μL of RNase-free water. The final concentration of each primer was 0.2 μM. Amplification was performed on a QuantGene 9600 Fluorescent Quantitative Detection System (BIOER Technology, Zhejiang, China) under the following cycling conditions: an initial denaturation at 95°C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s. A melting curve analysis was subsequently performed at 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 1 min, and 95°C for 1 s to verify amplification specificity. Each sample was assayed in triplicate. Statistical analysis

DMRs were identified using the MethylKit (version 3.21) (Akalin et al., 2012) with default settings, using 1,000-bp sliding windows with 500-bp overlap, and statistical significance defined as P < 0.05 (Jiang et al., 2024). DARs were identified using DiffBind (version 3.9.4) (Ross-Innes et al., 2012), with thresholds of |log2(fold change (FC))| > 1 and P < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler (version 4.16.0) (Wu et al., 2021), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All figures were generated in R (version 4.5.2).

Results

WGBS reveals extensive differential methylation and clutch length–related pathway enrichment in goose ovaries

WGBS generated 32.44–35.60 Gb of raw sequencing data per sample. After quality filtering, 28.57–31.41 Gb of clean data were retained, corresponding to clean read rates of 92.38–93.65%. The proportions of Q20 and Q30 bases were 99.19–99.34% and 96.18–96.74%, respectively, indicating high sequencing quality. The clean reads were mapped to the goose reference genome at rates ranging from 83.34 to 84.66%, while duplicate rates ranged from 9.41 to 11.64%. In addition, bisulfite conversion efficiencies were consistently high across all samples, ranging from 99.35 to 99.40% (Table S1). Collectively, these results demonstrated that the WGBS datasets were of sufficient quality and reliability for subsequent genome-wide DNA methylation analyses.

WGBS identified 630,909 DMRs between the two groups, comprising 180,077 hypermethylated DMRs associated with genes such as INHA, SMAD3, and BAK1, and 423,832 hypomethylated DMRs associated with genes such as GHR, FOXL2, and STAR (Fig. 1A; Table S3). The DMRs were mainly located in intronic and intergenic regions, with fewer in exons and promoters and the lowest proportion in downstream regions (Fig. 1B; Table S3). In addition, 101,454 differentially methylated CpG islands (CGIs) were identified, including 34,413 hypermethylated and 67,041 hypomethylated methylated CGIs (Fig. 1C; Table S4). Most differential methylated CGIs were intergenic, followed by intragenic CGIs and their corresponding shores, whereas promoter CGIs and 3′ transcript CGIs/shores represented the smallest fractions (Fig. 1D; Table S4). Metagene profiling showed no obvious difference in overall CpG methylation levels between groups; however, CpG methylation levels differed across gene features, with the highest levels in distal promoters, a gradual decrease across intermediate and proximal promoters as well as the first exon and first intron, the lowest levels in internal exons and introns, and a subsequent increase across the last intron and last exon, followed by relatively higher levels downstream (Fig. 1E). ATAC-seq reveals extensive differential accessibility and clutch length–related regulatory pathway enrichment in goose ovaries

ATAC-seq generated approximately 100 million raw reads, corresponding to 15.00 Gb of raw sequencing data, for each sample. After quality filtering, 98.73–98.96 million clean reads were retained, with clean read rates ranging from 98.73 to 98.96%. The proportions of Q20 and Q30 bases were 93.79–94.79% and 84.92–86.48%, respectively. Of the clean reads, 97.17–97.43 million were successfully mapped to the goose reference genome, yielding consistently high mapping rates of 98.35–98.53%. Duplication rates ranged from 5.23 to 6.18%, and 66.23–69.06 million final effective reads were retained per sample. Fragment size distributions were highly consistent among biological replicates and between the LC and SC groups, with all libraries showing pronounced enrichment of short fragments below 100 bp and progressively decreasing signals across longer fragment sizes (Fig. S2A–F). TSS enrichment scores ranged from 2.04 to 2.58, indicating detectable enrichment of accessible chromatin signals around transcription start sites (Table S2). Collectively, these quality-control metrics supported the use of the ATAC-seq data for subsequent chromatin accessibility analyses.

ATAC-seq identified 902 DARs between the two groups, comprising 503 upregulated DARs associated with genes such as PRSS35, ACTR3, and TRPM7, and 399 downregulated DARs associated with genes such as FGF17, AXIN1, and POU1F1 (Fig. 3A; Table S7). The DARs were mainly located in distal intergenic regions and promoters (≤1 kb), with fewer in the first intron and other intronic and the lowest proportion in downstream regions (Fig. 3B; Table S7). Filtering DARs within proximal promoter regions identified 72 promoter-associated DARs, including 37 upregulated DARs associated with genes such as FNIP1, ACTR3, and PRSS35, and 35 downregulated DARs associated with genes such as RSPO2, AXIN1, and FGF17 (Fig. 3C; Table S8). UpSet analysis of DAR annotations showed that the most prevalent intersections involved intergenic and distal intergenic categories, followed by gene body and intron, whereas intersections involving promoter, 5′ UTR, 3′ UTR, and downstream categories were comparatively infrequent (Fig. 3D, E; Table S7). The GO enrichment analysis of genes linked to promoter-associated DARs identified 156 significantly enriched terms, including odorant binding, olfactory receptor activity, G protein-coupled receptor activity, 4 iron, 4 sulfur cluster binding, and negative regulation of macrophage activation (Fig. 3F; Table S9). ATAC-seq and WGBS integration reveals differential chromatin accessibility and methylation signatures associated with clutch length in goose ovaries

Deduplication of DMRs obtained from WGBS resulted in 88,898 nonredundant DMRs (NR-DMRs). Among these, 7,596 hypermethylated NR-DMRs and 17,402 hypomethylated NR-DMRs were mapped to at least one ATAC peak (Fig. 5A; Table S15). Further analysis showed that 23,438 NR-DMRs were mapped to one ATAC peak, 1,555 to two ATAC peaks, and five to three ATAC peaks (Fig. 5B; Table S15). Additionally, 15,886 ATAC peaks were mapped to one NR-DMR, 4,791 to two NR-DMRs, and 365 to three NR-DMRs (Fig. 5C; Table S16). Discussion

Clutch length depends on the coordinated recruitment, selection, maturation, and survival of ovarian follicles throughout the laying cycle (Brun et al., 2003; Johnson, 2015). Integrated WGBS and ATAC-seq analyses identified epigenetic differences associated with clutch-length variation, but DNA methylation and chromatin accessibility showed limited one-to-one correspondence. This pattern suggests that the two regulatory layers provide largely complementary information (Ju et al., 2023; Klemm et al., 2019). The associated candidate genes could be organized into three biological themes: granulosa cell differentiation and endocrine competence, regulation of follicular fate, and periovulatory cytoskeletal and signaling remodeling. However, the strength of evidence differed among loci. RERE was supported by both WGBS and ATAC-seq, whereas FOXL2, STAR, and BAK1 were supported mainly by WGBS and FGF17, PRSS35, ACTR3, and AXIN1 mainly by ATAC-seq.

The first theme involved granulosa cell differentiation and endocrine competence. FOXL2 is a conserved ovarian transcription factor required for ovarian differentiation and follicular development in chickens (Major et al., 2019). Its knockdown in preovulatory granulosa cells promotes proliferation and DNA replication, and FOXL2 also regulates the actin cytoskeleton through RhoA/ROCK signaling (Luo et al., 2020; Li et al., 2024). The LC-associated hypomethylated DMR linked to FOXL2 is therefore consistent with a regulatory state favoring granulosa cell differentiation and follicular homeostasis. STAR mediates the rate-limiting transport of cholesterol into mitochondria and is essential for ovarian steroidogenesis in birds (Lu et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2023). STAR expression increases in preovulatory granulosa cells, and its regulation is closely associated with progesterone synthesis (Du et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2022). STAR has also been identified as a reproduction-related gene in goose ovaries (Luan et al., 2014). Thus, the hypomethylated STAR-associated DMR in the LC group may reflect enhanced steroidogenic competence. In contrast, the biological interpretation of FGF17 is less certain. Although the FGF17-associated DAR showed reduced accessibility and an AR-related motif, direct evidence for FGF17 in avian follicles is limited, and its relationship with clutch progression should be regarded as exploratory (Abramyan, 2015; Onagbesan et al., 2009; Machado et al., 2009).

RERE represented the strongest integrative candidate. RERE positively regulates retinoic acid signaling through complexes containing retinoic acid receptors and their cofactors (Vilhais-Neto et al., 2010), while retinoic acid signaling contributes to ovarian folliculogenesis and steroidogenesis (Damdimopoulou et al., 2019). In chickens, 9-cis retinoic acid modulates follicular steroid secretion, and retinoid X receptor (RXR) transcripts are present in ovarian stroma and follicles at multiple developmental stages (Pawłowska et al., 2008). Vitamin A supplementation also alters ovarian RARα and RXRα expression and improves reproductive performance in broiler breeders (Chen et al., 2016), and retinoid-associated proteins have been detected in goose ovaries (Cao et al., 2018). In the present dataset, the RERE locus showed LC-associated hypomethylation and increased chromatin accessibility, with complete overlap between the NR-DMR and DAR. Genome-track visualization and sample-level correlations provided additional support for this locus. These findings suggest that RERE may be associated with retinoic acid-dependent transcriptional programs involved in granulosa cell differentiation, steroidogenic competence, and ovarian homeostasis. Nevertheless, its function in avian follicles requires direct experimental validation.

The second theme concerned follicular fate. BAK1 is a proapoptotic effector of the mitochondrial pathway (Youle and Strasser, 2008), and granulosa cell apoptosis is a major determinant of follicular selection and atresia in geese (Ran et al., 2023; Kang et al., 2018). In chickens, BAK1 expression increases during postovulatory follicle regression together with mitochondrial apoptotic signaling (Lin et al., 2018). The hypermethylated BAK1-associated DMR in the LC group is therefore consistent with reduced apoptotic activity and improved maintenance of the functional follicle pool. PRSS35 may participate in extracellular matrix remodeling during follicular maturation and ovulation. Although direct evidence in avian ovaries is limited, mammalian studies associate PRSS35 with developing and ovulatory follicles and with oocyte developmental competence (Wahlberg et al., 2008; Mohan Jeena et al., 2022). The increased promoter accessibility associated with PRSS35 may therefore indicate enhanced periovulatory remodeling, but this interpretation remains provisional.

The third theme involved periovulatory cytoskeletal and signaling remodeling. ACTR3 is a core component of the Arp2/3 complex, which mediates branched actin assembly and is conserved in birds (Terasaki et al., 2002). Cytoskeletal integrity influences steroidogenesis in hen granulosa cells, and Arp2/3-dependent actin dynamics are required for normal oocyte maturation in mammals (Iczkowski and Hertelendy, 1991; Sun et al., 2011). Accordingly, the upregulated ACTR3-associated DAR may reflect increased capacity for actin-dependent follicular remodeling. AXIN1 is a negative regulator of canonical WNT/β-catenin signaling, a pathway involved in granulosa cell proliferation, steroidogenesis, and follicle selection in chickens (Wang et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2024; Nie et al., 2024). However, because AXIN1 was supported only by a single accessibility signal and lacked direct expression or functional evidence, it should be considered a secondary exploratory candidate rather than a central mechanistic finding.

This epigenomic study complements our previous transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic analysis (Wang et al., 2026). Several limitations should be considered. First, WGBS and ATAC-seq included three biological replicates per group, limiting statistical power and requiring cautious interpretation of individual DMRs and DARs. Independent validation in a larger population is needed. Second, whole ovarian tissue contains heterogeneous follicular and somatic cells; variation in follicular hierarchy, ovulatory stage, or cellular composition may have contributed to the observed differences. Third, the limited overlap between DMRs and DARs may reflect both biological complementarity and analytical sensitivity. RT-qPCR and sample-level correlations supported selected genes but did not establish direct regulatory or causal relationships. Future studies should validate selected loci using targeted bisulfite sequencing and chromatin accessibility assays, followed by functional perturbation, cell-type-resolved profiling, and chromatin interaction analyses. Finally, fixed daily egg records limited precise estimation of inter-egg intervals. Despite these limitations, this study provides an exploratory epigenomic resource and prioritizes RERE for further investigation of clutch-length variation in geese.

Conclusion

Genome-wide epigenomic profiling identified a broad set of candidate DNA methylation and chromatin accessibility differences between the two groups of geese, suggesting that variation in clutch length may be associated with ovarian epigenetic remodeling. These candidate epigenetic signatures were mainly related to genes potentially involved in granulosa cell differentiation and endocrine competence, follicular fate regulation, and periovulatory cytoskeletal and signaling remodeling. Through multi-omics integration, RERE was prioritized as a major candidate locus associated with clutch length, whereas FOXL2, STAR, BAK1, FGF17, PRSS35, ACTR3, and AXIN1 were supported primarily by evidence from individual omics layers and therefore require further validation. Overall, this study provides an exploratory epigenomic resource and a set of testable candidate genes for future investigation of the molecular basis of clutch length in geese.
