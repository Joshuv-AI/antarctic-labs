Association of the triglyceride-glucose index with subclinical left ventricular dysfunction in type 2 diabetes mellitus: mediation by myocardial energetic efficiency and resting perfusion
Authors: Li Jiang, Yuan Li, Hua‑Yan Xu, Xue-Ming Li, Ke Shi, Jin Wang, Shi-Qin Yu, Yu Jiang, Han Fang, Wei‑Feng Yan, Ying‑Kun Guo, Zhi-Gang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe triglyceride-glucose(TyG) index, a surrogate marker of insulin resistance, has been linked to cardiac dysfunction; however, its underlying associated pathways in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus(T2DM) remain unclear. This study used cardiac magnetic resonance(CMR) to explore the association of TyG index with subclinical left ventricular(LV) myocardial dysfunction and whether imaging indicators statistically mediate this relationship.MethodsIn this retrospective cross-sectional study, a total of 235 T2DM patients who underwent CMR examination were included and assigned to three groups based on the tertiles of their TyG indexes as follows: low(< 8.73, n = 78), moderate(8.73–9.36, n = 79), and high TyG index(≥ 9.36, n = 78) groups. LV geometry, function, myocardial energetic efficiency index (MEEi), resting first-pass perfusion, and global peak strain in radial(GRPS), circumferential(GCPS), and longitudinal(GLPS) directions were measured. Univariate and multivariate linear regression models and exploratory mediation analysis were used to analyze the associations of TyG index on LV global strain.ResultsCompared with the low and moderate TyG index groups, the high TyG index group had significantly higher LV remodeling index, lower LV global function index, lower MEEi, impaired resting myocardial perfusion, and reduced LV global peak strain (all p ≤ 0.002). Multivariate analysis showed that TyG index remained independently associated with reduced LV strain after adjusting for confounders (GRPS β = −0.261; GCPS β = 0.271; GLPS β = 0.381; all p < 0.001). And MEEi and upslope were also independently associated with reduced LV GRPS and GLPS (all p < 0.05). Further mediation analysis revealed the statistically mediated proportions of the association between the TyG index and LV global strain were 8.2–8.6% for MEEi and 4.3% to 12.5% for upslope. In model comparison analyses, the TyG index demonstrated substantially better model fit than either component alone across all strain directions (all Akaike Information Criterion difference > 150).ConclusionsIn patients with T2DM, a higher TyG index is independently associated with decreased LV global strain, and this relationship is statistically mediated by reduced MEEi and impaired resting perfusion upslope. These findings generate hypotheses regarding myocardial energetics and resting perfusion as potential pathways associated with diabetic myocardial dysfunction that warrant prospective investigation.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03308-8.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Triglyceride-glucose index, Left ventricular dysfunction, Mediating effect, Myocardial energetic efficiency, Resting myocardial perfusion

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) imposes a substantial public health burden, in which cardiovascular complications, particularly left ventricular (LV) dysfunction, are the predominant drivers of morbidity and mortality [1−3]. This burden is largely driven by insulin resistance (IR), a core pathophysiological defect in T2DM, which plays a pivotal role in the development of diabetic cardiomyopathy, independent of concomitant coronary artery disease or hypertension [4−7]. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, calculated from fasting triglycerides and glucose, has emerged as a reliable, cost-effective surrogate marker of IR, demonstrating good sensitivity and specificity compared with hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp techniques [8, 9]. Although accumulating evidence has linked an elevated TyG index to various cardiac abnormalities, including LV diastolic and systolic dysfunction [10−13], the underlying pathway and the manner in which the TyG index is associated with LV dysfunction in T2DM patients remain incompletely understood.

As a complex functional organ, the heart operates as an integrated whole, and LV dysfunction may manifest as structural and/or functional impairment [14]. Previous studies have linked abnormalities in myocardial metabolism, remodeling, and microcirculation to decreased LV global strain [15−17]. In this context, we speculate that the TyG index may be associated with LV dysfunction through both direct and indirect associations. The direct association relates to insulin resistance‑induced cardiomyocyte injury; the indirect association relates to intermediate factors such as changes in cardiac structure and myocardial metabolism. These domains are not direct measures of LV dysfunction but represent distinct potential factors that are associated with impaired myocardial deformation. However, whether these factors, such as myocardial energetic efficiency (MEE) and microvascular perfusion function act as mediators between the TyG index and subclinical LV myocardial dysfunction have not been systematically investigated.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR), characterized by multi-parameter, multi-sequence, serves as the gold-standard imaging modality and a credible “one-stop-shop” for the non-invasive assessment of cardiac structure, function, myocardial perfusion, myocardial strain (deformation), and MEE [18, 19]. Subclinical LV myocardial dysfunction is a specific early alteration that is often overlooked but holds potential for benefit through early intervention. Therefore, in the present study we focused on subclinical LV myocardial dysfunction and excluded patients with LV ejection fraction (LVEF) < 50% or overt heart failure symptoms to avoid confounding by systolic heart failure, thereby allowing us to specifically investigate early myocardial impairment that occurs despite preserved ejection fraction.

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to use CMR to explore the association between the TyG index and subclinical LV myocardial dysfunction in T2DM patients, and to further investigate whether LV MEE and resting perfusion parameters statistically mediate this association. The incremental value of the present study lies in the simultaneous integration of multiparametric CMR assessment (myocardial strain, resting perfusion indices, and MEEi) with formal mediation analysis to provide hypothesis‑generating evidence for the pathways linking insulin resistance to diabetic cardiomyopathy.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cross-sectional study adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) statement, and the completed checklist is provided in Supplementary Table S1. The study protocol received approval from the institutional Biomedical Research Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design.

Study population

We retrospectively enrolled patients with a confirmed diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) according to American Diabetes Association criteria [20], who had undergone a CMR examination at our institution between January 2014 and February 2023, and who met the following inclusion criteria: (i) age ≥ 18 years; (ii) available TyG index data (fasting triglycerides and glucose within 3 months of CMR); (iii) absence of heart failure symptoms and LVEF ≥ 50% (to focus on subclinical dysfunction). Patients were excluded if any of the following applied: (i) Primary cardiomyopathies (hypertrophic, restrictive, or dilated cardiomyopathy); (ii) Congenital cardiac defects; (iii) Secondary (potentially secondary) cardiac diseases, including coronary artery disease, significant valvular dysfunction, severe arrhythmias, or a history of malignancy‑related therapy; (iv) Severe dysfunction of the liver, kidneys, or respiratory system; (v) Suboptimal or missing CMR images leading to incomplete post‑processing. The final analysis included 235 eligible T2DM patients. Based on tertiles of the TyG index, the cohort was divided into three groups: low TyG index (TyG index < 8.73, n = 78), moderate TyG index (‌8.73 ≤ TyG index<9.36, n = 79), and high TyG index (TyG index ‌≥ 9.36, n = 78) groups (Fig. 1). The TyG index was computed as Ln[fasting triglycerides (mg/dl) × fasting glucose (mg/dl)/2] [13].

CMR protocol

All CMR examinations were conducted on 3.0 T whole-body Siemens MAGNETOM scanners (Trio Tim or Skyra scanner, Siemens Healthcare, Inc.) with participants in the supine position. Electrocardiogram (ECG)‑gated breath‑hold techniques were employed throughout. For the evaluation of LV geometry, function, and strain, cine images were performed using balanced steady-state free precession sequences in standard short-axis and long-axis (two-, three-, and four-chamber) views with following parameters: repetition time [TR] 3.4/2.8 ms, echo time [TE] 1.3/1.2 ms, flip angle 50°/35°, slice thickness 8 mm, inter-slice spacing 0 mm, field of view 340 × 285 mm2, and matrix size 256 × 166 pixels. 25 frames were reconstructed in each cardiac cycle. For the analysis of LV resting myocardial perfusion, the first-pass myocardial perfusion images were acquired concurrently in three standard short-axis slices (basal, mid-ventricular, and apical slices) and one four-chamber slice using an inversion recovery prepared echoplanar sequence with the following parameters: TR 149/163 ms; TE 1.1/1.0 ms; flip angle 10°; matrix 154 × 192 pixels, and field of view 258 × 322 mm2, by injecting gadobenate dimeglumine (MultiHance; Bracco, Milan, Italy) intravenously at a dose of 0.2 mL/kg body weight at a flow rate of 2.5–3.0 mL/s.

CMR analyses

All CMR data were processed using commercial software (cvi42, v.5.11.2; circle cardiovascular imaging, Calgary, Canada) by two radiologists, each with at least five years of experience in CMR analysis. After importing the offline images into the appropriate functional module, the software automatically delineated the LV endocardial and epicardial borders as well as the blood pool (for perfusion analysis), with manual adjustments made as needed.

LV geometry and global function: From the cine images, we automatically derived LV end‑diastolic volume (EDV), end‑systolic volume (ESV), stroke volume (SV), myocardial mass, and heart rate. The LV remodeling index was then computed as LV mass divided by EDV [21]. The LV global function index (GFI) was calculated using the formula: {LV SV/ [(LV EDV + LV ESV)/2 + (LV mass/1.05)]} × 100) [21, 22]. All volumetric measures were indexed to body surface area as appropriate. (Fig. 2A1–A4)

LV MEE represents the ratio of the mechanical work performed by the heart to the energy consumed per beat. It was approximated using the formula: MEE = stroke work/myocardial oxygen consumption ≈ (SBP×SV)/ (SBP×HR) = SV/HR. To adjust for myocardial mass, MEE was indexed to LV mass (MEEi), which estimates the ideal amount of blood pumped by each gram of LV mass per second [23].

LV myocardial resting perfusion‑derived parameters: signal intensity‑time curves were generated from resting (non‑stress) first‑pass perfusion images for 16 LV myocardial segments (Bull’s eye plot) and for the blood pool. From these curves we derived the following semi‑quantitative microvascular perfusion parameters: upslope, maximal signal intensity (MaxSI), time to maximum signal intensity (TTM), and the perfusion index (myocardial upslope divided by blood‑pool upslope). Global LV perfusion values were obtained by averaging the 16 segmental measurements. (Fig. 2B1–B3)

LV myocardial global strain: Global peak strain in the radial (GRPS), circumferential (GCPS), and longitudinal (GLPS) directions were obtained. This was achieved by voxel‑based myocardial tracking using the short‑axis and long‑axis (two‑, three‑, and four‑chamber) cine images. Since the negative signs of LV GCPS and GLPS indicate directions, less negative values (i.e., smaller absolute values) represent more severe impairment of myocardial deformation; therefore, when describing these strain parameters, we refer to their absolute values. (Fig. 2C1–C8)

Analyses of CMR parameter reproducibility

To evaluate intra‑observer reproducibility, the primary radiologist (with nine years of CMR experience) re‑analyzed cine and perfusion images from a randomly selected subset of 80 subjects on two separate occasions, at least 30 days apart. For inter‑observer reproducibility, a second radiologist (with five years of CMR experience), blinded to the first reader’s results and to clinical data, independently analyzed the same set of images.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 24.0 (IBM SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). There were no missing data for the main variables (TyG index and CMR parameters) or key covariates (age, sex, BMI, hypertension, dyslipidemia, statin use). For a small proportion of missing HbA1c values (< 3%), complete case analysis was performed because the missing rate was low. Unless otherwise specified, a two‑tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Normality of continuous variables was assessed with the Shapiro‑Wilk test and Q‑Q plots. Normally distributed data are reported as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using Student’s t‑test or one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post‑hoc tests as appropriate (a two‑tailed P < 0.017 was considered statistically significant). Non‑normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range) and were analyzed using the Mann‑Whitney U test or Kruskal‑Wallis rank test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies (percentages) and were compared using the chi‑square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Candidate variables were first screened by univariate linear regression; those with P < 0.1 and without collinearity (variance inflation factor < 2.5) were then entered into multivariate backward linear regression models to identify independent associations with LV global strain. Regression diagnostics, including residual analysis, heteroscedasticity testing, and multicollinearity assessment, were performed to evaluate the effectiveness and reliability of the multivariate linear regression models. Furthermore, based on clinical background, indicators considered relevant to cardiovascular injury (e.g., age, sex, body mass index, systolic blood pressure, resting heart rate, medication history, hypertension, and dyslipidemia) were included in the multivariate analysis regardless of univariate outcomes. To further evaluate the incremental value of the TyG index over its individual components (Triglycerides [TG] and fasting blood glucose [FBG]), we compared model fit among four separate models: ln(TG) alone, ln(FBG) alone, TyG alone, and ln(TG) + ln(FBG) combined. TG and FBG were natural log-transformed to maintain mathematical consistency with the TyG formula [TyG = ln(TG × FBG / 2) = ln(TG) + ln(FBG) − ln(2)]. All models were adjusted for the same set of covariates. Model fit was assessed using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with lower values indicating better fit.

To investigate potential linear or nonlinear relationships between the TyG index and LV MEEi, myocardial upslope, remodeling index, and global strain, restricted cubic spline (RCS) plots were employed. Mediation analysis was performed using the ‘mediation’ package in R. A nonparametric bootstrap method with 5000 resamples was used to generate bias‑corrected 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for the indirect, direct, and total effects. The proportion mediated was calculated as (indirect effect / total effect) × 100%. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine the relationship between MEEi and resting perfusion upslope prior to mediation analysis. Serial mediation was not performed due to the lack of an established temporal hierarchy between these two parameters and the exploratory cross-sectional nature of the study. All models included the following covariates: age, sex, BMI, SBP, resting heart rate, dyslipidemia, hypertension, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), antidiabetic medications, diabetes duration, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). To assess whether MEEi provides incremental information beyond LV remodeling index, we performed likelihood ratio tests comparing two nested models. The base model included established risk factors and LV remodeling index, while the extended model additionally included MEEi. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to assess reproducibility.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study cohort

The clinical baseline characteristics of the study cohort are summarized in Table 1. The mean TyG index values among low, moderate, and high TyG index groups were 8.34 ± 0.30, 9.05 ± 0.19, and 9.89 ± 0.49, respectively. There were no significant differences in sex, smoking history, heart rate, estimated glomerular filtration rate, duration of diabetes, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure among low, moderate, and high TyG index groups (all p > 0.05). The moderate and high TyG index groups showed higher body mass index than low TyG index group (24.53 ± 2.89 kg/m2 vs. 24.63 ± 3.10 kg/m2 vs. 23.27 ± 2.86 kg/m2, p = 0.006). For indicators related to glycolipid metabolism, as the TyG index increased, the proportion of patients with dyslipidemia rose progressively (18[23.1%] vs. 37[46.8%] vs. 58[74.3%], p < 0.001). Levels of triglycerides, total cholesterol, high density lipoprotein cholesterol, fasting blood glucose and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) also increased significantly (all p < 0.05).

Statistical mediation analysis of LV MEEi and upslope in the TyG index‑LV global strain relationship

RCS plots showed no nonlinear association between the TyG index and LV global strain, MEEi, or upslope (all P‑overall < 0.05; all P‑nonlinear > 0.1) (Fig. 3). Pearson correlation analysis revealed a modest but significant correlation between MEEi and resting perfusion upslope (r = 0.209, p = 0.001). Based on the multivariate regression results and RCS plots, mediation modeling was performed with the TyG index as the predictor (X), LV global strain (three directions) as the outcome (Y), and LV MEEi and upslope as mediators (M). As shown in Fig. 4and Supplementary Table S2, the mediation analysis indicated that LV MEEi and upslope exhibited statistically mediated effects in the association between the TyG index and LV global strain. Across all models, the p‑values for the total, indirect, and direct effects were all statistically significant (p < 0.05), even after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, SBP, resting heart rate, dyslipidemia, hypertension, HbA1c, antidiabetic medications, antihypertensive medication, diabetes duration, and eGFR. The statistically mediated proportions of the association between the TyG index and LV global strain were 8.2–8.6% for MEEi and 4.3–12.5% for upslope.

Incremental value of the TyG index over its individual components

In supplementary model comparison analyses (Supplementary Table S3), we directly compared the model fit of the TyG index with that of its individual components, ln(TG) and ln(FBG), alone. Across all three LV strain directions, the TyG model consistently demonstrated substantially lower AIC and BIC values than either single-component model. For LV GRPS, the TyG model showed an AIC of 552.06, compared with 966.34 for ln(TG) alone and 952.18 for ln(FBG) alone. Similar patterns were observed for GCPS (TyG: 535.75 vs. ln(TG): 719.59 vs. ln(FBG): 760.42) and GLPS (TyG: 433.97 vs. ln(TG): 706.01 vs. ln(FBG): 744.08). Notably, the combined model including both ln(TG) and ln(FBG) together showed the lowest AIC and BIC values among all models.

Incremental value of MEEi beyond LV remodeling index

In nested model comparisons (Supplementary Table S4), adding MEEi to the base model (which included established risk factors and LV remodeling index) significantly improved model fit across all three LV strain directions: GRPS (Δχ² = 13.99, Δdf = 1, P < 0.001), GCPS (Δχ² = 20.22, Δdf = 1, P < 0.001), and GLPS (Δχ² = 15.37, Δdf = 1, P < 0.001).

Discussion

In this study, we used CMR to comprehensively evaluate the association between the TyG index and subclinical LV myocardial dysfunction in patients with T2DM, and further quantified the statistically mediated associations of LV MEEi and resting perfusion upslope within this relationship. The main findings are as follows: (1) T2DM patients with a higher TyG index exhibited more adverse LV geometric remodeling (higher LV mass and remodeling index), lower LV global function index, reduced MEEi, impaired resting myocardial perfusion, and decreased LV global strain. (2) The TyG index, LV MEEi and myocardial upslope remained independently associated with LV global strain after adjusting for confounders. (3) Reduced MEEi statistically accounted for approximately 8.2–8.6% of the total association of the TyG index with LV global strain, while resting perfusion upslope accounted for approximately 4.3–12.5%. (4) Furthermore, supplementary model comparison analyses demonstrated that the TyG index provided better explanatory power for LV strain impairment compared with either TG or FBG alone. These findings provide novel imaging-based evidence consistent with a potential pathway linking the TyG index, a simple surrogate of insulin resistance, to diabetic myocardial injury in T2DM patients.

TyG index, insulin resistance and myocardial injury

The TyG index has been validated as a reliable surrogate marker of IR, showing good performance compared with the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp in certain populations [8, 9]. In the present study, T2DM patients with a high TyG index exhibited significant LV geometric remodeling (increased LV mass, higher remodeling index), decreased global function index, and impaired myocardial strain, despite preserved LV ejection fraction. Our results align with previous CMR studies, which have reported associations between a higher TyG index and adverse ventricular remodeling, as well as an increased risk of heart failure and atrial fibrillation [24, 25]. These observations are in line with the concept that IR is associated with reduced cardiac efficiency and adverse cardiac remodeling, potentially through increased fatty acid oxidation, disruption of systemic metabolic and inflammatory milieus, and activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system [26−28]. Notably, these abnormalities were observed in patients with preserved LV ejection fraction, which is consistent with the idea that the TyG index may capture early, subclinical myocardial dysfunction [29]. Meanwhile, supplementary analyses confirmed that the TyG index outperformed either its individual component alone in explaining LV strain impairment, supporting its incremental value as an integrated marker of insulin resistance that captures the synergistic metabolic burden beyond isolated glucose or lipid abnormalities.

Statistically mediated roles of myocardial energetic efficiency

MEEi reflects the heart’s ability to convert oxygen consumption into external work [23]. In the insulin-resistant state, the heart preferentially utilizes fatty acids as a substrate; however, the oxygen cost for generating equivalent ATP is higher than for glucose, which has been associated with reduced myocardial energy metabolism [23, 30, 31]. Consistent with the above concept, our study found that MEEi was significantly lower in the high TyG index group, and that this reduction partially served as a statistical mediator in the association between the TyG index and LV global strain, with a statistically mediated proportion of 8.2–8.6%. Multivariate regression further showed that MEEi was independently associated with LV global strain. These findings are consistent with, but not proof of, the hypothesis that IR, as reflected by the TyG index, may be associated with reduced mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation efficiency in the myocardium, which could in turn contribute to relative energy deficiency and impaired myocardial fiber shortening and relaxation [32, 33]. However, as MEEi is an indirect hemodynamic surrogate, these mechanistic interpretations remain speculative and require direct metabolic validation.

Previous studies have reported associations between reduced MEEi and impaired myocardial strain in metabolic syndrome and hypertensive populations [34–36]. Our study extends this line of evidence to the TyG index, suggesting that MEEi may serve as a potential marker reflecting metabolic factors that could link IR to cardiac mechanical dysfunction, though this interpretation warrants further investigation with direct metabolic imaging. On the other hand, considering the formula used to derive MEEi, we speculate that the association between MEEi and LV global strain may partly reflect their shared dependence on LV geometry and cine-derived measurements. Even so, MEEi remained independently associated with LV global strain after adjustment for LV remodeling index, and nested model comparisons also confirmed its incremental value beyond structural indices, supporting its interpretation as a marker of myocardial energetic efficiency rather than a mere reflection of LV geometry. However, because cross-sectional studies preclude causal inferences, these hypotheses require further validation.

Statistically mediated roles of resting perfusion upslope

Microvascular dysfunction is another hallmark of diabetic cardiomyopathy [37, 38]. In our study, upslope was significantly decreased in the high TyG index group and was independently associated with decreased LV global strain, which is in line with prior studies that have linked the TyG index to coronary microvascular dysfunction in patients with chronic coronary syndrome and to adverse outcomes in acute coronary syndrome [10, 11]. IR and chronic hyperglycemia have been implicated in damage to coronary microvascular endothelium, which has been associated with impaired endothelium-dependent vasodilation, reduced capillary density, and consequently reduced vasodilatory reserve and impaired first-pass perfusion [10, 39]. More importantly, upslope independently exhibited a statistical mediation effect in the association between the TyG index and LV global strain, with a mediated proportion of 4.3–12.5%. These findings are consistent with the following hypothesis-generating sequence: insulin resistance, microvascular endothelial dysfunction, impaired myocardial perfusion, limited oxygen and substrate delivery, energy deficiency, and reduced systolic and diastolic function as reflected by decreased global peak strain [40, 41]. However, in our study, upslope is a semi-quantitative parameter derived from resting first-pass perfusion. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of coronary microvascular dysfunction, and future validation using stress perfusion or quantitative myocardial blood flow measurement is required.

Clinical implications

The TyG index is a simple, inexpensive biomarker calculated from routine blood tests [13]. Our findings show that a higher TyG index is independently associated with subclinical LV myocardial dysfunction in T2DM patients with preserved LVEF, suggesting it may serve as a practical risk stratification tool to identify high-risk individuals for early cardiac imaging and closer follow-up. The exploratory mediation analysis generates the hypothesis that myocardial energetic efficiency and resting perfusion may represent potential pathways linking insulin resistance to myocardial dysfunction, warranting prospective investigation. At present, the TyG index should be considered an adjunctive screening tool rather than a replacement for established risk markers. Its routine use could facilitate earlier detection of diabetic cardiomyopathy before LVEF declines, supporting targeted preventive strategies. Although the combined ln(TG) + ln(FBG) model showed slightly better statistical fit, the TyG index as a single integrated biomarker offers greater simplicity and practicality for routine clinical use, striking a favorable balance between predictive utility and clinical operability.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this single‑center, retrospective cross‑sectional study with a long study period may have introduced selection bias and limited extrapolation to broader T2DM populations. Our cohort consisted of patients referred for clinically indicated CMR at a single tertiary center over approximately one decade, and therefore may not be representative of the general T2DM population. This should be considered when interpreting the potential clinical implementation of TyG‑based risk stratification. Moreover, its cross‑sectional design precludes causal inference, and the mediation findings are exploratory and require validation in future longitudinal or prospective studies with larger sample sizes. Second, MEEi is a surrogate of myocardial energy metabolism, and our findings require validation with direct metabolic imaging. Third, despite adjustment for multiple variables, residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Fourth, due to the limitations of the retrospective design and the lack of T1 mapping images, we did not perform tissue characterization (e.g., T1 mapping, extracellular volume fraction). This limits our ability to determine whether myocardial fibrosis or other structural abnormalities contribute to the observed associations, and these aspects warrant further investigation in future research. Fifth, coronary artery disease was excluded based on available clinical and imaging data, but silent non-obstructive coronary artery disease cannot be completely excluded because not all patients underwent invasive or CT angiography. Sixth, the incremental value of the TyG index over its individual components needs to be clarified in future studies.

Conclusions

In T2DM patients, a higher TyG index is associated with adverse LV geometric remodeling, reduced myocardial energetic efficiency, impaired resting myocardial perfusion, and decreased LV global strain. Reduced MEEi and impaired resting myocardial perfusion were found to statistically mediate the association between the TyG index and LV global strain, accounting for approximately 8.2–8.6% and 4.3–12.5% of the total association, respectively. These findings generate hypotheses regarding myocardial energetics and resting perfusion as potential pathways associated with diabetic myocardial dysfunction that warrant prospective investigation.
