Harnessing chloride to drive catalyst reconstruction for durable and efficient seawater electrolysis
Authors: Huangcong Tang, Zemin Feng, Di Feng, Shunchun Yao, Kui Shen, Liyu Chen, Yingwei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDirect seawater electrolysis offers an attractive route for sustainable hydrogen production, yet its practical implementation remains constrained by the corrosive nature of chlorion (Cl−). Here, we reveal that trace amounts of Cl−, rather than being detrimental, can play a constructive role in directing the structural evolution of transition-metal catalysts. Guided by this insight, NiS2 nanoparticles with controlled loading amounts on NiFe-MOF nanosheets (denoted as NiFe-MOF-S) are designed to enable fine regulation of Cl− proximity to the active metal sites. This design promotes the in situ reconstruction of catalyst into a dense γ-NiFeOOH phase with short Ni–Fe bond lengths, which triggers a mechanistic shift from the lattice-oxygen-mediated pathway to oxide path mechanism. NiFe-MOF-S achieves high oxygen evolution performance in alkaline seawater (1 M KOH + seawater), requiring a much lower overpotential of 213 mV to reach 500 mA cm−2 in alkaline seawater than that in alkaline water (1 M KOH) (268 mV). Furthermore, the electrocatalyst demonstrates high durability with a degradation rate of only 1.4 μV h−1 for over 7000 hours at 1.0 A cm−2 in alkaline seawater. A kilowatt-level alkaline seawater electrolyzer equipped with NiFe-MOF-S/NF operates reliably for over 1500 hours at industrially relevant conditions.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Metal-organic frameworks, Electrocatalysis

Introduction

With the intensive exploitation of oceanic energy, direct seawater electrolysis is regarded as a promising technique to facilitate on-site conversion of renewable energy sources to produce green hydrogen (H2)1–4. Direct seawater electrolysis avoids the additional cost, energy consumption, and infrastructure associated with reverse osmosis purification, making it particularly advantageous for distributed hydrogen production5,6. Despite the potential benefits of this technique, the complex compositions of seawater pose substantial challenges to efficient and stable electrolysis. Especially, chloride ion (Cl−) in seawater (~0.5 M) would induce competitive side reaction and irreversibly damage the active metal sites, leading to severe catalyst degradation7,8. Currently, the commercial alkaline pure water electrolyzer electrodes (i.e., Raney Ni) merely operate below 0.2 A cm−2 in seawater electrolysis to avoid the aggressive Cl− attack. Therefore, developing Cl−-resistant catalysts is imperative to enable seawater electrolysis.

Transition-metal-based compounds are frequently employed as cost-effective and high-performance anode catalysts for water electrolysis9–13. To mitigate Cl−-induced corrosion in seawater, conventional approaches usually rely on passive strategies, such as coating protective layers onto catalyst surfaces, but these catalysts usually exhibit inferior activity and durability in seawater compared to pure water14–22. Recent studies reveal that Cl− can contribute to enhanced catalyst performance in seawater. These catalytic systems typically consist of two distinct types of metal sites, in which one metal site with a stronger bonding affinity for Cl− can effectively prevent the poisoning of Cl− to another metal (i.e., active site)23–28. The metal sites coordinated with Cl− can further regulate the electronic structure of the neighboring metal active sites for enhanced activity. Nevertheless, this promotional effect is observed only at relatively low current densities (e.g., 0.2 A cm−2), as the auxiliary metal sites cannot accommodate the more Cl− ions at ampere-level current density required for industrial seawater electrolysis. Therefore, understanding and addressing the challenges of Cl−-induced corrosion and deactivation at industrial scales (e.g., kilowatt (kW)-level systems and ampere-level current density) is critical for commercialization, yet remains largely underexplored.

In this study, we discovered a different promotional mechanism offered by Cl−, which can facilitate a beneficial reconstruction of transition metal compounds into dense metal oxyhydroxides with enhanced oxygen evolution reaction (OER) performance. Guided by the understanding of the different roles of Cl− concentration, we designed NiS2 nanoparticles with controlled loading amount on NiFe-MOF nanosheets (denoted as NiFe-MOF-S) to achieve regulated proximity of Cl− at metal active sites. The in-situ formation of SO42− can repel most Cl− but allows traces of Cl− to accelerate the structural transformation of NiFe-MOF-S to form dense γ-NiFeOOH species. The dense γ-NiFeOOH with short Ni–Fe bond lengths enables the OER mechanism switching from the lattice-oxygen-mediated mechanism (LOM) pathway to the oxide path mechanism (OPM) pathway for enhanced activity. NiFe-MOF-S achieves high activity in alkaline seawater (1 M KOH + seawater) with an overpotential of 213 mV at 500 mA cm−2, lower than that in alkaline water (1 M KOH) (268 mV). NiFe-MOF-S/NF also exhibits high durability for over 7000 hours at a current density of 1.0 A cm−2 in alkaline seawater with activity degradation of only 1.4 μV h−1, meeting the technical target of 2.3 μV h−1 set by the United States Department of Energy (DOE) for the year 2026. Furthermore, a kW-level alkaline seawater electrolyzer assembled by NiFe-MOF-S/NF achieves a low cell voltage of 1.84 V at 0.5 A cm−2 and maintains stable operation over 1500 hours in 30 wt% KOH + seawater at 80 °C.

Results

Effect of Cl− concentration on OER performance

We first systematically investigated the effects of Cl− concentrations on OER performance using NiFe-MOF grown on Ni foam (denoted as NiFe-MOF/NF) as a catalyst. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of NiFe-MOF display diffraction peaks indexed to the simulated NiFe-terephthalic acid (Supplementary Fig. S1). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of NiFe-MOF show a petal-like nanosheet morphology (Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3a). The corresponding selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns further verify the NiFe-MOF phase (Supplementary Fig. S3b). Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) polarization curves show that the OER activity of NiFe-MOF/NF improves as the Cl− concentration increases, reaching a maximum at 0.05 M (Supplementary Fig. S4a). A further increase in concentration leads to a decline in activity. The corresponding Tafel slopes confirm optimal OER kinetics at 0.05 M Cl−, highlighting the promotional effect of low Cl− levels (Supplementary Fig. S4b and d). In addition, inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) after chronopotentiometry (CP) tests for 24 h reveals minimal Ni and Fe leaching below 0.05 M Cl− (Supplementary Fig. S4c). When the Cl− concentration in the electrolyte exceeds 0.05 M, metal leaching increases sharply, with levels rising to 9 and 3-fold higher for Ni and Fe in 2.0 M Cl−, respectively, compared to those in 0.05 M Cl−. These results indicate that excessive Cl− induces severe corrosion, accelerating leaching of Ni/Fe and reducing OER activity (Supplementary Fig. S4e)29. The observed corrosion and deactivation at Cl− concentrations above 0.05 M are consistent with previous reports showing that most catalysts undergo significant metal dissolution and performance loss in seawater containing ~0.5 M Cl−. In contrast, the origin of the activity enhancement observed at Cl− concentrations below 0.05 M remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Previous studies have attributed the enhanced OER activity in alkaline seawater compared to alkaline pure water to the electronic regulation of metal active sites by the adjacent metal after adsorbing Cl−23–28. This mechanism well explains the different behavior of dual-metal and single-metal systems in alkaline pure water and seawater. However, in our catalytic system, even the single-metal catalysts (Ni-MOF/NF and Fe-MOF/NF) show improved activity at low Cl⁻ concentrations compared with that in alkaline water (Supplementary Fig. S5). This finding suggests that low levels of Cl− can activate metal sites in both single- and dual-metal MOFs through an alternative Cl−-induced mechanism.

To further probe the specific Cl−-induced mechanism, we designed a regulated Cl−-resistant layer on the surface of the catalyst to control the proximity of Cl− to metal active sites. NiFe-MOF was partially converted to metal sulfide, which would in situ form sulfate ion (SO42−) during electrolysis to electrostatically repel Cl−. By tuning the deposition amount of sulfides on the catalytic support, the proximity of Cl− to metal sites can be controlled to suppress excess Cl− while permitting a small fraction to promote activity (Supplementary Fig. S4f)23,24,27.

Synthesis and characterizations of NiFe-MOF-S/NF

NiFe-MOF-S/NF was synthesized through a sulfide vapor calcination process of NiFe-MOF/NF (Supplementary Fig. S6). The XRD patterns of NiFe-MOF-S display diffractions indexed to NiS2 (PDF#11-0099) while retaining the NiFe-MOF structure, indicating partial conversion of NiFe-MOF to NiS2 (Fig. 1c). The Raman spectrum of NiFe-MOF-S shows peaks located at 270, 283, and 485 cm−1, assigned to Tg, Eg, and Ag modes of NiS2, respectively (Fig. 1d), which is not observed in NiFe-MOF. Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR) spectra of both NiFe-MOF-S and NiFe-MOF display absorption bands at 540, 748, 820 cm−1, assigned to M–O vibration, C–H bending vibration, and M–OH vibration of NiFe-MOF, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S7). NiFe-MOF-S shows an additional absorption band at approximately 600 cm−1, assigned to Ni–S vibrations, which confirms the presence of NiS2 species in the NiFe-MOF-S. SEM images display that the nanosheet array architecture is preserved, while its surface is densely decorated with uniformly distributed nanoparticles (Fig. 1a, b, and Supplementary Fig. S8). TEM images show the NiS2 nanoparticles with an average size of 21 nm (Fig. 1f). The high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image and the corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns identify the (200) and (210) crystal planes of NiS2 (Fig. 1h). SAED patterns confirm the coexistence of NiFe-MOF and NiS2 phases (Fig. 1g). High-angle annular dark field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) and the corresponding energy dispersive spectroscopy analysis reveal the homogeneous distribution of Ni, Fe, S, and O in NiFe-MOF-S (Supplementary Fig. S9). Notably, the density of NiS2 nanoparticles on NiFe-MOF-S can be tuned by varying the amount of sulfur, and higher sulfur loadings give denser NiS2 coverage while maintaining similar particle sizes (Supplementary Fig. S10).

The structural and electronic properties of the catalysts were further investigated by X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectroscopy. The Ni K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectrum of NiFe-MOF-S closely resembles that of NiO, indicating Ni predominantly exists in the +2 oxidation state (Supplementary Fig. S11). The Fe K-edge XANES spectrum of NiFe-MOF-S exhibits an absorption edge position similar to that of Fe2O3, confirming that Fe predominantly exists in the +3 oxidation state (Supplementary Fig. S12). Fourier-transform (FT) extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra of NiFe-MOF-S reveal two prominent peaks at 2.06 and 2.39 Å, assigned to Ni–O and Ni–S bonds, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S13). Likewise, the FT-EXAFS spectra at the Fe K-edge display two peaks at 1.98 and 2.17 Å, assigned to Fe–O and Fe–S coordination, confirming the existence of Fe–S bond (Supplementary Fig. S14). The wavelet-transform (WT) EXAFS plots further corroborate these findings, showing intensity maxima associated with the M–O and M–S coordination (Supplementary Fig. S15). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis provides additional insights into the chemical states of the elements. The Ni 2p XPS spectrum of NiFe-MOF-S exhibits peaks at 873.4 and 855.5 eV, consistent with Ni2+ species (Supplementary Fig. S16). The Fe 2p XPS spectrum shows peaks at 725.4 and 712.6 eV, indicative of Fe3+ (Supplementary Fig. S17). In the S 2p XPS spectrum, two prominent peaks at 162.6 and 163.8 eV are attributed to M–S bonds, further verifying the successful incorporation of sulfide in the NiFe-MOF-S (Fig. 1e)30,31.

OER activity and durability of NiFe-MOF-S/NF

The OER performance of NiFe-MOF-S/NF was evaluated in a three-electrode system with 90% iR correction. LSV polarization curves reveal that NiFe-MOF-S/NF exhibits enhanced OER activity across a broad Cl− concentration range (0.01–2 M) compared to alkaline water (Supplementary Figs. S18 and S19). This Cl−-induced activity enhancement is also evident in alkaline seawater electrolysis. NiFe-MOF-S/NF requires an overpotential of only 151 and 213 mV to achieve 10 and 500 mA cm−2 in alkaline seawater, i.e., 38 and 55 mV lower than that in alkaline water, respectively (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Figs. S20 and S21). The smaller Tafel slope observed in alkaline seawater (19.4 mV dec−1) relative to alkaline water (35.5 mV dec−1) confirms its accelerated OER reaction kinetics (Fig. 2b). Moreover, NiFe-MOF-S/NF delivers a high turnover frequency of 1.46 s−1 at an overpotential of 200 mV in alkaline seawater, which is ten times higher than that in alkaline water (0.13 s−1) (Supplementary Fig. S22). The Arrhenius analysis based on temperature-dependent OER activity reveals that the activation energy of NiFe-MOF-S/NF decreases from 40.9 kJ mol−1 in alkaline water to 25.2 kJ mol−1 in alkaline seawater (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. S23). These results highlight the high intrinsic activity of NiFe-MOF-S/NF in Cl−-containing environment. Notably, NiFe-MOF-S/NF achieves competitive activity among the state-of-the-art catalysts in seawater electrolysis (Supplementary Table S1)32–35.

Beyond activity enhancement, the influence of Cl− on the long-term durability of NiFe-MOF-S/NF was systematically investigated. CP tests reveal that NiFe-MOF-S/NF maintains better stability in alkaline seawater than in alkaline water over 1500 hours of continuous operation (Supplementary Fig. S24). ICP-MS analysis shows that the concentrations of dissolved Ni and Fe species in alkaline water are approximately three times higher than those in alkaline seawater (Supplementary Fig. S25), confirming that Cl−-involved environment is beneficial to enhance the OER durability. Encouraged by this durability improvement, NiFe-MOF-S/NF was further tested under industrially relevant high-current conditions. The electrode operates stably at 1.0 A cm−2 for over 7000 hours with a low degradation rate of 1.4 μV h−1 in alkaline seawater, well below the 2026 United States DOE target of 2.3 μV h−1 (Fig. 2d)35. The LSV curve of the used catalyst after 7000-h CP test is nearly identical to that of the fresh catalyst, demonstrating its high stability (Supplementary Fig. S26). This represents one of the longest operational stabilities reported for seawater electrolysis (Supplementary Table S1). UV-vis absorption spectroscopy using N,N-diethyl-p-phenylenediamine as a detector shows negligible signals of ClO− in the electrolyte after the stability tests (Supplementary Fig. S27), confirming the high OER selectivity of NiFe-MOF-S/NF. Gas collection analysis reveals a Faradaic efficiency of 99% for OER, verifying the high OER selectivity of NiFe-MOF-S/NF (Supplementary Figs. S28 and S29).

XRD pattern of NiFe-MOF-S/NF after 7000-h CP test shows no discernible diffraction peaks, confirming its amorphous nature (Supplementary Fig. S30). SEM image reveals that the flake-like architecture is largely retained, although the surface becomes noticeably rougher (Supplementary Fig. S31). The S 2p XPS spectrum of NiFe-MOF-S/NF after reaction clearly exhibits characteristic peaks corresponding to the S–O bonds of SO42− species (Supplementary Fig. S32), confirming that SO42− species remain at the electrode interface after 7000-hour CP test and thus provide sustained Cl− repulsion to enable stable operation. For comparison, the addition of free Na2SO4 in alkaline seawater suffered from rapid degradation after 230 h at 1.0 A cm−2 (Supplementary Fig. S33), indicating that dissolved SO42− alone cannot provide the long-term stabilization effect achieved by sulfide-derived surface SO42− species.

To further assess industrial applicability, durability tests were conducted in concentrated alkalis (e.g., 30 wt% KOH + seawater). NiFe-MOF-S/NF maintains stable operation for 1200 hours at 1 A cm−2 in 30 wt% KOH + seawater (Fig. 2e). An anion exchange membrane water electrolyzer (AEMWE) was then assembled with NiFe-MOF-S/NF as both anode and cathode (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. S34). The LSV polarization curves (without iR compensation) show higher electrolytic activity in 30 wt% KOH + seawater, achieving 1.0 A cm−2 at 1.88 V, compared to 1.96 V in 30 wt% KOH (Fig. 2g). Moreover, the AEMWE operates stably at 2.0 A cm−2 for 600 hours without notable degradation (Fig. 2h). Collectively, these results demonstrate that NiFe-MOF-S/NF is a highly active and durable catalyst for seawater electrolysis.

Effect of loading amount and morphology of sulfides on performance

Besides NiFe-MOF-S/NF, the Cl−-induced enhancement in OER activity is also observed for Ni-MOF-S/NF and Fe-MOF-S/NF, both of which exhibit higher performance in alkaline seawater compared to alkaline water (Supplementary Fig. S35). This observation highlights the pivotal role of sulfide nanoparticles in promoting activity regardless of bi- or monometallic catalysts.

To further elucidate the influence of sulfide loading on the Cl−-promotion effect, control samples NiFe-MOF-S-0.5/NF and NiFe-MOF-S-1.5/NF were synthesized using 0.5 and 1.5 g sulfur powder during the synthesis process, respectively. However, both samples display inferior OER activity in alkaline seawater relative to alkaline water (Supplementary Fig. S36). Moreover, NiFe-MOF-S-0.5/NF exhibits inferior durability after 200 hours of operation in alkaline seawater, as evidenced by a pronounced increase in overpotential required to achieve 200 mA cm−2 compared to NiFe-MOF-S/NF and NiFe-MOF-S-1.5/NF (Supplementary Fig. S37). ICP-MS measurements reveal severe dissolution of Ni and Fe species for NiFe-MOF-S-0.5/NF (Ni: 7.6 μg L−1; Fe: 28.8 μg L−1) in alkaline seawater, which are much higher than those for NiFe-MOF-S/NF and NiFe-MOF-S-1.5/NF (Supplementary Fig. S38). Therefore, the insufficient loading of NiS2 nanoparticles on NiFe-MOF-S-0.5/NF fails to effectively repel Cl−, resulting in accelerated corrosion. Conversely, excessive NiS2 loading in NiFe-MOF-S-1.5/NF suppresses the accessibility of Cl−, thereby limiting activity enhancement. These results confirm that an optimal loading amount of NiS2 nanoparticles is essential for establishing a balanced local Cl− concentration near the active metal sites, thereby suppressing corrosion and improving OER activity in seawater.

To further assess the influence of the morphology of sulfides, sulfides were introduced onto NiFe-MOF/NF via a hydrothermal method, yielding NiFe-MOF-S-HM/NF. SEM images reveal that NiFe-MOF-S-HM/NF possesses large and densely packed flaky sulfides on the NiFe-MOF surface (Supplementary Fig. S39). LSV measurements indicate that NiFe-MOF-S-HM/NF exhibits inferior OER activity in alkaline seawater compared to alkaline water (Supplementary Fig. S40). Corrosion tests of NiFe-MOF-S-HM/NF show a lower corrosion potential (0.18 V vs. reversible hydrogen electrode, RHE) and a higher corrosion current density (66 μA cm−2) than NiFe-MOF-S/NF (0.36 V vs. RHE; 6 μA cm−2) (Supplementary Fig. S41). The poorer corrosion resistance of NiFe-MOF-S-HM/NF can be attributed to its dense flaky morphology, which provides limited exposed surface area for SO42− generation. Consequently, the insufficient SO42− cannot effectively inhibit the ingress of corrosive Cl−. In contrast, the dispersed NiS2 nanoparticles with high surface exposure in NiFe-MOF-S/NF facilitate adequate SO42− formation, leading to the establishment of a robust Cl−-resistant interfacial layer.

Self-reconstruction behavior of NiFe-MOF-S/NF

The effect of Cl− in promoting the performance of NiFe-MOF-S was then systematically examined. The Bode phase plots of NiFe-MOF-S exhibit a distinct electrochemical process in the low-frequency region (100–101 Hz) in both alkaline water and alkaline seawater, which is related to the inhomogeneous charge distribution caused by the surface oxidized species (Supplementary Fig. S42). These results suggest that NiFe-MOF-S undergoes structural transformation during electrocatalysis. To further probe this behavior, cyclic voltammetry (CV) test was performed (Fig. 3g, h, and Supplementary Fig. S43). The CV curves without iR compensation reveal that the onset potential for Ni2+ oxidation shifts from 1.32 V in alkaline water to 1.30 V in alkaline seawater, indicating that the oxidation of Ni2+ to Ni3+ occurs more readily in alkaline seawater (Fig. 3i). The reduction peak current densities stabilize after several CV cycles in both electrolytes, signifying that the catalyst surfaces reach a steady state (Fig. 3j)36. Notably, NiFe-MOF-S/NF shows higher reduction peak current densities in alkaline seawater, implying a greater extent of Ni reconstruction37. Correspondingly, the OER activity of NiFe-MOF-S/NF progressively improves during this process. The reduction peak current densities were correlated with the potential required to achieve 100 mA cm−2 for NiFe-MOF-S/NF in both alkaline water and alkaline seawater (Supplementary Fig. S44), indicating that the in-situ formation of Ni3+ species serve as catalytically active sites. Moreover, a clear Cl− concentration–activity relationship was observed (Supplementary Fig. S45). As the Cl− concentration increased from 0 to 0.5 M, the reduction peak current density increased progressively, while the overpotential required to reach 100 mA cm−2 decreased correspondingly. This quantitative correlation indicates that Cl− facilitates the generation of electrochemically active Ni3+ species, thereby accelerating catalyst reconstruction and enhancing OER kinetics. For the metal sulfide-free MOFs, CV cycling tests show that 0.05 M Cl− increases the reduction peak current relative to Cl−-free conditions, indicating beneficial effects of trace Cl− for structural transformation and OER activity (Supplementary Figs. S46 and S47). In contrast, 0.5 M Cl− causes lower reduction peak current densities, owing to excessive Cl−-induced corrosion.

Furthermore, current–time (i–t) measurements at 1.64 V vs. RHE show that NiFe-MOF-S/NF exhibits a higher current density in alkaline seawater than in alkaline water (Supplementary Fig. S48). In contrast, unprotected NiFe-MOF/NF shows a lower and gradually decaying current density, indicating that the sulfate layer allows beneficial trace Cl− penetration while suppressing severe corrosion. These observations collectively demonstrate that Cl− facilitates the generation of active Ni3+ species to enhance the OER activity and stability.

To monitor surface reconstruction in real-time, in situ Raman spectroscopy was conducted in both alkaline water and alkaline seawater (Fig. 3k, l, and Supplementary Fig. S49). The peak at 480 cm−1 appears in both alkaline water and alkaline seawater at low potentials beginning at 1.25 V, which is assigned to the bending vibration of Ni2+–O in Ni(OH)2. As the potential rises, new peaks appear at 475 and 555 cm−1, corresponding to the bending (δ(Ni–O)) and stretching (ν(Ni–O)) vibrations of Ni3+–O in NiOOH, respectively. The phase transition occurs at 1.35 V in alkaline seawater, lower than that in alkaline water (1.40 V), suggesting that Cl− promotes the formation of NiOOH. Furthermore, the peak intensity ratio of δ(Ni–O) to ν(Ni–O) (defined as Iδ/ν) is lower in alkaline seawater than that in alkaline water, implying the formation of a denser γ-NiOOH phase in alkaline seawater (Fig. 3m, Supplementary Figs. S50 and S51).

To gain deeper insight into the structural and electronic properties after reconstruction, NiFe-MOF-S after the OER test (denoted as post-OER NiFe-MOF-S) was collected and analyzed. After the OER test, the Ni and Fe K-edges XANES spectra of the post-OER NiFe-MOF-S catalyst shift toward higher energies relative to those of the pristine catalyst (Fig.3a and Supplementary Fig. S52), indicating increased oxidation states after reconstruction. Moreover, the post-OER Ni 2p and Fe 2p XPS spectra exhibit positive binding energy shifts in alkaline seawater compared to alkaline water (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. S53), indicating higher oxidation states of both metals in alkaline seawater. The FT-EXAFS spectra display dominant peaks corresponding to M–O/S and Ni/Fe–M coordination (Fig. 3b, c, and Supplementary Figs. S54–S57), confirming the in situ formation of metal oxyhydroxides, consistent with the WT-EXAFS results (Fig. 3e). Importantly, the Ni–M (Fe–M) bond distances decrease from 3.13 Å (3.15 Å) in alkaline water to 2.93 Å (2.96 Å) in alkaline seawater, indicating the formation of a more compact metal oxyhydroxide phase with shortened Ni–Fe bonds under alkaline seawater conditions (Fig. 3b–d, and Supplementary Table S2). These findings verify that Cl− accelerates the surface reconstruction of NiFe-MOF-S, leading to the formation of compact metal oxyhydroxides. In addition, S 2p1/2 XPS spectra of post-OER NiFe-MOF-S show binding energies at 171.0 and 165.6 eV, assigned to S–O of the adsorbed SO42− species and Ni–S of Ni2S (Supplementary Fig. S58), respectively, indicating that the partial Ni2S would be oxidated to form SO42− species and adsorbed on the γ-NiOOH surface.

The above findings indicate that the reconstruction of NiS2 into active NiOOH in alkaline seawater is significantly influenced by Cl−. The transformation proceeds via nucleophilic attack by OH−, releasing sulfur and forming Ni(OH)2, which is subsequently oxidated to NiOOH (NiS2 + 2OH− → Ni(OH)2 + 2S + 2e−; Ni(OH)2 + OH− → NiOOH + H2O + e−)38. During this process, the adsorbed Cl− weakens the Ni–S covalent bonds through a strong penetration effect, altering the local electron density around Ni centers. This facilitates Ni–S bond cleavage and promotes the formation of a compact γ-NiOOH phase with shorter Ni–Ni bond lengths. Consequently, Cl− induces the formation of denser γ-NiOOH compared to Cl−-free environments, leading to a greater extent of compact γ-NiOOH.

The conversion degree of Ni2+ to Ni3+ was evaluated by holding the catalysts at 1.23 V. The i–t curves exhibit gradual current decay, corresponding to progressive Ni oxidation. The integrated charge, reflecting the conversion degree, is calculated to be 2.6 C in alkaline seawater and 2.0 C in alkaline water. The LSV curves normalized with integrated charge reveal higher current density in alkaline seawater, suggesting that the denser γ-NiOOH formed in alkaline seawater has higher intrinsic OER activity (Supplementary Fig. S59).

OER mechanism of dense γ-NiOOH

Given that Cl− promotes rapid surface reconstruction to form dense γ-NiFeOOH with enhanced OER performance, we further investigated the underlying reaction mechanism in both alkaline water and alkaline seawater. Potential-dependent in situ attenuated total reflection-surface enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR-SEIRAS) was used to monitor surface intermediates during the OER (Fig. 4a and b). The broad band near 1155 cm−1 is assigned to SO42− species generated from the oxidation of NiS2 species39. Notably, in alkaline seawater, a characteristic *O–O* vibration appears at 1104 cm−1 (Supplementary Table S3), indicative of oxygen bridge formation between dual active sites via the OPM pathway40. In comparison, the distinctive peak located at 1205 cm−1 observed in alkaline water corresponds to *O–O intermediate formed through the LOM pathway41,42.

To further verify these mechanistic differences, in situ differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) was conducted using 18O isotope labeling (Fig. 4d and e). NiFe-MOF-S/NF was first labeled with O isotopes through CV activation in H2O-based KOH. The labeled catalysts were then rinsed with H216O and subsequently analyzed during LSV measurements to monitor real-time m/z = 32 (16O16O), m/z = 34 (16O18O), and m/z = 36 (18O18O) signals. The ratio of O2 and O2 signals in alkaline seawater is 15.9 times higher than that in alkaline water, confirming that OER in alkaline seawater follows the OPM pathway (Fig. 4f)42. The suppression of active lattice oxygen participation in alkaline seawater explains the higher durability of NiFe-MOF-S/NF, as the OPM mechanism pathway mitigates catalyst degradation caused by lattice oxygen involvement23,26,43. Further evidence is obtained from the O 1 s XPS spectra, where post-OER NiFe-MOF-S in alkaline water exhibits a higher concentration of oxygen vacancies than in alkaline seawater (Fig. 4c). Moreover, NiFe-MOF-S/NF in alkaline water shows stronger pH-dependent OER activity than in alkaline seawater, suggesting reduced reactivity of lattice oxygen under Cl−-containing conditions (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. S60). The tetramethylammonium hydroxide (TMAOH) chemical probe measurements also reveal a more pronounced polarization curve shift in alkaline water than in alkaline seawater (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. S61). Therefore, Cl− facilitates the mechanistic transition of γ-NiFeOOH from the LOM pathway in alkaline water to the OPM pathway in alkaline seawater, thereby enhancing both activity and durability during the OER.

Density function theory (DFT) calculations were further conducted to elucidate the OER pathway of the reconstructed γ-NiFeOOH phases. Two structural models with different bond lengths of dual-site Ni–Fe were constructed, including conventional L-NiFeOOH and compact S-NiFeOOH with shortened Ni–Fe bond lengths (Supplementary Fig. S62). The projected density of states (PDOS) shows that S-NiFeOOH possesses a higher density of states near the Fermi level compared to L-NiFeOOH, suggesting the facilitated electron transfer (Fig. 4i). The calculated free energy barriers for the rate-determining step (RDS) on S-NiFeOOH are 3.22, 1.72, and 2.93 eV via the adsorbate evolution mechanism (AEM), OPM, and LOM pathway, respectively, demonstrating that the OPM pathway is most thermodynamically favorable on S-NiFeOOH (Fig. 4j, k, and Supplementary Fig. S63). In contrast, for L-NiFeOOH, the lowest RDS energy corresponds to the LOM pathway (1.9 eV), which is more favorable than AEM (3.02 eV) and OPM pathway (2.82 eV) (Supplementary Fig. S64). These results align with experimental observations, demonstrating that the Cl−-induced formation of S-NiFeOOH with shorter Ni–Fe bonds promotes the OPM pathway, thereby enhancing both the intrinsic OER activity and durability in seawater (Fig. 4l and m).

Industrial seawater electrolysis applications

Building on the high activity and durability of NiFe-MOF-S/NF demonstrated in small-scale studies, we further evaluated its scalability for industrial H2 production using a kW-scale alkaline seawater electrolyzer (ASWE). The scale-up performance of the NiFe-MOF-S/NF anode was assessed in an industrial system equipped with a 1.0 kW electrolyzer stack comprising 13 cells (Fig. 5a). Each cell possesses an anode area of 78.5 cm2, giving in a total effective anode area of 1020.5 cm2 for the entire stack (Fig. 5b). Each single cell consists of a NiFe-MOF-S/NF anode, a commercial Raney Ni cathode, a commercial diaphragm, and auxiliary components, including porous transport layers, sealing rings, and bipolar plates (Fig. 5b, c, and Supplementary Fig. S65).

a Optical image for an industrial H2 production system. b Optical image for the large-area NiFe-MOF-S/NF electrode. c Schematic diagram of a kW-scale electrolyzer stack. d Polarization curves with no iR-compensation for the ASWE assembled with different catalysts at 80 °C. e A comparison of the electricity expense calculated from polarization curves for NiFe-MOF-S/NF. f Comparison of chamber voltages for 10 hours. g The corresponding H2 production cost based on an electricity price of $0.02 kWh−1. h Durability test for NiFe-MOF-S/NF-based ASWE at a current density of 0.5 A cm−2 (insets show the thermal infrared images of the operating kW-scale ASWE).

Polarization curves (without iR compensation) measured at 80 °C reveal that NiFe-MOF-S/NF-based ASWE exhibits markedly enhanced electrolysis activity in 30 wt% KOH + seawater, requiring only 1.84 V to achieve a current density of 0.5 A cm−2, compared to 1.94 V in 30 wt% KOH. In contrast, the commercial Raney Ni-based ASWE demands 2.05 V to achieve the same current density (Fig. 5d). At 0.5 A cm−2, the H2 production energy consumption for NiFe-MOF-S/NF decreases from 4.6 kWh Nm−3 H2 in 30 wt% KOH to 4.4 kWh Nm−3 H2 in 30 wt% KOH + seawater (Fig. 5e). CP tests further confirm that NiFe-MOF-S/NF surpasses both Ni foam and Raney Ni in terms of activity and durability in 30 wt% KOH + seawater (Fig. 5f). With electricity priced at $0.02 kWh−1, H2 production using the NiFe-MOF-S/NF-based ASWE is estimated at $1.83 kg−1, which is below the DOE’s 2026 target of $2.0 kg−1 (Fig. 5g)44–51. Furthermore, the kW-scale ASWE maintains highly stable operation for 1500 hours at a current density of 0.5 A cm−2 in 30 wt% KOH + seawater at 80 °C without noticeable degradation, with thermal images confirming a uniform temperature distribution (Fig. 5h). In addition, the overall system pressure was monitored throughout the 1500 h operation, which remained stable at approximately 0.1 MPa (Supplementary Fig. S66), indicating no significant increase in flow resistance occurred due to channel clogging. Moreover, no signs of flow channel clogging could be observed (Supplementary Fig. S67). SEM image of the post-test electrode shows that the catalyst retains its flake-like morphology after operation (Supplementary Fig. S68). The XRD patterns display only the characteristic diffraction peaks of the NF substrate, without other crystalline phases (Supplementary Fig. S69). This result confirms that the catalyst was reconstructed into an amorphous γ-NiFeOOH phase after long-term operation. The NiFe-MOF-S/NF-based system demonstrates high durability for seawater electrolysis under comparable industrial conditions9,23,44–47. These results validate the feasibility of industrial-scale seawater electrolysis using NiFe-MOF-S/NF, establishing its promise for efficient and durable industrial hydrogen production from seawater.

Discussion

In summary, this study demonstrates that trace amounts of Cl− can promote catalyst structural transformation to form dense γ-NiFeOOH, thereby triggering OPM pathway with high activity and durability. A NiFe-MOF-S/NF catalyst is rationally designed with an optimize loading of surface sulfides, which in situ generate SO42− to electrostatically repel most Cl− ions, mitigating corrosion while allowing traces of Cl− to induce the structural evolution towards highly active dense γ-NiFeOOH. NiFe-MOF-S/NF exhibits a low overpotential of 213 mV to achieve 500 mA cm−2 and maintains high stability for over 7000 h at 1.0 A cm−2 with a low degradation rate as low as 1.4 μV h−1 in alkaline seawater to meet the DOE’s 2026 durability target. Furthermore, the NiFe-MOF-S/NF anode can be operated stably for 1500 h in a 1.0 kW industrial ASWE at 0.5 A cm−2. These findings establish a new paradigm for harnessing traces of Cl− to regulate catalyst reconstruction and reaction pathways, advancing the practical realization of efficient and durable seawater electrolysis.

Methods

Chemical and materials

Ethanol (C2H6O, 99.9%), N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, C3H7NO, 99.9%), muriatic acid (HCl, 98%), nickel chloride hexahydrate (NiCl2·6H2O, 98%), ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O, 98%), thioacetamide (C2H5NS, 99%), terephthalic acid (C8H6O4, 99%), sulfur powder (S, 99.5%), potassium chloride (KCl, 99%), and potassium hydroxide (KOH, 99%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology. Ni foam (thickness: 1.0 mm) was purchased from Tianjin Annuohe New Energy Technology Co., Ltd. All chemicals were used directly without further purification. The natural seawater solution used for the tests was collected from Nantun Wharf in Qingdao, Shandong Province, China.

Synthesis of Ni-MOF-S/NF, Fe-MOF-S/NF, and NiFe-MOF-S/NF

NiFe-MOF/NF was synthesized via a solvothermal method. Briefly, commercial Ni foam was ultrasonically cleaned sequentially in ethanol, HCl, and distilled water. A precursor solution containing 25 mg of FeCl3·6H2O, 22 mg of NiCl2·6H2O, and 62 mg of terephthalic acid was dissolved in 16 ml of a mixed solvent of DMF, ethanol, and distilled water. After thorough stirring to ensure uniform dispersion of the solution, the solution was transferred into a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 120 °C for 12 h. After cooling to room temperature (25 °C), the obtained sample was rinsed repeatedly with distilled water and ethanol, followed by drying in a vacuum oven. NiFe-MOF/NF was obtained by calcination in a tube furnace at 300 °C for 2 h under a nitrogen atmosphere. Ni-MOF/NF and Fe-MOF/NF were synthesized by the same procedure except for the use of Fe and Ni precursors, respectively.

For sulfide deposition, NiFe-MOF/NF and 1.0 g of sulfur powder were placed in separate porcelain boats and heated in a tube furnace at 300 °C for 2 h under a nitrogen atmosphere, yielding NiFe-MOF-S/NF. Samples with varied sulfur loadings—NiFe-MOF-S-0.5/NF and NiFe-MOF-S-1.5/NF—were obtained with 0.5 and 1.5 g sulfur powder, respectively. Similarly, Ni-MOF-S/NF and Fe-MOF-S/NF were obtained under identical conditions as NiFe-MOF-S/NF except for the use of Ni-MOF/NF and Fe-MOF/NF as precursors, respectively. The loadings of MOF-S on NF are ~2.7, ~2.8, and ~3.1 mg cm−2 for Ni-MOF-S/NF, Fe-MOF-S/NF, and NiFe-MOF-S/NF, respectively.

For the large-area electrodes used in the 1.0 kW ASWE, NiFe-MOF-S/NF was synthesized using the same procedure as that employed for the laboratory-scale samples. Briefly, 1.0 g of FeCl3·6H2O, 0.88 g of NiCl2·6H2O, and 2.48 g of terephthalic acid were dissolved in 272 mL of a mixed solvent composed of DMF, ethanol, and distilled water. The solution was transferred into a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave containing a circular Ni foam substrate with a diameter of 10 cm and subsequently heated at 120 °C for 12 h. After cooling, the sample was thoroughly rinsed, dried, and subsequently calcined at 300 °C under N2 atmosphere for 2 h. Subsequent sulfide deposition was performed using sulfur powder at 300 °C for 2 h under an N2 atmosphere, yielding large-area NiFe-MOF-S/NF electrodes for the 1.0 kW ASWE system.

Synthesis of NiFe-MOF-S-HM/NF

The NiFe-MOF-S-HM/NF was synthesized via a hydrothermal sulfurization route. NiFe-MOF/NF and 115 mg of thioacetamide were dispersed into 40 mL of distilled water and stirred for 30 min. The mixture was transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 120 °C for 12 h. After cooling to room temperature, the sample was washed several times with distilled water and ethanol, then dried in a vacuum oven. NiFe-MOF-S-HM/NF shows a loading of ~2.9 mg cm−2.

Material characterizations

SEM images were obtained on a Hitachi Regulus 8100. TEM and HRTEM analyses were performed on a JEOL JEM-2100 F operated at 200 kV. XRD was recorded on a Rigaku D/MAX-IIIA diffractometer (40 kV, 30 mA) using Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 0.1543 nm). XPS measurements were conducted with a Mg Kα (300 W) X-ray source using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha. The metal contents of different samples were quantified by ICP-MS using Agilent 7700. FTIR spectra were collected using a Thermo Scientific iN10 spectrometer.

Operando electrochemical Raman measurements

In situ Raman spectroscopy test was performed on a confocal Raman microscope (Renishaw inVia) using a 532 nm excitation laser. The NiFe-MOF-S/NF electrode served as the working electrode, with a Pt counter electrode and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode. Potentiostatic experiments were carried out using a CHI 760E electrochemical workstation.

ATR-SEIRAS measurements

The ATR-SEIRAS measurements were conducted using an INVENIO-R FTIR (Bruker) spectrometer equipped with a liquid nitrogen-cooled HgCdTe (MCT) detector. The working electrode was prepared by pressing the NiFe-MOF-S/NF catalyst into a thin sheet and mounting it onto a single-crystal Si prism, which was then integrated into a custom-designed spectroelectrochemical cell connected to the ATR accessory. A Pt wire and an Ag/AgCl electrode served as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. The background spectrum was recorded at open-circuit potential, followed by the acquisition of spectra under a series of applied potentials. All spectra were presented in absorbance mode. To prevent oxidation of the Si substrate under strong alkaline conditions, 0.5 M KOH solutions prepared in pure water and seawater were used as electrolytes.

DEMS measurements

DEMS measurements were conducted using a Linglu QAS 100 DEMS. An optical image of the DEMS electrochemical cell is provided (Supplementary Fig. S70). A polytetrafluoroethylene membrane with a pore size of 0.22 μm was used in the DEMS electrochemical cell. NiFe-MOF-S/NF was used as the working electrode. Ag/AgCl electrode and a Pt wire were used as reference and counter electrode, respectively. Electrolyte flow was maintained at 30 mL min−1 using a self-propelled syringe. NiFe-MOF-S/NF was first labeled with 18O by CV for 50 cycles at 50 mV s−1 in 1 M KOH prepared with H218O, allowing isotopic exchange between 16O from the catalyst lattice and 18O from the electrolyte. The cell and electrodes were then thoroughly rinsed with H216O to remove residual 18O. LSV measurements were subsequently conducted at 50 mV s−1 in 1 M KOH prepared with either pure water (H216O) or seawater (H216O). The gas products were monitored by mass spectroscopy during the LSV tests.

XAFS measurements

XAFS measurements were conducted on RapidXAFS HE Ultra (Anhui Absorption Spectroscopy Analysis Instrument Co., Ltd.). X-rays were generated from a Mo target X-ray tube operating at 20 kV and 20 mA. A spherically bent Si(553) crystal analyzer with a curvature radius of 500 mm was employed as the monochromator. The absorption edge positions were calibrated against standard Ni and Fe foils. All XAFS measurements were carried out in a single time period.

XAFS data processing

The acquired XAFS data were processed with the Athena module within the IFEFFIT software package. After subtracting the post‑edge background from the total absorption, the data were normalized relative to the edge‑jump step to yield the EXAFS spectra. The χ(k) data converted to real space via Fourier transformation with a Hanning window (dk = 1.0 Å−1) to separate contributions from distinct coordination shells. Quantitative structural parameters were derived from least‑squares fitting using the ARTEMIS module.

Electrochemical measurements

The electrochemical measurements were measured in a three‑electrode configuration on a CHI 760E electrochemical workstation (Shanghai Chenhua Instrument Co., Ltd., China). A graphite rod and an Hg/HgO electrode served as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. The as-synthesized catalysts on NF served as the working electrode, with a geometric surface area of 1 × 0.5 cm2.

For seawater electrolysis, natural seawater was sourced from Nantun Wharf, located in Qingdao (Shandong Province, China). The electrolytes were formulated by mixing seawater with 1 M KOH (pH = 13.91 ± 0.04) or with 30 wt% KOH. Both solutions were centrifuged prior to electrochemical testing to remove any precipitated Mg(OH)2 and Ca(OH)2. For each measurement, a freshly prepared electrolyte was employed.

LSV measurements were performed at a scan rate of −5 mV s−1 on a CHI760E electrochemical workstation, with automatic 90% iR compensation applied. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy was conducted at 1.3 V. The frequency range was set from 0.01 to 100 kHz. The reference electrodes were standardized in a hydrogen‑saturated electrolyte, with a Pt sheet and a Pt mesh serving as the counter and working electrodes, respectively. CV measurements were conducted at 5 mV s−1. The thermodynamic potential was obtained by averaging the zero‑current crossover potentials from three independent CV scans. AEMWE measurements

For AEMWE construction, NiFe-MOF-S/NF (geometric area: 1 cm2) was employed as both anode and cathode, separated by an anion‑exchange membrane (1.2 × 1.2 cm2, thickness: 60μm) which was pretreated by soaking in 1 M KOH overnight and then rinsed with fresh 1 M KOH immediately before each electrochemical measurement. ASWE measurements

The assembled ASWE stack consisted of 13 cells with a total active anode area of 1020.5 cm2 (each cell: 78.5 cm2). Commercial Raney Ni and a commercial diaphragm were used as the cathode and separator, respectively. The seawater electrolyte (30 wt% KOH + seawater) was pretreated to remove Mg/Ca precipitates by centrifugation before being circulated at 80 °C. Prior to tests, a leak check was performed to ensure no gas leakage in the system. The system pressure was kept constant at 0.1 M Pa during tests. The cell voltage was recorded without iR compensation. Long-term testing was performed at 0.5 A cm−2.

The TEA model adopts the well‑established analytical framework reported in the literature48–51. The production costs were estimated based on a plant capacity of 1.0 tons per day over a 30‑year operational lifetime. Electrolyzer capital cost

The total electrolyzer cost was estimated by multiplying the reference unit price (450 $ kW−1) and reference current density (400 mA cm−2) by the power requirement and scaling to the target current density (500 mA cm−2)49,50.

The capital expenses were converted to daily costs using a capital recovery factor (CRF), which was calculated assuming a discount rate of 7% and a material service life of 20 years.

The total capital cost of the electrolyzer was then multiplied by the CRF, and the resulting value was divided by the number of operational days and the daily hydrogen output. All calculations assumed a plant capacity factor of 0.950,51.

Catalyst and membrane costs

Catalyst and membrane costs were set at 5% of the total electrolyzer cost50,51. The cost per tonne of H2 was calculated based on an assumed catalyst and membrane lifetime of 5 years.

Theoretical computation details

All density functional theory (DFT) calculations were conducted using the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package. The exchange‑correlation potential was described using the generalized gradient approximation with the spin‑polarized Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional. The electron/ion interactions were described using the additive plane‑wave method, with a plane‑wave cutoff energy of 400 eV. Structural relaxations were performed with convergence thresholds of 10−5 eV for total energy, 2 × 2 × 1 for the Gamma‑centered k‑point grid, and 0.02 eV Å−1 for maximum stress. To effectively eliminate interactions between adjacent surfaces, a vacuum layer thicker than 10 Å was applied in the slab model. The system energy and PDOS were computed using a single‑point energy convergence threshold of 10−6 eV and a Gamma‑centered 3 × 3 × 1 k‑point grid. The CIF files of the S-NiFeOOH and L-NiFeOOH models are provided in Supplementary Data 1. It should be noted that the DFT calculations are intended for qualitative mechanistic interpretation, owing to the inherent simplifications of the computational models.
