The immunity paradox: how do plants survive without adaptive immunity?
Authors: Peter Balint‐Kurti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYHigher plants and mammals exist in similar microbial environments and face similar challenges from microbial pathogens. Both taxonomic groups encode proteins capable of recognizing and triggering responses to pathogens; however, their recognitional capacities differ substantially. The mammalian innate immune system encodes several dozen receptors capable of recognizing a variety of pathogen‐associated molecular patterns and, in addition, has an adaptive immune system with a vast recognitional capacity based on the potential production of up to approximately 1016–1018 distinct antibodies through somatic recombination in specialized immune cells. By contrast, plants possess only innate immune receptors. The ability of fast‐evolving microbial pathogens to evade the recognitional capabilities of plant innate immune receptors has been clearly demonstrated. Under these circumstances, how do plants, especially species with lifespans of hundreds of years, successfully counteract their populations of rapidly evolving pathogens? Here I discuss the ways that plants navigate their relationship with the microbial world without an adaptive immune system. I suggest that the importance of plant innate immune receptors in resisting infection, though crucial, may often have been overemphasized in the literature. I propose a view of plant immunity which highlights the ability of the plant to suppress, contain and tolerate infections and ways in which this perspective might influence our future actions.

INTRODUCTION

All multicellular organisms host microorganisms, their microbiome, over much of their external and internal surfaces (Hammer et al., 2019). While many of the species that constitute any microbiome are non‐threatening or beneficial, others are potentially pathogenic (Berg et al., 2005; Brown et al., 2012; Indermaur & Westrick, 2026; Pfeilmeier et al., 2024). All branches of the tree of life have developed immune systems as defenses against microbial pathogens. While these systems are remarkably diverse, they can be very broadly divided into innate and adaptive systems. Innate immune systems rely on features explicitly encoded in the host genome, are largely invariant over the life of the host, and are inherited by their progeny. In contrast, adaptive immune systems ‘learn’ to recognize the pathogens upon initial encounter, create a ‘memory’ of them, and are then able to trigger a more rapid defense response upon subsequent contact. Adaptive immunity is generally not inherited but develops over the life of the host.

My goal here is to interrogate the possibility that we may sometimes interpret the plant immune system through paradigms informed by our knowledge of, and in reference to, our own mammalian immune systems. I argue that we sometimes overemphasize features of the plant immune system that bear some similarity to our own, while paying too little attention to features that are more unfamiliar. I suggest that we may ascribe too much importance to plant defense responses triggered by the recognition of specific pathogen‐derived molecules, or of their activities, while undervaluing other mechanisms that confer quantitative levels of resistance and tolerance. I discuss the possible implications of this recalibrating our view of this situation.

THE IMPORTANCE OF IMMUNITY

The fundamental importance of immune systems is perhaps most obvious in their absence. For example, human acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), which compromises the immune system, has caused more than 40 million deaths worldwide since the early 1980s, in most cases due to secondary infections by diseases otherwise rare in immunocompetent individuals (Blaser & Cohn, 1986; Payagala & Pozniak, 2024). Similarly, patients with the genetic condition ‘Severe Combined Immunodeficiency’ (SCID) have severely weakened immune systems and generally die within a year without treatment (Wadbudhe et al., 2023).

Plants that are immunocompromised, through either chemical or genetic means, can suffer infections that are not apparent on their immunocompetent counterparts. For example, maize mutants deficient in the defense hormone jasmonic acid are unable to grow to maturity in non‐sterile soil, succumbing as seedlings to infection by opportunistic pathogens (Yan et al., 2012). Likewise, Arabidopsis mutant lines lacking three defense hormone signaling systems displayed reduced survival in field soil, which correlated with the presence of an unidentified oomycete (Lebeis et al., 2015).

RECOGNITION‐BASED IMMUNITY IN PLANTS AND MAMMALS

Here, I am defining recognition‐based immunity (RBI hereafter) as immune responses triggered by immune system‐mediated recognition of specific pathogen‐derived molecules or of processes catalyzed by specific pathogen‐derived molecules. Detailed descriptions of both the mammalian and plant RBI systems are available (e.g. Jones & Dangl, 2006; Nicholson, 2016). I will not recapitulate these, but rather will, very briefly, compare some key elements of the RBI systems of each, discuss how and why they might differ and what we can infer from these differences. I should also point out that in this commentary I am not discussing anti‐viral RNA interference (RNAi) mechanisms possessed by both plants and mammals that are often considered a form of adaptive immunity (Sioud, 2007).

Mammalian RBI

For obvious reasons, human and mammalian immune systems have been the subject of much more extensive study than those of other branches of the tree of life. The existence of mammalian antibodies with specific recognitional properties was established in the late 19th century (Ehrlich, 1901; Valent et al., 2016) and the V(D)J somatic recombination system was first elucidated in the late 1970s (Hozumi & Tonegawa, 1976; Jung & Alt, 2004). The first plant ‘resistance genes’ were identified in the early 1990s (Bent et al., 1994; Johal & Briggs, 1992; Jones & Dangl, 2006; Martin et al., 1993), more‐or‐less contemporaneously with the elucidation of the mammalian recognition‐based innate immune system (Chen et al., 2025; Matzinger, 1994; Medzhitov, 2009).

Mammalian innate and adaptive immune systems function synergistically. Both systems use RBI. The mammalian adaptive immune system uses rounds of somatic recombination followed by somatic hypermutation (Neuberger & Milstein, 1995) in specialized immune B cells to produce extremely high levels of antibody diversity. The size of the effective human immune recognitional repertoire is a matter of some debate. Briney et al. (2019) suggested that the human immune system is capable of generating antibody diversity in the region of 1016–1018 distinct antibodies. However, since a much smaller number (maybe approximately 5 × 109) of antibody‐bearing B cells are in circulation at any one time in a healthy human, the number of different antibodies available for pathogen recognition is likely to be substantially lower than this ‘headline’ 1016–1018 number (Cohn, 2017).

This system includes processes for avoiding recognition of self‐molecules and for matching non‐self‐antigens with their cognate antibodies, which are then clonally multiplied and directed to the site of infection, giving it the ability to recognize essentially any protein. After clearing the infection, some cells producing these antibodies are retained, allowing the organism to ‘remember’ the antigen and to respond much more rapidly upon subsequent infections.

Mammalian genomes also encode a few dozen pattern recognition receptors (PRRs). PRRs recognize conserved molecules associated with microbial pathogens called pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), as well as molecules released by damaged or dying host cells called damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and, upon recognition, trigger an immune response (Chen et al., 2025; Li & Wu, 2021). In contrast to antibodies, PRRs are germline encoded and are therefore considered part of the innate immune system. While they are encoded in every cell, they tend to be expressed in specialized immune cells circulating in the blood and lymphatic systems and in epithelial cells (Hopkins & Sriskandan, 2005). Depending on their type, mammalian PRRs can be located either in the cell plasma membrane, intracellular membranes, in the cytoplasm, or extracellularly (Li & Wu, 2021).

Plant RBI

Plants do not have specialized immune cells, or a circulatory system that would allow their movement. Consequently, each plant cell must individually invest in its own defense, rather than, as is the case with mammals, ‘outsourcing’ this function to a specialized class of immune cells.

Plant RBI is often described as two distinct systems, each triggering a defense response. One, termed pattern triggered immunity (PTI), is initiated by recognition of extracellular PAMPs or DAMP at the cell surface. The other, termed effector triggered immunity (ETI), is initiated by recognition events within the cell (Hou et al., 2019; Tanaka & Heil, 2021). The PTI response is usually sufficient to confer resistance to pathogens not specifically adapted to the host (Newman et al., 2013) and is triggered when PAMPs interact with receptors which are also known as PRRs, the term being borrowed from mammalian immunity due to the functional similarities between the two systems, rather than any direct orthology or evolutionary relationships (Nürnberger & Brunner, 2002). Here I will differentiate mammalian and plant PRRs using the initialisms mPRR and pPRR.

While mPRRs are located both intracellularly and on the plasma membrane, pPRRs are generally defined as exclusively located on the plasma membrane (Zipfel, 2014). pPRRs are receptor‐like kinases (RLKs) or receptor‐like proteins (RLPs) and act with RLK coreceptors to transduce the signal into the cell after PAMP recognition. Interestingly, pPRRs and mPRRs recognize some similar PAMPs, most notably flagellin, though the specific recognition mechanisms for this molecule differ (Zipfel & Felix, 2005).

Plant pathogens that are adapted to a particular host are often able to suppress PTI via proteins, termed effectors, usually (though not always) acting within the host cell to facilitate the pathogenesis process, in some cases by modulating the PTI signal transduction pathway (Ceulemans et al., 2021; Toruño et al., 2016). Most resistance (R‐) proteins detect the presence of specific effectors inside the cell either directly or because of their effects on other molecules that are monitored (or ‘guarded’) by the R‐protein (Bent & Mackey, 2007; Chai et al., 2023). Most R‐proteins carry nucleotide‐binding and leucine‐rich repeat domains and are consequently known as NLR proteins (Chai et al., 2023). Detection of effectors by NLRs triggers an ETI defense response that often, though not always (Laflamme et al., 2020), includes a ‘hypersensitive response’ (HR), a rapid localized cell death response at the point of pathogen penetration (Balint‐Kurti, 2019). Since the effector repertoire of most microbial pathogens is quite variable across the population, resistance conferred by NLRs is generally race‐specific with respect to the pathogen (Sánchez‐Martín & Keller, 2021).

The distinctions often made between ETI and PTI and the types of molecules they recognize are somewhat ill‐defined in the literature (Thomma et al., 2011). Furthermore, ETI and PTI responses appear to be intimately associated and potentiate each other such that triggering of one system amplifies the response generated by triggering the other (Ngou et al., 2021; Yuan, Jiang, et al., 2021; Yuan, Ngou, et al., 2021). It may therefore be more helpful to think of them as two components of a single RBI system which are differentiated by where the pathogen‐derived molecule is recognized.

Mammalian versus plant RBI

Mammalian innate immunity based on mPRRs can arguably be seen as equivalent to the plant innate immune system, since both systems include cell membrane and intracellular receptors, recognizing both pathogen‐derived proteins (PAMPs and effectors) and DAMPs. Furthermore, plant NLRs and pPRRs and mammalian mPRRs use several common protein domains and motifs, such as leucine‐rich repeats, nucleotide‐binding domains, Toll‐like domains and kinase domains (Dardick & Ronald, 2006; Duxbury et al., 2021). It appears that, rather than evolving from common ancestors, the similarities between animal and plant innate immune receptors and the mechanisms through which they function are an example of convergent evolution, having evolved independently through the shuffling and combination of shared ancient protein motifs (Duxbury et al., 2021; Urbach & Ausubel, 2017).

While mammalian genomes encode a few dozen mPRRs, plant genomes often encode substantially more innate immune receptors. Recent surveys have reported that most plant species encode between about 50 and a 1000 immune receptors (though the functions of the large majority of these are inferred from sequence and have not been validated; Baggs et al., 2020; Ngou et al., 2022, 2024). Considerable variation in this number was observed, with several aquatic, predatory and parasitic plants encoding very few immune receptors, while some annuals, like bread wheat (Triticum aestivum), carry more than 1200 NLRs (Steuernagel et al., 2020). The reason for this relatively high number of NLRs is unclear but may be due to wheat's hexaploid nature. At the extreme, some species, especially longer‐lived perennial tree species, encode several thousand immune receptors.

In some cases, immune receptor loci carry multiple tandem repeats and appear to undergo increased meiotic and mitotic recombination (Hulbert & Bennetzen, 1991). Over evolutionary time this can create novel recognitional specificities and increase variability. The immune receptor variation encompassed in an entire natural plant population is of course considerably higher than is apparent in any individual, though no studies have yet determined how much higher it is (Sutherland et al., 2025).

So, while plants may have more innate immune receptors than mammals by perhaps one or even, in some cases, two orders of magnitude, this ‘advantage’ pales into insignificance compared with the mammalian capacity to produce distinct antibodies with many orders of magnitude higher variation. Clearly, mammals have a vastly superior ‘recognitional repertoire’ compared with plants.

THE IMMUNITY PARADOX

The microbial pathogens that challenge mammals and plants are largely drawn from the same groups: bacteria, fungi and viruses. In each case, these pathogens have much shorter life cycles and consequently much higher evolutionary potentials than their multicellular hosts. Furthermore, mechanisms of horizontal gene transfer are widely deployed, especially within the bacteria (Moura de Sousa et al., 2023). This means that any RBI mechanisms used by the metazoan hosts are liable to be overcome in a relatively short evolutionary period. Mammals and other vertebrates have confronted this challenge (albeit not entirely successfully—see below) through the adaptive immune system and its almost infinite capacity to recognize essentially any pathogen‐derived protein, together with a ‘memory’ that allows it to respond much more quickly to subsequent infections by the same or very similar pathogens. Plants face very similar disease challenges, but their capacity to recognize, respond to and remember pathogens is, by comparison, relatively minimal. How then do plants deal successfully with their microbial pathogens?

WHAT WE TALK ABOUT WHEN WE TALK ABOUT PLANT IMMUNITY

Many papers in the field of molecular plant pathology (including a number written by the author) contain statements resembling the following:‘Plant health depends on the sensing of and effective response to biotic stresses. Initial recognition is most frequently mediated by innate immune receptors’ (Snoeck et al., 2025). ‘The sophisticated innate immune system of plants prevents infection from diverse pathogens. Cell surface immune receptors, often termed pattern recognition receptors, perceive conserved microbe‐associated molecular patterns, pathogen‐associated molecular patterns, or host‐derived damage‐associated molecular patterns …. To counteract the perturbations caused by effectors and stop disease progression, plants have evolved intracellular nucleotide‐binding domain leucine‐rich repeat receptors to perceive pathogen effectors and subsequently initiate robust immune responses …. These molecular surveillance systems confer resistance to plant diseases, including those caused by many devastating pathogens on major crops’ (Duxbury et al., 2021). ‘Plants, unlike mammals, lack mobile defender cells and a somatic adaptive immune system. Instead, they rely on the innate immunity of each cell and on systemic signals emanating from infection sites’ (Jones & Dangl, 2006).

I will make the argument here that these types of statements, while largely correct, are perhaps misleading in characterizing plant RBI as the primary mechanism by which plants deal with disease, rather than portraying it as a crucial player in an ensemble of connected strategies for avoiding and ameliorating the worst effects of disease.

The limits of ETI

Given the appropriate selection pressures, pathogen recognition imparted by both NLRs and pPRRs can, in almost all cases, be evaded by an adapted pathogen. This is most clear in the case of NLRs, most of which confer high levels of race‐specific resistance to particular diseases (Chai et al., 2023). The fact that ETI mechanisms are generally race‐specific implies that they are liable to be overcome, since, by definition, there are races that are not detected. Many studies over the years have described the generally low durability of major resistance genes when deployed over large areas (Brown, 2015; Valent, 2025).

ETI is furthermore not an effective response against large groups of plant pathogens. Almost all known NLRs that confer disease resistance do so against biotrophic rather than necrotrophic pathogens, although there are a few exceptions (Staal et al., 2008; Zhu et al., 2017). This is likely connected to the fact that programmed cell death (HR) is often a part of the ETI response. Necrotrophic pathogens are often able to use these dead cells for sustenance and growth, actually improving their pathogenic potential (Govrin & Levine, 2000). There are also several examples of necrotrophic pathogens evolving to trigger ETI as part of their pathogenesis strategy (Lorang, 2018).

Surmounting PTI

While NLRs generally recognize pathogen‐ and race‐specific effectors, pPRRs often recognize more conserved microbial molecules, such as chitin (Yamada et al., 2017) and flagellin (Chinchilla et al., 2006), which are widely present in fungal and bacterial pathogens, respectively. This, combined with the fact that the PTI response often confers a partial rather than absolute level of resistance (Tuleski et al., 2020; Zipfel et al., 2004), and so exerts a weaker selection pressure, would lead to the expectation that these recognition mechanisms are less frequently overcome than those involving ETI. This may be true; however, less frequently is a relative term.

Here, I use the two most studied PAMPs, chitin and flagellin, as case studies: Fungi have several ways of preventing chitin perception by their hosts (Bakhat et al., 2023). They include production of effectors that modify plant chitinases or protect fungal cell walls so that chitin is not released from the cell wall (Naumann et al., 2009; van den Burg et al., 2006). Other effectors can modify fungal chitin or sequester chitin oligosaccharides so that they are unrecognizable by the pPRRs (de Jonge et al., 2010; Naqvi et al., 2016), and yet others can suppress signal transduction after chitin recognition (Irieda et al., 2019).

Similarly, bacteria have evolved a number of ways to evade the plant's flagellin detection system (Sanguankiattichai et al., 2022). Some bacteria express alternative ‘evading’ forms of flagellin which lack the epitope recognized by FLS2, the main pPRR that detects flagellin (Colaianni et al., 2021). Other bacteria mask the flagellin epitope from detection using flagellin binding proteins (Deng et al., 2019). Altering external pH to interfere with detection mechanisms (Yu et al., 2019), degradation of flagellin monomers by apoplastic effectors (Bardoel et al., 2011), and downregulation of flagellin expression in planta (Bao et al., 2020) are also effective ways of avoiding flagellin detection.

While the recognition of other PAMPs is less well‐studied, it seems likely that recognition can be avoided or suppressed in similar ways. In the evolutionary arms race between the plant RBI system and the evolutionary capacity of microbial pathogens, the cards are stacked against the host plant.

WHAT IS THE PURPOSE OF PLANT RBI?

Plant RBI can be readily evaded by rapidly‐evolving microbial pathogens, so why does the plant have an RBI system at all? Speculation on evolutionary outcomes is an exercise in informed guesswork and inevitably unverifiable. With these caveats in mind, I offer three hypotheses.

Immunity to non‐adapted plant pathogens

The evolution of processes for evasion of PTI and ETI‐based recognition by a pathogen requires strong selection pressure. Different host species and different genotypes within a host species carry different recognitional repertoires and thus present different selection pressures for the pathogen. Both theoretical modeling and real world experiments have suggested that microbial adaptation to one environment (or in this case host genotype) is often associated with fitness loss in other environments (Elena & Lenski, 2003). Plant non‐host resistance is often defined as ‘the ability of all genotypes of a plant species to confer resistance to all genotypes of a pathogen species’. Mechanistic causes of non‐host resistance are varied, but in many cases it is underpinned by the innate recognitional immune system (Panstruga & Moscou, 2020).

Reducing aggressiveness of adapted pathogens

The recognitional immune system has, in many cases, evolved to detect molecular features of microbial pathogenesis that are required by the pathogen for optimal infection and growth in the host. For example, FLS2, the pPRR that detects flagellin, recognizes flg22, a 22 amino acid motif in the flagellin molecule (Chinchilla et al., 2006). Flagellin molecules carrying altered versions of the flg22 epitope that evade recognition, often confer decreased motility (Colaianni et al., 2021; Parys et al., 2021). Another example (among many) is the AvrBs2 effector protein produced by Xanthomonas campestris pathovar vesicatoria, which encodes a xanthosan synthetase (Wang et al., 2025). AvrBS2 is important for bacterial nutrition but is recognized by the pepper R‐protein BS2 (Tai et al., 1999). In the absence of AvrBs2, the pathogen avoids host recognition conferred by Bs2; however its fitness on hosts without Bs2 is compromised (Kearney & Staskawicz, 1990).

Plant R‐proteins often recognize the action of microbial effectors upon host proteins rather than recognizing pathogen effectors directly (de Wit, 2002). This so‐called ‘guard’ function (Van Der Biezen & Jones, 1998) would seem to be a particularly effective strategy since it means that the pathogen cannot evade detection without losing the function of the effector. R‐proteins often guard the functions of specific key proteins required for the plant defense response. This ensures that the pathogen cannot simply deactivate the immune response by suppressing the function of a few key components (Mukhtar et al., 2011). Thus, pathogens often adapt to infect their specific hosts by ‘disarming’ themselves to some extent to avoid detection by the plant RBI system (Müller et al., 2026).

A failsafe way to engage the defense response

As well as PAMPs, pPRR receptors also detect host‐derived DAMPs. DAMP detection allows plant cells to initiate an immune response when endogenous molecules, usually located within the cell, appear in the apoplastic space due to cell damage (Tanaka & Heil, 2021). The disadvantage of using DAMP detection to initiate the immune response is that detection happens after cellular damage has already occurred and the pathogen is, to some extent, already established. The advantage, of course, is that DAMP detection is generally not something that the pathogen can evade or interfere with since it may occur some distance from the microbe itself. Thus DAMP detection can ensure that the plant ‘knows’ it is being infected even if the adapted pathogen is able to avoid the initial PTI and ETI detection systems (Ge et al., 2022).

WHY DO PLANTS LACK ADAPTIVE IMMUNITY?

There is of course no definitive answer to this question either, but it may be informative to speculate. Plants may lack the pre‐requisites for an adaptive immune system, the most obvious of these being a circulatory system. The mammalian blood and lymphatic circulatory systems are necessary for the initial matching of antibodies with cognate antigens (Knight & Stagg, 1993) and for the subsequent movement of lymphocytes and antibodies to the site of infection. While plants have transport systems, xylem and phloem, that allow the movement of water, sugar and nutrients from areas of high concentration to low concentration, they lack a true circulatory system that could support this function.

The vertebrate adaptive immune system is also believed to be very important for the identification and elimination of cancerous cells (Corthay, 2014). The lack of a circulatory system, together with the fact that plant cells are fixed within an extracellular matrix, means that plants, unlike vertebrates, do not develop metastatic cancers (Doonan & Sablowski, 2010) and therefore that this function of adaptive immunity is not required.

We are then left with the rather obvious answer that the absence of adaptive immunity in plants likely reflects evolutionary and architectural constraints and the sufficiency of alternative strategies under prevailing selection pressures. In other words, plants do not need an adaptive immune system.

THE ART OF SELF‐DEFENSE

Because of the profound differences in development and structure between plants and mammals, plants can use defensive strategies unavailable to mammals. The use of these strategies may explain, to some extent, the absence of adaptive immunity in plants.

Plastic fantastic

Mammals have a fixed body plan, established during embryogenesis, and exhibit determinate growth in which there is no significant further growth or development once the adult form is reached. Conversely, plants generally exhibit modular, plastic and often indeterminate growth, producing repeated semi‐autonomous ‘modules’ that allow for a flexible body plan that can change and grow throughout the lifespan, adjusting to suit the specific environment (Hallé, 1986). These modules can also be used for a form of clonal propagation. Additionally, as noted above, unlike mammalian cells, plant cells have outer cell walls and are generally fixed in position within a cell matrix dominated by the polysaccharides cellulose, hemi‐cellulose, and pectin, often also including lignin.

The fact that individual parts of the plant are dispensable allows mechanisms like HR to kill relatively large parts of the plant without severe effects. The pathogen can be contained in portions of the plant which can be walled off or can fall off without catastrophic consequences (Shigo, 1984; Tobias & Guest, 2014). Sections of the plant can be flooded with phytoalexins and other antibiotic chemicals (Jeandet et al., 2014). Cell walls and the surrounding extracellular matrix can be thickened and strengthened as part of the defense response to make it more difficult for the pathogen to grow in the plant and penetrate plant cells. In cases where the majority of the population has succumbed to a disease, resistant plants can rapidly repopulate an area using asexual reproduction processes, such as runners and tuber production.

Slowing things down

Quantitative or partial resistance describes the phenomenon whereby the pathogenesis process is reduced or slowed down, but not eliminated (Nelson et al., 2017). Over the last approximately 20 years, a number of the extremely varied mechanisms underlying plant quantitative resistance have been elucidated (Gou et al., 2023). These include (but are not limited to) modifications to immune signaling pathways, cell wall strengthening, hormonal signaling, metabolite transport, protein degradation, gene expression, as well as mechanisms based on RBI. Slowing down the progress of pathogenesis may be a particularly effective strategy in cases where plants have limited life spans or in cases where discrete parts of the plant can be discarded or isolated at the appropriate time.

Differential levels of quantitative resistance may be based on pre‐existing (‘pre‐formed’) differences between more resistant and more susceptible plants (Vergne et al., 2010) or they may be based on differential induction of defense responses after infection (Yang et al., 1997, 2017). In the latter case, one could argue that RBI must be involved since the plant must detect the pathogen in order to induce a defense response. It is indeed the case that plants can almost always recognize infection at some point during the pathogenesis process. The ultimate outcome of infection is often based upon the speed with which the pathogen is detected and a response initiated (Yang et al., 1997). Detection by plant pPRRs and NLRs of their cognate PAMPs and effectors usually leads to a rapid response. In the absence of this type of recognition, and as discussed above (‘A Failsafe Way to Engage the Defense Response’), cell damage causing the release of DAMPs and their subsequent detection may be an important pathway for the plant to initiate a defense response (Choi & Klessig, 2016). This response may be too slow to confer high levels of resistance but may, in some cases, act to slow down the infection process.

Mapping studies consistently identify dozens of loci associated with quantitative variation in plant disease resistance (e.g. Kump et al., 2011). The causal genes underlying these effects have been identified in a very small fraction of cases and, in the cases where they have been, they are generally at the largest effect loci. There are certainly therefore many other mechanisms, often quite subtle, involved in plant quantitative resistance that have yet to be discovered. Due to its dependence on multiple mechanisms, each of which would be expected to invoke a mild selection pressure, quantitative resistance is generally highly durable (Cowger & Brown, 2019).

Living with the enemy

Disease tolerance can be defined as the ability to maintain yield (or fitness) despite pathogen infection (Schafer, 1971). Several studies demonstrate genetic tolerance to plant diseases; that is, they show that different genotypes display differential fitness in the face of similar levels of infection or symptom levels (see table 1 in Pagán & García‐Arenal, 2018) and at least one tolerance gene has been identified (Tsuji et al., 1991). In fact, in any study where strong apparent levels of disease symptoms are accompanied by disproportionately modest yield losses, one could argue that tolerance is being demonstrated. For example, in a study in which relatively high levels of the maize disease southern leaf blight were developed using artificial inoculation, we observed average yield loss attributable to disease of only approximately 5% (Santa‐Cruz et al., 2014).

Theoretical studies have modeled the evolution of disease tolerance strategies in plants (Mauricio et al., 1997; Pagán & García‐Arenal, 2018). Roy and Kirchner (2000) predicted that as a gene conferring tolerance to a specific disease spreads in a plant population, the prevalence of the disease rises, leading in turn to increased selection for the tolerance gene and eventual fixation in the population. This reasoning would predict that plants are likely to use many tolerance strategies. Reported mechanisms that mediate tolerance include increased photosynthesis in healthy parts of the plant which compensates for reduced levels in infected areas (Inglese & Paul, 2006), diversion of resources toward reproduction in response to pathogen stress (Pagán et al., 2008), and modification of phytohormone balances (Li et al., 2017).

Plants also often harbor facultative pathogens—pathogens that colonize the plant without causing apparent symptoms and induce ‘disease’ (i.e., symptom development) only under specific circumstances often associated with stress. Indermaur and Westrick (2026) suggest that, with the exception of obligate biotrophic pathogens, many plant pathogenic organisms can be considered facultative pathogens. It is likely that the facultative pathogenic lifestyle developed from the evolutionary pressure to derive sustenance from the host without killing it, the so‐called ‘milker‐killer dilemma’ (van Baalen & Sabelis, 1995). Facultative pathogens may be able to maintain this delicate balance with the plant for long periods until environmental, developmental cues or abiotic or biotic stress cause a shift toward a more pathogenic growth phase (Indermaur & Westrick, 2026). This balance may allow both the host and pathogen to complete their life cycles.

IS IMMUNITY ACHIEVABLE?

The mammalian adaptive immune system, as discussed above, is formidable, with a vast recognitional capability. Nevertheless, as is obvious to any of us who regularly get sick, it is often evaded and overcome by microbial pathogens (Finlay & McFadden, 2006). Many of these pathogens use bespoke strategies to evade the immune system. For example, the Streptococcus pneumoniae bacterium surrounds itself with a polysaccharide capsule that interferes with recognition by the innate and adaptive immune systems (Ramos‐Sevillano et al., 2019). Salmonella typhimurium can regulate the PAMPs it presents when it is in the host environment, decreasing the induction of defense responses (Kawasaki et al., 2004). Many viruses like the influenza virus and HIV can rapidly change their surface antigens (Mosa, 2020), and the HIV virus also targets and debilitates the adaptive immune system itself.

It should also be borne in mind that invertebrates, like plants, lack an adaptive immune system, so this is clearly not a pre‐requisite for animal survival. Hedrick (2004) has argued that ‘in the endless host‐parasite coevolution, the immune system can never gain an upper hand on the millions of parasitic microbes and viruses’. He further suggests that evolution of the immune system has been driven more by the constant need to gain a selective advantage over others of the same species rather than the need to ‘prevent infection in an absolute sense’ and that evolution of more complex immune systems simply selects for microbial pathogens with increased capabilities of overcoming them.

If microbial pathogens inevitably hold the upper hand, why is it that multicellular life is able to continue to exist and thrive? One answer may be that, as discussed above in the case of facultative pathogens, both host and pathogen have an evolutionary incentive to reach a ‘compromise’ (Read, 1994) that allows the host to survive infection. A dead host that cannot make more host bodies is a selective disadvantage to both parties.

A HOLISTIC VIEW OF PLANT IMMUNITY

Therefore, it seems clear that neither plant RBI, nor any immune system, can provide permanent complete protection against a specialized microbial pathogen if the selection pressures to evade the immune system are sufficient, and that when we consider the ways that plants survive and reproduce in the presence of microbial pathogens, the role of plant RBI, crucial though it is, has often been overstated in the literature (see above ‘What We Talk About When We Talk About Plant Immunity’). It is likely that this overemphasis on RBI is influenced by our extensive (and very understandable) use of model systems in which major‐gene resistances to biotrophic pathogens have received disproportionate attention, combined with, perhaps, an unconscious reference to the mammalian immune system, with its much more complex and powerful adaptive immune system and a consequent greater reliance on RBI. It is also likely that, due to their more subtle effects, we do not always recognize the crucial importance of mechanisms that allow the plant to contain, slow down the progress of, and tolerate plant diseases.

While recognizing that each plant‐pathogen interaction has evolved under specific circumstances and is therefore unique, a general integrated resistance model can be proposed. In this model, the first and most effective layer is the physical denial of entry for the pathogen into the plant via the cuticle and epidermis, after which plant RBI could be considered akin to a border security screening system. It stops many of the obvious (poorly adapted) threats and often reduces the potential damage that can be caused by microbes that evade it, since they may have been forced to jettison important ‘tools of their trade’ to evade RBI. For those pathogens, the plant can deploy a wide variety of strategies of delay and containment. In other cases, the plant is able to tolerate the pathogen and complete its lifecycle despite infection (Figure 1).

Holistic view of plant disease resistance.

The temporal progress of potential microbial pathogens that are able to enter the plant tissue relative to the lifespan of the host is depicted as blue arrows with the thickness of the lines indicating the aggressiveness of the pathogens. The plant recognition‐based immunity (RBI) system confers resistance to most poorly adapted potential pathogens. Others can evade plant RBI, in some cases with reduced pathogenicity levels. Those that evade RBI are often impeded by other varied host features or mechanisms. Some pathogens that persist through plant reproduction/end of life are tolerated.

In the case of plants with a limited lifespan, such as annuals, a combination of these mechanisms is often sufficient to limit infection enough to allow the plant to complete its lifecycle or, with respect to crops, to suffer only moderate yield loss to most diseases. Plants with longer indefinite lifespans, such as trees which can live hundreds of years, face particularly acute challenges, since pathogens have millions of generations to adapt to an essentially static host RBI system. In these cases, it seems that the same strategies are employed, though perhaps in an enhanced way. Trees often have an expanded repertoire of thousands of immune receptors compared with the 50–200 receptors typical of annuals (Ngou et al., 2022; Plomion et al., 2018), and, as described above, they also display enhanced mechanisms for containing the pathogen and for shielding the metabolically active parts of the plant from pathogens (Shigo, 1984; Tobias & Guest, 2014).

There are of course numerous examples where plant immunity fails completely, leading to devastating famine or profound ecological consequences. Two prominent examples among many are the potato late blight epidemic that contributed to the Irish famine in the 19th century (Ristaino, 2026), and the Chestnut blight epidemic in the early 20th century that eliminated about 4 billion American chestnut trees on the east coast of the United States (Rigling & Prospero, 2018). These situations generally involve the introduction of a new pathogen or race of a pathogen to an environment; a sudden unpredictable event that disrupts the ‘evolutionary compromise’ (see above, ‘Is Immunity Achievable?’) reached between host and pathogen. Human activity is often a root cause of this unbalance. In these cases, the most appropriate and most commonly pursued response is to utilize resistance or tolerance mechanisms developed by host genotypes at the pathogen's place of origin (Fernandes et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2023).

A PLEA FOR TOLERANCE

One of our explicit goals as plant breeders and plant pathologists is the creation of varieties, through conventional or biotechnological means, with durable, high‐level disease resistance. Based on the perspective presented here, I propose that this goal is, in most cases, unrealistic except in the relatively short term. Furthermore, the intense selection pressure imposed by modern agricultural systems means that the ‘short term’ might indeed be very short. Is the pursuit of high levels of resistance therefore a Sisyphean exercise? Are there ways of more effectively targeting the development of varieties that are better able to tolerate disease?

New plant varieties are developed through modern biotechnological approaches or through more traditional plant breeding and, increasingly, through a combination of both (Glenn et al., 2017). In the more than three decades since the cloning of the first plant R‐genes (Johal & Briggs, 1992; Jones et al., 1994; Martin et al., 1993; Song et al., 1995; Whitham et al., 1996), the academic, government and non‐profit plant molecular pathology research agenda has been dominated by the elucidation of the plant RBI system. While this has led to a series of remarkable discoveries (Dodds et al., 2024; Jones et al., 2024) and an increasingly profound understanding of these systems, significant practical outputs in terms of commercially available disease‐resilient crop varieties based on the biotechnological application of these discoveries have yet to be realized. The possible reasons for this, as well as the basis for continued optimism in this area, have recently been discussed (Balint‐Kurti & Liu, 2025).

Most (though by no means all) of the reported transgenic, biotechnological approaches to imparting increased disease resistance in crops have been based on manipulating the plant RBI system or its downstream signaling mechanisms (Balint‐Kurti & Liu, 2025). Is it time for a reassessment of this approach? Is there an argument for rebalancing resources so that more research effort is devoted to understanding the mechanisms of quantitative resistance and disease tolerance? The value of any transgenically derived trait must be substantial in order to offset the substantial costs of developing and deregulating a transgenic variety. This has been cited as a reason that using biotechnological approaches to modify levels of quantitative resistance is not a feasible commercial proposition. It may be time to re‐examine this assumption. Our burgeoning ability to modify the genome in increasingly subtle ways and the improving regulatory environment (Molitorisová et al., 2024) may have the result that the development of such crops is less costly in the future. Financial models that emphasize the expected lifespan of biotechnological traits may also favor the utilization of quantitative resistance/tolerance mechanisms.

The traditional plant breeding process is largely agnostic of mechanisms; the best yielding varieties are generally selected using data from multi‐location trials. Nevertheless, there may be points during the breeding process where this alternative perspective might influence some decisions. For example, lack of disease symptoms, rather than the ability of a variety to yield well despite the presence of visible symptoms, that is, to tolerate the disease, may often be used as a criterion for the selection of lines to create breeding populations. Similarly, the selection process often includes disease nurseries for which ratings of symptom levels, rather than yield, are the primary trait data collected and used. Maybe this approach could be reconsidered?

The genetics governing disease tolerance in plants are extremely poorly understood. How much genetic variation for tolerance exists in breeding populations? How heritable is this trait? In crops like maize, which are grown as hybrids, how predictive is tolerance in inbred parents of the tolerance in the resulting hybrids? These are very difficult and costly questions to answer. Rating disease tolerance is complicated and time‐consuming. The yield effects of disease as well as symptom levels must be assessed using paired yield trials with and without infection and this must be replicated over seasons and environments. Due to limited funding and the relatively short‐term issues that constrain both academic and commercial research, establishing a research program on the genetics and mechanisms of plant disease tolerance is a daunting, and likely career‐ending, proposition. Given the potential importance of this trait, I suggest that consideration be given to the establishment of programs that provide long‐term support to research in this area.

CONCLUSIONS

Most of the ideas presented here have, as noted, been stated before. I hope that this recapitulation and reframing will nevertheless be helpful. I also offer the caveat that these thoughts are based on my understanding of the current state‐of‐the‐art in this field. New discoveries have upended the field several times during the last 30 years, and this will very likely be repeated over the coming decades. For example, the further elucidation of the role of the microbiome in mediating pathogenesis and the defense response may fundamentally change our conception of plant immunity (Pereira et al., 2023). Nevertheless, it is my hope that an adjustment of our viewpoint to one in which plant RBI is considered a necessary but insufficient mechanism for coping with microbial pathogens, along with perhaps a recognition of the limits of our current understanding, might eventually lead to more effective ways of producing disease‐resilient varieties.
