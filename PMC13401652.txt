A novel approach to the management of levodopa-induced dyskinesia using deep transcranial magnetic stimulation: A case report
Authors: In-Uk Song, Byungseok Kim, Yong An Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLevodopa-induced dyskinesia remains one of the most prevalent and disabling complications in patients with Parkinson's disease receiving long-term levodopa therapy. Although levodopa-induced dyskinesia can substantially impair the quality of life, therapeutic strategies are often limited to levodopa dose adjustment, amantadine, or deep brain stimulation. These treatment options may be ineffective, poorly tolerated, or declined by some patients. We applied low-frequency deep repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation over the supplementary motor area in two patients with Parkinson’s disease and clinically significant levodopa-induced dyskinesia who had limited conventional treatment options. Both patients completed a 2-week protocol without medication changes and demonstrated short-term reductions in dyskinesia severity, as assessed using the Unified Dyskinesia Rating Scale and motor-complication measures. No serious adverse events occurred. These observations suggest that supplementary motor area–targeted deep repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation is a feasible noninvasive neuromodulation approach for selected patients with levodopa-induced dyskinesia. However, this is a two-patient case report without sham stimulation or extended follow-up; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Larger controlled studies with objective outcome measures and longer follow-up are needed to determine the efficacy, durability, and optimal stimulation parameters.Keywords: Levodopa-induced dyskinesia, Parkinson's disease, deep transcranial magnetic stimulation, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, supplementary motor area

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the gradual deterioration of motor and non-motor functions. Despite advances in treatment, symptomatic management still largely relies on levodopa. Chronic levodopa therapy can lead to late motor complications, including motor fluctuations, wearing off, and levodopa-induced dyskinesia (LID). Among these, LID is one of the most common and disabling complications in patients receiving long-term levodopa therapy. 1 Although LID is known to worsen the quality of life, treatment options remain limited and commonly include levodopa dose reduction, amantadine, or deep brain stimulation (DBS). Therefore, additional therapeutic strategies are needed for patients whose dyskinesia remains disabling despite conventional management or who are ineligible for or decline invasive intervention. Although the exact pathophysiology of LID remains unclear, it is believed to involve an imbalance between excitatory and inhibitory inputs from the basal ganglia to the motor cortex. 2 This imbalance may lead to reduced inhibition of thalamocortical neurons and overactivation of the motor and premotor cortical areas. A previous neuroimaging study has demonstrated overactivation of the primary motor cortex and the supplementary motor area (SMA) in patients with PD and dyskinesia. 3

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) has potential as a therapeutic tool for movement disorders, including PD, because it can induce sustained changes in synaptic excitability within motor networks. In general, high-frequency stimulation (≥5 Hz) tends to facilitate cortical excitability, whereas low-frequency stimulation (≤1 Hz) tends to reduce it. 4 Therefore, the therapeutic effect of rTMS in PD depends on both stimulation frequency and target region. Considering previous evidence implicating SMA hyperactivity in dyskinesia, we hypothesized that low-frequency rTMS targeting the SMA could reduce medication-induced dyskinesia by modulating abnormal cortical excitability. 5

In this case report, we describe short-term clinical changes in dyskinesia in two patients with LID after low-frequency deep rTMS targeting the SMA. Unlike conventional figure-of-eight rTMS, deep rTMS may engage deeper cortical and adjacent network structures, which may be relevant when targeting SMA-related motor circuitry. Case report

The reporting of this study conforms to the Case Report (CARE) guidelines. 6 Written informed consent for treatment and separate written informed consent for publication of this case report were obtained from both patients. This case report was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Catholic Medical Center, the Catholic University of Korea, Seoul, Republic of Korea (Approval Number: OC25DISI0016; Approval Date: February 2025). All patient details have been deidentified; ages are reported as ranges, and dates are reported as month/year or as relative periods only. No figures containing patient identifiers are included.

Patient 1 was a man in his late 60s with a 12-year history of PD characterized by predominantly right-sided resting tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia. He had a 2-year history of peak-dose dyskinesia. His medical history was unremarkable except for diabetes. For the preceding 3 years, he had been taking levodopa (150 mg) and pramipexole (0.5 mg) three times daily, an additional dose of 100 mg levodopa early in the morning, and 50 mg opicapone at bedtime, without changes in medication. He had maintained a stable motor state until approximately 2 years previously, when involuntary movements developed predominantly in the face and neck about 2 h after medication intake. During the subsequent 6 months, these symptoms progressively worsened and significantly limited his ability to perform outdoor and leisure activities after medication intake. Under a clinical diagnosis of LID, levodopa dose reduction was recommended. However, the patient preferred to maintain his current medication regimen; therefore, low-frequency deep rTMS was selected as an alternative therapeutic strategy.

Patient 2 was a woman in her mid-70s with a 10-year history of PD characterized by bradykinesia, a left-sided resting tremor, and bilateral rigidity that was more pronounced on the left side. She had no significant medical history except diabetes. Approximately 4 years earlier, she had reported mild dyskinesia. During the year before the treatment, her dyskinesia had worsened considerably, markedly disrupting activities of daily living and causing major difficulty with ambulation. Until 2 years previously, she had been taking levodopa (125 mg) and entacapone (200 mg) four times daily, amantadine (50 mg) and ropinirole (2 mg) three times daily, and rasagiline (1 mg) in the morning. Because of severe dyskinesia, her medication regimen had been adjusted 1 year previously to levodopa (50 mg) and entacapone (200 mg) four times daily, amantadine (50 mg) and ropinirole (4 mg) three times daily, and rasagiline (1 mg) in the morning. These doses were maintained; however, dyskinesia progressively worsened despite medication, and even slight levodopa dose reduction caused marked akinesia that made ambulation impossible. Under a clinical diagnosis of LID, DBS was recommended because pharmacological adjustment was no longer effective. As the patient declined DBS, the medication regimen was maintained, and low-frequency deep rTMS was selected as an alternative therapeutic approach.

Motor function and dyskinesia were assessed before treatment and 2 weeks after completion of the procedure using the Hoehn and Yahr (HY) stage, Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS), and Unified Dyskinesia Rating Scale (UdysRS).7–9 The HY scale classifies PD severity, starting from stage 1, indicating unilateral involvement only, to stage 5, indicating wheelchair-bound or bedridden status. UPDRS Part III assesses clinician-rated motor signs, including tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia, with higher scores indicating greater motor impairment. UPDRS Part IV quantifies motor complications, including dyskinesia duration and disability. UdysRS Part 1 captures the patient-reported impact of on-dyskinesia on daily activities, and Part 3 provides clinician-rated objective assessment of dyskinesia severity and disability during examination; higher scores indicate greater impairment. Both patients were evaluated in a standardized ON-medication state, approximately 60–90 min after the morning levodopa dose, at both pretreatment and post-treatment time points. No changes in medication type, dose, or timing were made during the 2-week rTMS course for either patient.

Both patients received deep rTMS three times weekly for 2 weeks over the SMA. Deep rTMS was delivered using the H1-coil (BrainsWay Ltd; Jerusalem, Israel), which can stimulate cortical regions at depths of approximately 3–4 cm. SMA targeting was based on anatomical landmarks; the coil was positioned 2 cm anterior to the hand motor hotspot along the midline. The hand motor hotspot was identified over the left primary motor cortex using single-pulse transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) while monitoring motor evoked potentials from the right first dorsal interosseous muscle. Resting motor threshold (rMT) was defined as the minimum stimulus intensity producing motor evoked potentials of ≥50 μV in ≥5 of 10 consecutive trials. Both patients tolerated the full 2-week course of deep rTMS sessions well. No headaches, scalp discomfort, or neck pain were reported during or after stimulation. No transient worsening or paradoxical exacerbation of dyskinesia was observed during stimulation, and no seizures or serious adverse events occurred. Post-treatment evaluations showed reductions in dyskinesia severity as measured using the UdysRS and improvements in motor-complication scores as assessed using the UPDRS Part IV. In Patient 1, the HY stage remained 1, UPDRS Part III decreased from 6 to 5, UPDRS Part IV decreased from 6 to 2, UdysRS Part 1 decreased from 17 to 7, and UdysRS Part 3 decreased from 7 to 2. Functionally, he reported a marked reduction in the frequency and amplitude of involuntary movements and was able to resume short outdoor walks and hobby activities without significant interference due to dyskinesia. The greatest improvement was observed in face and neck dyskinesia.

In Patient 2, the HY stage remained 2.5, UPDRS Part III decreased from 18 to 16, UPDRS Part IV decreased from 12 to 2, UdysRS Part 1 decreased from 30 to 16, and UdysRS Part 3 decreased from 26 to 12. Before treatment, she had severe generalized dyskinesia affecting the face, neck, trunk, and all four limbs, which made independent ambulation extremely difficult and substantially interfered with dressing, hygiene, and eating tasks. After the 2-week course, she reported improved ability to perform dressing and hygiene tasks independently, and walking became less precarious. The modest changes in UPDRS Part III compared with the larger changes in the UdysRS and UPDRS Part IV suggest a more specific short-term anti-dyskinesia effect rather than a broad improvement in parkinsonian motor signs.

The patients’ perspectives were consistent with the scale-based findings. Both patients reported that dyskinesia reduction was meaningful in daily life because it reduced interference with walking and self-care activities. However, no follow-up assessment beyond the 2-week post-treatment evaluation was performed (Table 2). Therefore, the durability of the benefits remains unknown. Previous conventional rTMS studies targeting the SMA or primary motor cortex for LID have reported improvements that were generally transient, lasting from days to a few weeks, and repeated stimulation courses may be required to sustain the benefits. Future studies should include extended follow-up and evaluate optimal retreatment intervals. No artificial intelligence (AI) tools were used for data collection, analysis, or interpretation.

Discussion

This report describes the cases of two patients with PD and LID who showed short-term reductions in dyskinesia severity after 1-Hz deep rTMS targeting the SMA. The treatment was well tolerated and was not associated with serious adverse events. These observations support the hypothesis that SMA-related cortical excitability contributes to LID in selected patients with PD, although the uncontrolled design prevents us from drawing a causal inference.

The mechanisms underlying LID remain incompletely understood. Low-frequency rTMS is generally believed to reduce cortical excitability, likely through long-term depression-like effects at excitatory synapses. 10 Previous studies have shown that low-frequency rTMS over the SMA can attenuate LID in patients with PD, possibly by reducing abnormal cortical excitability.1,11 The SMA is a key node in the basal ganglia–thalamo–cortical loop, which is critically involved in voluntary movement control.1,12 Neuroimaging studies, including positron emission tomography, single-photon emission computed tomography, and functional magnetic resonance imaging, have shown altered activity in the SMA and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex in PD. 12 In LID, abnormal dopaminergic stimulation is believed to disrupt basal ganglia–thalamo–cortical network balance, resulting in cortical hyperexcitability involving the SMA.1,3,11 Low-frequency rTMS may help normalize this cortical hyperactivity and stabilize motor network function. 13 Several studies have reported that 1-Hz rTMS targeting the SMA reduces dyskinesia, consistent with the present observations.5,14 Other studies have also reported improvements in dyskinesia after the application of 1-Hz rTMS to the primary motor cortex.11,15

The present report differs from previous conventional rTMS studies because deep rTMS was applied using an H-coil. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case report to describe low-frequency deep rTMS over the SMA for the management of LID in PD. Compared with conventional figure-of-eight TMS, which is largely restricted to superficial cortical layers, deep rTMS using an H-coil can stimulate cortical regions at depths of approximately 3–4 cm and may engage the full thickness of the SMA, adjacent premotor regions, and related cortico–striato–thalamo–cortical projections. In the present report, SMA-targeted stimulation was guided by the left primary motor cortex hand hotspot. Although LID may manifest bilaterally, unilateral SMA stimulation may exert bilateral motor effects through interhemispheric and transcallosal connectivity. This possible mechanism should be tested in future studies using neurophysiological or imaging measures.

Evidence supporting the use of deep rTMS with H-coils for PD motor symptoms is growing. High-frequency deep rTMS targeting motor and prefrontal regions has demonstrated motor improvement in open-label and randomized studies.16,17 A comprehensive review by Hanlon et al. has concluded that deep TMS is generally safe in PD and may provide modest motor benefits, particularly when targeting motor and frontostriatal networks. 18 Mechanistically, these effects have been attributed to modulation of cortico–striato–thalamo–cortical circuits and interhemispheric motor network connectivity, which are also implicated in LID. However, previous deep TMS studies have not specifically targeted LID or systematically applied low-frequency inhibitory stimulation over the SMA. Therefore, the present report adds to the previous literature by describing the application of low-frequency deep rTMS over the SMA specifically for LID management. Whether deep rTMS provides greater or more durable dyskinesia reduction than conventional rTMS remains unknown. Future studies should incorporate cognitive and neuropsychiatric assessments given the proximity of stimulation to prefrontal and premotor networks. 19

This report has certain important limitations. First, the absence of sham stimulation means that specific neurophysiological effects cannot be distinguished from nonspecific effects, including placebo responses. Second, only two patients were included, limiting the generalizability of our results. Third, the intervention was given during 6 sessions over a 2-week period, and follow-up was limited to the immediate post-treatment evaluation; therefore, the durability of benefit is unknown. Fourth, the modest improvement in UPDRS Part III scores, in contrast to the larger changes in UdysRS and UPDRS Part IV, suggests a relatively specific anti-dyskinesia effect that requires confirmation. Fifth, objective kinematic measures of dyskinesia, such as accelerometry and inertial measurement units, were not used. Such measures would provide more sensitive and quantitative outcome data in future trials. Finally, optimal stimulation parameters, treatment frequency, total session number, and retreatment intervals remain to be established.

Conclusion

In these two patients with PD and LID, low-frequency deep rTMS over the SMA was well tolerated and was followed by short-term reductions in dyskinesia severity without medication changes. These observations are preliminary and do not establish efficacy. Controlled studies with larger samples, sham stimulation, objective dyskinesia measures, and longer follow-up are required before this approach can be recommended in routine clinical practice.
