Executive Summary
Recreational boats account for 99% of the vessels in the U.S. (approximately 12 million), are
signiﬁcant to the outdoor recreational economy, and support more than 800,000 jobs and
35,000 American businesses across the country [1]. About 9.9 – 10.8 million recreational
vessels use gasoline spark-ignition engines, which can achieve higher performance and
efficiency with higher-octane fuel. Currently, 0.04 million barrels per day of biofuels other
than ethanol, renewable diesel, and biodiesel are domestically produced, a fraction of
which is gasoline produced from biomass [2]. The recreational ﬂeet has a slow turnover rate,
making fuel additives for conventional gasoline more appealing, as they ensure
compatibility with existing marine engines and infrastructure. This study evaluates the
technical compatibility, affordability, energy and environmental performance, and scale-up
potential of bio-based gasoline components and renewable gasoline (RG) as high-
performance domestically produced fuel options from biomass that are drop-in compatible
with the existing ﬂeet and can support the conventional gasoline market through blend-
compatible supply, octane enhancement, and improved fuel for future recreational vessels,
while meeting the ASTM D4814-21c gasoline speciﬁcations [3]. Bio-based fuel components
and RG provide opportunities to use surplus biomass from the domestic supply chain to
produce high-performance fuels with a high octane (AKI) rating, while supporting the goal of
energy independence.

ES.1. Objective and scope: The report assesses the role of bio-based gasoline components
and RG produced from multiple feedstocks as blend-compatible fuels that can support the
conventional gasoline market, including through blending with conventional gasoline
blendstock for oxygenate blending (BOB). We consider all the fuel blends found in the
literature. The analysis compiles and normalizes published data to evaluate key fuel
properties— Anti-Knock Index (AKI), Reid Vapor Pressure (RVP), Final Boiling Point (FBP), T10
and T50 distillation points, density, Lower Heating Value (LHV), and water solubility—against
ASTM D4814 ranges relevant to marine fuels. Life-cycle criteria pollutants (NOx, SOx, PM2.5)
and carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e), energy efficiency, techno-economic performance,
technology readiness, and scale-up potential are also assessed using published models and
the literature.

ES.2. Methodology: Oxygenate types and concentrations in the U.S. are approved by the
Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) through its mechanism of the substantially similar
rule and waivers, along with restrictions on vehicle and equipment use [3, 4]. While EPA
regulations may specify the type and concentration of oxygenate, meeting fuel property
requirements is essential for a blended fuel to be compatible with existing infrastructure. The
methodology focuses on examining variations in properties identiﬁed across bio-based fuel

 9

components and RG, as well as the economic and environmental impacts of known
production pathways.

 • Literature data for blending components and BOBs were grouped by ASTM BOB
 octane and distillation class. Data is analyzed across a range of bio-component
 volume fractions.
 • Fuel properties were evaluated to determine whether blends remain within ASTM
 D4814 speciﬁcations for distillation, meet or exceed AKI ≥87 and MON ≥82
 thresholds commonly associated with market gasoline, and maintain density and
 LHV near conventional benchmarks.
 • Life cycle analysis (LCA) and minimum fuel selling price (MFSP), a techno-economic
 indicator, were compiled from the literature to compare RG pathways and bio-based
 fuel components on emissions, energy efficiency, and MFSP. Prices of blended fuels
 were projected using market-inferred gasoline BOBs and the MFSP for individual fuel
 components.
 • Technology readiness and scale-up considerations were synthesized using the
 Department of Energy’s Technology Readiness Level (TRL) guidance and U.S.
 feedstock availability assessments (Billion-Ton 2023), focusing on woody biomass,
 corn, corn stover, and municipal solid waste (MSW).

ES.3. Key ﬁndings: Fuel property and engine/infrastructure compatibility

 • Octane rating: Most bio-based blend components exhibit synergistic effects on the
 octane rating of fuel, measured by AKI, MON, and RON, as blend fraction increases.
 Several alcohols (e.g., ethanol, methanol, isopropanol, isobutanol) and selected
 hydrocarbons increase AKI, with many blends meeting the ≥87 AKI threshold
 stipulated by ASTM D4814-21c. High-AKI components, such as di-isobutylene, also
 raise blend AKI at elevated blend levels. Overall, the selection of a suitable BOB
 strongly inﬂuences the feasibility of achieving target octane thresholds.
 • Volatility and distillation (RVP, FBP, T10, T50): Increasing bio-blend fractions typically
 causes a marginal decrease in RVP relative to the BOB, supporting warm-weather
 marine use where lower RVP fuels help control evaporative emissions. Across the
 surveyed blends, FBP values generally fall between ASTM T90 and end-point limits for
 the corresponding volatility classes, indicating acceptable volatility behavior. T10
 and T50 results highlight the importance of matching blends to seasonal conditions;
 very low T10/T50 values can aid cold starts, while excessively low values can lead to
 hot-fuel handling issues in summer. Most blends in classes above the low-volatility

 10

 limit (identiﬁed as AAA) fall within ASTM T50 ranges, with isolated outliers that would
 require adjustment to avoid vapor-lock risks.
 • Density and energy content: Blend densities fall within the conventional gasoline
 range, with most components showing neutral to slightly synergistic effects as blend
 fraction increases. LHV for nearly all studied blends remains within ±10% of
 conventional gasoline, indicating minimal impact on vessel range and fueling
 intervals. Di-isobutylene can increase blend LHV relative to the assumed BOB
 benchmark, while very high fractions of some components (e.g., Dimate at 80 vol%)
 can depress LHV outside the desirable window.
 • Storage and water tolerance: Due to the higher water absorption potential of ethanol
 (1000 g/L), ethanol-blended gasoline is susceptible to water absorption issues during
 storage; studies show RVP can drop below acceptable summer limits within weeks,
 and phase separation can occur depending on exposure and tank conﬁguration.
 These behaviors underscore the need for careful management of ethanol-containing
 blends in seasonal marine layup and motivate evaluation of non-ethanol
 bio-components where appropriate. Concerns about water phase separation and
 storage stability remain important considerations for higher ethanol fractions in
 marine applications with current vessel and engine designs, though design changes
 can enable the use of higher alcohol blends while alleviating these concerns.

ES.4. Key ﬁndings: Environmental performance, energy efficiency, and economics

 • Techno-economics: Under favorable assumptions, several RG pathways achieve
 competitive MFSP relative to historical gasoline retail prices, with cost drivers
 including gasiﬁer conﬁguration, hydrogen source, and coproduct value (e.g.,
 electricity, Liqueﬁed Petroleum Gas). Wholesale price projections indicate that fuels
 with gasoline BOB and bio-based fuel components (particularly methanol, ethanol,
 butanol, propanol, and furans) are priced lower in high-AKI (93-97) performance fuel
 markets than the expected price of conventional gasoline at such AKI levels.
 • Energy efficiency: Cradle-to-gate energy efficiency varies by pathway, with corn
 fermentation to n-butanol, algae to RG via IH2, forest residue to RG via IH2, and MSW
 to RG via gasiﬁcation showing higher than 50% efficiency. These results highlight the
 importance of efficiency variability arising from underlying process assumptions and
 the choice of feedstock and technology. Such ﬁndings are essential for expanding
 industrial-scale fuel production by maximizing output through the availability of bio-
 based feedstocks.

 11

 • CO2e Emissions: Integrated Hydropyrolysis and Hydroconversion (IH2) pathways
 achieve low life-cycle CO2e intensities when combined with co-produced hydrogen.
 Municipal solid waste (MSW)-based routes offer a promising means of using waste
 and avoiding landﬁll disposal.
 • Criteria Air Pollutants: Criteria pollutant outcomes vary by feedstock and processing
 conﬁguration; additional evaluation is needed for NOx, SOx, and PM2.5 to resolve
 pathway differences and data gaps. Engine testing literature indicates that higher
 aromatic content can increase NOx formation due to a lower H/C ratio and higher
 ﬂame temperatures, while testing with higher ethanol blends (E15, E25) in selected
 road engines has shown substantial reductions in particulate matter (e.g., 40% and
 60%). Because emissions of recreational marine fuels often occur near population
 centers and along urban waterways, reductions in NOx and PM2.5 are particularly
 relevant for local air-quality beneﬁts in this sector.

ES.5. Key ﬁndings: Technological readiness and scalability

 • Feedstock availability and scale: Corn stover to ethanol and isobutanol, and corn to
 n-butanol pathways show the highest single fuel-product production potential at 9.9,
 7.8, and 8.6 billion GGE, respectively. Corn stover to RG shows production potential
 of up to 8.3 billion GGE. These scale-up projections, based on 2023 Billion-Ton
 feedstock availability, highlight production opportunities for the mature market in
 2040.
 • TRL and deployment: While sub-processes such as gasiﬁcation and syngas
 upgrading are mature, end-to-end RG production remains at varying TRLs and is not
 yet widely commercial for gasoline. Indirect liquefaction (IDL) woody-to-gasoline
 pathways integrate mature unit operations but are still in the research and
 development (R&D) stage for fully integrated, commercial-scale gasoline production.
 Pyrolysis, hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL), and fermentation are recognized as
 mature technologies for biofuel production; however, widespread RG production may
 not be feasible, as the latter requires additional reﬁning and distillation.

ES.6. Key ﬁndings: Implications for the U.S. recreational marine sector

 • Near-term opportunity: RG and bio-based blend components that meet ASTM D4814
 can be incorporated into the existing gasoline market for use in SI engines and fueling
 infrastructure, minimizing disruption to boat owners and marinas while supporting

 12

 the current fuel supply system. Fuel blends with differing properties can be used with
 modiﬁed engines after obtaining an EPA waiver for production and market.
 • Importance of BOB selection: Achieving target AKI and volatility behavior depends
 strongly on the gasoline BOB and the synergistic or antagonistic property of blended
 fuel; blends designed with high-quality BOBs more consistently meet performance
 thresholds.
 • Seasonal performance and storage: Warmer-weather marine operation beneﬁts from
 lower-T50 classes, while seasonal layup demands robust storage stability and water
 tolerance. Alcohol-containing blends (particularly methanol, ethanol, isopropanol,
 and n-propanol) require careful management to mitigate risks of water uptake,
 weathering, and phase separation.
 • Pathway beneﬁts: Corn stover, woody biomass, and MSW -based RG pathways show
 low CO2e emissions and can be competitive in cost with appropriate process design,
 coproduct strategies, and feedstock availability.

With appropriate formulation to satisfy ASTM D4814 volatility classes and other relevant
gasoline speciﬁcations, bio-based fuel components and RG can support the conventional
gasoline market through two complementary roles: (1) helping maintain current AKI and fuel-
property targets within the existing gasoline pool and (2) enabling elevated-AKI fuel
formulations for improved performance. Under the ﬁrst scenario, bio-based components
can be deployed to meet existing fuel property and performance requirements at a
competitive cost. Under the second scenario, selected bio-based fuel components with
high octane ratings may offer a cost-effective way to increase AKI compared with
conventional gasoline while preserving compatibility with existing marine infrastructure. In
both scenarios, the use of domestic biomass- and waste-derived feedstocks may enhance
supply-chain resilience and strengthen U.S. energy security. Given the sector’s scale,
proximity to population centers, and slow ﬂeet turnover, bio-components blended with
conventional gasoline represent a practical near- to medium-term strategy for advancing
fuel performance, compatibility, and energy security in the U.S. marine recreational boating
sector. Table ES.1 summarizes the properties of bio-based fuel components, relevant
production technology pathways, and associated environmental and economic

Introduction
The marine sector plays a crucial role in recreation, global transportation, and commerce,
contributing signiﬁcantly to economic activities and connectivity. Within this sector, the
recreational marine industry holds signiﬁcant economic importance, serving millions of
enthusiasts and supporting local economies through tourism, ﬁshing, and water-based
activities. Approximately 99% of all registered vessels in the U.S. are recreational vessels,
which equates to approximately 12 million vessels [5, 6]. The outdoor recreation industry in
the U.S. is worth approximately $640 billion, of which recreational boating contributes $230
billion in economic impact [7, 8]. About 85 million Americans partake in such recreational
activities annually [9]. According to the National Marine Manufacturers Association, the
recreational boating industry supports over 800,000 jobs and more than 36,000 American
businesses [10]. Boat manufacturing is an important sector for the U.S. economy, with 95%
of boats sold domestically being manufactured in the United States [9].

The recreational marine sector accounts for a signiﬁcant share of the gasoline market, with
vessels consuming approximately 1.5 billion gallons annually. These vessels, especially
those operating in inland waterways, rely on gasoline-powered spark-ignition (SI) outboard
engines. In fact, approximately 94.7% of recreational boats utilize gasoline SI engines, while
larger inland craft depend on diesel engines [11]. There is an opportunity to strengthen the
domestic gasoline supply mix and reduce reliance on foreign suppliers by incorporating
domestically produced, bio-based fuel components into the existing gasoline market.

Exploring additional domestic fuel options without compromising operational safety, engine
performance, or economic viability is of prime importance for the U.S. inland marine sector
[12-18]. Although battery-electric propulsion and hydrogen-based systems are gaining
attention for short-range marine operations, their technical maturity, infrastructure
requirements, and cost remain prohibitive for widespread near-term deployment. In
contrast, biofuels such as renewable gasoline (RG) can serve as infrastructure-compatible
options that complement and support the existing gasoline market.RG Fuels with properties
similar to conventional gasoline are important for the existing ﬂeet, as the annual
replacement rate for these vessels is only 1.7% [5]. RG meets the ASTM D4814 speciﬁcation
as petroleum gasoline, offering the same engine and infrastructure compatibility [14]. Fuel
components produced from biomass (e.g., ethanol, isobutanol) can be blended with
gasoline BOB, offering potential performance and cost advantages, and can typically be
used in existing SI engines, depending on engine certiﬁcation and environmental conditions.
RG fuels and bio-based gasoline components can increase energy security when used
alongside conventional gasoline by leveraging domestic feedstocks and supporting U.S. job
creation.

 16

However, the long-term integration of bio-based gasoline components and RG in the inland
marine sector will depend on its compatibility with infrastructure, economic, and
environmental metrics. The primary concern being their compatibility with the existing
recreational marine engines. Marine use of RG raises speciﬁc questions related to storage
stability during seasonal layups, water tolerance, volatility control, and material
compatibility. Ethanol blends, for instance, are viewed with skepticism in the recreational
marine industry due to the risks of water-phase separation and reduced storage stability
[23]. Addressing these challenges is critical to achieving optimal fuel properties for use in
existing infrastructures [19, 20].

In this context, the goal of this study is to assess the role of bio-based gasoline blend
components, blended fuel, and RG from multiple feedstocks. The primary objective is to
assess the opportunities and limitations of using these fuels in the U.S. recreational marine
sector. To achieve this objective, select fuel properties that assess fuel-blending
compatibility with existing marine engine technologies are evaluated via a literature survey
and stakeholder input. The properties that indicate compliance with ASTM D4814 are
investigated. They are (i) Anti-Knock Index (AKI), (ii) Reid Vapor Pressure (RVP), (iii) Final
Boiling Point (FBP), (iv) T10 and T50 distillation points, (v) Density, (vi) Lower Heating Value
(LHV), and (vii) water solubility. In addition, environmental, energy, and economic
performance is assessed via life cycle analysis (LCA), energy-efficiency estimation, and
technoeconomic analysis (TEA) for RG and bio-based fuel components that can be used in
blends.

Fuels were evaluated for cradle-to-gate CO2e emissions, which include upstream and on-
site combustion emissions. The life cycle analysis boundary includes emissions from
feedstock production and procurement, as well as upstream materials and energy inputs,
and the burden during conversion. In addition, the following criteria pollutants are assessed:
Nitrogen Oxides (NOx), Sulfur Dioxide (SOx), and PM2.5. Energy efficiency is estimated to
assess the energy-conversion potential of pathways (all hydrocarbons) using life-cycle
energy inputs. Finally, TEA assessment reports the minimum fuel selling price (MFSP) for the
pathways, identifying their minimum acceptable market price for production. Further, to
assess large-scale adaptation of the pathways, scale-up and technology readiness level
(TRL) assessments are conducted, informed by feedstock availability and examples of lab-,
pilot-, and commercial-scale implementations. Whenever possible, the properties are
compared with conventional petroleum-based gasoline speciﬁcations to assess
compatibility. This property-based evaluation ensures that performance, safety, and
regulatory compliance are maintained. Challenges, if there were any, were identiﬁed for all
metrics, and potential solutions were discussed. Though several past studies have
investigated the effects of blended gasoline on SI engines, we did not ﬁnd any that

 17

comprehensively examine the wide range of bio-components considered in this analysis,
with a focus on applicability in inland marine vessels; therefore, the present work ﬁlls this
gap. Overall, this analysis provides a comprehensive technical review of the potential to
incorporate RG and bio-based fuel components into the conventional gasoline market via
gasoline blending.

The goal of this research is to evaluate the technical feasibility of integrating RG and bio-
based fuel components into the inland marine gasoline market in ways that support existing
fuel use, engines, and infrastructure. What factors strengthen the case for RG and
renewable-blend components, and what additional steps need to be taken to overcome the
challenges? Through this lens, the research identiﬁes several technical factors that may
either enable or constrain large-scale integration of RG and bio-based fuel components
derived from multiple feedstocks.

Fuel Options and Production Pathways
Converting biomass into renewable gasoline or fuel components that can be blended with
conventional gasoline offers promising avenues to diversify and strengthen the conventional
gasoline supply. In this section, we highlight studies that identify fuel components of interest
for inland marine transportation and that assess biomass-based production pathways using
TEA and LCA. Table 1 summarizes the fuel components considered in the analysis, their
potential biomass feedstocks, and references to LCA and TEA studies.

The production of conventional gasoline currently available for internal combustion engines
(ICEs), including SI engines used in the U.S., typically includes a fraction of ethanol derived
from bio-based sources. A volume fraction of 10% or less is considered infrastructure-
compatible, i.e., suitable for current gasoline-based marine engines [21]. However, due to
the known issues of phase separation and increased water solubility, the storage stability of
ethanol-blended gasoline is often questioned in the marine sector. Hence, the marine sector
currently prefers 100% crude oil gasoline or with a minimum ethanol blend. An extensive
data-driven analysis was commissioned by the International Council of Marine Industry
Associations and performed by Ricardo consultancy on emissions and economic
implications of using biomass-based fuels and infrastructures for the different types of
vessels in the recreational marine industry – conventional gasoline ICE, E-gasoline,
biomass-derived fuel, hybrid electric, fully electric, hydrogen ICE, and fuel cell driven
vessels. The study identiﬁed biomass-based fuel options as among the most promising ways
to keep CO2e emissions lower and reduce cost increases over the life of the vessels [22]. The
study also highlights the importance of use-proﬁle (i.e., years of operation, hours of
operation, major maintenance schedules during the lifetime, etc.) for the different types of

 18

recreational vessels, for which biomass-derived fuels with compatible fuel properties and
high-performance characteristics (high-AKI) are identiﬁed as a prominent candidate for
increasing energy options for recreational boating and enhancing gasoline’s performance
and cost.

Although ethanol content >10% by volume may not be attractive for marine use, it is
important to highlight studies that used 15% (E15) and 25% (E25) bioethanol blends in
selected road-vehicle prototype and legacy-ﬂeet engines, assessing their performance and
impact on engine operation and emissions. The study by Takada et al. evaluates the
performance of E15 and E25, among other low-emission intensity fuels, in SI engines. The
study ﬁnds that fuels with higher Research Octane Number (RON) demonstrated higher
torque [23] while fuels with higher aromatic content have a lower Hydrogen/Carbon ratio,
which results in higher ﬂame temperature and higher NOx emissions [23]. The study did not
identify adverse effects on engine performance (power output and emissions) from the use
of E15 and E25 gasoline. The particulate matter pollutants in tailpipe emissions are seen to
be reduced by 40% and 60% for E15 and E25 fuels, respectively, compared to the base fuel
[23]. Such improvement in emissions proﬁle is also seen in light of minimum impact on fuel
economy, ranging within 1% difference for E15 and 2.5% for E25 [24].

Apart from ethanol, Gevo commercially produces several bio-based gasoline components:
i-Butanol, renewable naphtha, and Isooctane [25]. It further upgrades corn-based i-Butanol
to isocetane, which becomes the base blendstock for RG production. Such commercial
production pathways show promising potential to produce RG as an additional blend-
compatible option that complements ethanol and supports the broader gasoline market.
Isobutanol can be produced from biomass (cellulosic and lignocellulosic) via fermentation
(Table 1). Isooctane can be chemically reduced from Isobutylene [26]. Isobutylene can be
produced fermentatively from corn stover via ethanol and n-butanol intermediates. Hence,
production pathways for Diisobutylene (DIB) can also be used to produce isooctane.

Individual fuel components may have limited properties and are chemically dissimilar to
those of conventional gasoline. Hence, producing biomass-based RG yields a fuel that is
much more compatible with conventional SI engines and has properties similar to those of
conventional gasoline. Several researchers have investigated the environmental and
economic performance of biomass-based RG production. In the following paragraphs, the
researched literature and fuel properties are discussed in relation to the use case of marine
engines.

Co-produced bio-based fuel components (e.g., from gasiﬁcation of Methanol-to-Gasoline
(MTG), Fischer-Tropsch (FT) upgrading, or integrated bioreﬁneries) can serve as blendstocks.
However, meeting speciﬁcations may require varying blending to adjust volatility and

 19

aromatic fractions; compositional extremes (e.g., aromatic-rich streams) can raise Particle
Number (PN)/soot concerns and may require reforming or hydroprocessing [27-30].

The aqueous-phase hydrodeoxygenation (APHDO) process can convert carbohydrates into
high-octane, gasoline-range components (with an estimated RON of ~96.5). Though certain
limitations exist, including high vapor pressure and water solubility, necessitating careful
blending with conventional gasoline [31, 32]. Hydropyrolysis and ex-situ Hydro-
deoxygenation (HDO) processes can produce low-oxygen (<6 wt.%) reﬁnery-compatible
liquids [33]. Integrated Hydropyrolysis and Hydroconversion (IH2) yields renewable gasoline
and renewable diesel hydrocarbons. A pilot-scale work shows a 65–70% gasoline fraction
[34-38].

Plant oils can be converted to paraffins, a component found in conventional gasoline,
through oxidative cleavage and decarboxylation of fatty acids (e.g., oleic acid) to C7–C8
paraffins as high-octane blend components [39].

Lignocellulosic biomass can be converted to renewable gasoline through gasiﬁcation & MTG
processes. Paciﬁc Northwest National Laboratory’s (PNNL) commercial MTG assesses the
production process of direct vs. indirect gasiﬁcation [40]. National Laboratory of the Rockies
(NLR) future design case MTG with yield values of about 55 gal/U.S. ton [28]. The study also
highlights the need to blend with other gasoline components to meet fuel speciﬁcations
comparable to those of conventional gasoline.

An energy company, Topsoe, developed an improved Gasoline Synthesis (TIGAS) process,
demonstrating syngas-to-renewable gasoline production feasibility, containing aromatic-
rich gasoline fractions [27, 29]. Topsoe is a Denmark-based global energy company that
partners with other companies to enhance energy resilience by transforming renewable
resources into fuels and chemicals.

Ethyl tertiary butyl ether (ETBE) is produced by chemically reacting ethanol with isobutylene
in the presence of heat and a catalyst [41, 42]. ETBE can also be produced from ethanol and
natural gas [43, 44]. While both ethanol and isobutylene can be produced from biomass,
isobutylene is readily available from a petroleum reﬁnery. ETBE has been identiﬁed to be fully
fungible with the current gasoline distribution infrastructure, hence presents promise for
marine use [44].

Prenol has been identiﬁed to have gasoline-like properties and can be produced from
biomass. It can be produced biochemically from biomass sugars with aerobic fermentation
[45]. Among several U.S. DOE R&D efforts on co-optimization of fuels and engines, the co-
optima study identiﬁed Prenol as one of the top 10 contenders for bio-based fuel
components (bioblendstocks) for gasoline SI engines [26, 46].

In the U.S., Ethyl Tertiary Butyl Ether (ETBE) is primarily produced from Ethanol (43% by mass)
and Isobutylene (57% by mass) via etheriﬁcation. Ethanol is produced from corn via a
fermentation process, and Isobutylene is produced from a natural gas liquefaction process
[47]. Approximately 43% of the input feedstocks are bio-based. Theoretically, RNG can

Fuel Properties
Assessing fuel properties is essential to ensure compatibility with existing marine
infrastructure and engines, safeguarding performance and reliability. The U.S. gasoline
speciﬁcations relevant to marine supply are primarily governed by ASTM D4814, the
Standard Speciﬁcation for Automotive Spark-Ignition Engine Fuel [3]. Incompatibility can
lead to engine issues, unexpected tailpipe emissions composition, or equipment damage,
reducing infrastructure reliability. Table 2 lists the properties of bio-based fuel components.

As regulatory demands for cleaner fuels grow, precise formulations are critical for balancing
performance, environmental compliance, and infrastructure compatibility. While the
properties of individual fuel components may or may not be compatible with conventional
gasoline, when blended with gasoline BOB, they can interact synergistically or
antagonistically, inﬂuencing fuel properties. It is critical to assess how blending fuel
components with gasoline BOB affects the ﬁnal fuel's properties.

Bio-based fuel components, such as alcohols and ethers that increase oxygen content when
blended with gasoline BOB, are classiﬁed as oxygenates. As per ASTM D4814 – 21c, EPA's
substantially similar rule of 2008 identiﬁes gasoline-oxygenate blends given that fuel
contains up to 0.3% by volume methanol, and/or up to 2.75% by volume of methanol with
equal volume of n-butanol or alcohols of higher molecular weight [3, 4]. An overall limit of
2% by mass of oxygen content and 2.7% by mass of oxygen if containing alcohols and/or
ethers. In case bio-based fuel components and blended fuel don't meet the oxygenate
speciﬁcations, fuel producers can obtain a waiver from EPA, which can either be nationwide
or state-speciﬁc, either to address time-speciﬁc production limitations (e.g. due to a
reﬁnery’s reduction in throughput) or for a new blended fuel [58].

Octane Rating
The octane rating of fuel is governed by three interrelated fuel properties - Anti Knock Index
(AKI), Motor Octane Number (MON), and Research Octane Number (RON). AKI is a critical
consideration for gasoline blends and is calculated as the average of MON and RON. It
represents the fuel's ability to resist knocking or pinging during combustion, which occurs
when the air-fuel mixture ignites prematurely under high pressure and temperature. Fuels
with higher AKI values are better suited for high-performance engines, as they ensure
smoother combustion, optimize power output, and enhance engine longevity. MON
measures a fuel's ability to resist knocking under high-speed, high-temperature conditions,
complementing RON in determining AKI. As marine engines are often operated at maximum

 23

capacity, fuel’s MON characteristics are essential for gauging its compatibility with engines.
ASTM D4814 guides AKI and MON values for commercially available gasoline [3]. The
standard speciﬁes a minimum AKI of 87 and MON of 82 for commercially available gasoline
with an ethanol content up to 10% vol., with AKI values of 87 (regular), 89 (mid-grade), and
91 (premium). Table 2 lists the AKI values of bio-based fuel components.

Several studies have investigated the effects of blending fuel components with gasoline
BOBs. Christensen et al. (2011) investigated oxygenates such as ethanol, higher alcohols
(propanol, n-butanol, pentanols), levulinates, and furans, blended into three different
gasoline BOBs [19]. The scope included evaluating physical and chemical properties, such
as vapor pressure, distillation, density, octane rating, and water tolerance, against ASTM
speciﬁcations. The three base BOBs represent the expected variations in fuel properties
across seasons. The greater improvements in octane rating were observed for ethanol, 1-
pentanol, i-butanol, 2-butanol, and 2-propanol compared to 1-butanol, 3-methyl-1-butanol,
methyl levulinate, and MTHF [19].

Building on the interest in higher alcohols and ethers, a 2016 study speciﬁcally compared
binary blends of gasoline with isobutanol and ethyl tert-butyl ether, contrasting their effects
with ethanol [20]. The research examined properties relevant to engine operation, including
RVP, distillation curves, and density. A higher oxygenate fraction in ethanol (34.7%) than in
isobutanol (21.6%) and Ethyl Tertiary Butyl Ether (ETBE) (15.7%) limits its blending fraction
to gasoline BOB, to maintain optimal oxygen fraction in blended fuel; with the latter two
having higher potential in increasing the renewable component in gasoline [20]. An earlier
study in 1990 by Phillips Petroleum Co. identiﬁed ETBE as having higher potential in
improving octane rating compared to Ethanol [59]. In addition, drivability tests also identiﬁed
no adverse impact of using ETBE on engine conditions.

Through the U.S. DOE’s R&D efforts on identifying complementary fuels for transportation
purposes, a multi-group effort in 2019, the Co-Optimization of Fuels & Engines project aimed
to identify biomass-derived fuel components that, when blended into BOBs, would enable
higher efficiency in turbocharged spark-ignition (TSI) engines using a quantiﬁed efficiency
merit function [26]. To do this, the team developed a three-tiered screening process and
evaluated over 400 candidate molecules, mixtures, and reformates using small-volume
tests, property measurements, kinetic modeling, and blending experiments in
representative BOBs. The merit function explicitly linked key fuel properties – RON, octane
sensitivity (S), heat of vaporization (HoV), and particulate matter index (PMI) – to predicted
engine efficiency gains. Candidates were assessed as blends up to 30 vol% (and some
co-blended with 10% ethanol) and benchmarked against an E10 premium fuel. Ten top fuel
blends were identiﬁed with merit-function scores exceeding E10 premium, notably ethanol,

 24

methanol, isobutanol, isopropanol, n-propanol, prenol, cyclopentanone, di-isobutylene, a
mixed furan blend, and fusel alcohol mixtures. Synergistic blending of alcohol increases
RON and S, helping improve the merit function scores for blended fuels.

Properties related to the Distillation Curve
Volatility and distillation are key factors in gasoline’s compatibility in engine fuel systems and
its combustion. Reid Vapor Pressure (RVP) measures the volatility of fuel, or its tendency to
evaporate at a given temperature. RVP is essential for ensuring proper engine performance,
fuel efficiency, and environmental compliance. Higher RVP is expected in winter climates to
ensure reliable engines start at lower temperatures. Conversely, during the summer, lower
fuel RVP is preferred as it prevents excessive evaporation and avoids vapor lock in fuel
systems. Using high-RVP fuel in the summer can cause engine issues and increase volatile
organic compound (VOC) emissions.

Distillation is a property that depends on the fuel's vapor pressure. When the vapor pressure
approaches the external atmospheric pressure, the fuel vaporizes quickly. In general, the
distillation curve of a fuel shows the temperature evolution during vaporization from 0% to
100%, with intermediate points at 10% (T10), 50% (T50), and 90% (T90) vaporization by
volume. The temperature at which the fuel begins to vaporize is referred to as the Initial
Boiling Point (IBP), while the Final Boiling Point (FBP) is the temperature (in ºC) at which the
fuel is 100% vaporized. Therefore, fuels with higher RVP vaporize at lower temperatures,
whereas fuels with lower RVP vaporize at higher temperatures. T10 and T50 properties
indicate how readily the lightest and mid-grade fractions in fuel vaporize and affect cold
start, warm up, and drivability [60]. T10 inﬂuences cold-start vaporization; a lower T10
temperature can facilitate engine start during cold conditions. T50 affects fuel vaporization
during mid-temperature and transient conditions, such as acceleration from idle or light load
[61]. If a fuel's T50 differs signiﬁcantly from conventional gasoline, the engine's calibration
may not deliver the required fuel's vapor-liquid ratio during these transitions, potentially
causing temporary rich/lean operation and increased emissions [62].

ASTM D4814-21c identiﬁes seven vapor-pressure and distillation classes for the seasonal
adaptability of fuels. They are identiﬁed as AAA, AA, A, B, C, D, and E [3]. AAA and A, C, and
E classes; and are considered to represent lower and higher bounds among the categories.
These speciﬁcations are limits set by season and region and pertain to the distillation curve
parameters. Seasonal and regional volatility requirements are further regulated by the
Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), which imposes speciﬁc RVP limits that vary by
region and season [63, 64].

 25

Several studies have investigated the impact of blending fuel components with conventional
BOB on RVP and distillation points of the blended fuel. Christensen et al. found that
individual blends containing 10% (vol.) ethanol and <5% (vol.) 2-propanol increased vapor
pressure [19]. The study also developed a physically based equation-of-state model to
predict vapor pressure from molar fractions and compared the predictions with
experimental results. Blend behavior observed vapor pressure to decrease as oxygenates
were blended into gasoline at oxygenate mole fractions > 0.2 [19]. While ethanol signiﬁcantly
increases RVP, ETBE linearly increases RVP, and isobutanol causes only a slight increase up
to certain blend levels, after which it reduces RVP [20]. Such minimal impact of isobutanol
blending on RVP with gasoline BOB was also reported in the literature [65, 66]. While these
studies identify the impact of individually blending ethanol and ETBE to gasoline BOB, when
ETBE and ethanol are blended with gasoline while keeping the oxygen content of the blended
fuel at E10 estimate, it is found that increasing the ETBE fraction reduces the vapor pressure
of blended fuel, hence can help to reach vapor pressure compliance for blended fuel [67].
Such a reduction of vapor pressure with the addition of ETBE, relative to ethanol, has also
been identiﬁed as a beneﬁcial property for blended fuel stabilization and use [59].

It is noted that the collected gasoline BOBs are speciﬁcally produced for ethanol blending
and rely on the increase in RVP from ethanol blending. Hence, just adding isobutanol to the
considered fraction would make several studied gasoline BOBs not meet the EPA's RVP
requirements of 6.4 psi for reformulated gasoline [65, 66]. A gasoline BOB with a higher RVP,
when blended with isobutanol, may result in a blended fuel RVP within the requirements.
Further exploration into renewable blend components in 2020 investigated renewable ethers
like cyclic ketals and glycol ethers, derived from diols such as 2,3-butanediol [68]. It is also
noted that the addition of these compounds did not negatively affect gasoline volatility [68].
Butyl levulinate was found to increase the distillation temperature higher than the allowed
standard, which can adversely affect engine performance [19].

Density
The physical density of fuel governs its ﬂow characteristics within the fuel system of SI
engines. Fuels with signiﬁcantly different densities from conventional gasoline may ﬂow
differently through engine fuel systems, potentially making those fuel options incompatible
with current engine designs and requiring modiﬁcations. Table 2 shows that density varies
widely among the gasoline-blend fuel components, ranging from 0.69 g/cm3 for Dimate to
0.92 g/cm3 for 2-Methyl Furan. The densities of bio-blended fuels are inﬂuenced by the
properties of the underlying BOB and the bio-component.

 26

Christensen et al., 2011 found an increase in densities for the blended fuels with oxygenates
(ethanol, propanol, n-butanol, pentanols, levulinates, and furans); however, they remained
within gasoline's expected density [19]. An increase in the density of blended fuel is also
observed by Fotouh et al., with ethanol blended to gasoline BOB [69-71]. When di-
isobutylene and dimate are individually blended with gasoline BOB, a decrease in the density
of blended fuel is observed with increasing blend fraction [71].

Lower Heating Value
Energy density is often measured by the Lower Heating Value (LHV), deﬁned as the amount
of heat released when a fuel is completely combusted, excluding the heat of vaporization of
water. Among the fuel components studied, LHV varies from 20.1 MJ/kg for Methanol to 44.3
MJ/kg for Di-isobutylene (Table 2). If blended fuel has signiﬁcantly lower LHV, it can reduce
travel range and require a higher fuel ﬂow rate to SI combustion engines to achieve similar
throttle power output.

The LHV of blended gasoline is inﬂuenced by the LHVs of the underlying BOB and the
blended component, blended at a speciﬁc volume fraction. Geng et al. in 2010 studied
various methods of estimating the heating value of ethanol-blended gasoline fuel, which are
functions of physical density, highlighting that the heating value of blended fuel is not exactly
linear [72]. However, the results show an approximate linear relationship between lower
heating values and the ethanol vol. blend fraction. For simplicity across the various fuel
blend components in gasoline BOB, the current effort approximates the LHV of fuel blends
using linear interpolation.

Water Solubility
Water solubility inﬂuences storage stability of fuels and can vary widely, 0-1000 g/L across
fuel components (Table 2). Lower estimates are preferred for marine use. Hence, it is
imperative to assess the metric for RG and gasoline BOB blended with bio-components to
determine their compatibility with inland marine infrastructure. Water solubility of a fuel
blend (i.e., the maximum amount of dissolved water before phase separation occurs)
depends strongly on the component fractions in BOB. For gasoline blended with bio-based
fuel components, the individual characteristics of each component and its volume fraction
inﬂuence the observed properties of the ﬁnal blended fuel. Physical properties, such as
temperature, duration, and humidity, also inﬂuence the water solubility of blended fuel.

 27

Non-alcoholic oxygenates exhibited lower water absorption than alcoholic options, with
higher alcohols, especially propanol and n-butanol isomers, showing greater water
solubility, with a maximum observed for 1-butanol at 0.74% (wt.%) water in the gasoline
phase (Table 2). Ethyl levulinate and butyl levulinate, when blended with gasoline, are found
to have no solubility issues at lower temperatures up to -40 °C, at which the latter also
behaves as a single-phase liquid [19].

A 2016 study examined moisture uptake from humid air into gasoline-ethanol blends [73].
Gasoline with 10% ethanol (E10) absorbs water from humid air much faster than ethanol-
free gasoline (E0). In a capped test-tube experiment with a small oriﬁce, E10 water uptake
was about 81 ppm/day at high headspace volume (HV) and 109 ppm/day at low headspace
volume (LV) with six weeks of exposure; and in small engine tanks, uptake was about 52–57
ppm/day with three months of exposure. The study also notes that, due to the evaporation of
volatile compounds, E10 gasoline becomes unﬁt for use well before phase separation is
observed, as other properties related to distillation temperature, sulfur content, etc., are
adversely affected. For conventional 100% fossil gasoline, water uptake estimates range
from 6.0 to 6.3 ppm/day for capped test-tube experiments and from 0.4 to 0.6 ppm/day for
small-engine tank experiments. Here, observations from three months of exposure are
particularly important, as this timeframe may be the minimum duration for which
recreational boats are stored inactive over a year. Further, the study observed E10
undergoing phase separation in a test tube experimental setting at around 8 weeks and in a
speciﬁc fuel tank at around 12 months. Due to evaporation of light components, the
estimated RVP dropped below the acceptable summer limit (5.5 psi) within 6-8 weeks [73].
Further research is required to assess the effects of other bio-based alcohols and non-
alcohol components on the water solubility of blended fuels.

Methodology of Evaluating Fuel Properties
In this report, the following properties were investigated: AKI, MON, RVP, FBP, T10, T50,
Density, LHV, and water solubility. In addition, selected pathways producing RG and bio-
based fuel components are evaluated for life-cycle CO2 impacts, life-cycle criteria
pollutants (SOx, NOx, PM2.5), life-cycle energy efficiency, and MFSP. To produce
infrastructure-compatible fuels, studies have blended bio-based fuel components in
different proportions with fossil-based gasoline, named as Blendstocks of Oxygenated
Blending (BOB). As the properties of the ﬁnal blended fuel differ from those of either
component, it is crucial to assess the impact of the blending components on fuel properties.
The assessment was performed by collecting data from several relevant journal articles and
published reports on RG production from biomass and bio-based fuel components blended
with gasoline BOBs. After careful arbitration, a systematic approach, as described next, was
identiﬁed for tabulating the properties of fuels derived from gasoline BOBs and bio-based
fuel components.

Relevant studies are those that provide the AKI for the conventional gasoline BOB and the
blended fuel. Knowing the AKI of BOB and that of the blended fuel is required to determine
the blending level needed for different BOBs to be suitable for use in conventional gasoline
engines. Thus, if both RON and MON values were provided, the resulting AKI is calculated
from them based on the relationship, 𝐴𝐴𝐴𝐴𝐴𝐴 = (𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅 + 𝑀𝑀𝑀𝑀𝑀𝑀)/2. Further, the data for RVP,
FBP, and Density were tabulated for each BOB and the corresponding blends. This paired
tabulation of properties for the blends and their respective BOBs helped assess suitable
blend fractions for the different bio-components to be used in gasoline engines.

Anti Knock Index (AKI)
AKI values of fuels blended with bio-components are grouped by their gasoline BOB’s AKI
value. Interactions among the blend components, their volume fractions, and the gasoline
BOB composition determine the AKI of the resulting blended fuel. Figure 1.a. shows the
resultant AKI values of blended fuel for AKI groups E10 AKI <80, E10 AKI 85-87, and E10 AKI
87-89. Figure 1.b. shows AKI values for AKI groups E10 AKI 89-91 and E10 AKI 91-92. It is
noted that the E10 AKI 80-83 and E10 AKI 83-85 groups lack data and are therefore not
included in the plotted groups. The box plots show the blended fuel across different BOBs.
AKI values of blend fuels that were <80 are omitted from this chart. Blend fuels with AKI >87
are considered compatible with conventional gasoline infrastructure (with respect to AKI
compatibility), whereas those with AKI <87 will require further adjustments.

It is observed that all studied blends, except 1-Butanol, exhibit a synergistic effect on the
blended fuel AKI, i.e., increasing the AKI of the blend fuel as the volume fraction of the
blended component increases. 1-Butanol exhibits a synergistic effect with BOBs of AKI 87–
89 (Figure 1a), but an antagonistic effect for BOBs with AKI 91–92 (Figure 1b). This is highly
dependent on the AKI of the BOB and 1-Butanol. Since 1-Butanol has an AKI close to 91,
blending it with BOB, which has an AKI of 87, will result in a higher-AKI fuel. However, when
blended with a BOB of AKI 92, the resulting fuel will have a lower AKI. This will continue to
increase or decrease depending on the level of 1-Butanol blended into the BOB, which alters
the chemical properties of the ﬁnal fuel blend and affects its combustion characteristics
and knock resistance.

Further, blends with AKI <87 were mostly based on a BOB with AKI <87. If those fuel
components are blended with BOBs of higher AKI, they fall within the qualifying range; for
example, i-Butanol at 16-30% blending. On the other hand, Di-isobutylene (AKI 96.5), when
blended at a very high fraction of 80% with low-AKI BOB, results in qualifying blends. Di-
isobutylene is found to have a synergistic effect on AKI, as shown in Figure SI1, when
considering all data points, including those with AKI <80. In addition, components such as i-
Propanol (AKI 105.0) and Ethanol (AKI 99.5) signiﬁcantly increase the AKI of blend fuels.
When Prenol has a higher RON (93.5) than gasoline BOB (84.8) and has a lower MON (74.2)
than gasoline BOB (82.5), a synergistic increase in blended fuel’s AKI is observed and
reaches AKI 89-91 when blended at 30% vol., with gasoline BOB of AKI 87-89. Such
improvement in the antiknock performance of blended fuel indicates that Prenol is a
promising bio-based fuel component for blending with gasoline BOB.

Among the fuel options investigated, those satisfying ASTM D4814’s AKI requirement are
also found to satisfy MON requirements. A similar analysis of MON for blended fuels is
provided in the SI.

Final Boiling Point (FBP)
As FBP property is relative to fuel’s vapor and distillation class properties, here the grouping
by distillation classes is preserved, as it is done for the RVP data set. From Figure 3, it is found
that all blends have FBP between T90 (190 C) and end point (225 C) values as speciﬁed in
the ASTM standard, i.e., the qualifying range. In case of a BOB with FBP lower than AAA (BOB
Class < AAA), the FBP is closer to the ASTM-speciﬁed endpoint limit of 225 Celsius. However,
when a BOB of class C–E is blended with a bio-component with a high RVP, the FBP is very
close to the T90 limit. As per the ASTM standard, the T90 limit for class AAA fuel is 190
Celsius, while for class C it is 185 Celsius. This reduced T90 limit for class C is due to its
higher characteristic RVP, which facilitates its vaporization or boiling at much lower
temperatures. Therefore, compared to the speciﬁc T90 limit of 185 Celsius for class C fuels,
its FBP is well above the limit. In fact, the FBP of class C fuels is as much higher than its T90
limit as the FBP of class AAA is lower than the end point. Thus, it can be said that all the fuel
blends are within the acceptable range for use in gasoline engines as their FBP lies between
T90 and end point limits, mentioned in the ASTM standard.

T10 and T50 Distillation points
In addition to FBP, fuel characteristics such as T10 (temperature at which 10% vol. of fuel
evaporates) and T50 (temperature at which 50% vol. of fuel evaporates) are essential for
evaluating use-speciﬁc compatibility. These properties indicate how readily the lightest and
mid-grade fractions in fuel vaporize and affect cold start, warm-up, and drivability [60]. T10
inﬂuences cold-start vaporization; a lower T10 can facilitate it. T50 affects fuel vaporization
during mid-temperature and transient conditions, such as acceleration from idle or light load
[61]. If a fuel’s T50 differs signiﬁcantly from conventional gasoline, the engine’s calibration
may not deliver the required fuel’s vapor-liquid ratio during these transitions, potentially
causing temporary rich/lean operation and increased emissions [62].

Data from the surveyed literature were collected for T10 and T50 distillation points and
grouped by vapor pressure and distillation class. Figures 4 and 5 show the plotted data for
T10 and T50, respectively. The minimum, midpoint, and maximum values from the ASTM
D4814 standard for T10 and T50 were added for reference. In Figure 5, it was observed that
for BOB fuel blends at lower vapor pressure than AAA (i.e., BOB Class < AAA in the ﬁgure),
T10 estimates were higher, generally falling between 60 °C (class AAA) and 70 °C (class C).
These fuel mixes are therefore better suited for summer temperatures, helping minimize
vaporization losses during engine start. For the BOB vapor pressure class, BOB Class A - C
has a higher T10 estimate than BOB Class C - E, indicating the latter is more suitable for
colder temperatures.

From Figure 5, it is observed that fuel blends belonging to the BOB vapor class greater than
AAA have T50 values between 77 °C and 121 °C (minimum and maximum, respectively,
speciﬁed in the ASTM standard). More speciﬁcally, most blends fall below 110 °C (the ASTM
standard for class E). Thus, the T50 values of the fuel blends fall within the suitable range for
operation in gasoline engines. Data points in the other two classes are higher than A (up to
C), and those higher than C mostly have T50 values above the minimum of 77 °C (for class
AAA). However, in some cases, it also has lower values than 77 °C, making those blends
unsuitable for engine operation due to hot fuel handling problems caused by over-
vaporization and vapor lock in hot weather [62].

Based on observations from the data, it can be concluded that the characteristic T10 and
T50 estimates of the blended fuel depend on the underlying BOB and biofuel components

climates, fuels belonging to lower vapor pressure and distillation class may be more

Water Solubility
Documented understanding of the water-solubility characteristics of ethanol-blend fuel is
available. While an ethanol fraction >10% is not advisable for use with current marine
engines, it is important to understand how water solubility impacts the ethanol fraction at
10% vol.

A 2016 study assessed moisture uptake from humid air into gasoline-ethanol blends [73].
Gasoline with 10% ethanol (E10) absorbs water from humid air much faster than ethanol-
free gasoline (E0). In a capped with a small oriﬁce test-tube experiment, E10 water uptake
was about 81 ppm/day at high headspace volume (HV) and 109 ppm/day at low headspace
volume (LV) with six weeks of exposure; and in small engine tanks, uptake was about 52–57
ppm/day with three months of exposure. The study also notes that, due to the evaporation of
volatile compounds, E10 gasoline becomes unﬁt for use well before phase separation is
observed, as other properties related to distillation temperatures, including sulfur content,
are adversely affected. For conventional 100% fossil gasoline, water uptake estimates range
between 6.0-6.3 ppm/day for capped test-tube experiments and 0.4-0.6 ppm/day for small
engine tank experiments. Here, observations from three months of exposure are particularly
important, as this timeframe may be the minimum duration for which recreational boats are
stored inactive over the course of a year. Further, the study observed E10 undergoing phase
separation in a test tube experimental setting at around ﬁve weeks and in a speciﬁc fuel tank
at around three months. Due to evaporation of light components, the estimated RVP
dropped below the acceptable summer limit (5.5 psi) within 6-8 weeks.

Higher water-soluble fuels may be made infrastructure-compatible through a modiﬁed
engine design. With such engineering advancement, fuel producers can propose EPA
waivers for certain fuel blends speciﬁcally tuned for modiﬁed engines. Further research is
required to assess the effects of other bio-based alcohols and non-alcohol components on
the water solubility of blended fuels.

Life Cycle and Technoeconomic Characteristics

Life Cycle Analysis
For inland marine applications, LCA is essential for quantifying the environmental impacts
of bio-based fuel components and production pathways. The system boundary accounts for
emissions from upstream feedstock production and supply, as well as from materials and
energy used during the conversion process and logistics. Robust LCA enables the screening
of production options and the assessment of the environmental performance and
operational realities (fuel energy density, blending, and distribution) of emerging fuels,
ensuring that upstream burdens do not offset apparent tailpipe beneﬁts. It also guides
investment by pinpointing hotspots—such as the choice of process fuel or hydrogen
sourcing—that are most inﬂuential for marine-scale deployment. In addition, the life-cycle
perspective is used to calculate the energy efficiency of production pathways.

A literature search was conducted to identify publications examining the life-cycle
emissions of RG and bio-based fuel component production pathways, measured in terms of
CO2e emissions (Table 1). Among the identiﬁed studies, no comprehensive LCA studies were
found for the bio-based fuel components: Pentanol, Dimate, and ETBE, highlighting gaps in
the LCA literature. While several of these identiﬁed studies have been conducted in R&D
GREET® (Greenhouse gases, Regulated Emissions, and Energy use in Technologies) for
pathways based on catalytic cracking of seed oils, those are the ones that estimate SOx,
NOx, and PM2.5 emissions [51, 74, 75]. Hence, unlike for CO2e estimates, only a limited
number of data points were available for the fuel-production pathways of SOx, NOx, and
PM2.5 emissions.

Figures 8-11 summarize the cradle-to-gate CO2e and criteria pollutant emissions for a
functional unit of one MJ of energy product, with emissions measured in mass units (grams
for CO2e, NOx, PM2.5; milligrams for SOx). Variations incorporated in conversion

Carbon Dioxide Equivalent (CO2e)
CO2e emissions of the surveyed conversion pathways of biomass to RG and bio-based fuel
components are shown in Figure 8. The cradle-to-gate CO2e estimates range from -12.4
gCO2e/MJ for MSW to RG via gasiﬁcation to 72 gCO2e/MJ for corn stover to DIB via
gasiﬁcation. Using MSW for fuel production not only diverts waste from landﬁlls but also
helps reduce CO2e emissions by avoiding landﬁll emissions from MSW disposal. Hydrogen
used in the hydrocarbon upgrading stage of the IH2 conversion process determines the
pathway’s CO2e performance. For corn stover to RG via IH2, when H2 is co-produced, shows
lower CO2e emissions (8.7 gCO2e/MJ) than H2 purchased externally, produced via steam
methane reforming (SMR) (29.5 gCO2e/MJ).

Corn stover-to-RG conversion via pyrolysis can be performed either in an integrated reﬁnery
or in a distributed reﬁnery. In an integrated bioreﬁnery, pyrolysis oil production and upgrading
to ﬁnal hydrocarbons occur at the same plant. However, for a distributed reﬁnery, stabilized
pyrolysis oil is transported to a stand-alone upgrading facility to produce ﬁnal hydrocarbons.
Hydrogen used for the pyrolysis process can be obtained through various avenues: (a)
purchased as SMR H2, (b) co-produced using fuel gas and supplemental natural gas, and (c)
produced from intermediate pyrolysis oil, eventually using excess biomass to sustain H2
production. The three methods yield CO2e estimates for the integrated bioreﬁnery of 13.7,
15.4, and -20.2 g CO2e/MJ. Option (c) obtains increased electricity generation credits,
assisting its CO2e estimate to be negative. The distributed reﬁnery for Corn Stover to RG via
Pyrolysis, with H2 options: (a) purchased as SMR H2 and (b) co-produced using fuel gas and
supplemental natural gas, has CO2e estimates close to 20.2 and 20 g CO2e/MJ, respectively.
A similar variation in hydrogen sources affects forest residue-to-RG production via pyrolysis,
with CO2e emissions ranging from 23 to 28.2 g CO2e/MJ.

Fermentation pathways converting corn stover or forest residue to alcohols (ethanol, n-
butanol, isobutanol, n-propanol, prenol), DIB, and Furans have CO2e estimates ranging from
9.8 g CO2e/MJ for the ethanol pathway to 55 g CO2e/MJ for the DIB pathway. Lower CO2e
emissions from bio-based fuel components shall help reduce the net CO2e emissions of the
blended fuel when blended with gasoline BOB. While ETBE is produced from corn-based
ethanol and natural gas-based isobutylene, it demonstrates competitive cradle-to-gate
CO2e emissions at 29.39 gCO2e/MJ.

Overall, the analysis shows several biomass-to-RG and biomass-to-fuel component
pathways that can be used alongside conventional gasoline, with the potential to reduce
cradle-to-gate CO2e emissions of ﬁnal fuel products. While RG can readily be utilized in
current infrastructure (SI engines), alcohol can be blended with conventional gasoline BOB
(while maintaining oxygen content in fuel).

Life Cycle Energy Efficiency
A fuel production pathway may require energy inputs during the feedstock procurement, pre-

Technology Readiness Level
Technology Readiness Level (TRL) assignments are used in Technology Readiness
Assessments (TRAs) to provide a snapshot of the maturity of critical technology elements
(CTEs) and to inform technology maturation plans (TMPs) when gaps exist. In this report, TRL
deﬁnitions are based on the DOE’s TRA guide and are considered as a standardized metric
for assessing technology maturity on a 1–9 scale [82]. For example, TRL 1 represents the
basic principles observed, while TRL 9 is the process or system operating successfully under
commercial-scale conditions.

Conversion technologies studied in the environmental and economic assessments have
been evaluated for their technology readiness. By identifying evidence of production
pathways for RG and bio-based fuel components from literature and companies’ websites,
we assign technology readiness to conversion technologies. Table 5 lists the technologies
for RG and bio-based fuel component production and summarizes technology-speciﬁc TRLs
based on evidence collected from literature and manufacturers’ websites.

Fermentation is a well-known technology for converting ﬁrst-generation feedstocks such as
corn into ethanol; however, the use of fermentation technology to produce other alcohols
and fuel components may not yet be at a commercial scale [83-85]. While gasiﬁcation and
fermentation are prominent technologies, their large-scale implementation to produce
gasoline or bio-based fuel components is not widely commercialized. The indirect

 58

liquefaction (IDL)- based woody biomass-to-gasoline conversion process incorporates high-
maturity sub-processes, e.g., the conversion of biomass to syngas. HTL for biofuel
production has been established as a prominent technology, with a large-scale processing
plant in Canada; however, RG production may not have been realized due to inconsistent
market demand [86]. Commercial-scale demonstrations exist for transesteriﬁcation
technology converting soybean oil to biodiesel, but RG may not be the primary fuel produced
by the process [87].

From the evaluation of TRLs, it's evident that market-ready technologies, such as
fermentation, pyrolysis, and HTL, exist and can be avenues for producing RG and bio-based

producing biofuels using these technologies, they may not target RG production, possibly
due to a lack of market demand, as additional reﬁning and upgrading will be required to meet

Scale-up Assessment
One of the key factors affecting the ability to expand production of RG and bio-based fuel
components is the feedstock availability. Evaluating the potential production scale based
on feedstock supply can help guide planning and availability assessments. A production
potential assessment is performed for the pathways identiﬁed during the environmental and
economic evaluation. Biomass availability data from the Billion Ton 2023 (BT23) study are
used to perform the analysis [97]. BT23 estimates the availability of woody biomass for
biofuel production based on logging residues and small-diameter trees. Logging residues
and small-diameter trees include estimates for hardwood lowland, hardwood upland, mixed
wood, natural softwood, and planted softwood. Estimates are modeled using the Forest

 60

Sustainable and Economic Analysis Model (ForSEAM, developed during an earlier iteration
of the Billion Ton 2016 study) [98]. The model considers a biomass price of $40 per dry ton
for logging residues, and $58.5 and $70.1 per dry ton for small-diameter trees in the near-
term and mature market scenarios, respectively [97, 99]. For MSW feedstocks, the resource
base includes paper and cardboard, plastics, wood, rubber, leather, textiles, and yard
trimmings.

The BT23 study considers ﬁve different future market scenarios to project the availability of
biomass and waste feedstocks. The “near-term” scenario includes both currently utilized
resources and resources that are not currently exploited but are considered likely to become
available for use, from a technical and operational standpoint, within a 5–10 year timeframe.
The mature-market – low, medium, and high scenarios differ by low-, mid-, and high-market
demand, deﬁned cumulatively over Business as Usual (BAU). In the mature-market low, no
additional energy crops are grown under the BAU projection. In the mature-market medium,
1% improvement in purpose-grown energy crops is additionally considered. In the mature-
market high scenario, a 3% improvement in purpose-grown energy crops, a 1.5x increase in
the USDA’s trend for conventional crop production, and consideration of waste prices with
increasing waste demand are included. The emerging-market scenario considers future
production of microalgae and macroalgae, as well as point-source CO2 capture, in addition
to the mature-market high scenario. In the current analysis, the mature-market medium
scenario is assessed for feedstock availability in 2040.

Figure 16 presents the availability of RG and bio-based fuel components across the
pathways. These estimates indicate the maximum feasibility, assuming 100% feedstock
availability, under the mature market-medium scenario. As some pathways co-produce
fuels, it is imperative that all such fuels are marketable to support the production process
economically. Corn stover to RG co-produces renewable diesel and is one of the most
prominent pathways in terms of producing the highest quantities of RG (8.27 billion GGE).
However, when corn stover is used for ethanol production, it yields more gasoline gallon
equivalents (9.86 billion GGE).

As noted in the introduction, inland marine vessels currently consume 1.5 billion GGE of
gasoline. Under the mature-market, medium-availability scenario, the corn stover-to-RG
pathway has a scale-up potential of 8.27 billion GGE, indicating that alternative feedstocks
could supply most, if not all, of the gasoline demand in the inland marine sector. However,
given the diverse distribution of biomass, further study is required to identify feedstock
allocations for fuel production pathways, as these allocations will require optimizing
geographic resource availability, economic, and environmental factors.

Figure 16: Fuel production potential for RG and bio-based fuel components considering U.S.

Much of the potential success of the RG and bio-based fuel components production relies
on economic feasibility, such that investors can obtain a return on investment while the fuel
production is economically self-sustainable. To encourage technological development and
assist with the initial phase of their market implementation, in the U.S., several policy
mechanisms can create signiﬁcant credit value for RG and bio-components blended into
gasoline. Under the federal statute of Renewable Fuel Standard (RFS2), qualifying bio-based
gasoline blendstocks can generate Renewable Identiﬁcation Numbers (RINs)—typically D5
(advanced) or D6 (renewable/fuel ethanol-equivalent), depending on feedstock and
pathway; provided registration from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA),
approved pathways, and compliance with fuel quality reporting are met. These RINs have
tradable value to obligated parties [100]. In addition, the Clean Fuel Production Credit (45Z)
provides a $/gal tax credit based on lifecycle CO2e emissions relative to a benchmark,
rewarding lower-CO2e producing RG-range products and certain co-processed or biomass-
derived components, subject to GREET-based (or approved) CO2e accounting,
 62

documentation, and prevailing-wage/apprenticeship rules to obtain the full value [101, 102].
At the state level, Low Carbon Fuel Standard (LCFS) programs—most notably California
LCFS (and similar systems in Oregon/Washington)—issue credits proportional to CO2e
emissions reductions (typically measured in gCO₂e/MJ) against a declining standard; bio-
based gasoline components can earn LCFS credits when supplied into the regulated market
and matched to an approved pathway (often via adapted version of the GREET model, also
know CA-GREET) [103, 104]. For marine applications, using these credits may help make the
MFSP of these blend-compatible fuel options competitive while providing additional
performance and environmental beneﬁts.

Conclusion
The analysis presented in this report highlights the significant potential of fuels produced by
blending bio-based fuel components with gasoline BOBs and RG to support the
conventional gasoline market in U.S. inland recreational marine applications. By leveraging
bio-based fuel components and optimizing blending strategies, fuel offers a promising
pathway to meet the sector's energy needs while addressing environmental and regulatory
challenges. The following key insights and conclusions can be drawn from the study:

 • Compatibility with Marine Infrastructure and Engines:

 RG blends, particularly those with higher AKI and MON, meet key ASTM D4814
 performance requirements, ensuring engine performance and reliability. The
 synergistic effects observed when blending bio-based fuel components with
 gasoline BOBs in enhancing AKI and MON highlight their suitability for high-
 performance marine engines, which often operate at maximum capacity. Studied
 properties such as RVP and FBP confirm the blends' adaptability to seasonal and
 regional conditions, ensuring proper fuel performance. The analysis reveals that
 most blends with AKI >80 fall within the stipulated RVP range, ensuring compliance
 with EPA volatility regulations. While most blends have little to no impact on FBP, di-
 isobutylene blends show a notable decrease in FBP as the blend fraction increases.
 This highlights the need for careful selection of blend components to maintain
 volatility and storage stability, particularly in the marine sector, where seasonal
 storage is common. The Distillation points, T10, and T50 demonstrate compatibility
 with cold-start and transient operations, while density values align with conventional
 gasoline, supporting seamless integration into existing infrastructure. The synergistic
 impact of bio-blends on density further supports infrastructure compatibility.
 However, exceptions such as Dimate and DIB require additional consideration to
 optimize blending ratios and maintain desired fuel properties.

 Across the blended fuels, LHV is within ±10% of conventional gasoline (41.74 MJ/kg),
 indicating that most formulations can deliver range and power outputs comparable
 to today’s conventional gasoline used as marine fuel. This minimizes operational
 disruption for owners and marina operators. Di-isobutylene (DIB) is a notable case
 that can increase blend LHV relative to the assumed gasoline BOB (43.45 MJ/kg),
 supporting energy-density targets without sacrificing infrastructure compatibility.
 Extremely high fractions of certain components (e.g., Dimate at 80 vol%) can depress
 LHV outside of the ±10% envelope; such formulations are less attractive for marine
 use unless offset by engine calibration or complementary BOBs.
 64

 Practical implication: Blends selected to stay within the ±10% LHV window should
 yield negligible effects on vessel range and fueling intervals. Water solubility
 assessments highlight the importance of maintaining stability during seasonal
 storage, particularly for ethanol-blended fuels, which require careful management to
 avoid phase separation and storage issues. Together, these properties ensure that
 bio-based components blended with gasoline BOBs can deliver reliable performance
 while meeting the unique demands of the marine sector.

 Methanol, ethanol, and n-butanol are of industrial interest due to their ability to meet
 critical fuel property criteria when blended with appropriate BOBs. These
 components not only enhance AKI and MON but also align with the broader goals of
 reducing CO2e emissions and improving energy security.

 A recurring theme in the analysis is the dependence of blended fuel performance on
 the properties of the base BOB. If the base BOB satisfies the required criteria, the
 resultant blended fuel is more likely to do so as well. This underscores the
 importance of selecting high-quality BOBs to maximize the potential of fuel blends.

• Life-cycle Energy, Environmental, and Cost performances:

 The analysis highlights the significant environmental and economic advantages of
 RG and bio-based fuel components. The LCA results demonstrate substantial
 cradle-to-gate CO2e reductions, with pathways such as Integrated Hydropyrolysis
 and Hydroconversion (IH2) achieving emissions reductions as low as 8.69 g CO2e/MJ
 when using bio-based feedstocks and low-emission hydrogen sources. MSW-based
 pathways also show negative emissions under avoided-waste-emission scenarios,
 underscoring their potential to repurpose waste. However, limited data on criteria
 pollutants such as NOx, SOx, and PM2.5 require further evaluation of pathways
 producing alcohols (except ethanol and methanol), Furans, DIB, and Prenols.

 Economic assessment indicates that RG can achieve a competitive MFSP under
 favorable conditions, with several pathways demonstrating costs below those of
 conventional gasoline. Further, the cost projection analysis for fuels with bio-based
 fuel components and gasoline BOBs reveals that several are competitively priced
 relative to conventional gasoline, especially at higher AKI levels (93–97). Fuel blends
 containing ethanol, methanol, butanol, propanol, and furans exhibit lower pricing for
 high AKI. Hence, bio-based fuel components make fuels attractive for marine use,
 particularly targeting premium and ultra-premium fuel markets.

 65

 Energy efficiency analysis reveals that the corn to n-butanol, forest residue-to-RG,
 algae-to-RG, and MSW-to-RG pathways achieve more than 50% life-cycle energy
 efficiency, driven by low cradle-to-gate energy inputs. Woody biomass pathways
 exhibit moderate efficiencies (46–49%), whereas fermentation of corn stover to
 isobutanol shows lower efficiency (28%) due to high feedstock-processing energy
 demands. These findings underscore the importance of optimizing feedstock
 logistics, conversion technologies, and coproduct utilization to maximize energy
 returns.

 In summary, these results highlight the potential of RG and gasoline BOB blended
 with bio-based fuel components to deliver environmental and economic benefits
 while supporting a more diverse, resilient, and lower-emission conventional gasoline
 market for the U.S. inland recreational marine sector.

• Technology Maturity and Scalability

 The TRL assessment highlights varying levels of technology maturity across the
 studied pathways. Fermentation technology is found to have a TRL of 6-9, while
 others, such as HTL, pyrolysis, gasification, and other technologies, are nearing
 commercial readiness (TRL 6–8), with integrated system demonstrations in
 operation. However, indirect liquefaction and transesterification pathways remain in
 the R&D stage (TRL 4–5), requiring further pilot-scale demonstrations and technology
 maturation. The analysis emphasizes the need for sustained investment in
 technology development and regional infrastructure to bridge the gap between pilot-
 scale and commercial-scale deployment.

 The scaling-up and TRL assessment underscore the feasibility and challenges of
 deploying RG and bio-based fuel components at scale. Feedstock availability
 analysis, based on the Billion Ton 2023 study, identifies significant production
 potential for pathways that utilize MSW, woody biomass, and corn stover in BT23’s
 mature market medium scenario. Corn stover-based pathways demonstrate the
 highest scalability, with hydrocarbon production potential reaching up to 15.57
 billion GGE in 2040 under current resource availability scenarios. Woody biomass
 pathways also show promising scalability, with production potential of 6.34 billion
 GGE in 2040 via pyrolysis technology. While ethanol produced from 1st-generation
 feedstocks via fermentation is the most widely produced bio-based fuel worldwide,

 66

 the availability of 2nd-generation and waste feedstocks for conversion into additional
 fuel components shows promise and has expansion potential.

 By leveraging scalable feedstocks, advancing technology readiness, and current
 federal and state renewable fuel credit programs, the production pathways for RG
 and bio-based fuel components have the potential to play a pivotal role in meeting
 the energy demands of the U.S. inland recreational marine sector.

In summary, the findings of this report underscore the transformative potential of bio-based
fuel components and RG in the U.S. inland recreational marine sector, based on an analysis
of their properties and economic and environmental performance. When formulated to
match ASTM D4814 volatility classes and AKI/MON requirements, gasoline blends can
deliver near-parity energy content and strong antiknock performance. When engine
modifications can be made to allow fuels with properties outside of those of conventional
SI engines, a wider palette of fuels shall be available. Their market success would require
either EPA certification or obtaining an EPA waiver for production and use. Moreover,
blended fuels are projected to be priced lower at higher AKIs (93–97), especially in the
premium and ultra-premium gasoline markets, compared to conventional gasoline options.

To improve the commercial acceptance of bio-based fuel alternatives in the marine sector,
comprehensive engine testing is needed to validate performance, emissions, and
durability under real-world conditions. Long-term storage stability studies should address
challenges like water solubility, phase separation, and volatility control during seasonal
layups. Refined LCAs and TEAs tailored to marine applications are essential for evaluating
environmental benefits, economic feasibility, and regional logistics. Pilot-scale
demonstrations of emerging technologies should be prioritized to accelerate
commercialization. Further R&D and scale-up planning for property-compliant production
of RG and bio-based fuel components, and for their integration into gasoline blending, can
support their long-term, economically feasible contribution to the conventional gasoline
market.
