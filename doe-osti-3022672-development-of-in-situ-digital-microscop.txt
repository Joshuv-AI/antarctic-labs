 Abstract
In-situ digital optical microscopy data was successfully collected during the potentiodynamic polarization
of iron in corrosive NaCl solution. This report serves as a foundation for future, more comprehensive
experiments that correlate electrochemical responses to near-real time surface morphology tracking. The
development of in-situ microscopy and roughness collection will help realize increases in data acquisition
of the surface morphology during electrochemical experiments.

 Introduction
Tracking the morphological changes at the surface of an electrode during an electrochemical experiment is
crucial for understanding the complex processes occurring at the electrode-electrolyte interface. The
combination of microscopy and electroanalysis can provide valuable insights into reaction mechanisms,
kinetics, and material performance. In-situ microscopy of electrochemical experiments has been conducted
at the micro- and nanoscale to study catalytic behavior1,2 electrodeposition3, and localized corrosion4,5,6.
Corrosion has similarly been monitored in-situ without electrochemical stimulus, allowing researchers to
study the natural degradation of materials at the microscopic level.7,8,9,10,11

Electrochemistry can be complex, with many interdependent variables and nonlinear interactions
controlling outcomes. A deposition or corrosion experiment may have 10+ electrical control variables, not
even considering the electrolyte components. Therefore, this is a field that can be vastly enriched and
invigorated with the addition of Machine Learning (ML).12,13,14,15,16 In-situ microscopy offers a powerful
way to generate data for such ML models, enabling direct correlation between surface changes and
electrochemical responses. However, most data collection for large-scale surface morphology changes is
performed ex-situ leading to high numbers of experiments needed to get a clear relationship of surface
changes with time. This process interruption approach compounds the amount of time spent testing several
samples of the same condition, where the only variable adjusted is time.

To significantly increase the data collection for electrochemical experiments, a capability is needed to
simultaneously measure the electrochemical response and topographical changes in the surface. Capturing
microscope data in-situ cannot be achieved without first addressing a combination of challenges. Imaging
the surface of the sample must not interfere with the operation of the electrochemical cell and it must
provide constant observation of the same area throughout the electrochemical test. Simultaneously, the
electrochemical processes must not damage the microscope lens or obstruct surface visibility (via gas
bubbling, accumulation of insoluble products, etc.).

The Keyence VK-X3000 digital microscope in our laboratory is an excellent choice for collecting in-situ
morphological data, though the approach we develop is adaptable to any optical microscope with some
limitations. The digital microscope can capture high-resolution profilometric data and micrographs using
laser confocal scanning and focus variation modalities. Unlike traditional optical microscopes limited by a
narrow depth of field, digital microscopes scan across a range of z-heights, recording focused images at
each step. Schneider and Ilevbare et al. reported on an in-situ microscopy technique using a laser scanning
microscope to monitor corrosion on aluminum alloy AA2024-T3.17,18,19 The experiments involved a 100×
magnification immersion objective and a step height of 0.6 µm. In our case, we want to observe a larger
area (i.e. lower magnification) without immersing the objective into the solution and additionally apply an
electrical stimulus. Larroumet et al.5 demonstrated this capability using a 20× magnification ultra long
working distance (ULWD) objective. The long working distance enabled viewing of an electrode in solution
with the objective outside of the liquid. With this setup, the researchers studied the corrosion of iron in-situ

 2

during a potentiostatic bias. In essence, we seek to combine the techniques and observe the morphological
change and electrochemical response during an electrochemically induced corrosion test. To demonstrate
the concept, we monitored corrosion of iron in sodium chloride solution during a linear sweep voltammetry
(LSV) test.

We present a relatively simple technique and design for rapidly capturing digital images of an electrode
surface during electrochemical experiments. The prototype cell and measurement were made with standard
tools that can be found in any modern electrochemical laboratory. We have successfully shown the ability
to collect good quality morphological data that coincides with the electrochemical response through the
experiment. This increase in data collection will allow for a substantial improvements to ML approaches to
refine process parameters, linking electrochemical response with morphological evolution.

 Experimental
Commercially pure iron was prepared by grinding using progressively higher grits of SiC sandpaper (120,
240, 400, 600, 800, 1200) with rinsing in DI water between each step. The counter electrode was fabricated
from a 0.5 mm thick copper plated steel sheet. Teflon nuts and threaded rods were purchased from
McMaster Carr. The electrochemical cell was designed in AutoCad and printed using a Bambu Lab X1E
3D printer. All parts were made with PLA at 100% infill. A BioLogic VSP-300 potentiostat was controlled
and recorded the electrochemical experiment. Micrographic data was collected using a Keyence VK-X3000
digital microscope and a 20× magnification Nikon SLWD (super long working distance, WD = 20.5 mm)
lens.

Figure 1a shows an exploded diagram of the electrochemical cell. Starting at the bottom of the diagram, a
large, shallow box acts as a secondary container to prevent spillage onto the microscope stage. On the
bottom of the secondary container, a nub was added so that it could be mounted onto the microscope stage.
The smaller box which acts as the electrochemical cell sits inside of the secondary containment. Four
vertical threaded rods were fixed to the bottom of the cell with superglue. The working electrode (WE) is
secured face-up with sample holders screwed down with Teflon nuts, guided by the threaded rods. The
counter electrode (CE) was fabricated with a 1 cm diameter hole in the center for viewing the WE, plus
four holes to align with the threaded rods. Finally, a standard microscope glass slide is added to isolate the
viewing area from surface disturbances caused by vibrations in the liquid and to eliminate any possible
curvature at the liquid surface which would cause problems with the optical path. The cell volume of
approximately 45 mL is chosen so that the electrochemical processes that occur will not significantly affect
the bulk concentration. In the case of iron dissolution, at higher concentrations, an insoluble iron chloride
salt can precipitate and cause difficulty viewing the surface of the sample.

 Figure 1: a) Exploded view and b) cross section of in-situ electrochemical cell.

The cross section in Figure 1b shows a channel beneath the WE where electrical connection is made. A
copper wire was fed through the channel to the pedestal where the WE is placed. A pea-sized amount of
museum wax was used to plug the hole in the pedestal to prevent the cell from leaking. Next, an O-ring was
placed on the pedestal and the wire was bent over the edge to provide electrical contact with sample. The
WE was balanced on the O-ring and stabilized using the two sample holders. To secure the sample Teflon
nuts were tightened down onto the sample holders.

The SLWD lens has an ample working distance of 20.5 mm allowing for up to several millimeters of
separation between the WE and CE. For this experiment the distance between the WE and CE was
approximately 6 mm and the distance between the WE and glass slide was 12 mm. In preliminary
experiments it was found that digital image quality deteriorated with greater distance between the WE and
the glass slide (i.e. depth of liquid). For relatively smooth surfaces (Sa < 2.0 µm) the 12 mm liquid depth is
sufficient for imaging. However, for rougher surfaces (laser powder bed fusion stainless steel S a > 10 µm)
it was not possible to capture high-fidelity topographical data of the surface and a smaller liquid depth may
be needed.

Figure 2 shows the in-situ cell on the microscope stage with the electrical connection to the potentiostat.
The cell was filled with 0.065 M NaCl solution such that the liquid made full contact with the glass slide
and no bubbles or air pockets remained. Because a 2-electrode configuration was used for this test,
potentials of the WE are reported in reference to CE. An LSV experiment was performed from +100 mV
to +1500 mV vs. CE at a scan rate of 1 mV/s. Laser confocal images of the WE were collected as quickly
as possible, or about 90 seconds to collect the data, save the results, and start the next acquisition.

 Results and Discussion
 Figure 3a shows the evolution of the current and surface roughness through the duration of the test. The x-
axes depict both time (bottom) and potential (top). Additional micrographs of interest are presented with
their corresponding potential. The linear sweep data (black curve) follows the electrochemical oxidation of
Fe as the primary reaction.

                                       𝐹𝑒(𝑠) → 𝐹𝑒 2+ (𝑎𝑞) + 2𝑒 −                                            [1]

Additionally, the precipitation of insoluble iron hydroxide occurs on the surface of the electrode with further
oxidation of aqueous Fe(II) near the electrode surface.20

                               𝐹𝑒 2+ (𝑎𝑞) + 3𝑂𝐻 − (𝑎𝑞) → 𝐹𝑒(𝑂𝐻)3 (𝑠) + 1𝑒 −                                 [2]

The current starts at 1 mA, where the steel is not aggressively corroded at the low overpotential. The current
profile is flat for the first 400 s (< 500 mV). During this time there is little change in the micrographs taken
at 175 mV and 650 mV, with just some small growth of the darkened areas. Later in the voltammogram the
current response transitions to a higher slope. Between 400 and 1000 s (500 – ~1100 mV) the rate increases
and the micrograph at 772 mV and 885 mV express a change in color of the general surface from gray to
brown. This color change is in line with the electrochemical oxidation of Fe2+ ions in Eq. [2]. The surface
roughness shows a linear decline from 1.8 to 1.2 µm over the first 1000 s. The initial decline in roughness
is indicative of a homogeneous etching of the surface. In the final 200 mV of the sweep the current increases
rapidly beyond 50 mA. The micrographs show the surface is fully covered by the corrosion product. The
final two data points of the surface roughness (1212 mV and 1391 mV) follow a rapid increase towards 2.0
µm. Large changes in the surface roughness point towards a more localized corrosion, or pitting, with
corrosion product concentrated in discrete areas.

In total, this electrochemical test with in-situ microscopy resulted in 10 images a span of less than 30
minutes. The current response of the LSV curve paired with the micrographs show the increasing current
is linked to progressive corrosion of the surface. Furthermore, the surface roughness analysis is consistent
with expected surface morphological changes. At the lower positive voltages the etching is homogeneous,
and the surface roughness decreases linearly. Later in the voltammogram, current and roughness increase
drastically. Localized pitting and corrosion buildup will yield such changes in surface topography to explain
these data.

To determine if the profilometry matches with any visible features the z-height data is overlaid with the
optical micrographs in Figure 4a – f. In this case no correlation was found between dark areas in the
micrograph and the high (red) or low (blue) z-heights on the sample surface. The line scan profilometry in
Figure 4g does show that the height data is not random. Specific features demarcated by the red ovals are
traceable from 1:15 through 14:58. Smoothening of the line through this time is consistent with the overall
Sa reported in Figure 3a. It is possible that the profilometry measurement detected a semi-transparent salt
film on the surface. Future testing will be needed to understand this effect, with a goal of tracking height
data that correlates with optical images.

These results were obtained using a prototype cell and a relatively simple electrochemical test. They
represent a proof-of-concept for in-situ digital microscopy. Importantly, every aspect of this work was
carried out using standard tools readily available in any modern electrochemical laboratory. The cell
fabrication uses AutoCad and a 3D printer, which allows for rapid design iteration. Moreover, no serious
modification was needed for the potentiostat nor for the digital microscope. The SLWD lens made data
collection possible without immersing the objective into solution, eliminating any concern for damaging
the equipment. This prototype used a 2-electrode cell as a design choice for minimizing the number of
connections needed. This cell had a low profile so that the scan head had enough travel without danger of
running into the electrode connections. Future iterations will allow for a 3-electrode cell, giving improved

 8

electrochemical data. However, there will need to be additional testing and verification to properly correlate
optical images with profilometry data.

There are some important notes on design considerations to discuss. The distance between the WE and the
glass slide is a major key to collecting good images. Small vibrations that disturb the surface of the liquid
will destroy the measurement. The glass slide must be used, as the surface tension overcomes any small
vibrations that disturb the surface of the liquid. Shorter distances between the WE and glass slide make for
easier data collection. However, this must be balanced with maintaining sufficient WE-to-CE distance.
With WE-CE distances that are too short, the electrochemical processes would likely be more affected by
secondary current distribution, leaving the center of the WE to experience different conditions than the
edges. The CE has a large hole in it to allow for viewing the WE by the microscope. If the hole is too small,
it reduces the amount of light that makes it down to the WE surface, and the microscope does not capture
enough data to construct a complete topographical image of the surface.

The surface roughness of the WE directly affects the amount of time for data collection. For example, the
surface roughness of the sample was < 2 µm for the full duration of the test, and each data image took about
90 s to collect. Images of a higher surface roughness would require several minutes to collect, leading to
slower data collection and fewer images. This limitation makes it more difficult to analyze processes like
electropolishing of advanced manufactured metals, where initial surface roughness can be more than 10 µm
and some polishing processes occur rapidly, within minutes.21 Although the Fe corrosion system chosen
here as a demonstration does not generate gas during the electrochemical measurement, many systems do.
Future cell designs will include a lateral flow provision to allow for clearing of bubbles from the imaging
field.

The cell design can be rapidly iterated with the help of 3D printing, allowing for easy modifications to study
new electrochemical systems. In future applications of interest (electropolishing and electrodeposition) it
will be important to maintain a uniform solution with a stable bulk concentration over an even longer time
(i.e. several hours). To accomplish this, a flow cell modification will be added so that a solution volume is
accessible while remaining compatible with the microscope stage. Flow capabilities will also be important
to include for electrochemical processes with gas evolution. Additional prototyping is needed for the
imaging of high roughness materials in-situ, addressing reduced liquid depth and increased acquisition
times. Finally, automating the acquisition process will be explored.

 Conclusion
The prototype electrochemical cell shows very promising results for collecting surface morphological data
during electrochemical experimentation. Importantly, the ability for rapid micrographic collection in-situ
is key for reducing development time in multi-variable processes, and producing large data sets for ML.
This in-situ approach enables the electrochemical community to generate larger datasets more efficiently,
freeing up resources to investigate a wider range of electrochemical conditions instead of dedicating
multiple samples to track changes over time.
