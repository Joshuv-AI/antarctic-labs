Executive Summary
As part of the Next-Gen Profiles (NGP) project, the profile capture and analysis of production
electric vehicles undergoing high-power charging (HPC) is conducted over a wide range of
conditions to explore variance and performance. Charge session parameters are collected from
both the electric vehicle (EV) and electric vehicle supply equipment (EVSE) at a rate of 10Hz and
entered into a time-series database for analysis. These charge profiles are captured under nominal
and off-nominal conditions, exploring the impact of starting battery state of charge (SOC), battery
temperature, vehicle condition, smart charge management (SCM), EVSE limitations and charging
adapter usage. Nominal conditions are defined as ideal conditions that should transfer the
maximum allowable energy in the minimum possible amount of time. Nominal condition profiles
are compared across EVs to characterize state-of-the-art EV charging performance against one
another. Off-nominal condition profiles are compared against their nominal condition profile
counterparts to highlight the variance across less desirable starting conditions within a single EV.

This EV Profile Capture 2025 report stands as an update to the previously published EV Profile
Capture 2023 and EV Profile Capture 2024 reports to include the additional EV & EVSE assets
tested and analyzed in 2025. Major updates within this report include:
• Early MW-scale charging characterization: Analysis of both stationary and mobile energy‐
 storage system (ESS) charging profiles at the megawatt scale.
• Evaluation of charging adapters: Benchmarking of adapter-based charging performance and
 comparative assessments of power profiles, voltage behavior, and SOC and temperature
 dependencies relative to native dispensers.
• Implementation of CCS communication monitoring: Integration of a CCS Charging
 Communication Listener to capture EV-EVSE High-Level Communication (HLC) and assess
 session-level message exchange.
• Expanded use of NGP time-series data: Continued integration of NGP datasets into national
 laboratory grid-modeling initiatives, extending the collaborative work documented in the
 previous year’s report.

Updated the existing portfolio of EV data with the 2025 collection and performed comparative
analysis. Power curve analysis shows the uniqueness in power vs time curves across all EVs,
breaking down the features of a typical profile and where variation is typically seen. Across all
vehicles, approximately 45% of charging time occurs below 50 kW, and only 3.6% occurs above
250 kW. With the addition of newer EV models to the NGP portfolio, time spent below 50 kW
has decreased, while the time in the 50–100 kW, 100–150 kW, and >250 kW bands has increased,
reflecting improvements in vehicle powertrain and battery designs that enable higher sustained
charging power at elevated SOC levels.

 ii

SOC and range performance yielded different top-performing EVs when exploring goals of
performance from SOC and range gained after 10-min (EV2) and 20-min (EV8), and time-to-
achieve 80% SOC (EV8) and 200 miles of range (EV1). No single EV dominates all performance
criteria.

Megawatt (MW) charging profiles generally mirror high-power DCFC behavior, with an initial
period of nearly constant high power followed by a power-tapering phase. MW sessions
constrained by limited power capacity exhibit flat, constant-power characteristics. The onset of
MW charging or discharging induces noticeable grid-voltage variability, indicating the need for
deeper investigation into harmonics, power-quality impacts, and system stability.
Charging adapters continue to expand charging-site flexibility. The degree to which adapters
influence charging behavior varies widely: some EVs show minimal changes, while others exhibit
reduced peak power when using adapters. Voltage-topology switching (e.g. between 400-V and
800-V modes) has been observed during adapter use, presumably to optimize allowable power
delivery. Further study is required to characterize adapter impacts on thermal performance and
charging efficiency.

CCS listener data reveal insights towards high-level communication strategies in that EV and
EVSE current limits may remain constant, shift discretely, or vary dynamically during a charging
session. Actual EVSE output current can be constrained by the EV’s maximum allowable current,
the EVSE’s current limit, or a combination of both. This communication-layer visibility enables a
deeper understanding of power negotiations and constraint propagation during charging sessions.

In 2025, new efforts that build upon and complement the profile capture data include the
development of a streamlined DC fast-charging (DCFC) test procedure in collaboration with EPA
and ANL, as well as applications in Athena ZEV, ESIA trip model, Superlabs, and eCHIP projects.

With the conclusion of the NGP project, the profile-capture work has been documented across
three comprehensive reports and has successfully met its objective of characterizing real-world
fast-charging behavior. The analysis encompasses variations due to EV architecture, battery
topology, charger capability, adapter configuration, environmental conditions, and EVSE
limitations. The findings demonstrate that no single performance metric provides a complete
picture; instead, power, SOC, range, energy throughput, ramp rates, and thermal behavior must all
be considered in tandem.

These high-fidelity datasets and analyses aim to provide critical insights for grid operators,
researchers, and current and future EV owners, offering a robust representation of the state of DC
fast charging across production EVs in the United States.

1 Electric Vehicle Profile Capture Introduction
This report presents data and observations from the electric vehicle (EV) profile capture activities
conducted for the Next-Gen Profiles (NGP) project. The analysis within this report is a
continuation of the analyses that were captured within a series of previous reports published: EV
Profile Capture 2023, EVSE Characterization 2023, Fleet Utilization 2023, High-Level Analysis
and Procedures 2023, EV Profile Capture 2024, EVSE Characterization 2024, and Fleet
Utilization 2024. The EV Profile Capture 2025 report summarizes the 2025 activities and serves
as the final version, concluding the multi-year EV Profile Capture activities under the NGP project.

Although this report is a continuation of the EV Profile Capture report series published over the
past two years, certain references and content are intentionally reiterated to ensure the document
can be read as a stand-alone resource. Material specific to earlier efforts within the project has
been omitted for brevity; however, readers are encouraged to consult the previous reports for a
comprehensive understanding of the methodologies and prior findings that underpin the present
work.

The test article nomenclature in this report is aligned for cross-report comparison. For example,
EV1_22_LD>500V refers to EV#1, a model-year 2022, light-duty vehicle with battery voltage
topology above 500 volts. EVSE2_H1 refers to EVSE#2 operating on the CCS-1 liquid-cooled
cable, the first of two charge dispenser cables. This nomenclature was chosen for EV and EVSE
anonymization to contain enough relevant information for comparison without identification.
Further details on the EVSE dispenser types are listed in the dedicated section for adapters.

1.1 EV Charge Profile Capture Significance Within NGP
This report studies the impact of initial vehicle conditions over the course of a charge session.
Several factors influence an electric vehicle’s charging profile during a full-charge event; the most
impactful of which are the starting state of charge (SOC) and initial battery temperature.
Additionally, an EV charging adapter can allow an EV to charge at different EVSE locations, but
it may impact the EV charging profiles. However, there is little public knowledge about how
charging characteristics change around varied boundary conditions. These boundary, or “off
nominal” conditions, are initial conditions of the vehicle and charger that may impact charging
performance. Understanding how changes in boundary conditions affect charging profiles allows
consumers to become knowledgeable and informed about the charging capabilities of electric
vehicles and compare available EVs in the market. In tandem with boundary conditions, vehicle
battery topology considerations are of particular interest, specifically 400-volt and 800-volt
systems. These EV battery topologies influence charging performance in several ways and are
explored in depth within this report.

 1

The work presented sets out to characterize 21 EVs across 20 sets of boundary conditions and 4
types of EVSEs. Of the different profile possibilities, 239 were captured with three different
initial SOC conditions, three battery temperature conditions, two vehicle conditions, one charge
management case, two EVSE limited conditions, and two adapter configurations.

1.2 What are EV charge profiles
EV charging profiles are shaped by the capabilities, limitations, and power-management behavior
of both the EV’s battery and the EVSE during a charging session. From a consumer’s perspective,
an EV’s charging capability is best understood in terms of how much the battery’s state of charge
(SOC) and driving range increase, like how an internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicle indicates
the fuel level in its tank. The amount of time required to achieve SOC and range milestones aids
drivers in minimizing time spent at DC fast charging stations. For the interest of original
equipment manufacturers (OEMs), grid operators, and researchers, charging power demand is
often the metric used for performance comparison. Power is the primary determinant in component
sizing, grid-planning methodologies, power-dispatch modeling, cost analysis, and achievable
charging rates. A rigorous understanding of power characteristics is therefore essential for the
optimization, management, and systematic classification of EV charging strategies.

1.3 What influences EV charge profiles
Employing High-Level Communication (HLC) protocols, electric vehicles transmit a wide range
of information to direct current (DC) EVSE. This information includes details such as limits, SOC,
requested current, etc. This exchange of information guides the EVSE to provide the necessary
requested current to the vehicles upon request, within the constraints set by the EVSE for the
charging session. The request for current is regulated by the vehicle's Battery Management System
(BMS), whose charge profile is programmed by EV’s OEM. The executed charge profile accounts
for factors such as SOC, temperature, and additional control techniques. The BMS of an electric
vehicle is designed to oversee the battery's requirements during charging and usage, ensure safety
threshold integrity, and manage the longevity of the traction battery's lifespan. Numerous specifics
related to these BMSs and charge profiles are deemed proprietary information protected by vehicle
OEMs.

1.4 Value Behind EV Charge Profiles
A robust and equitable EV charging market depends on enhanced understanding of EV charging
profiles by OEMs, energy utility providers, and consumers. Understanding the capabilities,
limitations, and precise factors influencing an EV during charging enables the optimization of
managed charging strategies and can inform consumer EV purchasing decisions.

1.5 What’s New in the 2025 Report
This year’s report introduces several new topics not covered in previous reports. It provides
commentary on early-stage megawatt (MW) charging technology and presents preliminary results
of MW charging profiles. Building on prior profile capture efforts, this report expands the analysis
to include five additional EVs. Similar to the in-depth analyses performed in previous reports,
these five vehicles were thoroughly examined, with detailed results presented in Section 4.3.

Furthermore, four of the five vehicles in this year’s study were tested with different adapter
configurations to evaluate the impact of charging adapters on EV charging profiles. These results,
analyzed in detail in Section 4.2, primarily focus on two widely used configurations: CCS1 and
the emerging NACS/SAE-J3400 standard. Complementing the adapter testing, this report also
addresses battery topologies, topology optimization, and their influence on charging behavior.

This year’s NGP EV Profile Capture activities incorporated a CCS Charging Communication
Listener to record EV–EVSE communication and better understand session-level negotiations
during charging. This report presents a detailed analysis of the High-Level Communication (HLC)
exchange between the EV and EVSE throughout a charge session.

Alongside the profile capture efforts, the collected data has been actively shared with various
research entities, and the integration of EV charging profile data into grid modeling and other
cross-project collaborations are discussed in Section 4.5. As the final report in the EV Profile
Capture series, this document concludes with a summary of the efforts undertaken under the NGP
project.

2.2.1 EVSE Measurement Locations

To produce consistent measurements across multiple charger topologies, the measurement
locations for each topology being tested need to be explicitly defined. Figure 1 depicts the two
types of charger topologies (paralleled at the dispenser and paralleled at the primary power cabinet)
used for testing the charging characteristics of EVs in this report. On the left of the figure, each
power cabinet is DC-coupled directly to the EVSE dispenser; this is considered a paralleled system
coupled at the dispenser. In the right image of the figure, the power cabinets are coupled to each
other and only have a single DC connection to the dispenser; this is considered a paralleled system
that is coupled at the primary cabinet. This difference in topology drives requirements on the
measurement locations for each system to properly characterize the power flow within the charging
system.

 Figure 1: EVSE System Topologies

As depicted in Figure 2, the alternating current (AC) electrical input to the HPC EVSE was
measured at the input to each power cabinet as supplied from (downstream of) the local service
panel. The DC output from the EVSE was measured at the dispenser input during all testing for
the EV charging characterization tests. If the EVSE topology included an additional DC-DC
converter internal to the dispenser, the DC measurement point was at the final output of the
additional DC-DC converter. This measurement point, at the input of the cable connection from
the dispenser to the EV inlet, was not diagrammed.

The auxiliary system measurements in the HPC EVSE include, but are not limited to, cooling,
controls, lighting, front touch panels, and other loads. These were measured at the source location
in the EVSE. The DC output and the AC auxiliary power measurement locations at the dispenser
are shown in Figure 3 for both EVSE topologies used in this project.

 7

The temperature measurements obtained in this project were measured as close to the locations
described as possible. Cable temperature was measured at the surface of the coolant system tubing
in the cable. The dispenser supply and returning fluid temperature were measured at the surface of

3 EV Profile Capture Testing Conditions
The EV profile-capture testing methodology was designed to characterize and quantify EV
charging behavior without imposing any risk of damage to vehicles. The objective was to obtain a
broad set of charging profiles across a representative sample of EVs, thereby characterizing the
charging power profiles resulting from the combined interactions of the vehicle and the EVSE.

3.1 Nominal & Off-Nominal Conditions
This section highlights the EV and EVSE testing conditions when characterizing EVs during a
charge session. Table 4 describes the EV testing condition parameters, and Table 5 describes the
EVSE testing condition parameters. As a result, multiple distinct charging profiles are possible for
each EV. Although not every vehicle could be characterized under all combinations of test
conditions, all EVs underwent profile capture under the nominal test configuration.

A nominal test was conducted under ideal conditions that should transfer the maximum allowable
energy in the minimum possible amount of time. The following EV parameters must be fulfilled
to complete a nominal test: “starting SOC” of the vehicle must be at 10%, “starting battery

parameters must have been fulfilled to complete a nominal test: “Outside Ambient Temperature”
must be at 23°C, “Smart Charge” values must all be FALSE, and no limits must be placed on the

4 EV Profile Capture Testing Results
4.1 MW Charging Profiles
4.1.1 Current State of the Art and Relevance to EV Profile Capture Efforts

Megawatt (MW) charging research in the United States is expanding, supported by rapid advances
in technology, industry standards, and market deployment. The most immediate demand comes
from heavy-duty trucking and freight; however, interest is also growing in areas such as mining
equipment, marine vessels, particularly those operating in ports, and even future electric aviation.
The CharIN Megawatt Charging System (MCS) serves as the primary standard driving this
progress, enabling charging capacities exceeding 1 MW and scalable up to 4.5 MW [10], far
surpassing the power capabilities of previous fast-charging technologies. The SAE J3271 standard
is being developed to translate the CharIN MCS Task Group’s industry requirements into a
formalized, consensus-based standard that is internationally recognized.

 Figure 5: MCS Connector: SAE J3271

Megawatt charging relevance within NGP stems from its implications towards grid planning and
operation. MW charging introduces extremely high loads, often several megawatts per connector,
that can place significant stress on local and regional electrical grids. Such concentrated demand
can influence power quality, voltage stability, and the need for infrastructure upgrades or advanced
energy management. Additionally, integrating MW charging with on-site renewable generation
and energy storage offers opportunities to mitigate these impacts, but doing so requires a detailed
understanding of charging load profiles and their temporal characteristics. For utilities and grid
operators, accurate MW charging profiles are therefore essential for strategic planning, enabling
informed investments, congestion management, and the development of demand-response
measures or tariff structures that maintain grid reliability while supporting EV adoption.
4.1.2 Early-Stage MW Charging Profiles

MW charging technology is still under active development, and this report provides only an initial
perspective on the characteristics of MW-level charging profiles, their differences from existing
fast DC charging profiles, and considerations for what to expect as MW charging evolves. To
obtain a preliminary charging profile, the Battery Energy Storage System (BESS) at ANL was
used as the test platform, available with a 1 MW charge and discharge capability with over 600
kWh of available storage.

4.2.4 Extended Adapter Testing in Future Studies

Although the 2025 NGP EV Profile Capture efforts explored the effects of adapter use on DC fast-
charging profiles, several important areas remain unaddressed. These gaps highlight the need for
further investigation to more comprehensively understand the influence of adapters on charging
behavior.
4.2.5 Effect of Adapters on Connector Temperature

The influence of adapters on connector and cable temperatures has not yet been examined and
remains an important topic for future work. Adapters may introduce additional electrical
resistance, altered heat dissipation pathways, and different mechanical contact characteristics
compared to native connectors. These factors could potentially affect thermal behavior during
high-power DC fast charging. A systematic evaluation, including measurements of connector shell
temperature, contact-pin temperature, cable thermals, and coolant behavior, would help determine
whether adapter use contributes to elevated temperatures, thermal derating, or accelerated wear
during charging.
4.2.6 Effect of an Adapter Rated with Lower Current/ Voltage/ Power Ratings

Further investigation is required to understand how adapters with different current, voltage, and
overall power ratings influence charging performance. Testing a broader range of adapters makes,
models, and designs, including varying thermal limits, conductor sizes, and internal electronics,
would clarify how these constraints interact with different EV battery topologies (e.g., 400-V, 800-
V configurations). Such studies would help identify whether lower-rated adapters introduce
measurable bottlenecks in the I–V trajectory, affect the initiation of thermal or electrical derates,
or limit achievable peak power.

4.3.1 Time Series Power

vehicle and charger sustain power delivery across the session. This analysis directly exposes
performance limitations, control handoffs, power derating, and deviations from expected charging
behavior that are not visible from energy delivered or session duration alone. Temporal power
analysis is also directly influential towards other areas of research outside of EV charging
performance such as grid load modelling, smart-charge scheduling, route planning, etc. Power
profile curves for EV charging vary among different EVs due to their battery capacities/
architectures, BMS strategies, powertrain design, charging technologies, initial conditions, and
accessory systems – resulting in distinct charging profiles. This uniqueness influences factors like
charging speed, peak power demands, deratings, and the duration spent at various charging levels,

4.3.2 State of Charge, Range and Energy Charged

Understanding charge performance metrics like SOC gained, range added, and kWh energy

managing their charging needs.

Different metrics or benchmarks are often used to characterize charge performance in terms of
SOC, range, and energy charged. The Figure 26 illustrates charging performance in terms of SOC
gained, energy delivered, and range added after 10 and 20 minutes of DC fast charging, sorted by

gained after 20 minutes of charging, but EV2 was the highest performing for SOC gained after 10

4.5 Cross-Project Utilization of NGP Data and Results
In 2025, new cross-project collaborative efforts were explored to further utilize the NGP data
collected to support other research areas. These efforts include the development of a streamlined
DC fast-charging (DCFC) test procedure in collaboration with the U.S. Environmental Protection
Agency (EPA), and applications in modelling platforms; NLR’s AthenaZEV, ANL’s ESIA route
forecasting tool, NLR/INL’s Superlabs model, and NLR’s eCHIP model. A summary of how NGP
results were used in each of these initiatives is provided below.
4.5.1 U.S. Emissions Protection Agency (EPA) DCFC Procedure Development

Leveraging DCFC data collection experience and procedures development within the NGP project,
ANL collaborated with EPA to develop a standardized test procedure for DCFC performance
analysis applicable for LD EVs. The proposed procedure was designed to be time-efficient,
streamlined, and practical to implement, minimizing both testing and computational burdens for
OEMs and testing authorities.

This test procedure is built upon the established frameworks of SAE J2953-4 and ISO 12906:2024,
while incorporating insights and best practices from ANL’s DCFC testing conducted under the
NGP project. The proposed draft harmonizes the EV discharge methodology defined in SAE J1634
with DCFC procedures, enabling seamless, sequential execution of discharge and charging tests.
This integration aimed to optimize test workflows, reducing both total duration and resource
requirements. The draft specifies detailed measurement requirements and locations for both the
EVSE and the EV, clearly illustrating where measurements must be performed to ensure accuracy
and repeatability.

A secondary goal of this initiative is to address the needs of the broader EV testing community.
By leveraging internationally recognized standards for vehicle discharge and DCFC, the
proposed procedure establishes a consistent, repeatable, and comparable testing framework based
on directly measured data rather than vehicle-reported values. While definitions and metrics for
DCFC performance may vary across regions, this harmonized data acquisition methodology is
designed to enable global comparability, supporting transparency and consumer confidence
through reliable, standardized performance information. A summary of the proposed DCFC test
performance procedure is illustrated and described below in Figure 38. A published version of
the full detailed procedures document is expected to be available to the public in early 2026.

4.5.2 NLR’s AthenaZEV Modelling Platform [8]

Athena, a joint initiative directed by NLR in collaboration with ORNL, was initiated in 2018 to
assist transportation hubs, including Dallas Fort Worth International Airport (DFW), as they
incorporate and adjust to transformative technologies that advance ambitious energy and climate
objectives. The collaboration is determining approaches to electrify airport operations with the
next phase designed to electrify transportation and mitigate airport investment risks, having an
emphasis on rental cars and named the Zero-Emissions Vehicles (AthenaZEV) project. As EVs
form a larger share of rental fleets, taxis, and airport ground transportation, pinpointing the
charging infrastructure strategies required to sustain the megawatt-scale charging demand of rapid
rental car electrification at airports calls for finely tuned optimization through advanced controls,
energy storage, and adaptable building loads.

NLR researchers produced data-driven models of both existing and forecasted mobility needs to
enable DFW decision-making support and actionable guidance in the deployment of electrified
transportation. NGP EV charging data was utilized for this work to anticipate/model expect
accurate EV charging loads. Machine learning and real-time data gathered from sensors were used
to build a “digital twin” of DFW roadways and transportation systems to model real-world
scenarios and energy requirements.

In the second phase, AthenaZEV will incorporate NLR's ARIES platform, previous DFW building/
transportation digital twins, and NLR's charging infrastructure analysis tools into the current
model. These will allow researchers to design cost-optimal, behind-the-meter energy storage and
solar photovoltaic systems based on the climate, building type, and utility rate structure of potential
EV charging sites to reduce investment risk and lower costs and grid impacts.

4.5.3 Superlabs Project lead by NLR and INL [9]

The Superlabs project represent a new model of national laboratory collaboration in which multiple
DOE facilities operate as a single, unified virtual laboratory through high-speed, real-time digital
connections. The concept is demonstrated through the successful integration of grid simulation
capabilities between the NLR and INL. By linking Real-Time Digital Simulators (RTDS), the two
laboratories created an environment where hardware and software at one location can directly
interact with hardware and software at the other, even though they are geographically distant. This
interconnected structure allows power flows to be handled by the local grids in Colorado and Idaho
while the simulations behave as if they belong to a single, cohesive research platform. The ability
to exchange data 1,200 times per second with only 28 milliseconds of delay makes it possible to
study complex grid behaviors, including transient responses triggered by sudden changes in
electricity supply or demand.

As more RTDS racks and specialized facilities join this framework, they collectively form a multi-
site system capable of addressing pressing energy challenges through coordinated
experimentation. NLR contributes its Energy Systems Integration Facility (ESIF), which merges
computer-based simulations with real energy hardware such as solar panels, batteries, and electric
vehicles, using NGP data to simulate EV interactions much more accurately. INL brings its
National Electric Grid Reliability Test Bed, a utility-scale transmission and customizable
distribution system that reflects real operational conditions. By connecting these facilities,
researchers can examine how components such as distribution lines, electrolyzers, and
transmission systems influence one another across laboratory boundaries. This creates a powerful
collaborative platform where diverse capabilities are combined to support grid modernization,
study renewable energy integration, and conduct advanced demonstrations.

4.5.4 ANL’s Energy Systems and Infrastructure Assessment (ESIA) Group Models
ANL’s ESIA group performed a study to simulate 60 real-world road trips across the continental
U.S. and Canada, ranging from 400 to 800 miles, using three representative battery electric vehicle
(BEV) models under varying environmental conditions, charging strategies, connector types, and
network configurations. Results showed that ambient temperature critically impacted EV range
and charging power, thereby affecting total trip time and stop frequency. Frequent, short duration

4.5.5 NLR’s eCHIP Project Simulation

NLR’s eCHIP project integrates NGP data to accurately emulate electric vehicle charging behavior
in both physical and simulated test environments.

PHIL (hardware testing): The eCHIP DC hub test platform at ESIF included several emulated
EVSEs. Emulated EVSEs are enacted by a lab power supply (DC/AC converter) or DC load bank
(controlled resistance) to draw the equivalent load from the DC distribution bus. Each emulated
EVSE was implemented as a python model of the EVSE and the connected EV’s charging
behaviors, which calculated the current charging power and sent this as a setpoint to the associated
lab hardware. The python script’s simulation and control frequency was 5 Hz. NGP test data was
used to implement a lookup table of “power acceptance” vs vehicle state-of-charge (SOC). Power
acceptance is the maximum rate that the vehicle can charge at given its current SOC (determined
by an EVs BMS). The actual charging rate for the emulated EVSE is the minimum of the EVSE’s
present maximum power limit and the EV’s power acceptance. Five EVs were simulated in the
PHIL / experimental test platform: Hyundai Ioniq 5; Nissan Leaf; Rivian; Ford F150 Lightning;
and Ford E-Transit. The power acceptance vs SOC values were taken from the NGP’s nominal
23°C charging tests (where it is assumed that the EVSE is always offering a higher power than the
vehicle is requesting).

 43

CHIL (simulation): Similar approaches were used for real-time simulation and offline
simulations, in which there wer simulated EVSEs and EVs. The simulation models used the same
power acceptance vs SOC lookup approach as described for PHIL testing. The EVSE and EV’s
actual charging power is then the minimum of the EVs power acceptance and the EVSEs power
limit set by the site controller or distributed droop curve.

Site modeling tool – EVI-EnSitePy: The EVI-EnSitePy tool has incorporated the same charge
acceptance profiles used in the CHIL/PHIL DC hub test platform.

In Development / Future Work: There is ongoing work to develop dynamic EV battery charge
models that provide more flexibility and accuracy than the fixed “charge acceptance curves”. This
work leverages the extensive NGP EV profiles dataset that includes charging data from multiple
temperatures and starting SOC to construct a simplified physics-based battery model. Once
development is complete, these models will be used in the eCHIP CHIL simulations and EVI-
EnSitePy to enable temperature-related analysis and performance demonstrations.

5 Summary of NGP’s EV Profile Capture Efforts
The NGP project was conducted over five years, with profile capture activities spanning four years
(2022-2025). These efforts focused on systematically collecting, analyzing, and disseminating
production EV charging data to support research, grid modeling, and the broader scientific
community. During the profile capture phase, data was collected from 21 EVs, 12 EVSEs, and 6
charging handle/adapter configurations tested under nominal, off-nominal, and edge-case
scenarios. These scenarios included variations in temperature, SOC, and EVSE configurations,
charging adapter configurations, and power limits, resulting in the generation of 239 unique EV
charging profiles.

The analysis performed on this NGP dataset uniquely provided detailed insights into a wide range
of performance metrics, including charging power, SOC dependence, gained energy and range
(miles), charging current and C-rates, battery architecture analysis, ramping rates, and OCPP
curtailments and transient response time, thermal evaluations, and impact of adapter usage on
charging under both nominal and off-nominal conditions.
Beyond profile capture, the NGP project also contributed to EVSE characterization and fleet
characterization, with dedicated reports published in previous years documenting these efforts.
NGP datasets have supported more than 10 research initiatives across the United States, providing
a benchmark for EV charging performance studies. To maximize accessibility and support
reproducible research, the complete NGP datasets (as well as EVSE and Fleet data) have been
uploaded to NLR’s Livewire Data Platform [12] for future research. The profile capture effort
provided an early examination of emerging megawatt charging technology by collecting and
analyzing initial MW-scale charging profiles.

NGP’s EV profile capture effort has significantly advanced understanding of EV charging
behavior across a diverse set of vehicles, adapters, and operational conditions. By making these
high-resolution datasets publicly available, the NGP project has created a valuable resource for the
research community, supporting innovation in grid integration, EV technology, and future electric
mobility solutions.
