Correlated uniform attachment trees
Johannes Bäumler, Miklós Z. Rácz, Nathan Ross, Anirudh Sridhar

Abstract
We introduce and study a new model of correlated uniform attachment (UA) trees, where correlation is sprinkled throughout the time evolution of the process. In this model, two UA trees are grown in parallel, and at each time step a new node is added to each tree, with an edge between it and a uniformly chosen existing vertex in the respective tree. The two choices of attachment are correlated: with probability α \alpha , the edges attach to nodes with the same time label in both trees, and with probability 1 − α 1-\alpha , the choices are made independently. We study fundamental detection and estimation questions for this model, given two unlabeled trees. In our main result, we construct a consistent estimator of the correlation parameter α \alpha , as the size of the trees goes to infinity. The construction of our statistic relies on two key ideas. First, we use Jordan centrality to identify subsets of vertices of each tree whose intersection has a sufficient number of common early vertices. The second idea is that, across multiple time scales, it is possible to approximately determine the labels of vertices that have attached to these early vertices, using the sizes of fringe subtrees. Our analysis includes novel quantitative bounds on the fraction of early vertices that remain central, which are of independent interest in the network archaeology literature.

Introduction and overview

Networks arise naturally in a number of fields, such as protein-protein networks in biology, social networks in sociology, and power grids in engineering, just to name a few.
Motivated by understanding such data, there has been an enormous effort over the last
decades

Modern introductions to this literature include the texts

Detecting correlation among the networks and estimating aspects of the correlated structure, such as latent matchings of the vertices across networks, are central questions.

Initially, these focused on the simplest such model, namely correlated Erdős–Rényi random graphs, introduced by

In this paper, we contribute to this line of research by introducing and studying a novel model of correlated randomly growing graphs.
The main feature of this model is that correlation is sprinkled throughout the time evolution of the process.
This feature makes the model substantially different from the one studied by

We focus our attention on

Approximately matching early vertices is closely connected to a thread of recent work on

1.1
The model and results

An unlabeled uniform attachment (
\mathrm{UA}
) tree sequence
(T_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is constructed sequentially by adding one vertex with an edge at each time step. Initially,
T_{0}
consists of a single node, and, given
T_{n}
,
T_{n+1}
is constructed by adding a single node with an edge connected to a node of
T_{n}
chosen uniformly at random. Distributionally we write
T_{n}\sim\mathrm{UA}(n)
.
It is also natural to consider a
labeled
model, where the label of a vertex is the time of its arrival: the initial vertex has label
0
and the vertex added at step
n
is labeled
n
.

We introduce and study a model of two
correlated

\mathrm{UA}
tree sequences
(T_{n}^{1},T_{n}^{2})_{n\geq 0}
.
The model has a single parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]
, which measures the amount of dependence between the two trees.
The two trees are grown sequentially together,
and marginally they are both UA trees,
but the edge choices at each time step are correlated.
Specifically,
at each time step,
with probability
\alpha
the edge choices are
aligned
,
and with probability
1-\alpha
they are
independent
,
defined as follows:

•

Aligned choice:

the two new nodes connect to a node in their respective trees with the
same
time label, chosen uniformly at random.
That is, at time step
n
, choose
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
uniformly at random, and in both trees node
n
connects to node
i
.

•

Independent choice:

the two new nodes make edge choices in their respective trees independently.
That is, at time step
n
, choose
i_{1},i_{2}\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
independently and uniformly at random,
in the first tree node
n
connects to node
i_{1}

and in the second tree node
n
connects to node
i_{2}
.

Figure 1:
An illustration of one step in the model.
Before this step, the two trees with nodes
\{0,1,\ldots,5\}
are in black.
Subsequently, a new node, labeled
6
, is added to both trees.
The edge choices in the two trees are correlated, as follows.
With probability
\alpha
, the two new nodes connect to a node in their respective trees with the
same
time label, chosen uniformly at random (
aligned choice
, illustrated on the left).
With probability
1-\alpha
, the two new nodes make edge choices in their respective trees independently (
independent choice
, illustrated on the right).

The model is illustrated in Figure
1
.
The time labels are an essential part of the definition of this model of correlated UA trees.
Marginally,
T_{n}^{1}
and
T_{n}^{2}
are both uniform attachment trees, and
they are correlated through the “aligned choice” steps in their time evolution,
where the time labels play a central role.
Nonetheless, since
our focus is on inference problems given two such trees
without
knowledge of the time labels,
in what follows we write

\left(T_{n}^{1},T_{n}^{2}\right)\sim\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}\left(n\right)

for a pair of
unlabeled

\mathrm{UA}
trees constructed in this way.
When
\alpha=0
, the two trees are independent—we also write
\mathrm{UA}(n)^{\otimes 2}
for
0{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)
—and when
\alpha=1
, we have
T_{n}^{1}=T_{n}^{2}
. Thus
\alpha\in[0,1]
quantifies the amount of dependence between the two trees.

Given this model, a natural first question is whether it is possible to asymptotically distinguish between a pair of trees generated according to
\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)
for
\alpha>0
and an independent pair of
\mathrm{UA}(n)
trees.
An inherent difficulty in this problem (and subsequent estimation problems) is that the data consists of an
unlabeled
pair of trees; while the “aligned choice” step aligns the choices of nodes in terms of
labels
, such a step may actually make the pair of
unlabeled
trees go
further apart
.
1
1

1

As an example, consider
n=2
, let
T_{2}^{1}
and
T_{2}^{2}
both be paths on the node set
\{0,1,2\}
, with the central node in
T_{2}^{1}
having time label
0
and the central node in
T_{2}^{2}
having time label
1
.
Now suppose that the next step is an “aligned choice” step, with node
3
attaching to node
0
in both trees. Then
T_{3}^{1}
is a star on four nodes and
T_{3}^{2}
is a path on four nodes,
so the two trees are not isomorphic after the “aligned choice” step, despite being isomorphic before it.

Despite this, we are able to distinguish correlated trees from independent ones.
To state this result, recall that the total variation distance between two probability measures
P
and
Q
on a discrete set
\mathcal{X}
is defined by

\mathrm{TV}(\textsf{P},\textsf{Q}):=\sup_{A\subseteq\mathcal{X}}|\textsf{P}(A)-\textsf{Q}(A)|.

(1.1)

Theorem 1.1

(Detecting correlation)
.

For every
\alpha\in(0,1]
we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{TV}\left(\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}\left(n\right),\mathrm{UA}(n)^{\otimes 2}\right)=1.

(1.2)

We initially attempted to prove Theorem
1.1
using various simple statistics, such as counting leaves in the two trees.
However, while such approaches are able to show that the
\mathrm{TV}
distance in (
1.2
) is bounded away from
0
(see Appendix
A
for the details for counting leaves),
these approaches cannot show that the limiting
\mathrm{TV}
distance is
1
.
To show this, we devise a statistic that is inherently multiscale in nature.
This statistic computes the (normalized) sum of the degrees of the
K_{j}
most central vertices (measured according to Jordan centrality) in both trees, across a range of different values
\{K_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\ell}
with
K_{j}=2^{j}K
;
see Section
1.3
for further discussion
and Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4

for details.
A key ingredient of our proof is a result that quantifies in UA trees the size of the intersection of the earliest vertices and the most central vertices.
This is of independent interest in the network archaeology literature; see Section
1.2
and Theorem
1.5
below for details.

We can go a step further and ask
whether it is possible to asymptotically distinguish between two pairs of trees generated according to
\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)
and
\alpha^{\prime}{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)
for
\alpha\not=\alpha^{\prime}
.
Our next result affirmatively answers this question by constructing a consistent estimator of
\alpha
.

Theorem 1.2

(Consistent estimation of
\alpha
)
.

There exists an estimator

\widehat{\alpha}_{n}:=\widehat{\alpha}_{n}(T_{n}^{1},T_{n}^{2})

such that for every
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
if
(T_{n}^{1},T_{n}^{2})\sim\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)
,
then
\widehat{\alpha}_{n}\to\alpha
in probability as
n\to\infty
.

The construction of the estimator
\widehat{\alpha}_{n}
and the proof of Theorem
1.2
build on the statistics and proof of Theorem
1.1
, but are more involved.
In particular,
\widehat{\alpha}_{n}
is also multiscale in nature and relies on estimating early vertices using the most central vertices.
However, instead of computing degrees, the estimator
\widehat{\alpha}_{n}
takes a more fine-grained approach and computes, for central vertices, the number of neighbors whose fringe subtrees have sizes that fall in appropriate intervals.
Effectively, the sizes of fringe subtrees allow us to approximately estimate the time labels of late-arriving vertices (at least in aggregate), and doing this across multiple time scales allows to estimate
\alpha
.
We note that the estimator
\widehat{\alpha}_{n}
is computable in polynomial time in
n
.
We refer to Section
1.3.2
for further details.
The existence of this consistent estimator immediately implies the following corollary.

Corollary 1.3

(Distinguishing different levels of correlation)
.

For every
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in[0,1]
with
\alpha\neq\alpha^{\prime}
, we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{TV}\left(\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}\left(n\right),\alpha^{\prime}{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)\right)=1.

While Corollary
1.3
implies Theorem
1.1
, we state it separately because the proof of Theorem
1.1
is fundamentally different and simpler, and moreover it provides insights into the structure of UA trees in general, including the location of their most central vertices, which is a further contribution of the paper. We continue this introduction by discussing this latter contribution, followed by outlines of the proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
in Section
1.3
, then discussing open problems in Section
1.4
, and finally giving the plan of the rest of the paper in Section
1.5
.

1.2
Finding early vertices

A key element of our proofs is identifying collections of vertices of each tree that are likely to contain early vertices and have many labels in common. Statistics related to such vertices can then give information about the correlation between the two trees.
The problem of finding the root and early vertices of unlabeled randomly growing trees, including UA, has received significant attention in recent years, catalyzed by
Bubeck et al. 2017a
.
In particular, closest to our paper are the works of

Lugosi and Pereira 2019
and
Devroye and Reddad 2019
,
who study the problem of finding the
seed
, that is, the tree induced by the earliest vertices.
However, for reasons discussed below, the results
therein
are insufficient for our purposes,
so we derive a related result that is of independent interest in network archaeology.
Specifically, we quantify in UA trees the size of the intersection of the earliest vertices and the most central vertices; see Theorem
1.5
below.

Like many related works,
we use the following notion of centrality to identify early vertices.

Definition 1.4

(Centrality)
.

For a vertex
v
of a tree
T
, let
\psi_{T}(v)
denote the size of the maximal component after removing all edges connected to
v
, and call
\psi_{T}(v)

the
centrality
of vertex
v
.

For a tree
T
, we refer to the
K
vertices of
T
with the
smallest
centrality (with ties broken uniformly at random) as the
K

most central vertices
of
T
.
(This notion of centrality is referred to as
Jordan centrality
in many network archaeology works, but that terminology is also used for a different notion of centrality—in terms of graph distances—in other areas.)
The recent paper
Josifov et al. 2026
discusses properties of the most central vertices (for this centrality measure and other common ones) in the context of root finding in UA trees, among others; see also

Jog and Loh 2017
;
Jog and Loh 2018
,

Banerjee and Bhamidi 2022
,
and references therein.

Here, we quantify the overlap of the most central vertices with the earliest vertices.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
T_{n}\sim\mathrm{UA}(n)
and let
A_{K}(n)
be the set of the
K
most central vertices of
T_{n}
. Fix
c>1
,
\delta>0
,
and set

c^{\prime}:=c\left(1-e^{-1/c}\right)-\delta.

Then there exists a constant
C<\infty
that depends only on
c
and
\delta
such that for every

\varepsilon>0
,
the following holds for all
K\geq(C/\varepsilon)\log(1/\varepsilon)

and all large enough
n\geq n_{0}(K,\delta,c)
:

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|A_{\lceil cK\rceil}(n)\cap\{0,1,\ldots,K-1\}\right|\geq c^{\prime}\,K\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

The theorem says that for large enough
K
, with probability close to one, the proportion of the first
K
vertices that are also among the
cK
most central is at least
c(1-e^{-1/c})-\delta
for any
\delta>0
. This proportion is optimal with our proof, but it is an interesting question to study the behavior of
|A_{K}(n)\cap\{0,\ldots,K-1\}|/K
as
K\to\infty
.
Figure
2
is a simulation illustrating the theorem for
K=50
and
c=2
, where the overlap between the
50
first arriving vertices and the
100
most-central vertices is 41. Further simulations suggest the mean overlap is around 40 with a standard deviation of a bit more than
2
, which aligns well with the threshold
2(1-e^{-1/2})\times 50=39.35
given by Theorem
1.5
.

A simulation of a UA tree with 1000 vertices illustrating Theorem
1.5
with
K=50
and
c=2
. The 41 purple nodes are those within both the 50 first arrivals and the 100 most central nodes, orange are the 9 remaining of the 50 first arrivals, and green are the 59 remaining of the 100 most central.

We use Theorem
1.5
to derive the following corollary that gives a lower bound on the overlap of the
K
-centrality sets of any two UA trees for large
K
;
this corollary is a key component of our subsequent proof for detecting correlation.

Corollary 1.6
.

Let

(T_{n}^{1},T_{n}^{2})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of pairs of UA trees with any joint distribution, and denote their vertex-labeled centrality sets by
(A_{r}^{1}(n),A_{r}^{2}(n))
,
r\in\N
.
Then there exists a constant
C<\infty
such that for all
K
and for all large enough
n\geq n_{0}(K)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|A_{K}^{1}(n)\cap A_{K}^{2}(n)\right|\geq 0.2K\right)\geq 1-\frac{C\log(K)}{K}.

Let us now compare our results with those of
Lugosi and Pereira 2019
and
Devroye and Reddad 2019
,
who study finding the seed in UA trees.
There are two main differences between these works and ours.
First, most of the results in
Lugosi and Pereira 2019
and
Devroye and Reddad 2019

focus on settings where the seed is either a known fixed graph (e.g., a path or a star), or certain properties of it are known (e.g., its number of leaves).
Our interest is in the setting where the seed is itself a UA tree, so these results do not apply.
The exception is
(
Lugosi and Pereira 2019
, Theorem 1.5)
,
which shows that there exists a constant
c>0
such that for large
K
, with probability close to one,
the
cK/\log K
most central vertices
are all among the
K
earliest vertices.
However, for our purposes we would like to find
\Omega(K)
of the
K
earliest vertices (in fact, to obtain the linear overlap in
K
in Corollary
1.6
by a union bound, without any assumptions on the joint distribution of
(T_{n}^{1},T_{n}^{2})
, we want to find at least
(1/2+\varepsilon)K
of the earliest
K
vertices), so the aforementioned result is insufficient for our application.

This brings us to the second main difference between these works and ours,
which is more conceptual.

Lugosi and Pereira 2019
and
Devroye and Reddad 2019
focus on seed-finding algorithms that output, with probability close to one, a set that either contains the seed (i.e., it must contain all seed nodes) or is contained in the seed (i.e., it cannot contain nodes outside of the seed).
In contrast, our goal is more relaxed, allowing both types of errors: we are fine with outputting a set which does not contain all of the earliest vertices, and which contains vertices that are not early.
The upside of this flexibility is that it allows us to find a large fraction of the
K
earliest vertices, as detailed in Theorem
1.5
.
Focusing on the overlap between the earliest vertices and the most central vertices is natural in its own right, so Theorem
1.5
may find other applications.

The proof of Theorem
1.5
follows the strategy (used in broad strokes in other works studying centrality in UA trees, e.g.,
Jog and Loh 2018
,
Lugosi and Pereira 2019
) of relating
centrality to the sizes of so-called
fringe subtrees
of early and late vertices; see Definitions
2.2
and
2.3
, and Lemma
2.4
. For a
\mathrm{UA}
tree, fringe subtrees evolve as standard Pólya urns started with one ball of the counted color, which we can understand in detail; see Section
2.2
.

1.3
The statistics and proof ideas

We now give a high level overview of the key ideas behind our proofs and statistics. Recall that the input to our statistics is a pair of
unlabeled
trees
(T_{n}^{1},T_{n}^{2})
.
Imagine, however, that we actually
knew
the identity of the initial vertex (labeled
0
) in both trees.
We now explain how this can help with distinguishing the distributions
\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)
and
\mathrm{UA}(n)^{\otimes 2}
.

Let
D_{n}^{i}:=\deg_{T_{n}^{i}}(0)
denote the degree of the initial vertex in
T_{n}^{i}
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
.
A natural idea is to use the pair of degrees
(D_{n}^{1},D_{n}^{2})
as a statistic to distinguish the distributions
\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)
and
\mathrm{UA}(n)^{\otimes 2}
.
Observe that we can write

D_{n}^{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}^{i}
,
where
X_{j}^{i}
is the indicator random variable that is
1
precisely when node
j
attaches to node
0
in the
i
-th tree.
Since
\mathbb{E}[X_{j}^{i}]=1/j
,
we have that
\mathbb{E}[D_{n}^{i}]=\sum_{j=1}^{n}1/j\approx\log n
,
and in fact marginally
D_{n}^{i}
is approximately Poisson distributed with mean
\log n
.
Under the independent model
\mathrm{UA}(n)^{\otimes 2}
,
we have that
D_{n}^{1}
and
D_{n}^{2}
are independent.
On the other hand, under
\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)
, the indicators
X_{j}^{1}
and
X_{j}^{2}
are dependent.
Specifically, we have that

\mathbb{P}(X_{j}^{1}=X_{j}^{2}=1)=\alpha/j+(1-\alpha)/j^{2}
,
which implies that

\Cov(D_{n}^{1},D_{n}^{2})\approx\alpha\log n
.
Now consider the normalized (centered and scaled) degrees

Z_{n}^{i}:=(D_{n}^{i}-\log n)/\sqrt{\log n}

for
i\in\{1,2\}
.
By the preceding discussion, we have that the pair

(Z_{n}^{1},Z_{n}^{2})

is approximately standard Gaussian under
\mathrm{UA}(n)^{\otimes 2}
,
while under
\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)
it is approximately Gaussian with
mean
(0,0)

and covariance matrix

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&\alpha\\
\alpha&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
For every
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the total variation distance between these two Gaussian distributions is in
(0,1)
,
so
\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)
and
\mathrm{UA}(n)^{\otimes 2}
can be distinguished with positive probability.

There are two main issues with this approach:
(1) the identities of the initial vertices in the two trees are
not known
,
and
(2) this only distinguishes with positive probability, not with probability close to one.
The solution to the first issue is to turn to network archaeology and
estimate
the earliest vertices in the two trees,
while the solution to the second issue is to consider such statistics across multiple time scales.
We dive into the details in Sections
1.3.1
and
1.3.2
below.

Before proceeding further, we note that
observable statistics can only be in terms of an
unlabeled
version of the tree
T
, but many times probabilities of statistics are more conveniently written in terms of the
labeled
version of the tree.
To mitigate confusion around this point, we will write
v\in T
to denote a vertex in the unlabeled tree (e.g., when indexing a sum), and we will use
\{0,\ldots,n\}
to denote the vertices of labeled versions of the trees.

Both statistics used in our proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
are based on centrality sets defined using Definition
1.4
, and we denote the
K
most central vertices of
T_{n}^{i}
by
A_{K}^{i}(n)
,
1\leq K\leq n+1
.

1.3.1
Distinguishing between independent and correlated models

To construct our separating statistic
to distinguish between independent and correlated pairs of UA trees,
fix
\ell\in\N
and integers
0<K_{0}<K_{1}<\cdots<K_{\ell}
, and define

S_{K_{j}}^{i}(n):=\sum_{v\in A_{K_{j}}^{i}(n)}\deg_{T_{n}^{i}}(v).

In words,
S_{K_{j}}^{i}(n)
is the sum of the degrees of the
K_{j}
vertices with largest centrality in
T_{n}^{i}
.

Our separating statistic is

\frac{1}{(\ell+1)}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\frac{\bigl(S_{K_{j}}^{1}(n)-S_{K_{j}}^{2}(n)\bigr)^{2}}{K_{j}\log(n)},

(1.3)

for
K_{j}:=2^{j}K
and large enough
K
and
\ell
.

To explain why this statistic is separating, first assume that the labels of the vertices in the sets
A_{K_{j}}^{i}(n)
,
j=0,\ldots,\ell
,
i=1,2
, are
known
(just like in the discussion at the beginning of Section
1.3
), and
that these sets are
fixed
for all large enough
n
.
It is then possible to estimate the mean and variance of the separating statistic (
1.3
), and, if there is enough overlap between the sets
A_{K_{j}}^{1}(n)
and
A_{K_{j}}^{2}(n)
, we expect the mean of the separating statistic to be smaller for the correlated case. This is because in the independent model,
\Cov(S_{K_{j}}^{1}(n),S_{K_{j}}^{2}(n))=0
,
while for the
\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)
model,
\Cov(S_{K_{j}}^{1}(n),S_{K_{j}}^{2}(n))=\Theta\bigl(\alpha\lvert A_{K_{j}}^{1}(n)\cap A_{K_{j}}^{2}(n)\rvert\log(n)\bigr)
.
Assuming further that
\lvert A_{K_{j}}^{1}(n)\cap A_{K_{j}}^{2}(n)\rvert\geq 0.2K_{j}
,
Lemma
3.1

below shows that
under the independent model, the mean of a modification of the separating statistic (which can be shown to be close to the original) is at least
(2-0.01\alpha)
; see (
3.3
);
while under the correlated
\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}
model,
the mean is no more than
(2-0.1\alpha)
; see (
3.4
). The lemma also gives bounds on the variance under both models, and Chebyshev-type probability bounds on the probability of deviation from the mean in (
3.7
) and (
3.8
).
Here we can also see that the scaling choice of
K_{j}=K2^{j}
is crucial for these deviation probabilities to be small for large
\ell
.
It is important to note that while these calculations rely on the vertex labels, the resulting bounds only rely on the assumption that the sets
A_{K_{j}}^{i}(n)
are eventually fixed and have sufficient overlap for
i=1,2
.

Given this setup, the high-level structure of the proof is to show that with good probability the sets

A_{K_{j}}^{i}(n)
, for
j=0,\ldots,\ell
and
i=1,2
,
are fixed for all
n
large enough,
and that there is enough overlap between them. That the sets are eventually fixed follows directly from Theorem 2 of
Jog and Loh 2018
(recorded below as Theorem
3.7
),
where this property is referred to as
persistence
and is used in root-finding algorithms; see also
Banerjee and Bhamidi 2022
.
That there is sufficient overlap is the content of Corollary
1.6
, which follows from Theorem
1.5
; see Section
1.2
.

Finally, the reason that we are not able to use this statistic to
distinguish between
\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)
and
\alpha^{\prime}{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)
for all
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in[0,1]
with
\alpha\neq\alpha^{\prime}
is that we would need a sharper version of Corollary
1.6
with precise estimates on the sizes of the sets
|A_{K}^{1}(n)\cap A_{K}^{2}(n)|
in terms of
\alpha
, in order to control the mean of the separating statistic (
1.3
).

1.3.2
Estimating the correlation parameter
\mathbf{\alpha}

For our more general result, we need some notation to describe how to define our consistent estimator of
\alpha
. For distinct vertices
u,v
of a tree
T_{n}^{i}
, we write
K_{v}\left(T_{n}^{i}\setminus\{u\}\right)
for the subtree that contains
v
when removing
u
and all of its edges from
T_{n}^{i}
. For a tree
T_{n}^{i}
and vertices
u,v\in T_{n}^{i}
, we write
u\sim_{i}v
if there is an edge between
u
and
v
in the tree
T_{n}^{i}
. For a vertex
u
of a tree
T_{n}^{i}
, define

\widehat{U}_{\ell,j}^{T_{n}^{i}}(u)=\sum_{v\in T_{n}^{i}}\mathbbm{1}\left\{v\sim_{i}u,\left|K_{v}\left(T_{n}^{i}\setminus\{u\}\right)\right|\in\left(\frac{n}{e^{j}},\frac{n}{e^{\ell}}\right]\right\},

(1.4)

as the number of neighbors
v
of
u
where the number of vertices of
K_{v}\left(T_{n}^{i}\setminus\{u\}\right)
falls in the interval
(ne^{-j},ne^{-\ell}]
. Note that for each vertex
u
, this statistic is observable from the unlabeled tree. We show below in Lemma
4.1
that a consistent estimator of
2(1-\alpha)
is

\min_{u_{1}\in A_{K}^{1}(n),u_{2}\in A_{K}^{2}(n)}\frac{1}{k^{3}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\widehat{U}_{jk^{2},(j+1)k^{2}}^{T_{n}^{1}}(u_{1})-\widehat{U}_{jk^{2},(j+1)k^{2}}^{T_{n}^{2}}(u_{2})\right)^{2}

(1.5)

for an appropriate choice of
K=K(n)
and
k=k(n)
.
To explain how this statistic is consistent, consider a vertex

u\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
for some large but fixed
M
. Exactly one neighbor
v
of
u
has a smaller label than
u
, and for this neighbor
v
, it can be shown that
\left|K_{v}\left(T_{n}^{i}\setminus\{u\}\right)\right|\geq n/M^{3}
with good probability.
For any other neighbor
v
of
u
,
K_{v}\left(T_{n}^{i}\setminus\{u\}\right)
is equal to the
fringe subtree
of
v
in
T_{n}^{i}
, which consists of the subtree of
T_{n}^{i}
induced by all vertices whose path to
0
contains
v
(this includes
v
); see Definition
2.2
. Writing
F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)
for the size of the fringe subtree of
v
in
T_{n}^{i}
, it is then possible to judiciously choose
\ell,j
so that with good probability,

\widehat{U}_{\ell,j}^{T_{n}^{i}}(u)\approx U_{\ell,j}^{T_{n}^{i}}(u):=\sum_{v=M+1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\left\{v\sim_{i}u,F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)\in\left(\frac{n}{e^{j}},\frac{n}{e^{\ell}}\right]\right\}.

The fringe subtree of vertex
v\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
evolves as the number of red balls in a standard Pólya urn started with
1
red ball and
v
blue balls, so
up to first order in large
n
and
v
, we have that

F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)\approx n/v,

and so the size of the fringe subtree roughly identifies
v
. Using this identification, we can show that for appropriate
\ell,j
,

U_{\ell,j}^{T_{n}^{i}}(u)\approx\sum_{v=e^{\ell}}^{e^{j}-1}\mathbbm{1}\left\{v\sim_{i}u\right\}.

Putting this all together, for any fixed

u_{1},u_{2}\in\{0,\ldots,M\}
,
we have that

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{U}_{jk^{2},(j+1)k^{2}}^{T_{n}^{1}}(u_{1})-\widehat{U}_{jk^{2},(j+1)k^{2}}^{T_{n}^{2}}(u_{2})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\left({U}_{jk^{2},(j+1)k^{2}}^{T_{n}^{1}}(u_{1})-{U}_{jk^{2},(j+1)k^{2}}^{T_{n}^{2}}(u_{2})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\left(\sum_{v=e^{jk^{2}}}^{e^{(j+1)k^{2}}-1}\left(\mathbbm{1}\left\{v\sim_{1}u_{1}\right\}-\mathbbm{1}\left\{v\sim_{2}u_{2}\right\}\right)\right)^{2},

where the summands on the right-hand side are independent, mean zero with

\Var\left(\mathbbm{1}\left\{v\sim_{1}u_{1}\right\}-\mathbbm{1}\left\{v\sim_{2}u_{2}\right\}\right)\sim\frac{2(1-\alpha\mathbbm{1}\{u_{1}=u_{2}\})}{v}.

Thus the mean of our statistic identifies
2(1-\alpha)
as long as
u_{1}=u_{2}
. Furthermore, we show that for
k
large, the statistic is close in probability to its mean; see Proposition
4.3
, which is the source of our choice of scaling of order
e^{jk^{2}}
in the definition of
\widehat{U}_{jk^{2},(j+1)k^{2}}
.
Noticing that the mean is smaller when the vertices are the same, we should then take the minimum over a fixed set of vertices of each tree that have at least one vertex in common. As with our previous proof, this is accomplished with the sets
A_{K}^{1}
and
A_{K}^{2}
, which, with good probability, can be thought of as fixed after some large time
M
, using
(
Jog and Loh 2017
, Theorem 2)
, and have non-empty intersection, by Corollary
1.6
.

1.4
Open problems

We conclude the introduction by collecting open problems and future directions of research that stem from this work.

•

(Rates of convergence)

What is the rate of convergence of
\widehat{\alpha}_{n}
to
\alpha
in Theorem
1.2
? More generally, if we have a sequence of parameters
\alpha_{n}\to\alpha
as
n\to\infty
, how quickly can
\lvert\alpha_{n}-\alpha\rvert\to 0
with also
\mathrm{TV}(\alpha_{n}{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n),\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n))\to 1
? The best possible rate of convergence that would come from our approach is
1/\mathrm{polylog}(n)
, which is likely not optimal.

•

(Correlated centrality sets)

As previously mentioned, our first approach to separating the models with
\alpha\not=\alpha^{\prime}
in general fails because our probabilistic bounds on
\left|A_{K}^{1}\cap A_{K}^{2}\right|/K
are independent of
\alpha
.
Can this quantity be more precisely understood?
Is there a limit

\lim_{K\to\infty}\left|A_{K}^{1}\cap A_{K}^{2}\right|/K
?
These questions are interesting in their own right in the context of understanding centrality, and could provide alternative proofs to some of our results.

•

(Locations of central vertices)

Theorem
1.5
shows that for
c>1
and appropriately large
K
and
n
,

\frac{|A_{\lceil cK\rceil}(n)\cap\{0,\ldots,K-1\}|}{K}\geq c(1-e^{-1/c})-\delta

with probability close to one. Is the lower bound optimal? What else can be said about the ratio? A tantalizing conjecture is that it converges to
c(1-e^{-1/c})
as
K\to\infty
.

•

(Other statistics)

Our statistics for detection and estimation all involve a two step procedure of
(1) fixing a set of vertices based on centrality and
(2) computing local statistics related to those vertices,
and then, importantly, doing this across multiple time scales.
It is natural to ask if there are simpler statistics (e.g., ones that are not multi-scale in nature) that can detect correlation or consistently estimate the correlation parameter.

Our first approach to the detection problem was to build a statistic from the joint empirical degree distributions. Marginally, these evolve according to a generalized Pólya urn, and, as shown by
Janson 2005
, their centered and scaled limits are jointly Gaussian.
The joint empirical degree distributions of correlated UA trees evolve according to a stochastic recursion, and applying central limit theorems in that setting (e.g.,
Fabian 1968
and
Hernandez and Spall 2019
) yields that the centered and scaled joint degree counts are asymptotically jointly Gaussian with a covariance depending on
\alpha
. While we were able to
follow this program to show that

\mathrm{TV}\left(\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}\left(n\right),\mathrm{UA}(n)^{\otimes 2}\right)

is asymptotically bounded away from
0
(specifically, by counting leaves in the two trees), we were not able to show that it converges to
1
. See Appendix
A
for more details.

Another approach to the detection problem is to consider the size of the largest common subtree of the two trees. However, even studying this statistic for two independent UA trees is interesting and challenging; see the forthcoming work by

Bäumler et al. 2026+
.

•

(Properties of
\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}
)
The UA model (also called the random recursive tree) is a beautiful mathematical object with a long history and connections to other areas, such as extreme value theory, with many fine properties understood and studied to this day (e.g.,

Björklund et al. 2025
,

Eslava 2022
,

Eslava et al. 2023
,

Janson 2005
;
Janson 2019
,

Lodewijks 2024
,

Meir and Moon 1978
,

Pittel 1994
). Deriving analogs of single-tree properties for the
\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}
model is an interesting avenue of future research.

•

(Other models of correlated networks)

Studying the questions of this paper in different models with a similar flavor is also of interest. A concrete model is the weighted random recursive tree (see, e.g.,
Borovkov and Vatutin 2006
). Here vertex
i
arrives with a (random or deterministic) weight
w_{i}
, and vertex
n>i
attaches to vertex
i
with probability
w_{i}/\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}w_{j}
. The case where the weights are all equal is the UA tree. There are several ways to introduce correlation into this model. First, the joint weights
\{(w_{i}^{1},w_{i}^{2})\}_{i\geq 0}
could be i.i.d. random but
w_{i}^{1}
and
w_{i}^{2}
could be correlated in some way, and the edge-choice mechanism then conditionally independent given the weights. Another option—analogous to the model studied in this paper—is to have fixed weights, but the edge choice mechanisms are aligned with probability
\alpha\in[0,1]
, and independent if not aligned.

Another natural extension of correlated UA trees is to consider correlated UA
graphs
, where now each arriving vertex has
m>1
edges connecting independently and uniformly to existing vertices; here
m
may be deterministic or random.
We believe that our high-level approach to the detection problem should extend to this setting, with some modifications.
While the notion of centrality we use to identify early vertices is tree-specific,
we conjecture that
replacing it with some other statistic that identifies early vertices and studying analogous subsequent local statistics such as degrees
should yield similar results.
For example,
Briend et al. 2023
use “double cycles” instead of centrality to identify early vertices in the setting of UA graphs.
On the other hand, the local statistic of our consistent estimator of
\alpha
is tree-specific, and it is not clear how to extend it to graphs.

Finding early vertices

The goal of this section is to prove Corollary
1.6
via the following result, which both implies, and is a more detailed version of Theorem

from Section

.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
T_{n}\sim\mathrm{UA}(n)
and recall that
A_{K}:=A_{K}(n)
denotes the set of the
K
most central vertices in
T_{n}
.
There exists a universal constant
C<\infty

such that
for every
K\in\N
,
c>1
, and
\delta\in(0,c-1)
,
the following holds for all large enough
n\geq n_{0}(K,\delta,c)
:

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigl\lvert A_{\lceil cK\rceil}(n)\cap\bigl\{0,\ldots,K-1\bigr\}\bigr\rvert\geq\bigl(c(1-e^{-1/c})-\delta\bigr)K\right)\geq 1-C\left(\frac{c\log(cK)}{\delta^{2}K}+(0.7)^{\delta K/2}\right).

As previously mentioned,
the strategy to prove Theorem
2.1
is
to relate centrality to certain subtree sizes. For this, we introduce a kind of inverse or negative centrality of a vertex
v
in a tree
T
on
\lvert T\rvert
vertices, which is simply

\Psi_{T}(v):=\lvert T\rvert-\psi_{T}(v).

In words,
\Psi_{T}(v)
is the number of vertices left in
T
when removing the largest component subtree formed by removing vertex
v
and all edges connecting to it.
The reason for looking at this quantity over
\psi_{T}(v)
is that in a rooted tree
T
, if the largest subtree is the one containing the root, then
\Psi_{T}(v)
is exactly the size of the so-called
fringe subtree
, which we now define.

Definition 2.2

(Fringe subtree size)
.

The
fringe subtree
of a vertex
v
of
T
having root
0
is the subtree of
T
induced by the collection of vertices whose path to the root
0
contains
v
(note that this set includes
v
). We denote the number of vertices in the fringe subtree of
T
by
F_{T}(v)
.

With this heuristic in mind, we introduce the following (deterministic) definition of trees
where vertices having “large” fringe trees appear early on.

Definition 2.3
.

For
K\in\N
,
\delta\in(0,1)
, and
c_{1}\geq 1
, we say a tree
T
on vertices
\{0,\ldots,n\}
is
(K,\delta,c_{1})
-good if

(i)

\sum_{\delta K/2-1<k\leq n}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{F_{T}(k)\geq 0.4n\right\}}=0
,

(ii)

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{F_{T}(k)\geq n/(c_{1}K)\right\}}\leq\lceil(c_{1}+\delta)K\rceil-\delta K/2
, and

(iii)

\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{F_{T}(k)\geq n/(c_{1}K)\right\}}\geq(c_{1}(1-e^{-1/c_{1}})-\delta/2)K
.

The way to understand the definition is that if the fringe subtree of a vertex is big enough, then it will be central. Then
(ii)
caps the total number of vertices with large fringe subtrees, and
(iii)
implies that many vertices early in the tree will have such large fringe subtrees. (The first point controls differences between
\Psi_{T}
and
F_{T}
.)
In particular, the definition allows us to quantify
where the most central vertices are located.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
T
is a
(K,\delta,c_{1})
-good tree on
\{0,\ldots,n\}
with centrality sets denoted by
A_{r}
,
r=1,\ldots,n
, then

\bigl\lvert A_{\lceil(c_{1}+\delta)K\rceil}\cap\bigl\{0,\ldots,K-1\bigr\}\bigr\rvert\geq\bigl(c_{1}(1-e^{-1/c_{1}})-\delta\bigr)K.

To apply Lemma
2.4
to a
\mathrm{UA}
tree, we lower bound the probability that a
\mathrm{UA}
tree is
(K,\delta,c_{1})
-good. For this, we note that for a
\mathrm{UA}
tree
T_{n}
, the quantity
(F_{T_{n}}(j))_{n\geq j}
evolves as the number of blue balls in a classical Pólya urn which starts (at step
n=j
) with 1 blue ball and
j
white balls. (A standard Pólya urn means that at each step, a ball from the urn is chosen uniformly at random and returned along with another of the same color.) Thus we can estimate the mean of the quantities appearing in Definition
2.3
by estimating probabilities of such Pólya urns, which we do using de Finetti/beta-binomial representations and couplings. We can further estimate variances by using that for vertices
j<l
,
(F_{T_{n}}(j),F_{T_{n}}(l))_{n\geq l}
evolves like a multicolor Pólya urn started (at step

) with
l+1
total balls.
See Section
2.2
for details.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
T_{n}
be a vertex-labeled
\mathrm{UA}
tree and fix parameters

,
\delta\in(0,1)
, and

. Then there exists a universal constant

Given these lemmas, we can prove Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem

.

Applying Lemmas

and

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\biggl(\bigl\lvert A_{\lceil cK\rceil}(n)\cap\bigl\{0,\ldots,K-1\bigr\}\bigr\rvert\geq\bigl((c-\delta)&(1-e^{-\frac{1}{c-\delta}})-\delta\bigr)K\biggr)\\

.

Proof.

n-k
draws and replacements. We use the de Finetti representation
F_{T_{n}}(k)-1\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n-k,\eta_{k})
, where
\eta_{k}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1,k)
; see
(
Freedman 1965
, Theorems 2.1 and 2.2)
. Conditioning on the value of
\eta_{k}
and using stochastic monotonicity of the binomial distribution implies that for any threshold value
t
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(F_{T_{n}}(k)\geq\beta n)\leq\mathbb{P}(F_{T_{n}}(k)\geq\beta n|\eta_{k}\leq t)+\mathbb{P}(\eta_{k}\geq t)\leq\mathbb{P}(B_{n,t}\geq\beta n)+(1-t)^{k},

where
B_{n,t}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n,t)
. Azuma’s inequality implies that for
0<t<\beta
we have

\mathbb{P}(B_{n,t}\geq\beta n)\leq\exp\Bigl\{-\frac{(\beta n-tn)^{2}}{2n}\Bigr\}.

Choosing
t=\beta-s
gives the first inequality (
2.4
).

For (
2.5
),
Lemma
5.2
below with
r=1
,
b=k
and
m=n-k
implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(F_{T_{n}}(k)\geq\beta n)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\eta_{k}\geq\frac{\beta n+2(k+1)}{n-k}\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\eta_{k}\geq\frac{\beta+2\gamma+2/n}{1-\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle=\biggl(1-\frac{\beta+2\gamma+2/n}{1-\gamma}\biggr)^{k},

where the second inequality is because of monotonicity in
k
.
∎

We use Lemma
2.6
to bound moments of the quantities related to the
(K,\delta,c_{1})
-good Definition
2.3
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
T_{n}\sim\mathrm{UA}(n)
. For any
\delta\in(0,0.01)
,

c_{1}>1
, and
K<n^{1/4}/c_{1}
, there is a universal constant
C
, and an
n(K,\delta,c_{1})
depending only on
(K,\delta,c_{1})
such that for all
n\geq n(K,\delta,c_{1})
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\delta K/2-1<k\leq n}\mathbb{P}(F_{T_{n}}(k)\geq 0.4n)

\displaystyle\leq C(0.7)^{\delta K},

(2.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbb{P}(F_{T_{n}}(k)\geq n/(c_{1}K))

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(1+\tfrac{\delta}{4c_{1}}\bigr)c_{1}K,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{P}(F_{T_{n}}(k)\geq n/(c_{1}K))

\displaystyle\geq(c_{1}(1-e^{-1/c_{1}})-\delta/4)K,

(2.8)

and for any
L\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,n\}
, we have

\Var\biggl(\sum_{k=0}^{L}\mathbbm{1}_{\{F_{T_{n}}(k)\geq n/(c_{1}K)\}}\biggr)\leq C\bigl(c_{1}K\log(c_{1}K)\bigr).

(2.9)

Proof.

For (
2.6
), we use (
2.4
) with
\beta=0.4
and
s=0.1
to find

\displaystyle\sum_{k=\delta K/2-1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(F_{T_{n}}(k)\geq 0.4n)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=\delta K/2-1}^{n}\bigl((0.7)^{k}+e^{-0.005n}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq 0.7^{\delta K/2-1}\frac{1}{0.3}+ne^{-0.005n}

\displaystyle\leq C\,(0.7)^{\delta K}

for some constant
C
, and using that
n
is large enough.

For (
2.7
), we use (
2.4
) with
\beta=1/(c_{1}K)
and
s=n^{-1/4}
(noting that
\beta>s
by assumption) to find

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbb{P}(F_{T_{n}}(k)\geq n/(c_{1}K))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n}\bigl((1-(c_{1}K)^{-1}+n^{-1/4})^{k}+e^{-\sqrt{n}/2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(c_{1}K)^{-1}-n^{-1/4}}+(n+1)e^{-\sqrt{n}/2}

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(1+\tfrac{\delta}{4c_{1}}\bigr)c_{1}K,

for
n
large enough.

For (
2.8
), we use (
2.5
). Set
\gamma
small enough and
n
large enough (depending on
\delta
) so that

1-\frac{(c_{1}K)^{-1}+2\gamma+2/n}{1-\gamma}\geq 1-\frac{1+\iota}{c_{1}K},

(2.10)

where
\iota
is a parameter that can be chosen sufficiently small later. With this choice of
\gamma
and possibly enlarging
n
so that
K<\gamma n
, (
2.5
) with
\beta=1/(c_{1}K)
and (
2.10
) implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{P}(F_{T_{n}}(k)\geq n/(c_{1}K))

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\left(1-\frac{1+\iota}{c_{1}K}\right)^{k}=\frac{1-\left(1-\frac{1+\iota}{c_{1}K}\right)^{K}}{1-\left(1-\frac{1+\iota}{c_{1}K}\right)}

\displaystyle\geq c_{1}K\left(\frac{1-e^{-\frac{(1+\iota)}{c_{1}}}}{1+\iota}\right)\geq(c_{1}(1-e^{-1/c_{1}})-\delta/4)K,

which follows by shrinking
\iota
, and then choosing
n
large enough.

Next we bound the variance (
2.9
), and to save space we denote
I_{k}:=\mathbbm{1}_{\{F_{T_{n}}(k)\geq n/(c_{1}K)\}}
.
We compute

\Var\biggl(\sum_{k=0}^{L}I_{k}\biggr)=\Var\biggl(\sum_{k=1}^{L}I_{k}\biggr)=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\Var(I_{k})+2\sum_{1\leq k<l\leq L}\Cov(I_{k},I_{l}).

(2.11)

For the first term of (
2.11
), we have the bound

\sum_{k=1}^{L}\Var(I_{k})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{L}\mathbb{P}(F_{T_{n}}(k)\geq n/(c_{1}K))\leq 2c_{1}K,

(2.12)

where we used (
2.7
) in the last inequality.

Considering the covariance terms
\Cov(I_{k},I_{l})
for
k<l
,
let
E_{k,l}
denote the event that vertex
l
connects to the fringe subtree of
k
in
T_{l}
(and is hence in the fringe subtree for all time). Two key facts to note are that 1) the event
E_{k,l}
is independent of
\{F_{T_{n}}(l)\geq n/(c_{1}K)\}
(since the two events are functions of disjoint collections of the edge variables of
T_{n}
), and 2) on the event
E_{k,l}^{c}
, the process
(F_{T_{m}}(k),F_{T_{m}}(l))_{m\geq l}
evolves like a three color standard Pólya urn, and so
F_{T_{n}}(k)
and
F_{T_{n}}(l)
are negatively related, which implies the conditional covariance of
I_{k}
and
I_{l}
is non-positive. Thus we have

\displaystyle\Cov(I_{k},I_{l})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[I_{l}(I_{k}-\mathbb{E}[I_{k}])\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbbm{1}_{E_{k,l}}(I_{k}-\mathbb{E}[I_{k}])|I_{l}=1\bigr]\mathbb{E}[I_{l}]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbbm{1}_{E_{k,l}^{c}}I_{l}(I_{k}-\mathbb{E}[I_{k}])\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl(E_{k,l}|I_{l}=1\bigr)\mathbb{E}[I_{l}]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[I_{l}(I_{k}-\mathbb{E}[I_{k}])|E_{k,l}^{c}\bigr]\mathbb{P}(E_{k,l})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(E_{k,l})\mathbb{E}[I_{l}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k}\bigl((1-(c_{1}K)^{-1}+n^{-1/4})^{l}+e^{-\sqrt{n}/2}\bigr),

where the second inequality is because of the independence and negative conditional covariance already mentioned, and the third uses (
2.4
) with
\beta=1/(c_{1}K)
and
s=n^{-1/4}
. Thus we find that the covariance term of (
2.11
) is, for
n
large enough, upper bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq k<l\leq L}\Cov(I_{k},I_{l})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq k<l\leq L}\frac{1}{k}\bigl((1-(c_{1}K)^{-1}+n^{-1/4})^{l}+e^{-\sqrt{n}/2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{L}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{l=k+1}^{L}\bigl(1-(2c_{1}K)^{-1}\bigr)^{l}+L^{2}e^{-\sqrt{n}/2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{L}\frac{1}{k}(2c_{1}K)\bigl(1-(2c_{1}K)^{-1}\bigr)^{k}+n^{2}e^{-\sqrt{n}/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\bigl(c_{1}K\log(c_{1}K)\bigr),

which together with (
2.12
) implies (
2.9
).
∎

We can use the moment information in Lemma
2.7
to prove Lemma
2.5
.

Proof of Lemma
2.5
.

We show that the probability of each of the complements of the three events in Definition
2.3
is of order at most
\log(K)/K
, and the result then follows from a union bound.
For
(i)
, Markov’s inequality and (
2.6
) imply

\mathbb{P}\biggl(\sum_{\delta K/2<k\leq n}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{F_{T_{n}}(k)\geq 0.4n\right\}}\geq 1\biggr)\leq\sum_{\delta K/2<k\leq n}\mathbb{P}(F_{T_{n}}(k)\geq 0.4n)\leq C(0.7)^{\delta K}.

For
(ii)
, the bounds (
2.7
) and (
2.9
) on the mean and variance combined with Chebyshev’s inequality imply

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\biggl(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{F_{T}(k)\geq n/(c_{1}K)\right\}}>\lceil(c_{1}+\delta)K\rceil-\delta K/2\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\bigl(c_{1}K\log(c_{1}K)\bigr)}{\bigl(\lceil(c_{1}+\delta)K\rceil-\delta K/2-(1+\delta/(4c_{1}))c_{1}K\bigr)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{16C\bigl(c_{1}\log(c_{1}K)\bigr)}{\delta^{2}K}.

Similarly, for
(iii)
, the bounds (
2.8
) and (
2.9
) on the mean and variance combined with Chebyshev’s inequality imply

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\biggl(\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{F_{T}(k)\geq n/(c_{1}K)\right\}}<(c_{1}(1-e^{-1/c_{1}})-\delta/2)K\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{16C\bigl(c_{1}\log(c_{1}K)\bigr)}{\delta^{2}K}.\qed

3
Distinguishing independent trees from correlated ones

In this section, we show that the total variation distance between a pair of independent
\mathrm{UA}
trees and a pair of correlated ones tends to one, as the number of vertices tends to infinity. As previously described, if we knew the labels of
A_{K}^{i}(n)
for
i=1,2
, and the overlap between them, then the mean and variance of the separating statistic could be computed. We first derive the appropriate results in this setting in Lemma
3.1
, and then use them in
Section
3.2
to show how to distinguish.

3.1
Mean and variance of sums of degrees of sets

Fix
\ell\in\N
and an increasing sequence
0<K_{0}<K_{1}<\cdots K_{\ell}\leq N
of integers, and let
(M_{K_{j}})_{j=0}^{\ell}
and
(\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}})_{j=0}^{\ell}
be collections of subsets of
\{0,\ldots,N\}
such that for all
j=0,1,\ldots,\ell
,

\begin{split}|M_{K_{j}}|=|\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}|=K_{j},\text{ and }|M_{K_{j}}\cap\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}|\geq 0.2K_{j}.\end{split}

(3.1)

Let
X_{N+1},X_{N+2},X_{N+3},\ldots
, and
Z_{N+1},Z_{N+2},Z_{N+3},\ldots
, be independent random variables such that
X_{i}
and
Z_{i}
are uniformly distributed on
\{0,\ldots,i-1\}
. Further let
(U_{i})_{i\in\N}
be i.i.d. random variables that have a Bernoulli
(\alpha)
-distribution and are independent of
X_{N+1},X_{N+2},X_{N+3},\ldots
, and
Z_{N+1},Z_{N+2},Z_{N+3},\ldots
. Define
Y_{N+1},Y_{N+2},Y_{N+3},\ldots
by

Y_{i}=\begin{cases}Z_{i}&\text{ if }U_{i}=0,\\
X_{i}&\text{ if }U_{i}=1.\end{cases}

Note that the random variable
Y_{i}
is a mixture of
X_{i}
and
Z_{i}
. For
n\geq N+1
, define the random variables

\begin{split}S_{j}^{X}(n)=\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{a}\in M_{K_{j}}\}},\ \ S_{j}^{Y}(n)=\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y_{a}\in\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}\}},\ \ S_{j}^{Z}(n)=\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{a}\in\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}\}}.\end{split}

(3.2)

The main result of this section is the following.

Lemma 3.1
.

Recall the random variables
S_{j}^{X}(n),S_{j}^{Y}(n)
, and
S_{j}^{Z}(n)
,
j=0,1,\ldots,\ell
just defined, and assume they satisfy (
3.1
). Then there is an
m\in\N
depending only on
\ell,N,(K_{j})_{j=0}^{\ell}
, and
\alpha
, such that for
all
0\leq j\leq i\leq\ell
and
n>m
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(S_{j}^{X}(n)-S_{j}^{Z}(n)\right)^{2}\right]\geq\left(2-0.01\alpha\right)K_{j}\log(n),

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(S_{j}^{X}(n)-S_{j}^{Y}(n)\right)^{2}\right]\leq\left(2-0.1\alpha\right)K_{j}\log(n),

(3.4)

\displaystyle\Cov\left(\left(S_{j}^{X}(n)-S_{j}^{Z}(n)\right)^{2},\left(S_{i}^{X}(n)-S_{i}^{Z}(n)\right)^{2}\right)\leq 10K_{j}^{2}\log(n)^{2},\text{ and }

(3.5)

\displaystyle\Cov\left(\left(S_{j}^{X}(n)-S_{j}^{Y}(n)\right)^{2},\left(S_{i}^{X}(n)-S_{i}^{Y}(n)\right)^{2}\right)\leq 10K_{j}^{2}\log(n)^{2}.

(3.6)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\biggl(\frac{1}{\ell+1}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\frac{\bigl(S_{j}^{X}(n)-S_{j}^{Z}(n)\bigr)^{2}}{K_{j}\log(n)}\leq 2-0.02\alpha\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{20\sum_{0\leq j\leq i\leq\ell}(K_{j}/K_{i})}{(\ell+1)^{2}(0.01\alpha)^{2}},

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\biggl(\frac{1}{\ell+1}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\frac{\bigl(S_{j}^{X}(n)-S_{j}^{Y}(n)\bigr)^{2}}{K_{j}\log(n)}\geq 2-0.09\alpha\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{20\sum_{0\leq j\leq i\leq\ell}(K_{j}/K_{i})}{(\ell+1)^{2}(0.01\alpha)^{2}},

(3.8)

and for any
t>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(\bigl\lvert S_{j}^{X}(n)-S_{j}^{Z}(n)\bigr\rvert\geq t\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\log(n)}{t^{2}}K_{j},

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(\bigl\lvert S_{j}^{X}(n)-S_{j}^{Y}(n)\bigr\rvert\geq t\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\log(n)}{t^{2}}K_{j}.

(3.10)

The left hand side of the lower bound (
3.3
) does not depend on
\alpha
, and the right hand side could replace
0.01\alpha
with any small enough constant, but we prefer to state things this way for easier comparison to the upper bound (
3.4
).

Before proving the lemma, we record a useful result that follows easily by direct computation.

Proposition 3.2
.

If
X,Y,\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y}
are square-integrable and mean-zero random variables such that
(X,\widetilde{X})
is independent of
(Y,\widetilde{Y})
, then

\Cov\bigl(XY,\widetilde{X}\widetilde{Y}\bigr)=\Cov\bigl(X,\widetilde{X}\bigr)\Cov\bigl(Y,\widetilde{Y}\bigr).

(3.11)

If, in addition,
Z
is a square integrable random variable such that
Y
is independent of
(X,Z)
, then

\Cov(X,YZ)=0.

(3.12)

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

We start with computing
\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\left(S_{j}^{X}(n)-S_{j}^{Z}(n)\right)^{2}\Bigr]
. Since the random variables
(X_{a})_{a\geq N+1}
are independent, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[S_{j}^{X}(n)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{a}\in M_{K_{j}}\right)=\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\frac{K_{j}}{a},

\displaystyle\Var\left(S_{j}^{X}(n)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\Var\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{a}\in M_{K_{j}}\}}\right)=\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\left(\frac{K_{j}}{a}-\left(\frac{K_{j}}{a}\right)^{2}\right)\approx K_{j}\log(n),

where we have used from (
3.1
) that
|M_{K_{j}}|=K_{j}
.
Since
|M_{K_{j}}|=|\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}|
, we see that
S_{j}^{X}(n),S_{j}^{Z}(n)
, and
S_{j}^{Y}(n)
are identically distributed, and in particular, they all have the same mean and variance as above. Since
S_{j}^{X}(n)
and
S_{j}^{Z}(n)
are independent, we easily find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\left(S_{j}^{X}(n)-S_{j}^{Z}(n)\right)^{2}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=2\Var\left(S_{j}^{X}(n)\right)=2\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\left(\frac{K_{j}}{a}-\left(\frac{K_{j}}{a}\right)^{2}\right)

(3.13)

\displaystyle\geq 2\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\frac{K_{j}}{a}-5K_{j}^{2}\geq\left(2-0.01\alpha\right)K_{j}\log(n),

for
n
large enough, noting that we have introduced
0.01\alpha
in line with the remark following the statement of Lemma
3.1
. This shows inequality (
3.3
).

For (
3.4
), the sets
M_{K_{j}}
and
\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}
satisfy
|M_{K_{j}}|=|\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}|=K_{j}
and
|M_{K_{j}}\cap\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}|\geq 0.2K_{j}
so that the symmetric difference of the sets
M_{K_{j}}\Delta\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}=\bigl(M_{K_{j}}\cup\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}\bigr)\setminus\bigl(M_{K_{j}}\cap\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}\bigr)
satisfies
|M_{K_{j}}\Delta\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}|\leq 1.8K_{j}
.
Using this, we can upper bound the second moment of
S_{j}^{X}(n)-S_{j}^{Y}(n)
by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(S_{j}^{X}(n)-S_{j}^{Y}(n)\right)^{2}\right]=\Var\left(\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{a}\in M_{K_{j}}\}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y_{a}\in\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}\}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\Var\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{a}\in M_{K_{j}}\}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y_{a}\in\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}\}}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{a}\in M_{K_{j}}\}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{a}\in\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}\}}\right)^{2}\right]+(1-\alpha)\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{a}\in M_{K_{j}}\}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{a}\in\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}\}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{a}\in M_{K_{j}}\Delta\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}\}}\right)^{2}\right]+(1-\alpha)\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\Var\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{a}\in M_{K_{j}}\}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z_{a}\in\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}\}}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\frac{\bigl\lvert M_{K_{j}}\Delta\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}\bigr\rvert}{a}+2(1-\alpha)\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\left(\frac{K_{j}}{a}-\frac{K_{j}^{2}}{a^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha 1.8\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\frac{K_{j}}{a}+2(1-\alpha)\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\frac{K_{j}}{a}=\left(2-0.2\alpha\right)\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\frac{K_{j}}{a}\leq\left(2-0.1\alpha\right)K_{j}\log(n),

for
n
large enough, which proves (
3.4
).

Next, we estimate the covariances of
\bigl(\bigl(S_{j}^{X}(n)-S_{j}^{Y}(n)\bigr)^{2}\bigr)_{j\in\{0,\ldots,\ell\}}
to prove inequality (
3.6
). The proof does not depend on the fact that
\alpha>0
, and thus the exact same proof (with
Y
replaced by
Z
) also shows inequality (
3.5
).
First define the random variables
R_{a}:=\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{a}\in M_{K_{j}}\}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y_{a}\in\widetilde{M}_{K_{j}}\}}
,
a\geq N+1
, which are independent and satisfy
R_{a}\in\{-1,0,1\}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{a}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[R_{a}^{3}\right]=0,\text{ and }\mathbb{E}\left[R_{a}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[R_{a}^{4}\right]\leq 2\mathbb{P}\left(X_{a}\in M_{K_{j}}\right)\leq\frac{2K_{j}}{a}.

(3.14)

We define their index-
i
counterpart random variables
T_{a}:=\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{a}\in M_{K_{i}}\}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y_{a}\in\widetilde{M}_{K_{i}}\}}
for
a=N+1,\ldots,n
, so that

(S_{j}^{X}(n)-S_{j}^{Y}(n))=\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}R_{a},\ \text{ and }\ (S_{i}^{X}(n)-S_{i}^{Y}(n))=\sum_{c=N+1}^{n}T_{c}.

Now, we calculate the covariance of
\bigl(S_{j}^{X}(n)-S_{j}^{Y}(n)\bigr)^{2}
and
\bigl(S_{i}^{X}(n)-S_{i}^{Y}(n)\bigr)^{2}
for
i,j\in\{0,\ldots,\ell\}
with
j\leq i
.
Noting that
(R_{a},T_{a})_{a\geq N+1}
are independent mean-zero bivariate random vectors, we find that

\displaystyle\Cov\Bigl(\bigl(S_{j}^{X}(n)-S_{j}^{Y}(n)\bigr)^{2},\bigl(

\displaystyle S_{i}^{X}(n)-S_{i}^{Y}(n)\bigr)^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b=N+1}^{n}\sum_{\{c,d\}\cap\{a,b\}\not=\emptyset}\Cov(R_{a}R_{b},T_{c}T_{d})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\Cov(R_{a}^{2},T_{a}^{2})+2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\in\{N+1,\ldots,n\}:\\
a\neq b\end{subarray}}\Cov(R_{a}R_{b},T_{a}T_{b}),

where the second equality follows because

\Cov(R_{a}^{2},T_{a}T_{c})=0
for
c\not=a
, and

\Cov(R_{a}R_{b},T_{a}T_{c})=0
for
a\not=b
and
c\not\in\{a,b\}
(again by independence and zero means).
Using (
3.11
), we have that for
a\not=b
,

\Cov(R_{a}R_{b},T_{a}T_{b})=\Cov(R_{a},T_{a})\Cov(R_{b},T_{b})
, and, using (
3.14
) and
\lvert T_{a}\rvert\leq 1
, that

\displaystyle\lvert\Cov(R_{a},T_{a})\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert\mathbb{E}[R_{a}T_{a}]\rvert\leq\mathbb{E}[\lvert R_{a}\rvert]\leq 2\frac{K_{j}}{a},

\displaystyle\lvert\Cov(R_{a}^{2},T_{a}^{2})\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[R_{a}^{2}T_{a}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[R_{a}^{2}]\leq 2\frac{K_{j}}{a}.

Combining the previous two displays gives

\displaystyle\Cov\Bigl(

\displaystyle\bigl(S_{j}^{X}(n)-S_{j}^{Y}(n)\bigr)^{2},\bigl(S_{i}^{X}(n)-S_{i}^{Y}(n)\bigr)^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a=N+1}^{n}\Cov(R_{a}^{2},T_{a}^{2})+2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\in\{N+1,\ldots,n\}:\\

for large enough

X_{i}^{k}=X_{i}\in\{0,1\}
for any
k>0
, there is a universal (combinatorial) constant
c_{4}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X^{4}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i_{1}}X_{i_{2}}X_{i_{3}}X_{i_{4}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq c_{4}\biggl(\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i_{1}}\right]+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},i_{2}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:\\
i_{1}\neq i_{2}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i_{1}}X_{i_{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:\\
|\{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\}|=3\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i_{1}}X_{i_{2}}X_{i_{3}}\right]+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:\\
|\{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}\}|=4\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i_{1}}X_{i_{2}}X_{i_{3}}X_{i_{4}}\right]\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq c_{4}\,\widetilde{C}\biggl(\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i_{1}}\right]+\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i_{1}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i_{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i_{1}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i_{2}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i_{3}}\right]+\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i_{1}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i_{2}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i_{3}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i_{4}}\right]\biggr)

\displaystyle=c_{4}\,\widetilde{C}\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{4}\mathbb{E}[X]^{j}\biggr),

where the second inequality uses the hypothesis (
4.16
). The result follows
by taking
\widehat{C}=4c_{4}\,\widetilde{C}
.
∎

To apply the proposition, we need to verify (
4.16
) for the Bernoulli sums in our setting, and have good bounds on their means. For the latter we use the following result.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
T_{n}\sim\mathrm{UA}(n)
, let
u\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
and let
r\in\left[1,n\right]
. Then

\sum_{v=r}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(v\sim u,F_{T_{n}}(v)>\frac{n}{r}\right)\leq 2,

(4.17)

and

\sum_{v=u+1}^{r-1}\mathbb{P}\left(v\sim u,F_{T_{n}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{r}\right)\leq 1.

(4.18)

Proof.

We start with a proof of (
4.17
). Noting that
(F_{T_{n}}(v)-1)
is distributed as the number of times a red ball is chosen in a standard Pólya urn started with
1
red and
v
blue balls after taking
n-v
steps, exchangeability implies

\mathbb{E}\left[F_{T_{n}}(v)\right]=1+\frac{n-v}{v+1}=\frac{n+1}{v+1}.

Since the events
\{v\sim u\}
and
\left\{F_{T_{n}}(v)>\frac{n}{r}\right\}
are independent, an application of Markov’s inequality implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{v=r}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(v\sim u,F_{T_{n}}(v)>\frac{n}{r}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v=r}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(v\sim u\right)\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}}(v)>\frac{n}{r}\right)\leq\sum_{v=r}^{n}\frac{1}{v}\frac{\mathbb{E}[F_{T_{n}}(v)]}{n/r}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{v=r}^{n}\frac{r}{v(v+1)}=2r\left(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{n+1}\right)\leq 2.

For (
4.18
), noting again the Pólya urn represpentation of
F_{T_{n}}(v)-1
, a direct calculation gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}}(v)-1=k\right)

\displaystyle=\binom{n-v}{k}\frac{\Gamma(1+k)\Gamma(n-k)}{\Gamma\left(1+n\right)}\frac{\Gamma\left(1+v\right)}{\Gamma(1)\Gamma(v)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-v)!k!(n-k-1)!v!}{k!(n-v-k)!n!(v-1)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-v)!v}{n!}\frac{(n-1-k)!}{(n-v-k)!}.

By considering consecutive ratios, the last expression is maximal when
k=0
. In particular, we have that for all
v\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\{x\in 1,\ldots,n\}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}}(v)=x\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}}(v)-1=0\right)=\frac{(n-v)!v}{n!}\frac{(n-1)!}{(n-v)!}=\frac{v}{n}.

Summing over all possible values
x\leq n/r
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{r}\right)=\sum_{x=1}^{\frac{n}{r}}\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}}(v)=x\right)\leq\sum_{x=1}^{\frac{n}{r}}\frac{v}{n}\leq\frac{v}{r}.

Using again that the events
\left\{v\sim u\right\}
and
\left\{F_{T_{n}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{r}\right\}
are independent for
v\geq u+1
, we find that

\displaystyle\sum_{v=u+1}^{r-1}\mathbb{P}\left(v\sim u,F_{T_{n}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{r}\right)=\sum_{v=u+1}^{r-1}\mathbb{P}\left(v\sim u\right)\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{r}\right)\leq\sum_{v=u+1}^{r-1}\frac{1}{v}\frac{v}{r}\leq 1.\qed

We can now prove the main result of this subsection.

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

As previously mentioned, it is enough to show that fourth moment of each of the six random variables in the decomposition (
4.15
) of
Z_{\ell,j}^{T_{n}^{i}}(u)
is uniformly bounded.
We only show that
\mathbb{E}\bigl[O_{\ell,j}^{T_{n}^{i}}(u)^{4}\bigr]
and
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\widetilde{O}_{\ell,j}^{T_{n}^{i}}(u)^{4}\bigr]
are uniformly bounded; the terms
\mathbb{E}\bigl[I_{\ell,j}^{1,T_{n}^{i}}(u)^{4}\bigr]
and
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\widetilde{I}_{\ell,j}^{1,T_{n}^{i}}(u)^{4}\bigr]
can be treated like the term
\mathbb{E}\bigl[O_{\ell,j}^{T_{n}^{i}}(u)^{4}\bigr]
, whereas the terms
\mathbb{E}\bigl[I_{\ell,j}^{2,T_{n}^{i}}(u)^{4}\bigr]
and
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\widetilde{I}_{\ell,j}^{2,T_{n}^{i}}(u)^{4}\bigr]
can be treated like the term
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\widetilde{O}_{\ell,j}^{T_{n}^{i}}(u)^{4}\bigr]
.

We start with the term
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\widetilde{O}_{\ell,j}^{T_{n}^{i}}(u)^{4}\bigr]
, and apply Proposition
4.4
. To verify (
4.16
), let
S\subset\left\{\lceil e^{\ell}\rceil,\ldots,\lfloor e^{j}-1\rfloor\right\}
be a collection of vertices corresponding to the summands from
\widetilde{O}_{\ell,j}^{T_{n}^{i}}(u)
.
A key fact we will use that given
v\sim_{i}u
for all
v\in S
,
the process
\bigl((F_{T_{m}^{i}}(v))_{v\in S}\bigr)_{m\geq\lfloor e^{j}-1\rfloor}
of sizes of the Fringe subtrees of vertices in
S
starting from time
\lfloor e^{j}-1\rfloor
evolve like a multi-color Pólya urn with random initialization.
In particular, they are conditionally negatively related.
Thus we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{v\in S}\left\{F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)>\frac{n}{e^{\ell}},v\sim_{i}u\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{v\in S}\left\{F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)>\frac{n}{e^{\ell}}\right\}\Big|\bigcap_{v^{\prime}\in S}\left\{v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right\}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{v^{\prime}\in S}\left\{v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\prod_{v\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)>\frac{n}{e^{\ell}}\Big|\bigcap_{v^{\prime}\in S}\left\{v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right\}\right)\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{v^{\prime}\in S}\left\{v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{v\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)>\frac{n}{e^{\ell}}\Big|\bigcap_{v^{\prime}\in S}\left\{v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right\}\right)\right)\prod_{v^{\prime}\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\prod_{v\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)>\frac{n}{e^{\ell}}\Big|v\sim_{i}u\right)\right)\prod_{v^{\prime}\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{v\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(v\sim_{i}u,F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)>\frac{n}{e^{\ell}}\right),

where the first inequality follows from the conditional negative relation already discussed, and the second inequality is because
\{v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\}
is either independent of the event
\{F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)>y\}
(if
v^{\prime}<v
), or is negatively related.
Thus, (
4.16
) is satisfied with
\widetilde{C}=1
. To bound the first moment, (
4.17
) from Lemma
4.5
implies

\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{O}_{\ell,j}^{T_{n}^{i}}(u)\right]=\sum_{v=e^{\ell}}^{e^{j}-1}\mathbb{P}\left(v\sim_{i}u,F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)>\frac{n}{e^{\ell}}\right)\leq\sum_{v=e^{\ell}}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(v\sim_{i}u,F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)>\frac{n}{e^{\ell}}\right)\leq 2,

and now finally applying Proposition
4.4
yields the result for this case.

Next, we bound
\mathbb{E}\bigl[O_{j,\ell}^{T_{n}^{i}}(u)^{4}\bigr]
in an analogous way.
Let
S\subset\left\{\lceil e^{\ell}\rceil,\ldots,\lfloor e^{j}-1\rfloor\right\}
be a collection of vertices, corresponding to the summands from
O_{j,\ell}^{T_{n}^{i}}(u)
, such that
|S|\leq 4
. Then

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{v\in S}\left\{F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{e^{j}},v\sim_{i}u\right\}\right)\\
&\qquad=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{v\in S}\left\{F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{e^{j}}\right\}\Big|\bigcap_{v^{\prime}\in S}\left\{v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right\}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{v^{\prime}\in S}\left\{v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right\}\right)\\
&\qquad\leq\left(\prod_{v\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{e^{j}}\Big|\bigcap_{v^{\prime}\in S}\left\{v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right\}\right)\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{v^{\prime}\in S}\left\{v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right\}\right)\\
&\qquad=\left(\prod_{v\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{e^{j}}\Big|\bigcap_{v^{\prime}\in S\setminus\{v\}}\left\{v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right\}\right)\right)\prod_{v^{\prime}\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right),\end{split}

(4.19)

where the inequality is due to conditional negative relation, and the second equality follows by direct calculation and independence, noting that
u\leq v^{\prime}\in S
.

To go further, the key observation is that the distribution of
F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)

conditional on the event
\cap_{v^{\prime}\in S\setminus\{v\}}\left\{v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right\}
is stochastically larger than
the conditional distribution of
F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)
given that
v^{\prime}
does not join the fringe subtree of
v
when it arrives, which we write as

\cap_{v^{\prime}\in S\setminus\{v\}}\{v^{\prime}\not\in{\mathcal{F}}_{T_{v^{\prime}}^{i}}(v)\}
, where
{\mathcal{F}}_{T_{m}}^{i}(v)
is the fringe subtree of
v
at time
m
, and which we say is empty if
m<v
.
This is because
(F_{T_{m}^{i}}(v))_{m\geq v}
evolves as the number of red balls in a Pólya urn started (at step
v
) with
1
red ball and
v
blue balls, and the first conditioning corresponds to saying draws
v^{\prime}\in S
with
v^{\prime}>v
result in a particular blue ball, whereas the latter conditioning corresponds to the same set of draws being any blue ball. It is intuitively clear that conditioning on choosing
any
blue ball at some set of times has a stronger bias to smaller values of red balls than conditioning on choosing a
particular
blue ball, and this yields the stochastic monotonicity. A formal proof using the correspondence to Pólya urns is given in Lemma
5.1
below.

Now, because the size of the fringe subtree of
v
evolves like the number of red balls in a Pólya urn started with
1
red ball and
v\geq 1

v^{\prime}
joins the fringe subtree is
1/(1+v)\leq 1/2
. Combining this fact with the positive association of the events
\{v^{\prime}\not\in{\mathcal{F}}_{T_{v^{\prime}}^{i}}(v)\}
for

, we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{v^{\prime}\in S\setminus\{v\}}\{v^{\prime}\not\in{\mathcal{F}}_{T_{v^{\prime}}^{i}}(v)\}\right)\geq\prod_{v^{\prime}\in S\setminus\{v\}}\mathbb{P}\left(v^{\prime}\not\in{\mathcal{F}}_{T_{v^{\prime}}^{i}}(v)\right)\geq 2^{-\lvert S\setminus\{v\}\rvert}\geq 2^{-3},

(4.20)

where in the last inequality we use that
\lvert S\rvert\leq 4
.
Using the stochastic monotonicity and the inequality (
4.20
), we can bound the terms appearing in the last line of (
4.19
) by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{e^{j}}\Big|\bigcap_{v^{\prime}\in S\setminus\{v\}}\left\{v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{e^{j}}\Big|\bigcap_{v^{\prime}\in S\setminus\{v\}}\{v^{\prime}\not\in{\mathcal{F}}_{T_{v^{\prime}}^{i}}(v)\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{e^{j}}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{v^{\prime}\in S\setminus\{v\}}\{v^{\prime}\not\in{\mathcal{F}}_{T_{v^{\prime}}^{i}}(v)\}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq 8\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{e^{j}}\right).

Inserting this into (
4.19
) and using again that
|S|\leq 4
, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{v\in S}\left\{F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{e^{j}},v\sim_{i}u\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\prod_{v\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{e^{j}}\Big|\bigcap_{v^{\prime}\in S\setminus\{v\}}\left\{v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right\}\right)\right)\prod_{v^{\prime}\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\prod_{v\in S}8\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{e^{j}}\right)\right)\prod_{v^{\prime}\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right)

\displaystyle=8^{|S|}\left(\prod_{v\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{e^{j}}\Big|v\sim_{i}u\right)\right)\prod_{v^{\prime}\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(v^{\prime}\sim_{i}u\right)

\displaystyle\leq 8^{4}\prod_{v\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{e^{j}},v\sim_{i}u\right),

verifying (
4.16
) with
\widetilde{C}=8^{4}
.
The first moment is bounded by (
4.18
) from Lemma
4.5
, which gives

\mathbb{E}\left[O_{\ell,j}^{T_{n}^{i}}(u)\right]=\sum_{v=e^{\ell}}^{e^{j}-1}\mathbb{P}\left(v\sim_{i}u,F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{e^{j}}\right)\leq\sum_{v=u+1}^{e^{j}-1}\mathbb{P}\left(v\sim_{i}u,F_{T_{n}^{i}}(v)\leq\frac{n}{e^{j}}\right)\leq 1.

The result now follows from Proposition
4.4
.
∎

5
Pólya urn lemmas

Here we record some results about
standard Pólya urns used above.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
(R_{m})_{m\geq 0}
be the sequence of counts of the number of red balls in a standard Pólya urn started with
r\in\N
red balls and
b\in\N
blue balls. Assume also that one of the blue balls is in the urn initially is ‘distinguished’ with the label
\mathfrak{d}
.
Let
{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell}
denote the event that a blue ball is chosen at step
\ell
, and
{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}
denote the event that the the ball marked
\mathfrak{d}
is chosen at step
\ell
.
Then for any sequence of draws
0<m_{1}<\cdots<m_{k}\leq m
,

\mathscr{L}\bigl(R_{m}|\cap_{i=1}^{k}{\mathcal{B}}_{m_{i}}\bigr)\leq_{st}\mathscr{L}\bigl(R_{m}|\cap_{i=1}^{k}{\mathcal{D}}_{m_{i}}\bigr).

(5.1)

Proof.

We claim that
for any bounded function
g
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(R_{m})|\cap_{i=1}^{k}{\mathcal{B}}_{m_{i}}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(R_{m})p(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{k}})|\cap_{i=1}^{k}{\mathcal{D}}_{m_{i}}\bigr],

(5.2)

where the change of measure function
p
is defined by

p(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{k}})=\mathbb{P}\bigl(\cap_{i=1}^{k}{\mathcal{B}}_{m_{i}}\bigr)^{-1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Bigl(1-\frac{R_{m_{i}-1}}{r+b+m_{i}-1}\Bigr).

This change of measure formula follows by considering any sequence of draws
n_{j}\in\{\mathrm{red},\mathrm{blue}\}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
with
j\not\in\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\}
. Here
n_{j}=\mathrm{red}
(
\mathrm{blue}
) means that a red (blue) ball is drawn at step
n_{j}
. Setting
{\mathcal{R}}_{m}:=\{j:n_{j}=\mathrm{red}\}
and
r_{\ell}=|\{j\leq\ell:n_{j}=\mathrm{red}\}|
, the chance of observing this sequence and
\cap_{i=1}^{k}{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
is given by

\frac{\prod_{\ell\in{\mathcal{R}}_{m}}r_{\ell-1}\times\prod_{\ell\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\setminus{\mathcal{R}}_{m}}(r+b+\ell-1-r_{\ell-1})}{\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}(r+b+j)},

whereas the chance of observing this sequence and
\cap_{i=1}^{k}{\mathcal{D}}_{i}
is given by

\frac{\prod_{\ell\in{\mathcal{R}}_{m}}r_{\ell-1}\times\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\setminus{\mathcal{R}}_{m}\\
\ell\not\in\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\}\end{subarray}}(r+b+\ell-1-r_{\ell-1})}{\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}(r+b+j)}.

Noting that
\mathbb{P}(\cap_{i=1}^{k}{\mathcal{D}}_{i})=1/\prod_{i=1}^{k}(r+b+m_{i}-1)
yields the claim.

Next, we use induction to prove that for any two non-increasing functions
f,g:\R^{\ell}\to\R
, any sequence of draws
0<m_{1}<\ldots<m_{\ell}
, and any set
A\subset\N
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell}})g(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell}})|\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\bigr]

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\geq\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell}})|\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\bigr]\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell}})|\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\bigr].

(5.3)

The statement is true in general for
\ell=1
. For the induction step from
\ell
to
\ell+1
, the law of total expectation gives that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell+1}})g(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell}})|R_{m_{1}}=t,\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\bigr]\mathbb{P}\left(R_{m_{1}}=t|\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\right).

Conditioned on
R_{m_{1}}=t
(and any other events involving times
m\leq m_{1}
), the process
(R_{m})_{m\geq m_{1}}
evolves like a standard Pólya urn started with
t
red balls and
r+b+m_{1}-t
blue at time
m_{1}
, so that the induction hypothesis implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell+1}})g(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell}})|R_{m_{1}}=t,\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(t,R_{m_{2}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell+1}})g(t,R_{m_{2}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell}})|R_{m_{1}}=t,\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\bigr]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(t,R_{m_{2}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell+1}})|R_{m_{1}}=t,\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\bigr]\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(t,R_{m_{2}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell+1}})|R_{m_{1}}=t,\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\bigr].

(5.4)

Define the functions
F
and
G
by

\displaystyle F(t):=\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(t,R_{m_{2}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell+1}})|R_{m_{1}}=t,\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\bigr],\text{ and }

\displaystyle G(t):=\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(t,R_{m_{2}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell+1}})|R_{m_{1}}=t,\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\bigr].

There exists a straight-forward coupling of
(R_{m})_{m\geq m_{1}}
of the measures
\mathbb{P}\left(\cdot|R_{m_{1}}=t,\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\right)
so that each random variable
(R_{m})_{m\geq m_{1}}
is increasing in
R_{m_{1}}=t
. Thus, we see that the functions
F
and
G
are non-increasing in
t
. Combining this with (
5.4
), we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell+1}})

\displaystyle g(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell}})|R_{m_{1}}=t,\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\bigr]\mathbb{P}\left(R_{m_{1}}=t|\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}F(t)G(t)\mathbb{P}\left(R_{m_{1}}=t|\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[F(R_{m_{1}})G(R_{m_{1}})|\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[F(R_{m_{1}})|\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[G(R_{m_{1}})|\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\right].

The induction step follows, since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[F(R_{m_{1}})|\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(t,R_{m_{2}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell+1}})|R_{m_{1}}=t,\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\bigr]\mathbb{P}\left(R_{m_{1}}=t|\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(R_{m_{1}},R_{m_{2}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell+1}})|\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\bigr],

and, analogously,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[G(R_{m_{1}})|\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\right]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(R_{m_{1}},R_{m_{2}},\ldots,R_{m_{\ell+1}})|\cap_{a\in A}{\mathcal{D}}_{a}\bigr].

The stochastic domination, (
5.1
), now follows by considering a non-increasing function
g
, and writing
\phi_{1}(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{k}},R_{m}):=g(R_{m})
,
\phi_{2}(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{k}},R_{m}):=p(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{k}})
, which are both non-increasing in their arguments. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(R_{m})|\cap_{i=1}^{k}{\mathcal{B}}_{m_{i}}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi_{1}(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{k}},R_{m})\phi_{2}(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{k}},R_{m})|\cap_{i=1}^{k}{\mathcal{D}}_{m_{i}}\bigr]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi_{1}(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{k}},R_{m})|\cap_{i=1}^{k}{\mathcal{D}}_{m_{i}}\bigr]\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi_{2}(R_{m_{1}},\ldots,R_{m_{k}},R_{m})|\cap_{i=1}^{k}{\mathcal{D}}_{m_{i}}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(R_{m})|\cap_{i=1}^{k}{\mathcal{D}}_{m_{i}}\bigr],

where the first equality is the change of measure formula (
5.2
), the inequality follows from (
5.3
), and the last equality is from the definition of
\phi_{1}
, and the change of measure formula (
5.2
) with
g\equiv 1
. This proves (
5.1
).
∎

The next lemma gives a close coupling of the marginal
distribution of a standard Pólya urn with a variable distributed as the limiting beta of the urn. It is essentially
Peköz et al. 2017
.

Lemma 5.2
.

[Lemma 3 of
Peköz et al. 2017
]
Let
R_{m}
be the number of red balls in a standard Pólya urn started with
r\in\mathbb{N}
red balls and
b\in\mathbb{N}
blue balls after
m
draws and replacements. Then there is a coupling of
R_{m}
and
B_{r,b}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(r,b)
such that

|R_{m}-mB_{r,b}|\leq 2(b\wedge r)(r+b),

and consequently

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R_{m}\geq t)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(mB_{r,b}\geq t+2(b\wedge r)(r+b)).

Proof.

The first assertion follows easily from
(
Peköz et al. 2017
, Lemma 3)
which says there is a coupling of
R_{m}
with
B=B_{r,b}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(r,b)
such that

|R_{m}-mB|\leq 2b(r+b).

Reversing the roles of
r
and
b
and noting that
m-R_{m}
counts the number of blue balls and
(1-B)\sim\mathrm{Beta}(b,r)
, we can reverse the roles of

r
and
b
and get a coupling of
(m-R_{m})
and
(1-B)
such that

|R_{m}-mB|=|(m-R_{m})-m(1-B)|\leq 2r(r+b).

Choosing the appropriate coupling based on which of
r
or
b
is smaller, implies there is a coupling of
R_{m}
and
B
such that,

|R_{m}-mB|\leq 2(b\wedge r)(r+b).

The consequence now easily follows by using the coupling to find

\mathbb{P}(R_{m}\geq t)=\mathbb{P}(mB\geq t-(R_{m}-mB))\geq\mathbb{P}(mB\geq t+2(b\wedge r)(r+b)).\qed

Appendix A
Counting leaves

For
\alpha\in[0,1]
, let
(T_{n}^{1},T_{n}^{2})\sim\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)
be a pair of correlated, unlabeled trees,
and let
L_{1}(n)
and
L_{2}(n)
denote the fraction of leaves in
T_{n}^{1}
and
T_{n}^{2}
, respectively.
In this section we show that counting leaves in the two trees allows to distinguish between independent trees and correlated trees with positive power. Specifically, we show the following result.

Theorem A.1
.

For
\alpha\in[0,1]
, let

\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}:=\frac{(1-\alpha)(3+\alpha)}{6(3-\alpha)}
.
For every
\alpha\in(0,1)
we have that

\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{TV}\left(\mathrm{UA}(n)^{\otimes 2},\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)\right)\geq\mathrm{TV}\left({\mathcal{N}}\left(0,1/6\right),{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}\right)\right)>0,

where
{\mathcal{N}}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
denotes a Gaussian random variable with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
.

We provide an overview of the analysis here, and leave the details to the interested reader.
Define the sets

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)

\displaystyle:=\left\{0\leq v\leq n:\deg_{T_{n}^{1}}(v)=\deg_{T_{n}^{2}}(v)=1\right\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{1\times}(n)

\displaystyle:=\left\{0\leq v\leq n:\deg_{T_{n}^{1}}(v)=1,\deg_{T_{n}^{2}}(v)\geq 2\right\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\times 1}(n)

\displaystyle:=\left\{0\leq v\leq n:\deg_{T_{n}^{1}}(v)\geq 2,\deg_{T_{n}^{2}}(v)=1\right\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\times\times}(n)

\displaystyle:=\left\{0\leq v\leq n:\deg_{T_{n}^{1}}(v)\geq 2,\deg_{T_{n}^{2}}(v)\geq 2\right\}.

Note that these sets partition the set
\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
, while also capturing similarities in leaves with the same node label across
T_{n}^{1}
and
T_{n}^{2}
.
In particular, the difference between the number of leaves in the two trees is precisely captured by

|{\mathcal{S}}_{1\times}(n)|-|{\mathcal{S}}_{\times 1}(n)|=(n+1)(L_{1}(n)-L_{2}(n))
.

Deriving stochastic recursions.

The first step of the analysis is to characterize the evolution of the set sizes
|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|,|{\mathcal{S}}_{1\times}(n)|
and
|{\mathcal{S}}_{\times 1}(n)|,|{\mathcal{S}}_{\times\times}(n)|
, which depend on the sequence of aligned and independent choice steps in the joint construction of the two trees.

As an example, consider the evolution of
|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|
.
We first note that when vertex
n+1
is added, it is clearly a leaf in both trees; thus
n+1\in{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n+1)
. Depending on the type of choice (aligned or independent) and the specific connections made by vertex
n+1
, some vertices in
{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)
may not be present in
{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n+1)
. We outline the possibilities below:

•

Aligned choice.
If
n+1
attaches to vertex
u
in both trees and
u\in{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)
, then
u\notin{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n+1)
. Otherwise, all vertices in
{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)
are also present in
{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n+1)
. Hence

|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n+1)|-|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|=1-\mathbf{1}(u\in{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)).

•

Independent choice.
Suppose that
n+1
attaches to
u^{1}
in
T_{n}^{1}
and
u^{2}
in
T_{n}^{2}
. If
u^{1}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)
, then
u^{1}\notin{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n+1)
. The same logic holds for
u^{2}
.
Consequently,

|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n+1)|-|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|=1-\mathbf{1}(u^{1}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n))-\mathbf{1}(u^{2}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n))+\mathbf{1}(u^{1}=u^{2}\text{ and }u^{1}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)),

where the addition of the third indicator is due to double-counting by the first two indicators when
u^{1}=u^{2}
.

Putting everything together, we obtain the recursion

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n+1)||{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|+1-\frac{\alpha|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|}{n+1}-(1-\alpha)\left(\frac{2|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|}{n+1}-\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|}{(n+1)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|+1-(2-\alpha)\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|}{n+1}+(1-\alpha)\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|}{(n+1)^{2}}.

The normalized set size evolves as

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n+1)|}{n+2}\right|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\right]=\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|}{n+1}+\frac{1}{n+2}\left(1-(3-\alpha)\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|}{n+1}+(1-\alpha)\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|}{(n+1)^{2}}\right),

(A.1)

and similar arguments give the following recursions for
|{\mathcal{S}}_{1\times}(n)|
and
|{\mathcal{S}}_{\times 1}(n)|
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\biggl[&\left.\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}_{1\times}(n+1)|}{n+2}\right|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\biggr]\\
&=\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}_{1\times}(n)|}{n+1}+\frac{1}{n+2}\left(-\frac{2|{\mathcal{S}}_{1\times}(n)|}{n+1}+(1-\alpha)\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|}{n+1}-(1-\alpha)\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|}{(n+1)^{2}}\right),\end{split}

(A.2)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\biggl[&\left.\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}_{\times 1}(n+1)|}{n+2}\right|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\biggr]\\
&=\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}_{\times 1}(n)|}{n+1}+\frac{1}{n+2}\left(-\frac{2|{\mathcal{S}}_{\times 1}(n)|}{n+1}+(1-\alpha)\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|}{n+1}-(1-\alpha)\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|}{(n+1)^{2}}\right).\end{split}

(A.3)

Asymptotic normality.

Well-established results on stochastic approximation such as
Fabian 1968
and
Hernandez and Spall 2019
allow us to characterize the limiting distribution of the iterates
|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|/n,|{\mathcal{S}}_{1\times}(n)|/n
, and
|{\mathcal{S}}_{\times 1}(n)|/n
.
By applying known results to the coupled stochastic recursion (
A.1
), (
A.2
), and (
A.3
), we obtain that

\sqrt{n}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}_{1\times}(n)|}{n}-\frac{1-\alpha}{2(3-\alpha)}\\
\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}_{\times 1}(n)|}{n}-\frac{1-\alpha}{2(3-\alpha)}\\
\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|}{n}-\frac{1}{3-\alpha}\end{pmatrix}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,Q\right),

(A.4)

where
Q
is a
3\times 3
matrix with entries given by

\displaystyle Q_{33}

\displaystyle=\frac{4-3\alpha}{(3-\alpha)^{2}(5-2\alpha)}

\displaystyle Q_{23}=Q_{32}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\alpha)(4\alpha-17)}{2(3-\alpha)^{2}(5-2\alpha)(4-\alpha)}

\displaystyle Q_{22}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(1-\alpha)(2\alpha^{3}-23\alpha^{2}+105\alpha-192)}{12(4-\alpha)(3-\alpha)^{2}(5-2\alpha)}

\displaystyle Q_{13}=Q_{31}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\alpha)(4\alpha-17)}{2(3-\alpha)^{2}(5-2\alpha)(4-\alpha)}

\displaystyle Q_{12}=Q_{21}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\alpha)(2\alpha^{4}-15\alpha^{3}+25\alpha^{2}+12\alpha+12)}{12(4-\alpha)(3-\alpha)^{2}(5-2\alpha)}

\displaystyle Q_{11}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(1-\alpha)(2\alpha^{3}-23\alpha^{2}+105\alpha-192)}{12(4-\alpha)(3-\alpha)^{2}(5-2\alpha)}.

The form of the matrix
Q
is computed based on the stochastic approximation results of
Hernandez and Spall 2019
. At a high level, the calculation first involves computing the covariance matrix of the martingale differences corresponding to the stochastic recursion of interest, and applying a certain recursive, entry-wise transformation to compute
Q
.

We make a few remarks about the asymptotic normality result (
A.4
) and its relation to known results.

•

When
\alpha=1
, the two jointly generated trees are identical, and the number of leaves in each tree is captured by
{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)
. The display in (
A.4
) shows that
|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}|/n
concentrates around
1/2
in this case, with asymptotically normal fluctuations of variance
Q_{33}=1/12
. This recovers known distributional results on the number of leaves in a single UA tree; see
Janson 2005
.

•

For any
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
the fraction of leaves in
T_{n}^{1}
is equal to
L_{1}(n)=(|{\mathcal{S}}_{11}(n)|+|{\mathcal{S}}_{1\times}(n)|)/n
.
According to (
A.4
), this concentrates around
1/2
with asymptotically normal fluctuations of variance
Q_{33}+Q_{11}+2Q_{13}=1/12
. Hence the marginal characterization of leaves in a single UA tree aligns with the results of
Janson 2005
.

Distributional differences in the fraction of leaves.

The convergence in (
A.4
) implies that

\sqrt{n}(L_{1}(n)-L_{2}(n))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,\frac{(1-\alpha)(3+\alpha)}{6(3-\alpha)}\right).

(A.5)

Thus, we can use the difference between the fraction of leaves in the two trees as a differentiating statistic.
Specifically,
if
(T_{n}^{1},T_{n}^{2})\sim\mathrm{UA}(n)^{\otimes 2}
, then

\sqrt{n}(L_{1}(n)-L_{2}(n))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,1/6\right)
,
while if
(T_{n}^{1},T_{n}^{2})\sim\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)
, then

\sqrt{n}(L_{1}(n)-L_{2}(n))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}\right)
,
where recall that

\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}:=\frac{(1-\alpha)(3+\alpha)}{6(3-\alpha)}
.
This proves Theorem
A.1
by noting that
\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}\neq 1/6
for
\alpha>0
.

Since
\sigma_{\alpha}\to 0
as
\alpha\to 1
, it is straightforward to see that
\mathrm{TV}\left(\mathrm{UA}(n)^{\otimes 2},\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)\right)\to 1
as
\alpha\to 1
and
n\to\infty
. For example, if
X_{\alpha}=\sigma_{\alpha}Z
and
Y=\sqrt{1/6}Z
with
Z\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
, then as
\alpha\to 0
, we have
\mathbb{P}(\lvert X_{\alpha}\rvert>\sqrt{\sigma_{\alpha}})=\mathbb{P}(\lvert Z\rvert>1/\sqrt{\sigma_{\alpha}})\to 0
and
\mathbb{P}(\lvert Y\rvert>\sqrt{\sigma_{\alpha}})=\mathbb{P}(\lvert Z\rvert>\sqrt{6\sigma_{\alpha}})\to 1
.

Beyond leaves.

A natural question is whether comparing the fraction of higher degree vertices across the two trees can allow us to distinguish
\mathrm{UA}(n)^{\otimes 2}
and
\alpha{\text{-}}\mathrm{CUA}(n)
with power close to 1. While we expect that asymptotic normality can be established for joint degree counts in a similar sense as (
A.4
), the corresponding more general covariance matrix
Q
does not seem to admit a tractable representation for analysis.
Even if there is no closed form for
Q
, understanding its qualitative properties would shed insight into the joint structure of correlated UA trees and may also lead to better algorithms for identifying and estimating correlated regions in the two trees.
