Supercritical CO2 engineered niosomes as functional materials for oral drug delivery

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Scalable production: Single-step, solvent-free fabrication via supercritical CO₂.•High reproducibility: Consistent vesicle size, composition, and batch quality.•Tailored for oral delivery: Robust niosomes designed for gastrointestinal transport.•Improved transport: Vesicles penetrate mucus and support epithelial uptake.•In vivo performance: Demonstrated intestinal delivery and minimal gastric retention.

Introduction

Scaling up the production of lipid-based nanovesicles while maintaining control over particle properties remains one of the main challenges in translating drug delivery systems from lab-scale to industry [1]. Conventional techniques adopted to produce these nanocarriers, such as thin-film hydration, reverse-phase evaporation, ethanol injection, or microfluidics, are batch-based processes and, therefore, limited by low scalability and poor reproducibility [2,3]. Moreover, high solvent residues, low drug encapsulation efficiency, and the need for time-consuming multistage operations to reduce vesicle size are additional factors that often restrict their applicability in large-scale pharmaceutical manufacturing [4].

To overcome these limitations, we developed SuperSomes (Fig. 1), a supercritical carbon dioxide (SC-CO2)-based process designed for the continuous and scalable production of lipid-based nanovesicles [5]. The novelty of this method is based on the mechanism of nanovesicles formation that is completely different from conventional techniques [6,7]. In fact, it relies on the generation of aqueous droplets within a gas-expanded medium (i.e., a mixture between SC-CO2 and the ethanolic solution containing lipids or surfactants) that are rapidly surrounded by surfactant or lipid molecules, enabling a precise control over particle size, morphology, and composition; but also a high drug encapsulation efficiency of the produced niosomes [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. The mild operating conditions of SC-CO2 (Tc = 31.1°C, Pc= 73.8 bar) also ensure to preserve the integrity of the encapsulated compounds; whereas, the process operating conditions promote the efficient removal of the organic solvent, resulting in nanovesicles with low solvent residue and excellent long-term stability [7].Fig. 1Schematic representation of niosomes composition and SuperSomes process: (1) aqueous solution pump; (2) ethanolic solution pump; (3) CO2 high-pressure pump; (4) saturator; (5) formation chamber; (6) reservoir; (7) separator.Fig 1 dummy alt text

As a proof of concept, we applied the SuperSomes process to produce niosomes, vesicular nanocarriers with an internal aqueous core and a single or double layer of non-ionic surfactants [10,11]. Niosomes were selected because of their biocompatibility, high chemical stability, and ability to encapsulate both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs, making them promising candidates for oral drug delivery [12,13]. For example, compared to liposomes, niosomes are cheaper and exhibit high stability over time, as surfactants are chemically more stable than phospholipids and are not affected by oxidative degradation [14,15]. Moreover, the more packed structure of niosomes compared to liposomes makes them more effective in encapsulating large amounts of drugs, even those with high molecular weight [16].

In recent years, nanomedicine has undergone a remarkable transformation, with a growing interest in the development of personalized and less invasive therapies [17]. Therefore, several studies have focused on the production of niosomes as oral drug delivery platforms, since this is the simplest and preferred route of administration for patients [18]. Ghadi et al. [19] produced hyaluronan-based niosomes for the delivery of curcumin using thin-film hydration, followed by sonication to obtain vesicles with a nanometric size (250 ± 6 nm). In vivo studies revealed that curcumin-loaded hyaluronan niosomes exhibited higher anti-inflammatory activity compared to the empty counterpart. Using the same production method, Abonashey et al. [20] produced niosomes loaded with tiamulin to enhance the oral bioavailability in Mycoplasma infections. After sonicating the aqueous suspension for 30 min, niosomes of 340 nm were obtained, with a zeta-potential of -10 mV. The loaded niosomes were then tested in vitro and in vivo to demonstrate the improvement in bioavailability and systemic exposure of tiamulin. Rezaei et al. [21] used a modified thin-film hydration method to develop niosomes co-loaded with curcumin and cisplatin, achieving vesicles of about 150 nm, and a zeta-potential of -25 mV. The chloroform used as the organic solvent for the lipid solution was removed from the suspension by rotary evaporation. According to in vitro studies, the co-encapsulation of the drugs can lead to a reduction in cell viability when compared to single-drug formulations.

While these examples highlight the potential of niosomes for enhancing drug therapeutic efficacy, their translation to oral drug delivery remains limited, mainly due to challenges in the scalability and reproducibility of conventional techniques, notably in achieving vesicles of nanometric size with single-step operations [[22], [23], [24]]. Moreover, physiological barriers such as acidic stomach pH, digestive enzymes, and low intestinal permeability still restrict oral drug bioavailability [[25], [26], [27]].

Therefore, this work aims to produce niosomes designed explicitly for oral drug delivery using the SuperSomes process, thereby overcoming the main drawbacks associated with traditional methods [10,11]. Niosomes were produced for the first time using a continuous supercritical CO2-based process involving a combination of non-ionic surfactants, namely polysorbate 80 (Tween80) and sorbitan monooleate (Span80), together with the anionic surfactant capric acid (C10). Since enhancing intestinal permeation remains a significant challenge in oral drug delivery, particularly for hydrophilic or large molecules, C10 and Span80 were compared as potential permeation enhancers to facilitate transport across the epithelial barrier [28]. To address this, an optimization of the composition and mass ratios between surfactants was systematically carried out, investigating also how key physico-chemical parameters like hydrophilic–lipophilic balance (HLB), critical packing parameter (CPP), and gel–liquid transition temperature (Tc), influence niosome formation and stability over time [[29], [30], [31], [32]]. The resulting nanovesicles were comprehensively characterized in terms of particle size, size distribution, surface charge, and morphology. Stability studies were conducted under both refrigerated (4°C) and ambient conditions, as well as across a range of physiologically relevant pH values, to study the integrity of the vesicles under different environmental conditions. Finally, in vitro and in vivo experiments were conducted to identify the optimal niosomal formulation that combines gastric stability with enhanced intestinal permeation, supporting its potential as a promising carrier for the oral administration of drugs.

Materials and methods

Materials

Sorbitan monooleate (Span80; Mw= 428.62 g/mol, HLB= 4.3), polysorbate 80 (Tween80; Mw= 1310 g/mol, HLB= 15), capric acid (C10; Mw= 194.25 g/mol, HLB= 14) and rhodamine B powder (Mw= 479.02 g/mol, purity ≥99%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Milan, Italy). Anhydrous ethanol (purity ≥99%) was obtained from Carlo Erba (Milan, Italy), while ultrapure water was produced in our laboratory. Carbon dioxide (purity >99.4%) was supplied by Morlando Group Srl (Naples, Italy). For cell culture and in vitro experiments, Caco-2 cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM), high glucose with GlutaMAX™, FluoroBrite™ DMEM, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), TrypLE™ express with phenol red, penicillin/streptomycin (10,000 U/mL of penicillin and 10,000 µg/mL of streptomycin), MEM non-essential amino acids solution (100X), fetal bovine serum (FBS), calcium, magnesium and phenol red-free Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS), paraformaldehyde 4% solution in PBS, CellMask Deep Red Plasma Membrane Stain, and Hoechst 33342 trihydrochloride trihydrate were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Roskilde, DK). Corning Transwell® multiple well plate with permeable polyester membrane inserts of pore size 0.4 µm and area 0.33 cm2 were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Roskilde, DK). FITC-labelled dextran of 4 kDa (FD-4) was purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

SuperSomes process: mechanism of niosome formation

A simplified layout of the lab-scale SuperSomes process is shown in Fig. 1. It consists of three main components: (4) the saturator (internal volume: 150 cm3), operating at 40°C and 100 bar; (5) the formation chamber (internal volume: 500 cm3), operating at 40°C and 100 bar; and (7) the separator, operating at 25°C and 10 bar [[6], [7], [8], [9]].

The ethanolic solution of surfactants was delivered to the saturator using a Gilson pump (2, model 305, Villiers Le Bel, France), while the aqueous phase was pumped into the formation chamber (i.e., a high-pressure stainless-steel vessel) using a separate Gilson pump (1, model 305, Villiers Le Bel, France) and atomized through a nozzle with an internal diameter of 80 µm. For both solutions, the flow rate was set at 7 mL/min. CO2 was stored in a tank, cooled with a cryostat (Julabo FL100), and pumped to the saturator using an Ecoflow pump (3, LDC-M-2, Lewa, Leonberg, Germany) at a flow rate of 7.4 g/min. The saturator was a high-pressure static mixer in which the ethanolic solution of surfactant and SC-CO2 were mixed at a constant gas-to-liquid ratio (GLR) of 1.06 on a mass basis. Contact between the two phases was enhanced by stainless-steel packings (specific surface area: 1889 m−1; void fraction: 0.94; ProPak, Scientific Development Company, USA), to promote the formation of a gas-expanded liquid that was delivered to the high-pressure vessel through a capillary tube. Inside the formation chamber, water-based droplets were fast coated by the hydrophilic heads of surfactants, forming reverse micelles. Upon reaching the aqueous bulk at the bottom of the chamber, the interaction between the hydrophobic tails of surfactants allowed the formation of a second lipid layer, completing the vesicle structure. The resulting niosomes suspended in water were collected in a high-pressure reservoir (6) located downstream of the formation chamber and recovered at regular intervals by opening an on-off valve. At the end of the process, depressurization was carried out by opening a micrometering valve (1335G4Y, Hoke, Spartanburg, USA). The ethanol was separated from CO2 and recovered in a separator located downstream of the formation chamber. Process parameters like CO2 flow rate, temperature and pressure were monitored using a volumetric meter (Sacofgas 1927 SpA, Italy), a type J thermocouple (Watlow, Corsico, MI, Italy), and pressure gauges (MP1, OMET, Italy), respectively. Each experiment was performed in triplicate (N= 3).

Physico-chemical characterization of niosomes

Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) analysis was performed using a Zetasizer Nano S (Malvern Instrument, Worcestershire, UK) to measure the mean hydrodynamic diameter (MHD), polydispersity index (PDI) and ζ-potential of the niosomal formulations. For size and PDI determination, measurements were carried out at a fixed backscattering angle of 173°, using disposable cuvettes containing 1 mL of each sample. Data was obtained from three independent measurements (n= 3), each consisting of 11 runs of 10 s run. Surface charge was measured using disposable folded capillary cells, with 20 runs per measurement, also performed in triplicate (n= 3).

The concentration of nanovesicles (expressed as particles/mL) was determined by Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis using a NanoSight Pro (Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK), equipped with a 488 nm laser. Prior to the analysis, samples were diluted in ultrapure water to obtain the optimal concentration range (i.e., 107–109 particles/mL). For each sample, five measurements of 1 min were performed at a cell temperature of 25°C and pump rate of 30 µL min−1.

Niosome morphology was observed using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, Supra35, Carl Zeiss). A 30 µL aliquot of the niosomal suspension was placed on a glass slide to form a thin film, air-dried and coated with a thin layer of gold using a sputter coater (108 A, Agar Auto Sputter Coater, Stansted, UK) operating at 40 mA for 180 s. The samples were observed at magnifications of 20,000x and 50,000x to obtain detailed information about their morphology. Rhodamine B (RhB) encapsulation efficiency was determined after the samples were centrifuged at 6500 rpm and 4°C for 50 min, using Amicon® Ultra-15 centrifugal filters with a cutoff of 30 kDa (Millipore Comp., Copenhagen, Denmark). The absorbance of the supernatant (i.e., the aqueous phase containing unencapsulated rhodamine B) was measured at ʎ= 543 nm using a calibration curve, and the encapsulation efficiency (EE%) was calculated using the following equation:(4)EE%=(1−mfreeRhBmtotalRhBloaded)·100where: mfree RhB and mtotal RhB loaded refer to the amount of free rhodamine and the initial amount of loaded rhodamine during noisome formulation, respectively.

Drug loading capacity (DLC%) was calculated as the ratio between the mass of rhodamine encapsulated within the niosomes and the total mass of niosomal formulation (that included surfactants and rhodamine), according to the following equation:(5)DLC%=mencapsulatedRhBmtotalniosomalformulation·100

QCM-D

Interactions between niosomes and biological barrier components were investigated using two models: gastric mucin and phosphatidylcholine, the main lipid component of epithelial cell membranes. Quartz Crystal Microbalance with Dissipation (QCM-D) experiments were performed on a Q-Sense E4 system (Gothenburg, Sweden), employing gold-coated quartz sensors for mucin and silicon oxide-coated sensors for phosphatidylcholine, both with a fundamental frequency of 4.95 MHz (QSX301, Q-Sense, Gothenburg, Sweden), following an adapted previously reported protocol. Sensors were thoroughly cleaned prior to experiments by sequential rinsing using a mixture of ultrapure water, ammonia, and hydrogen peroxide in a 5:1:1 ratio, then with ultrapure water and 98% ethanol, and finally dried under nitrogen. Experiments were conducted at 37°C, with flow rates of 50 μL/min for mucin (0.2 mg/mL) and 100 μL/min for phosphatidylcholine (0.1 mg/mL). Frequency (Δf) and dissipation (ΔD) shifts were monitored simultaneously across multiple overtones (3rd, 5th, 7th, 9th, 11th, and 13th). The measurement protocol began at pH 4 (to simulate gastric environment) in buffer solutions (for gastric mucin) or ultrapure water (for phosphatidylcholine) to stabilize baseline signals. Next, the mucin or phosphatidylcholine solution was introduced to form a surface layer. After stabilization, excess unbound material was removed by rinsing. Niosomes (3.4 × 1011 particles/mL as quantified by NTA) were then flowed over the prepared layer to probe interactions, followed by a final water rinse to eliminate loosely bound particles. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the reported results focus on the seventh overtone for consistency.

Cell culturing

Caco-2 cells were cultured in a T75 culture flask (Nunc EasYFlask 75 cm2, Fisher Scientific, Roskilde, DK) at 37°C and 5% CO2 in medium consisting of DMEM supplemented with 10%v/v FBS, 1%v/v penicillin/ streptomycin and 1%v/v non-essential amino acids solution. The cells were washed with PBS and media was refreshed every two to three days, and cells were passaged upon reaching 80–90% confluency with TrypLE™. For in vitro experiments, the cells were seeded onto Transwell® inserts in a 24-well plate, at a seeding density of 30,000 cells/insert. The cells were kept with 100 µL and 600 µL in the apical and basolateral compartments, respectively, of the same cell medium as described for flask culturing. The medium was carefully changed from both compartments every other day. Cells at passage numbers 10–25 were used for in vitro experiments, where generally n= 3−4.

TEER value monitoring

The transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) of a Caco-2 monolayer was monitored to study the permeation enhancing ability of the NPs. A Millicell® ERS 3.0 digital voltohmmeter (Millipore Corporation, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) was used to check the TEER of the monolayer 21 days after seeding on Transwell® inserts. All monolayers considered for experiments had an initial TEER value of at least 400 Ω cm2. The cell media was then replaced by calcium-free HBSS, after a washing step, for 1 h in the incubator to allow equilibration. In accordance with established protocols for C10-based transport studies, calcium-free conditions were employed to prevent the formation of poorly soluble calcium soaps in the presence of sodium caprate (C10), which can lead to precipitation and compromise experimental reproducibility [35,36]. The different formulations investigated, previously exchanged into the test buffer, were then added to the test groups. Additionally, for formulations S80-Tw 10% and S80-Ca 10%, the dose dependency of the vesicles permeation enhancement effect was also evaluated by testing the formulations at concentrations of 0.5x, 2x or 4x the original concentration (3.4 × 1011 particles/mL as quantified by NTA, corresponding to approximately 1.0 × 1011 particles/cm2 based on the Transwell surface area of 0.33 cm2). TEER was measured at times 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2 and 3 h. The vesicle treatment was removed after 3 h of incubation and the cells were rinsed twice with warm HBSS. The cell monolayers were left in growth media, and TEER was measured again after 24 h to evaluate the recovery of the barrier function. All TEER measurements were expressed as retained TEER (%), corresponding to the relative TEER value of each monolayer with respect to their initial TEER after equilibration in HBSS.

Transport studies

To further assess the effect on permeability and the vesicles potential for oral drug delivery of macromolecules, the transport of FD-4 across Caco-2 cell monolayers on Transwell® inserts was measured upon treatment with the S80-Tw 10%. Furthermore, the transport of the particles themselves was also investigated with rhodamine B-loaded nanoparticles (RhoB@NPs). The cells were allowed to differentiate for 21 days, and TEER was checked to confirm their fully developed barrier function. After equilibration with HBSS, 100 µL of either a solution of 0.8 mg/mL of FD-4 (control group), RhoB@NPs, or a mixture of FD-4 and RhoB@NPs were added to the apical compartment. Samples of 100 µL were collected every 20 min for 2 h into a black bottom 96-well plate from the basolateral compartment. The volume was immediately replenished with calcium-free HBSS to preserve sink conditions in the basolateral chamber. The fluorescence signal of FITC were measured using a plate reader with excitation/emission wavelengths of 495/520 nm. Standard calibration lines of FD-4 were used to determine their cumulative transported mass.

Cell staining and confocal microscopy

The internalization of NPs and/or FD-4 by fully differentiated Caco-2 cells upon treatment was evaluated via confocal fluorescence microscopy. After a 3 h incubation with either a mixture of RhoB@NPs and FD-4, NPs and FD-4, or FD-4 alone as a control, cells were rinsed twice with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 15 min at room temperature. After fixation, cells were washed three times with PBS. For nuclear staining, cells were incubated with 100 µL of Hoechst 33342 at a final concentration of 1 μg/mL in PBS for 20 min at room temperature while kept dark. Excess stain was removed by washing three times with PBS. The cell membrane was stained by incubating the cells with 100 µL of a 1:2000 dilution of CellMask Deep Red Plasma Membrane Stain in Fluorobrite DMEM for 10 min. The cells were washed once and left in DMEM Fluorobrite for subsequent imaging. Images were acquired using a Leica TCS SP8 CARS microscope (Leica Microsystems, Mannheim, Germany) with confocal laser scanning microscope functionality. A Fluotar VISIR 25x water immersion objective (NA= 0.95) was used. Images of the different fluorophore channels were collected in sequence and defined to avoid crosstalk. Hoechst was excited using a 405 nm laser line, and emission was collected between 420–680 nm. With a white light laser, FITC was excited at 495 nm while emission was collected at 510–696 nm, rhodamine B was excited at 569 nm and detected at 588–644 nm, and CellMask Deep Red was excited at 645 nm with emission collected at 660–782 nm. Images were analyzed with Leica’s LAS X software.

In vivo rat studies

All animal procedures complied with the European Directive 2010/63/EU and Danish legislation on animal experimentation. Experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Welfare Committee appointed by the Danish Ministry of Justice (license no. 2015-15-0201-00610). Male Wistar Han rats (Crl:WI, Charles River Laboratories, Wilmington, MA, USA), weighing 290–330 g at the time of study, were housed in groups of three under controlled environmental conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, 20–22°C, 55% relative humidity) with free access to standard chow and water. Animals were allowed at least 7 days of acclimatization prior to experimentation. Before dosing, rats were fasted for 16 h with a libitum access to water. On the day of the experiment, animals received 500 µL of a suspension of RhoB@NPs (3.4 × 1011 particles/mL, quantified by NTA) in PBS via oral gavage. A total of eight rats were included in the study, with pairs of animals euthanized at 30, 60, 80, and 100 min post-dosing by exposure to a gas mixture composed of 90.5% CO2 and 9.5% O2. The gastrointestinal tract, from stomach to colon, was excised, segmented, placed in Petri dishes, and imaged using a hybrid microcomputed tomography–fluorescence tomography (µCT-FLT) system (U-CT OI, MILabs B.V., Utrecht, The Netherlands) with excitation at 540 nm and emission at 586 nm.

Fluorescence image analysis

Fluorescence images were analyzed to quantify the spatial distribution and retention of the rhodamine labeled niosomes along the gastrointestinal tract of rats. Images were overlaid with jet colormaps, and centerlines for the stomach, small intestine, cecum, and colon were manually traced and exported as JSON arrays of (x, y) coordinates. Each centerline was resampled to 200 evenly spaced points, and local tangent vectors were computed to generate perpendicular unit normals for fluorescence sampling. Images were converted to 8-bit grayscale, and intensities were sampled along a 16-pixel-wide perpendicular band (±8 pixels). Mean values across the band were used to construct one-dimensional fluorescence profiles normalized to organ length (0–100%). Profiles were normalized to each animal’s maximum intensity, and a baseline (defined as the 15th percentile of the pooled normalized data) was subtracted, with negative values set to zero. Baseline-corrected profiles were integrated along the physical centerline using the trapezoidal rule, and the results were multiplied by the band width (16 pixels) to yield total fluorescence in pixel² × intensity units. For each animal, cumulative fluorescence plots and bar plots of organ-wise total fluorescence were generated, and ROI masks were overlaid on raw images for quality control.

Results and discussion

Evaluation of physico-chemical parameters for niosome formation

As discussed in the Introduction, niosomes are commonly prepared using non-ionic surfactants such as Tween or Span, since these molecules promote the formation of stable vesicles and ensure compatibility with different active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) [33]. In this study, niosomes were produced for the first time using a novel combination of non-ionic and anionic surfactants: specifically, Tween80 and Span80 were used as non-ionic surfactants; whereas capric acid (C10) was selected as an anionic surfactant. Before starting the experiments, a preliminary thermodynamic study was conducted to assess the feasibility of producing niosomes using a new combination of surfactants (i.e., C10). Therefore, some physico-chemical parameters such as the hydrophilic-lipophilic balance (HLB), critical packing parameter (CPP), and gel-liquid transition temperature (Tc) were analyzed to ensure the formation of double-layer vesicles [37].

Previous studies have shown that a 90:10 (w/w) Span80 to Tween80 ratio yields nanometric niosomes with high drug encapsulation efficiency and long-term stability [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Therefore, the same ratio was adopted for the novel formulation, in which Tween80 was replaced by C10 as the hydrophilic surfactant, since they shared similar HLB values [38]. C10 was selected as an alternative surfactant owing to its permeation-enhancing properties in oral drug delivery, which are primarily attributed to its ability to modulate epithelial membrane fluidity and transiently open tight junctions, thereby facilitating paracellular and transcellular transport of therapeutic agents [28]. To evaluate the effect of increasing the amount of hydrophilic surfactants (i.e., Tween80 or C10), an 80:20 mass ratio was also tested for both Span80/Tween80 and Span80/C10 systems, while keeping the total surfactant concentration constant. The primary purpose of these experiments was to determine whether a higher amount of Tween80 or C10 could improve drug absorption by enhancing the opening of tight junctions.

As shown in Table 1, all tested formulations exhibited HLB values between 4 and 8, which are suitable for niosome formation; whereas surfactants with HLB values in the range of 14 to 17 are not recommended [39]. Moreover, all CPP values were much higher than 1, indicating the formation of reverse micelles under specific process conditions [40]. Tc, which is influenced by the alkyl chain length of non-ionic surfactants, also plays a vital role during the formation of niosomes, since it affects not only membrane fluidity and permeability, but also vesicle stability. For all the systems tested, the calculated Tc values were much lower than the operating temperature of the SuperSomes process (i.e., 40°C). Therefore, all the experiments were performed under conditions where T > Tc, ensuring sufficient membrane fluidity required for effective vesicle formation [41]. Following this thermodynamic evaluation, the selected formulations were treated by the SuperSomes process.

Production, characterization, and long-term stability of niosomes

In this work, the production of niosomes was carried out using the SuperSomes process under the following operating conditions: a pressure of 100 bar, a temperature of 40°C, aqueous and ethanolic solution flow rates of 7 mL/min, and a gas flow rate of 7.4 g/min. In all experiments, the total surfactant concentration was maintained at 10 mg/mL, while the mass ratio between the individual surfactants was adjusted according to the formulation (i.e., 90:10 or 80:20, w/w). Niosomes were characterized in terms of mean hydrodynamic diameter (MHD), polydispersity index (PDI), ζ-potential, and particle concentration (expressed as number of particles per milliliter of suspension) using Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) and Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA).

The results obtained are summarized in Table S1 of the Supporting Information.

For formulations containing Span80 and Tween 80 (i.e., S80-Tw 10% and S80-Tw 20%), it was observed that increasing the amount of the hydrophilic surfactant from 10% to 20% (w/w) resulted in larger vesicle size, with MHD values rising from 185.0 ± 1.2 nm to 198.0 ± 1.7 nm, respectively. This is because, as the HLB increases, the number of hydrophilic groups also increases. As a result, stronger interactions with water molecules occur, promoting the formation of vesicles with high surface energy and, consequently, larger size [42]. On the other hand, niosomes produced using Span80 and C10 (i.e., S80-Ca 10% and S80-Ca 20%) exhibited even larger average diameters, measuring 220.0 ± 3.0 nm and 235.0 ± 3.6 nm, respectively. This increase in size could be attributed to stronger repulsive electrostatic forces between vesicle layers, induced by the anionic surfactant [43]. Moreover, its negative charge may enhance water uptake within the double layer of vesicles, leading to an overall increase in vesicle size compared to those formed only with non-ionic surfactants [44].

All tested formulations exhibited a unimodal particle size distribution (Fig. 2A, i); however, ζ-potential values varied significantly depending on the surfactant used. Specifically, S80–Tw 10% and S80–Tw 20% niosomes showed ζ-potential values of −33.0 ± 2.5 mV and −38.8 ± 3.0 mV, respectively. In contrast, S80-Ca 10% and S80-Ca 20% vesicles exhibited higher absolute ζ-potential values of −67.6 ± 5.3 mV and −77.7 ± 6.1 mV, respectively, due to the presence of the anionic surfactant within the niosomal membrane [45]. All the samples were also analyzed by FE-SEM to obtain information about their morphology. As shown in Fig. 2B, niosomes prepared with Span80/Tween80 exhibited a spherical shape, with a smooth surface and a uniform size distribution, in agreement with DLS results. This was observed for both 90:10 and 80:20 w/w formulations. In contrast, the use of Span80 and C10 led to the formation of vesicles with a less regular shape, suggesting that the anionic surfactant affected their structural organization. This effect was most pronounced for the S80-Ca 20% formulation (Figure S1), where the higher amount of C10 resulted in vesicles with a tendency to aggregate. Therefore, the surfactant composition affected not only the physico-chemical properties of niosomes, but also their final morphology.

Stability tests were performed over a period of 4 weeks, with all samples stored at 4°C (Fig. 2A, ii-iii). DLS analyses were conducted at various time intervals (0, 1, 2, and 4 weeks) to determine particle size and surface charge. All formulations showed good stability under storage conditions, as no significant changes in the mean hydrodynamic diameter or ζ-potential values were observed over time. Since a variation in temperature can affect drug encapsulation efficiency, fluidity and colloidal stability of niosomal membrane, additional tests were also carried out at room temperature, for the same period. Under these conditions, the vesicles also preserved their main characteristics (i.e., size, polydispersity index, and surface charge), indicating the absence of aggregation phenomena during storage at room temperature (Table S2).

In summary, all the tested formulations demonstrated promising results in terms of physicochemical properties and long-term stability at both refrigerated and room temperatures. Therefore, all samples were subjected to further in vitro studies to identify the most suitable ones for oral drug delivery.

Particle mucin interaction (QCM-D)

We next investigated the interaction of niosomes with porcine gastric mucin (PGM) in the gastric buffer [46]. To assess the impact of composition, we compared formulations containing Span80/Tween80 with those containing Span80/C10 (Fig. 3). In our previous studies, we demonstrated that while interaction with mucin can influence nanoparticle retention, excessively strong or irreversible interactions may hinder transport across the gastrointestinal (GI) mucus layer [47]. To assess these interactions, we employed quartz crystal microbalance with dissipation monitoring (QCM-D), comparing S80-Tw 10% and S80-Ca 10% formulations. The experimental workflow included four steps: (i) introduction of mucin into the chamber, (ii) removal of unbound mucin, (iii) perfusion of nanoparticles, and (iv) washing to remove unbound particles [48]. The final change in frequency (Δf) provided insights into the strength and reversibility of nanoparticle-mucin interactions. We observed that both nanoparticles initially interacted with mucin, indicating measurable affinity for the mucin layer. Upon washing, however, S80-Tw 10% was removed entirely (Δf= 0); whereas S80-Ca 10% retained partial interaction with the mucin layer (Δf= 5 Hz). These results suggested that S80-Tw 10% exhibited weaker and more reversible interactions with mucin compared to S80-Ca 10%, which showed a higher degree of retention. Although both formulations share approximately 90% of their composition, exhibit negative surface charge, and have similar sizes, the substitution of C10 with Tween80 appeared to modulate their interaction with mucin, likely by altering surface hydrophobicity and transient interactions with mucin glycoproteins. This interpretation is consistent with previous reports showing that Tween80, when used as a surfactant coating on poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles, reduced hydrophobic interactions and binding to mucin, thereby facilitating improved diffusion through mucus [49]. Furthermore, SEM analysis revealed that S80-Ca 10% particles were not consistently spherical in shape. Their irregular morphology, in line with the mechanical theory of mucoadhesion, may favor physical interlocking or entrapment within the mucin mesh, thereby enhancing retention compared with the smoother, more uniformly spherical S80-Tw 10% particles [50,51].Fig. 3Representative QCM-D traces showing the interaction of (i) S80-Tw 10% and (ii) S80-Ca 10% niosomes with a mucin-coated gold sensor in buffer. Effect of niosomes on the intestinal barrier via TEER measurements

While niosomes have not been extensively studied in the context of oral drug delivery, some evidence suggests they can enhance permeation across intestinal barriers [52,53]. For instance, in flux experiments using Caco-2 cell monolayers, Song et al. demonstrated that molecules encapsulated in niosomes exhibited significantly higher permeation compared to their free counterparts, highlighting the potential of niosomes to improve intestinal absorption [54].

To assess the suitability and potential of the niosomes for oral delivery of macromolecular drugs, we first performed in vitro studies using Caco-2 cell monolayers cultured on Transwell® inserts, a widely established model of the intestinal epithelium (Fig. 4). As an initial step, we examined whether the different formulations influenced epithelial barrier integrity over 24 h. A decrease in transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) indicates the opening or disruption of tight junctions; if the TEER value recovers, this suggests that any junction-opening effect is transient, the formulations are well tolerated, and do not compromise epithelial integrity (Fig. 4A). To this end, TEER was monitored over time during and after exposure of Caco-2 monolayers to the four niosome formulations (S80-Tw 10%, S80-Tw 20%, S80-Ca 10%, S80-Ca 20%). The experiments were first conducted at a constant particle concentration of (3.4 × 1011 particles/mL as quantified by NTA) to establish a baseline comparison across formulations. Subsequently, a concentration-dependent study was performed to investigate whether the extent of barrier modulation was related to the applied particle doses. TEER values were expressed as the percentage change (ΔTEER%) relative to untreated cells (buffer alone), which served as the negative control.

When comparing the four formulations (Fig. 4B, i), we observed a significant decrease in TEER values as early as 15 min after initiating the incubation. Formulations containing Tween80 induced a more pronounced disruption of tight junctions compared to those containing C10, and this trend was consistent throughout the experiment. No significant difference was detected between S80-Tw 10% and S80-Tw 20%. In contrast, a composition-dependent effect was evident for the C10-based formulations, with S80-Ca 10% showing greater modulation of epithelial integrity than S80-Ca 20%. TEER values reached their maximum decrease after 60 min (ΔTEER% S80-Tw 10%: -19.89 ± 1.28, S80-Tw 20%: -22.01 ± 1.80, S80-Ca 10%: -17.25 ± 1.74, S80-Ca 20%: -8.16 ± 2.87) and subsequently recovered, with complete restoration observed after 24 h (ΔTEER% S80-Tw 10%: 4.59 ± 1.65, S80-Tw 20%: 7.34 ± 0.99, S80-Ca 10%: 4.34 ± 1.83, S80-Ca 20%: 2.97 ± 0.86), indicating that the formulations did not induce permanent toxicity in the cell monolayers, as previously demonstrated by other niosomal formulations [52]. These results corroborate previous findings, confirming that niosomes can modulate tight junctions and act as effective permeation enhancers, facilitating the transport of molecules across the intestinal barrier [54]. Notably, Tween80 exhibited a more substantial effect on tight junctions compared with C10, which is commonly used as a permeation enhancer. Previous studies on niosomes with various surfactants, although C10 was not included, also reported that Tween80 consistently led to higher systemic delivery [52]. Notably, prior work primarily focused on overall permeability without directly examining the interactions of individual components with Caco-2 cells. In such cases, the observed increase in permeability could be attributed to enhanced drug solubility rather than genuine modulation of tight junctions [55,56]. Here, we also demonstrated that Tween80 specifically interacts with the epithelial layer, suggesting that its permeation-enhancing effect is at least partly due to direct modulation of cell-cell junctions.

Since no difference in ΔTEER% was observed between Tween80 at 10% and 20%, while S80-Ca at 10% produced a more favorable effect than at 20%, we selected the 10% formulations of both Tween80 (Fig. 4B, ii) and C10 (Fig. 4B, iii) for subsequent experiments. These were then studied across a range of concentrations to assess whether a saturation point in TEER modulation could be identified. For S80-Tw 10% (Fig. 4B, ii), we observed a different trend compared to the previous single-concentration experiment. While at lower concentrations, a transient decrease in ΔTEER% with a peak at 60 min was noted, higher concentrations caused a more sustained reduction in barrier integrity, with ΔTEER% continuing to decline for at least three hours. Furthermore, we observed a saturation behavior, as increasing particle concentrations beyond 6.8 × 1011 particles/mL (reported as 2x in the graph) did not cause further decreases in ΔTEER%, indicating that the effect on the epithelial barrier reaches a plateau at this concentration. For all tested concentrations, TEER values recovered fully after 24 h, indicating that the formulations did not cause irreversible barrier disruption due to toxicity. For S80-Ca 10% (Fig. 4B, iii), a concentration-dependent trend was observed, where higher concentrations induced greater modulation of tight junctions over the three hours. TEER measurements also showed full recovery after 24 h, suggesting that the formulation does not cause permanent damage to the epithelial layer.

When comparing the two formulations, S80-Tw 10% and S80-Ca 10%, both were found to be safe and allowed for full recovery of TEER over 24 h. However, at the same concentration, S80-Tw 10% induced a greater decrease in ΔTEER%, indicating a more pronounced modulation of the epithelial barrier. At equivalent particle concentrations, S80-Tw 10% consistently produced a greater reduction in TEER compared with S80-Ca 10%. This enhanced effect may be related to the niosome composition, as Tween80 in the bilayer could alter membrane fluidity or interact more efficiently with the epithelial surface, thereby transiently enhancing paracellular permeability; whereas C10-containing niosomes appear less effective in modulating the barrier [56]. Taken together, these results suggested that the enhanced permeation-promoting effect of Tween80-containing niosomes is associated with stronger interactions with the epithelial barrier. Based on this, S80-Tw 10% was selected for subsequent studies on epithelial uptake and drug permeation.

To further evaluate their potential for drug delivery, we conducted two complementary experiments. First, we evaluated the permeability of FD4, used as a model macromolecular compound, across Caco-2 cell monolayers in the presence and absence of niosomes (Fig. 5A, ii). To gain mechanistic insight into niosome–cell interactions and their role in modulating transport, we fluorescently labeled the vesicles with Rhodamine B (RhoB@N), which was employed as a model tracer to enable the visualization of the vesicle uptake and distribution, alongside that of FD4 (Fig. 5B, i). The Rhodamine B-loaded niosomes exhibited an encapsulation efficiency (EE%) of 68% and a drug loading capacity (DLC%) of 0.68%.

Permeation studies of FD4 demonstrated that S80-Tw 10% niosomes increased transport across Caco-2 epithelial monolayers by up to 2× compared to controls, indicating enhanced paracellular permeability. These results are consistent with previous reports showing that niosomes can enhance flux across the epithelial layer, leading to higher permeation levels compared with the control [54]. Confocal imaging revealed substantial intracellular uptake of FD4 in the presence of niosomes; whereas no uptake was observed in their absence, suggesting that niosomes facilitate both transport across and into the epithelial cells. Similarly, rhodamine-labeled niosomes were internalized and distributed throughout the cytosol of Caco-2 cells, suggesting that their uptake may result from the strong interactions with the cell membrane previously observed in QCM-D experiments. This intracellular localization implied that niosomes may modulate tight junctions not only at the cell surface but also via intracellular mechanisms, potentially through interactions with cytoskeletal components or signaling pathways that regulate junctional integrity [59].

Collectively, these results indicate a dual mechanism of action: S80-Tw 10% niosomes transiently loosen tight junctions to enhance paracellular transport while simultaneously promoting endocytic uptake of cargo, allowing macromolecular therapeutics to bypass epithelial barriers that would normally restrict their absorption. These findings underscore the potential of Tween80-containing niosomes for oral delivery of both small molecules and biologics, as they can improve both systemic exposure and intracellular access, highlighting their versatility as a nanomedicine platform.

Particle stability at physiological pH and in vivo studies

The stability of niosomes under physiologically relevant gastric conditions was further evaluated using DLS. S80–Tw 10% niosomes were incubated at 37°C in Fasted State Simulated Gastric Fluid (FaSSGF) at a 1:5 (v/v) sample-to-medium ratio [52] (Figure S2). DLS measurements performed over time (up to 120 min) showed no significant changes in particle size or distribution, with the main peak remaining centered around ∼100–200 nm. This indicates that niosomes maintain their structural integrity under biorelevant gastric conditions.

Given their stability at gastric pH, we proceeded to determine the in vivo behavior of rhodamine-labeled S80-Tw 10% niosomes in rats. Particles were administered in solution by oral gavage (Fig. 6A, i). Fluorescence imaging was used to track their distribution at different time points and across compartments of the GI tract (Fig. 6A, ii-iii). Consistent with our QCM-D findings, gastric transit was highly efficient. Quantitative fluorescence analysis revealed a rapid decline in gastric retention over time, with ∼35% of the administered niosomes remaining in the stomach at 30 min, decreasing to 23% at 60 min, 11% at 80 min, and only 6.5% at 100 min. These results indicate that the niosomes do not become trapped in the gastric environment, but progress efficiently through the gastrointestinal tract. When examining the distribution across the stomach, small intestine, cecum, and colon, we found that the vast majority of the fluorescent signal localized in the small intestine, reaching up to ∼91% after 100 min. Only minor amounts were detected in the more distal compartments, suggesting that the niosomes primarily accumulate and persist in the small intestine, the key site for nutrient absorption and oral drug uptake. This preferential accumulation highlights the ability of the niosomes to reach and persist in the small intestine, which is a key site for drug absorption. Conclusions and future perspectives

In this work, a novel method, named SuperSomes, was developed for the scalable production of niosomes. By exploiting the unique properties of supercritical CO2 (i.e., liquid-like density, and gas-like viscosity), this process enabled the production of niosomes with different types of surfactants, while maintaining consistent vesicle size across batches, thus demonstrating its robustness and suitability for large-scale nanovesicles manufacturing. Moreover, the SuperSomes process allowed the production of long-term stability vesicles by minimizing the amount of the organic solvents, thereby representing a green and sustainable alternative to conventional techniques.

In the context of oral drug delivery, the resulting niosomes exhibited excellent stability over time and across a range of pH values, highlighting their potential for gastrointestinal applications. Initial in vitro studies indicated low interaction with the mucus barrier and high interaction with lipid membranes, suggesting that these niosomes could function both as mucus-penetrative particles and as carriers for cellular uptake. Importantly, Caco-2 cell experiments showed that the niosomes enhanced both intracellular uptake and paracellular permeation, providing a flexible transport mechanism that can be leveraged depending on the drug or therapeutic target, whether intracellular delivery or passage across the epithelium is desired. Finally, in vivo studies in rats demonstrated rapid transit to the small intestine with minimal retention in the stomach, supporting their suitability as carriers for oral drug delivery.

Overall, the SuperSomes process enables the reproducible production of niosomes with tunable surface chemistry and permeability-enhancing functionality, offering a promising platform for the oral delivery of drugs that face absorption barriers in the gastrointestinal tract. Building on these results, future studies will focus on the encapsulation of active pharmaceutical ingredients to evaluate drug loading, release profiles, and the performance of the final drug-loaded nanocarrier. Moreover, further development toward pharmaceutical applications will require additional studies addressing process scalability, critical manufacturing parameters, and regulatory requirements for nanovesicle-based products.
