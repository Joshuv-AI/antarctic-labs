Towards Differentially Private Reinforcement Learning with General Function Approximation


Abstract
We present the first theoretical guarantees for differentially private online reinforcement learning (RL) with general function approximation, extending beyond prior work restricted to tabular and linear settings. Our approach combines a batched policy update scheme with the exponential mechanism, together with a novel regret analysis. We show that, even under general function approximation, the regret in the model-free setting under differential privacy matches the state of the art for the linear case, scaling as O ~ ​ ( K 3 / 5 ) \widetilde{O}(K^{3/5}) , where K K denotes the number of episodes. As an important by-product, we also establish the first regret bound for online RL with batch update that depends on the standard complexity measure of coverability , complementing existing results based on a newly introduced Eluder-Condition class. In addition, we uncover fundamental gaps in recent results for private RL with linear function approximation, thereby clarifying its landscape.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) provides a principled framework for learning sequential decision-making strategies in unknown environments through repeated interaction. By observing the consequences of its actions and adjusting its behavior accordingly, an RL agent can progressively improve its performance over time. This paradigm has become a key enabler of modern personalized systems, with successful applications ranging from online recommendation
[
32
]
to individualized healthcare interventions
[
53
]
, social robotics
[
19
]
, and, more recently, large language models (LLMs) optimized via reinforcement learning from human feedback or verified reward
[
40
,
22
]
. In these applications, the agent engages with users over multiple rounds, choosing actions based on the current interaction context and receiving feedback—either as per-step process reward or per-trajectory outcome reward—which is then leveraged to refine the agent’s decision-making policy.

While highly effective, RL-based personalization inevitably raises privacy concerns, as the feedback collected during interaction often reflects sensitive user information. For example, in personalized medical decision-making, the state may encode private attributes such as a patient’s medical history or physiological measurements, while rewards reflect treatment effectiveness or adverse reactions. Similarly, in LLMs, user prompts, preference feedback, and interaction outcomes may reveal personal intentions, beliefs, or proprietary content. Although users may be willing to share such information to obtain better personalized services, they typically do not consent to having their private data inferred by third parties through the outputs or internal updates of the learning algorithm. Indeed, the existing non-private RL approaches have been shown to suffer from privacy leakages
[
41
,
15
,
34
]
. Thus, it is essential to develop RL methods that can learn from user interactions while providing formal guarantees on individual privacy.

Motivated by these concerns, a recent line of theoretical work has investigated how to incorporate differential privacy (DP)
[
13
]
into RL, with the goal of understanding the fundamental tradeoffs between privacy protection and sample efficiency (e.g.,
[
48
,
10
,
42
,
57
,
36
]
). Since standard DP is incompatible with sublinear regret, this literature has largely focused on a relaxed notion named
joint differential privacy
(JDP)
[
28
]
, under which meaningful guarantees become attainable. They have established sublinear regret bounds for various private RL algorithms, providing the first principled understanding of privacy–utility tradeoffs in RL. While pioneering, existing results are almost exclusively limited to tabular environments or linear function approximation. These structural assumptions are crucial in prior analyses, as they enable private learning through low-dimensional sufficient statistics, simple confidence sets, or carefully controlled policy switching. However, such assumptions substantially limit applicability in modern RL systems, where value functions or policies are typically represented using rich, non-linear function classes. Extending theoretical guarantees beyond the linear regime introduces significant new challenges, as many standard exploration mechanisms, such as confidence-set-based optimism, do not readily admit private implementations in the general function approximation case.

This gap naturally motivates our study of sample-efficient private RL with
general function approximation
. In particular, we ask the following fundamental question:

Can we still enjoy sublinear regret for private RL with general function approximation?

We answer this question in the affirmative by establishing the first sublinear regret bound for RL with general function approximation under JDP privacy constraint. Specifically, our main contributions can be summarized as follows.

1.

We study online RL in episodic Markov decision processes (MDPs) with
general function approximation
under formal privacy guarantees via joint differential privacy (JDP). We establish a regret bound of order
K^{3/5}
, where
K
is the number of episodes, with additional dependence on the
coverability
parameter that characterizes the exploration difficulty. This
K^{3/5}
-regret bound matches the state-of-the-art bound for the linear case in the model-free setting. Our results apply to both general stochastic MDPs (Theorem
3.1
) and deterministic MDPs with outcome-based rewards (Theorem
3.4
), a modeling framework motivated by LLM applications, where a much weaker representation condition (i.e., realizability) is required.

2.

Our results are achieved via a carefully designed algorithmic framework built on several key ingredients (Algorithms
1
and
2
). First, instead of the confidence-set-based constrained optimization commonly used for exploration in non-private RL, we adopt an unconstrained optimization objective that explicitly balances exploration and exploitation. This formulation enables the direct application of standard privacy mechanisms, such as the exponential mechanism, to privatize each policy update. Second, to control the cumulative privacy loss incurred by repeated updates, we employ a fixed-size batching strategy with a balanced choice of batch size, updating the policy only at the beginning of each batch.

3.

In addition, our analysis yields several by-products of independent interest. First, even without privacy considerations, our regret analysis under fixed-size batching provides new guarantees for RL with adaptivity constraints, where the regret depends directly on
coverability
(cf. (
2
)), matching the standard exploration complexity measure and avoiding the need for newly introduced conditions in prior work. Second, our generic and modular regret analysis offers a simple recipe for designing private RL algorithms beyond the specific privacy mechanisms used in this paper. Finally, we clarify the state of the art for private RL with linear MDPs by identifying fundamental gaps in recent claims of improved regret bounds.

Preliminaries

2.1
RL with General Function Approximation

Markov decision processes (MDPs).
We study RL under a finite-horizon episodic Markov decision process specified by a tuple
M=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},H,P,R,\rho)
, with state space
\mathcal{S}
, action space
\mathcal{A}
, horizon length
H
, transition kernel
P=\{P_{h}\}_{h=1}^{H}
,
{P}_{h}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})
, reward function
R=\{R_{h}\}_{h=1}^{H}
,
R_{h}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
, and initial state distribution
\rho\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
. A (randomized) policy
\pi
consists of
H
-step functions:
\pi=\{\pi_{h}:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})\}_{h=1}^{H}
. The policy induces a distribution over trajectories
(s_{1},a_{1},r_{1}),\ldots,(s_{H},a_{H},r_{H})
via the following process. The initial state
s_{1}
is first drawn via
s_{1}\sim\rho
, and then for each
h\in[H]
,
a_{h}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{h})
,
r_{h}=R_{h}(s_{h},a_{h})
1
1

1

For simplicity, we consider deterministic reward and our result can be easily extended to the random case.
,
s_{h+1}\sim P_{h}(\cdot|s_{h},a_{h})
and
s_{H+1}
is the terminal state with zero reward. Accordingly, we let
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\cdot]
and
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[\cdot]
denote expectation and probability under this process, respectively.

The value function and
Q

\pi
at each step
h\in[H]
are given by

\displaystyle V_{h}^{\pi}(s)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{h^{\prime}=h}^{H}r_{h^{\prime}}\middle|s_{h}=s\right],\quad Q_{h}^{\pi}(s,a):=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{h^{\prime}=h}^{H}r_{h^{\prime}}\middle|s_{h}=s,a_{h}=a\right].

The total expected reward under a policy
\pi
is thus given by

\displaystyle J(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{h=1}^{H}r_{h}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{s_{1}\sim\rho}V_{1}^{\pi}(s_{1}).

Let
\pi^{\star}
denote an optimal policy (i.e.,
\pi^{\star}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\pi}J(\pi)
) and
V_{h}^{\star}:=V_{h}^{\pi^{\star}}
,
Q_{h}^{\star}=Q_{h}^{\pi^{\star}}
be the corresponding value function and
Q

s\in\mathcal{S}
,

and
h\in[H]
:

\displaystyle V_{h}^{\star}(s)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{h}^{\star}(s,a),\quad Q_{h}^{\star}(s,a)=R_{h}(s,a)+\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}_{h}(\cdot|s,a)}V_{h+1}^{\star}(s^{\prime}).

This motivates us to define the so-called Bellman operator
\mathcal{T}_{h}
as follows: for any
f:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{T}_{h}f
is defined as

\displaystyle[\mathcal{T}_{h}f](s,a):=R_{h}(s,a)+\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}_{h}(\cdot|s,a)}\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}f(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}).

Throughout the paper, as in previous papers (e.g.,
[
51
,
9
]
), we assume that the rewards are normalized such that
\sum_{h=1}^{H}r_{h}\in[0,1]
.

Online RL.
We study RL in the online setting where the learner repeatedly interacts with an unknown MDP
M^{\star}
via taking a policy for each episode and observing the trajectory, to maximize the total reward. Formally, the learning protocol runs in
K
episodes, where at each episode
k\in[K]
, the learner (i) selects a policy
\pi_{k}=\{\pi_{k,h}\}_{h=1}^{H}
to run in unknown MDP
M^{\star}
and (ii) observes the resultant trajectory
\{(s_{h}^{(k)},a_{h}^{(k)},r_{h}^{(k)})\}_{h=1}^{H}
. The performance of the learner is measured via the regret

\displaystyle\mathsf{Reg}:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}J(\pi^{\star})-J(\pi_{k}).

General function approximation.
To achieve sublinear regret guarantees without dependence on the size of
\mathcal{S}
, we work with model-free value function approximation, where the learner has access to a
general value function class

\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{F}_{H}
that attempts to model the value functions under
M^{\star}
, with each
\mathcal{F}_{h}\subseteq(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1])
. To derive non-trivial regret bounds for online RL, some structural conditions on the function class
\mathcal{F}
and its interplay with
M^{\star}
are often required. The first set is the so-called
representation conditions
, which require that
\mathcal{F}
can model some necessary value functions. Two common conditions in this set are
realizability
and
Bellman completeness
.

Assumption 2.1

(Bellman completeness and realizability)
.

For all
h\in[H]
, we have
\mathcal{T}_{h}f_{h+1}\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
for all
f_{h+1}\in\mathcal{F}_{h+1}
. This directly implies realizability that
Q^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}
.

In contrast to statistical learning, online RL requires another set of conditions, called
exploration conditions
, which roughly limit the amount of exploration to learn the optimal policy. To this end, we adopt the notion of
coverability

[
51
]
.

Definition 2.2

(Coverability)
.

The coverability constant
C_{\mathsf{cov}}>0
for a given MDP
M
and policy class
\Pi
is

\displaystyle C_{\mathsf{cov}}(\Pi;M):=\min_{\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{H}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})}\max_{h\in[H],\pi\in\Pi}\left\|\frac{d_{h}^{\pi}}{\mu_{h}}\right\|_{\infty},

where
\left\|\frac{d_{h}^{\pi}}{\mu_{h}}\right\|_{\infty}:=\max_{s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{d_{h}^{\pi}(s,a)}{\mu_{h}(s,a)}
and
d^{\pi}_{h}(s,a):=\mathbb{P}_{\pi}[s_{h}=s,a_{h}=a]
is the occupancy measure of
\pi
in
M
. For notation simplicity, we let
C_{\mathsf{cov}}:=C_{\mathsf{cov}}(\Pi_{\mathcal{F}};M^{\star})
,
where
\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}=\{\pi_{f}\mid f\in\mathcal{F}\}
with
\pi_{f}
being the greedy policy with respect to
f
via
\pi_{f,h}(s)\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}f_{h}(s,a)
.

With Bellman completeness,
[
51
]
shows that low coverability is sufficient for sample-efficient online RL in the sense that sublinear regret, i.e.,
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{KC_{\mathsf{cov}}})
is achieved. Our main goal is to establish the corresponding regret bound under formal privacy guarantees in RL, introduced below.

2.2
Differential Privacy in RL

To adapt standard DP
[
13
]
for RL, following prior work
[
48
,
58
]
, the goal here is to roughly ensure that changing one trajectory in the learning process will not impact the outputs (e.g., actions and final policy) too much. More formally, we assume there is a user
u_{k}
associated with each episode trajectory (e.g., a multi-step interaction between a user and LLMs) and write
U_{K}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{K})\in\mathcal{U}^{K}
to denote a sequence of
K
unique users in the process, where
\mathcal{U}
is the set of all users. A
k
-th neighboring sequence
U^{\prime}_{K}
differs from
U_{K}
in only one user at
k\in[K]
, i.e.,
u_{k}\neq u^{\prime}_{k}
and
u_{j}=u^{\prime}_{j}
for
j\neq k
. The output of a learning mechanism
\mathcal{M}
on a user sequence
U_{K}
is denoted as
\mathcal{M}(U_{K})
, which contains all the actions in the
K
episodes as well as the final policy. With these, a natural adaptation of standard DP is to require that
\mathcal{M}(U_{K})\approx_{\varepsilon,\delta}\mathcal{M}(U^{\prime}_{K})
for any neighboring sequences. However, this is problematic as changing a user
u_{k}
will naturally change the corresponding actions recommended to her during episode
k
(e.g., a different user with a different prompt for LLMs leads to different responses), thus failing to achieve meaningful DP guarantees. In fact, as shown in
[
46
]
, even for contextual bandits (i.e., RL with
H=1
), a linear regret has to be sacrificed for meaningful DP guarantees.

To resolve this conflict, as in prior work on private RL, we adopt the notion of
joint differential privacy
(JDP)
[
28
]
from the privacy literature, which slightly relaxes the standard DP by excluding the actions corresponding to the differing user
u_{k}
, hence accommodating the intrinsic personalization in RL. Formally, we let
\mathcal{M}_{-k}(U_{K}):=\mathcal{M}(U_{K})\setminus\{a_{h}^{k}\}_{h=1}^{H}
denote the output obtained by excluding the actions during episode
k
and define JDP in RL as follows as in
[
48
,
58
]
.

Definition 2.3

(JDP in RL)
.

An RL learning algorithm
\mathcal{M}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-jointly differentially private if for all
k\in[K]
, all
k
-th neighboring user sequences
U_{K}
,
U^{\prime}_{K}
, and all events
E\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{H\times[K-1]}\times\Pi
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{M}_{-k}(U_{K})\in E\right)\leq e^{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{M}_{-k}(U^{\prime}_{K})\in E\right)+\delta,

where
\Pi
is the class for the final output policy.

Remark 2.4
.

We remark that JDP remains a strong privacy guarantee: it protects the information associated with any individual user
u_{k}
against arbitrary collusions of other users, provided that
u_{k}
does not disclose the actions prescribed to her.

Therefore, our ultimate goal is to design a private online RL algorithm that satisfies JDP while achieving a sublinear regret, which will be the main focus of the next section.

Optimistic exploration.
One key challenge in online reinforcement learning is strategic exploration. In tabular and linear settings, exploration is typically achieved via upper confidence bound (UCB)–type bonuses (e.g.,
[
4

3

25

3.2
Deterministic MDPs with Outcome Rewards

We show that for deterministic MDPs with outcome rewards (Definition
3.3
), one can eliminate both the Bellman completeness condition and the max-min optimization, while still achieving
K^{3/5}
-regret bound under JDP. This can be viewed as the private counterpart of
[
9
]
.

Definition 3.3

(Deterministic MDPs with outcome rewards)
.

An MDP is said to be deterministic if its transition kernel P is deterministic, i.e., for any
h\in[H]
and
s_{h}\in\mathcal{S}
,
a_{h}\in\mathcal{A}
, there is a unique state
s_{h+1}\in\mathcal{S}
such that
P_{h}(s_{h+1}|s_{h},a_{h})=1
. An outcome reward is a single reward signal
r=\sum_{h=1}^{H}R_{h}(s_{h},a_{h})
for a trajectory.

A key consequence of deterministic transitions is that the Bellman equation holds almost surely, rather than only in expectation. Specifically, for any
(s,a)
in step
h

\displaystyle Q_{h}^{\star}(s,a)

\displaystyle=R_{h}(s,a)+V_{h+1}^{\star}(s_{h+1}).

This stands in sharp contrast to the stochastic setting, where the Bellman equation involves an expectation over next states. Hence, for any trajectory
\tau
, it holds that

\displaystyle r(\tau)=\sum_{h=1}^{H}R_{h}(s_{h},a_{h})=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left[Q_{h}^{\star}(s_{h},a_{h})-V_{h+1}^{\star}(s_{h+1})\right].

For any
f\in\mathcal{F}
, let
f_{h}(s):=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}f_{h}(s,a)
and
f_{H+1}\equiv 0
. We define the outcome reward model
R^{(f)}:(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})^{H}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle R^{(f)}(\tau):=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left[f_{h}(s_{h},a_{h})-f_{h+1}(s_{h+1})\right].

Motivated by the above observation, we replace the Bellman error loss used in general MDPs with the Bellman residual loss. Given a dataset
\mathcal{D}
consisting of collected trajectories, the loss is defined as

\displaystyle L_{\mathcal{D}}^{\mathsf{BR}}(f):=\sum_{(\tau,r)\in\mathcal{D}}\left(\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left[f_{h}(s_{h},a_{h})-f_{h+1}(s_{h+1})\right]-r\right)^{2}.

Unlike the stochastic case, this loss does not require subtracting an infimum over the function class to address the double-sampling issue. At each batch update, we combine this loss with the distributional optimistic value

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{1}:=\mathbb{E}_{s_{1}\sim\rho}\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}f_{1}(s_{1},a),

where
\rho
is known as part of the MDP specification. This choice aligns the update rule with the regret objective
J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{s_{1}\sim\rho}V^{\pi}(s_{1})
and avoids making the score depend on a private sampled initial state.

3.3
Proof Sketch

We present a proof sketch for Theorem
3.1
while also mentioning the key difference in the proof for Theorem
3.4
.

Privacy analysis.
By the
Billboard Lemma

[
21
]
, for
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-JDP guarantees, it suffices to show that the computation of the sequence of
f^{(k)}
satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP in the standard sense. Since there is a total of
M=K/B
updates, by the advanced composition theorem
[
14
]
, for a final
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP, it suffices to ensure that each update is
(\varepsilon_{0},0)
-DP with
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon/(2\sqrt{2M\log(1/\delta)})
. Thus, setting
\beta_{\varepsilon,\delta}=\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{2\Delta_{S}}
in the exponential mechanism gives the result, where
\Delta_{S}=O(H)
is the sensitivity of our score function
S(f)
.

Regret analysis.

We start with the regret decomposition (for the fixed initial state assumed for Theorem
3.1
).

\displaystyle\mathsf{Reg}=\underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{K}V_{1}^{\star}(s_{1})-f_{1}^{(k)}(s_{1})}_{\mathsf{T}_{1}}+\underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{K}f_{1}^{(k)}(s_{1})-V_{1}^{\pi_{k}}(s_{1})}_{\mathsf{T}_{2}}.

To bound
\mathsf{T}_{1}
, we first
suppose that
f^{(k)}
is obtained via max rather than softmax
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\eta f_{1}^{(k)}(s_{1})-L_{\mathcal{D}_{[k]}}^{\mathsf{BE}}(f^{(k)})\geq\eta V_{1}^{\star}(s_{1})-L_{\mathcal{D}_{[k]}}^{\mathsf{BE}}(Q^{\star}),

where we let
[k]
denote the starting point of the batch that
k
belongs to and let
\mathcal{D}_{[k]}
be the dataset at the start of episode
[k]
. That is, if
i\cdot B+1\leq k<(i+1)\cdot B+1
for some
i\geq 0
, then
[k]=i\cdot B+1
and
\mathcal{D}_{[k]}
contains all episodes from
1
to
i\cdot B
. The above inequality holds directly by the batching schedule and the updated rule, provided
f^{(k)}
is the maximum of the score function. However, due to the softmax of the exponential mechanism, we only have approximate maximization, thus giving that w.p.
1-\alpha
,

\displaystyle\eta f_{1}^{(k)}(s_{1})-L_{\mathcal{D}_{[k]}}^{\mathsf{BE}}(f^{(k)})\geq\eta V_{1}^{\star}(s_{1})-L_{\mathcal{D}_{[k]}}^{\mathsf{BE}}(Q^{\star})-\mathsf{Err}_{\mathsf{priv}},

where
\mathsf{Err}_{\mathsf{priv}}
is the privacy cost, and in particular for the exponential mechanism
\mathsf{Err}_{\mathsf{priv}}=O(1/\beta_{\varepsilon,\delta}\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha))
. Thus, re-arranging the above inequality, we bound
\mathsf{T}_{1}
as

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{1}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\eta}\cdot\left(L_{\mathcal{D}_{[k]}}^{\mathsf{BE}}(Q^{\star})-L_{\mathcal{D}_{[k]}}^{\mathsf{BE}}(f^{(k)})+\mathsf{Err}_{\mathsf{priv}}\right).

Further, by the standard uniform convergence bound (cf.
[
24
]
), we can bound the empirical difference by the expectation of the Bellman error plus some statistical error, under the Bellman completeness assumption (Assumption
2.1
). With this result, we have the final high probability bound for
\mathsf{T}_{1}
as

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{1}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\eta}\left(K\cdot\mathsf{Err}_{\mathsf{stat}}+K\cdot\mathsf{Err}_{\mathsf{priv}}\right)-\frac{1}{\eta}{\left({\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t<[k]}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}[e_{h}^{(k)}(s_{h},a_{h})^{2}]}\right)},

(1)

where
\mathsf{Err}_{\mathsf{stat}}
is the statistical error, which can be bounded by
H\log(|\mathcal{F}|KH/\alpha)
for a finite class
\mathcal{F}
and
e_{h}^{(k)}(s,a):=f_{h}^{(k)}(s,a)-[\mathcal{T}_{h}f_{h+1}^{(k)}(s,a)]
.

We now turn to
\mathsf{T}_{2}
, i.e., the on-policy error. First, by the standard performance difference lemma
[
26
]
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{2}=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}}[e_{h}^{(k)}(s_{h},a_{h})].

The ultimate goal is to relate such “test” error above to the “training error”, i.e., (
1
), through the notion of coverability. This is the key challenge in our analysis due to batching, where the standard result (e.g.,
[
9
]
) cannot be applied. To this end, we derive a new inequality for such a key step in the batching setting. Specifically, we establish that for any
\lambda\geq 1

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}}\left|e_{h}^{(k)}(s_{h},a_{h})\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim C_{\mathsf{cov}}\cdot B\cdot\log K+\sqrt{C_{\mathsf{cov}}\log K\cdot\left(K\lambda+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t<[k]}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}[e_{h}^{(k)}(s_{h},a_{h})^{2}]\right)},

where the first term is the cost due to batch update, and the second term is related to the negative term in
\mathsf{T}_{1}
(cf. (
1
)), allowing for the cancellation effect.

Finally, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, plugging the above bound into
\mathsf{T}_{2}
, and merging with
\mathsf{T}_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{Reg}\lesssim\underbrace{(1/\eta)\cdot K\cdot\mathsf{Err}_{\mathsf{stat}}+\eta\cdot H\cdot C_{\mathsf{cov}}\log K}_{\text{(a)}}+\underbrace{H\cdot C_{\mathsf{cov}}\cdot B\cdot\log K}_{\text{(b)}}+\underbrace{(1/\eta)\cdot K\cdot\mathsf{Err}_{\mathsf{priv}}}_{\text{(c)}}

(2)

where (a) is the same term as in the non-private case, (b) is the cost due to batching and (c) is the cost due to privacy, which depends on
B
. Thus, tuning
B
and
\eta
optimally gives us the final regret bound.

Remark 3.7
.

With the above final generic regret bound, one can see that without privacy, by setting
B=O(\sqrt{K})
, the regret bound still maintains as
\sqrt{K}
with dependence on the coverability. Hence, as a by-product, our result also gives a
\sqrt{K}
regret with
\sqrt{K}
update frequency under the coverability, in contrast to the new notion of Eluder-Condition class in
[
52
]
.

Remark 3.8

(Difference in the proof of Theorem
3.4
)
.

The proof essentially follows from the same flow as in the general MDPs for Theorem
3.1
. The key technical difference is a new inequality to relate the on-policy “test error” to the “training” error, by fully utilizing the deterministic nature, which is our key technique contribution. As in the general MDPs, we believe that this inequality can find broad applications. Another difference is the treatment of the initial state: the algorithm uses the distributional optimistic term
\bar{f}_{1}=\mathbb{E}_{s_{1}\sim\rho}\max_{a}f_{1}(s_{1},a)
rather than the value at a sampled initial state, and the proof conditions on a fixed
s_{1}
before averaging over
s_{1}\sim\rho
. Finally, the Bellman-error loss is replaced by the outcome Bellman-residual loss
L_{\mathcal{D}}^{\mathsf{BR}}(f)
, so the proof requires only realizability rather than Bellman completeness, while the score sensitivity becomes
O(H^{2})
.

4
Concluding Remarks

General MDPs with outcome rewards.
In this setting, we can easily extend our algorithm and analysis to derive a
K^{3/5}
-regret bound under JDP. In particular, following the non-private case
[
9
]
, we add another loss for reward model estimation to our Algorithm
1
and modify the way of data collection with a helper reference policy. These changes will not impact the final regret order. In addition, if
\mathcal{F}
is parametric with parameter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one can potentially bound the term
\mathsf{Err}_{\mathsf{priv}}
via DP-ERM for the empirical loss
F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta):=\eta f_{\theta,1}(s_{1})-L_{\mathcal{D}}^{\mathsf{BE}}(f_{\theta})
, thus bridging with the literature on private (minimax) optimization (e.g.,
[
8
,
6
,
55
]
).

Coverability vs Eluder condition.
Instead of using coverability, another related approach is based on Bellman-Eluder dimension, such as the Eluder-Condition class of
[
52
]
, which provides a broad framework beyond several standard structural models. However, Bellman-Eluder-dimension-based analyses can be suboptimal in some cases, yielding a
T^{2/3}
regret rate even when a
\sqrt{T}
bound is achievable under coverability, see Proposition 11 in
[
51
]
. Going beyond coverability may require modifying the
\mathsf{SEC}
of
[
51
]
, in a way similar to how
[
52
]
modified the Bellman-Eluder dimension; we leave this as an interesting open problem.

Infinite function class.
Our general regret bound in Section
3.3
provides us with some guidelines for dealing with an infinite function class
\mathcal{F}
. Specifically, we only need to focus on the two error terms
\mathsf{Err}_{\mathsf{stat}}
and
\mathsf{Err}_{\mathsf{priv}}
. For
\mathsf{Err}_{\mathsf{stat}}
, one can follow the standard approach in the non-private case to adopt a covering number argument, replacing the
|\mathcal{F}|
by the covering number (with some approximation error). For
\mathsf{Err}_{\mathsf{priv}}
, one can also potentially
explicitly
construct a covering set in the algorithm and apply the exponential mechanism on the finite covering set. Due to the page limit, we give a formal guarantee in Appendix
B
for more details.

Gaps in private RL with linear MDPs.
In both works
[
38
,
45
]
, the authors adopt the idea of the rare policy switch via the standard determinant trick argument over the design matrix. This is a
data-dependent adaptive
update rather than the fixed-size batching in our paper, hence enabling only a total of
\log K
updates, which further reduces the privacy composition. However, due to its data-dependent nature, one also has to privatize the design matrix. This leads to the gap in their analysis, as the standard condition for the determinant trick fails for a private design matrix. In particular,
[
38
]
incorrectly handles monotonicity in both Lemma 31, concerning log-order switches, and Equation 20, when applying the standard determinant trick
[
1
]
. These errors subsequently propagate to the follow-up work by
[
45
]
, see Appendix
C
for more detail.

Conclusion.
In this paper, we established the first theoretical guarantees for private online RL with general function approximation. By combining a batched policy update scheme with an unconstrained exploration objective and the exponential mechanism, we showed that sublinear regret remains achievable under JDP, with regret scaling as
K^{3/5}
and depending on the standard notion of coverability. Our analysis not only matches the best known model-free bounds for linear function approximation, but also yields new regret guarantees for RL with adaptivity constraints even in the non-private setting. We also clarified fundamental gaps in recent claims of improved regret bounds for private RL with linear MDPs. We believe our framework provides a principled foundation for future work on private RL with rich function classes, outcome rewards, and alternative privacy mechanisms.

Appendix A
Detailed Related Work

Private RL.

Under the central trust model of joint differential privacy (JDP), the first theoretical guarantees for private reinforcement learning were established by
[
48
]
in the tabular setting, via privatized
UCB-VI
–type algorithms (e.g.,
[
4
]
). Subsequently,
[
10
]
improved the regret bounds and introduced the first policy-based (as opposed to value-based) private tabular RL algorithm. The current state of the art in the tabular regime is due to
[
42
]
, which leverages Bernstein-type exploration bonuses.

Moving beyond tabular environments, existing theoretical results for private RL are largely restricted to linear function approximation. In particular,
[
57
]
obtained
\sqrt{K}
regret bounds for private RL with linear function approximation in the model-based setting via linear mixture MDPs
[
3
]
, while
[
36
]
provided the first model-free result for linear MDPs
[
25
]
, achieving a regret bound of order
K^{3/5}
. Motivated by this separation, several recent works
[
38
,
45
]
revisit the model-free setting and claim to close the gap by deriving
\sqrt{K}
regret bounds. However, we identify a common and fundamental gap in their analyses (cf. Section
4
), rendering these improved bounds ungrounded. As a result, for private RL with linear function approximation, the best known regret guarantee in the model-free setting remains
K^{3/5}
. We also note in passing that a parallel line of work studies private RL under the stronger local differential privacy model—without a trusted central learner—which is beyond the scope of this paper
[
18
,
10
,
33
]
.

RL with general function approximation.

In the non-private setting, substantial recent progress has been made in understanding exploration complexity beyond linear function approximation. Prominent complexity measures include the Eluder dimension
[
49
,
44
,
39
]
, the Bellman-Eluder dimension
[
24
]
, Bellman rank
[
23
]
, witness rank
[
47
]
, bilinear classes
[
12
]
, and decision-estimation coefficients (DEC)
[
16
]
. Among these,
[
51
]
introduced the notion of
coverability
and showed that it suffices to guarantee sample-efficient online RL with general function approximation.

On the algorithmic side, while most prior works rely on constrained optimization over confidence sets,
[
35
]
proposed an unconstrained optimization framework and established sublinear regret guarantees in terms of the generalized Eluder coefficient (GEC)
[
56
]
. This complexity measure is closely related to the sequential extrapolation coefficient (SEC), introduced by
[
51
]
as a generalization of coverability. While these works typically assume access to per-step process rewards,
[
9
]
recently took the first step toward studying RL with general function approximation under outcome rewards, relying on a similar unconstrained optimization approach in
[
35
]
with additional algorithmic changes for outcome reward.

RL with adaptivity constraints.

This line of work studies RL under restrictions on how frequently the policy can be updated, motivated by practical concerns such as policy switching costs. A simple and data-independent approach is fixed-size batching, where the policy is updated only at the beginning of each batch. This setting has been extensively studied in bandits (e.g.,
[
17
,
20
]
) and later extended to RL with linear function approximation
[
49
]
. More recently,
[
52
]
analyzed batching under general function approximation and established sublinear regret guarantees for a newly introduced class of MDPs termed the Eluder-Condition.
An alternative approach is data-dependent rare policy switching, dating back to
[
1
]
in the bandit setting. Compared to fixed-size batching, rare policy switching typically enables
\sqrt{K}
regret with only
O(\log K)
policy updates, rather than
O(\sqrt{K})
. Such results have been obtained for tabular
[
5
]
, linear
[
50
]
, and general function approximation settings
[
29
,
43
,
52
]
.
Both batching and rare policy switching have also been employed in private bandits and private RL to control privacy loss composition. For example,
[
11
]
applies batching to study shuffle privacy in linear contextual bandits, and
[
36
]
used it for private RL with linear function approximation. Meanwhile, rare policy switching with
O(\log K)
updates is the primary technique used in
[
38
,
45
]
to claim
\sqrt{K}
regret bounds for private RL; however, due to additional privacy noise, these analyses are flawed, as discussed in detail in Section
4
.

Appendix B
Detailed Discussion for Infinite Function Class

To handle an infinite function class, we define the covering number as follows:

Definition B.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\{f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{H}):f_{h}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\}
, and define

\displaystyle\|f-g\|_{\infty}:=\max_{h\in[H]}\sup_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}|f_{h}(s,a)-g_{h}(s,a)|.

For
\beta\geq 0
, a set
\mathcal{C}_{\beta}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
is a
\beta
-cover of
\mathcal{F}
, if for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
, there exists
\tilde{f}\in\mathcal{C}_{\beta}
such that
\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{\infty}\leq\beta
. The
\beta
-covering number is

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\beta):=\min\{|\mathcal{C}_{\beta}|:\mathcal{C}_{\beta}\text{ is a }\beta\text{-cover of }\mathcal{F}\}.

Algorithm.
To handle an infinite function class, we only need to change the
input
of Algorithm
1
, specifically, fix
\beta>0
, explicitly construct a finite
\beta
-cover
\mathcal{C}_{\beta}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
and replace
\mathcal{F}
by
\mathcal{C}_{\beta}
in Algorithm
1
. Hence, in each update episode
k=iB+1
, we compute scores on this finite
\mathcal{C}_{\beta}
instead of infinite
\mathcal{F}
, which allows us to run the exponential mechanism.

Thus, we can derive a formal corollary:

Corollary B.2

(Infinite class reduction via explicit covering)
.

Assume the same conditions as Theorem
3.1
, except that
\mathcal{F}
can be infinite. Fix
\beta>0
and run the above revised algorithm with an explicit
\beta
-cover
\mathcal{C}_{\beta}
, there exist proper choices of
\beta_{\varepsilon,\delta}
,
\eta
and
B
, such that the revised algorithm is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-JDP while achieving the following regret bound with probability at least
1-\alpha

\displaystyle\mathsf{Err}_{\mathsf{stat}}\lesssim\log\left(\frac{\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\beta)KH}{\alpha}\right)\quad\mathsf{Err}_{\mathsf{priv}}\lesssim\log\left(\frac{\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\beta)K}{\alpha}\right)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathsf{Reg}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\delta}H\sqrt{C_{\mathsf{cov}}K\log\left(\frac{\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\beta)KH}{\alpha}\right)\log K}

\displaystyle\quad+H(C_{\mathsf{cov}}K\log K)^{3/5}\cdot\left(\frac{\log(\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\beta)K/\alpha)}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2/5}+\beta K.

Remark B.3
.

The first two terms are exactly the finite-class bound with
|\mathcal{F}|
replaced by
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F},\beta)
, and
\beta K
is the additional approximation error over
K
episodes from restricting the Exponential Mechanism to sample exclusively from
\mathcal{C}_{\beta}
.

Appendix C
Gaps in Private RL with Linear MDPs

In this section, we further clarify the gaps in prior work.

As discussed in Section
4
, because rare policy switching is data-dependent, applying the standard determinant-trick argument requires privatizing the design matrix. This introduces Gaussian privacy noise, which is not a non-negative rank-one update. Consequently, the monotonicity property need not hold, making the follow-up arguments ungrounded.

Monotonicity of the design matrix is required for two claims: (a)
log-order switches
(Lemma 31 in
[
38
]
, key for privacy accounting), and (b)
norm factor bound
(Eq. 20 in
[
38
]
), namely that the norms induced by two design matrices are within a constant factor of each other.

For (a), we dive into Lemma 31 in
[
38
]
, where the determinant doubling trick is adopted. In particular, without monotonicity, the following step in their proof would fail:

\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{h=1}^{H}\det(\tilde{\Lambda}_{h}^{k_{i}})}{\prod_{h=1}^{H}\det(\tilde{\Lambda}_{h}^{k_{i-1}})}=\frac{\det(\tilde{\Lambda}_{h^{\star}}^{k_{i}})}{\det(\tilde{\Lambda}_{h^{\star}}^{k_{i-1}})}\times\prod_{h\neq h^{*}}\frac{\det(\tilde{\Lambda}_{h}^{k_{i}})}{\det(\tilde{\Lambda}_{h}^{k_{i-1}})}>2

To see this, note that the first term is greater than 2 by definition of the switching design (triggered by a particular
h^{\star}\in[H]
, see line 6 of Algorithm 1 in
[
38
]
), but the second term may be less than 1 if the design matrix is not monotone. This indeed can happen because their Algorithm 1 will add fresh privacy noise (via Binary tree mechanism) to each design matrix at each
h\in[H]
(see line 20 of Algorithm 1 in
[
38
]
), which can make the private design matrix non-monotone, even though it is still PSD.

For (b), the norm factor bound (Eq. 20 in
[
38
]
) fails because the standard determinant trick (Lemma 12 in
[
1
]
) strictly requires monotonicity. Their analysis relies on the following inequality holding for the current private matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{h}^{k}
and the last-updated matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{h}^{\tilde{k}}
:

\displaystyle(\phi_{h}^{k})^{\top}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{h}^{\tilde{k}}\right)^{-1}\phi_{h}^{k}\leqslant 2(\phi_{h}^{k})^{\top}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{h}^{k}\right)^{-1}\phi_{h}^{k}

However, as noted in (a), Algorithm 1 (line 20) in
[
38
]
injects fluctuating noise via the Binary tree mechanism at every episode. This inherently breaks monotonicity (
\tilde{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{h}^{k}\nsucceq\tilde{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{h}^{\tilde{k}}
), meaning
\tilde{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{h}^{k}
can shrink along certain feature directions. Without monotonicity, the inverse matrix relationship breaks down. Instead of being bounded by a constant factor of
2
, the ratio can blow up exponentially in dimension
d
, fundamentally invalidating the claimed
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{K})
regret bound.

Appendix D
Experiments

While our main focus is to provide theoretical understandings of DP-RL, rather than extensive empirical studies, we conducted a small
proof-of-concept
simulation in a deterministic outcome-reward setting designed to mirror the structure of Algorithm
2
while remaining simple enough to implement exactly. All experiments were run on a standard CPU machine and took less than a few minutes; no GPU was used.

Environment construction.
We consider a finite-horizon deterministic episodic MDP with horizon
H=4
, setting a binary action space
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}
. At the beginning of each episode, a binary context

\displaystyle x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{6})\in\{0,1\}^{6}

is sampled uniformly at random. Thus, the state at stage
h
is
s_{h}=(x,a_{1},\dots,a_{h-1},h)
, so the transition is deterministic, since the next state is obtained by appending the chosen action.

There is no intermediate reward; the environment provides only a terminal outcome reward
r\in\{0,1\}
at the end of the episode, which is determined by a hidden target hypothesis.

An episode receives a reward of
1
if the full action sequence matches the target sequence prescribed by the hidden hypothesis for the realized context, and
0
otherwise. Thus, this is a deterministic outcome-reward MDP.

Finite nonlinear hypothesis class.
We use a finite nonlinear hypothesis class with
|\mathcal{F}|=243
. Each hypothesis consists of a binary gate
g(x)\in\{0,1\}
, chosen from

\displaystyle g_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle g_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=x_{5}\oplus x_{6},

\displaystyle g_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=x_{1}\oplus x_{3}\oplus x_{5},

and one
stagewise action rule
for each stage
h=1,2,3,4
, chosen from

\displaystyle u^{(0)}(x,\text{prefix})

\displaystyle=x_{1}\oplus x_{2},

\displaystyle u^{(1)}(x,\text{prefix})

\displaystyle=x_{3}\oplus a_{h-1}\quad(a_{0}:=0),

\displaystyle u^{(2)}(x,\text{prefix})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{parity}(a_{1},\dots,a_{h-1})\oplus x_{4}.

Hence, each hypothesis defines a nonlinear, context-dependent, and history-dependent target action sequence.

Easy and hard instances.
We report results on two instances. In the
easy environment
, the hidden target gate is
g_{0}(x)=1
, and the stage rules are
(u^{(0)},u^{(1)},u^{(0)},u^{(1)})
. In this case, every context is rewardable, so positive outcomes are relatively dense.

In the
hard environment
, the hidden target gate is
g_{2}(x)=x_{1}\oplus x_{3}\oplus x_{5}
, and the stage rules are
(u^{(2)},u^{(1)},u^{(2)},u^{(1)})
. In this case, only a subset of contexts are rewardable, and the correct sequence depends more strongly on action-history parity, making learning more difficult.

Methods compared.
We compare four methods:

•

non_private_no_batch
, a non-private method updated after every episode;

•

non_private_batched
, a non-private method restricted to batched updates;

•

private_eps_8
, a private batched selection method with privacy/noise parameter
\varepsilon=8
;

•

private_eps_5
, a private batched selection method with privacy/noise parameter
\varepsilon=5
.

For the private methods, we use the same batched update structure as in the proof-of-concept implementation and replace exact maximization with exponential-mechanism-style sampling over the finite hypothesis class. The batch size is selected according to the theory.

Reporting metric: plateau episode.

We summarize each regret curve by its
plateau episode
. We define the plateau episode as the first episode at which
95% of the final cumulative regret
has already been incurred. This gives a robust scalar summary of how quickly the regret curve enters its flat regime.

Interpretation.
These results show the expected trend clearly. In both environments, the non-private methods plateau fastest. Among the private methods, the larger privacy parameter,
\varepsilon=8
, reaches the plateau regime substantially earlier than
\varepsilon=5
. The hard environment requires more episodes than the easy environment across all methods. In particular, in the easy environment, the plateau time increases from 37 episodes at
\varepsilon=8
to 127 episodes at
\varepsilon=5
. In the hard environment, it increases from 87 episodes at
\varepsilon=8
to 218 episodes at
\varepsilon=5
.

Thus, even in this minimal proof-of-concept simulation, weaker privacy constraints lead to faster convergence, while batching introduces a visible but smaller additional slowdown compared with the non-batched non-private baseline.

Summary.

This experiment is intentionally small-scale, but it directly validates the qualitative privacy-utility tradeoff emphasized in the paper: as privacy becomes stronger, more episodes are required to reach the stable learning regime. It also shows that the deterministic outcome-reward setting with general nonlinear finite function approximation already exhibits the expected separation among non-private, batched non-private, and private learning.

Lemma E.2

Proof.
For any
f\in\mathcal{F}
, define

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{1}:=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\rho}\max_{a}f_{1}(s,a).

In Algorithm
2
, the score function is

\displaystyle S(f):=\eta\bar{f}_{1}-L_{\mathcal{D}}^{\mathsf{BR}}(f).

Let
[k]
denote the beginning of the batch containing episode
k
, and let
\mathcal{D}_{[k]}
be the dataset available at that time. By the exponential mechanism and a union bound over all batch updates, with probability at least
1-\alpha
, for every episode
k
,

\displaystyle\eta\bar{f}_{1}^{(k)}-L_{\mathcal{D}_{[k]}}^{\mathsf{BR}}(f^{(k)})\geq\eta\bar{Q}^{\star}_{1}-L_{\mathcal{D}_{[k]}}^{\mathsf{BR}}(Q^{\star})-\frac{2\Delta_{S}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha),

where
\bar{Q}^{\star}_{1}:=\mathbb{E}_{s_{1}\sim\rho}\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q^{\star}_{1}(s_{1},a)=J^{\star}
.

Rearranging, we have

\displaystyle J^{\star}\leq\bar{f}^{(k)}_{1}-\frac{1}{\eta}\left(L_{\mathcal{D}_{[k]}}^{\mathsf{BR}}(f^{(k)})-L_{\mathcal{D}_{[k]}}^{\mathsf{BR}}(Q^{\star})\right)+\frac{2\Delta_{S}}{\eta\varepsilon_{0}}\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha).

Thus we subtracting
J(\pi_{k})=\mathbb{E}_{s_{1}\sim\rho}V^{\pi_{k}}(s_{1})
and obtain

\displaystyle J^{\star}-J(\pi_{k})\leq\bar{f}_{1}^{(k)}-J(\pi_{k})-\frac{1}{\eta}\left(L_{\mathcal{D}_{[k]}}^{\mathsf{BR}}(f^{(k)})-L_{\mathcal{D}_{[k]}}^{\mathsf{BR}}(Q^{\star})\right)+\frac{2\Delta_{S}}{\eta\varepsilon_{0}}\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha).

Since
\pi_{k}
is greedy with respect to
f^{(k)}
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{1}^{(k)}-J(\pi_{k})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s_{1}\sim\rho,\pi_{k}}\left[\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left(f_{h}^{(k)}(s_{h},a_{h})-f_{h+1}^{(k)}(s_{h+1})-R_{h}(s_{h},a_{h})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s_{1}\sim\rho,\pi_{k}}\left[R^{(f^{(k)})}(\tau)-r(\tau)\right].

Denote
e^{(k)}(\tau):=R^{(f^{(k)})}(\tau)-r(\tau)
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{1}^{(k)}-J(\pi_{k})=\mathbb{E}_{s_{1}\sim\rho,\pi_{k}}\left[e^{(k)}(\tau)\right].

Recall that

\displaystyle L_{\mathcal{D}_{[k]}}^{\mathsf{BR}}(f):=\sum_{t<[k]}\left(R^{(f)}(\tau^{(t)})-r^{(t)}\right)^{2},

where
(\tau^{(t)},r^{(t)})
denotes the data collected in episode
t
.

By the uniform convergence of the square loss(Proposition 19 in
[
9
]
)there exists an event of probability at least
1-\alpha
such that, for all
k
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t<[k]}\mathbb{E}_{s_{1}\sim\rho,\pi_{t}}\left[e^{(k)}(\tau)^{2}\right]\leq L_{\mathcal{D}_{[k]}}^{\mathsf{BR}}(f^{(k)})-L_{\mathcal{D}_{[k]}}^{\mathsf{BR}}(Q^{\star})+\kappa,

(5)

where
\kappa=O\left(H^{3}\log\frac{|\mathcal{F}|K}{\alpha}\right)
.

Rearranging terms and applying (
5
), we obtain

\displaystyle J^{\star}-J(\pi_{k})\leq\frac{\kappa}{\eta}+\frac{2\Delta_{S}}{\varepsilon_{0}\eta}\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha)+\mathbb{E}_{s_{1}\sim\rho,\pi_{k}}\left[e^{(k)}(\tau)\right]-\frac{1}{2\eta}\sum_{t<[k]}\mathbb{E}_{s_{1}\sim\rho,\pi_{t}}\left[e^{(k)}(\tau)^{2}\right].

Summing over
k=1,\ldots,K
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(J^{\star}-J(\pi_{k})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K\kappa}{\eta}+\frac{2K\Delta_{S}}{\varepsilon_{0}\eta}\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{s_{1}\sim\rho,\pi_{k}}\left[e^{(k)}(\tau)\right]-\frac{1}{2\eta}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t<[k]}\mathbb{E}_{s_{1}\sim\rho,\pi_{t}}\left[e^{(k)}(\tau)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{K\kappa}{\eta}+\frac{2K\Delta_{S}}{\varepsilon_{0}\eta}\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha)+\mathbb{E}_{s_{1}\sim\rho}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}}\left[e^{(k)}(\tau)\middle|s_{1}\right]-\frac{1}{2\eta}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t<[k]}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}\left[e^{(k)}(\tau)^{2}\middle|s_{1}\right]\right].

Lemma E.4
.

Let
\lambda\geq 1
. For any initial state
s_{1}\in\mathcal{S}
, consider the sequence
\{f^{(k)},\pi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
generated by Algorithm
2
, where
\pi_{k}=\pi_{f^{(k)}}
is the greedy policy with fixed deterministic tie-breaking. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}}\left[e^{(k)}(\tau)\middle|s_{1}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{BN(s_{1})}{2\log 2}\log\left(1+\frac{KH}{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2\sqrt{2N(s_{1})\log\left(1+\frac{KH}{\lambda}\right)}\cdot\sqrt{4K\lambda+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t<[k]}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}\left[e^{(k)}(\tau)\middle|s_{1}\right]\right)^{2}},

where
N(s_{1})
is the total number of reachable state-action pairs from
s_{1}
under policy class
\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}
.

For a fixed
s_{1}
, applying Lemma
E.4
, Jensen’s inequality
\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}\left[e^{(k)}(\tau)\middle|s_{1}\right]\right)^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}\left[e^{(k)}(\tau)^{2}\middle|s_{1}\right]
and Cauchy-Schwarz in the form
xy\leq\eta x^{2}+\frac{1}{4\eta}y^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}}\left[e^{(k)}(\tau)\middle|s_{1}\right]\leq\frac{BN(s_{1})}{2\log 2}\log\left(1+\frac{KH}{\lambda}\right)+\eta N(s_{1})\log\left(1+\frac{KH}{\lambda}\right)+\frac{K\lambda}{\eta}+\frac{1}{2\eta}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t<[k]}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}\left[e^{(k)}(\tau)^{2}\middle|s_{1}\right].

Combining the inequalities above and setting
\lambda=\kappa
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(J^{\star}-J(\pi_{k})\right)\leq\frac{2K\kappa}{\eta}+\frac{2K\Delta_{S}}{\varepsilon_{0}\eta}\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha)+\mathbb{E}_{s_{1}\sim\rho}\left[\frac{BN(s_{1})}{2\log 2}\log\left(1+\frac{KH}{\kappa}\right)+\eta N(s_{1})\log\left(1+\frac{KH}{\kappa}\right)\right].

For each
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
h\in[H]
, define

\displaystyle S_{h}(s;\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}):=\left\{(s^{\prime},a):\exists\pi\in\Pi_{\mathcal{F}},\text{ under }\pi\text{ and }s_{1}=s,\text{ it holds that }s_{h}=s^{\prime},a_{h}=a\right\}.

and let
N_{h}(s;\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}):=\left|S_{h}(s;\Pi_{\mathcal{F}})\right|
and
N(s):=\sum_{h=1}^{H}N_{h}(s;\Pi_{\mathcal{F}})
.

Under deterministic MDPs, given
s_{1}
, every greedy policy in
\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}
with fixed deterministic tie-breaking reaches a single element in
S_{h}(s_{1};\Pi_{\mathcal{F}})
at each layer
h
. Hence the layer-wise coverability of
M_{s_{1}}^{\star}
is characterized by
N_{h}(s_{1};\Pi_{\mathcal{F}})
, and therefore

\displaystyle N(s_{1})\leq H\cdot C_{\mathsf{cov}}(\Pi_{\mathcal{F}};M_{s_{1}}^{\star}).

Taking expectation over
s_{1}\sim\rho
, we derive

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s_{1}\sim\rho}[N(s_{1})]\leq H\cdot C^{\prime}_{\mathsf{cov}}.

Substituting back, with
C:=HC^{\prime}_{\mathsf{cov}}\log\left(1+\frac{KH}{\kappa}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(J^{\star}-J(\pi_{k})\right)\leq\frac{2K\kappa}{\eta}+\frac{2K\Delta_{S}}{\varepsilon_{0}\eta}\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha)+\eta\cdot C+B\cdot C.

(6)

For the non-private term, we balance the standard regret term and the regularization term:

\displaystyle\frac{2K\kappa}{\eta}=C\eta\quad\rightarrow\quad\eta=\sqrt{\frac{2K\kappa}{C}}.

Substituting this back, the non-private part is

\displaystyle\frac{2K\kappa}{\eta}+\eta C=O\left(\sqrt{K\kappa C}\right).

For the private case, we balance

\displaystyle\frac{K\Delta_{S}}{\eta\varepsilon_{0}}\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha)=C\eta.

Given by the
Advanced Sequential Composition
(Lemma
E.2
), we consider the worst-case privacy loss:

\displaystyle\varepsilon=O\left(\sqrt{\frac{K}{B}\log(1/\delta)}\cdot\varepsilon_{0}\right).

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0}=\Omega\left(\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{B}}{\sqrt{K\log(1/\delta)}}\right).

Substituting back, we obtain

\displaystyle\eta=O\left(\frac{K^{3/4}\Delta_{S}^{1/2}\left(\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha)\right)^{1/2}\left(\log(1/\delta)\right)^{1/4}}{B^{1/4}\varepsilon^{1/2}C^{1/2}}\right).

Finally, substituting this choice of
\eta
back to (
6
), the privacy contribution is

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{K^{3/4}C^{1/2}\Delta_{S}^{1/2}\left(\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha)\right)^{1/2}\left(\log(1/\delta)\right)^{1/4}}{B^{1/4}\varepsilon^{1/2}}\right).

We balance this term with the batching term
B\cdot C
:

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{K^{3/4}C^{1/2}\Delta_{S}^{1/2}\left(\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha)\right)^{1/2}\left(\log(1/\delta)\right)^{1/4}}{B^{1/4}\varepsilon^{1/2}}\right)=O(B\cdot C),

which gives

\displaystyle B=O\left(\frac{K^{3/5}\Delta_{S}^{2/5}\left(\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha)\right)^{2/5}\left(\log(1/\delta)\right)^{1/5}}{\varepsilon^{2/5}C^{2/5}}\right).

We now turn to bound the sensitivity
\Delta_{S}
. Note that

\displaystyle S_{\mathcal{D}}(f)=\eta\bar{f}_{1}-L_{\mathcal{D}}^{\mathsf{BR}}(f).

Since the first term
\eta\bar{f}_{1}
does not depend on
\mathcal{D}
, the sensitivity is entirely from
L_{\mathcal{D}}^{\mathsf{BR}}(f)
. Recall

\displaystyle L_{\mathcal{D}}^{\mathsf{BR}}(f)=\sum_{(\tau,r)\in\mathcal{D}}\left(R^{(f)}(\tau)-r\right)^{2}.

For each
h
, we have
f_{h}(s_{h},a_{h})\in[0,1]
and
f_{h+1}(s_{h+1})\in[0,1]
, so

\displaystyle R^{(f)}(\tau)=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left[f_{h}(s_{h},a_{h})-f_{h+1}(s_{h+1})\right]\in[-H,H].

Since
r\in[0,1]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left|R^{(f)}(\tau)-r\right|\leq H+1.

Consequently, changing one trajectory changes
L_{\mathcal{D}}^{\mathsf{BR}}(f)
by at most
(H+1)^{2}
, and we conclude that

\displaystyle\Delta_{S}=O(H^{2}).

Similarly, by applying the
billboard lemma
(Lemma
E.3
), Algorithm
2
satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-JDP.

Setting

\displaystyle\eta=\max\left\{\sqrt{\frac{2K\kappa}{C}},\frac{K^{3/4}\Delta_{S}^{1/2}\left(\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha)\right)^{1/2}\left(\log(1/\delta)\right)^{1/4}}{B^{1/4}\varepsilon^{1/2}C^{1/2}}\right\},

and using the above choice of
B
, we have the final bound

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(J^{\star}-J(\pi_{k})\right)\leq O\left(\sqrt{K\kappa C}\right)+O\left(\frac{K^{3/5}C^{3/5}\Delta_{S}^{2/5}\left(\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha)\right)^{2/5}\left(\log(1/\delta)\right)^{1/5}}{\varepsilon^{2/5}}\right).

Plugging in

\displaystyle C=H\cdot C^{\prime}_{\mathsf{cov}}\log\left(1+\frac{KH}{\kappa}\right),\quad\kappa=O\left(H^{3}\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha)\right),\quad\Delta_{S}=O(H^{2}),

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathsf{Reg}\leq 2H^{2}\sqrt{2C^{\prime}_{\mathsf{cov}}K\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha)\log K}+H^{7/5}\frac{(C^{\prime}_{\mathsf{cov}}K\log K)^{3/5}\left(\log(|\mathcal{F}|K/\alpha)\right)^{2/5}(\log(1/\delta))^{1/5}}{\varepsilon^{2/5}}

∎

Proof of Lemma
E.4
.

Fix an initial state
s_{1}\in\mathcal{S}
. Define the index set of reachable state-action pairs

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}:=\{(h,s,a):h\in[H],(s,a)\in S_{h}(s_{1};\Pi_{\mathcal{F}})\}\subseteq[H]\times\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A},\quad|\mathcal{I}|=N(s_{1}).

For each episode
k\in[K]
, define
\psi^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}|}
by

\displaystyle\psi^{(k)}_{(h,s,a)}:=f_{h}^{(k)}(s,a)-f_{h+1}^{(k)}(s^{+}_{h}(s,a))-R_{h}(s,a),

where
s^{+}_{h}(s,a)
is the unique next state reached from
(s,a)
at step
h
under the deterministic transition, and
f_{h+1}^{(k)}(s):=\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}f_{h+1}^{(k)}(s,a^{\prime})
with the convention
f_{H+1}^{(k)}\equiv 0
.

And define the occupancy vector
\phi^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}|}
by

\displaystyle\phi^{(k)}_{(h,s,a)}:=\mathbb{P}_{\pi_{k}}(s_{h}=s,a_{h}=a|s_{1}).

Then, by the definition of
e^{(k)}(\tau)
and the deterministic transition, the on-policy error in episode
k
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}}\left[e^{(k)}(\tau)\middle|s_{1}\right]=\langle\psi^{(k)},\phi^{(k)}\rangle.

Thus, our target is to bound
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\langle\psi^{(k)},\phi^{(k)}\rangle\right|
.

Define the covariance matrices based on historical visitations

\displaystyle V_{k}:=\lambda I+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\phi^{(j)}\phi^{(j)\top}.

Since the policy is updated only at batch boundaries, the historical covariance matrix available at the beginning of the batch containing episode
k
is

\displaystyle V_{[k]-1}=\lambda I+\sum_{t<[k]}\phi^{(t)}\phi^{(t)\top},

where
[k]
denotes the first episode of the batch containing episode
k
.

Define the bad-round set
\Psi
where the covariance matrix at the beginning of the batch significantly underestimates the real-time matrix

\displaystyle\Psi:=\left\{k\in[K]:\frac{\|\phi^{(k)}\|_{V_{[k]-1}^{-1}}}{\|\phi^{(k)}\|_{V_{k-1}^{-1}}}>2\right\}.

We decompose the total sum into bad and good rounds

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\langle\psi^{(k)},\phi^{(k)}\rangle\right|=\sum_{k\in\Psi}\left|\langle\psi^{(k)},\phi^{(k)}\rangle\right|+\sum_{k\notin\Psi}\left|\langle\psi^{(k)},\phi^{(k)}\rangle\right|.

For good rounds (
k\notin\Psi
), by definition
\|\phi^{(k)}\|_{V_{[k]-1}^{-1}}\leq 2\|\phi^{(k)}\|_{V_{k-1}^{-1}}
. Hence, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\|\langle\psi^{(k)},\phi^{(k)}\rangle\|\leq\|\psi^{(k)}\|_{V_{[k]-1}}\cdot\|\phi^{(k)}\|_{V_{[k]-1}^{-1}}\leq 2\|\psi^{(k)}\|_{V_{[k]-1}}\cdot\|\phi^{(k)}\|_{V_{k-1}^{-1}}.

Summing over good rounds and applying Cauchy–Schwarz again to the sum

\displaystyle\sum_{k\notin\Psi}\left|\langle\psi^{(k)},\phi^{(k)}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\min\left\{1,\|\phi^{(k)}\|_{V_{k-1}^{-1}}^{2}\right\}}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\max\left\{1,\|\psi^{(k)}\|_{V_{[k]-1}}^{2}\right\}}.

By the elliptical potential lemma
[
31
]
, and using
\|\phi^{(k)}\|_{2}^{2}\leq H
, the first term is bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\min\left\{1,\|\phi^{(k)}\|_{V_{k-1}^{-1}}^{2}\right\}\leq 2N(s_{1})\log\left(1+\frac{KH}{N(s_{1})\lambda}\right).

For the second term involving
\psi^{(k)}
, by the definition of
V_{[k]-1}
,

\displaystyle\|\psi^{(k)}\|_{V_{[k]-1}}^{2}=\lambda\|\psi^{(k)}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{t<[k]}\langle\phi^{(t)},\psi^{(k)}\rangle^{2}.

Moreover, for every
t<[k]
,

\displaystyle\langle\phi^{(t)},\psi^{(k)}\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}\left[e^{(k)}(\tau)\middle|s_{1}\right].

By the definition of
\psi^{(k)}
, we have
\|\psi^{(k)}\|_{\infty}\leq 2
and obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\max\left\{1,\|\psi^{(k)}\|_{V_{[k]-1}}^{2}\right\}\leq 4K\lambda N(s_{1})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t<[k]}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}\left[e^{(k)}(\tau)\middle|s_{1}\right]\right)^{2}.

Now we bound the bad rounds. For those bad rounds (
k\in\Psi
), since
\pi_{k}
is greedy with respect to
f^{(k)}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{(k)},\phi^{(k)}\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}}[e^{(k)}(\tau)|s_{1}]=f_{1}^{(k)}(s_{1})-V^{\pi_{k}}(s_{1}).

By the normalization of rewards and the range of functions in
\mathcal{F}
, both terms lie in
[0,1]
. Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\Psi}\left|\langle\psi^{(k)},\phi^{(k)}\rangle\right|\leq|\Psi|.

We bound
|\Psi|
using a determinant argument. Let
t_{m}:=(m-1)B+1
denote the first episode of batch
m
, with the convention
t_{M+1}:=K+1
. For any
k\in\Psi
, suppose
k
belongs to batch
m
, so that
t_{m}=[k]
. By the definition of
\Psi
,

\displaystyle\frac{\|\phi^{(k)}\|_{V_{t_{m}-1}^{-1}}}{\|\phi^{(k)}\|_{V_{k-1}^{-1}}}>2.

Since
V_{t_{m}-1}\preceq V_{k-1}
, the standard determinant comparison inequality implies

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\det(V_{k-1})}{\det(V_{t_{m}-1})}\right)>2\log 2.

Moreover, since
V_{k-1}\preceq V_{t_{m+1}-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\det(V_{t_{m+1}-1})}{\det(V_{t_{m}-1})}\right)>2\log 2.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{\Psi}
be the set of batch-start indices
m
whose batch contains at least one bad round. Summing the above inequality over these bad batches and bounding it by the total log-determinant growth yields

\displaystyle|\mathcal{M}_{\Psi}|\cdot(2\log 2)\leq\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}_{\Psi}}\log\frac{\det(V_{t_{m+1}-1})}{\det(V_{t_{m}-1})}\leq\log\frac{\det(V_{K})}{\det(V_{0})}.

Applying the standard determinant bound

\displaystyle\log\frac{\det V_{K}}{\det V_{0}}\leq N(s_{1})\log\left(1+\frac{KH}{N(s_{1})\lambda}\right),

we can solve for the number of bad batches

\displaystyle|\mathcal{M}_{\Psi}|\leq\frac{N(s_{1})}{2\log 2}\log\left(1+\frac{KH}{N(s_{1})\lambda}\right).

Since each batch has size
B
, the total number of bad rounds
|\Psi|
is at most
B\cdot|\mathcal{M}_{\Psi}|
. Therefore

\displaystyle|\Psi|\leq\frac{BN(s_{1})}{2\log 2}\log\left(1+\frac{KH}{N(s_{1})\lambda}\right).

Combining the bounds for good and bad rounds and rescaling
\lambda\leftarrow\frac{\lambda}{N(s_{1})}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}}\left[e^{(k)}(\tau)\middle|s_{1}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{BN(s_{1})}{2\log 2}\log\left(1+\frac{KH}{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2\sqrt{2N(s_{1})\log\left(1+\frac{KH}{\lambda}\right)}\cdot\sqrt{4K\lambda+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t<[k]}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}}[e^{(k)}(\tau)\middle|s_{1}]\right)^{2}}.

This completes the proof.
∎
