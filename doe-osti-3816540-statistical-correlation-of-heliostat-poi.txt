Executive Summary
This study was conducted as part of the Heliostat Consortium (HelioCon) Field Deployment
subtask with the aim of developing a reduced order model framework for correlating wind speed
and pointing deviation of a heliostat facet. There are only sparse field measurements of heliostat
pointing deviations and accompanying wind conditions published in the literature. Heliostat test
standards, such as International Electrotechnical Commission 62862-4-3, propose a suite of tests,
including laser pointing repeatability at wind speeds below 4 meters per second (m/s), and
provide technical requirements for heliostat slope and tracking deviations in coarse average wind
speed bins of 4 m/s, 6 m/s, and 8 m/s. To address the gap in the variation of heliostat pointing
deviation with wind speed, our team analyzed field measurements of laser pointing on a grid
target and wind conditions in this study at the Third-Party Metrology Platform at the National
Laboratory of the Rockies Flatirons Campus.

Horizontal pointing deviations were found to follow a logarithmic relationship with peak wind
speed, whereas vertical pointing deviations follow an exponential relationship with peak wind
speed. Both horizontal and vertical pointing deviations also follow a second order polynomial
relationship, as expected from the proportionality of loads and deformations with the square of
wind speed. The results indicate heliostat facet pointing deviations in the vertical direction
increase at a faster rate than in the horizontal direction with increasing wind speed over the tested
range; however, these are dependent on the heliostat structural design.

The recommended next steps are for additional field measurements (1) to confirm a linear
relationship of pointing deviation with applied moment on a heliostat facet and (2) to distinguish
between gravity-induced and wind-induced pointing deviations at different elevation angles. The
derived correlations in the preliminary analysis in this report serve as a case study for heliostat
developers and plant operators to estimate the wind-induced pointing deviations and their
variation with peak gust wind speed. Recommendations for future work include higher-
resolution and longer-duration datasets for different elevation angles and wind directions to
reduce uncertainties and variance of collected beam spot data and their correlations with binned
average wind speed.

1 Introduction
The inclusion of dynamic wind effects is needed in optical modeling of heliostats and heliostat
fields to quantify the influence of wind on heliostat facet surface deviations, heliostat tracking
deviations, and beam deviations (K. B. J. Blume et al. 2022; Zhu et al. 2023; Emes et al. 2024).
Although sparsely published field measurements detail wind effects on heliostat optical errors
with limited time series and limited resolution, there remains a lack of guidelines for heliostat
developers to account for wind in the measurement and analysis of heliostat optical errors in
field environments. A near-term priority in the 2025 update to the Heliostat Consortium
(HelioCon) roadmap report is the dissemination of wind load field measurement data analysis
into actionable metrics and procedures for industry to quantify wind-induced heliostat surface
and tracking deviations (Schell et al. 2025).

International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) Project Team 62862-4-3 “Technical
requirements and design qualification of heliostats for solar power tower plants” specifies total
heliostat slope deviations of less than 2.5 milliradians (mrad), and total tracking deviations of
less than 2.5 mrad, 4.0 mrad, and 6.0 mrad at average wind speeds of 4 meters per second (m/s),
6 m/s, and 8 m/s, respectively. Section 8 of the IEC 62862-4-3 heliostat design and test standard
prescribes a laser pointing repeatability test at wind speeds below 4 m/s, static load testing and
measurements of facet deflections, bending moments, and drive torsional stiffness. However, it
has been acknowledged by stakeholders in the Project Team 62862-4-3 that dynamic wind load
testing and its effect on heliostat tracking deviations are not well understood (Meyen et al. 2025;
Schell et al. 2025).

This report includes a literature review of wind-induced heliostat surface, tracking, and pointing
deviations in Section 1.1. A framework for a reduced order model correlating heliostat surface,
tracking, and pointing deviations to wind is developed in Section 1.2. A methodology to collect
and analyze field data as inputs to the reduced order model is outlined in Section 2. Results of
the correlation between heliostat deviations and wind fluctuations are discussed in Section 3, and
conclusions and next steps are provided in Section 4.

1.1 Literature Review of Heliostat Wind-Induced Deviations
Heliostat deviations are typically categorized as surface deviations intrinsic to the heliostat, and
beam deviations corresponding to the reflected beam upon the target (IEC 2024). Surface (slope)
deviations represent measurements of the pointing vectors of finite element areas of the heliostat
concentrator surface. Tracking deviation is an angular error that represents the deviation of the
actual tracking position from the commanded positions. It is measured as the deviation of the
actual surface normal vector from the desired surface normal vector. Pointing deviation is an
angular error of the beam and represents the deviation of the actual beam pointing direction from
the desired beam pointing direction. It can be measured by observing the beam position on a
white target, which can be calculated geometrically from the tracking deviation (IEC 2024).

 1

1.1.1 Surface or Slope Deviations
Typical surface or slope deviations for heliostats are stated as a root-mean-square (RMS) value
of 2 mrad (Röger et al. 2017). A single-axis slope error equivalent of 2 mrad has been assumed
in a techno-economic analysis study (Zolan et al. 2024) from industry feedback as a measure of
conservatism beyond the System Advisor Model™ 2021 baseline of 1.53 mrad.

The slope error of a 40-m2 heliostat with a measured focal length of 104.1 m in the CESA–I field
at the Plataforma Solar de Almeria was measured to be 1.35 mrad in the x-direction and
1.44 mrad in the y-direction. This corresponds to a total RMS value of 1.98 mrad. Wind
conditions during measurements were not specified. The resolution of 1,000 × 1,000
measurement points per heliostat have a local measurement error of below 0.2 mrad (Ulmer et al.
2011).

The 48.5-m2 Stellio heliostat developed by Schlaich Bergermann Partner has a two-dimensional
slope error of 1.5 mrad (Keck et al. 2019; Balz et al. 2016). Deflectometry and dynamic
photogrammetry field measurements were conducted by the German Aerospace Center on an
isolated Stellio heliostat concentrator to characterize wind-induced and total slope deviations of a
single Stellio facet and wind-induced RMS tracking deviations of a Stellio concentrator (K.
Blume et al. 2020). Total slope deviation of a single facet, not including the influence of wind
load, was determined by deflectometry at a 45° elevation angle and a 160° azimuth angle as
1.31 mrad RMS by an average of RMS slope deviations of all facets. During the time step of
largest deformations at a mean wind speed of 4.8 m/s and turbulence intensity of 26%, the spatial
average of the wind-induced slope deviations was measured using dynamic photogrammetry as
0.75 mrad RMS on a single Stellio facet. The wind-induced slope deviation was therefore
estimated to represent approximately one-third of the total slope deviation of a Stellio heliostat
(K. Blume et al. 2020).

The RMS slope error of a single sandwich facet of the 2.23-m2 Helio100 heliostat with 40-m
focal length ranged from 0.5 mrad to 0.85 mrad. Slope errors varied as 0.24 μrad/(Nm) with
applied moment on the facet because of local deformations at the mounting points normal to the
mirror plane and inconsistencies in the facet fabrication process (Landman 2017).

1.1.2 Tracking Deviations
Typical tracking deviations for heliostats are stated as the RMS value of the slope deviation as
2 mrad and the RMS tracking accuracy as between 0.8 and 1.6 mrad (Röger et al. 2017).

The 48.5-m2 Stellio heliostat has a one-dimensional tracking error of 0.6 mrad (Keck et al. 2019;
Balz et al. 2016). Total tracking deviation of the Stellio heliostat was assumed to be
0.8 mrad RMS without the impact of wind load (Röger et al. 2017). The total wind-induced RMS
tracking deviation was measured using dynamic photogrammetry as 0.44 mrad RMS, composed
of 0.16 mrad RMS about the horizontal x-axis and 0.41 mrad about the vertical y-axis of the
concentrator plane. The wind-induced tracking deviation was therefore estimated to represent
approximately one-third of the total tracking deviation of a Stellio heliostat (K. Blume et al.
2020).

 2

1.1.3 Pointing Deviations
The 1.14-m2 ganged-type heliostat developed by eSolar has an RMS beam pointing error of
1.4±0.1 mrad (Schell 2011). Pointing error of the 2.23-m2 Helio100 sandwich facet heliostat
varied as 17 microradian per Newton-meter (μrad/(Nm)) with applied moment on the facet,
where the measured pointing error ranged between 1.2 mrad and 2 mrad (Landman 2017).

Field measurements (Smith and Ho 2014) by Sandia National Laboratories (Sandia) showed an
RMS average pointing error of 1.5 mrad from approximately 50,000 observations on 200
heliostats with 37.1-m2 reflective area at the National Solar Thermal Test Facility (NSTTF).
Additional field measurements (Strachan and Houser 1993) by Sandia on a 146.9-m2 ATS
heliostat at NSTTF, during wind speeds ranging from 4.9–12 m/s, had a maximum beam
deviation of 3.7 mrad at 9.3 m/s, average deviations of 0.92 mrad, and average maximum beam
centroid deviations of 1.9 mrad for all observations. In comparison, field measurements on a
200.3-m2 SPECO heliostat, during wind speeds ranging from 5.4–11.6 m/s, had a maximum
beam deviation of 4.7 mrad at 7.9 m/s, average deviations of 0.86 mrad, and average maximum
beam centroid deviations of 1.9 mrad for all observations.

Out-of-plane bending mode 2 measured by Sandia on the 37-m2 NSTTF heliostat resulted in
maximum linear beam deviations of 0.17 m and 1.58 m in the horizontal and vertical directions,
respectively, on the NSTTF tower target (Ho et al. 2012). For a distance 𝐷 = 192 m from the
NSTTF tower located 132 m to the west, 137 m to the south, and 29 m above the heliostat, the
horizontal and vertical beam deviations correspond to angular deviations of 0.88 mrad and
8.21 mrad, respectively. These compared with linear beam deviations of 0.1 m and 0.25 m on the
target, and angular deviations of 0.52 mrad and 1.30 mrad, respectively, as a result of gravity in
the absence of wind (Ho et al. 2012). Field measurements were conducted by Sandia during low-
wind periods of 2.24–4.47 m/s and high-wind periods of 8.94–13.4 m/s (Griffith et al. 2015).

1.2 Reduced Order Model Framework
Publicly available field data in the literature in Section 1.1 reveal an absence of detailed wind
measurements during optical characterization of heliostats. Surface and tracking deviations are
intrinsic to the heliostat facet support design. For a heliostat facet with sufficient stiffness during
low to moderate wind conditions, measurements of the reflected beam position on a white target
are closely correlated to the heliostat structural design. Deviations of a heliostat surface, tracking
orientation, and reflected beam pointing on a fixed target generally increase with increasing wind
speed. Average wind speeds were specified in some studies in Section 1.1, such as at one
elevation angle and wind speed. Wind loads on a heliostat vary nonlinearly with the square of
wind speed. Measurements on a 2.23-m2 facet showed an approximately linear increase in
pointing error from 0 to 1 mrad with an increase in applied bending moment on the facet from 0
to 60 Nm (Landman 2017).
Assuming a linear relationship of pointing deviation with applied moment on a facet, a nonlinear
relationship may be expected between wind speed and pointing deviation. The wind speed
magnitude 𝑈 is decomposed into an average component 𝜇𝑈 , and a fluctuating component 𝜎𝑈 ,
such as the RMS of the wind speed during a measurement period in the order of minutes. It is
also plausible the RMS of pointing deviation is related to the RMS of wind speed and is a
function of gravitational loads, which vary with the facet elevation angle (°), and temperature
(°C).

2 Methodology
Field measurements analyzed in the current study were completed at the Third-Party Metrology
Platform at the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) Flatirons Campus. A laser and target
are mounted on tripods within 5.4 m of the heliostat facet plane in Figure 1, such that the mirror
reflects the laser beam toward the target. The target is covered with mm-grid paper and mounted
at a distance that ensures a measurable pointing accuracy to less than ±0.1 mrad. The orientation
of the heliostat, in azimuth and elevation angles, is measured using a compass and a digital level,
respectively. A second laser is mounted on a third tripod pointing directly at the target, to detect
any movement of the target itself because of wind. This way, it is possible to target movement
from the movement of the first laser spot position, which then is only related to the movement of
the heliostat as a result of wind.

A camera is mounted on a fourth tripod and positioned so that it can take high-resolution images
of the laser spots on the target. With the heliostat in a fixed position, a series of videos of the
beam spot are recorded. All orientations and timestamps are recorded corresponding to the
videos. Following the day of the test, wind data were downloaded from the M2 tower database
(Jager and Andreas 1996) and saved in 1-minute intervals for analysis. For each minute during
the test period, the mean and standard deviation values of the laser spot from processing 1-
minute segments of the video recordings are correlated with the mean wind speed, peak (gust)
wind speed, turbulence intensity at 2-m height on the M2 tower, and the calculated gust factor as
the ratio of the peak wind speed to the mean wind speed.

Figure 1. Laser pointing test setup (left) and target (right) at the Third-Party Metrology Platform at
 the NLR Flatirons Campus. Photos by Matthew Emes

The laser point identification is conducted in two steps, shown in Figure 2. Video frames are
extracted with Open–CV, and ArUco fiducial markers are used to identify the target in each
image. This has the added benefit of allowing us to see if the target itself is moving relative to
the camera. In Figure 3, a second laser pointed directly at the target is used to confirm.

 Figure 2. Basic procedure used to identify laser beam centers

After the frames are extracted, the marker inner corners are used to perform a perspective
transform, normalizing the target image. A simple red channel filter and thresholding method
adequately removes the grids and crosshair from the target background. Finally, the beam is
contoured, and the intensity-weighted center is identified. Figure 3 shows the original image with
the markers identified, and the contoured beam on the perspective transformed target image. This
workflow allows us to quickly and consistently process thousands of video frames.

3 Results and Discussion
Figure 4 shows 1-minute statistics of the laser spot mean position as a function of average wind
speed, peak wind speed, standard deviation of wind speed, turbulence intensity, and gust factor at
2-m height on the M2 tower during the 10-minute period on June 18, 2026. There is considerable
scatter in the mean position of the laser beam spot in the horizontal (x) and vertical (y) directions
on the white target. Minute-average laser beam positions are clustered between ±1 mm
(±0.185 mrad) at average wind speeds below 5 m/s, and standard deviation wind speeds below
1 m/s. There are no clear relationships of the mean beam position with wind speeds, turbulence
intensity, and gust factor. This suggests the average beam position is not directly correlated to
average or unsteady components of wind.

Figure 5 shows minute statistics of the laser spot position standard deviation as a function of
average wind speed, peak wind speed, standard deviation of wind speed, turbulence intensity,
and gust factor at 2-m height on the M2 tower during the 10-minute period on June 18, 2026.
Minute-statistics of the standard deviation of laser beam position are less than ±1 mm
(±0.185 mrad) at average wind speeds below 5 m/s, peak wind speeds below 7 m/s, and standard
deviation wind speeds below 1 m/s. As the wind speeds increase above these values, there is an
observed increase in the horizontal (x) and vertical (y) standard deviation of the beam position.
This suggests the standard deviations of the laser pointing beam position on the white target are a
result of deformations of the mirror surface caused by the unsteady wind fluctuations during the
minute periods analyzed. There are no clear relationships of the standard deviation beam position
with turbulence intensity and gust factor.

Figure 6 shows histogram distributions of the standard deviation of the laser beam spot position
during the low-wind period on June 18, 2026, and during the moderate-wind period on June 17,
2026. The distributions of the horizontal (x) and vertical (y) deviations during the low-wind
period are relatively small in magnitude, with a standard deviation of 0.04 mrad and maximum
magnitudes of 0.15 mrad and 0.14 mrad, respectively. They can be approximated by a Gaussian
distribution with a near-zero mean value. The total deviation, calculated as the vector sum of the
horizontal and vertical deviations, has a standard deviation of 0.06 mrad and a maximum
magnitude of 0.15 mrad. The estimated uncertainty of the laser beam center position is
±0.1 mrad (± 0.54 mm).

The distributions of the horizontal (x) and vertical (y) deviations during the moderate-wind
period on June 17, 2026, are larger in magnitude than during the low-wind period, with standard
deviations of 0.09 mrad and 0.10 mrad, respectively. They deviate from a Gaussian distribution
with positive skewness and a long tail because of the influence of unsteady wind turbulence at
increasing peak wind speeds. The RMS of the total deviation is 0.14 mrad, and the maximum
magnitude horizontal, vertical, and total deviations are 0.61 mrad, 0.74 mrad, and 0.75 mrad,
respectively. This indicates the horizontal deviation and particularly the vertical deviation of the
laser beam spot are sensitive to unsteady wind fluctuations at increasing wind speeds.

Figure 7 shows the laser spot standard deviation as a function of binned averages of peak wind

𝐷 = 5.4 m from the heliostat to the target in the laser pointing test, a deviation 𝛿 = 2 mm on the

approximation for the target grid oriented orthogonal to the incoming laser spot. The standard

induced tracking deviations of 0.16 mrad about the horizontal x-axis and 0.41 mrad about the
vertical y-axis of the Stellio heliostat concentrator at a mean wind speed of 4.8 m/s and
turbulence intensity of 0.26 (K. Blume et al. 2020). The differences can be explained as
variations in the heliostat facet structural support and the resulting deformation in response to
wind. There are also differences in the approaching wind, such as a smaller average turbulence
intensity of 0.22 during the 10-minute period in the current study.

The logarithmic best fit of horizontal beam deviations suggests the rate of increasing deviation
reduces with increasing wind speed. In contrast, the exponential best fit of vertical beam
deviations indicates deviations increase at a faster rate with increasing peak wind speeds. It is
expected that elastic deformations vary linearly with applied loads, such as hinge moments about
the central elevation axis. Loads are proportional to the square of wind speed. Therefore, a
second order polynomial relationship of deformations and resulting pointing deviations is shown
with wind speed. Given the size, and absence in some cases, of the error bars and narrow range
of velocities observed in Figure 7, a second order polynomial or linear fit may be sufficient.
Additional data would be required to verify the fit equations and quantify an extrapolated
increase in horizontal and vertical pointing deviations at wind speeds above 8.5 m/s.

4 Conclusions and Next Steps
This study developed a reduced order model framework for best-fit correlations between field
measurements of peak wind speed and laser pointing deviation on a white target after reflection
from a heliostat facet. Nonlinear variations of horizontal and vertical pointing deviations were
observed with wind speed over the tested range. Horizontal pointing deviations follow a
logarithmic relationship with peak wind speed, whereas vertical pointing deviations follow an
exponential relationship with peak wind speed. Both horizontal and vertical pointing deviations
also follow a second order polynomial relationship, as indicated by the proportionality of loads
and deformations with the square of wind speed.

Recommended next steps include additional field measurements to confirm a linear relationship
of pointing deviation with applied moment on a heliostat facet. It is also important to consider
the variation of the RMS pointing deviation for different wind directions and at different
elevation angles of the facet to account for and distinguish between the gravity-induced and
wind-induced pointing deviations. It would also be worth testing different heliostat structural
designs to assess their sensitivity of pointing deviations to wind with the laser method in this
study. Measurements of sub-minute and sub-second wind fluctuations of longer total duration
may lead to refined correlations on pointing deviations with gust wind speed and atmospheric
turbulence parameters.
