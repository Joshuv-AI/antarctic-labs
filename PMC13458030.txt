Dual Targeted Therapy as a Treatment Strategy for Refractory Psoriatic Arthritis: Real‐World Effectiveness and Safety From a Prospective Cohort
Authors: André Lucas Ribeiro, Zahra Al‐Zahir, Virginia Carrizo Abarza, Khalad Maliyar, Siddhartha Sood, Ahmed Bagit, Muskaan Sachdeva, Sahil Koppikar, Dafna D Gladman, Jensen Yeung, Vinod Chandran, Lihi Eder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe patient profiles, treatment patterns, clinical outcomes, and safety of intentional dual biologic or targeted synthetic disease‐modifying antirheumatic drug (b/tsDMARD) therapy in psoriatic arthritis (PsA). A subset of patients with PsA continue to experience persistent inflammation despite b/tsDMARD monotherapy. Dual b/tsDMARD therapy has emerged as a potential strategy in refractory cases, but evidence in PsA remains limited.MethodsWe conducted a nested study within the International Psoriasis and Arthritis Research Team prospective cohort. Adults satisfying Classification Criteria for Psoriatic Arthritis who received two concurrent b/tsDMARDs (TNFi, IL‐17i, IL‐23i, JAKi, TYK2i, with or without apremilast) for ≥60 days were included. Baseline features, previous therapies, indications for dual therapy, and disease activity measures (tender joint count in 68 joints, swollen joint count in 66 joints, Disease Activity Index for Psoriatic Arthritis [DAPSA], Psoriasis Area and Severity Index [PASI], body surface area, patient visual analog scale) were collected. Clinical outcomes were reassessed at three‐ to six‐month intervals, with effectiveness assessed only for dual bDMARD + JAK/TYK2i combinations. Safety was evaluated through all‐cause adverse events.ResultsThirty‐nine patients initiated dual therapy: 24 patients received bDMARD + JAK/TYK2i and 15 patients with bDMARD + apremilast. Patients were highly treatment‐experienced (median two conventional synthetic DMARDs and five b/tsDMARDs). Indications included refractory peripheral arthritis, concurrent joint and skin activity, palmoplantar or nail psoriasis, and persistent enthesitis. Among bDMARD + JAK/TYK2i combinations, improvements were observed in joint counts, DAPSA, PASI, and patient‐reported outcomes. Adverse events were infrequent and mild.ConclusionsIn this real‐world cohort, dual b/tsDMARD therapy was feasible, well‐tolerated, and associated with clinical improvement in patients with refractory PsA. These findings support its individualized use and highlight the need for controlled studies to define long‐term safety and optimal combination strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Achieving sustained remission in psoriatic arthritis (PsA) remains challenging, even with the availability of biologic or targeted synthetic disease‐modifying antirheumatic drugs (b/tsDMARDs). Across trials and observational data sets, only 30% to 40% of patients achieve remission or low disease activity with current guideline‐based therapies. 1 This therapeutic gap has accelerated efforts to better characterize patient who fail sequential advanced therapies. 2 , 3 , 4 Recently, both the Group for Research and Assessment of Psoriasis and Psoriatic Arthritis (GRAPPA) and the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) have proposed frameworks to define this heterogeneous difficult‐to‐treat (D2T) population. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Although terminology varies, both initiatives distinguish true treatment refractoriness where objective persistent inflammation exists from treatment difficulty driven by noninflammatory factors.

Despite the expanding portfolio of b/tsDMARD mechanisms, 10 , 11 refractoriness persists, underscoring that treatment outcomes may depend as much on treatment strategy as on drug class. 12 Treat‐to‐target and early intensive approaches, including biologic‐first strategies and optimized sequencing, have demonstrated modestly superior outcomes compared with conventional step‐up care, yet a relevant proportion of patients continues to exhibit active disease. 13 , 14 , 15 Trials evaluating early biologic monotherapy, with or without conventional DMARDs, further suggest that timely suppression of inflammation is necessary but not sufficient for all patients. 16 , 17 In routine practice, this often manifests as partial responses across domains and limited benefit from repeated cycling of monotherapy.

Dual targeted therapy, defined as the concomitant use of two advanced agents with distinct mechanisms of action (MOAs), represents a biologically plausible but understudied escalation strategy for such patients. Historical experiences combining biologics in rheumatoid arthritis raised safety concerns, tempering enthusiasm for this approach. 18 However, more recent reports in PsA and spondyloarthritis, though limited to small series, suggest that combinations targeting complementary pathways may offer clinical benefit without unexpected safety issues. 19 , 20 , 21 In gastroenterology, the VEGA trial in ulcerative colitis provided controlled proof‐of‐concept that dual biologic therapy may enhance efficacy without compromising safety, 22 further supporting this approach for trials in PsA. 23 Nevertheless, systematic prospective data on the effectiveness and safety of intentional dual b/tsDMARD therapy in PsA are lacking.

To address this gap, we examined real‐world dual b/tsDMARD therapy within a longitudinal prospective PsA cohort. Our objectives were to characterize patients selected for combination therapy, assessing short‐term effectiveness and safety. By focusing on patients with clinically confirmed inflammatory activity, supported by imaging when attribution was uncertain, this study provides pragmatic evidence to inform clinical decision‐making and to support the design of future strategy‐based trials in treatment‐refractory PsA.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study design and setting

This study was conducted as a nested analysis within the two Toronto sites of the International Psoriasis and Arthritis Research Team (IPART) prospective cohort. IPART enrolls adult patients with PsA fulfilling the Classification Criteria for Psoriatic Arthritis (CASPAR) and follows them with structured clinical assessments. 24 , 25 Standardized data capture includes demographics, comorbidities, disease manifestations, medication start and stop dates, disease activity measures, and adverse events (AEs), allowing accurate reconstruction of therapeutic exposures and outcomes.

All patients treated with dual b/tsDMARD therapy were identified from January 2016 through June 2025 using a two‐step process. First, the entire IPART cohort database was searched for patients with overlapping use of two b/tsDMARDs based on the start and stop dates. Second, all identified cases underwent manual review of their electronic medical record (EMR) to confirm dual b/tsDMARD therapy, clinical indication, and accuracy of timing.

Study population

Eligible patients were adults (≥18 years) who received intentional dual b/tsDMARDs therapy for at least 60 consecutive days. Dual therapy was defined as the concomitant use of two agents from the following classes: TNF inhibitors (TNFi), interleukin‐17 (IL‐17) inhibitors (IL‐17i), IL‐12/23i, IL‐23i, JAK inhibitors (JAKi), TYK2 inhibitors (TYK2i), or PDE4 inhibitors. The ≥60 days threshold was selected to distinguish true therapeutic combinations from short transition periods, brief washouts, or administrative overlap during medication switching, as registry‐based medication dates may transiently overlap during treatment changes. Episodes below this threshold were manually reviewed, and none reflected intentional dual therapy stopped within 60 days because of toxicity.

Escalation to dual b/tsDMARD therapy occurred as part of routine clinical care and was not governed by prespecified study inclusion criteria. All potential overlap episodes identified in the database underwent manual EMR review to confirm that the overlap represented an intentional combination rather than a switch, and to verify the clinical indication for adding the second agent. In practice, treatment escalation was reserved for patients with persistent clinically relevant inflammatory activity despite a partial good response to the first advanced therapy, including residual activity in one or more domains (eg, adequate musculoskeletal control with residual skin activity). At the time of escalation, treating rheumatologists documented objective evidence of inflammation based on clinically apparent musculoskeletal disease, active psoriasis, or both. When attribution of symptoms to inflammatory disease activity was uncertain, such as in cases of isolated enthesitis or axial complaints, imaging was used at the discretion of the treating physician to support inflammatory activity. Imaging was therefore used selectively rather than according to a uniform protocol.

Analyzed variables

For baseline characteristics, baseline was defined as the recorded assessment closest to initiation of dual b/tsDMARD therapy, allowing a window of up to six months before treatment start. Extracted variables included age, sex, race, body mass index (BMI), family history, PsA manifestations, previous treatments, and comorbidities of interest.

Clinical assessments were performed at baseline and during routine follow‐up visits every three to six months. Disease activity measures included tender joint counts (TJC) and swollen joint counts (SJC) in 68 and 66 joints, respectively, Disease Activity Index for Psoriatic Arthritis (DAPSA), axial involvement (defined by clinical symptoms and imaging confirmation), and assessed by the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Activity Index (BASDAI), enthesitis count by the Spondyloarthritis Research Consortium of Canada index (SPARCC), dactylitis count, modified Nail Psoriasis Severity Index (mNAPSI), and psoriasis severity evaluated by Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) and body surface area (BSA). Patient‐reported outcomes (PROs) included global assessment of PsA activity and pain (numeric rating scale 0–10), dermatology life quality index (DLQI), and fatigue by Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy‐Fatigue (FACIT) questionnaires.

Treatment history, including previous conventional synthetic DMARDs and b/tsDMARDs, as well as the clinical indications for dual b/tsDMARD therapy, were systematically documented. Treatment failure, defined as discontinuation due to lack of efficacy or AEs, was determined by the treating rheumatologist. Safety monitoring captured all AEs, including infections, cardiovascular events, malignancies, or laboratory abnormalities as reported in the IPART database and medical records.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (IQR), whereas categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Given the small sample size, heterogeneous treatment regimens, and exploratory design, effectiveness outcomes were summarized descriptively without formal inferential pre–post testing.

To account for variable treatment durations across regimens, safety events were summarized using incidence rates (IRs). IRs were calculated as the number of AEs divided by the total patient‐years (PYs) of exposure, multiplied by 100. Exposure time (“time at risk”) was defined as the number of days during which the patient was receiving both agents concurrently, converted into PYs.

Because apremilast has a distinct pharmacologic profile and limited immunosuppressive effects, effectiveness analyses were restricted to combinations of bDMARDs with JAK or TYK2 inhibitors. Apremilast‐containing combinations were included in safety analyses only. This study was designed to describe observed changes over time and was not intended to formally test comparative effectiveness. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.

Ethics

The IPART cohort is conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Research Ethics Board of Women's College Hospital (protocol 2013‐0052‐E). All participants provided written informed consent. This subanalysis was approved with a waiver for additional consent (protocol 2024‐0024‐E). Patient‐level data included in the supplementary material were reviewed and presented in de‐identified form using study case IDs only, without direct patient identifiers or dates.

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

A total of 39 of 2,053 (1.9%) patients initiated dual b/tsDMARD therapy at the Toronto sites of IPART. Of these, 24 of 39 (61.5%) patients received a combination of a bDMARD with a JAK or TYK2 inhibitor, 15 of 39 (38.5%) patients received a bDMARD combined with apremilast, and 1 of 39 (2.5%) patients received dual bDMARD therapy. One patient received triple therapy (bimekizumab + upadacitinib + apremilast) and is described in both combination subgroups for descriptive purposes. Baseline characteristics of patients combining bDMARD + JAK/TYK2i are shown in Table 1. The baseline characteristics of apremilast‐based combinations are shown in Supplementary Table S1. Briefly, 11 of 24 patients were women (45.8%) and 10 of 24 patients were White (41.6%); median age at initiation was 51 years (IQR 45.7–63.2). Median BMI was 31.2 (IQR 30.0–39.7). Comorbidities of interest included fibromyalgia in 3 of 24 (12.5%) patients, anxiety or depression in 7 of 24 (29.2%) patients, uveitis in 1 of 24 (4.1%) patients, and inflammatory bowel disease in 1 of 24 (4.1%) patients.

Treatment patterns and duration

Across the 24 patients using bDMARD + JAK/TYK2i, 30 dual targeted regimens were initiated, as some patients transitioned through sequential combinations during follow‐up. The complete list of all combinations is shown in Supplementary Tables S2A to S2D, with the most common being bimekizumab + deucravacitinib (7 of 30 patients, 23.3%), followed by risankizumab + deucravacitinib (n = 5, 16.6%). The median duration of dual b/tsDMARD therapy was 418 days (IQR 312–519; range 75–2,231 days). At last follow‐up, 23 of 30 regimens were ongoing (77%), and 7 of 30 regimens (23%) had been discontinued (4 for lack of efficacy and 3 due to AEs).

Exploratory observations with apremilast combinations

All apremilast‐containing regimens are shown in Supplementary Tables S2A to S2D. These regimens were primarily used for persistent cutaneous disease, including nail and palmoplantar psoriasis. Cumulative exposure amounted to 23.7 PYs for IL‐12/23i or IL‐23i combined with apremilast, 26.6 PYs for IL‐17i plus apremilast, and 12.6 PYs for TNFi plus apremilast, with one patient briefly exposed to JAKi plus apremilast (1.6 PYs).

AEs were infrequent and mild, consisting of diarrhea and URIs (Table 4). One patient experienced fatal decompensated heart failure while receiving ustekinumab and apremilast; causality could not be established in the context of pre‐existing tachycardia‐induced cardiomyopathy and concurrent nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drug use. As prespecified, apremilast combinations were not included in primary effectiveness analyses.

DISCUSSION

In this real‐world study from a structured PsA cohort, we describe one of the largest experiences to date with intentional dual b/tsDMARD therapy. Most patients had long‐standing, multidomain disease, and treatment escalation was often driven by residual activity in a specific domain rather than by globally uncontrolled disease. Overall, dual targeted therapy was associated with numerical improvement in musculoskeletal and skin, in addition to PROs, whereas AEs were infrequent and predominantly mild. Collectively, these findings support dual b/tsDMARD therapy as a pragmatic, individualized option in selected patients with refractory PsA.

These observations fit within the broader shift toward strategy‐based care in PsA. Recent trials have underscored that outcomes depend not only on drug mechanism but also on treatment sequencing, timing, and escalation principles. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Within this paradigm, dual b/tsDMARD therapy can be viewed as a strategy‐level intervention for a subset of refractory patients, complementing rather than competing with existing step‐up and treat‐to‐target approaches.

In clinical practice, unlike existing trials, the introduction of dual targeted therapy often followed a pragmatic, stepwise pattern rather than simultaneous initiation of two agents. Most patients had already achieved a partial response to the first b/tsDMARD, and a second agent was added to address residual inflammatory activity in another domain. This stepwise escalation reflects real‐world therapeutic reasoning and may partly explain the observed efficacy and safety profiles, as patients were typically stabilized on one agent before addition of a second one with a distinct MOA.

Evidence for dual b/tsDMARD therapy in PsA remains sparse, consisting largely of case reports and small case series, 19 , 20 , 21 with broader support coming from the systematic review by Lignou et al, 26 together with the VEGA trial. 22 Our findings extend this literature by providing prospective real‐world data from a structured PsA cohort that focused on distinguishing refractoriness due to inflammation from noninflammatory etiologies. 27 , 28 , 29 Notably, the effectiveness and safety outcomes observed in this study were driven predominantly by combinations of IL‐17 or IL‐23 inhibitors with JAK or TYK2 inhibitors, which accounted for most exposure time and all primary effectiveness analyses.

In this cohort, dual b/tsDMARD therapy was generally well‐tolerated. AEs were infrequent and predominantly mild, consisting mainly of self‐limited respiratory or mucosal infections; no severe or opportunistic infections, hospitalizations, malignancies, or cardiovascular events were observed. A few patients elected to discontinue therapy because of recurrent but mild URIs, but no regimen was stopped for serious safety concerns. These findings suggest that, in selected patients, dual therapy may provide clinical benefit without a clear signal of short‐term toxicity.

Patients with features of D2T‐PsA may represent one group most likely to benefit from such escalation, as this phenotype increasingly requires strategies beyond sequential monotherapy. 30 , 31 , 32 In our cohort, many patients exhibited features typical of D2T‐PsA, 8 , 9 including persistent multidomain activity and repeated biologic failures. Notably, escalation was often driven by inflammatory activity in nonperipheral domains, particularly skin disease, even when swollen joint burden was low or absent, highlighting the importance of a thorough clinical assessment.

Ensuring such careful selection requires a rigorous distinction between inflammatory and noninflammatory contributors to symptoms. Dual b/tsDMARD therapy should therefore be reserved for patients in whom symptoms are reliably attributed to active inflammation, as these regimens are potent, costly, and not risk‐free. 33 , 34 In this cohort, inflammatory activity was confirmed clinically in all patients, with targeted ultrasonography or magnetic resonance imaging used when the clinical picture was uncertain. Although imaging was not performed uniformly, it helped distinguish true inflammatory refractoriness from alternative explanations in more complex cases. 35 , 36 , 37 This distinction is important because apparent treatment failure in PsA may reflect mechanisms other than drug‐resistant inflammation, including suboptimal adherence, chronic pain, and comorbidities. 38 , 39 , 40 , 41

Our results should be interpreted with caution considering the study's design. The sample size was modest, and combination regimens were heterogeneous, reflecting the selective use of dual targeted therapy in routine clinical practice. Inclusion was restricted to patients with clinically evident inflammatory disease activity, supported by imaging when required, which limited the cohort size but ensured appropriate attribution of treatment escalation. The requirement for at least 60 consecutive days of overlap increased confidence that recorded exposures represented intentional combination therapy rather than transition periods. Although manual review did not identify any intentional dual‐therapy episode discontinued within the first 60 days because of toxicity, very early events not leading to discontinuation, or incompletely captured in routine documentation, may still have been underdetected. In addition, because a second targeted agent was generally added only after the first had shown acceptable tolerability, the cohort was likely enriched for patients already able to tolerate advanced therapy, introducing clinician‐selection and survivor bias. Follow‐up duration varied across patients, precluding definitive conclusions regarding long‐term outcomes or rare AEs. As with other registry‐based analyses, some clinical variables and PROs were variably documented.

Nevertheless, our study also has notable strengths. It was conducted within a long‐standing, well‐structured prospective cohort with standardized data capture, including systematic documentation of comorbidities, such as fibromyalgia and osteoarthritis. Importantly, escalation to dual b/tsDMARD therapy occurred only in patients with clinically supported ongoing inflammation, with targeted imaging used when needed. This pragmatic attribution process minimized the risk of misclassifying noninflammatory causes of persistent symptoms as biologic‐refractory PsA, thereby enhancing the interpretability and clinical relevance of our findings.

Overall, the findings from this study have direct implications for the increasing number of patients with refractory PsA. Dual b/tsDMARD therapy demonstrated clinically meaningful improvement with no new safety concerns, supporting its cautious, individualized use when sequential monotherapy fails. This study also highlights the value of structured registries for monitoring dual b/tsDMARD therapy and lays the groundwork for prospective multicenter studies aimed at defining optimal combinations, treatment duration, and selection criteria for dual therapy in PsA.
