PDE-constrained optimization for virtual sensing in structural dynamics: Full-field displacement and force recovery from sparse sensors
Minjae Kim, Jaehwan Jeong, Jaemin Kim

Abstract
Virtual sensing — recovering full-field structural response from sparse sensor measurements — is a fundamental challenge in structural health monitoring. This study formulates virtual sensing as a PDE-constrained optimization (PDE-CO) problem, where the governing elastodynamic equation serves as an equality constraint, the applied force distribution is the optimization variable, and the full-field displacement and force are jointly recovered. Gradients of the Tikhonov-regularized objective are computed via reverse-mode automatic differentiation through the forward PDE solver, and L-BFGS iteratively finds the optimal state. The framework couples an offline FEniCSx stage for finite element assembly with an online GPU-accelerated JAX stage, and is verified on three examples of increasing complexity: a cantilever plate, a 90 ∘ 90^{\circ} elbow pipe, and a reactor pressure vessel (RPV) representative of a 300 MW pressurized nuclear reactor. PDE-CO consistently outperforms modal expansion across all three cases, reducing displacement errors by factors of 2 2 to 17 × 17\times with sub-percent accuracy on every example. Unlike modal expansion, where force is derived by back-calculation from truncated modal coordinates and is not jointly optimized, PDE-CO recovers displacement and force simultaneously through the PDE constraint; the increased computational cost is offset by GPU acceleration delivering up to 64.8 × 64.8\times speedup over CPU.

Introduction

Virtual sensing — the recovery of full-field displacement, strain, and acceleration fields across an entire structure from sparse sensor measurements — is a key capability for structural health monitoring (SHM)
(
23
;
17
)
, fatigue assessment, and damage detection
(
8
)
(see Figure
1
).
The structural dynamics community has developed several approaches for virtual sensing, including modal least-squares regression
(
16
)
, Kalman filter methods
(
20
;
18
)
, reduced-order modeling approaches
(
22
;
13
;
12
)
, and physics-guided machine learning approaches
(
10
)
.
Among these, modal expansion is the most widely adopted: the displacement is represented as a linear combination of the
N_{d}
lowest-frequency (dominant) eigenmodes, and the modal coordinates are fitted to sensor data via least-squares.
However, modal expansion suffers from fundamental limitations.
First, retaining only
N_{d}
modes introduces modal truncation error, and
N_{d}
is bounded by the sensor count (
N_{d}\leq N_{s}-1
for an overdetermined system), so the sparse sensor data cannot constrain a higher-order modal basis.
Second, the governing PDE is never enforced: the sensor data determines only
N_{s}
degrees of freedom, and no physical law propagates this information to the remaining
N_{f}-N_{s}
DOFs.
When
N_{s}\ll N_{f}
, this purely kinematic approach lacks the information to reconstruct the response accurately away from sensor locations.

The scientific computing community, in parallel, has developed differentiable simulation frameworks
(
26
;
3
)
that formulate inverse problems as PDE-constrained optimization (PDE-CO), with automatic differentiation handling all sensitivity computations.
PDE-CO treats the governing equation as an equality constraint, ensuring the recovered solution is physically consistent at every optimization iteration.
By enforcing the PDE, sparse sensor information is propagated to the entire domain through the physics, filling the information gap that modal expansion cannot bridge.
The PDE-CO framework offers several key advantages over modal expansion for virtual sensing.
First, it eliminates modal truncation error. PDE-CO uses the full dynamic stiffness matrix
\mathbf{D}_{ff}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{f}\times N_{f}}
rather than a truncated modal basis
\bm{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{f}\times N_{d}}
, so the solution space is the entire
N_{f}
-dimensional FE space.
Second, the PDE constraint provides physics-based regularization. Every trial solution is consistent with the governing equation, and the inverse operator
\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}
acts as a smoothing operator that attenuates high-frequency spatial components.
Third, gradients are computed via reverse-mode AD and supplied to L-BFGS, a unified paradigm that extends identically to nonlinear forward models, material identification, and topology optimization.
Fourth, the full-field displacement emerges directly from the primal PDE solve at the optimal force, so both the displacement field and the force distribution are recovered simultaneously within a single optimization framework without auxiliary post-processing steps.

Despite their conceptual similarity, the two threads — modal-based virtual sensing and PDE-constrained optimization — have not converged for full-field inference in structural dynamics.
The adjoint-based PDE-CO framework has been applied to static inverse problems such as elasticity imaging

, structural weakness identification
(

, and constitutive inverse design
(

)
.

26

In structural dynamics,
7
employed the adjoint method for sizing optimization under transient dynamic loads, but this is a design optimization problem rather than an inverse sensing problem.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no prior work has applied PDE-CO to recover full-field displacement from sparse vibration sensors in structural dynamics.
Existing virtual sensing methods rely on modal expansion
(
11
)
, Kalman filters
(
18
)
, physics-guided machine learning
(
10
)
, or transfer function inversion, but do not exploit the full PDE constraint in a differentiable optimization framework.

This study addresses this gap through the following contributions.
Virtual sensing under harmonic excitation is formulated as a PDE-CO problem, where the dynamic stiffness equation
\mathbf{D}(\omega)\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}=\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}
serves as the equality constraint and the force parameter
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\theta}}
is the optimization variable.
The force on the candidate loaded boundary
\Gamma_{N}
is parameterized via a Boolean selection matrix
\mathbf{P}
, and the gradient is computed via JAX reverse-mode AD; L-BFGS then iteratively recovers the optimal force distribution and the corresponding full-field displacement.
It is demonstrated analytically and numerically that PDE-CO achieves superior displacement recovery compared to modal expansion, because the PDE constraint eliminates modal truncation error and propagates sensor information through the exact governing equation.
The framework is verified on three examples of increasing complexity — a cantilever plate (

64.8\times

2
Modal expansion-based virtual sensing and its limitations

2.1
Modal expansion procedure

The standard approach to full-field recovery from sparse sensor measurements is modal expansion
(
15
)
.
The method begins by solving the generalized eigenvalue problem associated with the free-DOF system:

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{ff}\,\bm{\phi}_{i}=\omega_{i}^{2}\,\mathbf{M}_{ff}\,\bm{\phi}_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,N_{f},

(1)

where
\omega_{i}
is the
i
-th natural frequency and
\bm{\phi}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{f}}
is the corresponding eigenmode.
The eigenmodes are normalized to be mass-orthonormal, so the modal matrix
\bm{\Phi}=[\bm{\phi}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\phi}_{N_{d}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{f}\times N_{d}}
composed of the first
N_{d}
dominant modes satisfies the orthogonality conditions:

\displaystyle\bm{\Phi}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{ff}\,\bm{\Phi}=\mathbf{I}_{N_{d}},\quad\bm{\Phi}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{ff}\,\bm{\Phi}=\bm{\Lambda},

(2)

where
\bm{\Lambda}=\mathrm{diag}(\omega_{1}^{2},\ldots,\omega_{N_{d}}^{2})
is the spectral matrix.

The displacement field of the free DOFs is then approximated by a truncated modal superposition:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}\approx\bm{\Phi}\,\mathbf{q},

(3)

where
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{d}}
is the modal coordinate vector to be determined from sensor data.

Under harmonic excitation at frequency
\omega
, the sensor acceleration data
\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}}
is first converted to sensor displacements via the kinematic relationship
\hat{\mathbf{a}}=-\omega^{2}\hat{\mathbf{u}}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{obs}}=-\frac{\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}}{\omega^{2}}.

(4)

The sensor displacements are related to the full free-DOF displacement through the sensor selection matrix
\mathbf{S}_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times N_{f}}
(a Boolean matrix that extracts sensor DOFs):

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{obs}}=\mathbf{S}_{f}\,\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}\approx\mathbf{S}_{f}\,\bm{\Phi}\,\mathbf{q}=\bm{\Phi}_{m}\,\mathbf{q},

(5)

where
\bm{\Phi}_{m}\coloneqq\mathbf{S}_{f}\,\bm{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times N_{d}}
is the modal matrix restricted to sensor DOFs.

2.2
Full-field reconstruction

The modal coordinates
\mathbf{q}
are recovered by minimizing the Tikhonov-regularized least-squares cost function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{modal}}(\mathbf{q})=\|\bm{\Phi}_{m}\,\mathbf{q}-\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{obs}}\|^{2}+\alpha_{m}\|\mathbf{q}\|^{2},

(6)

where
\alpha_{m}>0
is a regularization parameter that penalizes large modal coordinates.
Setting
\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{modal}}=\mathbf{0}
yields the closed-form solution:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{q}}=(\bm{\Phi}_{m}^{\top}\bm{\Phi}_{m}+\alpha_{m}\,\mathbf{I}_{N_{d}})^{-1}\bm{\Phi}_{m}^{\top}\,\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{obs}}.

(7)

When
N_{s}>N_{d}
and the system is well-conditioned, the unregularized pseudoinverse (
\alpha_{m}=0
) can be used.
In practice, however, the number of available sensors is often limited (
N_{s}\leq N_{d}
) or the system is ill-conditioned, making regularization essential for a stable solution.

The full-field displacement at all free DOFs is then reconstructed as:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}^{\mathrm{modal}}=\bm{\Phi}\,\hat{\mathbf{q}}.

(8)

The inversion involves only an
N_{d}\times N_{d}
system, making modal expansion computationally efficient and widely used for real-time virtual sensing.
However, the entire procedure is purely kinematic: it fits mode shapes to sensor data without ever enforcing the governing PDE or using knowledge of the applied forces.
Modal expansion recovers an approximate displacement field but with accuracy limited by modal truncation error.

2.3
Limitations: force recovery from modal displacement

A natural question is whether the applied force can be recovered by inverting the governing equation once the displacement field has been obtained.
Given the free-DOF dynamic stiffness matrix
\mathbf{D}_{ff}=\mathbf{K}_{ff}-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}_{ff}
(to be derived in Section
4.2
), the modal force estimate is:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}^{\mathrm{modal}}=\mathbf{D}_{ff}\,\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}^{\mathrm{modal}}.

(9)

This produces a poor force estimate for two reasons.

The first is error amplification through the dynamic stiffness operator.
The true displacement can be expanded in the complete set of
N_{f}
eigenmodes as
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}^{\mathrm{true}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{f}}\bm{\phi}_{i}\,q_{i}^{\mathrm{true}}
, where
q_{i}^{\mathrm{true}}
is the true modal coordinate for mode
i
.
The modal expansion retains only the first
N_{d}
modes, so
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}^{\mathrm{modal}}=\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}^{\mathrm{true}}+\bm{\epsilon}
, where the truncation error is:

\displaystyle\bm{\epsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{d}}(\hat{q}_{i}-q_{i}^{\mathrm{true}})\,\bm{\phi}_{i}-\sum_{i=N_{d}+1}^{N_{f}}\bm{\phi}_{i}\,q_{i}^{\mathrm{true}}.

(10)

The first sum represents the least-squares fitting error (typically small), and the second sum represents the modal truncation error from omitted higher modes.
Applying the dynamic stiffness operator yields the force error
\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}^{\mathrm{modal}}=\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}^{\mathrm{true}}+\mathbf{D}_{ff}\,\bm{\epsilon}
.
Since the eigenmodes satisfy
\mathbf{K}_{ff}\,\bm{\phi}_{i}=\omega_{i}^{2}\,\mathbf{M}_{ff}\,\bm{\phi}_{i}
and
\mathbf{D}_{ff}\,\bm{\phi}_{i}=(\omega_{i}^{2}-\omega^{2})\,\mathbf{M}_{ff}\,\bm{\phi}_{i}
, the force error from the truncated modes becomes:

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{ff}\,\bm{\epsilon}\approx-\sum_{i=N_{d}+1}^{N_{f}}(\omega_{i}^{2}-\omega^{2})\,\bm{\phi}_{i}\,q_{i}^{\mathrm{true}}\;+\;\text{fitting error terms}.

(11)

The amplification factor
(\omega_{i}^{2}-\omega^{2})
for each omitted mode grows rapidly with mode number because natural frequencies increase as
\omega_{i}\propto i^{2}
for plate structures.
Consequently, while the omitted modes contribute
q_{i}^{\mathrm{true}}
to the displacement error — which decreases with mode number — they contribute
(\omega_{i}^{2}-\omega^{2})\,q_{i}^{\mathrm{true}}
to the force error, where the amplification factor increases faster than
q_{i}^{\mathrm{true}}
decreases.
Higher modes may therefore contribute negligibly to the displacement field but significantly to the force field.
This is a manifestation of the general principle that differentiation amplifies high-frequency content: the dynamic stiffness operator, being a second-order spatial operator, functions as a spectral amplifier that magnifies the truncation error.

The second reason is loss of spatial localization.
The true external force is spatially localized on the loaded boundary
\Gamma_{N}
: in the FE discretization,
\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}^{\mathrm{true}}
is a sparse vector with at most
N_{\theta}
nonzero entries out of
N_{f}
total.
However, the modal force estimate (
9
) is generically a dense vector with nonzero entries at all
N_{f}
free DOFs, because each eigenmode
\bm{\phi}_{i}
has support over the entire domain and the full-rank operator
\mathbf{D}_{ff}
maps any nonzero vector to a dense vector.
The modal force estimate therefore predicts nonzero forces at interior nodes and at the clamped boundary — locations where the true force is identically zero.
Modal expansion has no mechanism to enforce that the recovered force is localized to a specific boundary region.

3
PDE-constrained optimization as an alternative

The limitations identified in Section
2
— modal truncation error, absence of a physics constraint, and force recovery failure — motivate an alternative framework.
PDE-constrained optimization (PDE-CO) addresses all three by enforcing the governing equation as an equality constraint, operating on the full-order FE system without modal reduction, and recovering the unknown force distribution by minimizing the sensor data misfit.

3.1
Key idea and general formulation

The advantages of PDE-CO over modal expansion are summarized in the Introduction. The key idea is to treat the unknown force
\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{f}}
as the optimization variable and enforce the governing PDE as an equality constraint.
Given a trial force
\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}
, the forward solve yields the displacement:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}=\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}\,\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f},

(12)

where
\mathbf{D}_{ff}=\mathbf{K}_{ff}-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}_{ff}
is the dynamic stiffness matrix (to be derived in Section
4.2
).
The predicted sensor response is then extracted as
\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{s}=-\omega^{2}\,\mathbf{S}_{f}\,\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}
, where
\mathbf{S}_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times N_{f}}
is a Boolean selection matrix.
The optimization minimizes the misfit between
\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{s}
and the observed data
\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}
:

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}}\;\mathcal{J}=\bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{s}-\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}\bigr\|^{2}+\alpha\,\bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}\bigr\|^{2},

(13)

where
\alpha
is the Tikhonov regularization weight.
The PDE constraint (
12
) is embedded in the objective through the forward solve, so any minimizer automatically satisfies the governing equation.

More generally, the force is parameterized as
\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}=\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}
, where
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{f}\times N_{\theta}}
is a parameterization matrix that selects or maps the optimization variable
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\theta}}
to the nodal force vector.
When the loaded boundary
\Gamma_{N}
is known,
\mathbf{P}
is a Boolean selection matrix that restricts forces to the DOFs on
\Gamma_{N}
, with
N_{\theta}
equal to the number of candidate loaded DOFs.

Remark 1
(Role of
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{S}_{f}
)

The two matrices
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{S}_{f}
play complementary roles in the PDE-CO pipeline:

\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{f}\times N_{\theta}}
maps the optimization variable
\bm{\theta}
into the full DOF space (input selection), while
\mathbf{S}_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times N_{f}}
extracts sensor DOFs from the full DOF space (output selection).
Both are Boolean (0–1) matrices when used as selectors, and the information flow is:

\bm{\theta}\;\xrightarrow{\;\mathbf{P}\;}\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}\;\xrightarrow{\;\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}\;}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}\;\xrightarrow{\;\mathbf{S}_{f}\;}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{s}.

Here the hat notation
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}
denotes the harmonic displacement amplitude at free DOFs, specific to the problem of this paper.
In the general formulation below, the same role is played by the full FE solution vector
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Conceptually,
\mathbf{U}
can be partitioned as
\mathbf{U}=[\mathbf{U}_{s};\,\mathbf{U}_{\bar{s}}]
, where
\mathbf{U}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}}
collects the sensor DOFs and
\mathbf{U}_{\bar{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-N_{s}}
collects the remaining (unobserved) DOFs.
The sensor extraction
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{s}=\mathbf{S}_{f}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}
in the diagram above corresponds to
\mathbf{U}[i]
at the observed DOF indices
i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{obs}}
in the general formulation, i.e.,
\mathbf{U}_{s}
.

In abstract notation, the PDE-CO formulation for an inverse problem reads:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\theta}}\;\mathcal{J}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{U})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\bigl(\mathbf{U}[i]-U_{i,\mathrm{obs}}\bigr)^{2}+\alpha\,\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{U},\bm{\theta})=\mathbf{0},

(14)

where
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the FE solution vector,
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\theta}}
is the unknown parameter,
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{U},\bm{\theta})=\mathbf{0}
is the discrete residual form of the governing PDE,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{obs}}
is the set of observed DOF indices, and
\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta})
is a regularization term.
For the linear problem considered in this study, the residual takes the form
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{U},\bm{\theta})=\mathbf{K}\mathbf{U}-\mathbf{F}(\bm{\theta})
, where
\mathbf{K}
denotes the discrete PDE operator and
\mathbf{F}(\bm{\theta})
is the load vector parameterized by
\bm{\theta}
.
For a static problem
\mathbf{K}
reduces to the stiffness matrix
\mathbf{K}_{ff}
; for the harmonic problem of this study it corresponds to the dynamic stiffness matrix
\mathbf{D}_{ff}=\mathbf{K}_{ff}-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}_{ff}
.

A key advantage of PDE-CO is that the PDE constraint resolves the underdetermined nature of the inverse problem through physics.
In practice, the number of sensor DOFs
N_{s}
is much smaller than the total DOFs
N
(e.g.,
N_{s}=15
vs.
N=1{,}872
in this study), so the sensor data alone cannot determine the full state.
The PDE constraint
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{U},\bm{\theta})=\mathbf{0}
acts as a physics-based regularizer: for each trial
\bm{\theta}
, the entire state
\mathbf{U}(\bm{\theta})
is uniquely determined by the governing equation.
The optimization therefore searches only over the parameter space
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\theta}}
, and the PDE propagates the sparse sensor information to the entire domain.

3.2
Reduced-space formulation

Problem (
14
) can be approached in two ways.
The full-space (or all-at-once) approach treats
(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{U})
as simultaneous unknowns and enforces the PDE constraint via Lagrange multipliers
(
4
)
.
The reduced-space approach, adopted in this study, eliminates
\mathbf{U}
by solving the PDE for each trial
\bm{\theta}
.

In the reduced-space approach, the PDE constraint
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{U},\bm{\theta})=\mathbf{0}
implicitly defines
\mathbf{U}
as a function of
\bm{\theta}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}\bigl(\mathbf{U}(\bm{\theta}),\,\bm{\theta}\bigr)=\mathbf{0}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{U}(\bm{\theta}).

(15)

Substituting into the objective gives the reduced objective
\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\bm{\theta})\coloneqq\mathcal{J}\bigl(\bm{\theta},\,\mathbf{U}(\bm{\theta})\bigr)
, which is a function of
\bm{\theta}
alone.
The optimization problem then reduces to the unconstrained problem
\min_{\bm{\theta}}\,\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\bm{\theta})
.

In this study,
\bm{\theta}
enters only in the right-hand side
\mathbf{F}
, so the forward model
\mathbf{U}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{F}(\bm{\theta})
is linear in
\bm{\theta}
because
\mathbf{K}
is a constant operator.
The reduced objective takes the explicit form:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\Bigl(\bigl[\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{F}(\bm{\theta})\bigr]_{i}-U_{i,\mathrm{obs}}\Bigr)^{2}+\alpha\,\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta}),

(16)

which is a convex quadratic in
\bm{\theta}
.
Although a closed-form solution via normal equations exists in principle, forming the explicit sensing matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times N_{\theta}}
requires
N_{\theta}
forward solves; when
N_{\theta}\sim N_{f}
(e.g., unknown load boundary), this is computationally prohibitive.
In practice, the problem is solved iteratively using L-BFGS with gradients computed via reverse-mode AD (see Section
4.9
).
The concrete form of Eq. (
16
) for the harmonic problem is derived in Section
4.7
as Eq. (
40
).

In this study, the force on the candidate loaded boundary
\Gamma_{N}
is parameterized via a Boolean selection matrix
\mathbf{P}
, so
\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}=\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}
.
Each
\theta_{j}
represents the nodal force at a candidate loaded DOF, and the optimization determines the force amplitudes from the sensor data.

Remark 2
(Why force parameterization helps displacement recovery)

The force parameter
\bm{\theta}
is the internal optimization variable, not the output of interest.
The key insight is that the mapping
\bm{\theta}\mapsto\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}=\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}
is a smoothing operator: high-frequency spatial components in
\bm{\theta}
are strongly attenuated in the resulting displacement.
This means the optimization need not find the exact force — it suffices to find any
\bm{\theta}^{*}
whose displacement field matches the data, and the PDE ensures this field is physically consistent everywhere.

3.3
Gradient computation

Gradient-based optimization requires the total derivative
d\hat{\mathcal{J}}/d\bm{\theta}
.
By the chain rule:

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{\mathcal{J}}}{d\bm{\theta}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial\bm{\theta}}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial\mathbf{U}}\,\frac{d\mathbf{U}}{d\bm{\theta}}.

(17)

The first term
\partial\mathcal{J}/\partial\bm{\theta}
is the direct dependence of the objective on
\bm{\theta}
(from the regularization term).
The second term involves the sensitivity matrix
d\mathbf{U}/d\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N_{\theta}}
, which captures how the PDE solution changes with the parameter.

Computing
d\mathbf{U}/d\bm{\theta}
explicitly (the direct method) requires solving
N_{\theta}
linear systems — one per column of the sensitivity matrix — yielding a total cost of
N_{\theta}
PDE solves.
When
N_{\theta}
is large, this means
N_{\theta}
forward solves per gradient evaluation, which is computationally prohibitive.

The adjoint method reduces this cost to a single additional PDE solve, regardless of
N_{\theta}
.
The key idea is to introduce an adjoint variable
\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
— not a physical unknown but a computational device — that satisfies:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}}{\partial\mathbf{U}}\right)^{\top}\bm{\lambda}=-\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial\mathbf{U}}\right)^{\top}.

(18)

The right-hand side
\partial\mathcal{J}/\partial\mathbf{U}
represents how the objective is sensitive to each DOF of the displacement.
Solving Eq. (
18
) propagates this sensitivity backward through the PDE operator, yielding
\bm{\lambda}
.
The total derivative is then obtained without forming
d\mathbf{U}/d\bm{\theta}
:

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{\mathcal{J}}}{d\bm{\theta}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial\bm{\theta}}+\bm{\lambda}^{\top}\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}}{\partial\bm{\theta}}.

(19)

While the direct method requires
1+N_{\theta}
PDE solves per gradient (e.g.,
1{,}873
for
N_{\theta}=1{,}872
in this study), the adjoint method
(
4
;
21
;
28
)
requires only two (one forward, one adjoint), regardless of
N_{\theta}
.
Conceptually, the adjoint solve is analogous to backpropagation in neural networks: the forward solve maps the input
\bm{\theta}
to the output
\mathcal{J}
, and the adjoint solve propagates the output sensitivity back to the input.
In modern differentiable simulation frameworks (e.g., JAX-FEM
(
26
)
), the adjoint gradient (
19
) is computed automatically via reverse-mode AD through the forward PDE solver: the user implements only the forward model, and the framework (JAX
(
5
)
in this study) generates the adjoint solve automatically via
jax.grad
.
This eliminates the need to derive and implement the adjoint equation (
18
) manually, while retaining the same
O(1)
PDE-solve cost per gradient evaluation.

Given the efficient adjoint-based gradient, the reduced objective (
16
) is minimized iteratively using the L-BFGS quasi-Newton method, which approximates the inverse Hessian from successive gradient evaluations without forming or storing the full Hessian matrix.
Each L-BFGS iteration requires one gradient evaluation (= two PDE solves), and convergence is typically achieved in
O(10)
–
O(100)
iterations.

Remark 3
(Why iterative L-BFGS is more efficient than the closed-form solution)

The forward model of this study is linear:
\mathbf{U}_{f}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}
, and the reduced objective (
16
) is a convex quadratic.
A closed-form solution therefore exists via the normal equations:

(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}+\alpha\mathbf{I})\,\bm{\theta}^{*}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}},

where
\mathbf{A}=-\omega^{2}\,\mathbf{S}_{f}\,\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}\,\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times N_{\theta}}
is the sensing matrix.
However, forming
\mathbf{A}
explicitly requires
N_{\theta}
forward solves (one per column of
\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}\mathbf{P}
).
When
N_{\theta}
is large, this is computationally prohibitive.

By contrast, the AD-based L-BFGS approach never forms
\mathbf{A}
.
Each L-BFGS iteration evaluates the gradient via one forward solve (
\mathbf{D}_{ff}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}=\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}
) and one adjoint solve (
\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}=\mathbf{w}
), both reusing the same sparse LU factorization of
\mathbf{D}_{ff}
.
If L-BFGS converges in
k
iterations, the total cost is
2k
back-substitutions.
In practice
k\sim O(10)
–
O(100)
, so the iterative approach requires
O(20)
–
O(200)
PDE solves, far fewer than the
N_{\theta}
solves needed for the closed-form approach when
N_{\theta}
is large.

The correctness of the AD-computed gradient is verified via the Taylor test, a standard procedure in PDE-CO
(
26
)
: the first-order Taylor remainder must converge at
O(\epsilon^{2})
as the perturbation step
\epsilon\to 0
.
The detailed derivation and convergence criteria are provided in Appendix
B
.

4
A specific PDE-CO formulation for structural dynamics

This section develops the PDE-CO formulation step by step, starting from the governing PDE and arriving at an AD-based iterative solution for the full-field displacement and strain.
Each subsection introduces one building block, and the connections between them are summarized in Figure
2
at the end.

4.1
Governing equations

Consider a structure occupying domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with boundary
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{D}\cup\Gamma_{N}
, where
\Gamma_{D}
is the clamped (fixed) boundary and
\Gamma_{N}
is the boundary on which external surface traction is applied.
The displacement field
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)
satisfies the linear elastodynamics initial-boundary value problem:

\displaystyle\rho\ddot{\mathbf{u}}-\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{x},t)\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T],

(20)

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{t}(\mathbf{x},t)\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{N},

(21)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{D},

(22)

where
\rho
is the mass density,
\bm{\sigma}
is the Cauchy stress tensor,
\mathbf{b}
is the body force per unit volume,
\mathbf{n}
is the outward unit normal on
\Gamma_{N}
, and
\mathbf{t}
is the surface traction.
The constitutive relation for linear isotropic elasticity is:

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}=\lambda\,\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\varepsilon})\,\mathbf{I}+2\mu\,\bm{\varepsilon},

(23)

where
\lambda=E\nu/\bigl((1+\nu)(1-2\nu)\bigr)
and
\mu=E/\bigl(2(1+\nu)\bigr)
are the Lamé parameters,
E
is Young’s modulus,
\nu
is Poisson’s ratio, and the small strain tensor is
\bm{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\top})
.

Remark 4
(The inverse problem)

In Eq. (
21
), the surface traction
\mathbf{t}
on
\Gamma_{N}
is the quantity to be recovered.
In a standard forward problem,
\mathbf{t}
is given and
\mathbf{u}
is computed.
In the present inverse problem,
\mathbf{t}
is unknown and must be recovered from sparse observations of
\mathbf{u}
(or its derivatives) at a few sensor locations inside
\Omega
.

4.2
Harmonic excitation and dynamic stiffness

The present study focuses on the case of harmonic (single-frequency) excitation, which enables an algebraic reformulation that avoids time integration entirely.
Under harmonic excitation at circular frequency
\omega
, all time-dependent quantities are expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)=\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x})\,e^{i\omega t},\quad\mathbf{t}(\mathbf{x},t)=\hat{\mathbf{t}}(\mathbf{x})\,e^{i\omega t},\quad\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{x},t)=\hat{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x})\,e^{i\omega t},

(24)

where the hat (
\hat{\cdot}
) denotes the frequency-domain (complex) amplitude.
The frequency
\omega
is assumed known — it can be identified from the frequency content of sensor time histories (e.g., via FFT) or is prescribed in controlled laboratory tests.

Substituting Eq. (
24
) into Eq. (
20
) and canceling the common factor
e^{i\omega t}
, the time derivative becomes a multiplication by
-\omega^{2}
:

\displaystyle\rho\,\ddot{\mathbf{u}}=\rho\,(i\omega)^{2}\,\hat{\mathbf{u}}\,e^{i\omega t}=-\omega^{2}\rho\,\hat{\mathbf{u}}\,e^{i\omega t}.

(25)

The governing PDE (
20
) thus reduces to the frequency-domain form:

\displaystyle-\omega^{2}\rho\,\hat{\mathbf{u}}-\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}(\hat{\mathbf{u}})=\hat{\mathbf{b}}\quad\text{in }\Omega.

(26)

Although a single frequency is assumed here, the extension to broadband excitation is straightforward under linearity: the sensor time histories are decomposed into frequency components via FFT, the PDE-CO problem is solved independently at each frequency
\omega_{k}
(with the corresponding dynamic stiffness
\mathbf{D}_{ff}(\omega_{k})
), and the results are superposed via IFFT to reconstruct the time-domain full-field response.
The single-frequency formulation therefore serves as the fundamental building block for general dynamic loading.

Remark 5
(Advantage of harmonic assumption)

Equation (
26
) is a time-independent PDE for the amplitude
\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x})
.
The original dynamic problem (a hyperbolic PDE in space-time) has been reduced to a single elliptic-like boundary value problem in space alone.
This is the key simplification that makes the inverse problem tractable: only one linear system per frequency needs to be solved, rather than marching through time.

4.3
FE discretization and semi-discrete system

Equation (
26
) is discretized using the standard Galerkin finite element method.
The displacement field is approximated as
\mathbf{u}^{h}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{I=1}^{N_{\mathrm{node}}}N_{I}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{d}_{I}
, where
N_{I}
is the shape function associated with node
I
and
\mathbf{d}_{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the nodal displacement vector.

Multiplying Eq. (
20
) by a test function
\mathbf{v}^{h}
, integrating over
\Omega
, applying integration by parts, and substituting the FE approximation yields the semi-discrete equation of motion:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}\ddot{\mathbf{U}}(t)+\mathbf{K}\mathbf{U}(t)=\mathbf{R}(t),

(27)

where
\mathbf{U}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the global nodal displacement vector,
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is the consistent mass matrix,
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is the stiffness matrix, and
\mathbf{R}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the consistent nodal force vector assembled from body forces and surface tractions.
Note that damping is omitted for simplicity; the formulation extends naturally to include viscous or proportional damping via a damping matrix
\mathbf{C}
.

For harmonic excitation, substituting
\mathbf{U}(t)=\hat{\mathbf{U}}\,e^{i\omega t}
and
\mathbf{R}(t)=\hat{\mathbf{R}}\,e^{i\omega t}
into Eq. (
27
) and defining the dynamic stiffness matrix
\mathbf{D}(\omega)\coloneqq\mathbf{K}-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}(\omega)\,\hat{\mathbf{U}}=\hat{\mathbf{R}}.

(28)

The dynamic stiffness
\mathbf{D}(\omega)
combines the elastic restoring force (
\mathbf{K}
) and the inertial reaction (
-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}
) into a single operator.
At low frequencies (
\omega\to 0
),
\mathbf{D}\approx\mathbf{K}
and the problem reduces to the quasi-static regime.
At a natural frequency
\omega=\omega_{n}
,
\mathbf{D}
becomes singular (resonance).
The excitation frequency
\omega
must therefore be chosen away from any natural frequency.

4.4
DOF partitioning and boundary condition elimination

The global DOFs are partitioned into two disjoint sets based on their boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{U}}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}\\
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{c}\end{bmatrix},\quad\hat{\mathbf{R}}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{f}\\
\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{c}\end{bmatrix},

(29)

where subscript
f
denotes free DOFs (unknown displacements) and subscript
c
denotes clamped DOFs (prescribed as
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{c}=\mathbf{0}
on
\Gamma_{D}
).

The dynamic stiffness system (
28
) partitions as:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{D}_{ff}&\mathbf{D}_{fc}\\
\mathbf{D}_{cf}&\mathbf{D}_{cc}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}\\
\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{c}\end{bmatrix},

(30)

where
\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}
is the external force on the free DOFs (including the unknown traction on
\Gamma_{N}
), and
\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{c}
is the reaction force at the clamped boundary (not needed for the present problem).
Since
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{c}=\mathbf{0}
, the first block row gives:

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{ff}\,\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}=\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f},

(31)

where
\mathbf{D}_{ff}=\mathbf{K}_{ff}-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}_{ff}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{f}\times N_{f}}
is the free-DOF dynamic stiffness matrix.
This is the
governing equation
of the present inverse problem: given observations related to
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}
, the objective is to recover the right-hand side
\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}
.

Remark 6
(An important distinction from Dirichlet-type inverse problems)

In many inverse problems, the unknown is a prescribed displacement on part of the boundary (Dirichlet BC), which requires partitioning into free, clamped, and prescribed sets.
In the present formulation, the unknown is a prescribed force on
\Gamma_{N}
(Neumann BC).
Since the loaded boundary has unknown forces but free displacements, those DOFs remain in the “free” set.
This simplifies the partition: only free and clamped DOFs are present, with no prescribed-displacement partition needed.

4.5
Force parameterization: the key modeling step

The force vector
\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{f}}
has
N_{f}
components — one for each free DOF.
Although the loaded boundary
\Gamma_{N}
may be known from engineering context (e.g., an inner pressure surface or a contact face), the distribution of force across
\Gamma_{N}
is unknown and must be recovered from sensor data.

To this end, a force parameterization is introduced that maps a reduced set of unknowns on
\Gamma_{N}
to the full force vector.
Let
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\theta}}
be the vector of unknown force amplitudes at the
N_{\theta}
candidate DOFs.
The relationship between
\bm{\theta}
and the full force vector is:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}=\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta},

(32)

where
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{f}\times N_{\theta}}
is the force mapping matrix (also called the load distribution matrix).
Together with the sensor extraction matrix
\mathbf{S}_{f}
,
\mathbf{P}
defines the input–output pipeline of PDE-CO:
\mathbf{P}
maps
\bm{\theta}
into the full DOF space (input selection), while
\mathbf{S}_{f}
extracts sensor DOFs from the solution (output selection); see Remark 1 for the complete information flow diagram.
When the loaded boundary
\Gamma_{N}
is known a priori,
\mathbf{P}
is constructed as a Boolean selection matrix that picks out the DOFs on
\Gamma_{N}
:

\displaystyle P_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if free DOF $i$ corresponds to loaded DOF $j$},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(33)

Each
\theta_{j}
then represents a nodal force value (in N) at a known loaded DOF, and
N_{\theta}
equals the number of loaded DOFs on
\Gamma_{N}
.
When
\Gamma_{N}
is a localized region,
N_{\theta}
can be significantly smaller than
N_{f}
; in general,
N_{\theta}
typically remains larger than
N_{s}
, so the inverse problem is underdetermined from a purely data-driven perspective.
PDE-CO nevertheless produces accurate solutions because the PDE constraint
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{U},\bm{\theta})=\mathbf{0}
provides additional information beyond the sensor data: for each trial
\bm{\theta}
, the governing equation uniquely determines the full displacement field
\mathbf{U}(\bm{\theta})
, enforcing spatial coherence across the entire domain.
The Tikhonov regularization
\alpha\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2}
further selects the minimum-norm solution, ensuring uniqueness even when multiple force distributions could produce similar sensor responses.

Remark 7
(Physical meaning of
\mathbf{P}
)

The Boolean matrix
\mathbf{P}
encodes the spatial pattern of the candidate load (which DOFs can carry external force), while
\bm{\theta}
encodes the amplitude at each candidate DOF.
Each
\theta_{j}
has units of force (N), and the product
\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}
scatters these values into the full
N_{f}
-dimensional force vector, with zeros at non-loaded DOFs.
Since
\Gamma_{N}
is known,
\mathbf{P}
localizes the force by construction; the optimization only needs to determine the force amplitudes
\bm{\theta}
.

4.6
Sensor model and observation equation

In practice, accelerometers are the most common sensor type for digital-twin and virtual-sensing applications in structural dynamics
(
18
;
15
;
11
;
19
;
24
;
16
)
.
Under harmonic excitation, the acceleration at a point is related to the displacement by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{x},t)=\ddot{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x},t)=-\omega^{2}\,\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x})\,e^{i\omega t}.

(34)

Thus the acceleration amplitude is simply
\hat{\mathbf{a}}=-\omega^{2}\,\hat{\mathbf{u}}
: a scalar multiple of the displacement amplitude.
As noted in Section
4.2
, the single-frequency formulation extends naturally to broadband excitation via FFT-based frequency decomposition and superposition.

Suppose
N_{\mathrm{sensor}}
accelerometer nodes are placed at interior locations (away from both
\Gamma_{D}
and
\Gamma_{N}
), providing
N_{s}=3\times N_{\mathrm{sensor}}
scalar observations (three components per node).
The observed acceleration amplitudes are collected in a vector
\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}}
. To relate
\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}
to the full free-DOF displacement vector
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}
, a sensor selection matrix
\mathbf{S}_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times N_{f}}
is introduced.
This is a Boolean matrix that extracts sensor DOFs from the free DOF vector:

\displaystyle(S_{f})_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if sensor DOF $i$ corresponds to free DOF $j$},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(35)

Each row of
\mathbf{S}_{f}
contains exactly one nonzero entry.
The observation equation then reads:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}=-\omega^{2}\,\mathbf{S}_{f}\,\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}.

(36)

4.7
PDE-CO formulation: from physics to optimization

All the building blocks are now in place to formulate the full-field inference in structural dynamics as a PDE-constrained optimization problem.
The idea is simple:
find the force parameter
\bm{\theta}
such that the predicted sensor accelerations match the observed accelerations as closely as possible, subject to the structure satisfying its governing equation
.

Step 1: Forward model.

For a given trial load
\bm{\theta}
, the structural response is uniquely determined by the PDE constraint.
Substituting Eq. (
32
) into Eq. (
31
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{ff}\,\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}=\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}.

(37)

This equation maps the load parameter
\bm{\theta}
to the full displacement field
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}
through the governing PDE.
Every entry of
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}
— not just the sensor DOFs — is determined.

Step 2: Predicted acceleration.

Using Eqs. (
36
) and (
37
):

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{pred}}(\bm{\theta})=-\omega^{2}\,\mathbf{S}_{f}\,\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}(\bm{\theta})=-\omega^{2}\,\mathbf{S}_{f}\,\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}\,\mathbf{P}\,\bm{\theta}.

(38)

Step 3: Forward operator.

Since all operations in Eq. (
38
) are linear in
\bm{\theta}
, the entire forward model can be written as a single matrix-vector product:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{pred}}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbf{A}\bm{\theta},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{A}\coloneqq-\omega^{2}\,\mathbf{S}_{f}\,\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}\,\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times N_{\theta}}.

(39)

Each column
\mathbf{a}_{j}
of
\mathbf{A}
represents the sensor acceleration pattern produced by a unit force at loaded DOF
j
.
The matrix
\mathbf{A}
encapsulates the full physics: it maps an arbitrary load distribution
\bm{\theta}
on
\Gamma_{N}
, through the governing PDE, to the resulting acceleration at the sensor locations.

Remark 8
(Linearity and matrix-free computation)

The linearity of Eq. (
39
) is a consequence of the linearity of the governing PDE and the linear force parameterization.
Although
\mathbf{A}
exists as a mathematical object, it is never formed explicitly when
N_{\theta}
is large: doing so would require
N_{\theta}
forward solves.
Instead, the matrix-vector products
\mathbf{A}\bm{\theta}
(forward) and
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{r}
(adjoint, where
\mathbf{r}=\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{pred}}-\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}
is the residual) are computed implicitly via PDE solves, and the gradient is obtained via reverse-mode AD.
For nonlinear problems, the same AD + L-BFGS framework applies without modification.

The quantities in this framework are classified as follows.
The stiffness and mass matrices
\mathbf{K}_{ff}
,
\mathbf{M}_{ff}
are assembled from the FE model; the force mapping matrix
\mathbf{P}
is constructed from the chosen parameterization; the sensor selection matrix
\mathbf{S}_{f}
encodes the sensor layout.
These are known offline.
The excitation frequency
\omega
(prescribed or identified via FFT of the sensor signal) and the measured sensor accelerations
\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}
are known online.
The only unknown is the force parameter vector
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\theta}}
.
The displacement field
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}
is not an independent unknown: once
\bm{\theta}
is fixed,
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}
is uniquely determined by the PDE constraint (
37
).
The optimization therefore searches over
\bm{\theta}
alone; the full-field acceleration (and hence displacement) at every mesh node is a by-product of the PDE solve.

Step 4: Optimization problem.

The optimization problem is:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\theta}}}\;\mathcal{J}(\bm{\theta})=\underbrace{\|\mathbf{A}\bm{\theta}-\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}\|^{2}}_{\text{data misfit}}+\underbrace{\alpha\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2}}_{\text{regularization}},

(40)

where
\alpha>0
is the Tikhonov regularization weight.
The objective
\mathcal{J}(\bm{\theta})
measures how well the predicted sensor response (from the trial load
\bm{\theta}
via the PDE) matches the observed sensor data.

4.8
Regularization

The Tikhonov term
\alpha\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2}
in Eq. (
40
) plays different roles depending on the problem setting:

1.

Underdetermined case
(
N_{\theta}>N_{s}
): The data misfit alone has infinitely many minimizers (a family of loads producing identical sensor readings). The regularization selects the minimum-norm solution by penalizing large force amplitudes.

2.

Overdetermined case
(
N_{s}>N_{\theta}
): The system
\mathbf{A}\bm{\theta}=\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}
is generically inconsistent (no exact solution exists, e.g., due to measurement noise). The regularization stabilizes the least-squares solution against ill-conditioning and noise amplification.

3.

Noise-free, well-conditioned case
: When the data is exact and
\mathbf{A}
is well-conditioned,
\alpha
can be set to a very small value (e.g.,
10^{-6}
) to provide minimal bias.

The weight
\alpha
controls the trade-off between data fidelity (small
\alpha
, trust the data) and solution regularity (large
\alpha
, penalize large
\bm{\theta}
).
In practice,
\alpha
can be selected via the L-curve method, generalized cross-validation, or the discrepancy principle
(
9
)
.

4.9
AD-based iterative solution via L-BFGS

When
N_{\theta}
is large, forming the explicit sensing matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times N_{\theta}}
requires
N_{\theta}
forward solves and is computationally expensive.
Instead, Eq. (
40
) is solved iteratively using L-BFGS with gradients computed via reverse-mode automatic differentiation (AD).

Each L-BFGS iteration requires evaluating the objective
\mathcal{J}(\bm{\theta})
and its gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{J}
.
Without forming
\mathbf{A}
explicitly, this proceeds in two steps:

1.

Forward solve
: Given trial
\bm{\theta}
, solve
\mathbf{D}_{ff}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}=\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}
for
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}
, then compute
\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{pred}}=-\omega^{2}\,\mathbf{S}_{f}\,\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}
and the residual
\mathbf{r}=\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{pred}}-\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}
.

2.

Adjoint solve
: Scatter
\mathbf{w}=-\omega^{2}\,\mathbf{S}_{f}^{\top}\,\mathbf{r}
back to the free DOF space, solve
\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}=\mathbf{w}
for the adjoint variable
\bm{\lambda}
, and compute

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{J}=2\mathbf{P}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}+2\alpha\bm{\theta}.

(41)

Since
\mathbf{D}_{ff}
is symmetric, the adjoint solve uses the same factorization as the forward solve.
The total cost per iteration is two linear solves with
\mathbf{D}_{ff}
, regardless of
N_{\theta}
.
With a pre-computed sparse LU factorization of
\mathbf{D}_{ff}
, each back-substitution costs
O(N_{f})
, making each gradient evaluation efficient.

In practice, the gradient (
41
) is not derived by hand but computed automatically via reverse-mode AD through the forward solver.
In the present study, JAX
(
5
)
is used to differentiate through
jnp.linalg.solve
: the call
jax.grad(objective)
produces a function that evaluates Eq. (
41
) automatically, including correct handling of the implicit differentiation through the linear solve.
This is mathematically equivalent to the hand-derived adjoint gradient but eliminates the need for manual derivation, reducing implementation effort and potential for errors.

The gradient is passed to the L-BFGS-B optimizer (SciPy
minimize

(
25
)
), which approximates the inverse Hessian from successive gradient evaluations.
For the linear (quadratic) case, L-BFGS converges rapidly because the Hessian
2(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}+\alpha\mathbf{I})
is constant.

Remark 9
(Computational cost)

Each L-BFGS iteration requires two
\mathbf{D}_{ff}
solves.
With a sparse LU factorization (computed once), each back-substitution costs
O(N_{f}\,b)
where
b
is the bandwidth.
For
k
iterations of L-BFGS, the total cost is
O(k\cdot N_{f}\cdot b)
, compared to
O(N_{\theta}\cdot N_{f}\cdot b)
for forming
\mathbf{A}
explicitly (
N_{\theta}
forward solves).
Since typically
k\sim 10
–
100\ll N_{\theta}
, the iterative approach is more efficient when
N_{\theta}
is large.

4.10
Full-field reconstruction

Once the optimal load
\bm{\theta}^{*}
is obtained, the complete structural response is recovered by a single forward solve:

\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}^{*}=\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}^{*},\qquad\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{full}}^{*}=-\omega^{2}\,\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}^{*}.

(42)

The output of PDE-CO is the full displacement and acceleration fields over the entire domain
\Omega
, not just at sensor locations.
The force parameter
\bm{\theta}^{*}
is an internal optimization variable; the primary output is the reconstructed full-field state
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}^{*}
.
This is possible because the PDE constraint (
37
) uniquely determines the full state from the force parameter.

5
Implementation using JAX-FEM

Figure
2
illustrates the overall pipeline, which consists of two stages.
The offline stage is executed on a CPU using FEniCSx and proceeds as follows:
(i) a finite element mesh is generated for the target structure;
(ii) the global stiffness and mass matrices
\mathbf{K}
and
\mathbf{M}
are assembled;
(iii) the degrees of freedom are partitioned into clamped (Dirichlet) and free sets;
(iv) the free-DOF submatrices
\mathbf{K}_{ff}
and
\mathbf{M}_{ff}
are extracted;
(v) the Boolean force mapping matrix
\mathbf{P}
is constructed to map the reduced force parameter
\bm{\theta}
to the full free-DOF force vector;
(vi) an eigenvalue problem is solved to select the excitation frequency
\omega
; and
(vii) the frequency-domain dynamic stiffness matrix
\mathbf{D}_{ff}=\mathbf{K}_{ff}-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}_{ff}
is formed.
The online stage is executed on a GPU using JAX.
Given sensor observations
\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}
, the offline-computed matrices
\mathbf{D}_{ff}
and
\mathbf{P}
are transferred to the GPU (dashed arrows in Figure
2
), and the following operations are performed:
(i) the forward problem
\mathbf{D}_{ff}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}=\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}
and the gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{J}
are evaluated via automatic differentiation;
(ii) the L-BFGS optimizer iteratively updates
\bm{\theta}
to minimize
\|\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{pred}}(\bm{\theta})-\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}\|^{2}+\alpha\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2}
; and
(iii) upon convergence, the full-field displacement
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}^{*}=\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}^{*}
and the full-field force distribution
\mathbf{f}^{*}=\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}^{*}
are reconstructed.

5.1
Finite element implementation (offline stage)

The spatial discretization follows the standard Galerkin finite element method using FEniCSx
(
2
)
.
The displacement field is interpolated using continuous Lagrange shape functions as described in Section
4.3
.
The trial and test function spaces are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{h}

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\mathbf{u}^{h}\in[H^{1}(\Omega)]^{n_{\mathrm{dim}}}\;\big|\;\mathbf{u}^{h}=\bar{\mathbf{u}}\text{ on }\Gamma_{D}\bigr\},

(43)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{h}

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\mathbf{v}^{h}\in[H^{1}(\Omega)]^{n_{\mathrm{dim}}}\;\big|\;\mathbf{v}^{h}=\mathbf{0}\text{ on }\Gamma_{D}\bigr\},

(44)

where
n_{\mathrm{dim}}
denotes the spatial dimension.
The weak form of the governing equation (
20
) is stated as follows: find
\mathbf{u}^{h}\in\mathcal{S}^{h}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\bm{\sigma}(\mathbf{u}^{h}):\bm{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v}^{h})\,\mathrm{d}V+\int_{\Omega}\rho\,\ddot{\mathbf{u}}^{h}\cdot\mathbf{v}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}V=\int_{\Gamma_{N}}\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{v}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}S\qquad\forall\,\mathbf{v}^{h}\in\mathcal{V}^{h}.

(45)

The small strain tensor is discretized as

\displaystyle\bm{\varepsilon}^{h}=\sum_{a=1}^{n_{\mathrm{node}}}\mathbf{B}_{a}\,\mathbf{u}_{a},

(46)

where
\mathbf{B}_{a}=\nabla^{s}N_{a}
is the symmetric gradient of the shape function associated with node
a
.
Substituting the FE interpolation into Eq. (
45
), the global stiffness matrix
\mathbf{K}
, consistent mass matrix
\mathbf{M}
, and consistent nodal force vector
\mathbf{R}
are assembled from element-level contributions:

\displaystyle K_{ab}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{B}_{a}^{\top}\,\mathbb{C}\,\mathbf{B}_{b}\,\mathrm{d}V,

(47)

\displaystyle M_{ab}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\rho\,N_{a}\,N_{b}\,\mathbf{I}\,\mathrm{d}V,

(48)

\displaystyle R_{a}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{N}}N_{a}\,\mathbf{t}\,\mathrm{d}S,

(49)

where
\mathbb{C}
is the fourth-order elasticity tensor corresponding to the linear isotropic constitutive relation (
23
), and
\rho
is the mass density.
The surface traction
\mathbf{t}
on
\Gamma_{N}
is the unknown quantity in the present inverse problem, and the consistent nodal force vector is parameterized as
\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}=\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}
via the Boolean selection matrix
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{f}\times N_{\theta}}
defined in Eq. (
33
).

Applying the DOF partition (Eq. (
29
), Section
4.4
) to the global stiffness and mass matrices yields the block structure:

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}_{ff}&\mathbf{K}_{fc}\\
\mathbf{K}_{cf}&\mathbf{K}_{cc}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{M}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}_{ff}&\mathbf{M}_{fc}\\
\mathbf{M}_{cf}&\mathbf{M}_{cc}\end{bmatrix}.

(50)

Since the homogeneous Dirichlet condition
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{c}=\mathbf{0}
eliminates the coupling terms
\mathbf{K}_{fc}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{c}
and
\mathbf{M}_{fc}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{c}
from the governing equation (
31
), only the free-DOF sub-matrices
\mathbf{K}_{ff}
,
\mathbf{M}_{ff}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{f}\times N_{f}}
are required.

For the modal expansion method (Section
2
), which will be compared with the proposed method in the numerical examples (Section
6
), the generalized eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{ff}\bm{\phi}_{n}=\omega_{n}^{2}\,\mathbf{M}_{ff}\bm{\phi}_{n}

(51)

is solved to obtain the natural frequencies
\omega_{n}
and mode shapes
\bm{\phi}_{n}
.
The dynamic stiffness matrix is then formed at the excitation frequency
\omega
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{ff}=\mathbf{K}_{ff}-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}_{ff}.

(52)

5.2
Automatic differentiation using JAX (online stage)

In the online stage, the optimization problem (
40
) is solved iteratively using L-BFGS with gradients computed via reverse-mode automatic differentiation (AD).
The implementation leverages JAX
(
5
)
for AD and SciPy
(
25
)
for the L-BFGS-B optimizer.

The dynamic stiffness matrix
\mathbf{D}_{ff}
assembled in the offline stage is first factorized as

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{ff}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{U},

(53)

where
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{U}
are the lower and upper triangular factors, respectively.
This factorization is performed once and reused across all L-BFGS iterations, so that each subsequent linear solve reduces to a pair of triangular back-substitutions.

At each iteration, the objective function
\mathcal{J}(\bm{\theta})
and its gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{J}
are evaluated via the forward and adjoint solves described in Section
4.9
.
Rather than deriving the adjoint gradient analytically, JAX differentiates through the linear solve automatically: the call
jax.grad(objective)
produces a function that evaluates Eq. (
41
), including implicit differentiation through
\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}
.
The resulting gradient is passed to the L-BFGS-B optimizer, which approximates the inverse Hessian from successive gradient evaluations and updates
\bm{\theta}
accordingly.
Upon convergence, the full-field displacement is reconstructed via a single additional back-substitution as described in Section
4.10
.

7
Discussion

The consistent superiority of PDE-CO across all three examples demonstrates two key properties.
(i) Robustness to geometric complexity — from the flat plate to the curved pipe (both with free-end face loading), PDE-CO maintains sub-percent displacement error and correctly identifies the loading region.
(ii) Robustness to loading type — from boundary face loading (plate, pipe) to inner-surface distributed loading (RPV), PDE-CO continues to outperform modal expansion in displacement recovery and uniquely provides a spatially resolved force estimate.

7.1
Why PDE-CO achieves superior displacement recovery

The numerical results across all three examples confirm the theoretical analysis of Section
4.7
.
PDE-CO achieves superior virtual sensing accuracy through two complementary mechanisms.

First, the PDE constraint enforces exactness.
The governing equation
\mathbf{D}_{ff}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}=\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}
ensures that, for any trial force
\bm{\theta}
, the resulting displacement field
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}
is exactly consistent with the full-order FE system.
Unlike modal expansion, which truncates the kinematic space to
N_{d}
modes, PDE-CO uses the complete FE system, eliminating modal truncation error within the FE discretization.

Second, the inverse dynamic stiffness operator
\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}
acts as a smoothing operator, strongly attenuating high-frequency spatial components in
\bm{\theta}
when mapped to displacement.
The optimization finds
\bm{\theta}^{*}
whose displacement
\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}^{*}
matches the sensor data, and the PDE constraint propagates this accuracy from the sensor locations to the entire domain.

As a further advantage, the PDE-CO framework simultaneously recovers the optimal force distribution
\mathbf{f}^{*}=\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}^{*}
as a natural by-product of the optimization; while modal expansion can derive a force estimate by back-calculation (
\hat{\mathbf{f}}=\mathbf{D}_{ff}\bm{\Phi}\mathbf{q}
), this estimate inherits modal truncation error and is not jointly optimized with the displacement.
This is particularly valuable for structural health monitoring scenarios where the loading acts on inaccessible surfaces, such as the inner surface of a pressure vessel.

It should be noted that displacement accuracy is assessed quantitatively via DOF-wise error norms (Table
1
), whereas load recovery is assessed qualitatively in this study.
A direct scalar error norm for the recovered force is not reported because the optimization variable
\bm{\theta}^{*}
is a distributed nodal force vector whose spatial distribution and sparsity differ fundamentally from the concentrated or Gaussian ground-truth load; comparing them as vectors would conflate spatial distribution differences with amplitude errors.
Instead, correctness of the recovered force is judged by whether the identified loading region and directional pattern match the known true load, which they do in all three examples.

7.2
Why PDE-CO outperforms modal expansion

The numerical results reveal an important nuance: modal expansion itself is a strong virtual sensing baseline.
For the pipe (
1.11\%
) and RPV (
1.86\%
), modal expansion achieves sub-
2\%
displacement errors that would be acceptable in many engineering applications, and the improvement brought by PDE-CO is meaningful but incremental in these cases.
The picture is more dramatic for the thin plate (
7.69\%
), where modal truncation is severe due to the high-frequency bending modes that dominate the response near the clamped boundary.

Displacement recovery, however, is only one facet of the comparison.
PDE-CO’s distinctive advantage lies in load identification: the force
\bm{\theta}
is the primary optimization variable, jointly optimized with the displacement through the PDE constraint. Modal expansion can derive a force estimate by back-calculation (
\hat{\mathbf{f}}=\mathbf{D}_{ff}\bm{\Phi}\mathbf{q}
), but this estimate inherits modal truncation error and is not directly optimized against sensor data.
This capability — qualitatively verified in all three examples — is practically irreplaceable for structural health monitoring scenarios where the loading is unknown, inaccessible, or spatially distributed.

Considering both criteria, the three examples yield displacement improvement ratios of
17\times
for the plate (
7.69\%\to 0.45\%
),
4.0\times
for the pipe (
1.11\%\to 0.28\%
), and
2.1\times
for the RPV (
1.86\%\to 0.885\%
).
The decreasing ratio from plate to RPV does not indicate that PDE-CO weakens; rather, it reflects that modal expansion performs less catastrophically as the sensor-to-mode ratio improves (
N_{\rm sensor}/N_{d}=12/11\to 24/23\to 108/50
) and as the structure becomes thick-walled (pipe, RPV) rather than thin (plate).
Nonetheless, PDE-CO consistently achieves lower displacement error in absolute terms across all cases, including the RPV where the load acts on the inner surface — a scenario that modal expansion was never designed to handle.

The underlying mechanisms are detailed in Section
7.1
. Briefly, the modal truncation of the kinematic basis (
N_{d}\leq N_{s}-1
) directly limits displacement accuracy — most severely for the thin plate where high-frequency bending modes are discarded — while the absence of equilibrium enforcement renders any back-calculated force estimate dynamically inconsistent. PDE-CO eliminates both deficiencies through the governing equation constraint.

7.3
Computational considerations

The computational cost of PDE-CO consists of one LU factorization of
\mathbf{D}_{ff}
at cost
\mathcal{O}(N_{f}^{3})
for dense matrices (or
\mathcal{O}(N_{f}\,b^{2})
for banded matrices), followed by
K
L-BFGS iterations each requiring two back-substitutions at cost
\mathcal{O}(2KN_{f}^{2})
.
The three examples span three orders of magnitude in optimization time: the plate (
N_{f}=1{,}872
,
K=11
iterations to convergence) required
t_{\rm opt}=0.54
s (total including JIT compilation:
2.9
s); the pipe (
N_{f}=11{,}160
,
K=200
iterations, at the maximum allowable iterations) required
615
s (
3.1
s per iteration); and the RPV (
N_{f}=7{,}206
,
K=2{,}000
iterations, at the maximum allowable iterations) required
15{,}294
s (
7.6
s per iteration, higher due to the inner-surface scatter operation).
In comparison, modal expansion wall times are negligible:
0.54
s (plate),
58.8
s (pipe, dominated by eigenvalue decomposition), and
14.1
s (RPV).

The large PDE-CO wall times motivate GPU acceleration.
The core bottleneck is the dense linear solve
\mathbf{D}_{ff}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
, called twice per L-BFGS iteration via JAX’s cuSOLVER backend on an NVIDIA RTX 5070 Ti (16 GB VRAM).
The GPU outperforms the CPU beyond
N_{f}\approx 500
; at
N_{f}=12{,}144
(RPV coarse mesh,
l_{c}=300
mm) it delivers a
64.8\times
speedup (
1.77
s vs.
114.6
s per solve), as reported in Table
2
.
This reduces the effective RPV iteration cost from
{\sim}7.6
s to
{\sim}0.15
s, and implies a reduction from
615
s to approximately
12
s for the pipe at the same 200 iterations — making near-real-time monitoring feasible.
In contrast, modal expansion requires one eigenvalue decomposition (
\mathcal{O}(N_{d}N_{f}^{2})
) plus a single
N_{s}\times N_{d}
least-squares solve (
\mathcal{O}(N_{d}^{3})
), which is fast but does not benefit from iteration-level GPU parallelism.
The additional cost of PDE-CO is therefore justified by its accuracy gains and force identification capability, particularly when GPU acceleration is available.

7.4
Limitations

The current formulation rests on several assumptions that define its scope.
The framework assumes linear elastodynamics, so geometric or material nonlinearity is not addressed.
The dynamic stiffness formulation is applicable to single-frequency harmonic loading with a known excitation frequency
\omega
; broadband or transient loading requires extension to the time domain.
Furthermore, the excitation frequency must be chosen away from the natural frequencies of the structure, since
\mathbf{D}_{ff}(\omega)
becomes singular at resonance; the present study uses
\omega=\omega_{1}/2
for all examples, and the sensitivity of the recovered solution to the choice of
\omega
has not been systematically investigated.
For large structural models with
N_{f}\gtrsim 10^{4}
DOFs, the dense LU factorization becomes computationally prohibitive, and sparse direct or iterative solvers must replace it.
Finally, in the presence of significant measurement noise, the regularization parameter
\alpha
must be selected carefully — for example, via the L-curve method or cross-validation — to balance data fidelity against solution regularity.

7.5
Future extensions

The differentiable virtual sensing framework based on PDE-CO admits several natural extensions.
Projection-based reduced-order models (ROMs)
(
6
;
13
;
12
;
27
)
can be incorporated to reduce the forward solve cost while retaining the physics constraint advantage, enabling ROM-accelerated PDE-CO for large-scale applications.
Extension to the time domain via Newmark-
\beta
time integration would enable transient full-field inference and broaden applicability to impact and broadband loading scenarios.
Multi-frequency and broadband problems can be addressed through superposition of harmonic solutions or frequency-domain sweeps.
Geometric or material nonlinearity can be incorporated by reformulating the PDE constraint in residual form, enabling force identification in structures where modal expansion is inapplicable.
Finally, the differentiable solver is directly compatible with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo sampling, enabling Bayesian inference for principled uncertainty quantification of both the recovered displacement field and the identified force distribution.

8
Conclusions

This study presented a PDE-constrained optimization (PDE-CO) framework for virtual sensing in structural dynamics, recovering full-field displacement and applied loading from sparse acceleration sensor measurements.
The framework couples an offline finite element stage (FEniCSx) with an online GPU-accelerated optimization stage (JAX), and was verified on three examples of increasing complexity: a cantilever plate, a
90^{\circ}
elbow pipe, and a double-loop cylindrical reactor pressure vessel (RPV) representative of a 300 MW pressurized nuclear reactor.

PDE-CO consistently outperformed modal expansion in displacement recovery across all three examples.
Modal expansion itself achieved respectable accuracy for the pipe (
\epsilon_{u}=1.11\%
) and RPV (
\epsilon_{u}=1.86\%
) where the sensor-to-mode ratio was favorable, but PDE-CO reduced these errors to
0.28\%
and
0.885\%
, respectively.
The improvement was most dramatic for the thin cantilever plate, where modal truncation is severe and modal expansion yielded
\epsilon_{u}=7.69\%
; PDE-CO reduced this to
0.45\%
, a
17\times
gain.
Notably, despite a substantial increase in problem size from the plate (
N_{f}=1{,}872
) to the pipe (
N_{f}=11{,}160
), the pipe exhibited lower error for both methods, reflecting the structural advantage of a thick-walled geometry where beam-bending modes dominate and modal truncation is less severe.

The accuracy advantage of PDE-CO stems from the PDE constraint
\mathbf{D}_{ff}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}=\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}
, which enforces exact consistency with the full-order FE system.
This eliminates modal truncation error and propagates sparse sensor information through the governing equation to the entire displacement field.
The recovered displacement is therefore exactly dynamically consistent with the identified loading, regardless of the number or placement of sensors.

Force identification is PDE-CO’s most distinctive advantage over modal expansion. While modal expansion can derive a force estimate through back-calculation from recovered modal coordinates (
\hat{\mathbf{f}}=\mathbf{D}_{ff}\bm{\Phi}\mathbf{q}
), this estimate inherits modal truncation error and is not jointly optimized with the displacement.
PDE-CO simultaneously recovers the optimal force distribution
\mathbf{f}^{*}=\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}^{*}
as a natural by-product of the optimization.
The recovered spatial loading pattern correctly identifies the loading region and directional character in all three examples, and for the RPV this extends to inner-surface load identification from outer-surface sensors alone — a virtual sensing capability with direct relevance to pressure vessel structural health monitoring.
Load recovery is assessed qualitatively rather than by scalar error norms, since the difference in spatial distribution between the recovered distributed force and the concentrated or Gaussian ground-truth load makes vector norm comparison an unsuitable quality metric.

The dominant computational cost is the dense linear solve
\mathbf{D}_{ff}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
, called twice per L-BFGS iteration via JAX’s cuSOLVER backend.
GPU acceleration on an NVIDIA RTX 5070 Ti provides up to
64.8\times
speedup over CPU at
N_{f}\approx 12{,}000
, reducing the RPV per-iteration cost from
{\sim}7.6
s to
{\sim}0.15
s and making near-real-time monitoring feasible at the mesh sizes considered.
However, as the number of force parameters
N_{\theta}
increases substantially — for example, when the candidate loaded boundary is large or finely discretized — the L-BFGS convergence requires more iterations, and total optimization cost grows accordingly; GPU infrastructure is therefore essential for real-time digital twin applications at operational scale.
The AD-based gradient via JAX reverse-mode automatic differentiation eliminates the need for hand-derived adjoint equations; the Taylor test confirms first-order convergence of the discrete gradient, verifying correctness automatically.

The virtual sensing framework provides a foundation for extension to time-dependent problems via Newmark-
\beta
integration, nonlinear systems via residual-form PDE constraints, broadband excitation via multi-frequency superposition, and ROM-accelerated large-scale applications via projection-based model reduction.

Appendix A
Analytical derivation of the forward operator and objective gradient

This appendix provides a detailed derivation of the forward model linearity and the gradient computation used in the AD-based optimization.

A.1
Dynamic stiffness derivation

For harmonic excitation
\mathbf{U}(t)=\hat{\mathbf{U}}e^{i\omega t}
, the semi-discrete equation of motion (
27
) becomes:

\displaystyle-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}\hat{\mathbf{U}}+\mathbf{K}\hat{\mathbf{U}}=\hat{\mathbf{R}},

(54)

which can be written as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}(\omega)\hat{\mathbf{U}}=\hat{\mathbf{R}},\quad\mathbf{D}(\omega)=\mathbf{K}-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}.

(55)

After eliminating clamped DOFs:

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{ff}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}=\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f},

(56)

where
\mathbf{D}_{ff}=\mathbf{K}_{ff}-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}_{ff}
.
Note that
\mathbf{D}_{ff}
is singular when
\omega
coincides with a natural frequency.
The excitation frequency must be chosen away from resonance for the linear solve to be well-conditioned.

A.2
Linearity of the forward model

The forward model maps the unknown load parameter
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\theta}}
to the predicted sensor acceleration
\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{pred}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}}
through a chain of three linear operations.
First, the force parameterization (
32
) assembles the full force vector
\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}=\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}
, which is linear in
\bm{\theta}
because
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{f}\times N_{\theta}}
is a constant matrix depending only on the mesh geometry:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}=\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}.

(57)

Second, the governing equation (
31
) maps the force to the displacement field, which is also linear in
\bm{\theta}
because the dynamic stiffness
\mathbf{D}_{ff}=\mathbf{K}_{ff}-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}_{ff}
is independent of
\bm{\theta}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}=\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{f}=\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}.

(58)

Third, the sensor selection matrix
\mathbf{S}_{f}
extracts sensor DOFs and the kinematic relation
\hat{\mathbf{a}}=-\omega^{2}\hat{\mathbf{u}}
converts displacement to acceleration:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{pred}}=-\omega^{2}\,\mathbf{S}_{f}\,\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}=-\omega^{2}\,\mathbf{S}_{f}\,\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}\,\mathbf{P}\,\bm{\theta}.

(59)

Composing the three steps, the entire forward model reduces to a single matrix-vector product:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{pred}}=\underbrace{-\omega^{2}\,\mathbf{S}_{f}\,\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{-1}\,\mathbf{P}}_{\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times N_{\theta}}}\,\bm{\theta}=\mathbf{A}\bm{\theta},

(60)

since the composition of linear maps is linear.

A.3
Objective function and its properties

The Tikhonov-regularized objective is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\bm{\theta})=\underbrace{\|\mathbf{A}\bm{\theta}-\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}\|^{2}}_{\text{data misfit}}+\underbrace{\alpha\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2}}_{\text{regularization}},

(61)

where
\alpha>0
.
Expanding term by term:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\bm{\theta}^{\top}\bigl(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}+\alpha\mathbf{I}\bigr)\bm{\theta}-2\,\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\,\bm{\theta}+\|\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}\|^{2}.

(62)

This is a quadratic function of
\bm{\theta}
with Hessian
\mathbf{H}=2(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}+\alpha\mathbf{I})
.
Since
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}
is symmetric positive semi-definite and
\alpha>0
, the Hessian is symmetric positive definite:

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\,\mathbf{v}=2\bigl(\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\|^{2}+\alpha\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}\bigr)>0\quad\forall\,\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0},

(63)

guaranteeing that
\mathcal{J}
is strictly convex with a unique global minimum.

A.4
Gradient derivation (adjoint method)

Differentiating Eq. (
62
) with respect to
\bm{\theta}
yields the analytical gradient:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{J}=2(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}+\alpha\mathbf{I})\bm{\theta}-2\,\mathbf{A}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}.

(64)

When
\mathbf{A}
is formed explicitly (feasible for small
N_{\theta}
), setting the gradient to zero gives the closed-form normal equation:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}+\alpha\mathbf{I})\bm{\theta}^{*}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}.

(65)

When
N_{\theta}
is large, forming
\mathbf{A}
explicitly is computationally prohibitive.
Instead, the gradient is computed in matrix-free form via the adjoint method: the forward solve
\mathbf{D}_{ff}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}=\mathbf{P}\bm{\theta}
yields the predicted acceleration
\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{pred}}=-\omega^{2}\mathbf{S}_{f}\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{f}
and residual
\mathbf{r}=\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{pred}}-\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathrm{obs}}
; the adjoint solve
\mathbf{D}_{ff}\bm{\lambda}=-\omega^{2}\mathbf{S}_{f}^{\top}\mathbf{r}
(exploiting
\mathbf{D}_{ff}^{\top}=\mathbf{D}_{ff}
) then yields the gradient as
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{J}=2\mathbf{P}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}+2\alpha\bm{\theta}
.
This procedure requires exactly two
\mathbf{D}_{ff}
solves per gradient evaluation, regardless of
N_{\theta}
.
In practice, the gradient is computed automatically via reverse-mode AD (JAX), which internally implements this adjoint computation.

A.5
Computational cost

The LU factorization of
\mathbf{D}_{ff}
is performed once at cost
\mathcal{O}(N_{f}b^{2})
for sparse FE matrices with bandwidth
b
.
Each L-BFGS iteration then requires two back-substitutions at cost
\mathcal{O}(N_{f}b)
each, giving a total cost of
\mathcal{O}(N_{f}b^{2}+2KN_{f}b)
for
K
iterations.
With
K\sim 200
iterations and sparse factorization, this is substantially cheaper than forming
\mathbf{A}
explicitly, which would require
N_{\theta}
forward solves at total cost
\mathcal{O}(N_{\theta}N_{f}b)
.

For nonlinear problems where
\mathbf{K}(\bm{\theta})
depends on
\bm{\theta}
, the objective is no longer quadratic, but the same AD + L-BFGS framework applies.
The gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{J}
is computed via reverse-mode AD through the nonlinear forward solver, and L-BFGS iteratively converges to a local minimum.
The primary difference is that the Hessian is no longer constant, potentially requiring more iterations and restarts for non-convex objectives.

Appendix B
Gradient verification: Taylor test

The Taylor test provides a rigorous, implementation-independent verification that the computed gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{J}
is consistent with the objective function
\mathcal{J}(\bm{\theta})
.

Choose an arbitrary base point
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, a random perturbation direction
\delta\bm{\theta}
, and a sequence of decreasing step sizes
\epsilon_{k}
(e.g.,
\epsilon_{k}=2^{-k}
,
k=0,1,\dots
).
For each
\epsilon_{k}
, compute the perturbed objective:

\displaystyle r(\epsilon)\coloneqq\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\bm{\theta}_{0}+\epsilon\,\delta\bm{\theta})-\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\bm{\theta}_{0}).

(66)

By continuity of
\hat{\mathcal{J}}
, the Taylor expansion gives
r(\epsilon)=\epsilon\,\nabla\hat{\mathcal{J}}\cdot\delta\bm{\theta}+O(\epsilon^{2})
, so
|r(\epsilon)|=O(\epsilon)
as
\epsilon\to 0
; halving
\epsilon
should halve
|r(\epsilon)|
.
This zeroth-order test verifies only that the objective is smooth and does not test the gradient.

Subtracting the linear term predicted by the gradient yields the first-order remainder:

\displaystyle\left|r(\epsilon)-\epsilon\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\cdot\delta\bm{\theta}\right|=O(\epsilon^{2})\quad\text{as }\epsilon\to 0.

(67)

If the gradient is correctly implemented, halving
\epsilon
reduces this remainder by a factor of
4
(quadratic convergence); convergence at only
O(\epsilon)
indicates an error in the gradient implementation.

In practice, one plots
\log|r(\epsilon)|
and
\log|r(\epsilon)-\epsilon\,\nabla\hat{\mathcal{J}}\cdot\delta\bm{\theta}|
versus
\log\epsilon
.
The zeroth-order curve should have slope
\approx 1
and the first-order curve slope
\approx 2
.
Deviation from slope
2
at very small
\epsilon
is expected due to floating-point cancellation; the test is valid in the range
\epsilon\in[10^{-8},10^{-1}]
for 64-bit arithmetic.
