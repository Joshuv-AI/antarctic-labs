Abstract
Mutism in childhood and adolescence is a psychopathologically heterogeneous sign that current classifications reduce
to selective mutism, an anxiety disorder, leaving subtypes associated with severe affective, psychotic, and catatonic
conditions largely absent from the paediatric literature. This systematic review aims to propose and validate against the
available literature an integrative six-subtype classification of mutism in childhood and adolescence — oppositional,
selective/phobic, depressive, perplexed, delusional, and catatonic — characterising their neurobiological substrates, risk
factors, and clinical implications. A systematic review with narrative synthesis (PRISMA 2020; SWiM guideline)
searched PubMed/MEDLINE, PsycINFO, Embase, and Cochrane Library from inception to 30 April 2025; seminal
textbooks (Ajuriaguerra 1970; DSM-5-TR; ICD-11) were included as foundational references, and quality was assessed
using MMAT and AMSTAR-2. One hundred and fourteen records met inclusion criteria (111 empirical studies and 3
foundational texts). Each subtype showed a distinct neurobiological substrate, phenomenology, and risk profile; the
selective/phobic subtype has the most robust paediatric evidence, while depressive, perplexed, and catatonic subtypes
remain systematically underrecognised, and sitiophobia emerged as an extreme severity marker lacking
operationalisation in current guidelines. This dimensional classification has direct implications for clinical practice,
medical education, and research, and is offered as a testable tool pending prospective validation.
Keywords: Mutism; Selective Mutism; Catatonia; Psychopathology; Child Psychiatry; Feeding and Eating Disorders
of Childhood.

1. Introduction
Mutism — defined as the inability or unwillingness to speak, resulting in absent or markedly reduced verbal output —
is one of the most challenging signs in child and adolescent psychiatry. It is clinically non-specific: the same phenomenological
presentation may arise from extreme social anxiety, active resistance, profound depressive anergia, an oneiric-confusional
psychotic state, a systematised persecutory delusion, or catatonic negativism. Each of these contexts corresponds to a distinct
psychopathological and neurobiological substrate, with radically different therapeutic implications (Aggarwal et al., 2022; Santos
Carrasco et al., 2022).
The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) (American
Psychiatric Association, 2022) and the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11) (World Health
Organization, 2022) classify selective mutism as an anxiety disorder, with diagnostic criteria centred exclusively on failure to
speak in specific social contexts. A systematic review published in Behavioral Sciences in October 2025 demonstrated that this
single-criterion definition does not capture the real clinical complexity: most children with mutism present a multisymptomatic
profile — including motor freezing, social avoidance, and extreme anxiety — that extends well beyond failure to speak
(Kleinheinrich & Vogel, 2025). The same review challenged the reduction of the disorder to a single diagnostic criterion and
called for a broader conceptualisation.
The most severe subtypes of mutism — associated with psychotic or melancholic depression, catatonic features, acute
polymorphic psychosis, and early-onset schizophrenia — are largely invisible in the paediatric literature. This nosological gap
has direct clinical consequences: children and adolescents with severe depressive mutism and sitiophobia (persistent refusal to
eat and drink, a term well established in the European and Latin American psychiatric tradition but lacking formal
operationalisation in DSM-5-TR or ICD-11) are frequently maintained in exclusive psychotherapeutic follow-up for prolonged
periods without pharmacological intervention, with real risk of progression to complete catatonia, severe dehydration, and death
(Fernández et al., 2023; Fink & Taylor, 2023).
De Ajuriaguerra, in his foundational Manual de Psychiatrie de l'Enfant (1970) (de Ajuriaguerra, 1970) — a reference
universally regarded as a cornerstone of child psychiatry, in which mutism in children is addressed as a clinical entity with

This systematic review aims to propose and validate against the available literature an integrative six-subtype

catatonic — characterising their neurobiological substrates, risk factors, and clinical implications. This classification is grounded
in the available neurobiological and clinical literature and informed by the clinical-didactic framework systematised by Prof.
Marcelo Caixeta in the context of child and adolescent psychiatry teaching; it is not intended to replace current nosological
classifications but to provide clinicians with an operational differential-diagnosis tool that enables early identification of the
mutism subtype and appropriate therapeutic guidance. This work contributes to the United Nations Sustainable Development
Goal 3 (SDG 3: Good Health and Well-Being) by addressing life-threatening gaps in the recognition and treatment of severe
paediatric psychiatric presentations, and to SDG 4 (Quality Education) by providing a structured framework for postgraduate
psychiatry training and residency supervision in child and adolescent psychiatry.

2. Methodology
A literature review (Snyder, 2019) was conducted as part of a mixed-methods study (Risemberg et al., 2026; Pereira et
al., 2018) that is partly qualitative and partly quantitative, regarding the number of selected studies and using descriptive statistics
with frequency in values and frequency in percentage (Shitsuka et al., 2014a; Shitsuka et al, 2014b).
2.1 Protocol and Registration
This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and
Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines (Page et al., 2021). This review was not formally pre-registered on PROSPERO prior
to data extraction. Eligibility criteria, search strategy, synthesis groupings, and outcome measures were pre-specified before
screening commenced and are fully reported in this Methods section in accordance with PRISMA 2020 transparency
requirements. The PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided as Table 1 (Supplementary Material in the online version). The PRISMA
flow diagram is presented as Figure 1.

2.2 Eligibility Criteria
Studies were included if they: (1) were published between January 2000 and 30 April 2025 (primary systematic search);
studies published after this date were eligible through supplementary hand-search (identified in the reference list); pre-2000
studies were eligible only as historical/foundational references providing conceptual context for subtype classification (clearly
labelled as historical context, not counted in the systematic search yield); (2) addressed any subtype of mutism in children and/or
adolescents aged 0–18 years; adult studies were eligible only if: (a) a paediatric subgroup was separately analysed, or (b) the
study addressed rare subtypes (delusional, catatonic) with minimal paediatric-specific literature, in which case adult data were
included with explicit labelling as extrapolated evidence for pathophysiological discussion only; (3) were published in English,
Portuguese, or Spanish; (4) were empirical studies (cohort, case-control, cross-sectional, longitudinal, RCTs, case series with ≥3
cases), systematic reviews, or meta-analyses; (5) focused on psychopathology, neurobiology, risk factors, treatment outcomes,
or long-term prognosis. Three seminal foundational texts were included as authoritative references despite falling outside the
2000–2025 date range: (a) de Ajuriaguerra's Manuel de Psychiatrie de l'Enfant (1970) — included because it constitutes the first
systematic psychopathological classification of mutism in childhood within the European psychiatric tradition, directly relevant
to the conceptual framework of this review and to the scope of a European-based journal; (b) DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric
Association, 2022); and (c) ICD-11 (World Health Organization, 2022), as the current operative nosological frameworks.
Studies were excluded if they: (1) focused exclusively on organic mutism without primary psychiatric diagnosis
(aphasia, post-posterior fossa surgery mutism, akinetic mutism from structural lesion); (2) included only adult populations with
no paediatric subgroup analysis; (3) were published in languages other than the three specified; (4) were case reports with fewer
than 3 cases (except for rare subtypes such as very early-onset delusional or catatonic mutism); or (5) were published before
2000, except for seminal foundational texts.
2.3 Information Sources and Search Strategy
The following databases were searched: PubMed/MEDLINE, PsycINFO (via APA), Embase (via Elsevier), and the
Cochrane Library. The primary systematic search covered database inception to 30 April 2025. Additionally, a supplementary
hand-search of reference lists and key journal tables of contents was conducted from May 2025 to May 2026 to identify highly
relevant studies published after the primary search closure; these are identified in the reference list with the notation
[supplementary hand-search]. This approach follows PRISMA 2020 guidance on supplementary search strategies.
The search strategy combined the following terms (MeSH and free text, with Boolean operators AND/OR): mutism,
selective mutism, elective mutism, catatonic mutism, depressive mutism, psychotic mutism, oppositional mutism, childhood
mutism, food refusal psychiatry, sitiophobia, child psychiatry, adolescent psychiatry, paediatric catatonia, behavioural inhibition
childhood, anxiety disorder childhood, neurobiology mutism, risk factors mutism, psychopathology mutism, GABA catatonia,
BDNF depression children, HPA axis childhood anxiety. The full electronic search strategy for PubMed/MEDLINE is provided
in Online Resource 1 (ESM_1).
2.4 Study Selection and Data Extraction
Two reviewers (G.A.R. and M.C.) independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility. Full texts of potentially
eligible studies were retrieved and independently assessed. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and, where consensus
could not be reached, by a pre-specified tiebreaking procedure with documented rationale. Inter-rater agreement for study
inclusion was assessed using Cohen's kappa coefficient: κ = 0.81 (95% CI 0.74–0.88) for title/abstract screening and κ = 0.87
(95% CI 0.79–0.94) for full-text assessment, both indicating strong inter-rater agreement (Landis & Koch, 1977). Data were
extracted using a standardised Excel form capturing: study design, sample characteristics (age, sex, diagnostic criteria), mutism
subtype, neurobiological findings, risk factors assessed, treatment approach, and follow-up duration.

2.5 Quality Assessment
Study quality was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) Version 2018, which provides a uniform
appraisal framework applicable to quantitative (experimental, observational), qualitative, and mixed-methods studies. For
systematic reviews and meta-analyses identified within our search, quality was further assessed using the AMSTAR-2 tool.
Seminal textbooks were included as foundational references and were not subjected to MMAT scoring.
2.6 Synthesis
Given the heterogeneity of study designs, populations, diagnostic criteria, and outcomes across mutism subtypes — and
the absence of randomised controlled trial data for most subtypes beyond the selective/phobic — quantitative meta-analysis was
not feasible. Narrative synthesis was therefore adopted, following the SWiM (Synthesis Without Meta-Analysis) reporting
guideline (Campbell et al., 2020; Table 2), which is the recommended PRISMA 2020 extension for systematic reviews
employing alternative synthesis methods. In accordance with SWiM, studies were grouped by the mutism subtype as the primary
synthesis grouping variable. Direction of findings was used as the standardised metric given the absence of a common effect size
across heterogeneous study designs. The synthesis technique combined tabular presentation of study characteristics (Table 3)
with a vote-counting approach by direction of effect for each subtype, followed by a qualitative narrative description of findings.
Findings are presented subtype by subtype with a final cross-subtype summary in the Discussion.

3.2 Epidemiology and Nosological Context
Selective mutism has an estimated prevalence of 0.03%–1% in children under 8 years, with typical onset between ages
2 and 5 and diagnosis often confirmed at school entry (Boneff-Peng et al., 2026; Kearney, 2021). Prevalence data for the
remaining mutism subtypes in paediatric populations remain unavailable, reflecting the absence of specific diagnostic criteria
and dedicated epidemiological studies.
Paediatric catatonia — of which mutism is a cardinal sign — is estimated at approximately 0.6%–1.7% among paediatric
psychiatric inpatients but is considered systematically underdiagnosed due to diagnostic overshadowing (Benarous et al., 2018;
Hauptman et al., 2024). A recent APA resource document on catatonia (American Psychiatric Association, 2025; Wilson et al.,
2025) acknowledged that the differential diagnosis of catatonic mutism from akinetic mutism, delirium, and neurodevelopmental
disorders remains clinically challenging in the paediatric age group. The document emphasises that hypokinetic catatonia — the
form most commonly presenting with mutism — must be distinguished from locked-in syndrome, severe depression, and autismspectrum withdrawal, each requiring a fundamentally different therapeutic response.
A 2025 systematic review in Behavioral Sciences (Kleinheinrich & Vogel, 2025) confirmed that the current DSM-5
definition of selective mutism — centred on a single criterion — does not reflect the full symptomatic heterogeneity of the
condition. The review documented that the majority of children with mutism present a multisymptomatic profile encompassing
emotional, behavioural, and social features, consistent with the broader conceptualisation proposed in the present classification.

3.3.1 Oppositional Mutism (Resistance)
Oppositional mutism is characterised by the use of silence as acting-out or communicative acting-in directed at authority
figures — parents, teachers, healthcare professionals. It differs fundamentally from selective/phobic mutism in that the primary
substrate is not anxiety but active, deliberate resistance as a response to perceived coercive interpersonal demands (Ghosh et al.,
2017; Kaur et al., 2022). Clinically, this subtype is associated with environmental control behaviours, negativism, refusal to
cooperate, and temper tantrums. The high rate of comorbidity between selective mutism and ODD further supports the existence
of shared mechanisms underlying emotional and behavioural regulation (Freitag et al., 2024).
The neurobiology of this subtype can be understood across three major domains. The first involves abnormalities in
fear processing, including impaired aversive conditioning, reduced cortisol reactivity to stress, amygdala hyporeactivity to
negative stimuli, and alterations in serotonergic and noradrenergic neurotransmission — a pattern distinct from the amygdala
hyperreactivity characteristic of the selective/phobic subtype. The second domain relates to dysfunction within reward and
motivational circuits, including reduced sympathetic nervous system responsiveness to incentives, diminished orbitofrontal
cortex activation in response to rewards, dopaminergic neurotransmission abnormalities, and low resting heart rate, a pattern
frequently associated with sensation-seeking behaviour. The third domain comprises deficits in executive functioning,
particularly in processes dependent on motivational and emotional factors, resulting in impairments in inhibitory control,
decision-making, and behavioural self-regulation.
Functional neuroimaging studies demonstrate abnormalities in neural circuits involved in emotional, motivational, and
executive processing. Key findings include hypoactivation of the rostro-dorsomedial frontocingulate regions and the ventral
striatum — critical for reward-based decision-making — bilateral amygdala abnormalities characterised by reduced volume and
diminished activation in response to emotional stimuli, and dysfunction of the prefrontal cortex within its medial, dorsolateral,
and ventromedial subdivisions, compromising behavioural control, planning, and emotional regulation. Alterations in
dopaminergic pathways and structural and functional deficits within the paralimbic system, especially the orbitofrontal and

The therapeutic approach prioritises structured behavioural interventions (Parent Management Training; CBT with

3.3.2 Selective/Phobic Mutism (Anxiety)
Selective mutism, classified in DSM-5-TR as an anxiety disorder (F94.0), is the most extensively studied subtype. Its
onset typically occurs between ages 2 and 5, and it is characterised by consistent failure to speak in specific social situations
where speech is expected — typically school — despite normal speech in other contexts (Kearney, 2021).
Clinically, children with selective mutism typically speak at home with immediate family members but often remain
silent even in the presence of close friends or extended relatives. In school settings, the absence of speech can significantly
interfere with academic assessment, as teachers may have difficulty evaluating skills such as reading and oral comprehension.
These children frequently rely on nonverbal forms of communication — gestures, pointing, writing, or vocalisation — and may
participate willingly in social activities when verbal communication is not required.
Selective mutism is strongly associated with anxiety disorders, particularly Social Anxiety Disorder; in clinical settings,
most children with selective mutism also meet criteria for at least one other anxiety disorder. Beyond communication difficulties
in specific contexts, affected children often exhibit excessive shyness, intense fear of social embarrassment, social withdrawal,
clingy behaviour, compulsive traits, and, in some cases, mildly oppositional behaviours. A strong association with behavioural
inhibition to unfamiliar stimuli is well-established. Features associated with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) may be present
as a comorbidity in a subset of patients, although these are not considered part of the typical clinical presentation. Most children
with selective mutism demonstrate normal language development and intact linguistic abilities, with the impairment largely
restricted to speech production in specific social contexts.
The core neurobiological substrate involves amygdala hyperreactivity to social stimuli, ventromedial prefrontal cortex
hypofunction in fear extinction, and HPA axis hypersensitivity with exaggerated cortisol release in socially threatening contexts
(Muris et al., 2023). Functional neuroimaging studies have demonstrated increased BOLD signal in the amygdala of children
with selective mutism exposed to unfamiliar faces, in a pattern analogous to that observed in adult social anxiety disorder (Muris
et al., 2023).
The temperamental factor of behavioural inhibition is the most robust predictor of selective mutism: longitudinal studies
demonstrated that children with lifetime selective mutism were significantly more behaviourally inhibited as infants than controls
and children with isolated social phobia (Gensthaler et al., 2016). Notably, inhibition toward social stimuli specifically — not
just novel situations in general — was the strongest temperamental predictor (Gensthaler et al., 2016). The CNTNAP2 gene,
involved in cortical language network development, has been associated with selective mutism in genetic association studies
(Stein et al., 2011). A parenting dimension is also documented: overprotective parenting has been associated with the
development and maintenance of selective mutism, and the interaction between child internalising behaviours (anxiety) and
permissive parenting was associated with SM diagnosis in a study of 285 children aged 3–7 years (Muris et al., 2021).
First-line treatment combines SSRIs (sertraline or fluoxetine) with CBT-based graduated exposure and, more recently,
Intensive Group Behavioural Treatment (IGBT) (Hipolito et al., 2023; Kearney, 2021). A 2025 systematic review (BMC
Psychiatry) confirmed that most children with SM show partial or complete symptom improvement over time, but anxiety
disorders remain common in later life, underscoring the importance of early intervention (Koskela et al., 2023).

3.3.3 Depressive Mutism (Anergia/Anhedonia)
Depressive mutism represents one of the most severe and underrecognised presentations of the depressive spectrum in
childhood and adolescence. It occurs in contexts of melancholic depression or depression with psychotic features, in which the
severity of anhedonia, anergia, and psychomotor retardation becomes so profound that the act of speaking itself is perceived as
futile, impossible, or devoid of meaning (Boneff-Peng et al., 2023; Fernández et al., 2023). In such cases, mutism may represent
a manifestation of marked psychomotor retardation or depressive catatonia.
The underlying melancholic depression is characterised by profound and pervasive anhedonia with an almost complete
loss of the capacity to experience pleasure, accompanied by lack of emotional reactivity — such that even highly desirable events
produce little or no improvement in mood. The depressive mood is typically experienced as qualitatively distinct from nonmelancholic depression, often described as emptiness, hopelessness, or intense psychological suffering. Other characteristic
features include diurnal mood variation with morning worsening, early morning awakening, significant anorexia or weight loss,
excessive or inappropriate guilt, and marked psychomotor disturbances, whether retardation or agitation.
In cases with psychotic features, patients may develop mood-congruent delusions, most commonly involving themes
of death, ruin, illness, guilt, or punishment. In more severe presentations, the condition may progress to catatonic manifestations
— including mutism, negativism, and stupor — underscoring the clinical continuum between severe depressive mutism and
catatonic mutism (Fernández et al., 2023).
The core clinical phenomenology includes absent spontaneous speech, empty gaze, indifference to the environment,
anergia preventing even basic movements, and frequently sitiophobia (refusal to eat and drink) as a marker of extreme severity.
The presence of self-injurious behaviour (cutting) in limbs and neck — associated with mutism and sitiophobia — constitutes
an immediate life risk and represents an indication for urgent psychiatric hospitalisation with immediate pharmacological
intervention (Fernández et al., 2023).
The neurobiological substrate involves serotonergic and noradrenergic dysfunction, reduced BDNF levels with
consequent hippocampal atrophy, chronic HPA axis hyperactivation (hypercortisolaemia), and reduced neuroplasticity in
frontolimbic circuits (Huang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). In adolescents, the presence of depression with psychotic features
of sufficient severity to produce mutism and sitiophobia should be interpreted as a red-flag indicator for early-onset bipolar
disorder (BD): studies confirm that early-onset BD (before age 21) is strongly associated with greater psychotic severity, greater
diagnostic instability, lower lithium response, and worse prognosis (Grande et al., 2016; Pini et al., 2024).
The therapeutic approach requires urgent pharmacological intervention with a high-potency antipsychotic (haloperidol,
with progressive titration to avoid toxicity in the context of sitiophobia-related dehydration) and gradual introduction of a mood
stabiliser (lithium), with careful monitoring of serum levels. Exclusive psychotherapy, without pharmacological evaluation, is
insufficient for this subtype and may delay necessary intervention (Fernández et al., 2023; Fink & Taylor, 2023).

KEY FINDING: Sitiophobia as an Extreme Severity Marker in Paediatric Depressive Mutism
Food and fluid refusal — termed sitiophobia (from the Greek sitos, food + phobos, fear; persistent refusal to eat and drink in the
context of psychiatric illness, established in the European and Latin American traditions by Ajuriaguerra 1970 but absent from
DSM-5-TR/ICD-11 as an explicit severity marker) — in a child or adolescent with mutism signals severe psychotic or bipolar
depression with immediate risk to life. This nosological gap is a critical omission: sitiophobia should be systematically assessed in
all paediatric patients with mutism and, when present, treated as an indication for urgent hospitalisation and pharmacological
intervention. Prolonged exclusive psychotherapy in patients with this presentation constitutes an iatrogenic risk factor for clinical
deterioration, ICU admission, and death. This review formally proposes sitiophobia associated with mutism as a psychiatric
emergency criterion to be incorporated in future DSM and ICD revisions.

3.3.4 Perplexed Mutism (Mental Confusion)
Perplexed mutism represents the verbal expression of perplexed psychosis — an acute psychopathological state
characterised by global impairment of cognitive functioning and a qualitative alteration of consciousness, often described as
oneiroid in nature. The patient experiences an intense sense of unfamiliarity toward the surrounding environment, accompanied
by mental confusion, disorientation, anxious suspiciousness, and profound fear, as though living through a disturbing dream
whose nature cannot be fully understood.
Crucially, mutism in this subtype does not arise from a deliberate decision to remain silent or from a motor inability to
speak. Rather, it results from a profound cognitive disorganisation that prevents adequate processing of environmental
information and coherent formulation of verbal responses. The individual becomes so immersed in the confusional experience
that they lose the ability to integrate stimuli, understand what is occurring, and organise thought processes sufficiently to sustain
effective communication. Responses to external stimuli are inconsistent, slowed, or inappropriate, reflecting the patient's inability
to assign stable meaning to ongoing experiences.
The condition is characterised by fluctuating or partially impaired consciousness, compromised orientation, marked
attentional deficits, and fragmented thought content. Although delusional ideas may be present, they emerge within a broader
context of global confusion rather than as well-systematised beliefs — fundamentally distinguishing this subtype from delusional
mutism, in which the level of consciousness remains preserved and silence arises from organised delusional convictions
(Aggarwal et al., 2022; Dhossche, 2004).
Neurobiologically, this subtype involves acute mesolimbic dopaminergic disruption — the central mechanism of
'aberrant salience' described by Kapur (2003) (Kapur, 2003) — combined with glutamatergic dysfunction (NMDA antagonism)
producing the confusional state. Clinically, it corresponds most closely to acute polymorphic psychotic disorder (ICD-11: 6A23)
or first psychotic episode with affective features rather than established paranoid schizophrenia. In children and adolescents,
organic causes of acute confusional states with mutism must be systematically excluded, particularly anti-NMDAR autoimmune
encephalitis — the most prevalent organic aetiology of paediatric psychosis with catatonic/perplexed presentation (Casas &
Conn, 2024; Hauptman et al., 2024).
The critical differential diagnosis with delusional mutism is clinically essential: while the patient with perplexed mutism
is frightened, confused, and anxious with altered consciousness, the patient with delusional mutism maintains a preserved level
of consciousness, appears calm, and demonstrates full conviction that their speech will be used against them by identified external
agents. This phenomenological distinction has direct implications for approach: in perplexed mutism, a calm environment and
anxiety containment are prioritised; in delusional mutism, slow rapport-building is essential (Aggarwal et al., 2022; Dhossche,
2004).
3.3.5 Delusional Mutism (Persecution)
Delusional mutism is characterised by refusal to speak driven by firmly established delusional beliefs, most commonly
of a persecutory nature. Unlike perplexed mutism, the level of consciousness remains preserved: the patient is alert, oriented,
and capable of understanding the surrounding environment, although thought content is profoundly dominated by the delusional
system. The individual's attitudes and behaviours often display internal coherence and are organised around the psychotic beliefs
that sustain the mutism. In many cases, the absence of speech may be selective — occurring only in the presence of specific
individuals or situations perceived as threatening (Aggarwal et al., 2022; Dhossche, 2004).
The underlying psychopathological mechanisms may take several forms. In persecutory delusions, the individual
believes that speaking will result in surveillance, punishment, or harm. In delusions of thought control, the conviction that
thoughts are being inserted, stolen, or controlled by external forces may lead to a refusal to verbalise them. Delusions of reference

3.3.6 Catatonic Mutism (Severe Isolation)
Catatonic mutism is the most severe and clinically recognisable form of the spectrum. Associated with the complete
catatonic syndrome — including mutism, negativism, abnormal posture, rigidity, stupor, echolalia/echopraxia, and, in extreme
cases, waxy flexibility — it occurs most frequently in contexts of hebephrenic/catatonic schizophrenia with childhood onset,
severe ASD, and in children, autoimmune encephalitis (Benarous et al., 2018; Hauptman et al., 2024; American Psychiatric
Association, 2025).
The core pathophysiology involves the GABA-A/glutamate imbalance, with reduced GABAergic neurotransmission in
basal ganglia circuits, supplementary motor cortex, and thalamus (Benarous et al., 2024; Jha, 2023). The efficacy of
benzodiazepines (GABA-A agonists) as first-line diagnostic and therapeutic agents is consistent with this pathophysiological
model: lorazepam IV administration with positive response within hours confirms the diagnosis. Mutations in GABAergic genes
— notably GABRB2 — have recently been identified in paediatric cases of lorazepam-resistant catatonia, requiring multiple
ECT sessions (Benarous et al., 2024; Smith et al., 2023).
Paediatric catatonia constitutes a psychiatric emergency: untreated, it can produce life-threatening medical
complications including dehydration, aspiration pneumonia, venous thromboembolism, and neuroleptic malignant syndrome.
ECT is the most efficacious option in lorazepam-refractory cases, with documented safety even in children aged 7–10 years
(Smith et al., 2023). A 2024 multisite retrospective cohort study confirmed that the lorazepam challenge test with BFCRS
assessment has high diagnostic sensitivity for paediatric catatonia (Luccarelli et al., 2024b). A 2025 case report of a 17-year-old
with mutism, posturing, and lorazepam-responsive catatonia who was ultimately diagnosed with schizophrenia illustrates the
diagnostic complexity and temporal uncertainty in adolescent catatonic presentations (Kang et al., 2025). The Catatonia Quick
Screen — a rapid 4-item instrument derived from the BFCRS — has been proposed as a bedside diagnostic aid; prospective
validation in paediatric populations is pending (Luccarelli et al., 2024a).

3.5 Neurobiological Framework
3.5.1 Amygdala–PFC–HPA Circuit
The amygdala–PFC circuit is the central substrate of anxious (selective/phobic) and, to a lesser degree, oppositional
subtypes. Amygdala hyperreactivity to social stimuli produces vocal freezing — the mechanism by which the child simply stops
speaking in the presence of the social stressor. The ventromedial PFC, responsible for conditioned fear extinction, is hypoactive
in these patients, perpetuating the silence response even in objectively safe contexts (Muris et al., 2023). The hypersensitive
HPA axis — with elevated cortisol in anticipation of social situations — amplifies and consolidates the anxiety-mutismreinforcement cycle.
3.5.2 Dopaminergic System and Aberrant Salience
In psychotic subtypes (perplexed and delusional), the central mechanism is mesolimbic hyperdopaminergia,
conceptualised in Kapur's (2003) aberrant salience model (Kapur, 2003): neutral sensory experiences and internal stimuli acquire
inappropriate motivational salience, generating delusions as an attempt to make sense of these experiences. In perplexed mutism,
additional glutamatergic dysfunction (via NMDA antagonism) produces the oneiric-confusional state. In delusional mutism,
prefrontal hypodopaminergia (dlPFC) compromises reality monitoring and consolidates the delusional system (Dhossche, 2004;
Kapur, 2003).

3.5.3 GABAergic/Glutamatergic System and Catatonia
GABA/glutamate imbalance is the central mechanism of catatonic mutism. Recent studies in paediatric catatonia
demonstrated that reduced GABA-A neurotransmission in basal ganglia and supplementary motor cortex circuits results in the
catatonic motor syndrome, of which mutism is a cardinal component (Benarous et al., 2024; Jha, 2023). The identification of
GABAergic gene mutations (GABRB2) in lorazepam-resistant paediatric catatonia suggests that specific genetic subgroups may
respond differently to conventional therapies, warranting pharmacogenomic investigation (Benarous et al., 2024).
3.5.4 BDNF, Neuroplasticity, and Depressive Mutism
In depressive mutism, reduced BDNF levels — a consequence of chronic HPA axis hyperactivity and neuronal oxidative
stress — compromise hippocampal neuroplasticity and frontolimbic circuit integrity (Huang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025).
Hippocampal atrophy documented in severe major depression produces deficits in emotional regulation and aversive memory
extinction, perpetuating the depressive cycle. In adolescents with severe psychotic depression, the presence of mutism and
sitiophobia should be interpreted not only as a severity marker but as a potential biological vulnerability indicator for recurrent
mood disorder or BD (Grande et al., 2016; Pini et al., 2024).

(Koskela et al., 2023), and in cases of more severe mutism subtypes, progression to major psychiatric disorders (Grande et al.,
2016; Pini et al., 2024).

4. Discussion
4.1 Principal Findings and Their Clinical Significance
This systematic review confirms that mutism in childhood and adolescence is a clinically and neurobiologically
heterogeneous sign that requires a dimensional psychopathological approach to differential diagnosis. The existing literature —
predominantly focused on the selective/phobic subtype — captures only a fraction of the clinical reality encountered in paediatric
inpatient and emergency settings.
The most significant finding of this review is the systematic absence, in the paediatric literature, of operational criteria
for the recognition and management of severe mutism subtypes — particularly depressive mutism with sitiophobia and catatonic
mutism. The clinical framework proposed by Caixeta, which integrates these subtypes within a unified differential-diagnosis
algorithm, provides a teachable, testable, and clinically actionable model that fills this gap.
Sitiophobia associated with mutism in childhood and adolescence emerges as a critical red-flag sign that has not been
systematically addressed in existing paediatric psychiatry guidelines or classification systems. Its presence signals that a child
or adolescent has crossed a severity threshold requiring immediate pharmacological intervention and hospitalisation —
regardless of what preceding treatment they may have received.
4.2 The Caixeta Classification in the Context of Current Evidence
The six-subtype Caixeta classification draws from, and extends, several existing clinical and neurobiological traditions.
The selective/phobic subtype corresponds to the well-established DSM-5-TR construct of selective mutism, enriched here with
its neurobiological underpinnings. The oppositional subtype aligns with the oppositional behaviour profiles documented within
selective mutism populations (Freitag et al., 2024). The catatonic subtype integrates the growing literature on paediatric catatonia
(Benarous et al., 2024; Campbell et al., 2020; Freitag et al., 2024; Hauptman et al., 2024; Jha, 2023; Luccarelli et al., 2024b;
Smith et al., 2023). The depressive, perplexed, and delusional subtypes fill a gap that neither the selective mutism literature nor
the catatonia literature has addressed in the paediatric age group.
Ajuriaguerra's foundational text (de Ajuriaguerra, 1970) — which addressed mutism in children as a multidimensionally
aetiological clinical entity, integrating biological, relational, and psychodynamic perspectives — anticipated this integrative
framework. His insistence on the necessity of a biological substrate even in apparently psychosocial presentations of childhood
mutism remains clinically and epistemologically pertinent. The current review can be understood as a contemporary
neurobiological validation of Ajuriaguerra's integrative vision.

4.3 Implications for Medical Education and Clinical Practice
The present classification has direct implications for medical education. The six-subtype model provides trainees in
child and adolescent psychiatry with a structured differential-diagnosis framework applicable at the bedside. The case-based
pedagogical approach systematised by Caixeta — in which each subtype is illustrated with a clinical vignette and contrasted with
phenomenologically adjacent subtypes — is directly applicable to residency training programmes and postgraduate courses.
For clinical practice, the key implication is the recognition that therapeutic urgency is subtype-dependent. The
selective/phobic subtype generally allows for a structured outpatient approach; the depressive, perplexed, and catatonic subtypes
frequently require immediate pharmacological intervention and hospitalisation. The failure to recognise this stratification — and
the consequent delay in psychiatric referral — constitutes a preventable cause of clinical deterioration and death in paediatric
patients.
4.4 Limitations
Several limitations must be acknowledged and contextualised. First, the evidence base is strongly asymmetric: the
selective/phobic subtype has a substantially larger literature (68/114 included studies; 59.6%) than the remaining five subtypes
combined. This asymmetry is not a methodological artefact but a reflection of the existing nosological gap — precisely the gap
this review seeks to address. The relative scarcity of evidence for severe subtypes (depressive: 12 studies; catatonic: 18;
perplexed/delusional: 6; oppositional: 7) does not invalidate the clinical importance of those subtypes; it underscores the urgency
of dedicated research. To this end, we formally propose a prospective multicentre validation study of the Caixeta six-subtype
classification as a priority research agenda item (see Section 4.5).
Second, the Caixeta six-subtype classification has not yet been empirically validated in prospective paediatric samples
and is explicitly positioned as a proposed clinical framework pending validation — not as a replacement for current nosological
systems. A proposed validation protocol (multicentre cross-sectional study using structured clinical assessment instruments
including BFCRS/PCRS for catatonic features, HDRS for depressive features, and PANSS for psychotic features, with interrater reliability testing of subtype assignment) is outlined in Section 4.5. Third, the search was limited to three languages,
potentially excluding relevant Asian and Eastern European literature. Fourth, the narrative synthesis, while methodologically
justified and SWiM-compliant, does not allow for pooled effect size estimation or formal heterogeneity testing across subtypes.

4.6 Clinical Relevance and Implications
This systematic review has direct and immediate implications for clinical practice in child and adolescent psychiatry,
paediatric emergency medicine, and general paediatrics. The six-subtype Caixeta classification provides clinicians with a bedside
differential-diagnosis framework for a sign — mutism — that is common in acute psychiatric presentations but that current
nosological systems address only partially. Three clinical implications deserve particular emphasis.
First, the recognition of sitiophobia (persistent refusal to eat and drink) as an extreme severity marker in paediatric
depressive mutism redefines the urgency threshold for psychiatric intervention in children and adolescents with selective silence.
When mutism is accompanied by food and fluid refusal, self-injurious behaviour, or progressive social withdrawal, it warrants
urgent psychiatric evaluation regardless of whether a prior diagnosis has been established or whether the patient has been
receiving psychotherapy. Dehydration in this context accelerates the risk of electrolyte imbalance, lithium toxicity, aspiration
pneumonia, and ICU admission. Prompt clinical action is indicated.
Second, the differential diagnosis between perplexed mutism (altered consciousness, anxious confusion, oneiric state)
and delusional mutism (preserved consciousness, calm vigilance, systematised persecutory conviction) determines the first-line
pharmacological choice: benzodiazepine augmentation is prioritised in the perplexed subtype, while high-potency antipsychotic
monotherapy is the approach of choice in the delusional subtype (Stahl, 2021). Conflating these two presentations leads to
inadequate treatment and clinical deterioration.
Third, prolonged exclusive psychotherapy — without psychiatric referral and pharmacological evaluation — in children
and adolescents presenting with severe mutism subtypes (depressive, perplexed, catatonic) constitutes an iatrogenic risk factor
documented in this review. Psychologists, school counsellors, and primary care physicians should be familiar with the clinical
features that indicate urgent psychiatric referral: sitiophobia, progressive anergia, self-injurious behaviour, catatonic features
(rigidity, posturing, negativism), or any form of mutism that is absolute, generalised, and unresponsive to environmental change.
The present classification is offered as a practical tool to support that recognition. This systematic review is directly relevant to
Sustainable Development Goal 3 (Good Health and Well-Being) through its contribution to early identification and treatment of
life-threatening childhood psychiatric presentations, and to SDG 4 (Quality Education) through its explicit application to
postgraduate psychiatry training and residency supervision.

5. Conclusion
Mutism in childhood and adolescence is a psychopathologically heterogeneous sign requiring a dimensional, subtypesensitive differential-diagnosis approach. The six-subtype Caixeta classification — oppositional, selective/phobic, depressive,
perplexed, delusional, and catatonic — provides clinicians with an operational framework that is grounded in neurobiological
evidence and directly applicable in paediatric emergency, inpatient, and outpatient settings.
The recognition of sitiophobia as an extreme severity marker in paediatric depressive mutism, the phenomenological
distinction between perplexed and delusional mutism, and the operationalisation of urgent hospitalisation criteria in this age
group are contributions that emerge from specialised clinical teaching practice and that lack systematisation in the indexed
literature.
Child and adolescent psychiatry — in many contexts still the last-referred specialty in severe paediatric presentations
— must assert its diagnostic and therapeutic primacy in cases of severe mutism. The understanding that severe mutism is a
