1. BACKGROUND
With the issuance of NSS-13 1, INFCIRC/225, Rev.5, in 2011, the international guidance for nuclear
security identified the need for each State to establish two thresholds related to radiological
sabotage of nuclear facilities: unacceptable radiological consequences (URC) and high radiological
consequences (HRC). NSS-13 recommends that URC be established to allow for a graded protection
approach against sabotage events resulting in radiological consequences NSS-13 recommends that a
design basis threat (DBT) for radiological sabotage be established for facilities where it is possible
that an act of sabotage could result in a radiological release that exceeds the threshold for HRC 2.
Furthermore, NSS-16 details further use of HRC (and URC) thresholds in the Vital Area Identification
process, providing additional implications for the thresholds of URC & HRC serving as important
cornerstones of physical security at a nuclear facility. It should be noted that the IAEA is in the
process of merging NSS-16 with NSS-4 (Engineering Safety Aspects of the Protection of Nuclear
Power Plants Against Sabotage).
NSS-13 provided no specific thresholds for URC and HRC, as each State needs to establish its own
thresholds in consideration of their tolerance for risk. Prior to the issuance of this revision of
INFCIRC/225 as NSS-13, States with well-developed regulatory programs had generally already
identified when requirements for physical protection were needed and when a DBT should apply. As
such, most of these States had not established URC and HRC thresholds. NSS-27G 3 provides
additional information beyond that provided in NSS-13 as to factors to consider related to
radiological sabotage. However, no international guidance has been issued on how a State might
establish such thresholds. This poses a challenge for States with less developed regulatory programs
or those that are planning to introduce large research reactors or advanced reactors/small modular
reactors (ASMRs) into their country. States that are considering the introduction of such facilities will
be challenged to develop these thresholds or to establish proxies in lieu of them.
While some States may have previously defined levels that correspond to URC and HRC, it appears
that most States around the world have not taken this step. Rather, some States have relied on
proxies, such as qualitative design limits like significant core damage, to define the threshold for
requiring physical protection against a DBT. However, with the anticipated deployment of ASMRs,
this approach may be inadequate. Because many of these advanced reactor designs, such as pebble
bed reactors, do not operate with a traditional core design, the threshold established in some
regulatory programs may not be applicable. In addition, due to ASMRs’ proposed sizes and passive
safety features, a malevolent act may not be capable of producing a release that would exceed HRC
or even URC. Furthermore, in some cases, there may be a different approach for the on-site storage

2. OBJECTIVE
Given the importance of the URC and HRC thresholds as part of a physical security program that is
designed to protect against radiological sabotage, it is important to provide guidance on how to
assign those thresholds consistent with a State’s level of risk tolerance. Without such guidance, most
States will be challenged to establish appropriate thresholds or appropriately establish regulations.
This document provides guidance on establishing dose limits and where they apply, as well as the
timeframe for which the dose limits should be measured. For dose limits, it offers examples of
established thresholds based on safety and emergency response thresholds. Whereas, for
timeframes, a process to identify possible timeframes is offered.

 7

3. DISCUSSION

3.1. ESTABLISHING DOSE LIMITS

There are three approaches that can be taken to establish dose thresholds for URC and HRC: defined
based on a technical analysis and risk tolerance of the State, using proxies defined by plant
conditions, or using existing safety or emergency response thresholds.
Defining thresholds based on risk tolerance and performing a technical analysis would require a
careful assessment of the State’s risk tolerance and an assessment of what would be required to
exceed the State’s tolerance for risk. Once the thresholds are determined, the State would need to
document why the levels are appropriate and discuss why any differences in existing dose thresholds
make sense. Security thresholds differing from safety or emergency response thresholds will be
challenged and will likely be more difficult to implement.
Establishing proxies for the thresholds using plant conditions has been done in the past. The most
common one is significant core damage as a proxy for HRC. This has been most commonly used with
large reactors. Many of the ASMR designs do not have traditional cores, such as pebble bed reactors.
As such, the use of this proxy may not be appropriate for new reactors being deployed.
The most common approach is to use existing safety or emergency response thresholds for these
security thresholds. All the States that participated in the technical exchanges organized by the
National Nuclear Security Administration’s Office of International Security (INS) in 2024 took
advantage of these thresholds for HRC and most used them for URC as well. In most cases, URC
thresholds were established based on shelter-in-place emergency response thresholds or a safety
threshold. HRC thresholds tended to align with emergency evacuation thresholds or a safety
threshold. The importance of having these security-based values correlate or align with either
existing State regulations for safety or emergency events or international guidance was additionally
noted by many participating agencies. Such alignment provides the public with consistent messaging
and eases the burden for revision and acceptance by correlating the value with an already accepted
value from another arena of nuclear regulation within the State. Specific examples of dose
thresholds are provided in Appendix 1.
Since the establishment of dose thresholds has generally been through the use of existing safety or
emergency response regulations or guidance, a methodology is not provided for the selection of dose
thresholds.

 8

3.2. BOUNDARY CONSIDERATIONS
The applicable boundary (distance from nuclear facility) for the URC and HRC limits is also an
important topic to address. The most common boundary identified in URC/HRC determinations is the
site boundary. This tends to focus protection on people that may be in locations that are not under
control of the operator. An alternate approach is the “nearest residential dwelling” approach
(consistent with the French approach) which favors the installation of nuclear sites in areas far from
populations. It is less conservative than the site boundary, as residential dwellings tend to be some
distance from the site boundary, resulting in lower doses at this distance. A facility that would exceed
the HRC if it were in a high-density area (like an urban location) might not even exceed the URC limit
if it were located a long distance from a population center. In practice, however, operators cannot
always control the urban planning around them. As such, the potential for proximity of dwellings can
become a risk for them. If dwellings were ever to be built within the URC or HRC threshold radii of
certain facilities, the operator may need to enhance the level of protection for the targets concerned.
Retrofitting security measures can be an unexpected and expensive cost if required. Consideration of
the future surroundings for those sites with regulations that extend beyond the facility boundary
should be considered by licensees.
The State will need to decide which boundary location the dose limit will be measured at. The site
boundary is more conservative but tends to be unlikely to change. The residential dwelling boundary
is less conservative and promotes siting of NPPs in less developed areas. However, if residential
dwellings encroach on the NPP, additional security measures may need to be added to the facility.

3.3. ESTABLISHING TIME PERIOD
The time period that should be established to measure the dose from a sabotage event should cover
the period for which a significant dose is released. For most NPPs, there are two sources for potential
radiological release: the reactor and the spent fuel stored on site. For some ASMR designs, other
sources may be present, such as activated coolants. Generally, each of these sources should be
evaluated separately.
The examples in Appendix 1 provide a wide range of time periods for which dose should be assessed.
Most of these were developed when only large reactors were operating. They range from 2 hours to
365 days. As such, it would be difficult to select a time period from examples developed by States to
date. Each State will need to perform a technical evaluation, which will likely include modelling of
radiation release and dispersion to identify the appropriate time period to associate with its URC and
HRC levels.
First, consider the reactor. For large reactors that are currently operating, most of the radiation that
is released is released in the first 24 hours. This is not the case for ASMRs. Smaller portions of the
radiation would be released during the first 24 hours and the overall release tends to extend for a
longer period of time. There is also a significant variability of dose releases over time for different
ASMR designs. It is important to note that the amount of radiation that can be released by large
reactors tends to be orders of magnitude larger than for ASMRs.
This conclusion was drawn from the analysis of two severe accident scenarios (large break loss of
cooling accident and long-term station blackout) for light water reactors, as well as severe accident
scenarios for a heat pipe reactor and a pebble bed modular reactor.

 9

In addition to the reactor, spent fuel stored on site should be considered. The potential radiation
release from spent fuel storage should be assessed to understand how radiation would be released
over time, along with dose levels at the prescribed distances. In the past, there was a limited variety
of fuels in reactors. The major types were dependent on reactor designs, predominantly: boiling
water reactors, pressurized water reactors, and CANDU reactors. With the potential deployment of
ASMRs, the fuel types will be more varied. The spent fuel will likely have a broader range of
characteristics.
Finally, any additional sources that could contribute to radiological release from sabotage should be
assessed to determine the amount released over time and its contribution to dose levels at prescribed
distances.

4. RECOMMENDATIONS

4.1. DOSE LEVEL
The dose level is an indication of the State’s risk tolerance for radiation exposure. As such, specific
threshold values would be appropriate for URC and HRC. Given the number of dose levels found in
safety and emergency response regulations and guidance, it is generally best to tie URC and HRC
levels to existing thresholds. As such, the State should identify the appropriate linkages to safety or
emergency response thresholds and incorporate them into the regulations for security.

4.2. BOUNDARY SELECTION
In most cases, States have selected the site boundary as the point where the dose will be measured
against the threshold. This establishes a stable requirement for the operator to meet. In some cases,
the nearest residential dwelling might be a reasonable choice, especially in remote areas where new
residences are unlikely to be built near the reactor site. This would allow much larger releases to be
deemed less of a concern due to their lower impact on the population.

4.3. TIME PERIOD
The time period associated with URC/HRC limits should be an appropriate length to cover the time
when a significant release of radiation occurs. As was pointed out earlier, the time period for the
release is fairly consistent for currently operating large nuclear power plants. However, there is
significant variability amongst the variety of ASMRs that may be deployed.
Given the high degree of variability introduced by ASMRs related to radiation release over time from
accidents or acts of sabotage, it may be appropriate to establish timeframes for URC and HRC in the
licensing process, rather than as an element of regulations.
As such, it is important to model the radiation dispersion over time that would result from a severe
accident or act of sabotage for the reactor being licensed. This can be accomplished using software
modeling codes like MELCOR and MACCS.
Similar analyses should be performed for spent fuel stored onsite. Typically, spent fuel at operating
LWRs and CANDU reactors is stored in spent fuel pools or dry casks. The dispersion of the
radioactivity over time is generally understood and consistent across reactors. The differences seen
will likely be associated with the amount of time that the fuel has been out of the reactor, allowing
for fission product decay.
However, there is a wide variety of fuel forms for ASMRs, as well as storage approaches. Some
ASMRs may use traditional spent fuel pools and dry cask storage. However, Triso pebbles will be
stored in dry casks as soon as they are removed from the reactor in PBMRs. Also, the physical form of
the fuel varies amongst the designs, to include metal, ceramic, Triso, and liquid solutions.
The radiation dispersion over time of spent fuel that results from an act of sabotage will need to be
modeled. Factors that will need to be considered in the dispersion modeling include the source term,
physical form of the fuel, method of sabotage, and barriers that can mitigate the dispersion.

 11

Similar modeling will need to be conducted for other potential targets for radioactive sabotage, such
as activated coolants.
These dispersion modeling results will need to be combined to identify the period of time that should
be identified for measuring the dose released that will be compared to URC/HRC thresholds. Once
this time period is determined, it should be incorporated as a license condition for the operator.

A.1. 2024 TECHNICAL EXCHANGES
To identify potential approaches that could be shared with the broader nuclear community, the US
Department of Energy National Nuclear Security Administration’s Office of International Nuclear
Security (INS) organized technical exchanges with nuclear agencies in countries that had developed
thresholds or were in the process of developing them. The exchanges took the forms of (1) a three-
day virtual meeting and (2) a separate one-day virtual meeting. Each participating nuclear agency
provided a presentation on its approach and lessons learned. The participants in the technical
exchanges were the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (US NRC), the Canadian Nuclear Safety
Commission (CNSC), the United Kingdom’s Office of Nuclear Regulation (UK ONR), and the
Department for Energy Security and Net Zero (DESNZ), the Netherlands Authority for Nuclear Safety
and Radiation Protection (ANVS), the Lithuanian State Nuclear Power Safety Inspectorate (VATESI)
and [2] France’s Ministry of Ecological Transition and Territorial Cohesion (MTECT).
Participating nuclear agencies detailed a variety of methodological approaches to their assignments
of URC and HRC values. Some agencies (noted in their respective summaries) also reported
completing their own surveys of their peers to obtain values for comparison to support their
selections.

A.1.1. SUMMARY OF MODELING RESULTS USED IN DEVELOPING THIS GUIDE
To assist in this guidance development, the MACCS code was used to simulate the radiological
release from several severe reactor accident scenarios. MACCS has been developed by Sandia
National Laboratories for the past 50 years on behalf of the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) to
characterize the consequences of severe reactor accidents by simulating the atmospheric dispersion
of radioactive particles and modeling their effects on the surrounding area. For this project, MACCS
simulated releases from a prototypical LWR undergoing large break loss-of-coolant (LBLOCA) and
long-term station blackout (LTSBO) accidents and two advanced reactor designs. Ultimately, the
results were used to inform the development of guidance on determining URC and HRC limits in a way
that allows States and regulators to incorporate their levels of risk tolerance, operated and planned
reactor designs, and existing regulations.
Detailed MACCS modeling projects require significant effort to develop inputs, such as evacuation
timings and pathways, local weather timings, and local population and economic info that represent
the accident site with the best possible accuracy. However, the purpose of this effort is not to
conduct a fully accurate site-specific consequence analysis but to develop informative and contextual
MACCS results to assist with the development of URC and HRC thresholds. For this reason, a
“generic” site model was developed for use in this effort; using a generic model makes the results
more widely applicable than if they were based on a specific accident location with unique site
characteristics. This generic site model follows the model parameters set in the LNT Point-Estimate
sample problem included in the MACCS distribution package. Other limited changes were made to
better suit the needs of the project, such as using a uniform population distribution around the site
instead of a location-specific site file, changing the distance measuring intervals to improve
resolution, changing some of the sizes and behaviors of dispersed particles, release height,
surrounding building height, and protective actions. This model was used for both the LWR and
advanced reactor modeling. After the MACCS model was created, the only input that changed
between the scenarios was the source term, which includes several hundred data points declaring the
activity released and the time at which it is released. The MACCS calculations for each scenario were
also run with three different decontamination factor (DF) values (1, 50, and 1000) to provide a better
idea of the range of possible consequences that could come from a radioactive material release.
These DF values represent the effectiveness of the containment building at retaining radionuclides
that are released by severe accidents, which is an important consideration for advanced reactors that
may or may not be planning on constructing traditional large containment buildings.
MACCS has several options available for reporting the consequences of radiological release, but this
effort chose to focus on the population dose and peak dose options. These options were chosen
because regulations like 10 CFR § 50.67 focus on the dose to a single individual. The population dose
option reports the dose given to a single organ (the whole-body effective dose was chosen instead of
a single organ in this case) within a certain distance of the accident site while the peak dose
calculates the maximum dose experienced by any grid element within a certain distance of the
accident site. An average dose value was also calculated by dividing the population dose by the
population living within the measured area. These results were calculated at the end of every 24
hours, starting from when the first plume reaches the surrounding area and ending 30 days after that.

 19

The results show that most of the dose is delivered within the first day (between 88 and 90 percent of
the total dose, depending on what distance the dose is measured at), and little additional dose is
delivered between the end of week 2 and the end of the 30 th day.
When comparing the LBLOCA and LTSBO scenario results, the LTSBO release scenario results in less
severe consequences to the surrounding population. This is because of the different source terms,
and releases of certain chemical groups are orders of magnitude lower in the LTSBO scenario than
the LBLOCA scenario. Similarly to the LBOCA scenario, most of the dose is delivered within the first
day. Depending on the distance of measurement, 90 to 92 percent of the 30-day dose is delivered
within the first day.
The first advanced reactor modeled in this project was a heat pipe reactor (HPR). Over the last
several decades nuclear industry has spent significant time and effort researching HPRs, and designs
such as Westinghouse’s eVinci Microreactor are currently being developed for deployment. HPR
designs provide several advantages, such as compact design, passive safety features like negative
void coefficient and temperature coefficients, and effective passive heat removal with fewer
components and fewer moving parts.
The LBLOCA and LTSBO accidents delivered between 88 and 92 percent of their population dose
within the first day while the HPR accident scenario only delivers between 23 and 30 percent of its
total population dose within the first day, with much more dose being delivered during the following
days and weeks.
The second advanced reactor modeled in this project was a pebble bed design. Pebble bed reactors
have also received significant research attention in the last several decades as an alternative to
traditional LWRs, and pebble bed designs like X-energy’s Xe-100 design are being developed. Pebble
bed reactors offer several advantages, such as online refueling, the safety advantages of TRISO fuel,
and high thermal efficiency.
The PBMR modeling results are somewhat similar to the HPR results, in that both follow the same
trends of the LBLOCA and LTSBO releases but with very different magnitudes. The population dose
increases with time and distance while the average dose and peak dose increase with time and
decrease with distance. However, the population dose from the HPR is many orders of magnitude
higher than the population dose from the PBMR. The percentage of dose delivered in the first day by
the HPR is roughly one order of magnitude higher at 29 to 30 percent depending on the distance,
while the PBMR delivered roughly 3 percent of its dose within the first day.
