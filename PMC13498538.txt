Identification of disulfidptosis-related molecular subtypes in pleural mesothelioma demonstrates a correlation with the immune infiltration, prognosis and therapy efficacy
Authors: Jing Ding, Xuefeng Huang, Wenxiu Yao, Tongyu Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPleural mesothelioma (PM) is a rare and aggressive malignancy with increasing incidence, high mortality, and limited treatment options. Disulfidptosis, a newly identified form of regulated cell death, may play an important role in cancer, but its relevance in PM remains unclear.MethodsTranscriptomic and clinical data of PM were obtained from public databases. PM was classified into molecular subtypes based on disulfidptosis-related genes (DRGs). We then compared prognosis, tumor microenvironment, metabolic features, predicted response to immune checkpoint blockade, and chemotherapy sensitivity between subtypes. In addition, a prognostic signature was established, and key genes were identified using random survival forest analysis.ResultsTwo novel DRG-defined molecular subtypes were identified in PM. The DRGs-high subtype (Cluster 2) was associated with significantly worse overall survival, an immunosuppressive microenvironment dominated by M2 macrophages, and a lower predicted likelihood of response to immune checkpoint blockade. In contrast, the DRGs-low subtype (Cluster 1) showed metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis and fatty acid oxidation, increased NK cell infiltration, and reduced sensitivity to chemotherapy. An 11-gene prognostic signature demonstrated predictive performance, with an AUC of 0.95 for 5-year survival (95%CI 0.881–1.013). Random survival forest analysis identified LMNB2 and CDCA2 as key genes, both of which were highly expressed in tumor tissues.ConclusionsPM can be classified into two DRG-defined molecular subtypes with distinct prognostic, immune, and metabolic features. These findings provide insights into PM heterogeneity and may support future studies on therapeutic stratification. The prognostic signature remains exploratory and requires validation in independent external cohorts before clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05557-1.

Introduction

Malignant mesothelioma is a rare malignant tumor that originates from the mesothelial lining of the lungs (pleura), heart, abdomen, and testicles. Pleural mesothelioma (PM) is the predominant form of malignant mesotheliomas, accounting for 80–90% of cases [1]. PM primarily occurs in elderly males exposed to asbestos. Most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, leading to a median overall survival of about one year and a 5-year survival rate of 5–10% [2]. PM exhibits diverse morphological differentiation and is classified into three types: epithelioid, biphasic, and sarcomatoid. Each type has a different prognosis and response to therapy [3, 4]. Research has linked various genetic mutations to the tumor development of PM, mainly involving loss-of-function in tumor suppressor genes like BAP1, CDKN2A, NF2, LATS2, and TP53 [5–8]. However, the biological determinants underlying inter-patient heterogeneity, particularly the molecular programs shaping immune infiltration and treatment response, remain incompletely defined [9]. Therefore, further research into prognostic biomarkers and molecular subtypes of PM is crucial for better clinical strategic decision-making and improving patient stratification for optimized treatment.

Targeting cancer metabolism is widely acknowledged as an effective strategy for selectively eliminating cancer cells [10]. A recently identified form of regulated cell death, disulfidptosis, is characterized by disulfide stress and actin cytoskeleton collapse under glucose starvation, and has shown potential relevance in multiple diseases, including cancer [11]. Mechanistically, elevated expression of solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) under glucose starvation causes NADPH depletion and intracellular accumulation of disulfides (e.g., cystine), ultimately leading to disulfide-induced cytoskeletal collapse and cell death [12]. Studies have further explored the role of disulfidptosis in the tumor microenvironment, prognosis, and immunotherapy across various cancer types [13–15]. However, its specific role in PM remains unclear.

Fifteen disulfidptosis-related genes (DRGs) were identified from prior research: FLNA, FLNB, MYH9, TLN1, ACTB, MYL6, MYH10, CAPZB, DSTN, IQGAP1, ACTN4, PDLIM1, CD2AP, INF2, and SLC7A11 [12, 13]. Among them, SLC7A11 is the core protein involved in disulfidptosis, and the other 14 genes encode actin-related proteins with disulfide bonds. This study is the first to examine the role of DRGs in various cancers, with a particular focus on PM. Notably, we discovered two distinct subtypes of PM based on the expression patterns of DRGs. These subtypes exhibit unique molecular biological characteristics, immune microenvironments, prognoses, and responses to treatment. An 11-DRG prog nostic signature, in which LMNB2 and CDCA2 had highest significance, was first established. Our research characterizes DRG expression patterns in PM and provides insights into their associations with immune infiltration, prognosis, and predicted treatment response, with the flowchart as depicted in Fig. 1.

Results

General overview of DRGs in pan-cancer and PM patients

Radar charts were created to analyze the expression differences of 15 DRGs between primary tumors and adjacent normal tissues across 33 cancer types (Fig. 2a). In some cases, normal tissues were unavailable for comparison, revealing abnormal DRGs expression in various cancers. The SLC7A11 gene is significantly upregulated across 18 cancer types, such as BRCA, CESC, CHOL, COAD, ESCA, HNSC, KICH, KIRC, KIRP, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, PAAD, PCPG, PRAD, READ, STAD, and UCEC.14 DRGs were upregulated in CHOL versus adjacent normal tissues, whereas the expression of MYH10 was not significantly different. In LUAD tissues, 13 DRGs showed differential expression compared to adjacent normal tissues. Specifically, SLC7A11 and CD2AP were upregulated, while INF2, PDLIM1, ACTN4, MYH9, MYH10, IQGAP1, FLNA, TLN1, MYL6, ACTB, and DSTN were downregulated. To improve the visualization of DRGs expression patterns across cancer types, we generated violin plots focusing on the eight major cancer types for selected key disulfidptosis-related genes (SLC7A11, ACTB, FLNA, FLNB, IQGAP1) (Fig. 2b).

The DRGs were further analyzed using the GEPIA 2.0 database to assess their impact on patient prognosis in 33 types of cancers. The results showed that DRGs were negatively linked to overall survival (OS) in multiple tumor types. Especially in KIRC, DSTN, MYL6, TLN1, IQGAP1, MYH10, ACTN4, PDLIM1 and CD2AP were positively correlated with OS (Fig. 3a). Figure 3b illustrates the correlation between DRGs expression GSVA scores and immune cell infiltration across different cancer types. The study found a positive correlation between the GSVA score and high infiltration scores in COAD, LGG, PCPG, READ, KICH, and LIHC, whereas a negative correlation was identified in KIRP, CESC, and HNSC (P < 0.05, FDR < 0.05). A significant negative correlation was observed between gamma/delta T cell infiltration and the GSVA score across various cancers, including LIHC, THCA, SKCM, BLCA, PAAD, ACC, LUAD, STAD, LUSC, KIRP, THYM, and HNSC (P < 0.05, FDR < 0.05). A significant negative correlation between CD8 + T cell infiltration and the GSVA score was observed in 14 cancer types (P < 0.05, FDR < 0.05), with the exception of UVM, which exhibited a positive correlation (P < 0.05, FDR < 0.05). Positive correlations between the GSVA score and macrophages were identified in nine cancer types (P < 0.05, FDR < 0.05). Furthermore, a positive correlation was observed between iTreg infiltration and the GSVA score of DRGs across 15 cancer types (P < 0.05, FDR < 0.05).

Genes correlation, signaling pathways regulation, and immune analysis of DRGs in PM

The correlation between DRGs and commonly mutated genes of PM was explored. BAP1 exhibited a positive correlation with the mRNA expression of FLNA, FLNB, and SLC7A11, whereas it was negatively correlated with INF2 mRNA expression. Other commonly mutated genes, such as VHL, TP53, TMEM127, SETD2, MSH6, GATA3 and BRCA2, were positively correlated with multiple different DRGs. However, WT1, MTAP, and CDKN2A were negatively correlated with several DRGs (Fig. 3c). DRGs were also analysed to identify the pathways that are activated and inhibited in PM. Most DRGs activated the EMT and RTK pathways, while inhibiting the DNA damage response, cell cycle, and AR hormone pathways. However, numerous DRGs exhibited varied effects on different pathways (Fig. 3d). The correlation of DRGs and eight immune checkpoint genes showed that 12 DRGs were significantly correlated with distinct immune checkpoint genes, for example, CD274, CTLA4, PDCD1LG2, and TIGIT was positively associated with ACTB, ACTN4, CAPZB and MYH9 expression (Fig. 3e). In addition, the differences in immune infiltration among the DRGs geneset CNV groups in PM patients were investigated. Compared to the wild-type group, lower infiltration scores, cytotoxicity, and iTreg infiltration were found in the CNV group, while greater memory T cell infiltration was found in the CNV group (Fig. 3f).

Identification of DRG-defined molecular subtypes in PM

A consensus clustering algorithm was applied to the expression data of 15 DRGs, analyzing 87 samples from the TCCA-MESO dataset. Clustering analysis identified two distinct subtypes, namely, Cluster 1 (41 samples) and Cluster 2 (46 samples) (Fig. 4a–c). Further analysis through PCA demonstrated that the samples in these two Clusters were significantly separated (Fig. 4d). Heatmap analysis revealed higher expression levels of DRGs in Cluster 2 compared to Cluster 1 (Fig. 4e). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated significant differences in patient outcomes between the two clusters. Patients in Cluster 1 demonstrated superior OS, disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free survival (PFS) relative to Cluster 2 (Fig. 4f–h). The two clusters were well-matched in key clinicopathological characteristics, including age, sex, histological type and disease stage (all P > 0.05) (Supplementary Table S1).

Genetic mutation landscape in different subtypes of PM based on DRGs

To compare the somatic mutations between the two subtypes of PM, we utilized the maftools package to generate key visualizations. Supplementary Fig. S1a-b provides a comprehensive overview of the genetic alterations in each Cluster. Cluster 1 had 81.58% of its genes altered, and Cluster 2 had a slightly greater alteration rate of 90.48%. Upon further analysis using an oncoplot, specific genes that exhibited a higher mutation frequency were identified in each Cluster. In Cluster 1, the genes BAP1, NF2, TTN, SETD2, and TP53 had mutation frequencies of 29%, 21%, 18%, 16%, and 13%, respectively. In Cluster 2, NF2, BAP1, TP53, LATS2, and FAT4 exhibited mutation frequencies of 29%, 29%, 19%, 12%, and 10%, respectively. These findings demonstrated that there was a differential mutational spectrum between the two Clusters. A cohort summary plot was created to depict the distribution of variants by classification, type, and SNV class for each subtype. Supplementary Fig. S1c-d illustrates the mutation load for each sample alongside the ten most frequently mutated genes. The analysis revealed that missense mutations were the most common type in both clusters. Among the types of variants classified, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) emerged as the most frequent type. Furthermore, the most common mutation type detected in both Clusters was C > T.

Immune profiles and response to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) in two PM subtypes

We utilized the CIBERSORT algorithm to compare the two PM subtypes, Cluster 1 and Cluster 2. Cluster 1 exhibited greater infiltration of B cell memory and activated NK cells, while Cluster 2 showed greater infiltration of M0 macrophages and M2 macrophages, suggesting a different immune profile distinct from that of Cluster 1 (Fig. 5a, b). Figure 5c illustrates the percentage abundance of 22 distinct immune cell types across each sample. Notably, there was no significant difference in eight immune checkpoint gene expression between the two clusters (Supplementary Fig. S2). We utilized the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm to forecast the therapeutic outcomes of ICB in patients with the two PM subtypes. Cluster 2 had a higher TIDE score due to elevated levels of exclusion, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), whereas Cluster 1 showed a higher score for the microsatellite instability (MSI) expression signature. Overall, these results suggested that PM patients in Cluster 2 may have poorer responses to ICB therapy compared to those in Cluster 1 (Fig. 5d, e).

Comparison of the mRNA-based malignancy index (mRNAsi) and drug sensitivity between different subtypes of PM

The mRNAsi score analysis revealed that Cluster 1 exhibited significantly higher cellular stemness than Cluster 2, as indicated by its elevated mRNAsi score (Fig. 6a). The sensitivity of these subtypes to nine drugs, either commonly used or under investigation for PM treatment, was evaluated. These drugs include paclitaxel, docetaxel, vinorelbine, cisplatin, gemcitabine, belinostat, vorinostat, crizotinib, and pazopanib. Compared to Cluster 2, Cluster 1 exhibited higher IC50 values for all of the tested drugs, indicating lower sensitivity to the drugs (Fig. 6b–j). These findings suggested that different subtypes of PM, characterized by varying degrees of DRGs, may exhibit distinct therapeutic responses to both chemotherapy and targeted therapy.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis and functional enrichment between two subtypes of PM

The upregulated DEGs in Cluster 1 showed significant enrichment in Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways associated with immune and metabolic processes (Fig. 7a). The upregulated DEGs in Cluster 2 were mainly enriched in cellular division, migration, adhesion, and extracellular matrix (ECM) regulation (Fig. 7b). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of GOBP and KEGG pathways indicated that Cluster 1 exhibited a notably enhanced metabolic and immune phenotype, with significant enrichment of metabolism-related and immune-related biological processes (Fig. 7c, d). GSEA of the Reactome pathways further revealed the activation of many metabolic processes in Cluster 1 (Fig. 7e). These findings suggested that Cluster 1 is characterized by an overall increase in metabolic activity and immune responses. In contrast, Cluster 2 demonstrated a significantly enhanced malignant phenotype with the activation of multiple invasive cancer pathways (Fig. 7f, g). GSEA of the hallmark pathways revealed significant activation of numerous procancer signalling pathways in Cluster 2, such as the EMT pathway, G2/M checkpoint pathway, E2F target pathway, IL2/Stat5 signalling pathway, hypoxia pathway, Kras signalling pathway, glycolysis, TNFα/NF-kB signalling pathway, mTORC1 signalling pathway, angiogenesis and HIF1 TF pathway (Fig. 7h, Supplementary Fig. S3). The results suggest that Cluster 2 is characterized by enhanced pathways associated with cancer development and progression.

Multi-gene prognostic signature for PM patients

Initially, 200 DEGs were identified between the two subtypes, showing a strong association with PM patient prognosis. We utilized LASSO regularization with 10-fold cross-validation to enhance gene selection, achieving a minimum lambda value of 0.223 (Fig. 8a, b). Cox coefficients were maintained for 11 genes, including TROAP, CDCA2, LOXL2, LMNB2, ADH1B, HAPLN1, ARHGEF26, OXTR, HACD2, HPR and SULT1B1.An 11-gene risk score was derived using Cox coefficients with the formula: risk score = (0.0608)*TROAP + (0.1159)*CDCA2 + (0.0057)*LOXL2 + (0.2817)*LMNB2 + (-0.0365)*ADH1B + (0.0166)*HAPLN1 + (0.0147)*ARHGEF26 + (0.0592)*OXTR + (0.053)*HACD2 + (− 0.0381)*HPR + (0.0211)*SULT1B1. Patients were categorized into high-risk (n = 43) and low-risk (n = 43) groups based on risk scores, as depicted in Fig. 8c. This figure also illustrates patient survival status and includes a heatmap of the 11 prognostic genes’ expression levels. The high-risk group exhibited significantly poorer overall survival (OS) than the low-risk group in Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, and the HR was estimated using a Cox proportional hazards model (HR = 4.820, 95% CI = 2.812–8.260, p < 0.0001; Fig. 8d). The multigene prognostic signature demonstrated favorable predictive performance for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year overall survival, with AUC values exceeding 0.80 in the time-dependent ROC analysis, especially with 5-year AUC of 0.95 (95%CI 0.881–1.013) (Fig. 8e). Additionally, our analysis revealed a significant elevation in risk within Cluster 2 when compared to Cluster 1, as illustrated in Fig. 8f (P < 0.001). The Sankey diagram revealed that most patients in the low-risk cohort were linked to Cluster 1 and survived, unlike those in the high-risk cohort (Fig. 8g). These findings indicate that the multigene prognostic signature can effectively predict the overall survival of PM patients.

Nomogram construction for PM patients

Clinical data, including age, sex, and stage, were incorporated into the analysis to further assess the prognostic significance of the risk score. Cox regression analyses, both univariate and multivariate, identified the risk score as an independent prognostic factor for PM (Fig. 8h-i). Using the nomogram, patients can calculate a survival percentage for 1, 2, and 3 years by integrating scores from clinical data (Fig. 8j). The calibration curve exhibited a strong correspondence between the nomogram’s prediction and the observed probability (Fig. 8k). The nomogram’s AUC values were 0.771, 0.793, and 0.872 for the 1, 2, and 3-year marks, respectively (Fig. 8l).

Analysis of PM patients revealed differences in immune infiltration and ICB response between the two risk groups

The CIBERSORT algorithm was used to compare immune profiles between high- and low-risk PM patient groups. The low-risk group exhibited higher infiltration levels of naive B cells, plasma cells, activated NK cells, and activated dendritic cells compared to the high-risk group (Fig. 9a). A correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between the multi-gene prognostic signature’s risk score and immune infiltration. A significant correlation was observed between the risk score and the infiltration of B cells (r=− 0.28, p = 0.008), CD4 + T cells (r=− 0.25, p = 0.020), CD8 + T cells (r=− 0.26, p = 0.016), and uncharacterized cells (r=− 0.46, p = 6.89e-06), though these correlations were weak to moderate (Fig. 9b). Moreover, the relationship between risk scores and the proportions of tumour-infiltrating immune cells throughout the cancer-immunity cycle was analysed using the tumor immunophenotype (TIP) system. Risk scores were positively associated with step1 (release of cancer cell antigens, p = 0.036), step2 (cancer antigen presentation, p = 0.001), step4 (basophil recruitment, p = 0.000), and step6 (recognition of cancer cells by T cells, p = 0.007). Negative correlations were identified between risk scores and steps 4 (neutrophil recruitment), 5 (immune cell infiltration into tumors), and 7 (cancer cell killing), with p-values of 0.000, 0.000, and 0.039, respectively (Fig. 9c). The TIDE algorithm was utilized to examine the therapeutic outcomes of ICB in the two risk groups of PM patients. The high-risk group exhibited a greater TIDE score, characterized by elevated levels of exclusion, MDSCs, and CAFs. The findings indicate that PM patients classified as high-risk may have less favorable responses to ICB therapy compared to those in the low-risk category (Fig. 9d, e).

Expression of the critical genes LMNB2 and CDCA2

A forest plot illustrating the hazard ratios for 11 candidate genes was generated using univariate Cox regression analysis (Fig. 10a). After Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction, all genes remained statistically significant (Supplementary Table S1). The random survival forests (RSF) algorithm was used to assess the significance of each gene in the multigene model of PM. LMNB2 showed the greatest significance, with CDCA2 and TROAP2 following in order (Fig. 10b). In TCGA database, we observed most model genes had differential mRNA expression in various cancers compared to paracancerous tissues. Specifically, LMNB2 and CDCA2 demonstrated elevated expression levels in cancerous tissues (Fig. 10c–f). The immunohistochemical staining results of LMNB2 and CDCA2 proteins were acquired from the The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database, which indicated a high or moderate staining intensity of LMNB2 and CDCA2 in several cancerous tissues, while LMNB2 and CDCA2 were not detected in the corresponding normal tissues (Fig. 10g–h).

Hypothetical treatment-response framework based on DRG-defined subtypes

To summarize the potential translational relevance of the DRG-defined subtypes, we generated a cluster-comparison summary (Table 1) and proposed a hypothetical treatment-response framework (Fig. 11). These analyses were based on indirect transcriptomic and immune-related indicators, including TIDE score, MSI signature, and immune cell infiltration, and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than clinical recommendations. Cluster 1 showed lower immune suppression and potentially higher predicted sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade, whereas Cluster 2 displayed higher M2 macrophage infiltration and TIDE scores, suggesting a more immunosuppressive phenotype and potentially lower predicted ICB response.

Discussion

PM is an aggressive and rare cancer linked to chronic inflammation and oxidative stress from asbestos fiber inhalation. Although advancements have been made in understanding the causes of the disease, the prognosis for most sporadic PM patients remains poor [16, 17]. Effective treatment options remain limited. While ICB has shown clinical activity in pleural mesothelioma [18–20], its comparative benefit and optimal role in the treatment paradigm remain under active discussion [21, 22]. Consequently, there is a need to investigate novel biomarkers or molecular subtyping techniques to better predict treatment outcomes and enhance patient prognosis. The metabolic reprogramming of PM cells towards a glycolytic (Warburg) phenotype involves the disruption of multiple signaling pathways, such as the PI3K-AKT, HIF, p53, MYC, and AMPK pathways [23]. Research on disulfidptosis has been pivotal in clarifying the involvement of disulfides and their dysregulation in glucose metabolism during cell death [12]. However, the specific characteristics of DRGs in PM remain poorly understood.

This study identified 15 DRGs associated with cancer development and immune microenvironment regulation. In PM, the mRNA expression of 11 DRGs was associated with OS, whereas the expression of MYL6, TLN1, IQGAP1, and INF2 was not associated with OS. Moreover, the DRGs were correlated with several commonly mutated genes in PM. BAP1 was positively associated with FLNA, FLNB and SLC7A11 mRNA expression. BAP1 loss-of-function mutations are found in families predisposed to mesothelioma and in about 60% of sporadic mesothelioma cases [24]. Elevated SLC7A11 expression in cancer cells is often linked to mutations like BAP1 or KEAP1 loss [25]. BAP1 gene loss-of-function mutations significantly alter glycolysis and TCA cycle metabolite levels [26], leading to decreased mitochondrial respiration and increased glucose consumption and lactate production [27]. Most DRGs are involved in signaling pathways regulation in PM, especially EMT activation. Nine DRGs showed a positive correlation with the expression of various checkpoint genes. ACTB expression showed a positive correlation with the expression of CD274, CTLA4, HAVCR2, PDCD1LG2, and TIGIT. These checkpoints exert suppressive effects on anti-tumor immunity, with CTLA4 primarily influencing the induction of T cells and PD1 predominantly involved in regulating T cell function in peripheral tissues [28]. Although ACTB and CTLA4 are positively correlated across all samples, CTLA4 expression did not differ significantly between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2. This suggests that CTLA4 expression alone may not serve as a distinguishing biomarker for subtype-specific treatment. The DRGs geneset CNV group had a lower infiltration score and cytotoxicity, compared to the wide-type gene set in PM. Therefore, these results indicated that DRGs play a pivotal role in PM progression.

The existing classification approaches for PM primarily rely on the morphological distinction of malignant cells, resulting in the division of the disease into three subtypes. Recent genomic evidence highlights the need for a more detailed and clinically relevant classification. This study categorized PM into two subtypes using DRGs. These subtypes displayed noticeable variations in prognosis, gene mutation patterns, immune characteristics, cellular stemness, treatment response and signaling pathways. The analysis of 87 PM samples revealed that the DRGs-high subtype (Cluster 2) correlate with poorer prognosis and increased gene mutation rates. Because disulfidptosis is known to occur under glucose starvation, the elevated expression of selected DRGs, particularly SLC7A11, in Cluster 2 may suggest a potential association with disulfide-stress-related vulnerability. However, this inference remains indirect and requires functional validation. Increased SLC7A11 expression in cancer cells is dependent on specific nutrients, namely, glucose and glutamine [25]. Conversely, the presence of DRGs, such as upregulated SLC7A11, INF2, or ACTN4, exerts a pro-cancer influence, leading to more pronounced malignant behaviour in tumors harbouring these genes [25, 29, 30]. Previous investigations have reported that SLC7A11 is overexpressed in various cancer types, elucidating its role in preserving intracellular redox equilibrium and impeding the accumulation of intracellular ROS, thus conferring resistance to ferroptosis [31–33].

Cluster 2 exhibited increased infiltration of M2 macrophages, together with higher TIDE scores and immune-exclusion-related features, suggesting an M2-dominant immunosuppressive microenvironment. This finding is consistent with established mechanisms by which M2-polarized macrophages promote immune evasion, including secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10 and TGF-β), ARG1-mediated L-arginine depletion, and surface PD-L1 upregulation [27, 34–36]. M2 macrophages can also undermine ICB efficacy by forming T-cell–exclusionary niches and scavenging anti-PD-1 antibodies via Fcγ receptors [37, 38]. In our analysis, the enrichment of M2 macrophages in Cluster 2 therefore provides a plausible explanation for its stronger immune-evasion phenotype and lower predicted ICB sensitivity. We also observed upregulation of glycolysis, hypoxia, and HIF-1 signaling pathways in Cluster 2. Although lactate metabolism pathways were not directly enriched, this metabolic signature may imply elevated lactate production. Under hypoxic conditions, HIF-1α induces LDHA and MCT4 (SLC16A3), which mediate pyruvate-to-lactate conversion and export; lactate in turn reinforces M2 polarization through HIF-1α stabilization and arginase-1 expression [27, 39, 40]. Although whether active disulfidptosis directly contributes to this phenotype remains to be experimentally determined, DRG-associated redox imbalance may also be linked to an M2-like immunosuppressive state. NADPH depletion and SLC7A11 overexpression, both features of disulfidptosis, can activate redox-sensitive transcription factors such as STAT3 and NF-κB [11, 41, 42]—key drivers of M2 programming—thereby restricting T-cell infiltration/function and contributing to the immunosuppressive phenotype and lower sensitivity to ICB observed in Cluster 2.

GO/KEGG and GSEA analyses revealed that the Cluster 2 of PM is marked by the activation of malignancy-associated pathways, including those related to EMT, cell proliferation, adhesion, migration, and the G2/M phase transition of the cell cycle. In contrast, Cluster 1 exhibited increased metabolic and immunological activity, with the activation of many metabolic processes and NK cell infiltration. NK cells are a subset of innate lymphoid cells known for their potent cytotoxic effector capabilities. In the realm of cancer, these cells demonstrate exceptional effectiveness in eliminating malignant cells and impeding tumor metastasis [43]. Tumors adopt various strategies to escape NK cell-mediated destruction, such as using a collagen coating to activate inhibitory NK receptors and employing platelets as a shield against NK cell detection [44]. Interestingly, the low DRG-defined subtypes exhibited a heightened degree of cellular stemness, potentially leading to reduced susceptibility to chemotherapy and targeted therapies. Cancer stem cells (CSCs) have been identified as primarily glycolytic or dependent on OXPHOS or mitochondrial function, depending on the tumor type [45–47]. To address the resistance mechanism enabling certain CSCs to overcome OXPHOS inhibition, it is necessary to combine metformin and other drugs that target mitochondrial ATP production with agents that counteract this resistance mechanism [48]. A review identified several autocrine loops linking stemness pathways to cisplatin and pemetrexed resistance in PM-initiating cells [49]. Hence, targeting cellular metabolism might be an effective treatment approach to overcome chemotherapy resistance among the low DRG-defined subtypes of PM.

Given these distinct immune and stemness features, we explored whether the DRG-defined subtypes could generate hypotheses relevant to current immunotherapy strategies in PM. To this end, we compared key molecular and immune-related features between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 (Table 1). Cluster 1 exhibited significantly higher mRNAsi, indicating increased tumor stemness and plasticity, which has been associated with improved immunogenicity and T cell infiltration in certain cancers [50]. Cluster 2, by contrast, showed significantly higher TIDE scores, reflecting a greater degree of immune evasion and potential resistance to checkpoint blockade [51]. These findings suggest that the two subtypes may differ in their predicted immune responsiveness. In the context of current first-line strategies, nivolumab plus ipilimumab has demonstrated clinical activity in unresectable PM in the CheckMate 743 trial [52], whereas pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy has also shown benefit in untreated advanced PM in the KEYNOTE-483 trial [53]. In our analysis, the relatively lower immune-suppression profile and higher MSI expression signature of Cluster 1 may provide a rationale for future studies evaluating whether this subgroup is more likely to respond to immune checkpoint blockade, including dual checkpoint blockade or pembrolizumab-based strategies [54]. Conversely, the M2 macrophage-enriched and immune-exclusionary features of Cluster 2 may support future investigation of whether combination approaches involving chemotherapy and immunotherapy could modulate the suppressive tumor microenvironment. Although tumor mutational burden (TMB) did not differ significantly between clusters, their low absolute levels are consistent with the generally low mutation burden of PM, limiting its utility as a subtype-discriminating marker [55]. Overall, these interpretations remain hypothesis-generating and require validation in independent treatment-annotated cohorts and prospective clinical studies.

We concentrated on the top 200 prognosis-related DEGs between two subtypes of PM samples to construct a disulfidptosis-related prognostic signature. Using LASSO regression analysis, 11 novel DRGs were identified that exhibit promise as potential prognostic markers. Patients in the high-risk group had a significantly worse prognosis than those in the low-risk group. This finding underscored the potential efficacy of the multigene prognostic signature in predicting the survival outcome of PM patients and informing therapeutic interventions. Besides, our analysis yielded a possible finding, indicating a negative correlation between the risk score and the presence of immune cell types, namely B cells, CD8 + T cells, and CD4 + T cells. Further analysis indicated that neutrophil recruitment, immune cell infiltration, and cancer cell killing were compromised in the high-risk group. The high-risk group exhibited an elevated TIDE score. The TME has the potential to impede the ability of T cells to accumulate within cancerous cells. MDSCs and CAFs can impede T cell infiltration into tumors, correlating with poor clinical outcomes [56]. This suggests that individuals classified as high-risk may exhibit a compromised immune response against the tumor, potentially due to an immunosuppressive microenvironment.

The model identified 11 novel DRGs with elevated expression levels in various cancers relative to adjacent normal tissues. Among them, nine genes—LMNB2, CDCA2, TROAP, HACD2, HAPLN1, SULT1B1, OXTR, ARHGEF26 and LOXL2—were significantly associated with worse prognosis (HR > 1) in PM (Fig. 10a). The RFS method discovered a distinct positive correlation was discovered between prognosis and two key genes, namely LMNB2 and CDCA2. LMNB2 engages with diverse proteins within the inner nucleus and nuclear envelope, influencing intracellular pathways that affect cytoskeleton and chromatin functions [57–59]. LMNB2 is primarily located on the inner side of the nuclear envelope, with smaller amounts found in the nucleoplasm and near the nucleolar periphery [60, 61]. LMNB2 is essential for maintaining the integrity of the nuclear envelope and nucleolus, and it is involved in cell proliferation, senescence, DNA repair, and gene expression [57, 61]. Moreover, recent research has reported a correlation between aberrant LMNB2 expression and the progression of various malignancies [62]. CDCA2 is crucial for chromatin organization in post-mitotic nuclei [63]. In our study, we have identified the genes that exhibit obvious prognostic value in PM, thus warranting further investigation. Direct comparison with external transcriptomic frameworks (e.g., OncoCast) will require standardized implementation and sample-level subtype mapping, which is a priority for future validation studies.

Our research provides a comprehensive analysis of the DRG-defined phenotype and its influence on immune infiltration, therapy, and prognosis in PM patients. Additionally, we have made the interesting discovery of two subtypes related to DRGs and have developed a novel multiple DRGs model that can predict prognosis and immunotherapy outcome in PM. However, this study had several limitations. First, the prognostic model was developed from a relatively small TCGA-MESO cohort and lacked independent external validation. Although cross-validated LASSO regression was used, potential overfitting cannot be fully excluded. Thus, the AUC estimates should be interpreted cautiously and further validated in larger independent PM cohorts with standardized transcriptomic profiling, complete survival follow-up, and treatment-response information. Second, the subtype classification was based on the expression patterns of 15 DRGs rather than direct evidence of active disulfidptosis. We did not assess disulfide accumulation, NADPH depletion, or actin cytoskeleton collapse in PM tissues or cell lines. Therefore, the DRGs-high subtype should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of active disulfidptosis, and functional experiments are needed to validate whether disulfidptosis occurs in PM and contributes to the observed immune and prognostic phenotypes. Third, because this study was based on bulk transcriptomic data, it could not define the cellular distribution or spatial organization of the DRG-defined subtypes. Future single-cell and spatial transcriptomic studies are needed to characterize subtype-specific cell composition, functional states, and tumor–immune interactions in PM [64]. Fourth, although LMNB2 and CDCA2 were identified as key prognostic genes, their associations with PM prognosis remain correlational. Future studies should apply causal inference approaches, such as Mendelian randomization, together with functional experiments to determine whether LMNB2 and CDCA2 directly contribute to PM progression and patient outcomes [65, 66].

Conclusion

This study uniquely analyzes and integrates the disulfidptosis-related subtype pattern with immune infiltration in PM patients. Identifying clusters within PM enhances our understanding of the immune microenvironment and reveals differences in treatment efficacy and prognosis among these clusters. The prognostic signature may support future risk stratification in PM, but independent external validation is required before clinical application. Our study provides a hypothesis-generating framework for future investigation of individualized therapeutic stratification in PM. Nevertheless, further clinical and experimental studies are necessary to validate these findings.

Methods

Genomic data and clinical information

Data on gene expression patterns and clinical characteristics of various cancer patients were sourced from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. The differential expression profiles of 15 DRGs were analyzed using “ggplot2[3.3.6]” R package to compare cancerous tissues with adjacent normal tissues in pan-cancer. Furthermore, we also utilized the GEPIA2.0 platform to to generate a comprehensive survival map for the 15 studied genes we investigated across all 33 different types of cancer.

GSCA and GSCALite

Using the GSCA platform (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/GSCA/#/), we assessed the relationship between immune cell infiltration and the GSVA score of DRGs expression across various cancers. ImmuCellAI was used to assess the infiltration of 24 different immune cells. The GSVA score was determined using the GSVA R package. We investigated the immune infiltration differences between gene set Copy Number Variation (CNV) groups and the wild type group in PM.

Utilizing the GSCALite platform (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/web/GSCALite/), we concentrated on DRGs in PM, examining the activation or inhibition of key cancer-related pathways. The pathways encompassed TSC/mTOR, RTK, RAS/MAPK, PI3K/AKT, Hormone ER, Hormone AR, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), DNA damage response (DDR), cell cycle, and apoptosis.

Consensus clustering analysis

The expression profiles of these DRGs from TCGA-MESO data were analyzed using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” R package (v1.54.0) to evaluate clustering consistency. Kaplan-Meier analysis and the log-rank test were used to assess survival rate differences among clusters. Heatmaps were generated to visualize the clustering results using the R package ‘pheatmap’.

Genetic alteration analysis

To visualize the data of mutation in different clusters, we utilized the “maftools” package in the R software. To present certain genes with a high frequency of mutation across the different clusters, we displayed these genes in a histogram.

Estimation of immune profiles and ICB response

We assessed the reliable results of immune score evaluation with transcriptome profiling data from TCGA by the R package “immuneeconv”. We employed the CIBERSORT algorithm to quantify the abundance of 22 immune cell types in PM by analyzing the expression distribution across two distinct clusters. We used the R package circlize (v0.4.1) to analyze the correlation between DRGs and immune checkpoint genes (CD274, CTLA4, HAVCR2, LAG3, PDCD1, PDCD1LG2, TIGIT, SIGLEC15), and compared their expression between the two PM clusters. We used TIDE algorithm [51] to predict the potential response of ICB therapy. The TIDE algorithm addresses two tumor immune escape mechanisms: dysfunction of tumor-infiltrating cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTL) and CTL rejection by immunosuppressive factors. The algorithm employs gene expression markers to assess mechanisms and predict ICB response likelihood. The analysis methods were executed using R, utilizing packages like “ggplot2” and “ggpubr (0.4.0)”. TMB scores were obtained from TCGA data [67]. TMB was calculated using the maftools package based on somatic mutation counts normalized per megabase.

mRNAsi and drug sensitivity estimation

Malta et al. [47] developed the Optimized Composite Least Regression (OCLR) algorithm to calculate the mRNAsi, assessing a tumor’s malignant potential. To predict the chemotherapeutic response of each sample, we obtained data from the GDSC database. In order to perform the prediction process, we employed a R package called “pRRophetic”. To gain further insights from the data, we compared the IC50 scores of drugs between two clusters groups.

GO term and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis and GSEA

Enrichment analysis was performed using GO terms, KEGG pathways, and GSEA. The “limma” package was employed to analyze the DEGs in two PM Clusters. The study defined differential gene expression using two criteria: an adjusted P-value of less than 0.05 and a Log2 Fold Change greater than 1 or less than − 1. We utilized the “ClusterProfiler” package for functional analysis of the identified DEGs, examining their roles in carcinogenesis through GO database categories (biological process, cellular component, molecular function) and KEGG pathway enrichment [68–70]. Subsequently, we conducted GSEA to investigate the potential signaling pathways within the two distinct clusters. To visualize the results of GSEA, the “ggplot2” package was utilized. A pathway was considered statistically significant if p < 0.05 or FDR < 0.05.

Establishment of a multi-gene prognostic signature

To forecast the prognosis of PM patients, we initially employed univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis to identify prognosis-related genes among DEGs in the two PM clusters. The hazard ratio was specified as different from 1, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. The leading 200 prognosis-related genes were selected as candidate genes for subsequent analysis. We utilized LASSO Cox regression via the “glmnet” package to develop a multigene signature.

To determine the optimal lambda value, we utilized tenfold cross-validation. After LASSO regularization, eleven genes were retained based on their Cox coefficients, and a prognostic risk score was established:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{Risk Score}=\sum_i Coefficient (mRNA_{i}) \times Expression(mRNA_{i})$$\end{document}

Patients were categorized into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median risk score. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was conducted to assess survival differences between the two groups. We employed the “timeROC” package for time-dependent ROC analysis to assess the predictive accuracy of the risk score. We utilized the “stats” and “car” packages to compare the multigene model risk scores, offering a comprehensive insight into the disparities between the two PM clusters. We also employed the “ggalluvial” and “ggplot2” packages to visually represent the composition and corresponding circumstances of the data within each category of clusters, risk score groups, and survival state. We examined the correlation between the multigene model’s risk score and the immune score using the EPIC algorithm via the “ggstatsplot” package. A butterfly diagram is drawn for visualizing the correlation between the model risk score and immune score by the TIP system, as well as the correlation analysis between immune scoring itself. The anti-cancer immune response is a sequence of events termed the cancer-immunity cycle. The TIP system combines the ‘ssGSEA’ and ‘CIBERSORT’ methods to monitor and evaluate the proportions of immune cells infiltrating tumors throughout the cancer-immunity cycle. Immunohistochemical staining images of key proteins were sourced from the HPA database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/), specifically from the following links: LMNB2 pathology (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000176619-LMNB2/pathology), LMNB2 tissue (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000176619-LMNB2/tissue), CDCA2 pathology (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000184661-CDCA2/pathology), and CDCA2 tissue (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000184661-CDCA2/tissue).

Construction of nomogram

A nomogram was created using the disulfidptosis risk score to predict PM prognosis. Utilizing the ‘rms’ R package, this nomogram integrates diverse clinical features and risk scores of PM patients to estimate survival probabilities at 1, 2, and 3 years based on individual patient scores. The accuracy of the nomogram’s predictions was evaluated using ROC and Calibration curves.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.2.1 or 4.0.3) and SPSS software. Cutoff values were determined using the median gene expression method. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to evaluate the statistical significance between two groups. The Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test were used for survival analysis. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to assess the influence of clinical factors and risk scores on patient outcomes. Spearman’s correlation was employed to examine the correlation between gene expression levels and any associated statistical significance. All p-values from differential expression and survival analyses were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR procedure. Statistical tests were two-sided, with a p-value or adjust p-value under 0.05 indicating significance.
