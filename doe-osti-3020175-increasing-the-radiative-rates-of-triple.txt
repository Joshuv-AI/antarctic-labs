Project Objective

The objective of the project is to develop and validate white organic light emitting device
WOLEDs with internal efficiencies of 100% and lifetimes exceeding a time at which the
luminance drops by 30% (T70) of 50,000 hr at an initial luminance of L0 = 3000 cd/m2.

Task 2.3 Demonstrate device operational lifetimes extended by PF

We have established the transient EL & PL setup for the photophysics inside the blue OLEDs.
The relative lifetimes are compared in Fig. 2.3.1. In the preliminary comparative study, the
Ir(dmp)3 device lifetime shows a proportional increase with the exciton decay rate. In this
project, the notation for devices with different Purcell factors is as follows:
 • C1: Al cathode, thick EML (>30nm), ITO anode. Control device with Purcell factor = 1;
 • C2: Ag cathode, thick EML (>30nm), ITO anode. A second control device.
 • H1: Ag cathode, thin EML (<20nm), ITO anode.
 • F1: Ag cathode, thin EML (<20nm), ITO anode, bottom distributed Bragg reflector (DBR).
 • F2: Ag cathode, thin EML (<20nm), ITO/Ag/ITO anode.
 • F3: Ag cathode, thin EML (<20nm), ITO/Ag/ITO anode, bottom distributed Bragg reflector
 (DBR).
Due to the intrinsic low PLQY of mCBP:Ir(dmp)3 blend (around 47%), at this stage we could
deduce an almost linear power law between the Purcell factor vs. device lifetime. To further
study the quantitative relationship, on the one hand, we repeated the experiments in the next
period on high PLQY host:dopant matrix, including stable Ir-complexes and TADF molecules.
On the other hand, we needed to make sure that the degradation happens in the emissive layer. To
do this, we needed to choose organic structures that effectively confine the recombination zone
in the EML, and also eliminate the leakage from the half cavity and full cavity devices. We found
the leakage came from the sidewall introduced during patterning. In Fig. 2.3.2, we have designed
a way for patterning a customized ITO and ITO/Ag/ITO electrode stack. Instead of direct
patterning via etching ITO and Ag at the same time, we pattern an insulation barrier layer such as
parylene. By tuning the taper angle, we have achieved stable J-V characteristics and passed the
lifetime test for custom-patterned C2, H1 and F1, as shown in Fig. 2.3.2 and Fig. 2.3.3. In the
next period, optimized the cavity structures for Ir-complexes and cMa TADF molecules, and
systematically study the exciton photophysics and device lifetime (LT70 with initial luminance of
1000cd/m2 for control device C1).

Task 3: Achieving short emission lifetime, high stability, triplet controlled emitters.

Task 3.1: Tailored (carbene)M(amide) (cMa) emitters.

The plan to achieve this goal was to explore a range of carbene and amide ligands in two-
coordinate (carbene)M(amide) complexes of gold that emit exclusively via a thermally assisted
delayed fluorescence (TADF) mechanism. The choice of gold over copper or silver was to keep
the highest possible level of spin orbit coupling and thus the highest rate of intersystem crossing
between S1 and T1, a requisite for fast TADF. Our previous work in the area suggested a set of
ligands that we expected would decrease lifetimes significantly. Working with a wide range of
carbene and amide ligands we initially modeled them theoretically and settled on a series of ligands
to study. Through the approach we identified two carbenes (BZI and BZAC) and one amide (bim)
that give rise to deep blue emission with photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) of 1 and a
lifetime < 300 ns (250 and 280 ns) (Figure 1).1 We later found that by adding two methyl groups
to the bim ligand we could further reduce the lifetime to 190 ns while maintaining the deep blue
color and high PLQY.2

 Figure 1: short emission lifetime deep blue emitting phosphors.

OLEDs were prepared with both (BZI)Au(bim) and (BZAC)Au(bim) and we achieved external
quantum efficiencies of 15%. The reliability of the OLEDs were not good, due to the materials
we had to use in their fabrication. The cMa complexes have inherently shallow HOMOs and this
means that the host materials and adjacent layers must have very shallow LUMOs to prevent from
forming exciplexes. These shallow LUMO materials are inherently poor electron conductors and
lead to device failure. In our work to solve this problem we have prepare acceptor substituted bim
complexed. The acceptor groups stabilize the HOMO substantially so we can use conventional
OLED materials in the device studies. The acceptor groups include CN and CF 3 groups. They
both stabilize the HOMO by roughly 0.6 eV and give deep blue emission when paired with the
right carbene. The CF3 substitution is preferred on a linewidth and radiative lifetime basis. We

Task 4: Achieving short emission lifetimes of binuclear and heavy-metal phosphors

Task 4.1 Demonstrate devices with radiative rates of metalorganic phosphors and TADF molecules
decreased by PF.:

The goal in this task was to prepare materials where the S 1 state lies within the triplet manifold of
states. This would give high ISC rates and potentially short lifetimes for Ir based phosphors. The
hope was that this would lead to greater stability of the complexes. To this end we prepared a
series of triazine substituted Ir complexes (Figure 4).3 While we expected to see a marked increase
in the radiative rates on bringing the CT into the picture, we did not observe this at all. We have
investigated the theoretical models for these complexes and believe that the problem is a poor S 1
rate. In a TADF process, like the one expected for the CT states here, the energy between the S 1
and T1 states is important as well as the radiative rate out of S1. These complexes have the requisite
small DEST for fast and efficient TADF, but this has come at the expense of the S1 rates. The
oscillator strengths of the S1 states of the triazine substituted complexes are < 0.01. Thus, while
the CT state contributes for both complexes, even in toluene, the “normal” MLCT T 1 emission is
the dominant process. When the CT is lowered in energy it acts as a nonradiative trap, leading to

 10

the poor PLQY. This is a disappointing result as it suggests that we will see this same effect in
other materials designed to have ZFS > DEST. We believe that for this approach to work we need
systems with the S1 oscillator strength > 0.1, which seems highly unlikely. Without that we do not
believe that we will see a benefit to pursuing ZFS > DEST.

Task 4.2: Demonstrate device operational lifetimes extended by PF.:

We have investigated binuclear complexes in the hope of achieving a higher radiative rate for
emission due to exciton coupling of the two potentially independent excitons formed by excitation
from the two carbazoles to the core carbene.4 Based on the spectroscopic measurements we have
determined that if exciton coupling is active in these materials is exceedingly weak. Interestingly,
we do achieve a very high radiative rate and have related that to a small DEST. We believe that the
reason exciton coupling is not observed here is that the two carbazoles are independent and can
rotate about the metal-carbazole bond. This rotation can alter the energy of the charge transfer
transition and leads to exciton localization on the lower energy conformer. The use of the Janus
biscarbene appears to limit the binuclear complexes to green and lower energy emission.

Task 5.2: Symmetric-binuclear,  -extended cMa compounds:

Achieve increased WOLED stability employing metalorganic phosphors and TADF molecules
and binuclear complexes in different cavities to decrease radiative lifetimes. (18-24 mo); Use
materials and designs for stacked, graded OLEDs to achieve T70 = 10,000 h starting at a
luminance of L0 = 3000 cd/m2, EQE >12%. (13-24 mo)

We have shown extended lifetime of blue OLED components employing heavy-metal phosphors
including Ir(cb)3 and Ir(dmp)3 in the several microcavities. We achieved 4x lifetime
enhancement, high EQE and flexible color tunability in these devices. The related work is
submitted to a peer-reviewed journal.

We have discovered plasmon-exciton-polaritons (PEPs) on metal/BPyTP2 surfaces which
increases the Purcell factor and device stability. In Fig. 5.2.1, we show the principle of improving
the device performance by using Ag/DBR cavity devices and PEPs. The PEPs increase Purcell
factor, while the DBR increases EQE and tuning color of emission. The angle-resolved
reflectance shows the presence and the optical density of states (ODoS) of PEPs on Al/BPyTP2
and Ag/BPyTP2 interface. The rich ODoS of PEPs enables efficient coupling of triplet energy
(2.5-3.0 eV, 400-500 nm) to PEPs and thereby increases Purcell factor and device lifetime.

 We have compared the PL lifetimes and device lifetimes for blue phosphors in Ag/thin ETL half
cavities, Ag/thin ETL/DBR full cavities (Ag/DBR) and Ag/thin ETL/ITO/Ag/ITO full cavities
(Ag/IAI). Figure 5.2.2 and Table 5.2.1 show the decreased radiative lifetime and extracted
Purcell factor of the 30-50nm EML in these microcavities. Here we chose the highly efficient
phosphor Ir(cb)3, whose PLQY reaches >85% in the 1:1 mixed mCBP:SiTrzCz2, to minimize
non-radiative dissipation. Note that since the vertical dipoles are nearly 100% coupled to the
plasmonic modes, the detectors can only observe the outcoupled signal from horizontal dipoles.
Therefore, extracted Purcell factors mainly account for horizontal dipoles, and
underestimate the Purcell factors of the structures. For an isotropic dipole moment
orientation, as in the case of homoleptic Ir-complexes, the horizontal dipole moment takes up
66.7% on average while the vertical dipole moment takes up 33.3%. The highest Purcell factor of

Task 7: Exploring manufacturability of cavities and materials for long lifetime

Task 7.1;
Use the proposed model to evaluate the economic/manufacturing expectations using the state-of-
the-art commercial white OLEDs.

Background of the study:
Previously we utilized plasmon-exciton-polaritons (PEPs), which form via strong coupling
between the singlet exciton in the electron transporting layer (ETL) and the silver surface
plasmon polariton (SPP) modes. By tuning the optical properties of the electron transporting
layer adjacent to the metal surface, we can effectively manipulate PEP characteristics to achieve
an enhanced Purcell effect in OLEDs. Previously, we demonstrated that leveraging the PEP-
enhanced Purcell effect can improve the operational stability of blue phosphorescent OLEDs.
Our primary focus was on the development of high efficiency and long lifetime pure blue
phosphorescent OLEDs. Achieving superior device performance in this challenging spectral
region requires innovative engineering at the materials and device architecture levels. In
particular, we sought to explore and identify new electron transporting layer (ETL) materials
able to support the formation of plasmon-exciton-polariton (PEP) modes in combination with a
silver (Ag) cathode.
Based on our previous findings, we came up with the following design guidelines for polaritonic
ETL selection in blue phosphorescent OLEDs: (1) a high singlet extinction coefficient and
oscillator strength for maximizing coupling with plasmonic modes; (2) an absorption onset
wavelength shorter than the emission wavelength to prevent emission re-absorption; and (3)
careful matching of peak absorption and oscillator strength to ensure the Purcell factor meets the
threshold for enhancing device efficiency. Matching these properties makes it more likely that
we can take advantage of PEP effects to make high effiejncy and long lifetime blue OLEDs.

Description of research work:
To achieve our objectives, we systematically screened a range of ETL candidates, prioritizing
materials that combine high electron mobility with strong singlet absorption characteristics.
Strong light-matter coupling key for effective PEP mode formation demands ETL materials with
large singlet extinction coefficients and high oscillator strengths, especially in spectral regions
close to Ag-supported surface plasmon polariton (SPP) resonances. Additionally, we ensured that
candidate materials possessed an absorption onset at a shorter wavelength than the blue emission,

 33

suppressing re-absorption losses that can otherwise diminish device efficiency. Our screening
process included both commercially available and newly synthesized organic compounds.
Initial photophysical measurements (Figure 1) and theoretical calculations of PEP dispersion
indicate that 2DPTrz-ICz is a promising candidate for a polaritonic ETL material. As
demonstrated in Figures 2 and 3, the PEP dispersion exhibits a clear anti-crossing behavior
between the Al surface plasmon polariton (SPP) mode and the 2DPTrz-ICz singlet exciton at 390
nm, providing strong evidence for PEP formation.

After confirming that 2DPTrz-ICz satisfies all the key prerequisites for a polaritonic ETL
including good mobility, suitable absorption properties, and appropriate energy alignment—we
fabricated bottom-emitting blue phosphorescent OLEDs incorporating 2DPTrz-ICz as the ETL
and Ag cathode. For direct comparison, we also constructed reference devices using the
established polaritonic ETL BPyTP2. The current density-voltage-luminance (JVL)
characteristics of the devices with both the new and reference ETLs are presented in Figure 5.
The OLEDs with 2DPTrz-ICz showed a maximum external quantum efficiency (EQE) of 18 ±
0.5%, which is notably higher than the 16 ± 0.5% measured for devices with BPyTP2. However,
devices with 2DPTrz-ICz displayed relatively lower luminance compared to the reference and
this could be to the higher resistance nature of 2DPTrz-ICz, as also observed in the JV curves.
We also assessed the operational stability of the devices at a constant current density of 7
mA/cm2. The initial luminance measured was 1803 cd/m² for BPyTP2/Ag and 2050 cd/m² for
2DPTrz-ICz /Ag. Notably, 2DPTrz-ICz-based OLEDs exhibited a slight improvement in
operational stability relative to the reference devices.
Future work:

In summary, 2DPTrz-ICz has demonstrated significant promise as a polaritonic ETL material for
blue phosphorescent OLEDs, with notable gains in efficiency and operational stability. Moving
forward, we will focus on mitigating its resistive characteristics to enhance luminance while
preserving the improvements in device performance. In the upcoming quarter, our efforts will
center on further optimizing 2DPTrz-ICz and evaluating additional polaritonic ETL candidates.
These ongoing developments are expected to advance the realization of longer-lasting, highly
efficient blue phosphorescent OLEDs marking a substantial step forward for OLED technology.
