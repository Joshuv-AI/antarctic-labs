 SECTION 1.0
 INTRODUCTION

 One of the primary roles of waste pretreatment at the Hanford Tank Waste Treatment and
Immobilization Plant (WTP) is to separate the majority of the radioactive components from the
majority of the non-radioactive components in retrieved tank wastes, producing a high level
waste (HLW) stream and a low activity waste (LAW) stream. This separation process is a key
element in the overall strategy to reduce the volume of HLW that requires vitrification and
subsequent disposal in a national deep geological repository for high level nuclear waste. After
removal of the radioactive constituents, the LAW stream, which has a much larger volume but
smaller fraction of radioactivity than the HLW stream, will be immobilized and disposed of in
near-surface facilities at the Hanford site.

 Pretreatment to prepare the LAW stream at Hanford will include removal of insoluble
solids by ultrafiltration and removal of 137Cs by ion exchange from the tank waste supernate,
with the separated solids and 137Cs sent to HLW vitrification. Spherical resorcinol formaldehyde
(sRF) is the baseline ion exchange resin selected for cesium removal at the WTP pretreatment
facility [1]. The sRF resin has undergone extensive testing for treating Hanford tank waste. It is
an organic ion exchange resin that can be regenerated by elution with a dilute acid, which
permits multiple loading and elution cycles. Although the disposal path for the spent sRF resin
has not been finalized, the eluted resin can be disposed of as solid waste without contributing to
the HLW volume. Based on its use in the WTP pretreatment facility, the sRF resin was selected
as the baseline for the direct feed LAW (DFLAW) system; however, it is now expected that
crystalline silicotitanate (CST), a non-elutable inorganic ion exchange media will be used in a
Tank Side Cesium Removal (TSCR) system. Under the DFLAW flow-sheet, the WTP
pretreatment facility will be bypassed in order to permit earlier start-up of LAW vitrification.
The sRF resin has also been considered for use in Small Column Ion Exchange (SCIX) for the
proposed supplemental LAW pretreatment [2, 3].

 Although elutable organic resins have certain advantages, subjecting organic materials to
radiation fields and high cumulative exposures can potentially alter their physical properties and
cause loss of mechanical strength [4]. Possible radiation induced generation of gases, especially
hydrogen, during long-term storage or disposal of organic ion-exchange resin is another concern
[4]. Inorganic ion-exchange media are generally more resistant to the effects of radiation. Zeolite
based media have been used successfully for cesium (and strontium/plutonium) removal at the
West Valley Demonstration Project (WVDP) [5].

 The Savannah River Site (SRS) has also developed plans to deploy a SCIX system using
inorganic ion-exchangers as a supplementary technology to augment the baseline Salt Waste
Processing Facility (SWPF) capability [6]. The SRS selected CST to remove Cs and
monosodium titanate (MST) to remove strontium and actinides from salt solution, with the spent
media to be transferred and blended with a sludge batch for treatment at the Defense Waste
Processing Facility (DWPF); however, implementation of SCIX was suspended due to funding

 9

constraints [7, 8]. Unlike the sRF resin, most zeolite based ion exchange media are non-elutable
and require disposal as HLW after loading. For example, the spent zeolite at WVDP was mixed
with tank sludge before vitrification into borosilicate glass [9]. The impacts of SCIX spent media
on DWPF glass formulations have also been investigated [10] while vitrification of 137Cs loaded
CST for disposal has been demonstrated [11]. In a test program, liquid waste from the Melton
Valley Storage Tanks at Oak Ridge was treated with CST [12] before the loaded sorbent was
vitrified. The demonstration processed about 20 kg of Cs loaded CST [11, 12].

 In previous work, glass formulations with CST (IONSIV® R9120-B) and synthetic
chabazite (IONSIV® R9160-G) have been developed, both alone and in combination with WTP
HLW streams [13]. Glass formulations with waste loadings as high as 70% with CST and
chabazite alone were achieved while meeting all WTP processing and product quality
constraints. In particular, these glasses did not precipitate significant secondary phases upon heat
treatment, which indicated that even higher waste loadings may be achievable. Glasses were also
developed with CST and chabazite combined with fully washed HLW solids previously
evaluated as part of the HLW direct feed option under consideration for early WTP operations
[14]. Overall, the test results demonstrate the ability to incorporate the ion-exchange media (CST
or chabazite) at reasonable waste loadings with Hanford HLW streams in acceptable glass
formulations. In the case of CST, the impacts of introducing considerable levels of Nb2O5 and
TiO2 into HLW glasses will require further investigation. This is highlighted by the fact that the
WTP baseline HLW composition-property models were developed for a glass compositional
region with no Nb2O5 and < 1.2 wt% TiO2, whereas > 3 wt% Nb2O5 and > 5 wt% TiO2 can be
expected in glasses loaded with CST. In particular, TiO2 can have potentially important effects
on spinel T1% since the crystallized spinel phases were found to incorporate significant amounts
of titanium. Crucible scale vitrification of the spent media showed good melting characteristics
and the X-ray diffraction (XRD) results of the resulting glasses showed that they were crystal
free. Vertical gradient furnace (VGF) melt rate screening tests of feeds containing spent CST and
chabazite showed conversion of a CST feed to be moderately fast and that of a chabazite feed to
be very fast, suggesting that processing these media is not likely to limit the glass production
rates. However, to date, there have been no melter tests with these formulations, which is the
subject of the present work. This report presents test results from the vitrification of these
compositions with CST and chabazite using a continuously fed DM100 melter system installed
at the Vitreous State Laboratory (VSL).

1.1 Test Objectives

 The primary objective of these tests outlined in the Test Plan for this work [15] and
described herein was to demonstrate the vitrification of ion exchange media for cesium removal,
either alone or in combination with a representative Hanford HLW stream. This included one
formulation with chabazite as the only waste component and two formulations containing direct-
feed fully washed AY-102 HLW solids combined with either CST or chabazite. Feed processing
characteristics, glass production rate, melter emissions, and feed carryover were determined for
each of the three feed compositions. This was accomplished through VGF and DM100 melter
system testing.

1.3 DM100 Melter System

        1.3.1    DM100 Feed System

 A schematic diagram of the DM100 vitrification system is shown in Figure 1.1. The
melter feed is introduced in batches into a feed container that is mounted on a load cell for
weight monitoring. The feed is stirred with a variable speed mixer and constantly recirculated
except for periodic, momentary interruptions during which the weight is recorded. A peristaltic
pump is used in order to provide a uniform delivery of feed to the melt surface. Feed is directed
from the recirculation loop that extends to the top of the melter and then diverted to the
peristaltic pump, which regulates the flow of feed through a Teflon-lined feed line and
water-cooled feed tube into the melter.

 1.3.2 Melter System

 Cross-sectional diagrams of the DM100-BL melter are shown in Figures 1.2.a-c. The
DM100-BL unit is a ceramic refractory-lined melter fitted with five electrodes: two pairs of
opposing Inconel 690 plate electrodes and a bottom electrode. Power can be supplied in either
three-phase or single-phase configurations. All of the tests in the present work were performed
with the upper and lower electrodes on each side connected together and powered by a
single-phase supply; the bottom electrode was not powered. Melt pool agitation is achieved by
either a removable lance entering from the top of the melter or a permanent bubbler installed
through the bottom electrode. In these tests, the lance bubbler was used. The glass product is
removed from the melter by means of an airlift discharge system. The melter has a melt surface

 11

area of 0.108 m 2 and a variable glass inventory of between 110 kg, when only the bottom pair of
 P P

electrodes is used, and about 170 kg when both pairs of electrodes are used, which was the case
in the present tests.

 1.3.3 Off-Gas System

 For operational simplicity, the DM100-BL is equipped with a dry off-gas treatment
system involving gas filtration operations only. Exhaust gases leave the melter plenum through a
film cooler device that minimizes the formation of solid deposits. The film-cooler air has
constant flow rate and its temperature is thermostatically controlled. Consequently, under
steady-state operating conditions, the exhaust gases passing through the transition line (between
the melter and the first filtration device) can be sampled at constant temperature and airflow rate.
The geometry of the transition line conforms to the requirements of the 40-CFR-60 air sampling
techniques. Immediately downstream of the transition line are cyclonic filters followed by
conventional pre-filters and high efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters. The temperature of the
cyclonic filters is maintained above 150°C while the temperatures in the HEPAs are kept
sufficiently high to prevent moisture condensation. The entire train of gas filtration operations is
duplicated and each train is used alternately. An induced draft fan completes the system.

1.4 Experimental Procedures and Methods

 1.4.1 Feed Conversion by Vertical Gradient Furnace (VGF) Testing

 Figure 1.3 shows a schematic diagram of the VGF setup. The temperature gradient inside
the VGF is maintained by two separate sets of heating elements, both of which are arranged in
cylindrical form and aligned along their axes. The inner heater is set at 1150ºC, which is the
nominal temperature of the glass pool, and the ambient heater is set at 600ºC, which is similar to
the melter plenum temperature. A ceramic crucible (4 inches tall) is used to contain the reacting
melter feed. The temperature gradient in the furnace is shown in Figure 1.4. For a typical feed
conversion test, 10 grams of glass of identical chemical composition to the test feed (expressed
on an oxide basis) is preheated in the ceramic crucible positioned in the inner heater before the
dried melter feed (to yield 20 grams of glass) is introduced. Feed reactions under the controlled
temperature gradient are allowed to continue for the designated test duration (typically, from 5 to
60 minutes) and then stopped by rapid cooling in room temperature air. The top surface of the
reacted feed material is then inspected and photographed. The crucibles with their feed contents
are then cross-sectioned to reveal the conversion progress of feed blends. The saw cuts of the
crucibles are performed dry (without lubricant) to avoid loss of any soluble material.

 To characterize the reacted feed material, visual inspection and digital imaging of the top
(by photography) and cross section (using an optical scanner) of the reacted sample are
performed. The results are assessed by comparison to results obtained previously from a wide
range of other feeds that have known processing rates from continuous melter testing.

 1.4.2 Feed Samples from Melter Tests

 Feed samples were taken directly from as received drums and the melter feed
recirculation line during each test. Feed samples were poured into a platinum/gold crucible and
placed into a programmed furnace for drying and fusion to form a glass. The glass produced
from this fusion is ground to less than 200 mesh and sealed in 20-ml vials for subsequent
analysis by X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy (XRF), or by acid digestion followed by
direct-current plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (DCP-AES) on the resulting solution. The
feed samples are also characterized for their density, pH, water content, and glass yield.

 1.4.3 Glass Product

 The glass product is discharged from the melter into 5-gallon steel pails periodically
using an air-lift system. The discharged product glass was sampled at the end of each test by
removing sufficient glass from the top of the cans for compositional analysis and secondary
phase determination. Sample preparation for chemical analysis typically involves size reduction
and sieving. All samples were subjected to XRF analysis to determine the concentrations of all
elements except boron and lithium. A series of National Institute of Standards and Technology
(NIST) reference materials were used for confirmation of the XRF data. Boron and lithium were
determined by total acid dissolution of ground glass samples in HF/HNO3 and subjecting the
resulting solutions to DCP-AES analysis. All of these procedures are routinely conducted at VSL
and, therefore, standard operating procedures are in place.

 Secondary phases in the glass samples were determined by optical microscopy and
Scanning Electron Microscopy- Energy Dispersive X-Ray Spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) together
with XRD. Secondary phases due to crystallization and phase separation can be identified using
these methods. Quantitative determination of the amount of crystals in glass samples were made
by SEM in conjunction with image analysis.

 1.4.4 Melter Emission Samples

 Melter emission fluxes were measured to complete the mass balance for melter tests with
each composition. Isokinetic melter exhaust samples (exhaust gas flow velocity equal to velocity
through the gas sample probe tip) were combined with the Fourier Transform Infrared
Spectroscopy (FTIR) continuous monitoring data for gaseous species to characterize fluxes from
the melter. The off-gas sampling port used for isokinetic sampling is located prior to filtration in
the transition line between the two temperature monitors, whereas the sample for FTIR analysis
is pulled after HEPA filtration (Figure 1.1). Standard Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)
isokinetic off-gas sampling trains and methods (EPA Methods 1A, 2, 4, 5, 26, 29), composed of
particulate filters and liquid impingers, were used to collect materials that were subjected to
chemical and physical analyses.

 SECTION 2.0
 WASTE SIMULANTS AND GLASS FORMULATIONS

 Three different waste streams were used for the present melter tests; they include (i)
synthetic zeolite (after cesium removal from LAW); (ii) synthetic zeolite blended with fully
washed AY-102 HLW solids; and (iii) crystalline silicotitanate (CST) blended with fully washed
AY-102 HLW solids. This section describes the compositions of the selected waste streams, the
corresponding simulants, and the target glasses.

2.1 Waste Compositions and Simulated Wastes

 Previous work at VSL evaluated the HLW direct feed option under consideration for
early WTP operations [14]. Extension of that work was subsequently performed to include glass
formulation development for the direct feed waste blended with selected inorganic ion-exchange
media from LAW pretreatment (Cs removal) [13]. A limited number of glass formulations were
also developed and tested to process the ion-exchange media alone with no HLW blending. The
present tests employed the blended wastes and simulants defined in those earlier studies.

 2.1.1 HLW Simulant

 In support of the HLW direct feed option being considered for early WTP operations,
glass formulation and melter testing was performed with blends of solids and supernate that
might be produced from HLW direct feed processing of wastes from Hanford tank AY-102 [14].
The composition of the AY-102 HLW simulant selected for the present tests is identical to that
tested for HLW direct feed [14]. Four blends of direct feed simulants (i.e., AY-102 HLW and
AY-102 LAW) were considered to support the earlier testing, each of which represents different
blending of the solids and supernate fractions that corresponded to various extents of washing of
the solids to remove the soluble fraction. The end-members of this series of compositions are the
fully washed solids and the pure supernate. The only waste stream tested in the present work is
the fully washed solids with no LAW contribution. It is assumed that settling of the fully washed
solids produces a suspension containing 15 wt% un-dissolved solids.

 The composition of the AY-102 fully washed solids is based on the inventory data from
the Best Basis Inventory (BBI) Hanford Tank Waste Operations Simulator (HTWOS) model run
from April 17 of 2012. After application of wash factors to the AY-102 solids [18, 19], the
simulant comprises nearly 50 component oxides; they are listed in Table 2.1. In order to maintain
a manageable number of components and to avoid the use of radioactive materials, all minor
components (i.e., < 0.1 wt%), radioactive oxides, and noble metals were omitted form the
definition of the HLW simulant. The abridged HLW simulant composition contains 99.1 wt% of
the original oxides and the final AY-102 HLW simulant composition was then obtained by

 14

renormalization. Table 2.2 presents the simulant composition in terms of both wt% non-volatile
oxides and the volatile components (expressed in g/100 oxides). Although this waste is not
leached (caustic leaching has been considered as a pretreatment option), the simulant
composition in Table 2.2 remains typical of many HLW simulants tested in that it is high in
Fe2O3 and Al2O3; these two oxides together account for over 60 wt% of the waste. The other
significant oxides in the AY-102 simulant include Na2O, SiO2, and MnO.

 2.1.2 Inorganic Ion-Exchange Media

 The earlier work evaluated three inorganic ion-exchange media that were chosen based
on available data and projected compatibility with HLW vitrification [13]. They included CST
(commercially available as IONSIV R9120-B), a synthetic zeolite (IONSIV R9160-G, which has
a chemical composition similar to that of natural sodium chabazite), and a naturally occurring
zeolite (clinoptilolite). Cesium removal testing from a selected LAW simulant (AN-105)
identified CST to have superior performance when compared to the other two media, exhibiting
a high capacity for Cs sorption but relatively slow kinetics. By contrast, the synthetic zeolite
R9160-G showed poorer performances with the LAW simulant, even though the zeolite IE-96
(similar to R9160-G) was successfully employed at the West Valley Demonstration Project.
Deterioration of the effectiveness of the synthetic zeolite in removing Cs is attributed to the high
pH of the simulant [20]. Finally, natural clinoptilolite performed more poorly than the synthetic
chabazite and is not suited to processing Hanford LAW. Acceptable glass formulations were
subsequently developed and tested to process [13]:

 • the ion-exchange media CST (IONSIV R9120) alone,
 • synthetic chabazite (IONSIV R9160) alone,
 • blends of AY-102 HLW solids with CST, and
 • blends of AY-102 HLW solids with synthetic chabazite.

The present work evaluated test glass formulations for all of the waste streams listed above
except CST by itself. Melter testing of the glass formulation to process CST alone was not
performed because of the high cost of CST.

 The chemical compositions of R9120-B and R9160-G were analyzed by XRF. Analysis
of these two media was also performed on solution samples, obtained by microwave-assisted
digestion, using DCP-AES to provide information on the concentrations of boron and lithium,
which were not measured by XRF. Both boron and lithium were determined to be present in
R9120-B and R9160-G at < 0.1 wt% [13]. Table 2.3 reproduces the XRF analytical results. After
omitting the minor components and renormalizing, the resulting compositions for R9120-B and
R9160-G formed the bases for glass formulation development [13]. They are given in Table 2.4.
Table 2.4 also provides the loss-on-ignition (LOI) data measured for R9120-B and R9160-G as
received; these data are required for waste blending and glass formulation development.

2.2 Glass Formulations

 Glass formulations were developed and tested using the compositional bases listed in
Table 2.4 for the inorganic ion-exchangers and Table 2.2 for the AY-102 HLW solids [14].

 The target formulation tested for synthetic chabazite vitrification is HLW-ZEO-01. Table
2.5 presents the waste loading, glass former additives, and target composition of HLW-ZEO-01.
Since many of the major components in R9160 are also glass forming oxides, this glass has a
relatively high waste loading of 70 wt%. Note that the waste loading is given on an oxide basis
and will be different from the corresponding value based on material weights due to the water
content of the zeolite (see Table 2.4). The characterization data collected for HLW-ZEO-01
showed that the target formulation meets all applicable processing and product quality
constraints for HLW glasses [13]. In particular, heat treatment between 850°C and 900°C did not
result in any secondary phases, while the PCT performance was better than that of the
benchmark DWPF-EA glass. The viscosity and electrical conductivity of the glass melt were also
within their respective acceptable processing ranges. Table 2.5 summarizes the measured
properties of HLW-ZEO-01.

 Additional formulations were tested for the vitrification of IONSIV R9160 blended with
AY-102 HLW solids with HLW waste loadings between 30 and 35 wt% and chabazite loadings
between 15 and 32 wt% [13]. The target formulation selected for the present testing is
HLW-IXC1-04, which has a total waste loading of 50 wt%, including 30 wt% of HLW solids
and 20 wt% of chabazite. The oxide contributions in HLW-IXC1-04 from the different waste
streams and additives are given in Table 2.6. With the blended AY-102 HLW solids, the Fe2O3
content in HLW-IXC1-04 is considerably higher than that in HLW-ZEO-01 and heat treatments
of this glass between 850°C and 1000°C resulted in varying amounts of spinel crystals (e.g., 0.68
vol% after 70 hours at 950°C). Nevertheless, all the measured glass properties, which are given
in Table 2.6, show that HLW-IXC1-04 is an acceptable glass formulation for melter testing.

 The final target glass composition tested was formulated to process AY-102 HLW solids
blended with CST (IONSIV R9120). The chemical composition of IONSIV R9120 in Table 2.3
suggests that the primary impact on HLW glass compositions upon CST addition is the increase
of TiO2, followed by Nb2O5 and ZrO2. The major influence of increased TiO2 concentration is
expected to be on crystallization behavior in the HLW glasses, with lesser impacts on other
properties such as viscosity and durability. The target glass selected is HLW-IXC4-05. The
waste loading and composition of HLW-IXC4-05 are given in Table 2.7 together with the glass
characterization data. This glass has a total waste loading of 36 wt%, including 31 wt% of
AY-102 HLW solids and 5 wt% of CST. It incorporates moderate amounts of TiO2 (1.85 wt%)
and Nb2O5 (1.16 wt%); however, they both exceed the compositional region for which the WTP
baseline HLW composition-property models were developed (no Nb2O5 and < 1.2 wt% TiO2).
The primary crystalline phase that resulted from heat treatment of this glass was spinel (e.g.,
0.08 vol% after 70 hours at 950°C and 0.35 vol% at 850°C). Examination of the spinel crystals
by scanning electron microscopy/energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDS) showed the
presence of titanium.

 To evaluate the melt rates of the glass formulations designed for processing only the

 DM100 MELTER OPERATIONS

 Melter tests were conducted on the DM100-BL vitrification system between 8/28/17 and
 9/15/17 with ion exchange media (chabazite and CST) and simulated HLW AY-102 waste

 Additional testing was conducted while optimizing bubbling rate to determine the maximum
 sustainable feed rate with feed containing chabazite. Tests were conducted with the

 Attempts were made to replicate the melter configuration and operating conditions used for

 cap, which is between 80-95% melt surface coverage for the DM100 since a 100% cold cap
 tends to lead to "bridging" in smaller melters. The bubbling rate was either fixed at 9 lpm or

 The feed and glass were processed without significant difficulties throughout the majority
of the tests. Cold cap conditions were largely similar to the range of conditions observed in many

 FEED SAMPLE AND GLASS PRODUCT ANALYSIS

4.1 Analysis of Feed Samples
 4.1.1 General Properties

 Feed samples were taken during each melter test from either an inline sampling port or
directly from the feed tank. Sample names and measured properties are given in Table 4.1.
Density, pH, water content, glass conversion ratio, and oxide composition by XRF and DCP
were measured on all samples; VGF tests were conducted on one dried feed sample from each
feed composition. The analysis shows the intended changes in water content, glass yield, and
density as a result of modifications to the as-received feed and addition of the ion exchange
media. Conversely, analysis of samples from feeds made with only chabazite and the additives
boric acid, lithium carbonate, and sodium carbonate appeared to be eleven to eighteen percent
low in solids content and correspondingly high in water. This observation is contradicted by the
amount of glass produced from feed during melter testing, which showed virtually the same
processing rate calculated from feed consumed and glass discharged at nominal bubbling rates
(see Table 3.1). Measured deficiencies in the amount of solids in the HLW-ZEO-01 feed samples
are probably due to the difficulty in obtaining a representative sample from a feed that consists of
a granular media in a dilute aqueous solution. Undoubtedly, a fraction of the chabazite was not
included in the samples due to a sampling bias against the granular media. The average measured
glass conversion ratios for the two feed samples from melter tests with the HLW-IXC1-04 and
HLW-IXC4-05 glass compositions were within six and a half and five percent of the target on a
weight per weight basis, respectively, validating the use of the target conversion ratio for
calculating glass production rates. The water content, density, glass yield, and pH varied within a
narrow range for the feed samples for each of the melter feed compositions. As expected, feed
with the HLW AY-102 simulant, which contains hydroxides, had higher measured pH than feed
with only chabazite as the waste component.

 4.1.2 Chemical Composition

 The methods used for analysis of feed sample chemical compositions are described in
Section 1.4. The boron and lithium oxide concentrations from the DCP-AES analysis were used
for normalizing the XRF data since their concentrations were not determined by XRF. The
analyzed compositions of the as-received, and melter test feeds are compared to the target
compositions in Tables 4.2 - 4.4 for each glass composition. The results from the melter feed
samples generally show agreement with the target composition and corroborate the consistency
of the feed for the major elements except for low sample bias for the ion exchange media. Any
disparities in the HLW-ZEO-01 and HLW-IXC4-05 target concentrations and the analyzed
concentrations are attributable to the segregation of the ion exchange media during sampling. For
the HLW-ZEO-01 composition, all elements associated with the chabazite were measured below

target concentrations with corresponding high bias for the additives present in solution. Also,
there was a significant difference between the two samples analyzed, indicating differing
proportions of chabazite in the two samples analyzed. None of these deviations were observed in

 4.1.3 VGF Feed Testing

 The potential melt rate of feed from each of the three formulations was evaluated at the
crucible scale using a VGF, as described in Section 1.4.1. Dried melter feed was tested for 15
and 30 minutes, as shown in Figure 4.1, and compared to other feeds with HLW AY-102
composition previously tested, as given in Table 4.5. All the feeds with the ion exchange media
were very fast melting with all feed being converted to glass in 30 minutes resulting in the
highest rank of 1. This suggests that if the feeds used in the melter test contained less water,
processing rates would be significantly higher, and that the high water content of the feeds used
in the melter tests was a significant factor in limiting the glass production rates. The same result
for the HLW-ZEO-01 composition was previously obtained on a feed sample made at the
crucible scale during glass formulation testing [13]. VGF testing also showed that the feeds with
the ion exchange media are inherently faster melting than direct feed compositions with HLW
AY-102 [14]. The faster melting rates may be attributable to the ion exchange media providing a
source of silica that has a lower melting point than the quartz commonly used as the source of
most of the silica.

4.2 Analysis of Glass Samples

 Over a metric ton of glass was produced in these tests. The glass was discharged from the
DM100 periodically into 5-gallon carbon steel pails using an air lift system. The discharged
product glass was sampled by removing sufficient glass from the top of each pail for total
inorganic analysis. Product glass masses and discharge date are given in Tables 4.6 – 4.8. Glass
samples were also taken by inserting a threaded metal rod directly into the glass pool. These
“dip” samples serve to document the composition of the glass pool before and after each test. No
macroscopic secondary phases were observed in any of the discharged glasses and dip glass
samples.

 4.2.1 Compositional Analysis of Discharge and Dip Sample Glasses

 All discharge glass samples were crushed and analyzed directly by XRF. Since boron and
lithium are not determined by XRF, boron and lithium concentrations were calculated from the
measured concentration in the glass pool prior to testing, the target concentrations and the
nominal glass volume of the melter. The XRF analyzed compositions of discharged and dip glass
samples are provided in Tables 4.9 - 4.12. A comparison of analyzed discharge glasses with their
respective target compositions is provided in Tables 4.13 – 4.15. The majority of the XRF
analysis results from glass discharged at the end of each test compare favorably to their
corresponding target values with any significant deviations resulting from incomplete turnover of
constituents that had a very different concentration at the beginning of the test. Accordingly, the
average glass composition discharged during each test was farther from the target composition
since it represents an intermediate point between the glass composition at the start of each test
and the final glass discharged during each test. The only oxides with a target concentration
greater than one weight percent that showed greater than 10% deviation from the target value
from glass discharged at the end of processing the HLW-ZEO-01 composition was iron with an
absolute surplus of only 0.33 wt%, and from glass discharged at the end of processing the
HLW-IXC1-04 composition was manganese with an absolute surplus of only 0.31 wt%, and iron
which transitioned from about three percent towards a target of over eleven and a half percent.
Larger deviations from target were observed for the last glass discharged while processing the
HLW-IXC4-05 glass composition due to there being only a little more than one turnover of the
glass pool during the test and constituents unique to the CST in the glass. Comparison of the last
glass discharged to a glass composition calculated from the target composition, the measured
composition in the melt pool at the start of the test, and the amount of glass produced during the
test (203 kg) shows that all oxides are within about ten percent of their target concentrations
while niobium and titanium are within about one percent of their target concentrations. The
analysis of discharge glasses more closely approximate the target compositions than the analysis
of feed samples with no under representation of ion exchange media evident in feed samples (see
Section 4.1.2). While processing the HLW-ZEO-01 composition, bismuth, chlorine, chromium,
manganese, nickel, phosphorus, lead, tungsten, strontium, zirconium, and zinc were measured in
the product glass at low concentrations despite not being included in the target compositions as a
result of them being present in the melt pool prior to these tests and being present in the feed as a
contaminant. Despite not being included in the HLW- IXC1-04 and HLW-IXC4-05 target
compositions, bismuth, chlorine, tungsten, strontium, zirconium, and zinc were measured in the

 23

product glass at lower concentrations (0.01 – 0.05 wt% oxide). None of these deviations are
expected to affect the processing properties of glass during testing.

 Compositional trends for selected constituents shown in Figures 4.2.a-4.2.m illustrate the
approach of the majority of the glass constituents to the target compositions over the course of
the tests. The composition of the glass in the melter prior to the testing reflects the
HLW-ZEO-01 composition as a result of melt pool turnover with chabazite, boric acid, lithium
carbonate, and sodium carbonate. However, at the onset of the present tests, increases in silicon
and sodium are observed at the expense of aluminum and elements not in the target composition
such as manganese, chromium, and potassium. During the transition from the HLW-ZEO-01 to
the HLW- IXC1-04 composition, elements in the AY-102 HLW such as iron, manganese,
phosphorus, cerium, and lead increase in concentration at the expense of additives boron,
lithium, sodium, and silicon. During the transition from the HLW-IXC1-04 to the
HLW-IXC4-05 composition, niobium, titanium, and zirconium, which are mainly unique to
CST, increase in concentration at the expense of additives boron and aluminum. The AY-102
HLW loading in the two glasses is about the same so elements in this waste continue to move
closer to their target concentrations while processing the HLW-IXC4-05 composition. Calcium
concentrations in the glass product increase over the course of testing even though the target
concentrations decrease due to dilution of the AY-102 waste simulant. Conversely, potassium
and magnesium decrease in concentration over the course of the tests with the decreasing target
concentrations, as expected. Chromium and nickel increase in concentration after the idle period
following the test processing the HLW-ZEO-01 composition as a result of corrosion of the
refractory and Inconel melter components. Increases in chromium concentration in glass are
common in high alkali LAW glasses [14, 44, 45], which is likely the reason why it was observed
after processing the HLW-ZEO-01 composition in a glass pool with about sixteen percent soda
but not after processing the HLW- IXC1-04 composition, which contained about thirteen percent
soda.

 4.2.2 SEM Analysis of Melter Glass Samples

 Melt pool samples from before and after each of the tests were subjected to SEM and
XRD analysis to determine the extent of crystal formation. The results are summarized in Table
4.16. Minimal crystalline phases were detected by SEM in the samples and measured amounts
decreased over the course of testing. No crystal phases were detected by XRD. The highest
concentration of crystalline phases was 0.2 volume percent after the turnover and appears to be
residual from preceding tests. The HLW-ZEO-01 composition does not contain chromium,
nickel, or manganese, which are elements in the Mn-Ni-Fe-chromite crystals observed. Also,
chromite as a waste component was fed to the melter in the preceding test series, resulting in
significant amounts of chromite spinel deposition on the melter floor [46]. The lack of significant
crystalline phases in these melter glasses confirms observations made during the development of
these glass formulations [13].

 SECTION 5.0
 MONITORED OFF-GAS EMISSIONS

5.1 Particulate Sampling

 The melter exhaust was sampled for metals/particles according to 40-CFR-60 Methods 3,
5, and 29 at steady-state operating conditions and nominal bubbling during each of the three
tests. The concentrations of off-gas species that are present as particulates and gaseous species
that are collected in impinger solutions were derived from laboratory data on solutions extracted
from air samples (filters and various solutions) together with measurements of the volume of air
sampled. Particulate collection required isokinetic sampling, which entails removing gas from
the exhaust at the same velocity that the air is flowing in the duct (40-CFR-60, Methods 1-5).
Typically, a sample size of 30 dscf was taken at a rate of between 0.5 and 0.75 dscfm. Total
particulate loading was determined by combining gravimetric analysis of the standard particle
filter and chemical analysis of probe rinse solutions. An additional impinger containing 2 N
NaOH was added to the sampling train to ensure complete scrubbing of all acid gases and,
particularly, iodine. The collected materials were analyzed using direct current plasma atomic
emission spectroscopy for the majority of the constituents and ion chromatography (IC) for
anions. Melter emission fluxes are compared to feed fluxes and emission samples taken while
processing the five feed compositions in Table 5.1. Notice the distinction that is made between
constituents sampled as particles and as "gas". The "gaseous" constituents are operationally
defined as those species that are scrubbed in the impinger solutions after the air stream has
passed through a 0.3 µm heated filter. All three samples were well within the 90 – 110% limits
for isokinetic sampling.

 Particulate emissions constituted from 0.2 to 0.92 percent of feed solids for feeds
processed at bubbling fixed at 9 lpm. The 0.2 percent carryover while processing the
HLW-ZEO-01 composition is lower than while processing other HLW feeds with similar solids
contents, including partially washed HLW AY-102 solids (0.53 percent solids) [14],
C-106/AY-102 SIPP (0.61 to 0.81 percent) [26], a C-106/AY-102 high waste loading
formulation (0.66 and 0.71) [22], and HLW AZ-101 (0.46 percent) [34] due in part to a lower
volatile content in the feed. Carryover while processing the two feeds with HLW AY-102 (0.51
and 0.92 percent) was two to five times higher than while processing the HLW-ZEO-1
composition due to the higher feed water content and the introduction of volatiles in the HLW
AY-102, but within the range of other tests conducted with HLW AY-102 feeds at similar solids
contents (0.52 – 1.31 percent) [14]. Collectively, the data confirm the expectation that the
inclusion of the ion exchange media does not contribute to higher solids carryover, consistent
with the lack volatile elements in the ion exchange media.

 As expected, the feed element emitted at the lowest melter decontamination factor (DF)
was sulfur with about ten to sixteen percent of the feed sulfur being emitted as particulate. Other
elements exhibiting some volatile behavior were boron, chromium, and potassium. The expected
increasing volatility of alkali metals with increasing molecular weight was observed: potassium

 25

carryover being the highest followed by sodium then lithium. Chlorine and fluorine were
detected in the melter exhaust at low concentrations even though the elements are not included in
the target compositions due to these volatile halides being present in the feed as contaminants.
Boron was the only element targeted in the feed that was detected in the impinger solutions
collected downstream of the heated particle filter in the sampling train, which constitutes the
“gas” fraction of the melter emissions.

5.2 Particulate Sampled from Off-Gas System

 Material accumulated in the cyclones downstream of the film cooler was sampled and
analyzed after each testing sequence to further characterize particulate carryover during these
tests. Analysis of material accumulated in the cyclones is provided in Table 5.2 for mineral
content and chemical composition. Cyclone solids contain all of the feed components in similar
proportions to the feed for most of the elements, enrichments over feed for volatile elements such
as boron, potassium, and sulfur, and depletions of non-volatile silicon. These proportions of
volatile and non-volatile elements in the cyclone particulate indicate bulk carryover of feed from
the melter as well as loss of the limited number of volatile components from glass pool and cold
cap. Small amounts of chlorine, bismuth, strontium, thallium, tungsten, zinc, and zirconium were
detected in the cyclone deposits despite not being included in the target composition as a result
of residue from preceding tests or being a feed contaminant. The results from XRD analysis
support bulk entrainment as the mechanism for particulate carryover: the most abundant feed
mineral in the first test (chabazite) and in the second two tests (quartz) was identified as the most
prevalent component in the particulate in the respective tests. Spinels (detected as magnetite)
were present in all cyclone particulate samples but they were up to twice as abundant in tests
after processing the AY-102 HLW.

5.3 Gases Monitored by FTIR

 Melter emissions were monitored in each test for a variety of gaseous components, most
notably CO and nitrogen species, by Fourier Transform Infra-Red Spectroscopy (FTIR). The
off-gas system temperature is maintained well above 100°C beyond the sampling port
downstream of the DM100 HEPA filter to prevent analyte loss due to condensation prior to
monitoring. The data, therefore, represent the relative concentrations of volatile gaseous species
in the melter exhaust. A summary of the range and average concentrations of gaseous species
monitored during the three tests subdivided into fixed bubbling and optimized bubbling test

detected in the off-gas stream by FTIR. The concentrations of three of the most abundant

The amount of moisture in the exhaust was in proportion to the amount of water in the feed and
the rate at which feed is introduced into the melter. As a result, the moisture content increases
with higher feed rates associated with optimized bubbling. The amount of carbonate in each of
the three feeds is similar and therefore the concentration of carbon dioxide increased with

 SECTION 6.0
 SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

 Inorganic ion-exchange media have been used at various facilities such as WVDP for
cesium removal and are under consideration at SRS and WTP. These materials are resistant to
effects of radiation and can be vitrified with other waste streams after use. Glass formulations
have been developed for two such media, CST (IONSIV® R9120-B) and synthetic chabazite
(IONSIV® R9160-G), both alone and in combination with WTP HLW streams [13]. In the work
described herein, the vitrification of ion exchange media for cesium removal either alone or in
combination with a representative Hanford HLW stream is demonstrated in continuous melter
tests. This included one formulation with chabazite as the only waste component and two
formulations containing direct-feed fully washed AY-102 HLW solids combined with either
CST or chabazite. Feed processing characteristics, glass production rate, melter emissions, and
feed carryover were determined for each of the three feed compositions. This was accomplished
through VGF and DM100 melter system testing.

 A series of melter tests were conducted on the DM100-BL vitrification system with ion
exchange media (chabazite and CST) and simulated HLW AY-102 waste solids processed with
glass forming additives optimized for each combination. The tests are distinguished by
processing three different blends of ion exchange media, HLW AY-102 oxides, and glass
forming additives: the HLW-ZEO-01 glass composition with 70 wt% chabazite, the
HLW-IXC1-04 glass composition with 30 wt% AY-102 HLW oxides and 20 wt% chabazite, and
the HLW-IXC4-05 glass composition with 31 wt% AY-102 HLW oxides and 5 wt% CST. Tests
on the DM100 melter system were conducted at 1150oC at the nominal bubbling rate of 9 lpm
and also with optimized bubbling to achieve maximum production rates; these conditions were
selected to allow comparison to results obtained previously with HLW simulants. The feed rate
was adjusted to provide the desired complete cold cap. The principal results of these tests can be
summarized as follows:

 • All feed formulations with the ion exchange media were readily processed with ion
 exchange media waste loading up to 70 wt% and AY-102 HLW waste loading up to
 31 wt% while meeting all WTP processing and product quality requirements and
 maintaining acceptable glass and feed processing properties.

 • Glass production rates ranged from 800 kg/m 2 /day for the HLW-IXC4-05
 P P

 composition with AY-102 HLW oxides and CST to 1150 kg/m 2 /day for the P P

 HLW-ZEO-01 composition with chabazite. These glass production rates were
 obtained despite the relatively high water contents of the feeds.

 • Using bubbling optimization, glass production rates increased by 33% (900 vs. 1200
 kg/m 2 /day) while processing the HLW-IXC1-04 composition with AY-102 HLW
 P P

 oxides and chabazite and by 57% (1150 vs. 1800 kg/m 2 /day) while processing the
 P P

 HLW-ZEO-01 composition with chabazite.

 28

 • All feeds with ion exchange media processed at comparable or faster rates than other
 HLW feeds with comparable solids contents.

 • VGF testing of dried melter feeds showed each of the three compositions to be
 rapidly melting. This suggests that the limiting factor during melter testing was the
 high water content.

 • Ion exchange media processing rates ranged from 805 kg/m 2 /day for the P P

 HLW-ZEO-01 composition with chabazite to 40 kg/m 2 /day for the HLW-IXC4-05
 P P

 composition with AY-102 HLW oxides and CST (bubbling fixed at 9 lpm). Ion
 exchange media processing rates were dependent on the ion exchange media waste
 loading in the glass as well as the overall glass production rate.

 • HLW oxide processing rates ranged from 248 kg/m 2 /day for the HLW-IXC4-05

 HLW-IXC1-04 composition with AY-102 HLW oxides and chabazite (bubbling fixed
 at 9 lpm). HLW oxide processing rates were dependent on the HLW oxide waste
 loading in the glass as well as the overall glass production rate.

 Melter exhaust was sampled as each feed composition was processed at the nominal
bubbling rate to determine the effects of the incorporation of ion exchange media on particulate
and gaseous emissions. Particulate emissions constituted from 0.2 to 0.92 percent of feed solids
and increased with decreasing ion exchange media content. The amount of solids carryover while
processing feed containing ion exchange media was less than or equal to the solids carryover for
other HLW feeds with comparable water contents indicating that the incorporation of ion
exchange media into the melter feed does not increase solids carryover. Melter decontamination
factors were determined for most elements in the feed. The most volatile species was sulfur,
which was expected given the lack of other volatile elements in the target glass composition.
Other elements exhibiting some volatile behavior were boron, chromium, and potassium.
Gaseous emissions of nitrogen oxides and byproducts of incomplete combustion were
nonexistent to minimal due to the lack of nitrates and organic carbon in the ion exchange media
and the very low concentrations in the fully washed HLW waste.

6.1 Recommendations for Future Work

 The results of the testing presented herein demonstrate the viability of the vitrification of
ion exchange media either with or without the incorporation of HLW oxides. This strategy of
blending inorganic ion exchange media with HLW with minimal or no pretreatment has the
potential to increase overall waste loading without sacrificing HLW oxide waste loading or
processing rate and thereby presents a viable path for dispositioning of spent inorganic ion
exchange media. As a result, it is recommended that testing and evaluation of these strategies be
continued in order to provide a solid basis for their broad implementation in order to maximize

 29

the cost and schedule benefits while minimizing technical risk. Further work that is
recommended for optimization of processing of ion exchange media is outlined below.

• Cesium Retention in the Product Glass during the Vitrification of Ion Exchange Media:
 Ideally, cesium captured on the ion exchange media would be incorporated into the glass
 upon vitrification. Conducting melter tests with ion exchange media containing cesium is
 required to determine the partitioning of cesium to the glass and melter exhaust during
 vitrification.

• Incorporation into WTP Direct Feed HLW Pretreatment Strategy: The present testing was
 conducted with fully washed HLW solids. Incorporation of ion exchange media with
 unwashed or partially washed tank waste would provide additional opportunity for increasing
 total waste loading, waste processing rates, and reduction of pretreatment steps.

• Other HLW Tank Waste: The present testing was based on a single HLW tank composition
 from the Hanford tanks. Subsequent work should extend these results to address a
 representative range of HLW direct feeds expected to be processed at the WTP.

• Glass Formulation: Despite the limited nature of the present study, very significant
 enhancements of waste processing rates (through increases in both waste loading and melt
 rate) have been demonstrated. The results from the glass formulation work indicate that
 further improvements may be possible through a second phase of glass formulation
 optimization using the results of the present work as a basis.

• Scale-Up Testing: As in the previous enhancement work for ORP, testing should be extended
 to larger-scale melter systems in order to address potential risks associated with scale-up,
 particularly with respect to processing rates. Testing should be conducted at the DM1200
 WTP HLW Pilot Melter scale (1.2 MT/m 2 /day). Optimization of bubbling rate is a critical
 P P

 variable and therefore testing with bubblers in the prototypical orientation at larger scale is
 required to confirm these findings.

• Integrated System Testing: Testing on the DM1200 WTP HLW Pilot Melter system provides
 data from a one-third scale system with a prototypical feed delivery system and off-gas
 treatment train. Prototypical primary off gas system components with an evaporator and the
 capacity to recycle off gas solutions back to the melter feed are installed on a DM10 melter
 (0.021 m2 melt surface area) providing an avenue to collect much of the same data at greatly
 reduced cost. Such testing is necessary to evaluate potential interactive effects on system
 operation arising from implementation of processing ion exchange media and to provide data
 on the performance of each unit operation, input for flow-sheet models and regulatory
 requirements, and information about recycle streams.
