Solvent‐Responsive Porous Organic Cage‐Based Membranes With Switchable Pore Openings for Molecular Sieving and Heavy Metal Recovery
Authors: Jisha Kuttiani Ali, Emad Alhseinat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPorous organic cages are an innovative type of material used in membranes, characterized by their ability to generate high‐quality films, hierarchical porous structures, adjustable porosity, and varied functionalities. These materials with unique structures have garnered significant attention due to their ability to alter pore size in response to solvent or chemical stimuli. Here, solvent‐responsive porous organic cage‐based membrane (PCM) for heavy metal recovery and molecular sieving is demonstrated. PCM dominated membrane were fabricated on PTFE polymer support prepared from 1,3,5‐triformylbenzene and tris(4‐formylphenyl)amine in methanol as solvent at room temperature. PCM exhibits a low penetration of heavy metal ions (∼4%) and enhanced efficiency in rejecting dyes (94.5%) in water compared with methanol as a solvent. These solvent stimuli influence PCM to alter its pore size, which was confirmed using Powder X‐ray diffraction analysis. Investigations into dye removal studies revealed that the pore size of PCM decreased when the solvent transitioned from methanol to water, and this change was reversible. Moreover, the recovery of retained heavy metal ions achieving 52% recovery rate for Ni2+ ions and 45.4% for Zn2+ ions. This study presents the use of PCM for extracting heavy metal ions from water by adjusting the pore size via external stimuli.

Introduction

Membrane separation technology is regarded as a highly effective and energy‐efficient alternative for water treatment [1, 2]. Developing high‐performance membrane materials is vital to membrane separation applications [3, 4]. Until now, the source of most membranes has been developed using conventional amorphous polymers such as polyvinylidene fluoride, polyimide, polysulfone, polyether sulfone, chitosan, and poly(amide–imide)‐based crosslinked polymers and so forth [1, 3]. Due to the unordered pore channels, limited porosity, lack of uniform pore size, and less interconnectivity, these membranes have a trade‐off limitation between selectivity and permeability [1]. In recent years, porous materials such as metal‐organic frameworks (MOFs) and covalent‐organic frameworks (COFs) with well‐organized structures have gained attention as potential materials for membrane development [1, 5]. However, the practical use of these membranes in industry is restricted because of challenges in the processability of these framework materials [1, 5, 6]. Furthermore, the primary obstacle lies in developing continuous membranes with elongated porous structures that function solely as size‐based molecular sieves rather than selective adsorbents [5, 7]. Cooper and co‐workers reported a new type of 3D discrete porous organic cages (POC) with a central cavity and molecule‐accessible windows [8, 9]. POC molecular materials are an emerging subclass of porous materials, and imine condensation and boronic ester formation are the main strategies for constructing these materials [8, 9, 10]. They are discrete molecules linked by weak non‐covalent interactions between molecules via intrinsic cages and can be assembled into modular structures with structural diversity [9, 11]. These molecules can persist in crystals, amorphous solids, or even moderate‐sized solvents forming porous liquids [11]. In addition, these POC membranes are easier to fabricate with tunable porosity compared to other polymeric membranes and porous material‐based membranes. Recently, due to their porous nature and solution processability, these materials have been found in the areas of gas storage and separation materials, host‐guest chemistry, sensors, packing materials for chromatography, and synthetic templates for metal nanoparticles [8, 12]. Moreover, attributable to its good film‐forming ability, hierarchical porous structures, tunable porosity, and diverse functionalities, it is widely considered a suitable porous material for membrane fabrication, for example, supported 2D crystalline membranes (layer‐by‐layer assembly), supported amorphous membranes using spin coating, and mixed matrix membranes through casting [11, 12]. Literature was evident that various porous organic cage‐based membranes were developed for the treatment of wastewater for removing metal ions, dyes, and other contaminants [7, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Since an inner cavity in POCs could catalyze the water permeance of the membrane by acting as a water transport channel, and windows act as the barrier for contaminants, porous organic cage‐based membranes displayed high‐removal performance and good permeance for water [13]. Most importantly, POC's structures are defined by non‐covalent intermolecular interactions, which can be switched using chemical /solvent stimuli to alter their bulk porosity [7]. Ai He et al. reported solvent‐responsive porous organic cage membrane on polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) support using the monomers 1,3,5‐triformylbenzene and (1R,2R)‐1,2‐diaminecyclohexane with switchable pore apertures for graded molecular sieving. In this work, pores of POC can be switched in methanol due to solvent stimuli, and larger pores were generated, resulting in high methanol permeance and higher molecular weight cut‐offs compared to water as solvent [7]. In addition, the membranes displayed different selectivity under different water/methanol ratios because of the pore switching between the two phases due to solvent stimuli [7]. The porous organic cage membranes have demonstrated potential for molecular separations due to high levels of porosity because of extrinsic voids between the cage molecules, pore switching property by chemical/solvent stimuli, and tunable selectivity [6, 17]. However, little work has been reported on the solvent‐responsive pore size switching property of porous organic cage‐based membranes. Furthermore, the application of porous organic cage material‐based membranes for recovery of heavy metals has not been studied yet. This study presents the synthesis of novel membranes made of porous organic cage materials on a PTFE support membrane. The membranes exhibit the ability to change their pore size in response to solvents and demonstrate excellent performance in recovering heavy metals.

Here, we introduce the fabrication of novel, close‐packed, and defect‐free membranes of shape‐persistent imine porous organic cage dominated membrane on PTFE polymer support prepared from 1,3,5‐triformylbenzene and tris(4‐aminophenyl)amine. The impact of solvent stimuli on the porous organic cage‐based membrane supported by PTFE polymer was investigated using contaminants such as heavy metals and dyes, with water and methanol serving as solvents. The membrane's ability to switch pores hinders the passage of organic dyes and heavy metals in water, which can easily pass through when exposed to methanol due to the opening of pores caused by the solvent's stimulation. Moreover, the ability of these porous organic cage membranes to respond to solvents presents a new opportunity for the recovery of heavy metals. This study also examined the retrieval of heavy metals using porous organic cage‐based membranes and discovered a greater retrieval rate when methanol was used as the solvent. Furthermore, it is worth noting that COF derived from identical monomers has been documented in previous literature [18, 19, 20]. So, in addition, we also synthesized a COF using the same monomers and explored different characterization techniques to establish the structural distinction between the COF and the innovative porous organic cage‐based material.

Results and Discussion

Material Characterization

The porous organic cage material‐based membrane (PCM) was synthesized by mixing the monomers 1,3,5‐triformyl benzene (A) and tris(4‐aminophenyl) amine (B) in methanol over the PTFE support membrane at room temperature. The reaction was carried out in a closed system. The obtained membrane sample after 2 days was washed with DMF, DCM, and methanol and dried at 70° for 24h. The final product, reddish PCM membranes, is further used for different characterizations. In this in situ membrane fabrication process, porous support was impregnated with aldehyde monomers, followed by the addition of amine monomers in methanol. The number of monomers were optimized as 0.185 mmol for aldehyde, and 0.103mmol for amine as 1:1 molar ratio of monomers resulted discontinues nonuniform membranes due to the stoichiometry at the membrane forming interface differs from the monomers ratio in the bilk solution. Since the covalent organic framework (COF) from the same monomers was also reported in the literature [18, 19, 20], the COF membrane from the same monomers was also prepared using DMF as a solvent to confirm the structure of PCM membranes. Compared to COF, fast reactions were observed in the formation of the PCM membrane in methanol; that is, a layer of membrane was visible after 6 h. While the layer of the COF membrane was observed after 2 days. The experimental procedure is summarized in Figure 1a. As seen in Figure 1a, PCM and COF samples displayed significant color differences, as PCM is reddish and COF is yellow. Besides, the PCM did not show any visible cracks on the surface, while COF displayed significant visible cracks on the surface (Figure 1a). To confirm the structure of PCM, an array of characterization techniques was done and compared with COF.

(a) Experimental scheme for the fabrication of PCM and COF, (b) PXRD spectra of PCM and COF, and (c) N2‐adsorption‐desorption isotherm of PCM and COF.

The confirmation of different functional groups in PCM and COF were first analyzed by Fourier transform infrared (FT‐IR) spectroscopic studies (Figure S1). The appearance of a peak at 1620 cm−1 in PCM and COF compared to monomers corresponds to ‐C = N vibration bands confirms the successful formation of imine bonds in both compounds (Figure S1a,b). Interestingly, as seen in Figure S1a, the FTIR spectra of PCM and COF are similar, which indicates that both have the same kinds of functional groups and chemical bonds in their structure. Moreover, complete consumption of the amine monomers was confirmed with the disappearance of amino N−H stretching at 3404 and 3332 cm−1 in both PCM and COF (Figure S1a,b). However, the peak at 1693‐1670 cm−1 indicated the unreacted aldehyde end in the material as a side product. The structural periodicity of PCM and COF was analyzed using powder X‐ray diffraction (PXRD) analysis. The PXRD pattern of monomers was summarized in Figure S2 to confirm the formation of PCM and COF. As observed from the XRD pattern shown in Figure 1b, PCM shows peaks at 7°, 11.8o°, 18.1°, and 22.1° corresponding to the [1 0 0] [1 1 0], [210], and [001] planes, respectively. Meanwhile, COF shows slightly lower peak values of 6.8°, 11.5°, and 22.7°. Similar XRD patterns for COF were reported in the literature [18, 19, 20, 21] and confirms that COF has an interlayer structure as a covalent organic framework. Moreover, PCM's pore size was 12.63 Å, whereas COF showed 13 Å based on the first [1 0 0] planes of PCM and COF calculated using the Equation (S1) given in supporting information [22, 23]. According to literature, synthesized COF has a similar structure to covalent organic framework with a pore size of 13 Å by consuming three monomers from aldehyde and amine monomers [20]. The surface area and pore volume of PCM and COF were demonstrated in N2 sorption isotherms at 77 K (Figure 1c). They show type I sorption behavior with BET surface areas of 6.7 and 90 m2 g−1 and total pore volumes of 0.22 and 0.40 cm3 g−1 for PCM, and COF, respectively. The pore size distribution curves for PCM and COF indicated that the pore size of 1.23 nm for PCM, and COF have 0.83 and 1.26 nm (Figure S3). The lower surface area, smaller pore size, and pore volume of PCM, compared to COF, suggest that PCM has a different structural construction, most probably a cage‐like structure, compared to COF.

To verify the atomic level construction of PCM and COF skeleton, solid‐state 13C‐cross‐polarization magic angle spinning nuclear magnetic resonance (13C‐CP MAS NMR) analysis was employed. As seen in Figure 2a, b, strong resonance peaks at 117–129 ppm correspond to aromatic carbons (C = C) in benzene rings, whereas the peak observed at 137 ppm corresponds to aromatic carbon attached to a nitrogen atom. Notably, 146 and 157 ppm correspond to the carbon atom in the ‐C = N bond and C‐N present in the PCM and COF skeleton. The 13C‐CP MAS NMR spectra exhibit a similar pattern in the region of 137–157 ppm for both PCM and COF, while the region 117–129 for COF shows the splitting of peaks. The similarity in the spectra for both compounds confirmed the formation of a similar environment around carbon atoms in PCM and COF. A similar 13C‐CP MAS NMR was reported for COF in the literature [19]. The thermal stability of these materials was also investigated via TGA analysis in an inert N2 atmosphere (Figure S4). Comparison of the two curves allows the conclusion that the thermal degradation of PCM and COF occurs essentially in two steps, suggesting the breaking of the same type of chemical bonds. The first degradation event that occurs below 80°C for COF is attributed to eliminating adsorbed water molecules. However, PCM shows serious decomposition due to the collapse of the molecular structure starting at 200°C, and displays a mass loss of 37 wt%. Interestingly, COF demonstrated the framework's collapse with a mass loss of 30 wt% after 200°C compared to PCM, showing that COF has higher thermal stability because of its interlayer structure with a connected framework. Given that the linkage types are the same among the two samples according to FTIR analysis, and 13C NMR results, the latter result suggests a more robust interlayer packing and more favorable, higher‐energy 𝜋 orbital stacking interactions in COF compared to PCM [23, 24]. These results are consistent with the results of PXRD. The above results suggest that PCM and COF exist in different structural frameworks even though they have the same chemical linkages, especially COF, which is a covalent organic framework.

One promising set of tools for identifying the molecular mass of materials is matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization‐MALDI‐TOF‐MS. The molecular masses obtained from this soft ionization method have been widely used in the structural elucidation of POC materials [25]. So, MALDI‐TOF‐MS was applied to further confirm structural differences in PCM and COF. Notably, the MALDI‐TOF‐MS of COF showed a peak at 1283 m/z, which indicated that the COF exists as a covalent organic framework with the condensation of 3 amine monomers and 3 aldehyde monomers (Figure S5). Meanwhile, the MALDI‐TOF‐MS spectrum of PCM has a higher molecular mass of 1583 m/z than COF, which indicates a different structural formation for PCM than COF (Figure 2c). To confirm the structure of PCM, if 4 monomers of amine and aldehyde condense to form a COF like structure, then the obtained molecular weight should be around 1594 m/z while 5 monomers of amine and aldehyde condense to form a COF with a molecular weight of 1992 m/z. As seen in Figure 2c, the molecular mass of PCM obtained from MALDI‐FT‐ICR‐MS analysis is 1586 m/z, confirming the condensation of 4 monomers of amines and aldehydes. According to this molecular mass, there are two possible structural confirmations for PCM from the condensation of 4 monomers of aldehyde and amine. One is the COF formation (Figure S6c), and the second one is the cage structure as seen in Figure 2d. If it is a COF‐like structure, the pore size of PCM will be higher than COF since the condensation of 4+4 monomers as seen in Figure S6c. According to PXRD analysis, PCM has a lower pore size than COF, and also, TGA analysis confirmed that PCM has lower stability than COF. In addition, BET analysis confirmed that pore volume for PCM is 50% less than that reported for COF (Figure 1c). Based on these results, the proposed structure for PCM is illustrated in Figure 2d. In order to evaluate the possibility of the formation of COF or PCM using the same monomers in DMF, different experimental conditions were applied to the reaction between amine monomers and aldehyde monomers in DMF. The experiments were carried out by stirring the monomers at room temperature (COF 2) and stirring the monomers at 50°C (COF 3) in DMF. The MALDI‐TOF‐MS spectra of COF 2 and COF 3 indicated the same molecular weight, that is, 1283 m/z, similar to COF, suggesting that solvent DMF has a role in forming the covalent organic framework structure for COF, COF2, and COF3 (Figures S7 and S8). Since methanol is a polar protic solvent with a lower polarity index of 5.1 compared to polar aprotic DMF (6.4), cage‐like structure formation is more dominant in methanol while DMF favored only covalent organic framework like structure [26, 27]. Importantly, the scanning electron microscopic (SEM) images further revealed morphological differences in PCM and COF. As seen in Figure 2g, h, SEM images of PCM showed compact and well defined spherical molecules in a larger size and dense internal contrast, and COFs exist as agglomerated interconnected nanoparticles in small‐sized spherical particles (Figure 2i,j). As seen in Figure 2k, l, TEM analysis further revealed the structural difference between PCM and COF. The PCM showed dense well defined spherical particles, whereas COF exhibited irregular interconnected aggregates composed of nanosized domains. Figure S9 again confirms that COF2 and COF 3 have similar COF morphology, indicating COF formation in DMF at different experimental conditions rather than PCM. The SEM images of COF are also consistent with the literature [20]. Based on these characterization analyses, it is confirmed that PCM exists as a porous organic cage‐like structure, and the proposed 3D structures of PCM are shown in Figure 2e, f. The SEM analysis of the top surface and cross‐sectional images of PCM membranes was also conducted. The SEM images' top view showed that the PCM membrane had well‐ordered packing over the PTFE support and no obvious pinholes or cracks (Figure 3a). The cross‐section of SEM images showed that the PCM membranes have a thickness of 95.62 µm (Figure 3b). The top surface and cross‐section of PCM membranes (PCM 2 and PCM 3) with a higher concentration of monomers were also analyzed (Figure S10). It can be observed that the thickness of the membranes increased to 104.12 and 113.83 µm from 95.62 µm as the concentration increased to 50 and 75 mg, respectively, in 30 mL of methanol.

Application of PCM Membranes

Heavy Metal Ion Permeation Test

We investigated the heavy metal ion transport properties of PCM membranes. The cation transport experiments were conducted using a self‐designed glass filter, and the PCM membrane of area 0.758 cm2 is fixed at the joint between the two filter bowls (Figure S11). The single‐component feed solution of 25 mg/L of Ni2+ and Zn2+ in two solvents, water and methanol, was used as separate systems. The DI water is used on the permeate side for water as a solvent, and methanol on the permeate side for methanol as a solvent for the feed solution. Firstly, the heavy metal cation permeation performance was noted for the membrane support. For the PTFE membrane substrate in both solvents, 12.4 and 12.48 mg/L of Ni2+ and Zn2+ ions were spotted on the permeate side after 3 hours, indicating the highly porous nature of the PTFE support. We further investigated the permeation of Ni2+ and Zn2+ ions through the PCM in water and methanol in a separate system. The experimental results indicate that both cations in water could not permeate through PCM in ambient conditions, even if the filtration time lasted for 4 days (Figure 3c–e). Only around 1 mg/L concentration (∼ 4% based on feed concentration) was displayed on the permeate side for Ni2+ and Zn2+ in water with a very low permeate rate of 0.01 mmol/h/cm2 for day 1 and 0.003 mmol/h/cm2 for day 4. Meanwhile, both cations in the methanol displayed higher concentrations on the permeate side (∼34% based on feed concentration) with a high permeate rate of 0.06–0.05 mmol/h/cm2 for day 1 and 0.21 mmol/h/cm2 for day 4 (Figure 3c–e). Moreover, the Ni2+ and Zn2+ ions permeation through PCM in water showed equilibrium after Day 1, while in methanol, it displayed continued permeation and a 40%–45% increase after Day 4 compared to Day 1 (Figures 3c, d and S12). A similar trend of ion permeation in water and methanol was also observed with PCM 2 and PCM 3 for Ni2+ and Zn2+ (Figures S13 and S14). These results suggested that the PCM membranes displayed significantly different cation permeation in water and methanol.

Dye Permeation Test

In order to confirm the different permeation effects of PCM membranes in water and methanol, dye filtration performance was also done. According to the size exclusion mechanism, it was expected that only pollutant molecules with smaller sizes than the pore sizes of PCM could pass through the membranes. The Rh B dye was selected as the size of Rh B (12.5 and 15.6 Å) is smaller size than the pore size of the membrane [23] (Figure S15). The Rh B was dissolved in water and methanol separately to get the feed solution of 25 mg/L to explore the filtration performance using a vacuum filtration setup with a membrane area of 9.61 cm2. Before dye filtration, the solvent permeate flux of PCM for water and methanol was analyzed and found to be 82 Lm−2h−1 for water and 115 Lm−2h−1 for methanol. The higher methanol permeation is primarily attributed to the methanol stimulated expansion of PCM pore structure, which increases the effective transport pathways through the membrane [7]. The Figure S16 shows PCM membrane has lower contact angle (50°) in methanol compared to water (72°). The lower contact angle observed for methanol indicates that improved membrane wettability in methanol due to pore expansion of PCM in methanol, which further facilitates solvent penetration and transport. Therefore, the enhanced methanol flux results from the combined effects of solvent responsive pore expansion and improved wettability. The PCM with a feed solution of Rh B in methanol displayed a low % rejection performance of 19.9% (Figure 3f). Meanwhile, the feed solution of Rh B in water displayed a 94.5% rejection performance (Figure 3f), indicating the changes in the pore size of PCM in water, similar to the case of heavy metal ion permeation in water. In addition, a similar trend of Rh B performance in water and methanol was also observed with PCM 2 and PCM 3 (Figure S17).

Reversibility and Recovery Performance Test

The fact that the Rh B dye and heavy metal ions showed significantly different rejections in water and methanol suggests a dynamic contraction of the PCM cage structure, resulting in changes in pore size. To confirm this, two experiments were conducted: (1) one was to analyze the reversibility of changes in this pore aperture during the Rh B filtration by adjusting the concentration of methanol in water, and (2) the other one was the extraction of Ni2+ and Zn2+ions in methanol from the PCM membrane that collected after the ion permeate experiment in water. As seen in Figure 4a, the rejection rate for Rh B increased from 19.9% to 94.5%, with the water concentration increased from 0 to 100% in methanol. As it approached 50% of methanol and water concentration, the rejection rate was closer to 33.3%, indicating that the host‐guest interaction of methanol is still predominant at 50% of methanol. While closer to 75% of water in methanol, 82.9% rejection performance was noted, indicating the self‐regulating contraction resulting in lower pore size in response to a higher water concentration. As the water content is above 75% in methanol, external stimuli from water become dominant, leading to a contracted structure of PCM with lower pore size, resulting in good rejection for Rh B in water. Figure 4b displayed absorbance differences of permeate Rh B solution in different water methanol ratios, and Figure 4c represents the digital images of permeate in different solvent mixture ratios. Videos S1 and S2 show the Rh B removal performance of PCM in 100% water and 100% methanol as solvents, respectively. Since PCM did not show any adsorption for the heavy metals (Figure S18) and FTIR spectra of PCM samples after the ion permeation experiment after 4 days did not show a significant difference in vibration bands, it indicated that heavy metal ions did not adsorb in the PCM by any physical or chemical interactions (Figure S19). Moreover, Figure S20 also confirmed the there is no significant change in the surface of PCM membranes after the heavy metal recovery. In addition, as seen in Figure S21, EDX mapping of PCM after the ion permeation experiment after 4 days confirms the presence of Ni2+ and Zn2+ ions in the PCM. It is suggested that Ni2+ and Zn2+ ions are inside the PCM membranes due to their contracted structure in water as a solvent. (2) So, in the second experiment, to extract the heavy metal ions from PCM membrane, PCM membranes with Ni2+ ions were kept in methanol for two days in the shaker at 100 rpm, and the Ni2+ concentration in methanol with time was monitored. Figure 4d shows that the amount of Ni2+ ions released from the used PCM membranes increased with time. After 2 days, the amount of Ni2+ ion released to methanol from PCM membranes was 5.03 mg/L. Similar experiments were also conducted with Zn2+ ion, and 4.80 mg/L of Zn2+ ion was found in the methanol after 2 days (Figure 4d). In addition, Figure 4e explains the percentage of Ni2+ and Zn2+ ions inside the PCM after the ion permeation test in water and methanol and the recovery of Ni2+ and Zn2+ ions from PCM using methanol. It was found that 37.6% and 41.8% of Ni2+ and Zn2+ ions were inside the PCM (CPCM (%)) during the ion permeation test in water, respectively. Moreover, the recovery rate of Ni2+ and Zn2+ ions from PCM (CRecovery (%)) using methanol was found to be 52% and 45.4%, respectively. Mechanism for Switchable Pores, High Performance, and Recovery of Heavy Metals

As discussed, the PCM membrane shows significantly different dye rejection and cation permeation in water and methanol; it is hypothesized that there might be a change in pore size when switching the solvent from methanol to water. A similar kind of solvent‐responsive pore size switching property for porous materials was also reported in the literature [7, 23, 28]. When solvents were changed, the pore size of PCM changed due to the self‐regulating pore size property when it suffers from an external stimulus, such as water and methanol. The literature revealed that PXRD is a useful tool to identify the pore size regulation of porous materials due to solvent stimuli [17, 23, 28]. To investigate the pore size alternation of PCM in methanol and water, initially, the PCM membranes were solvated with water and methanol separately, and PXRD was analyzed for both samples and pore size was calculated using Equation (S1) given in supporting information. As shown in Figure 5a, the first PXRD peak of the PCM in methanol is almost the same as that of a dry PCM.While the peaks of PCM, which was solvated with water, shifted from 7° to 11.4°, 12.1° to 14.2°, and 22.1° to 28.9°, suggesting that the PCM switches to a lower pore size of 7.76 Å in water from 12.63 Å in methanol. When PCM in methanol, due to the strong host‐guest interaction between the flexible cage and methanol, the cage structure no longer undulates, thus resulting in no obstruction for the passage of contaminants. When the membrane is immersed in water, the flexible bonds in organic linkers, contract, leading to changes in bond angles and node distances, resulting in a lower pore size of 7.76 Å [7, 17, 23]. The top, side, and bottom views of PCM in methanol and water using 3D ChemDraw are shown in Figure S22. In water as a feed solution solvent, PCM has a lower pore size (7.76 Å) than the size of Rh B (12.5 Å), hence, Rh B is removed by the molecular sieving mechanism, while, in methanol as solvent, PCM has a higher pore size (12.63 Å) than the size of Rh B, leading to lower removal performance (Figure 6). In the case of heavy metals, hydrated diameter of heavy metals (Zn2+ for 8.6 Å, and Ni2+ for 8.08 Å) higher than the pore size of membrane (7.76 Å) in water and solvated diameter of heavy metals (Zn2+ and Ni2+ for ∼10 Å) lower than the pore size of membranes ((12.63 Å)) in methanol [29, 30], therefore, it passed through the membrane in methanol while heavy metals blocked in the PCM in water (Figure 6). In addition, according to Figure 5b, the PCM membranes showed negative surface charge at natural pH in water and hence displayed high rejection performance also due to the electrostatic interaction between cationic form of Rh B with the membrane surface in water than in methanol. Moreover, in water, the surface negative charge of PCM also blocks the heavy metal ions via electrostatic interaction, resulting in the restriction of movement of the heavy metals to the other side of the membrane, subsequently the lower permeation in water. While the electrostatic interaction becomes non‐dominant due to the larger pore size (12.63 Å) of the membrane in methanol by the self‐regulating structural changes, and thus contaminants (Rh B and heavy metal ions) easily pass through the membrane. In addition, AFM analysis of the PCM membrane showed RMS roughness of 61.79±3.5 nm, indicating that high roughness also helps to increase the high contact surface area of the membrane with the contaminants, leading to the high performance in water (Figure 5c). In the case of recovery of heavy metal ions, as discussed, when the heavy metal ions‐contaminated PCM, after the permeation test, is dipped in methanol, which leads to the generation of larger pores in PCM due to the methanol stimuli, hence heavy metal ions are released from the PCM membrane into the methanol (Figure 4f).

Recycling and Long‐Term Stability Test

The recycling studies of the PCM membrane for Rh B in both water and methanol solvents are summarized in Figure 7a. It was found that after successive 5 cycles, the PCM membrane retained the ability to remove the Rh B (R) (93%–89%). In addition, removal of Rh B (86.5%–88.2%) from the surface of the PCM membrane in methanol as feed solution (these removal % (Rmethanol) denotes the Rh B removed from the surface of PCM membrane based on the concentration of Rh B in methanol after filtration say “permeate”) was noticed, indicating that pore switching properties of the PCM membrane were still maintained after 5 cycles. These recycling studies confirmed that the reversibility of the pore switching property of the PCM membrane in water and methanol was maintained after the 5th cycle for Rh B. In addition, long‐term stability and recyclability of PCM membrane against Ni2+ and Zn2+ ions (25 mg/L) were also studied for three cycles in water and methanol (Figure 7b). One cycle means the PCM membrane facing Ni2+ ions in water (15 mL) in the feed side and DI water in the permeate side(15 mL) for two days. After two days, Ni2+ in water in the feed side was replaced by methanol (15mL) and 15 mL of methanol in the permeate side, then kept for the next two days without changing the membrane. The same procedure was also performed for Zn 2+ ions. The Ni2+ ions (1.5% to 2.2%) and Zn2+ ions (1.9% to 4.7%) displayed constant ion transport through the PCM membrane in water for three cycles. In methanol, 43% of the Ni2+ ions and 30% of Zn2+ ions were transported through the PCM in the permeate side after continuous 3 cycles (Figure 7b). This confirms the long‐term stability of the PCM membrane for stable performance. According to Table S1, only very limited studies reported on the recovery of Ni 2+ and Zn 2+ ions from wastewater. However, harsh reaction conditions with multistage methods were widely used to get high recovery performance for heavy metal ions. So, the present work highlighted the simple and efficient way of recovery of Ni 2+ and Zn 2+ ions from wastewater and also PCM membrane showed higher removal performance for Rh B than reported porous membranes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a novel porous organic cage dominated membrane (PCM) was fabricated via imine condensation between the amine and aldehyde monomers. The structure of PCM was elucidated with various characterizations due to the chance of the formation of COF from the same monomers. Interestingly, results show that these membranes can regulate their pore size in methanol and water and show significantly different rejection performance and heavy metal ion permeation. In methanol as a solvent, high heavy metal ion permeation (∼34%) was noted by PCM, while in water as a solvent, significantly lower ion permeation (∼4%) was observed due to the regulation of the size of pores of the membrane by solvent stimuli such as water. Similarly, 94.5% rejection for Rh B was displayed for PCM membranes in water as the solvent for feed solution, whereas only 19.9% removal in methanol. As verified in PXRD analysis, the peaks attributed to PCM were shifted to a higher degree in water, which suggests the pore size changes to a smaller size (12.63 to 7.76 Å) when the membranes solvated with water compared to methanol. Moreover, the reversibility of changes in this pore aperture was observed when the solvents were switched from methanol to water, resulting in improved dye rejection performance. In addition, recovery of heavy metal ions using PCM was also studied in methanol. The 52% and 45.4% recovery for Ni2+ and Zn2+ were obtained with PCM in methanol. This study provides significant guidance for fabricating porous organic cage‐based membranes and tuning the pore size in response to external stimuli, such as solvents, for the recovery of heavy metal ions and wastewater treatment.
