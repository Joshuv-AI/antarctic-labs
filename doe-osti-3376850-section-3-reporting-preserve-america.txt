Brookhaven National Laboratory
Introduction
Brookhaven National Laboratory (BNL) is a multidisciplinary laboratory with seven Nobel Prize-winning
discoveries, 37 R&D 100 Awards, and countless advances in science and technology. For over 75 years,
the Laboratory has played a leading role in the Department of Energy (DOE) Science and Technology
mission and continues to contribute to the DOE’s missions in energy resources, environmental quality,
and national security. The Laboratory is operated and managed by Brookhaven Science Associates
(BSA), which was founded by the Research Foundation for the State University of New York on behalf
of Stony Brook University, and Battelle, a non-profit applied science and technology organization. BNL
is committed to longstanding partnerships with researchers, academic institutions, industry, students,
teachers, and the surrounding community.

BNL is located near the center of Suffolk County on Long Island, New York within Brookhaven
Township, about 60 miles east of New York City. Most of BNL’s principal facilities are located near the
center of the 5,265-acre (8.23 square mile) site (Figure 1). The developed area encompasses
approximately 1,820 acres, consisting of:

▪ 500 acres originally developed by the Army (as part of WW II Camp Upton) and still used for offices
 and other operational buildings.
▪ 200 acres occupied by large, specialized research facilities.
▪ 520 acres occupied by outlying facilities, such as the Sewage Treatment Plant, research agricultural
 fields, housing facilities, and fire breaks.
▪ 400 acres of roads, parking lots, and connecting areas.
▪ 200 acres developed for the Long Island Solar Farm

 Figure 1. Aerial view of Brookhaven National Laboratory.

The balance of the site, approximately 3,400 acres, is largely wooded and represents a native pine barrens
ecosystem. In November 2000, DOE set aside 530 acres of undeveloped land at BNL as the Upton
Ecological and Research Reserve. The Upton Reserve preserves this portion of the pine barrens
ecosystem and provides an area for ecological research and education activities.

BNL was established in 1947 by the Atomic Energy Commission (AEC), a predecessor to the present
DOE, on land formerly associated with Camp Upton, a World War I and World War II (WW II) military
training facility. The AEC provided the initial funding for BNL’s research into peaceful uses of the atom.
The objective was to promote basic research in the physical, chemical, biological, and engineering aspects
of the atomic sciences, which resulted in the creation of a regional laboratory to design, construct, and
operate large scientific machines that individual institutions could not afford to develop on their own.
Although BNL no longer operates any research reactors, the Laboratory’s first major scientific facility
was the Brookhaven Graphite Research Reactor (BGRR), which was the first reactor to be constructed in
the United States following World War II.

Today, BNL supports a broad range of scientific research programs, including high-energy and nuclear
physics, environmental and climate sciences, energy technologies, national security research, and the
operation of major national user facilities. Because of the Laboratory’s continuing mission, cultural
resource considerations are routinely integrated into facility management, infrastructure modernization,
environmental restoration, and new construction activities.

The BNL Cultural Resource Management (CRM) Program is administered through the Environmental
Protection Division (EPD) under the direction of the U.S. Department of Energy Brookhaven Site Office
(DOE-BHSO). The program is responsible for identifying, evaluating, documenting, protecting, and
managing archaeological sites, historic buildings and structures, cultural landscapes, and other historic
properties in accordance with the National Historic Preservation Act (NHPA), Archaeological Resources
Protection Act, DOE directives, and applicable federal and state regulations. The BNL CRM Program is
managed by one full-time Secretary of the Interior (SOI)-Qualified Manager, and implementation is
supported by environmental professionals, facility managers, project personnel, and qualified cultural
resource consultants when specialized studies or evaluations are required. The program coordinates
regularly with DOE-BHSO, the New York State Historic Preservation Office (SHPO), and other
consulting parties as appropriate.

Section 106 compliance is integrated into BNL’s environmental review and project planning processes.
Proposed undertakings are evaluated to determine whether they have the potential to affect historic
properties. When a project has the potential to affect cultural resources, the Laboratory identifies historic
properties within the Area of Potential Effects, evaluates National Register eligibility, when necessary,
assesses potential effects, and develops measures to avoid, minimize, or mitigate adverse impacts. Section
106 Review consultations and documentation are developed by the BNL CRM Program, then forwarded
to DOE-BHSO for review, and concurrence. If the Review is acceptable, the package is submitted to the
New York State Historic Preservation Officer (SHPO) electronically through the New York State Cultural
Resource Information System (CRIS). The NYSHPO has 30 business days, from the date of receipt, to
review and comment. Cultural resource reviews are coordinated with National Environmental Policy Act
(NEPA) reviews and other environmental compliance activities to ensure that cultural resource
considerations are incorporated early in project planning.

Prior to 2023, BNL completed numerous cultural resource evaluations and management activities
associated with the former Camp Upton military installation, post-1947 scientific experiments, and
archaeological resources located throughout the site. Previous investigations included archaeological
surveys, historic building inventories, mapping and documentation of World War I-era trenches and
foundations, National Register of Historic Places (NRHP) evaluations, historic context development,
documentation of significant resources, and mitigation strategies developed and documented through
Memorandum of Agreement (MOA). These efforts established the foundation for the current CRM
Program and identified properties associated with various periods of history within the overall BNL site.

Approximately seven percent of the 483 buildings, structures, or infrastructure at BNL have been
determined eligible for listing on the National Register of Historic Places. This includes 33 buildings
representing the scientific history of the Laboratory, one representative World War II Series 700 barracks,
and one 1940s-era water tower. Additional historic properties at BNL include two archaeological sites
dating to the mid-19th century; World War I trenches and foundations that are scattered across the site;
and the Gamma Forest (a scientific facility that studied the effects of gamma radiation on ecosystems).
These resources reflect pre-1900 tenant farming, 20th century military history of Camp Upton, and the
internationally significant scientific achievements associated with BNL.

The Laboratory maintains an active inventory and evaluation program for historic properties that is
managed through the Facility Information Management System (FIMS). All buildings, structures, and
infrastructure greater than 50 years of age have been evaluated (48% of buildings) leaving 254 buildings,
structures, and infrastructure less than 50 years old not evaluated. Archaeological resource management
activities encompass approximately 3,400 acres of woods, of which 573 acres have been surveyed by
surface reconnaissance (367 acres) or Phase I/II subsurface survey (206 acres), representing 17%
coverage. Geographic Information System (GIS) tools are used to support cultural resource planning,
project review, and resource stewardship. All known/recorded archaeological sites have been mapped in
BNL’s GIS and the NYS CRIS.

The BNL Cultural Resource Management Program uses the Cultural Resource Management Plan
(CRMP) as the primary framework for resource stewardship, compliance, and long-term planning. The
plan establishes procedures for identification, evaluation, treatment, monitoring, consultation,
documentation, and public interpretation of cultural resources and provides a mechanism for integrating
cultural resource considerations into Laboratory operations while supporting mission requirements.
BNL’s cultural resources include WWI earthwork features (Figure 2), WWII barracks, Civilian
Conservation Corps features, and historic scientific buildings and features, structures, programs, and
discoveries associated with high-energy physics, research reactors, and other science conducted at
BNL. The Cultural Resources program also maintains the Camp Upton Historical Collection, historic
scientific equipment, photo/audio/video archives, and institutional records. As various cultural resources
are identified, plans for their long-term stewardship are developed and implemented. The Cultural
Resource Management Program ensures that the contributions BNL and the site have made to local and
national history and culture are documented and available for interpretation.

Identifying Historic Properties
BNL’s identification methods have not changed since the last reporting period (2020-2023). All
buildings, structures, and infrastructure greater than 50-years of age have been evaluated (48% of
buildings) leaving 254 buildings, structures, and infrastructure less than 50-years old not evaluated.
Approximately seven percent of the 483 buildings, structures, or infrastructure at BNL have been
determined eligible for listing on the National Register of Historic Places. This includes 33 buildings
representing the scientific history of the Laboratory, one representative World War II Series 700 barracks,
and one 1940s-era water tower. Additional historic properties at BNL include two archaeological sites
dating to the mid-nineteenth century; World War I trenches and foundations that are scattered across the
site; and the Gamma Forest (a scientific facility that studied the effects of gamma radiation on
ecosystems). There has been no change since the 2023 progress report.

No new policies or programs have been implemented in the last three years.

Most Section 106 undertakings and agreements have occurred after identification of historic properties
under Section 110. No new Section 106 agreements have been developed since 2023.

Protecting Historic Properties
There has been no change in how BNL manages compliance with NHPA since 2023. However, the
amount of time devoted to cultural resources continues to increase with the need to complete evaluations
of buildings, structures, and infrastructure greater than fifty-years of age, and several new additions to the
list of National Register eligible buildings, structures, sites and new construction potentially impacting
cultural resources. To meet this demand, BNL hired its first full-time Cultural Resources Manager in late
2024.

No program alternatives to Section 106 have been developed since the 2023 report.

No new partnerships have been developed with outside organizations for the protection of cultural
resources. Evaluation of historic properties has occurred through contracts with qualified
individuals/commercial organizations.

Using Historic Properties
Since its inception, BNL has utilized many of the original WW II Camp Upton buildings and other
facilities, including roads, railroad lines, firebreaks, and landfills. Several buildings were relocated and/or
joined together to form larger structures. While many WW II-era structures have been replaced as part of
ongoing development at BNL, many original camp buildings are still in use today. Major ground
disturbing actions have included construction of the major science and support facilities and associated
utility (water, electric, communications, etc.) infrastructure. Additional actions include the development
of an additional landfill (all landfills have been closed and capped), and environmental remediation
activities such as access ways, well drilling, and soil removal. Although some Camp Upton artifacts have
been recovered during excavation activities, the highly developed areas of BNL are unlikely to yield any
substantial below-ground cultural resources.

Historic properties dating to the post-1947 era and associated with scientific and technological
advancements continue to be used for scientific experiment and administrative purposes.

Successes, Opportunities and Challenges
The Cultural Resource Program is involved in public education and outreach that serves to highlight the
history of the Laboratory and its historic resources, as well as encourage stewardship of historic resources
through archaeology and historic preservation. These activities draw members of the surrounding public
to the Laboratory for on-site educational programming and provide off-site resources in educational
settings. Several programs, including architectural walking tours and history presentations, have proven
successful in drawing attention to the history of the Laboratory, the significance and success of scientific
innovation, and the importance of historic preservation.

Past efforts in evaluating properties over fifty-years of age and negotiating various MOAs between DOE-
BHSO and the New York SHPO have highlighted the potential for increased historic exposure for the Lab
and the local community. For instance, several kiosks were developed for compliance with Memoranda of
Agreement (MOA) for the Brookhaven Medical Research Reactor (BMRR) Stack Removal project and
Discovery Park (which included the 1960s Era Apartments Demolition project). The kiosks provide
historical information and imagery highlighting several aspects of BNL site history, including the WWI
and II hospital area, roadways through the Camp Upton property, and medical research that took place at
the BMRR and Medical Department. Four of these kiosks have been installed around the Science and

User Support Center (SUSC), which is open to the public; two kiosks were installed outside of Building
490 (Figure 3).

 Figure 3. Kiosks with historic site information at Brookhaven National Laboratory.

Community outreach in 2024 included support for an exhibit and programming at the Bellport-
Brookhaven Historical Society. Their 2024 summer exhibit, entitled The Innovators: Inventors
and Scientists of Bellport, featured the histories of several scientists and inventors of BNL and their
families, particularly those that settled in the Bellport-Brookhaven area. BNL supported the exhibit with a
loan of historic materials and with a lecture about BNL history entitled Technology and the Future of
Medicine. In addition, two public-facing presentations were made in 2024 on the history of Camp Upton
at the Sayville Library and the Middle Country Library.

Community outreach in 2025 included an update on cultural resources to the Community Advisory
Council (CAC) in May, and a short presentation entitled “Remembering Camp Upton”
at BNL’s Open House event in June. A lecture on the history of WW II Camp Upton titled “Boots to
Breakthroughs: The Legacy of Camp Upton” was also presented at the Southampton History
Museum to complement their exhibit on WW II at the Southampton Veterans Memorial Hall in
September. In addition, several educational visits were made to local elementary and middle schools in
the William Floyd, Eastport-South Manor, and Shoreham-Wading River school districts to
support Science, Technology, Engineering, and Math (STEM) clubs who were participating in a
global robotics competition that focused on the theme “Unearthed.” These visits provided the
students with access to a professional in archaeology who could answer their questions about the work
archaeologists do and the potential problems that they face in the field to meet the requirements of their
project designs. Presentations on archaeology were also given to the Long Island History class and the
entire ninth grade Global Studies student body at the Eastport-South Manor Junior Senior High School in
November.

In 2026, the Cultural Resource Management Program developed curatorial exhibits and an historic lecture
on Brookhaven’s site history from 1776 through Camp Upton and its evolution into Brookhaven
Laboratory for BNL’s Open House events in June, July, and August in celebration of the United States
semiquincentennial.

The Cultural Resource Management Program has also demonstrated success in stewardship and
professional development by hosting interns yearly through the Science Undergraduate Laboratory
Internship (SULI) program. In 2024, one intern conducted historical research on magnet development for
accelerator science at the Cosmotron, the Alternating Gradient Synchrotron (AGS), and the Relativistic
Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC). Two interns were hosted in 2025: one intern conducted an analysis of WWI
uniforms and how they can give us insights into the experiences of the soldiers who wore them, and
another intern examined 19th Century lifeways in the Pine Barrens through an archaeological lens (Figure
4). The latter research project was extended with the same intern returning in 2026, and research results
were presented at the Council for Northeast Historical Archaeological Annual Conference in November
of 2025 and the Theoretical Archaeology Group in New York City in 2026.
