 SECTION II: EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The purpose of this project was to investigate the synthesis of ethylene via the endothermic
ethane dehydrogenation (EDH) reaction using solar thermal energy as process heat. Ethylene is
a critical chemical precursor and has among the highest carbon- and energy-intensity of
commodity chemicals. Thus, ethylene production is a prime candidate for decarbonization
efforts. Incorporation of renewable solar thermal heat into an EDH reactor was proposed by
means of thermal energy storage (TES) particle media heated in a solar receiver with
concentrated sunlight. The TES media would circulate into an EDH reactor to transfer stored
heat to ethane reactant gas streams. Key technical results in this work include:

 • Demonstration of ethylene production via heat transferred by TES media, achieving
 targets of > 80% selectivity and > 60% yield
 • Sustained ethylene production by heat stored in the TES media
 • Identification of TES materials thermally and chemically compatible with EDH reactor
 conditions

These results were conveyed through several technical conference presentations, publications,
and manuscript submissions. Further non-technical results include the mentorship of several
undergraduate Chemical Engineering students by senior team members from Sandia National
Laboratories and the University of New Mexico.

Major Goals and Objectives
The primary goal of this project was to evaluate strategies for integrating renewable solar
thermal process heat into an ethane dehydrogenation (EDH) reactor for production of ethylene.
Demonstrating the potential of TES media to store and transfer heat to ethane reactant gas for
dehydrogenation to ethylene was a primary objective. Storage of excess thermal energy in TES
media was proposed for overcoming the intermittency of solar light by providing heat during
off-sun hours. Chemical and thermal stability of the TES media in EDH reactors was thus central
to project objectives. Computational modeling informed by experimental EDH results were
planned to yield insight into operational requirements for scaling a benchtop EDH reactor
toward an industrial solar thermal ethylene process.

Technical Achievements
This project verified the potential of thermal energy storage media to effectively transfer heat
to gas-phase reactants within ethane dehydrogenation reactors and to retain heat for sustained
ethylene production beyond that achieved in conventional empty tube reactors. Alumina and
quartz media maintained thermal and chemical compatibility with the operating temperatures
and gaseous environments expected for ethane dehydrogenation reactors. These media
resulted in significantly lower rates of coke formation than commercial solar thermal media
containing transition metal oxides. The minor amounts of coke that formed on alumina and
quartz media during the EDH reaction could be removed through oxidation in air. By cycling the
media between the dehydrogenation reactor and solar receiver, or by introducing air to the

EDH reactor during a regeneration step, the minor coke that forms could be effectively
removed from the reactor via combustion. This process was identified as an effective strategy
for reheating the media, as well as preventing pressure drop within the reactor due to excessive
coke accumulation.

Impact
Results from this project inform future decarbonization efforts using solar thermal energy for
renewable, carbon-free process heat. TES media are a crucial component of these systems to
store excess heat for use during off sun hours to ensure process continuity. While TES media
have been studied for heating within air, this project further evaluated various media within
high temperature redox environments including hydrocarbon gases. Compatible TES media
materials were identified according to their maintained structural and chemical stability under
EDH reactor operating conditions, as well as their minimized coke byproduct formation. These
results validate the use of heated media for direct heat transfer to gas phase hydrocarbon
reactants for chemical upgrading applications.

project scope. Kinetic modeling of the ethane dehydrogenation reaction proved difficult due to
the significant influence of heat transfer effects, which limit the extent of reaction. Use of
circulating particle reactor systems proved to be outside the current project scope but is a
recommended area of study for future work. After consultation with the DOE technical
manager, the 6-month NCTE period was used to expand the experimental test matrix of
reaction conditions and TES materials, while the computational effort was limited to answer
key scale-up questions rather than exploring designs for prototype systems.

Background on ethylene production

Page 3 of 25

 Bush, Riley & Spoerke

Ethylene is a critical chemical precursor to plastics and other commodity chemicals with a
current global annual production volume near 200 million metric tons.[1] Ethylene is
conventionally produced via industrial steam cracking of concentrated ethane or naphtha
feedstocks. These feedstocks are cofed with steam into furnace coils heated to 850 – 950 °C
through natural gas combustion. High temperatures are required given the high
endothermicity of ethylene forming reactions, exemplified by the ethane dehydrogenation
reaction (C2H6 → C2H4 + H2, ΔHrxn = 137 KJ/mol). Cryogenic separation of product gases adds
significantly to process energy requirements. All told, ethane steam cracking is among the most
energy- and carbon-intensive of chemical processes, requiring approximately 16 GJ and
emitting over 1 ton of carbon dioxide per ton of ethylene produced.[2]

Decarbonization efforts have proposed use of alternative chemical pathways, feedstocks, and
energy sources for ethylene production. Oxidative dehydrogenation of ethane studies leverage
catalysts and either oxygen or carbon dioxide co-reactants to produce ethylene through less
endothermic reaction pathways. However, addition of an oxidizing agent enables highly
exothermic reactions, which favor combustion of ethane to water and carbon dioxide rather
than dehydrogenation to ethylene.[3] Thus, these reactions typically suffer from reduced
ethylene selectivity and yield. Use of alternative feedstocks with reduced ethane concentration
can enable similar ethylene yields at lower temperatures than conventional steam cracking.
This effect is related to the thermodynamic equilibrium conversion of ethane to ethylene,
which is inversely correlated to ethane concentration. Dehydrogenation of ethane within
natural gas, which typically has 3-15% concentration, is thus more favorable than in steam
cracking processes with ethane concentrations near 67%. Given the high thermal energy
requirements of ethane dehydrogenation, this project explores renewable sources of process
heat to provide a valuable pathway to decarbonize ethylene production. This project thus aims
to explore strategies for use of carbon-free solar thermal energy in ethane dehydrogenation
reaction.

SECTION III: TASKS AND MILESTONE PROGRESS

Benchmarking performance in lab-scale EDH reactors
Initial experiments evaluated the EDH reaction within empty 4 millimeter (mm) inner diameter
quartz tube reactors with and without packed beds of TES media. Tube reactors were mounted
vertically within a clamshell-style furnace. Gas was fed vertically downward through the
reactor into the heated furnace chamber. 70% ethylene yield was achieved with 96% ethylene
selectivity when flowing a 5% ethane in nitrogen feed gas at 20 ml/min into the reactor at 775
°C (Figure 1). These results achieved the performance metrics specified in Milestone 0.0. 20
mg of sintered bauxite beads were added to the reactor and the same experimental conditions
repeated. Similar ethylene selectivity and yields were achieved with the beads. Ethane
conversion both with and without the packed bed was approximately 75%. Ethylene was
produced using heat transferred to the gas phase by heated TES media, which accomplished
project Milestone 1.1.

nitrogen feed gas

Evaluation of reaction kinetics
The kinetics of EDH reaction were next evaluated in empty quartz tube reactors by evaluating
the effects of residence time and applying 1st order kinetic models to experimental data.
Residence time (τ) is a measure of how long gaseous reactants spend within the heated region
of the reactor and was varied by changing both the quartz tube inner diameter and the total
flow rate of 5% ethane through the reactor tubes. Figure 2a shows ethane conversion as a
function of temperature using a 4 mm inner diameter quartz tube reactor and varying the total
flow from 20 to 40 ml/min. Increasing total flow rate caused a decrease in residence time and a

resulting drop in ethane conversion. As expected, this result indicated that an increase in
reactant throughput will cause a decrease in ethylene yield when operating at similar
temperatures, which poses a challenge for scaling ethylene production operations. But this can
be overcome by increasing operating temperature, up to a limit, where a drop in selectivity
starts to impact overall yield.

Figure 2b shows fractional ethane conversion observed experimentally as data points for the 4
mm inner diameter quartz tube reactor with residence time of 1.4 seconds and a 2 mm inner
diameter quartz tube reactor with a residence time of 0.18 seconds. 1st order kinetic fits were
applied to the experimental data accounting for the reverse reaction and are shown as the red
and purple curves. Extracted rate constants for the forward reaction (ethane dehydrogenation)
were not identical for the two reactors when operated at the same temperature but different
residence times, as listed in Table 1. This result indicated difficulty in fitting the experimental
data to predicted global kinetic models that ignore the effect of other factors. For instance,
heat transfer is known to limit the extent of highly endothermic reactions and was predicted to
play a significant role in EDH results obtained in this work.

Scaling reactor and operational parameters
Subsequent experiments were conducted in which the reactor dimensions and operating
parameters were scaled from initial tests. Ethane concentrations of 5, 50, and 95% in nitrogen
were fed into a 22 mm inner diameter quartz reactor tube housing a quartz rod to achieve an

Page 6 of 25

 Bush, Riley & Spoerke

annular reactor configuration. Total flow rates of 400 and 800 standard cubic centimeters per
minute (sccm) were used. Results provided in Figure 3 show target metrics of > 60% ethylene
yield per Milestone 2.1 were achieved, even while scaling total flow rate of 5% ethane from
initial test conditions. In agreement with thermodynamic conversion limitations of the
endothermic EDH reaction, ethane conversion decreased as its concentration in the feed gas
increased. Use of an annular reactor configuration was expected to improve heat transfer to
the gas phase reactants.

Figure 3. Ethane conversion (left) and ethylene yield (right) measured in an annular quartz tube reactor using 5, 50,
and 95% ethane feed gas concentrations and total flow rates of 400 and 800 sccm.

After evaluating 5-95% ethane feedstocks, an intermediate ethane concentration of 67% was
used for subsequent testing in empty quartz tube reactors of varying inner diameter (4, 10.5,
and 22 mm). This ethane concentration is representative of that used in industrial steam
crackers. Total gas flow rate was varied to change space velocity of the reactors, which was
calculated as the ratio of total flow rate to the volume inside the reactor tube situated within
the 12 inch (in)-long heated chamber of the horizontal tube furnace. Temperatures within the
tube reactors were measured at 1 in intervals to verify that similar temperature profiles existed
within the tubes. At similar space velocities, larger reactor tubes resulted in a significant drop
in ethane conversion (Figure 4). This finding was explained by heat transfer limitations with
increasing tube diameter, i.e., as reactor dimensions scaled, the transfer of heat from the
exterior of the reactor walls to the gas phase reactants became less efficient. Process heat
inputs and reactor wall temperatures would need to be increased as the reactor was scaled to
achieve the same levels of ethane conversion and ethylene yield.

Transitioning reactor design toward industrial relevance
Quartz tube reactors were used in early experimental work due to chemical inertness, thermal
stability, and optical transparency, which enabled visual observations of coke formation and
TES media degradation. However, the poor scalability and brittleness of quartz would limit use
as an industrial reactor material. More industrially-relevant 316 stainless-steel tube reactors
with 22 mm inner diameter were evaluated in subsequent experiments. An inert silicon-based
coating was applied to the tubes by SilcoTek prior to testing. The previous experimental
conditions were applied to the stainless-steel tube reactors. A furnace set point temperature of
760 °C was initially applied to the stainless-steel reactors to match the conditions used for the
quartz tubes. However, this temperature was increased to 775 °C in later trials to achieve the
same maximum temperature within the stainless-steel reactors as measured in the 22 mm
inner diameter quartz tube. Ethane conversion was calculated via mass spectrometer
measurements of ethane and argon concentrations of feed gas directed through a bypass line
compared to measurements of gas exiting the reactor at temperature. A calibration curve was
prepared to convert ethane and argon mass spectrometer signals to mole percent by adjusting
the flow rates of these gases simultaneously fed through the bypass line into the spectrometer.

Differences in the temperature profiles of the quartz and stainless-steel reactor tubes, shown in
Figure 5, exemplified the dissimilar thermal properties of these two materials. Improved
thermal conductivity of stainless-steel facilitated thermal energy transport away from the
heated center of the furnace and throughout the reactor walls, leading to a lower maximum
internal temperature. Accordingly, ethane conversion was lower in the stainless-steel tube
than in the quartz tube at the same furnace set point temperature. However, when the set
point temperature was increased to achieve similar maximum internal temperatures, the
stainless-steel tube achieved comparable ethane conversion to the quartz tube as a function of
space velocity (Figure 5b). These results illustrate the challenge of effectively transferring

sufficient heat to perform highly endothermic reactions by externally heating tube reactors
made of industrially-relevant materials, and eventually at industrially-relevant scales.

Assessment of TES media for scaled ethylene production
Incorporation of TES media into the EDH reactor was expected to facilitate heat transfer to the
gas phase reactants by limiting the amount of free volume and increasing the area of heated
surfaces. Alumina ball milling media of 1, 3, or 5 mm diameters were added as 12 in-long
packed beds between quartz wool plugs within the heated region of 22 mm inner diameter
stainless-steel reactor tubes. 67% ethane in argon was again fed through the reactor tubes,
and the total flow rate was varied to achieve a similar range of space velocities as in the empty
tube reactors. When calculating space velocity, free volume of the alumina-loaded tubes was
calculated by weighing the mass of media required to fill half the tube volume and dividing by
the reported milling media solid density. Volume of the media was subtracted from the total
volume of the heated region of the empty tubes.

At the highest space velocity tested in both empty and loaded reactor configurations, addition
of alumina TES media increased ethane conversion by approximately 10% (Figure 6). Ethane
conversion was similar among reactors loaded with 1, 3, and 5 mm alumina media at low space
velocity. However, addition of 5 mm media improved conversion by several percent over the
smaller particles at high space velocity. This finding was somewhat counter-intuitive, as
particles of smaller size provide higher total surface area, which would be expected to facilitate
heat transfer to the gas phase. Previous work demonstrated that addition of a catalyst or inert
materials, such as metal oxides, with high surface area to EDH reactors caused a drop in
performance.[4] Small particles decrease the size of voids in which the thermochemical gas-
phase reactions that convert ethane occur. Surfaces may also provide active sites to quench
the involved free radical intermediates of these reactions, potentially contributing to decreased

Page 9 of 25

 Bush, Riley & Spoerke

ethane conversion. Nonetheless, further work is required to fully understand these TES particle
size effects.

Figure 6. Ethane conversion as a function of space velocity (a) and ethane conversion over time after furnace
power was shut off (b) using a 22 mm stainless-steel reactor with and without various alumina media.

A key operational feature of TES media is heat retention within the EDH reactor to sustain
ethylene production. This property was evaluated by stabilizing temperature within the empty
and loaded stainless steel reactor tubes containing the variously sized alumina ball milling
media. Total flow rate of 67% ethane feed gas was adjusted to achieve the same space velocity
within each reactor configuration. Furnace power was subsequently shut off and ethane
conversion was monitored over time as the reactor cooled. The resulting profiles are plotted in
Figure 6b. Regardless of particle size, inclusion of TES media with the stainless-steel reactor
sustained similar extents of ethane conversion for 4-5 min longer than observed in the empty
stainless-steel tube reactor. Collectively, these results demonstrated effective heat transfer
from the TES media to the gas phase for ethylene production and heat retention for sustained
ethylene production in-line with project Milestone 3. While the ethylene signal in mass spectra
increased corresponding to a decrease in the ethane signal, the characteristic ethane signal
overlaps that of ethylene and prevents an exact quantification of ethylene yield. While the
alumina TES and silicon-coated reactor surfaces are assumed to be inert, future work is
recommended to verify ethylene yields with GC analysis of EDH in the stainless-steel reactors.

Chemical and thermal stability of TES media
Compatibility of TES materials under the chemically and thermally severe environments of EDH
reactors was identified as a critical requirement of proposed solar thermal-circulating particle
chemical reactors. Accelerated degradation tests were conducted by loading 5 g packed beds
of TES media within 22 mm inner diameter quartz tubes. 1 mm Alumina milling beads, quartz
sand, silicon carbide, and sintered bauxite (Carbo HSP 40/70 Carbobeads) were compared.
Media were exposed to redox cycling conditions, which included 100% ethane flow at 825 °C
for 30 minutes, followed by cooling and purging of the reactor with argon, and then flowing air
at 800 °C for 1 hr. These conditions were intended to simulate circulation of the particles

Page 10 of 25

 Bush, Riley & Spoerke

through a solar receiver in air and subsequent flow through an EDH reactor with hydrocarbon
exposure. Samples were initially subjected to four cycles of this treatment. Aliquots of media
were removed between redox cycles to investigate signs of degradation via materials
characterization. A summary of characterization methods is provided in the Appendix.

Digital and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the quartz sand and alumina milling
media before and after redox cycles are shown in Figure 7. These media showed no signs of
degradation after testing. Individual particles maintained structural integrity, and surface
morphology appeared unaffected. Alumina media were subjected to six additional redox
cycles, but degradation was still not observed visually. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns similarly
showed no evolution of the quartz and corundum phases of the sand and alumina media,
respectively. No amorphous peaks, indicative of significant coke formation, were observed
(Figure 8). Quartz sand similarly maintained structural integrity after four redox cycles. Carbo
HSP, on the other hand, demonstrated significant degradation during redox cycles. While
silicon carbide media was not evaluated for signs of degradation, this media was down-selected
from further testing given the significant coke formation that was visibly observed after ethane
exposure in the first redox cycle. TES media degradation and related characterization results
are discussed further in the Appendix. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of spent TES media in
Figure 9 verified significant coking of this media after exposure to 100% ethane, as evidenced
by substantial mass loss during reoxidation in air. Judging from the massive exothermic peak in
the corresponding differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) profile, heat released during
reoxidation of the Carbo HSP likely created severely high local temperatures at the particle
surface and facilitated fracturing of the particles.

Figure 7. Digital images (top row) and SEM secondary electron images (bottom row) of quartz sand (a) and 1 mm
alumina milling media (b) in as-received or fresh state and after 4 and 10 redox cycles with exposure to 100%
ethane at 825 °C for 30 minutes, followed by air exposure at 800 °C for 1 hour.

As compared to those for Carbo HSP, TGA and DSC profiles of the spent alumina milling media
and quartz sand showed negligible mass loss when the sample was reoxidized. These materials
thus were determined to not promote significant coke formation. Alumina and quartz are
highly chemically inert and are not expected to catalyze coke formation, in contrast to
observations for transition metals. Further, alumina milling media and quartz sand are
relatively inexpensive materials currently produced at industrial scales. High sphericity,

Computational modeling of solar ethylene production
A preliminary scaling analysis was performed to estimate the attainable ethylene production
from a concentrating solar thermal (CST)-driven EDH process. An exit particle and gas
temperature of 750 °C was assumed in consideration of experimentally observed (qualitative)
reaction kinetics, ethane chemical conversion, gas selectivity, and ethylene yield, with the

Page 12 of 25

 Bush, Riley & Spoerke

intention of maintaining the project metrics of >80% conversion and >60% yield. The exit
temperature value was intentionally generous in the context of the range of experimental
results as a function of variables such as particle size, ethane concentration, and flow rate to
produce a reasonable upper-level estimate of scaled-up plant performance.

Figure 10 depicts (a) the relative mass flow rate of TES media (here, Carbo HSP 4070, as it is
well-characterized at elevated temperatures) for a pilot plant producing 1 ton C2H4 ∙ day-1 and
(b) the ratio of TES mass to feed ethane volume flow rates for an equilibrium process. The
figures depict the decreased TES inventory required as the ΔT across the EDH reactor increased.
The red curves in this scaling analysis indicated that, under ideal conditions, a pilot plant
roughly the scale of the Generation 3 Particle Pilot Plant (G3P3) at Sandia (in terms of ~1 kg/s
continuous TES mass flow rate) could be used to produce the target of 1 ton C2H4 in a day,
assuming the falling particle receiver could be used to heat TES media to 900 – 1000 °C with a
solar multiple capable of charging sufficient TES volume to run 24/7. While these parameters
are not identical to those for G3P3, the scaling analysis provides a rough order of magnitude
estimate of the CST system scale required. This result must be considered in the context of the
~105 ton C2H4 ∙ day-1 production of the US ethylene industry meaning that, while pilot scale
deployment is feasible, full decarbonization of the C2H4 industry may require more in-depth
systems considerations, including reducing operational temperature, hybridization, and
alternative feedstocks amenable to smaller-scale operations.

the ratio of TES media mass to feed ethane volume flow rates as a function of particle entrance temperature (b),

If a process were devised in which the reaction temperature range could be decreased to a
lower-end value of 500 instead of 750 °C, the required input could be cut by more than half, as
shown by the blue lines in Figure 10. However, while the thermodynamics of the EDH reaction

predict C2H4 formation at these temperatures, the kinetics and selectivity of the process would
like present engineering and economic challenges, meaning advancements to enhance both
characteristics would be necessary for a lower-temperature process to be feasible. Therefore,
the computational modeling study did not consider such low temperature ranges except for
preheating and heat recuperation. To make a high-temperature process more appealing, hybrid
processes may be of interest if they could be justified economically and operationally. These
include utilization of the exiting particles for heat exchange processes to drive electricity
production, steam generation, desalination, hot water heating, or thermochemical air
separation. Other, lower temperature cycles for the production or processing of hydrocarbons
may prove more appealing from a location and capabilities standpoint, however.

A thermodynamic system model of a CST-integrated EDH plant was next constructed to
understand the impact of reactor configurations, recuperation/preheat, and solar receiver
thermodynamics on the upper-level performance of a future plant. The model, depicted
schematically in Figure 11, was based upon an architecture previously used in the analysis of
solar thermochemical processes.[5, 6] An idealized, isothermal particle receiver was assumed,
with thermal loses restricted to radiative heat transfer and a solar concentration ratio of 1000.
An EDH reactor was defined in which heat and mass balances and chemical equilibrium at the
gas exit were enforced. Heat recuperation and/or feed gas preheating via counterflow gas/gas
and particle/gas heat exchangers was included. The EDH reactor model was configured to
consider co-current or countercurrent operation and enforce yields below thermodynamic
equilibrium to better match experimentally-observed conversion or yield limitations imposed
by reaction kinetics and/or secondary reactions.

While C2H4, C2H6, H2, and a nonreacting sweep gas (e.g., CH4 or N2) were the only species
considered in the EDH reactor, the solar receiver did support the consideration of heating and
CO2 production from the combustion of deposited coke on the TES particles. However, this
component was neglected in the reported model simulations due to the finding of minimal
coking with quartz and alumina media. The TES media were modeled as Carbo HSP, meaning a
real system likely would need to incorporate multiple particle media streams and heat
exchangers to avoid coking while maintaining a sufficiently large, solar-receiver with compatible
TES inventory for 24/7 production—or to identify a more chemically inert alternative to Carbo
HSP as a combined TES and reactor bed media.

In general, the system model predicted some expected trends for an equilibrium CST-paired
EDH process. Both efficiency and conversion eventually decreased with growing feed rate,
capturing the “depletion” of the TES media as the gas flow rate grew. Additionally, a gas
concentration dependence for conversion was identified due to the difference in specific heats
between C2H6 and CH4, while the dependence for efficiency was largely influenced by the
impact of parasitic heating of the nonreacting balance gas. A countercurrent reactor
configuration was found to be preferable for scale-up in terms of efficiency, conversion, and
maximizing the gas-to-particle flow rate, but would present additional engineering challenges
associated with a downward-flowing packed bed.

Recommendations for solar ethylene reactor design
The experimental and computational findings of this project have several important
implications for the design and scale-up of EDH reactors to product C2H4 utilizing solar thermal
heat. Carbobead HSP 40/70 has been selected for the G3P3 project due to an appealing balance
of properties relating to size, shape, stability, and thermal transport. Beyond being highly stable
at elevated temperatures in air, the particles have high solar absorptance, increasing the
theoretical solar receiver efficiency. The 40/70 size range of the media additionally allows for
rapid heating in relatively short receiver residence times while mitigating some of the
agglomeration behaviors exhibited by sub-100 µm particles at high temperatures.[5] These
particles have also already been found economically competitive for meeting SETO levelized
cost targets [6] and have been characterized for thermal transport, flowability [7], attrition,
abrasion [8], and oxidative surface thermal aging.[9] While sand is being researched as an
alternative low cost Gen 3 media [10], most candidates have significantly lower surface
absorptance properties. Alumina, which was also observed to maintain stability for EDH
conditions, suffers from a similar problem, along with being potentially expensive. For these
reasons, for a concentrating solar process, Carbo HSP represents a significantly lower risk
option than novel alternatives studied here. For particle applications which are (1) high
temperature, (2) moving, and (3) integrated with thermal storage, they are an appealing first
choice, and all these characteristics are applicable to a process like solar EDH heat transfer.

On the other hand, alumina media have precedence for use in catalytic processes as catalysts
and catalyst support media, for example in fluid catalytic cracking (FCC) in the petrochemical
industry, or as supports for precious metals in catalytic converters used for air pollution control.
Stationary solids in direct contact with reactive gases—shown to be problematic for Carbobead
HSP—has been thoroughly demonstrated in these and other applications.
Deactivation/poisoning and physical breakdown of valuable catalyst media must be mitigated,
however, meaning the unique dynamic conditions for CST particles have not been so
thoroughly de-risked in media intended to facilitate a chemical reaction. With this context in
mind, we make the following recommendations for future solar ethylene reactor design and
scale-up:

 1. Multiple media inventories, indirect contact: This concept is generally depicted in Figure
 15. Separating the roles of particle media into heat storage particles which only directly

 interact with containment surfaces and air and reactor bed media which remain sealed
 from the environment addresses several operational complexities. First, challenges
 related to maintaining an air-free reactive environment for the non-oxidative EDH
 reaction are mitigated. While not a problem in our bench-scale studies, such an
 engineering challenge would otherwise emerge when designing seals and particle
 conveyance devices, and may be particularly challenging when working with dense
 and/or powdery flowing beds. Second, media are optimized for their respective roles:
 flowable, absorptive (and typically redox-active iron-containing) proppants for heat
 transfer and storage in an economical manner, and more chemically inert ceramics, with
 larger particle sizes if necessary to prevent pressure drop issues, as internal surface area
 enhancement media for transferring heat to the gas stream. Not only does such a design
 mitigate coking, which our work showed was challenging to avoid using Carbobead HS
 40/70, but when de-coking must occur, it can be done in a closed-system manner which
 allows greater ease of CO2 capture. Third, by not requiring the particle media internal to
 the EDH reactor to pass through the solar receiver and thermal energy storage, attrition
 of the media may be mitigated and media lifetimes extended.
 2. Counterflow operation: The advantages of a countercurrent configuration between the
 heat storage media and gas streams takes advantage of gravitational conveyance of
 particles with the potential effect of mitigating buoyancy effects in the gas domain. Such
 operation also allows for greater potential for recuperative and/or preheating of media
 outside the EDH reactor zone. A high-heat transfer coefficient reactor design such as a
 fluidized bed may be necessary to ensure indirect heat transfer from the thermal
 storage media to the EDH reactor media, which would have the added advantage of
 diminishing temperature gradients within the EDH reactor.
 3. Stationary reactor beds: Non-moving bed media achieve the goal of heat transfer
 enhancement via increased surface area without the technical complexity of particle
 conveyance at high temperatures. This reactor configuration is more industrially
 familiar, increasing the ease of integration for a future pilot demonstration. A non-
 moving system also offers greater compatibility with potential catalytic processes, in
 which avoiding thermal and mechanical degradation of catalyst media is essential.

 SECTION IV: Conclusions and Recommendations

This project evaluated key design elements of a solar thermal reactor for producing ethylene
through the ethane dehydrogenation reaction. Chemical and thermal compatibility of thermal
energy storage media materials within ethane dehydrogenation reactor environments were
evaluated. Media loaded within bench-scale dehydrogenation reactors effectively transferred
heat to the gas phase ethane reactant for ethylene production and further retained heat within
the reactor to extend this reaction for greater lengths of time after powering off the heating
source than that achieved in conventional empty tube reactor configuration. Alumina milling
media and quartz sand were identified as thermally and chemically compatible thermal energy
storage materials, which are advantageously inexpensive and currently manufactured at
industrial scales. Commercial media materials containing transition metals showed phase
evolution during reaction and significant coke formation. These media degraded during
reheating in air to combust coke.

While the systems model provided useful information for future reactor design and the tradeoff
between performance metrics, the simple scaling analysis provided some of the most
important implications for process-scale up. Modeling indicated that one ton ethylene per day
pilot-scale production could be achieved with a Generation 3 Particle Pilot Plant (G3P3)-like
concentrating solar thermal facility. However, some factors suggest that an independent solar
ethylene facility may have significant scaling challenges. The high receiver temperature and
relatively small temperature drop of the EDH would result in a large amount of unused sensible
heat from the solar receiver and require large particle inventories for 24/7 operation. This
consideration is particularly impactful considering the degradation observed for Carbo HSP
media during the EDH reaction. Together, these challenges may require a few specific design or
operational strategies for future development:

 1. Hybridization: temperatures above the ~800 °C operation range of Generation 3 falling
 particle receivers combined with moderate temperature drops through the ethylene
 reactor may necessitate pairing ethylene production with other, lower-temperature
 processes to improve plant economics. These may include electricity production via a
 sCO2 or conventional steam Rankine cycle, steam generation and hot water heating for
 industrial heat processes, desalination, thermochemical air separation to produce high-
 purity O2, or co-production of other hydrocarbons or downstream processing of ethane
 to produce other commodity chemicals. Dedicated technoeconomics modeling will be
 necessary in future work to justify the economic feasibility of continuing the high-
 temperature pathway versus pursuing chemistries with lower reaction temperatures.
 2. Lower temperature processes: catalytic or thermochemical methods to drive the ethane
 reaction temperature lower may mitigate heat losses and improve plant economics

Page 20 of 25

 Bush, Riley & Spoerke

 while still enabling renewable thermal integration. In our project, alumina media,
 already used in catalytic processes, showed promise for direct heat transfer to the
 reacting gas stream. Such processes are of particular interest to industrial entities
 working in the areas of catalysis, reactor engineering, and chemicals production, with
 whom the project team have socialized our project. Catalyst media generally must be
 able to not just enhance the reaction chemistry in a lab setting, but also demonstrate
 many reaction cycles without deactivation or have a low-cost method for periodic
 regeneration, so the technical risk of pursuing a novel process should be weighed
 against the economic advantages it may offer.
 3. Alternative feedstocks: the significant scale of conventional ethylene production from
 natural gas is also a challenge for the development and economics of solar integration,
 suggesting that smaller-scale production with alternative feedstocks (e.g., biogas) may
 be more feasible for pilot-scale demonstration and scale-up. Direct conversion of dilute
 ethane within natural gas would leverage more favorable thermodynamics than
 conversion of concentrated ethane feed gases and could enable a decrease in
 operational temperature.
 4. Multiple particle media streams: The large attainable scale and flowability of particles
 like Carbo HSP are highly appealing for solar thermal systems, but less economical
 media like alumina are more appealing for the ethylene dehydrogenation chemistry to
 limit coking. Therefore, future development might consider heat exchangers which
 enable the interfacing of a large inventory of flowing, renewably heated media with
 stationary or minimally-conveyed heat transfer/catalyst media to facilitate the process
 chemistry.
 More chemically-resistant particles: There is ongoing parallel work from previous
 collaborators in developing solar heat transfer media which are more chemically inert.
 Commercial options, including Carbo Kryptosphere, may also be of future interest,
 although they have not yet been evaluated for EDH reaction conditions. Inert particle
 coatings may also be worthy of consideration, although they face challenges including
 coating degradation due to particle-particle and particle-surface attrition. The costs and
 engineering risks of designing a compatible material with a long lifetime would need to
 be weighed against those of Strategy 4.

 APPENDIX

Materials and Methods
TES media were purchased through commercial chemical and equipment suppliers. These
materials include quartz sand (50-70 mesh size, Aldrich), alumina milling media (1, 3, and 5 mm,
MSE Supplies, LLC), silicon carbide, and sintered bauxite (HSP 40/70 Carbobeads, Carbo
Ceramic, Inc.). TES media were characterized before and after use in EDH reactors via x-ray
diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), and
differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). XRD analysis was conducted with a Bruker D2 Phaser
operating with Bragg-Brentano geometry and a Cukα x-ray source. Samples were measured
from 10-80 ° 2θ range with 0.02 ° step size. An SNE ALPHA SEM was used to collect sample
images in secondary electron mode. TGA and DSC analysis were performed using a NETZSCH
STA 449F3. A summary of reactor, thermal energy storage media, and test parameters is
provided in Table S1.

Characterization of HSP Carbo in redox cycles
Carbo HSP were evaluated in the previously described cyclic redox testing, involving exposure
to flowing 100% ethane at 825 °C for 30 minutes, followed by air flow at 800 °C for 1 hour.
Digital and SEM images of the beads before and after 4 redox cycles are shown in Figure A1.
Carbo HSP degraded chemically and structurally during this testing. The coloration of the beads
changed from dark gray to reddish-brown after reoxidation. Individual beads also fragmented
into a fine powder. This change in structure would affect flowability of the beads in a
circulating particle reactor. XRD patterns in Figure A2 show how the crystalline phases present
within Carbo HSP changed throughout redox cycling. Transition metal oxide phases, such as
manganese aluminate spinel formed after exposure to 100% ethane. Graphitic carbon peaks
were also detected. SEM micrographs in Figure A3 show carbon filaments which formed over
the bead surface during ethane exposure. TGA data presented in Figure 9 corroborate
significant coke formation over the beads. DSC data taken during reoxidation of the beads
measured a significant exotherm upon combustion of the coke. Heat released during this
process likely raised temperatures at the bead surface much higher than the furnace set point
temperature. These high temperatures, as well as the evolution of crystalline phases during
redox cycling, likely caused degradation of the Carbo HSP media. Similar redox testing of silicon
carbide was initiated through its loading in a 4 mm inner diameter quartz tube. However, this
testing was halted after significant coking was observed within the reactor following initial
ethane exposure (Figure A4). On the other hand, the alumina milling media and quartz sand
did not show evolution in crystalline phase nor significant coking and thus were not prone to
degradation.
