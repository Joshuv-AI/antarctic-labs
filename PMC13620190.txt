Unsupervised Clustering of Intradialytic Blood Pressure Patterns and Their Associations With Body Composition in Patients Undergoing Chronic Hemodialysis
Authors: Hongtae Kim, Soie Kwon, Jin Ho Hwang, Jungho Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntradialytic blood pressure (BP) abnormalities are common in patients undergoing chronic hemodialysis (HD), however, their relationship with body composition remains unclear. We investigated the association between intradialytic BP patterns and body composition using an unsupervised clustering approach.MethodsAdult outpatients receiving chronic HD who underwent segmental multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analysis were included. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) trajectories during HD were mapped to prespecified 30-minute time points from 0 to 240 minutes, and changes relative to predialysis SBP were clustered using k-means clustering. Body composition parameters were compared across BP clusters. Linear mixed-effects models with a random intercept for patient identifier were used to account for repeated HD sessions.ResultsAmong 928 HD sessions from 179 patients, three SBP patterns were identified: marked decreasing (16.8%), mild decreasing (46.2%), and rising (37.0%) patterns. Body mass index did not differ across clusters. However, fat-free mass index and skeletal muscle mass index increased from the marked decreasing to the mild decreasing and rising clusters, whereas percent body fat decreased. The extracellular water-to-total body water ratio was highest in the rising cluster, and visceral fat area showed a decreasing trend from the marked decreasing cluster to the rising cluster. These associations remained significant after adjustment. Similar findings were observed in sensitivity analyses.ConclusionUnsupervised clustering identified reproducible intradialytic BP phenotypes that closely associated with volume status and body composition in patients undergoing chronic HD.Keywords: Blood pressure, Body composition, Clustering, Hemodialysis

INTRODUCTION

Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with end-stage kidney disease undergoing chronic hemodialysis (HD) [1]. Blood pressure (BP) is a major determinant of cardiovascular risk; therefore, it should be controlled within acceptable target ranges [2,3]. However, in patients on chronic HD, the association between BP levels and cardiovascular outcomes is less straightforward than in general population, particularly with the distinctive patterns of BP measurements influenced by the HD treatment [4]. In routine chronic HD care, office BP measurements, including peridialytic and intradialytic BP, are frequently recorded and are essential for ensuring safe and effective HD treatment. Despite their clinical utility, the mechanisms underlying HD-related BP changes remain incompletely understood, and the management of abnormal intradialytic BP patterns, such as intradialytic hypotension and hypertension, remains unsatisfactory. Moreover, heterogenous and arbitrary definitions of abnormal intradialytic BP patterns may further hinder a clear understanding of their nature.

Recent advances in body composition assessment have increased its accessibility in clinical practice and enhanced our understanding of its clinical implications. In many HD centers, body composition analysis is regularly performed to guide dry weight adjustment and identify abnormal conditions such as malnutrition and sarcopenia [5]. Emerging evidence suggests a strong connection between body composition and intradialytic BP patterns. During HD treatment, BP decreases as retained extracellular water (ECW) is removed [4]. Excessive ECW removal beyond an individual’s tolerance can precipitate a BP drop and contribute to the development of intradialytic hypotension [6], whereas ECW overload has been associated with intradialytic hypertension [7]. These findings support the role of volume status as a key determinant of abnormal intradialytic BP patterns. In addition, prior studies have reported the associations between intradialytic hypotension and low muscle and fat mass [8,9]. Taken together, body composition analysis can help characterize intradialytic BP patterns and provide insights into the pathophysiologic mechanisms underlying intradialytic hypotension and hypertension.

In this study, we investigated the association between intradialytic BP patterns and body composition in patients undergoingchronic HD. We classified intradialytic BP patterns into clusters using an unsupervised approach to minimize investigator-driven bias. We then explored how these clusters were related to volume status and to muscle and fat mass.

METHODS

Patients

This retrospective study included adult patients with end-stage kidney disease who underwent outpatient chronic HD and body composition analysis between October 2016 and October 2023. Among 185 eligible patients, six were excluded because of missing baseline laboratory data or insufficient BP measurements (BP recordings available for < 180 minutes). Ultimately, 179 patients receiving chronic HD were included, contributing 928 paired BP and body composition measurements.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chung-Ang University Hospital (number: 2310-022-19496). The requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data were obtained from electronic medical records, including age, sex, dialysis vintage, cause of end-stage kidney disease, and comorbidities. Comorbidity burden was assessed using the modified Charlson Comorbidity Index [10]. Additionally, dialysis-related parameters including interdialytic weight gain, dry weight, ultrafiltration volume, and calculated ultrafiltration rate were retrieved. The number of prescribed antihypertensive medications was also collected. Fasting blood samples were obtained before the midweek dialysis session to measure hemoglobin, albumin, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, calcium, phosphorus, uric acid, sodium, potassium, total carbon dioxide, and total cholesterol. Postdialysis blood urea nitrogen was measured to assess dialysis adequacy.

Body composition analysis

Body composition was assessed every 6 months in outpatients undergoing chronic HD using a segmental multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) device (InBody S10; BioSpace, Seoul, Korea). Measurements were performed after midweek dialysis sessions in the supine position. Eight tactile electrodes were placed on the tips of the thumbs and middle fingers of both hands and between the malleoli and calcanei of both feet. The device was regularly calibrated and operated by trained nursing staff according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Parameters obtained from BIA included fat-free mass, skeletal muscle mass, percent body fat (PBF), visceral fat area (VFA), and the ratio of ECW to total body water (ECW/TBW). Body mass index (BMI), fat-free mass index (FFMI), and skeletal muscle mass index (SMI) were calculated as the corresponding mass divided by height squared (kg/m2).

BP measurement

HD was performed using either Fresenius 5008S (Fresenius Medical Care, Homburg, Germany) or Artis (Gambro Dasco SpA, Medolla, Italy) machines. Patients were treated with bicarbonate-based dialysate at standard temperature two or three times weekly for 4 hours per session, with a blood flow rate of 200–350 mL/min.

BP measurements, including predialysis, postdialysis, and intradialytic BP, were obtained in the supine position using automated devices built into the HD machines, which underwent regular calibration. BP data were collected on the same day as the BIA measurements. All systolic and diastolic BP measurements were recorded from predialysis (0 minute) to at least 180 minutes. Because all sessions were scheduled for 4 hours, incomplete BP series were primarily attributable to missed recordings or early session termination.

Clustering of intradialytic BP patterns

Systolic blood pressure (SBP) values were mapped to prespecified 30-minute time points (0, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, 180, 210, and 240 minutes). For each time point, measurements obtained within a ±15-minute window were assigned, and the mean value was used when multiple measurements were available within the window. The change in SBP at each time point was calculated relative to the predialysis SBP (ΔSBP_t = SBP_t − SBP_0). For sporadically missing SBP values between recorded measurements, missing values were imputed using within-session linear interpolation. Because the last SBP value could not be imputed, sessions yielded three types of complete trajectories through 180, 210, or 240 minutes, depending on the availability of the final measurements.

Unsupervised clustering was performed using k-means in a complete-case subset of sessions that included ΔSBP values at all time points from 0 to 240 minutes. Nonstandardized raw ΔSBP values were used for clustering to preserve the clinical meaning of absolute BP changes and to allow cluster distinctions reflecting both trajectory magnitude and shape. Candidate numbers of clusters (k = 2–8) were evaluated using the within-cluster sum of squares (elbow method), and cluster separability was assessed using the mean silhouette width based on Euclidean distance. K-means was fitted with 50 random starts (nstart = 50) and a maximum of 1,000 iterations per run (iter.max = 1,000).

Sessions with complete data up to 180 or 210 minutes but missing later measurements were assigned to the nearest centroid derived from the 0–240-minutes k-means model. Euclidean distances between the session vector and each centroid were computed at the prespecified time points, and the cluster with the minimum distance was assigned.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations, and categorical variables as numbers (%). Between-group differences were compared using one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables. Because multiple HD sessions could belong to the same patient, linear mixed-effects models with a random intercept for each patient were fitted to account for repeated sessions within individuals. In the adjusted models, cluster membership, age, sex, predialysis SBP, and ultrafiltration rate were included as fixed effects. Adjusted marginal means were estimated, and pairwise comparisons among clusters were performed using Bonferroni correction.

We performed sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness and reproducibility of the identified BP clusters. First, a repeated random one-session-per-patient resampling was conducted to address potential bias from multiple sessions per patient. For each of 200 iterations, one HD session was randomly selected from each patient from the complete-case training dataset. Second, we repeated the clustering using an alternative number of clusters to assess whether the associations between BP patterns and body composition were preserved regardless of the specific choice of k. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Study population and dataset preparation

A total of 928 HD sessions from 179 patients were included in the final analysis dataset. The median number of sessions per patient was 3 (1, 8). The mean age was 68.2 ± 13.0 years, and 55.9% of the patients were male. The prevalence of diabetes and hypertension was 58.1% and 80.4%, respectively.

Of the 928 HD sessions, 826 (89.0%) had complete intradialytic BP measurements through 240 minutes and were used for k-means model training. The remaining sessions were assigned to clusters using centroid-based classification if complete measurements were available through 210 minutes (n = 75 [8.1%]) or 180 minutes (n = 27 [2.9%]).

Intradialytic BP clustering

Clustering was performed using the 826 sessions with complete dataset. The elbow profile supported k = 3, with a pronounced decrease in the within-cluster sum of squares from k = 2 to k = 3, followed by progressively smaller reductions thereafter (Supplementary Fig. 1). The mean silhouette width also favored k = 3 over k = 4 (0.293 versus 0.237). Based on these criteria, k = 3 was selected as the primary clustering scheme, whereas k = 4 was retained for sensitivity analysis of phenotype characterization.

After integration of the trained clustering model with centroid-based assignment, all 928 HD sessions were classified into three clusters according to their characteristic intradialytic SBP patterns: marked decreasing (n = 156, [16.8%]), mild decreasing (n = 429 [46.2%]), and rising (n = 343 [37.0%]) (Fig. 1). Characteristics across the intradialytic BP clusters are summarized in Table 1. The marked decreasing cluster had significantly higher ultrafiltration rate than the mild decreasing cluster, whereas patients in the rising cluster were prescribed a significantly higher number of antihypertensive drugs.

Association between intradialytic BP clusters and body composition analysis

We compared body composition parameters across intradialytic BP clusters (Table 1). Significant differences across clusters were observed for FFMI, SMI, PBF, VFA, and ECW/TBW, whereas BMI did not differ significantly.

After accounting for repeated measurements using linear mixed-effects models adjusted for age, sex, predialysis SBP, and ultrafiltration rate, the overall pattern remained evident (Fig. 2). Compared with the mild decreasing cluster, the rising cluster showed higher ECW/TBW, FFMI, and SMI, but lower PBF. In contrast, the marked decreasing cluster showed lower FFMI and SMI than the mild decreasing cluster. BMI did not differ across the clusters.

Results of sensitivity analysis

We evaluated whether the findings were maintained in sensitivity analyses. The repeated random one-session-per-patient resampling analysis with k = 3 preserved the general trajectory structure of the three clusters, but the degree of stability differed across clusters (Supplementary Fig. 2). The marked decreasing and rising centroids showed high similarity to the original centroids, with mean correlations of 0.965 and 0.981, respectively. In contrast, the mild decreasing cluster showed lower correlation-based stability (mean correlation, 0.054), although its root mean square error was 6.08 mmHg, consistent with relatively small absolute deviation for a flat trajectory. Overall, the repeated resampling analysis supported preservation of the three-trajectory structure.

In a 4-cluster solution, the overall phenotypic structure observed in the 3-cluster analysis was preserved. Among the 928 HD sessions, 61 (6.6%), 269 (29.0%), 374 (40.3%), and 224 (24.1%) were classified as severe decreasing, moderate decreasing, near-stable, and rising patterns, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3). The 4-cluster solution represented a finer stratification of the same underlying trajectory structure.

Body composition differences across the 4-cluster solution were consistent with those observed in the primary 3-cluster analysis (Supplementary Table 1). In the adjusted mixed-effects models, FFMI and SMI remained highest in the rising cluster, whereas PBF and VFA were highest in the severe decreasing cluster. In addition, ECW/TBW was lowest in the severe decreasing cluster. BMI remained similar across the clusters. These findings support the robustness of the primary results.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we used an unsupervised clustering approach to objectively classify dynamic intradialytic BP trajectories and evaluated their independent associations with body composition parameters. We identified three distinct, reproducible intradialytic BP phenotypes: marked decreasing, mild decreasing, and rising clusters. These clusters were strongly and independently associated with the patients’ volume status, muscle mass, and fat mass, even after rigorous adjustment for dynamic fluid removal stress.

Intradialytic BP monitoring is fundamental for ensuring patient safety during HD. Importantly, extreme intradialytic BP deviations—such as intradialytic hypotension and hypertension—are well-established risk factors for subsequent all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [11,12,13,14]. Given this established prognostic significance, understanding the underlying pathophysiological drivers of these high-risk BP trajectories is of paramount clinical importance. However, clinical decision-making based on arbitrary diagnostic cutoffs and binary thresholds may overlook the continuous physiological heterogeneity among patients [15,16,17]. In contrast, machine learning techniques like clustering learn directly from data, which helps elucidate the true relationship between clinical phenotypes and underlying pathophysiology. Using an unsupervised clustering approach, we visualized that intradialytic SBP alterations exist on a continuous spectrum and demonstrated that these data-driven phenotypes are intricately linked to the patients’ body composition characteristics.

Through the k-means clustering algorithm, we identified three distinct intradialytic BP phenotypes based on their trajectories over the HD session. Notably, these trajectory features were consistently maintained both when the repeated random one-session-per-patient resampling analysis with 200 iterations and when a 4-cluster solution (k = 4) was selected in the sensitivity analyses. We initially anticipated that the algorithm might differentiate the phenotypes based on the specific timing of the BP changes (e.g., early versus late drops); however, the clusters were primarily distinguished by the absolute magnitude and direction of the BP alterations. The features were confirmed both in the repeated random resampling analysis and the 4-cluster solution. A previous study by Wang et al. [18] determined intradialytic BP patterns using a dynamic time warping algorithm and applied normalization to the BP sequences, proposing five patterns based on density peak clustering, distinguishable by the timing and shape of the BP change. In contrast, we considered both the specific time interval after HD initiation and the absolute amount of change in SBP to be clinically crucial. This methodological choice allowed our clustering to capture the true magnitude of hemodynamic shifts, effectively revealing the trajectories representing highly reproducible biological signatures with the exceptional numerical stability.

Our clustering approach revealed that the dynamic intradialytic BP alterations are intricately linked to patients’ fluid status and body composition. It is noteworthy that a continuous, stepwise gradient in volume, muscle, and fat parameters was observed across the clusters, which was robustly confirmed in our 4-cluster sensitivity analysis. ECW/TBW indicates volume status and has been used to guide dry weight adjustment [5]. Based on our results, ECW/TBW tended to increase as the trajectory shifted from a marked decreasing to a rising pattern, while the lowest interdialytic weight gain and ultrafiltration rate and the highest number of antihypertensive drugs were observed in the rising cluster. The association between hypervolemia and intradialytic hypertension has been well demonstrated in previous literature [7,14]. Taken together, these findings suggest a state of volume overload in which the prescribed dry weight may be higher than the true euvolemic state. On the other hand, the excessive or rapid removal of ECW can precipitate a profound BP decline during HD [6]. Our results support the importance of individualized dry weight assessment and volume management for stable HD treatment, with attention to both marked decreasing and rising intradialytic BP patterns.

Indicators of muscle mass, such as FFMI and SMI, exhibited a stepwise increase toward the rising cluster, while indicators of fat mass, such as PBF and VFA, a stepwise decrease These findings should be interpreted with extreme caution. Because BIA inherently estimates fat-free mass based on TBW, ECW excess can lead to a substantial overestimation of muscle mass [19]. However, there is research supporting the association between sarcopenia and intradialytic hypotension [8], and some evidence even shows the protective effect of intradialytic exercise against intradialytic hypotension [20]. Furthermore, visceral adiposity acts as an active endocrine organ secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines [21,22], which may exacerbate endothelial and autonomic dysfunction, rendering patients highly vulnerable to BP drops. Our finding aligns with a previous study by Tian et al. [9], which reported that sarcopenic obesity, the combination of high fat mass and low lean mass, conferred the highest risk of intradialytic hypotension. Our results support previous findings, highlighting the critical role of sarcopenic obesity and adipose tissue in hemodynamic alterations over the HD course.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective, single-center study, which limits the generalizability of our findings to broader HD populations. Furthermore, this study design precludes causal inference regarding the relationship between intradialytic BP patterns and body composition parameters. Second, this study assessed body composition using BIA, which can limit the interpretation since it is easily affected by volume status, although we used a multifrequency BIA and measured following the recommended protocol [19]. Third, BP was measured using automated devices built into the HD machines. This approach may introduce measurement discrepancies compared to standardized measurement.

In conclusion, our unsupervised clustering successfully classified the dynamic trajectories of intradialytic BP into three distinct, reproducible phenotypes: marked decreasing, mild decreasing, and rising patterns. These data-driven BP trajectories are intricately linked to the patients’ underlying body composition parameters. This insight provides actionable therapeutic targets, such as more rigorous volume assessment in the rising pattern and nutritional and physical interventions for sarcopenic obesity in the marked decreasing pattern. Ultimately, integrating routine body composition and volume assessments into clinical practice can improve the interpretation of intradialytic BP patterns, paving the way for more individualized management strategies for patients undergoing chronic HD.
