 Summary of Research Results:

 Purpose

 Converting electrons and water to hydrogen ‘at scale’ provides cross-sector benefits. In
 addition to long-duration energy storage, this includes use as a feedstock for chemicals,
 fuels, and industrial processes. Understanding the science of scaling from 250-kilowatt (kW)
 to 1.5-megawatt (MW) is not a trivial goal and the National Laboratory of the Rockies
 (NLR) Energy Systems Integration Facility (ESIF) engineering staff will scale the design of
 their existing electrolyzer to advance NLR’s initiatives and capabilities while supporting the
 sponsor’s goals in this technology space. For example, water management, gas pressure
 safety, heat rejection and electrical energy inputs would now achieve relevant scales for
 commercial deployments.

 This project supports Department of Energy (DOE)/NLR initiatives, including H2@Scale
 and Electrons-to-Molecules. These system evaluations support DOE’s research and
 development objectives and will provide valuable data on electrolyzer scale-up challenges
 that will assist in future system development at the ESIF and Flatiron Campus.

 Finally, to investigate their membrane electrode assemblies (MEAs), Nel would like NRL to
 build upon existing model capabilities to study variations in membrane and membrane
 electrode assembly (MEA) properties. Model functionality and parametric studies will be
 discussed with and/or provided by Nel. Specific capabilities, if not currently available, may
 be extended/developed within the framework of this statement of work as specifically
 discussed between Nel and NLR. If necessary, experimental studies will also be conducted
 according to the task list below.

 Statement of Work

 The sponsor is developing a large-scale electrolysis stack (rated capacity of 1.25 MW with
 the ability to operate up to 1.5 MW) to compete in the market with European proton
 exchange membrane (PEM) electrolysis providers. The sponsor wants an independent
 testing facility to validate the full-scale stack in preparation for commercial release, as well
 as to perform acceptance testing on the first several commercial stacks.

 TASK 1: Requirements definition and procurement

 The sponsor will provide product requirements and design guidance to NLR before initiation of
 the formal agreement to define the enhanced and new capability at the ESIF. ESIF Engineering
 Staff will complete the design/build process prior to delivery of the electrolyzer stacks and
 supporting equipment from the sponsor. NLR will rely heavily on the sponsor’s engineering staff
 to ensure that the equipment and components provided to NLR meet the specifications for the
 application.

 Based on the requirements and guidance from the sponsor, NLR will develop a detailed plan and
 list of materials to accomplish the upgrades of the NLR facilities within the proposed budget and
 timeline.

 Task 3: Modeling of Ac-to-DC electrical system

 Nel Hydrogen USA (NEL) is interested in obtaining research and development support from
 NLR in the area of center-point grounding in a DC high-power electrolyzer stack system.
 Specifically, the topic area of interest is in a configuration utilizing a AC/DC power supply
 (rectifier) to power two cell stack loads operating in electrically in series. Nel is seeking support
 in the area of modeling the AC-to-DC power system with center-grounded electrolyzer stacks
 (loads) and insight into relevant codes and standards that would support the deployment of this
 configuration.

 NLR will perform modeling of the AC-to-DC electrical system including the electrolyzer stack
 configuration.

 Critical questions that NLR will address include, but are not limited to:

 1. How much leakage and circulating currents are created under full load? What
 instrumentation could be used to monitor leakage currents and what would be
 recommended Pass/Fail criteria?

 2. Where in the system are the stray currents most likely to occur?

 a. Based on these results, where in the system does monitoring make most sense to
 ensure the integrity of the ground connection?

 b. What would be the pass/fail criteria for a good/bad connection to ground?

 3. Is insulation monitoring from the ungrounded conductors to ground still required?

 3

 4. Is this system configuration unique to the existing codes and standards or does it fall into
 an existing category of system; for example, a Reference Grounded DC System or PV
 systems?

 • What codes and standards are applicable to this system configuration with respect to
 grounding and safety? What level of effort is required to ensure compliance to the
 applicable codes and standards?

 The specific topic areas of research aim to improve understanding of the feasibility of this
 center-grounded connection between two series-connected loads with respect to safety and
 compliance to applicable standards.

 Task 3.1:

 NLR will perform modeling of the input AC-to-DC electrical power system, including the
 electrolyzer stack configuration using resistive and capacitive elements to simulate stack
 response.

 a. The modeling will take two approaches, one focused on material properties and
 magnetics, while the other on the electrical power system. Both approaches aim to
 identify not only where the leakage/circulating currents may appear, but to also the
 magnitude of them.

 b. The electrolyzer balance of plant will be considered in the modeling efforts as flowing
 water and process connections could provide pathways for current when/if infusion
 degrades.

 Task 3.2:

 NLR will help identify relevant codes and standards to guide the design, monitoring and to
 defend the unique stack configuration.

 Task 3 Results:

 No work was completed under this task because the resources required to model these complex
 systems were not available.

 Task 4.1 Results:

 The sponsor designed, built, and delivered a 100-cell PEM electrolyzer stack to NLR. Figure 1
 below shows the stack upon arrival at NLR positioned on the shipping crate. The hydrogen
 production rate of the electrolyzer stack is approximately 145 normal cubic meters per hour
 (Nm3/h).

 Fig. 1. 100-cell PEM electrolyzer Stack
 Task 4.2:

 NLR will install, commission, and complete 5,000 hours of steady-state constant-current
 operation on the stack provided in Task 4.1. NLR’s commissioning process will include a
 comprehensive validation of low and high alarms and faults that will shut down the system – Nel
 will confirm the warning and alarm setpoints prior to system startup.

 5

 System parameters like deionized (DI) water and hydrogen temperature, flow, and pressure will
 be monitored, recorded, and provided to Nel via file transfers at 500, 1000 and every 1000 hours
 after or as requested by Nel. Stack voltage and current will be monitored and recorded along
 with the DI water and hydrogen data. Cell-level voltage data will be sampled once per minute
 and averaged hourly before archiving. Cell-level voltage data will also be recorded upon startup
 following a system shutdown. Nel will provide desired sample rates for these data prior to
 startup.

 NLR will provide 1 Liter of water sampled from the hydrogen/water high-pressure phase
 separator at T-10 hours, 100, 500, 1000 and every 1000 hours after – including the final water
 sample at 5,000 hours of operation. IV characteristic curves at ~ 60°C, 30-bar will be performed
 every 500 hours starting at 500A and increasing to 3500A in 500A steps. NLR will
 decommission and crate the stack at the end of the 5,000 hours and work with NEL who will
 provide shipping back to Wallingford, Connecticut.

 Task 5 Results:

 In the course of this project, NLR discussed in meetings with Nel the potential origins of S-
 shaped polarization curves observed in Nel's electrolyzers. Through collaborative consultation,
 NLR identified contamination effects as the most likely cause of the observed performance
 issues. This hypothesis was based on NLR’s expertise in electrolyzer systems and supported by
 data exchange and discussions under Task 1.

 NLR presented the ongoing work and findings during these meetings. As a result of these
 discussions, NLR developed concepts to model the impact of contaminants and proposed in-situ
 methods for verifying performance effects linked to contaminants entering the electrolyzer via
 the water feed stream. Based on the joint conclusion that the S-shape originates from
 contaminant exposure, it was decided that the model development outlined in Task 2 was
 unnecessary. Consequently, also no parametric studies under Task 3 were performed. Instead,
 results from contamination experiments carried out under separate DOE-funded efforts were
 communicated to Nel as part of the information exchange activities in Task 1.
