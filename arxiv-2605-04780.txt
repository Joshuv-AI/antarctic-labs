Minimal generating sets of transfer systems for more non-Abelian Groups
Bheemarasetty Chakravarthy, Surojit Ghosh

Abstract
. For a finite group G G , N ∞ N_{\infty} operads encode collections of norm maps, and by work of Blumberg–Hill and Rubin their homotopy category is equivalent to the poset of G G –transfer systems on the subgroup lattice of G G . In [ 1 ] the authors defined the width w ⁡ ( G ) w(G) as the minimal size of a generating set for the complete G G –transfer system and identified it with the number of conjugacy classes of proper meet irreducible subgroups of G G , and the complexity c ⁡ ( G ) c(G) as the maximum, over all transfer systems T T , of the size of a minimal generating set for T T . We compute w ⁡ ( G ) w(G) for the semidihedral groups SD 2 n \operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}} ( n ≥ 4 n\geq 4 ) and the affine Frobenius groups AGL ⁡ ( 1 , p n ) ≅ 𝔽 p n ⋊ 𝔽 p n × \operatorname{AGL}(1,p^{n})\cong\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}\rtimes\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}^{\times} , extending existing calculations and highlighting how subgroup lattice structure governs equivariant multiplicative complexity. We also compute c ⁡ ( D p n ) c(D_{p^{n}}) for dihedral groups of order 2 ​ p n 2p^{n} with p p an odd prime, establishing c ⁡ ( D p n ) = ⌊ 3 ​ n / 2 ⌋ + 1 c(D_{p^{n}})=\lfloor 3n/2\rfloor+1 , and derive the lower bound c ⁡ ( SD 2 n ) ≥ ⌊ 5 ​ ( n − 1 ) / 2 ⌋ c(\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}})\geq\lfloor 5(n-1)/2\rfloor .

1.
Introduction

Equivariant homotopy theory has undergone a remarkable conceptual expansion
over the past decade, driven in large part by a deeper understanding of
multiplicative structures in genuine
G
–spectra.
In contrast with the nonequivariant setting, commutativity in equivariant
stable homotopy theory is not a single notion but a hierarchy of structures
governed by the existence of norm maps which encode multiplicative induction
along subgroup inclusions.
These norms play a central role in modern developments, including the solution
of the Kervaire invariant problem and subsequent advances in equivariant
algebraic topology.

A systematic framework for organizing such multiplicative structures was
introduced by Blumberg and Hill in
[
5
]
through the theory of

N_{\infty}
operads.
An
N_{\infty}
operad is a genuine equivariant operad whose algebras admit
a specified collection of norm maps, interpolating between naive

E_{\infty}
–ring
G
–spectra (with no nontrivial norms) and fully genuine
commutative ring
G
–spectra (with all norms present).
A central insight of
[
5
]
is that the homotopy theory of
N_{\infty}

operads is governed by purely combinatorial data, called
indexing
systems
, consisting of certain collections of finite
H
–sets closed under
natural operations.

Rubin subsequently recast this structure in a strikingly economical form.
In
[
9
,
10
]
, he introduced the notion of a
G
–transfer
system
– a partial order on the subgroup lattice of a finite group
G

satisfying axioms reflecting the formal properties of norms.
He proved that the homotopy category of
N_{\infty}
operads is equivalent
to the poset of
G
–transfer systems.
Thus the intricate homotopy–theoretic problem of classifying multiplicative
equivariant structures becomes a problem in lattice theory: understanding
certain compatible refinements of the subgroup lattice.
Bonventre and Pereira
[
7
]
developed a parallel theory of genuine
equivariant operads, and Balchin, Barnes, and Roitzheim
[
3
]

discovered surprising links with Catalan numbers and associahedra: the number
of transfer systems on a chain of length
n
equals the
(n+1)
-st
Catalan number
\operatorname{Cat}(n+1)
.

In a recent article, Adamyk, Balchin, Barrero, Scheirer, Wisdom, and Zapata
Castro
[
1
]
introduced two numerical invariants attached to a group

G
in this context: the
width
and the
complexity
.
The width
w(G)
is defined as the size of a minimal generating set for the
complete
G
-transfer system (the one containing every possible edge

K\to H
with
K\leq H
).
A key result of
[
1
]
identifies
w(G)
with the number of
conjugacy classes of proper
meet irreducible subgroups
of
G
.
The complexity
c(G)
is the companion invariant defined as the maximum
cardinality of a minimal generating set over all transfer systems in

\operatorname{Tr}(G)
; it resists a direct lattice-theoretic description and requires
careful combinatorial work.

Widths have now been computed for several important families of groups.
In particular, Klanderman, Lewis, Monson, Shibata, and Van Niel
[
8
]

determined
w(G)
for dihedral, quaternion, and dicyclic groups, revealing
systematic patterns tied to the structure of their subgroup lattices.
These calculations demonstrate that width reflects subtle interactions between
normal subgroups, maximal subgroups, and chains in
\operatorname{Sub}(G)
.
The first exact complexity computation appears in
[
1
]
, where it is
shown that

c(C_{p^{n}q})=\begin{cases}3k+1&n=2k,\\
3k+2&n=2k+1,\end{cases}

for distinct primes
p
and
q
.

The purpose of the present paper is to extend this program to three further
families of groups whose subgroup structures exhibit distinct features: the
semidihedral
2
–groups, the affine linear groups over finite fields and Modular maximal-cyclic groups.
We additionally compute the complexity for the dihedral groups
D_{p^{n}}
,
whose subgroup lattice presents a genuinely richer structure than the cyclic
case, requiring a new bridge-exclusion argument.

.

For the Semidihedral group
\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}}
with
n\geq 4
, one has

w(\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}})=2n-2
.

See Theorem

For the Frobenius group

the total number of prime factors of

.

2.
Background

Throughout this paper,
G
denotes a finite group.
We write
\operatorname{Sub}(G)
for the set of all subgroups of
G
, partially ordered
by inclusion.
Equipped with intersection as meet and subgroup generation as join,

\operatorname{Sub}(G)
forms a finite lattice.
Many of the structures arising in equivariant homotopy theory are controlled
by additional order-theoretic data imposed on this lattice.

2.1.
Transfer systems

Transfer systems were introduced as a combinatorial model for the homotopy
theory of
N_{\infty}
-operads.
They encode, in purely order-theoretic terms, which transfer (or norm) maps
are present between subgroups.

Definition 2.1
.

A
G
-transfer system
is a partial order
\to
on
\operatorname{Sub}(G)

such that:

(i)

K\to H
implies
K\leq H
;

(ii)

H\to H
for every
H\leq G
;

(iii)

L\to K
and
K\to H
imply
L\to H
;

(iv)

K\to H
and
L\leq H
imply
(K\cap L)\to(H\cap L)
;

(v)

K\to H
implies
gKg^{-1}\to gHg^{-1}
for all
g\in G
.

Reflexive edges are customarily omitted from diagrams.

Condition (i) ensures that transfers only occur along subgroup inclusions.
Conditions (ii) and (iii) state that
\to
is reflexive and transitive,
hence a refinement of the inclusion order.
Condition (iv) encodes compatibility with restriction along subgroups and
reflects the double-coset behavior of transfers in equivariant homotopy
theory.
Finally, condition (v) enforces conjugation invariance, since transfer data
depends only on subgroups up to conjugacy.

A transfer system may therefore be viewed as a choice of certain subgroup
inclusions along which transfer maps are permitted, subject to natural closure
conditions.
The collection of all
G
-transfer systems forms a poset under inclusion of
relations.

2.2.
Meet irreducible subgroups

The structure of transfer systems is governed by the lattice-theoretic
properties of
\operatorname{Sub}(G)
, and in particular by certain distinguished
subgroups.

Definition 2.2
.

A proper subgroup
K<G
is
meet irreducible
if it cannot be written
as the intersection of two strictly larger subgroups.
Equivalently,
K
possesses a unique minimal overgroup (the smallest
subgroup properly containing
K
).

Definition 2.3

(
[
1
, Definition 4.1]
)
.

The
width

w(G)
is the cardinality of a minimal generating set of
the complete
G
-transfer system (the transfer system containing all edges

K\to H
with
K\leq H
).

Here, a generating set is a collection of relations whose closure under
axioms (ii)–(v) yields the complete transfer system.
In other words, starting from these chosen edges and repeatedly applying
transitivity, restriction via intersections, and conjugation, one obtains
every relation
K\to H
with
K\leq H
.
The width therefore measures the minimal number of primitive transfer
relations required to force all possible transfers.

The fundamental connection between width and meet irreducible subgroups is
given by the following result.

Proposition 2.4

(
[
1
, Proposition 4.5]
)
.

For any finite group
G
,
w(G)
equals the number of conjugacy classes
of proper meet irreducible subgroups of
G
.
Moreover, the set
\{[H]\to G\mid H<G\text{ meet irreducible}\}
is a
minimal generating set for the complete transfer system.

Thus computing
w(G)
reduces to classifying conjugacy classes of meet
irreducible subgroups.

2.3.
Complexity and partial rainbows

The complexity invariant is a companion to the width, recording the hardest
transfer system to generate rather than the easiest.

Definition 2.5

(
[
1
]
)
.

If
m(T)
denotes the size of any minimal generating set for
T\in\operatorname{Tr}(G)

(well-defined by
[
1
, Corollary 2.14]
), the
complexity
of

G
is

c(G):=\max\bigl\{m(T)\mid T\in\operatorname{Tr}(G)\bigr\}.

A key tool for producing lower bounds on complexity is the notion of a
partial rainbow.

Definition 2.6

(
[
1
, Definitions 3.1–3.2]
)
.

A
rainbow
on
\operatorname{Sub}(G)
is a collection of
P
-arcs

(j_{1},k_{1}),\dots,(j_{r},k_{r})
that are pairwise nested (i.e., strictly
ordered by inclusion as intervals).
A
partial rainbow
is a set
S
of arrows in
I(\operatorname{Sub}(G))
whose

P
-arcs form a rainbow, with at most one arrow chosen from each conjugacy
class.

By
[
1
, Proposition 3.3]
, every partial rainbow is a minimal
generating set for the transfer system it generates, so any partial rainbow
of size
s
witnesses
c(G)\geq s
.

3.
Width computations

We now compute the width invariant for the families of groups appearing in
the main results, starting with the semidihedral
2
–groups.

3.1.
Semidihedral groups
\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}}

We now determine the width of the semidihedral
2
–groups.
Throughout this subsection fix an integer
n\geq 4
and let

G=\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}}=\langle a,b\mid a^{2^{n-1}}=b^{2}=1,\;bab=a^{2^{n-2}-1}\rangle.

Set
N=2^{n-1}
and
k=N/2-1=2^{n-2}-1
.
A direct computation shows
k^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{N}
.
Conjugation by
a
acts on
b
via

a^{r}ba^{-r}=ba^{r(k-1)},

and since
k-1=N/2-2
is even, the exponent
r(k-1)
is divisible by
2
,
so
a^{r(k-1)}\in\langle a^{2}\rangle
.
The centre of
G
is

Z(G)=\{1,a^{N/2}\}=:\langle z\rangle,

which has order two.

Theorem 3.1
.

For
n\geq 4
one has
w(\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}})=2n-2
.

Proof.

By Proposition 4.5 of
[
1
]
, the width equals the number of
conjugacy classes of proper meet irreducible subgroups of
G
.
We determine these subgroups explicitly.

The group
\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}}
possesses three maximal subgroups of index two, each
normal:

M_{c}=\langle a\rangle\cong C_{2^{n-1}},\quad M_{d}=\langle a^{2},b\rangle\cong D_{2^{n-1}},\quad M_{q}=\langle a^{2},ab\rangle\cong Q_{2^{n-1}}.

Their pairwise intersections equal
\langle a^{2}\rangle\cong C_{2^{n-2}}
.
Subgroups contained in two distinct maximals lie in this intersection and
therefore cannot be meet irreducible; consequently any proper meet irreducible
subgroup is contained in exactly one maximal.

The maximal
M_{c}
itself is meet irreducible, its unique cover being
G
;
no proper subgroup of
M_{c}
qualifies.

Now consider
M_{d}
.
Its rotation subgroup
\langle a^{2}\rangle
has order
2^{n-2}
; the
reflections are the elements
x_{s}=a^{2s}b
(
0\leq s<2^{n-2}
).
Each reflection lies in a unique Klein four

K_{s}=\{1,z,x_{s},x_{s}z\}
, which is its unique cover.
All reflections are conjugate under
a
, yielding one conjugacy class.
Each
K_{s}
similarly has a unique cover, the dihedral group
D_{8}

generated by
a^{2^{n-3}}
and
x_{s}
; again one class.
For
k=3,\dots,n-2
, the dihedral subgroups

D_{2^{k}}=\langle a^{2^{n-k}},x_{s}\rangle
form a chain, each with unique
cover
D_{2^{k+1}}
(or
M_{d}
when
k=n-2
).
All subgroups of a given order are conjugate, contributing one class per
order
2^{3},\dots,2^{n-2}
.
Counting orders
2,4,8,\dots,2^{n-2}
gives
n-2
classes from
M_{d}
.

Turning to
M_{q}
, a generalised quaternion group.
The elements of order four outside
\langle a^{2}\rangle
are

y_{s}=a^{2s+1}b
; each
\langle y_{s}\rangle
is cyclic of order four and
has unique cover the quaternion group
Q_{8}
generated by
a^{2^{n-3}}

and
y_{s}
.
All such cyclic subgroups are conjugate, giving one class.
For
t=3,\dots,n-2
, the quaternion subgroups

Q_{2^{t}}=\langle a^{2^{n-t}},y_{s}\rangle
form a chain, each with unique
cover
Q_{2^{t+1}}
(or
M_{q}
when
t=n-2
), and all subgroups of a
given order are conjugate.
This yields
1+(n-4)=n-3
classes from
M_{q}
.

The three maximals are pairwise non-conjugate because their isomorphism types
differ; they contribute
3
classes.
Therefore the number of conjugacy classes of proper meet irreducible subgroups
of
G
equals
2n-2
, and hence
w(\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}})=2n-2
, as claimed.
∎

3.2.
Frobenius groups
\operatorname{AGL}(1,p^{n})

We now compute the width for the affine linear groups over finite fields.
Let
p
be prime and
n\geq 1
, and set

G=\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}).

Write
V=\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}^{+}
for the additive group of the field and

H=\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}^{\times}
for its multiplicative group.
Then
V
is an elementary abelian
p
-group of order
p^{n}
,

H
is cyclic of order
m=p^{n}-1
, and
G=V\rtimes H
,
where
H
acts on
V
by multiplication.
This is a Frobenius group with kernel
V
and complement
H
.

Every subgroup of
G
can be written uniquely in the form
A\rtimes D
,
where
A\leq V
is an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-subspace and
D\leq H
normalises
A
.
Conjugation by an element
h\in H
sends
(A,D)
to
(hA,D)
, while
conjugation by
V
preserves the subgroup
D\leq H
.
In particular, subgroups with distinct
D
-parts are never conjugate.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
G=\operatorname{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}})
with
p
prime and
n\geq 1
. Then

w(G)=\Omega(p^{n}-1)+\tau(n),

where
\Omega(m)
denotes the total number of prime factors of
m

counted with multiplicity, and
\tau(n)
denotes the number of positive
divisors of
n
.

Proof.

As before we first classify proper meet irreducible subgroups into three
families.
The complement
H
is maximal, hence meet irreducible; all complements are
conjugate, so this contributes one conjugacy class.

Next, consider subgroups of the form
V\rtimes D
with
D<H
.
These are normal.
Their overgroups are
V\rtimes D^{\prime}
with
D^{\prime}\geq D
.
Since
H
is cyclic,
V\rtimes D
has a unique minimal overgroup among
subgroups of the form
V\rtimes D^{\prime}
precisely when
m/|D|
is a prime
power (i.e., there is exactly one divisor
e
with
|D|<e\leq m
).
The proper subgroups
D
with this property are exactly those of order

m/p^{t}
for each prime power
p^{t}\mid m
; there are
\Omega(m)
of
them.
Each yields a distinct conjugacy class.

Now let
r
be a proper divisor of
n
, and let
L\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}

be the unique subfield of order
p^{r}
.
Then
L^{\times}\leq H
is cyclic of order
p^{r}-1
.
Choose an
L
-hyperplane
A\subset V
, i.e., an
L
-subspace with

\dim_{L}A=n/r-1
.
The subgroup
K_{r,A}=A\rtimes L^{\times}
is well defined.
We claim
K_{r,A}
is meet irreducible: any proper overgroup must be either

V\rtimes L^{\times}
or
G
, and because
L^{\times}
is the full
stabiliser of
A
, the unique maximal overgroup is
G
.
For fixed
r
, all
K_{r,A}
are conjugate under
H
; different
r

give distinct conjugacy classes.
The number of proper divisors of
n
is
\tau(n)-1
, contributing that
many classes.

Any other proper subgroup
A\rtimes D
with
A<V
and
D<H
fails to
be meet irreducible.
Let
L
be the subfield generated by
D
; then
D\leq L^{\times}
and

A
is an
L
-subspace.
If
A
is not a hyperplane over
L
, it has at least two distinct

L
-superspaces, yielding incomparable overgroups.
If
A
is a hyperplane but
D\neq L^{\times}
, then
A\rtimes D
is
contained in both
A\rtimes L^{\times}
and
V\rtimes D
, which are
incomparable.
If
A=V
we are in the
V\rtimes D
case already covered.
Subgroups of the form
D
alone (
A=0
) lie in both
H
and

V\rtimes D
, giving two covers unless
D=H
(which is maximal).

Summing the three families:

w(G)=1+\Omega(m)+(\tau(n)-1)=\Omega(m)+\tau(n)=\Omega(p^{n}-1)+\tau(n),

as claimed.
∎

Example 3.3
.

Take
p=2
,
n=3
.
Then
G=\operatorname{AGL}(1,8)
has order
56
,
m=7
,
\Omega(7)=1
,

\tau(3)=2
, so
w(G)=3
.
The three classes are:
H\cong C_{7}
;
V
itself (since
7/1
is
prime); and an
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-hyperplane
A
of order
4
(the subfield case
with
r=1
).

3.3.
Modular maximal-cyclic groups
M_{n}(2)

Throughout this subsection, fix an integer
n\geq 4
and set

G=M_{n}(2)=\langle a,b\mid a^{2^{n-1}}=b^{2}=1,\;bab^{-1}=a^{1+2^{n-2}}\rangle.

The cyclic subgroup
\langle a\rangle
has order
2^{n-1}
, and conjugation
by
b
raises
a
to the power
1+2^{n-2}
, which is an automorphism of
order two.
The centre and Frattini subgroup of
G
coincide:

Z(G)=\Phi(G)=\langle a^{2}\rangle\cong C_{2^{n-2}}.

Conjugation by
a^{r}
maps
b
to
a^{r\cdot 2^{n-2}}b
, so two elements

a^{s}b
and
a^{t}b
generate conjugate subgroups if and only if

s\equiv t\pmod{2^{n-2}}
; this governs the conjugacy structure among
subgroups meeting the coset
\langle a\rangle b
.

Theorem 3.4
.

For
n\geq 4
one has
w(M_{n}(2))=2(n-1)
.

Proof.

We classify proper meet-irreducible subgroups of

G=M_{n}(2)=\langle a,b\mid a^{2^{n-1}}=b^{2}=1,\;bab^{-1}=a^{1+2^{n-2}}\rangle

into conjugacy classes directly.

Since
G/\Phi(G)\cong C_{2}\times C_{2}
, there are exactly three maximal
subgroups, all normal and of index two:

M_{1}=\langle a\rangle\cong C_{2^{n-1}},\quad M_{2}=\langle a^{2},b\rangle\cong C_{2^{n-2}}\times C_{2},\quad M_{3}=\langle ab\rangle\cong C_{2^{n-1}}.

Each is meet-irreducible with unique cover
G
; being normal, each forms its
own conjugacy class, contributing three classes.
Any subgroup contained in two distinct maximals cannot be meet-irreducible.
Proper subgroups of the cyclic groups
M_{1}
and
M_{3}
each lie in the unique
cyclic subgroup of half their order, which itself lies in all three maximals,
so no proper subgroup of
M_{1}
or
M_{3}
qualifies.

It remains to examine
M_{2}\cong C_{2^{n-2}}\times C_{2}
.
For
1\leq j\leq n-3
, the subgroup lattice of
M_{2}
at each level
j
contains
exactly two meet-irreducible members: the subgroup

\langle a^{2^{j}},b\rangle\cong C_{2^{n-1-j}}\times C_{2}
, whose unique cover
inside
M_{2}
is
\langle a^{2^{j-1}},b\rangle
, and the cyclic subgroup

\langle a^{2^{j-1}}b\rangle
of order
2^{n-j}
, whose unique cover is
likewise
\langle a^{2^{j-1}},b\rangle
.
The purely cyclic subgroup
\langle a^{2^{j}}\rangle
at the same level is not
meet-irreducible, as it is covered by both
\langle a^{2^{j-1}}\rangle
and

\langle a^{2^{j}},b\rangle
.
All subgroups at a fixed level are normal, so each forms a singleton conjugacy
class; the
n-3
levels together contribute
2(n-3)
classes.

At the bottom level,
\langle a^{2^{n-2}}\rangle
has three covers and is
not meet-irreducible.
The involutions
b
and
a^{2^{n-2}}b
each have

\langle a^{2^{n-2}},b\rangle
as their unique cover; since

a\cdot\langle b\rangle\cdot a^{-1}=\langle a^{2^{n-2}}b\rangle
, they are
conjugate and form one conjugacy class, contributing a single class.

Summing over all families,

w(G)=3+2(n-3)+1=2n-2=2(n-1),

as claimed.
∎

4.
Complexity of dihedral groups
D_{p^{n}}

The dihedral group
D_{p^{n}}
presents a genuinely richer subgroup lattice
than the cyclic case
C_{p^{n}q}
.
While
\operatorname{Sub}(C_{p^{n}q})/C_{p^{n}q}
is a grid, the quotient

\operatorname{Sub}(D_{p^{n}})/D_{p^{n}}
carries a conjugacy class of reflections

d_{0}=[\langle s\rangle]
whose intersections with cyclic subgroups are
governed by the coprimality
\gcd(2,p)=1
.
This introduces a cross-restriction phenomenon
(Lemma
4.5
) with no analogue in the
C_{p^{n}q}
setting, and
its management requires a new bridge-exclusion argument
(Proposition
4.7
).
Despite this additional complication in the proof, the final answer equals
that for
C_{p^{n}q}
.

4.1.
Setup and the rank function

Fix an odd prime
p
and an integer
n\geq 1
. Set

G=D_{p^{n}}=\langle\,r,s\mid r^{p^{n}}=s^{2}=e,\;srs^{-1}=r^{-1}\,\rangle.

Every subgroup of
G
belongs to one of two families:

C_{p^{k}}:=\langle r^{p^{n-k}}\rangle,\quad 0\leq k\leq n,\qquad D_{p^{k}}:=\langle r^{p^{n-k}},s\rangle,\quad 0\leq k\leq n,

where
C_{p^{0}}=1
and
D_{p^{0}}\cong C_{2}
is generated by a reflection.
Since
p
is odd,
C_{p^{k}}
is the unique subgroup of order
p^{k}
(hence
normal in
G
), and all copies of
D_{p^{k}}
are conjugate in
G
.
We write
c_{k}
and
d_{k}
for the corresponding conjugacy classes.

The key arithmetic fact used throughout is:

(1)

\langle s\rangle\cap C_{p^{k}}=1\qquad\text{for all }k\geq 0,

which holds because
|\langle s\rangle|=2
and
|C_{p^{k}}|=p^{k}
are coprime.

Definition 4.1
.

Define
P\colon\operatorname{Sub}(G)\to\{0,1,\dots,n+1\}
by

P(C_{p^{k}})=k,\qquad P(D_{p^{k}})=k+1.

This is the restriction to
\operatorname{Sub}(D_{p^{n}})
of the rank map of

[
1
, Definition 2.10]
.

Proposition 4.2
.

P
is strictly order-preserving:
H\subsetneq K
implies
P(H)<P(K)
.

Proof.

We enumerate every possible type of proper inclusion.

•

C_{p^{j}}\subsetneq C_{p^{k}}
: occurs iff
j<k
, giving

P(C_{p^{j}})=j<k=P(C_{p^{k}})
.

•

C_{p^{j}}\leq D_{p^{k}}
: occurs iff
j\leq k
; then

P(C_{p^{j}})=j\leq k<k+1=P(D_{p^{k}})
.

•

D_{p^{j}}\subsetneq D_{p^{k}}
: occurs iff
j<k
, giving

P(D_{p^{j}})=j+1<k+1=P(D_{p^{k}})
.

•

D_{p^{j}}\leq C_{p^{k}}
: impossible. For
j\geq 1
,
D_{p^{j}}
is
non-abelian while
C_{p^{k}}
is abelian. For
j=0
, one would need

|D_{p^{0}}|=2
to divide
|C_{p^{k}}|=p^{k}
, which fails since
p
is odd.
∎

By Proposition
4.2
,
P
extends to a well-defined map on
intervals: for
(H,K)\in I(\operatorname{Sub}(G))
we write
P(H,K)=(P(H),P(K))
and call
this the
P
-arc
of the arrow.

Definition 4.3
.

Non-trivial arrows in
I(\operatorname{Sub}(G))
, up to conjugacy, are classified as
follows:

•

a
C
-arrow
:
(c_{i},c_{j})
for
0\leq i<j\leq n
;

•

a
D
-arrow
:
(d_{i},d_{j})
for
0\leq i<j\leq n
;

•

an
X
-arrow
:
(c_{i},d_{j})
for
0\leq i\leq j\leq n

(including vertical arrows
i=j
).

An arrow is
left-anchored
if its source is
c_{0}
or
d_{0}
.
For a minimal generating set
S
, we write
C
,
D
,
X
for the counts of

C
-,
D
-, and
X
-arrows in
S
respectively.

Remark 4.4
.

The three types of Definition
4.3
cover all non-trivial
arrows, since Proposition
4.2
rules out inclusions

D_{p^{j}}\leq C_{p^{k}}
.

Lemma 4.5

(Left-anchoring constraints)
.

Let
S
be a minimal generating set for
T\in\operatorname{Tr}(D_{p^{n}})
.

(i)

At most one left-anchored arrow of each type belongs to
S
.

(ii)

If
(d_{0},d_{k})\in S
, then
(c_{0},c_{j})\notin S
for all
j\leq k
.
The same conclusion holds with
(d_{0},d_{k})
replaced by any
X
-arrow

(c_{0},d_{k})\in S
.

Proof.

(i) We treat
C
-arrows; the other two types are identical.
Suppose
(c_{0},c_{j}),(c_{0},c_{l})\in S
with
j<l
.
Apply the restriction axiom to
(c_{0},c_{l})
with
L=C_{p^{j}}\leq C_{p^{l}}
:

(c_{0}\cap c_{j},\;c_{j})=(1\cap C_{p^{j}},\,C_{p^{j}})=(c_{0},c_{j})\in\langle S\rangle.

Hence
(c_{0},c_{j})\in\langle S\setminus\{(c_{0},c_{j})\}\rangle
, contradicting
minimality.

(ii) Suppose
(d_{0},d_{k})\in S
and fix

.
Since

, apply the restriction axiom to
(d_{0},d_{k})

with

(d_{0}\cap c_{j},\;c_{j})=\bigl(\langle s\rangle\cap C_{p^{j}},\,C_{p^{j}}\bigr)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:cop}}}{{=}}(1,C_{p^{j}})=(c_{0},c_{j})\in\langle S\rangle.

Hence
(c_{0},c_{j})\notin S
by minimality.
The case
(c_{0},d_{k})\in S
is identical.
∎

Lemma 4.6

(Inductive splitting)
.

Let
U\subseteq S
be the arrows of any two specified types in
S
, and set

U^{\prime}=\{a\in U:a\text{ is not left-anchored}\}
.
Then
U^{\prime}
is a minimal generating set for
\langle U^{\prime}\rangle
, viewed as a
transfer system on the sub-lattice
\operatorname{Sub}(G)_{\geq 1}\cong\operatorname{Sub}(D_{p^{n-1}})
.

Proof.

The map
\iota\colon\operatorname{Sub}(D_{p^{n-1}})\to\operatorname{Sub}(D_{p^{n}})
defined by

c_{k}\mapsto c_{k+1}
and
d_{k}\mapsto d_{k+1}
is a lattice embedding
preserving all inclusion and conjugacy relations.
Under
\iota
, the arrows of
U^{\prime}
biject with arrows of the corresponding
types in
\operatorname{Sub}(D_{p^{n-1}})
.
Suppose for contradiction that some
a\in U^{\prime}
satisfies

a\in\langle U^{\prime}\setminus\{a\}\rangle
.
Since
U^{\prime}\subseteq S
, we have

\langle U^{\prime}\setminus\{a\}\rangle\subseteq\langle S\setminus\{a\}\rangle
,
so
a\in\langle S\setminus\{a\}\rangle
, contradicting minimality of
S
.
∎

Proposition 4.7

(Bridge-exclusion bounds)
.

Let
S
be a minimal generating set for
T\in\operatorname{Tr}(D_{p^{n}})
. Then:

(1)

C+D\leq n
, with equality implying

(c_{0},c_{n}),(d_{0},d_{n})\in\langle S\rangle
.

(2)

C+X\leq n+1
, with equality implying
(c_{0},d_{n})\in\langle S\rangle
.

(3)

D+X\leq n+1
, with equality implying
(d_{0},d_{n})\in\langle S\rangle
.

Proof.

All three bounds are proved by induction on
n
.
In each part we let
U
denote the arrows of the relevant pair of types in

S
, set
U_{0}=\{a\in U:a\text{ is left-anchored}\}
, and put
U^{\prime}=U\setminus U_{0}
.
By Lemma
4.6
and the inductive hypothesis,
|U^{\prime}|
is bounded by
the corresponding statement for
D_{p^{n-1}}
.

Base cases.

When
n=0
,
G=D_{1}\cong C_{2}
has the single non-trivial arrow
(c_{0},d_{0})
,
which is an
X
-arrow.
Bounds (2) and (3) hold trivially.
For (1) at
n=1
: the only
C
-arrow is
(c_{0},c_{1})
and the only
D
-arrow
is
(d_{0},d_{1})
.
By Lemma
4.5
(ii) with
k=j=1
, if
(d_{0},d_{1})\in S
then

(c_{0},c_{1})\notin S
, giving
C+D\leq 1=n
.

Proof of (1):
C+D\leq n
.

Let
U
be the
C
- and
D
-arrows of
S
.
By induction
|U^{\prime}|\leq n-1
.

Case 1:

U_{0}
contains no
D
-arrow.
By Lemma
4.5
(i),
|U_{0}|\leq 1
, so
C+D\leq n
.

Case 2:

U_{0}
contains a
D
-arrow
(d_{0},d_{k})
for some
1\leq k\leq n
.
By Lemma
4.5
(ii), any
C
-arrow in
U_{0}
must be
(c_{0},c_{l})

with
l>k
, and by Lemma
4.5
(i) there is at most one such

C
-arrow.

Sub-case 2a:

U_{0}
contains no
C
-arrow.
Then
|U_{0}|=1
and
C+D\leq n
by
|U^{\prime}|\leq n-1
.

Sub-case 2b:

U_{0}
contains exactly one
C
-arrow
(c_{0},c_{l})

with
l>k
.
Then
|U_{0}|=2
.
We claim
|U^{\prime}|\leq n-2
, giving
C+D\leq n
.
Define

U^{\prime}_{A}:=\{a\in U^{\prime}:\text{both endpoints of }a\text{ have }P\text{-value}\leq k\},

U^{\prime}_{B}:=\{a\in U^{\prime}:\text{both endpoints of }a\text{ have }P\text{-value}\geq l\}.

We show
U^{\prime}=U^{\prime}_{A}\sqcup U^{\prime}_{B}
by ruling out bridges crossing the gap
(k,l)
.

No
D
-bridge.

Suppose
(d_{a},d_{b})\in U^{\prime}
with
1\leq a\leq k
and

.
Since

for all

, restricting

(d_{0},d_{k})
to

(d_{0},d_{a})\in\langle S\rangle
.
The composition axiom applied to
(d_{0},d_{a})
and
(d_{a},d_{b})
yields

(d_{0},d_{b})\in\langle S\rangle
.
Restricting to

(valid since

) and
applying (

):

\bigl(\langle s\rangle\cap C_{p^{l}},\,C_{p^{l}}\bigr)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:cop}}}{{=}}(1,C_{p^{l}})=(c_{0},c_{l})\in\langle S\rangle,

contradicting

.

No
C
-bridge.

Suppose
(c_{a},c_{b})\in U^{\prime}
with
1\leq a\leq k
and

.
Restricting
(d_{0},d_{k})

; composing with the left-anchored

.

.

Corollary 4.8
.

For any minimal generating set
S
of
T\in\operatorname{Tr}(D_{p^{n}})
,

, adding the three bounds:

4.3.
Lower bound via a partial rainbow

We construct a partial rainbow on
\operatorname{Sub}(D_{p^{n}})
achieving the upper bound
of Corollary
4.8
.

Lemma 4.9
.

For
0\leq j<k\leq n+1
, let
\alpha(j,k)
denote the number of

G
-conjugacy classes of non-trivial arrows in
I(\operatorname{Sub}(G))
with
P
-arc

(j,k)
. Then

\alpha(j,k)=\begin{cases}1&j=0,\;k=n+1,\\
2&j=0,\;1\leq k\leq n,\\
2&1\leq j\leq n,\;k=n+1,\\
3&1\leq j<k\leq n.\end{cases}

Proof.

A subgroup of
P
-value
j
is
C_{p^{j}}
(when
j\leq n
) or
D_{p^{j-1}}

(when
j\geq 1
).
Since
G
acts transitively on conjugates of each
D_{p^{l}}
, the number of
conjugacy classes of arrows with a given
P
-arc equals the number of valid
(source type, target type) pairs, each contributing exactly one class.
We enumerate:

(a)

C_{p^{j}}\hookrightarrow C_{p^{k}}
(requires
j<k\leq n
): both
normal, conjugacy-fixed:
1
class.

(b)

C_{p^{j}}\hookrightarrow D_{p^{k-1}}
(requires
j\leq k-1
,

k\leq n
): since
C_{p^{j}}\unlhd G
, conjugation acts transitively on the
target:
1
class.

(c)

D_{p^{j-1}}\hookrightarrow D_{p^{k-1}}
(requires
1\leq j<k
,

k\leq n
):
G
acts transitively on conjugates of
D_{p^{j-1}}
, each
inside the corresponding conjugate of
D_{p^{k-1}}
:
1
class.

(d)

D_{p^{j-1}}\hookrightarrow C_{p^{k}}
: impossible by
Proposition
4.2
:
0
classes.

Combining subject to boundary conditions:

j=0,\,k=n+1
: unique inclusion
1\hookrightarrow G
,
\alpha=1
;

j=0,\,1\leq k\leq n
: types (a) and (b) give
\alpha=2
;

1\leq j\leq n,\,k=n+1
: types (b) and (c) give
\alpha=2

(type (a) would require
C_{p^{n+1}}
, which does not exist);

1\leq j<k\leq n
: types (a), (b), (c) give
\alpha=3
.
∎

.

, so
|S|=m(\langle S\rangle)\leq c(D_{p^{n}})
.

Even case,
n=2m
.

The arc
(0,n+1)
has
\alpha=1
; each inner arc
(i,2m+1-i)
,
1\leq i\leq m
,
has
\alpha=3
.
Hence
|S|=1+3m=\lfloor 3n/2\rfloor+1
.

Odd case,
n=2m+1
.

The arc
(0,n)=(0,2m+1)
has
j=0
,
k=n
, giving
\alpha=2
.
Each inner arc
(i,2m+1-i)
,
1\leq i\leq m
, satisfies
1\leq i<2m+1-i\leq n-1
,
giving
\alpha=3
.
Hence
|S|=2+3m=\lfloor 3n/2\rfloor+1
.
∎

4.4.
Proof of Theorem D

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and
n\geq 1
. Then

c(D_{p^{n}})=\left\lfloor\frac{3n}{2}\right\rfloor+1=\begin{cases}3k+1&n=2k,\\
3k+2&n=2k+1.\end{cases}

Proof.

Corollary
4.8
gives
c(D_{p^{n}})\leq\lfloor 3n/2\rfloor+1
.
Proposition
4.10
produces
T=\langle S\rangle\in\operatorname{Tr}(D_{p^{n}})

with
m(T)=\lfloor 3n/2\rfloor+1
, so
c(D_{p^{n}})\geq\lfloor 3n/2\rfloor+1
.
The parity formula is elementary arithmetic.
∎

Remark 4.12
.

Theorem
4.11
is the direct non-abelian analogue of

[
1
, Corollary 6.6]
.
The two results share identical arithmetic, but diverge structurally in the
proof of the upper bound.
For
C_{p^{n}q}
, top and bottom arrows are independent under restriction at
the left boundary, so each pairwise bound in
[
1
, Lemma 6.3]

follows in a single induction.
For
D_{p^{n}}
, the cross-restriction of Lemma
4.5
(ii) (a
consequence of
\gcd(2,p)=1
) entangles
C
- and
D
-arrows at the left
boundary, necessitating the bridge-exclusion argument in Sub-case 2b, which
has no counterpart in
[
1
]
.

The lower bounds are more parallel.
In both cases the interior arcs (those with
1\leq j<k\leq n
) contribute

\alpha=3
, and the boundary arcs contribute
1
or
2
.
This coincidence of
\alpha
-values reflects the fact that
D_{p^{n}}
and

C_{p^{n}q}
have the same count of conjugacy classes of proper inclusions
between non-extremal subgroups, forcing the two complexities to be equal.

5.
Complexity of semidihedral groups
\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}}
: a lower bound

We now turn to a lower bound for the complexity of
\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}}
.
Throughout this section fix
n\geq 4
and
G=\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}}
with the notation of
Section 3.1.
Since
G
is a
2
-group, the rank function of
[
1
, Definition 2.10]

takes the form
P(H)=\log_{2}|H|
for every
H\leq G
, ranging over

\{0,1,\dots,n\}
.

Remark 5.1
.

In this section, we write
\mathcal{C}_{2^{r}}
,
\mathcal{D}_{2^{r}}
,

\mathcal{Q}_{2^{r}}
for the conjugacy class of subgroups of
\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}}
that
are cyclic, dihedral-type (inside
M_{d}
), and quaternion-type (inside
M_{q}
)
of order
2^{r}
, respectively.
We abbreviate these as
C
-type,
D
-type, and
Q
-type subgroups at rank
r
.
This notation is distinct from the
D_{p^{k}}
of Section 4, which denotes a
specific dihedral subgroup of
D_{p^{n}}
of order
2p^{k}
.

5.1.
Forbidden inclusions and arrow types

The three-strand structure of
\operatorname{Sub}(G)
noted in Section 3.1 imposes
constraints on which inclusions between subgroups are possible.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
H
be a
D
-type subgroup of order
2^{j}
and
K
be a
Q
-type
subgroup of order
2^{j}
in
\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}}
, with
j\geq 1
and
j\geq 2

respectively. Then:

(i)

No
D
-type subgroup of order
2^{j}
(
j\geq 1
) embeds in any cyclic
subgroup;

(ii)

No
Q
-type subgroup of order
2^{j}
(
j\geq 2
) embeds in any cyclic
subgroup;

(iii)

No
Q
-type subgroup of order
2^{j}
(
j\geq 2
) embeds in any
D
-type
subgroup;

(iv)

No
D
-type subgroup of order
2^{j}
(
j\geq 1
) embeds in any
Q
-type
subgroup.

Consequently the only valid inclusion types between proper subgroups are

C\hookrightarrow C
,
C\hookrightarrow D
,
C\hookrightarrow Q
,

D\hookrightarrow D
, and
Q\hookrightarrow Q
.

Proof.

All cyclic subgroups of
G
lie in
M_{c}=\langle a\rangle
, which is abelian.
Hence no non-abelian subgroup embeds in any cyclic subgroup; this gives (ii)
for
j\geq 3
(since
Q
-type subgroups of order
\geq 8
are non-abelian) and
all of (i) for
j\geq 2
.

For (i) with
j=1
: the
D
-type subgroup of order
2
is
\langle x_{s}\rangle=\langle a^{2s}b\rangle
for some
s
, a reflection. No reflection is a
power of
a
, since
b\notin M_{c}=\langle a\rangle
. Hence no reflection
embeds in any cyclic subgroup. ✓

For (ii) with
j=2
: the
Q
-type subgroup of order
4
is
\langle y_{s}\rangle=\langle a^{2s+1}b\rangle
, where
y_{s}^{2}=a^{(2s+1)2^{n-2}}\neq e

for
n\geq 4
(since
(2s+1)2^{n-2}\not\equiv 0\pmod{2^{n-1}}
as
2s+1

is odd). So
y_{s}
has order
4
, and
\langle y_{s}\rangle\cong C_{4}
is
non-abelian-free but not contained in the cyclic
\langle a\rangle
since

y_{s}\notin M_{c}
. ✓

For (iii): every element of a
Q
-type subgroup outside its cyclic part has
order
4
(as computed above), while every element of a
D
-type subgroup
outside its cyclic part is a reflection of order
2
. An element of order

4
cannot belong to a
D
-type subgroup. ✓

For (iv): every
D
-type subgroup contains a reflection (an element of order

2
that is non-central, i.e., not equal to
z=a^{2^{n-2}}
), while in
every
Q
-type subgroup the unique element of order
2
is
z
(central in

G
). A non-central involution cannot embed in any
Q
-type subgroup.
✓∎

5.2.
The
\alpha
-function for
\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}}

For
0\leq j<k\leq n
, let
\alpha(j,k)
denote the number of
G
-conjugacy
classes of non-trivial arrows in
\mathcal{I}(\operatorname{Sub}(G))
with
P
-arc
(j,k)
.
At each rank level
r
, the conjugacy classes of subgroups are:
rank
0
, one class (
C_{1}
); rank
1
, two classes (one
C
-type
\langle z\rangle

and one
D
-type
\langle b\rangle
, both of order
2
);
ranks
2\leq r\leq n-1
, three classes (
C
-type,
D
-type,
Q
-type, all
of order
2^{r}
);

n
, one class (
G
).
Since
G
acts transitively on conjugates of each
D
-type and
Q
-type
subgroup, each valid (source type, target type) pair contributes exactly one
conjugacy class of arrows.

Lemma 5.3
.

\alpha(j,k)=\begin{cases}1&j=0,\;k=n,\\
2&j=0,\;k=1,\\
3&j=0,\;2\leq k\leq n-1,\\
2&j=1,\;k=n,\\
3&2\leq j\leq n-1,\;k=n,\\
4&j=1,\;2\leq k\leq n-1,\\
5&2\leq j<k\leq n-1.\end{cases}

Proof.

We enumerate valid (source type, target type) pairs in each region.

For
j=0
,
k=n
: the unique inclusion
C_{1}\hookrightarrow G
gives

\alpha=1
.
For
j=0
,
k=1
: target types are one
C
-type and one
D
-type of rank

1
, both reachable from
C_{1}
, giving
\alpha=2
.
For
j=0
,
2\leq k\leq n-1
: target types are
C
-type,
D
-type, and

Q
-type of rank
k
, all reachable from
C_{1}
, giving
\alpha=3
.

For
j=1
,
k=n
: source types are the
C
-type and the
D
-type of rank

1
, both of which embed in
G
, giving
\alpha=2
.
For
2\leq j\leq n-1
,
k=n
: source types are
C
-type,
D
-type, and

Q
-type of rank
j
, all of which embed in
G
, giving
\alpha=3
.

For
j=1
,
2\leq k\leq n-1
: source types are
C
-type and
D
-type of rank

1
; target types are
C
-type,
D
-type, and
Q
-type of rank
k
.
Lemma
5.2
(i) forbids
D\hookrightarrow C
and
Lemma
5.2
(iv) forbids
D\hookrightarrow Q
.
The remaining valid pairs are
(C,C)
,
(C,D)
,
(C,Q)
,
(D,D)
,
giving
\alpha=4
.

For
2\leq j<k\leq n-1
: source types are
C
-type,
D
-type, and

-type
of rank
j
; target types are
C
-type,
D
-type, and

-type of rank

.
Lemma

forbids
D\hookrightarrow C
(i),
Q\hookrightarrow C

(ii),

(iv).

(C,C)
,
(C,D)
,

,

.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

The value
\alpha=5
in the interior region

reflects a
structural feature absent in the dihedral case

: the three-strand

with mutual non-embeddings between the

5.3.
Lower bound via a partial rainbow

Proposition 5.5
.

For each
n\geq 4
there exists a partial rainbow on
\operatorname{Sub}(\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}})
of
size
\lfloor 5(n-1)/2\rfloor
.

Proof.

Write
n=2m+1
(odd) or
n=2m
(even).

Odd case,
n=2m+1
, so
m\geq 2
since
n\geq 5
.

Take the rainbow

R=\{(0,n),\,(1,n-1),\,(2,n-2),\,\dots,\,(m,m+1)\}

of
m+1
strictly nested arcs on
\{0,\dots,n\}
.
By Lemma
5.3
:
the arc
(0,n)
has
\alpha=1
;
the arc
(1,n-1)
has
j=1
and
k=n-1=2m\geq 2
(since
m\geq 2
), giving

\alpha=4
;
each arc
(i,n+1-i)
for
2\leq i\leq m
has
2\leq i<n+1-i\leq n-1
(since

i\leq m
implies
n+1-i\geq m+2>i
), giving
\alpha=5
.
There are
m-1
such inner arcs.
Choosing one arrow from each conjugacy class per arc yields a partial rainbow

S
with

|S|=1+4+5(m-1)=5m=\left\lfloor\frac{5(n-1)}{2}\right\rfloor.

Even case,
n=2m
, so
m\geq 2
since
n\geq 4
.

Take the rainbow

R_{A}=\{(1,n),\,(2,n-1),\,\dots,\,(m,m+1)\}

of
m
strictly nested arcs on
\{0,\dots,n\}
, with
0
omitted.
By Lemma
5.3
:
the arc
(1,n)
has
j=1
,
k=n
, giving
\alpha=2
;
each arc
(i,2m+1-i)
for
2\leq i\leq m
has
j=i\geq 2
and

k=2m+1-i\leq 2m-1=n-1
(since
i\geq 2
) and
j<k
(since
i\leq m<2m+1-i
),
giving
\alpha=5
.
There are
m-1
such inner arcs.
Hence

|S|=2+5(m-1)=5m-3=\left\lfloor\frac{5(n-1)}{2}\right\rfloor.

In both cases the
P
-arcs of
S
form a rainbow by construction, and exactly
one arrow is chosen from each conjugacy class, so
S
satisfies

[
1
, Definition 3.2]
.
By
[
1
, Proposition 3.3]
,
S
is a minimal generating set for

\langle S\rangle
, giving
m(\langle S\rangle)=|S|\leq c(\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}})
.
∎

Theorem 5.6
.

For
n\geq 4
,

c(\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}})\geq\left\lfloor\frac{5(n-1)}{2}\right\rfloor=\begin{cases}5m-3&n=2m,\\
5m&n=2m+1.\end{cases}

Proof.

Immediate from Proposition
5.5
and

[
1
, Proposition 3.3]
.
∎

The first few values of the lower bound are
c(\operatorname{SD}_{16})\geq 7
,

c(\operatorname{SD}_{32})\geq 10
,
c(\operatorname{SD}_{64})\geq 12
, and
c(\operatorname{SD}_{128})\geq 15
,
corresponding to
n=4,5,6,7
respectively.

6.
Concluding remarks

The formulas obtained fit neatly into the pattern observed for dihedral and
quaternion groups in
[
8
]
.
For
\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}}
the value
2n-2
closely parallels
w(D_{2^{n}})=2n-1

and
w(Q_{2^{n+2}})=2n+2
; the slight discrepancy reflects the presence of
three rather than two maximal subgroups.
The quasidihedral structure, being a close cousin of the dihedral case,
simplifies the count via the same chain arguments used for
M_{d}
and

M_{q}
.
For
\operatorname{AGL}(1,p^{n})
the expression
\Omega(p^{n}-1)+\tau(n)
exhibits an
attractive interplay between the arithmetic of
p^{n}-1
and the divisor
lattice of
n
.

The complexity computation for
D_{p^{n}}
(Theorem
4.11
) resolves
the question raised for this family:
c(D_{p^{n}})=\lfloor 3n/2\rfloor+1
,
matching
c(C_{p^{n}q})
exactly despite the structurally richer subgroup
lattice.
The coincidence is explained by the equal
\alpha
-values for interior arcs
(Remark
4.12
).
Theorem
5.6
establishes that
c(\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}})\geq\lfloor 5(n-1)/2\rfloor
, driven by the larger interior
\alpha
-value of
5
arising
from the three-strand, no
D
-
Q
interaction structure of
\operatorname{Sub}(\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}})
.
The values
n=4,5,6,7
have been verified against the exact complexity using
the
ninfty
software package of
[
2
]
, confirming equality in
each case.
We therefore conjecture that

c(\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}})=\left\lfloor\frac{5(n-1)}{2}\right\rfloor

for all
n\geq 4
.
Establishing the matching upper bound would require pairwise bounds on the
five arrow-type counts analogous to Proposition
4.7
, together
with bridge-exclusion arguments exploiting the behaviour of the central element

z=a^{2^{n-2}}\in Z(\operatorname{SD}_{2^{n}})
under the restriction axiom.
Computing the complexity for the affine groups
\operatorname{AGL}(1,p^{n})
and extending
the analysis to higher-dimensional affine groups
\operatorname{AGL}(d,q)
remain
further open problems.
