 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 NLR conducted a conceptual design study for scaling up biomass conversion technology from
 Green Fortress Engineering (GFE). GFE has proprietary technology where cellulosic biomass is
 thermally converted to a tar-free syngas. A ceramic membrane separation unit (MSU)
 previously developed with NLR can extract high-purity hydrogen from the syngas. The energy
 content of the retentate stream is sufficient to power the process with a gas-powered genset.
 Design details including mass and energy balances, process and instrumentation diagrams
 (P&ID), and major equipment schedules were delivered.

 Task 1: Conduct Study on Power Needs for MSU

 Subtask 1:1

 The participant will provide the Contractor with the relevant information to conduct the study
 outlined in task 1.2. This includes dimensions and materials plus operating conditions such as
 pressure and temperature for each MSU.

 Subtask 1.2

 The contractor will conduct a study of the parasitic power required for a three-stage MSU
 (needed for 6-9’s purity) contemplating heat economizing from thermal sources within the
 overall Biomass Super Gasifier (BMSG) system. The Contractor will provide the study to the
 Participant, the Participant will review the study and provide feedback to the Contractor.

 The study will quantify power requirements, economize thermal energy, and create a flow chart
 of control design for a three-stage MSU. Most hydrogen off-take applications call for high purity
 hydrogen (e.g. 99.9999%), which will require a series of three individual MSU with interstage
 blowers because the native separation per stage is expected to be 99.5% with is sufficient for
 International Organization for Standardization (ISO) purity, but not the full spectrum of
 commercial applications.

 The project kicked off with a series of meaningful discussions with GFE to elucidate the
 operating conditions for the hydrogen purification process. Initial discussions focused on
 temperature and pressure requirements for an individual MSU stage. The first design task
 assessed mass and energy balances for a high-temperature (500-600°T), high-pressure (60-120
 psig) process requiring significant heat recovery and economizing. By re-evaluating material
 properties and performance requirements, the Contractor recommended a moderate-temperature
 (< 200°C), low pressure (< 15 psig) process requiring minimal thermal management. This
 represents significant savings in terms of footprint, power requirements, capital expenditures,
 and operating expenses for the overall process. The optimizing MSU unit with heat exchange
 and recompression is shown in Figure 1.

 This refinement of operating conditions allowed for detailed equipment selection to calculate
 power requirements. The energetics of rotating machinery (blowers and compressors) are
 evaluated in terms of isentropic efficiency, which determines both power requirements and
 adiabatic heating inside the process. The high-fidelity mass balances for the MSU units defined
 the retentate gas composition, which was evaluated as a generator fuel stream operating at 30%
 thermal efficiency. These inputs allowed us to demonstrate 40% of the power generated is
 required to operate the process, with 60% available for net metering to the grid as shown in
 Table 1.
