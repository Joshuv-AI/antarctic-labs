A dual-probe system for autofluorescence-free determination of trace-level polystyrene nanoplastics
Authors: Bei-Bei Wang, Ling Sun, Kai Liu, Jin-Qiu Wang, Fang Geng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanoplastics (NPs) are new types of pollutants that have emerged in food which has attracted widespread attention. However, due to the small size and low concentration, and the complexity of food matrices, the selective and sensitive determination of NPs in food is challenging. Herein, we report a dual-probe system for determination of trace-level polystyrene (PS) NPs with high sensitivity and selectivity. The proposed dual-probe system was composed of magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles functionalized with PS-specific peptides (Fe3O4@Au-PSBP) and a high-quality Li+-doped ZnGa2O4:Cr3+ persistent luminescence nanoparticles (PLNPs) modified with ethylene glycol chitosan. By integrating peptide-based specific recognition, persistent luminescence signaling, and magnetic separation with pH-switchable charge reversal, the proposed system enables effective capture and sensitive detection of PS NPs in complex matrices. Under the optimized conditions, the method showed a linear range of 50–800 pg mL−1 and a detection limit of 8.11 pg mL−1. The precision for the determination of 50 pg mL−1 PS NPs was 5.24% (RSD, n = 11). The method was successfully applied to the analysis of PS NPs in different matrices, including bottled purified water, saltwater (3.5% salinity), artificial lake water, and tea beverage, with recoveries ranging from 91.25% to 110.53%. More importantly, the proposed system can be readily adapted for the analysis of other targets by replacing the recognition unit, providing a versatile and selective sensing strategy for trace-level hazardous analytes.

Introduction

Microplastics (1 μm‒1mm, MPs) and nanoplastics (<100 nm, NPs) are emerging global pollutants in recent years, posing a significant threat to the environment and human health (Guo et al., 2025; Nissen et al., 2024). Studies have shown that micro/nano plastics (MPs/NPs) have been detected in the ocean, freshwater, soil and air (H. Zhao et al., 2025a). MPs/NPs in the environment will enter and accumulate in drinking water and food along the food chain (Rose et al., 2023). For instance, MPs/NPs present in aquatic environments can accumulate in aquatic products such as fish, shrimp, and crabs through contaminated water (Kim et al., 2022; R. Zhao et al., 2025b), while MPs/NPs in soil environments can migrate into plant-based foods (e.g., fruits and vegetables) as well as animal-derived foods (e.g., meat, eggs, and milk) (Gamarro et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2022). In addition, plastic materials such as polystyrene (PS), which exhibit excellent thermal insulation, high light transmittance, and long-term stability, are widely used in food packaging and pre-packaged food containers (Ajaj et al., 2021; Lv et al., 2019). The extensive use of plastic packaging inevitably increases the risk of MPs/NPs migrating from packaging materials into food (Guazzotti et al., 2022; Norte et al., 2026; Pilevar et al., 2019; Yurtsever and Cüvelek, 2024). The existence of M/NPs will have a very serious impact on human health, such as causing oxidative damage, endocrine disorders, as well as problems related to reproduction and growth capabilities. (Fard et al., 2025; Q＀＀J.-L. Liu et al., 2021a). As the input of MPs/NPs in food and the environment continues to increase, the pollution caused by them has become a major global issue and a focus of attention. Therefore, the research on the detection methods, and analytical means of MPs/NPs in different environmental and food media has become an area that urgently needs exploration.

In response to the common problems in current detection, such as strong interference from food matrix and cumbersome pre-treatment procedures, persistent luminescence nanoparticles (PLNPs) offer a promising new approach to break through these limitations. PLNPs can continue to emit persistent luminescence (PL) for several minutes to several days even after the light source is turned off (Luo et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). This distinctive characteristic enables detectable signal output without real-time excitation during measurement, thereby effectively avoiding background fluorescence and light-scattering interference associated with conventional fluorescence techniques. These remarkable advantages position them as a revolutionary tool with broad application prospects across multiple fields (Fu et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2022; J.-L＀＀Q. Liu et al., 2021b; Sun et al., 2024). In the field of biological imaging and detection, benefiting from their self-sustaining luminescence, PLNPs are ideal contrast agents for optical imaging in biomedical application (Yan et al., 2024). In analytical chemistry, PLNPs are being integrated into advanced sensors for in-situ and long-term monitoring of pollutants in complex environments, allowing continuous in situ monitoring of pollutants or pathogens over hours or even days within complex matrices such as environmental water. This dramatically broadens the temporal and spatial scope of analytical detection (Gao et al., 2024; Lai et al., 2021; Luo et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2022). Consequently, PLNPs provide an unprecedented high signal-to-noise ratio platform that substantially enhances analytical sensitivity and reliability. Although PLNPs have been successfully applied in biological imaging, in vitro diagnostics, and detection of environmental pollutants, the systematic utilization of their PL properties for highly sensitive detection of trace contaminants in food is still in the initial exploration stage. Further research is needed in aspects such as probe design, signal conversion mechanism, and practical sample applicability.

Herein, we report a dual-probe system for the determination of trace-level PS NPs in real samples with high sensitivity and selectivity. The system consists of two key components: PS-specific peptide-functionalized magnetic Fe3O4 for selective recognition and separation, and PLNP probe for low-background optical readout. Among various persistent luminescence materials, Cr3+-activated gallate spinel nanoparticles are attractive for analytical sensing because of their long-lived emission, good chemical/photostability, and suitability for low-background optical detection in complex media. In particular, the persistent luminescence properties of ZnGa2O4:Cr3+ can be effectively tuned through defect/trap engineering by ion doping. Based on these considerations and our previous studies on gallate-based persistent phosphors (B. Wang et al., 2021; B. Wang et al., 2025b), Li+ doping was introduced to regulate the trap-state distribution of ZnGa2O4:Cr3+, thereby enhancing the emission intensity and enabling efficient persistent luminescence under ambient conditions.

To construct a pH-responsive signal transduction platform, glycol chitosan (GCS) was further introduced to fabricate Li+-doped ZnGa2O4:Cr3+ PLNPs@GCS, which exhibited reversible surface charge switching under different pH conditions. After Fe3O4@Au-PSBP captured PS NPs from the sample, the surface became more electronegative, allowing adsorption of the positively charged PLNPs@GCS in an acidic medium. Under alkaline conditions, PLNPs@GCS was released due to charge reversal, and the persistent luminescence signal was restored, thereby enabling rapid, highly sensitive, and selective detection of PS NPs. This dual-probe system integrates specific recognition, magnetic separation, and sensitive signal output, and it may also be extended to other NPs or hazardous analytes by replacing the recognition unit.

Results and discussion

Schematic diagram of PLNPs@GCS synthesis and dual-probe system for detecting PS NPs

Scheme 1 illustrates the synthesis of Li+-doped ZGO:Cr PLNPs and the construction of a dual-probe system for the determination of trace-level PS NPs. The material with a Li+ doping ratio of x = 0.3 was chosen as the background-free luminescent probe owing to its highest persistent luminescence intensity. GCS was subsequently grafted onto the surface of Li+-doped ZGO:Cr PLNPs to obtain the pH-responsive probe PLNPs@GCS, whose surface charge varied markedly with pH (Scheme 1a). As shown in Scheme 1b, Fe3O4@Au-PSBP first specifically captures PS NPs in the sample. As the amount of captured PS NPs increases, the zeta potential of Fe3O4@Au-PSBP becomes more negative. Under acidic conditions, positively charged PLNPs@GCS is electrostatically adsorbed onto the magnetic probe, and the adsorption amount increases with the amount of captured PS NPs. After the first magnetic separation, the supernatant containing unbound PLNPs@GCS is discarded. Upon switching to alkaline conditions, PLNPs@GCS is released from Fe3O4@Au-PSBP-PS owing to electrostatic repulsion. The magnetic complexes are then removed, and the PL intensity of the released PLNPs@GCS in the supernatant is positively correlated with the PS NPs concentration. Based on this mechanism, a highly sensitive method for PS NPs detection was established. Effect of Li + -doping on the morphology of ZGO:Cr PLNPs

To show the effect of Li+ doping, a series of LixZn1-xGa2O4:Cr3+ (x = 0–0.5) PLNPs were prepared by tuning the doping of Li+ ions in the precursor solution. Without Li+ ion doping (i.e., x = 0), the original ZnGa2O4:Cr3+ showed highly crystalline typical cubic spinel structure with spherical and monodispersed morphology. With the increase of Li + ions addition, the morphology of LixZn1-xGa2O4:Cr3+ gradually changed from spherical to cubic (Fig. 1a), while the particle size remained unchanged of 13.73 ± 4.31 nm (Fig. 1b and Fig. S1). The prepared LixZn1-xGa2O4:Cr3+ (x = 0–0.5) PLNPs all exhibited a typical cubic spinel structure (PDF#86-0413) (Fig. 1c), indicating that Li+-doping did not change the basic crystal form of ZGO:Cr PLNPs. As the amount of Li+-doping increased, the crystallinity of the 311 crystal plane gradually increased, reaching a maximum at x = 0.3 (Fig. 1d). It might be attributed to the lattice contraction caused by ion substitution. In the tetra-coordinated structure, the radius of the Zn2+ was 0.60 Å, and Li+ was slightly smaller than Zn2+, with a radius of 0.59 Å. This indicated that Li+ was likely to replace the Zn2+ in the quaternary structure. Subsequently, the composition of the synthesized LixZn1-xGa2O4:Cr3+ was characterized by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). The results showed that the material was composed of Li, Zn, Ga and Cr elements (Table S1). Effect of Li + -doping on the optical properties of ZGO:Cr PLNPs

Fig. 2a showed that the emission spectra still maintain the typical R-type characteristic of ZGO:Cr PLNPs, which is attributed to the 2E → 4A2 transition of Cr3+ in a distorted octahedral site (B. Wang et al., 2025a; Q. Wang et al., 2018). This indicates that Li+ doping did not alter the shape of the emission peak or the position of the maximum emission peak, suggesting that Li+ doping did not affect the luminescent center. However, the PL emission intensity of PLNPs changed significantly, relying on the Li + doping contents. LixZn1-xGa2O4:Cr3+ (x = 0.3) PLNPs showed the highest PL emission intensity, about 1.7 times compared to that of the undoped sample ZnGa2O4:Cr3+ (x = 0). The excitation spectrum (Fig. 2b) exhibits three intense peaks, which are consistent with the excitation peaks of ZnGa2O4:Cr matrix reported in the literature (Mian et al., 2022; Shrivastava et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2021). The first peak, which is the strongest and centered at approximately 260 nm, arises from the overlap of the ZnGa2O4 matrix excitation band and the O–Cr charge transfer band. The second and third peaks are attributed to the 4A2→4T1 transition (centered at ∼420 nm) and the 4A2→4T2 transition (centered at ∼570 nm), respectively (Tuerdi and Abdukayum, 2019). Furthermore, after Li+ doping, the excitation peak exhibits a slight red shift, and its intensity first increases and then decreases with increasing Li+ content, reaching a maximum at x = 0.3, which is consistent with the trend observed for the emission peak. This slight red shift may be related to the change in the band structure caused by the introduction of Li+-doped, as well as the slight lattice contraction. Further we investigated the effect of Li+-doping on the PL decay time (Fig. 2c). The average PL decay time (τav) of the Li+-undoped sample (x = 0) was 8.84 s. Li+-doping significantly enhanced the τav, which reached a maximum value of 36.07 s at x = 0.5, 308% longer than that of the nanocrystals without Li+-doping. The next highest value was observed at x = 0.3 (τav = 24.69 s, a 179% increase). All prepared PLNPs demonstrated typical phosphorescent behavior, as evidenced by their characteristic triple-exponential decay in phosphorescence intensity. The characteristic was widely attributed to complex multi-level energy transitions involving electron self-excitation and self-recombination (Shen et al., 2023). The specific profile of the PL decay curve was governed by energy transfer processes and electron hopping within the material. In such PL systems, excited electrons could persist in metastable states for extended durations after the cessation of excitation, followed by energy release via a triple-exponential decay pattern. Further, a three-exponential decomposition analysis was conducted on the PL decay curve to quantitatively compare the PL decay time of each doping ratio. The fitting results (Table S2 and Table S3) showed that the residual light decay of all samples contained three decay processes: fast (A1), medium (A2), and slow (A3), corresponding to the re-release processes of shallow traps, medium-depth traps, and deep traps for carriers. The A1 values were all close to 1, indicating that the fast decay process dominates the total luminescence. Regardless of the doping concentration, the τ1 values of all Li + -doped samples (x = 0.1–0.5) were higher than those of the undoped samples (x = 0), suggesting that Li+-doping significantly extends the PL time of carriers in shallow traps. The change trend was consistent with the variation pattern of the fast-decaying component τ1, which further indicated that Li+ doping optimized the distribution of shallow trap energy levels, achieving control of the PL lifetime. All the prepared PLNPs exhibited excellent long-lasting PL properties. The PL signal rapidly decayed within a short period of time, then gradually stabilized and remained stable for a long time (Fig. 2d). Moreover, PLNPs can be repeatedly excited by red LED lights (650 nm) to restore the PL. Compared with the undoped PLNPs, in the absence of external light supply, the near-infrared (NIR) PL of the Li+-doped PLNPs still showed an obvious detectable signal after 6 h, and the NIR PL signals were higher when the doping levels were 0.3 and 0.5 (Fig. 2e). It is indicated that Li+-doped PLNPs possess excellent NIR imaging properties and can be utilized for in vivo detection. An attempt was made to adjust the Li + -doping ratio to 100% (x = 1), where Li+ ions completely replaced Zn ions. However, the particle size reached about 1.8 μm of the resulting Li2Ga2O4:Cr, and showed no photoluminescence (Fig. 2f). In conclusion, the PL intensity and lifetime of ZnGa2O4:Cr3+ could be effectively enhanced by appropriate Li + -doping. Mechanistic study of the effect of Li + -doping on the optical properties of ZGO:Cr PLNPs

The optical band gap (Eg) of LixZn1-xGa2O4:Cr3+ (x = 0–0.5) was estimated by combining Tauc plotting with Kubelka-Munk function transformation of the diffuse reflectance spectra (Fig. S2). The calculated Eg values were 4.12 eV (x = 0), 4.42 eV (x = 0.1), 4.12 eV (x = 0.2), 3.96 eV (x = 0.3), 3.91 eV (x = 0.4), and 4.27 eV (x = 0.5). After Li+ ion doping, the band gap of LixZn1-xGa2O4:Cr3+ (x = 0.3 and x = 0.4) became slightly narrower than that of the undoped sample (x = 0). It indicated that under UV excitation, electrons were more readily excited from the valence band to the conduction band. The electronic transition efficiency was thus enhanced to some extent, which was conducive to improving the excitation efficiency by visible light.

To investigate the luminescence mechanism, the thermoluminescence (TL) spectrum of the material was further studied (Wang et al., 2025b; Zheng et al., 2025). TL measurement is a universal tool for studying the properties and distribution of electron traps in phosphors. It should be noted that the TL glow curves exhibit relatively high noise, which is likely attributable to the limited sample mass available for measurement. This limitation does not affect the identification of the main trap peaks or the derived trap depth values, as the peak positions remain clearly distinguishable despite the noise level. As shown in Fig. 3a, the undoped ZGO:Cr PLNPs exhibited an original TL peak near 338.2 K. In contrast, Li+-doping significantly enhanced the intensity of the TL peak, confirming that the concentration of inherent electron traps in the material increased due to doping. Additionally, as the Li+-doping level increased, the position of the TL peak related to the intrinsic electron traps also underwent slight changes. In the LixZn1-xGa2O4:Cr3+ samples, the corresponding TL peak temperatures were 338.2 K (x = 0), 345.2 K (x = 0.1), 339.2 K (x = 0.2), 345.6 K (x = 0.3), 345.2 K (x = 0.4), and 351.4 K (x = 0.5). The concentration and depth of electron traps are key parameters for regulating PL performance, either too high or too low values of trap concentration or depth can lead to reduced PL intensity or shortened decay time (Gao et al., 2023). From the TL curve of LixZn1-xGa2O4:Cr3+ (x = 0.3), we observed two distinct thermoluminescence peaks located at 345.6 K and 410.4 K. The synthesis and characterization of PLNPs@GCS

For detection sensitivity, the initial PL signal intensity directly determines the signal-to-noise ratio, while a sufficiently long PL decay time ensures a wide time window for signal collection after excitation ceases, which is advantageous for reducing background interference in complex sample matrices. Although x = 0.5 exhibits the longest PL decay time, x = 0.3 was selected as the optimal ratio because it offers the strongest PL intensity while still providing adequate PL decay time for reliable signal collection during the detection process. Therefore, the Li+-doped ZGO:Cr PLNPs with x = 0.3 were chosen to construct the detection probe PLNPs@GCS. The Li+-doped ZGO:Cr PLNPs (x = 0.3) exhibiting the highest PL were selected to construct the detection probe PLNPs@GCS. TEM image revealed that the Li0.3Zn0.7Ga2O4:Cr3+ PLNPs exhibited a regular cubic morphology, and the average particle size was approximately 14 nm (Fig. 4a). After modification with GCS, the PLNPs@GCS composite displayed a clearly discernible core-shell structure. The uniform GCS shell thickness was approximately 2 nm, and the particle size of PLNPs@GCS was about 23 nm (Fig. S3). The results of elemental mapping analysis revealed that the characteristic elements of Zn, Ga, and O, which constitute the PLNPs core, were predominantly enriched in the core region. In contrast, the elements of N and C, characteristic components of GCS, were uniformly distributed throughout the entire material structure, including both the core region and the peripheral shell layer (Fig. 4b). The potential of Li0.3Zn0.7Ga2O4:Cr3+ PLNPs was −8.31 mV, and following modification with the shell of GCS through amide reaction, the PLNPs@GCS exhibited reversible electrostatic conversion behavior across different pH environments: a positive potential at pH = 5.5 and a negative potential at pH = 7.4 (Fig. 4c). The absorption peak corresponding to the amide bond (−CO−NH−) at 1636 cm−1, and C−H stretching vibration peaks at 2929 cm−1 and 2858 cm−1, as well as the GCS stretching vibration at 1091 cm−1, were further confirmed the successful modification of GCS on PLNPs (Fig. 4d). Fig. 4e showed the zeta potential variation of PLNPs@GCS under different pH conditions, demonstrating the pH-switchable capability of PLNPs@GCS probe. To evaluate the effect of GCS coating, the PL emission spectra and decay curves of PLNPs before and after coating were compared (Fig. S4). The results show no obvious change, confirming that the GCS coating does not affect the luminescence properties or the subsequent detection application. The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) curve in Fig. 4f indicated that mass loss below 200 °C primarily resulted from desorption of adsorbed water. The weight loss (4.5% loss) observed between 200 and 800 °C corresponded to the thermal decomposition of the GCS shell layer. Optimization of PS NPs determination

Using a fixed volume of 200 μL Fe3O4@Au-PSBP probe to detect PS NPs at a concentration of 400 pg mL−1, the effect of PLNPs@GCS probe amount, detection time, and separation time were analyzed. For the effect of PLNPs@GCS amount, the PL intensity enhanced and stabilized when the probe addition of 400 μL (Fig. 5a). Further increasing the amount of PLNPs@GCS did not result in any significant change in the PL signal. It indicated that PLNPs@GCS probe was sufficient to bind with Fe3O4@Au-PSBP at the addition volume of 400 μL. As shown in Fig. 5b, the PL signal reached the maximum at a separation time of 40 min and then 40 min was selected as the optimal separation time.The effect of detection time on the PL signal was shown in Fig. 5c. The PL intensity exhibited a trend of first increasing and then decreasing with detection time, and reached a peak at 60 min. A detection time of 60 min was selected to guarantee the complete reaction between Fe3O4@Au-PSBP-PS and PLNPs@GCS to form a composite through electrostatic interactions. Based on the optimal amount of PLNPs@GCS and detection time, the effect of separation time on the detection system was investigated. After Fe3O4@Au-PSBP captures PS (Fig. S5), the surface negative charge quantity increases (Fig. S6), which is conducive to its formation of a composite with positively charged PLNPs@GCS through electrostatic interaction. However, after the formation of the composite, the light shielding effect of the black Fe3O4@Au-PSBP will cause the fluorescence signal of PLNPs@GCS to be reduced (Fig. S7 and Fig. S8) (Zeng et al., 2016). The highly sensitive signal output comes from the released PLNPs@GCS. Therefore, a two-step separation procedure was designed to further enhance the detection sensitivity: unbound probe PLNPs@GCS was removed in the first step. Under optimal detection conditions, the PL intensity of the reaction system increased with the concentration of PS NPs from 50 pg mL−1 to 800 pg mL−1 (Fig. 5d). Fig. 5e showed the PS NPs concentration exhibited a linear relationship with ΔPL. The linear equation was Y = 147.5 + 1.029X (X: the concentration of PS NPs; Y: ΔPL induced by PS NPs), with R2 = 0.9172. The limit of detection (3s) was 8.11 pg mL−1. The selectivity of the developed method for PS NPs determination was tested in Fig. 5f. The developed method exhibited a specific response to PS NPs, while showing no significant response to other NPs, including polypropylene (PP), polyethylene (PE), polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), and polyvinyl chloride (PVC), suggesting that the developed dual-probe system method had high specificity in the quantitation of PS NPs. To further verify that the recognition was based on specific peptide capture rather than simple electrostatic adsorption, we additionally employed NH2-modified PS NPs (NH2−PS) as a control (Fig. S9a). The NH2−PS system also yielded a good linear relationship (100–800 pg mL−1) (Fig. S9b), confirming the specific binding nature of the peptide toward the PS surface. Given that unmodified PS NPs are more environmentally relevant, all subsequent experiments were carried out using pristine PS NPs.

Detection of PS NPs in samples

The developed method demonstrated spike recoveries for the target PS ranging from 91.25% to 110.53% across different matrices, including bottled purified water, saltwater (3.5%), artificial lake water and tea beverage (Table 2). High recovery results indicated that the method possessed good accuracy and reliability. Furthermore, the relative standard deviation (RSD) was all less than 10%, indicating good repeatability, which confirmed that the precision of this method met the analytical requirements. Detection of PS in spiked samples.

The current detection methods of PS NPs have limitations in terms of sensitivity, linear range or applicable scenarios (Table S4). In terms of detection sensitivity, the developed method in this work demonstrated a significant advantage, with a limit of detection (LOD) as low as 8.11 pg mL−1 and a linear range covering 50 pg mL−1–800 pg mL−1. This greatly surpassed other existing techniques, showing its exceptional sensitivity and broad quantitative capability. In comparison, although Py-GC/MS and the electroaffinity-SERS coupling technique also offered high sensitivity (with LODs of 0.08 ng mL−1 and 0.03 ng mL−1), their high operational complexity and costly instrumentation limited broader application. Thermal lens spectrometry and fluorescence-based methods showed moderate sensitivity, reaching 0.5 ng mL−1 and 20 ng mL−1, respectively. Though SERS provided a wide detection range (500 μg mL−1–1.0 μg mL−1), it suffered from the highest LOD (10 μg mL−1) among the listed methods, indicating insufficient sensitivity for trace-level analysis. In summary, the developed system method in the work not only achieved a breakthrough in sensitivity but also offered a wide linear response range, demonstrating strong potential for ultrasensitive NPs detection in complex environmental samples. It thus provided a powerful analytical tool for environmental monitoring and risk assessment.

Conclusions

The developed dual-probe system composed of Fe3O4@Au-PSBP and PLNPs@GCS demonstrated excellent analytical performance for the detection of PS NPs, showing high accuracy and reliability, as confirmed by satisfactory spike recoveries and low relative standard deviations in diverse solution systems. In comparison with existing techniques, the proposed method exhibited superior sensitivity, featuring an ultralow detection limit and a wide linear range. By combining the low-background persistent luminescence of PLNPs with an efficient two-step magnetic separation strategy that markedly minimizes matrix interference, this method effectively addresses key limitations of current PS NP detection approaches, including narrow working ranges and inadequate sensitivity. Therefore, the developed system represents a robust, sensitive, and practical analytical platform, particularly well suited for the trace-level quantification of PS NPs in environmental water and beverage matrices.
