Exploring Normal to Abnormal Variations of Grooming Behaviours in Cats
Authors: Mathilde Guillon, Léa Naudin, Noëlle Cochet‐Faivre, Emmanuelle Titeux, Caroline Gilbert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGrooming is a maintenance behaviour accounting for 4% of a cat's activity budget. It consists of several short bouts, and is modified by pruritus and some psychogenic diseases in cats.ObjectivesThis study aimed to describe cats' grooming behaviour, investigating normal variations in relation to hair length and grooming modifications in cats with psychogenic alopecia (fPA).Materials and MethodsWe analysed 118 videos of 29 healthy cats and seven fPA shorthair cats. We studied five activities for nine body parts performed by cats during grooming, the duration and number of grooming bouts, according to hair length and health status.ResultsMajor individual variations occurred between cats, with no differences regarding mean grooming bout duration according to hair length or health status. Most (95%) grooming bouts consisted in grooming two to seven body parts. Number of parts groomed by fPA cats was significantly lower than healthy shorthair cats; no differences were noted between healthy cats with different hair length. Hairless cats chewed their skin longer than other cats. fPA cats chewed their skin 10 times longer than healthy shorthair cats (p < 0.001). Licking and rubbing were shorter bouts (p = 0.021). Time spent grooming each zone did not differ with hair length. fPA cats groomed their internal hind limbs significantly longer (p = 0.016) and their neck shorter (p = 0.003) than healthy shorthair cats.Conclusions and Clinical RelevanceThis study allows a better understanding of cats' grooming behaviour, helping to identify abnormal grooming behaviours, and supports classification of psychogenic alopecia as an abnormal repetitive behaviour.Keywords: abnormal behaviour, domestic cat, grooming, hair length, psychogenic alopecia, welfare

Introduction

Grooming is a necessary maintenance behaviour for the health and well‐being of cats. It serves several purposes, such as cleaning the coat, and removing dirt and scales, shed hair and ectoparasites (e.g., fleas) [1]. Cats exhibit autogrooming and allogrooming behaviour, the latter being a social behaviour [2]. Here, we use the term ‘grooming’ to refer to ‘autogrooming’.

Grooming in cats represents 4% of their daily activity budget, thus being the second largest waking activity after foraging behaviour [1]. Eckstein and Hart were the first to detail grooming bouts, by observing cats isolated in small cages during a 24 h period [3]. A grooming bout can be divided in two distinct behaviours, oral grooming and scratch grooming [3, 4]. Oral grooming is divided between licking or chewing of the fur, and rubbing of the front paw on the face of the animal after licking it (after referred to as ‘rubbing’). This latter behaviour is classified as oral grooming, because the cat indirectly applies saliva on its face during this activity. These activities are interspersed with short pauses that authors consider as part of the grooming bout when they last < 1 min [3]. Furthermore, Sichet distinguished nine body parts, and showed that grooming bouts are organised in loops with cats performing back‐and‐forth grooming behaviours on their different body parts [5]. However, other studies have shown that each body part was not equally groomed, as most time grooming is spent on the head and least time on the tail [3, 4].

Neurologically, grooming behaviour involves areas of the brain (tectum and pons) and is influenced by serotonin concentration [6, 7]. Although authors consider grooming determinism as mostly central, it also is influenced by environmental factors. Cats lick themselves more during the hour following a bath, when infected with fleas or after wearing an Elizabethan collar [1, 3, 6]. Health problems such as chronic pain or chronic stress also are known to modify grooming behaviour [8, 9]. Psychogenic overgrooming takes different forms across species: acral lick dermatitis in dogs, feather picking in birds and trichotillomania in humans. In cats, different forms of psychogenic overgrooming are described, such as excessive licking, scratching, chewing and hair‐pulling [10]. Grooming could then represent as much as 50% of the time‐budget in cats with poor welfare, inducing psychogenic alopecia (fPA) or head and neck excorations [11, 12, 13]. These diseases are repetitive behaviours often described as stereotypies, which are usually linked with poor welfare [14, 15, 16].

However, in cats, alopecia can be of various origins, especially pruritic [16]. Some authors consider symptoms of psychogenic self‐induced alopecia to be a circumscribed alopecic lesion with clinically healthy skin, associated with a poor welfare score [17]. However in cats, self‐induced alopecia is most commonly caused by pruritic diseases, such as those resulting from allergies, and their clinical presentation can overlap that of fPA. Most authors consider the latter as a diagnosis of exclusion, once all organic causes have been investigated and excluded. However, other authors consider that fPA should not be a diagnosis of exclusion [16, 17]. A better understanding of fPA cats' grooming bouts could corroborate this hypothesis.

Only a few studies have explored the influence of cats' characteristics on grooming behaviour. Indeed, although a strong interindividual variation of grooming bout duration was described, variation between short‐ and longhair cats has been scarcely documented [4, 5]. To the best of our knowledge, no study has investigated hairless cats' grooming behaviour, although we hypothesise that hairless cats' grooming behaviours could vary compared to other cats, as they are known to present a modified hair shaft structure and increased Malassezia sp. carriage [18, 19]. Knowing how cats from different breeds groom themselves could help discriminating between normal and abnormal grooming behaviour. Indeed, although cats with psychogenic alopecia are known to overgroom, no study has yet compared the grooming modalities of healthy cats and cats with fPA [15].

The aim of this study was therefore to investigate the organisation of normal grooming bouts in healthy domestic cats, suggesting that it could vary with hair length. We expect no difference between shorthair and longhair cats, in accordance with previous studies. We expect differences between hairless and other cats, linked with their skin and fur characteristics. We also aimed to compare the grooming behaviours of healthy cats with those of cats suffering from psychogenic alopecia. We expect fPA cats to modify their grooming behaviour.

Material and Methods

Animals and Protocol

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria for Healthy Cats

A convenience sample was used to include cats, because no data were available to pre‐determine a sample calculation. Cats of any breed or sex, aged between 1 and 12 years and with a body condition score between 3/9 and 6/9 were eligible for enrolment. They had to be physically healthy and free from any dermatological or systemic disease that could interfere with grooming (such as obesity or osteoarthritis) [8, 20]. They had to have received continuous flea control with Lotilaner for ≥ 3 months. Cats that received any treatment, presented any condition or disease (or suspicion thereof), or that were not regularly treated against flea infestation were not included.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria for fPA Cats

Psychogenic alopecic cats were recruited after a diagnosis by both referral clinicians and veterinary behaviourists. First, referral clinicians suspected a psychogenic origin, in accordance with the following criteria. The suspected diagnosis was related to the cats' general examination (e.g., absence of parasites, medical history and previous dermatological treatments) and the localisation of lesions, their history and aspect (including cytological aspect). Alopecia had to have started on the abdomen, present a regular, square‐like shape, with no other skin lesion than alopecia. When any other lesion than alopecia (erythema, crusts and scales) was present, if alopecia was multicentric from the start, or if cats were diagnosed or suspected with any systemic disease, they were not included in the present study. Moreover, cats had to have been previously medicated with antipruritic drugs, and other dermatological treatments, with no improvement. Hence, after exclusion of causes other than psychogenic, cats were referred to veterinary behaviourists. The fPA diagnosis was then established, after careful analysis of cats' grooming behaviour (e.g., frequency, occurrences during the day and frustration triggers) and environment and emotional state assessment (through an impaired welfare score). Moreover, cats had to be physically healthy with no other disease (or suspicion thereof), and should have received continuous flea control with Lotilaner for at least 3 months.

Cats' Enrollment

This study was approved by an ethic committee (COMERC No.: 2020‐09‐25‐2). Healthy cats were recruited using social media, between May 2019 and March 2021. They were categorised as shorthair, longhair or hairless according to their breed or fur characteristics. fPA cats were referred by the dermatological department. Indeed, the cats were first examined by referral clinicicans, who excluded dermatological causes for alopecia and referred them to a behavioural medicine veterinary surgeon. A welfare score was then established [13]. All owners gave their written consent to participate in the study. The consent and recruitment forms mentioned descriptions of what could be classified as grooming behaviours (including pauses). We made sure that owners did not have any question about which behaviour they should record.

Welfare Score

Welfare scores for fPA cats were established using those proposed by Titeux et al. [13]. Welfare scores range from 0 (good welfare) to 21 (poor welfare), and evaluate the cats' ability to control their environment regarding food, water, hiding places and enrichment access, as well as the quality of human–cat relationship and the cats' perception of its environment. There is no clear cut‐off value to assess whether a cat has good or poor welfare; however, in the original publication, healthy cats' welfare scores ranged between 5 and 8 and cats with ulcerative dermatitis scores between 12 and 19. Treated cats scored between 4 and 10. We can hypothesise that a score < 10 was indicative of acceptable welfare.

Sampling of Videos

The cats' owners were asked to videotape their cats grooming in their usual environment with minimal disturbance. Grooming had to be interrupted spontaneously by the cat. In order to observe enough grooming bouts, we asked owners to provide at ≥ 10 min of footage. Several grooming bouts could be added and videos did not necessarily have to be taken on the same day.

The videos were analysed using BORIS (The Behavioral Observation Research Interactive Software) [21]. To analyse the grooming behaviour, grooming bouts were divided into five activities:

In order to analyse the groomed area, nine body parts were distinguished (Figure 1) [5]. The type of each activity and the time spent grooming each body part were noted.

Statistical Analyses

Data were analysed using the software sigmaplot v16 [22]. Owing to the small size of our samples, we chose to use nonparametric tests.

Cats' body weight was compared between groups of cats using Kruskal–Wallis followed by Dunn's post hoc tests. Cat's body weight and number of groomed body parts are reported in the text as median/interquartile range (IQR). Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to compare the duration of the grooming bouts between the three healthy groups, and individual variations within the four groups.

The duration of each activity on the nine body parts was calculated on a 10 min grooming bout duration basis, in order to perform comparisons. Friedman's tests were used to compare the duration of each of the five grooming activities and the time spent grooming each of the nine body parts within the four groups (healthy and fPA cats). If differences were significant, post hoc Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests with multiplicity correction were used. Comparisons of the duration of each of the five activities and of the time spent grooming each of the nine body parts between the three healthy groups were analysed using Kruskal–Wallis tests, with post hoc Dunn's tests.

Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney U‐tests were performed to compare durations of the five grooming activities and the number of groomed body parts, between shorthair and fPA cats. The number of body parts groomed was compared with a Student's t‐test (normality and variance passed, n = 62).

A Spearman test was used to assess the evolution of the number of groomed body parts (one to nine) and of the number of pauses in relation to the duration of the grooming bout of the four groups of cats.

Results

Demographics

Thirty healthy cats and nine fPA cats were recruited (19 male/20 female, 4.6 ± 3.2 years of age; 4.7 ± 1.5 kg weight). Most cats were neutered, except for seven of them (one shorthair male, one longhair female, one hairless female, three fPA males and one fPA female).

We recruited 11 shorthair cats, 10 longhair cats, nine hairless cats and nine fPA cats. Shorthair cats comprised 10 domestic short hair cats (DSH) and one British shorthair. Longhair cats were seven domestic long hair cats, one Persian cat, one Siberian and one Norwegian cross‐bred cat. One cat was excluded from analyses because of a too short duration of grooming recorded by the owner. Hairless cats were eight sphynx and one Doskoy. Owing to enrolment methods, healthy cats' welfare scores were not calculated. Eight fPA cats were DSH, one was a Chartreux. Because cats included had alopecia that started in the abdominal area, all fPA cats had alopecia on the abdomen (100%). Seven cats also had alopecia on the hind limbs (internal face) (78%), two also on the tail (22%) and one also on the forelimbs (internal face) (11%). fPA cats' welfare scores ranged from 10 to 16 (mean 12 ± 2; median 11.5). Two fPA cats presenting skin inflammation before consulting in the dermatology department were treated and cured before the video footage was taken. Two fPA cats were excluded from analyses: one because of the duration of grooming recorded by the owner was too short, and one because of an ongoing treatment with gabapentin. Finally, data from 11 shorthair cats (four males/seven females; 4.8 ± 3.2 years, 4.2 kg [median 3.9, IQR 5]), nine longhair cats (five males/four females, 3.6 ± 2.7 years, 5 kg [4.5, 5.35]), nine hairless cats (four males/five females, 2.3 ± 1.8 years, 3.5 kg [3.1, 4.0]) and seven fPA cats (four males/three females, 7.7 ± 2.6 years, 5.5 kg [4.3, 8.8]) were analysed.

Differences in body weight within groups were checked. Significant differences were found (H [3] = 15.2, p = 0.002): Hairless cats weighed less than fPA and longhair cats (medians shorthair 4 kg, longhair 5 kg, hairless 3.5 kg, fPA 5.5 kg; post hoc tests, hairless versus fPA cats p = 0.003, hairless versus longhair cats p = 0.009).

Videos

One‐hundred‐and‐eighteen videos were obtained (Table 1): 40 for shorthair, 27 for longhair, 29 for hairless and 22 for fPA cats.

Grooming Bout Durations According to Cat Hair Length for Healthy Cats

We obtained between one and six videos per cat. Videos lasted between 1.2 and 15.5 min. For each cat and each group, we calculated the total duration of grooming, and the mean duration and standard deviation (SD) of grooming bouts (Table 1).

The mean duration of grooming bouts did not vary with hair length. Mean duration was 4.5 ± 2.5 min for shorthair, 5.2 ± 2.1 min for longhair and 4.9 ± 3.4 min for hairless cats (Kruskal–Wallis: H [2] = 0.12; p = 0.945). Figure 2 shows the distribution of grooming durations for the different groups. The total mean duration of grooming bouts was 4.8 ± 2.7 min for healthy cats.

Box plots of grooming bouts duration for all groups. The box shows the middle 50% of the values (25th to 75th percentile). The line inside the box represents the median. The whiskers indicate the lowest and highest values.

Important individual variations were observed in the duration of grooming bouts. Some cats showed both short and long bouts, whereas for some other cats, bouts were of similar durations. However, no difference was found in the duration of individual grooming bouts, in shorthair (Kruskal–Wallis: H [10] = 15.57; p = 0.076), longhair (Kruskal–Wallis: H [8] = 12.84; p = 0.170) or hairless cats (Kruskal–Wallis: H [8] = 8.89; p = 0.448).

Comparison of Activities During Grooming Bouts According to Hair Length for Healthy Cats

The durations of each activity were compared within groups (Figure 3). For shorthair cats, duration of activities was significantly different (Friedman: χ2[4] = 35.13; p < 0.001): licking lasted longer than other activities except pausing, and scratching shorter than pausing and rubbing (post hoc tests: p < 0.05) (Data S1). For longhair cats, duration of activities was significantly different (Friedman: χ2[4] = 25.36; p < 0.001): licking lasted longer than scratching and pausing (post hoc tests: p < 0.05) (Data S1), yet did not differ from rubbing and chewing. For hairless cats, licking, chewing and pausing durations did not differ, yet licking lasted longer than scratching and rubbing (Friedman: χ2[4] = 27.15; p < 0.001; post hoc tests: p < 0.05) (Data S1).

(b) Chewing was similar for healthy longhair and hairless cats, yet differed between shorthair and hairless cats. (c) Chewing differed between healthy shorthair and fPA cats.

Only chewing differed significantly according to hair length (Kruskal–Wallis: H [2] = 11.71; p = 0.003). Hairless cats chewed themselves longer than shorthair cats (Dunn's test: p = 0.03) (Figure 4). Licking did not differ significantly among the three groups (Kruskal–Wallis: H [2] = 1.056; p = 0.590), as well as rubbing, scratching and pausing (Kruskal–Wallis: H [2] = 1.08; p = 0.583; H [2] = 0.942; p = 0.625; H [2] = 1.979; p = 0.372, respectively) (Figure 4).

Percentage of time spent on each body part in each group.

Time Spent Grooming the Nine Body Parts for Healthy Cats

Within each group, the proportion of time spent on each region (as described in Figure 1) was compared. These proportions differed for shorthair cats (Friedman: χ2[8] = 53.57; p < 0.001), longhair cats (Friedman: χ2[8] = 43.12; p < 0.001) and hairless cats (Friedman: χ2[8] = 32.33; p < 0.001). We measured the percentage of time spent grooming each body part within the different groups. We observed variations in the proportion of time spent on each body part (Figure 5).

Number of body parts groomed per grooming bout.

In shorthair cats, lips and lower back were groomed for the shortest duration (post hoc tests: significant differences with forelimbs, Thorax and flanks (TF), head and hind limbs for both lips and lower back; lips significantly with abdomen). Thorax and flanks, forelimbs, hind limbs and head were groomed for the longest duration (post hoc tests: significant only with lips and lower back). Abdomen, neck and anogenital/tail (AT) body parts were groomed for an intermediate duration (post hoc tests: significant only with lips and lower back; abdomen only with lips) (Data S1).

In longhair cats, lips and lower back were groomed for the shortest duration (post hoc tests: significant differences with forelimbs and hind limbs). Forelimbs, hind limbs and head were groomed for the longest duration (post hoc tests: no differences except above). TF, neck, AT and abdomen were groomed for an intermediate duration (no post hoc differences) (Data S1).

In hairless cats, time spent grooming the lips was significantly shorter than TF, forelimbs and hind limbs (post hoc tests: p < 0.05). Time spent grooming TF, hind limbs, forelimbs and abdomen was longer than any other body part, albeit not significantly (post hoc tests: p > 0.05 except above). AT, neck, head and lower back were groomed for an intermediate duration (post hoc tests: p > 0.05) (Data S1).

Although we observed differences between groups, time spent grooming each zone did not differ with hair length, probably owing to important individual variations (lips: H [2] = 0.21; p = 0.90, neck: H [2] = 2.79; p = 0.25, head: H [2] = 1.31; p = 0.52, forelimbs: H [2] = 3.66; p = 0.16, TF: H [2] = 4.67; p = 0.10, abdomen: H [2] = 3.06; p = 0.22, hind limbs: H [2] = 2.70; p = 0.26, lower back: H [2] = 2.33; p = 0.31, AT: H [2] = 2.63; p = 0.27).

Structuration of Grooming Bouts for Healthy Cats

The number of body parts groomed per bouts was calculated (Figure 5). Cats groomed multiple body parts for almost all grooming bouts: most (95%) grooming bouts consisted in grooming two to seven body parts (median 5 [3, 6]) (with no significant differences, H [2] = 0.56; p = 0.75). Only 2% of all bouts were focusing on one body part only, and 3% on eight or nine body parts. The number of visited body parts increased with the duration of bouts for shorthair (p < 0.00001; r = 0.670), longhair (p = 0.013; r = 0.471) and hairless cats (p = 0.0007; r = 0.591).

Grooming Behaviour of fPA Cats

Within fPA cats, no individual significant differences were found for grooming durations (Kruskal–Wallis: H [6] = 4.05; p = 0.671) (Table 1).

Significant differences were shown between the five activities of fPA cats (Friedman: χ2[4] = 23.77; p < 0.001) (Figure 3). Licking and chewing lasted significantly longer than rubbing and scratching (post hoc tests: p < 0.05) (Data S1). No other differences were found between other activities.

Grooming durations were different between the nine body parts (Friedman: χ2[8] = 29.59; p < 0.001). Lips and neck were groomed for the shortest duration (post hoc tests: p < 0.05, significantly different from hind limbs for both, lips significantly different from TF). Hind limbs, TF and forelimbs were groomed for the longest duration (not significant except above, post hoc tests: p > 0.05). Abdomen, head, AT and lower back were groomed for an intermediate duration (post hoc tests p > 0.05) (Data S1).

In a similar way to healthy cats, the number of visited body parts increased with the duration of bouts (p < 0.00001; r = 0.793).

Comparison of Grooming Between fPA Cats and Healthy Shorthair Cats

Because fPA cats were all shorthair, we compared grooming between healthy shorthair and fPA cats. Grooming bout durations did not differ between healthy (4.5 ± 2.5 min) and fPA cats (4.7 ± 2.6 min) (U = 27; p = 0.711) (Table 1).

When comparing activities, no difference was found for scratching (U = 26; p = 0.243) and pausing durations (U = 29; p = 0.390). However, fPA cats chewed their fur significantly longer (approximately 10‐fold longer) than shorthair cats (U = 1; p < 0.001). However, licking and rubbing lasted less time: approximately five‐fold less for rubbing (U = 15; p = 0.033) and 1.5‐fold less for licking (U = 13; p = 0.021).

When comparing body parts, fPA cats groomed their internal hind limbs significantly longer (U = 12, p = 0.016) than their neck (U = 6, p = 0.003). Grooming durations for the other body parts were not significantly different (lips, head, forelimbs, TF abdomen, lower back and AT; p > 0.05).

For fPA cats, 7% of bouts consisted in grooming one body part, and none consisted in grooming eight to nine parts. Grooming bouts focusing on two to seven parts were 93%. The average number of parts groomed by fPA cats was 3.8 ± 1.8, significantly lower than that for shorthair cats (5.0 ± 1.5; t = −2.65; p = 0.01).

Discussion

This study investigated and compared grooming behaviours of cats of different hair length and fPA cats, generally considered as cats with poor welfare [15, 16, 17]. To investigate this specific point, we used a welfare score based on a previous study which found out that poor welfare could be characterised by a welfare score of approximately 16 of 21 [13]. Another study showed that fPA cats had a median welfare score of 14 with a healthy skin [16]. In our study, psychogenic alopecic cats had a median score of 11.5, lower than that found previously. This could be a consequence of the way welfare scores were calculated, because this scoring system could require training before being used. Knowing how overgrooming is performed by cats suffering from different affections could be particularly relevant for veterinary surgeons as a diagnostic tool. Moreover, recognising normal and abnormal grooming is essential for pruritus assessment, which is key for diagnosis and follow‐up in dermatological veterinary practice [12].

Our study focused on studying grooming behaviour in healthy cats with different hair length, and overgrooming behaviour of fPA cats. Our results, in accordance with previous studies, show that grooming is organised in short bouts divided in several activities focusing on several body parts [1, 3, 4, 5]. In a previous study, 91% of bouts focused on several body parts and 9% on one body part [1]. In our study, 95% bouts of healthy cats focused on two to seven body parts, and only 2% were directed to one single region. This difference could be explained by study design: In this previous study, cats were filmed continuously. In our study, owners were asked to record videos of their cats grooming, which may have led them to miss short bouts.

Grooming bout duration did not vary with hair length, with a mean duration of 4.8 ± 2.7 min. However, we observed variations in the repetition of the four different activities during grooming bouts. Indeed, hairless cats spent more time licking (57.3% of total grooming bouts duration) and chewing their skin (21.6%). In shorthair and longhair cats, the only activity that was performed longer than the others was licking (respectively 66.3% and 61.3% versus 5.8% and 13.4% for chewing). A previous study also found no difference in grooming patterns of shorthair and longhair cats [4]. To our knowledge, this is the first description of hairless cats' grooming pattern.

We also compared grooming bouts of cats suffering from fPA with those of healthy cats. Shorthair cats groomed their hindlimbs and abdomen for a longer time than other regions (respectively 13.8% and 11.3% of the grooming bouts), in accordance with a previous study [3]. This finding could explain why fPA is often directed to these body parts: because shorthair cats already spend a lot of time grooming their abdomen and hind limbs, these areas could be more subjected to repetitive grooming [23]. In our study, fPA cats had alopecia on not only their abdomen (100% of fPA cats following our inclusion criteria), but also their hind limbs (78%). Likewise, in another study, fPA was often directed to the abdomen (75% of cats) and on at least one limb (50%) [16].

We found that fPA cats' grooming bouts did not last longer than healthy shorthair cats' (4.7 ± 2.6 versus 4.5 ± 2.5 min). In particular, fPA cats did not groom their abdomen longer than healthy cats. We hypothesise that alopecia could be a consequence of two differences in grooming bout structure. First, licking the fur accounted for a longer time in healthy cats than in fPA cats (respectively 66.3% and 42.8% of the grooming bouts). We observed the opposite for chewing (42.1% of fPA cats' grooming bouts versus 5.8% for healthy cats). We hypothesise that fPA cats pull their hairs with their teeth more than they lick it, leading to alopecia. This phenomenon could be similar to human trichotillomania, a disorder in which a person pulls their hair, possibly seeking a soothing effect [24]. Because the total grooming time per day was impossible to determine, these cats also could groom for a longer time daily, leading to alopecia. Secondly, a further difference between healthy and fPA cats lies in the number of body parts groomed per grooming bouts. The average number of parts groomed were 5 ± 1.5 for healthy versus 3.8 ± 1.8 for fPA cats. Moreover, the number of grooming bouts directed to only one region differed (2% for healthy versus 7% for fPA cats). Our observations are in favour of a classification of psychogenic alopecia as a repetitive behaviour, consisting in repetitively chewing a few body parts when grooming. A previous study investigated the welfare of cats suffering from head and neck excoriations, also qualifying this disease as a repetitive behaviour [13]. Cats with such lesions were not included in this study because this work focused only on psychogenic alopecia.

Our findings also helped us to understand grooming determinism. Grooming bouts often focused on several areas and hair length had only a small influence on grooming, which is supports a central determinism. However, the differences observed in fur and skin chewing behaviours between groups according to hair length show a peripheral influence (i.e., grooming happening in response to a stimulus independent of the brain). Some authors argued that grooming was mostly centrally controlled, although a peripheral influence also is observed, which is consistent with our results [1, 3, 6, 7].

Some limitations to our study have to be taken into account, however. As previously stated, owners may have chosen only some grooming bouts and missed others (short ones for instance). In particular, grooming bouts were only recorded during owner's free time (in the morning, the evening or during weekends) and not at night. Grooming bouts also were recorded indoors, even when cats had outdoor access. Our study can thus only present qualitative data. We do not present here any information about the total time spent grooming in a 24 h period, which could vary between healthy and fPA cats.

Conclusions

The aim of our study was to describe cats' grooming behaviour, and to understand the influence of hair length and fPA. We found differences in the organisation of grooming according to hair length. We also observed differences between healthy cats and fPA cats. These findings provide a better understanding of the mechanisms underlying fPA. Multidisciplinary approaches could lead to better understanding and treatment of psychogenic alopecic cats in the future.
