Exclusion of Infinite Spin for N-body problem in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d}
Xiang Yu, Lei Zhao

Abstract
We show that there is no infinite spin at total collisions for ( − κ ) (-\kappa) -homogeneous N-body problem in higher dimensional Euclidean space ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , in which 0 < κ < 2 0<\kappa<2 ( κ = 1 \kappa=1 the Newtonian case), provided the limiting normalized central configuration is isolated and is of dimension d d or d − 1 d-1 . In the Newtonian case κ = 1 \kappa=1 , this extends the work of [ 5 ] to d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 and in the d = 3 d=3 case offers a different approach as compared to the current preprint [ 10 ] .



1
Introduction

In the study of the Newtonian N-body problem, it is now well-known that when a total collision happens, the configurations formed by the particles after proper normalization tend to the set of central configurations. The rotational symmetry of the problem makes it
a priori
possible for a total collision orbit to rotate infinitely many times before ending up colliding each other.

When the limiting central configuration is non-degenerate, this is shown by Chazy
[
1
]
and Saari
[
6
]
to be impossible. In the planar N-body problem, this problem was recently studied by
[
9
]
and by Moeckel-Mongomery
[
5
]
who showed that infinite spin does not happen if the limiting central configuration is isolated. Several extensions has been obtained
[
3
,
7
]
.
Very recently, this is extended to the spatial N-body problem by Pinzari-Zgliczynski
[
10
]
by means of constructing canonical variables in the Hamiltonian formalism to perform reduction by the
SO(3)
-symmetry of the problem.

In this article, we extend the approach of Moeckel-Mongomery
[
5
]
to deal with the non-abelian rotational symmetry
SO(d)
-symmetry of N-body problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Two steps need to be carried out, namely reducing the symmetry of the problem to get a reduced equations of motion, and use these reduced equations of motion together with a Lojasiewicz-type argument established in
[
5
]
to conclude that along a total collision orbit the projection to the rotational part of the system is finite. In contrast to
[
10
]
, we perform non-abelian Lagrange-Routh reduction at
0
- angular momentum level.

Remind that central configurations form dilation-invariant, rotation-invariant families. A central configuration is called normalized if the moment of inertia with respect to the center of mass is
1
. A central configuration is called reduced if it represents an equivalent class of central configurations obtained by rotating a fixed central configuration. To better illustrate the universality of the method of
[
5
]
we state our results for N-body problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
(-\kappa)
-homogeneous potential for
0<\kappa<2
.

Theorem 1.1

In the
(-\kappa)
-homogeneous
N
-body system in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
0<\kappa<2
), along any solution leading to a total collision, if the corresponding limiting reduced normalized central configuration
s_{0}
is of dimension
d
or
d-1
, and is isolated, then there is no infinite spin: Without reducing the rotational symmetry, the normalized configuration tends to a fixed limiting normalized central configuration.

When
d=3,\kappa=1
, all collinear central configurations are non-degenerate and therefore it follows from the works of Chazy
[
1
]
and Saari
[
6
]
that also no infinite spin happens. This gives a complete result in
d=3
.

We organize the article as follows: In Section
2
we explain our general settings of the problem as well as the separation of rotation part and dilation part from the corresponding reduced normalized configuration. In Section
3
we show that the reduced equations of motion can be analyzed as in
[
5
]
, which lead to a simple proof of Theorem
1.1
.

2
General Setting

2.1
The problem of N-particles and reduction by center of mass

We consider the
N
-body problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d},d\geq 1
, in which the particles carry positive masses
m_{1},\cdots,m_{N}
, in the center of mass frame. The configuration space

\mathcal{Q}:=\{q=(q_{1},\cdots,q_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\sum m_{i}q_{i}=0,q_{i}\neq q_{j},\forall i\neq j\}.

is equipped with the mass-weighted product

\langle q,\tilde{q}\rangle=\sum m_{i}q_{i}\cdot\tilde{q}_{i}

in which
\cdot
denotes the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

For
q\in\mathcal{Q}
we set its length as
r=\sqrt{\langle q,q\rangle}=:\|q\|\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and its corresponding normalized configuration as

\hat{q}\in\mathcal{N}:=\{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{Q};\|\hat{q}\|=1\}.

This
N
-body system is a Lagrangian system, with the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}=\dfrac{1}{2}\langle\dot{q},\dot{q}\rangle+U(q).

The
-\kappa
-homogeneous force function is assumed to take the form

U(q)=\sum\dfrac{m_{i}m_{j}}{\|q_{i}-q_{j}\|^{\kappa}}.

In particular

U(q)=U(\hat{q})\,r^{-\kappa}.

The group
SO(d)
acts diagonally on
\mathcal{Q}
(and on
\mathcal{N}
) by rotating each particle around their center of mass. When
d\geq 3
, this group is non-abelian. The corresponding conserved quantities are the components of the angular momentum bivector

C:=\sum m_{i}q_{i}\wedge\dot{q}_{i}.

The symmetry reduction procedure is more complicated when
d\geq 3
as in the case
d=1
(trivial) and
d=2
(abelian).

2.2
Coordinates separating dilation, rotation and absolute shape

We write
\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{e}_{d}\}
for a positive orthonomal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

For a non-zero vectors
\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a vector subspace
\pi\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
we denote by

OC(\tilde{x},\pi)

the orthogonal component of
\tilde{x}
with respect to
\pi
. If
\{\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{p}\}
is a positive orthonormal basis of
\pi
, then

OC(\tilde{x},\pi)=\tilde{x}-\sum_{i=1}^{p}(\tilde{x}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{i})\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{i}.

We assume

\hbox{span}\{q_{1},\cdots,q_{N}\}\geq d-1,

so up to permutation of subindices we may assume

\hbox{span}\{q_{N-(d-2)},\cdots q_{N}\}=d-1.

Under this assumption, there exists a unique rotation
R\in SO(d)
such that

•

R^{-1}q_{N}\in R_{+}\,\mathbf{e}_{1}
;

•

R^{-1}\,OC(q_{N-1},\hbox{span}\,q_{N})\in R_{+}\,\mathbf{e}_{2}
;

•

R^{-1}\,OC(q_{N-2},\hbox{span}\,\{q_{N-1},q_{N}\})\in R_{+}\,\mathbf{e}_{3}
;

\cdots

•

R^{-1}\,OC(q_{N-(d-2)},\hbox{span}\,\{q_{N-(d-1)},q_{N}\})\in R_{+}\,\mathbf{e}_{d-1}
;

This way we write

q=rRs,

in which
s\in\mathcal{Q}
is such that

\|s\|=1,

and

•

s_{N}=\hat{b}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}
;

•

OC(s_{N-1},\mathbf{e}_{1})\in\hat{b}_{2}\,\mathbf{e}_{2}
;

•

OC(s_{N-2},\hbox{span}\,\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}\})\in\hat{b}_{3}\,\mathbf{e}_{3}
;

\cdots

•

OC(s_{N-(d-2)},\hbox{span}\,\{q_{N-(d-1)},q_{N}\})\in\hat{b}_{d-1}\,\mathbf{e}_{d-1}
;

with
\hat{b}_{d-1},\cdots\hat{b}_{1}>0
.

The velocity is then computed as

\dot{q}=\dot{r}Rs+r\dot{R}s+rR\dot{s}=R\left(\dot{r}s+rR^{-1}\dot{R}s+r\dot{s}\right).

or particle-wise

\dot{q}_{i}=\dot{r}Rs_{i}+r\dot{R}s_{i}+rR\dot{s}_{i}=R\left(\dot{r}s_{i}+rR^{-1}\dot{R}s_{i}+r\dot{s}_{i}\right).

The Lagrangian is

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle\dot{q},\dot{q}\rangle+U(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\dot{r}^{2}+\frac{r^{2}}{2}\langle R^{-1}\dot{R}s+\dot{s},R^{-1}\dot{R}s+\dot{s}\rangle+\frac{U(s)}{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\dot{r}^{2}+\frac{r^{2}}{2}\left[\langle R^{-1}\dot{R}s,R^{-1}\dot{R}s\rangle+\langle\dot{s},\dot{s}\rangle+2\langle R^{-1}\dot{R}s,\dot{s}\rangle\right]+\frac{U(s)}{r^{\kappa}}.

For bivectors we have some useful expressions:

a\wedge a=0,\qquad Ra\wedge Rb=R\,a\wedge b\,R^{-1}.

in which
R^{-1}=R^{*}
is the adjoint of
R
. If we express
a\wedge b
using matrix then
R^{*}
is correspondingly represented by
R^{T}
.

The angular momentum bivector is then written in these coordinates as

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\sum m_{i}q_{i}\wedge\dot{q}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum m_{i}(rRs_{i})\wedge R(\dot{r}s_{i}+rR^{-1}\dot{R}s_{i}+r\dot{s}_{i})

\displaystyle=r^{2}\sum m_{i}R\,(s_{i}\wedge R^{-1}\dot{R}s_{i}+s_{i}\wedge\dot{s}_{i})\,R^{-1}.

The main problem we want to study here is the dynamics of a N-body system approaching a total collapse. We address the problem in the following setting:

•

d\geq 3
;

•

the force function of the mutual attraction of two particles in the initial system takes the form
U_{i,j}=m_{i}m_{j}\|q_{i}-q_{j}\|^{-\kappa}
(
-\kappa-
homogeneous potential), and we suppose
0<\kappa<2
.

In this case,
[
2
, Théorème Fondamental]
asserts that a total collapse is possible only if

(a) the angular momentum bivector
C
is zero, which is given by the relationship

\sum m_{i}(s_{i}\wedge R^{-1}\dot{R}s_{i}+s_{i}\wedge\dot{s}_{i})=0,

(1)

and
(b) the limiting normalized configuration
s
tends to a connected component of the set of (normalized) central configurations.

A normalized central configuration is called isolated if it is isolated after the rotational symmetry on
\mathcal{N}
is reduced. The dimension of a configuration in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the dimension of the vector subspace generated by this configuration. We shall work with the following assumption to have a unique limiting normalized configuration up to rotations:

Assumption 2.1

The limiting central configuration is isolated, and is of dimension
d
or
d-1
.

2.3
Non-abelian Lagrange-Routh Reduction at zero-moment level

To proceed, we need to compute the Lagrange-Routh reduced system. This procedure has been carried out in
[
4
]
which we briefly sketch and apply to our particular case.

Our symmetric group is the Lie group
G=SO(d)
, which is compact, with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=so(d)
, which can be identified with its dual
so(d)\cong so(d)^{*}
by means of a
SO(d)
-invariant metric (say the negative Killing form). An element
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}=so(d)
can then be represented by a
d\times d
anti-symmetric matrix

(\xi_{ij})_{d\times d},\xi_{ij}=-\xi_{ji},

or equivalently by a bivector

\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{2}\xi_{{ji}}e_{i}\wedge e_{j}.

For
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
, its associated fundamental vector field is denoted by
\underline{\xi}
. So
\underline{\xi}(q)
is a vector at each
q\in\mathcal{Q}
. We write
\exp(t\xi)
for the corresponding 1-parameter subgroup of
G
, which acts particle-wise as

\exp(t\xi)\cdot q_{i}=\exp(t\xi)q_{i}

and thus the action of
\mathfrak{g}
is given particle-wise as

\xi\cdot q_{i}=\xi q_{i},

in which the right-hand side is given by matrix multiplication.

By abuse of notation we have

\xi q_{i}=q_{i}\lrcorner\xi,

in which on the left-hand side
\xi
represents an anti-symmetric matrix, and on the right-hand side
\xi
represents the associated bivector.

We may as well write the particle-wise action as

\xi\cdot q_{i}=q_{i}\lrcorner\xi.

So we have

\underline{\xi}(q)=(\xi q_{1},\cdots,\xi q_{N})=(q_{1}\lrcorner\xi,\cdots,q_{N}\lrcorner\xi)\in T_{q}\mathcal{Q}.

The moment map
\mu:TQ\mapsto\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is define by the relationship

\mu(v_{q})\cdot\xi=\langle v_{q},\underline{\xi}(q)\rangle.

In our case we may write
v_{q}=(q,v)
, so we obtain

\mu(v_{q})\cdot\xi=\sum_{i}m_{i}\langle v_{i},\xi q_{i}\rangle.

Using the identity

\langle v_{i},\underline{\xi}(q_{i})\rangle=\langle v_{i},q_{i}\lrcorner\xi\rangle=\langle q_{i}\wedge v_{i},\xi\rangle

and natural identification of
\mathfrak{g}
with
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
we get the angular momentum

\mu(v_{q})=\sum m_{i}q_{i}\wedge v_{i}=C.

A mechanical connection
\alpha:T\mathcal{Q}\mapsto\mathfrak{g}
is an equivariant
\mathfrak{g}
-valued 1-form such that
\alpha(\underline{\xi}(q))=\xi
, and the horizontal distribution
\ker\alpha
is orthogonal to the vertical distribution.

Fix
c\in\mathfrak{g}\cong\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. Then the corresponding reduced Routhian is given, as in
[
4
, III.B]
by

R^{c}(v_{q})=L(v_{q})-\langle c,\alpha(v_{q})\rangle,

in which
\mu(v_{q})=c
.

We are interested only in the case
c=0
, so the reduced Routhian is simply

R^{0}(v_{q})=\dfrac{1}{2}\|v_{q}\|^{2}-\mathcal{V}(q).

To write down the reduced equations of motion, we need in principle to study the contribution of the curvature of
\alpha
, defined as the
\mathfrak{g}
-valued 2-form
\beta
such that

\beta(u_{q},v_{q})=d\alpha(Hor(u_{q}),Hor(v_{q})).

This has been extensively discussed in
[
4
, III. F, III. H]
. In the case that interests us, we only need to observe that when
c=0
, the curvature 2-form brings no contribution to the reduced equations of motion.
Part of the reduced equations of motion as given in
[
4
]
, namely (III. 37) is trivial. The only non-trivial equations, (III. 37) of
[
4
]
then take the form:

\begin{cases}\dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac{\partial R^{0}}{\partial\dot{r}}-\dfrac{\partial R^{0}}{\partial r}=0\\
\dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac{\partial R^{0}}{\partial\dot{s}}-\dfrac{\partial R^{0}}{\partial s}=0.\end{cases}

2.4
Precise computations of the reduced equations of motion

We now carry out computations in our problem more precisely.

We have set

q=rRs,

thus

\dot{q}=\dot{r}Rs+r\dot{R}s+rR\dot{s}=R\left(\dot{r}s+rR^{-1}\dot{R}s+r\dot{s}\right),

and thus the Lagrangian takes the form:

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle\dot{q},\dot{q}\rangle+U(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\dot{r}^{2}+\frac{r^{2}}{2}\langle R^{-1}\dot{R}s+\dot{s},R^{-1}\dot{R}s+\dot{s}\rangle+\frac{U(s)}{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\dot{r}^{2}+\frac{r^{2}}{2}\left[\langle R^{-1}\dot{R}s,R^{-1}\dot{R}s\rangle+\langle\dot{s},\dot{s}\rangle+2\langle R^{-1}\dot{R}s,\dot{s}\rangle\right]+\frac{U(s)}{r}.

To compute
R^{0}
, we determine the term
R^{-1}\dot{R}
directly from the zero-angular momentum condition (
1
):

\sum m_{i}(s_{i}\wedge R^{-1}\dot{R}s_{i}+s_{i}\wedge\dot{s}_{i})=0.

This can be rewritten into the form

\mathcal{I}(s)\cdot R^{-1}\dot{R}+G(s,\dot{s})=0,

in which

\mathcal{I}(s)\cdot R^{-1}\dot{R}=\sum m_{i}s_{i}\wedge R^{-1}\dot{R}s_{i},\qquad G(s,\dot{s})=\sum m_{i}s_{i}\wedge\dot{s}_{i}.

As
R^{-1}\dot{R}
is anti-symmetric, it corresponds to a bivector

\Gamma=\frac{1}{2}(R^{-1}\dot{R})_{{ji}}e_{i}\wedge e_{j}.

The inner product of a bivector is the natural one induced from the inner of vectors, namely

\langle v_{1}\wedge v_{2},v_{3}\wedge v_{4}\rangle=\langle v_{1},v_{3}\rangle\langle v_{2},v_{4}\rangle-\langle v_{1},v_{4}\rangle\langle v_{2},v_{3}\rangle.

We denote the induced norm for a bivector (and thus also for an antisymmetric matrix on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) still by
\|\cdot\|
.

The product
R^{-1}\dot{R}s_{i}
can be written in the equivalent form using contraction of a vector with a bivector as

R^{-1}\dot{R}s_{i}=s_{i}\lrcorner\Gamma.

So the moment of inertia tensor
\mathcal{I}(s)
can be equivalently written as

\mathcal{I}(s)\Gamma=\sum m_{i}s_{i}\wedge(s_{i}\lrcorner\Gamma)=\sum m_{i}\Bigl(\|s\|^{2}\Gamma-s_{i}\lrcorner(s_{i}\wedge\Gamma)\Bigr).

Lemma 2.2

\mathcal{I}(s)
is positive semi-definite. It is positive-definite when

\hbox{span}\{s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{N}\}\geq d-1.

Proof.
We compute the quadratic form

\langle\Gamma,\mathcal{I}(s)\Gamma\rangle=\langle\Gamma,\sum m_{i}s_{i}\wedge(s_{i}\lrcorner\Gamma)\rangle=\sum m_{i}\langle\Gamma,s_{i}\wedge(s_{i}\lrcorner\Gamma)\rangle=\sum m_{i}\langle s_{i}\lrcorner\Gamma,s_{i}\lrcorner\Gamma\rangle\geq 0.

In the last step we have used the identity to transpose “
s_{i}\wedge
” to “
s_{i}\lrcorner
”.

The degeneracy can happen only if all the
s_{i}
lie in the annihilator space of a non-zero bivector
\Gamma
. This is not possible if

\hbox{span}\{s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{N}\}\geq d-1.

Indeed the annihilator space of a non-zero bivector has dimension at most
d-2
.

\square

We therefore make the following assumption:

Assumption 2.3

The limiting normalized central configuration is isolated and is not contained in any vector-subspace of
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}
of dimension
d-2
.

With this assumption we have
\mathcal{I}(s)>0
in a neighborhood of such a limiting normalized central configuration. If we do not distinguish an antisymmetric matrix with its corresponding 2-vector, we may write

R^{-1}\dot{R}=-\mathcal{I}(s)^{-1}G(s,\dot{s}).

(2)

Thus

\displaystyle R^{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\dot{r}^{2}+\frac{r^{2}}{2}\left[\langle R^{-1}\dot{R}s,R^{-1}\dot{R}s\rangle+\langle\dot{s},\dot{s}\rangle+2\langle R^{-1}\dot{R}s,\dot{s}\rangle\right]+\frac{U(s)}{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\dot{r}^{2}+\frac{r^{2}}{2}\|R^{-1}\dot{R}s+\dot{s}\|^{2}+\frac{U(s)}{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\dot{r}^{2}+\frac{r^{2}}{2}\|\mathcal{I}(s)^{-1}G(s,\dot{s})s-\dot{s}\|^{2}+\frac{U(s)}{r}.

We see that the term

F(s,\dot{s}):=\|\mathcal{I}(s)^{-1}G(s,\dot{s})s-\dot{s}\|^{2}=\dot{s}^{T}A(s)\dot{s}

is a quadratic form on
\dot{s}
. We show

Lemma 2.4

The quadratic form
F(s,\dot{s})
is positive-definite.

Proof.

This quadratic form is clearly non-negative. It is 0 only if

\mathcal{I}(s)^{-1}G(s,\dot{s})s-\dot{s}=0.

Let
\Omega=\mathcal{I}(s)^{-1}G(s,\dot{s})(=-R^{-1}\dot{R})
, seen as an antisymmetric matrix. We have

\Omega\,s_{i}-\dot{s}_{i}=0,\qquad\forall i=1,\cdots,N.

We have set

s_{N}=\hat{b}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1},\quad s_{N-1}=\hat{c}_{21}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\hat{b}_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2},\cdots,s_{N-d+1}=\hat{c}_{d-1,1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+\hat{b}_{d-1}\mathbf{e}_{d-1},

within which
\hat{b}_{1}>0,\cdots,\hat{b}_{d=1}>0
.

From the identity on
s_{N}
:

\Omega\,s_{N}-\dot{s}_{N}=0,

we get

\hat{b}_{1}\Omega\mathbf{e}_{1}-\dot{\hat{b}}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}=0.

Since
\Omega
is antisymmetric, with the Euclidean inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have
\langle\mathbf{e}_{1},\Omega\mathbf{e}_{1}\rangle=0
. As
\hat{b}_{1}>0
we obtain

\Omega\mathbf{e}_{1}=0,\dot{\hat{b}}_{1}=0.

Next we consider the identity on
s_{N-1}
:

\Omega\,s_{N-1}-\dot{s}_{N-1}=0,

which gives

\hat{c}_{21}\Omega\mathbf{e}_{1}+\hat{b}_{2}\Omega\mathbf{e}_{2}=\dot{\hat{c}}_{21}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\dot{\hat{b}}_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}.

From the discussion above,
\Omega\mathbf{e}_{1}=0
, so we have

\hat{b}_{2}\Omega\mathbf{e}_{2}=\dot{\hat{c}}_{21}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\dot{\hat{b}}_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}.

Since
\mathbf{e}_{2}
is orthogonal to both
\mathbf{e}_{1}
and
\Omega\mathbf{e}_{2}
, we get

\dot{\hat{b}}_{2}=0,

thus

\Omega\mathbf{e}_{2}=k_{21}\mathbf{e}_{1}

for some
k_{21}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Consider the identity on
s_{N-2}
:

\Omega\,s_{N-1}-\dot{s}_{N-1}=0.

We get

\hat{c}_{31}\Omega\mathbf{e}_{1}+\hat{c}_{32}\Omega\mathbf{e}_{2}+\hat{b}_{3}\Omega\mathbf{e}_{3}=\dot{\hat{c}}_{31}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\dot{\hat{c}}_{32}\mathbf{e}_{2}+\dot{\hat{b}}_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}.

From the discussion above we have
\Omega\mathbf{e}_{1}=0,\Omega\mathbf{e}_{2}=k_{21}\mathbf{e}_{1}
, so we obtain

\hat{c}_{32}k_{21}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\hat{b}_{3}\Omega\mathbf{e}_{3}=\dot{\hat{c}}_{31}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\dot{\hat{c}}_{32}\mathbf{e}_{2}+\dot{\hat{b}}_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}.

As
\mathbf{e}_{3}
is orthogonal to
\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2},\Omega\mathbf{e}_{3}
, we obtain

\dot{\hat{b}}_{3}=0

and thus

\Omega\mathbf{e}_{3}=k_{31}\mathbf{e}_{1}+k_{32}\mathbf{e}_{2}.

Continuing in this way, we see that in the basis
\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{e}_{d}\}
the antisymmetric matrix
\Omega
has a zero (upper or lower) triangular part. Thus
\Omega
is the zero matrix. This means

G(s,\dot{s})
is zero, thus
\dot{s}=0
.

\square

This way we have defined a norm on tangent vectors
\dot{s}
at base point
s
by

\|\dot{s}\|_{FS}:=\sqrt{F(s,\dot{s})}.

The subscript “FS” is adopted from
[
5
]
, in which the norm corresponds to the Fubini-Study metric on
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}(N-2)
.

We now write the reduced Routhian as

R^{0}=\frac{1}{2}\dot{r}^{2}+\frac{r^{2}}{2}F(s,\dot{s})+\frac{U(s)}{r^{\kappa}}.

The reduced equations of motion

\begin{cases}\dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac{\partial R^{0}}{\partial\dot{r}}-\dfrac{\partial R^{0}}{\partial r}=0,\\
\dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac{\partial R^{0}}{\partial\dot{s}}-\dfrac{\partial R^{0}}{\partial s}=0,\end{cases}

are thus

\begin{cases}\ddot{r}=r\dot{s}^{T}A(s)\dot{s}-\dfrac{\kappa}{r^{\kappa+1}}U(s),\\
\ddot{s}=\dfrac{1}{r^{\kappa+2}}A^{-1}(s)\nabla U(s)-2\dfrac{\dot{r}\dot{s}}{r}+\dfrac{1}{2}A^{-1}(s)\nabla(\dot{s}^{T}A(s)\dot{s})-A^{-1}(s)\dot{A}(s)\dot{s}.\end{cases}

3
The Reduced Equations of Motion and Finite Spin

3.1
Analysis of the reduced equations of motion

To further analyze the dynamical behavior near a total collapse, we make a time-change by setting
dt=r^{\frac{\kappa}{2}+1}d\tau
. The equations of motion then take the form

\begin{cases}r^{\prime\prime}=(\dfrac{\kappa}{2}+1)\dfrac{r^{\prime 2}}{r}+rs^{\prime T}A(s)s^{\prime}-\kappa\,rU(s),\\
s^{\prime\prime}=A^{-1}(s)\nabla U(s)+(\frac{\kappa}{2}-1)\dfrac{r^{\prime}s^{\prime}}{r}+\frac{1}{2}A^{-1}(s)\nabla(\dot{s}^{T}A(s)\dot{s})-A^{-1}(s)\left(DA(s)\left(s^{\prime}\right)\right)s^{\prime}.\end{cases}

Further we set
r^{\prime}=vr
,
s^{\prime}=w
to blow-up the set of total collisions
\{r=0\}

à la
McGehee. This way we get

\begin{cases}r^{\prime}=vr,\\
v^{\prime}=\dfrac{\kappa}{2}v^{2}+w^{T}A(s)w-\kappa U(s),\\
s^{\prime}=w,\\
w^{\prime}=A^{-1}(s)\nabla U(s)+(\frac{\kappa}{2}-1)vw+\dfrac{1}{2}A^{-1}(s)\nabla(w^{T}A(s)w)-A^{-1}(s)\left(DA(s)\left(w\right)\right)w.\end{cases}

Setting

\widetilde{\nabla}=A^{-1}(s)\nabla,

and

\widetilde{D}_{\tau}w=w^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\nabla}(w^{T}A(s)w)+A^{-1}(s)\left(DA(s)\left(w\right)\right)w,

the system is now written in a more compact form as

\begin{cases}r^{\prime}=vr,\\
v^{\prime}=\dfrac{\kappa}{2}v^{2}+w^{T}A(s)w-\kappa U(s),\\
s^{\prime}=w,\\
\widetilde{D}_{\tau}w=\widetilde{\nabla}U(s)+(\frac{\kappa}{2}-1)vw.\end{cases}

The energy equation is

\dfrac{1}{2}v^{2}+\dfrac{1}{2}F(s,w)-U(s)=r^{\kappa}h.

The collision manifold
\{r=0\}
is invariant. Any equilibrium point on it can be expressed in these coordinates as
(0,v_{0},s_{0},0)
, where from the energy equation
v_{0}^{2}=2U(s_{0})
and

\widetilde{\nabla}U(s_{0})=0\Leftrightarrow\nabla U(s_{0})=0\Leftrightarrow\hbox{$s_{0}$ is a (reduced) normalized central configuration}.

Now let
q(t)
be a total collision solution, with total collision happens at
t=t^{*}
, with normalized configuration converging to an isolated normalized central configuration
s_{0}
. Then the corresponding solution
\gamma(\tau)=(r(\tau),v(\tau),s(t),w(\tau))
converges as
\tau\to\infty
to an isolated equilibrium point
P(0,v_{0},s_{0},0)
with

v(\tau)\to v_{0}=-\sqrt{2U(s_{0})}<0.

From
r^{\prime}=vr
we see that
r(\tau)
converges to 0 exponentially.

We want to show the arclength

L(s)=\int\|s^{\prime}(\tau)\|_{FS}\,d\tau=\int\|w(\tau)\|_{FS}\,d\tau.

is finite.

Proposition 3.1

L(s)=\int\|w(\tau)\|_{FS}\,d\tau

(3)

is finite for a total collision solution.

The proof is really that of Moeckel-Montgomery
[
5
]
, who established this result in the planar N-body problem with Newtonian potential. In the following sketch we explain why this holds in our case and indicate some rather minor differences.

Sketch of Proof.
As compare to the argument of
[
5
]
, the dimension is increased and we need to carefully put coefficients depending on
\kappa
in various places. In the
(\delta r,\delta v)
directions the block is now
\begin{pmatrix}v_{0}&0\\
0&\kappa v_{0}\end{pmatrix}
giving the rate of exponential convergence of
r(\gamma)\to 0
, complemented by the block

B=\begin{pmatrix}0&Id\\
D\widetilde{\nabla}U(s_{0})&(\kappa/2-1)v_{0}Id\end{pmatrix}

in the
(\delta s,\delta w)
directions.

We look for eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the matrix
B
. If
(\delta s,\delta w)
is an eigenvector with eigenvalue
\lambda
, then we get

\delta w=\lambda\delta s,\qquad D\widetilde{\nabla}U(s_{0})\delta s+(\kappa/2-1)v_{0}\delta w=\lambda\delta w

which is reduced to the system

\delta w=\lambda\delta s,\qquad D\widetilde{\nabla}U(s_{0})\delta s=(\lambda-(\kappa/2-1)v_{0})\lambda\delta s=:c\,\delta s.

So
\delta s
is an eigenvector of the central configuration
s_{0}
with eigenvalue
c
, just with the gradient
\nabla
replaced by
\widetilde{\nabla}=A^{-1}(s)\nabla
.

Solving
\lambda
from
c
we get

\lambda_{\pm}=\dfrac{(\kappa-2)v_{0}\pm\sqrt{(\kappa-2)^{2}v_{0}^{2}+16c}}{4}.

Since
0<\kappa<2,v_{0}<0
, nonreal eigenvalues have positive real parts, corresponding to unstable directions. Also, we have
\lambda_{-}=0
if and only if
c=0
.

So if the matrix
D\nabla U(s_{0})
is non-singular, the corresponding point
P
is hyperbolic and any solution approaching
P
lies in its stable manifold, thus with exponential rate of convergence. Thus the integrand in (
3
) is finite.

In the case when
D\nabla U(s_{0})
is singular, we may proceed as in
[
5
]
using center manifold theorem
[
8
]
. Besides the change of dimension which does not affect anywhere in the argument, the precise formulas slightly differ from those in
[
5
]
by coefficients depending on
\kappa
, which thanks to our assumption
0<\kappa<2
do not change the sign nor the order estimates of the formulas. Consequently,
[
5
, Lemma 4.1]
still holds. The argument leading to
[
5
, Lemma 4.2]
is completely formal and works also in our situation. Also
[
5
, Lemma 4.3]
holds by the very same argument as in
[
5
]
, just now we need to write
k=-\frac{2}{\kappa-2}v(0)^{-1}
. Nothing changes for the arguments leading to
[
5
, Lemma 4.4]
and to
[
5
, Lemma 4.5]
. Consequently
[
5
, Theorem 4.6]
holds in our situation. This is the statement of the Proposition.

\square

3.2
Finite spin

Let
s_{0}
be an isolated normalized reduced central configuration. It then represents an
SO(d)
-invariant family
Orb(s_{0})
of normalized central configurations in
\mathcal{N}
. Then there exists an
SO(d)
-invariant neighborhood
\mathcal{U}(s_{0})
of
Orb(s_{0})
in
\mathcal{N}
such that along any normalized total collision orbit with limiting normalized reduced central configuration
s_{0}
and with total collision time
t^{*}
, there exists
t_{-}
, such that the solution in the time interval
(t_{-},t^{*})
lies entirely in
\mathcal{U}(s_{0})
.

Lemma 3.2

In the time interval
(t_{-},t^{*})
, then there exists a positive number
C_{1}>0
depending only on
\mathcal{U}(s_{0})
, such that

\|R^{-1}R^{\prime}\|\leq C_{1}\|w\|_{FS}.

Proof.

Treating
\mathcal{I}(s),A(s)
as positive-definite symmetric operators we have at any isolated normalized central configuration
s_{0}
with dimension
\geq d-1
, there hold

2c_{1}:=\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}(s_{0})=\min_{\|\Gamma\|=1}\langle\Gamma,\mathcal{I}(s_{0})\Gamma\rangle>0\qquad 2c_{2}:=\lambda_{A}(s_{0})=\min_{\|w\|=1}\langle w,A(s_{0})w\rangle>0.

Shrinking
\mathcal{U}(s_{0})
if necessary we may assume for all
s\in\mathcal{U}(s_{0})
we have

\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}(s)=\min_{\|\Gamma\|=1}\langle\Gamma,\mathcal{I}(s)\Gamma\rangle>c_{1}>0\qquad\lambda_{A}(s)=\lambda_{A}(s)=\min_{\|w\|=1}\langle w,A(s_{0})w\rangle>c_{2}>0.

Changing time in Eq. (
2
), we have

R^{-1}R^{\prime}=\mathcal{I}(s)^{-1}G(s,s^{\prime})=\mathcal{I}(s)^{-1}G(s,w).

Then

\|R^{-1}R^{\prime}\|<c_{1}^{-1}\|\mathcal{I}R^{-1}R^{\prime}\|=c_{1}^{-1}\|G(s,w)\|.

Now

\|G(s,w)\|\leq\sum_{i}m_{i}\|s_{i}\|\|w_{i}\|\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i}m_{i}\|s_{i}\|^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i}m_{i}\|w_{i}\|^{2}}.

As we are on
\mathcal{N}
, we have

\sqrt{\sum_{i}m_{i}\|s_{i}\|^{2}}=\|s\|=1,

so we get

\|G(s,w)\|\leq\|w\|\leq c_{3}(s)\|w\|_{FS}\leq c_{4}\|w\|_{FS},

in which the second inequality is based on equivalence of norms on finite-dimensional vector spaces. Moreover
c_{3}(s)
can be controlled by
c_{2}
and the latter continuous. Further shrinking
\mathcal{U}(s_{0})
if necessary, we get the last inequality. Combining all these we get the assertion.

\square

With this we prove Theorem
1.1
:

Proof of Theorem
1.1

By equivalence of norms on a finite-dimensional vector space, there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
such that

\|RR^{-1}R^{\prime}\|\leq C_{2}\|R\|\|R^{-1}R^{\prime}\|

holds.

By Lemma (
3.2
)

\|R^{\prime}\|=\|RR^{-1}R^{\prime}\|\leq C_{2}\|R\|\|R^{-1}R^{\prime}\|\leq C_{2}C_{1}\,\|w\|_{FS}.

The theorem now follows from Proposition
3.1
.

\square
