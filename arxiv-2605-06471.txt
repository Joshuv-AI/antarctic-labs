Abstract
. Leap generators have been introduced in [Duchon et al.’04] for exact-size random generation of structures in a class of the form 𝒞 = Seq ⁡ ( ℬ ) \mathcal{C}=\mathrm{Seq}(\mathcal{B}) (sequence construction), in the supercritical case. We extend these generators to supercritical composition schemes 𝒞 = 𝒜 ∘ ℬ \mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B} . Compared to the sequence construction, the obtained exact-size random generator for 𝒞 \mathcal{C} still has linear time complexity (under conditions on the sampling complexity in 𝒜 \mathcal{A} and ℬ \mathcal{B} ), but perfect uniformity of the distribution is lost in general. However the distribution on 𝒞 n \mathcal{C}_{n} , called leap distribution, is asymptotically uniform, the total variation distance from the uniform distribution being ( c + o ( 1 ) ) n − 1 / 2 (c+o(1))n^{-1/2} for an explicit constant c c . These generators are simple to implement and can be applied to several classes of walks and trees, in particular Pólya trees. Leap generators can also be given for certain critical composition schemes, those relating planar map families, where this time the total variation distance to the uniform distribution is ∼ c n − 1 / 3 \sim c\,n^{-1/3} for an explicit constant c c .



1.
Introduction

A random generation algorithm aims at efficiently sampling an object of a given size
n
from a combinatorial class (e.g. of trees, permutations, partitions, graphs, maps,…) uniformly at random. An important motivation is to obtain simulations of large random objects and conjecture their asymptotic behavior, in particular for parameters that are difficult to analyze. Fast running time (ideally linear) is thus paramount.
Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms
[
27
,
13
]
give a robust and flexible approach for random generation, however the mixing time can be difficult to analyze (and the complexity is often superlinear).
Regarding combinatorial methods, bijective random samplers are typically fast, but require a correspondence to a class where counting and random generation are easier; such generators are known for instance for classes of trees (e.g. via Prüfer code), planar maps
[
35
]
, and automata
[
8
]
.
On the other hand, two automatic methods of random generation have been developed for classes that admit a decomposition in terms of the basic constructions of symbolic combinatorics
[
19
]
. (i) The recursive method
[
20
,
14
]
based on counting coefficients achieves exact-size sampling, but at the cost of superlinear precomputations that limit the size to the range of thousands. (ii) Boltzmann sampling
[
16
,
18
]
, based on evaluations of the generating functions at a well-chosen value, often achieves linear-time complexity, however at the cost of a fluctuating output size. In approximate-size sampling, Boltzmann samplers perform very well and make it possible to draw random objects of size in the range of millions. However, in practice it is convenient in simulations to have very large samples at certain sizes (e.g. powers of two) in order to obtain precise histograms of parameters, such as distance parameters, and to measure how the distribution scales with the size, see
[
6
, Sec. 3]
. In that context Boltzmann samples are not really well suited, as they require heavy rejection to be tuned at an exact size, which turns the complexity from linear to quadratic.

There are however certain situations where Boltzmann sampling yields exact-size linear-time samplers: for a class
\mathcal{C}=\mathrm{Seq}(\mathcal{B})
(sequence construction) in the supercritical case, a “leap” process
[
16
, Sec.7.1]
on top of Boltzmann samplers for
\mathcal{B}
yields an exact-size uniform random generator for
\mathcal{C}
of linear-time complexity. In this article, we extend such leap generators to the more general setting of a supercritical composition scheme
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B}
, and show that they still perform in linear time for exact-size sampling (under conditions on the sampling complexity in
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
). In contrast to the sequence construction, perfect uniformity is lost in general, but the obtained leap generators are asymptotically uniform, in the sense that the total variation distance to the uniform distribution is
o(1)
: we prove more precisely that it is
(c+o(1))n^{-1/2}
for some explicit
c
(via a simple further rejection step it can be improved to
O(n^{-r})
for any fixed
r
, if needed). This is sufficient to observe limit behavior of parameters, as limit laws are the same under an asymptotically uniform distribution as under the uniform distribution. These generators can be applied to a variety of structures, notably classes of unlabeled trees such as Pólya trees, and they extend to certain critical composition schemes such as those of planar maps
[
5
]
, where this time the total variation distance to the uniform distribution is
(c+o(1))n^{-1/3}
. We provide several simulations (inspired by those recently performed in
[
7
]
) showing the practical efficiency of our approach, and illustrating that the empirical (or exact) laws of parameters —such as the height of Motzkin walks or of Pólya trees— are very close under the leap and uniform distributions.

The idea of relaxing perfect uniformity to asymptotic uniformity —in random sampling and analysis of random discrete structures— already appeared in different contexts. In MCMC generators the uniform distribution at a given size is only reached asymptotically in the number of steps. In practice one performs a number of steps large enough to be close to the uniform distribution. This required number of steps (mixing time) is often hard to analyze, but it can also be validated experimentally, as for instance in the recent random generator
[
7
]
of Pólya trees. Regarding the bijective approach, in the context of fixed-genus factorizations of an
n
-cycle into transpositions, an asymptotically uniform bijective random generator has been developed in
[
17
]
, and used to prove convergence in law of a chord process associated to the factorization.
Asymptotically uniform random samplers have also been developed for graphs with prescribed vertex-degrees
[
36
,
26
,
9
]
, based on switching operations on multigraphs to get rid of loops and multiple edges. Note that even in the context of theoretically uniform samplers (e.g. Boltzmann samplers), typical practical implementations are not perfectly uniform, as they rely on fixed precision evaluation. For a sufficiently high precision chosen depending on the output size, this gives an asymptotically negligible bias from the uniform distribution. Finally, in the context of analysing random discrete structures, it is known that establishing a limit law (e.g. scaling limit) for random structures in an unrooted class can often be reduced to the easier task of establishing the same limit law for an associated rooted class, if one can prove that rooting is asymptotically unbiased in the total variation sense, i.e., that the distribution induced by rooted objects on unrooted objects is asymptotically uniform. This property is known for various families of planar maps, since planar maps with a non-trivial automorphism are exponentially rare
[
28
,
32
]
. It has also been recently established for free trees
[
38
]
, where it is shown that the total variation distance between the uniform distribution on free trees and the distribution induced by Pólya trees is
n^{-a}
for any
a<1/2
.

2.
Background and tools

2.1.
Symbolic combinatorics

We recall that a combinatorial class
\mathcal{C}
is a set of objects equipped with a size function
\gamma\to|\gamma|\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for each
n\geq 0
the set
\mathcal{C}_{n}=\{\gamma\in\mathcal{C}\ |\ |\gamma|=n\}
is finite, each object
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
being made of
n
atoms (e.g. nodes of a tree, steps of a walk…). In the unlabeled setting the atoms carry no label, and the associated (ordinary) generating function is
C(z)=\sum_{n}|\mathcal{C}_{n}|z^{n}
; while in the labeled setting the atoms carry distinct labels in
[1..n]
and the associated (exponential) generating function is
C(z)=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{n!}|\mathcal{C}_{n}|z^{n}
.

2.1.1.
Dictionary of symbolic combinatorics

We use the usual dictionary of symbolic combinatorics
[
19
]
, with the two base classes
\mathbf{1}
and
\mathcal{Z}
of respective generating functions
z\to 1
and
z\to z
, and the operators
+
(disjoint union),
\times
(product). We will also use the constructions for sequence, cycle, and set of structures, and their restriction with a prescribed number of components, all these constructions being defined both in the labeled and unlabeled setting
(in the unlabeled setting these constructions allow the same component to appear multiple times).

Finally, of crucial importance for our method is the substitution operator: for two labeled combinatorial classes
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
where
\mathcal{B}
has no object of size
0
, the
composed class

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B}

is the set of objects
\gamma
that are obtained from an object
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
and a sequence
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{|\alpha|}
, where atom
i
of
\alpha
is considered as “substituted” by
\beta_{i}
; the size of
\gamma
is
n:=|\beta_{1}|+\cdots+|\beta_{k}|
, and a further relabeling function assigns distinct labels in
[1..n]
to the atoms (the atom-set of
\gamma
being the union of the atom-sets from
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}
).
The integer
k
is called the
core-size
of
\gamma
. In the unlabeled case the substitution operator is well defined if the atoms of objects in
\mathcal{A}
are unambiguously distinguishable (e.g. the steps of a walk, or the nodes of a binary tree), so that one can rank the atoms, even without them carrying a label. In both the labeled and unlabeled case (when applicable) the generating functions are related by

C(z)=A(B(z)).

2.3.
Boltzmann samplers

For an unlabeled (resp. a labeled) combinatorial class
\mathcal{C}
, with generating function
C(z)
, and for
x>0
such that
C(x)
converges, the
Boltzmann distribution

\mathbb{P}_{x}^{\,\mathcal{C}}
is the probability distribution on
\mathcal{C}
given by

(3)

\mathbb{P}_{x}^{\,\mathcal{C}}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{C(x)}x^{|\gamma|}\ \mathrm{(unlabeled\ case)},\ \ \ \ \ \mathbb{P}_{x}^{\,\mathcal{C}}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{C(x)}\frac{x^{|\gamma|}}{|\gamma|!}\ \mathrm{(labeled\ case).}

For
\gamma
drawn under
\mathbb{P}_{x}^{\,\mathcal{C}}
, the expectation
\mu(x)
and standard deviation
\sigma(x)
of the random variable
|\gamma|
are given by

(4)

\mu(x)=\frac{xC^{\prime}(x)}{C(x)},\ \ \ \ \sigma(x)=\sqrt{x\mu^{\prime}(x)}.

A Boltzmann sampler
[
16
]
is a procedure
\Gamma C(x)
that draws a random object in
\mathcal{C}
under the distribution
\mathbb{P}_{x}^{\,\mathcal{C}}
, for any fixed
x>0
such that
C(x)
converges. It is said to be of
linear time complexity
if the runtime of every generation is linearly bounded by the size of the output object.

Explicit sampling rules allow to obtain linear time Boltzmann samplers for combinatorial classes that are recursively specified from the base classes in terms of the standard constructions. These rules are described in
[
16
]
and complemented in
[
22
]
(substitution operator) and in
[
18
,
11
]
(set and cycle constructions in the unlabeled setting).

2.4.
Asymptotically uniform random sampling

For a finite set
E
and two probability distributions
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
on
E
, the total variation distance between
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
is defined as

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi,\pi^{\prime}):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in E}|\pi(x)-\pi^{\prime}(x)|.

For
\mathcal{C}=\cup_{n}\mathcal{C}_{n}
a combinatorial class, a random generator
\Gamma C[n]
on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
is called
asymptotically uniform
if the total variation distance between the uniform distribution
\pi_{n}
on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
and the distribution
\pi_{n}^{\prime}
induced by
\Gamma C[n]
satisfies
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{n},\pi_{n}^{\prime})=o(1)
.

Remark 1
.

If we consider a parameter
X=X_{n}
on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
(i.e., a function from
\mathcal{C}_{n}
to
\mathbb{R}
), and let
\pi_{X,n}
(resp.
\pi^{\prime}_{X,n}
) be the law of

X_{n}
under
\pi_{n}
(resp. under
\pi_{n}^{\prime}
), then we obviously have
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{X,n},\pi^{\prime}_{X,n})\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{n},\pi_{n}^{\prime})
. Hence, if
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{n},\pi_{n}^{\prime})=o(1)
then
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{X,n},\pi_{X,n}^{\prime})=o(1)
, possibly with smaller order of magnitude.

3.
Leap generators

3.1.
Description of the generator

We consider an admissible composition scheme

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B},

and assume we have a fixed-size uniform random generator
\Gamma A[k]
for the class
\mathcal{A}
, and a Boltzmann sampler
\Gamma B(x)
for the class
\mathcal{B}
.
Letting
\rho
be the radius of convergence of
C(z)
, the
leap generator

\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C[n]
proceeds as follows (see also Figure
1
).

Figure 1
.
The leap generator process.

At each trial we generate a sequence
(\beta_{i})_{i\geq 1}
of objects in
\mathcal{B}
one by one, each object being drawn (independently) by a call to
\Gamma B(\rho)
, and we stop at the largest
k
such that the total size
S:=|\beta_{1}|+\cdots+|\beta_{k}|
is at most
n
. If
S<n
the trial fails and we restart.
If
S=n
, we call
\alpha\leftarrow\Gamma A[k]
, and for
i\in[1..k]
we substitute the
i
th atom of
\alpha
by
\beta_{i}
.
We return the composed object
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
thus obtained (if we are in the labeled setting, randomly permuted labels in
[1..n]
are assigned to the atoms of
\gamma
).

Remark 2
.

In the special case
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{Seq}
, the leap generator for
\mathcal{C}=\mathrm{Seq}(\mathcal{B})
is exactly the leap generation process for
\mathcal{C}
as described in
[
16
, Section 7.1]
. In that case it gives a uniform random generator on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
.

3.2.
Analysis of the generator

We denote respectively by
\mu
and
\sigma
the expectation and standard deviation of the size of
|\Gamma B(\rho)|
, as given by (
4
).

Proposition 1
.

Let
q_{n}
be the probability of success at each trial in
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C[n]
. Then

(5)

q_{n}=[z^{n}]\frac{1}{1-B(\rho z)/B(\rho)}=\frac{1}{\mu}+O(r^{n})\ \mathrm{for\ some\ }r\in(0,1).

If the fixed-size random generator
\Gamma A[k]
has time complexity
O(k)
and the Boltzmann sampler has linear time-complexity, then the expected time complexity of
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C[n]
is
O(n)
.

Proof.

We give the proof for
q_{n}
in the unlabeled case (the labeled case proof is very similar).
A trial is a success if it generates a sequence
(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k})
of total size
n
. For each given such sequence, the probability that it is generated is
\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}_{\rho}^{\mathcal{B}}(\beta_{i})
, with
\mathbb{P}_{\rho}^{\mathcal{B}}(\beta)=\rho^{|\beta|}/B(\rho)
.
Hence the probability that the sequence is generated is
\rho^{n}/B(\rho)^{k}
. The total contribution of all structures of size
n
in
\mathcal{B}^{k}
is thus
\rho^{n}[z^{n}]B(z)^{k}/B(\rho)^{k}=[z^{n}]B(\rho z)^{k}/B(\rho)^{k}
. Summing over
k\geq 0
we find that the total contribution (which is
q_{n}
) is
[z^{n}]Q(z)
,
with
Q(z):=\frac{1}{1-B(\rho z)/B(\rho)}
, as claimed. Since the generating function
B(z)
is aperiodic, by the Daffodil lemma the maximum value of
|B(z)|
on
|z|\leq\rho
is uniquely attained at
z=\rho
. Thus the meromorphic function
Q(z)
has a unique pole on
|z|\leq 1
, at
z=1
. Moreover, we clearly have

\lim_{z\to 1}(1-z)Q(z)=\frac{B(\rho)}{\rho B^{\prime}(\rho)}=\frac{1}{\mu}.

Transfer theorems
[
19
, Section VI.3]
thus ensure that
q_{n}=\frac{1}{\mu}+O(r^{n})
for some
r\in(0,1)
(taking
r
such that
Q(z)
has no pole
\neq 1
on the disk
|z|\leq 1/r
).

Regarding the complexity statement, by a standard formula for rejection sampling, the overall expected cost of
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C[n]
is equal to the expected cost of one trial divided by
q_{n}
. Since
q_{n}\to 1/\mu
, it remains to show that the expected cost of one trial is
O(n)
. We use the above notation for
k,S,\beta_{i}
in the description of
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C[n]
. Let
\tilde{S}:=S+|\beta_{k+1}|
.
Since the samplers
\Gamma A[k]
and
\Gamma B(x)
have linear time complexity, the expected cost of one trial is
O(\mathbb{E}(\tilde{S}))+O(k)
, the second term given by the possible call to
\Gamma A[k]
. By definition of the stopping time,
S\leq n
and

\tilde{S}>n
. Note that
k\leq n
(as
\mathcal{B}
has no object of size
0
).
Hence, letting
M_{n}:=\mathrm{max}(|\beta_{1}|,\ldots,|\beta_{n}|)
, we have

\tilde{S}\leq n+M_{n+1}
. Since
|\Gamma B(\rho)|
has an exponentially decaying tail, we classically have
\mathbb{E}(M_{n})=O(\log(n))
,
so that
\mathbb{E}(\tilde{S})\sim n
. Hence the expected cost of one trial is
O(n)
. ∎

The probability distribution on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
given by
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C[n]
is called the
leap distribution
on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
and is denoted by
\pi_{n}^{\prime}
, while the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
is denoted by
\pi_{n}
. We also use the notation
\delta_{n}
to be
1
in the unlabeled setting and
1/n!
in the labeled setting.

Remark 3
.

It is also possible to perform the leap process a single time, without rejection: one then calls
\Gamma A[k]
with
k
number of leaps done before stopping. With the notation used to describe
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C[n]
, the obtained structure has a random size
Z_{n}=S\leq n
very close to
n
. Indeed, letting
Z
be the size of
\Gamma B(\rho)
, for any fixed
i\geq 0
one has

\mathbb{P}(n-Z_{n}=i)=q_{n-i}\ \!\mathbb{P}(Z>i)\sim\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbb{P}(Z>i).

Thus
n-Z_{n}
converges to the discrete law
p(i)=\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbb{P}(Z>i)
, which has an exponential tail.
Moreover, the distribution induced on any fixed size
n^{\prime}\leq n
is the leap distribution at that size.

Lemma 2
.

Let
a_{k}=[y^{k}]A(y)=\delta_{k}|\mathcal{A}_{k}|
and
c_{n}=[z^{n}]C(z)=\delta_{n}|\mathcal{C}_{n}|
. Let
\mathcal{C}_{n,k}
be the set of structures in
\mathcal{C}_{n}
having core-size
k
.
Then for every
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}_{n,k}
one has

(6)

\pi_{n}^{\prime}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{n}|}d_{n,k},\ \ \mathrm{with}\ \ d_{n,k}:=\frac{1}{q_{n}}\frac{c_{n}\rho^{n}}{a_{k}B(\rho)^{k}}.

Proof.

Letting
\pi_{n}^{\mathrm{first}}(\gamma)
be the probability that
\gamma
is generated at the first trial, we clearly have

\pi_{n}^{\prime}(\gamma)=\sum_{i\geq 1}\pi_{n}^{\mathrm{first}}(\gamma)\,(1-q_{n})^{i-1}=\frac{\pi_{n}^{\mathrm{first}}(\gamma)}{q_{n}},

where the
i
th term is the probability that
\gamma
is generated at the
i
-trial (after failure of the first
i-1
trials).

In the unlabeled case, let
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{k}
and
(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k})\in(\mathcal{B}^{k})_{n}
be the structures composing
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
. Then the probability that the leap process outputs
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}
is
\rho^{n}/B(\rho)^{k}
(as seen in the proof of Proposition
1
), and then the probability that
\alpha
is drawn from
\Gamma A[k]
is
1/a_{k}
. Hence

\pi_{n}^{\mathrm{first}}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{a_{k}}\frac{\rho^{n}}{B(\rho)^{k}}.

In the labeled case, for any given ordering (among
k!
possibilities) of the
\mathcal{B}
-components of
\gamma
, and for any ordering of the atoms among each of the
\mathcal{B}
-components, we let
\alpha
be the induced structure in
\mathcal{A}_{k}
(its atoms are labeled according to the ordering of the components). And let
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}
be the
\mathcal{B}
-components, where the atoms of each
\beta_{i}
are labeled in
[1..|\beta_{i}|]
according to their prescribed ordering. The probability that the leap process outputs
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}
is equal to
\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{B(\rho)}\frac{\rho^{|\beta_{i}|}}{|\beta_{i}|!}
. Then the probability that
\alpha
is chosen by
\Gamma A[k]
is equal to
1/|\mathcal{A}_{k}|
.
And finally the probability that the relabeling function assigns the correct labels to the atoms of
\gamma
is
1/n!
. Since the number of possibilities for the orderings is
k!\prod_{i=1}^{k}|\beta_{i}|!
, we conclude that

\pi_{n}^{\mathrm{first}}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{n!}\frac{k!}{|\mathcal{A}_{k}|!}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{B(\rho)}\rho^{|\beta_{i}|}=\frac{1}{n!}\frac{1}{a_{k}}\frac{\rho^{n}}{B(\rho)^{k}}
.
∎

Remark 4
.

The quantity
d_{n,k}
, which is the ratio
\pi_{n}^{\prime}(\gamma)/\pi_{n}(\gamma)
for objects
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}_{n,k}
, is called the
distortion factor
.
Note that, for a given large
n
, it tends to increase with
k
when
s<1
, and it tends to decrease with
k
when
s>1
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
c_{n,k}=a_{k}[z^{n}]B(z)^{k}=\delta_{n}|\mathcal{C}_{n,k}|
.
Then

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{n},\pi_{n}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}S_{n,k},

where

(7)

\displaystyle S_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{n,k}}{c_{n}}\left|1-d_{n,k}\right|

Proof.

By (
6
) we have
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{n},\pi_{n}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}S_{n,k}
, where

\displaystyle S_{n,k}

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{C}_{n,k}|\cdot\left|\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{n}|}-d_{n,k}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{n}|}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathcal{C}_{n,k}|}{|\mathcal{C}_{n}|}\cdot\left|1-d_{n,k}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{n,k}}{c_{n}}\left|1-d_{n,k}\right|.

∎

We will now prove that the leap generator is asymptotically uniform. The first step is to have estimates on the two factors
c_{n,k}/c_{n}
and
|1-d_{n,k}|
in
S_{n,k}
.

Lemma 4
.

Recall the notation
\mu,\sigma
for the expectation and standard deviation of
|\Gamma B(\rho)|
.
Let
X_{n}
be the random variable such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=k)=\frac{c_{n,k}}{c_{n}}
. Then the estimate

(8)

\mathbb{P}\Big(X_{n}=n/\mu+t\,\sigma\sqrt{n/\mu^{3}}\Big)\sim\frac{\sqrt{\mu^{3}}}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^{2}/2}

holds uniformly for
t
in any fixed compact set.

Moreover, the following tail bound holds: there exist positive constants
\kappa,\xi,\zeta

such that, for
n\geq 0
and
0\leq t\leq\zeta\sqrt{n}
,

(9)

\mathbb{P}\Big(\big|X_{n}-n/\mu\big|\geq t\,\sigma\sqrt{n/\mu^{3}}\Big)\leq\xi\,e^{-\kappa\,t^{2}}.

Proof.

The probability generating function
p_{n}(u)=\sum_{k}\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=k)u^{k}
of
X_{n}
is given by

p_{n}(u)=\frac{[z^{n}]C(z,u)}{[z^{n}]C(z)},\ \ \mathrm{with}\ C(z,u):=A(uB(z)).

Let
\rho(u)
be the radius of convergence of
z\to C(z,u)
. Recall that
B(z)
is analytic at
\rho
, with
B(\rho)=\rho_{A}
. Hence,
\rho(u)
is given by the equation
B(\rho(u))=\rho_{A}/u
for
u
in a neighborhood of
1
. Moreover, by singularity analysis, we have the singular expansion

(10)

[z^{n}]C(z,u)\sim\frac{\kappa(u)}{\Gamma(s)}\,\rho(u)^{-n}\,n^{s-1}

uniformly for
u
in a neighborhood of
1
, with
\kappa(u)
analytic at
1
.
The conditions of the quasi-power theorem in its local limit formulation
[
19
, Theo.IX.14]
are satisfied: defining

\mathfrak{e}=-\frac{\rho^{\prime}(1)}{\rho},\ \ \ \mathfrak{s}^{2}=-\frac{\rho^{\prime\prime}(1)}{\rho}-\frac{\rho^{\prime}(1)}{\rho}+\frac{\rho^{\prime}(1)^{2}}{\rho^{2}},

we have the Gaussian estimate

\mathbb{P}\Big(X_{n}=\mathfrak{e}\,n+t\,\mathfrak{s}\,\sqrt{n}\Big)\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{1}{\mathfrak{s}\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^{2}/2}

uniformly for
t
in any fixed compact set. Moreover, from the equation
B(\rho(u))=\rho_{A}/u
it is easy to check that
\mathfrak{e}=1/\mu
and
\mathfrak{s}^{2}=\sigma^{2}/\mu^{3}
.

Regarding the tail bound, we can establish it classically by a Chernoff bound, with calculations similar to those for the sum of independent random variables, see e.g.
[
30
, Theo.15]
. From the estimate (
10
) there exists a real neighborhood
U_{1}
of
1
and a constant
\tilde{\xi}
such that, for all
n\geq 0
and
u\in U_{1}
,

p_{n}(u)\leq\tilde{\xi}\,\big(\rho(u)/\rho\big)^{-n}.

Since
p_{n}(u)=\mathbb{E}(e^{sX_{n}})
for
u=e^{s}
, there is a real neighborhood
U_{0}
of
0
such that, for all
n\geq 0
and
s\in U_{0}
,

\mathbb{E}\big(e^{s(X_{n}-n/\mu)}\big)\leq\tilde{\xi}\,\exp(n\,g(s)),\ \ \mathrm{with}\ g(s)=-\log(\rho(e^{s}))+\log(\rho)-s/\mu.

The function
g(s)
satisfies
g(0)=g^{\prime}(0)=0
, so that in a real neighborhood
[-\tilde{\zeta},\tilde{\zeta}]
of
0
we have
g(s)\leq\tilde{\kappa}s^{2}
for some
\tilde{\kappa}>0
.
Thus, for
n\geq 0
and
s\in[-\tilde{\zeta}\sqrt{n},\tilde{\zeta}\sqrt{n}]
,

\mathbb{E}(e^{s(X_{n}-n/\mu)/\sqrt{n}})\leq\tilde{\xi}\,\exp(\tilde{\kappa}s^{2})

and in that case Markov’s inequality gives

\mathbb{P}\big(|X_{n}-n/\mu|\geq t\sqrt{n}\big)\leq 2\,\tilde{\xi}\,\exp(\tilde{\kappa}s^{2}-ts)

for any
t>0
. If
t\leq 2\tilde{\kappa}\,\tilde{\zeta}\,\sqrt{n}
, then the optimal value
s=\frac{t}{2\tilde{\kappa}}

satisfies
s\leq\tilde{\zeta}\sqrt{n}
, so we have

\mathbb{P}\big(|X_{n}-n/\mu|\geq t\sqrt{n}\big)\leq 2\,\tilde{\xi}\,\exp(-t^{2}/(4\tilde{\kappa})).

The tail bound estimate follows, taking
\xi=2\tilde{\xi}
,
\kappa=\frac{1}{4\tilde{\kappa}}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\mu^{3}}
, and
\zeta=2\tilde{\kappa}\,\tilde{\zeta}\frac{\sqrt{\mu^{3}}}{\sigma}
.
∎

Lemma 5
.

Let
\epsilon_{n}=o(\sqrt{n})
.
For
k=\frac{n}{\mu}+t\frac{\sigma}{\mu^{3/2}}\sqrt{n}
such that
|t|\leq\epsilon_{n}
, one has

(11)

1-d_{n,k}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n\mu}}(s-1)\,t+O((1+t^{2})/n).

Proof.

We have, for
k=\Theta(n)
,

\frac{c_{n}\rho^{n}}{a_{k}B(\rho)^{k}}=\frac{\kappa_{C}\,n^{s-1}\,\big(1+O(1/n)\big)}{\kappa_{A}\,k^{s-1}\,\big(1+O(1/k)\big)}=\frac{\kappa_{C}}{\kappa_{A}}\Big(\frac{k}{n}\Big)^{1-s}\,\big(1+O(1/n)\big).

For
\frac{k}{n}=\frac{1}{\mu}\big(1+t\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n\mu}}\big)
with
|t|\leq\epsilon_{n}
, this gives, using
\frac{\kappa_{C}}{\kappa_{A}}=\mu^{-s}
(as seen in (
2
)),

\frac{c_{n}\rho^{n}}{a_{k}B(\rho)^{k}}=\frac{1}{\mu}\Big(1+\frac{\sigma(1-s)t}{\sqrt{n\mu}}\Big)+O((1+t^{2})/n).

Combined with (
5
), this yields (
11
).
∎

Proposition 6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B}
be an admissible composition scheme of singular exponent
s
.
Then

(12)

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{n},\pi_{n}^{\prime})\sim\frac{|s-1|\sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi\mu}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}.

Proof.

For
y>0
let

\mathrm{Int}_{n}(y):=\Big[n/\mu-y\,\sigma\sqrt{n/\mu^{3}},\,n/\mu+y\,\sigma\sqrt{n/\mu^{3}}\Big]\cap\Big[1..n\Big].

Let
I_{n}:=\mathrm{Int}_{n}(\log(n)^{2})
, and
J_{n}:=[1..n]\backslash I_{n}
, so we have

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{n},\pi_{n}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in I_{n}}S_{n,k}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in J_{n}}S_{n,k}.

We first show that
\sum_{k\in J_{n}}S_{n,k}
is asymptotically negligible.
By (
9
) and the fact that
d_{n,k}=O(n^{(s-1)_{+}})
, we have

\sum_{k\in J_{n}}S_{n,k}=O\Big(n^{(s-1)_{+}}\sum_{k\in J_{n}}\frac{c_{n,k}}{c_{n}}\Big)=O\big(n^{|s-1|}e^{-\kappa\log(n)^{2}}\big)=O(n^{-b})\ \mathrm{for\ any\ }b>0.

We now consider the contribution
\sum_{k\in I_{n}}S_{n,k}
.
Let
m>0
. Then from (
9
) and (
11
) we obtain, for
k=\frac{n}{\mu}+t\,\sigma\sqrt{n/\mu^{3}}
,

S_{n,k}\sim\frac{\mu\,|s-1|}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,n}|t|\,e^{-t^{2}/2},

uniformly over
k\in I_{n}(m)
, so that

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in I_{n}(m)}S_{n,k}\sim\frac{\mu\,|s-1|}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,n}\sum_{k\in I_{n}(m)}|t|\,e^{-t^{2}/2}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\mu\,|s-1|}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,n}\frac{\sigma\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{\mu^{3}}}\,\int_{-m}^{m}|t|\,e^{-t^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma\,|s-1|}{\sqrt{2\pi\,\mu\,n}}\,\int_{-m}^{m}|t|\,e^{-t^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}t

On the other hand, for
0\leq m\leq\log(n)^{2}
, by Lemma
4
and Lemma
5
we have the existence of universal constants such that

\sum_{k\notin I_{n}(m)}\frac{c_{n,k}}{c_{n}}=O(e^{-\kappa m^{2}}),\ \ \ |1-d_{n,k}|=O\big(m/\sqrt{n}\big)\ \mathrm{for}\ k\in I_{n}(m)\backslash I_{n}(m+1)

Hence, for
0\leq m\leq\log(n)^{2}
, we have

\sum_{k\in I_{n}(m+1)\backslash I_{n}(m)}S_{n,k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}O(m\,e^{-\kappa m^{2}}),

By summation we obtain

\sum_{k\in I_{n}\backslash I_{n}(m)}S_{n,k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}O(m\,e^{-\kappa m^{2}})

Hence, for every
m\geq 1

\sum_{k\in I_{n}}S_{n,k}=(1+o(1))\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{\sigma\,|s-1|}{\sqrt{2\pi\,\mu}}\,\int_{-m}^{m}|t|\,e^{-t^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}O(m\,e^{-\kappa m^{2}}),

where the little
o
depends on
m
, but not the big
O
.
Letting
m
get large, we obtain

\sum_{k\in I_{n}}S_{n,k}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{\sigma\,|s-1|}{\sqrt{2\pi\,\mu}}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}|t|\,e^{-t^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}t=2\frac{|s-1|\sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi\mu}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}.

∎

3.3.
Acceleration of convergence

We show here that a simple rejection step on top of leap generators makes it possible to accelerate the rate of convergence toward the uniform distribution.
A
rejection sequence
is given by
w_{n,k}\in[0,1]
, for
0\leq k\leq n
.
Given such a sequence, and an admissible composition scheme
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B}
, the
rejection leap generator
for
\mathcal{C}
proceeds as follows:

\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C^{\mathrm{\,rej}}[n]
:

repeat

\gamma\leftarrow\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C[n]

let
k=
core-size of
\gamma

until
\mathrm{Bern}(w_{n,k})

return
\gamma

Lemma 7
.

Let
W_{n}:=\sum_{k}\frac{c_{n,k}}{c_{n}}d_{n,k}w_{n,k}
, and let
\pi_{n}^{\mathrm{rej}}
be the distribution on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
given by
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C^{\mathrm{\,rej}}[n]
.

Then, for
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}_{n,k}
, we have

\pi_{n}^{\mathrm{rej}}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{C}_{n}}d_{n,k}^{\mathrm{rej}}\ \ \ \ \mathrm{with}\ d_{n,k}^{\mathrm{rej}}:=\frac{1}{W_{n}}d_{n,k}w_{n,k}.

Moreover,

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{n},\pi_{n}^{\mathrm{rej}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{c_{n,k}}{c_{n}}\big|1-d_{n,k}^{\mathrm{rej}}\big|.

Proof.

For
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
of core-size
k
, let
\pi_{n}^{\mathrm{trial}}(\gamma)
be the probability of drawing
\gamma
at a given trial.
Then

\pi_{n}^{\mathrm{trial}}(\gamma)=\pi_{n}^{\prime}(\gamma)w_{n,k}.

The probability of success at each trial, denoted
W_{n}
, is thus the sum of these probabilities over all
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
, so that

W_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\pi_{n}^{\mathrm{trial}}(\gamma)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}|\mathcal{C}_{n,k}|\pi_{n}^{\prime}(\gamma)w_{n,k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{|\mathcal{C}_{n,k}|}{|\mathcal{C}_{n}|}d_{n,k}w_{n,k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{c_{n,k}}{c_{n}}d_{n,k}w_{n,k}.

Then we have, for
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
,

\pi_{n}^{\mathrm{rej}}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{W_{n}}\pi_{n}^{\mathrm{trial}}(\gamma),

which gives the claimed formula.
∎

Our aim here is to exhibit easy-to-compute rejection sequences giving asymptotic uniformity at a faster rate of convergence than in Proposition
6
. We say that a generating function
F(z)
of singular exponent
s
has a

if there exist coefficients
f_{\infty}^{1},f_{\infty}^{2},\ldots
such that, for every

,

Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B}
be an admissible composition scheme such that
A(y)
has a complete asymptotic expansion (an assumption to be satisfied by all examples to be given in Section
4
). Then
C(z)
also has a complete asymptotic expansion, whose coefficients are obtained from those of
A(y)
and from the derivatives of

at

5
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B}

\epsilon_{n}=o(\sqrt{n})

Proposition 9
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B}
be an admissible composition scheme of singular exponent

, and such that

has a complete asymptotic expansion.

Let

(e.g.

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{n},\pi_{n}^{\mathrm{rej}})\sim\frac{c}{2\,n^{\frac{r+1}{2}}}

The probability of success of each trial tends to
a/\mu
as

, so that the expected complexity of
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C^{\mathrm{\,rej}}[n]

With the notation of Lemma

\displaystyle d_{n,k}^{\mathrm{rej}}

(15)

\displaystyle=1+\big(p_{\infty}^{r+1}(t)-I^{(r)}\big)n^{-\frac{r+1}{2}}+o\big(n^{-\frac{r+1}{2}}\big)+O\left(\Big(\frac{1+t^{2}}{n}\Big)^{\frac{r+1}{2}}\right)

Remark 7
.

For

we have
y_{n}^{(0)}(t)=1
, so that
w_{n,k}=a
. Hence
\pi_{n}^{\mathrm{rej}}=\pi_{n}^{\prime}
whatever the chosen value of
a\in(0,1)
, and taking the limit value
a=1
, the generator
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C^{\mathrm{\,rej}}[n]
is exactly
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C[n]
.

3.4.
Extensions

As briefly described below, the leap generation method and the (possibly accelerated) asymptotic uniformity work in more general settings.

3.4.1.
Composition schemes with a distinguished first component

One can consider more generally a composition scheme of the form

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{D}\times\,\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B}.

Such a scheme is called
supercritical
if
\rho_{B}>\rho_{C}
and
\rho_{D}>\rho_{C}
.
It is called
admissible
if furthermore
B(z),C(z)
are aperiodic and the support of
A(z)
is
\mathbb{N}
. In that case (with
\rho:=\rho_{C}
) we let
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C[n]
generate a sequence
\delta\leftarrow\Gamma D(\rho),\beta_{1}\leftarrow\Gamma B(\rho),\ldots,\beta_{k}\leftarrow\Gamma B(\rho)
until the total size
S:=|\delta|+|\beta_{1}|+\cdots+|\beta_{k}|
satisfies
S\geq n
(possibly
k=0
) and we reject and restart if
S>n
. If
S=n
we call
\alpha\leftarrow\Gamma A[k]
and return the pair
\gamma
made of
\delta
and of the composition of
\alpha
with
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}
(with a final relabeling operation if in the labeled setting). The formulas for
q_{n}
and
d_{n,k}
are slightly modified to

(16)

q_{n}=[z^{n}]\frac{D(\rho z)}{D(\rho)}\frac{1}{1-B(\rho z)/B(\rho)},\ \ \ d_{n,k}=\frac{1}{q_{n}}\frac{c_{n}\rho^{n}}{D(\rho)\,a_{k}\,B(\rho)^{k}},

while the formula for the total variation distance remains

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{n},\pi_{n}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}S_{n,k}
, with
S_{n,k}=\frac{c_{n,k}}{c_{n}}|1-d_{n,k}|
.
The estimates (
5
) and (
12
) are still valid, as well as the linear running time of
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C[n]
, assuming that the samplers
\Gamma A[k],\Gamma B(x),\Gamma D(x)
have running time linearly bounded by the size of the output.

3.4.2.
Periodic case

If the support of
A(y)
is not
\mathbb{N}
, then each trial of sequence generation
(\delta,\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k})
is declared a success if
S=n

and

k
is in the support of
A(z)
. As before, once a successful trial is obtained, one calls
\alpha\leftarrow\Gamma A[k]
and returns the pair made of
\delta
and of
\alpha
composed with
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}
.
This situation with gaps in the support of
A(y)
typically has to be considered when
A(y)
is periodic.
We take the example where the support of
D(z)
is
d\,\mathbb{N}
and the supports of
A(y),B(z),C(z)
are
1+d\,\mathbb{N}
for some fixed
d\geq 2
. Then the formulas for
q_{n}
and
d_{n,k}
are still given by (
16
)
under the condition that
n,k\in 1+d\,\mathbb{N}
. And in the formula for
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{n},\pi_{n}^{\prime})
the sum is restricted to
k\in 1+d\,\mathbb{N}
.
The estimate (
5
) is still valid upon conditioning on
n\in 1+d\mathbb{N}
and replacing
\frac{1}{\mu}
by
\frac{1}{d\mu}
, and the sampler
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C[n]
still has expected running time
O(n)
if the samplers
\Gamma A[k],\Gamma B(x),\Gamma D(x)
have running time linearly bounded by the size of the output.
The estimate (
12
) is also still valid, with singular exponent defined in the
d
-periodic setting, and the constant factor multiplied by
\sqrt{d}
(again conditioning on
n\in 1+d\mathbb{N}
).

3.4.3.
Weighted case

The method also extends to weighted structures. In a
weighted class

\mathcal{C}
, to each structure
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}
is associated a weight
w(\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. In that case, the associated generating function and the
w
-distribution

\pi_{n}
on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
are given by

C(z)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{C}}\delta_{|\gamma|}w(\gamma)z^{|\gamma|},\ \ \ \pi_{n}(\gamma)=\frac{\delta_{n}w(\gamma)}{[z^{n}]C(z)}\ \ \mathrm{for}\ \gamma\in\mathcal{C}_{n}.

The weights are usually required to follow multiplicative rules when assembling structures. Thus,
for a composition scheme
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B}
, and for
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}
made of
\delta\in\mathcal{D}
and of
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
composed with
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}
, we require

w(\gamma)=w(\delta)w(\alpha)\prod_{i=1}^{k}w(\beta_{i})
, so that the generating functions are related by
C(z)=D(z)\,A(B(z))
. The definitions of subcritical and admissible composition schemes extend straightforwardly, as well as the description of the leap generator
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C[n]
, assuming
the generator
\Gamma A[k]
draws a structure in
\mathcal{A}_{k}
under the
w
-distribution, while the generator
\Gamma B(x)
(and similarly
\Gamma D(x)
) draws a structure in
\mathcal{B}
under the
w
-weighted Boltzmann distribution
\mathbb{P}_{x}^{\mathcal{B}}(\beta)=\delta_{|\gamma|}w(\beta)x^{|\beta|}/B(x)
. As before, the sampler
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C[n]
has expected running time
O(n)
if the samplers for the component classes have running time linearly bounded by the size of the drawn object.
Letting
\pi_{n}^{\prime}
be the induced distribution on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
, the estimate (
12
) remains valid, so that
\pi_{n}^{\prime}
is asymptotically the
w
-distribution
\pi_{n}
, in the total variation sense.

3.4.4.
Critical composition schemes

Finally, the method can also be extended to
critical
composition schemes. In such a scheme
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B}
we have
\rho_{B}=\rho_{C}
and
B(\rho_{B})=\rho_{A}
(and
\rho_{D}\geq\rho_{C}
). The leap generator
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C[n]
can be defined exactly as in the supercritical case. While we do not carry a general analysis, we will see in Section
4.3
an important example (and extensions) of such a scheme: the block decomposition of planar maps. We will show that the expected linear time complexity and the asymptotic uniformity (up to using a rerooting operation on top of
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\smallfrown$}}}}{\Gamma}C[n]
) are still satisfied for such a scheme, the order of magnitude of
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{n},\pi_{n}^{\prime})
being of order
n^{-1/3}
instead of
n^{-1/2}
.

4.
Applications

We describe here how the strategy applies to various classes, of walks, trees and planar maps. In each case we take advantage of a decomposition in terms of a core-structure for which exact-size uniform random sampling can be done in linear time.

4.1.
Walks

We first show how leap generators can be obtained for Motzkin and Schröder walks.
For these families, exact-size uniform random samplers of linear time complexity have been developed
[
2
,
25
,
3
,
4
]
, so that applying leap generation is of limited interest. We rather consider these as toy examples where the strategy is simplest to implement and to validate experimentally (see Section
5
).

4.1.1.
Motzkin walks

A
Motzkin walk
of length
n\geq 0
is a walk with
n
steps in
\{NE,E,SE\}
, starting at the origin, ending at
(n,0)
, and staying in the upper half-plane
\{y\geq 0\}
.

Dyck walks
are Motzkin walks with no horizontal step. Let
\mathcal{C}
be the class of Motzkin walks where the size is the length, and
\mathcal{A}
the class of Dyck walks where the size is the semi-length. A Motzkin walk is uniquely obtained from a Dyck walk where each point can be extended into a (possibly empty) sequence of horizontal steps. Since a Dyck walk of semi-length
k
has
2k+1
points, this yields

\mathcal{C}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathcal{A}_{k}\mathcal{Z}^{2k}\mathrm{Seq}(\mathcal{Z})^{2k+1}.

Hence, letting
\mathcal{B}=(\mathcal{Z}\times\mathrm{Seq}(\mathcal{Z}))^{2}
and
\mathcal{D}=\mathrm{Seq}(\mathcal{Z})
, we have

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B}.

The composition scheme is admissible, with
\rho_{B}=\rho_{D}=1>\rho_{C}=1/3
, and the class
\mathcal{A}
has simple exact-size uniform random samplers (e.g. via the cyclic lemma). In the leap generation process, each call to the Boltzmann sampler
\Gamma B(1/3)
returns
the pair
(i,j)
(considered to have size
i+j+2
),
with
i,j
drawn independently under a
\mathrm{Geom}(1/3)
law.

4.1.2.
Schröder walks

A
Schröder walk
of size
n\geq 0
is a walk with steps in
\{N,E,SE\}
,
starting at the origin, ending at
(n,n)
, and staying in
\{y\geq x\}
.
Upon rotating by
45
degrees, these correspond to Motzkin walks whose length plus number of horizontal steps is equal to
2n
. Similarly as before, each Schröder walk is obtained as a Dyck walk with a possibly empty sequence of horizontal steps inserted at each point (compared to the Motzkin case, only the size parameter behaves differently). Letting
\mathcal{C}
be the class of Schröder excursions, and
\mathcal{A}
the class of Dyck walks where the size is the semi-length, this gives

\mathcal{C}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathcal{A}_{k}\mathcal{Z}^{k}\mathrm{Seq}(\mathcal{Z})^{2k+1}

Hence, letting
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{Z}\times\mathrm{Seq}(\mathcal{Z})^{2}
and
\mathcal{D}=\mathrm{Seq}(\mathcal{Z})
, we have

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B}.

The composition scheme is again admissible, with
\rho_{B}=\rho_{D}=1>\rho_{C}=3-2\sqrt{2}
. In the leap generation process, each call to the Boltzmann sampler
\Gamma B(\rho_{C})
returns
the pair
(i,j)
(considered to have size
i+j+1
),
with
i,j
drawn independently under a
\mathrm{Geom}(\rho_{C})
law.

4.2.
Trees

Leap generators can also be obtained for various classes of unlabeled rooted trees, a main motivation for this work.

4.2.1.
Pólya trees

Recall that a
Pólya tree
(resp. a rooted Cayley tree) is a rooted unlabeled (resp. labeled) tree where the children of nodes are not ordered, the size being the number of vertices.
The class
\mathcal{A}
of rooted Cayley trees is given by

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{Z}\times\mathrm{Set}(\mathcal{A}),\ \ A(z)=z\exp(A(z)),\ \ |\mathcal{A}_{n}|=n^{n-1},

and one can perform uniform random generation in
\mathcal{A}_{n}
in linear time (e.g. via Prüfer code).
The class of Pólya trees is the unlabeled class
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
associated to
\mathcal{A}
, it is given by

(17)

\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{Z}\times\mathrm{MSet}(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}),\ \ \widetilde{A}(z)=z\exp\big(\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{1}{i}\widetilde{A}(z^{i})\big).

Letting
\mathcal{C}=\mathrm{Sym}(\mathcal{A})
, we have

(18)

\mathcal{C}_{n}\simeq n!\times\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n},

so that asymptotically uniform random sampling in
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
reduces to asymptotically uniform random sampling in
\mathcal{C}_{n}
, as recalled in Section
2
. Let
(\sigma,\gamma)\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
, with
\gamma\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
and
\sigma
an automorphism of
\gamma
.
Following
[
29
]
, one can consider the
core
of
(\sigma,\gamma)
as the subtree (containing the root-vertex) formed by the vertices that are fixed by
\sigma
.

Figure 2
.

For each vertex
v
of the core, let
\tau_{v}
be the subtree made of
v
and the subtrees hanging from the unfixed children (if any) of
v
.
Letting
\mathcal{B}
be the subclass of
\mathcal{C}
such that the root-vertex is the only fixed vertex, one thus has (see Figure
2
)

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B}.

This is reflected at the level of generating functions. Indeed, the equation for
\widetilde{A}(z)
is equivalent to

\widetilde{A}(z)=\Big(z\exp\big(\sum_{i\geq 2}\frac{1}{i}\widetilde{A}(z^{i})\big)\Big)\exp(\widetilde{A}(z)),

hence one has
\widetilde{A}(z)=A(B(z))
, where
B(z)=z\exp\big(\sum_{i\geq 2}\frac{1}{i}\widetilde{A}(z^{i})\big)
.
Moreover (
18
) ensures that
\widetilde{A}(z)
equals the exponential generating function
C(z)
of
\mathcal{C}
. And Pólya theory ensures that
B(z)
is the exponential generating function of
\mathcal{B}
; indeed the cycle index sum of sets endowed with a permutation with no fixed point is
\exp(\sum_{i\geq 2}\frac{1}{i}s_{i})
.

The composition scheme is known to be admissible, with
\rho_{C}\approx 0.338
and
\rho_{B}=\rho_{C}^{1/2}
.
The Boltzmann sampler
\Gamma B(x)
(to be called at
\rho_{C}
in the leap generation process) is deduced from the known (ordinary) Boltzmann sampler
\Gamma\widetilde{A}(x)
for Pólya trees
[

]
. It is shown in Figure

4.2.2.
Unlabeled phylogenetic trees

A phylogenetic tree is a rooted binary tree such that the two children at each node are unordered. For convenience, the tree with a single leaf is also included in the class; the size
n
is the number of leaves.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be the class of labeled (at leaves) phylogenetic trees, given by

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{Z}+\mathrm{Set}_{2}(\mathcal{A}),\ \ \ A(z)=z+\frac{1}{2}A(z)^{2},\ \ \ |\mathcal{A}_{n}|=(2n-3)!!,

for which uniform random sampling at size
n
can be done in linear time (e.g. by Prüfer codes, or Rémy’s algorithm).
Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
be the associated unlabeled class, given by

\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{Z}+\mathrm{MSet}_{2}(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}),\ \ \ \widetilde{A}(z)=z+\frac{1}{2}\big(\widetilde{A}(z)^{2}+\widetilde{A}(z^{2})\big)

To obtain a leap generator for
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
one can proceed similarly to Pólya trees.
We consider the labeled class
\mathcal{C}=\mathrm{Sym}(\mathcal{A})
, related to
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
by
\mathcal{C}_{n}\simeq n!\times\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
. For
(\sigma,\gamma)\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
we note that
\sigma
naturally extends to a permutation of the nodes, so that
\sigma
can be seen as a permutation on the vertex-set of
\gamma
. As before, the
core
of
\gamma
is the subtree
\tau
formed by the vertices that are fixed by
\sigma
. Letting
\mathcal{B}
be the subclass of
\mathcal{C}
such that the root-vertex is the only fixed vertex, we note that each leaf
v
of
\tau
has a (possibly trivial) hanging subtree in
\mathcal{B}
. So the core-decomposition yields

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B}.

Again this is reflected at the generating function level: the series
\widetilde{A}(z)
is the exponential generating function of
\mathcal{C}
, and the above equation for
\widetilde{A}(z)
rewrites as
\widetilde{A}(z)=A(B(z))
, where
A(z)=z+\frac{1}{2}A(z)^{2}
and
B(z):=z+\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{A}(z^{2})
is the exponential generating function of
\mathcal{B}
.

The composition scheme is known to be admissible, with
\rho_{C}\approx 0.403
and
\rho_{B}=\rho_{C}^{1/2}
. The Boltzmann sampler for
\mathcal{B}
is deduced from the known
[
18
]
Boltzmann sampler
\Gamma\tilde{A}(x)
for
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
. It is shown in Figure
4
.

4.2.3.
Unlabeled
k
-ary mobiles

A
mobile
is a rooted tree where the children of each node are cyclically ordered.
For fixed
k\geq 2
, let
\mathcal{A}
be the class of
k
-ary mobiles with labeled leaves (the size being the number of leaves), which is given by

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{Z}+\mathrm{Cyc}_{k}(\mathcal{A}),\ \ \ A(z)=z+\frac{1}{k}A(z)^{k},

with the following explicit expression for the counting coefficients:

|\mathcal{A}_{n}|=\frac{(m+n-1)!}{m!k^{m}}\ \mathrm{for}\ m:=\frac{n-1}{k-1}\in\mathbb{N},\ \ \ |\mathcal{A}_{n}|=0\ \mathrm{for}\ n\neq 1\ \mathrm{mod}\ k-1.

Note that this represents an extension of the previous section, since the case
k=2
gives phylogenetic trees (indeed, for
k=2
the
\mathrm{Cyc}_{k}
construction is the same as
\mathrm{Set}_{k}
).

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
be the associated unlabeled class, given by (where
\mathrm{Cyc}_{k}
is the construction for unlabeled classes)

\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{Z}+\mathrm{Cyc}_{k}(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}),\ \ \ \widetilde{A}(z)=z+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{d|k}\phi(d)\widetilde{A}(z^{d}),

with
\phi(.)
the Euler totient function.
As before we consider the labeled class
\mathcal{C}=\mathrm{Sym}(\mathcal{A})
, and for
(\sigma,\gamma)\in\mathcal{C}
, extend
\sigma
to the whole vertex-set of
\gamma
, and define the core as the subtree made of the vertices (nodes or leaves) that are fixed under
\sigma
. Letting
\mathcal{B}
be the subclass of
\mathcal{C}
such that the root is the only fixed vertex, we have

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B}.

again reflected at the generating function level by the fact that
C(z):=\widetilde{A}(z)
is the exponential generating function of
\mathcal{C}
,
and
B(z):=z+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{d>1,d|k}\phi(d)\widetilde{A}(z^{d})
is the exponential generating function of
\mathcal{B}
.

Remark 8
.

For
k\geq 3
we have not succeeded in applying a similar strategy to the class
\mathcal{A}
of rooted non-embedded
k
-ary trees, which is given by

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{Z}+\mathrm{Set}_{k}(\mathcal{A}),\ \ \ A(z)=z+\frac{1}{k!}A(z)^{k},

The unlabeled class
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
is specified by

\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{Z}+\mathrm{MSet}_{k}(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}),\ \ \ \widetilde{A}(z)=P_{k}(\widetilde{A}(z),\ldots,\widetilde{A}(z^{k})),

where

P_{k}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})=[u^{k}]\exp\Big(\sum_{i\geq 1}\tfrac{1}{i}u^{i}s_{i}\Big).

For instance for
k=3
, letting
C(z)=A^{(3)}(z)
, we have

C(z)=z+\frac{1}{6}C(z)^{3}+\frac{1}{2}C(z)C(z^{2})+\frac{1}{3}C(z^{3}).

The term involving
C(z)C(z^{2})

makes it difficult to write
C(z)
as
A(B(z))
with
\rho_{B}>\rho_{C}
and the coefficients of
A(z)
having a simple closed form (or at least such that the functional inverse of
A(z)
is rational).

4.2.4.
Unlabeled Schröder mobiles

A
Schröder mobile
is a mobile with no node having one child; the size is the number of leaves. The class
\mathcal{A}
of Schröder mobiles that are labeled at leaves is given by

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{Z}+\mathrm{Cyc}_{\geq 2}(\mathcal{A}),\ \ A(z)=z+\log\Big(\frac{1}{1-A(z)}\Big)-A(z).

There is no simple closed formula for the counting coefficients, but the equation for
A(z)
can be
written as
A(z)=z\Phi(A(z))
, with
\Phi(y)=\frac{1}{1-\tfrac{1}{y}\big(\log(1/(1-y))-y\big)}
. Thus the class
\mathcal{A}
fits into the setting of Galton-Watson trees for random generation. Devroye’s algorithm
[
15
]
allows to sample uniformly at random in
\mathcal{A}_{n}
in linear time.

The associated unlabeled class
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
is given by (with
\mathrm{Cyc}_{\geq 2}
the construction for unlabeled classes)

\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{Z}+\mathrm{Cyc}_{\geq 2}(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}),\ \ \widetilde{A}(z)=z+\sum_{d\geq 1}\frac{\phi(d)}{d}\log\Big(\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{A}(z^{d})}\Big)-\widetilde{A}(z).

Again one can consider the symmetry class
\mathcal{C}=\mathrm{Sym}(\mathcal{A})
, the core of an object
(\sigma,\gamma)\in\mathcal{C}
; and letting
\mathcal{B}
be the subclass of
\mathcal{C}
such that the root is the only fixed vertex, one has
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B}
. The equation for
\widetilde{A}(z)
reflects this decomposition:
C(z):=\widetilde{A}(z)
is the exponential generating function of
\mathcal{C}
, and
B(z):=z+\sum_{d\geq 2}\frac{\phi(d)}{d}\log\Big(\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{A}(z^{d})}\Big)
is the exponential generating function of
\mathcal{B}
.

4.3.
Planar maps

A planar map, shortly called a map hereafter, is a connected graph embedded on the sphere, considered up to continuous deformation. It is rooted by marking and orienting an edge that is called the root.
In this section we show that leap generators can be successfully applied to known composition schemes relating map families.

4.3.1.
Planar maps decomposed into blocks

We demonstrate the strategy for maps decomposed into blocks, before discussing extensions in Section
4.3.2
. A map is called 2-connected if its edge-set
E
can not be partitioned as
E=E_{1}\cup E_{2}

4.3.2.
Extensions

Obtaining a leap generator for random maps is of minor interest, as there is a known exact-size bijective random generator for rooted maps of linear time complexity
[
33
]
. For presentation purpose we chose to give the analysis of this case in detail. Leap generators can actually be applied to any critical map composition scheme
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B}
where there is an exact-size linear time generator for the core-class
\mathcal{A}
, and a Boltzmann sampler for
\mathcal{B}
of linear complexity in the size of the output (in general such a sampler for
\mathcal{B}
is derived from the composition scheme and the exact-size sampler for
\mathcal{A}
). Letting

\mu=\frac{\rho B^{\prime}(\rho)}{B(\rho)},\ \ \ c=\mu\Big(\frac{b_{1}}{3b_{3/2}}\Big)^{2/3},

where
B(z)=b_{0}-b_{1}(1-z/\rho)+b_{3/2}(1-z/\rho)^{3/2}+O\big((1-z/\rho)^{2}\big)
,
the same analysis will give
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{n},\pi_{n}^{\prime})\sim d/n^{1/3}
, for

d=\frac{7\mu}{4c}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{map}}(x)|x|\mathrm{d}x.

One can thus obtain leap generators for composition schemes where no bijective linear-time exact-size sampler for
\mathcal{C}
is known. For instance, if we require 2-connected blocks to have at least two edges, then the composed class
\mathcal{C}
is made of maps that are bridgeless and loopless.
As another extension, one can consider the model of block-weighted planar maps
[
12
,
37
]
, where each map is given a weight
u^{\mathrm{\#blocks}}
. It has been recently studied in detail
[
21
]
, showing a phase transition at
u=5/9
: the composition scheme is critical for
u<5/9
and subcritical for
u>5/9
. For
u<5/9
, we can thus obtain leap generators of linear time complexity.
Finally, the approach can also be applied to cubic planar graphs, where
\mathcal{A}
is the class of rooted 3-connected cubic planar graphs
5
5

5

Precisely, one restricts to the class
\mathcal{C}
of vertex-rooted cubic planar graphs whose root-vertex is on a 3-connected component. Upon unrooting, the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
induces a distribution on cubic planar graphs of size
n
that is asymptotically uniform, at total variation distance
O(n^{-1/2})
from the uniform distribution.
.
By Whitney’s theorem and duality, this class is in bijection with rooted simple triangulations, for which a bijective random generator is known
[
31
]
.
