A comprehensive study on cervical cancer diagnosis using deep learning and artificial intelligence techniques

Nilasree Kannadoss, Kumar Rangasamy

Abstract

Cervical cancer is a significant health problem across the globe, especially in nations with low or middle incomes, where access to early screening is limited and contributes to high mortality rates. Traditional diagnostic methods, including Papanicolaou smears (Pap smears) and Human Papillomavirus testing (HPV testing), often suffer from accessibility constraints, lower accuracy, and scalability issues. Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) have exhibited significant efficacy by improving detection accuracy and streamlining the diagnostic procedure. This review discusses AI-driven methodologies, including deep learning models (EfficientNetB0, Cervical Net), hybrid frameworks, and ensemble techniques. This study examines important obstacles related to class imbalance, data privacy, and model interpretability while also presenting a solution known as the Adaptive Synthetic Minority Over-Sampling Technique combined with Tomek Links (Adaptive SMOTE Tomek) for data augmentation and federated learning (FL) for secure, distributed AI model training. The outcomes emphasize AI’s vital role in improving cervical cancer screening and correspond with the World Health Organization’s (WHO) 2030 goals for cervical cancer eradication. Future research should focus on real-world validation, improved generalization, and the integration of AI in clinical settings to ensure widespread usage and reliability. This work is useful for medical professionals in cervical cancer diagnosis, in addition to traditional screening methods.

Introduction

Cervical cancer, a treatable and preventable condition when detected early, is a threat worldwide as a result of infection with high-risk strains of human (HPV) . It ranks as one of the top four cancers among women globally, with the World Health Organization (WHO) documenting approximately 604,000 new cases and 342,000 fatalities in the year 2022. The management of cervical cancer is a significant concern in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), accounting for 87% of all cervical cancer worldwide, . They comprise poor health system infrastructure, insufficient screening activities, low health literacy, and cultural practices discouraging women from reporting to screening programs. Screening is crucial because cervical cancer progresses from pre-malignant dysplasia to invasive cancerous lesions over several years. HIV-positive women have a significantly higher incidence of cervical cancer . There is a need for enhanced and more regular screening of HIV-positive women, which is difficult due to the lack of resources, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) . However, insufficiently trained human resources, inconsistencies in quality assurance and delivery, and logistical challenges hinder their application in developing countries. Using artificial intelligence (AI)-based diagnostic apps as an example, new prospects for enhanced screen performance, accuracy, and coverage have been incorporated in contemporary medicine today, . These technologies employ analysis of images and algorithmic learning to identify anomalies and reduce reliance on the human angle on such matters. However, there are some challenges when implementing such technologies, such as high costs, scarce availability of annotated datasets, or resistance to incorporating the computer systems in the list of clinical workflows . To address these challenges, we propose interventions such as awareness campaigns in public health, infrastructure development, and scaling up new solutions tailored to low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Global commitment to intensifying cervical cancer prevention, screening, and treatment is necessary for promoting an equitable target for eliminating cervical cancer, which has been set by the World Health Organization (WHO) to be achieved by 2030 .

Numerous challenges are associated with the early detection of cervical cancer, and their existence can result in difficulties during the process of the design and development of powerful diagnostic tools . It is significant to point out one fundamental limitation in medical databases: class distributions are highly imbalanced, where benign cases greatly outnumber malignant cases. This is a situation likely to result in the biasing of the machine learning models towards the majority class, thus having a significant impact on sensitivity and specificity in classifying minority cases . Consequently, malignant cases are likely to be misclassified, thus putting patients at risk of poor prognosis through delayed diagnosis. In addition, the issue of data privacy is a problem, as health-related data involves individual information that is protected by law, such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). The laws complicate decentralization in medical data, and this presents numerous challenges in accessing and exchanging the large-scale data required in the effective training of machine learning . In addition, architectures in decentralized data settings present challenges in standardization, thus introducing variability that can be detrimental to the performance of models. Further, staging of cervical cancer is highly challenging due to the faint difference between cancerous and precancerous cytologic changes in the cervix . Conventional supervised machine learning models have poor ability in transferring learning between different datasets due to their high dimensionality in general as well as variability in medical images . This is further compounded by the lack of data with sufficient annotation due to the time-consuming process of annotating medical images and the need to engage healthcare workers . The second challenge is the model’s interpretability, an important requirement in medicine where the underlying reason for the decision should be explained and understandable to doctors and other health workers . Solving such complex problems requires special techniques to facilitate management of biased data, protect the privacy of users, and improve the performance of classification models .

The article presents the state-of-the-art advancements in detecting and classifying cervical cancer, with an enabling role served by artificial intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML). Such technologies assume prime importance in improving the accuracy of diagnosis and screening efficiency, a prime cause of cancer mortality among women. Traditional techniques such as Papanicolaou smears (Pap smears), Human Papillomavirus (HPV) testing, and Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid (VIA) have been effective but have lower sensitivity, specificity, and scale. AI and ML, contrastingly, hold revolutionary promise: the ability to use large-scale datasets to train models that could identify abnormalities with high accuracy. Advanced AI models such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), transformers, and hybrid models are superior in feature extraction and multi-class classification and can efficiently process imbalanced datasets. In addition, ML algorithms such as random forests, Support Vector Machines (SVMs), and ensemble methods also contribute to early detection by analyzing the risk factors and cytological patterns. Such technologies reduce the need for specialized people in their deployment. The research study also enlists challenges such as data privacy, unavailability of annotated data, and seamless integration into clinical workflows. It mentions upcoming directions on federated learning (FL) as a solution to achieve private model training and adaptive sampling techniques to handle class imbalances. The article enlightens such advancements in favour of AI/ML for attaining the World Health Organization’s (WHO’s) goal of eliminating cervical cancer as a public health problem by 2030. describes the different types of screening methods, their symptoms, and advanced methods of cervical cancer screening.

Research objectives

- To evaluate the effectiveness of artificial intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) techniques in improving the results and performance of cervical cancer detection and classification than traditional diagnostic methods.
- To identify the key challenges in implementing artificial intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) technologies in cervical cancer screening, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).
- To explore the potential of AI-driven approaches in overcoming issues related to data privacy, class imbalance, and model interpretability in cervical cancer detection.

Research questions

- How do artificial intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML)-based diagnostic tools compare to traditional methods (such as Papanicolaou smears (Pap smears) and Human Papillomavirus (HPV) testing) in terms of accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity for detecting cervical cancer?
- What are the primary barriers to the adoption and successful integration of artificial intelligence (AI)-based diagnostic tools in cervical cancer screening programs in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs)?
- How can artificial intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) techniques address the challenges of class imbalance, data privacy concerns, and model interpretability in cervical cancer detection, and what impact do these solutions have on model performance in real-world clinical settings?

Research methodology

The research uses a Systematic Literature Review (SLR) to carefully look at the latest methods for detecting and classifying cervical cancer, focusing on how Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) can help, while also considering privacy through federated learning and addressing data imbalance using the Adaptive Synthetic Minority Over-Sampling Technique (SMOTE) Tomek. We methodically selected peer-reviewed studies from the Scopus, Springer, IEEE Xplore, and Elsevier databases between 2021 and 2024. We employed keywords such as “Cervical Cancer Detection,” “Deep Learning,” and “Machine Learning” to guide the search, resulting in the retrieval of a focused dataset of 63 studies. The review process followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines to make sure everything was clear, starting from 920 records and narrowing down to relevant and high-quality peer-reviewed studies. Assessments were provided for methodology, performance measures like accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, and scalability. We prioritized the selected literature to ensure its focus on enhancing early cervical cancer detection and classification. This review is in line with the latest PRISMA 2020 guidelines of systematic review and meta-analysis reporting. It examines ML DL hybrid diagnostic systems for identifying cervical cancer, highlighting privacy preserving (federated learning) and imbalance handling techniques (Adaptive SMOTE-Tomek).

Systematic review process

Our systematic review process was conducted with methodological rigor and complete disclosure, in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The search in the database yielded 920 records, we took out 210 duplicates. Next, we examined 710 full texts on their titles and abstract level. After the first step, about 370 papers were rejected as they were not directly related to machine learning and deep learning applications for detecting and classifying cervical cancer.

In the next phase, we checked the content of all documents thoroughly to find out if they fit the scope of our project and if they were scientifically, and clinically accurate. The reasons why 277 studies have been removed can be listed as; 142 used neither machine learning nor deep learning techniques, 85 papers did not tackle class imbalance and/or private architectures, the remaining 50 provided either no detailed validation or performance indicators. At the end of the process 63 well-written, high-quality articles have been selected in line with all the required criteria. They were the basis for a series of studies including: ML\DL workflow improvements, privacy-preserving structures, and class imbalance strategies (see ).

Methodological quality and critical appraisal framework

To check the technical validity and possible risk of bias in all the included studies (N = 63), a structured 5-domain critical appraisal checklist for medical AI and digital diagnostic systems was used by two independent researchers to appraise each paper along five important critical analytical areas and the levels of concerns were rated as low, some, or high/unclear:

Domain 1 (D1) Evidence/Data Source: This domain evaluates the reliability of the dataset and the suitability of the sample size. One key factor is that ground-truth data used to train the models (like Pap smear images, whole-slide images, or CT scans) should also be clinically or pathologically verified. Finally, the demographic distribution of the dataset should be transparently described.

Domain 2 (D2) Methodological Transparency: This domain checks to see if one can reproduce the algorithm by simply using the code available. The network architecture should be clearly described, with hyperparameters, loss functions, and pre-processing steps.

Domain 3 (D3) Validation/Evidence Appraisal: This domain evaluates the validation protocol: whether a training set, a validation set, and a testing set can be clearly distinguished or whether a testing set is independent of the training/validation set. It should also be mentioned whether cross-validation is used, and if so, whether it is a good design. Finally, it is checked and reported that there is no leakage in the data when data augmentation is performed, or sampling is split.

Domain 4 (D4) Evaluation/Outcome Assessment: This domain checks whether all the metrics that are typically used in evaluating a diagnostic algorithm are included in the work or if the selection of some metrics might have been made simply because they were reported earlier in the field and not because they best served the purpose. For example, metrics like Accuracy Sensitivity Specificity, ROC-AUC, F1-score, and Dice Similarity Coefficient have to be reported in a complete manner to assess the effectiveness of the method.

Domain 5 (D5) Reporting/Limitations / Applicability: This domain evaluates how clearly and comprehensively the authors acknowledge the shortcomings of their study, the clinical context or setting that inspired their work or in which their system is expected to be applied, and the practical deployment constraints of their system like hardware limitations, computational demands, and user interaction.

Out of the 63 studies reviewed, 35 (55.6%) were overall low concern papers, as they were accompanied by very good validation procedures and open descriptions of their pipeline. The other 28 papers (44.4%) either raised some concerns or lacked clarity in certain areas, usually because operational limitations had not been disclosed in Domain 5 or the external validation was not reported sufficiently in Domain 3. contains the critical appraisal summary across reviewed papers.

Smote TOMEK for handling class imbalances

The cervical cancer dataset suffers from severe class imbalances, which contain around 96% of non-cancerous data and only 4% of cancerous. This serious issue can lead the model trained with this data to classify inappropriately, resulting in poor detection. SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) addresses this issue by integrating both oversampling and under sampling techniques. The SMOTE technique helps in classification by creating synthetic samples for the minority class data by interpolation between an instance and its nearest neighbors. We discover and eliminate Tomek links—pairs of examples from opposing classes that are in close proximity—after adding the synthetic samples. This stage aids in eliminating noisy or marginal samples and minimizes class overlap. This results in enhanced training data, which enhances the classifier’s potential to identify more accurate boundaries among the two categories . k: Number of closest neighbours (often 5)Output:X_augmented: Inclusion of synthetic instances to minority class samplesProcedure:1. For each sample xi in X_min:
 1.1 Identify the k nearest neighbours of xi within X_min utilising Euclidean distance. Select n neighbors at random from the k neighbours according to the specified oversampling ratio.
2. For each chosen neighbour xj:
 2.1 Create a synthetic sample x_syn as outlined below: x_syn = xi + random(0,1) * (xj − xi), where random(0, 1) denotes a random number within the interval [0, 1].
3. Repeat steps 1–2 until a cumulative total of N synthetic samples is generated.
4. Integrate all generated x_syn into the original minority class set to create the final augmented dataset X_augmented.

RQ3. How can AI and ML techniques address the challenges of class imbalance, data privacy concerns, and model interpretability in cervical cancer detection, and what impact do these solutions have on model performance in real-world clinical settings?

The application of AI and ML can help solve three primary issues related to the utility of sensitive diagnostic tools in cervical cancer screening, namely class imbalance and privacy. Class imbalance is a situation where a data set contains few malignant cases while the models are inclined to relapse on the dominant classes instead of the minority ones. Adaptive SMOTE Tomek is a strong technique to handle this problem by combining synthetic data generation for underrepresented classes with noise reduction for mislabelled or ambiguous samples. Privacy concerns can be addressed by federated learning, which is a decentralized model training framework that eliminates the need for data centralization. This will work in a way to respect regulations like GDPR and exploit more diverse datasets towards more generalized models. Benchmarks for testing and medical photography modalities

The diagnostic capability and versatility of artificial intelligence (AI) tools in healthcare are highly contingent on the structure and integrity of the datasets from which they are derived. Most studies reported here use public, standardized benchmark repositories with private clinical cohorts: Standard, Publicly Available Datasets: Majorly, cell-level identification algorithms have been trained with publicly available reference data like Herlev, which includes $2,010$images, SIPaKMeD with 4,049images, and even CRIC with 11,551images. Even though, these sets provide standardized answers, their limited sample sizes, and single-pre-segmented cell compositions do not represent well, the real-life conditions such as noise and multiple-cell backgrounds that are commonly seen in routine liquid-based cytology (LBC) or conventional Pap smear preparations. Clinical Whole-Slide Images (WSI) and Colposcopy: Those works that involve the use of whole Slide image scanning (WSI) or cervicography/colposcopy data may have more practical usefulness but they are plagued by class imbalances at a serious level. Only very small proportions of the patients with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN2+) are detected in cervical cancer screening programs thereby introducing extremely uneven class proportions which negatively affect performance of classifiers that are not corrected for the class distributions.

Critical appraisal of validation methods and data leakage

This methodological review identified some levels of validation malignancy in the synthesized literature: Validation protocols: More than 65% of the primary research in the review only employed k-Fold or Stratified K-Fold validation modalities on single Source datasets. Although indispensable for model hyperparameter optimization, single-cohort validation will often report overoptimistic performance measures owing to known background features in a particular scanner, staining protocol, clinic population and so on. Pre-processing data leakage: Quality appraisal Domain 3 (Validation Appraisal) illuminated the frequency of data leakage during pre-processing: a minority of studies used synthetic oversampling protocols (e.g., SMOTE) or image transformations before dataset partitioning into train-test files, allowing the validation fold to absorb synthetic distortions created from the test set distribution. This compromises validation results, vastly inflating accuracy measures. External and Multi-center validation: only a minority of studies employed independent external validation on other hospital cohorts or multi-scanner test beds. Absent external cross-site validation, claims that AI exceeds experienced cytotechnologists, or traditional work flows in large biopsy closed populations, are unsubstantiated.

Completeness of evaluation metrics

A fatal flaw detected during the review: a general dependence on Global Accuracy measures for performance summaries: the accuracy paradox in healthcare data: in imbalanced cervical screening databases, where CIN+ diagnoses occur in less than 5% of samples, a formal model guessing all negatives still achieves over 95% accuracy, but fails to identify detecting CIN+ lesions altogether. Comprehensive metric requirements: There can be no rigorous diagnostic evaluation without cross-equal reporting of Sensitivity (Recall) Specificity Positive Predictive Value, False Negative Rate and area under the ROC curve. Screening models must achieve high sensitivity in the absence the many false negatives would dominate a final cost–benefit calculation, while secondary triaging guides require high specificity.

Methodological limitations and real-world implementation

Promised computational processes becoming part of working systems bring along several challenges:

High computing requirements versus edge deployment

Models with Vision Transformers (ViTs) or deep CNNs have a large number of parameters and because of this a very high need for GPU computing power which makes it impossible to deploy such systems in low-resourced small clinics—the very places where a point-of-care digital screening would be most useful.

Privacy-preserving methods (federated learning)

Decentralized approaches like federated learning (FL) that allow multiple organizations to train a single medical AI while keeping their data private are a promising way to address issues like data scarcity and privacy. Yet, practical deployment of FL is hindered by communication costs, clinical data that is non-independently and identically distributed (non-IID) across sites, and vulnerabilities to membership inference attacks.

Class imbalance solutions (Adaptive SMOTE-Tomek)

Techniques which combine noise removal and data augmentation in minority class such as Adaptive SMOTE-Tomek were found to A lot reduce the dominance of the majority class. Though, one has to be very careful while using such techniques not to interfere with the diagnosis.
