First-Order Optimality Conditions for Mathematical Programming with Equilibrium Constraints
Louis Shuo Wang

Abstract
We present a systematic introduction to first-order optimality conditions for mathematical programs with equilibrium constraints (MPECs), emphasizing the limitations of classical nonlinear programming techniques. The goal is twofold. First, we explain why a direct application of standard optimality conditions—based on reformulating MPECs via KKT systems or differentiable exact penalty functions—is often inadequate, as such approaches typically require strong and restrictive assumptions, including nondegeneracy and smoothness conditions. Second, we develop a first-principles framework for analyzing MPECs by focusing on the geometric structure of the feasible region. In particular, we study stationarity concepts and provide a detailed characterization of the tangent cone at feasible points, which leads to appropriate constraint qualifications tailored to MPECs. These results form the foundation for rigorous first-order analysis and clarify the relationship between the original MPEC formulation and its KKT-based representation, offering practical guidance for handling these inherently challenging optimization problems.



1
Introduction

A mathematical program with equilibrium constraints (MPEC) is an
optimization problem where the upper-level variables are constrained by the
solution set of a lower-level variational inequality or complementarity
problem. Such models arise in engineering design, network equilibrium,
contact mechanics, economics, and hierarchical control.

A central message of these notes is that a naive KKT reformulation of the
lower-level equilibrium system can obscure the true local geometry of the
MPEC feasible set. In particular, the tangent cone is generally nonconvex,
so the standard nonlinear programming linearization is not the right
first-order object.

2
Background Prerequisites for Chapter 3

This section collects the minimum optimization background needed to read
Chapter 3 on first-order optimality conditions for MPECs. The goal is not
to develop full general theory, but to explain the objects that appear in
the chapter and why they matter.

2.1
Vectors, gradients, Jacobians, and Hessians

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a vector of variables and let

\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be differentiable.

Definition 2.1

(Gradient)
.

The gradient of
\phi
at
x
is

\nabla\phi(x)=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{1}}(x)\\
\vdots\\
\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{n}}(x)\end{bmatrix}.

It gives the first-order change of
\phi
:

\phi(x+d)\approx\phi(x)+\nabla\phi(x)^{T}d.

Definition 2.2

(Jacobian)
.

If
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is differentiable, its Jacobian is the
matrix

\nabla g(x)=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla g_{1}(x)^{T}\\
\vdots\\
\nabla g_{m}(x)^{T}\end{bmatrix}.

Each row is the gradient of one component function.

Definition 2.3

(Hessian)
.

If
\phi
is twice differentiable, its Hessian is

\nabla^{2}\phi(x)=\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(x)\right]_{i,j=1}^{n}.

It captures second-order curvature.

Remark 2.4
.

In optimization, first-order theory is based mainly on gradients and
Jacobians. Hessians enter second-order theory.

2.2
Local minimizers and feasible directions

Consider the constrained optimization problem

\min_{x}\ \phi(x)\qquad\text{subject to }x\in\Omega,

where
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the feasible set.

Definition 2.5

(Local minimizer)
.

A point
\bar{x}\in\Omega
is a local minimizer if there exists
\varepsilon>0

such that

\phi(\bar{x})\leq\phi(x)\qquad\forall x\in\Omega\cap B_{\varepsilon}(\bar{x}).

Definition 2.6

(Feasible direction)
.

A vector
d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a feasible direction at
\bar{x}\in\Omega
if
one can move from
\bar{x}
a little in direction
d
while staying feasible,
at least to first order.

The mathematical object that collects all such first-order feasible
directions is the tangent cone.

2.3
Tangent cone and linearized cone

Definition 2.7

(Tangent cone)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\bar{x}\in\Omega
. The tangent cone to

\Omega
at
\bar{x}
is

T(\bar{x};\Omega):=\left\{d:\exists\,x^{k}\in\Omega,\ x^{k}\to\bar{x},\ \exists\,t_{k}\downarrow 0\text{ such that }\frac{x^{k}-\bar{x}}{t_{k}}\to d\right\}.

Remark 2.8
.

The tangent cone contains all first-order directions in which feasibility can
be approached. It is the correct geometric object for first-order necessary
conditions.

If
\bar{x}
is a local minimizer, then no tangent direction can be a descent
direction for the objective.

Theorem 2.9

(Primal first-order necessary condition)
.

If
\bar{x}
is a local minimizer of

\min_{x\in\Omega}\phi(x),

and
\phi
is differentiable, then

\nabla\phi(\bar{x})^{T}d\geq 0\qquad\forall d\in T(\bar{x};\Omega).

In practice,
T(\bar{x};\Omega)
may be hard to compute exactly. For a smooth
nonlinear program

\min_{x}\ \phi(x)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad g_{i}(x)\leq 0,\ i=1,\dots,m,\qquad h_{j}(x)=0,\ j=1,\dots,p,

one often uses the
linearized cone
.

Definition 2.10

(Linearized cone)
.

Let
\bar{x}
be feasible and define the active inequality index set

I(\bar{x}):=\{i:g_{i}(\bar{x})=0\}.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\Omega
be the feasible region for the mathematical program under consideration.
The linearized cone is

\mathcal{L}(\bar{x};\mathcal{F}):=\left\{d\in\mathcal{T}(\bar{x};\Omega):\nabla g_{i}(\bar{x})^{T}d\leq 0\ \forall i\in I(\bar{x}),\,\,\,\nabla h_{j}(\bar{x})^{T}d=0\ \forall j\right\}.

Remark 2.11
.

Always,

T(\bar{x};\mathcal{F})\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\bar{x};\mathcal{F}).

If equality holds, then the abstract tangent-cone condition becomes a
computable linear condition.

2.4
Lagrangian and KKT conditions

For the nonlinear program above, define the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}(x,\lambda,\mu)=\phi(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}g_{i}(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mu_{j}h_{j}(x).

Definition 2.12

(KKT conditions)
.

A feasible point
\bar{x}
satisfies the KKT conditions if there exist
multipliers
\bar{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\bar{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\mathcal{L}(\bar{x},\bar{\lambda},\bar{\mu})

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{(stationarity)},

\displaystyle g_{i}(\bar{x})

\displaystyle\leq 0,\quad i=1,\dots,m\qquad\text{(primal feasibility)},

\displaystyle h_{j}(\bar{x})

\displaystyle=0,\quad j=1,\dots,p\qquad\text{(primal feasibility)},

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}_{i}

\displaystyle\geq 0,\quad i=1,\dots,m\qquad\text{(dual feasibility)},

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}_{i}g_{i}(\bar{x})

\displaystyle=0,\quad i=1,\dots,m\qquad\text{(complementary slackness)}.

Remark 2.13
.

The KKT system is a
primal–dual
form of first-order optimality.
It augments the primal variable
x
with multiplier variables.

2.5
Constraint qualifications

KKT conditions are not automatically valid at every local minimizer. Some
regularity assumption is needed.

Definition 2.14

(Constraint qualification)
.

A constraint qualification (CQ) is an assumption ruling out pathological
degeneracy of the constraints, so that tangent-cone geometry and
multiplier-based conditions behave well.

Two standard examples are LICQ and MFCQ.

Definition 2.15

(LICQ)
.

The linear independence constraint qualification holds at a feasible point
if the gradients of all active inequalities together with the gradients of all
equalities are linearly independent.

Definition 2.16

(MFCQ)
.

The Mangasarian–Fromovitz constraint qualification holds at a feasible
point if the equality gradients are linearly independent and there exists a
direction that preserves equalities while strictly decreasing all active
inequalities.

Remark 2.17
.

Under standard CQs, the tangent cone of an NLP matches the linearized
cone, and KKT conditions hold at local minimizers.

2.6
Variational inequalities

MPECs involve a lower-level equilibrium problem. One standard way to
model equilibrium is through a variational inequality.

Definition 2.18

(Variational inequality)
.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be closed and convex, and let

F:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The variational inequality problem

\mathrm{VI}(F,K)
is to find
y\in K
such that

F(y)^{T}(v-y)\geq 0\qquad\forall v\in K.

Remark 2.19
.

Interpretation: at the equilibrium point
y
, moving to any other feasible
point
v\in K
cannot produce a negative first-order directional change in
the equilibrium map.

Definition 2.20

(Solution set)
.

The solution set of the VI is denoted

\mathrm{SOL}(F,K).

Example 2.21
.

If
K=\mathbb{R}^{m}
, then the VI condition becomes

F(y)=0.

So VIs generalize systems of equations.

Definition 2.22

(Affine variational inequality)
.

If

F(y)=My+q

with matrix
M
and vector
q
, the problem is called an affine
variational inequality (AVI). We use the notations
\mathrm{AVI}(q,M,K)
and
\mathrm{SOL}(q,M,K)
to represent the affine variation problem and its solution set. Here,
K
is the same as in Definition
2.18
.

2.7
Complementarity problems

A very important special case of a VI is a complementarity system.

Definition 2.23

(Nonlinear complementarity problem)
.

Given
F:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the nonlinear complementarity
problem (NCP) is to find
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

y\geq 0,\qquad F(y)\geq 0,\qquad y_{i}F_{i}(y)=0\ \ \forall i.

Remark 2.24
.

For each index
i
, complementarity means:

\text{either }y_{i}=0\text{ or }F_{i}(y)=0,

possibly both. Thus the product condition enforces a switch-like behavior.

Definition 2.25

(Linear complementarity problem)
.

If
F(y)=My+q
, then the NCP becomes a linear complementarity problem
(LCP):

y\geq 0,\qquad My+q\geq 0,\qquad y^{T}(My+q)=0.

Remark 2.26
.

Complementarity systems appear naturally in contact mechanics,
equilibrium networks, market clearing, and KKT systems of optimization
problems.

2.8
From lower-level equilibrium to MPEC

An MPEC combines an upper-level optimization problem with a lower-level
equilibrium problem.

Definition 2.27

(MPEC: informal form)
.

A mathematical program with equilibrium constraints has the form

\min_{x,y}f(x,y)\qquad\text{s.t. }(x,y)\in Z,\quad y\in S(x),

where
S(x)
is the solution set of a lower-level VI or complementarity
problem.

Remark 2.28
.

The variable
x
is an upper-level design/control variable, while
y

must satisfy an equilibrium condition depending on
x
.

At first sight one may think: “replace the lower-level VI by its KKT
conditions and then apply ordinary nonlinear programming theory.” The
main lesson of Chapter 3 is that this is generally too naive.

2.9
Why MPEC is harder than ordinary NLP

For ordinary NLPs, under a suitable CQ, the tangent cone is often a single
polyhedral cone described by linearized constraints. For MPECs, however,
the feasible set may have a kinked or branch-like local structure.

Remark 2.29
.

Near a feasible point, an MPEC feasible set may look like the union of
several smooth pieces. Therefore its tangent cone can be a
union of
polyhedral cones
, and that union is generally nonconvex.

This is exactly why Chapter 3 introduces an MPEC-specific linearized cone
instead of relying on the single-cone NLP picture.

2.10
Polyhedral cones

Definition 2.30

(Cone)
.

A set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a cone if for every
d\in K
and every

\alpha\geq 0
, one has
\alpha d\in K
.

Definition 2.31

(Polyhedral cone)
.

A cone is polyhedral if it can be written as the solution set of finitely many
linear equalities and inequalities.

Example 2.32
.

The nonnegative orthant

\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{i}\geq 0,\ i=1,\dots,n\}

is a polyhedral cone.

Remark 2.33
.

Polyhedral cones are important because optimization over them is much more
tractable than over general nonlinear sets.

2.11
Primal versus primal–dual stationarity

Definition 2.34

(Primal stationarity)
.

A first-order condition written directly in terms of feasible directions,
for example

\nabla f(\bar{x})^{T}d\geq 0\qquad\forall d\in T(\bar{x};\Omega),

is called a primal stationarity condition.

Definition 2.35

(Primal–dual stationarity)
.

A first-order condition written using both primal variables and
multiplier variables is called a primal–dual stationarity condition.
KKT conditions are the standard example.

Remark 2.36
.

Chapter 3 begins with primal stationarity for MPEC and then, under
appropriate MPEC-specific constraint qualifications, derives a more
computable primal–dual form.

2.12
The role of the chapter-specific objects

The following terms in Chapter 3 are specializations of the background
concepts above.

Definition 2.37

(Lower-level multiplier set)
.

The set
M(z)
collects multipliers associated with the KKT system of the
lower-level variational inequality at a feasible point
z=(x,y)
.

Definition 2.38

(Lifted critical cone)
.

The lifted critical cone
K(z,\lambda)
is an MPEC-specific cone built from
active lower-level constraints and a lower-level multiplier
\lambda
. It
refines the usual linearized-cone idea.

Definition 2.39

(Directional critical set)
.

For a fixed upper-level direction
dx
, the set
K(z,\lambda;dx)
collects
the lower-level directions
dy
compatible with that
dx
.

Definition 2.40

(Affine variational inequality arising from linearization)
.

After linearizing the lower-level VI at a feasible point, one obtains an AVI
in the variable
dy
. Solving that AVI describes candidate tangent
directions of the MPEC.

Remark 2.41
.

So the chapter replaces the single linearized cone of ordinary NLP by a
family of cones and AVIs indexed by lower-level multipliers.

3
The MPEC Model and Stationarity

Definition 3.1

(MPEC)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\to\mathbb{R}
, let
Z\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
, and define

C(x):=\{\,y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\ g(x,y)\leq 0\,\}.

Let

S(x):=\operatorname{SOL}(F(x,\cdot),C(x)).

The mathematical program with equilibrium constraints is

\displaystyle\min_{x,y}

\displaystyle f(x,y)

(1)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle(x,y)\in Z,

\displaystyle y\in S(x).

Its feasible set is denoted by
\mathcal{F}
.

Definition 3.2

(Multiplier set)
.

For a feasible point
z=(x,y)\in\mathcal{F}
, define the active index set

\mathcal{I}(z):=\{\,i:\ g_{i}(z)=0\,\}.

The multiplier set of the lower-level variational inequality is

M(z):=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}:F(z)+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\lambda_{i}\nabla_{y}g_{i}(z)=0,\ \lambda\geq 0,\ g(z)\leq 0,\ \lambda^{T}g(z)=0\right\}.

Definition 3.3

(Stationary point)
.

A feasible point
\bar{z}=(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathcal{F}
is called a

stationary point
of (
1
) if

\nabla_{x}f(\bar{z})^{T}dx+\nabla_{y}f(\bar{z})^{T}dy\geq 0\qquad\forall(dx,dy)\in T(\bar{z};\mathcal{F}),

where
T(\bar{z};\mathcal{F})
is the tangent cone to the feasible set.

Remark 3.4
.

Every local minimizer of the MPEC is stationary in the above sense.

We give an example to show why the standard NLP viewpoint fails.

Example 3.5

(Nonconvex tangent cone)
.

Consider an MPEC whose feasible region near
\bar{z}
is the union of two
smooth branches. Then

T(\bar{z};\mathcal{F})=K_{1}\cup K_{2}

for two polyhedral cones
K_{1},K_{2}
, so the tangent cone is nonconvex.
This already shows that a single convex NLP-type linearized cone cannot
capture the correct first-order geometry.

3.1
The Tangent Cone and the MPEC Linearization

Fix a feasible point
\bar{z}=(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mathcal{F}
.

Definition 3.6

(SBCQ)
.

The
sequence boundedness constraint qualification
(SBCQ) holds at

\bar{z}
if for every sequence
\{(x^{k},y^{k})\}\subset\mathcal{F}
converging to

\bar{z}
, there exists for each
k
a multiplier
\lambda^{k}\in M(x^{k},y^{k})

such that the sequence
\{\lambda^{k}\}
is bounded.

Definition 3.7

(VI Lagrangian)
.

The Lagrangian of the lower-level variational inequality is

L(z,\lambda):=F(z)+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\lambda_{i}\nabla_{y}g_{i}(z),\qquad(z,\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m+\ell}.

Definition 3.8

(Lifted critical cone)
.

For
\lambda\in M(\bar{z})
, define the
lifted critical cone

\mathcal{K}(\bar{z},\lambda):=\left\{(dx,dy)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}:\begin{array}[]{l}dx^{T}\nabla_{x}g_{i}(\bar{z})+dy^{T}\nabla_{y}g_{i}(\bar{z})\leq 0,\quad i\in\mathcal{I}(\bar{z}),\ \lambda_{i}=0,\\[3.00003pt]
dx^{T}\nabla_{x}g_{i}(\bar{z})+dy^{T}\nabla_{y}g_{i}(\bar{z})=0,\quad i\in\mathcal{I}(\bar{z}),\ \lambda_{i}>0\end{array}\right\}.

Definition 3.9

(Directional critical set)
.

For
dx\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda\in M(\bar{z})
, define

\mathcal{K}(\bar{z},\lambda;dx):=\{\,dy\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:(dx,dy)\in\mathcal{K}(\bar{z},\lambda)\,\}.

Definition 3.10

(Set-valued linearization map)
.

For each
\lambda\in M(\bar{z})
, define

\mathscr{L}\mathscr{S}_{(\bar{z},\lambda)}(dx):=\operatorname{SOL}\bigl(\nabla_{x}L(\bar{z},\lambda)\,dx,\,\nabla_{y}L(\bar{z},\lambda),\,\mathcal{K}(\bar{z},\lambda;dx)\bigr).

Definition 3.11

(MPEC linearized cone)
.

The
MPEC linearized cone
at
\bar{z}
is

\mathcal{L}(\bar{z};\mathcal{F}):=T(\bar{z};Z)\cap\left(\bigcup_{\lambda\in M(\bar{z})}\operatorname{Gr}(\mathscr{L}\mathscr{S}_{(\bar{z},\lambda)})\right).

Remark 3.12
.

The cone
\mathcal{L}(\bar{z};\mathcal{F})
is generally nonconvex. In many cases it is a
union of finitely many polyhedral cones.

Lemma 3.13

(Tangent directions induce an affine VI)
.

Assume SBCQ holds at
\bar{z}\in\mathcal{F}
. If

dz=(dx,dy)\in T(\bar{z};\mathcal{F}),

then
dz\in T(\bar{z};Z)
and there exists a multiplier

\bar{\lambda}\in M(\bar{z})
such that
dy
solves the AVI

\operatorname{AVI}\bigl(\nabla_{x}L(\bar{z},\bar{\lambda})dx,\,\nabla_{y}L(\bar{z},\bar{\lambda}),\,\mathcal{K}(\bar{z},\bar{\lambda};dx)\bigr).

Equivalently,

T(\bar{z};\mathcal{F})\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\bar{z};\mathcal{F}).

Proof sketch.

Take a tangent sequence
z^{k}=(x^{k},y^{k})\in\mathcal{F}
converging to
\bar{z}

with difference quotients converging to
(dx,dy)
. By SBCQ one may
choose bounded multipliers
\lambda^{k}\in M(z^{k})
; after extracting a
subsequence,
\lambda^{k}\to\bar{\lambda}\in M(\bar{z})
. First-order expansion
of the active constraints yields
(dx,dy)\in\mathcal{K}(\bar{z},\bar{\lambda})
, and
first-order expansion of the VI equation yields the stated AVI.
∎

Definition 3.14

(Extreme-point cone)
.

Let
M^{e}(\bar{z})
denote the set of extreme points of
M(\bar{z})
. Define

\mathcal{L}^{e}(\bar{z};\mathcal{F}):=T(\bar{z};Z)\cap\left(\bigcup_{\lambda\in M^{e}(\bar{z})}\operatorname{Gr}(\mathscr{L}\mathscr{S}_{(\bar{z},\lambda)})\right).

Proposition 3.15
.

If CRCQ holds at
\bar{z}\in\mathcal{F}
, then

T(\bar{z};\mathcal{F})\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{e}(\bar{z};\mathcal{F}).

If, in addition,
T(\bar{z};Z)
is polyhedral, then

\mathcal{L}^{e}(\bar{z};\mathcal{F})
is the union of finitely many polyhedral cones.

3.2
Critical Multipliers and Directional Structure

Definition 3.16

(Critical multipliers)
.

For a given direction
dx\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define
M^{c}(\bar{z};dx)
as the set of
optimal solutions of the linear program

\max_{\lambda\in M(\bar{z})}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\lambda_{i}\,dx^{T}\nabla_{x}g_{i}(\bar{z}).

Proposition 3.17
.

Let
\lambda\in M(\bar{z})
. Then

\mathcal{K}(\bar{z},\lambda;dx)\neq\varnothing\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\lambda\in M^{c}(\bar{z};dx).

Moreover, for every
\lambda\in M^{c}(\bar{z};dx)
, the set

\mathcal{K}(\bar{z},\lambda;dx)
is independent of
\lambda
and coincides with
the optimal solution set of the dual linear program

\min_{dy\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\ dy^{T}F(\bar{z})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad dx^{T}\nabla_{x}g_{i}(\bar{z})+dy^{T}\nabla_{y}g_{i}(\bar{z})\leq 0,\quad i\in\mathcal{I}(\bar{z}).

Idea.

If
dy\in\mathcal{K}(\bar{z},\lambda;dx)
, then the complementarity relations give
the slackness conditions needed for optimality of
\lambda
in the primal
LP. Conversely, if
\lambda
is primal-optimal, then an optimal dual
solution
dy
exists and complementary slackness implies

dy\in\mathcal{K}(\bar{z},\lambda;dx)
.
∎

3.3
Constraint Qualifications for MPEC

Definition 3.18

(Basic, extreme, and full CQ)
.

We say that the
basic constraint qualification
holds at

\bar{z}\in\mathcal{F}
if there exists a nonempty subset
M^{\prime}\subseteq M(\bar{z})

such that

T(\bar{z};\mathcal{F})=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(\bar{z};\mathcal{F}),

where

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(\bar{z};\mathcal{F}):=T(\bar{z};Z)\cap\left(\bigcup_{\lambda\in M^{\prime}}\operatorname{Gr}(\mathscr{L}\mathscr{S}_{(\bar{z},\lambda)})\right).

If this holds with
M^{\prime}=M^{e}(\bar{z})
, we call it the
extreme CQ
. If
it holds with
M^{\prime}=M(\bar{z})
, we call it the
full CQ
.

Remark 3.19
.

If
M(\bar{z})
is a singleton, then the basic, extreme, and full CQs
coincide.

3.4
Stationarity Under the Full CQ

Corollary 3.20

(Equivalent primal characterizations of stationarity)
.

Assume SBCQ holds at
\bar{z}\in\mathcal{F}
and the full CQ holds at
\bar{z}
,
that is,

\mathcal{L}(\bar{z};\mathcal{F})=T(\bar{z};\mathcal{F}).

Then the following are equivalent:

[label=
()
]

1.

\bar{z}
is a stationary point of the MPEC;

2.

dz\in\mathcal{L}(\bar{z};\mathcal{F})\Longrightarrow\nabla f(\bar{z})^{T}dz\geq 0;

3.

dz=0
is an optimal solution of

\min_{dz}\ \nabla f(\bar{z})^{T}dz\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad dz\in\mathcal{L}(\bar{z};\mathcal{F});

4.

for every scalar
\alpha\geq\|\nabla f(\bar{z})\|
and every

dz\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
,

\nabla f(\bar{z})^{T}dz+\alpha\,\operatorname{dist}(dz,\mathcal{L}(\bar{z};\mathcal{F}))\geq 0.

Proof.

The equivalence of (a) and (b) follows directly from the definition of
stationarity and the equality
T(\bar{z};\mathcal{F})=\mathcal{L}(\bar{z};\mathcal{F})
. The
equivalence of (b) and (c) is immediate. The implication (d)
\Rightarrow
(c)
is obvious. For (c)
\Rightarrow
(d), choose
dz^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}(\bar{z};\mathcal{F})
with

\|dz-dz^{\prime}\|=\operatorname{dist}(dz,\mathcal{L}(\bar{z};\mathcal{F})).

Then

\nabla f(\bar{z})^{T}dz+\alpha\,\operatorname{dist}(dz,\mathcal{L}(\bar{z};\mathcal{F}))\geq\nabla f(\bar{z})^{T}dz^{\prime}+(\alpha-\|\nabla f(\bar{z})\|)\|dz-dz^{\prime}\|\geq 0.\qed

3.4.1
Primal–dual stationarity

Assume now that the upper-level feasible set is polyhedral:

Z=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}:Gx+Hy+a\leq 0\},

where
G\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times n}
,
H\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times m}
, and
a\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
.

Definition 3.21

(Degenerate and positive multiplier index sets)
.

For
\lambda\in M(\bar{z})
, define

I_{0}(\bar{z},\lambda):=\{\,i\in\mathcal{I}(\bar{z}):\lambda_{i}=0\,\},\qquad I_{+}(\bar{z},\lambda):=\{\,i\in\mathcal{I}(\bar{z}):\lambda_{i}>0\,\}.

Theorem 3.22

(Primal–dual characterization under the full CQ)
.

Under the assumptions of Corollary
3.20
,
\bar{z}
is a stationary
point of (
1
) if and only if for each vector

\lambda\in M(\bar{z})
and each pair of index sets

\alpha,\bar{\alpha}
partitioning
I_{0}(\bar{z},\lambda)
, there exist
multipliers

\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{s},\qquad\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell},\qquad\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

such that

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}f(\bar{z})+G^{T}\zeta+\nabla_{x}g(\bar{z})^{T}\eta

\displaystyle=\nabla_{x}L(\bar{z},\lambda)^{T}\pi,

\displaystyle\nabla_{y}f(\bar{z})+H^{T}\zeta+\nabla_{y}g(\bar{z})^{T}\eta

\displaystyle=\nabla_{y}L(\bar{z},\lambda)^{T}\pi,

\displaystyle\pi^{T}\nabla_{y}g_{i}(\bar{z})

\displaystyle\leq 0,\qquad i\in\alpha,

\displaystyle\pi^{T}\nabla_{y}g_{i}(\bar{z})

\displaystyle=0,\qquad i\in I_{+}(\bar{z},\lambda),

\displaystyle\eta_{i}

\displaystyle\geq 0,\qquad i\in\bar{\alpha},

\displaystyle\eta_{i}

\displaystyle=0,\qquad i\notin\mathcal{I}(\bar{z}),

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle\geq 0,\qquad\zeta^{T}(G\bar{x}+H\bar{y}+a)=0.

Corollary 3.23

(Finite verification under CRCQ and extreme CQ)
.

If, instead of SBCQ and full CQ, one assumes CRCQ and extreme CQ at

\bar{z}
, then Theorem
3.22
remains valid with
M(\bar{z})

replaced by the finite set
M^{e}(\bar{z})
. Hence stationarity reduces to
finitely many linear inequality systems.

3.5
Special Case: NCP-Constrained MPECs

Definition 3.24

(NCP-constrained MPEC)
.

An important special case occurs when

g(x,y)=-y.

Then the lower-level equilibrium condition becomes the nonlinear
complementarity system

y\geq 0,\qquad F(x,y)\geq 0,\qquad y^{T}F(x,y)=0.

Definition 3.25

(NCP index sets)
.

For a feasible point
\bar{z}=(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, define

\displaystyle\alpha(\bar{z})

\displaystyle:=\{\,i:\ \bar{y}_{i}>0=F_{i}(\bar{z})\,\},

\displaystyle\beta(\bar{z})

\displaystyle:=\{\,i:\ \bar{y}_{i}=0=F_{i}(\bar{z})\,\},

\displaystyle\gamma(\bar{z})

\displaystyle:=\{\,i:\ \bar{y}_{i}=0<F_{i}(\bar{z})\,\}.

The indices in
\beta(\bar{z})
are called
degenerate
.

Remark 3.26
.

In the NCP case, the multiplier set is a singleton:

M(\bar{z})=\{F(\bar{z})\}.

Moreover, for any
dx\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the directional critical set becomes the
constant cone

\mathbb{R}^{|\alpha(\bar{z})|}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{|\beta(\bar{z})|}\times\{0\}^{|\gamma(\bar{z})|},

and the affine variational inequality in Lemma
3.13
reduces to a
mixed linear complementarity problem.

3.6
Engineering Interpretation

Remark 3.27

(Why this matters in engineering)
.

In engineering applications, an equilibrium subproblem often models
contact, traffic, flow balance, or market clearing, while the upper-level
optimization controls geometry, design parameters, or operating points.
The results above show that:

[label=0.]

1.

local optimality must be checked against the true tangent cone,
not merely an NLP reformulation;

2.

the MPEC linearized cone is the correct first-order replacement;

3.

suitable MPEC-specific constraint qualifications are essential;

4.

stationarity can be expressed in both primal and primal–dual form.

. Because
M(\overline{z})=\{F(\overline{z})\}
, the degenerate index set
\mathcal{I}_{0}(\overline{z},\lambda)
contains indices where both
y_{i}=0
and
F_{i}(\overline{z})=0
. This is the exact definition of the set
\beta(\overline{z})
.
Bijection: Theorem 3.3.4 partitions
\mathcal{I}_{0}
into subsets
\alpha
and
\overline{\alpha}
. By mapping
\alpha\mapsto\beta_{1}
and
\overline{\alpha}\mapsto\beta(\overline{z})\setminus\beta_{1}
, the generic complementarity conditions directly collapse into the
2^{|\beta(\overline{z})|}
disjunctive linear systems shown in Theorem 3.3.6.

Question 4

(a) Counter-example Logic: If only MFCQ holds,
M(\overline{z})
is a compact polytope but not a singleton. In the proof of Proposition 3.4.3, we must bound the sequence
\tau_{k}^{-1}(\lambda^{k}-\overline{\lambda})
. If SMFCQ fails, this sequence can diverge.
(b) Geometric Failure: Without SMFCQ, the normalized direction sequence
\overline{d}=\lim(\lambda^{k}-\overline{\lambda})/\|\lambda^{k}-\overline{\lambda}\|
might be non-zero while satisfying
\sum\overline{d}_{i}\nabla_{y}g_{i}(\overline{z})=0
. This allows the KKT tangent directions to contain unbounded multiplier components that do not project onto valid primal directions in
\mathcal{T}(\overline{z};\mathcal{F})
, making the projection a strict superset.

Question 5

Proof Sketch: By Proposition 3.3.8, strong nondegeneracy means active gradients are linearly independent and strict complementarity holds (
\overline{\lambda}-g(\overline{x},\overline{y})>0
). This implies
\mathcal{I}_{0}(\overline{z},\overline{\lambda})=\emptyset
.
Collapse: Because the degenerate set is empty, the power set has only one element (
\emptyset
). The directional critical set
\mathcal{K}
transitions from a polyhedral cone to a linear subspace (an affine subspace for a given
dx
). The complementarity multiplier vanishes from the MPEC Lagrangian because the active constraints are strictly binding, effectively acting as standard equality constraints locally and smoothing the feasible region into a differentiable manifold.

Question 6

Conclusion: The ”for all” requirement is no longer sufficient.
Proof Sketch: Proposition 3.4.1 relies critically on the convexity of
g_{i}(x,\cdot)
to guarantee that a KKT neighborhood
W\times\mathbb{R}^{l}
maps cleanly to a primal feasible neighborhood
W\cap\mathcal{F}
. If
C(x)
is non-convex, a point
(\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{\lambda})
could be a local minimum of the KKT NLP formulation (acting as a stationary point or saddle in the primal space), but
(\overline{x},\overline{y})
may fail to be a valid local minimizer of the true MPEC because the primal feasible space lacks the convexity required to bound the neighborhood objective values.
