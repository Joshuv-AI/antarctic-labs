Task Vector Geometry Underlies Dual Modes of Task Inference in Transformers Thanks: Code is available at https://github.com/ezyhdxm/mini-ICL .
Hao Yan, Haolin Yang, Yiqiao Zhong

Abstract
Transformers are effective at inferring the latent task from context via two inference modes: recognizing a task seen during training, and adapting to a novel one. Recent interpretability studies have identified from middle-layer representations task-specific directions, or task vectors , that steer model behavior. However, a lack of rigorous foundations hinders connecting internal representations to external model behavior: existing work fails to explain how task-vector geometry is shaped by the training distribution, and what geometry enables out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization. In this paper, we study these questions in a controlled synthetic setting by training small transformers from scratch on latent-task sequence distributions, which allows a principled mathematical characterization. We show that two inference modes can coexist within a single model. In-distribution behavior is governed by Bayesian task retrieval, implemented internally through convex combinations of learned task vectors. OOD behavior, by contrast, arises through extrapolative task learning, whose representations occupy a subspace nearly orthogonal to the task-vector subspace. Taken together, our results suggest that task-vector geometry, training distributions, and generalization behaviors are closely related.



1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) exhibit impressive performance, yet the mechanism underlying their generalization remains unclear. Since the emergence of in-context learning, empirical studies have suggested two modes of task inference
[
4
,
25
]
:
memorization-based task retrieval
, where the model updates an implicit posterior over in-distribution (ID) training tasks and retrieves previously learned behaviors, and
context-based task generalization
, where it solves out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks whose latents lie outside the training support. However, the internal representations supporting these two modes remain obscure. This motivates two central questions:
How do hidden states summarize context and retrieve memorized tasks? How does the representation geometry change for novel tasks outside the training support?

Figure 1
:

Connections between data distribution and representation geometry.

This diagram illustrates the synthetic
rolling biased dice
experiment. Each sequence is generated from an unobserved latent
z
controlling the outcome distribution. A transformer trained on mixture data learns internal task vectors that encode these latents. Two near-orthogonal subspaces largely determine a model’s inference mode and generalization behavior.

To understand these external behaviors, researchers have analyzed internal representations, broadly centering on the
linear representation hypothesis
(LRH)
[
23
,
22
,
28
]
, which posits that hidden states encode concepts as linear subspaces. For example,
[
16
]
discovered that in ICL, LLMs use in-context examples internally to form task-encoding vectors, commonly called
task vectors
. Concretely, given a prompt
s=(x_{1},f(x_{1}),x_{2},f(x_{2}),\ldots,x_{n},f(x_{n}),x_{\mathrm{test}})
, an LLM can infer the mapping
f
from the demonstrations and correctly predict
f(x_{\mathrm{test}})
. Internally, the hidden state in a middle layer contains a task-specific component
{\bm{\theta}}_{f}
that steers the model toward the correct continuation.

Challenges: lack of rigorous foundations for representation geometry.

While prior work on internal representations and task vectors provides valuable insights
[
14
,
3
]
, the mechanistic analysis is often model-dependent and its applicability is rarely verified. For example, various notions of task vectors are proposed
[
16
,
39
,
21
]
, sometimes leading to contradictory results in the literature. A central missing piece is a theoretical framework that rigorously defines task vectors and clarifies how they relate to the geometry of internal representations. Without such a foundation, existing results are largely heuristic: task vectors are often introduced operationally or extracted empirically, but their precise meaning, validity, and connection to representation structure remain unclear. As a result, current analyses fail to provide a clear mechanistic picture of how pretrained LLMs internally encode task information, how this encoding is shaped by the training data distribution, and how internal representations mediate the link between external statistical structure and model behavior.

Bridging the gap via latent-variable modeling.

To develop foundations for task vector representation geometry grounded in rigorous statistical principles, we mainly study synthetic experiments where we train small transformers from scratch on carefully controlled data. As shown in Figure
1
, at a high level, we view tasks as latent variables (or simply, latents) in a space
{\mathcal{Z}}
. We model training data as a latent-task mixture: for each sequence, we first sample an unobserved latent
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathrm{train}}\subset{\mathcal{Z}}
and then sample the sequence from its task-specific distribution
p_{z}
. Echoing the “multitask learner” heuristic of GPT-2
[
31
]
, this latent-variable view is standard in classical text analysis (e.g., topic models) and has recently provided useful insights on ICL
[
46
,
13
]
, induction heads
[
24
,
10
]
, etc.

This setup allows us to broadly define task vectors as mappings of latents, and characterize how a model infers tasks from interactions between hidden states and task vectors. We adopt a “gray-box” perspective that assumes access only to hidden states, making our analysis agnostic to architectural details. Our approach unifies prior work on representation geometry
[
34
,
33
]
and inference modes
[
6
,
20
,
26
]
, and identifies a counterexample that exposes a failure case of existing heuristic approaches to defining and extracting task vectors, thereby illustrating the value of our rigorous characterization of task vectors (Sec.
7
).
Our main findings are summarized below.

1.

A principled framework unifies representation geometry and generalization.
We mathematically define task vectors as representations of latent tasks, and characterize two distinct inference modes: Bayesian task retrieval and extrapolative task learning (Sec.
4
).

2.

Task-vector geometry governs Bayesian task retrieval
. For ID sequences, the model infers latent tasks primarily by adjusting the coefficients of task vectors in its hidden states, which eventually converge to the true task vector as sequence length grows (Sec.
5
).

3.

Near-orthogonal subspace governs extrapolative task learning.
For OOD sequences, the model can often generalize by using context-related statistics encoded through near-orthogonal subspaces, which emerge only at sufficiently large task diversity (Sec.
6
).

2
Related work

Task vectors in language models.

Task vectors have emerged as a prominent paradigm for explaining how language models extract task information from context and use it to steer subsequent predictions. However, where such vectors should be obtained from, and which internal activations truly constitute task vectors, remains underexplored and still somewhat controversial. Existing work typically extracts task vectors using heuristic procedures such as dummy task prompts
[
16
]
, averaging across multiple task prompts
[
39
]
, or opaque optimization-based selection
[
17
]
, and does so from sources including intermediate ICL hidden states
[
18
]
, attention-head outputs
[
47
]
, and MLP activations
[
21
]
. However, these construction procedures are largely ad hoc: candidate vectors are typically selected from a small set of layers or components and validated mainly by intervention outcomes, rather than derived from a principled characterization of the underlying data distribution or representation geometry.

Bayesian inference and OOD generalization.

Early empirical studies observe that LLMs can operate in two modes: retrieve seen tasks and learn new tasks in context
[
25
,
44
]
. Explanations such as Bayesian beliefs
[
46
,
34
]
and induction heads
[
12
,
24
,
35
]
focus on either ID or OOD generalization, without reconciling the coexistence of both inference modes. Synthetic experiments in
[
13
,
6
,
20
,
26
]
reveal that task diversity in training shapes the inference mode in specific settings, yet no analysis of internal representations is provided.

Near-orthogonal representation geometry.

A central idea in mechanistic interpretability (MI) is the
superposition principle

[
11
]
: models often accommodate a large number of feature vectors by arranging them in near-orthogonal positions, echoing the classical literature in sparse coding
[
7
]
and compressed sensing
[
8
,
5
]
. Near-orthogonal geometry is empirically confirmed in transformers
[
36
,
33
]
and serves as the foundation for MI work
[
3
,
38
,
27
,
15
]
. However, near-orthogonality has not been established as a geometric foundation for the coexistence of ID and OOD inference modes.

3
Synthetic experiment setup

Data generation.

In all synthetic experiments, we first draw a latent
z
from a finite training set
{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathrm{train}}\subset{\mathcal{Z}}
and then sample a sequence from the latent-specific distribution.

E1. Rolling biased dice.

Each latent
z
corresponds to a probability vector
{\bm{p}}
drawn from a Dirichlet prior
\mathrm{Dir}(\bm{1}_{6})
. For each sequence, we first sample
{\bm{p}}
and then sample tokens independently from
{\bm{p}}
.

E2. In-context linear regression.

Following
[
32
]
, we take the latent
z:={\bm{w}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{D})
as the
D
-dim regression weight vector (
D=6
). For each sequence, we first sample
{\bm{w}}
and then
T/2
input-output pairs (treating each vector/scalar as a token)
{\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2},y_{2},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{T/2},y_{T/2}
, where

{\bm{x}}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{D}),y_{t}={\bm{x}}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{w}}+\varepsilon_{t},
and
\varepsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.25)\,.

E3. Mixture of Markov chains.

A latent
z:={\bm{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times V}
(
V=6
) is a transition matrix for a first-order Markov chain. As in
[
10
]
, we draw rows of
{\bm{P}}
independently from a Dirichlet prior
\mathrm{Dir}(\bm{1}_{V})
, then sample the Markov sequence
s_{1:T}
using
{\bm{P}}
, starting from a uniform initial distribution.

Remark 1

(on Markov property)
.

In synthetic setup
E1
–
E3
, sequences satisfy the first-order Markov property given
z
:
p(s_{t+1}\mid z,s_{\leq t})=p(s_{t+1}\mid z,s_{t})
. Thus we expect a performant model to infer the unobserved
z
by internally compressing the context into a ‘belief’ about
z
.

Model and training.

We use a decoder-only transformer with rotary positional embedding (RoPE)
[
37
]
.
For experiments
E1
and
E3
, the transformer has
6
layers,
2
heads, and hidden dimension
d=128
; for
E2
, we follow
[
2
]
by increasing the number of layers to
16
(other hyperparameters remain the same).
All models are trained autoregressively with AdamW
[
19
]
; a complete list of hyperparameters is provided in Sec.
A
.

4
A mathematical framework for representation geometry

This section develops the mathematical framework used throughout the paper. Sec.
4.1
defines task vectors and four geometric properties of hidden states; Sec.
4.2
links these properties to Bayesian task retrieval, while Sec.
4.3
studies extrapolative task learning and shows when it must move beyond the task-vector subspace. Secs.
5
–
6
provide empirical evidence for the two-mode picture.

4.1
Theoretical properties of task-vector geometry

Let
{z}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
be the unobserved latent and
s_{1},s_{2},\ldots
be the observed sequence generated from the distribution
P_{z}
.
For a fixed transformer layer
\ell
, denote by

{\bm{h}}_{t}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the residual-stream hidden state at
position
t
after processing the prefix
s_{\leq t}
.
Throughout this section, we fix a layer and suppress the superscript
\ell
. Since the results below do not depend on the specific parameter space of any experiment, we identify the training latents
z
with the index set
[K]:=\{1,\ldots,K\}
and write
{\bm{\mu}}_{t}:=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{h}}_{t}]
for the mean hidden state at position
t
.
As noted in Sec.
3
, tasks
E1
–
E3
satisfy the first-order Markov property:
\mathbb{P}(s_{t+1}\mid z,s_{\leq t})=\mathbb{P}(s_{t+1}\mid z,s_{t})
.

Under the Markov property,
\mathbb{P}(s_{t+1}\mid z,s_{\leq t})
depends only on
(z,s_{t})
, so a well-trained model’s hidden state should likewise be approximately determined by these two factors. Define cell means
{\bm{\mu}}_{t,k,a}:=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{h}}_{t}\mid z=k,s_{t}=a]
and write

{\bm{h}}_{t}\;=\;{\bm{\mu}}_{t,z,s_{t}}\;+\;{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{t},

(1)

where
{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{t}:={\bm{h}}_{t}-{\bm{\mu}}_{t,z,s_{t}}
is the residual not explained by
(z,s_{t})
. We state four properties characterizing the hidden-state geometry when task retrieval succeeds.

P0. Long-context stability.

The residual in Eq.
1
vanishes asymptotically:
\mathbb{E}\|{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{t}\|^{2}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. In other words, the hidden state becomes a deterministic function of
(z,s_{t})
, meaning the model has successfully summarized the context. While heuristic task-vector extractions
[
39
,
16
]
implicitly assume
P0
, we show in Sec.
7
that this property is not always satisfied.

P1. Long-context decoupling.

Given
P0
, we can further ask how the cell means
{\bm{\mu}}_{t,k,a}
are structured. The task-specific component naturally gives rise to the definition of
task vectors

[
16
]
.

Definition 1
.

For a given layer,
task vectors
are
\{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}\}_{k\leq K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined by

{\bm{\theta}}_{k}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\big(\mathbb{E}[{\bm{h}}_{t}\mid z=k]-{\bm{\mu}}_{t}\big),

(2)

whenever the limit exists. Centering ensures
\sum_{k\leq K}{\bm{\theta}}_{k}={\bm{0}}
.

P1
requires that the cell means decompose additively into task and token components. There exist token-encoding vectors
\{{\bm{\nu}}_{a}\}_{a\in{\mathcal{V}}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\mathbb{E}\Bigl\|{\bm{h}}_{t}-\bigl(\underbrace{{\bm{\mu}}_{t}}_{\text{global mean}}+\underbrace{{\bm{\theta}}_{z}}_{\text{task vector}}+\underbrace{{\bm{\nu}}_{s_{t}}}_{\text{token encoding}}\bigr)\Bigr\|_{2}^{2}\to 0\qquad\text{as }t\to\infty.

(3)

This additive structure is a form of the
linear representation hypothesis

[
28
]
; in ANOVA terms,
P1
requires that the interaction between
z
and
s_{t}
is negligible.

P2. Finite-context interpolation.

At finite context, the model represents its knowledge about latent tasks via a convex combination of task vectors. There exist nonnegative coefficients
\{\beta_{t,k}\}_{t\geq 1,\,k\leq K}
satisfying
\sum_{k\leq K}\beta_{t,k}=1
(depending on context
s_{1},\ldots,s_{t}
) such that

{\bm{h}}_{t}\approx{\bm{\mu}}_{t}+\sum_{k\leq K}\beta_{t,k}\,{\bm{\theta}}_{k}+{\bm{\nu}}_{s_{t}}.

(4)

P2
formalizes the
superposition principle

[
11
]
: hidden states are linear combinations of task vectors.

P3. Bayesian posterior alignment.

P3
strengthens
P2
by pinning down the same coefficients
\beta_{t,k}
: they approximate the Bayesian posterior,

\beta_{t,k}\approx\alpha_{t,k}:=\mathbb{P}(z=k\mid s_{\leq t}).

(5)

This provides a mechanistic characterization of
Bayesian inference
: while the Bayesian perspective has been used to explain ICL
[
46
]
, its internal geometry has remained unclear. These properties are progressively stronger:
P3

\Rightarrow

P2
,
P1
and
P1

\Rightarrow

P0
in the long-context limit; see Sec.
B.1
.

4.2
Task retrieval: task-vector geometry mirrors Bayesian inference

When a model has memorized a task
z
during training, it can retrieve the latent task
z
from test prompts via approximate Bayesian prediction; we call this the
Bayesian task retrieval mode
(
M1
). Formally, let
\pi_{\mathrm{train}}
be the prior distribution over
{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathrm{train}}\subset{\mathcal{Z}}
. The optimal prediction under
M1
is

\mathbb{P}(s_{t+1}\!=\!a\mid s_{\leq t})\!=\!\int_{{\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}(s_{t+1}\!=\!a\mid z,s_{t})\,p(z\mid s_{\leq t})\mathrm{d}z,~~p(z\mid s_{\leq t})=\frac{p_{z}(s_{\leq t})\,\pi_{\mathrm{train}}(z)}{\int_{{\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime}}p_{z}(s_{\leq t})\,\pi_{\mathrm{train}}(z)},

(6)

where
{\mathcal{Z}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathrm{train}}
and
p(z\mid s_{\leq t})
is the exact
\alpha_{t,k}
in Eq.
5
. By Eqs.
4
and
5
,
{\bm{h}}_{t}
carries the same posterior weights as Eq.
6
: after a shared centering map removes
{\bm{\mu}}_{t}
, the informative component is
\sum_{k\leq K}\alpha_{t,k}({\bm{\theta}}_{k}+{\bm{\nu}}_{s_{t}})
. Thus Bayesian retrieval reduces to a finite lookup-and-mix computation: lookup
\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid z=k,s_{t})
from the task/token anchors and mix the results with
\alpha_{t,k}
. Sec.
B.2
gives the constructive proof.

Theorem 1

(Informal; task-vector subspace can implement Bayesian inference)
.

Suppose that at some layer
{\bm{h}}_{t}
satisfies
P0
–
P3
. Under the structural conditions formalized in Sec.
B.2
, two additional transformer blocks and a suitable unembedding can make the ID prediction distribution approximate the Bayesian predictive distribution in Eq.
6
.

4.3
Task learning: OOD inference operates outside task-vector subspace

How can a model infer
{z}^{\prime}\notin{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathrm{train}}
from context and achieve OOD generalization? One possibility is that it applies Eq.
6
with an uninformative prior over the full space
{\mathcal{Z}}
rather than only the training support, extrapolating beyond seen latents. We call this the
extrapolative task-learning mode
(
M2
).

M2
relies on context statistics (empirical unigram for
E1
, ridge solution for
E2
, and empirical bigram for
E3
) rather than memorized task vectors (Sec.
B.3
).
The key question is whether such extrapolative inference behavior is enabled by a different geometry compared with
M1
.
We give a theoretical result: when the OOD prediction map has intrinsic dimension
d_{0}>k_{\star}
, where
k_{\star}
is the task-vector subspace dimension, a model implementing
M2
cannot confine its representations to that subspace.

More formally, suppose
{\mathcal{V}}
is discrete and fix
a_{\star}\in{\mathcal{V}}
. Let

{\bm{p}}({z}):=\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid{z},a_{\star})
denote the next-token distribution.
Assume
P0
holds and that, for
{z}
near an interior point

{z}_{0}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
, the limit

{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z}):=\lim_{t\to\infty}{\bm{\mu}}_{t,{z},a_{\star}}
exists. Define the
task subspace

\mathcal{T}:=\operatorname{span}({\bm{\theta}}_{2}-{\bm{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\theta}}_{K}-{\bm{\theta}}_{1})
,
set
k_{\star}:=\dim\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{S}:={\bm{\nu}}_{a_{\star}}+\mathcal{T}
. Let

d_{0}:=\operatorname{rank}\nabla{\bm{p}}({z}_{0})
be the local intrinsic dimension
of
{\bm{p}}
at
{z}_{0}
.

Theorem 2

(Informal, distance to task subspace lower bound)
.

Assume
P0
. Suppose that on some neighborhood
U\ni{z}_{0}
, the prediction
\hat{{\bm{p}}}({z})
decoded from
{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z})
is locally
L
-Lipschitz:

\|\hat{{\bm{p}}}({z})-\hat{{\bm{p}}}({z}^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq L\,\|{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z})-{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z}^{\prime})\|_{2},

for all

{z},{z}^{\prime}\in U.

Additionally, assume that

\|\hat{{\bm{p}}}({z})-{\bm{p}}({z})\|_{2}\leq\delta,

for all

{z}\in U

and
\{{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z}):{z}\in U\}
is bounded. Then there exist local constants
c,C>0
such that

\sup_{{z}\in U}\inf_{{\bm{c}}\in\mathcal{S}}\|{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z})-{\bm{c}}\|_{2}\geq\frac{c-\delta}{L}-\frac{C}{2^{d_{0}/k_{\star}}-1}.

See Sec.
B.4
for the formal theorem. The second term in the lower bound decays exponentially in
d_{0}/k_{\star}
, so when
d_{0}\gg k_{\star}
the model cannot implement
M2
accurately while staying close to
\mathcal{S}
; smoother decoding (smaller
L
) forces a larger deviation. This provides a theoretical basis for
M2
representations to leave the task subspace. Sec.
6
will in fact show a stronger empirical result:
M2
is implemented by a near-orthogonal subspace.

Task-vector geometry represents Bayesian posterior beliefs

4.1
predicts the following behavior under the Bayesian task-retrieval mode (
M1
).

Task-vector geometry represents Bayesian inference
: For ID sequences, the model represents training tasks as learned task vectors and updates their mixture weights from context, approximately tracking the Bayesian posterior.

Evaluation results of properties

7
Non-Markovian case: when hidden states don’t summarize context

The previous sections rely on a Markovian premise. In contrast, here we present a non-Markovian counterexample to show why that premise matters—when long-range structure breaks local summarization, even commonly assumed property
P0
fails. This demonstrates that the validity of common heuristics depends sensitively on the underlying data-generating assumptions, highlighting the need for a principled framework and the value of the theoretical grounding it provides.

Planted Dyck language.

A Dyck language (Dyck-1) is the collection of all balanced bracket strings, underlying arithmetic expressions and programming code. For example, the Dyck language of length
6
consists of
5
strings:

((())),~~(()()),~~(())(),~~()(()),~~()()().

We view latent
z

2L

z

T

\{0,1,\ldots,V-1\}

\rho=0.25
and plant the next character of
z

z
into a pure-noise background of
T
uniformly random symbols. As an example, with
V=2
and
z=(()())
, one possible sequence is

0,1,(,0,(,1,1),0,(,1,1,),).

In our experiments, the data-generating process uses
V=6
and
L=10
, yielding
C_{10}=16796
possible latents.

Long-context dependence.

We train a small autoregressive transformer to near-optimal loss and, on held-out sequences, measure the variance of
{\bm{h}}_{t}
conditioned on the latent
z
and a local window of preceding tokens. In Figure

j
(the
j

not

for the prefix-probe construction and quantitative validation accuracies at every prefix length.

8
Limitations and Future Work

This work presents a modest attempt to bridge generalization and representation geometry in transformers. First, our analysis of
E1
–
E3
relies on the restrictive first-order Markov property on synthetic data; a more realistic setup may extend a single conditioning token
a
to a local window. Second, our near-orthogonal subspace does not provide a mechanistic account of OOD generalization, which is complex in general. Third, our evaluation and analysis are theory-inspired, but not yet a formal theory of generalization or learning dynamics, which is left to future work.

B.1
Theoretical relationship between properties
P0
–
P3

This subsection establishes the implication chain among the four geometric properties
P0
–
P3
introduced in Sec.
4.1
:

\texttt{P3}\;\Rightarrow\;\texttt{P2},\qquad\texttt{P3}\;\Rightarrow\;\texttt{P1}\ \text{in the long-context limit},\qquad\texttt{P1}\;\Rightarrow\;\texttt{P0}.

This justifies treating the empirical evidence for
P3
reported in Sec.
5.1
as evidence for the weaker properties
P0
–
P2
as well.

Proposition 1

(
P3

\Rightarrow

P2
)
.

If
P3
holds, then
P2
holds.

Proof.

By
P3
, the hidden state admits the decomposition
{\bm{h}}_{t}\approx{\bm{\mu}}_{t}+\sum_{k\leq K}\beta_{t,k}\,{\bm{\theta}}_{k}+{\bm{\nu}}_{s_{t}}
with coefficients
\beta_{t,k}\approx\alpha_{t,k}:=\mathbb{P}(z=k\mid s_{\leq t})
.
Because
\{\alpha_{t,k}\}_{k\leq K}
is a probability distribution, we have
\alpha_{t,k}\geq 0
and
\sum_{k\leq K}\alpha_{t,k}=1
, so the coefficients
\beta_{t,k}
are nonnegative and sum to one.
This is precisely the simplex constraint required by
P2
.
∎

Proposition 2

(
P3

\Rightarrow

P1
in the long-context limit)
.

Suppose
P3
holds and the posterior concentrates on the true latent, i.e.,
\alpha_{t,k}\to\delta_{k,z}
almost surely as
t\to\infty
, where
z
denotes the true latent.
Then
P1
holds in the limit
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

Substituting
\alpha_{t,k}\to\delta_{k,z}
into the
P3
decomposition yields

{\bm{h}}_{t}\;\approx\;{\bm{\mu}}_{t}+\sum_{k\leq K}\alpha_{t,k}\,{\bm{\theta}}_{k}+{\bm{\nu}}_{s_{t}}\;\longrightarrow\;{\bm{\mu}}_{t}+{\bm{\theta}}_{z}+{\bm{\nu}}_{s_{t}},

recovering exactly the additive structure required by
P1
.
The hypothesis
\alpha_{t,k}\to\delta_{k,z}
a.s. holds in our experiments by the ergodic theorem for finite-state Markov chains (
E1
,
E3
) and an analogous argument for
E2

[e.g.,
40
, Ch. 10]
.
∎

Proposition 3

(
P1

\Rightarrow

P0
)
.

If
P1
holds, then
P0
holds.

Proof.

Write
{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{t}={\bm{h}}_{t}-{\bm{\mu}}_{t,z,s_{t}}
, where
{\bm{\mu}}_{t,z,s_{t}}=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{h}}_{t}\mid z,s_{t}]
is the cell mean.
Because the conditional expectation minimizes mean squared error over all functions of
(z,s_{t})
, we have

\mathbb{E}\bigl\|{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{t}\bigr\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\bigl\|{\bm{h}}_{t}-{\bm{\mu}}_{t,z,s_{t}}\bigr\|^{2}\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}\bigl\|{\bm{h}}_{t}-g(z,s_{t})\bigr\|^{2}

for every measurable
g\colon[K]\times{\mathcal{V}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Taking
g(z,s_{t})={\bm{\mu}}_{t}+{\bm{\theta}}_{z}+{\bm{\nu}}_{s_{t}}
, which depends on
(z,s_{t})
alone, gives

\mathbb{E}\bigl\|{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{t}\bigr\|^{2}\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}\bigl\|{\bm{h}}_{t}-({\bm{\mu}}_{t}+{\bm{\theta}}_{z}+{\bm{\nu}}_{s_{t}})\bigr\|^{2}\;\longrightarrow\;0\qquad\text{as }t\to\infty,

where the right-hand side vanishes by the
P1
guarantee (Eq.
3
).
∎

B.2
Proof of Theorem
1
in Section
4.2

This subsection gives the formal statement and proof of Theorem
1
from Sec.
4.2
. It shows that posterior-aligned representations (properties
P2
–
P3
) are sufficient for a transformer to approximate the Bayesian predictive distribution under the task-retrieval mode
M1
.
The high-level idea is that, once the residual stream encodes the posterior weights
\alpha_{t,k}
as the coefficients in front of the task vectors
{\bm{\theta}}_{k}
, the remaining Bayesian computation

{\bm{q}}_{t+1}\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{t,k}\,{\bm{p}}_{k,s_{t}}

reduces to two operations, each realizable by one transformer block: (i) a finite lookup that mixes the per-pair conditionals
{\bm{p}}_{k,a}
into the residual stream, followed by (ii) an approximate coordinatewise logarithm whose softmax recovers
{\bm{q}}_{t+1}
.
The output unembedding is also part of the construction below, so the theorem does not assume any property of a pre-existing unembedding matrix.
The full construction is given in Sec.
B.2.1
and relies on two technical lemmas stated and proved in Sec.
B.2.2
.

B.2.1
Detailed Statement of Theorem
1
and Proof

We now state the formal version of Theorem
1
, an
expressivity
statement.
Posterior-aligned residuals (
P2
–
P3
) together with the structural conditions (i)–(iv) below suffice for two appended transformer blocks to approximate the Bayesian predictive
{\bm{q}}_{t+1}
within
\ell_{\infty}
tolerance
\delta
.
The lookup MLP in Block 1 has hidden width
KV
.
Block 2 has hidden width
m_{\log}(\delta,\varepsilon,V)+2V
, where
m_{\log}
is the width of a coordinatewise ReLU spline approximation to
\log
on
[\varepsilon,1]
at the accuracy used below.
These are constructive upper bounds; we do not claim width optimality, nor do we address whether training finds this configuration or whether conditions (i)–(iv) hold for arbitrary trained models.

Theorem 3

(Internal geometry mirrors Bayesian inference; formal version of Theorem
1
)
.

Consider a LayerNorm-free transformer with residual-stream dimension
d
, vocabulary
{\mathcal{V}}
of size
V
(identified with
[V]
), and training latent set
{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathrm{train}}=[K]
.
Suppose the following conditions hold.

(i)

Uniform posterior-aligned representation.

At some layer
\ell
, fix a set of positions
\mathcal{I}
.
For every
t\in\mathcal{I}
, the residual stream satisfies

{\bm{h}}_{t}={\bm{\mu}}_{t}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{t,k}{\bm{\theta}}_{k}+{\bm{\nu}}_{s_{t}},\qquad\alpha_{t,k}=\mathbb{P}(z=k\mid s_{\leq t}),

where
\{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are task vectors and
\{{\bm{\nu}}_{a}\}_{a\in{\mathcal{V}}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are token-encoding vectors (cf. properties
P2
–
P3
in Sec.
4.1
).

(ii)

Shared centering map.

Let

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{I}}:=\operatorname{span}\{{\bm{\mu}}_{t}:t\in\mathcal{I}\},\qquad\mathcal{U}:=\operatorname{span}\bigl(\{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}\}_{k\leq K}\cup\{{\bm{\nu}}_{a}\}_{a\in{\mathcal{V}}}\bigr).

Assume
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{I}}\cap\mathcal{U}=\{0\}
.
Equivalently, there exists a fixed linear map
{\bm{C}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

{\bm{C}}{\bm{\mu}}_{t}=0,\qquad{\bm{C}}{\bm{\theta}}_{k}={\bm{\theta}}_{k},\qquad{\bm{C}}{\bm{\nu}}_{a}={\bm{\nu}}_{a}

for all
t\in\mathcal{I}
,
k\leq K
, and
a\in{\mathcal{V}}
.
We call such a map a shared centering map.

(iii)

Linear independence.

The family

\{{\bm{\theta}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\theta}}_{K}\}\cup\{{\bm{\nu}}_{a}:a\in{\mathcal{V}}\}

is linearly independent in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; in particular
d\geq K+V
.

(iv)

Bounded-away transition probabilities.

There exists
\varepsilon\in(0,1/V)
such that

\mathbb{P}(s_{t+1}=b\mid z=k,\,s_{t}=a)\geq\varepsilon

for all
t\in\mathcal{I}
,
b\in{\mathcal{V}}
,
k\in[K]
, and
a\in{\mathcal{V}}
.

Then for every
\delta>0
, there exist an output unembedding
{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d}
and two additional transformer blocks, all shared across
t\in\mathcal{I}
, with the blocks appended after layer
\ell
, such that for every
t\in\mathcal{I}
the model’s next-token prediction
\hat{{\bm{p}}}_{t+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
satisfies

\|\hat{{\bm{p}}}_{t+1}-{\bm{q}}_{t+1}\|_{\infty}<\delta,

where

{\bm{q}}_{t+1}:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{t,k}{\bm{p}}_{k,s_{t}},\quad{\bm{p}}_{k,a}:=\bigl(\mathbb{P}(s_{t+1}=b\mid z=k,\,s_{t}=a)\bigr)_{b\in{\mathcal{V}}}.

Moreover, the first appended MLP has hidden width
KV
and the second has hidden width
m_{\log}(\delta,\varepsilon,V)+2V
.

Proof.

We first fix a gauge for the construction.
Although task and token effects are defined earlier in a centered gauge, the lookup construction is most convenient in an uncentered gauge.
In particular, we do not impose
\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\bm{\theta}}_{k}=0
or
\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{V}}}{\bm{\nu}}_{a}=0
, since either constraint would create a nontrivial linear dependence and conflict with condition (iii).
All structural assumptions in the theorem, including the shared-centering map
{\bm{C}}
, are understood in this uncentered gauge.
This is only a change of coordinates at the level of represented states: setting

\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}:=K^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\bm{\theta}}_{k},\qquad\bar{{\bm{\nu}}}:=V^{-1}\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{V}}}{\bm{\nu}}_{a},

and replacing

({\bm{\mu}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{k},{\bm{\nu}}_{a})\mapsto({\bm{\mu}}_{t}+\bar{{\bm{\theta}}}+\bar{{\bm{\nu}}},\,{\bm{\theta}}_{k}-\bar{{\bm{\theta}}},\,{\bm{\nu}}_{a}-\bar{{\bm{\nu}}})

leaves
{\bm{\mu}}_{t}+\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}{\bm{\theta}}_{k}+{\bm{\nu}}_{a}
unchanged for every
{\bm{\alpha}}\in\Delta^{K-1}
and token
a
.

We append two transformer blocks after layer
\ell
.
In both blocks, set the attention output projection to zero, so each attention sublayer contributes only the residual identity.
It remains to construct the two MLP sublayers and the output unembedding.

Let
{\bm{x}}_{t}:={\bm{h}}_{t}
be the input to the first appended block and set

{\bm{u}}_{t}:={\bm{C}}{\bm{x}}_{t}.

By conditions (i)–(ii),

{\bm{u}}_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{t,k}{\bm{\theta}}_{k}+{\bm{\nu}}_{s_{t}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{t,k}({\bm{\theta}}_{k}+{\bm{\nu}}_{s_{t}}).

Write

{\bm{u}}_{k,a}:={\bm{\theta}}_{k}+{\bm{\nu}}_{a}.

Also define the token-embedding matrix

{\bm{E}}:=({\bm{\nu}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\nu}}_{V})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times V}.

By condition (iii),
{\bm{E}}
has full column rank.

Step 1: Build the Bayesian mixture while keeping
{\bm{\mu}}_{t}
.

Define

{\bm{f}}(k,a):={\bm{E}}{\bm{p}}_{k,a}-{\bm{u}}_{k,a}={\bm{E}}{\bm{p}}_{k,a}-{\bm{\theta}}_{k}-{\bm{\nu}}_{a}.

By Lemma
1
, there is a width-
KV
two-layer ReLU MLP
\texttt{MLP}_{\mathrm{lookup}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that, for
{\bm{u}}_{t}=\sum_{k}\alpha_{t,k}{\bm{u}}_{k,s_{t}}
,

\texttt{MLP}_{\mathrm{lookup}}({\bm{u}}_{t})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{t,k}{\bm{f}}(k,s_{t})={\bm{E}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{t,k}{\bm{p}}_{k,s_{t}}-{\bm{u}}_{t}={\bm{E}}{\bm{q}}_{t+1}-{\bm{u}}_{t}.

Composing the first-layer affine forms with
{\bm{C}}
, the map
{\bm{x}}_{t}\mapsto\texttt{MLP}_{\mathrm{lookup}}({\bm{C}}{\bm{x}}_{t})
is still a two-layer ReLU MLP with hidden width
KV
.
Therefore the first residual block outputs

{\bm{y}}_{t}:={\bm{x}}_{t}+\texttt{MLP}_{\mathrm{lookup}}({\bm{C}}{\bm{x}}_{t})={\bm{\mu}}_{t}+{\bm{u}}_{t}+({\bm{E}}{\bm{q}}_{t+1}-{\bm{u}}_{t})={\bm{\mu}}_{t}+{\bm{E}}{\bm{q}}_{t+1}.

Thus Block 1 does not cancel the nuisance component
{\bm{\mu}}_{t}
.
It carries
{\bm{\mu}}_{t}
forward and only converts the task/token component into the embedded Bayesian predictive
{\bm{E}}{\bm{q}}_{t+1}
.

Step 2: Read out
{\bm{q}}_{t+1}
, cancel only at the logits, and apply softmax.

Since
\operatorname{col}({\bm{E}})=\operatorname{span}\{{\bm{\nu}}_{a}:a\in{\mathcal{V}}\}\subset\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{I}}\cap\mathcal{U}=\{0\}
, we have

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{I}}\cap\operatorname{col}({\bm{E}})=\{0\}.

By condition (iv),
{\bm{q}}_{t+1}\in\Delta_{V}^{\varepsilon}
.
Applying Lemma
2
with
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{I}}
gives a constructed unembedding
{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d}
and a width-
m_{\log}(\delta,\varepsilon,V)+2V
two-layer ReLU MLP
\texttt{MLP}_{\mathrm{out}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that, for
{\bm{y}}_{t}={\bm{\mu}}_{t}+{\bm{E}}{\bm{q}}_{t+1}
,

\left\|\mathrm{softmax}\!\left({\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}\bigl({\bm{y}}_{t}+\texttt{MLP}_{\mathrm{out}}({\bm{y}}_{t})\bigr)\right)-{\bm{q}}_{t+1}\right\|_{\infty}<\delta.

Using this MLP as the second block’s MLP sublayer proves the claim.
All maps used above, namely
{\bm{C}}
,
{\bm{E}}
,
\texttt{MLP}_{\mathrm{lookup}}
,
{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}
, and
\texttt{MLP}_{\mathrm{out}}
, are fixed across
t\in\mathcal{I}
, so the construction is uniform over positions.
∎

B.2.2
Technical Lemmas and Proofs

This subsection states and proves the two technical lemmas invoked in the construction of Sec.
B.2.1
: Lemma
1
is used in the first appended block, and Lemma
2
is used in the second.
Both isolate a single capability of a two-layer ReLU MLP and are stated in self-contained form, independent of the transformer setting.

Lemma 1

(Width-
KV
lookup and convex-linearity in the latent index)
.

Let
K,V,d_{\mathrm{out}}\in\mathbb{N}
.
Suppose

\{{\bm{\theta}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\theta}}_{K},{\bm{\nu}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\nu}}_{V}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

is linearly independent, and define

{\bm{u}}_{k,a}:={\bm{\theta}}_{k}+{\bm{\nu}}_{a},\qquad(k,a)\in[K]\times[V].

For any map
{\bm{f}}:[K]\times[V]\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}
, there exists a two-layer ReLU MLP
L:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}
with hidden width
KV
such that

L({\bm{u}}_{k,a})={\bm{f}}(k,a)

for all
(k,a)
, and, more generally,

L\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}{\bm{u}}_{k,a}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}{\bm{f}}(k,a)

for every fixed
a\in[V]
and every
{\bm{\alpha}}\in\Delta^{K-1}:=\{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}:\alpha_{k}\geq 0,\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}=1\}
.

Proof.

By linear independence, there is a biorthogonal dual family

\{{\bm{\theta}}_{1}^{*},\ldots,{\bm{\theta}}_{K}^{*},{\bm{\nu}}_{1}^{*},\ldots,{\bm{\nu}}_{V}^{*}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

satisfying

\langle{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{*},{\bm{\theta}}_{k}\rangle=\mathbf{1}\{i=k\},\qquad\langle{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{*},{\bm{\nu}}_{a}\rangle=0,

and

\langle{\bm{\nu}}_{j}^{*},{\bm{\nu}}_{a}\rangle=\mathbf{1}\{j=a\},\qquad\langle{\bm{\nu}}_{j}^{*},{\bm{\theta}}_{k}\rangle=0.

For each pair
(i,j)\in[K]\times[V]
, define

{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}:={\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{*}+{\bm{\nu}}_{j}^{*}.

Then

\langle{\bm{\phi}}_{ij},{\bm{u}}_{k,a}\rangle=\mathbf{1}\{i=k\}+\mathbf{1}\{j=a\}.

Define one hidden unit per pair:

h_{ij}({\bm{u}}):=\sigma\bigl(\langle{\bm{\phi}}_{ij},{\bm{u}}\rangle-1\bigr),\qquad\sigma(r):=\max\{r,0\}.

On the vertices
{\bm{u}}_{k,a}
, this gives

h_{ij}({\bm{u}}_{k,a})=\mathbf{1}\{i=k,\,j=a\}.

Now fix a token
a
and a simplex vector
{\bm{\alpha}}
.
For

{\bm{u}}_{a}^{({\bm{\alpha}})}:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}{\bm{u}}_{k,a}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}{\bm{\theta}}_{k}+{\bm{\nu}}_{a},

we have

\langle{\bm{\phi}}_{ij},{\bm{u}}_{a}^{({\bm{\alpha}})}\rangle=\alpha_{i}+\mathbf{1}\{j=a\}.

Therefore,

h_{ij}({\bm{u}}_{a}^{({\bm{\alpha}})})=\begin{cases}\sigma(\alpha_{i})=\alpha_{i},&j=a,\\
\sigma(\alpha_{i}-1)=0,&j\neq a,\end{cases}

because
0\leq\alpha_{i}\leq 1
.
Finally define

L({\bm{u}}):=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{V}h_{ij}({\bm{u}})\,{\bm{f}}(i,j).

This is a two-layer ReLU MLP with hidden width
KV
, output weights
{\bm{f}}(i,j)
, and zero output bias.
The vertex and convex-linearity claims follow from the displayed identities.
∎

Remark 2

(Connection to key–value memory)
.

The construction above can be interpreted through the lens of key–value memory, as discussed in
14
.
In our setting, the vectors
\{{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}\}
play the role of keys, which detect whether the input corresponds to a specific pair
(i,j)
via inner products and thresholding.
The second layer associates each key with a value
{\bm{f}}(i,j)
, and the MLP output is obtained by aggregating the retrieved values.

Lemma 2

(Probability recovery with a constructed unembedding)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a linear subspace, let
{\bm{E}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times V}
have full column rank, and assume

\mathcal{M}\cap\operatorname{col}({\bm{E}})=\{0\}.

For every
\varepsilon\in(0,1/V)
and
\delta>0
, there exist an output unembedding
{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d}
and a two-layer ReLU MLP
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
of hidden width
m_{\log}(\delta,\varepsilon,V)+2V
such that

\sup_{{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M},\,{\bm{p}}\in\Delta_{V}^{\varepsilon}}\left\|\mathrm{softmax}\!\left({\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}\bigl({\bm{m}}+{\bm{E}}{\bm{p}}+F({\bm{m}}+{\bm{E}}{\bm{p}})\bigr)\right)-{\bm{p}}\right\|_{\infty}<\delta,

where

\Delta_{V}^{\varepsilon}:=\left\{{\bm{p}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}:p_{b}\geq\varepsilon\text{ for all }b\in[V],\ \sum_{b=1}^{V}p_{b}=1\right\}.

Proof.

Because
M\cap\operatorname{col}({\bm{E}})=\{0\}
, we have

M+\operatorname{col}({\bm{E}})=M\oplus\operatorname{col}({\bm{E}})
.
Thus every vector in this sum has a unique decomposition

{\bm{m}}+{\bm{E}}{\bm{r}}
with
{\bm{m}}\in M
and
{\bm{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
.
Define a linear map

{\bm{P}}:M\oplus\operatorname{col}({\bm{E}})\to\mathbb{R}^{V}
by

{\bm{P}}({\bm{m}}+{\bm{E}}{\bm{r}})={\bm{r}}.

Extend
{\bm{P}}
linearly to all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and set

{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}:={\bm{P}}.

Then

{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}{\bm{m}}={\bm{0}}\quad({\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}),\qquad{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}{\bm{E}}={\bm{I}}_{V}.

In particular, the required unembedding is constructed from the decomposition; no pre-existing output head is assumed.

Choose
\eta>0
such that
e^{2\eta}-1<\delta
.
Let
G:\mathbb{R}^{V}\to\mathbb{R}^{V}
be a two-layer ReLU network satisfying

\sup_{{\bm{p}}\in\Delta_{V}^{\varepsilon}}\|G({\bm{p}})-\log{\bm{p}}\|_{\infty}<\eta.

Such a
G
can be obtained coordinatewise by a piecewise-linear ReLU spline approximation to
\log
on the compact interval
[\varepsilon,1]
; equivalently, this follows from standard single-hidden-layer approximation results such as
29
.
Let
m_{\log}(\delta,\varepsilon,V)
denote the width of this coordinatewise approximation.

Define

F({\bm{y}}):={\bm{E}}\,G({\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}{\bm{y}})-{\bm{E}}{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}{\bm{y}}.

The first term is a two-layer ReLU MLP of width
m_{\log}(\delta,\varepsilon,V)
, obtained by composing the input layer of
G
with
{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}
and the output layer with
{\bm{E}}
.
The second term
-{\bm{E}}{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}{\bm{y}}
is a rank-at-most-
V
linear map, so it can be represented exactly using at most
2V
ReLU hidden units.
Indeed, if
{\bm{E}}{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}={\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}
with
{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}
,
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
, and
r\leq V
, then

-{\bm{E}}{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}{\bm{y}}=-{\bm{A}}\bigl(\sigma({\bm{B}}{\bm{y}})-\sigma(-{\bm{B}}{\bm{y}})\bigr).

Concatenating the hidden units for the two terms gives a two-layer ReLU MLP of width
m_{\log}(\delta,\varepsilon,V)+2V
.

For
{\bm{y}}={\bm{m}}+{\bm{E}}{\bm{p}}
with
{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}
and
{\bm{p}}\in\Delta_{V}^{\varepsilon}
, we have
{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}{\bm{y}}={\bm{p}}
.
Therefore

{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}\bigl({\bm{y}}+F({\bm{y}})\bigr)={\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}{\bm{y}}+{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}{\bm{E}}\,G({\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}{\bm{y}})-{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}{\bm{E}}{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}{\bm{y}}=G({\bm{p}}),

where we used
{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{U}}{\bm{E}}={\bm{I}}_{V}
.
The output distribution is therefore
\mathrm{softmax}(G({\bm{p}}))
.
Write
G({\bm{p}})=\log{\bm{p}}+{\bm{e}}
with
\|{\bm{e}}\|_{\infty}<\eta
.
For each coordinate
b
,

\mathrm{softmax}(G({\bm{p}}))_{b}=\frac{p_{b}e^{e_{b}}}{\sum_{c=1}^{V}p_{c}e^{e_{c}}}.

Since
e^{-\eta}\leq e^{e_{c}}\leq e^{\eta}
for every
c
, the ratio
\mathrm{softmax}(G({\bm{p}}))_{b}/p_{b}
lies in
[e^{-2\eta},e^{2\eta}]
.
Thus

\left|\mathrm{softmax}(G({\bm{p}}))_{b}-p_{b}\right|\leq e^{2\eta}-1<\delta.

Taking the maximum over
b
proves the lemma.
∎

B.3
Details About Extrapolative Task Learning Mode: Explicit Formulas

This subsection derives the closed-form predictors that define the extrapolative task-learning mode
M2
introduced in Sec.
4.3
.
Concretely,
M2
is the Bayesian posterior predictive obtained from Eq.
6
by replacing the prior
\pi_{\mathrm{train}}
over the finite training support
{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathrm{train}}
with the
generative
prior over the full latent space
{\mathcal{Z}}
, i.e. the same parametric distribution that was used to sample tasks during training (symmetric Dirichlet for
E1
and
E3
, isotropic Gaussian for
E2
; cf. Sec.
3
).
For each of
E1
–
E3
, conjugacy of this prior with the per-token likelihood collapses the integral over
{\mathcal{Z}}
into a closed-form predictor that depends on the observed sequence only through a low-dimensional context statistic: a smoothed empirical unigram for
E1
, the ridge solution for
E2
, and a smoothed empirical bigram for
E3
.
The derivations below thus pin down the concrete predictive targets that a model implementing
M2
must encode; whether the trained model actually encodes them, and where in the residual stream, is a separate empirical question taken up in the orthogonal-subspace decomposition of Sec.
D.7
.

Biased dice (
E1
).

Each latent task
z={\bm{p}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}=\Delta^{V-1}
is sampled from the symmetric Dirichlet prior

\mathrm{Dir}(\bm{1}_{V})
.
More generally, for
{\bm{p}}\in\Delta^{V-1}
drawn from
\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_{0}\bm{1}_{V})
, the probability density function is

f\left(p_{1},\ldots,p_{V};\alpha_{0}\right)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{0}V)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{0})^{V}}\prod_{a=1}^{V}p_{a}^{\alpha_{0}-1}.

Recall the token count
n_{a}(t)
defined in Equation (
18
).
The posterior predictive after observing
s_{\leq t}
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(s_{t+1}=a\mid s_{\leq t})

\displaystyle=\int_{{\bm{p}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\mathbb{P}(s_{t+1}=a\mid{\bm{p}},s_{\leq t})\,\mathbb{P}({\bm{p}}\mid s_{\leq t})\,d{\bm{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{{\bm{p}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\mathbb{P}(s_{t+1}=a\mid{\bm{p}})\,\mathbb{P}(s_{\leq t}\mid{\bm{p}})\,\mathbb{P}({\bm{p}})\,d{\bm{p}}}{\int_{{\bm{p}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\mathbb{P}(s_{\leq t}\mid{\bm{p}})\,\mathbb{P}({\bm{p}})\,d{\bm{p}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{{\bm{p}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}p_{a}\prod_{b=1}^{V}p_{b}^{n_{b}(t)+\alpha_{0}-1}\,d{\bm{p}}}{\int_{{\bm{p}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\prod_{b=1}^{V}p_{b}^{n_{b}(t)+\alpha_{0}-1}\,d{\bm{p}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(n_{a}(t)+\alpha_{0}+1)\prod_{b\neq a}^{V}\Gamma(n_{b}(t)+\alpha_{0})}{\Gamma(t+V\alpha_{0}+1)}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(t+V\alpha_{0})}{\prod_{b=1}^{V}\Gamma(n_{b}(t)+\alpha_{0})}

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{a}(t)+\alpha_{0}}{t+V\alpha_{0}},

which is a smoothed empirical unigram estimator.

Noisy linear regression (
E2
).

Each latent task
z={\bm{w}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}=\mathbb{R}^{D}
is drawn from an isotropic Gaussian prior

{\bm{w}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},\,\tau^{-2}\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}}_{D}),

which matches the generative prior of Sec.
3
when
\tau^{2}=\sigma^{2}
.
Conditional on
{\bm{w}}
, inputs are i.i.d.
{\bm{x}}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{D})
and

y_{t}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{w}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{w}},\,\sigma^{2})
.
For the prediction of
y_{t+1}
at a fresh covariate
{\bm{x}}_{t+1}
, let

{\bm{X}}_{\leq t}=({\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{t})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{t\times D}
and

{\bm{y}}_{\leq t}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{t})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{t}
collect the observed pairs.
By Gaussian–Gaussian conjugacy, the posterior over
{\bm{w}}
is again Gaussian:

{\bm{w}}\mid{\bm{X}}_{\leq t},{\bm{y}}_{\leq t}\;\sim\;\mathcal{N}\!\left(\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{\mathrm{ridge}}(t),\;\sigma^{2}\,{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}\right),\qquad{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}:=\bigl({\bm{X}}_{\leq t}^{\top}{\bm{X}}_{\leq t}+\tau^{2}{\bm{I}}_{D}\bigr)^{-1},

where
\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{\mathrm{ridge}}(t)
is the ridge solution
[
46
,
32
,
2
]

\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{\mathrm{ridge}}(t)\;=\;\bigl({\bm{X}}_{\leq t}^{\top}{\bm{X}}_{\leq t}+\tau^{2}{\bm{I}}_{D}\bigr)^{-1}{\bm{X}}_{\leq t}^{\top}{\bm{y}}_{\leq t}.

Marginalising
{\bm{w}}
yields the posterior predictive

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(y_{t+1}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t+1},{\bm{X}}_{\leq t},{\bm{y}}_{\leq t}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}\mathbb{P}(y_{t+1}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t+1},{\bm{w}})\,\mathbb{P}({\bm{w}}\mid{\bm{X}}_{\leq t},{\bm{y}}_{\leq t})\,d{\bm{w}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\!\left({\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\top}\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{\mathrm{ridge}}(t),\;\,\sigma^{2}\bigl(1+{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}\,{\bm{x}}_{t+1}\bigr)\right).

Hence the posterior predictive mean is the ridge regressor evaluated at
{\bm{x}}_{t+1}
,
and the predictive variance inflates the noise level
\sigma^{2}
by the
input-dependent factor
1+{\bm{x}}_{t+1}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}{\bm{x}}_{t+1}
that captures
remaining uncertainty about
{\bm{w}}
.

Mixture of Markov chains (
E3
).

Each latent task
z={\bm{P}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}=(\Delta^{V-1})^{V}
is a row-stochastic

V\times V
transition matrix, with each row drawn independently from the
symmetric Dirichlet prior used to generate the training tasks (Sec.
3
):

{\bm{P}}_{a,\cdot}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\;\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_{0}\bm{1}_{V}),\qquad a=1,\ldots,V.

For a Markov sequence
s_{\leq t}
generated under
{\bm{P}}
from a uniform initial
distribution, let

N_{ab}(t)\;:=\;\sum_{\tau=2}^{t}\mathbf{1}\{s_{\tau-1}=a,\,s_{\tau}=b\}\;\in\;\mathbb{N},\qquad N_{a\cdot}(t)\;:=\;\sum_{b=1}^{V}N_{ab}(t)\;\in\;\mathbb{N},

denote the count of
a\to b
transitions and the row total, respectively.
The likelihood factorises across rows,

\mathbb{P}(s_{\leq t}\mid{\bm{P}})\;=\;\prod_{a=1}^{V}\prod_{b=1}^{V}P_{a,b}^{N_{ab}(t)},

which is a product of multinomial likelihoods. Combined with the
row-independent Dirichlet prior, Dirichlet–multinomial conjugacy gives a
posterior that factorises across rows,

{\bm{P}}_{a,\cdot}\mid s_{\leq t}\;\sim\;\mathrm{Dir}\!\left(\alpha_{0}+N_{a1}(t),\,\ldots,\,\alpha_{0}+N_{aV}(t)\right),\qquad a=1,\ldots,V.

The posterior predictive at the next position, given current state
s_{t}=a
,
is then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(s_{t+1}=b\mid s_{t}=a,\,s_{\leq t}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{Z}}}P_{a,b}\,\mathbb{P}({\bm{P}}\mid s_{\leq t})\,d{\bm{P}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[P_{a,b}\mid s_{\leq t}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{ab}(t)+\alpha_{0}}{N_{a\cdot}(t)+V\alpha_{0}},

where the last equality uses the mean of a Dirichlet distribution.
This is a smoothed empirical bigram estimator, exactly analogous to the
smoothed empirical unigram derived for
E1
: it pools every observed
transition out of state
a
and interpolates with the uniform prior at rate
controlled by
\alpha_{0}
.

Summary.

In all three experiments, the predictor underlying
M2
is the exact
Bayesian posterior predictive under the parametric prior used to generate the
training tasks, but evaluated over the
full
latent space
{\mathcal{Z}}
rather
than only the finite training support
{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathrm{train}}
. Conjugacy of
each prior–likelihood pair (Dirichlet–categorical for
E1
,
Gaussian–Gaussian for
E2
, and row-wise Dirichlet–multinomial for

E3
) yields a closed-form predictor that depends on the observed
sequence only through a sufficient context statistic: the empirical unigram
counts
n_{a}(t)
for
E1
, the ridge solution
\hat{{\bm{w}}}_{\mathrm{ridge}}(t)

for
E2
, and the empirical bigram counts
N_{ab}(t)
for
E3
.
This explains why these context statistics arise naturally as the targets that
the model must encode in order to implement
M2
.

B.4
Proof of Theorem
2
in Section
4.3

This subsection states and proves the formal version of Theorem
2
.
The result makes precise the intuition that the extrapolative task-learning mode
M2
cannot be implemented from inside a task-vector subspace whose dimension
k_{\star}
is smaller than the intrinsic dimension
d_{0}
of the optimal prediction map: a volumetric packing argument forbids the required separation of latents within a
k_{\star}
-dimensional affine slab unless the alignment error
\varepsilon
grows accordingly.
Theorem
4
below gives the explicit lower bound on
\varepsilon
, which informally takes the form
\varepsilon\gtrsim(c-\delta)/L-C/(2^{d_{0}/k_{\star}}-1)
reported in Sec.
4.3
.

Notation.

We collect the notation used throughout this appendix, extending the conventions of Sec.
4
.
Let
{\mathcal{Z}}
denote the latent space with training set
{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathrm{train}}=[K]
.
At a fixed layer,
{\bm{h}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the hidden state at position
t
;

\{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}\}_{k\leq K}
and
\{{\bm{\nu}}_{a}\}_{a\in{\mathcal{V}}}
are the task vectors and token-encoding vectors (Definition
1
);

{\bm{\mu}}_{t,k,a}:=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{h}}_{t}\mid z{=}k,\,s_{t}{=}a]
are the cell means (Eq.
1
).
We assume
P0
together with the existence of the limiting cell mean

{\bm{\mu}}({z},a):=\lim_{t\to\infty}{\bm{\mu}}_{t,{z},a}
for each
({z},a)
in the
neighborhood of interest. (Note that
P0
alone controls only the
conditional residual variance
\mathbb{E}\|{\bm{h}}_{t}-{\bm{\mu}}_{t,z,s_{t}}\|^{2}
and does not by
itself guarantee convergence of
{\bm{\mu}}_{t,{z},a}
; we therefore impose the
existence of the limit explicitly.) Under these assumptions the (long-context)
decoder maps
{\bm{\mu}}({z},a)
to the Bayes-optimal next-token distribution

\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid{z},a)
.

Setup.

We now introduce the symbols specific to the simplex-confinement question, augmenting the notation of Sec.
4.3
. Fix an anchor token
a_{\star}\in{\mathcal{V}}
and write

{\bm{p}}({z}):=\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid{z},a_{\star})\in\Delta^{V-1},\qquad{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z}):={\bm{\mu}}({z},a_{\star})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

for the conditional next-token distribution and the limiting cell mean at
a_{\star}
. Let

\mathcal{T}:=\operatorname{span}({\bm{\theta}}_{2}-{\bm{\theta}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\theta}}_{K}-{\bm{\theta}}_{1}),\qquad\mathcal{S}:={\bm{\nu}}_{a_{\star}}+\mathcal{T},

denote the task-vector subspace and its affine translate at
{\bm{\nu}}_{a_{\star}}
, and set
k_{\star}:=\dim(\mathcal{T})\leq K-1
. We assume
k_{\star}\geq 1
, as otherwise
\mathcal{S}
is a singleton and the conclusions are trivial.

Discrete-output scope.

The theorem is stated for finite discrete output spaces:
a_{\star}\in\mathcal{V}
and

{\bm{p}}(z)=\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid z,a_{\star})\in\Delta^{V-1}
. It therefore directly covers the discrete-output settings
E1
and
E3
. The linear-regression setting E2 is an empirical continuous-output analogue. A formal continuous-output version would require replacing the simplex-valued map
{\bm{p}}
by an appropriate notion of distance between posterior-predictive regression distributions. We omit this extension for simplicity.

Gauge convention

The affine anchor in
\mathcal{S}={\bm{\nu}}_{a_{\star}}+\mathcal{T}
is a gauge choice. The proof below uses only the affine set
\mathcal{S}
and distances
\operatorname{dist}({\bm{\mu}}_{\star}(z),\mathcal{S})
, not the separate decomposition of its anchor into global-mean and token components. Indeed, if
P1
is written in the centered gauge

{\bm{h}}_{t}\approx{\bm{\mu}}_{t}+{\bm{\theta}}_{z}+{\bm{\nu}}_{s_{t}}

and
{\bm{\mu}}_{t}\to{\bm{\mu}}_{\infty}
, then the long-context ID cell means at anchor token

a_{\star}
lie in

{\bm{\mu}}_{\infty}+{\bm{\nu}}_{a_{\star}}+\mathcal{T}.

We absorb this common translate into the affine anchor, equivalently writing

\bar{{\bm{\nu}}}_{a_{\star}}:={\bm{\mu}}_{\infty}+{\bm{\nu}}_{a_{\star}},\qquad\mathcal{S}=\bar{{\bm{\nu}}}_{a_{\star}}+T,

and then drop the bar. This is only a notational convention for interpreting
\mathcal{S}

under
P1
; Theorem
2
itself assumes only the limiting cell means
{\bm{\mu}}(z,a)
specified above.

Definition 2

(Local regularity setup)
.

Fix a base point
{z}_{0}\in\operatorname{int}({\mathcal{Z}})
and assume that
{\bm{p}}
is
C^{1}
on a neighborhood of
{z}_{0}
. Let

{\bm{J}}_{0}:=\nabla_{{z}}{\bm{p}}({z}_{0}),\qquad d_{0}:=\operatorname{rank}({\bm{J}}_{0})\geq 1,\qquad\sigma_{0}:=s_{d_{0}}({\bm{J}}_{0})>0,\qquad\mathcal{E}:=(\ker{\bm{J}}_{0})^{\perp},

where
s_{d_{0}}({\bm{J}}_{0})
denotes the smallest nonzero singular value of
{\bm{J}}_{0}
. Choose a radius
r_{0}>0
with
B({z}_{0},r_{0})\subseteq{\mathcal{Z}}
, and define

U_{\mathcal{E},0}:=({z}_{0}+\mathcal{E})\cap B({z}_{0},r_{0}).

Then
U_{\mathcal{E},0}
is a
d_{0}
-dimensional ball in the affine subspace
{z}_{0}+\mathcal{E}
along which
{\bm{p}}
has full local rank; the integer
d_{0}
makes precise the “number of independent directions along which
{\bm{p}}({z})
varies” from Sec.
4.3
.

The next theorem formalizes the informal claim of Theorem
2
in Sec.
4.3
. Throughout, we write

\operatorname{dist}({\bm{x}},\mathcal{S}):=\inf_{{\bm{c}}\in\mathcal{S}}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{c}}\|_{2}

for the Euclidean distance from a point
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to the affine set
\mathcal{S}
, and we collect the regularity assumptions used in the theorem.

Definition 3

(Local decoder regularity)
.

Let
\hat{{\bm{p}}}({z})
be the predictor obtained by passing
{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z})
through the model’s decoder. We say the setup is
(M,L,\delta)
-regular on a neighborhood

\mathcal{U}\subseteq{\mathcal{Z}}
of
{z}_{0}
if there exist constants
M<\infty
,
L>0
, and
\delta\geq 0
such that, for all
{z},{z}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}
,

\displaystyle\|{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z})-{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z}^{\prime})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq M,

(7)

\displaystyle\|\hat{{\bm{p}}}({z})-\hat{{\bm{p}}}({z}^{\prime})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq L\,\|{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z})-{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z}^{\prime})\|_{2},

(8)

\displaystyle\|\hat{{\bm{p}}}({z})-{\bm{p}}({z})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\delta.

(9)

We further define the worst-case distance from
{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}
to the affine task subspace on
\mathcal{U}
,

\varepsilon:=\sup_{{z}\in\mathcal{U}}\operatorname{dist}\bigl({\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z}),\mathcal{S}\bigr).

(10)

Theorem 4

(Finite-context packing obstruction)
.

Assume the setup of Definition
2
and that the

(M,L,\delta)
-regularity of Definition
3

holds on a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}\subseteq{\mathcal{Z}}
of
{z}_{0}
.
Then there exists
r_{\mathrm{loc}}\in(0,r_{0}]
such that

U_{\mathcal{E}}:=({z}_{0}+\mathcal{E})\cap B({z}_{0},r_{\mathrm{loc}})\subseteq\mathcal{U},\qquad\alpha:=\sigma_{0}/2,

and the following hold.

(a) Local co-Lipschitz estimate for the ground-truth map.

For all
{z},{z}^{\prime}\in U_{\mathcal{E}}
,

\|{\bm{p}}({z})-{\bm{p}}({z}^{\prime})\|_{2}\geq\alpha\,\|{z}-{z}^{\prime}\|_{2}.

(11)

(b) Finite-context packing inequality.

For any
0<\rho<r_{\mathrm{loc}}
such that

\frac{\alpha\rho-2\delta}{L}>2\varepsilon,

one has

\left(\frac{r_{\mathrm{loc}}}{\rho}\right)^{d_{0}}\leq\left(1+\frac{2M}{\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\alpha\rho-2\delta}{L}\right)^{2}-4\varepsilon^{2}}}\right)^{k_{\star}}.

(12)

(c) Lower bound on the confinement radius.

For any
0<\rho<r_{\mathrm{loc}}
such that
\alpha\rho>2\delta
,

\varepsilon\geq\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(\frac{\alpha\rho-2\delta}{L}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{2M}{\left(r_{\mathrm{loc}}/\rho\right)^{d_{0}/k_{\star}}-1}\right)^{2}\right]_{+}^{1/2},

(13)

where
[x]_{+}:=\max\{x,0\}
.

Proof.

Choose a linear isometry

{\bm{W}}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\to\mathcal{E}.

Define

\widetilde{{\bm{p}}}({\bm{u}}):={\bm{p}}({z}_{0}+{\bm{W}}{\bm{u}}),\qquad{\bm{u}}\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}}({\bm{0}},r_{0}).

Then
\widetilde{{\bm{p}}}
is
C^{1}
, and

\nabla\widetilde{{\bm{p}}}({\bm{0}})={\bm{J}}_{0}{\bm{W}}.

Since
{\bm{W}}
is an isometry onto
\mathcal{E}=(\ker{\bm{J}}_{0})^{\perp}
, the smallest singular value of

\nabla\widetilde{{\bm{p}}}({\bm{0}})
equals
\sigma_{0}
. By continuity of

\nabla\widetilde{{\bm{p}}}
at
{\bm{0}}
, after shrinking
r_{\mathrm{loc}}\in(0,r_{0}]
if necessary,
we may assume both that
U_{\mathcal{E}}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
and that

\sup_{\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2}<r_{\mathrm{loc}}}\|\nabla\widetilde{{\bm{p}}}({\bm{u}})-\nabla\widetilde{{\bm{p}}}({\bm{0}})\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{\sigma_{0}}{2}.

Now fix
{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}}({\bm{0}},r_{\mathrm{loc}})
. By the fundamental theorem of calculus,

\widetilde{{\bm{p}}}({\bm{u}})-\widetilde{{\bm{p}}}({\bm{v}})=\nabla\widetilde{{\bm{p}}}({\bm{0}})({\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}})+\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl(\nabla\widetilde{{\bm{p}}}\bigl({\bm{v}}+\tau({\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}})\bigr)-\nabla\widetilde{{\bm{p}}}({\bm{0}})\Bigr)({\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}})\,d\tau.

Therefore

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{{\bm{p}}}({\bm{u}})-\widetilde{{\bm{p}}}({\bm{v}})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\|\nabla\widetilde{{\bm{p}}}({\bm{0}})({\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}})\|_{2}-\frac{\sigma_{0}}{2}\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sigma_{0}}{2}\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{v}}\|_{2}.

Transporting this estimate back through
{\bm{W}}
gives (
11
). This proves part (a).

Fix
0<\rho<r_{\mathrm{loc}}
, and let

\{{z}_{1},\dots,{z}_{N}\}\subset U_{\mathcal{E}}

be a maximal
\rho
-separated subset. Since the set is maximal,

U_{\mathcal{E}}\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(({z}_{0}+\mathcal{E})\cap B({z}_{i},\rho)\bigr).

Taking
d_{0}
-dimensional Euclidean volumes inside the affine space
{z}_{0}+\mathcal{E}
, the standard ball-volume comparison (e.g. Corollary 4.2.13 in
[
41
]
) yields

N\geq\left(\frac{r_{\mathrm{loc}}}{\rho}\right)^{d_{0}}.

(14)

For
i\neq j
, (
11
) and
(
9
) give

\displaystyle\|\hat{{\bm{p}}}({z}_{i})-\hat{{\bm{p}}}({z}_{j})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\|{\bm{p}}({z}_{i})-{\bm{p}}({z}_{j})\|_{2}-\|\hat{{\bm{p}}}({z}_{i})-{\bm{p}}({z}_{i})\|_{2}-\|\hat{{\bm{p}}}({z}_{j})-{\bm{p}}({z}_{j})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\|{z}_{i}-{z}_{j}\|_{2}-2\delta

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\rho-2\delta.

Using (
8
), we obtain

\|{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z}_{i})-{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z}_{j})\|_{2}\geq\lambda(\rho),\qquad\lambda(\rho):=\frac{\alpha\rho-2\delta}{L}.

Let
\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}
be the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{S}
, and set

{\bm{y}}_{i}:=\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z}_{i})\in\mathcal{S},\qquad{\bm{u}}_{i}:={\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z}_{i})-{\bm{y}}_{i}\in\mathcal{T}^{\perp}.

By (
10
),

\|{\bm{u}}_{i}\|_{2}=\operatorname{dist}\!\bigl({\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z}_{i}),\mathcal{S}\bigr)\leq\varepsilon.

Since
{\bm{y}}_{i}-{\bm{y}}_{j}\in\mathcal{T}
and
{\bm{u}}_{i}-{\bm{u}}_{j}\in\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
, the Pythagorean theorem gives

\|{\bm{y}}_{i}-{\bm{y}}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}=\|{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z}_{i})-{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z}_{j})\|_{2}^{2}-\|{\bm{u}}_{i}-{\bm{u}}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\lambda(\rho)^{2}-4\varepsilon^{2}.

If
\lambda(\rho)>2\varepsilon
, the points
{\bm{y}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{y}}_{N}
are therefore
\Delta(\rho):=\sqrt{\lambda(\rho)^{2}-4\varepsilon^{2}}
-separated in
\mathcal{S}
.

Setting
{\bm{c}}_{0}:=\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}{\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z}_{0})
, the
1
-Lipschitz property of
\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}
and (
7
) give
\|{\bm{y}}_{i}-{\bm{c}}_{0}\|_{2}\leq M
, so

\{{\bm{y}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{y}}_{N}\}\subseteq\mathcal{S}\cap B({\bm{c}}_{0},M).

Assume now that
\lambda(\rho)>2\varepsilon
. The balls
\mathcal{S}\cap B({\bm{y}}_{i},\Delta(\rho)/2)
,
i=1,\dots,N
, are pairwise disjoint and contained in
\mathcal{S}\cap B({\bm{c}}_{0},M+\Delta(\rho)/2)
. Comparing
k_{\star}
-dimensional Euclidean ball volumes once more (Corollary 4.2.13 in
[
41
]
) gives

N\leq\bigl(1+2M/\Delta(\rho)\bigr)^{k_{\star}}.

Combining with (
14
) yields (
12
), proving part (b).

Finally, fix
0<\rho<r_{\mathrm{loc}}
with
\alpha\rho>2\delta
, and define

A(\rho):=\frac{2M}{\left(r_{\mathrm{loc}}/\rho\right)^{d_{0}/k_{\star}}-1}.

If
\lambda(\rho)^{2}\leq A(\rho)^{2}
, then (
13
) is immediate,
since its right-hand side is zero. Assume therefore that
\lambda(\rho)^{2}>A(\rho)^{2}
.
If, toward a contradiction, (
13
) failed, then we would have

4\varepsilon^{2}<\lambda(\rho)^{2}-A(\rho)^{2}.

In particular,
\lambda(\rho)>2\varepsilon
, so part (b) applies and yields

\left(\frac{r_{\mathrm{loc}}}{\rho}\right)^{d_{0}/k_{\star}}\leq 1+\frac{2M}{\sqrt{\lambda(\rho)^{2}-4\varepsilon^{2}}}.

Rearranging,

\sqrt{\lambda(\rho)^{2}-4\varepsilon^{2}}\leq\frac{2M}{\left(r_{\mathrm{loc}}/\rho\right)^{d_{0}/k_{\star}}-1}=A(\rho),

which contradicts
4\varepsilon^{2}<\lambda(\rho)^{2}-A(\rho)^{2}
. Hence
4\varepsilon^{2}\geq\lambda(\rho)^{2}-A(\rho)^{2}
, i.e.,

\varepsilon\geq\frac{1}{2}\bigl[\lambda(\rho)^{2}-A(\rho)^{2}\bigr]_{+}^{1/2},

which is (
13
). This proves part (c).
∎

From Theorem
4
to the informal Theorem
2
.

The informal bound stated in Sec.
4.3
is the special case
of part (c) obtained by fixing the inner radius to a constant fraction of the
outer radius and discarding the squares.

Corollary 1

(Distance-to-task-subspace lower bound; formal form of Theorem
2
)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
4
, choose

\rho:=r_{\mathrm{loc}}/2
and assume
\alpha\rho>2\delta
, where

\alpha=\sigma_{0}/2
is the local co-Lipschitz constant from
Theorem
4
. Then

\sup_{{z}\in\mathcal{U}}\operatorname{dist}\bigl({\bm{\mu}}_{\star}({z}),\mathcal{S}\bigr)\;\geq\;\frac{c-\delta}{L}\;-\;\frac{C}{2^{d_{0}/k_{\star}}-1},

(15)

with

c:=\alpha r_{\mathrm{loc}}/4
and
C:=M
.

Proof.

With
\rho=r_{\mathrm{loc}}/2
one has
(r_{\mathrm{loc}}/\rho)^{d_{0}/k_{\star}}=2^{d_{0}/k_{\star}}
, and part (c) of Theorem
4
reduces to

\varepsilon\;\geq\;\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\,A^{2}-B^{2}\,\Bigr]_{+}^{1/2},\qquad A:=\frac{\alpha r_{\mathrm{loc}}/2-2\delta}{L},\quad B:=\frac{2M}{2^{d_{0}/k_{\star}}-1}.

Since
\sqrt{A^{2}-B^{2}}\geq A-B
whenever
A\geq B\geq 0
(as
A+B\geq A-B
), we obtain

\varepsilon\;\geq\;\tfrac{1}{2}[A-B]_{+}\;\geq\;\frac{\alpha r_{\mathrm{loc}}/4-\delta}{L}-\frac{M}{2^{d_{0}/k_{\star}}-1}.

Identifying the right-hand side with
\frac{c-\delta}{L}-\frac{C}{2^{d_{0}/k_{\star}}-1}
yields (
15
).
∎

Corollary
1
thus instantiates the informal
Theorem
2
of the main text: the local constants

c
and
C
depend on the local co-Lipschitz constant
\alpha
, the
confinement radius
r_{\mathrm{loc}}
, and the local diameter
M
, but not
on the latent dimensions
d_{0}
or
k_{\star}
. The decay in

2^{d_{0}/k_{\star}}
is therefore the genuine source of the obstruction: when

d_{0}\gg k_{\star}
, the second term in (
15
) is
negligible, leaving a distance-to-
\mathcal{S}
lower bound of order

(c-\delta)/L
. Theorem
4
is strictly
stronger, since it provides a packing inequality that holds at every scale

\rho<r_{\mathrm{loc}}
and quantifies the interaction between the prediction
error
\delta
, the decoder Lipschitz constant
L
, and the confinement
radius
\varepsilon
.

C.1
Extracting and Estimating Task Vectors

This subsection records the precise procedure used to extract residual-stream hidden states from the trained transformers and to estimate the task vectors
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{k}
that play the role of simplex anchors throughout Sec.
5
.
The extraction recipe and averaging-based estimator below are reused without modification by every empirical analysis in Sec.
C
(variance and additivity tests, simplex-projection coefficients
\beta_{t,k}
, simplex interventions) and by the orthogonal-subspace interventions in Sec.
D
.
We write
{\bm{H}}^{(\ell)}=[{\bm{h}}_{1}^{(\ell)},\dots,{\bm{h}}_{T}^{(\ell)}]^{\top}
for the
collection of residual stream representations across positions.
We consider a Pre-LayerNorm (LN) decoder-only transformer, where each layer updates a residual stream
{\bm{H}}^{(\ell-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}
via

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\bm{H}}}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle={\bm{H}}^{(\ell-1)}+\texttt{MHA}\bigl(\texttt{LN}({\bm{H}}^{(\ell-1)})\bigr),

\displaystyle{\bm{H}}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{{\bm{H}}}^{(\ell)}+\texttt{MLP}\bigl(\texttt{LN}(\widetilde{{\bm{H}}}^{(\ell)})\bigr).

We extract representations from the residual stream after the full layer
update, i.e., from
{\bm{H}}^{(\ell)}
, and for each position
t
collect the
layer-wise trajectory
\{{\bm{h}}_{t}^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=0}^{L-1}
.

Extraction positions.

For discrete-token tasks (
E1
,
E3
,
E4
), the input
sequence is
[s_{1},\dots,s_{T}]
and we extract at all positions,
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
. For the linear regression task
(
E2
), the sequence alternates between inputs and targets,

[{\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2},y_{2},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{T/2},y_{T/2}]
; we extract only at
input positions
{\bm{x}}_{t}
, which are the prediction locations for
y_{t}
.

Having specified the residual-stream extraction recipe above, we now describe how the task-vector estimates
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{k}
themselves are computed from the extracted hidden states.
This averaging-based estimator produces every
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{k}
used in the empirical evaluations of Sec.
C.2
–Sec.
C.4.3
and in the simplex-/orthogonal-subspace interventions of Sec.
C.5
and Sec.
D.5
; it is faithful to Definition
1
in the long-context limit.

Estimation by averaging.

We estimate task vectors directly from the definition in Eq.
2
.
For each layer
\ell
and a window of late context positions
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{est}}
, we pool hidden states across positions and sequences for each task
k
:

\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{k}\;=\;\underbrace{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{est}}|\,B}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{est}}}\sum_{i:\,z_{i}=k}{\bm{h}}_{t,i}}_{\text{task-conditional mean}}\;-\;\underbrace{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{est}}|\,B}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{est}}}\sum_{i:\,z_{i}=k^{\prime}}{\bm{h}}_{t,i}\Bigr)}_{\text{grand mean}},

where the sums over
i
run over all
B
sequences assigned to each task.
By construction
\sum_{k}\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{k}={\bm{0}}
.

\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{est}}
is a contiguous window of
\approx 15
–
30

late
context positions, chosen so that the Bayesian posterior
\mathbb{P}(z\mid s_{\leq t})
has largely concentrated and
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{h}}_{t}\mid z=k]
approximates its long-context limit (Eq.
2
); empirically, this is the regime where
P0
–
P1
hold to high accuracy (Sec.
C.2
, Figure
9
).

\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}({\bm{h}}_{t}\mid z,s_{t})/\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}({\bm{h}}_{t})
, the fraction of hidden-state variance that is
not
explained by the task identity
z
together with the current token
s_{t}
.
A small ratio at large
t
indicates that the task and current token jointly account for almost all variation in
{\bm{h}}_{t}
, which is the empirical content of
P0
–
P1
.
This complements Table

(a) in the main text (which only reports the value at the last position) by displaying the full layer-by-layer trajectory across context positions for

–

.

C.2.2
Testing additive separability

We test whether the conditional mean
\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{t,k,a}:=\hat{\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{h}}_{t}\mid z=k,s_{t}=a]
decomposes additively as
\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{t,k,a}\approx\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{t}+\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{k}+\hat{\bm{\nu}}_{a}
, where the task effect
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{k}
and token effect
\hat{\bm{\nu}}_{a}
do not interact. The key idea is to compare an
additive
model against a
full
model that includes a task–token interaction term.

Fix a layer
\ell
and position
t
. For experiments
E1
and
E3
with discrete tokens
a\in{\mathcal{V}}
, the additive model is

{\bm{h}}_{t}\;=\;{\bm{\mu}}_{t}+{\bm{\theta}}_{k}+{\bm{\nu}}_{a},

while the full model allows a separate mean for each
(k,a)
cell. This is a standard two-way ANOVA. A two-way decomposition of the between-cell variance gives

\underbrace{\sum_{k,a}\|\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{t,k,a}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{t}\|^{2}}_{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{between}}}\;=\;\underbrace{V\sum_{k}\|\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{k}\|^{2}}_{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{task}}}\;+\;\underbrace{K\sum_{a}\|\hat{\bm{\nu}}_{a}\|^{2}}_{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{token}}}\;+\;\underbrace{\sum_{k,a}\|\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{t,k,a}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{t}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{k}-\hat{\bm{\nu}}_{a}\|^{2}}_{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{interaction}}},

where
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{k}=(1/V)\sum_{a}\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{t,k,a}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{t}

and
\hat{\bm{\nu}}_{a}=(1/K)\sum_{k}\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{t,k,a}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{t}
.

For experiment
E2
, where the current token
{\bm{x}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
is a continuous covariate, the additive model is

{\bm{h}}_{t}\;=\;{\bm{\mu}}_{t}+{\bm{\theta}}_{k}+{\bm{B}}\,{\bm{x}}_{t},

where
{\bm{B}}
is a shared slope matrix, while the full model replaces
{\bm{B}}
with task-specific slopes
{\bm{B}}_{k}
. Both models are fit by ordinary least squares (ANCOVA).

In both cases, we measure the
interaction proportion
: the fraction of explained variance attributable to the interaction term. For discrete tokens this is
\eta^{2}_{\mathrm{interaction}}=\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{interaction}}/\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{between}}
. For continuous covariates, we define the analogous quantity
\eta^{2}_{\mathrm{interaction}}=(R^{2}_{\mathrm{full}}-R^{2}_{\mathrm{additive}})/R^{2}_{\mathrm{full}}

C.3
OLS probe decomposition for experiment

This subsection complements the aggregate analysis in Sec.

with a layerwise variance decomposition of the hidden states in
E3
.
The simplex-projected interpolation
R^{2}
(Figure

) plateaus at
0.60
and
0.52
in the last two layers, and the ANOVA interaction proportion (Table

(b)) flags a parallel breakdown of additivity, but neither metric identifies what the residual interaction encodes.
We regress each hidden state on three factors (the task label, the current token, and the model’s own output logits) and use the logits as a diagnostic for residual-stream directions aligned with the next-token distribution.
Including the logits absorbs essentially all of the late-layer interaction, indicating that the apparent breakdown of additivity is
not a genuine task–token interaction
but reflects hidden-state components devoted to computing the next-token prediction.
The decomposition further shows that task identity is most cleanly represented in middle layers and is progressively subsumed into the next-token signal in later layers, providing a quantitative account of why task vectors are best extracted from middle layers
[
16
]
.

Probe construction.

We regress the residual-stream representation
{\bm{h}}_{t}
on three feature groups computed from the same forward pass: the task label
z
, the current token
s_{t}
, and the model’s output logit
\hat{\bm{\ell}}_{t}
.
The first two are the variables that property
P1
predicts to enter
{\bm{h}}_{t}
additively, so any contribution to the late-layer interaction must be absorbed by the third.
We use
\hat{\bm{\ell}}_{t}
because it is precisely the direction of the residual stream that the unembedding reads off, and therefore serves as a known summary of the next-token-prediction signal carried by
{\bm{h}}_{t}
.
Before fitting we subtract the per-position mean across sequences,
\bar{{\bm{h}}}_{t}\coloneqq{\bm{h}}_{t}-\bar{{\bm{h}}}_{\cdot,t}
, to suppress position-dependent variation induced by the rotary positional code.
The OLS model is

\bar{{\bm{h}}}_{t}\;=\;{\bm{W}}_{z}\,\tilde{{\bm{e}}}_{z}\;+\;{\bm{W}}_{s}\,\tilde{{\bm{e}}}_{s_{t}}\;+\;{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{logit}}\,\hat{\bm{\ell}}_{t}\;+\;{\bm{b}}\;+\;{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{t},

(16)

where
\tilde{{\bm{e}}}_{z}\in\{0,1\}^{K-1}
and
\tilde{{\bm{e}}}_{s_{t}}\in\{0,1\}^{V-1}
are the one-hot encodings of
z\in[K]
and
s_{t}
with the last column dropped (to avoid collinearity with the intercept),
\hat{\bm{\ell}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
is the output logit vector, and
{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an intercept.
We fit on
2^{13}
sequences from the fully trained model for experiment
E3
(
K{=}3
major tasks, no minor tasks), evaluated at positions
t\in[170,190)
.

Marginal and partial

.

For a feature group

is the

from regressing

on

(17)

quantifies the unique contribution of

is the

of the reduced model obtained from Eq.

Reporting both is necessary because the regressors overlap:

); the task and token indicators are nearly orthogonal (

The partial

Layerwise findings.

The decomposition pinpoints the source of the late-layer non-additivity: the next-token-prediction signal

progressively dominates the residual stream at deeper layers

As a corollary, the task label is encoded as a near-independent direction only at middle layers (2–3), while the current token retains a dominant unique contribution at all depths.
Quantitatively, the joint model attains

\bar{{\bm{h}}}_{t}
.
The next-token-prediction signal

rises monotonically with depth:

0.05

C.4.1
Bayesian Posterior Computation under
M1

This subsubsection derives the closed-form expressions for the oracle Bayesian posterior
\alpha_{t,k}:=\mathbb{P}(z=k\mid s_{1},\ldots,s_{t})
that serve as the comparison target throughout the paper.
Under the task-retrieval mode
M1
(Sec.
4.2
) we take the prior to be uniform over the finite training support
{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathrm{train}}=[K]
, so that the integral in Eq.
6
reduces to a finite sum that admits a closed form for each of
E1
–
E3
.
The resulting
\alpha_{t,k}
play two roles in our analyses: they are the targets against which the empirical simplex coefficients
\beta_{t,k}
are compared to assess Property
P3
(Sec.
5.1
, Figure
3
; Sec.
C.4.3
), and they define the
M1
predictive distribution used in the KL phase-transition analysis of Sec.
6
.
Throughout, we use the task index convention to refer to the latent variables for simplicity.

Biased Dice (
E1
)

Recall that each task
z=k
is parameterized by a probability vector

{\bm{p}}_{k}=(p_{k,1},\ldots,p_{k,V})\in\Delta^{V-1}
over
V
outcomes
(the “faces of the die”). Tokens
s_{1},s_{2},\ldots
are drawn i.i.d. from
\mathrm{Categorical}({\bm{p}}_{k})
.
The posterior at position
t
factorizes as

\mathbb{P}(z=k\mid s_{\leq t})\;=\;\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(s_{\leq t}\mid z=k\right)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}\left(s_{\leq t}\mid z=k\right)}=\frac{\prod_{\tau=1}^{t}p_{k,s_{\tau}}}{\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}\prod_{\tau=1}^{t}p_{k^{\prime},s_{\tau}}}.

Equivalently, let

n_{a}(t):=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\mathbf{1}\{s_{\tau}=a\}

(18)

be the count of token
a
in the first
t
tokens, the log-likelihood is

\log\mathbb{P}(s_{\leq t}\mid z=k)\;=\;\sum_{a=1}^{V}n_{a}(t)\,\log p_{k,a}.

The posterior is obtained by normalizing across all
K
tasks:

\alpha_{t,k}\;=\;\frac{\prod_{a=1}^{V}p_{k,a}^{\,n_{a}(t)}}{\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}\prod_{a=1}^{V}p_{k^{\prime},a}^{\,n_{a}(t)}}.

(19)

Noisy Linear Regression (
E2
)

Recall that each task
z=k
is parameterized by a weight vector
{\bm{w}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
The sequence consists of input–output pairs

({\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1}),({\bm{x}}_{2},y_{2}),\ldots
where

{\bm{x}}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},{\bm{I}}_{D})
and

y_{t}={\bm{w}}_{k}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}
with

\varepsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
independently.

At position
t
, define
{\bm{X}}_{\leq t}=({\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{t})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{t\times D}

and
{\bm{y}}_{\leq t}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{t})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{t}
. The cumulative
log-likelihood under task
k
is

\log p({\bm{y}}_{\leq t}\mid{\bm{X}}_{\leq t},z=k)\;=\;-\frac{t}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})\;-\;\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}(y_{\tau}-{\bm{w}}_{k}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{\tau})^{2}.

We define the cumulative sum of squared residuals

\mathrm{SSR}_{k}(t)=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}(y_{\tau}-{\bm{w}}_{k}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{\tau})^{2}
.
Because the filtering posterior at position
t
conditions only on past observations

({\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,({\bm{x}}_{t-1},y_{t-1})
, the SSR is evaluated at
t{-}1
:

\alpha_{t,k}\;=\;\frac{\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,\mathrm{SSR}_{k}(t{-}1)\bigr)}{\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\,\mathrm{SSR}_{k^{\prime}}(t{-}1)\bigr)},

(20)

where
\mathrm{SSR}_{k}(0)=0
(no observations available at the first position).

Latent Markov Chain (
E3
)

Recall that each task
z=k
is parameterized by a transition kernel
{\bm{P}}^{(k)}
. The state space is
{\mathcal{V}}
.
The first tokens carry no task-discriminative information; therefore
\alpha_{t,k}=1/K
for

t=1
. For
t>1
, each new token
s_{t}
provides a
likelihood contribution:

\mathbb{P}(s_{t}=a\mid s_{<t},\,z=k)\;=\;{\bm{P}}^{(k)}_{s_{t-1},a},

The posterior accumulates these contributions:

\mathbb{P}(z=k\mid s_{\leq t})\;=\;\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(s_{\leq t}\mid z=k\right)}{\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}\left(s_{\leq t}\mid z=k^{\prime}\right)}

which yields

\alpha_{t,k}\;=\;\frac{\prod_{\tau=2}^{t}{\bm{P}}^{(k)}_{s_{\tau-1},s_{\tau}}}{\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}\prod_{\tau=2}^{t}{\bm{P}}^{(k^{\prime})}_{s_{\tau-1},s_{\tau}}}.

(21)

C.4.2
Computing projection coefficients
\beta_{t,k}

This subsubsection details the two-step projection used to extract the simplex-constrained coefficients
\beta_{t,k}
that appear in the interpolation model of Eq.
4
(Sec.
5
).
These coefficients are the empirical proxies for the Bayesian posterior weights
\alpha_{t,k}
used in the posterior-alignment property
P3
, and the procedure below is the one used to produce every
\beta_{t,k}
reported in Figure
3
.
Let
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{1},\dots,\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the centered task vectors (Sec.
C.1
), and let

\bar{{\bm{h}}}_{t}\;=\;{\bm{h}}_{t}^{(\ell)}-\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t}

be the centered hidden state at position
t
and layer
\ell
, where
\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t}
is the empirical grand mean.
Following the main text, we obtain
\beta_{t,k}
by projecting
\bar{{\bm{h}}}_{t}
onto the task-vector simplex

\bigl\{\sum_{k}\beta_{k}\,\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{k}:\beta_{k}\geq 0,\,\sum_{k}\beta_{k}=1\bigr\}
,
implemented in two steps.

Step 1: Affine projection.

We first find coefficients satisfying

\bar{{\bm{h}}}_{t}\approx\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{t,k}\,\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{k}
with
\sum_{k}\beta_{t,k}=1
,
without enforcing nonnegativity.
Using
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{K}
as an anchor reduces this to an unconstrained least-squares problem
in
K-1
variables, solved via the pseudoinverse of the matrix of pairwise differences

(\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{1}-\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{K},\cdots,\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{K-1}-\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{K})
.
The resulting affine coefficients satisfy sum-to-one but may be negative.

Step 2: Simplex projection.

To enforce
\beta_{t,k}\geq 0
, we project the affine coefficients onto
\Delta^{K-1}

via the standard Euclidean simplex projection
[
9
]
: sort in decreasing order and apply a uniform threshold so that the result is nonnegative and sums to one.
The projected coefficients
\beta_{t,k}
are what Sec.
5
refers to as the simplex-projected interpolation coefficients.

C.5
Simplex Intervention Details

This subsection details the simplex-intervention experiment of Sec.
5.2
: at a chosen layer
\ell
we replace the task-subspace component of the residual stream with an arbitrary point
\sum_{k}\alpha_{t,k}^{*}\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{k}
in the task-vector simplex (with
{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}^{*}
drawn from
\Delta^{K-1}
), leave the orthogonal complement untouched, and measure how closely the steered model’s output matches the Bayesian mixture prediction that
{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}^{*}
dictates.
The purpose is causal: the alignment between
\beta_{t,k}
and
\alpha_{t,k}
established by Property
P3
(Sec.
5.1
) is observational, and could in principle reflect a hard task-selection rule rather than a true continuous interpolation.
The simplex steering, paired with the
mode-output
baseline introduced below, addresses both points: it shows that the model’s output tracks
{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}^{*}
throughout the simplex (not merely near vertices), supporting the main-text claim that the task-vector subspace is the variable through which the model interpolates between training tasks.
Let

{\bm{P}}_{\mathrm{task}}=\hat{{\bm{\Theta}}}\,(\hat{{\bm{\Theta}}}^{\top}\hat{{\bm{\Theta}}})^{\dagger}\hat{{\bm{\Theta}}}^{\top}

(22)

denote the orthogonal projector onto the task subspace, where
\hat{{\bm{\Theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times K}
stacks the centered task vectors as columns and
{\bm{A}}^{\dagger}
denotes the pseudoinverse of the matrix
{\bm{A}}
.
We substitute the task-subspace component with a target interpolation, replacing the model’s original coefficients
\beta_{t,k}
with coefficients
\alpha_{t,k}^{*}
randomly drawn from the simplex
\Delta^{K-1}
:

{\bm{h}}_{t}^{(\ell),\mathrm{int}}=\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{t,k}^{*}\,\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{k}+({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}_{\mathrm{task}})\bigl({\bm{h}}_{t}^{(\ell)}-\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{t}\bigr).

This steers the hidden representation to a chosen point in the task-vector simplex while leaving the orthogonal complement unchanged.
The model then completes the forward pass from layer
\ell
onward using the modified hidden state.

Comparing with an additional baseline: Mode-output.

A central claim of Sec.
5.2
is that task vectors implement a
continuous interpolation
over tasks rather than a discrete selection of the most likely one.
The natural alternative hypothesis is a
hard-selection
(nearest-neighbor) mechanism: the model identifies the dominant task
k^{*}=\arg\max_{k}\alpha_{t,k}^{*}
and predicts as if that task were certain.
To rule this out, we compare simplex steering against a
mode-output
baseline that realizes this hard-selection rule using the oracle Bayesian posterior over tasks. Across all three experiments, simplex steering tracks the target mixture
uniformly across
\Delta^{K-1}
, while the mode baseline matches the model
only near the vertices
and degrades sharply in the interior.
This shows that task vectors cannot be reduced to a nearest-neighbor or hard-assignment mechanism, strengthening the main-text conclusion that they encode and combine task information continuously across the simplex.

Mode-output baseline.

Given target coefficients
{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}^{*}
, the dominant task is

k^{*}\;=\;\arg\max_{k}\alpha_{t,k}^{*},

and the mode-output prediction is the Bayesian-optimal next-token distribution under that single task,
\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid z=k^{*},s_{t})
.
This baseline is computed entirely from the ground-truth generative model and does
not
involve a forward pass through the trained model; as such, it provides the strongest realization of the hard-selection alternative defined above and an upper bound on the performance any nearest-neighbor mechanism in task space could achieve.

Empirical comparison.

Figure
14
reports the prediction error (KL divergence or RMSE, depending on the experiment) of simplex steering and the mode-output baseline against the target mixture across
\Delta^{K-1}
for
E1
–
E3
.
Simplex steering attains uniformly low error throughout the simplex, including the interior region where multiple tasks contribute comparably and any hard-selection rule must discard substantial posterior mass.
The mode baseline, by contrast, matches the target only near the vertices and degrades sharply toward the interior, with error growing roughly with the entropy of
{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}^{*}
.
This pattern is precisely what the interpolation hypothesis predicts and what the hard-selection hypothesis cannot accommodate.

Major/Minor Training Mixture

This subsection describes the major/minor task-mixture training distribution used throughout Sec.

The minor-pool size

is the central experimental knob: small

Task pools.

Before training begins, two disjoint task pools are sampled once from the

•

Major pool.
Three tasks
\{z^{\mathrm{maj}}_{1},z^{\mathrm{maj}}_{2},z^{\mathrm{maj}}_{3}\}

\pi

•

Minor pool.

N_{\mathrm{minor}}

\{z^{\mathrm{minor}}_{1},\ldots,z^{\mathrm{minor}}_{N_{\mathrm{minor}}}\}

drawn independently from the same prior
\pi
, conditioned on being disjoint
from the major pool. We allow
N_{\mathrm{minor}}=0
as a degenerate case
in which the minor pool is empty and all sequences are drawn from the major
pool only.

The minor-pool size
N_{\mathrm{minor}}
is the primary control variable. The
full diversity sweep (Sec.

Sequence sampling.

For
N_{\mathrm{minor}}\geq 1
, each training sequence is generated by the
following two-stage procedure:

1.

Pool selection.
With probability
p_{\mathrm{minor}}=0.1
,
draw from the minor pool; with probability
1-p_{\mathrm{minor}}=0.9
,
draw from the major pool.

2.

Task selection.
Sample a task
z
uniformly at random from the
selected pool, then generate a sequence of length
T
from
p_{z}
.

For the degenerate case
N_{\mathrm{minor}}=0

Bernoulli pool-selection step is skipped and every sequence is drawn from the
major pool (equivalently, the effective

For

, this gives major tasks a dominant

:
1

weight over the minor pool regardless of
N_{\mathrm{minor}}
, so each

OOD tasks.

Each OOD task is obtained by drawing a fresh sample from the same
task-specific prior used to construct the major and minor pools: a Dirichlet
draw over categorical distributions for
E1
and
E3
, and an
isotropic Gaussian draw over weight vectors for
E2
. These draws are
made independently of the stored training pools and are not cached: every
batch of OOD tasks is generated afresh from the prior. Because the prior is
continuous, with probability one the resulting task does not coincide with
any task in the major or minor pool, so OOD tasks are never seen during
training. This ensures that OOD performance reflects genuine
out-of-distribution generalization rather than minor-task memorization.

Relation to task diversity.

When
N_{\mathrm{minor}}\in\{0,1\}
the minor pool is empty or contains a
single task, which the model can readily memorize; for small
N_{\mathrm{minor}}

the model essentially extends task-retrieval behavior to the minor pool. As

N_{\mathrm{minor}}
grows, the per-task training weight
0.1/N_{\mathrm{minor}}

becomes negligible and no individual minor task can be memorized, forcing the
model to develop a context-based generalization strategy. The transition in
representation geometry visible in Figure
6
reflects
this shift.

D.4
Pretrained LM Experiments

This subsection draws a qualitative analogy between our controlled two-mode framework and the representation geometry of a real pretrained language model, Qwen2.5-7B
[
30
]
. The comparison should be read in light of the property framework of Sec.
4.1
and the two-mode geometry developed in Sec.
4.2
, Sec.
4.3
, and Sec.
6.1
. In the synthetic setting,
P0
–
P3
can be tested directly because the latent tasks, task prior, token space, and oracle Bayesian posterior are all known. In a pretrained LLM, none of these objects are available: natural-language tasks do not come with a specified latent prior, the relevant token space is open-ended, and there is no ground-truth posterior over tasks. We therefore do not treat the pretrained-LLM experiments as formal validations of
P0
–
P3
; instead, we ask whether the same qualitative geometric signatures appear.

Figure
7
in Sec.
6.1
provides a trajectory-level view: ID prompts move toward the corresponding task-vector vertices, mirroring the long-context stabilization of
P0
and the finite-context interpolation of
P2
, while OOD prompts have small projection
R^{2}
onto the ID task subspace, giving a real-LLM analog of the near-orthogonal
M2
geometry of Sec.
6.1
. Figure
17
provides a complementary coefficient-level view: on ID prompts, the affine coefficient associated with the prompted task rises toward one as demonstrations accumulate, echoing the posterior-concentration behavior of
P3
in the synthetic experiments. The analogy is intentionally limited: a direct test of
P1
would require reliable estimation of task–token interaction terms over a large set of real tokens, and a direct test of
P3
would require an oracle Bayesian posterior over natural-language tasks. Taken together, the pretrained-LLM results support the external plausibility of the same geometric motifs—task-subspace retrieval for ID prompts and near-orthogonal representations for OOD prompts—while the formal property tests remain those carried out in the controlled synthetic setting.

D.6
Orthogonal Subspace Intervention: Implementation Details

This appendix gives a self-contained algorithmic description of the orthogonal
subspace intervention introduced in Sec.
6.2
.

Recall that the training distribution mixes a small set of

major tasks

{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathrm{major}}
(probability
0.9
) with a large
pool of
minor tasks

{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathrm{minor}}
(probability
0.1
);
OOD tasks are fresh prior draws disjoint from both pools (see
Sec.
D.1
).
At high diversity (
N_{\mathrm{minor}}\gg 1
), each individual minor task
receives negligible weight and cannot be memorized; the model must instead
rely on in-context demonstrations, just as it does for OOD tasks.
We exploit this similarity by using minor-task sequences to search for directions in the orthogonal complement
whose suppression maximally disrupts next-token prediction.
Crucially,
no major-task data enters the optimization
, so the procedure
provides no explicit guarantee that major-task performance will be preserved.
The finding that major-task loss nonetheless remains near zero across a range
of middle layers (Table
2
) therefore constitutes strong
evidence that major-task and OOD/minor-task inference operate through distinct
computational mechanisms.
All evaluation metrics are computed on independently sampled held-out
sequences that are disjoint from the data used to find optimization
directions, avoiding overfitting.

We extract hidden states
{\bm{h}}^{(\ell,b)}_{k,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from layer
\ell
of the
frozen model at batch sample
b
, task
k
, and sequence position
t
.
All operations below are performed independently per layer
\ell
; we suppress
the superscript
\ell
for readability.

Step 1: Task and token subspace estimation.

The task subspace basis
\hat{{\bm{\Theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times K}
is obtained from
the averaging-based task vectors

\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{1},\dots,\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{K}

(stacked as columns;
K=3
is the number of major tasks)
exactly as in Sec.
C.1
.
In addition, we estimate
token encoding vectors

\hat{{\bm{\nu}}}_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as the token main effects from the same two-way
(token
\times
task) ANOVA used for the separability analysis
(Sec.
5.1
): after forming cell means

\bar{{\bm{H}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times K\times d}

from token-conditioned hidden states and removing positional effects,

\hat{{\bm{\nu}}}_{v}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k}\bar{{\bm{H}}}_{v,k}-\bar{{\bm{h}}}
.

Step 2: Protected subspace construction.

Next-token prediction in
E1
–
E3
depends on the current
token
s_{t}
(e.g. the bigram structure in
E3
or the input

{\bm{x}}_{t}
in
E2
), so suppressing directions that encode token identity
would trivially degrade predictions for
all
task types and render
the intervention uninformative.
We therefore protect the token subspace alongside the task subspace.
The token encoding vectors
{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{tok}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d}

(rows
\{\hat{{\bm{\nu}}}_{v}\}
) are projected onto

\operatorname{col}(\hat{{\bm{\Theta}}})^{\perp}
via

The top-

The task and token directions are concatenated and re-orthonormalized via

orthogonal complement basis

Step 3: Direction optimization.

We search for a rank-

subspace within
\operatorname{col}({\bm{U}}_{\perp})

whose suppression maximally disrupts minor-task prediction

The learnable parameter

is initialized as a

0.01
,
gradient clip norm
1.0
) for up to

for
E1

90

E3

E2

).
At each step the intervention directions are

{\bm{V}}_{Q}=\mathrm{QR}({\bm{U}}_{\perp}{\bm{G}})
,
giving the intervention

{\bm{h}}^{\prime}\;=\;{\bm{h}}-\gamma\,{\bm{V}}_{Q}{\bm{V}}_{Q}^{\top}{\bm{h}},

\gamma=2

.
Here

The objective is maximized over batches of size

Step 4: Evaluation.

All evaluation is performed on
independently sampled

disjoint from the batches used during Step 3.
On held-out batches of size
B

Hyperparameters.

Unless stated otherwise, we use

\rho=\lfloor 0.9\cdot\operatorname{rank}({\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{res}})\rfloor

|{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathrm{eval}}|=T
(all positions), and

D.7

Method.

For each layer

Appendix E
Non-Markovian Case: Dyck Path Experiment (
E4
) Details

This section provides the methodology and detailed results behind the planted Dyck experiment (
E4
) used as the motivating non-Markovian counterexample in Sec.
7
. The experiment matters for our main thesis because it shows that property
P0
(long-context stability) is conditional on the data-generating structure rather than an innate property of trained transformers: when the source is non-Markovian and admits no compact sufficient statistic, the model retains the full prefix history instead of collapsing onto a low-dimensional summary. The main text (Figure
8
) reported the residual variance left in
{\bm{h}}_{t}
after conditioning on the task
z
and a local context window, and visualized the cluster structure of the final-layer hidden states at a single prefix length.
This section sharpens that diagnosis along two complementary axes.
First, we train linear probes that classify hidden states by the identity of the
full
planted Dyck prefix at every length
l=1,\ldots,7
and report the resulting validation accuracies, providing direct quantitative evidence (beyond the visual cluster separation shown in the main text) that the model retains an essentially lossless record of the prefix history rather than a compact summary statistic.
Second, we show that this record is recoverable through a single shared low-dimensional projection across all prefix lengths and mask realizations, indicating that the prefix information is arranged along consistent directions in hidden-state space.
Together these results constitute a sharp failure of property
P0
in
E4
that does not occur in
E1
–
E3
.

Sequence construction.

The main text introduces
E4
(Sec.
7
) by describing each sequence as a length-
T
background of i.i.d. uniform tokens into which the latent Dyck string
z
of length
2L
is planted at positions selected with probability
\rho
. We expand on the planting rule here so the case
\rho T>2L
is unambiguous. Concretely, given
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{2L})
we draw a Bernoulli planting indicator vector
\bm{\xi}\in\{0,1\}^{T}
with
\xi_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\rho)
, then enumerate its successes
1\leq\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\cdots
in left-to-right order and overwrite background token
b_{\tau_{j}}
with
z_{j}
for every
j\in\{1,\ldots,\min(2L,\,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\xi_{t})\}
. In other words,
z
is embedded
in order
into the first
2L
Bernoulli successes of
\bm{\xi}
, and
all
other positions, including any successes beyond the
2L
-th, keep their original background token. With the experimental settings used throughout this paper,
T=192
,
2L=20
,
\rho=0.25
, the expected number of Bernoulli successes is
\rho T=48\gg 2L
, so undersampling (fewer than
2L
successes) occurs with vanishingly small probability and the entire latent string
z
is essentially always planted; the suffix of the sequence after the
2L
-th plant is pure background and therefore carries no further task-conditional signal.

Prefix probe.

Consider the model trained on experiment
E4
with
K
Dyck tasks (balanced bracket strings of length
2L=20
).
At each sequence position, define the
Dyck prefix length

l
as the number of planted bracket characters observed so far.
Two tasks that share the same first
l
planted characters are indistinguishable given the observed Dyck prefix up to that point.
We group tasks by their length-
l
prefix and define the
prefix class
of a hidden state as the identity of this prefix.
The number of distinct prefix classes grows with
l
: for instance,
l=2
yields only
2
classes (open or close), while
l=7
yields
35
classes.

We train a linear probe consisting of a shared
2
-dimensional linear projection
{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times d}
followed by a separate two-layer
MLP
classifier per prefix length.
The probe is trained jointly across all prefix lengths
l=1,\ldots,7
, using hidden states extracted from layer
5
(the final layer).
Training and validation sets are constructed by generating sequences under multiple random planting masks and collecting hidden states at positions where exactly
l
Dyck characters have been planted.

Probing results.

The probe achieves near-perfect validation accuracy (
>95\%
) for all prefix lengths up to
l=7
(the longest tested), reaching
98.6\%
at
l=7
despite the rapid growth in the number of classes.
Figure
22
shows a shared
2
-dimensional linear projection at
l=7
, where the
35
distinct prefix classes form well-separated clusters. Points are colored by the number of unmatched left parentheses, and the shaded regions depict the decision boundaries of the per-length
MLP
classifier. This visualization is identical to the right panel of Figure
8
in projection and coloring; the only difference is that here each cluster is labeled by its literal bracket prefix, whereas in the main-text panel the same prefixes are encoded compactly in hexadecimal for legibility. The hexadecimal encoding is obtained by mapping each step to a bit (
\text{`('}\!\to\!1
,
\text{`)'}\!\to\!0
), dropping the leading ‘(’ (present for all prefixes), padding the remaining bits to a multiple of
4
, and converting to hexadecimal; for example, the length-
7
prefix
(()())(
yields bits
1,0,1,0,0,1
after dropping the first ‘(’, and padding to
8
bits gives
10100100_{2}=\texttt{A4}
.

This result has two implications.
First, the hidden states encode a representation of the full Dyck prefix, not a compressed statistic such as the running difference between opening and closing brackets (which would yield only
\lfloor l/2\rfloor+1
distinguishable states at prefix length
l
, not the valid Dyck-prefix classes observed).
Second, because this encoding is recoverable through a shared
2
-dimensional linear projection, the prefix information is arranged along consistent directions across different prefix lengths and mask realizations.
