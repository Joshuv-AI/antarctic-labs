Photonic AI for deeper understanding of the Planck epoch

Aleksandr Raikov

Abstract

This article addresses the requirements for a photonic artificial intelligence (AI) capable of supporting a deeper understanding of the Planck epoch in the origin of the Universe. The article continues the previous author’s articles, which outlined requirements for AI to support studies of protein evolution on Earth, cosmic strings (CS) detection, and a non-standard view on the photon’s nature. Modern digital generative AI cannot support its solution due to a lack of training datasets and limited energy resources for conducting adequate physical experiments. This article analyses the states of the Planck epoch, when there was no space–time, and when entropy began to emerge. It outlines additional requirements for an AI and proposes an adequate full-analogue photonic AI (PAI) that can be helpful for the research. The main idea of the PAI is to reject digitising the natural analogue signal and to use single-step Fourier convolution on many images simultaneously for representing states of the Planck epoch. To create PAI, a single holographic plate must replace a multilevel digital neural network, thereby eliminating the need for traditional multi-step machine learning. PAI is also expected to provide effective solutions to complex tasks such as Markov chains, genetic algorithms, and Kolmogorov (Fokker-Planck) equations, which are necessary for this research. The author’s convergent methodology helps make the research focused and stable. This methodology is based on fundamental thermodynamics and the inverse-problem-solving method in topological spaces. The PAI project has a starting technology readiness level: methodology, principles, and design.

Introduction

Several challenging non-standard physical problems are beyond the reach of conventional methods, including traditional artificial intelligence (AI) support. An example of such problems is understanding the Planck epoch with its hidden singularity at the origin of the Universe. This problem stems from limitations, including the absence of a theory of quantum gravitation (QG), the incompleteness of the Standard Model, and the lack of access to Planck-scale energy resources on Earth.

Can AI help to overcome such challenges? To address this issue, a thought experiment concerning the Planck epoch was developed.

The articles (; ) have outlined requirements for AI to partially overcome the limitations, using examples to support studies of protein evolution on Earth, CS detection, and a non-standard view of the photon’s nature. This article continues this research by examining the possible characteristics of the states in the Planck epoch, when there was no space–time, and, perhaps, when entropy had emerged.

In the Planck epoch, light and its carrier, the photon, did not exist. In this epoch, the traditional applications of quantum physics and the special theory of relativity (STR) are not applicable. However, certain classical analogies can help understand some emerging phenomena. In the Planck epoch, phenomena occur that, later, according to theories of general relativity (GR) and quantum physics, lead to the formation of a unified curved space–time and fundamental constants. In this epoch, the Planck length of the change of states is exactly compensated by a time interval equal to the Planck time, and the speed of light is determined (; ).

Energy can exist without quantum particles and mass. In GR, the source of gravity (mass), and hence energy, is the energy-momentum tensor of physical fields without quantum particles. A pure electromagnetic field (a beam of light) has energy. However, the classical electromagnetic wave does not contain photons, even though it can form bundles of radiation energy. In the Planck epoch, energy existed, but rest mass did not. There is a difference between rest mass (invariant mass) and energy. Energy can exist without rest mass if it is moving at the speed of light. This is an important feature that can characterise the final state of the Planck epoch. The energy density of the Universe at this epoch was enormous but finite () (the Planck density is ≈ 5.2 × 1093 g/cm3); the quantum gravitational effects dominated at the Planck scale. It could take the form of pure field energy and the kinetic energy of massless quanta.

Due to , the Universe must have a beginning. Space–time could be created in the Planck epoch as a causal connection with a fundamental speed limit. Another well-known idea, based on the holographic principle, holds that space–time is a projection of information stored on a holographic surface; the area of any surface limits the amount of information about space–time regions (). The relationships between parts of space–time may be encoded as a web of entanglement on this surface. In the Planck epoch, the entropy could have emerged.

Let us also take a look at some complex methods that are challenging to calculate on large datasets and multidimensional objects, which are useful for solving non-standard issues. Examples of such methods are as follows: computational fluid dynamics (CFD), density functional theory (DFT), Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) (; ), Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov (FPK) equation, genetic algorithms (GA), restricted Boltzmann machine (RBM) (), and stochastic differential equation (SDE). They can be used in various applications, such as molecular dynamics, materials synthesis, and micromechanics, which typically require calculations on supercomputers that consume extremely large amounts of energy and time. Currently, all such approaches require a previously digitised representation of the natural continuous signal, which can be approximated by invoking the universal approximation theorem (). For example, CFD and DFT tasks are traditionally solved using numerical methods that require data discretisation into points, grids, or meshes (). However, when these discrete methods are run at very high resolutions to capture detailed physics, they are both extremely expensive and often infeasible.

It may be convenient and cost-effective to utilise AI for accelerating complex traditional methods and calculations, for example, those that use physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) (). Traditional digital PINNs’ architecture includes physical laws through partial differential equations. However, digitalisation or the use of fine-grained sampling can also pose challenges for PINNs, such as difficulty in optimisation, poor convergence, and high time and energy consumption. Digitalisation requires taking time and filtering out the high-frequency components of natural analogue signals, thereby discarding the non-formalisable cognitive semantics of AI models (). The term “non-formalisable” means that human thoughts and emotions cannot be represented using only mathematical logic and the theory of formal systems.

To use AI to study the aforementioned challenging issues, including those beyond the Standard Model and highly complex calculations, the AI must be immersed (embedded) in natural phenomena and events as much as possible. However, traditional digital AI creates a gap between the analogue natural signal and its digital simulation, thereby truncating the natural signal’s spectrum.

In modern observational cosmology, understanding the signal from the Planck epoch requires detecting a very weak signal in the cosmic microwave background (CMB) to establish a relationship between large-angle correlations, odd-parity dominance, and the effect of primordial gravitational waves on temperature anisotropies (). Information from the Planck epoch cannot be obtained now; perhaps it can be detected through primordial gravitational waves from the inflationary epoch, or by measuring tiny spectral distortions. At the same time, interference from our galaxy, in the form of dust, must be eliminated ().

To address some of the aforementioned challenges, this article proposes a full-analogue photonic AI (PAI) (). It rejects the requirement to digitalise a natural signal, replaces the multilevel neural network with a single holographic plate, and replaces traditional multistep training with single-step multi-image Fourier convolution. The article outlines the theoretical requirements for creating the required AI. The study is conducted as a thought experiment.

In the Planck epoch, the concept of space–time is inapplicable; the particles in it have just begun to form, and all the fundamental forces are unified. Modern quantum field theory (the Standard Model) is inapplicable because it describes how particles interact on a smooth space–time background, but there were no particles. The theory of QG should describe the transition when space–time itself begins to obey quantum laws and reconcile scientists’ views on the origins and structure of reality.

The study of CS and the nature of photons, which can be the information carriers in the CMB, can help to understand the Planck epoch. CS could have begun to form in the Grand Unification epochs (; ). The understanding of photons has a rich history that covers such stage as follows: the Newtonian corpuscle, which cannot explain interference and diffraction; the Einsteinian light quantum, which does not account for light’s wave-like properties; the quantum electrodynamics (QED) field excitation, which is based on the most precisely tested theory and explains the photon’s defining properties; the virtual photon concept, which sounds like an excellent mathematical tool for calculations within the framework of QED theory; the modern wave-particle framework, which views the photon as a quantum object characterised by probabilities of measured outcomes, exhibits localisation and discrete energy exchange. According to the Standard Model, a photon has no internal structure, and its origin or appearance during the initial birth of the Universe is unclear.

The electromagnetic spectrum of photons is continuous (analogue, not discrete) and can be bounded by the Planck frequency. In practise, a physically detected single photon does not have a single frequency; it has a continuous frequency spectrum and, according to the Fourier method, can be composed of an infinite number of frequencies. It must be taken into account in experimental studies of photon behaviour and information carrying ().

For example, the CMB, with its tiny temperature fluctuations, consists of photons spanning frequencies from the lowest radio waves (104 Hz) to gamma rays (1020 Hz). The Bose-Einstein distribution can be used to determine the average number of photons in a single quantum state (). For the CMB today (T = 2.7 K) at the peak frequency (160.2 GHz), the probability of finding a photon in that state is less than 1; at this frequency, the CMB is a stream of individual photons and acts like a classical wave, which an antenna can measure. At a frequency of 1 MHz (radio), each quantum state must contain millions of photons, and the CMB behaves as a smooth, continuous electromagnetic wave. For most of the CMB spectrum (microwaves to infrared), the CMB behaviour must be explained in terms of individual photons, helping to understand it through photon interactions.

In theory, the CMB photons exist at all frequencies, including the infrared and visible ranges. However, its detectability is limited by the sensitivity of modern instruments with digital detectors, by the overwhelming foreground noise from our galaxies, and by interference from infinitely brighter light from distant cosmic objects. Observations of CMB above 217 GHz are affected by these interferences and are limited. Radio and optical telescopes are used to detect faint signals. For example, the Planck mission observes from 30 to 857 GHz (), but practical constraints prevent it from being used effectively at the high frequencies of this range.

Any signal from the Planck epoch, including a gravitational one, perhaps, could be imprinted on the CMB. This epoch may exhibit signal characteristics that differ significantly from those of other epochs. It may involve non-Gaussianities with specific shapes and signatures, discrete symmetries, parity-odd correlations, statistical anisotropies, violations of Lorentz invariance at ultra-high energies, quantum foam seeding CS or other topological defects, and current constraints imposed by measurement instrument limitations ().

The Planck epoch’s footprints in the CMB can be characterised by oscillation frequencies in the spatial-scale domain, which may manifest as angular patterns. If the original oscillations of the Universe are imprinted in the CMB, they also can have extremely low frequencies that are far below the range of detectable electromagnetic waves.

A few parameters can effectively represent the quantisation effects of some signals in CMB experiments, and at middle and large multipoles (specific point source configurations), the quantisation excess noise can be removed from the final power spectrum during data analysis; however, for low multipoles (<30), quantisation removal is less accurate ().

Modern CMB experiments use signal digitisation (pixels), employ a finite number of detectors, and, as a result, have finite observing time and minimal angular scales for sky coverage (i.e., maximal multipoles). Digitisation of the analogue angle and intensity CMB signal into discrete samples limits the probe of the earliest Universe by the maximum resolvable value, beyond which signals are aliased or lost, becoming unobservable; it adds a random error by quantisation noise, which follows, losing the ability to measure subtle features like the primordial signal from the Planck epoch. The high-bit analogue-to-digital converter, combined with a random noise added to the signal, makes quantisation noise negligible compared to the beams’ diffraction, photon, and detector noise. However, for ultra-sensitive experiments aimed at recognising the Planck epoch’s signal, the digitisation problem remains, and information about the Planck epoch that would appear from extremely small-scale gravitational waves or oscillatory features can be buried in other kinds of noise.

The former have already helped interpret photon absorption and emission processes and optical experiments, for example, by using the Schrödinger equation in its approximate form, by considering DFT, and by using the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulations of systems consisting of molecules, particles, fields, and their interactions. As for the latter, the solution to the problems of creating a 3D rewritable holographic memory is still ahead. Many encouraging results show the usefulness of the convergence of AI methods with creating materials technologies (; ). It is limited by high computational cost. Empirical force-field models, such as the interatomic potential method, can provide estimates at orders-of-magnitude lower cost; however, they may require significant human effort to label the dataset. In all these cases, there is a noticeable deficit of research and development required for PAI 3D rewritable holographic memory.

For a long time, the main focus in creating holographic memory has been on using iron-doped lithium niobate oxide (; ). Potentially, this approach is based on the photorefractive effect that can ensure the creation of the memory at the molecular, quantum particle, and field level, which can be characterised as follows:

- Super-high storage density: many orders of magnitude higher than semiconductor approaches.
- Store multiple images in a single cell via angular or wavelength multiplexing.
- Effective embedding into natural analogue processes, for example, signals from space.
- Analogue recording that ensures the storage of a space-charge field with amplitude/phase data.
- It is based on trapped charges that are not vulnerable to upsets in space applications.

Despite the disadvantages of the approach (reliability, research and development readiness level, lack of fast and random access), it can be invaluable for solving the above-mentioned non-standard and complex issues, where the well-known advantages of a traditional digital pixel detector array with semiconductor memory (high-volume manufacturing; fast, random access with simple sensing; error correction; integration on chip with logic, processing, and communication) may be fail.

Many types of photonic materials can be used for rewritable holographic memory (; ). However, millions of experiments are required to find adequate materials for PAI (). For example, the following types of photonic materials can be used, such as photorefractive materials: doped KLTN (K1-xLixTa1-yNbyO3) crystal; iron-doped lithium niobate (LiNbO3: Fe2+/Fe3+), strontium barium niobate (SBN) crystals; phase-change materials (antimony trisulfide Sb2S3, GST – Ge2Sb2Te5); photochromic materials: diarylethenes, pyrrylfulgide, and Bi18SeO29: Ho3+ phosphors; azobenzene polymers: azobenzene-functionalized polymers, azo-carbazole copolymers; nanoparticle-based materials: nanoparticle assemblies in a recording medium; hybrid platforms: Sb2S3 phase-change layer integrated with a humidity-responsive hydrogel.

Most photonic memory projects focus on integrated photonic circuits and extensions of silicon photonics, or on 2D (two dimensions) diffractive surfaces (). They are mainly used to write 2D pixel or weight values for digital multilevel neural network architectures. The analogue 3D holographic memory requires 3D refractive-index modulation patterns for laser writing and rewriting. The required material must ensure the following ():

- One cell of the memory matrix must store up to 100 rewritable 3D Fourier images, which can be achieved by storing the same number of quantum-stable states.
- One holographic memory matrix measuring 1 mm3 must store about 50 3D cells (theoretically, it can store much more).
- The new Fourier image must be recorded by a new inclination or wavelength of the laser beams.
- It must ensure that it writes, erases, and rewrites every image hundreds of thousands of times.
- The holograms must remain stable for years without power.
- The writing operation must require too little energy per image (femtojoules).
- The deflector system must be capable of highly accurately redirecting the laser beams.
- It must have low optical absorption during reading/writing/translucency images.

Discussion and conclusion

This article identifies the requirements for creating full-analogue photonic AI capable of addressing complex scientific phenomena, such as the Planck epoch. Due to the convergent conditions in a random way, the emergence of the local stable states at the Planck epoch could serve as the basis for the formation of entropy. To study this issue, a thought experiment with working hypotheses concerning the appearance and description of the Planck epoch was made.

The connection of cosmology to AI implementation was made through the study of the Planck epoch. This study requires recognising a special pattern in a very weak signal in the CMB, with spectra much wider than can be obtained now using digital detector matrices (pixels) in traditional telescopes. Traditional telescopes apply digitalisation using the sampling theorem. The PAI system ensures recognition of a useful signal without previously sampling the natural signal, and it supports restoration of the signal from high-frequency noise using a holographic approach. That should help to make detected spectra much wider (7–9 orders of magnitude). The detection signals from the Planck epoch require a complex approach that is discussed in this article. Understanding the special pattern of the signal from the Planck epoch also requires creating the PAI.

For studying the Planck epoch, the concepts of energy, entropy, and topological states were used. The characteristics and parameters of the Planck epoch at the point 10−43 s are considered known. The availability of quantitative data at this point and the absence of such data at its beginning (the hidden singularity) motivated us to use thermodynamic and inverse-problem-solving methods to find a holistic, adequate representation of the Planck epoch and, accordingly, to determine the requirements for creating the AI needed to simulate the Planck epoch.

It has been noted that the crucial role in detecting, representing, and processing natural optical signals can be played by the differences between digital and analogue approaches. Digital approaches are usually used in traditional computation and AI applications, for example, to prevent aliasing. However, it cuts off high-frequency data, thereby discarding useful information (; ); during signal compression, it injects quantisation noise, reducing sensitivity; and, as a result, the residual uncertainty at large angular scales can reduce the most valuable signal.

To overcome the limitations of signal quantisation, the article suggests an analogue alternative. To do so, it is necessary to avoid the Kotelnikov-Nyquist-Shannon sampling (). The analogue approach eliminates quantisation noise, preserves the signal’s frequency integrity, and maintains the high-frequency spectrum. One promising way to realise an analogue alternative is to use PAI ().

The idea of PAI physics can be briefly summarised as follows. Individual AI operations can be performed without sampling using the wave properties of light. This involves replacing the discrete notion of the electron in digital calculations with the analogous notion of the photon wave transformation. Both particles are excitations of fields; however, their fundamental properties—spin and charge—dictate opposite ways of processing information.

Electrons are fermions; they repel each other, readily switch current, and are easy to control using transistors for logic operations. An electronic AI is based on nonlinearity and discreteness: a small signal controls a large one. The electron current experiences resistance, generating heat. Digital calculations and AI require significant time and energy.

Photons are bosons; they can be next to each other in the same state. They almost do not interact with each other; it is difficult to control them discretely to provide a logical switch. Photon transformations are linear: to change the beam transformations, you need a complex environment and special photonic materials. Photon transformations are not energy-intensive, making them ideal for fast calculations. Where an electron needs to perform a million operations, a photon will do it in one (for example, Fourier convolution). However, the photon rejection of electronic digital storage and sampling faces the following problems:

- Noise and accuracy: electrons can be more easily controlled; in the photonic case, noise errors can be accumulated in a cascade.
- Memory: an electron provides feedback and parameter accounting more easily; to do this, the photon must be looped in waveguides or resonators, which is more challenging.
- Implementation of logic: discretisation allows building universal logic, which is convenient for programming; optics, so far, allows building only special operators (for example, pattern recognition, FT, matrix multiplication).

Thus, photonic analogue computing can be used as an AI accelerator, where accuracy is less important than the speed of processing large matrices. The suggested PAI, using the Fourier method to convolve many modulated laser beams into a single point, can simulate complex non-standard scientific concepts, for example, the EP of non-Hermitian space, thereby helping to understand better phenomena such as the single-step transition from the Planck epoch to the Grand Unification epoch, forming a statistical mixture of local steady states, and thereby generating entropy.

This article hypothetically proposes that digital computation is fundamentally inadequate for describing the behaviour of Planck-epoch states. However, a digital Markov chain was presented to demonstrate this behaviour. It cannot describe the Planck-epoch transformations because there was no multi-step evolution due to the absence of space–time. It serves only as a conceptual illustration of how a system may evolve from an unstable state to a more stable one. This example shows that, using an analogue optical approach, we can transform traditional multistep evolution into a single-step model through optical (photonic) methods.

In the article, hypothetical statements about the Planck epoch were formulated to facilitate the development of an advanced photonic AI. To experimentally confirm the formulated statements, it is necessary to create an embodied PAI (; ) that could provide a simulation based on optical processing of natural signals, without prior high-frequency spectrum reduction through digitalisation. This photonic AI must:

- Extract useful information from high-frequency noise by the holographic method.
- Use 3D holographic memory to replace many-level neural networks with a single holographic plate and the multi-step training of the neural network with a single step of Fourier convolution.
- Provide the multi-step evolution of chaotic states in methods such as inverse problem-solving with GA, Markov chains, and Kolmogorov equations, by a single step.

The main challenge in creating PAI is the reliability of optical equipment and the development of new photonic materials for 3D rewritable holographic memory. Research on some autonomous components of PAI, using the example of creating an optical chip for multiple image classification, has been conducted ().

A special experiment is required to realise the following:

- Given the , propose an analogue pattern of the Planck epoch by describing the appearance of informational, quantum, or Boltzmann entropy.
- Include detectors based on PAI in a telescope to detect the analogue pattern from the CMB that must carry traces of relic gravitational waves and quantum fluctuations.
- Using PAI, find the data matches experimentally, and finally declare success.

The measurable outputs of the PAI are expected to increase the range of detectable high-frequency signals by 7–9 orders of magnitude (; ). The detection of the pattern can be based on PAI in combination with the gravitational-optical resonance method, for example, using a Fabry-Perot interferometer (; ). Such an interferometer, together with the PAI, could detect gravitational waves generated during inflation after the Planck epoch and carrying the imprint of the signal from this epoch. The frequency spectrum of these waves is significantly lower than the Planck frequency (). , with its complex and non-Hermitian character, is consistent with the well-known hypothesis that such a signal has aspatial (geometric) structure.
