Carcinoid syndrome revealed by chronic diarrhea and isolated severe tricuspid regurgitation
Authors: Darpan Kothia, Otto Valdes, Tushar Patel, Asaad Mouselli, Gurpreet Singh, Maria Carmona
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarcinoid syndrome is an uncommon manifestation of metastatic neuroendocrine tumor (NET) and may present without classic flushing or bronchospasm. We describe a woman in her 50s with hypertension treated with hydrochlorothiazide who presented with confusion, severe hyponatremia with a serum sodium concentration of 114 mmol/L (reference range, 135-145 mmol/L), chronic watery diarrhea, bilateral leg edema, and 40-lb weight loss. Computed tomography (CT) revealed a calcified mesenteric mass and multiple hepatic lesions. Serum chromogranin A was 1434 ng/mL (SI: 1434 µg/L) (reference range, <100 ng/mL [SI: <100 µg/L]), and 24-hour urinary 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) was 108 mg/24 hours (SI: 565 µmol/24 hours) (reference range, <6 mg/24 hours [SI: <31 µmol/24 hours])—well above the threshold associated with carcinoid heart disease (CHD). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated severe tricuspid regurgitation with retracted leaflets, consistent with CHD. Liver biopsy confirmed a well-differentiated grade 2 NET. Urine sodium <20 mmol/L and urine osmolality 188 mOsm/kg indicated volume depletion from gastrointestinal losses, aggravated by hydrochlorothiazide. Hydrochlorothiazide was discontinued; isotonic saline, octreotide, and furosemide improved hyponatremia, diarrhea, and edema. Chronic diarrhea with isolated right-sided valvulopathy should prompt evaluation for carcinoid syndrome even when electrolyte derangement dominates.Keywords: carcinoid syndrome, neuroendocrine tumor, carcinoid heart disease, tricuspid regurgitation, hyponatremia

Introduction

Neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) of midgut origin may secrete serotonin and other vasoactive mediators, producing carcinoid syndrome with diarrhea, flushing, bronchospasm, and, in advanced cases, carcinoid heart disease (CHD) [1-3]. Cardiac involvement results from serotonin-driven fibrotic injury to the right-sided valves and is a major determinant of morbidity and mortality [3-5]. Although CHD has been reported in up to 50% of patients with carcinoid syndrome historically, earlier detection and somatostatin analog therapy appear to be contributing to a declining prevalence [4, 5]. Because flushing and bronchospasm may be absent and gastrointestinal symptoms may precede diagnosis by years, carcinoid syndrome can be overlooked until metastatic disease or valvular dysfunction becomes apparent [2, 3].

The distinctive feature of this case is that the classic vasomotor manifestations—flushing and bronchospasm—were absent, and the presentation was dominated by severe hyponatremia, a nonspecific derangement with a broad differential. Rather than arising from indiscriminate imaging, the diagnosis followed from a focused clinical question: what single process unifies chronic watery diarrhea, a calcified mesenteric mass, and isolated severe tricuspid regurgitation? Recognition of this coherent triad led directly to the diagnosis. The case also demonstrates that urine studies remain decisive in severe hyponatremia when competing mechanisms are plausible, and underscores why CHD requires early recognition and surveillance [5, 6].

Case presentation

A woman in her 50s with hypertension treated with hydrochlorothiazide, lisinopril, and metoprolol presented to the emergency department with acute confusion, dizziness, and headache after a ground-level fall at work. She denied loss of consciousness, seizure activity, or focal neurologic symptoms. Additional history from her daughter revealed an emergency department visit approximately 4 years earlier for abdominal pain and constipation, when incidental liver lesions and a mesenteric mass had been identified on imaging but were not pursued to a diagnosis and outpatient follow-up was not completed.

During the subsequent years, constipation evolved into chronic watery diarrhea, worsening to 3 to 4 stools daily in the year before admission. She also reported progressive bilateral leg edema, fatigue, exertional dyspnea, and unintentional 40-lb weight loss, and had begun restricting oral intake because eating worsened the diarrhea. Notably, she denied flushing and bronchospasm or wheezing—vasomotor and bronchial features characteristic of carcinoid syndrome—as well as fever, nausea, vomiting, alcohol use, tobacco use, prior cardiac surgery, and congenital heart disease.

On presentation, the heart rate was 102 beats per minute; blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature, and oxygen saturation were within normal limits. Examination showed dry mucous membranes, mild ascites, hyperactive bowel sounds, and a holosystolic murmur at the left lower sternal border that increased with inspiration. She was confused but oriented to time and person, without focal neurologic deficit.

Orthostatic hypotension was also present and, with the dry mucous membranes and chronic diarrhea, indicated intravascular volume depletion despite a normal supine blood pressure. This assessment of hypovolemia, rather than hypotension, was the rationale for initial isotonic fluid resuscitation. After volume repletion, bilateral pitting edema to the knees became apparent, reflecting underlying right-sided heart failure masked by volume depletion.

Diagnostic assessment

Initial laboratory testing showed severe hyponatremia with a serum sodium concentration of 114 mmol/L (reference range, 135-145 mmol/L), potassium 3.2 mmol/L (reference range, 3.5-5.0 mmol/L), chloride 95 mmol/L (reference range, 98-107 mmol/L), bicarbonate 15 mmol/L (reference range, 22-28 mmol/L), blood urea nitrogen 54 mg/dL (SI: 19.3 mmol/L) (reference range, 7-20 mg/dL [SI: 2.5-7.1 mmol/L]), creatinine 2.7 mg/dL (SI: 239 µmol/L) (reference range, 0.6-1.2 mg/dL [SI: 53-106 µmol/L]), and albumin 2.6 g/dL (SI: 26 g/L) (reference range, 3.5-5.0 g/dL [SI: 35-50 g/L]) (Table 1). The calculated anion gap was 4 mmol/L, indicating a non-anion gap metabolic acidosis from gastrointestinal bicarbonate loss.

The initial differential diagnosis for chronic secretory diarrhea, weight loss, and severe hyponatremia included chronic infectious or inflammatory diarrhea, a vasoactive intestinal peptide–secreting tumor, adrenal insufficiency, and a functional NET such as carcinoid syndrome. The hyponatremia had several plausible contributors—gastrointestinal losses, hydrochlorothiazide use, right-sided heart failure, and hepatic pseudocirrhotic change—each impairing renal sodium and water handling through distinct mechanisms.

To distinguish among these mechanisms, urine sodium and osmolality were measured. The urine sodium was <20 mmol/L and the urine osmolality was 188 mOsm/kg (reference range, 50-1200 mOsm/kg). A low urine sodium with dilute urine reflects renal sodium conservation, an objective marker of true intravascular volume depletion rather than its cause. These findings established hypovolemic hyponatremia from gastrointestinal losses as the dominant mechanism and argued against the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), which characteristically produces a urine sodium greater than 30 mmol/L and concentrated urine.

Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated a calcified mesenteric mass measuring approximately 2.5 cm (Fig. 1) and multiple hypodense hepatic lesions (Fig. 2), with interval progression compared with imaging from approximately 4 years earlier. The liver had a nodular contour consistent with metastatic pseudocirrhotic change rather than primary cirrhosis.

Because chronic diarrhea, weight loss, and a calcified mesenteric mass with desmoplastic reaction strongly suggested a functional midgut NET, targeted biochemical testing was obtained. Serum chromogranin A was 1434 ng/mL (SI: 1434 µg/L) (reference range, <100 ng/mL [SI: <100 µg/L]), and 24-hour urinary 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA), the principal urinary metabolite of serotonin, was 108 mg/24 hours (SI: 565 µmol/24 hours) (reference range, <6 mg/24 hours [SI: <31 µmol/24 hours]), confirming serotonin excess.

Transthoracic echocardiography, performed to evaluate the holosystolic murmur and suspected right-sided heart failure, showed severe tricuspid regurgitation with thickened, retracted, immobile leaflets, a vena contracta of 2.2 cm, right atrial and right ventricular dilation, preserved left ventricular systolic function, and no pulmonary hypertension (Fig. 3). B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) was 432 pg/mL (reference range, <100 pg/mL), supporting clinically significant right-sided cardiac involvement. Isolated right-sided fibrotic valvulopathy without pulmonary hypertension or left-sided valve disease is characteristic of CHD.

(B) Color Doppler image demonstrates severe tricuspid regurgitation (arrow indicating the regurgitant jet). Right atrial and right ventricular dilation are present, with preserved left ventricular systolic function.

CT-guided biopsy of a hepatic lesion demonstrated a well-differentiated grade 2 NET. The tumor cells were strongly and diffusely positive for synaptophysin, chromogranin, pankeratin, and cytokeratin 8/18 (CK 8/18), with the Ki-67 proliferation index highlighting up to 3% of tumor cells focally; these immunohistochemical findings supported the diagnosis. Serotonin immunohistochemistry was not performed; given the markedly elevated urinary 5-HIAA and characteristic valvulopathy, the diagnosis of serotonin-secreting NET was established biochemically and clinically, although serotonin immunostaining could have provided direct tissue confirmation. Somatostatin receptor-based imaging—Octreoscan or gallium-68 DOTATATE (1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7,10-tetraacetic acid-octreotate) positron emission tomography/CT—was not performed during the index hospitalization, limiting receptor-based staging and assessment of eligibility for lutetium-177 (Lu-177) DOTATATE peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT); it was planned for the outpatient setting.

Treatment

Hydrochlorothiazide was discontinued, and isotonic saline was administered with close sodium monitoring to avoid rapid overcorrection; serum sodium rose from 114 to 128 mmol/L over 3 days (Table 1). Renal function and the metabolic acidosis improved with volume repletion. After biochemical confirmation of a functional NET, short-acting octreotide was started at 100 mcg subcutaneously every 8 hours, followed by transition to octreotide long-acting release 30 mg intramuscularly every 4 weeks [7].

Once intravascular volume had been restored, right-sided heart failure was treated with furosemide, titrated cautiously because of the recent severe hyponatremia and acute kidney injury. Lisinopril was held during the acute kidney injury, and metoprolol was continued. Management was coordinated across oncology, cardiology, gastroenterology, radiology, and pathology.

Outcome and follow-up

By discharge, the patient was alert, tolerating oral intake, with improvement in diarrhea, edema, and renal function. The serum sodium was 128 mmol/L and the creatinine 1.2 mg/dL (SI: 106 µmol/L). She was discharged on octreotide long-acting release 30 mg every 4 weeks, with short-acting octreotide for breakthrough symptoms, and on oral furosemide for right-sided heart failure.

A structured outpatient surveillance plan was established, including serial abdominal imaging with chromogranin A and 24-hour urinary 5-HIAA at 3- to 6-month intervals, repeat transthoracic echocardiography within 6 to 12 months with BNP as an interim marker, and somatostatin receptor–based imaging for staging. Lu-177 DOTATATE PRRT and telotristat ethyl were identified as options for progressive disease or refractory diarrhea, respectively; neither was used during the index admission [8, 9]. Tricuspid valve intervention was to be reconsidered, once oncologic disease was stabilized, if symptoms or right ventricular dysfunction progressed.

Discussion

The distinctive feature of this case is that carcinoid syndrome presented without any classic vasomotor manifestation. Flushing and bronchospasm were absent, and the picture was dominated by severe hyponatremia. The diagnosis was reached not through indiscriminate imaging but by recognizing a coherent triad—chronic secretory diarrhea, a calcified mesenteric mass with hepatic metastases, and isolated severe tricuspid regurgitation—as the signature of metastatic midgut NET with serotonin excess [1, 2]. In patients with right-sided fibrotic valvulopathy without pulmonary hypertension or left-sided valve disease, CHD should be considered promptly even when vasomotor features are absent [3-5].

Although diarrhea is the most common gastrointestinal manifestation of carcinoid syndrome, the electrolyte and acid–base consequences of secretory diarrhea—hypokalemia, non-anion gap metabolic acidosis, and prerenal azotemia—are well recognized and were all present here. Hyponatremia is likewise expected from substantial gastrointestinal losses and was not, in itself, surprising. What made this presentation misleading was its severity and the simultaneous presence of several independent contributors—thiazide use, right-sided heart failure, and hepatic pseudocirrhotic change—which drew attention away from the underlying NET, especially as the vasomotor symptoms that would ordinarily suggest carcinoid syndrome were absent.

Here, urine studies were decisive: a low urine sodium with dilute urine identified volume depletion as the dominant mechanism and excluded SIADH. This illustrates a broadly applicable principle—in severe hyponatremia with several plausible causes, simple urine studies can identify the predominant mechanism and prevent inappropriate treatment, such as fluid restriction in a volume-depleted patient [6].

The markedly elevated urinary 5-HIAA quantifies the serotonin excess that drives carcinoid valvular fibrosis. Serotonin promotes fibroblast proliferation and deposition of plaque-like fibrous tissue on the downstream surfaces of the right-sided valves, producing the retracted, immobile leaflets and severe tricuspid regurgitation seen here. This patient's value of 565 µmol/24 hours is well above the approximately 300 µmol/24 hours threshold associated with the development and progression of CHD, concordant with her valvulopathy [4, 5]. BNP elevation further supported clinically important valvular involvement and provided a useful follow-up marker [5].

Somatostatin analog therapy remains first-line treatment for symptomatic metastatic well-differentiated NET, reducing hormonal symptoms while stabilizing tumor burden [7]. For diarrhea persisting despite optimized therapy, telotristat ethyl is an evidence-based adjunct, and Lu-177 DOTATATE PRRT is established for progressive somatostatin receptor–positive disease [8, 9]. No medical therapy reverses established carcinoid valvular fibrosis; patients with CHD require serial echocardiography and referral for valve intervention when symptoms or right ventricular dysfunction progress [5, 10].

This case has several limitations. Somatostatin receptor–based imaging was not performed during the index hospitalization, limiting complete staging and assessment of eligibility for Lu-177 DOTATATE PRRT, and serotonin immunohistochemistry was not obtained, although the diagnosis was firmly supported biochemically and clinically. Longitudinal cardiac and oncologic outcomes will be reported as follow-up matures. These limitations do not diminish the central message: chronic diarrhea with isolated right-sided valvulopathy should prompt evaluation for carcinoid syndrome even when vasomotor features are absent, and incidental mesenteric or hepatic lesions should be pursued to a definitive diagnosis before an indolent NET progresses.
