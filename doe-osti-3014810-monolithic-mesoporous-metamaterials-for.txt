Abstract
The objective of the project was to develop and validate monolithic mesoporous metamaterials
(MMMs), to which we also refer as Mesoporous Optically Clear Heat Insulators (MOCHIs) capable
of serving as High-thermal-barrier Glazing (including Windows, Skylights, and Daylighting) with
Transparency ≥99%, Color Rendering Index ≥99%, Haze ≤1%, Thermal Insulation per inch ≥R-15,
Manufacturing Cost per Square Foot ≤$0.3; In addition, MMM-Based Frame Insulation and
Glazing Products with Thermal Insulation per inch R-10 to R-15. All objectives of the project were
successfully accomplished.
 We have developed optically transparent, highly thermally insulating pre-engineered
metamaterials made from mesopore networks of polysiloxane tubes as a viable alternative for
window insulation. By tuning mesoscale structure and solid content, we achieved thermal
conductivity of 10-12 mW K-1 m-1, much lower than that of still air (~27 mW K-1 m-1), as well as
average visible-range light transmission >99% with the haze coefficient < 1%. We scaled
production of these mesoporous optically clear heat insulators to square-meter lateral
dimensions and slab thickness > 1.2 inches, to overall building-relevant scales, while retaining
their thermal and optical properties. We fabricated wall-grade insulated glass units with thermal
resistance R ≈ 15 h ft2 °F Btu−1 (2.64 m2 K W-1) and higher for geometric form-factors (thickness)
comparable to that of conventional double pane windows, while also meeting stringent
requirements for such glazing applications. Additionally, among applications beyond windows,
we have demonstrated how such transparent insulation allows for efficient harnessing of
unconcentrated solar thermal energy. Our monolithic mesoporous metamaterials could
potentially help convert inefficient windows and overall building envelopes into energy-
generating autonomous power plants.

Public Executive Summary
 Buildings are responsible for consuming ~ 40% of all generated energy globally, and much of
that energy is wasted in the heating and cooling the building interior (1-7) because building
envelopes commonly lack the ability to passively maintain temperature needed for the
comfortable indoor environment. Windows of buildings leak much of the cooling/heating energy
(2-5): despite constituting only ~8% by area of the building envelopes, they are responsible for
~50% of the heat transfer (2-5). Approaches aiming to address this challenge involve transparent
cellulose or silica aerogels and vacuum-insulated glass units, but their deployment is still
challenged by issues like scaling limitations, seal integrity, and high manufacturing costs (2-14).
Recently, cellulose-based aerogel films could be scaled in lateral dimensions while achieving
optical characteristics acceptable for window retrofitting applications (11), albeit their thickness
could not be scaled beyond several millimeters while retaining transparency, limiting the overall
thermal resistance that could be achieved in window units and retrofits. The high nanometers-
to-micrometer polydispersity of pores in conventional aerogels is the primary contributor to their
hazy appearance, making it difficult for aerogels to meet the stringent optical requirements for
applications in windows (7-14), as well as making them less effective as thermal barriers (15, 16).
Despite the recent progress in meticulous nanoscale morphology control (11), the intrinsic
limitations of conventional aerogels stem from the lack of well-defined length scale controlling
pores to stay much smaller than both the wavelength of light and mean free path of air molecules
at ambient conditions (~60 nm), as needed to achieve high optical transparency and low thermal
conductivity of porous materials (4).
 Mesoporous materials, including mesoporous silicas, have been developed to exhibit highly
controlled monodisperse pores, which are often templated by lyotropic liquid crystalline
mesophases or by kinetic processes involving mesoscale self-organization of micelles (17-23).
However, such materials have not been reported to be manufactured on building-relevant scales.
Similarly, the target physical properties like thermal insulation and optical transparency have not
been systematically studied or optimized.
 We have demonstrated transparent monolithic mesoporous metamaterials (MMMs) with
lower than still air’s thermal conductivity, comparable to that of aerogels used in the Mars Rover
(4-12, 26). Cold-temperature insulation of our MMM-IGUs stopped 95% of heat transfer, making
windows as insulated as high-performing building walls (thermal resistance R15-R20). Self-
assembly-based fabrication and optical-contact-enabled scaling of MMM slabs and films with
diverse geometric form-factors is suitable for applications in various parts of a building envelope.
Thus, our MMMs can greatly expand the use of insulated glazing in envelopes of future energy-
efficient buildings, including in windows, skylights and porous-material-based bricks for
daylighting (27,28). Although silicone-based sealants are already widely used in IGUs, our MMMs
could replace the conventional gas fillers with “frozen air” nanosponge-like methyl-silicone-
based fillers. Potential applications of MMMs may extend to greenhouse coverings, protective
and heat-insulating apparel (29), space exploration (15) and in many other instances where the
unique combination of optical transparency and thermal insulation is needed. Furthermore, our
MMMs can be surface-functionalized or modified by addition of nanoparticles (30) to achieve
custom-designed properties ranging from solar gain control to carbon dioxide capture and air
 3

purification (31), including potential development of disposable insulating air-cleaning windows
(24, 38). Our technology was highlighted in many news articles, like by the Forbes Magazine here:
https://www.forbes.com/sites/lauriewinkless/2025/12/18/invisible-material-could-make-
windows-super-insulating/ (see additional references 42-51 in the end of the report)

Technoeconomic Evaluations
Technoeconomic analysis of MMM
 The technoeconomic analysis was conducted to determine the fully loaded production cost
of commercial large-scale mass production of monolithic MMM layer as a window-insulation
product. This cost analysis provides an initial upper bound for the fully loaded production cost,
considering factors such as bulk material freight-on-board costs, factory operating and capital
expenditures, including direct and indirect labor, labor burden, production and ancillary energy,
waste management, building lease, production equipment, equipment maintenance, plant
design and installation, and building build-out costs. The quantitative assumptions employed in
the model include a baseline production volume of one million square feet annually for a 3-mm
thick MMM. The production equipment capacity is scaled to meet this volume and manufacture
MMM products in approximately 1 m × 2 m sheets. Extensive use of chemicals and solvent
recycling is incorporated into the model (which was implemented and tested also during the
laboratory scale production). Assuming a 5-year straight-line depreciation / amortization for
capital expenditures, the estimated fully loaded production cost of the MMM is about $25.83 per
square meter, with a potential maximum calculated cost of approximately $55 per square meter.
In terms of double MMM-IGUs with one low-e coated and one clear glass panes ($8 - $13.50 per
square meters) including IGU assembly cost ($16 - $20 per square meters), the most optimal total
cost of the MMM-IGU would be in the $50 - $60 range.
Cost comparison analysis for different technologies:
 High-performance glazing units can include different technologies, such as triple-pane
glazing with air and other filler gases between panes, as well as insulated glazing units with
MMM, silica aerogel, and silanized cellulose aerogels (SiCellA) (11) replacing the gas fillers, as
well as vacuum-insulated glazing (VIG). Generally, they have higher initial costs as compared to
more conventional glazing options due to additional materials and/or more complex
manufacturing processes. However, these high-performance units can allow for long-term
energy savings and, potentially, significantly lower overall building energy costs because of their
superior insulation properties. While triple glazing can yield relatively good energy efficiency,
 8

typically achieving about one-third of thermal resistance value R ≈ 20.7 h ft2 °F Btu−1 (3.65 m2 K
W-1) that we demonstrate (Fig. 10), its increased overall thickness and weight often pose
structural challenges for deployment, along with reduction in overall transparency, with the still
limited energy efficiency. The price of the high-performance R ≈ 7 h ft2 °F Btu−1 triple-pane glazing
units on average ranges from $400 to $1,000 per window in the USA. Alternatively, technologies
based on MMM, silica aerogel, SiCellA and VIGs offer enhanced performance with a slimmer
form-factor and potentially competitive costs at large scale manufacturing, making them ideal
for applications where space is limited and better thermal insulation is desired.
 Assuming IGUs of R ≈ 10 h ft2 °F Btu−1 based on different high-performance technologies, we
can compare the production and sales costs for conditions of large-scale production. The
estimated production cost of MMM material is approximately $2.40 per sq-ft ($25.83 per m2) for
a 3-mm thick films of dimensions 1 × 2 m2 at 500,000 sq-ft annual production with and extensive
use of chemical & solvent recycling, and assuming a 5-year straight-line depreciation /
amortization for the capital equipment expenditures. For MMM-IGUs with 3-mm thick MMM,
there are two configurations possible that can have R-values of around R-5.9 h ft² °F Btu⁻¹ or R-
3.9 h ft² °F Btu⁻¹ , depending on air gaps being on both sides or single sides of MMM, respectively.
To achieve an R-value of R ≈ 11 h ft2 °F Btu−1, and considering the simplest design with single air
gap, we require approximately 19-mm thick MMM. This thickness can be achieved by stacking
six sheets of 3-mm MMM on top of each other, ensuring optical contact. This thickness can be
reduced by using one low-e coated glass pane instead of clear glass. Assuming six MMM sheets,
the production cost rises to $14.40 per sq-ft ($155 per m²). In terms of MMM-IGUs comprising
one low-emissivity coated and one clear glass pane, the glass pane cost ranges from $0.75 to
$1.25 per sq-ft ($8 - $13.50 per 1 m2) plus the IGU assembly cost of ~$1.50 per sq-ft ($16 - $20
per m2). Therefore, an optimized total production cost of the MMM-IGU would be about $16.72-
$17.65 per sq-ft ($180 - $190 per m²). In general, the costumer's purchasing price would be
roughly three or four times the production cost due to various commercial markups, which
results in $50-$70 per sq-ft ($540 - $760 per m²).
 For silica-aerogel insulated glazing, the average cost of granular translucent aerogel is
between $3,500 - $4,000/m3, equivalent to $10.50 - $12 per m2 for a 3-mm thick aerogel (6, 57-
59). Another report (39) indicates that 3-mm thick transparent silica aerogel costs around $2 per

achieve comparable thermal performance and applying a corresponding price markup for
stacking 3-mm films, the production cost is approximately $11.24 - $12.90 per sq-ft ($121 - $138

I. Fabrication of slabs and films of MMMs
 We fabricated square-meter films and multi-centimeter-thick slabs of MMMs (Fig. 1A, B)
using an optimized procedure that leveraged the out-of-equilibrium self-assembly of surfactant
molecules into graph-like networks, which then templated networks of thin nanotubes of
polysiloxane to eventually yield our predesigned MMMs (Fig. 1C-J). Briefly, methyl trimethoxy
silane (MTMS) was added to a solution of an aqueous cetylpyridinium chloride (CPCL) surfactant,
followed by additions of glacial acetic acid to hydrolyze the silane. Tetramethyl ethylene diamine
(TEMED) was used to cross-link the polysiloxane network (24). This mixture was transferred to a
prefabricated mold, which was sealed and then kept in a closed chamber to complete gelation at

 10

55 oC within 12 to 72 hours, for sample thicknesses of 0.5 to 37.5 mm, respectively (24). After
removal from the mold, the polysiloxane hydrogels were washed with water and transferred to
ethanol at 40oC. Repeated washing with fresh/distilled ethanol completed the solvent exchange
and removed the surfactant (24). Subsequent carbon dioxide (CO2)-based supercritical drying
replaced ethanol with CO2 that was then exchanged with air within the mesoporous tubular
networks after removal using a custom-built supercritical dryer (fig. S3F). The ensuing highly
porous MMMs were pre-designed to have a polysiloxane solid content of only 5 to 15% by volume
(Fig. 1, fig. S5), fabricated according to the recipes shown in tables S1 and S2 (24).

I-1. Sample preparation
 Our MMMs are pre-designed to exhibit thermal superinsulation, very high optical
transparency, mechanical robustness, and super-hydrophobicity, all enabled by highly controlled
nanoscale morphology, while also allowing for scalable manufacturing and cutting to desired
shapes and sizes. Fabrication of MMM utilizes commercially available materials and solvents,
taking advantage of out-of-equilibrium formation of a liquid crystalline network of cylindrical
surfactant micelles that are used for templating the nanoscale porous structure. Precursor
methyl trimethoxy silane (MTMS), cationic surfactant cetylpyridinium chloride (CPCL), N,N,N′,N′-
tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED), glacial acetic acid and ethanol were all sourced from TCI
America. Briefly, in a typical optimized MMM manufacturing process, CPCL was dissolved in the
appropriate amount of deionized (DI) water while stirring. Subsequently, MTMS was added,
followed by the addition of glacial acetic acid (0.1 mM to 0.5 mM) to facilitate the hydrolysis of
the silane. Finally, TEMED (5 mM to 10 mM) was introduced to crosslink the polysiloxane network
through the sol-gel process, which was then followed by the removal of surfactant and
byproducts, solvent exchange, and supercritical drying. In this fabrication process, lyotropic liquid
crystal-templated self-assembly allows us to produce scalable MMMs with bicontinuous pore
structure and pre-engineered desired optical and thermal properties.
 The optimized compositions of MMM samples at different porosities ranging from 85% to
97%, are presented in table S1. In a specific example of optimized MMM synthesis (for 91 %
porosity), 1.1 g of CPCL was dissolved in 27.48 g of DI water in a 100 mL beaker. Then, 12.58 g of
MTMS and 1.58 μL of glacial acetic acid were added to the solution and stirred for 4 hours to
ensure complete dissociation and hydrolysis of silane. 56 μL of pre-cooled TEMED diluted in 8.2
g of DI water was added to the precursor solution slowly, while stirring for 2 min. The initially
acidic precursor solution with a pH of 3-5, became basic with a pH of 8-12 after mixing. The final
TEMED-treated precursor solution was transferred into specially fabricated molds and sealed
with two-component epoxy glue. The initial gelation of this precursor took around 30-60 min to
complete, after which molds were placed in a preheated oven (55 °C) for 72 hours to finish curing
and gelation. Once the aging was completed, the hydrogel was unmolded and washed with DI
water to remove any unreacted water-soluble CPCL, TEMED, and the byproducts from a sol-gel
process. The hydrogel was then moved to a container filled with warm (40 °C) ethanol for solvent
exchange. Multiple steps of solvent exchange were carried out to replace water and other
unreacted components with ethanol resulting in alcogels ready for supercritical drying.
 To optimize the base (TEMED) concentration for the MMM precursor solution, a series of
base compositions was studied and analyzed with respect to the gelation time of the precursor
and the final transmission characteristics for MMM films of the same thickness (Fig. 2A-C). This
was done by varying the volume of TEMED in the base solution while keeping the precursor
solution constant. The precursor solution was prepared by maintaining a surfactant
concentration of 2.61 wt.% and following the recipe outlined in table S1. Different batches of
base solutions with varying base concentrations were prepared, ensuring that the total volume
of the base solution remained constant. These batches were then mixed with the precursor
solution to initiate gelation. The TEMED concentration in each batch was set to 2.5, 5, 7.5, 10,
12.5, 15, 20, and 50 mM. Each sample was placed into a separate mold for gelation and then

 12

supercritically dried to form distinct MMM samples. These samples were subsequently analyzed
to determine the mechanical, optical, and thermal properties that yielded the best results for
certain concentrations/compositions (Fig. 2A, B). The optimized MMM composition for different
porosities with optimal TEMED concentration to be 7.5 mM. Next, to optimize the CPCL
surfactant concentration, a series of precursor solutions with varying concentrations of CPCL
(from 0.5 wt.% to 13.1 wt.%) were prepared, while keeping the base solution constant (7.5 mM
TEMED). The amount of CPCL in the precursor solution varied between 0.2 to 4 times the amount
used for optimized MMM preparation. The samples were prepared by mixing the respective
precursor solutions with the base solution for different amounts of surfactants, and these
samples were filled into separate molds. The kinetics and mechanism of the gelation of the
precursor solution to the complete hydrogel state were analyzed through monitoring gelation
time and morphology at the nanometer scale (Fig. 2A-F), extracting parameters and properties
such as average pore diameter in the MMM network (Fig. 2E, analyzed with transmission electron
microscope, TEM), total and diffuse transmission of the supercritically dried MMM (Fig. 2D). The
nanoscale TEM imaging was found to be consistent with the small angle X-ray scattering (SAXS)

 While parts of the fabrication procedures can be modified for making samples with different
geometry, like thin films versus thick slabs, our overall approach comprises the following
essential steps: (1) Acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of silica or silsesquioxane precursors in the presence
of cationic surfactant like CPCL and/or adhesion-promoting additives; (2) Base-catalyzed

 14

polycondensation of said precursors; (3) Casting of the resulting solution to the desired shape
and dimensions; (4) Thermal curing and/or aging of the cast MMM gels; (5) Unmolding and/or
unrolling of the cured MMM gels; (6) Solvent exchange of the resulting hydrogels to transform
them into organogels; (7) Supercritical, critical point, or ambient drying of the organogels to
obtain monolithic MMM metamaterials, though ambient drying and other approaches will be
presented elsewhere. Below, we detail how this general fabrication process is adopted to
produce MMM samples with different form-factors, like thick slabs and relatively thin (1-5 mm)
large-area films, including when adhered to plastic or other support substrates.

I-2. Fabrication of small to square-meter-sized MMM films
 To fabricate small MMM films, the molds were typically made using two glass substrates,
laminated with a 300 mm-thick Mylar sheet (Fig. 3). The thickness of the MMM film was defined
by silicone rubber spacers on the periphery. Such molds were then sealed on all sides using two-
component epoxy glue, leaving a small opening for injecting the final TEMED-treated precursor
solution. Depending on the need, both free-standing MMM films and ones adhered to plastic or
glass substrate support could be fabricated (Fig. 3). Adhering MMM to plastic/glass substrates
allows for making protective layers for MMM retrofit products, which then exhibit further
improved mechanical properties (Fig. 3H, I). By adding dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, ~0.5 wt.% of
the final sample mass) and amino propyl trimethoxy silane (APTMS, 0.02 vol.% relative to the
silane precursor), we promoted gel adhesion to the plastic films and glass panes, which was
maintained during hydrogel-organogel transformation and subsequent drying. While many
fabrication steps remain the same as the lateral dimensions of MMM films increase from inches
to meters, several manufacturing steps need to be modified to maintain monolithic samples with
optical and thermal properties optimized, as we demonstrate below using specific examples.

 For the fabrication of square meter-sized MMM films (for example in Fig. 1A), the molds
were prepared in two steps, and the sample fabrication was customized as follows. First, the base
substrate was prepared onto which the precursor solution would be poured (Fig. 4A), and then
the top part was laminated using a second pane. Initially, a 3-mm thick glass substrate (41″ × 41″)
was covered with clear polyester film (40″ × 40″), and the edges were covered with silicone

rubber spacers that define the thickness of the films (1.6 mm, 3.2 mm and 4.8 mm). The rubber
spacers (41″ × 0.75″) slightly overlapped with the polyester film to ensure a leak-proof casting
process. Meanwhile, the top pane was prepared by using a 40″ × 40″ Mylar sheet (Fig. 4A, B)
adhered to a 3-mm thick glass pane, reinforced with a 40″ aluminum support bar to prevent
warping. Commercially available food-grade silicone spray (WD-40, purchased from Amazon) was
applied to ensure the Mylar film adhered firmly to the glass pane. Typically, for making a 1.6 mm
thick square-meter sized MMM film, 1446.3 g of DI water was added to 58.26 g of CPCL in a 4 L
glass beaker. Subsequently, 277.9 g of MTMS was added and the solution was stirred at 150 rpm.
With continuous stirring, 10 mL of DMSO, followed by 48 µL of APTMS, was added. Finally, 83.7
µL of glacial acetic acid was added, and the beaker was covered and stirred for 15 min. to achieve
a transparent precursor solution. At the same time, 3.36 mL of TEMED was dissolved in 434 g of
water in a 500 mL beaker. After complete dissolution of the components in the precursor
solution, the TEMED solution was added slowly (~15-30 s), yielding a final TEMED concentration
of 10 mM and a CPCL surfactant concentration of 2.6 wt.%. The mixture was stirred for one
minute before being carefully poured into the square-meter-sized base mold (Fig. 4B). Any air
bubbles resulting from pouring were removed using lint-free tissue paper. Approximately 10 min.
post-casting, the top pane assembly was carefully placed on top of the mold. Following gelation
of the hydrogel mixture, which occurred in 12-14 min., the mold was sealed using two-
component epoxy glue (Fig. 4C, D) and transferred to a home-built oven at 55 °C for curing for 3
days. Once cured, the sample was carefully unmolded by detaching the top pane assembly (Fig.
4C, D) and submerged in a 100 L aqueous solution containing 20 vol.% ethanol. The glass pane-
polyester film-hydrogel assembly was kept in this solution for a day. The circulation of the
aqueous solution within the tank was facilitated by using a pump motor. 1-H Nuclear Magnetic
Resonance analysis of the water:ethanol mixture after 24 hours of solvent exchange revealed
that over 80 % of the CPCL surfactant was removed during this initial step. Next, the polyester
film-gel assembly was affixed to and wrapped around a plastic mesh cylinder, then placed in a
tubular container. The sample then underwent at least three solvent exchanges with 20 L of
ethanol over a 24-hour period, followed by supercritical drying in a large cylindrical chamber,
while in the compact roll described above (Fig. 4F). After drying, the co-adhered polyester-MMM
film was unwrapped from the mesh cylinder, resulting in a transparent and flexible MMM
metamaterial adhered to the clear polyester film (Fig. 1A).

I-3. Fabrication of thick (0.5-2 inch) MMM slabs
 The fabrication of thick MMM samples (Fig. 5) mostly followed the recipe outlined in the
previous sections and in table S1, with only casting/molding, solvent exchange, and drying
procedures being different. As an example, to create a 1″-thick MMM metamaterial slab with
lateral dimensions of 5.5″ × 30″, the casting and molding phase was undertaken first. Clean glass

 18

substrates with dimensions of 6.5″ × 31″ were used, along with 1″-thick plastic spacers with
smooth surfaces to define thickness. Mylar sheets were used (Fig. 4A) to cover all inner sides of
the rectangular mold to promote a smooth surface and facilitate unmolding after curing. The
molds were sealed on all sides with a two-component epoxy resin, leaving a small opening on
one end. In parallel, the precursor solution was prepared according to table S1 and mixed with
the TEMED solution, ensuring that the total volume of the final TEMED-treated precursor solution
matches the volume of the mold. The solution was carefully poured into the molds through the
opening, and any air bubbles emerging during this process were removed using lint-free tissue
paper. Following initial gelation, the molds were sealed and transferred to a homemade oven
chamber at 55 °C for three days (Fig. 4E). After baking, the molds were carefully unmolded, and
hydrogels were placed in a heated (~40 °C) ethanol container for the solvent exchange. Ethanol
was replaced at regular intervals to ensure the complete removal of unreacted base, surfactant,
and byproducts of polycondensation, such as water, resulting in a transparent polysiloxane
alcogel ready for supercritical drying.

I-4. Drying of MMMs
 The supercritical drying of the MMM samples was conducted in a supercritical chamber,
which varied in size based on the dimensions and thickness of the alcogel. For smaller samples,
a commercial critical point dryer (CPD, Tousimis Automegasamdri) measuring 6.5″ in diameter
and 1″ in depth was used. The alcogels were wrapped in metal mesh and loaded into the chamber
pre-filled with pure ethanol, and the chamber was sealed and subsequently cooled to 5 °C. Liquid
carbon dioxide (CO2) was injected into the chamber and ethanol was slowly purged out. Once the
ethanol molecules were replaced with CO2 molecules, the chamber was brought to a supercritical
state by heating it to temperatures above 32 °C while maintaining the pressure above 1070 psi.
Once the chamber reached a supercritical state, the pressure was released slowly to bring it to
atmospheric pressure, and the chamber was subsequently cooled. The MMM samples were then
retrieved after opening the chamber.
 The large-scale MMM metamaterials were dried using a home-built supercritical drying
setup (Fig. 4F) that involved pumpless extraction, as previously described (11). The pumpless
extraction method involved a primary vessel for drying the MMM alcogels and two secondary
vessels that supplied supercritical fluid at high pressures to ensure smooth flow. Initially, the
alcogels were wrapped in flex-foam and placed within a perforated mold, which was then loaded
into the main vessel filled with ethanol (Fig. 4F). This prevented damage to the porous MMM
structure due to capillary forces or phase transition effects. The two secondary vessels were filled
with liquid CO2, maintained at approximately 2-3 °C, allowing for uninterrupted flow of liquid
CO2. The main vessel’s temperature and pressure were maintained at around 2 °C and 1250 psi,
respectively. Over the course of several hours, depending on MMM thickness, CO2 molecules
replaced the ethanol within MMM pores, and the ethanol-CO2 mixture was drained to a
collection vessel under ambient conditions. The liquid CO2 underwent a phase transition to
gaseous form, leaving only ethanol, which was subsequently distilled and reused. Once ethanol
molecules were replaced with liquid CO2 within the MMM, the supercritical drying process began.
The main vessel’s temperature gradually increased above the CO2 supercritical point at 1070 psi

 19

and 32 °C. Simultaneously, the pressure in one or more secondary vessels was also elevated
beyond the main vessel’s pressure by heating well above 32 °C, facilitating a flow of supercritical
CO2 from secondary to main vessels. This ensured any remaining alcohol molecules within the
MMM pores were replaced by supercritical CO2, preventing pore collapse during drying due to
capillary or surface tension forces. Finally, MMMs were obtained by transitioning from
supercritical CO2 to gaseous CO2 by slowly reducing the main vessel’s pressure below the critical
point, while maintaining the temperature above 32 °C. Once the pressure was well below the
critical threshold, the temperature gradually lowered to room temperature, bringing it to
atmospheric conditions, allowing for careful removal of the MMMs in the forms of film rolls or
thick slabs (Fig. 1A, B and Fig. 5) from the main vessel.

II. Nanoscale Characterizations
 Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging and tomographic reconstruction (Fig. 1C -
F and Fig. 2) confirmed that MMM were composed of graph-like networks of thin bicontinuous
tubes of polysiloxane. The inner pore diameters were roughly twice the length of CPCL surfactant
molecules, and their nanometer-scale thickness and density varied with porosity (Fig. 1C-I) (24).
Dimensions of outer inter-nanotube network pores were typically ~30 nm or less. The nanotube
orientations exhibited weak orientational correlations with respect to the geometry of the slabs
or films that was related to the symmetry-breaking at the fabrication-stage solvent exchange and
drying (24).
 The morphology of MMM (Fig. 1D) was much different from that of common aerogels that
are typically composed of networks of interconnected particles like nanospheres and nanofibers
with polydisperse pore dimensions spanning many orders of magnitude (4-11), often from
nanometers to tens of micrometers. Controlling pore and nanoparticle dimensions in
nanocellulose and silica aerogels has been instrumental for achieving desired properties (4-11),
such as optical transparency. However, progress has been limited, and so far, thick slabs of
transparent aerogels have not been reported. By exploiting the surfactant self-assembly into
cylindrical micellar networks as a guiding mechanism for nano-templating polysiloxane, we
define dimensions of inner pores of nanotubes, distances between them within the graph-like
networks, as well as their large-scale partial orientational order and anisotropic properties (Figs.
1 and 7).

II-1. TEM nanoscale imaging and tomography
 For TEM nanoscale imaging (for examples, see Fig. 1C, F and the inset in Fig. 2D, E), MMM
samples were cut to ~2 × 2 × 2 mm3-sized pieces and their fragments were deposited on 300
mesh carbon-coated copper grids without prior plasma treatment. The micrographs shown are
representative of grid holes at different locations on the TEM grids. TEM imaging was carried out
using a TITAN KRIOS G3i electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the Selectris-X
energy filter and Falcon 4 detector and the image processing/analysis was done using the FIJI
image processing package (https://fiji.sc/). To ensure representative data, the average network
diameter was determined by measuring the width of the polysiloxane network in two different
micrographs, collected at separate locations on the TEM grid. In every micrograph, the
polysiloxane network diameter was measured in 30 random spots. From these values, the
average and standard deviation of the polysiloxane network width were calculated for different
MMM compositions (Fig. 2). The nanoscale tomography was correlated with properties of MMM
metamaterials (Fig. 2A-F).
 Sample grids for TEM tomography were prepared in two steps, involving first coating of the
grids with gold nanoparticle fiducials and then by deposition of the small fragments of the MMM
sample on the grid. For this, 10 µL of cetyltrimethylammonium chloride (CTAC) -capped gold
nanoparticles on 300 mesh carbon-coated TEM grids were casted. These gold nanoparticles were
prepared following the procedure reported by Chateau et al. (32) and used as-synthesized. After
30 seconds, an excess of gold nanoparticle solution was removed from the TEM grid using filter

 21

paper and the grids were then dried in air. Next, a ~2 × 2 × 2 mm3-sized piece was cut from a
MMM sample and small fragments of it were placed in between two clean razor blades. These
MMM fragments were deposited on gold nanoparticle-coated TEM grids by conformal contact.
After transferring the sample to the microscope, the grid was examined to identify MMM
fragments suitable for tomography. Next, the grid was put at the eucentric height of the
microscope, and the unobstructed tilt range was checked. Generally, tilt angles between -65o and
+65o were suitable for obtaining the tilt series of images. Next, an automated tilt series
acquisition was started using SerialEM, taking an image at every integer tilt angle in the tilt range.
Nominal magnifications of 87k were used for the tilt series, also using a defocus value of ~0.2 µm.
Reconstruction of the tomograms was done using the IMOD software package, using the back-
projection algorithm described elsewhere (33).

II-2. Cryo-electron microscopy sample preparation, image acquisition, and processing
 Cryo-electron microscopy allowed us to obtain insights into the kinetic evolution of
nanoscale surfactant molecule self-assembly that templates formation of polysiloxane nanotube
networks, which was achieved by “freezing” the samples during different stages of MMM
formation. Quantifoil R2/2 sample grids for cryo-transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM)
were obtained from Electron Microscopy Sciences (EMS, Hatfield, PA, USA). Before their use,
these grids were rendered hydrophilic by using a plasma cleaning setup for 30 s at ~10–1 Torr
(~0.002 psi). Next, 4 µL of sample was cast on the grid, followed by single-sided blotting (~100 %
relative humidity, T=4 oC) for 5-7 s using a Leica EM GP2 plunge freezer. Afterward, samples were
plunged into liquid ethane and either stored at liquid nitrogen temperature or transferred to the
electron microscope (VITROBOT Mark V) while keeping the temperature below -160 oC to prevent
devitrification of the sample. After transferring to the electron microscope, SerialEM was used to
minimize the electron exposure dose and limit or prevent radiation damage to the sample (34).
Images were acquired at the microscope’s eucentric height, using a defocus setting of ~-1
through -3 mm (Fig. 2C). Cryo-electron tomography reconstruction of the precursor mixture (fig.
S1C), showing the precursor consisting of numerous ~5 nm-sized templating particles (see the

micelle diameter was found to be 5.5 ± 0.9 nm (30 samplings) and is shown in the inset of Fig.

II-3. Porosity and nanoscale morphology characterization
 The specific surface area of the MMM samples was determined and nanoscale morphology
was further characterized using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method (Fig. 6). Porosity
analysis for MMM samples were performed through probing nitrogen adsorption-desorption
using a Quantachrome NOVA Touch pore analyzer at 77 K. Prior to these measurements, different
MMM samples (approximately 30–50 mg of each) were dried at 70 °C for 48 hours, followed by
outgassing under vacuum at 100 °C for at least 24 hours. The samples were then placed in tube-
shaped sample holders for the BET analysis (36).
 Specific surface area calculations were performed using the BET multi-point method with
ASiQwin software, while pore size distribution was evaluated using density functional theory
(DFT) models integrated into the instrument’s software. The specific surface area was
determined by the BET methods from the linear region of the isotherms in the relative pressure
(P/P0) range of 0.03-0.3. Total pore volume was estimated from the volume of nitrogen adsorbed
at P/P0 = 0.99, with the porosity of the studied MMM samples ranging between 85-96 % (Fig. 6A-
C). The adsorption isotherms, total surface area, individual and cumulative pore surface area of
the MMM samples were characterized (as detailed in Fig. 6D-F). Nitrogen was used as an
adsorbate, and the volume of gas adsorbed at the sample surface is determined at the boiling
point of liquid nitrogen (77 K). A BET plot was constructed by plotting (W[P0/P-1])-1 versus relative
pressure P/P0. From the linear region of the BET plot (P/P0 = 0.05–0.3), the intercept I and slope
S were determined. Using the method outlined by Lowell et al. (37), the weight of the adsorbed
monolayer (Wm) was calculated as Wm = 1/(S + I). The total surface area St follows from St=
(Na×Ax×Wm)/M, where Na is Avogadro’s number, M is the adsorbate molecular weight, and Ax is
the adsorbate cross-sectional area (16.2 Å2 for nitrogen). The specific surface area was obtained
by dividing St by the sample mass. To determine the pore size and distribution, a nitrogen
adsorption-desorption isotherm was recorded over a P/P0 range of 10-3 to 1. From this isotherm,
the pore size distribution was calculated using the non-localized DFT model integrated into the
instrument’s software.

III. Optical and thermal Characterizations, and other properties
III-1. Optical Characterizations
 Our MMMs were highly transparent and weakly optically anisotropic (Figs. 1A, B and 7),
effectively behaving like "frozen air", in contrast to hazy conventional aerogels that are often
referred to as "frozen smoke". Figure 7B illustrates dramatic differences between transmission
of light incident obliquely on edge faces of MMM and conventional window glass, where glass is
non-transparent for large incidence angles due to the large contrast between refractive indices
of glass (1.52) and air (1.0003). Because the refractive index of MMM (1.025-1.030) nearly
matched that of air, MMM remained highly transparent even at large incidence angles, Fig. 7A-
D. The very small <0.03 difference between the indices of air and MMM as compared to that of
glass (>0.5) also caused the net total measured reflection from two MMM-air interfaces to be
~0.02%, much lower than the corresponding ~8% reflectivity value for a slab of glass (Fig. 7D,
inset).
 Furthermore, even the very low reflection from air-MMM interfaces disappeared when two
slabs of MMM were pressed against each other to achieve optical contact (Fig. 7, E and F), which
was easier to achieve than for glass because of the comparatively soft nature of MMM. As a
result, > 99% of normal incident visible light was transmitted, with transmittance remaining high
(>90%) even for incidence angles >70° and over the entire visible spectrum (Fig. 7B and F).

 24

Conventional laser cutting (inset of Fig. 7F) allowed fabrication of multi-centimeter-thick slabs of
MMM of arbitrary geometric shapes that could be invisibly tiled with other slabs through creating
optical contacts (24).

III-2. Thermal Characterizations
Mesoporous morphology was also key to enabling the thermal properties of MMM (Fig. 10A).
Because the pores were much smaller than the mean free path between collisions of air
molecules at ambient temperatures and pressures (60 to70 nm), these molecules collide with the
pore walls more often than with each other, so gas conduction of nanoconfined air was strongly
inhibited. However, the tubular polysiloxane network of MMM was also a poor thermal
conductor because of its geometric complexity, poor thermal contacts at tubular junctions, and
small solid fraction by volume.

IV. Highly Insulated Glazing Applications
IV-1. Windows and Skylights
 The thermal resistance of 2.64 m2 K W-1 (≈ 15 h ft2 °F Btu−1) of optimized MMM of 2.5cm slab
thickness allowed for making window units insulated better than conventional building walls
while having geometric form-factors (thickness) comparable to that of the common double-pane
windows (Fig. 10C-I, and Figs. 11-12). Although relatively thin (3 mm) films of MMM could be
rolled and super-critically dried for applications in large-area window retrofits (Fig. 1A), scaling
of thick slabs was challenging as the cost of vessels capable of operating at the elevated pressures
of CO2-based supercritical drying is prohibitive for large samples. However, the ability to form
good "optical contact" between edge faces of MMM slabs (Fig. 7E and F) or large-area films
allowed for scaling the size of high-R MMM-IGUs made from overall laterally large and thick
MMM slabs (Fig. 10C-I). These slabs could be laser-cut to various geometric shapes (inset of Fig.
7F), providing the ability to make large complex-shaped high-insulation IGUs (Fig. 7G) (24).
 Because MMM effectively blocked thermal-range blackbody radiation under window-
relevant conditions (Fig. 7B), high-R thermal insulation could be achieved even without using low-
emissivity coatings on glass (Fig. 7C-I) (24), with experimentally measured R-values being
consistent with the results of computational modeling (Fig. 14, and Table 1) (24, 25). On the other
hand, 3 to 4 mm thick films of MMM, when adhered to the interior surfaces of single pane
windows, boosted their efficiency to a value comparable to that of conventional double pane
windows, R ≈ 0.53 m2 K W-1 (3 h ft2 °F Btu−1) or higher.

Future Perspectives
 The combination of visible-range transparency and thermal insulation is of interest for many
applications beyond windows. In one example (Fig. 18), we show how it allows for solar thermal
energy harnessing, with the potential utility in boosting energy efficiency of building envelopes,
especially in cold climate zones. By encasing a broadband blackbody solar absorber with our
MMM that has high transparency within the entire solar spectrum (Fig. 18A), we can entrap the
absorbed solar energy as solar thermal energy that can be then used for needs like heating water
or building interior (Fig. 18B-D). This solar heat harnessing yields high stagnation temperatures
of ~300 ℃ of the absorber for both solar simulator and ambient solar radiation (Fig. 18B). At the
high temperatures of blackbody receiver, the radiation spectrum shifts to shorter wavelengths,
where MMM becomes more transparent (Fig. 18C). Therefore, differently from the ambient
temperatures case, the radiative heat transfer becomes important at high stagnation
temperatures of the blackbody receiver, making MMM less effective as heat barriers and causing
some heat loss (Fig. 18D). While one can further modify MMM to make them more opaque in
the range 3-6 µm to further boost entrapment of heat during the solar thermal energy
harnessing, the demonstrated stagnation temperatures (Fig. 18B) are the highest temperatures
achieved so far for unconcentrated solar radiation, to the best of our knowledge. The harnessed
thermal energy can be channeled for heating building interior or water using heat exchangers.
Importantly, this can be done even on a cloudy day, when ambient solar energy is at ~0.3 sun,
which could be particularly advantageous in cold climates, like in Alaska, effectively allowing to
convert some small parts of the opaque component of the building envelope into solar heat-
based power generators capable of reducing the energy consumption of buildings during winter-
type weather conditions.

have performed Energy plus simulations to estimate both the energy saving with the help of more
efficient MMM-IGUs and solar thermal energy generation that could replace other energy
sources used for heating and cooling the building interior, like electricity and gas. The use of wall-
grade energy-efficient MMM-IGUs would allow for increasing the fraction of building envelopes
occupied by windows. This would allow for boosting the windows area percentage from the
current 8% to 30% while reducing the energy wasted for heating (Fig. 10I and Figs. 19-20). The
global variations of solar thermal energy generation per square-meter of MMM-based solar
thermal panels which can be converted into interior-heating or cooling energy or water heating
energy in buildings. Interestingly, in most cases only a fraction of a typical single-family-home
opaque envelope would need to be used to fully satisfy building's heating and cooling needs.
Having larger areas of solar thermal harnessing panels would then allow for generation of energy
beyond building's heating and cooling needs, effectively converting it into an energy generating
plant.

 We have demonstrated transparent MMM metamaterials with lower than still air’s thermal
conductivity, comparable to that of aerogels used in the Mars Rover (4-12, 26). Cold-temperature
insulation of our MMM-IGUs stopped 95% of heat transfer, making windows as insulated as high-
performing building walls. Self-assembly-based fabrication and optical-contact-enabled scaling
of MMM slabs and films with diverse geometric form-factors is suitable for applications in various
parts of a building envelope. Thus, our MMM can greatly expand the use of insulated glazing in
envelopes of future energy-efficient buildings, including in windows, skylights and porous-
material-based bricks for daylighting (27,28). Although silicone-based sealants are already widely
used in IGUs, our MMM could replace the conventional gas fillers with “frozen air” nanosponge-
like methyl-silicone-based fillers. Potential applications of MMM may extend to greenhouse
coverings, protective and heat-insulated apparel (29), space exploration (15) and in many other
instances where the unique combination of optical transparency and thermal insulation is
needed. Our MMM can be surface-functionalized or modified by addition of nanoparticles (30)
to achieve custom-designed properties ranging from solar gain control to carbon dioxide capture
and air purification (31), including potential development of disposable insulating air-cleaning
windows (24).
