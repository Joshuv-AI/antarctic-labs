A proof of purely singular splitting conjecture
Ka Hin Leung, Tao Zhang

Abstract
A set M M of nonzero integers is said to split a finite abelian group G G if there exists a subset S ⊆ G S\subseteq G such that M ⋅ S = G ∖ { 0 } M\cdot S=G\setminus\{0\} . Such a splitting is called purely singular if every prime divisor of | G | |G| divides some element of M M . In 1995, Woldar [ 32 ] conjectured that the finite abelian groups admitting a purely singular splitting by the set { 1 , 2 , … , k } \{1,2,\dots,k\} are precisely the cyclic groups of orders 1 1 , k + 1 k+1 , and 2 ​ k + 1 2k+1 . In this paper, we prove this conjecture. Keywords : Purely singular splitting, lattice tiling, semi-cross. AMS subject classifications : 52C22, 05A18, 11H71, 20K01.

Introduction

Problems involving tilings of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by clusters of cubes have a long and rich history, dating back at least to the early work of Minkowski
[
20
]
. In order to formalize this setting, let

Q=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):0\leq x_{i}<1,\ x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\}

denote the unit cube, which we regard as being placed at the origin.

Given a vector
e\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a unit cube placed at
e
is defined as the translate

e+Q=\{e+x:x\in Q\}.

Building on this notion, a cluster of cubes is defined as a finite (or, more generally, discrete) union of pairwise disjoint translates of
Q
, namely

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{B}+Q=\{e+Q:e\in\mathcal{E}\},

where
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

With this terminology in place, we can now describe packings and tilings. A set of pairwise disjoint translates of
\mathcal{C}
is called a packing of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
\mathcal{C}
. If, in addition, these translates cover the entire space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then the packing is said to be a tiling.

Furthermore, when the set of translation vectors forms an additive subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, the tiling is called a lattice tiling. This algebraic structure allows for a useful discrete reformulation: if
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, then a tiling of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
\mathcal{C}
is equivalent to a tiling of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
\mathcal{B}
.

Motivated by applications to error-correcting codes for flash memories, Wei and Schwartz
[
31
]
introduced the notion of a limited-magnitude error ball. For integers
n\geq t\geq 1
and
k_{+}\geq k_{-}\geq 0
, the
(n,t,k_{+},k_{-})
-error ball is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(n,t,k_{+},k_{-}):=\{{\bf a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:\ a_{i}\in[-k_{-},k_{+}],\ \mathrm{wt}({\bf a})\leq t\},

where
\mathrm{wt}({\bf a})
denotes the Hamming weight of
{\bf a}
.

As a first observation, when
n=t
, the set
\mathcal{B}(n,t,k_{+},k_{-})
coincides with the cube
[-k_{-},k_{+}]^{n}
. Therefore, in what follows, we restrict our attention to the nontrivial case
1\leq t\leq n-1
.

The study of tilings (and packings) of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
\mathcal{B}(n,1,k_{+},k_{-})
has attracted considerable attention in recent years, both for its intrinsic combinatorial interest and for its applications to flash memory. In particular, extensive research has been devoted to the cases of the cross
\mathcal{B}(n,1,k,k)
and the semi-cross
\mathcal{B}(n,1,k,0)
(see, for example,
[
4
,
7
,
8
,
10
,
15
,
16
,

,

,

,

,

,
30
,
32
]
).

Subsequently, Schwartz
[
22
]
generalized these shapes to the quasi-cross
\mathcal{B}(n,1,k_{+},k_{-})
, which has since been the subject of extensive investigation (see
[
23
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
,
39
,
42
]
and the references therein).

In contrast, for the case
t\geq 2
, only a limited number of results are known (see
[
1
,
6
,
16
,
18
,
26
,
41
]
and the references therein). This highlights a significant gap in our understanding of higher-weight error balls.

It is also worth noting that the cross
\mathcal{B}(n,1,1,1)
coincides with the Lee sphere of radius
1
. More generally, the existence of tilings of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by Lee spheres is a long-standing open problem, known as the Golomb–Welch conjecture
[
5
]
. Except for the cases of radius
1
or dimension
2
, it is widely conjectured that such tilings do not exist. We refer the reader to
[
2

In this paper, we focus on lattice tilings of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by semi-cross
\mathcal{B}(n,1,k,0)
. It is well known that this geometric problem can be reduced to an algebraic one, namely the problem of splitting finite abelian groups.

To this end, let
G
be a finite abelian group, written additively. For
s\in G
and a nonnegative integer
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we denote by
ms
the sum
s+\cdots+s
, where
s
appears
m
times.

Definition 1.1
.

A
splitting
of
G
consists of a pair of sets
M\subset\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, called the
multiplier set
, and
S=\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n}\}\subseteq G
, called the
splitter set
, such that every nonzero element of
G
admits a unique representation of the form
ms
, where
m\in M
and
s\in S
.

Let
k
be a positive integer, and define

S(k):=\{1,2,\dots,k\}.

The connection between tilings and group splittings is given by the following classical result.

Theorem 1.2
.

[
10
,
27
]

There exists a lattice tiling of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by the semi-cross
\mathcal{B}(n,1,k,0)
if and only if there exists a finite abelian group
G
of order
nk+1
such that
S(k)
splits
G
.

Moreover, the above problem can be further reduced to the cyclic case.

Theorem 1.3
.

[
9
,
27
]

If
S(k)
splits a finite abelian group
G
, then it also splits the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{|G|}
.

Consequently, the existence of lattice tilings by semi-crosses is closely tied to the structure of splittings of cyclic groups. In particular, the following necessary condition is known.

Theorem 1.4
.

[
27
, Chapter 4, Theorem 3]

Let
n\geq 3
and
k
be integers. If
S(k)
splits a finite abelian group
G
of order
nk+1
, then
k\leq n-2
.

To further investigate splittings of finite abelian groups, we recall the following definition from
[
27
]
.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group, and let
M
and
S
be the multiplier and splitter sets forming a splitting of
G
. The splitting is called
nonsingular
if
\gcd(|G|,m)=1
for every
m\in M
. Otherwise, it is called
singular
. Moreover, if for every prime divisor
p
of
|G|
there exists some
m\in M
such that
p\mid m
, then the splitting is called
purely singular
.

This terminology provides a useful framework for the study of splittings, since the classification problem for splittings of finite abelian groups can be reduced to the classification of nonsingular and purely singular splittings
[
27
]
. Although a number of results are known for nonsingular splittings, the purely singular case remains much less understood. One result established by Woldar
[
32
]
is the following.

Theorem 1.6
.

[
32
]

If
S(k)
splits a finite abelian group
G
purely singularly, then either
\gcd(|G|,6)=1
or
G
is one of
\mathbb{Z}_{1}
,
\mathbb{Z}_{k+1}
, and
\mathbb{Z}_{2k+1}
.

He further proposed the following conjecture, which, according to Woldar, was verified by Hickerson for all
k\leq 3000

[
32
]
.

Conjecture 1
.

[
32
]

If
S(k)
splits a finite abelian group
G
purely singularly, then
G
is one of
\mathbb{Z}_{1}
,
\mathbb{Z}_{k+1}
, or
\mathbb{Z}_{2k+1}
.

The main goal of this paper is to prove Conjecture
1
. In view of Theorem
1.4
, it is sufficient to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
n
and
k
be integers. If
S(k)
splits
\mathbb{Z}_{nk+1}
purely singularly, then
k\geq n
.

2
Proof of Theorem
1.7

In this section, we prove our main result, namely Theorem
1.7
. We begin by recalling the following lemma, which will play an important role in the proof.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
29
]

If
p
is an odd prime and
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{t}}\setminus\{0\}=M\cdot S
is a splitting, then either
M
or
S
consists entirely of elements relatively prime to
p
.

Suppose that
S(k)
splits the cyclic group
G
in a purely singular manner. By Theorem
1.6
, we may further assume that
\gcd(|G|,6)=1
. Let
p
be the smallest prime divisor of
|G|
, and write
|G|=p^{\alpha}m
, where
\gcd(p,m)=1
and
m\geq 1
. Let
S=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}
be a splitter set corresponding to this splitting. Then

p^{\alpha}m=kn+1.

For each integer
i
, we define

G_{i}=\{x\in G:p^{i}\parallel\circ(x)\}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{i}=S\cap G_{i}.

Here
\circ(x)
denotes the order of
x
in
G
, and
p^{i}\parallel\circ(x)
means that
p^{i}\mid\circ(x)
but
p^{i+1}\nmid\circ(x)
.

We first consider the elements of
G_{\alpha}
. Counting these elements via the splitting yields

\left(k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor\right)|S_{\alpha}|=p^{\alpha-1}(p-1)m.

It follows that there exist integers
0\leq\beta\leq\alpha-1,d,
and
m^{\prime}
such that

k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor=p^{\beta}dm^{\prime},

where
d=\gcd\!\left(k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor,\,p-1\right)
and
m^{\prime}\mid m
.
Consequently, there exist integers
0\leq l\leq l^{\prime}
,
1\leq\beta_{i}\leq\alpha_{i}
, and distinct primes
p_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq l^{\prime}
such that

m=\prod_{i=1}^{l^{\prime}}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\quad\text{and}\quad m^{\prime}=\prod_{i=1}^{l}p_{i}^{\beta_{i}}.

As
(p^{\beta}m^{\prime})\mid(p^{\alpha}m)
, there exists a unique subgroup
M^{\prime}
of
G
of order
p^{\beta}m^{\prime}
. Without loss of generality, assume that

S\cap\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}m}^{*}=\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{r}\}.

For each
1\leq i\leq r
, define

W_{i}=\{\,s_{i}+x:x\in M^{\prime},\ s_{i}+x\in\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}m}^{*}\,\}.

Then

|W_{i}|=p^{\beta}\prod_{j=1}^{l}u_{j},

where

u_{j}=\begin{cases}p_{j}^{\beta_{j}},&\text{if }\beta_{j}<\alpha_{j},\\[4.0pt]
p_{j}^{\beta_{j}-1}(p_{j}-1),&\text{if }\beta_{j}=\alpha_{j}.\end{cases}

Let
T=[1,d]
, and define

TW_{i}=\{\,t\omega:1\leq t\leq d,\ \omega\in W_{i}\,\}.

Since
d<p<p_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq l^{\prime}
, we have

\{1,2,\ldots,d\}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}m}^{*}
. Consequently,

|TW_{i}|=d|W_{i}|
. Therefore,

|TW_{i}|\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}dp^{\beta}+dp^{\beta}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}p_{j}^{\beta_{j}-1}(p_{j}-1),&\mbox{ if }\beta_{j}<\alpha_{j}\mbox{ for some }1\leq j\leq\ell,\\
dp^{\beta}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}p_{j}^{\beta_{j}-1}(p_{j}-1),&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(1)

Lemma 2.2
.

(a)

TW_{i}\cap TW_{j}=\emptyset
for all distinct
1\leq i,j\leq r
.

(b)

\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}TW_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}m}^{*},

and
r|TW_{i}|\leq|\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}m}^{*}|.

Proof.

(a) Suppose, to the contrary, that there exist
t_{1},t_{2}\in T
and
x_{1},x_{2}\in M^{\prime}
such that

t_{1}(x_{1}+s_{i})=t_{2}(x_{2}+s_{j}).

Multiplying both sides by
p^{\beta}m^{\prime}
, we obtain

p^{\beta}m^{\prime}t_{1}(x_{1}+s_{i})=p^{\beta}m^{\prime}t_{2}(x_{2}+s_{j}).

Since
p^{\beta}m^{\prime}x_{1}=p^{\beta}m^{\prime}x_{2}=0
in
G
, it follows that

p^{\beta}m^{\prime}t_{1}s_{i}=p^{\beta}m^{\prime}t_{2}s_{j}.

Observe that
1\leq p^{\beta}m^{\prime}t_{1},\ p^{\beta}m^{\prime}t_{2}\leq k.

Hence the above equality contradicts the uniqueness of representations in the splitting. Therefore,
TW_{i}\cap TW_{j}=\emptyset

whenever
i\neq j
.

(b) By the definition of
W_{i}
, we have
W_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}m}^{*}.

Since both
T
and
W_{i}
are subsets of
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}m}^{*}
, it follows that
\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}TW_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}m}^{*}.

The inequality
r|TW_{i}|\leq|\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}m}^{*}|
now follows immediately from part (a).
∎

Next, define the following subsets of integers:

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\{x\in[1,k]:\gcd(x,\textstyle\prod_{i=1}^{l}p_{i})=1\},

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\{x\in[1,\lfloor k/p\rfloor]:\gcd(x,\textstyle\prod_{i=1}^{l}p_{i})=1\},

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\{x\in[1,k-\lfloor k/p\rfloor]:\gcd(x,\textstyle\prod_{i=1}^{l}p_{i})=1\},

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\{x\in[1,k]:\gcd(x,p\textstyle\prod_{i=1}^{l}p_{i})=1\},

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\{x\in[1,k]:\gcd(x,|G|)=1\}.

It is immediate to see that
A=(dp^{\beta}m^{\prime}+B)\cup C
and
(dp^{\beta}m^{\prime}+B)\cap C=\emptyset,

and hence
|A|=|B|+|C|
.
Recalling that
k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor=p^{\beta}dm^{\prime}
, we then obtain
|C|=p^{\beta}d\prod_{i=1}^{l}p_{i}^{\beta_{i}-1}(p_{i}-1).
Observe that

\displaystyle A\setminus D

\displaystyle=\{x\in[1,k]:p\mid x,\ \gcd(x/p,\textstyle\prod_{i=1}^{l}p_{i})=1\}

\displaystyle=\{py:y\in[1,\lfloor k/p\rfloor],\ \gcd(y,\textstyle\prod_{i=1}^{l}p_{i})=1\}

\displaystyle=\{py:y\in B\}.

Therefore,
|A\setminus D|=|B|
and consequently,

|D|=|A|-|A\setminus D|=|A|-|B|=|C|=p^{\beta}d\prod_{i=1}^{l}p_{i}^{\beta_{i}-1}(p_{i}-1).

In view of (
1
) and the definitions of
D
and
E
, we deduce that

|TW_{i}|=p^{\beta}d\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}p_{i}^{\beta_{i}-1}(p_{i}-1)\geq|D|\geq|E|.

Moreover, note that
|D|>|E|
if
l<l^{\prime}
, and that
|TW_{i}|>|D|
if
\beta_{j}<\alpha_{j}
for some
1\leq j\leq\ell
.
On the other hand, by Lemma
2.2
,

|\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}m}^{*}|\geq\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}TW_{i}\right|\geq r|D|\geq r|E|=|\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha}m}^{*}|.

It follows that equality must hold throughout. In particular,
|TW_{i}|=|D|=|E|
.
By the observations above, we conclude that
p,p_{1},\dots,p_{l}
are precisely the prime divisors of
|G|
, that is,
\ell=\ell^{\prime}
, and moreover,

\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,\ell
.
Consequently,

|G|=p^{\alpha}\prod_{i=1}^{l}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}},\ k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor=p^{\beta}dm\mbox{ and }|S_{\alpha}|=p^{\alpha-1-\beta}\left(\frac{p-1}{d}\right).

To complete the proof of Theorem
1.7
, we first show that
\beta=\alpha-1.

Lemma 2.3
.

k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor=p^{\alpha-1}dm.

Proof.

Recall that

k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor=p^{\beta}dm

for some
\beta\leq\alpha-1
. It remains to show that
\beta=\alpha-1
. The case
\alpha=1
is trivial, so we may assume
\alpha\geq 2
.
Write the base-
p
expansion of
k
as

k=b_{r}p^{r}+\cdots+b_{1}p+b_{0},

where
0\leq b_{i}\leq p-1
and
b_{r}\neq 0
. Since
p^{\beta}\parallel\bigl(k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor\bigr)
, we have

b_{\beta}=b_{\beta-1}=\cdots=b_{1}=b_{0},

and if
r>\beta
, then
b_{\beta+1}\neq b_{\beta}
.

We claim that

|S_{\alpha-1}|=\cdots=|S_{\alpha-\beta}|=0.

By counting elements in
G_{\alpha}
and
G_{\alpha-1}
via the splitting, we obtain

\left(k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor\right)|S_{\alpha}|=p^{\alpha-1}(p-1)m,

(2)

\left(k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor\right)|S_{\alpha-1}|+\left(\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p^{2}}\right\rfloor\right)|S_{\alpha}|=p^{\alpha-2}(p-1)m.

(3)

Multiplying (
3
) by
p
and comparing with (
2
), we obtain

p\left(k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor\right)|S_{\alpha-1}|=\left(k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor-p\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor+p\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p^{2}}\right\rfloor\right)|S_{\alpha}|=(b_{0}-b_{1})|S_{\alpha}|=0,

since
b_{0}=b_{1}
. Hence
|S_{\alpha-1}|=0
.

Proceeding inductively, assume that
|S_{\alpha-1}|=\cdots=|S_{\alpha-u}|=0
for some
u<\beta
. Since
1\leq\alpha-\beta\leq\alpha-u-1
, by considering elements in
G_{\alpha-u-1}
, we obtain

\left(k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor\right)|S_{\alpha-u-1}|+\left(\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p^{u+1}}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p^{u+2}}\right\rfloor\right)|S_{\alpha}|=p^{\alpha-u-2}(p-1)m.

(4)

Note that by assumption
|S_{\alpha-1}|=\cdots=|S_{\alpha-u}|=0
.
Arguing as before, we obtain

\displaystyle p^{u+1}\left(k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor\right)|S_{\alpha-u-1}|

\displaystyle=\left(k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor-p^{u+1}\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p^{u+1}}\right\rfloor+p^{u+1}\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p^{u+2}}\right\rfloor\right)|S_{\alpha}|

\displaystyle=((b_{u}p^{u}+\cdots+b_{0})-(b_{u+1}p^{u}+\cdots+b_{1}))|S_{\alpha}|=0,

since
b_{0}=b_{1}=\cdots=b_{u+1}
. Thus
|S_{\alpha-u-1}|=0
, proving the claim.

Case (1):
m>1
.

In this case,
k>p^{\beta+1}
, and hence
b_{\beta+1}\neq b_{0}
. If
\alpha>\beta+1
, then by considering the elements in
G_{\alpha-\beta-1}
and arguing as before, we obtain

\displaystyle p^{\beta+1}\left(k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor\right)|S_{\alpha-\beta-1}|

\displaystyle=\left(k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor-p^{\beta+1}\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p^{\beta+1}}\right\rfloor+p^{\beta+1}\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p^{\beta+2}}\right\rfloor\right)|S_{\alpha}|

\displaystyle=p^{\beta}(b_{0}-b_{\beta+1})|S_{\alpha}|.

Substituting
k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor=p^{\beta}dm
into the above identity yields

p^{\beta+1}dm|S_{\alpha-\beta-1}|=(b_{0}-b_{\beta+1})|S_{\alpha}|.

Since
p<p_{1}
and
1\leq|b_{0}-b_{\beta+1}|\leq p-1
, it follows that
p_{1}\mid|S_{\alpha}|
. On the other hand,

|S_{\alpha}|d=(p-1)p^{\alpha-\beta-1},

which implies that
p_{1}\nmid|S_{\alpha}|
, a contradiction. Therefore,
\beta=\alpha-1
.

Case (2):
m=1
.

In this case,
|G|=p^{\alpha}
, where
p
is an odd prime. By Lemma
2.1
and the fact that
p\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
, the splitter set
S
must consist entirely of elements of order
p^{\alpha}
. Hence,
|S_{\alpha}|=|S|
. Since
k|S|=kn=p^{\alpha}-1
, we have
p\nmid|S_{\alpha}|
. However,

|S_{\alpha}|=p^{\alpha-1-\beta}\left(\frac{p-1}{d}\right)
, which is impossible unless
\beta=\alpha-1
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Recall that
p^{\alpha}m=kn+1
and

k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p}\right\rfloor=p^{\alpha-1}dm.

Since
|G|\neq p
, either
\alpha\geq 2
or
m>1
. In either case,

k\geq p^{\alpha-1}dm>p>\frac{|G|-1}{k}=n.

This completes the proof of Theorem
1.7
.
