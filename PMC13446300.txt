Management of parastomal variceal bleeding in an adult with metastatic liver cancer: A case-based review
Authors: Joshua Bruinsma, Hugo C. Temperley, Padraig Cronin, Niall J. O’Sullivan, Benjamin M. Mac Curtain, Kevin P. Sheahan, Zi Qin Ng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Parastomal variceal bleeding is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication in patients with portal hypertension and intestinal stomas, for which evidence-based management strategies remain poorly defined. A case-based review was performed synthesizing available literature and institutional experience to evaluate diagnostic approaches and treatment modalities for parastomal variceal bleeding, with emphasis on acute control, prevention of recurrence, and multidisciplinary management. Reported management strategies include medical optimization of portal hypertension, local measures, endovascular embolization, transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt placement, and surgical revision, each with variable efficacy and recurrence risk; no single approach was universally effective. The management of parastomal variceal bleeding in adults should be individualized and directed by severity of bleeding, etiology of portal hypertension, hepatic reserve, comorbidities, prognosis and anatomical suitability for intervention. We recommend using a multi-disciplinary, stepwise approach although treatment should be guided by underlying etiologies and patient specific goals.

Background

Parastomal varices are extraperitoneal, ectopic, abnormally dilated veins that develop at stoma sites due to elevated portosystemic venous pressure. The pathophysiology involves redirection of portal flow through low-resistance systemic collateral pathways at the mucocutaneous junction of a stoma. Unlike esophageal or gastric varices, parastomal varices often present with slow, intermittent superficial bleeding, though brisk hemorrhage can occur. These varices can be classified as type 1, also known as non-occlusive or oncotic type, due to portal hypertension, or type 2, also known as occlusive type, which is caused by venous occlusion [1]. Parastomal varices develop in an estimated 50% of patients with concomitant portal hypertension, with over a quarter of these patients presenting with a parastomal variceal bleed [1]. Parastomal varices are classically associated with patients who have developed primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) with concomitant ulcerative colitis and who have undergone ileostomy formation following proctocolectomy [2].

Case summary

A 48-year-old female with metastatic rectal adenocarcinoma reported intermittent bleeding from the peri-stomal region. She had originally presented six months earlier with pelvic pain and a 12-month history of altered bowel habits. Her history was significant for obesity and chronic alcohol use. Initial CT abdomen/pelvis demonstrated a rectal mass with bilobar, non-resectable liver metastases. There were no definite radiological features of cirrhosis at presentation. Retrospective review of staging and follow-up imaging was undertaken to assess for earlier signs of portal hypertension, including splenomegaly, ascites, portosystemic collaterals, portal or mesenteric venous thrombosis or peristomal collateralization however they were not identified. Colonoscopy was unable to traverse the obstructing rectal tumor. Her case was reviewed at the multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, and a laparoscopic loop colostomy was performed due to impending large bowel obstruction. Baseline CEA was 200 µg/L. Liver function tests demonstrated a total bilirubin of 12 µmol/L, ALT 16 U/L, ALP 145 U/L, GGT 150 U/L, and albumin 42 g/L.

She subsequently received 12 cycles of palliative chemotherapy (FOLFOX with bevacizumab), with interval imaging after 6 months showing a reduction in the rectal primary and liver metastases. She remained stoma dependent.

She re-presented with intermittent bleeding around the stoma. Examination showed a small parastomal hernia and prominent superficial vascular channels around the mucocutaneous junction. CT abdomen/pelvis demonstrated dilated peri-stomal varices communicating with mesenteric venous branches without other acute pathology (Fig. 1). Following multidisciplinary team discussion, the patient underwent interventional radiology–guided transhepatic venous embolization under general anesthesia. Transhepatic embolization was selected because cross-sectional imaging demonstrated a dominant, targetable inflow to the variceal network. This patient had advanced metastatic malignancy, preserved liver function, and an embolizable bleeding source. The aim was to obtain local hemorrhage control whilst avoiding the morbidity of stoma revision surgery or systemic consequences of TIPS. Percutaneous right portal vein access was obtained, allowing catheterization of the inferior mesenteric vein and its sigmoid tributaries. Initial portal venography demonstrated marked retrograde flow within the inferior mesenteric vein and extensive portosystemic collateralization at the level of the stoma (Fig. 2), with selective catheterization delineating the dominant sigmoid venous inflow to the peristomal varices (Fig. 3). Portal venous pressure was measured at 33 mmHg. Stepwise embolization was then performed using multiple 6-8 mm detachable coils within the sigmoid and inferior mesenteric venous branches (Fig. 4); post-coil venography demonstrated reduced but persistent variceal flow (Fig. 5). Subsequent deployment of MVP vascular plugs (MVP7Q and MVP5Q) achieved complete occlusion of variceal inflow on completion venography (Fig. 6). The transhepatic access tract was closed with a 3:1 Lipiodol–Histoacryl mixture. Presentation and diagnosis

Parastomal variceal bleeding (PVB) is the most common presentation to emergent care postoperatively in this cohort, with a reported incidence of 27% [3]. First described in the 1960s, these bleeds typically occur 48 months post-stoma formation in these patients [4]. They usually present with intermittent, non-massive peri-stomal bleeding. Hemodynamic stability and preserved liver function suggested a low-risk variceal bleed. The presence of liver metastases without cirrhosis indicated possible pre-sinusoidal or sinusoidal portal hypertension due to hepatic tumor burden. Differential diagnoses include granulation tissue bleeding, mucosal irritation, and stomal trauma.

Although the diagnostic approach to portal hypertension has similarities, the driving etiology has important implications on management. In cirrhotic disease, assessment should focus on hepatic reserve, ascites, encephalopathy, renal function, MELD or Child-Pugh status, and suitability for transplant [5,6]. In comparison, non-cirrhotic disease should evaluate hepatic tumor burden, portal or mesenteric vein patency, systemic disease, performance status, prognosis, and goals of treatment [7,8].

Diagnosis is typically established through biphasic contrast-enhanced CT or Doppler ultrasound demonstrating serpiginous variceal channels arising from mesenteric venous tributaries [9]. Cross-sectional imaging can delineate feeding vessels and evaluate suitability for endovascular therapy. In clinical practice, the assessment of portal hypertension is conducted through history, examination, laboratory markers, cross-sectional imaging, and multi-disciplinary input [5]. Important features include ascites, splenomegaly, thrombocytopenia, portosystemic collaterals, portal or mesenteric venous patency, liver morphology, and synthetic function [10]. When intervention is undertaken, portal venography and direct portal pressure measurement may define future management [11].

Management

Most peristomal variceal bleeds can initially be managed conservatively with manual compression or local epinephrine injection via endoscopy. However, failure of these measures necessitates escalation of care. Available treatment options for PVB include pharmacological management, local therapies, percutaneous transvenous embolization, transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt stent insertion (TIPSS), and stoma re-siting or revision [12]. To guide clinical decision-making, we propose a pragmatic framework to support decision making in the assessment and management of PVB. This framework is not intended as a universal guideline, but rather a structured approach to support decision-making based on factors such as bleeding severity, etiology of portal hypertension, hepatic reserve, performance status, prognosis, and goals of care (Fig. 8). Patients with PVB are frequently poor surgical candidates in both emergent and elective settings, with comorbidities such as hypoalbuminemia and anemia associated with impaired wound healing and increased postoperative mortality [13,14]. The presence of concomitant cirrhosis further exacerbates morbidity, with reported complication rates of up to 83%, including prolonged hospitalization due to ascites, renal failure, bleeding, and infection [15]. All cases should be reviewed in a multidisciplinary forum involving interventional radiology, hepatology, and surgery to guide subsequent management based on bleeding severity, recurrence, liver function, anatomical suitability, performance status, and comorbidities. In patients with advanced cirrhosis or decompensated portal hypertension, management decisions should factor in MELD or Child-Pugh scores, encephalopathy risk, ascites, renal function, and candidacy for transplant [5,16]. In patients with portal hypertension or chronic liver disease, optimization of liver function and reduction of portal pressure remain important components of management. Although there is no direct evidence supporting the use of non-selective beta-blockers (NSBBs) in the management of ectopic variceal bleeding, their established role in gastro-esophageal varices allows cautious extrapolation to this setting [9]. NSBBs such as propranolol or carvedilol have been shown to reduce portal venous pressures by approximately 15% and may therefore be considered as adjunctive therapy, alongside standard management of ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, and alcohol-related liver disease [17]. While unlikely to provide definitive hemorrhage control in active PVB, these measures may contribute to portal pressure reduction and lower recurrence risk when used in conjunction with definitive local or portal decompressive interventions.

Image-guided interventions have become the cornerstone of treatment for PVB, offering effective hemorrhage control with lower morbidity than surgical approaches. These include percutaneous venous embolization techniques and portal decompressive procedures such as TIPSS [18]. Endovascular embolization may be performed via transhepatic or percutaneous venous access, targeting feeding vessels, including inferior mesenteric or stomal tributaries, with technical success rates of >90%. Embolic agents, such as coils, glue, sclerosants, or a combination of techniques, are used to exclude variceal inflow while preserving main mesenteric venous drainage [12].

TIPS remains an effective strategy for addressing the underlying portal hypertension and reducing rebleeding risk, particularly in patients with recurrent or refractory bleeding. When combined with percutaneous antegrade transhepatic venous obliteration (PATVO), reported success rates approach 95%, although rebleeding rates of approximately 20% persist in the literature [19]. For longer-term outcomes, rebleeding following embolization alone has been reported in 20–30% of patients at one year. In the absence of progressive portal hypertension, recurrence risk may be lower; however, progression of hepatic metastatic disease or worsening liver function may increase susceptibility. In cases of recurrent bleeding, repeat embolization or alternative access routes may be required, and reassessment for TIPSS should be undertaken where portal hypertension remains significant. However, TIPS may be unsuitable in some patients, particularly those with advanced liver disease, high risk of encephalopathy, limited hepatic reserve, unfavorable anatomy, or limited prognosis [16]. In the setting of metastatic liver disease, any decision to pursue portal decompression should factor in hepatic tumor burden, oncological trajectory, and systemic treatment options [7].

Surgical intervention should be reserved for highly selected patients in whom interventional radiology options are not feasible or have failed and who are deemed suitable operative candidates. Stoma revision or relocation may be considered in these circumstances; however, recurrence of bleeding remains likely if the underlying portal hypertension is not addressed. Surgical approaches are therefore more appropriate when bleeding is predominantly driven by local stomal factors rather than systemic portal hypertension and should be undertaken with caution, given the high perioperative risk in this patient population. In patients with decompensated cirrhosis or advanced portal hypertension, revision or relocation of the stoma is unlikely to provide long term control unless the portal hypertension is managed [19,20]. In select patients who have advanced liver disease, referral for liver transplantation may be required, although this is distinct from the management considerations of patients with advanced metastatic disease.

Follow up

Following definitive management, patients require structured follow-up to monitor for recurrent bleeding, stoma-related complications, and progression of underlying liver disease or portal hypertension. Clinical review should evaluate recurrence of bleeding, stoma application function, peristomal skin integrity, local trauma, and appliance related irritation which can precipitate bleeding. Laboratory assessment should include full blood picture, liver function tests, coagulation profile, and renal function testing.

In patients who have cirrhosis, the follow-up should include hepatology review, optimization of ascites and encephalopathy, MELD or Child-Pugh status assessment, and consideration of liver transplant [20]. In patients who have metastatic liver disease, their follow-up should be coordinated with oncology, and considerations include hepatic tumor burden, portal or mesenteric venous patency, systemic treatment options, prognosis, and goals of care [7].

In the event there is concern for recurrent bleeding, worsening stoma appearance or progression of portal hypertension, repeat contrast enhanced CT or Doppler ultrasound should be considered. In the current case, contrast-enhanced CT was performed at 6 months to assess for residual or recurrent varices.

Conclusion

Parastomal variceal bleeding is a rare but clinically significant manifestation of portal hypertension in adult patients with intestinal stomas. The management requires prompt stabilization, imaging-based diagnosis, assessment of portal hypertension severity and etiology, and careful selection for definitive treatment. The diagnostic approach is similar across causes of portal hypertension however the management differs substantially between cirrhotic induced and metastatic disease induced. Embolization may provide effective local control in patients with a focal and targetable inflow, while TIPS and other portal decompressive strategies may be more appropriate in recurrent or refractory bleeding. Surgical revision should only be reserved for well selected patients where less invasive strategies have failed. Given the limited evidence base, the management should be patient centered and arranged following multidisciplinary discussion, with ongoing surveillance to detect recurrent bleeding or underlying disease progression.
