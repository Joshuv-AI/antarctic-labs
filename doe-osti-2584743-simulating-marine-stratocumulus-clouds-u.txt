  1. Summary

 This study investigates the simulation of marine stratocumulus (stratoCu) clouds –
  common in many offshore and coastal wind energy regions - using Lagrangian Superdroplet
  Method (SDM) implemented in Energy Research and Forecasting (ERF) model. Developed by
  the Wind Energy Technologies Office (WETO), ERF is a high-resolution atmospheric
  modeling tool that relies on traditional bulk microphysics schemes. Under an LLNL-led
  Laboratory Directed Research and Development (LDRD) project, SDM was integrated into
  ERF to improve cloud process fidelity by explicitly tracking individual “superdroplets”. This
  enables a realistic representation of the droplet size spectrum, cloud-atmosphere interactions,
  and potential feedbacks from wind plants on atmospheric flow and cloud structure.

 To evaluate performance, ERF-SDM was benchmarked against ERF with Kessler bulk
  microphysics scheme (Kessler, 1969), other large-eddy simulation (LES) models, and
  observational data from the DYCOMS-II RF02 campaign - a well-documented drizzling
  marine stratocumulus event. Both ERF-SDM and ERF-Kessler produced persistent
  stratocumulus decks consistent with other LES models. Notably, SDM uniquely captured
  observed drizzle that bulk schemes did not. Although SDM tended to overproduce drizzle, this
  was attributed to its explicit sedimentation and the absence of aerosol replenishment, leading
  to fewer but larger droplets over time (Twomey effect). Consequently, simulations showed
  reduced cloud fraction, lower liquid water path, and weaker planetary boundary layer (PBL)
  turbulence relative to Kessler scheme.

 This work highlights the value of physically based microphysics for simulating cloud
  processes relevant to offshore wind forecasting. It also identifies the need for improvement,
  such as aerosol replenishment, to better align model results with observations. Overall, ERF-
  SDM shows strong potential for advancing marine cloud modeling for wind energy
  applications.

  2. Method
 a. Model frameworks

 To address the challenge of accurately simulating marine stratocumulus (stratoCu)
 clouds and their interactions with offshore wind energy systems, this study
 utilized Energy Research and Forecasting (ERF) model. ERF is a next-generation, open-
 source regional atmospheric modeling tool developed to meet the needs of the wind
 energy, atmospheric science, and environmental research communities. ERF is
 performance portable across CPUs and GPUs, supporting both fully compressible and
 anelastic equation sets for atmospheric flows. It includes advanced physics models for

 1

turbulence (LES, PBL), microphysics (Kessler, SAM 1-Moment, Morrison 2-moment,
SDM), and wind turbine modeling (Fitch and Actuator Disk Model).
 A novel Lagrangian Superdroplet Method (SDM: Shima et al., 2009) was
integrated into ERF to enhance the representation of cloud microphysics, including
condensation/evaporation, collision-coalescence, sedimentation and droplet activation.
SDM is a particle-based, Lagrangian approach for simulating cloud and precipitation
microphysics in atmospheric models. Unlike traditional bulk scheme, SDM explicitly
tracks the evolution of individual “superdroplets”, each representing a large number of
real droplets with identical physical and chemical properties such as position, velocity,
terminal velocity, radius, aerosols and their masses. As a result, SDM can resolve the
full droplet size spectrum, not just mean or bulk properties, making the method more
physically based.

b. Case study setup

 To rigorously evaluate the performance of ERF model coupled with the
Superdroplet Method (ERF-SDM), we validated its output against established large-
eddy simulation (LES) models and available observational data. This benchmarking
process is essential to ensure that ERF-SDM can reliably simulate the complex
processes governing marine stratocumulus (stratoCu) clouds, which are critical for
understanding the wind resource variability and atmospheric boundary layer dynamics
off the coast of California.

 A central focus of the assessment was to determine the ability of ERF-SDM to
capture key features of marine stratoCu, including the formation of drizzle, the
structure and intensity of turbulence, and the representation of cloud optical properties.
ERF-SDM system was compared with the traditional Kessler bulk microphysics
scheme. This comparison allowed for a detailed analysis of both microphysical and
dynamical outcomes, highlighting differences in drizzle rates, boundary layer
stratification, and cloud optical depth between the Lagrangian (SDM) and bulk
(Kessler) approaches.

 The validation experiments were conducted benchmarking the DYCOMS-II
Research Flight 02 case, a well-documented event involving drizzling marine
stratocumulus clouds. We followed the case setup documented in Ackerman et al.
(2009). The simulation domain was configured as a 1.6 km × 1.6 km × 1.6 km, with a
horizontal grid spacing of 50 meters and a vertical resolution of 5 meters to resolve
fine-scale boundary layer processes. This configuration follows the set up used in a
similar study by Yin et al. (2024), which demonstrated that this resolution produced
reasonable results. A simplified interactive radiation model was integrated into ERF-
SDM, accounting for cloud top cooling, cloud base warming, and free tropospheric
cooling, all of which are critical for maintaining realistic cloud dynamics. Surface heat
fluxes (sensible heat flux = 16 W/m2 and latent heat flux = 93 W/m2) and subsidence
warming which balances the radiation cooling at cloud top (assuming large scale
horizontal winds of 3.75e-6 s-1) were prescribed to match observational constraints. For

 2

 microphysical representation, both the Kessler bulk scheme and the Lagrangian SDM
 were employed, with the latter leveraging two log-normal distributions of ammonium
 bisulfate aerosol as cloud condensation nuclei (CCN). The total number concentrations
 for the two modes are 125 and 65 cm-3 with the mode radii and geometric standard
 deviations of 0.011 and 0.06 m, and 1.2 and 1.7, respectively. The simulation is run
 for 6 hours. The detailed case set up can be found in Ackerman et al. (2009).

 Figure 4 presents the time series of vertically integrated turbulent kinetic energy

 stratification induced by SDM leads to weaker turbulence compared to Kessler, which

 c. The reason for the stronger drizzle in SDM
 In microphysical processes, cloud droplets grow large through condensational
 growth and/or collision-coalescence. Due to gravity, these large cloud droplets or ice
 particles fall more rapidly through the atmosphere relative to the surrounding air – a
 process known as sedimentation. Sedimentation removes liquid water from the cloud
 layer, which can thin the cloud, reduce its optical depth, and influence cloud longevity.
 As precipitation generated by sedimentation falls and evaporates near the surface, it
 can cool and stabilize the PBL, thereby reducing turbulence and mixing.
 SDM explicitly tracks the size distribution and sedimentation velocities of
 individual “superdroplets”, enabling a more physically realistic simulation of
 sedimentation effects. In contrast, Kessler microphysics scheme does not include a
 sedimentation parameterization. Consequently, although SDM may overestimate
 drizzle, it is able to produce precipitation, whereas Kessler scheme does not.

 Enhanced sedimentation of cloud droplets removes liquid water from the cloud top,
 which reduces evaporative cooling at the cloud top. Since evaporative cooling is a key
 driver of turbulent mixing and the entrainment of dry air from above the cloud, reduced
 cooling leads to weak entrainment. Several studies have discussed the relationship
 between cloud sedimentation and cloud-top entrainment, including Ackerman et al.

 5

       (2004), Bretherton et al., (1997), Wood (2012), Stevens et al. (1998). As shown in Fig.5,
 entrainment in SDM is lower than that in ERF-Kessler and other LES results. Although
 ERF-Kessler does not include cloud sedimentation, and the cloud fraction remains
 close to 100% throughout the simulation, and the liquid water path is near the upper
 quartile of values simulated by other LES models that have a sedimentation
 parameterization. It is therefore somewhat puzzling that the entrainment rate is lower
 compared to other LES models; this may indicate that ERF intrinsically has a lower
 entrainment rate.
 In other microphysics model, throughout the simulation, cloud droplet
 concentration is fixed. However, as the sedimentation process removes cloud droplets
 from the cloud layer, SDM currently lacks an aerosol replenishment mechanism. As a
 result, the aerosol concentration in SDM-simulated clouds continues to decrease over
 time. With decreasing aerosol loading, a given amount of cloud water is distributed
 among fewer aerosols, resulting in fewer but larger cloud droplets. This increases the
 likelihood of cloud sedimentation and precipitation, contributing to a shorter cloud
 layer lifetime. This phenomenon is known as the “Twomey effect”, or the first indirect
 aerosol effect (Twomey, 1977).
 The physically based sedimentation process in SDM, combined with Twomey
 effect, explains why ERF-SDM produces strong drizzle, heavier than observed in
 measurements. As a results, cloud fraction and liquid water path are reduced, and PBL
 turbulence is weak due to PBL stabilization. With the implementation of aerosol
 replenishment, we expect the drizzle rate to decrease and the simulated cloud and PBL
 properties to align more closely with observed values.

5. Challenges Encounters and Lessons Learned
 a. Resource Constraint
 i. Due to budget limitations, I needed to identify and utilize free
 computational resources.
 ii. ERF project bank on the Kestrel machine at NREL and LC machines at
 LLNL were used for this project
 b. Simulation issues

 6

 i. Initially, I encountered difficulties sustaining the cloud layers in the
 simulation. The issue was traced to the model’s failure to maintain the initial
 wind speed profile, as winds in ERF tended to diminish to near zero over
 time.
 ii. After implementing a function to nudge the domain mean wind speed
 toward the initial wind speed profile, I was able to successfully simulate the
 stratocumulus deck.
 iii. This experience underscored the importance of accurately maintaining both
 the wind speed and its vertical structure to sustain stratocumulus cloud
 layers.
