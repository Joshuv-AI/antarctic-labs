Pandemic-associated shifts in microbial ecology and antimicrobial resistance in a high-volume liver transplant intensive care unit: a 10-year surveillance study
Authors: Adem Kose, Leyla Erdemci, Seyma Yasar, Funda Memisoglu, Sibel Altunisik Toplu, Cigdem Eren, Gulsah Yazlak, Elif Seren Tanriverdi, Sertac Usta, Yasemin Ersoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundHealthcare-associated infections (HAIs) remain a major source of morbidity in solid organ transplant recipients, particularly in liver transplant (LT) intensive care units (ICUs), where profound immunosuppression and extensive antimicrobial exposure shape local microbial ecology. While reductions in overall infection incidence have been reported in structured surveillance programs, longitudinal changes in pathogen distribution and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in transplant-specific ICUs remain insufficiently characterized. We aimed to evaluate pandemic-associated shifts in microbial ecology and AMR patterns across a 10-year surveillance period in a high-volume LT ICU.MethodsThis retrospective analysis was based on prospectively collected surveillance data from adult LT recipients admitted between January 2015 and December 2024. The study period was categorized into prepandemic (2015–2019), pandemic (2020–2021), and postpandemic (2022–2024) phases. All analyses were performed at the HAI-episode level. Isolation density was calculated per 1000 patient-days. Incidence rate ratios were estimated using Poisson regression models with patient-days included as an offset. Period-related differences in AMR were evaluated using logistic regression. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsAmong 7717 patients corresponding to 48,001 patient-days, 380 clinically significant isolates were analysed. Gram-negative organisms remained predominant throughout the study period, while Gram-positive isolates demonstrated a significant decreasing trend over time. Acinetobacter spp. (31.3%), Klebsiella spp. (25.8%), and Pseudomonas spp. (15.0%) were the leading pathogens. Isolation densities of Acinetobacter spp. and Escherichia coli were significantly higher in the prepandemic period than in the postpandemic period. In contrast, the pandemic and postpandemic phases were associated with significant increases in resistance probabilities among major Gram-negative pathogens. Acinetobacter spp. showed higher resistance to meropenem and amikacin, Klebsiella spp. demonstrated increased aminoglycoside and fluoroquinolone resistance, and Pseudomonas spp. exhibited increased ciprofloxacin resistance in 2020–2024 compared with 2015–2019.ConclusionsIn this transplant-specific ICU, microbial ecology and resistance trajectories evolved independently of overall infection incidence trends. Pandemic-associated healthcare disruptions were accompanied by organism-specific resistance shifts rather than uniform ecological changes. Continuous surveillance and locally tailored antimicrobial stewardship strategies are essential to preserve therapeutic efficacy and mitigate the growing threat of multi-drug-resistant (MDR) pathogens in transplant ICUs.

Introduction

Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) continue to represent a major threat to patient safety in intensive care units (ICUs), particularly among populations exposed to extensive invasive procedures and broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy [1,2]. In solid organ transplant (SOT) recipients, and especially in liver transplant (LT) patients, the risk of infection is amplified by profound immunosuppression, complex peri-operative management, repeated device exposure, and prolonged hospitalization. In this setting, infections not only are frequent but are also commonly caused by multi-drug-resistant (MDR) organisms, complicating empirical therapy and adversely affecting clinical outcomes [3,4].

While reductions in overall HAI incidence have been reported in centres implementing structured surveillance and infection prevention programs, trends in microbial ecology and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) within transplant-specific ICUs remain less well characterized [5,6]. Incidence data alone do not fully capture the evolving microbiological landscape that directly informs empirical treatment strategies and antimicrobial stewardship policies. Longitudinal assessments of pathogen distribution and resistance profiles are therefore essential to understand whether infection prevention efforts translate into shifts in microbial aetiology or merely reduce infection frequency [7]. In transplant-specific ICUs, continuous surveillance data also provide an essential foundation for antimicrobial stewardship programs by informing local empirical treatment protocols and monitoring emerging resistance threats.

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic introduced unprecedented structural and operational changes in healthcare systems worldwide. Alterations in ICU staffing models, patient flow, antimicrobial prescribing practices, and infection control dynamics created conditions that may have influenced pathogen transmission dynamics, antimicrobial consumption patterns, and AMR trajectories [8]. Furthermore, disruptions in antimicrobial stewardship activities and changes in healthcare resource allocation during the pandemic may have affected resistance selection pressure and microbial ecology in high-risk patient populations. Reports from general ICUs have demonstrated heterogeneous effects of the pandemic on AMR patterns, with some institutions observing increases in MDR organisms and others reporting stable or declining rates [9].

Leveraging a decade of continuous surveillance data from a high-volume LT ICU, we examined changes across three predefined periods – pre-COVID, COVID-19, and post-COVID – to evaluate whether pandemic-associated system-level disruptions were accompanied by measurable and sustained shifts in pathogen distribution and resistance profiles. We hypothesized that, independent of overall infection incidence trends, the COVID-19 pandemic period would be associated with detectable alterations in HAIs microbial composition and AMR patterns among major Gram-negative pathogens. By integrating long-term surveillance data with pandemic-period comparisons, this study aims to provide information that may support both infection prevention strategies and antimicrobial stewardship efforts in transplant-intensive care settings.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted using prospectively collected HAI surveillance data from a dedicated LT ICU at a high-volume tertiary transplant centre in Turkey. Continuous, unit-based infection surveillance has been performed in this ICU since January 2015 as part of the institutional infection prevention program. The present analysis covers the period from January 2015 to December 2024.

A subset of this surveillance cohort has been previously analysed with respect to long-term trends in overall HAI incidence and device-associated infection rates. The previous analysis addressed epidemiological trends in infection burden, whereas the present study was specifically designed to evaluate longitudinal changes in pathogen distribution and AMR patterns. Accordingly, the current study focusses on a distinct research question and does not reproduce any tables, figures, or primary analyses from the companion study. At the time of submission of the present manuscript, the companion study was under independent peer review and had not yet been published.

Study population

All adult patients (aged ≥18 years) admitted to the LT ICU in the immediate postoperative period following liver transplantation during the study period were eligible for inclusion. Each ICU admission was considered separately. Patients who died within 72 h after transplantation or whose care was transferred to another institution were excluded.

All analyses were performed at the HAI-episode level. For analyses of microbial aetiology and AMR, only HAI episodes with at least one clinically significant isolate available for species identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were included.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Inonu University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 2025/8564). Owing to the retrospective design and use of anonymized surveillance data, informed consent was waived. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration.

This study represents a secondary analysis of an existing surveillance cohort. No tables, figures, or primary outcome analyses from previous investigations were reproduced, and the present manuscript addresses a distinct research objective focussed specifically on microbial ecology and antimicrobial resistance patterns.

Surveillance definitions

HAIs were identified through continuous, prospective, patient-based surveillance conducted by trained infection prevention personnel. Surveillance definitions and diagnostic criteria were based on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention/National Healthcare Safety Network (CDC/NHSN) Patient Safety Component guidance applicable during the corresponding surveillance years [10].

Device-associated infections included ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), catheter-associated urinary tract infection (CAUTI), and central line–associated bloodstream infection, defined according to NHSN criteria. Other HAIs included laboratory-confirmed bloodstream infections, pneumonia, urinary tract infections, and other clinically relevant infections fulfilling CDC/NHSN definitions.

Only clinically significant isolates associated with active infection episodes were included in the present analysis. Colonization isolates, surveillance cultures, screening cultures, and repeated isolation of the same micro-organism from the same infectious episode were excluded. Clinical relevance was determined using compatible clinical findings together with CDC/NHSN infection definitions.

In polymicrobial infections, each clinically relevant pathogen meeting CDC/NHSN infection criteria was included in microbiological analyses.

During the study period, routine surveillance screening for selected MDR organisms, including nasal methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) screening and rectal vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales screening, was performed according to institutional infection prevention policies. Surveillance cultures and screening samples were not included in the present analysis.

Surveillance findings were routinely reviewed by the institutional infection prevention and control team and were used to support local infection control measures and antimicrobial stewardship activities throughout the study period.

No microbiologically confirmed MDR organism outbreak requiring formal outbreak investigation, ward closure, or cohorting measures was documented during the study period.

Infection rates were expressed per 1000 patient-days. Device use ratios were calculated as the number of device-days divided by patient-days [11].

Microbiological procedures

Clinical specimens were processed according to standard laboratory protocols. Micro-organisms were identified using conventional phenotypic methods until December 2016 and by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry thereafter [12].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using disk diffusion methodology before 2016 and the VITEK 2 automated system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l'Étoile, France) thereafter. Colistin susceptibility testing was performed using the broth microdilution method.

Results were interpreted according to European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) clinical breakpoint standards applicable during the year of isolate recovery [13].

Molecular characterization of MDR isolates was not performed systematically and therefore was not included in the present analysis.

Definitions of antimicrobial resistance

AMR was evaluated at the isolate level based on EUCAST breakpoints. MDR was defined as acquired non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories, according to internationally accepted criteria [14].

For organism-specific analyses, resistance to clinically relevant antimicrobial classes, including carbapenems, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, and polymyxins, was evaluated when sufficient isolate numbers permitted stable statistical analysis [15].

Peri-operative prophylaxis

Peri-operative antimicrobial prophylaxis consisted of piperacillin/tazobactam (4.5 g intravenously every 6 h) administered for 48 h in accordance with institutional protocols and guideline-based recommendations [16]. This prophylaxis strategy remained unchanged throughout the entire study period, including the COVID-19 pandemic period. Postoperative prophylaxis approaches were similarly stable, with no protocol modifications during the study interval.

Aminoglycoside use

Aminoglycosides were not used routinely in this ICU setting [17]. Their administration was restricted to selected cases based on microbiological culture results and antimicrobial susceptibility testing, most commonly as part of combination therapy targeting MDR pathogens. Empirical aminoglycoside use was not part of standard treatment protocols.

Pandemic period classification

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages.

Temporal trends in pathogen distribution and resistance proportions were evaluated across calendar years and pandemic periods. Regression models incorporating calendar year as a continuous variable were used to assess longitudinal trends.

Isolation density comparisons across pandemic periods were performed using Poisson regression models with patient-days included as an offset variable. AMR analyses were conducted using logistic regression models. Results were reported as incidence rate ratios (IRRs), odds ratios (ORs), and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), where applicable.

A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Study cohort and surveillance volume

Between 2015 and 2024, 7717 adult LT recipients were followed in the LT ICU, corresponding to 48,001 patient-days. Microbiological aetiology and AMR analyses included only HAI episodes with at least one clinically significant isolate available for identification and susceptibility testing. Overall HAI incidence and device-associated infection trends from this surveillance cohort were analysed in a separate companion manuscript that was under independent peer review and had not yet been published at the time of submission of the present study. Therefore, these analyses are not presented in the current manuscript.

Overall microbial aetiology of HAIs

Across the study period, Gram-negative organisms predominated among HAI-associated isolates. No significant temporal change was observed in the proportion of Gram-negative isolates (P = 0.167), whereas Gram-positive isolates demonstrated a significant decreasing trend over time (P = 0.004). Fungal isolates showed a borderline downward trend that did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.055). In correlation analyses, the Gram-negative proportion was inversely associated with both Gram-positive and fungal proportions (P < 0.001).

The most frequently isolated pathogens were Acinetobacter spp. (119/380, 31.3%), Klebsiella spp. (98/380, 25.8%), and Pseudomonas spp. (57/380, 15.0%), followed by Escherichia coli (43/380, 11.3%) and Candida spp. (28/380, 7.4%) (Table I). Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive 10-year evaluation of microbial ecology and AMR trends among HAIs in a dedicated LT ICU. To our knowledge, this is one of the few longitudinal studies specifically examining pandemic-associated changes in pathogen distribution and resistance dynamics in a transplant-exclusive ICU population. While previous analyses from this surveillance cohort demonstrated substantial reductions in overall HAI incidence and device-associated infection rates, the present study reveals that favourable epidemiological trends do not necessarily translate into microbiological stability. Despite declining infection burden, significant shifts in pathogen-specific resistance patterns emerged during and after the COVID-19 pandemic period, highlighting the need for continuous microbiological surveillance beyond conventional incidence monitoring.

A second important finding is that microbial ecology and AMR trajectories appeared to evolve independently of overall infection incidence. This observation has direct clinical implications because empirical antimicrobial therapy in transplant recipients is primarily guided by local pathogen distribution and resistance profiles rather than infection rates alone. Therefore, understanding long-term microbiological trends may be as important as monitoring infection incidence when developing infection prevention and antimicrobial stewardship strategies in transplant ICUs.

Gram-negative organisms remained the predominant pathogens throughout the study period, accounting for the majority of clinically significant isolates. This finding is consistent with previous reports demonstrating the dominant role of MDR Gram-negative bacteria in LT recipients and other SOT populations [18,19]. The sustained predominance of Gram-negative organisms likely reflects the unique ecological characteristics of transplant ICUs, where prolonged hospitalization, repeated invasive procedures, extensive antimicrobial exposure, and profound immunosuppression create a favourable environment for Gram-negative colonization and infection.

In contrast, Gram-positive organisms demonstrated a significant decline over time, including reductions in Enterococcus spp. and MRSA isolates. Although some studies reported increased rates of resistant Gram-positive pathogens during the COVID-19 pandemic in general ICU settings, our findings suggest a different trajectory in transplant-specific critical care environments [9,20]. Stable peri-operative prophylaxis protocols, continuous infection prevention activities, and sustained surveillance efforts may have contributed to this decline.

At the species level, Acinetobacter spp. emerged as the leading pathogen throughout the study period. The predominance of Acinetobacter spp. in transplant ICUs has been reported previously and remains clinically important because of its remarkable ability to persist in healthcare environments and acquire resistance determinants [21]. Notably, Acinetobacter isolation density was significantly higher during the prepandemic period than during the postpandemic period, suggesting that improvements in infection prevention practices may have reduced transmission without completely altering the underlying ecological dominance of this organism.

Another noteworthy observation was the relatively low proportion of Candida spp. isolates and the absence of significant temporal changes in fungal epidemiology. Although invasive candidiasis remains a recognized complication after liver transplantation, Candida spp. accounted for only 7.4% of all clinically significant isolates in our cohort and demonstrated a non-significant downward trend over time. This finding may reflect effective antifungal prophylaxis strategies, improved peri-operative management, and enhanced infection prevention measures implemented during the study period. Importantly, all fungal analyses were restricted to clinically significant infection episodes, and surveillance or colonization cultures were excluded, reducing the likelihood of overestimating fungal burden.

Pandemic-period analyses revealed heterogeneous pathogen-specific responses rather than uniform ecological changes. Isolation densities of Acinetobacter spp. and E. coli were significantly higher in the prepandemic period, whereas no significant period-related differences were observed for several other major pathogens. Similar heterogeneity has been described in previous reports evaluating the impact of COVID-19 on HAIs and AMR [22,23]. Together, these findings suggest that pandemic-related healthcare disruptions may influence individual pathogens differently depending on local epidemiological and ecological factors.

Of greater concern was the increase in AMR among major Gram-negative pathogens during the pandemic and postpandemic periods. Acinetobacter spp. demonstrated significant increases in meropenem and amikacin resistance, whereas Klebsiella spp. exhibited higher resistance probabilities to aminoglycosides and fluoroquinolones. Likewise, Pseudomonas spp. showed increased ciprofloxacin resistance. These findings are consistent with global reports documenting the continued expansion of resistance among clinically important Gram-negative pathogens and emphasize the growing challenge of preserving effective treatment options in transplant populations [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31]].

Several mechanisms may explain the observed increase in resistance. Pandemic-associated increases in empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic use, disruptions in antimicrobial stewardship activities, altered healthcare workflows, and increased healthcare system pressure may all have contributed to resistance selection and transmission. However, antimicrobial consumption data were not available at the unit level for the entire study period. Therefore, direct associations between antibiotic use patterns and resistance trends could not be evaluated and should be interpreted cautiously.

Interestingly, resistance trends appeared more pronounced than changes in pathogen distribution itself. This finding suggests that the principal microbiological consequence of pandemic-associated healthcare disruption in our setting may have been the selection of resistant strains rather than major alterations in pathogen composition. The absence of significant seasonal variation in resistance patterns further supports the hypothesis that long-term selective pressures, rather than short-term fluctuations, drive resistance evolution in transplant ICU environments.

When interpreted together with our companion incidence-focused surveillance analysis, which was under independent peer review and had not yet been published at the time of submission, the current findings provide a more complete understanding of infection epidemiology within a transplant ICU. While the earlier study demonstrated reductions in overall HAI burden, the present analysis reveals ongoing evolution of pathogen ecology and resistance profiles. These complementary observations underscore the importance of integrating epidemiological surveillance with detailed microbiological monitoring when evaluating infection prevention performance.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, this was a single-centre study conducted in a high-volume LT ICU, which may limit generalizability to other transplant programs or mixed ICU populations. Second, although surveillance data were collected prospectively, the current analysis was retrospective and therefore subject to residual confounding. Third, analyses were performed primarily at the isolate level, and resistance estimates for less frequently isolated pathogens were limited by small sample sizes. Fourth, detailed longitudinal antimicrobial consumption data were unavailable, preventing direct evaluation of the relationship between antibiotic use and resistance evolution. Finally, molecular characterization of resistance mechanisms was not performed systematically; therefore, the genetic determinants underlying observed resistance trends could not be assessed.

In conclusion, in this dedicated LT ICU, microbial ecology remained dominated by Gram-negative pathogens throughout a decade of surveillance, whereas significant pandemic-associated shifts were observed in AMR patterns. Increases in resistance among Acinetobacter spp., Klebsiella spp., and Pseudomonas spp. indicate that important microbiological changes may occur even when overall infection incidence declines. These findings support the need for continuous pathogen-specific surveillance and locally tailored antimicrobial stewardship programs in transplant ICUs. Nevertheless, the absence of antimicrobial use data and molecular resistance characterization should be considered when interpreting the mechanisms underlying the observed resistance trends.
