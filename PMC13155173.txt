Molecular and Serological Evidence of Oropouche Virus Circulation in Asymptomatic Blood Donors During the 2023–2024 Outbreak in Manaus, Brazil
Authors: Pamela Milani, Graham Simmons, Allyson Guimarães da Costa, Daniel Chafets, Leilani Montalvo, Nelson Fraiji, Rachel Martinelli, Rosilene Marinho de Souza, Theomario Theotonio Azevedo da Cruz, Cecilia Salete Alencar, Renata Buccheri, Xutao Deng, Ester Cerdeira Sabino, Brian Custer, Michael P Busch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOropouche virus (OROV) re-emerged in Brazil in 2023, causing over 26 000 confirmed cases through December 2025. A reassortant lineage was linked to neurologic complications and congenital infections due to vertical transmission. Although transfusion transmission has not been reported, asymptomatic viremia in blood donors could represent an unrecognized route of infection.MethodsWe conducted nucleic acid testing (NAT), genomic, and serological surveillance among blood donors in Manaus, Brazil, during the 2023–2024 OROV outbreak. Minipools (MPs) of 18 donations from November 2023 to May 2024 were tested using a validated RT-qPCR assay. A subset of NAT-reactive pools underwent whole genome sequencing and phylogenetic analysis. Serosurveys in November 2023 and June 2024 assessed changes in population immunity. The RNA-detectable window was estimated by integrating NAT yield with seroincidence.ResultsAmong 661 MPs, representing 11 898 donations, 43 (6.5%) were OROV RNA-reactive, peaking in January 2024. About half contained viral RNA concentrations above the limit of quantification. Donor-derived viral genomes clustered with the reassortant lineage circulating in Brazil. Donor seropositivity increased from 13.0% to 29.7%, corresponding to approximately 390 000 estimated infections in Manaus, substantially higher than reported case numbers. The estimated MP RNA-detectable window was 5.4 days (95% CI: 3.8–7.0).ConclusionsOROV RNA detection in donor samples, together with high-titer viremia, phylogenetic identity to the outbreak strain, and postoutbreak seroconversion, raises questions about blood safety. Continued donor and recipient surveillance will be vital to determine transfusion-transmission risk and inform transfusion safety policies.

Oropouchevirus (OROV) is an arthropod-borne orthobunyavirus first identified in 1955 in Trinidad and Tobago after its isolation from both a febrile patient and mosquitoes [1]. The virus is primarily transmitted to humans by Culicoides paraensis midges [2], though Culex mosquitoes may also contribute to transmission [3]. While transfusion-transmitted OROV infections have not been documented, this route of transmission remains a possibility due to asymptomatic viremia during the acute phase. Since the 1950s, OROV has been recognized as a cause of acute febrile illness in South America and is considered endemic in the Amazon basin, where it circulates continuously [4]. Despite a sizable level of seropositivity seen in cross-sectional serosurveys [5], periodic large outbreaks occur; in 2024, nearly 6000 laboratory-confirmed cases were reported in the 7 states constituting the Brazilian Amazon region [6]. The 2023–2024 OROV outbreak in Brazil was widespread and extensive [7, 8], with the virus seen outside the typical endemic areas around the Amazon basin [9], and autochthonous transmission reported in 15 non-Amazonian states [6].

Clinically, OROV infection presents as an acute febrile illness with symptoms resembling dengue or other arboviral diseases with fever, headache, myalgia, and arthralgia common [10]. While the disease is usually self-limiting, the infection often follows a biphasic course, where symptoms recur about 1 week after onset [11]. The high attack rate during the recent outbreak has highlighted neurologic complications and vertical transmission that have raised public health concerns, with the first deaths associated with OROV also reported [12]. Indeed, case reports from Brazil have described fetal abnormalities such as microcephaly in pregnancies with confirmed or suspected OROV infection [13]. These clinical observations are supported by recent experimental evidence showing that OROV readily infects human trophoblasts and full-term placental explants [14], highlighting the virus's ability to breach the maternal–fetal interface.

As with other arboviruses, OROV infection is characterized by high viremia during the acute phase, often in the absence of overt symptoms, raising concern for potential blood transfusion transmission. Despite this risk, there are currently no routine nucleic acid testing (NAT) or serological screening protocols for OROV in blood donors, and little is known about the prevalence of viremia or recent exposure in healthy donor populations. To address this gap, we conducted a retrospective surveillance study using blood donation specimens collected in Amazonas state during the 2023–2024 arboviral season. NAT screening of minipooled donations was performed to detect acute infections, in conjunction with serological testing of cross-sectional samples collected at the beginning and end of the season, to assess recent exposure and seroconversion. Our findings revealed a substantial number of NAT-reactive donors during peak transmission, with viral genomes from donors belonging to the same reassortant lineage as the one detected in clinical cases, and an approximately 17% increase in seropositivity by season's end. We present data on the detection window of viremic donors and discuss the implications for blood safety during large-scale OROV outbreaks.

METHODS

Specimens and Consent

Specimens used for reverse-transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) testing included 661 minipools (18 plasma samples/pool, MP18), from November 2023 to May 2024. The use of MP18 was based on the existing structure of routine blood-safety screening in Brazil, where donations are processed in pools of 6. Because residual plasma was only available in these preformed units, 3 such pools were combined to ensure a sufficient volume for testing. Specimens used for serology included 500 individual donor sera from November 2023 (termed preoutbreak) and 500 sera from June and the first week of July 2024, as well as 1 specimen from May 2024 (termed postoutbreak), all collected by Fundação Hemoam, Manaus, Brazil.

Informed consent for donation was obtained from all donors in accordance with institutional and regulatory guidelines. Deidentified testing for OROV was conducted on residual donor samples with waiver of written informed consent (Brazilian National Ethics Committee approval 53543316.3.1001.0065, Fundação Hemoam Ethics Committee approval 53543316.3.2006.0009, and UCSF IRB approval 20-32390). Molecular, genomic, and serological analyses were performed at the Vitalant Research Institute in San Francisco, USA. The shipment of samples from Brazil was registered and authorized through the SISGEN (Sistema Nacional de Gestão do Patrimônio Genético e do Conhecimento Tradicional Associado) under registration number A533584.

Samples used to assess clinical specificity of the RT-qPCR assay were obtained from Creative Testing Solutions (Tempe, Arizona, USA). These samples were obtained under voluntary donor consent for the use of deidentified data and residual blood specimens for research purposes as part of the standard blood donation process approved by the Vitalant IRB of record. Therefore, no additional informed consent or specific regulatory approval was required.

An anonymized, RT-PCR–confirmed and IgM-positive serum sample from a traveler returning from Cuba in 2024, as well as the viral isolate OROV 240023 obtained from another traveler to Cuba in 2024, was provided by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The virus was expanded and endpoint-titrated in Vero E6 cells (ATCC CRL-1586).

Nucleic Acid Testing

RNA was extracted from plasma samples with a silica-membrane protocol (QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit, Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Detection of OROV RNA was performed using a one-step RT-qPCR assay targeting the S segment, adapted from the assay described by Naveca et al [15]. Quantitative standard-curve preparation and assay validation employed in vitro-transcribed (IVT) RNA templates generated from a synthetic OROV fragment. Each run incorporated a midrange IVT calibrator to verify extraction performance and correct for run-to-run variability. Detailed protocols for IVT preparation, RT-qPCR reaction setup, quality-control procedures, and analytical performance evaluation are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Genome Sequencing and Phylogenetic Analysis

Whole genome sequencing of OROV-positive samples was performed using a custom multiplexed tiled-amplicon approach. Primer sets were designed from representative global OROV genomes encompassing the S, M, and L segments to ensure coverage across circulating lineages. Amplicons were prepared for sequencing with the Illumina Nextera XT kit, followed by paired-end sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform.

Reads were aligned to reference genomes, and processed for primer trimming and consensus generation using iVar v1.3.1 [16]. Phylogenetic relationships were inferred by maximum-likelihood analysis of segment-specific consensus sequences together with representative global OROV isolates, with branch support evaluated by ultrafast bootstrapping.

Detailed protocols for primer design, multiplex PCR conditions, library preparation, sequence processing, and tree construction are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Luciferase Immunoprecipitation Assay

Serological testing used a luciferase immunoprecipitation system (LIPS) assay targeting codon-optimized nucleoprotein (N) and glycoprotein Gc, designed from GenBank sequences of OROV isolates from Amazonas in 2024 and expressed as fusion proteins with secreted nanoluciferase (secNLuc). Fusion proteins were expressed in 293T clone 17 cells, and clarified lysates were incubated with appropriately diluted serum. Immune complexes were captured on protein A/G beads in 96-well filter plates, washed, and luminescence was measured on a CLARIOstar (BMG LABTECH) plate reader. Results were expressed as signal-to-noise ratios, defined as the sample signal divided by the mean of negative controls. Full assay development, conditions, and controls are detailed in the Supplementary Material.

A subset of seroreactive samples was further evaluated for neutralizing antibody activity, as described in the Supplementary Material.

Estimation of Detection Periods

The duration of the MP18 NAT-detectable period in days was estimated using a prevalence–incidence framework previously described [17, 18]. The mean RNA detection window was derived from the ratio of NAT prevalence to seroincidence combined across months, with 95% confidence intervals (CI) obtained from the delta method.

RESULTS

Analytical Validation of OROV RT-qPCR Assay

For NAT, we adapted the RT-qPCR assay described by Naveca et al [15] and performed analytical validation to confirm its suitability for blood-donor MP screening, where viral loads in asymptomatic infections may be low. Analytical performance was established using IVT standards. Amplification efficiency using IVT diluted in water was 100.8%. Linearity was confirmed using IVT spiked into OROV-negative plasma lysate (R2 = 1.00) and serial dilutions of cultured OROV isolate 240023 (R2 = 0.98). Probit analysis based on 20 replicates per dilution established a 95% limit of detection (LOD95) of 167 copies/mL (95% CI 86.9–304) and a 50% LOD (LOD50) of 19.2 copies/mL (95% CI 6.3–33.9). The lower limit of quantification (LLOQ), defined by a 25% coefficient of variation threshold, was 1000 copies/mL. Clinical specificity testing of 44 healthy donor plasma samples from the United States confirmed 100% specificity (95% CI 92.0–100). Full validation data and standard-curve characteristics are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Presence of OROV RNA in Blood-Donor MPs During the 2024 Season

The study utilized samples covering the period from November 2023 through May 2024, when OROV transmission reached its peak during the 2023–2024 season in the Amazon region. A total of 661 MPs (each representing 18 individual donations, corresponding to 11 898 individual donors) were tested. These samples had previously been screened for dengue, chikungunya, and Zika virus using a Research Use Only triplex arbovirus assay (Grifols Diagnostic Solutions) [19]. All MPs tested negative for chikungunya and Zika virus, with only 2 testing positive for dengue virus; no coinfections with OROV were detected.

Of the 661 MPs tested, 43 (6.5%) were reactive for OROV RNA (Figure 1Aand Supplementary Table 3). The highest detection rates were observed in January 2024 (31 out of 80 MPs, 39%), coinciding with peak case reports in the Amazonas state. December 2023 detected 5 positive MPs (6%), while February through May 2024 showed lower but persistent detection rates ranging from 1% to 3%. No positive MPs were detected in November 2023. The temporal distribution of reactive MPs overlapped with the period of highest reported OROV activity in Manaus and in Amazonas State overall.

Because a reactive MP may contain 1 or more PCR-positive donations, the proportion of reactive MPs does not scale linearly with the proportion of PCR-positive donors. To facilitate donor-level interpretation, we inferred the estimated proportion of donors that were PCR-positive from MP18 reactivity using standard pooled-testing relationships, as described in the Supplementary Method. During the peak month of January 2024, MP18 reactivity corresponded to an MP18-inferred donor-level NAT positivity of 2.69% (95% CI, 1.81%–3.81%). These donor-level estimates, being MP18-inferred, may be subjected to potential underestimation due to dilution-related loss of analytical sensitivity inherent to MP. Month-by-month estimates are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Among the 43 reactive MPs, 22 had normalized concentration values above the LLOQ of 1000 copies/mL, enabling quantitative viral-load determination. The remaining 21 positive samples had detectable but nonquantifiable viral loads (Figure 1B).

OROV Genome Sequencing Confirms Reassortant Lineage Circulation in Blood-Donor MPs

We developed a PrimalSeq tiled-amplicon assay to generate near-complete OROV genomes from RNA-positive donor MPs with Cq ≤ 30 (n = 10) and assess whether viral genotypes in asymptomatic donors corresponded to the lineage driving the 2023–2024 outbreak. The assay achieved high sequencing efficiency across all samples, with >95% genome coverage at ≥10 × depth for the M and L segments and 84%–99% for the S segment (Supplementary Figure 5). Detailed assay validation and performance benchmarking against a metatranscriptomic workflow are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Phylogenetic analysis showed that sequences from the 10 OROV-positive donor MPs clustered consistently with contemporary clinical isolates from Amazonas, other Brazilian states, and Cuba (Figure 2). The M segment phylogeny placed donor-derived genomes within the M1 lineage described by Naveca et al [8], distinct from the M2 lineage circulating in Peru, Colombia, and Ecuador between 2008 and 2021. The L and S segment trees showed concordant topologies, with donor-derived sequences grouping with recent Brazilian strains but remaining clearly separated from historical isolates from Trinidad and Tobago (1955), the Brazilian prototype strain (1960), and earlier Brazilian sequences (2009–2018). These results confirm that asymptomatic donor infections were caused by the reassortant lineage responsible for the 2023–2024 OROV epidemic.

Increases in OROV Seropositivity Following the 2024 Season

500 blood-donor specimens collected in Amazonas prior to the 2024 outbreak and 501 specimens collected after the 2024 arbovirus season were tested for the presence of specific antibody against OROV nucleoprotein (N) or glycoprotein Gc using the LIPS. Results for each specimen were plotted for N versus Gc results (Figure 3) with excellent correlations between the 2 assays. A lack of confirmed positive specimens prevented empirical determination of cutoff values for the assays. Thus, potential cutoffs of 1.8 log10 signal/noise (S/N) for both antigens were selected based on 2 distinct antigenic populations, particularly in the postoutbreak specimens. The cutoffs were then evaluated by selecting borderline specimens for microneutralization testing (Figure 3). Only 1 dual negative specimen and 1 single-positive specimen using the 1.8 cutoffs were found to be neutralization positive. Furthermore, using these cutoffs, no positives were detected using a panel of 100 continental US blood-donor samples collected before 2020 (data not shown). Overall, in preoutbreak blood donations, we observed a baseline seropositivity of at least 13.0% (65 out of 500) based on dual-positive specimens for N and Gc seroreactivity, with an upper limit of 20% including single positives (30 out of 500 for N and 5 out of 500 for Gc). Following the 2024 epidemic, these results increased to 29.7% (149 out of 501) with an upper limit of 31.5% (single-positive specimens: 8 out of 501 for N and 1 out of 501 for Gc).

An age-stratified analysis of OROV seroreactivity preoutbreak revealed that adults aged 30–49 had higher odds of reactivity than 16–29-year-olds (OR 1.97; 95% CI, 1.03–3.92; P < .05) (Table 1). Seroreactivity increased substantially after the outbreak across all strata: among 16–29-year-olds it rose from 8.6% (95% CI, 5.0%–13.5%) to 23.5% (17.5%–30.4%), in 30–49-year-olds from 15.6% (11.3%–20.7%) to 31.5% (25.8%–37.6%), and in 50–69-year-olds from 15.9% (8.2%–26.7%) to 39.4% (28.0%–51.7%). By sex, rates were similar preoutbreak (females 13.1% vs males 13.0%; OR 0.99) and postoutbreak (females 29.3% vs males 29.9%; OR 1.03), with absolute increases of approximately 16%–17% points in both.

As previously performed for other arboviruses [17, 18], we estimated the length of the MP-NAT detectable period in blood donors by combining monthly incidence data with the seasonal increase in seropositivity, yielding a duration of 5.4 days (95% CI 3.8–7.0 days). We also applied the attack rate of approximately 17% during the 2023–2024 OROV outbreak to Manaus city's 2024 population (2 280 000) [20] to estimate that 390 000 infections occurred during this outbreak. This projection, derived from a blood-donor population, should not be interpreted as a direct population-wide estimate, but rather as an approximation of the outbreak's magnitude.

DISCUSSION

Blood transfusion represents a potential transmission route for OROV, particularly during large outbreaks when donations from asymptomatic viremic donors may enter the blood supply. Transfusion transmission has been documented for other arboviruses [21, 22]. However, no confirmed cases of transfusion-transmitted OROV have been reported to date. This may reflect surveillance limitations and the difficulty of distinguishing transfusion-acquired from vector-acquired infections during intense community spread, rather than true absence of transmission.

We conducted what is, to our knowledge, the first combined NAT and serological surveillance of OROV in blood donors. This surveillance was implemented during the 2023–2024 outbreak season in Amazonas, Brazil, a state experiencing peak transmission [6].

We performed extensive validation of the RT-qPCR assay to confirm its suitability for testing MPs of 18 donations (MP18). We detected OROV RNA in 6.5% of MPs during the study period. The temporal pattern of donor reactivity broadly aligned with clinical case reports in Amazonas State, peaking in January 2024. However, donor MP-NAT positivity declined sharply in February while clinical case numbers of Amazonas state decreased more gradually. When donor MP reactivity was instead compared with confirmed clinical cases reported specifically for Manaus, rather than with aggregated state-wide data, the temporal trends appeared more closely aligned, indicating that part of the discrepancy reflected geographic aggregation and heterogeneity in transmission intensity. Nonetheless, even when restricted to Manaus-only confirmed cases, the decline in MP reactivity remained steeper than the decrease observed in clinical case counts. This is likely due to biological and surveillance factors, including the detection of acute and presymptomatic infections by NAT, reporting delays inherent to clinical surveillance, and dilution-related sensitivity limitations of MP testing as incidence declines.

Nevertheless, donor NAT remained reactive through May 2024, demonstrating sustained viral transmission. The overall temporal concordance between donor viremia and clinical case peaks supports the utility of donor surveillance as a real-time sentinel indicator of community transmission intensity.

The reassortant OROV lineage responsible for this outbreak is associated with neurologic complications, vertical transmission, and the first reported OROV-related deaths. Phylogenetic analysis showed that donor-derived viruses were genetically indistinguishable from those linked to severe disease, indicating that asymptomatic infections were caused by the same epidemic strain and thus shared its pathogenic potential. Furthermore, viral-load analysis revealed that some donors had viremia levels comparable to symptomatic patients, exceeding 108 copies/mL [23].

Serology from blood donors indicates an attack rate of approximately 17% during the 2023–2024 OROV outbreak in Manaus. Applying this rate to the city's 2024 population (2 280 000) [20] yields an estimate on the order of ∼390 000 infections. Because blood donors constitute a selected and generally healthier subset of the community [24], this projection should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive for the general population; nevertheless, the large disparity relative to the approximately 3200 reported clinical cases [6] suggests substantial under-ascertainment and that most infections were mild, subclinical, or misclassified as other arboviral diseases. We also observed a small proportion of single-antigen seroreactivity, particularly for the N protein. Biologically, N-only reactivity could represent previous infection with strains harboring antigenically diverse Gc proteins, potentially due to reassortment of the M segment. Pre-existing OROV immunity was higher among donors ≥30 years than among 16–29-year-olds, consistent with exposure during prior epidemics and low endemic transmission between outbreaks. Notably, Manaus experienced a large 1980/81 outbreak with a 12.5% seroincidence [25] and smaller episodes such as in 2007/08 [26].

By integrating NAT yield with seroincidence using methods previously established for dengue [18] and chikungunya [17] in blood-donor populations, we estimated a 5.4-day MP18 RNA-detectable window in asymptomatic donors. This duration is comparable to that observed for chikungunya by MP-NAT (5.1 days) [17], suggesting similar viral dynamics in infected donors among these arboviruses.

While this investigation has several strengths, including a validated RT-qPCR assay with robust analytical performance, integration of NAT surveillance with preoutbreak and postoutbreak serosurveys, and a large sample size spanning the 2023–2024 season, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the full spectrum of viremia and duration of asymptomatic infection cannot be established without individual donor-NAT (ID-NAT). Second, the study was conducted in an endemic region with substantial baseline immunity, which may preclude reinfections and limit generalizability to immunologically naive populations. Third, because this was a retrospective surveillance study conducted on deidentified specimens, we lacked the regulatory approval and individual-donation samples required to deconstruct the NAT-positive MPs and identify specific infected donors, which precluded lookback investigations to monitor recipient outcomes [27]. Such investigations, as well as traceback studies, are further complicated in settings with high background levels of autochthonous transmission, making it difficult to distinguish transfusion-acquired from vector-acquired infections. Finally, virus isolation and infectivity assays were not performed to directly correlate RNA detection with infectious titers. Importantly, a distinction must be made between laboratory evidence of infection and clinical penetrance. As observed with other arboviruses like dengue, even when transfusion transmission is documented, molecular evidence of infection does not necessarily translate into symptomatic disease [27]. Therefore, the clinical impact of potential transfusion-transmitted OROV infections, should they occur, also remains to be determined. Some of these limitations will be addressed as additional studies tracking the spread of OROV are conducted. Similar studies are planned to test donation samples from the Rio de Janeiro state in late 2024 and 2025, where the epidemic subsequently spread, employing both MP-NAT and ID-NAT and preoutbreak and postoutbreak serosurveys. These data will provide a complementary perspective to the findings from Amazonas, enabling quantification of the proportion of viremic donations missed by pooling, characterization of the full distribution of viral loads in asymptomatic donors, and assessment of serological dynamics in a more immunologically naive donor population.

To investigate transfusion-transmission risk, several approaches could be employed [28, 29]. The most definitive design—a prospective donor–recipient cohort with linked samples—would allow confirmation of transfusion transmission and define infectious viral loads, but is logistically and ethically challenging [18, 27, 30]. Epidemiologically, one could monitor cohorts of chronically transfused individuals, such as persons with sickle-cell disease, by comparing preoutbreak and postoutbreak seroprevalence and assessing whether seroincidence exceeds that of the general donor population. Experimentally, infectivity studies using animal models could help establish biological plausibility and identify viral-load thresholds for transmission.

In conclusion, our findings highlight more widespread OROV infection than previously reported, potential risks of transfusion transmission during outbreaks, and support the need for adaptive blood-safety measures, including donor deferral and NAT screening, whether MP- or ID-NAT, according to epidemic intensity and resource availability. Continued genomic and epidemiologic surveillance across endemic and emerging regions will be essential to quantify and mitigate OROV transfusion risk as the virus keeps expanding its range beyond the Amazon basin.
