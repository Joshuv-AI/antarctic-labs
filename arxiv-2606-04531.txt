Gaussian-Process Dynamics of Diagonal Expectation Propagation under Variance-Profile Gaussian Measurements
Fangqing Xiao, Dirk T. M. Slock

Abstract
State-evolution analyses of approximate-message-passing and expectation-propagation-type algorithms rely on an effective-channel principle: after a suitable Onsager, orthogonal, or extrinsic correction, the nonlinear module receives a fresh scalar Gaussian observation. This paper studies this principle for diagonal expectation propagation under variance-profile Gaussian sensing matrices. The model preserves Gaussian conditioning, but removes the isotropy that supports the usual scalar decoupling arguments. We prove a finite-time large-system description in which the linear EP module remains Gaussian at the coordinate level, but is generally not a fresh scalar channel. Instead, the residuals form a coordinate-dependent Gaussian process whose covariance is shaped by the variance profile and by the finite linear history of the algorithm. The standard diagonal EP cavity cancels the instantaneous response of the incoming message, but may leave a component predictable from past residuals. We characterize this process through a conditioned matrix-Dyson-equation deterministic equivalent and a Schur-complement representation of the linear module. A Gaussian-regression decomposition then separates the predictable memory from the orthogonal innovation and yields an oracle state-evolution-level correction. Thus, under variance-profile measurements, the limiting object for diagonal EP is a Gaussian-process dynamics with profile-dependent memory rather than the conventional fresh-noise scalar state evolution.



I
Introduction

I-A

Motivation

High-dimensional linear inference is a basic model behind compressed sensing, multiuser detection, Bayesian linear estimation, low-rank estimation, coding, and signal recovery with discrete or structured priors
[
6
,
38
,
24
,
32
]
. In its simplest form, one observes

\bm{y}=\bm{A}\bm{x}+\bm{v}
, where
\bm{A}
is a known sensing matrix,
\bm{x}
is the unknown signal, and
\bm{v}
is noise. Classical compressed sensing emphasizes sparse signals, and early message-passing algorithms were often motivated by this setting. The large-system inference problem itself is broader: the prior on
\bm{x}
may be any separable distribution for which scalar posterior inference is well defined. Sparse priors, such as Bernoulli–Gaussian priors, are then special cases rather than the defining assumption.

A major reason for the success of message-passing methods is that they can turn a high-dimensional inference problem into a sequence of effective scalar estimation problems. This idea appears in statistical physics through Thouless–Anderson–Palmer-type equations and in belief-propagation-type algorithms for dense linear systems
[
33
,
23
,
30
]
. In the compressed-sensing literature, approximate message passing (AMP) made this principle explicit: with an appropriate correction term, the input to the nonlinear denoiser behaves, in the large-system limit, like a scalar Gaussian observation. The resulting scalar recursion is known as state evolution (SE)
[
13
,
8
,
17
]
.

The scalar Gaussian interpretation is not automatic. Since the same sensing matrix is reused at every iteration, the current iterate and the matrix are statistically dependent. For independent Gaussian matrices, the Onsager correction cancels the leading predictable dependence, and the SE can be made rigorous by conditioning on the past iterates
[
8
]
. When the matrix departs from this homogeneous Gaussian setting, the same scalar SE need not remain valid, and the standard AMP recursion may require additional modifications. This observation motivated a wide class of extensions, including generalized AMP, expectation propagation (EP), expectation consistent inference, vector AMP (VAMP), orthogonal AMP (OAMP), and turbo-type message-passing methods
[
37
,
31
,
34
,
36
,
28
]
.

Although these algorithms are derived from different principles, many of them enforce a common decoupling mechanism. The linear and nonlinear modules should exchange information that is asymptotically extrinsic, orthogonal, or cavity-like relative to the information already used. In independent Gaussian, right-orthogonally invariant, unitarily invariant, or rotationally invariant models, such mechanisms can lead again to scalar or finite-dimensional Gaussian descriptions
[
36
,
40
,
16
,
43
,
45
]
. Diagonal EP is a natural framework in which to examine this mechanism because its cavity construction explicitly removes the instantaneous contribution of the incoming message before forming the effective observation sent to the prior module.

Variance-profile Gaussian measurements form a different regime. The entries of the sensing matrix are still Gaussian, but their variances are prescribed by a deterministic profile. Thus Gaussian conditioning remains available, while rotational invariance and coordinate homogeneity are lost. Conditioning on past algorithmic information no longer leaves an isotropic residual matrix; it leaves a Gaussian field whose covariance depends on the variance profile and on the revealed history. Consequently, the usual implication from an extrinsic correction to a fresh scalar Gaussian observation is no longer evident.

This leads to the basic question considered in this paper. For diagonal EP under variance-profile Gaussian measurements, does the standard cavity construction still produce the fresh Gaussian information required by an ordinary scalar SE description? Or does the variance profile force one to track a richer object than one or two scalar effective variances? The issue is not merely whether a Gaussian approximation can be written at one iteration, but whether the Gaussian information delivered to the prior module is genuinely fresh relative to the information delivered in previous iterations.

I-B

Proof Strategy and Main Technical Difficulty

The question raised above cannot be answered by looking only at the marginal distribution of a single linear-module output. At iteration
t
, the effective observation passed to the prior module is generated by the same sensing matrix that has already produced all previous messages. It is therefore statistically coupled with the past algorithmic history. The central issue is not whether one can postulate a Gaussian approximation at one step, but whether the current effective observation contains a Gaussian innovation that is fresh relative to the information already used.

This is the same type of dependence that underlies rigorous analyses of AMP and EP-type algorithms. For independent Gaussian matrices, the conditioning method represents the past dependence through finitely many linear observations of the sensing matrix, after which the Onsager correction leaves a fresh Gaussian component
[
8
]
. For unitarily invariant measurements, a related conditioning program characterizes the residual Haar randomness after partial information has been revealed
[
40
]
. More recent analyses for rotationally invariant models use spectral invariance, free-cumulant Onsager corrections, or reductions to orthogonal and long-memory message passing to obtain state evolutions
[
16
,
45
,
43
,
27
,
14
]
. These works show that, under suitable invariance structures, the algorithmic correction can restore an effective fresh Gaussian channel.

The variance-profile Gaussian setting requires a different route. Since the entries of the sensing matrix are Gaussian, conditioning on finitely many linear observations still leaves a Gaussian matrix. However, the conditional covariance is no longer isotropic. It is deformed by the variance profile and by the history revealed by the algorithm. Consequently, the remaining randomness is Gaussian, but it is not automatically exchangeable across coordinates, nor is it automatically fresh across iterations. This is the main technical distinction from the independent Gaussian and unitarily invariant settings.

Our proof therefore does not attempt to derive a scalar SE directly. It first identifies the Gaussian object produced by the linear module. To keep the conditioning step exact, we formulate a predictable-precision version of diagonal EP in which the conditioning field contains only the finite linear history of the sensing matrix. Nonlinear finite-dimensional resolvent quantities, such as the actual diagonal posterior variances and adaptive precisions generated by the current random matrix, are not included in this conditioning field. They are connected back to the adaptive recursion later through a separate perturbation argument.

After conditioning on the linear history, the linear module contains the resolvent of a correlated Gaussian matrix with a bounded deterministic deformation. We control this resolvent through a regularized matrix-Dyson-equation deterministic equivalent for correlated random matrices
[
2
,
15
]
. This external random-matrix input is used only at the level needed for the EP-conditioned block linearization: the proof verifies the required bounded-deformation, flat-covariance, and positive-loading hypotheses, but does not reprove the underlying matrix-Dyson-equation local law.

The next step is a coordinate-wise Schur-complement expansion of the linear module. This expansion separates the instantaneous response to the incoming message from the residual fluctuation left by the conditioned matrix. The instantaneous response is the component that the diagonal EP cavity is designed to remove. The remaining fluctuation is Gaussian, because it is a finite-dimensional projection of the conditioned Gaussian matrix, but its covariance is inherited from the variance profile and from the past linear history. Thus, the linear module naturally produces a coordinate-dependent Gaussian process rather than a fresh scalar Gaussian innovation.

Once this Gaussian process has been identified, the role of the EP cavity can be examined precisely. The standard diagonal cavity removes the instantaneous contribution of the incoming message. What remains, however, may still contain the component of the current Gaussian residual that is predictable from previous residuals. The final step is therefore a finite-dimensional Gaussian regression: the current residual is decomposed into its conditional mean given the past residual history and an orthogonal Gaussian innovation, using standard Gaussian conditioning identities
[
3
]
. This regression is the mechanism by which the fresh part of the effective observation is identified.

The memory correction appearing in this decomposition is an oracle state-evolution-level construction: it characterizes the innovation part of the limiting Gaussian process, and is not proposed as a finite-sample algorithm. Auxiliary concentration and interpolation tools used later in the proof follow standard high-dimensional Gaussian arguments
[
11
]
. Designing practical estimators of the memory coefficients, or low-complexity approximations of the oracle correction, is left outside the present scope.

I-C

Related Work

Classical rigorous SE theory for AMP concerns independent Gaussian or universality classes close to it. The original compressed-sensing AMP and its rigorous dense-graph analysis are now standard references
[
13
,
8
]
. Subsequent work extended this program to more general AMP recursions, spatial coupling, independent but non-identically distributed Gaussian matrices, universality for symmetric matrices, and non-separable nonlinearities
[
22
,
7
,
9
,
17
]
. These results establish scalar or low-dimensional SEs for AMP-type recursions with carefully designed Onsager terms. The present paper studies a different object: the resolvent-based linear module and cavity operation of diagonal EP under a deterministic variance profile.

Closest to the present matrix model are recent AMP results for variance-profile matrices. Sparse symmetric variance profiles were analyzed in
[
21
]
, elliptic non-symmetric matrices were treated in
[
18
]
, and a general non-symmetric model with variance and correlation profiles was developed in
[
19
]
. A leave-one-out approach to non-asymptotic AMP with Gaussian variance profiles was proposed in
[
5
]
. Related inhomogeneous-noise models also appear in low-rank matrix estimation with block or entrywise heterogeneity
[
20
,
35
]
. These works show that variance profiles naturally lead to coordinate-dependent Gaussian descriptions, rather than a single scalar variance. Their focus, however, is the construction of AMP Onsager terms and the corresponding density-evolution equations. Here the issue is different: diagonal EP already prescribes an extrinsic cavity, and the question is whether that cavity leaves a fresh Gaussian channel once the LMMSE/resolvent linear module is driven by a variance-profile Gaussian matrix.

A second line of work studies message passing beyond independent Gaussian matrices by exploiting orthogonal, unitary, or rotational invariance. OAMP and VAMP provide state-evolution descriptions for right-orthogonally invariant or related matrix ensembles
[
28
,
36
]
. Rigorous EP dynamics for unitarily invariant measurements were established in
[
40
]
. Long-memory and spatially coupled OAMP/VAMP-type constructions further clarify convergence and optimality mechanisms under right-orthogonally invariant models
[
41
,
42
]
. These works are close to diagonal EP in algorithmic structure, but they rely on invariance of singular vectors or Haar-type conditioning. A deterministic variance profile breaks this structure at the entrywise level.

Rotationally invariant AMP theory has recently become substantially more developed. Free-cumulant Onsager corrections and spectral-invariance arguments yield AMP dynamics for rotationally invariant matrices
[
16
]
. Orthogonally invariant ensembles with multivariate nonlinearities and spectral initialization are treated in
[
45
]
. Rotationally invariant generalized linear models are analyzed through RI-GAMP-type constructions
[
43
]
, and a unified construction of AMP algorithms for rotationally invariant models is obtained by reducing general iterative templates to long-memory OAMP
[
27
]
. Spiked matrix models with rotationally invariant noise have also been analyzed through OAMP-type dynamics and optimized denoisers
[
14
]
. These works reinforce the importance of matrix structure in determining the correct Onsager or extrinsic correction, but their invariance assumptions are fundamentally different from an entrywise deterministic variance profile.

Model mismatch, replica predictions, and the relation between algorithmic dynamics and statistical-mechanics free energies form another relevant direction. VAMP in mismatched generalized linear models with rotation-invariant matrices has been analyzed through macroscopic SE, replica-symmetric free energy, and de Almeida–Thouless instability calculations
[
39
]
. The effect of sensing-matrix spectra on EP-type recovery for generalized linear inverse problems has also been studied in
[
29
]
. These works are complementary to ours: they emphasize rotationally invariant spectra, replica or macroscopic performance predictions, and fixed-point stability, whereas the present paper studies the finite-time conditional law of the diagonal EP linear module under an entrywise variance profile.

Memory and universality results provide further context. Memory AMP and sufficient-statistic memory AMP use previous messages as algorithmic variables to improve convergence or enforce state-evolution consistency for non-i.i.d. or right-unitarily invariant systems
[
25
,
26
]
. The word “memory” has a different meaning here. We do not introduce a long-memory algorithm; memory is the predictable component of the Gaussian residual process left by the standard diagonal EP cavity. Universality results show that certain AMP state evolutions extend beyond the Gaussian or rotationally invariant ensembles from which they were derived, including generalized Wigner and white-noise ensembles with heterogeneous entrywise variances
[
44
]
. Non-asymptotic concentration results are also available for generalized AMP/VAMP-type algorithms with right rotationally invariant designs
[
10
]
. These results ask whether a known AMP/VAMP recursion and its SE persist over broader ensembles or at finite sample sizes. Our question is instead whether the standard diagonal EP cavity itself produces fresh Gaussian information under a variance-profile Gaussian ensemble.

Finally, the proof uses tools from random matrix theory for correlated matrices. Matrix-Dyson-equation methods provide deterministic equivalents and stability theory for random matrices with non-identically distributed or correlated entries
[
4
,
12
,
1
,
2
,
15
]
. We use this framework as an external random-matrix input to control the resolvent of the EP-conditioned variance-profile matrix. The novelty is not a new local law, but the way the conditioned matrix-Dyson-equation response enters the EP dynamics: it determines both the coordinate-dependent linear response and the covariance kernel of the Gaussian residual process. This is the mechanism through which variance profiles replace the scalar fresh-noise picture by a profile-dependent Gaussian memory process.

I-D

Contributions

In contrast to existing scalar state evolutions for independent Gaussian, unitarily invariant, or rotationally invariant models, the results below show that a variance profile changes the nature of the effective Gaussian channel itself.

This paper develops a finite-time large-system analysis of diagonal EP under variance-profile Gaussian measurements. The contribution is threefold. First, we identify the conditional Gaussian object generated by the EP linear module. Conditioning only on the finite linear history of the sensing matrix, we derive a coordinate-wise Schur representation in which the linear-module output is decomposed into an instantaneous response and a residual Gaussian process. The covariance of this process is described by a conditioned matrix-Dyson-equation response and therefore depends on the variance profile and on the revealed history.

Second, we show that this Gaussian description does not in general reduce to the fresh scalar Gaussian channels that underlie the usual SE picture for independent Gaussian or rotationally invariant models. The standard diagonal EP cavity removes the instantaneous response of the incoming message, but it need not remove the part of the current residual that is predictable from previous residuals. Thus, under a general variance profile, the effective state is not characterized only by one or two scalar variances; it is governed by coordinate-dependent response vectors and a profile-dependent covariance kernel.

Third, we characterize the innovation component of this Gaussian process. By applying finite-dimensional Gaussian regression, we decompose the Schur residual into a predictable memory term and a fresh Gaussian innovation. This yields an oracle state-evolution-level correction that exposes the fresh channel hidden inside the residual process. The correction is used as a theoretical decoupling device rather than as a finite-sample algorithm. A separate perturbation argument connects the predictable-precision dynamics used in the conditioning proof to the adaptive precision recursion of diagonal EP under finite-time regularity conditions.

II
Preliminaries

The purpose of this section is to collect the probabilistic and random-matrix
tools used throughout the proof. The role of this section is analogous to the
preliminary section in rigorous EP analyses for unitarily invariant
measurements: before introducing the algorithmic recursion, we state the
definitions and limit results that will be repeatedly invoked later. In the
present variance-profile Gaussian setting, the Haar-matrix tools are replaced by
Gaussian conditioning under finite linear observations, a weakly dependent
Gaussian empirical law, and a regularized correlated-Gaussian matrix Dyson
equation (MDE) input.

II-B

Definitions

We first define the random matrix class studied in this paper.

Definition 1

(Variance-profile Gaussian matrix)
.

For each pair
(M,N)
, let

\bm{S}=\bm{S}_{M,N}=(s_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq M,\,1\leq j\leq N}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{M\times N}

be a deterministic variance profile. A random matrix

\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
is called a

variance-profile Gaussian matrix
with profile
\bm{S}
if

A_{ij}=\sqrt{\frac{s_{ij}}{M}}\,Z_{ij},\qquad Z_{ij}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

The profile is called
uniformly elliptic
if there exist constants

0<s_{\min}\leq s_{\max}<\infty
, independent of
M
and
N
, such that

s_{\min}\leq s_{ij}\leq s_{\max}

for all
i,j,M,N
. Throughout the paper, the proportional asymptotic regime is
assumed:

\frac{M}{N}\to\delta\in(0,\infty).

The normalization
1/M
in Definition
1
keeps the column norms of

\bm{A}
of constant order. If

, the model reduces to the
standard i.i.d. Gaussian sensing matrix. In general, no separability, low-rank

memory defect
is the predictable Gaussian-regression term

\mathfrak{m}_{t,j}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}\mid\mathcal{Z}_{<t,j},\mathcal{P}_{t}].

Thus the current residual is fresh if and only if its memory defect vanishes.
When the EP cavity is rescaled by a deterministic factor, the corresponding
memory defect is rescaled by the same factor; this is the origin of

\mu_{t,j}
in Section
IV-A
.

Results

We now state the probabilistic and random-matrix results used later. Standard Gaussian conditioning and regression identities are recalled in the exact form used by the proof; see, for example,
[
3
, Ch. 2]
.

Proposition 1

(Polynomial growth bound)
.

For any
k\geq 1
, there exists a constant
C_{k}>0
such that

(a+b)^{k}\leq C_{k}(a^{k}+b^{k}),\qquad a,b\geq 0.

Consequently, if
\psi\in\mathrm{PL}(k)
, then there exists a constant
C_{\psi}>0
such
that

|\psi(\bm{x})|\leq C_{\psi}(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{k})

for all
\bm{x}
.

Proof:

For
k\geq 1
, the convexity of
x\mapsto x^{k}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
gives

\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)^{k}\leq\frac{a^{k}+b^{k}}{2}.

Hence

(a+b)^{k}\leq 2^{k-1}(a^{k}+b^{k}).

Thus the first claim holds with
C_{k}=2^{k-1}
.

For the second claim, apply Definition
3
with
\bm{y}=\bm{0}
:

|\psi(\bm{x})|\leq|\psi(\bm{0})|+L\|\bm{x}\|\left(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{k-1}\right).

Since
\|\bm{x}\|\leq 1+\|\bm{x}\|^{k}
for all
k\geq 1
, there is a constant

C_{\psi}>0
such that

|\psi(\bm{x})|\leq C_{\psi}(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{k}).

∎

Lemma 1

(Gaussian conditioning under finite linear observations)
.

Let

\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}

be a variance-profile Gaussian matrix. Define

\bm{A}_{\mathrm{vec}}:=\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A})\in\mathbb{R}^{MN}.

Then

\bm{A}_{\mathrm{vec}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{A}),

where

\bm{\Sigma}_{A}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{s_{ij}}{M}\right)_{i,j}.

Let
\bm{L}
be any deterministic matrix with finitely many rows, and
consider the finite linear observation

\bm{L}\bm{A}_{\mathrm{vec}}=\bm{B}.

Then the conditional distribution of
\bm{A}_{\mathrm{vec}}
given

\bm{L}\bm{A}_{\mathrm{vec}}=\bm{B}
is Gaussian and can be written as

\bm{A}_{\mathrm{vec}}\mid\bm{L}\bm{A}_{\mathrm{vec}}=\bm{B}=\bm{A}_{\parallel,\mathrm{vec}}+\bm{A}_{\perp,\mathrm{vec}},

where

\bm{A}_{\parallel,\mathrm{vec}}=\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{L}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger}\bm{B},

and

\bm{A}_{\perp,\mathrm{vec}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{A|L})

with

\bm{\Sigma}_{A|L}=\bm{\Sigma}_{A}-\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{L}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger}\bm{L}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}.

Moreover,

\bm{0}\preceq\bm{\Sigma}_{A|L}\preceq\bm{\Sigma}_{A}.

Proof:

Let

\bm{Y}=\bm{L}\bm{A}_{\mathrm{vec}}.

Since
\bm{A}_{\mathrm{vec}}
is Gaussian, the pair

(\bm{A}_{\mathrm{vec}},\bm{Y})
is jointly Gaussian. Its covariance
matrices are

\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{A}_{\mathrm{vec}},\bm{Y})=\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}^{\mathsf{T}},

and

\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{Y})=\bm{L}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}^{\mathsf{T}}.

The standard Gaussian regression formula for a jointly Gaussian vector

[
3
]
, with the Moore–Penrose inverse used to allow possible
rank deficiency, gives the conditional mean

\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}_{\mathrm{vec}}\mid\bm{Y}=\bm{B}]=\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{L}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger}\bm{B}.

The conditional covariance is

\bm{\Sigma}_{A}-\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{L}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger}\bm{L}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}.

This proves the conditional Gaussian representation.

It remains to show the covariance contraction. Let

\bm{C}=\bm{L}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}^{1/2}.

Then

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{A|L}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{A}^{1/2}\left[\bm{I}-\bm{C}^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{C}\bm{C}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger}\bm{C}\right]\bm{\Sigma}_{A}^{1/2}.

The matrix

\bm{P}_{\bm{C}}:=\bm{C}^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{C}\bm{C}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger}\bm{C}

is the orthogonal projection onto the row space of
\bm{C}
. Hence

\bm{0}\preceq\bm{P}_{\bm{C}}\preceq\bm{I}
, and consequently

\bm{0}\preceq\bm{I}-\bm{P}_{\bm{C}}\preceq\bm{I}.

Therefore

\bm{0}\preceq\bm{\Sigma}_{A|L}\preceq\bm{\Sigma}_{A}.

∎

Corollary 1

(Flat covariance of the conditioned residual)
.

Let

\bm{A}\mid\bm{L}\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A})=\bm{B}=\bm{A}_{\parallel}+\bm{A}_{\perp}

be the matrix form of Lemma
1
. Then, for any
deterministic vectors
\bm{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and

\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\bm{p}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}_{\perp}\bm{q}\mid\bm{L}\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A})=\bm{B}\right)\leq\frac{s_{\max}}{M}\|\bm{p}\|^{2}\|\bm{q}\|^{2}.

Proof:

Let

\bm{H}:=\operatorname{vec}(\bm{P}\bm{Q}^{\mathsf{T}})\in\mathbb{R}^{MN}.

Then

\bm{P}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}_{\perp}\bm{Q}=\bm{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A}_{\perp}).

By Lemma
1
,

\bm{\Sigma}_{A|L}\preceq\bm{\Sigma}_{A}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\bm{p}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}_{\perp}\bm{q}\mid\bm{L}\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A})=\bm{B}\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}_{A|L}\bm{H}

\displaystyle\leq\bm{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{H}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{s_{ij}}{M}P_{i}^{2}Q_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{s_{\max}}{M}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}P_{i}^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}Q_{j}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{s_{\max}}{M}\|\bm{p}\|^{2}\|\bm{q}\|^{2}.

∎

The next result is a weak law for empirical averages of weakly dependent
Gaussian arrays. It will be invoked after an empirical Gaussian-law argument has shown that
the average off-diagonal covariance is negligible.

Theorem 1

(Weakly dependent Gaussian empirical law)
.

Let

\{\bm{\xi}_{j,N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:1\leq j\leq N\}

be a triangular array of jointly Gaussian random vectors. Suppose that, for
some
\epsilon>0
,

\sup_{j,N}\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\xi}_{j,N}\|^{4+\epsilon}<\infty,

and that the average off-diagonal covariance vanishes:

\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j\neq k}\left\|\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{\xi}_{j,N},\bm{\xi}_{k,N})\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\longrightarrow 0.

Then, for every
\psi\in\mathrm{PL}(2)
,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})]\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof:

We first prove the claim for bounded continuously differentiable functions

\psi
with bounded gradient. The bounded Lipschitz case follows by standard
mollification, and the pseudo-Lipschitz case is treated at the end by
truncation.

Let

Y_{j,N}:=\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})-\mathbb{E}[\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})].

It suffices to show

\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})\right)\to 0.

We have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\operatorname{Var}(\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N}))

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j\neq k}\operatorname{Cov}(\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N}),\psi(\bm{\xi}_{k,N})).

Since
\psi
is bounded, the diagonal term is
O(N^{-1})
.

We now control the off-diagonal term. For jointly Gaussian vectors

(\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta})
, the Gaussian interpolation identity
(the “smart path” formula; see, e.g.,
[
11
]
) gives

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(\psi(\bm{\xi}),\psi(\bm{\eta}))=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla\psi(\bm{\xi}_{s})^{\mathsf{T}}\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta})\nabla\psi(\bm{\eta}_{s})\right]ds,

where
(\bm{\xi}_{s},\bm{\eta}_{s})
is the usual Gaussian interpolation
between independent and fully coupled copies. Therefore, if

\|\nabla\psi\|_{\infty}\leq L
, then

\left|\operatorname{Cov}(\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N}),\psi(\bm{\xi}_{k,N}))\right|\leq L^{2}\left\|\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{\xi}_{j,N},\bm{\xi}_{k,N})\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Consequently,

\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j\neq k}\left|\operatorname{Cov}(\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N}),\psi(\bm{\xi}_{k,N}))\right|\to 0.

Thus the variance of the empirical average converges to zero, and Chebyshev’s
inequality yields the desired convergence in probability for bounded
continuously differentiable
\psi
with bounded gradient. A bounded Lipschitz
function can be approximated uniformly by such functions; hence the conclusion
also holds for bounded Lipschitz
\psi
.

It remains to extend the result to
\psi\in\mathrm{PL}(2)
. Let
\chi_{K}
be a smooth
cutoff satisfying
0\leq\chi_{K}\leq 1
,
\chi_{K}(\bm{x})=1
for

\|\bm{x}\|\leq K
, and
\chi_{K}(\bm{x})=0
for
\|\bm{x}\|\geq 2K
.
Define

\psi_{K}(\bm{x})=\psi(\bm{x})\chi_{K}(\bm{x}).

Then
\psi_{K}
is bounded Lipschitz for each fixed
K
, so the result already
proved gives

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{K}(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[\psi_{K}(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})]\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

By Proposition
1
, since
\psi\in\mathrm{PL}(2)
,

|\psi(\bm{x})|\leq C_{\psi}(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{2}).

Thus

|\psi(\bm{x})-\psi_{K}(\bm{x})|\leq C_{\psi}(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{2})\bm{1}\{\|\bm{x}\|>K\}.

Using Hölder’s inequality and the uniform
(4+\epsilon)
-moment bound,

\sup_{j,N}\mathbb{E}\left[(1+\|\bm{\xi}_{j,N}\|^{2})\bm{1}\{\|\bm{\xi}_{j,N}\|>K\}\right]\longrightarrow 0

as
K\to\infty
. Hence both the empirical and expectation-level truncation
errors vanish uniformly in probability as
K\to\infty
. Letting first

N\to\infty
and then
K\to\infty
completes the proof.
∎

[

15

Theorem 2

(External RMT input: regularized correlated-Gaussian MDE)
.

Let

\bm{K}_{N}=\bm{D}_{N}+\bm{W}_{N}

be an
n_{N}\times n_{N}
real symmetric random matrix, where
\bm{W}_{N}
is
centered Gaussian. Define its covariance operator by

\mathcal{S}_{N}[\bm{R}]=\mathbb{E}[\bm{W}_{N}\bm{R}\bm{W}_{N}].

Assume that the following primitive conditions hold.

1.

Flat covariance:
for all admissible deterministic vectors

\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
,

\operatorname{Var}(\bm{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{W}_{N}\bm{b})\leq\frac{C}{N}\|\bm{a}\|^{2}\|\bm{b}\|^{2}.

2.

Bounded deterministic deformation:

\|\bm{D}_{N}\|\leq C.

3.

Regularized block loading:

\bm{D}_{N}
has the block form

\bm{D}_{N}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{I}_{M}&\bm{B}_{N}\\
\bm{B}_{N}^{\mathsf{T}}&-\bm{\Gamma}_{N}\end{pmatrix},

with

\|\bm{B}_{N}\|\leq C,\qquad 0<\gamma_{\min}\bm{I}_{N}\preceq\bm{\Gamma}_{N}\preceq\gamma_{\max}\bm{I}_{N}.

4.

Perturbation uniformity:
the same conclusions below hold
uniformly for

\bm{K}_{N}(\theta)=\bm{D}_{N}+\theta\bm{H}_{N}+\bm{W}_{N}

for all
|\theta|\leq\theta_{0}
and all bounded admissible deterministic
insertions
\bm{H}_{N}
.

Then the MDE

\bm{M}_{N}^{-1}=\bm{D}_{N}-\mathcal{S}_{N}[\bm{M}_{N}]

has a unique bounded stable solution. Moreover, if

\bm{G}_{N}=\bm{K}_{N}^{-1},

then, for admissible deterministic vectors
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
,

\bm{a}^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{G}_{N}-\bm{M}_{N})\bm{b}=o_{p}(1).

Furthermore, the averaged diagonal deterministic equivalent holds:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|\bm{E}_{M+j}^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{G}_{N}-\bm{M}_{N})\bm{E}_{M+j}\right|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Remark 1

(Source and use of the MDE input)
.

Theorem
2
is not an additional algorithmic assumption. It
is the regularized quadratic-form version in which the correlated-Gaussian MDE
stability and local-law theory is used in this paper. The underlying existence,
stability, and deterministic-resolvent approximation are standard consequences
of the MDE framework for random matrices with correlations

[
1
,
2
,
15
]
. In the present
EP application, the compact positive loading in the linear module keeps the
block resolvent in a stable off-singular regime. The EP-specific work is to
verify that, after conditioning on the finite linear history, the resulting
block matrix has flat covariance, bounded deterministic deformation, and
compact loading; these verifications are carried out in
Appendix
C
. The perturbative two-resolvent
form in Corollary
2
follows by applying the same stable
MDE input to a bounded deterministic insertion and differentiating the stable
MDE solution.

The perturbation-uniform part of Theorem
2
yields a
two-resolvent deterministic equivalent.

Corollary 2

(Perturbative two-resolvent equivalent)
.

Let
\bm{K}=\bm{D}+\bm{W}
and
\bm{M}
satisfy the assumptions and
conclusion of Theorem
2
. Let
\bm{H}
be a bounded
admissible deterministic insertion, and define

\bm{G}(\theta)=(\bm{K}+\theta\bm{H})^{-1}.

Let
\bm{M}(\theta)
be the MDE solution associated with

\bm{D}+\theta\bm{H}
:

\bm{M}(\theta)^{-1}=\bm{D}+\theta\bm{H}-\mathcal{S}[\bm{M}(\theta)].

Define

\mathcal{L}[\bm{H}]:=-\left.\frac{d}{d\theta}\bm{M}(\theta)\right|_{\theta=0}.

Then
\mathcal{L}[\bm{H}]
is the solution of

\mathcal{L}[\bm{H}]=\bm{M}\bm{H}\bm{M}+\bm{M}\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{L}[\bm{H}]]\bm{M},

and, in the admissible quadratic-form sense of
Theorem
2
,

\bm{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl(\bm{G}\bm{H}\bm{G}-\mathcal{L}[\bm{H}]\bigr)\bm{b}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0

for all admissible deterministic test vectors
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
.

Proof:

Since

\frac{d}{d\theta}\bm{G}(\theta)=-\bm{G}(\theta)\bm{H}\bm{G}(\theta),

the desired random two-resolvent object is obtained by differentiating the
resolvent. On the deterministic side, differentiating

\bm{M}(\theta)^{-1}=\bm{D}+\theta\bm{H}-\mathcal{S}[\bm{M}(\theta)]

at
\theta=0
gives

-\bm{M}^{-1}\bm{M}^{\prime}(0)\bm{M}^{-1}=\bm{H}-\mathcal{S}[\bm{M}^{\prime}(0)].

With

\mathcal{L}[\bm{H}]=-\bm{M}^{\prime}(0),

this becomes

\mathcal{L}[\bm{H}]=\bm{M}\bm{H}\bm{M}+\bm{M}\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{L}[\bm{H}]]\bm{M}.

The perturbation-uniform deterministic equivalent in
Theorem
2
justifies the differentiation at the level of
admissible quadratic forms.
∎

Finally, we record the Gaussian regression identity used to separate the
predictable memory component from the fresh innovation.

Lemma 2

(Gaussian regression with a possibly singular covariance)
.

Let

\begin{pmatrix}\bm{Y}_{<}\\
Y_{t}\end{pmatrix}

be a jointly Gaussian vector with covariance

\begin{pmatrix}\bm{C}_{<,<}&\bm{c}_{<,t}\\
\bm{c}_{t,<}&c_{t,t}\end{pmatrix}.

Then

Y_{t}=\bm{c}_{t,<}\bm{C}_{<,<}^{\dagger}\bm{Y}_{<}+G_{t},

where
G_{t}
is Gaussian and independent of
\bm{Y}_{<}
, with variance

\nu_{t}=c_{t,t}-\bm{c}_{t,<}\bm{C}_{<,<}^{\dagger}\bm{c}_{<,t}.

The Moore–Penrose inverse automatically removes redundant Gaussian-history
directions, so no full-rank assumption on
\bm{C}_{<,<}
is required.

Proof:

For a jointly Gaussian vector, the conditional expectation of
Y_{t}
given

\bm{Y}_{<}
is the orthogonal projection of
Y_{t}
onto the closed
linear span of
\bm{Y}_{<}
in
L^{2}
. This projection is

\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}\mid\bm{Y}_{<}]=\bm{c}_{t,<}\bm{C}_{<,<}^{\dagger}\bm{Y}_{<}.

Define

G_{t}=Y_{t}-\bm{c}_{t,<}\bm{C}_{<,<}^{\dagger}\bm{Y}_{<}.

Its covariance with the past is

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(G_{t},\bm{Y}_{<})

\displaystyle=\bm{c}_{t,<}-\bm{c}_{t,<}\bm{C}_{<,<}^{\dagger}\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{Y}_{<},\bm{Y}_{<})

\displaystyle=\bm{c}_{t,<}-\bm{c}_{t,<}\bm{C}_{<,<}^{\dagger}\bm{C}_{<,<}.

For a valid covariance matrix, the row vector
\bm{c}_{t,<}
lies in
the row space of
\bm{C}_{<,<}
. Hence

\bm{c}_{t,<}\bm{C}_{<,<}^{\dagger}\bm{C}_{<,<}=\bm{c}_{t,<},

and therefore

\operatorname{Cov}(G_{t},\bm{Y}_{<})=\bm{0}.

Since
(G_{t},\bm{Y}_{<})
is jointly Gaussian, zero covariance implies
independence. The variance of
G_{t}
is

\operatorname{Var}(G_{t})=c_{t,t}-\bm{c}_{t,<}\bm{C}_{<,<}^{\dagger}\bm{c}_{<,t}.

This proves the claim.
∎

III
System Model and Diagonal Expectation Propagation

In this section, we introduce the measurement model and formulate the diagonal
EP recursion analyzed in this paper. The presentation follows the standard
two-module interpretation of EP: a linear Gaussian module incorporates the
likelihood, while a separable prior module incorporates the prior distribution
of the signal. We then rewrite the recursion in terms of estimation errors.
This error-domain representation is the starting point of the conditioning
argument developed in the sequel.

III-A

Assumptions

We consider the real-valued linear measurement model

\bm{y}=\bm{A}\bm{x}+\bm{w},

(1)

where
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the unknown signal vector,

\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
is the measurement matrix, and

\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is the noise vector. The large-system limit is
taken in the proportional regime

M/N\to\delta\in(0,\infty).

(2)

We first state the assumptions used throughout the paper.

Assumption 1

(Signal prior)
.

The signal vector

\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})^{\mathsf{T}}

(3)

has independent and identically distributed elements drawn from a non-degenerate
distribution
P_{X}
. The scalar random variable
X\sim P_{X}
satisfies

0<\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]<\infty,\qquad\mathbb{E}|X|^{4+\epsilon}<\infty

(4)

for some
\epsilon>0
.

No centering or unit-variance normalization is imposed. The scalar denoiser

\eta(\cdot;\pi)
and posterior variance
v_{B}(\cdot;\pi)
are always defined
with respect to the actual prior
P_{X}
. The finite moment condition is used to
obtain empirical energy bounds, no-spike conditions, and uniform integrability
in the pseudo-Lipschitz convergence arguments. The exponent
4+\epsilon
is a
technical regularity condition of the present proof rather than a claimed
optimal requirement. In particular, finite second moment alone does not imply
finite fourth moment; a more refined truncation argument may weaken this
condition, but such a sharpening is not pursued here.

Assumption 2

(Variance-profile Gaussian measurements)
.

The measurement matrix
\bm{A}
is a uniformly elliptic
variance-profile Gaussian matrix in the sense of
Definition
1
. Equivalently,

A_{ij}=\sqrt{\frac{s_{ij}}{M}}Z_{ij},\qquad Z_{ij}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

(5)

where the deterministic profile satisfies

0<s_{\min}\leq s_{ij}\leq s_{\max}<\infty

(6)

uniformly in
i,j,M,N
. The matrix
\bm{A}
is independent of

\bm{x}
and
\bm{w}
.

Unlike unitarily invariant measurement models, the ensemble in
Assumption
2
does not possess a Haar singular-vector
representation in general. The proof therefore cannot rely on rotational
invariance. Instead, it will use Gaussian conditioning under finite linear
histories and the correlated-Gaussian MDE input stated in
Theorem
2
.

Assumption 3

(Noise)
.

The noise vector is Gaussian,

\bm{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\gamma_{w}^{-1}\bm{I}_{M}),

(7)

and is independent of
\bm{A}
and
\bm{x}
. The noise precision

\gamma_{w}>0
is assumed known by the algorithm.

The Gaussian noise assumption is not essential for the linear algebraic part of
the proof, but it keeps the Bayesian interpretation of the linear module
transparent and avoids additional moment assumptions on transformed noise
vectors.

III-B

Diagonal Expectation Propagation

We now formulate the diagonal EP recursion. The algorithm alternates between a
linear Gaussian module, referred to as module A, and a separable prior module,
referred to as module B. At iteration
t
, module A receives a Gaussian
message from module B with mean

\bm{r}_{B\to A}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

(8)

and diagonal precision matrix

\bm{\Gamma}_{t}=\operatorname{diag}(\gamma_{t,1},\ldots,\gamma_{t,N}).

(9)

The Gaussian message from module B is the diagonal Gaussian factor

q_{B\to A}^{t}(\bm{x})=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\bm{x};\bm{r}_{B\to A}^{t},\bm{\Gamma}_{t}^{-1}\right).

(10)

Thus the word “diagonal” refers to the covariance retained by the EP message,
not to an additional probabilistic assumption on the true signal.

Combining this Gaussian message with the likelihood induced by
(
1
), module A forms the Gaussian belief

b_{A}^{t}(\bm{x})\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{\gamma_{w}}{2}\|\bm{y}-\bm{A}\bm{x}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{r}_{B\to A}^{t}\|_{\bm{\Gamma}_{t}}^{2}\right\},

(11)

where

\|\bm{v}\|_{\bm{\Gamma}_{t}}^{2}=\bm{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Gamma}_{t}\bm{v}.

(12)

Thus the covariance and mean of the module-A belief are

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\gamma_{w}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\bm{\Gamma}_{t}\right)^{-1},

(13)

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{x}}_{A}^{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{C}_{t}\left(\gamma_{w}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{y}+\bm{\Gamma}_{t}\bm{r}_{B\to A}^{t}\right).

(14)

Let

d_{t,j}=[\bm{C}_{t}]_{jj},\qquad\bm{D}_{t}=\operatorname{diag}(d_{t,1},\ldots,d_{t,N}).

(15)

The diagonal EP approximation retains only the diagonal marginal variances of
the Gaussian belief. The outgoing Gaussian message from module A to module B
is obtained by subtracting the incoming Gaussian natural parameters from the
diagonal marginal belief. Its diagonal precision matrix is

\bm{\Pi}_{t}=\bm{D}_{t}^{-1}-\bm{\Gamma}_{t},

(16)

and its mean
\bm{r}_{A\to B}^{t}
is defined through

\bm{\Pi}_{t}\bm{r}_{A\to B}^{t}=\bm{D}_{t}^{-1}\widehat{\bm{x}}_{A}^{t}-\bm{\Gamma}_{t}\bm{r}_{B\to A}^{t}.

(17)

In stable implementations, the diagonal precisions in
(
16
) and in the corresponding prior-to-linear update below are
kept in compact positive intervals. We follow this standard convention and
record it as a regularity condition rather than as a separate algorithmic
variant.

Module B processes the components of
\bm{r}_{A\to B}^{t}
separately.
For a scalar precision
\pi>0
, define the scalar Gaussian observation model

R=X+\pi^{-1/2}Z,\qquad Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\qquad X\sim P_{X},

(18)

with
X
and
Z
independent. The scalar posterior mean and variance are
denoted by

\displaystyle\eta(r;\pi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X\mid R=r],

(19)

\displaystyle v_{B}(r;\pi)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}(X\mid R=r).

(20)

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\eta(r;\pi)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int x\,p_{X}(x)\exp\{-\frac{\pi}{2}(r-x)^{2}\}\,dx}{\int p_{X}(x)\exp\{-\frac{\pi}{2}(r-x)^{2}\}\,dx},

(21)

\displaystyle v_{B}(r;\pi)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int(x-\eta(r;\pi))^{2}p_{X}(x)\exp\{-\frac{\pi}{2}(r-x)^{2}\}\,dx}{\int p_{X}(x)\exp\{-\frac{\pi}{2}(r-x)^{2}\}\,dx}.

(22)

\widehat{x}_{B,j}^{t}=\eta(r_{A\to B,j}^{t};\pi_{t,j}),\qquad j=1,\ldots,N,

(23)

where

\bm{\Pi}_{t}=\operatorname{diag}(\pi_{t,1},\ldots,\pi_{t,N}).

(24)

\bm{V}_{B,t}=\operatorname{diag}(v_{B,t,1},\ldots,v_{B,t,N}),\qquad v_{B,t,j}=v_{B}(r_{A\to B,j}^{t};\pi_{t,j}).

(25)

extrinsic subtraction of natural parameters. Its diagonal precision matrix is

\bm{\Gamma}_{t+1}=\bm{V}_{B,t}^{-1}-\bm{\Pi}_{t},

(26)

and its mean
\bm{r}_{B\to A}^{t+1}
satisfies

\bm{\Gamma}_{t+1}\bm{r}_{B\to A}^{t+1}=\bm{V}_{B,t}^{-1}\widehat{\bm{x}}_{B}^{t}-\bm{\Pi}_{t}\bm{r}_{A\to B}^{t}.

(27)

The iteration is initialized with a deterministic Gaussian message, for example

\bm{r}_{B\to A}^{0}=\bm{0},\qquad\bm{\Gamma}_{0}=\gamma_{0}\bm{I}_{N}

(28)

for some
\gamma_{0}>0
in the admissible precision range.

III-C

Error Recursion

For the large-system analysis, it is convenient to rewrite the diagonal EP

\displaystyle\bm{q}_{t}

(34)

Substituting the system model (

\bm{x}

\displaystyle\bm{M}_{t}

(43)

IV
Main Results

This section states the main asymptotic results of the paper. The guiding
question is whether the diagonal EP cavity delivered by the linear module can
be interpreted as a fresh scalar AWGN observation of each signal coordinate.
Such a fresh-channel interpretation is the basis of scalar state evolution in
unitarily invariant models. In the variance-profile Gaussian setting, however,
the correct conclusion is more subtle: a Gaussian description still exists,
but the Gaussian term is generally not an innovation. It contains a
predictable component generated by the past Gaussian history.

The main results below make this statement precise. We first introduce the
MDE-generated quantities that replace the scalar state variables of classical
EP state evolution. We then state a finite-time dynamic theorem, from which
the memory defect of standard diagonal EP and the memory-corrected
coordinate-wise state evolution follow.

V

I
.
The memory-defect and corrected-state-evolution consequences are proved in
Section
VI
. The precision-replacement theorem is
proved in Section

, with auxiliary
estimates collected in Appendix
J

IV-A

Fresh-AWGN Principle and Profile-Dependent State Variables

The prior module in diagonal EP applies a scalar Bayesian denoiser to the
incoming message from the linear module. This denoising step is matched if the
message can be interpreted, asymptotically, as a scalar AWGN observation

r_{A\to B,t,j}=x_{j}+\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j},\qquad W_{t,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),

(51)

with
W_{t,j}
fresh relative to the past in the sense of
Definition
5
. In unitarily invariant settings,
this fresh-AWGN mechanism is what ultimately leads to a scalar state evolution.
In the present variance-profile Gaussian setting, the linear module still
produces a Gaussian kernel, but the resulting Gaussian process need not be an
innovation process. The purpose of this subsection is to define the
coordinate-wise state variables that describe this phenomenon.

For each iteration
t
, let

\bm{C}_{t}=\left(\gamma_{w}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\bm{\Gamma}_{t}\right)^{-1}

(52)

be the linear-module covariance matrix defined in
(
13
). The diagonal entries

d_{t,j}=[\bm{C}_{t}]_{jj}

(53)

will be shown to admit deterministic equivalents generated by a conditioned
MDE. We denote these equivalents by

T_{t,j}.

(54)

Thus, in the finite-time limit considered in this paper,

d_{t,j}=T_{t,j}+o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1).

(55)

The precise MDE construction of
T_{t,j}
is given in
Section
V
. At the level of the main results, it is
sufficient to regard
T_{t,j}
as the MDE response of the
j
th signal
coordinate at iteration
t
.

The variance profile enters the Gaussian kernel through a conditioned
column-covariance response of the measurement residuals. We make this object
explicit because it is the point at which the variance-profile model differs
from the rotationally invariant case.

Definition 6

(Conditioned two-resolvent response)
.

Fix
r,s\leq t
and a coordinate
j
. Let

\bm{\Xi}_{j|r,s}
denote the conditional covariance operator of the
unexposed component of the
j
th Gaussian column after the linear history and
the previously revealed Schur scalar projections have been conditioned upon.
The number

\Theta_{j}^{r,s}

(56)

is defined as the deterministic equivalent, produced by the conditioned MDE
and its linearized two-resolvent response, of the quadratic form

(\bm{u}_{r})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Xi}_{j|r,s}\bm{u}_{s}.

(57)

Equivalently,
\Theta_{j}^{r,s}
is the output of the linearized MDE response
with the block insertion corresponding to the conditioned covariance of the

j
th column.

In the absence of history conditioning, the conditional covariance reduces to

M^{-1}\bm{S}_{j}
and Definition
6

reduces to the intuitive profile-weighted residual quadratic form

\frac{1}{M}\bm{u}_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{S}_{j}\bm{u}_{s},

(58)

where
\bm{S}_{j}
is the column-profile matrix in
Definition
2
, and

\bm{u}_{t}=\bm{w}-\bm{A}\bm{m}_{t}

(59)

is the measurement residual defined in (
40
). In the sequel,

\Theta_{j}^{r,s}
always denotes the conditioned response in
Definition
6
;
(
58
) is only its unconditioned special case.

The covariance kernel
of the coordinate-wise Schur Gaussian process is then

\zeta_{j}^{r,s}=\gamma_{w}^{2}T_{r,j}T_{s,j}\Theta_{j}^{r,s}.

(60)

For each coordinate
j
, define the past Gaussian-history vector

\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{<t,j}=(\mathcal{Z}_{0,j},\mathcal{Z}_{1,j},\ldots,\mathcal{Z}_{t-1,j})^{\mathsf{T}},

(61)

and the covariance blocks

\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,<t}=(\zeta_{j}^{r,s})_{0\leq r,s<t},

(62)

\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t}=(\zeta_{j}^{t,0},\ldots,\zeta_{j}^{t,t-1}),\qquad\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,t}=(\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t})^{\mathsf{T}}.

(63)

The Gaussian regression of
\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}
onto its past is

\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}=\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t}(\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{<t,j}+G_{t,j},

(64)

where
G_{t,j}
is Gaussian and independent of

\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{<t,j}
, conditionally on the MDE-generated environment. Its
variance is

\nu_{t,j}=\zeta_{j}^{t,t}-\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t}(\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,t}.

(65)

When
t=0
, the past is empty and the convention is

\nu_{0,j}=\zeta_{j}^{0,0}.

(66)

Let

\bar{\pi}_{t,j}=\operatorname{Proj}_{[\pi_{\min},\pi_{\max}]}\left(T_{t,j}^{-1}-\gamma_{t,j}\right)

(67)

be the MDE-level diagonal EP cavity precision, where

\operatorname{Proj}_{[a,b]}(x)=\min\{b,\max\{a,x\}\}
. Define the cavity
scaling factor

\alpha_{t,j}=\frac{1}{\bar{\pi}_{t,j}T_{t,j}}.

(68)

The predictable memory component and the innovation variance of the EP cavity
are then

\displaystyle\mu_{t,j}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t,j}\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t}(\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{<t,j},

(69)

\displaystyle\tau_{t,j}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t,j}^{2}\nu_{t,j}.

(70)

The collection

\mathcal{P}_{t}=\{T_{s,j},\Theta_{j}^{r,s},\zeta_{j}^{r,s},\bar{\pi}_{s,j},\tau_{s,j}:0\leq r,s\leq t,\ 1\leq j\leq N\}

(71)

will be called the MDE-generated environment up to time
t
.

The central point is that
\mu_{t,j}
is generally nonzero. In the terminology
of Definition
5
, it is the EP-cavity memory defect. Hence the
standard diagonal EP cavity is not merely corrupted by Gaussian noise; it is
shifted by a component predictable from the past Gaussian history. The next
theorem states the finite-time dynamic result from which all main conclusions
follow.

IV-B

General Finite-Time Dynamic Theorem

The following theorem is the technical backbone of the paper. It is the
variance-profile counterpart of a finite-time EP state-evolution theorem.
Rather than producing two scalar variance recursions, it produces a
coordinate-wise Gaussian history governed by the MDE-generated environment
defined above. All variables in this theorem belong to the
predictable-precision dynamics used in the conditioning proof. The actual
finite-dimensional adaptive precision recursion is connected to this dynamics
only later, through Theorem
6
.

Theorem 3

(General finite-time dynamic theorem)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
–
4
hold. Fix an
arbitrary finite iteration horizon
T
. Then, for every
t\leq T
, the
predictable-precision diagonal EP dynamics associated with
Section
III
satisfies the following properties.

1) MDE response of the linear covariance.
The retained diagonal
variance satisfies

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|d_{t,j}-T_{t,j}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

(72)

2) Coordinate-wise Gaussian kernel.
There exist Schur Gaussian process coordinates

\{\mathcal{Z}_{s,j}:0\leq s\leq t,1\leq j\leq N\}
such that

m_{t,j}=\gamma_{t,j}T_{t,j}q_{t,j}+\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}+\Delta_{t,j},

(73)

where

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\Delta_{t,j}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

(74)

Conditionally on
\mathcal{P}_{t}
, the Gaussian history

(\mathcal{Z}_{0,j},\mathcal{Z}_{1,j},\ldots,\mathcal{Z}_{t,j})

(75)

has covariance kernel

\operatorname{Cov}(\mathcal{Z}_{r,j}^{\mathrm{G}},\mathcal{Z}_{s,j}^{\mathrm{G}}\mid\mathcal{P}_{t})=\zeta_{j}^{r,s},\qquad 0\leq r,s\leq t.

(76)

3) Empirical Gaussian law.
Let
\mathcal{X}_{j,N}^{t}
denote any finite
coordinate history formed from the variables generated by the EP recursion up
to time
t
, and let
\mathcal{X}_{j,\mathrm{G}}^{t}
denote the corresponding
Gaussian-kernel reference history obtained by replacing the linear-module
residuals with the Gaussian Schur variables in (
73
). Then,
for every
\psi\in\mathrm{PL}(2)
,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi(\mathcal{X}_{j,N}^{t})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{G},j}\!\left[\psi(\mathcal{X}_{j,\mathrm{G}}^{t})\mid\mathcal{P}_{t}\right]\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

(77)

4) EP cavity decomposition.
The standard diagonal EP cavity error
satisfies

h_{t,j}=\mu_{t,j}+\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j}+\varepsilon_{t,j},

(78)

where
W_{t,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is independent of the past Gaussian history
conditionally on
\mathcal{P}_{t}
, and

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\varepsilon_{t,j}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

(79)

5) Regularity closure.
The vectors

\bm{m}_{t},\quad\bm{h}_{t},\quad\bm{p}_{t},\quad\bm{q}_{t+1}

(80)

remain signal-side admissible, and the measurement residual

\bm{u}_{t}=\bm{w}-\bm{A}\bm{m}_{t}

(81)

remains measurement-side admissible. Hence the finite-time induction can be
continued up to iteration
T
.

The proof of Theorem
3
is given in
Section
V
. Its structure follows the error recursion of
Section
III-C
: the past iterates impose finite linear
constraints on
\bm{A}
; Gaussian conditioning decomposes

\bm{A}
into a bounded deformation and a centered correlated Gaussian
residual; the external MDE input gives the linear response
T_{t,j}
; and a
conditioned Schur-kernel and empirical Gaussian-law argument upgrades the
coordinate-wise Gaussian kernel to the empirical law
(
77
). The theorem deliberately avoids
conditioning on actual retained diagonal resolvents or adaptive cavity
precisions. Those nonlinear finite-sample quantities are handled separately by
the precision-replacement theorem.

IV-C

Consequences: Memory Defect and Corrected State Evolution

Theorem
3
has two immediate consequences. The first is
a negative statement for the usual fresh-AWGN interpretation of standard
diagonal EP: the cavity is Gaussian, but it is generally shifted by a
predictable memory term. The second is a positive statement: after removing
this predictable component, one obtains a fresh coordinate-wise AWGN channel.

Theorem 4

(Memory defect of standard diagonal EP)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3
, the standard
diagonal EP message from module A to module B satisfies

r_{A\to B,t,j}=x_{j}+\mu_{t,j}+\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j}+o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1).

(82)

Equivalently,

h_{t,j}=\mu_{t,j}+\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j}+o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1).

(83)

Thus the standard EP cavity is not, in general, a fresh AWGN observation of

x_{j}
. It is a shifted Gaussian observation whose shift is predictable from
the past Gaussian history.

The proof of Theorem
4
is given in
Section
VI-A
.

The term
\mu_{t,j}
is the memory defect. It is not a small technical
remainder; it is the conditional mean of the current Gaussian residual given
its past. Therefore, unless this conditional mean vanishes, the scalar
posterior mean

\eta(r_{A\to B,t,j};\pi_{t,j})

(84)

is not matched to the fresh channel

X+\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W.

(85)

Corollary 3

(No-memory special case)
.

If, for every fixed
t
and all coordinates
j
,

\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t}=\bm{0},

(86)

then

\mu_{t,j}=0,

(87)

and the standard diagonal EP cavity is asymptotically fresh:

h_{t,j}=\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j}+o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1).

(88)

The corollary follows immediately from Theorem
4
and the
Gaussian-regression formula (
64
).

Condition (
86
) is the profile-dependent analogue of
the innovation property available in rotationally invariant settings. For a
general variance profile, it need not hold.

Remark 2

(Sanity check: collapse to the innovation case)
.

If the conditioned MDE response is temporally diagonal in the sense of
(
86
), then the Gaussian regression in
(
64
) has zero predictable part. Consequently the
standard and corrected cavities coincide up to the vanishing empirical
remainder, and the coordinate-wise SE reduces to the usual fresh-AWGN form.
This is the precise sense in which the present theorem is consistent with the
innovation mechanism proved in rotationally invariant EP analyses. The point
of the variance-profile result is that temporal diagonality of the conditioned
kernel is no longer automatic.

The correction below is an oracle state-evolution construction. It is
introduced to identify the innovation channel implied by the Gaussian regression
of the Schur residual process. It is not claimed here that the memory
coefficients can be estimated from one finite-dimensional observation or that
(
89
) is a directly implementable finite-sample EP
update.

We now define the memory-corrected cavity by subtracting the predictable
Gaussian-history component:

\widetilde{h}_{t,j}=h_{t,j}-\mu_{t,j},\qquad\widetilde{r}_{A\to B,t,j}=x_{j}+\widetilde{h}_{t,j}.

(89)

This correction is a Gaussian innovation decomposition at the level of state
evolution. It removes the conditional mean of the Gaussian residual and leaves
the fresh innovation.

Theorem 5

(Memory-corrected coordinate-wise state evolution)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3
, the corrected
cavity satisfies

\widetilde{h}_{t,j}=\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j}+o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1).

(90)

Equivalently,

\widetilde{r}_{A\to B,t,j}=x_{j}+\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j}+o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1).

(91)

Let
\mathcal{X}_{j,N}^{t,\mathrm{corr}}
denote any finite coordinate history
generated by the corrected scalar channel up to time
t
, and let

\mathcal{X}_{j,\mathrm{SE}}^{t,\mathrm{corr}}
denote its state-evolution
reference generated by

R_{t,j}^{\mathrm{SE}}=X_{j}+\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j},

(92)

with posterior updates applied componentwise using the scalar prior
P_{X}
.
Then, for every
\psi\in\mathrm{PL}(2)
,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi(\mathcal{X}_{j,N}^{t,\mathrm{corr}})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{SE},j}\!\left[\psi(\mathcal{X}_{j,\mathrm{SE}}^{t,\mathrm{corr}})\mid\mathcal{P}_{t}\right]\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

(93)

The proof of Theorem
5
is given in
Section
VI-C
.

The state evolution in Theorem
5
is coordinate-wise and
quenched with respect to the MDE-generated environment
\mathcal{P}_{t}
. It is
not, in general, reducible to a pair of scalar variance recursions. The
reduction to a scalar SE requires additional symmetry of the variance profile.

Remark 3

(Meaning of the correction)
.

The correction in (
89
) should be understood as an
SE-level Gaussian regression correction. It identifies the fresh scalar
channel obtained after removing the predictable part of the Gaussian residual.
Without further structural assumptions, it is not claimed that all memory
coefficients can be estimated from a single finite-dimensional realization.
Designing practical low-complexity estimators or approximations of this oracle
correction is left for future work.

IV-D

Precision Replacement for Adaptive Diagonal EP

The dynamic theorem above is stated in the form used by the conditioning proof,
where the diagonal precision sequence is represented by its predictable
MDE-level counterpart. The diagonal EP recursion in
Section
III
, however, computes the precisions from the
finite-dimensional retained variances and scalar posterior variances. The
following theorem connects these two descriptions.

For a vector
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, write

\|\bm{a}\|_{N}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\|\bm{a}\|^{2}.

(94)

Theorem 6

(Branch-wise precision replacement)
.

Fix a finite horizon
T
and assume the additional replacement regularity
conditions stated in Section
VII-A
. Consider
either of the following two branches:

b\in\{\mathrm{std},\mathrm{corr}\},

(95)

where
\mathrm{std}
denotes the standard diagonal EP branch using

\bm{h}_{t}
, and
\mathrm{corr}
denotes the memory-corrected branch using

\widetilde{\bm{h}}_{t}
.

Let

(\bm{q}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{act}},\bm{\gamma}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{act}})

(96)

be the finite-dimensional adaptive recursion generated by the diagonal EP
updates of Section
III
, and let

(\bm{q}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{orc}},\bm{\gamma}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{orc}})

(97)

be the corresponding predictable recursion described by the MDE-generated
state variables in Theorem
3
. If the two recursions
are initialized identically, then, for every fixed
t\leq T
,

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\gamma}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{act}}-\bm{\gamma}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{orc}}\right\|_{N}

\displaystyle\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0,

(98)

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{q}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{act}}-\bm{q}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{orc}}\right\|_{N}

\displaystyle\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

(99)

The proof of Theorem
6
is given in
Section
VII-E
.

Theorem
6
is branch-wise. It does not state that
the standard and corrected branches are asymptotically equivalent to each
other. Rather, it states that within each branch, the finite-dimensional
adaptive precision update can be replaced, in empirical norm, by its
MDE-predictable counterpart. Thus the predictable-precision dynamic theorem
applies to the adaptive diagonal EP recursion.

V
Proof of the General Finite-Time Dynamic Theorem

This section proves Theorem
3
. The proof follows the
same high-level strategy as rigorous finite-time EP analyses for rotationally
invariant matrices, but the conditioning mechanism is different. In the
unitarily invariant setting, the past iterates leave a fresh Haar component in
the orthogonal complement of the history. In the present variance-profile
Gaussian setting, the past iterates impose finite linear observations on

\bm{A}
. Conditioning therefore leaves a centered correlated Gaussian
matrix rather than a Haar matrix. The role of rotational averaging is replaced
by the regularized correlated-Gaussian MDE input, a Schur-complement
representation under the actual conditioned history, and an empirical Gaussian
law for the resulting column-wise Gaussian process.

Throughout this section, the diagonal precision sequence is considered in the
predictable form needed for the conditioning proof. More precisely, at the
beginning of the
t
th linear step, the diagonal loading

\bm{\Gamma}_{t}=\operatorname{diag}(\gamma_{t,1},\ldots,\gamma_{t,N})

(100)

is regarded as part of the regular finite-time environment and satisfies the
compactness condition in Assumption
4
. The finite-dimensional
adaptive update of the diagonal EP precisions is connected to this
predictable-precision form in Section
VII
. The
present section proves the Gaussian dynamic theorem under the predictable
loading, which is the form in which conditioning on the past leaves only finite
linear information about
\bm{A}
.

V-A

Proof Strategy and Induction Hypotheses

Let

\bm{Q}_{t}=(\bm{q}_{0},\ldots,\bm{q}_{t-1}),\quad\bm{M}_{t}=(\bm{m}_{0},\ldots,\bm{m}_{t-1}),

(101)

\bm{H}_{t}=(\bm{h}_{0},\ldots,\bm{h}_{t-1}),\quad\bm{P}_{t}=(\bm{p}_{0},\ldots,\bm{p}_{t-1}),

(102)

and

\bm{U}_{t}=(\bm{u}_{0},\ldots,\bm{u}_{t-1})

(103)

be the finite histories up to, but not including, iteration
t
. These
histories are used for Gaussian conditioning only through the finite linear
constraints displayed below. The scalar-module histories
\bm{H}_{t}
and

\bm{P}_{t}
are tracked in the induction regularity event, but they are
not themselves added to the Gaussian conditioning field. This convention
prevents the conditioning argument from accidentally conditioning on nonlinear
resolvent information about
\bm{A}
.

The induction is carried out on a regularity event, denoted by

\mathcal{R}_{t}
. Informally,
\mathcal{R}_{t}
is the event that the finite
history up to time
t
is admissible in the sense of
Definition
4
, the diagonal precisions remain in compact
positive intervals, and the finite normalized Gram matrices associated with the
history are tight after removing asymptotically redundant directions. The
Moore–Penrose inverse is used whenever a finite history contains redundant
directions. This convention avoids unnecessary full-rank assumptions and is
consistent with the Gaussian regression formula in
Lemma
2
.

The induction has three transitions. The linear transition identifies the
conditional law of the LMMSE module under the predictable linear history. The
cavity transition converts the Schur residual produced by the linear module into
a Gaussian process with a regression memory term. The scalar transition passes
this effective channel through the separable prior module and verifies that the
new histories remain regular. Thus the proof is not a collection of separate
limits; each proposition below answers one of the questions required to advance
from iteration
t
to iteration
t+1
.

For every fixed horizon
T
, the induction establishes

\mathcal{R}_{t}\Longrightarrow\text{all conclusions of Theorem~\ref{thm:general_dynamic} at time }t,

(104)

and then proves the closure implication

\mathcal{R}_{t}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{R}_{t+1}.

(105)

Since the initialization is deterministic and regular,
\mathcal{R}_{0}
holds
with probability tending to one. Finite induction then proves the theorem.

The remainder of the section follows this order. First, the exact history
constraints are used to condition the measurement matrix and to obtain the MDE
responses
T_{t,j}
and
\Theta_{j}^{r,s}
. Second, a current-step Schur complement
separates the instantaneous response from a column-wise Gaussian process, and an
empirical Gaussian law upgrades the coordinate description to pseudo-Lipschitz
convergence. Third, Gaussian regression extracts the fresh innovation seen by
the corrected cavity, and the scalar module closes the regularity event. The
proofs of the technical propositions are deferred to the appendices; this
section records their roles and assembles them into the induction.

V-B

Conditioned Linear Module

We start from the exact history constraints derived in
Section
III-C
. By the definition

\bm{u}_{t}=\bm{w}-\bm{A}\bm{m}_{t},

(106)

the past linear-module errors satisfy

\bm{A}\bm{M}_{t}=\bm{w}\bm{1}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{U}_{t}.

(107)

Furthermore, the normal equation of the linear module gives

\gamma_{w}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{u}_{s}=\bm{\Gamma}_{s}(\bm{m}_{s}-\bm{q}_{s}),\qquad 0\leq s<t.

(108)

Stacking over
s<t
yields

\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{U}_{t}=\gamma_{w}^{-1}\left(\bm{\Gamma}_{0}(\bm{m}_{0}-\bm{q}_{0}),\ldots,\bm{\Gamma}_{t-1}(\bm{m}_{t-1}-\bm{q}_{t-1})\right).

(109)

Equations (
107
) and (
109
) are
finite linear observations of the variance-profile Gaussian matrix

\bm{A}
. Equivalently, after vectorization, there exist a finite-rank
linear operator
\bm{L}_{t}
and a vector
\bm{b}_{t}
such that

\bm{L}_{t}\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A})=\bm{b}_{t}.

(110)

The predictable linear-history filtration used below is

\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\rm lin}=\sigma\!\left(\bm{x},\bm{w},\bm{\Gamma}_{0},\ldots,\bm{\Gamma}_{t},\bm{L}_{t},\bm{b}_{t}\right).

(111)

The notation
\mathcal{F}_{t}
will be used below as shorthand for

\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\rm lin}
. This filtration contains the external variables,
the predictable diagonal loadings, and the finite linear observations
(
110
). It does not contain the actual
retained variance
\bm{D}_{s}
, the actual finite-sample cavity precision

\bm{\Pi}_{s}
, or any other nonlinear resolvent functional of

\bm{A}
. The MDE-level precision variables used by the predictable
recursion are deterministic functions of the reference environment and are not
additional observations of the Gaussian matrix. The adaptive precisions
computed from the finite-dimensional diagonals are connected to this
predictable history only in the precision-replacement argument of
Section
VII
. Consequently, for the conditional
law of
\bm{A}
, conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{t}
is precisely
conditioning on finite linear observations and predictable quantities. The
matrix therefore remains Gaussian after conditioning, although its covariance is
no longer entrywise independent in general.

The first question in the linear transition is therefore: what is the law of

\bm{A}
after these linear observations have been fixed? The answer is
the Gaussian analogue of the conditional Haar representation used in
rotationally invariant analyses. The matrix remains Gaussian, but its residual
covariance becomes history-dependent.

Proposition 2

(Conditional Gaussian representation)
.

Assume that the regularity event
\mathcal{R}_{t}

(113)

and

\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}_{\perp,t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]=\bm{0}.

(114)

Moreover, for all deterministic vectors

\bm{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\bm{p}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}_{\perp,t}\bm{q}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)\leq\frac{C}{M}\|\bm{p}\|^{2}\|\bm{q}\|^{2},

(115)

where

is independent of
M,N,t
.

Proof:

See Appendix
B
.
∎

The conditional mean

is a finite-rank
deformation determined by the history. The next proposition states that this
deformation remains bounded on the regular history event and that the resulting
conditioned linearization falls within the class covered by the external MDE

Proposition 3

(Bounded deformation and MDE response)
.

Assume
\mathcal{R}_{t}

\|\bm{A}_{\parallel,t}\|=O_{p}(1).

Consequently, the block linearization

\mathcal{F}_{t}

denotes the corresponding MDE solution,

gives the deterministic

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|d_{t,j}-T_{t,j}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof:

See Appendix
C
.
∎

Proposition

: the retained diagonal variance of the linear

. The next step is
to identify the Gaussian kernel produced by the linear estimator.

V-D

Cavity Decomposition and Induction Closure

The EP cavity is obtained by subtracting the incoming Gaussian natural
parameters from the diagonal marginal belief:

\bm{\Pi}_{t}\bm{h}_{t}=\bm{D}_{t}^{-1}\bm{m}_{t}-\bm{\Gamma}_{t}\bm{q}_{t}.

(131)

Substituting the Gaussian Schur kernel

d_{t,j}^{-1}m_{t,j}-\gamma_{t,j}q_{t,j}=(T_{t,j})^{-1}\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}+o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1).

\pi_{t,j}=\bar{\pi}_{t,j}+o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1),

(135)

h_{t,j}=\alpha_{t,j}\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}+o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1),\qquad\alpha_{t,j}=\frac{1}{\bar{\pi}_{t,j}T_{t,j}}.

(136)

predictable component of
\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}
with respect to its own history.

VI
Consequences of the Dynamic Theorem

The general finite-time dynamic theorem identifies the precise Gaussian object
created by the linear module. We now translate this dynamic statement into the
main consequences for diagonal EP. The key point is that the Gaussian process
in Theorem
3
is not necessarily an innovation process.
The standard EP cavity removes the instantaneous self-response, but it does not
in general remove the component of the current Gaussian residual that is
predictable from its past.

The results in this section are first stated for the predictable-precision
dynamic of Theorem
3
. The adaptive diagonal EP
recursion of Section
III
is connected to this form by the
precision replacement theorem proved in Section
VII
.
Thus the purpose of the present section is not to introduce new random-matrix
arguments, but to show how the Gaussian dynamic theorem yields the memory
defect and the corrected coordinate-wise state evolution.

VI-A

Memory Defect of Standard Diagonal EP

We first prove Theorem
4
. The message delivered by
module A to module B is

\bm{r}_{A\to B}^{t}=\bm{x}+\bm{h}_{t}.

(150)

By Theorem
3
, the EP cavity error admits the
decomposition

h_{t,j}=\mu_{t,j}+\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j}+\varepsilon_{t,j},

(151)

where, conditionally on the MDE-generated environment
\mathcal{P}_{t}
,

W_{t,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is independent of the past Gaussian history, and

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\varepsilon_{t,j}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

(152)

Substituting (
151
) into

\bm{r}_{A\to B}^{t}=\bm{x}+\bm{h}_{t}
gives

r_{A\to B,t,j}=x_{j}+\mu_{t,j}+\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j}+\varepsilon_{t,j}.

(153)

Equivalently,

r_{A\to B,t,j}=x_{j}+\mu_{t,j}+\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j}+o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1).

(154)

This is exactly (
82
), and therefore proves
Theorem
4
.

The interpretation is immediate. The standard diagonal EP cavity is Gaussian,
but it is not generally a fresh AWGN observation of
x_{j}
. The term

\mu_{t,j}=\alpha_{t,j}\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t}(\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{<t,j}

(155)

is the conditional mean of the current Gaussian residual given its past
Gaussian history. Hence
\mu_{t,j}
is a predictable memory component, not a
vanishing error term. Unless this component is asymptotically negligible, the
scalar denoiser in the standard EP recursion is driven by the shifted channel

X+\mu_{t,j}+\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W

(156)

rather than by the fresh channel

X+\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W.

(157)

Thus Gaussianity survives under variance-profile measurements, but freshness
does not hold in general.

VI-B

The No-Memory Special Case

We next prove Corollary
3
. Suppose that, for every fixed

t
and all coordinates
j
,

\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t}=\bm{0}.

(158)

Then, by the definition of the memory term,

\mu_{t,j}=\alpha_{t,j}\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t}(\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{<t,j}=0.

(159)

Substituting this identity into
(
151
) yields

h_{t,j}=\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j}+\varepsilon_{t,j},

(160)

with

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\varepsilon_{t,j}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

(161)

Therefore

h_{t,j}=\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j}+o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1),

(162)

which proves (
88
).

The condition

\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t}=\bm{0}

(163)

means that the current Gaussian residual is orthogonal to its own past. In
that special case, the Gaussian process produced by the linear module is
already an innovation process, and the standard EP cavity itself is fresh.
This is the profile-dependent counterpart of the innovation mechanism available
in rotationally invariant models.

VI-C

Memory-Corrected Coordinate-Wise State Evolution

We finally prove Theorem
5
. Define the corrected cavity
by subtracting the predictable Gaussian-history component:

\widetilde{h}_{t,j}=h_{t,j}-\mu_{t,j},\qquad\widetilde{r}_{A\to B,t,j}=x_{j}+\widetilde{h}_{t,j}.

(164)

Using (
151
), we obtain

\widetilde{h}_{t,j}=\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j}+\varepsilon_{t,j}.

(165)

Since

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\varepsilon_{t,j}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0,

(166)

it follows that

\widetilde{h}_{t,j}=\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j}+o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1).

(167)

Equivalently,

\widetilde{r}_{A\to B,t,j}=x_{j}+\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j}+o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1).

(168)

This proves the fresh-channel part of
Theorem
5
.

It remains to identify the corresponding scalar state-evolution update. The
matched scalar reference channel associated with
(
168
) is

R_{t,j}^{\mathrm{SE}}=X_{j}+\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j},\qquad W_{t,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(169)

Since
\tau_{t,j}
is the innovation variance, the matched scalar precision is

\tau_{t,j}^{-1}
. Thus the corresponding state-evolution posterior error is

P_{t,j}^{\mathrm{SE}}=\eta(R_{t,j}^{\mathrm{SE}};\tau_{t,j}^{-1})-X_{j},

(170)

and the scalar posterior variance is

V_{B,t,j}^{\mathrm{SE}}=v_{B}(R_{t,j}^{\mathrm{SE}};\tau_{t,j}^{-1}).

(171)

The empirical convergence statement follows from the empirical Gaussian law in
Theorem
3
. Indeed, after subtracting the predictable
memory component, the Gaussian reference history is generated by the fresh
scalar channel (
169
). The scalar posterior mean and
variance maps preserve
\mathrm{PL}(2)
empirical convergence on compact precision
intervals by Assumption
4
. Therefore, for any

\psi\in\mathrm{PL}(2)
,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi(\mathcal{X}_{j,N}^{t,\mathrm{corr}})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{SE},j}\left[\psi(\mathcal{X}_{j,\mathrm{SE}}^{t,\mathrm{corr}})\mid\mathcal{P}_{t}\right]\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

(172)

This is (
93
), and hence proves
Theorem
5
.

The corrected state evolution should be understood as an innovation
decomposition of the Gaussian process identified in
Theorem
3
. The correction does not alter the linear
Gaussian kernel; it removes only the conditional mean of the current residual
given its past. After this removal, the prior module sees the matched scalar
channel (
169
). This is the coordinate-wise analogue of
the fresh AWGN channel underlying scalar state evolution.

VII
Precision Replacement for Adaptive Diagonal EP

The dynamic theorem in Section
V
was proved in a
predictable-precision form. This form is natural for the conditioning argument:
at the beginning of the
t
th linear step, the diagonal loading

\bm{\Gamma}_{t}
is already part of the regular finite-time environment,
so conditioning on the past imposes only finite linear observations on the
Gaussian matrix
\bm{A}
. The actual diagonal EP recursion, however,
updates its precisions from finite-dimensional retained variances and scalar
posterior variances. This section shows that the two descriptions are
asymptotically equivalent.

The result is a stability statement, not a new state evolution. Small
empirical perturbations in the incoming Gaussian message and its diagonal
precision lead to small perturbations in the linear posterior, the retained
diagonal variances, the cavity message, the scalar prior update, and finally the
next outgoing precision and mean. This closes the gap between the
predictable-precision dynamic theorem and the adaptive diagonal EP recursion
formulated in Section
III
.

VII-A

Regularity Conditions for Replacement

We use two replacement regularity conditions in this section. They refine the
standing scalar-module regularity of Assumption
4
only for
the deterministic perturbation argument connecting predictable precisions to
finite-sample adaptive precisions. They are not used as additional Gaussian
conditioning information.

Assumption 5

(Additional replacement stability of the scalar module)
.

Assumption
4
gives the compact precision intervals used throughout
the paper. For the replacement argument we additionally assume that the scalar
posterior variances used in the prior module are projected onto a compact
positive interval,

0<v_{\min}\leq v_{B,t,j}\leq v_{\max}<\infty,

(173)

for all fixed
t
and all coordinates
j
. Moreover, the scalar maps

(r,\rho)\mapsto\eta(r;\rho),\qquad(r,\rho)\mapsto v_{B}(r;\rho)

(174)

are empirically stable on compact precision intervals. Specifically, whenever

\|\bm{r}_{N}-\bm{r}^{\prime}_{N}\|_{N}\to 0,\qquad\|\bm{\rho}_{N}-\bm{\rho}^{\prime}_{N}\|_{N}\to 0,

(175)

with all entries of
\bm{\rho}_{N},\bm{\rho}^{\prime}_{N}
lying in a compact
positive interval, we have

\left\|\bm{\eta}(\bm{r}_{N};\bm{\rho}_{N})-\bm{\eta}(\bm{r}^{\prime}_{N};\bm{\rho}^{\prime}_{N})\right\|_{N}\to 0,

(176)

and

\left\|\bm{v}_{B}(\bm{r}_{N};\bm{\rho}_{N})-\bm{v}_{B}(\bm{r}^{\prime}_{N};\bm{\rho}^{\prime}_{N})\right\|_{N}\to 0.

(177)

Assumption
5
is a replacement-stability condition only; it is not used to justify Gaussian conditioning. It is satisfied by many standard priors after the usual variance projection. In particular, finite-alphabet and bounded-support priors lead to
regular scalar posterior mean and variance maps on compact precision intervals.

The corrected branch also requires that the Gaussian regression used to remove
the predictable memory component be well conditioned. This condition is not a
numerical artifact; it means that the past Gaussian-history directions retained
by the regression are not asymptotically degenerate.

Assumption 6

(Stable memory regression)
.

For the corrected branch and every fixed
T
, the Gaussian-history covariance
matrices satisfy the following regularity condition. After removing exactly
redundant zero directions by the Moore–Penrose inverse, the nonzero spectrum
of

\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,<t}

(178)

is uniformly bounded away from zero: there exists
c_{\zeta}>0
such that

\lambda_{\min}^{+}\!\left(\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,<t}\right)\geq c_{\zeta}

(179)

for all
t\leq T
and all relevant coordinates
j
, with probability tending to
one. Equivalently,

\left\|(\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}\right\|\leq c_{\zeta}^{-1}

(180)

on the retained Gaussian-history subspace. In addition, the innovation
variances of the corrected scalar channels satisfy

0<\tau_{\min}\leq\tau_{t,j}\leq\tau_{\max}<\infty

(181)

for all fixed
t\leq T
and all relevant
j
, with probability tending to one.

Assumption
6
excludes only nearly redundant
Gaussian-history regressors. Such degeneracies are not the phenomenon studied
here. Our purpose is to remove the nonzero predictable memory generated by the
variance profile; for this operation to be stable, the corresponding finite
Gaussian regression must be well conditioned.

VII-B

Actual and Predictable Recursions

We compare two recursions. The superscript “act” refers to the actual
finite-dimensional adaptive diagonal EP recursion, while the superscript
“orc” refers to the MDE-predictable recursion analyzed by
Theorem
3
. The word “oracle” here does not mean that
additional observations are given to the algorithm; it only indicates that the
finite-dimensional retained variances are replaced by their MDE-predictable
counterparts.

At iteration
t
, the actual linear module uses

\bm{C}_{t}^{\mathrm{act}}=\left(\gamma_{w}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\bm{\Gamma}_{t}^{\mathrm{act}}\right)^{-1},

(182)

and

\bm{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{act}}=\bm{C}_{t}^{\mathrm{act}}\left(\gamma_{w}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{w}+\bm{\Gamma}_{t}^{\mathrm{act}}\bm{q}_{t}^{\mathrm{act}}\right).

(183)

Its retained diagonal variance is

d_{t,j}^{\mathrm{act}}=[\bm{C}_{t}^{\mathrm{act}}]_{jj},

(184)

and the outgoing cavity precision is

\pi_{t,j}^{\mathrm{act}}=\operatorname{Proj}_{[\pi_{\min},\pi_{\max}]}\left((d_{t,j}^{\mathrm{act}})^{-1}-\gamma_{t,j}^{\mathrm{act}}\right).

(185)

The predictable recursion has the same finite-dimensional linear estimate
with
\bm{\Gamma}_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}}
and

\bm{q}_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}}
, namely

\bm{C}_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}}=\left(\gamma_{w}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\bm{\Gamma}_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}}\right)^{-1},

(186)

\bm{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}}=\bm{C}_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}}\left(\gamma_{w}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{w}+\bm{\Gamma}_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}}\bm{q}_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}}\right),

(187)

but its diagonal cavity precision is generated from the MDE response:

\pi_{t,j}^{\mathrm{orc}}=\operatorname{Proj}_{[\pi_{\min},\pi_{\max}]}\left((T_{t,j}^{\mathrm{orc}})^{-1}-\gamma_{t,j}^{\mathrm{orc}}\right).

(188)

The corresponding cavity error is

h_{t,j}^{\mathrm{orc}}=(\pi_{t,j}^{\mathrm{orc}})^{-1}\left((T_{t,j}^{\mathrm{orc}})^{-1}m_{t,j}^{\mathrm{orc}}-\gamma_{t,j}^{\mathrm{orc}}q_{t,j}^{\mathrm{orc}}\right).

(189)

The actual cavity is defined in the same way as in Section
III
,
using
d_{t,j}^{\mathrm{act}}
and
\pi_{t,j}^{\mathrm{act}}
.

The two branches differ only in the scalar input fed to the prior module. In
the standard branch,

s_{t,j}^{\mathrm{std}}=h_{t,j},\qquad\rho_{t,j}^{\mathrm{std}}=\pi_{t,j}.

(190)

In the corrected branch,

s_{t,j}^{\mathrm{corr}}=h_{t,j}-\mu_{t,j},\qquad\rho_{t,j}^{\mathrm{corr}}=\tau_{t,j}^{-1}.

(191)

Thus a branch
b\in\{\mathrm{std},\mathrm{corr}\}
is described by a scalar
input pair

(s_{t,j}^{b},\rho_{t,j}^{b})

(192)

and the prior module applies

\widehat{x}_{B,t,j}^{b}=\eta(x_{j}+s_{t,j}^{b};\rho_{t,j}^{b}),

(193)

p_{t,j}^{b}=\widehat{x}_{B,t,j}^{b}-x_{j},

(194)

v_{B,t,j}^{b}=v_{B}(x_{j}+s_{t,j}^{b};\rho_{t,j}^{b}).

(195)

The outgoing precision and mean error are then

\displaystyle\gamma_{t+1,j}^{b}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Proj}_{[\gamma_{\min},\gamma_{\max}]}\left((v_{B,t,j}^{b})^{-1}-\rho_{t,j}^{b}\right),

(196)

\displaystyle\gamma_{t+1,j}^{b}q_{t+1,j}^{b}

\displaystyle=(v_{B,t,j}^{b})^{-1}p_{t,j}^{b}-\rho_{t,j}^{b}s_{t,j}^{b}.

(197)

The goal is to prove, for each fixed branch
b
and each fixed
t\leq T
,

\left\|\bm{\gamma}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{act}}-\bm{\gamma}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{orc}}\right\|_{N}\to 0,\qquad\left\|\bm{q}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{act}}-\bm{q}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{orc}}\right\|_{N}\to 0

(198)

in probability.

VII-C

Stability of the Linear Module

The first step is a deterministic perturbation bound for the linear Gaussian
module. Its proof is based on two identities: one for the posterior mean and
one for the covariance resolvent.

Proposition 9

(Linear-module stability)
.

Assume that, for some fixed
t
,

\left\|\bm{q}_{t}^{\mathrm{act}}-\bm{q}_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}}\right\|_{N}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0,\qquad\left\|\bm{\gamma}_{t}^{\mathrm{act}}-\bm{\gamma}_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}}\right\|_{N}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

(199)

Then

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{act}}-\bm{m}_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}}\right\|_{N}

\displaystyle\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0,

(200)

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{d}_{t}^{\mathrm{act}}-\bm{T}_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}}\right\|_{N}

\displaystyle\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0,

(201)

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\pi}_{t}^{\mathrm{act}}-\bm{\pi}_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}}\right\|_{N}

\displaystyle\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0,

(202)

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{h}_{t}^{\mathrm{act}}-\bm{h}_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}}\right\|_{N}

\displaystyle\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

(203)

Proof:

The detailed empirical-norm estimates are given in
Appendix
J
. We indicate the two identities on
which the proof rests. Let

\bm{C}_{a}=\bm{C}_{t}^{\mathrm{act}},\qquad\bm{C}_{o}=\bm{C}_{t}^{\mathrm{orc}},

(204)

and similarly write

\bm{\Gamma}_{a},\bm{\Gamma}_{o},\bm{q}_{a},\bm{q}_{o}
.
Using the normal equations for the two linear posterior means gives

\bm{m}_{a}-\bm{m}_{o}=\bm{C}_{a}\left[\bm{\Gamma}_{a}(\bm{q}_{a}-\bm{q}_{o})+(\bm{\Gamma}_{a}-\bm{\Gamma}_{o})(\bm{q}_{o}-\bm{m}_{o})\right].

(205)

Since the diagonal loadings are bounded below by
\gamma_{\min}
,

\|\bm{C}_{a}\|
is uniformly bounded. Together with admissibility of the
oracle history and (
199
), this yields
(
200
).

For the covariance matrices,

\bm{C}_{a}-\bm{C}_{o}=-\bm{C}_{a}(\bm{\Gamma}_{a}-\bm{\Gamma}_{o})\bm{C}_{o}.

(206)

The right-hand side is controlled in normalized Frobenius norm because

\bm{\Gamma}_{a}-\bm{\Gamma}_{o}
is diagonal and

N^{-1/2}\|\bm{\Gamma}_{a}-\bm{\Gamma}_{o}\|_{F}=\|\bm{\gamma}_{a}-\bm{\gamma}_{o}\|_{N}.

(207)

Combining this bound with the MDE response

d_{t,j}^{\mathrm{orc}}=T_{t,j}^{\mathrm{orc}}+o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1)
yields
(
201
). The projection map and the inverse map are
Lipschitz on compact positive intervals, which gives
(
202
); substituting the stable quantities into the
cavity formula gives (
203
).
∎

Proof:

VIII
Conclusion

This paper studied diagonal expectation propagation under variance-profile Gaussian measurements. The main finding is that variance profiles do not destroy the Gaussian nature of the linear-module output, but they generally destroy its freshness. After conditioning on the finite linear history, the linear module produces a coordinate-dependent Gaussian process rather than a fresh scalar Gaussian channel. The standard diagonal EP cavity removes the instantaneous response of the incoming message, but it may leave a predictable memory component inherited from previous residuals.

We characterized this effect through a conditioned matrix-Dyson-equation response and a Schur-complement representation of the linear module. A Gaussian-regression decomposition then identifies the innovation part of the residual process and yields an oracle state-evolution-level correction. Thus, under a general variance profile, the natural limiting object for diagonal EP is a Gaussian-process dynamics with profile-dependent memory rather than a conventional scalar fresh-noise state evolution.

Several questions remain open. The most important one is algorithmic: the memory correction in this paper is an oracle decoupling device, and practical procedures for estimating and removing the memory term from finite-dimensional iterates remain to be developed. Other natural directions include extensions to non-Gaussian or sparse variance-profile matrices, and the study of fixed-point stability and variational interpretations of the resulting memory-aware EP dynamics.

Appendix A
Auxiliary Probability and Empirical-Convergence Lemmas

This appendix collects several probability lemmas used throughout the proof.
They are independent of the EP recursion and of the particular random-matrix
linearization. Their role is to justify empirical pseudo-Lipschitz convergence
from weak Gaussian dependence, to control truncation errors, and to record the
Gaussian regression identity used in the memory decomposition.

A-A

Pseudo-Lipschitz Growth and Truncation

We first record a standard polynomial growth consequence of the
pseudo-Lipschitz condition.

Lemma 3

(Polynomial growth of pseudo-Lipschitz functions)
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be pseudo-Lipschitz of order
k\geq 1
.
Then there exists a constant
C_{\psi}<\infty
such that

|\psi(\bm{x})|\leq C_{\psi}(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{k}),\qquad\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Moreover, for every
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(1+\|\bm{x}+\bm{y}\|^{k})\leq C_{k}(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{k}+\|\bm{y}\|^{k})

for a constant
C_{k}
depending only on
k
.

Proof:

Taking
\bm{y}=\bm{0}
in the definition of pseudo-Lipschitz
continuity gives

|\psi(\bm{x})|\leq|\psi(\bm{0})|+L\|\bm{x}\|\left(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{k-1}\right).

Since
\|\bm{x}\|\leq 1+\|\bm{x}\|^{k}
for
k\geq 1
, the first
claim follows.

For the second claim, use

\|\bm{x}+\bm{y}\|\leq\|\bm{x}\|+\|\bm{y}\|

and the convexity inequality

(a+b)^{k}\leq 2^{k-1}(a^{k}+b^{k}),\qquad a,b\geq 0.

Absorbing constants gives the result.
∎

The following truncation lemma is used to pass from bounded Lipschitz test
functions to pseudo-Lipschitz functions of order two.

Lemma 4

(Uniform truncation for
\mathrm{PL}(2)
functions)
.

Let
\{\bm{\xi}_{j,N}:1\leq j\leq N\}
be a triangular array of random
vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying, for some
\epsilon>0
,

\sup_{j,N}\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\xi}_{j,N}\|^{4+\epsilon}<\infty.

Let
\psi\in\mathrm{PL}(2)
. Then

\lim_{K\to\infty}\sup_{N}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[|\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})|\bm{1}\{\|\bm{\xi}_{j,N}\|>K\}\right]=0.

Consequently, if
\psi_{K}
is any sequence of bounded Lipschitz functions
satisfying
\psi_{K}(\bm{x})=\psi(\bm{x})
for

\|\bm{x}\|\leq K
and

|\psi_{K}(\bm{x})|\leq C(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{2})

uniformly in
K
, then

\lim_{K\to\infty}\sup_{N}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}|\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})-\psi_{K}(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})|=0.

Proof:

By Lemma
3
, there is
C_{\psi}<\infty
such that

|\psi(\bm{x})|\leq C_{\psi}(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{2}).

Fix
K>0
. By Hölder’s inequality, with

r=\frac{4+\epsilon}{2}>1,\qquad r^{\prime}=\frac{r}{r-1},

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(1+\|\bm{\xi}_{j,N}\|^{2})\bm{1}\{\|\bm{\xi}_{j,N}\|>K\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\mathbb{E}(1+\|\bm{\xi}_{j,N}\|^{2})^{r}\right)^{1/r}\mathbb{P}(\|\bm{\xi}_{j,N}\|>K)^{1/r^{\prime}}.

The first factor is uniformly bounded by the assumed

(4+\epsilon)
-moment condition. The second factor converges to zero uniformly
in
j,N
by Markov’s inequality:

\mathbb{P}(\|\bm{\xi}_{j,N}\|>K)\leq K^{-(4+\epsilon)}\sup_{j,N}\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\xi}_{j,N}\|^{4+\epsilon}.

This proves the first claim. The second claim follows from

|\psi(\bm{x})-\psi_{K}(\bm{x})|\leq C(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{2})\bm{1}\{\|\bm{x}\|>K\}.

∎

A-B

Gaussian Covariance Comparison

The next lemma is a convenient covariance comparison for smooth functions of
jointly Gaussian vectors. It is the basic tool behind the weakly dependent
Gaussian empirical law.

Lemma 5

(Gaussian covariance interpolation)
.

Let
(\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta})
be a centered jointly Gaussian vector in

\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let

\bm{\Sigma}_{\xi\eta}=\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta}).

If
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
are continuously differentiable with bounded
gradients, then

\left|\operatorname{Cov}(f(\bm{\xi}),g(\bm{\eta}))\right|\leq\|\nabla f\|_{\infty}\|\nabla g\|_{\infty}\|\bm{\Sigma}_{\xi\eta}\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

More generally, if
\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta}
have dimensions

d_{1},d_{2}
, then the right-hand side becomes

\|\nabla f\|_{\infty}\|\nabla g\|_{\infty}\|\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta})\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Proof:

We give the proof for equal dimensions; the rectangular case is identical.
Let
(\bm{\xi}_{0},\bm{\eta}_{0})
be a Gaussian pair with the same
marginal distributions as
(\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta})
but with

\bm{\xi}_{0}
independent of
\bm{\eta}_{0}
. For
s\in[0,1]
, let

(\bm{\xi}_{s},\bm{\eta}_{s})
be a centered Gaussian pair with the
same marginal covariances and cross-covariance

\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{\xi}_{s},\bm{\eta}_{s})=s\bm{\Sigma}_{\xi\eta}.

Define

\Phi(s)=\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{\xi}_{s})g(\bm{\eta}_{s})].

The Gaussian interpolation identity gives

\Phi^{\prime}(s)=\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla f(\bm{\xi}_{s})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\xi\eta}\nabla g(\bm{\eta}_{s})\right].

Since

\Phi(1)-\Phi(0)=\operatorname{Cov}(f(\bm{\xi}),g(\bm{\eta})),

we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\operatorname{Cov}(f(\bm{\xi}),g(\bm{\eta}))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left|\nabla f(\bm{\xi}_{s})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\xi\eta}\nabla g(\bm{\eta}_{s})\right|ds

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla f\|_{\infty}\|\nabla g\|_{\infty}\|\bm{\Sigma}_{\xi\eta}\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

∎

A-C

Weakly Dependent Gaussian Empirical Law

We now prove the empirical convergence result used after the empirical Gaussian
decoupling argument.

Lemma 6

(Weakly dependent Gaussian empirical law)
.

Let

\{\bm{\xi}_{j,N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:1\leq j\leq N\}

be a triangular array of jointly Gaussian random vectors. Suppose that, for
some
\epsilon>0
,

\sup_{j,N}\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\xi}_{j,N}\|^{4+\epsilon}<\infty,

and

\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j\neq k}\left\|\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{\xi}_{j,N},\bm{\xi}_{k,N})\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\longrightarrow 0.

(228)

Then, for every
\psi\in\mathrm{PL}(2)
,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof:

We first prove the result for bounded continuously differentiable functions
with bounded gradient. Set

Y_{j,N}=\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})-\mathbb{E}\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N}).

Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\operatorname{Var}(\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N}))

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j\neq k}\operatorname{Cov}\big(\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N}),\psi(\bm{\xi}_{k,N})\big).

The diagonal term is
O(N^{-1})
because
\psi
is bounded. For the off-diagonal
term, Lemma
5
yields

\left|\operatorname{Cov}\big(\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N}),\psi(\bm{\xi}_{k,N})\big)\right|\leq\|\nabla\psi\|_{\infty}^{2}\left\|\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{\xi}_{j,N},\bm{\xi}_{k,N})\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Therefore (
228
) implies that the off-diagonal
contribution vanishes. Hence the variance of the empirical average converges
to zero, and Chebyshev’s inequality gives convergence in probability.

The same conclusion holds for bounded Lipschitz functions. Indeed, every
bounded Lipschitz function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be approximated uniformly by
bounded smooth functions with gradients bounded by a constant depending only on
the Lipschitz constant. Applying the previous paragraph to the smooth
approximants and letting the approximation error vanish proves the bounded
Lipschitz case.

Let now
\psi\in\mathrm{PL}(2)
. Choose a smooth cutoff
\chi_{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]

such that
\chi_{K}(\bm{x})=1
when
\|\bm{x}\|\leq K
and

\chi_{K}(\bm{x})=0
when
\|\bm{x}\|\geq 2K
, and define

\psi_{K}(\bm{x})=\psi(\bm{x})\chi_{K}(\bm{x}).

For fixed
K
,
\psi_{K}
is bounded Lipschitz, so

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{K}(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\psi_{K}(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

By Lemma
4
,

\lim_{K\to\infty}\sup_{N}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}|\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})-\psi_{K}(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})|=0.

Markov’s inequality gives the corresponding convergence of the empirical
truncation error in probability. Therefore, letting first
N\to\infty
and
then
K\to\infty
, the desired convergence follows.
∎

A-D

Gaussian Regression with a Singular Covariance

The memory decomposition uses Gaussian regression onto a finite past history.
Since some past directions may be exactly redundant, the covariance matrix need
not be invertible. The Moore–Penrose inverse removes those redundant
directions.

Lemma 7

(Gaussian regression with Moore–Penrose inverse)
.

Let

\begin{pmatrix}\bm{Z}\\
Z\end{pmatrix}

be a centered jointly Gaussian vector with

\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{Z})=\bm{\Sigma},\qquad\operatorname{Cov}(Z,\bm{Z})=\bm{c}.

Then

Z=\bm{c}\bm{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\bm{Z}+G,

where
G
is centered Gaussian and independent of
\bm{Z}
. Moreover,

\operatorname{Var}(G)=\operatorname{Var}(Z)-\bm{c}\bm{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\bm{c}^{\mathsf{T}}.

Proof:

Define

G=Z-\bm{c}\bm{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\bm{Z}.

Since
(Z,\bm{Z})
is jointly Gaussian,
(G,\bm{Z})
is also
jointly Gaussian. It is therefore enough to show that

\operatorname{Cov}(G,\bm{Z})=\bm{0}.

We compute

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(G,\bm{Z})

\displaystyle=\bm{c}-\bm{c}\bm{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{Z},\bm{Z})

\displaystyle=\bm{c}-\bm{c}\bm{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\bm{\Sigma}.

For a valid covariance matrix of
(Z,\bm{Z})
, the row vector

\bm{c}
lies in the row space of
\bm{\Sigma}
. To see this, if

\bm{v}\in\ker(\bm{\Sigma})
, then

\operatorname{Var}(\bm{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{Z})=\bm{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{v}=0,

so
\bm{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{Z}=0
almost surely. Hence

\operatorname{Cov}(Z,\bm{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{Z})=0,

which gives
\bm{c}\bm{v}=0
. Thus

\bm{c}
is orthogonal to
\ker(\bm{\Sigma})
, i.e., it lies in
the row space of
\bm{\Sigma}
. Therefore

\bm{c}\bm{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{c},

and
\operatorname{Cov}(G,\bm{Z})=0
. Since
(G,\bm{Z})
is
jointly Gaussian, zero covariance implies independence.

Finally,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(G)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}(Z)-2\bm{c}\bm{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\bm{c}^{\mathsf{T}}+\bm{c}\bm{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\bm{c}^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}(Z)-\bm{c}\bm{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\bm{c}^{\mathsf{T}},

because
\bm{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\Sigma}^{\dagger}=\bm{\Sigma}^{\dagger}
.
∎

A-E

Gaussian Maximum and No-Spike Bounds

We finally record a simple Gaussian maximum bound used to verify the no-spike
part of admissibility.

Lemma 8

(Gaussian maximum bound)
.

Let
\bm{g}_{N}=(g_{1,N},\ldots,g_{N,N})^{\mathsf{T}}
be a centered
Gaussian vector, not necessarily with independent entries. Suppose that

\max_{1\leq j\leq N}\operatorname{Var}(g_{j,N})\leq C

uniformly in
N
. Then

\frac{\|\bm{g}_{N}\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{N}}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

If, in addition,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\operatorname{Var}(g_{j,N})=O(1),

then

\frac{1}{N}\|\bm{g}_{N}\|^{2}=O_{p}(1).

Proof:

For any
\epsilon>0
, the union bound and the Gaussian tail inequality give

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\|\bm{g}_{N}\|_{\infty}>\epsilon\sqrt{N})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}(|g_{j,N}|>\epsilon\sqrt{N})

\displaystyle\leq 2N\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}N}{2C}\right),

which converges to zero. This proves the no-spike claim.

For the energy bound,

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\|\bm{g}_{N}\|^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\operatorname{Var}(g_{j,N})=O(1).

Markov’s inequality then gives

\frac{1}{N}\|\bm{g}_{N}\|^{2}=O_{p}(1).

∎

Lemma 9

(Stability of empirical
\mathrm{PL}(2)
averages under

o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}
perturbations)
.

Let
\{\bm{\xi}_{j,N}\}_{j=1}^{N}
and

\{\bm{\eta}_{j,N}\}_{j=1}^{N}
be arrays in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|\bm{\xi}_{j,N}-\bm{\eta}_{j,N}\|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Assume further that, for some
\epsilon>0
,

\sup_{N}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\xi}_{j,N}\|^{4+\epsilon}<\infty,\qquad\sup_{N}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\eta}_{j,N}\|^{4+\epsilon}<\infty.

Then, for every
\psi\in\mathrm{PL}(2)
,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})-\psi(\bm{\eta}_{j,N})\right|\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof:

By the pseudo-Lipschitz property,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|\psi(\bm{\xi}_{j,N})-\psi(\bm{\eta}_{j,N})\right|

\displaystyle\leq L\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|\bm{\xi}_{j,N}-\bm{\eta}_{j,N}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(1+\|\bm{\xi}_{j,N}\|+\|\bm{\eta}_{j,N}\|)^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where we used Cauchy–Schwarz. The first factor converges to zero in
probability by assumption. The second factor is tight by the uniform moment
bounds. The product therefore converges to zero in probability.
∎

Appendix B
Gaussian Conditioning under EP Histories

This appendix proves the conditional Gaussian representation used in
Proposition
2
. The proof is purely
Gaussian. The only algorithm-specific input is that the finite EP history
imposes the two linear constraints

\bm{A}\bm{M}_{t}=\bm{w}\bm{1}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{U}_{t}

and

\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{U}_{t}=\gamma_{w}^{-1}\left(\bm{\Gamma}_{0}(\bm{m}_{0}-\bm{q}_{0}),\ldots,\bm{\Gamma}_{t-1}(\bm{m}_{t-1}-\bm{q}_{t-1})\right).

Once the finite history is fixed, these are linear observations of the
variance-profile Gaussian matrix. Conditioning a Gaussian vector on finitely
many linear observations gives another Gaussian vector. The covariance
contraction of this conditional Gaussian law is the source of the flat
covariance bound used later in the MDE input.

B-A

Linear Form of the EP History

Let

\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}

be a variance-profile Gaussian matrix,

A_{ij}=\sqrt{\frac{s_{ij}}{M}}Z_{ij},\qquad Z_{ij}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Define the vectorized matrix

\bm{a}=\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A})\in\mathbb{R}^{MN}.

Then

\bm{a}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{A}),

where

\bm{\Sigma}_{A}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{s_{ij}}{M}\right)_{1\leq i\leq M,\ 1\leq j\leq N}.

The uniformly elliptic profile assumption implies

\frac{s_{\min}}{M}\bm{I}_{MN}\preceq\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\preceq\frac{s_{\max}}{M}\bm{I}_{MN}.

Fix an iteration index
t
. The past signal-side and measurement-side
histories are

\bm{M}_{t}=(\bm{m}_{0},\ldots,\bm{m}_{t-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times t},

and

\bm{U}_{t}=(\bm{u}_{0},\ldots,\bm{u}_{t-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times t}.

The first history constraint is

\bm{A}\bm{M}_{t}=\bm{R}_{t},\qquad\bm{R}_{t}:=\bm{w}\bm{1}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{U}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times t}.

(229)

The second history constraint is

\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{U}_{t}=\bm{B}_{t},

(230)

where

\bm{B}_{t}:=\gamma_{w}^{-1}\left(\bm{\Gamma}_{0}(\bm{m}_{0}-\bm{q}_{0}),\ldots,\bm{\Gamma}_{t-1}(\bm{m}_{t-1}-\bm{q}_{t-1})\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times t}.

Both mappings

\bm{A}\mapsto\bm{A}\bm{M}_{t},\qquad\bm{A}\mapsto\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{U}_{t}

are linear in
\bm{A}
. Therefore, for fixed

\bm{M}_{t},\bm{U}_{t},\bm{R}_{t},\bm{B}_{t}
, there is
a deterministic matrix
\bm{L}_{t}
and a vector
\bm{b}_{t}
such
that the two constraints can be written compactly as

\bm{L}_{t}\bm{a}=\bm{b}_{t}.

(231)

For example, the first constraint contributes

\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A}\bm{M}_{t})=(\bm{M}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\bm{I}_{M})\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A}),

and the second constraint is represented similarly after applying the
commutation matrix relating
\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A})
and

\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}})
. The explicit Kronecker form
is not important; what matters is the linearity of
(
231
).

We shall condition on a regular finite history satisfying
(
229
)–(
230
). Redundant
constraints are allowed. They are handled by Moore–Penrose inverses below.

B-B

Conditional Gaussian Decomposition

We now compute the conditional law of
\bm{a}
given

\bm{L}_{t}\bm{a}=\bm{b}_{t}
.

Lemma 10

(Gaussian conditioning under the EP history)
.

Conditionally on the finite linear history

\bm{L}_{t}\bm{a}=\bm{b}_{t},

the vector
\bm{a}
admits the decomposition

\bm{a}=\bm{a}_{\parallel,t}+\bm{a}_{\perp,t},

where

\bm{a}_{\parallel,t}=\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{L}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{t}

is the conditional mean, and

\bm{a}_{\perp,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{A|t})

with conditional covariance

\bm{\Sigma}_{A|t}=\bm{\Sigma}_{A}-\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{L}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger}\bm{L}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}.

(232)

Moreover,

\bm{0}\preceq\bm{\Sigma}_{A|t}\preceq\bm{\Sigma}_{A}.

(233)

Finally,

\bm{L}_{t}\bm{a}_{\perp,t}=\bm{0}\quad\text{almost surely}.

Proof:

Since
\bm{a}
is Gaussian and

\bm{L}_{t}\bm{a}
is a linear transform of

\bm{a}
, the pair

(\bm{a},\bm{L}_{t}\bm{a})

is jointly Gaussian. The covariance matrices are

\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{a},\bm{L}_{t}\bm{a})=\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}},

and

\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{L}_{t}\bm{a})=\bm{L}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}.

The standard conditional Gaussian formula, with the Moore–Penrose inverse used
to allow redundant constraints, gives the conditional mean

\mathbb{E}[\bm{a}\mid\bm{L}_{t}\bm{a}=\bm{b}_{t}]=\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{L}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{t}

and the conditional covariance (
232
).
This proves the Gaussian decomposition.

It remains to prove the covariance contraction. Define

\bm{C}_{t}=\bm{L}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}^{1/2}.

Then

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{A|t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{A}^{1/2}\left[\bm{I}-\bm{C}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{C}_{t}\bm{C}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger}\bm{C}_{t}\right]\bm{\Sigma}_{A}^{1/2}.

The matrix

\bm{P}_{t}=\bm{C}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{C}_{t}\bm{C}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger}\bm{C}_{t}

is the orthogonal projector onto the row space of
\bm{C}_{t}
.
Therefore

\bm{0}\preceq\bm{P}_{t}\preceq\bm{I},\qquad\bm{0}\preceq\bm{I}-\bm{P}_{t}\preceq\bm{I},

which implies

\bm{0}\preceq\bm{\Sigma}_{A|t}\preceq\bm{\Sigma}_{A}.

Finally, since the conditional law is supported on the affine subspace

\{\bm{a}:\bm{L}_{t}\bm{a}=\bm{b}_{t}\},

the residual

\bm{a}_{\perp,t}=\bm{a}-\bm{a}_{\parallel,t}

lies in the corresponding homogeneous subspace:

\bm{L}_{t}\bm{a}_{\perp,t}=\bm{0}

almost surely. This can also be checked algebraically from
(
232
), since

\bm{L}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{A|t}\bm{L}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}=\bm{0}.

∎

Returning to matrix notation, let

\bm{A}_{\parallel,t}=\operatorname{mat}(\bm{a}_{\parallel,t}),\qquad\bm{A}_{\perp,t}=\operatorname{mat}(\bm{a}_{\perp,t}),

where
\operatorname{mat}
is the inverse of the chosen vectorization. Then
Lemma
10
gives

\bm{A}=\bm{A}_{\parallel,t}+\bm{A}_{\perp,t}.

(234)

Furthermore, the residual matrix satisfies the homogeneous history constraints

\bm{A}_{\perp,t}\bm{M}_{t}=\bm{0},\qquad\bm{A}_{\perp,t}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{U}_{t}=\bm{0}

almost surely under the conditional law.

B-C

Flat Covariance of the Conditioned Residual

We next prove the flat covariance estimate used in the MDE input. The bound
is a direct consequence of the covariance contraction
(
233
).

Lemma 11

(Flat covariance of the conditioned residual)
.

Let
\bm{A}_{\perp,t}
be the centered Gaussian residual in
(
234
). For any conditioning-measurable vectors

\bm{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and

\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\operatorname{Var}\left(\bm{p}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}_{\perp,t}\bm{q}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)\leq\frac{s_{\max}}{M}\|\bm{p}\|^{2}\|\bm{q}\|^{2}.

(235)

Consequently, if

\bm{X}_{t}=\sqrt{\gamma_{w}}\bm{A}_{\perp,t},

then

\operatorname{Var}\left(\bm{p}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{X}_{t}\bm{q}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)\leq\frac{\gamma_{w}s_{\max}}{M}\|\bm{p}\|^{2}\|\bm{q}\|^{2}.

(236)

Proof:

Let

\bm{h}=\operatorname{vec}(\bm{p}\bm{q}^{\mathsf{T}}).

With the convention

\bm{p}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}_{\perp,t}\bm{q}=\bm{h}^{\mathsf{T}}\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A}_{\perp,t}),

we have

\operatorname{Var}\left(\bm{p}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}_{\perp,t}\bm{q}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)=\bm{h}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}_{A|t}\bm{h}.

Using

\bm{\Sigma}_{A|t}\preceq\bm{\Sigma}_{A},

we get

\displaystyle\bm{h}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}_{A|t}\bm{h}

\displaystyle\leq\bm{h}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{h}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{s_{ij}}{M}p_{i}^{2}q_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{s_{\max}}{M}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}p_{i}^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{j}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{s_{\max}}{M}\|\bm{p}\|^{2}\|\bm{q}\|^{2}.

This proves (
235
). Multiplying the residual matrix
by
\sqrt{\gamma_{w}}
gives (
236
).
∎

B-D

Conditional Entrywise Covariance and Profile Bounds

For later use in the covariance-kernel computation, we also record the
entrywise form of the conditional covariance. Define

\kappa_{t}(ia,kb)=\operatorname{Cov}\left(\sqrt{\gamma_{w}}A_{\perp,t,ia},\sqrt{\gamma_{w}}A_{\perp,t,kb}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right),\qquad 1\leq i,k\leq M,\quad 1\leq a,b\leq N.

Then
\kappa_{t}
is the covariance kernel used in the conditioned Gaussian
linearization. The covariance contraction implies, in particular,

\operatorname{Var}\left(A_{\perp,t,ia}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)\leq\frac{s_{ia}}{M}\leq\frac{s_{\max}}{M}.

Therefore

|\kappa_{t}(ia,kb)|\leq\frac{\gamma_{w}s_{\max}}{M}

whenever the covariance is evaluated through Cauchy–Schwarz. More generally,
for deterministic or conditioning-measurable arrays

\bm{R}=(R_{ia})
and
\bm{T}=(T_{ia})
,

\operatorname{Cov}\left(\sum_{i,a}R_{ia}\sqrt{\gamma_{w}}A_{\perp,t,ia},\sum_{k,b}T_{kb}\sqrt{\gamma_{w}}A_{\perp,t,kb}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)

is controlled by the flat covariance estimate
(
236
) whenever the arrays are rank-one,

R_{ia}=p_{i}q_{a}
,
T_{ia}=p^{\prime}_{i}q^{\prime}_{a}
. This is precisely the class of
bilinear forms needed in the MDE input and in the conditioned covariance
calculation.

Appendix C
Bounded Deformation and MDE Input Verification

This appendix proves Proposition
3
. The
regularized correlated-Gaussian MDE input itself is stated in
Lemma
2
. The purpose of the present appendix is
only to verify that the random matrix obtained after conditioning on the finite
EP history satisfies the hypotheses of that input.

The proof has two parts. First, we show that the conditional mean

\bm{A}_{\parallel,t}
is a bounded deformation:

\|\bm{A}_{\parallel,t}\|=O_{p}(1).

Second, we check that the residual block matrix is centered Gaussian with flat
covariance and that the block loading is uniformly regularized. These facts
allow Lemma
2
to be applied conditionally on the
EP history.

C-A

Conditioned Block Linearization

By Appendix
B
, conditioning on the
finite EP history gives

\bm{A}=\bm{A}_{\parallel,t}+\bm{A}_{\perp,t},

(237)

where

\bm{A}_{\parallel,t}=\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]

and
\bm{A}_{\perp,t}
is centered Gaussian conditionally on

\mathcal{F}_{t}
. Moreover, for all conditioning-measurable

\bm{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and

\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\operatorname{Var}\left(\bm{p}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}_{\perp,t}\bm{q}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)\leq\frac{s_{\max}}{M}\|\bm{p}\|^{2}\|\bm{q}\|^{2}.

Define

\bm{X}_{t}=\sqrt{\gamma_{w}}\bm{A}_{\perp,t}.

Then

\operatorname{Var}\left(\bm{p}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{X}_{t}\bm{q}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)\leq\frac{\gamma_{w}s_{\max}}{M}\|\bm{p}\|^{2}\|\bm{q}\|^{2}.

(238)

Thus the conditioned residual has the flat covariance required by the MDE
input.

For the
t
th linear module, the block linearization is

\bm{K}_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{I}_{M}&\sqrt{\gamma_{w}}\bm{A}\\
\sqrt{\gamma_{w}}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}&-\bm{\Gamma}_{t}\end{pmatrix}.

Using (
237
), write

\bm{K}_{t}=\bm{D}_{t}+\bm{W}_{t},

where

\bm{D}_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{I}_{M}&\sqrt{\gamma_{w}}\bm{A}_{\parallel,t}\\
\sqrt{\gamma_{w}}\bm{A}_{\parallel,t}^{\mathsf{T}}&-\bm{\Gamma}_{t}\end{pmatrix},

and

\bm{W}_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{X}_{t}\\
\bm{X}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}&\bm{0}\end{pmatrix}.

(239)

The rest of the appendix verifies that
\bm{D}_{t}
is bounded and that

\bm{W}_{t}
satisfies the Gaussian flatness assumptions.

C-B

Boundedness of the Conditional Mean Deformation

The only nontrivial point is to prove

\|\bm{A}_{\parallel,t}\|=O_{p}(1).

The proof uses the fact that
t
is fixed. Although the history constraints
contain
Mt+Nt
scalar equations, they are generated by finitely many
signal-side and measurement-side history directions. After removing
asymptotically redundant directions, their normalized Gram matrices are
well-conditioned on the regularity event
\mathcal{R}_{t}
.

We make this precise through the following representation lemma.

Lemma 12

(Finite-history regression representation)
.

On the regularity event
\mathcal{R}_{t}
, the conditional mean

\bm{A}_{\parallel,t}
admits a representation of the form

\bm{A}_{\parallel,t}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_{t}}c_{\ell,t}\frac{1}{M}\operatorname{diag}(\bm{p}_{\ell,t})\bm{S}\operatorname{diag}(\bm{q}_{\ell,t}),

(240)

where

\bm{S}=(s_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq M,\,1\leq j\leq N}

is the variance-profile matrix,
L_{t}=O(1)
for fixed
t
, and

c_{\ell,t}=O_{p}(1),\qquad\|\bm{p}_{\ell,t}\|_{M}=O_{p}(1),\qquad\|\bm{q}_{\ell,t}\|_{N}=O_{p}(1).

(241)

Here

\|\bm{p}\|_{M}^{2}=\frac{1}{M}\|\bm{p}\|^{2},\qquad\|\bm{q}\|_{N}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\|\bm{q}\|^{2}.

The vectors
\bm{p}_{\ell,t}
are finite linear combinations of
measurement-side history vectors such as

\bm{w}-\bm{u}^{s}
and
\bm{u}^{s}
, while the vectors

\bm{q}_{\ell,t}
are finite linear combinations of signal-side
history vectors such as
\bm{e}_{2}^{s}
,

\widehat{\bm{e}}_{2}^{s}
, and

\bm{\Gamma}_{s}(\widehat{\bm{e}}_{2}^{s}-\bm{e}_{2}^{s})
, for

s<t
.

Proof:

By Appendix
B
, the conditional mean is

Lemma 13

(Bounded conditional mean deformation)
.

On the regularity event
\mathcal{R}_{t}

\|\bm{A}_{\parallel,t}\|=O_{p}(1).

Proof:

It suffices to bound each term in
(
240

\bm{B}_{\ell,t}=\frac{1}{M}\operatorname{diag}(\bm{p}_{\ell,t})\bm{S}\operatorname{diag}(\bm{q}_{\ell,t}).

For arbitrary deterministic unit vectors

and

\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

\displaystyle|\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{B}_{\ell,t}\bm{y}|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{i}p_{\ell,t,i}s_{ij}q_{\ell,t,j}y_{j}\right|

We next express the measurement residual as the upper block of the same

Appendix E

The diagonal covariance of the linear module is

\bm{C}_{t}=\left(\gamma_{w}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\bm{\Gamma}_{t}\right)^{-1},

\bm{w}
has bounded normalized energy, and

\bm{q}_{t}
is signal-side admissible, (
258
)
implies

\|\bm{m}_{t}\|_{N}=O_{p}(1).

Moreover, on the same event, the diagonal entries
d_{t,j}
are bounded away
from zero and infinity. Indeed,

\lambda_{\max}(\bm{C}_{t})\leq\gamma_{\min}^{-1}
, while

\lambda_{\min}(\bm{C}_{t})\geq\left(\gamma_{w}\|\bm{A}\|^{2}+\gamma_{\max}\right)^{-1}

on a probability-one limiting event. Hence

|r_{t,j}^{\rm sch}|=|d_{t,j}^{-1}m_{t,j}|\leq C|m_{t,j}|
, which proves the
claim.
∎

Appendix
D
gives the averaged diagonal MDE response

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|d_{t,j}-T_{t,j}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Combining (
260
) with

d_{t,j}=T_{t,j}+(d_{t,j}-T_{t,j})
, we get

m_{t,j}=T_{t,j}r_{t,j}^{\rm sch}+\Delta_{t,j},

where

\Delta_{t,j}=(d_{t,j}-T_{t,j})r_{t,j}^{\rm sch}.

By Lemma
14
and the product-stability lemma in
Appendix
A
,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\Delta_{t,j}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Define the history-conditioned Schur residual

\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}=\gamma_{w}T_{t,j}\bm{a}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\overline{\bm{u}}_{t}^{(j)}.

Then

m_{t,j}=\gamma_{t,j}T_{t,j}q_{t,j}+\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}+\Delta_{t,j},

with
\|\bm{\Delta}_{t}\|_{N}\to 0
in probability.

\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}
is not a fresh innovation at this stage. The

\bm{a}_{j}

Appendix F
Column-Wise Gaussian Kernel under the Conditioned History

This appendix proves the coordinate-wise Gaussian-kernel statement used in
Proposition
6
. The proof has two logically
separate parts. First, after the predictable linear history has been fixed, we
reveal only finitely many scalar projections of one column of the Gaussian
matrix. This gives a genuine finite-dimensional Gaussian vector; no deleted
EP recursion is introduced. Second, the covariance of this vector is identified
through the conditioned two-resolvent MDE response from
Appendix
D
. Keeping these two steps separate is useful:
the Gaussianity is a consequence of finite-dimensional Gaussian conditioning,
whereas the numerical value of the covariance is a random-matrix deterministic
equivalent.

Appendix
E
established the Schur expansion

m_{t,j}=\gamma_{t,j}T_{t,j}q_{t,j}+\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}+\Delta_{t,j},\qquad\|\bm{\Delta}_{t}\|_{N}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0,

where

\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}=\gamma_{w}T_{t,j}\bm{a}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\overline{\bm{u}}_{t}^{(j)}.

(261)

Here
\overline{\bm{u}}_{t}^{(j)}
is the one-step column-cavity
residual of the current linear system. It is a Schur-complement object, not a
new trajectory of the algorithm. The variable
\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}
will be
shown to be a coordinate of a Gaussian history. It should not be confused with
the fresh innovation obtained later by Gaussian regression.

F-A

Column-Wise Revealed History

Fix a coordinate
j
and a finite time
t
. We use

\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\rm lin}
for the predictable linear-history filtration in
(
111
). Conditionally on this history,
Appendix
B
gives

\bm{A}=\bm{A}_{\parallel,t}+\bm{A}_{\perp,t},

where
\bm{A}_{\parallel,t}
is history-measurable and

\bm{A}_{\perp,t}
is centered Gaussian. Let

\bm{a}_{j}
denote the
j
th column of
\bm{A}
. Its
conditioned residual covariance is denoted by

\bm{\Xi}_{j|t}=\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(\bm{a}_{j}-\mathbb{E}[\bm{a}_{j}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\rm lin}]\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\rm lin}\bigr).

By covariance contraction,

\bm{0}\preceq\bm{\Xi}_{j|t}\preceq M^{-1}\bm{S}_{j},\qquad\bm{S}_{j}=\operatorname{diag}(s_{1j},\ldots,s_{Mj}).

The following revealed history is used only inside the proof. It records the
information that has already been exposed from the
j
th column before the
next scalar Schur projection is evaluated:

\mathcal{G}_{0,j}=\sigma(\mathcal{F}_{0}^{\rm lin},\bm{A}_{-j}),\qquad\mathcal{G}_{r+1,j}=\sigma(\mathcal{G}_{r,j},\mathcal{Z}_{r,j}),\quad 0\leq r<t.

Equivalently,
\mathcal{G}_{r,j}
contains the predictable linear history up to
time
r
, the matrix with the
j
th column removed, and the previously
revealed scalar projections

\mathcal{Z}_{0,j},\ldots,\mathcal{Z}_{r-1,j}
. It does not contain a deleted
EP trajectory.

Lemma 15

(Measurability and column-wise Gaussianity)
.

For every fixed
r\leq t
, the one-step column-cavity vector

\overline{\bm{u}}_{r}^{(j)}
is
\mathcal{G}_{r,j}
-measurable.
Moreover, conditionally on
\mathcal{G}_{r,j}
, the unexposed part of

\bm{a}_{j}
is Gaussian. Thus there exist a

\mathcal{G}_{r,j}
-measurable vector
\bm{a}_{j|r}
and a positive
semidefinite matrix
\bm{\Xi}_{j|r}
such that

\bm{a}_{j}=\bm{a}_{j|r}+\bm{\xi}_{j|r},\qquad\bm{\xi}_{j|r}\mid\mathcal{G}_{r,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Xi}_{j|r}).

(262)

Consequently, for fixed
t
, the vector

\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{0:t,j}=(\mathcal{Z}_{0,j},\ldots,\mathcal{Z}_{t,j})^{\mathsf{T}}

(263)

is finite-dimensional Gaussian under the column-wise revealed conditioning.

Proof:

The vector
\overline{\bm{u}}_{r}^{(j)}
is computed from

\bm{w}
,
\bm{A}_{-j}
,
\bm{q}_{r}
, and

\bm{\Gamma}_{r}
through the regularized current linear system with the

j
th coordinate removed. These quantities are contained in

\mathcal{G}_{r,j}
; hence the coefficient vector in the scalar projection
(
261
) is fixed once
\mathcal{G}_{r,j}
is given.

The conditional law of
\bm{a}_{j}
given

\mathcal{F}_{r}^{\rm lin}
and
\bm{A}_{-j}
is Gaussian, because the
original matrix is Gaussian and the predictable history gives only finitely many
linear observations of
\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A})
. Passing from

\mathcal{G}_{r,j}
to
\mathcal{G}_{r+1,j}
conditions further on the scalar
linear projection
\mathcal{Z}_{r,j}
. A Gaussian vector conditioned on a
finite number of linear projections remains Gaussian, with mean and covariance
updated by the standard Gaussian regression formula. Iterating this argument
for
r=0,\ldots,t
proves (
262
) and the joint
Gaussianity of (
263
).
∎

F-B

Covariance Identification

The preceding lemma gives Gaussianity. It remains to compute the covariance
seen by the Schur projections. This computation is where the conditioned MDE
enters.

Lemma 16

(Covariance of the column-wise Schur kernel)
.

For every fixed
0\leq r,s\leq t
, the finite-dimensional covariance of the
Schur residual satisfies

\widehat{\zeta}_{j,N}^{r,s}:=\operatorname{Cov}(\mathcal{Z}_{r,j},\mathcal{Z}_{s,j}\mid\mathcal{G}_{\max(r,s),j})=\gamma_{w}^{2}T_{r,j}T_{s,j}\Theta_{j}^{r,s}+o_{p}(1),

(264)

where
\Theta_{j}^{r,s}
is the conditioned two-resolvent response of
Definition
6
.

Proof:

By Lemma
15
, the covariance before the MDE
replacement is

\widehat{\zeta}_{j,N}^{r,s}=\gamma_{w}^{2}T_{r,j}T_{s,j}(\overline{\bm{u}}_{r}^{(j)})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Xi}_{j|r,s}\overline{\bm{u}}_{s}^{(j)},

(265)

where
\bm{\Xi}_{j|r,s}
is the conditional covariance of the
unexposed part of the
j
th column after the scalar projections already
revealed before the pair
(r,s)
have been removed. This covariance is a
projection of
\bm{\Xi}_{j|t}
, and therefore

\bm{0}\preceq\bm{\Xi}_{j|r,s}\preceq M^{-1}\bm{S}_{j}.

The cavity residual in (
265
) is a one-coordinate
Schur complement of the current regularized linear system. The corresponding
rank-one resolvent perturbation gives, for fixed
r\leq t
,

\|\overline{\bm{u}}_{r}^{(j)}-\bm{u}^{r}\|_{M}=o_{p}(1),\qquad\|\overline{\bm{u}}_{r}^{(j)}\|_{M}=O_{p}(1),\qquad\|\bm{u}^{r}\|_{M}=O_{p}(1).

(266)

Since
\|\bm{\Xi}_{j|r,s}\|\leq C/M
, equation
(
266
) implies

\widehat{\zeta}_{j,N}^{r,s}=\gamma_{w}^{2}T_{r,j}T_{s,j}(\bm{u}^{r})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Xi}_{j|r,s}\bm{u}^{s}+o_{p}(1).

(267)

The remaining quadratic form is exactly the conditioned column-covariance
insertion handled by the two-resolvent response of
Appendix
D
. Thus

(\bm{u}^{r})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Xi}_{j|r,s}\bm{u}^{s}=\Theta_{j}^{r,s}+o_{p}(1).

(268)

Combining (
267
) and
(
268
) proves
(
264
).
∎

The covariance kernel used in the main theorem is therefore

\zeta_{j}^{r,s}=\gamma_{w}^{2}T_{r,j}T_{s,j}\Theta_{j}^{r,s}.

(269)

If no history conditioning is present, the conditional covariance insertion
reduces to
\bm{\Xi}_{j|r,s}=M^{-1}\bm{S}_{j}
, and
(
268
) becomes the intuitive profile-weighted
identity

\frac{1}{M}(\bm{u}^{r})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{S}_{j}\bm{u}^{s}=\Theta_{j}^{r,s}+o_{p}(1).

In the theorem and in the proof, however,
\Theta_{j}^{r,s}
always denotes the
conditioned MDE response.

F-C

Gaussian Reference Process

Let

\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{0:t,0:t}=(\zeta_{j}^{r,s})_{0\leq r,s\leq t}.

This matrix is positive semidefinite because it is the finite-dimensional limit
of covariance matrices of
\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{0:t,j}
. Hence there is
a centered Gaussian reference vector

\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{0:t,j}^{\rm G}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{0:t,0:t})

conditionally on the MDE environment
\mathcal{P}_{t}
.

For every bounded Lipschitz

\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{t+1}\to\mathbb{R}
, the Gaussian covariance interpolation
lemma from Appendix
A
gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{0:t,j})\mid\mathcal{G}_{t,j}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\rm G,j}\left[\varphi(\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{0:t,j}^{\rm G})\mid\mathcal{P}_{t}\right]=o_{p}(1).

Thus the coordinate-wise Schur residual history is asymptotically equivalent,
for fixed-dimensional bounded-Lipschitz tests, to the Gaussian reference
history with covariance kernel (
269
). This proves the
coordinate-wise Gaussian-kernel statement of
Proposition
6
. The empirical
pseudo-Lipschitz upgrade over
j=1,\ldots,N
is proved in
Appendix
G
.

Appendix G
Empirical Gaussian Law under the Conditioned History

This appendix upgrades the coordinate-wise Gaussian kernel of
Appendix
F
to the empirical pseudo-Lipschitz law in
Proposition
6
. The argument is independent
of the Schur-complement algebra. Once the coordinate kernels have been
represented by a joint Gaussian array, it remains only to show that the
cross-covariances between different coordinates are negligible on average.
The weakly dependent Gaussian empirical law in
Theorem
1
then applies.

Throughout the appendix, the horizon
t\leq T
is fixed. We write

\|\bm{a}\|_{N}^{2}=N^{-1}\|\bm{a}\|^{2}.

G-A

Gaussian Reference Array

For each coordinate
j
, let

\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{j}^{\rm G}=(\mathcal{Z}_{0,j}^{\rm G},\ldots,\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}^{\rm G})^{\mathsf{T}}

be the Gaussian reference history constructed in
Appendix
F
. Its diagonal covariance block is

\operatorname{Cov}_{\rm G}(\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{j}^{\rm G}\mid\mathcal{P}_{t})=\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{0:t,0:t}.

For two distinct coordinates, write

\bm{\zeta}_{jk}^{0:t,0:t}=\operatorname{Cov}_{\rm G}(\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{j}^{\rm G},\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}^{\rm G}\mid\mathcal{P}_{t}),\qquad j\neq k.

The next lemma is the covariance estimate that makes the empirical law possible.
It is the variance-profile analogue of the asymptotic orthogonality of distinct
coordinates in rotationally invariant EP proofs.

Lemma 17

(Average off-diagonal covariance bound)
.

For every fixed
t\leq T
,

\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j\neq k}\left\|\bm{\zeta}_{jk}^{0:t,0:t}\right\|_{\rm op}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

(270)

Proof:

It is enough to work with one scalar covariance entry indexed by

(r,s)
, because
t
is fixed. Let
\bm{P}_{j}
be the selector that
extracts the
j
th column from
\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A})
. Before
conditioning, different columns are independent:

\bm{P}_{j}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{P}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}=\bm{0},\qquad j\neq k.

After conditioning on the finite linear history

\bm{L}_{t}\operatorname{vec}(\bm{A})=\bm{b}_{t},

the off-diagonal column covariance is created entirely by the Gaussian
regression correction. From Lemma
1
,

\bm{\Sigma}_{jk|t}=-\bm{P}_{j}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{L}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger}\bm{L}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{P}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad j\neq k.

(271)

This formula is the source of the small cross-covariances. The history has
fixed rank after redundant directions have been removed, whereas each column
contributes only a normalized
N^{-1/2}
-scale projection to the retained
history coordinates.

We now make this normalization explicit. On the regularity event, choose a
retained history basis

\bm{r}_{1,t},\ldots,\bm{r}_{R_{t},t}
, where

R_{t}=O(1)
, for the range of

\bm{L}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}^{1/2}
. In this basis the nonzero part of
the profile-weighted history Gram has eigenvalues bounded away from zero and
infinity. Therefore

(\bm{L}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{L}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger}=\sum_{a,b=1}^{R_{t}}c_{ab,t}\,\bm{r}_{a,t}\bm{r}_{b,t}^{\mathsf{T}}+\bm{R}_{t,N},\qquad\max_{a,b}|c_{ab,t}|=O_{p}(1),

where the remainder
\bm{R}_{t,N}
acts only on discarded redundant
directions and contributes an averaged negligible term below. More explicitly,
the discarded subspace is chosen so that its profile-weighted Gram eigenvalues
vanish on the regularity event; multiplication by the two admissible
column-history factors in (
272
) therefore gives a
contribution whose
N^{-2}
-average over
j\neq k
is
o_{p}(1)
. Thus
only the retained finite-dimensional subspace can produce a non-negligible
cross-column covariance.

For any measurement-side admissible vectors
\bm{v}
and

\bm{w}
, the entrywise covariance identity

\operatorname{Cov}(A_{ij},\bm{p}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}\bm{q})=\frac{s_{ij}}{M}p_{i}q_{j}

shows that the two factors

\bm{r}_{a,t}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{L}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{P}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{v},\qquad\bm{r}_{b,t}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{L}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{P}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{w}

(272)

are separable in the column indices
j
and
k
. With the normalization

M/N\to\delta
, they can be written in the form

\bm{r}_{a,t}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{L}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{P}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{v}=N^{-1/2}\rho_{a,j}^{(t)}(\bm{v}),\qquad\bm{r}_{b,t}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{L}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{A}\bm{P}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{w}=N^{-1/2}\sigma_{b,k}^{(t)}(\bm{w}),

(273)

where admissibility of the retained history vectors and boundedness of the
profile imply

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\rho_{a,j}^{(t)}(\bm{v})|^{2}=O_{p}(1),\qquad\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\sigma_{b,k}^{(t)}(\bm{w})|^{2}=O_{p}(1).

(274)

Apply (
271
) with

\bm{v}=\bm{u}^{r}
and
\bm{w}=\bm{u}^{s}
. The
scalar pairing

\mathcal{C}_{jk}^{r,s}=(\bm{u}^{r})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}_{jk|t}\bm{u}^{s}

therefore satisfies the finite-rank separable bound

|\mathcal{C}_{jk}^{r,s}|\leq\frac{C}{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_{t}}|\rho_{\ell,j}^{(t)}||\sigma_{\ell,k}^{(t)}|+\varepsilon_{jk,N}^{r,s},

(275)

where
L_{t}=O(1)
and

\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j\neq k}|\varepsilon_{jk,N}^{r,s}|\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

The factor
N^{-1}
in (
275
) is the product
of the two
N^{-1/2}
-scale column-history projections in
(
273
); the bounded retained Gram inverse only
changes the constant.

The off-diagonal covariance of the Gaussian Schur variables has the same form
up to bounded MDE response factors:

\operatorname{Cov}_{\rm G}(\mathcal{Z}_{r,j}^{\rm G},\mathcal{Z}_{s,k}^{\rm G}\mid\mathcal{P}_{t})=\gamma_{w}^{2}T_{r,j}T_{s,k}\mathcal{C}_{jk}^{r,s}.

Since
T_{r,j}
and
T_{s,k}
are uniformly bounded, Cauchy–Schwarz and
(
274
) give

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j\neq k}\left|\operatorname{Cov}_{\rm G}(\mathcal{Z}_{r,j}^{\rm G},\mathcal{Z}_{s,k}^{\rm G}\mid\mathcal{P}_{t})\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{C}{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_{t}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\rho_{\ell,j}^{(t)}|\right)\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\sigma_{\ell,k}^{(t)}|\right)+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle\quad=O_{p}(N^{-1})+o_{p}(1)\longrightarrow 0.

(276)

There are finitely many time indices, so summing
(
276
) over
0\leq r,s\leq t
proves
(
270
).
∎

G-B

Let
\bm{X}_{j,N}^{t,\rm G}
denote the coordinate-wise Gaussian
reference state used in the state evolution. It consists of deterministic or

-measurable coordinates, such as

, predicted precisions,

\mathcal{P}_{t}

,
\Theta_{j}^{r,s}
, and
\zeta_{j}^{r,s}
are bounded on the
regularity event. Lemma
17
gives the vanishing

Theorem
1
. Applying that theorem conditionally on

\mathcal{P}_{t}
proves (
277
).
∎

Appendix H
Cavity Cancellation and Memory Decomposition

This appendix proves Proposition
7
. The
previous appendices have already identified the output of the linear module:
it is the sum of an instantaneous response and a coordinate of a Gaussian
history. The role of the present appendix is to show exactly what the EP
extrinsic subtraction removes. It cancels the instantaneous response, but it
does not in general remove the part of the Gaussian history that is predictable
from its past.

The starting point is the Schur-kernel representation

m_{t,j}=\gamma_{t,j}T_{t,j}q_{t,j}+\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}+\Delta_{t,j}.

(281)

The first term in (
281
) is the linear response to the
incoming EP error
q_{t,j}
. The remaining term
\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}
is a
Gaussian-process coordinate. It becomes a fresh Gaussian only after its
Gaussian regression on the past history has been subtracted.

Throughout this appendix,
t
is fixed and all statements are made on the
regularity event
\mathcal{R}_{t}
. We use the shorthand

\|\bm{a}\|_{N}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\|\bm{a}\|^{2}.

For a coordinate array
r_{t,j}
, the notation

r_{t,j}=o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1)

means

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|r_{t,j}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

H-A

Preliminary Bounds

We first record the deterministic bounds used to pass from the finite-
N

diagonal quantities to their MDE-level counterparts.

By the regularized diagonal loading,

\bm{\Gamma}_{t}\succeq\gamma_{\min}\bm{I}_{N}.

Moreover, on the regularity event and by the boundedness of the
variance-profile ensemble,

\|\bm{A}\|=O_{p}(1).

Consequently, the covariance

\bm{C}_{t}=\left(\gamma_{w}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A}+\bm{\Gamma}_{t}\right)^{-1}

has eigenvalues bounded away from zero and infinity with probability tending to
one. Hence there exist deterministic constants
0<c_{D}<C_{D}<\infty
such that,
with probability tending to one,

c_{D}\leq d_{t,j}\leq C_{D},\qquad c_{D}\leq T_{t,j}\leq C_{D}

(282)

for all
j
. The corresponding cavity precisions are projected into a
compact positive interval:

0<\pi_{\min}\leq\pi_{t,j}\leq\pi_{\max}<\infty.

Recall the MDE-level cavity precision

\bar{\pi}_{t,j}=\operatorname{Proj}_{[\pi_{\min},\pi_{\max}]}\left((T_{t,j})^{-1}-\gamma_{t,j}\right).

The finite-
N
EP cavity precision is

\pi_{t,j}=\operatorname{Proj}_{[\pi_{\min},\pi_{\max}]}\left(d_{t,j}^{-1}-\gamma_{t,j}\right).

Lemma 20

(Replacement of diagonal cavity precisions)
.

If

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|d_{t,j}-T_{t,j}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0,

then

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\pi_{t,j}-\bar{\pi}_{t,j}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Furthermore,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\pi_{t,j}^{-1}-\bar{\pi}_{t,j}^{-1}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof:

On the event (
282
), the map
x\mapsto x^{-1}
is Lipschitz
on the interval
[c_{D},C_{D}]
. Therefore

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|d_{t,j}^{-1}-(T_{t,j})^{-1}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

The projection map onto an interval is
1
-Lipschitz, hence

\displaystyle|\pi_{t,j}-\bar{\pi}_{t,j}|

\displaystyle\leq|d_{t,j}^{-1}-(T_{t,j})^{-1}|.

This proves the first claim. The second follows because

x\mapsto x^{-1}
is Lipschitz on

[\pi_{\min},\pi_{\max}]
.
∎

We shall also use the following consequence of the Gaussian kernel. From
Proposition
5
,

m_{t,j}=\gamma_{t,j}T_{t,j}q_{t,j}+\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}+\Delta_{t,j},\qquad\|\bm{\Delta}_{t}\|_{N}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

(283)

The covariance kernel in Proposition
6
gives
bounded empirical second moments:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}^{2}=O_{p}(1).

Indeed, the conditional expectation of the left-hand side equals

N^{-1}\sum_{j}\zeta_{j}^{t,t}
, which is bounded by the MDE response and the
regularity assumptions.

H-B

Cancellation of the Instantaneous Response

The EP cavity identity is

\bm{\Pi}_{t}\bm{h}_{t}=\bm{D}_{t}^{-1}\bm{m}_{t}-\bm{\Gamma}_{t}\bm{q}_{t}.

Coordinate-wise,

\pi_{t,j}h_{t,j}=d_{t,j}^{-1}m_{t,j}-\gamma_{t,j}q_{t,j}.

(284)

Substituting (
283
) into
(
284
) gives

\displaystyle\pi_{t,j}h_{t,j}

\displaystyle=d_{t,j}^{-1}\left(\gamma_{t,j}T_{t,j}q_{t,j}+\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}+\Delta_{t,j}\right)-\gamma_{t,j}q_{t,j}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{t,j}\left(\frac{T_{t,j}}{d_{t,j}}-1\right)q_{t,j}+d_{t,j}^{-1}\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}+d_{t,j}^{-1}\Delta_{t,j}.

We now show that the first and third terms are negligible in empirical

\ell_{2}
norm, and that
d_{t,j}^{-1}\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}
can be replaced by

(T_{t,j})^{-1}\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}
.

First, since
\bm{q}_{t}
is admissible and

\gamma_{t,j}
is uniformly bounded, Cauchy–Schwarz and
(
282
) imply

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|\gamma_{t,j}\left(\frac{T_{t,j}}{d_{t,j}}-1\right)q_{t,j}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|T_{t,j}-d_{t,j}|^{2}|q_{t,j}|^{2}.

Since

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}|T_{t,j}-d_{t,j}|^{2}\to 0

in probability and

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}q_{t,j}^{2}=O_{p}(1),

the right-hand side converges to zero in probability after the usual
truncation argument on
|q_{t,j}|
. Thus

\gamma_{t,j}\left(\frac{T_{t,j}}{d_{t,j}}-1\right)q_{t,j}=o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1).

Second,

d_{t,j}^{-1}\Delta_{t,j}=o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1)

because
d_{t,j}^{-1}
is uniformly bounded and

\|\bm{\Delta}_{t}\|_{N}\to 0
.

Third,

(d_{t,j}^{-1}-(T_{t,j})^{-1})\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}=o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1).

Indeed, the empirical
\ell_{2}
norm of

d_{t,j}^{-1}-(T_{t,j})^{-1}
converges to zero, while

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}^{2}=O_{p}(1)
; the same truncation/Cauchy–Schwarz
argument applies.

Combining these three estimates yields

\pi_{t,j}h_{t,j}=(T_{t,j})^{-1}\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}+o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1).

Multiplying by
\pi_{t,j}^{-1}
, and then replacing

\pi_{t,j}^{-1}
by
\bar{\pi}_{t,j}^{-1}
using
Lemma
20
, gives

h_{t,j}=\alpha_{t,j}\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}+r_{t,j},\qquad\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|r_{t,j}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0,

(285)

where

\alpha_{t,j}=\frac{1}{\bar{\pi}_{t,j}T_{t,j}}.

Equation (
285
) is the EP cancellation identity. The term

\gamma_{t,j}T_{t,j}q_{t,j}
, which is the instantaneous response of the
linear module to the incoming error, is removed by the extrinsic subtraction.
The remaining object is the Schur residual
\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}
, a Gaussian-process coordinate identified in Appendix
F
.

H-C

Gaussian Regression of the Residual History

The cancellation identity has reduced the EP cavity to a scaled version of

\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}
. The last step is purely Gaussian: decompose this current
coordinate into its regression on the past Gaussian history and an independent
innovation.

For each coordinate
j
, define

\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{<t,j}=(\mathcal{Z}_{0,j},\mathcal{Z}_{1,j},\ldots,\mathcal{Z}_{t-1,j})^{\mathsf{T}}.

The covariance blocks are

\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,<t}=(\zeta_{j}^{r,s})_{0\leq r,s<t},

\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t}=(\zeta_{j}^{t,0},\ldots,\zeta_{j}^{t,t-1}),\qquad\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,t}=(\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t})^{\mathsf{T}}.

By Proposition
6
, conditionally on the
MDE-generated environment
\mathcal{P}_{t}
, the vector

(\mathcal{Z}_{0,j},\ldots,\mathcal{Z}_{t,j})

is Gaussian with covariance kernel
\zeta_{j}^{r,s}
. Applying the Gaussian
regression lemma with a possibly singular covariance matrix gives

\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}=\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t}(\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{<t,j}+G_{t,j},

(286)

where
G_{t,j}
is Gaussian and independent of
\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{<t,j}

conditionally on
\mathcal{P}_{t}
. Its conditional variance is

\nu_{t,j}=\zeta_{j}^{t,t}-\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t}(\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,t}.

For
t=0
, the past is empty and we use the convention

\nu_{0,j}=\zeta_{j}^{0,0},\qquad\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{0,<0}(\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<0,<0})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{<0,j}=0.

Combining (
285
) and (
286
), we get

\displaystyle h_{t,j}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t,j}\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t}(\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{<t,j}+\alpha_{t,j}G_{t,j}+r_{t,j}.

Define the predictable memory term

\mu_{t,j}=\alpha_{t,j}\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t}(\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{<t,j},

and the innovation variance

\tau_{t,j}=\alpha_{t,j}^{2}\nu_{t,j}.

If
\nu_{t,j}>0
, write

W_{t,j}=\frac{G_{t,j}}{\sqrt{\nu_{t,j}}}.

Then
W_{t,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
conditionally on
\mathcal{P}_{t}
, and it
is independent of the past Gaussian history. If
\nu_{t,j}=0
, then

G_{t,j}=0
almost surely and the term

\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}W_{t,j}
is interpreted as zero, with

W_{t,j}
chosen as an arbitrary standard Gaussian independent of the past.

Thus,

h_{t,j}=\mu_{t,j}+\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j}+r_{t,j},

(287)

with

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|r_{t,j}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

This proves the desired cavity memory decomposition.

H-D

Interpretation and Completion of the Proposition

Equation (
287
) has two distinct parts. The term

\sqrt{au_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j}

is the fresh innovation obtained after regressing the Schur residual process on
its past.
The term

\mu_{t,j}=\mathbb{E}[h_{t,j}\mid\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{<t,j},\mathcal{P}_{t}]+o_{p}^{\ell_{2}}(1)

is the predictable component inherited from the temporal covariance of the
Gaussian kernel. Therefore the standard EP cavity is fresh if and only if the
regression coefficient

\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t}(\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}

vanishes, up to negligible empirical error. In general variance-profile
ensembles, this coefficient need not vanish.

The preceding derivation proves Proposition
7
:
under the conclusions of the history-conditioned Schur kernel and the covariance
kernel theorem, the EP cavity satisfies

h_{t,j}=\mu_{t,j}+\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}\,W_{t,j}+\varepsilon_{t,j},

where

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\varepsilon_{t,j}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

The variables
W_{t,j}
are standard Gaussian innovations conditionally on

\mathcal{P}_{t}
and independent of the past Gaussian history for each fixed
coordinate
j
.

Appendix I
Regularity Closure

This appendix proves Proposition
8
. The goal is to
show that the induction hypotheses defining the regularity event

\mathcal{R}_{t}
are propagated by one iteration of the predictable-precision
EP dynamics. The proof uses only the compact precision bounds, the empirical
Gaussian law, and the regularity of the scalar prior module.

Throughout, the notation

\|\bm{a}\|_{N}^{2}=N^{-1}\|\bm{a}\|^{2},\qquad\|\bm{b}\|_{M}^{2}=M^{-1}\|\bm{b}\|^{2}

is used. A signal-side vector is called admissible if it has bounded empirical
second moment and satisfies the no-spike condition in
Definition
4
; the measurement-side definition is analogous.

I-A

Signal-Side Quantities

By Proposition
5
,

m_{t,j}=\gamma_{t,j}T_{t,j}q_{t,j}+\mathcal{Z}_{t,j}+\Delta_{t,j},\qquad\|\bm{\Delta}_{t}\|_{N}\xrightarrow{p}0.

The first term is admissible because
\gamma_{t,j}
and
T_{t,j}
are
uniformly bounded and
\bm{q}_{t}
is admissible on
\mathcal{R}_{t}
.
The Schur residual has bounded empirical moments by the empirical Gaussian law
of Proposition
6
. Hence

\|\bm{m}_{t}\|_{N}=O_{p}(1),\qquad\|\bm{m}_{t}\|_{\infty}/\sqrt{N}\xrightarrow{p}0.

Thus
\bm{m}_{t}
is signal-side admissible.

The cavity decomposition of Proposition
7

gives

h_{t,j}=\mu_{t,j}+\sqrt{\tau_{t,j}}W_{t,j}+\varepsilon_{t,j},\qquad\|\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}\|_{N}\xrightarrow{p}0.

The regression coefficients defining
\mu_{t,j}
are finite-dimensional and
bounded on the regularity event, while
\tau_{t,j}
is bounded above and
below by the stable-regression assumption. Therefore
\bm{h}_{t}
has
bounded empirical moments and no spikes. Hence
\bm{h}_{t}
is
signal-side admissible.

The prior-module output error is

p_{t,j}=\eta(x_{j}+s_{t,j};\rho_{t,j})-x_{j},

where
(s_{t,j},\rho_{t,j})
is either the standard or corrected scalar input.
The scalar maps are empirically stable on compact precision intervals by
Assumption
4
. Since the inputs have bounded empirical
moments, the output
\bm{p}_{t}
is signal-side admissible. The
extrinsic update has the form

\gamma_{t+1,j}q_{t+1,j}=v_{B,t,j}^{-1}p_{t,j}-\rho_{t,j}s_{t,j},

with all precisions and scalar posterior variances clipped to compact positive
intervals. Consequently
\bm{q}_{t+1}
is also signal-side admissible.

I-B

Measurement-Side Residual

It remains to control

\bm{u}_{t}=\bm{w}-\bm{A}\bm{m}_{t}.

The empirical energy is bounded because
\bm{w}
is measurement-side
admissible,
\|\bm{A}\|=O_{p}(1)
under the variance-profile Gaussian
model, and
\bm{m}_{t}
is signal-side admissible:

\|\bm{u}_{t}\|_{M}\leq\|\bm{w}\|_{M}+\|\bm{A}\|\,\sqrt{N/M}\,\|\bm{m}_{t}\|_{N}=O_{p}(1).

For the no-spike condition, decompose under the conditioned history as

\bm{A}\bm{m}_{t}=\bm{A}_{\parallel,t}\bm{m}_{t}+\bm{A}_{\perp,t}\bm{m}_{t}.

The deformation term is controlled by the finite-history representation of
Appendix
C
and the no-spike property of the
history vectors. The centered Gaussian term is controlled by the flat
covariance bound and the Gaussian maximum lemma in
Appendix
A
; conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, each
coordinate has variance uniformly bounded by a constant times

M^{-1}\|\bm{m}_{t}\|^{2}=O_{p}(1)
, hence

\frac{\|\bm{A}_{\perp,t}\bm{m}_{t}\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{M}}\xrightarrow{p}0.

Together with the no-spike property of
\bm{w}
, this gives

\|\bm{u}_{t}\|_{\infty}/\sqrt{M}\xrightarrow{p}0.

Therefore
\bm{u}_{t}
is measurement-side admissible.

All objects required in the next conditioning step are admissible, and the
finite nonredundant history Gram convention is preserved for the fixed horizon

T
. Hence
\mathcal{R}_{t+1}
holds with probability tending to one. This
proves Proposition
8
.

Appendix J
Precision Replacement Estimates

This appendix proves the perturbation estimates used in
Section
VII
. The conditioning proof in
Section
V
is deliberately carried out with predictable
precisions; it never conditions on the actual finite-sample diagonal variances,
which are nonlinear resolvent functionals of the measurement matrix. The role
of this appendix is to bridge that predictable recursion with the adaptive
finite-dimensional diagonal EP updates. Within each branch, the actual messages
and precisions are shown to be asymptotically equivalent, in empirical norm, to
their predictable counterparts. No new random-matrix limit is proved here; the
arguments are deterministic stability estimates combined with the regularity
conditions in Assumptions
5
and

6
.

Throughout this appendix,

\|\bm{a}\|_{N}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\|\bm{a}\|^{2}.

The superscript “act” denotes the actual adaptive recursion, and “orc”
denotes the MDE-predictable recursion. When no ambiguity is possible, we use
the shorter notation

a\equiv\mathrm{act},\qquad o\equiv\mathrm{orc}.

J-A

Elementary Empirical-Norm Perturbation Rules

We first collect elementary stability facts repeatedly used below.

Lemma 21

(Lipschitz stability on compact intervals)
.

Let
0<c<C<\infty
. The maps

x\mapsto x^{-1},\qquad x\mapsto\operatorname{Proj}_{[c,C]}(x)

are Lipschitz on
[c,C]
and on
\mathbb{R}
, respectively. In particular,
if
x_{j},y_{j}\in[c,C]
and

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|x_{j}-y_{j}|^{2}\to 0,

then

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|x_{j}^{-1}-y_{j}^{-1}|^{2}\to 0.

Moreover, if
u_{j},v_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
, then

\left|\operatorname{Proj}_{[c,C]}(u_{j})-\operatorname{Proj}_{[c,C]}(v_{j})\right|\leq|u_{j}-v_{j}|.

Proof:

For
x,y\in[c,C]
,

|x^{-1}-y^{-1}|=\frac{|x-y|}{|xy|}\leq c^{-2}|x-y|.

The Euclidean projection onto a closed interval is non-expansive, hence

|\operatorname{Proj}_{[c,C]}(u)-\operatorname{Proj}_{[c,C]}(v)|\leq|u-v|.

The empirical-norm statements follow by summing over
j
.
∎

Lemma 22

(Empirical product stability)
.

Let
\{a_{j,N}\}
and
\{b_{j,N}\}
be two arrays. Suppose that

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|a_{j,N}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0,

and that
\{a_{j,N}\}
is uniformly bounded with probability tending to one:
there exists
C_{a}<\infty
such that

\max_{j\leq N}|a_{j,N}|\leq C_{a}

with probability tending to one. Suppose further that the empirical second
moments of
b_{j,N}
are uniformly integrable, i.e.,

\lim_{K\to\infty}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|b_{j,N}|^{2}\bm{1}\{|b_{j,N}|>K\}\right]=0.

Then

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|a_{j,N}b_{j,N}|^{2}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof:

Fix
K>0
. On the event
\max_{j}|a_{j,N}|\leq C_{a}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|a_{j,N}b_{j,N}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq K^{2}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|a_{j,N}|^{2}

\displaystyle+C_{a}^{2}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|b_{j,N}|^{2}\bm{1}\{|b_{j,N}|>K\}.

The first term converges to zero in probability for fixed
K
. The second
term can be made arbitrarily small in probability by choosing
K
large,
using Markov’s inequality and uniform integrability. This proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 23

(Diagonal extraction from Frobenius convergence)
.

For any square matrix
\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
,

\|\operatorname{diag}(\bm{R})\|_{N}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\|\bm{R}\|_{F}.

Consequently, normalized Frobenius convergence of matrices implies empirical
convergence of their diagonals.

Proof:

Since

\|\operatorname{diag}(\bm{R})\|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}|R_{jj}|^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|R_{ij}|^{2}=\|\bm{R}\|_{F}^{2},

dividing by
N
gives the result.
∎

J-B

Linear-Module Stability

We now prove Proposition
9
. At a fixed
iteration
t
, define

\bm{B}=\gamma_{w}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{A},

\bm{C}_{a}=(\bm{B}+\bm{\Gamma}_{a})^{-1},\qquad\bm{C}_{o}=(\bm{B}+\bm{\Gamma}_{o})^{-1},

and

\bm{m}_{a}=\bm{C}_{a}(\gamma_{w}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{w}+\bm{\Gamma}_{a}\bm{q}_{a}),

\bm{m}_{o}=\bm{C}_{o}(\gamma_{w}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{w}+\bm{\Gamma}_{o}\bm{q}_{o}).

The diagonal matrices are

\bm{\Gamma}_{a}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\gamma}_{a}),\qquad\bm{\Gamma}_{o}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\gamma}_{o}).

Assume

\|\bm{q}_{a}-\bm{q}_{o}\|_{N}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0,\qquad\|\bm{\gamma}_{a}-\bm{\gamma}_{o}\|_{N}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

(288)

Also assume that the oracle history is admissible:

\|\bm{q}_{o}\|_{N}=O_{p}(1),\qquad\|\bm{m}_{o}\|_{N}=O_{p}(1),

with uniformly integrable empirical second moments. This is available from
the regularity closure of the predictable recursion.

Lemma 24

(Stability of the linear posterior mean)
.

Under (
288
),

\|\bm{m}_{a}-\bm{m}_{o}\|_{N}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof:

The normal equations are

(\bm{B}+\bm{\Gamma}_{a})\bm{m}_{a}=\gamma_{w}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{w}+\bm{\Gamma}_{a}\bm{q}_{a},

and

(\bm{B}+\bm{\Gamma}_{o})\bm{m}_{o}=\gamma_{w}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{w}+\bm{\Gamma}_{o}\bm{q}_{o}.

Writing the second equation with the operator

\bm{B}+\bm{\Gamma}_{a}
gives

(\bm{B}+\bm{\Gamma}_{a})\bm{m}_{o}=\gamma_{w}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{w}+\bm{\Gamma}_{o}\bm{q}_{o}+(\bm{\Gamma}_{a}-\bm{\Gamma}_{o})\bm{m}_{o}.

Subtracting from the first equation yields

(\bm{B}+\bm{\Gamma}_{a})(\bm{m}_{a}-\bm{m}_{o})=\bm{\Gamma}_{a}(\bm{q}_{a}-\bm{q}_{o})+(\bm{\Gamma}_{a}-\bm{\Gamma}_{o})(\bm{q}_{o}-\bm{m}_{o}).

Thus

\bm{m}_{a}-\bm{m}_{o}=\bm{C}_{a}\left[\bm{\Gamma}_{a}(\bm{q}_{a}-\bm{q}_{o})+(\bm{\Gamma}_{a}-\bm{\Gamma}_{o})(\bm{q}_{o}-\bm{m}_{o})\right].

(289)

Since

\bm{B}+\bm{\Gamma}_{a}\succeq\gamma_{\min}\bm{I},

we have

\|\bm{C}_{a}\|\leq\gamma_{\min}^{-1}.

The first term in (
289
) is bounded by

\|\bm{\Gamma}_{a}(\bm{q}_{a}-\bm{q}_{o})\|_{N}\leq\gamma_{\max}\|\bm{q}_{a}-\bm{q}_{o}\|_{N}\to 0.

For the second term, write

(\bm{\Gamma}_{a}-\bm{\Gamma}_{o})(\bm{q}_{o}-\bm{m}_{o})=(\bm{\gamma}_{a}-\bm{\gamma}_{o})\odot(\bm{q}_{o}-\bm{m}_{o}).

The vector
\bm{\gamma}_{a}-\bm{\gamma}_{o}
is uniformly bounded
because both precisions lie in

[\gamma_{\min},\gamma_{\max}]
, and its empirical
\ell_{2}
norm tends to
zero. The vector
\bm{q}_{o}-\bm{m}_{o}
has uniformly integrable
empirical second moments. Lemma
22
therefore gives

\|(\bm{\Gamma}_{a}-\bm{\Gamma}_{o})(\bm{q}_{o}-\bm{m}_{o})\|_{N}\to 0.

Combining the two terms proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 25

(Stability of the linear covariance diagonal)
.

Let

\bm{d}_{a}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{C}_{a}),\qquad\bm{d}_{o}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{C}_{o}).

Then

\|\bm{d}_{a}-\bm{d}_{o}\|_{N}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Moreover, if the MDE response for the oracle recursion satisfies

\|\bm{d}_{o}-\bm{T}_{o}\|_{N}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0,

then

\|\bm{d}_{a}-\bm{T}_{o}\|_{N}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof:

The resolvent identity gives

\bm{C}_{a}-\bm{C}_{o}=-\bm{C}_{a}(\bm{\Gamma}_{a}-\bm{\Gamma}_{o})\bm{C}_{o}.

Since

\|\bm{C}_{a}\|,\|\bm{C}_{o}\|\leq\gamma_{\min}^{-1},

we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\|\bm{C}_{a}-\bm{C}_{o}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{C}_{a}\|\|\bm{C}_{o}\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\|\bm{\Gamma}_{a}-\bm{\Gamma}_{o}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{\min}^{-2}\|\bm{\gamma}_{a}-\bm{\gamma}_{o}\|_{N}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Lemma
23
gives

\|\bm{d}_{a}-\bm{d}_{o}\|_{N}\to 0.

The second claim follows from the triangle inequality.
∎

Lemma 26

(Stability of cavity precision and cavity mean)
.

Let

\pi_{a,j}=\operatorname{Proj}_{[\pi_{\min},\pi_{\max}]}(d_{a,j}^{-1}-\gamma_{a,j}),

and

\pi_{o,j}=\operatorname{Proj}_{[\pi_{\min},\pi_{\max}]}(T_{o,j}^{-1}-\gamma_{o,j}).

Assume

\|\bm{d}_{a}-\bm{T}_{o}\|_{N}\to 0,\qquad\|\bm{\gamma}_{a}-\bm{\gamma}_{o}\|_{N}\to 0,

and that
d_{a,j}
and
T_{o,j}
are bounded away from zero and infinity
with probability tending to one. Then

\|\bm{\pi}_{a}-\bm{\pi}_{o}\|_{N}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Furthermore, if

\|\bm{m}_{a}-\bm{m}_{o}\|_{N}\to 0,\qquad\|\bm{q}_{a}-\bm{q}_{o}\|_{N}\to 0,

then the cavity errors

h_{a,j}=\pi_{a,j}^{-1}(d_{a,j}^{-1}m_{a,j}-\gamma_{a,j}q_{a,j}),

and

h_{o,j}=\pi_{o,j}^{-1}(T_{o,j}^{-1}m_{o,j}-\gamma_{o,j}q_{o,j})

satisfy

\|\bm{h}_{a}-\bm{h}_{o}\|_{N}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof:

The first claim follows from Lemma
21
:

\displaystyle|\pi_{a,j}-\pi_{o,j}|

\displaystyle\leq|d_{a,j}^{-1}-T_{o,j}^{-1}|+|\gamma_{a,j}-\gamma_{o,j}|.

Taking empirical
\ell_{2}
norms gives

\|\bm{\pi}_{a}-\bm{\pi}_{o}\|_{N}\to 0.

Since
\pi_{a,j},\pi_{o,j}\in[\pi_{\min},\pi_{\max}]
,

\|\bm{\pi}_{a}^{-1}-\bm{\pi}_{o}^{-1}\|_{N}\to 0.

For the cavity error, write

\bm{h}_{a}-\bm{h}_{o}=\bm{\Pi}_{a}^{-1}\bm{r}_{a}-\bm{\Pi}_{o}^{-1}\bm{r}_{o},

where

r_{a,j}=d_{a,j}^{-1}m_{a,j}-\gamma_{a,j}q_{a,j},\qquad r_{o,j}=T_{o,j}^{-1}m_{o,j}-\gamma_{o,j}q_{o,j}.

It is enough to show

\|\bm{r}_{a}-\bm{r}_{o}\|_{N}\to 0

and

\|\bm{r}_{o}\|_{N}=O_{p}(1).

Indeed,

\bm{h}_{a}-\bm{h}_{o}=\bm{\Pi}_{a}^{-1}(\bm{r}_{a}-\bm{r}_{o})+(\bm{\Pi}_{a}^{-1}-\bm{\Pi}_{o}^{-1})\bm{r}_{o}.

The first term converges to zero because

\|\bm{\Pi}_{a}^{-1}\|\leq\pi_{\min}^{-1}
. The second term converges
to zero by Lemma
22
, since

\bm{\Pi}_{a}^{-1}-\bm{\Pi}_{o}^{-1}
is uniformly bounded and
converges in empirical
\ell_{2}
, while
\bm{r}_{o}
has uniformly
integrable empirical second moments by admissibility.

It remains to verify
\|\bm{r}_{a}-\bm{r}_{o}\|_{N}\to 0
. Decompose

\displaystyle r_{a,j}-r_{o,j}

\displaystyle=(d_{a,j}^{-1}-T_{o,j}^{-1})m_{a,j}+T_{o,j}^{-1}(m_{a,j}-m_{o,j})

\displaystyle-(\gamma_{a,j}-\gamma_{o,j})q_{a,j}-\gamma_{o,j}(q_{a,j}-q_{o,j}).

The second and fourth terms converge to zero directly from the boundedness of

T_{o,j}^{-1}
and
\gamma_{o,j}
. The first and third terms are handled
by Lemma
22
, using the empirical convergence of

d_{a}^{-1}-T_{o}^{-1}
and
\gamma_{a}-\gamma_{o}
, and the admissibility of

\bm{m}_{a}
and
\bm{q}_{a}
. This proves the cavity stability.
∎

Combining Lemmas
24
–
26
proves
Proposition
9
.

J-C

Prior-Module Stability

We now prove Proposition
10
. Fix a branch

b\in\{\mathrm{std},\mathrm{corr}\}
. The prior-module input consists of a
scalar shift
s_{t,j}^{b}
and a scalar precision
\rho_{t,j}^{b}
. The scalar
posterior error and variance are

p_{t,j}^{b}=\eta(x_{j}+s_{t,j}^{b};\rho_{t,j}^{b})-x_{j},

and

v_{B,t,j}^{b}=v_{B}(x_{j}+s_{t,j}^{b};\rho_{t,j}^{b}).

Assume

\|\bm{s}_{a}-\bm{s}_{o}\|_{N}\to 0,\qquad\|\bm{\rho}_{a}-\bm{\rho}_{o}\|_{N}\to 0,

and that all entries of
\bm{\rho}_{a},\bm{\rho}_{o}
lie in a
compact positive interval. By Assumption
5
,

\|\bm{\eta}(\bm{x}+\bm{s}_{a};\bm{\rho}_{a})-\bm{\eta}(\bm{x}+\bm{s}_{o};\bm{\rho}_{o})\|_{N}\to 0,

and

\|\bm{v}_{B}(\bm{x}+\bm{s}_{a};\bm{\rho}_{a})-\bm{v}_{B}(\bm{x}+\bm{s}_{o};\bm{\rho}_{o})\|_{N}\to 0.

Since the same
\bm{x}
is subtracted in both posterior errors, this
implies

\|\bm{p}_{a}-\bm{p}_{o}\|_{N}\to 0,\qquad\|\bm{v}_{B,a}-\bm{v}_{B,o}\|_{N}\to 0.

The outgoing precision is

\gamma_{+,j}=\operatorname{Proj}_{[\gamma_{\min},\gamma_{\max}]}(v_{B,j}^{-1}-\rho_{j}).

Because
v_{B,j}\in[v_{\min},v_{\max}]
, the inverse map is Lipschitz.
Together with the non-expansiveness of the projection,

\|\bm{\gamma}_{+,a}-\bm{\gamma}_{+,o}\|_{N}\to 0.

The outgoing mean error is defined by

\gamma_{+,j}q_{+,j}=v_{B,j}^{-1}p_{j}-\rho_{j}s_{j}.

Let

r_{+,j}=v_{B,j}^{-1}p_{j}-\rho_{j}s_{j}.

We first show

\|\bm{r}_{+,a}-\bm{r}_{+,o}\|_{N}\to 0.

Indeed,

\displaystyle r_{+,a,j}-r_{+,o,j}

\displaystyle=(v_{B,a,j}^{-1}-v_{B,o,j}^{-1})p_{a,j}+v_{B,o,j}^{-1}(p_{a,j}-p_{o,j})

\displaystyle-(\rho_{a,j}-\rho_{o,j})s_{a,j}-\rho_{o,j}(s_{a,j}-s_{o,j}).

The second and fourth terms converge to zero directly from boundedness of

v_{B}^{-1}
and
\rho
. The first and third terms are controlled by
Lemma
22
, because the coefficient differences
converge in empirical norm and are uniformly bounded, while

\bm{p}_{a}
and
\bm{s}_{a}
have uniformly integrable empirical
second moments by the regularity of the scalar module and the input history.
Thus

\|\bm{r}_{+,a}-\bm{r}_{+,o}\|_{N}\to 0.

Finally,

\bm{q}_{+}=\bm{\Gamma}_{+}^{-1}\bm{r}_{+},

and
\gamma_{+,j}\in[\gamma_{\min},\gamma_{\max}]
. Since

\|\bm{\gamma}_{+,a}^{-1}-\bm{\gamma}_{+,o}^{-1}\|_{N}\to 0

by Lemma
21
, we conclude

\|\bm{q}_{+,a}-\bm{q}_{+,o}\|_{N}\to 0.

This proves Proposition
10
.

J-D

Stability of the Memory Map

We prove Proposition
11
. This part is needed
only for the corrected branch.

For each coordinate
j
, define the finite Gaussian-history covariance blocks

\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{<t,j}=(\mathcal{Z}_{0,j},\ldots,\mathcal{Z}_{t-1,j})^{\mathsf{T}},

\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,<t}=(\zeta_{j}^{r,s})_{0\leq r,s<t},\qquad\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t}=(\zeta_{j}^{t,0},\ldots,\zeta_{j}^{t,t-1}).

The memory map is

\mu_{t,j}=\alpha_{t,j}\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t}(\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{<t,j},

where

\alpha_{t,j}=(\bar{\pi}_{t,j}T_{t,j})^{-1}.

The innovation variance is

\tau_{t,j}=\alpha_{t,j}^{2}\left[\zeta_{j}^{t,t}-\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{t,<t}(\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{<t,t}\right].

We compare two versions, actual and oracle. We assume that the corresponding
finite covariance blocks, scalings, and past Gaussian histories are empirically
close:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\alpha_{a,j}-\alpha_{o,j}|^{2}\to 0,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|\bm{\zeta}_{a,j}^{t,<t}-\bm{\zeta}_{o,j}^{t,<t}\|^{2}\to 0,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|\bm{\zeta}_{a,j}^{<t,<t}-\bm{\zeta}_{o,j}^{<t,<t}\|_{F}^{2}\to 0,

and

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{a,<t,j}-\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{o,<t,j}\|^{2}\to 0.

These closeness relations are part of the branch-wise induction environment in
the corrected recursion.

Assumption
6
gives

\left\|(\bm{\zeta}_{a,j}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}\right\|\leq C_{\zeta},\qquad\left\|(\bm{\zeta}_{o,j}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}\right\|\leq C_{\zeta}

with probability tending to one. Since
t
is fixed and the nonzero spectra
are bounded away from zero, the Moore–Penrose inverse is locally Lipschitz on
the retained subspace. Hence

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\|(\bm{\zeta}_{a,j}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}-(\bm{\zeta}_{o,j}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}\right\|_{F}^{2}\to 0.

(290)

A direct expansion of

\alpha\bm{\zeta}^{t,<t}(\bm{\zeta}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{<t}

then gives

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\mu_{a,t,j}-\mu_{o,t,j}|^{2}\to 0.

Indeed, the difference is the sum of four terms, obtained by perturbing
successively
\alpha
,
\bm{\zeta}^{t,<t}
,

(\bm{\zeta}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}
, and
\bm{\mathcal{Z}}_{<t}
. Each term
is controlled by Cauchy–Schwarz, the uniform boundedness of the remaining
factors, and the empirical convergence assumptions above.

The same argument applies to the innovation variance. Expanding

\zeta^{t,t}-\bm{\zeta}^{t,<t}(\bm{\zeta}^{<t,<t})^{\dagger}\bm{\zeta}^{<t,t}

around the oracle quantities, using (
290
), and multiplying by
the stable factor
\alpha^{2}
, yields

\|\bm{\tau}_{a}-\bm{\tau}_{o}\|_{N}\to 0.

Since

0<\tau_{\min}\leq\tau_{a,j},\tau_{o,j}\leq\tau_{\max}<\infty

with probability tending to one, Lemma
21
gives

\|\bm{\tau}_{a}^{-1}-\bm{\tau}_{o}^{-1}\|_{N}\to 0.

Finally,

\widetilde{\bm{h}}_{a}-\widetilde{\bm{h}}_{o}=(\bm{h}_{a}-\bm{h}_{o})-(\bm{\mu}_{a}-\bm{\mu}_{o}),

so the stability of
\bm{h}
and
\bm{\mu}
gives

\|\widetilde{\bm{h}}_{a}-\widetilde{\bm{h}}_{o}\|_{N}\to 0.

This proves Proposition
11
.

J-E

Closing the Branch-Wise Replacement Induction

We finally show how the estimates above close the induction used in
Theorem
6
.

Assume that, for a fixed branch
b\in\{\mathrm{std},\mathrm{corr}\}
,

\|\bm{q}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{act}}-\bm{q}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{orc}}\|_{N}\to 0,

and

\|\bm{\gamma}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{act}}-\bm{\gamma}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{orc}}\|_{N}\to 0.

By Proposition
9
,

\|\bm{m}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{act}}-\bm{m}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{orc}}\|_{N}\to 0,

\|\bm{\pi}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{act}}-\bm{\pi}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{orc}}\|_{N}\to 0,

and

\|\bm{h}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{act}}-\bm{h}_{t}^{b,\mathrm{orc}}\|_{N}\to 0.

For the standard branch,

\bm{s}_{t}^{\mathrm{std}}=\bm{h}_{t},\qquad\bm{\rho}_{t}^{\mathrm{std}}=\bm{\pi}_{t}.

Therefore the input stability condition of
Proposition
10
holds, and so

\|\bm{\gamma}_{t+1}^{\mathrm{std},\mathrm{act}}-\bm{\gamma}_{t+1}^{\mathrm{std},\mathrm{orc}}\|_{N}\to 0,

\|\bm{q}_{t+1}^{\mathrm{std},\mathrm{act}}-\bm{q}_{t+1}^{\mathrm{std},\mathrm{orc}}\|_{N}\to 0.

For the corrected branch,

\bm{s}_{t}^{\mathrm{corr}}=\widetilde{\bm{h}}_{t}=\bm{h}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t},\qquad\bm{\rho}_{t}^{\mathrm{corr}}=\bm{\tau}_{t}^{-1}.

The stability of
\bm{h}_{t}
follows from the linear module, and the
stability of
\bm{\mu}_{t}
and
\bm{\tau}_{t}^{-1}
follows from
Proposition
11
. Hence

\|\bm{s}_{t}^{\mathrm{corr},\mathrm{act}}-\bm{s}_{t}^{\mathrm{corr},\mathrm{orc}}\|_{N}\to 0,

and

\|\bm{\rho}_{t}^{\mathrm{corr},\mathrm{act}}-\bm{\rho}_{t}^{\mathrm{corr},\mathrm{orc}}\|_{N}\to 0.

Applying Proposition
10
gives

\|\bm{\gamma}_{t+1}^{\mathrm{corr},\mathrm{act}}-\bm{\gamma}_{t+1}^{\mathrm{corr},\mathrm{orc}}\|_{N}\to 0,

and

\|\bm{q}_{t+1}^{\mathrm{corr},\mathrm{act}}-\bm{q}_{t+1}^{\mathrm{corr},\mathrm{orc}}\|_{N}\to 0.

Thus, in either branch, closeness at time
t
implies closeness at time

t+1
. Since the initial actual and oracle recursions are identical and the
horizon
T
is fixed, finite induction proves the branch-wise precision
replacement theorem.
