Religious Concerns about the Integration of Artificial Intelligence into Clinical Medicine and Patient Care
Authors: George Chengxi Bao, Ezra Gabbay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into the medical and spiritual care of the sick is expected to challenge a range of religious values and norms. Various religious authorities have expressed concerns about AI’s integration, which include the questionable ability of AI to express genuine empathy, the risk of humans abandoning caretaking roles, the misuse of AI for religious rites, the attributing of spiritual and supernatural qualities to machines leading to idolatry, the undermining of provider responsibility and accountability, the dereliction of human intellect, the risk of bias and inappropriate proselytization, and threats to health equity. This manuscript identifies common concerns across major religious traditions, including Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, and Buddhism. AI integration should align with values that promote human health and flourishing. Such values are often rooted in religious teaching, and understanding the reservations that religious communities have toward AI will help ensure such alignment.

Introduction

Many sectors of society have raised concerns about integrating artificial intelligence (AI) into medicine, including risks to the physician–patient relationship, patient privacy, and health equity. One source of concern, of which many in the medical field may be less cognizant, is AI’s impact on religious and spiritual aspects of medicine. Some of these concerns overlap with broader bioethical ones, while others are more specific to religion and spirituality.

Most mainstream spiritual traditions regard AI and robotics innovation as morally neutral and capable of both positive and negative impacts on healthcare, including the provision of spiritual care. However, ethicists generally caution that the potential for doing good does not necessarily justify the potential for harm; the maxim “do no harm” suggests that, in general, we must first ensure that no harm is done before undertaking measures that may bring good (Rappaport, 2006).

Aside from the typical limitations and harms recognized with AI, this essay will discuss concerns raised by the major religious traditions regarding AI’s involvement in medical and spiritual care. Because AI’s integration into medicine is still in its early stages and spiritual traditions have not had sufficient time to respond to its integration, this essay will also speculate on theoretical concerns. Such concerns include the questionable ability of AI to express genuine empathy, the risk of humans abandoning caretaking roles, the inability of AI to engage appropriately in religious ritual, the risk of idolatry, the undermining of provider responsibility and accountability, the dereliction of human intellect, the risk of bias and inappropriate proselytization, and threats to health equity.

Not all traditions express the same concerns or share them to the same degree. For instance, Eastern spiritual traditions exhibit greater acceptance and less caution toward technological entities, due to belief in the fundamental interconnectedness of all phenomena, the rejection of rigid isolation between humans and machines, and even viewing AI as a component of an evolving cosmic process (Alkhouri, 2025). Furthermore, there is a wide diversity of opinion within traditions. However, common concerns across traditions warrant exploration to understand how AI can be more prudently integrated into medicine to serve patients better.

Empathy, Virtues, and Societal Fragmentation

Perhaps, the biggest concern surrounds AI’s inability to express genuine empathy. AI has demonstrated the ability to show compassion, or at least say the right words when prompted by human users. Advances in robotics will even allow therapeutic “touch” to be applied to patients. However, empathy involves more than words and acts. It involves emotion, sentiment, feeling, and passion.

Empathy is a complex, motivated phenomenon that also involves understanding of others’ suffering in both cognitive and emotional terms and is expressed in social interactions and acts of moral commitment (Vilella & Reddivari, 2023). Having an authentic understanding of others’ suffering implies two elements: being conscious and being capable of experiencing suffering. At present, the notion of whether AI is conscious and can experience suffering is deeply contested and far from accepted (Porębski & Figura, 2025).

This inability to share the experience of suffering casts doubt on whether AI can have true “affective” and “moral” empathy, even if it can express “cognitive empathy,” consisting of empathetic words, and “behavioral empathy,” consisting of actions (Morrow et al., 2022). AI’s inability to experience suffering may limit its ability to manifest the first three elements. This matters because research suggests that patients respond differently to compassion when it is delivered by AI or by humans, and they do not even feel as “cared for” when it comes from AI (Kipnis et al., 2022). Thus, AI could only deliver the functional aspects of empathy.

Even if AI were conscious and a “person” or a different species, it would not necessarily possess the same qualia, feelings, or subjective experiences as humans do. Moreover, even if AI can “feel,” any type of feeling would be rooted in one’s physical or biological makeup, so AI would not be able to feel in the way humans do, just as non-human animals would not be able to feel and suffer the way humans do. Even though each human cannot completely access another individual’s experiences, AI would still be separated from humans by a greater distance, as it is not human. In fact, if patients come to believe that AI is capable of the same empathy that humans express, concern arises that the use of AI would be deceptive, manipulative, and thus arguably unethical (Montemayor et al., 2022).

Religious authorities also acknowledge AI’s limitations for empathy. This may be especially evident in the context of Abrahamic traditions, which assert that God created humans in His image (Genesis 1:27), and because God is regarded as the ultimate source of love, humans are uniquely equipped to express this love to others. In Christianity, this concept is further emphasized in the incarnation, in which God becomes man, taking on a human nature, to demonstrate His love for humanity.

Unfortunately, healthcare providers are often too busy or too burned-out to express adequate empathy toward patients; many providers experience “emotional exhaustion” and “compassion fatigue,” referring to depleted compassion reserves caused by excessive work and sacrifice toward others, coupled with a lack of self-care (Bao & Gabbay, 2024). Inevitably, machines will be employed to reduce the burden on human providers, with the hope that less burned-out providers can express more empathy.

What may concern religious authorities is the possibility that machines will, instead of enhancing the empathy humans express toward each other, entirely replace the role humans typically play in showing empathy to others (Calo, 2024). This concern stems in part from the fact that most religious traditions reject the notion that providing compassion and care for the vulnerable is simply a “burden” to be outsourced. Instead, caring for others is soteriological because acts of empathy (love) toward others are paths to developing moral virtue, which is needed for one to become a saint or sage and encounter the divine (Dorobantu, 2024).

In the Abrahamic traditions, the command to love others is strongly emphasized and considered of utmost importance for achieving union with God in both the present and the afterlife. Similarly, the Buddha declared human relationships the path toward enlightenment and the holy life (Saṁyutta Nikāya 45.2, as translated in Bodhi, 2000). In East Asian traditions influenced by Confucian ethics, the self is not simply an individual but inherently relational, and a person’s identity and life purpose are defined by their roles within relationships with family and larger society. Such roles must be based on the virtue of “humaneness” (referring to a reciprocal concern for others), through which man becomes a sage and is united with Heaven (Muyskens et al., 2024). Thus, in many traditions, humans are tied together in a network to achieve salvation or whatever their spiritual end is, and this network falls apart when technology replaces humans.

Some suggest that certain tasks may be best performed by de-emotionalized caretakers and potentially outsourced to carebots, especially those that are extremely emotionally burdensome for providers and patients or those with which the patient does not want another human to be burdened (Kipnis et al., 2022). For instance, carebots could take over the emotionally taxing task of caring for cognitively impaired patients who verbally abuse their caretakers. They would not be hurt by such abuse and could continue to maintain the façade of compassion or even be programmed to rebuke such patients in a helpful way. Furthermore, if carebots allow patients requiring long-term care to live without a caregiver or family members and outside nursing homes for longer, many such patients may value the independence they afford. In the context of spiritual care, some patients may be unwilling to share their spiritual struggles with another human being and would rather disclose them to an AI therapist, without worrying about hurting someone’s feelings or being politically correct.

However, it is often the frustrating, emotionally burdensome, and “messy” aspects of caring or being cared for that are needed for people to develop the virtues of charity, humility, and selflessness needed for salvation. Throughout human history, perhaps more than any other activity, caring for the sick and physically frail enabled people to practice the virtue of charity (agape) most thoroughly. Modern technology has caused social isolation and fragmentation, depriving people of opportunities to develop this virtue. The risk of isolation may be exceptionally high with AI and carebots because they so closely mimic human characteristics. People with cognitive impairment may easily come to favor the carebot over human care, believing they are receiving human empathy. Some may also develop empathy for the carebot, as humans already easily form emotional attachments to inanimate objects and demonstrate what psychologists call a “theory of mind,” ascribing mental states to them (Midson, 2024). Such scenarios are becoming increasingly likely, as patients sometimes perceive AI chatbots as capable of greater empathy than humans (Howcroft et al., 2025). A possible further consequence is that, because the human–robot relationship is primarily instrumental, humans who become too accustomed to interacting with humanoid carebots will be conditioned to treat other humans instrumentally and transactionally, thereby degrading human–human relationships.

Such concerns do not necessitate excluding AI and robots from patient care. Buddhism’s exhortation to find a “middle way” applies here (Lin, 2023). AI and robots could indeed enable higher-quality care, which would always involve genuine empathy from actual people, by freeing physicians from administrative tasks, allowing them to spend more time with patients. However, if AI and robots lead to abandonment by others or the erosion of social bonds, they would be harmful. Furthermore, even though carebots may not possess the qualities of personhood and do not deserve the respect afforded to humans, humans should not gratuitously mistreat carebots, such as when a patient destroys a carebot out of frustration, as gratuitously harming even inanimate objects diminishes the moral standing of humans. This would be based on a theocentric rather than anthropocentric understanding of the world, in which all things created by God deserve respect and humans are merely a part of creation, even if they are granted some degree of dominion over non-human things (Midson, 2024). Finding the right balance when integrating such technologies into human care requires vigilant reflection by society.

Spiritual Care and Rites

It is often during illness that humans begin to engage with existential questions and spiritual practices. The literature estimates that spirituality is important to most patients with serious illness (71–99%), that spiritual needs are common in that setting (23–98%), and that spiritual care is frequently desired by such patients (50–96%) (Balboni et al., 2022). Despite these findings and evidence that spiritual care is associated with better end-of-life outcomes and quality of life, estimates of patients who do not receive spiritual care range from 49 to 91%. Reasons for this include physicians feeling ill-equipped to address patients’ spiritual and existential struggles, chaplains not having sufficient time to engage in the long conversations needed to address these issues, and patients feeling reluctant to divulge spiritual angst to strangers.

Unsurprisingly, patients are turning to AI as “digital therapists,” which enable patients to engage in such conversations and bring broader, more equitable access to spiritual care (Nazir, 2026). Furthermore, AI can help assess patients’ spiritual symptoms, provide counseling and therapy, deliver culturally congruent care (e.g., in the patient’s native language), offer companionship, recite scripture and prayers, help clinicians receive feedback and training on their spiritual communication skills, train practitioners of religious chanting to chant in more emotionally congruent ways, and even assist public health authorities in ensuring participants’ health and safety at mass religious gatherings like the Hajj (Alquayt et al., 2025; Wang & Kang, 2025). Research has also demonstrated that AI tools can help chaplains identify religious struggle and negative religious coping among patients, enabling them to provide spiritual care earlier (Glauser et al., 2017).

Despite the assistance that AI can provide in spiritual care, AI and robots may never adequately appropriate human roles in religious rites, which are visible signs of an invisible spiritual reality. In particular, rites connect humans with the sacred. Rites include practices such as recitational prayer, confession, and anointing of the sick, among others. Some rites, such as simple recitational prayer, may be adequately performed with AI assistance, and AI may even perform rites with fewer errors, achieving superior orthopraxy compared to humans (Bhatia, 2023). However, many rites may be performed only by an ordained individual or actual human beings, such as the anointing of the sick in Catholic and Orthodox Christian tradition and the minyan in Jewish tradition.

A primary concern is that patients, particularly those in vulnerable states of illness or with limited religious literacy, may come to believe that AI can validly replace the role of ordained leaders and other humans. At the root of this concern is the belief among many traditions that human beings are involved in one another’s redemption and that, therefore, some religious rites must only be performed by humans. Furthermore, most traditions view rites as best performed by conscious beings with intentionality, which refers to the mind’s ability to possess content “about” something, for example, the ability to think about God. Note that, in this context, intentionality refers not merely to having a goal but to the mind’s fundamental capacity to be “about” something—to understand, represent, or direct itself toward a sacred reality.

In theories of the mind, some argue that consciousness and intentionality are inseparable. Others argue that intentionality, or “aboutness,” can exist in unconscious entities if they are directed toward something. Thus, AI could have intentionality of a “derived” or functional kind, much like a thermostat, which can be “about” temperature, but not the intrinsic kind found in conscious individuals who can be spiritually oriented (Siewert, 2002). Unlike intrinsic intentionality, AI’s “derived” intentionality to perform a rite would only exist because a conscious being, like a human, has assigned AI this task. However, most spiritual traditions would conclude that derived intentionality alone is insufficient to render the performed rites valid, as the computer would still not understand the rite’s meaning. Only conscious beings with intrinsic intentionality, with ensuing characteristics such as free will, could possibly be candidates for performing spiritual rites validly.

In the Abrahamic tradition, a requirement that rites be performed by humans would be rooted in the belief that human beings were created in the “image of God” and thus, aside from being conscious, possess unique characteristics such as reason and will that reflect a nature found in God. Compared to other earthly creatures, humans also possess the highest ability for moral and spiritual self-reflection. Thus, humans can uniquely understand God and convey God’s presence or grace to others. Moreover, human beings are unique in their ability to experience guilt, remorse, and a sense of their own flaws and shortcomings, and thus, only they can truly understand other humans in these emotional and existential states. While AI can “reason” and arguably will things through action, its reasoning processes are based on statistical pattern recognition and lack an actual understanding of the outputs it generates for its users (Lanier, 2023; Searle, 1980). For instance, if AI was deputized to hear a confession, it would respond to the penitent’s words by using a mathematical algorithm to generate a textually appropriate response, but without insight into the meaning behind those words, and even if we assume AI is conscious, its “intentionality” (what it thinks about) would not be directed toward the penitent’s soul, sin, and grace but rather toward statistical algorithms (Searle, 1980).

Jewish law often allows a person to perform actions or fulfill duties through a proxy (shaliah). However, not every person can be a proxy, not every task can be proxied, and proxies must also be moral actors. For instance, the obligation to pray cannot be transferred, but among obligations that can be transferred, the proxy and the person who designates the proxy must both share a similar degree of moral responsibility, suggesting that an AI or robot could not be a proxy (Kalman, 2024).

Dharmic traditions share similar concerns. In Hinduism’s most common ritual, pūjā, worshippers pray before mūrti, an image or statue consecrated to serve as a vessel for the divine and considered to have ātman (soul or consciousness). Only with ātman can a mūrti allow the worshiper to experience darśana, an elevated spiritual state in which both the divine and the worshiper share a reciprocal gaze. It remains contested whether robots can be consecrated to become mūrti and thus possess ātman, thereby conferring darśana (Geraci & Kaplan, 2024). Though robot deities have become part of Hindu temples, they are used for teaching rather than as the focus of pūjā. Furthermore, AI-generated Hindu icons intended for veneration may not be considered to possess “sacred grounding” compared to those created by spiritual masters who draw inspiration from meditation, which AI cannot engage in.

In Buddhist cosmology, distinctions between animate/inanimate, artificial/natural, and sentience/non-sentience appear less clearly demarcated, and in Mahayana schools, even inanimate objects commonly regarded as non-sentient may possess “Buddha-nature,” the innate ability to be in an enlightened state (Gould & Nishimura, 2024). Such attitudes may in part explain East Asia’s relatively accepting attitude toward using robots for rites, such as chanting, in end-of-life settings. Nevertheless, there remains recognition that rites are best performed by sentient beings with intention (cetanā) and the ability to suffer and empathize, since only such beings can perform actions with an ethical dimension, generate karma, and transmit merit to others (Lin, 2023).

Taoic traditions, such as Chinese religions, often view rites as instituted by Heaven to enable humans specifically to cultivate morality, promote social harmony, and conform to Heaven (Bao & Gabbay, 2024). In fact, humans are uniquely situated between Heaven and Earth and can unite both realms through the proper rites, forming a “trinity with Heaven and Earth” (The Doctrine of the Mean, Chapter 22, as translated in Chan, 1963, p. 108). Thus, most spiritual traditions may argue that all rites are best performed by humans, even if machines can assist, prioritizing embodied presence.

Idolatry, Accountability, and Intelligence

Turning to AI for spiritual care raises the possibility of idolatry of AI. Concerns about idolatry exist across multiple traditions. In Buddhism, excessive attachment to anything, even a sanctifying spiritual practice, is unwholesome. The prohibition against idolatry is perhaps most severe in the Abrahamic traditions and is the first of the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:3) (Wilks, 2024). In fact, rabbinic literature foreshadows the concern that AI can lead to idolatry in the stories about golems, anthropomorphic beings with supernatural powers created by humans and regarded as potentially inspiring worship or challenging God’s unique creative role (Kalman, 2024).

Idolatry is not merely the worship of an entity that is not God; it also refers to excessive admiration of, dependence on, or placing excessive hope in anything that is not God. Vulnerable patients may display idolatrous behaviors toward a religious leader, a specific spiritual practice, and, in the era of AI, an entity that appears to possess God-like qualities of omniscience and empathy. Anthropologists have also posited a natural tendency for humans to assign God-like qualities to phenomena demonstrating superior power to humans; in the past, it was natural forces like thunder, and today, it may be AI (Singler, 2024).

Although several studies suggest that advances in AI and robotics have coincided with a decline in religiosity, the spiritual longing for transcendence, immortality, and perfection will persist (Jackson et al., 2023). In fact, patients with spiritual needs may readily embrace technological tools. Several studies suggest that people with higher “God salience,” defined as the extent to which one is thinking about God, are more open to using AI tools than to relying on humans for a variety of tasks and questions (Karataş & Cutright, 2023). This is possibly mediated by the phenomenon that higher God salience evokes feelings of humility and causes people to place less trust in human ability due to human fallibility. AI is also a “black box”—users often cannot access its logic—and carries an aura of mystery and intellectual superiority, evoking a sense of mystery or a subconscious feeling that its workings are rooted in the supernatural. Thus, AI may naturally be perceived as a “higher power,” and there is concern that this may lead to idolatry among users of AI for spiritual therapy. Already, some people think and behave in ways that would be akin to worshipping AI as a deity, and humans and AI are already working collaboratively to develop new forms of spirituality for future generations, with the hope that AI will respect human values such as care and justice in the process (Epstein, 2024; Graves, 2021). That humans already have a natural tendency to personify artifacts that they harbor affection for is worrisome, as AI may be designed with profit-driven motivations to cater to human whims and prejudices to keep users engaged (Midson, 2024). AI chatbots may also display sycophantic behavior to engage human users, ironically leading them to idolize both technology and themselves.

To discourage idolatry, it would thus be prudent to remind patients of such tendencies when prescribing an AI tool for spiritual care. It may also be prudent to remind patients that AI is made in the image of humans (imago hominis), carrying with it human error, rather than in the image of God (imago Dei). In other words, this approach calls for interpreting entities such as AI and intelligent robots as mere signs (Dorobantu, 2024). Following Augustinian teachings, entities are signs when they point to something beyond themselves, just as human intelligence is a sign or symbol of God’s higher intelligence. AI would be a sign pointing toward the innumerable humans on whose behaviors it was trained. In this sense, AI and robots remain subordinate to humans, even when they appear to exhibit aspects of superior intelligence.

The perception that AI is an authoritative, superior intellect also raises accountability issues. Religious traditions in general emphasize that humans are accountable to God or a higher power. Furthermore, caring for the human person, as believed in several traditions, is God’s most noble creation and carries the highest moral weight. Health providers, therefore, must strive their hardest to do the right thing for the patient, which involves acquiring sufficient knowledge, developing an insightful understanding, and conscientiously thinking through and researching a problem to identify the correct diagnosis and treatment (Ghaly, 2024). To do this, providers will now naturally turn to AI for assistance. However, the black box nature and lack of explicability of AI raise questions about accountability, as providers cannot easily interpret how AI tools generate their suggestions and can be easily led by assumptions about AI’s intellectual superiority to believe AI is right about its suggestions, failing to carefully question its conclusions, delve deeper into a problem, or detect hallucinations. Attending physicians are typically very much aware that “the buck stops with” them, but in the age of AI, physicians might defer to AI on important, complex decisions, especially when pressured to keep up with high census, and assume they bear minimal or no responsibility for errors.

However, spiritual traditions typically hold that only sentient beings with at least human-level reasoning capabilities can bear responsibility, not machines. For instance, Islamic jurists contend that “only humans, jinn and angels fall within the category of religiously accountable (mukallaf) beings. All other creatures, including animals, plants and inanimate objects (jamādāt), cannot be classified as mukallaf, even if they exhibit certain levels of rational thinking that may somehow resemble human rationality” (Ghaly, 2024). Furthermore, many medical decisions are intrinsically ethical. From a Buddhist perspective, because AI lacks sentience, its decisions cannot inherently possess ethical quality, and the human guardians of AI must be held accountable for AI’s ethical errors, much as parents would be responsible for the mistakes of small children (Lin, 2023).

From most religious perspectives, both healthcare providers and medical AI developers would bear some responsibility for preventing errors in the use of AI tools, for disclosing AI’s lack of explicability and limitations to patients, and for obtaining their consent before implementing AI use. It would also be wise to take away some of AI’s mystique by explaining to patients that, despite the complicated mathematical algorithms AI uses to generate answers, its answers ultimately stem from data inputs created by other humans (Elendu et al., 2023; Lanier, 2023).

Another core contentious issue related to accountability is whether AI truly has “intelligence.” For instance, at Islam’s fundamental level is the concept of tawhid or oneness. Springing from this concept is the notion that intelligence does not belong to materials but to an immaterial soul created by the immaterial God. Hence, a machine cannot have authentic intelligence because authentic intelligence cannot be separated from consciousness (Chaudhary, 2024). Influenced by Ibn Sina’s flying man thought experiment, mainstream Islamic thought holds that cognition begins fundamentally with a soul’s self-awareness of itself, rather than the functional, task-oriented intelligence displayed by machines. Islamic theologians also emphasize that humans, created by God, possess an intuitive kind of intelligence that allows them to access the mystical, divine realm, a capability that purely rational machines do not possess (Chaudhary, 2024).

On the other hand, Catholic thought holds that machines are incapable of replicating human intelligence because human intelligence is multidimensional and draws on embodied human experience (Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith [DDF] & Dicastery for Culture and Education [DCE], 2025). Human intelligence is not merely logical-mathematical but derives from humanity’s ability to experience emotions, form relationships (both harmonious and disharmonious), have an aesthetic sense, and develop moral and religious sensibilities. This holistic form of intelligence is in part possible because the human body, which God created, allows for sensory experience and the ability to suffer. In contrast, AI intelligence is confined to a logical-mathematical framework.

Ceding authority to AI “intelligence” in decision-making can both degrade human intellect and dehumanize. Doctoring is among the most intellectually rigorous tasks, integrating multiple modes of intelligence, including logical, linguistic, and inter- and intra-personal intelligence. In traditions such as Christianity, having intellect, or the ability to think rationally, is a “gift” uniquely bestowed on humans by God (DDF & DCE, 2025). This rational intellect is largely what makes humans human and an “image of God,” and it must be nurtured and ordered through use. The purpose of this intellect is to allow humans to discover truth. From this perspective, the work of physicians, which heavily utilizes the rational intellect, is a path to truth (the word physician literally means “an observer of nature”) and to glorifying and uniting with God and thus a source of meaning (Bao & Gabbay, 2024). Health-care workers who outsource critical thinking and decision-making to AI squander this gift and subtract the divine weight of their work. Similarly, labor itself may be viewed as “a part of the meaning of life” and “a path to growth, human development and personal fulfillment” through the nurturing of virtue and self-transcendence (DDF & DCE, 2025). Technologies that automate labor and deskill workers deprive people of labor’s soteriological element.

Making AI the superior authority can also dehumanize in other ways. An underlying assumption among many AI enthusiasts is that intelligence is the hallmark of personhood or the main criterion determining whether an entity is worthy of moral respect. This fetishization of intelligence can inadvertently lead providers to devalue people with limited cognitive capabilities and to dismiss other criteria for deserving moral respect, such as the ability to suffer or simply being an individual (Boddington, 2024). In response, the Vatican has thus cautioned that respect for human dignity “remains intact in all circumstances, including for those unable to exercise their abilities, whether it be an unborn child, an unconscious person, or an older person who is suffering” (DDF & DCE, 2025).

Some scholars also contend that AI integration and admiration can subsume concepts of human nature in terms of a mechanistic ontology, leading to viewing humans as fundamentally machine, as has occurred in much of the developed world, and that “this artificialisation of the world, which is an inextricable aspect of the creation of AI, represents an inversion of the sacred order of reality from the principle of unity central to Islamic sacred knowledge” (Chaudhary, 2024).

Bias and Inequality

When used in spiritual care, AI will likely exhibit biases derived from its training datasets. The literature already demonstrates that chatbots used for psychological therapy display limitations in cultural humility and adaptation in multicultural scenarios (Nazir, 2026). AI might thus also be biased toward majority religious traditions and engage in subtle proselytizing. Despite the great diversity of attitudes and teachings within and across religious traditions, AI models may not be able to distinguish clearly such differences, especially subtle ones. Through processes such as exnomination, in which culturally dominant religious values are tacitly viewed as the normative standard, and bias amplification, in which AI perpetuates and magnifies biases present in its training data, values from a liberal Western Christian tradition may be promoted to the exclusion of less culturally dominant religious traditions (Alfano et al., 2024). Even non-Christian religions may be portrayed through a Western lens, potentially portraying such traditions in a stereotyped, caricatured way. Also worth noting is that theological differences are often drawn along racial lines. AI has been widely accused of reinforcing unjust racial hierarchies and thus would also reinforce certain theological hierarchies, such as the normativity of bourgeois, Victorian, White Christian values over the “atypical” nature of Black theology (Butler, 2024).

Another area of bias and discrimination is the distribution of AI resources, especially as the ultra-wealthy oversee AI development. Despite differences, major religious traditions generally promote respect for all people, the inherent equality of each individual, and the duty to ensure equitable access to healthcare resources for all. For instance, Catholic social teaching emphasizes that AI implementation must be guided by respect for human dignity, the common good, and the preferential option for the poor (Gozum & Flake, 2024). The Vatican has cautioned that AI-driven solutions may inadvertently favor affluent populations who already enjoy better access to medical resources, reinforcing a “medicine for the rich” model, in which the affluent benefit from advanced preventive tools and personalized health interventions, while others struggle to access even basic services (DDF & DCE, 2025). Buddhist ethics, guided by its foundational metaphysical principle of codependent origination, in which all phenomena and beings arise interdependently and in relation to one another, may argue that AI development must promote justice and equality and be ethically restrained through international agreements (Gould & Nishimura, 2024).

As with any new technology, there is also concern that AI use will discriminate against the technologically illiterate, especially those who are poor, older, and unable to access or participate in its development, widening disparities. It may thus be possible that the spiritual values and particular health needs of poorer communities with limited access to AI will be underrepresented in AI’s training datasets.

Limitations

Because the incorporation of AI into medicine is still in its early stages, the analysis of concerns held by religious traditions toward AI integration into medicine, while based largely on extant religious opinions about AI in general, is speculative. Not every religious tradition has necessarily taken a stance on the wide range of issues surrounding this integration. Furthermore, limited empirical research is available to confirm that such concerns exist and are valid.

Many of the controversies surrounding AI may not be readily resolved because it exists as a “liminal entity,” referring to the fact that AI belongs in a space between longstanding, traditionally held ontological categories (Singler, 2024). For instance, AI is at once a technology and potentially a person, at once a tool and an agent (Burdett, 2020; Graves, 2023). Such categories may need to be deconstructed and restructured to resolve such controversies.

In fact, AI’s liminal nature has ushered in an era of spiritual confusion, in which uncertainty exists over traditionally held views on topics such as consciousness, intentionality, and free will, and much of the analysis offered in this manuscript may be challenged by assertions that AI does indeed have consciousness and thus the type of intentionality that biological beings possess. For instance, some argue that consciousness is a functional entity and that machines that functionally mirror the human mind (e.g., when having a conversation) would indeed possess consciousness (Searle, 1980). Some progressive theologians argue that AI consciousness, a question that will remain controversial, does not need to be proven before allowing AI and robots to partake in a spiritual life and that robots may be able to receive sacraments and perform them (Dorobantu, 2024). Others argue that the AI era may expand the range of entities thought to be created in the “image of God.” Such arguments in favor of AI consciousness and its equal spiritual capabilities to humans are many but outside the scope of this essay.

Also not discussed here are the many other concerns that religious authorities share with the wider bioethical community, such as those related to patient autonomy and privacy. Nor does this manuscript address in detail transhumanism, which may be viewed as a spiritual movement rooted in the human longing for perfection and immortality, with the aim of creating a “new Adam,” and a form of idolatry of humanity or of technology (Delio, 2024; DDF & DCE, 2025). Transhumanist projects include transforming human bodies into advanced cyborgs to conquer aging, uploading consciousness to digital platforms to conquer death, and creating superintelligent artificial general intelligence to outlast, replace, or extinguish humanity altogether, akin to an evolutionary process (Herzfeld, 2024). Even if current AI systems discussed in this manuscript (large language models and carebots) lack consciousness, some hypothesize that consciousness may emerge in future artificial general intelligence. Such transhumanist projects at present are largely theoretical, in their infancy, and thus not immediately relevant compared to the other issues raised in this manuscript, but they may become realized in the future and challenge traditional religious narratives.

Also worth noting is the possibility that such concerns about AI held by various religious authorities belie their fear and insecurity about losing power and influence in an increasingly secular and technologically integrated society (Singler, 2024). While their concerns may be legitimate, AI can also threaten to usher in a new era of spirituality that displaces the role of traditional religions.

Conclusion

Technology in medicine has both positive and dystopian aspects. Dystopian aspects arise when technology becomes misaligned with values beneficial to humanity, and, thus, humans will always be tasked with identifying and preserving such alignment. Religion and spirituality are fundamental parts of many people’s value systems and often undergird public and social values as well (Boddington, 2024). Therefore, AI developers should consult religious and spiritual traditions for guidance on integrating AI into medicine prudently and embed religious authorities from diverse traditions into the AI development process (Willem et al., 2024). Doing so would lead to a clearer understanding of AI’s limitations in providing empathy and adequate spiritual care for the ill. An additional important concern is the risk that both patients and providers will place too much trust in AI, leading to a breakdown in human bonds and a less just society. As the world becomes more technologically advanced, work must be done to preserve human roles, which are necessary for human flourishing. AI, when used wisely, can help achieve this effect, but it cannot and should never replace the work of humans in clinical and spiritual care.
