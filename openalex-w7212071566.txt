Abstract
Background: Lung cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Immune evasion
and chronic inflammation are central determinants of tumor progression and therapeutic resistance. We aimed to
analyze recent scientific literature addressing the role of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in lung cancer,
focusing on their mechanisms of action and clinical relevance. Methods: An integrative literature review was
conducted using the PubMed and Virtual Health Library (BVS) databases, employing the descriptors “MyeloidDerived Suppressor Cells” and “Lung Neoplasms.” The search was restricted to English-language articles published
between 2020 and 2025. Study selection followed the PRISMA guidelines, resulting in a final sample of 61 original
studies. Results: Evidence indicates that MDSCs—particularly the polymorphonuclear (PMN-MDSC) and monocytic
(M-MDSC) subsets—are actively recruited to the tumor microenvironment by chemokines such as CCL2 and
CXCL5. These cells exert potent immunosuppressive effects through the production of reactive oxygen species
(ROS), arginase-1, and the expression of PD-L1, leading to inhibition of cytotoxic T lymphocytes and natural killer
cells. Elevated MDSC levels were consistently associated with poor prognosis, metastatic progression, and resistance
to therapies, including immune checkpoint inhibitors and targeted treatments. Conclusion: MDSCs function as central
regulators of tumor aggressiveness and constitute major barriers to effective anticancer immunity. Current literature
supports the targeting of MDSC depletion or functional reprogramming as a promising strategy to overcome
immunosuppression and improve clinical outcomes in lung cancer.
Keywords: MDSC; Immunosuppression; Lung neoplasms; Non-small cell lung cancer; NSCLC.

1. Introduction
Overview of lung cancer
Lung cancer is the most frequently diagnosed malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality
worldwide. According to 2022 data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) of the World Health
Organization (WHO), lung cancer accounts for 12.4% of all newly diagnosed cancer cases globally. Regarding mortality, it
ranks first among men (22.7%) and second among women (13.5%), surpassed only by breast cancer. Tobacco smoking,
including passive exposure, remains the predominant risk factor and is responsible for approximately 85% of lung cancer cases
(INCA, 2022). Additional risk factors include occupational exposure to carcinogenic agents such as cadmium, asbestos,
beryllium, and arsenic, which collectively contribute to 17–29% of cases, according to estimates from the International Labour
Organization (ILO).
The lung cancer survival rate reflects the proportion of patients who remain alive for a defined period following
diagnosis. The average five-year relative survival rate is approximately 18%, indicating that only one in five patients survives
beyond this timeframe when compared to cancer-free individuals of the same age and sex (INCA, 2022).
The four most common histological subtypes of lung cancer are squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, large cell
carcinoma, and small cell carcinoma. The first three—adenocarcinoma, large cell carcinoma, and squamous cell carcinoma—
are collectively classified as non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), as they share similar responses to chemotherapy and

Antitumor immune response
Antitumor immune responses are primarily mediated by cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) through mechanisms of
immune surveillance (Abbas et al., 2023). Following antigen uptake by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) via phagocytosis,
macrophages and dendritic cells migrate from the tumor microenvironment (TME) to the draining lymph nodes (Palucka et al.,
2012).
In these secondary lymphoid organs, tumor antigens are presented to naïve CD8⁺ T cells through major
histocompatibility complex class I (MHC I) molecules, known in humans as human leukocyte antigens (HLA), promoting Tcell activation, clonal expansion, and differentiation (Kumar, 2011). The high expression of tumor-associated antigens
characteristic of malignant cells enables their recognition by CD8⁺ CTLs, leading to tumor cell killing via the induction of
programmed cell death (Abbas et al., 2023). CD4⁺ effector T cells, particularly T helper 1 (Th1) cells, also play a critical role
within the TME by promoting macrophage activation and natural killer (NK) cell cytotoxicity (Zou, 2005; Abbas et al., 2023).
Despite these mechanisms, tumor cells employ multiple strategies to evade immune surveillance. Reduced expression
of immunogenic antigens and downregulation of MHC I molecules impair CD8⁺ T-cell recognition and activation, allowing
tumor cells to escape immune-mediated elimination (Korkolopoulou et al., 1996). Another major immune evasion mechanism
involves the upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules. Cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) competes
with CD28 for binding to B7 molecules expressed on APCs, thereby inhibiting costimulatory signaling and inducing long-term
T-cell anergy (Pardoll, 2012). Tumor cells may also overexpress programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) and/or PD-L2, which
interact with PD-1 receptors on CD8⁺ T cells, delivering inhibitory signals that suppress T-cell activation and effector function
(Pardoll, 2012).
Additionally, tumor cells secrete immunosuppressive factors such as transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β),
interleukin-10 (IL-10), indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) (Meireson A et al., 2020), tryptophan metabolites (Zou, 2005),
prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) (Kumar, 2011) that can directly impact tumor
progression. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) are also expanded and activated within the TME, contributing significantly to tumorassociated immunosuppression (Zou, 2005).
Specifically, in lung cancer, immune evasion mechanisms largely mirror those described in other malignancies.
Increased frequencies of CTLA-4–expressing Tregs capable of inhibiting CTL activity have been documented (Woo et al.,
2001). Altered PD-1 expression on T CD8+ cells and increased PD-L1 expression by non-small cell lung cancer have also
been reported (Zhang et al., 2010; Chen et al., 2012). Furthermore, lung tumor cells have been shown to directly secrete
immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β (Domagala-Kulawik et al., 2014).
Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs)
Myelopoiesis is the process responsible for the generation of leukocytes, including monocytes and granulocytes
(eosinophils, basophils, and neutrophils). Under pathological conditions such as infection, tissue injury, or cancer, emergency
myelopoiesis may be triggered to rapidly replenish myeloid cells (Brusa et al., 2013).
While myelopoiesis is typically transient and self-limited, chronic inflammatory states—such as cancer—can lead to
sustained and dysregulated myelopoiesis, resulting in the accumulation of immature myeloid cells with aberrant differentiation
and activation profiles (Bronte et al., 2016). These immature myeloid cells are collectively termed myeloid-derived suppressor
cells (MDSCs) and are broadly classified into two major subsets: polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs) and monocytic

MDSCs (M-MDSCs) (Movahedi et al., 2008; Youn et al., 2008). A third population, known as early-stage MDSCs (eMDSCs),
comprises more immature progenitors and has also been described (Bronte et al., 2016).
Considering MDSCs identification, in murine models, PMN-MDSCs are phenotypically defined as CD11b⁺ Ly6G⁺
Ly6Clow, whereas M-MDSCs are characterized as CD11b⁺ Ly6G⁻ Ly6Chigh (Peranzoni et al., 2010; Gabrilovich et al., 2012;
Bronte et al., 2016). In humans, MDSCs are also classified into monocytic MDSCs (mMDSCs; HLA-DR⁻/low CD33⁺ CD11b⁺
CD14⁺), granulocytic or polymorphonuclear MDSCs (gMDSCs; Lin⁻ HLA-DR⁻/low CD33⁺ CD11b⁺ CD15⁺), and early-stage
MDSCs (eMDSCs; Lin⁻ HLA-DR⁻/low CD33⁺) (Bronte et al., 2016). Beyond their phenotypic characteristics, the defining
feature of MDSCs is their potent immunosuppressive activity.
MDSCs suppress multiple immune populations, including NK cells and B lymphocytes (Elkabets et al., 2010; Hongo
et al., 2014; Li et al., 2014). However, inhibition of T-cell responses—particularly CD8⁺ T cells—remains the gold standard
for functional MDSC identification (Bronte et al., 2016). The close phenotypic resemblance of PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs
to neutrophils and monocytes, respectively, represents a major challenge for their accurate discrimination and functional
characterization.
Although MDSCs were initially described in the context of cancer, their roles have since been explored in
autoimmunity, viral and bacterial infections, fungal diseases, sepsis, and maternal–fetal tolerance. In autoimmune diseases,
MDSCs display heterogeneous and sometimes contradictory roles, reflecting their phenotypic plasticity (Xiong et al., 2024). In
viral infections—including SIV, murine AIDS (LP-BM5), hepatitis C virus, and SARS-CoV-2—marked expansion and
immunosuppressive activity of MDSCs have been reported (Koushki et al., 2021). In sepsis, MDSCs exert a dual role: they
initially limit excessive inflammation and tissue damage but subsequently contribute to prolonged immunosuppression, which
is associated with poor clinical outcomes (Zhang et al., 2023). In some fungi infections such as the one caused by
Paracoccidioides brasiliensis, MDSCs promote immunosuppressive environments that exacerbate disease severity (Kaminski
et al., 2025). Moreover, in pregnancy, the accumulation of MDSCs contributes to maternal–fetal immune tolerance, proper
placentation, and fetal development, whereas their deficiency is associated with complications such as preeclampsia, preterm
birth, stillbirth, and recurrent miscarriage (Shah et al., 2023; Fragoso et al., 2026).
In lung cancer, MDSCs play a pivotal role in tumor development and progression by shaping an immunosuppressive
TME that favors immune evasion (Yang et al., 2020). These cells suppress dendritic cell function through the secretion of
TGF-β and IL-10, which also promote Treg expansion and differentiation (Zhang et al., 2016). TGF-β, in particular, inhibits
NK cell activation (Husain et al., 2016) and impairs B-cell function by disrupting IL-7 signaling, leading to reduced STAT5
activation and decreased IgG production (Wang et al., 2018). MDSCs further inhibit CTL activity through multiple
mechanisms, including PD-L1 expression (Yamauchi et al., 2018), T-cell receptor (TCR) signaling interference, and increased
adenosine production via CD39 and CD73 pathways, ultimately suppressing T-cell activation and effector function (Li et al.,
2017).
Considering all the abovementioned data, in this article, we aimed to analyze recent scientific literature addressing the
role of MDSCs in different types of lung cancer, focusing on their mechanisms of action and clinical relevance. Particular
attention was given to the mechanisms underlying MDSC recruitment, expansion, and immunosuppressive activity within the
lung cancer tumor microenvironment, as well as their interactions with other immune and tumor-associated cells. In addition,
we sought to examine the association between MDSC accumulation and disease progression, metastasis, prognosis, and
therapeutic resistance, highlighting their potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets in lung cancer.

2. Methodology
This study is an integrative literature review (Page et al., 2021; Snyder, 2019), employing a quantitative approach to
describe the 61 articles selected for the research corpus and a qualitative approach to analyze and discuss the role of myeloidderived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in lung cancer (Risemberg et al., 2026; Pereira et al., 2018). To conduct this integrative
review, the descriptors “(Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cells) AND (Lung Neoplasms)” were used to search the PubMed and
Virtual Health Library (BVS) databases. In PubMed, results were filtered to include studies published within the last five years
(2020–2025). In the BVS database, the “complete BVS collection” was used, applying filters for publications from the last five
years, lung neoplasms as the main subject, and English language. Synonyms or variations of the term “Lung Neoplasms” were
not employed, as it is the official Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) descriptor and is widely accepted in the biomedical
literature to comprehensively represent pulmonary tumors. The exclusive use of this term aimed to ensure greater precision,
specificity, and standardization across databases.

3. Results
The database search identified a total of 544 articles, including 325 from PubMed and 219 from BVS. Initial
screening based on titles and abstracts resulted in the exclusion of 404 studies that did not align with this review. The main
exclusion criteria at this stage included review articles, studies not addressing MDSCs, or studies in which lung cancer was not
the primary tumor site.
Following this screening, 140 articles were considered potentially eligible and subjected to full-text evaluation.
During this eligibility phase, 79 studies were excluded. Twenty-seven were removed as duplicates, 7 were identified as review
articles that had not been excluded during the initial screening, and 45 were excluded after full-text assessment because they
did not meet the inclusion criteria or because the full text was unavailable. Consequently, 61 studies remained and were
included in the final analysis.
Finally, the 61 eligible articles were included in the final analysis and discussion, as illustrated in the study selection
flow diagram (Figure 1). The main characteristics and key findings of the selected studies—including study focus, lung cancer
subtype, first author, and year of publication—are summarized in Tables 1–3.
Table 1 presents studies addressing the role of MDSCs in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), summarizing evidence
regarding their accumulation, immunosuppressive mechanisms, and association with disease progression and therapeutic
response. The studies included in this table provide an overview of the clinical and biological relevance of MDSCs in NSCLC
and highlight their contribution to tumor-associated immune dysfunction. Table 2 summarizes studies specifically investigating
MDSCs in lung adenocarcinoma, with emphasis on their role in the tumor microenvironment and their interactions with
immune and tumor cells. The findings presented in these studies highlight the involvement of MDSCs in tumor progression,
immune regulation, and potential mechanisms underlying poor clinical outcomes. Table 3 encompasses studies using the Lewis
lung carcinoma model to investigate the role and mechanisms of MDSCs in lung cancer. These experimental studies provide
insights into MDSC recruitment, expansion, immunosuppressive activity, and interactions with other components of the tumor
microenvironment.
The selected articles are subsequently discussed in thematic blocks, organized according to the main biological
mechanisms and clinical implications of MDSCs in lung cancer. This approach allows the evidence from different lung cancer
subtypes and experimental models to be integrated and critically discussed.

4. Discussion
Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) have emerged as central determinants of the immune landscape in nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC), acting both systemically and locally to modulate antitumor immune responses. Recent studies
consistently demonstrate that the accumulation of immature myeloid populations—phenotypically heterogeneous yet
functionally defined by their capacity to suppress T-cell responses—is a recurrent feature of lung tumors associated with poor
prognosis and reduced sensitivity to immunomodulatory therapies (Henick et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2021; Bronte et al., 2022).
Translational evidence further indicates that the presence and functional activity of MDSCs do not merely reflect the host
immune status but actively contribute to tumor progression, metastatic niche formation, and resistance to multiple therapeutic
modalities, including chemotherapy, targeted therapies, and immunotherapy (Bronte et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022; Bahhar et al.,
2023).
Phenotypic heterogeneity and immunosuppressive mechanisms of MDSCs in non-small cell lung cancer
The literature consistently demonstrates an increased prevalence of MDSCs in patients with NSCLC, although the
dominant subset (M-MDSC versus PMN-MDSC) and the phenotypic markers employed vary across studies. Tian et al. (2021)
identified a LOX-1⁺ PMN-MDSC population with high ARG1 expression and potential diagnostic and prognostic utility,
whereas Kumata et al. (2023) described a CD33⁺/CD14⁺ LILRB4⁺ population consistent with M-MDSCs within the tumor
stroma, highlighting spatial and functional differences between intratumoral compartments and peripheral circulation. Henick
et al. (2022) further demonstrated variability according to histological subtype and molecular drivers (e.g., KRAS versus
EGFR), reinforcing the need for standardized phenotypic definitions to ensure reproducibility across cohorts. Complementing
these findings, Wen et al. (2022) showed that patients with NSCLC exhibit elevated circulating levels of total MDSCs and
specific subsets compared with benign pulmonary diseases. These elevated MDSC levels were positively correlated with
regulatory T cells (Tregs) and tumor stage, and negatively correlated with CD4⁺ T lymphocytes, underscoring the systemic
impact of these myeloid populations.
Functionally, MDSCs exert immunosuppressive effects through multiple redundant mechanisms, including amino
acid depletion via arginase-1, nitric oxide production (iNOS), generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), secretion of
immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, and expression of coinhibitory molecules including PD-L1 and cFLIP (Bronte et al., 2016). Koh et al. (2020) demonstrated that PMN-MDSCs induce T-cell exhaustion through increased
CD39 expression, whereas Bahhar et al. (2023) described a broad suppressive profile encompassing Arg1, iNOS, ROS, and
PD-L1 in clinical cohorts. Consistently, Lei et al. (2022) showed that tumors resistant to anti-PD-1 therapy maintain highly
suppressive HLA-DRlow myeloid populations enriched in hypoxia, angiogenesis, and immune tolerance pathways, reinforcing
the central role of MDSCs in sustaining immunosuppression and therapeutic resistance in NSCLC.
MDSCs as prognostic biomarkers and predictors of therapeutic response of non-small cell lung cancer patients
Elevated baseline levels of MDSCs are consistently associated with advanced disease, poorer therapeutic responses,
and reduced overall and progression-free survival in NSCLC (Zahran et al., 2021; Bronte et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022; Bahhar
et al., 2023). Koh et al. (2020) demonstrated that low pretreatment frequencies of MDSCs combined with reduced CD39

prognostic value.
From the perspective of intrinsic tumor biology, Piñeiro-Hermida et al. (2023) demonstrated that TERT (Telomerase
Reverse Transcriptase) overexpression in NSCLC tumors is associated with increased MDSC infiltration and reduced CD8⁺ Tcell presence, suggesting that telomerase activity directly contributes to the establishment of an immunosuppressive
microenvironment. Conversely, induction of telomeric dysfunction reverses this phenotype, indicating that telomerase acts as a
mechanistic link between tumor genomic stability and suppressive myeloid recruitment (Piñeiro-Hermida et al. (2023).
Additional evidence highlights that MDSC dynamics in NSCLC are highly sensitive to both therapeutic interventions
and clinical context. Han et al. (2021) showed that anti-PD-1 immunotherapy alone may paradoxically induce expansion of
circulating MDSCs, a phenomenon associated with secondary resistance. The combination with autologous cytokine-induced
killer (CIK) cells prevented this myeloid rebound, resulting in improved response rates and progression-free survival,
indicating that cellular strategies can counteract MDSC-mediated immunosuppression during checkpoint blockade (Han et al.
(2021).
In the perioperative setting, Deboever et al. (2022) reported that lung resection in NSCLC induces a transient increase
in circulating M-MDSCs accompanied by depletion of CD8⁺ T cells and effector memory populations. Although reversible
within weeks, this period of acute immunosuppression underscores the role of MDSCs as systemic modulators of immune
balance, independent of surgical technique, and suggests a biologically vulnerable window for tumor progression or immune
escape Deboever et al. (2022). Methodologically, Salvia et al. (2023) added a critical functional dimension by demonstrating
that simple quantification of PD-L1 expression on MDSCs may be insufficient. Using functional cytometry to assess
conformational changes in PD-L1, the authors showed that suppressive capacity depends on the functional state of the
molecule rather than its basal expression, reinforcing the importance of functional assays for accurate prognostic and predictive
interpretation. Salvia et al. (2023).
In the neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy context, Wen et al. (2022) observed that patients achieving pathological
complete response exhibited significantly lower baseline levels of MDSCs and Tregs, supporting their predictive value for
therapeutic selection. Consistently, Adamo et al. (2023) demonstrated that responders to immunotherapy show functional
attenuation of MDSCs, evidenced by reduced c-FLIP expression in monocytes, whereas persistence of this marker and
suppressive activity characterizes primary resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors ICIs Adamo et al. (2023).
Intrinsic resistance observed in KRAS/LKB1-mutant tumors was further explored by Lv et al. (2022), who confirmed
that standard chemoimmunotherapy fails to remodel the tumor microenvironment due to persistence of MDSCs, Tregs, and M2
macrophages. Restoration of TUSC2 via nanovesicles was decisive in reducing these suppressive populations, promoting
immune memory, and converting a refractory tumor microenvironment into one permissive to immunotherapy, reinforcing that
functional elimination of MDSCs is a critical step to overcome molecularly driven resistance Lv et al. (2022).
Molecular mechanisms of MDSC recruitment and expansion in non-small cell lung cancer
Multiple tumor-derived pathways converge to promote recruitment and retention of MDSCs within the pulmonary
microenvironment. Preclinical and translational evidence highlights chemokines such as CXCL1-CXCR2 (Liang et al., 2023;
Hu et al., 2025), CCL11/CCR3 (Li et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2022), and MCP-1/CCL2 (Cheng et al., 2025;
Tang et al., 2025) as recurrent mediators of myeloid influx. Tumor genetic alterations—including LKB1 loss, KRAS

Fu et al. (2024) further demonstrated that deletion of MED23 in KRAS^G12D models accelerates tumorigenesis by

favoring MDSC-mediated immune evasion independently of external therapeutic pressure Fu et al. (2024).
Skoulidis et al. (2024) showed that tumors harboring KEAP1 and STK11 mutations—traditionally considered
refractory to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade—exhibit resistance sustained by a myeloid-dominated immunosuppressive
microenvironment. CTLA-4 blockade significantly reduced suppressive myeloid populations and restored effector T-cell
infiltration, resulting in measurable clinical benefit. These data consolidate the concept that resistance in these subgroups is not
absolute but rather dependent on MDSC maintenance and can be overcome by strategies inducing deep myeloid remodeling
Skoulidis et al. (2024). Jeong et al. (2025) further demonstrated that intrinsic tumor PD-L1 function activates the JAK2/STAT3
pathway, increasing IL-6 secretion and promoting recruitment and activation of both M-MDSCs and PMN-MDSCs,
establishing an immune evasion axis independent of classical PD-1/PD-L1 interactions (Jeong et al., 2025).
MDSC-driven establishment of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in NSCLC
MDSC infiltration within the tumor microenvironment of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) has emerged as a key
determinant of disease progression and an independent predictor of poor prognosis. Gao et al. (2024), analyzing five
independent cohorts, demonstrated that elevated MDSC infiltration correlates inversely with patient survival and promotes an
immunosuppressive landscape characterized by impaired antigen presentation, reduced T-cell cytotoxicity, and activation of
proliferative, glycolytic, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathways Gao et al. (2024).
Several studies have identified tumor-intrinsic genetic drivers that actively promote MDSC recruitment. Li et al.
(2022), Hu et al. (2023), and Huang et al. (2024) identified H2AFZ, the oncogene PRR11, and PSMD11, respectively, as
biomarkers whose overexpression facilitates MDSC infiltration and is associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Similarly,
Limbu and McCloskey (2023) and Ashok et al. (2024) demonstrated that stemness-associated genes (ORC1L, KIF20A,
DLGAP5) and the highly O-glycosylated, mutated MUC5B gene contribute to the maintenance of an aggressive
immunosuppressive phenotype (Limbu and McCloskey, 2023; Ashok et al., 2024).
Chronic inflammation and dysbiosis further reinforce this immunosuppressive environment. Liu et al. (2023) showed
that the fungus Aspergillus sydowii activates the Dectin-1/CARD9 pathway, inducing IL-1β secretion and promoting
recruitment and expansion of PMN-MDSCs and Tregs. Consistently, Yuan et al. (2022) demonstrated that IL-1β blockade
reduces tumor burden and remodels the immune microenvironment by decreasing PMN-MDSC populations and restoring
CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, highlighting the central role of inflammatory signaling in MDSC-driven tumor establishment.
Molecular and metabolic mechanisms governing MDSC expansion and function in NSCLC
The expansion and functional programming of MDSCs are driven by interconnected molecular and metabolic
pathways that reshape myelopoiesis and immune regulation. Gao et al. (2024) demonstrated that downregulation of IRF8
activates the IL-6–JAK–STAT3 axis, skewing myeloid differentiation toward MDSC generation and identifying IRF8 as a
protective prognostic factor. In parallel, Jiang et al. (2022) showed that SEMA4B overexpression promotes MDSC
recruitment, likely via NF-κB signaling, while its silencing significantly reduces myeloid infiltration.

In addition to intracellular pathways, intercellular communication through extracellular vesicles plays a critical role in
MDSC regulation. Liang et al. (2025) demonstrated that tumor-derived exosomes carrying PVRL4 drive MDSC expansion and
TGF-β1–mediated immunosuppression, while PVRL4 silencing reduces suppressor cell induction from peripheral blood
mononuclear cells. Tumino et al. (2022) further showed that circulating PMN-MDSCs release exosome-associated miRNAs
that impair NK-cell function, extending immunosuppressive effects beyond the tumor microenvironment and supporting their
potential as non-invasive biomarkers.
MDSCs in tumor progression, invasion, and metastasis in NSCLC
Beyond their immunosuppressive activity, MDSCs directly contribute to tumor progression by promoting invasive
and metastatic phenotypes. Translational studies demonstrate that MDSCs secrete pro-invasive chemokines, particularly
CCL11, which activate ERK/AKT signaling via CCR3 and induce epithelial–mesenchymal transition in NSCLC cells, thereby
facilitating tumor dissemination (Li et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2022). Lin et al. (2021) further showed that the
MDSC–CCL11–CCR3 axis operates independently of classical immunosuppression, highlighting a direct protumoral function.
Consistently, Zhang et al. (2024) demonstrated that silencing of APE1 reduces MDSC accumulation and function, leading to
decreased metastatic spread, further supporting the role of MDSCs as active drivers of tumor dissemination.
MDSCs as drivers of therapeutic resistance in NSCLC
Accumulating evidence indicates that MDSCs are central mediators of resistance to both immunotherapy and targeted
treatments. Elevated pretreatment MDSC levels predict primary resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (Bronte et al.,
2022; Liu et al., 2022), while increased myeloid recruitment has been associated with resistance to EGFR tyrosine kinase
inhibitors and other targeted therapies (Wang et al., 2023; Shao et al., 2024).
In models resistant to PD-1/CTLA-4 blockade, Rodriguez (2023) demonstrated accumulation of Ly6C⁺ monocytes
that preferentially differentiate into MDSCs or M2 macrophages; blockade of this pathway restored immunotherapy efficacy.
Similarly, Meraz et al. (2022) showed that KRAS/LKB1-mutant tumors exhibit a highly immunosuppressive
microenvironment driven by MDSC expansion, which can be reversed by TUSC2 restoration combined with chemotherapy
and anti-PD-1 therapy. These findings collectively establish MDSCs as key orchestrators of therapy resistance, linking tumorintrinsic alterations to immune escape mechanisms.
Therapeutic targeting and reprogramming of MDSCs in NSCLC
Given their central role in tumor progression and therapeutic resistance, MDSCs represent a promising target for
combinatorial treatment strategies. Experimental approaches include depletion, induction of differentiation, inhibition of
recruitment, metabolic reprogramming, and epigenetic modulation. Liang and Shen (2020) and Yang et al. (2020) reported
effective suppression of MDSCs using LXR agonists and thymosin α-1, respectively. Huang (2022) demonstrated that
glutaminolysis inhibition with JHU083 reduces MDSC suppressive activity, while Cheng et al. (2024) showed that liposomal
honokiol decreases MDSC and M2 macrophage infiltration, enhancing responses to anti-PD-1 therapy.

MDSCs in Lewis lung carcinoma
Integrated analysis of the included studies demonstrates that MDSCs play a central role in Lewis lung carcinoma
(LLC), profoundly modulating the tumor microenvironment, immune response, and therapeutic efficacy. Consistently,
expansion of these cells is directly associated with accelerated tumor growth, systemic immunosuppression, and poorer
prognosis, reinforcing their accumulation as a hallmark of this experimental lung cancer model (Huang et al., 2024; Qin et al.,
2025).
Unmodified LLC tumors exhibit marked expansion of MDSCs in blood, spleen, and tumor tissue, associated with
elevated G-CSF levels and splenomegaly, contributing to immune evasion and rapid tumor growth (Huang et al., 2021).
Similarly, deletion of microRNA miR-150 exacerbates this phenomenon, promoting PMN-MDSC expansion, increased Arg1,
ROS, and iNOS expression, and systemic elevation of IL-6 and G-CSF, thereby intensifying immunosuppressive activity and
accelerating tumor growth (Qin et al., 2025). These findings support the notion that multiple signaling pathways—including
STAT3, IRE1α, and NF-κB—sustain MDSC expansion and activation during tumor establishment and progression.

Chemotherapy and pharmacological interventions further influence MDSC dynamics. Nie et al. (2024) observed that

thereby enhancing local immune responsiveness. Interventions such as STING agonists further reduce MDSC populations and
facilitate CAR-T cell infiltration, reinforcing the role of MDSCs as a major barrier to immunotherapy (Zhu et al., 2025).
Complementarily, Zhou et al. (2022) demonstrated that the neddylation pathway regulates MDSC infiltration via the
CXCL5/CXCR2 axis, highlighting disruption of the neddylation–NF-κB–CXCL5 axis as a promising strategy to block
myeloid recruitment.
Collectively, these studies reinforce that MDSCs constitute a fundamental regulatory axis in Lewis lung carcinoma,
suppressing antitumor immunity through ROS, NO, Arg1, and IL-10 production, as well as activation of STAT3, IRE1α, and
NF-κB pathways. Their expansion—favored by cytokines such as G-CSF and IL-6—correlates with increased tumor
aggressiveness, poorer prognosis, and reduced therapeutic efficacy. Conversely, strategies that limit MDSC generation,
recruitment, or function significantly restore antitumor immunity and constrain tumor growth. Accordingly, findings from this
integrative review underscore that MDSCs should be considered priority therapeutic targets in Lewis lung carcinoma, not only
due to their direct role in immune evasion but also because of their capacity to remodel the tumor microenvironment and
modulate other immune populations essential for effective antitumor responses.

5. Final Considerations
This integrative review consolidates the understanding that myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) play a central
role in lung cancer pathophysiology. Analysis of the 61 selected studies demonstrates that, in both non-small cell lung cancer
and experimental models such as Lewis lung carcinoma, MDSCs not only orchestrate a profoundly immunosuppressive tumor
microenvironment but also actively drive tumor progression, invasion, and resistance to conventional and immunotherapeutic
strategies.
The functional plasticity of MDSCs and their dynamic interactions with other immune populations, including
regulatory T cells and macrophages, establish complex immunosuppressive networks that sustain tumor development and limit
therapeutic efficacy. Importantly, the identification of MDSC-associated biomarkers and the success of combinatorial
approaches in preclinical settings highlight the translational potential of targeting MDSC recruitment, expansion, and function
as a strategy to improve clinical outcomes in lung cancer.
Several limitations of this review should be acknowledged. The exclusion of studies due to lack of full-text
availability or restricted institutional access may have limited the inclusion of relevant data. In addition, restriction to selected
languages and to publications from the past five years, while ensuring topical relevance, may have resulted in the omission of
earlier or seminal studies. Despite these limitations, the findings highlight the urgent need to translate molecular insights into
MDSC biology into clinical practice, aiming to develop more effective and personalized therapies for patients with lung
malignancies.
