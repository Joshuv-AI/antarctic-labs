Dietary iodine and selenium exposure and thyroid function in adults: a total diet study with biomarker assessment
Authors: Dlkhwaz A Hama, Karzan A Mohammed Hawrami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND/OBJECTIVESExcessive intake of iodine and selenium is increasingly recognized as a potential public health concern in populations undergoing dietary transition, yet integrated evidence linking food-based exposure, biomarkers and thyroid function remains limited. This study evaluated dietary iodine and selenium exposure and examined their association with thyroid function in healthy adults in Halabja City, Kurdistan Region, Iraq, using a combined dietary and biomarker approach.SUBJECTS/METHODSA cross-sectional study was conducted among 201 healthy adults (18–60 yrs) from Halabja City, Kurdistan Region, Iraq. Dietary intake was assessed using a semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire combined with a total diet study approach, in which composite food samples were analysed by inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry. Urinary iodine (iodine-to-creatinine ratio), serum selenium and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) were measured. Multivariable regression models were used to examine associations between iodine intake and TSH after adjustment for demographic and nutritional covariates.RESULTSMean iodine and selenium intakes were 206.99 µg/day and 291.14 µg/day, respectively. Cereal and cereal-based foods were the major contributors to both iodine and selenium intake, accounting for 65.97% and 68.82% of total intake, respectively. Biomarker findings indicated adequate-to-high iodine and selenium status at the population level. Most participants remained within the euthyroid range, and dietary iodine intake was not independently associated with TSH after multivariable adjustments.CONCLUSIONThis study suggests novel evidence that staple-based dietary patterns can drive excessive iodine and selenium exposure in adults. Although no independent association between dietary iodine intake and TSH was observed after multivariable adjustment, most participants remained within the euthyroid range, suggesting possible physiological adaptation; however, subclinical effects cannot be excluded. These findings highlight the importance of monitoring iodized salt practices and dietary exposure pathways in populations with reliance on staple foods, and demonstrate the value of integrating total diet study methodology with biomarker assessment into nutritional surveillance.Keywords: Iodine, selenium, biomarkers, thyroid gland, adult

INTRODUCTION

Iodine is an essential trace element required for thyroid hormone synthesis and, consequently, for growth, neurodevelopment and metabolic regulation [1,2]. Both iodine deficiency and iodine excess can disturb thyroid homeostasis, although the clinical expression of excess exposure depends on dose, duration and host susceptibility [3,4]. Estimating iodine intake from foods is challenging because iodine concentrations vary substantially across food systems according to soil conditions, agricultural practices, processing and the use of iodised salt during manufacture and cooking [5,6]. For this reason, dietary data alone may misclassify exposure, particularly in settings where food-composition tables are incomplete and discretionary salt use is not quantified reliably [6]. Total diet studies (TDS) address part of this problem by analyzing foods as prepared for consumption and can therefore provide population-relevant estimates of exposure from the habitual diet [7].

Thus, reliable population assessment of iodine nutrition must be based on both dietary and biochemical information. The World Health Organization (WHO) has recommended urinary iodine concentration (UIC) as the indicator of choice for assessing population iodine status, with spot urine samples normally being collected, because more than 90% of absorbed iodine is excreted in urine [8]. However, spot UIC is influenced by hydration and recent intake, resulting in substantial within-person variation that restricts its utility for diagnosis at the individual level [9]. In order to lessen the impact of urine dilution on concentration values, the urinary iodine-to-creatinine ratio is often used as a complementary parameter; however, creatinine excretion depends on sex, age, muscle mass, and body composition, which should be taken into account in its interpretation [6].

Food frequency questionnaires (FFQs) are widely used to estimate habitual intake in nutritional epidemiology, but their performance depends on the cultural specificity of the food list, the reference period and the quality of the nutrient database [10]. Recent work published in the Journal of Human Nutrition and Dietetics has reinforced the value of carefully designed nutrient-specific FFQs for iodine assessment, while also emphasizing the need to interpret questionnaire-derived intake alongside biomarkers [11]. Accordingly, combining FFQ-based intake estimates with laboratory analysis of composite foods and biomarker measurements provides a stronger framework for evaluating iodine exposure than any single method alone.

Selenium should be considered alongside iodine because several selenoproteins are involved in thyroid hormone metabolism and antioxidant defense within the thyroid gland [12,13]. Inadequate selenium may impair deiodinase activity and increase susceptibility to oxidative damage, whereas very high selenium exposure may also have biological consequences beyond the thyroid [12,14]. Thus, interpretation of thyroid-related outcomes in populations with high iodine exposure is strengthened when selenium status is measured concurrently.

In the Kurdistan Region of Iraq, excessive and inconsistent use of iodized salt may contribute substantially to iodine exposure, and recent evidence from northern Iraq has already suggested that both iodine and selenium intakes may be high in some communities [15]. Nevertheless, integrated data on dietary exposure, urinary biomarkers, serum selenium and thyroid function from the same adult population are scarce. Halabja is a relevant setting because its diet is cereal-dominant and local cooking practices may amplify exposure through iodized salt added during food preparation. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate dietary iodine and selenium exposure, biomarker status and thyroid function among adults in Halabja, and to examine whether selenium status modified the relationship between iodine exposure and serum TSH. However, no previous studies in this region have simultaneously integrated dietary exposure, biomarker assessment, and thyroid function within a single analytical framework.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Study design

The study was conducted in Halabja Province, Kurdistan Region of Iraq, between August 2024 and February 2025. A modified food basket approach within a total diet study framework was used to estimate population-level dietary exposure to iodine and selenium among community-dwelling adults. This approach was selected because it captures foods as actually prepared and consumed, thereby improving the relevance of exposure estimates for comparison with urinary and serum biomarkers [6,7]. Adults aged 18–60 yrs were eligible for inclusion. Sex refers to biological differences; gender was not assessed in this study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before the start of data collection, and ethical approval was granted by the Sulaimani Polytechnic University Ethical Committee (approval No. 75/245; 24 March 2025). Exclusion criteria were pregnancy or lactation, chronic inflammatory disease, hepatic disease, treated anemia, current infection, or use of iodine- or selenium-containing supplements during the previous 3 mon. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

Data collection and dietary assessment

Participants completed a structured interview and dietary assessment, each lasting approximately 45 min. Habitual diet was assessed using a semi-quantitative FFQ adapted to local dietary practices in Halabja and informed by established FFQ development and validation methodologies [11,16]. Consumption frequencies were converted to daily equivalents and combined with analytically measured iodine and selenium concentrations in prepared composite foods to estimate mean daily intake. Because discretionary salt use and salt iodine concentration were not measured directly, the dietary estimates should be interpreted as food-based exposure estimates that may partly incorporate salt added during preparation.

Food selection, preparation, trace element analysis

The first stage of the TDS involved constructing a food sampling frame using intake data from the enrolled adults. Foods were selected to reflect habitual consumption across major food groups, including cereals, legumes, vegetables, fruits, dairy products, meats, eggs and nuts. Foods were selected based on FFQ data and represented the most commonly consumed items within each food group in the study population. A composite sampling strategy was then applied, whereby each food item was purchased from 5 geographically distinct retail outlets, with multiple brands or varieties included when available [7,17]. Standardized recipes based on local household practice were used for meal and mixed-dish preparation. Importantly, foods were prepared as they are commonly consumed, using locally sourced tap water and typical amounts of seasonings, including salt and spices, so that analytically measured iodine and selenium concentrations would reflect realistic dietary exposure.

Because iodine is volatile and can be lost during acidic digestion, iodine was extracted using an alkaline preparation to improve analyte stability and recovery [18]. Selenium was determined after closed-vessel microwave-assisted acid digestion. Calibration standards, internal standards, procedural blanks and duplicate analyses were used throughout to support analytical quality assurance, and limits of detection and quantification were established in accordance with laboratory procedures.

Each homogenized sample (0.2 g) was weighed into microwave digestion vessels. Separate aliquots were prepared for iodine and selenium analyses to optimize analyte recovery. Total selenium was measured after microwave-assisted digestion in 62% HNO3, whereas iodine extraction was performed using 5% TMAH to minimize volatilization losses. All samples were analyzed by inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry (ICP-MS; iCAP RQplus; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and concentrations were expressed as µg/kg fresh weight.

Biomarker assessment

Urine and fasting blood samples were collected from the same participants who completed the dietary assessment. Spot urine samples were collected in iodine-free containers and stored at −40°C until analysis; morning collection was preferred to reduce diurnal variation. UIC was measured by ICP-MS, urinary creatinine was measured on a Roche COBAS c311 analyzer, and the urinary iodine-to-creatinine ratio (µg/g creatinine) was used as the primary urinary iodine metric to reduce the influence of urine dilution. We recognize, however, that creatinine adjustment does not eliminate all within-person variability and that WHO population cut-offs are formally defined for UIC (µg/L), not the urinary iodine-to-creatinine ratio [6,15]. Serum TSH was measured using a Roche COBAS e411 analyzer, with the laboratory reference interval of 0.4–4.0 mIU/L used to classify results. Serum selenium was determined by ICP-MS after acid digestion in trace-element–free tubes; hemolyzed samples were excluded [19].

Quality control and assurance

NIST 1573a Tomato Leaves and NIST 1567b Wheat Flour were analyzed in triplicate using the same digestion and extraction protocols as the study samples. Mean recoveries were 97% of the certified values. Procedural blanks were included in every analytical batch, and urine assays were verified using Seronorm™ level 1 and level 2 certified reference materials. Internal standards and calibration verification checks were used to monitor instrumental accuracy and drift.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range), depending their on distribution. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to examine associations between dietary intake estimates and urinary biomarkers. Group comparisons were performed using parametric or non-parametric tests as appropriate. Multivariable linear regression was used to examine the association between iodine intake and serum TSH after adjustment for age, sex, body mass index and serum selenium. A 2-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical assumptions were assessed prior to analysis, and variables were transformed where necessary to meet model assumptions.

Prevalence of excess intake and nutritional status

Table 6 shows that approximately one-third to two-fifths of participants achieved iodine intakes within the ≥ recommended dietary allowance–tolerable upper intake level (UL) range, whereas no participants exceeded the UL, and approximately 10–19% had selenium intakes above the upper level. No participant was classified as having low selenium intake on the basis of dietary reference values. A considerable proportion of participants had iodine intakes below the estimated average requirement (EAR), particularly among females. Table 7 indicates that most participants were classified as having excessive or severely excessive iodine status and excessive selenium status. Low status categories were uncommon for both micronutrients. These data support the interpretation that excess exposure, rather than deficiency, is the principal nutritional concern in this study population.

Thyroid function and association with iodine intake

Table 8 shows that most participants had TSH concentrations within the reference range across all urinary iodine categories. Overall, 170 of 201 participants (84.6%) had normal TSH concentrations, while 28 participants (13.9%) had elevated TSH levels and 3 participants (1.5%) had suppressed TSH levels. Participants with urinary iodine-to-creatinine ratios > 300 µg/g creatinine accounted for the largest number of elevated TSH concentrations (19 participants, 9.5% of the total study population), although normal TSH remained the predominant finding in this group. The Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test showed no statistically significant association between urinary iodine-to-creatinine ratio categories and TSH status (P = 0.644). These findings indicate that, despite the relatively high prevalence of excessive iodine intake, thyroid function as assessed by TSH concentrations remained within the reference range for the majority of participants.

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationship between iodine exposure indicators and serum TSH concentrations. A weak but statistically significant positive correlation was observed between urinary iodine-to-creatinine ratio and serum TSH concentrations (ρ = 0.153, P = 0.031). In contrast, a weak but statistically significant negative correlation was observed between dietary iodine intake and serum TSH concentrations (ρ = −0.148, P = 0.037). These findings suggest that urinary iodine status, as assessed by the urinary iodine-to-creatinine ratio, was weakly associated with TSH levels, whereas estimated dietary iodine intake showed no significant relationship with TSH in the study population.

DISCUSSION

This study was designed as a public health nutrition investigation rather than as a purely endocrine analysis. To our knowledge, this study is among the first in the Kurdistan Region to integrate total diet study methodology with biomarker and thyroid function assessment in a single population. By combining a total diet study, a FFQ tailored to local eating habits, and biomarker measurements from the same adults, it provides a more comprehensive assessment of iodine and selenium exposure than studies based on dietary data or urinary biomarkers alone. That integrated approach aligns well with the type of dietary assessment and nutritional status work prioritized in the Journal of Human Nutrition and Dietetics [11]. The central finding is that adults in Halabja appear to be exposed to high amounts of both iodine and selenium, with cereals and cereal-based foods acting as the dominant contributors to intake.

The dietary pattern observed here is important for interpretation. Cereal products were consumed frequently and in large quantities, with rice and bread making the greatest contribution by weight. In principle, cereals are not usually considered intrinsically rich iodine sources [20]; therefore, the very large iodine contribution attributed to this food group is unlikely to reflect the native iodine content of grains alone. A more plausible explanation is that cereal-based foods, especially bread and cooked rice dishes, act as vehicles for iodized salt added during household preparation or commercial baking [21]. This is a critical point scientifically because it shifts the interpretation from “high-iodine cereals” to “high iodine exposure through staple foods as prepared and consumed.” That interpretation is consistent with the TDS design used in this study and with previous evidence from the Kurdistan Region showing that iodized salt can markedly increase total iodine exposure [15].

This distinction also helps explain why dairy products contributed little to iodine intake in our dataset. In many European settings, dairy is a major iodine source because of iodinated feed and sanitizing practices [22]. In Halabja, however, dairy intake was modest and analytically measured iodine concentrations in dairy composites were low, so dairy contributed minimally to total exposure. From a journal-fit perspective, this is a useful public health nutrition message: the dietary drivers of iodine status are context-specific and cannot be assumed from high-income country food patterns.

The selenium findings require similarly careful interpretation. Mean selenium intake was high, and cereals accounted for more than two-thirds of total selenium intake. Unlike iodine, high selenium in cereals may plausibly reflect the underlying food system, including soil selenium content, irrigation sources and local grain sourcing, although the present study was not designed to identify these upstream determinants. The coexistence of high iodine and high selenium exposure is noteworthy because selenium participates in deiodinase activity and antioxidant defense, yet excess selenium is not necessarily benign [12,23]. Therefore, selenium status in this population may be physiologically relevant without being straightforwardly protective.

A notable strength of the study is the concordance between the dietary analysis and the biomarker pattern at the population level: the dietary analysis indicates that selenium deficiency was uncommon, whereas a substantial proportion of participants had iodine intakes below the EAR. Nevertheless, urinary iodine biomarkers suggested generally adequate to high iodine status at the population level [24]. However, the thyroid findings are more nuanced. Despite high iodine exposure, most participants had TSH concentrations within the laboratory reference range, and dietary iodine intake showed a weak inverse association with serum TSH concentrations, although the strength of the correlation was small. This does not imply that excess iodine is harmless. Rather, it suggests that in a generally healthy adult population, TSH alone may be insufficiently sensitive to detect early or subclinical effects of chronic excess exposure. The thyroid is capable of autoregulatory adaptation, including the acute Wolff–Chaikoff effect and escape from it, which may preserve euthyroidism in many individuals despite high iodine intake [25].

At the same time, the absence of a detectable association should not be over-interpreted. First, the study was cross-sectional and therefore cannot address duration of exposure or temporal changes in thyroid adaptation. Second, only TSH was measured; free T4, free T3, thyroglobulin and thyroid autoantibodies were not available. Although a statistically significant inverse association was observed between dietary iodine intake and serum TSH concentrations, the correlation was weak (ρ = −0.148). Therefore, the clinical relevance of this association should be interpreted cautiously. Although TSH is a sensitive marker of thyroid function, it may not fully reflect subtle thyroid alterations associated with chronic iodine excess. Thyroid autoregulatory mechanisms, including adaptation to excess iodine, may maintain normal TSH concentrations despite underlying physiological changes. Therefore, the absence of additional thyroid biomarkers (free T4, free T3, thyroglobulin, and thyroid autoantibodies) limits the ability to detect subclinical thyroid dysfunction, and the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence that excessive iodine exposure has no biological consequences. Third, the urinary biomarker was based on a spot sample, which is appropriate for population monitoring but subject to substantial within-person variability even after creatinine adjustment [9,26]. For these reasons, the present results are better interpreted as evidence of population-level excess exposure without clear overt disturbance in TSH, rather than as evidence that high iodine intake has no thyroid consequences.

Another important limitation is the interpretation of urinary iodine categories. WHO cut-offs were developed for median UIC (µg/L) at the population level, whereas this study relied primarily on urinary iodine-to-creatinine ratio to account for urine dilution. Although that choice is reasonable analytically, it means that the prevalence estimates for “excessive” and “severely excessive” iodine status should be interpreted cautiously [24,27]. Similarly, because salt iodine concentration and discretionary salt use were not measured directly, the study cannot quantify the relative contribution of household salt, breadmaking salt and naturally occurring food iodine [28]. These are important targets for follow-up work. An additional finding was the higher prevalence of elevated TSH concentrations among women than men in the highest urinary iodine-to-creatinine ratio category (> 300 µg/g creatinine). Although the study was not specifically designed to investigate sex-specific susceptibility, this observation is consistent with previous evidence indicating that women are generally more prone to thyroid dysfunction and autoimmune thyroid disorders than men [29]. It is therefore possible that women may be more vulnerable to the thyroidal effects of excessive iodine exposure. However, thyroid autoantibodies were not measured in the present study, and the observed sex difference should be interpreted cautiously. Further studies incorporating thyroid autoantibody measurements and longitudinal follow-up are needed to clarify whether the higher prevalence of elevated TSH among women reflects increased susceptibility to iodine-related thyroid dysfunction.

In addition, a formal interaction analysis was conducted to evaluate whether selenium status modified the association between dietary iodine intake and serum TSH concentrations. No significant interaction was observed (P = 0.871), suggesting that selenium status did not significantly influence the relationship between iodine exposure and thyroid function in this study population. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as the relatively limited sample size and the narrow range of selenium status may have reduced the ability to detect modest interaction effects. Further studies involving larger populations and broader variation in selenium exposure are warranted to clarify the potential interactive role of selenium in thyroid regulation.

Sex differences also merit consideration. Men had higher estimated iodine and selenium intakes and higher serum selenium concentrations than women, but the urinary iodine-to-creatinine ratio did not differ significantly by sex. This pattern probably reflects a combination of higher absolute food intake in men and biological differences in creatinine excretion and selenium distribution [6,30]. It also illustrates why dietary intake and biomarker data should be interpreted together rather than interchangeably.

When placed in an international context, the Halabja estimates are strikingly high, particularly for iodine [15,20,31,32,33]. The comparison strengthens the originality of the paper, but the more important scientific message is not simply that intake is “higher than elsewhere.” It is that a cereal-dominant dietary pattern in a salt-iodized setting can generate excess iodine exposure even when classic iodine-rich foods such as dairy and seafood are not major contributors [21,22]. That is a practically relevant message for dietitians and public health nutritionists because interventions may need to focus on salt iodization practices within staple foods, not only on individual food choices [21,24].

Overall, the discussion points towards 3 priorities for future research. First, the iodine concentration of household salt and bakery salt in Halabja should be measured directly. Second, repeat urine collections or 24-h urinary iodine assessment would improve exposure classification. Third, future studies should incorporate a broader thyroid panel and, ideally, prospective follow-up to determine whether sustained co-exposure to iodine and selenium has longer-term clinical significance. These steps would move the evidence base from descriptive surveillance towards causal understanding.

This total diet study provides new evidence that adults in Halabja are exposed to high levels of iodine and selenium, largely through cereal-based foods as prepared and consumed. The dietary and biomarker data together suggest that excess exposure, rather than deficiency, is the dominant micronutrient issue in this setting. Although TSH remained within the reference range for most participants, only a weak inverse association was observed between dietary iodine intake and serum TSH concentrations, and that finding should be interpreted cautiously because TSH alone may not capture early thyroid effects of chronic excess exposure. The study therefore supports continued monitoring of iodized salt practices, repeated biomarker surveillance and more comprehensive thyroid assessment in populations with staple-based diets and widespread salt iodization. These findings support the need for monitoring iodized salt practices and comprehensive thyroid assessment in high-exposure populations.
