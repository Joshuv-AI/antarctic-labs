Tuberculous brain abscess mimicking a neoplastic lesion in a patient with well-controlled human immunodeficiency virus infection: illustrative case
Authors: Daniel J Valdivia, Henry Noren, Brennan Cook, Yaxel Levin-Carrion, Elizabeth E Ginalis, Peter-Okaka Uchenna, Misha Movahed-Ezazi, Sarag Boukhar, Ada Baisre, Srihari Sundararajan, Hai Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDTuberculous brain abscess is a rare manifestation of CNS tuberculosis, typically occurring in patients with significant immunosuppression. Intracranial tuberculosis is an uncommon but potentially devastating manifestation of Mycobacterium tuberculosis, accounting for approximately 5%–10% of extrapulmonary disease, and is associated with high rates of morbidity and mortality.1,2 CNS involvement encompasses a spectrum of pathologies, of which tuberculous meningitis is the most common type, representing 70%–80% of cases.1 Parenchymal disease accounts for the remaining cases and most commonly presents as tuberculomas, which represent about 20%–30% of CNS tuberculosis. Tuberculomas are focal, well-circumscribed granulomatous lesions that may be noncaseating or caseating and typically reflect an organized host immune response.1,3

Tuberculous brain abscesses are one of the rarest and least frequently encountered forms of CNS tuberculosis.4 Unlike tuberculomas, tuberculous abscesses are pus-filled cavities lacking organized granulomatous inflammation, and they are thought to arise from uncontrolled bacillary proliferation rather than effective immune containment.5 These lesions are classically described in patients with profound immunosuppression, particularly those with advanced human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection or impaired cell-mediated immunity.6,7 Their occurrence in patients without overt immunodeficiency is atypical and may delay consideration of tuberculosis in the differential diagnosis of intracerebral lesions.8 Furthermore, tuberculous abscesses may closely resemble neoplastic lesions, particularly when presenting as solitary, well-circumscribed, peripherally enhancing masses, thereby influencing diagnostic pathways and management decisions.9

Here, we report a case of a pathologically confirmed intracerebral tuberculous abscess in a patient with well-controlled chronic HIV infection, preserved CD4 counts, and a remote history of treated pulmonary tuberculosis. This case demonstrates the potential for tuberculous abscess formation in the absence of apparent immunodeficiency and emphasizes the importance of considering tuberculosis in the evaluation of solitary intracerebral lesions, as diagnostic suspicion may influence medical and surgical management decisions.

Illustrative Case

The patient is a 43-year-old man born in Honduras with a history of well-controlled HIV infection (diagnosed 14 years prior to presentation; CD4 count of 767 cells/µL with undetectable viral load) and previously treated pulmonary M. tuberculosis, who presented with a 6-month history of persistent and progressively worsening left-sided headaches. The pain was localized behind the left ear and radiated toward the left temporal region and jaw. Previous evaluations by infectious disease and otolaryngology specialists found no evidence of active infection, sinus disease, or otological pathology. The pain was refractory to multiple medical therapies, including gabapentin, duloxetine, naproxen, and tramadol. The patient had not experienced any nausea, vomiting, dizziness, visual disturbances, focal weakness, sensory deficits, or seizures. He also denied any history of trauma, fever, or weight loss.

Given the refractory nature of his symptoms, an outpatient brain MRI study was performed. Three days after MRI, while results were still pending, the patient presented to the emergency department with an acute headache exacerbation accompanied by intermittent blurry vision. CT angiography of the head and neck with and without contrast revealed a lesion of the left posterior temporal lobe parenchyma containing approximately three coarse calcifications and no associated vascular abnormality (Fig. 1). During this ED visit, the patient received analgesics and was instructed to follow up outpatient.

Axial CT angiograms of the left posterior superior temporal lesion with (A) and without (B) contrast, demonstrating a lobulated cluster of coarse intraparenchymal calcifications within the left posterior temporal lobe, without associated vasogenic edema or abnormal enhancement (arrows). The largest calcification measured 12 × 7 mm.

One week later, final MRI results confirmed a 1.3 × 1.5 × 1.4–cm lobulated parenchymal mass in the left posterior superior temporal lobe (Fig. 2). On T1-weighted imaging, the lesion appeared hypointense (Fig. 2B) with postcontrast peripheral enhancement (Fig. 2A). T2-weighted imaging demonstrated marked hypointensity (Fig. 2C), while FLAIR imaging showed no evidence of surrounding vasogenic edema (Fig. 2D). T2-weighted gradient echo imaging exhibited magnetic susceptibility (Fig. 2E). Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and corresponding apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) sequences demonstrated no definite restricted diffusion, with the lesion appearing hypointense on both sequences (Fig. 2F and G). Given the lesion’s radiographic appearance and the absence of systemic infectious findings, the differential diagnosis included neoplastic and, less likely, infectious or granulomatous etiologies; however, a neoplastic process was favored, and the patient was referred to our neurosurgery clinic for further evaluation.

Owing to concerns for potential involvement of eloquent cortex, functional MRI and diffusion tensor imaging were obtained, which demonstrated the lesion’s proximity to dominant-hemisphere language areas. Neuropsychological Montreal Cognitive Assessment of the patient revealed performance on the cusp of mild neurocognitive disorder. On the Quick Aphasia Battery–Spanish, the patient demonstrated mild aphasia-level performance in language comprehension. Assessment also revealed auditory working memory below expected levels, processing speed within normal limits, and reduced mental flexibility. As part of routine preoperative evaluation, a chest radiograph obtained 2 weeks before surgery showed no acute cardiopulmonary abnormality or radiographic evidence of active pulmonary tuberculosis.

Operative Findings

The patient underwent an awake craniotomy with intraoperative language mapping to maximize the safety of resection in the eloquent language area. Intraoperatively, the lesion appeared firm, well circumscribed, and clearly demarcated from the surrounding brain parenchyma. Using real-time mapping, we achived a gross-total resection without intraoperative complication except for mild word-finding difficulty. Naming and comprehension remained intact.

Pathological Analysis: Frozen and Permanent and Molecular Findings

Pathological examination demonstrated a multilobulated chronic brain abscess composed of a thick fibrous wall (Fig. 3A and B) encapsulating a layer of granulomatous inflammation (Fig. 3C) and a central cavity filled with necrotic debris and abundant calcifications. The fibrous wall was surrounded by a rim of gliotic brain parenchyma (Fig. 3D). Rare organisms compatible with mycobacteria were identified using Kinyoun acid-fast staining (Fig. 3E) and a modified Truant fluorescent acid-fast stain (Fig. 3F). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)–based molecular analysis of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue detected M. tuberculosis complex DNA using IS6110 and IS1081 primer sets, confirming the diagnosis of a tuberculous brain abscess.

Postoperative Course

Postoperative MRI demonstrated expected postsurgical changes following resection of the lesion (Fig. 4). The patient had persistent word-finding difficulty without additional neurological deficits. His hospital course was otherwise uncomplicated, aside from brief episodes of asymptomatic bradycardia and transient gait unsteadiness, both of which resolved with observation and supportive care. He was discharged home in stable condition and subsequently started on antituberculosis therapy (rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol). At the 1-month follow-up, he had no new neurological deficits and demonstrated continued improvement in headache severity and speech.

Discussion

Observations

Intracranial tuberculosis typically occurs in markedly immunocompromised patients, and tuberculous brain abscess represents one of the rarest parenchymal manifestations of CNS disease.10–14 Tuberculous abscesses are pathologically distinct from tuberculomas, consisting of a pus-filled cavity with abundant bacilli and lacking the organized granulomatous architecture characteristic of tuberculomas. They are most often described in patients with severe immunosuppression, particularly advanced HIV infection, chronic immunosuppressive therapy, or impaired cell-mediated immunity.15,16

In contrast, this patient had well-controlled HIV infection with preserved CD4 counts and no clinical evidence of systemic immunodeficiency, highlighting that tuberculous brain abscess can occur despite an apparently adequate immune status. Similar cases have been reported in immunocompetent individuals, often with a prior history of tuberculosis, suggesting that remote infection may represent a persistent risk factor for later intracranial disease.17,18

Radiographically, the lesion in this case appeared as a well-­circumscribed peripherally enhancing mass without surrounding edema, thereby closely mimicking a neoplastic process. Additional atypical features included coarse calcifications and marked T2 hypointensity. The lack of definite restricted diffusion on DWI/ADC, rather than the classic diffusion restriction often associated with pyogenic abscess, lowered suspicion for an infectious etiology. In the appropriate clinical context, these findings may raise consideration of granulomatous infection, including tuberculous disease, although the imaging findings remain nonspecific and may still overlap with neoplastic lesions. The absence of systemic infectious symptoms further reduced preoperative suspicion for an infectious etiology. Importantly, a chest radiograph obtained 2 weeks before surgery showed no acute cardiopulmonary abnormality or radiographic evidence of active pulmonary tuberculosis. No broader preoperative systemic imaging for disseminated tuberculosis was performed because clinical suspicion for active infection remained low. Pathological examination, supported by acid-fast staining and PCR confirmation, ultimately established the diagnosis of tuberculous brain abscess, demonstrating the limitations of neuroimaging alone in reliably distinguishing infectious from neoplastic intracerebral lesions.

Management of tuberculous brain abscess generally consists of prolonged antituberculous therapy, with surgical intervention considered in select cases. The goals of surgery include reducing mass effect, decreasing bacillary burden, and obtaining tissue for definitive diagnosis.19 Reported surgical strategies include aspiration, stereotactic drainage, repeated burr hole aspiration, and craniotomy with excision. Because the literature is limited largely to case reports and small series, there are no high-quality comparative data establishing the superiority of medical therapy alone versus combined surgical and medical management.19,20 In practice, aspiration or excision is generally considered in cases of diagnostic uncertainty, large or multiloculated lesions, thick-walled abscesses, significant mass effect, neurological deterioration, posterior fossa location, or failure to improve with medical therapy alone.21,22

In the present case, management options included stereotactic biopsy, empiric medical therapy if infection were strongly suspected, or open resection. Although stereotactic biopsy would have been a reasonable diagnostic alternative, awake craniotomy with language mapping was favored because the preoperative diagnosis overall favored a neoplastic process, the patient had progressive symptoms, and the lesion was both accessible and adjacent to dominant-­hemisphere language cortex. This approach permitted both definitive tissue diagnosis and therapeutic resection in a single procedure.

This case adds to the limited literature describing tuberculous brain abscess in patients with well-controlled HIV infection and underscores the need to consider tuberculosis when evaluating solitary intracerebral masses with tumor-like imaging characteristics.

Lessons

Although pathological confirmation remains the diagnostic gold standard for tuberculous abscess, neuroimaging alone may be insufficient to reliably distinguish these lesions from neoplastic processes. Imaging characteristics such as peripheral enhancement, signal abnormalities, or surrounding edema are nonspecific and may overlap with high-grade glioma, metastasis, lymphoma, or other infectious etiologies.23–25 Importantly, the absence of systemic infectious features does not exclude tuberculous abscess, and reliance on imaging findings alone may lead to diagnostic delay or misclassification.26

The lesion’s proximity to the dominant language region and progression of symptoms supported an upfront surgical approach, which provided both definitive diagnosis and therapeutic benefit. Tuberculous brain abscess may occur even in patients without overt immunodeficiency, including those with well-controlled HIV infection and remote tuberculosis exposure, highlighting the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis and pursuing timely tissue confirmation for tumor-like intracerebral lesions to avoid delays in treating either infection or malignancy.
