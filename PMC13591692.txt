Surgical outcomes of four inferior oblique weakening techniques: a graded, severity-based approach
Authors: Ceren Gürez, Ata Baytaroğlu, Zahid Hüseyinhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInferior oblique muscle overaction (IOOA) is a common cause of vertical strabismus. Most published comparisons cover two or three techniques. We evaluated 1-year outcomes of a severity-guided algorithm comparing four weakening techniques for IOOA: disinsertion, myectomy, anterior transposition (AT), and nasal transposition (NT).MethodsIn this retrospective comparative cohort study, 286 eyes of 200 patients with IOOA (grade + 1 to + 4) were treated by disinsertion (n = 141), myectomy (n = 29), AT (n = 94), or NT (n = 22), selected by the operating surgeon according to preoperative IOOA severity (minimum 1-year follow-up). Pre- and postoperative IOOA grade, abnormal head posture (AHP), and horizontal deviation were compared with non-parametric tests; a patient-level sensitivity analysis was performed. Success (postoperative IOOA grade 0 or + 1 without underaction) is reported with 95% Wilson confidence intervals.ResultsThe cohort’s median preoperative IOOA grade was + 3 and fell to 0 at 1 year (p < 0.001). As expected from the indication-based design, preoperative IOOA differed across groups (Kruskal-Wallis p < 0.001): disinsertion was used for a median grade of + 3 (range + 1 to + 4); transposition techniques were used for more severe cases (median + 3.5 to + 4). At 1 year, the median postoperative IOOA was 0 in every group (p = 0.098 between techniques). Patient-level sensitivity analysis preserved the same conclusion (Kruskal-Wallis postoperative p = 0.075). Success (postoperative IOOA grade 0 or + 1) was achieved in 92 to 95% of patients in each arm, with overlapping 95% confidence intervals (Disinsertion 93.1%, 95% CI 86.4 to 96.6%; Myectomy 89.5%, 95% CI 68.6 to 97.1%; anterior transposition 92.1%, 95% CI 82.7 to 96.6%; nasal transposition 94.1%, 95% CI 73.0 to 99.0%). AHP prevalence fell from 54.5% to 15.0%, and no case of anti-elevation syndrome was observed.ConclusionsWithin a severity-guided, real-world algorithm, the four techniques achieved comparable short-term IOOA control when applied within their intended severity ranges; because assignment was indication-driven, this reflects within-indication performance, not equivalence. This short-term parity should be read cautiously and may not hold over time: a 1-year follow-up cannot capture the late recurrences. Prospective studies with longer follow-up, etiology stratification, dissociated vertical deviation documentation, and functional endpoints are needed.

Background

Inferior oblique muscle overaction (IOOA) is a common cause of vertical strabismus, often presenting with hypertropia and V-pattern deviation. IOOA may be associated with dissociated vertical deviation (DVD), where IOOA acts as a clinical feature that frequently occurs alongside DVD rather than resulting from it. IOOA can significantly impact binocular vision, cause abnormal head posture, and lower quality of life, especially in children [1–4]. The condition is a major component of pediatric strabismus, with a high prevalence. Literature indicates that IOOA develops in up to 72% of patients with congenital esotropia and about 30–34% of those with accommodative esotropia or intermittent exotropia [5]. Patients often adopt a compensatory abnormal head posture (AHP), such as tilting or turning their head, to maintain single binocular vision and prevent diplopia. When fusion is not possible, the AHP may be used to increase the separation of diplopic images [3]. If this condition continues, especially during childhood, it can lead to long-term secondary effects, including facial asymmetry and permanent musculoskeletal changes such as neck muscle contracture and torticollis [3, 5]. These functional consequences support timely surgical intervention. Conservative treatments such as prismatic correction are often ineffective for IOOA because the deviation is incomitant and varies with different gaze positions [6]. Surgical intervention is often required when conservative treatments fail or when IOOA is associated with complex strabismus patterns [7–9]. IOOA results from primary muscle hyperfunction, secondary adaptation to superior oblique palsy (SOP), or anatomical anomalies. The anatomical course and neurovascular supply of the inferior oblique (IO) muscle influence both overaction development and surgical outcomes. Surgical weakening aims to reduce the muscle’s elevating and extorting effects, thereby correcting vertical and torsional deviations [8, 10, 11]. The mechanism of action of the previously described surgical techniques involves reducing muscle force by detaching a segment with minimal risk of overcorrection, which is the primary goal of disinsertion and myectomy. Transposition procedures change the IO muscle’s vector, converting its elevating and extorting actions into depressor and intorter functions; this mechanism is particularly relevant in DVD [11, 12]. Using a similar approach, nasal transposition (NT) redirects muscle action nasally, maintaining temporal function and neurovascular supply, which is particularly helpful in complex or recurrent cases [9, 13]. The current literature increasingly stresses individualized, severity-matched surgical planning based on the IOOA presentation and associated findings [14]. Most published series compare two or three techniques, or focus on a single etiology such as SOP. Large-scale cohort studies evaluating four distinct procedures in parallel, including the less commonly reported nasal transposition, remain limited [15]. Recently, Hao et al. described a modified inferior oblique muscle belly transposition for V-pattern exotropia with mild to moderate IOOA and reported an 87% success rate for IO overaction correction [15]. However, that report addressed a single transposition variant and did not compare multiple weakening techniques. Similarly, while several recent studies have compared two or three IO weakening procedures [2, 16–18], large-scale comparative data encompassing four distinct surgical approaches, including the rarely reported nasal transposition, remain scarce. Here, we report the 1-year outcomes of a severity-guided algorithm comparing four different inferior oblique weakening techniques in a large cohort that included both primary and secondary IOOA.

Methods

This was a retrospective comparative cohort study conducted at the Strabismus Unit of Beyoğlu Eye Training and Research Hospital and reported in accordance with the STROBE statement for observational research. The departmental surgical database (2014 to 2023) was screened for consecutive patients undergoing inferior oblique weakening surgery. A total of 286 eyes from 200 patients with IOOA (either primary or secondary) met inclusion criteria and form the analytic cohort. Approval for the study was granted by the Non-Interventional Local Ethics Committee of Prof. Dr. Cemil Tascioglu City Hospital (Approval No: 1119), and the work followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants or, for minors who could not provide consent themselves, from a parent or legal guardian. Patients were included if they had documented IOOA (grade + 1 to + 4) requiring surgical intervention, underwent one of four surgical techniques (disinsertion, myectomy, anterior transposition, or nasal transposition), and completed at least 1 year of postoperative follow-up. Patients with previous extraocular muscle surgery, additional ocular diseases affecting ocular motility, incomplete follow-up data, or infantile esotropia with very large-angle deviations (> 40 prism diopters) were excluded. All patients underwent ophthalmologic examination including best-corrected visual acuity (decimal-Snellen), cycloplegic refraction, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, and fundoscopy. Orthoptic assessment included the cover-uncover test, the alternate cover test with prism neutralization at near (33 cm) and distance (6 m) fixation, assessment of ocular motility in nine diagnostic positions of gaze, and grading of IOOA on a 4-step scale from + 1 (mild) to + 4 (severe) according to the method of Guyton. IOOA grade was assigned by the operating strabismus surgeon at the examination chair; two surgeons (Z.H. and C.G.) contributed cases, routinely cross-graded complex cases, and applied the same institutional severity-based algorithm, and grading was repeated at the immediate preoperative assessment. To check for surgeon-level variation, preoperative IOOA grade and the distribution of assigned techniques were compared between the two surgeons. The presence and direction of AHP (face turn, head tilt, or chin position) was documented descriptively on chart. Superior oblique function was assessed by version testing, the Bielschowsky head-tilt test, and fundus extorsion when available; patients with superior oblique palsy (SOP) were identified as having secondary IOOA.

The choice of surgical method was determined by the operating surgeon’s preference, guided by the preoperative severity of IOOA and clinical presentation. In general, disinsertion was chosen for milder IOOA (grades + 1 to + 3), myectomy for moderate cases (grades + 2 to + 4), and anterior transposition (AT) or nasal transposition (NT) for more severe cases (grades + 3 to + 4), especially when associated with dissociated vertical deviation (DVD) or SOP. Within the overlapping + 2 to + 3 range, myectomy rather than disinsertion was preferred when the overaction was at the upper end of that range, when the muscle appeared bulky or fibrotic on intraoperative inspection, or when a larger primary-position hypertropia indicated that a more definitive weakening effect was required; disinsertion was reserved for the milder end of the overlap and for shorter operating times in young children. Because this overlap decision rests on intraoperative judgement of muscle bulk and hypertropia rather than a fixed cut-off, it is inherently surgeon-dependent and may limit the reproducibility of the algorithm at other centres. Graded inferior oblique recession was not used as a weakening procedure in this cohort. Our institutional practice favors the four techniques described above because, in our hands, they are technically faster, minimize the number of needle passes through sclera in pediatric eyes, and provide a predictable graded weakening effect that is matched to the preoperative IOOA grade. We acknowledge that recession is widely practiced internationally and that the exclusion of recession limits the generalizability of our algorithm to centers that use it as a first-line graded option.

All surgeries were conducted under general anesthesia by experienced strabismus surgeons. The IO muscle was accessed via an inferior temporal fornix incision. The conjunctiva and Tenon’s capsule were opened 8 mm posterior to the limbus in the inferior temporal quadrant.

Disinsertion

After isolating and hooking the IO muscle, it was detached from its scleral insertion. The cut end was carefully cauterized to achieve hemostasis, and the muscle was released to retract freely within Tenon’s capsule without reattachment.

Myectomy

After identifying and hooking the IO muscle, two hemostatic clamps were placed near its insertion, leaving a 5 mm segment of muscle between them. This segment was excised. Hemostasis was achieved by cauterizing the cut ends, and the remaining muscle was released within Tenon’s capsule.

Anterior Transposition (AT): After hooking the IO muscle, a double-armed 6 − 0 polyglactin (Vicryl) suture was placed through the full width of the muscle, with each needle exiting through a separate muscle pole (temporal and nasal), 1 mm posterior to its insertion. The muscle was detached from its original insertion and reattached adjacent to the inferior rectus (IR) muscle insertion. The placement was graded according to preoperative IOOA severity: at + 2 IOOA, the muscle was placed 1 mm lateral and 3–4 mm posterior to the IR insertion; at + 3 IOOA, 1 mm lateral and 1–2 mm posterior to the IR insertion; and at + 4 IOOA, immediately adjacent to the IR insertion. Both temporal and nasal portions of the IO muscle were sutured along the same horizontal line to minimize the risk of anti-elevation syndrome.

Nasal Transposition (NT): After hooking the IO muscle, a double-armed 6 − 0 polyglactin suture was placed through the muscle 1 mm posterior to its insertion, with each needle exiting through separate muscle poles. The muscle was detached from its original insertion and sutured immediately nasal to and 2 mm posterior to the IR muscle insertion. In our nomenclature, this procedure is a nasal transposition of the inferior oblique, distinct from anterior nasal transposition, in which the muscle is secured at or adjacent to the inferior rectus insertion so that anteriorization is combined with a nasal shift, and from the nasal myectomy of Stager et al., in which a nasal muscle segment is excised [13, 19]. Because the new insertion lies nasal and slightly posterior to the inferior rectus insertion, the muscle belly remains posterior to the equator of the globe, which preserves elevating force in upgaze and avoids the anti-elevation leash effect associated with anterior placement [7, 20]. This technique was mainly chosen for severe cases (+ 3 to + 4 IOOA), recurrent cases, or those with SOP, as it offers effective weakening while preserving the temporal portion of the muscle and its neurovascular supply.

In bilateral cases, the same surgical technique was applied symmetrically to both eyes when IOOA severity was similar. In cases with asymmetric IOOA, each eye was treated individually based on the severity of overaction.

Patients were examined at 1 week, 1 month, and 1 year after surgery. At each visit, the IOOA grade, ocular alignment at near and distance, presence of AHP, and any complications were assessed. Particular attention was given to the development of anti-elevation syndrome (AES), defined as limited elevation in abduction.

AHP was assessed at every visit by the examining strabismus surgeon while the patient fixated a distance accommodative target with both eyes open; the presence or absence of a clinically apparent face turn, head tilt, or chin position deviation from the primary head posture was recorded as a categorical outcome (present or absent). Quantitative goniometric measurement was not part of routine practice during the study period and was therefore not available for the historical cohort. Anti-elevation syndrome (AES) was defined as a postoperative limitation of elevation in abduction associated with a secondary upshoot of the contralateral adducting eye; suspected cases were further evaluated with version testing and, where available, the forced duction test, in accordance with the criteria described by Kushner.

The primary outcome measure was the postoperative IOOA grade at 1-year follow-up. Surgical success was defined as a reduction of IOOA to grade 0 or + 1 without the development of IO underaction or other complications. Secondary outcomes included the resolution of AHP, changes in horizontal deviation, and complication rates.

All statistical analyses used a p-value of less than 0.05 as the cutoff for significance. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality for all continuous variables. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables and as median with interquartile range (IQR) for those that are not. Because not all variables were normally distributed, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for group comparisons, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test evaluated preoperative and postoperative differences, and the Mann-Whitney U test was used for subgroup analyses. The unit of analysis was defined a priori for each outcome. Continuous ocular measurements and IOOA grade were analyzed at the eye level, whereas surgical success and the adjusted models were analyzed at the patient level to preserve statistical independence. For the patient-level analyses each patient contributed a single observation defined by the worse eye, taken as the eye with the higher residual (postoperative) IOOA grade; when the two eyes had undergone different procedures, the worse eye determined the assigned technique, so that no patient was counted more than once. Bilateral cases, whether symmetric or asymmetric in overaction, were handled in this way. To mitigate the indication bias inherent in severity-driven procedure selection, adjusted analyses were performed at the patient level: postoperative IOOA grade was modeled with ordinal (proportional-odds) logistic regression, and surgical success with multivariable logistic regression using the Firth penalized-likelihood method to accommodate the small number of failures. Both models included surgical technique, baseline IOOA grade, age, laterality (unilateral versus bilateral surgery), deviation type (esotropia versus exotropia), and preoperative AHP. A distinct primary-versus-secondary etiology field was not available in the historical records, so deviation type served as an etiological proxy and the pooling of etiologies is acknowledged as a limitation. Odds ratios (OR) are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and the independent contribution of technique was assessed with a likelihood-ratio test for the three-degree-of-freedom technique block. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.6.1 for macOS (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts, USA) and in Python 3.12 with NumPy, pandas, SciPy, and statsmodels.

Results

A total of 286 eyes from 200 patients who underwent surgery for IOOA were included in the study. The median age at the time of surgery was 7.0 years (IQR, 4.0–15.0). The gender distribution was balanced (female: 99/200, 49.5%; male: 101/200, 50.5%). Esotropia was present in 163 patients (81.5%), and exotropia in 37 patients (18.5%). The relatively small mean horizontal deviation (approximately 10–12 PD) is explained by the exclusion of patients with infantile esotropia with very large-angle deviations. Preoperative near and distance deviations measured 12.0 PD (IQR, 8.0–14.0) and 10.0 PD (IQR, 10.0–14.0), respectively, decreasing after surgery to 0.0 PD (IQR, 0.0–0.0) at both near and distance. An AHP was observed in 109 patients (54.5%) before surgery and persisted in only 30 patients (15.0%) afterward. The operated eye was the right in 49 patients (24.5%), the left in 65 (32.5%), and bilateral in 86 (43.0%). The clinical and demographic characteristics of the cases are summarized in Table 1.

IOOA grades were not normally distributed; therefore, all comparisons used non-parametric tests and are presented as medians with IQR. In eye-based analysis, the preoperative IOOA grade was 3.0 (IQR, 3.0–4.0) in right eyes (n = 135) and 3.0 (IQR, 2.0–4.0) in left eyes (n = 151), decreasing significantly after surgery to 0.0 (IQR, 0.0–0.0) in both eyes (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 0.001 for both; Δ median 3.0 in each eye).

When analyzed by surgical technique (Table 2), the preoperative IOOA severity differed significantly between groups (Kruskal-Wallis H = 64.8, p < 0.001): median preoperative grades were 3.0 (2.0–3.0) for disinsertion (n = 141), 3.0 (3.0–4.0) for myectomy (n = 29), 3.5 (3.0–4.0) for anterior transposition (n = 94), and 4.0 (3.0–4.0) for nasal transposition (n = 22). After surgery, IOOA grades decreased in all groups, and there was no significant difference between techniques (Table 3, H = 7.8, p = 0.098), with median postoperative grades of 0.0 across all groups (IQR 0.0–0.0, except 0.0-0.5 for the nasal transposition group). The level of improvement also varied significantly among techniques (H = 38.5, p < 0.001), although all methods achieved a median reduction of about 3 grades (Δ median 3.0; IQR 2.0–4.0) across techniques (Tables 2 and 3).

Surgical success, defined as a postoperative IOOA grade of 0 or + 1 in the worse eye without underaction, was achieved in 185 of 200 patients overall (92.5%, 95% Wilson CI 88.0 to 95.4%). Success rates were similar across the four techniques: disinsertion 94/101 (93.1%, 95% CI 86.4 to 96.6%), myectomy 17/19 (89.5%, 95% CI 68.6 to 97.1%), anterior transposition 58/63 (92.1%, 95% CI 82.7 to 96.6%), and nasal transposition 16/17 (94.1%, 95% CI 73.0 to 99.0%); chi-square test for between-technique difference, p = 0.93. The overlapping confidence intervals indicate that each procedure achieved a high rate of short-term success within its intended severity range; because assignment was indication-driven rather than randomized, this should not be interpreted as equivalence between techniques, and the adjusted analysis below was performed to address this confounding.

To address the non-independence of paired eyes from the 86 bilateral patients, a patient-level sensitivity analysis was performed using the worse eye per patient. At the patient level, preoperative IOOA still differed significantly across techniques (Kruskal-Wallis H = 42.8, p < 0.001), reflecting indication-based selection; the postoperative comparison again showed no statistically significant difference between techniques (Kruskal-Wallis H = 6.9, p = 0.075), confirming that the principal finding does not depend on the unit of analysis. The median patient-level improvement was 3 IOOA grades (IQR 2 to 3) and was concordant with the eye-level result.

Adjusted analyses were performed to separate the contribution of surgical technique from the indication-based selection reflected in the baseline grades. In the ordinal (proportional-odds) logistic regression for postoperative IOOA grade, baseline IOOA grade was the only independent predictor of a higher residual grade (OR 2.49, 95% CI 1.45 to 4.28, p = 0.001), whereas none of the four techniques was independently associated with the postoperative grade (myectomy OR 1.88, 95% CI 0.54 to 6.52, p = 0.32; anterior transposition OR 1.00, 95% CI 0.38 to 2.64, p = 0.99; nasal transposition OR 0.98, 95% CI 0.26 to 3.70, p = 0.98, each relative to disinsertion), and the technique block did not improve model fit (likelihood-ratio p = 0.76). The multivariable Firth penalized logistic model for surgical success was concordant: a higher baseline grade was associated with lower odds of success (OR 0.49, 95% CI 0.24 to 0.99, p = 0.048), whereas technique was not (technique block likelihood-ratio p = 0.54), and age, laterality, deviation type, and preoperative AHP were not independently associated with the outcome (Table 4). After adjustment for baseline severity and these covariates, no single technique was independently associated with either the postoperative grade or surgical success, consistent with outcomes being driven by appropriate severity-matched selection rather than by an intrinsic advantage of any one procedure.

Subgroup analyses showed no significant differences between sexes in IOOA severity. Preoperative IOOA grades were similar for male and female eyes (both medians 3.0; Mann-Whitney U, p = 0.447), and postoperative levels also remained comparable (both medians 0.0; p = 0.075). However, deviation type was linked to preoperative IOOA severity: eyes with esotropia had a higher preoperative IOOA grade than those with exotropia (median 3.0 [IQR, 3.0–4.0] vs. 3.0 [IQR, 2.0–3.0]; Mann-Whitney U, p = 0.0013). This difference was no longer evident after surgery, as both esotropic and exotropic eyes showed a median IOOA grade of 0.0 (IQR, 0.0–0.0; p = 0.517). Because two surgeons contributed cases and grading was not masked to the intended procedure, we compared them directly. Neither preoperative IOOA severity nor the mix of chosen techniques differed systematically between them: the mean preoperative IOOA grade was 3.21 versus 3.16 in right eyes (p = 0.90) and 3.03 versus 3.01 in left eyes (p = 0.97), and the distribution of assigned techniques (coded 1 to 4 from disinsertion to nasal transposition) was likewise comparable (right eye p = 0.96; left eye p = 0.94). Surgeon-level variation is therefore an unlikely source of confounding. Regarding AHP distribution among surgical technique groups, preoperative AHP prevalence was similar across all four groups (52% in disinsertion, 58% in myectomy, 55% in AT, and 59% in NT; p = 0.724), with no significant difference between techniques. Bilateral surgery was performed in 86 patients (43%). Unilateral and bilateral surgery groups showed similar postoperative IOOA reduction (median postoperative grade 0.0; p = 0.412), consistent with prior reports. Concomitant horizontal muscle surgery was performed in 112 patients (56%), including medial rectus recession, lateral rectus resection, or combined procedures, based on the type and degree of horizontal deviation. All IO weakening procedures in this cohort were performed in the same operative session as any concomitant horizontal-muscle surgery. No cases of anti-elevation syndrome (AES) were observed during the 1-year follow-up period. No milder, subclinical limitation of elevation in abduction that fell short of the full criteria for AES was recorded in the anterior transposition group, and no new upshoot of the contralateral adducting eye was noted. A representative patient with + 4 IOOA treated by anterior transposition is shown in the nine diagnostic positions of gaze before and after surgery in Fig. 1, with the corresponding intraoperative steps in Fig. 2. No other significant complications, including overcorrection (IO underaction), scleral perforation, or infection, were documented. All four surgical techniques were followed by a statistically significant reduction in IOOA and improvement in AHP.

Concomitant horizontal muscle surgery (medial rectus recession, lateral rectus resection, or combined procedures, performed in the same operative session) was carried out in 112 of 200 patients (56.0%), based on the type and magnitude of the horizontal deviation. The dominant role of concomitant horizontal surgery in improving primary-position horizontal alignment in this cohort means that the postoperative horizontal-deviation values reflect the combined effect of inferior oblique weakening and the concurrent horizontal-rectus procedure rather than the isolated horizontal effect of inferior oblique weakening; the secondary horizontal-deviation outcome should be interpreted accordingly. Documented reoperations within the 1-year follow-up window comprised three additional weakening or transposition procedures in three patients (1.5%); no eye underwent a second operation for AES.

Discussion

All four oblique weakening techniques reduced the cohort’s median IOOA from + 3 preoperatively to 0 at 1 year. Between-technique comparison of postoperative IOOA at 1 year did not reach statistical significance (eye-level Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.098), and the patient-level sensitivity analysis yielded a concordant result (p = 0.075). Success rates were 92 to 95% across the four techniques with overlapping 95% confidence intervals (chi-square p = 0.93). Because procedure assignment was indication-driven rather than randomized, these findings indicate that each technique achieved comparable short-term IOOA control when applied within its intended severity range, rather than that the techniques are equivalent. This interpretation is reinforced by the adjusted analysis, in which no technique was independently associated with the postoperative grade or with success once baseline severity was taken into account.

However, assuming that all procedures are equally effective based on a superficial interpretation would be incorrect. A central feature of these data is the marked indication-based confounding visible in the preoperative grades (Table 3). The between-group difference in preoperative IOOA severity (Kruskal-Wallis p < 0.001) reflects a stepwise, indication-driven surgical selection rather than randomized assignment. Myoreductive procedures (disinsertion, myectomy) were preferred for milder to moderate IOOA (+ 1 to + 3.0), while vector-changing transposition procedures were reserved for more severe IOOA (median preoperative grade + 3.5 for AT, + 4 for NT).

These data do not prove that the techniques are fundamentally identical; instead, it offers a large-scale, real-world validation of a graded clinical algorithm. The postoperative outcome with a p = 0.098 value confirms that the result is consistently successful when the surgical procedure is properly matched to the severity of overaction, rather than indicating a failure to detect a difference. This aligns well with many reports in the literature supporting this type of stepwise surgical strategy. In this approach, milder IOOA (+ 1 to + 2) is treated with disinsertion, moderate IOOA (+ 2 to + 3) with myectomy, and more severe IOOA (+ 3 to + 4) with anteriorization or nasal transposition (Fig. 3). The main contribution of this study is that it documents this effective, real-world treatment paradigm in a large cohort.

Disinsertion was the most performed procedure in this group (n = 141), effectively reducing IOOA across a range of preoperative grades (+ 1 to + 4, with a median of + 3). Although the median preoperative grade was + 3, the disinsertion group spanned the full IOOA range from + 1 to + 4, with 57 eyes (40%) in the milder + 1 to + 2 range. Its popularity stems from its technical simplicity, as it is generally quicker and avoids the risks of scleral perforation associated with reattachment sutures [16]. However, the excellent one-year outcomes reported here should be interpreted with caution. Disinsertion is known for an unpredictable reattachment rate and a high risk of long-term recurrence [17]. A recent study reported a 53% recurrence of overaction [18]. Although other studies have shown much higher success rates, approaching 88–92%, the 1-year follow-up in this cohort is not long enough to capture this procedure-specific late complication [21]. Based on these data, the favorable 1-year disinsertion results should not be interpreted as evidence of long-term stability, and counseling should reflect this uncertainty. Myectomy (n = 29) also showed excellent results, with complete resolution of the median + 3.0 IOOA. This supports other studies indicating that myectomy and disinsertion are equally effective [18]. Prior reports have suggested that myectomy may be more effective at reducing primary-position hypertropia, particularly when the preoperative deviation exceeds 15 PD, and that it may be preferable to recession at higher IOOA grades [1]. Myectomy is also technically simpler and less time-consuming than recession or anteriorization. A recent study by Hassan et al. compared graded marginal myotomy with myectomy and found both techniques effective, with marginal myotomy offering the advantage of adjustable weakening [22]. The data from this study support myectomy as a dependable and effective treatment for moderate to severe IOOA at 1 year, although the literature notes that myectomy, like disinsertion, leaves the cut muscle without an anchored insertion and can therefore be followed by reattachment to sclera or Tenon’s capsule and a late recurrence of overaction; this risk is not fully captured by a 12-month follow-up [21]. Anterior transposition (n = 94) was used for severe cases (median preoperative + 3.5), consistent with its established role as a strong weakening procedure for moderate-to-severe IOOA [7, 9, 23]. However, in modern strabismus surgery, the main indication for AT is not only the degree of IOOA but also the presence of DVD. The vector-changing mechanism of AT, which turns the IO muscle into a depressor, directly opposes the dissociated upward drift caused by the DVD. DVD status was not systematically documented in our cohort, so the specific interaction between AT and DVD cannot be conclusively evaluated from these data. The modern indication for AT in the DVD-IOOA combination is well established: head-to-head comparisons of AT with anterior nasal transposition in this population have shown that both procedures reduce DVD, V-pattern, and IOOA, with anterior nasal transposition producing greater DVD correction in primary gaze but a higher rate of consecutive hypotropia and horizontal drift [24]. In another series where anteriorization was performed more often, an average horizontal effect of 4 PD (range 0–20) was reported for both near and far vision. Because IO transposition has a measurable horizontal effect, the horizontal component should be incorporated into preoperative planning [10]. An important consideration for AT is iatrogenic anti-elevation syndrome (AES). AES is marked by limited elevation, particularly in abduction. Although initially described as a restrictive hypotropia, Kushner showed that the forced duction test is usually negative in these cases, indicating that the underlying mechanism involves a leash effect rather than actual restriction [25]. The risk of AES increases as the muscle is positioned more anteriorly relative to the inferior rectus insertion [19]. No case of AES was observed in this cohort. This is due to several surgical precautions: avoiding overly anterior placement of the IO muscle, ensuring both muscle poles are sutured along the same horizontal line, and preferring unilateral AT when appropriate. Reported risk factors for AES include unilateral AT performed in the absence of DVD, and inadvertent muscle resection at the time of disinsertion. Unilateral transposition was avoided in patients without a clear indication such as DVD or significant SOP, which may explain the absence of vertical or torsional imbalance in this cohort. Nasal transposition (n = 22), consistent with the literature, was reserved for the most severe cases (median preoperative + 4.0). This is consistent with its established role in severe, recurrent, or complex strabismus, including congenital SOP and cases with absent superior oblique tendons [23]. Its potent mechanism converts the IO into an intorter and depressor and, unlike anterior transposition, does not place the inferior oblique anterior to the equator of the globe; this preserves the muscle’s elevating force in upgaze and mechanically eliminates the leash effect that underlies anti-elevation syndrome, which is the principal complication of anterior transposition [20]. This nasal transposition, in which the muscle is fixed nasal and slightly posterior to the inferior rectus insertion, therefore differs from anterior nasal transposition, in which anteriorization is deliberately combined with a nasal shift [7, 19]. The clinical criteria for choosing NT instead of temporal transposition (AT) in our practice include: the presence of SOP with significant excyclotorsion, recurrent IOOA after previous surgery, cases requiring preservation of the temporal IO function and neurovascular supply, and patients with absent or anomalous superior oblique tendons. Torsional outcomes were not systematically quantified in this historical cohort, as neither subjective torsion (double Maddox rod) nor objective fundus torsion was recorded at every visit. In practice, torsion influenced the choice of procedure rather than serving as a measured endpoint: nasal transposition was preferred over anterior transposition when significant excyclotorsion accompanied SOP, because nasal repositioning of the inferior oblique augments intorsion while still weakening elevation, whereas anterior transposition mainly addresses the vertical and DVD components. The absence of pre- and postoperative torsional measurements, particularly in the nasal transposition subgroup, is a limitation, and quantitative torsion should be a primary endpoint in future prospective work. Although the NT subgroup is small, its inclusion is a particular strength of the present analysis. The slight residual overaction (IQR 0.0 to 0.5) in this group reflects its use for the most refractory cases. Kasem et al. described retro-equatorial IO myopexy as an alternative technique for IOOA, reporting effective weakening with potentially lower complication rates [6]. The significant drop in AHP prevalence from 54.5% before surgery to 15.0% afterward clearly shows functional success. This result is consistent with prior reports indicating that IO weakening, regardless of the specific technique, can correct compensatory head postures [1]. Residual AHP of about 15% is in line with prior reports; persistent AHP may remain after successful ocular realignment, particularly in long-standing cases with secondary musculoskeletal changes or non-ocular comorbidities [26]. Esotropia was associated with more severe preoperative IOOA in our cohort. The cohort was 81.5% esotropic, and this subgroup exhibited significantly more severe preoperative IOOA (p = 0.0013). This finding is consistent with the literature, which reports IOOA in 65 to 72% of congenital esotropes [5]. However, this link also highlights a major limitation: the study combined primary and secondary IOOA cases. It is closely linked with bilateral presentation and infantile esotropia, whereas secondary IOOA results from paresis of the ipsilateral superior oblique. These are different pathophysiological conditions that may respond differently to various procedures. Pooling these conditions limits the ability to evaluate how each of the four surgeries performs separately in primary versus secondary IOOA. Although primary and secondary IOOA are conventionally treated as separate entities, several lines of evidence suggest that this distinction has limited bearing on how an overacting muscle responds to weakening. Ultrastructural studies have found the same changes (mitochondrial aggregation, vacuolisation, and fibre atrophy) in primary and secondary overacting inferior oblique muscles, and have argued that primary overaction may itself reflect a subclinical superior oblique paresis, placing the two on a shared pathophysiological continuum [27–29]. Because the mechanical target of surgery is the same in both, namely an overacting muscle with an elevating and extorting vector, the effect of a given weakening procedure should depend more on the severity of the overaction than on its underlying cause. This expectation is borne out clinically, in that the four techniques we used are precisely those validated in purely secondary (superior oblique palsy) cohorts, where success rates of roughly 88 to 92% overlap those reported in primary-IOOA series [1, 16, 23, 30]. In our own cohort the vector-changing transposition tier was, if anything, applied preferentially to eyes with superior oblique palsy, excyclotorsion, or recurrent overaction, that is, to cases enriched for secondary IOOA, yet its outcomes matched those of the myoreductive groups. Pooling the two etiologies therefore seems unlikely to have materially biased the within-technique comparison, although it remains a limitation and an etiology-stratified prospective analysis is still warranted. Regarding unilateral versus bilateral surgery, Mostafa and Kassem reported comparable outcomes between unilateral and bilateral IO recession/anteriorization for unilateral IOOA, with bilateral surgery potentially offering advantages in symmetry [31]. In our cohort, 43% of patients underwent bilateral surgery, with comparable success rates between unilateral and bilateral groups. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the largest single-center series to compare four distinct inferior oblique weakening techniques side by side. Strengths of this study include the cohort size of 286 eyes and the inclusion of four weakening techniques, including the rarely reported nasal transposition. Together, these features provide a real-world overview of a graded surgical approach with high short-term success rates. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, primary IOOA (associated with infantile esotropia) and secondary IOOA (resulting from superior oblique palsy) were pooled in a single analysis. These are pathophysiologically distinct conditions that may respond differently to surgical intervention. Although a formal subgroup analysis was planned, the unequal distribution of etiologies across surgical groups and insufficient sample sizes within each etiology-technique subgroup precluded meaningful statistical comparison. This is the most consequential limitation of the present analysis and restricts the generalizability of the findings. Second, the absence of systematic DVD documentation limits the ability to evaluate the specific efficacy of AT in the DVD-IOOA combination, which is its primary modern indication. Third, the indication-based selection inherent to a retrospective design and the unequal sample sizes across surgical groups reduce statistical power for between-group comparisons. Fourth, objective functional outcomes such as stereopsis, vertical fusional amplitude, and torsional measurements were not assessed; these should be included as primary endpoints in future prospective studies. Fifth, IOOA was graded clinically by the operating surgeon and grading was not masked to the procedure performed; although complex cases were cross-graded by a second surgeon, a degree of observer-related bias in the ordinal grade cannot be excluded. Reassuringly, a direct comparison of the two operating surgeons showed no systematic difference in preoperative IOOA grade or in technique choice (all p > 0.90), which makes surgeon-level variation an unlikely explanation for the results, even if it cannot be entirely ruled out. A further constraint is that within the + 2 to + 3 overlap the choice between disinsertion and myectomy was made from intraoperative muscle bulk, hypertropia magnitude, and operating time rather than a fixed threshold; this judgement is surgeon-dependent and limits the reproducibility of the algorithm outside our unit. Finally, the 1-year follow-up is insufficient to evaluate long-term success and recurrence, particularly for disinsertion, which has been associated with unpredictable reattachment and late recurrence in prior series.

A deliberate methodological choice in this study was the omission of graded inferior oblique recession, which is widely used internationally as a first-line graded weakening procedure and which provides predictable graded weakening with adjustable magnitude. Our institutional practice favors disinsertion, myectomy, and the two transposition variants because the four procedures together cover the full IOOA severity range used in our algorithm and, in our experience, are technically faster and minimize sclera-suture passes in young patients. We acknowledge that the exclusion of recession limits the external validity of the algorithm to centers in which recession is not used as the first-line graded option; a complementary comparison incorporating recession should be a target of future multicenter work [20]. For centers in which graded recession is the preferred first-line procedure, the present algorithm can be mapped onto recession-based practice without major change. Graded recession, which provides titratable and reversible weakening with an anchored insertion, could substitute for disinsertion and myectomy across the mild-to-moderate range (grades + 1 to + 3), where its adjustable dose-response and lower theoretical risk of late reattachment recurrence are advantages; the transposition tier would remain reserved for severe overaction (+ 3 to + 4), for DVD, and for cases with significant torsion, where a vector change rather than simple weakening is required [4, 18, 31]. The graded, severity-based principle is therefore transferable even where the specific myoreductive procedure differs, and a direct comparison of recession against disinsertion and myectomy within the same severity strata is a logical next step.

A further methodological consideration is that 56% of patients underwent concomitant horizontal muscle surgery in the same operative session; the postoperative horizontal-deviation values therefore reflect the combined effect of inferior oblique weakening and the concurrent horizontal-rectus procedure. The horizontal alignment outcomes reported here should not be interpreted as the isolated horizontal effect of IO weakening; a stratified analysis restricted to the subgroup without concomitant horizontal surgery is constrained by sample size in this cohort and is best addressed by purpose-designed prospective studies.

Conclusions

This large retrospective comparative study of four inferior oblique weakening techniques shows that a graded, severity-based surgical approach in which the procedure is matched to the preoperative grade of IOOA achieved comparable short-term IOOA control when each technique was applied within its intended severity range, with success rates of 92 to 95% per technique and overlapping confidence intervals. This short-term parity should not be mistaken for durable equivalence, because a 1-year follow-up cannot capture the late recurrence recognized after the myoreductive procedures, and the comparability between techniques may narrow with longer follow-up. Because procedure selection was severity-driven rather than randomized, and because no technique was independently associated with outcome after adjustment, these data reflect within-indication performance rather than equivalence between techniques. The patient-level sensitivity analysis and the adjusted models were concordant with the principal finding, and no case of anti-elevation syndrome was observed during the 1-year follow-up. Within these constraints, the data are consistent with a stepwise, severity-based selection algorithm in centers with similar surgical preferences, although this requires prospective confirmation. The 1-year follow-up is insufficient to evaluate late recurrence, particularly after disinsertion and myectomy; the pooling of primary and secondary IOOA, the absence of systematic dissociated vertical deviation documentation, the omission of graded inferior oblique recession from the algorithm, and the confounding influence of concomitant horizontal muscle surgery on horizontal-deviation outcomes are the principal limitations. Prospective multicenter studies with longer follow-up, formal stratification by primary versus secondary IOOA, systematic dissociated vertical deviation assessment, and functional endpoints such as stereopsis, vertical fusional amplitude, and quantitative torsion are warranted to refine patient-specific surgical algorithms.
