Executive Summary
Lemnaceae species (commonly called duckweeds) are the world’s smallest aquatic flowering plants. They
have a much reduced morphology comprising leaf-like growing fronds, starch-filled resting fronds, and
simple roots. While they are sometimes regarded as invasive due to their ability to rapidly cover the
surface of freshwater ponds in the presence of nitrogen-rich agricultural runoff, duckweeds are native to
all continents but Antarctica, and their extreme growth rate is ideal for vast biomass production (Mateo-
Elizalde et al. 2023; Acosta et al. 2021). Lemnaceae in optimal conditions have an exponential growth
rate that can double the number of fronds in 30 hours and produce 64 grams of biomass per gram starting
weight in a week, which is far beyond that of terrestrial crops such as corn (2.3 g/g /week), and
unencumbered by secondary products such as lignin. Lemnaceae offer an attractive alternative to algae as
biofuel feedstocks because of their robust growth in open ponds and the relative ease of harvesting dry
material. Convenient metabolic labeling in culture makes Lemna a good system for pathway modeling
and engineering, as nutrients are taken up from liquid growth media, and non-responsive stomata can
utilize very high levels of atmospheric CO2.

With prior funding from DOE, the Long Island Biofuels Alliance laid the groundwork for harnessing the
potential of duckweed as a bioenergy crop including a survey of fatty acid and TAG composition across
30 Lemnaceae species, a preliminary constraint-based model of carbon flux, a reliable and rapid protocol
for stable transformation of Lemna japonica, and gene-knockdown by artificial miRNA. A patent
(US9957514B2) was issued for these last two advances in 2018. Building on these accomplishments,
support from this award enabled us to further expand critical genomic and epigenomic resources available
for the study and engineering of duckweeds (Ernst et al. 2025), and to complete design-build-test-learn
cycles that culminated in the successful engineering of high oil duckweeds. We shared our findings with
the public and scientific community in a series of peer-reviewed publications highlighted below, data
depositions in public repositories, and the lemna.org website. Critically, under the support of this award,
we successfully reengineered metabolism in duckweeds, dramatically increasing oil content by diverting a
substantial portion of accumulated carbon from starch (Liang et al. 2023). This sets the stage for the use
of fast growing aquatic duckweeds as robust, highly productive sources of biodiesel, with the capacity to
outperform soybean oil crops. The design principles and technologies we developed will have
applications in other energy crops.

Lemnaceae Genomics

At the time this award was issued in 2017, the Martienssen lab had produced draft genome assemblies of
Lemna gibba and Lemna japonica, but only one complete duckweed genome sequence had been
published - that of Spirodela polyrhiza, the giant duckweed. Subsequently, the Lam lab and collaborators
incorporated PacBio long-read sequencing, optical maps and DNA FISH to correct chromosome-level
misassemblies in the initial reference, and establishing the second reference genome for S. polyrhiza
(Hoang et al. 2018). With support from this award, we published the first long-read genome sequence of a
Wolffia species, along with a transcriptomic analysis revealing a reduction in time-of-day dependent gene
regulation in duckweeds (Michael et al. 2021). Wolffia species are the most morphologically reduced and
fastest growing members of Lemnaceae, and make the world’s smallest flowers. Broadening the scope of
duckweed genome references, the Martienssen lab and collaborators next produced eight chromosome-
resolved reference quality assemblies of W. australiana, L. gibba, L. minor, L. turionifera, and three

allotriploid hybrid L. japonica (L. minor x L. turionifera) clones, using Oxford Nanopore long reads and
Hi-C contact maps (Ernst et al. 2025). Comparisons of the chromosome-scale assemblies demonstrated a
high degree of synteny across all 21 chromosomes within the Lemna genus, with only a single
translocation consistently identified specifically in L. turionifera. W. australiana has 20 chromosomes
like S. polyrhiza, yet with significant architectural differences. In the Birchler Lab, antibodies against
centromeric histone H3 were raised for four species to directly determine centromere organization and
identify centromeric repeat sequences in Lemna species for the first time, revealing divergent centromere
architecture in L. minor and L. turionifera that persists in their hybrids. All single chromosome sets of the
sequenced species encode about 20,000 genes, significantly fewer than terrestrial monocots such as rice
and Brachypodium, and comparable to the algae Chlamydomonas reinhardtii. Methylome and small RNA
sequencing revealed dramatic differences between the three genera and in contrast to model species,
consistent with gene losses in known pathways of RNA directed DNA methylation. Analysis of missing
and diverged orthogroups across the Lemnaceae, 10 other monocots and 7 non-monocots uncovered
variations that likely account for these and other traits including: reduced morphology, aquatic habitat,
clonal reproduction, dormancy, high photosynthetic rate, and lipid production (Ernst et al. 2025). Several
studies have already utilized the novel genome references, which were made available before publication.
In one example supported by this award, the Lam lab and collaborators conducted a single-cell
transcriptomics study in Wolffia australiana, characterizing distinct tissue and cell types, and describing
cell-type specific variations in circadian gene expression (Denyer et al. 2024).

Follow-on experiments in the Lam lab confirmed that natural genetic variation in S. polyrhiza leads to
variable turion production. RNA-sequencing of two genotypes at the extremes of turion yield identified
turion-specific genes associated with dormancy, starch biosynthesis, and putative transcription factors that
may be involved in the developmental transition. In addition, turion-specific expression of genes involved
in lipid metabolism and oil biosynthesis were found in both S. polyrhiza as well as L. turionifera. In
collaboration with the Shanklin lab, a four- to six-fold increase in total TAG levels were found in turions
of both duckweed species, consistent with predictions from RNA-seq, providing novel leads to target
genes for directed modification of lipid content, and highlighting the potential to use dormant turions as
TAG storage end-points (Pasaribu et al. 2023).

Lemnaceae microbiomes

Additional genomic analysis focused on uncovering the composition and function of duckweed
microbiomes, suggesting they could be powerful models for plant-microbe interactions with the potential
to uncover microbial contributions to plant health, development, and nutrient use. The Lam lab optimized
existing molecular assays to detect and quantify duckweed-associated bacteria, developing strain-specific
primers and imaging methods that allowed us to quantify and visualize bacterial colonization at the root–
frond interface (Acosta et al. 2023). We determined that duckweeds host bacterial communities
taxonomically similar to terrestrial plant leaves, and identified the core microbiome taxa (Acosta et al.
2020). Among the constituents are endophytic auxin-producing bacteria that influence morphology and
colonization, which are candidates for growth promoting use in agriculture. Not all auxin producers affect
the plant equally; some alter root architecture, while others enhance colonization through specific host
pathways (Gilbert et al. 2018, 2022). In rice paddy ecosystems, we observed that both host species and

tissue type orchestrate microbiome structure, with duckweed and rice sharing a core set of bacterial taxa
that could be utilized for sustainable agriculture (Huang et al. 2020). Further, we demonstrated that
Wolffia species do not synthesize vitamin B12 endogenously as previously reported, but instead are
colonized by bacterial producers of this essential nutrient (Acosta et al. 2024).

Metabolic modeling

The Schwender, Shanklin and Martienssen labs conducted an integrated metabolic, transcriptomic, and
13
 C-metabolic flux analysis to investigate how Lemna gibba adapts its central metabolism in response to
different nitrogen sources (Shi et al. 2023). Fronds grown on glutamine exhibited a 1.4-fold increase in
growth rate, higher protein accumulation, and reduced cell wall biosynthesis compared to those grown on
nitrate. These physiological changes were accompanied by significant reorganization of carbon fluxes,
particularly around the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, which redirected metabolic intermediates toward
amino acid biosynthesis. Despite these pronounced metabolic shifts, transcriptomic analysis revealed
minimal changes in gene expression, indicating that regulation occurs primarily at the post-transcriptional
or enzymatic level. Additionally, both the Calvin-Benson-Bassham cycle and the oxidative pentose
phosphate pathway remained highly active under both conditions, forming a futile cycle that consumes
ATP. These findings underscore the importance of flux analysis in metabolic studies and establish L.
gibba as a robust model for quantitative systems biology in aquatic plants, as detailed in a commentary on
this study published in a separate technical report (Schwender and Shanklin 2024).

Optimizing Triacylglycerol Production

In previous work from the Shanklin, Schwender, and Martienssen labs, we expressed an Arabidopsis
WRINKLED1, (WRI1) the master transcriptional activator of fatty acid synthesis in Lemna japonica line
Lj8627. This resulted in <1% of DW of TAG along with large reduction in growth rate and significant
developmental abnormalities. Next, we constructed a CFP-N terminally tagged version of the Arabidopsis
WRI1 under the control of inducible estradiol inducible XVE promoter, co-expressed with a sesame
Oleosin 1 gene variant, in which its degradation signals had been minimized to optimize its TAG
protective function, along with a very strong mammalian DGAT2 to create Lj8627-33 (OWD)
transgenics. Growth of OWD transgenics cultured in the presence of 100uM estradiol for four days
resulted in the accumulation of 16.4% total fatty acid by DW compared to 5.2% in the parental line and
8.7% TAG per DW compared to 0.07 (124-fold increase) (Liang et al. 2023). Subsequent detailed
analyses confirmed strong TAG production and accumulation of very long chain fatty acids in TAG, but
revealed that estradiol induction of WRI1 in OWD lines results in an allocation tradeoff of starch to TAG
and a reduction in growth rate. Thus decoupling biomass growth and induction of TAG accumulation
would produce optimal yields in these strains. Because of their prolific vegetative growth, duckweeds
accumulating 10% TAG would outproduce soybean oil and approach the annual productivity of oil palm
on the same acreage (Liang et al. 2023).
