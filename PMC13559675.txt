Efficacy and safety of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone in stage IIIB–IV squamous non-small cell lung cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis of phase 3 randomized controlled trials
Authors: Chaolin Pan, Runqi Zhang, Yongshan Niu, Wenxiong Zhang, Maolan Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough programmed death-1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors plus chemotherapy (PC) has improved outcomes in advanced squamous non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), the consistency of survival benefit and the predictive value of PD-L1 expression remain uncertain, particularly following the publication of several recent pivotal phase 3 randomized controlled trials (RCTs). This study aimed to systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of PC versus chemotherapy alone in patients with stage IIIBIV squamous NSCLC and to assess the predictive value of PD-L1 expression.MethodsPubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and the Cochrane Library were systematically searched up to May 25, 2026. Phase 3 RCTs comparing PC with chemotherapy alone in patients with stage IIIB–IV squamous NSCLC were included. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane tool, and evidence certainty was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach. Hazard ratios (HRs) were pooled for time-to-event outcomes, and risk ratios (RRs) were calculated for binary outcomes using fixed- or random-effects models according to heterogeneity.ResultsFourteen phase 3 RCTs involving 4,481 patients (2,466 patients in the PC arm and 2,015 patients in the chemotherapy arm) were included. The certainty of evidences ranged from moderate to high. Patients receiving PC demonstrated significantly improved overall survival (OS) {HR: 0.73 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.67, 0.79], P<0.001} and progression-free survival (PFS) [HR: 0.54 (95% CI: 0.48, 0.60), P<0.001] compared with those treated with chemotherapy alone. OS benefits in the PC group were observed across a follow-up period of 6 to 60 months, while PFS improvement was evident from 6 to 24 months. Subgroup analysis indicated that patients with a PD-L1 expression ≥50% experienced superior survival outcomes with PC. Regarding tumor response, PC was associated with a longer duration of response [HR: 0.48 (95% CI: 0.41, 0.55), P<0.001] and a higher objective response rate [RR: 1.50 (95% CI: 1.40, 1.61), P<0.001]. Safety analysis revealed a higher incidence of total and grade 3–5 immune-related adverse events (irAEs). Clinically important adverse events (AEs) were also increased, including treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs)/treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) leading to discontinuation and TEAEs leading to death, highlighting the need for careful monitoring.ConclusionsPC improves survival and tumor response in stage IIIB–IV squamous NSCLC, particularly in patients with higher PD-L1 expression, but increases irAEs, warranting careful benefit-risk assessment and toxicity monitoring.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Programmed death-1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors plus chemotherapy significantly improved overall survival (OS), progression-free survival, duration of response, objective response rate, and disease control rate compared with chemotherapy alone in patients with stage IIIB–IV squamous non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). The greatest survival benefit was observed in patients with PD-L1 expression ≥50%, although immune-related adverse events (irAEs) were increased.

What is known and what is new?

• Chemo-immunotherapy is an established first-line strategy for advanced NSCLC, but its benefit-risk profile in squamous NSCLC remains heterogeneous across trials.

• This updated meta-analysis integrates 14 phase 3 randomized clinical trials and provides squamous-specific evidence, including landmark survival analyses and updated PD-L1 subgroup evaluation.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy should be considered an important first-line option for appropriate patients with advanced squamous NSCLC. Treatment decisions should incorporate PD-L1 expression, comorbidities, and toxicity risk, with careful monitoring for irAEs.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for ~85% of all lung cancers, with squamous histology representing 20–30% of NSCLC cases (1). For decades, treatment options for advanced squamous NSCLC were largely limited to platinum-based chemotherapy, which offers modest survival benefit at the cost of substantial toxicity (2). The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly programmed death-1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors, has reshaped the treatment landscape, and their combination with chemotherapy has become a promising first-line strategy (3,4).

Several phase 3 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have evaluated PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy (PC) in advanced squamous NSCLC, yet results have been inconsistent (5-18). For instance, KEYNOTE-407 showed significant overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) gains with pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy (14), whereas IMpower131 reported PFS benefit but no OS improvement for atezolizumab plus chemotherapy (13). These discrepancies underscore unresolved clinical uncertainties regarding the consistency of survival benefit across different PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and trial populations. Prior meta-analyses either included mixed NSCLC histologies without squamous-specific synthesis (19) or focused on non-squamous or PD-L1-negative subgroups (20), and they predate recent large-scale trials from both global and Chinese populations. Consequently, key questions remain unanswered: the robustness of OS benefit in squamous histology, the predictive value of PD-L1 expression, and efficacy specifically in PD-L1-negative disease.

To address these gaps, we conducted an updated meta-analysis of 14 phase 3 RCTs—including both global studies and recent Chinese trials—representing the largest and most geographically comprehensive dataset to date for stage IIIB–IV squamous NSCLC. Beyond pooled survival estimates, we provide landmark survival analyses and updated PD-L1 subgroup evaluations to offer clearer, clinically actionable guidance for first-line treatment selection. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1-0328/rc).

Methods

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and the Cochrane Library up to May 25, 2026. Both controlled vocabulary terms [e.g., Medical Subject Headings (MeSH)] and free-text keywords related to “PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors”, “Lung cancer”, and “Randomized” were used. Detailed search strategies for each database are provided in Table S1. Reference lists of relevant studies and review articles were manually screened to identify additional eligible trials. Clinical trial registry platforms were also reviewed to identify updated publications and ongoing studies.

Selection criteria

Studies were considered eligible according to these criteria: (I) phase 3 RCTs published in English; (II) included patients with stage IIIB–IV squamous NSCLC; (III) included subgroup analysis for squamous NSCLC or were entirely focused on advanced squamous NSCLC patients; (IV) compared the PC group with chemotherapy alone (chemotherapy group); (V) reported at least one outcome, such as OS, PFS, response rates, or adverse events (AEs).

The exclusion criteria included: (I) phase 1 or 2 trials; (II) studies lacking relevant survival or safety data; (III) trials offering only pooled data without subgroup analysis for squamous subtype; and (IV) duplicate articles or conference abstracts with incomplete data.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (C.P. and M.C.) separately gathered information from qualified studies using a uniform template. For multi-arm trials, only treatment arms fulfilling the pre-defined intervention/comparator criteria (i.e., PC versus chemotherapy alone) were extracted for analysis; other experimental arms were excluded. The extracted information included the first author, publication year, study title, trial phase, size of the squamous NSCLC subgroup, treatment arms, patient characteristics (e.g., age, gender), survival data, response rates [e.g., objective response rate (ORR)], and AEs. Time-point overall survival rate (OSR), progression-free survival rate (PFSR), and duration of response rate (DORR) data were extracted by digitizing Kaplan-Meier curves using Engauge Digitizer. Any discrepancies were settled through dialogue or by involving another evaluator if necessary.

Outcome assessments

OS and PFS were regarded as the primary endpoints, assessed using hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Subgroup analyses were conducted by stratifying variables such as age, sex, race, region, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS), smoking history, disease stage, PD-L1 expression, type of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, and chemotherapy regimen.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included RCTs was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool. Studies were judged as having low risk of bias, some concerns, or high risk of bias (21). Additionally, the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was employed to determine the strength of evidence, categorizing it from very low to high certainty (22).

Statistical analysis

Data synthesis was performed using STATA 17.0 and RevMan 5.4. For each trial, the most recent and complete report was prioritized to avoid double counting. HRs for OS, PFS, and DOR were preferentially extracted directly from published trial reports or supplementary materials. When HRs were not directly reported, survival data were estimated from Kaplan-Meier curves using Engauge Digitizer. PFS data were extracted according to the assessment method reported in each trial, including investigator-assessed or independently reviewed PFS when available. HRs were pooled for time-to-event outcomes, and risk ratios (RRs) were calculated for binary efficacy and safety outcomes. For AEs, absolute event rates were also summarized when data were available to improve clinical interpretation of toxicity burden. Landmark OSR, PFSR, and DORR were estimated from Kaplan-Meier curves at predefined time points. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic and Cochran’s Q test. A fixed-effect model was used when heterogeneity was low, whereas a random-effects model was applied when I2>50% or P<0.10, because this model better accounts for between-study variability. Prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed according to individual trials, study geography, PD-1 versus PD-L1 inhibitor class, chemotherapy backbone, PD-L1 expression, and risk-of-bias status. Meta-regression was considered but not performed because the limited number of studies for several outcomes and covariate categories could produce unstable estimates. Funnel plots were used to assess potential publication bias when sufficient studies were available. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Search results

A total of 32 articles from 14 phase 3 RCTs, involving 4,481 participants with advanced squamous NSCLC, were included in the meta-analysis (Table S2). Figure 1 shows the study selection process, following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. According to Cochrane RoB 2 tool, 7 RCTs were judged as having low risk of bias and 7 RCTs as having some concerns (Figure S1). The main reason for some concerns was that several trials enrolled broader NSCLC populations rather than exclusively squamous NSCLC patients; therefore, subgroup-specific randomization balance and outcome reporting for the squamous population could not be fully confirmed. According to the GRADE system, evidence certainty ranged from moderate to high (Table S3). Six trials were conducted in China (5,7,10,12,15,18), while eight were global multicenter (6,8,9,11,13,14,16,17). Seven studies focused specifically on squamous NSCLC (5-7,13-15,18), and the remaining seven studies included subgroup analyses of squamous NSCLC (8-12,16,17). Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses of OS and PFS showed that PC provided survival benefits across most baseline characteristics (see outcome assessments). PD-L1 expression ≥50% was a positive predictor for both OS and PFS in the PC group (Table 2). By examining the sources of heterogeneity across subgroups, we identified PD-L1 expression level as the primary contributor to heterogeneity in OS and PFS.

Responses

Compared to chemotherapy alone, the PC group showed higher ORR [RR: 1.50 (95% CI: 1.40, 1.61), P<0.001], disease control rate (DCR) [RR: 1.12 (95% CI: 1.03, 1.22], P=0.006), complete response (CR) [RR: 2.14 (95% CI: 1.40, 3.26), P=0.0004], and partial response (PR) [RR: 1.46 (95% CI: 1.35, 1.57), P<0.001]. The PC group had lower rates of stable disease (SD) [RR: 0.66 (95% CI: 0.55, 0.80), P<0.0001] and progressive disease (PD) [RR: 0.56 (95% CI: 0.45, 0.69), P<0.001] (Figure S4, Table 3).

Safety

The PC group showed higher rates of grade 3–5/serious treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), grade 3–5/serious treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs), and total/grade 3–5/serious immune-related adverse events (irAEs). Clinically important AEs, including TEAEs and TRAEs leading to treatment discontinuation or death, were also increased, while rates of irAEs leading to discontinuation or death were numerically higher but not statistically significant. No notable differences were observed in total TEAEs and TRAEs between the groups (Table 4, Figure S6).

In the PC arm, a higher incidence of any-grade alopecia, nausea, hypoalbuminaemia, increased aspartate aminotransferase, constipation, peripheral sensory neuropathy, pyrexia, arthralgia, rash, and hypothyroidism was observed (Table S4). Furthermore, grade 3–5 decreased appetite occurred more often in this group (Table S5).

For irAEs, the PC group showed increased rates of any-grade rash, hypothyroidism, hepatitis, pneumonitis, severe skin reactions, hyperthyroidism, infusion reactions, and colitis (Table S6). Additionally, grade 3–5 hepatitis, pneumonitis, colitis, severe skin reactions, and rash were more common in the PC arm (Table S7).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the pooled HRs/RRs for OS, PFS, DCR, and total TRAEs, reinforcing the reliability of the results. No single trial significantly influenced heterogeneity or the overall estimates, indicating that the results were not driven by any one study (Figure S7).

Publication bias

The funnel plots for OS, PFS, ORR, and total TEAEs were symmetrical, suggesting no substantial publication bias (Figure 6).

Discussion

Advanced squamous NSCLC presents unique therapeutic challenges, distinct from its non-squamous counterpart. Historically, treatment options for stage IIIB–IV squamous NSCLC have been limited, with platinum-based chemotherapy offering modest survival benefits and significant toxicity (41). The introduction of ICIs, especially PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, has transformed the therapeutic approach for NSCLC. Although PC improve outcomes in NSCLC, the benefit and risk of PC versus chemotherapy alone in advanced squamous NSCLC remain uncertain (42). This updated meta-analysis seeks to fill this gap by rigorously assessing the benefit and risk of PC versus chemotherapy alone in this population. Our comprehensive analysis demonstrated that PC significantly improved OS and PFS. Additionally, PC led to better DOR, ORR, and DCR. However, the PC group exhibited more total and grade 3–5 irAEs.

The survival benefits of PC in our study strongly support its therapeutic potential in advanced squamous NSCLC, which has historically been associated with poor prognosis and limited treatment options (43). Unlike non-squamous NSCLC, which often responds to targeted therapies, squamous NSCLC has a different molecular and genetic landscape (44). Tumors in this subtype often show higher tumor mutational burden (TMB), a greater neoantigen load, and a more inflamed tumor microenvironment, all of which have been linked to improved responses to immunotherapy (45). These biological characteristics make squamous NSCLC particularly responsive to ICIs, which can leverage these immune-inflammatory features to enhance the effectiveness of the treatment. The significant improvements in both OS (HR: 0.73) and PFS (HR: 0.54) observed in our meta-analysis are consistent with findings from previous studies (5,7,15). The biological foundation for these findings lies in the high PD-L1 expression commonly seen in squamous NSCLC, particularly in tumors associated with a history of smoking (46). This, combined with the immune cell infiltration observed in squamous tumors, provides a fertile ground for the efficacy of ICIs. Although high PD-L1 expression may enhance immunotherapy efficacy, survival benefits from PC were observed even in patients with low/negative PD-L1 expression (47). Clinically, this finding suggests that PD-L1 expression may help enrich patients most likely to derive larger benefit, but should not be used as the sole criterion to exclude patients with PD-L1-low or PD-L1-negative squamous NSCLC from chemo-immunotherapy (48). This implies that even in the absence of significant PD-L1 expression, combining chemotherapy with ICIs can overcome some of the resistance mechanisms to immunotherapy, which is especially relevant for squamous NSCLC (49). In fact, there is growing evidence that chemotherapy can upregulate PD-L1 expression and enhance the immunogenicity of tumors by inducing immunogenic cell death, which in turn activates the immune system. This phenomenon likely contributes to improving the efficacy of ICIs in squamous NSCLC, even in those with low baseline PD-L1 levels (50). Additionally, the long-term survival advantage observed in this analysis (sustained OS improvement from 6 to 60 months in the PC group) offers hope for patients with advanced squamous NSCLC, who have traditionally faced poor long-term survival rates. Chemotherapy not only helps to control tumor growth but also triggers immunological responses that are augmented by the addition of ICIs. These prolonged survival benefits could be attributed to the synergistic effect of chemotherapy and immunotherapy, where chemotherapy-induced tumor cell death presents a wider range of antigens for T-cell activation (51). This dual approach may significantly extend survival for patients who would otherwise have a limited prognosis. Heterogeneity in OS outcomes was noted among individual agents, with some (e.g., atezolizumab) showing significant PFS but non-significant OS benefit. This discrepancy may be explained by several trial-specific factors. The allowance of crossover to subsequent immunotherapy in the control arm can dilute OS differences. Variability in the efficacy and availability of post-progression therapies across studies also impacts final survival endpoints. Furthermore, differences in follow-up maturity may delay the translation of early PFS advantages into measurable OS separation, and some subgroup analyses may lack statistical power for OS. Therefore, while the pooled analysis confirms a class-level survival benefit for the PC combination, the OS outcome for any single agent should be interpreted in the context of its specific trial design and analytical circumstances. Although PD-1 inhibitor-based regimens appeared to show more consistent survival benefits than PD-L1 inhibitor-based regimens across included trials, this observation should be interpreted cautiously because indirect comparisons are limited by differences in trial design, patient characteristics, follow-up duration, crossover, and subsequent therapies (52). Nevertheless, this survival benefit must be balanced against the increased risk of severe toxicity, including TEAEs leading to treatment discontinuation or death. Optimizing the net clinical benefit therefore requires careful patient selection beyond predictive biomarkers and mandates vigilant monitoring for early signs of critical irAEs, such as pneumonitis. Proactive management is essential to mitigate severe outcomes and preserve the opportunity for long-term survival. Regarding chemotherapy backbone, our subgroup analyses did not identify sufficient evidence to establish one platinum-doublet regimen as clearly superior. Therefore, chemotherapy selection should continue to be individualized according to patient comorbidities, performance status, toxicity profile, and standard clinical practice.

The PC group also showed improved tumor response, with notably higher ORR and DCR. The better tumor response suggests that the combination treatment significantly improves the likelihood of achieving tumor shrinkage and disease stabilization compared to chemotherapy alone (5,6). These results align with other key trials, including KEYNOTE-407 and IMpower131, which showed improved ORR and DCR with pembrolizumab and atezolizumab combined with chemotherapy in advanced squamous NSCLC patients (13,14). Squamous NSCLC tumors are typically marked by increased immune cell infiltration, especially CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, and an inflammatory tumor microenvironment (53). These characteristics are believed to enhance sensitivity to immunotherapy. The presence of these immune cells may lead to a stronger immune response when immune checkpoints like PD-1/PD-L1 are blocked (54). Additionally, squamous NSCLC tumors are typically associated with a high TMB, which increases the likelihood of generating neoantigens-foreign proteins that the immune system can recognize as targets (45). The immune response to these neoantigens can be further amplified by chemotherapy, which induces immunogenic cell death and helps expose these antigens to the immune system. PC therapy utilizes several mechanisms to promote more sustained tumor responses. Chemotherapy enhances tumor vulnerability to immune attacks by promoting antigen release, while PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors block immune suppression, boosting T-cell activity (53). This combination therapy enhances both the initial tumor response and the durability of that response, as evidenced by the longer DOR observed in the PC group. These synergistic effects make PC a promising strategy for improving tumor control in squamous NSCLC.

Despite the substantial efficacy benefits, our meta-analysis also highlights the increased risk of irAEs associated with immunotherapy. The PC group exhibited higher rates of total and grade 3–5 irAEs compared to chemotherapy alone, with common irAEs including rash, hypothyroidism, pneumonitis, hepatitis, and colitis. These AEs are characteristic of ICIs, as they involve immune activation that can lead to immune-mediated damage to healthy tissues (55). While these irAEs can be severe, most are manageable with early recognition and appropriate intervention, including corticosteroids or immunosuppressive therapy (56). The risk of irAEs may be higher in squamous NSCLC patients, in part due to the inherent immune-inflammatory nature of these tumors. Squamous NSCLC is more frequently associated with a history of smoking, which can increase the overall immune activity within the tumor microenvironment (15). The increased immune activity in these patients may make them more susceptible to an intensified immune response when treated with immunotherapy, resulting in a higher frequency of irAEs (17). Additionally, squamous NSCLC tumors often present with central lesions and cavitation, which can increase the risk of pneumonitis, a potentially life-threatening complication. The development of pneumonitis has been linked to both the underlying tumor biology and the effects of immune modulation by ICIs, making close monitoring and early management essential (14). Consistent with these biological considerations, our pooled analysis demonstrated increased risks of both any-grade and grade 3–5 pneumonitis in the PC group. It is important to note that, in addition to irAEs, clinically significant AEs such as TEAEs and TRAEs leading to treatment discontinuation or death were also more frequent in the PC group, whereas irAEs leading to discontinuation or death were less common. Overall, although PC therapy is associated with higher rates of serious and clinically important AEs, the safety profile remains acceptable in the context of the substantial survival and tumor response benefits (5,6). Moreover, emerging data suggest that the development of irAEs may actually correlate with improved outcomes, possibly reflecting a strong immune response that leads to enhanced anti-tumor immunity. Studies have shown that patients who develop irAEs often have better responses to immunotherapy, making these AEs a potential biomarker for therapeutic success (57). Clinicians should be vigilant in monitoring for irAEs, and should manage these events promptly with the use of immunosuppressive agents when necessary (58). In this context, the benefits of PC far outweigh the risks for the majority of patients, provided that proper management strategies are in place. Tailored treatment approaches, including careful patient selection, early detection, and appropriate intervention for irAEs, will be key to optimizing the clinical benefit of this combination therapy.

The treatment landscape of advanced squamous NSCLC is continuing to evolve. Newer strategies, including dual immune-checkpoint blockade, anti-angiogenic combinations, antibody–drug conjugates, and bispecific agents, may further reshape first-line treatment selection (59). Although these approaches were beyond the scope of the present meta-analysis, our findings provide an important reference for the benefit–risk profile of conventional PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy regimens. Future trials and real-world studies will be needed to determine how these standard regimens should be positioned as newer immunotherapy-based combinations become available.

This meta-analysis has some limitations. First, differences in patient populations, treatment protocols, and study designs across the included studies could lead to heterogeneity. Second, subgroup analyses, including PD-L1 expression and smoking status, were limited by the availability of data. Third, variations in follow-up durations among studies could impact the evaluation of long-term outcomes. Fourth, the use of digitized Kaplan-Meier curves, while standard practice, introduces a potential for minor extraction error; however, our core conclusions remain robust as they are consistent with the directly reported HRs. Fifth, only published data were analyzed, and the absence of individual patient data may reduce the precision of the results. Lastly, because only English-language published RCTs were included in this study, potential language bias cannot be completely excluded. Additionally, real-world studies and prospective registries would be valuable for confirming these findings in broader, more diverse populations.

Conclusions

In summary, PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy improves survival and tumor response outcomes compared with chemotherapy alone in patients with stage IIIB–IV squamous NSCLC, despite an increased risk of immune-related toxicity. Although benefit was generally observed across most patient subgroups, those with PD-L1 expression ≥50% appeared to derive the greatest survival advantage, suggesting that treatment benefit is not uniform across all patients. Therefore, treatment decisions should be individualized according to biomarker status, clinical characteristics, comorbidities, and toxicity risk. Future research should focus on optimizing patient selection, refining biomarker interpretation, and improving toxicity management to maximize clinical benefit.
