Safety and efficacy of a feed additive consisting of phenylcapsaicin (aXiphen) for chickens for fattening (aXichem AB)
Authors: EFSA Panel on Additives and Products or Substances used in Animal Feed (FEEDAP), Roberto Edoardo Villa, Giovanna Azimonti, Eleftherios Bonos, Henrik Christensen, Mojca Durjava, Birgit Dusemund, Ronette Gehring, Boet Glandorf, Maryline Kouba, Marta López‐Alonso, Francesca Marcon, Giovanna Martelli, Carlo Nebbia, Alena Pechová, Miguel Prieto‐Maradona, Katerina Theodoridou, Georges Bories, Maria Bastos, Jurgen Gropp, Rosella Brozzi, Jaume Galobart, Fabiola Pizzo, Maria Vittoria Vettori, Alberto Navarro‐Villa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollowing a request from the European Commission, EFSA was asked to deliver a scientific opinion on the safety and efficacy of phenylcapsaicin (aXiphen) as zootechnical feed additive (other zootechnical) for chickens for fattening. The additive is already authorised as a novel food. The use of phenylcapsaicin in animal nutrition is considered safe for chickens for fattening and the consumers. The use of phenylcapsaicin as a feed additive was considered safe for the environment. The Panel concluded that the additive has the potential to reduce Salmonella load in poultry excreta and the environment when added to the feed for chickens for fattening at 15 mg/kg complete feed.Keywords: efficacy, phenylcapsaicin, safety, zootechnical additives

Data

The FEEDAP Panel used the data provided by the applicant together with data from other sources, such as previous risk assessments by EFSA or other expert bodies, peer‐reviewed scientific papers, other scientific reports and experts' (elicitation) knowledge, to deliver the present output.

Methodologies

The approach followed by the FEEDAP Panel to assess the safety and the efficacy of phenylcapsaicin (aXiphen®) is in line with the principles laid down in Regulation (EC) No 429/2008 5 and the relevant guidance documents: Guidance on the assessment of the safety of feed additives for the consumer (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017), Guidance on the assessment of the safety of feed additives for the environment (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2019) and Guidance on the assessment of the efficacy of feed additives (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2024).

ASSESSMENT

The feed additive consisting of phenylcapsaicin (aXiphen) is intended to be used as a zootechnical additive (functional group: other zootechnical additives; reduction of Salmonella) in feed for chickens for fattening (10–15 mg/kg complete feed).

The additive was characterised and its safety and efficacy evaluated in a previous opinion of the FEEDAP Panel (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2023). Regarding the safety, the Panel concluded that the additive is safe for chickens for fattening (up to 15 mg/kg complete feed) and is not genotoxic. However, the FEEDAP Panel could not conclude on the safety for the consumer. Regarding the safety of the additive for the environment, the FEEDAP Panel could not conclude on the safety of phenylcapsaicin for the groundwater, the terrestrial and aquatic compartments. Finally, the FEEDAP Panel could not conclude on the efficacy of the additive in chickens for fattening at the proposed conditions of use.

In the current submission, the applicant requested a change in the functional group of the additive from physiological condition stabilisers to other zootechnical additives (reduction of Salmonella) 6 and provided additional data to address the data gaps on the safety for the consumer and environment and the efficacy.

Safety

Safety for the consumer

In its previous assessment (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2023), the Panel noted that, although the metabolic profile of phenylcapsaicin had been sufficiently characterised in rats, the available data did not demonstrate metabolic similarity between laboratory animals and the target species. As a result, the marker residue in target animals could not be identified. Without this information, consumer exposure to residues could not be estimated and, consequently, the Panel was unable to conclude on consumer safety. For the current assessment, the applicant provided an in vitro study to demonstrate the metabolic similarity between the target species and the laboratory animals and a residue study to allow the calculation of the consumer exposure.

Absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion

The applicant provided a study conducted with cryopreserved hepatocytes from male Sprague Dawley rat and male yellow feathered chicken incubated with [14C]phenylcapsaicin as the substrate (the radiolabel on the [14C]phenylcapsaicin was positioned on the phenyl moiety). 7 The identification of the [14C]phenylcapsaicin metabolites (120 min incubation at 37°C) was accomplished with LC–RAM and liquid chromatography–high‐resolution mass spectroscopy (HRMS).

Two main metabolites were detected: M1 (MW = 513.54; RT 27.27 min) and M2 (MW = 174.2; RT 29.95), that were tentatively identified as a glucuronide (M1) and a dealkylated (M2) derivative, respectively. The FEEDAP Panel notes that the glucuronidation metabolite (M1) identified in this study in both rats and chickens hepatocytes was also identified as a metabolite in the in vivo rat study reported in the NDA Panel opinion in its assessment of phenylcapsaicin as novel food (EFSA NDA Panel, 2019). However, the FEEDAP Panel also notes that the applicant did not justify the site of glucuronidation for metabolite M1 or clarify the origin of the proposed dealkylated metabolite M2.

The FEEDAP Panel considers that the study presents several limitations that are described below.

The test system used by the applicant (i.e. cryopreserved hepatocytes) is not the one recommended in the Guidance on the assessment of the safety of feed additives for the consumer (i.e. freshly isolated hepatocytes) (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017). It is noted that cryopreserved hepatocytes are reported to express a lower metabolic competence (Griffin & Houston, 2004) although, a comparable metabolic performance between cryopreserved hepatocytes and freshly isolated rat and human hepatocytes for a range of drugs is also reported in the literature (McGinnity et al., 2004; Naritomi et al., 2003).

The in vitro study was able to detect two metabolites only, contrary to several (27) reported in the literature (Feng et al., 2021). Therefore, the FEEDAP Panel considers that the limited detection of metabolites in the study indicates the poor metabolic capacity of the test system (i.e. cryopreserved cells) and is in line with the low rate of the in vitro metabolism of a standard substrate (7‐ethoxycoumarin).

The radiolabelling was limited to the phenyl group and therefore no information would be available in case of the hydrolysis of the amide bond. This hydrolysis has been shown to occur with the parent compound capsaicin, with the appearance of vanillin type of metabolites. The applicant justified that choice stating that the vanillylamine moiety is common to capsaicin and that its metabolic fate is already well established.

Finally, based on the outcome of the study, the only common metabolite between rat and chicken is the glucuronide(s). The applicant stated that metabolite M1 with a MW of 514 g/mol would correspond to the mono‐glucuronidation of the 4‐hydroxy group of the vanillylamine part of the molecule. Considering the reported hydroxylation of the similar vanillylamine portion of capsaicin at C5 but also the O‐demethylation of its methoxy group generating a hydroxyl group at C3 (Surh et al., 1995), the glucuronidation position on phenylcapsaicin should have been justified. Although it can be assumed that the glucuronidation position(s) not identified in the study would occur at the same positions in phenylcapsaicin, no evidence was made available.

Overall, the FEEDAP Panel considers that the dominant metabolic pathway for phenylcapsaicin in both laboratory animals (e.g. rats) and chickens is O‐glucuronidation. This is in line with the conclusions on adsorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME) in humans, reached by the NDA Panel in its assessment of phenylcapsaicin as novel food (EFSA NDA Panel, 2019). However, based on the data available, the potential formation of metabolites other than glucuronides cannot be ruled out. Therefore, the FEEDAP Panel concludes that based on the data available, the metabolic similarity between the target species and laboratory animals is not fully demonstrated.

Residue studies

The applicant submitted data on potential presence of residues of phenylcapsaicin and O‐glucuronidated phenylcapsaicin (glu‐phenylcapsaicin) in liver, kidney, muscle and skin/fat samples from six chickens fed the additive at 15 mg/kg feed for 35 days without a withdrawal period, as well as from one untreated control animal. 8 Residues of phenylcapsaicin and glu‐phenylcapsaicin in animal tissues were determined by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (extraction by acetonitrile).

Phenylcapsaicin deposition was low in muscle with 5/6 below the limit of detection (LOD; 0.2 ng/g). Nevertheless, the highest phenylcapsaicin concentrations were observed in skin/fat, suggesting rapid uptake into this tissue and slow metabolism. Glu‐phenylcapsaicin is rarely found in muscle tissue and in skin/fat (3/6 samples below LOD). The FEEDAP Panel notes that the liver sample from the single control animal contained glu‐phenylcapsaicin at 1.7 ng/g as possible contamination during tissue dissection or homogenisation. Since control samples should not contain analytes, the Panel considers this finding a limitation to the reliability of the study.

The FEEDAP Panel notes that no metabolites other than glu‐phenylcapsaicin derived from the additive under assessment were evaluated for possible deposition in animal tissues and organs. The residue data is listed in Table 2.

Assessment of consumer exposure and consumer safety

The toxicological profile of phenylcapsaicin was evaluated in the previous assessment performed by the FEEDAP Panel, which concluded that the substance raises no genotoxicity concern. The reference point identified for phenylcapsaicin was 37.2 mg/kg bw per day, derived from a 90‐day repeated‐dose oral toxicity study in rats as the lowest model‐averaged BMDL20 for increased plasma alanine aminotransferase (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2023).

Applying an uncertainty factor of 100 (to account for inter‐ and intra‐species variability) to the reference point identified of 37.2 mg/kg bw per day in rats, the FEEDAP Panel established a health‐based guidance value (HBGV) of 0.372 mg/kg bw per day.

In the current assessment, the FEEDAP Panel performed an exposure assessment following the methodology described in the Guidance on consumer safety (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017).

Residue data for phenylcapsaicin and its metabolite glucuronidated phenylcapsaicin, were obtained from a residue study in chickens for fattening fed the additive (Section 3.1.1.2). Glucuronidated compounds are generally assumed to be less readily absorbed as such in the gut but can release extensively the original compounds after caecal microbiota hydrolysis. In the absence of specific data, the Panel considered the worst‐case scenario where all glucuronidated residues would release phenylcapsaicin. For the exposure assessment, the Panel considered phenylcapsaicin as the marker residue and calculated the input data as:

–the sum of phenylcapsaicin and of the phenylcapsaicin moiety calculated from the glu‐phenylcapsaicin (the phenylcapsaicin moiety was calculated considering its contribution of 65.56% of the molecular weight of the glu‐phenylcapsaicin)

the sum of phenylcapsaicin and of the phenylcapsaicin moiety calculated from the glu‐phenylcapsaicin (the phenylcapsaicin moiety was calculated considering its contribution of 65.56% of the molecular weight of the glu‐phenylcapsaicin)

Overall discussion on the dataset available

Phenylcapsaicin raises no genotoxicity concern, and the reference point identified is 37.2 mg/kg bw per day, which derives from a 90‐day repeated‐dose oral toxicity study in rats as the lowest model‐averaged BMDL20 for increased plasma alanine aminotransferase.

Based on the information available, the potential formation of metabolites, other than glucuronides, cannot be ruled out. Therefore, the FEEDAP Panel concludes that the metabolic similarity between the target species and laboratory animals is not fully demonstrated.

Despite the remarkable limitations of both the metabolic and the residue studies, the FEEDAP Panel notes that the consumer exposure to phenylcapsaicin from chickens for fattening fed the additive under assessment is far below HBGV identified and at least one order or magnitude below the level considered safe by the NDA Panel for its use as a novel food.

Conclusions on safety for the consumer

The FEEDAP Panel concludes that the use of phenylcapsaicin under the proposed conditions of use is considered safe for the consumer.

Safety for the environment

The additive is intended to be used in chickens for fattening (10–15 mg phenylcapsaicin/kg complete feed). The environmental risk assessment has been performed assuming that all the phenylcapsaicin ingested (at the maximum dose of 15 mg/kg in complete feed) is excreted as such in the environment.

The opinion adopted by the FEEDAP Panel in 2023 states that in the absence of appropriate data, the FEEDAP Panel cannot conclude on the safety of phenylcapsaicin for the environment. The log Kow of phenylcapsaicin is less than 3, indicating that the substance is unlikely to bioaccumulate and the risk of secondary poisoning is considered low (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2023).

The environmental risk assessment on the additive has been updated considering the new information submitted.

Ecotoxicity studies

Toxicity to terrestrial compartment

Effects on plants

In the terrestrial plant growth study, 14 six different concentrations of phenylcapsaicin were tested in soil for six plant species, each belonging to a different plant family: Brassica napus, Glycine max, Cucumis sativus, Solanum lycopersicum, Lolium perenne and Allium cepa.

In the study, phenylcapsaicin was mixed with fine quartz sand and incorporated into ■■■■■ soil at nominal concentrations of 8.23, 24.7, 74.1, 222, 667 and 2000 mg/kg dry soil. The test item was initially dissolved in acetone, applied to quartz sand and, after solvent evaporation, the treated sand was thoroughly mixed with the soil. Two control groups were included: (i) a solvent control containing quartz sand treated with acetone only, at levels equivalent to those used in the treated groups; and (ii) an untreated control with clean quartz sand incorporated into the soil. For each species, at least 20 seeds per treatment were used. Plants were exposed for 14 or 21 days following 50% seedling emergence in the respective controls, with observations recorded on Days 7, 14 and, where applicable, 21. The study was considered reliable, as validity criteria were met.

No effects on emergence or mortality were observed. Phytotoxic effects were limited, consisting of chlorosis in C. sativus at 2000 mg/kg soil, while other effects were sporadic and without a clear dose–response relationship. EC50 values could not be calculated for all plants and effects, most of the EC50 values are higher than the highest concentration tested (> 2000 mg/kg), while the lowest EC50 was calculated for fresh weight in B. napus and was 600 mg/kg. The lowest endpoint was EC10 value of 7.47 mg/kg soil for plant height in C. sativus and this value will be used for the risk characterisation.

Effects on earthworms

An earthworm (Eisenia foetida) acute toxicity test was conducted according to OECD Guideline 207. 15 The concentration levels of phenylcapsaicin were 1.0, 10, 30, 100, 300 and 1000 mg/kg dry soil. A blank control and the solvent control were also tested. Four replicates of ten individuals were included with each test concentration and controls. After 14 days of exposure, no mortality was observed either in controls or in any of the treatment groups. Consequently, the acute toxicity of phenylcapsaicin to the earthworm (E. foetida) corresponds to a LC50(14d) > 1000 mg/kg dry soil.

Effects on soil microorganisms

The effect of phenylcapsaicin on soil microorganisms was evaluated in a study conducted in line with OECD Guideline 2016. 16 Three concentrations of phenylcapsaicin were tested in triplicate, for 28 days and soil nitrogen production and adverse effects related to ammonium, nitrite and nitrate content in treatment groups were compared to the control soil. Phenylcapsaicin was applied to quartz sand using acetone as a carrier, followed by solvent evaporation and subsequently incorporated into lucerne‐enriched soil at concentrations of 28, 280 and 2300 μg/kg dry soil. An acetone‐treated quartz sand control was included. The study design, including the use of a sodium chloride reference item in a separate GLP‐compliant test, was appropriate. The study was well conducted, and the results were considered reliable and suitable for evaluation. On day 28, the differences in nitrogen, ammonium, nitrate and nitrite contents in treated groups and the control were far less than 25%. Overall, the 28‐day study indicates that phenylcapsaicin at concentrations up to 2300 μg/kg soil (dry weight) does not adversely affect microbial nitrogen transformation or soil nitrate content.

Toxicity to aquatic organisms

All the studies presented in the current dossier on the toxicity of phenylcapsaicin to aquatic organisms (green algae, 17 crustaceans, 18 and fish 19 ) were already evaluated in the previous FEEDAP opinion (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2023). In that assessment the FEEDAP Panel noted that some of the concentrations tested considerably exceeded the phenylcapsaicin water solubility reported earlier. Therefore, the acceptability of the studies was considered questionable. For the present evaluation the Panel re‐assessed the above mentioned studies considering the new water solubility value and new information provided and concluded that they can be used for the present assessment as follow: 72‐h ErC50 value for the green algae P. subcapitata being 4.53 mg/L, 48h‐EC50 value for D. magna being 12.3 mg/L and LC50 value for fish being >17.7 mg/L. For sediment‐dwelling organisms the equilibrium partitioning approach was considered.

Bioaccumulation and risk of secondary poisoning

No data on bioaccumulation have been submitted. Since the log Kow of phenylcapsaicin is below 3, the substance is unlikely to bioaccumulate and the risk of secondary poisoning is considered low.

Conclusions on safety for the environment

No concern for the environment is expected when the additive is used according to the proposed condition of use.

Efficacy

Efficacy for chickens for fattening

In the previous opinion (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2023), in the absence of positive effects on the performance or other parameters relevant to the improvement of the physiological condition of the birds, the FEEDAP Panel could not conclude on the efficacy of phenylcapsaicin as a zootechnical additive, functional group physiological condition stabilisers, in chickens for fattening.

In the current submission the applicant changed the intended functional group of the additive to ‘other zootechnical additives’ with the claim to ‘reduce Salmonella load in chicken and pen environment’. 20 To support the efficacy of the additive, the applicant submitted four new studies in chickens for fattening evaluating the zootechnical performance of the birds and Salmonella prevalence. 21 The four studies shared a similar experimental design ■■■■■ ■■■■■ ■■■■■

■■■■■

■■■■■

In study 2 no significant differences between IT and IUC Salmonella counts were observed in excreta or boot swabs at the end of the study, therefore, this study does not support the efficacy of the additive (Table 16). The results of Study 3 showed a statistically significant reduction in Salmonella counts in the IT group compared with the IUC group in both excreta and boot swab samples at day 35. In Study 4, a statistically significant reduction in Salmonella counts, either in excreta or boot swaps, was observed at all sampling time points (days 14, 26 and 35). In study 1, a statistically significant reduction in Salmonella counts in boot swabs in IT relative to IUC was observed on day 35. The Panel notes the unexpected early high Salmonella levels in the UUC group in this study, both in excreta and boot swabs, and acknowledged the decline of Salmonella counts over time in all groups. However, it is reasonable to assume that the initial high UUC Salmonella counts are due to hatchery background contamination. Therefore, the Panel considers that this initial background contamination did not invalidate the results of the study since the difference in Salmonella counts among groups disappears on day 14 (probably due to gut microbiota maturation/immunity development) and the potential of the additive to reduce Salmonella counts at the end of the trial was shown in boot swabs (day 35).

Conclusions on efficacy

Overall, it can be concluded that the additive has the potential to reduce Salmonella load in poultry excreta and the environment when added to feed at 15 mg of phenylcapsaicin/kg complete feed.

CONCLUSIONS

The FEEDAP Panel concludes that the use of phenylcapsaicin under the proposed conditions of use is considered safe for the consumer.

It is concluded that no concern for the environment is expected when the additive is used according to the proposed condition of use.

The additive, when added to feed at 15 mg of phenylcapsaicin/kg complete feed, has the potential to reduce Salmonella load in poultry excreta and the environment.
