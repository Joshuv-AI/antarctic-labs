Use of a 3D-printed smartphone microscope adapter in a general biology lab course
Authors: Erik R. Hill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHigh school and undergraduate students often use personal cell phones to document experimental data or to capture images of microscopic observations on their phones. We utilized a 3D-printed smartphone mount adapter for microscopes in a predominantly first-year undergraduate general biology classroom. The hands-free mount is low-cost, works with different brands of phones, different kinds of microscopes, and does not damage the phone or microscope. At the start of the semester, a 10-question pre-use survey was conducted using a 4-choice Likert scale (n = 330). 73% of respondents strongly agreed with wanting to use their personal cell phone to record laboratory experiment data. Over a semester, students used 3D-printed cell phone microscope adapters as a part of four different general biology lab modules. As examples, the adapters were used to take stable microscopic images of pond water, Gram stain results, and live Daphnia pulex organisms to measure heart rate. At the end of the course, students were asked to participate in a post-use survey (n = 186). Students believe the adapter helped them engage with lab content. The adapter was most often used to visualize microscopic items live and simultaneously with their lab partner. The survey data show that use of a 3D-printed device to hold phones steady on a microscope was viewed positively by students.

INTRODUCTION

When students are positively engaged in a class laboratory, it can foster collaborative efforts, immerse students in scientific inquiry, and increase aspiration for a career in science (1). Novel modifications to laboratory protocols that increase accessibility of the laboratory experience have improved student learning outcomes (2). Activities that increase student ownership increase student engagement in the educational content (3, 4). Increasing student ownership in classroom laboratory experiments improves information retention and overall student classroom experiences (5, 6).

To make a laboratory more engaging, we sought to look at what students are already engaged with. Almost every American college student owns a smartphone (97% of 18–30-year-olds; Pew Research Data, 30 November 2025). Examples of smartphones adapted into microscopes in K-12 and undergraduate laboratory settings are becoming more common (7–9). High school and undergraduate students can use personal cell phones to document experimental data or to capture images during a laboratory (10).

Bringing new devices into a classroom may be difficult for students to use, could be expensive to maintain, or could quickly become obsolete (11). No manufacturer standard for smartphone specifications exists (e.g., phone size, camera layout/position, or number of cameras) (12). Therefore, our goal was to incorporate a microscope mount design that was functional for major smartphone brands/devices and would work with different microscopes. To this end, we wanted to determine how students in a first-year undergraduate general biology course felt about a 3D-printed smartphone microscope adapter.

PROCEDURE

Biosafety

Enrolled students were shown a biosafety training video on the first day as a part of lab safety training. All safety protocols were enforced by faculty or graduate teaching staff for the duration of the course, including when working with microbial strains. For labs involving microbial strains, students utilized aseptic techniques and 70% ethanol sterilization as a part of bench cleanup.

Intended audience and instructor preparation

This laboratory device was designed for college-level students and utilized in a 1000-level cell biology course for first-year STEM major students. A 1-h weekly lab prep period was needed for setting up the lab including distributing the adapters on the microscopes in the classroom. Students were available to use the adapters or remove them at any point in their 2.5 h lab session.

3D printing and assembly procedure

The adapter was found on Thingiverse (https://www.thingiverse.com/thing:3426135). The device was expanded to 125% of scale using Fusion 360 software v.2.0.19941 (Autodesk). Adapters were printed in polylactic acid (PLA) filament using a Dremel 3D40 printer. One device took approximately 6 h to print. Assembly also included two 5 mm hex nuts and two 5 mm M8 bolts to attach the adjustable phone seat. The eyepiece adapter is secured with two 6-inch zip ties to hold the top and bottom pieces together.

Implementation of experiments in the undergraduate laboratory

Working pairs of lab students were provided with smartphone microscope adapters in four weekly 2.5 h labs. These labs involved the use of either compound or dissecting microscopes. Labs and sample experiments were as follows (including observations made): Lab 2: Microscopy (observing type font and fixed slides, simple and Gram-stained organisms); Lab 3: Protists (fixed slides, live pond water samples, Paramecium digestion); Lab 4: Plants (fixed slides of plant gonads and stained onion cells); Lab 10: Daphnia cardiovascular system (live Daphnia pulex organism observations) (Carolina Biological Co).

Survey instrument and administration

A 10-question survey was developed with a four-choice Likert scale valued from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree) and was used for most questions. Survey questions included students’ perceptions of laboratory courses, use of 3D-printed materials, and contribution toward learning gains. Values were examined in a pre-semester/post-semester fashion. No demographic questions were asked. Responses were anonymous and not linked to any respondent. Pre- and post-responses were not linked. Pre-survey was delivered (via Qualtrics) following a 5 min introduction to the 3D-printed adapter delivered by faculty. Post-survey was delivered (via Qualtrics) during the last weekly meeting of the course. Students voluntarily participated in the online Qualtrics surveys, and student responses were anonymous.

Data analysis

Mean and standard deviation of survey responses were used to determine if students’ perceptions of 3D-printed adapter contributions toward learning gains changed over the semester. Survey data were tabulated and calculated with Excel 365 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA).

CONCLUSIONS

Class instructors had previously observed students attempting to capture microscope images on their personal phones. Students held or hovered their phones on or over the ocular lens (Fig. 1A). This hold/hover method was unstable and tedious. Students might only gain one “good” image and spend a long time trying to get the perfect photo. Some students moved the phone against the eyepiece and could damage the microscopes. A removable phone mount would complement the student’s academic phone use and protect the microscopes.

Smartphones used in undergraduate laboratory. Sample images show how a student could use a smartphone to observe Daphnia pulex (Carolina Biological Co.) in a laboratory setting by either a two-handed hold-and-hover method to take images (A) or hands-free with the use of a 3D-printed smartphone holder with an iPhone 13 (B) or iPhone 11 (C) on a dissecting microscope (AmScope SE305) as part of a general biology laboratory class.

Commercially available microscope mounts for smartphones often only work on one brand or one specific model of smartphone. We wanted a phone mount for most phone brands/devices and one that could work on compound and dissecting microscopes. A 3D-printed mount could be scalable, modifiable, and low-cost. We printed and deployed about 40 smartphone mounts (https://www.thingiverse.com/thing:3426135) in a predominantly first-year undergraduate general biology laboratory (Fig. 1B and C). This smartphone mount design accepts a wide range of smartphone sizes and brands and also fits on many compound or dissecting microscopes that utilize a 24 mm –33 mm diameter eyepiece. The device is adjustable, easy to use, quickly removable, and does not damage the microscope.

In the first week of a majors general biology course, faculty gave a 5 min demonstration of how to use a 3D-printed smartphone adapter. The course had 386 enrolled students, predominantly first-year students (94%). A 10-question pre-use survey was conducted using a 4-choice Likert scale (n = 330) (Table S1). A majority of students had not used a 3D-printed item before. Respondents strongly agreed with wanting to use their phone to record laboratory data, pictures, or video. Most respondents anticipated that images recorded with 3D-printed mounts could be helpful to study laboratory content before graded assessments.

Next, working pairs of lab students were provided with adapters as a part of 4 weekly labs that involved observations through compound or dissecting microscopes. Students could use the 3D-printed cell phone microscope adapters to help stabilize recording images or videos of their observations. Recording images was not part of the lab assessment.

In the last meeting of the course, students were asked to participate in a post-use survey on the 3D-printed adapters. A 10-question follow-up survey (Qualtrics) and 186 individual responses were recorded (Table S2). Pre- and post-use surveys were not linked between individual respondents. To the question “Do you believe the smartphone adapter helped you engage with your lab work?” 56% of respondents selected strongly agree, and 35% of respondents selected agree. Respondents indicated that their main use of the adapters was so lab partners could simultaneously observe what was under the microscope. This could increase collaborative efforts for the lab pairs. Respondent answers agreed on the best and worst labs to use the adapters. The most-often reported ‘“best” lab to use the adapter, the protist lab, was also the least-reported “worst” lab and vice-versa for the plant lab. Respondents also were overwhelmingly interested in using these adapters in advanced biology courses, including microbiology or cell biology. Only 12% of respondents reported any difficulty in using the smartphone adapter. These response data suggest that the adapters had a positive impact on the students’ experience with laboratory course content (Tables S3 and S4).

Technology or equipment issues can disrupt lessons and cause frustration among both teachers and students. We chose a design for the smartphone adapter that would function with many major brands/phones. However, the use of adapters was limited by the diameter of microscope eyepieces. Adapters were unable to fit around the 38 mm eyepiece on Leica DM500 microscopes. Camera defects could also impact the value of smartphone data collected (13). Additionally, because a laboratory course combines theory and practice, novice students are likely to make mistakes in laboratory course procedures (14). Being able to use a shared adapter could mitigate some of these concerns. Student partners can share images afterward of lab objects that they saw together through the microscope. This would generate equity of technology use between students within the classroom. Providing equity during in-class technology experiences is an imperative for college education (15).

Overall response to the printed adapters was positive. Survey results showed high scores on ease of use and very few students reported any difficulty with the adapters. Students also reported wanting to use the devices again in upper-level classes. Students believe the adapter helped them engage with lab content. Adapters were most often used to visualize microscopic items simultaneously. The adapters helped raise students’ awareness of what 3D printing can accomplish. This work shows that a 3D-printed phone mount for microscopes could successfully be utilized in an undergraduate classroom.
