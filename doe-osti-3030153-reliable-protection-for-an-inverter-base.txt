 Executive Summary
The key project objectives are as follows:
 (1) Assess gaps in commonly used short circuit simulation software and outline a future
 development roadmap to communicate to software vendors.
 (2) Develop a time domain electrical-mechanical transient (EMT) co-simulation framework
 to analyze system fault signatures in high inverter-based resource (IBR) scenarios as well
 as interactions between system protection and IBR protection.
 (3) Develop a stability analysis software tool that can assess stability throughout a fault using
 power system protection and IBR control logic and calculate critical fault clearing time
 and stability margin under various IBR penetration levels.
 (4) Assess the impact of high IBR penetration on transmission protection systems and
 communicate effective IBR study tools and operational impacts to utilities’ engineering
 support and operations groups.
 (5) Develop and demonstrate new and practical protection schemes that enable safe and
 reliable grid operation with up to 100% IBR generation.
 (6) Develop a commercialization and implementation roadmap for developed protection
 technologies.

Over the reporting period, the project successfully progressed from initiation to advanced
laboratory and field demonstrations, delivering key technical milestones in electromagnetic
transient (EMT) co-simulation, inverter-based resource (IBR) modeling, and protection scheme
development. The team established a robust EMT co-simulation framework, completed
comprehensive short-circuit software gap assessments, and developed high-fidelity EMT models
for a representative Con Edison system section that met or exceeded all defined performance
metrics.

Significant advances include the acquisition and integration of authentic IBR manufacturer
models, development of real-time co-simulation interfaces (RTDS-PSCAD and RTDS-GTSOC),
and completion of convergent EMT simulations at 25% and 50% IBR penetration levels—
meeting key go/no-go criteria. Findings from the short-circuit software gap assessment directly
influenced industry tools, prompting patches and beta releases from ASPEN and CAPE, and
were formally documented in a Sandia report.

The project also strengthened industry collaboration through executed subcontracts, NDAs,
technical workshops, and presentations at major industry forums and conferences. Three in-
person utility lab/field demonstrations planning workshops were completed across Eastern and
Western Interconnections, engaging protection, planning, operations, and IT stakeholders.

Overall, the project materially advances protection modeling and simulation capabilities needed
to maintain reliable grid protection under high IBR penetration. The results build utility
confidence in operating power systems safely and reliably across a wide range of generation
mixes, supporting grid modernization goals.

 Background
The rapid growth of inverter-based resources (IBR) including utility-scale solar, wind, and
battery energy storage—has fundamentally altered bulk power system fault behavior, system
strength, and protection performance. Traditional transmission protection practices and short-
circuit analysis tools were developed assuming synchronous generator dominance and are
increasingly inadequate under high and near-100% IBR penetration. Recent industry and
academic work has therefore focused on three complementary areas: (1) enhanced EMT-based
simulation of inverter fault behavior, (2) assessment of protection misoperations under low and
fast-changing fault currents, and (3) development of wide-area and adaptive protection schemes
leveraging synchrophasor data.

Published research has demonstrated the value of EMT tools (e.g., PSCAD, RTDS, Typhoon) for
accurately capturing IBR fault response and control interactions, particularly under weak-grid
conditions and low inertia scenarios [1], [7], [8]. Parallel efforts have explored co-simulation and
model partitioning approaches to accelerate large-scale EMT studies and reduce computational
burden [20], [21]. In protection research, multiple studies have highlighted degradation in
distance, directional, and overcurrent elements due to reduced negative-sequence current and
altered fault signatures [1], [7]–[11]. Separately, synchrophasor-based wide-area monitoring and
protection concepts have matured over the past decade, with demonstrated applications in
backup protection, falling conductor protection, and system-wide situational awareness [12]–
[19].

Relationship to Published Work
1) Similarities to Prior Work
This project builds directly on established research demonstrating:
  The necessity of EMT simulation to accurately represent inverter fault response and
       control dynamics [1], [7], [8].
  The feasibility and value of hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) testing to evaluate relay behavior
 under high IBR penetration [1].
  The effectiveness of synchrophasor-based wide-area protection concepts for sensitive,
 fast, and coordinated backup protection [12]–[19].
  Prior demonstrations of PMU-based protection functions, including current-differential
 and falling conductor protection, on real utility systems [15]–[17].
2) Key Differentiators of This Project
Unlike much of the published work, which typically addresses either transmission protection or
IBR plant behavior in isolation, this project explicitly targets the interaction and coordination
between transmission system protection and IBR protection/control across a full range of
IBR penetration levels. Key technical differentiators include:
  A multi-platform EMT co-simulation framework (RTDS, PSCAD, Typhoon)
 integrated with stability analysis tools, enabling faster-than-real-time studies while
 maintaining high fidelity.
  A systematic, penetration-dependent assessment of fault signatures and protection
 performance, rather than assuming fixed or purely 100% IBR scenarios.
  Direct gap assessment of industry-standard short-circuit tools (ASPEN, CAPE), with
 findings communicated back to software vendors to influence commercial tool
 development.

 Page 5 of 21

  Development of a vendor-agnostic synchrophasor-based Wide-Area System
 Protection (WASP) scheme designed to operate without complex settings, remain
 coordinated across zones, and function reliably up to 100% IBR penetration.
  Commitment to lab and field demonstrations across diverse utility systems (urban
 meshed underground networks and rural radial overhead systems), advancing technology
 readiness beyond software-only validation.

3) Leveraging Prior Work to Accelerate and Improve the Project
The project fully leverages prior research, tools, and demonstrations to accelerate delivery and
improve quality. Proven RTDS–PSCAD and RTDS–Typhoon co-simulation architectures are
extended rather than reinvented, reducing development risk and enabling immediate focus on
large-scale, high-IBR scenarios [1], [20], [21]. Commercially available stability analysis tools
from V&R are integrated to avoid duplicative algorithm development while enhancing fault-
through stability assessment [2]–[6]. Established PMU-based protection concepts and prior
SDG&E demonstrations provide a mature foundation for WASP design, allowing the project to
target TRL-7 field demonstrations within the project timeline [12]–[19]. Collectively, this
approach shortens development cycles, improves model credibility, and ensures that results are
directly transferable to utility practice.

 Project Results and Discussion
This section summarizes project results relative to the Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO),
with comparisons between anticipated outcomes and realized results at the task and milestone
level. Quantitative performance metrics, methodological details, and evidence of milestone
completion are provided to establish confidence in the results and clearly convey progress
throughout Budget Period 1 (BP-1).

Task 1 – Modeling, Simulation, and Tool Development
Task 1.1 – Model and Simulation Requirements

Define technical requirements for a large-scale EMT model and co-simulation environment
capable of supporting high-IBR protection studies.

Task 1.1 was completed in Q1 as planned. The team established detailed modeling requirements
including:
  Bulk power system focus (≥138 kV)
  Load and generation capacity >400 MW
  Use of vendor-supplied black-box IBR models
  Offline (PSCAD-RTDS) and real-time (RTDS + GTSOC HIL) simulation capability
  Integration with stability analysis tools
These requirements directly guided subsequent model development and co-simulation
architecture design, with no deviations reported

Task 1.2 – Develop a Large-Scale Bulk Power System Model

Develop and validate an EMT model of a representative Con Edison transmission system
meeting accuracy and scale targets.
Milestone 1.2.1 (verbatim):
“Complete a ConEd transmission system model with a generation mix capable of supplying a
load capacity of >400 MW. Verify the calculated 3-phase bus fault currents difference between
CAPE and created RTDS model for 345 kV buses should stay within 10%.”

This milestone was completed in Q4. The team manually developed an EMT model in
RSCAD/RTDS representing a Con Edison transmission area with:
  90 three-phase buses
  23 generators
     1,000 MW of load
Model validation was performed by comparing EMT fault responses against the source ASPEN
short-circuit model across multiple fault types (ABC, BC, AG) and voltage levels (138 kV and
345 kV). Quantitative results show:
  Average fault current error <4% across all fault types
  Maximum observed error of 12% for one AG fault case, attributable to fictitious
 transformer equivalencing in the ASPEN reduction process
These results significantly exceeded the SOPO accuracy target of 10% error for three-phase
faults, thereby increasing confidence in the EMT model’s suitability for high-IBR studies.

Methodology:
Manual EMT model construction, systematic fault injection, and statistical error comparison
against a validated short-circuit source model.

Task 1.3 – Assess Gaps in Existing Short-Circuit Programs

Quantitatively assess limitations of commonly used short-circuit tools under high IBR
penetration and communicate findings publicly.
Milestone 1.3.1 (verbatim):
“Establish and document performance gaps for commonly used SC programs (e.g., CAPE and
ASPEN) under high IBR penetration in a public report, e.g., a SAND report published on
OSTI.gov.”

Milestone 1.3.1 was completed in Q4. The team conducted a large-scale comparative study
involving:
  ASPEN, CAPE, and CYME short-circuit programs
  PSCAD vendor-specific IBR EMT models as the reference
  1,000 automated fault simulation scenarios
Quantitative findings showed:
  RMS phasor-domain models can achieve <5% error for predictable IBR behaviors
  Significant convergence failures under close-in faults and high penetration levels
  Inability of static VCCS tables to represent pre-fault IBR operating state
The results were published as a Sandia National Laboratories report and directly influenced
vendor action: both ASPEN and CAPE released patches and beta versions addressing identified
gaps. Abstracts were also accepted by two major protective relaying conferences, further
validating the technical impact of the work.
Methodology:
Automated scripting, controlled variable testing (fault type, penetration level, IBR control
mode), and direct comparison to EMT reference models.

Task 1.4 – Build Time-Domain Co-Simulation Framework

Develop a co-simulation platform integrating EMT, real-time HIL, and stability analysis.

The co-simulation framework was completed in Q6. Key outcomes include:
  Validated RTDS-PSCAD co-simulation interface with negligible magnitude/angle error
  Fully functional RTDS-GTSOC hardware-in-the-loop interface hosting black-box
 inverter controllers
  Operational data-exchange workflow between RTDS and V&R’s stability analyzer
 (POM) using PSS/E RAW/DYR formats
The finalized GTSOC model supports automated testing and repeated scenarios without memory
overflow, enabling large-scale parametric studies with real inverter control logic. Stability
analysis has been verified for 0%, 25%, and 50% IBR penetration cases, meeting SOPO
performance objectives.

Methodology:
Real-time FPGA-based controller integration, benchmark comparison against pure EMT
simulations, and scripted test execution.

EPC Power controller model. This diversity significantly improves the robustness and
generalizability of study results

 Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
Significant Accomplishments
1. Successful Development and Validation of a Large-Scale EMT Model for a High-IBR
Transmission System
A major accomplishment of this award is the completion and validation of a detailed
electromagnetic transient (EMT) model of a representative Con Edison transmission system
section exceeding 1 GW of load, significantly surpassing the original >400 MW requirement.
Quantitative validation against the source ASPEN short-circuit model demonstrated average
fault-current errors below 4% across multiple fault types and voltage levels, exceeding SOPO
accuracy targets.
Significance: This result establishes a high-confidence EMT modeling foundation capable of
supporting protection and stability studies under high and transitioning IBR penetration levels,
which is a critical gap in current utility practice.

2. Completion of a Comprehensive Short-Circuit Software Gap Assessment with Industry
Impact
The project completed a rigorous, quantitative assessment of IBR modeling accuracy and
convergence behavior in commonly used short-circuit tools (ASPEN, CAPE, and CYME),
benchmarking them against vendor-specific EMT models using more than 1,000 automated
simulation scenarios. The findings were published in a Sandia National Laboratories report and
directly influenced software vendor patches and beta releases.
Significance: This work moved beyond academic analysis to produce actionable results that are
already improving commercial engineering tools used by utilities nationwide, accelerating near-
term industry readiness for high-IBR studies.

3. Deployment of a Real-Time EMT and Hardware-in-the-Loop Co-Simulation Framework
The project successfully integrated RTDS, PSCAD, GTSOC hardware, and a stability analysis
platform into a unified co-simulation framework. A real-time black-box inverter controller model
(developed with EPC Power) was demonstrated, enabling closed-loop EMT simulation with
authentic inverter control logic.
Significance: This capability enables protection and stability testing under realistic inverter
behavior that cannot be represented in traditional phasor-domain tools, supporting credible
evaluation of next-generation protection schemes.

4. Demonstrated EMT Model Convergence at 25% and 50% IBR Penetration Levels
The project achieved stable EMT simulation convergence during fault initiation, clearing, and
post-fault recovery at 25% and 50% IBR penetration, meeting a key go/no-go criterion.
Significance: EMT convergence at these penetration levels confirms the technical feasibility of
scaling the framework toward higher IBR scenarios, including near-100% IBR conditions
planned in subsequent budget periods.

5. Advancement of New Protection Concepts with Utility Engagement
Substantial progress was made on the design of Wide-Area System Protection (WASP) and
adaptive protection schemes, informed by extensive utility workshops at SCE, SDGE, Dominion,
and Con Edison. These engagements ensured alignment with real-world operational,
communications, and cybersecurity constraints.

                                          Page 15 of 21

Significance: Early and sustained utility involvement increases the likelihood of successful lab
and field demonstrations and eventual adoption, reducing the risk of developing solutions that
are technically sound but operationally impractical.

Challenges and Lessons Learned
Administrative and Contractual Delays
A significant early challenge was the delay in establishing subcontracts between the prime
recipient and subrecipients. This temporarily slowed progress on Tasks 1.3, 1.4, and early Task 2
activities.
Lesson Learned: Large, multi-organization DOE projects benefit from early parallelization of
technical planning and administrative processes. The team mitigated this issue through a no-cost
extension, which ultimately had no negative impact on technical quality or milestone
completion.

Limitations of Automated Model Conversion Tools
Automated conversion from ASPEN to EMT platforms proved unreliable due to missing
parameters, phasor-specific constructs, and numerical equivalencing artifacts.
Lesson Learned: For high-fidelity EMT studies, manual model construction and validation
remain essential, even when automated tools are available. This insight informed a deliberate
pivot to manual EMT modeling to preserve accuracy and confidence.

Short-Circuit Tool Convergence Issues Under High IBR Penetration
All evaluated short-circuit tools exhibited convergence failures or misleading results under
certain high-IBR conditions.
Lesson Learned: Engineers must treat short-circuit study results cautiously in high-IBR systems
and understand the conditions under which EMT or HIL studies are required. This reinforces the
value of the project’s gap assessment and guidance.

Conclusions
Overall, the project has met or exceeded all BP-1 technical milestones and go/no-go criteria. The
combination of validated EMT models, actionable short-circuit software insights, real-time co-
simulation capability, and strong utility engagement provides high confidence in the project’s
trajectory toward successful lab and field demonstrations. The work has already delivered
tangible benefits to industry tools and practices, positioning the project to make a lasting impact
on reliable protection of inverter-dominant power grids.
