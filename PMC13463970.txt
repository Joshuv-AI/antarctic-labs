Asymmetrical blending of Tenebrio molitor and Hermetia illucens meals in broiler nutrition: a novel strategy
Authors: Márk Tóth, Yazavinder Singh, Krisztián Balogh, Erika Zándoki, Szabina Kulcsár, Benjámin Kövesi, Zsolt Ancsin, Balázs Gregosits, Miklós Mézes, Mária Kovács-Weber, Márta Erdélyi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•First study on asymmetric TM:HI ratios (2:4 and 4:2) tested in broilers.•TM-rich group improved body weight gain and final body weight.•Both insect mixtures increased breast yield.•TM4-HI2 raised blood serum protein and HDL.•Insect diets shortened ileum and total small intestine, despite good performance.

Introduction

Since the earliest reports on the use of insect-derived proteins in broiler diets, this research area has gained remarkable momentum and has become a prominent topic within poultry nutrition (Al-Khalaifa et al., 2025; Tóth et al., 2026). Over the past decade, a growing body of research has demonstrated that insect larvae, particularly Hermetia illucens (HI) and Tenebrio molitor (TM), offer a viable and sustainable alternative to conventional feed ingredients in poultry production (Dabbou et al., 2018; De Marco et al., 2015; Schiavone et al., 2017). Interest has rapidly expanded in the utilization of insect-derived meals and fat, which can serve as valuable protein and energy sources when incorporated into broiler diets (Dalmoro et al., 2023). At the same time, the provision of whole larvae, either live or dried, is gaining attention for its potential to function as environmental enrichment for chickens (Schiavone & Castillo, 2024). In response to this expanding scientific evidence, regulatory authorities have updated their frameworks; the European Union has approved the use of insect-processed animal proteins in poultry feed (European Commission Regulation, 2021)

Although research advancements and recent legislative permissions have facilitated the use of insect-based feed ingredients, their integration into the commercial poultry feed sector remains problematic. A key limitation is the elevated price of insect meals, which continues to exceed that of traditional protein sources such as soybean meal and fishmeal. This price gap is largely driven by the limited production volume and the inconsistent availability of insect-based raw materials; current output remains far below the protein demand of the poultry industry, preventing large-scale supply and consequently keeping prices high (Gasco et al., 2023). In addition, most scientific evaluations suggest limiting insect meal inclusion to a maximum of around 10% in diets for modern broiler and layer strains to avoid negative effects on nutrient digestibility, growth performance, or meat quality (Biasato et al., 2023; Dalmoro et al., 2023; Gasco et al., 2023). However, some studies have already reported performance-reducing effects even at a 10% inclusion level (Biasato et al., 2025; De Souza Vilela et al., 2021; Petkov et al., 2024). Higher inclusion levels have been associated with reduced nutrient digestibility and subsequent impairments in gut health and performance traits (Biasato et al., 2023), likely related to the increased chitin intake, which has long been known to hinder digestibility (mainly crude protein) in poultry (Razdan & Pettersson, 1994).

Full-fat insect meals derived from different species differ markedly in their fatty acid profiles (Os Santos Aguilar, 2021; Siddiqui et al., 2025), amino acid (AA) composition (Biasato et al., 2025; De Marco et al., 2015), and chitin content (Biasato et al., 2025).

Studies evaluating combined inclusion of different insect meals in poultry diets remain scarce. To date, Biasato et al. (2025) investigated a 1:1 mixture of HI and TM meals at inclusion levels of 2.5%+2.5% and 5%+5%. While the higher total inclusion level (10%) negatively affected performance, the 5% mixture did not influence body weight (BW), body weight gain (BWG), or feed intake (FI) and resulted in an improved feed conversion ratio (FCR) compared to control diet. Compared with single-ingredient diets containing 5% TM or 5% HI, the mixed diet showed intermediate performance responses. These results highlight the need for further research exploring alternative inclusion ratios and combinations of insect meals. Based on the available literature, the present study evaluated the effects of combined full-fat HI and TM meals in broiler diets using previously untested asymmetric inclusion levels of 2% TM + 4% HI and 4% TM + 2% HI (6% total insect meal inclusion). These formulations were designed to assess the effects of differing insect meal proportions on broiler performance, nutrient utilization, and product quality. The experiment was conducted under semi-commercial conditions. The primary objective of the study was to evaluate the effects of these combined dietary inclusions of full-fat HI and TM meals on growth performance, carcass traits, meat quality, intestinal morphometry, and selected blood biochemical parameters in broiler chickens.

Materials and methods

Experimental diets

Three dietary treatments were used: a control diet (Control), a diet containing 2% TM+4% HI (TM2-HI4); and a diet containing 4% TM+2% HI (TM4-HI2). Each diet included a common concentrate (85%), composed of corn, wheat, sunflower meal, soybean meal, full-fat soybean, limestone, broiler grower complete premixture (Vitafort Plc, Dabas, Hungary). Owing to industrial confidentiality, the detailed ingredient composition of this concentrate is not disclosed. The remaining 15% was consisted of 6% full-fat combined insects meal (using asymmetric TM:HI ratios, 4:2 and 2:4), with variable amounts of corn, soybean meal, full-fat soybean, and crystalline AA adjusted to obtain isoenergetic and isonitrogenous diets in compliance with broiler nutrient requirements (Aviagen, 2018). The experimental diets were analyzed for dry matter, crude protein, sugar, starch, crude fiber, crude fat, and ash contents following the Association of Official Analytical Chemists methods (AOAC, 2012). Apparent metabolizable energy corrected for nitrogen (AMEn) was calculated based on analyzed chemical composition, whereas available calcium, available phosphorus, and standardized ileal digestible AA contents were derived from the formulation matrix. Experimental design and bird management

The study was conducted at the experimental poultry research facility of AgriSearch Hungary Ltd. (Pécel, Hungary,). All animal handling and husbandry procedures complied with the European Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes (European Commission Directive, 2010), as well as with the relevant Hungarian legislation (32/1999 /III.31/ and 178/2009 /XII.29/). Ethical approval was not required for this experiment, as only non-hazardous dietary ingredients were used, and no invasive procedures were applied.

A total of 1050 one-day-old male Ross 308 broiler chicks were obtained from a commercial hatchery (Babádi Baromfikeltető Ltd., Dabas, Hungary). Birds were vaccinated against Newcastle disease, infectious bronchitis, infectious bursal disease, and fowl pox prior to placement. Upon arrival, chicks were randomly allocated to three dietary treatments, each consisting of 14 replicate pens with 25 birds per pen. Pens measured 2.5 m² and were bedded with an 8-cm layer of wood shavings. This corresponded to a stocking density of 10 birds/m². The environmental conditions of the experimental house were controlled according to the recommendations of the Ross 308 Broiler Management Handbook (Aviagen, 2018). Continuous lighting (24 h/day) was provided during the first 3 days post-placement, after which the dark period was gradually increased until reaching 6 consecutive hours and then maintained until the end of the experiment. Ambient temperature was maintained at approximately 32–33 °C during the first week post-placement and gradually reduced with age, whereas relative humidity was generally kept within the recommended range of 50–70% throughout the trial. Feed and drinking water were provided ad libitum throughout the experimental period.

Growth performance

The individual BW of each bird and the FI of each pen were measured weekly throughout the 42-day experimental period to assess growth performance. Mortality and health status were monitored daily. BWG, average FI, FCR were calculated from recorded performance data.

Slaughter and carcass dissection traits

At 42 days of age, after overnight feed withdrawal, a total of 84 birds were randomly selected (without pre-selection on BW) for slaughter (28 birds/treatment, with two birds from each replicated pen). Selected birds were individually weighed and euthanized by severing the jugular vein and carotid artery. Carcass weight (CW) was determined after removal of feathers, head, neck, shanks, abdominal fat, and visceral organs. Subsequently, the carcasses were dissected into major cuts according to Petracci and Baeza (2011). The breast fillet, leg (thigh and drumstick combined), gizzard, and liver were excised and weighed. Carcass yield and organ weights were expressed relative to slaughter weight (SW), whereas breast and leg yields were calculated as proportions of the carcass weight.

Meat quality

Meat quality analyses were performed on a total of 42 birds (14 birds/treatment, one bird/ pen). At 24 h post-mortem, meat color parameters (lightness-L*, redness-a*, yellowness-b*) were recorded on right breast (m. pectoralis major) using a Minolta CR-330 chromameter (Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan), calibrated with a white plate. At the same point, muscle pH was measured in the same muscle using a calibrated portable pH meter equipped with a temperature-compensated piercing electrode (pH-STAR, Matthäus GmbH, Germany). For both color and pH, five measurements were taken per sample and averaged for statistical analysis.

Drip loss (DL) was determined using fresh samples of the right breast (m. pectoralis minor). Samples were weighed, suspended in plastic bags, and stored at 4 °C for 24 h. After storage, samples were gently dried with paper towels and reweighed to calculate DL.

The left breast (m. pectoralis major) was used to determine thawing loss (TL) and cooking loss (CL). Samples were placed in food-grade bags and stored at −20 °C for 30 days. Following frozen storage, samples were thawed at 4 °C for 24 h, removed from the bags, dried with paper towels and reweighed to calculate TL. CL was assessed by vacuum-sealing the thawed samples and cooking them in a water bath preset at 80 °C until the core temperature of the heaviest breast sample reached 74 °C. After cooling at room temperature (20 °C ± 1), samples were dried with paper towels and weighed again. Total loss was calculated as the sum of TL and CL.

Shear force (SF) was measured on cooked breast samples. Cylindrical cores with a cross-sectional area of 1 cm² were excised, and SF was determined using a TA.XT PLUS texture analyzer (Stable Micro Systems Ltd., Godalming, UK) equipped with a Warner-Bratzler blade, a 50 kg load cell, and a crosshead speed of 250 mm/min. Measurements were performed perpendicular to the muscle fiber orientation, with three readings obtained per sample.

For meat proximate composition, left thigh samples were deboned, ground and homogenized, and analyzed using near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy with a DA6200 meat analyzer (PerkinElmer Inc., MA, USA), operating in the wavelength range of 850–1050 nm to determine water, crude protein, fat, collagen, and ash.

Blood parameters

At 42 days of age, blood samples were collected from 14 birds per dietary treatment. Approximately 5 mL of whole blood was taken into serum-separator tubes (S-Monovette Serum Gel, Sarstedt AG & Co., Nümbrecht, Germany). Samples were centrifuged at 2500 g for 10 min to obtain serum, which was subsequently stored at −80 °C until analysis. Serum concentrations of total protein, total cholesterol, HDL, LDL, and triglycerides were determined using commercially available diagnostic kits (Diagnosticum Ltd., Budapest, Hungary).

Intestinal morphometry

Intestinal measurements were performed on the same 42 birds selected for meat quality evaluation. After evisceration, the small intestine and ceca were excised and the small intestine was divided into anatomical segments: duodenum (from the ventriculus to the entry of the pancreatic and biliary ducts), jejunum (from the bile duct entry to Meckel’s diverticulum), and ileum (from Meckel’s diverticulum to the ileocecal junction). The length of each intestinal segment and the ceca was measured and expressed as cm per 100 g of live BW. Total small intestinal length was calculated as the sum of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum.

Statistical analysis

All variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test prior to statistical analysis. Variables with normal distribution (growth performance, carcass characteristics, meat quality traits, blood parameters, and intestinal measurements) were analyzed by one-way ANOVA, with dietary treatments (Control, TM2-HI4, and TM4-HI2) as a fixed effect in a general linear model (PROC GLM, SAS® OnDemand for Academics 3.81 Enterprise Edition; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). For growth performance, pen was the experimental unit, and for carcass and meat physicochemical traits, single carcass was the experimental unit. Treatment means were compared using Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons, and statistical significance was declared at P< 0.05. Non-normally distributed data (mortality) were analyzed separately using chi-square procedures according to the method of (Marascuilo, 1966).

Results

Growth performance

The inclusion of combined full-fat HI and TM meals at a total inclusion level of 6% significantly affected body weight development across the experimental period (Table 3). Birds fed the TM4-HI2 diet consistently achieved greater BW than the Control group from day 14 onwards, with significant differences (P< 0.01) recorded at day 14 (435 g vs 423 g), day 21 (847 g vs 826 g), day 35 (1889 g vs 1834 g), and day 42 (2471 g vs 2407 g). The TM2-HI4 treatment resulted in a more comparable response, with significantly higher BW observed only on day 35 (1892 g vs 1834 g; P< 0.01). BWG during the 7–14-day period demonstrated significant treatment effects (Table 3), with the TM4-HI2 group reaching 38.1 g/d/bird compared to 36.7 g/d/bird in TM2-HI4 and 36.2 g/d/bird in the Control groups (P< 0.001). Over the entire 42-day trial period, BWG was significantly (P< 0.05) higher for TM4-HI2 (57.9 g/d/bird) than the Control (56.3 g/d/bird), whereas TM2-HI4 showed an intermediate value (57.4 g/d/bird). FI, FCR, and mortality were not significantly affected (P> 0.05) by dietary treatment (Table 4). Average daily FI across the entire 42-day trial period was 103 g/d/bird for all three groups (P> 0.05). FCR during the early growth phase (days 7–14) showed a significant effect (P< 0.05), with the Control group displayed a numerically higher FCR (1.21) compared to TM4-HI2 (1.15), though TM2-HI4 group (1.16) was intermediate. Considering the complete trial period (1–42 days) FCR was comparable across all treatments, ranging from 1.78 to 1.83 (P> 0.05). Meat quality

Breast meat quality was marginally influenced by dietary treatments (Table 6). Meat color parameters a* and b* were not significantly affected (P> 0.05), while the L* value showed a significant difference (P< 0.05), with the TM4-HI2 group exhibiting higher L* (64.6) compared to the TM2-HI4 (62.1). However, no significant differences (P> 0.05) were detected in pH, DL, TL, and SF. Whereas, CL was significantly affected by dietary treatments (P< 0.05), with TM2-HI4 group showing the highest value (29.7%), significantly elevated (2%) compared to the Control (27.3%), whereas TM4-HI2 group displayed an intermediate value (29.2%). Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is only the second study to investigate the use of combined insect meals (HI and TM) in broiler diets. Biasato et al. (2025) evaluated a 1:1 mixture of the two meals at two inclusion levels (2.5%+2.5% and 5%+5%) and reported that performance was maintained at the lower inclusion levels, but impaired when the total inclusion reached 10% compared with the control group. In contrast, the present experiment tested asymmetric TM:HI ratios (2:4 and 4:2) at a fixed 6% total inclusion level, thereby addressing a gap in the literature by testing whether performance, product quality, blood parameters, and intestinal morphometry can be modulated not only by inclusion level, but also by the relative proportion of the two insect species.

Growth performance

A clear pattern emerged whereby the TM‑rich combination (TM4-HI2) resulted in higher BW throughout most of the growing period, whereas the HI‑rich combination (TM2-HI4) maintained performance closer to the Control. This indicates that, at a nutritionally safe total inclusion level (6%), the balance between TM and HI is more critical for growth than the absolute amount of insect meal. These findings are consistent with earlier work on single‑species inclusions. Dabbou et al. (2018) and Schiavone et al. (2019) reported that low to moderate HI inclusion does not impair broiler performance when balanced diets are used, while higher levels or poorly adjusted formulations may reduce growth. Similarly, TM included at about 5% has been shown to maintain or slightly improve performance, depending on age, sex and basal diet (Biasato et al., 2017, 2018; Sedgh-Gooya et al., 2021).

(Biasato et al., 2025) demonstrated that a 5% total inclusion of a 1:1 HI+TM mixture was well tolerated, whereas raising the same mixture to 10% led to reductions in BW and BWG without FCR improvement. The absolute FCR values observed in the present study were somewhat higher than those typically reported under commercial conditions. This may partly be attributed to the use of a single-phase mash diet throughout the 42-day period, whereas commercial production commonly relies on multi-phase feeding programs and pelleted or crumbled feeds to maximize feed efficiency. In addition, the trial was conducted during the exceptionally hot summer of 2024, and despite environmental management according to Ross 308 recommendations, birds were likely exposed to periods of moderate heat stress, which may have adversely affected feed efficiency. Nevertheless, these conditions were identical across treatments, and therefore the relative differences among dietary groups remain valid. When these findings are considered together with the current observations, a coherent picture appears; up to 6% total inclusion, insect meals can be used safely in broiler diets; within this safe window, a higher TM proportion seems advantageous for growth. A likely explanation is linked to nutrient profile differences between the two insect species. TM is characterized by a favorable essential AA balance and usually lower chitin content, while HI, particularly in full‑fat form, contributes more lipid and medium‑chain saturated fatty acids (Biasato et al., 2018; Dabbou et al., 2018; Murawska et al., 2021). Diets richer in TM may therefore supply AAs closer to the broiler’s requirements for breast muscle accretion, whereas higher HI proportions may slightly dilute this advantage, especially if chitin and saturated fat displace other nutrients.

Carcass traits

Both insect‑fed groups showed a consistent increase in breast yield, without parallel increases in carcass yield or relative organ weights, which indicates a shift in nutrient partitioning towards breast muscle rather than a generalized increase in body mass. This selective enhancement of breast proportion is particularly relevant from an economic perspective, as breast is the highest‑value cut in commercial broiler production (Scheuermann et al., 2003). Similar trends have been reported in previous insect‑based studies. In TM‑fed birds, Biasato et al. (2018) observed numerically higher breast yields. For HI, Schiavone et al. (2019) and Dabbou et al. (2018) found largely unchanged carcass yields at moderate inclusion levels but reported that breast characteristics can be modulated under certain conditions. Biasato et al. (2025) further demonstrated that when a 1:1 HI+TM mixture was used, breast yield increased at 5% total inclusion but decreased at 10%, highlighting a non‑linear, dose‑dependent response. The current pattern, higher breast yield at 6% total inclusion, regardless of whether TM or HI predominates, fits well with this evidence and suggests that insect meals, when used at moderate levels and within balanced formulations, can support pectoral muscle development. From a biological standpoint, the improved breast proportion is consistent with the hypothesis that insect meals provide highly bioavailable nutrients and potentially bioactive compounds that can support gut function and nutrient capture, enabling more resources to be directed to fast‑growing muscle tissues. Bioactive components discussed in the insect literature include antimicrobial peptides (Xia et al., 2021) and insect‑associated lipids such as lauric acid (Borrelli et al., 2021), which may indirectly influence nutrient partitioning through gut health and inflammatory tone.

Meat quality

The overall stability of breast pH, DL, TL, and SF suggests that the inclusion level and formulation strategy did not induce major changes in post‑mortem glycolysis or myofibrillar integrity. This is broadly consistent with earlier work indicating that moderate HI inclusion as meal or fat source preserves the technological quality of broiler breast meat (Schiavone et al., 2017, 2019), and that TM inclusion can maintain technological traits in broilers (Šťastník et al., 2021).

The difference in L* may reflect diet‑driven changes in muscle microstructure and lipid composition. Studies replacing conventional fats with HI fat have shown that insect lipids can modify tissue lipid profiles and, consequently, optical properties of meat (Murawska et al., 2021; Schiavone et al., 2017). Full‑fat HI and TM differ in FA pattern (Dos Santos Aguilar, 2021; Siddiqui et al., 2025), and such differences may influence light scattering through changes in intramuscular fat distribution and membrane lipid composition.

The increase in CL and total losses in the HI‑richer diet resulted in a moderate reduction in WHC. The classic explanation involves pH‑dependent protein denaturation, a lower pH due to faster post‑mortem pH decline increases protein denaturation and reduce the ability of the muscle matrix to retain water during heating (Petracci & Cavani, 2011). Although pH differences were not statistically significant, even subtle variations in pH kinetics, combined with a higher intake of saturated FA from HI, could have contributed to the observed increase in CL due to membrane lipid profile changes which modify membrane stability.

In thigh meat, composition was largely unchanged except for an increase in lipid content in insect‑fed groups. This mirrors reports of greater abdominal fat and altered carcass fat deposition when HI or TM are included at higher levels (Biasato et al., 2017; Murawska et al., 2021). As protein and ash content were not reduced, the shift likely reflects redistribution of surplus dietary energy from insect fat toward storage in dark muscle and adipose depots, rather than a deficit in protein accretion.

Blood parameters

Blood biochemical variables provided additional insight into how combined insect meals influenced nutrient metabolism. The higher total protein concentration observed in the TM4-rich group may reflect more efficient AA utilization and hepatic synthesis of circulating proteins when TM provides a larger share of the insect fraction. Similar TM‑based diets have been associated with normal or improved protein and health indicators in broilers (Biasato et al., 2018; Sedgh-Gooya et al., 2021). A plausible explanation could be that the lower chitin content, the AA profile and protein digestibility of TM may allow more efficient synthesis of circulating proteins, including albumin and immune‑related proteins. The trend toward higher triglycerides in insect‑fed birds is coherent with the higher dietary fat intake when full‑fat insect meals replace part of the carbohydrate or protein-rich ingredients and is also consistent with increased lipid content in thigh meat. However, total cholesterol remained stable across treatments, and the TM‑rich diet was characterized by a higher HDL fraction without a corresponding rise in LDL. In general, this pattern is interpreted as metabolically favorable because it reflects enhanced reverse cholesterol transport rather than atherogenic cholesterol accumulation. Available literature on blood responses to insect feeding in poultry shows variable outcomes, likely due to differences in species, processing, and inclusion level, but several studies report no negative effect/changes in standard clinical biochemical markers at moderate inclusion levels (Bellezza Oddon et al., 2021; Biasato et al., 2018; Dabbou et al., 2018). The current serum biochemical profile, together with maintained or improved performance, therefore, supports the view that combined HI and TM at 6% do not induce metabolic stress, and that TM‑rich mixtures may even promote a more desirable lipoprotein pattern.

Intestinal morphometry

The gastrointestinal measurements indicate that the insect‑based diets induced localized structural changes predominantly in the distal small intestine and ceca. Both insect‑fed groups exhibited shorter total small intestinal length compared with the Control, mainly due to reductions in ileal length (and the reduced cecal length in the HI‑richer mixture). Despite these changes, growth and FCR were maintained or improved in the insect-fed groups.

The present findings differ from studies where high inclusion levels of insect meals were associated with longer intestinal segments. Bovera et al. (2016, 2018) reported increased small intestinal length or organ size when TM or HI meals were used at relatively high dietary levels in broilers and layers (often replacing soybean meal almost completely). In contrast, experiments with moderate TM inclusion (up to about 5%) revealed minimal change in intestinal length or only subtle histological adaptations (Biasato et al., 2017; Sedgh-Gooya et al., 2021). Similarly, studies with HI oil and meal inclusion demonstrated that gut morphometry responds to both dose and ingredient form, with some combinations leaving intestinal lengths unchanged and others inducing elongation or mucosal modifications (Cutrignelli et al., 2018; Kierończyk et al., 2020). These contrasting patterns can be interpreted using general principles of gut plasticity. When nutrient digestibility or availability is reduced (e.g. by viscosity‑forming NSP or less digestible fractions), the intestine can enlarge to increase absorptive surface. This generally accepted concept has been discussed in poultry nutrition (Smits & Annison, 1996) and is consistent with observations in other monogastric animals that digestive organ development can change under reduced digestibility conditions (Borin et al., 2006). Conversely, improved digestibility and nutrient availability may reduce the requirement for extensive gastrointestinal capacity. Previous studies indicate that the avian small intestine displays considerable phenotypic plasticity and may exhibit reduced structural development under highly digestible dietary conditions, while finely processed feeds can further limit gastrointestinal hypertrophy and lower the energetic cost of intestinal tissue maintenance (Starck, 1999; Uni et al., 1998; Zaefarian et al., 2016). In this context, the shorter ileum and ceca observed under the combined HI-TM diets, in the absence of performance impairment, are more compatible with adequate or even improved nutrient utilization than with malabsorption. Nevertheless, direct measurements of apparent ileal digestibility and microbiota composition were not included in the present work, so the underlying mechanism cannot be fully resolved. Future trials that concurrently evaluate digestibility, short‑chain fatty acid production and immune markers would help clarify whether the reduced distal gut length mainly reflects more efficient digestion, altered fermentation, or other physiological adjustments associated with insect‑derived components such as chitin and medium‑chain fatty acids.

Conclusion

A 6% total inclusion of full-fat TM and HI meals, used in asymmetric ratios (2:4 and 4:2) proved to be nutritionally safe for broiler chickens, maintaining FCR, carcass yield, and organ development while enhancing breast meat yield across both insect-fed groups. The TM4-HI2 combination demonstrated superior growth performance, reflecting complementary nutritional advantages of the two insect species when balanced appropriately. These findings indicate that low-level mixed insect meals represent a viable strategy for sustainable poultry nutrition, with TM-rich formulations offering particular promise for commercial application. Further research exploring digestibility, optimal ratios, and economic thresholds will support wider adoption.
