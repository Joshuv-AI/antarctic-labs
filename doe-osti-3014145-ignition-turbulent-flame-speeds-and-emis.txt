The team of Georgia Tech is addressing knowledge gaps on hydrogen containing fuel combustion
fundamentals with a focus on the investigation of autoignition, laminar and turbulent flame speeds,
and emissions for hydrogen containing fuels at practical gas turbine conditions. This effort includes
measurements of autoignition delays and ignition uniformity (such as associated flame propagation )
in a high-pressure shock tube, and turbulent flame speeds, and emissions measurements at different
turbulence levels in a high-pressure combustor. Knowledge gained from this program will largely
support the interest of DoE on high hydrogen blended fuels.
1. Investigation of autoignition of high hydrogen fuels
Autoignition delays of hydrogen containing fuels are measured at practical gas turbine conditions over
a broad range of conditions to create a comprehensive database. Although autoignition delay times
(IDTs) have been measured extensively for variety of fuels, IDTs have not been well studied for
hydrogen containing fuel at practical gas turbine conditions. In this project, experiments will focus on
the measurement of IDTs for H2/natural gas mixture and NH3/H2 mixture using a high-pressure shock
tube.
At the beginning of this project to determine the appropriate testing conditions, we tested H2/air
mixture with and without dilution at different conditions to determine the mixture composition for the
experimental campaign. The reason is that H2 is highly reactive and it may cause pre-matured ignition
even after the incident shock passage inside the shock tube. In this effort, we first conduct experiments
by repeating experiments reported in literature as a sanity check of our system at 10 atm using
H2/O2/Ar (6:3:91). Figure 1.1 shows the results from literature and our experiments, comparing with
simulation using FFCM1 kinetic model. It can be seen from Fig. 1.1 that our experiments agree well
with results in literature using highly diluted mixture. The agreement between experiments and
simulation also agrees well at higher temperature conditions. Below approximately 1100 K, the
deviation between experiments and simulation increases.

 Fig. 1.1 Comparison between experiments and simulation for IDTs of H2/O2/Ar mixture at 10 atm

Literature review also showed significant difference of H2/air mixture results between experiments
and simulation. For example, as shown in Fig. 1.2, experimentally measured IDTs of H2/air mixture
without diluent are much shorter than numerical simulation. Our next effort is to examine undiluted
H2/air mixture. We conducted three experiments using undiluted H2/air mixture at different conditions
and found that undiluted H2/air mixture was so reactive and it ignited right after the passage of incident
shock in locations far away from the end wall test section. This is evidenced by the significant
acceleration of incident shock. The incident shock normally attenuates while traveling to the end wall
region with decreased shock velocity measured by pressure sensors. If H2/air was used as testing
mixture, shock velocity increased and OH* emission measurement showed early ignition away from
the end wall region. This explains the increased incident shock velocity as ignition (heat release)
behind the incident shock push the shock forward. We then conducted a serious experiments using
H2/air mixture but with different level of diluent. The results are summarized in Table 1.1. From the
results we can see that up to 20% of N2 diluent of the test mixture, pre-ignition after incident shock
was observed. With 30% N2 diluent, no pre-ignition was observed but accelerating incident shock was
still observed. With 40% of N2 diluent, neither pre-ignition nor accelerating incident shock was
observed. The theoretical value of pressure after reflected shock agreed with experimental value.
Therefore, 40% diluent level of N2 is determined for the test mixture for experimental campaign
measuring IDTs.

During the tests, we also observed that when gas with small molecular weight was added into the test
mixture, the theoretical value from shock relations deviates more from the experimental value on
pressure. We conducted a series of measurements using non-reacting test mixture to verify this
phenomenon even though the cause is still unknown to us. From the results presented in Table 1.2, it

large diameter shock tube. However, if light gas, such as H2 or He, is added into the test mixture, the
deviation between theoretical values and experimental values increased from 3% to 7%. While we do
not know the cause of this phenomenon, we will include it as uncertainty in our IDT measurements.

As shown in Figure 1.17, a combination of pressure, OH* emission, and NH3 laser absorption signals
was used to provide an unambiguous determination of autoignition delay time in each experiment.
Adsorption of NH3 on stainless steel walls is known to affect mixture composition, and the issue was
mitigated by adopting the same passivation procedure used in Mathieu and Petersen’s work. The
shock tube interior was also cleaned using acetone before each experiment to minimize debris and
chemical residue. With the above measures in place, the discrepancies between model predictions and
experimental measurements could indicate a lack of understanding in NH3 oxidation kinetics that
needs further investigation.

Figure 1.18 show autoignition delay time measurements of NH3/O2/Ar mixtures with fuel
concentrations ranging from 1% to 22%. The experimental measurements were compared with
simulation results using kinetic mechanisms by Konnov, Mével et al., Klippenstein et al., Mathieu
and Petersen, Glarborg et al., Shrestha et al., Mei et al., Okafor et al., and Manna et al., performed
with Ansys Chemkin-Pro. As seen from the top left of Fig. 1.18, the measured IDTs clearly display
an Arrhenius-like temperature dependence at each fuel concentration, and the autoignition is
increasingly accelerated as the fuel concentration increases from 1% to 22%. For the stoichiometric
mixture with 1% of NH3, the measurements are found in excellent agreement with the experimental
data and model by Mathieu and Petersen. A large variation among model predictions is observed, with
the majority of them being over-reactive, especially the models by Konnov et al. and Mevel et al.. The
exception is the model by Okafor et al., which shows a stronger temperature dependence and becomes
increasingly under-reactive at lower temperatures. As the NH3 mole fraction is increased to 3%, the
measurements are in good agreement with model predictions by Mathieu and Petersen and by Mei et
al.. Most models are over-reactive, with the models by Konnov et al. and Mevel et al. being the most
significant, whereas the model by Okafor et al. is under-reactive across the entire temperature range.
With 7% of NH3 in the mixture, several models start to become under-reactive. The IDT
measurements are captured by the model by Shrestha et al. at the low-temperature end and approach

the model predictions by Konnov et al. and Mevel et al. towards the high-temperature end. The latter
two models still appear to be over-reactive at all temperatures. As the NH3 mole fraction is increased
to 15%, most models become under-reactive across the temperature range. The measurements have
the best agreement with the model by Mevel et al., although it still under-predicts the IDT at the low-
temperature end. The model by Konnov et al. is over-reactive at all temperatures and becomes
increasingly so at lower temperatures. Finally, as the NH3 mole fraction reaches 22%, which is
equivalent to the fuel concentration in the undiluted stoichiometric NH3/air mixture, all models except
the one by Konnov et al. become under-reactive across the temperature range. The model by Konnov
et al., showing a smaller temperature dependence than the measurements indicate, under-predicts the
IDT at higher temperatures and over-predicts at lower temperatures. The model by Mevel et al. better
captures the temperature dependence of IDT, although it is now under-reactive at all temperatures.

Several important observations can be made from these experiments and model comparisons.
Variations larger than an order of magnitude exist among predictions by selected kinetic models.
While the measured IDTs show the best agreement with the model by Mathieu and Petersen at lower
fuel concentrations and by Mevel et al. at higher fuel concentrations, none of the models can
consistently predict the IDT across the entire fuel concentration range. The predicted effect of
increasing fuel concentration is not as aggressive as indicated by experimental measurements, and
many of the models shift from being over-reactive to under-reactive as the fuel concentration
increases.

The effect of 500-ppm NO addition in NH3/O2/Ar mixtures is investigated at 3% and 15% NH3 mole
fractions. While the models that best agree with measurements without NO addition predict a very
slight decrease in autoignition delay time with NO added, the measured results are comparable to and
show a minimal increase from those without NO addition. The reason for this discrepancy is not yet
known, and more measurements at other conditions would be needed to confirm such observation.

Reaction path and sensitivity analyses were performed on the models by Mathieu and Petersen and
by Mevel et al., which satisfactorily predict the IDT at low and high fuel concentrations, respectively,
to have a better understanding of the fuel concentration effect. The reaction path diagrams shown in
Fig. 3 are generated from the two models at conditions where they closely predict experimental
measurements. For each model, the reaction paths remain mostly unchanged between the low and
high fuel concentration cases. Several paths are common to both models, including the ones that
convert NH3 to N2 through NH2/NH, HNO, and NNH. A major difference is the reaction sequence
involving N2H4/N2H3/N2H2 that was only found in the model by Mevel et al. and is not included in
the model by Mathieu and Petersen. This additional path of consuming NH3 and producing radicals
could partially explain that the model by Mevel et al. appears more reactive and predicts shorter IDTs
than the model by Mathieu and Petersen at all conditions investigated so far.

The brute-force sensitivities of the IDT to a number of reactions for both models at low and high fuel
concentrations are shown in Fig. 1.20. Reactions with a positive sensitivity increase the IDT and
prohibit the autoignition when their rate constants are increased, whereas reactions with a negative
sensitivity decrease the IDT and promote the autoignition. At 1% fuel concentration, the chain-
branching reaction of H+O2 is the dominant ignition promoter in both models. At 15% fuel
concentration, the most important ignition promoter in the model by Mathieu and Petersen is NH2 +
NO → NNH + OH, followed by those related to the oxidation of NH2 to form HNO. In the model by

Mevel et al., the reactions NH2 + O2 → HNO + OH and H + O2 → OH + O play a more prominent
role, followed by the NH3-N2H3-N2H2-NNH sequence. It is noted that in the model by Mathieu and
Petersen the reaction H2NO + NH2 → HNO + NH3 (R172) only strongly inhibits ignition in the high
fuel concentration case, which possibly explains the model being under-reactive at high fuel
concentrations. Similarly, in the model by Mevel et al. the reaction NH3 + NH2 → N2H3 + H2 (R165)
that more selectively promotes ignition in the low fuel concentration case is likely to account for the
model being over-reactive at low fuel concentrations. In addition, the two reaction channels of NH2 +
NO → NNH + OH and NH2 + NO → N2 + H2O appear to affect the IDT in opposite directions, and
they have greater sensitivities in the model by Mathieu and Petersen at 15% fuel concentration.

The influence of the N2Hx pathway, the reactions R172 and R165 that are shown to selectively impact
IDT depending on the fuel concentration, and the competition between the two NH2 + NO reaction
channels was examined by modifying them in the two models and comparing their predictions with
experimental measurements at 1% and 15% fuel concentrations. Figure 5 shows that the inclusion of
the N2Hx reactions in the model Mathieu and Petersen reduces the predicted IDTs at both fuel
concentrations. Similarly, In Figure 6, reducing the rate constant of R165 in the model by Mevel et
al. arbitrarily by a factor of 10 reduces the predicted IDTs at both fuel concentrations. In Fig. 1.23,
reducing the rate constant of R172 in the model by Mathieu and Petersen maintains good agreement
with measurements at 1% fuel concentration; at 15% fuel concentration, the predicted IDTs are only
reduced to a limited extent towards the low-temperature end. In Fig. 1.24, increasing the rate constants
of both NH2 + NO reaction channels arbitrarily by a factor of 2 does not have a significant impact on
the predicted IDTs, indicating the two competing channels balance each other at both low and high
fuel concentrations. It becomes clear that none of these attempted changes is sufficient to account for
the discrepancies of IDT prediction at low and high fuel concentrations, and further analysis to identify
important features that more selectively respond to the fuel centration effect is necessary.

Experimental IDT data for the effect of 500-ppm NO addition to NH3/O2/Ar mixtures had been
obtained at both low (3%) and high (15%) ammonia fuel concentrations, as shown in Figures 1.25
and 1.26, respectively. Based on the experimental data, the effect of NO addition was observed to

have a minimal impact on the IDT at both fuel concentrations. In contrast, the model predictions by
Mathieu and Petersen (2015) at 3% fuel concentration and by Mével et al. (2009) at 15% fuel
concentration indicate slightly reduced IDTs for all temperatures. Further investigations revealed that
the stability of NO in the presence of O2 also likely impacted the measurement results [Sahu et al.,
2022]. Simulations using both models by Mével et al. (2009) and by Mathieu and Petersen (2015)
show that conditions during the mixture preparation (for about 10 minutes at 300 K and 5 atm) are
sufficient to oxidize more than 60% of NO to NO2. According to simulations using both models,
however, the effect of the presence of NO2 is to shorten the simulated IDTs at both low and high fuel
concentrations across the entire temperature range, which was not observed in the experimental
measurements. In the next steps of investigation the effect of NO addition on the IDT of different
hydrogen/ammonia blends, a more consistent mixture preparation procedure will be adopted, with
particular attention to the time elapsed between the introduction of NO in the mixture and the instant
of shock heating. An additional set of IDT measurements will also be conducted with NO in the
mixture replaced by NO2 to establish a worse case scenario for comparison where NO is completely
converted to NO2.

IDT of stoichiometric hydrogen/ammonia mixtures was also investigated at 20-30 atm and 900-1400
K. Argon dilution was maintained at 74%, and the fuel concentration was maintained at 15%, with
hydrogen addition ranging from 0% to 70% in the fuel. The IDT measurements were shown in Figures
3 to 6, corresponding to 0%, 20%, 50%, and 70% hydrogen addition in the fuel, respectively. The
experimental measurements were also compared with literature data obtained from shock tubes and
rapid compression machines.
The effect of hydrogen blending in ammonia fuel is observed to decrease the IDT and increase the
overall reactivity of the mixture. With 0% hydrogen addition, models by Mevel et al. and by Konnov
et al. under-predict IDTs; other models , especially the one by Okafor et al., over-predict IDTs. At
20% and 50% hydrogen addition, the model by Glarborg has the best agreement with IDT
measurements over the entire temperature range. As hydrogen addition increases to 70%, most of the
models appear to over-predict IDTs, and the extent of over-prediction becomes more significant at
lower temperatures.
Premature ignition outside of the test section due to local hot spots is known to be a challenge in shock
tube IDT measurement with high hydrogen concentrations at low temperatures. The experimental
conditions were carefully controlled to ensure the validity of IDT results. Figure 1.32 shows the
signals from an experiment with 70% hydrogen addition in fuel (12% hydrogen in mixture) at 973 K,
producing an IDT above 2 ms. The flat pressure and OH* emission signals followed by a steep rise
upon ignition indicate the absence of remote ignition and ensure the confidence in IDT results.

In this project, we worked on the development of NH3 absorption spectroscopy. The absorption
diagnostic for NH3 detection utilizes a fiber-coupled distributed-feedback diode laser. The collimated
output beam is tunable over the range of 2200.50 ± 0.25 nm (4544.42 ± 0.52 cm-1), with a narrow
linewidth below 3 MHz and an ex-fiber optical power of 2 mW. It probes the absorption feature of
NH3 near 2.2 μm, consisting of 3 closely spaced R-branch transitions, aR(7,4), sR(10,9), and sR(7,2),
in the ν2+ν3 combination band. Lineshape characterization of the three transitions of interest was
conducted for the accurate determination of line strengths, self-broadening coefficients, and
broadening coefficients with collisional partners including O2, N2, and Ar. The diode laser was
operated in scanned-wavelength direct-absorption (scanned-DA) mode in a static absorption cell for
the measurement of room-temperature lineshape parameters, and in both scanned-DA and fixed-
wavelength direct-absorption (fixed-DA) mode behind shock waves in a shock tube for high-
temperature measurements. In addition to addressing the incomplete knowledge of spectroscopic
parameters of the target transitions, the capability of the sensor was demonstrated through NH3 time-
history measurements in a shock tube, which provides the opportunity of investigating NH3 chemical
kinetics at engine and turbine relevant conditions.

Figure 1.35 presents the room-temperature lineshape measurements of NH3 with several collisional
partners obtained in an absorption cell. A Multi-spectral Voigt fitting routine allows to simultaneously
infer the collisional FWHMs for all three target transitions shown in Figure 1.36, from which the
room-temperature collisional broadening coefficients can be determined. The room-temperature pure
NH3 lineshape measurements also allow to infer the room-temperature line strengths of these
transitions. High-temperature lineshape measurements conducted behind shock waves provide
collisional broadening coefficients at various temperatures shown in Figure 1.37, and allow to infer
the temperature-dependence exponents of collisional broadening coefficients. These inferred
spectroscopic parameters and comparison with HITRAN [2] data are summarized in Tables 1.4 and
1.5. Performance of the absorption diagnostic was demonstrated by NH3 time-history measurements

in the High-Pressure Shock Tube. Figures 1.38 and 1.39 show the measured NH3 time-histories and
comparison with model predictions at 3% and 15% fuel loading, respectively. The newly developed
NH3 absorption diagnostic provides the opportunity to study NH3 chemical kinetics with improved
affordability.

The experimental observations for all three cases—two with 3% H2 and one with 5% H2—consistently
revealed autoignition behavior characteristic of homogeneous ignition. In each case, a single flame
kernel initiated near the center of the shock tube and rapidly expanded outward, resulting in nearly
instantaneous volumetric ignition. This ignition behavior differs markedly from that associated with
inhomogeneous ignition, where weak pre-ignition typically originates at the wall and propagates
circumferentially inward. Main volumetric ignition then followed couple hundreds microseconds
later. Such behavior has been previously reported by Ninnemann et al. [5] for low-concentration
hydrogen mixtures. It was also worth mentioning that conventional pressure measurement did not pick
up this weak pre-ignition effects in inhomogeneous ignition in Ninnemann’s study. Inhomogeneous
ignition also occurs when there exists local flame kernels which results in multiple hot-spot induced
pre-ignition. In all of our cases, no such hot-spot induced local ignition were observed.

In contrast, the endwall imaging sequences in this study showed no evidence of pre- ignition or wall-
driven ignition phenomena. Rather, the ignition events commenced centrally and progressed in a
spatially uniform manner, providing strong visual confirmation of homogeneity in flame development.
Furthermore, the comparison between endwall imaging and pressure-based IDTs measurements
consistently showed a time lag: high-speed imaging often captured earlier ignition onset relative to
the recorded IDTs. This discrepancy is primarily attributed to spatial limitations inherent in the

pressure diagnostics, which are fixed 1.5 inches downstream from the endwall. The imaging system,
by contrast, offers a more global perspective of the ignition process, including regions not probed by
the pressure transducers.

These findings underscore the limitations of relying solely on pressure measurements for interpreting
ignition phenomena. In scenarios involving inhomogeneous ignition, weak pre-ignition events may go
undetected by pressure diagnostics. High-speed optical diagnostics—particularly endwall imaging—
thus serve as a critical tool for diagnosing flame homogeneity.

In summary, high-speed endwall imaging has proven to be a powerful diagnostic technique for
assessing the homogeneity of autoignition in shock tube environments. The integration of optical
access with advanced imaging capabilities enabled a comprehensive evaluation of ignition behavior
across all test cases. While discrepancies between experimental and simulated IDTs persist, all cases
consistently demonstrated spatially homogeneous ignition. Moving forward, to more precisely
determine the axial location of ignition onset and further resolve spatial gradients, high-speed sidewall
imaging is recommended as a complementary diagnostic approach.

In shock tube experiments, it is generally expected that the test mixture closest to the endwall, having
first encountered the reflected shock and reached region-5 conditions, will have the longest residence
time and thus initiate ignition earliest under zero-dimensional chemical kinetics. This assumption
relies on the premise that the thermodynamic condi- tions behind the reflected shock wave are spatially
uniform which can be considered to be 0-dimension. However, in Case No. 7 where the ignition front
initialed at a distance away from the end wall, this 0-D, uniform thermodynamic condition behind
reflected shock wave no longer holds. Instead, a 1-D axial temperature-gradient should be considered.

This temper- ature gradient is introduced by Susa [9] in his work as they observed similar downstream
ignition cases using methane mixture. As the reflected shock wave travels further away from the
endwall, the strength of the shock wave also reduces. This temperature gradient will cause the test gas
behind reflected shock wave to heat up non-uniformly thus creating a axial ignition location away from
the end wall. Measured IDTs will also be effected by this phenomenon. As in Case No. 7, the measured
IDTs is 754 µs, however, larger discrepancy in Chemkin model predication arises as Aramco 2.0 under
predicted the IDTs by a factor of 1.8 and the other two mechanisms FFCM-1 and San Diego all under
predicted IDTs by a factor beyond 3.

The sidewall OH* chemiluminescence imaging technique provides critical spatial and temporal
insights into ignition phenomena that are not attainable through pressure transducer measurements
alone. By visualizing the development of ignition fronts along the axial length of the shock tube, these
images allow us to assess not only the ignition timing but also the ignition mode and spatial location
of autoignition initiation.

For the 3% H2 mixture, sidewall imaging confirmed that ignition occurred near the end- wall, consistent
with classical assumptions of homogeneous ignition at region-5 conditions. The agreement between

experimental IDTs (250 µs) and Chemkin predictions (280 µs) suggests that under these conditions,
zero-dimensional (0-D) modeling is appropriate. This case supports the long-standing assumption that
ignition initiates at the endwall, where reflected shock conditions are best defined, and propagates
axially downstream in a uniform fashion.

In contrast, for the 5% H2 mixture, the ignition behavior exhibits more complex characteristics
depending on the post-shock temperature. At higher temperatures (Cases No. 8 and No. 9, T5 > 1080
K), strong, spatially localized ignition fronts appear in the viewing window from the endwall side,
again indicating endwall ignition. Despite ideal test conditions and clear ignition modes, the
experimentally measured IDTs were shorter than those predicted by the Aramco 2.0 mechanism by a
factor of 1.5, revealing discrepancies in the kinetic model at high temperatures.

At lower temperatures (Case No. 7, T5 < 1080 K), however, sidewall imaging reveals a fundamentally
different ignition behavior. Instead of a distinct ignition front near the endwall, only weak, distributed
flame kernels were visible downstream. This spatial decoupling of ignition from the endwall suggests
the breakdown of the 0-D assumption. Here, a one-dimensional (1-D) axial temperature gradient is
likely present, resulting in ignition at locations further downstream from the endwall. This
phenomenon significantly affects IDTs measurements, as the “time zero” location no longer
corresponds to the actual ignition location. Consequently, the measured IDTs of 754 µs differs
markedly from the kinetic model predictions, with Aramco 2.0 underpredicting IDTs by a factor of
1.8 and others even more so.

These findings underscore the necessity of incorporating spatial effects, particularly axial temperature
gradients, into both the interpretation of experimental IDTs and the validation of chemical kinetic
models. Sidewall imaging serves as a powerful diagnostic to identify non-idealities in ignition
behavior and supports more accurate determination of IDTs under both ideal and non-ideal ignition
conditions. Future work should aim to quantify axial gradients and integrate this understanding into
model validation and shock tube test design.

This study investigated the autoignition characteristics of stoichiometric H2/O2/Ar mixtures with
hydrogen mole fractions of 3% and 5% behind reflected shock waves. Ignition delay times (IDTs)
were measured using pressure transducers and compared with simulations using three detailed kinetic
mechanisms: FFCM-1, Aramco 2.0, and the San Diego mechanism in ANSYS CHEMKIN-PRO.

For the 3% H2 mixture, Aramco 2.0 exhibited good agreement with experimental data at higher
temperatures (T > 1060 K), while FFCM-1 was more accurate at lower temperatures. The San Diego
mechanism consistently over-predicted the IDTs across the entire temperature range. For the 5% H2
mixture, Aramco 2.0 still performed best but generally over-predicted IDTs by a factor of
approximately 1.5, while the other two mechanisms showed larger deviations.

High-speed endwall imaging revealed spatially uniform, volumetric ignition across the test section in
most cases, with no indications of wall-initiated hot spots or boundary layer ignition. Single ignition
kernel generally formed near the center of the cross-section and expanded radially, suggesting
predominantly homogeneous autoignition under the tested

conditions. However, ignition timing inferred from imaging was consistently earlier than pressure-
based IDTs measurements, due to the axial offset between the pressure transducer (1.5 inches
downstream of the endwall) and the actual ignition location.

Sidewall imaging revealed more nuanced ignition behavior. Two distinct ignition modes were observed
depending on mixture strength and test conditions. In several tests at higher temperatures, ignition was
first observed near the endwall, ignition front entering the side- wall imaging field of view from the
endwall. This indicated that ignition initiated closer to the endwall. In contrast, cases at lower
temperatures, showed initial light emission appearing within the sidewall field of view. This suggests
that the ignition kernel and front formed farther into the shock tube, with little or no upstream actions
before full ignition. These observations highlight a potential spatial dependence in ignition onset that
is not captured by 0-D modeling. When compared with simulations, sidewall imaging and measured
IDTs suggest that the ideal ignition onset location near the endwall is critical to estimating IDTs in this
study.

In summary, the combination of pressure measurements and high-speed imaging pro- vided a
comprehensive characterization of ignition behavior. The imaging revealed both homogeneous and
axial ignition onset locations, emphasizing the need for high-speed imaging diagnostics. For future
work, synchronized multi-angle imaging (e.g., simultaneous sidewall and endwall views) could
provide even more accurate mapping of the ignition front and further elucidate any residual
inhomogeneities.

Discussion of Endwall Imaging Analysis
The use of endwall OH* chemiluminescence imaging in these shock tube experiments pro- vides
valuable insight into the spatial and temporal characteristics of ammonia autoignition under reflected
shock conditions. While traditional diagnostics such as pressure transducers and photomultiplier tubes
yield precise IDTs at a fixed axial location, high-speed imaging reveals the full-field development of
ignition across the tube’s optical axis.
In both Case No. 1 and Case No. 2, the imaging sequences showed that ignition initiates as a single,
centralized flame kernel, followed by volumetric propagation. The absence of spatially localized hot-
spot ignition or circumferential wall-driven ignition strongly suggests that under the examined
conditions, ammonia-air mixtures ignite homogeneously. This behavior supports kinetic modeling
assumptions that presume spatial uniformity in ignition behavior and strengthens confidence in using 0-
D simulation tools such as CHEMKIN for IDTs prediction under well-mixed conditions. Additionally,
the discrepancies observed between the first visible ignition onset in the imaging and the pressure-
based ignition delay times emphasize the importance of diagnostic location. In Case No. 1, ignition
was observed to begin earlier than recorded by the pressure sensor, likely due to an axial ignition
location away from the sensor’s fixed position. In Case No. 2, where ignition occurred closer to the
sensor, the imaging and pressure-based IDTs aligned more closely. These findings underline that

endwall imaging complements conventional point-based diagnostics by capturing the global ignition
behavior and revealing potential biases introduced by spatial sensor placement. Moreover, the
qualitative flame structure observed in both cases offers direct validation of ignition homogeneity—a
crucial aspect in assessing the chemical reactivity and safety implications of alternative fuels such as
ammonia. Homogeneous ignition behavior suggests minimal influence of local thermal gradients or
wall-heat under the tested conditions, which is a favorable trait for reproducible and predictable
combustion performance.
Overall, endwall imaging not only enhances understanding of ignition structure but also provides
critical spatial context for interpreting IDTs. This highlights the necessity of integrating multi-modal
diagnostics—including sidewall imaging—to fully characterize ignition phenomena in future high-
fidelity studies.

Downstream-Initiated Ignition

In contrast, Cases NO. 3 and 4 ( Table 1.7) represent ignition events that initiated further downstream
from the endwall. These tests were conducted in the lower temperature regime, where T5 < 1500 K.
For Case No. 4, a T5 of 1486.7 K and an IDTs of 854 µs were recorded shown in Figure 1.69, while
the Konnov-2009 model predicted an IDTs of 900 µs. The sidewall OH* imaging (Figure 1.70)
revealed the initial appearance of localized flame kernel formation at 840 µs within the window, with
no evidence of an incoming ignition front from the endwall. The ignition then rapidly developed into
a broader front propagating downstream. This suggests that ignition initiates in the sidewall window
viewing area, at a position away from the endwall.
Case No. 3 presented a more extreme example of downstream ignition. At a T5 of 1450 K, the
measured IDTs was 1696 µs shown in Figure 5.14, while the model prediction was 1530 µs. OH*
imaging (Figure 4.15) revealed that flame kernels entered the sidewall window field of view from the
downstream side, approximately 4.17 inches from the end- wall, at 1645 µs. These kernels propagated
upstream towards the endwall, confirming that ignition had initiated well downstream.
In both cases, sidewall imaging detected ignition signatures earlier than the pressure transducer near
the endwall. This temporal mismatch, also seen in endwall imaging results, highlights the limitations
of relying solely on endwall pressure signals to characterize ignition location and timing when ignition
occurs away from the endwall. It is typically assumed in shock tube studies that the region adjacent
to the endwall experiences the earliest and longest post-reflected shock temperatures and, therefore, is
the most likely the location for ignition onset under uniform conditions. This zero-dimensional
approximation presumes that the post-reflected shock region is isothermal and spatially homogeneous.
However, the downstream ignition observed in Cases 3 and 4 indicates that this assumption may not
hold under lower-temperature conditions. As the reflected shock propagates downstream from the
endwall, its strength weakens, creating a temperature gradient along the axial direction. This gradient
can enable earlier ignition downstream. In such cases, a one-dimensional interpretation of the thermal
field behind the reflected shock is necessary to accurately describe ignition behavior. Although a one-
dimensional axial temperature gradient was present and influenced the downstream-initiated ignition
cases, the measured ignition delay times (IDTs) still showed good agreement with Chemkin simulation
results. This observation suggests that further refinement of the Konnov-2009 modeling framework
may be necessary under the lower temperature regime where stronger gradients or multidimensional
effects become significant.

Discussion of Sidewall Imaging Analysis
The integration of high-speed OH* chemiluminescence sidewall imaging in these shock tube
experiments provided valuable spatial and temporal resolution of ignition events, offering insights
not accessible through traditional pressure measurements alone. The distinction between endwall-
initiated and downstream-initiated ignition modes observed in this study reveals the importance of
accounting for non-uniformities in post-reflected shock conditions, particularly at lower
temperatures.
For the high-temperature cases (Cases 5–7), ignition was clearly initiated near the end- wall and
propagated downstream, in agreement with the classical assumption of uniform thermodynamic
conditions behind the reflected shock. These cases exhibited strong ignition fronts and
synchronization between the pressure-based IDTs and imaging-based ignition onset, reaffirming
the validity of zero-dimensional modeling assumptions under such conditions. The strong
agreement with Chemkin predictions using the Konnov-2009 mechanism further reinforces the
reliability of both the experimental setup and the kinetic model in this temperature regime.

In contrast, the lower-temperature cases (Cases 3–4) demonstrated that ignition can occur well
downstream from the endwall, where post-shock conditions away from the endwall are weaker due
to shock attenuation. These observations challenge the traditional assumption of spatial uniformity
behind the reflected shock wave and highlight the presence of a one-dimensional axial temperature
gradient. The formation of flame kernels within or entering the sidewall imaging field of view from
the downstream side, combined with delayed pressure signatures, confirms the necessity of spatially
resolved diagnostics for accurate ignition characterization in such regimes. Despite these non-
uniform conditions, the measured IDTs still exhibited good agreement with zero-dimensional
Chemkin simulations. This suggests that, while global IDTs remain useful for model validation,
localized lower temperature region may deviate from zero-dimensional assumptions. Future
kinetic modeling efforts may benefit from incorporating one-dimensional or multi-zone modeling
frameworks, especially for low-temperature ignition regimes or fuel-oxidizer mixtures with weak
reactivity.
Overall, sidewall imaging proves to be a powerful diagnostic tool for resolving ignition phenomena
in shock tubes. It enables identification of ignition onset location, reveals ignition front
propagation characteristics, and provides critical context for interpreting IDTs measurements.
These insights underscore the value of combining traditional pressure measurements with advanced
optical diagnostics to improve the fidelity of experimental and computational studies of
combustion kinetics.
Conclusion of imaging study
This study investigated the autoignition behavior of stoichiometric ammonia/oxygen/argon
mixtures behind reflected shock waves using a combination of pressure-based diagnostics and
high-speed OH* chemiluminescence imaging. The primary objectives were to characterize
ignition delay times (IDTs), evaluate the predictive capability of chemical kinetic mechanisms,
and gain spatial insight into ignition structure using imaging techniques.
Ignition delay time measurements for 10% NH3/7.5% O2/82.5% Ar mixtures at pressures of 5–6
atm and post-reflected shock temperatures between 1450–1650 K were found to be in good
agreement with Chemkin simulations using the Konnov-2009 mechanism. In contrast, other
mechanisms such as Mevel-2009 and Manna-2023 underpredicted IDTs across the tested
conditions.
High-speed endwall and sidewall OH* imaging provided critical spatiotemporal insights into the
ignition processes. Endwall imaging revealed homogeneous ignition characterized by capturing
single central flame kernel formation followed by volumetric propagation. The timing
discrepancies between imaging and pressure-based diagnostics highlighted the importance of
spatial sensor location and the complementary role of sidewall visualization.
Sidewall imaging exposed two distinct ignition modes: endwall-initiated ignition at higher
temperatures and downstream-initiated ignition at lower temperatures. In particular, downstream
ignition suggested the presence of axial temperature gradients behind the reflected shock wave,
challenging the classical assumption of isothermal and spatially uniform post-shock 0-D
conditions. Despite this, the experimentally measured IDTs still correlated well with simulation
predictions, implying robustness in the Konnov-2009 model while also indicating the need for
future 1-D or multidimensional modeling at lower temperatures.

Overall, this work demonstrates the utility of integrating imaging diagnostics with conventional
pressure-based measurements for comprehensive ignition analysis. The imaging results confirm
the homogeneous ignition nature of ammonia mixtures under certain conditions and reveal
complex spatial behavior at lower temperatures. These findings have important implications for
the use of ammonia as a carbon-free fuel and suggest directions for further refinement of kinetic
models and experimental diagnostics.

2. Investigation of turbulent flame speeds of high hydrogen fuels at high turbulence levels
The turbulent flame speeds of high hydrogen blended natural gas fuels are measured at gas turbine
relevant conditions. Turbulent flame speed influences combustor operability, including blowoff
and flashback limits, as well as combustion instability conditions (through its influence on flame
length). Turbulence modifies the average conversion rate from premixed reactants to combustion
products, a parameter measured by the turbulent global consumption speed (ST,GC). The objective
has been to develop a universal correlation for 𝑆𝑆𝑇𝑇 under varying operating conditions, enabling
prediction in regimes where direct measurements are not available. Several correlations in the
literature claim such predictive capability. Accordingly, the primary goal for this quarter was to
evaluate the performance of these existing 𝑆𝑆𝑇𝑇 correlations against our turbulent flame speed
dataset, hereafter referred to as the Georgia Tech database.
The goal of this task is to experimentally measure the turbulent flame speeds for a wide range of
H2 containing fuel blends. Data were taken at pressures up to 30 bar and preheat temperature of
800 K including H2/CH4 blends and H2/NH3 blends.
The planned experiments “slice” through the multi-dimensional test matrix of fuel composition,
operating condition, and equivalence ratio with the following test approaches. The primary focus
was on implementing changes to the experimental facility as well as fixing some existing issues
pertaining to the experimental rig which include changes to the turbulence generator system,
ignition system and the burner design. The test matrix for the studies have also been formulated.
The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 2.1.

The new design of the turbulence generator system comprises of a worm gear assembly with the
worm wheel supported on the main shaft on which the slotted turbulence generator plate is
mounted and the worm is supported on an auxiliary shaft orthogonal to the main shaft. The gear
assembly is actuated by a stepper motor which in turn rotates the shaft and the plate mounted on
it. Variation in turbulence intensity is enabled by the varying blockage ratio between the rotating
plate mounted on the shaft and the stationary plate bolted down to the cylindrical plenum. Slots
for roller bearings are made on the plate to reduce friction between the plates. A rotary shaft seal
between the rotating shaft and the stationary gear box plates have been installed to reduce the leak
that was previously encountered in the rig. This configuration prevents slip between the gears and
ensures minimal friction between the components.

reflect lack of quasi-steadiness in the internal flame structure, due to the rapidly varying stretch
rate, and that the instantaneous burning velocity of the turbulent flame leading edge would be

lower under said non-quasi-steady conditions. They also proposed a two-parameter model to
capture these time scale effects:

 The remainder of the section is dedicated to testing the hypothesis stated above. The key idea
is the following – rather than trying to determine SL,ref from physical considerations, we empirically
determine what flame speed value each data set must be normalized by to collapse them into a
single line. As such, we invoke the following procedure:
 1) Determine a reference data set, that all other measurement points will be collapsed to. We
 must assume a reference flame speed for this dataset
 2) Determine SL,ref empirically that iteratively minimizes least square residual of ST,GC/SL,ref as
 a function of urms/SL,ref against this reference data set at every test condition over the
 measured turbulence intensity range.
Note that this process requires assuming an SL,ref value for the reference data set, which is actually
an unknown quantity. As such, we repeated this process twice, using two different reference data
sets to determine if the results were consistent. We describe the first exercise of this process in
detail below, and then summarize the results of the second trial.
 We selected the reference test condition as H2= 90%, Pc=8 bar, Tin=340K, U0=20 and
φ=0.49, and assumed that SL,ref=SL,max was the reference value for this condition. This data set was
chosen as it was one of the highest stretch sensitive cases, and that the high pressure would cause
the fastest time response to the temporally evolving stretch rate. The blue line in Figure 2.23 is a
least-squares best fit through this reference data set, that all other conditions across all datasets
were normalized to. All the other data points shown in the figure are the normalized burning

velocity values from all four datasets. Note that, by construction, all the data points are clustered
around the blue line as the SL,ref value was calculated using an unconstrained minimization routine
to minimize the L2 residual of the data from this reference line.

determined to collapse data from all four datasets onto reference data set, shown by blue line
 Figure 2.23 is the key output of this procedure, showing a histogram of the empirically
determined SL,ref value needed to collapse these data. It indicates that there exist a large number
of conditions where parameter SL,ref>>SL,max(~54% of the data sets) . Less obvious because of the
scale is that there are also data sets where SL,ref <<SL,0 (~5% of the data). All told, only about 40%
of the data falls within the inequality shown in Eq (1.2.5) which contradicts the proposed
hypothesis.
 As noted above, this procedure requires assigning a known SL,ref value to the reference data
set, which is itself unknown. As such, we repeated this process using a different reference data
set, in this case, choosing the least stretch sensitive result, with relatively slow kinetic response to
time varying stretch rates. For this case, pure CH4, Pc=2 bar, Tin=300K and φ=0.74 was chosen
as the reference dataset and the procedure for calculating SL,ref was repeated as above. Here, it was
found that only 54% of the calculated SL,ref falls within the limits mentioned in Eqn (1.2.5).
Moreover, for this case, 32% of the SL,ref values exceeded SL,max and about 14% of SL,ref values lie
below SL,0.

and pressure effects through a reference flame speed alone is insufficient. In other words, we
believe the attempt to capture pressure effects through a time scale ratio as proposed in our prior
publication is incorrect. Rather, it appears that the SL,ref approach is useful for capturing fuel
composition sensitivities at a given set of thermodynamic conditions. The next section presents
some additional ideas and hypotheses for capturing pressure effects.

Feature selection is a critical step in developing machine learning models, especially when dealing
with high-dimensional datasets. Input features (Xi) are evaluated and only the most relevant
features for prediction of the target variable (Y) are retained. Three classes of feature selection
algorithms were employed to build the 𝑆𝑆𝑇𝑇 model: (1) Filter methods (e.g. Pearson’s coefficient, F-
test, mutual information): These techniques assess the statistical relationship between individual
features and the target variable and are model-agnostic. (2) Wrapper methods (e.g. forward
selection and backward elimination): These involve iterative training of models using different
subsets of features. Features are added or removed based on their contribution to model
performance. (3) Implicit/Embedded methods (e.g. Gini/permutation importance using random
forests [50]): These integrate feature selection into the model training process by evaluating the
contribution of each feature to the model’s predictive performance. In this study, a combination of
feature selection strategies is used to identify the most informative features influencing 𝑆𝑆𝑇𝑇 . Figure

Measurements of NOx and CO emissions from turbulent hydrogen fueled flames were performed
in this quarter. Hydrogen, at the same equivalence ratio, burns at a higher flame temperature and
exhibits nearly four times the unstretched laminar flame speed compared to methane. This poses
challenges for flame stability in premixed low NOx combustors. In addition to the higher
diffusivity of hydrogen, the highly turbulent environment in which these combustors operate
creates local hotspots with compositions that differ from the average reactant mixture. These
hotspots can reach temperatures much higher than the average flame temperature expected based
on the mixture- averaged equivalence ratio. As a result, this can lead to increased levels of thermal
NOx from lean, high hydrogen flames as compared to what would otherwise be expected from
these mixtures. Therefore, it is vital to understand the impact of flow turbulence on emissions from
high hydrogen blended flames. In this study, single-point emissions measurements were conducted
on turbulent premixed hydrogen-enriched methane flames from a contoured nozzle Bunsen burner
with variable turbulence. The influence of turbulence on NOx and CO emissions was investigated
under two key conditions: (a) constant flame temperature and (b) constant unstretched laminar
flame speed. Emissions were measured at varying residence times and incoming flow turbulence
intensities. Samples are dried sufficiently with an inline drier to obtain dried emissions
measurements.
 Post flame residence time computation is crucial for comparison of emissions results across
different cases. With increasing turbulence intensity in the system, the flame height decreases as
the flame propagation speed increases. This leads to variation in post flame residence time with
increasing turbulence intensity. To account for this variation, flame height is captured for all cases
using OH* Chemiluminescence and the post flame residence time of the emissions measurements
are corrected using the computed flame height. Finally, residence time τres is calculated as:
