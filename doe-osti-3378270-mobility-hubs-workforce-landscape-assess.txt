 Background
 Equiticity is a nonprofit organization based in the North Lawndale neighborhood of Chicago,
 Illinois focused on community mobility. Equiticity applied to E2C Expert Match to request
 technical assistance in building out future workforce development opportunities as part of their
 development of community mobility hubs. This includes aligning their efforts with local and
 state government agencies and regional transportation planning efforts.

 This report responds to Equiticity’s request for technical assistance in building out future
 workforce development opportunities and aligning their efforts with local and state government
 agencies and regional transportation planning efforts. It is divided into two scopes: (1)
 workforce development (WFD) opportunities, and (2) local transportation planning and
 workforce landscape assessment.

 Within the WFD scope, Task 1 is composed of a review of skills offered by Equiticity’s bike-
 based WFD training program, and associated career pathways and relevant programs for long-
 term career opportunities. Task 2 covers information about relevant certifications and trainings,
 as well as the process for obtaining certifications or providing credits.

 Within the local transportation planning and workforce landscape assessment scope, Task 3 is to
 identify relevant local transportation planning efforts and Task 4 is to identify relevant workforce
 development programs. Tasks 3 and 4 were developed separately by the Illinois Alliance for
 Clean Transportation and are not included here.

 1.2 Skills Needed for the Broader Local Transportation Sector
 Skills and mindset obtained from the GoHub programs are likely to be transferred to broader
 local transportation sector. For purposes of discussing career paths and relevant skills, the
 broader local transportation sector is categorized generally to include:

 • Micromobility vehicles
 • Transit, medium-/heavy-duty, and light-duty vehicles
 • Electrical systems.

 1.2.1 Micromobility
 Micromobility is experiencing rapid growth in the United States and globally, enabled by the
 advent of electrified two, three, and even four-wheeled devices. Chicago is no exception, where
 the Divvy bikeshare system has been a leader in e-bikeshare technology adoption, most notably
 through electrification of their docking stations to avoid the need for time, energy, and labor
 intensive battery swapping (Greenfield 2024). E-bike delivery is also becoming more common in
 the city, expanding beyond small courier services to third-party-app-enabled deliveries by
 individuals using personally-owned e-bikes or leasing e-bikes from companies like Whizz
 (Bolton 2025) and Joco (Bellan 2024) that now have a presence in Chicago.

   1.2.1.1 Bike riding
 Delivery service by bike is a long-standing employment sector, at least dating back to Western
 Union’s use of bike couriers in the 1890s, that is easy to enter with bike- riding skills. It is
 estimated that Chicago had 1,500 bike couriers in the 1980s (Struett 2025). While the count of
 bike messengers working for courier businesses has declined significantly (~ 50 people in 2025),
 reporting shows that the quality of the jobs has improved, including, for example, employee
 ownership at Cut Cats Courier and payroll jobs instead of contract work.

 6

 Delivery work using both e-bikes and electric cargo bikes is likely to increase due to the shift in
 the U.S. towards microfreight in urban areas (Rula et al., 2025), which uses micromobility
 vehicles (e.g., cargo e-bikes) to deliver goods. This includes the newer growth in grocery and
 restaurant deliveries, as well as larger volumes and heavier package delivery, for example
 through UPS, FedEx, and Amazon.

 1.2.1.2 Bike maintenance and fleet management
 The traditional work of bike shops, providing maintenance services and sales, will continue, but
 expertise in e-bike technology has become important given that e-bikes accounted for about 30%
 of bicycle market share in the U.S. in 2024. As with any vehicle, bike maintenance is an
 important part of micromobility fleet management. As e-bike delivery and microfreight delivery
 becomes more common, experts may be needed who can manage these fleets, including
 designing or selecting safe charging systems and using software and other tools for larger fleets
 (e.g., for routing, scheduling, etc.).

 1.2.1.3 Entrepreneurial opportunities
 With the growth in e-bikes and microfreight, opportunities for entrepreneurship may increase. A
 wide variety of work has been created using bikes, and that will continue— everything from bike
 tours to independent bikeshare (e.g., Queens Bike) to meeting the needs for fleets of cargo bikes
 (Net Zero Logistics n.d.). Needs for battery recycling (Call2Recycle n.d.) and reuse will present
 additional opportunities for entrepreneurship, though fire risks and life safety concerns are
 paramount.

 1.2.2 Transit, medium/heavy duty, and light-duty vehicles
 The Chicago Transit Authority (CTA) has approximately 2,000 buses and more than 1,500 rail
 cars (CTA 2025). CTA’s “Charging Forward” plan maps out a path to achieve conversion to
 100% electric buses by 2040 (CTA n.d.-a). For current CTA bus mechanic positions (CTA n.d.-
 b), skill requirements include those relevant to motor vehicle engines and transmissions, as well
 as several that can be fostered through other experiences such as bike-based WFD efforts,
 including:
 ● Good oral and written communications skills
 ● Basic math and computer skills
 ● Ability to read, interpret, and understand diagrams, schematics, blueprints, sketches,
 bulletins, and procedures
 ● Ability to use pertinent test equipment including computers, gauges, and various types of
 handheld, mechanical and electronic testing or diagnostic equipment.

 These same skills that the CTA highlights are relevant for work across the vehicle spectrum,
 from light duty to heavy duty.

 1.2.3 Electrical systems
 For all classes of motor vehicles, from light duty to medium/heavy duty, the transition to EVs
 will continue to create demand for electrical expertise. Electrical expertise can also be a pathway
 to a career as an electrician. To foster exposure to electronic systems, bike-based WFD
 curriculum can be designed to include experience with tools and resources such as:
 ● Multi-meters

 7

 o Can be used to measure voltage, resistance, and current
 ● Oscilloscopes
 o Display voltage over time for diagnostic purposes or other analysis
 ● Controller area network (CAN) bus systems
 o CAN bus is a communication system for electronics to manage data flows and
 system operations. Now commonly used in motor vehicles, this technology was
 originally used in shifting systems for high-end analogue bicycles and is also
 beginning to appear in cargo and high-end e-bikes.
 ▪ For example, Shimano’s Di2 electronic shifting dates back to 2009.
 ▪ For e-bikes, especially cargo bikes, it can be used for seamless integration
 of torque control, battery monitoring, GPS tracking, etc.
 o Modern CAN bus systems in e-bikes are increasingly aligned with safety
 standards (Freya 2025).
 ● Relevant standards
 o Familiarity with these standards could be beneficial for anyone working in the e-
 mobility space. These standards categories include but are not limited to:
 ▪ UL 2849 (e-bike electrical safety)
 ▪ UL 2271 (lithium-ion battery management systems, communications, and
 protection)
 ▪ UL 2272 (other powered mobility devices, including e-scooters)
 ▪ ISO 11898 (CAN protocol standard)
 More in-depth information can be found in the U.S. Consumer Product Safety
 Commission’s 2025 draft “Safety Standard for Lithium-Ion Batteries Used in
 Micromobility Products and Electrical Systems of Micromobility Products Containing
 Such Batteries.” Understanding standards also requires knowledge of e-bike and battery
 components, for example that batteries are composed of cells and the cells and batteries
 are treated independently in the standard certification process (e.g., the battery is UL
 certified but the individual cells are not). Similarly, the charging equipment are treated
 independently.
 ● Electrical training
 o Technical training (Benjamin 2025)
 ▪ System fundamentals of batteries and electric motors
 ▪ Software training to understand proprietary systems such as error codes,
 updating the software, etc.
 o Electrical safety - especially relevant to mechanical work on motor vehicles and
 includes but is not limited to:
 ▪ Safe practices and protocols for both low and high voltage (e-bikes, while
 typically lower, can be 72 V or more).
 ▪ Appropriate personal protective equipment for working with batteries and
 electrical systems
 ▪ Fire safety and hazard response in the event of a damaged or defective
 battery.
 o Circuits
 ▪ Direct current (DC) circuits, wiring diagrams, and troubleshooting
 electrical problems are relevant for working with batteries in e-bikes and
 EVs.

 1.3 Transferability of Skills Developed in BikeForce Curriculum to
 Other Career Pathways Beyond Transportation
 The BikeForce curriculum and future GoHub bike-based training programs will continue to
 cover intermediate to advanced bike mechanic skills. Details about those skills are provided in
 Equiticity’s BikeForce curriculum, and the degree to which advanced bike mechanic skills may
 be covered in the GoHub bike-based WFD program may be determined at a later time. The
 description here is of skills that can be fostered in bike-based training programs and have
 relevance to other career pathways beyond those previously discussed specific to transportation.
 For three skill types (mechanical aptitude, diagnosis/problem-solving, and soft skills), three
 items are provided: a description, a discussion of how Equiticity’s WFD can foster the skill, and
 examples of career paths where the skill is transferable or foundational. For some example
 careers, the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) data is provided in the following section,
 indicating the projected trend in job growth. Additionally, consideration is given to how these
 skills are relevant and transferable to the broader green energy economy.

 Figure 6. Equiticity’s BikeForce workshop

 1.3.1 Skill: Mechanical aptitude
 Mechanical aptitude involves understanding how systems work (e.g., a bicycle
 drivetrain/transmission, etc.) and breaking it down into components for maintenance and repair,
 including preventative maintenance. Such skills are applicable to most transportation
 technologies, including cars, motorcycles, buses and trains. In addition, familiarity with tools and
 their maintenance is relevant to any job involving hands-on work, such as carpentry, automotive

 9

 repair, or construction. Increasingly, electronic systems and software are part of mechanical
 work, such as auto mechanics working with onboard diagnostics systems.

 How can Equiticity’s WFD programs continue fostering mechanical aptitude?
 Given the trend in electrification of transportation, increasing familiarity with electronic systems
 and batteries could be valuable training. Ensuring participants have holistic knowledge about the
 parts of the bike as mechanical systems (e.g., the bicycle drivetrain), and highlighting parallels
 elsewhere (e.g., a motor vehicle drivetrain) could be beneficial.

 Examples of career paths where these skills are transferable:
 ● Construction and extraction skilled trades (e.g., electrician, plumber, construction,
 welding, etc.)
 o Projected to grow at a rate much faster than average of all sectors through 2033
 (BLS 2025)
 ● Architecture and engineering
 o Projected to grow at a rate much faster than average of all sectors through 2033
                 (BLS 2025).

 1.3.2 Skill: Diagnosis and problem-solving
 Bike mechanics develop problem-solving skills by observing, testing, and troubleshooting, doing
 so with precision and attention to detail. These analytical skills, including the use of specialized
 tools and diagnostic equipment and the ability to apply skills across different brands, models, and
 technologies (e.g., bikes vs. e-bikes), are transferable to many fields. Diagnostic skills are also
 essential for mechanics across the transportation industry, enabling them to identify problems in
 engines, transmissions, and electrical systems.

 How can Equiticity’s WFD programs continue fostering diagnosis and problem-solving skills?
 Diagnostic and problem-solving skills could be fostered in Equiticity’s WFD programs through
 exercises such as mystery repair challenges (i.e., having to repair a bike with an unusual and not
 obvious problem, and no input from instructors) or a race to fix flat tires.

 1.3.3 Skill: Soft skills that are relevant across almost any job or career path
 These include a variety of valuable skills such as interpersonal and other communication skills,
 organizational and time management skills, and teamwork and collaboration.

 How can Equiticity’s WFD programs continue fostering soft skills development?
 ● Organizational and time management skills, such as managing workloads and managing
 inventory, could be fostered through systems of project planning, record keeping, and
 tracking the tasks performed.
 ● Interpersonal and other communication skills could be fostered through respectful and
 productive team dynamics and having experiences interacting with members of the
 public.
 o For Equiticity WFD participants, this may be gained through describing their
 experiences in the program, for example in a public speaking exercise or through
 writing about the experience (e.g., for a blog post or newspaper).
 o The Equiticity BikeForce curriculum has an emphasis on interpersonal dynamics
 and collaboration that can be built on in other bike-based WFD efforts.

 1.3.4 Skills for the broader energy sector

 With GoHubs incorporating solar and geothermal energy and building energy efficiency into its
 own buildings, additional opportunities emerge to give participants foundational skills for
 working in the energy sector. Across all three of these areas, the Occupational Safety and Health
 Administration (OSHA)’s 10-hour construction safety training (often referred to as an OSHA 10
 card or OSHA-10 certification) may be a required prerequisite for involvement in any onsite
   work (OSHA 2025).

 While each of these sectors requires in-depth training and certification (see Task 2: Relevant
 certifications and relevant processes: energy), one possibility for the GoHubs is to develop a
 curriculum providing orientation to each of the three energy areas and interactions with energy
 professionals, so that participants can make informed decisions about which technologies and
 skill sets are of most interest for them to pursue further. General overview topics are provided

 11

 below, and more information about certifications is provided in Task 2: Relevant certifications
 and relevant processes: energy.

 1.3.4.1 Solar energy
 Basic exposure to electrical systems is a good foundation for solar photovoltaic (PV) work, as is
 an introduction to installation and safety specific to PV panels.

 1.3.4.2 Geothermal energy

 The most common geothermal technology relies on ground source heat pumps to support both
 the heating and cooling of buildings. Understanding the mechanics, loop design, and plumbing
 specific to geothermal wells is a good introduction to this technology.

 1.3.4.3 Building energy efficiency
 Improving energy efficiency of buildings can be considered under the umbrella of “building
 science.” This is a topic focused on understanding how air, moisture, and heat move through and
 interact with a structure, and an orientation to these concepts is a good foundation for energy
 efficiency work.

 2.2 Relevant Certifications and Initiatives in Chicago and Illinois:
 Energy
 For OSHA workplace safety training, including the 10-hour construction safety course, the
 Construction Safety Council is Chicago’s authorized OSHA Training Institute (OTI) Education
 Center.

 Additional training and certifications are offered by Chicago-area programs identified by Anne
 Schneider of the Illinois Alliance for Clean Transportation. Those programs and several others
 are described below.

 Passed in 2021, the Illinois Climate and Equitable Jobs Act (CEJA) created at least seven
 programs for workforce development by 2025, including CEJA Workforce Hubs and Climate
 Works Pre-Apprenticeship Centers (Illinois Department of Commerce and Economic
 Opportunity n.d.).

 CEJA Workforce Hubs are centers designated by the Illinois Department of Commerce
 and Economic Opportunity for free training under the Illinois CEJA law, providing youth
 with a Bridge Program for foundational skills followed by job-specific training.
 Workforce Hubs must offer at least two job-specific training options.

 CEJA Workforce Hub job-specific training options that cover the GoHub technology
 areas include: solar PV training, energy auditor training, weatherization training, HVAC
 training, and auto mechanic with hybrid/EV focus.

 Two CEJA Workforce Hubs are planned for Chicago (one serving the South Side and one
 serving the Southwest and West Sides). The administrator for Chicago CEJA Hubs is the
 Chicago Cook Workforce Partnership. One member of the partnership is the Calumet
 Area Industrial Commission, which has a South Side hub focused on industrial green
 skills and solar energy. Another is the Association House of Chicago, which specializes
 in the Bridge Program, a Solar PV Installer Training (including a North American Board
 of Certified Energy Practitioners [NABCEP] credential), and the National Center for

 14

 Construction Education and Research (NCCER) Core Curriculum. Others include
 Revolution Workshop, the Greater West Town Project, and OAI, Inc.

 As of late 2025, the Climate Works Pre-Apprenticeship Program has designated the 548
 Foundation as a training provider in Chicago. They offer renewable energy career training
 aligned with NCCER standards including NCCER Core, Solar, and Energy Auditor. Their
 training programs also offer OSHA 30-hour training and NABCEP credentials. Programs are 13
 weeks, offering stipends and wrap-around services for participants.

 Several statewide initiatives exist separately from CEJA. The Illinois Power Authority has a
 Long-Term Renewable Resources Procurement Plan that includes the Illinois Solar for All
 Energy Workforce Equity programs. The Illinois Green Alliance manages the Building Energy
 Hub for decarbonization and efficiency resources. The state of Illinois maintains an Energy
 Workforce Equity Portal, a database for finding graduates of vetted green energy programs as
 well as current job openings.

 Locally, and also separate from CEJA, Powering Chicago is a collaboration between
 International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers (IBEW) Local 134 and National Electrical
 Contractors Association for unionized green energy apprenticeships. IBEW offers other
 programs through their Renewable Energy Fund. The city of Chicago runs the Greencorps
 Chicago program, which provides introductory certifications such as first aid, CPR, and OSHA
 10. Elevate, a nonprofit based in Chicago, also offers programs that include OSHA 10 and
 NABCEP PV Associate certification.

 Geothermal-specific workforce programs are less common. One example is the Grow Geo
 Chicagoland organization, which is a local organization dedicated to education and increasing
 expertise in geothermal technology.

 An inventory of national programs and those beyond Illinois is provided in the December 2024
 DOE and U.S. Environmental Protection Agency publication A Report on Actions for Medium-
 and Heavy-Duty Vehicle Energy and Emissions Innovation (U.S. Department of Energy and U.S.
 Environmental Protection Agency, 2024).

 2.3 Options for Equiticity’s Workforce Training and Certification
 To explore options for certification, connecting with LEVA would be a logical step to explore
 whether their long-standing certification process might be appropriate for Equiticity’s bike-based
 WFD efforts, or could be tailored for this purpose through a collaboration.

 Equiticity could also explore offering their own certification for graduates of BikeForce and
 future WFD programs.

 With respect to the green energy sector, Equiticity’s efforts may benefit from existing and future
 connections to the many existing programs described in the previous section to determine ways
 that partnerships or new program development can meaningfully increase workforce preparation
 for GoHub participants.

 Any effort to invest in certification would benefit from market research to understand the
 potential value of such a certification in the local region. Relevant stakeholders who could
 provide unique insights include existing industry partners, potential employers of graduates, and
 former BikeForce participants.
