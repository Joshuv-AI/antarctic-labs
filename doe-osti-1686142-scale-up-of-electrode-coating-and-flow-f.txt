 Abstract of CRADA Work:
 Hydrogen peroxide is currently produced at central chemical plants via the anthraquinone
 oxidation process. This process produces environmental pollutants that are costly to remediate,
 requires hazardous long-distance shipping of highly concentrated peroxide (50% or 70%), and
 necessitates extra handling costs related to storage and dilution. Peroxygen Systems, Inc. (PSi) is
 developing breakthrough technology for on-site hydrogen peroxide production. PSi’s on-site on-
 demand electrolyzer can reduce the cost of producing hydrogen peroxide by 50%, while also
 completely eliminating the cost and safety issues associated with shipping and handling of high
 concentration hydrogen peroxide. The challenge for PSi is scaling. To support the next step

 1

 toward commercialization (customer pilot tests), scaling the prototype into larger single cells and
 20-40 cell stacks is required. In addition to internal hardware and flow-field design efforts at PSi,
 NREL will address three critical problems for this scale-up effort: (1) demonstrating a large scale
 roll-to-roll (R2R) process to coat uniform electrode materials for 100 cm2 and 500 cm2 stack
 testing, (2) demonstrating an in-line diagnostic to achieve better electrode quality control, and (3)
 performing in situ cell/stack testing to better understand and optimize the performance of the
 flow field design.

 Summary of Research Results:

 process. Includes formulation and process conditions for PTFE pretreatment on electrode

 B. Provide slot-die coated material to PSi for testing (Complete 1 month after subtask A)

 Results for Task 1:
 To address ongoing challenges at PSi with respect to the stability and adhesion of their electrode
 ink, we performed a detailed assessment of polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) dispersion and stability
 behaviors in various formulations using rheology, as seen in Figure 1. We identified that the PTFE
 dispersion is most stable in a totally aqueous solvent, rather than the majority alcohol solvent that
 was being used by PSi. As a result, we were able to formulate inks using a surfactant, TritonX,
 with stable PTFE content and with suitable rheology for the slot die coating intended to be used by
 Kodak (Eastman Business Park, EBP), with whom PSi is partnering for pilot-scale production. As
 shown in Figure 1, the inks reformulated with surfactant and higher active material solids (4 & 4.5
 weight%) had higher viscosity (though about the same shear thinning behavior) than the original
 formulations from PSi. The dotted line data on the graph shows the target viscosity behavior
 identified by EBP for coating on their pilot line. [Addresses Task 1A]

 2

 Figure 1. Modified electrode inks, adjusting solvent and active material solids weight percent; the
 4.5 wt% active material ink meets the target viscosity behavior for slot die coating.
 Based on this initial ink formulation study, we performed lab-scale coating of the various active
 material wt% samples and observed vastly different levels of penetration of the ink into the
 electrode substrate. Cross-sectional microscopy was performed to evidence this behavior (see
 example in Figure 10 below, under Task 2), and substrates coated across this range of active
 material wt% were also provided to PSi for in situ testing. [Addresses Task 1A]

 Even with the reformulations described above, the electrode inks still exhibited excessive
 foaming during mixing. To address this, a lower surfactant ratio (~1:1) was tried, and foaming
 during mixing was reduced. Using this new ink, electrodes were Mayer-rod coated and sent to
 PSi for in situ testing. The results at PSi showed comparable in situ performance to their targets.

 Given the positive electrochemical performance result from the Mayer-rod coated samples, we
 utilized our pilot-scale R2R coating line to coat several feet of electrode onto the untreated
 electrode substrate at the solids target loading of 16 g/m2 (calculated via mass balance, not
 measured). We attempted both gravure and slot die techniques and found the ink to be too
 viscous for gravure coating. However, slot-die coating was very successful, resulting in a
 visually uniform coating. Our reformulated ink performed very well with respect to coatability,
 as seen below in Figure 2. [Addresses Tasks 1A-C]

 Figure 2. Initial NREL slot die coated electrode (black coating) on untreated electrode substrate
 (lighter gray substrate) using reformulated aqueous ink.
 With this initial success on the untreated electrode substrate, we decided to slot-die coat several
 meters of electrode onto the 20 cm-wide PTFE-treated substrate – the target substrate – as shown
 3

 in Figure 3. We used the reformulated aqueous ink at various active material loadings for the run.
 The coating was successful under all coating conditions (web speeds + flow rates). We provided
 these coated materials to PSi for cell fabrication and in situ testing. Testing results showed good
 performance of the NREL-coated electrodes. Sections of the coated materials of varying loadings
 were kept at NREL and provided to the QC team for their efforts. [Addresses Tasks 1A&B]

 Figure 3. NREL slot die coating of reformulated aqueous ink onto 20 cm-wide PTFE-treated
 electrode substrate.
 With these positive coating and performance results, PSi tested our ink formulation during their
 next coating run at EBP. PSi found limited success during this pilot coating run, however, due to
 mixing and agglomeration issues encountered when dealing with much larger volumes of ink
 than dealt with at NREL. When mixing NREL’s ink formulation, using a multi-shaft Meyers mill
 with a high-shear cowles blade and co-axial low-rpm concentric paddles, PSi found that the ink
 formed a frothy foam like gel which prevented mixing flow from occurring. This was not an
 issue during lab scale testing at NREL using an ultra-Turrax mixer (homogenizer), likely due to
 the relatively small ink volume compared to the mixing head interaction volume. The
 foaming/gelation experienced at EBP resulted in excess frictional heating of the ink (to near
 boiling temperatures) and inhomogeneous mixing quality throughout the batch volume. This
 resulted in the formation of large agglomerates in the ink which repeatedly clogged the slot die.
 [Addresses Task 1A]

 Based on this learning experience, we purchased a particle size measurement gauge (based on
 the fineness of grind, or FOG, technique) to understand agglomeration in the PSi inks as a
 function of formulation and mixing. In addition, we clearly documented our mixing processes,
 including images and videos as well as viscosity and particle size measurements, at each stage of
 the process, and provided this information to PSi for a detailed comparison to ink handling
 methods used at EBP. A key result of the NREL mixing study was that the Cowles blade (at 500
 rpm) mixed inks exhibit much lower viscosities, indicating a lower particle volume fraction and
 higher agglomeration, than those mixed with the Turrax high-shear mixer. During the initial
 mixing steps (active material + water + surfactant) the Turrax-mixed ink remained mostly a
 flowing liquid with a foam head. The blade-mixed ink formed a gel to the point that its container
 could be entirely inverted without loss of ink. The blade and Turrax mixed inks were also studied

 4

 with the FOG gauges along each step of the mixing process. In contradiction to the rheology
 data, the FOG data showed particle sizes in the 5-7 µm range for both inks. Upon visual and
 tactile inspection, it was found these particles were most likely agglomerated PTFE and that not
 much significant information about the other solid particles in the ink could be extracted using
 the FOG technique. Given the subjectivity and lack of fine resolution of the FOG method, we
 discontinued the use of this technique for these inks. [Addresses Task 1A]

 Knowing that PSi would have to use a blade mixer at EBP, we decided to attack the at-scale
 mixing problem by adjusting the formulation with different surfactants to further minimize
 foaming and gelation. Three non-ionic surfactants shown in Figure 4 were selected for testing
 based on their different structures (linear, branched, and aromatic hydrophobic tails). The first
 two, Brij and Tergitol, were identified by NREL. The third, Triton X, was the originally
 identified surfactant. [Addresses Task 1A]

 Figure 4. Chemical formulations and structures of Brij, Tergitol and Triton X surfactants.
 In an initial pure-component solution comparison, the Brij surfactant resulted in the least
 foaming, and was thus the focus of further studies. Subsequently, a study of Brij concentration
 was performed. First, 1, 2, 3 and 4 wt% Brij inks were studied. The 1, 2, and 3 wt% inks formed
 gels and did not perform well in the mixing protocol due to lack of mixing flow and subsequent
 phase separation due to insufficient surfactant. However, the 4 wt% ink remained liquid-like
 during the entire mixing process. A further study was performed between 3 and 4 wt% Brij to
 identify a local optimum. 3.25 and 3.5 wt% inks remained gel-like. The 3.75 wt% ink was thick
 during several mixing steps but did flow after overnight stirring. Overall, the 4 wt% Brij
 condition was judged to be the best. The rheology of the studied Brij inks (across the range of
 Brij solids%) as well as the viscosity at decadal shear rates as a function of Brij wt% are shown
 in Figure 5. This data show a strong drop in viscosity with increasing wt%, indicating effective
 micellization of the active material particles, through 4 wt% Brij. [Addresses Task 1A]

 5

 Figure 5. Rheology data for active material inks with Brij surfactant: shear viscosity (left) and
 viscosity at decadal shear rate as a function of Brij concentration (right).
 At room temperature, Brij is a waxy-like solid which does not lend itself well to solution-
 processing at scale, as received. For the above lab-scale measurements, we had dissolved the Brij
 over night by stirring globs of it at room temperature. This technique significantly increases ink
 preparation time. To address this inefficiency, we performed a study which tested the effect of
 Brij dispersion method on ink rheology to identify potentially faster and more scalable methods.
 We tested 1) heating to 50ºC during low rpm stirring, 2) melting the Brij (~35ºC) prior to mixing
 for easy pouring and mixing, and 3) stirring at room temperature as the control. As seen in
 Figure 6, the low-shear behavior varied between the inks made using all three Brij dispersion
 methods due to instrument noise, but the behavior of the inks at higher shear conditions
 representative of actual processing conditions was very similar and agreed with the 4 wt% Brij
 ink rheology from the in Brij concentration study (black circles). [Addresses Task 1A]

 Figure 6. Rheology data for inks made using Brij surfactant solutions prepared at different
 dispersion conditions.
 6

 We also compared mixing with the ultra-Turrax homogenizer to a “marine prop” style blade
 mixer, the latter of which is more similar to the standard equipment used at EBP. As seen in
 Figure 7, the blade mixing produced a homogenous translucent white solution with minimal
 foam, while the homogenizer mixing produced an undesirable head of thick foam regardless of
 rpm and exposure time. [Addresses Task 1A]

 Figure 7. “Marine prop” blade mixing of Brij solution (left), homogenizer mixing of Brij solution
 (right) – light white is foam (different total volumes, do not compare heights).
 Using this information PSi returned to EPB to perform another pilot coating run, this time
 utilizing the new Brij-based ink formulation and ink mixing methodology. According to PSi, the
 ink mixing and clogging issues that they had been experiencing at EBP have largely been
 eliminated, leading to high-quality coatings. They stated that, with these improvements, they feel
 that they can spend more time trying to improve the performance of their product rather than
 spending time struggling with acceptable mixing and coating. [Addresses Task 1A]

 To further explore these new formulations of active material inks having good mixing properties,
 we performed another R2R slot die coating run at NREL, using five different ink formulations.
 These test formulations, suggested by PSi based on our final baseline Brij formulation, included
 different surfactant and total solids contents. [Addresses Task 1A]

 20 cm-wide PTFE-treated electrode material was used as the substrate. Coating was applied at 1
 m/min line speed. Some flow instabilities were observed for the inks with lower solids content.
 For example, excess ink was observed to pool on the top lip of the die, then horizontally
 overflow to the edges of the substrate. The ink flowrate was lowered to attempt to mitigate the
 horizontal overflow, with somewhat successful results. Further increase of solids in the
 formulation, which would allow for lower flow rates to achieve the same solids loading, would
 likely also help minimize this behavior. Also, the higher Brij content inks appeared to facilitate
 better wetting of the ink to the hydrophobic substrate (as would be expected) and reduced
 horizontal overflow at the same conditions compared to the other formulations. Coating results
 were presented to PSi and the coated materials shipped to PSi for testing. [Addresses Task 1B]

 7

 In accordance with the intent of Task 1D to regularly report on progress, we held regular phone-
 or web-conference-based meetings to review results and plan next steps. The partner did not
 require a final report.

 In summary for the Task 1 work, we have taken PSi’s baseline ink, identified its issues with
 respect to scalability, and reformulated the ink through two main iterations to arrive at an ink that
 is very amenable to R2R slot die coating. We have provided PSi meters of coated electrodes to
 test throughout the ink developmental pathway to ensure performance was not sacrificed as we
 worked to improve processability. We determined that gravure coating is not feasible compared
 to slot die coating due to limitations in maximum wet film thickness while achieving the desired
 solids loading of 16 g/m2. Throughout all of this work we have regularly held meetings with PSi
 to provide updates and to discuss desired next steps.

 Results for Task 2:
 To initiate the Task 2 effort, optical scanning of a series of coated electrodes and uncoated
 substrates provided by PSi was performed to determine if the technique is sensitive to the
 loading of the electrode and pre-treat coatings. Our existing bench-top optical scanning test-beds
 were used for this study. As shown in Figure 8, the technique is very sensitive to the pre-treat
 (PTFE) loading and is fairly sensitive to the electrode loading, though somewhat less so.
 [Addresses Task 2A]

 8

 Figure 8. Optical reflectance responses and statistics for an initial series of PSi electrodes and
 substrates (in the table, IPTFE signifies the relative loading of the PTFE pre-treatment, and Iink
 signifies the relative loading of the active material ink).
 A similar study was performed using thermal scanning of a similar series of samples (using the
 same test-beds). As shown in Figure 9, the thermal technique is quite sensitive to both pre-treat
 and electrode loading. For follow-on optical and thermal scanning evaluation, we are in the
 process of fabricating a set of electrodes with nominal and ±20% loading (see discussion of
 loading measurement below). [Addresses Task 2A]

 Figure 9. Thermal scanning responses and statistics for a series of PSi electrodes and substrates
 (in the table, IPTFE signifies the relative loading of the PTFE pre-treatment, and Iink signifies the
 relative loading of the ink).

 9

 Due to the variability in mass of the electrode substrate, PSi had not developed a viable method
 to measure the coat weight of the electrode. This posed a problem for the study mentioned above,
 where coating of specific electrode loadings was desired. In response, we performed a detailed
 mass measurement variability study. Initially, 5 cm2 samples of the substrate were punched and
 weighed. It was determined that the variability of the mass measurement was on the same order
 as the nominal mass loading of the electrode, obviating the ability to validate the coated
 loadings. However, by increasing the size of the specimens to 50 cm2, we obtained a mass
 variability of only 1/8 of the nominal electrode loading, thus enabling determination of electrode
 coat weight at a level reasonably above the uncertainty of the measurement. With this
 development, we were able to proceed with the above described electrode loading study.
 [Addresses Task 2A]

 As discussed above, understanding the penetration of the coated active material ink into the
 highly porous electrode substrate is critical to ensuring proper ink formulation and coating
 conditions, as well as to enabling suitable device performance. As PSi did not have an
 established methodology for this measurement, we developed a methodology to easily
 characterize the depth of penetration of the pretreat and electrochemically active coatings on the
 PSi electrode substrate. The method involved the development of a unique jig (designed and 3D-
 printed) to hold a full-cross-web (wide-area) sample of the coated substrate, use of a high-
 resolution desktop scanner, and image post-processing. Figure 10 shows an example image,
 which is post-processed for scaling of size and intensity, of a cross-section of the entire cross-
 width sample. The depth of the active layer (dark, bottom), pretreatment (lighter, top), and
 uncoated area in the middle of the thickness (between the two yellow lines) can all be clearly
 observed. PSi currently has no way to perform wide-area imaging through the thickness of their
 coated material, to be able to measure and (therefore) optimize their coating conditions for the
 desired penetration. Furthermore, the ability to image a wide-area sample with high resolution is
 uncommon, and not developed for these materials. PSi was extremely excited about these results,
 and a record of invention was filed on the method. [Addresses Tasks 2A&B]

 Figure 10. Image of the sample cross-section, showing penetration depth of coatings.
 Taking advantage of the many-meter sample of R2R-coated electrode discussed above, we
 performed simultaneous optical reflectance imaging and thermal imaging, real-time, on our R2R
 metrology web-line. This was the first time we’ve demonstrated simultaneous in-line diagnostic
 techniques. Figure 11 shows pictures of the optical inspection station (left) and the thermal
 imaging setup (right) with the coated electrode substrate. The optical imaging showed variations
 in the substrate and coating. However, the thermal imaging was not sensitive to the variations in
 loading of the coatings. [Addresses Task 2A]

 10

 Figure 11. Images of the optical (left) and thermal (right) imaging setups on NREL’s metrology
 web-line.

 Results for Task 3:
 We held initial discussions with PSi to define and agree on in situ testing protocols as well as
 design considerations for setting up a liquid electrolyte test stand at NREL. We also reviewed the
 hazards associated with the operation of the test stand, relative to the existing Safe Operating
 Procedure in NREL’s cell testing lab, and obtained required concurrences with NREL’s ES&H
 staff. With the loaned pumping/handling system as well as a cell and testing hardware provided
 by PSi, we performed in situ testing and confirmed operation of our test system by measurement
 of product sodium hydroxide liquid. A schematic of the test station is given in Figure 12. A
 decrease in output was measured compared to PSi internal testing, however. A possible
 explanation was the difference in atmospheric pressure between the NREL and PSi sites (as
 NREL is more than a mile in altitude above sea level), and thus a backpressure system was set up
 to explore this further. Modifications to our in situ testing stand were then completed and
 additional in situ testing of a PSi-supplied cell and hardware, in voltage-control mode, were
 performed. We achieved similar cell performance to PSi’s standard test results, however
 experienced several ongoing difficulties related to handling of the liquid electrolyte in the test
 hardware. [Addresses Tasks 3A-E]

 Figure 12. Schematic of NREL test station setup and operation.

 Subsequent to further improvements to the design of the test station, we performed a second
 round of in situ testing, this time in current-control mode. With a new cell in the test hardware,
 we performed a 5-hour run, followed by measurements of polarization and electrochemical
 impedance spectroscopy (EIS) to understand performance and resistances, and then a 15-hour
 run. Voltage and current, as well as feed and exhaust pressures and temperatures were measured
 throughout the testing. Figure 13 shows the operating conditions of the test station in constant
 current mode. Figure 14 shows the applied current, voltage response, and system and ambient
 pressures during the initial 5 hours of testing. It can be seen that the cell response is very stable.
 Figure 15 shows the cell polarization data comparisons between two test runs, showing the
 improvement achieved by the various test station modifications. Figure 16 gives the EIS data,
 showing the expected decrease in cell resistance with increasing current density. This test
 capability is something that PSi doesn’t have, and potentially provides additional insights into
 cell operation. [Addresses Tasks 3A&E]
