Abstract
We investigate Private Information Retrieval (PIR) in the context of synthetic DNA-based data storage. While PIR is a well-studied primitive for digital databases, extending it to DNA-based databases presents unique challenges arising from biochemical query mechanisms and their complexity. We propose two approaches for adapting two-server PIR protocols to DNA-based storage, balancing privacy, efficiency, and feasibility. These approaches illustrate how information-theoretic privacy trade-offs manifest in DNA-based storage systems.



I
Introduction and Motivation

Private Information Retrieval (PIR) allows a user to retrieve a specific record from a database without revealing which record is accessed
[
8
]
. In the classical setting, PIR protocols assume digital storage with efficient random access and in-memory algebraic computation. Multi-server PIR schemes achieve information-theoretic privacy by distributing queries across non-colluding servers and combining their responses using simple algebraic operations such as XOR. These protocols have been extensively studied and optimized for electronic storage systems, e.g.,
[
25
,
3
,
26
,
29
,
11
,
14
,
28
,
23
]
.

In parallel, synthetic DNA-based data storage has emerged as a promising medium for ultra-dense and long-term archival storage, see e.g.
[
9
,
12
,
13
,
7
,
31
,
10
,
19
,
4
,
2
,
20
]
. In such systems, digital data is encoded into large pools of synthetic DNA molecules, with millions or billions of distinct strands collectively representing the database. DNA-based storage offers storage densities and durability far beyond conventional electronic media
[
10
,
13
,
2
]
, but its access model differs fundamentally from that of digital memory
[
27
,
19
]
. This mismatch motivates a careful re-examination of information retrieval in the context of DNA-based storage. In particular, we focus on PIR from DNA-based data, which, to the best of our knowledge, has only been studied in the independent and concurrent work of Wang et al.
[
30
]
for the single-server setting.

We consider a DNA-based storage system with a database replicated over multiple servers, and a user privately retrieving a certain record. Each server stores the database as a pool of synthetic DNA strands and can answer queries by performing biochemical operations, followed by sequencing and digital post-processing. The user may also perform biochemical operations, most notably, simple DNA synthesis. A key parameter of the model is the user’s biochemical capability, which varies across the scenarios we consider and determines the privacy guarantees and the achievable efficiency, as we shall explain.

In classical two-server PIR
[
8
]
, a database
{\mathbf{D}}
containing
n
files is stored across the servers. To retrieve file
{\bm{d}}_{f}
, the user draws a random sequence
{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}
and sends the query
{\bm{q}}_{1}={\bm{u}}
to server
1
and
{{\bm{q}}_{2}={\bm{u}}\oplus{\bm{e}}_{f}}
to server
2
, where
{\bm{e}}_{f}
is the
f^{\mathrm{th}}
standard basis vector and
\oplus
is addition modulo 2, i.e., the XOR operation. The servers respectively respond with the dot product
{\bm{a}}_{i}=\langle{\mathbf{D}},{\bm{q}}_{i}\rangle
, and the user computes
{\bm{d}}_{f}={\bm{a}}_{1}\oplus{\bm{a}}_{2}
.

A direct implementation of classical PIR in DNA storage has a prohibitive cost. Each server would need to synthesize a
primer
(short DNA strand) for every selected record per query, that is
\mathcal{O}(n)
individual synthesis operations. Moreover, unlike electronic storage, where servers can compute algebraic combinations of records in memory, DNA-based storage does not support in-pool computation: any algebraic processing, such as XOR or linear combinations of records, can only be performed after sequencing. PIR schemes must therefore be redesigned so that privacy does not rely on pre-sequencing algebraic manipulation. At the same time, synthesizing primers with
random
sequences is inexpensive
[
2
,
18
]
, and
polymerase chain reaction
(PCR) amplification time is, in principle, independent of the pool size
[
19
]
. These properties are the key features of DNA-based storage that our protocols exploit: queries are constructed primarily from randomly synthesized primers, with targeted synthesis limited to a single sequence.

We investigate how multi-server PIR protocols can be adapted to DNA-based storage under these constraints. We focus on a single core approach: exploiting inexpensive random primer pool synthesis. We introduce two novel PIR protocols for DNA-based storage and consider three scenarios of increasing generality that differ in the biochemical capabilities assumed for the user, the privacy guarantees achieved, and the number of servers used. These protocols and scenarios illustrate the fundamental trade-offs involved in PIR from large-scale DNA-based storage systems.
We assume a noiseless setting unless stated otherwise, deferring the important analysis of DNA-specific error sources such as strand dropouts, amplification bias, and sequencing noise to future work.

II-B

Private Information Retrieval

Private Information Retrieval (PIR)
[
8
]
is a primitive that allows a user to retrieve a record
{\bm{d}}_{f}
from a database
{\mathbf{D}}=({\bm{d}}_{1}^{\top},\dots,{\bm{d}}_{n}^{\top})^{\top}
stored at
m
servers, while keeping the index
f
private. The user generates a query
{\bm{q}}_{j}
for each server
j
and sends it to that server. Each server processes its query on
{\mathbf{D}}
and returns an answer
{\bm{a}}_{j}
. The queries are constructed such that any set of
z<m
servers learns nothing about
f
and that the user can reconstruct
{\bm{d}}_{f}
from the collection of answers.

Correctness.
Let
\mathsf{D}_{f}
be the random variable for record
{\bm{d}}_{f}
. A PIR protocol is said to be

if

\entropy(\mathsf{D}_{f}\mid{\bm{a}}_{1},{\bm{a}}_{2},\dots,{\bm{a}}_{m},{\bm{q}}_{1},{\bm{q}}_{2},\dots,{\bm{q}}_{m})=0.\vskip-2.15277pt

Let
\mathsf{F}
be the random variable for the queried file index
f

\mathsf{Q}_{i}
be the random variable for a query
{\bm{q}}_{i}
and
z<m
be a privacy parameter. A PIR protocol achieves
perfect information-theoretic (IT)
privacy if
\mutualInfo(\mathsf{Q}_{i_{1}},\dots,\mathsf{Q}_{i_{z}};\mathsf{F})=0
for any
z
queries. A PIR protocol achieves

privacy if
\mutualInfo(\mathsf{Q}_{i_{1}},\dots,\mathsf{Q}_{i_{z}};\mathsf{F})\leq\varepsilon
for a desired
\varepsilon>0
. The term
\varepsilon
is defined as the

, also analyzed in
[
17
,
21
,
6
]
.

Consider the two-server PIR scheme mentioned in the introduction, which forms the basis of our constructions in Section

II-C

DNA-Based PIR Model

We consider a multi-server PIR with replicated data for synthetic DNA-based data storage. Under this setting, each server stores the database
{\mathbf{D}}
as a pool of synthetic DNA strands and answers queries using biochemical operations followed by sequencing and digital post-processing. A key parameter of the model is the user’s biochemical capability, which determines how queries are generated. We progressively introduce the different scenarios with varying user biochemical capabilities in Section
III
, after presenting our protocols.

Strand and database structure.

In DNA-based storage, digital data is encoded into synthetic DNA molecules called
strands
. A strand is a short sequence over
\mathcal{S}
, typically hundreds to thousands of symbols
long. Large collections of such strands are synthesized and stored together in solution, forming a
pool
. Unlike electronic memory, strands in a pool are unordered and cannot be addressed by position; instead, each strand carries its own address as part of its sequence. Formally, each record
{\bm{d}}_{i}
is stored as a strand of fixed length
\ell=\ell_{\mathrm{bc}}+\ell_{\mathrm{pl}}+\ell_{\mathrm{u}}
nucleotides, partitioned into three parts:

{\bm{d}}_{i}=[{\bm{b}}_{i},{\bm{p}}_{i},{\bm{u}}],\qquad{\bm{b}}_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{\ell_{\mathrm{bc}}},\quad{\bm{p}}_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{\ell_{\mathrm{pl}}},\quad{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\ell_{\mathrm{u}}},\vskip-4.30554pt

where
{\bm{b}}_{i}
is the
barcode
(address),
{\bm{p}}_{i}
is the
payload
(content), and
{\bm{u}}
is a short universal sequence (serving as a template for a universal reverse primer) common to all strands in the pool. The database is then the pool
\mathcal{D}=\{{\bm{d}}_{1},{\bm{d}}_{2},\dots,{\bm{d}}_{n}\}.

Barcodes serve as unique identifiers:
{\bm{b}}_{i}\neq{\bm{b}}_{j}
for all
i\neq j
. We write
\mathcal{B}=\{{\bm{b}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{b}}_{n}\}
for the set of barcodes in
\mathcal{D}
. We write
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{S}^{\ell_{\mathrm{bc}}}
for the universe of all sequences of length
\ell_{\mathrm{bc}}
, so
u\triangleq\left\lvert{\mathcal{U}}\right\rvert=4^{\ell_{\mathrm{bc}}}
and
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
. See Fig.
1
for a schematic representation of the database setup.

Selective amplification via PCR.

Random access to individual strands within a DNA pool is achieved via PCR. In our model, each strand carries a unique barcode at one end and a short universal sequence
{\bm{u}}
at the other. To retrieve the strand with barcode
{\bm{b}}_{i}
, a primer matching
{\bm{b}}_{i}
is introduced into the pool alongside the universal reverse primer. Enzymatic amplification then selectively replicates only the target strand. Importantly, PCR can amplify multiple distinct targets simultaneously by introducing several primers at once, and the amplification time does not scale with the pool size
n
. This is a key feature of DNA-based storage that our protocols exploit.

Barcode separation requirement.

PCR primers bind to their target via hybridization, which tolerates a small number of sequence mismatches. To prevent a primer designed for barcode
{\bm{b}}_{i}
from inadvertently co-amplifying a strand with a similar barcode
{\bm{b}}_{j}
, the barcodes must be sufficiently separated in Hamming distance
2
2

2

The tolerance of PCR to primer mismatches is more accurately captured by edit (Levenshtein) distance, which accounts for insertions and deletions in addition to substitutions. We use Hamming distance here as a mathematically convenient approximation.
. We refer to such inadvertent co-amplification as PCR primer cross-talk. We require that for all
i\neq j
,
\dH({\bm{b}}_{i},{\bm{b}}_{j})\geq 2\rho+1
,
where
\rho\geq 1
is the
proximity tolerance
, that is, the maximum number of mismatches under which PCR may still inadvertently amplify a non-target strand. In coding-theoretic terms, the barcode set
\mathcal{B}
must be a code over
\mathcal{S}^{\ell_{\mathrm{bc}}}
with minimum distance
2\rho+1
.

To prevent primer cross-talk, the Hamming spheres of radius
\rho
around each barcode must be disjoint. By the Hamming (sphere-packing) bound, this restricts the maximum number of usable barcodes to:

n=|\mathcal{B}|\leq\frac{4^{\ell_{\mathrm{bc}}}}{\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\rho}\binom{\ell_{\mathrm{bc}}}{k}3^{k}}.

Setting
\rho=2
, which is typical in practice, as PCR tolerates up to one or two substitutions under standard experimental conditions
[
32
]
, we have
\sum_{k=0}^{2}\binom{\ell_{\mathrm{bc}}}{k}\cdot 3^{k}>\ell_{\mathrm{bc}}^{2}
for all
\ell_{\mathrm{bc}}>0
, yielding
the convenient bound

n<\frac{4^{\ell_{\mathrm{bc}}}}{\ell_{\mathrm{bc}}^{2}}.

Query model and constraints.

The user wishes to retrieve record
{\bm{d}}_{f}
while preserving privacy. To do so, the user generates primers from
\mathcal{U}
and sends them to the servers. Each server adds the received primers to its pool, performs PCR amplification, sequences the result, and returns a digital response. Any computation on the data is performed only after sequencing. Servers thus operate in a
molecular-then-digital
pipeline. Concretely, a server can apply PCR to selectively amplify target strands, sequence the resulting material, and compute arbitrarily on the sequenced output.

A direct implementation of the two-server PIR scheme
[
8
]
would require each server to receive a binary query
{\bm{q}}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times 1}
and synthesize a targeted PCR primer for every index
i
with
{\bm{q}}[i]=1
. This is infeasible in the DNA setting: on average, half the entries of
{\bm{q}}
are
1
, meaning
\mathcal{O}(n)
specific primers must be synthesized per query, which is prohibitively expensive for large databases. At the same time, DNA-based storage offers properties that can be exploited for PIR. Synthesizing
random
primers is cheap, and PCR amplification time is independent of the pool size — so accessing a record costs the same regardless of how large the database is. Our protocols are designed around these two properties.

For our protocols, we assume a noiseless biochemical setting. Specifically, we assume that PCR amplification is perfect, meaning there is no inadvertent amplification of non-target strands, provided that the barcodes are designed with the appropriate Hamming distance. Furthermore, we assume that
\Copy
operations are perfect. We briefly discuss the effects of imperfect copying in Scenario 3 and will provide a comprehensive treatment of experimental noise in future work.

Remark 1
.

A PIR scheme is evaluated by the

IV
Three PIR Scenarios

We now instantiate
\mathsf{PIRwithXOR}
across three scenarios. For each scenario, depending on the reliability of the output of a single instantiation, we utilize
m/2
instantiations over
m/2
different server pairs for a successful recovery with overwhelming probability.

Remark 2
.

Algorithms
\mathsf{Answer1}
and
\mathsf{Answer2}
work on the whole records and not just over the payloads. For the first two scenarios below, these algorithms can work solely on the payloads, cutting down communication and computation costs. For the third scenario, as we explain, processing the barcodes is essential for the user to verify that the payload it recovered belongs together with the barcode it initially queried for.

IV-A

Scenario 1

In this scenario, the user can check whether
{\bm{b}}_{f}\in\mathcal{R}
, and the copy
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{R}
is perfect.
In practice, when synthesizing random sequences, the user cannot avoid sampling
{\bm{b}}_{f}
specifically; it is as likely to occur as any other sequence. Yet, we assume that the user can check the contents of
\mathcal{R}
. This way, it can sample a new
\mathcal{R}
until
{\bm{b}}_{f}\neq\mathcal{R}
to use for generating its queries. Thus, in Algorithm

\mathcal{U}\setminus\{{\bm{b}}_{f}\}

In this case, because the user can guarantee that

can be retrieved from just

servers.

To analyze the leakage of
\mathsf{PIRwithXOR}

of the random pool. This is intuitive from a technical perspective, but we show that it does not achieve perfect IT privacy.

Theorem 1
.

Let
r\triangleq\left\lvert{\mathcal{R}}\right\rvert
be a constant s.t.
0\leq r\leq u-1
. The leakage
\varepsilon=\mutualInfo{(\mathsf{Q}_{j};\mathsf{F})}
,
j\in[2]
, is given by

\displaystyle\varepsilon=\log n-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\binom{n-1}{k}\binom{u-n}{r-k}}{\binom{u-1}{r}}\log((k+1)(n-k))

and
\varepsilon\geq 1-\log(1+1/n)
with equality if and only if
n=1
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we give the proof for
\mathsf{Q}_{1}
and hence drop the subscript. We compute the leakage as

\varepsilon\triangleq\mutualInfo(\mathsf{Q};\mathsf{F})=\entropy(\mathsf{Q})-\entropy(\mathsf{Q}\mid\mathsf{F}).

(1)

To compute the second term, note that given
\mathsf{F}
, the randomness of
\mathsf{Q}
stems from:

(i)

receiving
\mathsf{Q}=\mathcal{R}
or
\mathsf{Q}=\mathcal{R}_{f}
, which happens with probability
1/2
each; and

(ii)

the randomness of sampling a subset
\mathcal{R}
of size
r
uniformly at random from
\mathcal{U}\setminus\{{\bm{b}}_{f}\}
.

Hence,

\entropy(\mathsf{Q}\mid\mathsf{F})=1+\log\binom{u-1}{r}.

(2)

Now let us analyze
\entropy(\mathsf{Q})
. According to the protocol,
\Pr[\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{R}]=\Pr[\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{R}_{f}]=1/2
. Then,
\forall\mathcal{R}\subset\mathcal{U}
with
\left\lvert{\mathcal{R}}\right\rvert=r
,
\mathcal{R}
can be selected if and only if
{\bm{b}}_{f}\notin\mathcal{R}
. Given
{\bm{b}}_{f}\notin\mathcal{R}
, the probability of selecting
\mathcal{R}
is
1/\binom{u-1}{r}
. Any element of
\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{R}
can be chosen as the queried index
{\bm{b}}_{f}
with probability
1/n
, so

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathsf{Q}=\mathcal{R}]=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{n-\left\lvert{\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{R}}\right\rvert}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{\binom{u-1}{r}}.

Similarly,
\forall\mathcal{R}_{f}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
with
\left\lvert{\mathcal{R}_{f}}\right\rvert=r+1
,
\mathcal{R}_{f}
can be obtained if and only if
{\bm{b}}_{f}\in\mathcal{R}_{f}
. Since
\mathcal{R}_{f}=\mathcal{R}\cup\{{\bm{b}}_{f}\}
for a unique pool
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{U}\setminus\{{\bm{b}}_{f}\}
, any element of
\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{R}_{f}
can be chosen as the queried index
{\bm{b}}_{f}
with probability
1/n
, so

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathsf{Q}=\mathcal{R}_{f}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\Pr[{\bm{b}}_{f}\in\mathcal{R}_{f}]\cdot\frac{1}{\binom{u-1}{r}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\left\lvert{\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{R}_{f}}\right\rvert}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{\binom{u-1}{r}}.

To compute
\entropy(\mathsf{Q})
, we can now iterate over all
k=\left\lvert{\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{R}}\right\rvert
by choosing
k
elements from
\mathcal{B}
and
r-k
elements from
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{B}
and over all
k=\left\lvert{\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{R}_{f}}\right\rvert
by choosing
k
elements from
\mathcal{B}
and
r-k+1
elements from
\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{B}
. Then,
\entropy(\mathsf{Q})
can be written as

\displaystyle\entropy(\mathsf{Q})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\binom{u-n}{r-k}\cdot\frac{\frac{n-k}{2n}}{\binom{u-1}{r}}\cdot\log\left(\frac{\frac{n-k}{2n}}{\binom{u-1}{r}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\binom{u-n}{r-k+1}\cdot\frac{\frac{k}{2n}}{\binom{u-1}{r}}\cdot\log\left(\frac{\frac{k}{2n}}{\binom{u-1}{r}}\right),

where terms with
n-k=0
in the first sum and
k=0
in the second vanish by the convention
0\log 0=0
. We apply the identity
\binom{n}{k}(n-k)=n\binom{n-1}{k}
to the first sum, and reindex
k\mapsto k-1
in the second sum using
\binom{n}{k}k=n\binom{n-1}{k-1}
. This way, the common factor
\binom{n-1}{k}\binom{u-n}{r-k}
appears in both sums and the upper limit of both becomes
n-1
:

\displaystyle\entropy(\mathsf{Q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\binom{n-1}{k}\binom{u-n}{r-k}}{2\binom{u-1}{r}}\cdot\left[\log\binom{u-1}{r}+\log\frac{2n}{n-k}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\binom{n-1}{k}\binom{u-n}{r-k}}{2\binom{u-1}{r}}\cdot\left[\log\binom{u-1}{r}+\log\frac{2n}{k+1}\right].

We use Vandermonde’s identity as
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{k}\binom{u-n}{r-k}=\binom{u-1}{r}
. Combining the two sums and simplifying,

\displaystyle\entropy(\mathsf{Q})

\displaystyle=\log n+\log\binom{u-1}{r}+1

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\binom{n-1}{k}\binom{u-n}{r-k}}{\binom{u-1}{r}}\log((k+1)(n-k)).

(3)

From
Sections

IV-A
,
2
and
1
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon=\log n-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\binom{n-1}{k}\binom{u-n}{r-k}}{\binom{u-1}{r}}\log((k+1)(n-k)).

Observe for the second term on the RHS that the weights are the hypergeometric probability mass function, so they sum to 1.
Also, the expression
(k+1)+(n-k)=n+1
is constant in
k
, so
(k+1)(n-k)\leq((n+1)/2)^{2}
pointwise.
Then,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\binom{n-1}{k}\binom{u-n}{r-k}}{\binom{u-1}{r}}\log((k+1)(n-k))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\log(n+1)-1

with equality if and only if
n=1
. Finally,
\varepsilon\geq\log n-\log(n+1)+1=1-\log((n+1)/n)=1-\log(1+1/n)
.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{R}\subset\mathcal{U}
be a random pool of sequences and for any sequence
{\bm{r}}\in\mathcal{U}\setminus\{{\bm{b}}_{f}\}
, let
\Pr[{\bm{r}}\in\mathcal{R}]=p
for
0<p<1
. Then the leakage
\varepsilon=\mutualInfo{(\mathsf{Q}_{j};\mathsf{F})}
,
j\in[2]
is given by

\varepsilon=\log n+\frac{1}{2}\log p(1-p)-\frac{1}{2np(1-p)}\\
\cdot\sum_{q=0}^{u}\binom{u}{q}p^{q}(1-p)^{u-q}\sum_{k=0}^{q}\frac{\binom{n}{k}\binom{u-n}{q-k}}{\binom{u}{q}}c_{k}\log c_{k},

where
c_{k}:=np+k(1-2p)
.

Proof.

Given
\mathsf{F}
, the randomness of
\mathsf{Q}
stems from:

(i)

u-1
Bernoulli random variables with success probability
p
,

(ii)

and the uniformly random choice between
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}_{f}
.

Thus

\entropy(\mathsf{Q}\mid\mathsf{F})=(u-1)\binaryEntropy(p)+1.

Now we analyze
\entropy(\mathsf{Q})
. For
q=\left\lvert{\mathcal{Q}}\right\rvert
,
k=\left\lvert{\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{Q}}\right\rvert
, we can write

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathsf{Q}=\mathcal{Q}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Pr[{\bm{b}}_{f}\in\mathcal{Q}]p^{q-1}(1-p)^{u-q}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\Pr[{\bm{b}}_{f}\notin\mathcal{Q}]p^{q}(1-p)^{u-q-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}p^{q-1}(1-p)^{u-q-1}(np+k(1-2p))

since
\Pr[{\bm{b}}_{f}\in\mathcal{Q}]=k/n
. We can now compute
\entropy(\mathsf{Q})
as

\displaystyle\entropy(\mathsf{Q})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{q=0}^{u}\sum_{k=0}^{q}\binom{n}{k}\binom{u-n}{q-k}\Pr[\mathsf{Q}=\mathcal{Q}]\log\Pr[\mathsf{Q}=\mathcal{Q}].

The sum above can be split into four parts since

\log\Pr[\mathsf{Q}=\mathcal{Q}]=-\log 2n+(q-1)\log p\\
+(u-q-1)\log(1-p)+\log(np+k(1-2p)).

This way, we can write
\entropy(\mathsf{Q})=h_{1}+h_{2}+h_{3}+h_{4}
and calculate each part individually. For
h_{1}
,
h_{2}
and
h_{3}
below, we utilize Vandermonde’s identity as

\sum_{k=0}^{q}\binom{n}{k}\binom{u-n}{q-k}c_{k}=np\binom{u}{q}+n(1-2p)\binom{u-1}{q-1}.

\displaystyle h_{1}

\displaystyle=\log 2n\sum_{q=0}^{u}\sum_{k=0}^{q}\binom{n}{k}\binom{u-n}{q-k}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\frac{1}{2n}p^{q-1}(1-p)^{u-q-1}(np+k(1-2p))

\displaystyle=\frac{\log 2n}{2n}\sum_{q=0}^{u}p^{q-1}(1-p)^{u-q-1}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{q}\binom{n}{k}\binom{u-n}{q-k}(np+k(1-2p))

\displaystyle=\frac{\log 2n}{2}\sum_{q=0}^{u}p^{q-1}(1-p)^{u-q-1}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\left(p\binom{u}{q}+(1-2p)\binom{u-1}{q-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\log 2n}{2(1-p)}+\frac{\log 2n}{2p(1-p)}\frac{1-2p}{u}up=\log 2n.

\displaystyle h_{2}

\displaystyle=-\log p\sum_{q=0}^{u}\sum_{k=0}^{q}\binom{n}{k}\binom{u-n}{q-k}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\frac{1}{2n}p^{q-1}(1-p)^{u-q-1}(np+k(1-2p))(q-1)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\log p}{2n}\sum_{q=0}^{u}(q-1)p^{q-1}(1-p)^{u-q-1}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\left(p\binom{u}{q}+(1-2p)\binom{u-1}{q-1}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\log p}{2(1-p)}(up-1+(u-1)p(1-2p))

\displaystyle=-\left((u-1)p-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\log p.

\displaystyle h_{3}

\displaystyle=-\log(1-p)\sum_{q=0}^{u}\sum_{k=0}^{q}\binom{n}{k}\binom{u-n}{q-k}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\frac{1}{2n}p^{q-1}(1-p)^{u-q-1}(np+k(1-2p))(u-q-1)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\log(1-p)}{2n}\sum_{q=0}^{u}(u-q-1)p^{q-1}(1-p)^{u-q-1}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\left(p\binom{u}{q}+(1-2p)\binom{u-1}{q-1}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\log(1-p)}{2}(2u(1-p)+2p-3)

\displaystyle=-\left((u-1)(1-p)-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\log(1-p).

\displaystyle h_{4}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{q=0}^{u}\sum_{k=0}^{q}\binom{n}{k}\binom{u-n}{q-k}p^{q-1}(1-p)^{u-q-1}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot(np+k(1-2p))\log(np+k(1-2p))

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2np(1-p)}\sum_{q=0}^{u}\binom{u}{q}p^{q}(1-p)^{u-q}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{q}\frac{\binom{n}{k}\binom{u-n}{q-k}}{\binom{u}{q}}(np+k(1-2p))\log(np+k(1-2p)).

Then the mutual information can be computed as

\mutualInfo(\mathsf{Q};\mathsf{F})=h_{1}+h_{2}+h_{3}+h_{4}-\entropy(\mathsf{Q}\mid\mathsf{F}).

Combining
h_{2}
and
h_{3}
,

\displaystyle h_{2}+h_{3}

\displaystyle=-\left((u-1)p-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\log p

\displaystyle\quad-\left((u-1)(1-p)-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\log(1-p)

\displaystyle=(u-1)\binaryEntropy(p)+\tfrac{1}{2}\log p(1-p).

Since
h_{1}=\log 2n
and
\entropy(\mathsf{Q}\mid\mathsf{F})=(u-1)\binaryEntropy(p)+1
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle=h_{1}+h_{2}+h_{3}+h_{4}-\entropy(\mathsf{Q}\mid\mathsf{F})

\displaystyle=\log 2n+(u-1)\binaryEntropy(p)+\tfrac{1}{2}\log p(1-p)

\displaystyle\quad+h_{4}-(u-1)\binaryEntropy(p)-1

\displaystyle=\log n+\tfrac{1}{2}\log p(1-p)+h_{4},

and the result follows.
∎

Corollary 1

Proof.

Theorem 3
.

Let
r\triangleq\left\lvert{\mathcal{R}}\right\rvert
be a constant s.t.
0\leq r\leq u
. For a given
\lambda>0
, the failure probability of our protocol naively repeated over
m\geq 2\lambda/(2\ell_{\mathrm{bc}}-\log r)
servers is at most
2^{-\lambda}
. For any
j\in[2]
, the leakage is
\varepsilon=\mutualInfo{(\mathsf{Q}_{j};\mathsf{F})}=((u-r)/(2u))\log n.

Proof.

The user will succeed in retrieving the queried record if at least one of the
(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}_{f})
pairs are different. This is due to the fact that
\mathcal{R}\neq\mathcal{R}_{f}
guarantees
\mathcal{R}_{f}\setminus\mathcal{R}={\bm{b}}_{f}
since
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
. So,

\displaystyle\Pr[\text{failure}]=\Pr[{\bm{b}}_{f}\in\mathcal{R}]^{m/2}.

Thus, we just need to find the probability of
{\bm{b}}_{f}
being in
\mathcal{R}
. Because the user samples
\mathcal{R}
of size
\left\lvert{\mathcal{R}}\right\rvert=r
from a universal pool of size
\left\lvert{\mathcal{U}}\right\rvert=u
,
\Pr[{\bm{b}}_{f}\in\mathcal{R}_{j}]=r/u
. For a negligible failure probability, we write

\displaystyle 2^{-\lambda}

\displaystyle\geq\Pr[{\bm{b}}_{f}\in\mathcal{R}_{j}]^{m/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{r}{u}\right)^{m/2}

and obtain
m\geq 2\lambda/(2\ell_{\mathrm{bc}}-\log r)
since
u=4^{\ell_{\mathrm{bc}}}
.

For the leakage, we start by modeling a random variable
\mathsf{R}
for the random pool
\mathcal{R}
, and a random variable
\mathsf{C}
that models the choice between
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}_{f}
for
\mathcal{Q}
. This way, we can model
\mathsf{Q}
as the joint distribution of
\mathsf{R}
and
\mathsf{C}
. Because
\mathsf{C}
is independent of
\mathsf{R}
, we conclude that
\entropy(\mathsf{Q})=\entropy(\mathsf{R})+\entropy(\mathsf{C})
. Then,

\displaystyle\mutualInfo(\mathsf{Q};\mathsf{F})

\displaystyle=\entropy(\mathsf{Q})-\entropy(\mathsf{Q}\mid\mathsf{F})

\displaystyle=\entropy(\mathsf{R})+\entropy(\mathsf{C})-\entropy(\mathsf{R}\mid\mathsf{F})-\entropy(\mathsf{C}\mid\mathsf{F})

\displaystyle=\entropy(\mathsf{C})-\entropy(\mathsf{C}\mid\mathsf{F})

where the last equality is due to the fact that
\mathcal{R}
is sampled independently of
\mathsf{F}
.

The RV
\mathsf{C}
chooses
\mathcal{R}
with probability
(r/u)+(1/2)\cdot(1-r/u)=(u+r)/(2u)
. For any
i\in\left[{n}\right]
,
\mathsf{C}
chooses
\mathcal{R}_{f}=\mathcal{R}\cup\{{\bm{b}}_{i}\}\neq\mathcal{R}
with probability
(1/n)\cdot(1/2)\cdot(1-r/u)=(u-r)/(2un)
.
Thus,

\entropy(\mathsf{C})=\frac{u+r}{2u}\log\frac{2u}{u+r}+n\frac{u-r}{2un}\log\frac{2un}{u-r}

Similarly, notice that given
\mathsf{F}
, the RV
\mathsf{C}
chooses
\mathcal{R}
with probability
(r/u)+(1/2)\cdot(1-r/u)
, and
\mathcal{R}_{f}=\mathcal{R}\cup{\bm{b}}_{f}
with probability
(1/2)\cdot(1-r/u)
. Hence,

\entropy(\mathsf{C}\mid\mathsf{F})=\frac{u+r}{2u}\log\frac{2u}{u+r}+\frac{u-r}{2u}\log\frac{2u}{u-r}.

and the result follows.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{R}\subset\mathcal{U}
be a random pool of sequences and for any sequence
{\bm{r}}\in\mathcal{U}

The user will succeed in retrieving the queried record if at least one of the
(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}_{f})
pairs are different. This is due to the fact that
\mathcal{R}\neq\mathcal{R}_{f}
guarantees
\mathcal{R}_{f}\setminus\mathcal{R}={\bm{b}}_{f}
since
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
. So,

\displaystyle\Pr[\text{failure}]=\Pr[{\bm{b}}_{f}\in\mathcal{R}]^{m/2}=p^{m/2}.

For the leakage, we model the random variables
\mathsf{R}

V
Discussion and Future Work

In this work, we established a foundational framework for PIR in DNA-based data storage. Several interesting challenges remain, e.g., more realistic experimental noise is considered in our ongoing work as our current formulations largely assume noiseless biochemical operations. Specifically, we are investigating the effects of imperfect PCR, where a primer designed for barcode
{\bm{b}}_{i}
inadvertently amplifies unintended records
{\bm{d}}_{j}
(with
i\neq j
) due to barcode/primer similarity. The impact of imperfect pool copying, briefly introduced in Scenario 3, is also a focus of current extensions. Moreover, as in digital PIR, configurations with more than two servers are of interest.

This research direction aims to improve the functionality, flexibility, and privacy aspects of DNA-based data storage.
