Prehabilitation for femoroacetabular impingement syndrome: intervention development using the Medical Research Council Framework
Authors: Anuj Punnoose, Leica Claydon Mueller, Vianca Shah, Alison B. Rushton, Vikas Khanduja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionEffectiveness of pre-operative or pre-surgical rehabilitation also known as “prehabilitation” in patients undergoing hip arthroscopy for femoroacetabular impingement syndrome (FAIS) is unknown. The aim of this study was to develop a novel prehabilitation intervention using structured processes as per the Medical Research Council (MRC) for this cohort.MethodsA multi-method intervention development process was undertaken using the MRC framework. Patient and public involvement (PPI) explored patient experiences of hip pain, barriers to exercise engagement, and preferences for intervention delivery. A systematic review and meta-analysis of prehabilitation in orthopedic surgery informed intervention dosage and delivery characteristics. An international expert consensus identified core domains to be targeted, and a focused literature review synthesized electromyographic evidence using maximum voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC) percentage to select and grade exercises based on muscle activation and symptom provocation risk.ResultsThe resulting PREHAB femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) program is an 8-week, two-phase intervention comprising progressive strengthening, range of motion, balance and proprioception, cardiovascular conditioning, and education components. Phase 1 (weeks 1–4) emphasizes low- to moderate-intensity exercises (16–40% MVIC) targeting hip abductors, extensors, adductors, and trunk muscles, while phase 2 (weeks 5–8) progresses to higher-intensity strengthening (>40% MVIC). Exercises that positioned the hip in impingement or had a high likelihood of symptom aggravation were excluded. The program is delivered using a combination of supervised and home-based sessions, supported by digital monitoring to promote adherence. The intervention was designed to optimize physical readiness for hip arthroscopy while minimizing symptom exacerbation during the pre-operative waiting period.ConclusionThis prehabilitation program was developed through multiple structured methods and is supported by PPI involvement, published evidence, and a consensus statement as per the MRC framework for development of an intervention. The developed program will be utilized in a feasibility study (PREHAB FAI) that will assess the feasibility, suitability, acceptability, and safety of this novel intervention in patients with FAIS undergoing arthroscopic surgery.Level of evidenceNot applicable.

INTRODUCTION

Musculoskeletal diseases are a leading cause of disability worldwide [1]. Hip pain is common across musculoskeletal disorders, including osteoarthritis, tendinitis, acetabular dysplasia, and femoroacetabular impingement syndrome (FAIS) [2]. While hip pain in older adults is commonly associated with degenerative conditions, younger adults (aged 16–50 years) more frequently experience hip pain related to acetabular dysplasia and FAIS [3].

FAIS results from abnormal contact between the acetabulum and the femoral head-neck junction and is classified as cam, pincer, or mixed morphology [4,5]. Prevalence estimates vary [6,7], with a recent UK study reporting radiographic features in 47% of the general population [8]. Improved understanding of FAIS and advances in diagnostic imaging have increased identification rates [9,10], alongside a marked rise in hip arthroscopy procedures in the UK [11]. Although randomized controlled trials (RCTs) demonstrate improved outcomes following hip arthroscopy [12,13], only 50–70% of patients achieve clinically meaningful benefit [12,14].

Level 4 evidence suggests several pre-operative factors such as presence of osteoarthritis, dysplasia, suboptimal correction of the cam/pincer, and soft-tissue injury sustained during the procedures influence post-operative recovery after hip arthroscopy [15]. Non-surgical factors have also been associated with poorer outcomes, including prolonged waiting times and physical deconditioning [16], deficits in muscle strength [17], psychological distress [18], and co-existing conditions such as gluteal tendinopathy [19], athletic pubalgia [20], and low back pain [21]. The pre-operative waiting period therefore presents an opportunity to optimize modifiable physical and psychological risk factors [22].

Level 1 evidence supporting prehabilitation in orthopedic surgery is derived primarily from studies in total knee replacement (TKR), total hip replacement (THR), and lumbar spine surgery, with moderate- to high-certainty evidence demonstrating improvements in pre-operative pain, function, muscle strength, health-related quality of life, and early postoperative function following TKR and lumbar surgery [23]. However, evidence for prehabilitation in FAIS remains limited. The development of such interventions aligns with the Medical Research Council (MRC) framework for complex interventions, which emphasizes phased development and patient and public involvement (PPI) throughout the process [24].

This study therefore aimed to systematically develop a novel prehabilitation intervention for patients with FAIS undergoing hip arthroscopy, guided by the MRC framework. The research question was as follows: How can best available evidence, expert consensus, and PPI be integrated to develop a feasible, acceptable, and patient-centered prehabilitation program for FAIS?

METHODS

Design

A three stage mixed methods approach was used to develop the intervention, consistent with Phase 1 of the MRC framework and the Guidance for reporting of intervention development (GUIDED) checklist [24,25] (Supplemental File 1). Stage 1 explored patient and public perspectives on hip pain, exercise, and adherence. Stage 2 defined the core components and parameters of the intervention using evidence from a systematic review and international expert consensus. Stage 3 synthesized evidence for specific exercise domains and combined this with PPI input to co-develop the PREHAB femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) program. Exercise prescription was guided by percentage maximum voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC) and American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) principles [26]. Stage 1: gathering patient and public opinion and perspectives

PPI was integral to intervention development, ensuring the program was patient-centered rather than generating research data [27]. Patients awaiting hip arthroscopy and their relatives contributed to the informal discussions facilitated by a National Institute for Health and Care Research PPI co-ordinator. No formal qualitative analysis was undertaken. Key points from these discussions were recorded using contemporaneous notes and used pragmatically to inform intervention delivery format, educational content, and strategies to support adherence. Stage 2: defining the components of the intervention

The systematic review and international expert consensus informing this stage have been published previously and are summarized here to demonstrate methodological transparency in line with MRC guidance rather than to re-report findings.

Systematic review with meta-analyses of the effectiveness of prehabilitation for orthopedic surgical procedures

A systematic review determined the effectiveness and optimal characteristics of prehabilitation in all orthopedic conditions due to limited FAIS-specific evidence [23]. The review was conducted by authors A.P., L.C.M., A.R., and V.K. according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) reporting guideline and recommendations were made using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework [28].

International expert panel consensus on prehabilitation for hip arthroscopy in FAIS

An international expert consensus process was conducted (by A.P. and V.K.) to inform the core domains, dosage parameters, and delivery characteristics of the prehabilitation intervention for patients with FAIS awaiting hip arthroscopy. An informal expert panel consensus method was used [29].

Twenty experts were purposively invited based on recognized clinical and research expertise in young adult hip pathology, professional diversity, geographical representation, and gender balance. Seventeen experts participated (14 physiotherapists, 2 surgeons, and 1 surgical care practitioner nurse). All participants were fluent in English and did not require translation. The panel size exceeded the suggested minimum of 10 participants for consensus meetings [30]. The consensus meeting comprised two iterative rounds. In round 1, predefined questions informed by a scoping review of the literature guided moderated discussion on key prehabilitation domains, safety considerations, and feasibility. In round 2, participants anonymously voted on each item using a secure mobile application to minimize dominance bias and enhance transparency. All panellists apart from the moderator (V.K.) had voting rights.

A priori, consensus was defined as a ≥70% agreement [31]. Items achieving this threshold were retained as core components of the prehabilitation program, while those failing to reach consensus were excluded from further development. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and ethical principles were upheld throughout the process. Findings from this consensus process have been published separately [32].

Stage 3: defining the PREHAB FAI intervention program to be evaluated in a feasibility trial

Literature review synthesizing evidence for specific exercise domains

A literature review (A.P. and V.S.) using a PubMed search informed exercise selection based on electromyographic (EMG) activity for the core domains identified through the international expert consensus in stage 2. Search terms included EMG OR electromyography AND hip OR trunk OR core to identify studies examining muscle activity during specific tasks. EMG quantifies neuromuscular activity by measuring electrical signals generated during muscle contraction [33]. Data normalization allows comparison across participants and testing sessions [34], with (percentage of MVIC) accepted as a reliable metric. Percentage MVIC values are classified as low (0–15%), low-moderate (16–20%), moderate (21–40%), high (41–60%), and very high (>60%) [35]. Exercises were graded accordingly, with low- to moderate-intensity exercises (16–40% MVIC) selected for phase 1 early prehabilitation (weeks 1–4) and higher-intensity exercises (>40% MVIC) for phase 2 later prehabilitation (weeks 5–8). Exercises were additionally appraised for hip positioning and potential to provoke impingement or symptom exacerbation.

Co-development of PREHAB FAI intervention program with PPI

PPI was used to support co-development of the prehabilitation education program and information leaflet, ensuring alignment with patient priorities [36], [37]. Three PPI members participated in two online advisory meetings (2 h each), facilitated by the lead author (A.P.) to provide feedback on content, clarity, and acceptability. These discussions were advisory in nature and aimed to ensure patient perspectives informed intervention materials. No formal qualitative analysis was undertaken, although key suggestions were documented. Ethical approval for these PPI activities was granted as part of the phase 2 feasibility trial by the East of England Research Ethics Committee (IRAS ID: 293927; REC Ref: 23/EE/0024).

Stage 2: defining the components of the intervention

Systematic review with meta-analyses of the effectiveness of prehabilitation for orthopedic surgical procedures

The systematic review of 48 trials (3570 participants) [23] identified evidence supporting prehabilitation across orthopedic procedures. High-certainty evidence supported reductions in pre-operative pain for spinal surgery, while moderate-quality evidence demonstrated improvements in muscle strength (TKR and THR), function (TKR), quality of life (THR), and post-operative function (TKR at 6 weeks; spinal surgery at 6 months). Characteristics of prehabilitation interventions included in the systematic review of orthopaedic surgery trials.

Most studies used exercise-based interventions delivered for at least 4–6 weeks at a minimum of two sessions per week. Programs combining supervised and unsupervised sessions were feasible and safe. Adherence reporting remained limited, with only 44% of trials reporting adherence and moderate adherence (>70%) achieved in fewer than half (40%) of these studies. These findings informed the core intervention parameters.

International expert panel consensus on prehabilitation for hip arthroscopy in FAIS

Seventeen international experts (14 physiotherapists, 2 surgeons, and 1 surgical care practitioner nurse) achieved consensus that the following domains are to be targeted: muscle strength (hip extensors, abductors, adductors, external rotators, and core), improvement of range of motion (ROM) using manual mobilizations and motor control retraining techniques, cardiovascular (CV) fitness, proprioception, reduction of anxiety and depression, and management of co-existing pathologies (Fig. 5). Stage 3: defining the PREHAB FAI intervention program to be evaluated in a feasibility trial

Literature review synthesizing evidence for specific exercise domains

Exercise selection and progression within the PREHAB FAI program were informed by EMG (percentage of MVIC) evidence, the domains identified through international expert consensus, and safety considerations relevant to individuals with FAIS. Exercises were selected to target hip abductors, extensors, adductors, external rotators, and trunk musculature while avoiding hip positions likely to provoke impingement or symptom exacerbation. Phase 1 (weeks 1–4) emphasized low- to moderate-intensity exercises (16–40% MVIC) performed in controlled positions, with phase 2 (weeks 5–8) progressing to higher-intensity strengthening (>40% MVIC) and more functionally demanding tasks. Exercises positioning the hip in impingement or with a higher likelihood of symptom aggravation were deliberately excluded, ensuring progression was guided by both muscle activation thresholds and pre-operative safety considerations. EMG metrics were therefore used to guide exercise grading and progression, with final exercise selection informed by functional relevance, patient tolerance, and symptom modification considerations.

A selection of 33 exercises from 12 studies addressing the core domains of the PREHAB FAI program were included in the final intervention and are summarized in Table 1. Three hip abduction and four hip extension strengthening exercises were included in phase 1 early rehabilitation (up to 4 weeks) because of low to moderate percentage MVIC values, and hip external rotation strengthening exercises were also commenced in this phase at 90-degree hip flexion. While adductor activities produce high to very high percentage MVIC, three exercises were selected for phase 1 as several studies in the past have underlined the importance of abductor: adductor strength ratios and its importance in improving hip joint stability and prevention of groin injuries [39,40]. Ten hip abduction, five hip extension, two hip adduction, and five trunk-strengthening exercises with high to very high percentage MVIC values were included in phase 2; later, rehabilitation (5–8 weeks) and hip external rotation strengthening exercises were progressed to 0-degree hip flexion positions.

The clam exercise for hip abduction strengthening, as well as lunge and squat exercises for hip extension strengthening, was excluded due to their low to moderate percentage MVIC and the potential for symptom aggravation. These exercises may increase compression of the gluteal tendons and posterior hip structures, including the quadratus femoris, thereby reducing the ischiofemoral space and potentially provoking symptoms in susceptible individuals, as well as positioning the hip in impingement-provoking ranges.

PREHAB FAI PROGRAM

The novel prehabilitation program for FAIS consisted of a 2-phase exercise program for 8 weeks to improve muscle strength, ROM, balance and neuromuscular control, and CV fitness, in combination with a patient education program to optimize patients prior to surgery. The specific exercises have been published in the study protocol and are now being evaluated in a feasibility trial [38].

DISCUSSION

This study describes the development of a novel prehabilitation intervention for patients with FAIS undergoing hip arthroscopy. To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically develop and report a complex, physiotherapist-led prehabilitation program at a tertiary hospital for this population using the GUIDED framework [25]. The intervention targets multiple domains including muscle strength, ROM, CV fitness, balance, and proprioception during the 6–8 weeks prior to surgery, in line with expert consensus and PPI recommendations. This novel intervention addresses commonly co-existing conditions such as gluteal and adductor tendinopathy and low back pain which often co-exist with FAIS [19,21,41] while providing structured education to reduce anxiety and improve expectation management. The PREHAB FAI program integrates a goal-based exercise intervention with PPI co-designed education materials and includes app-based engagements and physiotherapist follow-ups based on discussion group meetings with PPI. Intervention dosage and structure were informed by effective orthopedic prehabilitation programs [23] and aligned with consensus recommendations of at least two sessions per week over 6–8 weeks using a combination of both supervised and telerehabilitation sessions to facilitate access to sessions and improve adherence. Exercises are prescribed in multiple domains according to ACSM guidance [26], best evidence (Table 1, Table 2), and symptom provocation considerations. Clinical application is facilitated through a phased, physiotherapist-led model that allows individualized progression within routine pre-operative care pathways for patients listed for hip arthroscopy.

The PREHAB FAI program is intended to optimize patients prior to surgery rather than replace conservative non-operative management. It was specifically designed for individuals already listed for hip arthroscopy. Although there is overlap between prehabilitation and conservative physiotherapy for FAIS, prehabilitation is conceptually distinct as a time-limited, goal-oriented intervention focused on surgical preparation and post-operative recovery. Exercises prescribed within the PREHAB FAI program are intended exclusively for the pre-operative period, with post-operative rehabilitation directed by surgeon-specific protocols. Although symptom improvement during prehabilitation may lead some individuals to delay or avoid surgery, evaluating surgical avoidance is beyond the scope of this intervention-development study and will be examined in subsequent work.

Tailoring and sub-group considerations

The intervention development followed the MRC framework for complex interventions [24] integrating high-quality evidence, stakeholder input, and flexibility to meet patient needs. PPI insights highlighted prolonged waiting times, deconditioning, pain, and anxiety as potential barriers to adherence. Consistent with existing literature, this supported the inclusion of patient-centered education and adherence support strategies [42,43] including co-developed educational materials and use of the Physitrack app. Exercises were designed to allow progression or regression based on individual capability and symptom response. Given this adaptable structure, no further subgroup-specific modifications are anticipated. For the feasibility study, the intervention is delivered by physiotherapists with at least two years of musculoskeletal experience managing FAIS in tertiary settings, enabling individualized management of co-existing pathologies and symptom flare-ups. Delivery is flexible, combining face-to-face and telerehabilitation sessions to maximize accessibility.

Uncertainties at the end of intervention development

The next stage of intervention development is phase 2 feasibility testing [24]. The PREHAB FAI study will capture the feasibility, suitability, acceptability, and safety of the prehabilitation intervention through a mixed methods approach [38] with patient-reported outcomes collected on an exploratory basis, and the intervention will be described using the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) [44]. Adherence will be monitored via the Physitrack app (Physitrack PLC) and intervention fidelity assessed. Findings will inform refinement of the program and the design of a future definitive trial evaluating hip-specific outcomes and clinically meaningful change. Although exercises can be individually progressed or regressed, the intervention intensity is fixed (four supervised and six to eight telerehabilitation sessions); variability in intervention dosage will not be explored at this stage.

LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDY

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The absence of FAIS-specific RCT evidence necessitated extrapolation from broader orthopedic literature [23]. While this approach is methodologically justifiable, it limits external validity and generalizability. Generalizability may be further constrained as the program was developed in a tertiary care setting for patients listed for surgery. Although steps were taken to minimize bias within the informal expert consensus process [31,32], it remains methodologically weaker than more formal consensus methods such as Delphi processes, RAND/UCLA appropriateness methods, or structured nominal group techniques [45]. Consequently, the resulting recommendations may reflect prevailing clinical perspectives and should be interpreted alongside emerging empirical evidence. In addition, although muscle groups that needed to be targeted during the prehabilitation phase were identified via the expert panel consensus, EMG studies from the literature were utilized to select specific exercises. It should be acknowledged that, muscle activation alone does not necessarily correlate with clinical effectiveness, symptom improvement, or functional restoration.

CONCLUSIONS

This study integrates best available evidence, clinical expertise, and patient involvement to systematically develop a novel prehabilitation program for patients with FAIS prior to hip arthroscopy. Using a robust, theory-informed framework with meaningful PPI input, the PREHAB FAI program addresses an important gap in peri-operative care. Although prehabilitation has demonstrated benefits in other orthopedic populations, it remains under-researched in FAIS. This intervention is currently undergoing feasibility evaluation in a UK tertiary center to determine its acceptability, feasibility, and safety.
