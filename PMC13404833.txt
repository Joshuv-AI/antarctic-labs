Goal completion among participants of a region-wide hospital-based violence intervention program: an observational cohort study
Authors: Lashaunda Johnson, Taylor Kaser, Zoe Miller, Benjamin Cooper, Kateri Chapman-Kramer, Melik Coffey, Keyria Page, Vidya Eswaran, Nick Szoko, Micheal A Mancini, Kristen Mueller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHospital-based violence intervention programs (HVIPs) provide victims of violence with case management services to promote healing and reduce violent reinjury. Life Outside Violence (LOV), the St. Louis region-wide HVIP, is the first multisystem HVIP in the country. The objective of this study was to describe rates of individualized treatment plan (ITP) goal completion among adolescent and young adult LOV participants and explore differences across ITP goal categories.MethodsThis is a retrospective observational cohort study of patients aged 8–30 who presented to a LOV partner hospital for a violent injury between August 15, 2018 and December 31, 2024 and participated in LOV. The LOV REDCap database was queried for patient demographics, injury type, ITP goal category, and goal completion. Analysis included descriptive statistics of participant characteristics and goal categories set and completed.ResultsThis cohort included 263 LOV participants who were primarily black (96%), male (63%) young adults (51%) and injured by a firearm (63%). A majority of LOV participants (85%) identified an ITP goal; of these 139 (62%) completed at least one goal. The most common goal category identified was Health and healthcare (34%), within which the subcategory of Mental health was prominent (58%). Social and community goals were completed at the highest rate (70%).ConclusionsHVIPs are highly individualized programs that address the unique needs of violent injury survivors. Overall, LOV participants set and completed ITP goals at a high rate. Findings indicated robust support for social and community related goals. These findings can inform patient-centered service provision and evaluation methods among other HVIPs and violence intervention programs.

Background

Firearm injuries and death are a public health crisis in the USA that disproportionately affect black adolescents and young adults (AYA) living in areas of socioeconomic disadvantage.1–3 Patients who suffer a violent injury often receive care at hospitals; therefore, hospitals are an ideal setting to interrupt the cycle of violence for individuals at high risk of repeat injury.4

Hospital-based violence intervention programs (HVIPs) are multidisciplinary programs that support those at risk for community violence through tailored, individualized interventions. To do this, HVIPs combine the efforts of medical staff, social workers, and trusted community partners to provide trauma-informed longitudinal case management services and counseling to address unmet social needs.5 Nationally, HVIPs are guided by the Health Alliance for Violence intervention (HAVI), a national network that strives to advance HVIPs and coordinated, community-led systems that heal trauma and promote equity. Additionally, the HAVI provides tools to facilitate HVIP integration into the greater community violence intervention (CVI) ecosystem that encompasses a range of strategies aimed at reducing community firearm violence among those at highest risk (eg, street outreach, focused deterrence, cognitive behavioral interventions, mentoring/fellowship programs).5 6

St Louis, Missouri has one of the highest annual per capita homicide rates in the USA indicating an imperative need for violence prevention and intervention.7 The St. Louis region-wide HVIP, Life Outside Violence (LOV), is a response to this pressing crisis. LOV is the first HVIP in the USA to span multiple health systems, including adult and pediatric level I trauma hospitals. LOV’s mission is to promote healing, well-being, and safety among youth and young adults in the greater St. Louis area who experience violent injury through the provision of case management services and counseling.8 9 Participants are matched with a LOV clinical case manager (CCM) who is a clinically licensed social worker that provides therapeutic support and facilitates community resource connections for 6 months to 1 year after their injury.9 CCMs also work with participants to develop an individualized treatment plan (ITP), which includes the identification of personal goals to accomplish during their time in the LOV program. LOV CCMs carry a case load of 10–15 participants at a time to encourage individualized intervention where the CCMs have the time to walk alongside the patients and be present in their recovery process. LOV also provides service delivery where patients are physically located, reducing the need for patients to return to the hospital, which may be a loci of their psychologic trauma.10

The ability to support violently injured patients with meeting self-identified needs is essential for strengthening HVIPs.11 However, challenges remain in how HVIP outcomes are assessed. The most common measures of success are reduced rates of violent reinjury rates, convictions, and arrests for crime, but HVIP success is multifactorial and should include additional outcomes important to participants and their families.12 13 Prior studies have shown success in reducing reinjury rates by addressing client needs surrounding finances, mental health, employment, housing, education, and court advocacy, but limited research exists on the assessment of program success as measured by the achievement of participants’ personal goals.14 In addition, few studies have focused on these outcomes in AYA, a critical developmental period for support and intervention.

The objective of this exploratory study was to describe rates of ITP goal completion among AYA LOV participants and explore differences in completion across ITP goal categories. In doing so, we hoped to identify current gaps in support that could be met through program refinement.

Methods

Study design and setting

This is a retrospective observational cohort study of patients in St. Louis, Missouri, who presented to a LOV-partner adult or pediatric level I trauma center with a violent injury between August 15, 2018 and December 31, 2024 and enrolled and then discharged from the LOV program.

Participants

Patients who presented to the hospital were eligible for LOV program enrollment if they were aged 8–30 years at the time of injury, presented for care for a blunt assault, stabbing, or firearm injury, resided in St. Louis, MO, and are English-speaking. Previous work by our team indicates that >99% of violently injured patients eligible for LOV are non-Hispanic.9 15 16 At the time of enrollment, LOV CCMs met with participants for an initial interview to complete validated psychosocial assessments, and to establish an ITP, which includes devising goals to address while in the program. Throughout program engagement, CCMs document each touch point with participants in progress notes. We excluded patients who enrolled in the program yet never engaged in program services. In this study, we defined “never engaged” as meeting all of the following criteria: (1) No goals established, (2) No interviews nor assessments completed, and (3) No progress notes completed. Enrolled participants were considered discharged from the program if they had a discharge summary or if they enrolled prior to 2024, with an associated full year from enrollment to complete program services.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome of this exploratory study was overall rate of ITP goal completion among participants. Secondary outcomes included describing rates of ITP goal completion across six predefined primary categories and their associated secondary categories.17

ITP goal setting

Goals were set with the LOV CCM during development of participants’ ITP at the time of program enrollment. Goal setting was self-directed by the patient and then documented by the LOV CCM. In certain situations, the CCM may have provided coaching or goal options when needed (eg, helped patients identify that their postinjury anxiety, poor sleep, or appetite changes may have been related to post-traumatic stress and then helped them set goals around mental healthcare). Up to four goals could be set by LOV participants; goals were not given a priority ranking when documented by the LOV CCM.

ITP goal completion

CCMs met with enrolled participants in their preferred location (eg, home or school) weekly, on average, and addressed goal completion as part of standard program activities. The LOV CCMs would work with patients and their families and support networks (eg, teachers) to achieve their ITP goals. These activities included medical care coordination (eg, wound care supply assistance, appointment coordination, and attending appointments with LOV participants), counseling (eg, developing and acting on CCM-led therapeutic treatment plans to address underlying mental health conditions and post-traumatic stress disorder that may have resulted from the injury), and case management (eg, transportation and housing assistance).9 17 18 Goal completion was determined by self-report and then documented by the LOV CCM. When possible, this determination is supported by supplemental information, assessments (eg, changes to individual education plan reports regarding school attendance, report cards), screening tools (eg, Duke Health Profile), and patient support system feedback (eg, family member, teacher, coach).

Data source

All LOV program data are stored in a secure REDCap database V.15.0.10, which contains information about participants’ demographics, injury encounter, engagement with clinical CCMs, resource referrals, ITP goals, and discharge documentation including goal completion.

Variables

Data were queried for patient demographics (age in years, sex at birth, race (black, white, other)), injury type (blunt assault, stabbing, firearm injury), whether an ITP goal was set (yes/no) and completed (yes/no/unknown).

Our team previously developed a coding framework, guided by the Healthy People 2030 Social Determinants of Health Framework, to describe LOV participant ITP goals across six primary categories: Health and healthcare, Economic stability, Education, Social and Community Context, Other Basic Needs, Neighborhood and Built Environment.17 19 These primary goal categories were further stratified into secondary categories as noted in figure 1. For this analysis, 19% of participant goals were dual coded for both primary and secondary category, and Cohen’s kappa was computed to measure inter-rater reliability (IRR). Goal category coding was conducted by two independent reviewers (ZM, KC) with IRRs of 0.80 (95% CI 0.70 to 0.89) and 0.99 (95% CI 0.96 to 1.0) for primary and secondary categories, respectively. For computing IRRs for secondary categories, we excluded goals where coders disagreed on the primary category; secondary categories are not directly comparable as they are subcategories of different primary categories. Goal completion status (yes/no) was documented by CCMs in participant discharge summaries. In cases where goal completion status was missing, a member of our research team (ZM) reviewed progress and discharge notes when available to determine whether the goal was completed. If completion status could not be determined through note review, the completion status was deemed “unknown”.

Coding framework of primary and secondary categories for Life Outside Violence (LOV) participant individualized treatment plan (ITP) goals.

Data analysis

We computed descriptive statistics, reporting means and SDs or medians and IQRs for continuous variables as appropriate and frequencies and proportions for categorical variables. We reported participant characteristics and goals set and completed at the goal level across the primary and secondary goal categories. We calculated goal completion rate for each participant, defined as number of goals completed divided by total number of goals set (ie, the sum of the number of goals completed, not completed, and with an unknown completion status).

Results

LOV participant characteristics and goal setting

Between August 15, 2018 (program start date) and December 31, 2024, 273 patients presented to a LOV partner hospital for treatment of a violent injury and enrolled in LOV. Of those who enrolled, nine (4%) were excluded due to insufficient engagement and one was excluded due to inconsistent documentation of goals (<1%). The final cohort of 263 LOV participants was primarily black (96%), male (63%), young adult (51%), and injured by a firearm (63%) (table 1). There were near-equal rates of program participation by AYA.

Among the 263 participants, 223 (85%) set at least one goal during the study period (table 1). Of these, 175 (78%) set two or more goals. LOV participants collectively set 532 goals; of these, 253 (48%) were successfully completed, 248 were not completed (46%), and 31 goals had unknown completion status (6%). Discussion

HVIPs are highly individualized programs that address the unique needs of survivors of violent injury. Previous HVIP outcomes research has largely focused on reduced reinjury rates, criminal arrests, and convictions as metrics for program success.12 However, success in achieving these metrics typically takes a long time to materialize. Process metrics can be identified, measured, and reported as patient-centered interim outcomes of HVIP success. Our findings reinforce a growing call to include a range of health and social outcomes beyond violent reinjury to evaluate the impact of HVIP’s on patients’ lives.13 The literature additionally indicates that the presence of future orientation (hopes and goals for the future) is a protective factor against community violence among AYA risk behaviors.20–23 Categories used in this study were developed through qualitative analysis of goals set by patients in the LOV program.17 These categories, with a prioritization of protective factor enhancement in addition to risk factor reduction, align with, and provide external validation for, many of Monopoli et al’s recommended core set of outcome measures for HVIPs.24

Prior work also demonstrates that injured patients who participate in HVIPs are more likely to set and achieve short-term social and health-related goals than non-HVIP patients.13 14 Our study supports these findings that through achievement of ITP goals, HVIP participants engage with the healthcare system and address goals that may be tied to social determinants of health that impact their lives.13

We found that primary goals related to Health and healthcare, and specifically secondary mental health goals, were frequently cited among participants. This suggests that participants may have numerous unmet physical and mental health needs, given their recent traumatic injury. Participants may also be more likely to set healthcare-related goals, given the hospital-based nature of the program. Both physical and mental health goals had among the highest completion rates, suggesting HVIPs are well equipped to address healthcare needs after violent injury. This finding is supported by a recent study evaluating physical and mental health outcomes among engaged adult HVIP participants, compared with violently injured patients who did not receive services, which found that engaged participants had significantly better mental health outcomes 6–12 months after violent injury.25 Another study assessing goal completion among HVIP participants in Boston reported similar findings.26 In this study, the highest goal completion rates were in the Social and community category, supporting the principle that a sense of community and belonging is a fundamental human need that people tend to seek after their most basic needs are met.27 These relationships can influence mental, physical, and economic outcomes and overall well-being.28 29 Additionally, all of the LOV CCMs are intentionally clinically licensed therapists, with the skills to address both mental health needs and build interpersonal relationships with LOV participants and their families. For this reason, HVIPs such as LOV can help address gaps in available and accessible mental healthcare services in the USA for victims of violence and their families.24 30

In our study, the primary goal categories of Economic stability, Education, and Neighborhood and built environment had the lowest completion rates. Completion of these goals may be impacted by upstream structural factors known to be associated with risk for experiencing violent injury.31 For example, neighborhoods with more tree coverage have been shown to experience less firearm violence than those with fewer trees.32 Further work is needed to understand the barriers to goal completion within these domains, and to develop solutions and partnerships to better assist patients in achieving their goals.

The JAMA Summit on Firearm violence recently detailed the evidence base for intervention that demonstrated a significant reduction in firearm violence, highlighting CVI programs as a viable strategy moving forward.33 The present study, along with a Jay et al’s recent study demonstrating that participants who engage consistently with HVIPs have significant long-term reductions in community violence outcomes such as violent reinjury and violent perpetration, speak to the need to place HVIPs within the CVI ecosystem.34 35 Recognition of HVIPs as a key component of the CVI ecosystem offers the opportunity to bolster greater collaboration between HVIPs, community organizations, law enforcement, and governmental agencies to advocate for policies and programming that address structural racism and systemic inequities.33 35

Limitations

Our findings may be impacted by selection bias in our cohort, as the goals in this study were generated by participants in the LOV program, who represent a subset of all injured patients cared for in LOV partner hospitals. It is important to note that the goals represented in the present analysis are participant-identified and, therefore, illustrate how participants conceptualized their own goals for the future. CCMs’ work with participants was dynamic throughout the course of program participation, shifting to best meet participants’ needs. The goals represented in this analysis did not reflect the sum of work completed by the CCM/participant duo, as CCMs often supported participants in domains outside of these goals. For example, one participant cited goals related to mental health and employment. During her time in the program, she was also supported in enrolling her child in daycare (offspring well-being), obtaining cancer care for her grandmother (social service benefits), and moving residences (housing); this type of support was not captured in this study.

Another limitation of the present study was the use of binary (complete/incomplete) goal completion. This method led to an under-reporting of participant goal attainment, as participants’ progress towards their goals was not captured in this analysis. For example, one participant cited a goal to obtain his driver’s license. During their time in the program, they were supported in studying for and preparing to take the written permit examination. However, this goal was marked as incomplete, because they did not fully obtain his license during their time in the program. Another participant wanted to get a job in construction. During their time in the program, they were successful in obtaining employment, however it was not in a construction-related field. Therefore, the goal was marked as incomplete. These examples indicate a need to explore and identify more nuanced ways to capture participant progress towards goal completion. In the future, we plan to implement more robust tracking methods that will allow us to holistically assess goal attainment, and add priority ranking of goals to the CCM documentation system to better capture which individual goals are most important to participants.

Conclusion

This study evaluated AYA HIVP participant ITP goal completion as a measure of an HVIP program success. Overall, LOV participants are setting and completing ITP goals at a high rate, especially for goals related to physical and mental health. Findings indicated robust support for social and community related goals. These findings can inform patient-centered service provision and implementation of these patient-centered outcomes as key markers of program efficacy and evaluation for other HVIPs and violence intervention programs.
