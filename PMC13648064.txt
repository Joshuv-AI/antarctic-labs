Associations of Green
Space and Air Pollution Mixture
with Heterogeneous Biological Aging Patterns

Guoqiang Ma, Jing Xu, Yishu Li, Yuefei Wu, Qian Li, Xuan Cao, Zitong Zhao, Jingyan Gao, Lijun Gao, Lina Yan, Xiaolin Zhang

Abstract

Evidence on associations
between green space, air pollution
mixture,
and heterogeneous biological aging patterns remains limited. We investigated
the opposing effects of green space and air pollution mixture on biological
aging patterns using an accelerated longitudinal design based on repeated-measures
data from 11,760 adults (aged 20–65 years) in a high-pollution
Chinese city. Biological age acceleration (BAA) was derived via the
Klemera-Doubal method. Group-based trajectory modeling identified
three stable BAA profiles, labeled as “slow”, “moderate”,
and “accelerated” aging, which were primarily distinguished
by overall BAA magnitude rather than divergent slopes over age. Multinomial
generalized linear mixed models and weighted quantile sum regression
were applied to assess environmental associations. Higher green space
(500 m normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI)) was associated
with lower odds of membership in the “accelerated aging”
trajectory (OR: 0.84; 95% CI: 0.75, 0.95). Even under limited exposure
contrast in this high-pollution setting, higher exposures to PM2.5, PM10, SO2, and O3 were
associated with higher odds of membership in the accelerated aging
trajectory. The air pollution mixture showed a consistent positive
association (OR: 1.16; 95% CI: 1.08, 1.24), driven primarily by O3 and PM10. Mediation analysis revealed that 25.0%
of the protective association of green space was mediated by reduced
pollution exposure. These findings highlight green space as a vital
resilience resource, supporting synergistic urban greening and pollution
control strategies to promote healthy aging.

Introduction

As the global community confronts the
dual challenges of population
aging and rapid environmental change, elucidating how urban environments
shape biological aging has become an imperative in environmental health
sciences. According to the World Population Prospects 2024, the global
population aged 65 and older is projected to double from approximately
800 million currently to 1.6 billion (16% of the global total) by
2050. However, extended longevity does
not guarantee a commensurate expansion of healthspan. The burden of
chronic noncommunicable diseases escalates with advancing age, extending
years lived with disability and placing substantial strain on healthcare
systems. Consequently, “healthy
aging” has superseded lifespan extension as the central tenet
of global public health strategy. Achieving
this goal requires the precise identification and mitigation of modifiable
drivers governing the aging process.

Traditionally, aging research
has relied on chronological age (CA).
While CA merely reflects the passage of time, it overlooks profound
interindividual variability in physiological integrity and functional
decline. Aging is fundamentally a dynamic
process characterized by the progressive accumulation of damage at
the tissue, cellular, and molecular levels.
,
 To
quantify this interindividual variability, researchers have developed
“biological age” (BA) metrics based on clinical biomarkers. Unlike epigenetic clocks that primarily reflect
molecular aging processes, clinical biomarker-based algorithms such
as the Klemera-Doubal method (KDM) used in this study characterize
systemic physiological integrity and organ function decline, and may
be more sensitive to systemic environmental stressors. As a composite
measure of physiological wear-and-tear, functional decline, and disease
susceptibility, BA serves as a more robust predictor of morbidity
and mortality compared to CA.
,
 The plasticity of BA
offers a unique vantage point to assess the impact of external determinants,
including environmental exposures. Furthermore,
recent evidence suggests that biological aging does not follow a uniform
linear pattern but rather unfolds through distinct dynamic trajectories
across individuals.
,
 More recently, conceptual frameworks
on biological age clocks have highlighted important limitations of
cross-sectional biological age measures, emphasizing that single-time-point
assessments cannot distinguish stable age differences from changes
in aging patterns over time, thereby underscoring the value of repeated
measurements for characterizing biological aging trajectories. Despite this growing recognition, the environmental
factors associated with heterogeneous biological aging trajectories
remain poorly understood.

Long-term environmental exposure acts
as a critical external force
shaping these dynamic trajectories. Urbanization introduces a complex
exposome where air pollution and green space represent co-occurring
yet opposing environmental forces. Air pollution is a well-established
health hazard, linked to aging-related cardiovascular and metabolic
pathologies via systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and DNA damage
pathways.
−

 Conversely, urban green space constitutes a vital
ecosystem resource; higher exposure is consistently associated with
reduced mortality and improved physical and mental well-being.
,
 The potential mechanisms are multidimensional, encompassing stress
reduction, social cohesion, and the promotion of physical activity.
−

 The capacity of green space to improve local air quality provides
a critical mechanistic entry point for exploring the interplay between
these countervailing forces.

Recent studies have made notable
strides in linking environmental
exposures with biological aging, including investigations into multiplexed
environmental factors with multidimensional aging metrics, urban green and blue space with accelerated
aging, and air pollution and greenspace
in relation to biological aging. Despite
this progress, several limitations impede a comprehensive understanding
of how the environment modulates aging. First, the preponderance of
existing studies relies on cross-sectional designs or single-time
aging metrics, providing only “static” associations
between exposures and biological age; such snapshot approaches are
vulnerable to transient physiological fluctuations and random measurement
errors, failing to capture the stable, longitudinal dynamics of aging.
−

 Second, environmental factors are frequently assessed in isolation,
overlooking the complex “mixture effects” of co-occurring
pollutants, which may underestimate the cumulative exposure burden
in real-world scenarios.
,
 Third, emerging evidence
suggests that green space may buffer air pollution-related cardiometabolic,
respiratory, cerebrovascular, frailty, and mental health impacts,
and that reduced air pollution may mediate some health benefits of
greener environments.
−

 However, within the current framework of biological aging research,
it remains insufficiently explored whether the association between
green space and accelerated biological aging is independent of air
pollution or is partly explained by lower harmful pollutant exposures.

To systematically address these challenges, this study uses repeated
measurements from a longitudinal health examination cohort to identify
heterogeneous biological aging patterns at the population level. We
constructed an integrated analytical framework combining dynamic aging
trajectories, air pollution mixture effects, and mediation analysis.
We aim to comprehensively evaluate the complex impacts of long-term
exposure to green space and air pollution on longitudinal aging patterns
and to quantitatively disentangle the mediating role of air pollution
in this relationship.

Methods

Study Design
and Participants

We employed an accelerated
longitudinal (cohort-sequential) design using repeated health examination
records from working-age adults. The study was conducted in Shijiazhuang,
a major industrial metropolis in Northern China characterized by high
population density and substantial industrial pollution burden. This
setting provides a critical context for investigating environmental
influences on biological aging under high baseline exposure conditions.
The initial data set included 18,315 individuals who underwent annual
health examinations between January 1, 2021, and December 31, 2023.
Participants were eligible if they contributed at least two examination
records during the study period, enabling the synthesis of repeated
observations across overlapping age ranges. Exclusion criteria were:
(1) missing key baseline covariates (e.g., age, sex, workplace); (2)
baseline diagnosis of cancer or other severe diseases; and (3) >
50%
missingness in biomarkers required for biological age estimation at
any single examination. Following these exclusions, missingness for
most covariates and biomarkers among retained participants was generally
below 10%. Sporadically missing values in the retained sample were
imputed using a nonparametric random forest–based algorithm
(missForest package) to preserve sample size and reduce information
loss associated with complete-case analysis. This approach is well
suited for epidemiological data with mixed variable types and complex,
potentially nonlinear relationships and has been shown to outperform
conventional imputation methods under similar missingness patterns.

To identify robust and generalizable population-level
aging patterns, latent aging trajectories were constructed using the
full eligible sample of 16,615 individuals spanning the adult age
spectrum (age distribution detailed in Figure S2). This strategy maximized age coverage for trajectory derivation
within the accelerated longitudinal framework and was used exclusively
to derive the population-level BAA trajectory groups. Participants
aged >65 years contributed to trajectory derivation but were not
included
in any exposure-association model. All subsequent association analyses
were restricted to 11,760 adults aged 20–65 years (totaling
29,620 observations). This age restriction was applied to ensure the
validity of exposure assessment based on workplace addresses, as well
as to maintain physiological relevance and methodological consistency.
The detailed participant flowchart is presented in Figure S1. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee
of Hebei Medical University (No. 2026020), and all participants provided
written informed consent.

Exposure Assessment

The exposure
window for all environmental
metrics was defined as 2021–2023. We first calculated the annual
mean exposure for each year and then averaged these values to derive
a three-year mean, representing the cumulative and typical exposure
level during the study period. All assessments were based on geocoded
workplace addresses. We prioritized workplace exposure to capture
environmental conditions during the working population’s active
daytime hours. This period is typically characterized by peak traffic-related
pollution and increased respiration rates, making workplace environments
a biologically relevant proxy for personal exposure, particularly
for working-age adults.
,

Green Space

Green
space exposure was quantified using
the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI). We employed a high-resolution
(250 m) monthly NDVI data set covering China, derived from the MOD13Q1
product of the Aqua/Terra-MODIS satellite sensors. To estimate individual exposure, we calculated the annual
mean NDVI within four circular buffers (250, 500, 750, and 1000 m
radii) centered on the workplace address. An area-weighted average
of all NDVI pixels overlapping the buffer was computed to ensure precise
estimation. The 500-m buffer was selected as the primary metric, with
other sizes used for sensitivity analyses.

Air Pollutants

Long-term (annual mean) exposure to
six major air pollutants (PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, CO, and O3) was assessed using
the ChinaHighAirPollutants (CHAP) data set.
−

 The CHAP model has been extensively validated across
China, including Northern China, with consistently high cross-validated
performance reported for major pollutants (cross-validation R2 generally exceeding 0.80), supporting its applicability for
fine-scale exposure assessment in the present study area. This data
set is generated via machine learning fusion of ground-level monitoring,
satellite remote sensing, atmospheric reanalysis, and chemical transport
model data, yielding high-resolution (1 km × 1 km) gridded data.
To refine spatial precision, we employed bilinear interpolation at
each geocoded workplace coordinate. By calculating the distance-weighted
average of the four nearest grid centroids, this method mitigates
spatial boundary effects and captures continuous pollution gradients, thereby improving the characterization of the
ambient environment during working hours.

Outcome Assessment:
Biological Aging Trajectories

The
primary outcome was the biological aging trajectory. We applied a
three-step analytical framework to identify distinct aging patterns.

Step
1: Calculation of BA

We calculated sex-specific
BA using the KDM. Briefly, KDM is a multibiomarker algorithm that
estimates biological age by integrating age-related clinical biomarkers
into a single composite measure of physiological integrity. Detailed
formulas and biomarker selection criteria are provided in Methods S1.1.
,
 This method
has been extensively validated in various Chinese cohorts, confirming
its applicability and robustness for our target population.
−

 We first calculated the Pearson correlation coefficients between
each candidate biomarker and CA. Biomarkers with a correlation of
|r| > 0.1 were initially selected (Table S1). To address multicollinearity, for
pairs of highly
correlated biomarkers (|r| > 0.7), we prioritized
the one with greater clinical relevance or data completeness. Ultimately, 15 biomarkers were included in the
sex-specific models (Table S1). Non-normally
distributed biomarkers were log-transformed prior to calculation.

Step 2: Calculation of BAA

Because
BA is strongly correlated
with CA, BAA was calculated as the marginal residuals from a linear
mixed-effects model (LMM) regressing BA on CA. This approach removes
the population-level aging trend; positive BAA values indicate an
older biological age than expected for chronological age.
,
 The model yielded an adjusted Intraclass Correlation Coefficient
(ICC) of 0.65, indicating that most of the variance in BAA reflects
stable interindividual differences rather than transient fluctuations.
This longitudinal stability supports the subsequent use of trajectory
modeling to identify distinct aging patterns.

Step 3: Identification
of Heterogeneous Aging Trajectories

GBTM was employed to
identify latent subpopulations sharing similar
longitudinal BAA patterns. The resulting
trajectory membership was used as the categorical outcome in the exposure-association
analyses. Using the lcmm package in R, BAA was modeled as a quadratic
function of age to capture potential nonlinear trajectories across
the lifespan. Because BAA reflects deviations
from CA, the identified trajectories represent distinct longitudinal
patterns of biological aging acceleration relative to the population
mean, rather than absolute age-related trends.

By using age
as the time scale, we effectively implemented an accelerated longitudinal
design. This design allows for the synthesis of overlapping short-term
repeated measures from individuals across the entire adult age spectrum
into comprehensive life-course aging trajectories. This approach is particularly advantageous for distinguishing
sustained physiological aging patterns from short-term noise. Given
the unbalanced longitudinal data structure (individuals with 2 or
3 repeated measures), GBTM effectively incorporates all available
observations even for individuals with only two time points to inform
the estimation of trajectory shapes, thereby maximizing sample size
and statistical power. We fitted a series
of models with 1 to 5 latent classes. The optimal number of groups
was determined based on a comprehensive evaluation of statistical
fit indices and interpretability, including: lowest Bayesian Information
Criterion (BIC), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Integrated
Completed Likelihood (ICL); high average posterior probability of
assignment (APPA > 0.7); adequate group size (>5% of the sample);
and clinical meaningfulness of the trajectory shapes.
,−

 Based on these criteria, the three-group model was
selected as optimal. These trajectories were distinct and labeled
as “slow aging”, “moderate aging”, and
“accelerated aging”. Consequently, aging trajectory
was treated as a nominal categorical variable in subsequent analyses.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics of the
11,760 participants included in the final analysis were stratified
by aging trajectory. Continuous variables, including the three-year
mean environmental exposure levels (NDVI and air pollutants) representing
long-term typical exposure, are presented as mean ± SD or median
(IQR) and were compared using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) or the
Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables are presented as
frequency (percentage) and were compared using the Chi-square test.

To examine the association between environmental exposures and
aging trajectories, we constructed a series of generalized linear
mixed models (GLMMs) with the “slow aging” trajectory
serving as the reference group. To account for potential data clustering
within workplaces, a random intercept for “workplace”
was included in all models. Based on the literature and data availability,
the covariate-adjusted models included the following core potential
confounders: sex, baseline age (continuous), smoking status (never/ever),
alcohol consumption (never/ever), history of chronic noncommunicable
diseases (e.g., diabetes, hypertension), and individual-level socioeconomic
status, operationalized using household income categories (<100,000,
100,000–200,000, and >200,000 RMB/year). We additionally fitted unadjusted models that included
no fixed-effect covariates, retaining only the exposure and the workplace
random intercept, so that estimates before and after covariate adjustment
could be compared. Continuous exposure variables were standardized
(Z-scores) to facilitate comparison of effect sizes and improve model
convergence. We modeled green space exposures (at different buffer
sizes) and single air pollutants separately. Results are reported
as Odds Ratios (ORs) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) per standard
deviation (SD) increase in exposure.

Potential nonlinear relationships
were explored using generalized
additive mixed models (GAMMs). Standardized
exposure variables were fitted using penalized cubic regression splines,
with smoothness automatically determined via restricted maximum likelihood
(REML). Nonlinearity was assessed by testing whether the effective
degrees of freedom (EDF) of the smooth term was significantly greater
than 1. Concentration–response curves with 95% CIs were plotted
using the median exposure as the reference.

To evaluate the
joint effect of the air pollution mixture on aging
trajectories, we applied weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression. WQS regression summarizes highly correlated
pollutants into a single index, assigning each pollutant a non-negative
weight to reflect its relative contribution under a prespecified direction
of association. Given that standard WQS implementations have limited
support for multinomial outcomes, and preliminary analyses indicated
no significant differences between the “slow aging”
and “moderate aging” trajectories, we dichotomized the
outcome into “accelerated” versus “non-accelerated”
for this phase and subsequent mechanistic analyses, thereby enhancing
model stability and interpretability. To prevent overfitting, the
data set was randomly partitioned into a 40% training set (for weight
optimization) and a 60% validation set (for effect estimation). Weights
for the six pollutants (quartiled) were estimated using 1,000 bootstrap
samples. These weights (summing to 1) reflect the relative contribution
of each pollutant to the overall mixture effect. The derived WQS index
was then entered as a continuous variable into a covariate-adjusted
GLMM to estimate the association between the mixture and the odds
of accelerated aging.

We further investigated the complex interplay
between green space
(primary 500-m buffer) and the air pollution mixture (WQS index).
First, to test for interaction, we compared nested GLMMs: Model 1
(WQS index + covariates), Model 2 (Model 1 + NDVI main effect), and
Model 3 (Model 2 + WQS × NDVI interaction term). Model fit was
evaluated using the likelihood ratio test (LRT) and AIC/BIC. The P value of the interaction term served as the primary criterion
for statistical significance. Upon confirming no significant interaction
(P > 0.1), we proceeded to formal mediation analysis
to explore whether the air pollution mixture (WQS index) mediates
the potential protective association between green space (NDVI) and
aging trajectories. Using the mediation package in R, we estimated
the total effect (TE), natural direct effect (NDE), and natural indirect
effect (NIE) via a two-step regression approach (regressing the mediator
on exposure, then the outcome on both exposure and mediator), adjusting
for the same set of covariates. Confidence
intervals were obtained using quasi-Bayesian approximation (1,000
simulations), and the proportion mediated was calculated as NIE/TE.

The robustness of the findings was systematically assessed across
data, model specifications, and potential biases. (1) Robustness of
association: We additionally employed linear mixed models (LMM) with
continuous BAA as the outcome to verify the consistency of the results
with the primary trajectory analysis while accounting for the same
set of covariates and random effects. (2) Subgroup analysis: Stratified
by sex and age (<40 vs ≥ 40 years) to detect potential effect
modification; (3) Exposure categorization: Modeling continuous exposures
as quartiles; (4) Temporal stability: Repeating the primary analysis
using a one-year lagged exposure window (2020–2022); (5) Spatial
scale and alternative greenspace metric: Re-evaluating the primary
models using NDVI at alternative buffer sizes (250 m, 750 m, and 1000
m) and employing the Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI) to ensure the
robustness of the findings across different spatial scales and vegetation
indices; (6) Missing data: Repeating analyses on the complete-case
data set (without imputation); (7) Occupational confounding: additionally
adjusting for five broad occupational categories, classified using
individual-level occupational information and workplace unit names,
to assess potential residual confounding by occupational characteristics
related to workplace-based exposure assessment; (8) Outcome model
validation: Reidentifying trajectories using growth mixture modeling
(GMM), a method with more relaxed assumptions than GBTM; (9) Mixture model validation: Using quantile
g-computation (qgcomp) as an alternative mixture-modeling approach
to WQS, qgcomp estimates the overall
mixture effect by jointly shifting all pollutants by one quantile
and does not require the assumption of a unidirectional mixture effect.
We additionally tested parameter sensitivity by changing WQS quantiles
from quartiles to deciles; and (10) Unmeasured confounding: Calculating
E-values for significant associations to assess the potential impact
of unmeasured confounders.

All
statistical analyses were performed using R software (version
4.4.1). Key packages included lme4, lcmm, mgcv, gWQS, mediation, qgcomp,
missForest, and Evalue. All tests were two-sided, with P value <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics
and Environmental Exposure of Participants

The final analytical
cohort consisted of 11,760 adults (aged 20–65
years) who contributed a total of 29,620 observations during the study
period (mean follow-up frequency: 2.51; 6,100 participants completed
three visits). As detailed in , the overall mean (SD) BA of the population was 42.52
(11.7) years, with a mean BAA of 0.08 (7.73) years. Females accounted
for approximately 60% of the sample. The majority of participants
were never-smokers (87%) and never-drinkers (74%). Detailed baseline
characteristics and the distribution of environmental exposures are
provided in .

Identification of Heterogeneous Aging Trajectories

Based on GBTM, we identified three distinct latent aging trajectories
characterized by stable stratification across the studied adult age
spectrum ().
Although the model allowed for nonlinear evolution using quadratic
terms, the identified subgroups were primarily distinguished by differences
in intercepts (overall BAA magnitude at the population level) rather
than by divergent slopes over time. The “Slow aging”
group (33%, green profile) consistently remained below the population
mean, reflecting a sustained state of relative physiological resilience.
The “Moderate aging” group (50%, blue profile) followed
a trajectory centered around zero, consistent with normative physiological
aging. In contrast, the “Accelerated aging” group (17%,
red profile) exhibited systematically elevated BAA levels across the
entire age range, observable from early adulthood (age 20) through
later adulthood. The model selection criteria and fit indices are
presented in Table S2.

Association between Green
Space and Aging Trajectories

In fully adjusted models, we
observed an inverse association between
green space exposure and the odds of membership in the “accelerated
aging” trajectory. Among all buffer sizes tested, NDVI within
the 500-m buffer exhibited the most robust protective association
(A). Each 1-SD
increase in NDVI (500-m buffer) was associated with 16% lower odds
of membership in the “accelerated aging” trajectory
relative to the “slow aging” trajectory (OR: 0.84, 95%
CI: 0.75–0.95). The corresponding unadjusted NDVI estimates
were similar in direction and magnitude, although covariate adjustment
modestly strengthened the inverse associations with the “accelerated
aging” trajectory (Table S16). GAMM
smoothing tests suggested an approximately linear exposure-response
relationship (all EDF ≈ 1; A–D), with no evidence of meaningful
nonlinearity.

Association between Single Air Pollutants
and Aging Trajectories

As shown in B, multiple air pollutants were associated
with higher odds of adverse
aging trajectories. Compared to the “slow aging” trajectory,
each 1-SD increase in PM10 and NO2 was associated
with higher odds of membership in the “moderate aging”
trajectory, with ORs of 1.06 (95% CI: 1.02–1.11) and 1.05 (95%
CI: 1.00–1.10), respectively. For the “accelerated aging”
trajectory, significant associations were observed for PM2.5 (OR: 1.13, 95% CI: 1.05–1.21), PM10 (OR: 1.13,
95% CI: 1.06–1.20), SO2 (OR: 1.15, 95% CI: 1.05–1.26),
and O3 (OR: 1.15, 95% CI: 1.03–1.28). SO2 and O3 exhibited the strongest effect magnitudes. The
unadjusted pollutant estimates were also broadly comparable with the
covariate-adjusted estimates; adjustment modestly strengthened several
associations with the “accelerated aging” trajectory
but did not materially change the overall interpretation (Table S16). Inspection of the GAMM splines suggested
departures from linearity for PM2.5 (EDF ≈ 4.5, P < 0.001) and PM10 (EDF ≈ 2.6, P < 0.001) in the “accelerated aging” versus
“slow aging” comparison (E,F). However, both pollutants exhibited
uneven and multimodal exposure distributions, resulting in limited
data support across portions of their exposure ranges; the detailed
shapes of these fitted curves should therefore be interpreted cautiously.
Across matched percentile-based exposure contrasts, the GAMM- and
GLMM-derived estimates were broadly comparable over the exposure ranges
with the greatest data support (Table S15). In supplementary analyses based on Gaussian-mixture exposure components,
exposure-by-component interaction tests provided no statistical evidence
that the linear slopes differed between components for either PM2.5 (P
interaction = 0.731) or PM10 (P
interaction = 0.261) (Table S17). The association for SO2 approximated a linear positive trend (EDF ≈ 1.0, P < 0.01), whereas O3 showed suggestive nonlinearity
(EDF ≈ 2.6, P < 0.1; G,I).

Joint Effect of Air Pollution
Mixture

We applied binary
WQS models to estimate the joint effect of the six-pollutant mixture.
The WQS analysis demonstrated a significant positive association between
the air pollution mixture and the odds of “accelerated aging”.
Each 1-SD increase in the WQS index was associated with 16% higher
odds of membership in the “accelerated aging” group
(OR: 1.16, 95% CI: 1.08–1.24) (B). Weights analysis identified O3 and PM10 as the primary contributors to the mixture effect,
with weights of 0.39 and 0.26, respectively (A). These findings were corroborated by the
binary qgcomp model, which similarly indicated a significant positive
association between the mixture and odds of accelerated aging (OR:
1.30, 95% CI: 1.16–1.47), highly consistent with the WQS results.
Weight analysis in qgcomp also identified PM10 and O3 as the dominant positive contributors to the overall mixture
association (Figure S12, Table S4).

Interaction and Mediation Analysis

We first examined
potential interactions between green space (500-m NDVI) and the air
pollution mixture (WQS index) (Table S3). No significant multiplicative interaction was detected (P
interaction = 0.70), suggesting that these factors
may influence aging largely independently. Subsequent mediation analysis
() revealed
a significant total protective effect of green space exposure on the
odds of “accelerated aging”. Decomposition of this effect
indicated statistically significant natural direct and indirect effects.
Specifically, the air pollution mixture partially mediated the association
between green space and odds of membership in the “accelerated
aging” trajectory, with an estimated proportion mediated of
25% (95% CI: 8.7–67%). This suggests that approximately one-quarter
of the protective benefit of green space is attributable to the reduction
in ambient air pollution exposure.

Subgroup Analysis

Stratified analyses
by sex and age
were conducted to explore potential effect modification (Tables S5–S6). Stratified analyses by
sex suggested potential differences in susceptibility. In the comparison
of “accelerated aging vs. slow aging”, the association
with O3 was evident almost exclusively in females (Female
OR: 1.25, 95% CI: 1.10–1.42; Male OR: 1.00, 95% CI: 0.88–1.14; P
interaction = 0.003). Furthermore, although
interaction terms did not reach statistical significance (P
interaction > 0.05), the protective trend
of
green space and the association estimates for PM2.5, SO2, and NO2 appeared more pronounced in the female
subgroup.

Stratified analyses by age also showed variations
in associations, with distinct patterns across environmental factors
(Tables S7–S8). For green space,
the protective association against “accelerated aging”
was stronger in younger adults (<40 years), with significant interactions
across all buffer sizes (P
interaction:
0.004–0.031). Associations with PM2.5, SO2, and O3 were concentrated in the older group (≥40
years), whereas the associations with PM10 and NO2 appeared more pronounced in the younger group (<40 years). In
addition, the interaction for NO2 was statistically significant
(P
interaction = 0.006), with the association
largely confined to the younger group (<40 years OR: 1.13; ≥40
years OR: 0.97). A similar pattern was observed for CO (P
interaction = 0.007).

Sensitivity Analysis

A series of sensitivity analyses
systematically substantiated the robustness of our primary findings
(detailed in Supporting Information). First,
regarding the outcome definition, LMM with continuous BAA yielded
results highly consistent with the primary trajectory analysis. After
adjusting for the same set of covariates and random effects, higher
exposure to air pollutants (PM2.5, PM10, NO2, SO2, and O3) remained positively associated
with increased BAA, while green space (NDVI-500m) showed a protective
association (Figure S7). Second, regarding
data and exposure definitions, results remained materially unchanged
when using a one-year lagged exposure window (2020–2022), alternative
NDVI buffer sizes, an alternative greenness metric (EVI) (Figure S10), or the nonimputed complete data
set (Tables S11–S12). Third, regarding
model specification, categorizing continuous exposures into quartiles
yielded similar dose–response trends (Tables S9–S10). Fourth, to address potential confounding by
features of occupation, we further adjusted models for five broad
occupational categories. The associations between green space, most
air pollutants, and biological aging trajectories were materially
unchanged after this additional adjustment (Figure S13). The association for NO2 was modestly attenuated
and no longer reached statistical significance, while the overall
pattern and interpretation of the primary findings remained consistent.
Fifth, validation of the outcome model using growth mixture modeling
(GMM), which assumes more relaxed parameters, identified three aging
trajectories with shapes highly similar to those from GBTM. The concordance
between GBTM and GMM classifications was excellent (unweighted Cohen’s
Kappa = 0.95), indicating that our trajectory groups were robust to
model assumptions (Table S13). Sixth, rigorous
validation of the mixture analysis confirmed our results. Increasing
the WQS quantile resolution from quartiles to deciles did not alter
the direction or magnitude of associations (Table S4). Moreover, exploratory multinomial qgcomp analysis revealed
a gradient of higher odds, where the mixture was associated with both
“moderate aging” (OR: 1.13, 95% CI: 1.03–1.26)
and “accelerated aging” (OR: 1.39, 95% CI: 1.20–1.60)
compared to “slow aging” (Table S4). Finally, E-value calculations for significant associations
ranged from 1.18 to 1.39 (Table S14), values
consistent with typical observational environmental studies, suggesting
that unmeasured confounding is unlikely to fully explain the observed
associations.

Discussion

In this study, we identified
three distinct
biological aging trajectories
(“slow aging”, “moderate aging”, and “accelerated
aging”) characterized by significant heterogeneity. Recent
studies have linked multiplexed environmental factors, green/blue
space and air pollution with biological aging metrics.
−

 Building on this evidence, this study extends prior work by jointly
evaluating workplace-based green space and air pollution mixtures
in relation to heterogeneous biological aging trajectories, while
further exploring the potential mediating role of air pollution. Across
multiple analytical approaches, including concentration–response
modeling and sensitivity analyses using LMM, we observed consistent
associations between environmental exposures and aging trajectories.
Higher long-term exposure to green space was associated with lower
odds of membership in the “accelerated aging” trajectory
(OR: 0.84 per SD increase in NDVI-500m), whereas elevated exposure
to air pollutants (PM2.5, PM10, NO2, SO2, and O3) corresponded to higher odds
of membership. Despite the relatively low spatial variation of air
pollutants within the study area, significant associations were observed.
This suggests that in a high-pollution background setting, population-level
biological aging processes may be sensitive to even modest differences
in cumulative exposure burden. Mixture analysis further revealed a
positive association between the overall air pollution burden and
the odds of accelerated aging, with PM10 and O3 identified as the dominant mixture contributors. Notably, mediation
analysis indicated that approximately 25% of the protective association
between green space and biological aging trajectories may be statistically
mediated by lower air pollution levels in greener environments. However,
since environmental exposures were assessed concurrently, these mediation
findings should be interpreted as associative patterns consistent
with a mediation hypothesis rather than as evidence of definitive
causal pathways.

We observed that individuals with higher long-term
green space
exposure were more likely to exhibit a healthier aging pattern. This
finding is consistent with recent evidence linking urban green/blue
spaces with healthier biological aging metrics, as well as previous
cross-sectional studies; for instance, a study of 156,690 participants
reported a protective association between green space and epigenetic
age acceleration, and another cohort
study linked urban greenery to reduced KDM biological age. However, by identifying distinct aging patterns
based on repeated measurements, our study extends these “static”
associations to a dynamic longitudinal perspective. Rather than reflecting
transient physiological improvements, our findings suggest that green
space may contribute to sustained physiological resilience against
biological aging. In urban environments characterized by persistently
high pollution burdens, green space may therefore function as a vital
resilience resource. The potential biological mechanisms underlying
this protection are likely multifaceted. First, mediation analysis
provides quantitative support for an indirect pathway whereby the
protective association of green space is partly explained by lower
ambient air pollution levels. Second, the predominant direct effect
likely stems from the psychophysiological restorative functions of
nature. Evidence suggests that contact with natural environments can
alleviate chronic stress, improve mental health, and downregulate
the overactivation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis,
thereby mitigating systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, processes
intimately linked to biological aging.
,,
 Additionally, green spaces may promote physical activity,
a well-recognized behavioral pathway contributing to slower biological
aging. While physical activity could not be directly assessed in this
study, it likely operates as a complementary mechanism alongside stress
alleviation and air pollution mitigation.

Regarding air pollution,
this study substantiates and extends prior
evidence by showing that both individual pollutants and the overall
air pollution mixture were associated with heterogeneous BAA patterns.
The single-pollutant results were broadly consistent with prior findings
in European and Chinese populations showing that exposure to PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and O3 is
associated with accelerated biological aging.
,,
 Notably, these associations were detected
despite relatively limited interindividual variation in air pollutant
concentrations, likely reflecting three-year exposure averaging, clustering
of participants within shared workplaces, and the uniformly elevated
pollution background that generally exceeded WHO air quality guideline
values. Although such constrained exposure contrast requires cautious
interpretation of the exposure-response magnitude, the consistent
associations observed across analyses suggest that biological aging
may remain sensitive to even modest differences in pollution exposure.
This observation aligns with growing evidence that the adverse health
effects of air pollution persist without a clear safe threshold, leaving
physiological systems vulnerable to even small increments in exposure
under conditions of chronic environmental stress. Air pollution may
induce DNA damage, telomere attrition, and mitochondrial dysfunction
via oxidative stress and chronic inflammatory responses, thereby accelerating
cellular senescence and systemic physiological decline.
−

 Furthermore, alterations in DNA methylation and epigenetic aging
markers linked to multipollutant exposure provide molecular plausibility
for these long-term associations. In
this context, our KDM-based outcome should be interpreted as a clinically
grounded measure of systemic physiological aging rather than as a
direct molecular clock. Although KDM-BA does not directly capture
DNA methylation clocks or other omics-based markers and is constrained
by available routine clinical measurements, it is well suited to large-scale
health examination cohorts and captures multisystem physiological
integrity relevant to inflammation- and oxidative stress-related environmental
pathways. To evaluate environmental correlates of this systemic aging
process more comprehensively, our application of WQS and qgcomp models
captured the complexity of real-world joint exposures and consistently
indicated an adverse mixture association. Both models identified PM10 and O3 as the highest-weighted components, further
implicating oxidative stress as a core pathway driving pollution-related
accelerated aging. Although the GAMM splines suggested departures
from linearity for PM2.5 and PM10, their uneven
and multimodal exposure distributions limited data support across
portions of the exposure ranges; the detailed curve shapes should
therefore not be regarded as conclusive evidence of U-shaped or threshold
relationships. The broadly comparable GAMM and GLMM estimates across
data-supported contrasts, together with interaction tests that provided
no statistical evidence of slope heterogeneity between exposure components,
suggest that these local spline fluctuations did not materially alter
the overall interpretation of the primary GLMM results.

Beyond
delineating independent effects, we found that approximately
25% of the protective association between green space and healthier
aging patterns was statistically mediated by mitigation of air pollution
exposure. This suggests that green space may function as a dual-purpose
environmental resource: acting as a “passive barrier”
to pollutants while engaging in “active restoration”. The coexistence of direct and indirect effects
provides a novel perspective on the synergistic potential of these
environments. Our findings suggest that particularly in high-pollution
scenarios, green space exerts a substantial indirect protective effect
by facilitating pollutant deposition and dispersion. From a public health perspective, increasing urban greenery
may represent a robust strategy with complementary benefits even in
densely populated, high-pollution urban settings. Our findings provide
supportive evidence for synergistic urban governance approaches, in
which green infrastructure development is integrated with air quality
management to promote healthier aging.

Our subgroup analyses
revealed significant heterogeneity in the
associations between environmental exposures and biological aging,
delineating a complex “population susceptibility profile”.
Stratified analyses by sex indicated a heightened environmental sensitivity
among females. The association between O3 and accelerated
aging was observed almost exclusively in women (P
interaction = 0.003), and adverse associations with PM2.5, SO2, and NO2 also appeared more
pronounced in the female subgroup. This aligns with emerging consensus
in environmental epidemiology that women may be more vulnerable to
specific environmental stressors. Potential
mechanisms for this susceptibility are likely multifaceted. While
this observational study cannot verify specific biological pathways,
previous literature suggests that hormonal fluctuations, particularly
estrogens, may modulate inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways,
thereby altering physiological responses to pollutants. Behaviorally, sex-based differences in commuting
patterns and leisure activities may lead to distinct exposure profiles.

Stratified analyses by age further identified age as a critical
effect modifier. Although the protective association of green space
reached statistical significance only in the older group (≥40
years), both interaction tests and point estimates consistently indicated
a stronger protective trend in younger adults (<40 years). This
pattern, characterized by a stronger effect size but lower statistical
significance, likely reflects limited statistical power due to the
smaller sample size of the younger subgroup. Synthesizing these evidence,
we postulate that the protective role of green space may be particularly
pivotal in younger adulthood. This implies that accumulating an environmental
“dividend” (e.g., greater contact with nature) during
younger years may help shape more favorable aging-related patterns
and bolster physiological resilience against future environmental
challenges. However, this life-course–relevant
hypothesis warrants further validation through larger cohorts with
extended age spans. Associations of PM2.5, SO2, and O3 with accelerated aging
were concentrated in middle-aged and older adults (≥40 years),
likely reflecting the combined impact of cumulative damage and declining
physiological reserve. Associations of PM10 and NO2 with accelerated aging were more prominent in the younger
group (<40 years). While the exact mechanism remains to be elucidated,
this may point to unique exposure patterns (e.g., traffic-related
NO2 exposure during commuting) or distinct physiological
response pathways in younger individuals, necessitating further investigation.
Overall, these findings emphasize that future environmental health
research and interventions must move beyond “average effects”
to account for these critical modifiers, thereby enabling more precise
risk assessment and protection strategies.

Against the backdrop
of rapid urbanization and global population
aging, our findings have important public health relevance. This study
provides supportive evidence that urban environmental interventions
may represent a feasible strategy for promoting healthy aging. Specifically,
the expansion of green space and air pollution control should be viewed
as synergistic rather than isolated environmental strategies. Particularly
in cities with persistently high pollution levels, urban greenery
not only confers direct psychophysiological benefits but also plays
a substantial indirect protective role by mitigating local exposure
to pollutants. Collectively, these findings suggest that “greening”
and “pollution reduction” may be regarded as complementary
public health goals. Incorporating green infrastructure into urban
planning while simultaneously strengthening air quality management
may yield substantial benefits for promoting healthier aging in urban
populations.

This study possesses several strengths. First,
we adopted an accelerated
longitudinal (cohort-sequential) design and leveraged repeated measurements
across overlapping age groups to identify heterogeneous biological
aging patterns at the population level. Traditional cross-sectional
studies based on a single assessment are vulnerable to random measurement
error and short-term biological variability. In contrast, our repeated-measures
design increased the signal-to-noise ratio of biological aging indicators,
and the application of group-based trajectory modeling facilitated
the identification of latent, relatively stable aging patterns within
the population. Second, within a typical high-density urban setting
with persistently elevated pollution levels, we systematically evaluated
the independent, joint, and interactive effects of green space and
air pollution mixture on biological aging. Third, the robustness of
our findings was rigorously assessed using complementary modeling
strategies, including LMM with continuous biological age acceleration
as the outcome, which yielded results consistent with the primary
trajectory-based analyses. Several limitations should be acknowledged.
First, participants with missing key covariates or extensive biomarker
missingness were excluded before imputation. Although imputation addressed
sporadic missingness within the retained sample and complete-case
analyses yielded consistent findings, potential selection bias arising
from these preimputation exclusions cannot be ruled out. Second, despite
adjusting for major covariates, residual confounding cannot be entirely
excluded. However, the calculated E-values (1.18–1.39) suggest
that an unmeasured confounder would need to have substantially stronger
associations with both exposure and outcome than those observed to
fully explain the findings. Third, exposure assessment relied on workplace
addresses. While lacking residential data introduces nondifferential
misclassification, workplace environments capture the critical active
daytime window characterized by higher physical activity and respiration
rates. Such random error generally biases associations toward the
null, implying that the true effects may be even stronger. Therefore,
our findings complement residential-based evidence and highlight the
need to improve environmental quality in occupational settings. Furthermore,
workplace locations may correlate with certain features of occupation.
Although the study population was predominantly composed of indoor
and office-based workers, with limited representation of occupations
involving substantial occupational hazard exposures or prolonged outdoor
work, and we further adjusted for broad occupational categories to
account for differences in job type and potential exposure to hazards,
residual confounding related to unmeasured occupational characteristics,
such as work complexity and psychosocial work environments, cannot
be completely excluded. Fourth, although the accelerated longitudinal
design enabled the estimation of population-level aging patterns across
age groups, individual follow-up durations were relatively short.
Future studies with extended follow-up periods are warranted to further
capture the long-term intraindividual dynamics of environmental influences
on aging.

Conclusion

In summary, this study, employing an accelerated
longitudinal design
on a cohort of working-age adults in a typical high-pollution urban
setting in China, provides robust evidence regarding environmental
correlates of biological aging. We identified relatively stable biological
aging patterns within the population, showing that higher long-term
green space exposure was associated with healthier aging patterns,
whereas exposure to the air pollution mixture was associated with
higher odds of accelerated aging. Green space appears to function
as a vital resilience resource, potentially operating through two
complementary pathways: direct psychophysiological restoration and
indirect mitigation of air pollution exposure. Collectively, these
findings underscore the critical importance of adopting a multiexposure
perspective and provide a solid scientific basis for synergistic urban
interventions integrating green infrastructure with pollution control
to promote healthy aging in rapidly urbanizing populations.
