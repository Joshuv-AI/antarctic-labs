End-to-End Identifiable and Consistent Recurrent Switching Dynamical Systems
Carles Balsells-Rodas, Zhengrui Xiang, Xavier Sumba, Yingzhen Li

Abstract
Learning identifiable representations in deep generative models remains a fundamental challenge, particularly for sequential data with regime-switching dynamics. Existing approaches establish identifiability under restrictive assumptions, such as stationarity or limited emission models, and typically rely on variational autoencoder (VAE) estimators, which introduce approximation gaps that limit the recovery of the latent structure. In this work, we address both the theoretical and practical limitations of this setting. First, we establish identifiability of a broad class of recurrent nonlinear switching dynamical systems under flexible assumptions, significantly extending prior results. Second, we introduce Ω \Omega SDS, a flow-based estimator that enables exact likelihood optimization using expectation-maximisation. Through empirical validation on both synthetic and real-world data, our results demonstrate that Ω \Omega SDS achieves improved disentanglement compared to VAE-based estimators and more accurate forecasting of underlying dynamics.

Introduction

Many real-world time series exhibit nonstationary dynamics, where the underlying temporal mechanisms change over time. Examples include seasonal patterns in climate data
(
37
)
, or behavioural modes in human motion
(
41
)
. Regime-switching state-space models, including switching dynamical systems (SDSs;
(
17
)
) and Markov switching models (MSMs;
(
31
;
35
)
), target these scenarios by introducing discrete regimes (or switches) which explicitly model dynamical changes. Recent sequential latent variable models incorporate regime-switching as structured priors
(
30
;
11
;
2
;
32
;
16
)
, improving flexibility while preserving interpretable continuous states and discrete switches.

Interpretability of deep generative models relies on identifiability, which ensures that the data distribution determines the latent structure up to acceptable equivalences, such as permutations of switches or latent variables. Without identifiability, observationally equivalent model solutions may represent contradictory latent dynamics, and therefore interpretability may reflect arbitrary parametrisations rather than the underlying data-generating mechanisms. For flexible nonlinear latent variable models, identifiability fails without additional structure
(
23
)
. Existing results impose such structures either on the latent distribution, for example through auxiliary variables or temporal dependence
(
25
;
19
;
29
;
34
;
45
)
, or the emission map
(
28
)
. Switching dynamical systems introduce additional complexity, since the latent prior is a mixture over regime-dependent dynamics. Existing SDS identifiability results reflect this “no free lunch” principle between restricting emissions or latent dynamics: piece-wise linear emissions allow flexible nonlinear mixture transitions
(
4
)
, while general invertible emissions require more restrictive latent dynamics, such as stationary linear transitions
(
20
)
.

In this work, we push the above trade-off by establishing identifiability and estimation for recurrent switching dynamical systems (rSDS) with nonlinear transition dynamics and invertible emissions.

•

We show that (i) temporal latents are identifiable up to affine transformations, (ii) recurrent Markov switching dynamics are identifiable up to regime permutations, and (iii) regime-dependent covariances reduce affine ambiguity to permutation and scaling. These results provide an end-to-end identifiability framework for recurrent deep SDSs.

•

Motivated by the theory, we propose
\Omega
SDS, a likelihood-based estimator that extracts temporal features using a flow-based emission and learns prior dynamics using expectation-maximisation. Unlike VAE-based estimators,
\Omega
SDS optimises the exact likelihood, yielding consistency up to the identifiable equivalence class under standard MLE conditions for recurrent MSMs.

•

Empirically, exact likelihood improves latent disentanglement on synthetic data, while recurrent switches support interpretable dynamics and strong forecasting performance on controlled physical systems and real-world dancing videos.

Recurrent Switching Dynamical Systems

A recurrent Switching Dynamical System (rSDS), exemplified in Figure
1
, models sequential data by introducing continuous latent states
\bm{z}_{1:T}
and discrete switching variables
\bm{s}_{1:T}
. At each time step
t
, the observation
\bm{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is generated through a (noisy) transformation of the continuous latent variable
\bm{z}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. While recent nonlinear SDS approaches use additive-noise emissions
(
11
;
2
)
, we assume a flow-based emission similarly used in
40
:

Assumption A1

(Emission model)
.

Observations
\bm{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are generated via an invertible transformation
f_{\bm{\theta}_{f}}
of latent variables
\bm{z}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and i.i.d. noise
\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
d=n-m
.

\bm{x}_{t}=f_{\bm{\theta}_{f}}(\bm{z}_{t},\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}),\quad\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{2}I_{d}).

(1)

Figure 1
:
The considered rSDS model. Observations are generated through an invertible mapping of recurrent switching features and exogenous noises.

The latent dynamics
\bm{z}_{1:T}
follow a recurrent Markov Switching Model (rMSM)
(
12
)
. Therefore at time
t
, the discrete switch
s_{t}\in\{1,\dots,K\}
determines the transition dynamics of
\bm{z}_{t}
, while the switching process receives feedback from past latent variables
\bm{z}_{t-1}
. Under first-order Markov assumptions, the joint distribution of
(\bm{z}_{1:T},\bm{s}_{1:T})
factorises as follows:

p_{\bm{\theta}_{\bm{z}},\bm{\theta}_{\bm{s}}}(\bm{z}_{1:T},\bm{s}_{1:T})=p_{\bm{\theta}_{\bm{s}}}(s_{1})p_{\bm{\theta}_{\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{1}|s_{1})\\
\prod_{t=2}^{T}p_{\bm{\theta}_{\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{t}\mid\bm{z}_{t-1},s_{t})p_{\bm{\theta}_{\bm{s}}}(s_{t}\mid s_{t-1},\bm{z}_{t-1}).

(2)

We make the following assumption on the latent transition dynamics
p_{\bm{\theta}_{\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{t}\mid\bm{z}_{t-1},s_{t})
.

Assumption A2

(Analytic Gaussian autoregressive dynamics)
.

The latent variables
\bm{z}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
follow a regime-dependent Gaussian autoregressive process:

\bm{z}_{t}\mid s_{t},\bm{z}_{t-1}\sim\mathcal{N}\Big(\bm{z}_{t}\mid\bm{m}_{s_{t}}(\bm{z}_{t-1}),\Sigma_{s_{t}}\Big),\quad\Sigma_{s_{t}}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\sigma}^{2}_{s_{t}})

where each mean function
\bm{m}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is analytic, each covariance
\Sigma_{k}\succ 0
is diagonal with
\bm{\sigma}^{2}_{k}
denoting the diagonal, and both means
\bm{m}_{k}
and/or covariances
\Sigma_{k}
are distinct across regimes.

This assumption is in the same format as in
4
, where results are established for non-recurrent-switching, i.e.,
p_{\bm{\theta}_{\bm{s}}}(s_{t}\mid s_{t-1},\bm{z}_{t-1})=p_{\bm{\theta}_{\bm{s}}}(s_{t}\mid s_{t-1})
. In contrast, we consider a common recurrent formulation in rSDS
(
30
;
11
;
2
)
to increase the flexibility of the switching process, where switches can adapt to latent distributional changes rather than following fixed Markov transition matrices.

Assumption A3

(Recurrent switching process)
.

The switches are dependent on previous latents:

p_{\bm{\theta}_{\bm{s}}}(s_{t}=k\mid s_{t-1}=j,\bm{z}_{t-1})=Q_{jk}(\bm{z}_{t-1}),\quad Q:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to[0,1]^{K\times K},\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K}Q_{jk}(\bm{z}_{t-1})=1,

where
Q
is continuous almost everywhere in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

A.e. continuity of
Q
is a weak regularity assumption: it allows discontinuities on lower-dimensional boundaries, such as switching surfaces or collision boundaries, e.g., in bouncing balls dynamics.

Identifiability desiderata in SDSs.

Let
\bm{\Theta}
be a family of parameters which parametrise a recurrent switching dynamical system under Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
), where
\bm{\theta}:=\{\bm{\theta}_{f},\bm{\theta}_{\bm{z}},\bm{\theta}_{\bm{s}},m,K\}
,
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
. We aim to establish identifiability for every parameter of
\bm{\theta}
: (i) the latent dimensionality
m
, (ii) the number of regimes
K
, (iii) the distribution of the latent variables
\bm{z}_{t}
, (iv) the autoregressive components
p_{\bm{\theta}_{\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{t}\mid\bm{z}_{t-1},s_{t})
, and (v) the switching process
p_{\bm{\theta}_{\bm{s}}}(s_{t}\mid s_{t-1},\bm{z}_{t-1})
. Additionally, we denote
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{1:T})
and
p_{\bm{\theta}_{\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{1:T})
the observational and latent distributions induced by the model. Notably, our emission structure allows including the latent dimensionality
m
in the target equivalence class, which is non-standard in the literature
(
39
;
4
;
38
)
.

Definition 2.1

(Latent identifiability up to affine transformations)
.

The latent variables
\bm{z}
are identifiable up to affine transformations, if for any
\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\bm{\Theta}
such that
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{1:T})=p_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{1:T})
,
\forall\bm{x}_{1:T}\in\mathbb{R}^{Tn}
, we have
m=m^{\prime}
and there exists an invertible affine transformation
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
p_{\bm{\theta}_{\bm{z}}}=\psi^{(T)}_{\#}p_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{\bm{z}}}
, where
\psi^{(T)}:\mathbb{R}^{Tm}\to\mathbb{R}^{Tm}
is defined by
\psi^{(T)}(\bm{z}_{1:T}):=(\psi(\bm{z}_{1}),\dots,\psi(\bm{z}_{T}))
.

Definition 2.2

(Transition identifiability up to permutations)
.

The transition model is identifiable up to permutations if for any
\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\bm{\Theta}
such that
p_{\bm{\theta}_{\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{1:T})=p_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{1:T})
,
\forall\bm{z}_{1:T}\in\mathbb{R}^{Tm}
, we have
K=K^{\prime}
and there exists a permutation
\pi\in S_{K}
such that for any
k\in\{1,\dots K\}
,
p_{\bm{\theta}_{\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{t}\mid\bm{z}_{t-1},s_{t}=k)=p_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{t}\mid\bm{z}_{t-1},s_{t}=\pi(k))
, and for any
k,j\in\{1,\dots,K\}
,

p_{\bm{\theta}_{\bm{s}}}(s_{t}=k\mid s_{t-1}=j,\bm{z}_{t-1})=p_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{\bm{s}}}(s_{t}=\pi(k)\mid s_{t-1}=\pi(j),\bm{z}_{t-1}).

Def.
2.1
concerns identification of the latent representation up to affine transformations, while Def.
2.2
targets the regime-dependent dynamics once the continuous latent space is fixed. Since the rSDS is composed of several distinct elements, establishing end-to-end identifiability is a challenging task.

3
Identifiability Theory for Recurrent Switching Dynamical Systems

In this section, we present our main identifiability results as follows:

i)

Latent variable identifiability.
We establish identifiability of latent variable distribution
p_{\bm{\theta}_{\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{1:T})
up to affine transformations under invertible emissions in Section
3.1
.

ii)

rMSM identifiability.
We then establish identifiability of the transition dynamics up to permutations in Section
3.2
.

iii)

Further disentangling latent variables.
Finally, we impose regime-dependent assumptions that reduce the affine ambiguity of latent variables to permutation and scaling in Section
3.3
.

Importantly, each stage may be of independent interest, as we significantly strengthen previous results. For each result, we first state the additional assumptions required, followed by the corresponding theoretical result. We then provide a proof sketch and conclude with a discussion of its implications.

3.1
Latent Space Identifiability up to Affine Transformations

We first establish the affine identifiability of the continuous latent variables. Since we keep general invertible emissions, identifiability must come from the latent transition dynamics. The following assumptions ensure sufficient regime separation and local variability.

Assumption A4

(Sticky switching)
.

There exists a non-empty open set
\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

Q_{kk}(\bm{z})\geq 1-\epsilon(\bm{z}),\quad\epsilon(\bm{z})\in[0,\tfrac{1}{2}),\ \forall k\in\{1,\dots,K\},\ \forall\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z},

(3)

where
\epsilon:\mathcal{Z}\to[0,\tfrac{1}{2})
is continuous in
\mathcal{Z}
.

Assumption A5

(Transition separation)
.

Furthermore, there exist
\bm{z}^{*}\in\mathcal{Z}
, a constant
\ell^{*}>0
, and an integer
T^{*}\geq 2
such that, for any regime
i\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, there exists latent history

\bm{z}_{1}^{(i)}(\bm{z}^{*}),\dots,\bm{z}_{T^{*}}^{(i)}(\bm{z}^{*})\in\mathcal{Z},\ \bm{z}_{T^{*}}^{(i)}(\bm{z}^{*})=\bm{z}^{*},
for which

\frac{\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}_{\tau}^{(i)}(\bm{z}^{*})|\bm{z}_{\tau-1}^{(i)}(\bm{z}^{*}),s_{\tau}=i)}{\max_{j\neq i}\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}_{\tau}^{(i)}(\bm{z}^{*})|\bm{z}_{\tau-1}^{(i)}(\bm{z}^{*}),s_{\tau}=j)}>e^{\ell^{*}},\quad\tau=2,\dots,T^{*}-1,\quad\ell^{*}>\log\frac{1+\epsilon^{*}}{1-\epsilon^{*}},

(4)

where
\epsilon^{*}=\max\{\epsilon(\bm{z}_{1}^{(i)}(\bm{z}^{*})),\dots,\bm{z}_{T^{*}-1}^{(i)}(\bm{z}^{*})\}
, and
T^{*}
is chosen large enough so that

p(s_{T^{*}}=i\mid\bm{z}^{(i)}_{1:T^{*}}(\bm{z}^{*}))>\frac{1}{2},\quad\forall i\in\{1,\dots,K\}.

(5)

Assumption A6

(Covariance separation)
.

For each regime
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, there exists at least one dimension
i_{k}\in\{1,\dots,m\}
such that
\sigma_{k,i_{k}}\geq\sigma_{k^{\prime},i_{k}}
for any
k^{\prime}\neq k
.

Assumption A7

(Locally nondegenerate regime)
.

There exist a regime
1\leq k_{0}\leq K
and a point
\bm{z}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that the determinant of the Jacobian of
\bm{m}_{k_{0}}(\bm{z}^{*})
is non-zero:
\det J_{\bm{m}_{k_{0}}}(\bm{z}^{*})\neq 0.

Under these assumptions, we establish latent variable identifiability up to affine transformations.

Identifying Recurrent Prior Dynamics

Theorem 3.2
.

The rMSM is identifiable up to permutations under the following conditions:

(i)

Under Assumption (
A2
), the continuous latent transitions

, such that:

\bm{m}_{k}(\bm{z})=A\bm{m}^{\prime}_{\pi(k)}(A^{-1}(\bm{z}-\bm{b}))+\bm{b},\ Q_{jk}(\bm{z})=Q_{\pi(j)\pi(k)}(A^{-1}(\bm{z}-\bm{b})),\ k=1:K,\ \bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(8)

For other rSDS designs, Thm.
3.2
is independent to Section
3.1
provided we establish identifiability up to affine transformations (Def
2.1
). For example, emission piece-wise linearity
(
28
)
could replace (
A4
-
A7
), and Theorem
3.2
would still be valid to identify the transitions
\bm{m}_{k}
as in Eq. (
8
). However, for full identifiability including
Q(\bm{z}_{t-1})
, further restrictions would be necessary.

3.3
Disentanglement: Resolving the Affine Indeterminacy

Our last stage consists of resolving the structure of the affine mapping
\psi(\bm{z})=A\bm{z}+\bm{b}
in Eq. (
8
) to establish identifiable disentangled representations and permit nonlinear ICA
(
23
)
. This identifiability, presented in Thm.
3.3
, requires the following additional assumption.

Assumption A8
.

For a pair of regimes
k_{1}\neq k_{2}\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, with diagonal variances
\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\sigma}^{2}_{k_{1}}),\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\sigma}^{2}_{k_{2}})
, respectively, define the ratio vector
\bm{r}_{k_{1},k_{2}}
between variances:

\bm{r}_{k_{1},k_{2}}:=\left(\frac{\sigma_{k_{1},1}}{\sigma_{k_{2},1}},\quad\dots,\quad\frac{\sigma_{k_{1},m}}{\sigma_{k_{2},m}}\right)=\frac{\bm{\sigma}_{k_{1}}}{\bm{\sigma}_{k_{2}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(9)

For every pair of regimes
k_{1}\neq k_{2},\ k_{1},k_{2}\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, stack ratios
\bm{r}_{k_{1},k_{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
:

R\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{K}{2}\times m},\quad R_{(k_{1},k_{2}),i}=\bm{r}_{k_{1},k_{2},i},\quad i\in\{1,\dots,m\}.

(10)

That is, each row of
R
is a ratio vector formed by combining regime pairs. We assume that
R
has no two identical columns, i.e., for
i\neq j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, there exists
(a,b)
such that
R_{(a,b),i}\neq R_{(a,b),j}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
K\geq 2
. Let
\bm{z}^{\prime}_{t}=A\bm{z}_{t}+\bm{b}
, where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is invertible and
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Under Assumption (
A8
), from
\bm{z}^{\prime}_{t}
, we can recover an invertible matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
such that
(A^{\prime})^{-1}A=PD
, where
P
is a permutation matrix, and
D
is diagonal.

See Appendix
B.5
for the proof, which uses similar eigen-decomposition tricks introduced in
28
strategy. We also provide examples on how Eq. (
10
) is constructed, and partial disentanglement when (
A8
) is not satisfied. Theorem
3.3
shows that in regime-dependent settings, increasing the number of regimes with diverse variances strengthens identifiable latent space disentanglement.

Remark 3.4
.

For mixture models,
28
enables disentanglement via regime-dependent variances, which extends to the temporal case as follows. For
K
regimes with diagonal covariances, i.e.,
z_{t,i}\perp\!\!\!\perp z_{t,j}\mid s_{t},\bm{z}_{t-1}
for all
i\neq j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, there exists one pair of regimes
k_{1}\neq k_{2}\in\{1,\dots,K\}
such that the ratio vector
\bm{r}_{k_{1},k_{2}}
has distinct elements.
However, this condition does not scale well to high dimensions, since ratios with close values may challenge disentanglement in practical estimation. We note (
A8
) is weaker than
28
, as a single ratio vector with distinct elements forms
R
with distinct columns. Therefore, (
A8
) works as a scalable generalisation by fully exploiting the mixture setting of rSDS.

4
\Omega
SDS: Consistent estimation for SDSs

The identifiability results suggest a natural estimation approach: use an invertible emission to distinguish temporal features from exogenous noise, and perform exact inference on the resulting latent rMSM. We propose
\Omega
SDS, a likelihood-based estimator that follows this principle by combining an invertible flow-based emission model with latent-space inference based on rMSMs.
For simplicity, we drop the subscript
\bm{\theta}
in the model equations.
The flow defines a generative emission map from the latent dynamical variables and exogenous noise to observations, while its inverse acts as an encoder:
(\bm{z}_{t},\bm{\varepsilon}_{t})=f^{-1}(\bm{x}_{t}),\,1\leq t\leq T
. Using the change-of-variables formula, the marginal log-likelihood of an observed trajectory is expressed as follows.

\log p(\bm{x}_{1:T})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\left|\det J_{f^{-1}}(\bm{x}_{t})\right|+\log p(\bm{z}_{1:T})+\log p_{\bm{\varepsilon}}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{1:T}),\quad p_{\bm{\varepsilon}}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{t})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{t};0,\sigma_{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{2}I)

(11)

where
\sigma_{\bm{\varepsilon}}
is left as a hyper-parameter, and
J_{f^{-1}}
denotes the Jacobian of the inverse transformation. In our experiments, we parametrise
f
using a multi-layer TarFlow
(
47
)
,
f=(f^{(1)}\circ\dots\circ f^{(L)})
, where each layer is a masked autoregressive flow where the conditioning is implemented using causal transformers. To encourage a decomposition between dynamical variables and exogenous noise, we replace the permutation blocks in TarFlow with invertible LU mixing layers, as introduced in RealNVP
(
10
)
. For video experiments, we instead use a multi-scale flow architecture, inspired by Glow
(
27
)
. See Appendix
C.2
for details.

The latent sequence likelihood
p(\bm{z}_{1:T})
is defined by the rMSM prior with discrete regimes
\bm{s}_{1:T}
in Eq. (
2
). We optimise the likelihood in Eq. (
11
) by gradient ascent. The gradient of the latent log-likelihood
\log p(\bm{z}_{1:T})
can be written as an expectation under the exact switch posterior,
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log p(\bm{z}_{1:T})=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{p(\bm{s}_{1:T}\mid\bm{z}_{1:T})}[\log p(\bm{z}_{1:T},\bm{s}_{1:T})]
, which yields the following
(
11
)
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log p(\bm{z}_{1:T})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{1,k}(\bm{z}_{1:T})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log p(s_{1}=k)+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{l=1}^{K}\xi_{t,k,l}(\bm{z}_{1:T})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log Q_{lk}(\bm{z}_{t-1})

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{K}

\displaystyle\gamma_{1,k}(\bm{z}_{1:T})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log p(\bm{z}_{1}|s_{t}=k)+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{t,k}(\bm{z}_{1:T})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log p(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{z}_{t-1},s_{t}=k),

where
\gamma_{t,k}(\bm{z}_{1:T})=p(s_{t}=k\mid\bm{z}_{1:T})
and
\xi_{t,k,l}(\bm{z}_{1:T})=p(s_{t}=k,s_{t-1}=l\mid\bm{z}_{1:T})
are the marginal and two-step posterior distribution given
\bm{z}_{1:T}
. These quantities are computed using standard forward-backward recursions
(
6
)
, where details are deferred to Appendix
C.1
.

Training regime-switching models with nonlinear transitions can suffer from state collapse, where only a subset of regimes is used
(
11
)
. Rather than relying on annealing schedules, we initialise the rMSM parameters from PCA features and use a PCA-alignment auxiliary loss early in training. This stabilises the flow decomposition and provides a reliable early latent representation for regime learning. PCA-based initialisation is standard in SDSs
(
30
)
.

Consistency.

Unlike VAE-based approaches,
\Omega
SDS optimises the exact marginal likelihood. By invertibility of the emission,
(\bm{z}_{t},\bm{\varepsilon}_{t})=f_{\bm{\theta}_{f}}^{-1}(\bm{x}_{t})
, the latent
p_{\bm{\theta}_{\bm{z}}}(\bm{z}_{1:T})
is a MSM likelihood with covariate-dependent transitions
Q(\bm{z}_{t-1})
, aligning with the setting of MLE consistency results of
36
.
Therefore, under the usual assumptions for MSM and MLE consistency, including correct specification, global optimisation, positivity of the transition probabilities, ergodicity, and likelihood regularity, the resulting MLE is consistent up to the equivalence class characterised in Defs.
2.1
,
2.2
, and Theorem
3.3

(
42
;
36
)
. These regularity conditions are standard in likelihood-based consistency analyses and are additional to our identifiability assumptions, but compatible with them. This contrasts with VAE-based SDSs, which optimise an ELBO and require sufficient coverage of the variational family
(
25
)
. Collapsed-switching approaches marginalise
\bm{s}_{1:T}
conditionally on
\bm{z}_{1:T}
, but rely on an amortised variational posterior
q_{\phi}(\bm{z}_{1:T}|\bm{x}_{1:T})
which may introduce approximation gaps
(
4
)
. We further discuss this empirically in Appendix
C.3
.

6
Conclusions

We presented identifiability results for recurrent switching nonlinear dynamical systems by imposing restrictions on the latent dynamics, recovering the latent representation up to acceptable equivalences under general invertible emissions.
We also introduced
\Omega
SDS, a flow-based estimator that learns exact likelihood, which is consistent under further standard assumptions in MLE literature for rMSMs. Empirically, our method improves disentanglement compared to VAE-based estimators and shows strong forecasting performance due to recurrent switching.
The main limitation of our approach is the need to constrain the latent dynamics. Specifically, the likelihood-separation condition (
A5
) does not trivially generalise to arbitrary nonlinear dynamics. Still, it can be balanced with sticky switching (
A4
). Our consistency result also relies on assumptions beyond those required for identifiability. While these are compatible with our setting, a dedicated consistency analysis of nonlinear rSDS remains open. Future work will study weaker separation conditions, robustness to model misspecification, and extensions of likelihood-based models such as
\Omega
SDS to broader nonlinear ICA and causal representation learning settings.

Appendix A
Unidentifiability of Non-Temporal Mixture Generative Models

25
illustrates identifiability for unconditional priors. This notion also extends to models with mixture priors, where the latent variables are sampled from a mixture distribution:

\bm{x}=f_{\bm{\theta}_{f}}(\bm{z}),\quad\bm{z}\sim\sum_{k=1}^{K}p(s=k)p(\bm{z}\mid s=k).

(12)

In this setting, even the number of components
K
is unidentifiable from the observed data, which leads to an unidentifiable latent representation. To see this, consider two models with transformations
f_{\bm{\theta}}
and
f_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}
, and latent distributions with
K
and
K^{\prime}
mixture components, respectively, such that
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=p_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}(\bm{x})
for any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We can write the observed density as a pushforward measure of
\bm{z}
through
f_{\bm{\theta}_{f}}
(or
f_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{f}}
):

(f_{\bm{\theta}\#}p_{\bm{\theta}_{z}})(\bm{x})=(f_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\#}p_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{z}})(\bm{x}),

(13)

where the composition
f_{\bm{\theta}_{f}}^{-1}\circ f_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{f}}
can transform one latent distribution into another. Since a general invertible transformation can map any distribution to a uniform via its CDF, the composition is flexible enough to arbitrarily create or destroy mixture components. This makes
K
unidentifiable under the following construction:

p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=\left((f^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}}\circ f_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}})_{\#}p_{\bm{\theta}_{z}^{\prime}}\right)(\bm{x}),\quad\text{with }(f^{-1}_{\bm{\theta}}\circ f_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}})=F^{-1}_{p_{\bm{\theta}_{z}^{\prime}}}\circ F_{p_{\bm{\theta}_{z}}},

(14)

where
F^{-1}_{p_{\bm{\theta}_{z}^{\prime}}}
and
F_{\bm{\theta}_{z}}
are the inverse CDF of
p_{\bm{\theta}_{z}^{\prime}}
and the CDF of
p_{\bm{\theta}_{z}}
, respectively.
To solve the above problem,
28
proves identifiability of mixture priors under the assumption of piece-wise linear
f_{\bm{\theta}}
. This imposes restrictions on
f_{\bm{\theta}}
, but enables identifiability while maintaining a flexible GMM prior. Comparing to
25
, which instead constrains the prior, this highlights a “no free lunch” principle for identifiability: without restricting the generative mapping or the latent distribution, the model is fundamentally unidentifiable.

Now we finish the proof using an iVAE-style argument, with derivatives instead of auxiliary variables
[

As shown in Step

–

histories. Below, we provide a concentration result showing that histories aligned with a given regime induce filtering posteriors dominated by that regime.

Lemma B.1
.

Consider a regime path
(k_{1}^{*},\dots,k_{t}^{*})
, and latent variables
(\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{t})
, such that for all
\tau=2,\dots,t
,

Q_{k^{*}_{\tau-1},k^{*}_{\tau}}(\bm{z}_{\tau-1})=\max_{k^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,K\}}Q_{k^{*}_{\tau-1},k^{\prime}}(\bm{z}_{\tau-1})\geq 1-\epsilon^{*},

(49)

for some
\epsilon^{*}\in[0,\tfrac{1}{2})
. Denote
g_{k}(\bm{z}_{t}\mid\bm{z}_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}_{t};\bm{m}_{k}(\bm{z}_{t-1}),\Sigma_{k})
and assume

\frac{g_{k_{\tau}^{*}}(\bm{z}_{\tau}|\bm{z}_{\tau-1})}{\max_{k^{\prime}\neq k_{\tau}^{*}}g_{k^{\prime}}(\bm{z}_{\tau}|\bm{z}_{\tau-1})}\geq e^{\ell^{*}},

(50)

for some
\ell^{*}>0
. If

\ell^{*}>\log\frac{1+\epsilon^{*}}{1-\epsilon^{*}},

(51)

then, there exists
T^{*}<+\infty
, such that for all
t\geq T^{*}
, we have

p(s_{t}=k_{t}^{*}|\bm{z}_{1:t})>\frac{1}{2}.

(52)

Proof.

For
\tau=2,\dots,t
, define the predictive and filtering posteriors respectively as follows:

\pi_{\tau}(k)=p(s_{\tau}=k|\bm{z}_{1:\tau-1}),\quad\alpha_{\tau}(k)=p(s_{\tau}=k|\bm{z}_{1:\tau}).

(53)

Using Bayes’ rule:

\alpha_{\tau}(k_{\tau}^{*})=\frac{\pi_{\tau}(k_{\tau}^{*})g_{k_{\tau}^{*}}(\bm{z}_{\tau}|\bm{z}_{\tau-1})}{\pi_{\tau}(k_{\tau}^{*})g_{k_{\tau}^{*}}(\bm{z}_{\tau}|\bm{z}_{\tau-1})+\sum_{k\neq k_{\tau}^{*}}^{K}\pi_{\tau}(k)g_{k}(\bm{z}_{\tau}|\bm{z}_{\tau-1})},

(54)

where we find an upper bound to the denominator.

\sum_{k\neq k_{\tau}^{*}}^{K}\pi_{\tau}(k)g_{k}(\bm{z}_{\tau}|\bm{z}_{\tau-1})\leq\sum_{k\neq k_{\tau}^{*}}^{K}\pi_{\tau}(k)\max_{k\neq k_{\tau}^{*}}g_{k}(\bm{z}_{\tau}|\bm{z}_{\tau-1})\leq(1-\pi_{\tau}(k_{\tau}^{*}))g_{k_{\tau}^{*}}(\bm{z}_{\tau}|\bm{z}_{\tau-1})e^{-\ell^{*}}.

(55)

Let
R_{\tau}
be the posterior odds at time
\tau
, defined as follows:

R_{\tau}:=\frac{1-\alpha_{\tau}(k_{\tau}^{*})}{\alpha_{\tau}(k_{\tau}^{*})}.

(56)

Given
\alpha_{\tau}(k_{\tau}^{*})
and
1-\alpha_{\tau}(k_{\tau}^{*})
share the denominator,
R_{\tau}
has the following simpler form:

R_{\tau}=\frac{\sum_{k\neq k_{\tau}^{*}}^{K}\pi_{\tau}(k)g_{k}(\bm{z}_{\tau}|\bm{z}_{\tau-1})}{\pi_{\tau}(k_{\tau}^{*})g_{k_{\tau}^{*}}(\bm{z}_{\tau}|\bm{z}_{\tau-1})}\leq\frac{(1-\pi_{\tau}(k_{\tau}^{*}))g_{k_{\tau}^{*}}(\bm{z}_{\tau}|\bm{z}_{\tau-1})e^{-\ell^{*}}}{\pi_{\tau}(k_{\tau}^{*})g_{k_{\tau}^{*}}(\bm{z}_{\tau}|\bm{z}_{\tau-1})}=e^{-\ell^{*}}\frac{1-\pi_{\tau}(k_{\tau}^{*})}{\pi_{\tau}(k_{\tau}^{*})}

(57)

Now we want to find bounds for both
1-\pi_{\tau}(k_{\tau}^{*})
and
\pi_{\tau}(k_{\tau}^{*})
. Consider the predictive posterior on the aligned state at time
\tau
:

\pi_{\tau}(k_{\tau}^{*})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{\tau-1}(k)Q_{k,k_{\tau}^{*}}(\bm{z}_{\tau-1})\geq\alpha_{\tau-1}(k_{\tau-1}^{*})Q_{k_{\tau-1}^{*},k_{\tau}^{*}}(\bm{z}_{\tau-1})\geq\alpha_{\tau-1}(k_{\tau-1}^{*})(1-\epsilon^{*}).

(58)

Then, for
1-\pi_{\tau}(k_{\tau}^{*})
:

1-\pi_{\tau}(k_{\tau}^{*})\leq 1-\alpha_{\tau-1}(k_{\tau-1}^{*})(1-\epsilon^{*})=1-\alpha_{\tau-1}(k_{\tau-1}^{*})+\alpha_{\tau-1}(k_{\tau-1}^{*})\epsilon^{*},

(59)

and we can find a recursion for
R_{\tau}
:

\displaystyle R_{\tau}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\ell^{*}}\frac{1-\alpha_{\tau-1}(k_{\tau-1}^{*})+\alpha_{\tau-1}(k_{\tau-1}^{*})\epsilon^{*}}{\alpha_{\tau-1}(k_{\tau-1}^{*})(1-\epsilon^{*})}=e^{-\ell^{*}}\left(\frac{R_{\tau-1}}{1-\epsilon^{*}}+\frac{\epsilon^{*}}{1-\epsilon^{*}}\right)

(60)

\displaystyle\leq R_{\tau-1}\frac{e^{-\ell^{*}}}{1-\epsilon^{*}}+\frac{e^{-\ell^{*}}\epsilon^{*}}{1-\epsilon^{*}}=aR_{\tau-1}+b,

(61)

where we define

a:=\frac{e^{-\ell^{*}}}{1-\epsilon^{*}},\quad b:=\frac{e^{-\ell^{*}}\epsilon^{*}}{1-\epsilon^{*}}.

(62)

Therefore, for
R_{1}>0
, we know:

R_{t}\leq\left(\prod_{\tau=2}^{t}a\right)R_{1}+\sum_{\tau=2}^{t}b\prod_{i=\tau+1}^{t}a=a^{t-1}R_{1}+b\sum_{r=0}^{t-2}a^{r}.

(63)

We notice the sum on the right is a geometric sum, which simplifies as follows:

R_{t}\leq a^{t-1}R_{1}+b\frac{1-a^{t-1}}{1-a}.

(64)

Now, given Eq. (
51
), we obtain
a<1
:

\ell^{*}>\log\frac{1+\epsilon^{*}}{1-\epsilon^{*}}\implies\ell^{*}>\log\frac{1}{1-\epsilon^{*}}\implies a=\frac{e^{-\ell^{*}}}{1-\epsilon^{*}}<1.

(65)

Therefore, the above expression admits a floor
R_{\infty}
defined as

R_{\infty}=\frac{b}{1-a}=\frac{e^{-\ell^{*}}\epsilon^{*}}{1-\epsilon^{*}-e^{-\ell^{*}}}.

(66)

Then,

R_{t}\leq a^{t-1}R_{1}+(1-a^{t-1})R_{\infty}=a^{t-1}(R_{1}-R_{\infty})+R_{\infty}.

(67)

Moreover,

\ell^{*}>\log\frac{1+\epsilon^{*}}{1-\epsilon^{*}}\implies e^{-\ell^{*}}(1+\epsilon^{*})<1-\epsilon^{*}\implies\frac{e^{-\ell^{*}}\epsilon^{*}}{1-\epsilon^{*}-e^{-\ell^{*}}}<1\implies R_{\infty}<1.

(68)

Therefore, Eq. (
51
) implies
R_{\infty}<1
. Since
a<1
, the term
a^{t-1}(R_{1}-R_{\infty})
vanishes as
t\to\infty
. Given
R_{\infty}<1
, there exists
T^{*}<+\infty
such that for all
t\geq T^{*}
,
R_{t}<1
. Finally,

R_{t}<1\implies\frac{1-\alpha_{t}(k_{t}^{*})}{\alpha_{t}(k_{t}^{*})}<1\implies\alpha_{t}(k_{t}^{*})>\frac{1}{2}.

(69)

Therefore, we have
p(s_{t}=k_{t}^{*}|\bm{z}_{1:t})=\alpha_{t}(k_{t}^{*})>\frac{1}{2}
for
t\geq T^{*}
.
∎

The concentration result above shows that, given initial posterior odds
R_{1}
, a likelihood margin
\ell^{*}
, and stickiness
\epsilon^{*}
, there exists a finite time length
T^{*}
after which the posterior is dominated by the aligned regime. The sufficient length
T^{*}
is determined by
R_{1}
,
\ell^{*}
and
\epsilon^{*}
. Below, we provide the minimum length required for posterior dominance.

Corollary B.2
.

Let
\bm{z}_{1:t}
be a history from Lemma
B.1
, with associated regime path
(k_{1}^{*},\dots,k_{t}^{*})
. Define,

a:=\frac{e^{-\ell^{*}}}{1-\epsilon^{*}},\qquad R_{\infty}:=\frac{e^{-\ell^{*}}\epsilon^{*}}{1-\epsilon^{*}-e^{-\ell^{*}}},\qquad R_{1}=\frac{1-p(s_{1}=k^{*}\mid\bm{z}_{1})}{p(s_{1}=k^{*}\mid\bm{z}_{1})},

(70)

Assume
R_{1}>R_{\infty}
. Then any time index
t<+\infty
which satisfies the equation below ensures
p(s_{t}=k^{*}_{t}|\bm{z}_{1:t})>\frac{1}{2}

t\;\geq\;2+\left\lfloor\log\!\left(\frac{1-R_{\infty}}{R_{1}-R_{\infty}}\right)\frac{1}{\log a}\right\rfloor.

(71)

Proof.

By Lemma
B.1
,
R_{t}\leq a^{t-1}(R_{1}-R_{\infty})+R_{\infty}
. For
R_{t}<1
, we have

a^{t-1}(R_{1}-R_{\infty})+R_{\infty}<1\implies a^{t-1}(R_{1}-R_{\infty})<1-R_{\infty}.

Since
R_{1}>R_{\infty}
and
R_{\infty}<1
, dividing both sides gives

a^{t-1}<\frac{1-R_{\infty}}{R_{1}-R_{\infty}}.

Because
0<a<1
, we have
\log a<0
, so taking logarithms reverses the inequality:

t-1>\frac{\log\!\left(\frac{1-R_{\infty}}{R_{1}-R_{\infty}}\right)}{\log a}\implies t\;\geq\;2+\left\lfloor\log\!\left(\frac{1-R_{\infty}}{R_{1}-R_{\infty}}\right)\frac{1}{\log a}\right\rfloor.

∎

In Section
3.1
, we provide a simple example where posterior dominance is possible after one transition. In the example, we assume uniform initial posterior
p(s_{1}=k|\bm{z}_{1})=\tfrac{1}{K},\ k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
. Therefore, the initial posterior odds are
R_{1}=K-1
. Substituting
R_{1}
, together with
R_{\infty}
and

into Corollary

yields the one-step dominance condition

\ell^{*}>\log\frac{K-1+\epsilon^{*}}{1-\epsilon^{*}}.

To further illustrate the intuition behind Assumption

, we contextualise it under the nonlinear Gaussian transitions in Assumption

For simplicity, assume equal isotropic transition variances across regimes, i.e.

44
establishes that multivariate Gaussian distributions are identifiable if and only if the means or covariances are distinct. Wlog., assume covariances are identical, the distribution is unidentifiable on the set where pairs of mean functions coincide. For any model, we can determine this set:

\mathcal{C}_{k,k^{\prime}}:=\Big\{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{mL_{z}}:\bm{m}_{k}(\bm{\alpha})=\bm{m}_{k^{\prime}}(\bm{\alpha})\Big\},\quad\mathcal{C}:=\bigcup_{k\neq k^{\prime}}^{K}\mathcal{C}_{k,k^{\prime}}.

(88)

where for
\hat{p}
we can determine a similar collision set
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
finding similar
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{k,k^{\prime}}
for any
k\neq k^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,\hat{K}\}
. Given that the zero set of an analytic function has Lebesgue measure zero
33
,
\mathcal{C}_{k,k^{\prime}}
has Lebesgue measure zero. Therefore
\mathcal{C}
and
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
have Lebesgue measure zero since
K<+\infty
. Therefore, for any pair of models, Equation (
87
) holds for any
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{Z}
, where
\mathcal{Z}=\mathbb{R}^{mL_{z}}\setminus(\mathcal{C}\cup\hat{\mathcal{C}})
, and
\mu(\mathcal{Z})=\mu(\mathbb{R}^{mL_{z}})
.

Now we show that
\pi(\bm{\alpha},k)
is a constant almost everywhere, i.e.,
\pi(\bm{\alpha},k)=\pi(k)
. First, define the pairwise distance between the mean functions and the minimum distance:

d_{k,k^{\prime}}(\bm{\alpha}):=||\bm{m}_{k}(\bm{\alpha})-\bm{m}_{k^{\prime}}(\bm{\alpha})||,\quad\delta_{0}(\bm{\alpha}):=\min_{k\neq k^{\prime}}d_{k,k^{\prime}}(\bm{\alpha}).

(89)

Choose a non-collision point
\bm{\alpha}_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}
, where we know
\delta_{0}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})>0
, and
\bm{m}_{k}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})=\hat{\bm{m}}_{\hat{k}}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})
with
\hat{k}=\pi(\bm{\alpha}_{0},k)
for any
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
(from Eq. (
87
)). Define
\hat{\delta}_{0}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})>0
similarly for
\hat{\bm{m}}_{\hat{k}},\ \hat{k}\in\{1,\dots,K\}
. Now set
\delta:=\frac{1}{4}\min\{\delta_{0}(\bm{\alpha}_{0}),\hat{\delta}_{0}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})\}
. By continuity, for all
k,\hat{k}\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that we can construct an
\epsilon
-ball
B(\bm{\alpha}_{0},\epsilon)
using
\ell_{2}
-norm, where

||\bm{m}_{k}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})-\bm{m}_{k}(\bm{\alpha})||<\delta,\quad||\hat{\bm{m}}_{\hat{k}}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})-\hat{\bm{m}}_{\hat{k}}(\bm{\alpha})||<\delta,\quad\forall\bm{\alpha}\in B(\bm{\alpha}_{0},\epsilon).

(90)

Now for any
\bm{\alpha}\in B(\bm{\alpha}_{0},\epsilon)
and
k\neq k^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, we can compute a lower bound for
d_{k,k^{\prime}}(\bm{\alpha})
:

\displaystyle d_{k,k^{\prime}}(\bm{\alpha})

\displaystyle=||\bm{m}_{k}(\bm{\alpha})-\bm{m}_{k^{\prime}}(\bm{\alpha})||

\displaystyle\geq||\bm{m}_{k}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})-\bm{m}_{k^{\prime}}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})||-||\bm{m}_{k}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})-\bm{m}_{k}(\bm{\alpha})||-||\bm{m}_{k^{\prime}}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})-\bm{m}_{k^{\prime}}(\bm{\alpha})||

\displaystyle>\delta_{0}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})-2\delta\geq\delta_{0}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})/2.

And similarly, we can establish
\hat{d}_{k,k^{\prime}}(\bm{\alpha})>\hat{\delta}_{0}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})/2
for any
\bm{\alpha}\in B(\bm{\alpha}_{0},\epsilon)
with
\epsilon>0
.

Assume
\pi(\bm{\alpha},k)
is non-constant. Then
\exists\bm{\alpha}_{1}\in B(\bm{\alpha}_{0},\epsilon)
such that:

\hat{k}_{1}=\pi(\bm{\alpha}_{1},k)\neq\pi(\bm{\alpha}_{0},k)=\hat{k}_{0}

(91)

From Eq. (
87
), we have
\bm{m}_{k}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})=\hat{\bm{m}}_{\hat{k}_{0}}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})
and
\bm{m}_{k}(\bm{\alpha}_{1})=\hat{\bm{m}}_{\hat{k}_{1}}(\bm{\alpha}_{1})
and continuity gives

\displaystyle||\hat{\bm{m}}_{\hat{k}_{1}}(\bm{\alpha}_{1})-\hat{\bm{m}}_{\hat{k}_{0}}(\bm{\alpha}_{1})||\leq||\hat{\bm{m}}_{\hat{k}_{1}}(\bm{\alpha}_{1})-\bm{m}_{k}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})||+||\bm{m}_{k}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})-\hat{\bm{m}}_{\hat{k}_{0}}(\bm{\alpha}_{1})||<2\delta\leq\hat{\delta}_{0}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})/2,

but because
\hat{k}_{0}\neq\hat{k}_{1}
, we also have

||\hat{\bm{m}}_{\hat{k}_{1}}(\bm{\alpha}_{1})-\hat{\bm{m}}_{\hat{k}_{0}}(\bm{\alpha}_{1})||=\hat{d}_{\hat{k}_{0},\hat{k}_{1}}(\bm{\alpha}_{1})>\hat{\delta}_{0}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})/2,

and a contradiction is reached. Therefore, for any
\bm{\alpha}_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}
, there exists an open subset
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{Z}
where
\pi(\alpha,k)=\pi_{\mathcal{U}}(k)
for all
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{U}

\bm{m}_{k}(\bm{\alpha})=\hat{\bm{m}}_{\pi_{\mathcal{U}}(k)}(\bm{\alpha}),\quad\forall\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{U},

(92)

Since the mean functions on both models are analytic and distinct, equality on a nonempty open set
\mathcal{U}
implies equality everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{mL_{z}}
by the identity theorem for vector-valued real analytic functions. This implies two distinct permutations
\pi_{\mathcal{U}_{1}}\neq\pi_{\mathcal{U}_{2}}
cannot occur on disjoint open subsets
\mathcal{U}_{1}\subset\mathcal{Z},\ \mathcal{U}_{2}\subset\mathcal{Z}
. Otherwise,
\exists k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
such that
\pi_{\mathcal{U}_{1}}(k)\neq\pi_{\mathcal{U}_{2}}(k)
, and hence forcing
\hat{\bm{m}}_{\pi_{\mathcal{U}_{1}}(k)}\equiv\bm{m}_{k}\equiv\hat{\bm{m}}_{\pi_{\mathcal{U}_{2}}(k)}
contradicting the assumption of distinct means on
\hat{\bm{m}}_{k},\ k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
.

In summary, there exists a global permutation
\pi\in S_{K}
such that
\hat{k}=\pi(k),\ k\in{1,\dots,K}
and the following equivalence holds almost everywhere for
\bm{z}_{t-L_{z}:t-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{mL_{z}}
:

p(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{z}_{t-L_{z}:t-1},s_{t}=k)=\hat{p}\big(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{z}_{t-L_{z}:t-1},s_{t}=\pi(k)\big),\quad\forall\bm{z}_{t-L_{z}:t-1}\in\mathcal{Z},\quad\mu(\mathcal{Z})=\mu(\mathbb{R}^{mL_{z}}),

(93)

and from Gaussian assumptions on
\bm{z}_{t}
, and analyticity on
\bm{m}_{k}
,

\bm{m}_{k}\equiv\hat{\bm{m}}_{\pi(k)},\quad\Sigma_{k}\equiv\hat{\Sigma}_{\pi(k)}.

(94)

∎

B.4.2
Identifiability of switching process (Theorem
3.2
(ii))

Below we present the identifiability of the switching process up to permutation by further assuming (
A4
–
A5

Theorem B.6
.

The MSM is identifiable up to state permutation equivalence, further assuming (
A4
–
A5
) and that the transition matrix
p(s_{t}|s_{t-1},\bm{z}_{t-1})=Q(\bm{z}_{t-1})

\bm{z}_{t-1}
.

Proof.

From Theorem
3.2
, we have established that the number of discrete states
K
is identifiable, and the transitions
p(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{z}_{t-1},s_{t})
and one-step predictive filtering posteriors

are identifiable up to permutation equivalence. This identifiability result extends to all time steps

Now w.l.o.g., fix a particular ordering of the discrete state. Note that for
t>1

is also identifiable for all
k^{\prime}\in\{1,...,K\}
, as
p(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{z}_{t-1},s_{t}=k^{\prime})

p(s_{t}=k^{\prime}|\bm{z}_{1:t})\propto p(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{z}_{t-1},s_{t}=k^{\prime})p(s_{t}=k^{\prime}|\bm{z}_{1:t-1}).

(95)

Now fix
t\geq T^{*}
and choose
\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{Z}
as given by (
A4
–
A5
).
For each regime
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, (
A4
–
A5
) and
Lemma
B.1
provide a history
\bm{z}_{1:t}^{(k)}=(\bm{\alpha}_{k},\bm{\beta})
such that

Define the matrices

A(\bm{z})=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}(\bm{z})\\
\vdots\\
a_{K}(\bm{z})\end{pmatrix},\qquad a_{k}(\bm{z}):=p(s_{t}\mid\bm{z}_{1:t-1}=\bm{\alpha}_{k},\bm{z}_{t}=\bm{z}),

(96)

and

\vdots\\

(97)

For every
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

\bm{z}=\bm{\beta}

for any
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Therefore,
Q
is identifiable up to state permutation equivalence.

∎

Theorem B.8
.

Under assumption (

B.5
Extended discussion on disentanglement and Proof of Theorem
3.3

We first begin with a simple example of
28

Example.

Consider
m=3,\ K=2
with
\bm{\sigma}_{1}=(1,\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{4})
, and
\bm{\sigma}_{2}=(1,\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{1}{2})
. We note the above condition holds given
\bm{\sigma}_{1}/\bm{\sigma}_{2}=(1,2,\tfrac{1}{2})
.

The intuition of Theorem
3.3
starts by considering the case where the ratio vector partially satisfies having distinct elements. For a pair of regimes where
28
is violated, we have dimension groups with equal ratio (from labels
\{1,\dots,m\}
), such that we obtain identifiability across groups, but cannot resolve intra-group affine ambiguity. Therefore, with additional regimes, such that each regime resolves different groups, latent variable disentanglement could be obtained.

Example.

Assume
m=3
, but now
K=3
. Assume for
K=1,2
, the ratio vector is:

\displaystyle\sigma_{1,1}=1,\quad\sigma_{1,2}=0.25,\quad\sigma_{1,3}=0.25,

\displaystyle\sigma_{2,1}=1,\quad\sigma_{2,2}=0.25,\quad\sigma_{3,2}=0.5,

\displaystyle=(1,\quad 1,\quad 0.5).

Intuitively, using a similar Kivva-style SVD-based proof, we can distinguish the first and second groups. Therefore, the affine ambiguity is reduced, as the equivalence introduces a block-diagonal matrix.

\bm{z}^{\prime}=PB\bm{z}+\bm{b}
, where for this example the block diagonal matrix is as follows:

B=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}&b_{2}&0\\
b_{3}&b_{4}&0\\
0&0&b_{5}\\
\end{pmatrix}

Introducing additional regimes will introduce additional restrictions to the equivalence, and therefore, if we obtain another pair, e.g.,
K=2,3
, such that the relation for that becomes

B=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}^{\prime}&0&0\\
0&b_{2}^{\prime}&b_{3}^{\prime}\\
0&b_{4}^{\prime}&b_{5}^{\prime}\\
\end{pmatrix}

we will obtain a diagonal term in the equivalence.

B.5.1
Block-permutation disentanglement

We formalise the above example by proving block-permutation identifiability. Assume we have a non-temporal GMM for
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, expressed as follows:

\bm{z}\sim\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{k},diag(\bm{\sigma}^{2}_{k})).

(108)

Lemma B.9
.

Let
K\geq 2
and
c_{k}>0
for all
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
. Let
\bm{z}^{\prime}=A\bm{z}+\bm{b}
, where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is invertible and
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Assume there exist
k_{1}\neq k_{2}\in\{1,\dots,K\}
such that the ratio vector
\bm{r}_{k_{1},k_{2}}
induces a partition
\{1,\dots,m\}=I_{1}\cup\dots\cup I_{L}
where for any
\ell\in\{1,\dots,L\}
,
L\leq m
,

i,j\in I_{\ell}\ \iff\ r_{k_{1},k_{2},i}=r_{k_{1},k_{2},j}.

(109)

and for
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime},\ I_{\ell}\cap I_{\ell}^{\prime}=\emptyset
.
That is, each
I_{\ell}
contains exactly those dimension labels with the same variance ratio. Then from
\bm{z}^{\prime}
, we can recover an invertible matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
such that
(A^{\prime})^{-1}A=P_{1}BP_{2}
, where
P_{1},P_{2}
are permutation matrices, and
B
is a block diagonal matrix defined as follows:

B=\operatorname{blockdiag}(B_{1},\dots,B_{L})

(110)

where each
B_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{|I_{\ell}|\times|I_{\ell}|},\ \ell\in\{1,\dots,L\}
is invertible.

Remark B.10
.

In particular, if all ratios are distinct, i.e.,
|I_{\ell}|=1
for all
\ell\in\{1,\dots,L\}
(equivalently
L=m
),
B
is diagonal. This reduces to
28
.

Remark B.11
.

If coordinates are ordered by blocks,
P_{2}=I
, and
(A^{\prime})^{-1}A=P_{1}B
.

Proof.

Let
\bm{z}
be GMM-distributed with diagonal covariances
\Sigma_{k}=diag(\bm{\sigma}_{k}),\ k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
. Let
\hat{\Sigma}_{k}
denote the covariance matrix of the
k
-th component of
\bm{z}^{\prime}
. Under the affine transformation
\bm{z}^{\prime}=A\bm{z}+\bm{b}
, we have

\hat{\Sigma}_{k}=A\Sigma_{k}A^{\top},

(111)

where each
\hat{\Sigma}_{k},\ k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
is symmetric positive definite. Fix two regimes
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
(wlog.
k_{1}=1,k_{2}=2
) and consider the matrix
M:=\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}_{2}\hat{\Sigma}_{1}
:

M=\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}_{2}\hat{\Sigma}_{1}=A^{-\top}\Sigma_{2}^{-1}A^{-1}A\Sigma_{1}A^{\top}=A^{-\top}D^{2}A^{\top},

(112)

where
D=diag(\bm{r}_{(1,2)})
. Given Eq (
112
),
M
is similar to the diagonal
D^{2}
, and hence is diagonalisable. From the eigen-decomposition of
M
, we may find invertible
U
such that
M=UD^{2}U^{-1}
. Define
A^{\prime}:=U^{-\top}
. Then,
M=(A^{\prime})^{-\top}D^{2}(A^{\prime})^{\top}
. Combining with Eq. (
112
) yields (after transposing)

AD^{2}A^{-1}=A^{\prime}D^{2}(A^{\prime})^{-1}\implies SD^{2}=D^{2}S,

(113)

with
S=(A^{\prime})^{-1}A
. Given
D^{2}
is diagonal, we have

(d_{j}^{2}-d^{2}_{i})S_{ij}=0,\quad i,j\in\{1,\dots,m\}.

(114)

This implies
S_{ij}=0
whenever
d_{i}\neq d_{j}
, and no restriction appears if
d_{i}=d_{j}
. Given
d_{i}=r_{1,2,i}
,
R
is structured from the partition
\{I_{1},\dots,I_{L}\}
, since
I_{\ell}=\{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}:d_{i}=d_{\ell}\},\ \ell\in\{1,\dots,L\}
. Therefore,
S
is block-diagonal with respect to the partition up to coordinate re-ordering. That is, there exists a permutation matrix
P
, such that:

S=(A^{\prime})^{-1}A=P^{\top}BP,\quad B=\operatorname{blockdiag}(B_{1},\dots,B_{L}),

(115)

where each block
B_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{|I_{\ell}|\times|I_{\ell}|},\ell\in\{1,\dots,L\}
is invertible. Invertibility of each
B_{\ell}
follows from invertibility of
S
. Finally, note that the eigendecomposition of
M
is not unique, as we can reorder eigenvectors. Therefore, we may redefine
\tilde{A}^{\prime}:=A^{\prime}\tilde{P}
. Then,
\tilde{S}=(A^{\prime}\tilde{P})^{-1}A=\tilde{P}^{\top}S
. Since we have shown
S=P^{\top}BP
, it follows that

\tilde{S}=(\tilde{P}^{\top}P^{\top})BP.

(116)

Since the choice of
\tilde{P}
relabels coordinates, we therefore can obtain invertible
A^{\prime}
(replacing
\tilde{A}^{\prime}
by
A^{\prime}
) such that

(A^{\prime})^{-1}A=P_{1}BP_{2},

(117)

with
P_{1},P_{2}
permutation matrices and
B
block-diagonal with invertible blocks.
∎

B.5.2
Proof of Theorem
3.3

Lemma
B.9
shows block-pemutation identifiability for a single pair of regimes. When considering all possible pairs, we can construct the ratio matrix
R
presented under (
A8
), and the proof of Theorem

aggregates all regime pairs together to strengthen the block-permutation identifiability result into a single permutation of latent variables.

Proof.

The proof follows largely from Lemma
B.9
, where the previous commutativity requirement now induces increasing restrictions for every row of the ratio matrix

. For each pair of regimes

C.2
\Omega
SDS implementation details

Synthetic data.

As discussed in Section
4
, for the synthetic experiments we parametrise the emission map using TarFlow
47
. TarFlow provides an expressive invertible transformation that unmixes the observed features without requiring deep implementations. In contrast, affine couplings
[
10
]
require many coupling layers to perform nonlinear unmixing, which we found to destabilise training in higher-dimensional settings.

TarFlow was originally designed for image data. Considering the
n
-dimensional input vectors used in our synthetic dataset, we use the corresponding non-spatial configuration with channel size
1
and patch size
1
. All the synthetic data experiments use
4
TarFlow blocks, where the transformer architecture of each block uses
64
hidden dimensions,
2
layers, and
4
heads. However, instead of the permutation operation used in the original TarFlow architecture, we use invertible LU linear mixing layers, as in RealNVP
10
. We found this modification important in practice, as keeping the original permutation operation led to training failure with the rMSM failing to recover regime information. The output of the final flow block is split into rMSM features
\bm{z}_{t}
and exogenous noise
\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}
.

Video data.

To train videos, we instead use a multi-scale Glow-style architecture
[
27
]
, which provides scalability in high-dimensional image inputs. The architecture consists of invertible
1\times 1
convolutions, squeeze operations, split operations, and coupling layers. Following the original Glow design, the flow is organised into
B
blocks, each containing
L
coupling layers. At each block, the squeeze operation downsamples the spatial dimension by a factor of
2
, and rearranges the extra dimensions into additional channels. The split operation then discards half of the features from subsequent flow operations, but retains them as part of the latent representation.

This hierarchical structure provides a decomposition that we use to distinguish temporal features from exogenous noise. For an input image
\bm{x}_{t}
and
B
blocks, the inverse flow computes:

f^{-1}(\bm{x}_{t})=\big(\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}^{(B-1)},\bm{z}_{t}\big),

where the rMSM features represent the deepest features of the flow, and the remaining features are aggregated as exogenous noise
\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}=\operatorname{concat}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}^{(B-1)})
.
The marginal likelihood is then computed following Eq.
11
. For training, both the bouncing-ball and dancing-video experiments use
7
coupling layers per block with
128
channels. We use
4
blocks for bouncing balls, and
5
blocks for dancing videos. We found that using deeper architectures made the model more prone to state collapse.

rMSM implementation details

Our latent rMSM is implemented similarly to
4
. For synthetic data, the transitions consist of two-layer MLPs with
32
hidden dimensions and a nonlinear activation that matches the one used in the data-generation process (see Section
D.3
). For video experiments, we use three-layer MLPs with
128
hidden dimensions and GeLU activations. In this setting, the recurrent switching process
Q
is parametrised using a two-layer MLP.

C.3

To further motivate likelihood-based models in identifiable deep generative models, we extend the discussion in Section
5

\Omega

used in
4

q(\bm{z}_{1:T}\mid\bm{x}_{1:T})\,q(\bm{s}_{1:T}\mid\bm{x}_{1:T})

2

The results show that VAE-based estimators can recover the regime information accurately, but produce substantially worse continuous latent representations. By directly optimising the exact likelihood,
\Omega

We note this result is more pronounced in settings with many latent dimensions and a comparable number of observed dimensions. In our experiments, VAE-based disentanglement improves in lower-dimensional settings (
m=3
or

D.1
Metrics

Regime
F_{1}
score

We evaluate the performance of our method to extract regimes by computing the
F_{1}
score in terms of estimated switches
\hat{\bm{s}}_{1:T}
. We compute each
\hat{s}_{t}
by selecting the regime label with highest probability weight according to the marginal posterior conditioned on
\bm{z}_{1:T}
: i.e.,
\hat{s}_{t}=\arg\max_{k\in\{1,\dots,K\}}p(s_{t}=k\mid\bm{z}_{1:T})
.

MCC

Mean coefficient correlation (MCC) is a standard metric to evaluate identifiable generative models
[
25
]
. It evaluates whether the learned representation agrees with the ground truth up to permutation and component-wise nonlinearity. Therefore, it fits our linear scaling and permutation equivalence class for the continuous latent variables.

LPIPS

LPIPS
[
48
]
measures perceptual similarity between two images using deep visual features extracted from a pretrained network. Compared with pixel-wise errors such as MSE, LPIPS better reflects human-perceived visual differences, making it suitable for evaluating the perceptual quality of video rollouts.

For synthetic evaluation, we generate data using a similar procedure described in

Appendix E
Broader Impacts

This work contributes to identifiable and interpretable representation learning for sequential data with regime-switching dynamics. By improving the recovery of latent states and switching processes, the proposed framework may support more transparent modelling of complex time series, with potential applications such as climate data, motion analysis, behavioural modelling, and neuroscience. Such interpretability can be beneficial when downstream decisions require understanding of the underlying dynamics rather than relying only on opaque predictions. However, applying these models to real-world human behavioural data requires caution, and inferred regimes without expert validation should not be interpreted as definitive labels.
